Carl Størmer and his Numbers
Matthew Kroesche, Lance L. Littlejohn, Graeme Reinhart

Abstract
. In many proofs of Fermat’s Two Squares Theorem, the smallest least residue solution x 0 x_{0} of the quadratic congruence x 2 ≡ − 1 mod p x^{2}\equiv-1\bmod p plays an essential role; here p p is prime and p ≡ 1 mod 4 p\equiv 1\bmod 4 . Such an x 0 x_{0} is called a Størmer number, named after the Norwegian mathematician and astronomer Carl Størmer (1874-1957). In this paper, we establish necessary and sufficient conditions for x 0 ∈ ℕ x_{0}\in\mathbb{N} to be a Størmer number of some prime p ≡ 1 mod 4 p\equiv 1\bmod 4 . Størmer’s main interest in his investigations of Størmer numbers stemmed from his study of identities expressing π \pi as finite linear combinations of certain values of the Gregory-MacLaurin series for arctan ⁡ ( 1 / x ) \arctan(1/x) . Since less than 600 digits of π \pi were known by 1900, approximating π \pi was an important topic. One such identity, discovered by Størmer in 1896, was used by Yasumasa Kanada and his team in 2002 to obtain 1.24 trillion digits of π \pi . We also discuss Størmer’s work on connecting these numbers to Gregory numbers and approximations of π \pi .



1.
Introduction and Motivation

Fermat’s Two Squares Theorem, which G. H. Hardy said is
“ranked, very justly, as one of the finest of arithmetic”
(see
[
10
]
) states

Theorem 1.1
.

A prime number
p
can be written as the sum of squares of two positive
integers only when
p\equiv 1\bmod 4.
Furthermore, in this case, there exists
unique positive integers
a
and
b
such that

p=a^{2}+b^{2}.

From a historical point of view, L. E. Dickson
[
5
]
attributes the statement of the above theorem
to A. Girard in 1632. In a letter to Mersenne, Fermat’s announced the proof in
1640; E. T. Bell
[
3
, p. 89]
and Dickson
[
7
, p. 228]
say
that Fermat had an irrefutable proof by the method of infinite descent.
However, no record of Fermat’s proof has been found. In 1749, Euler gave the
first recorded proof of the existence part of Fermat’s theorem. Bell further
writes
“It was first proved by the great Euler in 1749 after
he struggled, off and on, for seven years to find a proof”
.
The first proof of uniqueness of the positive integers was given by Gauss in 1801.

Most proofs of the existence of the two squares initially involve a study of the quadratic
congruence
x^{2}\equiv-1\bmod p
. For example, Hermite
[
11
]
and Serret
[
14
]
who, independently in 1848, gave proofs of Fermat’s Two Squares Theorem assuming the least residue solution
x_{0}
of
x^{2}\equiv-1\bmod p
is known. Then, using the Euclidean Algorithm, they produced algorithms to find the positive integers
a,b
satisfying Fermat’s Two Squares Theorem. For further information, see the contribution by Brillhart
[
4
]
who gave an account of Serret’s and Hermite’s algorithms and improved both.

In 1855, British mathematician Henry John Stephen Smith gave an elegant existence proof
of Fermat’s Two Squares Theorem using the Euclidean Algorithm and elementary
theory of determinants and continuants; see
[
5
]
and
[
15
]
. We briefly describe Smith’s method which also emphasizes the importance of the quadratic congruence
x^{2}\equiv-1\bmod p
. For explicit details of Smith’s proof, see
[
5
]
and
[
15
]
.

For
m\in\mathbb{N}
and positive integers
q_{1},q_{2},\ldots,q_{m}\in\mathbb{N}
, the
continuant

[q_{1},q_{2},\ldots,q_{m}]
of length
m
is defined by
[q_{1}]=q_{1}
and, for
m>1
, it is the determinant

[q_{1},q_{2},\ldots,q_{m}]=\left|\begin{array}[c]{rrrrrr}q_{1}&1&0&\cdots&0&0\\
-1&q_{2}&1&0&\cdots&0\\
0&-1&q_{3}&1&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&&&&&\vdots\\
0&0&0&\cdots&q_{m-1}&1\\
0&0&0&\cdots&-1&q_{m}\end{array}\right|.

It is not difficult to see that

[q_{1},q_{2},\ldots,q_{n-1},q_{n}]=[q_{n},q_{n-1},\ldots,q_{2},q_{1}];

for additional properties of continuants, see
[
5
]
and
[
13
]
.
Continuants arise naturally from applying the Euclidean algorithm to the ratio
s/r
where
s,r\in\mathbb{N}
and
r<s
. Indeed, if

\displaystyle s/r

\displaystyle=q_{1}+u/r

\displaystyle r/u

\displaystyle=q_{2}+v/u

\displaystyle\vdots

\displaystyle x/y

\displaystyle=q_{n}+0,

then

s=[q_{1},q_{2},\ldots,q_{n}]\quad\text{and}\quad r=[q_{2},q_{3},\ldots,q_{n}].

Smith proves that if
p\equiv 1\bmod 4
, then there exists an integer
n
and a unique
x_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
satisfying
1<x_{0}\leq(p-1)/2
such that

p=[q_{1},q_{2},\ldots,q_{n-1},q_{n},q_{n},q_{n-1},\ldots,q_{2},q_{1}]

and

(1.1)

x_{0}=[q_{2},q_{3}\ldots,q_{n-1},q_{n},q_{n},q_{n-1},\ldots,q_{2},q_{1}].

Notice that this continuant representation of
p
is
palindromic
. The integer
x_{0}
is the smallest least residue of
x^{2}\equiv-1\bmod{p}
. Furthermore, using elementary properties of determinants, Smith shows

\displaystyle p

\displaystyle=[q_{1},q_{2},\ldots q_{n-1},q_{n}][q_{n},q_{n-1},\ldots,q_{2},q_{1}]

(1.2)

\displaystyle+[q_{1},q_{2},\ldots,q_{n-1}][q_{n-1},\ldots,q_{2},q_{1}]

\displaystyle=[q_{1},q_{2},\ldots,q_{n}]^{2}+[q_{1},q_{2},\ldots,q_{n-1}]^{2}.

Consequently, determining the least residue solution
x_{0}
of
x^{2}\equiv-1\bmod{p}
allows us, through the algorithm in (

), to explicitly find positive integers
a,b
satisfying
p=a^{2}+b^{2}
. To illustrate Smith’s argument, consider
p=13
; in this case
x_{0}=5
as can be seen from the following calculations using the Euclidean Algorithm,

\frac{13}{5}=\mathbf{2}+\frac{3}{5},\frac{5}{3}=\mathbf{1}+\frac{2}{3},\frac{3}{2}=\mathbf{1}+\frac{1}{2},\frac{2}{1}=\mathbf{2}+0\text{ so }p=[2,1,1,2]\text{ and }x_{0}=5=[1,1,2].

It is clear that
x_{0}\equiv-1\bmod 13
.
Moreover, employing (
1.2
), we see that

13=[2,1,1,2]=[2,1][1,2]+[2][2]=[2,1]+[2]^{2}=3^{3}+2^{2}.

As we will see in the next section,
x_{0}=5
is called the
Størmer number
for the prime

.

The key point in most proofs of Fermat’s Two Squares Theorem is to first identify the positive integer
x_{0}
satisfying the two conditions
1<x_{0}\leq(p-1)/2
and
x_{0}^{2}\equiv-1\bmod p
. In practice, however, when the prime
p\equiv 1\bmod 4
is a large prime, it is difficult to find
x_{0}
; see Tables 1 and 2 below where it seems that there is considerable randomness in the list of Størmer numbers. In this paper, we obtain necessary and sufficient conditions for testing when
x_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
is a Stormer number and, in the case that it is, we show how to find the associated unique prime number
p
; see Theorem
2.3
below.

The contents of this paper are as follows. In Section
2
, we define Størmer numbers and prove several basic facts about them, including our characterization result (see Theorem
2.3
) of Størmer numbers. As far as we can tell, the results in Section
2

3

[

in 2008 that this natural density is

. Section
4
gives a brief biographical sketch of Carl Størmer; Størmer was a well-known Norwegian number theorist who was also recognized for his ground-breaking work in astronomy and his research on the aurora borealis. Lastly, in Section
5
, we discuss Størmer’s work on approximating
\pi
using the MacLaurin/Gregory series for
\arctan(1/x)
, that is,

t_{x}:=\arctan\left(\frac{1}{x}\right)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{k}\frac{1}{(2k+1)x^{2k+1}}\quad(|x|\geq 1).

Of course, it is well known that
t_{1}=\pi/4
. Størmer (see
[
16

18

is a
non

can be written as a unique finite linear combination of

is a Størmer number (see Theorem
5.1
). We illustrate Størmer’s method by considering several examples.

Remark 2.2
.

For a prime
p,
it is well known by Euler’s Criterion (see
[
1
, Theorems

) that the quadratic congruence

(2.1)

has exactly two least residue solutions when
p\equiv 1\bmod 4
(and
no solutions when
p\equiv 3\bmod 4
). When
p\equiv 1\bmod 4,
one of these solutions lies in the interval

(1,(p-1)/2]
and the other solution
y_{0}=p-x_{0}
lies in the interval

((p-1)/2,p)
. Moreover, it is straightforward to see that
x_{0}=(p-1)/2
satisfies (
2.1
) only when
p=5

(with
x_{0}=2).
Hence, for primes
p>5
, which are
congruent to

its Størmer number
x_{0}
satisfies
the strict inequality
1<x_{0}<(p-1)/2.

Our first result is that Størmer’s function is injective.

Theorem 2.1
.

The Størmer function
S:\mathbb{P}_{4n+1}\rightarrow\mathbb{N}
, defined in Definition
2.1
, is 1-1;
that is, if
x_{0}=S(p)
for some prime number

then
x_{0}
cannot be the
Størmer number of any other prime
p^{\prime}\in\mathbb{P}_{4n+1}.

Proof.

Suppose
S(p_{1})=S(p_{2})
but suppose
p_{1}\neq p_{2}.
Suppose

p_{1}>p_{2}.
Since
p_{j}|(x_{0}^{2}+1)
and we assume
p_{1}\neq p_{2},
we
see that
p_{1}p_{2}|(x_{0}^{2}+1).
Hence

(2.2)

x_{0}^{2}+1\geq p_{1}p_{2}.

Furthermore, since
p_{j}
prime, we see that
p_{2}\leq p_{1}-2.
Since
x_{0}\leq(p_{2}-1)/2,
we see that

\displaystyle x_{0}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq(p_{2}-1)^{2}/4

\displaystyle=\left(p_{2}^{2}-2p_{2}+1\right)/4

\displaystyle\leq\left(p_{2}(p_{1}-2)-2p_{2}+1\right)/4

\displaystyle=(p_{1}p_{2}-4p_{2}+1)/4

and hence

(2.3)

4x_{0}^{2}-1\leq p_{1}p_{2}-4p_{2}<p_{1}p_{2}.

However,
x_{0}^{2}+1<4x_{0}^{2}-1

2.3
)

However

)

)

∎

Suppose

Suppose, to the contrary, that

can be the Størmer number of some prime

Theorem 2.3
.

Suppose

) where we assume the primes satisfy

. Moreover, if this condition

Otherwise,

Suppose

that is,

x_{0}\leq\frac{p_{m}-1}{2}.

Since
p_{m}|(x_{0}^{2}+1),
we see that

is the Størmer number for

Conversely, suppose

for

In fact, Everest and Harman

conjecture

(3.1)

\lim_{n\to\infty}\dfrac{|\{S(p)\mid p\leq n\}|}{n}=\ln 2.

We now give a heuristic/probabilistic argument of (
3.1
). We note that a rigorous proof still remains elusive. Let
x_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
. If
x_{0}=S(p)
for some prime
p\equiv 1\bmod 4
, then
p\geq 2x_{0}+1
. Further, since
x_{0}^{2}\equiv 1\bmod p
and
p\mid(x_{0}^{2}+1)
, we see that
p\leq x_{0}^{2}+1
. Thus, for any prime
p\equiv 1\bmod 4
,
S(p)
can only be the Størmer number for
p
if we have

2x_{0}+1\leq p\leq x_{0}^{2}+1.

Assuming each of the integers from
1
to
(p-1)/2
is equally likely to be the Størmer number for
p
, we can informally think of the “probability” that
x_{0}
is a Størmer number for the prime
p
as the reciprocal of
(p-1)/2
, that is,
2/(p-1)
. Then since
S(p)
is injective, so that
x_{0}
is a Størmer number for at most one prime, we can sum these all up to see that the “probability” that
x_{0}
is a Størmer number for any prime
p
is

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}2x_{0}+1\leq p\leq x_{0}^{2}+1\\
p\equiv 1\bmod 4\end{subarray}}\frac{2}{p-1}.

This sum behaves like

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}2x_{0}\leq p\leq x_{0}^{2}\\
p\equiv 1\bmod 4\end{subarray}}\frac{2}{p}

since the effect of altering
p
by 1 goes to zero in the limit. Since in the limit, there are the same number of primes of the form
p\equiv 1\bmod 4
and
p\equiv 3\bmod 4
(due to the Prime Number Theorem for arithmetic progressions), we write this as half the sum over all primes – not just the primes of the form
p\equiv 1\bmod 4
– to obtain

\sum_{2x_{0}\leq p\leq x_{0}^{2}}\frac{1}{p}.

Then using Merten’s estimate (see
[
2
, Lemma 4.10]
) we obtain

\sum_{p\leq x_{0}}\frac{1}{p}\sim\ln\ln x_{0},

we write this asymptotic as

\sum_{p\leq x_{0}^{2}}\frac{1}{p}-\sum_{p<2x_{0}}\frac{1}{p}\sim\ln(\ln x_{0}^{2})-\ln(\ln 2x_{0})\sim\ln\left(\frac{\ln x_{0}^{2}}{\ln 2x_{0}}\right)\sim\ln\left(\frac{2\ln x_{0}}{\ln x_{0}}\right)\sim\ln 2.

The gap in rigor in this argument is, of course, the introduction of probability and the assumption that
x_{0}
has a
2/(p-1)
chance of being the Størmer number for any given prime
p
.

4.
A Biographical Sketch of Carl Størmer

Figure 1.
Carl Størmer

Carl Størmer (1874-1957) was a preeminent Norwegian mathematician, botanist,
and astrophysicist. For his lifetime contributions to mathematics and astronomy,
particularly his study of the aurora borealis and the motion of charged particles in magnetic fields, he was bestowed several honors. A crater on the far side of the Moon is named after him. He was elected to
several Scandinavian academies, the Royal Society of London and the Paris
Academy of Sciences. He was given honorary degrees by the universities of
Oxford, Copenhagen and the Sorbonne. The Paris Academy of Sciences awarded him
their Janssen Medal in 1922. He was invited to give a one-hour lecture, on his
scientific research on the aurora borealis, at the International Congress
of Mathematicians in Toronto in 1924 and, in 1936, he was president of the
International Congress of Mathematicians which was held in Oslo.

Størmer entered the University of Christiania (Oslo’s previous name) in 1892
and obtained his candidates’ degree (similar to a Ph.D. degree) in 1897. By
this time, Størmer had written several mathematical articles, primarily in
number theory, as well as a number of short notes in botany. From 1898-1900,
Størmer studied at the Sorbonne under mathematical giants Émile Picard,
Henri Poincaré, Camille Jordan, Gaston Darboux, and Édouard Goursat.
His output of mathematical papers continued with twelve papers on series,
number theory, and the theory of functions between 1896 and 1902. He worked
with Sylow and Holst to produce two volumes in 1902 to celebrate the centenary
of Niels Henrik Abel’s (1802-1829) birth.

Between 1896-1899, Størmer published two papers on connecting Størmer numbers to Gregory numbers, the subject of this paper. In 1897, he proved that if
P
is a finite set of prime numbers, then there are only finitely many consecutive integers having only the numbers from
P
as their prime factors. Moreover, Størmer develops an algorithm for finding all such pairs of consecutive integers. To prove his result, he reduced the problem to solving a finite number of Pell’s equations. This paper
[
17
]
was highly praised by the number theorist Louis Mordell.

In 1903, Størmer was appointed professor of pure mathematics at the University of Oslo, a position he held until 1946. In 1934, Størmer published a paper in the Norwegian Mathematical Society entitled ‘Ramanujan – A Remarkable Genius’. At the insistence of fellow Norwegian Atle Selberg, a leading authority in number theory, this paper was reprinted in the same journal in 1989. Selberg was a teenager in 1934 and remarked that Størmer’s paper on Ramanujan contributed to arousing his interest in mathematics.

During his time at the
University of Oslo, Størmer’s interests were also drawn to another
scientific area: the study of the aurora borealis. A colleague of Størmer,
Kristian Berkeland, had put forward a theory in 1896 that auroras were caused
by electrons emitted by the sun which interacted with the earth’s magnetic
field. Poincaré had, in the same year, solved the differential equations
resulting from the motion of a charged particle in the field of a single pole.
This was not the situation for an aurora since the magnetic field of the earth
is a dipole. Størmer specifically attacked this problem. His papers on this
subject constitute a major part of his life’s work. Størmer wrote two books,

From the Depths of Space to the Heart of the Atom
, which was
translated into five languages, and
The Polar Aurora
which contains
both his experimental work on aurorae and his mathematical attempts to model
them. The 2013 book
Carl Størmer: Aurora Pioneer
, by Egeland and
Burke
[
8
]
is a comprehensive account of Størmer’s
contributions to the study of auroras.

We end this sketch with a rather humorous note about Carl Størmer. He was an avid
photographer which he put to use in his study of auroras. He purchased a
miniature
spy
camera that he carefully concealed in his jacket. He
would walk the streets of Oslo and take photographs of people. He
published two works of his ‘snapshots of famous people’ in 1942 and 1943. Long
afterwards, nearing the age of 70, these photographs formed the subject of a
major exhibition in Oslo. Many of these photographs exist on the internet and
they capture a fascinating look at life in Oslo during the early part of the 20th century.

5.
Application of Størmer Numbers: Gregory Numbers and Approximations of
\pi

As of this writing, there are more than
200

trillion
digits of
\pi
known. Indeed, using A. Yee’s y-cruncher (see
[
21
]
) computer program, the StorageReview Lab Team computed the first 202,112,290,000,000 digits of
\pi
in 2024; see
[
19
]
.
However, in the early 1900’s, there were less than

600
digits of
\pi
known. Of course,
manual
calculations were the only option then to approximate
\pi
long before the modern computer age. Archimedes, circa 250 BC, showed that

\frac{223}{71}<\pi<\frac{22}{7}

Zu’s ratio, discovered by the Chinese astronomer Zu Chongzhi in the 5th century, gives

\pi\approx\frac{355}{113}\approx 3.14159292035,

an approximation within
0.000009\%
of the value of
\pi
; in fact, Zu’s approximation
of
\pi
is the best rational approximation to
\pi
with a denominator of
four or fewer digits.

In the
18^{\text{th}}
century, due to the work of John Machin and Leonhard Euler, techniques were developed to approximate
\pi
using
Gregory numbers

t_{x}
, defined by

t_{x}:=\arctan\left(\frac{1}{x}\right)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{k}\frac{1}{(2k+1)x^{2k+1}}\qquad(|x|\geq 1).

Of course, it is well-known that that
t_{1}=\pi/4
; this particular series is known as the Mādhava-Gregory-Leibniz series. Convergence is slow; indeed, roughly speaking, one must take 10 times more terms to add one extra decimal
place of accuracy in the computation of
\pi
. Euler established

(5.1)

t_{1}=5t_{7}+2t_{79/3},

while
Machin proved that

(5.2)

t_{1}=4t_{5}-t_{239}

which, of course, is equivalent to the well-known trigonometric
identity

(5.3)

\pi=16\arctan\left(\frac{1}{5}\right)-4\arctan\left(\frac{1}{239}\right).

Convergence is faster with Machin’s formula; indeed, the first 100 terms of the series on the right-hand side of Machin’s formula (
5.2
) produces the first 140 digits of
\pi
.

In the late
19^{\text{th}}
century, Carl Størmer noticed an important connection between Gaussian primes and identities involving Gregory numbers. It is the purpose of this section to discuss his work; the mathematics behind the technique he develops is fascinating and deserves attention certainly not because of its application to approximating
\pi
in today’s technological world but for its sheer mathematical elegance and beauty. For further information on Størmer’s work on Gregory series and approximating
\pi
, see Størmer’s work in
[
16
,
18
]
as well as the book by Conway and Guy
[
6
]
.

Among his discoveries, in 1896, Størmer proved the identity

(5.4)

t_{1}=44t_{57}+7t_{239}-12t_{682}+24t_{12943}.

In 2002, the Japanese computer scientist Yasumasa Kanada and his team used this
identity to help obtain the first
1.2411
trillion digits of
\pi

which, at the time, was the world record for most digits of
\pi
. We next discuss Størmer’s theorem connecting Gregory series with Størmer numbers; for a reference, see
[
6
]
and
[
16
]
.

Theorem 5.1

(Størmer)
.

If
x\in\mathbb{N}
is not a Stormer number, then
t_{x}
can be uniquely expressed as a finite linear combination of
t_{n}
’s where each
n
is a Størmer number and each
n<x
.

We note that in Machin’s formula (
5.2
),
239
is not a Størmer number but both 1 and 5 are while, in Størmer’s formula (
5.4
), none of the integers 57, 239, 682, 12943 are Størmer numbers.

Recall that a complex number
z=a+bi
, where
a,b\neq 0
, is a Gaussian prime if
a^{2}+b^{2}
is prime. For example,
i\pm 1
and
3i\pm 2
are Gaussian primes but
3\pm i
is not. To illustrate Størmer’s work, we discuss an important relationship between Gregory numbers
t_{x}
, when
x\in\mathbb{N}
, and Gaussian primes. If
z=a+bi
, where
a>0
and
\theta=\arg(z)
, where
\arg(z)\in(-\pi/2,\pi/2)
, then
\tan\theta=b/a
showing that
\theta=\arctan(b/a)
. In particular, for
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we see that

(5.5)

\arg(n+i)=\arctan\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)=t_{n}.

Moreover, since
\arg(z_{1}z_{2})=\arg(z_{1})+\arg(z_{2})
, we see, for
c>0
and
k\in\mathbb{N}
, that

(5.6)

\displaystyle\arg(cz)

\displaystyle=\arg(z)

(5.7)

\displaystyle\arg(z^{k})

\displaystyle=k\arg(z).

Now suppose that
t_{n}=t_{n_{1}}+t_{n_{2}}
, where
n,n_{1},n_{2}\in\mathbb{N}
. Then, from (
5.5
),

(5.8)

\arg((n_{1}+i)(n_{2}+i))=t_{n_{1}}+t_{n_{2}}=t_{n}.

With an abuse of notation, but for better readability, we shall write (
5.8
) as

(5.9)

n+i=(n_{1}+i)(n_{2}+i).

This is the same notation adopted by Conway and Guy
[
6
]
.

In order to use Theorem
5.1
, we must make repeated use of the identities in (
5.5
), (
5.6
), (
5.7
), and (
5.8
). We describe how we do this in the next few paragraphs.

Suppose
n\in\mathbb{S}^{\text{c}}
; that is,
n
is not a Størmer number. To determine the representation of
t_{n}
guaranteed by Størmer’s Theorem, we first consider the Gaussian prime factorization of
(n+i)
. Within this factorization, there are four simple forms that factors may assume:

(I)

m\pm i
with
m\in\mathbb{S}
;

(II)

a+bi
with
a,b\in\mathbb{Z}
and
\gcd(a,b)=1
(which we may assume from (
5.6
));

(III)

l\pm i
with
l\in\mathbb{S}^{\mathsf{c}}
;

(IV)

\pm i
.

Because equality depends only on the argument, all positive scalars in the Gaussian prime factorization may be cast aside as they do not affect the argument of the terms.

These rules aim to maintain the
(n+i)
term and convert all other terms in the prime factorizaton to factors of the form (I). To aid in this
process there are a few
key identities
that are useful in our calculations:

(a)

(1-i)^{4}=(1+i)^{4}=-1

(b)

(1+i)^{7}=(1-i)

(c)

(1-i)^{7}=(1+i)

(d)

i=(1+i)^{2}

(e)

-i=(1-i)^{2}

(f)

i(a+bi)=(-b+ai)
;

again, equality in these ‘identities’ is in the sense of (
5.8
), namely up to positive integer multiples.

To simplify terms of form (II), we ‘flatten’ a complex number
a+bi
by replacing it by a complex number of the form
x\pm i
. To do this, we find
c,d,e\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
(a+bi)(c+di)=(e\pm i)
. Expanding and separating the real and imaginary components, this equation yields the system of equations:

\displaystyle ac-bd

\displaystyle=e

\displaystyle ad+bc

\displaystyle=\pm 1.

In particular, we must find a solution
(c,d)
to the linear Diophantine equation
ad+bc=1
. Ideally,
d=\pm 1
, so
(c+di)
is of the form (I). However, in many cases as we illustrate below, this process of flattening might need to be repeated several times.

Example 5.1
.

The complex number
18-5i
is of the form (II). To reduce it to a complex number of form (I), we solve the Diophantine equation
18d-5c=1
to obtain
(c,d)=(7,2)
which corresponds to
7+2i
. Since this latter number is not of the form (I), we solve
7d+2c=1
to obtain
(c,d)=(4,-1)=4-i
which is of the form (I).

By calculating the Størmer expansions of Gregory numbers in increasing order, terms of form (III) may be substituted with their expanded forms. Finally,
terms of form (IV) may be dealt with via our above key identities.

We note that others have studied Størmer numbers in their connection to the arctangent or cotangent functions. Since
\arctan(1/x)=\arccot(x)
, Lehmer
[
12
]
studied identities of the form

\arccot(a/b)=\arccot(n_{0})-\arccot(n_{1})+\arccot(n_{2})-\cdots

where the integers
n_{j}
are obtained by solving the recurrences

a_{j}=n_{j}b_{j}+b_{j+1}\quad(0\leq b_{j+1}<b_{j}),\quad a_{j+1}=a_{j}n_{j}+b_{j}

with initial conditions
a_{0}=a,b_{0}=b
.
Todd (see
[
20
]
and the references cited therein) uses the notation
(n)=\arctan(n)
instead of
t_{n}=\arctan(1/n)
so, in our language,
(n)=t_{1/n}
. Todd defines, for
n\in\mathbb{N}
, (
n
) to be
irreducible
if it cannot be written as a finite sum of the form

\sum\alpha_{j}(n_{j}),

where each
\alpha_{j}\in\mathbb{Z}
; otherwise,
(n)
is
reducible
. He proves the following theorem (see
[
20
, Theorem B]
) which is a slightly weaker version of Theorem
5.1
.

Theorem 5.2
.

For
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
n
is a Stormer number if and only if
(n)
is irreducible.

In
[
20
, Theorem A]
, Todd also proves the following characterization theorem:

Theorem 5.3
.

For
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
n
is a Stormer number if and only if all prime factors of
1+n^{2}
should occur among the prime factors of
1+m^{2}
for
m=1,2,\cdots n-1
.

In the examples that follow, we use our notation to find identities involving Gregory numbers
t_{n}
.

Example 5.2
.

The Gaussian prime factorization of
3+i
is given by

3+i=-i(1+i)(1+2i)=(1+i)(2-i)

so
t_{3}=t_{1}-t_{2}
. We remark that, using Todd’s notation, this formula is equivalent to
(3)=3(1)-(2)
.

Example 5.3
.

Vega’s identity is

(5.10)

t_{1}=2t_{3}+t_{7}.

The Gaussian prime factorizations of
3+i
and
7+i
are

(5.11)

\displaystyle 3+i

\displaystyle=-i(1+i)(1+2i)=(1+i)(2-i)

(5.12)

\displaystyle 7+i

\displaystyle=-i(1+i)(2+i)^{2}=(1-i)(2+i)^{2}.

From (
5.11
), we see that
t_{3}=t_{1}-t_{2}
while (
5.12
) yields
t_{7}=-t_{1}+2t_{2}
. Combining these two identities gives us (
5.10
).

Example 5.4
.

The number
n=21
is not a Størmer number by Theorem
2.3
since
21^{2}+1=2\cdot 13\cdot 17
and
2n+1=43>17
. The Gaussian Prime factorization of
21+i
is,

(5.13)

21+i=(-i)(1+i)(3+2i)(4+i).

Both
1
and
4
are Størmer numbers so we only need to convert
-i
and
3+2i
. A solution of the equation
3d+2c=-1
is
(c,d)=(1,-1)=1-i
. Multiplying both sides of (
5.13
) by
1-i
, we find that

(1-i)(21+i)=(-i)(1+i)(5-i)(4+i).

Next we use key identity (e) above, namely
(-i)=(1-i)^{2}
, to obtain

(1-i)(21+i)=(1-i)^{2}(1+i)(5-i)(4+i).

To isolate
21+i
, we multiply both sides by
1+i
and obtain

21+i=(1-i)^{2}(1+i)^{2}(5-i)(4+i)=(5-i)(4+i)

and this yields the identity
t_{21}=t_{4}-t_{5}
.

Example 5.5
.

Since
70^{2}+1=13^{2}\cdot 29
, Theorem
2.3
says that
n=70
is not a Størmer number. The Gaussian prime factorization of
70+i
is

(5.14)

70+i=-(2+3i)^{2}(2+5i).

We first eliminate the
2+5i
term. By inspection, a solution to
2d+5c=1
is
(c,d)=(1,-2)
so multiplying (
5.14
) by
1-2i
yields

(5.15)

(1-2i)(70+i)=-(2+3i)^{2}(12+i).

Solving
2d+3c=1
produces the solution
(c,d)=(1,-1)
; multiplying (
5.15
) by
(1-i)^{2}
yields

(1-i)^{2}(1-2i)(70+i)=-(5+i)^{2}(12+i).

Finally, multiplying both sides by
-i=(1-i)^{2}
(see key identity (d)), we obtain

-(1-i)^{2}(2+i)(70+i)=-(1-i)^{2}(5+i)^{2}(12+i),

which yields the identity
t_{2}+t_{70}=2t_{5}+t_{12}
or
t_{70}=-t_{2}+2t_{5}+t_{12}
.

Example 5.6
.

We use Størmer’s technique to establish Euler’s identity in (
5.1
). We begin by flattening
79+3i
; a calculation shows that

(5.16)

(79+3i)(-26+i)=-2057+i.

The Gaussian prime factorization of
-2057+i
is

\displaystyle-2057+i

\displaystyle=i(1+i)(1+2i)^{5}(1+26i)

(5.17)

\displaystyle=(1+i)(1+2i)^{5}(-26+i).

Substituting (
5.17
) into (
5.16
) yields

(5.18)

(79+3i)(-26+i)=(1+i)(1+2i)^{5}(-26+i),

or

(5.19)

79+3i=(1+i)(1+2i)^{5}.

We now flatten
1+2i
by solving
d+2c=1
to obtain
(c,d)=(-1,3)
. Calculations show
(-1+3i)^{5}=-(316+12i)=-4(79+3i)
and
(1+2i)^{5}(-1+3i)^{5}=(-7+i)^{5}
. Hence, multiplying both sides of (
5.19
) by
(-1+3i)^{5}
yields

-(79+3i)^{2}=(1+i)(-7+i)^{5}=-(1+i)(7-i)^{5},

or
2t_{79/3}=t_{1}-5t_{7}
.

Example 5.7
.

In our last example, we verify Machin’s identity in (
5.2
). The Gaussian factorization of
239+i
is given by

239+i=i(1+i)(2+3i)^{4}

Since
i=(1+i)^{2}
, we see that

(5.20)

239+i=(1+i)^{3}(2+3i)^{4}.

To eliminate
2+3i
, we solve
2d+3c=1
to obtain
(c,d)=(1,-1)
. Since
(1-i)(2+3i)=5+i
, we multiply both sides of (
5.20
) by
(1-i)^{4}
to obtain

(5.21)

(239+i)(1-i)^{4}=(1+i)^{3}(5+i)^{4}.

Lastly, multiply both sides of (
5.21
) by
(1+i)^{4}
and employing key identity (c) yields

239+i=(1+i)^{7}(5+i)^{4}=(1-i)(5+i)^{4},

and so
t_{239}=-t_{1}+4t_{5}
.
