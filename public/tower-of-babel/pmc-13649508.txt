Adoptive cell therapy in lung cancer: progress, challenges, and prospects
Authors: Jingyi Li, Chan Tong, Yongchang Chen, Bo Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLung cancer is the leading cause of cancer-related deaths worldwide. Over the past decade, immunotherapy, represented by immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) targeting programmed cell death protein-1/programmed cell death protein ligand-1 (PD-1/PD-L1) or cytotoxic T lymphocyte associated antigen-4 (CTLA-4), has revolutionized the treatment paradigm of lung cancer, reducing the risk of death by approximately 30%. However, approximately 70% of patients with advanced non-small cell lung cancer are either non-responsive to ICIs or initially respond to treatment but eventually experience disease progression due to acquired resistance. This subset of non-responsive or ultimately resistant patients is often characterized by complete exhaustion of endogenous anti-tumor T cells, which are unable to exert anti-tumor effects, thereby leading to ICI non-response or resistance. Correspondingly, the adoptive transfer of immune cells has shown promising efficacy in numerous clinical studies involving this patient population. Nevertheless, the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) has not yet approved any cell therapy strategy for lung cancer, primarily because of factors such as the hostile tumor microenvironment and T cell exhaustion. Herein, we summarize the current progress in cell therapy for lung cancer, analyze key challenges, and propose potential solutions.Keywords: Lung cancer, Immunotherapy, Adoptive cell therapy

Introduction

Lung cancer is a malignant tumor with high incidence and mortality rates. Globally, approximately 2.2 million new cases and 1.8 million deaths from lung cancer occur every year, of which approximately 85% are non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) and 15% are small cell lung cancer (SCLC).1,2 Although traditional treatment modalities, such as surgery, chemotherapy, and radiotherapy, have improved patient prognosis to a certain extent, the 5-year survival rate of patients with advanced lung cancer remains <20%.3 Tumor recurrence and drug resistance remain urgent challenges to be addressed in clinical treatment. Over the past decade, immunotherapy, represented by immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) targeting programmed cell death protein-1/programmed cell death protein ligand-1 (PD-1/PD-L1) or cytotoxic T lymphocyte associated antigen-4 (CTLA-4), has revolutionized the treatment landscape for lung cancer, increasing the 5-year survival rate of patients with advanced NSCLC to approximately 30%.4,5 Despite these remarkable achievements, PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors still have limitations, such as limited treatment response rates and primary or secondary resistance. The status of tumor-infiltrating CD8⁺ T cells is crucial for the response to PD-1/PD-L1 antibody therapy.6,7 Single-cell sequencing results have shown that tumor-infiltrating CD8⁺ T cells in patients with NSCLC constitute a highly heterogeneous population.6,7 T cell transcription factor 1 (TCF-1)⁺ precursor exhausted CD8⁺ T cells are the key cell subset mediating the response to PD-1/PD-L1 antibody treatment.8 In contrast, the core mechanism underlying poor response or acquired resistance to ICIs in patients is often the profound exhaustion of tumor-infiltrating CD8⁺ T cells, accompanied by a significant reduction in the proportion of TCF-1⁺ precursor exhausted CD8⁺ T cells.9 When endogenous TCF-1⁺ precursor exhausted CD8⁺ T cells are depleted or dysfunctional, adoptive cell therapy (ACT) emerges as a promising strategy.

Immune cells that directly exert anti-tumor effects include CD8⁺ T and natural killer (NK) cells. Current adoptive T cell therapies include tumor-infiltrating lymphocyte (TIL) therapy, chimeric antigen receptor (CAR)-T cell immunotherapy, and T cell receptor (TCR)-engineered T cell (TCR-T) therapy. TIL therapy involves the adoptive transfer of autologous tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes from patients. TIL therapy yields an objective response rate (ORR) of approximately 21.4% in patients with advanced NSCLC.10 CAR-T and TCR-T cell therapies utilize genetic engineering approaches to modify peripheral blood T cells into tumor-reactive T cells. Specifically, CAR-T cell therapy enables T cells to express CAR constructs that recognize the extracellular domains of tumor antigens, thereby allowing tumor cell death in a major histocompatibility complex (MHC)-independent manner. Currently, CAR-T therapies targeting epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR), human epidermal growth factor receptor-2 (HER2), mesothelin (MSLN), carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA), and other antigens are being explored in early-phase lung cancer trials.11,12 Unlike CARs, TCRs typically exhibit moderate antigen affinity and activate physiological signaling pathways, which may enhance T cell persistence and thus improve anti-tumor efficacy. Therefore, TCR-T therapy may have greater advantages for the treatment of solid tumors, such as better adaptation to the harsh solid tumor microenvironment (TME).13 TCR-T cells targeting New York esophageal squamous cell carcinoma-1 (NY-ESO-1) offer new hope for patients with advanced lung cancer. In a clinical case report published in Oncology Letters, a female patient with advanced lung adenocarcinoma, who was positive for both human leukocyte antigen (HLA)-A2 (HLA-A2), and NY-ESO-1, achieved a partial response (PR) lasting 4 months following adoptive immunotherapy with NY-ESO-1-targeted TCR-T cells.14 NK cell-based cytokine-induced killer (CIK) cell therapy and NK cell therapy have also achieved certain clinical breakthroughs. The combination regimen of CIK cell therapy with PD-1 inhibitors and chemotherapy significantly prolongs the progression-free survival (PFS) of patients with advanced NSCLC and shows a trend toward long-term survival benefits15; additionally, NK cell therapy has excellent potential in reversing EGFR-targeted drug resistance, substantially improving the response rate.16

Although lung cancer cell therapy is still in the clinical trial phase and the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) has not yet approved any cell therapy products for lung cancer, the available clinical data not only highlight the strategic value of immune cell therapy in the comprehensive treatment of lung cancer but also open up new avenues for overcoming the drug resistance in ICIs and targeted therapies. Despite the considerable clinical potential of ACT for lung cancer treatment, multiple challenges remain. For instance, the abnormal tumor vasculature and dense stroma in lung cancer not only restrict T cell infiltration into the tumor, but the immunosuppressive TME also impairs the persistent survival and functional activity of T cells. Furthermore, given the high heterogeneity of lung cancer, there is a significant variability in antigen expression among different tumor cells. Consequently, CAR-T or TCR-T therapies targeting a single antigen are prone to failure owing to the downregulation or loss of target antigens. In the future, as more clinical data accumulate and treatment strategies are optimized, cell therapy is expected to become an important component of precision immunotherapy systems for lung cancer, bringing hope to more patients. This review provides a comprehensive overview of the latest advances in lung cancer cell therapy, along with the challenges and corresponding strategies for future research and clinical applications.

Current ACT in lung cancer

ACT involves the infusion of TILs or T cells derived from the peripheral blood into patients. The development of ACT began with the ex vivo expansion and adoptive transfer of TILs, a process that amplifies a heterogeneous population of endogenous T cells found within harvested tumors. Subsequently, ACT has evolved to utilize autologous T cells obtained from peripheral blood, which are activated ex vivo and genetically engineered to express transgenic antigen receptors, such as TCRs or CARs.14,17 CAR-T and TCR-T cell therapies require the expansion of genetically engineered T cells directed against specific antigenic targets. Currently, ACT has achieved significant success in treating refractory B cell malignancies, multiple myeloma, and melanoma.18, 19, 20 In contrast, the efficacy of ACT in solid tumors, such as lung cancer, remains limited,21,22 and its application in the context of lung cancer is still at an early stage of development. Below, we discuss ongoing research on ACT in solid tumors, especially in lung cancer.

TIL therapy

Unlike hematological malignancies, lung cancer is comprised of a polyclonal cell population characterized by heterogeneous antigens. Consequently, TILs isolated from tumor tissue represent a heterogeneous population of polyclonal cells capable of recognizing a diverse array of tumor antigens.23,24 Autologous TIL therapy was first reported to possess potent anti-tumor activity in metastatic melanoma.25 To date, TIL therapy has made substantial progress in melanoma clinical research.26, 27, 28, 29 The most compelling results indicate that the ORR and complete response (CR) rate in patients with melanoma receiving TIL therapy are 40–50% and 20%, respectively, and that patients achieving CR show long-term disease-free survival.26 TIL therapy can also induce durable remission in a subset of patients with other solid tumors, such as cervical cancer,30 colorectal cancer,31 and breast cancer.32

In the earliest clinical study evaluating the efficacy of TIL therapy in NSCLC, all patients at pathological stage II, IIIa, or IIIb (according to the Mountain staging system) underwent surgical resection as primary treatment and received TIL therapy or standard adjuvant therapy. Patients in the experimental group had a median overall survival of 22.4 months, compared with 14.1 months for those receiving standard treatment.33 This study provides strong evidence supporting TIL therapy as a viable treatment option for patients with advanced lung cancer. In a study of patients with metastatic lung cancer resistant to anti-PD-1 therapy, 3 of 13 evaluable patients receiving TIL therapy achieved a confirmed response, and 11 showed a reduction in tumor burden. Notably, two patients achieved durable complete responses after 1.5 years.34 In a Phase I trial, 13 patients who progressed on nivolumab treatment achieved an ORR of 23% following autologous TIL therapy.34 A Phase II clinical trial is currently evaluating the efficacy of autologous TIL therapy in patients with solid tumors (NCT03645928).

Lifileucel is the first autologous TIL therapy approved for the treatment of solid tumors,20 specifically melanoma.35,36 Currently, research on its application in lung cancer has also made progress. TIL therapy may offer distinct advantages in the treatment of solid tumors. As previously mentioned, TILs exhibit diverse TCR clonality, enabling the recognition of multiple tumor antigens. Therefore, compared with other ACT modalities (such as CAR-T and TCR-T cell therapies), TILs may be superior in addressing tumor heterogeneity. Consequently, TILs demonstrate better clinical efficacy in solid tumors with high mutational burdens (such as melanoma) than other ACT methods.37 However, TIL therapy faces significant challenges regarding reproducibility and scalability. First, TILs are derived from tumor tissue, which is a paradoxical approach for patients who are candidates for surgical resection or who have tumor recurrence. Furthermore, although the manufacturing success rate of TILs is between 80% and 90%, there remains a risk of treatment cancellation due to manufacturing defects.23 Similarly, the complex TME of solid tumors affects the therapeutic efficacy of TILs. Immunosuppressive signals within the TME and chronic TCR stimulation can induce T cell exhaustion and dysfunction, further limiting T cell potency and persistence.18 Moreover, TIL therapy can lead to various treatment-related adverse events (TRAEs), including fever, hypotension, hypoxia, diarrhea, and myelosuppression.38 Currently, the method for in vitro culture and expansion of TILs relies on interleukin (IL)-2; however, high doses of IL-2 can induce systemic toxicity39 and may also promote regulatory T cells that suppress the anti-tumor response of TILs.40

TCR-T therapy

The TCR is a critical surface molecule that specifically recognizes antigens and mediates immune responses.41 TCR-T therapy employs genetic engineering techniques to express TCR gene sequences specific to tumor antigens in T cells, thereby endowing them with the ability to specifically kill tumor cells.13,42,43 TCR-T cells can recognize both surface antigens on tumor cells and intracellular antigens presented by specific MHC molecules, thereby possessing a broader capability for target antigen recognition. This not only enhances T cells’ ability to recognize tumor cells but also improves their persistence.13,44 Furthermore, TCR-T therapy utilizes peripheral lymphocytes, offering greater reproducibility and scalability than TIL technology.44 Currently, TCR-T cells targeting tumor-associated antigens (TAAs) or tumor-specific antigens (TSAs) have shown promising results in clinical trials for melanoma, colorectal cancer, synovial sarcoma, and esophageal cancer.39

Research on TCR-T cell therapy for lung cancer has also progressed in recent years. NY-ESO-1 is expressed in approximately 11.8–21% of NSCLC cases.45,46 A Phase I trial evaluated the safety and feasibility of NY-ESO-1 TCR-T cell therapy in four patients with metastatic NSCLC (mNSCLC) who were HLA-A2 positive (NCT02457650).14 Two patients had clinical responses to NY-ESO-1 TCR-T cell treatment, with one patient achieving a short-term PR within 4 months, and none of the four patients experienced severe toxicity. Phase I trials of NY-ESO-1 TCR-T cells used alone or in combination with pembrolizumab for advanced NSCLC are ongoing (NCT03029273 and NCT03709706). Another Phase I clinical trial evaluated the safety and efficacy of melanoma-associated antigen A10 (MAGE-A10)-specific TCR-T cells in patients with MAGE-A10+ advanced NSCLC.47 Among the 11 patients treated, 1 had a PR, 4 had stable disease, 5 had clinical or radiographic progressive disease, and 1 was not evaluable. Other clinical trials are currently underway to determine the feasibility, safety, and efficacy of this personalized therapy for NSCLC (Table 2).

Afami-cel is a melanoma-associated antigen A4 (MAGE-A4)-targeting TCR-T cell therapy, the first and currently only FDA-approved TCR-engineered T cell therapy for the treatment of advanced synovial sarcoma.48,49 In the Phase I SURPASS trial, lete-cel monotherapy demonstrated responses in patients with refractory solid tumors, such as ovarian cancer, gastroesophageal junction cancer, urothelial carcinoma, head and neck cancer, and synovial sarcoma (although responses have not yet been observed in lung cancer).50 However, trials are already evaluating the efficacy of lete-cel, used alone or in combination with nivolumab or pembrolizumab (NCT04044859), including patients with NSCLC. Lete-cel consists of TCR-T cells that recognize a shared epitope of the cancer testis antigens NY-ESO-1 and L antigen family member 1 isoform A (LAGE-1a). A study evaluating the efficacy of lete-cel alone or in combination with pembrolizumab in patients with advanced NSCLC found that the toxicity profiles of both monotherapy and combination therapy were manageable, but anti-tumor activity was limited. The widespread clinical application of TCR-T cell therapy in solid tumors faces numerous challenges,13 such as the heterogeneity of intratumoral antigen expression in lung cancer and the immunosuppressive TME. TCR-T therapy targets antigens in an HLA-restricted manner. Therefore, patients receiving treatment require a match for both the HLA allele and target tumor antigen, which presents new challenges for the clinical application of TCR-T therapy.18

CAR-T therapy

TCR-T and CAR-T therapies involve the genetic modification of T cells to express receptors that target tumor antigens. Unlike TCR-T cells, which rely on peptide–MHC complexes for tumor cell recognition, CAR-T therapy involves genetically engineering T cells to express CARs (which recognize specific tumor antigens in an MHC-independent manner).51, 52, 53 CARs are primarily composed of an extracellular antigen-recognition domain (ectodomain), a transmembrane domain, and an intracellular signaling domain (endodomain). The ectodomain, which determines the affinity of the CAR, includes a single-chain variable fragment (scFv) derived from an antibody, consisting of the antigen-binding regions composed of the heavy and light chains of a monoclonal antibody.54 CAR-T cells have garnered widespread attention due to their breakthrough success in hematological malignancies,55 with six FDA-approved therapies targeting CD19 or B-cell maturation antigen (BCMA). CAR-T cell therapy has also progressed in trials targeting lung cancer. Selection of surface antigens is crucial for CAR-T therapy. Currently, several CAR-T therapies targeting EGFR, HER2, PD-L1, CEA, prostate stem cell antigen (PSCA), Mucin 1 (MUC1), MSLN, glypican-3 (GPC3), and receptor tyrosine kinase-like orphan receptor 1 (ROR1) are being tested in Phase I trials, including those in patients with lung cancer.11,12,56

In a Phase I clinical study (NCT01869166), among 11 patients receiving EGFR-targeted CAR-T cell therapy, 2 achieved PR, and 5 had stable disease for 2–8 months; the treatment was well-tolerated with no severe toxicity.57 In a second trial (NCT03182816), among nine patients receiving EGFR-CAR-T cell therapy, one achieved a PR lasting over a year, six had stable disease, and two had progressive disease. The PFS for these nine patients was 7.13 months, and the median overall survival (OS) was 15.63 months.58 These two trials indicate that EGFR-CAR-T cell therapy is feasible and safe for treating patients with EGFR-positive NSCLC.

CEA is strongly expressed in lung cancer and is associated with a poor prognosis and metastasis. Therefore, clinical studies have evaluated the safety and efficacy of CEA CAR-T therapy in patients with CEA-positive lung cancer (NCT02349724 and NCT04348643). Similarly, MUC1 is considered a potential target for ACT, as it is highly expressed in lung cancer and promotes metastasis.11 CAR-T cells targeting MUC1 successfully eliminated NSCLC tumor cells11 and have been evaluated in lung cancer clinical trials (NCT03525782, NCT02587689, and NCT05239143).

Overexpression of the TAA MSLN is closely related to tumor invasiveness and is an attractive target in NSCLC.59 A Phase I study (NCT02414269) reported results of regionally delivered autologous MSLN-targeted CAR-T cell therapy for mesothelioma, metastatic lung cancer, and metastatic breast cancer. The results showed a median OS of 23.9 months (1-year OS rate of 83%) in patients receiving CAR-T cell infusion, with eight patients remaining stable for ≥6 months.60 Another Phase I/II clinical study of MUC1 CAR-T cells combined with PDCD1 (encoding PD-1) knockout for the treatment of patients with advanced NSCLC is also underway (NCT03525782). Among the 20 patients participating in the study, 11 had stable disease, and 9 had progressive disease.61

Delta-like ligand 3 (DLL3), an inhibitory ligand for the Notch receptor, is expressed in the majority of SCLC tumors.62 A Phase I study of DLL3-targeted CAR-T cell therapy was conducted in patients with relapsed/refractory SCLC (NCT03392064). Among the four patients, one achieved PR, two had stable disease (SD), and one had PD. The median PFS was 3.7 months, and the median OS was 7.4 months (NCT03392064). This first-in-human trial suggests that CAR-T cell therapy is promising for the treatment of SCLC. According to ClinicalTrials.gov, more CAR-T cell therapies are currently undergoing Phase I or Phase II clinical trials for lung cancer; these trials are summarized in Table 3.

Strategies to overcome the obstacles of ACT in lung cancer

There are a few barriers that hinder ACT from achieving therapeutic efficacy as satisfactory as that in lymphoma and multiple myeloma. As the most widely accepted subclasses in ACT, TIL, TCR-T, and CAR-T are faced with many challenges; each faces specific obstacles. With the development of gene-editing techniques, other immune cells, including NK cells and macrophages, have broadened the scope of engineered cellular immunotherapies for cancer. In addition, an accurate elucidation of the tumor immune microenvironment provides a glimpse toward personalized cellular therapy.

Strengthening adoptive cells through construction modification

In contrast to hematological malignancies, the more condensed structure and complex cellular components, along with continuous antigenic stimulation, in lung cancer hamper the transfer of adoptive cells, leading to T cell exhaustion and shortened persistence. From the perspective of the basic CAR structure, Chen et al65 showed that positively charged patches (PCPs) on the surface of CAR mediate tonic signaling, which disturbs CAR-T efficacy, even before tumor antigen stimulation. In addition to the antigen-recognition domain, costimulatory signals are important for the activation and maintenance of transferred T cells. Currently, CD28 and 4-1BB (CD137) are the most commonly used cytoplasmic signaling domains in CAR-T cells, and both costimulation domains contribute to CAR-T cell persistence and cytotoxicity.66 The CD3 domain is also an indispensable structure in T cell activation and CAR signal transduction. Among the four subunits (ε, δ, γ, and ζ), CD3ζ directly conducts the final step of T cell receptor signal transduction from the membrane and thus is acknowledged by the US FDA as the standard intracellular activation domain in addition to costimulatory domains in CAR structure. However, other subunits have also been studied in recent years,67,68 revealing that CARs containing CD3ε, CD3δ, and CD3γ outperformed the traditional CD3ζ CAR-T cells. Thus, a more precise CAR molecular design is worth exploring and manipulating for the treatment of lung cancer69 (Fig. 1).

It’s worth noting that some researchers believed that the artificial signaling domain on CAR molecules could not fully realize the potency of T cells.70 On this basis, Huang et al71 combined natural TCR signaling with endogenous CD3 signaling machinery and antigen-recognition domain from antibodies, constructing double-chain chimeric antigen receptor T cells, termed synthetic T cell receptor and antigen receptor T (STAR-T) cells (Fig. 1). STAR-T cells are engineered T cells that combine antibody-based tumor recognition with the natural T-cell receptor signaling system. Like CAR-T cells, STAR-T cells are designed to recognize tumor cells, but activate T cells in a more physiological manner, which may improve their sensitivity, persistence, and anti-tumor activity in solid tumors. STAR-T cells outperformed traditional BBz CAR-T (CAR-T with 4-1BB fused to the CD3ζ signaling chain) and 28z (CAR-T with CD28 fused to the CD3ζ signaling chain) CAR-T cells in proliferation, antigen sensitivity, and anti-tumor effects.72,73

In addition to CAR design, fast-growing omics and genetic engineering technologies have paved the way for decoding and manipulating gene expression to strengthen the anti-tumor efficiency of adoptive cells. The overexpression of forkhead box protein O1 (FOXO1)72,73 promotes a stem-like phenotype, thereby aiding the long-term persistence and clinical efficacy of therapeutic T cells. By assessing tumor-infiltrating T cells expressing the inhibitory molecules PD-1 and T cell immunoglobulin and mucin domain-containing protein 3 (Tim3), the nuclear receptor subfamily 4 group A (NR4A) family has been shown to be a key transcription factor that limits T cell function in solid tumors.74,75 Apart from the inhibitory chemical microenvironment, alterations in extracellular matrix (ECM) stiffness also impede T cell function. Osr2,76 which integrates biomechanical signaling, is selectively expressed in terminally exhausted CD8+ T cells, and could serve as a target for potentiating anti-tumor immunity in adoptive cells. In addition to modulating translational factors, another promising approach to address T cell dysfunction in tumors is engineering T cells to express immunomodulatory factors, including IL-2,77 IL-7,78,79 IL-15,80 IL-10,81,82 IL-12,83 IL-18,84 and Fms-like tyrosine kinase 3 ligand (Flt3L).85 As the core organs of gas exchange, the lungs possess a particular structure and cellular composition that are directly connected to the outside air. Given organ-specific and carcinogenic factors, elucidating the characteristics of antigen-specific T cells in the lung and the translational differences among their subclusters is urgently needed. Based on the currently investigated targets (including EGFR, Her2, PD-L1, CEA, PSCA, MUC1, MSLN, GPC3, and ROR112), the structure of the antigen-recognition and signal transduction molecule and the regulation of gene expression in adoptive cells require careful matching and evaluation (Fig. 1).

Engineering T-cell activation signals represents an important strategy to optimize the efficacy and persistence of engineered cell therapies. According to the three-signal model of T-cell activation, Signal 1 is initiated by antigen recognition through the TCR or CAR, where optimized TCR/CD3 components, as mentioned above, can modulate proximal signaling intensity and functional output. Signal 2 is provided by co-stimulatory domains, in which CD28 predominantly promotes rapid proliferation through phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K)–AKT–mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) signaling, whereas 4-1BB enhances mitochondrial fitness, memory formation and persistence through tumor necrosis factor receptor associated factor (TRAF)-mediated nuclear factor kappa-light-chain-enhancer of activated B cells (NF-κB) activation. Signal 3 is mediated by cytokine signaling and determines T-cell fate through Janus kinase (JAK)/signal transducer and activator of transcription (STAT)-dependent transcriptional programs. Therefore, rational manipulation of the three activation signals provides a comprehensive framework for next-generation cellular therapy engineering (Fig. 2).

Balancing efficiency and security

The adverse effects of ACT limit the full expression of its potent anti-tumor activity. A large number of clinical studies have been terminated because of unexpected toxicities. As we have elaborated on ways to strengthen the potency of adoptive cells, it is also of great concern to restrict the expression of the armoring transgene to the tumor site to avoid unwanted damage to normal tissue. As early as a dozen years ago, researchers from Maryland83 took advantage of the natural T cell activation pathway, restricting the expression of armored IL-12 by placing its gene under the control of an artificial nuclear factor of activated T cells (NFAT) promoter. Lim, from the University of California, San Francisco, developed synNotch T cells.77,86,87 SynNotch T cells are synthetic receptor-engineered T cells in which recognition of an initial antigen triggers the programmed expression of a second therapeutic module, such as a CAR, cytokine, or other payload. This “sense-and-respond” design can improve tumor selectivity and reduce off-tumor toxicity by restricting T-cell activation to specific tissue or tumor contexts. Simic et al88 have already applied this technology in treating diseases of the central nervous system. Based on the identified extracellular central nervous system (CNS)-specific antigens, synthetic Notch receptors could induce the expression of diverse payloads only in the brain. This research may have great value as a reference for the ACT of lung cancer. Based on the methods applied in this study, molecular features unique to the lung extracellular environment are also worth considering and could serve as a paving stone for lung cancer-specific payload delivery following T cell transfer. On the other hand, a team from Peter MacCallum Cancer Centre89 challenged the traditional transgene-induction approaches. Using the clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats (CRISPR) knock-in strategy, they leveraged the regulatory mechanisms of endogenous genes to drive transgene expression in a tumor-localized manner (compared to the spleen). The work mentioned above has prompted the development of a more precise and highly effective ACT that relies on organ-specific markers as triggers.

For CAR-T and TCR-T therapies, it is of great importance to determine the target antigen for the CAR or TCR molecules, as toxicity may arise from “on-target, off-tumor” effects. A prime example is a CAR-T clinical trial targeting the HER2 antigen. Since normal lung tissue vascular endothelial cells express low levels of HER2, the trial triggered rapid and fatal pulmonary toxicity in patients, ultimately leading to its termination.57 Unfortunately, lung cancer is known for its antigen heterogeneity.18 Among the tumor antigens in lung cancer, TAAs constitute the majority, whereas TSAs are rare. From this perspective, multi-target T cells (Fig. 1) have the potential to reduce “on-target” cytotoxicity in normal tissues through an elaborate design of the Boolean logic component. In 2024, a phase 1 trial90 reported reductions in tumor size in patients with recurrent glioblastoma after receiving treatment with bivalent CAR-T cells targeting EGFR and interleukin-13 receptor alpha 2 (IL13Rα2). Furthermore, the identification of tumor neoantigens is accelerating due to the rapid development of omics technologies and artificial intelligence.91 Therefore, results from clinical trials and advances in scientific techniques make it reasonable for us to anticipate more precisely targeted adoptive cells for lung cancer.

Combination with other therapies

Over the past few decades, ICIs targeting the PD-1-PD-L1 axis or CTLA4 have revolutionized the therapeutic landscape in lung cancer.18 In the context of malignancy, tumor-specific T cells undergo activation-induced upregulation of coinhibitory receptors, which, in turn, suppress their activity. As adoptive cells are highly tumor antigen-specific, ICIs hold promise to fully realize their potency.92 There are already trials evaluating the safety and efficacy of combining CAR-T therapy with pembrolizumab in malignant pleural diseases.60,93 Cherkassky et al93 developed an anti-MSLN CAR with a dominant negative receptor of PD-1, which would compete for PD-L1/PD-L2 with endogenous PD-1 and enhance CAR-T cell anti-tumor ability. In addition, deletion of CTLA4 restores CAR-T cytotoxicity and rescues T cell function in patients resistant to CAR-T cell therapy.94 As an elaborately engineered genetic product, the adoptive cell can also serve as a carrier for the ICI. Rafiq et al95 modified CAR-T cells to secrete PD-1-blocking scFV. This method has also been tested in clinical trials for EGFR-positive advanced solid malignancies, including lung cancer (NCT02862028) (Fig. 3).

Unlike transferred TILs, CAR-T and TCR-T cells are limited in their ability to recognize a single peptide despite the heterogeneity of antigens in solid tumors. The outgrowth of tumor cells expressing irrelevant antigens is one of the main causes of relapse following ACT. Hence, a number of groups are dedicated to combining cellular therapy with other treatments to stimulate host immunity and invigorate engineered cells. In addition to immunoregulatory cytokines, adoptive cells can also be engineered to express a bispecific T cell engager (BiTE). Bispecific T-cell engagers (BiTEs) are engineered molecules that simultaneously bind to tumor antigens and CD3 on T cells, thereby physically bridging T cells to cancer cells and promoting T cell-mediated killing. When incorporated into adoptive cell platforms, BiTE secretion can also help recruit bystander endogenous T cells to broaden the anti-tumor response and reduce antigen escape-mediated relapse (Fig. 3).96,97 Choi et al96 designed a CAR-T specific for EGFRvIII (the most common oncogenic deletion mutant of EGFR) that secretes a BiTE against EGFR. In this case, EGFR-expressing tumor cells that escape recognition and clearance by EGFRvIII CAR-T cells would be compelled to other T cells via the engager, increasing the likelihood of being killed. In an investigator-initiated, open-label study,98 CARv3-TEAM-E T cells contributed to dramatic and rapid tumor regression in recurrent glioblastoma. Apart from mobilizing bystander T cells from the host, researchers99,100 are using oncolytic viruses to deliver CAR targets to solid tumors. By forcing tumor cells to express a non-signaling, truncated CD19 protein, CD19-CAR-T cells can recognize and eradicate a broader range of tumor cells, accompanied by the release of viruses from dying tumor cells, resulting in a waterfall effect99 (Fig. 3).

Simplifying the manufacturing process and optimizing accessibility

ACT is known for its complex production process, long manufacturing cycle, and high cost. Diverse approaches are underway to increase the accessibility of ACT.

Allogeneic adoptive cells derived from healthy donors surpass autogenous cells in terms of culture stability and greater potency.101,102 The off-the-shelf character greatly reduces preparation time before being applied to the patient, which is crucial for cancer treatment (Fig. 4). Nonetheless, the immune system recognizes “self” from “non-self”, raising a dilemma for the tolerance of the transferred allogeneic cells in the recipient. Allogeneic T cell therapies must overcome the challenge of graft-versus-host disease (GVHD) caused by transferred cells to achieve broad use. Deletion of the MHC I molecule on donor cells may help evade recognition of the receiver’s T cells but can lead to increased killing by NK cells. Scientists from the Peking-Tsinghua Center for Life Sciences103 offered a solution by modifying the glycan profiles of the donor T cells. Another team took advantage of CD47, a commonly known “don’t eat me signal”, in CD19-CAR-T therapy.104 To protect the host from GVHD, two phase 1 studies105 enrolled patients to receive UCART19, a donor-derived anti-CD19 CAR-T product lacking a TCR.

Apart from the resources of adoptive cells, manufacturing methods have also been optimized for safer and more efficient output. Forced gene expression in T cells ex vivo commonly relies on randomly integrating vectors,106 mostly retroviruses or lentiviruses. The random integration of viral target genes into the host genome can lead to oncogenic transformation, clonal expansion, and gene silencing. Hence, researchers are developing next-generation targeted gene delivery methods based on the CRISPR/CRISPR-associated protein 9 (Cas9) technology. Zhang et al107 utilized an adeno-associated virus (AAV) vector as a template, combined with a non-viral insertion strategy, to develop a CAR-T product with high safety and effectiveness for treating B cell non-Hodgkin lymphoma. In addition to the CAR element, investigators have attempted to insert two chains of a neoantigen-specific TCR into the T cell receptor alpha constant (TRAC) gene locus of circulating T cells isolated from patients.108 This product was administered in a phase I clinical trial (NCT03970382) to evaluate its safety and potency for refractory solid cancers, including lung cancer.108

The methods discussed above rely on ex vivo engineering of adoptive cells before transfer to patients. Nevertheless, in vivo CAR-T cell therapy offers a promising future for directly manipulating immune cells using scalable, off-the-shelf biomedical products. Viruses and lipid nanoparticles (LNP)-based RNA delivery are the two major platforms for in vivo engineering. There are many in vivo CAR candidates with promising preclinical results that have entered clinical trials for hematological malignancies and solid tumors109, 110, 111, 112 (Fig. 4).

Exploiting other immune candidates beyond traditional T cells

The immune system consists of two unique but inseparable parts: the adaptive system, which is responsible for specific antigen recognition and durable protection; and the innate system, which plays a pivotal role in immune surveillance without specificity and immune memory. Currently, cells from the innate immune system are considered promising candidates for adoptive therapy and have been evaluated in preclinical and clinical studies.

NK cells derived from cord blood have been transduced with anti-CD19 CAR and interleukin-15 to enhance their in vivo expansion and persistence.113,114 The administration of CAR-NK cells contributed to a response rate of 73% in 11 patients with CD19-positive lymphoid tumors without inducing cytokine release syndrome, neurotoxicity, or other inflammatory side effects.114 Compared with traditional T-cell therapy, NK-cell therapy offers the following advantages: (1) greater safety, particularly lower risks of GVHD, cytokine release syndrome, and neurotoxicity; (2) greater suitability for off-the-shelf production, enabling faster manufacturing, greater accessibility, and greater potential for universal application; (3) independence from classic HLA restrictions, allowing the recognition of tumors with downregulated HLA-I or T-cell evasion; (4) wider applicability, making it particularly suitable for the elderly, frail patients, those with poor T-cell quality, and patients requiring rapid treatment. However, it faces challenges such as poor persistence in vivo and insufficient expansion; difficulties with homing, infiltration, and survival in solid tumors; and problems with CAR viral transduction. Consequently, NK-cell therapy is not widely used in clinical practice.115

Macrophages have also intrigued researchers owing to their abundant infiltration in tumors, their immunomodulatory properties, and their ability to phagocytose tumor cells directly. In 2020, Klichinsky et al116 reported that genetically engineered human macrophages with CARs were manipulated into a pro-inflammatory (M1) phenotype and contributed to antigen-specific phagocytosis in solid tumors. The second generation of CAR-macrophages (CAR-M), originating from pluripotent stem cells117 and with an elaborately modified intracellular domain, showed an enhanced anti-tumor effect compared to the first generation. Reiss et al118 conducted a first-in-human, phase 1, multicenter clinical trial evaluating the safety and efficiency of autologous anti-HER2 CAR-macrophages for advanced HER2-expressing solid tumors. This product reduces circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) levels and is associated with SD as the best overall response (BOR) in a subset of patients without dose-limiting toxicities (DLT) (NCT04660929). Compared with traditional CAR-T therapy, CAR-M therapy offers the following advantages: (1) Easier access to tumor tissue because macrophages naturally infiltrate tumor tissue, whereas CAR-T cells often face challenges in solid tumors, such as difficulty in penetrating the tumor, poor retention, and low survival rates; (2) CAR-M can not only directly phagocytose target cells, but also process and present tumor antigens, further activating the innate immune system and eliciting a broader and more sustained antitumor immune response; (3) CAR-M can reshape the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment. However, the following limitations exist: (1) Limited clinical evidence. CAR-T therapy already has multiple established success stories in hematologic malignancies, whereas CAR-M therapy is still in the early stages, and further data are needed to support its true clinical benefits. (2) Macrophages are not as easily expanded, transduced, or maintained long term as T cells, and their production process is more complex. (3) Macrophages may be “educated” within the tumor microenvironment, leading to reduced antitumor activity, and their persistence may be inferior to that of CAR-T.119,120

In addition, as the most abundant white blood cells in the circulation, neutrophils have also been engineered to express CAR molecules.121,122 CAR-neutrophils help deliver nanodrugs specifically to the TME in glioblastoma (GBM) beyond direct cytotoxicity.122 However, the short lifespan of neutrophils prevents long-term tumor control and immune surveillance with these cells, requiring novel strategies or therapy combinations.

Conclusion and perspectives

We discuss various approaches to achieving both potent anti-tumor activity and safety in healthy tissues in ACTs. Currently, signal transduction domains, immune regulatory transcription factors, cytokines, and tumor-associated targets, as well as numerous types of transferred cells, are under evaluation in both laboratory and clinical trials. Apart from the ongoing biological engineering attempts, it is of great importance to elucidate the characteristics of adoptive cells that best suit the tumor circumstances and help sustain long-term protection, and how adoptive immune cells interact with and influence the host.

ACT has brought cancer treatment into a new and promising era. The glory won from hematological malignancies fueled the confidence of clinical doctors and basic science researchers. Nevertheless, the complex cellular components, dense structure, and unpredictable antigen targets present difficulties for the use of adoptive cells in lung cancer. However, with the development of synthetic biology and artificial intelligence (AI), more tools are emerging for engineering and manipulating adoptive cells. A major priority is the mass production of off-the-shelf universal CAR-T cells. Advances in gene editing technologies such as CRISPR/Cas9 systems and base editing have made it increasingly feasible to eliminate endogenous TCR and HLA molecules, thereby reducing the risks of GVHD and host-mediated rejection. For example, knocking out signal peptide peptidase like 3 (SPPL3) using gene editing technology can alter glycosylation modifications on the surface of T cells, thereby enhancing the persistence of CAR-T cells in patients.103 In parallel, synthetic biology has enabled the design of CAR-T cells with programmable features, including logic-gated activation, inducible cytokine release, enhanced resistance to immunosuppressive signals, and controllable safety switches. These innovations could support the transition from patient-specific manufacturing to standardized allogeneic products, which would substantially reduce the production time, cost, and batch-to-batch variability, making CAR-T therapy more accessible in routine clinical settings. Another key direction is AI-guided personalized CAR design. One of the central barriers in solid tumor treatment is antigen heterogeneity and the lack of truly tumor-specific targets. AI-based integration of multiomics data, single-cell transcriptomics, spatial profiling, and proteomic datasets can accelerate the identification of antigen combinations that maximize tumor specificity while minimizing off-tumor toxicity. In the short to medium term, this approach may enable the rational design of personalized CAR constructs as well as adaptive treatment strategies tailored to the antigenic architecture of individual tumors.123 As the characteristics and components of cells in tumor immunology are unveiled,124 we look forward to adoptive cellular products with both potency and safety for lung cancer treatment.
