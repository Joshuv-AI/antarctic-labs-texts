A Qualitative Study of Patient and Caregiver Perspectives on Locally Advanced Head and Neck Cancer in the United States
Authors: Esther Yu, Veronica Ashton, OluYemisi Falope, Toby Hanna, Teresa Kessell, Matt Scott, Denise D’Andrea, Maureen Tucker O’Malley, Rebecca Genin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionLocally advanced head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (LA-HNSCC) has major physical and psychosocial burdens. Although treatment challenges are well recognized, few studies have explored the full LA-HNSCC experience from first symptoms through to post-treatment recovery and survivorship. This exploratory, qualitative study sought to understand patient and caregiver perspectives across the LA-HNSCC journey.MethodsUS adults with lived experience of LA-HNSCC (seven patients; two caregivers) participated in Johnson & Johnson’s Head & Neck Cancer Patient Engagement Research Council via virtual focus groups and online bulletin board activities. Transcripts and written responses were thematically analyzed using an a priori framework of three key stages: diagnosis and treatment planning, treatment, and post-treatment recovery and survivorship.ResultsDiagnostic journeys varied, with delays to diagnosis commonly perceived by participants as being attributed to multiple factors, including misinterpreted symptoms, difficulty accessing specialists, and limited provider awareness. Participants reported fear, uncertainty, and information overload during early decision-making. Treatment was described as burdensome, with side effects from radiation and chemotherapy leading to profound functional impairments affecting eating, swallowing, speech, and energy levels. Post-treatment long-term effects such as dry mouth, hearing loss, peripheral neuropathy, and anxiety about recurrence persisted and were often unexpected. Throughout these challenges, participants found strength, encouragement, and hope in peer support networks and valued knowledgeable and empathic communication with providers, as well as access to clear and digestible information; in particular, participants recommended early mental health support, tailored communication, and access to peer mentorship services.ConclusionsThese exploratory, qualitative insights highlight unmet informational, psychosocial, and support needs in LA-HNSCC. Patients and their caregivers reported experiencing substantial, long-lasting physical and mental health effects that providers should be aware of to better support and empower patients throughout the care experience. Key Summary Points

Why carry out this study?Patients with locally advanced head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (LA-HNSCC) and their caregivers experience physical, emotional, and informational burdens throughout diagnosis, treatment, and recovery.This exploratory, qualitative study sought to understand lived experiences, barriers, and sources of support across the LA-HNSCC journey of patients and caregivers, from first symptoms through to post-treatment recovery and survivorship.What was learned from the study?Participants reported delayed diagnoses, treatment toxicity and side effects, long-term functional impacts, and inconsistent access to psychosocial and peer support as key challenges across all stages of the journey.These findings suggest there is value in early mental health support, clear and empathic patient communications, and access to peer support networks to improve and empower patient and caregiver experiences in LA-HNSCC.

Introduction

Head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC) represents nearly 4% of all cancer cases in the USA [1], with oropharyngeal cancers specifically accounting for an estimated 59,660 new cases in 2025, resulting in a significant health burden [2]. HNSCC may often be caused by alcohol and tobacco use as well as cancer-causing variants of the human papilloma virus (HPV), though some cases may not be attributable to these risk factors [1]. Around 65% of oropharyngeal HNSCC cases are HPV-positive [3], and the strong association of HPV with cancer has been a driving factor in the introduction of HPV vaccination and prevention programs [4]. Mortality and overall disease burden from HNSCC are declining globally; however, its incidence remains substantial [5]. Most HNSCC is locally advanced HNSCC (LA-HNSCC) at diagnosis, with ~60% of US patients with HNSCC presenting with stage III/IV disease [6].

Due to the location of tumors in HNSCC, loss of essential functions can occur, resulting in physical impacts and deterioration in quality of life [1, 7]. Patients with HNSCC require combinations of treatments and rehabilitation that may result in intensive treatment journeys. Chemotherapy and radiation treatments for HNSCC are associated with side effects that may gradually worsen over time and further confound the symptom burden of HNSCC [1]. Some of these potential long-term impacts are a notable element of the HNSCC journey of patients that are less often seen in other cancers, owing to the particular essential functions associated with the head and neck region [1]. Additionally, there may be psychological impacts, including anxiety and mood disorders [7]. Despite these challenges, ongoing therapeutic advances have introduced effective strategies to further reduce the risk of recurrence or potentially minimize long-term side effects, and more research is under way [8–12].

Some qualitative studies have investigated the patient experience in HNSCC, highlighting the importance of physical, psychosocial, and informational support—such as peer support networks and patient advocacy groups [13–18]—but these did not focus on the LA-HNSCC population, and none have sought to explore the patient journey in its entirety from prediagnosis to long-term post-treatment follow-up in the USA.

Given the limited availability of patient experience data in the literature relating to patients with LA-HNSCC who are not candidates for surgery and therefore receive definitive radiation therapy with or without systemic therapy, the objectives of this study were (1) to understand the patient and caregiver journey in this population from diagnosis and treatment planning through to treatment and post-treatment recovery and survivorship, (2) to learn more about the barriers and challenges faced by patients and caregivers throughout the journey, and (3) to gain knowledge about additional supports needed from the perspectives of both patients and caregivers.

Methods

Study Design

This was a qualitative, exploratory study of patients and caregivers with lived experience of LA-HNSCC, designed to understand the patient and caregiver journey and the burden of disease and treatment. To map experiences from first symptoms to diagnosis, treatment, and other challenges, this multi-method approach combined virtual focus groups with online bulletin board activities, evaluated via thematic content analysis.

This study was reviewed and determined to be exempt from institutional review board review pursuant to the terms of the US Department of Health and Human Services Policy for Protection of Human Research Subjects at 45 C.F.R. §46.104(d). The study was conducted in accordance with the Helsinki Declaration of 1964 and its later amendments. Participants provided written informed consent, including for the publication of their quotes, and understood that participation was voluntary. This study is reported according to the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research [19] (Supplementary Material S1).

Study Population

Patients and caregiver participants are members of the Johnson & Johnson Head & Neck Cancer Patient Engagement Research Council (PERC) program. The PERC program comprises contracted US resident patient and caregiver consultants with experience of various chronic conditions or diseases. They share their insights through structured activities to inform research and promote improved understanding of patients’ needs and expectations.

Participants for the Head and Neck Cancer PERC were recruited via direct email outreach to patient advocacy groups, online advertising, and social media. Eligible individuals were English-speaking US adults living with or caring for someone with LA-HNSCC. Included participants had a verified diagnosis of stage III–IVb LA-HNSCC, were ineligible for surgery, and had experience with radiation therapy with or without systemic therapy or were a caregiver to someone meeting these criteria. They were excluded if they had received surgery for LA-HNSCC or were unable or unwilling to participate in virtual qualitative research.

Data Collection

Initial PERC engagements took place from March to April 2025, facilitated by a Patient Insight Researcher from Evidera (part of PPD Inc, a subsidiary of Thermo Fisher Scientific LLC) with experience and training in qualitative, patient-centered healthcare research. Participants did not know the moderator or observers prior to the engagements and were introduced to them, including their backgrounds, motivations, and research objectives at the start of the engagements.

The first engagement was a 3-h virtual focus group meeting, directed using a semistructured interview guide that had not been pilot-tested (Supplementary Material S2).

Participants then contributed to an online bulletin board for the second engagement to continue sharing their LA-HNSCC experiences. One 30-min activity was posted per day for 7 days regarding treatment decision-making, treatment barriers, and quality-of-life impacts. Individual responses were not visible to other participants.

After analysis of the focus group and bulletin board contributions, a 2-h follow-up virtual focus group was held with participants to further explore treatment challenges and burdens, treatment outcomes, and supports. A semistructured discussion guide was used for this third engagement (Supplementary Material S2). Self-reported participant demographic characteristics were collected in a short pre-session survey.

Data Analysis

Participant demographics and clinical characteristics were summarized descriptively. The focus groups were audiovisually recorded and transcribed. Transcripts were not returned to participants for comment or correction prior to analysis. These transcripts and the online bulletin board contributions were then de-identified and qualitatively coded independently of the research sponsor by the third-party Patient Insight Researchers using MAXQDA software (VERBI Software, Berlin, Germany). The coders employed thematic content analysis according to three key themes/stages of the patient journey, specified a priori: diagnosis and treatment planning, treatment, and post-treatment recovery and survivorship. Two independent coders used an analytical framework with a systematic and deductive approach to the coding process and applications, with codes identified from the discussion guides based on narrative patterns in insights and findings from across the research outputs; inductive codes not captured in the deductive code book were additionally allowed to emerge. Data analysis used a rapid implementation approach whereby data saturation was determined by the presence of similar themes across research engagements and coding disagreement was resolved between analysts via adjudication meetings [20]. Quotes were minimally edited for clarity, and nonverbatim edits are indicated using square brackets.

Two patients and one caregiver from the PERC are co-authors of this paper. They reviewed the analysis report and contributed to the interpretation and discussion from a patient and caregiver perspective.

Results

Participant Population

This study included nine participants with experience living with or caring for someone with LA-HNSCC. No participants declined participation or failed to complete the engagements. Three (33%) participants were female, seven (78%) were White, one (11%) was Black or African American, and one (11%) was Hispanic or Latino. Over half of participants were aged > 60 years (n = 5 [56%]); most had at least a bachelor’s degree (n = 7 [78%]). Participants lived across different US regions and urbanicities. The majority had been diagnosed within the last 5 years (n = 6 [67%]) and had received treatment at an academic medical center (n = 6 [67%]; Table 1).

Patient and Caregiver Journey Insights

The qualitative insights generated during the engagements were structured around three key themes/stages of the patient journey (Fig. 1).

Theme 1: Diagnosis and Treatment Planning

During diagnosis and treatment planning, participants experienced diagnostic delays, information overwhelm, and impacts to their mental and emotional wellbeing.

The journey to diagnosis varied widely and by individual participant circumstances. Diagnosis was often perceived as delayed owing to a lack of specificity in symptoms contributing to missed diagnoses, owing to a need to coordinate across different providers (e.g., dentists), or because of extended wait times to see a specialist. Patients with tumors in a visible location and those receiving care from specialist cancer centers were seemingly diagnosed more rapidly. Participants in rural areas reported often having to seek treatment further away from home to confirm their diagnosis or to receive a definitive treatment plan. For some participants, these perceived diagnostic delays led to an increased sense of urgency in selecting providers and initiating treatment plans due to the advanced stage of disease. Not all participants recalled being informed of their cancer stage upon diagnosis.

During the workup process, participants mentioned experiencing impacts on their mental and emotional well-being, including when waiting for scan and biopsy results; this was especially true for those who had previously lost loved ones to cancer:

The diagnosis part—the not knowing—is it that phase of cancer? How bad is it? Do I need to start recording videos for my children to say goodbye…? The horrible things that go through your mind. And Doctor Google does not help. That, to me, is maybe the worst part. The not knowing part.

—patient, aged 50–59 years

Participants reported a sense of shock at being diagnosed, and the multitude of additional patient education and treatment-preparation appointments and procedures that followed contributed to feelings of confusion and being overwhelmed. The burden of preparatory appointments, education, and navigation of complex work and insurance situations commonly left them feeling there was little time for emotional processing until after treatment ended. Some participants often perceived these intermediary steps and procedures as barriers to starting their actual, curative treatment quickly. Critically, the large amount of information often redirected cognitive energy away from fully understanding the importance of long-term side effects and the steps for minimizing their impact:

The learning curve is vertical, you’re struggling to make appointments, figure out what to do about your work situation, make sense of the insurance process. [It is] this proverbial cliché of drinking from the firehose.

—patient, aged 30–39 years

Once they were established with a trusted provider and/or cancer care team and had a cogent treatment plan, participants were more likely to defer to their expertise and follow the provided treatments. Some reported seeking second opinions, such as on treatment protocols, or needing to visit a specialized cancer treatment center. There was broad agreement among participants that understanding the reasons for not being surgical candidates helped clarify the treatment plan. They also explained how caregivers, patient peers, friends, and family played a significant role in providing emotional support and helping with treatment decisions:

I’d always heard people had cancer and you cut it out and you get treatment and you move on. And [my doctor] said, well, yours is inoperable. And I said, well, why not? Why can you not cut it out? He said we would have to remove your entire tongue because it’s on the base of your tongue. And he said that would mean you would never talk again, you would never eat food again. He just said it wasn’t an option.

—patient, aged 50–59 years

Several participants agreed that the sense of mental and emotional overload would be improved with a better understanding of the value of any intermediary workup, procedures, and appointments required before initiating treatment. The participants also recommended having this information provided in different mediums (e.g., print, video, doctor explanation). Participants commonly reported a desire to be made aware of and connected with peer-to-peer support networks during the treatment planning period to assist with processing new information, prioritizing and coping with the volume of information received, and setting expectations. Several organizations exist to provide such support, and participants recommended Imerman Angels [21], the Head and Neck Cancer Alliance [22], and Support for People with Oral and Head and Neck Cancer [23] (Table 2).

Theme 2: Treatment

During treatment, participants experienced side effects and lifestyle disruptions and desired better mental health and informational support.

Participants described three distinct stages of the treatment phase. Radiation treatment plans for LA-HNSCC typically require numerous daily radiation treatments, often with weekly chemotherapy infusions. Most participants said they experienced few, if any, debilitating side effects during early-stage treatment. However, as treatment progresses to the middle stage, the full effects of radiation and chemotherapy usually take effect, including significant mouth and swallowing problems such as pain and dysphagia, dermatitis, hair loss, hearing loss, fatigue, significant weight loss, and diminished overall functioning. Caregivers also reported this stage as extremely challenging; they described often feeling physically and emotionally exhausted from witnessing their loved one’s suffering and managing their care along with other responsibilities. Participants spoke about feeling unprepared for the physical effects during this stage. When discussing late-stage treatment, patients and caregivers expressed how they frequently doubted their abilities to complete the prescribed treatment course. All patients ultimately completed their treatments but noted that the physical effects and symptom management continued for many weeks to several months afterwards:

When I first got diagnosed and I started treatment, it seemed pretty easy because I had radiation and chemo and the first 2 weeks doesn’t really seem to do anything.... And then all of a sudden it’s like a trainwreck. Everything catches up with you….it drained most of my strength and the pain during treatment required me to be heavily medicated most of the time.

—patient, aged 60–69 years

Efforts reported by participants to minimize the impacts of the challenges of treatment included adopting certain habits, mindsets, and lifestyle factors within their control. These were described as playing a part in improving outcomes and maintaining hope for the future.

Of the persistent physical effects and impacts on quality of life, the most notable were pain and reduced saliva production, swallowing difficulties, and taste changes, all of which negatively impacted ability to eat, drink, and retain adequate nutrition. Several participants also described a decline in energy, hearing problems, difficulty sleeping due to pain, and lymphedema; some also mentioned unexpected complications such as eye and dental issues. Emotional and cognitive impacts experienced during treatment included trauma, isolation, loss of control, slower cognitive processing, and memory lapses or loss:

She struggles. Obviously, there’s all the physical side effects...she’s still dealing with, but at this point, the most lingering thing is just emotional health. She’s definitely got [post-traumatic stress disorder] from all this. She’s been seeing a therapist very regularly for a very long time, who’s been a lifesaver for both of us, honestly, but that’s something she’s accepted she’s [going to] need the rest of her life.

—caregiver, aged 30–39 years

Although the scenarios varied by individual, disruptions to lifestyles and work during treatment included unemployment, being unable to continue working despite accommodations, being advised to seek disability benefits, needing to give up physically active hobbies, and caregivers having to leave their jobs to manage the responsibilities required:

I got to ring the bell [on] January 6. And I thought, well, the worst is behind me. I got to go home. And then I realized I’m very weak. I’m not able… to work. And then my insurance [said], well, why are you not back to work? I said, I can’t drive. I can’t eat. I have a trach. I was having to suction every hour or I couldn’t breathe. And I was really bad.

—patient, aged 50–59 years

Several individual barriers experienced during treatment were shared by the participants. These included treatment interruptions when participants moved further from a major cancer center or changed insurance providers, financial impacts from direct and indirect costs and insurance denials, limited availability of knowledgeable local providers, and a lack of emotional assistance. Some reported mid-treatment feeding-tube challenges that resulted in treatment delays:

I did not do a feeding tube, and it was a challenge. It hurt, and I didn’t want to eat … I was actually scared of the feeding tube … As I continued to lose weight, [the care team] said, “If you don’t start putting on some weight, we’re going to have to [give you a feeding tube].”

—patient, aged 60–69 years

Participants’ goals for treatment and cancer care prioritized survival, eliminating or reducing the risk of recurrence, reducing the physical effects of treatment, and improving early detection. Also important was support throughout their patient journey, including when making treatment and supportive care decisions and when feeling as if they could not continue with treatment.

Participants stated they accessed support mainly through family, friends, religious institutions, members of their patient care team (e.g., physical therapists, occupational therapists, speech therapists, nutritionists, and counselors), and peer support networks. Patient peer support was reported to be critical during treatment as patients may have felt unable to complete treatment without the encouragement of other patients. Connection to a mentor with a similar patient journey helps create a trusted resource built on a shared experience that a caregiver or care team cannot provide; participants shared their experiences with organizations that provide this support [21–23].

However, some participants reported not receiving support until after treatment, or not receiving any organized support, which made them feel they were experiencing their cancer journey mostly alone. Recommendations from the participants for support included being “prescribed” appropriate information resources and further connections to peer support networks, a trusted and empathetic oncology care team, access to information in different mediums such as plain language, and practical advice from informational resources in addition to friends and family:

When you’re on your way down, it seems like there’s no bottom. And that’s the dark night of the soul when you’re going through this process, [it] is not having any idea what the outcome’s going to be. And this is where my mentors rescued me, quite frankly. They said, yes, we know what you’re going through and we can tell you there’s light at the end of the tunnel.

—patient, aged >70 years

The caregivers in particular recommended peer support programs tailored for caregivers; services such as respite care, meal support, and childcare; mental health counseling; and access to treatment information in plain language. They also said providers should inquire about caregivers’ well-being.

Theme 3: Post-treatment Recovery and Survivorship

During post-treatment recovery and survivorship, participants experienced unexpected and persistent side effects, but noted some positive impacts in having gone through their journey, as well as sources of encouragement and hope.

After treatment concludes, the impacts and challenges of long-term physical, cognitive, and mental health effects that negatively impact quality of life were described by participants as sometimes being unexpected. Long-term physical effects included sensitive skin, hearing loss, effects on heart and kidney function, peripheral neuropathy, hypothyroidism, persistent fatigue, speech and/or swallowing difficulties, difficulty regaining weight, and retinal detachment. Mental health impacts continued after treatment, according to both patients and caregivers, especially regarding the fear of recurrence and anxiety around restaging scans. Participants whose disease course was relatively smooth also reported feeling survivor’s guilt. Post-treatment impacts on employment and hobbies were also discussed:

Scanxiety, that’s a thing. A month or two before my MRI [magnetic resonance imaging]’s coming up, I start saying, is that a lump? … Do I have a sore throat? I’ve been coughing.

—patient, aged 50–59 years

There were also notable positive impacts and sources of hope associated with the patient experience of LA-HNSCC, including becoming more health conscious by focusing on a better diet, exercise, and stress management; slowing down and appreciating life; maturing as a person after a difficult journey and connecting with others more; reprioritizing how they spend their time; appreciating loved ones more; and learning to accept and provide help:

I think you go through some hard times and you become a different person. I also think, in managing my life, I focus on what I can control more than I used to. And when it comes to preventing cancer, living through cancer treatment, avoiding recurrence, the three things that affect it the most, I believe, are diet, exercise, and stress management. I find those really good things for good living anyway. Why wouldn’t I embrace that?

—patient, aged 50–59 years

Participants noted that the post-treatment burden may have been eased by receiving realistic information about possible physical effects, receiving ongoing connection to peer support, regular monitoring of the impacts to physical functioning and quality of life, and being connected with other members of the patient care team. They also expressed value in finding their new normal and being able to share their journey and reciprocate the same support and encouragement to other patients and survivors.

The caregivers reported that impacts on their quality of life included their relationship with their loved one. They desired to have been connected to caregiver peer support earlier in the process due to experiencing difficulties alone while trying to be strong for their loved one:

Really checking in on the caregiver and “how can we help you?” It’s all focused on the patient, which I get it. It’s rough. It’s brutal. But the anxiety and stress level ate me up alive … For the caregiver, just, “Are you okay?” Giving [them] a break. Making sure they’re taking care of themselves… That’s why these peers are so important, to really hold your hand and help you through.

—caregiver, aged 60–69 years

Discussion

This exploratory, qualitative study presents the experiences of a small group of participants, highlighting the considerable and often unexpected physical and mental health impacts and barriers along the patient journey that can be experienced by patients with LA-HNSCC and their caregivers. Though other qualitative studies have explored specific elements of the experiences of patients with head and neck cancer in other populations and contexts [13–18], this study is novel in reporting on the holistic journey of a LA-HNSCC population treated with definitive radiation therapy with or without systemic therapy in the USA, from diagnosis through to survivorship. Many reported barriers are shared and reflected across the broader cancer community and across all of the stages of the journey, such as information overload, provider access, mental health, and financial hardship. Some reported barriers are more specific to LA-HNSCC and are particular to the post-treatment and survivorship stage, such as feeding tube challenges and speech and swallowing issues.

During the initial stages of the patient journey, participants reported being overwhelmed by information. This has also been reported among different cohorts of HNSCC patients, who have frequently highlighted information overload as a challenge that needs addressing [24–26]. Such insights suggest that providers should be aware of how patients and caregivers feel to help tailor LA-HNSCC care and communication styles and to provide trusted and accessible information resources. Information provision should also be accompanied by careful ongoing monitoring of patient uptake and use of that information, as information alone without supportive dialogue may not be sufficient. This is critical because a possible downstream effect of the difficulty managing the breadth of information may include the de-prioritization of support that may help minimize long-term side effects, such as accessing peer support, mental health care, and rehabilitation therapies (e.g., physical, speech, swallowing, and occupational therapy).

The intensity and burden of nonsurgical treatments in LA-HNSCC can result in substantial, long-lasting, and often unexpected side effects and physical changes [1], as highlighted by the participants. One of the physical effects mentioned by participants as having considerable impact on quality of life and daily living was dysphagia; in keeping with this, other literature has reported that approximately half of survivors of HNSCC continue to experience dysphagia at least 2 years after treatment [27, 28]. Further, participants were concerned about the effects of current treatments and impacts on their quality of life. Accordingly, future treatments that may potentially be more effective and less toxic than currently available options may be of value to patients and caregivers, for example, robotic surgery, de-escalated radiation or proton beam radiation, intratumoral injections, and immunotherapy [8–12]. Patients should remain vigilant in their survivorship and be empowered to use these therapies and resources to reduce the quality-of-life impacts of the side effects of LA-HNSCC and its treatment.

In the current study, participants recounted how their mental health was often impacted and emotional support was lacking. Psychological impacts are known to be prevalent in LA-HNSCC, and mental health interventions and therapies have demonstrated benefits in this population [7, 29–31]. Care teams could be encouraged to engage in prescribing interactions with peer support and patient advocacy programs to address emotional impacts [32]. Further, counselors and nurse navigators could be introduced earlier on in the journey for patients and caregivers in real-world practice as support options, with frequent reiteration of the availability of these supports, as they may be required at multiple points. While many academic centers offer these resources, they are typically less widely available or accessible at community centers and smaller practices. Further, racial disparities in head and neck cancer, which are themselves driven by structural inequalities in the global healthcare system, can in turn reduce access to high-quality and specialized cancer care, negatively impacting diagnostic and treatment delays and contributing to higher mortality rates [33–35]. Cancer centers that have psychologists integrated within multidisciplinary head and neck oncology teams may also be of particular value to patients and caregivers; however, they are also rare.

In line with the recommendations shared by participants in this study, previous studies have outlined the requirement for psychosocial and informational support in LA-HNSCC and that variations in support needs exist over time and across different stages of the patient and caregiver journey [13–15, 17, 18, 36–39]. Further, although this study focused on a population receiving definitive radiation therapy, other literature in surgically treated head and neck cancers has validated the need for improved information-sharing and mental health support [40]. Many existing peer support networks are available to assist and mentor patients and caregivers, with current study participants naming Imerman Angels [21], the Head and Neck Alliance [22], and Support for People with Oral and Head and Neck Cancer [23] among other online informational resources (Table 2). Such psychosocial and informational education and support options may also benefit caregivers, who can often experience negative mental health impacts, by increasing confidence in decision-making and improving quality of life, consistent with prior reports [38, 40].

These exploratory findings describing the experiences of seven patients and two caregivers may be useful for informing residents, clinicians, and early-career providers to strengthen their patient and caregiver interactions. In particular, the findings suggest that providing early education, clarifying treatment expectations, and fostering compassionate communication, especially regarding long-term outcomes, may help improve patient and caregiver experiences.

Limitations

The study has several limitations. First regarding the sample population, the total number of participants was small, particularly the caregiver sample. As members of the Johnson & Johnson Head & Neck Cancer PERC, the participants were highly selected and likely to be more invested in their own healthcare than the general population of US patients with LA-HNSCC. They were also compensated for their participation, which may have impacted the opinions shared during the research engagements. The sample was largely White, highly educated, and English-speaking, and had been treated at academic medical centers—a population for whom communication barriers are presumed minimal. That these patients still reported feeling unprepared for treatment and survivorship suggests that current counseling approaches fall short even under near-ideal conditions and raises concern for populations facing greater sociodemographic, linguistic, or access-related barriers. This limited diversity—including limited racial/ethnic diversity and exclusion of non-English-speaking participants—hinders representation and restricts the transferability of findings to minority populations. Future research should expand council demographics to help drive equity in research in this space. In addition, the LA-HNSCC experience is highly variable and heterogeneous; therefore, the patient experiences discussed are unlikely to be representative of all journeys in the broader LA-HNSCC population. Owing to the small sample size and exploratory nature of the research, stratification by demographics and within-group comparisons were not possible. Data collection of these demographic characteristics was for the purpose of describing the study population. Additional quantitative clinical data beyond those reported in Table 1—for example, tumor subsite, stage distribution, and recurrence status—though not collected in this study, may have been useful for describing the study population in more clinical detail. Regarding the methodology, as with all qualitative studies of this nature, the participants may have been susceptible to reporting and recall bias. Additionally, the discussion guide materials were not pilot-tested, and data saturation assessment used a rapid implementation approach, which may have impacted the comprehensiveness of data collection. Taken together, these limitations suggest that the findings should be interpreted with caution.

Conclusions

This exploratory, qualitative study highlights the considerable physical and mental health impacts and barriers experienced by seven patients with LA-HNSCC and two caregivers. These participant perspectives emphasize the value in accessing and engaging with peer support networks, with many existing opportunities for support and mentoring throughout the LA-HNSCC journey and survivorship. Based on the patient and caregiver insights, potential improvements in LA-HNSCC care could focus on nonspecialist provider education and increasing awareness, access to trusted information, and support in comprehending information at each stage in the journey, access to mental health support and patient journey navigators, improved prevention and diagnosis, improved therapies with minimized side effects, and new supportive therapies to reduce the impact of side effects. These findings may inform future opportunities to better support and empower patients with LA-HNSCC and their caregivers along their journey from diagnosis to treatment and survivorship.
