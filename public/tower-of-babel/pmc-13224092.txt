Self-Dimerization Induced Proximity Targeting Chimeras (SDIPTAC) Lead to DCAF1 Loss of Function and Inhibition of HIV Replication
Authors: Mark F. Mabanglo, Smriti Srivastava, Yusuke Matsui, Zichong Li, Mahmoud Noureldin, Justin P. Pogmore, Laurent Hoffer, Fatemeh Taherian, Taraneh Hajian, Sarah Tucker, Ahmed Mamai, Taira Kiyota, Ahmed Aman, Rima Al-awar, Richard Marcellus, David E. Uehling, Melanie Ott, Jailall Ramnauth, Masoud Vedadi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
DCAF1 is a donut shaped WD40 repeat protein and a substrate receptor of two distinct E3 ligases. In HIV-1 infection, the viral protein Vpr binds to the top surface of DCAF1, changes its substrate specificity to degrade human proteins involved in antiviral activities, enabling HIV-1 to replicate. We hypothesized that artificial top-to-top self-dimerization of DCAF1 could result in DCAF1 loss-of-function and blocking of the Vpr-DCAF1 interaction. We designed and synthesized seven compounds we call SDIPTACs (Self-dimerization Induced Proximity Targeting Chimeras) which artificially induce DCAF1 self-dimerization through the Vpr interaction surface. Interestingly, SDIPTACs C8 and C9, inhibited Vpr-dependent HIV replication in CD4+ T cells. Biophysical data and crystal structures of four DCAF1-SDIPTAC-DCAF1 ternary complexes revealed more details on DCAF1-DCAF1 complex stability, conformation, and their contribution to compound efficacy. Using SDIPTACs with unique mechanism may therefore be an efficient strategy in the development of future therapeutics for HIV infection and other diseases.

Introduction

Polyubiquitination typically labels a protein for degradation via the proteasome. Protein degradation by the ubiquitin-26S proteasome system involves a multistep process of covalently linking ubiquitin to substrate proteins, which involves ubiquitin-activating (E1) and ubiquitin-conjugating (E2) enzymes, and ubiquitin ligases (E3). E3 ligases bind to substrate proteins through E3 ligase receptors. DCAF1 (DDB1-CUL4-associated factor 1) is a WD40 repeat protein and a substrate receptor of two distinct E3 ligases, CRL4DCAF1 (CUL4A-DDB1-DCAF1) and EDVP (EDD-DDB1-VprBP) , with nonoverlapping substrates, which play critical physiological roles in protein degradation. DCAF1 was initially discovered as a target protein hijacked by the human immunodeficiency virus-1 (HIV-1) viral protein, Vpr. Vpr acts as a substrate adaptor altering DCAF1 function to interact with and facilitate degradation of human proteins such as exonuclease 1 (Exo1), histone deacetylase 7 (SIRT7), ZIP and sZIP transcriptional regulators, and restriction factor SAMHD1 (Sterile alpha motif and HD domain-containing protein 1), leading to viral immune evasion and more efficient HIV-1 replication.

Primate lentiviral protein Vpx functions in a similar manner to Vpr. The crystal structure of a ternary complex of Vpx-DCAF1-carboxy-terminal region of human SAMHD1 (PDB id: 4Z8L) revealed that Vpx is an antiparallel V-shaped, three-helix bundle, stabilized by a zinc finger motif. Vpx wraps around the side and binds to a large cleft on the top surface of DCAF1’s β-propeller structure, creating a new molecular surface for interaction with proteins beside its canonical substrates, such as SAMHD1. Subsequently, the crystal structure of DDB1-DCAF1-Vpr-UNG2 (uracil-DNA glycosylase) (PDB id: 5JK7) revealed how Vpr presents the human UNG2 for ubiquitination by employing DCAF1. Although binding of Vpr to DCAF1 also creates an interface for UNG2 interaction and orients the protein in the correct position for ubiquitination, this recruitment is distinct from SAMHD1 binding by Vpx, as Vpr uses a variable loop for specific recruitment of the UNG2, mimicking DNA binding. The binding site on human UNG2 contacted by HIV-1 Vpr is identical to the site used for DNA binding. Like Vpx, Vpr forms a three-helix bundle in the complex and binds to a large cleft on the surface of the DCAF1 β-propeller structure. The cleft is lined with acidic residues at one end and hydrophobic residues at the center. The structure of Vpr in complex with the human nucleotide excision repair protein hHR23A (PDB id: 6XQI) also shows a distinct interaction with hHR23A and DCAF1 using overlapping binding surfaces on Vpr.

When the interaction of a drug target protein with a partner protein is essential for its disease-causing effect, antagonizing this interaction would be a reasonable approach toward the development of therapeutics. This could be achieved by development of small molecule antagonists, provided that a well-defined binding pocket exists and the interaction interface does not comprise multiple interaction sites distributed across a large surface area. , Alternatively, degradation of the drug target by proteasome system is an option. E3 ligase substrate receptors are often employed to degrade undesirable proteins using PROTACs (Proteolysis-Targeting Chimeras), heterobifunctional small molecules that bind to the protein of interest from one end and to an E3 ligase substrate receptor from the other, bringing the target protein into the proximity of the E3 ligase for polyubiquitination and degradation. Common E3 substrate receptors used for PROTACs are CRBN and von Hippel–Lindau (VHL). Mouse double minute 2 homologue (MDM2) and inhibitors of apoptosis protein (IAPs) have also been used, albeit with less frequency. Most recently, DCAF1 has also been used for degradation of drug targets by our lab and others.

However, achieving efficient and complete degradation may not always be feasible. , In addition, substrate receptors such as cereblon (CRBN) have been reported to mutate or become downregulated in resistant tumors. ,

Similarly, RIPTACs (Regulated Induced Proximity Targeting Chimeras) , are heterobifunctional small molecules and members of the chemically induced proximity family. One arm of a RIPTAC binds to the target protein, while the other binds to an essential protein required for cell survival. RIPTACs that elicit stable ternary complex formation bring the two proteins into proximity and abrogates the function of the essential protein. This leads to death of cells expressing the target protein through toxic gain-of-function. This approach leverages differentially expressed intracellular proteins. However, RIPTACs also have limitations. Their mechanism relies on toxicity arising from removing essential proteins when target proteins show significant differential expression, and may not be widely applicable. Drug toxicity is a major contributor to the clinical failure of drugs, and could significantly constrain the application of RIPTACs. Unintended toxicity in nontarget cells represents a significant liability in the development of RIPTAC-based drugs. As previously noted by authors, RIPTACs could potentially result in unpredictable outcomes in cells. , To our knowledge, this approach has not been widely employed and tested.

Here, we report the development of “Self-dimerization Induced Proximity Targeting Chimeras (SDIPTACs)”, which artificially induce DCAF1 top-to-top dimerization, resulting in loss-of-function, block Vpr-DCAF1 interaction, and inhibit HIV replication. Crystal structures of the ternary complexes of DCAF1-SDIPTAC-DCAF1′ (where DCAF1′ denotes the second DCAF1 molecule) further support the proposed mechanism of loss-of-function. With a unique mechanism, these SDIPTACs only consist of two identical ligands of the same drug target connected by a linker, exhibit no significant general toxicity, and are independent of protein degradation pathways, thereby avoiding most limitations associated with RIPTACs and PROTACs. This strategy could be applied to deactivate a wide range of drug targets regardless of their function and is practically effective for antagonizing the interaction between drug targets and their protein partners, especially when the interaction interfaces are large.

Results

We previously reported the discovery of OICR-8268 (Figure a), which binds to DCAF1 WDR domain with K D value of 38 ± 2 nM as determined by surface plasmon resonance (SPR). We later modified the pyrazole core in OICR-8268 to a pyrrole core (Figure a) to maintain its activity toward DCAF1 as a handle, along with providing the flexibility required for the incorporation of the substituents which act as a linker attachment site for the synthesis of various DCAF1-WDR5 PROTACs. , We also employed alkyl and PEG-based linkers with various lengths in order to optimize the distance between WDR5 and DCAF1.

(a) The pyrazole group of the DCAF1 ligand was changed to pyrrole group; (b) Structure of DCAF1 ligand with alkene and pyrazole substituted pyrrole core; (c) Structure of SDIPTACs bearing the DCAF1 handle with alkene-substituted pyrrole core, and 7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12, and 19 PEG linkers; (d) Structure of SDIPTAC bearing the DCAF1 handle with pyrazole-substituted pyrrole core and linked by a 9 PEG linker.

In this study, we synthesized SDIPTACs bearing identical DCAF1-binding handles on both ends, connected by linkers of varying lengths that bring two DCAF1 molecules into proximity, and enabling dimerization (Figure b–d). We initiated the synthesis of SDIPTACs using two PEG-9–linked constructs: one bearing an alkene substituent on the pyrrole ring and the other a pyrazole substituent in the DCAF1 ligand derived from the modification of OICR-8268 (Figure b). As the DCAF1 ligands with an alkene substituent on the pyrrole showed better calculated physicochemical properties such as lower molecular weight, polar surface area, and rotatable bonds, the subsequent SDIPTACs designs were focused exclusively on alkene-substituted pyrrole scaffolds of DCAF1 ligand. We synthesized seven SDIPTACs incorporating linkers with seven, eight, nine, 10, 11, 12, and 19 PEG molecules. Here on, we refer to them as C7, C8, C9, C10, C11, C12, and C19, respectively (Figure c).

Characterization of SDIPTACs

We tested SDIPTAC-induced dimerization of DCAF1 by running two single-cycle kinetic (SCK) SPR runs in tandem (Figures and S1). First, the biotinylated DCAF1 was immobilized on a streptavidin coated SPR chip (2500 RU) and binding of each SDIPTAC to immobilized DCAF1 was assessed and dissociation constant (K D) values were determined, with the exception of C19, whose binding was weak such that the K D values could not be determined reliably (Figure 1). The K D values for binding to the first DCAF1 molecule for SDIPTACs varied from 137 ± 7 nM for C11 to 6.2 ± 1.9 μM for C8 (Table ), indicating the conformation of SDIPTAC linker in solution could affect its affinity for DCAF1 to some extent.

Confirmation of ternary complex formation by SPR

Experiments were performed in triplicate (Figures and S1).

In continuation of the same experiment in the tandem SCK run, unbiotinylated DCAF1 was passed over the DCAF1-SDIPTAC complex on the same chip and formation of ternary complexes (DCAF1-SDIPTAC-DCAF1′) were monitored. Steady state dissociation constants were calculated indicating significant cooperativity for ternary complex formation for all SDIPTACs, showing much higher affinities (lower K D values), except C11. The highest cooperativity was observed for C8 with a significant decrease in K D for ternary complex formation (31-fold) (Table and Figures , S1). Interestingly, the residence time for C7 (31 min), C8 (26 min), C9 (28 min), and C12 (22 min) in complex were significantly increased, while the reverse effect was observed for C10 and C11 (Table ). The lower response units observed for C10 (Figure S1) and C11 (Figure ) compared to C7, C8 and C9, could also be explained by their 18- and 34-fold lower than C8 residence time, respectively. The ternary complexes of DCAF1-SDIPTAC-DCAF1′ with higher residence time are expected to be more stable. Multicycle kinetics SPR runs for C8 and C9 also confirmed stable ternary complex formation with K D values consistent with our SCK runs (Figure S2).

As DCAF1 acts as substrate receptor of two distinct E3 ligases (CRL4DCAF1 and EDVP), − we proceeded with testing the possibility of DCAF1 dimerization leading to DCAF1 degradation. In H460 cells expressing the DCAF1 WD40 domain, C11 and C12 exhibited dose-dependent DCAF1 degradation in the HiBiT assay, but only reaching maximal degradation (DCmax) values of 30.5 ± 4.3% and 31.6 ± 3.3% at 10 μM, respectively. Other SDIPTACs showed little to no degradation causing effect. In contrast, in the full-length DCAF1 HiBiT assay, only C11 induced measurable degradation with an average DCmax of 19.7% across 1.85–10 μM. None of the tested SDIPTACs resulted in a phenotypic effect in parental H460 cells up to 5 days of compound treatment, as assessed by ATPlite assays (Figure S3). Altogether, none of the SDIPTACs caused considerable DCAF1 degradation.

Effect of SDIPTACs on HIV Infection

Next, we proceeded to test the hypothesis that self-dimerization of DCAF1 induced by SDIPTACs will lead to DCAF1 loss-of-function and inhibition of HIV infection in macrophages and T cells. First, we assessed possible cytotoxic effect of SDIPTACs on macrophages. Cells were treated with either vehicle control or four concentrations (0.16, 0.8, 4, and 20 μM) of each SDIPTAC for 72 h (Figure a). Cytotoxicity was quantified using an ATP-based viability assay. C7 significantly reduced cell viability at concentrations of 0.8 μM and higher, whereas C12 showed a significant effect only at 20 μM. Other SDIPTACs similarly exhibited a trend toward decreased viability at 20 μM. To evaluate the effect of these SDIPTACs on HIV-1 infection in nondividing macrophages, cells were pretreated with SDIPTACs at 0.4 μM or 4 μM, and infected with replication-competent HIV-1-BaL-HSA (Figures b and S4). Six days postinfection, flow-cytometric analysis revealed significantly higher frequencies of HSA+ cells following treatment with C7, C11, and C12. C11 increased infection by 3.8-fold at 0.4 μM (p = 0.0098) and 5.7-fold at 4 μM (p = 0.0003) relative to DMSO control.

Interestingly, C8 and C9, and to a lesser extent C7, inhibited active HIV infection in activated primary CD4+ T cells over 4 days (Figure , Panel 1). In a dose–response experiment using cells from two independent donors, C8 displayed the most consistent antiviral activity. Inhibition was observed starting at 1.25 μM in donor 1 and at 0.31 μM in donor 2. C9 exhibited donor-dependent effects. In donor 1, statistically significant inhibition was only detected at 10 μM, whereas in donor 2, C9 behaved similar to C8, with inhibition beginning at 0.31 μM (Figure Panel 2). Across the tested concentration range (0.31–10 μM), neither C8 nor C9 demonstrated detectable cytotoxicity (Figure Panel 3).

To confirm that the effect of SDIPTACs on cell cycle in T-cells Vpr-dependent, we compared their effects in Vpr– and Vpr+ T-cells. The data confirm that the efficacy of the specific SDIPTACs that inhibit HIV infection (C7, C8, C9) correlate well with their effect on the Vpr-induced G2/M accumulation under nucleofection conditions.

However, considering primary monocyte-derived macrophages are not actively dividing, this type of cell cycle regulatory effect may not be relevant for macrophages. This may underlie the observed ineffectiveness of the SDIPTACs on HIV infection in macrophages. To investigate the mechanism by which C11 enhances HIV infection in monocyte-derived macrophages (MDMs), we performed mechanistic analyses using BaL-HSA and MDMs derived from multiple donors. To directly assess CD206 (mannose receptor C-type 1, MRC1) modulation independent of infection, MDMs were treated with C8, C9, or C11 for 3 days in the absence of virus, followed by analysis of surface CD206 expression by flow cytometry (Figures and S4). Our data revealed that C11, but not C8 or C9, induces a significant dose-dependent increase in CD206 expression in MDMs. Notably, C11, which showed the strongest enhancement of infection, also produced the most pronounced upregulation of CD206 (Figure ). In Donor 1, both C8 and C9 slightly increased CD206 expression at 4 μM, despite not producing a statistically significant increase in infection in parallel experiments. In Donor 2, C9 similarly slightly enhanced CD206 expression at 4 μM. In contrast, C11 induced a significant increase in CD206 expression in both donors. At 4 μM, C11 resulted in a 2.5-fold increase in Donor 1 (p = 0.0001) and a 1.7-fold increase in Donor 2 (p = 0.0037) compared to C8 and C9. CD206 is known to recognize high-mannose glycans on the HIV-1 envelope glycoprotein, thereby facilitating viral attachment and entry into target cells. Therefore, the C11-dependent increase in expression of CD206 could be the mechanism behind the significant increase in HIV-1 infection in macrophages upon C11 treatment (Figures and ).

Crystal Structures of DCAF1-SDIPTAC-DCAF1 Ternary Complexes

To better understand the mechanism by which SDIPTACs inhibit T-cell HIV infection, we solved the crystal structures of C7, C8, C9, and C11 ternary complexes with DCAF1 WDR domains (residues 1077–1390). Data collection and structure refinement statistics and PDB accession codes are shown in Table S1. The crystal lattice of C7-DCAF1 and C8-DCAF1 ternary complexes belong to the C121 space group. Their asymmetric units contain six DCAF1 molecules, of which two can be seen to form a ternary complex with C7 or C8. The other four can be seen to form a ternary complex with a symmetry-related DCAF1 molecule, but were modeled with bound pyrrole analogues of OICR-8268 (Figures S5 and S6). The crystal lattice of the C9-DCAF1 ternary complex belongs to the C121 space group with an asymmetric unit that contains eight DCAF1 molecules, of which four can be seen to form two separate ternary complexes with C9. The remaining four DCAF1 molecules are modeled with bound pyrrole analogues of OICR-8268 as before but form ternary complexes with symmetry-related DCAF1 molecules (Figure S7). Notably, the two ternary complexes in the asymmetric unit are structurally distinct, showing two different configurations for C9 (Figure S8a). One C9 molecule bound to DCAF1 chains B and D has an effective length of 51 Å (measured as the distance between the para fluorine atoms of the DCAF1 anchors). The other C9 molecule bound to DCAF1 chains F and H has a longer effective length (54 Å) and is tilted by 4.7° relative to the first (Figure S8b). Because the position of the second DCAF1 (Chain H) is only slightly changed relative to its equivalent (Chain D) in the other complex with C9 of shorter effective length, the latter C9 ternary complex will be used in subsequent discussions. Finally, the crystal lattice of the DCAF1-C11-DCAF1′ ternary complex belongs to the C121 space group. The asymmetric unit contains six DCAF1 molecules, four of which can be seen to form ternary complexes with two C11 molecules. The other two are modeled with bound pyrrole analogues of OICR-8268 as before but can be seen to form ternary complexes with symmetry-related DCAF1 molecules (Figure S9). The two C11 molecules in the model have the same effective length (46 Å) and differ only in their linker configurations (Figure S10a,b). Subsequent discussions will focus on the ternary complex formed by DCAF1 chains C and E, chosen due to overall better electron density map for the C11 linker and nearby protein residues that interact with it. The structures were refined to final resolutions of 2.07–2.60 Å, with an R work/R free of 0.2417–0.2685/0.2783–0.3167, and with more than 98% of residues in favored and allowed Ramachandran regions (Figure ).

In the four ternary complex structures two DCAF1 molecules are arranged in a top-to-top orientation as expected (Figure a–d). Ternary complexes with C7, C8 and C11 are structurally globally similar, while with C9, DCAF1′ is tilted on one side of DCAF1 akin to an “open clam”. The plane where DCAF1 and DCAF1′ meet to form the interface of the C9 ternary complex is therefore called the hinge, as indicated in Figure c. Thus, different types of DCAF1-DCAF1′ interactions stabilize the ternary complexes (Figure a–d). In the C7-DCAF1 ternary complex, the protein–protein interface buries 198 Å2 of surface area encompassing several residues (Figure a). Besides a hydrogen bond between D1198 of DCAF1 and E1318 of DCAF1′, a hydrophobic stacking interaction between the W1156 residues of the two opposing DCAF1 molecules is observed. This “twin tryptophan” motif is also observed at the interface of the C8- and C11-DCAF1 ternary complexes, although the tryptophan residues are stacked differently in the latter (Figure b, d). This motif is absent at the protein interface of the C9-DCAF1 ternary complex due to a different DCAF1′ orientation (Figure c). The linker curvatures of C7, C8, and C11 complement the shape of their respective twin tryptophan motifs, suggesting a possible role for the motifs in ternary complex stabilization through linker stabilization by providing a van der Waals interaction surface (Figure a,b,d). As expected, the protein interfaces of the C7, C8, and C11-DCAF1 ternary complexes cover similar surface areas.

We previously described the importance of the DCAF1 specificity loop (residues 1313–1333) in the recognition of substrate proteins, including those bound to PROTACs. This specificity loop also appears to be important in SDIPTAC-mediated ternary complexes, particularly with C7, C8, and C11. As in various DCAF1-PROTAC-WDR5 ternary complexes solved by our group, the DCAF1 specificity loop adjusts to the linker curvatures of C7, C8, and C11, albeit being incompletely modeled in these structures due to some flexibility (Figure a,b,d). Among the three, the specificity loop in the C8-DCAF1 ternary complex shape complements the C8 linker curvature the most and reaches closest to the surface of DCAF1′ (Figure b). In the C11-DCAF1 ternary complex, it is farther away from the middle part of the linker that twists in the opposite direction to form the curvature (Figure d). Interestingly, the specificity loop of DCAF1′ in the C7, C8, and C11 ternary complexes is also partially disordered but appear to cradle the first two to three PEG units near the DCAF1′ anchor of SDIPTACs (Figure a,b,d). Thus, the specificity loop of each DCAF1 molecule in the C7, C8, or C11 ternary complex structure interacts with SDIPTACs differently, allowing for the distinction between dimerized DCAF1 molecules. This phenomenon might arise from the random sequential binding of DCAF1 to SDIPTACs, whose linker lengths could not be divided equally between, and bound identically by, two DCAF1 molecules.

In the C9-DCAF1 ternary complex, the DCAF1 specificity loop is also partially disordered and farther away from C9 as its linker curves toward the hinge of the “open clam” assembly (Figures c and c). However, the DCAF1′ specificity loop might still provide some binding surface for the first two PEG units of the top C9 anchor. The hinge is stabilized by two interfacing salt bridges between K1196 of one DCAF1 molecule and D1198 of the other. Further, residues N1158, E1178, I1199, H1201, K1210, T1213, F1215, P1217, N1222, and K1224 participate in extensive van der Waals interactions to bury a surface area of 278 Å2 (Figure c).

In all four SDIPTAC ternary complexes, protein residues that interact with DCAF1 anchors are conserved as seen in related structures. Briefly, hydrogen bonding interactions (water-mediated or not) contribute to tight binding of SDIPTACs (Figure a–d) involving residues H1140, R1298, F1355 (using a main chain carbonyl group), and D1356. The tyrosyl side chain of Y1181 contributes to the binding of the DCAF1 anchor by providing a hydrophobic surface in all four structures. In the C7-DCAF1 ternary complex, an alternate rotamer for Y1181 is observed in both DCAF1 chains, enabling both a hydrogen bond with a C7 amide nitrogen atom, and hydrophobic stacking interaction with C7′s ortho fluoro, para chloro disubstituted ring (Figure a). Residues R1298 and F1330 also contribute by stabilizing van der Waals interactions with the DCAF1 anchor. The plane of the R1298 side chain directly stacks with C7′s ortho fluoro, para chloro disubstituted ring (Figure a–d). Postrefinement 2Fo-Fc maps for all structures show strong electron density for SDIPTACs, except for a few middle PEG linker atoms of C8, C9, and C11 that indicate some flexibility (Figure b,d). Nevertheless, the path of broken electron density indicated a plausible model for these flexible linker regions. The longer C11 linker is accommodated inside the complex through coiling of the first and last three PEG units nearest to the DCAF1 anchors, filling the DCAF1 donut hole (Figures d and d).

Discussion

The mechanism of homo- and heterodimerization has been extensively studied. Induced dimerization of proteins, including that achieved through molecular glues, has been reported. , The concept of molecular glue-induced protein dimerization is based on the use of low molecular weight organic compounds bearing bifunctional moieties which interact simultaneously with two proteins or protein domains. In this study, we investigated occluding Vpr binding to DCAF1 through induced artificial DCAF1 self-dimerization by SDIPTACs as a potential mechanism for inhibiting HIV replication. Our previous work with WDR5-targeting DCAF1-based PROTACs revealed that a linker with nine PEG units leads to the most stable ternary complex. We therefore designed SDIPTACs built from the DCAF1 inhibitor OICR-8268, utilizing linker lengths both shorter and longer than nine PEG units. , We then tested their ability to assemble stable ternary complexes with DCAF1 and inhibit HIV replication in T-cells.

Our data suggest that the divergent effects of SDIPTACs in macrophages and CD4+ T-cells primarily arise from how enforced DCAF1 dimerization alters the accessibility of CRL4DCAF1 to viral and host cofactors. DCAF1 is essential for T-cell growth and cell-cycle entry, and Vpr-mediated reprogramming of CRL4DCAF1 has long been linked to G2 arrest in CD4+ T cells. , In activated T-cells, SDIPTAC-induced top-to-top dimerization sterically occludes the Vpr-binding interface and generates CRL4DCAF1 assemblies that are incompatible with Vpr recruitment. Consistently, our data reveal that SDIPTAC C8 and C9 effects are Vpr-dependent. This model aligns with the potent antiviral activity of C8 and C9, and with our HiBiT data showing minimal DCAF1 degradation, apart from the modest turnover induced by C11 and C12 at high concentrations (10–30 μM). Together, these findings indicate that C8 and C9 primarily exert antiviral activity through self-dimerization-mediated inhibition of Vpr binding, rather than through DCAF1 depletion.

In contrast, previous studies have shown that in differentiated macrophages Vpr co-opts DCAF1 to degrade macrophage-specific restriction factors of Env, thereby enhancing Env expression, virion infectivity, and macrophage-dependent spread to CD4+ T-cells. , Surprisingly, however, we found that several SDIPTACs increased rather than decreased HIV infection in macrophages. This phenotype suggests a mechanistically distinct process, potentially involving partial or context-dependent inhibition of DCAF1 that disrupts basal CRL4DCAF1 activities critical for macrophage homeostasis, cytokine responsiveness, or innate antiviral pathways. , Importantly, CD206 (encoded by MRC1) is known to recognize high-mannose glycans on the HIV-1 envelope glycoprotein, thereby facilitating viral attachment and entry into target cells. Therefore, we also investigated the effect of SDIPTACs on CD206 expression in monocyte-derived macrophages as a potential mechanism for C11 action. Consistently, our BaL-HSA infection data suggest that SDIPTAC-mediated upregulation of CD206 may contribute to enhanced viral entry. Collectively, these results indicate that while engineered DCAF1 dimerization constitutes a promising strategy to block Vpr-dependent HIV replication in T-cells, macrophages respond to DCAF1 perturbation in a fundamentally different and more complex manner. Defining the macrophage DCAF1 interactome and determining how SDIPTACs rewire these networks as well as understanding their penetration and behavior within myeloid reservoirs will be essential for rationally tailoring DCAF1-based antivirals to specific HIV cellular niches.

Interestingly, C8 and C9 have the longest residence times for inducing DCAF1-DCAF1 dimerization, indicating ternary complex stability (Table ). This could increase the efficacy of their ternary complexes in antagonizing Vpr binding. Crystal structures support these observations. Superposition of C7-, C8-, C9-, and C11-DCAF1 ternary complex structures with that of DCAF1-Vpr complex (PDB id: 5JK7) shows that SDIPTAC-bridged DCAF1 dimers could overlap with Vpr at two sites: at the DCAF1 donut hole where the C-terminus and third helix of Vpr binds, and at the position of the two N-terminal helices of Vpr’s three-helix bundle (Figure a). Compression, tilting, and rotation of SDIPTACs (Figure b) inside ternary complexes position DCAF1′ at two distinct locations that could impede Vpr binding to DCAF1 (Figure c,d). The crystal structure of the DCAF1-C8-DCAF1′ ternary complex shows that the twin tryptophan motif formed during complex assembly, and the DCAF1 specificity loops could work together to provide stabilizing effects to the linker and to the ternary complex as a whole. The extent of these stabilizing effects might be less pronounced in the C11-ternary complex, as supported by relatively weaker electron density for the linker (Figure d). The DCAF1 specificity loop is positioned farther away from the C11 linker curvature than it is to that of C8, potentially attenuating its stabilizing effect (Figure S11). Differential and less ideal stacking in the twin tryptophan motif of the C11-DCAF1 ternary complex might also weaken the ternary complex (Figures d and S11). Furthermore, the C11 linker has more degrees of freedom and its compression inside the DCAF1 dimer interface could be strained. Together, these structural attributes could lead to faster breakdown of the ternary complex resulting in lower residence time as indicated by SPR data (Table ), allowing binding of incoming Vpr to DCAF1. Thus, although the position of DCAF1′ in the C8 and C11 ternary complexes is the same (Figure c), the latter is a less stable ternary complex and therefore, a weaker inhibitor of HIV replication in cells.

SDIPTAC-mediated dimerization of DCAF1 forms a superstructure that could abolish Vpr binding. (a) Superposition of the C7-, C8-, C9-, and C11-DCAF1 ternary complex structures with that of the DDB1-DCAF1-Vpr-UNG2 binary complex (PDB id 5JK7), but showing only DCAF1­(purple) and Vpr (yellow green). The α-helices of Vpr from the N- to C-terminus are numbered. DCAF1 from the Vpr complex is shown at the base, while DCAF1′ from each SDIPTAC ternary complex is shown above it. C9 positions DCAF1′ (pink cartoon) differently from C7, C8, and C11 (shown as one representative molecule, light blue cartoon). SDIPTAC backbones are shown as sticks in different hues of green and are magnified in (b). (b) Overlay shows C7 (PDB id 9ZL9), C8 (PDB id 9ZLE), and C11 (PDB id 9ZLI) with the same effective length achieved by linker curving, while C9 (PDB id 9ZLG) is longer and is rotated and tilted relative to the long axis of the other three. C11 achieves the same effective length as C7 and C8 by coiling the first three PEG units after the DCAF1 anchors. (c, d) Two orientations for DCAF1′ are observed in ternary complexes with SDIPTACs. Part of the linkers of C7 and C8 also overlap with Vpr; see (b). In the C9 ternary complex, the C9 linker curves away from the Vpr volume, minimizing overlap between DCAF1′ and Vpr.

Even though DCAF1′ has a different position in the C9-DCAF1 ternary complex and its overlap with Vpr would be less in the E3 ligase context, it could still be capable of abolishing Vpr binding as Vpr could approach the base DCAF1 from many directions in solution (Figure d). This DCAF1′ positioning almost doubles the buried surface area at the interface compared to that of the C7-, C8-, or C11-DCAF1 ternary complexes (Figure c). Furthermore, the C9 linker’s exploitation of an alternate binding groove on the surface of DCAF1 near the hinge of the open clam assembly (Figure S12) could lead to linker stabilizing effects (Figure b,c,d). Although the middle part of the C9 linker suggests multiple configurations based on broken electron density (Figure c), C9-mediated cooperative binding brings about the stable open clam structure of the DCAF1 dimer.

Biophysical and structural attributes support the observed potency of C8 and C9 in inducing DCAF1 self-dimerization to create a superstructure antagonizing Vpr binding and inhibiting HIV replication in T-cells. The fact that C7, C8, C9 and C11 are unable to place DCAF1′ at the position of UNG2, a natural substrate of DCAF1, in a putative E3 ligase complex might be a reason why they are less effective DCAF1 degraders in cells (Figures S13–S16).

Altogether, our data indicate that SDIPTAC-induced artificial self-dimerization of DCAF1 results in DCAF1 loss-of-function and inhibition of HIV replication in T-cells. In addition, we propose that the self-dimerization-based approach using SDIPTACs may prove to be a more efficient mechanism for the development of future therapeutics for other diseases, particularly when large protein–protein interaction interfaces need to be blocked. Finally, although DCAF1 is an essential protein, SDIPTACs do not show any significant cell toxicity within the range they inhibited HIV-1 replication in T-cells. Therefore, this approach may be a more efficient way to target essential drug target proteins.

Experimental Section

Cloning

Biotinylated DCAF1 DNA fragment encoding residues 1036–1400 with Avi-tag on upstream and 3XFlag/His-tag on downstream was designed to subclone under polyhedrin promotor of pFastBacDual with BirA sequence under p10 promotor control. However, unbiotinylated DCAF1 was designed in pFastBacDual containing the WDR domain coding sequence 1077–1390 with N-terminal His-tagged under polyhedrin promoter control only and both were synthesized by ThermoFisher.

Protein Expression

DCAF1 was purified as previously described. The constructs were transformed into DH10Bac Escherichia coli (E. coli; ThermoFisher; Cat. #10361012) to create, amplify, and isolate the Bacmid DNA of interest, according to a protocol by Hutchinson & Seitova (2021). Using the same protocol, recombinant baculovirus carrying the code for the proteins-of-interest were produced to infect large-scale suspension cultures. Briefly, Spodoptera frugiperda 9 (SF9) cells (ThermoFisher; Cat. #11496015) maintained in a 6-well plate format (ThermoFisher; Cat. #140685) were transfected (VWR; Cat. #CA89129-920) with the harvested Bacmid DNA, generating recombinant baculovirus. This virus was collected and subsequently utilized to infect larger quantities of SF9 suspension-culture. After approximately 48–72 h of incubation, a culture pellet was collected via centrifugation and stored at −80 °C until required for protein purification.

Protein Purification

SF9 cell pellets were resuspended in binding buffer: 50 mM Tris–HCl pH 7.5, containing 500 mM NaCl, 5 mM imidazole, and 5% glycerol (Bioshop; Cat. #s TRS002.5, SOD001.5, IMD508.1, and GLY004.4, respectively). The cell suspension was supplemented with 100 mM Benzamidine (Bioshop; Cat. # BEN601.100), 100 mM PMSF (Bioshop; Cat.# PMS123.100), 5 mM MgCl2, 1 mM Tris­(2-carboxyethyl)­phosphine hydrochloride (TCEP), 1X protease-inhibitor cocktail (0.25 mg/mL Aprotinin, 0.25 mg/mL Leupeptin, 0.25 mg/mL Pepstatin A and 0.25 mg/mL E–64; Sigma; Cat. #s 208337-1KG, C4706-50G, 11583794001, 11529048001, 11524488001, and 11585681001, correspondingly) and 1x Roche complete EDTA-free protease inhibitor cocktail tablet (ThermoFisher; Cat. #PIA32955). The cells were lysed chemically with NP40 at a final concentration 0.5% (ThermoFisher; Cat. #PI85124) and 120 unit/L Benzonase Nuclease (Sigma; Cat. #E1014-25KU), followed by sonication via a Qsonica 700 (ThermoFisher; Cat. #15338281) set to 70% amplitude (10 s on; 7 s off) The crude extract was clarified by high-speed centrifugation (20 min at 20,000 rpm, 4 °C), where the clarified lysate was then passed through a HispurTM Ni-NTA resin (ThermoFisher; Cat. #88223) column pre-equilibrated with binding buffer. The column was then washed and eluted with binding buffer containing additional imidazole at final concentrations of 30 mM and 250 mM, respectively. The protein was further purified by gel filtration on a Superdex200 26/60 (Cytiva; Cat. #28989336) using a BioRad system (BioRad; Cat. #7880009) pre-equilibrated with 50 mM Tris 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 5% glycerol, and 1 mM TCEP. The purity of the fractions was confirmed via SDS-PAGE, whereafter the fractions were pooled, concentrated and flash frozen. The purity of the final unbiotinylated DCAF1 was estimated to be more than 90%. However, some proteolysis was observed in the final biotinylated DCAF1.

Surface Plasmon Resonance (SPR) Single-Cycle Kinetics Experiments

Surface plasmon resonance (SPR) experiments were carried out on a Biacore 8K system using SA series S chips (Lot number 10365187). All experiments used 0.22 μm sterile-filtered HBS-P buffer composed of 10 mM HEPES pH 7.6, 150 mM NaCl, 0.05% Tween-20, and 0.2% DMSO. Proteins were buffer exchanged into HBS-P buffer using 7 kDa Zeba Spin columns. For each experiment, biotinylated DCAF1 WDR domain (1077–1390) was immobilized to a level of 2500 RU. Two single-cycle kinetic (SCK) protocols were run in tandem to load the immobilized DCAF1 with compound, then unbiotinylated DCAF1 WDR domain to form the ternary complex. SCK1 used 5, 50, 500, 5000, 50000 nM compound with an association time of 240 s, final dissociation time of 0 s, and a flow rate of 40 μL/min for each injection. SCK2 used 625, 1250, 2500, 5000, 10000 nM with an association time of 240 s, final dissociation time of 600 s, and a flow rate of 40 μL/min for each injection. Tandem SCK were performed within the same channel. Each compound and subsequent ternary complex formation was tested in triplicate. Negative control measurements used DMSO in place of compound. Dissociation constants reported were calculated from blank-subtracted sensorgrams in Biacore Insight evaluation software (Ver 5.0 18.22102).

SPR Multicycle Kinetics Experiments

Experiments were carried out on a Biacore 8K system using SA series S chips with the lot number 10365187. All experiments used 0.22 μm sterile-filtered HBS-P buffer composed of 10 mM HEPES pH 7.6, 150 mM NaCl, 0.05% Tween-20, and 0.2% DMSO. Proteins were buffer exchanged into HBS-P buffer using 7 kDa Zeba Spin columns. For each experiment, biotinylated DCAF1 WDR domain (1077–1390) was immobilized to a level of 2500 RU. A general injection of DMSO or 20 μM SDIPTAC in 8 channels was performed with an association time of 360 s, dissociation time of 60 s, and a flow rate of 20 μL/min immediately followed by a multicycle kinetic protocol where unbiotinylated DCAF1 was injected with an association time of 240 s, final dissociation time of 600 s, and a flow rate of 30 μL/min for each injection. Channels were washed with 50% DMSO in running buffer in between different replicates and SDIPTAC tested. Apparent dissociation constants reported were calculated from reference channel-subtracted sensorgrams in Biacore Insight evaluation software (Ver 5.0 18.22102).

Binding Kinetics

Binding kinetics were determined using a 1:1 langmuir interaction model applied to sensorgrams acquired on the Biacore 8K. The association rate constant k on and dissociation rate constant k off were obtained by globally fitting the sensorgram data using Biacore Insight Evaluation software. The equilibrium dissociation constant K D was calculated as the ratio of k off/k on. Parameter confidence was assessed using T-values, defined as the ratio of each fitted parameter to its standard error. The standard error of K D was derived by propagating the uncertainties of k on and k off. Since the dissociation time for SCK1 was 0 to accommodate loading of the compound to immobilized DCAF1, k off values may be inaccurate if the small dissociation times between each injection of the titration did not appreciably lower the RU to make a reliable estimation. In all cases excluding C19, a change in >5% decrease from total RU was detected for each dissociation after subsequent injections, indicating a sufficient change to calculate k off.

Monocyte-Derived Macrophages

Monocytes were isolated from human PBMCs (Vitalant) by CD14 negative selection (STEMCELL technologies, 19359) and cultured for 7 days in serum-free medium (STEMCELL technologies, 10961) supplemented with 50 ng/mL M-CSF (Fisher Scientific, Cat#300-25) to induce differentiation into macrophages. Every 3 days, half of the culture medium was replaced with fresh medium containing M-CSF.

ATP Cell Toxicity Assay

Macrophages were incubated with the SDIPTAC compound for 72 h, washed with PBS (Corning, 21-040), and subsequently treated with an ATP detection reagent (Promega, G8941). After incubation at 37 °C for 20 min, luminescence was measured using a microplate reader.

Macrophage Infection Assay

Macrophages were incubated with the SDIPTAC compound for 2 h, followed by infection with HIV-1 BaL-HSA. The following day, the culture medium was replaced with fresh medium containing SDIPTAC, and cells were harvested after 6 days. Prior to staining, cells were incubated with an Fc receptor blocker (eBioscience, 14-9162-42) for 15 min, and HSA-positive cells were labeled with an FITC-conjugated anti-HSA antibody (BioLegend, 101805). The percentage of HSA-positive cells was quantified by flow cytometry (LSRFortessaX-20).

Macrophage gating strategy: BaL-HSA infection in MDMs was analyzed using a BD LSRFortessa X-20 flow cytometer. Prior to staining, cells were incubated with an Fc receptor blocking reagent (BD, 564220) to minimize nonspecific antibody binding for 15 min. Representative data for fluorescence minus one (FMO), unstained, uninfected, and infected samples are shown in Figure S4. First, intact cells were identified by excluding debris based on forward and side scatter properties. Doublets were then excluded using FSC-A versus FSC-H and SSC-A versus SSC-W parameters. Viable cells were subsequently selected by excluding Zombie NIR (BioLegend, 423105)–positive dead cells (top panels). HSA expression was detected in the FITC channel (BioLegend, 101805). The threshold for HSA positivity was defined using the FMO control, with gates set to distinguish the clearly positive population from background signal (bottom panels).

CD4+ T Cell Infection Assay

Primary CD4+ T cells were activated with 1 μg/mL anti-CD28 (BD 555725) on anti-CD3–coated plates (BD 555336) for 2 days and maintained under the same conditions in STCM. Cells were then infected with HIV-GFP (NL4.3, X4 tropic) produced in HEK293T, and treated with either 0.1% DMSO or the DCAF1 SDIPTACs at the indicated concentrations in triplicates. After 4 days, cells were either unstained (Figure , Panel 1) or stained with Zombie Aqua (BioLegend 423101, for Figure , Panels 2 and 3) and analyzed by FACS for viability (FSC/SSC gating for Figure , Panel1, BV521 channel for Figure , Panels 2 and 3) and GFP. Data were processed in FlowJo and plotted as bar graphs showing mean ± SD, with significance assessed by two-tailed Student’s t tests (*p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001).

X-Ray Crystallography

DCAF1 ternary complexes with C7, C8, C9, and C11 were prepared by mixing 200 μM DCAF1 with 100 μM SDIPTAC and incubating for 1 h at 4 °C. Sitting drop crystallization trays were prepared using a Gryphon crystallization robot (Art Robbins Instruments) by mixing protein solutions with commercial screens (1:1 ratio) from Hampton Research and Mitegen, then incubating at 21 °C. Crystals grew to maximum dimensions within 1 week under various conditions. Crystals were harvested by flash freezing in liquid nitrogen using as cryoprotectant the mother liquor supplemented with 20% glycerol. X-ray diffraction data were collected locally using the Rigaku MicroMax-003 (λ = 1.54189 Å) with an Eiger R 1 M detector. The best diffracting crystals were grown in solutions containing 0.2 M sodium fluoride, 20% w/v PEG 3350, pH 7.3 (C7-DCAF1 ternary complex); 1.0 M ammonium tartrate dibasic, 0.1 M sodium acetate trihydrate, pH 4.6 (C8-DCAF1 ternary complex); 5% w/v 1-ethyl-3-methylimidazolium acetate, 15% w/v PEG 20,000, pH 6.5 (C9-DCAF1 ternary complex); and 0.1 M magnesium chloride hexahydrate, 0.1 M potassium chloride, 0.1 M PIPES, pH 7.0, 20% v/v PEG smear medium (C11-DCAF1 ternary complex). Diffraction data were indexed, integrated, and scaled using XDS or CrysAlisPro (Rigaku, Agilent Technologies; https://rigaku.com) and Aimless (CCP4). , The structure of each complex was solved by molecular replacement using Phaser in Phenix, and the ligandless structure of DCAF1 (PDB id 8F8E) as search model. The initial solution was refined in Phenix with simulated annealing and coordinate shaking to eliminate model bias. Subsequent iterative model building and refinement were performed in Coot and Phenix with individual coordinate, occupancy, and B-factor optimization. Data collection and refinement statistics are summarized in Table S1.

Degradation Assays

Cell Culture

H460 cells were purchased from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC). Cells were cultured in RPMI (Gibco, 11835030) supplemented with 10% FBS (Gibco, 12483020) at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 incubator. Cells provided by the ATCC were routinely authenticated using short tandem repeat.

ATPlite

The ATPlite firstep Luminescence Assay System (Revvity, 6016739) was used to measure ATP levels, which served as an indirect measure of cell number. NCI–H460 cells were plated across a 96-well plate at a density of 2000 cells per 100 μL. A range of 10 different concentrations of compounds (10 μM to 0.005 μM) were added using an HP D300e Digital Dispenser (Tecan; Model # 30100152). To measure ATP levels, 100 μL of reconstituted ATPlite reagent (containing luciferase and D-luciferin) was added to the cells after 5 days of compound treatment. The plate was then shaken for 10 min to ensure thorough mixing. Luminescence signal was measured using the Cytation 3 plate reader (BioTek).

WD40 Domain and Full Length DCAF1 HiBiT Degradation Assay

H460 cells engineered to constitutively express HiBiT-tagged WD40 DCAF1 or full length DCAF1 were plated across a 96-well plate at a density of 10,000–20,000 cells per 100 μL in RPMI medium supplemented with 10% FBS. A range of 10 different concentrations of compounds (10 μM to 0.005 μM) were added using an HP D300e Digital Dispenser (Tecan; Model # 30100152). The cells were incubated at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 for 24 h. Subsequently, Nano-Glo HiBiT Lytic reagent (Promega, Cat. #N3030) containing both LgBiT protein and the furimazine substrate was added to the wells. The HiBiT and LgBiT proteins interact to form a luminescent NanoLuc luciferase enzyme, which emits light in the presence of the furimazine substrate. The intensity of luminescence emitted is directly proportional to the amount of HiBiT-tagged protein present. Plates were shaken on an orbital shaker at 500 rpm for 10 min, followed by luminescence measurement using a Cytation 3 plate reader (BioTek, Model #CYTV3).
