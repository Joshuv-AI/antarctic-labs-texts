Reliability analysis of different geometric configurations of Helmholtz resonators for a sustainable performance
Authors: S. Saravanamurugan, Janush Elango, M. Thenarasu, Jana Petru
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
A Helmholtz resonator (HR), consisting of a cavity connected to a neck, is widely used for noise control at a specific tuning frequency determined by the neck cross-sectional area and length, cavity volume, and speed of sound. Uncertainty in geometric parameters and air properties can significantly influence the tuning frequency and transmission loss, thereby affecting HR performance. In this study, finite element analysis is employed to optimise the geometry of an HR integrated with a duct system. In addition, a reliability analysis is carried out by considering normally distributed random variables governing HR performance. Six neck–cavity configurations, namely cylinder–cylinder, cuboid–cylinder, cylinder–cuboid, cuboid–cuboid, cylinder–sphere, and cuboid–sphere, are analysed using a six-sigma-based reliability approach. The results indicate that the tuning frequency can deviate by up to 10% from the target value due to uncertainties. Experimental validation using an additively manufactured HR duct system demonstrates similar trends in frequency shift and peak transmission loss within the range of 6–10% from the desired tuning frequency. The findings highlight the importance of reliability-based design to ensure consistent and sustainable HR performance. Helmholtz resonators provide an effective and environmentally friendly passive acoustic solution for industrial noise control and can be extended to other noise-sensitive environments.

Introduction

Helmholtz resonators are frequently used to reduce duct noise because they effectively attenuate tonal noise at specific frequencies. Many factors, such as geometry, resonant frequency, and placement, must be carefully considered to create these resonators in the best possible way. Controlling noise in a duct system largely depends on the Helmholtz resonator’s tuning frequency. Since the Helmholtz resonators are made to reduce noise at frequencies, their effectiveness largely depends on accurate tuning to the intended frequency range1. Passive tuning, a traditional tuning methodology, is a simple technique adopted to design an HR, and the resulting tuning frequency is largely influenced by geometric parameters and properties of air. Hence, the random variations in these parameters may adversely affect HR performance2. Alster3 presented an analytical solution to predict the resonant frequency that was influenced by the cavity of HR. Chanaud4 developed an analytical expression from the wave equation to find the resonance frequency of an HR having a rectangular cavity with different geometric configurations of orifice. Selamet et al.5 studied the circular concentric resonator’s performance by analysing the effects of cavity dimensions through three different analytical models, and the results were validated through experiments. Xu et al.6 analysed the acoustic characteristics of a dual HR and presented a closed-form solution to predict transmission loss and tuning frequencies. Chenzhi et al.7 discussed the noise control capacity of an HR and suggested that it was influenced by the neck geometry and the cavity’s cross-sectional area. The author also presented the acoustic performance of dual and in-line HRs and compared the analytical solution with the numerical solution8. Helmholtz resonators’ resonance characteristics are examined over a 20 Hz to 10 kHz. Didkovskiy et al.9 identified several resonance frequencies that deviated from conventional harmonic behaviour. Mercier et al.10 demonstrated how the shape of the neck of Helmholtz resonators significantly affects their resonance frequency, which is determined by the neck’s length, minimum opening, and internal air surface area. Langfeldt et al.11 proposed a novel analytical model for determining the input impedance and resonance frequency of an HR with several necks. Catapane et al.12 introduced a semi-analytical method for predicting HR’s tuning frequencies considering geometric couplings with different neck-cavity ratios. Nick Abadi et al.13 introduced a novel noise control methodology using an HR consisting of a sound-absorbing meta structure. Also, they have developed an analytical model to estimate the noise control capacity of an HR and validated the results using finite element modelling. Irfan et al.14 analysed transmission loss and insertion loss using finite element modelling and presented the frequency response of the HR system.

Though HR is an efficient noise control device, it may face reliability issues due to uncertain geometric and material properties. The reliability of an HR may be evaluated based on its resonance frequency accuracy, transmission loss, and adaptability to different conditions and configurations. Bothien et al.15 studied the influence of geometrical dimensions and fluid inertia effects on the resonant frequency of an HR and presented a correction factor for density variations. Xia et al.16 presented an optimised model with uncertainties of sound transmission coefficient and the bulk modulus to develop an HR with metamaterial. Kopytko et al.17 analysed the influence of air mass between the neck and the free field on the performance of an HR. Ćosić et al.18 presented the HR’s acoustic characteristics influenced by hot gas intrusion into the neck. Sivadas et al.19 utilized acoustic energy for effective break up characteristics in liquid atomizers. Saravanamurugan et al.20,21 employed ansys workbench environment to carry out harmonic response analysis in mechanical systems and advanced second order moment method to analyse chatter reliability in a turning process22. Parida et al.23 employed FEM to analyse the influence of tool nose radius on temperature, cutting forces, and stresses during turning of Inconel 718, validated through experiments. Akkaş et al.24 used FEM to evaluate the strength of injection-mold support pins made from Monel 400 and AISI 4130. In recent years, Li et al.25 have presented a general analytical model to predict the sound-reduction frequency of side-branch resonators for different geometries. Such models are useful for predicting geometry-sensitive behaviour; however, they mainly focus on deterministic conditions. The present study, on the other hand, investigates the effects of uncertainty of the geometric and physical parameters of the resonator on its action. The framework of a combined FEM–DOE–DFSS is used to analyse robustness on a variety of geometrical configurations, underexplored in the literature.

It can be noted here that there has been limited research done on reliability analysis of HRs, hence this paper concentrates on a detailed reliability analysis of a HR. The shape uncertainty of the neck and cavity, the air properties and the absorption coefficient are considered to ensure a reliable prediction of the tuning frequency using six-sigma analysis along with finite element simulation. Integrating probabilistic analysis and geometry optimization, this study differs from previous analytical or semi-analytical studies by emphasising the reliability-based design. The key novelty is an ability to comparatively assess the robustness of several geometries of Helmholtz resonators in the presence of uncertainty. In recent years, research has been conducted to predict the acoustic performance, and optimize the dimensions of resonators, using data-driven methods such as machine learning and deep learning models. These techniques can process a complex nonlinear relationship effectively, but they generally rely on many observations and may not have physical interpretability. The present study, however, is based on a physics-based finite element framework and probabilistic analysis, which can be used to accurately predict, as well as provide engineering insight of the effects of uncertainty. The paper is organised into seven sections starting with the Introduction, followed by finite element simulations for optimisation of HR geometry, reliability analysis using DFSS, sensitivity analysis of the reliability model, experimental study, results and discussion, and finally the conclusions.

Finite element simulations for optimization of HR geometry

Helmholtz resonator (HR) is a noise-cancelling device which is tuned to a specific frequency. It is typically a constricted tube leading to a fluid-filled cavity. In acoustic analysis, HR is usually considered as a single degree of freedom (DOF) system and has a characteristic resonance frequency1. The resonant frequency of an HR is expressed as shown in Eq. Finite element modelling

Finite Element Modelling (FEM) HRs with six different neck-cavity configurations, (i) cylinder-cylinder HR, (ii) cuboid–cylinder HR, (iii) cylinder–cuboid HR, (iv) cuboid–cuboid HR, (v) cylinder–sphere HR, and (vi) cuboid–sphere HR as shown in Fig. 2 is established with 12,000–14,000 tetrahedral elements. To capture the acoustic behaviour, HR-circular duct systems at the targeted tuning frequency of 300 Hz, the element size of 2 mm is used for computational efficiency and obtaining accurate results. The air properties, speed of sound of 343 m/s and air density of 1.225 kg/m3 are assigned, and the boundaries of both ends of the ducts are considered to be open. The response surface optimisation model, using a face-centred central composite design and the six-sigma technique with Monte Carlo simulation, is adopted for optimisation and reliability studies, respectively. In the case of reliability analysis, the geometric parameters of duct, HR, and air properties are parametrised by keeping them as random variables following Gaussian distributions. The influence of uncertain input parameter on tuning frequency and transmission loss is presented through FEM.

Refining the designs of Helmholtz resonators (HR) designs with the design of experiments (DOE) approach implies the alteration of relevant design variables in a premeditated manner by a design to achieve superior sound quality. The main parameters that are taken into consideration in this research are the neck length, cavity length, sound pressure level, transmission loss, and the frequency. This optimization process starting with selection of the parameters to the evaluation of performance has been drawing a well-organised flowchart as illustrated in Fig. 3. The DOE method simplifies the process of establishing the most efficient HR designs to achieve improved acoustic properties when the parameters are analysed in their interactions. Response Surface Optimization (RSO) is used to create a statistical model to describe the relationships between the input parameters and the responses of a system in ANSYS Workbench through the Central Composite Design (CCD) framework.

Reliability analysis of HR using design for six-sigma (DFSS)

The ANSYS Six Sigma tool is used to perform a reliability-based analysis of Helmholtz resonators (HRs) to evaluate the effects of material variability, geometric uncertainties, and variations in air properties26–28. The IDOV (Identify, Design, Optimize, Validate) methodology is used in the Design for Six Sigma (DFSS) framework to improve acoustic performance and ensure robustness under operational variability. In contrast to conventional Six Sigma methodologies like DMAIC, which seek to enhance existing processes, DFSS incorporates reliability considerations from the beginning of the design process. The key acoustic metrics, such as sound pressure level and tuning frequency, are used to determine design objectives. Judged by the three-dimensional finite element simulations, the acoustic response is predicted with the changes in the cavity volume, the neck geometry, and orifice configuration. The statistical tool applicable in the optimisation stage to identify parameter sets that yield consistent performance and less sensitivity to uncertainty is response surface methodology (RSM). The final design is tested with probabilistic simulations to assure that the final design meets the requirements of the Six Sigma reliability in different situations. This type of noise analysis, based on reliability, involves the use of a limit state function to assess the similarity of the natural frequency of a system to a predefined tuning target. The aim is to make sure that frequency of operation of the system is within reasonable range of the target frequency of 300 Hz. The maximum permissible error ∆f = 25 Hz caused by errors in material and geometric properties and damping properties can be seen as a symmetric tolerance interval of ± 25 Hz. The mathematical equation of the respective limit state function is as described in Eq. (4).4\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:g\left(f\right)=\varDelta\:f-\left|f-{f}_{t}\right|$$\end{document} where f denotes the system’s current tuning frequency and ft = 300 Hz is the targeted tuning frequency. When g(f) > 0, which indicates that the frequency is within the permitted range of 275 Hz to 325 Hz, this function defines a safe operating condition. When this band is violated, g (f) < 0, it indicates a possible failure situation where performance detuning could happen. The PDFs of the output responses are further used to evaluate the probability of satisfying the limit state condition, thereby forming the basis for reliability estimation within the DFSS framework. In order to implement DFSS, the input parameters neck length, cavity length, neck diameter, duct length, duct diameter, absorption surface area, air density, and the speed of sound are selected as random variables. Each of these parameters is assigned a percentage standard deviation to account for potential sources of variability such as manufacturing tolerances, material property fluctuations, environmental effects, and calculation or modelling errors. The standard deviations of the simulation are neck length (0.5), cavity length (0.5), neck diameter (0.5), duct diameter (0.5), air density (0.015), speed of sound (2.5), duct length (3), and the absorption surface area (0.007). Assigned standard deviations are based on realistic engineering considerations. The geometric parameters (0.5 mm variation) are typical tolerances for the additive manufacturing processes. Environmental fluctuations (such as temperature and humidity) are caused by variations in air density (± 0.015 kg/m3) and speed of sound (± 2.5 m/s). The variation in duct length (± 3 mm) is a measure of assembly-level uncertainties, and the absorption surface variability is based on material inconsistencies. These values are in agreement with those reported in previous experimental and numerical studies. In Fig. 6 the distribution of the input variables is normal. The output performance metrics under consideration are the transmission loss and resonant frequency, which are important to determine the effectiveness of the resonator in attenuating the desired frequency bands. Thousands of design iterations were made by running the DFSS principles, with random perturbations inside the specified deviation limits. The output data are then statistically analysed and probability density functions (PDFs) are plotted to show the distribution of tuning frequency and transmission loss under uncertainty (Fig. 7). These PDFs are crucial for determining the system’s chances of attaining the set performance goals. Variability in acoustic performance due to uncertainties in the input is quantified with probability density functions (PDFs) of the output responses. The PDFs give information about the spread, central tendency and most probable values of tuning frequency and transmission loss, allowing for a probabilistic interpretation of the behaviour of the system.

Experimental Study

The experimental study was intended to qualitatively validate the resonance behaviour and frequency-response trends predicted by the simulations rather than directly quantify probabilistic reliability values. After optimization of the designs of the Helmholtz resonators (HR) in simulation in ANSYS Workbench, six different configurations were chosen to be fabricated using additive manufacturing methods (Fig. 10a). The resonators were made with the best neck and cavity sizes to match the intended acoustic performance. An accurate noise measuring system was implemented to confirm experimentally the performance of the resonators that were manufactured (Fig. 10b). To record acoustic data, a National Instruments Data Acquisition (NI DAQ) system was used that has a high sampling rate of 20,000 samples per second to record the acoustic signals accurately. A microphone was used, whose sensitivity is 55 mV/Pa to detect pressure changes produced by the resonators.

Results and discussion

A finite element simulation was carried out to determine the dimensions of the Helmholtz resonators to achieve the desired resonance frequency of 300 Hz. The first stage in this study involved a finite element simulation for the determination of the dimensions of the Helmholtz resonators to achieve the desired resonance frequency of 300 Hz. These simulations are then used to provide a preliminary understanding of the resonance behaviour, using the ANSYS Workbench harmonic acoustics template under more realistic conditions. To enhance the accuracy of tuning, a response surface optimization with different key parameters, neck length and cavity length were performed under different iterations. The process provided some design points and finally came up with optimised dimensions which were closer to the desired tuning frequency. Figure 4 is a plot of frequency vs. transmission loss, which demonstrates that the maximum transmission loss occurs near the target frequency of 300 Hz and the optimization based on simulation is also important to optimise design to have certain acoustic properties, leading to more predictable and repeatable performance of tuning across all the tuned resonators. Table 1 gives the optimum HR configuration dimensions, and the results indicate that although analytical calculations can provide a useful initial estimate in resonator tuning can be obtained, simulation-based optimization is essential. Response surface method integration enabled to achieve a finer tune of the response to the target frequency and the sensitivity of the acoustic behaviour of HRs to the geometrical parameters. Optimal dimensions obtained with the help of finite element models are taken as the mean values based on which the Gaussian distribution is developed to examine the reliability of the HR to obtain maximum transmission loss in case of unpredictable geometric and duct parameters and air properties.

After determining optimised dimensions of each Helmholtz resonator set up, a reliability simulation was carried out under the Design for Six Sigma (DFSS) model to determine how robust the designs are. In this analysis, the standard deviations of the parameters which were taken as critical inputs were introduced as: neck length, cavity length, neck diameter, duct diameter, duct length, absorption surface, air density, and speed of sound with standard deviation set to 0.5, 3.0, 0.007 and 2.5 respectively. This was aimed at analysing the sensitivity of the output responses to changes in these inputs. The Monte Carlo reliability simulation provided probability density plot of each parameter and output graphs of probability density of frequency response and transmission loss as illustrated in Figs. 8 and 9. Maximum transmission loss values of the cylinder-sphere HR and cylinder-cuboid HR are changed respectively to 278 Hz and 312 Hz, respectively, at the target tuning frequency of 300 Hz and 312 Hz.

These results were very informative concerning the effect of small changes of parametric on the sound performance of the resonators. The error between the optimized FEM results and the classical analytical predictions is indicative of the limitation of geometry insensitive formulations. The results for the six configurations show that the shape-dependent effects are stronger within an uncertain environment. The tuning frequency even in cases where the input variables vary significantly will change significantly. It emphasises the need to control important environmental and production variables to guarantee stable and dependable resonator operation, especially in those processes that need accuracy. All six configurations of the Helmholtz resonator were 3D-printed to experimentally evaluate the resonance behaviour and validate the overall frequency-response trends predicted by the simulation.The resonators were 3D printed using the preferred acoustically positive material of PLA, which provides good resistance, thermal stability and fabrication properties. To remove the internal supports, one part of each resonator was isolated, the cavity, and the other part, the neck and base plate. The components were 50% infilled, and 3 mm panels were used as a design to achieve structural integrity and still maintain acoustic performance. The resonators were then assembled and tested experimentally using the high-resolution data acquisition system as described in Sect. 4. The time domain data obtained were then fed to the Signal analyzer tool of MATLAB and power spectrum plots were generated. The power spectrums of the resonant peaks of each of the configurations are plotted in Fig. 12. The resulting peak frequencies were found to be in good agreement with the target frequencies and reflected the overall trend of frequency shift predicted by the simulations. Some deviations are seen, however, because of experimental uncertainties, manufacturing tolerances and modelling assumptions. It is worth mentioning that probabilistic reliability metrics are derived from a simulation-based analysis while the experimental observations are used to test the general trend of acoustic behaviours. Nevertheless, higher transmission loss is not maximum at the desired tuning frequency of 300 Hz in all six HR configurations as indicated in Fig. 13. The consistent amplitude response of the cylinder-cylinder HR across neighbouring frequencies indicated comparatively stable acoustic behaviour and lower sensitivity to frequency variations. Equally, the cuboid-cylinder HR also exhibited comparatively stable acoustic response behaviour with only marginal deviations between neighbouring frequencies. On the other hand, the amplitude drop at the surrounding frequencies in the cylinder-cuboid HR was steeper, which demonstrates that this HR was less tolerant to deviation and more sensitive. The cuboid-cuboid HR exhibited comparatively equal amplitude values, which means that there was reliable attenuation near the target frequency with less sensitivity to frequency change. Cylinder-Sphere HR on the other hand showed higher amplitude change at close frequencies which implies reduced reliability and higher demands on precise tuning. Finally, the cuboid-sphere HR had moderately varied variation, but its main attenuation was close to the target range and was reasonably robust. Such trends indicate that the configurations that have more smooth amplitude transitions in the frequency range like the cylinder-cylinder and cuboid-cuboid provide more reliable performance and they are more appropriate with applications that have slight environmental or design deviations. The experimental findings qualitatively demonstrated the sensitivity of the acoustic response to variations in geometric and acoustic parameters. Contrary to variations in acoustic density and sound speed, which produced more general variations in the acoustic profile, neck geometry and cavity volume variations are known to have a strong effect on the resonance frequency and transmission loss properties. This probabilistic reliability analysis outlines strong design choices, as well as assist in ranking the parameters that need a stronger approach in the manufacturing process. In the end, the application of DFSS allowed the adoption of proactive design optimisation approach where variability is anticipated and its consequences are alleviated to provide more trustworthy acoustic performance in practise with Helmholtz resonators.

Conclusion and outlook

The experiment in this work aimed at assessing the reliability of various HR geometry at varying operational conditions with an application of Six Sigma-based probabilistic design approach combined with ANSYS. The probabilistic methodology was good in reflecting the effects of input uncertainties on the performance measures. The outcome of the simulation showed that the cylinder-sphere HR and the cylinder-cuboid HR were 96.7% and 93.34% reliable, respectively, in achieving a tuning frequency of 300 Hz and the reliability of all other setups was between 93.34 and 96.7%. The experimental results showed good agreement with the simulation trends, particularly in terms of frequency deviation and response behaviour. The experimental observations showed good agreement with the simulation trends in terms of frequency deviation and acoustic response behaviour. The reported reliability levels for all the HR configurations were obtained from the probabilistic DFSS-based simulation. It should be noted that these reliability values are derived from probabilistic DFSS-based simulations, while the experimental results are intended to support overall trends in frequency response and do not directly quantify reliability percentages. The uncertain geometric parameters and material properties greatly influenced HRs reliability for cylinder-sphere and cylinder-cuboid. Furthermore, the sensitivity analysis revealed the sensitivity of the neck and cavity geometries to the tuning frequency whereas the sensitivity of the absorption coefficient to the transmission loss. The Six Sigma analysis guaranteed a strong performance of the design, while minimising the variability. The results show the potential of the probabilistic simulation as a design tool in the early design stage and provide a guideline for further reliability-based optimisation of the noise control performance of the Helmholtz resonator with respect to sustainability. Further studies in the future could involve integrating FEM-generated simulations with machine learning models for improved prediction and efficiency.
