A Holographic prescription for generalized Schwinger-Keldysh contours
Martin Ammon, Jette Germerodt, Christoph Sieling, and Julio Virrueta

Abstract
We provide a holographic prescription to compute real-time thermal correlators with arbitrary operator ordering. In field theory, these correlation functions are captured by a multi-fold Schwinger-Keldysh time contour. We propose a holographic dual for these contours, which generalizes the gravitational Schwinger-Keldysh geometry previously advocated in the literature. Our geometry consists of multiple AdS-black holes glued together at the future and past horizons, with matching conditions determined by unitarity and the KMS condition. As a proof of concept, we solve for a probe scalar field in this geometry and compute bulk-bulk and bulk-boundary propagators, in terms of which we evaluate the 4-point functions at tree-level. We show that in perturbation theory, the lowest-order diagrams that contribute non-trivially to the out-of-time order four-point function are exchange diagrams which explore the full four-fold geometry. Furthermore, these diagrams reduce to a simple factorized expression. We propose a conjecture on the structure of higher order observables and provide a partial proof by studying a subset of the contributing diagrams.

Introduction

The dynamics of quantum fields at finite temperature play a central role across many areas of theoretical physics, from condensed matter to cosmology. In the context of the holographic correspondence, thermal states offer a controlled setting for probing the quantum aspects of black holes, especially in asymptotically AdS spacetimes
Maldacena:1997re
;
Witten:1998qj
.

While the physics of systems at thermal equilibrium can be captured by well-understood Euclidean field theory techniques, the study of dynamics out of equilibrium requires a Lorentzian setting. This is commonly captured by an analytic continuation, where the appropriate
i\epsilon
prescription accounts for time ordering, as shown by the reconstruction theorem
Osterwalder:1973dx
. The analytic continuation can be avoided by employing a purely Lorentzian setting, provided by the Schwinger-Keldysh (SK) formalism
Schwinger:1960qe
;
Keldysh:1964ud
. This can be further extended to account for open quantum systems
Feynman:1963fq
. For systems with a holographic dual description, understanding out-of-equilibrium dynamics provides an avenue to study the gravitational path integral for Lorentzian spacetimes.

Several prescriptions have been developed to compute real-time observables in holography
Herzog:2002pc
;
Son:2002sd
;
vanRees:2009rw
;
Skenderis:2008dg
;
deBoer:2018qqm
. Among them, a successful approach identifies the SK generating functional with a bulk path integral over a complexified AdS black hole geometry
Glorioso:2018mmw
. This method has been effective in the analysis of open systems
Jana:2020vyx
;
Loganayagam:2022zmq
and effective hydrodynamic actions
Ghosh:2020lel
;
He:2021jna
;
He:2022jnc
;
He:2022deg
.

Despite these successes, certain 4-point functions lie beyond the reach of the conventional Schwinger-Keldysh framework; these are denoted as out-of-time-order correlators (OTOCs). A particular example is the correlator

C(t)=-\expectationvalue{[W(t),V(0)]^2}_{\beta}\,,

(1)

which has a close relationship with the phenomenon of quantum chaos
Larkin:1959
, since its early-time exponential growth of
C(t)\sim\frac{1}{N^{2}}e^{\lambda t}
can be related to the classical notion of a Lyapunov exponent. This behavior is of key importance for black hole physics, which can be shown to be maximally chaotic systems
Shenker:2013pqa
;
Maldacena:2015waa
.
Existing holographic treatments of the OTOC typically rely on shockwave geometries or eikonal approximations
Shenker:2014cwa
;
Shenker:2013pqa
;
Roberts:2014isa
;
Caceres:2023zft
;
Kawamoto:2025hnw
;
Chua:2025vig
;
Roberts:2016wdl
;
Roberts:2014ifa
;
Blake:2016wvh
, which are tailored to specific kinematic regimes. A more general, contour-based prescription would provide a more comprehensive handle on real-time gravitational dynamics.

Capturing the full structure of an OTOC requires a generalization of the Schwinger-Keldysh contour. A 2-fold time contour suffices for standard time-ordered or nested correlators, but computing genuine OTOCs requires at least a 4-fold contour
Aleiner:2016eni
;
Haehl:2017qfl
. This naturally raises the question of how to construct holographic duals for such multi-fold contours, and how to evaluate real-time Witten diagrams in these more elaborate backgrounds.

In this work, we propose a holographic construction of a bulk geometry dual to general time-folded SK contours. We focus on the 4-fold case relevant for the OTOC, but our construction naturally generalizes to higher folds. Our solution consists of multiple Lorentzian AdS black hole segments glued along their horizons in a pattern dictated by the boundary contour. This complexified geometry provides a non-trivial saddle point of the real-time gravitational path integral, and it allows for the systematic computation of Witten diagrams. In particular, we show that contributions to the OTOC arising from contact interactions vanish identically, while exchange diagrams yield the non-trivial structure of the OTOC.

This paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we review real-time contour techniques and the constraints of unitarity and KMS symmetry. Section
3
revisits the gravitational Schwinger-Keldysh (grSK) geometry and generalizes it to 4-fold contours. In Sections
4
, we analyze the dynamics of probe scalars in this extended geometry. We show that contact diagrams vanish, while exchange diagrams yield non-trivial contributions to OTOCs. We conclude in Section
5
with a discussion of possible generalizations and future directions. In Appendix
A
, we discuss some further details of the construction as well as how to recover the well-known 2-fold dual. The details on the Witten diagrams calculations can be found in Appendix
B
, and we evaluate the four-point function for a specific example in Appendix
C
. Finally, in Appendix
D
we outline an alternative construction of the multi-fold geometry.

2
Real-time correlators and time-folds

In this section, we review several aspects of the Schwinger-Keldysh (SK) formalism in quantum field theory, as well as its generalizations for multiple time-folds. Our main goal is to set up the notation, as well as the main physical properties that will guide our holographic construction. For very comprehensive reviews on the subject we recommend
Chou:1984es
;
Haehl:2016pec
;
Haehl:2017eob
;
Haehl:2017qfl
;
Liu:2018crr
.

The SK approach differs from the common understanding of perturbative QFT, where we compute the S-matrix between an initial and a final state. These S-matrix elements are obtained from the time-ordered vacuum correlators, which in turn can be obtained from the Feynman path integral. In contrast, the SK formalism directly targets real-time correlation functions, without requiring asymptotic in/out states, making it particularly well-suited for studying non-equilibrium or strongly coupled systems. These correlators are computed using a path integral along a contour in a complexified time coordinate, with the necessary operator insertions along the contour (see fig.
1
).

Although the SK formalism is applicable to very general states (see, for instance,
Sivakumar:2024iqs
;
Botta-Cantcheff:2019apr
;
Martinez:2021uqo
), here we will focus mainly on a system prepared in a thermal state, with inverse temperature
\beta
, characterized by a Gibbs density matrix
\rho_{\beta}
. This state is prepared using a Euclidean path integral, with the Euclidean time compactified on a circle
S^{1}_{\beta}
, of circumference
\beta
.

Figure 1
:
The Schwinger-Keldysh contour for a thermal state
\rho_{\beta}
. We include a segment of Euclidean time evolution, preparing the state, and the endpoints are identified as
\tau\sim\tau+\beta
.

The correlators are captured by a generating functional of the form

Z_{\text{SK}}[J]=\expectationvalue{\TSK\, \exp\left(i\int_{\CSK}dt\, \Op J\right)}_{\beta}=\text{Tr}\left[\rho_{\beta}\,\mathcal{T}_{\text{SK}}\,\exp\left(i\int_{\mathcal{C}_{\text{SK}}}dt\,\mathcal{O}J\right)\right]\,,

(2)

where we suppressed integration over the spatial directions. The expectation value is evaluated following a contour ordering, as denoted by the symbol
\mathcal{T}_{\text{SK}}
.

An equivalent way to understand this path integral is to consider a double theory. Instead of a single collection of operators
\{\mathcal{O}^{(i)}(t)\}
where
t\in\mathcal{C}_{\text{SK}}
, we take two sets of operators
\left\{\mathcal{O}^{(i)}_{\text{R}}(t),\mathcal{O}^{(i)}_{\text{L}}(t)\right\}
; now
t
is a real variable, but the operators are defined only in the forward or backward segments of
\mathcal{C}_{\text{SK}}
. The corresponding generating functional is

Z_{\text{SK}}[J_{R},J_{L}]=\expectationvalue{\TSK\, \exp\left(i\int dt\, \OpR\,\JR- i\int dt\, \OpL\, \JL\right)}_{\beta}\,,

(3)

where the sign accounts for the orientation of the SK contour
1
1

1

We do not consider operator insertions along the thermal cycle, which correspond to correlators with respect to some excited state
Botta-Cantcheff:2019apr
;
Christodoulou:2016nej
;
Martinez:2021uqo
.
.

Using the Schwinger-Keldysh formalism, we can compute all correlators of the form

\displaystyle\expectationvalue{ \mathcal{T}\, [\Op(x_1) \dots\Op(x_K)]\, \overline{\mathcal{T}}\,[\Op(x_{K+1})\dots\Op(x_N)]}\,,

(4)

where now
\mathcal{T}
acts on operators in the first branch of the contour and implements time ordering, while
\overline{\mathcal{T}}
acts on the second branch and enforces anti-time ordering.

This is already a rich class of correlators but, as outlined in the introduction, it does not capture all possible cases. For example, the correlator
\expectationvalue{ \Op(t_4)\Op(t_2)\Op(t_3)\Op(t_1)}
, with
t_{1}<t_{2}<t_{3}<t_{4}
, cannot be computed using the traditional Schwinger-Keldysh contour. This is an example of an out-of-time-order correlator.

It is clear that, to capture the OTOCs within a generating functional, we must generalize the SK contour to include multiple time n-folds (see fig.
2
for the case
n=4
) or we must consider more than two copies of the operator spectrum.

Figure 2
:
4-
fold contour for the thermal state.

The corresponding generating functional is

Z_{\text{SK}_{n}}[J_{\text{R}}^{(i)},J_{\text{L}}^{(i)}]=\expectationvalue{\exp\left[i\sum_{i=1}^{n/2}\int dt\left(\JR^{(i)}\OpR^{(i)} - \JL^{(i)}\OpL^{(i)}\right)\right]}_{\beta}\,,

(5)

which now captures also the OTOCs.

2.1
Unitarity and KMS constraints

The doubling of degrees of freedom in the SK contour leads to a large degree of redundancies in the formulation, which in turn capture the constraints imposed on the thermal correlators by the KMS condition and microscopic unitarity
Haehl:2016pec
;
Haehl:2016uah
.

From the point of view of the complex contour, the condition of microscopic unitarity can be seen as the possibility of collapsing the contour in the absence of operator insertions.
2
2

2

The evolution along the SK contour involves a unitary operator
U
and its inverse
U^{-1}
; in the absence of insertions, their product collapses to the identity,
UU^{-1}=\mathbb{I}
.
This contour collapsing can also be understood as the fact that we can represent the same single-copy correlator as two equivalent SK ones (see fig.
3
).

Figure 3
:
Two equivalent SK correlators, related by unitarity.

This redundancy motivates the introduction of a different basis for the operators
\{\mathcal{O}_{\text{R}},\mathcal{O}_{\text{L}}\}
, known as the average/difference basis:

\mathcal{O}_{\text{av.}}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\mathcal{O}_{\text{R}}+\mathcal{O}_{\text{L}}\right)\,,\qquad\mathcal{O}_{\text{d}}=\mathcal{O}_{\text{R}}-\mathcal{O}_{\text{L}}\,.

(6)

In this basis, the unitarity condition is expressed as

\expectationvalue{\TSK\, \Opd(t)\cdots}=0\,,

(7)

where the ellipsis stands for operator insertions at times
t_{i}<t
.

It is clear that this condition implies a weaker version

\expectationvalue{\TSK\,\prod_{i=1}^{k}\Opd(t_i)}=0\,,

(8)

for arbitrary times
t_{i}
. In our future analysis, we will mostly use this weaker version of the unitarity condition.

Moreover, thermal correlators satisfy the KMS condition
Kubo:1957mj
;
Martin:1959jp
, which results from the cyclic property of the trace and the specific form of the thermal density matrix. In the case of two-point functions, the KMS condition is

\expectationvalue{\Op_1(t)\Op_2(0)}_{\beta}=\expectationvalue{\Op_2(0)\Op_1{(t-i\beta)}}_{\beta}\,,

(9)

and this can be generalized for arbitrary
n
-point correlators
Haehl:2017eob
.

Just as in the case of the unitarity constraint, we can implement the KMS condition by introducing a new basis for the operators. This is known as the advanced/retarded basis
3
3

3

In the literature, the advanced/retarded operators are often referred to as
\mathcal{O}_{\text{F}}
and
\mathcal{O}_{\text{P}}
. Here we dispensed with this notation for the sake of clarity.
:

\mathcal{O}_{\text{d}}(t)=\mathcal{O}_{\text{R}}(t)-\mathcal{O}_{\text{L}}(t)\,,\qquad\mathcal{O}_{\text{KMS}}(t)=\mathcal{O}_{\text{R}}(t)-\mathcal{O}_{\text{L}}(t-i\beta)\,,

(10)

in terms of which, the KMS condition implies

\expectationvalue{\TSK\,\prod_{i=1}^{k}\OpKMS(t_i)}=0\,,

(11)

which also has a stronger version when only the earliest operator insertion is a KMS operator.

The KMS constraints are easier to implement in frequency space. To this end, we introduce the following shorthand notation:
x^{\mu}=(t,\vec{x})
and
k^{\mu}=(\omega,\vec{k})
, together with

\int_{k}=\int\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}\int\frac{d^{d-1}\vec{k}}{(2\pi)^{d-1}}\,,

(12)

and, for future applications, we also define the time-reversed momentum
\bar{k}^{\mu}=(-\omega,\vec{k})
.

The momentum-space correlators are then

\expectationvalue{\TSK\,\Op_1(x_1)\cdots\Op_n(x_n)}=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\int_{k_{i}}e^{ik_{i}\cdot x_{i}}\expectationvalue{\TSK\,\Op_1(k_1)\cdots\Op_n(k_n)}\,,

(13)

where the labels in the operators may refer to the SK species or a genuinely different kind of operators.

The weaker versions of both the unitarity and KMS conditions are easy to implement in momentum space:

\expectationvalue{\TSK\, \prod_{i=1}^{n}\Opd(k_i)}_{\beta}=0\,,\qquad\expectationvalue{\TSK\, \prod_{i=1}^{n}\OpKMS(k_i)}_{\beta}=0\,,

(14)

where

\mathcal{O}_{\text{d}}(k)=\mathcal{O}_{\text{R}}(k)-\mathcal{O}_{\text{L}}(k)\,,\qquad\mathcal{O}_{\text{KMS}}(k)=\mathcal{O}_{\text{R}}(k)-e^{-\beta\omega}\mathcal{O}_{\text{L}}(k)\,.

(15)

On the other hand, the stronger version of the constraints is not evident in momentum space since it is captured within the analytic structure of the correlators.

The unitarity and KMS conditions also constrain correlators in the multi-time-fold contours, and the collection of independent correlators has been classified in
Haehl:2017qfl
. Following
Chaudhuri:2018ymp
, we introduce a generalized advanced/retarded basis for the 4-fold geometry:

\begin{split}\mathcal{O}_{\text{KMS}}(\omega,\vec{k})&=\mathcal{O}_{\text{R}_{1}}(\omega,\vec{k})-e^{-\beta\omega}\mathcal{O}_{\text{L}_{2}}(\omega,\vec{k})\,,\\
\mathcal{O}_{\text{d}_{1}}(\omega,\vec{k})&=\mathcal{O}_{\text{R}_{1}}(\omega,\vec{k})-\mathcal{O}_{\text{L}_{1}}(\omega,\vec{k})\,,\\
\mathcal{O}_{\text{d}_{2}}(\omega,\vec{k})&=\mathcal{O}_{\text{L}_{1}}(\omega,\vec{k})-\mathcal{O}_{\text{R}_{2}}(\omega,\vec{k})\,,\\
\mathcal{O}_{\text{d}_{3}}(\omega,\vec{k})&=\mathcal{O}_{\text{R}_{2}}(\omega,\vec{k})-\mathcal{O}_{\text{L}_{2}}(\omega,\vec{k})\,,\end{split}

(16)

where we refer to fig.
2
for the definition of operators in the L/R basis. The last three of these combinations enforce the unitarity constraint, which can be understood as the possibility of displacing operators along the contour, leading to the vanishing of the corresponding correlators. Similarly, the first of these combinations enforces the KMS condition, which can be seen as moving an operator from the first to the last branch of the contour along the thermal circle.

The notion of the advanced/retarded basis can be similarly extended to an
n-
fold contour as

\begin{split}\mathcal{O}_{\text{KMS}}(\omega,\vec{k})&=\mathcal{O}_{\text{R}_{1}}(\omega,\vec{k})-e^{-\beta\omega}\mathcal{O}_{\text{L}_{n/2}}(\omega,\vec{k})\,,\\
\mathcal{O}_{\text{d}_{1}}(\omega,\vec{k})&=\mathcal{O}_{\text{R}_{1}}(\omega,\vec{k})-\mathcal{O}_{\text{L}_{1}}(\omega,\vec{k})\,,\\
\mathcal{O}_{\text{d}_{2}}(\omega,\vec{k})&=\mathcal{O}_{\text{L}_{1}}(\omega,\vec{k})-\mathcal{O}_{\text{R}_{2}}(\omega,\vec{k})\,,\\
&\;\;\vdots\\
\mathcal{O}_{\text{d}_{n-2}}(\omega,\vec{k})&=\mathcal{O}_{\text{L}_{n/2-1}}(\omega,\vec{k})-\mathcal{O}_{\text{R}_{n/2}}(\omega,\vec{k})\,,\\
\mathcal{O}_{\text{d}_{n-1}}(\omega,\vec{k})&=\mathcal{O}_{\text{R}_{n/2}}(\omega,\vec{k})-\mathcal{O}_{\text{L}_{n/2}}(\omega,\vec{k})\,.\end{split}

(17)

For the purpose of writing a generating functional of correlators, we also introduce the corresponding advanced/retarded basis for the sources. In the case of the 4-fold contour, this is

\begin{split}J_{\text{KMS}}(\omega,\vec{k})&=n_{\text{B}}(\omega)\left[-J_{\text{R}_{1}}(\omega,\vec{k})+J_{\text{L}_{1}}(\omega,\vec{k})-J_{\text{R}_{2}}(\omega,\vec{k})+J_{\text{L}_{2}}(\omega,\vec{k})\right]\,,\\
J_{\text{d}_{1}}(\omega,\vec{k})&=n_{\text{B}}(\omega)\left[-e^{\beta\omega}J_{\text{R}_{1}}(\omega,\vec{k})+J_{\text{L}_{1}}(\omega,\vec{k})-J_{\text{R}_{2}}(\omega,\vec{k})+J_{\text{L}_{2}}(\omega,\vec{k})\right]\,,\\
J_{\text{d}_{2}}(\omega,\vec{k})&=n_{\text{B}}(\omega)\left[e^{\beta\omega}\left(J_{\text{R}_{1}}(\omega,\vec{k})-J_{\text{L}_{1}}(\omega,\vec{k})\right)+J_{\text{R}_{2}}(\omega,\vec{k})-J_{\text{L}_{2}}(\omega,\vec{k})\right]\,,\\
J_{\text{d}_{3}}(\omega,\vec{k})&=n_{\text{B}}(\omega)\left[e^{\beta\omega}\left(-J_{\text{R}_{1}}(\omega,\vec{k})+J_{\text{L}_{1}}(\omega,\vec{k})-J_{\text{R}_{2}}(\omega,\vec{k})\right)+J_{\text{L}_{2}}(\omega,\vec{k})\right]\,,\\
\end{split}

(18)

where

n_{\text{B}}(\omega)=\frac{1}{e^{\beta\omega}-1}

(19)

is the usual Bose-Einstein distribution. This basis for the sources can be easily extended for the n-fold contour along the same lines as in (
17
).

In terms of these sources, we can write

\int_{k}\sum_{i=1}^{n/2}\left(J_{\text{R}_{i}}(-k)\mathcal{O}_{\text{R}_{i}}(k)-J_{\text{L}_{i}}(-k)\mathcal{O}_{\text{L}_{i}}(k)\right)=\int_{k}\left(J_{\text{KMS}}(-k)\mathcal{O}_{\text{KMS}}(k)+\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}(-1)^{i}J_{\text{d}_{i}}(-k)\mathcal{O}_{\text{d}_{i}}(k)\right)\,,

(20)

so that the generating functional is

Z_{\text{SK}_{n}}[J_{\text{KMS}},J_{\text{d}_{i}}]=\expectationvalue{\TSKI{n}\,\exp\, i\int_k \left(\JKMS(-k)\OpKMS(k) + \sum_{i=1}^{n-1}(-1)^{i}\JdI{i}(-k)\OpdI{i}(k)\right)}_{\beta}.

(21)

In the next section, we construct a gravitational dual for the generating functional of connected correlators
W_{\text{SK}_{n}}[J_{\text{KMS}},J_{\text{d}_{i}}]=\log Z_{\text{SK}_{n}}[J_{\text{KMS}},J_{\text{d}_{i}}]
, which we compute as a saddle-point of the bulk path integral.

3
Holographic time-fold contours

3.1
A review of the grSK geometry

As noticed in the introduction, the Schwinger-Keldysh contour has a well-known holographic dual given by the gravitational Schwinger-Keldysh geometry (grSK)
Glorioso:2018mmw
;
Jana:2020vyx
;
Loganayagam:2022zmq
. In this section, we will extend this construction to include the 4-fold time contour (fig.
2
) and argue how it can be further extended for arbitrary time-folds. To this end, we first review the construction of the original grSK geometry.

The main idea behind the construction is to consider a complexified geometry, which is a solution to Einstein equations, i.e., a saddle of the gravitational path integral, and whose asymptotic boundary is equal to
\mathcal{C}_{\text{SK}}\times\mathbb{R}^{d-1}
.

Drawing inspiration from the SK contour (fig.
1
), we can decompose the geometry into three segments. First, we have an Euclidean region:

ds^{2}=r^{2}f(r)d\tau^{2}+\frac{dr^{2}}{r^{2}f(r)}+r^{2}d\vec{x}^{2}_{d-1}\,,\qquad f(r)=1-\frac{r_{+}^{d}}{r^{d}}\,,

(22)

where
\tau\sim\tau+\beta
. This prepares the system in a thermal state, with inverse temperature

\beta=\frac{4\pi}{|f^{\prime}(r_{+})|}\,.

(23)

The other two segments are Lorentzian solutions and consist of two identical copies of the AdS-Schwarzschild black hole:

ds^{2}=-r^{2}f(r)dt^{2}+\frac{dr^{2}}{r^{2}f(r)}+r^{2}d\vec{x}^{2}_{d-1}\,,

(24)

where we consider only the domains of outer communication (
r\geq r_{+}
and
t\geq 0
). In order to distinguish between these two solutions, we use coordinates
(t_{\text{L}},r_{\text{L}})
and
(t_{\text{R}},r_{\text{R}})
, respectively.

The combined geometry is built by joining the Euclidean and Lorentzian segments at the time-reflection symmetric slice:
t_{\text{R}}=0+i\epsilon
and
t_{\text{L}}=0-i(\beta+\epsilon)
, and the black holes across their future horizon (see fig.
4
). This second joining condition accounts for the turning point of the SK contour, taken to be at
t\rightarrow\infty
.

t_{\text{R}}=t+i\epsilon
and

r=r_{+}-i\epsilon e^{i\theta}

It will be convenient to introduce ingoing coordinates:

so that the metric remains smooth when changing from one Lorentzian segment to the next, although the radial coordinate gains a constant contribution (the total manifold remains smooth since
r

We may introduce a mocked tortoise coordinate,
\zeta(r)
, of the form

\zeta(r)=\frac{2}{i\beta}\int^{r}\frac{dr^{\prime}}{r^{\prime 2}f(r^{\prime})}+\zeta_{0}\,.

(26)

In the present case, with just two Lorentzian segments, and taking the view of having a single complexified radial coordinate, we choose the normalization such that

\lim_{r\rightarrow\infty}\left(\zeta(r+i\epsilon)-\zeta(r-i\epsilon)\right)=1\,,

(27)

while the integration constant,
\zeta_{0}
, can be chosen such that

\lim_{r\rightarrow\infty}\zeta(r+i\epsilon)=0\,.

(28)

This allows us to cover the whole Lorentzian geometry with a single coordinate patch
(v,\zeta,\vec{x})
. For future reference, we note that the requirement of having only two Lorentzian segments is needed only in the definition of the analytic continuation of
\zeta(r)
from one segment to the next, while the definition (

) holds even in the case of multiple segments.

Once again, the joining across the horizons requires the introduction of coordinate patches covering any two segments of interest. To this end, it will be convenient to introduce the outgoing coordinate:

ds^{2}=-r^{2}f(r)du^{2}-2dudr+r^{2}d\vec{x}_{d-1}^{2}\,,\qquad du=dt-\frac{dr}{r^{2}f(r)}\,,

(29)

which we use to implement the matching at the past horizons.

The main idea now will be to extend the procedure we employed in the case of
\text{grSK}_{2}
locally for every pair of Loretzian segments we wish to match. The first Lorentzian segment is then described by coordinates
(v,r_{1},\vec{x})
, and similarly for the second Lorentzian segment but with a different radial coordinate
r_{2}
. Across their common future horizons we introduce a coordinate patch consisting of
(v,r_{+}-i\epsilon e^{i\theta},\vec{x})
, with
\theta\in[0,\pi]
, i.e., following the Hankel contour fig.
5
. For the next matching, we first change coordinates from ingoing to outgoing (this can be done at any region where the two patches overlap), and once again we follow a Hankel contour to introduce a coordinate patch which covers the region between the past horizons. This procedure is then performed once more, changing back into ingoing coordinates and introducing the necessary Hankel contours at the second pair of future horizons. The final result is the
\text{grSK}_{4}
geometry depicted in fig.
6
. This procedure can be naturally extended for an arbitrary number of time-folds.

A clear issue with this prescription is that we cannot introduce a single coordinate patch that covers the entirety of the Lorentzian manifold. The two-sheeted Riemann structure could be globally defined for
\text{grSK}_{2}
, but for
\text{grSK}_{n}
with
n>2
this is no longer possible. Hence the analytic structure of the geometry remains somewhat obscure due to the piece-wise nature of the construction. It may be possible that a single coordinate system with a complexified radial coordinate in a multi-sheeted Riemann surface exists, but while such a construction would be highly convenient, we shall not further explore this in the present work.

4
Probe scalars in 4-fold geometry

Even though we have now constructed the bulk geometry dual to the
n
-fold time contour, the discussion has remained somewhat formal. In order to be more concrete, it will be convenient to study the dynamics of fields in the
\text{grSK}_{n}
manifold. This will allow us to study the multi-sheeted structure of the geometry, which was previously obscured by the absence of a unified coordinate system.

For simplicity, we consider a minimally coupled massive scalar field, whose action takes the form

S[\varphi]=\int d^{d+1}x\sqrt{-g}\left[-\frac{1}{2}\left(\nabla_{A}\varphi\nabla^{A}\varphi+\Delta(\Delta-d)\varphi^{2}\right)+V(\varphi)\right]+S_{\text{ct}}\,,

(30)

where
S_{\text{ct}}
stands for the usual counterterms required by holographic renormalization to render the on-shell action finite. We impose Dirichlet boundary conditions for
\varphi
at the asymptotic boundary
5
5

5

As it is well-known, for
\Delta\in(\frac{d}{2}-1,\frac{d}{2})
, it is possible to impose Neumann conditions
Witten:2001ua
, in which case the scaling dimension of the dual operators is not
\Delta
but
d-\Delta
. Our construction works the same way in this case, but we must include additional boundary terms, as required by the variational principle. The same holds for the non-Markovian scalars introduced in
Ghosh:2020lel
.
.

The potential
V(\varphi)
is assumed to be polynomial in the fields and to be controlled by a coupling constant
\lambda\ll 1
. We will then study the boundary correlations functions in a perturbative expansion, which are computed in terms of Witten diagrams on the
\text{grSK}_{n}
geometry.

While this example will suffice to illustrate how the
\text{grSK}_{n}
geometry captures the generating functional of correlations for the
n
-fold time contour, it can be generalized to more complicated situations, such as having multiple interacting fields as well as non-minimally coupled scalars. In particular, the latter can be used to capture the dynamics of vector and tensor fields using the
designer scalar
formalism explored in
Ghosh:2020lel
;
He:2021jna
;
He:2022jnc
;
He:2022deg
.

4.1
Gaussian dynamics

While the traditional
\text{grSK}_{2}
geometry suffices for computing any two-point function, we still consider the Gaussian dynamics of the scalar field in
\text{grSK}_{n}
, as a preparation for the study of the Witten diagrams computing higher order correlators.

On any of the Lorentzian sections, we may write the equation of motion for the scalar field in the Fourier domain. In the ingoing coordinates, we perform an expansion

\varphi(v,r,\vec{x})=\int_{k}\,e^{-i\omega v+i\vec{k}\cdot\vec{x}}\,\phi(r,k)\,,

(31)

where we employ the shorthand notation introduced in (
12
). Using this decomposition, the equation of motion is

\frac{1}{r^{d-1}}\mathbb{D}_{+}\left(r^{d-1}\mathbb{D}_{+}\,\phi(r,k)\right)+\left(\omega^{2}-\left(\vec{k}^{2}+\Delta(\Delta-d)r^{2}\right)f(r)\right)\phi(r,k)=0\,,

(32)

where we introduced the differential operator:

\mathbb{D}_{\pm}=r^{2}f(r)\frac{d}{dr}\mp i\omega\,.

(33)

This way of writing the equation of motion makes the action of the time-reversal transformation,
v\rightarrow-v+i\beta\zeta
, manifest. In particular, we notice that

e^{\beta\omega\zeta}\mathbb{D}_{+}e^{-\beta\omega\zeta}=\mathbb{D}_{-}\,,

(34)

and from this property, it is easy to see that given a solution
\phi(r,k)
of (
32
), we can find its time-reversal conjugate as
\phi^{\text{rev.}}(r,k)=e^{-\beta\omega\zeta}\phi(r,\bar{k})
, which is again a solution of the equation of motion.

In writing these expressions, we employ the function
\zeta(r)
for convenience, as defined by the equation (
26
).
However, we emphasize that, unlike the case of
\text{grSK}_{2}
, it is not globally defined: its shift as one moves from one Lorentzian section to the next is not necessarily
\zeta\rightarrow\zeta-1
.

The behavior of the fields near the asymptotic boundary is

\phi(r\rightarrow\infty,k)=c_{1}\,r^{\Delta-d}\left(1+\cdots\right)+c_{2}\,r^{-\Delta}\left(1+\cdots\right)\,,

(35)

where, for simplicity, we assume that
2\Delta-d\notin\mathbb{Z}
in order to avoid logarithmic branches. The first term above corresponds to non-normalizable modes and hence
c_{1}
is fixed by the Dirichlet conditions:

\lim_{r\rightarrow\infty}r^{d-\Delta}\phi(r,k)=J(k)\,,

(36)

where
J(k)
is identified with the source of the dual field on the given segment of the time-fold contour.

Near the horizon, the fields admit an expansion of the form

\phi(r,k)=b_{1}(\omega,\vec{k})(1+\cdots)+b_{2}(\omega,\vec{k})(r-r_{+})^{\frac{i\omega\beta}{2\pi}}\left(1+\cdots\right)\,,

(37)

where the ellipsis stands for terms that vanish polynomially as
r\rightarrow r_{+}
. We see that the first of these terms is analytic at the horizon, while the second is not. As such, the first term has a trivial extension from one Lorentzian segment to the next, while the second term picks up a non-trivial monodromy under analytic continuation across the horizon, reflecting the multi-sheeted structure of the radial coordinate.

All these features may be captured by introducing an ingoing bulk-to-boundary propagator,
G_{\text{in}}(r,k)
, which is a solution of the equation of motion satisfying the condition:

\lim_{r\rightarrow\infty}r^{d-\Delta}G_{\text{in}}(r,k)=1\,,\quad\lim_{r\rightarrow r_{+}}G_{\text{in}}(r,k)=\text{regular}\,,

(38)

and a corresponding outgoing propagator

G_{\text{out}}(r,k)=e^{-\beta\omega\zeta}\,G_{\text{in}}(r,\bar{k})\,,

(39)

whose non-analytic behavior at the horizon is entirely captured by the function
\zeta(r)
. We recall that
\bar{k}^{\mu}=(-\omega,\vec{k})
.

The most general solution is then

\begin{split}\varphi(v,r,\vec{x})&=\int_{k}e^{-i\omega v+i\vec{k}\cdot\vec{x}}\left(a_{1}(\omega,\vec{k})\,G_{\text{in}}(r,k)+a_{2}(\omega,\vec{k})\,G_{\text{out}}(r,k)\right)\,,\end{split}

(40)

where the coefficients are constraint by the asymptotic boundary condition to be
a_{1}+a_{2}=J
.

This analysis can be performed in exactly the same way in outgoing coordinates, with

\varphi(u,r,\vec{x})=\int_{k}\,e^{-i\omega u+i\vec{k}\cdot\vec{x}}\,\tilde{\phi}(k,r)\,,

(41)

and the equation of motion

\frac{1}{r^{d-1}}\mathbb{D}_{-}\left(r^{d-1}\mathbb{D}_{-}\,\tilde{\phi}(k,r)\right)+\left(\omega^{2}-\left(\vec{k}^{2}+\Delta(\Delta-d)r^{2}\right)f(r)\right)\tilde{\phi}(k,r)=0\,.

(42)

By performing the change between ingoing and outgoing coordinates in (
40
), it is easy to see that we can write

\begin{split}\varphi(u,r,\vec{x})&=\int_{k}e^{-i\omega u+i\vec{k}\cdot\vec{x}}\left(a_{1}(\omega,\vec{k})\,G_{\text{out}}(r,\bar{k})+a_{2}(\omega,\vec{k})\,G_{\text{in}}(r,\bar{k})\right)\,.\end{split}

(43)

The coefficients
a_{1}
and
a_{2}
must be the same as in the ingoing case, since the two solution must match in a region where the ingoing and outgoing coordinates overlap. However, the analytic properties of the ingoing and outgoing bulk-to-boundary propagators are reversed, with the ingoing propagator being non-analytic at the past horizon while the outgoing propagator is fully regular.

In this way, we can construct the solution to the equation of motion on any of the Lorentzian sections, with corresponding asymptotic boundary conditions. Next, we consider how these solutions are related to each other by the matching conditions across the future and past horizons.

Let us recall how this is done in the simple situation of the
\text{grSK}_{2}
geometry. As noticed in the previous section, in this case we can define
\zeta(r)
such that is gains a shift
\zeta\rightarrow\zeta-1
as we move from one Lorentzian segment to the next, and, as we just discussed, this captures all the possible monodromies of the solution. If we then require the scalar field to be continuous across the horizon, we must equate the coefficients in the expansion so that

\begin{split}\phi_{1}(r,k)&=c_{1}\,G_{\text{in}}(r,k)+c_{2}\,e^{-\beta\omega\zeta}\,G_{\text{in}}(r,\bar{k})\,,\\
\phi_{2}(r,k)&=c_{1}\,G_{\text{in}}(r,k)+c_{2}\,e^{\beta\omega(1-\zeta)}\,G_{\text{in}}(r,\bar{k})\,,\end{split}

(44)

and the two integration constants are then determined by the asymptotic boundary conditions. The result is

\begin{split}\phi_{1}(r,k)&=\left((1+n_{\text{B}}(\omega))J_{\text{R}}-n_{\text{B}}(\omega)J_{\text{L}}\right)\,G_{\text{in}}(r,k)+n_{\text{B}}(\omega)(J_{\text{L}}-J_{\text{R}})\,e^{-\beta\omega\zeta}\,G_{\text{in}}(r,\bar{k})\,,\\
\phi_{2}(r,k)&=\left((1+n_{\text{B}}(\omega))J_{\text{R}}-n_{\text{B}}(\omega)J_{\text{L}}\right)\,G_{\text{in}}(r,k)+n_{\text{B}}(\omega)(J_{\text{L}}-J_{\text{R}})\,e^{\beta\omega(1-\zeta)}\,G_{\text{in}}(r,\bar{k})\,.\end{split}

(45)

Of course, we can write this solution in a more compact manner as

\begin{split}\phi(\zeta,k)&=\left((1+n_{\text{B}}(\omega))J_{\text{R}}-n_{\text{B}}(\omega)J_{\text{L}}\right)\,G_{\text{in}}(r,k)+n_{\text{B}}(\omega)(J_{\text{L}}-J_{\text{R}})\,e^{-\beta\omega\zeta}\,G_{\text{in}}(r,\bar{k})\,,\end{split}

(46)

where the field is defined along all of the Hankel contour, instead of in a piecewise fashion.
6
6

6

We use the convention where the solution, as written in (
46
), lives in the first branch of the Hankel contour (the R branch), and to get the second branch we do the shift
\zeta\rightarrow\zeta-1
. This is the reverse of the convention employed in
Jana:2020vyx
.

With this solution at hand, it is possible to evaluate the Gaussian contribution to the generating functional in terms of the renormalized on-shell action; this generating functional has been shown to satisfy the KMS and unitarity constraints
Glorioso:2018mmw
;
Jana:2020vyx
.

We now proceed to consider the case of a scalar in the
\text{grSK}_{n}
geometry, taking
n=4
as a working example. An immediate problem we encounter is that, at first glance, the system appears to be overdetermined. On any of the Lorentzian segments, the scalar field obeys a second order ordinary differential equation in the Fourier domain, and as such it seems entirely determined by a pair of boundary conditions, while we now would like to impose distinct conditions in four or more asymptotic boundaries. The answer to this puzzle lies on the analytic continuation of
\zeta(r)
across the multiple horizons.

As we have emphasized several times now, in the case of
\text{grSK}_{2}
, we were able to define
\zeta(r)
such that
\zeta\rightarrow\zeta-1
as we cross the future horizon. We will now relax this condition, and consider several possible monodromies for the non-analytic solution
G_{\text{out}}(r,k)
. We can write this in terms of
\zeta(r)
, with
\zeta_{(\alpha)}(r)\rightarrow\zeta_{(\alpha)}(r)+\alpha
, where
\alpha
is some constant. We remark that the integration constant in the definition of
\zeta_{(\alpha)}(r)
is taken so that
\zeta_{(\alpha)}(r)\rightarrow 0
in the asymptotic boundary.

The more general solution to the equation of motion is then a linear combination of the analytic solution
G_{\text{in}}(r,k)
and a collection of
G_{\text{out}}^{(\alpha)}(r,k)
. In practice, we will show that it is sufficient to consider two distinct analytic continuations:

\begin{split}\phi_{1}(r,k)&=a_{1}\,G_{\text{in}}(r,k)+a_{2}\,e^{-\beta\omega\zeta_{+}}\,G_{\text{in}}(r,\bar{k})+a_{3}\,e^{-\beta\omega\zeta_{-}}\,G_{\text{in}}(r,\bar{k})\,,\\
\phi_{2}(r,k)&=b_{1}\,G_{\text{in}}(r,k)+b_{2}\,G_{\text{in}}(r,k)+b_{3}\,e^{-\beta\omega\zeta_{+}}\,G_{\text{in}}(r,\bar{k})+b_{4}\,e^{-\beta\omega\zeta_{-}}\,G_{\text{in}}(r,\bar{k})\,,\\
\phi_{3}(r,k)&=c_{1}\,G_{\text{in}}(r,k)+c_{2}\,G_{\text{in}}(r,k)+c_{3}\,e^{-\beta\omega\zeta_{+}}\,G_{\text{in}}(r,\bar{k})+c_{4}\,e^{-\beta\omega\zeta_{-}}\,G_{\text{in}}(r,\bar{k})\,,\\
\phi_{4}(r,k)&=d_{1}\,G_{\text{in}}(r,k)+d_{2}\,e^{-\beta\omega\zeta_{+}}\,G_{\text{in}}(r,\bar{k})+d_{3}\,e^{-\beta\omega\zeta_{-}}\,G_{\text{in}}(r,\bar{k})\,,\\
\end{split}

(47)

where
\zeta_{\pm}
is defined by the condition

\zeta_{\pm}\rightarrow\zeta_{\pm}\pm 1\,,

(48)

across any of the horizons. This can be seen as an ambiguity on the choice of orientation of the Hankel contour. We remark that this choice is made to simplify the analysis below, but we could also work with more general monodromies, without changing the final result for the dynamics, as discussed in Appendix
A
.

In the expression above, it may seem unnecessary to separate the contributions from
G_{\text{in}}(r,k)
into two pieces on the solutions for the second and third Lorentzian segments. However, we must remember that we also need to impose matching conditions at the past horizon, where these functions become non-analytic. Concretely, we may use (
43
) to write the solutions in outgoing coordinates as

\begin{split}\tilde{\phi}_{2}(r,k)&=b_{1}\,e^{\beta\omega\zeta_{+}}\,G_{\text{in}}(r,k)+b_{2}\,e^{\beta\omega\zeta_{-}}\,G_{\text{in}}(r,k)+b_{3}\,G_{\text{in}}(r,\bar{k})+b_{4}\,G_{\text{in}}(r,\bar{k})\,,\\
\tilde{\phi}_{3}(r,k)&=c_{1}\,e^{\beta\omega\zeta_{+}}\,G_{\text{in}}(r,k)+c_{2}\,e^{\beta\omega\zeta_{-}}\,G_{\text{in}}(r,k)+c_{3}\,G_{\text{in}}(r,\bar{k})+c_{4}\,G_{\text{in}}(r,\bar{k})\,,\\
\end{split}

(49)

where the tilde refers to the Fourier mode in outgoing coordinates.

This construction can be extended for
n>4
by including more fields
\phi_{i}(r,k)
,
i=1,2,\cdots n
. However, we still require only two distinct analytic continuations of
\zeta(r)
. This follows from the fact that each horizon connects only two distinct Lorentzian segments. It is easy to count the degrees of freedom, boundary conditions, and matching conditions.

We now proceed to impose the matching conditions across the horizons, following fig.
6
. At the first future horizon, we match the first and second Lorentzian segments

a_{1}=b_{1}+b_{2}\,,\qquad a_{2}e^{-\beta\omega}=b_{3}\,,\qquad a_{3}e^{\beta\omega}=b_{4}\,.

(50)

Notice we match the two non-analytic contributions separately, as they are distinguished by their analytic continuation. This is so that the solution remains continuous in the coordinate patch covering the two Lorentzian segments.

The next matching, between the second and third segments, is imposed at the past horizon; we then need to use (
49
), leading to

b_{1}e^{\beta\omega}=c_{1}\,,\qquad b_{2}e^{-\beta\omega}=c_{2}\,,\qquad b_{3}+b_{4}=c_{3}+c_{4}\,.

(51)

Finally, the third and fourth segments are again matched across a future horizon, hence:

c_{1}+c_{2}=d_{1}\,,\qquad c_{3}e^{-\beta\omega}=d_{2}\,,\qquad c_{4}e^{\beta\omega}=d_{3}\,.

(52)

In addition to the matching at the future and past horizons, we also require the first and last solution to relate to each other at the point of time reflection symmetry, as required by their matching across the Euclidean region (see fig.
2
)
7
7

7

Here we assume no sources are present in the Euclidean boundary, and the solutions may differ by a normalizable contribution. A more general setting with Euclidean sources can be found in
Christodoulou:2016nej
.

\begin{split}\phi_{1}(v=i\frac{\beta}{2}\zeta,\vec{k})&=\int\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}e^{\frac{1}{2}\beta\omega\zeta}\left(a_{1}\,G_{\text{in}}(r,k)+(a_{2}+a_{3})\,e^{-\beta\omega\zeta}\,G_{\text{in}}(r,\bar{k})\right)\,,\\
\phi_{4}(v=i\frac{\beta}{2}\zeta-i\beta,\vec{k})&=\int\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}e^{\frac{1}{2}\beta\omega\zeta}e^{-\beta\omega}\left(d_{1}\,G_{\text{in}}(r,k)+(d_{2}+d_{3})\,e^{-\beta\omega\zeta}\,G_{\text{in}}(r,\bar{k})\right)\,,\end{split}

(53)

where we recall that, in ingoing coordinates, the points of time-reflection symmetry are located at
v=i\frac{\beta}{2}\zeta
and
v=i\frac{\beta}{2}\zeta-i\beta
.
In writing these expressions, we combined the two non-analytic solutions since the distinction between them is irrelevant for this matching, and notice that
\phi_{4}
is displaced by
-i\beta
along the thermal circle.

Since we have
\text{Im}(\zeta)>0
, the integral over frequency is performed by closing the contour in the upper half-plane. The function
G_{\text{in}}(r,k)
has no poles in the upper half-plane, and so only the terms proportional to
G_{\text{in}}(r,\bar{k})
lead to a non-trivial condition:

a_{2}+a_{3}=e^{-\beta\omega}(d_{2}+d_{3})\,.

(54)

In total, the matching conditions at the horizons and at the Euclidean region result in the following conditions:

\begin{split}a_{1}=b_{1}+b_{2}\,,\qquad a_{2}e^{-\beta\omega}&=b_{3}\,,\qquad a_{3}e^{\beta\omega}=b_{4}\,,\\
b_{1}e^{\beta\omega}=c_{1}\,,\qquad b_{2}e^{-\beta\omega}&=c_{2}\,,\qquad b_{3}+b_{4}=c_{3}+c_{4}\,,\\
c_{1}+c_{2}=d_{1}\,,\qquad c_{3}e^{-\beta\omega}&=d_{2}\,,\qquad c_{4}e^{\beta\omega}=d_{3}\,,\\
a_{2}+a_{3}&=e^{-\beta\omega}(d_{2}+d_{3})\,.\end{split}

(55)

Finally, we impose the asymptotic boundary conditions:

\lim_{r\rightarrow\infty}r^{\Delta-d}\phi_{i}(r,k)=J_{i}(k)\,,\qquad J_{i}=\{J_{\text{R}_{1}},J_{\text{L}_{1}},J_{\text{R}_{2}},J_{\text{L}_{2}}\}\,,

(56)

which leads to

\begin{split}J_{\text{R}_{1}}(k)&=a_{1}+a_{2}+a_{3}\,,\\
J_{\text{L}_{1}}(k)&=b_{1}+b_{2}+b_{3}+b_{4}\,,\\
J_{\text{R}_{2}}(k)&=c_{1}+c_{2}+c_{3}+c_{4}\,,\\
J_{\text{L}_{2}}(k)&=d_{1}+d_{2}+d_{3}\,.\end{split}

(57)

We can then solve the linear system given by (
55
) and (
57
). The result can be conveniently expressed as a matrix

\begin{pmatrix}\phi_{1}\\
\phi_{2}\\
\phi_{3}\\
\phi_{4}\end{pmatrix}=n_{\text{B}}(\omega)\begin{pmatrix}e^{\beta\omega}G_{\text{in}}-G_{\text{out}}&&-\left(G_{\text{in}}-G_{\text{out}}\right)&&G_{\text{in}}-G_{\text{out}}&&-\left(G_{\text{in}}-G_{\text{out}}\right)\\
e^{\beta\omega}\left(G_{\text{in}}-G_{\text{out}}\right)&&-G_{\text{in}}+e^{\beta\omega}G_{\text{out}}&&G_{\text{in}}-G_{\text{out}}&&-\left(G_{\text{in}}-G_{\text{out}}\right)\\

J_{\text{L}_{1}}\\
J_{\text{R}_{2}}\\
J_{\text{L}_{2}}\end{pmatrix}\,.

(58)

In writing these expressions, we have not differentiated between the possible analytic continuations of
\zeta(r)
. This makes the expressions shorter, but obscures their continuity property across the black hole horizons
8
8

8

In the language of coordinate patches, the solution (

) has been written in terms of ingoing patches away from the horizon crossings.
.

We see that the structure of the solution is clear. In this basis, the diagonal components correspond to non-normalizable modes, while the off-diagonal components are fully normalizable. Furthermore, if we write also the
\text{grSK}_{2}
solution as a matrix, we see that the solution above indeed collapses to the simpler
2
-fold case as we turn off specific sources (see equation (

)).

The different factors of
e^{\beta\omega}
in the solution (
58
) are the result of the continuity condition across the horizons and, in turn implement the KMS condition. This can be made manifest by changing the basis for the sources into the generalized advanced/retarded basis (
18
), in terms of which

\begin{pmatrix}\phi_{1}\\
\phi_{2}\\
\phi_{3}\\
\phi_{4}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}G_{\text{out}}&&-G_{\text{in}}&&0&&0\\
0&&-G_{\text{in}}&&-G_{\text{out}}&&0\\
0&&0&&-G_{\text{out}}&&-G_{\text{in}}\\
e^{\beta\omega}G_{\text{out}}&&0&&0&&-G_{\text{in}}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}J_{\text{KMS}}\\
J_{\text{d}_{1}}\\
J_{\text{d}_{2}}\\
J_{\text{d}_{3}}\end{pmatrix}\,.

(59)

As advertised earlier, we can now perform a simple counting argument to see that the generalization for arbitrary
n
holds. In this case, the most general solution will depend on
6
parameters from the first and last Lorentzian segments, and
4(n-2)
parameters for all the other sections, for a total of
2(2n-1)
undetermined coefficients. There are a total of
n-1
future and past horizons, and at each we have three conditions corresponding to the matching of the analytic and the two non-analytic solutions; in addition to this, we have
n
conditions in the asymptotic boundaries and one last constraint arising from the relation to the Euclidean segment. In total, we have
3(n-1)+n+1=2(2n-1)
independent linear equations, which exactly match the number of undetermined parameters. This counting argument confirms that our procedure generalizes naturally to
n>4
, and that in all cases it suffices to consider only two analytic branches of
\zeta(r)
. We notice that, in the case of
\text{grSK}_{2}
, we could also consider the two possible analytic continuations,
\zeta_{\pm}(r)
, but the matching to the Euclidean region reduces this to the simpler solution (
46
) (see Appendix
A
).

We can then evaluate the Gaussian on-shell action, which localizes at the boundary by virtue of the equation of motion. The general form of the action is then

S_{\text{on-shell}}^{(2)}=\lim_{r\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{4}(-1)^{i+1}\int_{k}r^{d-\Delta}\phi_{i}(r,-k)\pi_{i}(r,k)\,,

(60)

where
\pi_{i}(r,k)
is the renormalized canonical conjugate momentum in the radial direction, given by

\pi_{i}(r,k)=-\left(r^{\Delta-1}\mathbb{D}_{+}\phi_{i}(r,k)+\text{counterterms}\right)\,.

(61)

We then evaluate this action using the solution (
59
), the result is

S_{\text{on-shell}}^{(2)}=-\frac{1}{2}\int_{k}K^{\text{in}}(\omega,\vec{k})\left[J_{\text{KMS}}\left(J_{\text{d}_{1}}^{\dagger}-e^{\beta\omega}J_{\text{d}_{3}}^{\dagger}\right)+J_{\text{d}_{1}}^{\dagger}J_{\text{d}_{2}}-J_{\text{d}_{2}}J_{\text{d}_{3}}^{\dagger}\right]+\text{c.c.}\,,

(62)

where
J^{\dagger}(k)=J(-k)
and we define

K^{\text{in}}(\omega,\vec{k})=-\lim_{r\rightarrow\infty}\left(r^{\Delta-1}\mathbb{D}_{+}G_{\text{in}}(r,k)+\text{counterterms}\right)\,,

(63)

which corresponds to the retarded two-point function on the boundary.

The fact that the on-shell action has no diagonal terms, such as
J_{\text{KMS}}^{\dagger}J_{\text{KMS}}
or
J_{\text{d}_{i}}^{\dagger}J_{\text{d}_{i}}
implies via functional differentiation that
\expectationvalue{\OpKMS(-k)\OpKMS(k)}=\expectationvalue{\OpdI{i}(-k)\OpdI{i}(k)}=0
, in agreement with the weak forms of the unitarity and KMS conditions (see
Haehl:2017eob
;
Chaudhuri:2018ymp
).

As anticipated, the expression for the on-shell action contains no new dynamical information — it could be obtained entirely from the
\text{grSK}_{2}
geometry by a trivial extension of the sources. In order to truly obtain non-trivial results, we must consider higher order correlators.

4.2
Contact Witten diagrams

Now that we understand the Gaussian dynamics of a scalar field in the
\text{grSK}_{n}
geometry, we turn our attention to interactions, generated by polynomial potentials of the form
V(\varphi)=\lambda\varphi^{n}
.

We first consider the contact Witten diagrams. For the case of cubic interactions and the
\text{grSK}_{4}
geometry, these diagrams produce a contribution to the on-shell action of the form:

\begin{split}S^{(3)}_{\text{contact}}&=\lambda\int_{\text{grSK}_{4}}dr\sqrt{-g}\int_{k_{j}}\phi(r,k_{1})\phi(r,k_{2})\phi(r,k_{3})\,\delta^{(d)}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{3}k_{i}\right)\\
&=\lambda\sum_{a=1}^{4}(-1)^{a+1}\int_{r_{+}}^{\infty}dr\sqrt{-g}\int_{k_{j}}\phi_{i}(r,k_{1})\phi_{i}(r,k_{2})\phi_{i}(r,k_{3})\,\delta^{(d)}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{3}k_{i}\right)\,.\end{split}

(64)

In the first line, the integral over the radial coordinate formally runs over the entirety of the generalized gravitational Schwinger-Keldysh geometry. We can decompose the expression in the second line into simpler integrals over a single black hole geometry. Notice that we must be mindful of the orientation of the contour. The fields
\phi_{i}(r,k)
(
i=1,\cdots 4
) can be read directly from (
58
), which we can use to write the contribution to the cubic on-shell action in terms of the sources and the bulk-to-boundary propagators
G_{\text{in}}
and
G_{\text{out}}
.

In order to easily compute the different contributions, it will be convenient to introduce some additional notation. We notice that the solution (
58
) is written in terms of three basic linear combinations of the
G_{\text{in}}
and
G_{\text{out}}
solutions:

\begin{split}\mathfrak{g}_{1}(r,k)=e^{\beta\omega}G_{\text{in}}(r,k)-G_{\text{out}}(r,k)\,,\quad\mathfrak{g}_{2}&=G_{\text{in}}(r,k)-e^{\beta\omega}G_{\text{out}}(r,k)\,,\\
\mathfrak{g}_{3}(r,k)=G_{\text{in}}(r,k)&-G_{\text{out}}(r,k)\,,\end{split}

(65)

where the first two are non-normalizable, while the last is fully normalizable.

We then define the left/right bulk-to-boundary propagator as diagonal matrices whose entries correspond to sections of the solution supported by the corresponding sources

\begin{split}\mathbb{G}^{\text{R}_{1}}(r,k)&=n_{\text{B}}(\omega)\,\text{diag}\left(\mathfrak{g}_{1}(r,k)\,,e^{\beta\omega}\mathfrak{g}_{3}(r,k)\,,e^{\beta\omega}\mathfrak{g}_{3}(r,k)\,,e^{\beta\omega}\mathfrak{g}_{3}(r,k)\right)\,,\\
\mathbb{G}^{\text{L}_{1}}(r,k)&=-n_{\text{B}}(\omega)\,\text{diag}\left(\mathfrak{g}_{3}(r,k)\,,\mathfrak{g}_{2}(r,k)\,,e^{\beta\omega}\mathfrak{g}_{3}(r,k)\,,e^{\beta\omega}\mathfrak{g}_{3}(r,k)\right)\,,\\
\mathbb{G}^{\text{R}_{2}}(r,k)&=n_{\text{B}}(\omega)\,\text{diag}\left(\mathfrak{g}_{3}(r,k)\,,\mathfrak{g}_{3}(r,k)\,,\mathfrak{g}_{1}(r,k)\,,e^{\beta\omega}\mathfrak{g}_{3}(r,k)\right)\,,\\
\mathbb{G}^{\text{L}_{2}}(r,k)&=-n_{\text{B}}(\omega)\,\text{diag}\left(\mathfrak{g}_{3}(r,k)\,,\mathfrak{g}_{3}(r,k)\,,\mathfrak{g}_{3}(r,k)\,,\mathfrak{g}_{2}(r,k)\right)\,,\end{split}

(66)

and corresponding generalization for
\text{grSK}_{n}
. Of course, the diagonal entries are just the columns of the matrix in (
58
), the rows corresponding to the expression of the propagator on the
i
-th Lorentzian segment. The convenience of this matrix notation will become more apparent once we discuss exchange diagrams in the following sub-section.

In terms of these propagators, we can read off the contributions to (
64

\begin{split}S^{(3)}_{\text{contact}}&\supset\lambda\int_{k_{j}}\int_{r_{+}}^{\infty}dr\sqrt{-g}\,\Tr\left(\hat{S}\,\mathbb{G}^{\text{R}_{1}}(r,k_{1})\mathbb{G}^{\text{R}_{1}}(r,k_{2})\mathbb{G}^{\text{R}_{1}}(r,k_{3})\right)J_{\text{R}_{1}}(k_{1})J_{\text{R}_{1}}(k_{2})J_{\text{R}_{1}}(k_{3})\,,\\
\end{split}

(67)

where we omitted the momentum-preserving delta-function, and defined

\hat{S}=\text{diag}\left(1,-1,1,-1\right)

(68)

in order to account for the orientation of the contour.

We can also introduce corresponding bulk-to-boundary propagators in the generalized F/P basis, by simply reading the columns of the matrix in (

\begin{split}\mathbb{G}^{\text{KMS}}(r,k)&=\text{diag}\left(G_{\text{out}}(r,k)\,,0\,,0\,,e^{\beta\omega}G_{\text{out}}(r,k)\right)\,,\\
\mathbb{G}^{\text{d}_{1}}(r,k)&=-\text{diag}\left(G_{\text{in}}(r,k)\,,G_{\text{in}}(r,k)\,,0\,,0\right)\,,\\
\mathbb{G}^{\text{d}_{2}}(r,k)&=-\text{diag}\left(0\,,G_{\text{out}}(r,k)\,,G_{\text{out}}(r,k)\,,0\right)\,,\\
\mathbb{G}^{\text{d}_{3}}(r,k)&=-\text{diag}\left(0\,,0\,,G_{\text{in}}(r,k)\,,G_{\text{in}}(r,k)\right)\,.\end{split}

(69)

On this basis, the generalization for arbitrary

\begin{split}\mathbb{G}^{\text{KMS}}(r,k)&=\text{diag}\left(G_{\text{out}}(r,k)\,,0\,,\cdots\,,0\,,e^{\beta\omega}G_{\text{out}}(r,k)\right)\,,\\
\mathbb{G}^{\text{d}_{1}}(r,k)&=-\text{diag}\left(G_{\text{in}}(r,k)\,,G_{\text{in}}(r,k)\,,0\,,\cdots\,,0\right)\,,\\
\mathbb{G}^{\text{d}_{2}}(r,k)&=-\text{diag}\left(0\,,G_{\text{out}}(r,k)\,,G_{\text{out}}(r,k)\,,0\,,\cdots\,,0\right)\,,\\
\mathbb{G}^{\text{d}_{3}}(r,k)&=-\text{diag}\left(0\,,0\,,G_{\text{in}}(r,k)\,,G_{\text{in}}(r,k)\,,0\,,\cdots\,,0\right)\,,\\
&\vdots\\
\mathbb{G}^{\text{d}_{n-1}}(r,k)&=-\text{diag}\left(0\,,0\,,\cdots\,,0\,,G_{\text{in}}(r,k)\,,G_{\text{in}}(r,k)\right)\,.\end{split}

(70)

Using the propagators (

\begin{split}S^{(3)}_{\text{contact}}&\supset\lambda\int_{k_{j}}\int_{r_{+}}^{\infty}dr\sqrt{-g}\,\Tr\left(\hat{S}\,\mathbb{G}^{\text{d}_{1}}_{a}(r,k_{1})\mathbb{G}^{\text{d}_{1}}_{a}(r,k_{2})\mathbb{G}^{\text{d}_{1}}_{a}(r,k_{3})\right)J_{\text{d}_{1}}(k_{1})J_{\text{d}_{1}}(k_{2})J_{\text{d}_{1}}(k_{3})\,,\\
&=\lambda\int_{k_{j}}\int_{r_{+}}^{\infty}dr\sqrt{-g}\left[-G_{\text{in}}(k_{1})G_{\text{in}}(k_{2})G_{\text{in}}(k_{3})+G_{\text{in}}(k_{1})G_{\text{in}}(k_{2})G_{\text{in}}(k_{3})\right]J_{\text{d}_{1}}(k_{1})J_{\text{d}_{1}}(k_{2})J_{\text{d}_{1}}(k_{3})\\
&=0\,.\end{split}

(71)

A similar cancellation occurs for all diagonal source structures, such as
J_{\text{KMS}}^{3}
and
J_{\text{d}_{i}}^{3}

69

As with the Gaussian action, the vanishing of diagonal contact contributions reflects the constraints imposed by unitarity and KMS symmetry. This serves as a non-trivial consistency check of the holographic prescription. Notice this calculation is immediately generalized for the
\text{grSK}_{n}

It is now easy to see then, that the only non-zero contributions to the cubic on-shell action are off-diagonal in the sources, such as
J_{\text{KMS}}J_{\text{d}_{1}}J_{\text{d}_{1}}
, and furthermore these contributions are written in terms of just two types of integrals:

\begin{split}\int dr\sqrt{-g}\,e^{-\beta\omega_{3}\zeta}\,G_{\text{in}}(r,k_{1})\,G_{\text{in}}(r,k_{2})\,G_{\text{in}}(r,\bar{k}_{3})\,,\\
\int dr\sqrt{-g}\,e^{\beta\omega_{1}\zeta}\,G_{\text{in}}(r,k_{1})\,G_{\text{in}}(r,\bar{k}_{2})\,G_{\text{in}}(r,\bar{k}_{3})\,.\end{split}

(72)

Similarly, all non-vanishing contributions contact diagram contributions to n-point correlators are of the form:

\begin{split}&\int dr\sqrt{-g}\,e^{-\beta\omega_{n}\zeta}\,G_{\text{in}}(r,k_{1})\,G_{\text{in}}(r,k_{2})\,\cdots\,G_{\text{in}}(k_{n-1},r)\,G_{\text{in}}(\bar{k}_{n},r)\,,\\
&\int dr\sqrt{-g}\,e^{-\beta(\omega_{n}+\omega_{n-1})\zeta}\,G_{\text{in}}(r,k_{1})\,G_{\text{in}}(r,k_{2})\,\cdots\,G_{\text{in}}(\bar{k}_{n-1},r)\,G_{\text{in}}(\bar{k}_{n},r)\,,\\
&\qquad\hskip 142.26378pt\vdots\\
&\int dr\sqrt{-g}\,e^{\beta\omega_{1}\zeta}\,G_{\text{in}}(r,k_{1})\,G_{\text{in}}(r,\bar{k}_{2})\,\cdots\,G_{\text{in}}(\bar{k}_{n},r)\,.\end{split}

(73)

This structure for the contact diagrams follows from essentially the same argument employed in
Jana:2020vyx
;
Loganayagam:2022zmq
for the standard
\text{grSK}_{2}
geometry.

Furthermore, we notice that, as we increase the order of the correlator, many more diagrams vanish than we would expect from the unitarity and KMS conditions. Concretely, diagrams of not
neighboring
sources, such as
J_{\text{KMS}}J_{\text{d}_{1}}J_{\text{d}_{2}}
always vanish. These two features reflect the fact that contact diagrams cannot capture all of the higher order thermal correlators. As we shall argue shortly, contact Witten diagrams provide no additional contributions to the on-shell action that could not be obtained by adequate generalization of the sources, just as we observed for the Gaussian action.

Unlike vacuum correlators, thermal correlators are constrained by the KMS condition, which ensures that all thermal three-point functions can be captured by the standard Schwinger-Keldysh contour. Additional time-folds — and the full structure of
\text{grSK}_{n}
— are only needed for four-point functions and higher. The collection of independent correlators for a given
n-
fold can be captured using the language of spectral functions, as defined in
Chaudhuri:2018ymp
.

For two- and three-point functions, all the thermal correlators can be written in terms of a single spectral function:
\expectationvalue{[\Op_1,\Op_2]}_{\beta}
, and
\expectationvalue{[[\Op_1,\Op_2],\Op_3]}_{\beta}
, respectively. However, for the case of four-point functions we have not one but two spectral functions: the nested commutator,
\expectationvalue{[[[\Op_1,\Op_2],\Op_3],\Op_4]}_{\beta}
, and the commutator-squared
\expectationvalue{[\Op_1,\Op_2][\Op_3,\Op_4]}_{\beta}
.

Guided by this structure, let us compute the contact diagram contributions to
\expectationvalue{[\Op(t),\Op(0)]^2}_{\beta}
, which contains contribution from genuine OTOCs. In general, this correlator can be computed using the 4-fold contour as a specific permutation of the basic contour-ordered correlator.

\begin{split}\braket{\left[\mathcal{O}(t,\vec{x}),\mathcal{O}(0)\right]^{2}}_{\beta}&=\int_{k_{i}}e^{-i(\omega_{1}+\omega_{3})t+i(\vec{k}_{1}+\vec{k}_{3})\cdot\vec{x}}\left[\braket{\mathcal{T}_{\text{SK}_{4}}\,\mathcal{O}_{\text{R}_{1}}(k_{1})\mathcal{O}_{\text{L}_{1}}(k_{2})\mathcal{O}_{\text{R}_{2}}(k_{3})\mathcal{O}_{\text{L}_{2}}(k_{4})}_{\beta}\right.\\
&\quad\left.-\braket{\mathcal{T}_{\text{SK}_{4}}\,\mathcal{O}_{\text{R}_{1}}(k_{1})\mathcal{O}_{\text{L}_{1}}(k_{2})\mathcal{O}_{\text{R}_{2}}(k_{4})\mathcal{O}_{\text{L}_{2}}(k_{3})}_{\beta}\right.\\
&\quad\left.-\braket{\mathcal{T}_{\text{SK}_{4}}\,\mathcal{O}_{\text{R}_{1}}(k_{2})\mathcal{O}_{\text{L}_{1}}(k_{1})\mathcal{O}_{\text{R}_{2}}(k_{3})\mathcal{O}_{\text{L}_{2}}(k_{4})}_{\beta}\right.\\
&\quad\left.+\braket{\mathcal{T}_{\text{SK}_{4}}\,\mathcal{O}_{\text{R}_{1}}(k_{2})\mathcal{O}_{\text{L}_{1}}(k_{1})\mathcal{O}_{\text{R}_{2}}(k_{4})\mathcal{O}_{\text{L}_{2}}(k_{3})}_{\beta}\right]\,,\end{split}

(74)

where we find it more convenient to employ the left/right basis. With our choice of frequency and momenta, the first and last contributions correspond to OTOCs, while the second and third are (anti-) time-ordered contributions.

For a
\frac{\lambda}{4!}\varphi^{4}
interaction, all four contributions are obtained from the same diagram, just with different permutations of the momentum labels. As argued earlier, all the non-trivial contributions to the correlator can be written in terms of the following radial integrals

\begin{split}\mathfrak{F}_{1}(k_{1};k_{2},k_{3},k_{4})&=\lambda\int dr\,\sqrt{-g}e^{-\beta\omega_{1}\zeta}G_{\text{in}}(r,\bar{k}_{1})G_{\text{in}}(r,k_{2})G_{\text{in}}(r,k_{3})G_{\text{in}}(r,k_{4})\,,\\
\mathfrak{F}_{2}(k_{1},k_{2};k_{3},k_{4})&=\lambda\int dr\,\sqrt{-g}e^{-\beta(\omega_{1}+\omega_{2})}G_{\text{in}}(r,\bar{k}_{1})G_{\text{in}}(r,\bar{k}_{2})G_{\text{in}}(r,k_{3})G_{\text{in}}(r,k_{4})\,,\\
\mathfrak{F}_{3}(k_{1},k_{2},k_{3};k_{4})&=\lambda\int dr\,\sqrt{-g}e^{\beta\omega_{4}}G_{\text{in}}(r,\bar{k}_{1})G_{\text{in}}(r,\bar{k}_{2})G_{\text{in}}(r,\bar{k}_{3})G_{\text{in}}(r,k_{4})\,.\\
\end{split}

(75)

These functions have clear properties under permutations of the momentum labels, which we emphasize by the use of a semi-colon in the arguments. This kind of integrals have been studied for the case of the BTZ black hole in
Loganayagam:2022zmq
or for higher dimensional black holes in a gradient expansion
Loganayagam:2022teq
;
Rangamani:2023mok

The contact contributions to the 4-point correlator are then

\begin{split}\braket{\mathcal{T}_{\text{SK}_{4}}\,\mathcal{O}(k_{1})\mathcal{O}(k_{2})\mathcal{O}(k_{3})\mathcal{O}(k_{4})}_{\beta}&=\left(e^{\beta\omega_{1}}-1\right)\left(\mathfrak{F}_{1}(k_{1};k_{2},k_{3},k_{4})-\mathfrak{F}_{3}(k_{2},k_{3},k_{4};k_{1})\right)\\
&\hskip 9.24994pt+e^{\beta\omega_{1}}\left(e^{\beta\omega_{2}}-1\right)\left(\mathfrak{F}_{1}(k_{2};k_{1},k_{3},k_{4})-\mathfrak{F}_{3}(k_{1},k_{3},k_{4};k_{2})\right)\\
&\hskip 9.24994pt+e^{\beta(\omega_{1}+\omega_{2})}\left(e^{\beta\omega_{3}}-1\right)\left(\mathfrak{F}_{1}(k_{3};k_{1},k_{2},k_{4})-\mathfrak{F}_{3}(k_{1},k_{2},k_{4};k_{3})\right)\\
&\hskip 9.24994pt+\left(1-e^{-\beta\omega_{4}}\right)\left(\mathfrak{F}_{1}(k_{4};k_{1},k_{2},k_{3})-\mathfrak{F}_{3}(k_{1},k_{2},k_{3};k_{4})\right)\\
&\hskip 9.24994pt+\left(1-e^{\beta(\omega_{1}+\omega_{2})}\right)\left(\mathfrak{F}_{2}(k_{1},k_{2};k_{3},k_{4})-\mathfrak{F}_{2}(k_{3},k_{4};k_{1},k_{2})\right)\\
&\hskip 9.24994pt+\left(e^{-\beta\omega_{4}}-e^{\beta\omega_{1}}\right)\left(\mathfrak{F}_{2}(k_{1},k_{4};k_{2},k_{3})-\mathfrak{F}_{2}(k_{2},k_{3};k_{1},k_{4})\right)\\
&\hskip 9.24994pt+\left(1+e^{\beta\omega_{1}}(e^{\beta\omega_{2}}-1)-e^{-\beta\omega_{4}}\right)\left(\mathfrak{F}_{2}(k_{1},k_{3};k_{2},k_{4})-\mathfrak{F}_{2}(k_{2},k_{4};k_{1},k_{3})\right)\,,\end{split}

(76)

and from this expression we may compute
\braket{\left[\mathcal{O}(t,\vec{x}),\mathcal{O}(0)\right]^{2}}_{\beta}
according to (
74
).

While the full expression is somewhat intricate, the symmetry properties of the functions
\mathfrak{F}_{i}
under permutations lead to a cancellation of all terms

\braket{\left[\mathcal{O}(t,\vec{x}),\mathcal{O}(0)\right]^{2}}_{\beta}|_{\text{contact}}=0\,.

(77)

We conclude that contact diagrams yield no contribution to
\braket{\left[\mathcal{O}(t,\vec{x}),\mathcal{O}(0)\right]^{2}}_{\beta}
. They fail to probe the second spectral function and thus cannot capture the genuinely out-of-time-ordered nature of higher-point thermal correlators. More generally, all contact diagrams contributions to
\braket{\left[\mathcal{O}(t,\vec{x}),\mathcal{O}(0)\right]^{n}}_{\beta}
vanish due to the properties of the diagrams under permutations of the momentum labels.

10

10

The technical reasoning can be found in Appendix
B
, especially equation (

).

We can provide a heuristic geometric argument as to why contact diagrams do not capture all the information contained in the
\text{grSK}_{n}
geometry and, in particular, the OTOC, which is part of the commutator-squared. If we consider equation (
64
), we see that as we go from the first to the second line, the interactions resulting from a contact diagram occur piecewise on the geometric contour, i.e., we have no interactions relating two separate Lorentzian segments of the full geometry. Thus, contact diagrams reproduce the same contributions already captured by the simpler
\text{grSK}_{2}
geometry, as we saw directly in the calculation before; this is further supported by the observation before that diagrams of non-neighboring sources vanish. In order to obtain a non-trivial contribution to the commutator-squared, we must turn our attention to interactions that relate different Lorentzian segments, that is, we consider exchange diagrams.

It is important to remark that the statement of no contact diagram contributions to the OTOC is constrained only to our proposed bulk geometry. This geometry is certainly an extrema of the gravitational action, and it is consistent with the constraints of unitarity and KMS, as we have showed before. However, we do not claim it to be the dominant saddle. It may be possible that there exist a different bulk solution where the contact diagrams do contribute to the OTOC.

4.3
The bulk-to-bulk propagator and exchange Witten diagrams

The new ingredient we require to compute exchange Witten diagrams is the bulk-to-bulk propagator. For the case of
\text{grSK}_{2}
, the bulk-to-bulk propagator was computed in
Loganayagam:2022zmq
in the language of a single, complexified, radial coordinate. Here we will generalize this result for the
\text{grSK}_{n}
geometry in a piece-wise fashion using the same matrix notation implemented to describe the bulk-to-boundary propagators, (
66
) and (
69
).

On any of the Lorentzian segments, the bulk-to-bulk propagator for minimally coupled scalars is defined as a solution to the inhomogeneous equation

\left(-\Box+m^{2}\right)\mathcal{G}(X,X^{\prime})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{-g}}\delta^{(d+1)}(X-X^{\prime})\,,

(78)

where the differential operator acts on the unprimed coordinates. For future applications, we recall that the propagator is not symmetric under
X\leftrightarrow X^{\prime}
, due to the lack of translational symmetry in the radial direction. As in the case of the bulk-to-boundary propagator, we will solve this equation in the Fourier domain.

It is well-known that the inhomogeneous equation (
78
) can be solved in terms of functionally independent solutions to the homogeneous equation (
32
) using the so-called
Wronskian trick
. The bulk-to-bulk propagator is then given by

\mathcal{G}(r,r^{\prime};k)=\frac{1}{W(r^{\prime})}\left[\theta(r-r^{\prime})u_{1}(r)u_{2}(r^{\prime})+\theta(r^{\prime}-r)u_{1}(r^{\prime})u_{2}(r)\right]\,,

(79)

where
\theta(r)
is the Heaviside step function and
u_{1,2}(r)
are functionally independent solutions to the homogeneous equation, whose Wronskian is
W(r^{\prime})=\{u_{1},u_{2}\}(r^{\prime})
.

We can easily show by direct calculation that the Wronskian obeys the first order differential equation

r^{2}f(r)\frac{d\,W}{dr}-2i\omega W(r)=0\,,

(80)

with solution
W(r)=\mathcal{N}^{-1}(k)e^{-\beta\omega\zeta(r)}
11
11

11

The normalization constant
\mathcal{N}(k)
is chosen so that the solution of the differential equation agrees with the choice of homogeneous solutions, see
Loganayagam:2022zmq
.
. In this analysis, we only need the fact that
\zeta(r)
is a solution to the equation (
26
), and we do not need to specify its analytic continuation across the horizon; we shall indicate this later.

At this point, the solutions
u_{1,2}(r)
could be some linear combination of
G_{\text{in}}
and
G_{\text{out}}
. In order to determine the specific expressions, we require the bulk-to-bulk propagator to be fully normalizable in both
r
and
r^{\prime}
. Due to the step function, this implies the function
u_{1}(r)
must be proportional to the normalizable linear combination
\mathfrak{g}_{3}=G_{\text{in}}-G_{\text{out}}
, while
u_{2}(r)
remains unconstrained. In order to guarantee functional independence, we may take
u_{2}
to be a non-normalizable combination such as
\mathfrak{g}_{1}
or
\mathfrak{g}_{2}
, defined in (
65

Since the analysis above holds whenever both points lie within the same Lorentzian segment, we can write

\begin{split}\mathcal{G}_{aa}(r,r^{\prime},k)=\mathcal{N}(k)e^{\beta\omega\zeta(r^{\prime})}n_{\text{B}}(\omega)^{2}&\left[\theta(r-r^{\prime})\left(G_{\text{in}}(r,k)-G_{\text{out}}(r,k)\right)u_{a}(r^{\prime})\right.\\
&\left.+\theta(r^{\prime}-r)\left(G_{\text{in}}(r^{\prime},k)-G_{\text{out}}(r^{\prime},k)\right)u_{a}(r)\right]\,,\end{split}

(81)

with
a=1,2,\dots,n
. The functions
u_{a}(r)
are some choice of non-normalizable combination of
G_{\text{in}}
and
G_{\text{out}}
, while the prefactor of
n_{\text{B}}(\omega)^{2}
is introduced for convenience.

Before we determine the precise combinations
u_{a}(r)
, let us consider the connecting points of the bulk-to-bulk propagator, which lie in different Lorentzian segments. In this case, the right-hand side of (
78
) is always zero and, requiring the propagator to be fully normalizable, we have

\mathcal{G}_{ab}(r,r^{\prime};k)=\mathcal{N}(k)e^{\beta\omega\zeta(r^{\prime})}n_{\text{B}}(\omega)^{2}c_{ab}(\omega)\left(G_{\text{in}}(r,k)-G_{\text{out}}(r,k)\right)\left(G_{\text{in}}(r^{\prime},k)-G_{\text{out}}(r^{\prime},k)\right)\,,

(82)

for
a\neq b
. The
c_{ab}(\omega)
are at this point undetermined normalization coefficients, to be fixed by consistency with unitarity and KMS.

We can combine the results in (
81
) and (
82
) into a single matrix expression

\begin{split}\hat{\mathcal{G}}(r,r^{\prime};k)=\mathcal{N}(k)e^{\beta\omega\zeta(r^{\prime})}n_{\text{B}}(\omega)^{2}&\left[\theta(r-r^{\prime})\left(\left(G_{\text{in}}(r,k)-G_{\text{out}}(r,k)\right)\hat{\mathcal{M}}(r^{\prime},k)\right)\right.\\
&\left.+\theta(r^{\prime}-r)\left(\left(G_{\text{in}}(r^{\prime},k)-G_{\text{out}}(r^{\prime},k)\right)\hat{\mathcal{M}}(r,k)\right)\right]\,,\end{split}

(83)

where
\hat{\mathcal{M}}(r,k)
is an
n\times n
matrix whose diagonal components are the functions
u_{a}(r,k)
while its off-diagonal entries are the the coefficients
c_{ab}(\omega)\mathfrak{g}_{3}(r,k)
.

In order to determine the matrix
\hat{\mathcal{M}}(r,k)
, we need additional conditions. Following the derivation of the bulk-to-bulk propagator in
Loganayagam:2022zmq
, we require that the contributions of exchange diagrams to the on-shell action satisfy the unitarity and KMS conditions. As we have seen, these conditions are easily implemented on the generalized F/P basis, where they imply that there are no contributions of the form
J_{\text{KMS}}^{m}
or
J_{\text{d}_{i}}^{m}
.

Consider, for instance, the
J_{\text{d}_{1}}^{4}
contribution to the on-shell action in a theory with cubic interactions, arising from an exchange Witten diagram. This is given by

\begin{split}S^{(4)}_{J_{\text{d}_{1}}^{4}}&=\lambda^{2}\sum_{a,b}\int_{r_{+}}^{\infty}dr\sqrt{-g}\int_{r_{+}}^{\infty}dr^{\prime}\sqrt{-g}\,\mathbb{G}^{\text{d}_{1}}_{aa}(r,k_{1})\mathbb{G}^{\text{d}_{1}}_{aa}(r,k_{2})\hat{\mathcal{G}}_{ab}(r,r^{\prime};k_{1}+k_{2})\mathbb{G}^{\text{d}_{1}}_{bb}(r^{\prime},k_{3})\mathbb{G}^{\text{d}_{1}}_{bb}(r^{\prime},k_{4})\,,\end{split}

(84)

where we omitted both the momentum integrals and the momentum-preserving delta-function.
The bulk-to-boundary propagators
\mathbb{G}
were defined in (
66
).
Similar expressions can be written for all other
J_{\text{d}_{i}}^{4}
and
J_{\text{KMS}}^{4}
.

Once again, it will be convenient to write this expression as a trace over a matrix product, just as we did with the contact diagrams. To this end, we introduce a matrix
\hat{\mathcal{J}}
, which is unity on all its entries

\hat{\mathcal{J}}_{ab}=1\,.

(85)

In terms of this expression, we may write

5
Discussion

We have proposed a holographic dual for a generalized Schwinger-Keldysh contour, in the form of a multi-sheeted geometry which we denote as
\text{grSK}_{n}
. We described the general procedure for computing higher-order real-time correlators in terms of Witten diagrams in this geometry. Our construction generalizes the previous contour prescriptions applied to capture the holographic dual of the standard Schwinger-Keldysh contour
Glorioso:2018mmw
;
Jana:2020vyx
and, in particular, it allows for a direct calculation of fully out-of-time order correlators. While we focus much of our attention on the case of the 4-fold geometry, our procedure applies to an arbitrary number of time-folds. Similarly, for the sake of simplicity, we considered only the dynamics of probe scalars, but more general observables, such as the correlation functions of conserved currents, can be easily incorporated along the lines studied in
Loganayagam:2022zmq
. The Gaussian sector of these probe scalars in
\text{grSK}_{n}
reproduces the familiar results of
\text{grSK}_{2}
.

We would like to highlight some specific features of our constructions and the main results we are able to deduce from them.

Piecewise geometry and analytic continuation.

The
\text{grSK}_{n}
geometry consists of a collection of Lorentzian geometries, as well as a Euclidean section preparing the initial state of the system. A remarkable feature of our construction is its simplicity, with the only required building blocks being the ingoing bulk-to-boundary propagator and the corresponding bulk-to-bulk propagator; the latter being required to compute exchange Witten diagrams. From these ingredients, we are able to set up the calculation of any
n
-point correlator. The non-trivial nature of the geometry emerges only at the level of the analytic continuation across the different Lorentzian geometries and can be seen as the core feature of our prescription. We show that the analytic continuation we propose is highly constrained by the physical requirements of microscopic unitarity and the KMS condition, which translate into specific monodromies for the non-analytic bulk-to-boundary propagators.

Higher order spectral functions.

Despite the few building blocks we require in our prescription, we show that new independent observables emerge as we consider higher-order correlators and the corresponding multitude of inequivalent time orderings. Concretely, we show how our prescription allows for the calculation of spectral functions of the form
\expectationvalue{[\Op(t),\Op(0)]^{n/2}}_{\beta}
. In order to obtain non-trivial contributions to these observables, it is required to study theories beyond the Gaussian regime. Furthermore, we showed that contact Witten diagrams fail to probe this spectral function, so genuine OTO content originates from exchange diagrams that couple distinct Lorentzian segments. We demonstrated this directly for
\expectationvalue{[\Op(t),\Op(0)]^{2}}_{\beta}
in the
\text{grSK}_{4}
geometry, and proposed a conjecture as to the behavior of the spectral functions
\expectationvalue{[\Op(t),\Op(0)]^{n/2}}_{\beta}
.

Factorization channels.

In addition to obtaining non-trivial spectral functions, we also showed that the calculation of
\expectationvalue{[\Op(t),\Op(0)]^{2}}_{\beta}
has a factorization channel, along the lines explored in
Meltzer:2020qbr
. In principle, the exchange diagram contribution to an arbitrary 4-point function involves two coupled radial integrals, but for the case of the spectral function, these two radial integrals factorize because, in the commutator-squared combination, only the off-diagonal entries of the bulk-to-bulk propagator contribute, as depicted in (
90
). We conjecture that a similar behavior occurs for higher order spectral functions: a large enough number of exchanges must be considered in order to lead to non-trivial results, but a simple factorization of the radial integrals is present. We support this conjecture by the study of a subset of the required Witten diagrams, but postpone the detailed proof for future work.

5.1
Outlook

Finally, we mention some interesting generalizations of our construction and future research directions.

Lorentzian signature and complex geometries.

Here, we studied only thermal correlators, but by considering more general complex geometries using a similar piecewise construction, we could also access additional related observables. These include
\Tr[\rho^{\frac{1}{4}}_{\beta}\mathcal{O}\rho^{\frac{1}{4}}_{\beta}\mathcal{O}\rho^{\frac{1}{4}}_{\beta}\mathcal{O}\rho^{\frac{1}{4}}_{\beta}\mathcal{O}]
, correlation functions with respect to the thermo-field double state, as well as other multi-sheeted geometries associated with the quantum information theory description of black holes
Magan:2025hce
. Relatedly, we describe the
\text{grSK}_{n}
geometry in a piecewise fashion, but this specific (non-)analytic behavior suggests the possibility of reformulating the geometry as a complexified manifold over an
n
-sheeted Riemann surface, as has been done for the two-fold geometry
Jana:2020vyx
. It would be desirable to connect our analysis to the approach developed in
Loganayagam:2024mnj
, in which Witten diagrams on the complexified
\text{grSK}_{2}
geometry were studied.

Analytic continuations and gauge redundancies.
As noted above, the key nontrivial ingredient in our construction is given by the analytic continuation between Lorentzian segments. While in the main text we chose a specific form of the analytic continuation, which is consistent with unitarity and KMS invariance, other choices are possible (see Appendix
A
). For the case of the Schwinger-Keldysh contour, two-fold, the unitarity and KMS constraints have been elegantly formulated in terms of a gauge redundancy
Haehl:2016pec
;
Haehl:2016uah
. It would be highly desirable to extend this analysis for a large number of time-folds and study a possible relation between the corresponding gauge redundancy and the choice of analytic continuation.

The thermal bootstrap program.

It will be interesting to study whether the special behavior of the higher-order Witten diagrams for spectral functions we observed here leads to applications to the thermal conformal bootstrap program. The main idea is to use the results from the Witten diagram calculations as data to match with the corresponding OPE expansion, as explored recently in the case of the two-point function
Buric:2025fye
, or the equivalent Lorentzian inversion formula.

OTOCs and chaotic dynamics.

This project is deeply motivated by the study of quantum chaos, as described by the exponential growth of the out-of-time-order correlator
Shenker:2013pqa
. We have shown that our proposed dual geometry indeed captures a non-trivial spectral function which, while still constructed in terms of the simple data of the ingoing bulk-to-boundary propagator, is distinct from similar four-point functions studied within the context of the two-fold geometry
Loganayagam:2022zmq
. Furthermore, our formalism allows for the calculation of higher-order observables, which allows for a more detailed description of the dynamics of the theory
Vardhan:2025rky
. We do not directly show the emergence of exponential growth, as we expect this to be related to the presence of a long-lived mode, such as is observed in systems with a shift symmetry
Blake:2017ris
or in the case of soft modes due to a specific symmetry-breaking process
Haehl:2018izb
. As noticed earlier, it is clear how to extend our work in order to include these more general fields associated to long-lived modes, and it will be the subject of future research.

Complex geometries and the gravitational path integral.

While this work is deeply rooted within the context of the AdS/CFT correspondence, it can be seen in a wider context as part of the study of the real-time gravitational path integral. The
\text{grSK}_{n}
geometry we construct here is a saddle-point of this integral, and it shows that multi-sheeted complex geometries play a crucial role in understanding Lorentzian observables. While the fact that our geometry is a saddle of the gravitational path integral is true by construction, we make no claim as to whether it is the dominant saddle. A wider study of the path integral, including more general geometries and topology-changing processes, presents an exciting, albeit highly challenging, avenue of research. In this context, we see our study here as providing some sure initial footing.

Appendix C
A toy model: scalars in BTZ background

In order to be even more concrete in our calculations, it will be useful to consider the simpler case where the background geometry is the three dimensional BTZ black hole. We consider then a minimally coupled scalar with a cubic interaction

\begin{split}S[\varphi]&=\int d^{3}x\sqrt{-g}\left[-\frac{1}{2}\left(\nabla_{A}\varphi\nabla^{A}\varphi+m^{2}\varphi^{2}\right)-\lambda\varphi^{3}\right]+S_{\text{c.t.}}\,.\end{split}

(140)

The gaussian dynamics and contact interactions of this theory were studied in
Jana:2020vyx
within the context of the
\text{grSK}_{2}
geometry. A more general model was studied in
Loganayagam:2022zmq
, which allowed for non-minimal scalars whose dynamics are modulated by a dilaton coupling. Here we will consider the minimally coupled scalar for clarity of the notation, but remark that many of the calculations can be easily generalized to include scalars with a non-trivial dilaton.

As we have seen, the key ingredient for the evaluation of Witten diagrams in the
\text{grSK}_{n}
geometry is the ingoing bulk-to-boundary propagator. For the case of

32
) and boundary conditions (
37
