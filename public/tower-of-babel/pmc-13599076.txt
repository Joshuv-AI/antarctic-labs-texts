BERTopic-driven term extraction from biomedical texts toward ontology population: evaluating vaccine ontology with Plotkin’s vaccines corpus
Authors: B Damayanthi Jesudas, Sam Smith, Feng-Yu Yeh, Jie Zheng, John Beverley, William D Duncan, Yongqun He
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOntologies are essential for structuring biomedical knowledge, supporting semantic integration, reasoning, and data interoperability. In vaccinology, ontology population is particularly critical, as vaccines span diverse domains. A well-defined Vaccine Ontology enables consistent knowledge representation, integration across datasets, and supports applications such as decision support, literature mining, and semantic search. However, manual ontology population is tedious, time-consuming, and difficult to maintain in this dynamically evolving domain, underscoring the need for automated or semi-automated population approaches.MethodsWe present a semi-automated pipeline that uses BERTopic, an advanced topic modeling approach that leverages Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers (BERT)-based embeddings to extract ontology relevant concepts from biomedical text. To evaluate the effectiveness of this automated approach, the method is applied to Plotkin’s Vaccines corpus, a leading reference text in vaccinology that synthesizes scientific, clinical, and policy perspectives on vaccines. The workflow integrates multiple natural language processing (NLP) components: document preprocessing with spaCy part-of-speech tagging and vectorization, sentence embeddings generated by a lightweight transformer model (all-MiniLM-L6-v2), dimensionality reduction with Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP), clustering with Hierarchical Density-Based Spatial Clustering of Applications with Noise (HDBSCAN), and topic representation. To guide topic discovery toward vaccine-relevant concepts and filter irrelevant terms, the pipeline incorporates a curated set of vaccine-focused terms derived from the existing Vaccine Ontology (VO) as seed words to influence topic representations, while preserving the unsupervised nature of the clustering process. To enhance interpretability, the pipeline employs Keyword extraction using BERT embeddings (KeyBERT) for automatic keyword-based labeling, supplemented with disambiguated descriptive labels, and Bidirectional and Auto-Regressive Transformer (BART) summarization for topic-level summaries. The resulting hierarchical topic structures are further refined through a tree-merging module that unifies multiple topic hierarchies into a coherent ontology-like representation. The extracted topics are reviewed by the Subject Matter Experts (SMEs) to filter irrelevant terms and then mapped to Vaccine Ontology, a well-established ontology to assess their relevance and coverage, demonstrating how automated methods can reduce the labor-intensive effort required for manual ontology population.ResultsThe script is customized to generate a varying number of topics and keywords. In this study, the top 50 topics with 10 keywords per topic were extracted for each chapter of Plotkin’s vaccines. The pipeline produced coherent topic clusters representing key themes in vaccinology, including immune mechanisms, pathogen-specific vaccines, and vaccine types. The hierarchical tree-merging process is used to illustrate how semantically related concept groupings emerge and can suggest potential ontology subdivisions. This serves as a visualization of conceptual relationships derived from the data and is particularly helpful for SMEs to review, interpret, and validate candidate concepts.ConclusionsThis study demonstrates the feasibility of BERTopic-driven, a semi-automated approach for extracting ontology-relevant concepts from biomedical texts. The method was evaluated using a foundational vaccinology corpus and assessed against an existing, well-developed VO to determine the relevance and coverage of the extracted topics. Mapping the topics to the established ontology enabled identification of concept alignments and irrelevant terms, which were subsequently reviewed by SMEs. The results show that the proposed approach can effectively surface meaningful, ontology-relevant concepts while significantly reducing the time and effort for manual population, thereby providing a scalable strategy for supporting ontology maintenance and enrichment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13326-026-00353-w.Keywords: Ontology development, BERTopic, Topic modeling, Vaccinology, Plotkin’s vaccines, Vaccine Ontology (VO)

Introduction

While ontologies have become critical for data standardization, sharing, and integration, their development is typically time-consuming. The Vaccine Ontology (VO) is a community-based biomedical ontology in the domain of vaccinology [1–3]. The current version of VO (version 2026-06-12) contains over 15,000 terms (https://ontobee.org/ontostat/VO), covering various vaccine types (including licensed vaccines, vaccines in clinical trials, and vaccines in research verified in laboratory animal models), vaccine components, and vaccine responses [2]. The development of VO has required more than 15 years of manual annotation and community effort. However, it is expected that many vaccine-related terms remain uncovered, motivating the need for automated tools to assist in identifying ontology-relevant concepts and evaluating their coverage against VO.

The growing complexity and diversity of vaccine-related data in biomedical research necessitate structured frameworks to organize and interpret such information. VO enhances data management, retrieval, and analysis by providing a comprehensive and hierarchical classification system. However, the sheer volume and multifaceted nature of vaccine-related research make manual curation impractical. Ontology population [4], which involves adding instances or concepts to an ontology from textual sources, typically relies on Information Extraction (IE) [5] tasks such as Named Entity Recognition (NER) [6], Open Information Extraction (OpenIE) [7], Relation Extraction (RE) [8]. Approaches using NLP, machine learning, and deep learning techniques identify relevant entities and relationships from text [9], and perform remarkably across different domains, such as biomedical and cybersecurity [10, 11], although they often require substantial training data and expert supervision [12, 13]. Therefore, automated extraction of ontology-relevant concepts from large-scale textual data offers a promising solution for supporting the ontology population.

In this work, we apply advanced NLP techniques particularly BERTopic [14], to extract ontology-relevant concepts from Plotkin’s Vaccines [15], a comprehensive reference covering immunology, vaccine development, disease-specific strategies, clinical applications, and public health perspectives. BERTopic is used to identify coherent topics and conceptual groupings that can serve as candidate ontology classes. KeyBERT [16] was incorporated for automated keyword-based labeling, keyword extraction, and spaCy [17] supports part-of-speech (POS) tagging [18, 19], sentence-level vaccine term filtering to ensure semantic coherence of the generated topics. The approach is further enhanced by hierarchical clustering, enabling the organization of related topics into broader conceptual layers and providing a clear and interpretable taxonomy. Additionally, UMAP [20] and HDBSCAN [21, 22] are used for dimensionality reduction and clustering, ensuring that both global patterns and local semantic structures are captured. Descriptive labels are generated for each topic using domain-specific keywords, while BART [23]-based summarization is employed to produce concise topic summaries that enhance interpretability. This methodology is designed to be scalable and adaptable, allowing application across different biomedical domains.

By the conclusion of this work, we aim to demonstrate that BERTopic can semi-automatically extract ontology-relevant concepts from biomedical literature. The extracted topics are mapped to VO to evaluate their alignment, coverage, and potential gaps, and are subsequently reviewed by SMEs to assess their relevance. Through this approach, we show how automated NLP-driven methods can significantly reduce the manual effort required for ontology population while leveraging a well-established ontology as a benchmark for evaluation, thereby contributing to advances in biomedical informatics and the broader application of NLP in scientific research.

Background literature review

Recent studies have adopted diverse approaches, including ontology engineering, topic modeling, and hybrid Artificial Intelligence (AI) systems, to study vaccine hesitancy and develop vaccine-related ontologies. Babcock et al. [24] expanded the Infectious Disease Ontology (IDO) with modular extensions for viruses such as Severe Acute Respiratory Syndrome CoronaVirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2), following Open Biological and Biomedical Ontologies (OBO) Foundry principles to ensure interoperability and semantic rigor, though increasing module complexity remains a limitation. Ngo et al. [25] proposed a semantic search system for clinical ontologies that integrates dense embeddings with structural knowledge, improving retrieval performance over traditional methods but remaining constrained by data quality, term ambiguity, and computational cost. Additionally, NLP integrated with ontology-based modeling has demonstrated effectiveness in extracting structured knowledge from unstructured clinical text and enabling rule-based reasoning [26].

A cascaded ontology-driven framework combining PubMed Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers (PubMedBERT), Self-Alignment Pre-training for Biomedical Entity Representations (SapBERT), and the VO has been applied to vaccine name normalization, achieving strong accuracy but facing challenges with ambiguous terminology [27]. The Patient Safety Knowledge Graph (PSKG) enables large-scale integration and analysis of vaccine safety data but is limited by data quality and alignment issues [28]. Embedding-based approaches have also been used to support ontology population and relation discovery, improving scalability while introducing concerns such as bias and interpretability [29].

Hybrid ontology population methods combining rule-based and embedding approaches have been explored, though they are often constrained by predefined patterns [30]. Large language models (LLMs) further support ontology population but require expert validation due to hallucination and bias risks [31].

In parallel, topic modeling approaches have been widely applied to vaccine discourse. Latent Dirichlet Allocation (LDA) topic modeling and Valence Aware Dictionary and sEntiment Reasoner (VADER)-based analyses of Coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) tweets revealed sentiment trends but were limited by noise and demographic bias [32]. BERTopic has shown improved coherence and interpretability over LDA across multiple studies, including social media and narrative medicine datasets, though challenges remain due to noisy data and lack of ground-truth [33, 34]. Extensions combining BERTopic with Word2Vec and large-scale tweet analysis have enabled identification of disease patterns and behavioral insights but remain sensitive to preprocessing and representation biases [35].

In summary, prior work demonstrates that ontology engineering supports structured and interoperable knowledge representation but faces challenges in scalability, continuous updating, and maintenance. Topic modeling approaches, particularly BERTopic, provide flexible and interpretable means of capturing evolving vaccine-related discourse, although they remain sensitive to noise and bias in large textual corpora. Survey-based and hybrid methods offer valuable demographic and behavioral insights but lack scalability for extensive unstructured data. Collectively, these findings suggest that integrating contextual embeddings, density-based clustering, and keyword extraction provides a promising pathway for semi-automated ontology population, enabling a balance between data-driven discovery and semantic interpretability.

Methods

The primary aim of this work is to develop a reproducible computational framework for extracting labeled topics from Plotkin’s Vaccines corpus, with the VO used for evaluation. This corpus provides comprehensive coverage of vaccine science, including basic biology, immunology, epidemiology, clinical evidence, manufacturing, and public health implementation. Such breadth ensures that the extracted ontology-relevant concepts span multiple dimensions, enabling a multidisciplinary representation of vaccine knowledge and supporting a more comprehensive capture of relevant concepts and relationships.

Figure 1 illustrates the Guided BERTopic modeling pipeline used for extracting labeled topics. The process begins with the ingestion of chapter-wise text in .docx format, where each paragraph is treated as a document unit after removing empty segments. To reduce noise and ensure that BERTopic modeling is restricted to vaccine-related discourse, a pre-modeling sentence-level corpus filtering step is applied. Using spaCy, each paragraph is segmented into sentences, and only those containing predefined vaccine terms are retained and concatenated to form a vaccine-focused corpus, while paragraphs without qualifying sentences are excluded. This filtering step constrains the input corpus without modifying the underlying BERTopic modeling algorithm.

Guided BERTopic modeling pipeline

Subsequently, the filtered text undergoes preprocessing with spaCy, where POS tagging is used to retain key content-bearing tokens such as nouns, verbs, adjectives, and adverbs, while removing stop words. Parameter settings are provided in Appendix A to ensure reproducibility. In parallel, each document is embedded using the SentenceTransformer model (all-MiniLM-L6-v2), and Term Frequency and Inverse Document Frequency (TF–IDF) [36, 37] is used to generate lexical features with unigrams and bigrams. These representations are then passed into the BERTopic framework, which integrates UMAP for dimensionality reduction, HDBSCAN for clustering, and c-TF-IDF [38] for topic representation. This process yields topic assignments, document-level topic probabilities, and ranked keywords per topic, with non-coherent documents grouped as outliers (Topic −1).

Beyond the core BERTopic workflow, additional components are incorporated to enhance interpretability. KeyBERT is used to generate initial keyword-based labels for each topic, and BART-based summarization produces concise abstractions of topic content that can serve as draft definitions. A refinement step resolves duplicate or overly generic labels by incorporating information from summaries or top keywords, resulting in more descriptive topic labels. The resulting topics are then organized into hierarchical structures using BERTopic’s hierarchy functions, and chapter-level hierarchies are combined into a merged tree represented in American Standard Code for Information Interchange (ASCII) and Comma Separated Values (CSV) formats. This structure reflects broader-to-narrower thematic relationships and serves as an ontology-like scaffold for vaccine knowledge. Visualizations, including hierarchical clustering and topic bar charts, are generated alongside structured outputs (text, CSV, Excel, and tree files) to support analysis and reproducibility.

A refined collection of vaccine-related terms is obtained from the extracted topics and further curated through SMEs review to eliminate irrelevant and redundant entries. This curated set is subsequently mapped to the VO to assess coverage and identify gaps. This final step enables a systematic evaluation of the extracted concepts against an established ontology, demonstrating how the proposed pipeline can support ontology population by identifying relevant concepts, highlighting gaps, and facilitating expert-driven validation in a scalable and reproducible manner.

Several topics correspond to pathogen or disease-specific vaccine domains, where descriptive labels provide greater semantic clarity than the initial auto labels. For example, Topic 0 (Vaccine - Influenza) refines a generic label, while Topics 1 (Rotavirus - Vaccines), 2 (Pneumococcal - Meningococcal), 3 (Pertussis), and 12 (Meningococcal - Vaccine) capture distinct vaccine-pathogen associations. These topics yield candidate ontology classes aligned with relations such as is_a (vaccine), targets_pathogen, and confers_immunity_to. Similarly, Topics 9 (Vaccines - Cholera), 7 (Typhoid - Vaccines), and 11 (Anthracis) support ontology population across both human and zoonotic domains, suggesting extensions through relations such as applies_to (species) and zoonotic_association.

Other topics reflect vaccine platforms, development processes, and immunological mechanisms, where descriptive labeling resolves repetition among generic auto labels. For instance, Topics 6 (Vaccine - Development), Topic 10 (Vaccine - HPV), and Topic 8 (Pneumococcal - Vaccines) distinguish methodological and formulation-specific aspects. These clusters contribute candidate classes related to vaccine_platform, antigen_type, and manufacturing_approach, linked through relations such as implemented_by or derived_from. Historical and foundational aspects are also evident, in Topic 4 (Vaccine - Smallpox), and Topic 14 (Vaccination - Vaccinated), capturing early vaccination practices and population-level perspectives.

Population and disease-specific considerations emerge in Topic 13 (Encephalitis) and Topic 15 (Vaccines - Zostavax), highlighting dimensions relevant to target-populations and policy. Collectively, the combination of auto and descriptive labels yields a structured semantic landscape spanning pathogens, vaccine types, immunological strategies, historical developments, and demographic considerations. When mapped to VO and reviewed by SMEs, these topic-derived concepts support systematic ontology population through well-defined relations thereby bridging unstructured vaccine discourse with structured biomedical knowledge.

Additional clusters such as Topic 4 (smallpox), Topic 6 (vaccine development), and Topic 5 (Burgdorferi), further capture historical, methodological, and zoonotic dimensions of vaccinology. However, as these relationships are based on embedding-based similarity rather than formal semantic subsumption, they should be treated as exploratory groupings rather than explicit ontology concepts.

The topic modeling of Plotkin’s Vaccines reveals a structured thematic flow across the nine chapters, with each contributing distinct yet interconnected vaccine concepts. Chapter 1 establishes historical and foundational perspectives, with topics such as Smallpox - Variolation, Vaccine - Encephalitis, and Rotavirus - Vaccines, reflecting the transition from early immunization practices to targeted vaccine development. Chapter 2 builds on this foundation by focusing on immunological mechanisms, including Vaccine - Polysaccharides and Vaccines - Immunisation, linking concepts such as capsular polysaccharides and immunogenicity to practical vaccine formulations.

Subsequent chapters extend these themes to protein antigens, adjuvants, viral vectors, pediatric vaccination, immunological memory, epidemiology, and global vaccine implementation, collectively forming a layered representation of vaccinology from basic science to public health. Foundational concepts (e.g., smallpox, variolation) connect to mechanistic themes (e.g., antigens, antibodies, adjuvants), which extend into applied contexts such as vaccine technologies, clinical schedules, and global immunization strategies. Together, the combined results of extracted topics, keywords, and hierarchical structures provide a coherent framework for the ontology population. The hierarchical topic trees organize domain-specific entities such as influenza, cholera, anthrax, poliomyelitis, hepatitis, and pneumococcal vaccine alongside immunological processes, transforming flat outputs into a structured knowledge representation. This organization facilitates the identification of higher-level groupings and associated sub-concepts and serves as a visualization of conceptual relationships derived from the data, supporting SMEs in reviewing and validating candidate concepts. However, these relationships arise from statistical similarity in the embedding space rather than formally defined semantic hierarchies and should therefore be viewed as indicative associations rather than definitive ontology concepts.

Table 2 illustrates BART-generated topic summaries derived from BERTopic modeling of Plotkin’s Vaccines, Chapter 1, capturing key historical and scientific developments in vaccinology. The topics span pathogen-specific vaccines such as influenza, rotavirus, pneumococcal, pertussis, smallpox, and Lyme disease, with summaries highlighting milestone discoveries, vaccine formulations, and clinical trials. Together, these summaries contextualize the extracted topics by linking prominent keywords to historically grounded narratives, supporting interpretable and ontology-ready representations of vaccine knowledge. These summaries may introduce occasional inaccuracies or hallucinated details due to their generative nature. To mitigate this, summaries are used only as supportive interpretations for SMEs rather than being directly incorporated into ontology-related outputs.

Document-level topic assignments, where each document corresponds to an individual paragraph, further illustrate the model’s ability to capture coherent vaccine-related themes with varying levels of confidence. For instance, Document 17 is strongly associated with Topic 4 (Vaccine - Smallpox) and Document 50 with Topic 0 (Vaccine - Influenza), both with a probability of 1.0, indicating clear and unambiguous clustering. In contrast, Document 35 (Typhoid - Vaccines, probability 0.49), Document 79 (Vaccines - Zostavax, probability 0.70), and Document 117 (Vaccine - HPV, probability 0.38) exhibit moderate to lower assignment probabilities, reflecting partial thematic overlap or contextual ambiguity within the text. These variations demonstrate the probabilistic nature of BERTopic, where high-confidence assignments indicate well-defined topics, while lower probabilities highlight documents that may span multiple concepts, which is particularly relevant for identifying nuanced or emerging ontology-relevant terms. Importantly, these assignments provide a granular mapping between textual units and topic structures, supporting fine-grained evaluation and validation of ontology-relevant concept extraction.

The curated list of vaccine-related terms generated from the extracted topics was subsequently refined through SMEs review to remove irrelevant and repetitive entries. The finalized term set was then mapped to the VO to evaluate coverage and identify gaps. This analysis revealed that core concepts such as vaccine, vaccine components, storage, mechanisms, target pathogens, and recipients are well represented in VO. Notably, market-related aspects, vaccine adoption, and policy-driven themes are largely outside the current scope of VO, reflecting its primary focus on biological and experimental dimensions of vaccinology. Additionally, certain concepts appeared in VO under different semantic representations, indicating alignment challenges rather than true absence. The findings highlight that while VO provides strong coverage of foundational vaccine knowledge, there is potential to expand its scope through community-driven and expert-informed efforts to include complementary domains such as adoption and policy for improved completeness, where appropriate.

Discussion

Topic trees generated by BERTopic provide a hierarchical organization of concepts extracted from text by converting indentation-based topic structures into a directed ontology graph, where nodes represent candidate ontology classes and edges capture broader–narrower (parent→child) semantic relationships. When multiple chapters of Plotkin’s Vaccines are analyzed separately, each produces its own topic tree; merging into a single directed graph integrates overlapping concepts while preserving chapter-specific subtrees. To ensure a unified structure, orphan roots are attached to a synthetic top-level node (“Vaccine Ontology”), resulting in a coherent taxonomy that can be visualized and exported (e.g., as CSV) for further refinement and alignment with existing vocabularies. The resulting graph is rendered with a left-to-right hierarchical layout, with color-coded roles. Orange for the global root, blue for parent nodes, and green for leaves, facilitating the interpretation of branching depth and conceptual scope. This merged representation highlights both shared concepts and contextual specializations across domains such as immunology, vaccine technologies, epidemiology, and clinical practice, enabling SMEs to review, interpret, and align candidate concepts with existing biomedical ontologies.

The ontology tree visualization (Fig. 4) depicts the merged ontology tree constructed from Plotkin’s Vaccines, Chapters 1 and 2, illustrating a unified hierarchical representation of vaccine-related knowledge. A synthetic root node, "Vaccine Ontology"(shown in orange) anchors the structure and ensures a single connected graph across chapters. From this root, higher-level parent nodes (blue), represent broad thematic categories such as Vaccines-Immunology, Vaccination-Immune, Vaccines-Conjugate, and disease-linked grouping like Smallpox, Rubella, and Polio. These parent nodes branch into more specialized leaf-level concepts (green), which capture vaccine-scoped entities such as Vaccines-Antibodies, Vaccines-Antigens and Vaccine-Lymph, alongside disease-specific entries (e.g., Influenza, Pertussis, Tuberculosis, HPV), and vaccine-related constructs (e.g., Peptides , Genome, Immunogenic, Protection).

Ontology tree generated from Chapters 1 and 2

Several recurrent hub nodes, including Vaccines-Antigens, Vaccines-Antibodies, and Immunization, serve as semantic connectors linking immunological mechanisms with vaccine development and application, reflecting conceptual overlap across chapters. The dense interconnections highlight how foundational immune concepts bridge basic biology and applied vaccinology, while peripheral leaf nodes represent more context-specific or disease-focused knowledge. Overall, the multi-level structure demonstrates the feasibility of transforming topic hierarchies into an ontology-ready graph, where parent-child relationships approximate ‘is_a’ and subclass_of relations, and shared hubs expose cross-cutting concepts essential for vaccine ontology population.

While this approach demonstrates a scalable strategy for supporting ontology population, limitations remain, including ambiguity in topic labels, reliance on similarity-based rather than formally defined semantic relations, and challenges due to term overlap and synonymy. Future work will focus on integrating domain ontologies, semantic parsing, and expert validation to improve alignment, robustness, and applicability.

Conclusion

BERTopic supports ontology engineering by enabling the discovery of candidate concepts, generation of labels and synonyms, suggestion of taxonomic structures, and drafting of definitions, making it a powerful NLP-based approach for accelerating VO enhancement. This study demonstrates that a semi-automated, reproducible pipeline can bridge the gap between unstructured vaccinology literature and structured ontology population. By systematically processing Plotkin’s Vaccines through paragraph-level ingestion, POS-filtered tokenization, and BERTopic’s embedding-based clustering, coherent and interpretable thematic clusters were generated. The integration of keyword-based labeling, BART-based summarization for draft definitions, and hierarchical topic trees supported the refinement of these clusters into candidate ontology concepts with meaningful associations. The ontology tree visualization further illustrates the merged hierarchical structure generated across chapters, providing an interpretable view of concept groupings that assists SMEs in reviewing and validating candidate concepts. However, as these relationships arise from similarity in the embedding space rather than formally defined semantic hierarchies, they are intended as indicative groupings rather than definitive ontology constructs. The pipeline was configured to generate a user-defined number of topics and keywords (50 topics with 10 keywords per topic per chapter in this study), with outputs exported in TXT, CSV, and Excel formats along with visualizations to support expert validation. As BERTopic determines clusters dynamically using UMAP and HDBSCAN, all discovered topics are retained in the outputs, while user-specified limits apply to reporting and visualization.

A set of vaccine-related terms was derived from the extracted topics and refined through SMEs review to remove irrelevant and redundant entries. This refined set was mapped to the VO to evaluate coverage and identify gaps. The analysis indicates that core concepts, including vaccines, components, storage, mechanisms, target pathogens, and recipients are well represented in VO, while areas such as market dynamics, vaccine adoption, and policy remain outside its current scope, reflecting its emphasis on biological and experimental domains. Differences in semantic representation further highlight alignment challenges and opportunities for refinement. Overall, this work presents a semi-automated approach for the ontology population that addresses the limitations of manual, time-intensive curation. Using VO as a reference, the pipeline enables systematic extraction, refinement, and mapping of ontology-relevant concepts, demonstrating its potential as a scalable and adaptable framework for supporting ontology population and maintenance across biomedical domains.
