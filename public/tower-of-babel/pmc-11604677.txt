Neuropathic Pain Induced by Spinal Cord Injury from the Glia Perspective and Its Treatment
Authors: Ying Ye, Xinjin Su, Jun Tang, Chao Zhu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractRegional neuropathic pain syndromes above, at, or below the site of spinal damage arise after spinal cord injury (SCI) and are believed to entail distinct pathways; nevertheless, they may share shared defective glial systems. Neuropathic pain after SCI is caused by glial cells, ectopic firing of neurons endings and their intra- and extracellular signaling mechanisms. One such mechanism occurs when stimuli that were previously non-noxious become so after the injury. This will exhibit a symptom of allodynia. Another mechanism is the release of substances by glia, which keeps the sensitivity of dorsal horn neurons even in regions distant from the site of injury. Here, we review, the models and identifications of SCI-induced neuropathic pain (SCI-NP), the mechanisms of SCI-NP related to glia, and the treatments of SCI-NP.Graphical AbstractThe process of SCI-NP. Introduction

Spasticity and neuropathic pain are often associated with spinal cord injury (SCI), a common traumatic neurological condition that leads to significant dysfunction of sensory, motor, and autonomic function. The complexity of neuropathic pain, which has several underlying causes, contributes to difficulties in its management (Widerström-Noga 2023). After SCI, neuropathic pain is quite prevalent. 53% of people reported having neuropathic pain overall, with 27% reporting below-level neuropathic pain and 19% reporting at-level neuropathic pain. Pain that is either spontaneous or triggered by stimuli, together with dysesthesia and paresthesia, is what is known as SCI-induced neuropathic pain (SCI-NP) (Bryce et al. 2012). At-level SCI-NP is predominantly distributed in the neural distribution area of the segment corresponding to the spinal cord injury plane. Below-level neuropathic pain locates in part or all of the body below the level of SCI. The central somatosensory system in spinal cord and the peripheral somatosensory system in the nerve roots are both affected by disease, which may lead to this kind of SCI-NP. It is not uncommon for the two types of damage to be difficult to distinguish (Bryce et al. 2012; Siddall and Middleton 2015).

Unfortunately, SCI-NP is often challenging. The focus of treating SCI-NP is not only on reducing painful symptoms but also on developing effective treatment modalities that target the mechanisms involved in SCI-NP. After SCI, neuropathic pain is more often studied than nociceptive pain due to its recurrence and drug resistance. Nevertheless, nociceptive pain that persists and neuropathic pain that occurs after SCI might share some same neuro-inflammatory mechanisms. Glial cells are involved in neuropathic pain following SCI, but the mechanisms of glial cell involvement appear to be somewhat different in different pain models.

Notable advancements in our knowledge of SCI have occurred in the last several years, including the identification of certain pathways that contribute to post-SCI pain and the current methods for treatment. Rather than addressing all forms of pain after SCI, this review focuses on the models, glial mechanisms and therapy of SCI-NP. Finally, we highlight potential future advances and treatment approaches in future.

Models of SCI-NP and Their Identification

Clinicians have paid little attention to the causes of post-SCI chronic pain, and animal models for studying post-SCI behavior development and maintenance have just been available for use in the last few years. Even while SCI-NP models often include segments in the thorax, they may also have segments in the cervical or upper lumbar regions. There are several models that may be used to simulate spinal cord injuries, such as a spinal hemisection, ischemic SCI, anterolateral tract isolation, localized excitotoxicity caused by injection of glutamate receptor agonist, spinal contusion, and others (Hao et al. 1991; Christensen et al. 1996; Yezierski et al. 1998; Vierck et al. 2000; Young 2002; Bruce et al. 2002; Hulsebosch et al. 2009; Wieseler et al. 2010; Detloff et al. 2013). Mild bruising and pressure in the middle and lower thoracic regions (T6-T13) are the predominant SCI-NP models. The primary goal of using these injury models is to simulate the various levels of spinal cord injury in humans, which is why there are multiple models of SCI that are categorized by the plane of the injury. On the other hand, spinal cord injuries at the low to mid-thoracic level (T6-T13) produce a persistent and long-lasting state of neuropathic pain and are most commonly modeled by the SCI-NP (Palandi et al. 2020).

In animal studies, Von Frey measures tactile sensitivity and is a surrogate marker of abnormal pain in peripherally or centrally sensitized states. The measurement involves delivering a focal stimulus to a particular region of the rodent’s body (typically the bottom surface of the hind paw) and documenting the strength of the stimulus required to elicit a withdrawal reflex. Typically, these filaments are placed to a hindpaw’s underside. Increased heat sensitivity is another common indicator of pain, and it may be measured by observing how quickly one withdraws from a radiant stimulation administered to the same location (Hargreaves test) (Wang et al. 2022). An inherent constraint of the commonly employed evaluations of ‟pain” after SCI is that they primarily measure reflexes that are controlled by the spinal cord, thereby providing limited insight into the crucial emotional and cognitive dimensions of pain (Wiech et al. 2008; Mendell 2011). In recent years, additional testing methods have been introduced to assess the cognitive perception of pain using operant testing. All of these paradigms are based on the opinion that SCI-NP animals prefer escaping or avoiding a negative stimulus. Animals may choose to live in an unpleasant bright place free of painful stimuli, or in their naturally favored dark habitat with possibly painful stimuli (Chhaya et al. 2019; Li et al. 2020a; Dugan et al. 2021). The inclusion of a conscious component in these operant tasks has enhanced the usefulness of SCI-NP data in preclinical research, resulting in a more comprehensive understanding.

Heart rate variability (HRV) refers to the natural fluctuations in the duration of the heart’s R–R intervals. By studying HRV, several metrics that indicate the balance of the autonomic nervous system can be obtained. HRV analysis can be used to detect autonomic anomalies that are linked to long-lasting neuropathic pain in individuals with SCI. Individuals with SCI-NP exhibit decreased HRV as shown by the standard deviation of normal-to-normal intervals in the temporal domain. HRV analysis is a novel technique that can accurately evaluate SCI-NP in individuals (Karri et al. 2017).

It is unknown if there are gender variations in the severity of SCI pain symptoms since sex has not been taken into account in the majority of SCI. Recent studies using SCI-NP models have demonstrated that there are instances where male and female animals perceive nociceptive stimuli in distinct ways; however, this is not always the case (Lee et al. 2018, 2023b). Results from many models of neuropathic pain indicate that male and female patients may perceive pain differently, necessitating different approaches to therapy (Bartley and Fillingim 2013). The reduction in hindlimb mechanical withdrawal threshold is more pronounced in male rats compared to female rats following SCI (Gaudet et al. 2017). On the other hand, female rats exhibit less cold sensitization and more heat sensitization than male rats in an operant testing model of quisqualate injection, which causes excitotoxic SCI (Acosta-Rua et al. 2011; Zhao et al. 2024). Sex- and SCI-related experiments can provide an important foundation for preclinical studies. Future studies could attempt to explore similar or different cellular mechanisms involved regarding sex differences in SCI-NP.

Mechanisms of Glial Involvement in SCI-NP

SCI affects several pain pathways in both CNS (central nervous system) and PNS peripheral system), potentially resulting in the onset of neuropathic pain. Possible mechanisms may include the following: (1) either partially intact sensing pathways or the abnormal branching of dorsal horn neurons (Vardeh et al. 2016); (2) activated glial cells and central sensitization of ions involved in intracellular signaling mechanisms in CNS (Hains and Waxman 2006; Zhao et al. 2007; Gwak and Hulsebosch 2009; Zhang et al. 2016; Brown et al. 2021); (3) occurrence of pro-inflammatory reactions, multiplication of microglia cells and their activation, which are associated with potassium channels and defective zymogens (Gattlen et al. 2020; Brown et al. 2021; Yuan et al. 2023); (4) cytokines and cytokine receptors in the spinal cord (Bobinski et al. 2018); (5) changes in the expression of ion channels, resulting in an increased release of neurotransmitters (Boroujerdi et al. 2011; Roca‐Lapirot et al. 2018; Pan 2022); (6) BDNF and TrkB tyrosine kinase signaling pathways (Geng et al. 2010); (7) modifications in membrane transport proteins (Hasbargen et al. 2010); (8) neuroplasticity caused by the activation of cannabinoid and vanilloid receptors, as well as interactions with chemokines (Knerlich-Lukoschus et al. 2011); (9) depletion of GABA inhibitory interneurons (Meisner et al. 2010); (10) dopamine receptors in the brain (Voulalas et al. 2017); (11) NMDA receptors (Yu et al. 2022); (12) inflammatory mediators (Zhou et al. 2003, 2016; Peng et al. 2006; Sefiani and Geoffroy 2021); and (13) genetic susceptibility considerations (Zorina-Lichtenwalter et al. 2018). Studies conducted on living organisms and in laboratory conditions have indicated that activated astrocytes and microglia are essential for the development and maintenance of SCI-NP (Hains and Waxman 2006; Gwak et al. 2012). SCI causes activation of astrocytes and microglia across the entire spinal dorsal horn, which has an important role in neuropathic pain (Hu et al. 2023; Lee et al. 2023a; Ramos and Cruz 2023) (Fig. 1). After SCI, reactive astrocytes develop a glial scar, which restricts the growth of the injury and the regeneration of axons. In a healthy spinal cord, microglia actively seek out infections and regulate the density of synapses. However, following SCI, microglia can become hyperactive for extended periods, boosting plasticity while also aggravating injury. Oligodendrocyte precursor cells (OPCs) and oligodendrocytes play a crucial role in maintaining efficient transmission of nerve impulses through myelination and providing nourishment to the spinal cord. However, after SCI, these cells work hard to multiply and repair the damaged axons by remyelinating them (Fig. 1).

Astrocytes React to SCI-NP

The level of astrocyte enlargement after SCI varies with the severity of the lesion, the amount of time that has passed since the injury, and the proximity of the insult to the astrocytes (Sofroniew 2005, 2009). Initially, astrocytes proliferate and aggregate at the edges of severely damaged and irreparable tissue after SCI. This state continues for about 7–10 days (Wanner et al. 2013; Li et al. 2022a) (Fig. 3). In the first 3 days after injury, astrocytes enlarge abnormally and glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) levels rise somewhat, which is called astrogliosis (Karimi-Abdolrezaee and Billakanti 2012). Nevertheless, in a phenomenon called isomorphic gliosis, astrocytes keep to their own domains without seeming to invade the domains of neighboring astrocytes (Sofroniew and Vinters 2010). The modifications can be undone by reducing the impact of signaling molecules upstream. Between 3 and 14 days after SCI, reactive astrocytes start to show signs of hypertrophy, proliferation, and substantial GFAP expression. Individual astrocyte territories are disrupted and tissue is distorted as a result of these massive enlargements (Sofroniew 2009). A glial scar forms around the SCI core area at this point (Kang and Hébert 2011). Astrocytes form scars around injured regions to contain inflammatory cells and to save healthy brain tissue from further harm (Wanner et al. 2013). After 14 days, the number of astrocytes decreased further. In humans, astroligosis develops and becomes increasingly apparent as SCI proceeds through its intermediate and chronic phases. An immunohistochemical analysis of human SCI tissues at different time intervals revealed a notable escalation in astrogliosis at the 11-day mark post-injury, which continued to persist even one year after SCI (Buss et al. 2007). Scar formation after SCI is caused by both reactive astrocytes and meningeal fibroblasts. In order to control astrocyte and meningeal fibroblast segregation and the creation of glial-fibrotic scars after SCI, cell contact-mediated bidirectional communication between ephrinB2-expressing astrocytes and ephrinB2-bearing meningeal fibroblasts starts intracellular signaling cascades (Bundesen et al. 2003) (Fig. 2). Another study further demonstrated that astrocytes inhibited the dissemination of inflammatory cells at the injury site after promoting scarring and has shown that STAT3 signaling is essential in regulating certain elements of reactive astrogliosis (Herrmann et al. 2008).

In recent years, studies have identified numerous causative factors for astrogliosis, including cytokines and chemokines (Merrill and Benveniste 1996; Reilly and Kumari 1996; Kahn et al. 1997; Escartin et al. 2007; Gris et al. 2007; Lu et al. 2018; Rabchevsky et al. 1998). The IL-6/JAK2/STAT3 pathway activates microglia and astrocytes, as well as enduring below-level pain after SCI (Lee et al. 2023a) (Fig. 2).

Curiously, reactive astrocytes autonomously generate the bulk of these stimulating chemicals, which then activate additional astrocytes and perpetuate the glial scar through self-perpetuating processes (Ridet et al. 1997). In addition, reactive astrocytes are frequently implicated in blocking axon regeneration within and outside of the injury site because of their increased synthesis of inhibitory extracellular matrix molecules (ECMs), including keratan sulfate proteoglycans (KSPGs) and chondroitin sulfate proteoglycans (CSPGs) (Morgenstern et al. 2002; Fitch and Silver 2008). Astrogliosis is linked to the onset and durability of SCI-NP. Astrocytic aquaporin-4 (AQP4) are related to below-level SCI-NP. AQP4 is involved in neuronal excitability, microglial activation and the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines, indicating that AQP4 upregulation could affect central sensitization of the L4/5 spinal dorsal horn (SDH) (Song et al. 2024) (Fig. 2). After SCI, astrocyte connexin 43 (Cx43), which functions as a key mediator of chronic pain and CNS inflammation, plays a critical role in the development of chronic neuropathic pain (Chen et al. 2012) (Fig. 2). TrkB.T1, the only isoform of tyrosine receptor kinase B (trkB) receptors present on astrocytes, demonstrates a role in exacerbating neuropathic pain and neurological impairment after SCI (Matyas et al. 2017) (Fig. 2).

Reactive astrocytes, which make up the main part of glial scarring, are acknowledged as harmful (Silver and Miller 2004). Nevertheless, recent research has shown convincing evidence that reactive astrocytes play vital roles in inhibiting inflammation after SCI (Hamby and Sofroniew 2010; White and Jakeman). The targeted expansion of juxtavascular astrocytes serves to restore the integrity of the blood–brain barrier (BBB) and limit the propagation of inflammation (Bush et al. 1999; Voskuhl et al. 2009; Bardehle et al. 2013). Transected axons of SCI do not regenerate in the fully developed CNS. Astrocytic scars are commonly believed to be the cause of this failure. Contrary to popular perception, it has been shown that astrocyte scarring promotes rather than inhibits the CNS’s ability to regenerate axons (Lukovic et al. 2014; Anderson et al. 2016). Glial scarring is therefore an essential physiological response in CNS that aids in the restoration of homeostasis. Rebuilding cells and repairing the BBB are aided by isolating healthy tissue from the original wound that is bleeding and necrotic. This prevents tissue damage, cell death, and an amplification of the inflammatory response from happening during the secondary injury (Faulkner et al. 2004).

Microglia React to SCI-NP

Following SCI, microglia become active and perform a crucial function in triggering neuropathic pain. Numerous ways have been proposed to activate microglia, and each process is thought to have a unique influence on neuropathic pain (Shen et al. 2019; Sideris-Lampretsas and Malcangio 2021). On day 3 after SCI, microglia occurred. By day 7 after SCI, the whole microglial population peaked. Two weeks after SCI, there was a drop in the total relative microglial population in the spinal cord, but reactivation of microglia (Li et al. 2022a) (Fig. 3). After SCI, one of their primary roles is to cause inflammation, which may lead to neuropathic pain (Chen et al. 2018). The involvement of microglia in the progression of neuropathic pain after SCI primarily occurs through the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines and chemokines.

Initially, when microglia become active, they release pro-inflammatory cytokines. These cytokines play a part in the formation of neuropathic pain through various mechanisms (Shen et al. 2019). The presence of inflammation can activate other cells, such as astrocytes, which in turn intensifies neuropathic pain (Liddelow et al. 2020). Microglia contribute to central sensitization following SCI by triggering the NLRP3 inflammasome (Grace et al. 2016). Therefore, targeting the NLRP3 inflammasome has been proposed as a potential strategy to mitigate the extent of SCI in mice (Jiang et al. 2017) (Fig. 2). Activation of microglia via the TLR4/MyD88/NF-κB inflammatory pathway leads to the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines that activate and sensitize spinal nociceptive neurons, thereby exacerbating neuropathic pain (Li et al. 2022b) (Fig. 2). Multiple researches have demonstrated the essential role of microglia in the development of glial scars after SCI. The interplay between activated astrocytes and microglia in the glial scar is mediated by fibronectin. The reduction of microglia impairs the proliferation of astrocytes and the development of astrocytic scar, leading to the propagation of inflammation through the partial inhibition of STAT3 phosphorylation in astrocytes following spinal cord injury (Yoshizaki et al. 2021; Zhou et al. 2023). They also have the ability to produce IGF-1, which, 2-week post-injury, promotes the movement and growth of astrocytes, assisting in the development of scars (Bellver-Landete et al. 2019) (Fig. 2). The release of excitotoxic molecules (such as glutamate) by microglia is a vital process that causes and maintains SCI-NP (Umebayashi et al. 2014) (Fig. 2). The patient’s age and gender could potentially impact the balance of microglial responses in neuropathic pain following a deep brain injury. Previous research has indicated that male rats, as opposed to female rats, have a higher number of microglia in spinal cord and a more prominent inflammatory response after SCI. In humans with SCI, females are more likely to experience severe pain. One research investigated sex differences in male and female rats with photochemical (laser) SCI at T13; females had stronger mechanical sensitivity than men (Dominguez et al. 2012; Lee et al. 2023b). In contrast, another research found that following severe T8 contusion SCI, more male rats than female rats had at-level mechanical hypersensitivity (Hubscher et al. 2010). This may be due to the use of specific injury models and pain tests. Another speculation is that neuropathic pain in male and female rodents may have different underlying cellular mechanisms (Sorge et al. 2015; Tawfik et al. 2020).

Microglia can also assist in the mitigation of inflammation and promote tissue regeneration following SCI. They remove cellular debris and dead cells, thereby safeguarding neurons and assisting in tissue regeneration (Zhou et al. 2020). During the initial phases of SCI, microglia may absorb injured and deteriorating tissues and conduct phagocytosis successfully while maintaining their viability (Greenhalgh and David 2014). Within 30 days after SCI, more microglia cells with effective phagocytic activity may help preserve the integrity of the brain tissue without adversely compromising functional recovery (Akhmetzyanova et al. 2018). Activation of GLP-1R has been demonstrated to attenuate microglia-induced neuroinflammation after SCI. Stimulation of GLP-1R in microglia blocks the generation of reactive astrocytes (Qian et al. 2022) (Fig. 2). Microglia also synthesize compounds that possess anti-inflammatory properties, therefore mitigating the detrimental effect on cells such as astrocytes (Ahmad et al. 2021). A previous investigation has demonstrated that newborn mice have the ability to repair wounds without scarring and to spontaneously regenerate axons following SCI (Li et al. 2020b). Another study has shown that microglia are essential for the healing process following SCI and help coordinate the response to damage in glial cells residing in CNS and the immune cells that infiltrate the affected area. A reduction in microglia numbers exacerbates intensifies tissue harm and deteriorates functional recuperation. In contrast, restoring certain signaling pathways in microglia discovered using sequencing data in mice with reduced microglia prevents further harm and supports the process of healing. Additional bioinformatics analyses have indicated that the most effective healing following SCI could be accomplished using crucial ligand–receptor interactions involving microglia, astrocytes, and monocyte-derived macrophages (MDMs) (Brennan et al. 2022).

Treatments of SCI-NP

Pain is a prevalent secondary syndrome observed in individuals with SCI and its consequences are substantial. The primary issue is that pain following SCI is frequently intricate and influenced by several factors (Shiao and Lee-Kubli 2018). Furthermore, a correlation was identified in a cross-sectional study involving SCI patients, indicating a link between pain and the subjective evaluations of their cognitive, physical, and emotional capabilities (Murray et al. 2007). Chronic pain has a strong correlation with depression, chronic tiredness, and a diminished quality of life (Craig et al. 2013). Neuropathic pain is acknowledged to be long-lasting and resistant to many of the current therapies available. Recent studies show that after a year following their injury, between 30 and 50% of people with SCI-NP (Siddall et al. 2003). Pharmacological Treatment

Treatment options for SCI-NP include anticonvulsants, antidepressants, and both light and powerful opioid medications (Cardenas and Jensen 2006; Kupfer and Formal 2022). There are many methods of administration for SCI-NP, and in addition to intravenous drip administration, more invasive techniques such as intrathecal administration may be considered (Middleton et al. 1996; Siddall et al. 2000; Saulino 2007; Karri et al. 2022).

Analgesic

Opioids are one of the most commonly used painkillers in clinical practice and are mostly used in the treatment of intractable pain after SCI due to their analgesic effect, but they have been controversial in the treatment of SCI-NP due to their many side effects (Baastrup and Finnerup 2008; Finnerup and Baastrup 2012; Boldt et al. 2014; Bryce 2018). Morphine is a strong opioid. Siddall et al. investigated the effectiveness of intrathecal morphine and clonidine for the treatment of SCI-NP (Siddall et al. 2000, 2003). However, some experimental animal studies have found that morphine given during the acute phase of spinal cord injury decreases motor function recovery, increases pain response, and significantly increases cell loss at the site of injury (Hook et al. 2017). Therefore, the use of morphine should be carefully selected. Norrbrink et al. found in a trial that tramadol may reduce pain intensity while remaining relatively cost-effective. However, like other opioids, it is linked with undesirable side effects and may create dependency and withdrawal symptoms (Norrbrink 2009). Finnerup et al. discovered in a randomized controlled trial that Lidocaine decreased neuropathic pain at and below the level of damage, regardless of the presence or absence of evoked pain. Because Lidocaine, an anti-arrhythmics with sodium channel-blocking capabilities, might be a therapy option for SCI-NP (Abram 2006). Ketamine, an NMDA receptor antagonist, is used to alleviate neuropathic pain that is difficult to control. A study had shown that ketamine had a significant reducing effect on SCI-NP (Wang et al. 2022). Capsaicin can bind to TRPV1 receptors in the body, which are usually activated when heat or pain is felt. Capsaicin then depletes the TRPV1 pain receptor transmission mediator. Sandford et al. found that topical capsaicin should be explored in the treatment of localized radicular pain due to SCI (Sandford and Benes 2000).

Anticonvulsant

Anticonvulsants are routinely used drugs to treat SCI-NP in clinical practice, and the commonly used ones are gabapentin, pregabalin, lamotrigine, etc. Among them, gabapentin and pregabalin have remarkable efficacy in the treatment of SCI-NP (Davari et al. 2020). A research proposed that lamotrigine had an analgesic effect in SCI patients with incomplete lesions and evoked pain in the region with spontaneous pain (Finnerup et al. 2002).

Antidepressants

Antidepressants have been shown to reduce a variety of neuropathic pain conditions, and the tricyclic antidepressants amitriptyline and duloxetine are currently common in clinical practice. Vranken et al. found that duloxetine and venlafaxine did not have a significant impact on SCI-NP, as evidenced in the research (Vranken et al. 2011). The efficacy of tricyclic antidepressants such as amitriptyline seems to be debated. One research found amitriptyline is useful in treating SCI-NP (Rintala et al. 2007), while another study noted that amitriptyline had no major analgesic effect (Cardenas et al. 2002).

Antispasmodic

Baclofen, a GABA analogue that works on ubiquitous GABAB receptors, is commonly utilized as an anti-spasmodic drug. Intrathecal baclofen (ITB) has analgesic effects on all subtypes of neuropathic pain and may alleviate interference between neuropathic pain and activities of daily life (Kumru 2018). Botulinum toxin is commonly used to treat spasticity or dystonia. A research revealed that botulinum toxin type A (BTX-A) is beneficial for the treatment of neuropathic pain in patients with SCI (Han et al. 2016).

Stimulation Techniques

Spinal cord stimulation (SCS) has demonstrated efficacy in decreasing opioid use and enhancing functionality in individuals suffering from diverse pain disorders. This is particularly significant given the prevailing opioid addiction and abuse crisis. In contrast to pharmaceutical usage, SCS has no systemic adverse effects and may be altered and reversed, unlike neuroablation. Because of these features, SCS is a very good option for treating SCI-NP (Huang et al. 2019; Chalif et al. 2024). Following nerve damage, spinal adenosine A1 and A3 receptors (A1R and A3R) signaling may both provide tonic suppression and help SCS-induced inhibition of spinal nociceptive transmission (Cui et al. 2024). Research has shown that animals with SCI-NP experience the most pronounced pain relief from motor-targeted intraspinal microstimulation (ISMS). The analgesic benefits of ISMS, which are designed to improve voluntary motor function, extend to patterns of neuronal transmission at the population level (McPherson and Bandres 2023). Additionally, the study showed that photobiomodulation (PBM) inhibits activation of the NF-κB pathway and reduces CXCL10 expression in astrocytes and microglia (Zhang et al. 2023).

Surgery

Neurosurgical methods often used to treat pain can effectively alleviate SCI-NP. For example, dorsal rhizotomy, which involves damaging the dorsal root entry zone (DREZ), can decrease excessive nerve signals that reach the dorsal horn neurons at the site of damage. This approach typically leads to a substantial alleviation (> 50%) of neuropathic pain comparable to the level of the injury. On the other hand, controversial surgical techniques like cordotomy or cordectomy that cause direct injury to the spinal cord in order to block the transmission of pain-related signals (Tasker et al. 1992).

Inhibitions

It has been demonstrated that blocking T-type calcium channels decreases the occurrence of spontaneous continuing pain in rats with SCI, indicating that T-type channels play a significant role in the development of SCI-NP with potential therapeutic implications (Liu et al. 2023). Treatment with lipoxin A4 (LXA4) significantly reduces the degree of mechanical pain hypersensitivity as well as the levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines and microglial markers expressed in the spinal cord after SCI (Martini et al. 2016). In a prior work, the acute phase rapamycin treatment was shown to dramatically decrease microglial activation in the lumbar spinal cord, therefore improving locomotor function as well as mechanical and thermal sensitivities after SCI. This prevented subsequent neural tissue damage (Tateda et al. 2017). Reducing the concentrations of chemokine (C–C motif) ligand 2 (CCL2) in microglia might relieve SCI-NP (Li et al. 2023). Resolvin D2 (RvD2) belongs to the D class of resolvins and is derived from docosahexaenoic acid (DHA); it can reduce inflammation. Treatment with RvD2 can restore the autophagy process by inhibiting the production of miR-155, which is regulated by NF-κB. This results in the stimulation of anti-inflammatory microglia, leading to the inhibition of the inflammatory reaction and the relief of SCI-NP (Yang et al. 2023). Treatment with 17β-estradiol alleviates below-level SCI-NP by inhibiting microglia and astrocyte activation at the L4–L5 lumbar spinal dorsal horn (Lee et al. 2018). ONO-2506 is a new substance that reduces the activation of astrocytes by inhibiting the generation of S100B from astrocytes. It has been shown that ONO-2506 effectively prevents further damage and enhances motor function following SCI (Ishiguro et al. 2019). Following SCI, osteopontin (OPN), a strongly acidic protein released by various cells, provides protection against neuropathic pain and motor impairment. OPN stimulates angiogenesis after SCI, which may be part of the reason for OPN’s advantageous effects on SCI recovery (Weng et al. 2024). FTY720 improves post-traumatic allodynia by controlling neuroinflammation, protecting the integrity of the BBB, and preventing the creation of glial scars. This helps maintain the functionality of the descending inhibitory circuit and reduces neuropathic pain (Yamazaki et al. 2020).

Blood Therapy

Behroozi showed that platelet-rich plasma effectively decreased SCI-NP by modulating ATP signaling (Behroozi et al. 2021). In microglial cell lines, DHA, a crucial omega-3 fatty acid for the development and functioning of the nervous system, suppresses the generation of pro-inflammatory cytokines. It has been suggested that systemic DHA therapy might stop the development of SCI-NP (Georgieva et al. 2019).

Cell Therapy

Sertoli cells (SCs) are a desirable option because of their proven ability to secrete growth factors and their immunosuppressive impact. There is evidence showing that SCs reduce TRPC6 expression and probably decrease calcium uptake to reduce activation of microglia (Rahimi et al. 2022).

Exercise Training

Less than 10% of SCI rats acquire at-level allodynia after early exercise treatment, compared to approximately 40% of non-exercised rats (Detloff et al. 2014). Additionally, it has been proposed that using adipose-derived stem cells in conjunction with exercise is advantageous for the initial restoration of motor function and may help to partly alleviate SCI-NP (Cheng et al. 2023). Greater pain reductions were seen by certain users of the VRWalk interactive virtual reality (VR) walking interface, which enables patients with SCI-NP to manipulate a virtual gait and engage with the virtual environment (Trost et al. 2022). Intensive Locomotor Training (ILT) is beneficial for relieving neuropathic pain consequent to traumatic SCI (Dugan and Sagen 2015; Chhaya et al. 2019). The combination of ILT with GABAergic neural progenitor cell transplantation substantially amplifies the alleviation of neuropathic pain across many assessment criteria, including tests for allodynia, hyperalgesia, and persistent pain (Dugan et al. 2020).

Directions for Future Attention About SCI-NP

Previous research on SCI treatments in existing laboratories has focused on cellular sources of injury repair (new neurons) and glial scarring for axonal regeneration (inhibition of astrogliosis), which have laid important groundwork for SCI-NP. Gene therapy, as a new approach to SCI, might now provide some new directions for thinking about the treatment of SCI-NP. In vivo neural reprogramming has emerged as a promising technique that has the potential to produce new, functioning neurons from glial cells found in CNS. This is achieved by increasing the expression of neurogenic transcription factors (Wu et al. 2020). In both stably wounded brains and a mouse model of Alzheimer disease, reactive astrocytes can be transformed into fully functional neurons by the transcription factor NeuroD1, which is involved in neurogenesis (Guo et al. 2014). NeuroD1-based AAV-based gene therapy would help to regenerate SCI by turning internal glial cells into neurons (Puls et al. 2020). A question arises as to whether in vivo neuronal reprogramming could also play a role in SCI-NP. Scientists have developed a gene therapy that uses lentiviral vectors to deliver neurotrophic factor and other molecules into spinal cord neurons—a specific subpopulation of neurons involved in natural spinal cord repair—to promote their axon growth, traversal, and guidance. Within 4 weeks of receiving the treatment, 90% of the mice with complete SCI regained the ability to walk on their hind limbs and exhibited a gait comparable to that of mice with partial SCI. Using cutting-edge gene-editing technology, the research team successfully activated damaged neurons, reconnecting them and restoring function (Anderson et al. 2018; Squair et al. 2023).

The above studies prove that gene therapy can treat SCI. There is also research support for gene therapy for neuropathic pain relief. Gene therapy has become more appealing in recent years. Researchers administered a harmless adenovirus containing a pair of transgenes that encode GABA, an inhibitory neurotransmitter, to mice with SCI-NP. By employing this gene therapy, it was feasible to confine the administration and manifestation of the transgenic fragments GAD65 and VGAT are only applied to the mice’s sciatic nerve damage site. Effectively, this containment prevented certain unwanted consequences, such as loss of normal feeling or motor weakening. Mice’s pain signaling neurons were demonstrably suppressed by the transgenically generated GABA, and this suppression persisted for at least 2.5 months after treatment (Tadokoro et al. 2022). Therefore, a potential strategy for treating neuropathic pain may include blocking the inflammatory activation of glial cells or the expression of associated genes during glial cell activation in accordance with the mechanism of SCI-NP.

Conclusion

Research on SCI-NP remains a top priority to inform the development of new treatment methods for clinical use. While there have been some advancements in detecting neuropathic pain that occurs after SCI and understanding the exact processes behind it, only a limited number of treatment methods targeting these specific mechanisms have been used in clinical practice. As our understanding of the role and importance of glial cells in neuropathic pain due to SCI has greatly increased in recent decades, we can discover more therapeutic targets against SCI-NP through glia.
