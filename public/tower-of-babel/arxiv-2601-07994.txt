DyCP: Dynamic Context Pruning for Long-Form Dialogue with LLMs
Nayoung Choi, Jonathan Zhang, Jinho D. Choi

Abstract
Large Language Models (LLMs) increasingly operate over long-form dialogues with frequent topic shifts. While recent LLMs support extended context windows, efficient management of dialogue history in practice is needed due to inference cost and latency constraints. We present DyCP , a lightweight context management method implemented outside the LLM that dynamically identifies and retrieves relevant dialogue segments conditioned on the current turn, without offline memory construction. DyCP manages dialogue context while preserving the sequential nature of dialogue without predefined topic boundaries, enabling adaptive and efficient context selection. Across three long-form dialogue benchmarks— LoCoMo , MT-Bench+ , and SCM4LLMs —and multiple LLM backends, DyCP achieves competitive answer quality in downstream generation, with more selective context usage and improved inference efficiency.

Introduction

Large Language Models (LLMs)-powered dialogue systems support long-form, open-domain conversations that span hundreds of turns, where users frequently shift topics and revisit earlier subjects over the course of interaction. To preserve contextual coherence, many systems prepend the entire dialogue history to each new user utterance. While this approach helps maintain dialogue continuity, it introduces growing overhead. Longer contexts increase inference costs—whether in GPU resources or API usage—and slow down response latency. As the context expands, the model also faces a
needle-in-a-haystack
problem
7
, where identifying relevant information (the
needle
) becomes difficult, as key details are often buried in the surrounding dialogue (the
haystack
).

To mitigate these issues, alongside ongoing efforts to improve LLMs’ intrinsic long-context handling capabilities, prior work has explored external context management strategies, including summarization
(
20
;
8
)
and retrieval of past turns, either at the turn level
(
12
)
or at the segment level
(
14
;
11
)
. However, these external approaches involve trade-offs that introduce practical challenges. Summarization may omit key details necessary for the current turn. Although turn-level retrieval is query-targeted and efficient, it can disrupt coherence by ignoring discourse continuity and dependencies. Segment-level retrieval better preserves context by grouping related turns into topic-level segments, but existing methods assume fixed topic boundaries defined based on past dialogue context through pre-segmentation. In practice, however, these boundaries may vary with the current user utterance, suggesting that segmentation should instead be query-dependent. In addition, pre-segmentation commonly depends on extra LLM calls, which can increase overall cost.

Related Work

Long-Context Capabilities of LLMs
While recent LLMs increasingly support longer context windows as model scale grows, long-context processing has also benefited from advances in fine-tuning and inference techniques.
5
propose a fine-tuning method that extends the model’s positional encoding to support longer contexts while preserving original performance. Building on this,
15
enhance fine-tuning efficiency, enabling models to handle up to 128k tokens with significantly less training. Furthermore,
1
introduce an inference-time approach that chunks the input text and links the segments, allowing long-context processing without any additional training.

External Context Management Methods
Alongside advances in model-side long-context capabilities, a line of work has focused on managing dialogue history outside the model by reducing the amount of context referenced at inference time. To evaluate context management strategies in long-form open-domain conversations,
12
introduces the
LoCoMo
benchmark and shows that turn-level history retrieval produces better responses than feeding the entire dialogue history to LLMs.
11
presents the
MT-Bench+
benchmark and proposes
MemoChat
, a segment-level retrieval approach that leverages LLMs for both segmentation and retrieval.
14
demonstrates that segment-level retrieval outperforms turn-level and session-level methods, and presents
SeCom
, which combines LLM-based segmentation, lightweight retrieval, and context denoising.
19
introduces the
SCM4LLMs
benchmark and proposes a turn-level retrieval mechanism composed of multiple LLM-based agents, including memory summarization and memory controller modules. In addition,
21
present
CondMem
, a hybrid method that combines summarization-based dialogue compression with selective memory construction.

KadaneDial

In
KadaneDial
, the classic Kadane’s algorithm (
L7
–
L14
) is adapted to identify salient context segments in long-form dialogue. The relevance score sequence
\mathbf{S}
is reinterpreted as a one-dimensional signal, where consecutive high-scoring regions indicate discourse segments that are locally coherent and relevant to the current turn. To account for variation in score distributions across heterogeneous dialogues and queries, we standardize the relevance signal using z-score normalization and apply a gain shift controlled by
\tau
(
L1
–
L4
). Here,
z_{i}
is the normalized relevance signal at turn
i
, and
g_{i}=z_{i}-\tau
measures its excess over a threshold
\tau
: positive values increase a span’s cumulative gain, while negative values decrease it. The algorithm then iteratively identifies additional spans whose cumulative gain
\mathbb{G}
(i.e., the score sum of the best segment in each iteration) exceeds a stopping threshold
\theta
. This design reflects a conservative strategy that prioritizes recall, favoring the inclusion of borderline-relevant context over the risk of omitting critical context, while preserving the sequential nature of dialogue.

4.2.1
Growth of LLM Context Windows

Modern LLMs have steadily expanded their context limits over successive generations. As shown in Figure
2
, GPT models have grown from about 1K tokens in GPT-2 to up to 1M tokens in recent GPT-4 models. However, prior experiments such as
MemoChat
,
SCM4LLMs
, and
SeCom
were constrained to GPT-3.5–Turbo–level models (4k tokens), which cannot ingest the full dialogue history of long-form benchmarks—for example, even one-third of a single
LoCoMo
dialogue exceeds this limit. Thus, any
Full Context
baseline in those settings would have required truncation. Although
CondMem
uses GPT-4 for memory construction, it was not evaluated on publicly released long-form dialogue benchmarks, and its
Full Context
behavior remains unclear. In contrast, we conduct comparative experiments using modern models that support at least 128k tokens, enabling the entire dialogue history of all benchmarks to be included without truncation and allowing a direct comparison between long-context LLMs and context-management methods.

Offline and preprocessing cost.
Several methods require LLM calls before inference to construct or update memory.

4.3
Evaluation Metrics

We evaluate dialogue context management methods using three metrics: Answer Quality, Response Latency, and Retrieval Accuracy.

Answer Quality
We adopt GPT4Score, following prior work
11
;
14
;
21
, where GPT-4 acts as an automatic judge and assigns a score from 1 to 100 based on how well the model response answers the user question. Prior studies typically score responses using a related subset of the dialogue history as evidence for the current question. In contrast, we provide the full dialogue history up to the current turn and include the benchmark gold response as the reference for correctness (Figure
3
), allowing the judge to compare against the target answer directly. All methods are evaluated under the same prompt. To assess how well GPT4Score aligns with human judgment, we conduct a small-scale human evaluation (Section
4.5.2
). Given its limited scope, we treat the results as a sanity check rather than a definitive validation.

For the
LoCoMo
benchmark, which contains short, factual gold responses, we further report Exact Match (EM) and ROUGE
10
. For EM, we consider a prediction correct if the model response contains the gold response string. ROUGE is computed based on n-gram overlap between the model response and the gold response. These metrics are omitted for other benchmarks due to the length and verbosity of their gold responses, which limits the reliability of traditional lexical matching.

Response Latency
We measure online first-token latency—the time from receiving the current user utterance to generating the first token of the model response—to quantify the additional latency introduced by each memory management method. To observe when the first token is generated, we call all LLMs in streaming mode.

Retrieval Accuracy
We use Hit@k, Recall@k, and Precision@k to evaluate how accurately each method retrieves the context relevant to the current turn. As only
LoCoMo
benchmark provides ground-truth annotations of relevant context for each test question, we use
LoCoMo
for this evaluation. Each metric is defined as follows:

•

Hit@k
indicates whether any gold context appears in the top-k results.

•

Recall@k
is the proportion of gold contexts covered by the top-k results.

•

Precision@k
is the proportion of the top-k results that include gold context.

4.4
Experimental Details

For the implementation of
DyCP
, we set the gain threshold
\tau
to 0.6 and the stopping threshold
\theta
to 1.0 as hyperparameters of the
KadaneDial
. These values were chosen based on preliminary qualitative inspection of retrieved context spans and were found to produce stable pruning behavior. Because the relevance scores are z-score normalized before span selection, the thresholds operate on a standardized scale. As a result,
DyCP
is not overly sensitive to the specific numerical choices of
\tau
and
\theta
, and we therefore use the same configuration across all datasets.

To ensure a fair comparison in retrieval-based baselines, we match the total number of retrieved turns across methods wherever applicable. For segment-level approaches such as
SeCom
, we align their retrieval volume with that of
DyCP
by matching the product of TpS and RS reported in Table
7
. Concretely, this corresponds to retrieving the top 5 turns on
LoCoMo
, 2 on
SCM4LLMs
, and 3 on
MT-Bench+
.
MemoChat
performs automatic segment retrieval and therefore does not require specifying a fixed
k
. For turn-level retrievers such as
SCM4LLMs
and
CondMem
, we follow the original papers and use
k{=}5
and
k{=}3
, respectively, for all benchmarks. In all methods, retrieved items are reordered chronologically (i.e., according to their original dialogue order) before being provided to the conversational agent.

All experiments with API-based LLMs were conducted using a single NVIDIA RTX A6000 GPU to run retriever, while experiments involving open-source LLMs were run locally on four NVIDIA RTX A6000 GPUs.

4.5
Result

4.5.1
Answer Quality & Response Latency

We evaluate
DyCP
on three long-form benchmarks in a primary API-based setting using five LLMs. As shown in Table
5
,
DyCP
achieves strong answer quality while consistently exhibiting low response latency. Overall,
DyCP
matches or improves upon the
Full Context
baseline in answer quality across most agents and benchmarks, with clearer gains on GPT-4o, Claude 3.7, and GPT-4o mini. As models become stronger at long-context understanding (e.g., GPT-4.1 and Claude 4.0), the quality gap between
DyCP
and
Full Context
narrows, while
DyCP
continues to provide substantial efficiency gains.

As discussed in Section
4.2.1
, prior studies primarily evaluated LLMs with shorter context windows (e.g., 4k-token limit), where
Full Context
typically required truncation. In contrast, our setting uses modern long-context LLMs that can ingest the entire history, which strengthens the
Full Context
baseline and can narrow the quality gap with context-management methods. Differences in evaluation protocols (Section
4.3
), specifically the design of judging prompts with a stronger focus on answer correctness, may also contribute to these shifts in relative performance.

The latency benefits of
DyCP
stem from its design, which avoids additional LLM calls for memory construction or retrieval refinement, as introduced in Section
4.2.2
. By minimizing both the number of LLM calls and the input size of each call,
DyCP
reduces repeated reprocessing of dialogue history and supports efficient context management for long conversations.

Figure
4
illustrates the quality–latency trade-off, where
DyCP
consistently appears in the region corresponding to relatively high quality and low latency. This trend is more noticeable on
LoCoMo
, which contains the longest conversations in terms of turns. The key distinction of our method lies in its departure from existing segmentation and retrieval paradigms. Unlike approaches based on pre-segmentation or fixed top-
k
retrieval,
DyCP
dynamically segments and selects context for the current turn in a single pass, enabling flexible, structure-aware context selection. Further analysis is provided in Section
4.5.3
.

For the smaller GPT-4o mini model, answer quality decreases relative to GPT-4o while latency is reduced, with similar overall trends. For newer models, GPT-4.1 and Claude 4.0 show improved long-context handling; for example, on
LoCoMo
, GPT-4.1 improves over GPT-4o from 24.23 to 33.67 in EM and from 75.13 to 92.06 in GPT4Score. Under GPT-4.1, the
Full Context
condition achieves performance comparable to
DyCP
. Meanwhile,
DyCP
remains more than twice as fast (0.97s vs. 2.41s) and uses substantially fewer input tokens (Table
4
), indicating that selective context management remains important even as long-context LLMs improve.

4.5.3
Retrieval Accuracy

To better understand how each context management method identifies relevant context, we compare their retrieval accuracy in Table
6
among methods that incorporate a retriever.
DyCP
consistently outperforms both
SCM4LLMs
and
SeCom
, regardless of the retriever used. Interestingly, while
SCM4LLMs
achieves slightly higher retrieval accuracy than
SeCom
(both using the top 5 retrieved results on the
LoCoMo
benchmark),
SeCom
demonstrates comparable or even better answer quality, as shown in Table
5
. This suggests that segment-level retrieval—which better preserves the sequential nature and coherence of the previous context—may contribute to improved downstream generation quality.

A key difference among segment-level methods lies in how segment boundaries are constructed.
SeCom
and
MemoChat
segment the dialogue
offline
before the current turn, which limits query adaptivity and can merge relevant and irrelevant turns within the same segment. In contrast,
DyCP
constructs segments
on demand
based on relevance dynamics, allowing boundaries to shift with the current query. Table
7
reports descriptive statistics of segmentation and retrieval behavior (Turns per Segment, TpS; Retrieved Segments, RS).
DyCP
tends to use finer-grained segmentation on long, multi-topic dialogues (e.g.,
LoCoMo
) and adjusts retrieval volume more flexibly across turns. TpS is not uniformly smaller across datasets: when relevant evidence spans a longer coherent stretch (e.g., in
SCM4LLMs
),
DyCP
may retain longer contiguous spans rather than forcing shorter segments. Retrieval accuracy (Table
6
) directly measures segment–query alignment, while answer quality (Table
5
) captures its downstream effect.

4.6
Open-Source LLMs Evaluation

We further examine whether
DyCP
generalizes to open-source long-context models. We also include
CondMem
in this setting; as discussed in Section
4.2.2
, its preprocessing requires extensive LLM calls, making large-scale API-based evaluation costly in practice. We conduct experiments on Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct (1M context) and Mistral-Nemo-Instruct (128k context).

As shown in Table
8
,
DyCP
consistently achieves the strong answer quality across all three benchmarks, outperforming both
Full Context
and
CondMem
. While
DyCP
maintains low and stable online latency,
CondMem
shows substantially higher latency with large variance due to its iterative retrieval-refinement process. Although
CondMem
reports high ROUGE-1/2/L scores on Qwen, these gains are largely driven by keyword overlap: the method often reproduces short 1–2-gram fragments from the gold answer without providing the correct factual content. This inflates ROUGE despite low semantic accuracy, as reflected in both EM and GPT4Score. These results demonstrate that
DyCP
remains effective across models, including smaller open-source LLMs.

While locally deployed open-source models may benefit from reusing cached KV states across previous turns, many practical serving setups still recompute full-history prompts (e.g., when the model is accessed via an internal API). In such cases,
DyCP
can be particularly useful by reducing the effective input length. We further discuss these caching considerations in Section
8
. Here, we show that
DyCP
generalizes to open-source long-context models.

5
Analysis

To gain insight into when and how context pruning improves long-form dialogue, we analyze three factors. First, we compare GPT-4o and GPT-4.1 to assess how enhanced long-context capabilities in recent LLMs affect the benefits of pruning. We attribute observed shifts in performance to dialogue-level characteristics, such as dialogue length and the position of relevant context (Section
5.1
). Second, to understand what makes pruning effective, we examine different retrieval configurations, focusing on the trade-off between recall and precision (Section
5.2
). Third, we investigate whether continuity-preserving turns—those with low but positive relevance—meaningfully contribute to answer quality. To this end, we perform an ablation study that progressively removes the least-relevant turns within the segments selected by
DyCP
, analyzing how systematically dropping these turns affects downstream performance (Section
5.3
). All analyses in this section are conducted on the
LoCoMo
benchmark.

5.2
Recall or Precision?

To understand what makes pruning effective, we examine how different retrieval configurations influence the trade-off between recall and precision, and how this trade-off, in turn, affects both answer quality and latency. Figure
9
shows the relationship between retrieval recall, precision, and final answer quality across different retrieval configurations in
DyCP
.

We observe a strong positive correlation between recall and answer quality: as recall increases, from Top-1 to Dynamic selection, answer quality steadily improves. This trend suggests that ensuring the inclusion of relevant content (high recall), even at the cost of introducing some mildly irrelevant information (lower precision), is critical for downstream answer generation. However, further increases in recall yield diminishing improvements, indicating that retrieving additional segments beyond a certain point offers limited value. Furthermore, because higher recall typically increases response latency (Figure
10
), the goal is not simply to maximize recall, but to identify the point at which additional context no longer contributes meaningful gains.

These observations also help explain why the concept underlying

expands the retrieved span only when the relevance dynamics justify it—adding segments only when sustained positive gain is detected. When relevance drops, the method stops expanding the span, preventing unnecessary context accumulation. This form of adaptive control maintains high recall where it matters while avoiding the latency penalty associated with uniformly larger contexts. In this sense, the concept of
DyCP
provides a principled mechanism for balancing recall and efficiency in long-form, multi-topic dialogue.

5.3
Ablation on Continuity-Preserving Turns

To evaluate the contribution of low-relevance but continuity-preserving turns in
DyCP
, we conduct an ablation study that progressively removes the least-relevant turns from each selected segment while measuring the resulting changes in answer quality and latency. These removed turns correspond to low but positive relevance scores—turns that are not directly needed for answering the current user query but help maintain local continuity and discourse flow within the segment. When removal would leave a segment empty, we retain the single most relevant turn to ensure that each segment preserves at least one anchor point.

Figure
11
shows that removing these turns leads to small but consistent drops in answer quality, indicating that even weakly relevant turns contribute useful contextual signals that support downstream generation. Latency decreases slightly as more turns are removed, as expected from the shorter input. Overall, these turns appear to help maintain a coherent local context, and the mild quality drop suggests that the pruning level can be tuned to balance efficiency and quality under different deployment budgets.

6
Discussion

We raise the question:
"Do LLMs meaningfully process far less context than their actual input token limits?"
While models like GPT
-
4o support up to 128k tokens, our results indicate that they struggle to fully utilize even 25k tokens, especially when relevant information appears earlier in the input. This observation is consistent with prior findings such as
2
, who show that effective context lengths often fall short, typically not exceeding half of their training lengths. Interestingly, GPT
-
4.1, with its 1M token limit, handles the same 25k-token dialogues much more reliably. This contrast suggests that token limits may reflect throughput capacity rather than effective working memory, and that in practice, models may attend to only a smaller, attention-weighted subset of the input. Several factors may contribute to this discrepancy, including the challenge of learning long-range dependencies during training, position-sensitive encoding, and attention saturation. Thus, while token limits continue to grow, effective long-context understanding may remain limited, both due to architectural bottlenecks and practical concerns such as inference cost.

7
Conclusion

In this work, we present
DyCP
, a dynamic context pruning method for long-form dialogue that selects query-relevant segments in real time, without pre-segmentation or incurring additional LLM calls. Across three benchmarks,
DyCP
consistently shows strong answer quality while significantly reducing response latency. Our findings show that, high recall is more beneficial than precision for retrieval, and preserving the sequential nature of dialogue plays a key role in response generation. We further show that, despite ongoing improvements in long-context LLMs, context management remains important for effective control of latency and inference cost. We offer a simple yet effective segment-level context management strategy that is dynamic, scalable, and LLM-agnostic, making it well-suited for real-world deployment. 8
Limitations and Practical Considerations

Our goal is to study a lightweight, retriever-based strategy for reducing the effective input length of long-form dialogue history at inference time. While
DyCP
consistently reduces the number of prepended context tokens and achieves low first-token latency in our primary API-based setting, several deployment factors can change the speed–cost trade-off in practice.

KV cache reuse and what latency we measure.

Our latency metric is
first token latency
measured via streaming API calls (Section
4.5.1
). This reflects a common stateless serving pattern where each request is processed independently and developers cannot assume that key–value (KV) attention caches from previous turns are reused across calls. In contrast, in stateful local serving, a
Full Context
strategy can be competitive or even faster because most of the history has already been prefetched and cached; in that case, modifying the input (as
DyCP
does) may invalidate KV reuse and increase the prefill cost, potentially raising the latency. Therefore, our speedups should be interpreted as evidence for settings where KV reuse is unavailable, unreliable, or provider-managed in ways that are not exposed to the developer. A practical next step is to make pruning
cache-aware
, e.g., using
DyCP
when cache reuse is unlikely (e.g., long inter-turn gaps, non-persistent session routing) and falling back to a stable-prefix strategy when cache reuse is strong.

Provider-side prompt caching and billing effects.

Modern API providers may apply
prompt caching
(prefix caching) where repeated prompt prefixes are served more cheaply and/or with lower latency. This mechanism typically requires
exact prefix matches
and operates under provider-defined cache lifetimes. Because
DyCP
dynamically selects different history spans per query, it can reduce cache-hit rates compared to strategies that keep a more stable prefix across turns. Under stable prompt caching, a
Full Context
baseline may become cheaper, and the cost advantage of dynamic pruning can narrow or even reverse. Importantly, these caching policies are external to our method and can vary by provider, model, and request routing, so the realized cost–latency benefits depend on conditions that are not fully controllable by developers. We therefore emphasize that
DyCP
is a reliable way to reduce effective input length and improves response latency in stateless settings, while acknowledging that provider caching can change the monetary and latency calculus in production.

Overhead and robustness of pruning.

DyCP
does not introduce additional
LLM
calls for segmentation or refinement, but it does require (i) storing turn embeddings and (ii) computing a query embedding and relevance scores at each turn. In our setup, this overhead is minimal relative to LLM inference, but it is not zero and it adds system complexity (retriever serving and vector storage). In addition, like other retriever-based approaches,
DyCP
can fail when semantic similarity does not surface the needed evidence. These failure modes also explain cases where
DyCP
underperforms the
Full Context
baseline. Improving the retriever (or applying domain adaptation) is complementary to our contribution and directly improves pruning reliability.

Evaluation and generality.

We use GPT4Score for scalable evaluation and include a small human study as a sanity check. The human evaluation is limited in scale and is intended as a qualitative complement to the automatic evaluation.

When
DyCP
is most useful.

Taken together,
DyCP
is best viewed as a practical context-selection primitive for (a) stateless settings with limited KV reuse and provider-side caching, and (b) deployments where reducing input length is valuable for predictable latency and controllable budgeting.

Missing Critical Turns
The retrieved context omits essential dialogue turns that are required to answer the question. This category includes approximately 5% of cases where the retrieval fails to hit the gold context, i.e., failures in Hit (H) as reported in Table

Partial Recall
While the retrieved segments contain relevant information, they lack full coverage necessary for a complete answer. This often occurs when the answer depends on multiple segments scattered across the dialogue, and not all of them are successfully retrieved. In some cases, the issue is not due to retrieval failure, but rather stems from the subjective nature of the question, where referring to segments other than the gold context could still yield a reasonable answer.

Although the necessary information is included in the retrieved context, the model fails to handle it effectively due to surrounding content. These cases frequently resemble failures observed in the
