TADF‐Active Tetrazole Luminophores for Visible‐Light Functionalization of Nanoparticles and Surfaces
Authors: Ryoga Hojo, Katrina Bergmann, Siyuan L Xie, Connor S Durfy, William L Primrose, Athan T Gogoulis, Seja A Elgadi, Mitchell J Demchuk, Thomas Kalab, Peiqi Hu, Eva M Nichols, Zachary M Hudson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThermally activated delayed fluorescence (TADF) materials have found broad use in technologies spanning displays, bioimaging, and photocatalysis. In parallel, tetrazoles have become powerful “click” reagents for light‐activated, bioorthogonal ligations, yet remain unexplored in reagents with delayed emission lifetimes. Here we report the design and synthesis of TADF‐active tetrazole luminophores with photochemical reactivity for the rapid functionalization of nanoparticles and surfaces. The combination of electron‐rich phenoxazine donors with electron‐deficient tetrazole acceptors yields luminophores with bright emission and tunable TADF lifetimes, governed by the 1,3‐dipolar cycloaddition partner. Cycloaddition with alkene dipolarophiles affords saturated dihydropyrazoles that undergo photoinduced aerobic aromatization to generate pyrazole products exhibiting pronounced turn‐on TADF behavior. Leveraging their charge‐transfer character, we also demonstrate a gold surface modified with TADF luminophores, wherein we propose the first example of reversible visible light‐induced surface adsorption. Furthermore, we demonstrate the surface functionalization of organic nanoparticles using TADF‐active materials. This work introduces a class of functional materials that combine photochemical reactivity with TADF properties, introducing a light‐driven platform for surface functionalization using TADF materials.Keywords: Click Chemistry, Glassy Organic Dots, Photochemistry, Self‐Assembled Monolayers, Thermally Activated Delayed Fluorescence

Introduction

Photo‐induced cycloadditions are a powerful synthetic tool: their ability to install fluorogenic moieties under bioorthogonal conditions makes them valuable across diverse areas of chemistry [1, 2]. Combining click‐like reactivity with precise temporal control, photo‐induced cycloadditions are especially attractive for applications demanding fully organic, metal‐free reagents, such as in optoelectronics and the life sciences [3].

Photo‐induced 1,3‐dipolar cycloadditions featuring N‐phenyl tetrazoles have emerged as versatile reactions that proceed under mild, metal‐free conditions [4, 5]. Since the original report of this transformation by Huisgen and coworkers [6], efforts have been made to enhance reaction rates by modifying the structure of the dipolarophile [7], changing the pH of the reaction [8, 9], and making use of steric effects [10, 11]. Recent studies have also shifted the reactivity of N‐phenyl tetrazoles to the visible range by extending π‐conjugation or appending electron‐donating units to the tetrazole core [5, 12]. The applications of this reaction now expand beyond bioorthogonal chemistry [13] to their use as photo‐crosslinking agents in materials science [14, 15]. To date, however, only fluorescent tetrazole reagents with nanosecond emission lifetimes have been reported. With the increasing use of materials exhibiting long‐lived (µs‐ms) emission lifetimes in areas such as bio‐imaging [16], photodynamic therapy [17], and photocatalysis [18], modifying N‐aryltetrazoles beyond purely fluorescent molecules could significantly expand the utility of this photoreaction.

Luminophores exhibiting thermally activated delayed fluorescence (TADF) have emerged as useful materials with long emission lifetimes, as they can be prepared from purely organic elements without the use of heavy metals. The design of TADF materials requires minimizing the energy gap between the lowest singlet (S1) and triplet (T1) excited states, termed ΔE ST. One way to achieve this is by increasing the dihedral angle between donor and acceptor moieties in a π‐conjugated framework, reducing the energy of the electronic exchange interaction and therefore ΔE ST [19]. When ΔE ST is sufficiently small, both forward and reverse intersystem crossing (ISC and RISC) can become observable, often aided by spin‐vibronic coupling with higher‐lying triplet states [20, 21]. This interaction between ISC and RISC can yield long emission lifetimes in purely organic molecules.

With their relatively low LUMO energy, N‐aryltetrazoles have the potential to act as an acceptor in TADF‐active luminophores. We hypothesized that pairing N‐aryltetrazoles with suitable electron donors, such as phenoxazine, would induce charge‐transfer character and impart TADF properties to photo‐clickable reagents. As earlier reports indicated that triplet‐state intermediates are crucial for photocycloaddition [22], we further speculated that minimizing ΔE ST in tetrazole chromophores would increase the triplet population under photoexcitation, potentially improving their reactivity. The integration of the photoclickable tetrazole motif as both the acceptor unit and reactive handle within the donor–acceptor TADF framework enables photoinduced transformation of the acceptor itself, thereby directly modulating the electronic structure and photophysical properties of the luminophore, leading to TADF behavior. This design also provides a modular route to late‐stage functionalization with a broad range of cycloaddition partners while simultaneously enabling direct investigation of the intrinsic photoinduced cycloaddition reactivity of TADF‐active luminophores. Such studies formed an attractive target for research, since despite the abundance of work focused on optimizing TADF materials for bioimaging, photocatalysis, and organic light‐emitting diodes (OLEDs) [18, 23, 24], the investigation of photochemical transformations involving TADF molecules remains underexplored (Figure 1).

Previous work and overview of the current work on TADF‐active photoreagents.

Organic dots (Odots) are emerging as highly versatile nanomaterials owing to their excellent biocompatibility, bright emission, water dispersibility, and ease of preparation [25]. In our previous work, TADF luminophores were incorporated into the core of Odots through physical encapsulation, a strategy that limited control over surface chemistry and restricted opportunities for targeted functionalization [26, 27]. Photo‐induced click chemistry presents an attractive solution, offering a means to covalently modify the surface of Odots—and potentially other classes of molecules such as quantum dots or polymer dots—without the need for additional catalysts or reagents. Furthermore, the integration of TADF‐active luminophores on the surface of such nanoparticles could be useful in applications where the generation of reactive triplet species is desirable [3].

TADF‐active photoreagents would also offer the ability to introduce delayed fluorescence to materials and interfaces with spatiotemporal control. Photo‐responsive materials are of broad interest for their applications in lithography [28], optoelectronics [29, 30, 31, 32, 33], photovoltaics [34], biosensing [35, 36], and microfluidics [37, 38, 39]. Self‐assembled monolayers (SAMs) in particular have emerged as a versatile approach to achieving photoresponsive behavior on surfaces [40]. These SAMs use moieties such as spiropyrans [41], azobenzenes [42, 43, 44, 45], dithienylethenes [46, 47], and stilbenes [48] as molecular photochromic switches that undergo photocyclization or cis → trans photoisomerization upon irradiation. Such systems have been developed to modulate wettability, work function, and conductance/capacitance, granting the ability to tune the electronic and optoelectronic properties of surfaces precisely using light [32, 49, 50, 51, 52]. Although emission quenching is expected in close proximity to the gold surface, such surface‐induced quenching phenomena have been widely exploited in fluorescence ‘turn‐on’ sensing and responsive interfacial systems, where stimuli‐responsive distancing of the fluorophore from the gold surface enables emission recovery and signal transduction [53, 54, 55]. To the best of our knowledge, however, reversible photoinduced SAMs constructed from TADF materials have yet to be explored, and would represent an entirely new class of photofunctional surfaces. Beyond enabling photoclick functionalization, integrating the tetrazole directly into the donor–acceptor framework also provides a potential advantage for gold surface chemisorption. Upon photoexcitation, electron density is redistributed to the tetrazole acceptor, which may enhance its electronic interaction with the gold surface. In contrast, peripheral clickable groups in conventional TADF emitters are electronically decoupled from the emissive core and therefore do not experience analogous excited‐state charge redistribution.

Herein, we report two regioisomers of N‐aryltetrazoles with phenoxazine donors appended, yielding POX‐TET and POX‐TET2 (Figure 1). Both regioisomers exhibit TADF, with fast RISC rates and sky‐blue emission. These TADF‐active luminophores undergo efficient, regioselective photo‐induced dipolar cycloadditions with alkynes and alkenes, and theoretical calculations suggest that enhanced spin–orbit coupling (SOC) contributes to their high reactivity. POX‐TET proved to be the superior photo‐reagent, and further derivatization to the corresponding nitrile to generate POX‐TET‐CN improves visible‐light absorption and significantly increases photoluminescent quantum yield (PLQY). The resulting cycloadducts retain TADF characteristics in a reaction‐partner‐dependent manner, enabling the divergent synthesis of 6 new TADF molecules containing pyrazole acceptor motifs. The nonaromatized dihydropyrazolines that result from photocycloaddition with alkene substrates undergo oxidative photo‐induced aromatization, providing stimuli‐responsive activation of TADF. POX‐TET‐CN was also used for the surface modification of fully organic nanoparticles, while POX‐TET was employed for visible‐light‐induced SAM formation via charge transfer states. To the best of our knowledge, this represents the first demonstration of employing TADF‐active luminophores as visible‐light photoreagents for nanoparticle and surface functionalization.

Results and Discussion

Synthesis

The synthesis of POX‐TET and POX‐TET2 was achieved using an oxidative Cu‐based Chan–Evans–Lam coupling of aryltetrazoles followed by Buchwald‐Hartwig cross‐coupling to give the corresponding N‐aryltetrazoles, with the regioisomers determined by the halide position in the starting materials (Scheme 1) [56, 57]. POX‐TET‐CN was synthesized in a similar fashion to POX‐TET, appending the N‐phenylphenoxazine donor to the tetrazole carbon atom. (Scheme S1). The structures of the final compounds were confirmed by 1H and 13C{1H} NMR spectroscopy (Figures S2–S41), high‐resolution mass spectrometry, and X‐ray crystallography (Figures S43 and S44).

The photoinduced 1,3‐dipolar cycloaddition of POX‐TET and POX‐TET2 was then optimized using phenylacetylene as the dipolarophile (Table 1). While alkene substrates could also participate in the reaction, the products in these cases would be nonaromatic dihydropyrazoles, leading us to focus on alkyne partners to yield more conventional donor‐acceptor emitters. The photoexcited tetrazole and the resulting nitrile‐imine dipole are highly sensitive to solvent polarity, and we therefore screened solvents of varying dielectric constant to evaluate their effects on cycloaddition efficiency [58]. Higher yields were observed in nonpolar solvents, whereas more polar solvents like DMF and MeCN completely inhibited reactivity. Although the reaction can proceed under irradiation with 450 nm blue LEDs, higher absorption at higher energy made excitation with 405 nm violet LEDs optimal. Toluene gave the best results, yielding 78% for the photoreaction of POX‐TET and 47% for POX‐TET2 with phenylacetylene. The reaction was almost completely quenched in air, consistent with previous reports of triplet state involvement in the reaction pathway (Table 1) [59]. While reaction efficiency decreases under aerated conditions due to quenching by oxygen, previous studies have shown that tetrazole‐based photoligation reactions can still proceed effectively in complex biological media [11, 60].

Using the optimized conditions, we next investigated the scope of the reaction and explored the photophysical properties of the resulting photoproducts (Figures 2 and S10–S41). Compared to phenylacetylene, the CF3‐substituted phenyl alkyne afforded significantly higher yields for both POX‐TET and POX‐TET2, reaching up to 93% in the case of PT1‐CF3 . This observation is consistent with previous reports showing that increased electron deficiency in the dipolarophile enhances reactivity in cycloaddition reactions [5]. Electron‐rich heterocycles such as 3‐ethynylthiophene were also competent reaction partners, delivering thiophene‐appended luminophores PT1‐Thio and PT2‐Thio in 78 and 57% yield, respectively. Alkenes such as acrylates, styrene, and 4‐(trifluoromethyl)styrene were also found to undergo dipolar cycloaddition, albeit in much lower yield. In addition, the cyano‐substituted precursor, POX‐TET‐CN, readily underwent cycloaddition with phenylacetylene, furnishing an 81% yield of photoproduct (Figure 5). Both NMR spectroscopy and X‐ray crystallography confirm the regioselectivity of the cycloaddition reaction (Figures 3, S45, S46 and Table S1). In the case of POX‐TET, the dipolarophile phenyl substituent is oriented away from the electron donor, whereas in POX‐TET2, it is positioned toward the donor, giving distinct regioisomeric outcomes (Figure 3). Additionally, the use of bicyclononyne (1R,8S,9s)‐bicyclo[6.1.0]non‐4‐yn‐9‐ylmethanol), a strained alkyne previously reported to exhibit greatly enhanced reactivity in biologically relevant settings due to strain‐release effects, was investigated with POX‐TET [11]. Under identical NMR‐scale conditions, complete conversion was observed within 30 min, whereas only 23% conversion was achieved after 1 h of irradiation with phenylacetylene (Figure S42). These results further highlight the potential of these luminophores for bioorthogonal applications, where rapid reaction kinetics are essential [11].

Photophysical and Electrochemical Properties

Figure 4 presents the UV‐Vis absorption and PL spectra of all tetrazole‐ and pyrazole‐based luminophores in toluene. POX‐TET, POX‐TET2, and POX‐TET‐CN exhibit pronounced CT absorption bands centered around 375 nm. The PL decays of the emitters in toluene were measured using time‐correlated single‐photon counting (TCSPC) (Figure 4C). POX‐TET2 exhibited a significantly longer prompt lifetime of 7.5 ns in air, compared to 3.6 ns for POX‐TET. POX‐TET‐CN showed the longest prompt lifetime of the series at 10.5 ns in air. More importantly, all three emitters exhibited remarkably fast delayed fluorescence, with POX‐TET2 displaying sub‐microsecond decay times. In contrast, no delayed fluorescence was observed in toluene for all of the pyrazole‐based photoproducts (Table 2 and Figure S47).

Following cycloaddition, the pyrazole‐based compounds exhibit blue‐shifted absorption and emission (Figure 4 and Table 2). The photoproducts of POX‐TET (PT1‐Ph, PT1‐CF3 , and PT1‐Thio) show nearly identical absorption and PL spectra, suggesting similar electronic character for these derivatives. For the POX‐TET2‐derived products, a comparable pattern is seen, except for PT2‐CF3 , which shows a broader, intermediate blue shift, implying some influence of the CF3 group on the S1 state. In contrast, the cyano substituent in POX‐TET‐CN induces a bathochromic shift compared to POX‐TET, with the charge‐transfer emission maximum red‐shifted by 18 nm and green luminescence at λ max = 511 nm. Cycloaddition to form PT1‐CN results in a blue‐shifted emission at 456 nm, confirming that optical properties are tunable according to the dipolarophile chosen for the photoreaction. POX‐TET2 exhibits a higher PLQY (34%) than POX‐TET (12%) in toluene, with both PLQYs dropping significantly in aerated solutions, indicative of triplet‐state involvement (Table 2). The cyano group significantly enhances PLQY in degassed solution to 35%, giving nearly a threefold increase over POX‐TET.

Solvatochromism experiments were also performed to assess the charge‐transfer character of tetrazole‐ and pyrazole‐based compounds (Figure S48). All luminophores exhibited positive solvatochromic shifts, with broader emission observed in polar solvents such as MeCN, and more structured emission in nonpolar solvents like hexane. Overall, the photoproducts showed less positive solvatochromism compared to the starting materials used here.

The solid‐state photophysical properties of these tetrazole and pyrazole luminophores were then measured in 1 wt% PMMA (Figure S49 and Table 3). The emission wavelengths ranged from near‐UV to sky‐blue, with the pyrazole‐based photoproducts consistently showing blue‐shifted emission relative to their tetrazole counterparts, as observed in toluene solutions. Interestingly, for POX‐TET and POX‐TET2, the emission energies are reversed in the solid state: POX‐TET emits at 466 nm, while POX‐TET2 emits at 435 nm. This contrasts with their behavior in toluene, where POX‐TET2 exhibits more red‐shifted emission at 495 nm compared to POX‐TET at 476 nm (Table 2). The introduction of a cyano substituent in POX‐TET‐CN results in red‐shifted emission in the solid state, though the shift is less pronounced than in solution.

TCSPC and multichannel scaling (MCS) were employed to measure the prompt and long‐lived PL decays, respectively (Table 3, Figures S50 and S51). While all tetrazole‐ and pyrazole‐based emitters exhibit comparable prompt lifetimes in the range of 2–3 ns, their long‐lived components vary significantly. In general, the cycloaddition products show substantially longer emission lifetimes. For example, upon conversion of POX‐TET to PT1‐Ph, the delayed lifetime reaches 7.73 ms under inert conditions compared to 352 µs for the parent compound. A similar trend is observed for POX‐TET‐CN, where the PT1‐CN‐Ph photoproduct exhibits the longest lifetime in the series of 12.5 ms under inert atmosphere. Similarly, for the CF3‐substituted derivatives (PT1‐CF3 and PT2‐CF3 ), the delayed lifetimes are also significantly extended relative to their tetrazole precursors at 11.4 and 10.4 ms, respectively.

To confirm whether the long‐lived PL decay originates from TADF, temperature‐dependent lifetime measurements were performed from 100 to 298 K in a 1 wt% PMMA film (Figures 6 and S52). All tetrazole‐based starting materials (POX‐TET, POX‐TET2, and POX‐TET‐CN) exhibited TADF, as their delayed fluorescence was suppressed upon cooling to 100 K. However, an onset of phosphorescence was observed at longer timescales for POX‐TET and POX‐TET‐CN. The photoproducts derived from phenylacetylene (PT1‐Ph, PT1‐CN‐Ph) also showed clear TADF behavior, while PT2‐Ph exhibited no temperature dependence in its long‐lived decay, indicating the absence of TADF. In contrast, the CF3‐substituted PT2‐CF3 showed clear TADF behavior, with delayed fluorescence being quenched at low temperatures. Despite their similar long‐lived PL decay at 298 K, PT1‐CF3 and PT2‐CF3 exhibited distinct temperature‐dependent behaviors: PT1‐CF3 showed typical TADF characteristics, with a gradual onset of delayed fluorescence upon heating, whereas PT2‐CF3 displayed an early onset of phosphorescence at longer timescales upon cooling. Finally, the cyclized products derived from thiophene substrates (PT1‐Thio, PT2‐Thio) showed no evidence of TADF, with emission behavior more consistent with phosphorescence in the solid state (Figure S52).

Temperature‐dependent long‐lived PL decays of the emitters in 1 wt% PMMA (measured using MCS; λ ex = 313 nm).

Time‐gated and steady‐state emission measurements were performed at 100 K in 1 wt% PMMA to determine the S1 and T1 energies (Figure S53 and Table 4). Except for POX‐TET‐CN, all tetrazole‐ and pyrazole‐based luminophores exhibited large ΔE ST values (>0.4 eV), which accounts for their long‐delayed fluorescence lifetimes due to inefficient RISC. In general, the pyrazole‐based photoproducts displayed even larger ΔE ST, consistent with their longer delayed fluorescence compared to their parent tetrazole counterparts. The steady‐state and time‐gated emission spectra were also recorded at 298 K in 1 wt% PMMA. With the exception of PT2‐Ph, PT1‐Thio, and PT2‐Thio, which exhibit pronounced red‐shifted time‐gated emission consistent with room‐temperature phosphorescence rather than TADF behavior, all other emitters display largely preserved emission profiles, with only slight spectral shifts likely arising from weak phosphorescence contributions (Figure S53). Notably, all T1 energies were nearly identical, centered around ∼2.8 eV, suggesting that the T1 state at 100 K in PMMA likely originates from a similar conformer of the phenoxazine–tetrazole or phenoxazine–pyrazole donor–acceptor pair [61]. Numerous reports have described twisted intramolecular charge‐transfer TADF systems where the lowest‐lying emissive triplet can switch between 3LE and 3CT states as a function of the donor–acceptor dihedral angle across different environments of varying rigidity or viscosity [62, 63, 64, 65]. Several of these display large apparent ΔE ST values measured by phosphorimetry [66, 67, 68]. To explain the TADF behavior given the large ΔE ST values, further theoretical calculations were conducted (vide infra).

The electronic properties of the tetrazole‐ and pyrazole‐based emitters, including their HOMO and LUMO energies, were determined using cyclic voltammetry (CV) and Tauc plots (Figures S54 and S55, Table 4). In the oxidative sweep of the CV, a chemically reversible oxidation was observed for the phenoxazine donor across all luminophores. For PT1‐Thio and PT2‐Thio, additional irreversible oxidation peaks were detected, which are attributed to the thiophene moiety. On the reductive side, the tetrazole‐containing compounds (POX‐TET, POX‐TET2, and POX‐TET‐CN) exhibited irreversible reductions at similar potentials, with POX‐TET‐CN showing an additional reduction peak at a more negative potential corresponding to the nitrile group. In contrast, pyrazole derivatives generally showed no observable reduction peaks within the wide electrochemical window of 1,2‐difluorobenzene, except for the CF3‐substituted derivatives (PT1‐CF3 , PT2‐CF3 ), which displayed prominent irreversible reductions. Nevertheless, the optical gaps derived from Tauc plots, combined with the HOMO levels obtained from CV, enabled estimation of the HOMO–LUMO energy gaps for all emitters. These values were generally consistent with the S1 emission energies extracted from PL decay measurements (Tables 2 and 4).

Photoinduced Aromatization

Our cycloaddition scope also included alkene dipolarophiles, giving PT1‐Acryl and PT2‐Acryl in decent yields, with the acrylate group benefiting the 1,3‐dipolar cycloaddition through its electron‐withdrawing character (Figure 2). Previous studies have reported the aromatization of dihydropyrazolines to pyrazoles using chemical oxidants [69], photochemical oxidation in the presence of sensitizers [70], or through electron‐transfer pathways promoted by halogenated solvents [71]. We found that both PT1‐Acryl and PT2‐Acryl undergo clean and efficient aromatization to PT1‐Acryl‐Aro and PT2‐Acryl‐Aro in toluene under irradiation without any additives, in contrast to prior observations that metals or halogenated media are generally required to effect this transformation (Figure 7A,B). Importantly, no aromatization occurs under an inert atmosphere, indicating that molecular oxygen is involved in the reaction (Figures S57 and S58). Monitoring the relative rates of conversion with UV‐Vis spectroscopy in toluene and CH2Cl2 shows that PT1‐Acryl aromatizes substantially faster than PT2‐Acryl, with PT1‐Acryl (Figure 7C and Table S2). A pronounced solvent effect is also observed for PT2‐Acryl, for which the reaction proceeds more slowly (Figure S59). This may reflect the behavior of dihydropyrazolines previously reported by Soltani et al. in halogenated solvents, where multiple electron‐transfer events occur involving both the solvent and dissolved oxygen [71]. In toluene, however, the reaction likely proceeds through an oxygen‐dependent self‐sensitization pathway similarly reported by Ribagorda et al., in which excited PT1‐Acryl or PT2‐Acryl generates reactive superoxide species that trigger electron‐transfer steps for aromatization [70].

We then investigated the photophysical differences between the dihydropyrazolines (PT1‐Acryl and PT2‐Acryl) and their aromatized products (PT1‐Acryl‐Aro and PT2‐Acryl‐Aro) (Figures 8, S60, and S61). Overall, the photoinduced aromatization results in a bathochromic shift and induces TADF behavior in 1 wt% PMMA, as shown in the temperature‐dependent time‐resolved PL decay measurements (Figure 8). PT1‐Acryl‐Aro exhibits long‐lived delayed fluorescence with a lifetime of 3.03 ms, consistent with its larger ΔE ST value of 0.68 eV, while PT2‐Acryl‐Aro displays a smaller ΔE ST of 0.43 eV and a shorter delayed component of 990 µs (Tables S3 and S4). Although delayed fluorescence is not observed in toluene, PT1‐Acryl‐Aro shows a long‐lived PL decay in CH2Cl2 with a lifetime of 1.27 µs (Figures S60, S61 and Tables S5 and S6). To note, dual emission is observed in toluene and PMMA for all acryl‐based emitters, attributed to the trans and cis conformations of the ester (Figure 7A), while in CH2Cl2 the emission converges into broad and featureless CT bands (Figures S46B and S60). To the best of our knowledge, PT1‐Acryl and PT2‐Acryl represent the first example of a photochemically driven aromatization that induces TADF emission, suggesting potential applications in sensing where the simultaneous presence of light and oxygen serves as the activating stimulus [72, 73, 74]. Electrochemical characterization of the acryl series was also conducted, revealing behavior consistent with other phenoxazine‐based materials, where the donor moiety (POX) undergoes oxidation at approximately 1.2 V, and additional oxidation events above 1.5 V, attributed to the ester groups (Figure S55B).

Theoretical Calculations

Density functional theory (DFT) and time‐dependent (TD)‐DFT calculations were performed to provide an in‐depth understanding of the electronic and photophysical properties of these materials (Tables S7–S31). All emitters were analyzed with the nonempirically tuned range‐separated OT‐S‐LC‐whPBE functional, in combination with the 6–31G(d) basis set, with implicit toluene solvation modeled using the IEFPCM method (see details in Supporting Information).

The predicted HOMO and LUMO energies at the ground‐state geometries agree closely with experimental values, as do the calculated optical gaps (Table 4). The optimized geometries also correlate well with the crystal structures, finding both POX‐TET and POX‐TET2 to feature nearly orthogonal phenoxazine and tetrazole units. To simulate the photophysical data, the first 20 singlet vertical excitation energies were calculated at the ground‐state‐optimized geometries, yielding absorption spectra that reproduce the experimental profiles well (Figure S62). Emission spectra, simulated from the lowest singlet excited‐state geometries, also show excellent agreement with experiment (Figure S62, Tables S7 and 2). The adiabatic ∆E ST values obtained from TD‐DFT are smaller than those estimated from experimental fluorescence and phosphorescence onsets, but follow the same trends (Table 4, Table S7). Among the series, POX‐TET‐CN exhibits the smallest ∆E ST, consistent with its strong TADF behavior. In general, the tetrazole‐containing molecules are predicted to have singlet‐triplet gaps less than 200 meV, explaining their efficient TADF in toluene, while the corresponding dihydropyrazole analogues show much larger gaps (Table 4) and therefore do not exhibit TADF under the same conditions. Hole‐electron analyses confirm that the S1 states of all compounds possess pronounced charge transfer (CT) character, in agreement with the experimentally observed solvatochromism (Figures S45, S63, and S64). The T1 states are primarily locally excited (LE) on the phenoxazine donor, consistent with the experimental phosphorescence spectra, except for POX‐TET2 and POX‐TET‐CN, which display CT‐type T1 states analogous in character to their S1 states (Figures S53, S63, and S64). In these two molecules, the 3CT and 3LE states are predicted to be nearly degenerate in toluene (Table S8), implying that small environmental changes, such as transfer to a less polar host like PMMA, could easily result in the reordering of these states. Environment‐dependent reordering of triplet states can also rationalize the relatively large ΔE ST values observed by phosphorimetry, while still permitting TADF for POX‐TET2. The large apparent ΔE ST values are likely a feature of conducting the measurements in a frozen matrix, where dihedral rotation is inhibited, and reordering of triplet states is not possible. Consistent with experiment, the predicted emission of PT2‐CF3 is red‐shifted relative to PT1‐CF3 and PT2‐Ph, and its ∆E ST is more than 150 meV smaller than that of PT2‐Ph, consistent with the observation that only PT2‐CF3 exhibits TADF in PMMA (Figure 6 and Table 4).

The role of triplet‐state population in the photo‐induced cycloaddition reactivity of POX‐TET and POX‐TET2 was also probed computationally (Figure 9). For both molecules, the CT triplet ultimately converges to a lower‐energy minimum than the 3LE state localized on the phenoxazine unit (Figure 9), even though the LE state is lower in energy at the ground‐state geometry of POX‐TET (Table S8). While the adiabatic 3CT‐1CT energy gaps are similar for both molecules (0.21 eV for POX‐TET and 0.16 eV for POX‐TET2), their 3LE‐1CT gaps differ markedly (Table S7). This gap is quite large for POX‐TET (0.16 eV) but is only 0.01 eV for POX‐TET2. This difference arises from greater stabilization of the 1CT state in POX‐TET2, consistent with its smaller experimental optical gap (Table 4). In accordance with El‐Sayed's rule, the calculated spin‐orbit coupling constants between the 1CT and 3LE states are much larger than those between 1CT and 3CT (Figure 9 and Table S9). Consequently, the small 3LE‐1CT gap in POX‐TET2 promotes more efficient TADF, leading to its much shorter delayed fluorescence lifetime in toluene (Table 2). This behavior aligns with the well‐established spin‐vibronic mechanism of TADF involving coupling between LE and CT triplet states [21]. Accordingly, the larger 3LE‐1CT gap in POX‐TET prolongs its triplet lifetime, ultimately enhancing the yield of the photo‐induced cycloaddition (Table 1). This finding further supports the involvement of triplet states in facilitating this reaction [75, 76].

Energies of the 1CT, 3CT, and 3LE states of POX‐TET and POX‐TET2 at each of the three optimized excited‐state geometries. Energies are reported relative to the ground‐state minimum of each molecule. Hole‐electron (light‐dark) diagrams illustrate the electronic character of the optimized state in each geometry. Adiabatic energy gaps and spin‐orbit coupling constants between each triplet state and the 1CT state, calculated at the corresponding triplet geometries, are indicated.

Finally, DFT calculations were employed to assess the relative stabilities and electronic structures of the cis‐ and trans‐isomers formed within the acryl series, with the trans‐isomer calculated to be more stable in each case (Figure 7A) (Figure S64 and Tables S7–S9). Experimentally, TADF is activated post‐aromatization for both molecules (Figure 8). TD‐DFT rationalizes this behavior, revealing the adiabatic singlet‐triplet gaps of the nonaromatized compounds, PT1‐Acryl and PT2‐Acryl, to be prohibitively large for TADF, at 0.95 and 1.18 eV, respectively (Table S7). In contrast, the aromatized trans‐products show much smaller gaps, at 0.42 and 0.19 eV for PT1‐Acryl‐Aro and PT2‐Acryl‐Aro, respectively, enabling the experimentally observed TADF.

Surface Functionalization of Organic Dots via Cycloaddition

We next sought to apply these TADF‐based photoclickable reagents in the surface modification of water‐dispersible nanoparticles (Figure 10A). We tested the surface functionalization of organic dots, or Odots, which are fully organic nanoparticles formed from a rapidly cooled mixture of surfactant and the host material mCP (1,3‐Di(9H‐carbazol‐9‐yl)benzene), yielding nanoparticles formed from rigid organic glass [77]. Odots can be further doped with luminescent emitters that are encapsulated in the glassy mCP matrix as the nanoparticles form, and have a number of attractive photophysical properties. These include enhanced photostability of the doped emitters due to the rigidity of the glassy matrix, increased brightness due to matrix rigidity, and greater synthetic simplicity compared to other nanoparticles such as polymer dots [25]. While our group has previously used these particles to encapsulate TADF dyes in the glassy core for bioimaging [27, 78], here we hypothesized that photoclickable reagents would provide convenient access to the first corona‐functionalized TADF Odots, using an alkyne‐terminated surfactant (Figure 10A and Figures S65–S68). The photochemical nature of this approach, which does not require any additional catalyst, makes it particularly well‐suited for nanoparticle synthesis where simple and robust synthetic methods are desirable [79].

(A) Synthetic route to PT‐CN‐Odots functionalized with POX‐TET‐CN. (B–D) Nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA) plots of Odots prepared with varying surfactant compositions: (B) 100% nonfunctionalized alkyne surfactant, (C) 50:50 mixture of PT‐CN‐surfactant and nonfunctionalized alkyne surfactant (50% surface click), and (D) 100% PT‐CN‐surfactant. (E) Zeta potential measurements demonstrating progressive surface modification of Odots as a function of POX‐TET‐CN surface‐termination percentage. (F) Time‐gated (100 µs delay) emission spectra under air and inert conditions for 50% clicked PT‐CN‐Odots (F) and corresponding time‐correlated PL decays (G) of PT‐CN‐Odots in water (λ e x = 313 nm, Concentration: 2.7 × 109 particles mL−1).

Unlike conventional approaches, where a nonconjugated clickable handle is appended to a pre‐existing luminophore, the photoclick reaction reported here directly transforms the acceptor unit within the donor–acceptor framework. As a result, the conjugation event itself modifies the electronic structure and photophysical properties of the luminophore, providing the potential for intrinsic optical verification of successful attachment through changes in emission color, delayed fluorescence lifetime, or TADF efficiency. This feature may be particularly advantageous for the functionalization of alkyne‐terminated substrates, including surfactants, nanoparticles, and surfaces, where the conjugation event itself can serve as a built‐in reporter without requiring an additional fluorescent label.

Among the synthesized dyes, POX‐TET‐CN was selected for nanoparticle functionalization due to its red‐shifted absorption and the long‐lived PL decay of the photoproduct, both desirable traits for applications such as bioimaging and photodynamic therapy (Table 2) [80]. By first functionalizing the alkyne surfactant with POX‐TET‐CN followed by microwave synthesis of the nanoparticles using mCP, corona‐modified Odots, referred to here as PTCN‐Odot, were successfully prepared (Figure 10). Three batches were prepared containing 0%, 50%, and 100% of the functional surfactant in the reaction mixture, with the balance being composed of the nonfunctionalized, alkyne‐terminated surfactant precursor. Zeta potential measurements revealed changes in the nanoparticle surface charges consistent with increasing incorporation of the functional surfactant: −23.95 mV, −31.01 mV, and −41.69 mV for 0%, 50%, and 100% incorporation of PT‐CN‐Surfactant, respectively. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images were also obtained for Odots prepared using either 100% non‐alkyne‐functionalized surfactant or a 50:50 mixture of this and a PT‐CN surfactant (PT‐CN‐Odots). In both cases, the O‐dots displayed spherical morphologies (Figure S68). Furthermore, the Odots displayed clear oxygen sensitivity in aerated aqueous media, as evidenced by quenching of the delayed fluorescence, and confirming successful localization of the TADF dye on the nanoparticle surface (Figures 10F and S67, Table S32). The PLQYs of the PT‐CN‐Odots were also measured (Table S33). Under N2, the PLQY of the 100% PT‐CN‐functionalized O‐dots increased markedly from 2% to 19%, whereas that of the 100% alkyne‐terminated O‐dots increased only from 15% to 21%. These results further support successful functionalization of the O‐dot surface with the PT‐CN surfactant.

These experiments demonstrate the feasibility of functionalizing surfactants with photoclickable TADF reagents to prepare water‐dispersible Odots, providing a proof‐of‐concept for this strategy. Although the photoclick reaction was performed in an organic solvent, the resulting functionalized surfactants were successfully incorporated into water‐dispersible Odots, highlighting the potential of this approach for preparing functional nanomaterials for applications in aqueous environments. Future development of water‐soluble photoclickable TADF reagents capable of direct functionalization under aqueous conditions could further expand the applicability of this platform to nanoparticle surface modification and related life science applications. As the PT‐CN‐Surfactant photoproduct primarily absorbs high‐energy light (<405 nm, Figure S66A), designing photoclickable dyes with lower‐energy absorption will also be an important direction for enabling future bioimaging and photodynamic therapy applications.

Photoinduced SAM Formation

Given the widespread interest in photo‐responsive materials, we hypothesized that POX‐TET chromophores could be used as photoreagents to form self‐assembled monolayers (SAMs) on polycrystalline gold. Here, we used attenuated total reflectance surface‐enhanced infrared absorption spectroscopy (ATR‐SEIRAS) to both characterize and monitor the formation of surface‐bound species. The signal enhancement of ATR‐SEIRAS decays rapidly with distance from the metal surface, with strongest signals arising from molecules that are chemisorbed on a nm‐roughened gold surface [81, 82]. This spatial selectivity enables preferential observation of surface‐bound species with minimal contribution from molecules in solution. Moreover, surface selection rules restrict observable vibrations to those with transition dipole moments that are oriented perpendicularly to the gold surface, providing insight into the binding geometry of the molecule. Overall, ATR‐SEIRAS permits the detection of surface‐bound species at sub‐monolayer coverage, as well as the elucidation of how adsorbates are bound to the surface.

To investigate the possibility of POX‐TET adsorption on gold and its dependence on photoirradiation, a gold film was exposed to an argon‐sparged 1 mM solution of POX‐TET in toluene in the dark while time‐dependent ATR‐SEIRA spectra were collected in situ. Over 20 min, no significant changes to the ATR‐SEIRA spectra were observed (Figure 11Bi). Thereafter, the POX‐TET‐containing solution was removed and the surface rinsed with toluene, ensuring that only molecules adsorbed to the surface remained. The cell was then refilled with argon‐sparged toluene and irradiated with 405 nm light. Immediately upon irradiation, characteristic peaks of POX‐TET appeared at 1437 cm−1, 1362 cm−1, and 1283 cm−1 and continued to grow in over 20 min (Figure 11Bii). DFT calculations support the assignment of these vibrational modes as ring‐breathing N‐N stretches of the POX‐TET tetrazole (Figure S69). Following irradiation, the light was switched off, and the IR peaks associated with POX‐TET decreased significantly in intensity over 60 min (Figure 11Biii). Once a steady baseline was re‐established, the gold surface was again irradiated with 405 nm light for 20 min and the same peaks associated with POX‐TET reappeared (Figure 11Biv). Recorded intensities and kinetics were similar to those of the initial irradiation, suggesting that the photo‐responsive behavior observed with POX‐TET is reversible.

Demonstration of a photo‐responsive POX‐TET‐modified gold surface which is proposed to undergo reversible photoinduced molecular reorientation in a SAM of POX‐TET on gold. (A) Computational electrostatic potential (ESP) maps and CHELPG partial atomic charges of the ground (S0) and excited (S1) states of POX‐TET, showing electronic localization at the N3 position upon excitation. ESP isosurface values range from −0.211 to +0.211 e/bohr [3], with all values exceeding +0.09 e/bohr [3] shown in blue and all values below −0.09 e/bohr [3] shown in red. (B) Time‐dependent ATR‐SEIRA spectra and accompanying graphical representation of the proposed (i) physisorption of POX‐TET on Au in the dark, (ii) molecular reorientation and chemisorption via the electron‐rich N3 of POX‐TET upon irradiation of the rinsed surface with 405 nm light, (iii) photo relaxation and return to physically adsorbed configuration in the dark, and (iv) reversible chemisorption upon re‐irradiation with 405 nm light. Spectra in i are recorded in 1 mM POX‐TET solution in toluene; samples in ii‐iv have been rinsed to remove excess POX‐TET, and subsequent spectra are recorded in pure toluene.

Based on these data, we propose that POX‐TET experiences physisorption in the dark, presumably via gold‐π interactions that orient the molecule parallel to the surface [82, 83]. This configuration lacks surface polarization of the adsorbate and minimizes the surface‐normal transition dipole moments of the ring‐breathing modes, explaining the lack of ATR‐SEIRA absorbances during dark exposure (Figure 11Bi). The appearance of POX‐TET‐derived ATR‐SEIRA signals upon irradiation with 405 nm light (Figure 10Bii)—despite removing POX‐TET from the bulk solution prior to irradiation—provides strong evidence that POX‐TET was originally associated with the Au surface in the dark and that subsequent photoexcitation triggers molecular reorientation that results in the growth of SEIRAS signals. The appearance of ATR‐SEIRA absorbances following 405 nm irradiation suggests that photoexcitation triggers a chemical change leading to POX‐TET chemisorption and the formation of a SAM on the gold surface. We propose that the chemisorption is accompanied by a molecular reorientation which renders the POX‐TET transition dipole moment more perpendicular to the surface; these two changes explain why POX‐TET becomes visible to ATR‐SEIRAS upon photoexcitation.

We propose that these light‐triggered spectroscopic changes are due to the CT properties of POX‐TET, which render the tetrazole unit more electron‐rich upon photoexcitation. This can be rationalized by noting that CT enhances the Lewis basicity of N3, facilitating POX‐TET coordination to the surface through an Au‐N3 bond (Figure 11A). Molecular electrostatic potential maps of POX‐TET and charges from electrostatic potentials using a grid‐based method (CHELPG) show partial atomic charges for the tetrazole N atoms in the S0 and S1 states at the OT‐S‐LC‐whPBE/6‐31G(d)/IEFPCM(toluene) level of theory (Figure 11A). These calculations indicate that CT to S1 upon photoexcitation localizes negative charge in the tetrazole unit, effectively generating a photo‐induced dipole. Moreover, the calculated charge is most significantly localized on N3 (−7.88 e). We therefore propose that chemisorption of POX‐TET occurs via coordination of N3 to the Au surface (Figure 11A), with time‐dependent SEIRAS absorbance increases that are consistent with SAM formation. The disappearance of the POX‐TET SEIRA features upon switching off the 405 nm light suggests gradual cleavage of the N3‐Au interaction following photorelaxation (Figure 11Biii). The observation that irradiation of the surface reversibly results in growth of SEIRAS features (Figure 11Biv) is consistent with our proposed model of photo‐induced SAM formation and suggests that the presumed physisorption of POX‐TET in the ground state is critical for preventing diffusion of POX‐TET into bulk solution, which would slow subsequent SAM re‐formation. While many examples of SAMs with photo‐responsive properties have been reported, to the best of our knowledge, the present study reports evidence for the first example of reversible photo‐induced SAM formation. Our findings suggest that further investigation into the impact of photo‐induced SAM formation on surface properties, pertinent to optoelectronic and microfluidic applications, is an attractive target for future research.

Conclusions

This work describes TADF‐active N‐aryltetrazoles that enable regioselective cycloadditions under visible light. We propose that POX‐TET is capable of reversible photo‐induced SAM formation on gold substrates, introducing a new modality for engineering light‐responsive molecular interfaces on gold. Furthermore, these reagents enable the preparation of water‐dispersible TADF‐active Odots through covalent functionalization of surfactants via photocycloaddition, providing a proof‐of‐concept for this strategy. Collectively, these results demonstrate the feasibility of using TADF luminophores as photoreagents for the functionalization of nanoparticles and surfaces. We envision that future development of water‐soluble photoclickable TADF reagents could expand this platform to enable direct nanoparticle surface functionalization under aqueous conditions and broader applications in bioconjugate chemistry, bioimaging, and photodynamic therapy.
