Nano-functionalized probiotic treats atherosclerosis via inhibiting intestinal microbiota-TMA-TMAO axis
Authors: Zhezhe Chen, Qiongjun Zhu, Hong Xu, Yiqing Hu, Yanan Wang, Zhebin Chen, Yao Wang, Xingru Huang, Guosheng Fu, Boxuan Ma, Wenbin Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractMany patients are suffering from atherosclerosis without typical risk factors, which can cause severe cardiovascular complications. Trimethylamine N-oxide (TMAO), derived from gut microbes, is a key unconventional contributor to the development of atherosclerosis. Here we present a strategy performed by orally administered nano-functionalized probiotics (PDMF@LGG) to inhibit TMAO through the gut microbiota-trimethylamine (TMA)-TMAO axis. PDMF@LGG, composed of polydopamine-coated Lacticaseibacillus rhamnosus GG and nanoparticles based on a reactive oxygen species (ROS)-responsive polymeric prodrug of fluoromethylcholine (FMC), can promote the retention of probiotics and nanoparticles in the intestine to persistently scavenge elevated ROS and release drugs. This process suppresses TMA production and absorption, lowering plasma TMAO levels. The therapeutic effects on male ApoE−/− mice demonstrate that PDMF@LGG reduces TMAO levels, alleviates atherosclerotic plaque formation, and regulates gut microbial composition and various metabolites. Together, PDMF@LGG represents a potential candidate for atherosclerotic therapy caused by TMAO and broadens the range of treatable atherosclerosis.Subject terms: Atherosclerosis, Atherosclerosis, Microbiota, Biomedical materials, Nanoparticles

Introduction

Atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (CVD) is intricately associated with metabolic disorders and presents a substantial threat to human health1. Abnormal lipid profiles, insulin resistance, hypertension, and chronic inflammatory status are pivotal contributors to CVDs, making their management a fundamental cornerstone for effective treatment and prevention strategies2–4. Nevertheless, recent studies highlighted that a concerning proportion of patients exhibit no specific risk factors, experiencing with a markedly higher 30-day mortality and a lower prescription rate for guideline-directed therapeutics compared to those with identifiable risk factors5,6. This disparity underscores the pressing need to uncover intervenable targets and to pioneer pharmacotherapy options tailored to these populations.

Accumulating evidences indicated that high trimethylamine N-oxide (TMAO) levels are closely associated with the development and deterioration of atherosclerosis7. Multiple mechanisms are involved in the aggravation of atherosclerosis by TMAO, including accelerated foam cell formation, impaired endothelial cell function, chronic inflammation infiltration, excessive platelet activation, and dysregulation of cholesterol metabolism8–10. Synthesis of TMAO is traced to its gut-derived precursor, trimethylamine (TMA), produced from choline through the activity of the gut microbial choline TMA lyase CutC/D. Followed by hepatic oxidation through flavin monooxygenases (FMOs), TMA is converted to TMAO9,11. The abundant choline in Western diets contributes to excessive production of TMA12,13. Furthermore, this dietary pattern disrupts the intestinal homeostasis by altering the gut microbiome composition, increasing reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation, and compromising the intestinal mucosal barrier14. Such synergistic adverse effects thereby exacerbate the susceptibility to atherosclerosis. Accordingly, targeting the gut microbiome-derived TMAO metabolic pathway presents a promising therapeutic strategy for atherosclerotic patients induced by high TMAO levels.

Currently, approaches targeting the intestinal microbiota-TMA-TMAO metabolic axis are under active investigation. Low-choline dietary modification and FMO3 inhibition emerge as potent strategies for lowering systemic TMAO levels15–17. However, the deficiency of indispensable nutrient choline, the detrimental effects on FMO-mediated exogenous drug metabolism, and the accumulation of unmetabolized TMA greatly limit the ultimate benefits18–21. Another alternative is to inhibit the activities of the bacterial enzyme CutC/D. Inhibitors that act on microbial choline TMA lyase activity to reduce circulating TMAO levels appear to offer a safer therapeutic option for mitigating the progression of atherosclerotic plaques. Fluoromethylcholine (FMC), a fluorinated choline analog, was developed and demonstrated a significant ability to reduce plasma TMAO levels, but suffered from fast metabolism due to its good hydrophilicity22. Moreover, probiotics have shown therapeutic potential to improve atherosclerosis through regulating lipid metabolism, participating in immune-inflammatory responses, and inhibiting the production of harmful metabolites23,24. In addition, oral probiotic adjuvants hold promise in restoring the intestinal microenvironment and remodeling the intestinal barrier function, but their viability and retention are often compromised by the harsh conditions in the gastrointestinal tract25,26. Following a multi-pronged intervention strategy, artificially armed probiotics are designed to enhance their intestinal viability for better bacteriotherapy performance. The superior intestinal colonization ability additionally ensures that probiotics serve as stable carriers for nanoparticles and provide persistent drug release27.

In this work, we develop a nano-functionalized probiotic to treat atherosclerosis triggered by TMAO. First, the FMC drug is introduced to reduce the production of TMA from enteric microorganisms. In order to overcome the rapid drug loss, FMC has been grafted through an oxalate ester bond to a polymer backbone based on methyl thioethanol ester (MEMA), which can be self-assembled into the nanoparticles (PMF) and offer ROS-triggered drug release28–30. In further, PMF nanoparticles have been engineered onto the surface of the oral probiotic Lacticaseibacillus rhamnosus GG (LGG) through the amidation on a polydopamine coating, resulting in the construction of nano-functionalized probiotic PDMF@LGG. The MEMA component in PMF nanoparticles can scavenge overexpressed ROS, and the polydopamine coating provides enhanced stickiness to the nano-functionalized probiotic, which jointly lead to the protection to the probiotics from oxidative stress and more stable colonization in intestinal tracts. On the other hand, under the trigger of local ROS, the FMC drug in PMF nanoparticles can be persistently released to block the CutC/D enzyme and depress the production of TMA. At the same time, LGG promotes intestinal barrier repair and reduces the translocation of TMA into the bloodstream. Therefore, PDMF@LGG is expected to inhibit the intestinal microbiota-TMA-TMAO axis via reducing TMA production and absorption, resulting in a decrease in circulating TMAO levels and consequently an efficient treatment for atherosclerosis (Fig. 1). Furthermore, PDMF@LGG may affect the gut microbiota by altering microbial abundance and ameliorating dysbiosis, which can further assist in the management of metabolites related to atherosclerosis.

Illustration of the intestinal microbiota-TMA-TMAO axis resulting in atherosclerosis and the therapeutic strategy based on nano-functionalized probiotic PDMF@LGG.

The overproduction of intestinal microbiota-derived TMA caused by high-choline diets and the subsequent increase in plasma TMAO levels promote the progression of atherosclerosis. The nano-functionalized probiotic PDMF@LGG effectively inhibits the production and absorption of TMA, reduces plasma TMAO levels, regulates the microbial homeostasis, and ultimately alleviates atherosclerosis.

Results

Preparation of PMF nanoparticles and nano-functionalized probiotics PDMF@LGG

The PMF nanoparticles integrated on LGG probiotics were prepared with a polymeric prodrug of FMC, which was also endowed with the ROS responsiveness (Fig. 2a). The FMC drug was first modified into a monomer FAOE with ROS breakable oxalic ester bond, which was subsequently copolymerized with MEMA monomer through the RAFT polymerization. The chemical structure of FAOE polymer was confirmed by 1H NMR and HRMS (Supplementary Figs. 1, 2). And the molecular weight of PMF was calculated to be 4,300 g mol−1 according to the 1H NMR and GPC (Supplementary Figs. 3, 4), which could be defined as poly(MEMA10-co-FAOE9). Through self-assembly in PBS, the PMF polymer could form nanoparticles with a particle size of approximately 89 nm and a regular spherical morphology (Fig. 2b). The disruption of nanostructure in ROS was first characterized with the size variation under different concentrations of H2O2 (Fig. 2c). It was determined that PMF nanoparticles maintained a stable particle size in 0.01 mM H2O2 within 8 h, while higher concentrations of H2O2, exceeding 0.1 mM, could trigger the disassembly of nanoparticles and an increase in particle size. Moreover, the morphology of PMF nanoparticles after being treated with 1 mM H2O2 for 4 h was also observed by TEM (Fig. 2d), which indicated an obvious decomposition and aggregation of the PMF polymer. Afterwards, the H2O2 concentrations in colon tissues of different mice fed with high-choline diets were detected (Fig. S5). The H2O2 levels in the colons of male C57BL/6 J and ApoE−/− mice fed with high-choline diets exceeded 0.1 mM, which was sufficient to trigger the disassembly and drug release of PMF nanoparticles in the intestine.

Relying on the ROS responsiveness of PMF, the ability of H2O2 scavenging and the stimuli-triggered drug delivery were further investigated (Fig. 2e). With the increasing concentrations of PMF, the amount of H2O2 scavenged gradually increased, and almost 80% of H2O2 was cleared within 1 h at a PMF concentration of 2 mg mL−1 (Fig. 2f). As a result, due to the scavenging of H2O2, the protective effect of PMF on LGG against H2O2 was also evaluated by co-incubation in 1 mM H2O2 (Fig. 2g), and LGG could normally grow in the presence of H2O2 with the PMF concentration at 1 mg mL−1. Besides, the disassembly of PMF in response to ROS led to the breakage of oxalate ester bonds, which in turn facilitated the controlled release of the FMC drug. As shown in Fig. 2h, the speed and amount of FMC delivered from PMF nanoparticles exhibited a concentration-dependent response to H2O2. When the H2O2 concentration reached 1 mM, over 50% of FMC was released in 12 h and almost 90% was delivered after 48 h, enabling ROS-specific delivery of the FMC drug with intensive therapeutic precision and reduced drug loss.

Additionally, PMF nanoparticles were conjugated with LGG probiotic to construct a nano-functionalized probiotic through the amidation based on a polydopamine coating. The carboxyl groups on PMF were activated with EDC/NHS, enabling subsequent participation in dopamine polymerization on the surface of LGG (Fig. 2i). TEM images of free LGG, polydopamine-coated LGG (PD@LGG) and nano-functionalized probiotic (PDMF@LGG) were shown in Fig. 2j to determine the conjugation of PMF nanoparticles and LGG probiotics. FT-IR spectrum also indicated the polydopamine coating, amide bond, and methylthio group in PDMF@LGG (Supplementary Fig. 6). Fluorescent DiI-labeled PMF and Hoechst 33342-stained LGG were used to confirm the preparation of PDMF@LGG (Fig. 2k). In addition, the contents of polydopamine coating and PMF nanoparticles on LGG probiotics were also quantified using UV-vis and a multifunctional microplate reader (Supplementary Fig. 7). The viability of probiotics after being nano-functionalized was evaluated by monitoring the optical density at 600 nm with a 30-min interval for 12 h along with the measurements of CFU. Both polydopamine coating and nano-functionalization showed barely any influence on the growth of LGG (Fig. 2l, Supplementary Fig. 8). Moreover, the conjugated PMF could protect LGG from the damage of simulated gastric fluid, simulated intestinal fluid, and bile salt environment (Fig. 2m-o). Therefore, with the maintained activity and enhanced viability in harsh environments, the nano-functionalized probiotic PDMF@LGG was expected to achieve better colonization in the intestinal tract, and further regulate the intestinal microbial environment with precise delivery of FMC drug.

Intestinal colonization of PDMF@LGG

During the oral treatment with nano-functionalized probiotics, their colonization and retention in the intestinal tract were of great importance, which were first evaluated in vitro on HT29 cells31. After confirming the cytotoxicity of PMF nanoparticles (Supplementary Fig. 9), different formulations of PDMF@LGG were used to indicate the adhesion on HT29 cells within 2 h (Supplementary Fig. 10), where PMF was labeled with DiI (red) and LGG was stained with MycoLight Green JJ98 (green). Both PD@LGG and PDMF@LGG exhibited markedly enhanced adhesion capacity to the cells, which was owing to the viscous polydopamine coating. Furthermore, the adhesion capacity and retention time of probiotics in the intestine were also estimated in male C57BL/6 J mice (Fig. 3a). The short-term colonization was investigated by ex vivo imaging of the separated intestinal tract after 2 h oral treatment (Fig. 3b). The results showed that PDMF@LGG revealed the most reservation in the intestine (Fig. 3c), and the specific imaging of the cecum also confirmed the consistent results (Fig. 3d,e). Moreover, the sections of the cecum were observed under CLSM (Fig. 3f), where more and stronger fluorescent signals from PMF and LGG were found in the PDMF@LGG treated group (Fig. 3g). Subsequently, the mice were subjected to different treatments for a longer period of time to study the retention time of PDMF@LGG in the intestine. According to the fluorescent signals of PMF and LGG (Fig. 3h, Supplementary Fig. 11), free LGG and PMF nanoparticles were found in the intestinal tract of mice 6 h after oral administration. The decrease in fluorescent intensity over extended time indicated a loss of probiotics or nanoparticles (Fig. 3i, j), while free PMF showed a faster reduction because of its nanoscale. After being coated with polydopamine, PD@LGG presented enhanced intestinal retention due to the adhesive properties of dopamine. Moreover, it was notable that PDMF@LGG exhibited superior colonization ability compared to other formulations in the first 12 h, which resulted from the combined effect of the viscosity provided by dopamine and the protective role of PMF to the LGG in the intestine. Furthermore, the retention of PD@LGG and PDMF@LGG in the intestinal tract was observed to be nearly identical after a 24-h period, suggesting that PMF might provide early-time protection to LGG. Moreover, the fluorescent signals from sections of different parts of intestinal tracts suggested that the probiotics with various formulas were mainly found in the colon and cecum, while PMF nanoparticles showed a uniform distribution in the intestinal tracts (Supplementary Fig. 12). Therefore, the synergistic effects of the dopamine-given stickiness and the PMF-mediated protection could enhance the viability and promote extended colonization of PDMF@LGG for better therapeutic outcomes. In addition, the whole-body distribution and metabolism of PDMF@LGG was also determined by tracing the fluorescent signals of PMF in blood and main organs. While a considerable amount of PMF nanoparticles was observed to enter the bloodstream after PMF administration, PDMF@LGG treatment showed a negligible leakage of PMF in blood (Supplementary Fig. 13). And PMF nanoparticles in the blood were found to be mainly metabolized through the liver and kidneys (Supplementary Fig. 14).

In vivo regulation on intestinal microbiota-TMA-TMAO axis

Male C57BL/6 J mice were fed with high-choline diets to establish mice model with high plasma TMAO levels (Fig. 4a), and the regulation of PDMF@LGG on intestinal microbiota-TMA-TMAO axis was illustrated in Fig. 4b. Firstly, the appropriate dosage of PDMF@LGG administration was investigated. The concentration of 108 and 109 CFU showed comparable outcomes, as evidenced by circulating TMA and TMAO levels in plasma and the expressions of FMO3, ZO-1, Occludin and Claudin, which were superior to those of 107 CFU dosage (Supplementary Figs. 15–17). Thanks to the limited benefits brought by a larger dosage, 108 CFU probiotics every time for oral administration was considered appropriate. The biosafety of PDMF@LGG towards the mean organs and all parts of the gastrointestinal tracts was also confirmed (Supplementary Figs. 18, 19). Following 6 weeks of high-choline diets supplemented with oral administration of various treatments, the choline-related metabolites, including TMA and TMAO, were quantified (Fig. 4c–e, Supplementary Fig. 20). LGG could inhibit circulating TMA (Fig. 4c) and TMAO (Fig. 4d) to a certain extent, while PMF nanoparticles showed a better outcome, suggesting that the suppression on TMA production contributed more to the control of plasma TMA and TMAO, rather than blocking TMA from entering the bloodstream. On the other hand, combining the advantages of probiotics and nanomedicine, PDMF@LGG exhibited the most remarkable inhibition on systemic TMA and TMAO, almost restoring circulating choline to the levels under a normal diet (Fig. 4e).

Furthermore, the expression of the key metabolic enzyme FMO3 in the liver was investigated, which undertakes the conversion of TMA to TMAO. The immunofluorescence images exhibited that FMO3 was clearly suppressed after treatment with PMF and PDMF@LGG (Fig. 4f, g), and the quantitative western blot analysis also confirmed similar results (Fig. 4h, i). On the other side, typical proteins involved in the tight junctions of the intestinal epithelium such as ZO-1, Occludin, and Claudin, were detected to assess the integrity of the intestinal barrier. The LGG-treated group showed a more complete mechanical intestinal barrier, while PDMF@LGG demonstrated a better barrier integrity owing to the protective benefits of PMF for LGG (Fig. 4j, k, Supplementary Figs. 21, 22).

Besides, to better compare and analyze the effects of different components and combinations on treatment outcomes, groups of pure polydopamine (PDA), free FMC drug (FMC), and the physical mixture of PMF, PDA, and LGG were included in the experiment for efficacy comparison (Supplementary Figs. 23–25). Systemic TMA/TMAO levels in plasma and the expressions of FMO3, ZO-1, Occludin, and Claudin indicated that PDA exhibited no beneficial effect on treatment, while the therapeutic efficiency of FMC was significantly weakened due to its rapid metabolism. Although the mixture of PMF, PDA, and LGG demonstrated obvious outcomes, PDMF@LGG still exhibited the best therapeutic efficacy due to the good stability resulting from the integration of various elements.

In vivo treatment to atherosclerosis

A number of patients suffered from atherosclerosis without typical risk factors. One traceable culprit was identified to be TMAO from choline metabolism by enteric microorganisms32. Accordingly, the therapeutic strategy based on PDMF@LGG inhibiting the intestinal microbiota-TMA-TMAO axis to overcome TMAO-triggered and aggravated atherosclerosis was investigated in ApoE−/− mice with atherosclerosis by feeding high-choline diets (Fig. 5a). The in vivo regulation of PDMF@LGG on the intestinal microbiota-TMA-TMAO axis was firstly evaluated (Fig. 5b, c, Supplementary Fig. 26). The typical choline-related metabolites showed a similar trend to the results observed in C57BL/6 J mice. Plasma TMA (Fig. 5b) and TMAO (Fig. 5c) levels were discovered to be reduced under the treatment of PDMF@LGG. The immunofluorescence signals (Fig. 5d, e) as well as the Western blot results (Fig. 5f, g) also suggested that the expression of FMO3 in the liver was suppressed by PDMF@LGG. In addition, the tight junctions of the intestinal barrier were protected and repaired by PDMF@LGG to inhibit the absorption of TMA. Immunofluorescence images showed that the expressions of ZO-1, Occludin, and Claudin were maintained after PDMF@LGG treatment (Fig. 5h, i), while mRNA and protein expressions conducted by real-time PCR and Western blot also jointly confirmed the results (Supplementary Figs. 27, 28). Therefore, benefiting from the combined inhibition on plasma TMAO levels by reducing TMA production and hindering TMA absorption, PDMF@LGG was expected to suppress atherosclerotic progression (Fig. 5j).

As a crucial trigger for atherosclerosis, plasma TMAO could facilitate the progression of atherosclerotic plaques8. After treatment with PDMF@LGG and its different formulations, plaques in the aortas were stained with ORO and the en face views of the aortas were shown in Fig. 6a. Both LGG and PMF were found to deliver significant inhibition on the formation of plaques (Fig. 6b). Compared with LGG, PMF indicated a better effect on inhibiting the plaques, which was in accordance with the circulating TMAO levels of these groups. Moreover, PDMF@LGG showed the most effective suppression of atherosclerotic plaque progression, with aortic plaques comparable to those in the control group without high-choline diets. On the other hand, the various compositions in the plaques of aortic roots were also stained and quantified (Fig. 6c). While both LGG and PMF exhibited distinct limitations to the plaques’ progression, PDMF@LGG exhibited the most beneficial anti-atherosclerotic effect. After the treatment of PDMF@LGG, the plaques showed a significant reduction in lipid accumulation (Fig. 6d), collagen degradation (Fig. 6e), necrocytosis (Fig. 6f), macrophage infiltration (Fig. 6g), and MMP-9 expression (Fig. 6h), which suggested a delayed development and a stable plaque structure. Consequently, by inhibiting the intestinal microbiota-TMA-TMAO axis, PDMF@LGG could realize the suppression of circulating TMAO, thereby leading to the restraint of atherosclerotic progression.

Regulation on enteric microorganism and metabolism

Considering the critical role of PDMF@LGG in modulating intestinal microbial homeostasis and other potential additional benefits for atherosclerosis, 16S ribosomal RNA gene sequencing and targeted LC-MS/MS metabolomics assay were used to detect the composition changes in gut microbiome and serum metabolites (Fig. 7a). Conspicuously, the increased biodiversity in gut microbiome presented by inverse-Simpson diversity index could be observed in all treatment groups compared to saline group, indicating the protective and restorative capacity of LGG and PMF on intestine bacterial richness (Fig. 7b). The β-diversity analysis performed by non-metric multidimensional scaling (NMDS) and principal coordinates analysis (PCoA) plots revealed favorable clustering trends within the same treatment group, while isolated clusters occurred between various groups, suggesting segregated microbiome compositions (Fig. 7c, d). Further analyses at the family- (Fig. 7e) and genus-level (Fig. 7f) taxonomy were conducted to explore the relative abundance of gut microbiome after different treatments. Apparently, the bacterial composition of atherosclerotic mice with PDMF@LGG treatment was significantly different compared with other groups, and the relative abundance of characterized microbiota was investigated in detail at different levels. Benefiting from the treatment of PMF and PDMF@LGG, an increase in the abundance of Clostridia at the class-level taxonomy was observed (Fig. 7g). Meanwhile, PDMF@LGG treatment dramatically increased the relative abundance of Lachnospiraceae family (Fig. 7h) and Oscillospiraceae family (Fig. 7i). After relative abundance screening at genus-level taxonomy, Oscillibacter (Fig. 7j) and Roseburia (Fig. 7k) were disclosed to be effectively increased in the PDMF@LGG treated group. LDA effect size (LEfSe) analysis was further performed to explore the differential gut microbiome composition among the groups (Supplementary Fig. 29). The histogram of LDA discriminant showed the significant differences in gut microbiome in each group, and the cladogram visually presented the multi-taxonomy circle from inside out, which represented the taxonomy level from phylum to genus. Consistent with the above results, LefSe clearly exhibited the enrichment of the aforementioned microbiome after PDMF@LGG treatment. These findings confirmed that PDMF@LGG was beneficial in regulating gut microbiome abundance and ameliorating dysbiosis.

Although our study revealed that the therapeutic strategy of modulating the microorganism-TMA-TMAO axis relying on PDMF@LGG was advantageous in reducing atherosclerotic plaques, it remained unclear whether other gut microbiome-associated metabolites exerted anti-atherosclerosis effects. Therefore, LC-MS/MS metabolomics assay was used to identify the differential metabolites in the serum. Orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) plot showed the distinguished tendency of metabolites among the groups (Fig. 7l). Additionally, the heatmap of serum metabolic profiles also intuitively visualized the metabolite differences, including fatty acids, amino acid and its metabolites, glycoprotein, etc (Supplementary Fig. 30). To further understand the classification and functional properties of these metabolites, KEGG pathway annotation was conducted and key metabolic pathways were noted. Compared with the saline group, the LGG and PMF-treated groups were involved in carbohydrate digestion and absorption, ABC transporters, and starch and sucrose metabolism. Notably, LGG treatment significantly regulated the pathways of unsaturated fatty acids biosynthesis, arginine biosynthesis, and metabolism of alanine, aspartate and glutamate, and PMF treatment mainly participated in the vascular smooth muscle contraction and arachidonic acid metabolism (Fig. 7m, n). The metabolic pathways of primary bile acid biosynthesis, cholesterol metabolism, and choline metabolism in cancer, which were found to be tightly associated with the onset and progression of atherosclerosis, were significantly enriched after PDMF@LGG treatment (Fig. 7o).

Interactions between the gut microbiota and serum metabolites serve a vital function in atherosclerosis pathogenesis. Consequently, we investigated the association between differential microbes and metabolites under PDMF@LGG treatment in comparison to the saline group. The cluster heatmap based on genus-level taxonomy was drawn (Supplementary Fig. 31). Detailed information on the top 20 differential metabolites and microbes was also provided (Fig. 7p, q). The results showed that Roseburia and unidentified Clostridia were positively correlated with Trigonelline and Ectoine, and negatively correlated with 13(R)-HODE. Roseburia also kept a negative association with 12,13-EpOME and 13-HOTrE. Overall, in addition to regulating the intestinal microbiota-TMA-TMAO axis, PDMF@LGG treatment also modulated the composition of the gut microbiome and caused changes in key metabolites in the serum, thereby driving the enhanced therapeutic efficacy in hypercholine diet-induced atherosclerotic mice.

Discussion

As an atypical factor that aggravates atherosclerosis, elevated TMAO caused by excessive intake of choline-rich foods in Western diets attracted increasing attention, which can be traced back to the disorder of intestinal microbiota and choline metabolism33. Therefore, it provides an inspirational strategy to combat atherosclerosis by reducing circulating TMAO through inhibiting the intestinal microbiota-TMA-TMAO axis. Aiming at this purpose, we developed a nano-functionalized probiotic PDMF@LGG, where the PMF prodrug nanoparticles with ROS scavenging and stimuli-responsive drug delivery abilities were grafted onto the surface of LGG via a polydopamine coating. Through oral administration, PDMF@LGG could sustainably colonize and maintain activity in the intestinal tract, demonstrating a strong stickiness and resistance to oxidative stress injury. Furthermore, PDMF@LGG could improve the intestinal barrier function, regulate the microbial homeostasis, and persistently inhibit the generation and absorption of TMA. As a result, PDMF@LGG could suppress the circulating TMAO levels, thereby efficiently inhibiting the progression of atherosclerosis and providing a strategy for clinical administration of atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease.

In order to reduce TMA production, we introduced choline analogue FMC to interfere with the microbial TMA-generated enzyme CutC. The good solubility of FMC in water enables its rapid metabolism, thus limiting the retention in the intestinal tract22. PDMF@LGG integrating drug sustained-release and probiotics carrying effectively addressed this problem, enhancing drug utilization and reducing administration frequency. Furthermore, our study identified that the level of oxidative stress in the colon significantly increased after being given a high-choline diet. Accordingly, it was feasible to continuously release FMC drug in the intestinal tract by introducing ROS response structures. When exposed to a high level of ROS, the methyl sulfide groups in MEMA could realize a hydrophobic-to-hydrophilic conversion, and the oxalate ester bonds in FAOE could be broken, which enabled the release of FMC drug from PMF nanoparticles28,34–36.

LGG probiotics were employed to restore a beneficial microbial balance. However, the efficacy was severely limited due to the harsh environments in the gastrointestinal tract, the lack of colonization stability as well as the poor survival ability under pathological oxidative stress37. In this work, polydopamine was deposited on the surface of LGG through covalent bonds, enabling probiotics to efficiently resist the damage of gastric acid, intestinal fluid, and bile salts without affecting their proliferation ability. Meanwhile, the strong adhesiveness of polydopamine provided sufficient time for the retention of probiotics in the intestinal tract, and reduced the non-specific absorption of nanoparticles through the intestinal mucosa, significantly lowering the contents in the blood and main organs. What’s more, PMF nanoparticles could also scavenge hydrogen peroxide and help LGG probiotics overcome the pathological intestinal environment with high levels of oxidative stress and protect the activity of probiotics38. PDMF@LGG significantly improved the protection to the integrity of the intestinal barrier, thereby blocking the TMA from entering the blood for further conversion to TMAO39.

Within the construction of PDMF@LGG, FMC drug encapsulated in PMF nanoparticles was utilized to inhibit TMA production in the intestinal flora, while LGG probiotics were expected to protect the integrity of the intestinal barrier and prevent TMA invasion into the bloodstream40,41. TMA entering the bloodstream needs to be converted into TMAO under the action of FMO3 enzyme in the liver, which makes atherosclerosis susceptible. Interestingly, we found that the expression of hepatic FMO3 decreased under PDMF@LGG treatment. There was evidence pointing out that insulin resistance caused by high TMAO levels might increase the expression of FMO3, which supported our findings11,17,42. The multiple adverse effects of TMAO jointly exacerbate the progression and instability of atherosclerosis, including recruiting immune cells, triggering inflammation, and disrupting lipid metabolism. By reducing systemic TMAO, PDMF@LGG could effectively inhibit the progression of atherosclerosis. Our study demonstrated that PDMF@LGG reduced macrophages infiltration and lipid accumulation within plaques. Meanwhile, the reduction in collagen degradation and MMP-9 secretion contributed to enhancing the stability of plaque. Thus, PDMF@LGG reduced the circulating TMAO levels by decreasing the production and absorption of TMA, ultimately benefiting the anti-atherosclerotic efficacy and broadening the range of treatable atherosclerosis43,44.

What’s more, PDMF@LGG participated in the regulation of gut microbiota composition, which may affect the progression of atherosclerosis. Lachnospiraceae has been reported to be correlated inversely with cardiovascular diseases, possibly due to its role in intestinal barrier repair, microbiota homeostasis, and production of secondary metabolites45,46. The abundance of Clostridia and Oscillospiraceae was verified to prevent metabolic syndrome and exert anti-atherosclerotic effects through the regulation of lipid metabolism47–49. Roseburia is associated with regulating glycolysis-fatty acid metabolism and lowering inflammatory responses, thus contributing to cardiovascular health50–52. Furthermore, atherosclerosis is accompanied with metabolic remodeling in which gut microbiota may be involved53. The serum metabolomics and correlation analyses revealed a close association between the changes in the composition of gut microbiota and the differences in some serum metabolites after PDMF@LGG treatment. Among them, many metabolites associated with Roseburia, such as Trigonelline and Ectoine, were observed increased, which were beneficial for cardiovascular diseases for their strong antioxidant and anti-inflammatory activity54–56, while 13(R)-HODE decreased which was reported to exacerbate atherosclerosis through facilitating foam cell formation, inducing apoptosis, and promoting oxidative stress57,58. Roseburia also kept a negative association with 12,13-EpOME and 13-HOTrE, which influenced the immune inflammation responses and oxidative stress59,60. Therefore, we speculated that the partial therapeutic efficacy of PDMF@LGG against atherosclerosis was involved in the changes of gut microbiome abundance and metabolic remodeling, which provided more clues for subsequent studies on regulating intestinal microorganisms for atherosclerosis treatment.

Collectively, this study leveraged the advantages of probiotics and nanoparticles to improve intestinal microecology and reduce systemic TMAO levels, collaboratively achieving the suppression of high-TMAO-induced atherosclerosis. The convenient platform of surface-coated modified probiotics and nanotechnology-assisted drug delivery provided solid evidence for the development of oral treatment options for atherosclerosis and other diseases.

Methods

Bacteria and cell culture

Lacticaseibacillus rhamnosus GG (LGG) was purchased from China Center of Industrial Culture Collection (CICC 6141). LGG was cultured in modified MRS medium (Beijing Solarbio Science & Technology), which consisted of 10 g L−1 proteose peptone, 10 g L−1 beef extract, 5 g L−1 yeast extract, 20 g L−1 dextrose, 1 g L−1 polysorbate 80, 5 g L−1 sodium acetate, 2 g L−1 ammonium citrate, 2 g L−1 K2HPO4, 0.2 g L−1 MgSO4·7H2O, 0.05 g L−1 MnSO4·H2O, at the shaking speed of 225 rpm at 37 °C. The human colon adenocarcinoma (HT29) cells were purchased from Procell (CL-0118) and cultured at 37 °C in 5% CO2 atmosphere using DMEM medium (Gibco) supplemented with 10% (v/v) fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco) and 1% (w/v) penicillin-streptomycin (Gibco).

Animals

All animal experiments were approved by the Laboratory Animal Research Center of Sir Run Run Shaw Hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine. The animal protocols were assessed and approved by the Animal Laboratory Ethics Committee of Zhejiang University (grant number ZJU20220463). Male C57BL/6 J and ApoE−/− mice (age 6-7 weeks) were supplied by Shanghai Laboratory Animal Center, housed in SPF class animal facility at a controlled temperature of 20–22 °C, relative humidity of 50–60 % and 12 h light-dark cycles with ad libitum access to commercial pellet diet and water. The irradiated high-choline diets with 1.3 % choline were purchased from Ready Dietech.

Synthesis of PMF polymer

Fluorocholine (FMC, 1.6 g, 10 mmol, Adamas Reagent) was dissolved in dimethylsulfoxide (DMSO, 30 mL, Rhawn Chemistry) with the existence of triethylamine (TEA, 3 g, 30 mmol, Rhawn Chemistry). Then tetrahydrofuran solution (THF, 20 mL, Rhawn Chemistry) of allyl oxaloyl chloride (1.8 g, 12 mmol, Adamas Reagent) was dropwise added and further stirred for 24 h. The reaction solution was recrystallized with THF and purified by chromatography on silica gel. The product fluorocholine allyl oxaloyl ester (FAOE) was characterized with 1H NMR and HRMS spectra (2.57 g, yield 82.9%)1.H NMR spectra were performed on a spectrometer operating at 400 MHz (Bruker AMX-400). The HRMS spectra were recorded using a Shimadzu LCMS-IT-TOF instrument1. H NMR (DMSO-d6, 400 MHz): δ = 3.24 (s, 6H), 3.26 (t, 2H), 3.52 (t, 2H), 3.83 (m, 2H), 5.47 (s, 1H), 5.18 (s, 1H), 5.78 (m, 3H). HRMS (ESI+): calcd for C10H17FNO4 [M]+ 234.2469, found 234.1127.

The poly(MEMA-co-FAOE) polymer was further synthesized by RAFT polymerization. Specifically, 4-Cyanopentanoic acid dithiobenzoate (140 mg, 0.5 mmol, Sigma-Aldrich), 2,2’-azobis(2-methylpropionitrile) (AIBN, 20.5 mg, 0.125 mmol, Adamas Reagent), 2-(methylthio) ethyl methacrylate (MEMA, 0.8 g, 5 mmol, Adamas Reagent) and FAOE (1.35 g, 5 mmol) were dissolved in a mixed solution of THF and DMSO at a Schlenk flask. After three cycles of a freeze-pump-thaw procedure, the reaction was carried out under the protection of Ar at 70 °C for 24 h. The resulting solution was dialyzed against deionized water (MCWO = 3000) for 24 h, followed by freeze drying to obtain poly(MEMA-co-FAOE) (PMF) (1.97 g, yield 86.03%).

Preparation of PMF and DiI-labelled nanoparticles

PMF (10 mg) was dissolved in 1.0 mL of DMSO and added dropwise into 3.0 mL of PBS and stirred for 2 h. After dialysis against PBS for another 12 h, the solution was extruded through the 0.22 μm syringe filter to obtain the PMF nanoparticle solution (2.0 mg mL−1). In order to label the nanoparticles with DiI probe, the DiI (1 mg, Sigma-Aldrich) was added during the dissolution of PMF polymer, where the fluorescent nanoparticles DiI@PMF were prepared.

Characterization of the physicochemical properties of PMF nanoparticles

Dynamic light scattering (DLS) and transmission electron microscope (TEM) were used to characterize the micellar size and morphology of PMF nanoparticles. The particle size of nanoparticles was recorded by DLS on a Malvern Zetasizer Nano ZS. The morphology was observed by a Hitachi H-600 TEM with an accelerating voltage of 10 KV, and the sample was dyed with uranyl acetate before measurement.

To assess the ROS responsiveness of PMF, the nanoparticles were incubated in different concentrations of H2O2 solutions, where the size variation was recorded and the morphology was observed by TEM. Furthermore, the H2O2 concentrations in the colon tissue of male C57BL/6 J and ApoE−/− mice fed with high-choline diets were measured. Briefly, mice were randomly assigned to different groups and fed with diets supplemented with 1.3% choline. After 0, 3, and 7 days, the mice were sacrificed, and the colon tissues were harvested and washed, and then frozen in liquid nitrogen. According to the guidelines of the hydrogen peroxide detection kit (Beyotime Biotechnology), the colon tissues were weighed and thoroughly homogenized, and the supernatants were extracted for the measurement of H2O2 concentrations. On the other hand, the ROS-triggered FMC delivery was evaluated. Briefly, 1 mL of PMF nanoparticles solution was added into a dialysis bag (MWCO = 3500), which was incubated in 10 mL PBS with different concentrations of H2O2 at 37 °C. 0.5 mL sample was withdrawn at selected time intervals, and 0.5 mL fresh release medium was added. The released FMC was calculated based on the HPLC measurement. Furthermore, the ability of H2O2 scavenging of PMF nanoparticles was also determined by a catalase activity detection kit under various H2O2 concentrations for 1 h, while the protection of PMF on LGG was also indicated under 1 mM H2O2 solution by measuring the LGG viability after 12 h through CCK-8 assay (Yeasen Biotechnologies).

Preparation of PD@LGG and PDMF@LGG

LGG probiotics were cultured in the modified MRS medium at 37 °C with a shaking speed of 225 rpm until the OD 600 value was approximately 1.0. After being washed with PBS three times, dopamine solution (0.5 mg mL−1, Aladdin) was added, and LGG was stirred at the same speed and temperature for 4 h. Following PBS washing three times to remove unbound dopamine, PD@LGG was collected.

In order to arm the PD@LGG with PMF, modified PMF nanoparticles based on the EDC/NHS reaction were synthesized. Briefly, PMF nanoparticles (2 mg mL−1) in PBS solution were first modified with EDC (0.16 mg mL−1, Aladdin) and NHS (0.08 mg mL−1, Aladdin) for 1 h. At the same time, PBS solution of LGG (at an absorbance of 1.0 at 600 nm) was stirred for 1 h with the addition of dopamine (0.5 mg mL−1). After being washed with PBS three times, the LGG was further co-incubated with modified PMF nanoparticles (2.0 mg mL−1) and dopamine (0.5 mg mL−1) for another 3 h. The nano-functionalized LGG (PDMF@LGG) was purified through PBS washing and stored at 4 °C.

Characterization of PD@LGG and PDMF@LGG

To evaluate the integration of PMF nanoparticles on LGG probiotics, TEM (Thermo Scientific) was used to observe the morphology of LGG, PD@LGG, and PDMF@LGG. Meanwhile, DiI-labeled PMF was also used to arm LGG, which was stained with Hoechst 33342, and the fluorescent signals of PMF and LGG were detected under a CLSM (Nikon A1 Ti). LGG, PD@LGG, and PDMF@LGG were further prepared and freeze-dried, and the functional groups were determined by FT-IR spectrometer (ThermoFisher Scientific).

Binding ability detection of polydopamine and PMF nanoparticles on LGG

LGG was first incubated with dopamine solution (0.5 mg mL−1) for 1 h to prepare dopamine pre-coated LGG. Subsequently, after being washed with PBS three times, pre-coated LGG was co-incubated with dopamine solution (0.5 mg mL−1) or a mixture of dopamine solution (0.5 mg mL−1) and modified PMF nanoparticles (2.0 mg mL−1) for another 1, 2, or 3 h. Following centrifugation and washing, PD@LGG and PDMF@LGG at different incubation time points were resuspended and the absorption peaks at A275 nm were detected using UV-vis (ThermoFisher Scientific) to determine the contents of polydopamine coated on the surface of LGG.

The binding ability of modified PMF nanoparticles on PDMF@LGG was also investigated. Briefly, fluorescent DiI@PMF nanoparticles were synthesized and reacted with the aforementioned EDC/NHS solution to obtain modified DiI@PMF. Meanwhile, polydopamine pre-coated LGG was harvested through co-incubation of LGG and dopamine solution for 1 h. After being washed with PBS three times, dopamine solution (0.5 mg mL−1) with different concentrations of modified DiI@PMF nanoparticles (0.4, 1.0, and 2.0 mg mL−1) was added for incubation with LGG for another 1, 2, or 3 h. At the predetermined time points, the mixture was centrifuged at 400 x g for 15 min and the supernatant containing unbound DiI@PMF was collected and centrifuged again to remove LGG. The fluorescence intensity in the supernatant was measured using a multifunctional microplate reader (Molecular Devices) to estimate the concentrations of free DiI@PMF. Based on the concentration differences, the contents of PMF conjugated to PD@LGG were calculated.

Proliferation measurement of LGG, PD@LGG, and PDMF@LGG

To evaluate whether the adhesion of polydopamine coating and PMF nanoparticles would affect the viability and proliferation of LGG, the growth curves of LGG, PD@LGG, and PDMF@LGG were first measured. LGG, PD@LGG, and PDMF@LGG were diluted and seeded into a 96-well plate, with an initial absorbance at 600 nm of approximately 0.15. Over the following 12-h incubation at 37 °C, the OD values were recorded at 600 nm with a microplate reader every 30 min. Furthermore, the flat colony counting method was conducted to evaluate the viable bacteria at different time points. Briefly, equal amounts of LGG, PD@LGG, and PDMF@LGG were subjected to modified MRS medium at a dilution of 1:100 and incubated at 37 °C with a shaking speed of 225 rpm. At 0, 1, 2, 4, 8, and 12 h, the bacteria were collected and the bacterial solution was spread on MRS agar plates in 10-fold sequential dilutions. After incubation at 37 °C, the colonies were counted to calculate the colony-forming units (CFU).

In vitro environment resistance assay for PDMF@LGG

To simulate the gastrointestinal environments in vitro, SGF (pH 1.5) supplemented with pepsin (0.32%), SIF (pH 6.8) supplemented with trypsin (10 mg/ml), and bile salt (0.4%) solutions were prepared. Equal amounts of LGG and PDMF@LGG were exposed to the SGF, SIF, and bile salt solutions, and incubated at 37 °C with a shaking speed of 225 rpm for 1, 2, and 4 h. After PBS washing, the bacterial suspension was incubated with CCK-8 reagent (Yeasen Biotechnologies) for 1 h. The absorbance at 450 nm was measured to determine the proliferation rate.

In vitro cytotoxicity assessment

The in vitro cytotoxicity of PMF was characterized on HT29 cells via CCK-8 assay. Typically, a density of 5000 cells per well was seeded in 96-well plates. After 24 h of culturing, the mediums were replaced with 200 μL fresh medium containing PMF ranging from 1 to 100 μg mL−1. After another 24 h of incubation, 20 μL of CCK-8 solution (Yeasen Biotechnologies) was added and co-cultured with the cells for 2 h. The absorbance at 450 nm was measured to calculate the in vitro cytotoxicity.

In vitro adhesion evaluation of PDMF@LGG

HT29 cells were cultured in glass dishes (105 cells per dish) and treated with LGG, PD@LGG, PMF, and PDMF@LGG, where PMF was labeled with DiI and LGG was stained with MycoLight Green JJ98 (AAT Bioquest). After 2 h, the cells were washed with PBS three times before being stained with Hoechst 33342 and observed under CLSM (Nikon A1 Ti).

In vivo acute toxicity evaluation

Male C57BL/6 J mice were randomly assigned to four groups and orally treated with saline, LGG (108 CFU), PMF (10 mg kg−1), and PDMF@LGG (108 CFU). After 24 h treatment, the mice were sacrificed, and the major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney) and all parts of the gastrointestinal tracts (stomach, duodenum, jejunum, ileum, cecum, and colon) were isolated. The histological sections were prepared and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E, Beijing Solarbio Science & Technology).

In vivo and ex vivo colonization of PDMF@LGG in the intestinal canal

C57BL/6 J male mice were used to investigate the colonization and retention time of PDMF@LGG as well as its different formulations. Briefly, the mice were fed with LGG (108 CFU), PMF (10 mg kg−1), PD@LGG (108 CFU), and PDMF@LGG (108 CFU), where PMF were labeled with DiI and LGG were stained with MycoLight Green JJ98. Firstly, the adhesion ability of PDMF@LGG in the intestine was evaluated. The intestinal tracts were separated from the mice after 2 h since oral administration, and the ex vivo fluorescence of the entire intestinal tracts and cecum was measured. Subsequently, the cecum was prepared into sections and stained with DAPI. The distribution of fluorescent signals from PMF and LGG in the cecal tissue were observed under a CLSM (Nikon A1 Ti). Furthermore, the mice were administered different treatments over a longer period to investigate the retention time of PDMF@LGG in the intestine. At 6, 12, and 24 h post-administration, the mice were anesthetized and the fluorescent signals of PMF and LGG were detected in vivo by an IVIS spectrum system (Lumina LT). At the end of imaging, mice were sacrificed with isoflurane for intestine harvest and the ex vivo fluorescence of the entire intestinal tracts was determined. To further investigate the colonization and distribution of PDMF@LGG, the small intestine, cecum, and colon were separated and sliced into sections, and stained with DAPI. The distribution of fluorescent signals from PMF and LGG was observed under a CLSM (Nikon A1 Ti).

In vivo metabolism and distribution of PDMF@LGG

The major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney) were harvested at predetermined time points (6, 12, and 24 h) following administration of LGG (108 CFU), PMF (10 mg kg−1), PD@LGG (108 CFU), and PDMF@LGG (108 CFU), where PMF were labeled with DiI and LGG were stained with MycoLight Green JJ98. The fluorescence signals of PMF and LGG in each organ at different time points were observed through an IVIS spectrum system (Lumina LT).

To determine the absorption and metabolism profiles of PMF in the blood, mice were randomly orally administered PMF (10 mg kg−1) and PDMF@LGG (108 CFU), where PMF were labeled with DiI. At the time points of 10 min, 30 min, 1 h, 2 h, 4 h, 8 h, 12 h, 24 h, 36 h, and 48 h, blood samples were collected, and the fluorescence intensities of PMF were detected under a multifunctional microplate reader (Molecular Devices).

Histology, immunohistochemistry and immunofluorescence staining

The liver and colon tissues of mice were excised and fixed in 4 % paraformaldehyde. 5 μm-thick paraffin sections were prepared and underwent the procedures of dewaxing with xylene and rehydration by gradient dilutions of ethanol solution. Following permeabilization with 0.3 % TritonX-100, and blocking with 5 % bovine serum albumin (BSA) at room temperature, the sections were incubated with the following primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight: FMO3 (1:100, Abclonal) in liver tissues; ZO-1 (1:200, Proteintech), Occludin (1:200, Proteintech), and Claudin-1 (1:1000, Proteintech) in colon tissues. Subsequently, Alexa Fluor 594-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibody (1:1000, ThermoFisher Scientific) was added, and the cell nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. Fluorescence images were captured using a CLSM (Nikon A1 Ti), and the fluorescent intensities were quantified using Image J v1.51.

The aorta roots were isolated, frozen, and embedded in OCT compound to prepare serial sections with a thickness of 8 μm. Oil red O (ORO, Sigma-Aldrich), H&E (Beijing Solarbio Science & Technology), and Masson’s trichrome (Beijing Solarbio Science & Technology) staining were performed, respectively, under the guidance of manufacturer protocols. Following washing, drying, and mounting, the stained sections were observed and recorded under a light microscope (Olympus). The analyses were carried out using Image J v1.51.

Immunohistochemistry staining of mice aorta roots was also conducted. Briefly, the sections were subjected to antigen retrieval with Tris-EDTA buffer (pH 9.0) and blocked with 5 % BSA at room temperature. The primary antibodies CD68 (1:1000, Proteintech) and MMP-9 (1:100, Proteintech) were added to the sections at 4 °C overnight, followed by incubation with horseradish peroxidase(HRP)-conjugated goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody (1:100, Abclonal) and 3,3’-diaminobenzidine (DAB) staining (Beyotime Biotechnology). The cell nuclei were stained with hematoxylin. The mounted sections were observed and recorded under a light microscope (Olympus). The analyses were carried out using Image J v1.51.

Western blotting

The liver and colon tissues were harvested, and total proteins were extracted, respectively, with RIPA buffer. After quantification by BCA protein assay, proteins were separated by electrophoresis on a polyacrylamide gel and transferred onto a polyvinylidene difluoride membrane. The membranes were blocked by 5% non-fat milk for an hour at 37 °C. Then, the membranes containing liver proteins were incubated with corresponding primary antibodies against FMO3 (1:1000, Abcam) and HRP-conjugated GAPDH (1:5000, Proteintech) overnight at 4 °C. The membranes containing colon proteins were incubated with corresponding primary antibodies against ZO-1 (1:1000, Abcam), Occludin (1:1000, Abcam), Claudin-1 (1:1000, Abcam), and HRP-conjugated β-tubulin (1:5000, Proteintech) overnight at 4 °C. Afterwards, the membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary anti-rabbit antibody (1:10000, Proteintech) at 37 °C for 2 h. The images of protein bands were developed using a ChemiDoc MP system (BioRad). The analyses were carried out using Image J v1.51.

RT-qPCR

Total mRNA from colon tissues was extracted using the RNA-Quick Purification Kit (ESscience) following the manufacturer’s protocols. After quantification by NanoDrop 2000 (ThermoFisher Scientific), the mRNA was mixed with RT Premix and RNase free ddH2O for reverse transcription to synthesize cDNA. RT-qPCR was performed with the reaction system of cDNA, SYBR Green Master Mix, corresponding primers, and RNase-free ddH2O using a QuantStudio 6 Flex Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems). The relative expressions of genes were calculated with β-tubulin as a reference gene. The sequences of specific primers were detailed in the Supplementary Table 1.

In vivo dose-effect assay of PDMF@LGG

Male C57BL/6 J mice were randomly divided into five groups: a control group, a saline group, and three PDMF@LGG groups to determine the appropriate oral dose of nano-functionalized probiotics. Mice in the control group were fed with normal diets, while the other groups were fed diets supplemented with 1.3% choline. Mice in the PDMF@LGG groups were orally administered PDMF@LGG at different dosages (107, 108, and 109 CFU every time) every two days for 6 weeks, while mice in the saline group received no treatment other than saline. At the end of treatment, plasma was collected for the measurements of TMA and TMAO. Mice were sacrificed with isoflurane and the livers and intestines were harvested. Subsequently, the expressions of hepatic FMO3 and the intestinal barrier proteins, including ZO-1, Occludin, and Claudin-1, in colonic tissues were determined by immunofluorescence images.

In vivo regulation on TMAO metabolism

For the establishment of the model mice with high TMAO levels, male C57BL/6 J mice were fed with high-choline diet for 6 weeks. Along with a high-choline diet, the mice were randomly divided into four groups and orally treated with saline, PMF (0.2 mg every time), LGG (108 CFU every time), and PDMF@LGG (108 CFU every time) every two days. Mice in the control group were provided a normal diet and received no extra treatment. To further investigate the therapeutic effects of different formulations, additional groups were set up and administered with FMC (0.05 mg every time), polydopamine (0.05 mg every time), and physical mixture of polydopamine, PMF and LGG (a mixure of 0.05 mg polydopamine, 0.2 mg PMF, and 108 CFU LGG every time) every two days. After 6 weeks, the mice were sacrificed and the plasma was collected for the detection of systemic TMA, TMAO, and other choline metabolites.

In vivo treatment of PDMF@LGG to atherosclerosis

The male ApoE−/− mice were fed with a high-choline diet to construct a high-TMAO induced atherosclerosis model, which were randomly grouped and orally administered with saline, LGG (108 CFU each time), PMF (0.2 mg each time), and PDMF@LGG (108 CFU each time) every two days for 16 consecutive weeks. At the end of treatment, the mice were sacrificed, and the choline-related metabolites in plasma were measured. The aortic roots of atherosclerotic mice were embedded in OCT compound and sectioned. Sections were stained with ORO, H&E, and Masson’s Trichrome. In addition, immunohistochemistry staining, including CD68 and MMP-9, was also conducted. The images were observed and recorded under a light microscope (Olympus).

ORO staining of aortas

To investigate the atherosclerotic progression, the aortas of mice were separated and lengthwise opened, where the en face was stained with ORO. Briefly, the aortas were roughly immersed in the fresh ORO solution and stained at room temperature for 1 h. Then, the aortas were washed with 60% isopropyl alcohol to remove free ORO. After washing with ddH2O, the en face views of the aortas were taken. The analyses were carried out using Image J v1.51.

Microbiome and Metabolomic Analysis

After four months of different treatments in high-choline diet induced atherosclerotic mice, three pellets of fecal samples were collected and serum were extracted, which were frozen in liquid nitrogen immediately and sent to Metware Metabolic Biotechnology Co., LTD (Wuhan, China) for microbiome and metabolomics analysis. The data were analyzed using a free online platform called Metware Cloud (https://cloud.metware.cn). Briefly, total genome DNA from feces was extracted using CTAB method, and the purity and concentration were monitored. Genes of 16S V4 region were amplified used specific primers (515 F: GTGCCAGCMGCCGCGGTAA; 806 R: GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT) with the barcode. PCR products were mixed in equal density ratios and purified with Qiagen Gel Extraction Kit (Qiagen, Germany). Sequencing libraries were generated using TruSeq DNA PCR-Free Sample Preparation Kit (Illumina, USA), and the quantified libraries were sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq6000 platform, and 250bp paired-end reads were generated. Effective tags were obtained after data split, filtration, sequence assembly and chimera removal. ASV denoising was performed by Deblur (Version 1.1.1) in the QIIME2 software (Version 2023.2). The Silva Database was utilized to annotate taxonomic information. The inverse-Simpson diversity index was calculated to assess the alpha diversity, while NMDS and PCoA were performed to assess beta diversity. Relative abundance of gut microbiome at family and genus levels were visualized and quantitative data of representative microbiome at different levels were shown.

The pre-treated serum samples were transferred for LC-MS analysis using an LC-ESI-MS/MS system (UPLC, ExionLC AD; MS, QTRAP® System). The original data were qualitatively analyzed based on the Metware Database and the quantification was performed using multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) by triple quadrupole scans. Differential metabolites were determined according to VIP (VIP > 1) and P value (P value < 0.05, Student’s t test). VIP values were extracted from OPLS-DA models, which also contained score plots to show the inter-group differences. Identified metabolites were annotated using KEGG Compound database (http://www.kegg.jp/kegg/compound/) and annotated metabolites were then mapped to KEGG Pathway database (http://www.kegg.jp/kegg/pathway.html).

Statistics and reproducibility

All experiments in this study were conducted at least three times, and the quantitative data were presented as means ± standard deviation (s.d.). Differences between groups were performed using an unpaired two-tailed Student’s t test for data with normal distribution or a Mann-Whitney U test for data with non-normal distribution. Multi-group comparisons were performed using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s multiple comparisons. All statistical analysis was performed using SPSS (version 20.0) and GraphPad Prism (version 8.0). Statistical significance was assessed at P < 0.05. Detailed statistical tests are detailed in the figure legends.
