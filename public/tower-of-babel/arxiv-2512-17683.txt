Upper Bounds for Sequence Saturation
Shihan Kanungo

Abstract
. In this paper, we study the saturation function Sat ⁡ ( n , u ) \Sat(n,u) for sequences. Saturation for sequences was introduced by Anand, Geneson, Kaustav, and Tsai (2021), who proved that Sat ⁡ ( n , u ) = O ⁡ ( n ) \Sat(n,u)=O(n) for two-letter sequences u u and conjectured that this bound holds for all sequences. We present an algorithm that constructs a u u -saturated sequence on n n letters and apply it to show Sat ⁡ ( n , u ) = O ⁡ ( n ) \Sat(n,u)=O(n) for several families of sequences u u , including all repetitions of the form a ​ b ​ c ​ a ​ b ​ c ​ … abcabc\dots . We further establish Sat ⁡ ( n , u ) = O ⁡ ( n ) \Sat(n,u)=O(n) for a broad class of sequences of the form a ​ a ​ … ​ b ​ b aa\dots bb . In addition, we prove that for most sequences u u , there exists an infinite u u -saturated sequence. For three-letter sequences of the form a ​ b ​ c ​ … ​ x ​ y ​ z abc\dots xyz , where a , b , c a,b,c are distinct and x ​ y ​ z xyz is a permutation of a ​ b ​ c abc , we show—under certain structural assumptions on u u —that Sat ⁡ ( n , u ) = O ⁡ ( n ) \Sat(n,u)=O(n) . Finally, we describe a linear program that computes the exact value of Sat ⁡ ( n , u ) \Sat(n,u) for arbitrary n n and u u .

Remark 1

Consider the finite analogue of the sequence

The same argument shows that inserting a letter between

In

Throughout, let

on the letters

, omitting its initial two letters

. In particular, the terminal segment of

is a full copy of
s_{3}(u)
on

This definition of

is directly analogous to the construction of the

-saturated sequence in Theorem 3.10 of

]
.

The sequence

Proof.

The proof is analogous to the last part of the proof of Theorem 3.10 in

]

Thus, it remains to determine when

We begin by analyzing the length of

. If

To compute

Therefore,

Theorem 7
.

Let
u=abc\dots xyz
be a three-letter sequence with

distinct. Suppose

avoids

, and hence

.

Proof.

We use a similar argument to that of Theorem 3.10 in
[
1
]
, but the 3-letter case is much more complicated (which is also why we need to impose many more conditions). The proof proceeds by induction and a careful case analysis of where a hypothetical copy of
u
could appear. The parameters
f_{0}(u)
and
f_{1}(u)
control how much space such a copy would need to take.

We proceed by induction on
n
. The base case holds since
s[3]=s_{3}
is
u
-saturated, and therefore avoids
u
. Assume inductively that
s[n-1]
avoids
u
.

Suppose for contradiction that
s[n]
contains a copy of
u
. We distinguish two cases.

Case 1:
One of the letters is
z
.
It is clear from the conditions that
u
is strongly irreducible, so the other two letters must be

and

, as all other letters appear strictly before the first

. The subsequence of

consisting of

is of the form

Pxyzxyz\dots,

where the part after
P
is isomorphic to
s_{3}
and
P
contains only

. Because the first three letters of

are distinct, any copy of

in this subsequence can use at most two letters before the first

xyzxyz\dots\quad\text{and}\quad yxzxyz\dots,

with both sequences of length
|s_{3}|
. The first is isomorphic to
s_{3}
, hence avoids
u
. In the second, if a copy of
u
exists, it must use the initial

; otherwise, the same copy could be embedded in the first sequence, a contradiction. Thus the copy is on the alphabet

, and its length is at least

|u|+f_{0}(u)+2f_{2}(u)-2,

where we subtract

, this contradicts (

).

Case 2:
None of the letters is

.
Let the letters be
\alpha,\beta,\gamma
in the order of their first appearance in

. The first

appears when extending

to

for some

. Since

is strongly irreducible, some

must appear after some

is among the last two letters of

, the middle block has length

, and

contains only the last two letters of the middle block. We now consider three subcases, depending on the ending of the middle block.

Case 2.1:
The middle block ends
\alpha\beta\gamma
.
We must check the following sequences of length

\alpha\beta\gamma\dots\alpha\beta\gamma,\quad\beta\alpha\gamma\dots\alpha\beta\gamma,\quad\alpha\beta\gamma\dots\alpha\gamma\beta,\quad\beta\alpha\gamma\dots\alpha\gamma\beta.

The first two follow from Case 1. For the latter two, let

be the reversal of

,

, so

satisfies the theorem’s hypotheses (note that we must rename the letters

when computing

). The reversal of the third sequence is isomorphic to the second sequence of Case 1 for

. For the fourth, any copy of

must use both the initial

and the terminal

Since this is

yet

).

Case 2.2:
The middle block ends

Here the relevant sequences are

\alpha\beta\gamma\dots\alpha\beta\gamma\alpha,\quad\beta\alpha\gamma\dots\alpha\beta\gamma\alpha,\quad\alpha\beta\gamma\dots\alpha\beta\alpha\gamma,\quad\beta\alpha\gamma\dots\alpha\beta\alpha\gamma,

each of length

Case 2.3:
The middle block ends
\gamma\alpha\beta
.
Here the sequences are

\alpha\beta\gamma\dots\alpha\beta\gamma\alpha\beta,\quad\beta\alpha\gamma\dots\alpha\beta\gamma\alpha\beta,\quad\alpha\beta\gamma\dots\alpha\beta\gamma\beta\alpha,\quad\beta\alpha\gamma\dots\alpha\beta\gamma\beta\alpha,

|s_{3}|
. The argument is identical to the previous subcases.

In all cases we obtain a contradiction, so

avoids

grows linearly in

.
∎
