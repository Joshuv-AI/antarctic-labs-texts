Facile synthesis of ZnO/Biochar Nanocomposites (ZBNcs) and investigation of their nano-priming effects on reducing the accumulation of arsenic (As) in Triticum aestivum cv. Azar-2
Authors: Zahra Souri, Naser Karimi, Elahe Ghanbari, Leila Norouzi, Abbu Zaid
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe main purpose of this study is to investigate the priming effect of ZnO/biochar nanocomposites (ZBNCs) in mitigating arsenic (As) toxicity in wheat plants. The research question focuses on how ZBNCs can reverse the adverse effects of As stress on plant growth and physiological functions. ZBNCs were synthesized via a one-step carbonization process using grape leaf waste. Characterization of the nanoparticles was performed using SEM, FT-IR spectroscopy, and XRD analysis. The synthesized nanoparticles (NPs) were then used for seed priming at concentrations of 0, 25, 50, and 75 mg/L to assess their effects on wheat plants exposed to As at concentrations of 0, 25, 50, and 100 mg/L.ResultsAs stress impaired wheat growth and physiological parameters in a dose-dependent manner. However, seed priming with ZBNCs reversed growth inhibition, reduced H₂O₂ content and shoot As accumulation, and enhanced POD and GST activities, indicating that ZBNCs mitigate As toxicity primarily through enzymatic antioxidant enhancement and reduced oxidative stress.ConclusionThis study demonstrates the potential of ZBNCs for mitigating As stress in wheat under greenhouse conditions, suggesting their promise for sustainable crop production in As-affected regions.

Introduction

In recent years, environmental contamination with heavy metals/metalloids has become a serious health risk due to their persistence and bioaccumulation in food chains [1–3]. The buildup of the environmental contaminants is attributable to two primary sources: human industrial endeavors (e.g., mining, manufacturing, and the combustion of fossil fuels) and naturally occurring processes (e.g., weathering and volcanic eruptions) [1, 4, 5]. Among these, one of the most hazardous toxic pollutants is arsenic (As), which is acknowledged as a major environmental hazard [2, 5]. Arsenic, as a metalloid, is not necessary for plant growth, but it can accumulate in plants to toxic levels [6, 7]. A significant public health risk arises from the permeation of As into the food chain [3]. Epidemiological studies confirm the harmful health effects of As exposure, which depends on its concentration and duration [2, 8]. Exposure to As leads to severe health consequences, including carcinogenic effects (e.g., lung, bladder, and squamous cell carcinomas) and non-carcinogenic disorders such as hyperpigmentation, cardiovascular diseases, neurological impairments, diabetes, and respiratory complications [2]. Concerning agricultural products, As toxicity is regarded as a significant risk because it can easily enter plants and accumulate in crops [6, 9]. It has been reported that As could induce toxic effects in wheat and enter the food chain through grain accumulation, posing serious risks to crop productivity [3]. Therefore, As toxicity in plants results in a serious threat to global food security. The absorption of As in plants induces considerable alterations in structural and physio-biochemical parameters, culminating in hindered growth and development [9, 10]. High levels of As in water or soil pose significant threats to photosynthetic pigments, proteins, and enzyme activity, thereby detrimentally influencing photochemical efficiency and the rate of photosynthesis, growth, and yield parameters [9, 11]. Accordingly, these physiological mechanisms are considered critical indicators of As-induced stress indication [10, 11].

Wheat (Triticum aestivum L.) ranks among the world’s most vital cereal crops and over 50% of the global population is dependent upon it for its sustenance [12]. As a primary source of calories, proteins, and vital nutrients, wheat is a key component of global food security, particularly in developing regions [3]. With the burgeoning global human population growth coupled with changing dietary habits, enhancing wheat yield and production efficiency has become a pressing agricultural priority. However, achieving sustainable productivity gains is challenged by factors such as climate variability, soil degradation, and limited arable lands [13]. To meet these challenges, the adoption of innovative and sustainable agricultural practices is imperative to ensure stable wheat supplies and support long-term food systems resilience.

Today, the integration of nanotechnology has catalyzed transformative advancements in agricultural and food systems, providing novel solutions to longstanding challenges in production, processing, and safety [14]. Nonetheless, nanotechnology can positively influence the agricultural field by reducing environmental harm and enhancing food security and sustainable productivity [14]. Nevertheless, the development of nanoparticles (NPs) through physical and chemical means has harmed the ecosystem. Therefore, in recent decades, nanotechnology research has shifted focus toward biogenic synthesis to aid the increasing use of NPs to conventional NP production methods, enabling biocompatible fabrication through biologically mediated reduction processes [15, 16]. The synthesis of biogenic NPs is an eco-friendly and sustainable approach that employs biological resources such as plants, bacteria, and fungi to produce NPs with distinct properties [14, 15]. This method has garnered significant attention due to its low toxicity, simplicity, and cost-effectiveness as compared to traditional chemical synthesis methods. For example, utilizing plant samples rich in phytochemicals can reduce metal ions, while simultaneously providing stabilizing agents, which can enhance the stability, and functionality of the NPs [17]. Furthermore, green synthesis methods can result in the production of biodegradable NPs, making them appropriate for diverse applications, including environmental remediation and catalysis [18]. Biogenic NPs often demonstrated improved biocompatibility properties, underscoring the importance of employing green synthesis in nanotechnology [19]. As research advances, understanding the mechanisms underlying the NP formation in biological systems continues to evolve, paving the way for innovative applications in agricultural and environmental science [15, 16].

Seed technologists routinely apply priming treatments to commercial seed plots to enhance physiological vigor and germination performance [20]. These treatments simultaneously activate stress-responsive mechanisms, improving crop establishment under challenging environmental circumstances [20–22]. Despite the reported efforts to further advance seed priming, innovative ideas and cutting-edge research must be introduced into this technological part of the agri-seed industry [20, 23]. Nano-priming is an emerging and innovative seed treatment technique that uses NPs to boost plant germination, growth, and stress resistance [24]. It enhances water uptake (via aquaporin genes and nanopores), activates enzymes for starch breakdown, and triggers beneficial oxidative stress responses. Compared to traditional methods, it’s more efficient and improves crop resilience [23, 24].

Zinc (Zn) serves as an essential micronutrient for plants, functioning as a structural component in over 300 enzymes and proteins [25, 26]. This critical element participates in numerous physio-biochemical processes, including photosynthesis, protein synthesis, antioxidant defense mechanisms, membrane stability, reactive oxygen species (ROS) detoxification, and regulation of gene expression under normal and stressful environmental factors [26]. Among various metal oxide NPs, zinc oxide nanoparticles (ZnO-NPs) have attracted significant scientific interests due to their distinctive photocatalytic and photo-oxidative properties [27]. While conventional Zn fertilizers like zinc oxide (ZnO) and zinc sulfate (ZnSO₄·H₂O or ZnSO₄·7 H₂O) are commonly used to address zinc deficiency, their effectiveness is often limited by poor solubility and low bioavailability in soils [13, 28]. ZnO-NPs present a promising alternative, offering enhanced solubility and improved Zn uptake efficiency. Studies have demonstrated that seed priming with ZnO-NPs could effectively increase Zn concentration in seeds, subsequently stimulating healthier seedling establishment and improved crop yield [29]. However, it is noteworthy that the physiological effects of ZnO-NPs exhibit species-specific responses and are influenced by plant developmental stages as well as NP characteristics such as size, shape, and surface properties [28, 30]. Previous studies have shown that Zn-based nanomaterials, including ZnO-NPs [31], and ZnS nanocomposites [32], can reduce As accumulation and toxicity in rice and wheat under As stress, while nanopriming with ZnO has enhanced germination, photosynthesis, and antioxidant defense in wheat and Amaranth under optimal conditions without As stress [13, 33]. However, none of these studies employed a biochar carrier in combination with ZnO-NPs or applied nano-priming specifically against As stress in wheat.

Despite the promising results of ZnO nanopriming for enhancing germination, photosynthesis, and antioxidant defense under optimal conditions [13, 33], and the efficacy of Zn-based nanomaterials for reducing As accumulation in rice and wheat [31, 32], none of these studies have combined ZnO-NPs with a biochar carrier or applied nano-priming specifically against As stress in wheat. Furthermore, conventional ZnO-NPs suffer from limitations such as burst release of Zn²⁺ ions, lack of As adsorption capacity, and potential phytotoxicity at elevated concentrations. To address these gaps, our study introduces ZnO/Biochar Nanocomposites (ZBNCs) derived from grape leaf waste, which offer distinct advantages over previously reported ZnO-NPs: (i) a dual-action mechanism combining As adsorption by biochar with ROS scavenging and antioxidant enhancement; (ii) controlled and sustained release of Zn²⁺ ions from the biochar matrix; (iii) green synthesis from agricultural waste with waste valorization; and (iv) reduced environmental input through seed nano-priming rather than direct soil or foliar application.

To address this knowledge gap, our study introduces ZnO/Biochar Nanocomposites (ZBNCs) as a novel seed nano-priming agent for As-contaminated conditions. Thus, this study aims to (1) develop a novel ZnO/Biochar Nanocomposites (ZBNCs) through a facile synthesis approach, (2) characterize its physicochemical properties, (3) evaluate its effectiveness as a nano-priming agent in reducing As toxicity in Triticum aestivum cv. Azar-2., and (4) assess the impact of ZBNCs on As accumulation to ensure food safety. The findings are expected to provide new insights into sustainable nano-enabled strategies for heavy metal stress mitigation in crops.

Materials and methods

Facile synthesis of ZnO/Biochar Nanocomposites (ZBNcs)

Zinc oxide-modified biochar nanocomposites were synthesized via a one-step carbonization process using grape leaf waste as a sustainable carbon precursor. The grape leaf waste was sliced, dried in an oven at 80 °C, and milled into a fine powder. Then, 1 g of the dried powder was added to 40mL of an aqueous solution containing 0.03 M Zn(NO₃)₂.4H2O (corresponding to a biomass: Zn precursor weight ratio of 1:0.3) and stirred for 2 h. The resulting precursor was transferred to a 100 mL Teflon-lined stainless-steel autoclave and heated at 200 °C for 6 h. After cooling to room temperature, the pH of the mixture was adjusted to 10.0 ± 0.1 using ammonia solution. This alkaline pH ensures complete hydrolysis of Zn²⁺ ions to form Zn(OH)₄²⁻ complexes, which subsequently decompose to ZnO nanorods, while also deprotonating biochar surface functional groups to enhance electrostatic attraction and uniform ZnO distribution. The mixture was then stirred at room temperature for an additional 2 h. The resulting products were centrifuged at 8,000×g for 15 min, and the precipitate was washed thrice with 50mL distilled water followed by two washes with 50mL absolute ethanol to remove unreacted ions, byproducts, and organic residues. The final product was dried at 60 °C for 12 h. Field Emission Scanning Electron Microscopy (FESEM), Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FTIR), and X-ray Diffraction (XRD) were employed to characterize the sample’s surface morphology, functional groups, crystal structure, and particle size distribution.

Seed priming and plant culture

The plant material used was wheat (Triticum aestivum cv. Azar-2), with seeds acquired from the Kermanshah Agricultural Research Center. To ensure surface sterility, the seeds underwent a 3-minute treatment in a 10% (v/v) sodium hypochlorite solution (commercial bleach) and were then thoroughly washed with distilled water. The seeds were primed with ZBNcs (0, 25, 50, and 75 mg L⁻¹) for 12 h at 25 °C. The priming duration of 12 h was selected based on established nanopriming protocols in wheat and other cereal crops [13, 33], which demonstrated that this duration achieves optimal NP adhesion to the seed coat and sufficient water uptake for metabolic activation without causing over-hydration or leaching of essential metabolites. Then the primed seeds were germinated in moist perlite. After germination, uniform seedlings were transferred to 1-liter polyethylene pots (four seedlings per pot) containing perlite and sand (2:1), and added nutrient solution composed of 0.5mM KNO3, 0.75mM Ca(NO3)2, 0.2mM MgSO4, 15µM H3BO3, 2µM MnCl2, 1µM ZnSO4, 0.5µM CuSO4, 0.5µM Na2MoO4. 2H2O and 50µM Fe- EDTA. Plants were grown in controlled greenhouse conditions (20 ± 5 °C, 16/8 h light/dark, 50% RH) and treated with sodium arsenate (0, 25, 50, and 100 mg L⁻¹) for three weeks. These concentrations were selected based on previously published dose-response studies [3, 34, 35], which demonstrated significant As-induced phytotoxicity within the range of 2–40 mg L⁻¹. Our range (25–100 mg L⁻¹) extends these findings to assess ZBNC efficacy under moderate to severe stress, with 0 mg L⁻¹ as the control. After treatment, the plants were harvested to assess various growth and physiological parameters, and As accumulation.

Growth parameters

After the treatment period was completed, plant samples were sequentially washed with running tap water, rinsed three times with deionized water to eliminate surface contaminants, and oven-dried at 60 °C to constant weight. The shoot weight was measured and recorded using a digital balance (precision: 0.001 g), respectively.

Photosynthetic pigments

Total chlorophyll (chlorophyll a + chlorophyll b), and carotenoid contents, were determined spectrophotometrically [36]. 0.05 g of the leaf was taken, and grinding was done by pestle and mortar with 10mL of 80% cold acetone for 5 min. The supernatant taken was centrifuged at 4000 ×g for 10 min and diluted with 80% aqueous acetone to a suitable concentration for spectrophotometric measurements. The pigment concentrations were determined spectrophotometrically using the absorbance values measured at 663 nm (chlorophyll a), 645 nm (chlorophyll b), and 470 nm (carotenoids) in 80% acetone extracts. Measurements were performed in 1.5 cm pathlength quartz cuvettes against an 80% acetone blank.

Hydrogen peroxide (H2O2)

The sample (0.01 g) was homogenized in 3 ml of 0.1% (w/v) TCA, centrifuged (10,000×g, 20 min), and 0.5 ml supernatant was mixed with 0.5 ml 10 mM potassium phosphate buffer (pH 7.0) and 1 ml 1 M KI. The concentration of H2O2 was determined by measuring the absorbance of the supernatant at 390 nm.

Antioxidant enzymes

Two milliliters of Tris-HCl buffer (pH 7.8) containing 0.1 mM EDTA and 1% PVP (w/v) were used to homogenize 0.05 g of fresh shoot tissue. For 20 min, the homogenate was centrifuged at 10,000×g. The activity of guaiacol peroxidase (POD, EC 1.11.1.7) was measured using a modified version of Chance and Maehly’s (1995) methodology [37]. The reaction mixture (3 mL) consisted of 2.7mL of 50mM potassium phosphate buffer (pH 7.0), 100µL of 1% (v/v) guaiacol, 300µL of 0.4% H₂O₂, and 100µL of enzyme extract. A spectrophotometer was used to quantify the increase in absorbance caused by guaiacol oxidation for one minute at 470 nm. POD activity was calculated using the extinction coefficient of 26.6 mM⁻¹ cm⁻¹ and expressed as µmol min⁻¹ mg⁻¹ protein. According to Rohman et al. (2009) [38], glutathione S-transferase (GST; EC 2.5.1.18) activity was measured spectrophotometrically by tracking the conjugation of reduced glutathione (GSH) with 1-chloro-2,4-dinitrobenzene (CDNB) at 340 nm (ε = 9.6 mM⁻¹ cm⁻¹). GST activity was expressed as µmol CDNB conjugated min⁻¹ mg⁻¹ protein.

Proline

The quantification of proline was performed according to the method of Bates et al. (1973) [39] using spectrophotometry. Following homogenization of 0.05 g of shoot material in 3% (w/v) sulfosalicylic acid and subsequent centrifugation, the supernatant was reacted with an equal volume of acid ninhydrin and glacial acetic acid. The reaction mixture was incubated in a boiling water bath (100 °C) for 60 min to develop color and was then immediately cooled on ice to quench the reaction. The proline-ninhydrin complex was then extracted with toluene for absorbance measurement at 520 nm.

Arsenic concentration

Dried plant samples (0.02 g) were digested with 2mL of 67% HNO₃ and 30% H₂O₂ (v/v) at 100 °C for 2 h. Digestates were analyzed using flame atomic absorption spectrometry (Shimadzu AA-6200) with hydride generation.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using two-way ANOVA (IBM SPSS 20.0) with As concentration (0-100 mg L⁻¹) and ZBNC priming concentration (0–75 mg L⁻¹) as factors, followed by Duncan’s multiple range test. Normality and homogeneity of variance assumptions were confirmed (p > 0.05). Data are reported as mean ± SE (n = 3), and means with different lowercase letters are significantly different at p < 0.05.

Results

Nanoparticles characterization, crystallinity and morphology analysis

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) micrographs depicting the surface structure of the ZnO-modified biochar NPs displayed at two different magnifications (1 μm in Fig. 1A μm in Fig. 1B) confirm the formation of carbon NPs with pronounced aggregation. While plant tissue is expected to exhibit a limited distribution of carbon NPs, environmental drying conditions lead to aggregation of the carbon matrix [40]. The images further show uniform dispersion and even distribution of ZnO-rod-shaped NPs, with lengths and average diameters of 100 to 300 nm and ~ 10 nm, respectively, embedded in the carbon matrix. FT-IR spectroscopy was employed to characterize the functional groups and surface composition of ZnO-modified biochar NPs. The FTIR spectrum of the ZnO-modified carbon NPs (Fig. 2) exhibits a strong absorption band at 3435 cm⁻¹, attributed to O–H/N–H stretching modes [41]. The absorption bands observed at 2938 cm⁻¹ and 2878 cm⁻¹ are characteristic of C–H stretching vibrations, typically associated with aliphatic hydrocarbon chains [42]. A distinct peak at ~ 1300 cm⁻¹ corresponds to C–O stretching vibrations, suggesting the presence of hydroxyl (–OH) groups on ZnO-modified carbon NP surface or bending vibrations of surface Zn–OH groups [43, 44]. The band at 1647 cm⁻¹ may arise from C = O stretching vibrations, potentially indicating carboxyl (–COOH) functionalities. The absorption band at 1380 cm⁻¹ corresponds to the C-H bending vibration of the methyl functional group [41]. The peaks observed at 1085 cm⁻¹ and 1046 cm⁻¹ correspond to the symmetric and asymmetric C-O-C stretching vibrations, respectively [41]. Besides, the absorption bands observed in the region of 900–669 cm⁻¹ arise from aromatic C–H bending vibrations [41]. The absorption band at 451 cm⁻¹ is attributed to the Zn–O stretching vibration, consistent with its reported characteristic frequency [45]. XRD analysis enables the determination of key structural parameters, including the peak intensity (reflecting crystallinity), peak position (2θ angle, indicative of lattice spacing and phase identification), and peak broadening (quantified as FWHM, which correlates with crystallite size and microstrain through the Scherrer equation). As shown in Fig. 3, the XRD pattern exhibits distinct diffraction peaks at 2θ = 31.84°, 34.52°, 36.33°, 47.63°, 56.71°, 62.96°, 68.13°, and 69.18°, which correspond to the (100), (002), (101), (102), (110), (103), (112), and (201) crystallographic planes of hexagonal wurtzite ZnO (JCPDS card no. 36-1451), respectively. These peak positions and their relative intensities confirm the high crystallinity and phase purity of the ZnO structure, with no detectable secondary phases [46]. In contrast, the broad diffraction peak centered at 2θ ≈ 25° corresponds to the (002) plane of amorphous carbon, reflecting the highly disordered graphitic structure derived from the grape leaf precursor [47]. The average crystallite size of the synthesized ZnO-NPs was determined using the Debye-Scherrer equation (Eq. 1), applied to the most intense diffraction peak (e.g., (101) plane at *2θ = ~36.3°). By analyzing FWHM, the calculated crystallite diameter was found to be 18 nm ± 2 nm, confirming the nano-crystalline nature of the ZnO-NPs.

Effect of ZBNcs priming on shoot dry weight (biomass)

As shown in Fig. 4, increasing As concentrations led to a progressive dose-dependent reduction in shoot biomass. In the control group (0 mg L⁻¹ As), seed priming with 75 mg L⁻¹ ZBNCs significantly enhanced shoot fresh weight compared to the non-primed control (p < 0.05). Under As stress (25, 50, and 100 mg L⁻¹), the most pronounced protective effect of ZBNC priming was observed at 50 mg L⁻¹ ZnO, where shoot biomass was significantly higher than the corresponding As-alone treatment (p < 0.05). The minimal protective impact occurred at 25 mg L⁻¹ ZnO priming across all As stress levels. Notably, at the highest As concentration (100 mg L⁻¹ As), the 25 mg L⁻¹ ZBNC priming treatment resulted in the most severe biomass reduction, showing a 63% decrease compared to non-stressed controls (p < 0.05). These results indicate that the efficacy of ZBNC priming is concentration-dependent, with intermediate concentrations (50 mg L⁻¹) providing optimal protection against As-induced growth inhibition, while lower concentrations (25 mg L⁻¹) may exacerbate As toxicity under severe stress conditions.

Effect of ZBNcs priming on chlorophyll content

As shown in Fig. 5, priming with 50 and 75 mg L⁻¹ ZBNCs under control conditions (0 mg L⁻¹ As) significantly increased total chlorophyll content (Chl a + b) compared to the non-primed control (p < 0.05). However, increasing As concentrations progressively reduced chlorophyll levels across all treatments in a dose-dependent manner, with the most severe decline observed at 100 mg L⁻¹ As (p < 0.05). Remarkably, at both 25 and 100 mg L⁻¹ As stress levels, priming with 75 mg L⁻¹ ZBNCs significantly enhanced chlorophyll content relative to unprimed plants (p < 0.05), demonstrating its protective role against As-induced photosynthetic pigment inhibition. At the intermediate As concentration (50 mg L⁻¹), no statistically significant differences were observed between primed and unprimed plants (p > 0.05). These results indicate that ZBNC priming, particularly at 75 mg L⁻¹, effectively mitigates As-induced chlorophyll degradation in wheat leaves, while the protective effect is concentration-dependent and most evident under low and high As stress conditions.

Effect of ZBNcs priming on oxidative stress and As accumulation

ZnO NP priming (25 mg L⁻¹) significantly increased H₂O₂ content under control conditions (0 mg L⁻¹ As) compared to non-primed plants (p < 0.05) (Fig. 6). Arsenic exposure induced a dose-dependent increase in H₂O₂ levels across all treatments, with significant elevation observed even at the lowest As concentration (25 mg L⁻¹). Outstandingly, nano-priming exhibited concentration-specific effects at 25 mg L⁻¹ As, priming further increased H₂O₂ levels, while at 50 mg L⁻¹ As, both 25 and 50 mg L⁻¹ priming showed non-significant reduction trends. The highest As concentration (100 mg L⁻¹) revealed a dichotomous response − 25 and 50 mg L⁻¹ priming decreased H2O2 content, whereas 75 mg L⁻¹ priming paradoxically enhanced H2O2 accumulation, suggesting a threshold-dependent shift in oxidative stress regulation by ZBNcs under severe As toxicity. The POD activity in wheat shoots increased significantly with rising As concentrations (Fig. 7A). While ZnO-NP priming at all concentrations (25, 50, and 75 mg L⁻¹) enhanced POD activity under control, conditions (0 mg L⁻¹ As) compared to non-primed plants, these increases were not statistically significant. Notably, at 25 and 50 mg L⁻¹ As stress, priming with 50 and 75 mg L⁻¹ ZBNcs significantly reduced POD activity, whereas the 25 mg L⁻¹ priming showed minimal effects. Figure 7B reveals that GST activity responded differently to treatments. Under control conditions, 75 mg L⁻¹ priming significantly increased GST activity compared to non-primed plants. As exposure generally elevated GST activity in a dose-dependent manner compared to controls (p < 0.05). At 25 mg L⁻¹ As, all priming concentrations (25, 50, and 75 mg L⁻¹) significantly reduced GST activity compared to As-alone treatment (p < 0.05). However, at 50 and 100 mg L⁻¹ As, priming caused non-significant decreases in enzyme activity (p > 0.05). Thus, the protective effect of ZBNC priming on GST activity was only statistically significant at the lowest As concentration (25 mg L⁻¹).

ZnO-NP priming significantly mitigated As uptake, particularly at the highest stress level (100 mg L⁻¹ As), where 50 mg L⁻¹ priming showed the most substantial reduction (48%), followed by 75 mg L⁻¹ (43%) and 25 mg L⁻¹ (22%) priming treatments (p < 0.05). The results demonstrate that optimal NP concentrations (50–75 mg L⁻¹) could effectively decrease As bioaccumulation in wheat shoots under severe metal stress, suggesting their potential for phytotoxicity management in contaminated systems.

Discussion

The pervasive contamination of ecosystems by heavy metals and metalloids, characterized by their environmental persistence and bioaccumulative potential, represents a pressing global health crisis [3]. The limitations of conventional remediation methods for heavy metal contamination, including their high cost and potential for secondary ecological damage, have accelerated the development of sustainable alternatives. Consequently, biodegradable and eco-compatible NPs have gained prominence as a cost-effective and environmentally friendly nanotechnology for effective remediation. This study specifically explores the synthesis of ZnO/biochar nanocomposites derived from grape leaf waste through a one-step carbonization process, evaluating their potential to alleviate As toxicity in wheat.

ZnO-NPs release Zn, regulating plant growth by influencing auxin synthesis and membrane stability under stress [32, 48]. Zn maintains membrane integrity by binding to sulfhydryl groups and phospholipids [49]. It also enhances the uptake of essential nutrients, restoring the function of organelles like chloroplasts and mitochondria. Furthermore, Zn reduces oxidative stress by improving antioxidant enzyme activity [32]. Conversely, Zn deficiency can cause membrane disorganization and nutrient leakage, highlighting its importance in plant physiology [50].

The functional groups of ZBNCs, including hydroxyl and carboxyl groups, form a protective layer on the seed coat during priming, trapping As ions on root surfaces and reducing their bioavailability. Additionally, the Zn-O bond facilitates gradual release of Zn²⁺ ions, which act as cofactors for antioxidant enzymes, explaining the enhanced POD and GST activities observed in our study. While it appears that released, Zn²⁺ ions may also compete with As for shared uptake transporters and induce phytochelatin synthesis for vacuolar sequestration, these mechanisms were not directly investigated in the present study as parameters such as phytochelatin content and transporter gene expression were not measured. Furthermore, the 18 nm crystallite size of ZnO enables seed coat penetration and sustained Zn²⁺ release throughout the growth period. Therefore, the structural features of ZBNCs underpin their efficacy in reducing As accumulation and oxidative stress in wheat through seed priming, although further research is needed to confirm the proposed roles of transporter competition and phytochelatin induction.

Previous studies have demonstrated that ZnO-NPs [31, 51], and ZnS nanocomposites [32] reduce As accumulation in rice and wheat, while biochar-based amendments have been shown to mitigate heavy metal stress in cereals [52, 53]. However, none of these studies combined ZnO-NPs with biochar as a seed nano-priming agent for As stress. Furthermore, while ZnO nanopriming has been shown to enhance germination and photosynthesis under optimal conditions [13, 33]. Our study extends these findings by demonstrating that ZBNCs effectively mitigate As toxicity in wheat through dual functionality which includes As adsorption by biochar and antioxidant enhancement by ZnO, along with the added benefits of sustainable synthesis from agricultural waste and reduced environmental input through seed priming.

The present study demonstrates that ZnO-NP seed priming could effectively mitigate As toxicity in wheat (Triticum aestivum L. cv. Azar-2) through modulation of physiological and biochemical pathways. Our findings reveal that increasing As stress significantly reduced shoot biomass, chlorophyll content, and overall plant growth, consistent with previous reports on As-induced phytotoxicity [31, 32, 51]. However, ZnO-NP priming mitigated the As toxicity adverse effects and promoted biomass. Notably, nano-priming with 75 mg L⁻¹ ZnO-NPs under control conditions (0 mg L⁻¹ As) significantly improved shoot biomass and chlorophyll content, suggesting a growth-promoting effect via enhanced nutrient uptake and metabolic activity [54]. Our findings support and extend the work of Kumar et al. (2021), Yan et al. (2021), and Aamer et al. (2025), by demonstrating that the use of ZnO–NPs significantly enhanced the growth of plants under As stress [51, 55, 56]. This growth-promoting effect of ZnO–NPs has also been observed in plants exposed to other heavy metals [52]. Furthermore, the observed increase in biomass following ZnO-NP priming may be attributed to a reduction in As stress within the wheat plants. Data presented in Fig. 9 suggests that ZnO-NP application at concentrations exceeding 25 mg L− 1 further reduces As concentration in shoots by upto 48%, potentially due to increased As adsorption before root uptake, as indicated in our previous work [32]. However, it is important to acknowledge that excessive ZnO-NP priming levels can induce phytotoxicity, leading to a reduction in biomass.

The increasing intensity of As stress in wheat led to a rise in H2O2 levels (as an oxidative stress marker), which is consistent with the established premise that oxidative stress is a key mechanism of HM phytotoxicity. A notable finding was the significant increase in H₂O₂ under control conditions with 25 mg L⁻¹ ZBNC priming (Fig. 6), indicating NP-induced mild oxidative stress. Rather than a toxic effect, we interpret this as beneficial eustress, where low ROS levels act as signaling molecules to activate stress-responsive pathways and induce priming memory. This interpretation is supported by concurrent enhancement of POD and GST activities. Similar eustress responses have been reported in other nanopriming studies [13, 33]. However, this effect is concentration-dependent, as higher ZBNC doses (75 mg L⁻¹) under severe As stress overwhelmed the defense capacity, highlighting the need for concentration optimization. The priming of ZnO–NP in our study reduced oxidative damage by lowering H2O2 levels and further increased the activity of detoxifying enzymes such as POD and GST. A key observation was the concentration-dependent efficacy of ZnO-NPs. While lower As levels (25–50 mg L⁻¹) allowed significant mitigation via priming, higher As concentrations (100 mg L⁻¹) diminished these protective effects, indicating a threshold beyond which As toxicity overwhelms NP-mediated defense mechanisms [57]. These enzymes play an important role in scavenging ROS and conjugated toxic As metabolites for vacuolar sequestration [58]. The ZnO-NP enhanced activities of POD and GST in the As-stressed wheat plants in the present study endorse the results of Wu et al. (2020) in rice seedlings [59], Khan et al. (2022) in maize [58], Rani et al. (2023) in mungbean [60], and Zeeshan et al. (2024) in Soybean [61] under As stress.

The results demonstrate that ZBNCs are functionally superior to conventional ZnO-NPs for As mitigation in wheat. First, while Ahmed et al. (2021) reported 54–62% As reduction in rice using ZnO-NPs at 30 mg L⁻¹ under 25 µM As, our ZBNCs achieved 48% reduction in wheat shoots under a much higher As concentration (100 mg L⁻¹) with a lower effective Zn dose (50 mg L⁻¹ priming) [31], suggesting enhanced Zn use efficiency via the biochar carrier. Second, the controlled release mechanism of Zn²⁺ from the biochar matrix explains the concentration-dependent efficacy, where moderate priming (50 mg L⁻¹) was optimal while higher doses (75 mg L⁻¹) showed diminished effects, preventing Zn-induced phytotoxicity. Third, the As-adsorption capacity of biochar provides an additional protective layer absent in bare ZnO-NPs. This combined mechanism of extracellular As immobilization (by biochar) and intracellular detoxification (by Zn²⁺-enhanced antioxidants) represents a significant advancement over single-mechanism approaches.

However, a notable exception to this protective trend was observed at the highest As concentration (100 mg L⁻¹) when seeds were primed with 75 mg L⁻¹ ZBNCs. Under these conditions, H₂O₂ content paradoxically increased rather than decreased, whereas lower priming concentrations (25 and 50 mg L⁻¹) significantly reduced H₂O₂ levels under the same severe As stress. This threshold-dependent response can be explained by several mechanisms. First, excessive Zn²⁺ release from high-dose ZBNCs could induce pro-oxidant effects, as supra-optimal Zn levels can generate ROS through Fenton-like reactions while inhibiting antioxidant enzymes. Second, the biochar component may reach saturation at high As concentrations, limiting its As-adsorption capacity and compromising the protective barrier effect. Third, severe As stress (100 mg L⁻¹) may overwhelm the plant’s antioxidant defense capacity, such that the additional mild oxidative stress from high-dose priming exceeds the plant’s mitigation threshold. Fourth, high Zn concentrations could trigger programmed cell death (PCD) in root cells, involving a burst of H₂O₂ as a signaling molecule. Similar threshold-dependent effects have been observed in nanopriming studies, where Rai-Kalal and Jajoo, (2021) reported that while low ZnO-NP concentrations enhanced antioxidant defense in wheat, higher concentrations induced oxidative stress [13], and Geremew et al. (2025) demonstrated concentration-dependent effects of ZnO nanopriming in Amaranth [33]. These findings suggest that optimizing priming concentration is critical, and 75 mg L⁻¹ ZBNCs may approach the threshold for pro-oxidant effects under severe As stress.

This accumulation is significant as proline acts as a key osmolyte and non-enzymatic antioxidant, mitigating abiotic stress by scavenging ROS, activating antioxidant defenses, maintaining osmotic balance, and providing energy and nutrients [62]. In NP-primed seedlings under As stress, proline content showed a decreasing trend compared to As-alone treatments; however, these reductions were not statistically significant (p > 0.05). Therefore, we cannot conclude that ZBNC priming significantly reduces proline accumulation under the conditions tested. The positive effect of NP seed priming on alleviating oxidative stress may be attributed primarily to the enhancement of enzymatic antioxidants (POD and GST) rather than changes in proline levels. Beyond its role as an osmoprotectant, proline also functions as a HM chelator, forming complexes with As and potentially stimulating phytochelatin synthesis, thereby reducing metal toxicity [62], but this mechanism requires further investigation in the context of ZBNC priming.

Seed priming with ZnO NPs (50 mg L⁻¹) significantly mitigated As accumulation in wheat shoots. While 100 mg L⁻¹ As stress led to a 26-fold increase in shoot As concentration, NP priming reduced this accumulation by 48%. This protective effect is attributed to two potential mechanisms: (i) the precipitation of ZnO-NPs on the root surface, forming a barrier that limits As uptake and translocation; and (ii) a dilution effect resulting from Zn-induced growth promotion, where enhanced biomass dilutes the As concentration within the plant tissues.

While our greenhouse study demonstrates the efficacy of ZBNCs under controlled conditions, field application will require consideration of soil type (pH, texture, organic matter), microbial interactions, and cost-effectiveness. The use of grape leaf waste, an abundant agricultural residue, for biochar synthesis significantly reduces raw material costs and promotes waste valorization. Furthermore, the seed priming approach minimizes nanomaterial input compared to direct soil or foliar application, making ZBNCs economically attractive and sustainable. Future field studies should address soil-specific optimization, long-term environmental fate, and detailed cost-benefit analyses before large-scale recommendations.

We acknowledge that the present study did not include a biochar-only treatment group, which would have helped to distinguish the individual contributions of biochar and ZnO-NPs within the ZBNCs. However, the individual effects of biochar on wheat growth and heavy metals mitigation have been extensively documented in previous studies [32, 53]. Furthermore, our preliminary experiments with biochar-only priming showed limited dispersion stability in aqueous solutions and negligible As reduction compared to ZBNCs (data not shown). Therefore, while the synergistic role of the nanocomposite is evident from our results, future studies should include biochar-only and ZnO-only treatment groups to quantitatively delineate the contribution of each component.

Conclusion

This study introduces a facile, one-step carbonization process for the successful synthesis of ZnO/Biochar Nanocomposites (ZBNCs) from grape leaf waste, demonstrating their potential to alleviate As toxicity in wheat under controlled greenhouse conditions. Seed priming with ZBNCs reduced As accumulation in shoots (up to 48%), enhanced antioxidant defense (POD and GST), and reversed growth inhibition. The biochar matrix provides additional As adsorption capacity through surface functional groups, while the controlled release of Zn²⁺ from the nanocomposite ensures sustained protection against oxidative stress. Moreover, the green synthesis approach using agricultural waste offers a cost-effective and environmentally friendly alternative to chemically synthesized nanomaterials. Further studies are needed to evaluate long-term efficacy, grain production, and economic feasibility under field conditions. Nevertheless, ZBNCs offer potential advantages over conventional ZnO-NPs through their dual-action mechanism, controlled Zn²⁺ release, and sustainable synthesis approach using agricultural waste.
