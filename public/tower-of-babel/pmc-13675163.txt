Average annual occupational radiation exposure among dental staff: a 3-years retrospective observational study
Authors: Erada Almotiri, Sarra Alsabr, Amal Aljehani, Ebtesam Alsumairi, Sharifah Alharthi, Farrah Alnajjar, Fahad Khalifa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Diagnostic radiography is indispensable in modern dentistry for its diagnostic and therapeutic value, yet it presents potential health risks to healthcare providers from long-term occupational radiation exposure. This retrospective study aimed to quantify the average annual occupational radiation doses received by dental staff members at King Fahad Armed Forces Hospital (KFAFH) from 2022 to 2024, evaluate exposure variations across clinical specialties, and assess personnel compliance regarding Thermoluminescent Dosimeter (TLD) badge utilization. The study cohort comprised all active dental professionals (N = 277) enrolled in the institutional radiation safety program. Longitudinal dosimetry data were extracted from centralized monitoring records. Deep personal dose equivalents Hp(10) and shallow personal dose equivalents Hp(0.07) were monitored quarterly and annually using lithium fluoride detector crystals (LiF: Mg, Ti). Statistical tracking utilized a repeated-measures design via IBM SPSS Statistics. Over the three-year tracking period, the median annual cumulative Hp(10) dose rose from 0.00 mSv (Q1, Q3: 0.00, 0.00) in 2022 to 0.10 mSv (Q1, Q3: 0.00, 0.11) in 2023, reaching 0.34 mSv (Q1, Q3: 0.16, 0.45) in 2024. Concurrently, median annual Hp(0.07) doses shifted from 0.00 mSv (2022) to 0.10 mSv (2023) and 0.29 mSv (2024). Extremity radiation exposure remained at an absolute baseline of 0.00 mSv across all years. Multi-variable analysis revealed no statistically significant variations in dose metrics among clinical specialties (p > 0.05). All observed occupational metrics remained profoundly beneath the annual dose thresholds recommended by the International Commission on Radiological Protection (ICRP). Occupational radiation exposure at KFAFH is exceptionally low and structurally well-controlled. The statistically significant temporal increase in recorded doses from 2022 to 2024 is indicative of enhanced behavioral compliance in consistent TLD badge utilization.

Introduction

In contemporary healthcare, diagnostic radiography has become an indispensable cornerstone of both medicine and dentistry. Human populations are continuously exposed to ionizing radiation derived from a combination of natural background sources and synthetic applications. Fundamentally, radiation represents the propagation of energy through space or material media, categorized broadly into ionizing and non-ionizing forms. Among these, X-rays constitute a high-energy form of ionizing radiation utilized extensively to guide clinical interventions (Praveen et al. 2013).

Dental radiology plays a crucial and indispensable role in contemporary dental practice, with its utility continually expanding as imaging techniques rapidly evolve (White and Pharoah 2008). While it offers undeniable benefits for the diagnosis and treatment of dental issues, the use of ionizing radiation (IR) inherently carries potential health hazards. (Kamran et al. 2022). These hazards are classically classified into deterministic and stochastic effects. For deterministic, severity increases with doses, with some effects such as cataracts whereas for stochastic includes cancer caused by DNA damage. (Mallya and Lam 2004; Frommer 1996; Arnout and Jafar 2014).

Most commonly used dental imaging procedures include intraoral radiography, which has the longest history of use, followed by panoramic radiography, and more recently, cone-beam computed tomography (Özalp et al. 2018). It was reported in several studies that IR is one of the major occupational risks among dentists (Mehta and Gupta and Upadhyaya 2013; Sont et al. 2001; Leggat and Kedjarune and Smith 2007). Radiographic evaluation has a pivotal role in dental practice. Hence, to obtain the radiographic image, a calculated dose of radiation is inevitably transmitted to the patients; and would be kept As Low As Reasonably Achievable (ALARA) and in compliance with the new concept of As Low As Diagnostically Acceptable (ALADA) principles. (FDI 2014) Dentistry differs from other medical specialities, since the dental clinician performs the process of acquisition and interpretation of the oral radiograph. Although the radiation exposure is minimal, but it is crucial to reduce the radiation to avoid the accumulated dose to the dentists in their lifetime (Javali and Dantu 2018). Advancements in radiographic technologies and radiation protection practices have played an important role in decreasing radiation exposure to the workers (Linet et al. 2010). However, there has been an increase in the number of diagnostic procedures and thus an increase in the occupational radiation dose (Smith-Bindman and Miglioretti and Larson 2008). In dental radiology, the number of radiographs is rising worldwide. According to the United Nations Scientific Committee on the Effects of Atomic Radiation, 340 million dental radiology examinations were performed globally in 1988 (UNSCEAR 2006). This number increased in 2008 to approximately 480 million, which comprises 15% of all diagnostic X-ray examinations in health care (UNSCEAR 2008). Several international and local rules and regulations have been implemented to monitor and control the occupational radiation exposure received by healthcare workers. According to the International Commission on Radiological Protection (ICRP), the recommended dose limits for radiation workers, including dental workers, are as follows: 20 mSv for annual effective dose (whole-body), 20 mSv annual equivalent dose to the lens of the eye, and 500 mSv for an annual equivalent dose to the extremities (hands and feet) or the skin (ICRP 2007, 2012). The dose limit for a declared pregnant worker is 1 mSv, which is the equivalent dose to the fetus per month (ICRP 2007, 2012). Within the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia, these regulatory mandates are strictly monitored and enforced by the Nuclear and Radiological Regulatory Commission (NRRC), the Saudi Food and Drug Authority (SFDA), and the Armed Forces Health Services directives. At the institutional level, King Fahad Armed Forces Hospital (KFAFH) implements these safety policies through a dedicated Radiation Safety Committee. Under these legislative frameworks, healthcare facilities are legally mandated to issue personal radiation monitoring systems, such as thermoluminescent dosimeter (TLD) badges, to accurately log, track, and manage occupational exposures among vulnerable clinical staff. (Alyami and Nassef 2022). Despite the widespread use of diagnostic radiography in dental medicine, localized multi-year longitudinal assessments of occupational exposure in specialized tertiary military centers within Saudi Arabia remain limited. Therefore, this retrospective study aims to quantify the average annual occupational radiation doses received by dental staff at King Fahad Armed Forces Hospital (KFAFH) from 2022 to 2024, evaluate variations in exposure levels across different dental specialties, and assess staff compliance regarding Thermoluminescent Dosimeter (TLD) utilization. By analyzing these trends, this study seeks to provide a baseline for institutional adherence to international safety standards and optimize occupational radiation protocols.

Materials and methods

Study design

This retrospective study was designed and conducted at the dental department of King Fahad Armed Forces Hospital (KFAFH) in Jeddah, Saudi Arabia.

Data collection

277 dental workers were the total cumulative number of dental professionals affiliated with the hospital across the 3-year period, n = 96 (2022), n = 96 (2023), and n = 85 (2024) (both dentists and dental assistants), who were also part of the hospital’s radiation safety program.The Radiation Safety Program at KFAFH maintains records of all workers’ radiation doses. Radiation monitoring records of the study participants were reviewed retrospectively to identify date, quarterly radiation dose, and each year’s level of radiation doses, as well as the occupation of each worker. The above mentioned data were also classified by the institution. Moreover, the radiation doses of all staff were monitored using Thermoluminescent dosimeters (TLDs) made of lithium fluoride doped with magnesium and titanium (LiF: Mg, Ti) crystals are commercially known as Harshaw TLD-100. The badges were placed outside the lead apron at shoulder level on either side of the chest.

The badges were analyzed quarterly and the results were reviewed and communicated to each worker regularly. The dosimeter was exchanged prior to the first day of each calendar quarter (4 quarters per year) with a new badge. For calibration and quality control analysis, the Thermoluminescent Dosimeter (TLD) reader was rigorously calibrated under reproducible reference conditions utilizing an internal irradiator. To guarantee global measurement traceability and accuracy, the entire calibration and quality control process was carried out in strict compliance with international standards, specifically ISO 4037 (for the determination of reference X and gamma radiation fields) and IEC 61,066 (governing thermoluminescence dosimetry systems for personal and environmental monitoring). The reader was calibrated using an internal ⁹⁰Sr/⁹⁰Y source (0.50 mCi). The system has a minimum detectable limit of 1 µSv, a 5% linearity, and a sensitivity range of 10 µGy to 1 Gy. The heating cycle involved a 120 °C preheat phase followed by a 20 °C/s ramp up to 390 °C under a purified nitrogen flow (40–95 psi at 28 L/h).The technical specifications of these procedures have been reported previously by Alashban et al. (2021).

The quantity for reporting personal radiation doses is “effective dose” as defined by the International Commission of Radiological Protection limits ICRP (ICRP 2007, 2012). The unit used is Sievert (Sv), millisievert (mSv) Hp (10): is the dose equivalent at a tissue depth of 10 mm. Hp (0.07): is the dose equivalent at a tissue depth of 0.07 mm.

Statistical analysis

All data analyses were performed using Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) version 26 (Armonk, New York, IBM Corporation, USA). Descriptive statistics were conducted for demographic and clinical variables, including dental staff characteristics (such as clinic type or department), and levels of radiation exposure measured quarterly and yearly. Continuous variables were summarized using medians with interquartile ranges (Q1, Q3) due to a non-normal distribution determined by normality testing, while frequencies with percentages were reported for categorical variables.

To evaluate the overall changes in annual radiation levels over the three-year period (2022–2024), a Generalized Linear Mixed Model (GLMM) was utilized. This approach accounted for the dependent, repeated-measures nature of the yearly tracking and accommodated missing data resulting from staff turnover without resorting to listwise deletion. Year and department were treated as fixed effects, and worker identity was included as a random subject effect to analyze differences across categories for Hp (10), Hp (0.07), and extremity doses. Post hoc pairwise comparisons were performed using Estimated Marginal Means, with Bonferroni correction applied to control the error rate. Median (Q1, Q3) average annual effective radiation doses were reported and compared with the International Commission on Radiological Protection (ICRP) limits to evaluate the level of adherence to radiation protection practices. p-values ≤ 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

The total of all dental professionals affiliated with KFAFH over 3 years (2022–2024) was 277. The distribution of dental staff was 96 employees in the year 2022 and 96 in the year 2023, and 85 in the year 2024.

The predominant category was restorative dentistry in 2022: 28.13%; 2023: 27.08%; 2024: 25.88%. The next was endodontics, 2022: 15.63%; 2023: 14.58%; 2024: 15.29%. Meanwhile, most of the other departments contributed less than 10% each year. Specialty subgrouping was analyzed to evaluate whether high-volume imaging departments (such as Endodontics) experienced higher occupational exposure compared to other units. Although these departmental comparisons yielded non-significant differences (p > 0.05), reporting these outcomes is scientifically essential as it demonstrates the uniform effectiveness of KFAFH’s isolated, closed-room clinic design across all clinical workflows. Discussion

The present study critically quantified the occupational radiation doses—specifically deep dose equivalent Hp(10), shallow dose equivalent Hp(0.07), and extremity doses—received by 277 dental professionals at King Fahad Armed Forces Hospital (KFAFH) in Jeddah, Saudi Arabia, from 2022 to 2024. While a statistically significant upward temporal trend in cumulative median doses was observed over the three-year monitoring window (p < 0.001), all recorded exposures remained profoundly beneath the annual occupational dose thresholds strictly enforced by the International Commission on Radiological Protection (ICRP) of 20 mSv for Hp(10) and 500 mSv for Hp(0.07).

Comparative analysis of radiation dose magnitudes

When contextualized within local literature, our findings closely align with the lower spectrum of exposure levels reported in Saudi Arabia. For instance, Alashban et al. (2021) documented national average annual effective doses ranging from 0.55 to 1.02 mSv for dentists and 0.44 to 1.04 mSv for dental nurses between 2015 and 2019. Similarly, Basheer et al. (2021) observed an 8-month mean exposure of 0.554 mSv within a localized Saudi academic institution. Our peak median annual exposure, recorded in 2024 as 0.34 mSv (Q1, Q3: 0.16, 0.45 mSv) for Hp(10) and 0.29 mSv (Q1, Q3: 0.16, 0.42 mSv) for Hp(0.07), sits comfortably below these baselines.

However, a stark divergence emerges when comparing our data to recent regional and international studies (Table 6).

In Iraq, Kabier et al. (2025) reported vastly higher annualized mean effective doses of 5.623 mSv for dentists and 5.27 mSv for assistants. Likewise, Ibrahim (2024) noted elevated ranges of 1.26 to 3.64 mSv among dental assistants in Nigeria, with dental surgeons reaching up to 2.64 mSv.

Several institutional factors explain why KFAFH maintains radiation profiles that are magnitudes lower than those observed in Iraq and Nigeria:

Structural Shielding: As a major military tertiary medical center, KFAFH utilizes standardized architectural shielding, including heavy barium plaster walls and lead-lined doors that physically isolate operators during exposures.Technological Modernization: The exclusive utilization of high-frequency digital radiography units at KFAFH sharply reduces necessary exposure times and milliampere-seconds (mAs) values compared to conventional film radiography, which may still be widely used in developing regional clinics.Routine Quality Assurance: KFAFH enforces stringent, mandatory medical physics calibration cycles on all X-ray units, minimizing tube leakage and scatter.

The Impact of institutional and clinic configurations

A critical variable highlighted in the literature is the architectural layout of the dental workspace. Basheer et al. (2021) observed significant variations across clinical specialties in open-plan academic clinics, with endodontic suites exhibiting the highest Thermoluminescent Dosimeter (TLD) readings due to high-volume, rapid-succession periapical imaging. Specialty subgrouping was analyzed to evaluate whether high-volume imaging departments (such as Endodontics) experienced higher occupational exposure compared to other units.

Conversely, our analysis revealed no statistically significant differences in radiation doses among different dental divisions or clinic types across all three years (p > 0.05) across all categories in Table 5. For example, in 2024, median Hp(10) exposures were statistically uniform between Endodontics (0.38 mSv), Restorative Dentistry (0.19 mSv), combined specialty clinics (0.38 mSv), and other departments (0.23 mSv; (p = 0.712). reporting these outcomes is scientifically essential as it demonstrates the uniform effectiveness of KFAFH’s isolated, closed-room clinic design across all clinical workflows.

The dental department at KFAFH relies primarily on isolated, closed-room clinic configurations. This structural barrier effectively mitigates the “cross-firing” of scatter radiation from adjacent chairs—a phenomenon common in open-clinic configurations like those evaluated by Kabier et al. (2025) or high-volume student clinics like Basheer et al. (2021). Consequently, the protective architectural layout at KFAFH ensures that staff in high-exposure specialties like Endodontics or Oral Surgery are as safely insulated as those in lower-demand clinics.

Temporal trends and the paradox of staff compliance

The upward trajectory of median annual doses from 2022 to 2024 warrants critical interpretation. In 2022, the median recorded values for Hp(10), Hp(0.07), and extremity doses were uniformly 0.00 mSv. By 2023, overall Hp(10) rose to 0.10 mSv, ultimately reaching 0.34 mSv in 2024. Post hoc pairwise comparisons confirmed that these year-over-year increases were statistically significant (p < 0.05).

Rather than signaling a degradation of radiation safety or a sudden surge in radiographic workload, this trend most likely reflects shifting behavioral compliance regarding TLD badge usage among the staff (which fluctuated from 96 employees in 2022/2023 to 85 in 2024). The absolute zero readings captured across all parameters in 2022 strongly point toward a well-documented challenge in occupational health: low initial compliance in consistently wearing TLD badges during clinical shifts or storing them outside active X-ray zones.

As KFAFH administration intensified the enforcement of radiation safety protocols and conducted routine, mandatory safety briefings over the subsequent years, badge-wearing compliance improved. This is mirrored in the quarterly data for 2024, where median Hp(10) readings naturally varied and realistically climbed from 0.09 mSv in the second quarter to 0.19 mSv in the fourth quarter. Consequently, the rising values in 2023 and 2024 do not indicate worsening exposure conditions, but rather a more accurate, reliable representation of the actual workplace dose profile driven by enhanced protocol adherence.

Curiously, extremity doses remained at an absolute baseline of 0.00 mSv across all three years for all departments. While highly reassuring, this baseline profile confirms that staff are consistently adhering to the “inverse-square law” by maintaining a safe physical distance from the primary X-ray beam and completely avoiding the dangerous practice of manually stabilizing TLD badges or film receptors in the patient’s mouth during active exposure. Continuous monitoring remains vital to preserve this high standard of occupational safety.

Strengths and limitations

This study features several notable strengths, including a robust sample size of 277 dental professionals tracked over a longitudinal multi-year timeline (2022–2024). The utilization of data from a centralized, continuous institutional radiation monitoring program—which rigorously measures radiation exposure on both a quarterly and annual basis—allows for a reliable evaluation of occupational dose trends over time within a highly regulated healthcare environment.

Nevertheless, several limitations must be acknowledged when interpreting these results. First, the retrospective and single-center study design; because this study carries inherent risks of selection bias and unmeasured confounding. Furthermore, because data collection was restricted to a single tertiary military medical center, the findings may not be fully generalizable to smaller private practices, public open-plan clinics, or academic dental institutions with differing administrative frameworks and patient volumes. Second, the dataset lacked definitive identification regarding the exact type of ionizing radiation captured, the precise duration of TLD badge positioning on individual bodies, and machine-specific exposure parameters (such as tube current [mA], peak kilovoltage [kVp], or exact exposure times) across different radiographic units.Third, the absence of clinician workload tracking; it was impossible to account for individual worker operational burdens, such as the exact frequency and volume of radiographic procedures executed by each professional during their shifts. Forth, the unadjusted confounding variables; The analysis did not adjust for potential individual confounding characteristics, including staff age, specific occupational roles (e.g., dental assistants vs. dental specialists), and cumulative years of clinical experience, all of which could influence personal radiation handling habits and subsequent dose exposure profiles. Last, lack of procedure-specific modality tracking; the database did not record the exact frequency or distribution of specific imaging modalities—such as intraoral periapical, bitewing, panoram, or Cone Beam Computed Tomography (CBCT)—performed by individual staff members. The inability to correlate personal TLD doses with exact modality types limits our capacity to determine if individual exposure spikes were driven by specific procedural selections.

Conclusion

This retrospective study demonstrates that occupational radiation exposure among the 277 dental staff members at KFAFH remains exceptionally low and well within the strict annual dose limits recommended by the ICRP. Even at their peak in 2024, the median annual cumulative doses—0.34 mSv for deep dose Hp(10) and 0.29 mSv for shallow dose Hp(0.07)—represented a small fraction of the permissible 20 mSv and 500 mSv thresholds. Furthermore, the lack of significant dose variations across diverse clinical specialties confirms the uniform efficacy of the hospital’s protective infrastructure, modern digital radiography units, and isolated closed-room layouts.

Ultimately, these findings confirm that combining standardized structural shielding with active safety oversight effectively minimizes occupational hazards. Future multi-center research should investigate longitudinal radiation exposure across broader hospital settings over extended horizons to ensure high safety standards and tracking compliance are maintained.
