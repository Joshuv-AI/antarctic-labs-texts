Colossal and fully reversible barocaloric effect for sustainable cooling in a pressure-sensitive spin-crossover complex
Authors: Yue Kan, Shuo Meng, Si-Wei Yan, Rui-Xin Li, Qing-Zhen Huang, Yue-Xing Zhang, Yang Ren, Zi-Long Zhao, Ivan da Silva, Li-Jin Wang, Chang-Qing Jin, Zheng-Ying Tian, Han Liang, Yuan-Sha Chen, Quan-Jun Li, Jing Wang, Yun-Zhong Chen, Dao-Peng Zhang, Jia Yan Law, Victorino Franco, Oliver Gutfleisch, Feng-Xia Hu, Bao-Gen Shen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBarocaloric refrigeration has great potential to replace traditional vapor-compression systems, which are conspicuously harmful to the environment. Investigating barocaloric effect driven by a practical pressure remains challenging. Here, we report that the spin-crossover (SCO) complex Fe(PC4HH)2 (PC4HH = 4-hydroxy-N′-((pyridin-2-yl)-methylene)-benzohydrazide) exhibits a colossal and reversible barocaloric entropy change (ΔSr, up to ~125 J·K−1·kg−1) under a small pressure ∼7.5 MPa owing to the extremely high pressure sensitivity of transition temperature (dTc/dP, 5.56 K/MPa). Synchrotron radiation PXRD demonstrate that the source of the enhanced ΔSr and the ultra-large dTc/dP come from the gas (air) adsorption of pressure transimitting medium. The superior pressure sensitivity and outstanding barocaloric performance provide a feasible way to exploit novel barocaloric materials by utilizing the complexation properties of SCO materials with gas.Subject terms: Ligands, Ligands, Structural properties

Introduction

With the constant civilization and development of society, resources are being consumed at an astonishing speed. Refrigeration, which uses nearly 20% of the global electricity supply every year, is definitely a cause of the resource crisis1–4. However, refrigeration is essential in our lives, from temperature control of high-tech devices such as chips, computers, and consumer electronics to air conditioners and refrigerators. The current refrigerants used in large-scale air-conditioners and refrigerators are mostly harmful gases like hydrofluorocarbons, which have global warming potentials exceeding 2000 times that of CO2 and hence are unfriendly to our planet5,6. To reduce the emission of such harmful gases and achieve the carbon neutrality target, new types of environmentally benign and safe refrigerants and cooling technologies need to be developed7.

Solid-state refrigeration based on caloric effects has emerged in response to such needs8. The past few years have witnessed rapid development in this research area of magnetocaloric effect (MCE)9–13, electrocaloric effect (ECE)14–17, elastocaloric effect (eCE)18–20, and barocaloric effect (BCE)21–24. Particularly, the discovery of colossal BCE in plastic crystals was a milestone, with a barocaloric entropy change ΔS ~ 500 J·K−1·kg−1 for neopentyl glycol in 201925,26, and new findings were demonstrated in ionic plastic crystals with ΔS ~ 200 J·K−1·kg−1 six years later27, which has stimulated interest in this field. Moreover, a larger barocaloric energy change of ~720 J·K−1·kg−1 was discovered in n-alkanes associated with solid–liquid phase transition28. However, despite their large entropy changes, it is still unclear whether these materials can be practically used because the pressure required to achieve these values is usually large. The pragmatically related properties, the reversible entropy change (ΔSr) and reversible adiabatic temperature change (ΔTr), are generally very small under low pressure (<50 MPa) owing to considerable hysteresis and sluggish pressure sensitivity (small dTc/dP value)29, even though the material itself has a large entropy change.

In 2016, spin-crossover (SCO) materials were proposed as potential candidates for BCE materials30. SCO complexes are a huge family with diverse structure types31–40. They can contain one, two, or more central metal ions in the SCO unit, such as a single Fe ion or two Fe ions or one Co and one Mn ion, and the ligands connecting the metal cores are also diverse (e.g., N6, N2O4, or N2S2O2). The change of the spin state (SCO) of metal ions can be realized by energy gap variation. Taking the case of a single transition metal core (Fe2+) as an example (Fig. 1a), the crystal fields emanating from the ligands split the d orbital into t2g and eg orbitals, and the energy gap (Δ) between the t2g and eg orbitals determines the spin state of the metal ion. When the Δ originating from ligands is larger than the electron pair energy (Epair_energy), three pairs of electrons with opposite spin directions occupy the t2g orbitals, resulting in a low-spin (LS) state (i.e., the total spin quantum number S = 0, left part of Fig. 1a). While the ligands’ environments enable the Δ to be smaller than Epair_energy, the electrons remain as far from each other as possible, leading to a high-spin (HS) state with S = 2 (right part of Fig. 1a). Accompanying the SCO in the split d orbitals, the induced phonon/lattice change along with magnetic contributions leads to considerable calorics41–52. Differing from the mechanism of large BCE in plastic crystals25,27,29, SCO complexes provide an ideal platform for exploring ultra-sensitive barocaloric materials, considering the sensitivity of Δ to the ligands’ environments, stress, and diverse connections.

Herein, we report the SCO complex Fe(PC4HH)2 (1, PC4HH = 4-hydroxy-N′-((pyridin-2-yl)-methylene)-benzohydrazide) showing an extremely high-sensitivity pressure response (dTc/dP ~ 5.56 K/MPa at P < 5 MPa and 0.957 K/MPa at P > 20 MPa). The saturated entropy change across the phase transition is ΔS ~ 125 J·K−1·kg−1, and it reaches and then remains stable under pressures above 7.5 MPa, bringing about an adiabatic temperature change of ΔTad ~ 30 K. Because of this high pressure sensitivity, the phase transition can be driven by a very small pressure of ~7.5 MPa and gives rise to a reversible barocaloric entropy change as much as ΔSr ~ 125 J·K−1·kg−1 at ~300 K, which empowers practical potential for sustainable cooling rather than only the theoretical potential of most other BCE materials.

Results

Basic structure and magnetic properties of 1

We synthesized sample 1 as a dark-green polycrystalline powder (see the Methods for details). The structure of the HS state has been previously determined to be monoclinic with a space group of C 2/c and lattice parameters of a = 22.801(5) Å, b = 9.917(2) Å, c = 11.686(2) Å, α = γ = 90°, and β = 106.47(3)° using single-crystal X-ray diffraction (SC-XRD)53. The asymmetric unit of 1 is a typical neutral mononuclear structure with the octahedral Fe2+ ion located in the center coordinated by a N4O2 unit from two tridentate ligands that are almost perpendicular to each other (Fig. 1b, c and Fig. S1). Owing to the intermolecular O–H…N hydrogen bonds (Fig. 1d, Fig. S1) and π–π stacking interactions between the conjugated ligands, the individual asymmetric units link into a three-dimensional supramolecular network structure. Temperature-dependent magnetic susceptibility measurements verify the SCO nature of 1, switching between LS and HS states (Fig. 1e). The transition temperature is ~295 K/ ~ 285 K during heating/cooling with a hysteresis of ~10 K, showing typical character of the first-order transition.

Barocaloric performance of 1

The thermal effect under hydrostatic pressure with N2 being pressure transmitting medium was measured by pressure differential scanning calorimetry (P-DSC) with a temperature ramping rate of 0.5 K/min (Fig. 2a). The transition temperatures during heating and cooling at ambient pressure, defined as the peak positions in the P-DSC curves, were 297 K and 280 K, respectively, which were slightly different from the values obtained from the SQUID measurements (~295 K/ ~ 285 K) owing to the different sample environments. The corresponding entropy changes for both heating and cooling were calculated to be ~87 J·K−1·kg−1 at ambient pressure (this value is slightly larger compared with the original literature53, which is likely attributed to the different DSC devices used. See the Methods for details). However, an interesting phenomenon occurred when pressure was applied: the entropy change ΔS gradually increased from 87 J·K−1·kg−1 at 0 MPa to ~125 J·K−1·kg−1 and reached saturation above 7.5 MPa (Figs. S2, 3). Moreover, the transition temperature (Tc) showed quasi-unreasonable pressure sensitivity (Fig. 2b). The dTc/dP reached ~5.56 K/MPa at low pressure (P < 5 MPa) and 0.957 K/MPa when P > 20 MPa, which is the record-high ones among reported BCE materials (Table 1), except (NA)2CuBr4 in C3H854. Such a value deviates largely from the calculated 0.22 K/MPa (P < 5 MPa) and 0.70 K/MPa (P > 20 MPa) from the Clausius-Clapeyron relation and lattice parameters (vide infra in the PXRD section). This kind of anomaly has occurred before55, but the deviation was much smaller than in 1. Based on the series curves, the isothermal barocaloric entropy change ΔSiso and its counterpart, the adiabatic temperature change ΔTad, considering the specific heat (Cp) contribution, were quasi-directly determined (Fig. 2c and e)21–24. The maximum value of ΔSiso was ~125 J·K−1·kg−1 at a low pressure of 7.5 MPa, and the upper limit for ΔTad was ~30 K at 30 MPa.

Considering practical merit, the reversible entropy change ΔSr and reversible adiabatic temperature change ΔTr are more attractive after subtracting the hysteresis (Fig. 2d, f). The value of ΔSr reached a peak of ~125 J·K−1·kg−1 at a small pressure of ∼7.5 MPa, and the temperature span was ~40 K under 30 MPa, which results in a large reversible refrigeration capacity (RCrev) of ~4884 J/kg (the maximum ΔSr multiplied by the peak width at half height, Fig. 3, Fig. S3b), which can only be achieved under pressure more than 200 MPa for most other BCE materials25,26,42,52,56,57. The reversible ΔTr reached ~30 K at ~30 MPa and ~302 K (near room temperature). 1 shows outstanding BCE properties under low pressure compared with representative inorganic and organic BCE materials (Fig. 3, Table 1). Note that the RCrev ~ 4884 J/kg was nearly four times higher than the highest reported value in the literature (n-alkanes, ~1100 J/kg). Furthermore, in terms of the coefficient of refrigerant performance (CRP = |ΔTrΔSr/W | )42, which describes the efficiency of caloric materials for converting the input work W into reversible thermal changes (approximated by ΔTrΔSr), sample 1 (CRP > 5) stands out among reported BCE materials (Fig. S34a).

To verify the colossal BCE driven by low pressure, we further performed direct measurements of isothermal entropy change at a constant temperature of 310.7 K (Note S1, Fig. S9–S12, Table S8). The phase transition repeatedly occurred with three successive cycles of pressurization and depressurization, which is indicated by the conspicuous exothermic peaks from ~5.5 to ~13 MPa (Fig. 2h and Fig. S7) and endothermic peaks from ~5.5 to ~0.55 MPa (Fig. 2i and Fig. S8). The corresponding reversible isothermal entropy change was ~100 J·K−1·kg−1, which was in line with the entropy change determined by the quasi-direct method around 310 K considering the different pressure mode (Fig. 2d).

Variable-temperature/pressure powder XRD (VT/VP-PXRD)

To understand the large dTc/dP and large BCE driven by low pressure, we first performed variable-temperature powder XRD (VT-PXRD) to investigate the structural changes during phase transition. The structure of the HS state was previously determined by single-crystal XRD48, but the LS phase is unknown. In comparison of LS with HS phase (Fig. S14a, b), the peaks at 16°, 18°, 26°, and 27° in the PXRD patterns all disappeared after the transformation from the HS to the LS phase, and three new peaks appeared at 15.3°, 19.5°, and 26.7°. The first two peaks in the low-angle range (8.5° and 9.7°, i.e., the (200) and (110) peaks, respectively) were nearly identical for both spin states, indicating that the difference between the lattices was not large. We successfully refined the PXRD spectra at various temperatures by Le Bail method (Fig. 4d, f and Fig. S16–S28). The space group of the LS phase was the same as that of the HS phase (C2/c). The main differences in the transition from the HS to LS states occurred in the lattice parameters. The a parameter decreased, the b parameter was nearly unchanged, the c parameter increased, and the β angle decreased from the HS to the LS phase (Fig. S14c, d). The expansion of lattice volume from LS to HS phase was ~2.7% (Fig. S14e), which is not sufficiently large to support the significant dTc/dP and the corresponding barocaloric ΔSiso. Note that the estimated dTc/dP of 0.22 K/MPa was much smaller than the measured values of ~5.56 K/MPa (P < 5 MPa) and ~0.957 K/MPa (P > 20 MPa) using ΔS = 87 J·K−1·kg−1 based on the Clausius–Clapeyron equation (dTc/dP = ΔV/ΔS).

To explore the origin of high sensitivity of TC to pressure for sample 1, we then performed variable-pressure powder XRD (VP-PXRD) using a conventional diamond anvil cell (DAC) owing to the lack of a low-pressure (<30 MPa) device, and the results are shown in Fig. 4a, b, c, e. Because the sample was loaded into a DAC, the signal-to-noise ratio was not as good as that of VT-PXRD. However, some remarkable changes were still observed. The spectrum before compression was clearly that of the HS phase, although the peak positions were different from those obtained by VT-PXRD owing to the different source used (i.e., Cu target for VT-PXRD and Mo target for VP-PXRD) (Fig. 4a). During compression, the peak at approximately 4.5° disappeared while a new peak appeared at ~5° and moved to higher angle with higher applied pressure. In addition, a remote new peak appeared at ~19.7° from 0.36 GPa (cycled in Fig. 4b), which was absent during VT-PXRD (Fig. 4b). Such changes in the PXRD spectrum indicate that a new LS phase may be induced by external pressure. The lattice at 1.48 GPa was obtained following Le Bail refinement (Fig. 4e and Table S4), and the corresponding parameters were indicated by solid balls in Fig. S1c–e. Compared with the initial LS phase (LS-1), the a parameter greatly increased, the b parameter decreased, the c parameter changed little, and the β angle increased from 102.7° in LS-1 to ~108°. These changes caused the lattice volume to decrease from ~2548 Å3 for the HS phase to ~2260 Å3 for the possible new phase (LS-2), with ΔV/V ~ 13% (Fig. S14e). This volume change seems to be sufficiently large to support the large dTc/dP and the increase of ΔS (Fig. S3a). Note that the dTc/dP value of ~0.7 K/MPa approached the measured value of ~0.95 K/MPa using ΔS ~ 125 J·K−1·kg−1 based on dTc/dP = ΔV/ΔS.

Variable temperature/variable pressure (VT/VP) Raman spectroscopy

To verify the structural transition in sample 1, especially the pressure-induced LS-2 phase, we further performed VT/VP Raman measurements also using a DAC, and the results are shown in Fig. 5. Upon heating, the spectrum changed at approximately 270 K (transition temperature), which was ~20 K lower than that observed in other measurements owing to the thermal effect caused by the laser beam. When the temperature was below 263 K, the peak at 1366 cm−1 ascribed to ν(C–O) and the two peaks at ~1600 cm−1 attributed to ν(C = N–N = C) of hydrazone were unremarkable for LS-153. After heating to above 273 K, the ν(C–O) band became the most intense in the whole spectrum, and the pair of peaks for ν(C = N–N = C) also greatly increased compared with the nearly zero intensity of the peaks for LS-1. Furthermore, the small peak at ~590 cm−1 disappeared with the transition from LS-1 to HS. For the VP measurements, when the sample was loaded into the DAC without applied pressure, its spectrum was the typical one of HS phase characterized by an intense peak at 1366 cm−1 and a doublet at ~1600 cm−1. Upon compression to ~0.27 GPa, the spectrum was nearly the same as that of LS-1 with a characteristic small peak at ~590 cm−1 and weak ν(C–O) and ν(C = N–N = C) bands. When the pressure was further increased, a series of new spectra different from those of both LS-1 and the HS phase appeared from 0.41 GPa, as shown in Fig. 5b. The single peak located at ~1010 cm−1 under atmospheric pressure (HS phase) and missing at 0.27 GPa (LS-1) split into a pair of peaks for the new phase (LS-2) from 0.41 GPa. In addition, the wavenumber of ν(C–O) gradually blue-shifted with applied pressure, and the two peaks of the ν(C = N–N = C) band merged into a single peak with a larger peak width above 0.41 GPa. However, they all returned to the original position in the spectrum of the initial HS phase after pressure relief. Together, these phenomena confirmed the existence of the pressure-induced LS-2 phase (see details given in Note S4). Moreover, considering the dTc/dP∼0.22 K/MPa from VT-PXRD (Fig. S14), the results of VP-Raman spectra from 0 GPa (HS) to 0.11 GPa (HS) to 0.27 GPa (LS-1), i.e., the process of HS to LS-l transition driven by pressure (Fig. 5b), conform to the Clausius–Clapeyron equation. However, the practical P-T diagram measured from P-DSC with N2 being pressure transmitting medium (Fig. S13) seriously conflicts with the theoretical dTc/dP∼0.22 K/MPa from Clausius–Clapeyron equation.

Synchrotron radiation PXRD (SR-PXRD) under low pressure (<30 MPa)

Although some results were obtained via VT/VP-PXRD/Raman experiments with DAC, there were unresolved discrepancies in the relevant data. The main problem is that, due to the lack of low-pressure device, the applied pressure by DAC (0.11 ~ 1.48 GPa, pressure error is usually 50 MPa58) during our first-round VT/VP-PXRD/Raman experiments are much larger than the one used in our P-DSC measurements (<30 MPa). In order to solve this problem, we constructed a VT/VP device (250–330 K, resolution: 0.01 K; 0∼30 MPa resolution: 0.01 MPa) equipped on synchrotron radiation PXRD via gas being the pressure-transmitting medium. Then VP/VT-PXRD measurements were conducted in SSRF (Shanghai Synchronous Radiation Facilities) to reveal the mechanism behind the amazing BCE of sample 1. Unsurprisingly, we did not detect the emergence of the new LS-2, but indeed detected the SCO transition between HS and origin LS-1 under a small pressure <20 MPa.

As shown in Fig. 6a, the diffraction spectra at 260 K under ambient pressure were typically in the LS-1 phase and remained unchanged in the compression process up to 20 MPa, inferring that 1 is always in the LS-1 phase at 260 K. The same situation was observed at 290 K on heating, where 1 was also in the LS-1 state all the while during the VP procedure (Fig. 6b). The corresponding cell parameters at variable pressure at 260 K and 290 K were obtained via Le bail refinement and the results were illustrated in Fig. 7a, b, respectively. One can see that the a axis decreased while b and c axis increased slightly with increasing pressure for both temperatures, and no strange phenomenon was found here.

With further increasing temperature to 310 K, the diffraction spectrum became the HS phase under 0 MPa, which then converted into the original LS-1 under compression at around 11.76∼15.74 MPa (Fig. 6d). This phenomenon solidly confirmed that the SCO phase transition can be driven under this small pressure, and the value of this pressure exactly falls on the T-P diagram obtained from P-DSC with N2 being pressure transmitting medium (Fig. S13) considering that the pressurization process corresponds to the cooling process due to hysteresis42,59. As for the spectra obtained at 320 K (Fig. 6c), the diffraction spectra were all in the HS phase at all pressures, i.e., the SCO phase transition from HS to LS-1 cannot be driven up to 20 MPa at 320 K, also basically corresponds with the T-P diagram (Fig. S13). Upon cooling and when the sample reached 300 K, it should be still in the HS phase due to thermal hysteresis as per the T-P diagram under ambient pressure. Not surprisingly, the measured spectrum at 300 K (Fig. 6e) in cooling under ambient pressure is indeed still in the HS, but upon pressurization, the SCO phase transition from HS to LS-1 can be driven by a pressure as small as 5.83–8.79 MPa, and this value also falls on the T-P diagram obtained from P-DSC (Fig. S13). The critical pressure at 300 K is lower than the critical 11.76–15.74 MPa driving SCO phase transition at 310 K (Fig. 6d), because 300 K was closer to the Tc under 0 MPa (for cooling, Tc∼280 K).

Figure 7d, e show the cell parameters with pressure at 300 K, a temperature point involving both LS-1 and HS phase. One can see there is a conspicuous change in the three cell axes and the β angle under pressurization. Specifically, the a axis decreased from ~23 Å to ~21 Å after entering the LS-1 phase, while b axis increased from 9.97 Å to 10.07 Å from HS to LS-1 transition, and c axis was nearly unchanged. Meanwhile, the β angle is 106.5° in the HS phase, which also decreased to the typical value of 102.8° of the LS-1 phase according to the data from VT-PXRD (Fig. S14). This result again verified that the SCO transition can be driven under a small pressure of around 5.8–8.8 MPa, conforming well to the conclusion obtained from the above P-DSC with N2 being the pressure-transmitting medium (the maximal ΔSr was quasi-directly obtained at ~7.5 MPa, falling in this pressure range). In contrast, there was little change in the cell parameters with pressure at 320 K of HS phase (Fig. 7c) compared with the situation at 260 K and 290 K of LS-1 phase (Fig. 7a and b), indicating that the influence of pressure on the HS phase is less than that on LS-1 phase.

The corresponding cell volumes under various temperatures and pressures were summarized in Fig. 7f. What is out of our expectation is that, no matter whether it is in LS-1 or HS, the lattice volumes all increased with applied pressure instead of shrinking. It is so strange since materials’ volume generally would decrease under compression, but here the inverse trend was observed in 1. Considering the pressure-transmitting medium is gas (air) in our small-pressure device equipped on SR-PXRD, the only reason for this singular result is that the gas molecules enter the sample under pressure and therefore expand the lattice. Specifically, the trend of V-P shown in Fig. 7f is also different for different temperatures.

At 260 K of LS-1 phase, the V-P relationship is nearly a straight line without remarkable traits, while at 290 K closer to the SCO transition Tc ~ 297 K (upon heating, ambient pressure), the V-P relationship shows an interesting tendency: at low pressure range (0 ~ 5 MPa), the increment of V was fast, while in the high pressure range (10 ~ 20 MPa), this increment tended to become slower and slower, i.e., a saturation trend appears with progressively higher pressure. However, the V-P relation at 320 K of HS phase behaves quite differently (the sample retains HS up to 20 MPa, see Fig. 6c). The volume increases little and remains nearly unchanged with pressure even up to 20 MPa at 320 K (Fig. 7c), indicating that in the HS phase it is difficult for gas molecules to adsorb into the lattice of 1. Further, the results at 300 K again confirmed the V-P trend for both LS-1 and HS (Fig. 7f). At 300 K (in cooling), 1 was initially in the HS phase (Fig. 6e), and in the low pressure range below 5 MPa, the cell volume changed little with pressure (Fig. 7f); however, when the HS to LS-1 transition was completely driven by pressure above 8.79 MPa (Fig. 6e), the V-P relation becomes very similar with the LS-1 phase observed at 290 K (Fig. 7f), also having a saturation trend with pressure. This specific result further confirmed that the gas adsorption probability in LS-1 and HS phase is different: the LS-1 phase is more favorable with external gas compared with the HS phase. Here, it is noteworthy that the unit-cell volume (Fig. 7f) refined from SR-PXRD patterns represents only the crystallographic volume of the gas-absorbed solid, while the signals of the single gas (N2) acting as the pressure medium cannot be simultaneously detected. Gas adsorption enlarges the lattice of solid, probably through steric effects, guest–framework interactions, and relaxation of the flexible framework60,61, but does not imply a negative compressibility of the entire solid–gas system due to the compressible gas. Similar pressure-induced lattice expansion by gas adsorption was also observed in metal–organic frameworks (MOFs)60,61, where the uptake of gas molecules leads to an increase in the unit-cell volume with increasing external pressure.

Moreover, the discrimination of gas adsorption for LS-1 and HS was illustrated by DFT calculations as listed in Table S5. The initial lattice volumes after optimization for LS-1 and HS were 2655.00 Å3 and 2747.96 Å3, respectively, based on the structural information from Rietveld refinement of VT-PXRD and the.cif file from previous literature53. After the insertion of 20 N2 molecules (N2 accounts for ∼78% of air) to simulate gas adsorption, the volume for LS-1 and HS changed to similar values of 2869.23 Å3 and 2870.90 Å3, respectively. Because the initial volume of LS-1 was conspicuously smaller than that of HS, the expansion trend after the entry of N2 molecules into the LS-1 was more significant than HS, congruent with the results observed in Fig. 7f.

Based on above analysis, it can be inferred that the entropy change across SCO phase transition under pressure would be from two sources (take heating as example), i.e., one is the intrinsic SCO transition with entropy change ∼87 J·K−1·kg−1 (Fig. S2), while the other is the entropy change caused by the desorption of gas molecules adsorbed in the lattice of LS-1 under pressure, because HS is quite unfavorable for gas adsorption compared with LS-1 as discussed above. Hence after the spin state of the central Fe(II) ion transfers from LS-1 to HS, the gas molecules would immediately desorb from the lattice, which would also absorb heat; that is, this process also contributes an amount of entropy change. Therefore, the total entropy change would be (87+x) J·K−1·kg−1, where x is the entropy change contributed by gas desorption and finally the measured entropy change by P-DSC (also the total entropy change) would increase with pressure (Fig. S2a). In addition, as illustrated in Fig. 7f, the cell volume at 290 K (close to Tc under 0 MPa) rises gradually with pressure and shows a saturation trend, which means the amount of gas molecules adsorbed in the lattice at LS-1 would also show a similar trend with saturation effect. Since the entropy change from gas desorption x is proportional to the amount of gas existing in the LS-1, it can be reasonably concluded that the x would also be a function with saturation phenomenon under pressure, and finally the total entropy change (87+ x) J·K−1·kg−1 was born with such an effect as presented in Fig. S2.

Besides the anomalous increase of entropy change, the ultra-sensitive Tc with pressure may also be explained in parallel. As the sample was at the LS-1 phase, particularly around Tc, it is reasonable to say that the higher the pressure is, the more gas molecules would adsorb into the lattice, and meanwhile the binding effect between gas molecules and lattice would become stronger with increasing pressure. Thus, a higher temperature would be required to eliminate these gas molecules to complete the total SCO phase transition since the gas desorption and the transfer of the spin state of central Fe(II) is coupled together; consequently, the Tc would shift faster to higher temperatures under pressure. DFT calculations support this result (Inset of Fig. 7f). With inserting N2 (account for 78% of air) into LS-1 lattice, the total energy gradually decreases (left axis of inset in Fig. 7f), while the contributed energy per N2 molecule increases with increasing the number of N2 molecules, indicating that LS-1 phase with more N2 shows more stable trend and a higher temperature would be required to make the N2 desorption. The gradually increased energy contributed per N2 implies that a saturation trend may appear for LS-1 with more N2 (right axis of inset in Fig. 7f). Moreover, resembling the trend of cell volume at 290 K (close to Tc under 0 MPa), it rises fast under low pressure (<5 MPa) and shows a saturation effect (rising slowly under pressure >10 MPa), the amount of gas molecules adsorbed into the lattice would also show this trend with pressure, and finally the Tc-P relation would also conform to such a regularity as presented in Fig. 2b, i.e., Tc increase fast when P < 5 MPa, and the higher the pressure, the increase speed of Tc would be slower.

Discussion

The colossal and ultra-sensitive pressure response for the SCO Tc originates from the gas complexation with the sample 1, which was revealed by P-DSC and VP-SR-PXRD with gas (air) being the pressure-transmitting medium. From the initially measured VT-PXRD and lattice parameters (Fig. S14), the pressure sensitivity of Tc using Clausius–Clapeyron equation (dTc/dP = ΔV/ΔS) was calculated as 0.22 K/MPa, which is much smaller than the measured value from P-DSC. However, it is consistent with VP-Raman results (Tc between HS and LS-1 at P < 0.27 GPa, Fig. 5b) measured by a DAC with silicon oil as the pressure medium. Note that the temperature of the detected Raman area of 1 is practically around ∼320 K ( ~ 20 K higher than room temperature 300 K due to thermal effect of the laser as illustrated in Fig. 5a). As 0.11 GPa pressure was applied, the actual Tc (280 K) in cooling would shift to 304 K according to dTc/dP ~ 0.22 K/MPa, still below the sample practical temperature 320 K, so the sample is still in the HS (Fig. 5b). When the pressure increased to 0.27 GPa, the Tc would rise to 339 K (above 320 K), 1 transferred from HS to LS-1 (Fig. 5b). This means that under the DAC environment, the pressure was solely the compression effect on the sample, while the pressure medium of silicon oil cannot enter the lattice of 1 due to large size of oil molecules, so the dTc/dP conforms to Clausius–Clapeyron equation.

The gas molecules during P-DSC (N2) and SR-PXRD (air, 78% N2) measurements not only transmit pressure, but also penetrate into the sample because of the small-sized gas molecules. Hence, the Clausius–Clapeyron equation that only works for pure single BCE material, is invalid for evaluating BCE performance of sample 1. Furthermore, the penetration of gas molecules behaves quite differently with LS-1, much more favorable than that of HS, as shown in Fig. 7f. Such a phenomenon finally brought forth the singular BCE properties in 1, including the saturation trend of entropy change under pressure and the ultra-high pressure sensitivity of Tc.

From the perspective of sample 1, it is reasonable to say that the amazing BCE is the combination of the sample itself with the pressure-transmitting medium gas. It is due to the specific structure character of the sample that the external gas can then complex with the sample. This result infers that the structure of SCO complex itself is a key factor influencing the final BCE parameters. By designing various structures in the SCO complexes, it is likely that different BCE with influence from other media, possibly small molecules such as gas, H2O and methanal, etc. can be obtained, and more interesting and considerable BCE performances can be expected.

In conclusion, the SCO complex 1 has been confirmed to have potential for sustainable BCE refrigeration. An entropy change as much as ∼125 J·K−1·kg−1 can be reversibly driven by a practical pressure as small as ∼7.5 MPa, while the reversible ΔTr can be up to ∼30 K under ∼30 MPa, owing to the extremely high pressure sensitivity (dTc/dP) of the SCO transition. In-situ VT/VP-PXRD and VT/VP-Raman via DAC demonstrated that a new LS phase (LS-2) develops by pure pressure ~0.4 GPa. With gas being pressure medium, in-situ SR-PXRD that can work under low-pressure range (<30 MPa) amazingly proved that the gas (air) molecules not only act medium but also penetrate into the sample with disparate absorption for LS-1 and HS phase. Eventually, the unique BCE performances appear in 1, including the colossal BCE driven by small pressure, the ultra-high pressure sensitivity of Tc, and the increase and saturation trend of barocaloric entropy change with pressure. Our work demonstrates that with the combination of a pressure-transmitting medium and a spin-crossover complex, singular BCE performance can be achieved, benefiting from the intrinsic structural character of SCO complex, and a more attractive BCE material with larger dTc/dP can be expected by tailoring specific structures in SCO materials.

Methods

Sample preparations

Polycrystalline sample 1 was prepared according to the methods provided in previous literature53. An equimolar amount of solid NaOH (8.0 mg, 0.2 mmol) was added to a suspension of ligand LH (L = 2-Pyridinecarbaldehyde-4’-hydroxybenzoyl hydrazone, 48.2 mg, 0.2 mmol) in MeOH. The deprotonated ligand solution was obtained after stirring and then methanol solution of iron(II) perchlorate (27.2 mg, 0.1 mmol) was added. The reaction mixture was stirred at room temperature for 1 h. The black-green powder was collected by filtration. After washing with cold MeOH (3×5 mL), 1 was obtained after drying at 40°C under vacuum. Yield: 40.7 mg 76 %. Anal. Calcd for C26H20N6O4Fe: C, 58.23; H, 3.76; N, 15.67. Found: C 57.89; H, 3.45; N, 15.62 %.

Magnetic measurements

The variable-temperature magnetic susceptibility measurements were carried out for sample 1 ( ~ 10 mg) using a Quantum Design VSM-SQUID-Magnetometer at an external field of 1000 Oe in the heating and cooling mode within a temperature range from 2 to 340 K with a temperature scanning rate of 1 K/min. Gelatine capsules were used as sample containers for measurements. The M-T curve of the sample holder was first measured, and then the data with the sample in the sample holder was collected. To obtain the pure data from only the sample, the data of sample holder was subtracted. To verify the SCO nature of 1, the temperature dependence of the magnetic susceptibility was measured by a superconducting quantum interference device (SQUID) under a magnetic field of 1000 Oe (Fig. 1e). The χmT value was only ~0.032 cm3·K·mol−1 below 290 K, indicating the LS state of the central Fe2+. It reached ~3.5 cm3·K·mol−1 at approximately 300 K, which is typical of the HS state of the Fe2+ ion. In the cooling process, χmT retained the HS value until a temperature of ~290 K, and it then sharply decreased and returned to the original LS value at ~280 K, which confirmed the reversibility of the phase transition. The transition temperature, which is defined as the peak position of d(χmT)/dT, was ~295 K during heating and ~285 K during cooling, with a hysteresis of ~10 K, showing the typical character of the first-order phase transition.

Variable temperature powder X-ray diffraction (VT-PXRD)

PXRD at variable temperatures was measured by Rigaku Smartlab using Cu Kα radiation. The lattice parameters of the high-temperature state (HS) and low-temperature state (LS-1) were obtained by Le bail method using the GSAS suite.

Variable Pressure powder X-Ray Diffraction (VP-PXRD) via DAC

PXRD measurements under variable pressure were carried out using graphite-monochromated Mo-Kα radiation (λ = 0.71073 Å) on Rigaku Synergy Custom FR-X Diffractometer equipped with a Hybrid Photon Counting (HPC) X-ray detector (HyPix-6000HE). The powder sample was loaded into a diamond anvil cell (DAC) for testing. The CrysAlis software package was used for data collection and initial data reduction. Le Bail refinement was performed using GSAS software to obtain the lattice parameters.

Synchrotron radiation powder X-Ray Diffraction (SR-PXRD) under small pressure

The photo of the scene of our homemade device equipped on SR-PXRD is illustrated in Fig. S15. The pressure (0–30 MPa, resolution: 0.01 MPa) was controlled by a syringe pump and was monitored by a pressure gage (the pressure transmitting medium is air), while the temperature (250–330 K, resolution: 0.01 K) was controlled and monitored by the temperature sensor in the sample cell through PID method with the help of the thermostatic water tank providing a base temperature. The sample was sealed in the sample cell with both sides sealed by a beryllium slice (1 mm) to guarantee the gas tightness of the cell.

This pressure-controlled SR-PXRD was performed in SSRF (Shanghai Synchronous Radiation Facility), beamline BL12SW. The energy of the X-ray in our experiment is 98.5 keV, with the corresponding wavelength being 0.1259 Å. The X-ray spot is a square with side length of 0.5 mm, and the distance between the sample and the detector is 2.75 m. In addition, the detector is Pilatus 3 X CdTe 2 M, whose pixel size is 100 μm*100 μm. The 2D collected data with diffraction rings was then integrated with the GSAS II software to obtain the 1D Intensity-degree diagram.

Pressure DSC and specific heat measurements

The measurement of heat flow was performed using a pressure differential scanning calorimeter (μDSC7 evo microcalorimeter from SETARAM, temperature range: -40 ~ 120°C, pressure range: 0 ~ 100 MPa, calorimetric accuracy 40 nW, pressure resolution 0.01 MPa, temperature resolution 0.01 K). The sample mass for measurements is around ~10 mg. The temperature ramping rate was set to be 0.5 K/min during the whole process of heat flow measurement for barocalorics. High-purity N2 gas (99.999%) was exploited to apply hydrostatic pressure ranging from atmospheric pressure to 30 MPa. The specific heat capacity was also measured by the same calorimeter, the corresponding measurement part of which has been calibrated and tested by Naphthalene (Standard for GC, ≥99.5%(GC)). The entropy change of 1 in the original literature is 66 J·K−1·kg−1, smaller than the value obtained here. The value in ref. 53 is likely less accurate compared with the one obtained here due to the DSC device used. The DSC device used in ref. 42. is Q100 differential scanning calorimeter, which is around 15 years ago, while ours is the μDSC7 evo microcalorimeter from SETARAM, the latest product around the world since 2020, with a measurement accuracy of 40 nW, and therefore, the 46 J K-1mol-1 (87 J·K−1·kg−1) would be the right entropy change value due to the more accurate DSC device.

Variable temperature Raman spectroscopy (VT-Raman)

Raman spectra were measured with the Horiba Jobin Yvon HR800 confocal spectrometer, using a 532 nm laser as the excitation source. The Raman signal was recorded by a Princeton Instruments CCD detector and collected in the frequency range of 100–2400 cm-1. The Rayleigh scattering was removed using a holographic notch filter. In order to avoid laser damage to the powder sample or the formation of local heat at the laser focus that affects the true temperature of the sample, the intensity of excitation light is <1 mW and the penetration length for this sample is ~2 μm. The temperature control of the variable temperature process was performed by the temperature control platform LINKAM THMS600/TMS94. The temperature was stabilized for 5 min before each temperature point was measured to keep the temperature even and accurate.

Variable pressure Raman spectroscopy (VP-Raman) via DAC

Raman spectra at variable pressure were measured with the Horiba Jobin Yvon HR800 confocal spectrometer. The excitation source was a 532 nm laser. A diamond anvil cell (DAC) composed of low-fluorescence I-type diamond was adopted, and silicone oil was used as the pressure transmission medium.

Direct measurement of isothermal entropy change ΔSr driven by pressure

Direct measurement of isothermal entropy change ΔSr driven by pressure was performed by μDSC7 evo microcalorimeter from SETARAM. The quasi-isothermal condition was constructed by a temperature control device combined with the slow operation of pressurization and depressurization to minimize the perturbation caused by compression and decompression. At a constant temperature set controlled by the corresponding device, the sample loaded into the vessel was first placed at a certain temperature with ~30 min to be stable; then ΔSr induced by pressurization can be measured. After the compression, no action was done until the signal was stable again after the influence of noise disappeared. Next, the depressurization process (the inverse process of pressurization) was performed, and similarly the heat flow signal of the endothermic peak was detected. Through several pressurization-depressurization cycles, the reversible isothermal entropy change (ΔSr) at a certain temperature can be obtained.

Barocaloric isothermal entropy change curves (ΔSiso) were obtained quasi-directly by isothermal subtraction of entropy curves at variable pressure: ΔSisoT,P0→P1=ST,P1−ST,P0. Specifically, the ΔSiso for the pressurization process (P0 < P1) utilizes the Q (T, P) on cooling, while the ΔSiso for the depressurization (P0 > P1) process utilizes the Q (T, P) on heating. And the reversible isothermal entropy change ΔSr is the overlap of ΔSiso between pressurization and depressurization processes.

Barocaloric adiabatic temperature change (ΔTad) was obtained quasi-directly by adiabatic subtraction of entropy curves at variable pressure: ΔTadT,P0→P1=TS,P1−TS,P0. Similar to ΔSiso, the ΔTad for the pressurization process should be decided by entropy curves on cooling, while the ΔTad for the depressurization process be determined by entropy curves on heating. The reversible adiabatic temperature change ΔTr was obtained by the adiabatic subtraction of heating entropy curve at atmospheric pressure and the cooling entropy curve at the applied pressure57.

First-principle DFT calculation for gas adsorption

The initial crystal structure of LS-1 and HS were imported from the.cif files of the experimental crystal, C 2/c space group with a = 21.0725 Å, b = 10.0692 Å, c = 12.1339 Å, α = γ = 90° and β = 102.9826° for LS-1, and a = 22.801Å, b = 9.917Å, c = 11.686Å, α = γ = 90°, and β = 106.47° for HS. The imported crystal was then fully optimized using the Quantum ESPRESSO (v.7.3.1) software62,63 with all four independent Fe(II) ions in each unit cell being at high spin state (HS) and at low spin state (LS-1), respectively for simulation. In addition, to simulate the extra pressure-induced N2 insertion, N2 molecules were inserted randomly into the crystal unit cell. For all calculations, electron-ion interactions were described with scalar-relativistic optimized norm-conserving Vanderbilt (ONCV) pseudopotentials64,65. The kinetic cutoff energy for the wave functions was set to 80 Ry. The generalized gradient approximation (GGA) of Perdew, Burke, and Ernzerhof (PBE)66 was used.
