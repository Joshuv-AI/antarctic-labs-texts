Repurposing Resmetirom to Suppress MASLD/MASH-HCC in the Dysmetabolic Era
Authors: Amedeo Lonardo, Ming-Hua Zheng, Ralf Weiskirchen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMetabolic disorders are risk factors for hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) through complex proinflammatory, molecular, and cellular processes within a systemic dysmetabolic milieu. Despite significant advancements in the last two decades, HCC remains a challenging condition to treat. Resmetirom is a liver‑directed, selective THR‑β agonist that enhances mitochondrial β-oxidation, reduces de novo lipogenesis, improves lipid and cholesterol homeostasis, and, in preclinical HCC associated with metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease/steatohepatitis (MASLD/MASH-HCC) models, attenuates midkine/lipoprotein receptor-related protein 1 (MDK/LRP1)-mediated immunosuppressive crosstalk. In this article, we discuss the possibility and rationale for repurposing Resmetirom, which has recently been approved for the treatment of MASH, to potentially suppress MASLD/MASH-HCC. This discussion considers the evolving epidemiology of HCC, the ongoing challenges in HCC treatment, and the intricate connection between thyroid hormone signaling, MASLD, and HCC.Keywords: HCC, MASLD, Metabolic disorders, Resmetirom, Thyroid hormone signaling

HCC—Evolving Epidemiology and Burden

We are currently in an era marked by a widespread increase in metabolic disorders, hence called the “dysmetabolic era.” Metabolic disorders encompass a range of interconnected and dysregulated processes, including obesity, dysglycemia, dyslipidemia, and arterial hypertension. These conditions pose a significant and growing challenge for global health systems [1]. An obesogenic environment fuels the dysmetabolic era [2, 3] and the liver is a principal target organ of obesity and related metabolic disorders [4].

Primary liver cancer (PLC) is a diverse group of disorders that develop in individuals with chronic liver disease (CLD) due to various etiologies [5]. In 2021, PLC linked to high body mass index (BMI) and/or elevated fasting plasma glucose was estimated to have caused 59,970 deaths and 1,540,437 disability-adjusted life years globally [6].

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is the most common type of PLC, accounting for about 90% of liver tumors, representing the second leading cause of cancer-related deaths, and ranking as the seventh most common cancer worldwide [7]. As the potential end result of all forms of CLD irrespective of the variable etiologies, HCC and its risk factors accurately reflect the changing epidemiology of CLD [8]. Asia and Africa have the highest incidence rates of HCC, with China having the highest number of cases globally owing to its large population size and high rates of hepatitis B virus infection, making up about half of all cases worldwide [7]. Heterogeneity exists across Asian subregions with respect to HBV prevalence, metabolic risk factors, and access to care.

While the eradication of hepatitis C virus (HCV) infection is a desirable and increasingly achievable goal, steatotic liver disease (SLD) has increased after successful virologic response (SVR) with direct-acting antivirals (DAAs), even in individuals without previous SLD or typical metabolic markers [9]. Alongside the rise in obesity and type 2 diabetes, the reduction in chronic HCV infections has shifted the etiology of HCC towards a metabolic origin. Evidence shows a consistent global increase in PLC associated with metabolic risk factors from 1990 to 2021, with high BMI being the main contributing factor [6]. Various immunological, cellular, proinflammatory, molecular, and genetic processes in the systemic dysmetabolic environment increase the risk of PLC and HCC [5].

The incidence of HCC-associated with metabolic dysfunction-associated SLD or steatohepatitis (MASLD/MASH-HCC) is on the rise [10]. Established risk factors such as age, sex, and metabolic parameters remain significant in HCC development. HCC incidence is generally higher in men than in women, and sex-related differences in metabolic risk profiles may contribute to this disparity [6, 11].

However, MASLD, driven by contemporary lifestyle and dietary habits, should also be considered as a contributing factor, notably even among individuals who are not obese [6]. MASLD frequently goes undiagnosed, which limits the number of patients undergoing surveillance for HCC owing to the unclear definition of the surveillance target population beyond those with cirrhosis [6]. As a result, MASLD-HCC is often identified at an advanced stage, thereby reducing opportunities for timely curative intervention [6].

Unresolved Challenges in HCC Treatment

Although progress has been made in HCC, incidence and mortality rates remain high. Owing to advanced disease being discovered during HCC surveillance, only about 30% of patients have access to curative treatments, while over half require systemic therapies with limited survival benefits. Persistent challenges in molecular targeted therapy and immunotherapy arise from factors including drug resistance, tumor heterogeneity, a lack of actionable mutations, and unreliable biomarkers [7].

The treatment landscape for HCC is rapidly changing, with ongoing struggles to achieve consistent, curative results on a large scale. While combinations of immunotherapy such as atezolizumab plus bevacizumab have shown progress, current research aims to optimize these strategies for earlier disease stages, combine them with local treatments, and address resistance mechanisms.

Recent advances in HCC treatment involve combining transarterial chemoembolization (TACE) with systemic therapies, such as checkpoint inhibitors and targeted drugs, to improve outcomes for both advanced and early-stage disease [12, 13]. Trials including CHECKMATE-9DW have shown that dual checkpoint inhibitors (nivolumab and ipilimumab) outperformed traditional treatments such as sorafenib and lenvatinib in overall survival for advanced HCC [14]. IMbrave050 also demonstrated reduced recurrence risk with atezolizumab and bevacizumab in high-risk patients [15]. Investigational drugs including SCT-I10A and SCT510 offer new potential options [16]. Since sorafenib’s approval in 2007, the focus of treatments has shifted towards immunotherapy and combinations [17]. Despite improved survival rates, developing highly effective or curative therapies remains challenging owing to tumor heterogeneity, late diagnosis, and resistance [18].

HCC has a cure rate of around 25%, with an overall 5-year survival rate remaining below 30% [19], higher than real-world survival rates [20]. This raises questions about whether long-term survivors are truly cured. The number of new cases and deaths remain closely aligned [21], indicating that HCC is still largely incurable [22]. While oncology is moving towards precision medicine guided by clinical, molecular, and immune biomarkers, HCC lacks consistently actionable mutations, limiting the impact of such approaches [23].

These ongoing challenges can be effectively addressed by recognizing that MASLD/MASH‑induced hepatocarcinogenesis is facilitated through a dysmetabolic and immunosuppressive microenvironment. The use of a liver-targeted THR-β agonist such as Resmetirom, has the potential to address this unmet need through metabolic reprogramming and modulation of the hepatic microenvironment.

Thyroid Hormones Signaling, MASLD, and HCC

In the context of MASLD secondary to endocrine disorders [24], strong evidence supports the notion that primary hypothyroidism is closely linked to MASLD and the more severe histological forms of MASLD. A review of 28 studies with approximately 76.5 million participants revealed that primary hypothyroidism is associated with a higher risk of MASLD [odds ratio (OR)1.43, 95% CI 1.23–1.66] and metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH) or advanced fibrosis (OR 2.84, 95% CI 2.07–3.90) [25]. A nationwide case–control study using the ESPRESSO cohort, which includes Swedish liver biopsy data from 1969 to 2017, involved 12,172 MASLD patients, 56,831 matched population controls, and 5478 MASLD cases, 10,682 sibling controls [26]. Data indicate that hypothyroidism is associated with a 1.68-fold increase in the odds of MASLD. This association remained consistent when analyses were conducted using siblings as control subjects. Additionally, elevated genetically predicted thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) levels and hypothyroidism were linked to a higher risk for MASLD. Mediation analysis revealed that metabolic disorders accounted for approximately 41% of this risk [26].

Hypothyroidism may potentially explain otherwise “cryptogenic” HCC via MASLD and MASLD-cirrhosis [27]. A Mendelian randomization study using data from Biobank Japan, deCODE genetics, and the UK Biobank found that in East Asians, genetically predicted hypothyroidism, MASLD, and other factors were significantly linked to a higher HCC risk. Among the European population, MASLD and percent liver fat were significantly associated with a higher risk of HCC, with replication dataset and meta-analysis further confirming these results [28].

The liver predominantly expresses thyroid hormone receptor beta (THR-β). Activation of THR-β plays a critical role in regulating key metabolic pathways, including fatty acid oxidation, lipogenesis, and cholesterol metabolism [29]. Therefore, various pathomechanisms may be involved in the association of hypothyroidism with MASLD/MASH-HCC. First, elevated TSH levels may lead to SLD through decreased adenosine monophosphate (AMP) and increased sterol regulatory element-binding protein-1c (SREBP-1c), as well as decreased fT3 and fT4 levels driving impaired lipid metabolism, increased visceral adiposity, systemic chronic inflammation, and mitochondrial dysfunction [30, 31].

Individuals diagnosed with MASLD often exhibit intrahepatic hypothyroidism, characterized by reduced local thyroid hormone activity despite normal systemic concentrations. This commonly results from impaired conversion of T4 to its active form, T3, or elevated levels of the inactive reverse T3 (rT3) [32]. Disrupted thyroid hormone balance promotes the progression to MASH by triggering metabolic dysfunction, leading to lipogenesis, decreased mitochondrial efficiency, oxidative stress, and sterile inflammation. This process drives hepatocyte injury and liver fibrosis by reducing selective autophagy (such as lipophagy and mitophagy), which typically protects hepatocytes and whose loss contributes to proteotoxicity and genotoxicity [31].

Specific links to HCC involve oncogenic mechanisms, tumor suppression, and effects on prognosis. Altered thyroid hormone signaling creates a tumor-friendly environment by sustaining chronic inflammation and contributing to insulin resistance [33]. Active T3 may hinder HCC progression by activating the LKB1/AMPK pathway, inhibiting glycolysis, and regulating THRSP expression [34]. In established HCC cases, low TSH and high fT4 levels have been independently linked to poor prognosis and larger tumor sizes [35].

In the field of hepatology, the described endocrine–metabolic landscape has sparked research into thyromimetics. These drugs mimic the biological activities of thyroid hormones without the adverse side effects associated with thyroid hormone administration [36].

Resmetirom, Pharmacology, and Evidence of Efficacy in MASLD

Resmetirom (MGL‑3196) is a first-in-class, orally bioavailable, liver-directed, selective THR-β agonist designed to mimic the beneficial hepatic actions of triiodothyronine (T3) while minimizing extrahepatic thyrotoxic effects [37]. The liver predominantly expresses THR-β, which controls fatty acid oxidation, de novo lipogenesis, and cholesterol homeostasis. Pharmacological activation of THR-β offers a promising logical approach to address the metabolic imbalances underlying MASLD and MASH [38].

At the cellular level, THR-β agonism enhances mitochondrial β-oxidation, aids the removal of intrahepatic triglycerides, and suppresses lipogenic transcriptional programs (including SREBP-1c-driven pathways) [38]. It also increases LDL receptor expression and promotes the uptake of LDL into the liver, reducing atherogenic lipoproteins [39]. These effects result in reductions in hepatic fat content, serum aminotransferases, and atherogenic lipid fractions. Unlike systemic thyroid hormone therapy, THR-β agonists such as Resmetirom have a favorable safety profile with minimal risks of arrhythmia, bone loss, or muscle wasting at tested doses.

In rodent models of diet-induced steatosis and steatohepatitis, THR-β agonists, including Resmetirom, consistently reduce steatosis, hepatocellular ballooning, inflammation, and can even mitigate fibrosis, demonstrating their potential for translation. These effects are primarily mediated by suppressing STAT3 and NF-κB signaling pathways [40]. Notably, in a murine model replicating MASLD through MASH and advanced fibrosis to MASLD/MASH-HCC, Resmetirom reduced hepatic steatosis and tumor burden, even with different oncogenic combinations driving hepatocarcinogenesis, highlighting its robust disease-modifying effect across various genetic backgrounds [41].

Preclinical studies conducted in rodent models of diet-induced steatosis and steatohepatitis consistently demonstrate that THR-β agonists, including Resmetirom, significantly reduce steatosis, ballooning, inflammation, and fibrosis. These effects are associated with the suppression of STAT3 and NF-κB signaling [36, 39, 40].

These preclinical findings have been supported by multiple clinical trials. In a phase 2 study involving patients with biopsy-proven NASH/MASH, Resmetirom-treated patients (n = 78) showed a relative reduction of hepatic fat compared with control subjects receiving placebo (n = 38) at week 12 (−32.9% Resmetirom versus −10.4% placebo; least squares mean difference −22.5%, 95% CI −32.9 to −12.2; p < 0·0001) and week 36 (−37.3% Resmetirom [n = 74] versus −8.5% placebo [n = 34]; −28.8%, −42.0 to −15.7%; p < 0·0001). Adverse events were mostly mild or moderate and were balanced between groups, except for a higher incidence of transient mild diarrhea and nausea with Resmetirom [37].

Subsequent large phase 3 trials in adults with biopsy-proven MASH and stage F2–F3 fibrosis confirmed that Resmetirom significantly increased the proportion of patients achieving NASH/MASH resolution and at least one-stage fibrosis regression compared with placebo, while maintaining an acceptable safety profile [42]. A total of 966 patients were included in the primary analysis population, comprising 322 individuals in the 80-mg Resmetirom group, 323 in the 100-mg Resmetirom group, and 321 in the placebo group. MASH resolution without fibrosis worsening was observed in 25.9% of patients receiving 80 mg Resmetirom and 29.9% in the 100 mg group, compared with 9.7% in the placebo arm (p < 0.001 for both groups versus placebo). Improvement in fibrosis by > 1 stage without an increase in the NAFLD activity score occurred in 24.2% of subjects in the 80 mg group and 25.9% in the 100 mg group, versus 14.2% among placebo recipients (p < 0.001 for both comparisons). At week 24, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol levels decreased by 13.6% with 80 mg Resmetirom and by 16.3% with 100 mg, while remaining virtually unchanged (0.1%) in the placebo group (p < 0.001 for both treatment groups compared with placebo). Diarrhea and nausea were reported more often with Resmetirom than with placebo. The rates of serious adverse events were comparable between groups: 10.9% for the 80-mg group, 12.7% for the 100-mg group, and 11.5% for the placebo group [42]. This publication led to regulatory approval in the USA of Resmetirom as the first pharmacologic therapy targeting MASLD/MASH with fibrosis.

These advancements position Resmetirom as a fundamental therapy for patients with moderate-to-advanced MASLD, particularly those at the highest risk of cirrhosis and HCC. The long-term effects of treatment on reducing rates of hepatic decompensation, cardiovascular events, and incident HCC remain unknown and will require extended follow-up of well-powered cohorts.

Does Resmetirom Have Any Beneficial Role in Prevention of HCC?

A recent study by Zhang and colleagues provides the first in-depth preclinical exploration of Resmetirom as a potential modifier of MASLD-associated hepatocarcinogenesis [42]. Using hydrodynamic tail-vein injection models driven by NRasV12/Myr-AKT, ΔN90-β-catenin/c-Myc, or c-Myc/TP53 knockout, all combined with a Western diet and low-dose CCl4, the authors showed that Resmetirom treatment significantly reduced hepatic steatosis and liver tumor burden across these diverse oncogenic contexts. To mechanistically connect these antitumor effects with disease evolution, they established a multi-stage WD/CCl4 model that recapitulates MASLD, MASH, advanced fibrosis and frank MASLD/MASH-HCC, and performed single-cell RNA sequencing on 134,760 liver and tumor cells across all stages [42]. This integrative approach revealed progressive expansion of myeloid cell populations, especially M2-like macrophages and MASH-associated macrophages (TREM2+GPNMB+), alongside activation of hepatic stellate cells (HSCs) and the emergence of dysplastic hepatocytes (dys-Heps) with copy-number alterations, closely mirroring the cellular landscape of human MASLD/MASH-HCC. The study by Zhang [41] should be interpreted in the context of a broader body of work on THR-β agonism, metabolic reprogramming, and HCC biology described earlier in the manuscript.

Ligand–receptor interactome analysis identified midkine (MDK) as a central stromal and tumor-derived growth factor. It is largely produced by activated HSCs and dys-Heps, with its receptor low-density lipoprotein receptor-related protein 1 (LRP1) preferentially expressed by M2-like macrophages [41]. The strength of MDK–LRP1 interactions increased progressively from MASH through fibrosis to HCC, indicating that this axis plays a role in the metabolic and immunologic reprogramming that occurs during hepatocarcinogenesis in a dysmetabolic liver. At the malignant stage, Mdk expression showed an inverse correlation with Thrb and with lipolytic genes such as Cpt1a and Pdk4, but a positive correlation with de novo lipogenesis genes. This supports a model where the loss of THR-β activity favors an MDK-driven, lipogenic, tumor-promoting phenotype [42]. Consistent with this hypothesis, MDK knockout in Huh7 cells reduced palmitate-induced steatosis and downregulated key enzymes of fatty acid and cholesterol synthesis (ACLY, FASN, SCD1, HMGCR, HMGCS1), while MDK ablation in HSCs reduced their activation, linking MDK to both tumor cell metabolic reprogramming and fibrogenic signaling [41].

Importantly, MDK was found to be overexpressed in human HCC tissue and in independent transcriptomic datasets. The highest protein levels were observed in MASLD-related HCC compared with HBV-associated tumors, and high MDK expression predicted shorter relapse-free survival specifically in nonviral, nonalcohol-related HCC [41]. These findings suggest the MDK/LRP1 axis plays a significant role in MASLD/MASH-HCC rather than just being a generic marker of hepatocarcinogenesis. Functionally, conditioned media from MDK-expressing HCC cells or HSCs, as well as recombinant MDK, induced LRP1 and M2 markers (CD163, CD204, CD206) in THP-1-derived macrophages. It also upregulated cholesterol biosynthesis enzymes (HMGCR, HMGCS1, SQLE) and reduced proinflammatory cytokine production. Conversely, MDK knockout or LRP1 silencing mitigated these changes and shifted macrophages toward an M1-like phenotype characterized by increased IL-1β and TNF-α secretion [41]. Additionally, LRP1+ tumor-associated macrophages produced CXCL16, which engaged CXCR6 on CD8+ T cells and was associated with reduced Foxo1 expression and increased exhaustion markers PD-1 and CTLA-4. This promoted an auto-aggressive yet functionally exhausted CD8+ T-cell compartment.

Crucially, Resmetirom treatment downregulated MDK at both mRNA and protein levels in mouse tumors and in human HCC and HSC cell lines. It increased Thrb and Cpt1a expression, reduced tumor-infiltrating LRP1+ macrophages, and abrogated MDK-driven M2 polarization of macrophages. These findings indicate that THR-β activation can remodel lipid metabolism and simultaneously alleviate MDK/LRP1-mediated immunosuppression [41].

Liver-specific Mdk knockout in the NRasV12/Myr-AKT plus Western-diet model reduced tumor incidence and the abundance of LRP1+ macrophages while increasing intra-tumoral Thrb expression. This is consistent with reciprocal negative feedback between THR-β and MDK signaling in vivo. Pharmacologic MDK inhibition with iMDK reduced proliferation of HCC cell lines and LX-2 HSCs, downregulated lipogenic and cholesterologenic genes, suppressed AKT phosphorylation, and when initiated at the fibrosis stage, completely prevented tumor development in the WD/CCl4 model and markedly lowered tumor burden in the NRasV12/Myr-AKT/WD model [41]. Furthermore, combined Resmetirom and iMDK therapy resulted in greater reductions in liver tumor burden, NAFLD activity score, and LRP1+ macrophage infiltration than either agent alone. This suggests mechanistic complementarity between upstream THR-β activation and direct MDK blockade [41].

Collectively, these data support the notion that Resmetirom may exert antitumor effects in MASLD/MASH-HCC not only by reversing steatosis and improving hepatic metabolism, but also by attenuating MDK/LRP1-dependent immunosuppressive crosstalk between HSCs, tumor cells, tumor-associated macrophages, and exhausted CXCR6+CD8+ T cells (Fig. 1).

From a translational standpoint, the work of Zhang et al. provides a rationale to investigate Resmetirom as a chemo-preventive agent in high-risk MASH with advanced fibrosis, as adjuvant therapy after curative treatment of MASLD/MASH–HCC, and as a combination partner with MDK inhibitors or immunotherapies to reprogram the tumor microenvironment.

Several important limitations should be considered before applying these findings to clinical practice. Firstly, even advanced murine MASH–HCC models are unable to fully replicate the genetic, metabolic, and environmental diversity present in human HCC. Secondly, Resmetirom was primarily administered prior to end-stage or treatment-refractory disease. Thirdly, this study does not address pharmacokinetics or safety in patients with decompensated cirrhosis or post-transplant conditions. Fourth, the most pronounced relationship between MDK overexpression and poor relapse-free survival occurred in cases of non-viral, non-alcohol-related HCC. This suggests that the antitumor efficacy of Resmetirom may be limited to a specific MASLD/MASH–HCC subset, highlighting the necessity for biomarker-enriched clinical trials instead of studies involving unselected HCC populations.

Importantly, these mechanistic insights dovetail with epidemiological and Mendelian randomization data linking hypothyroidism, MASLD, and HCC risk [25, 26, 28]; mechanistic data on thyroid hormone signaling and HCC microenvironment [33]; and experimental work showing that thyroid hormones can inhibit tumor progression and enhance the antitumor activity of lenvatinib via metabolic reprogramming [34].

In summary, the study by Zhang et al. should be viewed as a mechanistic bridge between this larger body of endocrine-metabolic evidence and the specific case for Resmetirom as a chemopreventive and microenvironment-modulating agent in MASLD/MASH–HCC. However, it cannot yet be regarded as an established HCC therapy although it provides a detailed MDK/LRP1-centered framework for rational combination strategies and future clinical trial design.

Conclusions

Rising rates of MASLD and MASH, driven by metabolic disorders, are changing the epidemiology of PLC and increasing cases of MASLD/MASH–HCC, many of which go undetected. While HCC remains largely incurable despite new therapies, the thyroid hormone axis has become an important link between metabolism, liver disease, and cancer, making it a promising treatment target. Resmetirom, a THR-β-selective agonist approved for MASLD/MASH with fibrosis, shows potential in improving liver health and reducing cardiometabolic risk.

Zhang et al. report that Resmetirom activates THR-β in MASLD/MASH–HCC models, suppressing tumor growth, reducing steatosis, and altering the tumor environment by targeting the MDK/LRP1 axis and dampening immunosuppressive cell interactions. This identifies MDK/LRP1 as a mediator of metabolic changes, fibrosis, and immune evasion in MASLD/MASH–HCC. Long-term studies of Resmetirom in MASLD/MASH are now needed, prioritizing HCC incidence and recurrence. Future trials should use biomarkers including MDK/LRP1 and thyroid signaling, and explore combination therapies.

MDK/LRP1 axis: Using the evidence that MDK and LRP1 are overexpressed in MASLD/MASH–HCC, associated with immunosuppressive macrophage polarization, and correlate with worse relapse-free survival in nonviral, nonalcohol-related HCC, we propose tumoral and/or circulating MDK (and possibly LRP1 expression on tumor-associated macrophages) as candidate biomarkers to select patients most likely to benefit from Resmetirom-based strategies or combination therapy with MDK inhibitors.

Thyroid hormone-related markers: Based on the associations between primary and intrahepatic hypothyroidism and MASLD/MASH progression [25, 26, 29, 30], we suggest that serum TSH, fT3, fT4, and rT3, together with hepatic THR-β expression (where biopsy data are available), may help define a “thyroid-dysregulated” MASLD/MASH–HCC subgroup amenable to THR-β agonism.

These biomarker and staging considerations should guide future trial design, and unselected HCC populations are unlikely to be suitable for testing Resmetirom’s potential antitumor effects. Until more evidence is available, Resmetirom’s impact on MASLD/MASH–HCC remains to be proven. The MDK/LRP1 axis emerges as a promising target for liver cancer prevention and treatment. However, unselected HCC populations are unlikely to be suitable for testing Resmetirom’s potential antitumor effects.
