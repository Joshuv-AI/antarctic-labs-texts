Abstract
. Large language models (LLMs) achieve strong benchmark performance, yet user experiences remain inconsistent due to diverse preferences in style, tone, and reasoning mode. Nevertheless, existing alignment techniques such as reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) or Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) largely optimize toward population-level averages and overlook individual variation. Naïve personalization strategies like per-user fine-tuning are computationally prohibitive, and in-context approaches that prepend raw user signals often suffer from inefficiency and noise. To address these challenges, we propose POPI, a general framework that introduces a preference inference model to distill heterogeneous user signals into concise natural language summaries. These summaries act as transparent, compact, and transferable personalization representations that condition a shared generation model to produce personalized responses. POPI jointly optimizes both preference inference and personalized generation under a unified objective using reinforcement learning, ensuring summaries maximally encode useful preference information. Extensive experiments across four personalization benchmarks demonstrate that POPI consistently improves personalization accuracy while reducing context overhead by a large margin. Moreover, optimized summaries seamlessly transfer to frozen off-the-shelf LLMs, enabling plug-and-play personalization without weight updates.



1.
Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) have rapidly become integral tools for information access
(
Zhu et al. 2023
)
, creativity
(
Franceschelli & Musolesi 2025
)
, and decision support
(
Vrdoljak et al. 2025
)
. Yet, despite their versatility, users often report highly variable experiences: some strongly prefer one model variant over another, even when both achieve comparable benchmark scores
(
Li et al. 2024b
;
Guan et al. 2025
)
. A recent illustration is the community split between OpenAI’s GPT-5 and GPT-4o: while GPT-5 is praised for its rigor and cautious reasoning, GPT-4o is favored by others for a more conversational and "warm" tone
(
Wikipedia contributors 2025
)
. These differences highlight an emerging reality—
the “best” model is not universal but inherently user-dependent
. The central challenge is thus not only to build stronger general-purpose LLMs, but also to personalize them to meet diverse individual preferences.

However, personalizing LLMs remains technically demanding and poses two major challenges.
Challenge 1: training and maintaining a dedicated model for each user is infeasible due to prohibitive computational costs and limited user-specific data.
Naïvely applying existing preference alignment techniques, such as reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF)
(
Ouyang et al. 2022
)
and its successors
(
Bai et al. 2022
;
Rafailov et al. 2023
)
, to tailor a separate model for every individual would be computationally expensive and data-hungry. In practice, most users provide only a handful of preference pairs, far too few to support robust model learning.
Challenge 2: prepending heterogeneous user signals as additional context is inefficient and yields limited personalization performance.
Recent approaches adopt a meta-learning paradigm, by sharing a single generation model across users and conditioning it on available user signals (e.g., persona descriptions, interaction snippets, a handful of preference pairs, or user profiles distilled with a handcrafted pipeline)
(
Singh et al. 2025
;
Zhao et al. 2023a
;
Zhao et al. 2025
;
Li et al. 2025
;
Lee et al. 2024a
;
Zhou et al. 2024b
)
. While these user signals do encode valuable preference cues, they are often heterogeneous, noisy, verbose, and task-specific, consuming a large fraction of the model’s context window. This not only reduces the effective capacity for the main task but also leads to performance degradation given the phenomenon of “context rot”
(
Li et al. 2024a
;
Hong et al. 2025
)
, making such strategies unreliable for personalization.

To address these challenges, we propose
POPI
(
P
ersonalizing LLMs via
O
ptimized Natural Language
P
reference
I
nference). As shown in Figure
1
, POPI introduces an intermediate inference step: a
preference inference LLM
distills heterogeneous user signals into a concise natural language preference summary. Then, a shared
generation model
conditions on this summary to produce personalized outputs, eliminating the need for a per-user dedicated model. Importantly, our preference inference model is
trainable
and optimized jointly with the generation model under a
unified objective
. This ensures that the summaries are maximally informative for guiding downstream personalization, while drastically reducing context overhead, making the approach scalable, effective, and cost-efficient.

This design yields several other crucial benefits. First, the summaries remain
transparent and auditable
: developers or users can directly inspect or refine them. Second, the summaries are
transferable
, and can be used to adapt frozen off-the-shelf LLMs, including commercial APIs, without requiring parameter updates, enabling
plug-and-play personalization
. This combination of interpretability and transferability makes POPI particularly practical for real-world deployment.

The learned summaries can also steer frozen off-the-shelf LLMs, enabling plug-and-play personalization without accessing LLM parameters.

We validate POPI through extensive experiments on four benchmarks for personalized preference alignment. Across datasets ranging from sentiment and explanation tasks to forum discussions, POPI consistently improves the overall personalization performance over baselines across various metrics. Moreover, our plug-and-play evaluations show that optimized summaries transfer effectively to diverse off-the-shelf LLMs. Analytical results further reveal that our unified optimization objective encourages summaries to encode maximal preference information, offering a principled interpretation of its effectiveness.

In summary, this work makes the following contributions:

(1)

We introduce POPI, the first framework to jointly optimize preference inference and personalized generation via natural language preference summaries, establishing a scalable basis for LLM personalization;

(2)

We show that optimized summaries serve as efficient, interpretable, and transferable personalization signals, enabling plug-and-play adaptation of off-the-shelf models without weight updates;

(3)

We conduct comprehensive empirical studies across multiple benchmarks and off-the-shelf LLMs, demonstrating the effectiveness and robustness of POPI.

2.
Related Work

User Profiling outside LLM Alignment.

User modeling has a long history in NLP beyond LLM alignment. Applications include personalized query reformulation
(
Zhang 2022
;
Hui et al. 2024
)
, personalized summarization
(
Zhang et al. 2024
;
Ghodratnama & Zakershahrak 2024
)
, and authorship attribution
(
He et al. 2024
;
Deutsch & Paraboni 2023
)
, where systems infer stable user traits to guide generation or retrieval. Beyond NLP, in recommender systems, user preference inference from implicit and explicit signals has been central for decades. Classical approaches such as matrix factorization
(
Koren et al. 2009
)
and collaborative filtering
(
Sarwar et al. 2001
)
represent users as low-dimensional embeddings learned from interaction data. More recent methods explore natural language as a primary medium for representing user profiles
(
Ramos et al. 2024
;
Zhou et al. 2024a
;
Gao et al. 2024
;
Radlinski et al. 2022
)
. Related user profiling techniques also appear in market research
(
Yin et al. 2025
;
Rizwan et al. 2025
)
and adaptive education systems
(
Liu et al. 2025
;
Liu et al. 2024
)
. Our framework is similarly motivated by the challenge of extracting user preferences from heterogeneous signals, but differs by leveraging inferred natural language summaries as compact, interpretable, and effective representations for personalized LLM alignment.

Preference Alignment Methods for Average User.

Preference alignment methods adapt base LLMs to broad, population-level preferences
(
Wang et al. 2023
)
. Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) remains the canonical approach: a reward model trained on human judgments guides policy optimization, as in InstructGPT and related work
(
Christiano et al. 2017
;
Ouyang et al. 2022
)
. A closely related line replaces or supplements human labels with AI feedback (e.g., RLAIF)
(
Bai et al. 2022
)
. Direct Preference Optimization (DPO)
(
Rafailov et al. 2023
)
bypasses explicit reward modeling by directly fitting a policy to win pairwise comparisons relative to a reference model. Variants such as ORPO
(
Hong et al. 2024
)
and SimPO
(
Meng et al. 2024
)
further simplify optimization by removing explicit dependence on a reference model. Other directions reshape the loss with prospect-theoretic weighting (KTO)
(
Ethayarajh et al. 2024
)
, align log-probabilities across ranked candidates (RRHF)
(
Yuan et al. 2023
)
, or calibrate sequence likelihoods against preference judgments (SLiC-HF)
(
Zhao et al. 2023b
)
. Collectively, these methods achieve strong alignment with the “average” user but tend to smooth over individual variation, motivating approaches that explicitly target personalization
(
Xie et al. 2025
)
.

Personalized LLM Alignment.

Early approaches personalize LLMs by learning lightweight user-specific adapters or embeddings to adapt the LLM behaviors, such as HyRe
(
Lee et al. 2024b
)
, LoRe
(
Bose et al. 2025
)
, PAL
(
Chen et al. 2024
)
, and VPL
(
Poddar et al. 2024
)
. These methods mostly focus on reward modeling, and require optimization at inference time for each new user, which limits scalability in real-world deployment.

To avoid per-user optimization, subsequent work adopts a meta-learning paradigm: a shared generation model is trained while conditioning on user signals provided as additional context. Representative methods include GPO
(
Zhao et al. 2023a
)
, which applies supervised fine-tuning on the shared generation model; FSPO
(
Singh et al. 2025
)
, which applies DPO
(
Rafailov et al. 2023
)
and IPO
(
Azar et al. 2024
)
on the generation model; and NextQuill
(
Zhao et al. 2025
)
, which reweights losses using token-level causal influence. While effective, these approaches often share the problem of excessive context overhead and noisy input.

A more structured line of work seeks to distill user signals into certain profiles before being used as contexts. For instance,
Lee et al. 2024a
extracts user descriptions using handcrafted prompting templates,
Li et al. 2025
projects signals into 90 predefined preference dimensions, and
Garbacea & Tan 2025
employs hypothesis generation techniques to generate user descriptions
(
Zhou et al. 2024b
)
. Importantly, our framework is
complementary
to these works: we can treat these manually engineered user profiles as input to our inference model, so that they can be further refined to be effective, efficient, and immediately usable personalization signals.

3.
Method

We introduce POPI, a scalable framework for personalizing large language models (LLMs). Figure
2
presents an overview of POPI’s training framework. POPI is modular by design: it accommodates different underlying preference alignment algorithms, supports plug-and-play personalization of off-the-shelf LLMs, and can be extended to variants with prompt-dependent summaries.

Figure 2.
Training framework of POPI. Given raw user signals, the preference inference LLM generates a natural language preference summary. This summary, together with preference-labeled data (prompt, chosen response, rejected response), is used to compute a summary-augmented preference optimization objective, which serves as a unified optimization objective for both models. Training proceeds in two stages: (1) the inference LLM is optimized with reinforcement learning, and (2) the generation LLM is fine-tuned via standard backpropagation and gradient descent. For plug-and-play personalization, the second stage can be omitted, and the inferred summaries can be transferred to adapt external LLMs directly.

3.1.
Natural Language Preference Summaries as the Basis for Scalable Personalization

In a typical personalization setting, each user
i
is associated with a small dataset of preference pairs
\mathcal{D}_{i}=\{(x,y_{c},y_{r})\}
, where
x
is a prompt,
y_{c}
is the preferred response, and
y_{r}
is the disfavored response. In addition to these explicit labels, we often have access to
user signals

c_{i}
, such as descriptive persona, snippets of interaction history, or a few labeled examples. In some applications,
c_{i}
may also include task-specific user profiles extracted via handcrafted pipelines. These signals encode user preferences, but typically in a heterogeneous, noisy, and redundant form.

A straightforward but impractical approach is to train a dedicated model for each user. While effective in principle, this strategy is fundamentally unscalable: training and maintaining a separate model per user is computationally prohibitive. A more feasible alternative is an
in-context personalization strategy
, where a single shared generation model
\pi_{\theta}
is adapted by providing user-specific information as additional context.

This raises a central design question: what form should this information take? Directly feeding raw user signals
c_{i}
is simple, but problematic, because they are often (i)
noisy
, containing irrelevant or redundant details that obscure the underlying preference information, and (ii)
lengthy
, leading to severe inefficiency when appended to the generation model’s input.

To address these issues, we introduce an intermediate step of
preference inference
. Rather than conditioning directly on raw user signals, we learn a
preference inference model

\pi_{\phi}
that maps user signals
c_{i}
into a concise, natural language
preference summary
:

(1)

z\sim\pi_{\phi}(\cdot\mid c_{i}).

This summary
z
serves as a compact and interpretable representation of user inclinations. The generation model then conditions jointly on the prompt
x
and the inferred summary
z
to produce a personalized response:

(2)

y\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x,z).

Although one could instead represent the preference summaries as latent vectors in an embedding space, such representations are opaque to human interpretation and necessarily require retraining the generation model to leverage them. By contrast, natural language summaries are transparent and auditable, allowing developers to inspect them directly, and allowing LLMs to understand them immediately.

3.2.
Unified Optimization Objective for Preference Inference and Generation

Natural language preference summaries are appealing. However, since preference dimensions and their distributions vary widely across domains, summaries generated with an off-the-shelf LLM are often insufficient, overlooking key dimensions of preference, or failing to align with the downstream generation model. Therefore, it is essential to optimize both the inference model
\pi_{\phi}
and, when possible, the shared generation model
\pi_{\theta}
so that they jointly produce high-quality personalized responses.

Summary-Augmented Preference Alignment Objective.

To begin with, we first derive the optimization objective for the shared generation model
\pi_{\theta}
, by adapting existing "average-user” preference alignment algorithms. Although the same reasoning can apply to a broad class of alignment algorithms, we present the derivation concretely through the lens of Direct Preference Optimization (DPO)
(
Rafailov et al. 2023
)
as an illustrative example. The adaptation of other alignment algorithms is discussed later.

Hypothetically, if we were to optimize a dedicated generation model
\pi_{\theta_{i}}
for each user, the standard DPO loss would be:

(3)

\displaystyle\ell_{\text{dedicated},i}

\displaystyle=-\log\sigma\Bigg(\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta_{i}}(y_{c}\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{ref}}}(y_{c}\mid x)}-\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta_{i}}(y_{r}\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{ref}}}(y_{r}\mid x)}\Bigg),

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{dedicated}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_{c},y_{r})\sim\mathcal{D}_{i}}\left[\ell_{\text{dedicated},i}\right],

where
\pi_{\theta_{\text{ref}}}
is a fixed reference model and
N
is the number of users.

As we have argued, since training a dedicated model per user is infeasible, we instead adopt a shared generation model
\pi_{\theta}
that conditions on natural language summaries
z
. Therefore, we modify the above DPO loss by replacing
\pi_{\theta_{i}}(y\mid x)
with
\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x,z)
, obtaining:

(4)

\displaystyle\ell_{\text{SA}}

\displaystyle=-\log\sigma\Bigg(\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{c}\mid x,z)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{ref}}}(y_{c}\mid x)}-\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{r}\mid x,z)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{ref}}}(y_{r}\mid x)}\Bigg),

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{SA}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_{c},y_{r})\sim\mathcal{D}_{i},\ z\sim\pi_{\phi}(\cdot\mid c_{i})}\left[\ell_{\text{SA}}\right],

which we call the
summary-augmented
(SA) DPO objective. This formulation can be used for training
\pi_{\theta}
: it explicitly encourages
\pi_{\theta}
to prefer
y_{c}
over
y_{r}
when conditioned on
z
.

Unified Optimization.

Crucially, this summary-augmented objective
\mathcal{L}_{\text{SA}}
also provides a natural training signal for the inference model
\pi_{\phi}
: summaries that improve
\pi_{\theta}
’s ability to distinguish preferred responses from rejected ones are implicitly rewarded.

However, optimizing
\pi_{\phi}
with
\mathcal{L}_{\text{SA}}
is challenging, because sampling
z\sim\pi_{\phi}(\cdot\mid c_{i})
is non-differentiable. To address this, we propose to train
\pi_{\phi}
with
reinforcement learning (RL)
, treating
\pi_{\phi}
as the RL policy model and
\mathcal{L}_{\text{SA}}
as the negative RL reward. For efficiency and stability, we use Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)
(
Shao et al. 2024
)
as the RL algorithm, which has proven effective for fine-tuning LLMs under non-differentiable objectives
(
Zhang et al. 2025
;
Mroueh et al. 2025
)
.

To stabilize RL training and preserve the interpretability of summaries, we add KL regularization that anchors
\pi_{\phi}
toward a reference model
\pi_{\phi_{\text{ref}}}
, leading to our final optimization objective:

(5)

\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}_{\text{SA}}+\alpha\cdot\text{KL}\!\left(\pi_{\phi}(\cdot\mid c_{i})\,\|\,\pi_{\phi_{\text{ref}}}(\cdot\mid c_{i})\right).

Interestingly, we observe that this regularization naturally encourages
\pi_{\phi}
to produce shorter summaries, even without explicit penalties on length. This emergent property improves context efficiency and reduces inference overhead, sparing us from introducing explicit length-based constraints.

Two-Stage Training Strategy.

For training efficiency, we adopt a two-stage training procedure:

(1)

Stage 1:
Optimize the inference model
\pi_{\phi}
with RL while keeping the generation model fixed at
\pi_{\theta_{\text{ref}}}
.

(2)

Stage 2:
Freeze
\pi_{\phi}
and fine-tune the generation model
\pi_{\theta}
with respect to the optimized summaries.

This staged approach integrates seamlessly with existing efficient RL training frameworks. While in principle the two stages could be iterated for multiple rounds, we found in early experiments that additional rounds did not provide significant performance gains. This is likely because the unoptimized reference generation model
\pi_{\theta_{\text{ref}}}
is already sufficiently generalizable to discern which summaries are more informative and thus effectively guide the optimization of
\pi_{\phi}
. Consequently, we adopt a single-pass, two-stage procedure throughout this paper.

3.3.
Generalization to Alternative Preference Alignment Frameworks

Although we instantiate POPI with DPO as an example in Section
3.2
, the framework itself is agnostic to the underlying preference alignment algorithm. The central idea, augmenting optimization with natural language preference summaries, can be seamlessly integrated into a variety of existing preference alignment approaches.

As another example, consider Identity Preference Optimization (IPO)
(
Azar et al. 2024
)
. By conditioning the shared generation model
\pi_{\theta}
on preference summaries
z
, we can derive the
summary-augmented
IPO objective in a similar way as Section
3.2
:

(6)

\displaystyle\ell^{\prime}_{\text{SA}}

\displaystyle=\Bigg(\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{c}\mid x,z)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{ref}}}(y_{c}\mid x)}-\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{r}\mid x,z)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{ref}}}(y_{r}\mid x)}-\frac{1}{2\beta}\Bigg)^{2},

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{\prime}_{\text{SA}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_{c},y_{r})\sim\mathcal{D}_{i},\ z\sim\pi_{\phi}(\cdot\mid c_{i})}\left[\ell^{\prime}_{\text{SA}}\right].

This formulation parallels the structure of the summary-augmented DPO objective while imposing a distinct geometric constraint on log-likelihood ratios. Our ablation studies demonstrate that it delivers performance comparable to summary-augmented DPO, reinforcing that POPI is not tied to a specific optimization method but rather serves as a general-purpose framework for scalable personalization across diverse underlying preference alignment algorithms.

3.4.
Optimized Preference Inference for Plug-and-Play Personalization

A key advantage of our framework lies in the
transferability
of the preference inference model
\pi_{\phi}
. Once optimized in Stage 1 with respect to a generation model
\pi_{\theta}
,
\pi_{\phi}
can be directly reused to personalize other language models without additional fine-tuning. In particular,
\pi_{\phi}
generates preference summaries
z
that can be supplied as context to any off-the-shelf model, denoted
\pi_{\text{off-the-shelf}}
:

(7)

y\sim\pi_{\text{off-the-shelf}}(\cdot\mid x,z).

Notably,
\pi_{\text{off-the-shelf}}
need not share the same architecture or parameters as
\pi_{\theta}
, nor do we require access to or modification of its internal weights. In this way,
\pi_{\phi}
serves as a lightweight, model-agnostic personalization layer that adapts external LLMs entirely through contextual summaries.

This plug-and-play approach offers several benefits. First, since
\pi_{\text{off-the-shelf}}
remains unchanged, it fully preserves its generality across domains, retaining competence on tasks outside personalization. Second, deployment is highly flexible: a single optimized inference model can adapt a wide range of generation backbones, including frozen commercial APIs. Third, it is cost-efficient, as the expense of fine-tuning large generation models is avoided.

3.5.
Extension to Prompt-Dependent Summaries

We also explore a variant of POPI in which the preference summary
z
is conditioned not only on user signals
c_{i}
but also on the specific upcoming prompt
x
. Formally, we replace
z\sim\pi_{\phi}(\cdot\mid c_{i})
with:

(8)

z\sim\pi_{\phi}(\cdot\mid c_{i},x).

Conditioning on the prompt allows the inference model to produce task-specific summaries that emphasize aspects of user preferences most relevant to the immediate query. However, this benefit comes at the cost of efficiency: a new summary must be recomputed for every prompt, increasing inference overhead.

Empirically, we observe that prompt-dependent summaries yield modest improvements in personalization quality. Given the limited gains and reduced scalability, we focus on the prompt-independent variant in our main experiments and report prompt-dependent results only as an ablation.

3.6.
Analytical Interpretation

We now provide an information-theoretic interpretation of the summary-augmented DPO objective
\mathcal{L}_{\text{SA}}
(Equation
4
). Building on the Bradley–Terry (BT) model of preferences
(
Bradley & Terry 1952
)
and following the derivation in
Rafailov et al. 2023
, we can show that
\mathcal{L}_{\text{SA}}
is proportional to an upper bound of the negative conditional mutual information between the preference label
y_{c}\succ y_{r}
and the summary
z
, given the prompt
x
:

(9)

\mathcal{L}_{\text{SA}}\geq C-I(y_{c}\succ y_{r};z\mid x),

where
C
is a constant independent of the model parameters.

This interpretation reveals that minimizing
\mathcal{L}_{\text{SA}}
leads to maximizing the conditional mutual information
I(y_{c}\succ y_{r};z\mid x)
. In other words, the optimization encourages the preference summary
z
to encode as much information as possible about the user’s preference label
y_{c}\succ y_{r}
,
beyond
what is already contained in the prompt
x
. Thus, the summaries act as a compact channel for transmitting user-specific preference information into the generation process. We provide the full derivation of the bound in Appendix
A
.

4.
Experiments

To evaluate the effectiveness and versatility of POPI, we conduct extensive experiments across four benchmarks for personalized preference alignment.

4.1.
Experimental Setup

Datasets.

We evaluate POPI on four state-of-the-art public datasets for personalized preference alignment. The first three, introduced by Singh et al.
(
Singh et al. 2025
)
, include: (1)
Review
, which tests sentiment- and conciseness-sensitive reviews of movies, songs, and TV series; (2)
ELIX
, which measures explanation quality across five education levels in scientific domains; and (3)
Roleplay
, which contains over 1,500 complex personas for open-ended dialogue. Following
(
Singh et al. 2025
)
, we train with 20k samples per dataset, using only a few labeled preference pairs as the user signals (four pairs for
Review
and
ELIX
, eight pairs for
Roleplay
), simulating realistic low-resource personalization.

We also evaluate on the
AlignX
dataset
(
Li et al. 2025
)
, which contains 1.3M samples from forum-style discussions. User signals in
AlignX
include demographic attributes, user-generated posts, and labeled preference pairs. Besides, the dataset provides a 90-dimensional
preference direction
, extracted from the above signals, with handcrafted pipelines designed specifically for the forum discussion domain. We therefore run experiments in two settings: (1) using the original demographic attributes, user-generated posts, and labeled preference pairs as user signals; or (2) using the extracted 90-dimensional preference directions as user signals. Following the official protocol, we train with 92k randomly selected samples (7% of the dataset) and evaluate on four official test splits:
DEMO
(demographics only),
PAIR
(preference pairs only),
UGC
(user-generated content only), and
Arbitrary
(signals sampled probabilistically).

Baselines.

We perform two groups of comparisons, systematically covering all combinations of context formats and whether the inference/generation model is fine-tuned.

The first group does not fine-tune the generation model: (1)
Base-Model
, which conditions the generation only on the prompt; (2)
Raw-Prompting
, which prepends raw user signals as context during generation, conceptually encompassing
Zhou et al. 2024b
; (3)
Inference-Prompting
, which prepends summaries obtained from our unoptimized preference inference LLM
\pi_{\phi_{\text{ref}}}
as context during generation; and (4)
POPI-Plug-and-Play
, which uses optimized preference summaries to personalize the generation model (as described in Section
3.4
). These methods are particularly practical for real-world deployment, and can be applied to off-the-shelf generation LLMs without accessing their parameters.

On the other hand, the second group fine-tunes the generation model: (1)
Raw-Aligned
, which aligns the generation model, with raw user signals as context (conceptually encompassing
Singh et al. 2025
;
Li et al. 2025
;
Zhao et al. 2023a
;
Zhao et al. 2025
;
Lee et al. 2024a
); (2)
Inference-Aligned
, which aligns the generation model, with summaries from the unoptimized preference inference LLM
\pi_{\phi_{\text{ref}}}
as context; and (3)
POPI-Full
, our full method that jointly optimizes both
\pi_{\phi}
and
\pi_{\theta}
under the unified summary-augmented preference alignment objective. This group of methods typically exhibits stronger personalization performance than the first group in the targeted domain.

To ensure fairness, we use the same underlying preference alignment framework (DPO, unless otherwise specified) for all methods

Evaluation Metrics.

We report three complementary metrics. (1)
Win Rate with LLM-as-a-Judge (Win Rate)
evaluates personalization quality on
Review
,
ELIX
, and
Roleplay
. Each test instance in these datasets includes a
ground-truth persona
. These personas are
only available at evaluation time
and are never used during training. Following
Singh et al. 2025
, we use GPT-4o
(
OpenAI 2024a
)
as the judge, comparing model outputs and deciding which better matches the ground-truth persona description (see Appendix
B
for the GPT-4o prompting template). (2)
Reward Accuracy (Acc.)
measures alignment with ground-truth preference labels for unseen users in the test set; a prediction is correct if the log-probability margin assigned by the model between the chosen and rejected responses exceeds that assigned by the Base-Model. (3)
Average Context Overhead Length (Avg. Len.)
reports the token overhead introduced by each method, capturing context efficiency.

Hyperparameters.

In our DPO based experiments, we scale the KL regularization weight
\alpha
linearly with the DPO hyperparameter
\beta
to keep the two terms on a comparable scale. The intuition is that a larger
\beta
amplifies the log-ratio terms in the DPO loss; therefore, a proportionally larger KL weight is needed to maintain balance. With this coupling, we discover in our early experiments that training becomes robust to the choice of
\alpha

\alpha=0.002\cdot\beta
across all experiments. For IPO based experiments, we apply a similar principle and set
\alpha=0.002\cdot\tfrac{2}{\beta}
.
\beta
is chosen for each experiment with a grid search across
\beta\in\{0.1,0.05,0.01\}
. Unless otherwise stated, both
\pi_{\phi}
and
\pi_{\theta}
are initialized from
LLaMA-3.2-3B-Instruct

(
Dubey et al. 2024
)
, using its default chat template. We use a batch size of 8, a learning rate of 1e-6, and a cosine scheduler with 150 warmup steps for both training stages of POPI and all baselines.

5.
Conclusion

We introduced POPI, a framework for scalable personalization of large language models through optimized natural language preference inference. By training a preference inference model to distill heterogeneous user signals into concise summaries and aligning it jointly with a generation model under a unified objective, POPI achieves personalization that is both accurate and efficient. Our experiments demonstrate that optimized summaries reduce context overhead by large margins, enhance interpretability, and transfer effectively to frozen off-the-shelf LLMs, enabling true plug-and-play personalization without weight updates.

Beyond immediate empirical gains, POPI highlights a broader conceptual shift: rather than treating preference inference as a static step, we can make the inference process itself trainable via reinforcement learning, so that it is directly optimized for downstream personalization. This makes preference summaries maximally informative, while still naturally interpretable and readily transferable across models.

This perspective opens several promising directions, including lifelong personalization that evolves with user interactions, personalization with RAG, and preference inference from multimodal signals. Ultimately, we argue that personalization will be as critical to the future of LLMs as scale once was. By establishing trainable preference inference as a principled foundation, POPI takes a step toward LLMs that are not only powerful in general, but also attentive to the unique needs of each user.

Appendix A
Derivation of Inequality
9

We derive the information-theoretic interpretation of the summary-augmented DPO objective
\mathcal{L}_{\text{SA}}
. Following the standard treatment in
Rafailov et al. 2023
, we begin by deriving an implicit reward model parameterized by the generation model
\pi_{\theta}
:

(10)

\displaystyle r_{\pi_{\theta}}(x,y,z)=\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x,z)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{ref}}}(y\mid x)}+\beta\log Z(x,z),

\displaystyle Z(x,z)=\sum_{y}\pi_{\theta_{\text{ref}}}(y\mid x)\exp(\frac{1}{\beta}r_{\pi_{\theta}}(x,y,z)).

Under the Bradley–Terry (BT) model of preferences, the conditional probability that
y_{c}
is preferred over
y_{r}
, given prompt
x
and summary
z
, is expressed as:

(11)

P_{\pi_{\theta}}(y_{c}\succ y_{r}\mid x,z)=\sigma\Big(r_{\pi_{\theta}}(x,y_{c},z)-r_{\pi_{\theta}}(x,y_{r},z)\Big).

Substituting this into the
\mathcal{L}_{\text{SA}}
formulation, we can write it as:

(12)

\mathcal{L}_{\text{SA}}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\ \mathbb{E}_{(x,y_{c},y_{r})\sim\mathcal{D}_{i},\ z\sim\pi_{\phi}(\cdot\mid c_{i})}\Big[-\log P_{\pi_{\theta}}(y_{c}\succ y_{r}\mid x,z)\Big].

Next, we decompose this objective to reveal its information-theoretic structure:

(13)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{SA}}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\ \mathbb{E}_{(x,y_{c},y_{r}),z}\Bigg[\log\frac{P(y_{c}\succ y_{r}\mid x,z)}{P_{\pi_{\theta}}(y_{c}\succ y_{r}\mid x,z)}

\displaystyle+\log\frac{1}{P(y_{c}\succ y_{r}\mid x)}-\log\frac{P(y_{c}\succ y_{r}\mid x,z)}{P(y_{c}\succ y_{r}\mid x)}\Bigg]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\text{KL}\!\left(P(y_{c}\succ y_{r}\mid x,z)\,\|\,P_{\pi_{\theta}}(y_{c}\succ y_{r}\mid x,z)\right)

\displaystyle+H(P(y_{c}\succ y_{r}\mid x))-I(y_{c}\succ y_{r};z\mid x).

Since the KL divergence is always non-negative, we obtain the inequality:

(14)

\mathcal{L}_{\text{SA}}\ \geq\ H(P(y_{c}\succ y_{r}\mid x))-I(y_{c}\succ y_{r};z\mid x).

Finally, note that the entropy term
H(P(y_{c}\succ y_{r}\mid x))
is independent of model parameters and therefore acts as a constant throughout training. This leads directly to Inequality
9
, formalizing that summaries transmit user-specific preference information beyond prompts
