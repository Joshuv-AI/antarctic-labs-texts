Large-Scale Bayesian Tensor Reconstruction via Approximate Message Passing
Bingyang Cheng, Zhongtao Chen, Yichen Jin, Hao Zhang, Chen Zhang, Edmund Y. Lam * , and Yik-Chung Wu *

Abstract
While CANDECOMP/PARAFAC (CP) decomposition (CPD) is fundamental for tensor reconstruction, Bayesian CPD often scales poorly because variational updates require repeated matrix inversions. We develop CP generalized approximate message passing (CP-GAMP) for incomplete noisy Bayesian CPD. The algorithm uses Gaussian message approximations to avoid high-dimensional inversions, and it combines a Bernoulli-Gaussian prior with expectation-maximization updates to estimate effective CP rank and noise variance. We also give a formal state evolution (SE) recursion and relate its fixed points to replica-symmetric saddle points, so CP-GAMP’s SE-predicted error can be compared with the formal replica-symmetric minimum mean-squared error (MMSE) benchmark in the matched limit. Synthetic and image-inpainting experiments show that CP-GAMP substantially reduces runtime relative to variational Bayesian CPD while maintaining competitive reconstruction accuracy. Source code is available at https://github.com/BingyangCheng/CP-GAMP.git .



I
Introduction

Tensors provide a natural representation for multiway data in data mining and machine learning, including user-item-time interactions, relational events, spatio-temporal measurements, hyperspectral images, and scientific recordings
[
1
,
2
,
3
,
4
]
.
In these applications, the goal is often not only to fill in missing entries, but also to discover a compact set of latent factors that explain how different modes interact.
CANDECOMP/PARAFAC (CP) decomposition (CPD) is especially appealing because it expresses a tensor as a sum of rank-one components with factors tied to the original modes
[
5
,
6
]
.
This interpretability makes CPD a useful model for exploratory data analysis, recommendation, anomaly detection, and signal reconstruction.

For real data, observations are typically noisy, heavily incomplete, and collected under an unknown effective rank.
Classical optimization methods such as alternating least squares
[
6
]
can be efficient, but they usually require the rank to be chosen externally and do not provide posterior uncertainty.
Bayesian CPD addresses these issues by placing priors on factor matrices and inferring latent factors, noise levels, and rank-related hyperparameters within one probabilistic model
[
7
,
8
,
9
]
.
The difficulty is computational.
Sampling-based Bayesian tensor methods are often too slow for large tensors
[
10
]
, while variational inference (VI)-based Bayesian CPD methods repeatedly update covariance terms and perform matrix inversions whose cost grows rapidly with the rank and the mode dimensions
[
9
,
11
,
12
,
13
,
14
]
.
As a result, scalable Bayesian inference for large incomplete CP tensors remains challenging.

Approximate message passing (AMP) offers a different route to scalable Bayesian inference.
AMP and generalized AMP (GAMP) replace high-dimensional posterior computations by scalar denoising steps plus residual corrections, yielding efficient algorithms with state evolution characterizations in several high-dimensional models
[
15
,
16
,
17
]
.
Bilinear GAMP (Bi-GAMP) extends this principle to matrix factorization
[
18
,
19
]
, and tensor completion AMP (TC-AMP) applies bilinear inference after unfolding a tensor into matrices
[
20
]
.
Nevertheless, unfolding can discard the component-sharing structure that distinguishes CPD from independent matrix factorizations.
Existing tensor AMP analyses also tend to focus on idealized tensor estimation models, known priors, or fixed hyperparameters, which limits their direct use in incomplete real-data settings
[
21
]
.
These limitations motivate an AMP-style Bayesian CPD method that operates directly on the CP factor graph, preserves the multilinear structure, and estimates effective rank and noise parameters.

This paper develops CP generalized approximate message passing (CP-GAMP), a scalable AMP algorithm for Bayesian CP tensor reconstruction from incomplete noisy observations.
Starting from the CPD factor graph, we approximate the high-dimensional loopy belief-propagation messages by Gaussian messages and retain Onsager-style correction terms that compensate for self-feedback in the dense graph.
The resulting updates avoid the matrix inversions used by variational Bayesian CPD and reduce inference to multilinear aggregation, scalar output updates, and scalar factor denoising.
To make the method adaptive, we combine CP-GAMP with a Bernoulli-Gaussian (BG) prior and expectation-maximization (EM) updates
[
22
,
23
]
so that inactive components can be pruned and the noise variance can be estimated from the data.

The main contributions are as follows.
First, we derive CP-GAMP as a direct AMP approximation on the CP factor graph, rather than applying matrix factorization after tensor unfolding.
Second, we introduce an adaptive CP-GAMP variant that uses a BG prior and EM updates to estimate component activity probabilities and noise variance, supporting data-driven effective-rank selection.
Third, we provide a formal state evolution (SE) recursion for the matched Gaussian-prior large-system limit and relate its informative fixed points to replica-symmetric saddle point equations under explicit assumptions.
Finally, we evaluate CP-GAMP on synthetic tensor reconstruction and image inpainting, including ablations on prior mismatch and non-Gaussian noise.

Related Work

Classical tensor decomposition methods such as CPD and Tucker decomposition generalize matrix factorization to multiway data
[
24
,
5
,
6
]
.
Probabilistic CPD models handle missing entries and uncertainty
[

, while Bayesian CP factorization and nonnegative tensor completion can also determine the rank automatically
[
9
,
25

.
Their sampling or variational implementations remain costly at scale because of repeated posterior computations, including covariance updates and matrix inversions.

AMP was originally developed for compressed sensing and high-dimensional linear regression
[
15
,

.
GAMP handles separable nonlinear output channels
[

]
, while Bi-GAMP addresses bilinear matrix factorization
[
18
,
19
]
.
TC-AMP applies AMP to tensor completion by unfolding a tensor into matrices
[
20
]
, but this transformation does not directly enforce CP component consistency across all modes.
The CP-GAMP algorithm developed here keeps the CP factor graph and introduces rank and noise learning in the message passing loop.

Statistical physics and information theory have also studied tensor estimation through AMP, state evolution, and replica arguments
[
26
,
27
,
28
]
.
These works provide asymptotic thresholds and Bayes-optimal predictions for idealized rank-one tensor CPD models.
In contrast, our CP-GAMP construction is written for CPD with an arbitrary working rank
R
, so the message updates and SE discussion retain the multilinear coupling among multiple rank-one components.
The analysis follows the same tradition but is framed carefully: the Gaussian message approximation and the replica-symmetric characterization are modeling assumptions for the large-system analysis.

Nonparametric iterative machine teaching casts example selection as functional optimization
[
29
]
, with related methods accelerating implicit neural representations, graph property learners, and attention learners
[
30
,
31
,
32
]
.
These works reduce neural-network training cost; CP-GAMP instead reduces Bayesian tensor-inference cost on a multilinear factor graph.

III
Bayesian CPD Model

For an
N
-th order tensor
\mathbfcal{Z}\in\mathbb{R}^{I_{1}\times\cdots\times I_{N}}
, the CPD model used in
[
9
]
is

\mathbfcal{Z}=\sum_{r=1}^{R}\boldsymbol{a}^{(1)}_{\cdot r}\circ\cdots\circ\boldsymbol{a}^{(N)}_{\cdot r}=\llbracket\mathbf{A}^{(1)},\ldots,\mathbf{A}^{(N)}\rrbracket,

(1)

where
\mathbf{A}^{(n)}\in\mathbb{R}^{I_{n}\times R}
is the mode-
n
factor matrix,
\boldsymbol{a}^{(n)}_{\cdot r}
is its
r
-th column,
\circ
is the vector outer product, and the double brackets denote the CP/Kruskal composition rather than concatenation.
The entry indexed by
\boldsymbol{i}=(i_{1},\ldots,i_{N})
is

z_{\boldsymbol{i}}=\sum_{r=1}^{R}\prod_{n=1}^{N}a^{(n)}_{i_{n},r}.

(2)

We observe a noisy and incomplete tensor

\mathbfcal{Y}=\mathbfcal{O}\odot(\mathbfcal{Z}+\mathbfcal{W}),

(3)

where
\mathbfcal{O}
is a binary observation mask with entries
o_{\boldsymbol{i}}
,
\Omega=\{\boldsymbol{i}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}o_{\boldsymbol{i}}=1\}
is the observed index set,
\odot
is elementwise multiplication, and
w_{\boldsymbol{i}}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,v^{w})
is additive white Gaussian noise (AWGN).
For
\boldsymbol{i}\in\Omega
,
p(y_{\boldsymbol{i}}|z_{\boldsymbol{i}})=\mathcal{N}(y_{\boldsymbol{i}};z_{\boldsymbol{i}},v^{w})
; for
\boldsymbol{i}\notin\Omega
, the likelihood carries no information about
z_{\boldsymbol{i}}
.

To learn the effective CP rank, we place a BG prior on each factor entry:

p(a^{(n)}_{i_{n},r})=(1-\lambda_{r})\delta(a^{(n)}_{i_{n},r})+\lambda_{r}\mathcal{N}(a^{(n)}_{i_{n},r};0,1).

(4)

The shared activity parameter
\lambda_{r}
controls whether component
r
is active across all modes.
The posterior is proportional to the separable likelihood times the product of these factor priors:

p(\{\mathbf{A}^{(n)}\}_{n=1}^{N}|\mathbfcal{Y})\propto\prod_{\boldsymbol{i}\in\Omega}p\left(y_{\boldsymbol{i}}\middle|\sum_{r=1}^{R}\prod_{n=1}^{N}a^{(n)}_{i_{n},r}\right)\prod_{n,i_{n},r}p(a^{(n)}_{i_{n},r}).

(5)

Method

IV-A

Factor Graph and Message Approximation

The factor graph contains one likelihood node for each observed tensor entry and one variable node for each factor entry.
Fig.
1
(a) illustrates the complete CP factor graph.
The graph is dense and loopy: for instance, two observations that share mode indices are connected to common factor variables, creating short cycles through likelihood nodes and shared variable nodes, as shown in Fig.
1
(b).
Exact loopy belief propagation is therefore computationally infeasible because factor-to-variable messages require integrals over all neighboring factor entries
[
33
,
34
,
35
]
.

Assumption 1
(Gaussian message approximation)

In the large-system regime where
I_{1},\ldots,I_{N},R
grow with fixed ratios
I_{n}/R
, the aggregate messages entering each scalar denoiser can be approximated by Gaussian random variables whose means and variances are tracked by scalar state variables.

Assumption
1
is the standard AMP-style approximation used to obtain tractable updates and state-evolution characterizations
[
15
,
16
,
17
]
.
Under this approximation, CP-GAMP reduces the high-dimensional message updates to scalar posterior mean and variance computations.

IV-B

Algorithm

Let
\hat{a}^{(n)}_{i_{n},r}(t)
and
v^{a^{(n)}}_{i_{n},r}(t)
denote the approximate posterior mean and variance at iteration
t
.
For each tensor entry, CP-GAMP forms the predicted mean and variance

\displaystyle\hat{p}_{\boldsymbol{i}}(t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{r=1}^{R}\prod_{n=1}^{N}\hat{a}^{(n)}_{i_{n},r}(t),

(6)

\displaystyle v^{p}_{\boldsymbol{i}}(t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{r=1}^{R}\left[\prod_{n}\left(v^{a^{(n)}}_{i_{n},r}(t)+(\hat{a}^{(n)}_{i_{n},r}(t))^{2}\right)-\prod_{n}(\hat{a}^{(n)}_{i_{n},r}(t))^{2}\right].

(7)

Combining this Gaussian prediction with the likelihood gives the approximate posterior moments
\hat{z}_{\boldsymbol{i}}(t)
and
v^{z}_{\boldsymbol{i}}(t)
.
For the AWGN missing-data channel,

\displaystyle\hat{z}_{\boldsymbol{i}}(t)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\dfrac{y_{\boldsymbol{i}}v^{p}_{\boldsymbol{i}}(t)+\hat{p}_{\boldsymbol{i}}(t)v^{w}}{v^{p}_{\boldsymbol{i}}(t)+v^{w}},&o_{\boldsymbol{i}}=1,\\
\hat{p}_{\boldsymbol{i}}(t),&o_{\boldsymbol{i}}=0,\end{cases}

(8)

\displaystyle v^{z}_{\boldsymbol{i}}(t)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\left(1/v^{p}_{\boldsymbol{i}}(t)+1/v^{w}\right)^{-1},&o_{\boldsymbol{i}}=1,\\
v^{p}_{\boldsymbol{i}}(t),&o_{\boldsymbol{i}}=0.\end{cases}

(9)

The scaled residual and inverse residual variance are

\hat{s}_{\boldsymbol{i}}(t)=\frac{\hat{z}_{\boldsymbol{i}}(t)-\hat{p}_{\boldsymbol{i}}(t)}{v^{p}_{\boldsymbol{i}}(t)},\quad v^{s}_{\boldsymbol{i}}(t)=\frac{1}{v^{p}_{\boldsymbol{i}}(t)}\left(1-\frac{v^{z}_{\boldsymbol{i}}(t)}{v^{p}_{\boldsymbol{i}}(t)}\right).

(10)

For a factor variable
a^{(n)}_{i_{n},r}
, let
\mathcal{F}^{(n)}_{i_{n},r}
denote the set of observed entries adjacent to it, write
\mathcal{F}=\mathcal{F}^{(n)}_{i_{n},r}
in the following equations, and define
\hat{a}^{(\setminus n)}_{\boldsymbol{i},r}(t)=\prod_{\ell\neq n}\hat{a}^{(\ell)}_{i_{\ell},r}(t)
.
The effective scalar observation variance and mean are

\displaystyle v^{q}_{i_{n},r}(t)

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{\boldsymbol{i}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{F}}\left(\hat{a}^{(\setminus n)}_{\boldsymbol{i}^{\prime},r}(t)\right)^{2}v^{s}_{\boldsymbol{i}^{\prime}}(t)\right)^{-1},

(11)

\displaystyle\hat{q}_{i_{n},r}(t)

\displaystyle=\hat{a}^{(n)}_{i_{n},r}(t)+v^{q}_{i_{n},r}(t)\sum_{\boldsymbol{i}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{F}}\hat{a}^{(\setminus n)}_{\boldsymbol{i}^{\prime},r}(t)\hat{s}_{\boldsymbol{i}^{\prime}}(t)+\Delta_{\mathrm{Ons}},

(12)

where the Onsager correction is

\Delta_{\mathrm{Ons}}=\hat{a}^{(n)}_{i_{n},r}(t)v^{q}_{i_{n},r}(t)\sum_{\boldsymbol{i}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{F}}\omega^{(\setminus n)}_{\boldsymbol{i}^{\prime},r}(t)\left((\hat{s}_{\boldsymbol{i}^{\prime}}(t))^{2}-v^{s}_{\boldsymbol{i}^{\prime}}(t)\right),

(13)

with

\omega^{(\setminus n)}_{\boldsymbol{i},r}(t)=\prod_{\ell\neq n}\left(v^{a^{(\ell)}}_{i_{\ell},r}(t)+(\hat{a}^{(\ell)}_{i_{\ell},r}(t))^{2}\right)-\prod_{\ell\neq n}(\hat{a}^{(\ell)}_{i_{\ell},r}(t))^{2}.

(14)

This term comes from the first-order variance perturbation retained in the AMP expansion
[
16
,
18
,
36
]
.
The denoiser then computes

\displaystyle\hat{a}^{(n)}_{i_{n},r}(t+1)

\displaystyle=g(\hat{q}_{i_{n},r}(t),v^{q}_{i_{n},r}(t)),

\displaystyle v^{a^{(n)}}_{i_{n},r}(t+1)

\displaystyle=v^{q}_{i_{n},r}(t)g^{\prime}(\hat{q}_{i_{n},r}(t),v^{q}_{i_{n},r}(t)).

(15)

For the BG prior,
g
is the posterior mean under a scalar Gaussian observation and has a closed form.
The posterior activity probability is

\pi(\hat{q},v^{q};\lambda_{r})=\left[1+\left(\frac{\lambda_{r}}{1-\lambda_{r}}\frac{\mathcal{N}(\hat{q};0,1+v^{q})}{\mathcal{N}(\hat{q};0,v^{q})}\right)^{-1}\right]^{-1}.

(16)

Given this activity probability, the BG denoiser is

\displaystyle g(\hat{q},v^{q})

\displaystyle=\pi(\hat{q},v^{q};\lambda_{r})\gamma(\hat{q},v^{q}),

(17)

\displaystyle\gamma(\hat{q},v^{q})

\displaystyle=\frac{\hat{q}/v^{q}}{1+1/v^{q}},\quad\nu(\hat{q},v^{q})=\frac{1}{1+1/v^{q}}.

(18)

The corresponding posterior variance is

v^{q}g^{\prime}(\hat{q},v^{q})=\pi(\nu+|\gamma|^{2})-\pi^{2}|\gamma|^{2},

(19)

where
\pi
,
\gamma
, and
\nu
use the same arguments.
Equations (
17
)-(
19
) make the rank-learning mechanism transparent: when the scalar observation for a component is not sufficiently supported by the data,
\pi
decreases, the posterior mean shrinks toward zero, and EM subsequently reduces
\lambda_{r}
.

IV-C

EM Hyperparameter Learning

The EM update for the BG activity probability is

\lambda_{r}^{j+1}=\frac{1}{\sum_{n=1}^{N}I_{n}}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{i_{n}=1}^{I_{n}}\pi(\hat{q}_{i_{n},r},v^{q}_{i_{n},r};\lambda_{r}^{j}).

(20)

The noise variance update is

v^{w,j+1}=\frac{1}{|\Omega|}\sum_{\boldsymbol{i}\in\Omega}\left(|y_{\boldsymbol{i}}-\hat{z}_{\boldsymbol{i}}|^{2}+v^{z}_{\boldsymbol{i}}\right).

(21)

The pruning step in Algorithm
1
is used after the EM update to convert the over-parameterized model into a parsimonious CP representation.
It is a practical model selection step.

IV-D

Computational Complexity and Implementation

For a dense tensor, the dominant computation in each CP-GAMP iteration is the sweep over tensor entries used to update
\hat{p}_{\boldsymbol{i}}
,
v^{p}_{\boldsymbol{i}}
,
\hat{s}_{\boldsymbol{i}}
, and
v^{s}_{\boldsymbol{i}}
.
For an incomplete tensor, this sweep can be restricted to observed entries, while scalar denoising and BG activity updates scan the factor entries.
The per-iteration complexity is therefore

O\left(NR|\Omega|+R\sum_{n=1}^{N}I_{n}\right),

(22)

where
\Omega
is the observed index set.
The first term is the multilinear prediction and residual aggregation over observations, and the second term is the factor-side scalar denoising and activity update cost.
The noise-variance update costs
O(|\Omega|)
and is lower order.
In contrast, VI-based Bayesian CPD methods perform mode-wise covariance updates whose cost grows cubically in the rank in the matrix-inversion step and also requires repeated Khatri-Rao products
[
9
]
.
The advantage of CP-GAMP is most visible when
|\Omega|
is large but the target rank is moderate or over-specified.

The update equations are separable over tensor entries and over factor rows after the residuals are computed.
This gives a direct parallel implementation: entry-wise updates can be distributed across observed indices, while denoising can be performed independently for every factor entry.

V
State Evolution and Replica-Symmetric Characterization

We now summarize the formal asymptotic approximation used to interpret CP-GAMP performance.
The analysis is based on the following assumptions.

Assumption 2
(Large-system limit)

I_{1},\ldots,I_{N},R\to\infty
with fixed ratios
\alpha_{n}=I_{n}/R
.

Assumption 3
(Matched generative model)

Factor entries are generated independently from the prior used by the denoiser, the observation mask is independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) Bernoulli with probability
\rho
, and the noise is i.i.d. Gaussian with variance
v^{w}
.

Let
\chi_{a}^{(n)}=\mathbb{E}[(a^{(n)})^{2}]
and let
q_{a}^{(n)}(t)
denote the estimate second moment tracked by SE.
Define
\chi_{z}=R\prod_{n=1}^{N}\chi_{a}^{(n)}
.
Starting from a weakly informative initialization
q_{a}^{(n)}(0)=\rho_{0}\chi_{a}^{(n)}
with a small
0<\rho_{0}<1
, the SE recursion is

\displaystyle V(t)

\displaystyle=\chi_{z}-R\prod_{n=1}^{N}q_{a}^{(n)}(t),

(23)

\displaystyle\Sigma_{a}^{(n)}(t+1)

\displaystyle=\frac{v^{w}+V(t)}{\rho\prod_{\ell\neq n}I_{\ell}q_{a}^{(\ell)}(t)},

(24)

\displaystyle q_{a}^{(n)}(t+1)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\mathbb{E}[a^{(n)}|a^{(n)}+\sqrt{\Sigma_{a}^{(n)}(t+1)}\xi]\right)^{2}\right],

(25)

\displaystyle V(t+1)

\displaystyle=\chi_{z}-R\prod_{n=1}^{N}q_{a}^{(n)}(t+1),

(26)

where
\xi\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
.
For a Gaussian prior with variance
\chi_{a}^{(n)}
, (
25
) reduces to

q_{a}^{(n)}(t+1)=\frac{(\chi_{a}^{(n)})^{2}}{\chi_{a}^{(n)}+\Sigma_{a}^{(n)}(t+1)}.

(27)

Proposition 1
(Conditional correspondence)

Under the large-system, matched-prior, and replica-symmetry assumptions, and when CP-GAMP converges to the informative fixed point, the formal fixed-point equations of (
23
)-(
26
) match the replica-symmetric saddle point equations for the corresponding CPD model.

A derivation of this SE-replica equivalence, following the SE and replica strategy of multi-layer Bi-GAMP (ML-BiGAMP)
[
36
]
and the classical replica method
[
37
]
but adapted to the CP tensor factor graph, is given in Appendix
D
.
This also connects the replica calculation to the Bayes benchmark in tensor estimation
[
27
,
28
]
: at the replica-symmetric optimum, the tensor-error order parameter
V^{\star}
predicts the normalized minimum mean-squared error (MMSE)
V^{\star}/\chi_{z}
.
SE tracks the branch reached by CP-GAMP from its initialization.
It matches this formal MMSE benchmark only when the stable informative fixed point coincides with the replica-symmetric optimum, while coexistence of branches indicates a possible algorithmic gap.
For
N=2
, the CP model reduces to a bilinear factorization of the ML-BiGAMP type, but arbitrary order requires tracking mode-wise overlaps and multilinear Onsager terms, so the lift is not automatic.
This is a formal correspondence rather than a finite-sample convergence result for adaptive BG-EM.
The SE comparison below uses matched Gaussian-prior CP-GAMP without EM hyperparameter learning or pruning.

VI-F

Discussion

Across the tested settings, CP-GAMP retains rank and noise learning and factor uncertainty while reducing runtime relative to FBCP.
Its NMSE advantage over TC-AMP is consistent with preserving CP component sharing instead of unfolding the tensor.
The SE curves describe qualitative behavior only: because the comparison fixes rank and hyperparameters, residual gaps may arise from finite size and Gaussian-approximation error.

The implementation evaluates only AWGN, for which (
8
)-(
9
) are closed form.
Other separable channels retain the GAMP interface but require task-specific moments.
Hence, quadrature or lookup tables add per-entry cost.
Their implementation and real-data validation are left for future work.

VI-G

Limitations

CP-GAMP assumes low-rank CP structure.
Strong mode-specific subspaces or non-multilinear structure may require Tucker
[
24
]
, tensor-train
[
4
]
, or neural models
[
38
]
.
Its SE-replica analysis is formal, asymptotic, and matched-model, not a finite-sample result for adaptive BG-EM.
Experiments implement AWGN and the BG denoiser.
Other channels and priors require new scalar moments and, for pruning, suitable hierarchical rules.

VII
Conclusion

This paper presented CP-GAMP, a scalable Bayesian CP tensor reconstruction algorithm with EM-based rank and noise learning.
The method avoids matrix inversions, keeps the CP multilinear structure, and supports incomplete noisy observations.
We derived a formal SE recursion and related its fixed points to replica-symmetric saddle point equations under explicit large-system and matched-model assumptions.
Experiments show competitive reconstruction accuracy and lower runtime in the tested settings, and ablations suggest robustness to pruning thresholds and moderate model mismatch.

Derivation for CP-GAMP

This appendix summarizes the derivation that leads from loopy belief propagation to the update equations used in Section IV
[
33
,
34
,
35
]
.
For an observation index
\boldsymbol{i}=(i_{1},\ldots,i_{N})
, let

\mathcal{V}(\boldsymbol{i})=\{(\ell,k)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\ell=1,\ldots,N,\;k=1,\ldots,R\}
be the set of adjacent scalar factor variables.
The pair
\alpha=(\ell,k)\in\mathcal{V}(\boldsymbol{i})
denotes
a_{\boldsymbol{i},\alpha}=a^{(\ell)}_{i_{\ell},k}
.
Let
\Delta^{t}_{\boldsymbol{i}\to i_{n},r}(a^{(n)}_{i_{n},r})
denote the log-message from likelihood node
\boldsymbol{i}
to variable node
a^{(n)}_{i_{n},r}
, and let
\Delta^{t}_{i_{n},r\to\boldsymbol{i}}(a^{(n)}_{i_{n},r})
denote the reverse message.
For a neighboring variable index
\alpha
, write
m^{t}_{\alpha\to\boldsymbol{i}}(a_{\boldsymbol{i},\alpha})=\exp\{\Delta^{t}_{\alpha\to\boldsymbol{i}}(a_{\boldsymbol{i},\alpha})\}
.
The sum-product updates in log form are

\displaystyle\Delta^{t}_{\boldsymbol{i}\to i_{n},r}(a)

\displaystyle=\log\int p(y_{\boldsymbol{i}}|z_{\boldsymbol{i}})\prod_{\alpha\in\mathcal{V}(\boldsymbol{i})\setminus\{(n,r)\}}m^{t}_{\alpha\to\boldsymbol{i}}(a_{\boldsymbol{i},\alpha})

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\,d\mathbf{a}_{\boldsymbol{i},-(n,r)}+\mathcal{C},

(29)

\displaystyle\Delta^{t+1}_{i_{n},r\to\boldsymbol{i}}(a)

\displaystyle=\log p(a)+\sum_{\boldsymbol{i}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{F}^{(n)}_{i_{n},r}\setminus\boldsymbol{i}}\Delta^{t}_{\boldsymbol{i}^{\prime}\to i_{n},r}(a)+\mathcal{C}.

(30)

Here
d\mathbf{a}_{\boldsymbol{i},-(n,r)}
integrates over all
a_{\boldsymbol{i},\alpha}
with
\alpha\neq(n,r)
, and
\mathcal{C}
denotes terms independent of the target scalar
a
.
The integral in (
29
) is high-dimensional because
z_{\boldsymbol{i}}=\sum_{r}\prod_{n}a^{(n)}_{i_{n},r}
depends on all neighboring factor variables.

For the target component
r
and mode
n
, we use the notation

\hat{a}^{(\setminus n)}_{\boldsymbol{i},r}=\prod_{\ell\neq n}\hat{a}^{(\ell)}_{i_{\ell},r}

for the product of the other-mode means in the same rank-one term.
The corresponding product variance is
\omega^{(\setminus n)}_{\boldsymbol{i},r}
in (
14
).

Under Assumption
1
, the contribution of all variables except
a^{(n)}_{i_{n},r}
is approximated as Gaussian.
Conditioned on
a^{(n)}_{i_{n},r}=a
, write

z_{\boldsymbol{i}}\approx a\,\hat{a}^{(\setminus n)}_{\boldsymbol{i},r}+\hat{p}^{(\setminus n,r)}_{\boldsymbol{i}}+\epsilon_{\boldsymbol{i},i_{n},r},\quad\epsilon_{\boldsymbol{i},i_{n},r}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,v^{p}_{\boldsymbol{i},i_{n},r}).

(31)

Here
\hat{p}^{(\setminus n,r)}_{\boldsymbol{i}}
and
v^{p}_{\boldsymbol{i},i_{n},r}
collect the mean and variance of the aggregate contribution not represented by the argument
a
of the target factor entry
a^{(n)}_{i_{n},r}
times
\hat{a}^{(\setminus n)}_{\boldsymbol{i},r}
.
This reduces the factor-to-variable message to the scalar function

H(\hat{p},v^{p};y_{\boldsymbol{i}})=\log\int p(y_{\boldsymbol{i}}|z)\mathcal{N}(z;\hat{p},v^{p})\,dz.

(32)

Expanding
H
to second order around the current estimate gives the AMP residual terms

\hat{s}_{\boldsymbol{i}}=\frac{\partial H}{\partial\hat{p}},\quad-v^{s}_{\boldsymbol{i}}=\frac{\partial^{2}H}{\partial\hat{p}^{2}},

(33)

which are equal to (
10
) for the Gaussian output channel.
Let
c^{(n)}_{\boldsymbol{i},r}=\hat{a}^{(\setminus n)}_{\boldsymbol{i},r}
and expand the scalar factor message as the target entry is perturbed from its current estimate:

\hat{p}_{\boldsymbol{i}}(a)\approx\hat{p}_{\boldsymbol{i}}+(a-\hat{a}^{(n)}_{i_{n},r})c^{(n)}_{\boldsymbol{i},r}.

(34)

The Taylor approximation of the contribution from observation
\boldsymbol{i}
is therefore, up to constants independent of
a
,

(a-\hat{a}^{(n)}_{i_{n},r})c^{(n)}_{\boldsymbol{i},r}\hat{s}_{\boldsymbol{i}}-\frac{1}{2}(a-\hat{a}^{(n)}_{i_{n},r})^{2}\left(c^{(n)}_{\boldsymbol{i},r}\right)^{2}v^{s}_{\boldsymbol{i}}.

(35)

Summing these quadratic terms over
\mathcal{F}^{(n)}_{i_{n},r}
gives the edge-dependent Gaussian likelihood

\displaystyle\left(\tilde{v}^{q}_{i_{n},r}\right)^{-1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\boldsymbol{i}\in\mathcal{F}^{(n)}_{i_{n},r}}\left(c^{(n)}_{\boldsymbol{i},r}\right)^{2}v^{s}_{\boldsymbol{i}},

(36)

\displaystyle\tilde{q}_{i_{n},r}

\displaystyle=\hat{a}^{(n)}_{i_{n},r}+\tilde{v}^{q}_{i_{n},r}\sum_{\boldsymbol{i}\in\mathcal{F}^{(n)}_{i_{n},r}}c^{(n)}_{\boldsymbol{i},r}\hat{s}_{\boldsymbol{i}}.

(37)

Equations (
11
)-(
12
) are the node-aggregated version of these expressions with the first-order Onsager correction retained.
Substituting the quadratic approximation into (
30
) and completing the square yields a Gaussian effective likelihood for each factor variable,

\Delta^{t+1}_{i_{n},r}(a)\approx\log p(a)-\frac{(a-\hat{q}_{i_{n},r})^{2}}{2v^{q}_{i_{n},r}}+\mathcal{C}.

(38)

Taking the posterior mean and variance under this scalar density gives the denoising step (
15
).
The Onsager correction in (
12
) appears from removing the self-interaction between the current variable and the likelihood nodes that sent messages to it.
This correction distinguishes CP-GAMP from a naive Gaussian loopy belief-propagation (BP) approximation and provides the AMP-style self-feedback cancellation used by the formal SE derivation.

The expressions used in the main text are obtained after replacing edge-dependent quantities by node-dependent ones.
For example, an edge-wise Gaussian approximation would assign a different edge-dependent value
\hat{a}^{(n)}_{i_{n},r\to\boldsymbol{i}}
to every edge between a factor variable and an observation node.
Keeping all such edge quantities would require memory proportional to
R|\Omega|N
, which is impractical for large tensors.
The AMP expansion suggests that, in the large-system scaling, the difference between an edge quantity and its node aggregate is a lower-order correction once the Onsager term is retained.
Thus CP-GAMP stores only
\hat{a}^{(n)}_{i_{n},r}
and
v^{a^{(n)}}_{i_{n},r}
for every factor entry and uses the residual correction to compensate for the removed self-feedback.

For the AWGN output channel, the derivatives of
H
can be evaluated without numerical integration.
Combining the Gaussian pseudo-prior
z_{\boldsymbol{i}}\sim\mathcal{N}(\hat{p}_{\boldsymbol{i}},v^{p}_{\boldsymbol{i}})
with
y_{\boldsymbol{i}}|z_{\boldsymbol{i}}\sim\mathcal{N}(z_{\boldsymbol{i}},v^{w})
gives

p(z_{\boldsymbol{i}}|y_{\boldsymbol{i}},\hat{p}_{\boldsymbol{i}},v^{p}_{\boldsymbol{i}})=\mathcal{N}\left(z_{\boldsymbol{i}};\frac{y_{\boldsymbol{i}}v^{p}_{\boldsymbol{i}}+\hat{p}_{\boldsymbol{i}}v^{w}}{v^{p}_{\boldsymbol{i}}+v^{w}},\frac{v^{p}_{\boldsymbol{i}}v^{w}}{v^{p}_{\boldsymbol{i}}+v^{w}}\right).

(39)

For a missing entry, the likelihood is uninformative and the posterior equals the pseudo-prior.
These two cases yield (
8
)-(
9
).
Other separable channels can be used by replacing this scalar posterior computation while leaving the factor-side denoising unchanged.

Appendix B
EM Updates

The EM updates in (
20
)-(
21
) follow from maximizing the expected complete-data log-likelihood
[
22
]
.
For the activity probability
\lambda_{r}
, the relevant complete-data term is

\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{i_{n}=1}^{I_{n}}\log\left((1-\lambda_{r})\delta(a^{(n)}_{i_{n},r})+\lambda_{r}\mathcal{N}(a^{(n)}_{i_{n},r};0,1)\right).

(40)

Let
b^{(n)}_{i_{n},r}
be the latent Bernoulli indicator that equals one when
a^{(n)}_{i_{n},r}
is active.
The scalar posterior in CP-GAMP gives

\mathbb{E}[b^{(n)}_{i_{n},r}|\mathbfcal{Y}]=\pi(\hat{q}_{i_{n},r},v^{q}_{i_{n},r};\lambda_{r}^{j}).

(41)

The expected log-likelihood for
\lambda_{r}
is therefore

\displaystyle Q(\lambda_{r})

\displaystyle=\sum_{n,i_{n}}\Big[\pi(\hat{q}_{i_{n},r},v^{q}_{i_{n},r};\lambda_{r}^{j})\log\lambda_{r}

\displaystyle\qquad\quad+\left(1-\pi(\hat{q}_{i_{n},r},v^{q}_{i_{n},r};\lambda_{r}^{j})\right)\log(1-\lambda_{r})\Big]+\mathcal{C}.

(42)

Setting
\partial Q/\partial\lambda_{r}=0
yields (
20
).

For the noise variance, the complete-data term is

-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\boldsymbol{i}\in\Omega}\left[\log v^{w}+\frac{|y_{\boldsymbol{i}}-z_{\boldsymbol{i}}|^{2}}{v^{w}}\right].

(43)

The posterior expectation of the squared residual is

\mathbb{E}\left[|y_{\boldsymbol{i}}-z_{\boldsymbol{i}}|^{2}|\mathbfcal{Y}\right]=|y_{\boldsymbol{i}}-\hat{z}_{\boldsymbol{i}}|^{2}+v^{z}_{\boldsymbol{i}}.

(44)

Maximizing with respect to
v^{w}
gives the average residual energy over observed entries, which is exactly (
21
).

Appendix C
Rank Learning and Pruning

The BG prior introduces a component-level activity probability
\lambda_{r}
shared by the
r
-th columns of all factor matrices.
This differs from placing independent sparsity parameters on every factor entry.
The shared parameter encourages entire rank-one components to be removed, which is the desired behavior for CP-rank learning.
At an EM iteration,
\lambda_{r}
is updated by averaging scalar posterior activity probabilities over all entries in component
r
:

\lambda_{r}^{j+1}=\frac{1}{\sum_{n}I_{n}}\sum_{n}\sum_{i_{n}}\pi(\hat{q}_{i_{n},r},v^{q}_{i_{n},r};\lambda_{r}^{j}).

(45)

Thus a component remains active only if it is consistently supported across modes and factor rows.
This averaging makes the rank estimate less sensitive to isolated noisy entries than a rule based on individual coefficient magnitudes.

The pruning rule
\lambda_{r}<T_{\lambda}
is not required to define the posterior approximation.
It is used to reduce computation after EM has already assigned low posterior activity to a component.
Without pruning, inactive components remain in the factor matrices with means close to zero but still contribute to the cost of computing (
6
)-(
7
).
Pruning therefore acts as a computational shortcut and as a way to report an explicit effective rank.
The ablation in Table
II
shows that the reconstruction NMSE changes little when the threshold is reduced from 0.3 to 0.1 or removed entirely, suggesting that the posterior shrinkage is doing most of the statistical work and pruning mainly affects model compactness.

There is also a complementary view of rank learning based on rank identifiability and moment matching in
[
39
]
.
Such approaches can provide an initial estimate or upper bound for the rank before message passing starts.
In CP-GAMP, this information could be used to choose the maximum rank
R
or to initialize
\lambda_{r}
non-uniformly.
The BG-EM mechanism would then refine the rank estimate during inference.
This combination may reduce the amount of over-parameterization needed in large tensors and is a useful direction for future work.

Appendix D
SE-Replica Correspondence

This appendix gives the derivation behind Proposition
1
.
The argument follows the empirical-convergence and replica-symmetric strategy used for ML-BiGAMP
[
36
]
, with the bilinear product replaced by the CP multilinear product.
For
N=2
this collapses to the ML-BiGAMP-type bilinear case, but the extension to arbitrary order is nontrivial because the mode-wise overlaps and Onsager corrections must be tracked separately.

SE derivation.

Assume empirical convergence with bounded second moments in the pseudo-Lipschitz sense used in AMP SE
[
17
]
.
For fixed mode
n
, the CP-GAMP input to
a^{(n)}_{i_{n},r}
is a sum over
O(\prod_{\ell\neq n}I_{\ell})
neighboring entries.
After the Onsager correction in (
12
), the large-system central limit theorem (CLT) motivates the scalar channel

\displaystyle\hat{q}^{(n)}

\displaystyle=a^{(n)}+\sqrt{\Sigma_{a}^{(n)}}\xi,\quad\xi\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1),

(46)

\displaystyle q_{a}^{(n)}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\left(\mathbb{E}[a^{(n)}|\hat{q}^{(n)}]\right)^{2}\right].

(47)

Under the matched prior, the Nishimori identity
[
40
]
makes this second moment equal to the planted-estimate overlap.
The CP product then gives

V=\mathbb{E}[(z_{\boldsymbol{i}}-\hat{z}_{\boldsymbol{i}})^{2}]=\chi_{z}-R\prod_{n=1}^{N}q_{a}^{(n)}.

(48)

To see this product form, write the contribution of component
r
as

x_{\boldsymbol{i},r}=\prod_{n}a^{(n)}_{i_{n},r}
and its estimate as

\hat{x}_{\boldsymbol{i},r}=\prod_{n}\hat{a}^{(n)}_{i_{n},r}
.
The zero-mean independent components make the cross terms

\mathbb{E}[x_{\boldsymbol{i},r}x_{\boldsymbol{i},r^{\prime}}]
and

\mathbb{E}[x_{\boldsymbol{i},r}\hat{x}_{\boldsymbol{i},r^{\prime}}]
vanish for
r\neq r^{\prime}
.
For a fixed component, empirical convergence gives

\mathbb{E}[x_{\boldsymbol{i},r}\hat{x}_{\boldsymbol{i},r}]=\prod_{n=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}[a^{(n)}\hat{a}^{(n)}]=\prod_{n=1}^{N}q_{a}^{(n)},

(49)

where the last equality uses the matched-prior Nishimori relation.
Thus the tensor-level overlap is the product of the mode-wise overlaps, multiplied by the number of active rank components.
For the AWGN missing-data channel,
v^{p}_{\boldsymbol{i}}\to V
and averaging over the Bernoulli mask yields

\displaystyle v^{z}

\displaystyle=\rho\frac{v^{w}V}{v^{w}+V}+(1-\rho)V,

(50)

\displaystyle v^{s}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{V}\left(1-\frac{v^{z}}{V}\right)=\frac{\rho}{v^{w}+V},

(51)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\Sigma_{a}^{(n)}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\rho\prod_{\ell\neq n}I_{\ell}q_{a}^{(\ell)}}{v^{w}+V}.

(52)

These identities are equivalent to (
23
)-(
26
).

Replica correspondence.

The corresponding formal replica calculation
[
37
]
starts from the quenched log partition function, normalized according to the chosen large-system scaling, and applies the identity

\mathbb{E}\log p(\mathbfcal{Y})=\lim_{\tau\to 0}\partial_{\tau}\log\mathbb{E}p^{\tau}(\mathbfcal{Y}).

(53)

For integer
\tau
, the replicated factors share the same observation tensor.
Introduce overlaps

Q_{ab}^{(n)}=\frac{1}{I_{n}R}\sum_{i_{n},r}a_{i_{n},r}^{(n)(a)}a_{i_{n},r}^{(n)(b)},\quad a,b=0,\ldots,\tau,

(54)

where
a=0
denotes the planted replica.
The CP product gives replicated tensor covariance
R\prod_{n}Q_{ab}^{(n)}
.
Under the replica-symmetric (RS) ansatz,
Q_{aa}^{(n)}=\chi_{a}^{(n)}
and
Q_{ab}^{(n)}=q_{a}^{(n)}
for
a\neq b
; the conjugate saddle-point variables decouple each factor entry into the same scalar AWGN channel as SE.
Stationarity gives

\displaystyle q_{a}^{(n)}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\mathbb{E}[a^{(n)}|a^{(n)}+\sqrt{\Sigma_{a}^{(n)}}\xi]\right)^{2}\right],

(55)

\displaystyle\Sigma_{a}^{(n)}

\displaystyle=\frac{v^{w}+V}{\rho\prod_{\ell\neq n}I_{\ell}q_{a}^{(\ell)}},

(56)

\displaystyle V

\displaystyle=\chi_{z}-R\prod_{n=1}^{N}q_{a}^{(n)}.

(57)

These match the fixed-point equations of (
23
)-(
26
).
This is the fixed-point correspondence summarized in Proposition
1
.
The same
V
is the SE per-entry tensor error,
V=\mathbb{E}[(z_{\boldsymbol{i}}-\hat{z}_{\boldsymbol{i}})^{2}]
.
At the replica-symmetric Bayes branch it gives the formal MMSE prediction, with normalized error
V/\chi_{z}
.
Thus the SE curve is an algorithmic trajectory toward one saddle-point branch, while the lowest-error Bayes branch gives the formal MMSE benchmark.
The argument relies on replica symmetry, matched priors, and convergence to the informative branch.

The finite-size gap in the no-EM SE comparison may arise because the factor graph has many short cycles and the Gaussian approximation relies on averaging over many weakly dependent contributions.

Appendix E
Experimental Settings and Reproducibility

In our experiments, we use random initialization unless otherwise stated.
The pruning threshold
T_{\lambda}=0.3
is applied only after EM has updated
\lambda_{r}
, which reduces unnecessary components and accelerates later iterations.

For synthetic experiments, a ground-truth tensor is generated by drawing all factor entries independently from
\mathcal{N}(0,1)
and then forming the CP tensor in (
1
).
The noise variance is selected to match the target SNR,

\mathrm{SNR}=10\log_{10}\frac{\mathrm{Var}(\mathbfcal{Z})}{v^{w}}.

(58)

The observation mask is sampled independently with probability
\rho
for each entry.
All reported synthetic NMSE values are averages over 10 independent tensors and masks.

The compared methods are configured as follows.
For synthetic experiments, both adaptive CP-GAMP and FBCP use the initial working rank

R_{\mathrm{init}}=\max(\lfloor\max_{n}I_{n}/2\rfloor,R_{\mathrm{true}})
,
where
R_{\mathrm{true}}
is the ground-truth CP rank.
CP-GAMP then learns the active components through
\lambda_{r}
.
FBCP is run with automatic rank determination, following the published stopping rule.
TC-AMP is given the true rank because it does not have the same EM rank-learning mechanism.
This gives TC-AMP extra rank information and makes the comparison conservative with respect to rank selection.
The maximum iteration count is 1000 for all iterative methods.
The stopping threshold for CP-GAMP is
3\times 10^{-4}
on the relative change of the reconstructed tensor.

For image inpainting, each red-green-blue (RGB) image is represented as a
256\times 256\times 3
tensor.
The missing mask is applied independently to tensor entries, and AWGN is added at 10 dB SNR before masking.
Runtime is measured wall-clock time for the full reconstruction, including hyperparameter updates.
Synthetic runs used an Intel i5-8500 desktop and image runs used an Apple M2 Pro laptop, both with 16 GB memory.
Because hardware differs, runtime comparisons should be interpreted within each experimental group rather than across synthetic and image tasks.

Appendix F
Observation Channels and Priors

CP-GAMP separates multilinear aggregation from scalar inference.
The output module requires the posterior mean and variance of
z_{\boldsymbol{i}}
under a Gaussian pseudo-prior and the derivatives summarized by
\hat{s}_{\boldsymbol{i}}
and
v^{s}_{\boldsymbol{i}}
.
These are closed form for AWGN.
Unimplemented alternatives include Bernoulli, count, and heavy-tailed likelihoods
[
16
]
.
Their moments require closed forms, lookup tables, or quadrature, with
K
-point quadrature adding
O(K|\Omega|)
work per iteration.

For any separable prior
p_{0}(a)
, the factor module uses the scalar channel
\hat{q}=a+w
,
w\sim\mathcal{N}(0,v^{q})
, and returns its posterior mean and variance.
Gaussian, BG, and some mixture priors are closed form; nonconjugate priors require numerical evaluation.
The shared BG activity
\lambda_{r}
supplies rank shrinkage, whereas other priors need an analogous hierarchy for pruning, and their SE characterization must be rederived.

Appendix G
Complexity Comparison

This appendix gives a more explicit comparison between CP-GAMP and the main baselines.
Let
N
be the tensor order,
R
the working rank, and
\Omega
the observed entry set.
For CP-GAMP, the dominant cost per iteration comes from computing the multilinear predictions and residual statistics over
\Omega
, followed by the scalar denoising updates over all factor entries.
This gives the practical cost

O\!\left(NR|\Omega|+R\sum_{n=1}^{N}I_{n}\right),

(59)

where the first term dominates in large incomplete tensors.
The memory footprint is similarly modest because the algorithm stores scalar means and variances for factor entries and scalar residual terms for observations.

For FBCP
[
9
]
, the cost structure is different.
In addition to expectation updates over observed entries, VI requires covariance updates for latent factors and repeated products involving Khatri-Rao structure.
Using the same notation, its per-iteration cost is

O\!\left(NR^{2}|\Omega|+R^{3}\sum_{n=1}^{N}I_{n}\right).

(60)

These terms reflect rank-dependent sufficient-statistic accumulation, dense covariance updates, and matrix inversions.
Even when efficient implementations reduce constant factors, the resulting runtime grows much faster with
R
than the scalar-denoiser structure of CP-GAMP.
This explains the widening runtime gap in Fig.
2
as the rank or tensor dimension increases.

TC-AMP
[
20
]
is closer to CP-GAMP in spirit because it also avoids VI-style covariance updates.
Its main limitation is not computational but structural.
The unfolding step transforms the tensor problem into a matrix factorization problem and therefore weakens the shared-component interpretation of CP factors across all modes.
This is why TC-AMP can remain fast on the
256\times 256
image inpainting tasks while showing lower NMSE accuracy than direct CP-GAMP updates in our tests.
