Molecular Gas Morphological Analogues for the Milky Way
Neal J. Evans II, Davide Elia, Keith Hawkins, Sophia Stuber, Jiayi Sun

Abstract
Complete catalogs of molecular clouds in the Milky Way allow analysis of the molecular medium and the star formation properties of the Milky Way that closely follows the method used for nearby galaxies. We explore whether the big dip in the radial distribution of molecular gas in the Milky Way is peculiar and find several other galaxies with similar patterns, all with similar morphological classifications of YClxxGnR, indicating a clearly defined, long bar leading to a grand-design spiral. This category is fairly rare among galaxies in the PHANGS sample, but all galaxies with this classification have some evidence for dips in the radial distribution of CO emission. The lengths of the bars correlate with the extents of the dips. The Milky Way and the other galaxies with dips have similar stellar masses and star formation rates, both lying near the high ends of the distributions for all PHANGS galaxies.



I
Introduction

Comparison of the
Milky Way
to nearby galaxies can be illuminating. While we have the advantage of spatial resolution for studies in the
Milky Way
, the edge-on view within the Galaxy makes it difficult to be sure of the large-scale properties, which are readily apparent in other galaxies.
In addition, studies of molecular gas in our Galaxy are complementary to studies of its stellar distribution. While unaffected by extinction, CO surveys rely on distances that are poorly constrained from rotation curves

(
Russeil et al. 2017
, e.g.,)
. Studies of stars in the Milky Way, while providing a clearer picture of its spatial, chemical and dynamical nature, are hampered in the inner
Milky Way
by extinction, line-of-sight confusion, and the existence of multiple stellar populations

(
Bland-Hawthorn & Gerhard 2016
, e.g.,)
.

The time is right to place the stellar and molecular
Milky Way
in the extra-galactic context.
Over the last decade, we have entered a data-rich era that has enabled us to more completely map the molecular and stellar components of the Milky Way

(
Miville-Deschênes et al. 2017
;
Gaia Collaboration et al. 2023
, e.g.,)
.
Further surveys of its star formation and molecular gas properties are underway

(
Su et al. 2019
;
Sun et al. 2020
;
Zhou et al. 2025
;
Duarte-Cabral et al. 2021
;
Duarte-Cabral & The Sedigism
Consortium 2022
;
Neralwar et al. 2022
)
. In addition, the properties of the stellar component of the
Milky Way
are also being studied intensively. The recent explosion in data from large-scale astrometric
(
Gaia Collaboration et al. 2023
, Gaia,)
, spectroscopic
(
Kollmeier et al. 2025a
, e.g., SDSS-V,)
, and photometric
(
Gaia Collaboration et al. 2023
, e.g., Gaia, and many others,)
surveys, have ushered in a new data-rich industrial revolution transforming our broad understanding of the Milky Way from its stellar populations.

These large-scale datasets have been used to uncover multiple components that make up the Galactic disk, which includes a “thin” disk that is chemically, spatially, and dynamically distinct from a “thick” disk
(e.g.,
Gilmore & Reid 1983
;
Edvardsson et al. 1993
;
Feltzing & Chiba 2013
;
Hayden et al. 2015
;
Hawkins et al. 2015
;
Hayden et al. 2017
;
Imig et al. 2023
, and references therein)
. Stellar population analysis in the Galactic thin disk has revealed a complex chemical, dynamical, and spatially distinct set of structures that build the underlying thin disk. Some of these complexities include spiral arms in both gas and young stellar populations
(
Poggio et al. 2021
;
Poggio et al. 2022
;
Hawkins 2023
;
Hackshaw et al. 2024
;
Jurado et al. 2026
, e.g.,)
, the existence of negative radial (and vertical) metallicity gradients across a wide range in ages
(
Mayor 1976
;
Andrievsky et al. 2002
;
Daflon & Cunha 2004
;
Bergemann et al. 2014
;
Xiang et al. 2015
;
Anders et al. 2017
;
Yan et al. 2019
;
Hawkins 2023
, e.g.,)
, which implies inside-out formation
(
Larson 1976
;
Frankel et al. 2019
, e.g.,)
, and dynamically distinct substructures
(
Hunt et al. 2018
;
Jurado et al. 2026
, e.g.,)
. While significant progress has been made in understanding the (chemical, spatial, and dynamical) structure of the Milky Way through the detailed analysis of large samples (10
5-7
) its stars, a more complete picture of our Galaxy, in the context of others, will require a joint analysis of its stars and molecular gaseous components.

Comparison of CO emission in the
Milky Way
to that in other galaxies may allow insight into the molecular morphology of the
Milky Way
.
The detailed images of molecular gas in the PHANGS sample

(
Leroy et al. 2021a
)

have been used to construct molecular morphological classifications

(
Stuber et al. 2023
)
, hereafter referred to as Stuber classes.
This paper compares the radial distribution of molecular gas to those of galaxies in the PHANGS sample.

III
Is the Milky Way Odd?

The
Milky Way
has been described as a typical galaxy (“magnificently mediocre”) based on its stellar mass and luminosity

(
Kollmeier et al. 2025b
)

and as an unusual “green valley” galaxy

(
Bland-Hawthorn & Gerhard 2016
)

based on its specific star formation rate. It falls squarely on the whole-galaxy Kennicutt-Schmidt relation between star formation rate and total gas surface densities

(
Kennicutt & Evans 2012
)
. Milky Way analogues have been selected on the basis of many different properties

(
Kennicutt 2001
;
Fraser-McKelvie et al. 2019
;
Kormendy & Bender 2019
;
Vavilova et al. 2024
;
Boardman et al. 2020
;
Zhou et al. 2023
;
Kompaniiets et al. 2025
, e.g.,)
,
but comparisons based on the morphology of the molecular gas are new.

There is a widely shown
model
of the
Milky Way
based on
Spitzer
observations by the GLIMPSE team

(
Benjamin et al. 2005
)
, showing a bar and well-defined spiral arms, but the structure of the inner
Milky Way
is still quite uncertain. For example,

Balser & Burton 2025

found no evidence for spiral arms in the HI4PI data, and they suggest that the
Milky Way
is a flocculent galaxy.

The
Milky Way
has a dramatic feature in plots of molecular gas or SFR versus
R_{\rm Gal}
: a big dip from the CMZ that extends out to about 4 kpc, followed by a rise to a plateau extending to 5.5 kpc, then a steady decline to 20 kpc

(
Elia et al. 2022
;
Elia et al. 2025a
)
.
2
2

2

The small local peak around 8 kpc is presumably caused by small local clouds that may be missed at larger distances.
Extrapolation of the trend outside 5.5 kpc back to the center is roughly consistent with the molecular density there. The value of
\langle\mbox{$I_{\rm CO}$}\rangle
in the dip is about an order of magnitude below the trend interpolated between the center and the outer regions.
The region of the dip coincides with the long bar identified by

Benjamin et al. 2005
.
Estimates of the bar half-length range from 3.5 kpc

(
Queiroz et al. 2021
;
Lucey et al. 2023
)

to
5.0\pm 0.2
kpc

(
Wegg et al. 2015
;
Bland-Hawthorn & Gerhard 2016
)
.

Lucey et al. 2023
emphasize that their bar length of 3.5 kpc is based on dynamical considerations and that a spiral arm starting at the end of the bar extending to 4.8 kpc could reconcile the different measurements.

It is not clear whether the relative absence of molecular gas from 1-4 kpc is caused by the bar excluding it or by incorrect distance assignment because models of galactic rotation have not properly accounted for the bar.
While a lack of star formation can be seen in two-dimensional images of the
Milky Way

(
Elia et al. 2022
)
, the gaps are more prominent on the near side of the center.
The issues of distance determination are less for one-dimensional radial distributions, which are less affected by the kinematic distance ambiguity, so we concentrate on those.

Because bars and molecular gas distributions are much more easily discernible in other galaxies, the PHANGS sample provides a good comparison set.
Big dips are not easily apparent in the plots where all galaxies are plotted. However, examination of all the galaxies in
61
with CO radial distributions out to 8 kpc (26 galaxies) found 5 other galaxies with visually similar big dips. They all have similar Stuber classifications
(
Stuber et al. 2023
)
: YClxxGnR, meaning clearly visible disks (Y) having clear (C), long (l) bars leading to grand-design spirals (G) without non-central rings (nR). The detailed bar shapes and presence or absence of a central ring vary among the five and are thus indicated as xx here. These are plotted, along with the
Milky Way
in Figure
1
. To normalize for galaxy size and concentrate on shape, the plots are versus
R_{\rm Gal}
/
R_{\rm e}
, where
R_{\rm e}
is the effective (half-light) radius; plots versus
R_{\rm Gal}
alone still indicate the similarity. To place the PHANGS galaxies in context, the intensity in the CO
J=2\rightarrow 1
line has been converted to the intensity in the
J=1\rightarrow 0
line using a factor
R_{\rm 21}
that depends on
R_{\rm Gal}
via the dependence on
\Sigma_{\rm SFR}

(
Sun et al. 2025
)
.

There are three other galaxies in the archive with the class of YClxxGnR. As plotted in Figure
2

\mbox{$R_{\rm Gal}$}/\mbox{$R_{\rm e}$}

1

IV
Discussion

The distinctive big dip in the radial distribution of molecular gas and star formation in the
Milky Way
is seen in a small fraction of galaxies in the PHANGS study. All of those galaxies have a characteristic classification of their molecular morphology

(
Stuber et al. 2023
)
,
featuring clearly defined, long bars and grand-design spirals outside the bars. These data suggest that the
Milky Way
shares this classification. The presence of the bar agrees well with recent analysis of stars in the inner
Milky Way
, with evidence for a bar of length similar to that of the dip in molecular gas. The analysis of stars is complicated by the existence of different age and metallicity populations in the inner
Milky Way
, but analyses of the younger, more metal-rich stars find strong evidence for a bar

(
Queiroz et al. 2021
)
.

Because all the galaxies with dips have bars with lengths that correlate with the extent of the dip, this analysis provides a new line of support for the hypothesis of a bar with radius about 4 kpc in the
Milky Way
, as suggested by

Blitz & Spergel 1991

and many later papers

(
Lucey et al. 2023
, e.g.,)
.
The fact that the similar galaxies also have grand-design spirals is less relevant as discussed later because the big dip is likely caused by clearance of molecular gas by the bar.

The galaxies in Table 1, especially those above the horizontal line, are candidates for
Milky Way

molecular
analogues. In particular, the radial profile of
\langle\mbox{$I_{\rm CO}$}\rangle
for NGC 4548 is nearly identical to that of the
Milky Way
. These may provide alternatives to analogues identified based on stellar properties. None of the galaxies in Table 1 appear in a list of 176 analogues based on morphology of spiral arms, bar, and small bulge

(
Fraser-McKelvie et al. 2019
)
.
To a large extent, the lack of overlap is caused by the northern hemisphere bias of the SDSS sample used by

Fraser-McKelvie et al. 2019

versus the southern hemisphere bias of PHANGS (and any ALMA survey). Only three of our 8 galaxies are in the SDSS survey and one of those lies outside the stellar mass limits imposed by

Fraser-McKelvie et al. 2019
.

The evidence for a flocculent galaxy extends beyond gas. Large astrometric surveys, like Gaia, have paved the way for detailed mapping of the stellar content of the Milky Way and current studies of a wide range of stellar tracers point towards a Milky Way that is flocculent rather than grand-design in structure

(

disfavor a grand-design Milky Way.

The Milky Way global properties agree with both sub-samples.

Despite being selected based only on their radial
\langle\mbox{$I_{\rm CO}$}\rangle
profiles, all those galaxies including the Milky Way are at
the higher stellar mass end of the PHANGS sample (Figure

In particular, they lie above the stellar mass of 1
{}\times 10^{10}

M
⊙
, identified as important for the morphology of the gas inside the bar

(
Verwilghen et al. 2025
)
.

Comparison to recent studies of the
Milky Way
in stars may also be of interest.
Recent analyses differ, but we will compare to the detailed analysis by

Imig et al. 2025
.
They fit the total stellar surface density to an exponential with scale-length,
\mbox{$r_{\rm d}$}=2.83\pm 0.2
kpc. This is somewhat longer than the molecular data, fit to 5-14 kpc, of
\mbox{$r_{\rm d}$}=2.26\pm 0.14
found by
15
.

Imig et al. 2025

also comment on a possible truncation around 13 kpc that they suggest may be linked to the gas density dropping below a critical threshold for star formation. However,
15
see no truncation beyond 13 kpc in either the molecular or star formation surface densities, within the limits imposed by stochastic variations.

While few PHANGS galaxies have data much beyond 2
R_{\rm e}
, the radial profiles in Figures
1
and
2
suggest that
\langle\mbox{$I_{\rm CO}$}\rangle
declines more slowly in those galaxies than in the
Milky Way
.
Several factors may be at work. Lower sensitivity to small clouds on the far side of the
Milky Way
could cause an underestimate of
\langle\mbox{$I_{\rm CO}$}\rangle
, but this would be a factor of two at most.

V
Caveats

The existence of the big dip in the
Milky Way
can be questioned on two grounds: firstly, clouds in that region may be incorrectly placed elsewhere by the kinematic distance method; secondly, beam dilution might cause underestimation of the mean CO intensity at the distance of the dip.

Concern about the misplacement of clouds in fact first motivated us to look at the PHANGS sample. If no other galaxies had such a dip, we would have been very suspicious about its occurence in the
Milky Way
. The fact that PHANGS galaxies with bars similar to the bar suggested for the
Milky Way
have dips is the best argument that the dip is real. However, it could still be less deep than shown in Figure
1
if many clouds are misplaced. In particular, CO gas flowing along the bar has been identified by

Marshall et al. 2008

using extinction and by

Sormani et al. 2019
using CO emission with distinctive locations in the
(l,v)
and
(l,b)
planes. We identified all clouds in those ranges and checked their
R_{\rm Gal}
. Most are in fact placed in the region of the dip, but a few are placed in the CMZ. Placing all of them in the dip region has only a modest effect on the dip. More extreme experiments of moving clouds with uncertain distances into the dip region can make the dip more shallow but do not eliminate it.

Distance biases in the catalog were discussed in Appendix C of
Miville-Deschênes et al. 2017
. Figure 24 of that paper shows the limits on cloud radius and mass as a function of distance. For a distance of 4-8 kpc (the front side of the bar region), the mass limit increases from 1
{}\times 10^{3}
to 5
{}\times 10^{3}

M
⊙
. Most clouds lie well above this limit, though there is a group that cluster along the limit. Because most of the mass is in more massive clouds, missing smaller clouds probably has only a small effect on the depth of the dip.

Improved information about the dip will come from better resolution CO surveys underway and improved models of the kinematics of the bar region.

VI
Summary

The key results are the following.

•

The
Milky Way
has a big dip in the radial profile of
\langle\mbox{$I_{\rm CO}$}\rangle
.

•

This dip is also seen in a subsample of PHANGS galaxies, all with a characteristic Stuber classification of YCLxxGnR, meaning clearly defined, long bars and grand-design spirals outside the bar.

•

This similarity suggests that an external observer would assign a Stuber class of YCLxx?nR to the
Milky Way
,
with the question mark reflecting the current state of uncertainty about the appearance of the galaxy outside the bar.

•

The galaxies with this classification provide a set of
Milky Way
analogues selected in a very different way from other analogues.

•

The bar length correlates well with the extent of the dip for these galaxies.

•

The Milky Way matches well in
M_{\star}
and SFR with those galaxies despite their selection being based only on the morphology.

•

All morphology-matched galaxies are at the higher stellar mass end of PHANGS sample.
