Submandibular infection in a healthy child caused by Legionella maceachernii
Authors: Xiaoyun Ye, Ji-An Li, Shifu Wang, Shengwei Luan, Jie Zheng, Shichun Lv, Guoqing Zheng, Wenwen Jiang, Xinrui Huang, Jingjing Xu, Lingdong Zhu, Beibei Niu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Extra-pulmonary infections caused by Legionella maceachernii are exceptionally rare, particularly in immunocompetent children. This case report describes a young child with a submandibular infection caused by L. maceachernii, who achieved complete recovery following combination therapy with azithromycin and sulfamethoxazole-trimethoprim (SMZ-TMP). By detailing the diagnostic and therapeutic course in this case, we aim to raise clinical awareness of such soft tissue infections in pediatric patients caused by atypical pathogens, thereby reducing the risk of misdiagnosis and optimizing patient outcomes.Keywords: Legionella maceachernii, Submandibular infection, Healthy child, Metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS)

Background

Legionella species are natural intracellular pathogens of eukaryotic cells in environmental settings and are known to cause respiratory illnesses in humans. Among the 65 identified Legionella species, approximately half of which are known to be pathogenic to humans, with most considered potential pathogens [1]. L. pneumophila accounts for approximately 90% of all Legionella infections, while L. maceachernii, L. micdadei, L. bozemanii, and L. longbeachae represent important pathogenic species among non-pneumophila Legionella [2].

L.maceachernii, a Gram-negative intracellular parasitic bacillus commonly found in natural aquatic environments. It was first isolated from a water reservoir by Brenner in 1985 and classified within the genus Legionella based on phenotypic similarities [3]. Later that year, it was isolated from a patient with L. pneumonia at the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) in Atlanta, Georgia, USA, providing the first evidence that L. maceachernii can cause human disease [3].

There have been very few reports of L. maceachernii infections in the literature. We searched PubMed with the keywords “Legionella maceachernii and infection” and identified eight previously reported cases (excluding our own), which are mostly manifested as community - acquired pneumonia or disseminated infection in individuals with compromised immune function (Table 1) [3–10].Among them, only two cases involved cutaneous infections, and one of them was an elderly woman with underlying health conditions [8] (the other being ours).To the best of our knowledge, there are no previous reports of L. maceachernii causing soft tissue infections in the submandibular region. By sharing the diagnostic and therapeutic experience of this case, we aim to provide valuable clinical insights into the management of similar rare infections, reduce the risk of misdiagnosis, and improve outcomes for affected children.

Case presentation

A 4-year-and-6-month-old girl was admitted to our hospital with a chief complaint of persistent swelling in the right submandibular region lasting for over two weeks. Approximately two weeks prior to admission, she developed unexplained swelling and tenderness in the right submandibular area, measuring approximately 5.0 × 4.0 × 2.0 cm (Fig. 1a), without accompanying ulceration, fever, respiratory distress, or trismus. She first presented to a referring hospital at this stage (designated as Day 1 of illness). Laboratory testing at the referring hospital during the early stage of illness revealed a white blood cell (WBC) count of 10.76 × 109/L, with 55.4% neutrophils and 35.1% lymphocytes; C-reactive protein (CRP) was elevated at 8.66 mg/L, and serum amyloid A (SAA) was markedly elevated at 118.5 mg/L. Ultrasonography of the right submandibular region reveals a hypoechoic nodule within the right submandibular gland, measuring approximately 1.8 × 1.5 cm. The nodule has an indistinct border and a plump shape. Multiple enlarged lymph nodes are detected around the right submandibular gland, with the largest one measuring approximately 2.1 × 0.9 cm. She was initially diagnosed with “right submandibular lymphadenitis” at a local hospital and received oral azithromycin for 3 days (Days 1–3), followed by cefaclor for 7 days (Days 4–10) as part of an empirical anti-infective regimen. Although the swelling significantly subsided after this 10-day regimen, a residual lesion measuring approximately 2.0 × 1.5 × 1.5 cm remained.

Four days prior to admission, the patient received intravenous ceftriaxone for 3 days, but with minimal clinical improvement (Days 11–13). Her body temperature remained within normal limits throughout the illness and treatment period.There was no recent history of pneumonia, trauma, animal bites, environmental exposure to contaminated water sources, or recent travel. The patient was previously healthy with no significant past medical history or known underlying conditions.

The patient was admitted to our hospital on Day 15 of illness. Upon physical examination, a firm, immobile lesion of approximately 2.0 × 1.5 × 1.5 cm was identified in the right submandibular region, with no significant tenderness, and the overlying skin appeared smooth without ulceration (Fig. 1b-c). Intraoral examination revealed no dental caries, purulent discharge, or mucosal abnormalities. Systemic examination showed no hepatomegaly, splenomegaly, or lymphadenopathy in other regions.

On admission, laboratory investigations revealed WBC count of 8.93 × 109/L, with 42.0% neutrophils and 50.8% lymphocytes. The CRP level was within normal range at 0.22 mg/L. Ultrasound of the right submandibular region demonstrated a mixed echogenic lesion with internal liquefied anechoic areas, measuring approximately 1.9 × 1.4 × 1.4 cm, located adjacent to the submandibular gland. The lesion had well-defined margins but an irregular shape (Fig. 1d).These findings were suggestive of submandibular lymphadenitis with partial necrosis. Color Doppler ultrasonography of the parotid glands, neck, adrenal glands, abdominal cavity (including the liver, gallbladder, spleen, and pancreas), and retroperitoneum revealed no significant abnormalities.

Given the initial positive response to antibiotics, bacterial-induced inflammation remained a likely etiology. Atypical pathogens such as Mycobacterium tuberculosis were not initially suspected. Empirical treatment was initiated with intravenous cefazolin at 0.5 g every 12 h (Days 15–21). However, after 7 days of administration, there was no significant clinical improvement, and physical examination revealed no reduction in the size of the right submandibular lesion.Repeat ultrasonography revealed a dumbbell-shaped hypoechoic lesion (Fig. 1e) suggestive of coagulative necrosis, raising suspicion of atypical pathogens.

Upon further review of the patient’s history, the caregiver reported noticeable symptom improvement after 3 days of azithromycin therapy at the onset of illness. Therefore, intravenous azithromycin was reinitiated at a dose of 10 mg/kg once daily while awaiting results from the above investigations (Days 22–26). After 5 days of azithromycin administration, the lesion softened on palpation.

Immunological testing revealed the following absolute lymphocyte subset counts: natural killer (NK) cells, 195 cells/µL (4.86%); B lymphocytes, 1068 cells/µL (26.59%); and suppressor/cytotoxic T lymphocytes, 1185 cells/µL. Epstein-Barr virus (EBV) serology showed positive IgG for both viral capsid antigen (VCA) and nuclear antigen (EBV-NA), consistent with past EBV exposure and no evidence of active infection. Other laboratory parameters—including procalcitonin (PCT), erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR), interleukins (IL)-2, IL-4, IL-6, IL-10, tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), interferon-gamma (IFN-γ), and immunoglobulin levels (IgG, IgA, IgM)—were all within normal limits. Blood cultures, purified protein derivative (PPD) skin test, T-cell-based assay for tuberculosis infection (T-SPOT.TB), qualitative DNA testing, IgM antibody detection, and IgG antibody testing for Bartonella henselae were all negative. Despite an extensive diagnostic workup, the underlying pathogen remained unidentified.

A third ultrasound re-evaluation revealed superficial liquefaction of the previously noted dumbbell-shaped hypoechoic lesion (Day 27). To further clarify the etiology, ultrasound-guided aspiration of purulent fluid and core needle biopsy of the lesion were performed. Approximately 2 mL of yellowish-white purulent fluid was obtained and submitted for metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS) and microbiological culture. The biopsy specimen was sent for histopathological examination. Intravenous erythromycin was reinitiated at a dose of 10 mg/kg every 12 h while awaiting further results from days 28–30. Histopathology revealed inflammatory changes.

Following collection, pus samples were immediately processed for DNA extraction using the QIAamp UCP Pathogen DNA Kit (Qiagen) in strict adherence to the manufacturer’s instructions to preserve nucleic acid integrity for mNGS detection. Human DNA was depleted using Benzonase (Qiagen) and Tween 20 (Sigma). Total RNA was extracted with a QIAamp Viral RNA Kit (Qiagen) and ribosomal RNA was removed with a Ribo-Zero rRNA Removal Kit (Illumina). cDNA was generated using reverse transcriptase and dNTPs (Thermo Fisher). DNA and cDNA libraries were prepared with the NextEra XT DNA Library Prep Kit (Illumina, San Diego, CA). The resulting libraries were purified, and fragments were selected using magnetic beads. Quality assessment was performed with the Qubit dsDNA HS Assay Kit and the Agilent High Sensitivity DNA kit on an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer. Sequencing was carried out on an Illumina NextSeq CN500 platform with 75-cycle single-end reads, generating approximately 20 million reads per library. After obtaining the sequencing data, high-quality data were generated after filtering out adapter, low-quality, low-complexity, and shorter reads. Next, human reads were removed by mapping reads to the human reference genome using SNAP software. The remaining data were aligned to the microbial genome database using Burrows–Wheeler alignment. The database collected microbial genomes from NCBI. It contains more than 20,000 microorganisms, including 9945 bacteria, 6761 viruses, 1151 fungi, and 305 parasites.Analysis of the resultant microbial composition revealed L. maceachernii as the causative pathogen (Day 30). The pus culture used buffered charcoal yeast extract (BCYE) medium. Unfortunately, due to the low bacterial count, the culture result was negative. However, under the microscope, we did find a small number of Gram-negative bacterial cells (Fig. 1f).

Based on the Infectious Diseases Society of America (IDSA) guidelines [11] and previous clinical experience, the antibiotic regimen was adjusted to include azithromycin at 10 mg/kg/day, administered once daily for 3 days followed by a 3-day drug-free interval, in combination with SMZ-TMP at 20 mg/kg per dose, administered twice daily as continuous therapy. After one month of combined antibiotic treatment, the patient achieved complete resolution of symptoms. Antibiotics were discontinued thereafter, and no recurrence was observed during a three-month follow-up period. The timeline of disease progression and treatment is shown in Fig. 2.

Discussion

Submandibular enlargement is a common clinical presentation in pediatrics. Its evaluation poses a diagnostic challenge, as it may stem from lymph node enlargement, neoplasms, or inflammation of local anatomical structures. Infectious etiologies are the most prevalent, typically involving mixed pathogens derived from the resident flora of the oral cavity, skin, or upper respiratory tract. Common isolates include Streptococcus pyogenes, Staphylococcus aureus, the Streptococcus milleri group, Actinomyces, and various aerobic and anaerobic oral bacteria [12]. Consistent with this, a retrospective analysis by Huang et al. confirmed S. aureus as the predominant pathogen in submandibular infections [13].

Typical clinical manifestations consist of swelling or tenderness in the submandibular region, often with overlying skin changes; some patients may also present with fever. In the present case, the child exhibited persistent right submandibular swelling for two weeks. Given the mildly elevated white blood cell count at onset, increased inflammatory markers (CRP and SAA), and supportive ultrasonographic findings, bacterial submandibular lymphadenitis was initially suspected, prompting empirical treatment with intravenous cefazolin. However, after one week, no significant clinical improvement was observed. The absence of fever, lack of an identifiable contact history, only moderate elevation of inflammatory markers, and the poor response to antibiotics—coupled with imaging features of necrosis and liquefaction—collectively raised the possibility of an infection caused by atypical pathogens.

The differential diagnosis for a right submandibular infection should encompass a broad spectrum of potential etiologies. Among the most common are infections originating from oral or pharyngeal sources, such as dental caries, periodontitis, tonsillitis, or parotitis. Such conditions typically present with localized pain that may radiate to the maxillofacial region, often described as throbbing or persistent. In severe cases, patients may develop trismus or localized fluctuance. However, in the present case, the child’s initial symptoms and physical examination did not support these diagnoses. Furthermore, odontogenic infections are predominantly caused by oral commensal flora, frequently involving mixed Streptococcus species and anaerobic bacteria [14]. Auxiliary testing in such cases often reveals significant elevations in inflammatory markers, particularly neutrophils, and the infections generally respond well to β-lactam antibiotics. Given the child’s clinical presentation, examination and imaging findings, and the lack of response to cephalosporin therapy, a dental source of infection was confidently excluded.

The differential diagnosis further encompassed atypical infections, such as those caused by Mycobacterium tuberculosis and Bartonella henselae. Tuberculous lymphadenitis commonly presents as a localized, painless mass with a string-like configuration and may be associated with constitutional symptoms like low-grade fever; its confirmation relies on tests such as ESR, PPD, T-SPOT, and histopathology. In the present case, all relevant investigations yielded negative results, providing no support for tuberculosis. Cat-scratch disease, which typically follows a cat scratch or bite, presents with localized lymphadenopathy and is often self-limiting [15]. Our patient denied any animal contact, and the diagnosis was further excluded by the negative serological (IgM, IgG) and molecular (DNA) testing for Bartonella henselae.

Viral infections, particularly EBV, were considered in the differential diagnosis. However, the serological profile was inconsistent with an active infection. As noted in the case description, the positive VCA-IgG and EBV-NA IgG indicated prior exposure, while the absence of VCA-IgM and EBV DNA effectively ruled out a primary EBV-related cause for the submandibular pathology.

A variety of benign and malignant tumors must be considered in children with persistent submandibular swelling. Sialolithiasis, for instance, is typically characterized by glandular swelling, erythema, induration, and pain exacerbated by eating [16]. Although some of the child’s symptoms partially overlapped with this presentation, oral examination revealed no significant findings, and ultrasonography did not support a diagnosis of sialolithiasis.

Regarding malignancy, the immunologic profile showed only a mild decrease in NK cells and a mild increase in B cells, with total T-cell counts and the CD4/CD8 ratio remaining normal. Moreover, ultrasonographic examinations of the adrenal glands, retroperitoneum, liver, spleen, and mediastinum revealed no abnormalities, making a neoplastic process unlikely. This was further confirmed by a subsequent pathological biopsy, which found no evidence of malignancy.

Autoimmune diseases, such as Sjögren’s syndrome (SS) and sarcoidosis, may also present with systemic inflammation and lymphadenopathy. However, in this case, no other system-related clinical features apart from lymph node enlargement were observed, and the pathological examination results also provided no evidence suggesting an autoimmune disease.

The definitive diagnosis in this case was achieved through mNGS of the pus sample, which identified L. maceachernii as the primary pathogen. This technology allows for an unbiased, comprehensive detection of bacteria, viruses, fungi, and parasites without prior knowledge of the infectious agent. Key advantages of mNGS include its capability to identify rare, novel, or unculturable pathogens, providing a more comprehensive view of microbial communities compared to traditional culture-based methods [17]. Previous studies have confirmed the role of mNGS in diagnosing respiratory tract infections [18], hematological malignancies infections [19], central nervous system infections [20] and others. In this case, this technology played a crucial role, and its outcome directly guided the targeted antibiotic treatment plan, thereby leading to a successful clinical outcome.

Although culture-based methods are still regarded as the gold standard for diagnosing Legionella infections, their use in clinical practice has declined due to their time-consuming nature and relatively low positive rate. L. maceachernii, for instance, cannot grow on conventional blood agar or chocolate agar plates but can be cultured aerobically in BCYE medium. However, the growth of L. maceachernii is notably slow, often requiring several days to weeks to form visible colonies under optimal conditions of temperature (35–37℃) and humidity. Direct immunofluorescence assay (DFA) represents another method for identifying this pathogen. In this technique, clinical specimens such as respiratory secretions or pus are prepared as smears and stained with fluorescent-labeled antibodies specific to L. maceachernii antigens. Under a fluorescence microscope, specific fluorescence signals will be observed if L. maceachernii is present. Nevertheless, DFA may exhibit cross-reactivity with other Legionella species, limiting its specificity. Additionally, polymerase chain reaction (PCR) can detect specific gene fragments of L. maceachernii, including the 16 S rRNA gene and the mip gene. Some researchers also utilize the rpoB gene for the identification of non-Legionella pneumophila species [21]; however, its application in the diagnosis and differentiation of L. maceachernii remains under investigation.The advent of mNGS technology has revolutionized the diagnostic landscape for Legionella species, providing critical diagnostic clues for rare pathogens such as L. maceachernii [22].

Apart from L. maceachernii, other species of Legionella that cause cutaneous infections are also extremely rare. A retrospective literature published by Frostadottir et al. [2] summarized previous studies on cutaneous infections caused by Legionella, with the main bacteria including L. pneumophila, L. longbeachae, L. micdadei, L. bozemanii, and L. maceachernii, the affected areas included the neck, chest, perianal region, and limbs. Interestingly, the infection rate among females was as high as 78%. However, due to the limited sample size, the clinical spectrum and epidemiological distribution of cutaneous infections caused by Legionella still need to be further refined.

Macrolides and fluoroquinolones are currently considered the first-line therapeutic agents for Legionella infections [23]. They exhibit superior penetration into macrophages, achieving effective intracellular drug concentrations at lower minimum inhibitory concentrations. A meta-analysis by Jasper et al. did not reveal any significant differences in mortality or clinical cure rates between the two treatment groups [24]. Alternative therapeutic options include doxycycline, SMZ-TMP, and quinolones combined with rifampicin. Previous studies on pulmonary infections caused by Legionella have demonstrated sensitivity to macrolides; however, in vitro drug susceptibility data for this bacterium remain limited. The use of fluoroquinolones is generally contraindicated in children under 18 years of age or in individuals without skeletal maturity, as these agents may induce tendinopathy or tendon rupture, characterized by abrupt-onset sharp pain at rest or during movement .Furthermore, doxycycline poses a risk of permanent tooth discoloration in pediatric populations due to its binding with calcium ions in developing teeth, forming an insoluble tetracycline-calcium orthophosphate complex [25]. Consequently, both classes of antibiotics are typically avoided in children.

In this case, the child exhibited high susceptibility to azithromycin. Following initial anti-infection treatment at another hospital, marked reduction in submental swelling was observed after oral administration of azithromycin at a dose of 10 mg/kg/day for three consecutive days. However, subsequent treatment with β-lactam antibiotics failed to produce significant improvement, confirming the necessity of targeted therapy. Azithromycin rapidly accumulates at high concentrations within phagocytes and re-polarizes heterogeneous macrophage subpopulations toward the activated M2 phenotype, thereby enhancing the host’s immune response to infection. Due to its potent immunomodulatory effects and relatively low incidence of adverse effects, it is widely utilized in pediatric anti-infection regimens [26]. However, a prolonged medical history and repeated exposure to medication may increase the risk of drug resistance during azithromycin monotherapy. Consequently, combination therapy with SMZ-TMP was initiated. Although SMZ-TMP is considered a second-line agent for Legionella infections, it demonstrates efficacy against multiple pathogens through synergistic inhibition of folic acid metabolism. In this case, the combination of azithromycin and SMZ-TMP resulted in favorable therapeutic outcomes without significant adverse reactions. Upon re-evaluation after one month of treatment, liver and kidney functions remained within normal limits.

The incidence of Legionella infections has been progressively increasing on a global scale in recent years [27]; however, our understanding and management of these infections remain inadequate. Owing to the low diagnostic rate and underreporting of L. maceachernii cases, there is currently insufficient data regarding its transmission routes, serological typing, drug susceptibility testing, therapeutic approaches, and prognosis. Notably, despite previous reports indicating a high mortality rate associated with L. maceachernii infections, this case demonstrates a relatively mild clinical presentation that deviates from prior observations. The patient, a child, exhibited localized swelling of the submandibular area without evidence of systemic involvement or pathological manifestations in other organ systems. This discrepancy may be explained by the opportunistic nature of L. maceachernii, as most severe cases have been documented in immunocompromised hosts or individuals with significant comorbidities.

Conclusion

This report describes the first documented case of a submandibular L. maceachernii infection in a child. The diagnosis was ultimately established by mNGS after the infection proved refractory to β-lactam antibiotics, leading to a full recovery following combination therapy with azithromycin and SMZ-TMP. This case underscores the critical importance of considering atypical pathogens in pediatric submandibular infections, especially those that do not respond to conventional therapy, and highlights the pivotal role of mNGS in pathogen identification. Furthermore, it provides clinical evidence supporting the efficacy of macrolide-based regimens against L. maceachernii, offering valuable insights for future diagnosis and management.
