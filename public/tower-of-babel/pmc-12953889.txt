Volvariella volvacea polysaccharides alleviate acute liver injury by modulating Nrf2/NF-κB pathway in a dual manner
Authors: Xiaomin Li, Fengpei Zhang, Yun Li, Jiarong Cai, Linna Cai, Qianying Ren, Anyan Fan, Baisheng Xu, Junsheng Fu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Volvariella volvacea is an edible and medicinal fungus; however, its hepatoprotective effects have not been reported. In this study, we evaluated the hepatoprotective effects of V. volvacea polysaccharides (SMP) by constructing a cellular model of H2O2-induced oxidative damage and an animal model of tetrachloromethane (CCl4)-induced acute liver injury in mice. Moreover, we used ML385, a specific Nrf2 blocker, to explore how SMP might regulate the Nrf2 signaling pathway. SMP is a primarily glucose-based polysaccharide with antioxidant capacities. In vitro analyses revealed that the administration of SMP significantly activated Nrf2, upregulated the expression of HO-1 and NQO1, enhanced SOD and CAT enzyme activities, and attenuated reactive oxygen species in H₂O₂-injured hepatocytes. In vivo, SMP reduced the serum levels of ALT, TNF-α, IL-6, and MDA while increasing the activity of hepatic SOD and ameliorating CCl₄-induced histopathology. Our findings revealed that SMP acts via a dual mechanism involving Nrf2-mediated antioxidant pathway activation coupled with NF-κB inflammatory signaling suppression. Collectively, our findings provide a pharmacological foundation for the therapeutic application of SMP in hepatoprotection.

Introduction

Volvariella volvacea, commonly referred to as the straw mushroom, paddy straw mushroom, or Chinese mushroom, is a prominent edible and medicinal fungus belonging to the phylum Basidiomycota, order Agaricales, family Pluteaceae, and genus Volvariella1. Renowned for its rich composition of bioactive constituents, including proteins, amino acids, polysaccharides, and vitamins, this fungus possesses significant pharmacological properties, including antioxidant, immunomodulatory, and antitumor activities. These attributes highlight the dual role of this fungus as a nutrient-dense food source and a potent functional ingredient with health-promoting benefits2,3. Notably, Yang4 demonstrated that crude polysaccharides derived from V. volvacea markedly enhanced the proliferation and phagocytic activity of lymphocytes in normal murine models, thereby highlighting their immunomodulatory efficacy. Nevertheless, the hepatoprotective potential of V. volvacea polysaccharides remains to be fully investigated. We hypothesized that SMP may have antioxidant activity and hepatoprotective effects.

Under physiological conditions, aerobic metabolism in cells generates free radicals, including-OH, O2−, and H2O2, collectively termed ROS. An organism maintains homeostasis in free radical metabolism through an intricate antioxidant defense system5. However, the excessive accumulation of ROS disrupts this equilibrium, precipitating oxidative stress and subsequent cellular damage6. Chen et al.7 explored the protective effect and mechanism of evodiamine on hepatocyte injury by establishing a model of LO2 hepatocyte injury induced by hydrogen peroxide H2O2. In another study, Liu et al.6 employed an analogous model and demonstrated that the polysaccharides derived from the waste substrate of Cordyceps militaris post-harvest exhibited significant cytoprotective efficacy against oxidative stress-induced hepatic injury. Despite these advancements, nothing is known of the potential role of SMP in protecting against liver injury via antioxidant activity in vitro. To address this gap, the present study employed an H2O2-induced model of oxidative damage to systematically evaluate the capacity of SMP to modulate cellular oxidative stress and confer hepatoprotection.

As an important organ that regulates human metabolism and detoxification, the health of the liver is highly susceptible to damage caused by external factors, such as drugs, alcohol, and the environment. These factors can influence the regenerative potential of the liver, leading to cirrhosis and, in severe cases, damage may evolve into liver cancer, posing a significant threat to human health8,9. Liver disease has become one of the most common diseases that endangers human health today, and the main cause of death and morbidity worldwide is cirrhosis, accounting for 2.4% of global deaths10,11. The search for natural drugs that effectively prevent and treat liver damage has become a hot topic of research. ALI, a rapid-onset pathological state precipitated by diverse etiologies, is characterized by extensive hepatocyte necrosis or profound liver dysfunction, alongside the overproduction of ROS and lipid peroxidation byproducts such as malondialdehyde12. The CCl4-induced murine model of ALI is extensively employed in research owing to its close resemblance to human acute chemical-induced hepatic damage, rendering it an indispensable tool for exploring novel therapeutic interventions13.

The Nrf2 signaling pathway serves as a central pathway in modulating oxidative stress and inflammatory processes, offering therapeutic potential for related pathologies14. The activation of this pathway constitutes a fundamental cellular adaptive mechanism that orchestrates antioxidant defense systems to counteract oxidative insults14. Conversely, NF-κB operates as a master transcriptional orchestrator, modulating inflammatory mediators (IL-6, TNF-α), cell cycle regulators (Cyclin D1, p53), and immune response elements15. Notably, the overexpression of Nrf2 exerts inhibitory effects on NF-κB expression, thereby attenuating inflammatory responses through this regulatory crosstalk16. Current literature lacks a comprehensive elucidation of the dynamics of crosstalk in the Nrf2/NF-κB axis during SMP-mediated hepatoprotection in murine models of ALI. Based on the existing literature studies, we hypothesized that SMP may be able to counteract oxidative stress and protect the liver in mice by modulating the Nrf2 and NF-κB signaling pathways.

In this study, we first prepared polysaccharides from Volvariella volvacea (SMP) and evaluated antioxidant properties by radical scavenging assays targeting DPPH, hydroxyl, and ABTS radicals. A model of H2O2-induced oxidative stress was subsequently established in LO2 hepatocytes to evaluate the cytoprotective effects of SMP against oxidative damage in vitro. To investigate the regulatory influence of SMP on the Nrf2 signaling pathway, we utilized ML385, a specific Nrf2 inhibitor, to suppress pathway activity. Furthermore, a murine model of ALI was generated via CCl4 administration to validate the hepatoprotective efficacy of SMP in vivo. Mechanistic insights into the amelioration of SMP-mediated liver injury were elucidated by systematic analysis of the modulatory effects of SMP on both the Nrf2 and NF-κB signaling pathways. Collectively, our findings establish a theoretical framework for the pharmacological role of SMP as a hepatoprotective agent and highlight its translational potential for the management of ALI.

SMP exhibited good antioxidant activity

To evaluate the antioxidant capacity of SMP, we performed quantitative analyses to evaluate the ability of SMP to scavenge –OH, DPPH, and ABTS radicals. As shown in Fig. 1d–f, SMP exhibited concentration-dependent OH radical scavenging activity, with scavenging efficiency progressively increasing across the tested concentration range. The calculated EC50 value was 2.744 mg/mL based on the dose-response curve. Figure 1d shows that the scavenging activity of SMP on DPPH free radicals was concentration-dependent, with an EC50 value of 0.218 mg/mL, and had good DPPH free radical scavenging ability. Figure 1e depicts the concentration-dependent correlation between SMP dosage and scavenging efficacy. Within a specific range (0.025–0.5 mg/mL), radical elimination efficiency demonstrated proportional elevation with increasing SMP levels, eventually plateauing beyond a specific threshold concentration. Quantitative analysis further indicated that ABTS exhibited an EC50 value of 0.027 mg/mL. Notably, at a polysaccharide concentration of 1 mg/mL, the achieved scavenging rate (95.34% ± 0.75%) approximated that of the VC positive control (97.51% ± 2.17%), demonstrating the potent ABTS radical scavenging capacity of SMP.

After treating LO2 cells with different concentrations of H2O2 for 2 h, we found that the relative cell survival rate decreased sequentially with increasing H2O2 concentration. As shown in Fig. 2b, when the H2O2 concentration reached 1.5 mmol /mL, the cell survival rate was close to 50%, which had reached the LDL. Therefore, we used 1.5 mmol/mL as the experimental concentration of H2O2 to induce oxidative damage in LO2 cells to construct an experimental model.

Following the exposure of LO2 cells to 1.5 mmol/mL H2O2, cellular viability exhibited a concentration-dependent increase across experimental groups with increasing concentrations of polysaccharide (Fig. 2c). Relative to the control group, both the model group and the low-concentration intervention group (0.05–0.5 mg/mL) demonstrated marked statistical significance (P < 0.001). Relative to the model group, when the SMP concentration was between 0.025 and 5 mg/mL, the cell survival rate was significantly increased (P < 0.01 or P < 0.001). A 5 mg/mL concentration of SMP exhibited the highest cell survival rate of 94.0% ± 6.94%, thus indicating that SMP alleviated the oxidative damage caused by H2O2 at this pretreatment concentration. Consequently, this concentration was chosen to further investigate the ability of SMP to scavenge intracellular ROS.

Quantitative analysis (Fig. 2d) revealed an 88% elevation in ROS accumulation for the model group compared to the control group (P < 0.001). Compared with the model group, experiments were set up with three doses of SMP (0.05, 0.5, and 5 mg/mL) added to the cell culture solution, and the resultant ROS levels were all significantly reduced and linearly correlated with SMP concentration (P < 0.001). When SMP was added at a concentration of 5 mg/mL, the intracellular ROS level was lower than that of the control group, suggesting that SMP exerted good antioxidant efficacy and the ability to scavenge ROS.

SMP improved the activity of antioxidant enzymes in LO2 cells

Figure 2e depicts a significant reduction (P < 0.001) of SOD enzyme activity following H2O2-induced oxidative challenge (P < 0.01). Notably, under the intervention of SMP (0.05–5 mg/mL), the SOD enzyme activity in cells achieved dose-responsive restoration, demonstrating 1.43-, 2.02-, and 2.55-fold enhancement relative to the oxidative damage model group (P < 0.05 or P < 0.001). Analysis suggested that SMP could effectively scavenge intracellular superoxide anion radicals and ameliorate cell damage.

Figure 2f shows that CAT enzyme activity in the damage model group was significantly inhibited when compared to the control group (P < 0.001), and that the intracellular CAT enzyme activity in cells from the SMP groups was enhanced after pretreatment with three different SMP concentrations (1.62-fold, 1.83-fold and 2.17-fold of that of the model group, respectively, after pretreatment with three different SMP concentrations). Thus, SMP effectively increased the CAT enzyme activity of damaged cells and reduced the intracellular accumulation of ROS due to H2O2, thus ameliorating oxidative stress injury.

SMP activated the Nrf2 signaling pathway to alleviate oxidative damage in LO2 cells

Our previous results revealed that SMP reduced ROS levels and increased the activities of SODase and CATase, which in turn alleviated the oxidative damage in LO2 cells caused by H2O2. Nrf2 serves as a central regulatory mediator governing both intrinsic and extrinsic redox homeostasis mechanisms18. To further explore whether the up-regulation of antioxidant enzyme system activities due to SMP is induced through the activation of the upstream regulator Nrf2, we next investigated whether the regulatory ability of SMP on the Nrf2 signaling pathway by SMP could be influenced by the targeted inhibition of Nrf2 by fluorescence quantitative PCR. Analysis revealed that the expression levels of Nrf2 were down-regulated in the ML385 group when compared with those in the control group, indicating the efficacy of the Nrf2 inhibitor ML385 to influence the expression levels of Keap-1 and several downstream transcription factors (Nrf2, HO-1, CuZn-SOD, GCLC, and NQO1). Figure 3 shows that SMP significantly upregulated the expression levels of Nrf2 following the targeted inhibition of Nrf2. Compared with the ML385 group, after the targeted inhibition of Nrf2, SMP significantly upregulated the expression levels of HO-1, CuZn-SOD, GCLC, and NQO1, and significantly down-regulated the expression level of Keap-1, although there was no significant difference when compared to that in the control group. Effect of SMP on serum inflammatory factors in mice with ALI

ALI results in the secretion of several inflammatory factors. To further investigate the effect of SMP on the serum levels of inflammatory factors in mice, we used ELISA kits to detect the serum levels of inflammatory factors in each group of mice. Analysis showed that compared with the control group, the serum levels of inflammatory factors (TNF-α and IL-6) in the model group of mice was significantly higher (P < 0.05); compared with the model group, the serum levels of inflammatory factors (TNF-α and IL-6) in the SMP group and the positive control group of mice were significantly lower (P < 0.05) (Fig. 4g and h). These results indicated that SMP could alleviate the inflammatory state of cells and had the effect of alleviating ALI in mice.

Effect of SMP on liver histopathology in mice with ALI

Next, H&E staining was used to investigate liver pathology (Fig. 4i). H&E staining showed that the liver tissues of mice in the normal control had a normal structure, the hepatic cords were aligned, the hepatocytes were of uniform size, and there was no inflammatory cell infiltration or necrotic cells. In contrast, liver tissues from mice in the model group were obviously damaged; hepatocytes were of uneven size, a small amount of inflammatory cell infiltration was evident, there was evidence of hepatocyte steatosis, and there was an increase in the size of hepatocytes. Compared with the model group, the degree of inflammatory cell infiltration and hepatocyte necrosis in the liver tissues of mice in the SMP group was reduced, the hepatic cords were neatly arranged, and the hepatocytes were of uniform size. The structure of liver tissue in mice from the positive administration group was normal, with the presence of vacuoles in a small number of hepatocytes and a small amount of inflammatory cell infiltration. Collectively, these results showed that SMP had a protective effect on the morphological injury of hepatocytes caused by CCl4 and could reduce the state of inflammatory injury in the liver.

Discussion

Edible mushroom polysaccharides exhibit significant biological activities, including antioxidant, hepatoprotective, antitumor, and immunomodulatory effects. Polysaccharides are one of the main active components in V. volvacea and exert key biological activities, including antioxidant and immunomodulatory properties. Many studies have shown that polysaccharides can exert hepatoprotective activity. Polysaccharides from different sources have different functions and mechanisms of action due to their different structures. For instance, Yang et al.20 found that Ganoderma lucidum polysaccharides were mainly composed of glucose, galacturonic acid, arabinose, and other monosaccharides, and that 300 mg/kg/day of Ganoderma lucidum polysaccharides could reduce ALI caused by CCl4 in mice by regulating the ERK/MAPK signaling pathway. In another study, Fan et al.21 used CCl4 to construct a mouse model of ALI and found that a dose of 1.0 g/kg Cordyceps polysaccharide boiling tablets effectively improved liver injury. Shiitake polysaccharide (β-1,3-glucan) has been shown to exert a good antitumor effect and can alleviate the ALI induced by CCl4, at least to a certain extent22. Polygonatum sibiricum polysaccharide has been shown to activate autophagy by regulating the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway and reduce CCl4-induced ALI in rats23. However, the hepatoprotective effect and mechanism of SMP have yet to be fully investigated.

Free radicals compromise cellular defense systems by impairing enzymatic functions, subsequently accelerating aging processes and inflammatory cascades24. SOD and CAT, crucial endogenous antioxidant enzymes, maintain redox homeostasis by scavenging superoxide anions and hydroxyl radicals, serving as reliable biomarkers for the assessment of oxidative stress25. In a previous study, Liu et al.26 revealed the radical scavenging capacities of the extracellular and intracellular polysaccharides of Cordyceps militaris mycelium against ABTS, DPPH, and hydroxyl radicals. Our current findings demonstrated that SMP exhibits enhanced scavenging efficacy for these radicals, with EC50 values of 0.027, 0.218, and 2.744 mg/mL, respectively, thus indicating superior chemical antioxidant activity when compared to Cordyceps militaris mycelium polysaccharides. The biological activity of polysaccharides is known to be affected by various factors, including monosaccharide composition, molecular weight, and glycosidic bond type. Li et al.27 confirmed that the monosaccharide composition of Polygonatum sibiricum polysaccharides is closely related to its antioxidant activity, and that the scavenging rate of ABTS free radicals was directly proportional to the molar ratio of glucose. The high molecular weight of polysaccharides will affect their solubility and solution viscosity, which are not conducive to the absorption of polysaccharides in the body. The molecular weight of polysaccharides is very low, and there are too few active groups on polysaccharides to form an effective active spatial structure, thus resulting in the loss of biological polysaccharide activity28. Therefore, the appropriate molecular weight can form an effective active structure, which in turn confers biological activity. The molecular weight of SMP Peak 2 is 11.93 kDa, which is moderate and may be one of the factors contributing to the antioxidant and liver-protecting activities of SMP. SMP contains a high glucose content, thus providing good antioxidant activity. SMP polysaccharides are mainly composed of glucose, galactose, mannose, glucose, glucuronic acid, and galacturonic acid; it is possible that these may also represent the monosaccharide components of SMP that exert antioxidant and hepatoprotective effects. Our findings indicate that SMP plays a hepatoprotective role by activating the Nrf2 pathway and by inhibiting the NF-κB signaling pathway.

Because the hepatoprotective mechanisms of SMP have not been investigated previously, we established an H2O2-induced oxidative stress model in LO2 hepatocytes to evaluate the cytoprotective potential of SMP. Cytotoxicity assessment confirmed the safety profile of SMP, showing no significant reduction in viability in LO2 cells at concentrations of 0.05–5 mg/mL. H2O2 significantly reduced the viability of LO2 cells and affected the growth of LO2 cells. However, SMP intervention elevated cell viability, accompanied by markedly increased activities of SOD and CAT, along with a significant reduction in ROS levels. Collectively, these findings indicated that SMP alleviated H2O2-induced oxidative damage in LO2 cells.

The Nrf2 signaling pathway is one of the core pathways responsible for the cellular antioxidant response, which can induce endogenous antioxidant response in vivo29. As a pivotal nuclear transcription factor, Nrf2 activates downstream cytoprotective genes to mitigate oxidative stress and inflammation. Under physiological conditions, Nrf2 remains bound to Keap1, maintaining redox homeostasis by regulating antioxidant enzyme expression30. During oxidative challenge, Nrf2 dissociates from Keap-1, and Nrf2 is translocated to the nucleus and binds to the ARE, which in turn regulates the activation of transcription for certain antioxidant protective genes, such as HO-1, SOD, and GSH, and promotes their protein expression31,32. HO-1 is the rate-limiting enzyme for heme degradation, which catalyzes the degradation of heme groups to produce free iron, carbon monoxide, and biliverdin to inhibit oxidative stress and inflammatory responses33. In order to explore the role of the Nrf2 signaling pathway in the mechanism by which SMP can resist oxidative damage in hepatocytes, we first pretreated LO2 cells with a specific Nrf2 inhibitor (ML385). qPCR analysis showed that ML385 influenced the expression level of Keap-1 and key Nrf2 downstream transcription factors (HO-1, CuZn-SOD, GCLC, and NQO1). We found that SMP can activate the Nrf2 signaling pathway, and that following the targeted inhibition of Nrf2, SMP could still activate the Nrf2 signaling pathway and effectively modulate antioxidant gene expression and attenuate oxidative damage in hepatocytes.

ALI represents an acute hepatic disorder characterized by extensive hepatocyte necrosis or severe functional impairment, typically induced by a diverse range of etiological factors8,12. The pathogenesis of ALI involves multifactorial interactions, including oxidative stress, inflammatory cascades, cellular fibrosis, and apoptosis. Hepatotoxic agents, including drugs, ethanol metabolites, and viral pathogens, disrupt hepatic redox homeostasis, thus creating a pro-oxidative microenvironment that triggers excessive ROS generation. A large number of free radicals cause the oxidation of biological macromolecules such as proteins, nucleic acids, and lipids to varying degrees, leading to protein deterioration, DNA breakage, and lipid peroxidation, thereby increasing the level of lipid peroxidation products (MDA)8,12.

To validate the hepatoprotective efficacy of SMP in vivo, we next utilized a CCl₄-induced model of ALI. Serum ALT and AST are important indicators for evaluating liver function injury, and when liver injury occurs, a large amount of ALT and AST is released into the blood, thus resulting in elevated serum ALT and AST levels34. When compared with the control group, the serum levels of ALT and AST were significantly higher in the model group. Serum ALT and AST levels were significantly reduced after SMP treatment (P < 0.001), indicating that SMP alleviated CCl4-induced ALI, at least to a certain extent. SOD, a pivotal endogenous antioxidant enzyme, scavenges ROS to maintain hepatic redox equilibrium, serving as a critical clinical indicator for liver functional assessment35. Conversely, MDA, the terminal metabolite of lipid peroxidation, reflects the severity of oxidative stress in a quantitative manner. MDA is the final metabolite of lipid peroxidation, and the level of MDA reflects the degree of oxidative stress induced by CCl435. TNF-α and IL-6 are important inflammatory mediators associated with liver injury, which not only exacerbate the degeneration and necrosis of hepatocytes, but also promote the release of inflammatory factors, thus exacerbating the inflammatory response24,35. In this study, we demonstrated that the levels of MDA, TNF-α, and IL-6 were significantly elevated, and the level of SOD was significantly reduced, in liver tissues following CCl4-induced liver injury in mice. Compared with the model group, the levels of MDA, TNF-α, and IL-6 were significantly down-regulated, while SOD enzyme activity was significantly elevated, in liver tissues after treatment with SMP, thus suggesting that SMP can alleviate the inflammatory response of liver tissues, improve injury to the liver tissue, and exert a certain protective effect on CCl4-induced liver injury in mice. In a previous study, Li et al.36 found that an extract of Coreopsis tinctoria Nutt. increased the activity of SOD enzyme and reduced the levels of MDA, TNF-α, and IL-6, which in turn alleviated CCl4-induced ALI; these findings were similar to those produced in the present study. Our experimental data demonstrated that SMP ameliorated CCl₄-induced ALI by enhancing systemic antioxidant defenses and by attenuating lipid peroxidation-mediated hepatic inflammation. To delineate the molecular mechanisms underlying the hepatoprotective effects of SMP, we performed immunohistochemical analysis and western blotting to quantify key mediators of the Nrf2 and NF-κB pathways in murine hepatic tissues. Compared with the model group, SMP treatment significantly upregulated nuclear Nrf2 translocation and HO-1 expression (P < 0.01), while concurrently suppressing NF-κB p65 phosphorylation, TLR4 activation, and IL-6 production (P < 0.05). These findings confirm the dual regulatory capacity of SMP to activate the Nrf2-mediated antioxidant response and inhibit NF-κB-driven inflammatory signaling, thereby mitigating oxidative hepatocyte damage.

MAPK and PI3K/AKT are upstream regulators of Nrf237. Many studies have shown that an excess of ROS can activate proteins in the MAPK family, which are involved in NF-κB activation37,38. The activation of p38 MAPK can initiate the downstream NF-κB pathway, while inhibition of the MAPK pathway can reduce transcriptional and oxidative stress in pro-inflammatory mediators. Previous studies have shown that the phosphorylation of p38 MAPK contributes to CCl4-induced ALI38. PI3K/AKT signal transduction is an important functional regulator of NF-κB activity, playing a key role in regulating anti-inflammatory cytokines, immunomodulation, and the inhibition of apoptosis39. In a previous study, Mi et al.40 found that Schisandrin A (SCA) exerted a cardioprotective effect on isoproterine-induced acute myocardial infarction by improving oxidative stress and inflammation by modulating the PI3K-AKT/Nrf2/ARE and TLR4/MAPK/NF-κB pathways. In this study, we did not detect the upstream regulators of NF-κB and Nrf2; rather, we detected the expression levels of factors downstream of the Nrf2 and NF-κB signaling pathways (TNF-α, IL-6, and HO-1) and found that SMP played a role in regulating the expression of TNF-α, IL-6, HO-1, and GCLC. In subsequent research, we will explore the effects of SMP on signaling pathways such as MAPK and PI3K/AKT, and clarify the correlation between the MAPK and PI3K/AKT signaling pathways and the NF-κB signaling pathway, in order to enrich the mechanism of SMP’s liver protective effects.

The study of polysaccharide pharmacokinetics would predominantly include the absorption, distribution, metabolism, and elimination of polysaccharide drugs in the body41. The oral absorption of polysaccharides includes direct absorption, intestinal microbiota absorption, and intestinal Peyer's collective lymph node absorption41. Polysaccharides can be administered intravenously or orally, and the distribution of polysaccharides is highest in the kidneys and liver. Most in vivo pharmacokinetic models are two-chamber models. The elimination rate of polysaccharides is usually slow, and these drugs tend to remain in the body for a long period of time; subsequently, most are excreted by the kidneys42,43. Research related to the extraction and purification of polysaccharides, and the evaluation of efficacy, is relatively advanced; however, very few studies have involved pharmacokinetic analyses43. Polysaccharides are highly complex, predominantly due to molecular weight, structure, metabolism, and the lack of chromogenic groups and photo-absorbing groups43. The study of polysaccharide pharmacodynamics remains difficult due to the susceptibility of drug concentration detection in vivo to interference with other biological components. In particular, research involving human subjects remains weak. Future studies could utilize analytical techniques such as near-infrared marking and positron emission tomography to further investigate the pharmacokinetics of SMP and provide a theoretical basis for the further development and utilization of SMP.

This study provides a theoretical basis for the clinical application of grass mushroom polysaccharides and also provides a new direction for the development and application of health products for the prevention of ALI in the future. However, it is still necessary to combine knockout or overexpression technology, metabolomics, transcriptomics, and other technical means to comprehensively investigate the hepatoprotective mechanisms of grass mushroom polysaccharides, and further characterize and analyze the pharmacokinetics of SMP. These studies will enrich the pharmacological basis of SMP and provide a theoretical basis for the preclinical development of SMP.

SMP is a heteropolysaccharide composed of glucose, galactose, and mannose and exerts good antioxidant and hepatoprotective effects. In vitro studies found that SMP alleviated H2O2-induced oxidative damage in a cellular model; results arising from animal experiments showed that SMP could alleviate CCl4-induced ALI in mice by reducing the levels of ALT, AST, MDA, TNF-α, and IL-6, and by increasing the activities of hepatic SOD and CAT enzymes. Mechanistically, SMP upregulated the expression levels of Nrf2 protein and down-regulated the expression levels of Keap-1, NF-κB, and TLR4 proteins, before activating the Nrf2 signaling pathway and inhibiting the NF-κB signaling pathway, exerting hepatoprotective effects both in vitro and in vivo (Fig. 7). Our findings may provide a new direction for the development of SMP as a hepatoprotective drug and enrich the pharmacological basis of V. volvacea. In the future, SMP could be characterized and analyzed further. The structure-activity relationship and pharmacokinetics of SMP could also be investigated to examine the pharmacological foundation of SMP. Methods

Materials

The Volvariella volvacea were purchased from the Gutian Edible Fungi Wholesale Market (Ningde, China). Chemical reagents, including hydrogen peroxide, ABTS, DPPH, salicylic acid, DMSO, trypsin, ferrous sulfate, potassium persulfate, Vc, and absolute ethanol, were obtained from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd (Beijing, China). Penicillin-Streptomycin was acquired from Solarbio Life Sciences Co., Ltd (Beijing, China). DMEM medium was procured from Hyclone (Logan, USA), and fetal bovine serum (FBS) was sourced from Gibco (California, USA). The Nrf2 inhibitor ML 385 was purchased from Selleck Chemicals (Houston, TX, USA). Monoclonal antibodies against Nrf2, Keap1, HO-1, NF-κB, p-NF-κB, and TLR4 were provided by Proteintech Group Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China). SOD and CAT kits were purchased from Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute (Nanjing, China).

Human normal liver cells LO2 were purchased from Cellverse Co., Ltd (Shanghai, China). LO2 cells were tested to confirm that they were not contaminated with mycoplasma. Kunming mice (male-specific SPF grade), weighing 18–22 g, were purchased from Wu’s Animals Online (http://www.wssydw.com/m/index.asp). All experimental protocols and animal welfare procedures were conducted in accordance with the relevant ethical guidelines and approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Fujian Agriculture and Forestry University (PZCASFAFU21029). All animals were acclimatized under standardized laboratory conditions, including controlled temperature (22 ± 2 °C), humidity (55 ± 10%), and a 12 h/12 h light–dark regimen. Food and water were available ad libitum.

Extraction and preparation of SMP

The polysaccharide extraction method was previously described by Liu et al.44 with minor modifications. We dried the fresh fruiting products of V. volvacea and weighed 100 g of the dry product, which was then crushed and passed through a 60-mesh sieve. Then, we added water at a mass-volume ratio of 1:30 for incubation at 98 °C for 2 h. The filtrate was then centrifuged; after which, we added water to the precipitate and repeated the procedure. We combined the two filtrates for pressure reduction and concentration. Once the volume had been reduced to <1/4, we added anhydrous ethanol to the concentrated filtrate, such that the alcohol concentration of the solution was 80%. The solution was then placed in a refrigerator at 4 °C overnight. The following morning, the solution was centrifuged and reconstituted in a small amount of water. Next, we added a 1/3 volume of Sevag solution (chloroform:n-butanol = 4:1), mixed well, and centrifuged at 8000 r/min for 5 min. At this time, the solution was divided into three layers: the upper layer represented an aqueous phase, the intermediate layer represented a precipitated crude protein, and the lower layer represented an organic phase. We took the upper polysaccharide solution and repeated the operation multiple times until no protein appeared. The resultant polysaccharide solution was poured into a dialysis bag (boiling in water for 10 min), sealed, and placed in running water for 48 h. Using this procedure, small molecules of impurities were precipitated, and the treated polysaccharide was freeze-dried into SMP to await experimentation.

Infrared absorption spectroscopy of SMP

FTIR analysis of SMP was performed based on a protocol reported previously by Gong et al.18. In brief, 5 mg of SMP was mixed with KBr powder at a mass ratio of 1:200, ground evenly, and pressed into transparent flakes. Infrared absorption spectra were recorded using an FTIR spectrometer over a range of 500–4000 cm−¹.

Analysis of SMP monosaccharide components

A 5.00 ± 0.05 mg sample was added to a 2 M TFA acid solution and acid hydrolyzed at 105 °C for 6 h. Next, the reaction system was dried with nitrogen, mixed with methanol, washed and dried. The acidolysis residue was removed by three cycles of washing (2–3 times). The final product was redissolved in ultrapure water and transferred to an HPLC bottle for subsequent analysis.

The HPLC operational parameters were configured as follows: a sample loading volume of 5 μL via microsyringe injection, and an eluent system comprising solution A (0.1 M NaOH) and solution B (0.1 M NaOH, 0.2 M NaAc), delivered at an isocratic flow rate of 0.5 mL/min through a thermostatic column compartment maintained at 30 °C. The gradient elution protocol was programmed as follows: 0 min A phase/B phase (95:5 V/V), 30 min A phase/B phase (80:20 V/V), 30.1 min A phase/B phase (60:40 V/V), 45 min A phase/B phase (60:40 V/V), 45 min A phase/B phase (95:5 V/V), and 60 min A phase/B phase (95:5 V/V).

Molecular weight distribution of SMP

Accurately weigh 5 mg of the sample, add 1 mL of 0.05 M NaCl solution to the sample, and prepare a testing sample solution of 5 mg/mL. Filter using a 0.22 μm microporous filter membrane, then transfer the sample into a 2 mL sample vial for storage. Use high-performance gel permeation chromatography to detect the molecular weight distribution of SMP. Chromatographic method45: Instrument: Waters high-performance liquid chromatography; Detector: Waters differential detector; Chromatography column: two polymer-based water-soluble SEC (GFC) columns (8 × 300 mm) in series; Mobile phase: 0.05 M NaCl solution; Flow rate: 0.50 ml/min; Column temperature: 40 °C; Sample volume: 30 μL.

Chemical antioxidant activity

DPPH free radical scavenging activity was detected using the methodology described previously by Liu et al.46, with slight modifications. SMP was prepared into 0.025, 0.05, 0.25, 0.5, 1, 2, and 5 mg/mL solutions. A 50 μL sample was mixed with 0.2 mmol/L DPPH solution, incubated in the dark at 25 °C for 30 min, and the light absorption value at 517 nm was measured (and recorded as Ai). A0 represented the absorbance value of the reaction of anhydrous ethanol with DPPH solution, and the absorbance value of the reaction of anhydrous ethanol with polysaccharide was recorded as Aj. Each group was set up with five compound wells, and at least three biological replicates were conducted; final data were calculated using the following formula: DPPH free radical scavenging rate = [1−(Ai−Aj)/A0] × 100%.

ABTS radical scavenging activity was detected using the methodology described previously by Chen et al.47 with slight modifications. To prepare the ABTS stock solution, 19.2 mg of ABTS was dissolved in 5 mL of sterile water. Separately, 37.84 mg potassium persulfate (K2S2O8) was dissolved in 1 mL of deionized water to form an oxidizing agent solution. The ABTS+ working solution was generated by combining 5 mL of ABTS solution with 88 μL of potassium persulfate solution, followed by 12–16 h incubation in the dark at 4 °C. Prior to analysis, the concentrated solution was adjusted with deionized water to obtain an absorbance value of 0.70 ± 0.023 at a wavelength of 734 nm. The ABTS+ working solution was obtained by equilibrating at 30 °C for 30 min. SMP was prepared into 0.025, 0.05, 0.25, 0.5, 1, 2, and 5 mg/mL solutions. The same amount of ABTS+ working solution and different concentrations of SMP were added to the sample well, and recorded as Ai. The same amount of ABTS+ working solution and deionized water was added to the blank well, and recorded as A0. Each group was set up with five compound wells, and at least three biological replicates were conducted. The same amount of deionized water and different concentrations of SMP were added to the control well, which was recorded as Aj. ABTS scavenging rate = [1−(Ai−Aj)/A0]×100%.

OH free radical scavenging ability was determined using the methodology described previously by Du et al.48 with slight modifications. SMP was prepared into 0.025, 0.05, 0.25, 0.5, 1, 2, and 5 mg/mL solutions. Next, 1.0 mL of ferrous sulfate, 300 μL of sodium salicylate, and 700 μL of H2O2 were added to the treatment group, the blank group, and the control group, respectively. Following 30 min incubation in the dark at 37 °C, absorbance measurements were conducted at 510 nm. The treatment group absorbance was designated as Ai, while Aj represented measurements with H2O2 replaced by distilled water. A0 corresponded to control samples in which the polysaccharide was substituted with distilled water. Using VC as the standard antioxidant reference, quintuplicate experiments were performed for each group. Each group was set up with five compound wells, and at least three biological replicates were conducted. Data analysis employed the following computational formula: OH radical scavenging rate = [1−(Ai−Aj)/A0] × 100%.

Cytotoxicity assay

LO2 cells were cultured in RIPM 1640 medium containing 10% FBS and 1% penicillin–streptomycin solution in an incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO2. LO2 cells were seeded into 96-well dishes at a density of 5 × 103 cells for 24 h. After adherence, experimental groups were established: a control group, SMP groups (0.025, 0.05, 0.25, 0.5, 1, 2, and 5 mg/mL), and a blank control group, with five replicates per group. At least three biological replicates were conducted. Cells were incubated with blank RPMI 1640 medium or RPMI 1640 medium containing SMP. After 24 h of incubation, the medium was replaced with 20 μL of MTT (5 mg/mL) and 180 μL of RPMI 1640 medium per well. Following 4 h of incubation, 150 μL DMSO was added to each well; then, the OD at 490 nm was measured using a microplate reader.

Cell viability (%) = (OD SMP groups−OD Blank control group)/(OD Control group−OD Blank control group) × 100%.

Oxidative stress damage to LO2 cells

Referring to the method described by Liu et al.6, LO2 cells were seeded on a 96-well plate for 24 h (5 × 103 cells). After 24 h, the supernatant was discarded and different concentrations of H2O2 solution [0.0 (control group), 0.05, 0.5, 1, 1.5, 2, 4, and 8 mmol/L] were added to each well. After 2 h of culture, the supernatant was discarded, 20 μL of MTT solution and 180 μL of RPMI 1640 medium were added to each well, and the culture was continued for 4 h. The subsequent experimental steps and detection methods are described in “Cytotoxicity assay” section. Each group was set up with five compound wells, and at least three biological replicates were conducted.

Protective effects of SMP on oxidative damage induced by H2O2 in LO2 cells

For this analysis, we used the methodology described previously by Liu et al.6 with minor modifications. Cells were processed according to the methods described in “Cytotoxicity assay” section. Following culture, the supernatant was discarded, and 1 mmol/L H2O2 was added to the model group and the experimental group to establish a model of H2O2-induced peroxidation damage in LO2 cells. The subsequent experimental steps and detection methods are described in “Oxidative stress damage to LO2 cells” section. Each group was set up with five compound wells, and at least three biological replicates were conducted.

Determination of intracellular ROS content

Intracellular ROS content was determined according to the methodology described by Zhang et al.49 with slight modifications. LO2 cells were seeded on 96-well dishes at a density of 5 × 103 cells for 24 h. Several groups were established: a control group, a model group, and SMP groups (concentrations of 0.05, 0.5, and 5 mg/mL). Incubation 24 h, supernatants were discarded. The model group and SMP groups were supplemented with 1 mmol/L H₂O₂ for 2 h. Subsequently, 10 μmol/L DCFH-DA was added to each well and incubated in the dark for 30 min. Cells were washed twice with PBS, and fluorescence intensity was measured at excitation/emission wavelengths of 488/525 nm using a microplate reader. Each group was set up with five compound wells, and at least three biological replicates were conducted. Data were calculated according to the following formula.

Relative content of ROS = fluorescence intensity of treatment group/fluorescence intensity of control group × 100%.

Effect of SMP on SOD and CAT activities in cells

LO2 hepatocytes were plated onto 96-well microplates at a density of 1 × 105 cells/mL and acclimatized for 24 h. RPMI 1640 medium was added to the control group and model group, and SMP solutions containing different concentrations (0.05, 0.5, and 5 mg/mL) were added to the SMP group; culture was then continued for 24 h. Subsequently, the model group and SMP groups were supplemented with 1 mmol/L H₂O₂ for 2 h. Following the culture, the cells were collected and treated in accordance with the instructions provided with the SOD and CAT detection kits to detect the enzyme activities of SOD and CAT in each group. Each group was set up with five compound wells, and at least three biological replicates were conducted.

Total SOD activity (%) = [(OD Control group−OD Test group)/(OD Control group)]/50% × Dilution factor of reaction system/Sample protein concentration.

CAT content = (OD Control group−OD Test group)× 271 ×[1/(60 × Sampling volume)]/Sample protein concentration (mg prot/mL).

Targeted inhibition of Nrf2 and the expression of key antioxidative factors in SMP

When the cells grew to 70–80% confluency, we set up a control group, a model (ML 385) group, and an SMP + ML385 group. SMP was administered at a concentration of 0.5 mg/mL. The control group was supplemented with 1 mL of RPMI 1640 medium, and the model group was supplemented with 1 mL of ML385 containing 2.5 µM/L. The SMP group was supplemented with 1 mL of ML385 containing 2.5 µM/L for pretreatment for 24 h. After 24 h, the culture medium containing the inhibitor was discarded, and a complete medium containing SMP was added, and the culture continued for 24 h. Following the culture, we collected the cells for fluorescence quantitative PCR detection.

Construction of mice model of acute liver injury

All experimental procedures involving animals were reviewed and approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Fujian Agriculture and Forestry University (PZCASFAFU21029). These studies strictly followed the National Research Council’s Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory. Forty male mice were randomly divided into four groups (n = 10 per group): a blank group, a CCl4 model group, an SMP group (200 mg/kg), and a positive control group (Bifendate, 300 mg/kg). One week after the mice were acclimated, we began oral gavage. Mice in the blank, model, SMP, and positive control groups were gavaged with distilled water, distilled water, SMP, and bifendate, respectively. Except for the blank group, the other groups were all gavaged with 2% CCl4 vegetable oil solution on days 1, 4, and 7, with a 4h interval of treatment with SMP to establish a model of ALI. The body weights of mice in each group were measured every other day. After administration on day 7, the mice were fasted and allowed to drink. The next day, mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation following orbital blood sampling, and supernatant was collected by centrifugation. The liver was rapidly removed after dissection and used for subsequent indicator determination.

Detection of serum indices of liver function

The collected mouse serum was treated according to the instructions provided with ALT, AST, TC, and TG assay kits. Absorbance was measured using a multifunctional microplate reader. At least three replicates per group were analyzed.

Liver peroxide levels were detected using the method described previously by Wang et al.50 with slight modifications. In brief, 100 mg of liver tissue from mice in each group were collected, mixed with 1 mL of pre-cooled physiological saline, ground, diluted into a 10% liver homogenate, and centrifuged at 3500 r/min for 15 min. The upper layer of light yellow transparent serum was aspirated using a pipette and applied to MDA and SOD kits to detect the levels of MDA and SOD in the liver. Analysis involved at least three replicates per group.

H&E staining

H&E staining was performed by the methodology described previously by Dai et al.51 with slight modifications. Liver tissues were fixed in 10% formalin, followed by sequential dehydration, paraffin embedding, and sectioning at a thickness of 5 μm. Following deparaffinization in xylene, tissue sections were rehydrated through a graded series of alcohol concentrations. Antigen retrieval was performed using citrate buffer, with endogenous peroxidase activity inhibited by hydrogen peroxide treatment. Histopathological evaluation of liver injury was conducted by H&E staining using commercial reagents (Beyotime, China).

Western blot assays

For western blotting, we used the methodology described previously by Tan et al.52 with slight modifications. Liver tissues from each group were removed from a refrigerator at −80 °C and ground in liquid nitrogen. Cellular proteins were extracted with ice-cold RIPA Lysis Buffer containing a protease inhibitor cocktail (1 mM PMSF). Protein concentrations were determined by BCA kits. Sample buffer was added and denatured by heating in hot water; then, samples were left at −20 °C to await analysis. Western blot analysis was performed using standardized protocols. Protein samples (20 μg per lane) were separated by SDS-PAGE and electrophoretically transferred onto PVDF membranes. Membranes were subsequently blocked with 5% non-fat milk for 1 h at ambient temperature, followed by three 10-min TBST washes. Primary antibodies targeting Nrf2, Keap-1, NF-κB, p-NF-κB, TLR4, and GAPDH were applied for overnight incubation at 4 °C. After additional TBST rinses (3 × 10 min), membranes were exposed to HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (Goat anti-rabbit or goat anti-mouse) at room temperature for 1 h. Final detection was preceded by three additional TBST washing cycles (10 min each). ECL luminescent solution was added, and images were developed with an imaging luminescent instrument and photographed. At least three biological replicates were conducted. ImageJ software was used to analyze grayscale values for all bands. GAPDH was used as an internal reference, and the ratio of the target protein to the internal reference protein was classified as relative protein expression.

Statistical analysis

Data were processed using SPSS version 25.0 statistical software, and data are expressed as x ± s. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) and LSD were used for multiple comparisons between multiple groups, and the t-test was used to compare means between two groups. Differences were considered statistically significant at P < 0.05.
