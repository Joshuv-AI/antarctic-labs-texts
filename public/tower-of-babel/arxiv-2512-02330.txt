Unconventional superconductivity from crystal field fluctuations
M. A. Zeb

Abstract
We present a novel pairing mechanism for superconductivity in strongly correlated electron systems, which often have both localised and itinerant charge carriers. An effective anisotropic interaction between the itinerant particles originates from the fluctuations in the crystal field associated with virtual hopping of the localised particles, a process that is also responsible for the Kondo exchange. Interestingly, this interaction is attractive for charge transfer insulators such as cuprates. Considering a simple toy model for cuprates, without the antiferromagnetic exchange, this interaction leads to the correct d-wave superconducting gap, thus demonstrating its relevance.



Understanding the pairing mechanism
in unconventional superconductors
is a central problem in condensed matter physics.
While quantum fluctuations in spin, charge, or orbital degrees of freedom
can all mediate the pairing interactions,
the phase diagrams of
these systems are usually too rich and complex for a simple explanation
Lee et al. 2006
;
Paglione and Greene 2010
;
Scalapino 2012
;
Keimer et al. 2015
;
Stewart 2017
.
The dominant interactions may differ among various types of strongly correlated electron systems—such as heavy fermions
Löhneysen et al. 2007
, cuprates
Keimer et al. 2015
, nickelates
Botana and Norman 2020
, iron pnictides and chalcogenides
Paglione and Greene 2010
;
Fernandes et al. 2022
, and organic salts
Powell and McKenzie 2006
—a common feature of most unconventional superconductors in this class, however, is the presence of strong antiferromagnetic correlations
Scalapino 2012
, which are therefore considered the leading candidate for mediating the superconducting pairing interaction in these systems.

Another ubiquitous aspect of these systems is the presence of localized and itinerant states hybridising with each other,
well described by the Anderson lattice model
Anderson 1961
.
This is obviously the origin of Kondo exchange in heavy fermions
Coleman 2015
,
but it is also a shared motif among
many other systems
like cuprates and nikelates, etc.,
including the systems where the spin correlations are weak or absent.

Here, we reveal a new effective interaction in these systems,
which is attractive under widely applicable conditions
and can mediate Cooper pairing.
The origin of this new interaction can be understood in simple words as follows.
It is well known that
the particle hopping between localised orbitals and band states leads to
the Kondo exchange and
a scattering potential for the latter
Schrieffer and Wolff 1966
;
Coleman 2015
.
Since the particles are charged,
the hopping also produces another scattering potential due
to a fluctuation in the crystalline electric field
Griffith 2009
.
The combined effect of these two
coupled
scatterings
becomes an effective interaction
between the itinerant particles.
We show that this interaction can lead to a d-wave superconducting gap function in cuprates with the right symmetry,
{x^{2}-y^{2}}

Tsuei and Kirtley 2000
.

I
Effective interaction from crystal field fluctuations

The full quantum version of crystal field interaction
replaces the monopole charge in the standard crystal field theory
Griffith 2009

by the particle number operator (see Methods).
For an Anderson lattice model,
the contribution to the Hamiltonian
from the crystal field fluctuations
associated with the charge fluctuations at
the correlated sites
becomes

\displaystyle H_{\text{CFF}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i}\sum_{{\bf k}{\bf p}\nu\mu\sigma}\mathcal{F}^{{\bf k}{\bf p}}_{\mu\nu}e^{i({\bf p}-{\bf k})\cdot{\bf r}_{i}}~\delta n_{i}~\pi_{{\bf p}\mu\sigma}^{\dagger}\pi_{{\bf k}\nu\sigma},

(1)

where

\delta n_{i}
is the charge fluctuation in the orbitals localised around a site at position
{\bf r}_{i}
,

\pi_{k\nu\sigma}
annihilates a particle of spin
\sigma
at momentum
k
in the band
\nu
,

\mathcal{F}^{kp}_{\mu\nu}
are the corresponding interaction matrix elements,
and

N
is the total number of unit cells in the lattice.

We know that the Anderson lattice model leads to
the Kondo exchange
upon the Schrieffer-Wolf transformation
Schrieffer and Wolff 1966
.
When the Anderson lattice model is augmented with the above crystal field fluctuations,
the
Schrieffer-Wolf transformation
gives an additional effective interaction

V_{\text{eff}}=\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{P}[S,[S,H_{\text{CFF}}]\mathcal{P}
,
where
S
is the generator of the transformation
and
\mathcal{P}
projects onto the low energy space.
Taking

t_{k\mu}
to be the hybridisation amplitude
between the localised states and the band state
k\mu
at energy

\varepsilon_{k\mu}
,
and
\epsilon_{\pm}
to be the energies of doubly occupied and empty correlated sites,
we obtain

\displaystyle V_{\text{eff}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2N}\sum_{kk^{\prime}q}\mathcal{W}^{\mathbf{kk^{\prime}q}}_{\nu\mu\nu^{\prime}\mu^{\prime}}~\pi_{\mathbf{k^{\prime}-q}\mu^{\prime}\sigma^{\prime}}^{\dagger}\pi^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k+q}\mu\sigma}\pi_{\mathbf{k}\nu\sigma}\pi_{\mathbf{k^{\prime}}\nu^{\prime}\sigma^{\prime}},

(2)

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}^{\mathbf{kk^{\prime}q}}_{\nu\mu\nu^{\prime}\mu^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\xi=\pm}\xi\frac{t^{*}_{\mathbf{k^{\prime}}\nu^{\prime}}}{\varepsilon_{\mathbf{k^{\prime}}\nu^{\prime}}-\epsilon_{\xi}}~\frac{t_{\mathbf{k^{\prime}-q}\mu^{\prime}}}{\varepsilon_{\mathbf{k^{\prime}-q}\mu^{\prime}}-\epsilon_{\xi}}~\mathcal{F}^{\mathbf{kk+q}}_{\nu\mu},

(3)

where
a sum over the repeated indices is implied in the first line.
Here, we have ignored
a term that describes the scattering off the Kondo singlets (see Methods)
but do not contribute to superconductivity under the pairing approximation.

II
Superconductivity

Beside
V_{\text{eff}}
,
we have the standard Kondo exchange
which generates the renormalised “heavy fermion” bands
Coleman 2015
.
We consider the superconductivity in these renormalised bands
mediated by

V_{\text{eff}}
.
In case of a single band,
dropping the band indices for brevity,
an approximate expression for the pairing potential is

V_{{\bf k}{\bf k^{\prime}}}\equiv\mathcal{W}^{-{\bf k^{\prime}}{\bf k^{\prime}},{\bf k}-{\bf k^{\prime}}}\approx t_{{\bf k}}t_{{\bf k^{\prime}}}^{*}(-1/\Delta_{CT}^{2}+1/U^{2})V_{q}/2
,
where

\Delta_{CT}
is the charge transfer gap,

U
is the Hubbard repulsion for correlated sites,
and

V_{q}>0
is the magnitude of the crystal field fluctuation
in the uncorrelated band.
If this band originates from a single uncorrelated orbital,
V_{q}
is proportional to the intersite Coulomb repulsion between particles on the neighbouring
correlated and uncorrelated sites.
At
U>\Delta_{CT}
, a condition all charge-transfer Mott insulators satisfy by definition,

V_{\text{eff}}
is attractive.

III
d
-wave pairing in cuprates

To demonstrate the relevance of the idea on
a specific system,
we consider
hole doped cuprates,
where the
CuO
2
planes
play the key role in superconductivity.
Apart from being a simple and well studied example,
CuO
2
is a very relevant case here
as the doped holes are observed to predominantly
stay on the O atoms
Zaanen et al. 1985
;
Chen et al. 1991
;
Kowalski et al. 2021
,
which form the itinerant bands
that enjoy the effective attraction.
A CuO
2
plane has a square Bravais lattice,
with one Cu and two O atoms in the primitive unit cell.
The minimal model to describe its electronic structure
includes only
three orbitals,

d_{x^{2}-y^{2}}
orbital at Cu sites
that hybridises only with

p_{x}
orbital of one neighbouring O and
p_{y}
orbital of the other
Emery 1987
,
as shown in Fig.
1
(a).

An antiferromagnetic exchange between the neighbouring Cu sites
exists, which is usually considered to mediate the pairing
Scalapino 2012
, but
we ignore it completely, which can be justified at large doping concentrations.
If we assume a simple role for
this antiferromagnetic exchange, i.e., creating an antiferromagnetic order,
it would fold the bands in a perfect Neel state and split them at the resulting degeneracies.
To focus on the potential role of
V_{\text{eff}}
in superconductivity,
we do not consider the antiferromagnetic order.

Interestingly,
if we ignore the hybridisation between these two O orbitals,
we can work with
their collective state

\phi_{{\bf k}}=(S_{x}p_{{\bf k},x}-S_{y}p_{{\bf k},y})/\eta_{{\bf k}}
,
where

p_{{\bf k},x/y}
are the Fourier transform of lattice operators
p_{j,x/y}
,
j
being the unit cell index,
and, for brevity,
we have defined
S_{x}=\sin(k_{x}a/2),S_{y}=\sin(k_{y}a/2)

and
\eta_{{\bf k}}=\sqrt{S_{x}^{2}+S_{y}^{2}}
.
The hybridisation amplitude for this state, up to a phase, is
t_{{\bf k}}=2t\eta_{{\bf k}}
, while
the orthogonal state

\bar{\phi}_{{\bf k}}\propto S_{y}p_{{\bf k},x}+S_{x}p_{{\bf k},y}

does not hybridise with Cu
d_{x^{2}-y^{2}}
orbitals.
Crystal field fluctuations also induce an interband interaction
between
\phi_{{\bf k}}
and

\bar{\phi}_{{\bf k}}
band particles,
but
we can assume that the hybridisation of the former with the Cu states
move their spectral weights energetically away from each other,
and safely focus on the pairing in the heavy fermion bands.

To compute heavy fermion bands,
we decouple the Kondo interaction in the hybridisation channel
Coleman 2015
,
which is the standard mean-field approximation in this case.
Owing to the factorisable form of the Kondo exchange

J_{{\bf k}{\bf k^{\prime}}}=J\eta_{{\bf k}}\eta_{{\bf k^{\prime}}}
,
where
J\simeq t^{2}/\Delta_{CT}
is a constant,
the hybridisation field
has a simple form

V_{{\bf k}}=v\eta_{{\bf k}}
, where

v=\frac{J}{N}\sum_{k}\eta_{{\bf k}}\braket{\phi_{{\bf k}\sigma}^{\dagger}d_{{\bf k}\sigma}}

has to be calculated self-consistently.
The
renormalised heavy fermion states
can be written as
\Psi_{{\bf k}}=\alpha_{{\bf k}}d_{{\bf k}}+\beta_{{\bf k}}\phi_{{\bf k}}
,
where

\alpha_{{\bf k}},\beta_{{\bf k}}
are symmetric (
A_{1g}
),
thanks to
a symmetric hybridisation field
V_{{\bf k}}\propto\eta_{{\bf k}}
.
In these states, the pairing potential
projected onto the symmetric channel
for the spin-singlet pairing
Coleman 2015

splits into a factorised form for the s-wave (
A_{1g}
) and
d
-wave (
B_{1g}
) channels, given by

\displaystyle V_{{\bf k}{\bf k^{\prime}}}^{(s)}

\displaystyle=-g\left(\gamma_{{\bf k}}^{S}\gamma_{{\bf k^{\prime}}}^{S}+\gamma_{{\bf k}}^{D}\gamma_{{\bf k^{\prime}}}^{D}\right),

(4)

where

{\gamma_{{\bf k}}^{S}=\beta_{{\bf k}}^{2}(-2+\cos k_{x}a+\cos k_{y}a)}
and

{\gamma_{{\bf k}}^{D}=\beta_{{\bf k}}^{2}(\cos k_{x}a-\cos k_{y}a)}

are s- and d-wave form factors,
and

g=(t^{2}/\Delta_{CT}^{2}-t^{2}/U^{2})U_{pd}/4
is the coupling constant,

U
is the onsite Coulomb repulsion for Cu atoms
and
U_{pd}
is the intersite Coulomb repulsion for neighbouring Cu and O atoms.

g\sim 0.1
eV at typical parameter values.
It is interesting that no other symmetry channels, e.g.,
d_{xy}
(
B_{2g}
) exist at all. The odd-symmetry channels exist however, but are weaker and can be ignored.
Since experiments find a d-wave pairing in cuprates,
both these channels being attractive is inconsistent with our expectation from
a correct model.
Here, the repulsive Coulomb interaction (Hubbard U)
resolves the issue.
The Hubbard repulsion on the Cu atoms

V^{\text{Coul}}_{{\bf k}{\bf k^{\prime}}}=U\alpha_{{\bf k}}^{2}\alpha_{{\bf k^{\prime}}}^{2}

strongly suppresses the s-wave pairing
but the d-wave channel is saved by symmetry.
Defining

I=(I_{1}/I_{S})^{2}
,
where

I_{1}=\int d{\bf k}\alpha_{{\bf k}}^{2}\gamma_{{\bf k}}^{S}

and

I_{S}^{2}=\int d{\bf k}|\gamma_{{\bf k}}^{S}|^{2}
,
the total pairing potential
becomes

\displaystyle V_{{\bf k}{\bf k^{\prime}}}^{(s)}

\displaystyle=\left(UI-g\right)\gamma_{{\bf k}}^{S}\gamma_{{\bf k^{\prime}}}^{S}-g\gamma_{{\bf k}}^{D}\gamma_{{\bf k^{\prime}}}^{D}.

(5)

At
U<g/I
,
s-wave is attractive,
and it can even dominate the d-wave channel
at

U<(I_{S}^{2}-I_{D}^{2})g/I_{1}^{2}
,
where
I_{D}^{2}=\int d{\bf k}|\gamma_{{\bf k}}^{D}|^{2}
.
At
U>g/I
however,
which is more appropriate for strongly correlated systems like cuprates,
the s-wave channel becomes repulsive,
but the d-wave channel obviously stays attractive
leading to a d-wave pairing with gap function

\Delta_{{\bf k}}\propto\gamma_{{\bf k}}^{D}=\beta_{{\bf k}}^{2}(\cos k_{x}a-\cos k_{y}a)
.
For our simple model,
the shape of the fermi surface is dictated by
the equipotential contours of the hybridisation field

V_{{\bf k}}\propto\eta_{{\bf k}}
,
so the coefficients
\alpha_{{\bf k}},\beta_{{\bf k}}
do not vary on the fermi surface either.
Thus, the gap function further simplifies
on the fermi surface,

\displaystyle\Delta_{{\bf k}}=\Delta(\cos k_{x}a-\cos k_{y}a),

(6)

with
\Delta
being a constant.

The gap equation
Coleman 2015
has a simple form,

1=g\sum_{{\bf k}}\frac{|\gamma_{{\bf k}}^{D}|^{2}}{2E_{{\bf k}}}\tanh\frac{E_{{\bf k}}}{2T}
,
where

E_{{\bf k}}=\sqrt{\xi_{{\bf k}}^{2}+\Delta_{{\bf k}}^{2}}

is the Bogoliubov quasiparticle energy
Bogolyubov 1958
,

\xi_{{\bf k}}
is the heavy fermion band energy measured from the fermi energy,
and
T
is the temperature.
Considering a fixed set of heavy bands states,
we solve the gap equation to determine

\Delta_{{\bf k}}
at a given
g,T
.
Similarly, the critical temperature
T_{c}

can be determined as a function of

g
by solving
the linearised gap equation

1=g\sum_{{\bf k}}\frac{|\gamma_{{\bf k}}^{D}|^{2}}{2|\xi_{{\bf k}}|}\tanh\frac{|\xi_{{\bf k}}|}{2T}
.

By now, it is clear that

J,g
are the most important parameters.

J
determines the hybridisation field for a given doping concentration and temperature,
and gives the heavy fermion bands,
while

g
determines the gap function for pairing in these bands.
Due to the crude nature of the mean-field approximation
used to obtain the heavy bands,
where the hybridisation field depends on
the number of particles in the
\phi_{{\bf k}}
bands,
the self-consistency is achieved easier at larger doping concentrations.
We calculate the bands for a fixed high doping concentration and low temperature
and use these fixed bands to explore the superconductivity at an other relatively lower doping levels.

Fig.
1
(b) shows the symmetry of the gap function
on a chosen fermi surface using the bands
at
J=0.6
(
T=10^{-5}
and doping
x=0.7
),
which agrees to the experiments
Tsuei and Kirtley 2000
.
For the same bands and fermi surface,
the temperature dependance of the gap function
\Delta(T)

is shown in Fig.

(a),
while the zero temperature gap
\Delta(0)
and the critical temperature
T_{c}

are shown in Fig.

(b) as a function of the coupling
g
.
Everything seems reasonable
Lee et al. 2006
.
It is noteworthy that
the experimental data shows a strong dependance of
T_{c}
on the charge transfer energy
\Delta_{\text{CT}}

Ruan et al. 2016
;
Kowalski et al. 2021
;
Wang et al. 2023
,
which makes sense as
T_{c}\sim g\sim 1/\Delta_{\text{CT}}^{2}

according to our model.
These fixed bands calculations also produce a
dome shape for
T_{c}
as a function of the fermi energy (not shown),
but
relying on that
may be too much of a stretch.

IV
Some conjectures about the experimental phase diagram

Since a delocalised orbital cannot generate a strong enough crystal field fluctuation,
the effective interaction from the crystal filed fluctuations
critically depend on the spatial localisation of the correlated orbitals.
This can potentially explain the superconducting dome seen in the experimental phase diagrams of strongly correlated systems.
The correlated states delocalise more and more as we increase the doping,
weakening the pairing interaction.
Let’s assume it to be an exponential decay.
Since the mobile hole density strengthens the pairing, say linearly,
it will compete with this exponential decay,
leading to a peaked shape, explaining the dome.

The pairing interaction in our model
pairs the uncorrelated itinerant states,
whereas the Kondo exchange makes the Kondo singlets,
i.e., it binds the itinerant particles to the localised states.
This suggests a plausible explanation for the pseudogap phase,
where the experiments see a formation of spin-singlet pairs but not the superconducting pairing,
as simply linked to the Kondo screening
that neutralises the spin of the doped holes.
Here, also note that the
pairing interaction is the strongest near the anti-nodal regions,
i.e.,
(k_{x},k_{y})=(\pm\pi/a,0),(0,\pm\pi/a)
,
but the Kondo hybridisation field
V_{{\bf k}}
is weaker there,
which may lead to a strong renormalisation of the heavy bands
there and even locally breaking the fermi liquid picture,
something that is then observed by ARPES
as the quasiparticle broadening or loss of coherence
Chatterjee et al. 2011
;
Keimer et al. 2015
.

V
Methods

V.1
Fully quantum crystal field interaction

Crystal field interaction is obtained from the intersite direct Coulomb interaction as follows.
For two sites
i,j
at position
{\vec{R}_{i}=0}
,
{\vec{R}_{j}=\vec{R}}
,
consider
two points

r_{i},r_{j}

in aligned local coordinate frames attached to the two sites,
each one close to its labeled site.
The inverse distance between these points
can be factorised as

|\vec{r}_{i}-\vec{r}_{j}-\vec{R}|^{-1}=\sum_{LL^{\prime}}A_{LL^{\prime}}(\vec{R})V_{L}(\vec{r}_{i})V_{L^{\prime}}(\vec{r}_{j})
,
where
writing spherical harmonics as
{Y_{L}(\hat{r})}
with

{L=(l,m)}
denoting the usual angular momentum quantum numbers,
we have

V_{L}(\vec{r})=\left(\frac{r}{R}\right)^{l}Y_{L}(\hat{r})
,

A_{LL^{\prime}}(\vec{R})=\frac{1}{R}(-1)^{l^{\prime}}\gamma^{R}_{L}\gamma^{R}_{L^{\prime}}\gamma^{S}_{L+L^{\prime}}Y^{*}_{L+L^{\prime}}(\hat{R})
(
L^{\prime}=(l^{\prime},m^{\prime})
),

{\gamma^{R}_{L}=\sqrt{4\pi/(2l+1)(l-m)!(l+m)!}}
,

{\gamma^{S}_{L}=\sqrt{4\pi(l-m)!(l+m)!/(2l+1)}}
.
Here, we first use the addition theorem
of the spherical harmonics to
get a multipole expansion of the inverse distance
in the
i
th frame
(taking
|\vec{r}_{i}|<|\vec{R}+\vec{r}_{j}|
),
and then translating the irregular solid spherical harmonics
van Gelderen 1998
in this expansion
to the
j
’s frame (taking
|\vec{r}_{j}|<|\vec{R}|
).
The Coulomb tensor in atomic orbitals

\psi_{iL}(r)\equiv f_{iL}(r)Y_{L}(\hat{r})

can now be written in terms of atomic parameters

D^{i}_{LL_{a}L_{b}}=\int r^{2}drf_{iL_{a}}(r)(r/R)^{l}f_{iL_{b}}(r)

and Gaunt coefficients

C_{LL_{a}L_{b}}=\int d\hat{r}Y_{L_{a}}^{*}(\hat{r})Y_{L}(\hat{r})Y_{L_{b}}(\hat{r})
.
The intersite direct Coulomb interaction
\hat{V}_{ij}

becomes an expansion containing interaction between
combinations of monopoles and multipoles
transition operators
,

\hat{V}_{ij}=V_{0}~\hat{n}_{i}\hat{n}_{j}+\hat{n}_{i}\sum_{L_{3},L_{4}}V^{ij}_{L_{3}L_{4}}\hat{p}_{jL_{3}}^{\dagger}\hat{p}_{jL_{4}}\\
+\hat{n}_{j}\sum_{L_{1},L_{2}}V^{ji}_{L_{1}L_{2}}\hat{p}_{iL_{1}}^{\dagger}\hat{p}_{iL_{2}}\\
+\sum_{L_{1}L_{2}L_{3}L_{4}}M^{ij}_{L_{1}L_{2}L_{3}L_{4}}~\hat{p}_{iL_{1}}^{\dagger}\hat{p}_{iL_{2}}\hat{p}_{jL_{3}}^{\dagger}\hat{p}_{jL_{4}}.

(7)

Here,

{\hat{n}_{i}=\sum_{L}\hat{n}_{i,L}}

is the number of particles at site
i
,

{V_{0}=\frac{1}{2}A_{00}(\vec{R})}
,

V^{ji}_{L_{1}L_{2}}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{L\neq 0}A_{L0}(\vec{R}_{j}-\vec{R}_{i})C_{LL_{1}L_{2}}D^{i}_{LL_{1}L_{2}}
,
and
M^{ij}_{L_{1}L_{2}L_{3}L_{4}}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{L,L^{\prime}\neq 0}A_{LL^{\prime}}(\vec{R})C_{LL_{1}L_{2}}C_{L^{\prime}L_{3}L_{4}}D^{i}_{LL_{1}L_{2}}D^{j}_{L^{\prime}L_{3}L_{4}}

is the multipole interaction strength.

Compared to
V_{0}\sim 1/R
,
the multipole terms are suppressed
by faster decaying
D^{\prime}s\ll 1
.
However,
as we know from the crystal field theory
Griffith 2009

that describes the mean field version of the monopole terms,
such terms from only a few neighbouring sites
in a coordination cage in molecules or crystalline solids
create significant splittings in the atomic levels (e.g.,
\sim 1-3
eV in
d
-orbitals in transition metal oxides).
For our purpose,
we only need
the interaction involving
the monopole at the correlated site with highly localised orbitals,
which we write as

\hat{V}^{ij}_{CF}\simeq n_{i}\sum_{\alpha\beta\sigma}F^{ij}_{\alpha\beta}p_{j\alpha\sigma}^{\dagger}p_{j\beta\sigma}
,

F^{ij}_{\alpha\beta}=V_{0}\delta_{\alpha\beta}+V^{ij}_{\alpha\beta}
,
where we
simplified the notation for brevity
and included both spin states.
For a crystalline system with infinite sublattices
of correlated and uncorrelated orbitals,
the charge fluctuations

\delta n_{i}=n_{i}-\braket{n_{i}}

on the correlated sites
correspond to the crystal field fluctuations

\displaystyle H_{\text{CFF}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\braket{ij}}\delta n_{i}\sum_{\alpha\beta\sigma}F^{ij}_{\alpha\beta}p_{j\alpha\sigma}^{\dagger}p_{j\beta\sigma},

(8)

where
we only keep the nearest neighbour terms
in the sum, as denoted by
\braket{ij}
.
As discussed below, a unitary transformation to the desired uncorrelated band states
gives Eq.
1
.

V.2
Effective interaction in the Anderson lattice model

The Anderson lattice model that we consider is

H_{AL}=H_{\text{d}}+H_{\text{p}}+H_{\text{hyb}}
,
where

\displaystyle H_{\text{d}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\sigma}\epsilon_{d}d^{\dagger}_{i\sigma}d_{i\sigma}+Un_{i\uparrow}n_{i\downarrow},

(9)

\displaystyle H_{\text{p}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\alpha\sigma}\epsilon_{j\alpha}p^{\dagger}_{j\alpha\sigma}p_{j\alpha\sigma}+\sum_{<jl>\alpha\beta\sigma}t^{jl}_{\alpha\beta}p^{\dagger}_{j\alpha\sigma}p_{l\beta\sigma}+h.c

(10)

\displaystyle\equiv\sum_{{\bf k}\mu\sigma}\varepsilon_{{\bf k}\mu}\pi^{\dagger}_{{\bf k}\mu\sigma}\pi_{{\bf k}\mu\sigma},

(11)

\displaystyle H_{\text{hyb}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{<ij>\alpha\sigma}t^{ij}_{\alpha}p^{\dagger}_{j\alpha\sigma}d_{i\sigma}+h.c

(12)

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{i{\bf k}\mu\sigma}t_{{\bf k}\mu}e^{-i{\bf k}\cdot{\bf r}_{i}}\pi^{\dagger}_{{\bf k}\mu\sigma}d_{i\sigma}+h.c..

(13)

Here,

d^{\dagger}_{i\sigma}
creates a particle of spin
\sigma
in correlated orbital at site
i
,

n_{i\sigma}=d^{\dagger}_{i\sigma}d_{i\sigma}

is the corresponding number operator,
while

p^{\dagger}_{j\alpha\sigma}
creates a particle of spin
\sigma
in an uncorrelated orbital
\alpha
at site
j
.
These definitions now make the meanings of
various parameters obvious.
The diagonalisation of
H_{\text{p}}
at each
{\bf k}

gives the transformation matrices
U_{{\bf k}\mu\alpha}

that relate the
band operators and hopping parameters
to the Fourier transforms of corresponding tight-binding site variables, e.g.,

\pi^{\dagger}_{{\bf k}\mu\sigma}=\sum_{\alpha}U_{{\bf k}\mu\alpha}^{\dagger}p^{\dagger}_{{\bf k}\alpha\sigma}
,
where
p^{\dagger}_{{\bf k}\alpha\sigma}
are Fourier transforms
of
p^{\dagger}_{j\alpha\sigma}
, etc.

We can now transform
H_{\text{CFF}}
in Eq.
8

to the band basis, which is given in Eq.
1
with

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}^{{\bf k}{\bf p}}_{\mu\nu}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha\beta}U_{{\bf p}\mu\alpha}^{\dagger}\sum_{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{j}}e^{-i({\bf p}-{\bf k})\cdot\boldsymbol{\rho}_{j}}F^{ij}_{\alpha\beta}U_{{\bf k}\beta\nu},

(14)

where
\boldsymbol{\rho}_{j}

denotes the relative position of the uncorrelated nearest
neighbours with respect to the correlated site.
Note that
F^{ij}_{\alpha\beta}=F^{ij}_{\alpha\beta}(\boldsymbol{\rho}_{j})
does not depend on the absolute position of site
i
.
Adding
H_{\text{CFF}}
to
H_{AL}

(which is assumed to implicitly include
the average crystal field interaction),
we obtain our full model
H
.
We are interested in the low energy behaviour of the many-body system described by
H
.

Following Schrieffer and Wolff
Schrieffer and Wolff 1966
,
we define

\displaystyle S

\displaystyle=\sum_{\xi=\pm}S_{\xi}-S_{\xi}^{\dagger},
