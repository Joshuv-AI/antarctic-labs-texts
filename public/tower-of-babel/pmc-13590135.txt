Design, Synthesis, and Antifungal Activity of Natural Furan Carboxylic Acid Derivatives as Potential Succinate Dehydrogenase Inhibitors
Authors: Hongxin Luo, Yusen Wang, Yanchang Shi, Chengna Dong, Yadong Song, Qilin Zhang, Zhiqiang Hu, Yukun Qin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIn this study, a series of furan amide derivatives were designed and synthesized from natural furan carboxylic acids as starting materials, with the aim of developing potential succinate dehydrogenase (SDH) inhibitors. The antifungal activities of the target compounds against four species of plant‐pathogenic fungi were evaluated using in vitro bioassays. Structure–activity relationship (SAR) analysis revealed that the introduction of a trifluoromethyl group imparted superior antifungal activity to the compounds. Notably, compound 3m exhibited the most potent inhibitory effect against Rhizoctonia solani, with an EC50 value of 0.418 µg/mL, which was significantly better than that of the control agent Fenfuram. Meanwhile, compound 6b also demonstrated strong inhibitory activity against Fusarium oxysporum, with an EC50 value of 10.723 µg/mL, likewise markedly outperforming Fenfuram. Further in vivo antifungal experiments confirmed that compounds 3m and 6b achieved satisfactory control efficacy against R. solani and F. oxysporum, respectively. These results indicate promising application prospects for compounds 3m and 6b. Mechanistic investigations involving microscopic observation, SDH enzyme activity assays, and molecular docking provided further insight into the potential mode of action of 3m and 6b. The results indicate that compounds 3m and 6b represent structurally optimized lead candidates for the development of novel, natural product‐derived fungicides.Keywords: antifungal activity, F. oxysporum, natural furan carboxylic acids, R. solani, SARs

Introduction

Plant pathogenic fungi pose a major threat to sustainable agricultural production, causing substantial economic losses to global agriculture each year [1, 2, 3, 4]. For example, rice sheath blight caused by Rhizoctonia solani is one of the “three major rice diseases” and is considered the most widespread and devastating fungal disease in rice‐growing regions worldwide [5]. Fusarium oxysporum, a soil‐borne phytopathogenic fungus, is found ubiquitously across the globe and is capable of causing diverse plant diseases such as leaf spot, Fusarium wilt, and root rot [6]. Alternaria spp. are widely distributed in nature, and as one of the most virulent groups of pathogenic fungi worldwide, it poses a substantial threat to global agricultural production [7]. Fusarium incarnatum has a broad host range, infecting a variety of plants—including muskmelon, cucumber, tree peony, and maize—and causing diseases such as root rot, stem rot, and fruit rot [8]. In this context, chemical fungicides—particularly the new generation of broad‐spectrum succinate dehydrogenase inhibitors (SDHIs)—play a crucial role in crop protection systems [9, 10, 11, 12]. SDHIs primarily target mitochondrial respiratory chain complex II, also known as succinate dehydrogenase (SDH) [13, 14]. By inhibiting SDH activity, these fungicides disrupt the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle and mitochondrial electron transport, thereby suppressing fungal growth [15, 16]. Owing to this unique mode of action, SDHIs have been widely adopted in agricultural disease management, with 24 structurally diverse SDHIs commercialized to date [17, 18]. Structurally, SDHI fungicides consist of three main components: a polar head group, a hydrophobic tail, and an amide bond serving as the linker moiety (Figure 1) [19, 20].

Structural overview of the commercial SDHI fungicide Fenfuram.

However, the single‐target mode of action of SDHIs, combined with their extensive and repeated application, has led to the emergence of resistant fungal strains and raised environmental concerns [21]. Consequently, the development of eco‐friendly alternatives—particularly novel pesticides derived from natural products and greener, sustainable crop protection strategies—has become an urgent priority for safeguarding plant health and maintaining ecological balance [22, 23].

Natural products are biogenic organic compounds derived from natural sources such as plants, microorganisms, and marine organisms [24]. They typically exhibit diverse chemical structures and significant biological activities, which underpin a broad range of pharmacological properties and biological functions [24, 25]. These unique bioactive characteristics make natural products pivotal subjects in modern medicinal chemistry research [26].

Among the array of promising natural pharmacophores, furan occupies a pivotal position. In agriculture, it has garnered widespread attention due to its frequent use as a synthetic intermediate or protecting group in the synthesis of various pharmaceuticals, as well as its broad spectrum of biological activities, including anticancer [27], insecticidal [28], and antifungal [29] activities. Notably, our previous research successfully identified and isolated furan carboxylic acid derivatives with potent nematicidal activity from natural sources, including soil‐antagonistic fungi (e.g., Aspergillus fumigatus 1T‐2) [30] and marine‐derived fungi (e.g., Aspergillus luchuensis hy‐6) [31]. These findings provide valuable lead structures for the development of novel furan‐based pesticides.

According to relevant literature reports [32], both Fenfuram and furan carboxylic acid derivatives bind within the cavity formed by the B, C, and D subunits of the SDH enzyme, exhibiting highly similar spatial binding patterns. In the case of Fenfuram, its benzene ring extends into a hydrophobic pocket surrounded by B/Pro‐202, B/Ile‐251, C/Ile‐77, and C/Trp‐73, while the 2‐methylfuran ring is embedded in another hydrophobic region composed of B/Trp‐205, B/Trp‐206, C/Phe‐64, and C/Trp‐73. In contrast, furan carboxylic acid derivatives introduce an additional 2‐chlorophenyl group into the cavity, which forms π–π stacking interactions with D/Tyr‐128 and Cl–π interactions with B/His‐249. These two additional noncovalent forces are considered key factors responsible for their significantly higher SDH inhibitory activity compared to Fenfuram.

Based on the aforementioned research background, multiple SDHIs containing furan structures have been reported in the literature; however, the modification space for substitutions on the hydrophobic benzene tail of most furan‐based SDHIs remains to be explored. Given that naturally occurring furancarboxylic acids (e.g., 2‐furoic acid and 3‐furoic acid) and their derivatives share high structural similarity with the pharmacophore of the commercial succinate dehydrogenase inhibitor (SDHI) ofurace, this study constructs a previously unreported furan‐benzene molecular scaffold. Compared with previously reported furan‐based SDHIs, the primary difference of this compound series lies in the connection mode of pharmacophores: using natural furancarboxylic acids as starting synthetic materials and selecting Fenfuram as the template for molecular design, this study focuses on the critical “hydrophobic tail” region of SDH inhibitors, introduces benzene ring structures bearing strong electron‐withdrawing groups such as trifluoromethyl (─CF3), and carries out diversified modification on substituents. Therefore, the innovation of this work mainly stems from the selection of starting materials derived from natural products, the unique substitution pattern of the furan–benzene skeleton, as well as the subsequent investigation of structure–activity relationships (SARs) and biological activity. On this basis, this study designed and synthesized a series of furan–benzene skeleton derivatives (Figure 2), conducted SAR analysis and exploration of the action mechanism, and preliminarily clarified the antibacterial potential and mode of action of this class of compounds. This work provides an important theoretical basis for the structural optimization and mechanistic investigation of natural furan skeleton derivatives as potential SDHIs.

The synthetic route to the target compounds is illustrated in Scheme 1. Intermediates 2, 5, and 8 were prepared by reacting the corresponding starting materials with thionyl chloride and were used directly in subsequent steps without further purification. To further evaluate the antifungal efficacy of these highly active compounds, the half‐maximal effective concentration (EC50) was determined for compounds showing superior inhibition rates against R. solani and F. oxysporum (Table S1). Five compounds exhibited significant inhibitory effects against R. solani, with EC50 values below 1 µg/mL, outperforming the reference fungicide Fenfuram (EC50 = 1.103 µg/mL). Notably, compounds 3h and 3m (Figure S1) showed EC50 values of 0.475 and 0.418 µg/mL, respectively—approximately half that of Fenfuram—highlighting their superior efficacy against R. solani. Interestingly, four compounds exhibited potent inhibitory activity against F. oxysporum, with EC50 values below 20 µg/mL—markedly superior to that of Fenfuram (EC50 = 87.379 µg/mL). Among them, compound 6b (Figure S2) exhibited an EC50 value of 10.723 µg/mL, roughly one‐eighth that of Fenfuram, further underscoring its potent antifungal performance.

These findings indicate that this series of target compounds exhibits considerable potential in terms of both broad‐spectrum activity and inhibitory potency, with particularly pronounced efficacy against R. solani and F. oxysporum. They hold significant research value and promising practical application prospects.

Structure−Activity Relationships (SARs)

The antifungal activity and SARs of the target compounds were evaluated (Figure 3) based on the data presented in Tables 1 and S1. The results reveal that the introduction of electron‐withdrawing halogen atoms enhances antifungal efficacy, with compounds bearing a ─CF3 group exhibiting the most pronounced activity. Notably, compounds 3m, 3n, 6b, and 9b demonstrated substantial inhibitory activity against all four plant pathogenic fungi tested, indicating their broad‐spectrum antifungal potential.

Structure–activity relationships of the target compounds.

The results indicate that when halogen atoms (F, Cl, and Br) are introduced at the ortho, meta, and para positions, respectively, the antifungal activity at the para position is significantly superior to that at the ortho and meta positions. Furthermore, at the para position, the influence of different halogen atoms varies, following the order: 4‐Br > 4‐Cl > 4‐F. In contrast, the ortho position exhibits the lowest antifungal activity. When electron‐donating groups (─CH3, ─OCH3) are introduced at the meta and para positions, comparative studies reveal that electron‐withdrawing substituents demonstrate higher inhibitory activity than electron‐donating groups. Interestingly, the introduction of electron‐withdrawing trifluoromethyl (─CF3) groups at the meta and para positions resulted in compound 3m (3‐CF3) exhibiting potent inhibitory activity, with an EC50 value as low as 0.418 µg/mL against R. solani, thereby demonstrating its strong antifungal efficacy. Furthermore, preliminary studies on disubstituted derivatives indicated a decline in inhibitory activity compared to their monosubstituted counterparts.

Among compounds 3a–3q, 3h and 3m exhibited the most pronounced inhibitory activity against R. solani. Building on the SAR insights, structural modifications of the furan ring—exemplified by compounds 6a–6b and 9a–9b— revealed that compound 6b displayed considerable inhibitory activity against F. oxysporum, with an EC50 value of 10.723 µg/mL. These findings underscore the potential of targeted structural optimization in enhancing antifungal efficacy.

In Vivo Antifungal Activities

In vivo experiments were conducted to evaluate the antifungal activity of compounds 3m, 6b, and Fenfuram against R. Solani and F. oxysporum. As shown in Figures 4 and 5, at concentrations of 100 and 200 µg/mL, compound 3m exhibits excellent performance and significantly inhibits the growth of R. solani, with protective efficacies of 47.8% and 97.5%, respectively, outperforming the protective effect of Fenfuram (39.2% and 95.0%) (Figure S3). Meanwhile, at the same concentrations, compound 6b shows a remarkably significant protective effect against F. oxysporum, with protective efficacies of 47.9% and 89.0%, respectively, while the protective efficacies of Fenfuram are only 27.7% and 73.0% (Figure S4). These results indicate that compounds 3m and 6b not only possess potent antifungal activity but also outperform the commercial fungicide Fenfuram in certain aspects, suggesting considerable potential for development and warranting further investigation.

Enzymatic Inhibition Activity of SDH

To elucidate the antifungal mechanism of these furan derivatives, the SDH inhibitory activities of compounds 3m and 6b—which exhibited significant fungicidal efficacy—were evaluated. As shown in Table 2, both compounds demonstrated considerable SDH inhibition. Notably, compound 3m exhibited a strong inhibitory effect, with an IC50 value of 1.004 µg/mL, outperforming the reference fungicide Fenfuram (IC50 = 1.758 µg/mL). Compound 6b also displayed SDH inhibitory activity, with an IC50 value of 9.820 µg/mL. These results are consistent with the observed antifungal efficacy, suggesting that compounds 3m and 6b exhibit SDH inhibitory activity.

SEM Analysis

The ultrastructural morphological changes in the hyphae of R. Solani were examined using scanning electron microscopy (SEM), with the results shown in Figure 6. Untreated hyphae (negative control) displayed a relatively smooth, tubular, and unbranched morphology (Figure 6A). In contrast, treatment with 5 µg/mL Fenfuram, as well as compounds 3m and 6b, induced pronounced morphological alterations, including wrinkling, shrinkage, and coiling of the hyphae (Figures 6B–D). These findings demonstrate that compounds 3m and 6b can disrupt the surface architecture of the mycelium, thereby inhibiting hyphal growth.

Molecular Docking

To further investigate the binding mechanism between the target compounds and succinate dehydrogenase (SDH), molecular docking studies were conducted for compounds 3m, 6b, and Fenfuram. The results are illustrated in Figure 7.

Molecular docking results: Fenfuram (A), 3m (B), and 6b (C).

During molecular docking analysis against the target SDH protein (PDB code: 2FBW), a comprehensive conformational search was performed for each ligand. For the three tested compounds, the docking program generated multiple clusters of binding conformations with very similar docking scores (differing by less than 1.0 kcal/mol), indicating that the binding pocket of succinate dehydrogenase (SDH) possesses high conformational flexibility and can accommodate structurally similar ligands through multiple binding orientations with comparable binding energies. The representative conformations shown in Figure 7 were selected as the lowest‐energy conformation from the most populated cluster of each compound, rather than as a single fixed global minimum‐energy conformation.

Although these three inhibitors share similar structures, they exhibit markedly different binding orientations within the binding pocket of succinate dehydrogenase (SDH) and interact with distinct sets of surrounding amino acid residues.

From the perspective of macro‐structural localization, Fenfuram (Figure 7A) binds to the deeply buried ubiquinone‐binding pocket formed by the SDHC subunit. Within this local pocket, Fenfuram achieves high‐affinity binding via a dual hydrogen bond anchoring strategy: its amide moiety forms strong hydrogen bonds with key residues Ser17 and His42, with bond lengths of 3.01 and 3.12 Å, respectively. Meanwhile, the aromatic scaffold of methomyl is tightly enclosed by a three‐dimensional hydrophobic cavity formed by hydrophobic residues such as Leu106, Ile103, His46, and His98, which further stabilizes the molecular binding conformation through strong van der Waals interactions.

Compound 3m (Figure 7B) binds to the ubiquinone‐binding pocket of the SDHC subunit on the right side of SDH. The ligand is mainly anchored to the binding site via one strong hydrogen bond with a distance of 2.83 Å; specifically, the nitrogen atom on the amide group of the ligand forms a tight interaction with the backbone carbonyl oxygen of Gly46(P). Meanwhile, the aromatic skeleton of the ligand and the terminal trifluoromethyl (─CF3) group are enclosed in a strongly hydrophobic pocket formed jointly by residues including Leu20, Leu43, Leu49, Leu51, Gly47, Ser50, His46, and His98. These residues tightly enclose the ligand via extensive van der Waals interactions, which act synergistically with the core hydrogen bond to collectively maintain the high‐affinity stable conformation of 3m within the target pocket.

Compound 6b (Figure 7C) binds inside the SDHA flavoprotein subunit at the top of the SDH complex. The ligand stabilizes itself at the binding site mainly through one strong hydrogen bond with a bond length of 3.25 Å, formed between the carbonyl oxygen atom on its amide moiety and the nitrogen atom on the side chain of residue Arg63(B) at the bottom of the substrate pocket. Meanwhile, the aromatic ring skeleton of the ligand and the terminal trifluoromethyl (─CF3) group are surrounded by a network of hydrophobic residues distributed at the interface of subunits A and B; the residues involved in van der Waals interactions include Phe51, Ile196, Trp515, Val520, Glu521, Leu50, and Thr517. This binding mode, which combines a single strong hydrogen bond anchoring with synergistic stabilization from extensive surrounding van der Waals interactions, enables 6b to effectively occupy the target binding pocket.

To further evaluate the binding affinity of these compounds to SDH, docking scores were calculated and are summarized in Table S2. The molecular binding energies of compounds 3m and 6b (−8.2 and −8.1 kcal/mol, respectively) are significantly superior to those of Fenfuram (−7.2 kcal/mol). This indicates that the structural modifications of 3m and 6b not only retain binding ability but also greatly enhance binding affinity and stability toward the pocket, and both compounds exhibit favorable binding potential at the theoretical calculation level.

Conclusions

In this study, 21 target compounds based on a furan–benzene scaffold were designed, synthesized, and structurally characterized. Systematic evaluation of their antifungal activity against four representative phytopathogenic fungi revealed significant broad‐spectrum inhibitory effects. SAR analysis demonstrated that derivatives containing a trifluoromethyl (–CF3) group exhibited the most potent antifungal activity, confirming –CF3 as a key pharmacophore. Notably, compounds 3m and 6b showed the strongest inhibitory effects against R. solani and F. oxysporum, respectively, with activity markedly superior to that of the positive control Fenfuram. Preliminary mechanistic investigations indicated that compounds 3m and 6b suppress fungal growth by disrupting mycelial structural integrity. Further succinate dehydrogenase (SDH) inhibition assays and molecular docking studies confirmed that both compounds share a mode of action similar to Fenfuram, functioning through SDH binding. These results suggest that compounds 3m and 6b function as SDH inhibitors. Overall, the findings highlight that the introduction of benzene rings bearing different functional groups—particularly –CF3—into natural furancarboxylic acid derivatives effectively enhances antifungal potency. Future efforts will focus on structural optimization of the furan–benzene scaffold to develop candidate compounds with improved activity.

It should be specifically noted that succinate dehydrogenase is highly conserved across fungi, plants, and mammals, and the species selectivity of the compounds reported in this study has not been directly verified through relevant experiments. Structural analysis indicates that the natural furancarboxylic acid scaffold may form a hydrogen bond orientation distinct from that of traditional synthetic analogs in the fungal binding pocket, providing structural theoretical insights into potential selectivity. Meanwhile, the representative compounds did not exhibit significant phytotoxicity (Figure S5), suggesting possible selectivity for plant succinate dehydrogenase (SDH). However, all the above conclusions are based on indirect evidence and are insufficient to confirm selectivity. Therefore, the core contribution of this study lies in providing a novel class of SDH‐inhibiting chemotypes based on the natural furan scaffold, along with their systematic SARs, while rigorous selectivity evaluation and safety verification remain key directions for subsequent research.

In Vitro Antifungal Activities

The in vitro antifungal activities of all target compounds were evaluated using the mycelial growth inhibition method. Four plant pathogenic fungi—R. solani, F. incarnatum, F. oxysporum, and A. alternata—were selected as test strains, with the commercial fungicide Fenfuram as the positive control. The detailed experimental method was shown in the Supporting Information.

In Vivo Antifungal Activities

The in vivo fungicidal activity of compounds 3m, 6b, and Fenfuram was evaluated using an apple detached fruit bioassay, as described in the literature [33]. The detailed experimental method was shown in the Supporting Information.

SDH Activity Detection Kit

The SDH enzyme activity was evaluated using the SDH detection kit (Solarbio, BC0950) [10, 34, 35]. R. solani was selected as the test fungus. Compounds 3m and 6b were evaluated, and Fenfuram was used as the positive control. The detailed experimental method was shown in the Supporting Information [36].

SEM Analysis

The effects of compounds 3m, 6b, and Fenfuram on the hyphal morphology of R. Solani were characterized by SEM. The detailed experimental method was shown in the Supporting Information.

Molecular Docking

The crystal structure of SDH (PDB ID: 2FBW) was retrieved from the Protein Data Bank (https://www.rcsb.org) [37, 38]. Molecular docking was performed using CB‐Dock2 (https://cadd.labshare.cn/cb‐dock2/php/index.php) [39], an AutoDock Vina‐based tool for molecular docking [40]. CB‐Dock2 employs AutoDock Vina to automatically identify potential binding sites, calculate their central coordinates and dimensions, and customize the docking box size based on the ligand structure [41]. The docking results were visualized using LigPlot+ software.
