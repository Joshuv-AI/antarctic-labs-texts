The Tendon‐to‐Bone Seam: A Transtendinous Approach to Rotator Cuff Repair
Authors: James Tyler Frix, Ryan Serbin, Howard W. Harris, Marc R. Labbe, Patrick M. Connor
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis technique article describes the tendon‐to‐bone seam technique, a knotless, transtendinous approach to arthroscopic rotator cuff repair. The technique employs a single‐use deployment device preloaded with multiple PEEK anchors interconnected with a single polyethylene #2 suture, enabling the surgeon to create a continuous “seam” of independent fixation points along the rotator cuff footprint. By alternating arm position rather than portal placement, the surgeon can often access the entire footprint for repair through the standard anterolateral arthroscopic portal. The tendon‐to‐bone seam technique allows for medial and lateral row configurations without knot tying or complex suture management. The accompanying illustrations and video show the key steps and principles of this technique, which aims to simplify arthroscopic repair, enhance footprint coverage, and potentially reduce tendon stress by spreading contact forces across the entire footprint.

Rotator cuff tears are increasingly common, affecting 30% to 50% of patients over 50 years old. Consequently, rotator cuff repair (RCR) is one of the most common orthopaedic procedures performed in the USA, with an estimated 1,120,000 repairs annually. 1 This number is exponentially increasing, with a growth rate of nearly 12% per year. 2 Despite its prevalence, RCR carries high failure to heal rates, long immobilization and rehabilitation times, and painful recovery. Failure rates of rotator cuff healing range from 20% to 40%, with some studies reporting 90%. 1

Recent literature shows that double‐row (DR) and DR‐transosseous‐equivalent techniques have lower retear rates than single‐row repairs and that knotless DR‐transosseous‐equivalent techniques may reduce medial failures at the musculotendinous junction compared with knotted techniques, possibly by minimizing tissue strangulation. 3 , 4 , 5 Even with these improvements, Young et al. 1 estimated a 26% postoperative retear rate using modern techniques, most commonly from tendon pull‐through, with a 38.5% of retear patients experiencing clinical failure. Notably, healing failure and clinical failure are not synonymous in RCR. Patients can achieve good clinical outcomes despite incomplete healing, with satisfaction of 86% to 100%. 1 However, complete rotator cuff healing after arthroscopic repair yields superior clinical results and patient‐reported outcomes. 5

Failed RCR increases health care costs through additional treatments and revision surgeries, which have higher failure rates and worse outcomes. 1 , 6 Recent analysis estimates that failed primary arthroscopic RCRs in the USA in 2022 added nearly $440 million in short‐term health care expenditures. 1 A 5% improvement in successful structural healing of primary RCRs is estimated to reduce annual health care costs by over $84 million. 1 Despite this, RCR techniques and subsequent outcomes have not significantly improved in nearly 2 decades.

SURGICAL TECHNIQUE

The Tendon Seam (Integrity Orthopaedics, Minneapolis, MN) system consists of an FD‐ approved single‐use deployment device and disposable magazines containing four or eight 2‐mm‐diameter PEEK anchors with a preloaded ultrahigh molecular weight polyethylene #2 suture (Figure 1). The structure of the repair consists of an array of anchors linked by a continuous repair suture. Anchors are implanted typically 5 to 7 mm apart, and the suture spanning between each pair of anchors functions as a single, compressible stitch. Thus, a 4‐anchor array generates 3 stitches, whereas an 8‐anchor array produces 7 stitches. The system allows for transtendinous RCR using these multiple interconnected yet independent fixation points. The WIRB‐Copernicus Group Institutional Review Board tracking number is 20251035.

Patient Positioning and Portal Placement

The patient is positioned per surgeon preference. The anatomic landmarks of the acromion, distal clavicle, acromioclavicular joint, and coracoid are palpated and marked. A standard posterior portal is created for diagnostic glenohumeral arthroscopy. If a rotator cuff tear is confirmed, and no glenohumeral‐specific additional procedures are necessary, the midlateral subacromial portal is then created without placing an obligate, anterior rotator‐interval portal. This midlateral portal is placed approximately 4 to 5 cm lateral to the lateral border of the acromion with a superior projection. This enables the cannula to pass through the subdeltoid plane 1 to 2 cm from the lateral edge of the acromion, thus allowing efficient bursal and cuff debridement and greater access for tuberosity preparation.

Tendon Mobilization and Preparation

The rotator cuff tear is evaluated and its mobility is assessed using a looped grasper. If the tendon does not reach the lateral footprint, a traction suture is placed through the tendon while releasing subacromial adhesions, the rotator interval, superior glenohumeral ligament SGHL, middle glenohumeral ligament, coracohumeral ligament, and underlying glenohumeral capsule. Once adequate mobility is achieved, the tuberosity is conservatively debrided with a motorized shaver, preserving the cortical rim of the footprint. The goal is to enable anatomic tendon reduction to its footprint for transtendinous repair.

Rotator Cuff Repair

To determine the ideal anterosuperior portal position for the deployment device, a spinal needle is placed just medial to the anterolateral corner of the acromion and viewed from the posterior portal. The arm is taken through range of motion to ensure adequate access of the spinal needle to all footprint areas from this position (Figure 2). The goal is to position the device as perpendicular to the greater tuberosity as possible throughout the repair. For large or massive tears where the posterior aspect of the repair is difficult to access anterosuperiorly, the deployment device may be introduced through a posterosuperior portal. In these cases, the senior author prefers completing the posterior repair first before using the standard anterosuperior portal for the remainder of the repair (Table 1).

Pearls and Pitfalls

Before repair begins, the now mobilized rotator cuff tendon is reduced to its anatomic footprint. This can be performed with a grasper or a traction suture. Alternatively, the deployment device punch pin can be used to spear the tendon slightly lateral to the musculotendinous junction and reduce it laterally down to the bone (Video 1). It is important to ensure the tendon is fully or slightly over‐reduced to guarantee adequate coverage of the anatomic footprint.

Once the rotator cuff tendon is reduced, the transtendinous repair commences. The shoulder is forward flexed and adducted to access the anterior‐most medial anchor site (Figure 2C). The senior author employs a medial seam and lateral zigzag technique beginning with the medial seam, although different suture orientations can be used per surgeon preference (Figure 3). The deployment device is inserted and positioned at or near the articular margin as anatomy allows. The punch pin (carrying the anchor guide tube) is advanced through the rotator cuff tendon and gently tapped into bone, ensuring it is fully seated. The trigger is squeezed to initiate retraction of the pin, but the guide tube is left in place to maintain the pilot hole in the tuberosity. Once the pin is fully retracted, an anchor is loaded from the cartridge into the breech. The pin is then advanced once more to pass the anchor through the guide tube into the tuberosity. The trigger is squeezed again to retract the pin and guide tube, leaving the suture anchor behind. The suture is initially tensioned to rotate and seat the anchor under the cortical edge of the footprint and then finally tensioned, and the central locking suture is then pulled to lock the suture (Figure 4).

This process is repeated by moving the arm to access the next anchor position from the same portal (Figure 2). The shoulder is adducted and extended to work posteriorly along the medial footprint, abducted to access the lateral footprint, and forward elevated to work anteriorly across the lateral zigzag. The goal is to place a medial seam of anchors with sutures perpendicular to the superior cuff fibers and restore the cuff footprint on the tuberosity. The lateral zigzag is completed similarly with anchors along the lateral footprint to compress the entire cuff to the anatomic footprint. For small‐ and medium‐sized tears, a single 8‐anchor magazine is typically used (Figure 3B), with large or massive tears requiring more fixation points. The senior author often employs a zigzag lateral row to improve tendon‐bone contact area (Figure 3B).

Partial‐Thickness Tears

For partial‐thickness tears, the repair can be performed without completing the tear. For bursal‐sided tears, a grasper is used to reduce the tendon to the bone while placing the anchors as described above. For articular sided tears, the tear is first visualized within the joint to characterize the amount of footprint involvement. If the exposed tuberosity exceeds 50% of the medial to lateral distance, a transtendinous repair can be performed by inserting the 2 mm pointed punch pin through the cuff percutaneously after using a spinal needle to localize the trajectory. The tip of the device penetrates the tendon and is used to reduce the torn fibers laterally. The initial anchors are placed under direct visualization from the glenohumeral joint, reducing the tendon to the medial aspect of the footprint (Figure 5). Additional anchors can then be placed from the subacromial space to reinforce the lateral tendon, compressing the delaminated tendon back to the bone.

Postoperative Protocol

A “progressive” rehabilitation protocol has been implemented for most patients. This “progressive” protocol involves the following:•Weaning from the sling as tolerated•Active‐assisted range of motion as tolerated•Daily activities as tolerated while avoiding shoulder abduction and extension, lifting and quick, jerky, ballistic or uncontrolled shoulder movements or activities•Formal strengthening is initiated at ∼3 months

A “traditional,” 3‐phase postoperative rehabilitation protocol with sling immobilization for approximately 6 weeks is used in revision situations or massive or chronic tears with other risk factors or poor tissue quality. This “traditional” protocol may also be used for smokers, workers’ compensation, or elderly patients. The decision to follow a “progressive” versus “traditional” rehabilitation protocol is made intraoperatively by the surgeon based on the quality of the repair and other patient‐specific factors.

DISCUSSION

The tendon‐to‐bone seam technique offers a transtendinous approach to RCR. It enables multiple independent points of fixation throughout the repair without relying on suture management or knot tying (Table 2). The ability to independently tension and lock each anchor allows tailored compression across variable tendon quality, and the distributed compressive forces may reduce stress at the tendon‐suture interface and thus potentially aid in healing. Furthermore, this technique allows repair of partial‐thickness tears without tear completion, allowing preservation of native tissue and tendon integrity. Each of these points contributes to our senior author's use of a progressive rehabilitation protocol in many patients, allowing weaning from the sling as tolerated postoperative. Although this technique is beneficial in many cases, it should likely not be relied upon for tendon‐to‐tendon repair or for margin convergence; a hybrid repair can be considered for those patients. Finally, additional studies are needed to evaluate this method and its clinical effectiveness. Longer‐term studies with advanced imaging are underway to quantify retear rates and biomechanical studies are currently accessing optimal anchor configurations.
