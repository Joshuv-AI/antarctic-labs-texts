Examining the Effectiveness of Japanese Local and National Government Information Sources on COVID-19 Vaccine Uptake
Authors: Marina Richey, Satoko Yasuno
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:The effectiveness of different levels of government in generating compliance with public health initiatives during pandemics is not currently known. As public health is administered at both local and national levels, this study aimed to determine which government level is most effective in motivating the public so that resources can be efficiently allocated.Methods:The usage rates and subsequent vaccine uptake of the Japanese national government’s main COVID-19 vaccination website, “Vaccine Navi,” were compared to Japanese local governments’ websites and mailed paper flyers. Data were collected through an online survey of 1088 participants matched to national demographic data asking about their experience with Vaccine Navi and local resources in receiving information about the COVID-19 vaccine. T-tests and ordinary least squares regression modeling were used for statistical analysis.Results:The information resources created by local Japanese governments to aid in vaccine rollout were used significantly more than the national government’s Vaccine Navi. There was no statistically significant difference in the number of vaccines received after a local and national information source was used.Conclusions:Local health resources are beneficial to distributing information during health crises, and utilizing local public health offices is advantageous for public health guidance during pandemics.

Introduction

We do not know the effectiveness of public health initiatives of different levels of government in response to pandemics. Local and national governments have varying roles in public health initiatives based on the constitution of their country. Japan, for example, has a unitary governmental structure, and the national government has approximately total control over the local governments. However, local governments in Japan implement a large portion of national health expenditures and control how funds are utilized in their locality. Additionally, public opinion about the competence and trustworthiness of levels of government varies as citizens have significantly more contact with the local government and are often more trusted than the national government.

Both levels of government are crucial in public health, but their differences in implementation and effectiveness are largely ignored in the literature on public health effectiveness. Research shows that local public health departments are effective in the United States.[1] However, several assessments have shown that we do not have enough information to determine which level of government is more effective in public health provision due to a lack of consensus on what measurement system would be used to assess the viability of each level.[2] The vaccine outreach during the COVID-19 pandemic provides a clear way to measure effectiveness due to the need for massive vaccinations.

In Japan, either level of government could be effective in vaccine outreach. The national government’s Ministry of Health, Labor, and Welfare (MHLW) oversees the universal health insurance system. Therefore, the national government has detailed information and ways to reach the entirety of the citizenry on this insurance system. With more funding, advanced technology, and media connections, the national government also has more resources than the local governments and can potentially use these resources to mobilize public health initiatives better.

However, Japanese local governments implement the national government’s regulations and provide residents with public health centers. They also manage the local residence registration system called hokenjo, which keeps track of everyone’s address in their municipality. They use this system to frequently mail paper flyers to inform residents about town initiatives, including public health information.

Regarding Japan’s COVID-19 response, the MHLW initiated many critical disease prevention protocols while the local governments could only make recommendations and had no legal power. Crucially, both the national and local governments provided sources of information on COVID-19 vaccination sites, with “Vaccine Navi” as a national website for citizens to learn where and how to get vaccinated and each municipality promoting vaccinations through their respective websites, paper flyers, and newsletters. Thus, it is plausible that either level will be effective in vaccine promotion.

Regarding federalism, Japan is a unitary state in which the national government has nearly total power over the local governments.[3] Japan has 47 prefectures, each with numerous municipalities. The national government creates the most important legislation that local governments carry out without the ability to enact much independently, and the local governments have very limited autonomy. Thus, the local governments rely heavily on the national government. A significant portion of national funds is designated for local governments to use; in 2020, 14.8% of Japan’s federal budget was for local allocation tax grants.[4] Although the government strictly controls and limits the power of each local government, the local governments are the major source of the distribution of spending. In 2020, the national government disbursed 44.0%, and the local governments disbursed 56% of the 223 trillion-yen net total of national and local government expenditures.[5] Thus, the local governments spent most of Japan’s expenditures.

Trust in levels of government by citizens is also distinguished between local and national governments. Public trust in the local government is continuously rated higher than that of the national government, which could be attributed to the greater frequency of contact with municipal and prefectural governments compared to the national government.[6] Trust matters for vaccine uptake in Japan; Okada, Okuhara, Goto, and Kiuchi (2023)[7] found that trust in physicians is the most important predictor in receiving a COVID-19 vaccine. Approximately 42% of those surveyed contacted the municipal government at least once every few months, compared to only 7.3 for the national government.[8] With the increased trust in local governments but an established unitary governmental structure favoring national legislation and power of the national government, which level has a greater connection to the public is difficult to predict without empirical evidence.

Japanese public health is administered at both the national and local levels. At the national level, Japan has a universal healthcare system that all citizens and noncitizen residents can access. The national government runs this system under the MHLW, which oversees almost all aspects and assumes full responsibility. The national government manages the fee schedule, establishes regulations, and subsidizes local governments. In 2019, health expenditures were 10.74% of Japan’s GDP,[9] and per capita was $4360,[10] showing the national government’s vast power in controlling the public health insurance system. The national government establishes healthcare safety regulations to guarantee universal standards, and hospitals are required by law to be non-profit and run by physicians. Because the local governments have little unique legal power outside of that provided by the national government, the national government has more power over health policies in their ability to create regulations on a federal level.

In this system, however, the localities also have public health responsibilities. The prefectural governments implement regulations and oversee the management of medical facilities, along with roles outlined by the Medical Care Act. Public health clinics are under either prefectures or municipal jurisdiction and serve to provide public health services to address local concerns for residents. As of 2019, 472 local health clinics across Japan serve on matters concerning promoting health activities, vital statistics, and research among other responsibilities outlined in the Community Health Act.[11] In addition to public health centers, around 83% of medical facilities are privately owned by medical corporations or physicians, and healthcare is free to access; thus, patients have access to all facilities.[12] Municipalities that do not have local health clinics are required to provide public health services such as checkups and health education. Thus, both of Japan’s government levels are deeply entwined with public health initiatives.

Japanese response to the COVID-19 pandemic

The COVID-19 pandemic originated in Wuhan, China, and quickly spread around the globe, with the WHO declaring COVID-19 as a pandemic on March 11, 2020.[13] Japan was one of the first countries with a confirmed case, as on January 16, 2020, an infected person was identified that had recently returned from Wuhan.[14] COVID-19 cases on a cruise ship after visiting China led to an increased response to minimize the spread of the virus to Japan, such as school closings and travel restrictions to China and South Korea.[15] Additional concerns were raised as Japan has the largest percentage of the population being 65 years of age or older at 28.2%, and with COVID-19 having disproportionately higher mortality rates with increased age, there was a high need for a vaccine.[16]

After the first cases of COVID-19 in Japan, the MHLW published recommendations and guidelines for contacting a coronavirus consultation center to receive medical advice. On February 25, 2020, the MHLW created the Basic Policies for Novel Coronavirus Disease Control, a fundamental action plan for measures to be taken.[17] Prime Minister Abe ordered a national state of emergency on April 16.[18] Additionally, the national government established strict entry protocols for foreign visitors to prevent new variants from entering Japan. These critical preventative measures conveyed that the national government had the key responsibility to control the spread of COVID-19. Thus, it was possible for it to be the most effective level of government in utilizing resources to promote the COVID-19 vaccines because the public looked to it for guidance on the pandemic.

Additionally, the local government’s power was specified by the Act on Special Measures against Novel Influenza, which allowed prefectural governors to request people stay home and close schools and nonessential businesses but was limited to requests.[19] This law said that the government could not legally enforce and mandate any regulations and changes and was dependent on the public’s cooperation to fight the pandemic. There is also no penalty for not conforming to requests, which minimizes the effectiveness of declaring a state of emergency. In this situation, effective health communication to gain compliance with public health initiatives was crucial.

The Japanese national government aggressively pursued vaccine development in response to the pandemic. The first COVID-19 mRNA vaccine approved by Japan was by Pfizer–BioNTech on February 14, 2021.[20] The first rounds of the vaccine started with medical workers in February and slowly increased in availability.[21] The vaccine was administered in various local clinics, vaccination centers, and hospitals, and appointments could be made by phone or online reservation. Residents could find local vaccination sites by accessing their municipality websites and newsletters. In addition, the national government and the MHLW created the Vaccine Navi website, which finds local vaccination sites and has a phone number and website to make reservations. Vaccination sites are under local governments’ administration in accordance with the role of the local government in public health and the healthcare system.

Although Japan’s healthcare is led by the national government and administered by the local government, with the COVID-19 pandemic, both levels conducted vaccination efforts and played roles in vaccine rollout. With these disparities in information sources, power, funding, and trust, these levels of government may have differential abilities to gain compliance with public health initiatives. Thus, it is important to empirically establish which level of government had a more effective implementation of campaigns to promote COVID-19 vaccinations to help make better decisions on resource allocation during future public health emergencies.

This empirical question leads to the research question: did the local or national government have more success in promoting COVID-19 vaccinations among Japanese people? Regarding governmental outreach for health policies, there were either the local governments’ websites and low-tech traditional communication channels, such as newsletters and flyers and municipal websites, as well as the national government’s Vaccine Navi website. The hypotheses test whether the local governments’ websites and newsletters or the national government’s Vaccine Navi had greater reach and public usage for obtaining information on COVID-19 vaccination sites. This research compares the outreach of each information source. Additionally, there are hypothesis tests over whether the local government’s website and flyer or the national government’s Vaccine Navi led to greater COVID-19 vaccination rates by comparing the effectiveness of each information source.

As Vaccine Navi was the national government’s only information provision channel on COVID-19 vaccines, its usage is a proxy for all national government information provisions on the vaccines in Japan. This may lead to greater usage and vaccine uptake as Vaccine Navi was the national government’s vaccine source, and the national government is responsible for health regulation. Alternatively, the local government has a large role in community health, as with the local hokenjo system of family registrations for local government activities and greater general trust among residents. As the local governments have multiple avenues to connect to citizens, they may be more effective in having greater outreach and promoting vaccines using the preestablished trust with the community.

Materials and Methods

The data came from an online survey that was matched to nationally represented census data for age, gender, income, and geographic distribution, ensuring rural, suburban, and urban areas are covered. The sample was taken in early 2023 and consisted of 1088 participants. All prefectures were represented. The sample population came from volunteers from an online survey website, matched to representative data, and paid to take the survey. The respondents were given an informed consent form and completed it. The survey research project was exempted from Chuo University’s institutional review ethics board.

Hypotheses 1a and 1b were tested with the question, “Where is the source of information on where to receive the COVID-19 vaccine?” Possible answers were municipal websites, municipal flyers and newsletters, friends and family, internet searches, Vaccine Navi, other websites, and other sources. Multiple answers could be selected.

The dependent variable for the tests for Hypotheses 2a and 2b concerns the impact of different governmental information provision systems on how often the respondent gets vaccinated. It was tested with the question, “Have you received the COVID-19 vaccine?” Possible answers ranged from never to six or more times.

Figure 1 shows the distribution of the answers to this question on the number of times vaccinated. Interestingly, excluding those unvaccinated, the graph is approximately normally distributed. Around 15% of the sample had not received any vaccine. The modal number of times vaccinated is four, which matches the recommended vaccine schedule in this timeframe. The vaccine schedule has evolved over time, but at no point was one shot recommended, and Figure 1 shows that a trivial amount of the sample only received one vaccine. Six and above vaccinations were reserved for those immunocompromised at the time of the survey in Japan, which is why less than 1% received six shots.

Results

Figure 21 shows the mean and 95% confidence intervals for usage levels of different sources of information on COVID-19 vaccinations in Japan. Figure 2 shows that these data support hypothesis 1b, as the municipal government information sources had statistically significantly greater usage than Vaccine Navi. A large majority, around 68% (95% CI = 65%, 71%), used municipal websites. Municipal flyers were used by 29% (95% CI = 26%, 32%) of the participants, and Vaccine Navi was used by only 3% of the sample (95% CI = 2%, 4%). As can be seen in Figure 2, the municipal website, municipal flyer, and Vaccine Navi did not overlap with a 95% confidence interval. T-tests showed a statically significant difference comparing the means of municipal website use and municipal flyer use (P < 0.000), as well as when comparing the mean of municipal flyer use and Vaccine Navi use (P < 0.000). In sum, a majority used municipal websites, many used municipal flyers, and few used Vaccine Navi.

Box plot of mean and 95% confidence intervals for the source of information on COVID-19 vaccinations

Newsletters, friends and family, and internet searches were approximately equal at around 30%, and only 4% used other websites. Municipal flyers, family and friends, and internet searches had overlapping 95% confidence intervals, and the difference in usage was not statistically different. Vaccine Navi, other websites, and other sources of information were only marginally used, with no statistical difference. Comparing the governmental sources of information, the local government had significantly greater success in public outreach. Even low-tech information, such as flyers, was comparable to other major sources of information.

Figure 3 shows the mean and 95% confidence intervals for the number of times vaccinated by which source of information on COVID-19 vaccinations was used by the respondent. Figure 3 shows that these data do not support either hypothesis 2Ha and 2Hb as t-tests comparing means of times vaccinated and vaccine information source used show no statistically significant difference. A t-test comparing the mean number of times vaccinated for those who used the municipal website with a mean of 3.56 times (95% CI = 3.48, 3.63) and for those who used Vaccine Navi with a mean of 3.48 times (95% CI = 3.12, 3.84) had a P value of 0.653. Similarly, a t-test comparing the mean number of times vaccinated for those who used the municipal flyer with a mean of 3.60 times (95% CI = 3.49, 3.72) and for those who used Vaccine Navi had a P value of 0.739. There were little differences between the number of times vaccinated and the source of information used. The 95% confidence interval showed no statistically significant difference when comparing vaccination rates using municipal websites, municipal flyers, and Vaccine Navi. Municipal information sources had slightly more success for vaccinations than those who used family and friends and internet searches.

Respondents from different regions, males and females, showed no statistically significant difference in the times they were vaccinated from each source.

Discussion

This research compared information sources about COVID-19 vaccination sites from the municipal and national government levels and the effectiveness and outreach of each. The municipal website was the most utilized by a significant portion, and municipal flyers had less outreach but were comparable to other popular information sources. A very small proportion of the sample used the national government’s website, Vaccine Navi. However, there was no statistically significant difference in the number of times vaccinated for those who used the local versus national information sources. Users of both governments got the recommended number of vaccinations. In sum, the local government was more effective at outreach, and local government resources should be used to gain compliance during pandemics.

Future implications of this research signify the need to analyze public health information outreach and the effectiveness of various methods. Vaccine Navi was not successful in terms of usage, but it was successful for those who utilized this website. Thus, the Japanese national government should further promote the website and allow this beneficial information source to be useful to more people. On the contrary, municipal websites were very successful in their usage and effectiveness and should be continued for future health crises. Paper flyers and newsletters were shown to be as effective as more technologically sophisticated information sources and are an affordable, effective method of communicating public health information.
