Electrical storm after percutaneous coronary intervention for subacute anterior myocardial infarction with heart failure, treated with multidisciplinary therapy
Authors: Yasuhito Nakata, Shigeyasu Tsuda
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryElectrical storm (ES) following acute coronary syndrome is rare but often fatal. A man in his early 70s experienced syncope and dyspnoea while playing golf, approximately 2 weeks after experiencing back pain. He was diagnosed with subacute anterior myocardial infarction based on pulmonary congestion, abnormal anterior wall movement and negative T waves in the precordial leads. Percutaneous coronary intervention with drug-eluting stent implantation was performed from the left main trunk to the proximal left anterior descending artery. On days 4–5 of hospitalisation, he developed recurrent ventricular fibrillation (VF) consistent with ES. High-rate pacing, deep sedation with mechanical ventilation and intra-aortic balloon pumping were initiated. To achieve more physiological pacing in the setting of severe left ventricular dysfunction, a coronary sinus lead was placed; however, ES developed shortly after catheter placement and required veno-arterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation. Continuous VF, unresponsive to multiple direct current shocks, was finally terminated by direct-current shock after landiolol administration. Subsequent percutaneous stellate ganglion blocks stabilised the arrhythmia, allowing successful weaning from mechanical circulatory support.

Background

An electrical storm (ES) occurring after acute coronary syndrome is rare but potentially fatal. ES represents an electrically and physiologically unstable state characterised by recurrent ventricular arrhythmias, requiring rapid and multimodal therapeutic intervention. Although ES is generally rare, episodes that occur >48 hours after the onset of myocardial infarction may be associated with a worse prognosis. This case illustrates the therapeutic challenges of managing late-onset ES and highlights the potential usefulness of mechanical circulatory support (MCS) and sympathetic blockade.

Case presentation

A man in his early 70s with a history of diabetes mellitus and chronic kidney disease presented with syncope and dyspnoea while playing golf. He had experienced back pain for approximately 2 weeks before admission and had visited a local clinic, but no definitive diagnosis had been established.

On admission, his Glasgow Coma Scale score was E4V5M6. His heart rate was 99 beats/min, blood pressure was 112/82 mmHg, respiratory rate was 18 breaths/min, oxygen saturation was 95% on room air and body temperature was 36.5°C. Chest radiography revealed pulmonary congestion (figure 1A), and the patient was classified as Killip class II.

Serial chest X-ray. (A) On admission, bilateral pulmonary congestion is observed. (B) After weaning from extracorporeal membrane oxygenation, the patient developed acute heart failure, which was subsequently managed with diuretics and standard heart failure therapy, leading to gradual improvement. (C) At discharge, pulmonary congestion had resolved, and the implanted subcutaneous implantable cardioverter defibrillator (ICD) is visible following recovery of the general condition.

On admission, laboratory testing revealed a high-sensitivity troponin I level of 1839 pg/mL, which increased to 2570 pg/mL the following day. The peak creatine kinase (CK) level was 214 U/L, and the peak creatine kinase-MB (CK-MB) level was 10 ng/mL on hospital day 2. B-type natriuretic peptide was elevated at 797.5 pg/mL. The C-reactive protein (CRP) level was 0.85 mg/dL on admission and increased to 1.71 mg/dL on hospital day 3. The serum potassium level was within the normal range (3.95 mmol/L). The venous lactate level on admission was 2.5 mmol/L. Transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated hypokinesis of the anterior wall and moderate mitral regurgitation (MR), with the left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) visually estimated at approximately 30%. As this was the patient’s first visit to our institution, previous echocardiographic findings were unavailable.

ECG demonstrated widespread negative T waves in the precordial leads without pathological Q waves (figure 2A). Based on the clinical history and these findings, a diagnosis of subacute anterior myocardial infarction approximately 2 weeks after symptom onset was made.

Serial ECGs. (A) ECG on admission showed sinus rhythm at 95 beats/min with first-degree atrioventricular block, left anterior fascicular block and negative T waves in leads I, aVL and precordial leads. (B) Recurrent ventricular fibrillation and tachycardia were observed for about 90 min after returning to the intensive care unit following percutaneous coronary intervention. (C) ECG at discharge demonstrated stable sinus rhythm (80 beats/min) with persistent negative T waves in the precordial leads.

Coronary angiography was performed 4 hours after admission. It revealed severe stenosis extending from the left main trunk to the proximal left anterior descending artery, whereas no significant stenosis was observed in the other coronary arteries. Re-perfusion was achieved 4.5 hours after admission. A drug-eluting stent was implanted at the lesion site, and final Thrombolysis In Myocardial Infarction (TIMI) grade 3 flow was obtained (figure 3). No marked ECG changes were observed before or after reperfusion. The patient remained haemodynamically stable and was transferred from the intensive care unit (ICU) to the general ward on hospital day 2. On hospital day 4, the first episode of ventricular tachycardia (VT) after admission was observed. As the arrhythmia terminated spontaneously, intravenous amiodarone was initiated prophylactically at approximately 10 µg/kg/min and reduced to 5 µg/kg/min after 6 hours. However, ventricular fibrillation (VF) occurred on hospital day 5. As the frequency of VT and VF increased thereafter, repeat coronary angiography was performed to exclude ongoing myocardial ischaemia; however, no new significant stenosis or stent-related complications were identified. During the same session, a temporary pacemaker was inserted for overdrive pacing and backup support. The pacing lead was placed at the right ventricular apex, with a backup rate of 60 beats/min in ventricular pacing, ventricular sensing, and inhibited (VVI) pacing mode. In addition, intravenous magnesium sulphate was initiated at 2 mEq/h.

Coronary angiography and percutaneous coronary intervention. Left: emergency coronary angiography demonstrated severe stenosis with contrast delay at the proximal segment of the LAD (#6). Right: following plain old balloon angioplasty, intravascular ultrasound imaging was performed. Plaque was observed from the proximal LAD extending into the LMT. A crossover stent was deployed from the LMT to the LAD, achieving good stent apposition and complete resolution of the contrast delay on final angiography. On hospital day 6, systolic hypotension of approximately 70–90 mmHg persisted despite continuous norepinephrine infusion at 0.03–0.08 µg/kg/min, and severe left ventricular dysfunction was present. Therefore, intra-aortic balloon pumping (IABP) and coronary sinus pacing were planned for circulatory support. A coronary sinus lead was positioned within the main coronary sinus to achieve more physiological pacing in the setting of severe left ventricular dysfunction. The lead was inserted via the left internal jugular vein. However, ES developed immediately after catheter placement, before effective coronary sinus pacing could be established, and was refractory to repeated defibrillation and pharmacological therapy. Arterial blood gas analysis immediately before deterioration showed a pH of 7.33 and a lactate level of 1.9 mmol/L. As antiarrhythmic therapy, intravenous lidocaine (100 mg bolus) was administered in the catheterisation laboratory, followed by continuous infusion of nifekalant at approximately 6 μg/kg/min. The ongoing amiodarone infusion was discontinued. High-rate pacing was also attempted but was ineffective. As ventricular arrhythmias persisted despite these treatments, veno-arterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (VA-ECMO) was initiated. After returning to the ICU, continuous lidocaine infusion was started at approximately 9 μg/kg/min. Landiolol was initiated with a loading infusion of 100 μg/kg/min for 1 min and then titrated between 5 μg/kg/min and 40 μg/kg/min; during VT/VF episodes, the dose was increased to 100 μg/kg/min. After administration of landiolol and lidocaine, monomorphic VT was observed. Intravenous procainamide was subsequently administered over several minutes, and VT/VF was finally terminated by direct-current shock. Epinephrine was not used during resuscitation. High-rate coronary sinus pacing was performed in the ICU at rates of approximately 80–100 beats/min, achieving complete capture, although its effect on ventricular arrhythmia suppression appeared limited. VA-ECMO flow was generally maintained at 2–3 L/min (pump speed 3000–3500 rpm). However, blood pressure remained low, and norepinephrine infusion was continued at approximately 0.03 μg/kg/min. Arterial blood gas analysis after ICU admission showed improvement of lactate to 1.0 mmol/L and pH to 7.386. Thereafter, pH remained between 7.35 and 7.40, and lactate levels remained around 1.0 mmol/L.

The arrhythmias consisted predominantly of VT with intermittent VF. Continuous monitoring revealed occasional premature ventricular contractions; however, no clear trigger premature beats initiating VT/VF were identified. On 12-lead ECG, no apparent QTc prolongation was observed. Serum magnesium had not been measured on admission; at this time it was 4.5 mg/dL, and magnesium supplementation was adjusted accordingly.

On the day following initiation of VA-ECMO, percutaneous stellate ganglion block (PSGB) was performed by an anaesthetist. Ultrasound-guided injection of 7–10 mL of 0.75% ropivacaine was used as the local anaesthetic. Anisocoria was not specifically assessed after the procedure. Because the left cervical region had been punctured in the catheterisation laboratory shortly beforehand, the first PSGB was performed on the right side to reduce the risk of bleeding or haematoma. The subsequent two procedures were performed on the left side. No bleeding or neurological complications related to PSGB were observed. After the second PSGB, no VT/VF episodes occurred for 2 days, and VA-ECMO was successfully weaned on day 11 after initiation. However, VT/VF recurred frequently on the day of ECMO weaning and the following day, and a third PSGB was performed. After day 17 of hospitalisation, no further VT/VF episodes occurred. Subsequently, his respiratory condition improved (figure 1B,C), and he was successfully weaned from MCS (figure 4). A total of 54 direct-current shocks were delivered during the clinical course, most at 200 J.

Clinical course and pharmacological management. PSGB was initiated the day after VA-ECMO implantation and repeated at weekly intervals. No further VF or VT occurred after day 17. Following weaning from MCS, the patient underwent heart failure management, subcutaneous ICD implantation and tracheostomy before transfer for rehabilitation on day 63. IABP, intra-aortic balloon pumping; ICD, implantable cardioverter defibrillator; MCS, mechanical circulatory support; PSGB, percutaneous stellate ganglion block; VA-ECMO, veno-arterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation; VF, ventricular fibrillation; VT, ventricular tachycardia.

Follow-up echocardiography demonstrated progressive improvement in LVEF, from 21% on hospital day 4 to 26% before discharge, 43% at 6 months, and 52% at 10 months. MR also improved after treatment for heart failure, with only mild residual regurgitation before discharge. Follow-up ECG also did not reveal any clinically significant abnormalities. At discharge, the patient was prescribed mexiletine 300 mg daily, amiodarone 150 mg daily, carvedilol 2.5 mg daily, enalapril 2.5 mg daily, eplerenone 25 mg daily, aspirin 100 mg daily and prasugrel 3.75 mg daily.

Outcome and follow-up

VFs resolved completely after hospital day 17. A tracheostomy was required due to prolonged mechanical ventilation, but his heart failure gradually improved with guideline-directed therapy. He was transferred to a rehabilitation facility on day 63. No further episodes of VF/VT have occurred until the time of writing.

He has returned to independent daily living and continues to attend outpatient follow-up, now 11 months after the initial event. Ongoing surveillance includes periodic ECG and laboratory tests, as well as optimisation of heart failure therapy, with no evidence of recurrence of arrhythmias or clinical deterioration.

Discussion

ES is defined as the occurrence of three or more episodes of sustained VT or VF within 24 hours, which represents a life-threatening arrhythmic condition. The underlying conditions include ischaemic cardiomyopathy, electrolyte imbalance, decompensated heart failure and inherited arrhythmogenic disorders such as Brugada syndrome or catecholaminergic polymorphic VT.1 2 Among these, ischaemic cardiomyopathy is the most frequent substrate.3 The development of ES is considered to result from the interplay between (1) an electrophysiological/structural substrate, (2) precipitating triggers and (3) autonomic dysregulation. Re-entry is the most frequent mechanism, accounting for approximately 80% of ES episodes, which mainly arises as monomorphic VT through macro-re-entrant circuits. Micro-re-entry due to local inflammation or fibrosis and functional re-entry associated with sympathetic hyperactivation have also been described. In addition, triggered activity induced by inherited ion-channel abnormalities, drug toxicity or myocardial ischaemia constitutes another important mechanism. Re-entry and triggered activity are not mutually exclusive. In patients with a pre-existing re-entrant substrate, ischaemia, electrolyte disturbance or pharmacological factors may provoke triggered activity and initiate ventricular arrhythmias. Sympathetic activation augments delayed after-depolarisations and heterogeneity of repolarisation, further facilitating ES.4 5 In the present case, no electrolyte imbalance or uncontrolled heart failure exacerbation that could precipitate ES was identified, and no evidence of ongoing myocardial ischaemia was observed at the time of ES onset. In addition, VT predominated over VF. These findings suggest that the arrhythmogenic substrate formed after myocardial infarction may have played a major role in the development of ES. Follow-up echocardiography also demonstrated improvement in LVEF and MR, suggesting functional MR associated with left ventricular systolic dysfunction.

Kobayashi et al conducted a retrospective observational study involving 6003 patients with acute myocardial infarction (AMI) in Japan and reported an incidence of ES of approximately 0.9%.6 They classified patients into two groups: Early ES (occurring within 48 hours after AMI onset) and Late ES (occurring after 48 hours). In-hospital mortality was significantly higher in the Late ES group than in the Early ES group (79.9% vs 29.7%), suggesting a poorer prognosis for Late ES. The authors also noted that Early ES was more closely related to the severity of AMI, haemodynamic instability and, in some cases, reperfusion injury. Indeed, high peak CK levels and Killip class III–IV were identified as major risk factors. In contrast, Late ES appeared to be influenced more by systemic comorbidities such as diabetes mellitus and renal dysfunction, as well as elevated CRP levels, indicating the contribution of systemic inflammation to arrhythmic instability.6 In the present case, ES occurred approximately 1 week after AMI onset. Given the presence of diabetes, renal dysfunction and elevated CRP on admission, the presented case corresponds to Late ES, a form known to carry a poorer prognosis. Furthermore, Salaun et al7 investigated prognostic predictors in 151 patients with ES following AMI and reported that a history of hypertension and a New York Heart Association (NYHA) functional class of III or higher at presentation were independent risk factors for poor outcomes. In the same study, a PAINESD (chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, age>60 years, ischaemic cardiomyopathy, New York Heart Association class III/IV, left ventricular ejection fraction<25%, ES occurrence, diabetes) score ≥18, originally developed for risk stratification in VT ablation, was also associated with increased in-hospital mortality. Although the present patient had no history of hypertension, other risk factors, including NYHA class III or higher and a PAINESD score ≥18, were present, suggesting that this case represented a particularly high-risk clinical condition.

Current treatment options for ES include anti-arrhythmic drugs, deep sedation and catheter ablation; however, no single strategy has been firmly established, and management should be individualised.4 8

According to the Japanese Circulation Society (JCS) guideline,2 in patients with recurrent VF or pulseless VT despite repeated direct-current shocks and epinephrine administration, intravenous nifekalant or amiodarone with a class IIa level of evidence is recommended. A multicentre Japanese cohort also demonstrated a higher 1 month survival in patients receiving amiodarone, nifekalant or lidocaine compared with those who did not, although neurological outcomes remain uncertain.2 For patients with recurrent monomorphic VT associated with structural heart disease, intravenous procainamide may be considered when haemodynamic stability is preserved.9 In unstable cases, the drug should be used with appropriate haemodynamic backup, including the availability of MCS if necessary. β-blocker therapy also plays a key role in sympathetic suppression. The 2025 American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association (ACC/AHA) guideline for acute coronary syndromes recommends β-blockers as a first-line therapy, either alone or in combination with anti-arrhythmic agents for ES occurring after AMI.10 The JCS 2020 guideline2 similarly notes the benefit of ultra-short-acting β-blockers such as landiolol for ES that is refractory to amiodarone or nifekalant. In the present case, episodes of VF and pulseless VT were refractory to amiodarone, nifekalant and lidocaine. After the initiation of MCS, landiolol was administered intravenously and subsequent direct-current shock achieved successful arrhythmic suppression. Nademanee et al11 reported that sympathetic blockade with β-blockers was associated with improved survival both in the short term (1 week) and long term (1 year), and was also associated with a reduction in the number of VF episodes. In the present case, multiple anti-arrhythmic agents with different mechanisms of action were administered almost simultaneously, making it difficult to determine which drug exerted the most prominent effect. However, it is possible that sympathetic suppression induced by landiolol, in addition to the combined sodium-channel and potassium-channel blockade provided by the other anti-arrhythmic agents, contributed to the suppression of ES.

The 2017 American Heart Association/American College of Cardiology/Heart Rhythm Society (AHA/ACC/HRS) guideline12 states that sympathetic blockade may be considered as a therapeutic option (class IIb) in patients with ES when β-blockers, anti-arrhythmic drugs or catheter ablation are ineffective or not feasible. PSGB represents another effective sympatholytic approach. Using ultrasound guidance, local anaesthetic is injected around the stellate ganglion to suppress sympathetic efferent activity, and this has been reported to reduce refractory episodes of VF and VT. The largest clinical study supporting its efficacy is the STAR study,8 a multicentre trial enrolling 131 patients with drug-refractory ES, in which PSGB significantly reduced arrhythmic events. In particular, despite more than half of the cohort receiving antiplatelet or anticoagulant therapy, the incidence of bleeding or other major complications remained low, suggesting acceptable procedural safety. Motazedian et al13 analysed 15 published studies and concluded that PSGB significantly reduced ventricular arrhythmic events in patients with ES. In the same analysis, repeat PSGB procedures were required in 21% of cases because of recurrent arrhythmic episodes, as observed in the present case. Furthermore, additional evidence is expected from the ongoing GANGSTER trial, a double-blind, sham-controlled randomised study evaluating the efficacy of PSGB.13 More recently, low-level laser phototherapy targeting the stellate ganglion has been explored as a minimally invasive alternative.14 Catheter ablation remains the most definitive rhythm-control therapy for ES; however, in patients with critical illness or in centres with limited experience in emergent ventricular ablation, the procedural risk may be prohibitive.15 In such circumstances, PSGB can serve as a bridge therapy to stabilise arrhythmia until ablation becomes feasible.16–18 In the present case, PSGB was initiated after the introduction of MCS, during a period of ongoing haemodynamic instability. The first block was performed on the right side, as the patient exhibited active bleeding at the left cervical puncture site following percutaneous coronary intervention. Two subsequent blocks were performed on the left side for a total of three procedures. Although intermittent episodes of VF and pulseless VT persisted after the first and second blocks, no further recurrence was observed after the third procedure, allowing the ablation of the acute-phase catheter to be safely avoided. Although the effect of PSGB cannot be clearly separated from concurrent anti-arrhythmic drug therapy and MCS, the temporal association suggests that PSGB contributed in combination to achieve arrhythmic control.

High-rate pacing (overdrive pacing) has been used since 1964 as an effective adjunctive therapy for drug-resistant ES, but supporting evidence remains limited.19 In addition, depending on the concomitant administration of anti-arrhythmic agents and on the characteristics of VT, high-rate pacing may accelerate VT or progress to VF.4 Thus, the available evidence is extremely limited and descriptions in the Japanese guidelines are scarce. Nevertheless, Kurisu et al19 reported a case similar to ours in which overdrive pacing suppressed lethal arrhythmias in drug-refractory AMI-related ES, emphasising that it can be introduced as an adjunct to pharmacological therapy.

In the present case, VA-ECMO and IABP were introduced as MCS during episodes of VF and pulseless VT. Yamamoto et al20 reported the results of patients with ES who underwent MCS: among 17 cases, 6 died, whereas 11 patients were discharged alive. Although mortality remained high, the findings suggested that MCS is useful in cases of circulatory collapse due to ES. The usefulness of MCS for ES has been suggested in several reports, including that of Yamamoto et al20 21 whereas others have described cases in which MCS served as a bridge therapy to subsequent treatment or contributed to survival.5 Yamamoto et al pointed out significant differences in serum pH and lactate levels at the time of MCS initiation between survivors and non-survivors, indicating that such metabolic parameters may be associated with prognosis. In contrast, Durães-Campos et al21 did not identify lactate as independent prognostic factors, and therefore no consistent conclusions have been reached regarding prognostic indicators. Nonetheless, the introduction of MCS in an early stage, before the progression of circulatory failure, may be beneficial.20 21 In the present case, VA-ECMO and IABP were introduced relatively early, resulting in stabilisation of haemodynamics against life-threatening arrhythmias, and this early introduction may have contributed to survival.

Considering the above evidence and previously reported cases, several factors may have contributed to the favourable outcome in the present case. First, the ES occurred in the catheterisation laboratory, allowing the immediate establishment of MCS such as ECMO and IABP. Second, antiarrhythmic drugs and β-blockers were administered promptly according to the guideline-based treatment. Third, successful suppression of recurrent arrhythmias was achieved by PSGB. The clinical efficacy of PSGB has been demonstrated in large-scale studies, and several reports have described patients in whom PSGB enabled avoidance of acute-phase ventricular ablation, as in the present case. Emergency ablation procedures generally carry a higher risk of complications; the avoidance of emergent ablation even in an unstable haemodynamic condition may be of particular significance, as it can reduce the risk of complications and broaden subsequent therapeutic options. More evidence is needed to clarify optimal patient selection, timing, and the number of PSGB applications in refractory ES.

Patient’s perspective

I do not remember the most critical moments because I was unconscious at that time. However, I am now able to walk to the hospital by myself and live my daily life at home as usual, which brings me great joy. My wife is also very relieved and happy about my recovery.

I would appreciate it if my experience could contribute to the advancement of medicine.
