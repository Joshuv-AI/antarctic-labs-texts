The Yamada–Watanabe–Engelbert theorem for SPDEs in Banach spaces Thanks: The author is supported by the VICI subsidy VI.C.212.027 of the Netherlands Organisation for Scientific Research (NWO)
Esmée Theewis

Abstract
. We give a unified proof of the Yamada–Watanabe–Engelbert theorem for various notions of solutions for SPDEs in Banach spaces with cylindrical Wiener noise. We use Kurtz’ generalization of the theorems of Yamada, Watanabe and Engelbert. In addition, we deduce the classical Yamada–Watanabe theorem for SPDEs, with a slightly different notion of ‘unique strong solution’ than that corresponding to the result of Kurtz. Our setting includes analytically strong solutions, analytically weak solutions and mild solutions. Moreover, our approach offers flexibility with regard to the function spaces and integrability conditions that are chosen in the solution notion (and affect the meaning of existence and uniqueness). All results hold in Banach spaces which are either martingale type 2 or UMD. For analytically weak solutions, the results hold in arbitrary Banach spaces. In particular, our results extend the Yamada–Watanabe theorems of Ondreját for mild solutions in 2-smooth Banach spaces, of Röckner et al. for the variational framework and of Kunze for analytically weak solutions, and cover many new settings. As a tool, and of interest itself, we construct a measurable representation I I of the stochastic integral in a martingale type 2 or UMD Banach space, in the sense that for any stochastically integrable process f f and cylindrical Brownian motion W W , we have I ⁡ ( f ⁡ ( ω ) , W ⁡ ( ω ) , 𝙻𝚊𝚠 ⁡ ( f , W ) ) = ( ∫ 0 ⋅ f ​ 𝑑 W ) ​ ( ω ) I(f(\omega),W(\omega),\mathtt{Law}(f,W))=(\int_{0}^{\cdot}f\,\mathrm{d}W)(\omega) for almost every ω \omega .

Introduction

The
Yamada–Watanabe theorem
was originally proven by Yamada and Watanabe for stochastic differential equation (SDE) solutions in
[
48
, Cor. 1]
,
and can be roughly summarized as:

(0.1)

\displaystyle\text{weak existence $+$ pathwise uniqueness}\iff\text{ unique strong existence. }

A detailed proof can be found in
[
24
, Th. 1.1 Chap. 4]
.
In (
0.1
), ‘weak’ is meant in a probabilistic sense, and ‘unique strong existence’ is a comprehensive notion indicating that there exist probabilistically strong solutions, all uniquely determined by a single solution map
F
with several measurability properties, mapping the initial data and the Brownian motion to the solution to the SDE. In particular, ‘
\Leftarrow
’ in the above is trivial and ‘
\Rightarrow
’ is the useful implication.

Afterwards, it was observed that the proof would also extend to infinite dimensional SPDE settings, first by Brzeźniak and and Gątarek in
[
10
, p. 212]
.
A first rigorous proof was provided by Ondreját in his thesis
[
38
]
,
where the Yamada–Watanabe theorem was proved for mild solutions to SPDEs in 2-smooth Banach spaces.
Other important contributions to SPDE settings were made by Röckner, Schmuland and Zhang
[
42
]
(variational framework) and by Kunze
[
29
]
(analytically weak solutions), who all used the proof strategy from SDEs as in
[
24
]
.

Meanwhile, a reverse of the Yamada–Watanabe theorem was provided by Jacod
[
25
]
and by Engelbert
[
16
, Th. 2]
, leading to the equivalence result:

(0.2)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\text{weak existence $+$ pathwise uniqueness}\\
&\iff\text{ existence of a strong solution $+$ joint weak uniqueness, }\end{split}

commonly referred to as the
Yamada–Watanabe–Engelbert theorem
.
Here, the last line is much weaker than unique strong existence (see also
[
16
, Cor. p. 209]
). Thus ‘
\Leftarrow
’ provides new information compared to (
0.1
) and can be used for proving pathwise uniqueness.
A slight difference is that in the classical Yamada–Watanabe result, the left-hand side of (
0.1
) is assumed for all initial distributions, whereas in the Yamada–Watanabe–Engelbert result, the equivalence (
0.2
) holds for a fixed initial distribution.

A much more general version of the Yamada–Watanabe–Engelbert theorem (
0.2
) was proved by Kurtz
[
30
, Prop. 3.14]
. The result applies to general stochastic equations with a convexity structure and a compatibility structure capturing adaptedness properties, and is suited for S(P)DEs.
Subsequently, in
[
31
, p. 7, Th. 3.1, Th. 3.4]
, Kurtz extended this result by entirely dropping the convexity assumption and by treating more general compatibility structures, including larger classes of SDEs, e.g. those with distribution-dependent coefficients, which were not included in the former version
[
31
, Ex. 2.14]
.

In this paper, we use the results of
[
30
,
31
]
to prove both the Yamada–Watanabe–Engelbert theorem (Theorem
3.1
) and the classical Yamada–Watanabe theorem (Corollary
3.15
) for very general SPDE settings.
To the best of our knowledge, with the exception of
[
8
,
17
]
, all rigorous proofs of the Yamada–Watanabe theorem for SPDE settings have not been based on Kurtz’ results, but have followed the classical approach of
[
24
]
. Also the reverse, i.e. ‘
\Leftarrow
’ of (
0.2
), has been proved mostly without the use of Kurtz’ results (see e.g.
[
41
, Th. 3.1]
). Thus our approach is rather new. The advantage of using the results of Kurtz is that it reveals in a more direct way which structure is exactly needed of the equation and underlying spaces.

No examples of applications to SPDEs were given in
[
30
,
31
]
. Still, Kurtz’ result
[
30
, Prop. 3.14]
has been cited to deduce strong existence for various SPDEs
[
43
,
44
,
19
,
20
,
18
,
45
]
.
Our work in particular provides a detailed explanation of how Kurtz’ result can be applied in such settings.
When doing so, the main issues that need to be handled are the following:

•

The solution notion, including the actual stochastic equation that a solution needs to satisfy,
has to be characterized by the joint distribution of the solution and the noise.

•

Suitable measurability conditions and (Polish) function spaces have to be chosen for the solution and the coefficients in the SPDE.

For SPDEs, these issues are more delicate than for SDEs. In fact, one of the crucial parts of Ondreját’s proof concerned the first issue

[
38
, Th. 6]
.
To solve the first issue in our setting, essentially without extra effort, we prove existence of a measurable representation for stochastic integrals in martingale type 2 or UMD Banach spaces, see Theorem
3.7
.
That is, there exists a measurable map
I
such that for any stochastically integrable (possibly discontinuous) process
f
and cylindrical Brownian motion
W
, we have

I\big(f(\omega),W(\omega),\mathtt{Law}(f,W)\big)=\Big(\int_{0}^{\cdot}f\,\mathrm{d}W\Big)(\omega)\quad\text{for almost every $\omega$.}

Such representations are well known in finite dimensions, but as far as we know,
the above has not been proved for infinite dimensions (in other proofs of Yamada–Watanabe results, one fixes
\mathtt{Law}(f,W)
). The UMD case is especially interesting here, and a bit more involved.

The generality of the setting in this paper is such that our Yamada–Watanabe(–Engelbert) theorem can be applied to many different notions of solutions, in particular to analytically strong, analytically weak and mild solutions.
Furthermore, our method is flexible in several respects:

•

The solution and the coefficients in the equation take values in and are defined on arbitrary separable Banach spaces.

•

The solutions may be defined on a bounded time interval
[0,T]
or on
[0,\infty)
.

•

The space
\mathbb{B}
in which solutions are required to live (pointwise in
\omega\in\Omega
) may be chosen flexibly, as long as it is Polish and embeds continuously into some space
C([0,T];Z)
or
C([0,\infty);Z)
, with
Z
a separable Banach space. In particular, (weighted) Sobolev spaces and more exotic spaces can be used in the choice of
\mathbb{B}
. Also, weak continuity and positivity conditions can be included (see Example
2.13
).

•

The coefficients in the SPDE are allowed to depend on time and on the whole path of the solution. No specific (e.g. semilinear) structure is assumed.

•

Moment conditions (with respect to
\omega
) can be added to the notion of solution.

Some minor aspects are taken into account as well:

•

Treating the cylindrical Brownian motion as
\mathbb{R}^{\infty}
-valued or as trace class noise in a larger Hilbert space through a Hilbert–Schmidt embedding (Remarks
3.8
and
3.14
).

•

Allowing for all filtered probability spaces or only those which are complete or satisfy the usual conditions (Remark
3.2
).

As a consequence, we cover the well-known Yamada–Watanabe results of Ondreját
[
38
]
(Example
3.17
), Röckner, Schmuland and Zhang
[
42
]
(Example
a)
) and Kunze
[
29
]
(Example
3.16
).
More importantly, we obtain the Yamada–Watanabe–Engelbert theorem for various classes of solutions to SPDEs for which the result was not available yet, such as:

a)

Analytically weak solutions in Banach spaces that are not UMD nor of M-type 2, without structural assumptions on the SPDE.

b)

Mild solutions to SPDEs with time-dependent differential operators.

c)

Quasilinear SPDEs that cannot be treated in a variational setting. Examples are those in
[
1
]
and
[
6
, Th. 6.6]
.

d)

Semilinear SPDEs in critical spaces. Examples are the following equations in critical spaces with Gaussian transport noise, in an
L^{p}(L^{q})
-framework (
p>2
):
Allen-Cahn
[
2
, §7.1, (7.12)]
, Navier-Stokes
[
5
]
, Cahn-Hilliard
[
2
, §7.3, (7.24)]
, reaction-diffusion equations
[
4
]
.

The above examples do not fit in
[
38
,
42
,
29
]
for different reasons. For
b)
, one has to use evolution families instead of a semigroup, which had not been treated yet. Concerning
a)
and
c)
: the settings of
[
38
, p. 8, (0.2)]
and
[
29
, Hyp. 4.1, Def. 3.3]
are well-suited for semilinear equations, but not for quasilinear equations, due to the structural assumptions. For
d)
, note that the variational setting of
[
42
]
does not apply since
p>2
.
Moreover, the reason that
[
29
]
cannot be applied with critical spaces is that the smallest space in which solutions are continuous is a real interpolation space
(X_{0},X_{1})_{1-\frac{1+\kappa}{p},p}
. Now, the coefficients in the equations are not bounded on bounded subsets of this interpolation space. Therefore, the space
E
of
[
29
, Hyp. 3.1, Def. 3.3]
, in which solutions are continuous, cannot be chosen suitably. Similarly, the underlying spaces cannot be chosen in a way fitting
[
38
]
, here due to the transport noise.
The restrictions are overcome in our setting by the flexibility of Assumption
2.1
.

Overview of the paper

Section
1
consists of preliminaries. In Subsection
1.1
, we revisit Kurtz’ version of the Yamada–Watanabe–Engelbert theorem and provide some extra probabilistic details for the more analysis-oriented reader. In Subsection
1.2
, some relevant aspects of stochastic integration in Banach spaces are discussed. In Section
2
, we explain the settings and our solution notions for SPDEs (Definition
2.5
), and provide examples of settings that fit into the framework (Example
2.13
). In Section
3
, we prove existence of a measurable representation of the stochastic integral (Theorem
3.7
). This will then be used to prove the Yamada–Watanabe–Engelbert theorem (Theorem
3.1
) for SPDEs. Lastly, we prove the classical Yamada–Watanabe theorem with a stronger notion of ‘unique strong solution’ (Corollary
3.15
).

During the final phase of the completion of this manuscript, it came to our attention that related results have been obtained simultaneously and independently by Fahim, Hausenblas and Karlsen
[
17
]
. Their setting generalizes the Yamada–Watanabe–Engelbert theorem in a different direction: by including noise coming from a Poisson random measure, besides a Wiener process. On the other hand, the framework is only variational, with coefficients defined on Hilbert and reflexive Banach spaces that are part of a Gelfand triple.
In contrast, our work focuses on Gaussian noise and provides a Yamada–Watanabe(–Engelbert) theorem in Banach spaces, for various solution notions. The variational framework is a special case here. The coefficients may be Markovian as in
[
17
]
(see Lemma
2.2
), but may also depend on the whole time paths of the solution.

Lemma 1.4
.

Let
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
be a probability space and let

Suppose that

D\coloneqq\{(z,z):z\in E_{1}\times E_{2}\}\in(\mathcal{A}_{1}\otimes\mathcal{A}_{2})\otimes(\mathcal{A}_{1}\otimes\mathcal{A}_{2}).

If
\mathtt{Law}(\xi,\eta)=\mathtt{Law}(F(\eta),\eta)

Proof.

Let
\nu\coloneqq\mathtt{Law}(\eta)
. We have
\mathtt{Law}(\xi,\eta)=\mathtt{Law}(F(\eta),\eta)=\delta_{F(y)}(\mathrm{d}x)\nu(\mathrm{d}y)

Let

\mathbbm{1}_{C}(x,y)=\mathbbm{1}_{D}((F(y),y),(x,y))
. Since

and
(x,y)\mapsto((F(y),y),(x,y))
is
\mathcal{A}_{1}\otimes\mathcal{A}_{2}/(\mathcal{A}_{1}\otimes\mathcal{A}_{2})\otimes(\mathcal{A}_{1}\otimes\mathcal{A}_{2})
-measurable, it holds that
C\in\mathcal{A}_{1}\otimes\mathcal{A}_{2}

1.2.
Stochastic integration in Banach spaces

Here, we discuss some relevant aspects of stochastic integration.
Throughout this paper, we will use the following function space notations.
If
X
is a normed space and
(S_{1},{\mathcal{A}}_{1},\mu_{1})
is a measure space, then
L^{0}(S_{1};X)
and
L^{0}_{\mathcal{A}_{1}}(S_{1};X)
denote the linear space of strongly
\mu_{1}
-measurable
functions

, with identification of
\mu_{1}
-a.e. equal functions, and equipped with the topology of convergence in measure.
For

L^{p}(S_{1};X)\coloneqq\{f\in L^{0}(S_{1};X):\|f\|_{L^{p}(S_{1};X)}<\infty\},\quad\|f\|_{L^{p}(S_{1};X)}\coloneqq({\textstyle\int_{S_{1}}\|f\|_{X}^{p}\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{1})^{\frac{1}{p}}}

and we put

.
For a metric space

denotes the space of continuous functions
\bar{I}_{T}\to M
equipped with the topology of uniform convergence on compact subsets of
\bar{I}_{T}

We will use the following notation for point evaluation maps:

(1.9)

\pi_{M}^{t}\colon C(\bar{I}_{T};M)\to M\colon f\mapsto f(t),\qquad t\in\bar{I}_{T}.

Now, for stochastic integration, let us begin by defining a
U
-cylindrical Brownian motion.

Definition 1.7
.

Let

be a filtered probability space, let

\mathcal{F}_{t}

(iii)

Recall that we call a map from a measurable space to a metric space

for some Polish space

For elementary adapted rank one processes, the stochastic integral is defined by

\int_{0}^{t}\mathbbm{1}_{(t_{1},t_{2}]\times A}\otimes(u\otimes y)\,\mathrm{d}W\coloneqq\mathbbm{1}_{A}y\big(W(\mathbbm{1}_{(0,t_{2}\wedge t]}\otimes u)-W(\mathbbm{1}_{(0,t_{1}\wedge t]}\otimes u)\big),

It is then further extended by continuity (from Burkholder’s inequality for the M-type 2 case, and from the Itô isomorphism for the UMD case), by localization and by existence of a progressively measurable modification

and

. We now define stochastic integrability as in
[

Definition 1.10
.

Let

be a separable real Hilbert space and let

be a real Banach space. Let

be a

We say that

(

For a Hilbert space

\textstyle{\|\sum_{n=1}^{N}u_{n}\otimes y_{n}\|_{\gamma(U,Y)}\coloneqq\big(\mathbb{E}\big[\|\sum_{n=1}^{N}\gamma_{n}y_{n}\|_{Y}^{2}\big]\big)^{1/2},}

where

[

, §9.1]
for further details and useful properties. Here, let us only mention some simple examples:

•

(the Hilbert–Schmidt operators) if

[

,

•

\gamma(U,L^{p}(S_{1}))\cong L^{p}(S_{1};U)
if

[

, p. 285]
. Similar identifications can be made for Sobolev spaces.

If

has M-type 2, then a sufficient condition for stochastic integrability of

is that it is

-strongly measurable,
U
-strongly
(\mathcal{F}_{t})
-adapted and

[

Remark 2.7
.

We included all these different solution notions because they cannot be used interchangeably in general.
For example, the analytically weak and weakly mild notions can be used in a Banach space
Y
without additional geometrical structure, but for the analytically strong and mild solution notions one does need structure of
Y
so that the stochastic integrals can be defined.
Secondly, the notions of mild and weakly mild solutions only make sense if part of the coefficient
b
has some linear structure, while this is not needed for analytically strong and analytically weak solutions.
Lastly, even for semilinear equations in an M-type 2 or UMD space
Y
, to get equivalence between analytically weak and analytically strong or mild solutions, one may need to assume extra structure of the evolution family/semigroup or generator thereof, see e.g.
[
29
, Prop. 6.9, Hyp. 6.5]
and
[
47
, Prop. 7.5.12, (C2)]
.

\mathbb{B}
. Recall that a

need not be linear. Time integrability conditions can be put into

). In general, it may be useful to recall that an open or closed subset of a Polish space is again Polish. Additional ‘a.s. conditions’ can be added through

).

, in particular in probability. Now the Itô isomorphism yields (
3.10
).

Moreover, since
f\in C({\bar{I}_{T}};U)
everywhere on
\Omega
, (
3.9
) and (
3.10
) yield

\lim_{m\to\infty}\tilde{I}_{n,m}(f,W)=\int_{0}^{\cdot}\langle{P}_{n}f,\,\mathrm{d}W\rangle_{U}\text{ in probability in }C(\bar{I}_{T}).

On the other hand, for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, by
[
26
, Prop. 5.32]
, there exists a measurable map
\tilde{I}_{n}\colon C({\bar{I}_{T}};U)\times\mathbb{W}\times\mathcal{P}(C({\bar{I}_{T}};U)\times\mathbb{W})\to C({\bar{I}_{T}})
such that for any
C({\bar{I}_{T}};U)\times\mathbb{W}
-valued random variable
(\xi,\eta)
on any probability space, it holds that

\tilde{I}_{n,m}(\xi,\eta)\text{ converges in probability in }C(\bar{I}_{T};Y)\iff\lim_{m\to\infty}\tilde{I}_{n,m}(\xi,\eta)=\tilde{I}_{n}(\xi,\eta,\mathtt{Law}(\xi,\eta))\,\text{ a.s.}

In particular, for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
f
,
W
as above with
f\in C({\bar{I}_{T}};U)
everywhere,

(3.11)

\int_{0}^{\cdot}\langle{P}_{n}f,\,\mathrm{d}W\rangle_{U}=\tilde{I}_{n}(f,W,\mathtt{Law}(f,W))\,\text{ a.s.}

Next, note that
{P}_{n}f\to f
in
U
on
\bar{I}_{T}\times\Omega
, so by the Dominated Convergence Theorem,
{P}_{n}f\to f
in
L^{2}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\bar{I}_{T};U)
on
\Omega
, and by the Itô isomorphism
[
34
, Th. 5.5, (5.4)]
:

\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{0}^{\cdot}\langle{P}_{n}f,\,\mathrm{d}W\rangle_{U}=\int_{0}^{\cdot}\langle f,\,\mathrm{d}W\rangle_{U}\text{ in probability in }C(\bar{I}_{T}),

Hence, invoking the functional representation
[
26
, Prop. 5.32]
again, there exists a measurable map
\tilde{I}\colon C({\bar{I}_{T}};U)\times\mathbb{W}\times\mathcal{P}(C({\bar{I}_{T}};U)\times\mathbb{W})\to C({\bar{I}_{T}})
such that (
3.8
) is satisfied for all
f
,
W
as above.

It remains to extend the result to integrand processes
f
lying in
L^{2}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\bar{I}_{T};U)

instead of
C(\bar{I}_{T};U)
.
To this end, define

\displaystyle J_{n}\colon L^{2}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\bar{I}_{T};U)\to C({\bar{I}_{T}};U),\,J_{n}(h)(t)\coloneqq n\!\int_{(t-\frac{1}{n})\vee 0}^{t}h(s)\,\mathrm{d}s,

\displaystyle{I}_{n}\colon L^{2}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\bar{I}_{T};U)\times\mathbb{W}\times\mathcal{P}(L^{2}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\bar{I}_{T};U)\times\mathbb{W})\to C({\bar{I}_{T}})\colon(g,w,\mu)\mapsto\tilde{I}(J_{n}\circ g,w,(J_{n},\mathrm{Id}_{\mathbb{W}})\#\mu).

Note that for all
t\in\bar{I}_{T}
:
\|J_{n}(h)-J_{n}(g)\|_{C(\bar{I}_{t};U)}\leq{n}^{1/2}\|h-g\|_{L^{2}(I_{t};U)}
by Hölder’s inequality, thus each
J_{n}
is continuous, hence Borel measurable.
Moreover,
\mu\mapsto(J_{n},\mathrm{Id}_{\mathbb{W}})\#\mu
is
measurable by Lemma
3.5
.
Consequently, each
I_{n}
is measurable.

Furthermore, the maps
J_{n}
satisfy

(3.12)

\lim_{n\to\infty}J_{n}h=h\text{ in }L^{2}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\bar{I}_{T};U),\quad\qquad h\in L^{2}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\bar{I}_{T};U).

This follows from the
L^{p}
-convergence of approximate identities in
L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d};E)
with
E
Banach.
Putting
\varphi\coloneqq\mathbbm{1}_{[0,1]}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
and
\varphi_{n}(t)\coloneqq n\varphi(nt)
, we obtain from
[
22
, Prop. 1.2.32]
:

\lim_{n\to\infty}\varphi_{n}\ast\tilde{h}=\tilde{h}\,\text{ in }L^{2}(\mathbb{R};U),\quad\text{ for any $\tilde{h}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R};U)$},

where
\ast
denotes the convolution. Now, let
t\in\bar{I}_{T}
and
h\in L^{2}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\bar{I}_{T};U)
be arbitrary. Define
\tilde{h}\coloneqq\mathbbm{1}_{I_{t}}h\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R};U)
. For all
r\in I_{t}
we have:

\displaystyle\varphi_{n}\ast\tilde{h}(r)=n\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbbm{1}_{[0,1/n]}(r-s)\tilde{h}(s)\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=n\int_{r-1/n}^{r}\tilde{h}(s)\,\mathrm{d}s=n\int_{(r-1/n)\vee 0}^{r}{h}(s)\,\mathrm{d}s=J_{n}(h)(r).

Therefore,

\displaystyle\|J_{n}(h)-h\|_{L^{2}(I_{t};U)}

\displaystyle=\|(\varphi_{n}\ast\tilde{h}-\tilde{h})\mathbbm{1}_{I_{t}}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R};U)}\leq\|\varphi_{n}\ast\tilde{h}-\tilde{h}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R};U)}\to 0,

proving (
3.12
).

Suppose that
f\colon\bar{I}_{T}\times\Omega\to U
is
\mathcal{B}(\bar{I}_{T})\otimes\mathcal{F}/\mathcal{B}(U)
-measurable and
(\mathcal{F}_{t})
-adapted process satisfying
f\in L^{2}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\bar{I}_{T};U)
on
\Omega
.
Then the convergence in (
3.12
) holds pointwise on
\Omega
, hence in probability,
and the Itô homeomorphism
[
34
, Th. 5.5]
and (
3.8
) yield

{I}_{n}(f,W,\mathtt{Law}(f,W))\overset{\text{a.s.}}{=}\int_{0}^{\cdot}\langle J_{n}\circ f,\,\mathrm{d}W\rangle_{U}\to\int_{0}^{\cdot}\langle f,\,\mathrm{d}W\rangle_{U}\text{ in probability in }C({\bar{I}_{T}})

as
n\to\infty
.
On the other hand,
[
26
, Prop. 5.32]
gives a measurable map
I\colon L^{2}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\bar{I}_{T};U)\times\mathbb{W}\times\mathcal{P}(L^{2}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\bar{I}_{T};U)\times\mathbb{W})\to C({\bar{I}_{T}})
such that for any measurable
(f,W)\colon\Omega\to L^{2}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\bar{I}_{T};U)\times\mathbb{W}
:

\displaystyle(I_{n}(f,W,\mathtt{Law}(f,W)))_{n}

converges in probability

\displaystyle\iff I_{n}(f,W,\mathtt{Law}(f,W)){\to}I(f,W,\mathtt{Law}(f,W))\text{ in probability}.

For every
\mathcal{B}(\bar{I}_{T})\otimes\mathcal{F}/\mathcal{B}(U)
-measurable,
(\mathcal{F}_{t})
-adapted process
f\colon\bar{I}_{T}\times\Omega\to U
satisfying
f\in L^{2}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\bar{I}_{T};U)
on
\Omega
, we have
\mathtt{Law}(f,W)=\mathtt{Law}(\bar{f},W)
, and we conclude that (
3.7
) holds.
∎

It might be that Lemma
3.6
can also be proved with ‘
\mathtt{Law}(f,W)
’ replaced by ‘
\mathtt{Law}(f)
’. However, this is irrelevant for the Yamada–Watanabe theory of this paper.

Using the above representation for scalar valued stochastic integrals, we are also able to represent the stochastic integral in M-type 2 spaces and in UMD spaces. The proof relies on having a sequence of functionals in the dual space that separates the points. Such functionals give all necessary information for the desired measurability.
For UMD spaces, recall the conditions for stochastic integrability directly above and below Definition
1.11
.

Theorem 3.7
.

be a separable real Hilbert space, let

be a separable real Banach space.

If

through (
1.10
) using
(e_{k})
.

-algebra, except for

Proof.

We write

for the map obtained in Lemma

.

Now let

Corollary 3.10

is strongly measurable.

Proof.

Observe that
f(\cdot,x)\in L^{0}(S_{1};E)
for any fixed
x\in S_{2}
, i.e.
\hat{f}
is well-defined.
Since
f
is strongly measurable, we can approximate
f
by
\mathcal{A}_{1}\otimes\mathcal{A}_{2}
-simple functions. For
A\in\mathcal{A}_{1}\otimes\mathcal{A}_{2}
,
\mathbbm{1}_{A}(\cdot,x)\colon S_{1}\to\mathbb{R}
is measurable by Fubini’s theorem. By taking linear combinations in
E
and pointwise limits, it follows that
f(\cdot,x)
is strongly measurable, hence strongly
\mu_{1}
-measurable
[
22
, Prop. 1.1.16]
. So
\hat{f}
is well-defined. It remains to prove strong measurability of
\hat{f}
.

First consider real-valued functions. Define

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}\coloneqq\{h\colon S_{1}\times S_{2}\to\mathbb{R}\,:\;

\displaystyle h\text{ is bounded, measurable and }

\displaystyle\hat{h}\colon S_{2}\to L^{0}(S_{1};\mathbb{R})\colon x\mapsto h(\cdot,x)\text{ is strongly measurable}\}.

Note that
\mathcal{H}
is a linear space which is closed under bounded increasing pointwise (on
S_{1}\times S_{2}
) convergence, since the latter implies pointwise convergence on
S_{2}
in
L^{0}(S_{1};\mathbb{R})
.
Moreover,
\mathcal{H}\supset\{\mathbbm{1}_{A_{1}\times A_{2}}:A_{i}\in\mathcal{A}_{i}\}
. Indeed,
S_{2}\to L^{0}(S_{1};\mathbb{R})\colon x\mapsto\mathbbm{1}_{A_{1}\times A_{2}}(\cdot,x)=\mathbbm{1}_{A_{1}}(\cdot)\mathbbm{1}_{A_{2}}(x)
is itself an
\mathcal{A}_{2}
-simple function.
By the monotone class theorem, it follows that
\mathcal{H}
contains all bounded, measurable functions
S_{1}\times S_{2}\to\mathbb{R}
. In particular,
\mathcal{H}\supset\{\mathbbm{1}_{A}:A\in\mathcal{A}_{1}\otimes\mathcal{A}_{2}\}
.
Now put

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{\prime}\coloneqq\{f\colon S_{1}\times S_{2}\to E\,:\;

\displaystyle h\text{ is strongly measurable and }

\displaystyle\hat{f}\colon S_{2}\to L^{0}(S_{1};\mathbb{R})\colon x\mapsto f(\cdot,x)\text{ is strongly measurable}\}.

Note that
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}\supset\{h\otimes y:h\in\mathcal{H},y\in E\}
and
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}
is closed under linear combinations, thus
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}
contains all
\mathcal{A}_{1}\otimes\mathcal{A}_{2}
-simple functions
S_{1}\times S_{2}\to E
.
Furthermore,
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}
is closed under pointwise convergence on
S_{1}\times S_{2}
, again since the latter implies pointwise convergence on
S_{2}
in
L^{0}(S_{1};E)
.
Thus
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}
contains all strongly measurable
f\colon S_{1}\times S_{2}\to E
, completing the proof.
∎

The following result is standard.

Lemma A.5
.

Let
(S,\mathcal{A})
be a measurable space, let
Y
be a separable metric space and let
X
be separable Banach space. Let
\Phi\colon S\times Y\to X
be such that
\Phi(s,\cdot)\colon Y\to X
is continuous for each
s\in S
. Then
(i)
and
(ii)
are equivalent:

(i)

\Phi(\cdot,y)\colon S\to X
is
\mathcal{A}/\mathcal{B}(X)
-measurable for each
y\in Y
,

(ii)

\Phi\colon S\times Y\to X
is
\mathcal{A}\otimes\mathcal{B}(Y)/\mathcal{B}(X)
-measurable,

In addition, suppose that
Y
is a Banach space.
If
(i)
or
(ii)
holds, then

(iii)

if
\phi\colon S\to Y
is strongly
\mathcal{A}
-measurable, then
\Phi(\cdot,\phi(\cdot))\colon S\to X
is strongly
\mathcal{A}
-measurable.

Appendix B
Cylindrical Brownian motion

We consider a
U
-cylindrical Brownian motion
W
in the sense of Definition
1.7
. Some properties are discussed in Subsection
1.2
. In this Appendix
B
, we prove two additional lemmas.

We let
\mathcal{F}_{t}^{W}
be as in (
3.21
) and
\overline{\mathcal{F}_{t}}\coloneqq\mathcal{F}_{t}\cup\mathcal{N}
, where
\mathcal{N}
is the collection of all
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
-null sets. In addition, we define
\bar{\mathcal{F}}_{t^{+}}\coloneqq\cap_{h>0}\sigma(\overline{\mathcal{F}_{t+h}})
.
The next lemma shows that
W
is also a
U
-cylindrical Brownian motion with respect to
(\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{t})
,
(\bar{\mathcal{F}}_{t^{+}})
and
({\mathcal{F}}_{t}^{W})
.
This is standard for real-valued Brownian motions.
In the cylindrical case, independence aspects are slightly more subtle, so we include a proof.
To consider
W
as a
U
-cylindrical Brownian motion on a completed probability space, note that
L^{2}(\Omega,\overline{\mathcal{F}})\cong L^{2}(\Omega,\mathcal{F})
(but we do not have any inclusion as sets). Indeed, for
f,g
as in Lemma
A.2
,
\iota([f]_{\overline{\mathbb{P}}})\coloneqq[g]_{\mathbb{P}}
defines an isomorphism, where
[\cdot]_{\overline{\mathbb{P}}}
,
[\cdot]_{\mathbb{P}}
denote equivalence classes of a.s. equal functions, so we may identify
W
with
\iota^{-1}\circ W
and the latter will fulfill the conditions of Definition
1.7
with the filtrations
(\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{t})
,
(\bar{\mathcal{F}}_{t^{+}})
and
({\mathcal{F}}_{t}^{W})
.

Lemma B.1
.

Let
(e_{k})
be an orthonormal basis for
U
and let
W
be a
U
-cylindrical Brownian motion with respect to
(\Omega,{\mathcal{F}},{\mathbb{P}},(\mathcal{F}_{t}))
. Identify
W=(W^{k})
through (
1.10
).

Then for all
s\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
:
W(s+\cdot)-W(s)\perp\!\!\!\perp\mathcal{F}_{s}
as a
C(\mathbb{R}_{+};\mathbb{R}^{\infty})
-valued random variable. In particular,
W(t)-W(s)\perp\!\!\!\perp\mathcal{F}_{s}
for all
0\leq s<t
, as an
\mathbb{R}^{\infty}
-valued random variable.

Moreover,
W
is a
U
-cylindrical Brownian motion with respect to
(\Omega,\overline{\mathcal{F}},\overline{\mathbb{P}},(\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{t}))
,
(\Omega,\overline{\mathcal{F}},\overline{\mathbb{P}},(\bar{\mathcal{F}}_{t^{+}}))
and
(\Omega,\overline{\mathcal{F}},\overline{\mathbb{P}},({\mathcal{F}}_{t}^{W}))
.

Proof.

Let
s\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
.
By Lemma
A.1
, we have
\mathcal{B}(C(\mathbb{R}_{+};\mathbb{R}^{\infty}))=\sigma(\Pi_{n}^{r}:n\in\mathbb{N},r\in\mathbb{R}_{+})
, with
\Pi_{n}^{r}\colon C(\mathbb{R}_{+};\mathbb{R}^{\infty})\to\mathbb{R}^{\infty}\colon w\mapsto(w_{1}(r),\ldots,w_{n}(r),0,0,\ldots)
. Writing
\eta_{k}^{s}\coloneqq W^{k}(s+\cdot)-W^{k}(s)
for
k\in\mathbb{N}
, it follows that

\displaystyle\sigma(W(s+\cdot)-W(s))=\sigma\Big(\bigcap_{j=1}^{N}\big(\eta_{1}^{s}(s_{j}),\ldots,\eta_{n}^{s}(s_{j})\big)^{-1}(A_{j}):N,n\in\mathbb{N},s_{j}\in\mathbb{R}_{+},A_{j}\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\Big).

The intersections on the right-hand side form a
\pi
-system, thus it suffices to show that

(B.1)

\bigcap_{j=1}^{N}\big(\eta_{1}^{s}(s_{j}),\ldots,\eta_{n}^{s}(s_{j})\big)^{-1}(A_{j})\perp\!\!\!\perp{\mathcal{F}_{s}}.

The
W^{k}
are independent standard real-valued
({\mathcal{F}_{t}})
-Brownian motions, thus
B^{n}\coloneqq(W^{1},\ldots,W^{n})
is an
n
-dimensional standard
({\mathcal{F}_{t}})
-Brownian motion, as follows from Lévy’s characterization
[
27
, Th. 3.16]
. Therefore,

\big[B^{n}(s+s_{1})-B^{n}(s);B^{n}(s+s_{2})-B^{n}(s+s_{1});\ldots;B^{n}(s+s_{N})-B^{n}(s+s_{N-1})\big]\perp\!\!\!\perp{\mathcal{F}_{s}}
(viewing
B^{n}
as a row vector here). Putting
\phi\colon\mathbb{R}^{N\times n}\to\mathbb{R}^{N\times n},\,(\phi(A))_{jk}\coloneqq\sum_{i=1}^{j}A_{ik}
, we obtain

\displaystyle{\big(\eta_{k}^{s}(s_{j})\big)_{j=1}^{N}}{\vphantom{\big(\eta_{k}^{s}(s_{j})\big)}}_{k=1}^{n}=\big[B^{n}(s+s_{1})-B^{n}(s);\ldots;B^{n}(s+s_{N})-B^{n}(s)\big]

\displaystyle\quad=\phi\big(\big[B^{n}(s+s_{1})-B^{n}(s);B^{n}(s+s_{2})-B^{n}(s+s_{1});\ldots;B^{n}(s+s_{N})-B^{n}(s+s_{N-1})\big]\big)\perp\!\!\!\perp{\mathcal{F}_{s}},

using that
\phi
is measurable. In particular (
B.1
) holds, hence
W(s+\cdot)-W(s)\perp\!\!\!\perp\mathcal{F}_{s}
.
Also, we have
W(t)-W(s)=\pi_{\mathbb{R}^{\infty}}^{t-s}\circ(W(s+\cdot)-W(s))\perp\!\!\!\perp\mathcal{F}_{s}
, since
\pi_{\mathbb{R}^{\infty}}^{t-s}
defined by (
1.9
) is measurable.

Now let us prove the last part of the lemma.
The case
(\Omega,\overline{\mathcal{F}},\overline{\mathbb{P}},(\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{t}))
is trivial, noting that null sets do not affect independence in Definition
1.7
(iii)
.

The case
(\Omega,\overline{\mathcal{F}},\overline{\mathbb{P}},(\mathcal{F}_{t}^{W}))
is then almost trivial, noting that the
\mathcal{F}_{t}^{W}
-measurability of Definition
1.7
(ii)
holds if
f=\mathbbm{1}_{(0,s]}\otimes e_{k}
with
s\leq t
(recall (
3.21
)) and then using linearity and continuity (in
f
) of
W
and density in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+};U)
of the linear span of such
f
.

Regarding
(\Omega,\overline{\mathcal{F}},\overline{\mathbb{P}},(\bar{\mathcal{F}}_{t^{+}}))
, the non-trivial part is that
Wf\perp\!\!\!\perp\bar{\mathcal{F}}_{t^{+}}
for all
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+};U)
with
\mathrm{supp}(f)\subset[t,\infty)
. For such
f
, define
f_{n}\coloneqq f\mathbbm{1}_{[t+1/n,\infty)}
. Since
\mathrm{supp}(f_{n})\subset[t+1/n,\infty)
, we have
Wf_{n}\perp\!\!\!\perp\overline{\mathcal{F}_{t+1/n}}\supset\bar{\mathcal{F}}_{t^{+}}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Furthermore,
f=\lim_{n\to\infty}f_{n}
a.e. on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
, hence in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+};U)
by the Dominated Convergence Theorem. Consequently,
Wf_{n}\to Wf
in
L^{2}(\Omega)
and
Wf_{n_{j}}\to Wf
a.s. for a subsequence. Let
A\in\bar{\mathcal{F}}_{t^{+}}
and let
V\subset\mathbb{R}
be open. Then
\mathbbm{1}_{V}(Wf_{n_{j}})\to\mathbbm{1}_{V}(Wf)
a.s., so the Dominated Convergence Theorem gives

\mathbb{E}[\mathbbm{1}_{V}(Wf)\mathbbm{1}_{A}]=\lim_{j\to\infty}\mathbb{E}[\mathbbm{1}_{V}(Wf_{n_{j}})\mathbbm{1}_{A}]=\lim_{j\to\infty}\mathbb{E}[\mathbbm{1}_{V}(Wf_{n_{j}})]\mathbb{E}[\mathbbm{1}_{A}]=\mathbb{E}[\mathbbm{1}_{V}(Wf)]\mathbb{E}[\mathbbm{1}_{A}].

As the
\pi
-system of open sets generates
\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})
, we conclude that
Wf\perp\!\!\!\perp\bar{\mathcal{F}}_{t^{+}}
.
∎

Finally, we relate the
\sigma
-algebras generated by
W
and
W_{1}
, when
W_{1}
is a
Q
-Wiener process of the form (
2.10
) induced by
W
.
The next lemma shows that up to null sets,
W
and
W_{1}
share the same information.

Lemma B.2
.

Let
W
be a
U
-cylindrical Brownian motion on
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P},(\mathcal{F}_{t}))
and let
(e_{k})
be an orthonormal basis for
U
. Let
Z
be a separable metric space and let
u_{0}\colon\Omega\to Z
be measurable. Let
W=(W^{k})\colon\Omega\to C(\mathbb{R}_{+};\mathbb{R}^{\infty})
and
W_{1}\colon\Omega\to C(\mathbb{R}_{+};U_{1})
be defined by (
1.10
) and (
2.10
), respectively. For
t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\cup\{\infty\}
, define

(B.2)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\mathcal{F}_{t}^{W}\coloneqq\overline{\sigma((W^{k}(s))_{k\in\mathbb{N}}:s\in\bar{I}_{t})},\qquad{\mathcal{F}}_{t}^{u_{0},W}\coloneqq\overline{\sigma((u_{0},(W^{k}(s))_{k\in\mathbb{N}}):s\in\bar{I}_{t})},\\
&\mathcal{F}_{t}^{W_{1}}\coloneqq\overline{\sigma(W_{1}(s):s\in\bar{I}_{t})},\qquad\qquad\;{\mathcal{F}}_{t}^{u_{0},W_{1}}\coloneqq\overline{\sigma((u_{0},W_{1}(s)):s\in\bar{I}_{t})}.\end{split}

Then, the following holds for all
t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\cup\{\infty\}
:

(i)

{\mathcal{F}_{t}^{W}}=\overline{\sigma(W(\mathbbm{1}_{(0,s]}\otimes v):s\in\bar{I}_{t},v\in U)}
,

(ii)

\overline{\sigma(W|_{\bar{I}_{t}})}=\mathcal{F}_{t}^{W}=\mathcal{F}_{t}^{W_{1}}=\overline{\sigma(W_{1}|_{\bar{I}_{t}})}
,

(iii)

\overline{\sigma((u_{0},W|_{\bar{I}_{t}}))}=\mathcal{F}^{u_{0},W}_{t}=\mathcal{F}^{u_{0},W_{1}}_{t}=\overline{\sigma((u_{0},{W_{1}}|_{\bar{I}_{t}}))}.

Proof.

Using Lemma
A.1
with the continuous maps
\mathbb{R}^{\infty}\to\mathbb{R}\colon x\mapsto x_{k}
(
k\in\mathbb{N}
), we deduce that

(B.3)

\mathcal{F}_{t}^{W}=\overline{\sigma(W^{k}(s):s\in\bar{I}_{t},k\in\mathbb{N})}.

(i)
: ‘
\subset
’: This follows directly from (
B.3
) and (
1.10
).

‘
\supset
’: Let
s\in\bar{I}_{t}
and
v\in U
be arbitrary. By the Dominated Convergence Theorem, we have
\mathbbm{1}_{(0,s]}\otimes v=\sum_{j\in\mathbb{N}}\langle e_{j},v\rangle_{U}\mathbbm{1}_{(0,s]}\otimes e_{j}
in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+};U)
. Thus, as
W\in\mathcal{L}(L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+};U);L^{2}(\Omega))
, we find

(B.4)

W(\mathbbm{1}_{(0,s]}\otimes v)=\sum_{j\in\mathbb{N}}\langle e_{j},v\rangle_{U}W(\mathbbm{1}_{(0,s]}\otimes e_{j})

in
L^{2}(\Omega)
. In particular, the series converges in probability.
Each
\langle e_{j},v\rangle_{U}W(\mathbbm{1}_{(0,s]}\otimes e_{j})
is a centered Gaussian random variable, thus symmetric, hence the Itô-Nisio theorem
[
23
, Th. 6.4.1]
gives a.s. convergence of the series in (
B.4
). For each
N\in\mathbb{N}
, we have

\sigma\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{N}\langle e_{j},v\rangle_{U}W(\mathbbm{1}_{(0,s]}\otimes e_{j})\Big)\subset\mathcal{F}_{t}^{W}

since
\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathbb{R}\colon x\mapsto\sum_{j=1}^{N}\langle e_{j},v\rangle_{U}x_{j}
is measurable and by (
B.3
). Consequently,

\Omega_{0}\coloneqq\{\omega\in\Omega:\sum_{j\in\mathbb{N}}\langle e_{j},v\rangle_{U}W(\mathbbm{1}_{(0,s]}\otimes e_{j})(\omega)\text{ is Cauchy}\}\in\mathcal{F}_{t}^{W},

\mathbb{P}(\Omega_{0})=1
and

(B.5)

\sigma(\mathbbm{1}_{\Omega_{0}}W(\mathbbm{1}_{(0,s]}\otimes v))=\sigma(\mathbbm{1}_{\Omega_{0}}\sum_{j\in\mathbb{N}}\langle e_{j},v\rangle_{U}W(\mathbbm{1}_{(0,s]}\otimes e_{j}))\subset\mathcal{F}_{t}^{W},

where the last inclusion follows from the fact that
\mathbbm{1}_{\Omega_{0}}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\langle e_{j},v\rangle_{U}W(\mathbbm{1}_{(0,s]}\otimes e_{j})
is
\mathcal{F}_{t}^{W}
-measurable for each
N\in\mathbb{N}
, thus the same holds for the pointwise limit.
Since
\mathcal{F}_{t}^{W}
contains all null sets, (
B.5
) implies
\sigma(W(\mathbbm{1}_{(0,s]}\otimes v))\subset{\mathcal{F}_{t}^{W}}
, and ‘
\subset
’ follows.

(ii)
:
Viewing
W|_{\bar{I}_{t}}
and
W_{1}|_{\bar{I}_{t}}
as
C(\bar{I}_{t};\mathbb{R}^{\infty})
- and
C(\bar{I}_{t};U_{1})
-valued random variables respectively, it holds that

(B.6)

\mathcal{F}_{t}^{W}=\overline{\sigma(W|_{\bar{I}_{t}})},\quad\mathcal{F}_{t}^{W_{1}}=\overline{\sigma(W_{1}|_{\bar{I}_{t}})}.

Indeed, for
S=\mathbb{R}^{\infty}
and
S=U_{1}
, the continuous maps
\pi^{s}_{S,t}\colon C(\bar{I}_{t};S)\to S\colon w\mapsto w(s)
,
s\in\bar{I}_{t}
separate the points in
C(\bar{I}_{t};S)
, hence
\mathcal{B}(C(\bar{I}_{t};S))=\sigma(\pi^{s}_{S,t}:s\in\bar{I}_{t})
by Lemma
A.1
.
Recalling that
\pi^{s}_{\mathbb{R}^{\infty},t}\circ W=W(s)
and
\pi^{s}_{U_{1},t}\circ W_{1}=W_{1}(s)
yields (
B.6
). Thus it remains to show that
\mathcal{F}_{t}^{W}=\mathcal{F}_{t}^{W_{1}}
.

‘
\supset
’:
Let
s\in\bar{I}_{t}
. Recall that
W_{1}(s)=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}W(\mathbbm{1}_{(0,s]}\otimes e_{k})Je_{k}
in
L^{2}(\Omega)
, hence a.s., by the Itô-Nisio theorem
[
23
, Th. 6.4.1]
as before.
Thus, by the same arguments as in the proof of
(i)
, it suffices to show that
\sigma(W(\mathbbm{1}_{(0,s]}\otimes e_{k})Je_{k})\subset{\mathcal{F}_{t}^{W}}
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
.
This is trivial:
\mathbb{R}\to U_{1}\colon x\mapsto xJe_{k}
is measurable for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
, thus

\sigma(W(\mathbbm{1}_{(0,s]}\otimes e_{k})Je_{k})\subset\sigma(W(\mathbbm{1}_{(0,s]}\otimes e_{k}))\subset\mathcal{F}_{t}^{W}.

‘
\subset
’: Since
J
is a Hilbert–Schmidt operator, it is compact. By the proof of
[
35
, Th. 9.2]
, it has a singular value decomposition

(B.7)

Jv=\sum_{j\in\mathbb{N}}\tau_{j}\langle f_{j},v\rangle_{U}g_{j}\quad\text{in }U_{1},\quad\text{ for all }v\in U,

with
(f_{j})
an orthonormal basis for
U
,
(g_{j})
an orthonormal sequence in
U_{1}
and
\tau_{j+1}\geq\tau_{j}>0
for all
j
.
Now
[
35
, Th. 9.1(1)]
implies that
\mathrm{span}\{f_{j}:j\in\mathbb{N}\}
is dense in
U
, thus
(f_{j})
is an orthonormal basis for
U
.
By the a.s. convergence in
U_{1}
of (
2.10
) and by (
B.7
), we have for all
i\in\mathbb{N}
a.s.

\displaystyle\langle W_{1}(s),g_{i}\rangle_{U_{1}}=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}W(\mathbbm{1}_{(0,s]}\otimes e_{k})\langle Je_{k},g_{i}\rangle_{U_{1}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\sum_{j\in\mathbb{N}}\tau_{j}\langle f_{j},e_{k}\rangle_{U}W(\mathbbm{1}_{(0,s]}\otimes e_{k})\langle g_{j},g_{i}\rangle_{U_{1}}

\displaystyle=\tau_{i}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\langle f_{i},e_{k}\rangle_{U}W(\mathbbm{1}_{(0,s]}\otimes e_{k})

\displaystyle=\tau_{i}W(\mathbbm{1}_{(0,s]}\otimes f_{i}).

In the last equality we used the a.s. convergence of (
B.4
).
As
\tau_{i}>0
and
\langle\cdot,g_{i}\rangle_{U_{1}}\in U_{1}^{*}
, we obtain

\overline{\sigma(W(\mathbbm{1}_{(0,s]}\otimes f_{i}):i\in\mathbb{N})}=\overline{\sigma(\langle W_{1}(s),g_{i}\rangle_{U_{1}}:i\in\mathbb{N})}\subset\overline{\sigma(W_{1}(s))}.

Finally, by
(i)
,
{\mathcal{F}_{t}^{W}}
is independent of the chosen orthonormal basis

, giving with (
B.3
):

{\mathcal{F}_{t}^{W}}=\overline{\sigma(W(\mathbbm{1}_{(0,s]}\otimes f_{i}):s\in\bar{I}_{t},i\in\mathbb{N})}\subset\overline{\sigma(W_{1}(s):s\in\bar{I}_{t})}=\mathcal{F}_{t}^{W_{1}}.

(iii)
:
Since
u_{0}
and

take values in separable metric spaces, we have

\overline{\sigma((u_{0},W|_{\bar{I}_{t}}))}=\overline{\sigma(u_{0})\vee\sigma(W|_{\bar{I}_{t}}))}=\sigma(u_{0})\vee\mathcal{F}_{t}^{W}=\mathcal{F}_{t}^{u_{0},W}

and similarly for
W_{1}
. Applying
(ii)
to the second-last term completes the proof.
∎
