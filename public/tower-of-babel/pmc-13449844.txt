Prehabilitation and postoperative outcomes in colorectal surgery: an umbrella review
Authors: Molla Amsalu Tadesse, Agmuas Asichale Alimawu, Ludwig Matrisch, Melkam Mulugeta Abebe, Kidist Techale, Zenebe Bekele, Eniyew Assimie Alemu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundColorectal surgery caries a risk of postoperative morbidity and mortality. Prehabilitation may improve recovery and reduce complications in this population. This umbrella review evaluates the effects of prehabilitation on postoperative outcomes.MethodsGoogle Scholar, PubMed, the Cochrane Library, and HINARI were searched for systematic reviews comparing the effect of prehabilitation with usual care on postoperative complications, length of hospital stays and functional capacity in adults undergoing colorectal surgery. The methodological quality was assessed by using A Measurement Tool to Assess Systematic Reviews (AMSTAR-2). Overlap of primary studies was evaluated using the Corrected Covered Area (CCA), while evidence certainty by Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE). Heterogeneity was measured using I-squared statistic, and publication bias was examined using funnel plot, Egger’s test and Trim and Fill method.ResultsThis umbrella review included 19 systematic reviews with 27,065 participants, most of which were high quality (n = 13; 68.4%). Overall overlap was slight; however, moderate overlap was observed for postoperative complications, length of hospital stay (LOS), and functional capacity. Prehabilitation was associated with a 21% reduction in postoperative complications (risk ratio (RR) = 0.79, 95% CI: 0.69 to 0.89, I2 = 66.5%) with consistent finding in sensitivity analysis excluding short-duration nutritional interventions. It was also associated with a reduction in LOS of approximately 0.5 days (mean difference (MD) = -0.54 days, 95% CI: -0.87 to -0.20; I2 = 80.4%). Both with low certainty of evidence. Similarly, prehabilitation appeared to improve preoperative and postoperative functional capacity by approximately 25 m (MD = 24.78 m, 95% CI: 11.35 to 38.22, I2 = 67.84%) and 35 m (MD = 34.52 m, 95% CI: 6.44 to 62.60, I2 = 74.63%) respectively with low certainty evidence. Systematic reviews that did not present pooled results suggested that prehabilitation reduced postoperative complications (OR), had no significant effect on length of stay (SMD), and generally improved functional capacity (SMD or narrative synthesis).ConclusionPrehabilitation in colorectal surgery may reduce postoperative complications, may shorten hospital stay, may improve preoperative and postoperative functional capacity, highlighting faster recovery and improve postoperative quality of life. Findings should be interpreted with caution for the moderate overlap of primary studies, low level certainty, and significant heterogeneity.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s44158-026-00418-3.

Introduction

Colorectal surgery is the cornerstone for various benign and malignant conditions of the large bowel and rectum, such as colorectal cancer, ulcerative colitis, Crohn's disease, mechanical bowel obstruction, and recurrent diverticulitis [1]. Most colorectal conditions are treated with elective surgery, but emergency operations are also performed for bowel obstruction, perforation, and severe bleeding [2, 3]. Consequently, colorectal surgery represents a critical field with significant implications for public health and healthcare systems worldwide [4].

Despite advances in surgical techniques, anesthesia, and perioperative care, patients undergoing colorectal surgery experience postoperative complications, including anastomotic leak, postoperative ileus, functional decline, and prolonged hospital stay [1, 5]. Furthermore, patients may present with small bowel obstruction, deep vein thrombosis, or pulmonary embolism [6]. In patients undergoing colorectal cancer surgery, major morbidity and mortality occur in 8.7%, overall postoperative complications affect 31.6%, and 10–14% are readmitted within 30 days [7–9]. Old age [10], frailty [10, 11], multimorbidity [12] and malnutrition [13] are associated with adverse postoperative outcomes.

Prehabilitation interventions have been implemented in colorectal surgery to reduce perioperative morbidity and enhance postoperative recovery. According to a recent scoping review of 76 randomized trials, prehabilitation is defined as the process from diagnosis to surgery that incorporates a minimum of one intervention, such as respiratory training, exercise, nutritional support, or psychological preparation [14]. The interventions aim to improve physiological resilience and functional capacity, which may help patients recover more efficiently, tolerate surgical stress, and have better postoperative outcomes [14].

Randomized trials and systematic reviews have investigated the effects of prehabilitation on postoperative complications, length of hospital stay, and functional capacity in patients undergoing colorectal surgery [15–31]. Some systematic reviews stated that prehabilitation improves functional capacity, reduces complications, and shortens hospital stays [15, 16, 21]. In contrast, in other systematic reviews, prehabilitation interventions have no significant benefits [18, 30]. This discrepancy may be attributed to heterogeneity in patient characteristics, variability in control group definitions, variation in prehabilitation protocols, differences in adherence, and inconsistencies in outcome measurement and reporting. Therefore, this umbrella review synthesizes results across systematic reviews to resolve conflicting findings and evaluate the strength and consistency of the evidence, which allows identification of patterns and gaps across systematic reviews.

Methods

Protocol registration

The umbrella review was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Overviews of Reviews (PRIOR) [32]. The review was prospectively registered in the Prospective Systematic Review Registration of Overviews (PROSPERO) under registration identification alphanumeric code CRD420251038973.

Systematic search

Google Scholar, PubMed, the Cochrane Library, and HINARI were searched employing specific search terms on May 10, 2025. The search strategy for each database is detailed in (Supplementary Table 1). Restrictive filters were applied in selected databases to exclude non-systematic reviews and primary studies, to include human studies, and limit retrieval to full-text articles, ensuring eligibility and feasibility of data extraction. No language or time restrictions were applied to ensure comprehensive coverage of the evidence base. Manual screening of references and use of alternative search terms minimized selection bias.

The components of the review were developed based on the population, intervention, comparison, and outcome framework: population (adult patients undergoing colorectal surgery), intervention (prehabilitation), comparison (usual care), and outcomes (postoperative complications, length of hospital stay, and functional capacity). The PICO question was: In adult patients undergoing colorectal surgery, does prehabilitation, compared to usual preoperative care, reduce postoperative complications, improve functional capacity, and shorten the length of hospital stay? The search identified systematic reviews examining isolated nutritional interventions, exercise interventions, and multimodal prehabilitation. However, no systematic reviews evaluating psychological prehabilitation as a standalone intervention were retrieved.

Eligibility criteria

Inclusion criteria

Systematic reviews were included if they clearly described a systematic literature search, predefined eligibility criteria, and a process for study selection and data extraction, and if they reported pooled estimates comparing prehabilitation with usual care for at least one outcome, such as postoperative complications, length of hospital stay, or functional capacity in adults undergoing colorectal surgery. In addition, systematic reviews of mixed surgical populations were only included if they reported pooled effect estimates for at least one outcome relevant to colorectal surgery. Contemporary studies increasingly adopted a ≥ 7-day minimum duration for prehabilitation [14, 33]. However, given the exploratory nature of nutritional prehabilitation evidence, we used a broader inclusion criterion to capture all available studies. In addition, due to the limited number of isolated nutritional systematic reviews, applying a strict duration threshold was not feasible. This may introduce selection bias related to the inclusion of short-duration (< 7 days) nutritional interventions.

Exclusion criteria

We excluded non-systematic review studies, conference abstracts, and non-peer-reviewed reviews. Reviews of mixed surgical populations were excluded unless they reported colorectal surgery specific pooled effect estimates for prehabilitation interventions in adults.

Study selection

After identifying relevant systematic reviews, duplicate records were removed. Two independent reviewers (AAA and ZBT) screened titles and abstracts for eligibility according to predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Full texts of potentially eligible systematic reviews were then assessed independently by the reviewers, with disagreements resolved through consultation with a principal investigator (MAT). Systematic reviews meeting all criteria were included in the umbrella review, and reasons for exclusion at the full-text stage were documented.

Data extraction and retrieval

The standard data extraction form was designed by the principal investigator. The data extraction form contains different study characteristics, including study author, publication year, prehabilitation type, design of primary studies, diagnosis, and risk of bias assessment. Furthermore, it incorporated the effect estimate of postoperative complications, length of hospital stay and functional capacity with 95% confidence intervals, as well as the study conclusion. The data extraction form was pilot tested to ensure comprehensiveness and clarity.

Additionally, intervention characteristics were extracted when reported, including dose, intensity, sessions per week, frequency within each session, duration of prehabilitation, composition of nutritional interventions, and type of psychological support separately to help contextualize and reconcile conflicting findings (Supplementary Tables 2–4). Two investigators (AAA and ZBT) independently extracted the data, and the extracted information was compared for consistency. Discrepancies between them were resolved by discussion with the principal investigator. The extracted data were in Microsoft Word, and the outcomes with subgroup variables were converted to Excel to make the data easy for statistical analysis. When multiple systematic reviews addressed the same outcome, all were included irrespective of recency, sample size, quality, or number of datasets. Differences across estimates were documented and synthesized narratively to avoid double counting but transparently reflected variability across systematic reviews. Authors of systematic review were not contacted, and no new pooled estimates were calculated.

Handling of comparators

The control groups are variably defined in different systematic reviews, such as standard care, usual care, no prehabilitation, Enhanced Recovery After Surgery (ERAS), and rehabilitation. The included systematic reviews compared prehabilitation with one or more control groups. While ERAS pathways are increasingly adopted in colorectal surgery, their implementation is not yet universal. In the included studies, comparators described as ERAS were therefore considered as usual care for the purposes of analysis, acknowledging that this classification may not fully reflect practice variations across settings [34, 35].

Outcome measurement

Postoperative complications

Fifteen systematic reviews reported postoperative complications [15–18, 20, 23, 24, 26–30, 36–38]. The majority defined it using the Clavien-Dindo classification. Most of these systematic reviews focused on infectious complications, including surgical site infections, pulmonary complications, urinary tract infections, intra-abdominal abscesses, cardiac complications, and ileus, which typically occur within 30 days postoperatively. Six systematic reviews reported the risk of postoperative complications using odds ratios [15–18, 20, 23] and nine studies reported using risk ratios [24, 26–30, 36–39]. One systematic review was not included in the pooled analysis, as the reported odds ratio could not be reliably transcribed into a risk ratio [23].

Length of hospital stay

Fourteen systematic reviews examined the length of hospital stay; twelve reported using the mean number of days patients remained hospitalized following colorectal surgery [16–20, 23, 24, 26, 28, 29, 36, 37]. Two systematic reviews, however, presented the outcome as a standardized mean difference with 95% confidence intervals [15, 30].

Functional capacity

Thirteen systematic reviews reported functional capacity. Functional capacity was defined as a composite outcome encompassing both preoperative and postoperative measures of physical performance, including the six-minute walk test (6MWT), cardiopulmonary exercise testing or shuttle walk tests reported in the included studies. Data on functional capacity were extracted from each study, capturing changes from baseline to surgery (preoperative) [16, 18, 20], from surgery to follow-up (postoperative) [19, 24, 26, 29, 30, 40] and both preoperative functional capacity, postoperative functional capacity [15, 31, 41, 42].

The time of measurement of functional capacity is variably defined in the included systematic reviews. One study did not specify when preoperative measurements were taken [16], while others assessed functional capacity 14 days before surgery [20], immediately before surgery [15, 18, 19, 30, 41, 42], two weeks before surgery [11]. Postoperative functional capacity was typically evaluated 4 to 8 weeks after surgery [11, 15, 19, 24, 26, 29, 40, 42]. Most systematic reviews reported functional capacity using the six-minute walk test (6MWT). Three systematic reviews assessed functional capacity using the 6MWT alongside cardiopulmonary exercise testing (CPET) or shuttle walk tests [11, 20, 26]. Pooled effect sizes were only available for the 6MWT; data from CPET and shuttle walk tests could not be synthesized. The results pertain specifically to the 6MWT, with preoperative and postoperative assessments analyzed separately, and postoperative measurements corresponding to 4 to 8 weeks after surgery.

Seven systematic reviews reported functional capacity using mean differences at 95% CI [16, 18, 20, 24, 26, 27, 31] whereas five systematic reviews reported using standardized mean differences at 95% CI [15, 29, 30, 41, 42]. One study measured functional capacity but did not report the pooled statistic due to heterogeneity [19].

Assessment of qualities of included systematic reviews

Two investigators (AAA and KTZ) assessed the qualities of included systematic reviews using A Measurement Tool for Assessing Multiple Systematic Reviews (AMSTAR-2).

AMSTAR-2 contains 16 domains including seven critical. The domains include clear research questions, protocol registration, comprehensive literature searches, study selection, data extraction, risk-of-bias assessment, and appropriate statistical methods. It also considers bias, heterogeneity, publication bias, and conflicts of interest [43]. Systematic reviews are rated high (no or one non-critical weakness), moderate (multiple non-critical weaknesses), low (one critical weakness), or critically low (two or more critical weaknesses) [44].

Assessment of certainty of evidence (GRADE)

Certainty of evidence was assessed using GRADE based on five domains: publication bias, indirectness, imprecision, risk of bias and inconsistency. Only studies contributing to the pooled analyses were included for each outcome, (7 of 13 studies reporting functional capacity and 11 of 14 studies reporting LOS, 14 studies reporting postoperative complications) ensuring that certainty ratings reflected the quantitative evidence and were not influenced by studies reporting outcomes without effect estimates. Certainty was not stratified by prehabilitation type or study quality. Confidence in estimated effect was reflected in the rating was categorized as high, moderate, low or very low certainty [45, 46].

Assessment of overlap and non-independence

Given the presence of multiple systematic reviews addressing overlapping clinical questions, we adopted a second-order (meta-review) approach to summarize evidence at the review level. To assess potential non-independence arising from shared primary studies across systematic reviews, overlap was quantified using the corrected covered area (CCA), calculated overall and separately for postoperative complications, length of hospital stays, and functional capacity.

Statistical analysis

STATA version 17 was used to conduct statistical analysis. A random effects model was used considering population and methodological difference between the included systematic reviews. Heterogeneity across systematic reviews was assessed using the I2 statistic and categorized as low ≤ 25%, moderate ≤ 50% and significant > 50% [48].

Subgroup analyses were conducted for length of stay and postoperative complications based on the type of prehabilitation (exercise, nutrition, or multimodal) and the quality of the included systematic reviews (low and moderate versus high). However, subgroup analyses were not conducted for functional capacity because only a limited number of studies reported both preoperative and postoperative outcomes, resulting in insufficient data to support reliable subgroup comparisons.

Sensitivity analysis was also conducted to determine the effect of individual meta-analysis on the pooled effect estimates for postoperative complications and length of hospital stay. To address concerns regarding intervention duration and consistency with contemporary definitions of prehabilitation, we conducted a sensitivity analysis. This analysis excluded systematic reviews of nutritional interventions with durations of less than 7 days. This approach was undertaken to assess the robustness of the results when applying a ≥ 7-day threshold, as increasingly recommended in recent prehabilitation literature. Funnel plot was used for testing publication bias through visual assessment of asymmetry whereas Egger’s test to statistically confirm the observation in funnel plot. In addition, we conducted a Trim and fill analysis to validate the findings of Egger’s test or assess the impact of publication bias on the pooled effect estimate of LOS. For functional capacity, publication bias was not assessed because fewer than the recommended number of studies were available, rendering funnel plot interpretation and statistical tests for asymmetry unreliable. A p-value < 0.05 with 95% CI in all statistical tests were considered as significant.

Furthermore, the effects of prehabilitation on continuous outcomes (LOS, preoperative and postoperative functional capacity) were evaluated by pooling mean differences. These outcomes were reported in consistent units: days for length of hospital stay and meters for functional capacity. Since some number of studies reported the length of hospital stay [15, 30] and functional capacity [15, 25, 29, 30, 41] using standardized mean differences (SMD), we decided against a quantitative synthesis of these results to avoid generating imprecise or unreliable estimates. Conversions for mean differences (MD) and standardized mean differences (SMD) were not performed, as such transformations were not feasible.

The reviews evaluated exercise-only, nutritional-only, and multimodal prehabilitation interventions in colorectal surgical populations. Multi-modal prehabilitation was the most frequently examined intervention, followed by exercise interventions.

Exercise interventions included aerobic training (walking, cycling, ergometer-based exercise), resistance training (weights, resistance bands, or body weight), flexibility exercises, and, in some cases, inspiratory or respiratory muscle training. Exercise intensity ranged from low to high, including either continuous or interval training, with program durations of 1 to 8 weeks and exercise frequency of 2 to 7 sessions per week. The duration of each session ranged from approximately 20–60 min. The exercise interventions were fully supervised programs, fully unsupervised home-based programs, or mixed. Nutrition interventions primarily involved standard oral nutritional supplements and immunonutrition formulas enriched with arginine, omega-3 fatty acids, and nucleotides. The interventions were commonly self-administered, frequently accompanied by dietary counseling, and implemented for 5 to 14 days.

Multimodal interventions combined exercise and nutritional components and frequently incorporated psychological interventions such as anxiety reduction strategies, relaxation techniques, mindfulness, or cognitive-behavioral therapy. Several multimodal interventions include frail older adults, and tailored interventions to frailty status or comorbidity burden. Program duration in multimodal interventions typically ranged from 2 to 8 weeks preoperatively, with exercise frequencies of approximately 2–5 sessions per week and session durations of 30–60 min.

Reporting of adherence was inconsistent across reviews. When reported, adherence ranged from approximately 60% to 98%. Notably, reviews comparing intervention strategies consistently suggested that multimodal prehabilitation particularly the combination of exercise and nutritional interventions was more effective than unimodal approaches (Supplementary Table 3).

Quality of included systematic reviews

Most systematic reviews showed good adherence to several AMSTAR-2 criteria. Thirteen systematic reviews received high-quality scores [15, 16, 18, 20, 23–25, 28–31, 36, 37], one study was rated as moderate quality [26], and five studies were rated as low quality [17, 19, 27, 38, 41] (Supplementary Table 4).

Certainty of evidence (GRADE)

The GRADE assessment of the three prehabilitation outcomes in colorectal surgery consistently indicated low certainty of evidence. For postoperative complications and length of hospital stay, the main factors leading to downgrading were risk of bias and inconsistency, whereas imprecision, indirectness, and publication bias were not considered serious concerns. Preoperative functional capacity assessed using the six-minute walk test (6MWT) was rated as low certainty due to serious risk of bias and inconsistency. Similarly, postoperative functional capacity was judged to have low certainty, downgraded for serious risk of bias and inconsistency, while imprecision and indirectness were not serious, and publication bias was unable to assess (Supplementary Table 5).

Degree of overlap in primary studies

Overlap of primary studies across systematic reviews was quantified using the corrected covered area (CCA). When all outcomes were considered together, overlap was slight (CCA = 2.1%). Subgroup effect of prehabilitation type on postoperative complications

The between group difference was non-significant (p = 0.62) but the magnitude of effect consistently favored exercise and multimodal prehabilitation interventions. Exercise-based prehabilitation appeared to reduce postoperative complications by 28% compared with usual care (RR = 0.72; 95% CI: 0.55 to 0.90), with significant heterogeneity across studies (I2 = 65%). Assessment of publication bias

Funnel plots and Egger’s test

Visual inspection of the funnel plots suggested symmetry for postoperative complications, whereas asymmetry was observed for LOS (Supplementary Fig. 7). Consistent with this, Egger’s test did not indicate small-study effects for postoperative complications (β₁ = 0.00, SE = 0.67, p = 1.00), but suggested potential publication bias for LOS (β₁ = − 2.30, SE = 0.48, p < 0.01). However, trim-and-fill analysis for LOS did not impute any missing studies, and the adjusted pooled effect size remained unchanged (EXP[ES] = 0.55; 95% CI: 0.36 to 0.87) suggesting possible small-study effects.

Discussion

This umbrella review aimed to synthesize and critically evaluate existing systematic reviews, examine their methodological quality, assess the certainty of the evidence, and provide a comprehensive overview of the effects of prehabilitation on postoperative complications, LOS and functional capacity before and after colorectal surgery. Most included systematic reviews demonstrated good methodological compliance to AMSTAR-2 items. The majority were rated as high quality. Small number of moderate- and low-quality systematic reviews were identified, highlighting variability in review conduct.

The finding suggests that prehabilitation may decrease postoperative complications, may shorten hospital stays and may improve preoperative and postoperative functional capacity with low certainty of evidence and significant heterogeneity. This could reduce the degree of confidence in the effects consistency, magnitude and generalizability. Variability in control groups as well as variations in clinical diagnoses, prehabilitation protocols, adherence and patient characteristics such as frailty, nutritional status may all contribute to heterogeneity across outcomes. Moderate overlap across all outcomes may further affect confidence in consistency and limit the independence of pooled findings, emphasizing cautious interpretation of results.

The narrative evidence from included reviews consistently suggested that multimodal prehabilitation, particularly exercise combined with nutrition, may be more effective than unimodal interventions. However, pooled subgroup analyses showed no significant differences between intervention types for either postoperative complications or length of hospital stay (LOS). Within individual subgroup analyses, exercise and multimodal programs were associated with statistically significant reductions in postoperative complications, and multimodal and nutrition-focused programs were associated with reduced LOS compared with usual care. Nutrition-only interventions did not significantly reduce postoperative complications, and exercise-based programs had no measurable effect on LOS. Variations in baseline nutritional status, differences in intervention duration and intensity, inadequate adherence monitoring, and concurrent perioperative care practices may have attenuated observed effects across all intervention types. Exercise-based programs additionally require structured physiotherapy, additional resources, and active patient engagement, which may limit their effectiveness. In addition, results were generally consistent across reviews of varying methodological quality; however, effects on LOS may have been overestimated in lower-quality reviews.

The sensitivity analyses also showed consistent reduction of postoperative complications and shortens hospital stay duration. The effect of prehabilitation on postoperative complications remained consistent after exclusion of short-duration nutritional interventions, with similar pooled estimates across analyses. This is consistent with the recommendations from the European Society for Clinical Nutrition and Metabolism guidelines, which advocate preoperative nutritional support for at-risk patients ideally for 10–14 days (minimum 7 days) [50, 51]. However, the small number of systematic reviews with short duration nutritional interventions may need cautious interpretation.

The Egger’s test for postoperative complications indicated the absence of publication bias and a potential small-study effect for LOS. This was not confirmed by the trim-and-fill analysis, which itself has methodological limitations. Therefore, while the observed benefits appear consistent, uncertainty remains regarding the potential influence of unpublished or missing studies.

Prehabilitation programs improve postoperative complications through different mechanisms, including enhancing physiologic reserve, particularly muscle strength and cardiorespiratory function, which are critical to cope with surgical stress and facilitate functional recovery [52]. Furthermore, nutritional prehabilitation may improve malnutrition, immune function and reduced susceptibility to infection, and facilitate wound healing [53–55].

This umbrella review findings are supported by previous studies which stated that prehabilitation reduced the odds of postoperative complications compared to different control groups [56]. This study showed that prehabilitation is effective at reducing length of hospital stay only when exercise is added [56]. Most studies investigating the effectiveness of prehabilitation programs have introduced and recommended programs lasting approximately four weeks. However, this duration has been questioned due to concerns that a longer intervention may reduce patient compliance, ultimately decreasing acceptability and adherence to the program [57]. To address these concerns, some researchers have suggested a shorter duration of just seven days, which may enhance ease of application and improve tolerance among patients [58].

A notable gap in the literature is the lack of systematic reviews evaluating psychological prehabilitation as a standalone intervention, as most evidence is embedded within multimodal prehabilitation programs. Perioperative psychological interventions have been shown to reduce preoperative anxiety and stress, which may positively influence postoperative recovery and surgical outcomes [59]. A similar study stated that multimodal prehabilitation improved postoperative outcomes in cancer surgery, with nutritional prehabilitation forming a significant part of the therapy given [55]. A previous study showed that both parenteral and enteral perioperative nutritional support reduced postoperative morbidity, especially in high-risk patients, by modulating postsurgical immunosuppression and inflammatory responses [60]. The commonly utilized immunonutrition in the umbrella review includes arginine, omega-3 fatty acids, nucleotides, and glutamine. Arginine facilitates the wound healing by acting as a primary fuel cell for T-cells under stressful condition which further maintain immune function and decreases the risk of infection [61]. In the same manner, omega-3 fatty acids improve the immune function by increasing the production of resolvins and protectins, as well as by improving lymphocyte function, which play a role in the resolution of inflammation and enhance wound healing [62, 63].

Furthermore, nucleotides improve immune function by replicating T-cells for their maturation, proliferation, and function [63]. In addition, nucleotides increase villus heights, mucosal proteins, and brush border enzymes in the gastrointestinal tract [64], whereas glutamine improves the immune function by facilitating B-cell proliferation, cytokine production, T-cell proliferation, and neutrophil production, which can reduce the postoperative complications [65]. Dietary nucleotide supplementation has also been associated with enhanced intestinal mucosal integrity and supports tissue repair processes following injury and surgical stress [60]. Perioperative nucleotides have been associated with increases in immune markers (CD3, CD4 T cells), reductions in pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-6), reduce leukocyte and neutrophil peaks, indicating enhanced immune competence, decreased inflammation after surgery and decrease antibiotic treatment requirement [66, 67]. Even though the immunonutrition formulas are more costly, studies have suggest that they can reduce overall financial expense due to reductions in complications and hospital days in gastrointestinal surgery [68].

Strength and limitations of the study

This umbrella review has several potential strengths. First, the review incorporated different systematic reviews in both cancer and non-cancer patients supporting the generalizability of the findings to various colorectal surgeries. Moreover, the umbrella review addressed different relevant outcomes providing comprehensive evidence on the effect of prehabilitation in patients undergoing colorectal surgery. However, the umbrella review is not without limitations. First, some included systematic reviews contained overlapping primary studies, which could lead to potential double counting that may influence the apparent consistency of findings. Second, the need to convert odds ratios to risk ratios, along with heterogeneous reporting of length of hospital stay and functional capacity across reviews, may have reduced the certainty and interpretability of the evidence. Third, significant heterogeneity in patient characteristics, prehabilitation protocols, frequency of interventions, intensity for exercise intervention, type of nutritional interventions and variability in control groups may reduce the precision and generalizability of outcomes. Fourth, subgroup analysis and publication bias for functional capacity could not be assessed due to the limited number of studies, potentially limiting the reliability and certainty of the pooled estimate. Lastly, the low certainty of evidence in postoperative complications, LOS and preoperative functional capacity may limit the confidence in the findings, suggesting that the true effect of prehabilitation may differ from the observed results.

Conclusion

Prehabilitation in colorectal surgery may reduce postoperative complications, may shorten hospital stay, and may improve preoperative and postoperative functional status. However, the evidence for all outcomes is of low certainty, with significant heterogeneity and moderate overlap of primary studies. These findings suggest that patient-specific prehabilitation programs may support faster recovery, enhance functional performance, improve outcomes, and reduce hospitalization costs and resource use, particularly in frail patients.

Recommendation

Prehabilitation programs may be considered as part of perioperative care pathways for patients undergoing colorectal surgery, taking into account each patient’s circumstances and surgical indications. However, the low certainty of evidence, significant heterogeneity and moderate overlap of primary studies may suggest a careful patient specific programs and cautious interpretation. Researchers are encouraged to adopt standardized, evidence-based prehabilitation protocols and consistent outcome measures across studies to improve comparability and reduce heterogeneity. Future research on prehabilitation interventions should define the optimal components, intensity, and duration, with particular emphasis on quality of life and long-term functional recovery in patients undergoing colorectal surgery.
