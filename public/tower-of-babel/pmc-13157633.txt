Full-term neonatal bilateral Suppurative Parotitis: a case report
Authors: Mohammad Obada Alsadi, Dina Aldebs, Sarah Al Ali, Fadi Tanous, Sedra Nasr, Rasha Ebraheem, Sondos Alsaraireh, Abdallah Alnsour, Khaledah Aladwan, Nader Eid
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAcute suppurative parotitis is rare in neonates, typically unilateral, and linked to risk factors like prematurity. We report an extremely rare case of acute bilateral suppurative parotitis in a 10-day-old, full-term male neonate who lacked established risk factors, being exclusively breastfed without dehydration. The patient presented with fever and rapid bilateral parotid swelling. Initial labs showed leukocytosis and elevated CRP, but serum amylase was notably low, confirming its limited diagnostic utility in newborns. Due to rapid clinical deterioration despite empirical intravenous antibiotics (vancomycin/meropenem), bilateral incision and drainage were performed on day three. Drainage culture confirmed Staphylococcus aureus. The infant recovered completely after the surgical intervention and a 10-day antibiotic course. This case highlights the exceptional presentation of bilateral involvement and the absence of common risk factors in a full-term infant, emphasizing the need for prompt surgical drainage when antibiotics fail.Keywords: neonatal, bilateral, Suppurative parotitis, Staphylococcus aureus

Introduction

Inflammation of the salivary glands in the neonatal period is an uncommon clinical entity. Although acute suppurative parotitis is a common diagnosis in the adult population, its occurrence in pediatric patients, particularly newborns, is rare. Bacterial pathogens are the primary cause, and S. aureus is consistently reported as the most common etiological agent [1–3]. A total of 144 cases were reported in the literature reviewed up until 2011 [4]. This case details the presentation and management of a 10-day-old male neonate admitted with acute, bilateral, and rapidly progressing suppurative parotitis.

Case presentation

A 10-day-old male neonate, born full term via elective cesarean section (due to multiple prior cesareans), was admitted with a 3-day history of intermittent fever reaching 39°C and the mother noticed that the baby became irritable during feeding and spent less time at the breast. This was followed by an inflammatory swelling in front of both ears that rapidly increased in size (Fig. 1). The parents reported no history of trauma to the newborn’s head or face, and the mother denied any history of skin infections.

Clinical presentation of the 10-day-old male neonate on admission. The images display bilateral, firm, erythematous, and tender over the parotid regions, extending across the angle of the mandible, consistent with acute suppurative parotitis.

The antenatal and perinatal history were unremarkable. Birth weight was 3500 g, length 50 cm, and head circumference 36 cm. The infant was exclusively breastfed.

On examination, he was febrile (38°C) with bilateral, firm, erythematous, and tender swellings over the parotid regions extending across the angle of the mandible. Mucous membranes appeared normal, and there were no signs of dehydration.

Laboratory investigations revealed: WBC: 16000/mm3 (59% neutrophils, 31% lymphocytes) Hemoglobin: 14.1 g/dl Platelets: 585000/mm3 CRP: 9.8 mg/l Serum amylase: 3 U/l. A lumbar puncture was performed, and the results were normal.

Neck ultrasound showed bilaterally enlarged, hypoechoic parotid glands with periparenchymal fluid and increased vascularity (Fig. 2), suggestive of acute suppurative parotitis. Multiple reactive lymph nodes were also seen. Brain ultrasound was normal.

Doppler ultrasound images of the parotid gland. The scan demonstrates an enlarged, hypoechoic parotid gland parenchyma with a marked increase in vascularity within the gland.

Empirical intravenous antibiotics (vancomycin and meropenem) were initiated after obtaining blood, urine, and CSF cultures. Due to worsening swelling, bilateral incision and drainage of the parotid glands were performed on day 3 of hospitalization. Purulent drainage cultures yielded S. aureus, sensitive to multiple antibiotics including vancomycin, gentamicin, teicoplanin, clindamycin, and linezolid. While other cultures showed no pathogens.

The fever Improved significantly following surgical drainage. The infant completed a 10-day course of intravenous antibiotics and was discharged on oral ciprofloxacin and clindamycin. Repeat labs showed improvement with WBC 13200/mm3, platelets 261 000/mm3, and CRP 1 mg/l.

Discussion

Bilateral parotid gland infection is seldom reported in neonates [5], and it is typically characterized by unilateral involvement. In a comprehensive recent literature review, Mori et al. found that involvement was unilateral in approximately 83% of the reported cases (60 out of 72 cases) [2]. The presentation of our case as bilateral suppurative parotitis constitutes an extreme rarity, placing our patient within the minimal remaining minority of documented instances.

Risk factors associated with neonatal suppurative parotitis typically include low birth weight, prematurity, dehydration, oral trauma, and gavage feeding. These factors increase susceptibility to infection and often lead to feeding difficulties and dehydration [5, 6]. Consequently, the reported incidence is significantly higher in vulnerable populations, ranging from 3.8 to 14 per 10 000 newborns hospitalized in the Neonatal Intensive Care Unit (NICU), compared to an estimated 1 per 100 000 in full-term newborns [6]. In striking contrast to the typical profile and despite the extremely low incidence rate reported for full-term neonates, our patient lacked nearly all established risk factors. The infant was a full-term male neonate with a normal birth weight of 3500 g, exclusively breastfed, and showed no signs of dehydration or prior oral injuries. The complete absence of these well-documented predisposing factors renders this case exceptionally rare and creates a significant diagnostic and pathophysiological paradox.

It is noteworthy that while our patient was a healthy, full-term neonate, the mode of delivery might have served as a potential risk factor. Recent literature suggests that delivery via cesarean section may predispose infants to neonatal suppurative parotitis—a correlation that has remained largely under-reported in previous medical reviews. According to Mori et al. (2022), the prevalence of cesarean sections among affected neonates was approximately 49%, which significantly exceeds the global average of 21% [2]. Our case further supports this hypothesis, as the infant was born via elective cesarean section and subsequently developed an aggressive bilateral infection despite the absence of traditional risk factors like dehydration.

Regarding laboratory diagnostics, leukocytosis exceeding 15 000/mm3 with neutrophil predominance is a common finding, present in 71% of cases. However, serum amylase levels appear to be unreliable; they were only measured in 9 out of 32 patients, and even when measured, were elevated in less than half (45%). This lack of consistent elevation is postulated to be related to the immaturity of salivary isoenzyme activity in neonates [7, 8]. Our patient's findings corroborated these patterns, demonstrating a similar inflammatory response and confirming the limited utility of this marker in newborns, our patient's serum amylase level was notably low (3 U/l) despite the presence of acute parotid inflammation. However, viral testing was not performed as the bacterial etiology was definitively confirmed by purulent drainage culture.

Antibiotic treatment alone is effective for 78% of reported instances. However, failure to achieve noticeable clinical progress, specifically reduced parotid swelling, within a 24- to 48-h window necessitates escalation to surgical drainage [7, 8]. The aggressive infection, marked by the simultaneous worsening of bilateral swelling even after starting empirical antibiotics (vancomycin and meropenem), necessitated immediate surgical intervention. Therefore, the decision to proceed with bilateral incision and drainage on the third day of hospitalization was appropriate and consistent with established recommendations for antibiotic-resistant suppurative parotitis.

Conclusion

This case highlights the extreme rarity of acute bilateral suppurative parotitis in a full-term neonate who lacked common predisposing risk factors. The findings confirm that S. aureus remains the primary pathogen, and that serum amylase is an unreliable marker in this population. The clinical course emphasizes the critical need for prompt bilateral incision and drainage when immediate clinical improvement is not achieved within 48 hours of starting aggressive antibiotic therapy.
