A Phase-field Model for Apoptotic Cell Death
Daniel A. Vaughan 1,2 , Anna M. Piccinini 2 , Mischa Zelzer 2 , Etienne Farcot 1 , Bindi S. Brook 1 , Kris Van-der-Zee 1 , and Luis Espath 1

Abstract
. The process of programmed cell death, namely apoptosis, is a natural mechanism that regulates healthy tissue, multicellular structures, and homeostasis. An improved understanding of apoptosis can significantly enhance our knowledge of biological processes and systems. For instance, pathogens can manipulate the apoptotic process to either evade immune detection or to facilitate their spread. Furthermore of particular clinical interest is the ability of cancer cells to evade apoptosis, hence allowing them to survive and proliferate uncontrollably. Thus, in this work, we propose a phase-field model for simulating cellular apoptosis induced by a cytotoxin, including deriving the configurational mechanics underlying such phenomena. Along with exploring varying conditions needed to initiate or reduce apoptosis, this can serve as a starting point for computational therapeutic testing. To showcase model capabilities, we present simulations exhibiting different types of cellular dynamics produced when varying the mechanisms underlying apoptosis. The model is subsequently applied to probe different morphological transitions, such as cell shrinkage, membrane blebbing, cavity formation and fragmentation. Lastly, we compare the characteristics observed in our simulations to electron microscopy images, providing additional support for the model. 1.
Introduction

Apoptosis, the process of programmed cell death, is a key component in a range of processes including cellular turnover, robust immune response to pathogens, embryonic development and chemical-induced cell death
[
1
]
. Irregularities in this process, resulting in either enhanced or reduced apoptosis, are a causative factor in many human diseases such as autoimmune and neurodegenerative disorders (excessive cell loss precipitating conditions such as Alzheimer’s disease) and many types of cancer (inadequate cell clearance)
[
2
,
3
,
4
]
. Hence, the ability to modulate and understand the life or death of a cell is of high interest. Apoptotic cells can be identified by their distinct morphological features. These include separation from other cells, chromatin condensation and DNA fragmentation, irregular bulging in the plasma membrane (finger formation), cell shrinkage, and the formation of apoptotic bodies then degraded in lysosomes
[
5
,
6
]
. These morphological changes can be observed using microscopy techniques, with electron microscopy providing superior cellular details and reproducibility compared to light microscopy
[
7
]
. The highly complex and sophisticated mechanisms of apoptosis can be triggered by a range of factors including components of the immune system and cytotoxic molecules administered as part of a chemotherapeutic regime
[
1
]
. Given the complexity of apoptosis, a thorough understanding of how environmental cues shape this process could provide a valuable platform for devising targeted therapies and refining diagnostic strategies.

In this study we develop a multi-component phase-field model incorporating chemical reactions between a cyto (cell) and a cytotoxin phase, to understand and model the physical characteristics exhibited by a cell undergoing apoptosis. This employs a coupled set of partial differential equations for the phase fields
\varphi
(cyto) and
\sigma
(cytotoxin). Phase-field modelling of apoptosis remains a relatively unexplored area of modelling for cellular dynamics and therefore provides a opportunity for this model to serve as a novel tool for investigating apoptosis. The use of multi-component phase-field models with reaction equations is well-established, with extensive literature not only in general contexts
[
8
,
9
,
10
]
, but also in applications to biological phenomena, with published work to date focusing predominantly on modeling cell growth
[
11
,
12
,
13
]
. To obtain a chemo-mechanical phase-field model to produce the desired dynamics of finger formation, we take inspiration from Kobayashi’s work on solidification
[
14
]
. Furthermore, to ensure the reaction component of the apoptosis model stays in certain bounds we utilise the same substitution as Kobayashi
[
14
]
. The apoptosis model is also inspired by the system proposed by Fried and Gurtin
[
15
]
.

We address the need for a multi-component phase-field model for simulating apoptotic behavior by developing a novel framework. The framework builds upon the mathematical structure for modeling reaction kinetics developed by Krambeck et al.
[
16
]
which examines the fundamental properties of constitutive laws in chemically reacting systems, focusing on common physical assumptions and mass-action-based rate expressions. It also calls on the mechanics of phase-field models proposed by Clavijo et al.
[
17
]
. Additionally, further work by Clavijo et al.
[
18
]
is also used, as it presents a unified, thermodynamically consistent framework for modeling multicomponent, solid systems undergoing coupled diffusion and chemical reactions. In their work Clavijo et al. develop the extended Larché-Cahn framework to simulate how chemo-mechanical and reactive processes drive phase separation, domain evolution, and microstructural changes.

Numerical simulations illustrating finger formation (membrane blebs), nucleation and fragmentation are implemented in the Dedalus package
[
19
]
. This package allows for rapid mathematical development through the symbolic implementation of the system into the numerical solver. Furthermore, it allows for temporal and spatial analysis in 1, 2, and 3-dimensional domains with minimal alterations to pre-existing code for higher dimensional simulations.

To demonstrate the models capabilities we provide a comparison of numerical simulations at selected time points and their similarity with real-world experimental data (electron microscopy images from You et al.
[
20
]
) in two morphological categories: membrane blebbing (or finger formation) and size and number of cavity formations (throughout cavity formations will be referred to as nucleations). In this manuscript we describe nucleations as a void (
\varphi
=0) surrounded by the phase field
\varphi=1
and fragments as an area occupied by
\varphi=1
and surrounded by void (
\varphi
=0). While variability of biological systems does result in significant differences between images we demonstrate there are consistent similarities between the model output and cell imaging.

In section 2 we develop a chemo-mechanical phase-field model to describe the dynamics of the cyto (
\varphi
) and cytotoxic (
\sigma
) phase fields. Including the system that produces features observed in biological cells undergoing apoptosis. In section 3 we present computational simulations, used to illustrate finger formation, nucleation, and cell fragmentation during the degradation of the cyto (
\varphi
) phase field. Furthermore, we compare finger formation, nucleation, and cell fragmentation with electron microscopy images from You et al.
[
20
]
. As mentioned, continuum models for apoptosis remain largely unexplored, presenting a unique opportunity not only to extend the utility of these models to a new biological context, but also to gain mechanistic insight into the interplay between chemical signaling and mechanical deformation during programmed cell death.

2.
A continuum theory for apoptotic processes

Consider the existence of two kinematic processes given by the phase fields
\varphi
and
\sigma
. The process
\varphi
shrinks and fades due to the presence of the process
\sigma
; therefore, we refer to
\varphi
as the cyto phase field and
\sigma
as the cytotoxic phase field. The cytotoxic phase field may represent an external substance or internal or external stimuli. To mimic the cyto-cytotoxic coupling mechanism, we adopted the framework from Clavijo et al.
[
21
]
to model reaction-driven annihilation, where the phase fields
\varphi
and
\sigma
are treated as the concentrations of two species
1
1

1

Although we treat the phase fields as concentrations when dealing with chemical reactions, these are mere representations of the underlying cyto and cytotoxic agents.
, denoted by
\mathcal{A}_{\varphi}
and
\mathcal{A}_{\sigma}
, with
\varphi\coloneq[\mathcal{A}_{\varphi}]
and
\sigma\coloneq[\mathcal{A}_{\sigma}]
, the reaction describing these interactions is

(1)

\varsigma^{r}_{\varphi}\mathcal{A}_{\varphi}+\varsigma^{r}_{\sigma}\mathcal{A}_{\sigma}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle k}}{{\rightharpoonup}}\varsigma^{p}_{\varphi}\mathcal{A}_{\varphi},

where
\varsigma^{r}_{\varphi}
,
\varsigma^{r}_{\sigma}
, and
\varsigma^{p}_{\varphi}
are the stoichiometric coefficients for the reactants and products, respectively, and
k
is the reaction rate constant.

2.1.
Variational chemo-mechanical formulation

Here, we introduce a degenerate irreversible reaction model that describes a chemical-initiated annihilation of one (cyto) phase field by another (cytotoxic) phase field. This degenerate irreversible reaction is given by

(2)

r\coloneqq\,\hat{k}g(\varphi)\,\sigma^{\varsigma^{r}_{\sigma}},\hskip 10.00002pt\text{with}\hskip 10.00002ptg(\varphi)=\varphi(1-\varphi),

where
g(\varphi)
promotes the development of the reaction at the interface; hence the degeneracy. Thus, the resulting internal mass supply rates for phase fields
\varphi
and
\sigma
are then

(3)

(\varsigma_{\varphi}^{p}-\varsigma_{\varphi}^{r})r\hskip 20.00003pt\text{and}\hskip 20.00003pt-\varsigma_{\sigma}^{r}r,

where
(\varsigma_{\varphi}^{p}-\varsigma_{\varphi}^{r})r
represents the net production or depletion rate of the (cyto) phase field
\varphi
, while
-\varsigma_{\sigma}^{r}r
corresponds to the depletion rate of the (cytotoxic) phase field
\sigma
at the expense of annihilating the (cyto) phase field
\varphi
. Depending on the values of the stoichiometric coefficients, the evolution of the phase field
\varphi
can lead to either an increase or decrease in the (cyto) phase field
\varphi
. For example, if
\varsigma_{\varphi}^{p}>\varsigma_{\varphi}^{r}
, then the phase field
\varphi
increases, signifying growth, while
\varsigma_{\varphi}^{p}<\varsigma_{\varphi}^{r}
leads to a net depletion of the phase field
\varphi
. We propose the following coupled gradient flows to model the kinematic interplay between these processes, mimicking chemically induced apoptotic dynamics.

Next, we consider the following energy functional

(4)

\Psi[\varphi,\sigma]\coloneqq\int_{\Omega}\psi(\varphi,\nabla\sigma,\nabla\varphi)\,\mathrm{d}v,

where the free-energy density
\psi
is given as a function of the two kinematic processes and their derivatives, namely

(5)

\psi(\varphi,\nabla\sigma,\nabla\varphi)=f(\varphi)+\begin{bmatrix}\nabla\varphi&\nabla\sigma\end{bmatrix}\cdot\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\begin{bmatrix}\nabla\varphi\\
\nabla\sigma\end{bmatrix},

where
f
is a potential function depending on the phase field
\varphi
, and
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
is a symmetric positive definite matrix. The functional above splits the free energy into a potential function
f
, which may not be convex, and a regularization term depending upon the gradients of the phase fields
\varphi
and
\sigma
.

Then, we associate the evolution of the phase fields and the chemical reactions with gradient flow given by the energy functional (
4
) such that

(6)

\begin{bmatrix}\tau&0\\[4.0pt]
-\beta&\alpha\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\dot{\varphi}\\[4.0pt]
\dot{\sigma}\end{bmatrix}-\begin{bmatrix}(\varsigma_{\varphi}^{p}-\varsigma_{\varphi}^{r})r\\[4.0pt]
-\varsigma_{\sigma}^{r}r\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}-\dfrac{\delta\Psi}{\delta\varphi}\\[7.0pt]
-\dfrac{\delta\Psi}{\delta\sigma}\end{bmatrix}

this can be written as,

(7)

\tau\dot{\varphi}-(\varsigma_{\varphi}^{p}-\varsigma_{\varphi}^{r})r=-\frac{\delta\Psi}{\delta\varphi}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002pt\alpha\dot{\sigma}-\beta\dot{\varphi}+\varsigma_{\sigma}^{r}r=-\frac{\delta\Psi}{\delta\sigma},

where
\dot{\varphi}=\frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial t}
and
\dot{\sigma}=\frac{\partial\sigma}{\partial t}
. Expression (
7
) describes the evolution of the phase fields
\varphi
and
\sigma
. The parameters that dictate the relaxation towards equilibrium are
\tau>0
for the phase field
\varphi
, and
\alpha>0
and
\beta>0
for
\sigma
. The first variation of
\delta\Psi
with respect to the phase fields
\varphi
and
\sigma
are, respectively, given by

(8)

\dfrac{\delta\Psi}{\delta\varphi}=\dfrac{\partial\psi}{\partial\varphi}-\mathrm{div}\mskip 2.0mu\dfrac{\partial\psi}{\partial\nabla\varphi},\hskip 20.00003pt\text{and}\hskip 20.00003pt\dfrac{\delta\Psi}{\delta\sigma}=\dfrac{\partial\psi}{\partial\sigma}-\mathrm{div}\mskip 2.0mu\dfrac{\partial\psi}{\partial\nabla\sigma},

leading us to the following system

(9)

\left\{\begin{aligned} \tau\dot{\varphi}-(\varsigma_{\varphi}^{p}-\varsigma_{\varphi}^{r})r&=-\left(\dfrac{\partial\psi}{\partial\varphi}-\mathrm{div}\mskip 2.0mu\dfrac{\partial\psi}{\partial\nabla\varphi}\right),\\[4.0pt]
\alpha\dot{\sigma}-\beta\dot{\varphi}+\varsigma_{\sigma}^{r}r&=-\left(\dfrac{\partial\psi}{\partial\sigma}-\mathrm{div}\mskip 2.0mu\dfrac{\partial\psi}{\partial\nabla\sigma}\right),\end{aligned}\right.

for which the phase fields
\varphi
and
\sigma
are periodic in
\Omega
.

2.2.
Configurational mechanics

The evolution of the interfaces may be described by configurational mechanics. This approach proposed by Gurtin
[
22
]
generalizes the concept of Newtonian forces. Configurational forces are connected to the integrity of the material in a broad sense, explaining the evolution of material and immaterial interfaces. In what follows, we use the configurational balance suggested by Fried in
[
23
]
and Espath & Calo
[
24
]
.

The underlying local configurational force balances may be stated as

(10)

\mathrm{div}\mskip 2.0mu\boldsymbol{C}+\boldsymbol{f}=\boldsymbol{0},

where
\boldsymbol{C}
is the configurational stress tensor and
\boldsymbol{f}
is the internal configurational forces.

To determine the quantities entering the configurational balances, departing from the field Equations (
9
), we multiply the evolution of the phase field
\varphi
by
\nabla\varphi
and the evolution of the phase field
\sigma
by
\nabla\sigma
, yielding

(11)

\left\{\begin{aligned} (\tau\dot{\varphi}-(\varsigma_{\varphi}^{p}-\varsigma_{\varphi}^{r})r)\nabla\varphi&=-\left(\nabla\varphi\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial\varphi}-\mathrm{div}\mskip 2.0mu\left(\nabla\varphi\otimes\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial\nabla\varphi}\right)+\Delta\varphi\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial\nabla\varphi}\right),\\[4.0pt]
(\alpha\dot{\sigma}-\beta\dot{\varphi}+\varsigma_{\sigma}^{r}r)\nabla\sigma&=-\left(\nabla\sigma\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial\sigma}-\mathrm{div}\mskip 2.0mu\left(\nabla\sigma\otimes\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial\nabla\sigma}\right)+\Delta\sigma\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial\nabla\sigma}\right).\end{aligned}\right.

With the identity

\displaystyle\nabla\psi

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial\varphi}\nabla\varphi+\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial\sigma}\nabla\sigma+\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial\nabla\varphi}\Delta\varphi+\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial\nabla\sigma}\Delta\sigma,

(12)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{div}\mskip 2.0mu\left(\psi\boldsymbol{1}\right),

summing Equations (
11
) lead us to

(13)

(\tau\dot{\varphi}-(\varsigma_{\varphi}^{p}-\varsigma_{\varphi}^{r})r)\nabla\varphi+(\alpha\dot{\sigma}-\beta\dot{\varphi}+\varsigma_{\sigma}^{r}r)\nabla\sigma=-\left(\mathrm{div}\mskip 2.0mu(\psi\boldsymbol{1})-\mathrm{div}\mskip 2.0mu\left(\nabla\varphi\otimes\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial\nabla\varphi}\right)-\mathrm{div}\mskip 2.0mu\left(\nabla\sigma\otimes\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial\nabla\sigma}\right)\right).

The local configurational force balance resulting from Equation (
10
), gives the following configurational stress tensor

(14)

\boldsymbol{C}=\psi\boldsymbol{1}-\nabla\varphi\otimes\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial\nabla\varphi}-\nabla\sigma\otimes\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial\nabla\sigma},

and the internal configurational force becomes

(15)

\boldsymbol{f}=(\tau\dot{\varphi}-(\varsigma_{\varphi}^{p}-\varsigma_{\varphi}^{r})r)\nabla\varphi+(\alpha\dot{\sigma}-\beta\dot{\varphi}+\varsigma_{\sigma}^{r}r)\nabla\sigma.

2.3.
Thermodynamic potential

The thermodynamic potential,
f(\varphi)
, reflects a bistable state, promoting nontrivial equilibrium states for the phase field
\varphi
. We define it as

(16)

f(\varphi)=\frac{1}{4}\varphi^{4}-\frac{1}{2}\varphi^{3}+\frac{1}{4}\varphi^{2}.

The polynomial structure is chosen to create a double-well potential. This setup drives phase separation, shaping an energy landscape that enables transitions between metastable states.

5.
Conclusions

In conclusion, we have developed a novel framework to model apoptotic cell behavior using a chemo-mechanical phase field model. This framework is represented by the system in Equations (
18
), with an implementation that allows for rapid mathematical development and spatial analysis using the Dedalus package
[
19
]
.

Through calculus of variations we derived a phase field model coupled with a reaction framework, thus creating a chemo-mechanical model capable of describing apoptosis. We then applied this framework to simulate morphology changes observed in the cyto phase field
\varphi
by an external cytotoxic phase field
\sigma
. We developed the underlying configurational mechanics of this system to understand the evolution of the interfaces of the cyto phase field
\varphi
undergoing apoptosis induced by the cytotoxic phase field
\sigma
, allowing for the investigation of the topological transitions.

To demonstrate finger formation, nucleation, fragmentation, and shrinkage during apoptosis, we provide extensive numerical simulations. Through this we demonstrate four key parameters of
\tilde{\epsilon},\ k_{1},\ k_{2},
and
\beta
control finger formation. We explore how varying these parameters affects the structure and shape of the fingers that form as the cyto phase field
\varphi
degrades. We show that varying the interface width
\tilde{\epsilon}
changes the finger formation structure from present to nonexistent and determines whether phase fragmentation will occur during the degradation. Moreover increasing the reaction rates
k_{1}
and
k_{2}
increased the frequency of fragmentation, while increasing the value of
\beta
promoted cavity formation. We then used the internal configurational force to observe the formation of fingers protruding from the interface, hence predicting the regions of greatest degradation.

Simulations were then compared to electron microscopy images from You et al.
[
20
]
of PC3 and LNCaP cells exposed to the cytotoxic compound KML001 demonstrating similarities between the model output and microscopy images. Thus, we show the developed framework is capable of recreating cellular apoptotic morphology of varying cell types. However, differences in cell types, varying KML001 concentrations and intrinsic error associated with biological experiments show further experimental data (from a material sciences perspective) is needed to fine-tune the model and further assess its suitability.

The implementation of the model in Dedalus provides a platform that can support 3-dimensional simulations of cells undergoing apoptosis. While this would be more relevant, as cells are not 2-dimensional structures, this presents issues relating to access to computational resources and biological experimental data. However, a 3-dimensional simulation fine tuned to a specific biological system would move the model predictions beyond what is typically obtainable through microscopy techniques hence being of great interest in future studies.
