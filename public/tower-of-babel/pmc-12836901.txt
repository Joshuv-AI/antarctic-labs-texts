Cumulative of c-reactive protein-triglyceride glucose index exposure and risk of diabetes in middle-aged and older Chinese adults: findings from a national longitudinal cohort
Authors: Yuan-Feng Zhou, Qing-Tian Zeng, Xiang-Tao Zhang, Feng-Xiao Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe C-reactive protein-triglyceride glucose index (CTI) was a new biomarker to assess systemic inflammation and metabolic disturbances. However, research on its association with diabetes remained limited. This study aimed to investigate the association between CTI and the risk of developing new-onset diabetes.MethodsThis study employed a prospective observational design, focusing on middle-aged and elderly Chinese adults. Multivariate Cox regression analysis were used to assess the dose-response association between the CTI and the cumulative exposure of CTI (CumCTI) with diabetes risk. The K-means clustering was employed to classify the CTI trajectories.ResultsA total of 3,742 participants without diabetes were enrolled in this study. Over a 5-year follow-up period, 264 individuals developed diabetes, yielding an incidence rate of 7.5%. Overall, higher levels of CTI exposure were significantly associated with an increased risk of diabetes. Specifically, each 1-unit increment in CTI corresponded to a 35% elevation in diabetes risk (HR: 1.35; 95% CI: 1.14, 1.60), while each 1-unit increase in CumCTI was associated with 16% higher risk (HR: 1.16; 95% CI: 1.09, 1.25). When comparing exposure trajectories, individuals with persistently high CTI exposure (Cluster1) exhibited a 1.25 times elevated risk compared to those with consistently low exposure levels (Cluster3) (HR: 2.25; 95% CI: 1.53, 3.31).ConclusionElevated CTI exposure was significantly correlated with an increased risk of diabetes, highlighting its potential role in diabetes prediction, especially in the abnormal weight population. As a promising biomarker for diabetes risk monitoring, CTI might offer valuable insights for early detection and preventive strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13098-025-02056-1.Keywords: C-reactive protein-triglyceride glucose index, Diabetes, Middle-aged and older chinese adults, K-means clustering

Introduction

Diabetes is a chronic metabolic disorder characterized by pancreatic β-cell dysfunction and insulin resistance (IR), resulting in systemic metabolic dysregulation. This condition poses a substantial global health burden, as uncontrolled diabetes not only leads to increased disability and mortality but also significantly increases the risk of multiple diseases [1–3]. At the same time, the global prevalence of diabetes has escalated dramatically, imposing growing pressure on healthcare systems worldwide. According to the International Diabetes Federation (IDF), approximately 537 million adults aged 20–79 years were living with diabetes in 2021. What’s more, the number will rise to 12.2% (643 million individuals) by 2045 [4]. Given the substantial health and socioeconomic implications of diabetes, identifying modifiable risk factors and optimizing evidence-based prevention strategies are critical priorities in global public health efforts.

IR and chronic inflammation are well-established pathogenic drivers in the development and progression of diabetes. However, existing research has predominantly focused on evaluating individual factors affecting abnormal glucose metabolism, lacking a comprehensive analysis of their combined effects [4–6]. The C-reactive protein–triglyceride glucose index (CTI) has recently emerged as a promising composite marker for evaluating systemic inflammation and metabolic dysfunction. Prior studies have demonstrated its prognostic value in predicting survival outcomes among cancer patients and its utility in stratifying cancer-related mortality risk in the general population [7, 8]. What’s more, recent studies found that there was a strong association between the CTI and metabolic diseases such as cardiovascular disease (CVD) and diabetes [9, 10]. However, most existing research relied on cross-sectional designs or single-time-point measurements of CTI, limiting the ability to assess the long-term impact of CTI exposure on diabetes. To address this gap, the present study investigates the association between CTI, cumulative CTI exposure (CumcCTI), the CTI trajectories, and the risk of diabetes in a cohort of middle-aged and older adults in China.

Methods

Study population

The data of this study were obtained from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS), a nationally representative longitudinal survey hosted by Peking University, which aimed to systematically evaluate the health status, socioeconomic characteristics, and retirement security of individuals aged 45 years and older in China. Initiated in 2011, the project established a biennial follow-up survey mechanism. By 2020, five waves had been completed (2011, 2013, 2015, 2018, and 2020). This study adhered to rigorous ethical standards for scientific research and received approval from the Ethics Review Board of Peking University (ethics approval number: IRB00001052- 11015). All research procedures were conducted by the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki; all participants provided written informed consent. More details could be found on the official website: https://charls.pku.edu.cn.

This study was designed as a prospective observational study, with participants selected from those who participated in the initial CHARLS survey in 2011 and were followed up in 2020. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) aged < 45 years; (2) missing CTI data in 2012 and 2015;3) diagnosed with diabetes or the status of diabetes was unknown in 2011and 2015; 4) follow-up missed (Fig. 1).

Diagnosis of chronic diseases

The diagnosis of chronic diseases was based on a comprehensive assessment incorporating blood tests, physical examinations, and questionnaire responses. Hypertension was defined as self-reported history of hypertension, using antihypertensive medication, or average blood pressure ≥ 140/90 mmHg [11]. Diabetes was defined as self-reported history of diabetes, using antidiabetic medication, fasting blood glucose (FBG) ≥ 7.0 mmol/L, random blood glucose ≥ 11.1 mmol/L, or glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) ≥ 6.5% [12].

Calculation of indicators

TyG (triglyceride-glucose index) = ln (TG [mg/dL] × FPG [mg/dL]/2) [13];

CTI = 0.412×ln (CRP [mg/l]) + ln (TG [mg/dl] × FPG [mg/dl]/2) [11];

CumCTI = =(CTI₂₀₁₂+CTI₂₀₁₅)/2×time interval (2015 − 2012) [14];

NHDL-C (non-high-density lipoprotein) = TC (mg/dl) – HDL-C(mg/dl) [15];

RC (remnant cholesterol) = TC (mg/dl) – HDL-C(mg/dl) – LDL-C(mg/dl) [16].

Other variables

Demographic characteristics included age, sex, marital status (married/unmarried), residence (rural/urban), education level (primary school and below, high school and below, bachelor’s and above), smoking and drinking status, and use of lipid-lowering medications (as defined at the time of follow-up). Laboratory assessments encompassed fasting blood glucose (FBG), glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TG), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), and C-reactive protein (CRP). Body mass index (BMI) was equal to weight in kilograms divided by height in square meters. The estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) was estimated by the modification of diet in renal disease (MDRD) equation [17]. BMI ≥ 24 kg/m2 was defined as abnormal weight [18].

Missing data handling

Some variables contained missing values, including gender (4, 0.11%), education level (2, 0.05%), BMI (483, 12.91%), smoking status (9, 0.24%), drinking status (10, 0.27%), hypertension (9,0.24%), dyslipidemia (8,0.21%), HbA1c (14,0.37%) and LDL-C (1,0.03%). This study utilized multiple imputation by the “missRanger” package to minimize potential bias and enhance data completeness.

Data analysis

The study population was stratified into diabetic and non-diabetic groups based on diabetes occurrence. Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation(M ± SD) or median (interquartile range), depending on their distributional characteristics. Group comparisons were conducted using the t-test or the Wilcoxon rank sum test. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies (percentages), with group differences analyzed using the Pearson chi-square test. K-means clustering was employed to classify participants based on CTI. The optimal number of clusters was determined using the Elbow Method (K = 3). According to the temporal patterns of CTI, participants were categorized into three distinct trajectory groups: persistently high CTI exposure (Cluster 1), persistently moderate exposure (Cluster 2), and persistently low exposure (Cluster 3) (Fig. 2). To explore risk factors linked to new-onset diabetes, the univariate Cox regression analyses were performed. Then, three multivariate Cox regressions were used to assess the dose-response association between CTI, CumCTI, and CTI trajectories with the risk of diabetes. Specially, none adjusted for model I; age, gender, marital status, education, and residence for model II; further adjusted for BMI, smoking, drinking, hypertension, use of lipid-lowering drugs, and dyslipidemia based on model II for model III. Results were expressed as hazard ratio (HR) and 95% confidence intervals (95% CI). Furthermore, the restricted cubic spline plots (RCS) were utilized to evaluate the non-linear association between CumCTI and diabetes risk. The Kaplan-Meier survival curves were used to evaluate differences in diabetes incidence among different groups, and the Log-rank test was applied to compare the risks between groups. Additionally, subgroup analysis and interaction tests were performed to evaluate the robustness of the association between CumCTI and diabetes risk. Subgroup variables included age (< 60, ≥ 60 years), gender, BMI (< 24, ≥ 24 kg/m²), residence, marital status, hypertension, eGFR (≥ 60, < 60 ml/min/1.73m2), dyslipidemia, and use of lipid-lowering drugs. Finally, to further evaluate the predictive efficacy of different lipid metabolism indicators for diabetes, the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was employed. All statistical analyses were performed using R Studio (version 4.3.3) and EmpowerStats (www.empowerstats.com). A two-sided test was applied, with P < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

Characteristics of included and excluded populations

A total of 3,472 participants were included in the final analysis, while 13,966 individuals were excluded. Compared to the excluded group, the participants in the included group were younger and had a higher proportion of females and married individuals, and more lived in rural areas, but had a lower educational level. In terms of metabolic parameters, the included group demonstrated significantly lower levels of FPG, HbA1c, CRP, TG, TyG, CTI2012, CTI2015, and CumCTI than the excluded cohort (all p < 0.001). Conversely, the eGFR, LDL-C, and HDL-C were significantly higher (p < 0.001). Regarding clinical history and medication use, the prevalence of hypertension was notably lower among included participants (p < 0.001), whereas the prevalence of dyslipidemia was higher (p < 0.001). Besides, the proportion of individuals using lipid-lowering medications was slightly lower in the included group (p = 0.031) (Table S1).

Characteristics of participants before and after interpolation

To minimize the potential impact of missing data on the study outcomes, we conducted imputation for variables with missing values. Detailed results of the imputation are presented in Table S2. Comparative analyses revealed no significant differences in the indicators before and after imputation.

Baseline characteristics of the participants

Participants were classified into diabetic and non-diabetic groups based on the occurrence of diabetes (Table 1). In comparison to the non-diabetic population, individuals in the diabetic group were more married and had a higher BMI. Regarding laboratory tests, the diabetic group exhibited higher FBG, HbA1c, TC, LDL-C, TG, CRP, TyG, and CTI levels (including CTI2012, CTI2015, CumCTI) (p < 0.001), while lower HDL-C (p = 0.002). Additionally, the prevalence of hypertension and dyslipidemia was significantly higher in the diabetic group (p < 0.001).

Association between CTI and diabetes

Overall, the CTI was significantly associated with an increased risk of diabetes. Specifically, each one-unit increase in CTI2012, CTI2015, and CumCTI correlated with a 44% (HR = 1.44, 95% CI: 1.19, 1.73), 35% (HR = 1.35, 95% CI: 1.14, 1.60), and 16% (HR = 1.16, 95% CI: 1.09, 1.25) increase in diabetes risk, respectively. Moreover, individuals in the highest CTI quartile had a substantially elevated risk of new-onset diabetes compared to those in the lowest quartile: 1.08-fold in CTI2012 (HR = 2.08, 95% CI: 1.38, 3.13), 1.04-fold in CTI2015 (HR = 2.04, 95% CI: 1.37, 3.04), and 1.40-fold in CumCTI (HR = 2.40, 95% CI: 1.58, 3.64). Besides, compared to Cluster 3, the risk of diabetes in Cluster 2 and Cluster 1 increased 0.59 times (HR:1.59, 95%CI: 1.14, 2.22) and 1.25 times (HR: 2.25, 95%CI: 1.53, 3.31) (Table 3).

Comparison of diabetes prevalence

Kaplan-Meier survival analysis based on CumCTI quartiles and CTI trajectory groups revealed that individuals in the highest CumCTI quartile (group 4) had a significantly higher risk of developing diabetes compared to those in the lowest quartile (group 1) (p for Log-rank test < 0.0001) (Fig. 4A). Similarly, participants with sustained high-level CTI exposure (Cluster 1) showed a markedly increased risk of diabetes compared to those with sustained low-level exposure (Cluster 3) (Fig. 4B) (p for Log-rank test < 0.0001).

Subgroup analysis

Subgroup analyses and interaction tests were conducted to evaluate further the stability of the association between CumCTI and the risk of new-onset diabetes across different populations (Fig. 5). After adjusting for relevant covariates, this association remained consistent across subgroups stratified by age, gender, marital status, residence, hypertension, eGFR, and lipid-lowering drug use (p for interaction > 0.05). However, the association was significantly stronger in those with a BMI ≥ 24 kg/m2 (p for interaction = 0.027).

Predictive capability comparison

ROC curve analysis was conducted to assess the predictive performance of various indicators for new-onset diabetes. The results indicated that CTI (AUC = 0.630 for CTI2011, 0.6018 for CTI2022) outperformed traditional lipid markers and the TyG. Moreover, CumAIP (AUC = 0.6155) exhibited even greater predictive accuracy than single-time-point CTI (Fig. 6).

Discussion

This study demonstrated a significant association between CumCTI levels and the risk of developing diabetes, with elevated CumCTI values correlating with a higher likelihood of disease onset. Notably, this association remained statistically robust even after adjusting for potential confounding variables. Subgroup analyses further revealed that the association was more pronounced among individuals with BMI ≥ 24 kg/m2, suggesting that obesity might amplify the link between CumCTI and diabetes risk.

TyG and CRP, as the core components of CTI, played crucial roles in diabetes pathogenesis and risk assessment. TyG, an emerging metabolic index, had been widely recognized as a convenient and efficient alternative marker for IR [19]. It held important clinical value in the early detection of metabolic syndrome, cardiovascular disease, and chronic kidney disease [20–22]. Furthermore, TyG exhibited strong predictive efficacy in assessing the risk of glucose metabolism abnormalities. Multiple studies had established the significance of TyG in diabetes risk prediction. For example, the study of Luo et al. showed that each unit increase of TyG was associated with a substantial rise in diabetes risk (HR = 3.348, 95% CI: 3.004, 3.731) in the general population [23]. Similarly, Shan et al. also observed a significant correlation between TyG and diabetes [24]. In addition, TyG has demonstrated excellent predictive performance in special populations, including patients with non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) and hypertension, further highlighting its clinical significance in the early detection and risk assessment of diabetes [25, 26].

Chronic low-grade inflammation was also significantly linked to the onset of glucose metabolic disorder by inducing insulin resistance (IR) [27, 28]. As a widely recognized biomarker of inflammation, previous studies showed that elevated level of CRP was not only linked to a higher risk of cardiovascular disease, but also served as a potential predictor of diabetes and other metabolic disorders [29, 30]. The Mendelian randomization study conducted by Cheng et al. demonstrated that high CRP levels significantly increased the risk of developing type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) [31]. Additionally, the follow-up study on a pre-diabetic population conducted by Cheng revealed that lower levels of high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP) were strongly associated with glycemic recovery, significantly reducing the risk of progression to diabetes [32]. These findings further emphasized the critical role of chronic inflammation in diabetes pathogenesis, highlighting the potential of CRP as a key biomarker in metabolic health assessment and early intervention strategies.

While most existing studies have focused on the isolated effects of either IR or inflammation, few have comprehensively evaluated their combined impact. Recently, the CTI was proposed as an integrated parameter that simultaneously reflects both IR and inflammatory status. Emerging evidence suggested that higher CTI levels were strongly correlated with health risks, including depression, cancer mortality, and stroke, et al. [33–35]. However, its potential association with diabetes remained inadequately studied. The study of Shan et al. showed a strong positive association between elevated CTI and diabetes [24]. But the evaluation of CTI in their study was confined to a single time point, limiting the ability to explore its dynamic changes in relation to diabetes progression. At the same time, Ma et al. utilized repeated CTI measurements at two time points and demonstrated that higher CumCTI levels were significantly associated with increased cardiovascular disease (CVD) risk. Building upon this approach, our study employed CTI assessments at two distinct time points and found that elevated CTI levels at both time points were consistently associated with an increased risk of developing diabetes [14]. Additionally, we analyzed both CumCTI and CTI trajectories, with results consistently indicating that sustained exposure to elevated CTI levels markedly increases the future risk of diabetes onset. Specifically, for each 1-unit increase in CumCTI value, the risk of diabetes rose by 16%. And compared to Cluster 3, the risk of diabetes in Cluster 1 increased 1.25-fold. Furthermore, the results of the subgroup analysis and interaction test showed that the association between CumCTI and diabetes was notably stronger among individuals with a BMI ≥ 24 kg/m2. As demonstrated by Kim et al., a significant interaction existed between high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP) and overweight/obesity, whereby their combined impact on IR exceeded the sum of their individual effects [36]. So, we proposed that abnormal weight might intensify the influence of CTI on diabetes risk by increasing IR and inflammatory exposure.

In clinical practice, the IR assessment primarily relied on the glucose clamp technique and the homeostasis model-assessed insulin resistance index (HOMA-IR). Although these techniques were widely used in scientific research, their complexity and high cost limited their applicability for large-scale clinical screening. So, various simplified indices were proposed to evaluate individual IR status, including TG/HDL-C, RC, and NHDL-C, which were shown to be closely associated with the risk of developing diabetes [37–39]. Building upon this, we systematically compared the predictive performance of several traditional and non-traditional lipid metabolism markers for diabetes risk. Our findings suggest that, relative to the aforementioned indicators, the CumAIP demonstrated superior predictive efficacy in identifying individuals at elevated risk for diabetes.

Currently, the underlying mechanisms between CTI and diabetes remained insufficiently explored. The CTI offered an integrated evaluation of systemic inflammation and IR. So, the association between elevated CTI and increased diabetes risk likely stemmed from the synergistic effects of chronic inflammation and IR. CRP, a sensitive marker of inflammation, indicated heightened activity of cytokines such as IL-6 and TNF-α [40, 41]. Sustained low-grade inflammation could damage pancreatic β-cells via oxidative stress and interfere with insulin signaling pathways, leading to reduced insulin sensitivity and secretion [27, 42–44]. Prolonged exposure to inflammatory mediators might also impair endothelial function, further weakening insulin action [45, 46]. In addition, IR was often accompanied by dysregulation of lipid metabolism, typically manifested as elevated TG and reduced HDL-C. This metabolic disturbance led to the accumulation of fatty acids within pancreatic β-cells, triggering lipotoxicity that compromises cellular integrity and further impaired insulin secretion [47, 48]. Additionally, IR would also stimulate inflammatory cytokine release from adipose tissue, reinforcing a vicious cycle of inflammation and metabolic dysfunction [49].

There were several limitations in the present study. First, the study population primarily comprised individuals aged 45 years and older, which might restrict the generalizability of the findings to younger age groups. Future research involving more diverse populations was warranted to validate the robustness of the results. Second, a large number of participants were excluded due to missing critical information or diagnosed with diabetes before 2015, which might have introduced selection bias and affected the representativeness and accuracy of the study findings. Third, as a prospective observational study, it remained unclear whether reducing CTI levels could effectively lower the risk of developing diabetes. So, interventional studies were needed in the future. Lastly, the diagnosis of diabetes was partially based on self-reported questionnaire data; the recall bias might have been introduced.

Conclusion

The present study showed that persistent high-level CTI exposure was positively correlated with an increased risk of diabetes in the middle-aged and elderly Chinese population, reinforcing its potential as a valuable indicator for diabetes monitoring. As a comprehensive metabolic assessment tool, CTI integrated inflammation, lipid metabolism abnormalities, and IR, providing a robust reference for early detection and risk stratification of diabetes.
