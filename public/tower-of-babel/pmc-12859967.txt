The effect of probiotics on anxiety, depression, nicotine dependence, and metabolic biomarkers in smokers: a randomized clinical trial
Authors: Nasim Sadeghi, Shahram Shams, Reyhaneh-Sadat Mirlohian, Freshteh Haerifar, Mostafa Bakhshi-Kashi, Amir Ghaderi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCigarette smoking (CS) has long been associated with both metabolic disturbances and psychological dysfunction. Recent evidence suggests that the gut microbiome may play a role in these processes. This study examined whether probiotic supplementation could influence metabolic markers, nicotine dependence, and symptoms of anxiety and depression in smokers.MethodsIn this randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled clinical trial, seventy-four adult smokers were recruited (Kashan, Iran) and randomly assigned to receive either probiotic capsules containing Lactobacillus acidophilus, Bifidobacterium longum, Bifidobacterium bifidum, and Bifidobacterium lactis (1.8 × 10⁹ CFU/day; n = 36) or a matching placebo (n = 38) for 12 weeks. Primary endpoints included changes in depressive symptoms (Beck Depression Inventory-II; BDI-II), anxiety (Beck Anxiety Inventory; BAI), and nicotine dependence (Nicotine Dependence Syndrome Scale; NDSS). Secondary endpoints included changes in metabolic, inflammatory, and oxidative stress biomarkers.ResultsAfter 12 weeks, participants receiving probiotics showed significant reductions in fasting plasma glucose, triglycerides, very-low-density lipoprotein (VLDL) cholesterol, and C-reactive protein (CRP) compared with the placebo group (p = 0.025, p = 0.022, p = 0.026, and p = 0.004, respectively). High-density lipoprotein (HDL) cholesterol levels also increased significantly in the probiotic group (p = 0.007). No significant changes were observed in insulin, HOMA-IR, total cholesterol, LDL cholesterol, total antioxidant capacity, plasma nitric oxide, malondialdehyde, glutathione, or in scores for anxiety, depression, and nicotine dependence.ConclusionsTwelve weeks of probiotic supplementation produced favorable effects on fasting glucose, some lipid profile, and CRP levels in smokers, though no measurable benefits were observed for anxiety and depression symptoms or nicotine dependence. These findings highlight a potential metabolic benefit of probiotics in populations exposed to cigarette smoking.Trial registrationThis trial was registered at the Iranian Registry of Clinical Trials (IRCT20170420033551N13; registration date: December 6, 2022).

Background

Cigarette smoking (CS) remains a major global public health concern [1]. Beyond its well-documented role in cardiovascular and pulmonary diseases, smoking has been strongly associated with both metabolic abnormalities and psychological disturbances such as anxiety and depression [2]. Emerging evidence also highlights that cigarette smoke disrupts the composition and function of the gut microbiota and alters the gut–microbiome–lung axis [3, 4].

Probiotics, primarily including species such as Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium, are live microorganisms that can survive in the gastrointestinal tract, regulate host immunity, and modulate microbial homeostasis [5]. Through these mechanisms, probiotics have been linked to improvements in both metabolic and psychological health outcomes [6, 7]. For example, supplementation has been associated with reductions in symptoms of depression, anxiety, and stress, along with enhanced antioxidant enzyme activity in healthy adults [8, 9]. Experimental data further suggest potential benefits in smoke-induced pathologies: Budden et al. (2022) reported that administration of Bifidobacterium longum subsp. longum reduced inflammatory cytokine expression, adhesion factor expression, and lung inflammation in animal models exposed to cigarette smoke [10]. Similarly, probiotic intake in patients with diabetic retinopathy resulted in improvements in HbA1C, insulin, HOMA-IR, QUICKI, HDL-C, TAC, and GSH concentrations after 12 weeks, although effects on other metabolic parameters were absent [11]. Conversely, no significant improvements were observed in patients with schizophrenia following probiotic supplementation [12]. These inconsistent findings highlight that the clinical utility of probiotics across different populations remains under investigation.

Several biological mechanisms have been proposed to explain the beneficial effects of probiotics. These include enhanced nutrient absorption, regulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels, stimulation of neurotransmitter biosynthesis, upregulation of brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF), suppression of pro-inflammatory cytokines, antioxidant activities, and increased gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA) production [13–16]. Collectively, these activities may contribute to improvements in metabolic and psychological disturbances commonly observed in smokers.

However, clinical trials directly assessing the effects of probiotics on biomarkers of metabolic and psychological outcomes in smokers are scarce. Addressing this gap, the present clinical trial aims to investigate whether probiotic supplementation can improve (1) nicotine dependence, (2) symptoms of anxiety and depression, and (3) metabolic and inflammatory biomarkers, including serum lipid concentrations, insulin resistance, oxidative stress, and inflammatory mediators, among cigarette smokers.

Methods

Trial design

We conducted a double-blind, randomized, placebo-controlled clinical trial involving adult smokers from Kashan, Iran. The trial was prospectively registered with the Iranian Registry of Clinical Trials (IRCT20170420033551N13) and approved by the Ethics Committee of Kashan University of Medical Sciences (Approval code: IR.KAUMS.MEDNT.REC.1401.159). The study was carried out in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Before enrollment, all participants were fully informed about the study objectives, procedures, and potential risks and benefits. Written informed consent was obtained from each participant, confirming voluntary participation. The consent process was reviewed and approved by the KAUMS Research Ethics Committee to ensure adherence to ethical standards. This clinical trial was designed and reported in line with the CONSORT guidelines.

Setting

The trial was carried out in healthcare centers (Gholabchi Clinic) in Kashan, Iran. To ensure a sample that better reflected the general population of smokers. Instead, adult smokers who accompanied patients or visited the centers for other purposes were invited to participate, resulting in a more diverse and representative cohort.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Participants were eligible if they were current smokers between 17 and 50 years of age with a documented history of tobacco use. Eligibility was verified using the Fagerström Test for Nicotine Dependence (FTND), with a threshold score of 4 or higher. In addition, participants were required to have smoked continuously for more than one year, with a minimum consumption of one pack of cigarettes every two days.

Exclusion criteria included lack of consent to participate, pregnancy or lactation, use of probiotics or multivitamin/mineral supplements within the past three months, or a positive urine drug screen for illicit substances. Individuals with chronic systemic diseases—such as cardiovascular, hepatic, or renal disorders; type 1 or type 2 diabetes; AIDS; or parathyroid abnormalities—were excluded, as were those with uncontrolled hypertension (systolic ≥ 160 mmHg or diastolic ≥ 100 mmHg). Additional exclusions applied to individuals with diagnosed psychiatric conditions or ongoing use of psychiatric medications, a history of substance dependence, or intolerance to probiotic supplements during the study period.

Interventions

A total of 80 participants were randomly assigned in equal numbers to the probiotic group (n = 40) and the placebo group (n = 40). The intervention lasted 12 weeks, during which each participant consumed one capsule per day. In the probiotic arm, capsules contained 1.8 × 10⁹ colony-forming units (CFU) of a blend of four probiotic strains—Lactobacillus acidophilus, Bifidobacterium longum, Bifidobacterium bifidum, and Bifidobacterium lactis. These strains were selected for their established roles in supporting gut health and immune function. The capsules were produced and supplied by LactoCare® (Takgene Pharma, Tehran, Iran) in freeze-dried form to preserve viability and potency throughout the study. Placebo capsules were indistinguishable from the probiotic capsules in terms of appearance, taste, and packaging, ensuring full blinding of participants and investigators.

To preserve product integrity, participants were instructed to store capsules under the manufacturer’s recommended conditions. Participants received regular reminders to encourage adherence. Compliance was closely monitored through capsule counts and daily intake logs, and deviations were recorded systematically. Additional reminders were delivered via SMS and telephone calls. These measures ensured high adherence, minimized the risk of unblinding, and strengthened the validity of the trial findings.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was to evaluate the effect of 12-week probiotic supplementation on depressive and anxiety symptoms and nicotine dependence in adult smokers.

The secondary outcome was to assess its effects on metabolic biomarkers (FPG, insulin, HOMA-IR, lipid profile, CRP, TAC, GSH, MDA, and NO).

Anxiety and depression assessments

Symptoms of depression and anxiety were evaluated using validated Persian versions of the Beck Depression Inventory (BDI) and the Beck Anxiety Inventory (BAI) [17, 18]. The BDI comprises 21 items, while the BAI includes 20 items, each rated on a 0–4 scale. Higher total scores reflect greater severity of depressive symptoms or anxiety.

Nicotine dependence

Nicotine dependence at baseline was assessed using the Fagerström Test for Nicotine Dependence (FTND) [19] to determine eligibility and establish initial smoking severity. For evaluating trial outcomes, a more comprehensive approach was adopted with the Nicotine Dependence Syndrome Scale (NDSS), a 19-item instrument developed by Shiffman et al. [20], which captures multiple dimensions of nicotine dependence beyond what the FTND measures. While the FTND served as a practical screening tool, the NDSS was applied at both baseline and follow-up to provide a richer, multidimensional assessment of dependence throughout the study. Given the conceptual differences between the constructs measured by these two scales, no direct statistical comparisons were conducted, ensuring methodological rigor and the validity of outcome interpretation.

Biochemical assessments

Fasting venous blood samples (10 mL) were collected at baseline and again at the end of the 12-week intervention in the clinical laboratory of Matini Hospital, Kashan, Iran. Serum insulin concentrations were determined using a commercial ELISA kit (DiaMetra, Milan, Italy), with intra- and inter-assay coefficients of variation (CVs) below 5%. Insulin resistance was calculated using the Homeostatic Model Assessment of Insulin Resistance (HOMA-IR) method [21].

Fasting plasma glucose (FPG) and lipid profiles (including total cholesterol, high-density lipoprotein (HDL), low-density lipoprotein (LDL), and triglycerides) were measured using enzymatic kits (Pars Azmun, Tehran, Iran). The intra- and inter-assay CVs for these assays were less than 5%. Serum C-reactive protein (CRP) levels were quantified via ELISA (LDN, Nordhorn, Germany), while nitric oxide (NO) concentrations were assessed using the Griess reaction [22].

Other biomarkers included total antioxidant capacity (TAC), reduced glutathione (GSH), and malondialdehyde (MDA). These parameters were measured using standard laboratory protocols, with all assays demonstrating CVs below 5%, ensuring reliability and reproducibility of the results.

Sample size

The sample size was determined based on the ability to detect a meaningful change in depressive symptoms, as measured by the Beck Depression Inventory (BDI), drawing on data reported by Molavi et al. (2022) [23]. With a 95% confidence level and 80% statistical power, the calculation indicated that 36 participants per group would be sufficient to detect a mean difference of 7.9 points on the BDI. To safeguard against potential attrition, we increased the target enrollment to 40 participants per group, allowing for an anticipated dropout rate of approximately 10%. In line with the study’s emphasis on both depression (BDI) and anxiety (BAI) were designated as primary endpoints.

Randomization

Participants were selected for this clinical trial using a simple sampling method. Eligible smokers were randomly allocated to two study arms through balanced block randomization, with block sizes of four and a 1:1 allocation ratio generated using Epi Info software. This approach ensured equal distribution between the probiotic and placebo groups while minimizing the risk of selection bias.

Blinding

To preserve blinding, placebo capsules were manufactured to be indistinguishable from the probiotic capsules in size, shape, appearance, taste, and texture. The trial was conducted under a double-blind design: neither the participants nor the investigators were aware of group assignments until study completion. Supplements were dispensed in opaque, identically labeled containers (A = probiotic group; B = placebo group), and only the pharmacist responsible for packaging was informed of the contents. A quality control protocol was implemented throughout the study to maintain the integrity of both randomization and blinding.

Statistical methods

The normality of continuous variables was evaluated using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Baseline characteristics were compared between the probiotic and placebo groups using independent-samples t tests for continuous variables and chi-square tests for categorical variables. To assess intervention effects, analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) was applied separately to each clinical outcome, with models adjusted for baseline values to control for potential confounders and improve precision. Analyses were performed on the per-protocol population, which included only participants who completed the study with complete data. Results are reported as effect sizes with corresponding p values, and statistical significance was defined as a two-sided p < 0.05. All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS software, version 22 (IBM Corp., Chicago, IL, USA).

Results

Out of the 125 individuals screened for participation, 80 met the study’s eligibility requirements and were subsequently randomized into the trial. During the intervention period, six participants discontinued; two from the placebo group due to personal circumstances and four from the probiotic group who chose to stop taking the supplements. Ultimately, 74 participants completed the 12-week study and were included in analysis (38 subjects in the placebo arm and 36 subjects in the probiotic arm). A detailed overview of the recruitment, randomization, and follow-up procedures is presented in the CONSORT flow diagram (Fig. 1). No adverse events were reported in either group during the study period. As shown in Table 1, baseline demographic and clinical characteristics were well balanced between groups, confirming the success of randomization.

Findings from Table 3 indicate that probiotic supplementation, compared with placebo, was associated with significant improvements in several metabolic and inflammatory markers. Specifically, FPG, triglycerides, VLDL cholesterol, and CRP levels decreased significantly in the probiotic group (P < 0.05), while HDL cholesterol increased markedly. By contrast, no significant between-group differences were observed for insulin, HOMA-IR, total cholesterol, LDL cholesterol, NO, TAC, or reduced GSH. Although TAC and GSH values showed a tendency to increase and MDA levels declined in the probiotic group, these changes were not statistically significant.

Discussion

This clinical trial explored the effects of probiotic supplementation on metabolic health, nicotine dependence, and anxiety and depression symptoms among smokers. After 12 weeks of intervention, participants experienced significant improvements in several metabolic markers, including fasting glucose, triglycerides, VLDL cholesterol, and CRP. Additionally, HDL cholesterol levels increased, indicating a favorable shift in lipid metabolism and systemic inflammation. These findings reinforce the hypothesis that probiotics may positively influence metabolic and inflammatory status in individuals who smoke. However, no significant effects were detected for insulin levels, HOMA-IR, LDL cholesterol, total cholesterol, total antioxidant capacity, plasma nitric oxide, malondialdehyde, glutathione, symptoms of anxiety and depression, or nicotine dependence.

The absence of significant effects on nicotine dependence aligns with the limited evidence available in the literature. For instance, Pratummas et al. demonstrated that an 8-week synbiotic regimen reduced craving scores and plasma lipopolysaccharide (LPS) levels in smokers, while another study reported improvements in both nicotine dependence and gut permeability markers such as the lactulose-to-mannitol ratio (LMR) [24, 25]. Such discrepancies may be explained by differences in probiotic strains, dosage, intervention duration, and participant characteristics. Taken together, the evidence suggests a potential role for probiotics in modulating nicotine dependence, but current data remain inconclusive, and longer, more comprehensive studies are required.

Psychological outcomes in this trial also did not show meaningful improvements in anxiety or depression scores. This is somewhat surprising given that several meta-analyses and clinical trials have reported beneficial effects of probiotics on mood disorders [26–28]. However, these effects appear to depend heavily on strain specificity, intervention length, dosage, and baseline symptom severity. One systematic review, for example, found that probiotics reduced certain depressive symptoms, but results varied across scales, and the certainty of evidence was generally low [27]. Moreover, specific subgroups, such as cancer patients or individuals with high baseline distress, may respond more favorably to probiotic treatment [29]. These findings suggest that smokers, particularly those with mild or subclinical symptoms, may require tailored probiotic regimens to experience psychological benefits. The role of the gut–brain axis remains a compelling mechanistic framework, but its relevance to smokers is still uncertain.

The metabolic improvements observed here are consistent with prior research indicating that probiotics can regulate glucose and lipid metabolism while reducing systemic inflammation [30, 31]. Cigarette smoking is well known to disrupt metabolic homeostasis and alter gut microbial composition, and probiotics may counteract these effects by restoring microbial balance and dampening inflammatory signaling. Mechanistically, probiotics produce antioxidant metabolites, activate Nrf2 signaling, and modulate NF-κB and MAPK pathways, which collectively reduce oxidative stress and inflammation [32–34]. They also influence lipid metabolism through short-chain fatty acid production, bile acid modification, and inhibition of hepatic cholesterol synthesis [35, 36], while enhanced GLP-1 secretion from intestinal L cells may support improved glucose tolerance [37]. In this trial, reductions in glucose, VLDL cholesterol, and CRP levels, along with an increase in HDL cholesterol, provide compelling evidence of such effects. At the same time, the lack of significant changes in insulin resistance and oxidative stress markers reflects the mixed findings of earlier meta-analyses, which have reported both positive and null results depending on the study design and population [38–40].

Several limitations of this study should be noted. The intervention period was relatively short, which may explain the absence of effects on nicotine dependence and anxiety and depression symptoms. Dietary intake, physical activity, and antibiotic use were not systematically monitored, potentially introducing variability that could affect outcomes. The modest sample size also limits statistical power and generalizability. Future research should aim to include larger cohorts, longer follow-up periods, and more rigorous control of confounders such as diet, smoking intensity, and antibiotic exposure. Incorporating multi-omics profiling approaches may also help to identify individual response patterns and optimize probiotic formulations for smokers.

Conclusion

In summary, this trial provides preliminary evidence that probiotic supplementation can meaningfully improve select metabolic and inflammatory parameters in smokers, particularly glucose regulation, lipid metabolism, and systemic inflammation. These findings support the idea that probiotics may help counteract some of the metabolic disturbances associated with smoking. However, the lack of significant effects on nicotine dependence and anxiety and depression symptoms highlights the complexity of host–microbe interactions and suggests that benefits may depend on strain specificity, intervention duration, and individual susceptibility. While encouraging, these results should be interpreted with caution, given the short follow-up period and modest sample size. Larger, longer, and more rigorously controlled studies are needed to clarify the therapeutic potential of probiotics for both metabolic health and neurobehavioral outcomes in smokers.
