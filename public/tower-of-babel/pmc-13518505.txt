Palmitic Acid Amplifies Microglial Inflammatory Activation Involving C5aR1-Sensitive Inflammatory Signaling and JMJD3/H3K27me3-Related Epigenetic Changes
Authors: Liqing Chen, Sanqing Xu, Yan Liu, Xiaoping Luo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAbstractMicroglial pro-inflammatory activation contributes to neuroinflammatory processes in many neurological disorders. Saturated fatty acids such as palmitic acid (PA) are increasingly recognized as inflammatory cues, yet the molecular mechanisms linking PA to microglial inflammatory responses remain incompletely defined. In this study, using BV-2 microglia as an exploratory in vitro model, we investigated whether C5aR1-sensitive signaling and JMJD3/H3K27me3-related epigenetic changes are involved in PA-associated inflammatory responses. PA exposure increased Iba1 expression, altered CD86/CD206 expression, and enhanced the secretion of IL-1β, IL-6 and TNF-α. Exogenous C5a produced broadly similar inflammatory changes. Under heat-inactivated serum conditions, PA increased cell-associated C5a immunoreactivity and extracellular C5a levels, and these C5a-related readouts were reduced by PMX53. PMX53 also attenuated PA-associated increases in inflammatory markers and in the expression of TLR4, total NF-κB p65, c-Fos, and c-Jun. In parallel, PA and C5a increased JMJD3 expression and JMJD3 enrichment at selected IL-1β and IL-6 promoter regions, accompanied by reduced H3K27me3 enrichment at these loci. Pharmacological KDM6 inhibition with GSK-J4 attenuated PA-associated increases in IL-1β and IL-6 expression. Together, these findings support the involvement of C5aR1-sensitive inflammatory signaling and JMJD3/H3K27me3-related epigenetic regulation in PA-associated microglial inflammatory responses.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s11481-026-10308-8.Keywords: Palmitic acid, C5a, Histone demethylase, Inflammation, BV-2 microglia

Introduction

Microglia are the resident innate immune cells of the central nervous system (CNS), where they continuously survey the microenvironment to maintain tissue homeostasis under physiological conditions (Norden et al. 2016). In response to danger signals, microglia are rapidly activated and acquire diverse functional states (Hoogland et al. 2015). Although markers such as CD86 and CD206 are commonly used to describe inflammatory phenotypic changes, microglial activation is increasingly recognized as a heterogeneous and dynamic process rather than a strict M1/M2 dichotomy (Paolicelli et al. 2022; Ma et al. 2023). CD86 is commonly associated with pro-inflammatory microglial responses, whereas CD206, also known as the mannose receptor, is often linked to anti-inflammatory or tissue-reparative phenotypic features (Paolicelli et al. 2022; Zhang et al. 2023). Therefore, in the present study, CD86 and CD206 were used as representative phenotype related markers rather than definitive indicators of discrete polarization states. Increasing evidence indicates that aberrant microglial activation and microglia-mediated neuroinflammation contribute to a broad spectrum of CNS disorders, including anxiety, depression, Parkinson’s disease (PD), Alzheimer’s disease (AD), and epilepsy (Guo et al. 2023; Amelimojarad 2024; Lind-Holm et al. 2025; Altmann et al. 2022; Andoh et al. 2020; Patel et al. 2019).

Microglial inflammatory activation is regulated by both membrane receptor-associated signaling and transcriptional regulatory mechanisms (Qin et al. 2023; Scholz et al. 2024; Huang et al. 2023). As an important component of innate immune recognition, Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4) contributes to the sensing and transduction of inflammatory signals in immune and glial cells (Li et al. 2021; Abarca-Merlin et al. 2024; Squillace and Salvemini 2022). In parallel, complement activation generates C5a, an anaphylatoxin that signals mainly through C5a receptor 1 (C5aR1) (Cyranka et al. 2023; Feng et al. 2023). C5a/C5aR1 signaling has been reported to interact with TLR-related inflammatory pathways in several innate immune contexts, thereby contributing to inflammatory amplification (Luo et al. 2025; Shi et al. 2021; Shen et al. 2024). However, whether and how C5aR1-sensitive signaling is related to TLR4-associated inflammatory changes in PA-treated microglia remains incompletely defined.

In addition to receptor-associated signaling, chromatin-level regulation may influence the magnitude and persistence of inflammatory gene expression (Caldwell and Li 2024; Wang et al. 2024; Davis et al. 2021). Epigenetic mechanisms regulate immune cell activation and inflammatory transcriptional programs by modulating chromatin states and transcription factor accessibility (de Winther and Palaga 2021; Tarnowski et al. 2021). Among these modifications, trimethylation of histone H3 lysine 27 (H3K27me3) is generally associated with transcriptionally repressive chromatin, whereas removal of this mark can be associated with a more permissive chromatin environment (Davis et al. 2021; Cai et al. 2021; Audu et al. 2022). Jumonji domain-containing protein 3 (JMJD3), also known as lysine demethylase 6B (KDM6B), is an H3K27me3 demethylase that has been implicated in inflammatory gene regulation (Zhang et al. 2019). Previous studies have shown that JMJD3 can be induced by inflammatory stimuli and may cooperate with transcriptional regulators such as nuclear factor-kappa B (NF-κB) to promote the expression of pro-inflammatory genes, including IL-1β and IL-6 (Li et al. 2024; Bu et al. 2025; Zhang and Cao 2019).

High-fat feeding and increased saturated fatty acid availability have been linked to inflammatory reprogramming in peripheral tissues and to neuroinflammatory changes in the CNS (Kiran et al. 2022; Elzinga et al. 2022; Deng et al. 2023). Saturated fatty acids, particularly palmitic acid (PA), can elicit pro-inflammatory responses in immune cells and have been reported to activate microglia and promote cytokine production (Zhao et al. 2024; Chmielarz et al. 2023). However, the molecular mechanisms underlying PA-induced microglial inflammatory activation remain incompletely understood.

Based on these considerations, we examined whether PA-associated inflammatory responses in BV-2 microglia may involve C5aR1-sensitive inflammatory signaling, TLR4-associated molecular changes, and JMJD3/H3K27me3-related epigenetic regulation. Using BV-2 microglia as an exploratory in vitro model, we assessed the effects of PA and exogenous C5a on markers of microglial activation, inflammatory cytokine production, and selected molecular changes related to inflammatory signaling and chromatin regulation.

Materials and Methods

BV-2 Cell Culture

BV-2 microglial cells were obtained from Procell Life Science & Technology (China) and maintained in DMEM-based medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin in a humidified incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO₂. Cells were routinely passaged and used at comparable passage numbers across experiments. No poly-L-lysine (PLL) coating was used, and the main experiments were performed under routine culture conditions unless otherwise specified. For the additional C5a validation experiment, BV-2 cells were cultured in medium supplemented with heat-inactivated FBS (Procell, 164223) to reduce the potential contribution of serum complement activity.

Cell Treatment

BV-2 cells were assigned to the following main experimental groups: PBS (vehicle), PA, C5a, PA+PMX53, and PA + GSK-J4. PA was applied at a final concentration of 200µM for 24 h and C5a was applied at 100nM for 24 h. For C5aR1 blockade, cells were pre-incubated with PMX53 (100nM) for 1 h before PA exposure. For pharmacological KDM6 inhibition, cells were pre-treated with GSK-J4 (5µM) for 2 h before PA exposure. Unless otherwise stated, inhibitors were maintained during the subsequent 24 h PA treatment period. Reagents were purchased from MedChemExpress (USA) unless otherwise indicated.

Additional inhibitor-alone control experiments were performed to determine whether PMX53 or GSK-J4 affected basal inflammatory readouts. BV-2 cells were assigned to PBS, inhibitor alone, PA, and PA + inhibitor groups under the same inhibitor exposure conditions used in the main experiments. These results are presented in Supplementary Figures S1–S4.

Western Blot Assay

Whole-cell lysates were prepared in RIPA buffer (Meilunbio, MA0151) supplemented with protease inhibitors. Protein concentrations were determined using a BCA assay, and equal amounts of protein from each sample were separated by SDS–PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes. Membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk in TBST and incubated with primary antibodies against GAPDH (1:1000), Iba1 (1:1000), C5a (1:1000), TLR4 (1:1000), NF-κB p65 (1:1000), c-Jun (1:2000), c-Fos (1:10000), JMJD3 (1:1000), IL-1β (1:1000), and IL-6 (1:1000), followed by HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies. Detailed information on the primary and secondary antibodies used in this study is provided in Supplementary Table S1. Protein signals were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL; Affinity, KF8003) and quantified by densitometric analysis. Target protein levels were normalized to the corresponding GAPDH signal obtained from the same set of samples or the same Western blot batch.

Western blot analyses were performed using samples from at least three independent biological experiments. Some target proteins were analyzed in separate Western blot batches using identical experimental group designs. In each batch, the corresponding GAPDH served as the internal loading control, and quantitative analyses were performed only within the same batch.

For the additional C5a validation experiment performed under heat-inactivated serum conditions, whole-cell lysates were collected from PBS, PA, and PA+PMX53 groups, and cell-associated C5a immunoreactivity was examined by Western blotting.

Quantitative Real-Time Polymerase Chain Reaction (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was isolated from BV-2 cells using TRIzol reagent (Ambion, 15596-026) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA concentration and purity were assessed before reverse transcription. Equal amounts of RNA were reverse-transcribed into cDNA using a commercial reverse transcription kit. qRT-PCR was performed using SYBR Green-based chemistry under standard cycling conditions, including an initial denaturation step followed by 40 amplification cycles. Melting curve analysis was performed to verify amplification specificity. Primer sequences are listed in Table 1. Relative mRNA expression was normalized to Gapdh and calculated using the 2−△△Ct method. Each biological sample was analyzed in technical triplicate, and all qRT-PCR experiments were performed using samples from at least three independent biological experiments.

Immunofluorescence Double Staining

BV-2 cells were seeded on uncoated coverslips and allowed to adhere under routine culture conditions. No PLL coating was used. Cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100. After blocking with normal goat serum, cells were incubated with primary antibodies against Iba1 (Abcam, ab283319, 1:100) together with CD86 (Bioss, bs-1035R, 1:100) or CD206 (Cell Signaling Technology, 24595, 1:100). After washing, fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibodies were applied. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (Beyotime Biotech, C1002), coverslips were mounted with antifade medium, and images were acquired using a fluorescence microscope under identical exposure settings for each staining series.

For immunofluorescence quantification, three randomly selected non-overlapping fields were captured from each group under the same magnification and identical imaging settings. Fluorescence intensity was analyzed using image analysis software. The mean fluorescence intensity was calculated as integrated optical density (IOD) divided by the positive-staining area. Quantification was performed on a per-field basis rather than by single-cell counting. Formal colocalization coefficients, such as Pearson’s correlation coefficient or Manders’ overlap coefficient, were not calculated.

Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA)

Culture supernatants were collected 24 h after treatment and clarified by centrifugation. Secreted IL-6, IL-1β, and TNF-α were quantified using commercial ELISA kits (Jiangsu Meimian) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. In the additional validation experiment performed under heat-inactivated serum conditions, extracellular C5a levels in culture supernatants were also measured by ELISA. Absorbance was measured according to the kit protocols, and cytokine or C5a concentrations were calculated from standard curves. Each biological sample was measured in technical triplicate, and ELISA experiments were performed using samples from at least three independent biological experiments.

Chromatin Immunoprecipitation Followed by Quantitative PCR (ChIP-qPCR)

Chromatin immunoprecipitation followed by quantitative PCR was performed to assess H3K27me3 enrichment and JMJD3 occupancy at selected regions of the Il1b and Il6 promoters. BV-2 cells from PBS, PA, and C5a groups were cross-linked with 1% formaldehyde, lysed, and chromatin was fragmented by sonication to approximately 100–2000 bp. A portion of sheared chromatin was reserved as input. The remaining chromatin was immunoprecipitated with an anti-H3K27me3 antibody (Proteintech, 39055) to assess H3K27me3 enrichment or with an anti-JMJD3 antibody (Cell Signaling Technology, 3457S) to assess JMJD3 recruitment to the selected promoter regions. Normal IgG was included as a negative immunoprecipitation control.

After immunoprecipitation, cross-links were reversed, proteins were digested with proteinase K, and DNA was purified. Purified DNA was analyzed by qPCR using primers targeting selected regions of the Il1b and Il6 promoters (Table 2). H3K27me3 enrichment was normalized to input. For JMJD3 ChIP-qPCR, enrichment was normalized to input and expressed as fold enrichment over IgG to evaluate specific promoter occupancy. All ChIP-qPCR experiments were performed using samples from at least three independent biological experiments, and qPCR reactions were run in technical triplicate.

Statistical Analysis

All data are presented as mean ± SD from at least three independent biological experiments, with technical triplicates performed where applicable. Group differences were analyzed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post hoc multiple-comparisons test (GraphPad Prism 9.5). A P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were based on independent biological replicates rather than technical replicates.

Results

PA and C5a Increase Iba1 Expression, Alter Cd86/Mrc1 Transcript Levels, and Enhance Pro-Inflammatory Cytokine Secretion

Iba1 (ionized calcium-binding adaptor molecule 1), a commonly used marker of microglial activation, was examined by Western blotting. Compared with PBS, Iba1 protein levels were significantly increased after PA or C5a exposure (Fig. 1A). Iba1 expression was also higher in the PA group than in the C5a group.

Cd86 and Mrc1 transcript levels were next assessed by qRT-PCR. Cd86 mRNA expression was significantly increased following PA or C5a treatment, with a greater increase observed in the PA group than in the C5a group (Fig. 1B). In contrast, Mrc1 mRNA was decreased in both the PA and C5a groups compared with PBS (Fig. 1B).

ELISA analysis showed that PA and C5a treatment increased the secretion of IL-6, IL-1β and TNF-α compared with PBS (Fig. 1C). The levels of these cytokines were also higher in the PA group than in the C5a group.

PMX53 Attenuates PA-Associated Changes in Iba1, Cd86/Mrc1 Transcript Levels, and Cytokine Secretion

To examine the effects of PMX53 on PA-associated responses, BV-2 cells were treated with PA in the presence or absence of PMX53. Compared with PA alone, PA+PMX53 co-treatment reduced Iba1 protein levels (Fig. 1A).

At the transcriptional level, the PA+PMX53 group showed lower Cd86 and higher Mrc1 expression than the PA group (Fig. 1B).

ELISA showed that IL-6, IL-1β and TNF-α concentrations in the PA+PMX53 group were lower than those in the PA group (Fig. 1C). Although Iba1 and cytokine levels in the PA+PMX53 group remained above PBS levels, Cd86 and Mrc1 transcript levels did not significantly differ between the PA+PMX53 and PBS groups.

Additional inhibitor-alone control experiments showed that PMX53 alone did not significantly alter basal Cd86/Mrc1 transcript levels or IL-1β/IL-6 secretion compared with PBS, whereas PMX53 attenuated PA-associated changes under the same experimental conditions (Supplementary Figures S1 and S2).

Discussion

Microglial inflammatory activation is an important component of neuroinflammatory processes associated with metabolic stress and neurological disorders (Gao et al. 2023; Sadeghdoust et al. 2024; Heneka et al. 2025; Song et al. 2025). In the present study, we used BV-2 microglia as an exploratory in vitro model to investigate how PA influences inflammatory responses and whether complement-related signaling and epigenetic regulation are involved. Our results showed that PA increased Iba1 expression, altered Cd86/Mrc1 expression, and enhanced the secretion of IL-1β, IL-6 and TNF-α. Exogenous C5a produced broadly similar inflammatory changes, whereas PMX53 attenuated several PA-associated readouts. In parallel, PA and C5a increased JMJD3 expression, promoted JMJD3 enrichment at selected IL-1β and IL-6 promoter regions, and were associated with reduced H3K27me3 enrichment at these loci. Pharmacological treatment with GSK-J4 reduced PA-associated IL-1β and IL-6 expression. These findings suggest that PA associated microglial inflammatory responses involve both C5aR1-sensitive inflammatory signaling and JMJD3/H3K27me3 related epigenetic regulation.

PA is a major saturated fatty acid implicated in metabolic inflammation and neuroinflammatory responses (De Paula et al. 2024; Howe et al. 2022). In this study, PA was used under defined in vitro conditions to induce inflammatory responses in BV-2 microglia. Under these conditions, PA increased Iba1 expression and pro-inflammatory cytokine secretion. CD86 expression was increased, whereas CD206 expression was decreased after PA or C5a exposure. These findings support a PA associated pro-inflammatory microglial response. However, CD86 and CD206 should be interpreted as representative inflammatory phenotype-related markers rather than definitive indicators of complete microglial polarization states.

The C5a-related findings provide an additional link between PA exposure and complement associated inflammatory responses. C5a is generally generated through proteolytic cleavage of C5 during complement activation rather than by direct transcriptional upregulation (Thurman 2026). To reduce the potential contribution of serum complement activity, we performed experiments under heat-inactivated serum conditions. Under these conditions, PA increased cell-associated C5a immunoreactivity in whole-cell lysates and extracellular C5a levels in culture supernatant, while PMX53 reduced PA associated readouts. Nevertheless, the present data do not definitively determine whether the detected C5a is generated de novo by BV-2 cells or reflects other complement-related processes in the culture system. Future studies using serum-free conditions or C5 knockdown will be needed to clarify the precise source of C5a.

PMX53 reduced several PA associated inflammatory readouts, including Iba1 expression, cytokine production, and CD86/CD206 changes. These observations support the involvement of C5aR1-sensitive signaling in PA associated microglial inflammatory responses. However, the PMX53 data do not prove that PA induces inflammation specifically through endogenous C5a generation. PA may activate multiple inflammatory pathways in parallel, and C5aR1-sensitive signaling may act as a contributing or amplifying component rather than the sole upstream driver of the PA response.

The relationship between C5aR1-sensitive signaling and TLR4-related inflammatory changes remains an important but unresolved issue. PA and C5a were associated with increased expression of TLR4, total NF-κB p65, c-Fos, and c-Jun, and PA associated increases in these molecules were attenuated by PMX53. These data suggest that C5aR1-sensitive signaling may be associated with changes in the abundance of inflammatory signaling-related molecules under the present experimental conditions. However, we did not assess TLR4 activation status, p65 phosphorylation or nuclear translocation, c-Fos/c-Jun phosphorylation, or upstream MAPK activity. Therefore, these findings should be interpreted as changes in signaling-related molecule expression rather than direct evidence of functional TLR4/NF-κB/activator protein 1 (AP-1) pathway activation. It also remains possible that PA engages TLR4 associated inflammatory signaling independently of C5aR1, while C5aR1-sensitive signaling converges downstream to amplify inflammatory mediator expression.

A key contribution of this study is the incorporation of JMJD3/H3K27me3-related epigenetic regulation into the PA-associated microglial inflammatory response. PA and C5a increased JMJD3 expression and enhanced JMJD3 enrichment at selected IL-1β and IL-6 promoter regions. These changes were accompanied by reduced H3K27me3 enrichment at the same loci. Since H3K27me3 is generally associated with transcriptionally repressive chromatin, reduced H3K27me3 enrichment may be consistent with a more permissive chromatin environment for inflammatory gene expression (Davis et al. 2021; Cai et al. 2021; Audu et al. 2022). In addition, GSK-J4 reduced PA associated IL-1β and IL-6 expression. Together, these findings support the involvement of KDM6 family H3K27 demethylase activity and JMJD3 associated promoter recruitment in PA associated inflammatory gene expression.

The relationship between the C5aR1-sensitive component and the JMJD3/H3K27me3 related epigenetic component should be interpreted as complementary rather than as a fully defined linear cascade. PMX53 only partially attenuated PA associated inflammatory responses, suggesting that C5aR1-sensitive signaling does not fully account for the observed phenotype. The JMJD3/H3K27me3 related changes may therefore represent an additional regulatory layer that contributes to inflammatory cytokine expression. At present, however, we cannot determine whether JMJD3/H3K27me3 changes are downstream of C5aR1 signaling, whether these processes occur independently, or whether they converge at the level of inflammatory transcription. Combined PMX53 and GSK-J4 treatment was not assessed, and future studies will be needed to determine whether dual inhibition produces stronger suppression of PA-associated inflammatory responses.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, all experiments were performed in BV-2 cells, which do not fully reproduce the transcriptional, phenotypic, and functional properties of primary microglia. Therefore, the present findings should be considered exploratory in vitro evidence and require validation in primary microglial, organotypic brain models, and in vivo systems. Second, BV-2 cells were cultured under routine conditions without poly-L-lysine coating; thus, immunofluorescence analyses mainly reflected the adherent cell population retained under the experimental conditions used. Third, CD86 and CD206 were used as representative markers, but additional markers such as iNOS, TNF, IL-10, and Arg1, as well as classical reference stimuli such as LPS, IL-4, or IL-13, were not included. Fourth, although inhibitor-alone controls were added to evaluate basal effects of PMX53 and GSK-J4, pharmacological inhibitors may still have off-target or context-dependent actions. In particular, GSK-J4 is a pharmacological inhibitor of KDM6 family H3K27 demethylases and is not completely specific for JMJD3/KDM6B (Cribbs et al. 2020). Finally, H3K27ac and p65 promoter occupancy were not assessed, and therefore the chromatin level mechanism remains only partially defined.

In summary, the present study provides in vitro evidence that PA-associated microglial inflammatory responses involve increased pro-inflammatory cytokine production, C5aR1-sensitive inflammatory signaling, TLR4/NF-κB/AP-1 associated molecular changes, and JMJD3/H3K27me3 related epigenetic regulation. The additional experiments under heat-inactivated serum conditions and the JMJD3 ChIP-qPCR data strengthen the evidence for complement-related and epigenetic involvement. Importantly, these findings should be viewed as evidence of pathway association and partial functional involvement rather than proof of a complete linear PA-C5a-C5aR1-TLR4-JMJD3 signaling cascade. By linking saturated fatty acid exposure with C5aR1-sensitive inflammatory signaling and JMJD3 associated chromatin changes in microglia, this study provides a mechanistic framework for further investigation of lipid-driven neuroinflammatory responses.
