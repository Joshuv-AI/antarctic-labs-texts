Differentially Private Data-Driven Markov Chain Modeling
Alexander Benvenuti, Brandon Fallin, Calvin Hawkins, Brendan Bialy, Miriam Dennis, Warren Dixon, Matthew Hale

Abstract
Markov chains model a wide range of user behaviors. However, generating accurate Markov chain models requires substantial user data, and sharing these models without privacy protections may reveal sensitive information about the underlying user data. We introduce a method for protecting user data used to formulate a Markov chain model. First, we develop a method for privatizing database queries whose outputs are elements of the unit simplex, and we prove that this method is differentially private. We quantify its accuracy by bounding the expected KL divergence between private and non-private queries. We extend this method to privatize stochastic matrices whose rows are each a simplex-valued query of a database, which includes data-driven Markov chain models. To assess their accuracy, we analytically bound the change in the stationary distribution and the change in the convergence rate between a non-private Markov chain model and its private form. Simulations show that under a typical privacy implementation, our method yields less than 2 % 2\% error in the stationary distribution, indicating that our approach to private modeling faithfully captures the behavior of the systems we study.

INTRODUCTION

Markov chain modeling is a powerful tool for understanding a variety of behaviors including domestic time use
[
Widén et al. 2009
]
, traffic patterns
[
Gong et al. 2011
]
, and internet browsing
[
Dong et al. 2022
]
. Markov chains are often to model systems with random transitions among a finite set of states. In data-driven settings, transition probabilities are typically derived from databases of observed user behavior, as in
Rendle et al. 2010
and
Lundström et al. 2016
.
The development of these models often counts transitions between states in user data, and sharing the resulting models may leak information about the data used to generate the transition probabilities.
Patterns such as home occupancy
[
Lundström et al. 2016
]
or individual shopping behavior
[
Rendle et al. 2010
]
may be inferred even from aggregated data. Thus, without some form of protection, these models present significant privacy risks for users.
However, these models fundamentally require user data, which motivates our interest in protecting privacy while preserving model accuracy.

In this paper, we develop a framework for producing privacy-preserving Markov chain models that protects databases used to compute transition probabilities. We first develop a method for privatizing database queries whose outputs are stochastic vectors, i.e., vectors with non-negative entries that sum to
1
. We then extend this method to stochastic matrices in which every row is equal to a query whose output is a stochastic vector.

We develop this framework using differential privacy. Differential privacy is a statistical notion of privacy designed to preserve the privacy of sensitive databases when queried
[
Dwork et al. 2006
]
. We use differential privacy because (i) compositions of differentially private data remain differentially private, and (ii) it is immune to post-processing, in the sense that post-hoc computations on private data do not weaken differential privacy. These properties have led to the wide use of differential privacy for federated learning
[
Geyer et al. 2017
,
Agarwal et al. 2021
,
Chen et al. 2022
,
Noble et al. 2022
]
, optimization
[
Hsu et al. 2014
,
Wang et al. 2016
,
Munoz et al. 2021
,
Benvenuti et al. 2024a
]
, planning
[
Gohari et al. 2020
,
Chen et al. 2023a
,
Benvenuti et al. 2024b
]
, and machine learning
[
Ponomareva et al. 2023
,
Jayaraman and Evans 2019
,
Blanco-Justicia et al. 2022
]
.

In the context of Markov chains, property (i) allows us to privatize each row in a stochastic matrix to form a private Markov chain model, and property (ii) allows the private model to be shared and analyzed without harming the privacy of the underlying database. Additionally, the differential privacy literature has shown that highly accurate query outputs can sometimes be produced while enforcing strong privacy
[
Dwork et al. 2014
]
. Thus, we use differential privacy to generate and share accurate Markov chain models while still protecting users with strong privacy.

To privatize stochastic vectors, we extend the Dirichlet mechanism first introduced in
Gohari et al. 2021
. Common privacy mechanisms such as the Gaussian and Laplace mechanisms
[
Dwork et al. 2014
,
McSherry 2009
]
are ill-suited for stochastic vectors because they add noise with infinite support, which may cause private output vectors to no longer be stochastic. The resulting vectors could be projected onto the unit simplex to enforce that their entries are non-negative and sum to
1
, but
Gohari et al. 2021
showed such projections result in poor accuracy. We fundamentally differ from
Gohari et al. 2021
because
we privatize databases that are used to compute simplex-valued queries,
whereas
Gohari et al. 2021
privatized elements of the simplex
themselves (without there being an underlying database that generated them). Our approach provides event-level privacy to data, in the sense that the presence of individual events is protected by differential privacy.
This form of privacy is intended to be used for datasets in which individual users are not tracked over time, such as the taxi cab dataset in
NYC Taxi Limousine Commision 2025
, which
we use in simulation in Section
6
.

Of course, we expect the asymptotic and transient behaviors of a privatized Markov chain to differ from those of the comparable non-private Markov chain. To assess the impact of privacy on asymptotic behavior, we bound the change in the stationary distribution (i.e., the long-run probability
distribution of the Markov chain’s state) between a private and non-private Markov chain. To assess the impact on transient behavior, we bound the change in ergodicity coefficient (which is a proxy for the convergence rate to the stationary distribution) between a private and non-private Markov chain. These bounds quantify privacy’s impact on Markov chain models, and they can be used to tune privacy parameters based on accuracy.

To summarize, our contributions are as follows:

•

We develop a framework for privatizing queries whose outputs are stochastic vectors (Theorem
1
).

•

We bound the Kullback-Leibler divergence between a non-private stochastic vector and its privatized form (Theorem
2
and Corollary
1
).

•

Using
1
, we develop a framework for privatizing the computation of Markov chain transition probabilities computed from user data (Theorem
3
).

•

We bound the changes in asymptotic and transient behaviors between the original, non-private Markov chain and its privatized form by bounding the differences in the stationary distribution and the ergodicity coefficient (Theorems
4
and
5
).

•

We empirically validate the impact of privacy on performance on two real datasets: a class grade distribution and transit data from New York City taxis. Results show less than
2\%
error in the stationary distribution with
(3.73,6\times 10^{-6})
-differential privacy (Section
6
).

Related Work

In this work, we build on the original Dirichlet mechanism from
Gohari et al. 2021
. More recently,
Ponnoprat 2023
and
Chitra et al. 2022
have developed alternate formulations of the Dirichlet Mechanism. However, these formulations introduce a bias in private outputs, which is undesirable for the Markov chain models we consider. Thus, we base our approach on
Gohari et al. 2021
to yield unbiased private outputs.

Privacy for Markov processes has been extensively studied
[
Gohari et al. 2020
,
Chen et al. 2023b
,
Chen et al. 2023a
,
Fallin et al. 2023
,
Benvenuti et al. 2024b
]
, though only
Gohari et al. 2020
and
Fallin et al. 2023
privatize the transition probabilities, which we do in this work. However, those works consider the probabilities themselves as the sensitive data and apply input perturbation privacy to them. In contrast, we consider transition probabilities that are computed from data, and we privatize the underlying data, not the values of the probabilities. Thus,
Gohari et al. 2020
and
Fallin et al. 2023
represent special cases of this paper
where
the transition probabilities are the sensitive database.

PRELIMINARIES AND PROBLEM FORMULATION

In this section, we provide background on Markov chains and differential privacy, define the structure of the databases and queries we consider, and formalize problem statements.

2.1
Unit Simplex and Stochastic Matrices

Definition 1

(Unit Simplex)
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
. The unit simplex in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
denoted by
\Delta_{n}
is the set of all element-wise non-negative vectors of length
n
whose entries sum to
1
, where

\Delta_{n}=\Big\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\ \mid\ \sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}=1,\ x_{j}\geq 0\ \text{for all}\ j\in[n]\Big\}.\!\!\!\!\qed

(1)

We also refer to elements of the unit simplex as “stochastic vectors". We also consider the bordered unit simplex.

Definition 2

(Bordered Unit Simplex)
.

Fix
n\geq 3
and
\eta>0
. Then the bordered unit simplex is

\Delta_{n}^{(\eta)}=\Big\{x\in\Delta_{n}\,\mid x_{i}\geq\eta\text{ for all }i\in[n]\Big\}.\qed

(2)

The bordered unit simplex is a subset of
\Delta_{n}
whose components are a sufficient distance from
0
.

A matrix whose rows are all stochastic vectors is called a “stochastic matrix". For
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we define
\mathbb{S}_{n}
as the set of all
n\times n
stochastic matrices.

2.2
Markov Chains

Consider a discrete time stochastic process
(Y_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{N}}
on the state space
\mathcal{Y}
. The process
(Y_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{N}}
is said to be a discrete time Markov chain if the sequence satisfies the Markov property, namely

\mathbb{P}\left(Y_{t+1}\!=\!y_{t+1}\!\!\mid\!Y_{1}\!=\!y_{1},\!\cdots\!,\!Y_{t}\!=\!y_{t}\right)=\mathbb{P}\left(Y_{t+1}\!\!\mid\!Y_{t}\!=\!y_{t}\right)\!,

which states that the conditional probability of transitioning from state
y_{t}
to
y_{t+1}
is independent of the sequence of states prior to
t
. See
[
Norris 1998
]
for a detailed account of Markov chains.
We study Markov chains that are (i) finite, (ii) irreducible, and (iii) homogeneous. These are Markov chains in which (i) the state space
\mathcal{Y}
is finite, (ii) any state
y\in\mathcal{Y}
can be reached from any other state
z\in\mathcal{Y}
with positive probability, and (iii) the transition probabilities are independent of time. Given a finite-state Markov chain, we construct the transition probability matrix
G
such that
G_{ij}=\mathbb{P}\left(Y_{t+1}=y_{j}\mid Y_{t}=y_{i}\right)
. Row
i
of the matrix
G
, which we denote
G_{i}
, is the probability distribution of transitioning from state
y_{i}
to any other state in
\mathcal{Y}
. If
|\mathcal{Y}|=n
, then
G\in\mathbb{S}_{n}
.

2.3
Differential Privacy

We use differential privacy to provide privacy to a database
\mathcal{D}
, which we partition as
\mathcal{D}=\{D_{1},\ldots,D_{m}\}
, where each set
D_{i}

contains
N_{i}\in\mathbb{N}
entries and
D_{i}=\{d^{i}_{1},\ldots,d^{i}_{N_{i}}\}
. The goal of differential privacy is to make “similar" data appear “approximately indistinguishable" by using a randomized mapping or “privacy mechanism". Given two databases
\mathcal{D}
and
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
, the notion of “similar" is defined by an adjacency relation
[
Dwork et al. 2014
]
.

Definition 3

(Adjacency)
.

Consider two databases
\mathcal{D}=\{D_{1},\ldots,D_{m}\}
and
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}=\{D^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,D^{\prime}_{m}\}
, where
D_{i}
and
D_{i}^{\prime}
each
contain
N_{i}\in\mathbb{N}
entries
for all
i\in[m]
. Suppose
D_{i}=\{d^{i}_{1},\ldots,d^{i}_{N_{i}}\}

and similar for
D_{i}^{\prime}
.
We write
\adj(\mathcal{D},\mathcal{D}^{\prime})=1
if
(a) there exist indices
i,j
such that
d^{i}_{j}\neq d^{{}^{\prime}i}_{j}
and
(b) for all
(\ell,k)\neq(i,j)
, we have
d^{\ell}_{k}=d^{{}^{\prime}\ell}_{k}
.
That is,
\mathcal{D}
and
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
are adjacent if they
differ in one entry.
We write
\adj(\mathcal{D},\mathcal{D}^{\prime})=0
otherwise.
\blacktriangle

A privacy mechanism
\mathcal{M}(\cdot)
is a randomized mapping that enforces differential privacy.

Definition 4

(Differential Privacy;
[
Dwork et al. 2014
]
)
.

Fix a probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
. Let
\delta\in[0,\frac{1}{2})
and
\epsilon>0
be given. A mechanism
\mathcal{M}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
is
(\epsilon,\delta)
-differentially private if for all
\mathcal{D},\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
that are adjacent in the sense of Definition
3
, we have
\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{D})\in T\right)\leq e^{\epsilon}\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{D}^{\prime})\in T\right)+\delta

for all Borel measurable sets
T\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
.
\blacktriangle

The parameters
\epsilon
and
\delta
quantify the strength of privacy, and smaller
\epsilon
and
\delta
imply stronger privacy.

2.4
Databases and Queries

We consider databases
\mathcal{D}
that are partitioned via
\mathcal{D}=\{D_{1},\ldots,D_{m}\}\in\mathfrak{D}
, where
\mathfrak{D}
is the set of all possible database realizations. We sometimes consider the degenerate case where
\mathcal{D}=\{D\}
. To differentiate between these cases, we use
D
without a subscript
to mean
\mathcal{D}=D=\{d_{1},\ldots,d_{N}\}
.
A database entry
d^{i}_{j}
may come from one of
n
categories in a category set
\rho=\{\rho_{1},\ldots,\rho_{n}\}\in\mathcal{P}
, where
\mathcal{P}
is the collection of all category sets. We use
d^{i}_{j}\models\rho_{k}
to indicate that entry
d^{i}_{j}
is from category
\rho_{k}
.

Assumption 1
.

Each database entry
d^{i}_{j}
belongs to exactly one category
\rho_{k}\in\rho
.

Assumption
1
is mild since the categories may represent physical events that cannot happen concurrently.

Example 1

(City Traffic)
.

Consider a database
D
that contains one record
d_{j}
for each time a driver turns from a street called First Street onto another street.
Then
\mathfrak{D}
is the set of all traffic patterns in the city. We use
\mathcal{P}
to represent all streets in the city, and the category set
\rho
is the collection of streets that intersect First Street
that drivers may turn onto. In this scenario, Assumption
1
says that a single driver must turn off somewhere and cannot turn off onto multiple different roads.
\triangle

For a database
\mathcal{D}=\{D\}
with
N
entries,
consider the counting query
C:\mathfrak{D}\times\mathcal{P}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
with category set
\rho=\{\rho_{1},\ldots,\rho_{n}\}
, defined as

\!\!\!C(D,\rho)\!=\!\frac{1}{N}\!\begin{bmatrix}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\!\mathbb{I}\{d_{i}\!\models\!\!\rho_{1}\},\!\cdots\!,\!\sum_{i=1}^{N}\!\mathbb{I}\{d_{i}\!\models\!\!\rho_{n}\}\!\end{bmatrix}^{\!T}\!\!\!\!.

(3)

In words,
C(D,\rho)
is a vector whose entries are the fraction of records in
D
that belong to category
\rho_{i}
for each
i\in[n]
.

Next, we consider the more general case where
\mathcal{D}=\{D_{1},\ldots,D_{m}\}
, where each
D_{i}
contains
N_{i}
entries and we have the category set
\rho=\{\rho_{1},\ldots,\rho_{n}\}
. We then consider the mapping
P:\mathfrak{D}\times\mathcal{P}\to\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
, where each row of the output matrix is a count vector for a database
D_{i}
, namely

P(\mathcal{D},\rho)=\begin{bmatrix}C(D_{1},\rho)&\cdots&C(D_{m},\rho)\end{bmatrix}^{T}.

(4)

When
m=n
, the matrix
P(\mathcal{D},\rho)
in (
4
) is a stochastic matrix, and it represents the transition probability matrix of a Markov chain. One interpretation of
P(\mathcal{D},\rho)
is as follows:
D_{i}
encodes all of the transitions of users from state
i
to some other state, and
\rho=\{\rho_{1},\ldots,\rho_{n}\}
is the set of states that they can transition to. Then
\sum_{j=1}^{N_{i}}\mathbb{1}\{d_{j}^{i}\models\rho_{k}\}
is the count of how many users transitioned from state
i
to state
k
. Accordingly, each entry in
P(\mathcal{D},\rho)
is the empirical transition probability in a Markov chain.

2.5
Problem Statements

In this work, we solve the following problems:

Problem 1
.

Develop a mechanism to privatize
\mathcal{D}
when computing count vectors
C(\mathcal{D},\rho)
in the form of (
3
).

Problem 2
.

Bound the similarity between a privately generated count vector
\tilde{C}
and the original, non-private count vector
C(\mathcal{D},\rho)
.

Problem 3
.

Develop a privacy mechanism to privatize
\mathcal{D}
when computing the transition matrix of a Markov chain,
P(\mathcal{D},\rho)
.

Problem 4
.

Bound the difference in the asymptotic behavior (i.e., stationary distribution) and the transient behavior (i.e., convergence rate) between a Markov chain and its private form.

3
Dirichlet Mechanism for Differential Privacy of Simplex-Valued Queries

In this section, we solve Problems
1
and
2
. We consider the case where
\mathcal{D}=\{D\}
and denote this as
D
. To solve Problem
1
, we extend the Dirichlet mechanism, first introduced in
Gohari et al. 2021
.

Definition 5

(Dirichlet Mechanism;
[
Gohari et al. 2021
]
)
.

A Dirichlet mechanism with parameter
k>0
, which we denote
\mathcal{M}_{\text{Dir}}^{(k)}
, takes a vector
p\in\Delta^{\circ}_{n}
as input and outputs
x\in\Delta_{n}
according to the Dirichlet distribution centered on
kp
. That is,

\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{M}_{\text{Dir}}^{(k)}(p)\!=\!x\right)\!\!=\!\!\frac{1}{B(kp)}\!\prod_{i=1}^{n-1}x_{i}^{kp_{i}-1}\Bigg(\!1\!-\!\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}x_{i}\!\Bigg)^{\!\!kp_{n}-1}\!\!.\!\!\!\qed

(5)

The Dirichlet mechanism takes a sensitive vector
p
and outputs a private vector
\tilde{p}
. The level of privacy is tuned using the parameter
k
.
The Dirichlet mechanism in Definition
5
satisfies an alternate notion of privacy known as probabilistic differential privacy. To distinguish the two notions, we say “conventional differential privacy" to refer to Definition
4
.

Definition 6

(Probabilistic Differential Privacy;
[
Machanavajjhala et al. 2008
]
,
[
Meiser 2018
]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{M}
be a randomized privacy mechanism,
and let
\mathcal{S}
be the set of possible outputs of the mechanism.
The mechanism
\mathcal{M}
satisfies
(\epsilon,\delta)
-probabilistic differential privacy if the set
\mathcal{S}
can be partitioned into two disjoint sets
\Omega_{1}
and
\Omega_{2}

such that for all
\mathcal{D}
, we have
\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{D})\in\Omega_{2}\right)\leq\delta,

and for all
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
adjacent to
\mathcal{D}
and all
S\in\Omega_{1}
, we have
\log\Big(\frac{\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{D})=S\right)}{\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{D}^{\prime})=S\right)}\Big)\leq\epsilon.

\blacktriangle

In this work, we partition the output space
\Delta_{n}
into the sets

\Omega_{1}=\{x\in\Delta_{n}\mid x_{i}\geq\gamma\text{ for all }i\in[n]\}

and

\Omega_{2}=\{x\in\Delta_{n}\mid x\not\in\Omega_{1}\}
.

Assumption 2
.

When generating private outputs in
\Delta_{n}
,
\gamma
satisfies
\gamma\leq\frac{1}{n-1}
.

If a mechanism provides
(\epsilon,\delta)
-probabilistic differential privacy, then it provides conventional
(\epsilon,\delta)
-differential privacy
[
Machanavajjhala et al. 2008
]
. As mentioned in
Meiser 2018
, post-processing private data generated by mechanisms that satisfy
(\epsilon,\delta)
-probabilistic differential privacy only preserves conventional
(\epsilon,\delta)
-differential privacy. As a result, we use Definition
6
only as a means to design a mechanism that satisfies Definition
4
.

To solve Problem
1
, we input
C(D,\rho)
into the Dirichlet mechanism to compute a privatized form
\tilde{C}
.
We must then compute the privacy parameters
\epsilon
and
\delta
from Definition
6
. The mechanism in Definition
5
does not take a database as input, and we must extend that mechanism to the case where the queries are functions of a database. This extension primarily involves
\epsilon
.
See the Supplementary Material, Section
A
for details on computing
\delta
.

3.1
Computing
\epsilon

To compute
\epsilon
, we first have two assumptions that ensure the boundedness of the distribution of
\tilde{C}
.

Assumption 3
.

In the bordered unit simplex
\Delta_{n}^{(\eta)}
from Definition
2
, we require
\eta\in(0,\frac{1}{4})
.

Assumption 4
.

The Dirichlet parameter
k
from Definition
5
satisfies

k\geq\frac{3}{2\eta}.

Assumption
3
is easily enforced by the user selecting an appropriate
\eta
, since
\min_{i\in[n]}p_{i}\geq\eta
by the construction of
C(D,\rho)
.
Assumption
4
is also enforced by the user choosing a suitable value for
k
. Assumption
4
highlights that not every count vector can be made arbitrarily private. Instead, there exists some strongest level of privacy (i.e., a smallest
\epsilon
) for a given
C(D,\rho)
. We see in Section
6
that this strongest level of privacy is often well within the range of typically desired
\epsilon
values.
Below we use
\mathcal{C}(\rho)
to denote the set of all possible outputs of
C(D,\rho)
over all databases of size
N
whose entries satisfy Assumption
1
. Next we provide
(\epsilon,\delta)
-differential privacy to
C(D,\rho)
.
All proofs can be found in the Supplementary Material, Section
C
.

Theorem 1

(Solution to Problem
1
)
.

Fix a database
D
with
N\in\mathbb{N}
entries, a category set
\rho
such that
|\rho|=n\in\mathbb{N}
, and
\eta>0
.
Let
C(D,\rho)\in\Delta_{n}^{(\eta)}
be the count vector as defined in (
3
). Let Assumptions
1
-
4
hold. Then the Dirichlet mechanism with parameter
k>0
and input
C(D,\rho)
is
(\epsilon,\delta)
-probabilistic differentially private, where

\epsilon\geq\log\left(\frac{\Beta(k\eta,k(1-2\eta))}{\Beta\left(k\left(\eta+\frac{1}{N}\right),k(1-2\eta-\frac{1}{N})\right)}\right)+\\
\frac{k}{N}\log\left(\frac{1-(n-1)\gamma}{\gamma}\right)

(6)

and

\delta=1-\min_{C(D,\rho)\in\mathcal{C}(\rho)}\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{M}_{\text{Dir}}^{(k)}(C(D,\rho))\in\Omega_{1}\right).

(7)

Theorem
1
shows for the first time that the Dirichlet mechanism in Definition
5
provides
(\epsilon,\delta)
-probabilistic (and thus conventional) differential privacy for simplex-valued queries of databases.
A typical differential privacy implementation begins by choosing a value of
\epsilon

to implement. Theorem
1

and Assumption
4
together show that the smallest possible
\epsilon

here is

\epsilon^{*}=\log\Big(\frac{\Beta(\frac{3}{2},\frac{3}{2\eta}-3)}{\Beta\left(\frac{3}{2}+\frac{3}{2\eta N},\frac{3}{2\eta}-3-\frac{3}{2\eta N}\right)}\Big)+\frac{3}{2\eta N}\log\Big(\frac{1-(n-1)\gamma}{\gamma}\Big)
.
A user can select any
\epsilon\geq\epsilon^{*}
, and that choice can be implemented
by tuning
k
. This workflow therefore largely mirrors the standard workflow
for implementing differential privacy.

STATIONARY DISTRIBUTION AND CONVERGENCE RATE

In this section we solve Problem
4
.
At each time
t
, there exists a probability distribution over the state space of the Markov chain, which we denote
\mu_{t}\in\Delta_{n}
.
Let
\mu_{0}\in\Delta_{n}
denote the initial distribution. The distribution
\mu_{t}
can be computed by right-multiplying by the transition matrix
G
to find
\mu_{t}^{T}=\mu_{t-1}^{T}G
.

We analyze the steady-state behavior of a Markov chain by analyzing the limiting behavior of
\mu_{t}
. When a Markov chain is finite, irreducible, and homogeneous,
\lim_{t\to\infty}\mu_{t}
exists and is called the “stationary distribution", which we denote by
\pi
. The stationary distribution
\pi
satisfies
\pi^{T}=\pi^{T}G
.
A Markov chain in which
\mu_{t}
converges to
\pi
is called “ergodic". We ensure
P(\mathcal{D},\rho)
is ergodic via Assumptions
5
and
6

below. First we establish a key definition.

To analyze the transient behavior of a Markov chain, it is common to analyze the convergence rate to
\pi
. Many metrics exist for doing so, and we consider the “
\infty
-norm ergodicity coefficient" of ergodic Markov chains. See
Ipsen and Selee 2011
for a detailed account of ergodicity coefficients.

Definition 7

(
\infty
-norm Ergodicity Coefficient;
[
Ipsen and Selee 2011
]
)
.

Let
G\in\mathbb{S}_{n}
be the transition matrix of an ergodic Markov chain with stationary distribution
\pi
. Then the
\infty
-norm ergodicity coefficient of the Markov chain is defined as

\tau_{\infty}(G)=\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}\left\lVert z\right\rVert_{\infty}=1,z^{T}\mathbb{1}=0\end{subarray}}\left\lVert G^{T}z\right\rVert_{\infty}.

\blacktriangle

Let the eigenvalue moduli of
G\in\mathbb{S}_{n}
be
1=|\lambda_{1}|\geq\ldots\geq|\lambda_{n}|
. The eigenvalue modulus
|\lambda_{2}|
governs the rate of convergence of
G
to its stationary distribution if
G
is ergodic. Definition
7
provides a bound on
|\lambda_{2}|
when it is difficult to compute exactly.

To solve Problem
4
, we compute the total variation distance in the stationary distribution between a private Markov chain
\tilde{P}
and its non-private
form
P(\mathcal{D},\rho)
, as well as the absolute difference in their
\infty
-norm ergodicity coefficients. For a Markov chain to converge to its stationary distribution, we require it to be (i) finite, (ii) irreducible, and (iii) homogeneous. Condition (i) holds trivially since the state space has the same cardinality as
\rho
, which is finite. Condition (iii) holds since the database is not a function of time. Let the state space of the Markov chain model be
\mathcal{Y}
, and note that each category
\rho_{i}\in\rho
corresponds to a state
y_{i}\in\mathcal{Y}
. We apply the following assumptions on
\mathcal{Y}
and
\mathcal{D}
to ensure condition (ii) holds:

Assumption 5
.

Each state
y_{i}\in\mathcal{Y}
is reachable from any other state
y_{j}\in\mathcal{Y}
in finite time.

Assumption 6
.

In each
D_{i}
, every feasible transition has occurred at least once.

Returning to the traffic scenario in Example
1
, Assumption
5
enforces that all roads whose data are in
\mathcal{D}
can be reached in finite time from any other road with data in
\mathcal{D}
. Assumption
6
enforces that every possible turn on the roads with data in
\mathcal{D}
has been taken at least once.

The next two theorems both rely on a bound on
\|P(\mathcal{D},\rho)-\tilde{P}\|_{1}
, which results in them having some terms in common.
See the Supplementary Material, Section
B
for the derivation of a bound on
\|P(\mathcal{D},\rho)-\tilde{P}\|_{1}
.
Given a database and a category set that satisfy Assumptions
5
and
6
, we first bound the change in the stationary distribution, thus solving the first part of Problem
4
.

Theorem 4

(Solution to Problem
4
, Part 1)
.

Let
\tilde{\pi}
denote the stationary distribution of
\tilde{P}
.
Let Assumptions
1
-
6
hold. Then
P(D,\rho)
converges to a stationary distribution
\pi
.
Let
Z=(I-P(\mathcal{D},\rho)-\mathbb{1}_{n}\pi^{T})^{-1}
and let
\zeta_{i}(x)=\log\big(\frac{x+1}{N_{i}}\big)-\psi\big(\frac{(x+1)k_{i}}{N_{i}}\big)
for all
i\in[n]
. Then we have

\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lVert\pi-\tilde{\pi}\right\rVert_{TV}\right]\leq\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert Z\right\rVert_{1}\sqrt{2L}
,
where

L=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\pi(i)\big(\frac{n-1}{N_{i}}\zeta_{i}(0)+\frac{N_{i}-n+1}{N_{i}}\zeta_{i}(N_{i}-n)+\psi(k_{i})\big).

Theorem
4
provides a tool for users to understand the quantitative change in the stationary distribution as both the number of individuals in the database and the strength of privacy change. Since the digamma function
\psi(x)
grows as
\mathcal{O}(\log(x))
, Theorem
4
shows that the error in the stationary distribution is
\mathcal{O}(\log(k_{i}^{-1}))
,
which implies that accuracy can be substantially improved with small changes in privacy. Similarly, we develop a utility guarantee on the transient behavior (and solve the remaining part of Problem
4
) by bounding the change in the ergodicity coefficient.

Theorem 5

(Solution to Problem
4
, Part 2)
.

Let the conditions of Theorem
4
hold. Let
\tau_{\infty}(P(\mathcal{D},\rho))
be the ergodicity coefficient of
P(\mathcal{D},\rho)
in the sense of Definition
7
and let
\tau_{\infty}(\tilde{P})
be the ergodicity coefficient of
\tilde{P}
. Then

\mathbb{E}\left[|\tau_{\infty}(P(\mathcal{D},\rho))-\tau_{\infty}(\tilde{P})|\right]\leq\sqrt{2L}.

Theorem
5
, similar to Theorem
4
, also provides users with a tool to understand how privacy affects the privatized Markov chain transitions and also behaves as
\mathcal{O}(\log(k_{i}^{-1}))
. Combined, Theorems
4
and
5
provide insight into how the rate of convergence is affected by privacy and into the final error in the stationary distribution.

Conclusion

We have presented a framework privatizing databases that are used to compute both stochastic vectors and stochastic matrices. We proved that this approach is differentially private and provided utility guarantees that allow users to trade-off privacy, convergence rates, and accuracy of stationary distributions. Results show with

(Computation of

Fix a database

\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{K}(P(D,\rho)||\tilde{P})\right]=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\pi(i)\sum_{j=1}^{n}P(\mathcal{D},\rho)_{ij}\big(\log(P(\mathcal{D},\rho)_{ij})+\psi(k_{i})-\psi(k_{i}P(\mathcal{D},\rho)_{ij})\big).

(18)

\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{K}(P(D,\rho)||\tilde{P})\right]\\

(19)

\blacklozenge

Proof.

From Definition

\mathcal{K}(P(\mathcal{D},\rho)||\tilde{P})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\pi(i)\sum_{j=1}^{n}P(\mathcal{D},\rho)_{ij}\log\left(\frac{P(\mathcal{D},\rho)_{ij}}{\tilde{P}_{ij}}\right).

\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{K}(P(\mathcal{D},\rho)||\tilde{P})\right]=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\pi(i)\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{j=1}^{n}P(\mathcal{D},\rho)_{ij}\log\left(\frac{P(\mathcal{D},\rho)_{ij}}{\tilde{P}_{ij}}\right)\right].

\blacklozenge

Proof of Theorem

From the definition of the KL divergence, we have

\mathcal{K}(C(D,\rho)||\tilde{C})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}C(D,\rho)_{i}\log\left(\frac{C(D,\rho)_{i}}{\tilde{C}_{i}}\right).

(47)

Taking the expectation and using linearity yields

\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{K}(C(D,\rho)||\tilde{C})\right]=\sum_{i=1}^{n}C(D,\rho)_{i}\mathbb{E}\left[\log\left(\frac{C(D,\rho)_{i}}{\tilde{C}_{i}}\right)\right]=\sum_{i=1}^{n}C(D,\rho)_{i}\left(\log\left(C(D,\rho)_{i}\right)-\mathbb{E}\left[\log(\tilde{C}_{i})\right]\right).

(48)

The marginal distribution for a single element of a Dirichlet random vector is the Beta distribution
[
Kotz et al. 2019
]
. The expectation of the log of a

distributed random variable

is

\mathbb{E}\left[\log(X)\right]=\psi(\alpha)-\psi(\alpha+\beta).

(49)

Here,
\tilde{C}_{i}\sim\Beta(x;kC(D,\rho)_{i},k-kC(D,\rho)_{i})
. Then we have

\mathbb{E}\left[\log\left(\tilde{C}_{i}\right)\right]=\psi\big(kC(D,\rho)_{i}\big)-\psi\big(k-kC(D,\rho)_{i}+kC(D,\rho)_{i}\big)=\psi(kC(D,\rho)_{i})-\psi(k).

(50)

Substituting (
50
) into (
48
) gives us

\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{K}(C(D,\rho)||\tilde{C})\right]=\sum_{i=1}^{n}C(D,\rho)_{i}\big(\log(C(D,\rho)_{i})+\psi(k)-\psi(kC(D,\rho)_{i})\big)

(51)

as desired.

To determine an upper bound that is independent of the sensitive data, we consider the optimization problem

subject to

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}p_{i}(\log(p_{i})-\psi(kp_{i})+\psi(k))
