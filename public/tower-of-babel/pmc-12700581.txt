Building and Prospectively Evaluating a Prediction Model to Forecast Urgent Dialysis Needs across Four Tertiary Hospitals
Authors: Bryant Lim, Kevin Zhu, Katarina Zorcic, Christopher T.M. Chan, Michael Fralick
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionUrgent dialysis is labor-intensive and expensive because it requires specialized nursing staff. Most hospitals schedule a fixed number of nurses daily for urgent dialysis needs, but daily dialysis demand fluctuates, leading to inefficiencies.MethodsWe developed statistical, machine learning, and deep learning models to predict the next 7 days’ dialysis needs. Our study included a retrospective (April 1, 2018, to March 31, 2023) and prospective component (November 1 to 30, 2023, and May 31 to June 27, 2024) across four hospitals (hospital A for one hospital and hospital B for three hospitals combined). To avoid model over-fitting, we divided our data into three sets: training, testing, and validation. The latter was performed prospectively during two silent deployment periods. The primary outcome measure was the mean absolute error (MAE).ResultsThe mean daily dialysis volume in the retrospective data was 16.0 (standard deviation [SD], 5.7) for hospital A and 4.5 (SD, 2.3) for hospital B. The best performing models were autoregressive integrated moving average (ARIMA) and temporal convolutional network; both resulted in an MAE of 3.0 procedures for hospital A and 1.5 procedures for hospital B, compared to 4.4 and 1.9, respectively, for the benchmark. During our two prospective evaluations, the mean daily dialysis volume was 16.8 (SD, 4.5) for hospital A and 4.2 (SD, 2.5) for hospital B. The ARIMA model resulted in the lowest MAE at 2.2 and 1.5 procedures, respectively.ConclusionsOur multicenter, 6-year study demonstrated that urgent in-hospital dialysis needs can be accurately forecasted.

Introduction

Dialysis is a life-saving procedure for patients with acute renal failure with severe metabolic derangements (e.g., refractory hyperkalemia) [1]. However, dialysis is labor-intensive and expensive because it requires specialized nursing staff [2–4]. Most hospitals schedule a fixed number of nurses each day for urgent dialysis needs. Because urgent dialysis needs are dynamic, this mismatch can lead to inefficiency. This inefficiency can result in excess financial costs when nursing supply exceeds dialysis demand and potentially worse patient outcomes when dialysis demand exceeds nursing supply. Statistical techniques and machine learning have the potential to minimize inefficiency by predicting acute dialysis needs.

Multiple previous studies have shown that acute kidney injury can be predicted using machine learning techniques [5, 6]. Previous studies have also shown that patient volumes can be accurately forecast in settings like emergency departments and radiology clinics, to optimize staffing schedules and workflow [7–9]. These models have been relatively easy to implement because they do not rely on patient-level data within the electronic medical record and instead predominantly use administrative data (e.g., historical patient volumes or time-stamped patient registration data) and weather data. Prior studies have also shown that weather, in particular ambient temperature, is associated with acute kidney injury and acute dialysis needs [10–13]. Models to predict acute dialysis needs for the purpose of nurse scheduling are lacking. The objective for this current study was to train, test, and validate a model for urgent dialysis needs across four hospitals.

Methods

Our study took place at four hospitals in ON, Canada, and included both a retrospective component using data from April 1, 2018, to March 31, 2023, and a prospective component featuring two silent deployment periods: one from November 1, 2023, to November 30, 2023, and a second from May 31, 2024, to June 27, 2024. During the silent deployment periods, predictions were not sent to the end-user. The purpose of the silent deployment periods was to monitor model performance and identify potential data issues without impacting the end-user. Our study was reviewed by the quality improvement committee at our institution, who deemed the study low risk and that the study fell under quality assurance. We thus sought and received approval from the Division of Nephrology Leadership team to conduct this work.

Input variables included times series data of daily dialysis procedures completed, ambient temperature, indicator variables for holidays, and dummy variables for days of the week and month. Dialysis procedures were performed in emergency situations and in settings outside of the standard dialysis department (e.g., intensive care unit, emergency department, inpatient ward). Our model did not include patient-level data such as creatinine, electrolytes, or urine output. Results for hospital A were reported separately from the remaining three hospitals because approximately 80% of all daily dialysis procedures occurred at hospital A and the remaining 20% were spread across the three other hospitals. These three hospitals are collectively referred to as hospital B in this work, for clarity. Notably, it is the same 10 nurses that cover all of the four hospitals for urgent dialysis and each hospital is within walking distance. At baseline, the hospitals schedule a fixed number of nurses each day for urgent dialysis needs. The number is based on the nurse manager’s past experience of the average number of urgent dialysis procedures each day. Ambient temperature data for the city of Toronto were sourced from Environment Canada using station code 31688. The primary outcome was mean absolute error (MAE) in daily dialysis procedures, computed as the mean per day absolute difference between the model’s predicted dialysis counts and the ground truth. Predictions of the next 7 days’ daily number of dialysis procedures were generated once weekly and compared with actual patient volumes. We trained our models using data from April 1, 2018, to March 31, 2022. Data from April 1, 2022, to March 31, 2023, were held out as the test data. To validate our findings, we conducted two prospective deployment periods (November 2023 and June 2024).

As a benchmark, we calculated the average daily number of dialysis procedures in the retrospective dataset (April 1, 2018, to March 31, 2023). We then evaluated the performance of this historical average as a static daily prediction (i.e., every day will have the same number of dialysis procedures) by comparing with actual patient volumes in the prospective dataset and determining the MAE. At the included hospitals, the current practice of dialysis staffing involves assigning a fixed team of ten nurses per day to the acute dialysis service, regardless of daily fluctuations in demand. Because staffing remains constant, the historical average number of dialysis procedures offers a practical reference for evaluation.

We fit our data to an autoregressive integrated moving average (ARIMA) model using the pmdarima.arima.auto_arima packaging, with inputs of historical site count for the past 365 days (y), exogenous variables (X; including ambient temperature, indicator variables for holidays, and dummy variables for days of the week and month), period for seasonal differencing (m) of 7, and max iteration (iter) of 20 (https://alkaline-ml.com/pmdarima/modules/generated/pmdarima.arima.auto_arima.html). This generally outperformed other forecasting models we tested, including Holt and seasonal-trend decomposition (online suppl. Table 1; for all online suppl. material, see https://doi.org/10.1159/000549256). We also trained a temporal convolution network (TCN) [14–17] consisting of 6 dilated causal convolutional layers with a hidden size of 16 and kernel size 7, for 80 epochs using AdamW, with a learning rate of 0.001 that decreases by 50% every 10 epochs, and a weight decay of 0.0001. Each model uses the daily dialysis counts from the previous 365 days to forecast dialysis needs in the subsequent 7 days. For retrospective models, daily dialysis counts between April 1, 2022, and March 31, 2023, were held out for testing. All analyses were performed using Python version 3.9.7.

Results

The retrospective data included a total of 37,388 dialysis procedures, with a daily average of 16.0 (standard deviation [SD], 5.7) for hospital A and 4.5 (SD, 2.3) for hospital B (Table 1). In the training set, the ARIMA model had an MAE for hospital A of 3.5 and 1.5 for hospital B and the TCN model had an MAE of 3.1 for hospital A and 1.5 for hospital B. In the testing set, the ARIMA and TCN models achieved an MAE of 3.0 procedures for hospital A and 1.5 procedures for hospital B (Table 2) compared to 4.4 and 1.9, respectively, for the benchmark.

Discussion

In our multicenter study, we were able to train, validate, test, and implement a model that accurately forecasted urgent dialysis needs. These results suggest that a dynamic approach to staffing for urgent dialysis needs is feasible and potentially cost saving. We chose to use ARIMA as our predictive model due to its strong performance compared to the benchmark and TCN in both the retrospective and prospective components of our study.

From the perspective of a dialysis nurse or nephrologist, it may seem as though urgent dialysis needs are unpredictable. However, that may be a reflection of our own cognitive biases (e.g., recency bias) and our inability to recall what happened (i.e., the number of acute dialysis events) 1 year, 1 month, or even 1 week prior. In contrast, machine learning and statistical techniques can handle historical data with ease to identify patterns and make predictions. The results of our study demonstrate how it is possible to predict dialysis needs, and how scheduling can be directly informed by predicted patient volume outcomes [18, 19]. We acknowledge that it might not be feasible or desirable for nurses to be scheduled only 7 days in advance. In that scenario, the model can be adopted in two ways. First, a fixed number of nurses can be scheduled, and then an on-call pool can be created for scheduling in the subsequent 7 days. Second, in the scenario where the only feasible approach is to schedule a fixed number of nurses each day, the results of the model can be used to inform what to do when a nurse calls in sick. Absenteeism from illness is common across all fields of nursing and is typically a daily occurrence. The results of our model can be used to inform whether the absent nurse needs to be replaced (i.e., the predicted dialysis demand exceeds the nursing supply) or if no replacement would be required (i.e., the forecast is a relatively low number of acute dialysis procedures).

The results of our study align with the broader literature on using artificial intelligence to predict acute kidney injury. A recent review article summarized the results of multiple models developed to predict acute kidney injury in both the outpatient and in-hospital settings [20]. Among the in-hospital studies, most models had an area under the curve exceeding 0.8, demonstrating that the models were able to accurately discriminate between patients who would develop acute kidney injury and those who would not. However, none of these models were specifically predicting acute dialysis needs or assessing how the results could be used to inform scheduling of acute dialysis nurses. Instead, their outcome was typically defined as a rise in creatinine during the hospitalization which has less clinical relevance compared to acute kidney injury requiring acute dialysis.

There are multiple strengths of our study. First, we conducted the study across multiple hospitals, which improves model generalizability. Second, we divided our dataset into three sets: training, validation, and testing. Failure to separate datasets into distinct training, validation, and test sets remains common for clinical prediction tools and leads to overly optimistic model performance. Third, our study included a prospective evaluation period; by contrast, many papers that involve machine learning employ only a retrospective approach to develop their models. Fourth, our purposeful exclusion of patient-level clinical data improves the implementability of our model [21, 22].

Our study also has important limitations. First, all of the included hospitals were adult, academic teaching hospitals, and thus it is unknown how well results will generalize to nonacademic hospitals or pediatric hospitals. Furthermore, future studies are needed to demonstrate how well our results generalize to hospitals outside of Canada. Second, we purposefully did not include patient-level data to improve the ease of implementing the model into care. It is possible that model performance could be enhanced by including select patient-level data (e.g., creatinine, potassium, or urine output) or additional hospital-level data (e.g., overall occupancy, ICU occupancy). Future studies are needed to evaluate whether this provides substantial improvement in model prediction. Third, our model provides short-term predictions, rather than long-term predictions that are often preferred for scheduling. This decision was purposeful because longer term predictions will necessarily be less accurate. A relevant analogy is predicting the weather – relatively easy over short time horizons (e.g., hours, days) but almost impossible over longer time horizons (e.g., weeks, months). Finally, our study did not differentiate between dialysis subtypes such as sustained low-efficiency daily dialysis, intermittent hemodialysis, and continuous renal replacement therapy. Because each has varying resource requirements (e.g., duration of dialysis), accounting for these subtypes may improve model performance.

The results of our multicenter study, which included a prospective evaluation, identified that acute dialysis needs can be predicted using primarily historical acute dialysis data. Future prospective work is needed to identify whether implementing such a model could result in improved efficiency and cost savings.
