A dual-adjuvanted HA stem nanoparticle vaccine elicits a multifunctional antibody response that is associated with protection in newborn monkeys
Authors: Kali F. Crofts, Beth C. Holbrook, Courtney L. Page, Colby J. Hendrix, David Caudell, Rebecca A. Gillespie, Ralph B. D’Agostino, Syed M. Moin, Maya Sangesland, David A. Ornelles, Masaru Kanekiyo, Martha A. Alexander-Miller
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryThe absence of an approved influenza vaccine for young infants, combined with the annual requirement for updates, underscores the urgent need for a universal vaccine that is effective in newborns. The H1ssF vaccine, an influenza hemagglutinin stem-bearing nanoparticle, generates broadly protective responses in adult animal models and humans. Here, we use a nonhuman primate model to explore the ability of H1ssF, adjuvanted with R848 and AddaVax, to induce a protective response in the altered immune setting of newborns. We find that the vaccine induces strong stem-specific IgG responses that are broadly reactive. Notably, the vaccine promotes antibodies with neutralizing activity and broad Fc effector functions in all newborns. Finally, administration of this vaccine confers improved viral clearance after challenge. These findings demonstrate the potential of this dual-adjuvanted stem nanoparticle to serve as an effective vaccine approach that can elicit protective responses in newborns against influenza A virus infection.

Introduction

Young infants are particularly vulnerable to developing severe, life-threatening disease following influenza virus infection. In the US alone, infants under 6 months of age have a 4.7-fold higher rate of death following influenza virus infection compared to children over 2 years of age.1 This increased susceptibility is a result of an altered immune system coupled with the lack of an approved vaccine for infants under 6 months.

Newborns differ from older infants in their bias toward regulatory and tolerogenic responses.2 This bias supports the colonization of the microbiota but can also hinder the ability to mount protective responses against some pathogens, such as influenza virus, leaving newborns more vulnerable to severe infection.

Vaccine strategies for newborns must overcome the immune alterations present in this age group. Among these are diminished antigen-presenting cell (APC) function, decreased proinflammatory cytokines, increased T regulatory cells, and a TH2 bias (reviewed in Crofts and Alexander-Miller3). Newborns are also reported to have dampened germinal center (GC) responses, impacting the generation of high-avidity antibodies (Abs). Factors affecting GC function include altered B cell differentiation, lower levels of somatic hypermutation,4,5 delayed maturation of follicular dendritic cells (FDCs), and decreased functionality of T follicular helper cells (Tfh).6,7 Together, these changes present significant hurdles in eliciting protective Abs in response to vaccination.

Despite the alterations associated with the newborn immune system, there are examples of successful newborn vaccines, supporting the potential to modulate newborn immunity when appropriate stimulatory signals are present. Examples include the Bacillus Calmette-Guerin (BCG) and oral polio vaccines that are administered in some countries at birth.8 Both are live attenuated vaccines with inherent adjuvant properties due to the presence of multiple pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) that promote robust pattern recognition receptor (PRR) signaling. The effectiveness of these vaccines is attributed to inducing TH1-like immune responses.9 In the US, the only approved vaccine given at birth is the hepatitis B vaccine that is adjuvanted with alum.10,11 Efforts are underway to develop additional vaccines for newborns, e.g., HIV Env.12

As our understanding of accessory signals that drive potent immune responses increases, so does our ability to modulate the immune system to promote effective responses in newborns. One approach is the use of adjuvants to increase immune cell activation, resulting in improved Ab responses.8 Our laboratory is evaluating adjuvants that may provide the appropriate accessory signals needed to elicit protective immune responses against influenza A virus (IAV) in newborns. An area of active research is the potential for improved adjuvanticity when adjuvants are combined.13,14

Beyond the efficacy challenges associated with vaccination of young infants, current influenza vaccines offer limited protection against drifted or pandemic strains of IAV, as they predominantly generate neutralizing antibodies (nAbs) to the hypervariable head region of hemagglutinin (HA).15,16 The HA protein undergoes antigenic drift due to immune pressure,15 necessitating annual updates to the vaccine. To overcome this, recent efforts have focused on generating vaccines that promote broadly reactive Abs targeting conserved regions of viral proteins, such as the stem domain of HA.17,18 Efficient generation of stem-specific Abs requires overcoming the subdominant nature of these responses compared to those recognizing the HA head.19,20,21,22,23 Recent advances in HA vaccine candidates are nearing this goal.17,22,24,25 One approach utilizes a trimeric HA stem (derived from H1N1 A/New Caledonia/20/1999) displayed multivalently on a self-assembling ferritin nanoparticle (H1ssF).24 This construct has been shown to elicit B cell and serological responses recognizing diverse group 1 IAV subtypes that are capable of conferring protection in adult animal models24,26 and has elicited durable nAb responses in a human phase 1 clinical trial.27

Recently, we evaluated the effectiveness of the H1ssF vaccine delivered with AddaVax, an MF59-like preclinical adjuvant, using a newborn African green monkey (AGM) model.28 AddaVax was employed as it is similar to the adjuvant used in adult nonhuman primate (NHP) studies. Following prime/boost vaccination, H1ssF + AddaVax elicited high titers of stem-specific Abs that were reactive across group 1 HA strains compared to control animals. Although all newborns generated high levels of Abs, viral load in the lung following pdmH1N1 A/California/07/2009 (Ca09) challenge was variable. Newborns varied in the levels of neutralizing and antibody-dependent cellular phagocytosis (ADCP)-competent Abs; antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity (ADCC) and antibody-dependent complement deposition (ADCD) activities were absent. We found differential viral control was associated with variability in neutralizing and ADCP activity. Here, we sought to develop a second-generation vaccine that could drive robust Ab responses with broad Ab function (nAb, ADCP, ADCC, and ADCD) in newborn AGMs, resulting in improved viral control following challenge.

TLR7/8 agonists have yielded promising results as adjuvants in the context of newborn immunity.29,30,31,32,33,34,35,36,37 R848 can directly activate B cells as well as increase dendritic cell (DC) maturation.31,33,35,36,37,38 Our previous studies evaluating conjugation of R848 to inactivated influenza virus showed that this approach improved IFN-γ-producing T cell responses, Ab responses, and protection compared to a non-adjuvanted inactivated vaccine.31 Additionally, the R848-conjugated vaccine was more effective at generating HA stem-specific Abs compared to a non-adjuvanted vaccine.32 Based on the success of R848 for an inactivated influenza virus vaccine, we hypothesized that conjugation of R848 would improve protection mediated by H1ssF. We tested R848 alone and in combination with AddaVax.

We found that the inclusion of R848 alone resulted in poor Ab responses. In striking contrast, the dual-adjuvanted H1ssF-R848+AddaVax consistently promoted robust HA stem-specific Abs that were broadly reactive across group 1 HA molecules. Furthermore, these Abs exhibited neutralizing activity against both H1N1 strains and, in a subset of animals, H5N1 influenza virus. Importantly, ADCC, ADCP, and ADCD activity were induced in all newborns. The potent Ab response was associated with significant decreases in viral load in the lung compared to control animals. Thus, this combination of R848 and AddaVax adjuvants with the HA stem nanoparticle vaccine platform shows tremendous promise as a tailored vaccine for newborns.

Results

Development of an approach to conjugate R848 to the H1ssF nanoparticle

Multiple studies have shown the enhanced stimulatory capacity that results from linking adjuvant to antigen.34,39,40 The advantage of direct linkage lies in targeting both the immunostimulatory component and the antigen to the same cell, resulting in optimal maturation of the target cell and simultaneously reducing off-target stimulation. We previously developed a strategy for conjugating the TLR7/8 agonist R848 (R) to IAV via the heterobifunctional crosslinker SM(PEG)4 (S),designated R-S.29,30,31,32,38 The R848 used here is a chemically modified amine derivative, hereafter referred to as R848, that binds the N-hydroxysuccinimide (NHS) group on the crosslinker, leaving the maleimide group free to access available thiols on the viral particle.

To enhance conjugation of R848 to H1ssF, we replaced a surface-accessible serine residue on the ferritin protein in H1ssF with a cysteine (Cys) residue (S111C) (H1ssF-Cys).41 This resulted in 24 available sites for coupling SM(PEG)4-R848 on each S111C nanoparticle (24). Using electron microscopy, we demonstrated that the H1ssF-Cys mutant correctly self-assembled (Figure 1A). The H1ssF-Cys nanoparticle had a hydrodynamic diameter of 32.8 nm by dynamic light scattering (DLS), which is similar to that of the wild-type (WT) H1ssF at 30.3 nm (Figure 1B). Both WT and Cys mutant H1ssF exhibited similar heterogeneity (polydispersity index of 0.149 for H1ssF and 0.159 for H1ssF-Cys), suggesting that the addition of the Cys mutation did not alter the structural properties of the nanoparticle (Figure 1C). Next, we linked R848 to the H1ssF-Cys mutant, using the approach we previously established for IAV virions (Figure 1D).31 R848 was added to the heterobifunctional crosslinker at concentrations that saturated all NHS groups, leaving the maleimide group on the crosslinker accessible to thiols on the cysteine residues of the ferritin subunit. R-S was then conjugated to the H1ssF-Cys mutant. We added 100X, 300X, or 900X (relative to available ferritin Cys conjugation sites) of R-S to determine the amount needed to saturate conjugation. To remove unbound R848, H1ssF-R848 was dialyzed and subjected to multiple rounds of ultrafiltration.

We tested the H1ssF-R-S nanoparticles prepared with different saturation levels of R848 for their ability to elicit IgG responses in mice. Six-week-old BALB/c mice were immunized with 3 μg of H1ssF-Cys, H1ssF-R848 (conjugated using 100X, 300X, or 900X R-S), or H1ssF + AddaVax (as our positive control) (Figure 1E). IgG capable of binding trimeric NC99 stem was measured 14 days post vaccination (p.v.). There was no significant difference in stem-specific IgG in mice vaccinated with H1ssF-100XR-S compared to those receiving the non-conjugated vaccine (Figure 1E). In contrast, mice that received H1ssF-300XR-S had increased stem-specific IgG (Figure 1E). This did not increase further with H1ssF-900XR-S (Figure 1E). These data suggest that 300X R-S results in a vaccine with maximal stimulatory activity, presumably through saturation of all available sites on the nanoparticle. To ensure that the increase in HA stem-specific Ab was a result of R848 conjugation and not residual free R848, we immunized mice with non-conjugated H1ssF mixed with the same volume of flow-through (obtained at the final purification step for R848 100X, 300X, and 900X) present in the conjugated vaccine (designated H1ssF-XR-S FT, Figure 1E). This ensured that any activity observed with our conjugated H1ssF was the result of linked R848 and not residual free R848 present in the vaccine preparation. Importantly, there was no significant difference in the level of IgG in mice that received H1ssF-Cys together with the flow-through samples, indicating that the increase in IgG was not a result of free R848. Based on these data, we chose 300X R-S for vaccine preparation moving forward. H1ssF-300XR-S will hereafter be referred to as H1ssF-R848. We assessed this vaccine after conjugation by electron microscopy, finding symmetrical cores and small spikes, showing that the conjugation process did not alter the structural integrity of the H1ssF or the stem trimers on its surface (Figure 1F).

H1ssF-R848+AddaVax promotes a broadly reactive HA stem-specific Ab response in newborn AGMs

We next tested the efficacy of H1ssF-R848 alone or in combination with AddaVax in our newborn AGM model, which was previously established in our laboratory for analysis of experimental influenza vaccines.28,29,30,31,32,42,43,44,45 NHPs are a highly relevant preclinical model given their immunological, developmental, and physiological similarities to humans.46,47 In addition, they are similar to humans in TLR distribution and function,46 an important factor for evaluating IAV vaccines adjuvanted with the TLR7/8 agonist R848.

Newborn AGMs, aged 3–5 days old (equivalent to ∼12- to 20-day-old humans, Table S1), received a prime and boost dose of H1ssF-R848, H1ssF-R848+AddaVax, or non-adjuvanted H1ssF (Figure 2A). The H1ssF dose (40 μg) was selected based on previous studies with this vaccine in newborn AGMs.28 Non-vaccinated control (Ctrl) newborns received either PBS or an mRNA lipid nanoparticle vaccine expressing luciferase (leveraged from an ongoing study). Newborns vaccinated with H1ssF-R848+AddaVax showed a significant increase in HA stem-specific IgM (Figure 2B) and IgG (Figure 2C) at all time points following vaccination compared to those administered H1ssF or Ctrl animals. Responses in newborns receiving H1ssF without adjuvant were similar to Ctrl animals. Notably, as early as 10 days p.v., H1ssF-R848+AddaVax-vaccinated newborns exhibited elevated IgG levels compared to those administered H1ssF or Ctrl animals. Early in the study, we found that newborns vaccinated with H1ssF-R848 (n = 2) had detectable, though notably lower, responses compared to H1ssF-R848+AddaVax and to our historical data using H1ssF + AddaVax. Given the model used, we did not continue administering this vaccine. However, we believe this is an important result and show these data for comparison throughout the study.

Given the rapid and robust response observed with H1ssF-R848+AddaVax, we evaluated C-reactive protein (CRP) as a measure of systemic immune activation and inflammation. All animals had CRP levels under 10 mg/L at 1 day p.v., which is considered within the normal range48 (Figure S1A). Further, no significant differences were observed among the vaccine groups. We also measured temperature at 1 day p.v. All animals were within the normal range (Figure S1B). There was also no difference in weight gain across the groups over the course of the experiment (Figure S1C). Finally, no indication of adverse reactions at the site of vaccine administration was observed. These findings suggest that the dual-adjuvanted vaccine does not induce adverse local or systemic reactions.

Next, we assessed the capacity of HA stem-specific Abs to recognize divergent subtypes of HA, using samples from the time at which the response was most mature in our experimental regimen, 41–45 days p.b. H1ssF-R848+AddaVax elicited Abs capable of broad recognition of group 1 HAs (H1, H2, H5, and H9) (Figure 2D).

Given the outbred nature of the newborn AGMs, we were curious whether the binding pattern across the HA molecules differed among individual animals. To address this, we normalized recognition of each HA type vs. the NC99 (threshold titer of HA X:threshold titer of HA NC99). We observed differences among animals in their pattern of recognition of the individual group 1 HA molecules, i.e., animals that recognized H9 most efficiently do not necessarily exhibit the highest recognition of H5 (Figure 2E). Together, these data show that the stem-specific Abs elicited by the vaccine are capable of binding diverse group 1 HA subtypes; however, the efficiency of binding across the HA molecules in relation to the parent strain varied among animals. These data suggest that there may be inter-animal differences in the epitope specificity of the stem-specific polyclonal responses.

Two major epitopes have been identified in the HA stem, designated central and anchor.49 To better understand the specificities of plasma Abs to these two epitopes, we utilized a panel of mutant NC99 HA molecules: one in which binding to the central epitope is blocked by the introduction of an N-linked glycosylation site (I545N and G547T [H3 numbering], H1-N45),50 one in which binding to the anchor epitope is blocked by addition of a glycan (Q27N and N29T, H1-N27), and a dual mutant incorporating glycans at both the central and anchor epitopes (H1-N27/45).51 We evaluated the response to the central and anchor epitopes at 41–45 days p.b. in newborns that received H1ssF-R848+AddaVax. Vaccinated newborns generated a stronger Ab response to the central epitope than to either the anchor or non-central/anchor epitopes (Figures 2F and S2). Interestingly, we noted that the quantity of central stem Ab varied considerably among the H1ssF-R848+AddaVax animals (Figure 2F).

In some instances, the generation of HA stem Abs has been reported to increase susceptibility to autoimmune diseases in mice.52 To explore this possibility in our model, we measured IgM and IgG anti-nuclear antibodies (ANAs) (Figures S3A and S3B). There was no indication that the combination of R848 and AddaVax resulted in an increase in these Abs.

An important attribute of effective vaccines is the ability to drive increases in avidity with a second dose. To determine if a boost dose could increase the avidity of stem-specific Abs, we employed an assay wherein NaSCN is titrated in the ELISA to disrupt Ab binding. Boosting resulted in a significant increase in stem-specific IgG avidity in these newborns that was evident by 10–11 days p.b. (Figure 2G). The avidity of the stem-specific Ab did not further increase at 41–45 days p.b.

Lastly, we evaluated the capacity of the H1ssF-R484+AddaVax vaccine to promote IgA in the plasma at 41–45 days p.b. (Figure 2H). Remarkably, we observed a significant increase in the level of circulating IgA in the dual-adjuvanted infants compared to Ctrl and H1ssF newborns.

Vaccination with H1ssF-R848+AddaVax results in vaccine-specific T cell responses

CD4+ T cells are important contributors to the generation of Ab responses (Tfh), as well as serving as potent effectors (Th1) to combat infection.53 We evaluated the generation of cells capable of producing IFN-γ and/or IL-21 in response to stimulation with a mixture of overlapping peptides derived from the HA stem and ferritin. This approach was used to maximize detection of vaccine-induced T cells in our limited day 10 p.b. PBMC sample. Peptide stimulation resulted in IFN-γ+ as well as IL-21+ cells in vaccinated, but not Ctrl, newborns (Figure 3). The presence of IL-21 suggests that Tfh may be generated following vaccination; however, we cannot rule out production of this cytokine by other CD4+ T cell subsets based on our assay design. IL-4-producing cells were not detected (Figure 3). H1ssF-R848+AddaVax results in reduced viral load following Ca09 challenge in newborn AGMs

To evaluate the efficacy of H1ssF-R848+AddaVax in newborn AGMs, we challenged animals with H1N1 Ca09 virus (1 × 107 TCID50) at 41–45 days p.b. Viral load in the bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) was quantified on day 7 post challenge (p.c.). Animals vaccinated with H1ssF-R848+AddaVax had significantly lower viral load in the lung compared to both H1ssF and Ctrl animals (Figure 4A). H1ssF-R848 animals did not have evidence of reduced viral load; however, no significance testing was performed based on the number of animals tested. To more fully understand the protective capacity associated with vaccination, we evaluated disease in the lungs. On average, newborns administered H1ssF-R848+AddaVax exhibited reduced disease scores in the lung compared to Ctrl animals, but this did not reach statistical significance (Figure 4B). Thus, there is no evidence that vaccination was associated with a deleterious response that induced damage following challenge, and it may instead be reducing pathology. Finally, cytokines in the BAL and plasma were measured to assess the inflammatory response present following infection, given the reported contribution of high levels of these cytokines to influenza-mediated disease.54,55 We found significantly reduced IL-6 and IL-1β in the lungs of vaccinated newborns (Figure 4C). These data are noteworthy given the report showing the association between IL-6 levels and influenza disease severity.54 IL-8 levels trended lower (p = 0.0549). IL-8 was found to be significantly reduced in the plasma (Figure S4). We next investigated the Ab response present in the respiratory tract of challenged infants. H1ssF-R848+AddaVax-vaccinated animals had higher HA stem-specific IgG in the BAL than Ctrl or H1ssF groups (Figure 4D). Interestingly the two H1ssF-R848-vaccinated animals appeared to have similar IgG levels in the lung compared to H1ssF-R848+AddaVax-vaccinated animals. Although HA stem-specific IgA was readily detected in circulation, only one of the H1ssF-R848+AddaVax animals had detectable IgA in the BAL at this time (Figure 4E).

Reduced viral load in the lung correlates with onboard nAbs in newborn AGMs

HA stem-specific Abs can promote clearance through neutralizing activity.57,58 To assess the presence of nAbs, we used a panel of replication-restricted reporter influenza viruses that included A/New Caledonia/20/1999 (H1N1), A/California/07/2009 (H1N1), and A/Vietnam/1203/2004 (H5N1).59 In contrast to Ctrl or H1ssF animals, all H1ssF-R848+AddaVax-vaccinated newborns had detectable nAbs to both NC99 and Ca09 at 41–45 days p.b. (Figures 5A and 5B). The level of nAbs was not significantly different for NC99 and Ca09. Three of the seven animals generated detectable nAbs against H5N1 (Figure 5C). To understand the role of nAbs in viral clearance, we performed a correlation analysis between nAbs to NC99 and viral load in the BAL (Figure 5D). Abs from H1ssF-R848+AddaVax newborns exhibited significantly increased ADCP activity compared to H1ssF and Ctrl animals (Figure 6E). Interestingly, the scores for the H1ssF-R848-vaccinated animals were appreciably higher than for H1ssF-vaccinated newborns. This may suggest that R848 can promote Abs with ADCP activity, although this cannot be definitively concluded based on the small number of animals.

Finally, we evaluated the potential for Abs to induce complement activation at days 41–45 p.b., as deposition and killing are important mechanisms of pathogen control. This pathway can be initiated by IgM or IgG binding to viruses/infected cells, promoting C1q deposition.65 To measure ADCD, we evaluated C3 deposition on HA-bearing beads in the presence or absence of plasma.66 All H1ssF-R848+AddaVax animals had readily measurable ADCD activity (Figure 5F) compared to H1ssF and Ctrl animals. Together, these findings indicate that adjuvanting H1ssF with the combination of R848 and AddaVax can induce an Ab response in newborn AGMs possessing broad Ab effector functions.

To understand the relationship between Ab function and viral load in the lung at day 7 p.c., we explored whether there was a correlation between these activities and viral load. No correlation was observed for ADCC and viral clearance (−r = 0.46, p = 0.09) (Figure 6G). In contrast, both ADCP and ADCD were negatively correlated with viral load (higher activity associated with lower viral load) (Figures 6H and 6I).

Discussion

The development of a safe and effective broadly protective vaccine for newborns would represent a major step forward, given the heightened risk of severe disease and death in this vulnerable population following influenza virus infection. This is of growing importance given the threat of emerging IAV variants that could result in global pandemics. Our recent study suggested H1ssF offers promise in the setting of AGM newborns.28 However, only a subset of vaccinated animals showed evidence of significant viral clearance. Building on these foundational studies, we sought to increase the consistency and quality of the vaccine response through inclusion of the combination of AddaVax and the TLR7/8 adjuvant R848. The results reported here reveal that the combined presence of these adjuvants profoundly changes the response induced by H1ssF. We found a uniformly robust Ab response with a broad array of effector functions, including neutralization, ADCP, ADCD, and ADCC. Importantly, the immune response generated with the dual-adjuvanted vaccine resulted in significant reductions in viral load and inflammatory cytokine in the lungs of newborns compared to Ctrl animals.

A growing body of literature, including our own studies, has shown TLR7/8 agonists to be highly effective adjuvants for modulating the response in newborns.29,30,31,32,33,34,35,36,37 R848 signaling through TLR7/8 promotes proinflammatory responses from DCs and T cells, as well as activation of B cells.30,34,35,37,38,67 Given this, we were surprised to see the limited impact of adjuvanting H1ssF with R848 alone. However, the reported benefit of combination adjuvants,13,14 led us to explore the addition of AddaVax in combination with R848. Our data show that the combination of these two adjuvants promoted a robust humoral response, with stem-specific IgG reaching levels higher than those achieved with AddaVax alone (Figures S5A and SB [H1ssF + AddaVax originally reported in Crofts et al.28]). The finding that inclusion of both adjuvants extended the Ab effector functions to include ADCC and ADCD compared to H1ssF + AddaVax, which only induced Abs with ADCP activity, is remarkable (Figures S5C and S5E). While generating a higher level of nAbs is considered a correlate of protection against IAV, recent evidence highlights the role of non-neutralizing effector functions of stem-specific Abs as important contributors to protection.60,61 Together, our findings underscore the benefits of combining H1ssF-R848 with AddaVax to improve both the quantity and quality of Ab responses in newborn AGMs.

AddaVax, an MF59-like adjuvant, promotes the robust recruitment of innate cells to the vaccine injection site.68,69 Recruitment is driven by the release of ATP from damaged muscle cells, which acts as a danger-associated molecular pattern (DAMP) to attract immune cells.70 MF59 has also been shown to promote increased Ag uptake and trafficking of APCs to draining lymph nodes (dLNs), enhancing antigen accumulation and retention.71 These activities culminate in improved Ag presentation to T cells, ultimately boosting the magnitude of the Ab response.72 We hypothesize that AddaVax facilitates innate cell uptake of the nanoparticle at the injection site and trafficking to dLNs, which ensures sufficient antigen delivery and supports CD4+ T cell activation. CD4+ T cell activation is important for Tfh cell differentiation, naive B cell activation, and strong GC activity.73 We propose that R848 may enhance the response by modulating the inflammatory environment, both at the injection site and within the dLNs. R848 has been shown to promote maturation of newborn DCs and stimulate the secretion of proinflammatory cytokines,31,33,35,36,37,38 providing additional support to newborn APCs that often exhibit reduced maturation and increased cytokines that promote TH2 skewing. Additionally, R848 may be directly promoting activation of stem-specific B cells via their expression of TLR7.30 Based on these findings, we believe the combination of these adjuvants offers complementary mechanisms to enhance immune responses, leveraging both innate immune recruitment and adaptive immune activation, which may explain the increased Ab levels observed in H1ssF-R848+AddaVax-vaccinated animals.

The differences in Ab function found with AddaVax alone versus the dual adjuvant underscore the potential for modulating Ab effector functions through the use of adjuvants. Accumulating data indicate that adjuvants can shape Ab function through regulation of isotype/subclass as well as glycosylation (reviewed in Bowman et al.62). The inflammatory signals present at the time of immunization play a crucial role in this process. A recent study in NHPs immunized with the HIV envelope protein gp140 together with different adjuvant formulations found adjuvant-dependent changes in Ab glycan profiles that correlated with differences in Fc-mediated effector functions.74 In humans, administration of HA protein adjuvanted with MF59 preferentially enhanced Abs that stimulated neutrophil phagocytosis and complement activity compared to alum.66 This was attributed to increases in IgG1 and IgG3 production, enhancing binding to FcγRIIa.66 Much remains to be learned about the interplay between the stimulatory signals provided by combination adjuvants in the context of the newborn.

In conclusion, our study bolsters the potential for an HA stem-specific vaccine adjuvanted with the combination of linked R848+AddaVax to generate protective responses in newborns against IAV. The combination of these adjuvants was striking in the ability to increase Ab quantity and quality compared to either adjuvant alone. Especially noteworthy was the generation of a multifunctional Ab pool possessing the full array of effector activity, i.e., nAb, ADCC, ADCP, and ADCD. This work emphasizes the importance of identifying optimal adjuvant combinations to promote robust immune responses in newborns, paving the way for more effective vaccination strategies early in life. Furthermore, it supports the continued exploration of this universal influenza vaccine approach for protecting this vulnerable population against seasonal and pandemic influenza strains.

Limitations of the study

Our study has limitations. It was designed to identify a universal influenza vaccine approach that could be effective in the altered immune setting of the newborn, and thus responses were assessed in the absence of maternal Abs. This evaluation is a critical and necessary step in exploring this vaccine for use in this age group. Understanding how maternal Abs impact this response will be an important, although admittedly non-trivial, next step given the model. Further, the method of infection does not fully replicate the natural transmission route of IAV infection. AGMs were infected via the combined intranasal/intratracheal routes, which, although ensuring exposure of the respiratory tract to the virus, differs from the aerosol route typical of human infection. Further, our assays may not fully capture the breadth of Ab Fc effector function, as the Fc receptors on the cells used in these assays are from humans or macaques.

Experimental model and study participant details

Mice

Six-week-old female BALB/c (strain code 028) mice were obtained from Charles River Laboratories. The animals were housed in a controlled light and temperature environment at Wake Forest School of Medicine, Winston Salem, USA. All experimental procedures, protocols, and care of the animals in this study complied with the institutional guidelines approved by the Wake Forest Animal Care and Usage Committee.

African green monkeys

Newborn (3–5 day old) male and female African green monkeys (AGMs) were immunized, sampled, and housed at the Vervet Research Colony at Wake Forest University School of Medicine, Winston-Salem, USA. The animal care and use protocol adhered to the US Animal Welfare Act and Regulations and was approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee. AGMs were housed and cared for according to state, federal, and institutional guidelines in facilities accredited by the American Association for Accreditation of Laboratory Animal Care (AAALAC), following the standards established in the Animal Welfare Act and the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals.

Cell lines

MDCK-SIAT1-PB1 cells were developed and maintained at the Vaccine Research Center, NIH. No authentication was performed. Mycoplasma testing was performed monthly. KHYG-1 MmCD16 were received as a gift from Dr. David Evans. No further authentication was performed following receipt. CD16 expression was confirmed by flow cytometry. THP-1 cells were originally obtained from ATCC and procured through the WFUSM CCC Cell Engineering Shared Resource. THP-1 cells were authenticated at ATCC by STR profiling. Mycoplasma testing was performed by the Cell Engineering Shared resource. All cell lines were maintained in a humidified 5% CO2 incubator at 37°C. The sex of the cell lines in this study is unknown.

Method details

Conjugation of R848 to H1ssF-Cys mutant

H1ssF was generated as per.24 The native cysteine at position 31 of H. pylori ferritin was substituted to a serine to minimize the chance of disulfide bond formation during biosynthesis. Further, this cysteine is buried internally and not solvent exposed. Therefore, it is not suitable for chemical conjugation purpose. Thus, to conjugate R848 to H1ssF, an H1ssF was engineered that replaced a surface accessible serine residue on ferritin with a cysteine (Cys) residue (S111C) based on a previously reported approach.41 The Cys mutant provides an accessible free thiol on the surface of each ferritin subunit for coupling (24 available sites per H1ssF nanoparticle). We linked an R848 amine derivative to the heterobifunctional cross-linker SM(PEG)4 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) using the same protocol that we used to link R848 to influenza virions.31 R848 was added under conditions that maximally saturate the NHS group on SM(PEG)4. R848-SM(PEG)4 was then incubated with H1ssF-Cys mutant to allow maleimide binding to the free thiol available on the H1ssF-Cys nanoparticle. The optimal amount was determined through addition of titrated amounts (100X, 300X, or 900X) of R848-SM(PEG)4 based on the knowledge that for 1μg of H1ssF-Cys there are 1.4658x1013 potential R848-SM(PEG)4 binding sites. H1ssF-Cys and R848-SM(PEG)4 were incubated for 2h at 37°C. Non-bound R848-SM(PEG)4 was removed by dialysis using a 20K MWCO Slide-A-Lyzer MINI Dialysis Device (Thermo Scientific) (2 h at 4°C with rocking). The dialysis buffer was replaced and the sample was dialyzed again overnight. Finally, H1ssF-R848 was subjected to 3 rounds of ultrafiltration using a 10k Microcon Centrifugal Filter (Millipore) to ensure complete removal of non-bound R848. The flow through from the final column was saved and added to H1ssF as a control any effect of residual free R848 in the vaccine. The concentration of H1ssF-R848 was determined using the Pierce BCA Protein Assay Kit (#23227) (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Vaccine was snap frozen, stored at −80°C, and thawed on ice prior to administration.

Murine vaccination with H1ssF-R848

Mice were vaccinated intramuscularly in the hind leg with 100μL of vaccine that contained 3μg of H1ssF, H1ssF-100XR-S, H1ssF-300XR-S, H1ssF-900XR-S, H1ssF + AddaVax (InvivoGen), H1ssF+100XR-S flow through, H1ssF+300XR-S flow through, or H1ssF+900XR-S flow through. Submandibular blood collection was performed on d0 and d14p.v. Trimeric A/NewCaledonia/20/1999 (H1N1) stabilized HA stem protein24 was used to assess stem-specific Ab in serum. ELISA microplates (96-half well) (Greiner bio-one) were coated with 100 ng/well of antigen overnight at 4°C. Plates were blocked for 1 h with Casein Blocking Buffer (Sigma-Aldrich) and 2% goat serum (Lampire Biologicals) followed by washing (PBS with 0.1% Tween 20). Serum samples were serially diluted in 1X blocking buffer. Wells that contained no antigen served as a negative control. Sheep anti-mouse IgG-HRP (GE NA931V) (1:2000) was used to detect bound Abs. Plates were developed using 3,3′,5,5′-Tetramethylbenzidine dihydrochloride TMB (Sigma-Aldrich), stopped with and read at 450nm on a BioTek Elx800 Absorbance Microplate Reader. For each dilution, the OD450 from the non-stem-coated wells was subtracted from the stem-coated wells. Threshold titer was defined as the value that reached 3X the assay background, i.e., wells that only received 1X blocking buffer+stabilized stem (no sample).

Negative stain electron microscopy

H1ssF was imaged as previously reported.76 Protein samples were diluted to 0.020–0.075 mg/mL in 25 mM Tris, pH 8.0, with NaCl concentrations ranging from 0.15 to 2 M. 300 mesh copper grids (Ted Pella) were glow discharged immediately before use. Six μL of sample was applied to the grid for 1 min, then briefly dipped in a droplet of water before excess liquid was blotted away with Whatman no. 1 filter paper. Grids were stained with 6 μL of 0.75% (w/v) uranyl formate stain, immediately excess liquid was blotted away, then stained again with another 6 μL for 30 s. Grids were imaged on a Morgagni transmission electron microscope with a Gatan camera, and Gatan Digital Micrograph software was used to take images.

Dynamic light scattering

Vaccine samples were prepared at 400 μg/mL and centrifuged at 14,000 × g for 45 min to remove dust. Samples (70 μL) were placed in a cuvette and run on the ZetaSizer Nano ZS (Malvern Panalytical, Worcestershire, UK) equipped with a 633nM laser. A total of 10 measurements were made for each sample run at 25°C ± 0.1°C. The cumulative Z-Average and polydispersity index (PDI) was automatically determined using the ZetaSizer software.

Newborn AGM study design

Healthy newborn AGM (weighing ≥300g) were immunized at 3–5 days of age, corresponding to an approximately 12–20-day-old human. Sixteen newborn AGM were randomly assigned to different vaccine groups: PBS or Luciferase mRNA-LNP (non-HA vaccinated controls) (Ctrl) (n = 5), H1ssF (n = 4), H1ssF-R848 (n = 2), and (H1ssF-R848+AddaVax (n = 5). Animals were assigned to ensure an equal number of male and female animals. Newborns were administered 20 μg of H1ssF or H1ssF-R848 with or without AddaVax (125 μL of AddaVax (InvivoGen)) intramuscularly into the deltoid muscle of both left and right arms (40 μg of H1ssF per dose). A booster dose was similarly administered at d39/40p.v. Plasma samples were collected at d1p.v., d10p.v., d39/40p.v., d10/11p.b., d41/45p.b., and d7p.c. On d41/45p.b., newborn AGM were challenged with 1x107 TCID50 of A/California/07/09 (Ca09) (BEI Resources).

Detection of HA Ab and avidity assessment

Trimeric A/NewCaledonia/20/1999 (H1N1) stabilized HA stem protein24 was used to assess stem-specific Ab. For cross reactivity ELISAs, trimeric recombinant full length HA proteins were used. The panel included: A/New Caledonia/20/1999 (H1) (BEI Resources, NR-48873), A/California/07/2009 H1(BEI Resources, NR-42635), A/Singapore/1/1957 (H2) (BEI Resources, NR-52249), A/Wisconsin/67/2005 (H3) (BEI Resources, NR-49237), A/Vietnam/1204/2004 (H5) (VRC, NIH), A/Anhui/1/2013 (H7) (BEI Resources, NR-44365), and A/HongKong/33982/2009 (H9) (BEI Resources, NR-41792). We used FI6V3 HA-stem mAb as the positive control at 0.156 μg/mL (VRC, NIH).75 We utilized HA ectodomain glycan mutants on the stem to assess the epitope specificity of the stem response. Q27NHA2 and N29THA2 mutations were made to introduce a glycan at 27HA2 (H1-N27) to block the anchor epitope.51 I45NHA2 and G47T mutations introduced a glycan at 45HA2 (H1-N45) to block the central epitope.50 The H1-N45/27 contained all four mutations.51 Full length HA ectodomain from A/NewCaledonia/20/1999 (H1N1) was used as the H1-WT control.51 To assess Ab production, ELISA microplates (96-half well) (Greiner bio-one) were coated with 40 ng/well of antigen (NC99 trimeric stem, H1-N27, H1-N45, H1-N45/27, H1-WT) or 100 ng/well of recombinant HA protein diluted in PBS (stem and glycan mutant ELISAs) or sodium carbonate/bicarbonate coating buffer (pH 9.5) (cross-reactivity ELISA) overnight at 4°C. The plates were blocked with 1X blocking buffer (10X Casein Blocking Buffer, Sigma-Aldrich) plus 2% goat serum (Lampire Biologicals) for 1 h and then washed. Wash buffer contained PBS with 0.1% Tween 20. Plasma samples were serially diluted in 1X blocking buffer. For avidity assessment, NaSCN was diluted in 2-fold increments starting at 5M, added for 15 min prior to washing plate. Wells that contained no antigen served as a negative control. HRP-conjugated antibodies specific for goat anti-monkey IgM (Fitzgerald #43R-IG074hrp) (1:10,000) and goat anti-monkey IgG (Fitzgerald #43C-CB1603) (1:5,000) were used to detect bound antibodies. For detection of IgA, goat anti-monkey IgA-Biotin (Fitzgerald 43R-IG002bt) (1:5,000) was used, followed by NeutrAvidin-HRP (Thermo Fisher 31001) (1:5,000). Plates were developed using 25 μL of 3, 3′, 5, 5′-Tetramethylbenzidine dihydrochloride (TMB) (Sigma-Aldrich), and stopped after 30 min by addition of 25 μL of 2N H2SO4. Plates were read at 450nm on an Elx800 Absorbance Microplate Reader (BioTek). For each dilution, the OD450 from the no antigen wells was subtracted from the antigen-coated wells. Threshold titer was defined as the value that reached 3X the assay background, i.e., wells that only received 1X blocking buffer + antigen in the absence of plasma. To evaluate the binding pattern across HA molecules, for each animal the ratio of HA TT: NC99 HA TT was calculated. AUC was calculated with GraphPad Prism software using the trapezoidal method. The values used included: x axis = plasma dilutions, and y axis = absorbance 450nm. The plasma dilutions used were the untransformed values.

RT-PCR for viral detection

Viral RNA was extracted from the tracheal wash and bronchoalveolar lavage samples using a Quick-RNA Viral 96 Kit (Zymo Research). For cDNA synthesis and viral RNA quantification, the LUNA Universal Probe One-Step RT-qPCR Kit (New England BioLabs) was utilized, employing primers designed by the CDC to target the Influenza A virus M2 gene (primer sequences provided below). The primers and probes, following the CDC’s recommended sequences (see below), were purchased from Integrated DNA Technologies (IDT). The probe contained the reporter molecule 6-carboxyfluorescein (FAM) at the 5′ end with an internal ZEN quencher and the Iowa Black FQ quencher (IABkFQ) at the 3’ end. The one-step RT-qPCR reaction was carried out in a 10 μL volume, with 0.25 μM FAM-labeled probe, 0.45 μM of each forward primer, 0.65 μM InfA Rev1 and 0.2 μM InfA Rev2 primers. Quantitative RT-PCR (qRT-PCR) was performed using a BioRad CFX384 Real-Time System accompanied by BioRad CFX Maestro software for analysis. Viral genomes/mL (vg/mL) were calculated based on a standard curve generated using a synthetic DNA construct (purchased from IDT) containing target sequences for the Influenza A virus (GenBank accession number MN976418.1, A/Hawaii/66/2019[H1N1], segment 7, matrix gene). The sequence for the DNA construct used in standard curve generation is provided below, with uppercase letters indicating the influenza A and influenza B targets. The total vg/mL for each sample was calculated based on the RNA extracted from a 200 μL sample volume, with adjustments made to determine the total vg/mL. The CDC designed primer sequences used for RT-qPCR can be found in the STAR Resources table.

Cytokine quantification

Cytokines were measured using a human inflammatory cytokine bead array (BD Biosciences) performed per the manufacturer’s instructions on BAL collected on d7p.c. Samples were acquired on a BD Fortessa X20 (BD Biosciences) and data analyzed using FCAP Array software.

Immunohistochemical analysis on AGM lung sections

Samples were taken at d7 p.c. from the left and right cranial, middle, and caudal lobes. Tissues were fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin for a minimum of 24 h at a volume of 1:20 ratio tissue to fixative. Tissues were then placed in labeled tissue processing cassettes and were moved to 70% ethanol for holding and delivery. Cassettes were loaded onto an automated vacuum infiltration tissue processing instrument (Excelsior AS, Fisher Scientific). Samples were run on an overnight program as follows: 80% ethanol – holding solution until processing cycle begins, 85% ethanol – begins dehydration, 90% ethanol – continues dehydration, 95% ethanol - continues dehydration, 100% ethanol – removal of all not bound water in the samples, 100% ethanol - continues removal of all not bound water in the samples, 100% ethanol – completes dehydration of samples, Xylene – begins removal of all ethanol in the samples, Xylene – continues clearing of samples, Xylene – completes clearing of samples. Infiltration Paraffin – heated to 60°C-61°C to remain melted. Samples were removed from the tissue processor and were embedded in metal molds creating paraffin blocks for sectioning. Paraffin blocks were sectioned on a Leica RM 2255 semi-automatic microtome at 4 microns. Sections were mounted from a warm water bath on labeled glass slides and air-dried overnight. Slide drying and H&E staining were then performed on a Leica Spectra ST automatic stainer (Leica Biosystems, IL) according to the following program: Oven drying at 60°C (1 h), Xylene for deparaffinization (2 changes), 100% Ethanol to remove xylene (2 changes), 95% Ethanol to introduce water to sections (1 change), Run tap water rinse, Gill’s Hematoxylin II (Poly Scientific R&D Corp), run tap water to rinse, ammonia water (bluing solution for hematoxylin), run tap water rinse, Eosin/Phloxine B Stain, 95% Ethanol, 100% Ethanol for dehydration (2 changes), Xylene (2 changes), last Xylene – slides remained here until coverslips were applied using Permount Mounting Medium (# SP15-500, Fisher Scientific). Finished slides dried at room temperature overnight to secure the coverslips. One Hematoxylin and Eosin-stained section from each sample was examined by light microscopy in a blinded fashion using a custom scoring system developed by two veterinary pathologists. Pneumonia lesions were scored and graded based on their distribution throughout the pulmonary parenchyma, lesion patterns, consolidation, and disease stage. The distribution score was defined as normal (0), <25 = 1, 26–50 = 2, 51–75 = 3, and >75% = 4 of parenchyma affected. Lesion pattern scores were defined as normal (0), focal (1), multifocal (2), coalescing (3), or diffuse (4). The product of the distribution score and pattern score were added to the stage score for a total lesion score. Disease stage was defined as normal (0), Emerging (1), Early (2), Progressive (3), and Established (4). The maximum score was then ranked into a Final Grade (FG) (0–16) to reflect overall disease severity. For each lung section, the values from the four scored categories were summed. The average is used for the final disease score.

Microneutralization assay

The generation of replication-restricted reporter (R3ΔPB1) viruses for H1N1 subtypes (A/California/07/09 & A/New Caledonia/20/99) as well as the rewired R3ΔPB1 (R4ΔPB1) virus for H5N1 (A/Vietnam/1203/04) has been previously described.59 In brief, the R3/R4ΔPB1 viruses were generated by replacing the viral genomic RNA encoding the functional PB1 protein with a gene encoding the fluorescent protein TdKatushka2. These viruses were then rescued through reverse genetics and propagated in the complementary cell line that constitutively expresses PB1. Each R3/R4ΔPB1 virus stock was titrated by measuring the fluorescent units per mL (FU/mL) prior to experimental use. For titration, serial dilutions of the virus stock in OptiMEM were mixed with pre-washed MDCK-SIAT1-PB1 cells (8x105 cells/ml) and incubated in a 384-well plate in quadruplicate (25 μL/well). Plates were incubated at 37°C with 5% CO2 humidified atmosphere for 18–26 h. Following incubation, fluorescent cells were imaged and counted using a Celigo Image Cytometer (Nexcelom) with a customized red filter to detect TdKatushka2 fluorescence. For the microneutralization assay, serial dilutions of Receptor Destroying Enzyme (RDE)-treated plasma were prepared in OptiMEM and mixed with an equal volume of R3/R4ΔPB1 virus (∼8x104 FU/mL) in OptiMEM. After 1h of incubation at 37°C and 5% CO2 humidified atmosphere, pre-washed MDCK-SIAT1-PB1 cells (8 x 105 cells/well) were added to the plasma-virus mixtures and transferred to 384-well plates in quadruplicate (25 μL/well). Plates were incubated and counted as previously described. The target virus control range for this assay was 500 to 2,000 FU per well, with the cell-only control being acceptable up to 30 FU per well. Percent neutralization for each well was calculated by setting the virus control (virus plus cells) as 0% neutralization and the cell-only control (no virus) as 100% neutralization. A 7-point neutralization curve was plotted against serum dilution for each sample, and the 50% inhibitory concentration (IC50) was calculated using a four-parameter nonlinear fit generated in Prism (GraphPad).

Ab dependent cellular cytotoxicity

KHYG-1 cells transduced with rhesus macaque CD1664 were used to conduct the ADCC assay, adapted from.77 ELISA plates (Corning) were coated with 50 μL (2 μg/mL in PBS) of recombinant HA protein (A/California/07/09) (BEI Resources) and incubated overnight at 4°C. The plates were then washed and blocked with PBS containing 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) at 37°C in a 5% CO2 incubator for 30 min. After removing the blocking solution, heat inactivated plasma samples were serially diluted (starting dilution 1:10) and incubated at 37°C in a 5% CO2 incubator for 2 h. Following additional washes, 1x105 cells KHYG-1 MmCD16 cells were added per well (50 μL) in media (RPMI +10% FBS +1% L-glut, 0.2% Primocin). Recombinant human IL-2 (Biolegend, #589104) (10 U/mL, 50 μg/mL) was added to the cells. CD107a PE (Biolegend, #328608) (1:50) was added to 25 μL of warmed media and then added to plates containing cells. Golgi Plug (1:1000) and Golgi Stop (1:1500) (BD Biosciences) were then added to cells in 25 μL of warmed media. Cells (100 μL total) were incubated for 5 h at 37°C in a 5% CO2 incubator. After incubation, cells were transferred to a 96 V-bottom plate, washed with PBS, and stained with Zombie Violet (Biolegend) for 15 min at room temperature (RT) in the dark. Following additional washes with FMF, cells were fixed with 2% paraformaldehyde. ADCC activity was assessed using flow cytometry on a BD X20 Fortessa cytometer. The percentage of CD107a+ NK cells was calculated by subtracting the PBS control values from the HA specific values for each dilution.

Ab dependent cellular phagocytosis

THP-1 cell phagocytosis of HA coated beads was performed as previously detailed.78 Biotinylated HA A/California/07/2009 (kindly provided by the DCVC, Duke University) was adsorbed onto 1.0 μm of fluorescently labeled beads called FluoSpheres NeutrAvidin (yellow-green fluorescent (505/515)) at a ratio of 10 μg protein to 10 μL beads and incubated overnight at 4°C. In a 96-round bottom plate, 10 μL of HA coated beads were then incubated with 10 μL of heat-inactivated plasma diluted in PBS (2-fold dilutions, starting dilution 1:800) for 2 h at 37°C. After washing away any unbound Ab, THP-1 cells (RPMI +10% FBS +1% L-glut, 1% P/S) (5x104 cells/well) were added and incubated for 16 h at 37°C. Following incubation, the cells were fixed, and phagocytosis was analyzed using flow cytometry on a BD X20 Fortessa (BD Biosciences). Phagocytosis scores were calculated by multiplying the percentage of bead positive cells by the geometric mean fluorescence intensity and dividing by 1000 at the 1:800 dilution.

Ab dependent complement deposition

Ab dependent complement deposition of HA coated beads was performed as previously outlined.66 HA coated 1 μm non-fluorescent NeutrAvidin beads were prepared as in the ADCP assay and incubated serially diluted, heat-inactivated plasma samples (2-fold dilutions, starting dilution 1:5) for 2 h at 37°C. Lyophilized guinea pig complement (Cedarlane, CL4051) was reconstituted in ice-cold dH20, then diluted in 1:60 veronal buffer with 0.1% gelatin (GVB++. Boston BioProducts, #IBB-300X). Following this, 150 μL of the diluted complement was added to the opsonized beads and incubated for 20 min at 37°C. The beads were subsequently washed with 15 mM EDTA and stained with anti-guinea pig C3-FITC (MP Biomedicals, # 855385). After washing, ADCD was analyzed by flow cytometry using a BD X20 Fortessa (BD Biosciences). Complement scores were calculated by multiplying the percentage of C3+ beads by the GMFI and dividing by 1000, using the 1:5 dilution.

C-reactive protein quantification

C-reactive protein levels in the plasma were assessed at d1p.v. using a human C-Reactive Protein ELISA kit KR9710s (30-9710S) from ALPCO Diagnostics as per the manufacturer’s instructions. The plate was read at 450nm on a BioTek Elx800 Absorbance Microplate Reader. Amounts were calculated based on the standard curve generated using the control provided in the kit.

Detection of monkey anti-nuclear Abs

Monkey anti-nuclear IgM (# 670-115-ANM) and IgG (# 670-110-ANM) (Alpha Diagnostic International) was detected as per manufacturer’s instructions. Plasma samples were diluted at 1:50. The monkey ANA IgM and IgG ELISA kits is based on the binding of monkey Abs to extractable nuclear antigens (ENA). ENA antigen in these kits is a combination of several nuclear autoantigens (dsDNA, SSA/Ro, SSB/LA, Scl70, Sm, RNP, and Jo-1). Plates were read at 450 nm on an Elx800 Absorbance Microplate Reader (BioTek). The dotted line represents the limit of detection of the assay.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Multiple analytic methods were employed in these studies. The statistical details of each experiment can be found in the figure legends. For analyses assessing the effects of different vaccine groups over time, two-way repeated measures ANOVA models were utilized. If there was evidence of overall treatment effects, then group comparisons at each time point were made were made, adjusting for multiple comparisons using Tukey’s method. For analyses comparing vaccine groups at a single time point, one-way ANOVA models were applied. When overall treatment effects were observed, pairwise comparisons were conducted using either Tukey’s or Fisher’s Least Significant Difference method to account for multiple comparisons. Correlations were calculated and analyzed for associations between two continuous variables. For endpoints that could not be assessed with parametric methods (such as t-tests or ANOVA), Wilcoxon rank-sum or Kruskal-Wallis tests were performed. For all models, p-values were defined as ∗p = ≤ 0.05, ∗∗p = ≤0.005, ∗∗∗p = ≤0.001, ∗∗∗∗p = ≤0.0001. Depending on the analyses performed, the following three software packages were used Prism 10.1.2 (GraphPad, La Jolla, CA, USA), GraphPad or SAS Version 9.4.
