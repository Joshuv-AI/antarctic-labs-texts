Response to “Correction and clarification on reported percentage error of estimated continuous cardiac output”
Authors: Antonio Romanelli, Alessandro Calicchio, Salvatore Palmese, Sabato Pascarella, Bruna Pisapia, Renato Gammaldi
Published: 2025

Dear Editor:

We thank the authors of the Letter titled, “Correction and clarification on reported percentage error of estimated continuous cardiac output [1],” for their careful analysis of our work and for the opportunity to clarify several points.

In our discussion, we summarized published percentage error (PE) for estimated continuous cardiac output (esCCO) as “between 18% and 69%,” citing studies that spanned comparators and clinical settings [2-5]. We acknowledge that, for Joshi et al. [3], the PE we intended to report is 46.29%, and that the 69% appearing in our text is a typographical error. We apologize for this mistake.

Regarding the rationale for citing Taniguchi et al. [4], our intention was to provide a broad overview of the available evidence on esCCO performance across populations and reference methods. We were fully aware that their cohort was pediatric and assessed with transthoracic echocardiography, and we did not intend to extrapolate those results to adult practice.

We agree that, in several reports, the PE for esCCO exceeds the conventional 30% benchmark. However, the 30% threshold is a rule-of-thumb derived under specific assumptions (e.g., ≈20% precision of the reference method and homoscedastic errors) and is not a universal pass–fail criterion across all settings or comparators. In our article, we did not claim interchangeability of esCCO with invasive standards; rather, our goal was to explore the feasibility of estimating energy expenditure in critically ill patients by leveraging available hemodynamic information and to synthesize the heterogeneous evidence on esCCO performance.

Finally, regarding terminology (“minimally/less invasive” vs. “non-invasive”), we agree with the correspondents that esCCO, which relies solely on ECG, non-invasive blood pressure, and pulse oximetry, should be described as non-invasive. Any previous use of “minimally/less invasive” was intended as a generic contrast with invasive reference methods (e.g., thermodilution) and not as a specific classification of esCCO.

We believe the points raised are valuable and merit clarification; however, they do not alter the results or the conclusions of our study.
