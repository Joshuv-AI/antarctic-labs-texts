Comparative efficacy of zinc oxide nanoparticles, MSC-derived microvesicles, and platelet-rich fibrin in rabbit tendon regeneration
Authors: Mohamed Marzok, Mahmoud Hassan, Mohamed Abd Elkawi, Zakarya Al Mohamed, Abdelrahman Hereba, Yamen Hegazy, Mohamed El-Sherif
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTendon injuries represent a significant clinical challenge because of their poor vascularity and limited intrinsic healing potential. This study aimed to comparatively evaluate the regenerative efficacy of zinc oxide nanoparticles (ZnONPs), mesenchymal stem cell-derived microvesicles (MVs), and advanced platelet-rich fibrin (A-PRF) in promoting Achilles tendon repair in a rabbit model. Thirty-six adult New Zealand rabbits were assigned to four groups: untreated control, (0.5 mL of 0.2% sterile dispersion), (1 mL gel injected twice within one week), and A-PRF (membrane applied at surgery) groups. Standardized full-thickness Achilles tendon defects were surgically induced. The animals were euthanized at 2, 4, and 6 weeks via sedation with xylazine (5 mg/kg) followed by thiopental sodium (100 mg/kg IV). Histopathological healing scores and the mRNA expression of proinflammatory (TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6) and anti-inflammatory (TGF-β and IL-10) genes were analyzed via real-time PCR. Treatment resulted in the most advanced histological remodeling by week 6, characterized by continuous, well-aligned collagen fibers, minimal inflammation, and normalized cellularity. Compared with the control groups, both the ZnONPs and MVs groups presented significant downregulation of TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 and concurrent upregulation of TGF-β and IL-10 (p < 0.05). Compared with ZnONPs, A-PRF induced early vascularization and fibroblast proliferation but delayed collagen maturation. ZnONPs exhibited the greatest capacity to accelerate tendon regeneration through the modulation of inflammatory mediators and collagen synthesis, suggesting their potential as cost-effective biomaterials for tendon repair.Keywords: Achilles tendon, Healing, Nanoparticle, Platelet-rich fibrin, Stem cellSubject terms: Biotechnology, Cell biology, Diseases, Medical research, Stem cells

Introduction

The Achilles tendon, known as the strongest tendon in the animal body, plays a crucial role in transmitting forces from the gastrocnemius and soleus muscles to the calcaneus, enabling movement1. Tendon injuries and tendon tears are common musculoskeletal disorders that account for a significant percentage of limb injuries, ranging from 30% to 50%2,3. However, owing to their relatively weak vascular system, tendons tend to heal more slowly than other soft tissues do4,5. The initial stage of tendon repair involves the formation of scar tissue to restore continuity at the injury site. It is crucial to maintain mobility during the healing process to prevent adhesions and increase strength6. The aim of rehabilitation after tendon repair is to restore optimal function and gliding to avoid tendon rupture7. As a result, various studies have been conducted on how to strengthen the repaired tendon, with clinical and experimental research mostly concentrating on the type of repair method used8,9.

Several factors, including basic fibroblast growth factor (FGF), platelet-derived growth factor (PDGF), epidermal growth factor (EGF) and the transforming growth factor beta (TGF-β) subfamily, have been linked to tendon repair in previous studies8,10. TGF-β is a superfamily of growth factors that performs a variety of tasks, such as recruiting macrophages and fibroblasts, promoting the synthesis of collagen, decreasing proteinase activity, and enhancing the activity of metalloproteinase inhibitors11,12.

Recently, tissue engineering techniques have provided new approaches for tendon defect reconstruction and have achieved satisfactory results in Achilles tendon restoration with several tendon scaffolds13,14.

Platelet-rich fibrin (PRF) involves a concentration of autologous platelets on a fibrin membrane without added external factors and has high potential for tissue repair15,16. In angiogenesis, PRF works in the stimulation phase, followed by vascular growth and increased collagen production throughout fibroblast proliferation17. Therefore, its use has been recommended as a strategy to improve the cellular response to tendon injury and the quality of repair18.

There is a novel and promising experimental and clinical strategy in regenerative medicine19. The differentiation of postnatal somatic stem cells or mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) into different cell lineages and tissues, such as tendons, has been observed. Furthermore, it has been demonstrated that MSCs, particularly bone marrow-derived stem cells (BSCs), improve tendon healing20–22.

Currently, nanotechnology has many applications in biology and biomedical engineering, including as a vehicle to deliver therapeutic agents, as a scaffold for engineering various tissues and as an integrated part of bioimplants23. ZnONPs are employed as antibacterial agents that produce reactive free radicals and break membranes to kill gram-positive and gram-negative bacteria. By scavenging reactive free radicals and increasing antioxidant enzyme activities, ZnONP-induced bone and soft-tissue regeneration, among other possible uses, opens new possibilities for tissue engineering24.

The present study aimed to comparatively investigate the regenerative efficacy of ZnONPs, MSC-derived MVs and autologous A-PRF in tendon healing through histopathological, immunohistochemical and real-time polymerase chain reaction analyses.

Methods

Animal model and housing

In this study, thirty-six adult New Zealand albino rabbits, with an average body weight of 3.5 ± 0.5 kg, were utilized. The animals were maintained in stainless steel cages under standardized environmental conditions, including a controlled temperature of 25 ± 5 °C, a relative humidity of 60–70%, and a 12-hour light/dark cycle. They were provided ad libitum access to dry food pellets and tap water. Prior to the experiment, the rabbits were acclimatized for two weeks in the laboratory. A thorough examination of the hock and Achilles tendons was performed before the study to confirm the absence of inflammation, swelling, or rupture, thereby ensuring tendon integrity. The experimental rabbits were randomly and equally divided into four groups (n = 9), and each group was equally divided into three subgroups according to the observation period during the 2nd, 4th and 6th weeks (n = 3).

Biomaterial preparation

Preparation of MSC-derived MVs

MVs solution was prepared just before surgery via the use of 3 g of powdered heterogenic lyophilized MSC-derived extracellular vesicle solution purchased from (Bioluga®, Canada) reconstituted in 5 mL of sterile normal saline according to the manufacturer’s instructions and manually mixed until a homogeneous gel-like suspension was obtained.

Preparation of zinc oxide nanoparticles

dispersions with a concentration of 20 wt% H2O (Sigma‒Aldrich Chemicals, Nasr City, Cairo, Egypt) were purchased and stored in the dark at room temperature. The package contained 100 g of ZnO-NPs in the form of a milky white color dispersion in a dark glass bottle. The ZnONPs dispersion was diluted to 0.2% using 0.9% normal saline, following the method described by Fielding et al.25.

Preparation of the A-PRF membrane

The A-PRF membrane was prepared on the same basis as described in previous rabbit studies26,27.

Surgical procedure

After aseptic preparation, a slightly curved 1 cm incision was made laterally to the Achilles tendon to create a skin flap, ensuring that the tendon would not be injured under the skin wound. Two parallel incisions, 0.5 mm apart and 5 mm long, were made in the tendon, spanning from the tendon-bone insertion at the calcaneus to the mid-tendon. Micro scissors were used to remove the middle portion of the tendon, creating a full-thickness defect at the mid-substance28. The defect was injected with 0.5 ml of normal saline in the control group and filled with A-PRF membrane in the A-PRF-treated group. In the MV-treated group, the defect was injected with 1 ml of MVs gel between and around the defect twice within a one-week interval, whereas in ZnONPs treated group, 0.5 ml of 0.2% ZnONPs solution was injected into and around the tendon defect.

Experimental rabbit euthanasia

At each experimental endpoint (2, 4, and 6 weeks post-treatment), the animals were deeply anesthetized before euthanasia to ensure complete loss of consciousness and the absence of pain perception. Anesthesia was induced by intramuscular administration of xylazine hydrochloride (5 mg/kg, Xyla-Ject, ADWIA Co., Egypt) followed by intravenous injection of thiopental sodium (100 mg/kg, EIPICO, Egypt) via the marginal ear vein. After confirmation of deep anesthesia through the absence of corneal and pedal reflexes, euthanasia was performed in accordance with the American Veterinary Medical Association (AVMA) Guidelines for the Euthanasia of Animals (2020).

Assessment and evaluation

Histopathological examination

Achilles tendons were harvested by dissection just below the musculo-tendinous junction and at the calcaneal insertion. The harvested tendons were washed with physiological buffer, fixed in 10% buffered formalin for 48 h, dehydrated, cleared in xylene, and embedded in paraffin. Thin longitudinal sections with thicknesses of 4–5 μm were obtained from solidified paraffin blocks and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and Masson’s trichrome (MTC)29. Histological tendon repair was assessed via a scoring system based on six parameters: fiber structure, fiber arrangement, rounding of the nuclei, inflammation, angiogenesis, and cell density30–33. Each parameter was assigned a grading score ranging from 0 to 3 (Table 1). The histopathological score was evaluated by two pathologists (without previous information).

Gene expression analysis

Total RNA extraction

To extract total RNA from 50 mg of tendon tissue, an ultrasonic homogenizer (Sonics-Vibracell, Sonics and Materials Inc., USA) was used. The homogenization process was carried out via the use of 0.5 mL of TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen-Thermo Fisher, Amresco, LLC-Solon, USA) (33). Total RNA was obtained from skin tissues as directed by the manufacturer’s guidelines. The yield and purity of the resulting RNA were subsequently evaluated34.

Real-time qRT–PCR

The Revert Aid H Minus First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (#K1632, Thermo Scientific Fermentas, St. Leon-Ro, Germany) was used in strict accordance with the manufacturer’s guidelines to reverse transcribe identical quantities of total RNA from each sample. Maxima SYBER Green (Thermo Scientific Fermentas St. Leon-Ro, Germany) and a StepOne Real-Time PCR Detection System (Applied Biosystems) were used for real-time PCR. The specific primers used are presented in Table 2. Two micrograms of RNA per reaction were included, and 30 cycles of 95 °C for 10 s and 60 °C for 60 s were used for qRT‒PCR35. The expression of target genes was calculated and normalized to that of glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH), which was used as an internal control. The relative RNA abundances were evaluated via the comparative Ct method. To calculate the relative expression, the formula 2 (-ΔΔCt) was used36.

Statistical analysis

The results are presented as the median ± standard deviation. Statistical analyses were performed via one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Bonferroni’s multiple comparisons tests. A p value of ≤ 0.05 was considered statistically significant. The statistical analysis was conducted via GraphPad Prism 9 (version 9.5.1; La Jolla, CA).

Results

Histopathological assessment

The histopathological score revealed the superiority of the ZnONPs group over the other groups, which achieved the best histological outcomes by 6 weeks, with continuous fibers, compact/parallel arrangements, spindle-shaped cells, minimal inflammation (< 10%), and normal cellularity. The MV group exhibited balanced healing, with high early inflammation and vascularity (2w) supporting proliferation, followed by improved fiber structure and alignment and reduced inflammation by 6 weeks. The A-PRF group promoted early healing (2–4 w) with high inflammation, vascularity, and cellularity, which is consistent with growth factors stimulating fibroblast activity and angiogenesis. After 6 weeks, the fiber structure (slightly fragmented) and alignment improved, and inflammation was reduced, indicating progression to remodeling, which was not as advanced as that in the ZnONP group. The control group demonstrated poor tendon healing outcomes at 6 weeks, with severely fragmented fibers, disorganized arrangement, persistent inflammation (> 30%), and sustained high vascularity and cellularity (Table 3; Fig. 1).

Discussion

Tendons, which are highly organized connective tissues that transmit muscle forces to bones, possess limited self-repair capacity due to poor vascularity and cellularity, leading to fibrotic scarring after injury. Despite the exploration of various therapeutic strategies, including growth factors, mechanical stimulation, gene transfer, and tissue engineering, achieving complete biomechanical and histological restoration remains challenging, making the identification of effective interventions to restore near-normal tendon function a central objective in regenerative musculoskeletal research37–39–40. The rabbit model was selected for this study because of its established reliability in tendon research, offering appropriately sized tendons for precise surgical manipulation and assessment, biomechanical characteristics that approximate larger mammals, and a reproducible, ethically manageable framework for evaluating regenerative therapies in translational tendon repair studies41,42.

Histopathological assessment revealed that ZnONPs-treated tendons achieved superior regeneration by week 6, characterized by continuous, well-aligned collagen fibers; minimal inflammation (< 10%); and normalized cellularity resembling the native tendon architecture. These findings are consistent with previous studies reporting that zinc-based nanomaterials enhance collagen synthesis, regulate proteoglycan production, and promote fibroblast differentiation43,44. The enhanced collagen maturation in the ZnONPs group likely reflects the capacity of the nanoparticles to modulate cellular proliferation and accelerate the transition from immature type III collagen to mechanically superior type I collagen during the remodeling phase. In contrast, A-PRF-treated tendons presented improved fiber structure (slightly fragmented) and reduced inflammation by week 6, indicating progression toward remodeling but not as advanced as ZnONPs did. MVs-treated tendons exhibited balanced healing with improved fiber alignment and reduced inflammation, whereas control tendons demonstrated poor outcomes with severely fragmented fibers and persistent inflammation (> 30%).

All therapeutic interventions modulated neovascularization differently throughout the healing phase. A-PRF-treated tendons exhibited pronounced vascular proliferation at weeks 2 and 4, followed by regression by week 6, which is consistent with the findings of Dietrich et al., who reported that PRF-mediated growth factor release (PDGF, VEGF, and TGF-β) stimulates early angiogenesis before transitioning to vascular regression during remodeling45. The sustained release of growth factors from the natural fibrin matrix of PRF over four weeks promotes this temporal vascular pattern, which is essential for organized tissue maturation38,46–48. MVs-treated tendons demonstrated moderate, sustained vascularization accompanied by progressive fiber organization, suggesting a balanced angiogenic response supportive of structural restoration. This finding aligns with that of Shen et al., who showed that mesenchymal stem cell-derived extracellular vesicles attenuate inflammation and modulate macrophage polarization while supporting controlled angiogenesis49. ZnONPs-treated tendons presented modest but consistent vascular density during early healing, as reported by Singh et al., reflecting the dual role of these nanoparticles in promoting neoangiogenesis while maintaining regulated vascular development24. The control tendons maintained prolonged vascularization and hyperproliferation throughout week 6, indicating delayed maturation due to the absence of exogenous regulatory signals.

Gene expression analysis revealed significant downregulation of proinflammatory markers (IL-1β, TNF-α, and IL-6) in all the treatment groups, with the most pronounced suppression observed in the ZnONPs-treated tendons by week 6 (p < 0.05). These findings are consistent with those of Bisciotti et al. and Morita et al., who documented an early cytokine surge during acute inflammation followed by a progressive decline as healing progressed50,51,. IL-1β levels increased immediately post injury but decreased progressively after treatment, with ZnONPs demonstrating the earliest and most substantial reduction. This pattern reflects the critical role of IL-1β in initiating cellular degradation and immune cell recruitment during the inflammatory phase, followed by its necessary suppression for the transition to proliferative healing. TNF-α, which activates the NF-κB signaling pathway and triggers downstream synthesis of cyclooxygenase-2, prostaglandin E₂, and additional cytokines52,53, has similar temporal expression patterns, with peak levels occurring within the first two weeks post injury. Compared with the other groups, the ZnONP group presented significantly accelerated TNF-α downregulation, suggesting enhanced anti-inflammatory capacity. IL-6 expression is induced by activated T lymphocytes and amplified by IL-1β and TNF-α through autocrine and paracrine mechanisms54, followed by comparable kinetics, with notable suppression in the treated groups by week 4. The superior cytokine modulation in ZnONPs-treated tendons likely results from the intrinsic antioxidant properties of the nanoparticles, which reduce reactive oxygen species generation and support cellular viability while limiting prolonged inflammatory signaling55–57.

Concurrently, with respect to proinflammatory cytokine suppression, all the treatment groups demonstrated significant upregulation of the anti-inflammatory mediators TGF-β and IL-10, with the most pronounced increase in the ZnONPs-treated tendons by week 6. This cytokine shift reflects the successful transition from inflammation to matrix reconstruction, which is consistent with the findings of Chen et al., who described a similar temporal interplay between inflammatory suppression and growth factor activation during tendon healing58. TGF-β upregulation is particularly significant, as this cytokine orchestrates multiple regenerative processes: it suppresses residual proinflammatory mediators (TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6), facilitates macrophage recruitment, modulates neutrophil activity, promotes angiogenesis and granulation tissue formation, and stimulates fibroblast proliferation and myofibroblast differentiation, which are essential for collagen synthesis and wound contraction59–63. IL-10 elevation further reinforces the anti-inflammatory microenvironment necessary for organized matrix remodeling. The coordinated upregulation of these regenerative mediators in ZnONPs-treated tendons suggests optimal modulation of the healing cascade, enabling efficient progression through the inflammatory, proliferative, and remodeling phases. Several studies support these temporal patterns: Berglund et al. reported that IL-1β levels decreased two weeks post repair in rabbit flexor tendons; Eliasson et al. reported that TNF-α and IL-1β levels decrease within three days after rat Achilles tendon repair; and Koshima et al. reported that the IL-1β protein level decreases two weeks after rotator cuff repair in rabbits64,65,.

The synergistic effects of ZnONPs, PRF-derived growth factors, and MSC-derived microvesicles collectively promoted enhanced fibroblast recruitment, proliferation, and myofibroblast differentiation at the injury site, accelerating matrix synthesis and remodeling24,45,66. Fibroblasts serve as principal producers of type I collagen and extracellular matrix components essential for tensile strength, whereas endothelial cells establish the microvascular network supporting oxygen and nutrient delivery67,68. Compared with those in the control group, the increased density of actively migrating fibroblasts in the treated groups facilitated an earlier transition into the remodeling phase, resulting in accelerated collagen maturation and superior fiber alignment. This finding is consistent with the observations that controlled inflammatory modulation and growth factor availability are critical for organized tissue regeneration. During the remodeling phase, provisional type III collagen is progressively replaced by mechanically superior type I collagen through a process regulated by growth factors, including IGF-1, TGF-β, and BMPs, which collectively govern fibroblast differentiation, collagen synthesis, and matrix crosslinking69. The superior collagen organization observed in the ZnONPs-treated tendons reflects the optimal coordination of these cellular and molecular processes.

The present study demonstrated that ZnONPs effectively modulate inflammation and optimize collagen production, achieving histological outcomes closely resembling normal tendon architecture. Postoperative infection significantly delays tendon healing and compromises tissue integrity70, and ZnONPs address this challenge through dual antimicrobial and regenerative properties; they exert broad-spectrum antibacterial activity through reactive oxygen species generation and membrane disruption while simultaneously modulating oxidative stress, increasing cell proliferation, regulating regenerative gene expression pathways, and providing antioxidant protection that sustains cellular viability55–57. This multifunctional capacity positions ZnONPs as cost-effective biomaterials for clinical tendon repair applications.

The current study has several limitations related to resource constraints, which should be addressed in future research. Future studies should incorporate functional assessments of ultimate tensile strength, elastic moduli, and failure strain to correlate structural improvements with mechanical recovery and validate their clinical applicability. Moreover, studies with larger sample sizes are warranted to increase the robustness and generalizability of the findings. Comprehensive biomaterial characterization should also be conducted to ensure consistency, strengthen scientific rigor, and facilitate mechanistic interpretation.

Conclusions

This study demonstrated that, compared with mesenchymal stem cell-derived microvesicles (MVs) and advanced platelet-rich fibrin (A-PRF), zinc oxide nanoparticles (ZnONPs) enhanced tendon regeneration. ZnONPs promoted well-aligned collagen fiber organization, suppressed proinflammatory cytokines, and upregulated anti-inflammatory mediators, resulting in a tissue architecture closely resembling that of the native tendon. Owing to their combined antioxidant, antimicrobial, and pro-regenerative effects, ZnONPs are cost-effective biomaterials for tendon repair. The study was limited by sample size and resource constraints. Future investigations should include biomechanical assessments. Comprehensive biomaterial characterization and larger, long-term studies are needed to confirm reproducibility and clinical relevance.
