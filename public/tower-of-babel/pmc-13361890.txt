Biomimetic hybrid nanoparticles for dual regulation of macrophage pyroptosis and metabolism in rheumatoid arthritis
Authors: Guang Yang, Lingxiao Xu, Ruijie Zhang, Zhenyu Zhu, Kelei Wang, Kaipeng Jin, Yifan Wu, Hanxu Huang, Jiaxuan Zou, Wushi Cui, Xiaohua Yu, Lingling Sun, Zhenxuan Shao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Rheumatoid arthritis (RA), a chronic and debilitating autoimmune inflammatory disease, is critically driven by macrophages within the inflamed synovium. The inflammatory cascade is sustained by a synergistic imbalance between macrophage pyroptosis and metabolic dysfunction, resulting in the formation of a dual barrier to effective intervention. GSDME-targeted siRNA delivery effectively blocks pyroptosis; however, uncorrected metabolic defects leave “saved” macrophages in a proinflammatory state, thereby aggravating RA. To address this “dual dilemma”, folic acid-modified macrophage membrane–lipid hybrid nanoparticles (FA-MMLNPs) were engineered for spatiotemporally coordinated codelivery of GSDME siRNA (siG) and 4-octyl itaconate (4-OI). The biomimetic membrane promotes inflammatory homing, folic acid enhances FR-positive macrophage endocytosis, and the lipid core protects the siRNA and ensures synchronous release. Mechanistically, SiG inhibits pyroptosis, whereas 4-OI restores mitochondrial metabolism to reprogram macrophages to an anti-inflammatory phenotype. This integrated material platform organically combines pathway-specific gene silencing, metabolic correction, and precise biointerface engineering, providing a versatile paradigm for material design in RA and other inflammation-driven diseases.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12951-026-04502-2.

Introduction

Rheumatoid arthritis (RA), a chronic and progressive autoimmune disease, affects 0.5–1% of the global population [1]. Approximately 50% of patients develop varying degrees of disability within 10 years of disease onset [2]. Current clinical therapies for RA mainly rely on nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs (DMARDs), biologics, and small-molecule drugs, yet these therapies all have inherent limitations including limited efficacy, slow onset, high cost, and insufficient safety [3–5]. Consequently, the exploration of novel, safe, and highly effective therapeutic strategies is an urgent need in the field of RA research.

Inflammatory macrophages are the core driving force in RA pathogenesis. Their proinflammatory activation is not driven by a single mechanism but by synergistic dysregulation between aberrant pyroptosis activation and metabolic derangements. Gasdermin E (GSDME, also known as DFNA5), a member of the gasdermin protein family, plays a critical role in mediating pyroptosis [6]. In the inflammatory microenvironment, cleaved caspase-3 cleaves full-length GSDME (GSDME-FL) to liberate its N-terminal fragment (GSDME-N), which then forms plasma membrane pores to facilitate the release of proinflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β [7, 8]. This pathological cascade leads to aberrant GSDME-mediated pyroptosis, which exacerbates synovial inflammation and bone erosion and is a key pathological driver of RA and other autoimmune diseases [9–11]. Although siRNA-mediated GSDME silencing provides a targeted strategy for RA therapy by inhibiting macrophage pyroptosis [12–15], it cannot reverse metabolic reprogramming defects in proinflammatory macrophages. As a result, the “rescued” cells remain metabolically dysregulated and continue to release inflammatory mediators, thus exacerbating RA progression [16, 17]. This persistent metabolic dysfunction not only impairs the long-term efficacy of pyroptosis-inhibiting strategies but also highlights the critical need to target both pyroptotic cell death and metabolic homeostasis in RA therapy.

In the metabolic network of macrophages, dynamic fluctuations in mitochondrial metabolism and its metabolites are critical for modulating inflammatory macrophage phenotypes [18]. Among these metabolites, itaconate, a downstream metabolite of the mitochondrial tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle, exerts intrinsic anti-inflammatory effects by suppressing the transcription of proinflammatory genes [19, 20]. However, during RA progression, mitochondrial TCA cycle dysfunction leads to a characteristic alteration in intracellular itaconate levels: a transient early elevation followed by a rapid decline [20, 21]. Notably, glucocorticoid-mediated mitochondrial metabolic reprogramming exerts anti-inflammatory effects by increasing intracellular itaconate levels in macrophages [21], directly indicating that the intracellular itaconate metabolic profile is a key determinant of therapeutic outcomes in inflammatory diseases. Given the tight link between metabolic state and inflammation, inhibiting pyroptosis alone yields suboptimal therapeutic effects, as persistent metabolic dysfunction sustains the proinflammatory phenotype of macrophages independently of pyroptotic activity. Thus, a dual-target therapeutic strategy that simultaneously inhibits pyroptosis and restores metabolic homeostasis (e.g., mitochondrial itaconate metabolism) represents a promising approach for profound and durable inflammatory regulation in RA patients.

Despite this compelling rationale, conventional drug delivery systems encounter significant challenges in RA therapy due to the complex pathophysiology of RA, lesion heterogeneity, and persistent inflammatory microenvironment in synovial tissues—all of which impede precise drug accumulation at pathological sites and limit therapeutic efficacy [22]. To overcome these challenges, biomimetic cell membrane coating technology has emerged as an ideal nanocarrier platform for realising this combinatorial therapeutic approach. This technology not only retains the physicochemical properties of the nanoparticle core but also inherits the intrinsic biological functions of the source cell membrane [23]. Notably, macrophage membrane coatings confer unique advantages for RA targeted therapy: as innate inflammatory chemotactic cells, macrophages amounts of express adhesion molecules and chemokine receptors on their membrane surfaces, which endows the coated nanocarriers with directional homing ability to inflamed synovial tissues [24, 25]. Furthermore, the immune camouflage property of macrophage membranes reduces nanocarrier clearance and improves in vivo biocompatibility [26–28]. In addition, folic acid modification can further enhance targeting specificity, as inflammatory macrophages display high folate receptor (FR) expression, while FR expression is extremely low in normal tissues [29]. This distinct expression pattern allows folic acid-modified nanocarriers to specifically target FR-positive inflammatory macrophages via receptor-mediated endocytosis [29].

To overcome the dual challenge of macrophage pyroptosis and metabolic dysfunction in RA, we engineered folic acid-modified macrophage membrane-lipid hybrid nanoparticles (FA-MMLNPs) for the codelivery of GSDME siRNA (siG) and the itaconate derivative 4-octyl itaconate (4-OI) (Fig. 1). Specifically, siG is loaded to precisely silence GSDME expression, thereby effectively inhibiting the GSDME-mediated pyroptosis pathway. In addition, 4-OI replenishes intracellular metabolic substrates, restores mitochondrial itaconate metabolic homeostasis, and polarises proinflammatory macrophages toward an anti-inflammatory, reparative phenotype, thus targeting metabolic dysfunction in RA. By integrating these two synergistic effects, this spatiotemporally coordinated dual-target regulatory strategy overcomes the limitations of single-pathway interventions, thus providing a novel therapeutic paradigm for RA management.

Preparation and optimisation of FA-MMLNP@siG/4OI

To extract macrophage cell membranes, well-conditioned J774A.1 macrophages were first cultured to 80–90% confluency and harvested. The cell pellet was subsequently resuspended in an ice-cold hypotonic lysis buffer (20 mM Tris·HCl (pH 7.5), 10 mM KCl, 75 mM sucrose, 2 mM MgCl₂, and 1× protease/phosphatase inhibitors). The suspension was left to stand on ice for 10 min and then disrupted at 4 °C using an ultrasonic disruptor (100 W, 2 s on/3 s off interval) for 10 min. Subsequently, the lysate was centrifuged at 4 °C and 2000 × g for 25 min to remove cell nuclei and unbroken cell debris. The post-nuclear supernatant (PNS) was collected and further centrifuged at 4 °C and 100,000 × g for 35 min. The resulting pellet was washed twice with ice-cold ddH₂O to remove cytoplasmic protein contaminants, and the final pellet was designated as the macrophage membrane fraction. Finally, the macrophage membrane fraction was resuspended in ddH₂O, and the protein concentration of the membrane suspension was quantified using a BCA protein assay kit. The membrane suspension was aliquoted for immediate use, and the remaining aliquots were stored at − 80 °C to avoid repeated freeze-thawing.

Lipid nanoparticles (LNPs) were prepared using the standard thin-film hydration technique, which includes sequential steps of film formation, hydration, and extrusion through a 200 nm polycarbonate filter membrane [30]. First, 10 mg/mL of lipid materials including 1,2-distearoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphoethanolamine-N-[methoxy(polyethylene glycol)-2000] (DSPE-PEG2000, MW: 2000), hydrogenated soy phosphatidylcholine (HSPC), cholesterol (CHO), 1,2-dioleoyl-3-trimethylammonium-propane (DOTAP), and 1,2-distearoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphoethanolamine-N-[folate(polyethylene glycol)-2000] (DSPE-PEG-FA, MW: 2000) were individually dissolved in chloroform. To prepare folic acid-modified LNPs (FA-LNPs), DSPE-PEG2000, HSPC, CHO, and DOTAP were first mixed at a molar ratio of 12:1:19:14, followed by the addition of DSPE-PEG-FA at a molar ratio of 2% of the total lipids. Subsequently, 5 mL of the above lipid mixture was transferred to a round-bottom flask, and the organic solvent was slowly evaporated under reduced pressure (0.08 MPa) using a rotary evaporator at 37 °C and 100 rpm to form a uniform thin lipid film on the flask wall. Afterwards, deionised water was added to the flask, and the mixture was sonicated in an ice bath (80 W, 2 s on/3 s off interval) for 3 min to hydrate the film and obtain FA-LNPs. Macrophage cell membrane suspension and FA-LNPs were mixed at a protein-to-lipid mass ratio of 1:1 and sonicated in an ice bath (80 W, 2 s on/3 s off interval) for 10 min to prepare hybrid folic acid-modified macrophage membrane-LNPs (FA-MMLNPs). To obtain monodispersed FA-MMLNPs with uniform particle size, the above mixture was extruded 20 times through a 200 nm polycarbonate filter membrane using a liposome extruder at room temperature.

To prepare 4-OI-loaded FA-MMLNPs, 4-OI was incorporated via encapsulation during the rotary evaporation step for thin film fabrication in FA-LNP synthesis. The resulting FA-MMLNP@4OI was then mixed with siG, and the mixture was sonicated at 4 °C (80 W, 10 min, 2 s on/3 s off interval) under ice-bath cooling to minimise thermal damage to membrane proteins and siG and facilitate efficient cargo loading. Unencapsulated 4-OI and free siG were removed by dialysis against deionised water using a dialysis bag at 4 °C for 16 h with three water changes. All postprocessing steps were performed under mild low-temperature conditions to preserve the structural integrity and bioactivity of macrophage membrane proteins. The final dual-loaded nanoparticles (FA-MMLNP@siG/4OI) were collected and stored at 4 °C for subsequent in vitro and in vivo experiments.

Single-cell RNA sequencing data analysis

The experimental data and analytical methods were adopted from a previous study [31]. Dataset HRA003364 (application no.: HREQ008154) was retrieved from the National Genomics Data Center (NGDC, https://ngdc.cncb.ac.cn/) with the permission of the corresponding author.

Nanoparticle characterisation

The particle sizes, polydispersity indices (PDIs), and morphologies of FA-LNP@siG/4OI, MMLNPs (macrophage membrane–LNP hybrid nanoparticles) @siG/4OI, and FA-MMLNP@siG/4OI were analysed by dynamic light scattering (DLS) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM). Zeta potential was further measured for surface electrical property characterisation, with DLS and zeta potential tests performed on a Malvern Zetasizer Pro (Malvern Panalytical, Worcestershire, UK) and TEM observed on a JSM-6510 LV (JEOL, Tokyo, Japan). The encapsulation efficiency (EE) and drug loading capacity (DLC) of 4-OI were determined by high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC, Agilent 1260 Infinity, Agilent Technologies, CA, USA) on a C18 column (250 × 4.6 mm, 5 μm) with a mobile phase of methanol:0.1% formic acid water (70:30, v/v) at a flow rate of 1.0 mL/min and column temperature of 30 °C, with the detection wavelength set at 210 nm. The EE and LC of the siRNA were measured using an ultraviolet (UV) spectrophotometer (UV-2600, Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan) at the characteristic detection wavelength of 260 nm for nucleic acids.

Cellular uptake study

J774A.1 cells were seeded into 24-well plates at a density of 4 × 10⁴ cells per well and cultured for 48 h in the absence or presence of 1 µg/mL LPS. The cells were subsequently treated with 50 µg/mL DiO-labelled LNP@siG/4OI, FA-LNP@siG/4OI, MMLNP@siG/4OI, and FA-MMLNP@siG/4OI for 2 h, then washed three times with PBS. The cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) in PBS for 20 min and stained with 5 µg/mL DAPI for 10 min at room temperature in the dark. Cellular internalisation of nanoparticles was visualised using a fluorescence inverted microscope (IX73, Olympus, Tokyo, Japan).

Endosomal escape

J774A.1 cells were seeded onto glass coverslips in 24-well plates at a density of 2 × 10⁵ cells per well and incubated for 12–16 h at 37 °C with 5% CO₂. The cells were then incubated with 100 µg/mL FAM siRNA-loaded FA-MMLNPs for different time points. After incubation, the cells were washed three times with pre-cooled PBS (pH 7.4) and incubated with 50 nM LysoTracker Red (Beyotime, China) for 1 h at 37 °C in the dark. The cells were washed another three times with pre-cooled PBS to remove unbound probe, and the colocalisation of FAM siRNA and lysosomes was finally observed using a confocal laser-scanning microscope (LSM 880, Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany).

In vitro release study

The release profiles of siG and 4-OI from FA-MMLNP@siG/4OI were evaluated using a dialysis bag method [32]. Briefly, 1 mL of nanoparticle suspension was sealed in a dialysis bag (MWCO 10 kDa) and immersed in 20 mL of release medium at 37 °C with shaking (100 rpm). Two media were used: pH 7.4 PBS (simulating physiological conditions) and pH 5.0 acetate buffer (simulating endosomal/lysosomal acidity). At predetermined time points (0, 1, 2, 4, 8, 12, 24, 36 and 48 h), 1 mL of release medium was collected and replaced with fresh medium. The amount of 4-OI released was quantified by HPLC with acetonitrile/water (70:30, v/v) as the mobile phase (1.0 mL/min, detection at 210 nm). The amount of siG released was determined by measuring the fluorescence of Cy3-labelled siG using a microplate reader. All experiments were performed in triplicate. Cumulative release was calculated as follows: Cumulative release (%) = (amount released at time t/total loaded) × 100%.

Cellular treatment

J774A.1 cells were seeded in 6-well culture plates and cultured to 80–90% confluency at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO₂ for subsequent treatments. For pyroptosis induction, cells were cotreated with 20 ng/mL TNF-α and 10 µg/mL cycloheximide (CHX) for 6 h. For the sole inflammatory response assay, cells were treated with 1 µg/mL LPS for 24 h. After all treatments, the cells were washed three times with PBS to wash away free drugs and nanoparticles completely.

LDH and cytokine expression measurements

LDH release and proinflammatory cytokine (IL-1β, TNF-α, IL-6) secretion were detected using an LDH Cytotoxicity Assay Kit (Beyotime, China, C0016) and ELISA Kits (Multi Sciences Biotech, China, IL-1β: EK201B; TNF-α: EK282HS; IL-6: EK206), respectively, following the manufacturers’ instructions. J774A.1 cells were seeded into 96-well plates and subjected to the cellular treatments. Cell culture supernatants were collected and centrifuged at 4 °C, 1000×g for 5 min to remove cell debris.

Real-time quantitative PCR

Total RNA was extracted from J774A.1 cells using RNA Isolation Kit (Beyotime, China, R0017M) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The concentration and purity of total RNA were determined using a Nanodrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher, USA). A total of 2 µg of qualified total RNA was reverse-transcribed into cDNA using an iScript cDNA Synthesis Kit (Bio-Rad, USA, 1708891). qRT-PCR was performed in a reaction system containing cDNA, SYBR Premix Ex Taq (TaKaRa, Japan, RR420A), and gene-specific forward/reverse primers using a QuantStudio 6 Flex Real-Time PCR System (Thermo Fisher, USA). Table S1 presents the detailed primers designated for RT‑qPCR analysis.

Western blot assay

Cells were lysed with prechilled lysis buffer supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors, with all subsequent steps performed on ice to avoid protease‑mediated degradation. Following centrifugation, the supernatant was harvested, and protein concentration was quantified using a BCA assay kit (Beyotime, China). Protein samples were then denatured at 100 °C for 10 min before undergoing electrophoresis and wet transfer. The membrane was blocked in skimmed milk for 2 h and incubated overnight with primary antibodies. After treatment with secondary antibodies, target proteins were visualised accordingly.

Annexin V-mCherry/SYTOX Green assay

As previously described, an Annexin V-mCherry/SYTOX Green (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) dual-staining assay was performed to evaluate pyroptosis. Briefly, cells were seeded into 6-well plates or confocal dishes, followed by pretreatment with the designated drugs overnight. After the cells were incubated with 20 ng/mL TNF-α and 10 µg/mL CHX for 6 h, Annexin V-mCherry binding buffer, along with Annexin V-mCherry and SYTOX Green, was added. Following incubation at 20 °C for 20 min in the dark, the cells were washed and imaged using a confocal microscope (Leica, Wetzlar, Germany) to determine their fluorescence levels. Positive cells were identified by colocalisation with Annexin V-mCherry and SYTOX Green.

Intracellular ROS measurement and ATP assay

Intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels were detected using a DCFH-DA ROS Assay Kit (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) following the manufacturer’s instructions. For J774A.1 adherent cells seeded in 6-well plates, the cells were first treated with various formulations (stimulation time > 6 h), after which the culture medium was aspirated for probe loading. DCFH-DA was diluted 1:1000 with PBS to a final concentration of 10 µM, and 1 mL of the diluted probe was added to each well to fully cover the cells. The cells were incubated at 37 °C for 20 min, followed by three washes with PBS to remove uninternalised DCFH-DA. After cell collection, the fluorescence intensity was detected by flow cytometry at an excitation wavelength of 488 nm and an emission wavelength of 525 nm. The ROS levels are expressed as the mean fluorescence intensity (MFI).

ATP content was quantified using an Enhanced ATP Assay Kit (Cat. No. S0027, Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China), following the manufacturer’s recommended protocol. ATP concentrations were normalised to the untreated control group and expressed as relative percentage.

Mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm) assay

The mitochondrial membrane potential was determined using an Enhanced Mitochondrial Membrane Potential Assay Kit with JC-1 (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. J774A.1 adherent cells seeded in 6-well plates were treated with various formulations as indicated, after which the culture medium was aspirated, and the cells were incubated with the kit-provided JC-1 staining working solution at 37 °C for 20 min. After incubation, the cells were washed twice with PBS, harvested, and subjected to flow cytometry analysis. The fluorescence signals were detected at the respective excitation/emission wavelengths: JC-1 monomer (green fluorescence), 514 nm/529 nm; and JC-1 aggregates (red fluorescence), 585 nm/590 nm. The mitochondrial membrane potential is expressed as the ratio of red to green fluorescence intensity.

Biosafety assay

J774A.1 cells were seeded in 96-well plates and exposed to various concentrations of 4-OI or NPs for 48 h. After incubation, cell viability was determined using the CCK-8 assay. Additionally, to comprehensively evaluate the biocompatibility and in vivo toxicity of the NPs, an in vitro RBC haemolysis assay was first performed to assess the blood compatibility of the NPs. For in vivo toxicity assessment, the indicated drugs were intravenously injected into the tail veins of DBA/1J mice, with four injections administered at 1-day intervals; the control group received an equal volume of PBS. After the final injection, blood samples and major organs (liver, kidney, lung, heart, and spleen) were collected immediately. For blood sample analysis, whole blood was used for blood routine analysis, and the remaining blood was centrifuged to separate serum for serum biochemical analysis to evaluate systemic metabolic and haematological changes. The collected major organs were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde solution, embedded in paraffin, and cut into 4-µm-thick sections. The tissue sections were stained with haematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and observed under an optical microscope to assess histological alterations associated with NP treatment.

Accumulation of nanoparticles in arthritic joints

Various nanoparticles were labelled with the hydrophobic infrared fluorescent dye DiR and administered intravenously via the tail vein into collagen antibody–induced arthritis (CAIA) mice. At 6, 12, and 24 h post-injection, the mice were anaesthetised with 3% isoflurane gas, and in vivo fluorescence imaging was performed using an IVIS Spectrum system (Caliper, Hopkinton, MA, USA). At 24 h postadministration, the mice were euthanised, and major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lungs, kidneys) and inflamed, arthritis-affected joints were immediately isolated for ex vivo fluorescence analysis. Fluorescence intensity was subsequently quantified using ImageJ.

Treatment of CAIA mice

The CAIA model was established according to a previously reported method [33]. Briefly, on Day 0 of the experimental procedure, the primary immunisation was carried out by intraperitoneal injection of 1.5 mg of mouse anti-type II collagen 5-clone monoclonal antibody cocktail, followed by a booster immunisation with 50 µg of LPS via intraperitoneal injection on Day 3. On Days 2, 4, 5, and 7 after antibody injection, 100 µL of PBS or a solution containing various types of drugs (FA-MMLNPs, FA-MMLNP@siG, 4-OI, FA-MMLNP@4OI, or FA-MMLNP@siG/4OI, 1 mg/mL) was injected via the tail vein, and the mice were sacrificed on Day 9. Healthy mice without arthritis induction or drug treatment served as the control group. The degree of ankle swelling and body weight changes of the mice were monitored throughout the study.

Micro-CT

Micro-CT images were used to evaluate the extents of knee joint damage and bone erosion in response to various treatment interventions using a micro-CT device (SKYSCAN 1278, Bruker). The collected data were processed to generate three-dimensional structures of the joints (CTAN 3D image analysis). The bone volume density was analysed using DATAVIEWER.

Histological analysis of the knee joint

After the mice were sacrificed, the right posterior knee joints were carefully collected and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde solution overnight. The right posterior knees were decalcified using a 10% EDTA solution for 50 days. Following decalcification, the knee joints were embedded in paraffin and cut into 4-µm-thick sections. To visualise the tissue morphology, the sections were stained using HE and safranin O staining techniques. Histological images were acquired using a microscope (VS200, Olympus, Japan). The histological changes related to ankle synovitis inflammation and cartilage destruction were evaluated using the Histological Synovitis Score (HSS) and the Modified Osteoarthritis Research Society International (OARSI) scoring standards, respectively [34]. These scoring systems quantitatively assess the degree of synovial inflammation and cartilage damage observed in histological sections. During the histological analysis, the researchers were blinded to the group assignments.

Immunofluorescence staining

Cells or sections were washed with PBS, permeabilised with 0.2% Triton X-100 for 20 min, and blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin at 37 °C for 1 h. Subsequently, the sections were incubated with the primary antibody at 4 °C overnight, followed by incubation with a mixture of fluorescently labelled secondary antibodies at room temperature for 1 h. The cell nuclei were counterstained with DAPI for 15 min. The staining results were examined under a confocal microscope and quantified using ImageJ.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed in triplicate, and data are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). The data were analysed using SPSS (version 23.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA), and graphical representation was conducted using GraphPad Prism (version 10.6.1; GraphPad Software, Inc., San Diego, CA, USA). Student’s t-tests, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), and Tukey’s multiple comparison test were used where appropriate. Statistical significance was indicated as *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, and ****p < 0.0001.

Results and discussion

Macrophage pyroptosis and metabolic dysfunction form a dual barrier to effective RA therapy

As a chronic, progressive autoimmune disease, RA causes severe pain and impairs the quality of life in patients [3]. Despite current clinical therapies (including DMARDs and biologics), a large proportion of patients still show suboptimal treatment responses and poor disease remission rates, and face challenges such as adverse drug reactions and disease recurrence [4, 5]. This unmet clinical need highlights the urgency of exploring more effective therapeutic strategies. Given the central roles of multiple cell populations in RA pathogenesis, cell-targeted therapies hold particular promise [35, 36]. Among these cell types, monocyte-derived macrophages are especially critical: they present antigens, activate autoreactive T cells, infiltrate synovial tissues, differentiate into inflammatory macrophages, secrete cytokines, and ultimately drive osteoclast formation, leading to joint destruction [37, 38]. Single-cell analysis revealed a slight, statistically insignificant increase in the monocyte‒macrophage ratio in RA patients (18.1%) compared with healthy individuals (16.9%) (Figs. 2A–C). Although these numerical changes were minor, GO analysis revealed significant enrichment and robust activation of the pyroptosis pathway in RA monocyte‒macrophage subsets (Fig. 2D). Pyroptosis, a proinflammatory form of programmed cell death, triggers rapid cell lysis, massive inflammatory cytokine release, and consequent reduction in cell numbers [39]. However, the paradoxical expansion of the macrophage compartment in RA suggests that pyroptosis alone does not determine macrophage dynamics. These findings collectively indicate a more complex regulatory network governed by cell fate decisions and microenvironmental signals.

Given the above analysis of complex changes in the RA mononuclear macrophage population, it is critical to elucidate the phenotypic and functional heterogeneity of macrophages within the RA inflammatory microenvironment in depth. Macrophages exhibit significant heterogeneity in inflamed joints, with distinct phenotypes playing different roles in RA progression. Macrophages activated in inflamed joints—termed proinflammatory macrophages—drive disease progression through the secretion of large amounts of proinflammatory cytokines (e.g., TNF-α and IL-1β), whereas those mediating anti-inflammatory effects and tissue repair are referred to as anti-inflammatory subsets [40, 41]. To further elucidate macrophage heterogeneity, we stratified proinflammatory and anti-inflammatory macrophages using characteristic molecules (Fig. 2E). GO analysis showed that proinflammatory macrophages in the RA samples activated NF-κB and TNF-α signalling (pathways associated with inflammation and bone destruction) and the osteoclast differentiation pathway (Fig. 2G). Notably, NF-κB signalling is a key determinant of macrophage polarisation, and its blockade shifts macrophages towards an anti-inflammatory phenotype to reduce inflammation [42]. Mechanistically, itaconate (a downstream metabolite of the IRG1 gene) inhibits NF-κB signalling, interferes with proinflammatory gene transcription, and thereby exerts anti-inflammatory and tissue-protective effects [19, 43, 44]. Intriguingly, KEGG enrichment analysis revealed that IRG1 was significantly upregulated in proinflammatory macrophages but relatively weakly expressed in anti-inflammatory subsets (Fig. 2F). Previous studies have demonstrated that although inflammatory stimulation induces high IRG1 expression in macrophages, intracellular itaconate levels are markedly decreased due to impaired mitochondrial TCA cycle activity and enhanced aerobic glycolysis [21]. Supplementation with itaconate analogues (dimethyl itaconate or 4-OI) effectively suppresses proinflammatory cytokine production [21]. Collectively, these findings underscore that the metabolic profile of macrophages is a key determinant of their inflammatory functions.

Previous studies have confirmed that inhibiting pyroptosis can alleviate diverse inflammatory disorders [12, 15]. However, blocking pyroptosis alone without rectifying metabolic reprogramming defects traps rescued macrophages in a proinflammatory state, which perpetually secrete proinflammatory cytokines and accelerate RA progression (Fig. 2H). Therefore, in the design of RA therapeutic strategies, it is imperative to develop a comprehensive approach that simultaneously inhibits pyroptosis and reverses macrophage metabolic reprogramming defects, thus disrupting the inflammatory cycle and achieving effective disease control.

Construction and characterisation of FA-MMLNP@siG/4OI

To surmount the “dual dilemma” of pyroptosis and metabolic abnormalities, we fabricated FA-MMLNP@siG/4OI for the codelivery of siG and 4-OI, as illustrated in Fig. 3A. For nanoparticle characterisation, TEM was first performed, revealing that FA-MMLNP@siG/4OI, FA-LNP@siG/4OI, and MMLNP@siG/4OI all displayed similar vesicular morphologies after extrusion through 200 nm membranes (Fig. 3B). DLS further confirmed that these formulations exhibited comparable, homogeneous particle size distributions (Figs. 3D–E). Notably, FA-MMLNP@siG/4OI had an average diameter of 174.40 ± 1.70 nm and a narrow PDI of 0.13 ± 0.00, indicating its appropriate size and high uniformity as a nanocarrier—a critical feature for reliable biological performance. Its zeta potential of − 16.15 ± 0.338 mV was intermediate between the other two formulations (Fig. 3F), conferring optimal colloidal stability and cell interaction properties.

To verify the successful fabrication of the hybrid nanostructure, lipid bilayers and macrophage membranes were fluorescently labelled with DiI and DiR, respectively. After dialysis and membrane hybridisation, fluorescence microscopy revealed intense colocalisation of the two fluorescent signals (Fig. 3G), providing intuitive evidence for successful membrane fusion. Subsequent SDS-PAGE protein profiling confirmed that FA-MMLNP@siG/4OI inherited the protein components from macrophage membranes (Fig. 3C), which was consistent with the successful integration of synthetic lipid bilayers and natural macrophage membranes. In addition, there were no significant differences in the relative expression levels of CCR2 and CD44 on FA-MMLNP@siG/4OI before and after drug loading, indicating that the FA-MMLNP@siG/4OI fabrication process well preserved key functional proteins on the macrophage membrane surface and retained membrane-derived chemotactic and adhesive properties (Fig. S1). To validate the coencapsulation capacity, DiI-labelled FA-MMLNPs was loaded with Cy3-labelled siRNA and ICG. After dialysis to eliminate free unencapsulated agents, the highly overlapping fluorescence signals unambiguously confirmed that the nanoparticles could efficiently coencapsulate both siRNA and small-molecular drugs within a single nanostructure (Fig. 3H). Furthermore, FA-MMLNPs exhibited high EE and DLC: for 4-OI, the EE and DLC were 82.63 ± 0.59% and 4.80 ± 0.04%, respectively, whereas for siG, the EE and DLC reached 86.60 ± 0.63% and 0.18 ± 0.003%, respectively (Fig. S2). Such high encapsulation performance guarantees sufficient intracellular delivery of both therapeutic agents.

Prior to evaluating the biocompatibility of the nanoparticles, CCK-8 assays indicated that the safe concentration of free 4-OI in J774A.1 cells was below 200 µM (Fig. S3). Based on this safe window, we optimised the 4-OI loading content in FA-MMLNPs to match the high encapsulation capacity determined earlier. We then assessed the cytotoxicity of FA-MMLNP, FA-MMLNP@4OI, FA-MMLNP@siG, and FA-MMLNP@siG/4OI at concentrations ranging from 50 to 200 µg/mL in J774A.1 cells after 24 h incubation. The results showed that cell viability remained above 90% at all tested concentrations, indicating no obvious cytotoxicity (Fig. S4). Importantly, the actual 4-OI exposure derived from the nanoparticles was within the predetermined safe concentration range.

To evaluate the endosomal escape of siRNA during FA-MMLNP-mediated delivery, lysosomal tracking assays were performed using LysoTracker Red to label endo/lysosomes. The results revealed that FAM-labelled siRNA underwent efficient endosomal escape within 30 min of administration, as indicated by the distinct separation between FAM-siRNA fluorescence and lysosomal staining signals (Fig. 3I). To further validate the inflammation-responsive targeting property, we next assessed cellular uptake by labeling various nanoparticles with DiO for visualisation and quantification. Compared with unstimulated cells, cellular uptake of FA-MMLNP@siG/4OI was significantly elevated in cells stimulated with LPS (Figs. 3J–K). In addition, uptake of FA-LNP@siG/4OI, MMLNP@siG/4OI, and FA-MMLNP@siG/4OI by LPS-activated macrophages was markedly higher than that of unmodified LNP@siG/4OI. Notably, FA-MMLNP@siG/4OI exhibited the highest uptake efficiency, at more than threefold that of either FA-LNP@siG/4OI or MMLNP@siG/4OI. (Fig. 3K). This remarkably enhanced and selective uptake in the inflammatory microenvironment can be ascribed to the synergistic targeting effect of FA for folate receptors and macrophage membrane for homotypic targeting to inflammatory macrophages.

Collectively, these results demonstrate that FA-MMLNP@siG/4OI exhibit favorable physicochemical properties, high biocompatibility, efficient siRNA endosomal escape, and enhanced inflamed microenvironment targeting. These advantages highlight their potential as a targeted therapeutic platform for RA therapy.

Vitro release profiles of siG and 4-OI from FA-MMLNP@siG/4OI

To evaluate the coordinated release behaviour of the dual therapeutic payloads, in vitro release kinetics of siG and 4-OI from FA-MMLNP@siG/4OI were investigated at 37 °C under simulated physiological conditions (pH 7.4) and acidic endo/lysosomal conditions (pH 5.0). Under neutral pH, siG and 4-OI exhibited slow and sustained release, with cumulative release rates of only 30.35 ± 0.91% and 45.13 ± 0.92% at 24 h, respectively (Fig. S5A). In sharp contrast, under acidic conditions, both siG and 4-OI were released rapidly, with cumulative release exceeding 50% within 2 h, and reaching release plateaus of 89.02 ± 0.89% and 86.63 ± 1.42% at 36 h, respectively (Fig. S5B). Notably, no significant differences in the release kinetics between siG and 4-OI were observed after 2 h (p > 0.05) under acidic conditions (Fig. S5B), confirming that the two agents maintained synchronous release after the initial burst release phase. This pH-responsive and synchronous release behavior is highly consistent with the acidic microenvironment of endo/lysosomes, facilitating on-demand intracellular release of both therapeutics and laying a foundation for their synergistic therapeutic effects.

Superior inhibition of macrophage pyroptosis by FA-MMLNP@siG/4OI

To systematically evaluate the in vitro inhibitory effects of these formulations on pyroptosis, J774A.1 cells were pretreated with various formulations, followed by stimulation with TNF-α and CHX to induce pyroptosis. As illustrated in Fig. 4A, FA-MMLNP@siG/4OI was designed to suppress GSDME expression via the release of siG. The simultaneously released 4-OI, acting as a potent itaconate derivative, suppresses upstream inflammatory signalling and inflammasome activity to indirectly attenuate caspase‑3 activation and the generation of cleaved caspase‑3, thus jointly blocking macrophage pyroptosis [45]. This strategy leverages the central role of GSDME in pyroptotic cell death: upon cleavage by cleaved caspase‑3, the resulting GSDME‑N triggers plasma membrane pore formation, leading to cell lysis and the release of proinflammatory mediators [7, 8].

To validate the bioactivity of FA-MMLNP@siG/4OI, we performed Western blot analysis to measure the protein levels of GSDME-FL, GSDME-N, and cleaved caspase-3 (Fig. 4B), followed by densitometric quantification of these proteins. TNF-α/CHX stimulation significantly elevated the levels of cleaved caspase-3 and GSDME-N (Fig. 4D–E). Concomitantly with the generation of GSDME-N, total GSDME levels were markedly decreased (Fig. 4C), confirming that inflammatory stimuli induce caspase-3 activation and subsequent GSDME cleavage. Empty FA-MMLNPs showed no significant effects on these proteins. FA-MMLNP@siG reduced total GSDME levels by approximately 40% relative to the TNF-α/CHX-stimulated group, with a corresponding approximately 40% decrease in GSDME-N, demonstrating that FA-MMLNPs efficiently delivers siG to suppress GSDME expression and the generation of its cleaved active form GSDME-N. Furthermore, both free 4-OI and FA-MMLNP@4OI decreased cleaved caspase-3 levels to different extents, whereas FA-MMLNP@4OI led to a significantly greater reduction in GSDME-N than free 4-OI. This result indicates that FA-MMLNP-mediated 4-OI delivery facilitates caspase-3 inhibition and subsequent GSDME cleavage blockade. The superior efficacy of FA-MMLNP@4OI over free 4-OI was attributed to the enhanced intracellular delivery efficiency of FA-MMLNPs, which promoted targeted cellular uptake, reduced extracellular drug loss, and elevated the effective intracellular concentration of 4-OI, thus led to more potent inhibition of caspase-3 activation [46]. Notably, FA-MMLNP@siG/4OI achieved the most potent reduction in GSDME-N levels to approximately 30% that of the pyroptotic cell group, which was attributed to its synergistic effect through simultaneously suppressing GSDME expression and inhibiting caspase-3 activation.

To confirm the functional outcome of this synergistic molecular regulation on GSDME-mediated pyroptosis, we measured LDH release as a sensitive indicator of plasma membrane damage and lysis. Empty FA-MMLNPs showed no effect on LDH release. Compared with the TNF-α/CHX-stimulated group, cells coincubated with FA-MMLNP@siG/4OI showed a significant decrease of approximately 75% in LDH release (Fig. S6A). As a stable cytoplasmic enzyme, LDH release directly reflects plasma membrane integrity; the reduced LDH release verified that FA-MMLNP@siG/4OI effectively preserved membrane integrity. Notably, this inhibitory effect was stronger than that of FA-MMLNP@4OI or FA-MMLNP@siG alone, further validating the synergistic action of codelivered siG and 4-OI against pyroptotic cell lysis.

To morphologically confirm the inhibitory effect of FA-MMLNP@siG/4OI on pyroptosis, we observed the characteristic morphological hallmarks of pyroptosis—including cell swelling and large balloon-like vesicle formation prior to membrane rupture [6]—and performed Annexin V‑mCherry/SYTOX Green double staining to quantify pyroptotic cells. This assay identifies two key features of pyroptosis: phosphatidylserine externalisation (Annexin V‑mCherry) and loss of plasma membrane integrity (SYTOX Green nuclear staining) [47]. Empty FA-MMLNPs showed no obvious effect on pyroptotic morphology or fluorescence intensity. Confocal imaging showed that TNF‑α/CHX‑stimulated J774A.1 cells exhibited numerous balloon‑like vesicles and intense overlapping red/green fluorescence, indicative of extensive pyroptosis (Fig. 4F). In contrast, cells co‑incubated with FA-MMLNP@siG/4OI displayed markedly reduced vesicle formation and fewer double‑positive pyroptotic cells. Quantitative analysis revealed that the MFIs of both fluorescent probes were reduced by more than 50% (Figs. 4G–H). Moreover, this morphological protection was more pronounced than that of FA-MMLNP@siG or FA-MMLNP@4OI alone.

Given that pyroptosis is intimately linked to the release of proinflammatory mediators, ELISA was conducted to quantify the level of IL-1β. Compared with control macrophages, TNF-α/CHX-stimulated pyroptotic macrophages exhibited significantly increased IL-1β expression. By contrast, coincubation with FA-MMLNP@siG/4OI reduced IL-1β expression to approximately one-fifth of that in the pyroptotic cell group, and this inhibitory effect was significantly stronger than those of FA-MMLNP@4OI and FA-MMLNP@siG alone (Fig. S6B).

Collectively, these data demonstrate that FA-MMLNP@siG/4OI synergistically and potently inhibits macrophage pyroptosis in vitro via dual suppression of GSDME expression and caspase‑3‑dependent GSDME cleavage.

Reprogramming of inflammatory macrophages by FA-MMLNP@siG/4OI

As previously described, FA-MMLNP@siG/4OI effectively inhibited macrophage pyroptosis under a highly inflammatory state. However, mere inhibition of pyroptosis is insufficient to fully reverse inflammation. Rescued macrophages still exhibit a proinflammatory phenotype due to persistent metabolic defects, and their accumulation can further exacerbate inflammatory responses [48, 49]. To address this metabolic bottleneck, FA-MMLNP@siG/4OI was designed to restore mitochondrial TCA cycle activity by releasing 4-OI and exogenously supplementing intracellular itaconate. By delivering exogenous 4-OI to compensate for deficient endogenous itaconate and rescue TCA cycle flux, FA-MMLNP@siG/4OI fundamentally reverses the proinflammatory metabolic phenotype of macrophages, thereby driving their polarisation toward an anti-inflammatory phenotype (Fig. 5A).

To evaluate mitochondrial functional recovery, we assessed intracellular ATP levels, ROS generation, and ΔΨm, which serve as key hallmarks of mitochondrial energy production, oxidative stress, and membrane potential homeostasis. Inflammatory stimulation led to a drastic decrease in intracellular ATP levels (Fig. S7A) and MFI of JC-1 red fluorescence (Figs. 5B and E), as well as a marked increase in intracellular ROS levels (Figs. 5C and F) in LPS-stimulated cells, indicating severe mitochondrial dysfunction. FA-MMLNPs and FA-MMLNP@siG exerted no significant improvement in these parameters. Free 4-OI only moderately alleviated mitochondrial dysfunction and was significantly less effective than nano-formulated 4-OI groups. Meanwhile, FA-MMLNP@4OI and FA-MMLNP@siG/4OI presented comparable efficacy. Both formulations effectively restored intracellular ATP levels to approximately 90% of those in the normal group, returned the ΔΨm-associated MFI of red fluorescence to the level of the normal group, and scavenged excessive intracellular ROS to approximately one-tenth of the level in the LPS group, indicating the effective restoration of mitochondrial function.

The expression levels of iNOS (a canonical marker for proinflammatory macrophages) and Arg-1 (a key marker for anti-inflammatory macrophages) are well-established indicators of macrophage polarisation. To assess the effects of metabolic restoration and phenotypic reprogramming on macrophage activation, we performed immunofluorescence staining. Upon LPS stimulation, J774A.1 cells became polarised toward a proinflammatory phenotype, accompanied by sharply elevated iNOS fluorescence (Fig. S7B) and distinctly reduced Arg-1 fluorescence (Fig. S7C). However, treatment with FA-MMLNP@4OI and FA-MMLNP@siG/4OI reversed this trend: it decreased iNOS expression and upregulated Arg-1 to normal level, indicating the reprogramming of macrophages toward an anti-inflammatory phenotype.

Nuclear translocation of p65 serves as the core hallmark of NF-κB pathway activation and directly governs the transcription of downstream proinflammatory genes, as p65 functions as a critical regulator of proinflammatory responses. LPS stimulation triggered rapid nuclear translocation of red fluorescence-labelled p65, resulting in a marked accumulation of nuclear p65 (Fig. 5D). In contrast, in unstimulated control cells, the NF-κB p65 subunit was predominantly distributed in the cytoplasm with negligible nuclear localisation. Among the intervention groups, FA-MMLNPs and FA-MMLNP@siG exerted no significant effect on p65 translocation under LPS stimulation. Free 4-OI, however, only partially inhibited this process, with a substantial fraction of the red fluorescent p65 still retained in the nuclei. Notably, FA-MMLNP@4OI and FA-MMLNP@siG/4OI significantly suppressed LPS‑induced nuclear translocation of p65, and the staining pattern of p65 was comparable to that observed in the control group.

At the transcriptional level, RT-qPCR was performed to examine the mRNA levels of proinflammatory M1 macrophage markers, including COX-2, iNOS, and TNF-α. Compared with the FA-MMLNPs and FA-MMLNP@siG groups, FA-MMLNP@4OI and FA-MMLNP@siG/4OI significantly downregulated these genes (Figs. 5G–I). In addition, ELISA was conducted to quantify the secretion of key downstream proinflammatory cytokines IL-6 and TNF-α. FA-MMLNP@4OI and FA-MMLNP@siG/4OI significantly reduced extracellular IL-6 and TNF-α secretion by approximately 50% relative to the LPS-stimulated group (Figs. 5J–K). Together, these results demonstrate that treatment with FA-MMLNP@siG/4OI effectively alleviates the inflammatory response.

Overall, FA-MMLNP@siG/4OI reprograms inflammatory macrophages to an anti-inflammatory phenotype by rectifying itaconate metabolic dysregulation and restoring mitochondrial function, which further suppresses NF-κB p65 nuclear translocation, proinflammatory gene expression, and cytokine secretion.

Biocompatibility and arthritis joint targeting of FA-MMLNP@siG/4OI in vivo

Having confirmed the therapeutic effects of FA-MMLNP@siG/4OI at the cellular level, we next investigated its in vivo performance, with a focus on systemic biocompatibility and inflamed joint-targeting ability after intravenous administration—two key prerequisites for the clinical translation of nanocarriers.

Given the critical importance of in vivo safety for nanocarrier translation [50], we first assessed blood compatibility and organ toxicity in mice treated with different formulations. The nanoparticles exhibited no significant hemolytic activity (hemolysis rate < 5%) at all tested concentrations (Table S2), and hematological parameters showed no significant differences compared with the control group (Table S3), demonstrating favorable blood compatibility. Furthermore, no obvious hepatotoxicity or nephrotoxicity was observed, as all measured biochemical parameters, including alanine transaminase (ALT), aspartate transaminase (AST), blood urea nitrogen (BUN) and creatinine (CREA), also presented no significant differences relative to the control group (Table S3). Additionally, H&E staining-based histopathological examination of the heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys revealed normal tissue architecture without obvious cellular degeneration, necrosis, inflammatory cell infiltration, congestion, edema, or other organ damage (Fig. 6A), further confirming the excellent systemic biocompatibility and biosafety of FA-MMLNP@siG/4OI in vivo. This favorable safety profile provides a solid basis for subsequent repeated administration and long-term treatment in arthritis models.

After validating the robust systemic safety of FA-MMLNP@siG/4OI, we then examined its targeted delivery capacity in CAIA mice. Following successful model induction, DiR-labelled FA-LNP@siG/4OI, MMLNP@siG/4OI, and FA-MMLNP@siG/4OI were intravenously injected into arthritic mice, and fluorescence signals were monitored by in vivo optical imaging. Time-course analysis showed strong fluorescence signals in the inflamed limbs of FA-MMLNP@siG/4OI-treated mice as early as 12 h postadministration, with signal intensity peaking at 24 h (Fig. 6B). In contrast, only weak or negligible fluorescence signals were detected in the inflamed joints of mice injected with FA-LNP@siG/4OI or MMLNP@ siG/4OI at 24 h postadministration (Fig. 6B), clearly demonstrating the superior inflamed joint-targeting capacity of FA-MMLNP@siG/4OI. To further verify targeting specificity, major organs and arthritic joints were collected at 24 h postadministration for ex vivo fluorescence analysis. While the three formulations exhibited comparable fluorescence intensities in major visceral organs (Fig. 6C), FA-MMLNP@siG/4OI showed the highest ratio of fluorescence intensity in inflamed joints to that in the liver (Fig. 6D), thereby confirming its superior targeting specificity to inflamed joints.

Taken together, these in vivo results demonstrate that FA-MMLNP@siG/4OI possesses outstanding systemic biocompatibility without obvious blood or organ toxicity, while achieving enhanced and specific targeting to inflamed joints in the CAIA mouse model.

FA-MMLNP@siG/4OI effectively ameliorates arthritis progression and preserves joint integrity in CAIA mice

Building on the confirmed biocompatibility and inflamed joint-targeting ability of FA-MMLNP@siG/4OI, we further performed an in vivo study to evaluate its therapeutic efficacy in CAIA mice—a model that faithfully recapitulates key pathological features of human RA, including rapid synovitis, bone erosion and cartilage destruction [51]. Naive mice without CAIA induction served as normal controls, and successfully induced mice were randomised into six treatment groups: PBS, FA-MMLNP, free 4-OI, FA-MMLNP@4OI, FA-MMLNP@siG, and FA-MMLNP@siG/4OI. All formulations were administered intravenously on Days 2, 4, 5, and 7 after model induction. Body weight and ankle thickness were monitored from Day 0 to Day 9, and all mice were sacrificed on Day 9 for structural and pathological analyses (Fig. 7A).

PBS-treated CAIA mice developed progressive weight loss and ankle swelling, consistent with the typical RA pathological profile. In comparison, free 4-OI or single-agent nanoparticles (FA-MMLNP@4OI, FA-MMLNP@siG) only partially alleviated these abnormalities. Notably, on Day 9, FA-MMLNP@siG/4OI significantly reduced weight loss by 33.33 ± 0.05% and joint swelling by 47.31 ± 3.39% relative to the PBS group, exceeding the efficacy of all single-component treatments (Figs. 7B–C). Macroscopic joint examination further confirmed obvious reductions in swelling and deformity (Fig. 7D), in line with the quantitative ankle thickness data. We next assessed joint structural protection using Micro-CT and histological staining. Micro-CT of the right hind knee showed that FA-MMLNP@siG/4OI markedly mitigated bone surface erosion (Fig. 7E), with BV/TV restored to nearly 90% of normal levels (Fig. 7H).

As sustained synovial inflammation drives cartilage and bone destruction in RA, we evaluated synovial and cartilage pathology. H&E staining revealed that this formulation strongly suppressed synovial hyperplasia and inflammatory cell infiltration compared with other treatments (Fig. 7F), with corresponding histological scores reduced by approximately 75% (Fig. 7I). Articular cartilage was also remarkably preserved: while other groups displayed moderate-to-severe cartilage degradation, FA-MMLNP@siG/4OI-treated mice showed only mild damage (Fig. 7G), with scores decreased to approximately 27% of those in untreated arthritic mice (Fig. 7J).

Together, macroscopic, structural and histopathological analyses consistently demonstrate the strong synergistic therapeutic effect of FA-MMLNP@siG/4OI in CAIA mice. By targeting synovial inflammation—the primary driver of RA joint damage—this system effectively reduced bone loss, cartilage degradation and arthritic symptoms.

FA-MMLNP@siG/4OI regulates macrophage pyroptosis and metabolism in vivo

To elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying the in vivo therapeutic efficacy of FA-MMLNP@siG/4OI against joint inflammation, we examined its ability to suppress macrophage pyroptosis and restore mitochondrial metabolic homeostasis in the synovial microenvironment of arthritic joints. Immunofluorescence staining was first used to detect GSDME-N expression in synovial tissues of CAIA mice. Abundant GSDME-N was observed in PBS-treated CAIA mice (Fig. 8A). FA-MMLNP, free 4-OI, and single-loaded nanoparticles only moderately reduced GSDME-N levels, whereas FA-MMLNP@siG/4OI treatment decreased GSDME-N to 25% of that in the PBS group (Fig. 8G).

After confirming the in vivo pyroptosis-inhibiting effect of FA-MMLNP@siG/4OI, we next evaluated its ability to restore mitochondrial metabolism and reprogram synovial macrophage polarisation using double immunofluorescence staining. Synovial tissues were costained for the macrophage marker F4/80 (red) and the pro-/anti-inflammatory markers iNOS (green) and Arg-1 (green). PBS-treated CAIA mice showed markedly elevated iNOS and decreased Arg-1 expression in synovial macrophages (Fig. 8B–C). Single-agent treatments only partially reversed this proinflammatory phenotype, whereas FA-MMLNP@siG/4OI mediated an approximately 5‑fold reduction in iNOS and an approximately 6‑fold increase in Arg-1 (Fig. 8H–I), indicating efficient reprogramming of synovial macrophages toward an anti-inflammatory phenotype.

To validate the combined downstream effects of pyroptosis inhibition and macrophage reprogramming, we performed immunohistochemical staining to assess key proinflammatory cytokines (TNF‑α, IL‑6, and IL‑1β) in synovial tissues. PBS-treated CAIA mice exhibited highly elevated expression of all three cytokines (Figs. 8D–F), confirming the hyperinflammatory synovial microenvironment in RA, which is closely linked to metabolically dysfunctional proinflammatory macrophage activation and pyroptotic cell death. By contrast, free 4-OI and single-loaded formulations only partially attenuated cytokine expression, whereas FA-MMLNP@siG/4OI reduced TNF‑α, IL‑6, and IL‑1β to levels comparable to those in naive mice (Fig. 8J–L).

Collectively, these in vivo mechanistic results are consistent with our in vitro cellular data and demonstrate that FA-MMLNP@siG/4OI exerts strong anti-inflammatory activity in arthritic joints through a dual regulatory mechanism. Specifically, siG selectively inhibits GSDME-dependent macrophage pyroptosis to limit inflammatory cell death, while 4-OI replenishes itaconate and restores mitochondrial metabolic homeostasis, thereby reprogramming synovial macrophages toward an anti-inflammatory phenotype. These complementary, synergistic actions collectively alleviate the hyperinflammatory synovial microenvironment and ultimately mitigate RA-related joint destruction.

Conclusions

RA is characterised by chronic synovial inflammation driven by macrophage pyroptosis and metabolic dysfunction, two intertwined pathological processes constituting a “dual dilemma” for effective treatment and resulting in limited efficacy of conventional single-mechanism therapies. To address this unmet clinical need, we developed a folic acid-modified macrophage membrane-LNP hybrid nanoparticle system (FA-MMLNP@siG/4OI) for the codelivery of GSDME siRNA (siG) and the itaconate derivative 4-OI, and systematically validated its therapeutic efficacy and underlying anti-RA mechanisms both in vitro and in vivo.

Our in vitro studies demonstrated that FA-MMLNP@siG/4OI possessed favorable physicochemical properties and biocompatibility, and mediated efficient siRNA endosomal escape for targeted intracellular delivery. This nanosystem exerted a dual regulatory effect through two independent yet synergistic pathways: it effectively inhibited GSDME-mediated macrophage pyroptosis (as evidenced by reduced LDH release, decreased IL-1β secretion, and suppressed GSDME-N generation) and reprogrammed proinflammatory macrophages toward an anti-inflammatory phenotype by restoring mitochondrial itaconate metabolism and inhibiting NF-κB signalling.

In vivo evaluations in the CAIA mouse model further validated the translational potential of FA-MMLNP@siG/4OI. Benefiting from macrophage membrane coating and folate modification, the nanoparticles achieved enhanced inflamed joint targeting and improved systemic circulation, while exhibiting excellent biosafety. FA-MMLNP@siG/4OI treatment not only markedly alleviated arthritis symptoms (reducing ankle swelling by approximately 47% on Day 9) but also effectively protected joint structural integrity by mitigating bone erosion (restoring BV/TV to approximately 90% of normal levels), cartilage degradation, and synovial hyperplasia. Mechanistically, these therapeutic effects were attributed to suppressed macrophage pyroptosis and restored mitochondrial metabolic homeostasis in the synovial microenvironment, consistent with our in vitro observations.

Collectively, this work presents a dual-targeting and dual-mechanism synergistic nanotherapeutic strategy for RA: macrophage membrane hybridisation and folate modification jointly confer superior targeting specificity to inflamed joints, while codelivery of siG and 4-OI enables synchronous modulation of two core pathological drivers—macrophage pyroptosis and metabolic dysfunction. This strategy overcomes the limitations of conventional single-target therapies and achieves robust anti-arthritic efficacy by intervening at the root of chronic synovial inflammation. Our findings not only provide a promising targeted nanoplatform for RA treatment but also establish a rational design paradigm for hybrid membrane-nanoparticle systems codelivering nucleic acids and small molecules, with broad applicability to other inflammatory diseases driven by immune cell pyroptosis and metabolic disorders.

Future research will focus on further optimising the FA-MMLNP@siG/4OI formulation (e.g., developing long-circulating or sustained-release systems) and validating its efficacy and safety in larger animal models. Moreover, combination therapy with clinical first-line anti-RA agents will be explored to enhance therapeutic outcomes and facilitate clinical translation.
