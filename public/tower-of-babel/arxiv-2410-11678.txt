Strings as Hyper-Fractons
Erica Bertolini

Abstract
We systematically examine all possible Gauss laws obeying spatial rotation symmetry, characterising the corresponding conserved charges. In the case of conserved higher moments, this gives rise to fractonic behaviour. We show that many Gauss laws, including those arising from p p -form electrodynamics, in fact, produce an infinite tower of conserved moments, which we dub hyper-fractonic . In hyper-fractonic systems, a finite number of charged particles cannot be mobile due to an inability of fulfilling the infinite number of conservation laws with a finite number of degrees of freedom. Instead, mobile charged objects must have an infinite number of degrees of freedom. In particular, the strings and branes naturally coupling to p p -form potentials provide an example of hyper-fractonic matter.

The study of more complex cases is already complicated in
3+1
dimensions and even more so in other dimensions. It is the aim of this paper to investigate, through representation-theoretic tools, all possible (Gauss-like) constraints in a general number of spatial dimensions and for gauge fields of arbitrary rank, and classify them through the associated conserved quantities and quasiparticle behaviour. The analysis allows us to uncover new kinds of possible fractonic behaviour. For instance, what would happen if there were infinitely many conservation laws? One might be tempted to guess that such systems must be unphysical as no finite system of charged particles can withstand such a straitjacket. However, in this paper we show that the familiar theory of

[

Such

Note that the presence of this infinite tower of conserved moments may, but need not, imply integrability — for example, in geometric hydrodynamics, the even-dimensional incompressible Euler equation admits an infinite number of conserved enstrophy-type integrals despite not being integrable

Also, note that lattice regularisation and finite-size effects may break part of the tower of conservation laws

In addition, we provide a discussion of general Gauss laws in an arbitrary number of spacetime dimensions, assuming only spatial rotation and spacetime translation symmetry. A scan of all possible Gauss laws in low rank shows that such a hyper-fractonic behaviour is surprisingly common (see

In some cases action principles have been proposed, such as the covariant fracton model of

For instance, a symmetry should be imposed, under which the electric field must be invariant, being a physical observable. Moreover it also depends on the field content in terms of which the electric field is typically the conjugate momentum. In the context of this paper we will only discuss all possible Gauss laws, which could then be adapted to the models of interest.

Similar to the usual fractonic case

The approach presented in this paper starts from the analysis of all the possible Gauss laws in any dimensions and with gauge field of arbitrary rank, which then leads to the classification of conserved moments and fractonic behaviour. In contrast, Ref.

What is relevant to remark is that, as hoped, the results intersect, and enrich one another, in our case for example through the interpretation of the new hyperfractonic behaviour in the classification. Finally we observe that for Poincaré-invariant theories meeting the assumptions of the Coleman–Mandula theorem

•

For a

•

4
Examples of hyper-fractonic behaviour

In this section, we will analyse examples of new fractonic behaviour that arise from the analysis of Section
2
.

4.1
Polyharmonic equation

As a first simple (but perhaps physically unrealistic) example of hyper-fractonic behaviour, we can consider the Gauss law given by the inhomogeneous polyharmonic equation

\Delta^{m}E=\rho,

(35)

i.e. when in (
4
) when we have

Y\colon\square^{\odot(2m)}\otimes\mathbf{1}\to\mathbf{1},

(36)

and where both the electric field
E
and the charge
\rho
are scalars.

Let us consider the conserved moments of (
35
) according to
proposition

1
. This is the case where
R=S=\mathbf{1}
is the singlet representation. The branching
Y\colon\mathbf{1}\otimes\mathbf{1}\to\mathbf{1}
from (
6
) is trivial. The map
Z
(
14
) is given by

Z\colon\square^{\odot(l-2m)}\to\square^{\odot l}.

(37)

Since

\displaystyle\square^{\odot l}

\displaystyle=\square^{\vee l}\oplus\square^{\vee(l-2)}\oplus\dotsb

(38)

\displaystyle\square^{\odot(l-2m)}

\displaystyle=\square^{\vee(l-2m)}\oplus\square^{\vee(l-2m-2)}\oplus\dotsb,

(39)

we see that

\operatorname{coker}(Z)=\square^{\vee l}\oplus\square^{\vee(l-2)}\oplus\dotsb\oplus\square^{\vee(l-2m+2)}.

(40)

This corresponds to the conserved moments

Q^{(l,n)i_{1}\dotso i_{l}}=\int_{U}\mathrm{d}^{d}x\,|x|^{n}x^{\langle i_{1}}\dotsm x^{i_{l}\rangle}\rho\qquad(l\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0},\;n\in\{0,\dotsc,m-1\}).

(41)

As
l
is unbounded, we always have hyper-fractonic behaviour.

For
m=1
, for example, (
35
) is simply the Poisson (inhomogeneous Laplace) equation, which was discussed in
[
36
]
, where an infinite number of conservation laws emerge from the linearised hydrodynamic theory of isotropic dipole-conserving fluids. The conserved moments correspond to harmonic polynomials
x^{i_{1}\dotso i_{k}}
:

\displaystyle Q^{i_{1}\dotso i_{k}}

\displaystyle=\int_{U}\mathrm{d}^{d}x\,x^{i_{1}\dotso i_{k}}\rho.

(42)

4.2
p
-form electrodynamics

Consider a theory of
p
-form electrodynamics with action

S=\int\mathrm{d}^{d+1}x\,\left(-\frac{1}{2(p+1)!}F_{\mu_{0}\mu_{1}\dotso\mu_{p}}F^{\mu^{0}\mu^{1}\dotso\mu^{p}}+\frac{1}{p!}A_{\mu_{1}\dotso\mu_{p}}J^{\mu_{1}\dotso\mu_{p}}\right),

(43)

where
A_{\mu_{1}\dotso\mu_{p}}=A_{[\mu_{1}\dotso\mu_{p}]}
is a
p
-form gauge field,
J^{\mu_{1}\dotso\mu_{p}}=J^{[\mu_{1}\dotso\mu_{p}]}
is a
p
-form current that couples to it, and the field strength is

F_{\mu\nu_{1}\dotso\nu_{p}}=(p+1)\partial_{[\mu}A_{\nu_{1}\dotso\nu_{p}]}.

(44)

The equations of motion are then

\partial^{\mu}F_{\mu\nu_{1}\dotso\nu_{p}}+J_{\nu_{1}\dotso\nu_{p}}=0.

(45)

In particular, defining the electric field and charge as

\displaystyle E_{i_{1}\dotso i_{p}}

\displaystyle\coloneqq F_{0i_{1}\dotso i_{p}}

\displaystyle\rho_{i_{1}\dotso i_{p-1}}

\displaystyle\coloneqq J_{0i_{1}\dotso i_{p-1}},

(46)

we have the Gauss law

\partial^{j}E_{ji_{1}...i_{p-1}}=\rho_{i_{1}\dotso i_{p-1}}.

(47)

The case
p=1
is that of ordinary Maxwell theory.
However, for
p>1
, according to
proposition

1
these systems admit infinitely many conserved moments and are therefore hyper-fractonic: an infinite tower of conserved moments is given by

Q^{\alpha}=P^{\alpha}_{i_{1}\dotso i_{p-1}j_{1}\dotso j_{l}}\int_{U}\mathrm{d}^{d}x\,\rho^{i_{1}\dotso i_{p-1}}x^{\langle j_{1}}\dotsm x^{j_{l}\rangle},

(48)

where
P
is the projection to the
\operatorname{SO}(d)
-representation
\square^{\wedge(p-1)}\vee\square^{\vee l}
, that is, the highest-weight component of
\square^{\wedge(p-1)}\otimes\square^{\vee l}
.

At first sight, the appearance of hyper-fractonic behaviour for
p
-form electrodynamics, with its infinite number of constraints, would seem to preclude dynamical charges. However,
p
-form potentials naturally couple to
(p-1)
-branes, which may be dynamical.
That is, while a finite number of point charges cannot move due to the hyper-fractonic constraints, a continuum of point charges that arrange themselves into a string can. That is, in this case the hyper-fractonic constraints signal the appearance of extended objects.

Note that
p
-form electrodynamics meets the assumptions of the Coleman–Mandula theorem
[
38
,
39
]
, which requires that all conserved moments vanish since their generators do not commute with Poincaré symmetry. For example, for
p=2
, if one has a closed string (with current density
J_{\mu\nu}
and hence charge density
\rho_{i}\sim J_{0i}
) in some compact spatial region
U
, one has

\int_{U}\mathrm{d}^{d}x\,J_{0i}=0.

(49)

This differs from the usual discussion of higher-form symmetries
[
47
,
48
,
49
,
50
,
51
,
52
]
. Here we are integrating against
d
-dimensional spatial volume (in
d+1
-dimensional spacetime), whereas for higher-form symmetries, we would be integrating against a (one-dimensional) curve in space (or a two-dimensional worldsheet in spacetime).

For concreteness, let us consider the
p=2
case, such as the Kalb–Ramond field in Type 2 supergravity
[
53
]
, where
R=\square^{\wedge 2}
and
S=\square
. For an
l
th-order moment (
l\geq 0
), the map
Z
is

Z\colon\square^{\wedge 2}\otimes\square^{l-1}\to\square\otimes\square^{\odot l}.

(50)

For
l=0
, we have the zeroth moment (i.e. charge)

Q^{(0)i}=\int_{U}\mathrm{d}^{d}x\,\rho^{i}.

(51)

For
l=1
(i.e. a dipole moment), the map
Z
is

Z\colon\square^{\wedge 2}\to\square\otimes\square=\square^{\wedge 2}\oplus\square^{\odot 2},

(52)

whose cokernel is

\operatorname{coker}(Z)=\square^{\odot 2}=\square^{\vee 2}\oplus\mathbf{1}.

(53)

This corresponds to the dipole moment

\displaystyle Q^{(1)ij}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\int_{U}\mathrm{d}^{d}x\,x^{(i}\rho^{j)}.

(54)

For
l=2
(i.e. quadrupole moments), the map
Z
is

\displaystyle Z\colon

\displaystyle\square^{\wedge 2}\otimes\square=\square\oplus\square^{\wedge 3}\oplus(\square^{\wedge 2}\vee\square)

(55)

\displaystyle\to\square\otimes(\square^{\vee 2}\oplus\mathbf{1})=\square\oplus\square\oplus\square^{\vee 3}\oplus(\square^{\wedge 2}\vee\square).

We see that the cokernel is
\square\oplus\square^{\vee 3}
, which corresponds to the conserved vector and symmetric rank-3 quadrupole moments

\displaystyle Q^{(2)i}

\displaystyle=\int_{U}\mathrm{d}^{d}x\,\left(x^{i}(\rho\cdot x)+\frac{1}{2}\rho^{i}|x|^{2}\right)

(56)

\displaystyle Q^{(2)ijk}

\displaystyle=\int_{U}\mathrm{d}^{d}x\,\rho^{\langle i}x^{j}x^{k\rangle}.

(57)

For
l=3
(i.e. octupole moments),

\displaystyle Z\colon

\displaystyle\square^{\wedge 2}\otimes(\square^{\vee 2}\oplus\mathbf{1})=\square^{\wedge 2}\oplus\square^{\wedge 2}\oplus(\square^{\wedge 3}\vee\square)\oplus\square^{\vee 2}\oplus(\square^{\vee 2}\vee\square^{\wedge 2})

(58)

\displaystyle\to\square\otimes(\square^{\vee 3}\oplus\square)=\mathbf{1}\oplus\square^{\wedge 2}\oplus\square^{\vee 2}\oplus\square^{\vee 2}\oplus(\square^{\vee 2}\vee\square^{\wedge 2})\oplus\square^{\vee 4}.

Hence the cokernel of
Z
is
\mathbf{1}\oplus\square^{\vee 2}\oplus\square^{\vee 4}
, whose corresponding conserved octupole moments are

\displaystyle Q^{(3)}

\displaystyle=\int_{U}\mathrm{d}^{d}x\,(\rho\cdot x)|x|^{2}

(59)

\displaystyle Q^{(3)ij}

\displaystyle=\int_{U}\mathrm{d}^{d}x\,\left((\rho\cdot x)x^{\langle i}x^{j\rangle}+\rho^{\langle i}x^{j\rangle}|x|^{2}\right)

(60)

\displaystyle Q^{(3)ijkl}

\displaystyle=\int_{U}\mathrm{d}^{d}x\,\rho^{\langle i}x^{j}x^{k}x^{l\rangle}.

(61)

In general, the totally symmetric totally traceless moments

Q^{(l)i_{0}\dotso i_{l}}\coloneqq\int_{U}\mathrm{d}^{d}x\,\rho^{\langle i_{0}}x^{i_{1}}\dotsm x^{i_{l}\rangle}=\int_{U}\mathrm{d}^{d}x\,P^{i_{0}\dotso i_{l}}_{j_{0}\dotso j_{l}}\rho^{j_{0}}x^{j_{1}}\dotsm x^{j_{l}}

P\colon\square^{\odot(l+1)}\to\square^{\vee(l+1)}

(63)

is projection to the totally traceless totally symmetric part, but there are many other conserved moments in addition.

5
Discussion

In this paper, we have shown that a generalised Gauss law (
4
) in arbitrary dimensions allows for two kinds of possible conserved moments, (
11
) and/or (
12
), from which we can observe three behaviours:

1.

non-fractonic : only the charge is conserved and it has complete mobility ;

2.

fractonic : a finite number of higher moments is conserved, which present restricted mobility (complete or partial) ;

3.

hyper-fractonic : an infinite tower of conserved moments, which may signal stringy/brane-like behaviour.

The most surprising result that emerges from our analysis is the last case: a new general behaviour presenting an infinite tower of conserved charges. As unphysical and unrealistic as it may seem, a particular case of that was observed in
[
36
]
in the context of hydrodynamics and fractons. This provides a confirmation of our procedure, which then brought us to identify this feature also in higher-form gauge theories, which thus seem to exhibit a new (hyper-)fractonic behaviour. In addition, the analysis presented herein may then provide a guiding light to construct new (hyper-)fractonic theories. In fact, another way to obtain multipole conservation is through continuity equations of matter sources, which reflect the Gauss laws of the specific theory. For instance, in the scalar-charge theory, it is

\partial_{0}\rho+\partial_{i}\partial_{j}J^{ij}=0,

(66)

which, when integrated over a volume, implies that the total dipole must be conserved in time (up to boundary terms)
[
9
,
10
]
.
Equation

66
may then be seen as a Ward identity on the sources, from which it is thus possible to derive the symmetry transformation of the associated gauge field, from which finally the invariant action and the full theory can be reconstructed.

Appendix A
Proof of
proposition

2

Consider the Gauss law of the form (
18
), viz.

\tilde{Y}^{ai_{1}\dotso i_{k}}_{A}\Delta^{m}\partial_{i_{1}}\dotso\partial_{i_{k}}E^{A}=\rho^{a}.

(67)

Then, integrating by parts to obtain a conservation law, the following expression appears:

\Delta^{m}\partial_{\langle i_{1}}\dotsm\partial_{i_{k}\rangle}(x^{j_{1}}\dotsm x^{j_{1+2n}}).

(68)

Decomposing the string of
x
s into irreducible representations of
\operatorname{SO}(d)
, it suffices to show that

\Delta^{m}\partial_{\langle i_{1}}\dotsm\partial_{i_{k}\rangle}(|x|^{2n}x^{\langle j_{1}}\dotsm x^{j_{l}\rangle}).

(69)

vanishes precisely when either
n<m
or
l+n<k+m
, since this is the expression that remains when one integrates by parts the conserved charge.

The expression (
69
)
may be represented as a suitable projection

\Delta^{m}\partial_{\langle i_{1}}\dotsm\partial_{i_{k}\rangle}(|x|^{2n}x^{\langle j_{1}}\dotsm x^{j_{l}\rangle})=P^{i^{\prime}_{1}\dotso i^{\prime}_{k+2m}}_{i_{1}\dotso i_{k}}{\tilde{P}}_{j^{\prime}_{1}\dotso j^{\prime}_{l+2m}}^{j_{1}\dotso j_{l}}\partial_{i^{\prime}_{1}}\dotso\partial_{i^{\prime}_{k+2m}}(x^{j^{\prime}_{1}}\dotsm x^{j^{\prime}_{l+2n}})

(70)

where
P
and
\tilde{P}
are suitable projectors to subrepresentations:

\displaystyle P\colon\square^{\odot(k+2m)}

\displaystyle\to\square^{\vee k}

\displaystyle\tilde{P}\colon\square^{\odot(l+2n)}

\displaystyle\to\square^{\vee l}.

(71)

It is easy to see that

\partial_{i_{1}}\dotsm\partial_{i_{k+2m}}(x^{j_{1}}\dotsm x^{j_{l+2n}})=\begin{cases}\binom{l}{k}\deltaup_{(i_{1}}^{(j_{1}}\dotsm\deltaup_{i_{k+2m})}^{j_{k+2m}}x_{\vphantom{i_{k}}}^{j_{k+2m+1}\dotso j_{l+2n})}&\text{if $l+2n\geq k+2m$}\\
0&\text{if $l+2n<k+2m$}.\end{cases}

(72)

That is, the expression
\partial_{i^{\prime}_{1}}\dotso\partial_{i^{\prime}_{k+2m}}(x^{j^{\prime}_{1}}\dotsm x^{j^{\prime}_{l+2n}})
is either zero (if
k+2m>l+2n
) or a product of
l+2n-k-2m
occurrences of
x
followed by some Kronecker deltas.

The string of
x
s in the right-hand side of (
72
) transforms as the representation
\square^{\odot(l+2n-k-2m)}
. The Kronecker deltas (after separating out various traces) are the diagonal map
\operatorname{diag}_{\square^{\wedge k}}
on the representation
\square^{\wedge k}
, and
\operatorname{diag}_{\square^{\wedge k}}
transforms as
\square^{\vee k}\otimes\square^{\vee k}
.

So (
70
) may be evaluated by (a) starting with a string of
x
s, (b) contracting with Kronecker deltas, and (c) projecting out some traces.
That is, the projection involved here is

\displaystyle\square^{\odot(l+2n-k-2m)}

\displaystyle\xrightarrow{\operatorname{diag}_{\square^{\vee k}}\otimes\operatorname{id}_{\square^{\odot(l+2n-k-2m)}}}\square^{\vee k}\otimes\square^{\vee k}\otimes\square^{\odot(l+2n-k-2m)}

(73)

\displaystyle\xrightarrow{\operatorname{id}_{\square^{\vee k}}\otimes s}\square^{\vee k}\otimes\square^{\vee l}

where

s\colon\square^{\vee k}\otimes\square^{\odot(l+2n-k-2m)}\to\square^{\vee l}.

(74)

So, the criterion for (
69
) vanishing is whether

\square^{\vee k}\otimes\square^{\odot(l+2n-k-2m)}\overset{?}{\not\supset}\square^{\vee l}.

(75)

This is equivalent to

l\not\in\{k-(l+2n-k-2m),k-(l+2n-k-2m)+2,k-(l+2n-k-2m)+4,\\
\dotsc,k+(l+2n-k-2m)\},

(76)

which simplifies to

m>n\text{ or }k+m>l+n,

(77)

as desired.
