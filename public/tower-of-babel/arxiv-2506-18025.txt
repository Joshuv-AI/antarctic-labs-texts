Nanoscale imaging of reduced forward bias at V-pits in green-emitting nitride LEDs
C. Fornos, N. Alyabyeva, Y. W. Ho, J. Peretti, A. C. H. Rowe, J. S. Speck, T. Tak, C. Weisbuch

Abstract
Record wall-plug efficiencies in long-wavelength, nitride light-emitting diodes (LEDs) have recently been achieved in devices containing high V-pit densities. Numerical modeling suggests this may be due to improved electrical efficiencies (EE). In order to test this proposition, a novel scanning tunneling luminescence microscope (STLM) is used to map the local optoelectronic properties of commercial, green-emitting LED heterostructures around V-pits with nanoscale spatial resolution. Using the STLM tip as the hole injector, injection at the lips of V-pits is found to be drastically different from injection on the heterostructure’s c-plane. A ≈ \approx 3-fold improvement in internal quantum efficiency near V-pits is observed at low injection, and at higher injection a ≈ \approx 1.2 V reduction in the forward bias unambiguously confirms the EE hypothesis for hole injection.



The wall-plug efficiency (WPE) of light-emitting diodes (LEDs) is defined as the output optical power per unit input electrical power. Today, blue-emitting, III-nitride LEDs exhibit peak WPEs exceeding 95 %
Kuritzky et al. 2017
. However, as the Indium content in the InGaN quantum well alloys is increased in order to produce LEDs emitting at longer wavelengths, WPE drops precipitously
Weisbuch et al. 2015
, resulting in the so-called green gap
Usman et al. 2021
and the use of III-phosphide technologies in the red
Krames et al. 1999
. There are on-going efforts in academic and industrial research laboratories to explore strategies aimed at improving WPEs of long-wavelength III-nitride LEDs, with the goal being to produce efficient white-light emitters. Success would have significant technological and economic advantages for lighting and micro-LED screen applications
Fan et al. 2023
;
Hsiang et al. 2021
.

Recently, record-high WPE in green- and yellow-emitting nitride LEDs were obtained in devices containing high (
\approx 3\times 10^{8}
cm
-2
) densities of V-pits
Jiang et al. 2019
;
Zhang et al. 2020
. At yellow wavelengths, peak WPE reaches 40 %, a 3-fold improvement on previous efforts
Damilano and Gil 2015
. The mechanism which yields this significantly improved WPE is not yet clear. Early, ground-breaking work established that V-pits act to shield threading dislocations from charges injected into the LED’s
\left\{0001\right\}
or c-plane quantum wells
Hangleiter et al. 2005
. Since threading dislocations are associated with non-radiative recombination, V-pits thus reduce their impact on the LED’s internal quantum efficiency (IQE) as confirmed in numerous works on blue-emitting LEDs
Hao et al. 2006
;
Takahashi et al. 2000
;
Han et al. 2013
;
Yapparov et al. 2020
. This acts on the WPE according to:

\textrm{WPE}=\textrm{IQE}\times\textrm{LEE}\times\textrm{EE},

(1)

where the light extraction efficiency (LEE) describes the ability of photons to escape the high-refractive index structure, and EE is the electrical efficiency defined in Eq. (
3
) below. A new element in the discussion of long-wavelength LEDs
Quan et al. 2014
;
Li et al. 2016
;
Ho et al. 2022
suggests that V-pits may also improve EE in Eq. (
1
), either of holes alone
Quan et al. 2014
;
Li et al. 2016
, or of both electrons and holes
Ho et al. 2022
;
Quevedo et al. 2024
. Specifically, models indicate that the semi-polar nature of V-pit
\left\{1\overline{1}01\right\}
or s5 facets favor charge injection into the multi-quantum well active zone of the LED via reductions in internal polarization fields and potential barriers
Kivisaari et al. 2012
. As yet, no experimental work directly provides evidence for this.

An extensive experimental literature has progressively revealed the optoelectronic properties of V-pits
Hangleiter et al. 2005
;
Hitzel et al. 2005
;
Netzel et al. 2007
;
Meyer et al. 2011
;
Kim et al. 2014
;
Sheen et al. 2015
;
Zhou and Liu 2017
;
Ajia et al. 2018
;
Yapparov et al. 2020
. Recent works employing far-
Chow et al. 2023
and near-field
Yapparov et al. 2024a
;
Yapparov et al. 2024b
imaging of the photo- and electro-luminescence indirectly suggest lateral hole transport following injection at V-pit facets prior to recombination in c-plane quantum wells. This ingredient is present in numerical modeling that indicates V-pit-induced improvements to EE
Ho et al. 2022
;
Quan et al. 2014
;
Li et al. 2016
, and was invoked to explain the characteristics of operational LEDs containing V-pits
Wu et al. 2016
. However, these experimental techniques do not reveal the underlying physical mechanism for improved WPE. To do so requires local optoelectronic measurements at the scale of the V-pits. To date, only two works have made attempts in this direction. The first employs the high spatial resolution of self-emissive electron microscopy to provide local electrical information without correlating it with local optical properties
Tak et al. 2023
, and the second compares separate local conductive atomic force microscopy measurements with near-field luminescence imaging
Li et al. 2019
. A 20-fold increase in current at fixed bias is observed in the vicinity of V-pits and a separate, purely optical map, shows a 2-fold increase in electroluminescence intensity near V-pits. The spatial resolution of the maps is however comparable to the V-pit size.

Here we
simultaneously
map the local optoelectronic properties of V-pits in a commercial, green-emitting LED heterostructures with spatial resolutions (
<
10 nm) well below the size of the V-pits. Using the tip of a scanning tunneling microscope as a local hole injector, we provide the first direct experimental evidence to support the EE hypothesis.

The LED studied here is a commercial, green-emitting multi-quantum-well heterostructure manufactured by Seoul Semiconductor which exhibits a V-pit density of
\approx 5\times 10^{8}
cm
-2
. The LED is contacted with concentric, circular contacts (see Fig.
1
(a)) and the resulting characteristics at room temperature and 100 K, the temperature used for the scanning tunnelling luminescence microscope (STLM) experiments, are shown in Fig.
1
(b). The absolute
external
quantum efficiency (EQE) of the LED, the product of the first two terms in Eq. (
1
):

\textrm{EQE}=\textrm{IQE}\times\textrm{LEE},

(2)

is not directly measured here because the collection efficiency of the experimental optics is not known. Of interest however is the relative EQE, like that shown for the macroscopic LED in Fig.
1
(c). At an injected current of 30 mA the relative EQE at room temperature is maximized, and the spectrum of the electroluminescence at this current is centered around a photon energy,
h\nu
= 2.3 eV, as seen inset in Fig.
1
(c). At higher currents, efficiency droop sets in due to the onset of Auger recombination
Iveland et al. 2013
. It is also noted that because non-radiative recombination is frozen out as temperature drops, at 100 K the maximum relative EQE occurs at lower currents and is higher than at room temperature.

In the as-fabricated LED, V-pits are filled with a p-GaN growth that forms the p-type neutral zone. In order to be able to locally study the optoelectronic properties of V-pits with the STLM, they must be exposed at the surface. To this end, the 60 nm-thick p-type neutral zone (p-GaN) at the top of the LED heterostructure is removed using atomic layer etching (ALE)
Ho et al. 2023
. As indicated in Fig.
2
(a), the etch stops at the AlGaN electron blocking layer (EBL, blue) of the c-plane surface, but leaves the s5 facets of the V-pits themselves covered with remanent p-GaN (solid pink). Only the lips of the V-pits are exposed at the surface. The resulting heterostructure is contacted using the same mask as that shown in Fig.
1
(a), although now the inner circular electrode makes a Schottky contact directly with the EBL (rather than an ohmic contact to the p-GaN neutral zone) and the LED is no longer functional. Fig.
2
(b) shows the calculated
Li et al. 2017
band structure of the post-ALE heterostructure, and illustrates the injection and light emission processes occurring during the STLM measurements shown in Figs.
3
and
4
.

In the STLM measurements, the scanning tip of the STLM is grounded, and injects hot holes into the LED under the effect of a bias, V
{}_{\textrm{TUN}}
= -7 V, applied to the inner (Schottky) contact (see Fig.
2
(a)). A fraction of these hot holes reach the deepest lying quantum wells as illustrated in the schematic diagram in Fig.
2
(b). At the same time, the heterostructure itself is polarized with a small bias, V
{}_{\textrm{LED}}
= 0.8 V (see also Fig.
2
(a)), which populates these same quantum wells with electrons as indicated in Fig.
2
(b). Radiative recombination of the electron-hole pairs can then occur, resulting in the spontaneous emission of photons from a nanoscale sample volume below the tip. This electroluminescence, shown in green in Fig.
2
and labeled
h\nu
, is collected after transmission through the substrate using a steerable aspheric lens (NA = 0.6) installed in ultra-high vacuum, and then fibered to either a photo-multiplier in the case of the experiments shown in Fig.
3
or a spectrometer equipped with an electron-multiplied CCD camera for the experiments shown in Fig.
4
.

As the STLM tip scans as shown in Fig.
2
(a), the tip/EBL current is regulated in the usual way by adjusting the tip/EBL distance with a tunnel current feedback loop. For V
{}_{\textrm{TUN}}
= -7 V and a set point current of -20 nA, topography similar to that shown in Fig.
3
(a) can be mapped. As expected, the p-GaN-filled V-pits are visible at the surface, with line profiles (not shown) revealing filled structures similar to that sketched in Fig.
2
(a). In addition to the topography, the integrated intensity of the tunneling electroluminescence can be simultaneously mapped while the tip scans. In Fig.
3
, maps consist of 512
\times
512 pixels with a line scan time of 5.12 s corresponding to 10 ms per pixel. Using a photo-multiplier with a 10 ms gate, the number of photons emitted per second for a given pixel can be normalized to the number of holes injected per second from the tip to obtain a local (relative) EQE. The resulting EQE map, shown in Fig.
3
(b), can then be correlated with the topography map.

Inside the smaller of the two concentric forms in Fig.
3
(a) (dotted lines), the tip injects hot majority holes into the remnant p-GaN filling the V-pits. These hot holes rapidly thermalize and do not emit light. They do however traverse the heterostructure since the current is observed at the n-type contact as a differential current measurement demonstrates (see Supplementary Material). This suggests that they encounter electrons from the n-GaN neutral zone and undergo non-radiative recombination after transport vertically through the heterostructure near the threading dislocation, a known source of non-radiative recombination
Hangleiter et al. 2005
. This interesting phenomenon is of relevance for LEDs containing V-pits and is still not well understood. However, it is not the principal objective of this work and will not be discussed further.

Of more interest are the clear EQE maxima around the lips of the V-pits, which are approximately 2 to 3 times higher than the EQE obtained when the tip directly injects holes into the c-plane quantum wells. This is most obvious when superimposing the two images, although this is difficult to show directly here. Instead, two concentric forms (dotted, black lines) in Fig.
3
(a) indicate the inner and outer circumferences of the corresponding bright ring in Fig.
3
(b). Since light detection is made in transmission through the thick, patterned substrate, the local LEE does not depend on the topography of the device directly below the tip. Thus according to Eq. (
2
) peaks in EQE are the result of a local increase in IQE when injecting holes at the lips of V-pits.

Possible improvements in IQE at V-pits are present in models
Quan et al. 2014
;
Ho et al. 2022
but, unlike the EE to be discussed below, improvements in WPE of long-wavelength LEDs are not clearly attributed to this. It is therefore useful to give further details of the light emission at the lips of V-pits here. Fig.
4
shows a map of the photon energy corresponding to the peak in the local tunneling electroluminescence spectrum obtained by freezing the tip scan on an array of pre-defined points. The delay time at each of these points is 10 s, during which the current set-point is maintained at -20 nA (
V_{\textrm{TUN}}=-7
V) with the feedback loop, and the light is fibered to a short focal length monochromator equipped with an electron-multiplied CCD camera. Fig.
4
(b) shows a striking blue shift of the electroluminescence when the tip injects holes close to the lip of the V-pit rather than on the heterostructure’s c-plane. The blue shift is the order of 10 meV which is small compared to the linewidth of the macroscopic electroluminescence peak shown inset in Fig.
1
(c), and is also much smaller than the approximately 0.5 eV blue shifts observed in the photoluminescence of V-pit facets relative to c-planes
Yapparov et al. 2020
;
Netzel et al. 2007
;
Yapparov et al. 2024a
. Small blue shifts indicate that holes injected from the tip into the lips of the V-pits laterally transport to the c-planes prior to recombination with electrons in c-plane quantum wells, a conclusion previously drawn from purely optical measurements
Meyer et al. 2011
;
Yapparov et al. 2024b
. As such, the quantum-confined Stark effect in the quantum wells of the V-pit facets which can modify the local IQE by modifying the electron-hole wavefunction overlap, would seem to be irrelevant. Further work is therefore required to understand the observations reported on in Figs.
3
and
4
.

The question of the effect of V-pits on EE in Eq. (
1
) is now addressed. Inspired by scanning tunneling spectroscopy methods, Fig.
5
presents data obtained by opening the tunnel current feedback loop on a square array of predefined points, during which time V
{}_{\textrm{TUN}}
is scanned from -6 V to 0 V while measuring the resulting current. The voltage scan is made with 0.1 V steps every 10 ms. Between points the feedback loop is closed prior to scanning of the tip to the next point. In this way a topography map like that shown in Fig.
5
(a) can be compared with the maps of the forward bias voltage defined below and shown in Fig.
5
(b).

The first surprising observation in the local current-voltage characteristics obtained when scanning V
{}_{\textrm{TUN}}
with the feedback loop open is that currents well above -1
\mu
A occur when the tip injects holes on the c-plane and at V-pit lips. This occurs despite the fact that the current set point used to obtain the topography in Fig.
5
(a) is -20 nA for V
{}_{\textrm{TUN}}
= -7 V. The exact origin of this large current increase is not clear, but it is speculated that electrostatic image forces
Rideout and Crowell 1970
arising from unscreened charge in the semiconductor reduce the tunnel barrier and yield much larger currents. In this case the tip-surface junction might more closely resemble a local Schottky contact. Regardless of the details, this is somewhat serendipitous since it allows for V
{}_{\textrm{TUN}}
to be dropped entirely across the resulting, local Schottky contact and the heterostructure, and removes the partial drop across the tunnel junction. As such, the injection geometry becomes a local LED, albeit one with a non-Ohmic p-contact. The characteristics shown in Fig.
5
then better represent the true, local electrical properties of the LED heterostructure at the position of the tip.

The unique combination of nanoscale spatial resolution combined with the ability to perform local electrical and optical spectroscopies offered by the STLM reveals a clear picture of the role played by V-pits in WPE improvements recently obtained in long-wavelength-emitting nitride LED heterostructures. Holes injected at the lips of V-pits transport to the c-plane prior to radiative recombination as evidenced by meV-blue shifts in the electroluminescence where, under low forward biases, the local IQE is 2- to 3-times higher than that obtained when injecting holes injected directly on the c-plane of the heterostructure. This local improvement in the IQE has not been focus of the recent V-pit literature and would therefore merit further work. Indeed, the focus of this recent work has rather been on possible EE improvements at the semi-polar facets of V-pits. In the work reported here, holes are injected at V-pit lips at forward biases on average 1.2 V lower than required when injecting directly on the c-plane of the heterostructure. Local reductions in forward bias at V-pits is an unambiguous experimental signature of improved EE. With the STLM tip used as a local hole injector it is therefore experimentally confirmed that hole injection efficiencies are higher thanks to the presence of V-pits
Ho et al. 2022
;
Quan et al. 2014
;
Li et al. 2016
. However, since electrons are injected from a macroscopic electrical contact in the experiments reported here, it is not yet possible to judge whether V-pits also improve electron injection efficiencies
Ho et al. 2022
;
Quevedo et al. 2024
.
