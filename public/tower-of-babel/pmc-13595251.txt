Finding and Managing Heart Failure Earlier: Why Enhancing Primary Care Teams to Apply More Definitive Surveillance is the Solution
Authors: James Smith, David Wilson, Amy Rose Stewart, Trung Nguyen, Justin Beilby, Lisa Collison, Lynda Whiteway, Rachel Ambagtsheer, Rebecca Perry, David Playford, Yih-Kai Chan, Simon Stewart
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Typically, heart failure (HF) is definitively diagnosed and treated with guideline-directed medical therapy late in its natural history, with reported delays, missed investigation and inpatient diagnoses associated with poor outcomes. Accordingly, better, more decisive primary care can and should play a critical role in addressing this seemingly intractable problem. However, as reflected by a paucity of evidence to inform the recommendations of expert HF guidelines, primary care teams are rarely considered as part of the solution. In this review, we explore all the potential outcomes from more proactive screening for HF in primary care when introducing artificial intelligence-mediated portable cardiac ultrasound. Based on the strengths and weaknesses of the screening components that can now be applied, we consider the spectrum of early to late forms of HF likely to be revealed and more immediately diagnosed. Within this context, we present, as an example of what can be achieved in this space, a clinical algorithm that was developed for a prospective, multicentre project to find and manage HF earlier in the primary care setting.

Although recognised as a distinctive and often deadly condition for many centuries, the modern-day definition and subsequent diagnosis of heart failure (HF) have shifted from recognising more overt clinical signs and symptoms, to proactively detecting underlying structural and functional cardiac abnormalities.1 This change is potentially important when one considers that HF continues to be characterised by impaired quality of life and poor survival rates across all its subtypes.2–6 Worldwide, it is estimated that 64 million people are living (and dying) with the condition.7 However, such estimates likely reflect only those formally diagnosed with clinically overt HF. As such, the timely and accurate diagnosis of HF outside of specialist centres and those presenting to hospital in an acutely decompensated state remains problematic. Belying the problem, despite potentially important regional and sex-based differences globally, in high-income countries, the most common antecedents of HF (i.e. metabolic disorders/diabetes, hypertension and ischaemic heart disease) can be readily identified.8–11 Knowing who is most likely to develop HF is one thing, but diagnosing them in a timely manner is another – especially when it comes to primary care.

In a retrospective study of medical records generated within 43 general practices in Australia over a 5-year period (2013–2018), only 18.9% of cases of ‘definitive’ HF were documented.12 Similarly, a recent analysis of the diagnosis of HF in 412,173 people being treated by primary care clinics across England from 2000 to 2021 revealed that despite the presence of ‘known’ indicators of HF in two-thirds of cases (66.5%), time to diagnosis was close to 3 years (35.4 [6.4–54.7] months).13 Indeed, over the study time frame, this concerning figure had effectively doubled. Moreover, during the 5-year period 2015–2019, only 12.5%, 19.8% and 34.4% of patients with obvious indications of HF underwent natriuretic peptide testing, were investigated with echocardiography (echo) or were referred for specialist review, respectively. Overall, just over half of such cases (52.3%) were not investigated further for HF.13

Fundamentally, these and other studies from many parts of Europe to Latin America demonstrate that HF can and will be found in primary care when looked for.14–18 Sadly, for many people affected by this truly malignant syndrome, this means that the good intentions around its primary prevention have long passed.6,19 However, when someone is found, there is still time to prevent progressive HF and subsequent risk of repeated hospitalisations and/or premature death.20 This potential to change the typical clinical trajectory of HF for the better is encapsulated by the British Society for Heart Failure’s ‘25in25 initiative’ to reduce HF mortality in the first year after diagnosis by 25% in the next 25 years.21 The fundamental aims of this initiative are straightforward: to find undetected cases of HF to ensure patients receive life-saving treatments earlier and to make every healthcare encounter count in considering those most at risk of having undetected HF. In the primary care setting, this includes a role for every healthcare professional (i.e. not only general practitioners/primary care physicians, but also practice nurses and community pharmacists) who has contact with at-risk individuals in the community setting.

Passive or Proactive Primary Care?

In considering the above, one wonders if currently recommended diagnostic pathways (and the technology required to enact them) have been suitably adapted to the primary care setting, rather than reflecting a broader inability to identify the signs and symptoms of HF?22 As will be subsequently explained, for multiple reasons it is the former that needs to be addressed, not the latter. Despite highlighting the need for multidisciplinary teams to improve HF-related outcomes, across the extensive and highly influential guidelines published by the European Society of Cardiology (ESC) and the American Heart Association/American College of Cardiology (AHA/ACC), the term ‘primary care’ appears only a handful of times.23,24 Fundamentally, this reflects a paucity of evidence to inform specific recommendations. However, there are mitigating circumstances surrounding the apparent passivity in detecting HF early in the primary care setting, reflecting key issues surrounding its evolving definition and the historical availability of diagnostic tools.1

A key question remains in this space: if provided with the appropriate evidence, guidance and technological support, do general practitioners now have the personnel and the tools to meet the key aims and goals embodied by the ‘25in25 initiative’ and become more proactive in detecting HF earlier in the primary care setting?21 As will be discussed, the answer is a qualified yes, if they are more readily able to access and successfully incorporate a combination of natriuretic peptide level screening and newly developed technology in the form of artificial intelligence-mediated portable cardiac ultrasound (AI-PoCUS) to generate and interpret cardiac images into their routine clinical practice.

However, as will be further explained, these need to: first, be integrated into the diagnostic framework mandated by professional societies (noting multiple layers of evidence needing to be considered); second, reflect the strengths and weaknesses of each diagnostic component; third, consider the multiple clinical outcomes arising from interim screening; and, finally, ensure that often limited health resources (from primary to specialist care) are not overwhelmed – with newly found cases of HF appropriately managed prior to any specialist consultation.23,24 The following sections consider each issue/problem confronting primary care teams in this regard, providing a strategic, evidence-based principle that would potentially guide a feasible approach to finding (and then managing) HF earlier.

Detecting Heart Failure in Primary Care: but Which Definition?

If we are going to find more cases of undiagnosed HF earlier in primary care, there needs to be a clear understanding of how to find it and who has it. Unfortunately, despite attempts to create and apply a ‘universal definition’ of HF, primary care teams worldwide are confronted with a myriad of definitions (often with subtle variations evident) that represent a significant barrier to a timely diagnosis.25,26 This is especially so when one considers people who are reportedly asymptomatic at the time of assessment.

Guideline-defined Heart Failure

As defined by the AHA/ACC, HF is “a complex clinical syndrome with symptoms and signs that result from any structural or functional impairment of ventricular filling or ejection of blood”.24 Similarly, the ESC defines it as “a clinical syndrome consisting of cardinal symptoms that may be accompanied by signs. It is due to a structural and/or functional abnormality of the heart that results in elevated intracardiac pressure and/or inadequate cardiac output at rest and/or during exercise”.23 As indicated above, attempts to harmonise these and other definitions, have resulted in the following universal definition of HF: “a clinical syndrome with symptoms and/or signs caused by structural and/or functional cardiac abnormality and corroborated by elevated natriuretic peptides and/or objective evidence of pulmonary or systemic congestion”.27 As will be discussed in more detail below, all three definitions shape what is required to confirm the presence of HF, with common elements evident. This includes a strong focus on confirming the presence of symptoms and signs of ‘congestive’ HF.28 Where things become more complicated (even before considering the specific pathological mechanisms leading to the syndrome) is the need to apply guideline-directed medical therapy when identifying the main type of HF present.29 Accordingly, all three definitions presented here further delineate between those with: severely impaired systolic function as indicated by a reduced left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) ≤40% – HF with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF) OR a past history of LVEF ≤40% but now improved; moderately impaired systolic function (LVEF 41–49%) – HF with mildly reduced ejection fraction; and those with HF associated with otherwise normal systolic function (LVEF ≥50%) – HF with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF).

The Conundrum of Detecting Heart Failure with Preserved Ejection Fraction

While the first two subtypes of HF outlined above are based on a single cardiac index typically measured by transthoracic echo, the latter invokes much controversy around the diagnosis of diastolic dysfunction resulting in elevated intracardiac filling pressures.23,24,30–32 As succinctly described by Walter Paulus, many argue that “If HFpEF is that difficult to diagnose, it does not exist!”30 Fortunately, for the many women worldwide with preserved systolic function who still develop, suffer and die from HFpEF due to a historical lack of treatment options, this view has been largely dismissed.33

While it is beyond the scope of this review to discuss all the intricacies around measuring and quantifying diastolic dysfunction (this remains a highly specialised field), it is important to note that, while the American Society of Echocardiography places importance on both increased stiffness and filling pressures within the heart, it is the latter that is most amenable to assessment in non-specialist centres (i.e. via AI-PoCUS).31 Accordingly, the key parameters of interest to find elevated filling pressures in primary care are the early diastolic mitral annular tissue velocity averaged from both the septal and lateral mitral annulus (e’), the ratio of the early diastolic mitral inflow (E) velocity to the late diastolic mitral inflow (A) velocity (E/A ratio), the E/e’ ratio and the left atrial volume index.31,32 As recommended by a recent clinical consensus statement from the ESC’s Heart Failure Association (ESC-HFA) to detect HF in the community, uncovering a left atrial volume index of >34 ml/m2 (in sinus rhythm) and E/e’ (at rest) >9, along with a tricuspid regurgitant velocity of >2.8 m/s on transthoracic echo is enough to confirm the presence of diastolic dysfunction.34 These same parameters are central to published algorithms (such as the H2FPEF Score) seeking to clarify the specific diagnosis of HFpEF.30,35

Perhaps less controversial is the identification using echo of left ventricular modelling/hypertrophy that is the signature of an early adaptive response to progressive cardiac dysfunction. As recommended by the ESC-HFA, a left ventricular mass index of ≥95 g/m2 in females or ≥115 g/m2 in males, along with a relative wall thickness ratio of >0.42, indicates the presence of such structural cardiac changes leading to HF; noting recent evidence from the National Echo Database of Australia suggesting lower prognostic thresholds in both men and women.34,36 As such, specific patterns around the wall thickness and ventricular volume delineate, for example, between early left ventricular concentric remodelling versus more advanced concentric left ventricular hypertrophy.37

In considering all the above, one can see why time-poor non-specialist physicians, such as general practitioners, have difficulty keeping up with the often complex and convoluted logic underpinning the clinical recommendations published by major cardiac societies in relation to HF. This reinforces their continued reliance on specialist opinion and diagnosis. However, as reported by the National Echo Database of Australia (>600,000 adults undergoing routine echo at specialist centres), around one in five echo reports do not quantify key indices of cardiac function (e.g. LVEF and/or E/e’).32,38 Moreover, in contrast to the defined thresholds typically proposed by expert guidelines within this large, real-world cohort (many of whom are asymptomatic), there are clear gradients of prognostic risk across the full spectrum of left ventricular systolic (sex-specific) and/or diastolic function, valvular function and, as indicated above, left ventricular size (sex-specific).23,24,32,36,38,39,40,41

Are Symptoms Really That Important?

A potential discordance between objective and subjective (i.e. symptoms such as exercise intolerance) evidence of HF at earlier stages of the syndrome is explicitly recognised by the AHA/ACC. Specifically, they recognise three initial stages of HF with high relevance to primary care in respect to choosing who to screen for the syndrome (Stage A cases with pre-HF are identified as those with the common antecedents of the syndrome) and the likelihood of finding people who are yet to manifest or report HF symptoms (Stage B cases with echo or brain natriuretic peptide [BNP] evidence of abnormal cardiac structure and/or dysfunction) and symptomatic cases who meet all the other criteria (Stage C HF).24 With further relevance to primary care, a 2023 consensus statement from the ESC-HFA coined the concept of ‘heart stress’ when “NT-proBNP levels are elevated in an asymptomatic patient with risk factors for HF underlying the development of cardiac dysfunction and further increased risk”.42 Within the context of a historical shift from defining HF based on sign and symptoms to finding the structural and functional cardiac abnormalities that drive subsequent morbidity and mortality, Figure 1 summarises the full spectrum of HF from a primary care perspective. In framing the above, two opposing confounders are worth considering: first, many patients considered to be at high risk of developing HF are likely to be older and have a significant component of frailty due to multiple disease states that will potentially mirror the signs and symptoms of HF; and second, concurrently, those experiencing typical HF symptoms such as exertional dyspnoea are likely to modulate their activity/lifestyle to avoid them occurring.1,32,38,43,44 Starting with the text box below, the key clinical learnings from the literature for primary care teams to consider when seeking to proactively find HF cases earlier are summarised in Clinical Principles 1–6.

Clinical Principle 1

Apply a broader approach to finding earlier signs of cardiac dysfunction and remodelling indicative of HF that will inevitably lead to more symptomatic and advanced forms of HF in high-risk individuals (equivalent to AHA Stage A pre-HF) without proactive initiation of potentially life-saving treatment/management.

Finding Symptoms and Signs of Heart Failure

Any breakdown of the definitions used to describe the syndrome of HF shows that symptoms and signs are essential to its diagnosis. As articulated by the ESC, the cardinal symptoms of HF are breathlessness (functional New York Heart Association class II, III or IV), fatigue and ankle swelling.23 Moreover, they list six typical symptoms and four typical signs (including elevated jugular venous pressure), along with ten and sixteen atypical symptoms and signs, respectively, that should raise the suspicion of underlying HF.23 While these are informative, it is important to note that this list predominantly reflects manifestations of acute and more severe forms of HF.28 Moreover, in addition to considering that men and women experience and report the impact of HF differently, a systematic review of the published literature concluded that “there is a more diverse range of symptoms among HF population than has previously been described”.45,46 While primary care patients are more likely to be more stable, earlier in the natural history of HF and/or manifest more subtle signs of underlying cardiac dysfunction, anecdotally many people adapt their behaviours (e.g. becoming more sedentary) to avoid triggering symptoms such as exertional dyspnoea. These may explain why classical HF symptoms and signs such as dyspnoea on exertion (66% sensitivity and 52% specificity) and jugular venous reflux (50% sensitivity and 75% specificity) are imperfect markers of underlying HF.34 Despite these caveats, primary care teams have ready capacity to regularly monitor for any symptoms or signs of HF in high-risk individuals.

Clinical Principle 2

Apply a structured history and physical examination to determine the presence of atypical to typical symptoms and signs of HF. Their absence in a patient with the common antecedents of HF (equivalent to AHA Stage A pre-HF) will not necessarily preclude the presence of significant cardiac dysfunction/asymptomatic HF.24 Thus, further investigation will be conducted.

Interpreting the 12-lead ECG

As recommended by the ESC-HFA, the 12-lead ECG remains an important tool in screening for HF, but with important caveats.34 These have become more relevant with respect to detecting HFpEF (including in many women) and earlier, asymptomatic forms of HF.47,48 It has long been established that a normal ECG is highly sensitive when ruling out cases of HFrEF (historically the predominant form of HF), while demonstrating low specificity overall. In cases of HFpEF its sensitivity is less robust, for the simple reason that a reported 35–45% of affected patients have no abnormalities detected on their ECG.49 Despite these caveats, 12-lead ECG is a readily applied tool (often with automated measurements/alerts) that provides important diagnostic information such as the presence of AF (a US/Japanese study showed nearly all cases with AF have HFpEF), specific morphology and bundle branch blocks indicative of past ischaemic events (many of which may not have been treated/detected) and strain patterns typically associated with left ventricular hypertrophy.50,51

Clinical Principle 3

Perform a 12-lead ECG as a simple and readily accessible tool to investigate possible HF. An abnormal ECG (especially when AF is detected) should elevate suspicion of undiagnosed HF and the potential need for further investigation. A normal ECG indicates the absence of HFrEF but not HFpEF.

Finding Heart Failure with Natriuretic Peptides

The application of point-of-care natriuretic peptides assay (specifically N-terminal pro BNP; NT-proBNP) represents a potentially important tool in the detection of HF in primary care.52–55 Released by cardiomyocytes in the presence of elevated filling pressures, they provide an important non-invasive signal of early to late forms of cardiac dysfunction without specifically identifying the type of HF present.34 As an important caveat to the individual interpretation of NT-proBNP levels, in the setting of clinical uncertainty, the potential for advancing age and comorbid conditions such as renal dysfunction to raise levels – and, conversely, increased adiposity to suppress levels – need to be carefully considered.56,57 However, even when subsidised and recommended as a screening tool, it remains underused in primary care.13 Once again, there are mitigating circumstances. As such, different thresholds for the clinical detection of HF have been proposed (including adjusting for age and sex), while distribution of markedly elevated levels in the setting of acute presentations of HF are different from those observed in primary care.58,59 Accordingly, two commonly recommended thresholds of NT-proBNP that would trigger specialist referral for a HF diagnosis are >125 pg/ml (as recommended by the American Diabetes Association and ESC) and >400 pg/ml (as recommended by the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence in the UK).34,60,61 In a recently reported analysis of 229,580 primary care patients in England screened for the syndrome during the period 2004–2018, 21,102 (9.2%) were ultimately diagnosed with HF.61 Overall, the lower NT-proBNP threshold of ≥125 pg/ml had a sensitivity of 94.6% (95% CI [94.2–95.0]) and specificity of 50.0% (95% CI [49.7–50.3]) in diagnosing HF. In comparison the higher level of ≥400 pg/ml had a lower sensitivity of 81.7% (95% CI [81.0–82.3]), but more favourable specificity of 80.3% (95% CI [80.0–80.5]).61 The authors concluded that, while the higher NT-proBNP levels missed one in five cases, application of the lower threshold required more diagnostic assessments, while being a reliable rule-out test.61 An important caveat in considering these otherwise compelling findings is the potential for lower NT-proBNP levels among obese individuals. In a recent US study with detailed phenotyping and a multicentre, multinational, validation cohort, an even lower NT-proBNP threshold of >50 pg/ml to suspect HF was proposed for people with a BMI ≥35 kg/m2 (given that the sensitivity to HF using a threshold 125 pg/ml fell from 82% to 57%).50

Clinical Principle 4

Routine natriuretic peptide testing in high-risk individuals should be applied as part of any screening strategy to identify cardiac stress and trigger timely investigation for HF. Point-of-care NT-proBNP testing offers a simple, scalable way for primary care teams to rule out HF, detect early dysfunction and prioritise patients for further assessment.

Applying Artificial Intelligence-augmented Portable Cardiac Ultrasound

Transthoracic echo remains at the heart of formally diagnosing HF according to every definition routinely applied worldwide and this is unlikely to change.23,24 Unlike the 12-lead ECG or natriuretic peptides, until very recently transthoracic echo has remained the domain of trained sonographers operating specialised equipment. Critically, this is likely to be the case for many years to come, given the myriad of measurements and evolving techniques used to comprehensively assess cardiac structure/function.31,62 However, the possibility of applying a less complicated transthoracic echo protocol that still generates sufficient information to confirm the likely presence of early-to-late forms of cardiac dysfunction to the full syndrome of HF (Figure 1) has potentially important implications for primary care and those affected, particularly if it lays the foundation for specialist investigation (i.e. acting as a primary care-based triage strategy to complement more comprehensive echo) and earlier application of potentially life-saving guideline-directed medical therapies.21 It is for this reason that, when applied judiciously (including recognising all the caveats around its use), recent technological developments that facilitate the acquisition and interpretation of cardiac images on a portable basis (AI-PoCUS) represent an important development in the detection and diagnosis of early to late forms of HF (no matter which specific definition is applied) in primary care.63

In simple terms, early reports (albeit conducted in ideal conditions) suggest that AI-PoCUS offers three potentially important advantages in the context of detecting and diagnosing HF in primary care: first, the convenience of portable echo probes with AI guidance to rapidly find the correct view of the heart that will generate and capture anatomically insightful cardiac images; second, automated AI synthesis of those images to generate the key indices of cardiac structure and function; and, third, the rapid generation of automated reports/narratives linked to expert guidelines to help identify early cardiac remodelling to more advanced forms of HF.64 Once fully operational and in the hands of appropriately trained personnel (from primary healthcare nurses to general practitioners), imaging and reports can be feasibly generated within a 30-minute window.

However, it is important to consider that while early reports suggest that novices can be rapidly trained to competently and reliably apply AI-PoCUS to generate simple indices of cardiac structure/function, in reality, the type of images required to inform HF detection in primary care will require months of training/experience.63,65–67 Hence, AI-PoCUS, whilst an exciting development, is not perfect. Nevertheless, if proven as a reliable adjunct to current clinical practice (noting the need to deal with false positives and workflow integration in real-world clinical practice), it provides an important opportunity to shift the centre of gravity for HF diagnosis from scarce specialist services to frontline primary care.

Clinical Principle 5

Through guided image acquisition and automated interpretation, the application of AI PoCUS by trained non-sonographers has the potential to rapidly provide key indices of cardiac function/structure. When combined with NT-proBNP screening and clinical assessment, it offers an efficient, clinically robust and scalable method for detecting many more cases of prognostically significant cardiac dysfunction and more overt cases of HF in a single primary care encounter.

Consumers at the Heart of Screening

In considering all the above, it is worth re-emphasising that most patients with HF are only diagnosed when presenting to hospital with an acute decompensated episode/acute HF. Women and those with limited access to specialist services (whether it be due to socio-economic or geographical circumstances) are particularly vulnerable to a lack of timely diagnosis and definitive treatment. As succinctly shown by the STRONG-HF study in the setting of acute HF and soon to be confirmed by the THESUS-HF II study, such factors are not a barrier to better outcomes if the socio-economic and clinical context of HF presentations are carefully considered.68,69 Thus, while the fact that technological innovations may facilitate earlier diagnosis (previous section), their implementation must ultimately be judged through the lens of consumer experience and patient-centred outcomes (this section) that can and should be made on their behalf. From multiple perspectives (including the implications of informing someone they have been diagnosed with a potentially ‘malignant’ syndrome), it is critical to enshrine the position of patients/consumers as being at the very centre of any attempt to detect HF earlier.6 Accordingly, consumers have a clear range of expectations around the optimal approach to screening within primary care settings.70

As reflected in a co-designed Citizens’ Charter for Early Screening of Older Adults for Common Health Conditions in Australia, these include human connection (emotional safety, practical support, respect, dignity, inclusion, cultural/language appropriateness), communication (consent, transparency, simplicity, access to interpreters), continuity of care (co-ordinated services, connection to additional supports), accountability (achieving stated outcomes, meeting expected timeframes, affordability), access (nationwide availability, ease of access, integrated services) and resources (appropriate funding given to programs to implement screening and assessment). While admittedly aspirational, the ideals captured in this Charter set an achievable, actionable benchmark for health care providers and policymakers working in the primary care space when striving to detect and treat HF earlier.

Clinical Principle 6

The needs and experiences of consumers/patients should be placed at the very heart of the early detection of HF in primary care. This includes considering the time taken to investigate and produce definitive results, how they are conveyed and considering all the implications of what might happen next, especially if access to specialist investigation and management remains limited and there is an inability to definitively confirm the presence of HF.

Completing the Puzzle of Heart Failure Detection in Primary Care

As noted, there remains a paucity of evidence (particularly derived from prospective research) to ensure people with HF are found earlier by primary care teams. In response, as part of a funded primary care initiative (‘Strengthening Primary Care’ funded by the Medical Research Future Fund of Australia, 2023/MRF2031996), the authors of this review are conducting the PrActice Nurses to Augment the Clinical Evaluation and cAre of people at high-risk of Heart Failure (PANACEA-HF) Project (ACTRN12624001242516; anzctr.org.au). We proffer it here as an example of the type of research needed to address the problem of finding HF earlier in the primary care setting. This study specifically addresses the chronic lack of timely access to specialist HF investigation and management in many parts of Australia and worldwide.8 In doing so, within the context of real-world clinical teams servicing whole communities who are in desperate need for more proactive and efficient HF detection, it deals with potentially important issues such as clinical competency, governance, proper medico-legal frameworks, workflow constraints for typically time-poor clinicians and the potential to overwhelm already scarce specialist services with false positive HF cases. By necessity, and consistent with a consumer’s perspective, it seeks to avoid much of the dogma and potential demarcation disputes around who can diagnose HF.70

Specifically, aligning with the six principles outlined in this review, the clinical algorithm presented in Figure 2 is being applied across a collaborative network of our primary care clinics servicing geographically and socio-economically diverse communities located in peri-urban to rural areas of Australia. In the process, it and the outcomes it produces (including which individuals are referred to specialist investigation and review) will be reported from the screening of a real-world cohort men and women (target of 700) being routinely managed by their primary care teams who are aged 60 years or more with one or more risk factor for HF (typically hypertension and/or diabetes) and, at minimum, fulfil the AHA’s Stage A pre-HF criteria, but do not have a diagnosis of HF in their medical records.24 Successful implementation of this primary care-focused strategy requires targeted engagement, training and support for all members of the primary care team – including primary healthcare nurses, general practitioners, practice managers and cardiologists who receive referrals based on the results of HF screening.

A key component of this ‘novel’ approach to detecting HF earlier in primary care is the AI-PoCUS technology described earlier in this review. Undoubtedly, the options in this space will grow exponentially and, indeed, they need to become more user-friendly and reliable to truly operate outside of large, well-resourced organisations. In PANACEA-HF, two commercially available, automated deep-learning systems have been paired to firstly acquire and then interpret cardiac images.64,71,72 With automated and manual image acquisition, primary healthcare nurses across multiple sites capture 2D images of the heart’s long axis, apical two- and four-chamber views, colour Doppler and tissue Doppler. Together, these views generate a comprehensive list of echo variables (excepting peak tricuspid regurgitant velocity) to generate a basic but highly insightful report on cardiac structure, systolic function and diastolic function. There are many practical, physiological and technical reasons why AI-PoCUS (even when operated by a skilled operator) will not produce a definitive report – much like that occurs even in the best of circumstances.73 These circumstances (occurring outside of a specialist cardiac centre) necessitate the type of clinical algorithm that our group has purposefully developed for the primary care setting. This, and other approaches, will invariably need to be further tested and validated in different health systems.

If proven successful (after a robust evaluation including health economic analyses), careful consideration will be needed around the funding and resources needed to implement it more widely in Australia and beyond relative to the benefits of finding HF earlier. In this context, a recent report from Scotland estimated that earlier initiation of evidence-based treatment in every 1,000 patients with a high suspicion of HF would result in 84 patients avoiding a (costly) HF hospitalisation or death within 12 months, equating to a number needed to treat of 12 (95% CI [11–14) patients.20 Such favourable figures (given the enormous cost of HF-related hospitalisation and associated mortality) are likely to underpin an economic argument to support primary care teams to more proactively find and treat people with earlier forms of HF.74

Conclusion

HF continues to be diagnosed too late, too inconsistently and too far from the patient’s usual source of care. Primary care represents the obvious intervention point to find HF earlier and then initiate evidence-based treatments to prevent disease progression and poor outcomes.20 However, evidence-based strategies to achieve this are few and far between. Fortunately, there is now an opportunity (through careful application of the available evidence and the integration of new technologies) to re-position primary care (its nurses, general practitioners and teams) as the decisive front line for the early detection, diagnosis and initiation of evidence-based management of HF. As an example of this, the type of clinical algorithm described in this review, establishes an integrated, pragmatic and resource-sensitive pathway supported by modern diagnostics, enabling clinicians to identify HF across its continuum – from ‘heart stress’ without concrete evidence of causality, early to late structural remodelling to facilitating the preliminary identification and then appropriate specialist evaluation/confirmation of HFpEF to HFrEF based on the recommendations of expert guidelines.18,23,24,42 Finally, if such a clinical framework (as presented) were proven to be cost-effective, then routinely implemented, it would be possible to integrate a combination of emerging strategies such as new biomarkers to detect congestion and automated AI algorithms to improve the interpretation of the 12-lead ECG and even echo findings to further improve the earlier detection of HF in the primary care setting.73,75,76
