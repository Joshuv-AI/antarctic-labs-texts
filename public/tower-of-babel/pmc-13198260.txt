A tumor-intrinsic WNT-inhibitory NOTUM program drives immune resistance in microsatellite stable colorectal cancer
Authors: Julian Chua, Arshdeep Kaur, Fynnis Mitchell, Anneke Korver, Parnika Sakthivel, Elleine Allapitan, Mahdin Uddin, Areeba Maqsood, Naomi Chege, Aralia Leon-Coria, Constance AM Finney, Oliver F Bathe, Parham Minoo, Arshad Ayyaz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryImmunotherapy remains largely ineffective in colorectal cancer (CRC), particularly in microsatellite stable (MSS) tumors, which represent the majority of cases. However, the complexity of intratumoral heterogeneity has made it difficult to define tumor-intrinsic programs that drive immune resistance. Here, we identify a cancer cell population that emerges predominantly in advanced-stage MSS CRCs. These cells exhibit stem-like features but aberrantly activate a WNT-inhibitory transcriptional program marked by high NOTUM expression. We term these cells WNT/β-catenin inhibitory cancer cells (WICCs). WICCs are enriched in immune-excluded tumors, correlate with reduced CD8+ T cell infiltration, and are induced in both primary human CRC tumors and patient-derived tumoroids. Selective ablation of WICCs or genetic knockout of NOTUM enhances CD8+ T-cell-mediated cytotoxicity, uncovering a tumor-intrinsic mechanism of immune evasion and nominating the WICC-NOTUM axis as a selective and tractable therapeutic target to overcome immunotherapy resistance in CRC.Keywords: immunotherapy, colorectal cancer, microsatellite stable, cancer-stem-like cells, immunosuppression, NOTUM, WNT signaling, tumor microenvironment, CD8+ T cells, single-cell genomics

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) remains a leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide.1,2 In particular, cases with advanced disease frequently fail to achieve long-term tumor control.3,4 A major barrier to successful treatment is the presence of cancer-stem-like cells (CSCs), which survive therapies, reinitiate tumor growth, and drive metastatic progression.5,6,7,8 Thus, resistance to conventional chemoradiation therapies is generally linked to CSC programs.9,10,11 Recent studies have demonstrated that the CSC compartment in CRCs is highly plastic. Radioresistant “revival” CSCs (revCSCs), upon surviving therapeutic stress, can reconstitute a fresh pool of “proliferative” CSCs (proCSCs), which rapidly expand and drive cancer reinitiation and progression, ultimately contributing to tumor recurrence and poor survival outcomes in CRC patients.12,13

Advances in immunotherapy, particularly immune checkpoint blockade (ICB), have revolutionized the treatment of many cancers, especially microsatellite instability-high (MSI-H) CRCs that exhibit high mutational burden and strong immune infiltration.14,15 However, most CRCs (∼85%) are MSS and remain refractory to ICB therapies, likely due to their immune-excluded tumor microenvironment (TME).16,17 Furthermore, immunotherapies can induce severe immune-related toxicities, limiting their widespread application in CRC patients.18,19 Despite numerous efforts to improve treatment responses, a major gap remains in understanding the molecular programs that underlie immune evasion in MSS CRCs, particularly in the context of CSC populations that have now been shown to promote resistance against immunotherapies as well.20,21,22

Here, we performed integrative single-cell and bulk transcriptomic analyses of CRC tissues to uncover a previously uncharacterized tumor cell population, which we define as WNT/β-catenin inhibitory cancer cells (WICCs), distinguished by elevated expression of multiple extracellular WNT pathway antagonists, including NOTUM, NKD1, and APCDD1. WICCs were found to be selectively enriched in MSS CRCs and strongly associated with immunologically “cold” tumors, marked by reduced CD8+ T cell infiltration and increased lymph node metastasis. Functional validation using murine CRC tumoroid models and in vivo syngeneic assays demonstrate that targeted ablation of WICCs, or genetic deletion of NOTUM, significantly enhances CD8+ T-cell-mediated cytotoxicity and tumor clearance in an adoptive cell transfer setting. These results implicate WICC-derived NOTUM as a key effector of immune suppression.

Results

A WNT-inhibitory transcriptional program emerges in colorectal cancer

We compared transcriptional phenotypes in CRC with those in normal intestines, using single-cell transcriptomic profiles from primary CRC tissues, matched intestinal samples, and healthy volunteer samples, spanning five publicly available human cohorts23,24,25,26,27 (Figure 1A). We utilized Harmony for data integration.28 This approach separated major cellular compartments, including epithelial (both malignant and normal), lymphoid (T cells, B cells, and plasma cells), myeloid (macrophages, monocytes, and dendritic cells), mesenchymal, and endothelial populations (Figures 1B, S1A, and S1B). The sub-structure of the TME and that of normal intestinal epithelia were independently assessed and labeled, by using previously described cell-type annotations.23

We then isolated epithelial single-cell transcriptomes distinguishing cancerous cells from normal intestinal epithelial cells and performed unsupervised re-clustering.13,29 This revealed distinct subpopulations, including revCSCs, two populations of proCSCs, and differentiated absorptive-like and secretory-like lineages (Figure 1C; Table S1). Consistent with previous studies, both proCSC subtypes represented the most proliferative cell populations in human CRC tissues across tumors from all five datasets (Figures S2A and S2B). The proCSC1 subtype exhibits the highest proliferation rate, with over 95% of cells in the S, G2, or M phase. In contrast, proCSC2 displays a lower proliferative index, but still higher than any non-proCSC subtype, with 28% of cells in S/G2/M phases. In contrast, revCSC cells displayed a quiescent or slow-cycling phenotype, with 86% of cells arrested in G1 or G0 phase. Similarly, the vast majority of secretory-like (81%) and absorptive-like (89%) lineages were predominantly in G1 or G0, indicative of their differentiated state.

Our analysis also revealed another distinct transcriptional subtype (cluster #6) that exhibited a relatively quiescent phenotype (79% in G1 phase, 14% in S, and 8% in G2/M) and expressed multiple WNT/β-catenin signaling inhibitors, including NOTUM, NKD1, and APCDD1, which were enriched in cluster #6, with partial transcriptional overlap with several typical crypt base columnar (CBC) marker genes, such as LGR5, SMOC2, AXIN2, and ASCL2 (Figures 1C and S2C). Interestingly, the previously described proCSC state has also been shown to express a CBC gene signature, indicating a widespread enrichment of CBC-associated genes in CRC tissues. Indeed, when we performed standard, non-Harmony clustering on each dataset independently, this analysis revealed that in the datasets from all five individual CRC cohorts, single-cell clusters expressing the NOTUM, NKD1, and APCDD1 gene signature were transcriptionally distinct and formed a subpopulation of cells that also expressed canonical CBC genes, such as AXIN2 and LGR5 (Figure S2D).

Together, this analysis revealed a stem-like transcriptional phenotype characterized by activation of a WNT-inhibitory program.

The WNT-inhibitory program is restricted to transformed epithelium

Several markers of LGR5+ CBC cells were enriched in the proCSC population.12,13 However, these genes also showed partial expression overlap with cluster #6. To further contextualize these phenotypes, we performed a joint clustering analysis of normal and cancer-derived epithelial cells, revealing several notable trends: absorptive-like cancer cells, secretory-like cancer cells, and revCSC cells clustered closely together, whereas proCSC cells grouped with normal CBC cells derived from healthy or adjacent non-tumorous tissues, reflecting substantial transcriptional overlap (Figure 1D). Interestingly, despite partially expressing canonical CBC markers, cluster #6 cells were distinctively grouped away from normal CBC cells as well as any other normal or cancer-derived cell population. Accordingly, several cluster-6-specific genes were almost completely absent from normal human intestinal epithelial tissues and from most components of the TME, although limited expression of some of these genes was observed in certain mesenchymal and immune cell populations within tumors as well as in adjacent normal tissues (Figure 1E). To further validate these findings, we quantified the proportion of normal and cancerous epithelial cells expressing proCSC-, cluster #6-, or revCSC-specific gene signatures within each CRC patient cohort. This analysis revealed that, while the proCSC program was abundant in both normal and cancerous tissues, the cluster #6- and revCSC-specific programs were significantly upregulated in cancerous tissues (Figure S3A). Similar results were obtained when the abundance of cells expressing each of these three gene signatures was projected across individual CRC tumors (Figure S3A). Of note, we found no evidence of significantly elevated expression of NOTUM, APCDD1, or NKD1 in any colonic epithelial cell type from inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) patients with active disease or in corresponding normal colonic epithelial cells30,31 (Figure S3B).

Collectively, these findings suggest that the WNT/β-catenin inhibitory program is selectively induced in cancer cells; we therefore designate these cells as WNT/β-catenin inhibitory cancer cells (WICCs).

WICC program is induced in regionally disseminating MSS-type colon cancers

Our results revealed that WICC program was significantly enriched in MSS tumors, whereas revCSCs were preferentially induced in MSI-H tumors; in contrast, proCSC, absorptive-like, and secretory-like populations were comparably represented across both subtypes (Figures 2A and 2B). In addition, both WICC and revCSC programs were associated with larger tumors; however, only the revCSC program reached statistical significance, whereas the proCSC program was negatively associated (Figure S4A). Furthermore, pathological tumor (pT) staging analysis showed that, while revCSC- and proCSC-specific transcription was abundant across all pT stages, the expression of WICC genes, such as NOTUM, NKD1, and APCDD1, was markedly enriched in pT4 tumors, followed by pT3, and was largely absent in pT2 and pT1 cases, although these differences did not reach statistical significance when assessed as proportions of WICCs at the individual patient level, likely due to limited cohort size (Figures 2C and S4B). We next examined the distribution of the WICC program across nodal (N) stages, revealing that the WICC, but not the revCSC or proCSC, program was enriched in lymph-node-invasive tumors and absent in tumors excluded from the lymph nodes (Figures 2D and S4C). Consistent with these results, N2b tumors exhibited a significantly higher proportion of WICCs at the individual patient level compared with N0 tumors (Figure S4D).

Previous studies have shown that pT3 and pT4 tumors are more likely to be associated with lymph node metastasis.32 Consistent with this, we observed a positive correlation between induction of the WICC program and lymph node metastasis in pT3 and pT4 tumors, with this association reaching statistical significance within the pT4 group (Figure S4E).

We then curated epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EMT) markers across all three CSC phenotypes. As previously shown, EMT markers are consistently enriched in mesenchymal/CMS4 CRCs and in transforming growth factor beta (TGF-β)-driven stromal programs; accordingly, we focused on canonical EMT transcription factors (SNAI1/2, ZEB1/2, and TWIST1), mesenchymal cytoskeletal and adhesion genes (VIM, FN1, CDH2, and ITGAs/ITGBs), matrix-remodeling enzymes (MMP2/9/14), and extracellular matrix collagens.33,34,35 To assess these relationships, we performed patient-level Pearson correlation analyses between mean expression of curated EMT-associated gene sets and the relative abundance of CSC subpopulations, testing all gene-cell-type combinations in a multiple-hypothesis framework. Our results show that, across patients, revCSCs are positively correlated with canonical EMT programs (VIM, FN1, ZEB/SNAI/TWIST, MMP2/9/14, and ECM/adhesion remodeling) and inversely with broad epithelial modules—consistent with a strongly mesenchymal, invasion-biased state with limited epithelial retention (Figures 2E and S4F). In contrast, proCSCs display little to no EMT association and instead align with epithelial identity and junctional maintenance (CDH1, CTNNB1/CTNNA1/CTNND1, CLDNs, and TJP2–3), together with high cell-cycle/proliferation signals (MKI67, TOP2A, CCNB1, and CDK1), marking them as the proliferative engine rather than a motile state. WICCs exhibit an intermediate phenotype: they show EMT-leaning correlations yet exhibit an elevated CBC/progenitor program (LGR5, ASCL2, OLFM4, EPHB2, and AXIN2) and select epithelial adhesions (e.g., CDH1/CTNND1), while some structural epithelial genes such as KRT8, KRT18, and CLDN7 are significantly suppressed. This pattern defines a partial-EMT (pEMT)/hybrid E-M state: epithelial enough to maintain cohesive interactions and lymphatic access, but mesenchymal enough to migrate. However, we found that previously described pEMT gene signatures from head and neck, colorectal, and lung adenocarcinomas were predominantly enriched in revCSCs, suggesting that the pEMT program described here is specific to the WICC state.36,37

Nevertheless, these results suggest a clear difference among CSC states: WICCs maintain an epithelial-stem program within a pEMT identity, proCSCs drive proliferative expansion with minimal EMT, and revCSCs are EMT-skewed and therefore suppress epithelial traits more extensively.

Among other observations, revCSCs were the only cancer cell state with significantly higher proportions in right-sided tumors compared with left-sided tumors (Figure S4G). In contrast, the frequencies of WICCs, proCSCs, and absorptive or secretory epithelial cells did not associate with tumor laterality.

Together, our findings suggest that WICCs are selectively enriched in a subset of invasive, regionally disseminating MSS-type colorectal cancers and are associated with distinct clinical features, including advanced pT and N stages and loss of epithelial identity.

WICC program reflects a cell intrinsic transcriptional state

We next established patient-derived CRC tumoroids (Figure 3A). Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) was performed on two of these tumoroid lines established from primary tumor sites and compared with publicly available single-cell transcriptomic datasets from six additional CRC tumoroids, as well as matched normal intestinal tumoroids derived from adjacent non-malignant tissues and two additional healthy human intestines26,38 (Figures 3B, S5A, and S5B).

Integrated clustering of tumoroids with patient-matched normal organoids showed that the expression of WICC-specific genes, such as NOTUM, APCDD1, and NKD1, was confined to tumoroids and was nearly undetectable in normal organoids (Figure 3C; Tables S2 and S3).

Next, we computed per-cell transcriptomic correlations between individual tumor cells and reference profiles derived from CBC and transit-amplifying (TA) cells (Figure 3D). Our results showed that a substantial proportion of proCSCs and revCSCs exhibited strong transcriptional similarity to the CBC/TA compartment (59% and 55%, respectively). In contrast, only 23% of WICC cells aligned with this compartment, underscoring the transcriptional divergence and potentially aberrant identity of WICCs.

However, WICCs did express few CBC genes, such as LGR5 (Figure S4F). Therefore, we next adopted a gene-expression-driven labeling strategy, rather than relying on unsupervised clustering, to directly test cancer- or normal-epithelium-specific activation of the WICC-associated WNT/β-catenin inhibitory (WNT-inh) program relative to the proCSC program. Using Seurat’s AddModuleScore function, we computed composite enrichment scores for each program: the WNT-inh score was derived from the three typical WICC-associated genes (NOTUM, NKD1, and APCDD1), and an analogous approach was used to quantify the proCSC program based on previously described proCSC gene signature. Cells were then directly classified as WNT-inh-positive or proCSC-positive if their module score exceeded a log-normalized threshold of 0.5, allowing unbiased identification of program-high populations independent of cluster boundaries. This analysis revealed that while higher proportions of proCSC-program-positive cells were detectable in both CRC tumoroids and matched normal organoids (10.75% vs. 19.62%, respectively), the WNT-inh-program-positive cells were almost exclusively restricted to tumoroids (12.75% in tumoroids versus merely 0.24% in normal organoids) (Figure 3E). Notably, a small fraction of cells (2.49%) expressing both programs were detected in tumoroids, but almost none in normal organoids (0.03%), suggesting potential interconversion given the well-known plasticity within cancerous tissues.

Together, these results show that WICCs diverge from normal epithelial lineages and other stem-like cancer cell populations.

The WNT-inhibitory program marks an invasive, tumor-intrinsic cell state enriched in MSS colorectal cancers and is absent in MSI tumors

Next, we assessed phenotypic variability within each of the eight tumor-derived PDOs (tumoroids)—comprised of six MSS and two MSI-H cases—by re-clustering single-cell transcriptomes separately for each PDO (Figures S5C–S5E). Consistent with above-described observations from the analyses of native CRC tumors, where WICCs were found to be selectively enriched in MSS type CRCs (Figures 2A–2E), we found that the WNT-inh program was induced in five of the six MSS type PDOs and was absent in both of the MSI-H type PDOs (Figures 3F and 3G). Specifically, PDOs Tumoroid_08 (67.0%), Tumoroid_96 (61.9%), Tumoroid_92 (60.0%), Tumoroid_28 (46.3%), and Tumoroid_24 (41.5%) harbored prominent WNT-inh-program-positive populations, while Tumoroid_86 (0.59%), Tumoroid_CRCc01 (0.34%), and Tumoroid_CRCc03 (0.05%) did not show sufficient cells induced the WNT-inh program.

Among the six MSS-type CRC PDOs, only two (Tumoroid_28 and Tumoroid_96) were associated with metastatic invasion, both of which were enriched in WNT-inh cells (Figure 3F). To further clarify whether metastasis-associated enrichment of the WNT-inh program arises from primary tumors staged as pT3 or pT4, we examined two additional MSS PDOs (Tumoroid_CRCc04 and Tumoroid_CRCc05) derived from hepatic metastatic sites and compared them with two PDOs established from primary colonic tumors (Figure S5F). Quantitative PCR analysis of NOTUM expression in these four PDO lines, stratified by metastatic status (two M0 and two M1), with each pair containing either a pT2 or pT3 lesion, revealed that both M1 tumoroids displayed a trend toward elevated NOTUM expression; however, statistically significant upregulation was observed only in the pT3-derived Tumoroid_CRCc05 and not in the pT2 metastatic PDO (Tumoroid_CRCc04). In contrast, the expression of LGR5, a CBC marker expressed by both the WICC and the proCSC states, remained comparable across all lines, whereas CLU, a revCSC-associated gene, showed modest but non-significant reductions in PDOs derived from invasive tumors.

Taken together, these findings show that induction of the tumor-intrinsic inhibitory WNT module is a recurrent feature of advanced-stage MSS tumors.

WICC-mediated WNT/β-catenin inhibitory program is associated with immune exclusion and a pro-tumorigenic stromal niche

We found that WICC-positive tumors were significantly depleted of multiple immune effector populations, including CD8+ and CD4+ T cells, natural killer (NK) cells, dendritic cells, and macrophages, consistent with an immune exclusion phenotype. In addition, WICC enrichment was strongly correlated with the expansion of stromal cell types, including fibroblasts, endothelial cells, and mesenchymal lineages—hallmarks of a desmoplastic, angiogenic TME (Figures 4A, 4B, S6A, and S6B). This stromal reprogramming is consistent with WICC population’s intrinsic pEMT state described above (Figures 2E and S4B).

Next, we analyzed additional primary tumor specimens from an independent CRC cohort selected based on pT4 staging and MSS or MSI-H classification. smFISH (RNAscope) was performed to assess the expression of NOTUM, LGR5, and CD8A (Figure 4C), Among MSS tumors (n = 5), NOTUM expression was consistently detected: one high (A9), three medium (A6, A9/181, and A16), and one low (B8). In contrast, MSI-H tumors (n = 5) showed limited NOTUM expression: three very low (A5, A10, and A31) and two none (A11 and B22). This pattern supports selective activation of the WICC program in MSS cases.

LGR5 expression in MSS samples was variable: one high (A6), two medium (A9 and A16), and two none (A9/181 and B8). MSI-H tumors were predominantly negative for LGR5 (three none: A5, B22, and A10), with only one very low (A11) and one medium (A31). CD8A expression, on the other hand, showed a distinct inverse pattern. All MSS tumors exhibited very low or no CD8A expression (three none: A6, A9/181, and A16; two very low: A9 and B8), whereas MSI-H tumors showed higher immune infiltration: one high (A31), one medium (A5), three low (A10, A11, and B22), and none with negative expression.

Thus, the WICC program associates with immune exclusion and stromal remodeling.

WICC-mediated WNT/β-catenin inhibitory program is associated with poor clinical outcome

To determine how the WICC program associates with patient survival, we turned to the TCGA-COAD dataset. Using gene set variation analysis (GSVA),39 CRC patients were assigned specific cancer phenotype enrichment scores (Figure 5A; Table S4). The pseudobulk RNA-seq analysis applied to single-cell data confirmed that WICC-associated gene expression, as measured by bulk RNA-seq, robustly tracks the abundance of WICCs at the whole-tumor level (Figure S6C).

Our results showed that the proCSC signature aligned more closely with replicating stem-like program, while revCSC appeared more divergent, displaying a partially distinct enrichment profile across the patient samples. In contrast, the WICC program showed minimal overlap with most other signatures except for a close—statistically non-significant—association with absorptive phenotype (Figure 5A). In addition, despite not clustering together, WICC enrichment was positively associated with proCSC and proliferating gene signatures, likely due to their transcriptional overlap with the rapidly cycling normal CBC stem cell state.

Next, we assigned specific immune cell-type enrichment scores to individual CRC patient samples and examined their association with distinct cancer-cell-specific transcriptional phenotypes39 (Table S5). Consistent with our observations from single-cell transcriptomics and smFISH-based validation assays, WICC program enrichment exhibited the lowest association with the immune cell types among all identified CRC phenotypes (Figure 5B). In addition, while revCSC and proCSC cells were positively associated with multiple immune populations, including activated CD8+ and CD4+ T cells, WICC cells were negatively correlated with most immune cell subsets, including activated CD8+ and CD4+ T cells. One notable exception was a positive correlation between WICC cells and CD56+ bright NK cells, a subset primarily involved in antimicrobial immunity rather than tumor immunosurveillance, prompting us not to further pursue this observation.

Next, we stratified CRC patients into binary groups based on enrichment status for the WICC, proCSC, and revCSC programs, categorized separately as either positive or negative for each transcriptional phenotype. These patient groups included individuals who had received standard chemoradiation therapies. Survival analysis revealed that, while enrichment of the proCSC program had no statistically significant impact on overall survival, patients enriched for the WICC or revCSC programs showed significantly poor clinical outcomes (Figures 5C and 5D). However, stratification of patients by combined WICC and revCSC enrichment scores revealed no significant differences in overall survival across the four groups (Figure S6D), likely due to limited cohort size. Nevertheless, inspection of survival trajectories showed a trend whereby tumors enriched for revCSC exhibited poorer outcomes, particularly within the WICC-low context, consistent with the fact that these patients were treated with conventional therapies.

Next, we performed Kaplan-Meier survival analyses stratified by MSS, MSI-L, and MSI-H tumors (Figure S6E). While these analyses did not always reach statistical significance, likely due to smaller patient cohort that provides microsatellite status, a significant association was observed in the MSI-L cohort, where WICC-enriched tumors were linked to poorer clinical outcome. This context-dependent effect likely reflects fundamental biological differences between MMR subtypes, wherein immune-evasive, tumor-intrinsic programs exert greater clinical impact in immune-refractory MSS/MSI-L tumors than in highly immunogenic MSI-H disease. These results thus suggest that WICC abundance represents a largely microsatellite-independent indicator of adverse prognosis, including in patients receiving conventional therapies.

These findings further support the prognostic relevance of WICC and revCSC programs and highlight WICC in particular as a transcriptional state associated with poor clinical prognosis in CRC even during standard therapies.

WICC-program-enriched cancers possess a specific mutational profile

Next, we stratified 489 TCGA-COAD patient samples based on the averaged expression of three WICC marker genes NOTUM, APCDD1, and NKD1, defining the top 44% of tumors as WICC-high (Figures 6A and 6B). This analysis revealed APC (76%), TTN (62%), TP53 (60%), and KRAS (46%) as the most frequently mutated genes in WICC-high CRCs. Importantly, TP53 mutations (typically loss of function) and KRAS mutations (predominantly activating missense variants) often co-occurred with APC alterations. To further test which specific mutational combinations are required for WICC induction, we reanalyzed a publicly available single-cell transcriptomic dataset from engineered mouse intestinal tumoroids.13 This revealed that a minimum of three alterations, namely APC and TP53 loss of function, along with activating KRAS mutation (referred to as AKP thereafter), are needed to induce the WICC program (Figures 6C and S7A–S7D). Indeed, the genes Notum, Apcdd1, and Nkd1 are constitutively induced in approximately 12.8% of single cells that are specifically detected in AKP organoids, but not in WT or APC−/− organoids, nor in organoids harboring both APC−/− and KrasG12D mutations. These results are consistent with previous observations demonstrating that AKP mutations in transgenic mice lead to the development of MSS type metastatic colon cancers that associate with immunosuppressive TME.40 Because WICCs associate with a WNT-inhibitory program, we asked whether loss of APC—which renders cells independent of ligand-driven WNT signaling—was required for the emergence of this state. To test this, we attempted to generate KP organoids (harboring oncogenic KRAS-G12D and p53−/− mutations but lacking APC loss-of-function). However, these cultures failed to form viable tumoroids, preventing further analysis (Figure S7E). In addition, we found no significant difference in the distribution of canonical CRC mutations between WICC-high and WICC-low tumors, indicating that conventional somatic mutation patterns alone are insufficient to stratify tumors in WICC-enriched or WICC-deficient categories (Figure S7F).

WICC-high colorectal cancers exhibit distinct mutational features and require a specific combination of driver mutations

We next performed differential gene expression analysis between WICC-high and WICC-low tumors (Table S6). This analysis identified a discrete set of genes that are selectively depleted in WICC-high tumors, including key metabolic genes such as GPD1, DUOXA2, and PDE3A (Figure 6D). In addition, we observed robust upregulation of several DNA-binding transcription factors with established roles in epigenetic regulation and chromatin state control in WICC-high tumors. These included multiple homeobox and pioneer-like transcription factors implicated in developmental patterning, chromatin remodeling, and cell-state plasticity41,42 (Figure 6D).

Together, these results support a model in which genetically advanced CRC sustains the WICC program through coordinated epigenetic and transcriptional networks.

WICC program associates with a distinct signaling network activation

Both WICCs and proCSCs, but not revCSCs, express homeostatic intestinal stem cell/CBC genes that require canonical WNT/β-catenin activity.43 However, WNT-inhibitory program is only induced in WICCs, suggesting that diverging downstream signaling networks might define the biology of these CSC states. To explore this, we turned to human CRC single-cell transcriptomes and ran a theme-guided, preranked gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) on GO:BP and Reactome. As expected, WNT signaling terms were enriched in WICCs and proCSCs but not in revCSCs, validating the approach (Figures S8A–S8C). Beyond WNT, WICCs showed a clear TGF-β/SMAD bias (multiple SMAD/TGF-β terms with positive normalized enrichment score [NES] and better false discovery rate [FDR] than in the other compartments), plus NOTCH footprints—together suggesting a contact/adhesion-sensitive state. WICCs also score integrin/focal adhesion kinase (FAK) and broader cell-adhesion modules and show activity in GPCR/Ca2+-linked pathways (calcium transport/homeostasis, calmodulin/CaMK/ITPR features), matching their vesicular/secretory transcriptome. EGFR→MAPK and some PI3K-AKT-mTOR signals are present across groups but are less specifically elevated in WICCs; by contrast, revCSC shows comparatively stronger nuclear factor κB (NF-κB) and apoptosis readouts (stress/inflammatory bias) while proCSC is strongly associated with DNA synthesis (cell cycle/DNA replication bias).

Thus, distinct signaling networks associate WICC, proCSC, and revCSC states.

Targeted ablation of WICC cells enhances CD8+ T cell activation and eliminates colorectal cancer cells

We next developed an adoptive cell therapy (ACT) model utilizing murine AKP tumoroids and OT-1 CD8+ T cell co-culture system.44 To this end, normal organoids established from C57BL/6 mice were engineered using CRISPR-Cas9 to delete Apc and p53 genes, while simultaneously incorporating KrasG12D-GFP transgene45,46,47 (Figure S9A). Efficiency of Apc and p53 knockout and successful incorporation of KrasG12D-GFP were confirmed by quantitative RT-PCR (Figure S9B).

Previous studies suggested an intrinsic role of Notum in tumor growth.46 Therefore, we targeted Apcdd1 locus to incorporate a dual-function iCasase9-tdTomato (iCT) in AKP tumoroids5 (Figure 7A). Loss of Apcdd1 expression due to accurate integration of iCT cassette at Apcdd1 promoter was validated by quantitative PCR, while targeted Apcdd1 knockout did not impact AKP tumoroid formation or expansion (Figures S9C and S9D).

Next, tdTomato+ and tdTomato− single cells were plated individually in 96-well plates and assessed after 14 days, revealing higher tumoroid-forming efficiency of tdTomato+ cells (18.7%) compared to tdTomato− cells (8.3%) (Figures S9E and S9F). In addition, most tumoroids derived from tdTomato+ cells appeared relatively larger and more complex (Figure S9G).

AKP-Apcdd1-iCT tumoroids were engineered to express the chicken ovalbumin peptide SIINFEKL (cOVA), followed by isolation of cOVA-restricted CD8+ (OT-I) T cells from the spleens of OT-I mice and stimulation ex vivo using anti-CD3 and anti-CD28 antibodies and inflammatory cytokines interleukin-7 (IL-7) and IL-2 (Figures S10A and S10B). AKP-cOVA-Apcdd1-iCT tumoroids were then either treated with AP20187 or left untreated, followed by incubation in the presence or absence of activated OT-I CD8+ T cells (Figure S10C).

Our results show that co-culture of CD8+ T cells with AP20187-treated (WICC-deficient) tumoroids induced structural collapse, a phenotype not observed in other groups (Figure S10D). To quantify this, cultures were incubated with NucRed Dead 647, and tumoroids were scored by apoptotic fraction, categorizing them based on proportions of dead (positive) and alive (negative) cells (Figure 7B). AP20187-mediated WICC ablation alone did not affect organoid viability, while CD8+ T cell co-culture without WICC ablation induced moderate cell death. In contrast, WICC ablation prior to CD8+ T cell co-culture markedly increased tumor cell death, with 72% of tumoroids showing >50% apoptotic cells (NucRed-Dead-647-positive). Consistent with these observations, large numbers of CD8+ T cells clustered around AP20187-treated, WICC-deficient AKP tumoroids, in striking contrast to untreated control AKP tumoroids (Figure 7C).

Together, these data demonstrate that WICCs actively protect CRC cells from CD8+ T cell-mediated cytotoxicity.

NOTUM mediates WICC-driven immune suppression

Re-introduction of recombinant NOTUM into WICC-ablated tumoroid-CD8+ T cell co-cultures increased tumoroid survival, reflected by a shift toward higher proportions of viable tumoroids (Figure 7D). To further test this, we deleted Notum in AKP-cOVA tumoroids using CRISPR-Cas9, which did not impact tumoroid growth (Figures S10E and S10F). Subcutaneous syngeneic transplantation of AKP-cOVA tumoroids (Notum+/+ or Notum−/−) was performed in immunocompetent C57BL/6 mice, followed by adoptive transfer of OT-I CD8+ T cells on day 14 and tumor harvest on day 30 post-implantation (Figure 7E).

Our results revealed that even in the absence of adoptive CD8+ T cell transfer, Notum−/− tumors exhibited markedly attenuated growth and profound infiltration of endogenous CD8+ T cells (Figures 7F and S10G). These results are consistent with enhanced susceptibility of NOTUM-deficient tumors to endogenous immune surveillance but also indicates that loss of NOTUM alone is insufficient to achieve complete tumor elimination in this model. However, adoptive transfer of OT-1 CD8+ T cells resulted in complete elimination of established Notum−/− tumors, which were fully regressed to undetectable levels at resection (red crosses in Figure 7F). In contrast, Notum+/+ tumors persisted, continued to expand, and showed no signs of CD8+ T cell infiltration despite OT-1 treatment (Figures 7E, 7F, and S10G), indicating resistance to endogenous or antigen-specific cytotoxic T cell attack.

Tumors were next classified as WICC-high when WICCs comprised ≥19% of malignant epithelial cells; all others were WICC-low (Figure 1C). We then selectively extracted single-cell transcriptomes of CD8+ T cells from these tumors and performed transcriptional analyses comparing CD8+ T cells associated with WICC-high versus WICC-low primary samples. Expression of canonical CD8+ T cell exhaustion markers was detected in only a small subset of cells, and, moreover, their association with WICC status was non-uniform and gene-specific (Figure S10H). Specifically, expression of PD-1 (PDCD1) and TIM-3 (HAVCR2) was reduced in CD8+ T cells detected in WICC-low tumors, whereas CTLA-4 and LAG-3 displayed the opposite trend.

We next performed differential gene expression analysis, revealing suppression of a broader transcriptional program in CD8+ T cells associated with WICC-high tumors, including genes reported as direct or context-dependent targets of canonical WNT/β-catenin signaling48 (Figure S10H; Table S7). In addition, the expression of many other genes linked to T cell survival, metabolic fitness, and stress adaptation, such as BCL2, TXNIP, MT2A, and S100A6, were also suppressed.

We also found that WNT3A treatment increases the expression of WNT-responsive genes in activated CD8+ T cells (Figure S10I; Table S8). Importantly, addition of recombinant NOTUM to WNT3A-treated CD8+ T cells suppressed the expression of several WNT target genes, demonstrating that extracellular WNT inhibition is sufficient to dampen WNT-driven transcriptional programs in CD8+ T cells. Among the affected genes were TCF7 (TCF1)—a transcription factor associated with stem-like, self-renewing CD8+ T cells and essential for sustained activation, proliferative capacity, and long-term cytotoxic function,49,50 and CCR7, a key regulator of T cell migration and lymphoid tissue homing.51 We also observed suppression of BCL2, an anti-apoptotic gene that promotes CD8+ T cell persistence and has been linked to improved responses to immune checkpoint blockade.52,53

Together, these findings support a model in which WICC-derived secreted NOTUM attenuates WNT/β-catenin-dependent transcription in CD8+ T cells, thereby compromising CD8+ T cell fitness and motility, which in turn limits their ability to detect, infiltrate, and eliminate CRC tumors (Figure 7G).

Discussion

Despite advances in understanding the genetic basis of CRC, outcomes for advanced disease remain poor. Standard therapies, including chemoradiation, are often ineffective due to intrinsic and acquired resistance. Although immunotherapies such as ICB and ACT have transformed treatment in several malignancies, their efficacy in CRC is largely limited to MSI-H tumors. In contrast, the majority of CRCs (∼85%) are MSS and remain refractory to immunotherapy, largely due to an immune-excluded TME.15,54 Approaches aimed at modulating this environment, such as TGF-β pathway inhibition, have shown limited clinical success and are often associated with significant toxicity because of the pathway’s essential roles in maintaining normal stem and progenitor cell compartments.55

In this study, we identify a WNT/β-catenin inhibitory transcriptional program, termed the WICC program, which emerges predominantly in advanced MSS CRCs. Although WICCs display stem-like features, they are transcriptionally distinct from normal LGR5+ CBCs and are characterized by high expression of WNT antagonists such as NOTUM, NKD1, and APCDD1. While WICCs are enriched in immune-refractory MSS tumors, revCSCs, previously linked to chemoradiation resistance, are more frequent in MSI-H CRCs.12,13,56 This reciprocal distribution suggests functional specialization of stem-like states across CRC subtypes, with WICCs mediating immune evasion in MSS tumors and revCSCs driving therapy resistance in MSI-H disease.

Consistent with established CRC EMT frameworks, our gene-CSC state correlation analyses reveal that revCSCs are EMT-high, proCSCs remain epithelial/proliferative, and WICCs adopt a partial EMT (pEMT)/epithelial-stem hybrid program characterized by selective EMT gene activation alongside retained junctional modules, a pattern that aligns with the mesenchymal/CMS4 axis in CRC.34,35 These findings are concordant with prior reports linking CMS4/EMT programs and invasive-front biology in CRC and with emerging pEMT signatures in CRC models, thereby situating WICCs within a clinically relevant pEMT continuum.

WICCs are strongly associated with CD8+ T cell exclusion and lymphatic dissemination. This aligns with prior studies showing that tumor-initiating populations contribute to lymph node metastasis by facilitating immune evasion and enhancing invasive potential.57,58 Further validating this notion, functional studies in murine models demonstrate that selective ablation of WICCs enhances CD8+ T-cell-mediated tumor cytotoxicity both in vitro and in vivo, including in adoptive cell transfer settings where Notum-deficient tumors exhibit robust CD8+ T cell infiltration and tumor clearance. Together, these findings uncover a tumor-intrinsic mechanism of immune evasion that operates independently of stromal-derived cues.

Mechanistically, our data support a model in which WICCs drive immune exclusion primarily through secretion of the immunoregulatory factor NOTUM, directly influencing immune cell function. These findings reconcile prior models of WNT-driven immune exclusion by showing that WICCs arise in tumors with APC-mutation-driven WNT activation and subsequently deploy WNT antagonists to locally suppress immune competence, linking oncogenic WNT activation with spatially restricted WNT inhibition within the TME.59,60

Our results also suggest that epigenetic mechanisms in conjunction with oncogenic mutations may contribute to the immunosuppressive phenotype of WICCs. Tumor-intrinsic epigenetic remodeling has been shown to suppress antigen presentation and attenuate interferon signaling, features commonly observed in immune-resistant tumors.61,62,63 Future studies integrating single-cell epigenomics and spatial transcriptomics will be essential to dissect how chromatin architecture and cellular context shape WICC identity and function within the tumor ecosystem.

From a therapeutic perspective, WICCs offer a cancer-specific and targetable vulnerability in MSS CRCs. Given their selective activation of a WNT-inhibitory program, disrupting WICC-derived effectors such as NOTUM may restore anti-tumor immunity in CRC patients while sparing normal LGR5+ stem cells. Indeed, while pan-WNT inhibition has proven too toxic due to its impact on normal tissue homeostasis, targeting WICC-restricted pathways could provide a therapeutic window for safe and effective intervention.

This study thus establishes WICCs as a distinct immunosuppressive, stem-like population in MSS CRCs that underlies immune evasion, lymphatic dissemination, and therapy resistance. Targeting WICCs either directly or via their secreted products like NOTUM may enable the development of improved immuno-oncology strategies.

Limitations of the study

The precise effects of WICC-derived factors on other immune and stromal populations, such as dendritic cells, macrophages, regulatory T cells, and fibroblasts, remain to be defined. Determining how WICC ablation reshapes spatial immune architecture, cytokine networks, and antigen presentation will be critical to establish the therapeutic potential and mechanistic scope of WICC-targeted interventions.

Future work should also focus on causal testing of WICC-associated signaling pathways and identifying the responsible, therapeutically targetable molecules.

Mouse strains

All experiments involving mice were conducted with the approval of HREBA and LESACC at the university of Calgary. Mice were housed in 12-h light/dark cycles and had consistent temperatures and readily access to food and water. All experiments were done with n = 4 replicates. For both c57BL and C57BL/6-Tg(TcraTcrb)1100Mjb/J 8–10 week old female mice were used for invivo and T cell injection methods.AKP and AKP Notum (−/−) organoids were cultured in vitro and dissociated into single-cell suspensions. A total of 1 × 106 cells were resuspended in 100 μL of Matrigel and subcutaneously injected into the right flank of 8-week-old female C57BL/6 mice. Control groups received 100 μL of PBS as vehicle. Tumor growth was monitored until day 28 in blinded manner, followed by tumor resection at day 30.

Method details

Patient-derived tumoroid model development

Biopsy samples for PDO 1, 3, 4, and 5 were processed within 12 h of resection from the patient. Patient information cannot be publicized due to privacy policies. Tissue samples were placed in Advanced DMEM (Gibco) containing GlutaMAX (1X, Gibco), 100 U/mL penicillin (Gibco), 100 μg/mL streptomycin (Gibco), and 1X HEPES (Gibco). Samples were subsequently washed 5 times with 1X PBS (Gibco) and mechanically dissected using a razor blade in a 10cm Petri dish (Falcon). Accumax (innovative cell technologies) was added to the samples for enzymatic digestion for 30 min. Following 30 min the sample was spun down at 1240 rcf for 5 min. The supernatant was removed, and 5 mL of pre-warmed trypsin was added to the tissue for cell dissociation. The sample was incubated for 5 min at 37°C. This process was repeated an additional time and then added with 10% FBS (Gibco) and the sample was spun down at 1240 rcf for 5 min.

The supernatant was removed, and the pellet was resuspended with Matrigel (VWR). This mixture was plated on 24 well plates (Corning) and incubated for 15 min in an incubator at 37°C and 5% CO2 to allow time for the Matrigel to settle. Human organoid media was added to each well which comprised of Advanced DMEM (Gibco) containing GlutaMAX (1X, Gibco), 100 U/mL penicillin, 100 μg/mL streptomycin (Gibco), HEPES (1X Gibco), 1X B27 (Gibco), 10% FBS (Gibco), 50 ng/mL mouse recombinant EGF (Gibco), 100ng/mL beta-FGF (Gibco), 10 μM Y27632 (Sigma), 1 μM NECA (Sigma), and 1 μM SB202190 (Sigma). Tumoroids were passaged every 7 says for maintenance and were tested for mycoplasma.

Formation of AKP tumoroids

8 week old wildtype mouse tumoroids derived from male and female C57BL/6 mice were isolated from crypts according to Sato and Clevers.64 Constructs to induce Apc and Tp53 knockouts were generated using plasmid #98293 (Addgene) and previously established methods.65 Briefly, sgRNA for Apc and Tp53 were cloned into Plasmid #98293 (Addgene). Plasmid #116423 (Addgene) was used to induce a Kras Mutation in the wildtype intestinal tumoroids. Wildtype tumoroids were mechanically broken down using a 12mL syringe and 16-gauge blunt needle. These tumoroids were then treated with 5 mL of pre-warmed trypsin and incubated for 5 min at 37°C. This process was repeated an additional time, and 10% FBS (Gibco) was added to the sample and spun down at 1240 rcf for 5 min. Cells were mixed with a mixture of 295 μL of Opti-MEM (Gibco), 5 μL of lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen), 1 μg of each plasmid 1X B27 (Gibco), 1X N2 (Gibco), 1mM of N-acetylcysteine (Sigma) and Advanced DMEM (Gibco) and plated on top of 80 μL of Matrigel (VWR) in a 24 well plate (Corning). Cells were left for 16 h and subsequently media was removed and 60 μL of Matrigel (VWR) was added on top. Media containing 1X B27 (Gibco), 1X N2 (Gibco), 1mM of N-acetylcysteine (Sigma) and Advanced DMEM (Gibco) was added, and tumoroids were allowed to grow. Knockouts and mutations were validated using qPCR and tested for mycoplasma.

This method was also conducted following the formation of AKP tumoroids for cOVA truncated protein using the Plasmid #25097 (Addgene). Positive transfections were selected by the addition of 400 μg/mL of G418 (Gibco).

Quantitative real-time PCR

1 μg of RNA was extracted from tissue using the RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen) and was reverse transcribed using a reverse transcription kit (Applied Biosystems). Quantification was done using Sybr green (Bio-Rad) and a CFX Connected Real-Time PCR Detection System (Bio-Rad). Data was quantified using the ΔΔCt method normalized to the expression of Actb.

Single-molecule FISH

Single-molecule fluorescence in situ hybridization was done on paraffin-embedded sections according to the manufacturer’s recommendations, using the RNAscope Multiplex Fluorescent Reagent Kit v2 Assay (ACD). Probes for LGR5, CD8A, and NOTUM were designed by the manufacture (ACD). Manufactures recommendations were followed and TSA dyes (ACD) 520, 570, and 650 were used for LGR5, CD8A, and NOTUM respectively. Samples were mounted and counterstained using Prolong Gold antifade with DAPI (manufacture) and imaged on a Nikon A1R confocal microscope and Zeiss LSM 880 confocal with Airyscan Fast microscope.

Primer sequences used in CRISPR/Cas9, quantitate PCR, and RNA-Scope assays are enlisted in Table S8.

Single-cell preparation and RNA sequencing

PDOs were mechanical dissociated using a 12 mL syringe and 16-gauge needle followed by the addition of 5 mL of pre-warmed trypsin and incubated for 5 min at 37°C. The addition of trypsin and incubation at 37°C was repeated 3 additional times. 10% FBS (Gibco) was added and the sample and spun down at 1240 rcf for 5 min. Samples were washed twice with 1X PBS (Gibco) to remove residual trypsin. Cells were then stained with trypan blue (Gibco) and counted on a hemocytometer to access cell quantity and viability. Samples were run using the 3′ Chromium Single Cell 3′ Reagent Kit (10X genomics) and 10, 000 cell recovery was targeted.

PDO single-cell RNA sequencing data analysis

fastq files were processed through Cell ranger v7.0.1 and sequenced were aligned against GRCh38. Recommended parameters were used. Cell ranger outputs were converted into Seurat objects using Seurat version 4.0.1 and corresponding metadata was added. Additional single-cell transcriptomes of normal human organoids and tumoroids were acquired from GSE166556 and GSE203608.26,38 These samples were converted to Seurat objects, and all samples were merged into one object.

Quality control thresholds for mitochondria DNA percentage and the counts of transcripts were conducted on a case-to-case basis. The top 2000 variable features were identified in this cohort of samples and were used to scale the data. PCA was used with 50 total principal components. The package Harmony was used, and the first 5 embeddings were used for further analysis. Production of the UMAP used 25 dimensions total and the resolution was set to 0.25. Identification of normal and cancer subtype clusters were done using genes highly expressed from previous human CRC single-cell analysis to identify revSC, WICC, Cluster #6 and Normal CBC. These highly expressed genes were also visualized using a Dot plot to identify the percentage of cells expressing the gen and the level of expression.

Human single-cell RNA sequencing data analysis

Samples from GSE178341, GSE201349, GSE132465, GSE166555, and GSE225857 were extracted and samples were converted to Seurat objects using Seurat version 4.0.1 and corresponding metadata was also added. All samples were merged together and filtered to have cells containing more than 400 counts and less than 25% mitochondria DNA. We selected the top 2000 variable genes, scaled the data and selected 50 principal components to run principal component analysis (PCA). To combat batch effect the Harmony package was used to make embeddings.28 Default Harmony parameters were used with 5 total embeddings merged. 1:15 dimensions were used when clustering the data for visualization using 0.25 resolution and Louvain algorithm.

Weighted alluvial analysis comparing tumor and normal stem cells and progenitors

To assess the transcriptomic resemblance of tumor epithelial cells to specific normal intestinal epithelial subtypes, we employed a per-cell correlation-based assignment approach. First, we subsetted the tumor dataset to include cells labeled as WICC, proCSC, and revCSC. Normal epithelial reference profiles were derived by computing average gene expression across single cells annotated as “CBC Cells” and “Transit Amplifying” within the normal subset of the same dataset. These two normal populations were merged into a single reference group termed “CBCs and Transit-Amplifying Cells.”

For each tumor cell, we calculated the Pearson correlation coefficient (r) between its normalized transcriptomic profile and each of the averaged reference profiles. The normal subtype with the highest correlation was considered the most similar match. Cells with a maximum correlation value ≥0.5 were considered assigned to “CBCs and Transit-Amplifying Cells”; those falling below this threshold were categorized as “Unassigned.”

Calculation of WNT-inhibitory and proCSC module scores

To directly quantify transcriptional activity of the WNT-inhibitory (WNT-inh) and proCSC programs at single-cell resolution, we computed module scores using Seurat’s AddModuleScore function. For each patient-specific PDO (also called tumoroid), raw counts were independently normalized, scaled, and processed through PCA prior to score calculation to avoid cross-sample batch effects. The WNT-inh program was defined by a three-gene signature associated with WICCs (NOTUM, APCDD1, NKD1), whereas the proCSC program was derived from a previously described gene set associated with proCSC state (HMGB2, TUBA1B, PCLAF, UBE2C, DUT, TOP2A, H1F5, HSPD1, NCL, BIRC5, NPM1, RANBP1, CDK1, HSPE1, RAN, PCNA, GMNN, RRM2, H2AZ1, DTYMK, SNRPD1, LYAR, LSM2, NHP2). Module scoring was performed using default Seurat parameters, which calculate the average expression of signature genes relative to control genes matched by expression bin. Cells with a module score exceeding 0.5 (log2 units) were classified as positive for the corresponding program. For downstream comparisons of WNT-inh and proCSC enrichment across PDOs and for associations with clinical features, cells concurrently positive for both programs were excluded to prevent artificial inflation of either compartment due to overlapping transcriptomic features.

Single-cell and bulk RNA-seq interface and survival analysis

To investigate the clinical significance of identified transcriptional programs in human CRC, we integrated single-cell and bulk RNA-seq data using Gene Set Variation Analysis (GSVA). Transcriptomic profiles of indicated number of CRC patients were obtained from the TCGA-COAD dataset, all of whom had undergone chemoradiation therapy and had available clinical follow-up data. First, we constructed curated cell type-specific gene sets, representing transcriptional programs associated with five major CRC cell subtypes: replicating, proCSC, revCSC, WICC, and secretory/absorptive. These gene sets were compiled from single-cell RNA-seq–derived metagenes and formatted as a list object in R, with each subtype mapped to its respective gene signatures. To quantify the enrichment of each CRC program as well as indicated immune cell types in bulk RNA-seq samples, we applied the single-sample GSVA (ssGSEA) method using the gsva() function from the GSVA R package.66 Prior to GSVA, the bulk RNA-seq expression matrix was normalized (FPKM values), and gene symbols were ensured to be correct by appending suffixes to duplicated entries. The resulting GSVA scores provided a per-patient, per-subtype matrix of enrichment scores.

For survival analyses, patients were stratified into binary subcohorts for each CSC program based on sample-level GSVA enrichment scores. Enrichment thresholds were defined using score density distributions for each CSC program. Specifically, GSVA scores were first visualized as continuous distributions across all samples, and cutoffs were manually selected at inflection points within these distributions that separated high-density right-sided populations as presentative of tumors enriched for the indicated CSC programs. Thus, patients with GSVA scores exceeding these density-based cutoff were classified as CSC-high (+), whereas those below the cutoff were classified as CSC-low (−). Kaplan–Meier survival curves were generated to compare overall survival between these groups, and statistical significance was assessed using two-sided log rank tests. In addition, Cox proportional hazards regression models were fitted to estimate hazard ratios and corresponding 95% confidence intervals, quantifying the association between CSC program enrichment and patient survival outcomes.

Pseudobulk RNA-seq analysis and correlation with WICC abundance

To relate WICC abundance to tumor-wide transcriptional signatures while preserving patient-level resolution, we implemented a pseudobulk RNA-seq strategy using single-cell RNA-seq data derived from entire colorectal tumors, including both epithelial and microenvironmental compartments. First, for each patient, the relative abundance of WICCs was quantified as the percentage of these cells within all captured cells from that tumor. Patients lacking detectable WICCs were assigned a value of zero. In parallel, raw UMI count matrices were aggregated at the patient level by summing gene-level counts across all cells belonging to the same patient samples, yielding a pseudobulk expression matrix that approximates bulk RNA-seq profiles while retaining single-cell–derived cell composition information. This aggregation was performed using sparse matrix operations to avoid data densification and preserve computational efficiency. Pseudobulk libraries were normalized by library size and converted to counts per million (CPM), followed by log transformation. Expression values for WICC-associated genes (NOTUM, APCDD1, and NKD1) were extracted individually, and a composite WICC pseudobulk score was calculated as the mean logCPM expression of these three genes per patient. Finally, Spearman rank correlations were used to assess the association between WICC abundance and pseudobulk gene expression across patients, enabling identification of tumors enriched for the WICC transcriptional program at the whole-tumor level, including contributions from the tumor microenvironment.

Cell cycle phase distribution

Cell cycle marker genes were obtained through previous publication that mark cells in G1, G2M, and S phase.67 These signatures were plotted on the human CRC scRNA-seq data and cells expressing an average log2 expression of the gene signature were deemed to be in one of the cell cycle phases. Cancer cell subtypes were subset individually and the number of cells in each phase of each subtype was converted to a percentage of the total number of cells of the cell type. These values were then compared in a bar graph to identify cell cycle differences for each cancer cell type.

Differential gene expression analysis and visualization

To identify genes specifically enriched in each single-cell transcriptional cluster, we performed differential gene expression analysis using the MAST algorithm implemented in the Seurat package. For each cluster, a comparison was made against all other clusters in the dataset. Genes were considered differentially expressed if they met a minimum expression threshold of 25 percent across cells, with log fold-change and adjusted p-value filtering applied to define significance.

To generate a comparative gene expression heatmap for tumor and normal epithelia cell types, we extracted normalized expression data from the Seurat object using the FetchData function, and reshaped to long format, and for each gene–tissue pair, we computed the average expression and the percentage of cells expressing the gene. A matrix of average expression values was constructed and Z score normalized by gene. Sample metadata were extracted and matched with tissue categories, with tumor and normal samples annotated accordingly. Hierarchical clustering of both genes and cell types was performed using the pheatmap package to define reproducible dendrograms, while the ComplexHeatmap package was used to generate the final heatmap. Marked gene labels were added using the anno_mark function within the rowAnnotation object.

CD8a+ T cell isolation

Spleens were removed from OT1 mice aged from 8 to 24 weeks. The mouse was then sprayed with 70% ethanol until hairs were drenched. An incision was made in the abdominal area using surgical scissors to expose the peritoneal cavity and the spleen was removed aseptically. Spleens were first cut into three even sections and added to a mixture of 10 mL of RPMI (Gibco) and 10% FBS (Gibco) in a 10cm Petri dish (Falcon). The pieces of the spleen were mechanically crushed using the plunger of a 12 mL syringe. A 70 μm cell strainer (Falcon) was placed on a 50mL tube (Falcon) and 2mL of RPMI (Gibco) solution added to prime it. The resulting solution was filtered using a 70 μm filter (Falcon)to remove excess debris and the supernatant was centrifuged at 300 rcf for 5 min. The resulting pellet was resuspended in 5 mL ammonium chloride (Stem Cell Technologies), approximately 9x the volume of the pellet, and incubated in ice for 5 min to lyse the red blood cells in the pellet. 50 mL 1X PBS (Gibco) was added to the mixture and mixed using a pipette to neutralize the reaction. The solution was centrifuged at 500 rcf for 5 min, and the resulting pellet was resuspended in RPMI (Gibco). A portion of the resuspended pellet was mixed with trypan blue (Gibco) and counted on a hemocytometer to determine T cell quantity and viability.

Following this, CD8a+ cells were selectively isolated using the MidiMACS Separator and Starting Kit and the CD8a+ T cell Isolation Kit (Miltenyi Biotec, #130-104-075 and #130-090-329 respectively), according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

CD8+ T cells were plated on 6 well plates (Falcon) coated with anti-CD3 and anti-CD28 (BD Bioscience). CD8+ cells were added with 4.45 mL RPMI (Gibco), 10% FBS (Gibco), 50 μM 2-mercaptoethanol (Gibco), 1X Insulin-Transferrin-Selenium (Gibco), 1X L-Glutamine (Gibco), 100 U/mL penicillin (Gibco), 100 μg/mL streptomycin (Gibco), 1 ng/mL IL-7 (Invitrogen), and 2.5 ng/mL IL-2 (Gibco). These cultures were incubated at 37°C and 5% CO2.

CD8a+ T cell passaging

CD8a+ T cells culture in 6 well plates (Falcon) were scraped off the bottom of the well to lift T cells. T cells and T cell media was transferred to a 15 mL tube (Falcon) and centrifuged at 500 rcf for 5 min. Supernatant was then removed, and pellet was resuspended in 4.45 mL RPMI (Gibco), 10% FBS (Gibco), 50 μM 2-mercaptoethanol (Gibco), 1X Insulin-Transferrin-Selenium (Gibco), 1X L-Glutamine (Gibco), 100 U/mL penicillin (Gibco), 100 μg/mL streptomycin (Gibco), 1 ng/mL IL-7 (Gibco), and 2.5 ng/mL IL-2 (Gibco). Mixture was distributed onto a 6 well plate (Falcon) and incubated at 37°C and 5% CO2.

WICC ablation strategy

This protocol was adapted from Shimokawa et al.,.5 Briefly, two segments of the Apcdd1 gene (both ∼1 kbp) were selected to be cloned into the Plasmid #129094 (Addgene). The first segment, termed the 5′ homology arm, ended 27 nucleotides upstream of the gene translation start site and extended upstream. The second segment, termed the 3′ homology arm, started exactly at the translation start site and extended downstream. Each of these homology arms were amplified using c57 mouse DNA extracted from mouse tail snips using a (DNA extraction kit). 1 μg of DNA was used with 1 μM of forward and reverse primers with 12.5 μL of High-Fidelity Taq polymerase (Takara Bio) and water topped up to 25 μL. PCR cycles were run using manufacture recommendations.

1 μg 5′ homology arm was digested along with the Plasmid #129094 (Addgene with 1 μL enzymes NdeI (NEB), 1 μL enzyme SacI-HF (NEB), 5 μL of custmart buffer (NEB) topped up with water to 50 μL total reaction. Reactions were left in 37°C for one hour to complete. 1 μg 3’’ homology arm was digested along with the Plasmid #129094 (Addgene) with 1 μL enzymes BsaBI (NEB), 1 μL enzyme SphI-HF (NEB), 5 μL of custmart buffer (NEB) topped up with water to 50 μL total reaction. Reactions were left in 37°C for 30 min and then 60°C for 30 min for the reaction to complete. This would create overhangs for ligation of the arms into the Plasmid #129094 (Addgene). Ligation of each of the arms was done using 50ng of the cut 5′ and 3′ sections of Plasmid #129094 (Addgene) with 12.5 ng of the digest homology arm, 5 μL of 2X Quick Ligase Buffer (NEB), 1 μL of Quick Ligase (NEB) topped with water for a 11 μL total reaction. The reaction was incubated at room temperature for 10 min followed by 16°C for one hour. Ligation product was transformed into StbI3 bacteria (Invitrogen) and manufacture recommendations were followed.

Additionally, methods previously established by Shalem and Sanajan were used to construct Plasmid #98291 (Addgene) with sgRNA targeting the region between the 5′ and 3′ homology arms of the Apcdd1. The desired sgRNA was cloned into Plasmid #98291 (Addgene) and the complete plasmid was transfected alongside the Apcdd1 ablation vector to facilitate the incorporation of the ablation vector through homologous recombination. Selection was done using 4 μg/mL of puromycin (ThermoFisher) for 48 h. Ablation was induced by the addition of 4nM of AP20187 (Sigma) for 48 h.

Organoid coculture

Organoids were passaged and plated with Matrigel (VWR) with organoid media as stated earlier. These cells were grown for 3 days and were put into a 15 mL tube (Falcon) and spun down at 1240 rcf. The supernatant was taken out and the pellet was resuspended in Matrigel (VWR) and 500, 000 CD8a+ T cells that were in culture. 14 μL of this mixture was plated on 10mm circular coverslips (Thermofisher) and T cell media, as described earlier, was added to the well. Samples were cultured for 24 h at 37°C and 5% CO2. Following 24 h samples were fixed and organoid staining procedure was conducted.

Immunofluorescence

AKP ICT organoids were plated in 12 μL of Matrigel (VWR) on 10 mm circular coverslips (ThermoFisher) and grown in AKP organoid media as stated earlier. Samples were allowed to grow for 3 days, except for organoid-immune co-cultures that were maintained for 2 days. For organoid cultures, 1 mL of 1x PBS was added to each well with the addition of two drops of NucRed Dead 647 (Invitrogen) and incubated for 30 min at 37°C. Organoids or organoid-immune co-cultures were then fixed in 2 mL of 4% formalin (Sigma) diluted to 2% (in 1 x PBS) for 10 min; while primary tumors were fixed in formalin overnight prior to OCT embedding and sectioning. Both organoids and organoid-immune co-cultures were washed once with PBST (1× PBS (Gibco) containing 0.2% Tween (Sigma]), followed by two washes with 1 x PBS. Both organoid and in vivo tumor sections were then blocked with 1 x PBS with 1% BSA (Sigma) for 1 h before overnight incubation with rabbit anti-mouse CD8 antibody (Abcam; AB316778). The following day, organoids and tumor sections were incubated with Donkey anti-Rabbit Alexa Fluor Plus 647 secondary antibody (ThermoFisher; A32795) for 2 h. Next, 20 μM Hoechst (ThermoFisher) in fresh PBS was added to the organoids and tumor sections. Wells and tumor sections were washed twice with 1 mL of 1x PBS. Circular coverslips (ThermoFisher) were used to cover tumor sections, and both sections and organoids were mounted with 10 μL of ProLong Gold Antifade (Invitrogen).

In-vivo tumor and T cell injections

AKP and AKP Notum (−/−) organoids were cultured in vitro and dissociated into single-cell suspensions. A total of 1 × 106 cells were resuspended in 100 μL of Matrigel and subcutaneously injected into the right flank of 8-week-old female C57BL/6 mice. Mice were randomly assigned to one of four treatment groups based on organoid genotype (n = 4 per group): AKP Notum (+/+) tumor only, AKP Notum (+/+) tumor with T cell injection, AKP Notum (−/−) tumor only, and AKP Notum (−/−) tumor with T cell injection. Tumor length (L) and width (W) were measured every two days to monitor tumor progression, and tumor volume was estimated using the ellipsoid volume approximation formula.

Mice were observed daily for behavioral or health changes. Fourteen days after tumor cell injection, 1 × 106 OT-1 T cells in 100 μL of PBS were administered via tail-vein injection to the T cell treatment groups. Control groups received 100 μL of PBS as vehicle. Tumor growth was monitored until day 28 in blinded manner, followed by tumor resection at day 30.

Flow cytometry and CFSE assay

For each sample, 1×106 isolated CD8+ T-cells were resuspended in 5 mL of FACS buffer (2% FBS in 1X PBS; Gibco) and centrifuged at 500 × g for 5 min. After removing the supernatant, a staining solution containing 49 μL of FACS buffer and 1 μL of CD8 Pacific Blue antibody (BD Biosciences) was added to the pellet for all non-negative control conditions. The cell suspension was incubated at 4°C for 20 min in the dark. Following incubation, 5 mL of FACS buffer was added, the sample was centrifuged again at 500 × g for 5 min, the supernatant was removed, and the cells were resuspended in 100 μL of FACS buffer for analysis. The cells were run on LSRFortessa X-20 flow cytometer in FACS buffer and data analyzed using FlowJo v10.

After centrifugation and removal of the supernatant, T-cells were resuspended in 100 μL of 1X PBS (Gibco). 0.3 μL of BV510 (BD Biosciences) was added to the suspension and gently mixed by vortexing. The sample was incubated for 15 min at room temperature in the dark. Following incubation, the cells were washed once with 1X PBS (Gibco)and resuspended in 200 μL of FACS buffer for flow cytometry analysis.

CFSE was reconstituted in DMSO, and 50 μL of the aliquoted stock was diluted in 4 mL of 1X PBS (Gibco). T-cells were pooled into a 15 mL tube (Falcon), centrifuged at 500g, and the supernatant was discarded. The pellet was resuspended in 150 μL of 1X PBS (Gibco). 50 μL of the diluted CFSE solution (Thermoscientific) was added to the inner wall of the tube without direct contact with the cell suspension. The tube was then capped and immediately vortexed to mix. The sample was incubated at room temperature for 15 min in the dark. After staining, cells were washed three times with 1X PBS (Gibco). Following washing in PBS, the cells were then stained for viability BV510 (BD Biosciences) and run on LSRFortessa X-20 flow cytometer in FACS buffer.

Quantification and statistical analysis

All statistical and bioinformatic analyses were performed in R v4.3.1. For comparisons of gene expression or continuous variables between two groups, two-tailed Student t tests were applied unless otherwise specified. Kaplan–Meier survival curves were compared using the log rank test. Pearson correlation coefficients were used to assess associations between gene signatures and cellular abundances. Differences in cell-type composition across samples were evaluated using permutation tests. Differential gene expression analyses of single-cell transcriptomic data were performed using the MAST framework. For in vivo tumor growth experiments, tumor volume was approximated using the ellipsoid volume approximation formula: Vest =((π/6)LWˆ2., and longitudinal tumor growth was analyzed using a generalized estimating equation (GEE) model with an AR(1) working correlation structure to account for repeated measurements within individual mice. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05 and indicated as ∗ (p < 0.05), ∗∗ (p < 0.01), and ∗∗∗ (p < 0.001), while all replicates indicated throughout the manuscript represent biological replicates, unless otherwise specified.
