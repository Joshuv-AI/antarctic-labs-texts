Thoughtless use of generative artificial intelligence and college students’ self-directed learning: a multi-group SEM analysis of gender differences
Authors: Hui Zhao, Huijuan Gu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn recent years, generative artificial intelligence (GenAI) technologies have developed rapidly and have been increasingly integrated into educational settings. While students benefit from the convenience and efficiency provided by these tools, a growing tendency toward the thoughtless use of Generative Artificial Intelligence (TUGA) has emerged. However, the specific pathways and underlying mechanisms through which such thoughtless use influences undergraduates’ self-directed learning (SDL) remain insufficiently examined. Grounded in Social Cognitive Theory (SCT), this study surveyed 487 undergraduate students from Henan Province, China, using a snowball sampling approach to collect self-reported data. Structural equation modeling (SEM) was employed to investigate the relationships among the TUGA, self-efficacy (SE), motivation (MOV), and SDL. The results demonstrate that the TUGA exerts a significant negative effect on undergraduates’ SDL. Furthermore, SE and MOV partially mediate this relationship. Multi-group analysis further reveals substantial gender differences in the impact of the TUGA on SDL, MOV, and SE. By elucidating the internal mechanisms through which the TUGA undermines undergraduates’ SDL, this study provides both theoretical and empirical contributions, offering practical implications for higher education institutions seeking to promote responsible AI use and strengthen students’ SDL.Keywords: Generative artificial intelligence, Thoughtless use, Self-efficacy, Motivation, Self-directed learningSubject terms: Education; Information systems and information technology; Psychology; Psychology; Science, technology and society

Introduction

Generative artificial intelligence (GenAI) is rapidly transforming the learning practices of university students. Represented by tools such as ChatGPT, GenAI applications—owing to their powerful capabilities in content generation and human–computer interaction—have become important auxiliary resources supporting students’ self-directed learning (SDL)1. Recent studies indicate that more than 70% of university students have used GenAI tools to assist with academic tasks, with approximately 40% reporting at least weekly use2,3. By providing extensive learning resources, personalized learning recommendations, and efficient problem-solving support, GenAI tools can enhance learning efficiency and broaden students’ academic perspectives4–6.

However, the integration of GenAI into learning processes does not always produce positive outcomes. In practical learning contexts, students’ interactions with AI systems sometimes involve irrational or unconscious usage patterns. One emerging concern is the thoughtless use of generative artificial intelligence (TUGA), which refers to situations in which users adopt AI-generated answers, texts, or ideas without critically evaluating, verifying, or deeply understanding the outputs7. Some students may become overly dependent on AI tools and unreflectively copy generated content into academic work. Such practices may hinder the development of critical thinking and problem-solving skills and may also raise concerns about academic integrity8,9. Although GenAI technologies provide significant learning support, excessive or unreflective reliance on these tools may weaken students’ cognitive engagement in the learning process.

Several related constructs have been proposed to describe problematic patterns of AI use. For example, Fan et al.10 introduced the concept of unreflective reliance on ChatGPT, which highlights learners’ tendency to accept AI-generated responses without sufficient metacognitive monitoring. Similarly, Sardi et al.,11 discussed excessive dependence on generative AI, emphasizing behavioral overreliance on AI tools during learning tasks. Other studies have examined the general use of generative AI in educational contexts12, which mainly captures the extent or frequency of AI usage rather than problematic usage patterns. In contrast, the concept of TUGA proposed by Hou et al.,13 emphasizes a low-reflection cognitive processing pattern in which users automatically adopt AI-generated outputs without critical evaluation or verification. Compared with general AI usage constructs, TUGA more directly captures a maladaptive interaction pattern between learners and AI systems and is therefore particularly suitable for examining potential negative consequences for learning outcomes such as SDL. Although Hou et al.,13 reported that the proportion of TUGA ranges from approximately 10% to 16.7%, this behavioral pattern may still have disproportionately negative consequences for students’ learning processes.

SDL has long been recognized as a key competency in higher education. Existing research on SDL has gradually shifted from a focus on basic cognitive skills toward higher-order thinking and contextualized learning practices. From a theoretical perspective, scholars have examined the role of digital technologies in shaping SDL through multiple frameworks, including the Technology Acceptance Model14, Self-Determination Theory15, Self-Regulated Learning Theory16, and Self-Directed Learning Theory12. Within these perspectives, technology use may influence SDL through mediating mechanisms such as cognitive processes, motivation, and environmental conditions17,18. Among these frameworks, Social Cognitive Theory (SCT) provides a particularly useful lens for understanding learning behavior in technology-mediated environments. SCT emphasizes the triadic reciprocal interaction among personal cognition, behavioral patterns, and environmental factors, suggesting that learning outcomes are shaped by the dynamic interplay between individuals and their technological environments. Prior studies applying SCT in educational contexts have highlighted the central roles of self-efficacy (SE) and motivation (MOV) in shaping students’ learning engagement and autonomous learning behaviors19. Methodologically, these relationships have often been examined using structural equation modeling and experimental approaches to explore how psychological factors influence learning motivation and SDL20–22.

Despite these advances, several important research gaps remain. First, existing studies have largely emphasized the enabling and positive effects of educational technologies, while relatively little attention has been paid to the potential negative consequences of maladaptive technology use behaviors such as TUGA. Second, although gender has been identified as an important moderator of technology-related psychological processes and learning behaviors23, the ways in which gender shapes the relationships between AI usage behaviors, psychological mechanisms, and SDL remain insufficiently understood.

Against this backdrop, the present study aims to examine how the TUGA influences undergraduates’ SDL and the psychological mechanisms underlying this relationship. Specifically, this study addresses three key questions:

This study makes three main contributions. First, by introducing the concept of TUGA into SDL research, it shifts the focus from the predominantly positive narrative surrounding educational technologies toward a more balanced examination of their potential cognitive and motivational risks. Second, by integrating SE and MOV within an SCT framework, the study clarifies the psychological pathways through which AI-related behaviors influence learning outcomes. In particular, the identification of the sequential mediation pathway from SE to MOV provides new insights into how psychological resources shape autonomous learning in AI-mediated environments. Third, through multi-group analysis, this study examines the moderating role of gender, offering a novel perspective for understanding individual differences in human–AI collaborative learning contexts.

Literature review

Social cognitive theory

To establish the theoretical foundation of this study, Social Cognitive Theory (SCT) is adopted as the core analytical framework. Initially proposed by the psychologist Bandura24, SCT conceptualizes human functioning as the dynamic interaction among three core dimensions: personal factors, behavior, and environment. Specifically, the personal dimension encompasses individuals’ cognitions, beliefs, emotions, and physiological states; the behavioral dimension refers to individuals’ actions, decision-making processes, and self-regulatory capacities; and the environmental dimension includes both physical settings and sociocultural contexts. These dimensions do not operate in isolation but form a cyclical system of triadic reciprocal determinism, whereby personal attitudes and beliefs influence learning choices (behavior), behavioral outcomes modify environmental feedback, and environmental conditions, in turn, further shape individuals’ cognition and perceptions24.

With the continuous evolution of technological contexts, the application trajectory of SCT has undergone three key stages: from explaining traditional learning behaviors, to elucidating motivational mechanisms in digital learning, and ultimately to human–AI co-regulation in the age of intelligent agents. This developmental trajectory not only reflects the theoretical extensibility of SCT but also highlights its robust explanatory power across diverse technological contexts. In its early stage, SCT was primarily employed to explain the cognitive, behavioral, and motivational mechanisms underlying learning in traditional educational environments. Research during this period mainly focused on classic topics, including academic MOV, learning performance, and career development25–27. For instance, Schunk and Usher ,26 demonstrated that students’ mathematics SE significantly predicts their use of problem-solving strategies and academic achievement, underscoring the central role of self-beliefs in learning processes. Additionally, goal setting and self-monitoring were identified as crucial mechanisms for promoting deep learning and sustaining learning MOV28.

With the widespread adoption of Internet technologies and the rise of digital learning, the application scope of SCT has progressively expanded to areas such as blended learning, MOOCs, digital literacy, and health-related behaviors29–32. In particular, the large-scale implementation of online teaching during the COVID-19 pandemic stimulated a series of empirical studies grounded in this theoretical framework. For example, drawing on SCT, El-Sayad et al.,29 investigated the mechanisms through which online learning engagement influenced learning satisfaction among Egyptian university students during the pandemic. Their findings indicated that teaching presence and academic SE were critical factors in enhancing students’ behavioral and emotional engagement, with emotional engagement subsequently emerging as a central construct influencing learning satisfaction. Research at this stage not only validated the applicability of SCT in non-traditional learning environments but also deepened scholarly understanding of the dynamic interplay among technological environments, individual cognition, and behavioral regulation.

In recent years, with the rapid advancement of artificial intelligence technologies, the application of SCT has been further extended to emerging topics such as digital health interventions, sustainable development, and human–machine collaboration33–36. Researchers have increasingly focused on GenAI as a novel environmental factor and examined how it interacts with the cognitive mechanisms and learning behaviors of university students. For example, Shahzad et al.36 reported that generative AI technologies—huge language models (LLMs)—based tools, such as ChatGPT and conversational chatbots, significantly influence students’ learning performance through mechanisms including SE, perceived fairness and ethics, and creativity. Taken together, SCT offers a robust and integrative theoretical lens for understanding the psychological and behavioral mechanisms underlying human–AI interaction in AI-mediated learning environments.

Despite the continuous expansion of its application domains and the substantial progress achieved, the limitations of SCT have become increasingly evident as technological environments grow more complex and emerging technologies evolve at a rapid pace. First, within its traditional framework, SCT has broadly conceptualized environmental factors in terms of physical settings and conventional sociocultural contexts. However, in technology-rich environments dominated by GenAI, technology itself exhibits dynamic, autonomous, and interactive characteristics. Features such as real-time feedback and personalized content generation, enabled by large language models, extend beyond the static conception of the environment embedded in classical SCT, rendering the theory somewhat lagging in its explanation of the evolving “technology–cognition–behavior” interaction processes37. Second, existing SCT-based studies have predominantly focused on individual-level variables, such as SE and self-regulation, while investigations into the underlying mechanisms of gender differences remain fragmented. In particular, there is a notable lack of systematic theoretical support for explaining differentiated pathways across gender groups in terms of cognitive beliefs, behavioral decision-making, and environmental perceptions within the context of GenAI use38.

Therefore, to address the limitations above, SCT requires theoretical extension so that it can better accommodate the novel technological environments constructed by GenAI and provide more systematic explanations for phenomena such as gender differences within these contexts. Building on these theoretical constraints, the present study incorporates the TUGA into the SCT framework to examine the role of this behavior within the triadic interaction system. Specifically, the triadic reciprocal model of SCT enables the integrative consideration of thoughtless use behavior, personal factors such as students’ SE and MOV, and the environmental conditions constituted by GenAI, forming a dynamic system that elucidates how these elements jointly influence SDL. Moreover, SCT’s emphasis on individual differences in cognition and beliefs provides a robust theoretical basis for interpreting the gender differences observed in this study, thereby offering insights into the differentiated patterns of GenAI use and SDL across male and female students. Accordingly, under an extended SCT framework, this study employs multi-group analysis to systematically investigate the effects of the TUGA on SDL and the associated gender differences. By uncovering the underlying mechanisms of these effects, the study aims to contribute meaningful theoretical insights and empirical evidence for the application and advancement of SCT in emerging technology-enhanced learning environments.

Self-directed learning

SDL refers to an individual’s capacity to independently, proactively, and consciously accomplish learning tasks and is regarded as a core competence in modern education and lifelong learning39. Existing research on SDL spans multiple domains, including online learning40, metacognition and learning41, and self-regulated learning42. Prior studies have examined this construct from diverse disciplinary perspectives. For instance, research in the fields of metacognition and learning strategies emphasizes learners’ monitoring of their own cognitive processes and the application of optimization strategies. In contrast, self-regulated learning research conceptualizes SDL as a dynamic, cyclical system encompassing goal setting, process monitoring, and motivational regulation. With the continued evolution of technological environments, the online learning literature has increasingly focused on how features of online learning environments—such as platform functionalities, resource formats, and interaction patterns—influence learners’ ability to autonomously set goals, manage learning processes, and evaluate learning outcomes, as well as how instructional design optimization can enhance SDL efficiency. In recent years, artificial intelligence has emerged as a new research frontier in this domain. Ng and Sing,43 noted that artificial intelligence can exert a profound influence on students’ SDL by providing personalized learning resources and enhancing learners’ autonomy in the learning process. Collectively, these studies elucidate the formation mechanisms and influencing factors of SDL from multiple perspectives, offering a solid foundation for understanding its development across diverse learning contexts.

Although SDL is closely related to self-regulated learning (SRL), the two constructs emphasize different aspects of learner autonomy. SRL primarily focuses on learners’ cognitive and metacognitive regulation processes within specific learning tasks, including planning, monitoring, and strategy adjustment. In contrast, SDL represents a broader construct that refers to learners’ overall capacity to independently initiate, manage, and evaluate their learning activities across different learning contexts. In this sense, SRL can be regarded as an important process-level component within the broader framework of SDL. The present study adopts SDL as the focal outcome variable because it captures learners’ comprehensive autonomy in AI-supported learning environments rather than only their task-level regulatory strategies.

Current research on SDL has drawn on a variety of theoretical frameworks, including Self-Determination Theory44, Cognitive Learning Theory45, Self-Regulated Learning Theory46, and Social Cognitive Theory47. Among these theoretical perspectives, SCT offers a remarkably comprehensive analytical lens by emphasizing the triadic reciprocal determinism among personal factors, behavior, and environmental influences. For example, grounded in SCT, Ponton and Rhea47 empirically demonstrated that learners’ SE and levels of self-regulatory strategies have a significant positive effect on their SDL. A key strength of SCT lies in its capacity to move beyond purely psychological or environmental determinism, providing a dynamic explanation of how internal beliefs, external influences, and learning behaviors continuously interact to shape the development of SDL jointly.

Existing research has systematically examined the driving mechanisms of SDL from two complementary dimensions: internal individual factors and external environmental factors. First, internal personal factors serve as the intrinsic engine of SDL, encompassing elements such as intrinsic interest48, goal setting39, and self-motivation41. Among these factors, SE and intrinsic goal orientation provide learners with the initial impetus and sustained engagement in learning activities. At the same time, metacognitive abilities enable them to plan, monitor progress, and evaluate outcomes, thereby facilitating active regulation of the learning process. Second, external environmental factors provide the social and behavioral supports that promote SDL. These factors encompass teacher support49, peer interaction50, curriculum design51, technological platforms52, as well as learning strategies related to information access53 and time management54. By providing guidance, resources, and opportunities for practice, these external elements collectively shape and influence learners’ self-regulatory behaviors. Taken together, SDL emerges from the dynamic interaction between individuals’ internal motivational drivers and external environmental supports. This integrative framework provides a robust theoretical foundation for further investigating how specific contextual variables—such as GenAI—affect the development of SDL.

However, current research on SDL is subject to several notable limitations. First, existing studies have largely overlooked the potential negative impact of the TUGA as a specific behavioral tendency on SDL. Research examining the educational effects of technology has predominantly focused on the enabling and supportive roles of GenAI, with limited critical attention paid to how excessive reliance on such tools may undermine students’ metacognitive processes and self-regulatory capacities. Second, the existing literature has paid insufficient attention to gender as a key individual difference variable when examining relevant influencing factors. As a result, it has failed to systematically uncover potential gender-based differences in technology use patterns, self-regulatory strategies, and intrinsic MOV, thereby constraining the generalizability of findings and the precision of educational interventions. By focusing on the relationship between the TUGA and undergraduates’ SDL, and by incorporating gender as a moderating variable through multi-group analysis, this study seeks to address these research gaps and provide more nuanced and differentiated empirical evidence for understanding the development of SDL in the era of artificial intelligence.

Hypothesis

Thoughtless use of GenAI and self-directed learning

Existing research indicates that the TUGA is a significant negative predictor of SDL. For example, Fan et al.10 examined university students’ performance on writing tasks under different forms of learning support (ChatGPT, human experts, writing-check tools, and no support). They found that unreflective reliance on ChatGPT during writing led to metacognitive laziness and reduced self-monitoring, thereby weakening students’ SDL. Similarly, Sardi et al.11 reported that excessive dependence on GenAI may deprive some students of opportunities for independent thinking, consequently hindering the development of their SDL. Based on the existing literature, this study proposes the following hypothesis:

H1

The TUGA has a significant negative effect on undergraduates’ SDL.

Thoughtless use of GenAI, self-efficacy, and self-directed learning ability

SE refers to an individual’s belief in their ability to perform specific tasks and achieve desired goals successfully55. Prior research has shown that the TUGA exerts a significant negative influence on SE. For instance, Zhang et al.56 found that TUGA not only directly undermines students’ academic SE but also produces additional detrimental effects by fostering cognitive laziness, inhibiting creativity, and weakening critical and independent thinking abilities. Moreover, Li et al.57 conducted a quasi-experimental study among physical education majors comparing programming learning outcomes with and without the use of generative AI. Their findings indicated that although the unreflective use of GenAI could superficially enhance students’ programming SE by enabling rapid problem-solving, this apparent improvement was primarily grounded in dependence on external tools rather than genuine skill acquisition. As a result, such cognitive offloading may impair long-term knowledge mastery and confidence, ultimately leading to a decline in SE. Based on the existing evidence, this study proposes the following hypothesis:

H2

The TUGA has a significant negative effect on undergraduates’ SE.

Existing literature indicates that SE is a significant positive predictor of SDL. For example, Ou et al.58 found that AI-assisted practice applications enhanced students’ SE by providing support mechanisms such as real-time feedback, goal setting, and self-monitoring, which in turn significantly promoted their SDL. In addition, Liang et al.59 examined the effects of students’ interactions with GenAI on learning achievement. They reported that SE enhanced learning achievement by increasing students’ levels of cognitive engagement. Cognitive engagement, which reflects deeper thinking and greater involvement in problem-solving during the learning process, indirectly strengthens students’ SDL. Based on the existing evidence, this study proposes the following hypotheses:

H3

Undergraduates’ SE has a significant positive effect on SDL.

H4

SE mediates the relationship between the TUGA and SDL among undergraduates.

Thoughtless use of GenAI, motivation, and self-directed learning

MOV refers to the psychological processes and driving forces that energize individuals to initiate behavior and persist in striving toward specific goals60. Existing studies suggest a significant negative association between TUGA and MOV. For instance, Dong et al.61 found, in their investigation of the misuse of GenAI in programming instruction, that TUGA undermines students’ intrinsic learning MOV by weakening their SDL and reducing task challenge and feelings of accomplishment. Similarly, Im et al.62 examined how artificial intelligence influences students’ learning MOV in the digital era. They demonstrated that TUGA leads to declines in critical thinking ability, diminishes learners’ MOV for autonomous analysis and creative thinking, and consequently directly reduces learning MOV.

Based on the existing literature, this study proposes the following hypothesis:

H5

The TUGA has a significant negative effect on undergraduates’ MOV.

Existing research demonstrates that MOV is a significant positive predictor of undergraduates’ SDL. For example, Lin et al.63 investigated the impact of autonomous MOV on knowledge-based short-video learning contexts. They found that autonomous MOV—particularly intrinsic MOV—significantly enhances learners’ SDL by strengthening learning interest, goal identification, and self-driven engagement. Similarly, Siziba et al.64 investigated the mechanisms through which learning MOV influences SDL among students in township schools in South Africa. Their findings indicated that MOV can indirectly promote the development of SDL through coordinated support from family, school, and broader social systems. Based on the existing evidence, this study proposes the following hypotheses:

H6

Undergraduates’ MOV has a significant positive effect on SDL.

H7

MOV mediates the relationship between the TUGA and SDL among undergraduates.

Thoughtless use of GenAI, self-efficacy, motivation, and self-directed learning

A growing body of research has identified a significant positive association between SE and MOV in the context of GenAI use. For example, Sung et al.65 analyzed the intervention effects of personalized affective–motivational feedback provided by generative AI and found that AI-generated emotionally supportive feedback—such as affirming language and reflections on prior success experiences—strengthened learners’ SE, which in turn enhanced both intrinsic MOV (e.g., goal persistence) and extrinsic MOV (e.g., task engagement) when facing learning challenges. In addition, Bai and Phromphithakkul66 investigated the interactive learning behaviors of Chinese university students with GenAI. They reported that using GenAI as a learning aid significantly increased students’ learning-related SE, which subsequently stimulated learning MOV and ultimately improved academic performance. Based on the existing literature, this study proposes the following hypotheses:

H8

Undergraduates’ SE has a significant positive effect on MOV.

H9

SE and MOV jointly mediate the relationship between the TUGA and SDL among undergraduates.

Gender differences in the relationships among the thoughtless use of GenAI, self-efficacy, motivation, and self-directed learning

Existing research suggests that gender plays a significant role in the relationship between the careless use of GenAI and undergraduates’ SE. For example, Cachero et al.67 examined gender bias in university students’ perceptions of artificial intelligence, perceived impacts, and attitudes toward AI support. Their findings revealed that female students reported significantly lower self-assessments than male students in terms of AI-related knowledge, application skills, and confidence. Such lower self-perceptions may lead female students to experience reduced confidence when engaging with AI technologies. Similarly, Møgelvang et al.68 investigated gender differences in the use of GenAI chatbots in higher education, examining their associated characteristics and effects. The results showed that male students were more likely to view generative AI as a tool for enhancing efficiency and to use it extensively to strengthen their technological SE. In contrast, female students tended to place greater emphasis on the credibility and critical use of GenAI. While this orientation helps preserve cognitive autonomy, it may simultaneously constrain the accumulation of technological confidence. Based on the existing literature, this study proposes the following hypothesis:

H10

The effect of the TUGA on undergraduates’ SE differs significantly by gender.

Existing studies suggest that gender moderates the relationship between the TUGA and undergraduates’ MOV. For example, Xia et al.69 demonstrated that, in AI-assisted learning contexts, the pathways through which autonomy, competence, and relatedness influence SDL are significantly moderated by gender. Specifically, when female students experience lower satisfaction of autonomy and competence needs, motivational variables exhibit a more substantial predictive effect on SDL, whereas male students display different pathway patterns. In addition, drawing on Diffusion of Innovations theory, Raman et al.70 investigated the factors influencing undergraduates’ adoption of ChatGPT and found notable gender differences. Female students were more likely to have their usage MOV stimulated by relative advantage and trialability, reflecting a stronger emphasis on practical value and controllable user experience. In contrast, male students were more strongly driven by compatibility and observability, indicating greater sensitivity to technological fit and social visibility. Based on the existing evidence, this study proposes the following hypothesis:

H11

The effect of the TUGA on undergraduates’ MOV differs significantly by gender.

Existing research has shown that the relationship between the TUGA and undergraduates’ SDL exhibits significant gender differences. For example, drawing on Self-Determination Theory, Xia et al.69 found that female students are more likely to experience satisfaction of autonomy and competence needs under AI-supported learning conditions. Such satisfaction exerts a more substantial positive effect on their SDL, particularly when their prior AI knowledge is relatively limited. Similarly, Csizér and Albert71 reported that gender influences the pathways through which affective and motivational variables impact SDL. Their findings indicated that female students are more susceptible to the effects of anxiety, whereas male students are more strongly influenced by emotions such as pride and shame. Based on the existing literature, this study proposes the following hypothesis:

H12

The effect of the TUGA on undergraduates’ SDL differs significantly by gender.

Methods

Sample and data

Before completing the questionnaire, participants were provided with detailed information about the study, including its purpose and measures for protecting participant privacy. Only those who provided written informed consent were allowed to proceed with the survey, and participants retained the right to withdraw at any time. This study was reviewed and approved by the Human Research Ethics Committee of Zhoukou Normal University (Approval No. ZNU-2025-05-0007) and complied with the Declaration of Helsinki. The survey was conducted on the Wenjuanxing platform (https://www.wjx.cn/) from June 10 to August 12, 2025. Wenjuanxing was selected because it enables the rapid collection of large-scale data, supports multi-platform distribution, and allows for real-time monitoring of survey responses. A snowball sampling method was employed during the data collection process to ensure both the quantity and quality of responses, with participants encouraged to invite other eligible respondents to participate. Additionally, the platform’s response time-tracking function was utilized to monitor and assess data quality. Respondents who completed the survey and provided valid responses received a random cash incentive.

After the necessary preparatory procedures were completed, data were collected from 598 undergraduate students across four universities in Henan Province, China. To ensure sample quality, this study followed the guidelines recommended by Wu et al.72 and applied two criteria to screen the questionnaires. First, a pilot test indicated that the questionnaire usually required 1–5 min to complete. Responses completed in less than 60 s were considered to reflect careless or rushed participation and were therefore deemed invalid. Second, questionnaires in which all items were answered identically were also excluded from further analysis. Following this rigorous screening process, 111 invalid questionnaires were removed, resulting in a final sample of 487 valid responses for subsequent analysis. In the proposed research model, the maximum number of arrows pointing to any endogenous latent construct was three. According to Hair73, to achieve a minimum explanatory power of R2 = 0.10 at the 1% significance level, a minimum sample size of 176 is required. The final sample of 487 participants in this study substantially exceeds this threshold, thereby ensuring the robustness and reliability of the empirical results. In the context of this study, GenAI tools commonly used by participants included conversational AI systems such as ChatGPT and similar large language model–based platforms. Students typically used these tools for various academic purposes, including generating ideas for assignments, obtaining explanations of complex concepts, assisting with writing tasks, and solving academic problems. These usage patterns reflect the increasing integration of generative AI technologies into students’ everyday learning environments. From the perspective of SCT, such AI-supported learning environments constitute an important contextual factor that may shape learners’ cognitive beliefs and behavioral patterns, thereby influencing their self-directed learning processes.

Among the 487 valid questionnaires, 105 respondents were male (21.6%), and 382 were female (78.4%). In terms of academic year, 122 participants were freshmen (25.1%), 124 were sophomores (25.5%), 207 were juniors (42.5%), and 34 were seniors (7.0%). With respect to disciplinary background, 222 students majored in the humanities (45.6%), 72 in the social sciences (14.8%), and 193 in the natural sciences (39.6%) (Table 1). This representative sample provides a solid foundation for examining the effects of the TUGA on undergraduates’ SDL. The diversity and size of the sample contribute to the generalizability of the findings to a broader undergraduate population within the study context.

Measurement instruments

The measurement instruments consisted of two main sections. The first section collected participants’ demographic information, while the second section gathered self-reported data on the study constructs. All constructs were assessed using previously validated scales, which were appropriately adapted to align with the research context and objectives. These adaptations were made to more accurately capture participants’ actual experiences and perceptions within the specific context of this study.

In addition to basic demographic information, the measurement instrument comprised four key constructs: TUGA, SE, MOV, and SDL. Compared with a 7-point Likert scale, a 5-point Likert scale has been shown to offer notable advantages in enhancing scale reliability and validity74, reducing response bias, and increasing the sensitivity of statistical analyses75. Accordingly, all constructs were measured using a 5-point Likert scale, with response options ranging from (1) “strongly disagree” to (5) “strongly agree.”

The TUGA scale was adapted from Hou et al.13. It was designed to assess undergraduates’ automatic reliance on generative AI during learning, particularly their lack of purposefulness and reflective engagement when using such tools. The scale consists of four items (e.g., “I copy learning tasks or problem statements into generative AI to seek answers or ideas”). The scale demonstrated good reliability, with a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.899.

The SE scale, adapted from Kim and Kim76, was used to assess undergraduates’ beliefs regarding their learning capabilities, problem-solving abilities, and perceived control over learning outcomes during the process of SDL. The scale comprises four items (e.g., “I believe that I can independently master complex learning content”). The reliability of the scale was satisfactory, with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.85.

The SDL scale, adapted from Han77, was used to evaluate undergraduates’ self-management skills, goal-setting capacity, use of learning strategies, and levels of self-monitoring in learning. The scale consists of nine items (e.g., “I actively check my understanding of the learning content to ensure that I have grasped the key points”). The scale demonstrated excellent reliability, with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.941.

The MOV scale, adapted from González-Mohíno et al.78, was designed to measure undergraduates’ levels of intrinsic and extrinsic MOV in SDL, including their subjective perceptions of learning interest, perceived value, achievement MOV, and sensitivity to external rewards and sanctions. The scale comprises five items (e.g., “I am curious about and interested in the content of self-directed learning”). The scale demonstrated good reliability, with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.909.

Data analysis

This study adopted a two-stage analytical approach to test the hypotheses and develop the predictive model. In the first stage, partial least squares structural equation modeling (PLS-SEM) was employed to identify the linear relationships between exogenous and endogenous variables. Considering the prediction-oriented objective of this study and the relatively complex model incorporating multiple mediating mechanisms and multi-group analysis, PLS-SEM was deemed an appropriate analytical approach. PLS-SEM is particularly suitable for research focusing on prediction and variance explanation of endogenous constructs, whereas covariance-based SEM (CB-SEM) is typically used for theory confirmation and model fit evaluation79–81.

In addition, PLS-SEM offers methodological advantages in handling non-normally distributed data and complex research models73. The present study is based on a sample of 487 participants and includes four constructs measured by 22 items, forming a relatively complex analytical framework82. Therefore, PLS-SEM was considered a robust and appropriate technique for the analysis.

In the second stage, to more thoroughly examine the moderating role of gender, this study incorporated multi-group analysis (MGA). MGA is an effective technique for assessing moderating effects within research models and is widely used to test differences across categorical variables, such as gender and nationality83. Moreover, prior research has highlighted the strengths of MGA in uncovering group heterogeneity and in analyzing complex models with relatively small samples84,85. By integrating SEM with MGA, this study enhances the depth and precision of the data analysis, enabling a more comprehensive understanding of the effects of the TUGA on undergraduates’ SDL, as well as the moderating role of gender.

Results

Measurement model

The measurement model was evaluated using the following criteria.

Step 1: Indicator reliability: Indicator reliability was assessed by examining outer loadings, which should be equal to or greater than 0.7086.

Step 2: Internal consistency reliability: Internal consistency reliability was evaluated using two indices: Cronbach’s alpha (α), McDonald’s omega (ω) and composite reliability (CR). Cronbach’s alpha is the traditional measure of internal consistency, whereas McDonald’s omega provides a more accurate estimate of reliability under congeneric measurement models. Composite reliability is widely recommended in PLS-SEM because it does not assume equal indicator loadings. For all three indices, values of 0.70 or higher are considered acceptable85,87.

Discussion

Grounded in SCT, this study employed SEM to examine the pathways through which the TUGA influences undergraduates’ SDL. The findings indicate that TUGA not only exerts a direct effect on SDL but also indirectly affects it through two mediating variables—MOV and SE. Moreover, significant gender differences were observed in the effects of TUGA on SE, MOV, and SDL. These results not only support the proposed hypotheses but also extend the existing literature in multiple dimensions. This section systematically addresses the research questions from both theoretical mechanism and empirical validation perspectives and further discusses the study’s theoretical contributions, practical implications, and directions for future research.

First, this study found that the TUGA has a significant and direct negative effect on undergraduates’ SDL (p < .001), which is consistent with prior findings11. From an SCT perspective, thoughtless use, as a form of learning behavior, interacts negatively with students’ personal factors (e.g., SE and metacognitive skills) and the technology-mediated learning environment. Specifically, such behavior deprives students of critical opportunities to build academic SE through overcoming cognitive challenges, thereby weakening their core beliefs about their own learning capabilities. Moreover, it short-circuits essential metacognitive processes required for SDL—such as planning, monitoring, and reflection—preventing students from developing effective self-regulatory strategies. Finally, the immediate-feedback environment provided by generative AI behaviorally reinforces dependence on rapid answers rather than sustained cognitive engagement, thereby creating a learning environment that suppresses, rather than promotes, SDL. Accordingly, from an SCT standpoint, this instrumental form of “thoughtless use” undermines undergraduates’ SDL through its negative interactions with personal cognition and the learning environment, eroding SE, distorting learning MOV, and fostering an environment that inhibits deep thinking and metacognitive engagement.

Further analysis revealed that the effect of the TUGA on SDL is primarily transmitted through two significant mediating pathways: learning MOV (p < .001) and SE (p = .012). This finding is consistent with prior research93 and can be interpreted through the lens of SCT’s triadic reciprocal determinism. Specifically, thoughtless use does not directly “eliminate” students’ SDL; instead, it erodes critical internal psychological resources that underpin autonomous learning. The capacity of generative AI to provide immediate and complete solutions deprives students of opportunities to accumulate mastery experiences through exploration and problem-solving, thereby directly undermining their belief in their ability to handle learning tasks (i.e., SE) successfully. At the same time, this readily accessible problem-solving mode transforms intrinsic learning motives (such as curiosity and interest) into instrumental pursuits of external outcomes (e.g., completing assignments quickly), leading to a substantial decline in learning MOV. Ultimately, these weakened personal factors—lower SE and diminished MOV—feed back into students’ behavioral patterns, reducing their willingness and confidence to set goals and persist in effort proactively. Collectively, this dynamic manifests as a decline in overall SDL.

Second, the results reveal a significant sequential mediation pathway (p = .003), whereby the TUGA reduces SE, which in turn weakens learning MOV and ultimately diminishes SDL. This finding is consistent with the conclusions of Wu et al.94, who integrated the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) and the Theory of Reasoned Action in their analysis. Specifically, the lack of mastery experiences gained through overcoming learning challenges via one’s own effort directly undermines students’ core beliefs in their learning competence, namely SE. SE, in turn, serves as a powerful driver of MOV; when individuals do not believe they can succeed, their willingness to invest effort in learning naturally declines. Consequently, diminished SE leads to a further erosion of both intrinsic curiosity and extrinsic goal-oriented MOV. The disintegration of this combined “belief–MOV” system strikes at the psychological foundation of the SDL process, resulting in a decline in SDL. This sequential pathway highlights the crucial bridging role of psychological factors—specifically, SE and learning MOV—in fostering learning autonomy within AI-mediated learning environments.

In addition, the results reveal significant gender differences in the relationship between the TUGA and SDL (p = .003). Specifically, first, the effect of TUGA on MOV was significantly stronger among male undergraduates than among female undergraduates. From a SCT perspective, males are often socialized to adopt a more instrumental orientation toward technology use. Prior studies have shown that male students tend to focus more on efficiency and task completion when interacting with digital technologies, whereas female students are more likely to adopt reflective and evaluative learning strategies95,96. Thoughtless use aligns with an underlying belief that “technology equals efficiency,” thereby directly reinforcing extrinsic, task-completion–oriented MOV. In contrast, female students may place greater emphasis on understanding and relational meaning in learning and thus hold a more cautious stance toward the TUGA. This internalized critical orientation weakens the link between such behavior and motivational enhancement, making male students’ MOV more susceptible to this usage pattern. Second, the effect of TUGA on SE was significantly weaker among male students than among female students, reflecting gender differences in the mechanisms through which SE is formed. Male students’ SE tends to rely more heavily on hands-on mastery experiences and a sense of direct control. Thoughtless use deprives them of deep engagement and problem-solving processes; consequently, these “successes” are difficult to internalize as genuine competence, yielding limited or even inhibitory effects on SE. Female students, by contrast, may be more likely to view AI as a supportive learning tool. Previous research has suggested that female learners often demonstrate stronger reflective engagement and adaptive learning strategies in technology-mediated learning environments97. Successfully leveraging AI may therefore be interpreted as positive feedback, thereby maintaining or slightly enhancing confidence in completing tasks with technological assistance. Third, the effect of TUGA on SDL was significantly weaker among male students than among female students. This overall difference reflects the principle of triadic reciprocal determinism in SCT. Gender, as a sociocultural environmental factor, systematically shapes individuals’ cognitive beliefs and behavioral patterns in their interactions with technology. Male students may be more prone to habitual, low-reflection usage patterns, whereas female students may maintain higher levels of reflectiveness. These environmentally mediated differences in behavior subsequently exert differentiated influences on SDL, which relies heavily on self-regulation, ultimately resulting in significant gender-based moderating effects.

Conclusion

Grounded in SCT, this study systematically examined the effects of the TUGA on undergraduates’ SDL and the psychological mechanisms underlying this relationship. Using survey data collected from 487 undergraduate students across four universities in Henan Province, China and analyzed through SEM, the findings reveal that TUGA significantly undermines undergraduates’ SDL both directly and indirectly. Specifically, SE and MOV serve as key mediating mechanisms through which thoughtless AI use influences learning outcomes. Furthermore, multi-group analysis indicates that gender significantly moderates the relationships between TUGA and several psychological factors, suggesting that male and female students exhibit different patterns in how AI usage behaviors influence motivation, self-beliefs, and autonomous learning.

These findings provide several important theoretical contributions. First, this study introduces the concept of thoughtless use of generative AI into the research domain of SDL, shifting the focus from the predominantly positive narrative surrounding educational technologies toward a more balanced examination of their potential cognitive and motivational risks. Second, by integrating SE and MOV as mediating mechanisms within an SCT framework, this study clarifies the internal psychological pathways through which AI-related behaviors influence learning outcomes. In particular, the identification of the sequential mediation pathway of SE and MOV highlights the importance of psychological resources in sustaining autonomous learning in AI-mediated environments. Third, the findings demonstrate that gender functions as an important moderating factor shaping how students interact with generative AI technologies. This insight expands current theoretical understandings of human–AI interaction and highlights the importance of considering sociocultural variables when examining technology-enhanced learning.

The findings also offer practical implications for multiple stakeholders in higher education. From the perspective of policymakers, clear regulatory frameworks should be established to guide the responsible integration of generative AI technologies in higher education. Educational authorities may formulate explicit academic integrity guidelines that define appropriate uses of generative AI while discouraging excessive reliance on AI-generated content in academic tasks. In addition, policymakers should support the development of AI literacy initiatives and allocate funding for educational technology infrastructure that promotes responsible and transparent AI use.

At the institutional level, university administrators should design policies and support systems that help students use generative AI as a learning aid rather than a substitute for independent thinking. Universities may incorporate AI literacy and critical digital skills into curricula to strengthen students’ ability to evaluate and interpret AI-generated information. Institutional learning platforms may also employ learning analytics to identify students who show declining engagement or motivation in technology-mediated learning environments and provide targeted academic support.

From the perspective of teaching practice, educators should redesign instructional strategies to ensure that generative AI complements rather than replaces students’ cognitive engagement. For instance, instructors may design assignments that require students to combine AI-assisted exploration with independent analysis and reflection. Teachers can also encourage students to critically evaluate AI-generated outputs by comparing them with authoritative academic sources and explaining their reasoning processes. Such strategies can help maintain students’ motivation and self-efficacy while preventing excessive dependence on automated solutions.

Despite these contributions, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the sample was drawn exclusively from universities in Henan Province, China, and the gender distribution was uneven, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. Future studies should expand the sampling scope to include a wider range of universities and achieve a more balanced demographic distribution. Second, this study focused primarily on SE and MOV as mediating variables. However, additional factors—such as learning strategies, metacognitive skills, and social support—may also influence the relationship between AI use behaviors and SDL. In addition, MOV was treated as an overall construct rather than distinguishing between intrinsic and extrinsic motivation, as the validated measurement scale conceptualizes motivation as a unified construct reflecting students’ overall willingness to engage in learning activities. Future research could further differentiate these motivational dimensions to provide more nuanced insights into how generative AI usage patterns influence students’ learning processes. Finally, this study relied on cross-sectional data to examine the proposed mediation relationships. Although the mediation model was theoretically grounded in Social Cognitive Theory, cross-sectional mediation analyses cannot establish definitive causal ordering among variables. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted as evidence of statistical mediation rather than causal mediation. Future research could employ longitudinal designs to examine the developmental trajectories and long-term consequences of thoughtless generative AI use, thereby offering more profound insights into its temporal dynamics and sustained impacts on students’ SDL.
