Antibacterial Properties of Lemon Juice–Mediated Zinc Oxide Nanoparticle and Titanium Dioxide Nanoparticle
Authors: Mary Sheloni Missier, Mahesh Ramakrishnan, S Veerasankar, C Dhanalakshmi, Harish Ponniah, Shanmugam Rajeshkumar
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroduction:The emergence of antibiotic-resistant pathogenic bacteria has become a major global health concern, with the increasing prevalence of infections that are difficult to treat with conventional antibiotics. As a result, there is a critical need for alternative antimicrobial agents that can effectively control bacterial infections and combat the growing problem of antibiotic resistance.Materials and Methods:In this study, the antibacterial properties of lemon juice mediated zinc oxide nanoparticles (ZnO NPs) and titanium dioxide nanoparticles (TiO2 NPs) was evaluated against oral pathogens. The synthesized nanoparticles were characterized using UV-Vis spectroscopy. The antimicrobial activity of the nanoparticles was determined by the agar well diffusion assay.Results:Our results showed that both ZnO NPs and TiO2 NPs exhibited potent antimicrobial activity against both Streptococcus mutans and Streptococcus mitis. Our findings suggest that the antimicrobial activity of ZnO NPs and TiO2 NPs is attributed to the disruption of bacterial cell membrane integrity and inhibition of bacterial growth.Conclusion:Therefore, lemon juice mediated ZnO NPs and TiO2 NPs can be considered as promising antimicrobial agents for controlling bacterial infections.

INTRODUCTION

Nanotechnology is a rapidly evolving field that involves the engineering and manipulation of materials at the nanoscale level. One of the areas where nanotechnology has shown promise is in combating antimicrobial resistance (AMR).[1] Nanotechnology offers a range of approaches for developing new antimicrobial agents, such as nanomaterial-based antibiotics, and also for improving existing antimicrobial agents, such as drug delivery systems and diagnostics.[2]

Nanoparticles target the pathogens by penetrating into the microbial cells and interact with the cellular components, such as the cell membrane or the genetic material.[3] For example, silver nanoparticles have been shown to be effective against a wide range of bacteria, including multidrug-resistant strains, by disrupting their cell membrane and inducing cell death.[4] Similarly, gold nanoparticles have been shown to have antimicrobial properties by inhibiting bacterial growth and biofilm formation.[5]

Nanotechnology improves the efficacy of existing antimicrobial agents by enhancing their delivery to the site of infection, for example, it acts as drug carriers that can transport antibiotics to the site of infection, protecting them from degradation or elimination by the immune system.[6] This increases the concentration of the drug at the site of infection, reducing the likelihood of the development of resistance. Additionally, it improves the specificity of drug delivery by targeting the specific infected cells or tissues.[7]

Zinc oxide nanoparticles (ZnO NPs) and titanium dioxide nanoparticles (TiO2 NPs) are among the most widely studied types of NPs because of their biocompatibility, stability, and photocatalytic activity.[8] TiO2 NPs and ZnO NPs are studied for their antimicrobial properties and are substituted to traditional antibiotics, both are commonly used in various applications like cosmetics, sunscreens, and food additives for their antimicrobial properties.[9]

TiO2 NPs show broad-spectrum antimicrobial activity against bacteria, viruses, and fungi. They react by generating the reactive oxygen species (ROS) upon exposure to UV light or visible light, causing oxidative damage to the microbial cells.[10] TiO2 NPs are effective against Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria, including multidrug-resistant strains such as methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus and vancomycin-resistant Enterococcus. The antimicrobial activity depends on the size, shape, surface chemistry of the particles, and the environmental conditions like pH and temperature.[11]

ZnO NPs have also been shown to have antimicrobial activity against a wide range of microorganisms, including bacteria, viruses, and fungi. The antimicrobial mechanism of ZnO NPs is similar to that of TiO2 NPs, involving the generation of ROS that can cause oxidative damage to the microbial cells.[12]

Lemon juice, a natural product derived from the lemon fruit (citrus lemon), has been used for centuries as a home remedy for various ailments due to its antimicrobial and antioxidant properties.[13] Lemon juice contains citric acid and bioactive compounds like limonene, terpenes, and flavonoids, which possess antimicrobial, anti-inflammatory, and anticancer properties.[14]

In recent years, strains are resistant to antibiotics; therefore, natural products are used as antimicrobial agents. This led to the development of naturally derived antimicrobial agents. Several studies have reported the use of lemon juice as a natural antimicrobial agent.[15] The citric acid present in lemon juice has bactericidal effect against a range of bacteria, including Escherichia coli, Salmonella typhimurium, and S. aureus. Lemon juice is used as a natural reducing and stabilizing agent for nanoparticle synthesis as it has several advantages over traditional chemical synthesis.[16]

Recently, there has been growing interest in combining lemon juice with NPs to enhance their antimicrobial activity. The acidic nature of lemon juice enhances the release of metal ions from the NPs, which increases their antimicrobial activity. In this study, we focus on the antimicrobial properties of lemon juice–mediated ZnO NPs and TiO2 NPs against the cariogenic bacteria Streptococcus mutans and Streptococcus mitis.[17] The lemon juice–synthesized nanoparticles are effective against drug-resistant strains emerging as an alternative antimicrobial agent.[18]

In this study, we aim to evaluate the antimicrobial activity of lemon juice–mediated ZnO NPs and TiO2 NPs against S. mutans and S. mitis. This research has important implications for the development of effective and sustainable antimicrobial agents to address the growing problem of AMR.

MATERIAL AND METHODS

Preparation of lemon juice extract

Fresh lemons were brought from a local supermarket near Poonamallee. The fresh lemons were cut into two pieces and squeezed to get up to 50 mL extract. The collected extract was subjected to filtration process using a muslin cloth to remove the lemon seeds and pulp. The freshly collected lemon juice extract was used as a reducing and capping agent to synthesize both ZnO NPs and TiO2 NPs.

Green synthesis of titanium oxide nanoparticles and zinc oxide nanoparticles

For the preparation of titanium oxide nanoparticles, 0.395 g of titanium oxide was measured and added to 50 mL distilled water to attain a precursor solution for synthesizing TiO2 NPs. To the precursor solution, 50 mL of a filtered reducing agent (lemon juice extract) was added. Similarly, 20 mM of zinc sulfate was taken as precursor to synthesize ZnO NPs. The measured zinc sulfate powder was added to 25 mL distilled water. To that, 25 mL of filtered lemon juice extract was added. Uniform dispersion is very essential in synthesizing nanoparticles; therefore, both reaction mixtures were kept on an individual magnetic stirrer at 700 rpm for up to 48 h. To preliminarily confirm the synthesis of TiO2 NPs and ZnO NPs, UV–vis spectroscopy analysis was performed. Then to obtain both nanoparticles in pellet form, centrifugation was carried out at 8,000 rpm for 10 min. The pellet was collected from the two reaction mixtures and washed thoroughly twice using double distilled water. The supernatant was discarded and the obtained TiO2 NP and ZnO NP pellets were kept inside a hot air oven at 70°C for 2–3 h to attain a dried powder form. For characterization studies and testing biomedical applications, these dried nanoparticles were used.

Antibacterial activity

Agar well diffusion technique was adopted in this current study to evaluate the antibacterial potential of green-synthesized ZnO NPs and TiO2 NPs. To perform this activity, Sanguis Mutans agar was used as a culture medium which is also a selective media for S. mutans. An autoclave is utilized to sterilize the prepared culture medium at 121°C for 15 min. After the sterilization process, the sterilized medium was poured on the surface of sterile agar plates. Then, the plates were kept undisturbed for solidification process. A sterile polystyrene tip, which was around 9 mm in diameter, was used to cut four wells on each agar plate. On the agar surface, the test pathogens such as S. mutans and S. mitis were swabbed using a sterile cotton swab. Different concentrations (50 μL, 100 μL, and 150 μL) of lemon juice–mediated ZnO NPs and TiO2 NPs were loaded into the wells; to the fourth well, antibiotic solution (Amoxyrite) was used for comparison. The loaded plates were incubated at 37°C for 24 h. After 24 h, the zone of inhibition was observed and measured around each well to assess the efficacy of both nanoparticles [Figure 1].

RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

Antibacterial activity of TiO2 NPs

The study investigated the antimicrobial properties of TiO2 NPs against two bacterial pathogens, S. mitis and S. mutans. The results revealed that the nanoparticles exhibited significant inhibitory effects against both pathogens, as the zones of inhibition were observed in the agar well diffusion assay.

For S. mitis, the zones of inhibition ranged from 12 mm to 18 mm with increasing concentrations of TiO2 NPs (50 μL, 100 μL, and 150 μL). The highest zone of inhibition was observed at 150 μL, with a diameter of 18 mm. Similarly for S. mutans, the zones of inhibition ranged from 14 mm to 22 mm with increasing concentrations of TiO2 NPs. The highest zone of inhibition was observed at 150 μL, with a diameter of 22 mm.

The results of the study indicate that S. mutans was more sensitive to the antibacterial effects of TiO2 NPs compared to S. mitis. This is evident from the larger zones of inhibition observed in the agar diffusion assay for S. mutans at all three concentrations of TiO2 NPs tested.

At the lowest concentration of TiO2 NPs (50 μL), the zone of inhibition for S. mutans was 14 mm, while for S. mitis, it was only 12 mm. At the intermediate concentration (100 μL), the zone of inhibition for S. mutans increased to 17 mm, while for S. mitis, it was 14 mm. At the highest concentration (150 μL), the zone of inhibition for S. mutans was 22 mm, while for S. mitis, it was 18 mm. In comparison, the antibiotic (Ab) had a zone of inhibition of 23 mm against S. mitis and 25 mm against S. mutans, indicating stronger inhibitory effects than TiO2 NPs.

The results suggest that TiO2 NPs have potent antimicrobial activity against both S. mitis and S. mutans. Previous studies stated that green-synthesized TiO2 NPs showed high antibacterial activity against S. mutans due to their photocatalytic nature and chemical stability. Azadirachta indica leaf extract–mediated TiO2 NPs showed broad spectrum antimicrobial activity against Salmonella typhi, Klebsiella pneumonia, and E. coli.[19] Green-synthesized TiO2 NPs using leaf extract of Mentha arvensis have also been investigated for their antibacterial and antifungal activity [Figure 2].[20]

Antibacterial activity of ZnO NPs

The use of nanotechnology in developing antimicrobial agents has been of great interest due to its potential to overcome the limitations of conventional antibiotics. One such material is ZnO NPs, which have been reported to possess broad-spectrum antimicrobial properties against various pathogens. In this study, the antimicrobial activity of ZnO NPs was evaluated against two common oral pathogens, S. mitis and S. mutans.

The results of the study showed that the ZnO NPs exhibited significant antimicrobial activity against both S. mitis and S. mutans. The zone of inhibition increased with increasing concentrations of the nanoparticles. The 50-μL concentration of ZnO NPs resulted in a zone of inhibition of 14 mm for S. mitis and 16 mm for S. mutans. The 100-μL concentration resulted in a zone of inhibition of 16 mm for S. mitis and 18 mm for S. mutans, while the 150-μL concentration resulted in a zone of inhibition of 22 mm for S. mitis and 24 mm for S. mutans.

The results showed that the antimicrobial activity of ZnO NPs was more on higher concentration, which had larger zones of inhibition. Additionally, the antimicrobial activity of ZnO NPs was almost equal to that of the antibiotic, amoxyrite (Ab), which resulted in a zone of inhibition of only 26 mm for S. mitis and 28 mm for S. mutans.

These results suggest that ZnO NPs could be a promising alternative to conventional antibiotics for the treatment of oral infections caused by S. mitis and S. mutans. The ability of ZnO NPs to inhibit the growth of these pathogens at low concentrations and the superior antimicrobial activity compared to conventional antibiotics highlight the potential of this material for the development of new and effective antimicrobial agents.[21]

CONCLUSION

The evaluation of antimicrobial properties of lemon juice–mediated ZnO NPs and TiO2 NPs was specifically conducted against two common oral streptococcal pathogens, S. mutans and S. mitis, as they are causative factors of dental caries and oral infections. Nanoparticles effectively inhibit the growth of these pathogens, which suggests their potential use as effective antimicrobial agents for controlling oral infections. Furthermore, the use of lemon juice as a natural reducing and stabilizing agent for the synthesis of these nanoparticles offers several advantages over traditional chemical synthesis methods. The use of natural ingredients reduces the environmental impact and potential toxicity associated with chemical methods. Overall, the findings of this study provide a promising foundation for further research into the development and optimization of lemon juice–mediated ZnO NPs and TiO2 NPs as antimicrobial agents for various applications, including oral hygiene and medical treatments. Further studies are warranted to investigate their safety and efficacy in vivo, as well as their potential for use in combination with other antimicrobial agents to combat the growing problem of antibiotic resistance.
