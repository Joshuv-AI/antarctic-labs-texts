Sprinkled Decoupling for Hammersley’s Process
Leandro P. R. Pimentel, Roberto Viveros

Abstract
In this article we prove a sprinkled decoupling inequality for the stationary Hammersley’s interacting particle process [ 2 , 13 ] . Inspired by [ 5 , 15 ] , we apply this inequality to study two distinct problems on the top of this particle process. First, we analyze a detection problem, demonstrating that a fugitive can evade particles, provided that their jump range is sufficiently large. Second, we show that a random walk in a dynamic random environment exhibits ballistic behavior with respect to the characteristic speed of the particle system, under a weak assumption on the probability of being away of this critical speed.

1.1
Introduction

Sprinkling is a technique used to control the decay of correlations (decoupling) by introducing small perturbations. In summary, instead of considering two fixed increasing events
A
and
B
, we introduce slightly “sprinkled” versions of these events, denoted
A_{\epsilon}
and
B_{\epsilon}
, which occur with slightly higher probabilities due to the added randomness, and obtain a decoupling inequality of the form:

\mathbb{P}(A\cap B)\leq\mathbb{P}(A_{\epsilon})\mathbb{P}(B_{\epsilon})+\text{error}.

The addition of the sprinkling parameter
\epsilon
accounts for the extra randomness introduced to ensure better connectivity or event occurrence. This formulation is particularly useful in percolation theory of correlated systems, where small increments in probability can lead to large-scale connectivity changes. See for instance the pioneer works
[
21
,
23
,
24
]
, where sprinkling is used to control dependencies in a model of random interlacements. For more recent examples of applications of the sprinkling technique for percolation theory on Poisson cylinders and Gaussian fields we refer to
[
3
,
18
]
.

On the subject of conservative particle process, such as independent symmetric simple random walks
[
6
,
14
]
and symmetric simple exclusion process
[
5
,
15
]
, sprinkled decoupling has been applied to study detection problems and random walks on dynamic random environment. In both cases we have positive association and a slow decay of correlations, which makes the use of sprinkling a key tool to apply multiscale renormalization schemes. For the symmetric simple exclusion process, the symmetry of the dynamics played a crucial role in the proof methods (interchange process). The novelty of this article lies in establishing a sprinkled decoupling inequality (Theorem
1
) for a stationary, conservative and totally asymmetric particle process, known shortly as Hammersley’s process. This is achieved through a new decoupling approach that is based on geometric properties of a companion last-passage percolation model.

Detection Model

Suppose that particles are seen as detectors that are capable of detecting all targets within some distance
r>0
from their location. Targets can jump up to a certain range
N\geq 1
only at discrete times, and can predict the future movement of all detectors. Since particles eventually get arbitrarily close, it is not hard to see that, if the target jump range
N
is not large compared to the detection range
r
, then a target eventually gets detected with probability one. We will prove that, for sufficiently large
N
, a target starting at the origin can avoid detection forever with positive probability (Theorem
2
).

For asymmetric environments, such as Hammersley’s process or the totally asymmetric simple exclusion process, it is still conjectured the existence of a velocity, and Gaussian fluctuations away from the characteristic line. Along this critical line, it is expected the emergence of super-diffusive fluctuations lying within the Kardar-Parisi-Zhang universality class. This behavior was first predicted in the physics literature by T. Bohr and A. Pikovsky

Hammersley’s Process (HP)

Recall that we start the stationary Hammersley’s process (HP) with a configuration of particles distributed as a Poisson point process on
{\mathbb{R}}
with parameter

z^{\ast}

\eta_{t}\leq\zeta_{t}

[
11
]
. A function

is non-decreasing (non-increasing) if
\eta\leq\zeta

). We say that

\eta_{r}(A)=\zeta_{r}(A)\,\,\forall\,\,A\subseteq[x,y]\,,\,r\in[s,t]\,\,\implies\,\,f(\eta)=f(\zeta)\,.

Let
\mathbb{P}^{\lambda}
denote the probability measure on the space of càdlàg trajectories
\eta:{\mathbb{R}}\to\mathcal{M}

\lambda>0
. Let
\mathbb{E}^{\lambda}
be the associated expectation operator.

For
B=[x,y]\times[s,t]
we set
{\rm per}(B)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny$def.$}}}{{=}}y-x+t-s

{\rm d}(B_{1},B_{2})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny$def.$}}}{{=}}d_{h}+d_{v}\mbox{ where }\begin{cases}d_{h}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny$def.$}}}{{=}}\inf\{|x_{1}-x_{2}|\,:\,(x_{i},t_{i})\in B_{i}\,,i=1,2\}\\

Thus

and

and
B_{2}
. In Theorem

and

Let
\lambda,\lambda^{\prime}\in I=[\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}]\subseteq(0,\infty)

1.3
Detection by Hammersley’s Process

Suppose that in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
a moving object (e.g., an intruder) attempts to traverse without being detected by a detection system
\Phi
which consists of randomly placed particles, each with a detection radius. Each point
x\in\Phi
represents a sensor or obstacle and has a detection range modeled as a ball of radius
r>0
, forming the detected region:

D=\bigcup_{x\in\Phi}B(x,r),

where
B(x,r)
is the closed Euclidean ball of radius
r
centered at
x
. The object follows a path
\gamma:[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
and the goal is to find an infinite trajectory
\gamma
that avoids the detected region
D
, meaning:

\gamma(t)\notin D,\quad\forall t\geq 0.

If such a path exists, we say the object escapes detection. This problem automatically translates into the percolation of free space
\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus D
. The study of percolation in the vacant set has been a significant topic in continuum percolation theory. This research primarily focuses on understanding the conditions under which the unoccupied regions (vacant set) form an infinite connected component, allowing an object to traverse without intersecting any obstacles. For instance, see
[
1
]
and
[
25
]
for the study of the vacant set when
\Phi
is given by the realization of a Poisson point process.

In this paper, as an application of Theorem
1

r>0
from it. A target can make jumps of size
\{-N\Delta_{s},\dots,N\Delta_{s}\}
, where
\Delta_{s}>0
is a real space parameter, and
N\in\{1,2,\dots\}
is fixed, at times
t_{n}=n\Delta_{t}
for
n\geq 1
, where
\Delta_{t}>0
is a real time parameter. It also assumed that the target knows the future movement of all detectors, and the main question is whether it can escape detection forever with positive probability (Figure 2). It is not hard to see that, with probability one, eventually consecutive detectors gets arbitrarily close to each other. Hence, for
\Delta_{s}=1
,
r\geq 1/2
and
N=1

\lambda,r,\Delta_{s},\Delta_{t}>0
, a phase transition occurs in the probability of detection as

varies.

Theorem 2

There exists
N_{0}
such that if
N\geq N_{0}
, then the probability that a target starting at the origin never gets detected by some detector is positive.

1.4
Random Walk on Top of the Hammersley’s Process

Random walks in random environments (RWRE) refer to stochastic processes where a walker moves in a space where the transition probabilities are random variables. More specifically

\mathbb{P}_{\omega}(X_{n+1}=x+e_{i}|X_{n}=x)=\omega(x,n,e_{i}),

where
\omega
is an underlying random environment drawn from a probability space
\Omega
. This environment is usually fixed before the walk begins (quenched randomness), and the idea is to analyze the conditional behavior of the walker given the specific realization of
\omega
. Random walks in random environments have been widely studied, in both static (when doesn’t depend on
n
) and dynamic settings. A particularly interesting class of models arises when the environment is given by a conservative particle system, such as independent random walks
[
14
]
or the exclusion process
[
15
]
. In this case, the correlations in the environment are strong and the use of sprinkling, which helps in decoupling space-time events, has been crucial in analyzing these systems.

In this paper, also as an application of Theorem 1, we follow the multiscale strategy developed in
[
14
,
15
]
for dynamic random environments, and use our inequality to implement their framework for a random walk that evolves on top of Hammersley’s process. More specifically, we prove that under a suitable condition, the random walk remains with high probability on one side of a straight line aligned with the characteristic direction of the environment. Depending on the parameters, this side may be to the left or right of the characteristic. While this does not establish ballistic behavior in the strong sense (such as a law of large numbers), it does imply that the walker makes linear progress in a given direction.

Let
I_{x}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny$def.$}}}{{=}}(x-1/2,x+1/2)
denote the open interval of diameter one around
x
, and fix
p_{\bullet}\,,\,p_{\circ}\in(0,1)
. Define the random walk
(X_{n})_{n\geq 0}
on
{\mathbb{Z}}
starting at
X_{0}=0
and evolving on the top of
\eta\in{\mathcal{D}}
as follows:

•

If
\eta_{n}(I_{X_{n}})>0
then
\begin{cases}X_{n+1}=X_{n}+1\mbox{ with probability }p_{\bullet}\\
X_{n+1}=X_{n}-1\mbox{ with probability }1-p_{\bullet}\end{cases}
;

•

If
\eta_{n}(I_{X_{n}})=0
then
\begin{cases}X_{n+1}=X_{n}+1\mbox{ with probability }p_{\circ}\\
X_{n+1}=X_{n}-1\mbox{ with probability }1-p_{\circ}\end{cases}
.

Let
P^{\eta}
denote the probability law of
(X_{n})_{n\geq 0}
, given
\eta\in{\mathcal{D}}
, also known as the quenched law of the random walk, and let

{\mathbf{P}}^{\lambda}(\cdot)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny$def.$}}}{{=}}{\mathbb{E}}^{\lambda}\left[P^{\eta}(\cdot)\right]\,,

also known as the annealed law of the random walk.

We consider the coupled family
\{(X_{t}^{\mathrm{w}})_{t\geq 0},\;{\mathrm{w}}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\}
constructed as in Section 3.1 of
[
15
]

{\mathrm{w}}=(x,n)\in\mathcal{L}=(2\mathbb{Z})^{2}\cup\left((1,1)+(2\mathbb{Z})^{2}\right)
after one step is

X_{1}^{\mathrm{w}}=\begin{cases}x+2\,\mathbf{1}_{\{U_{\mathrm{w}}\leq p_{\bullet}\}}-1,&\text{if }\eta^{\lambda}_{n}(I_{x})>0,\\
x+2\,\mathbf{1}_{\{U_{\mathrm{w}}\leq p_{\circ}\}}-1,&\text{if }\eta^{\lambda}_{n}(I_{x})=0,\end{cases}

where
\{U_{\mathrm{w}}:{\mathrm{w}}\in\mathcal{L}\}
is an i.i.d. collection of uniform random variables in
[0,1]
. For any integer
m\geq 1
, the walk is defined by induction:

X_{m}^{\mathrm{w}}=X_{1}^{(X_{m-1}^{\mathrm{w}},m-1)},

which recursively constructs the trajectory starting from
{\mathrm{w}}\in\mathcal{L}
and then is extended to all possible starting points in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
assigning each point to the closest previously defined trajectory located vertically above it (see the details in Section 3.1 of
[
15
]
). This collection of random walks satisfies two fundamental properties:

•

Coalescence:
if two random walks meet at some vertex, they will follow the same trajectory from that point onward;

•

Monotonicity:
if one walk starts to the right of another, it will remain to the right for all future times.

We can add to this construction a coupling of HP at different intensities. More precisely, using the basic coupling of the family of measures
\{\mathbb{P}^{\lambda}:\lambda>0\}
, we can define a new coupled family of random walks
(X_{t}^{{\mathrm{w}},\lambda})_{t\geq 0},\;{\mathrm{w}}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
, where
\lambda
denotes the intensity of the underlying Poisson point process that initializes the environment. In this setting, each trajectory
(X_{t}^{{\mathrm{w}},\lambda})_{t\geq 0}
evolves in the dynamic environment
\eta^{\lambda}
given by the stationary Hammersley’s process with parameter
\lambda>0
. The use of the basic coupling ensures that if
\lambda<\lambda^{\prime}
, then the environment
\eta_{t}^{\lambda}
is contained in
\eta_{t}^{\lambda^{\prime}}
for all
t\in{\mathbb{R}}
, almost surely. As a consequence of the monotonicity of the walks with respect to the environment and the attractiveness of the basic coupling, it readily follows that if
p_{\circ}<p_{\bullet}
, then

X_{t}^{{\mathrm{w}},\lambda}\leq X_{t}^{w,\lambda^{\prime}}\quad\text{for all }t\geq 0,\quad\text{whenever }\lambda<\lambda^{\prime}.

For
{\mathrm{w}}=(w_{1},w_{2})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
let
\pi_{1}({\mathrm{w}})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny$def.$}}}{{=}}w_{1}
and
\pi_{2}({\mathrm{w}})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny$def.$}}}{{=}}w_{2}
, and denote by
{\mathbb{D}}_{1}
the set of all
{\mathrm{w}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
such that
|\pi_{1}({\mathrm{w}})|+|\pi_{2}({\mathrm{w}})|\leq 1
. As in
[
15
]
, we consider the following events

A_{H,{\mathrm{w}}}(v,\lambda)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny$def.$}}}{{=}}\left\{\exists\,{\mathrm{y}}\in\left({\mathrm{w}}+[0,H)\times\{0\}\right)\mbox{ s.t. }X^{{\mathrm{y}},\lambda}_{H}-\pi_{1}({\mathrm{y}})\geq vH\right\}\,

and

\tilde{A}_{H,{\mathrm{w}}}(v,\lambda)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny$def.$}}}{{=}}\left\{\exists\,{\mathrm{y}}\in\left({\mathrm{w}}+[0,H)\times\{0\}\right)\mbox{ s.t. }X^{{\mathrm{y}},\lambda}_{H}-\pi_{1}({\mathrm{y}})\leq vH\right\}\,.

In what follows, we parametrize the random walk on Hammersley’s process by the characteristic direction
\rho=\lambda^{-2}
, and define

p_{H}(v,\rho)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny$def.$}}}{{=}}\sup_{{\mathrm{w}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}}{\mathbf{P}}\left(A_{H,{\mathrm{w}}}(v,\lambda)\right)=\sup_{{\mathrm{w}}\in{\mathbb{D}}_{1}}{\mathbf{P}}\left(A_{H,{\mathrm{w}}}(v,{\lambda})\right)

and

\tilde{p}_{H}(v,\rho)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny$def.$}}}{{=}}\sup_{{\mathrm{w}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}}{\mathbf{P}}\left(\tilde{A}_{H,{\mathrm{w}}}(v,\lambda)\right)=\sup_{{\mathrm{w}}\in{\mathbb{D}}_{1}}{\mathbf{P}}\left(\tilde{A}_{H,{\mathrm{w}}}(v,\lambda)\right)\,.

We note that
p_{H}(v,\rho)
and
\tilde{p}_{H}(v,\rho)
depend on
p_{\bullet}\,,\,p_{\circ}\in(0,1)
as well. The following quantities are the natural candidates for the speed of the random walk:

v_{+}(\rho)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny$def.$}}}{{=}}\inf\left\{v\in{\mathbb{R}}\,:\,\liminf_{H\to\infty}p_{H}(v,\rho)=0\right\}\,,

and

v_{-}(\rho)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny$def.$}}}{{=}}\sup\left\{v\in{\mathbb{R}}\,:\,\liminf_{H\to\infty}\tilde{p}_{H}(v,\rho)=0\right\}\,.

If
p_{\circ}\leq p_{\bullet}
and
\lambda\leq\lambda^{\prime}
then
A_{H,{\mathrm{w}}}(v,\lambda)\subset A_{H,{\mathrm{w}}}(v,\lambda^{\prime})
. This implies that the function
p_{H}(v,\rho)
is non-increasing in
\rho
, which implies that
\rho\mapsto v_{+}(\rho)
is also non-increasing. Note that when
\rho=0
, the system can be interpreted as having an infinitely dense initial Poisson process. In this regime, the random walk always encounters a particle at every vertex and by the law of large numbers, this leads to:

\frac{X_{H}}{H}\to 2p_{\bullet}-1\quad{\mathbf{P}}-\text{a.s.}

as
H\to\infty
. This implies that
v_{+}(0)=v_{-}(0)=2p_{\bullet}-1
. Similarly, for
\rho\to\infty
, we have

\lim_{\rho\to\infty}v_{+}(\rho)<2p_{\circ}-1.

The same reasoning applies to the function
v_{-}(\rho)
. In summary, both
v_{+}(\rho)
and
v_{-}(\rho)
are non-increasing functions, and their range is included in
[2p_{\circ}-1,2p_{\bullet}-1].
On the other hand, if
p_{\bullet}\leq p_{\circ}
then
\rho\mapsto v_{\pm}(\rho)
is non-decreasing and their range in included in
[2p_{\bullet}-1,2p_{\circ}-1].
For
p_{\circ}<p_{\bullet}
we have that
\{\rho>0\,:\,v_{-}(\rho)>\rho\}
is non-empty and bounded from above, and
\{\rho>0\,:\,v_{+}(\rho)<\rho\}
is non-empty and bounded from below. Thus we can define

\rho_{c-}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny$def.$}}}{{=}}\sup\{\rho>0\,:\,v_{-}(\rho)>\rho\}\in(0,\infty)\,,

and

\rho_{c+}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny$def.$}}}{{=}}\inf\{\rho>0\,:\,v_{+}(\rho)<\rho\}\in(0,\infty)\,.

Since
v_{\pm}(\rho)
is non-increasing, we also have that

\rho_{c-}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny$def.$}}}{{=}}\inf\{\rho>0\,:\,v_{-}(\rho)<\rho\}\,,

and

\rho_{c+}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny$def.$}}}{{=}}\sup\{\rho>0\,:\,v_{+}(\rho)>\rho\}\in(0,\infty)\,.

We adapt the multiscale analysis in
[
15
]
to fit the requirement in Theorem
1
for the decoupling inequality, and prove ballisticity of the random walk with respect to the characteristic speed, under a weak condition on the probability of being away from this critical value

p_{\circ}<p_{\bullet}
. For any

1.5
Sprinkled Decoupling for Exponential Last-Passage Percolation

As mentioned before, the approach to decoupling is based on geometric properties of a Poisson last-passage percolation (LPP) model that is related to Hammersley’s process. It turns out that the same approach can be applied to a lattice LPP model with exponential weights to prove a similar sprinkled decoupling inequality. To introduce the model denote
{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny$def.$}}}{{=}}\{0,1,2,\dots\}
and assume that
\{w_{i,j}\,:\,(i,j)\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}^{2}\}
is an array of nonnegative numbers such that
w_{0,0}=0
. Let
\Pi_{ij}
be the set of oriented paths

\pi=\left((0,0)=(k_{0},l_{0}),(k_{1},l_{1}),\dots,(k_{i+j},l_{i+j})=(i,j)\right)\,,

with up-right steps:
(k_{n+1},l_{n+1})-(k_{n},l_{n})\in\{(1,0)\,,\,(0,1)\}
. Whenever we denote
(k,l)\in\pi
, it means that
(k,l)\in\{(k_{0},l_{0}),\dots,(k_{i+j},l_{i+j})\}
. The last-passage percolation time from
(0,0)
to
(i,j)
is defined as

(i,j)\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}^{2}\,\mapsto\,G(i,j)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny$def.$}}}{{=}}\max_{\pi\in\Pi_{ij}}\sum_{(k,l)\in\pi}w_{kl}\,,

and we say that
\pi\in\Pi_{ij}
is a maximal path if
G(i,j)=\sum_{(k,l)\in\pi}w_{kl}
. To introduce randomness, we pick
\alpha\in(0,1)
and take the random variables
\omega_{ij}
for
(i,j)\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}^{2}\setminus\{(0,0)\}
independent with marginal distributions described below:

\displaystyle w_{i0}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny$dist.$}}}{{=}}

\displaystyle{\rm Exp}(1-\alpha)\,,\,i\geq 1\,,

\displaystyle w_{0j}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny$dist.$}}}{{=}}

\displaystyle{\rm Exp}(\alpha)\,,\,j\geq 1\,,

\displaystyle w_{ij}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny$dist.$}}}{{=}}

\displaystyle{\rm Exp}(1)\,,\,i,j\geq 1\,.

Here
w\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny$dist.$}}}{{=}}{\rm Exp}(\alpha)
means that
w
has the exponential distribution with rate
\alpha
. We introduce the superscript
\alpha
to indicate parameter dependence.

This LPP model is related to the totally asymmetric simple exclusion process (TASEP), an interacting particle system
\left(\xi^{\alpha}_{t}(x)\,,\,x\in{\mathbb{Z}}\right)_{t\geq 0}
where each particle waits a rate one exponential random amount of time, then attempts to jump one step to the right and the jump is allowed only if there is no particle in the target site. The evolution takes place in
\{0,1\}^{{\mathbb{Z}}}
, where at each time
t\geq 0
,
\xi^{\alpha}_{t}(x)=1
indicates that there is a particle at site
x
, while
\xi^{\alpha}_{t}(x)=0
indicates that there is a hole (no particle) at site
x
. Initially, particles at sites
x\in{\mathbb{Z}}
obey independent Bernoulli with density
\alpha\in(0,1)
distributions. Then we have a time-stationary process:
\xi^{\alpha}_{t}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny$dist.$}}}{{=}}\xi_{0}
for all
t\geq 0
. We condition on
\xi^{\alpha}_{0}(0)=0
and
\xi^{\alpha}_{0}(1)=1
, and the particle initially at site one is labeled
0
, and the hole initially at the origin is labeled
0
. After this, all particles are labeled with integers from right to left, and all holes from left to right. As explained in
[
4
]
, the collection of last-passage percolation times
\{G^{\alpha}(i,j)\,:\,(i,j)\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}^{2}\}
has the same distribution as the collection of times where the
j
th particle and
i
th hole exchange positions. The TASEP also has a hydrodynamic limit described by a hyperbolic conservative law with
f(u)=u(1-u)
. In the stationary regime, the characteristic velocity is given by
f^{\prime}(\alpha)=1-2\alpha
. In the two-dimensional LPP model, this corresponds to the direction
\left((1-\alpha)^{2},\alpha^{2}\right)
, and the characteristic velocity
\beta=\beta(\alpha)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny$def.$}}}{{=}}(1-\alpha)\alpha^{-2}
. For a more detailed explanation, we refer the reader to section 3.4 in
[
4
]
.

The LPP model induces a discrete time evolution
W^{\alpha}=(W^{\alpha}_{n})_{n\geq 0}
defined as

W^{\alpha}_{n}((k,l])\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny$def.$}}}{{=}}G^{\alpha}(l,n)-G^{\alpha}(k,n)\,,\,\mbox{ for }(k,l]\subseteq{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}\,,

(1.1)

which is time stationary:
W^{\alpha}_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny$dist.$}}}{{=}}W^{\alpha}_{0}
for all
n\geq 0
(Lemma 4.2
[
4
]
). We have a partial order on the space of possible evolutions, defined as
W\leq W^{\prime}
if
W_{n}((k,l])\leq W^{\prime}_{n}((k,l])
for all
(k,l]\subseteq{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}^{2}
and
n\geq 0
. It is also true that if
w_{ij}
and
w^{\prime}_{ij}

are such that
w_{00}=\tilde{w}^{\prime}_{00}=0
,
w_{j0}\geq w^{\prime}_{0j}
,
w_{i0}\leq w^{\prime}_{i0}
and
w_{ij}=w^{\prime}_{ij}
for all
i,j\geq 1
then
W\leq W^{\prime}
(Lemma 4.3
[
4
]
). This implies that one can couple
(W^{\alpha},W^{\alpha^{\prime}})
with
\alpha^{\prime}\leq\alpha
such that
W^{\alpha}\leq W^{\alpha^{\prime}}
. In what follows we consider two monotonic functions
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
of
W
with support on space-time boxes
B_{1}
and
B_{2}
, respectively.

Theorem 4

Let
\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}\in I=[\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}]\subseteq(0,1)
, and let
\epsilon=\left|\beta(\alpha)-\beta(\alpha^{\prime})\right|
. There exist
\epsilon_{I},c_{I},C_{I}>0
, that depends on
I
, such that for all
\epsilon\in(0,\epsilon_{I})
and

{\rm d}(B_{1},B_{2})>C_{I}\epsilon^{-1}\big({\rm per}(B_{1})+{\rm per}(B_{2})\big)\,,

if
\alpha^{\prime}<\alpha
and
f_{i}
are non-decreasing then

{\mathbb{E}}^{\alpha}\left[f_{1}f_{2}\right]\leq{\mathbb{E}}^{\alpha}\left[f_{1}\right]{\mathbb{E}}^{\alpha^{\prime}}\left[f_{2}\right]+10e^{-c_{I}\epsilon^{4}{\rm d}(B_{1},B_{2})}\,,

if
\alpha<\alpha^{\prime}
and
f_{i}
are non-increasing then

{\mathbb{E}}^{\alpha}\left[f_{1}f_{2}\right]\leq{\mathbb{E}}^{\alpha}\left[f_{1}\right]{\mathbb{E}}^{\alpha^{\prime}}\left[f_{2}\right]+10e^{-c_{I}\epsilon^{4}{\rm d}(B_{1},B_{2})}\,.

Theorem 4 can be used to apply multiscale analyze to a random walk on the top of a exponential LPP environment, and deduce a result similar to Theorem
3
. In this model, for each
x\in{\mathbb{Z}}
and
n\geq 0
we introduce the derivative

D^{\alpha}\left(x,n\right)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny$def.$}}}{{=}}G^{\alpha}\left(\frac{n+x}{2},\frac{n-x}{2}+1\right)-G^{\alpha}\left(\frac{n+x}{2}+1,\frac{n-x}{2}\right)\,,

and the random walk located at position
x\in{\mathbb{Z}}
at time
n\geq 0
inspects the LPP environment at the space-time position where it lies on: if
D^{\alpha}\left(x,n\right)>0
, the random walk decides to jump to the right with probability
p_{+}
or to the left with probability
1-p_{+}
; if
D^{\alpha}\left(x,n\right)<0
, the random walk decides to jump to the right with probability
p_{-}
or to the left with probability
1-p_{-}
. This is part of a ongoing project of the authors, which also includes a more detailed study of the long time behavior of a random walk in last-passage percolation environments.

1.6
Overview of the Paper

In Section 2, we present the graphical construction of Hammersley’s process with sources and sinks, along with key results concerning the geometry of maximal paths, which serves as the relevant quantity for establishing domination at different densities. The section concludes with the proof of the sprinkled decoupling inequality (Theorem
1
). The proof of the analog result in the exponential LPP model (Theorem
4
) follows the same lines and we only present the script to achieve it. In Section 3, we demonstrate that, for sufficiently large jumps, an intruder can successfully evade detection (Theorem
2
). The random walk result (Theorem
3
) is obtained in Section 4. Both results are obtained through a combination of a renormalization scheme argument and the sprinkled decoupling inequality. For the sake of completeness, in the appendix, we provide the proof of a basic large deviation result for the Poisson distribution, that is used in the proofs.

2
Sprinkled Decoupling for Hammersley’s Process

2.1
Graphical Construction and Last-Passage Percolation

Hammersley’s process can be constructed by considering a Poisson point process
\omega
on
{\mathbb{R}}\times(0,\infty)
with parameter
1
, independently of
\eta^{\lambda}_{0}
, and by setting that if
(x,t)\in\omega
then at time
t
the particle closest to
x
, among those to the right of
x
, moves to
x
. This graphical construction allow us to describe this particle system in terms of a Poisson last-passage percolation model as follows. Call a sequence
(x_{1},t_{1}),\dots,(x_{k},t_{k})
of planar points an increasing path if
(x_{j},t_{j})<(x_{j+1},t_{j+1})
(i.e.
x_{j}<x_{j+1}
and
t_{j}<t_{j+1}
) for all
j=1,\dots,k-1
. The last-passage percolation time
L((z,s),(x,t))
between
(z,s)<(x,t)
is the maximal number of Poisson points in
\omega
among all increasing paths of Poisson points lying in the rectangle
(z,x]\times(s,t]
. For
x\in{\mathbb{R}}
and
t>0
define

L^{\lambda}_{t}(x)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny$def.$}}}{{=}}\sup\left\{M_{0}^{\lambda}(z)+L((z,0),(x,t))\,:\,z\in(-\infty,x]\right\}\,,

(2.1)

where

M^{\lambda}(z)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny$def.$}}}{{=}}\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\eta_{0}^{\lambda}((0,z])&\mbox{ for }z>0\\
-\eta_{0}^{\lambda}((z,0])&\mbox{ for }z\leq 0\,.\end{array}\right.

Then
L_{t}^{\lambda}(x)
is well defined (Lemma 6 from
[
2
]
), and it can be seen as the flux of particles through the space-time line from
(0,0)
to
(x,t)
:

\eta^{\lambda}_{t}((x,y])=L_{t}^{\lambda}(y)-L_{t}^{\lambda}(x)\,,

(2.2)

for all
t\geq 0
and
x,y\in{\mathbb{R}}
with
x<y
(Figure 3). In the exponential LPP model we have a similar evolution given by (
1.1
).

Figure 3:
In this picture,
L^{\lambda}_{t}(x)=3
,
L_{t}^{\lambda}(0)=1
and
\eta^{\lambda}_{t}((0,x])=2
. A maximal path from
(0,0)
to
(x,t)
is drawn with dotted broken lines. In the sources (
\bullet
) and sinks (
\triangle
) formulation with
B=[0,x]\times[0,t]
, we have two sources in
[0,x]\times\{0\}
and one sink in
\{0\}\times[0,t]
. In this setting, a maximal path picks one sink in
\{0\}\times[0,t]
and then follows a maximal path through the Poisson clocks (
\square
).

Now we introduce an useful representation of Hammersley’s process in terms of sources and sinks
[
8
]
. This representation is analogue to the boundary condition with parameter
\alpha\in(0,1)
that we have introduce in the exponential LPP model. Given a space-time box
B=[x_{0},x_{1}]\times[t_{0},t_{1}]
, denote by
\omega^{B}_{v}
the marks (called sinks) generated by the flux of particles through
\{x_{0}\}\times(t_{0},t_{1})
. Then for all
(s,t]\subseteq(t_{0},t_{1})
we have that

\omega^{B}_{v}((s,t])\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny$def.$}}}{{=}}L_{t}^{\lambda}(x_{0})-L_{s}^{\lambda}(x_{0})\,,

and
\omega^{B}_{v}
is a Poisson point process of parameter
1/\lambda

[
8
]
. Denote by
\omega^{B}_{h}
the position of the particles (called sources) in
(x_{0},x_{1})\times\{t_{0}\}
. Then for all
(x,y]\subseteq(x_{0},x_{1}]
we have that

\omega^{B}_{h}((x,y])\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny$def.$}}}{{=}}L_{t_{0}}^{\lambda}(y)-L_{t_{0}}^{\lambda}(x)\,,

and
\omega^{B}_{h}
is a Poisson point process of parameter
\lambda

[
8
]
. Finally let
\omega^{B}_{b}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny$def.$}}}{{=}}(x_{0},x_{1})\times(t_{0},t_{1})\cap\omega
denote the space-time clocks within
B
(Figure 3). Then the vector
(\omega^{B}_{v}\,,\,\omega^{B}_{h}\,,\,\omega^{B}_{b})
, with sinks, sources and clocks, has independent entries
[
8
]
. The dynamic programming property for last-passage percolation times states that we can represent the particle trajectories inside
B
as

{\mathit{H}}^{\lambda}(B)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny$def.$}}}{{=}}\left(L_{t}^{\lambda,B}(x)\,,\,(x,t)\in B\right)=F(\omega^{B}_{v}\,,\,\omega^{B}_{h}\,,\,\omega^{B}_{b})\,,

(2.3)

where
F
is the optimization deterministic function that constructs the last-passage percolation times
L^{\lambda,B}
inside
B
using
(\omega^{B}_{v}\,,\,\omega^{B}_{h}\,,\,\omega^{B}_{b})

[
8
]
as follows:

(x,t)\in B\mapsto L^{\lambda,B}_{t}(x)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny$def.$}}}{{=}}\sup\left\{M^{\lambda,B}(z)+L^{B}\left(z,(x,t)\right)\,:\,z\in[-(t_{1}-t_{0}),x_{1}-x_{0}]\right\}\,,

where

L^{B}(z,(x,t))\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny$def.$}}}{{=}}\begin{cases}L((z+x_{0},t_{0}),(x,t))\,\,\,\,\,\mbox{ if }z\in(0,x_{1}-x_{0}]\\
L((x_{0},t_{0}-z),(x,t))\,\,\mbox{ if }z\in[t_{0}-t_{1},0]\,,\end{cases}

and

M^{\lambda,B}(z)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny$def.$}}}{{=}}\begin{cases}M^{\lambda,B}_{h}(z)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny$def.$}}}{{=}}\omega^{B}_{v}\left((x_{0},x_{0}+z]\times\{t_{0}\}\right)\,\mbox{ if }z\in(0,x_{1}-x_{0}]\\
M^{\lambda,B}_{v}(z)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny$def.$}}}{{=}}\omega^{B}_{h}\left(\{x_{0}\}\times(t_{0},t_{0}-z]\right)\,\,\mbox{ if }z\in[t_{0}-t_{1},0]\,.\end{cases}

If
A
is a box, with southwest vertex
(a,s)
, that is contained in
B
we define

{\mathit{H}}^{\lambda}(B)\mid_{A}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny$def.$}}}{{=}}\left(L_{t}^{\lambda,B}(x)-L_{s}^{\lambda,B}(a)\,,\,(x,t)\in A\right)\,.

The dynamic programming property for last-passage percolation times also implies that

\mbox{ if }A\subseteq B\mbox{ then }{\mathit{H}}^{\lambda}(B)\mid_{A}={\mathit{H}}^{\lambda}(A)\,.

(2.4)

2.2
Domination via Exit-Points

The suprema in the definition of
L^{\lambda}_{t}(x)
is attained (possible in more than one point
z
), and one can define the right most “exit-point” as

Z^{\lambda}_{t}(x)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny$def.$}}}{{=}}\sup\left\{z\in(-\infty,x]\,:\,L^{\lambda}_{t}(x)=M^{\lambda}(z)+L((z,0),(x,t))\right\}\,.

(2.5)

The picture we have in mind is that, in the last-passage percolation model with boundary
M^{\lambda}
, a maximal path first collects the Poisson points in the horizontal axis until the “exit point”
Z^{\lambda}
, and then collects the Poisson clocks following and increasing path. In Figure
3
, the right most maximal path goes left
(Z^{\lambda}_{t}(x)<0)
to reach the maximal path thought the Poisson clocks. It is worth to notice the following monotonicity of exit-points:

Z^{\lambda}_{t}(x)\leq Z^{\lambda}_{t}(y)\mbox{ for all }y\geq x\mbox{ and }Z^{\lambda}_{u}(x)\leq Z^{\lambda}_{t}(x)\mbox{ for all }u\geq t\,.

The graphical construction allows us to run simultaneously two Hammersley’s interacting particle processes with different initial profiles and using the same “clocks”
\omega
, known as the basic coupling. If we take
\eta^{\lambda}_{0}
and
\eta^{\lambda^{\prime}}_{0}
independently, then
\eta^{\lambda^{\prime}}
is independent of
\eta^{\lambda}_{0}
. The key to compare particle configurations starting from different profiles, under the basic coupling, is the following lemma. Similar versions of this lemma can be found in
[
9
,
10
,
11
,
19
]
, and the proof in our context follows the same lines. For the sake of completeness, we decide to include it in this article.

Lemma 1

Let
a<b
and
t\geq 0
. If
Z^{\lambda}_{t}(b)\leq Z^{\lambda^{\prime}}_{t}(a)
then

L^{\lambda}_{t}(y)-L_{t}^{\lambda}(x)\leq L_{t}^{\lambda^{\prime}}(y)-L_{t}^{\lambda^{\prime}}(x)\,,\,\mbox{ for all }x,y\in[a,b]\mbox{ with }x<y\,.

Proof of Lemma
1

An increasing path
\gamma
from
(z,s)
to
(x,t)
will be viewed as the lowest non-decreasing continuous path connecting all the points, starting at
(z,s)
and ending at
(x,t)
. In this way, we can talk about crossings with other paths or with lines. The geodesic between
(z,s)
and
(x,t)
is given by the lowest path (in the sense we just described) that attains the maximum in the definition of
L((z,s),(x,t))
. We will denote this geodesic by
\pi((z,s),(x,t))
. Notice that

L((z,s),(x,t))=L((z,s),(y,r))+L((y,r),(x,t))\,,

for any
(y,r)\in\pi((z,s),(x,t))
.

Denote
z_{1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny$def.$}}}{{=}}Z_{t}^{\lambda}(y)
and
z_{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny$def.$}}}{{=}}Z_{t}^{\lambda^{\prime}}(x)
. Let
{\mathbf{c}}
be a crossing between the two geodesics
\pi((z_{1},0),(y,t))
and
\pi((z_{2},0),(x,t))
. Such a crossing exists because
a<x<y<b
and

z_{1}=Z_{t}^{\lambda}(y)\leq Z_{t}^{\lambda}(b)\leq Z_{t}^{\lambda^{\prime}}(a)\leq Z_{t}^{\lambda^{\prime}}(x)=z_{2}\,.

We remark that, by superaddivity,

L^{\lambda^{\prime}}_{t}(y)\geq M^{\lambda^{\prime}}(z_{2})+L((z_{2},0),(y,t))\geq M^{\lambda^{\prime}}(z_{2})+L((z_{2},0),{\mathbf{c}})+L({\mathbf{c}},(y,t))\,.

We use this, and that (since
{\mathbf{c}}\in\pi([z_{2}]_{0},[x]_{t})
)

M^{\lambda^{\prime}}(z_{2})+L((z_{2},0),{\mathbf{c}})-L^{\lambda^{\prime}}_{t}(x)=-L({\mathbf{c}},(x,t))\,,

in the following inequality:

\displaystyle L^{\lambda^{\prime}}_{t}(y)-L^{\lambda^{\prime}}_{t}(x)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle M^{\lambda^{\prime}}(z_{2})+L((z_{2},0),{\mathbf{c}})+L({\mathbf{c}},(y,t))-L^{\lambda^{\prime}}_{t}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle L({\mathbf{c}},(y,t))-L({\mathbf{c}},(x,t))\,.

By superaddivity,

-L({\mathbf{c}}\,,\,(x,t))\geq L^{\lambda}({\mathbf{c}})-L_{t}^{\lambda}(x)\,,

and hence (since
{\mathbf{c}}\in\pi((z_{1},0),(y,t))
)

\displaystyle L^{\lambda^{\prime}}(y,t)-L^{\lambda^{\prime}}_{t}(x)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle L({\mathbf{c}},(y,t))-L({\mathbf{c}},(x,t))

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle L({\mathbf{c}},(y,t))+L^{\lambda}({\mathbf{c}})-L^{\lambda}_{t}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle L^{\lambda}_{t}(y)-L^{\lambda}_{t}(x)\,.

\Box

Looking at (
2.2
) and Lemma
1
we see that, to dominate
\eta^{\lambda}_{t}
by
\eta_{t}^{\lambda^{\prime}}
in a finite interval
[a,b]
, one needs to control the relative positions of
Z^{\lambda}_{t}(b)
and
Z^{\lambda^{\prime}}_{t}(a)
, which can be obtained by using a large deviation bound for exit points. By translation invariance of
\eta_{0}^{\lambda}
and
\omega
,

Z_{t}^{\lambda}(x+h)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny$dist.$}}}{{=}}Z_{t}(x)+h\,,

(2.6)

and by the invariance of
\omega
under the map
(x,t)\mapsto(\lambda x,t/\lambda)
,

\lambda Z^{\lambda}_{t}(x)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny$dist.$}}}{{=}}Z^{1}_{t/\lambda}(\lambda x)\,.

(2.7)

The exit point
Z^{\lambda}_{t}(x)
satisfies a law of large numbers
[
8
,
9
]
,

\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{Z^{\lambda}_{t}(\lambda^{2}t)}{t}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny$a.s.$}}}{{=}}0\,.

Together with (
2.6
), this says that the location of the exit-point follows, going backward in time, the characteristic direction of the system. To control large deviations, we prove the following lemma.

Lemma 2

There exists an universal constant
c_{0}>0
such that for all
\delta\in(0,3/4]

\max\Big\{{\mathbb{P}}\left[Z^{1}_{t}(t)>\delta t\right]\,,\,{\mathbb{P}}\left[Z^{1}_{t}(t)<-\delta t\right]\Big\}\leq 5e^{-c_{0}\delta^{4}t}\mbox{ for all }t>0\,.

Proof of Lemma
2

By stationarity, one can think that we have Hammersley’s process running for
t\in{\mathbb{R}}
. The flux of particles through the vertical axis
(L^{\lambda}_{t}(0)\,,\,t\in{\mathbb{R}})
, seen as a point process, is also Poisson with parameter
1/\lambda
. By using reflection in the diagonal,

{\mathbb{P}}\left[Z^{\lambda}_{t}(x)<0\right]\leq{\mathbb{P}}\left[Z^{1/\lambda}_{x}(t)>0\right]\,.

By combining this with translation invariance (
2.6
), we get that

{\mathbb{P}}\left[Z^{1}_{t}(t)<-u\right]={\mathbb{P}}\left[Z^{1}_{t}(t+u)<0\right]\\
\leq{\mathbb{P}}\left[Z^{1}_{t+u}(t)>0\right]={\mathbb{P}}\left[Z^{1}_{t+u}(t+u)>u\right]\,,

and to obtain the desired bound we only need to deal with
{\mathbb{P}}\left[Z^{1}_{t}(t)>\delta t\right]
. For that, we parallel the arguments developed in
[
9
]
(see Theorem 4.2 there) in which they prove
2/3
asymptotics for
Z^{\lambda}
, which also works for large deviation bounds.

Let
\lambda>1
and take
M^{\lambda}
a Poisson process of parameter
\lambda
obtained by thickening of
M^{1}
. By definition of
L^{\lambda}
,

L\left((z,0),(t,t)\right)\leq L_{t}^{\lambda}(t)-M^{\lambda}(z)\,,\mbox{ for all }z\leq t\,,

and hence

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\left[Z^{1}_{t}(t)>u\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\left[\exists\,z\in(u,t]\,:\,M^{1}(z)+L\left((z,0),(t,t)\right)=L^{1}_{t}(t)\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\left[\exists\,z\in(u,t]\,:\,M^{1}(z)+L_{t}^{\lambda}(t)-M^{\lambda}(z)\geq L^{1}_{t}(t)\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\left[\exists\,z\in(u,t]\,:\,M^{\lambda}(z)-M^{1}(z)\leq L^{\lambda}_{t}(t)-L^{1}_{t}(t)\right]

By construction,
M^{\lambda}(z)-M^{1}(z)
is increasing as a function of
z
, and
M^{\lambda}-M^{1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny$dist.$}}}{{=}}M^{\lambda-1}
. Thus

{\mathbb{P}}\left[Z^{1}_{t}(t)>u\right]\leq{\mathbb{P}}\left[M^{\lambda}(u)-M^{1}(u)\leq L^{\lambda}_{t}(t)-L^{1}_{t}(t)\right]\,,

and to obtain a useful bound we maximize

{\mathbb{E}}\left[M^{\lambda-1}(u)\right]-{\mathbb{E}}\left[L^{\lambda}_{t}(t)-L^{1}_{t}(t)\right]=(\lambda-1)u-t\left(\lambda+\frac{1}{\lambda}-2\right)\,,

as a function of
\lambda>1
, and choose

\lambda_{u/t}=\left(1-u/t\right)^{-1/2}>1\,.

For all
u\leq\frac{3}{4}t
we have that

{\mathbb{E}}\left[M^{\lambda_{u/t}-1}(u)\right]-{\mathbb{E}}\left[L^{\lambda_{u/t}}_{t}(t)-L^{1}_{t}(t)\right]\geq\frac{u^{2}}{4t}\,,

and hence

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\left[Z^{1}_{t}(t)>u\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\left[M^{\lambda_{u/t}}(u)-M^{1}(u)\leq L^{\lambda_{u/t}}_{t}(t)-L^{1}_{t}(t)\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\left[M^{\lambda_{u/t}-1}(u)\leq{\mathbb{E}}\left[M^{\lambda_{u/t}-1}(u)\right]-u^{2}/(8t)\right]

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\left[L^{\lambda_{u/t}}_{t}(t)-L^{1}_{t}(t)\geq{\mathbb{E}}\left[M^{\lambda_{u/t}-1}(u)\right]-u^{2}/(8t)\right]

\displaystyle\leq

weights in the bulk. For the exponential LPP model, by continuity of the weights, we do not need to take the right most argument that maximizes this sum. Once we have that, the analogue to Lemma
1
follows by the same argument. Hence, in order to prove the exponential LPP version of Lemma
3
, we only need to have good control of the exit point location. For the exponential model we do not have invariance under linear maps that preserve volume, nevertheless given
I=[\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}]\subseteq(0,1)
one can find a constant
c_{I}>0
such that for all
\alpha\in I
it holds that

\max\Big\{{\mathbb{P}}\left[X^{\alpha}_{n}(\lfloor\beta n\rfloor)>\epsilon n\right]\,,\,{\mathbb{P}}\left[X^{\alpha}_{n}(\lfloor\beta n\rfloor)<-\epsilon n\right]\Big\}\leq 5e^{-c_{I}\epsilon^{4}n}\mbox{ for all }n\geq 1\,.

Recall that
\beta=\beta(\alpha)=(1-\alpha)^{-2}\alpha^{2}
is the characteristic speed of the exponential LPP model with parameter
\alpha\in(0,1)
. On one hand, we do not believe that this large deviation bound is sharp, and one can possibly improve it by using Theorem 3.5 of
[
22
]
(see also
[
12
]
) for the tail of the exit point distribution under the
n^{2/3}
scaling. On the other hand, it is enough for our purposes and can be obtained mutatis mutandis by paralleling the same lines as the ones for the Poisson model (Lemma
2
). In order to produce independence by modifying the boundary condition (using Lemma 4.2 of
[
4
]
), as in (
2.10
) and (
2.13
), one introduce null weights along the vertical axis, and notice that the analogue of (
2.11
) (
2.14
) still holds in the lattice LPP context. To conclude lateral decoupling, one can adapt the arguments in the proof of Lemma
4
to the exponential model, or use sharper results such as Corollary 3.6 of
[
22
]
. Once all these ingredients are gathered, the proof of Theorem 4 follows the same recipe used in the proof of Theorem 1.

3
Renormalization Scheme for the Detection Problem

The proof of Theorem
2
uses a multiscale renormalization scheme developed by Baldasso and Texeira in
[
5
]
, that requires a suitable decay of correlation. If the perimeter of a box is of order
l
and
\epsilon\sim l^{-\delta}
, then
\epsilon^{4}l\sim l^{1-4\delta}
and
\epsilon^{-1}l\sim l^{1+\delta}
. In order to use the renormalization scheme developed in
[
5
]
, it is enough to have that
1-4\delta>0
, to get a summable upper bound (Theorem
1
), and
1+\delta<3/2
, to ensure that boxes are far apart (see (2.24) and Lemma 2.7
[
5
]
). With this in mind,
\delta<1/4
will suffice for our purposes. We start by setting the details of the collection of oriented paths that will be used.

Definition 1

Fix
R\geq 3
and let
{\mathcal{S}}_{R}
be the collection of functions
f:{\mathbb{N}}\to{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}
that satisfies

f(n+1)-f(n)=(r,1)\,\mbox{ for some }|r|\leq R\,.

For
f\in{\mathcal{S}}_{R}
define
\tilde{f}:{\mathbb{R}}_{+}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
as the linear interpolation of
f
:

\tilde{f}(t)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny$def.$}}}{{=}}(1+\lfloor t\rfloor-t)f(\lfloor t\rfloor)+(t-\lfloor t\rfloor)f(\lfloor t\rfloor+1)\,.

We say that
f,g\in{\mathcal{S}}_{R}
can be concatenated when there exists
s,t\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
such that
\tilde{f}(t)=\tilde{g}(s)=z_{0}
for some
z_{0}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}
, and define a concatenation
h:{\mathbb{N}}\to{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}
of
f
and
g
at
z_{0}
as

h(n)=\begin{cases}f(n)\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\mbox{ if }n\geq s\,,\\
g(\lfloor t\rfloor-\lfloor s\rfloor+n)\,\,\mbox{ if }n>s\,.\end{cases}

We refer the elements of
{\mathcal{S}}_{R}
as (oriented)
paths
. Notice that the collection of paths
{\mathcal{S}}_{R}
is closed under concatenation in the sense that
h\in{\mathcal{S}}_{R}
for every
f
and
g\in{\mathcal{S}}_{R}
that can be concatenated.

For the multiscale renormalization scheme, we follow
[
5
]
and define a sequence of scales

l_{0}=10^{100}\,,\,l_{k+1}=\lfloor l_{k}^{1/2}\rfloor l_{k}\,\mbox{ and }\,L_{k}=\left\lfloor\left(\frac{3}{2}+\frac{1}{k}\right)l_{k}\right\rfloor\,.

Then

\frac{l_{k}^{3/2}}{2}\leq l_{k+1}\leq l^{3/2}_{k}\,\mbox{ and }\,l_{k}\leq L_{k}\leq 2l_{k}\,,

if
k
is large enough. We also have a sequence of sets

A_{k}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny$def.$}}}{{=}}[0,l_{k}]\times[0,L_{k}]\cup[l_{k},l_{k}+L_{k}]\times[L_{k},l_{k}+L_{k}]\,,

and a sequence of associated boxes
B_{k}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny$def.$}}}{{=}}[0,l_{k}+L_{k}]\times[0,l_{k}+L_{k}]
. We say that a path
f\in{\mathcal{S}}_{R}
is a crossing of
A_{k}
if there exists
T_{f}>0
such that

•

f(0)\in[0,l_{k}]\times\{0\}
;

•

\tilde{f}(T_{f})\in\{l_{k}+L_{k}\}\times[L_{k},l_{k}+L_{k}]
;

•

\tilde{f}(t)\in A_{k}
for all
t\in[0,T_{f}]
.

Given a probability space
(\Omega,{\mathbb{P}},{\mathcal{F}})
, a site percolation model is based on a random element
X:\Omega\to\{0,1\}^{{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}}
. In the percolation vocabulary,
X_{\mathbf{z}}=1
means that site
{\mathbf{z}}
is open (for percolation), and closed otherwise. We denote as
{\mathcal{I}}=\{{\mathbf{z}}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}\,:\,X_{\mathbf{z}}=1\}
the set of open sites, and the main question in this oriented percolation model is to characterize the almost sure existence of an infinite path
f\in{\mathcal{S}}_{R}
such that
f(n)\in{\mathcal{I}}
for all
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
. We say that
f\in{\mathcal{S}}_{R}
is an open crossing of
A_{k}
if it is a crossing of
A_{k}
and
X_{f(n)}=1
for all
n\in[0,T_{f}]\cap{\mathbb{N}}
. The multiscale renormalization scheme allow us attack this question by looking at the decay of the probability of the event

D_{k}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny$def.$}}}{{=}}\left\{\mbox{there exists no open crossing }\,f\in{\mathcal{S}}_{R}\,\mbox{ of }A_{k}\right\}\,.

For
{\mathbf{z}}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}
let
A_{k}({\mathbf{z}})
and
B_{k}({\mathbf{z}})
be the translated sets of
A_{k}
and
B_{k}
, respectively, and let
D_{k}({\mathbf{z}})
be the event that there exists no open crossing
f\in{\mathcal{S}}_{R}
of
A_{k}({\mathbf{z}})
. It is worth to notice that the event
D_{k}({\mathbf{z}})
has support in the box
B_{k}({\mathbf{z}})
, and that its characteristic function is non-increasing (with respect to the configuration of zeros and ones).

In our context we assume that we can construct simultaneously (coupling) a family of translation invariant random elements
X^{\rho}:\Omega\to\{0,1\}^{{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}}
, indexed by
\rho>0
, such that
{\mathcal{I}}^{\rho}\subseteq{\mathcal{I}}^{\rho^{\prime}}
for
\rho\leq\rho^{\prime}
, which is equivalent to
X_{\mathbf{z}}^{\rho}\leq X_{\mathbf{z}}^{\rho^{\prime}}
for all
{\mathbf{z}}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}
(that induces a partial order in
\{0,1\}^{{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}}
). We further require a sprinkling decoupling property for this family of random elements, as stated below. The probability law of each random element
X^{\rho}
is denoted
{\mathbb{P}}^{\rho}
, and expectation
{\mathbb{E}}^{\rho}
.

Hypothesis 1

Let
0<a<b
. If
u\in I=(a,b)
, there exist constants
C_{I},\epsilon_{I}>0
for which the following holds: for all
\epsilon\in(0,\epsilon_{I})
if
f_{i}:\{0,1\}^{{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}}\to[0,1]
for
i=1,2
are non-increasing functions with support
B_{i}
such that

{\rm d}(B_{1},B_{2})\geq C_{I}\epsilon^{-1}\left({\rm per}(B_{1})+{\rm per}(B_{2})\right)\,,

then

{\mathbb{E}}^{\rho}\left[f_{1}f_{2}\right]\leq{\mathbb{E}}^{\rho-\epsilon}\left[f_{1}\right]{\mathbb{E}}^{\rho-\epsilon}\left[f_{2}\right]+H\left(\epsilon,{\rm d}(B_{1},B_{2})\right)\,,

where
H
is non-decreasing in each of the variables with

\lim_{x\to\infty}x^{7}H(x^{-\delta_{0}},x)=0\,,

(3.1)

for some
\delta_{0}>0
.

We note that, since
H
is non-decreasing, if (
3.1
) holds for some
\delta_{0}>0
then it also holds for all
\delta\in(0,\delta_{0}]
. Fix
\rho>0
and
\delta>0
. Since
l_{k}
has a super-exponential growth, one can define an increasing sequence
(\rho_{k})_{k\geq 0}
with
\rho_{0}>0
,

\rho_{k+1}-\rho_{k}=c_{\rho}l_{k}^{-\delta}

and
\lim\rho_{k}=\rho
, where
c_{\rho}>0
is a scale factor that only depends on
\rho>0
and
l_{0}
. We can now announce the percolation result that we will use to prove Theorem
2
.

Theorem 5

Under Hypothesis
1
, let

p_{k}={\mathbb{P}}^{\rho_{k}}\left[D_{k}\right]\,.

There exists
\tilde{k}\geq 1
such that if (trigger inequality)

p_{k}\leq l_{k}^{-4}\mbox{ for some }k\geq\tilde{k}\,,

then

p_{n}\leq l_{n}^{-4}\mbox{ for all }n\geq k\,.

In particular,

{\mathbb{P}}^{\rho}\left[\mbox{there exists an infinite open path }f\in{\mathcal{S}}_{R}\right]=1\,.

The proof of this theorem is based on the proof of Theorem 2.5 and Theorem 2.9 from
[
5
]
, and it starts with a small modification of Lemma 2.7 of
[
5
]
, that relates the events
D_{k}
and
D_{k-1}
.

Lemma 5

Let
\upsilon\in(0,1/2)
. There exists
k_{0}\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that, for each
k\geq k_{0}
, there exists
M_{k}\subseteq{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}
satisfying:

1.

\#M_{k}\leq 10l_{k-1}^{1/2}
;

2.

If
D_{k}
happens, there exists
{\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}}\in M_{k}
such that
D_{k-1}({\mathbf{x}})
and
D_{k-1}({\mathbf{y}})
happen and

{\rm d}\left(B_{k-1}({\mathbf{x}}),B_{k-1}({\mathbf{y}})\right)\geq\left({\rm per}(B_{k-1}({\mathbf{x}})+{\rm per}(B_{k-1}({\mathbf{y}})\right)^{3/2-\upsilon}\,.

Proof of Lemma
5

Following the lines of
[
5
]
we define the collection of sets

\left\{A_{k-1}({\mathbf{x}}_{j}):0\leq j\leq\frac{L_{k}+l_{k}}{L_{k-1}}\right\}\,,

with
{\mathbf{x}}_{j}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny$def.$}}}{{=}}j(l_{k},L_{k})
and their corresponding reflection with respect to the diagonal of
A_{k}
. Through concatenation, open crossings of each of these
2\left(\frac{L_{k}+l_{k}}{L_{k-1}}+1\right)
sets produce an open crossing of
A_{k}

(see Figure 2.2 of
[
5
]
). The same can be said about the collection of sets

\left\{A_{k-1}({\mathbf{y}}_{j}):0\leq j\leq\frac{L_{k}}{L_{k-1}}\right\}

with
{\mathbf{y}}_{j}=(l_{k},L_{k})-j(l_{k-1},L_{k-1})
and their corresponding reflection with respect to the diagonal of
A_{k}
. As in
[
5
]
, we take
M_{k}
to be the set of all points
{\mathbf{x}}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}
such that
A_{k-1}({\mathbf{x}})
is in some of the two collections described above. Then (1) follows from Lemma 2.7 in
[
5
]
. By computation
(2.24)
from
[
5
]
, we have that

{\rm d}(B_{k-1}({\mathbf{x}}),B_{k-1}({\mathbf{y}}))\geq Cl_{k-1}^{\frac{3}{2}}k^{-2}\,,

for some
C>0
, which implies (2), since
l_{k}
has a super-exponential growth and
{\rm per}(B_{k-1})\sim l_{k-1}
.

\Box

Proof of Theorem
5

Lemma
5
allows to bound the probability of
D_{k+1}
by the probability that at least one element of each of these two previous collection of sets does not admit an open crossing. If we take
\upsilon\in(0,1/2)
such that
\delta<\min\{1/2-\upsilon,\delta_{0}\}
, set
\epsilon_{k}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny$def.$}}}{{=}}c_{\rho}l_{k}^{-\delta}
, and use (2) from Lemma

, then

{\rm d}\left(B_{k}({\mathbf{x}}),B_{k}({\mathbf{y}})\right)\geq\left({\rm per}(B_{k}({\mathbf{x}})+{\rm per}(B_{k}({\mathbf{y}})\right)^{3/2-\upsilon}\geq C_{I}\epsilon_{k}^{-1}\left({\rm per}(B_{k}({\mathbf{x}})+{\rm per}(B_{k}({\mathbf{y}})\right)\,,

for large enough

. The last inequality gives the condition needed for Hypothesis
1
. Taking
f_{1},f_{2}
as the non existence of an open crossing for
B_{k}({\mathbf{x}})
and
B_{k}({\mathbf{y}})
respectively, by (1)
from Lemma
5
together with the union bound,

p_{n+1}\leq\#M_{n}^{2}\left(p_{n}^{2}+H(c_{\rho}l_{n}^{-\delta},C_{I}l_{n})\right)\,.

Thus, by (
3.1
) in Hypothesis
1
, we can take
\tilde{k}\geq k_{0}
sufficiently large such that

100l_{k}^{-1}+100l_{k}^{7}H(c_{\rho}l_{k}^{-\delta},C_{I}l_{k})\leq 1\,,\,\forall\,k\geq\tilde{k}\,.

By induction, if
p_{k}l_{k}^{4}\leq 1
for some

, then for

we have that

\displaystyle p_{n+1}l_{n+1}^{4}

\displaystyle\leq\left(10l_{n-1}^{1/2}\right)^{2}l_{n+1}^{4}\left(l_{n}^{-8}+H(c_{\rho}l_{n}^{-\delta},C_{I}l_{n})\right)

\displaystyle\leq 100l_{n}\left(l_{n}^{3/2}\right)^{4}\left(l_{n}^{-8}+H(c_{\rho}l_{n}^{-\delta},C_{I}l_{n})\right)

\displaystyle\leq 100l_{n}^{-1}+100l_{n}^{7}H(c_{\rho}l_{n}^{-\delta},C_{I}l_{n})

\displaystyle\leq 1.

Finally, as in
[

, we notice that by Borel-Cantelli there is an open crossing of

for all

for all sufficiently large

at

implies the existence at

for the parameter

3.1
Proof of Theorem
2

For the sake of simplicity, we take
\Delta_{s}=\Delta_{t}=1
. For
r>0
let
I_{x,r}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny$def.$}}}{{=}}(x-r,x+r)
. Given
\rho>0
let
\lambda\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny$def.$}}}{{=}}\rho^{-1/2}>0
(so that
\rho=\lambda^{-2}
). To link the detection problem to a percolation model we define for
(x,n)\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}

X^{\rho}_{(x,n)}=\begin{cases}1\mbox{ (open) }\,,\mbox{ if }\eta^{\lambda}_{t}(I_{x,r})=0\,\,\forall\,t\in[n,n+1)\,,\\
0\mbox{ (closed) }\,,\,\mbox{ otherwise }\,.\end{cases}

If
X^{\rho}_{(x,n)}=1
then a target at
x
is not detected by Hammersley’s process for the period of time
t\in[n,n+1)
. Recall that
{\mathcal{I}}^{\rho}
denotes the collection of open sites, and that, and by attractiveness of Hammersley’s process, we can take
({\mathcal{I}}^{\rho})_{\rho>0}
as an increasing family of subsets of
{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}
. Then percolation of the set
{\mathcal{I}}^{\rho}
using the set of paths
{\mathcal{S}}_{R}
implies non-detection with positive probability. We have to verify that this percolation system satisfies the necessary conditions. By space-time stationarity of Hammersley’s process, it is clearly translation invariant, and Hypothesis
1
follows from Theorem
1
. To obtain the trigger inequality we set again
\lambda_{k}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny$def.$}}}{{=}}\rho_{k}^{-1/2}
(so that
\rho_{k}=\lambda_{k}^{-2}
), and emphasize the following geometrical remarks:

(i)

For
N_{k}=L_{k}+\lfloor l_{k}\rfloor+1
(which bounds the size of the jumps), to obtain an open
{\mathcal{S}}_{N_{k}}
-crossing of
A_{k}
it is sufficient and necessary to have an open vertical
{\mathcal{S}}_{N_{k}}
-crossing of
[0,l_{k}]\times[0,L_{k}]
since, in such a case, one can do one step of size
N_{k}
to exit on the right side of the top part of
A_{k}
;

(ii)

If at every horizontal level
[0,l_{k}]\times\{j\}
with
j\in[0,L_{k})
we have a open site, than one can build an open vertical
{\mathcal{S}}_{N_{k}}
-crossing of
[0,l_{k}]\times[0,L_{k}]
by jumping from level to level over open sites (recall that
N_{k}>l_{k}
is large enough for that).

Now, if
\eta^{\lambda_{k}}_{n}(I_{x,r})=0
and there is no
\omega
space-time Poisson clock in
I_{x,r}\times[n,n+1)
then
X^{\rho_{k}}_{(x,n)}=1
. Hence

\{X^{\rho_{k}}_{(x,n)}=0\}\subseteq J_{(x,n)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny$def.$}}}{{=}}\{\eta^{\lambda_{k}}_{n}(I_{x,r})>0\mbox{ or }\omega\left(I_{x,r}\times[n,n+1)\right)>0\}\,.

A simple calculation shows that

{\mathbb{P}}^{\lambda_{k}}\left[J_{(x,n)}\right]=1-e^{-2r\lambda_{k}}e^{-2r}=1-e^{-2r(\lambda_{k}+1)}\leq 1-e^{-2r(\lambda_{0}+1)}\,,

since
\lambda_{k}\leq\lambda_{0}
(recall that
0<\rho_{0}\leq\rho_{k}
). If
|x-y|\geq 2r
then
I_{x,r}\cap I_{y,r}=\emptyset
which implies that
J_{(x,n)}
and
J_{(y,n)}
are independent. Choosing
N_{k}=L_{k}+\lfloor l_{k}\rfloor+1
, and using remarks (i) and (ii),

p_{k}={\mathbb{P}}^{\rho_{k}}\left[\mbox{ no open vertical ${\mathcal{S}}_{N_{k}}$-crossing of $[0,l_{k}]\times[0,L_{k}]$ }\right]\\
\leq(L_{k}+1){\mathbb{P}}^{\lambda_{k}}\left[\bigcap_{x\in[0,l_{k}]\cap 2r{\mathbb{N}}}J_{(x,0)}\right]\leq(L_{k}+1)\left(1-e^{-2r(\lambda_{0}+1)}\right)^{\lfloor l_{k}/2r\rfloor}\\
\leq(2l_{k}+1)\left(1-e^{-2r(\lambda_{0}+1)}\right)^{\lfloor l_{k}/2r\rfloor}\,.

This inequality allows us to choose
\tilde{k}\geq 1
such that
l^{4}_{\tilde{k}}p_{\tilde{k}}\leq 1
, which implies the trigger inequality.

Let

By contradiction, assume that we have at most
C_{1}\epsilon_{k}^{-1}
indices
m^{\prime}\in M(X^{\mathrm{y}})
such that

occurs. Now, for each

and if
\tilde{A}_{m^{\prime}}(\tilde{v}_{k})
does not occur then

X^{\mathrm{y}}_{(i+1)hL_{k}}-X^{\mathrm{y}}_{ihL_{k}}\geq\tilde{v}_{k}hL_{k}\,.

Otherwise, we can bound the increment using the deterministic bound

X^{\mathrm{y}}_{(i+1)hL_{k}}-X^{\mathrm{y}}_{ihL_{k}}\geq-hL_{k}\,.

Therefore,

\displaystyle X^{\mathrm{y}}_{hL_{k+1}}-\pi_{1}({\mathrm{y}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{l_{k}}X^{\mathrm{y}}_{(i+1)hL_{k}}-X^{\mathrm{y}}_{ihL_{k}}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle-C_{1}\epsilon_{k}^{-1}hL_{k}+(l_{k}-C_{1}\epsilon_{k}^{-1})\tilde{v}_{k}hL_{k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle hL_{k+1}\left(\tilde{v}_{k}-\frac{C_{1}\epsilon_{k}^{-1}(1+\tilde{v}_{k})}{l_{k}}\right)\,.

In order to have that

\tilde{v}_{k}-\frac{C_{1}\epsilon_{k}^{-1}(1+\tilde{v}_{k})}{l_{k}}=\tilde{v}_{k}-\frac{C_{1}L_{k}^{1/16}(1+\tilde{v}_{k})}{l_{k}}>\tilde{v}_{k+1}=\tilde{v}_{k}-\frac{2C_{1}L^{1/16}_{k}}{l_{k}}\,,

we only need that
C_{1}(1+\tilde{v}_{k})<2C_{1}
, which is true since
v_{k}\in(0,1)
for all
k\geq k_{7}
. This yields to

X^{\mathrm{y}}_{hL_{k+1}}-\pi_{1}({\mathrm{y}})>\tilde{v}_{k+1}hL_{k+1}\,,

which contradicts (
4.2
).

\Box

(4.3)

Therefore, we can choose

Lemma 7

We have that

\max\big\{{\mathbb{P}}\left[{\rm Poi}^{\lambda}\geq\lambda+x\right],{\mathbb{P}}\left[{\rm Poi}^{\lambda}\leq\lambda-x\right]\big\}\leq e^{-\frac{x^{2}}{2(x+\lambda)}}\,,\,\forall\,x>0\,.

Proof of Lemma
7

The moment generating function method applied to the Poisson distribution
[
20
]
implies that

{\mathbb{P}}\left[{\rm Poi}^{\lambda}\geq\lambda+x\right]\leq e^{\inf_{\theta>0}\{\lambda(e^{\theta}-1)-\theta(\lambda+x)\}}=e^{-\frac{x^{2}}{2\lambda}h\left(\frac{x}{\lambda}\right)}\,\,\mbox{ for }x>0\,,

(A.1)

and

{\mathbb{P}}\left[{\rm Poi}^{\lambda}\leq\lambda-x\right]\leq e^{\inf_{\theta>0}\{\lambda(e^{-\theta}-1)+\theta(\lambda-x)\}}=e^{-\frac{x^{2}}{2\lambda}h\left(-\frac{x}{\lambda}\right)}\,\,\mbox{ for }0<x<\lambda\,,

(A.2)

where
h:[-1,\infty)\to{\mathbb{R}}
is the function defined as

h(x)\,=\,\begin{cases}2\frac{(1+x)\log(1+x)-x}{x^{2}}\,\,\mbox{ for }x\neq-1,0\\
2\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\,\,\,\,\,\mbox{ for }x=-1\\
1\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\,\,\,\,\,\mbox{ for }x=0\end{cases}\,=\,\int_{0}^{1}\frac{2(1-s)}{1+sx}ds\,.

To justify the second equality above
[
20
]
, one can use the integral form of Taylor’s theorem,

f(x)-f(0)-xf^{\prime}(0)=\int_{0}^{x}(x-t)f^{\prime\prime}(t)dt=\frac{1}{2}x^{2}\int_{0}^{1}2(1-s)f^{\prime\prime}(sx)ds\,,

with
f(x)=(1+x)\log(1+x)
. Hence,
h
is continuous, decreasing, non-negative, and
g(x)=(1+x)h(x)
is increasing (Figure
4
), which can be deduced straightforward by using the integral form of
h
. Therefore,
h(x)\geq h(0)/(1+x)=1/(1+x)
for
x\geq 0
, and

\frac{x^{2}}{2\lambda}h\left(\frac{x}{\lambda}\right)\geq\frac{x^{2}}{2(x+\lambda)}\,\implies\,{\mathbb{P}}\left[{\rm Poi}^{\lambda}\geq\lambda+x\right]\leq e^{-\frac{x^{2}}{2\lambda}h\left(\frac{x}{\lambda}\right)}\leq e^{-\frac{x^{2}}{2(x+\lambda)}}\,.

For the other bound, we have that

{\mathbb{P}}\left[{\rm Poi}^{\lambda}\leq\lambda-x\right]\leq e^{-\frac{x^{2}}{2\lambda}h\left(-\frac{x}{\lambda}\right)}\leq e^{-\frac{x^{2}}{2\lambda}h(0)}\leq e^{-\frac{x^{2}}{2(\lambda+x)}}\,,

for
0<x<\lambda
. Notice that for
x\geq\lambda
the upper bound is trivial.

\Box
