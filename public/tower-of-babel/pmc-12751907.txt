Evaluation of thermal rise on simulated pulp tissue during light-cured direct capping in vitro: an artificial microcirculation study
Authors: Onur Emir Özkan, Sena Kaşıkçı, Müslüm Arıcı, Olcay Özdemir
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTo evaluate the temperature values including initial, maximum, and rising caused by light-cured materials used for direct capping during polymerisation.MethodsA 3 mm deep, wide, and length class-I cavity was prepared to a premolar tooth, leaving hard tissue at the pulpal floor of 1 mm thickness with a 1 mm diameter of exposure. The sample was placed in a customised mimicry setup in which the temperature was standardised (37 °C) by a double-verified system. Light-curing capping materials (Theracal LC, Theracal PT, Harvard BioCal, and Ultrablend Plus) were placed, polymerised, and temperature values were recorded. Kruskal-Wallis test was used to compare parameters among groups, and Dunn’s test was used to define the group that caused the difference (p < 0.05).ResultsThere were statistically significant differences between the materials in terms of initial, maximum, and temperature rising values (pin=0.014, pmax=0.034, ptr= 0.016). The values of Theracal PT were measured highest in terms of temperature increase, while no statistically significant difference was observed between other materials during polymerisation.ConclusionsTheracal PT caused higher temperature rise, however, it did not exceed 5.6 °C, which is the critical value for the pulp. Theracal PT should be used with caution as its use during direct capping causes high temperature increases, and there is a need for a light-curing regime to be designed for its use.

Introduction

Recent basic science waves and clinical findings have revived vital pulp treatment approaches such as direct pulp capping and partial pulpotomy [1, 2]. The emergence of biological-based management aimed to reduce irreversible and extensive intervention not only in mature cariously affected teeth but also with signs and symptoms indicative of irreversible pulp tissue pathologies [2]. Driven by various brand-new materials, a better understanding of immunity, the biology of pulp tissue, and endodontic pathogens, vital pulp treatment has been in the forefront of management recommendations [3–5].

Many pulp capping agents or application form options such as calcium hydroxide-based, hydraulic calcium silicate-based, or mineral trioxide aggregate (MTA), ready-to-use, hand-mixing or light curing refrain from an inflammatory tissue response that could lead to vascular disorder and necrosis [6–8]. Ultrablend Plus (Ultradent, South Jordan, USA), is a calcium hydroxide-based light-curable cement that includes methacrylate monomers owing to the limited physical and mechanical properties of calcium hydroxide, which chemically-cured and a need for a more stable agent to place on the pulp tissue [9]. Similar to calcium hydroxide-based, to increase the physicochemical properties of MTA, which is the gold standard for vital pulp treatment, while maintaining the biological advantages of the material, light-cured resin-modified MTA-based cements like BioCal-CAP (Harvard Dental International GmbH, Germany) have been introduced [8]. The desirable properties of these components have led to the development of brand-new biomaterials with various compositions, such as resin-modified calcium silicate-based agents [10]. One of them is that TheraCal LC (Bisco Inc., Schaumburg, IL, USA) was introduced as a light-curing material for pulp capping, combining the handling characteristics of resin with the biological properties of calcium silicate [10–12]. More recently, a novel calcium silicate-based dual-cured resin-modified cement, Theracal PT (Bisco Inc., Schaumburg, IL, USA), has been placed on the market. According to the manufacturer, pulpotomies are the primarily indication for the cement and can also be used for directly capping of pulp [11].

The pulp tissue is also sensitive to biological, chemical, physical, and thermal changes because it is highly vascularized, so it responds to thermomechanical stimuli [13, 14]. During the polymerisation of light-curing materials, temperature increases due to the energy absorption based on irradiation and exothermic reaction [15, 16]. It is known that a temperature rise more than 5.6 °C in the pulp chamber can increase the possibility of irreversible damage, while an increase of 16.6 °C causes necrosis [17]. Lau et al. [18] highlighted the lack of evidence investigating the influencing factors and dental procedures regarding temperature changes in the pulp, and experimental models do not fully reflect clinical conditions. Besides, no data is available for the temperature rise of pulp while directly capping when applying light-curing agents.

Considering the response of pulp to the thermal stimuli and the lack of evidence regarding direct pulp capping using light-curing agents’ thermal effect, it is crucial to evaluate the temperature changes of light-polymerising agents for clinical prognosis. Therefore, this study aimed to assess the temperature values during polymerization of light-curing pulp capping agents in vitro, simulating pulp tissue and oral environment variables, with two null hypotheses that the temperature rise remains is in the limits of pulpal response and that there is no difference between the agents.

Methods

The approval of the study’s required ethics committee was obtained from the Kocaeli University Non-Interventional Ethics Committee (KU GOKAEK- 2023/13.12 project No.: 2023/236). The manuscript of this laboratory study has been written according to Preferred Reporting Items for Laboratory studies in Endodontology (PRILE) 2021 guidelines [19]. The sample size was identified via G*Power version V3.1.9.2 software (Kiel University, Kiel, Germany) [20]. It was determined to measure 4 times on a single sample for each group, with an α error probability of 0.05, β error 0.90, and effect size of 2.68136.

Sample selection and Preparation of the tooth model

A premolar tooth without caries, filling, or root canal treatment was included in the study. The sample was stored in a saline solution to prevent drying. In order to measure temperature changes in the pulp chamber, the crown was separated 2 mm, perpendicular to the long axis of the tooth to the cementoenamel junction. Afterwards, the pulp chamber was prepared with a diamond burr, and the pulp residues were removed using an excavator and low-speed carbide bur. Class-I cavity preparations 3 mm deep, 3 mm wide, and 3 mm length were performed, leaving hard tissue at the pulpal floor of 1 mm thickness with a 1 mm diameter of pulp exposure, which were measured using a digital caliper under magnification (Fig. 1).

Establishment of the experimental setup and measurement of temperature changes

The schematic representation of the mimicry design was shown in Fig. 2. In the designed setup, distilled water filled into a container was heated up by a water bath device, and an attempt was made to keep the temperature of the water inside the container constant at 37°C by circulating to simulate the intraoral environment. A setup with a heat exchanger was installed inside the 19-litre container so the fluid temperature could be controlled constantly. Plastic hoses with 3/8” diameter were used between the water bath device and the heat exchanger; the connection points of the hose were wrapped with teflon tape to prevent water leakage. Since the flow rate of the fluid is also a factor in keeping the temperature constant, the flow rate was controlled by a valve. The temperature was controlled via a constant temperature circulation bath (PolyScience PD15RCAL-A12E), adjusting flow passing through the hoses and the heat exchanger. Afterwards, a mechanism was placed that fixed the edges of the container and held the tooth fixed utilising plexiglass. The experimental setup was shown in Fig. 3.

The thermocouple placed inside the tooth was just below the exposure area and without touching any dentinal walls. In order to keep the thermocouple fixed in the adjusted position, it was attached to the tooth from the lower part with the help of flowable composite resin. Then, C-type silicone (Ac gel, Addcosil, Bursa, Türkiye) was placed into the pulp chamber to mimic the pulp connective tissue structure. The sample and the thermocouple cable were wrapped with teflon tape to prevent fluid leakage into the cavity. Half of the tooth sample was placed in the plexiglass device, which submerged in the water (Fig. 4).

The schematic representation of placement of the tooth sample

After the temperature was stabilised, polymerisation processes were conducted. The tooth sample was used 4 times individually for each material in 4 groups, 16 times in total. After each process, the tooth was removed from the setup, the cement residue was cleaned, and the tooth was filled with C-type silicone and wrapped again with Teflon tape. 1200 mW/cm² (3 M Espe Dental Products, St. Paul, MN, USA) was used as the light source at a wavelength of 430–480 nm, 3 mm away from the exposure area perpendicular.

The temperature before polymerisation, the highest temperature during polymerisation, and the temperature after polymerisation were recorded for each process. The temperature changes were calculated by subtracting the initial temperature from the maximum temperature during polymerisation. All curing procedures were conducted according to the time instructions.

After the exposure area was dried with a cotton pellet, the capping agents were applied to the exposure area as a single thin layer with the help of their own cannulas and cured with light according to the manufacturer’s instructions:

Statistical analysis

Data were analysed in IBM SPSS V22 (IBM Corp, Armonk, NY, USA). The normality of the data distribution was tested using the Shapiro–Wilk test, which revealed that the data were not normally distributed. Kruskal-Wallis test was used to compare parameters among groups, and Dunn’s test was used to define the group that caused the difference. The significance level was set at p < 0.05.

Results

In this study, which aimed to evaluate the temperature values including initial, maximum, and rising caused by light-cured materials used for direct pulp capping during polymerisation, the mean values for initial, maximum, and rising of Ultrablend Plus were 36.53 ± 0.05 °C, 39.55 ± 0.66 °C, and 3.03 ± 0.63 °C, respectively. Therecal LC presented 36.48 ± 0.22 °C, 38.33 ± 0.90 °C, and 1.85 ± 1.07 °C values. The initial temperature was 36.18 ± 0.24 °C for Harvard BioCal-Cap, the maximum value was 39.25 ± 1.04 °C, and 3.08 ± 1.11 °C was the change. Therecal PT was measured at 35.90 ± 0.12 °C at the initial, 40.60 ± 0.62 °C was the maximum, and the change was 4.70 ± 0.5 °C. Initial temperature values

There was a statistically significant difference between the various capping agents regarding initial temperatures (p = 0.014). As a result of post hoc analysis revealed that the initial temperature of the Theracal PT was found to be significantly lower than that of Ultrablend Plus (p = 0.013) and Theracal LC (p = 0.019).

Maximum temperature values

A statistically significant difference was found in maximum temperatures (p = 0.034). The maximum temperature of Theracal PT was found to be significantly higher than that of Theracal LC (p = 0.020).

Temperature rise values

A statistically significant difference was observed in terms of temperature changes (p = 0.016). The amount of change in the curing time interval of Theracal PT was found to be significantly higher than that of Ultrablend (p = 0.034) and Theracal LC (p = 0.009).

Discussion

When the pulp tissue senses a temperature change, diverse histopathological alterations that may lead to irreversible injury can occur. Unlike heat transfer to other tissues, due to the only passage of vascularisation being in the apical opening, the heat transfer process is coupled with different physiological processes, such as dentinal fluid flow and limits on pulp blood circulation that affect the thermal behaviour [18, 21]. The damage mechanism includes liquid dilatation in dentin tubules, increased outwards flow from the tubules, protoplasm coagulation, vascular compromise, disruption of tissue integrity, and necrosis [14, 17, 18, 21].

Although the available literature is limited in terms of topic, it has generally focused on the effects of thermal stimuli on pulp tissue from a restorative or prosthetic perspective [18]. Nevertheless, this situation has provided essential data to form an idea about the effects of thermal stimuli on the pulp, and it also reveals the necessity of evaluating the light-curing agents for directly application on the pulp tissue introduced to the market together with the developing material technology, especially when pulpal exposure and pulpotomies are taken into consideration. On the basis of this necessity, this study aimed to assess the temperature values during the polymerisation of light-curing pulp capping agents using a simulation of pulp tissue with its own and oral environment variables, with the two null hypotheses that the temperature changes are in the limits of pulpal response, and there is no difference between the agents. Based on the study results, the first null hypothesis was accepted; however, the second one was rejected for observing significant differences. Despite the differences among the materials, the temperature increase values for all groups were below the critical value of 5.6 °C [17].

The temperature increase during polymerization depends not only on the exothermic reaction of the material but also on the type, intensity, and wavelength of the light source used. The literature has reported that LED and laser-based devices generate different levels of heat during polymerization [22, 23]. Since the same LED device and the same light parameters (light intensity, duration, and distance) were used across all groups in this study, the device-induced temperature effect was standardized. Therefore, the observed differences are thought to be due to the physical properties of the materials, such as their resin content, thermal conductivity, and exothermic reaction profile.

The scientific evidence concluded that these materials (Ultrablend Plus, Harvard BioCal-Cap MTA, Theracal LC, and Theracal PT) are in vitro biocompatible and have significant biomineralisation behaviour [8, 9, 24–27]. However, the mentioned studies were conducted after the polymerisation process despite following the manufacturer’s instructions. The effects that may occur in tissues and cells during the application period, especially during the exothermic chemical polymerisation reaction due to the resin component in which light-curing is involved, constitute a clinical limitation of these studies. Aranha et al. [28] reported that light activation is a critical factor in reducing cytotoxicity, and various curing regimes may prevent the toxic effects on the pulp tissue. At this point, the data obtained from this study are fundamental in terms of clinical prognosis as providing the baseline values ​​that will be pioneers in regulating and developing the application methods of these materials that exhibit in vitro biocompatible properties.

In all evaluated parameters, including initial temperature, maximum temperature, and temperature change values, the only differed agent was Theracal PT. The initial temperature value of Therecal PT was the lowest compared to other pulp capping materials, besides the highest maximum temperature value was registered for this type, resulting into the most significant temperature rise, although the application recommendation was 20 s. This difference suggests that the significant temperature increase observed even under short-term light exposure may be due to higher exothermic heat production during the polymerization reaction depending on the content of the material. The temperature change caused by Theracal PT is below the limit of 5.6 °C, where harmful effects are observed according to the literature [17]; however, it should be noted that the value is highly close to the limits. Therefore, the temperature difference created by this product needs to be evaluated on a time basis during the process, and more evident data regarding the curing time and regime needs to be assessed. Therefore, it can be stated that this gap also constitutes a limitation of this study.

When the highest temperature rates of pulp reached by the materials and the temperature changes were evaluated, the lowest value was observed in the Theracal LC product. The difference between these two products developed by the same company may be due to the difference in the resin monomers they contain, and their exothermic reaction. Sanz et al. [11] reported that the newer introduced product, TheraCal PT, offers a developed in vitro mineralisation potential and cytocompatibility compared with TheraCal LC, its predecessor. According to these data, it should be noted that not only biocompatibility but also heat generation potential should be taken into consideration in material selection in clinical practice. Lakhani et al. [29] in their study examining the temperature increase in the pulp chamber after light activation of new pulp capping materials and composites, they found that TheraCal LC was the group with the lowest temperature increase in the pulp chamber among all pulp capping materials. In this study, Ultrablend Plus exhibited moderate temperature rise values ​​compared to both TheraCal materials. This can be attributed to its calcium hydroxide-based composition combined with a resin component that creates a controlled exothermic reaction during polymerization. Consequently, its thermal behavior places it between TheraCal LC and TheraCal PT in terms of temperature rise.

Most previous studies have employed the existing experimental setups for thermal effects on pulp tissue by using an incubator at 37 °C to create clinical conditions or just using an elastic filling material. This situation is a common limitation of this kind of in vitro studies due to the lack of blood circulation existing in vital pulp tissue and associated heat dissipation [18]. Since the dynamic microcirculation in pulp tissue is able to modulate the thermal changes and inflammatory response, there is a gap in the accurate thermodynamical simulation of the situation. This study designed a thermostatic mechanism to mimic oral conditions, including pulpal microcirculation and oral cavity by a water bath with liquid flow and dual mechanism temperature control that keeps the temperature constant in the pulpal area and the external surface of the crown. The authors believe that this mechanism fills the mimicry limitation that causes a gap in the literature, which is one of this study’s most critical outputs and a novelty. Studies on primary teeth have also used models designed with microcirculation systems that mimic in vivo conditions. The pulpal microcirculation simulation model developed by Ertuğrul et al. for primary teeth provides an environment close to in vivo conditions [30]. Compared to this study, although our model includes a similarly high level of simulation, thermal responses may have been slightly lower than those observed in primary teeth due to variables such as a larger pulp chamber volume and different dentin thickness. Therefore, it should be noted that the temperature increase values ​​we obtained in clinical practice may be slightly higher in primary teeth applications. This model used distilled water as a thermal medium, which cannot replicate human blood, which is a colloidal solution and has different thermodynamic properties. Furthermore, the use of condensation silicone as pulp tissue is also a limiting element, because C silicone is an insulating material and conducts heat poorly. These are all important in vitro limitations, so the results should be taken into account. It is possible that in vivo conditions the temperature increase is even higher than in vitro conditions.

Conclusion

This study compared the thermal effects of various resin-based light-curing pulp-capping agents. The comprehensive methodology ensured a controlled environment to accurately measure and compare temperature values and changes. The major conclusions of the study are as follows: Theracal PT caused higher temperature rise than other materials. However, it did not exceed the maximum limits, which is the critical value for the pulp. No significant difference was observed between temperature changes during the polymerisation of other materials. Theracal PT should be used with caution as its use during direct capping causes high temperature increases, and there is a need for a design of a light-curing regime for its use.
