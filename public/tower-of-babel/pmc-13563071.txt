An Explainable Machine Learning Framework for Predicting West Nile Virus and Uncovering Key Transmission Drivers
Authors: Dimitrios Sainidis, Konstantinos Tsaprailis, Charalampos Kontoes
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study introduces Disease Vector Intelligence (DVI), an explainable Machine Learning (ML) framework developed within the EYWA (EarlY WArning system for mosquito‐borne diseases) ecosystem. DVI integrates big Earth Observation (EO), socioeconomic, and estimated mosquito abundance data to predict the presence or absence of West Nile Virus (WNV) cases, expressed as a risk score at a high spatiotemporal resolution. The SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) method is employed for a local‐level feature importance analysis to uncover key drivers with a high impact on the prediction. The framework consists of two ML models: one intended to be used as an Early Warning System (EWS) predicting at a regional scale, and the other identifying areas susceptible to WNV transmission at a 2 × 2 km2 grid scale, independent of human‐defined administrative boundaries. Both models were trained on 11 years of historical WNV case data and validated in Greece. SHAP analysis on both models revealed temperature as a crucial driver of WNV transmission. The fine spatial resolution of the second model uncovered micro‐scale key drivers, such as elevation and land cover type, that the lower resolution model missed. DVI models could be used complementarily by health authorities to aid their decision‐making process and to provide critical insights into important drivers that influence the transmission of WNV.Keywords: vector‐borne diseases, mosquito‐borne diseases, machine learning, earth observation, West Nile virus, explainable AI

Introduction

Vector‐borne diseases (VBDs) are a significant threat to global public health systems, accounting for more than 17% of all infectious diseases and causing more than 700,000 deaths each year (World Health Organization, 2024). They disproportionately affected tropical and subtropical regions, but their geographic range is recently expanding to new territories (Socha et al., 2022) due to climate change, unplanned urbanization, and increased global travel (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, 2025b). Among VBDs, Mosquito‐Borne Diseases (MBDs) such as malaria, dengue, Zika, Chikungunya, and WNV are responsible for most deaths (Breedlove, 2022), making mosquitoes the deadliest animals on the planet. Because of that, research efforts focus on controlling the spread of MBDs and understanding the factors that influence their transmission. According to epidemiological studies (Lu et al., 2024; Paz & Semenza, 2013; Petrić et al., 2014), the spread of mosquitoes and MBDs is driven by environmental factors, climatic conditions such as temperature and precipitation, land types such as wetlands, socioeconomic factors such as unplanned urbanization, and human activities. To try and mitigate the growing problem of MBDs, researchers focus on two main directions: (a) modeling mosquito abundance, and (b) modeling MBD cases or outbreaks.

Regarding the mosquito abundance direction, several researchers have studied the effects of environmental indices and climatic data on the development stages of mosquitoes (egg, larva, pupa, and adult) and their breeding sites using various modeling approaches (Beck‐Johnson et al., 2013; Erraguntla et al., 2021). Some studies attempt to correlate environmental indices, climatic data, and socioeconomic features directly with the adult mosquito population using statistical modeling (DeMets et al., 2020; Myer et al., 2020; Srimath‐Tirumula‐Peddinti et al., 2015). ML, Deep Learning (DL), and remote sensing have also been widely used in predictive modeling. Several studies integrated satellite data, socioeconomic data, and landscape features to train ML models to predict either mosquito or egg abundance at different spatiotemporal resolutions (Scavuzzo et al., 2018; S. Chen et al., 2019; Ceia‐Hasse et al., 2023). A particularly relevant study introduced MAMOTH, a vector and site‐agnostic ML pipeline that predicts mosquito abundance at a spatial resolution of 2 × 2 km2 grid and a temporal resolution of 1 month (Tsantalidou et al., 2021). MAMOTH is also part of the EYWA ecosystem, and its predictions are used as an input feature in DVI models.

On the other hand, statistical modeling techniques are also employed to study MBD cases directly. Several studies incorporate remotely sensed indices, weather data, socioeconomic data, and wild bird populations into statistical models that attempt to predict WNV cases across different spatial and temporal scales (Angelou et al., 2021; Smith et al., 2020; Young et al., 2013). Several studies have explored ML and DL approaches for predicting MBD cases or outbreaks using satellite and climatic data (Chekol & Hagras, 2018; Kumar et al., 2024; Rajendran et al., 2023; Salim et al., 2021). Various models, including Support Vector Machines, Decision Trees, Naive Bayes, Multilayer Perceptron, and Long Short‐Term Memory networks, have been applied across different spatial scales and diseases and demonstrated the effectiveness of ML and DL models in predicting MBD cases and outbreaks months in advance. Another study used weather data, bioclimatic factors, environmental indices, and socioeconomic data to train a gradient‐boosted model for predicting annual WNV outbreaks. Furthermore, the SHAP method was applied to identify the key factors driving WNV transmission (Farooq et al., 2022).

The increasing threat of MBDs highlights the need for an accurate EWS capable of predicting transmission risk with high spatiotemporal granularity and also identifying key factors that influence disease spread. Such a system could provide valuable insights to health authorities, allowing them to deploy targeted interventions proactively. This study presents an explainable ML pipeline consisting of two models that predict the presence or absence of WNV cases expressed as a transmission risk score with a time lead of 1 month. The first model uses the NUTS3 (as defined by Eurostat [2025]) spatial resolution and will be referred to as the NUTS Model. The second model uses a 2 × 2 km2 grid as a spatial scale and will be referred to as the GRID Model. This study combines EO data from satellites, socioeconomic data from public databases, estimated mosquito population data from MAMOTH (Tsantalidou et al., 2021), and 11 years of historical WNV case data from Greece. The SHAP method is used to provide detailed global (i.e., average feature importance across the entire data set) and local (i.e., feature contributions to individual predictions) interpretation results, uncovering the important features and how the output of the model shifts across a range of feature values. Materials and Methods

Area of Study

Greece is located in Southeast Europe on the Balkan Peninsula and has a population of ∼10.4 million as of 2024, according to the Hellenic Statistical Authority (Hellenic Statistical Authority, 2024). The country is administratively divided into several levels according to the NUTS classification established by the European Union (EU). At the NUTS 1 level, Greece is divided into four major regions: Attica (EL3), Aegean Islands and Crete (EL4), Northern Greece (EL5), and Central Greece (EL6). These are further subdivided at the NUTS 2 level into 13 administrative regions. At the NUTS 3 level, these regions are further divided into 52 regional units, which correspond to the country's prefectures. Data Collection

This section describes the categories of raw data used in the study, their sources, and spatiotemporal resolution, as well as the engineered data derived from them.

Earth Observation Data

This study employs satellite‐derived EO data that offer consistent and well‐structured information about the environment, the weather, and landscape characteristics. EO data are shown to have a significant impact on the transmission, severity, and overall prevalence of MBDs such as WNV (Parselia et al., 2019). An important aspect of EO data is the spatial and temporal resolution. Each satellite carries a different sensor used to capture the Earth and is also placed in a different orbit, which in turn affects the revisit time and thus the temporal resolution.

EO data are subdivided into the following categories:

Environmental indices: Four indices were employed to quantify local landscape features, namely the Normalized Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI) for vegetation quantity, the Normalized Difference Water Index (NDWI) for water bodies, the Normalized Difference Moisture Index (NDMI) for moisture levels in vegetation, and the Normalized Difference Built‐up Index (NDBI) for urbanization and man‐made built‐up areas. These indices are calculated from three satellite missions, depending on the availability. Sentinel‐2 has been available from 2015 to the present day. It has a spatial resolution of 10 m2 and a revisit time of 5 days. Landsat TM7 is available from 2010 to 2021 with a spatiotemporal resolution of 30 m2 and 14 days, while Landsat TM8 is available from 2013 to today, with the same resolution as Landsat TM7.

Entomological Data

This study utilizes predicted mosquito abundance data of the genus Culex pipiens, which are the bearers of WNV (European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control, 2025). The entomological data refer to the number of mosquitoes that are present in a specific area on a particular date. Raw entomological data are collected at the trap level. This process requires an established network of mosquito traps spread around an entire country, and a dedicated crew to collect and measure the captured mosquitoes at regular intervals. This is an expensive procedure and lacks the systematic data collection regularity needed for developing a predictive model. This study uses the predictions of MAMOTH (Tsantalidou et al., 2021), an ML‐based pipeline that predicts mosquito abundance at the 2 × 2 km2/month spatiotemporal resolution, as an input feature. According to the authors of MAMOTH, their model can accurately predict the upcoming mosquito population.

Population data by age, Gross Domestic Product (GDP) per capita, and freight movement were included as socioeconomic indicators, as they serve as proxies for regional economic activity, demographic vulnerability, and human mobility, factors that may indirectly influence disease transmission dynamics. Bioclimatic Data

WNV transmission can be affected by the climatic conditions of the previous year (Farooq et al., 2022). To capture the climatic trends of the previous year, 19 bioclimatic variables (WoldClim, 2024) that are computed from EO‐derived temperature and precipitation daily data. The bioclimatic variables represent annual trends (e.g., mean annual temperature, annual precipitation), seasonality (e.g., annual range in temperature and precipitation), and extreme or limiting environmental factors (e.g., temperature of the coldest and warmest month, and precipitation of the wet and dry quarters).

Engineered Features

Some features are created using raw data from other sources to give better context to the learning algorithm. For each of the four environmental indices, two more input features are calculated that represent the mean and standard deviation of the neighboring pixels in a 3 × 3 window, resulting in eight more input features marked NDVI_mean, NDVI_std, etc.

The majority (∼99%) of WNV cases in Greece are recorded between May and October (National Public Health Organization, 2024a, 2024b). Therefore, this is marked as the transmission period of WNV, and the models are trained with data from these months. From January to April, before the transmission period begins, the average daily LST, the average nightly LST, and the average LST are calculated and added to the input feature set, resulting in 12 engineered features related to temperature, three for each of the 4 months. Additionally, the minimum and maximum temperatures of the previous month (in reference to the month of prediction) are also calculated and added to the feature set.

Finally, the accumulated precipitation values are calculated for 1 week, 2 weeks, and 1 month before the prediction, and from the beginning of January, resulting in four more input features related to precipitation.

Epidemiological Data

Epidemiological data refer to the historical WNV recorded cases and are the backbone of this study, being the target variable that the models learn. Epidemiological data are provided by the Hellenic NPHO (National Public Health Organization, 2024a, 2024b), which is the nation's official health authority and is a collection of 12 years of recorded WNV cases from 2010 to 2021 in all 52 regional units of Greece. The spatial resolution of the recorded cases is a 1 × 1 km2 grid spanning the entire country. The temporal resolution is one day, meaning that the day of the onset of symptoms is recorded in the data set. This combined spatiotemporal resolution of 1 × 1 km2 grid and one day is also the limiting factor (finest resolution) for selecting the desired spatiotemporal resolution for both models.

The raw data set from NPHO contains 4 columns: a unique identifier for the suspected site of transmission on the 1 × 1 km2 grid, a date for the onset of symptoms, the disease severity, and a unique identifier for the data management team responsible for each entry. Regarding disease severity, it is either West Nile Fever (WNF), or West Nile Neuroinvasive Disease (WNND) when the virus attacks the central nervous system of the infected person. This study doesn't differentiate between WNF and WNND. Each row of the data set represents one recorded human case, where the information mentioned in the columns is recorded. A data set sample can be found in Table S1 of Supporting Information S1.

Note that WNV epidemiological data contain three sources of error which introduce noise and can affect the quality of prediction:

Case Under‐reporting: Many individuals infected with WNV, especially young and healthy people, develop mild symptoms similar to those of the common cold that only last a few days. These individuals usually don't get tested for WNV and therefore are never reported (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, 2025a).

Spatiotemporal Harmonization

This study develops two separate models, the NUTS and the GRID; the former makes predictions at NUTS3/month and the latter at 2 × 2 km 2 /month spatiotemporal resolution. Although epidemiological data were originally available at 1 × 1 km2 resolution, the 2 × 2 km2 spatial scale was chosen for the second model. This was done to reduce the extreme class imbalance present in the original epidemiological file. Furthermore, the vast majority of existing studies operate at much larger administrative divisions (e.g., NUTS3 or municipal), so the 2 × 2 km2 is a substantial improvement in spatial granularity.

The harmonization process involves the creation of a 2 × 2 km2 grid covering the country of Greece. Each cell gets a unique identifier that starts with the NUTS3 code of the area, followed by four numbers from 0001 to 9999 incrementally (e.g., a cell in the city of Thessaloniki could have an identifier “EL5220120,” with EL522 being the NUTS3 code of Thessaloniki and 0120 the incremental number of the cell). A daily timeline is created for every day between 01 and 01–2010 and 31‐12‐2021, and for every cell of the grid. Each row of this timeline consists of a specific day (e.g., 20‐06‐2015), the unique identifier of the cell (e.g., EL5220120), and the latitude and longitude of the centroid of the cell (e.g., 40.648493, 22.945125) and defines a single “spatiotemporal point.”

The spatiotemporal points are used to create the data set for modeling. The earth observation data are computed based on the latitude and longitude of the centroid of the cell, and also the date. Geomorphological and land cover data generally don't change over time (e.g., elevation, distance to coast, etc.), thus a lot of the same values are observed in the timeline. Entomological data are generated using the centroid of each cell and the month part of the date. Socioeconomic data are filled in using the NUTS3 code of the cell and the year part of the date. Due to the lower resolution, many repeated values are also observed for socioeconomic data. The bioclimatic data and the engineered features are calculated for every spatiotemporal point. Bioclimatic data refer to the entire previous year; thus, repeated values are also observed. The epidemiological data are aggregated from their original 1 × 1 km2 to the desired 2 × 2 km2 spatial resolution. It is important to acknowledge that certain predictors are only available at lower spatial and temporal resolutions; thus, many repeated values are observed as a result of those spatiotemporal resolution mismatches. This may introduce spatiotemporal smoothing effects and reduce the sensitivity of the model to local heterogeneities.

To go from daily to monthly temporal resolution, all values of each cell are averaged over the month. Moreover, the accumulated monthly mosquito abundance is also computed, resulting in an extra feature. An additional spatial aggregation is performed only for the NUTS model. For the row to represent a NUTS3 region instead of a cell, all cells that fall inside a NUTS3 region are averaged. This aggregation drastically reduces the rows of the timeline since one row is now representing a NUTS3 region for 1 month. Specifically for the geomorphological features, three more aggregations are performed, namely, the minimum, maximum, and standard deviation, to represent the ground morphology of the region more accurately. Therefore, for every geomorphological feature, three more are created after the spatial aggregation. (e.g., from “elevation,” the features “elevation_min,” “elevation_max”, and “elevation_std” are produced).

The output of the spatiotemporal harmonization procedure is two distinct data sets. The first data set used for the GRID model has a spatiotemporal resolution of 2 × 2 km 2 /month and consists of 81 input features. The second data set used for the NUTS model has a spatiotemporal resolution of NUTS3/month and consists of 105 input features. Detailed information about all input features can be found in Table S2 of Supporting Information S1.

Model Training & Validation

The two data sets are used to train two separate ML models, NUTS and GRID. The difference in spatial resolution determines the output and the use case. Both models output a monthly WNV risk map. The maps of the NUTS model could be used as an EWS, revealing to health authorities high‐risk NUTS3 regions. The higher resolution maps of the GRID model could be interpreted as WNV susceptibility maps, which don't rely on human‐defined administrative boundaries.

Evaluation Metrics

The binary classifier after logistic transformation outputs a real number between 0 and 1; thus, a classification threshold (τ) decides if the output belongs in the positive or negative class. In many classification problems, this threshold is assumed to be τ=0.50. While this is an intuitive assumption, it is not always the better choice, especially for imbalanced data sets. To select the classification threshold (τ), the precision and recall are calculated on the aggregated training folds for every possible threshold τ∈{0,0.01,0.02,…,1}. Then, the FβScore is calculated according to (Equation 7), and the classification threshold (τ) that maximizes the FβScore is selected.

Uncertainty Estimation

In machine learning, uncertainty estimation refers to the process of quantifying how confident a model is in its predictions (Pitsiorlas et al., 2025). This is particularly important in high‐stakes domains, such as healthcare, where models must operate reliably under dynamically evolving conditions and must be able to indicate when their predictions may be uncertain or unreliable.

To quantify the uncertainty of both models, an ensemble‐based approach was adopted. Specifically, for every model (NUTS and GRID), 10 XGBoost classifiers were initialized with the same parameters but a different seed to ensure variability. For each classifier, the same temporal and spatial blocked validation schemes were used, each one with a unique seed. For every validation fold and for every classifier, the predicted probabilities and the corresponding ground truth labels were collected for every example in every test set. The example (row) indices were also recorded to enable later aggregation. For each example, the mean and standard deviation of the predicted probabilities across all folds and models were computed. The standard deviation represents a measure of epistemic uncertainty due to model variability. In addition, the Shannon entropy was calculated for all examples to assess the intrinsic uncertainty of the predictions. Entropy quantifies the ambiguity of the prediction itself. Explainability ‐ Shapley Values

Understanding the results of machine learning models is essential for several reasons. In high‐stakes domains such as healthcare, finance, legal matters, etc, understanding the rationale behind model predictions is important to ensure prediction confidence, fairness, accountability, and trust (Pitsiorlas et al., 2024). Moreover, model interpretability facilitates the identification of potential biases, enhances transparency, and informs future model development, contributing to more robust and reliable outcomes.

After the training is complete, a separate post‐process action takes place to explain the model's predictions using SHAP (Lundberg & Lee, 2017). SHAP is based on Shapley values from cooperative game theory, where the goal is to distribute the “payout fairly” (in this case, the prediction) among the “players” (features). For each prediction, SHAP calculates the contribution of every feature by considering all possible combinations of features and measuring their impact on the model's output. These contributions are averaged to ensure a fair distribution, considering how each feature interacts with others. The result is a set of SHAP values that explain how much each feature contributes to increasing or decreasing the predicted value, offering a clear and consistent explanation for individual predictions. An added benefit of the SHAP method is that each row of the data set can be isolated and explained separately from others (local interpretation). Limitations of SHAP

It should be noted that SHAP values in tree‐based models can be sensitive to collinearity among features. The environmental variables used in this study are inherently correlated due to the shared climate that drives them. In such cases, the importance of some features may be redistributed among other correlated features, and therefore, individual importance values should be interpreted cautiously. This study presents both broader environmental patterns and isolated variables.

Discussion

This study presented an explainable ML pipeline for predicting WNV transmission risk in Greece, developing two separate models at two spatial scales: the NUTS model at the NUTS3 scale and the GRID model at the 2× 2 km2 grid scale. Both models captured meaningful spatiotemporal transmission patterns, each with distinct strengths. The NUTS model achieved stronger classification performance under temporal validation (F1Score = 0.61, recall = 0.70), with reduced performance under spatial validation (F1Score = 0.41, recall = 0.65). The GRID model showed weaker performance but more consistent behavior across the two validation schemes (F1Score = 0.40, recall = 0.60–F1Score = 0.46, recall = 0.61).

Comparing these results with previous studies is not straightforward, since the vast differences in spatiotemporal scale, geographic area, and evaluation metrics make direct comparison difficult. The methodologically similar study of (Farooq et al., 2022) also used XGBoost and SHAP to classify NUTS3 regions across Europe on an annual basis, reporting ROC AUC scores of 0.97 and 0.93 on out‐of‐sample years. However, their model made predictions at an annual resolution, while ours used a monthly resolution, greatly increasing the difficulty of the task as well as the class imbalance. Also, the ROC AUC metric would not yield meaningful results in our heavily imbalanced problem, whereas our chosen PR AUC is more suitable in such settings. These differences help explain the lower performance numbers of DVI. Another comparable study of (Angelou et al., 2021) applied a process‐based epidemiological model to all municipalities of Greece over 2010–2019 and achieved a probability of detection of 0.72, which is comparable to DVI's recall of 0.70 under temporal validation. However, DVI's GRID model spatial resolution of 4 km2 represents a significant improvement, which the authors identify in their final section as a much‐needed next step.

The SHAP analysis showed that temperature‐related features were the dominant feature in both models in terms of importance. These temperature thresholds revealed by the SHAP analysis were lower than those commonly reported in the literature. Recent studies estimate a general ideal transmission temperature typically around 23−26°C (Shocket et al., 2020), and near 24°C across Culex mosquito species specifically (Heidecke et al., 2025). Another study estimated an optimum temperature at 23.7°C alongside a suitability range extending from 14°C to 34.3°C (Di Pol et al., 2022). However, interpretation was consistent with experimental evidence showing that WNV transmission in Culex pipiens is limited below 18°C and increases at temperatures around 25−30°C (Vollans et al., 2024). Temperature‐related features were also classified as important by (Farooq et al., 2022), reporting that the mean temperature of the warmest quarter bio10 of the preceding year was the strongest predictor of WNV outbreaks across Europe, with affected regions showing temperatures between 20°C and 26°C, which is close to the thresholds identified by DVI's GRID model (Angelou et al., 2021) showed that temperature conditions in May were the most critical predictor across municipalities in Greece, which is consistent with the pre‐season temperature features used in the DVI model (Farooq et al., 2022). found that the bioclimatic features of the previous year accounted for 50% of total model contributions. Similarly, the SHAP analysis of both NUTS and GRID models assigned very high contributions to these features, suggesting that carryover climate effects are a robust signal of WNV transmission. The complex relationship between precipitation and WNV risk, reflected in mixed positive and negative SHAP values, is also consistent with what other studies have reported (Smith et al., 2020). found that a dry year preceded by a wet year was associated with higher infection rates, and (Farooq et al., 2022) found that lower water availability during the first half of the year was a risk factor. The fact that elevation and land cover type appeared as important features in the GRID model but not in the NUTS model is an interesting result, as it suggests that local terrain characteristics are hidden when data is aggregated at the administrative unit level. This finding is supported by the geographic distribution of WNV cases in Greece, which (Angelou et al., 2021) showed to be concentrated in the low‐elevation agricultural areas of northern Greece.

Taken together, the results indicate that the two models serve different but complementary roles for public health. The NUTS model is more effective as a regional early‐warning system helping health authorities to prioritize surveillance and allocate resources. This is similar to the early warning concept proposed by (Smith et al., 2020), who argued that early predictions could give authorities more time for preventive actions such as mosquito spraying,and public messaging, whereas the GRID model provides continuous susceptibility maps independent of human‐defined administrative boundaries at a spatial resolution that is more relevant for field interventions. This is apparent in the 2021 example, where the GRID model identified coherent high‐risk clusters aligned with observed cases, despite overpredicting risk in some areas.

There are some limitations that should be considered when interpreting the results. WNV is known to be heavily under‐reported, as most infections are either asymptomatic or only mildly symptomatic, and the undocumented cases have been estimated to exceed reported cases by orders of magnitude. This means that many observations labeled as negative in the data set likely contain true infections, which introduces noise in model training and reduces the apparent performance metrics. Additionally, the location for each WNV case recorded by NPHO is the suspected and not the confirmed transmission site, which introduces spatial uncertainty. Finally, DVI was trained only on data from Greece and extending it to other countries would require retraining and adaptation to different local conditions.

Conclusion and Future Work

This study demonstrates that EO‐driven machine learning can support operational WNV risk assessment at multiple spatial scales using widely available data sources. The NUTS and GRID models provide complementary capabilities, combining regional early warning with fine‐scale spatial risk mapping.

Future work should focus on improving spatial generalization and on incorporating more robust uncertainty quantification that accounts for distributional shifts. Further validation in operational settings is also required to assess real‐world performance. Extending the framework to other regions will require adaptation to local ecological and epidemiological conditions.
