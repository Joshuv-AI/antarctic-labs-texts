Early Clinical and Cerebrospinal Fluid Predictors of 1‐Year Recurrence in Autoimmune GFAP Astrocytopathy
Authors: Qingting Hong, Yuping Xiao, Yuhan Wu, Ziyuan Huang, Weishan Liang, Li Xiao, Yaqing Shu, Tingting Lu, Yanyu Chang, Wei Qiu, Yuge Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveAutoimmune glial fibrillary acidic protein astrocytopathy (GFAP‐A) is an inflammatory central nervous system disorder with variable outcomes. Relapse occurs in a subset of patients, but early predictors remain unclear. We aimed to identify admission‐available features associated with 1‐year recurrence and develop an interpretable risk stratification model.MethodsWe retrospectively included 156 patients with CSF GFAP‐IgG‐positive GFAP‐A between January 2021 and February 2025. Patients were classified by recurrence within 1 year. Early demographic, clinical, CSF, serological, neuroimaging, and treatment‐related variables were compared between groups. Candidate predictors were screened using elastic net regression with repeated cross‐validation, followed by multivariable logistic regression and internal validation.ResultsThirty‐five patients (22.4%) relapsed within 1 year. Patients with and without recurrence within 1 year were broadly comparable in age, sex, baseline severity, neuroimaging findings, and treatment exposure. Patients with recurrence within 1 year had lower CSF white blood cell counts (42.0 [10.0–104.0] vs. 107.0 [34.0–200.0] × 106/L; p = 0.002) and more frequent movement disorders (71.4% vs. 43.8%; p = 0.004). The final model included CSF white blood cell count, movement disorders, CSF chloride, and CSF GFAP‐IgG titer category. Lower CSF white blood cell count, movement disorders, and higher GFAP‐IgG titer category were associated with increased recurrence risk. The model showed acceptable optimism‐corrected discrimination (AUC, 0.726), reasonable calibration, and potential clinical utility. Machine‐learning models did not outperform logistic regression.InterpretationIn GFAP‐A, 1‐year recurrence was associated with distinct early clinical and CSF features rather than baseline severity or treatment exposure alone. This admission‐based model may support recurrence risk stratification and follow‐up planning.Keywords: autoimmune GFAP astrocytopathy, recurrence, risk stratification

Introduction

Autoimmune glial fibrillary acidic protein astrocytopathy (GFAP‐A) is an immune‐mediated inflammatory disorder of the central nervous system defined by GFAP‐IgG, particularly in cerebrospinal fluid [1]. Since its initial description, GFAP‐A has been recognized as a clinically heterogeneous disease involving the brain, meninges, spinal cord, and optic nerve. Patients may present with encephalitis, meningitis, myelitis, or overlapping syndromes [2, 3, 4], and MRI may show radial perivascular enhancement, longitudinally extensive myelitis, and meningeal or spinal pia mater enhancement [5]. Although many patients respond to corticosteroids during the acute phase, subsequent studies have shown that relapse, steroid dependence, and incomplete recovery occur in a clinically meaningful subset of patients [3, 6].

Recent cohort studies have improved our understanding of the clinical phenotypes, imaging patterns, antibody profiles, treatment responses, and long‐term outcomes of GFAP‐A. However, most available studies remain primarily descriptive. For clinicians, an unresolved question is whether patients at higher risk of relapse can be identified during the first hospitalization. This question is important because early clinical improvement, baseline disability, and conventional MRI findings may not fully capture subsequent recurrence risk.

In this study, we analyzed a cohort of 156 patients with CSF‐confirmed GFAP‐A to identify early features associated with 1‐year recurrence. We further developed and internally validated an interpretable admission‐based prediction model using routinely available clinical and CSF variables.

Methods

Study Participants

We retrospectively screened inpatients with positive GFAP antibodies in the neurology department of the Third Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat‐sen University in Guangdong Province between January 2021 and February 2025. The primary outcome was recurrence within 1 year after the initial hospitalization. Patients without relapse during the first 365 days were classified as having no 1‐year recurrence. Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) Positive GFAP‐IgG antibodies in CSF confirmed by CBA [7]; (2) clinically diagnosed as GFAP‐A; (3) no evidence of other inflammatory disorders of the central nervous system; and (4) available follow‐up data. Exclusion criteria were: (1) serum‐only GFAP‐IgG positivity; and (2) incomplete clinical or diagnostic records. Patients were stratified according to the occurrence of clinical relapse within 1 year after the initial hospitalization. Relapse was defined as the recurrence of symptoms after a complete remission for at least 3 months or a significant worsening of symptoms following a previous stabilization [8].

Data Collection

Clinical and laboratory data were collected and evaluated at the time of initial admission by two experienced neurologists, including demographic characteristics, clinical manifestations, comorbidities, laboratory findings, and neuroimaging features. Demographic variables included age and sex. Clinical variables comprised major neurological symptoms at admission, including fever, headache, epilepsy, disturbance of consciousness, psychiatric symptoms, autonomic dysfunction, visual impairment, respiratory failure, and movement disorders. Movement disorders were defined as tremor, ataxia/gait instability, myoclonus, dystonia, or other involuntary movements recorded at presentation. Laboratory variables included cerebrospinal fluid parameters (CSF white blood cell count, CSF protein, CSF glucose, CSF chloride, and intracranial pressure) as well as selected serum laboratory indicators. Magnetic resonance imaging findings were recorded according to lesion location and enhancement pattern. Baseline MRI data were extracted from the original radiological reports, which were issued by the same two experienced neuroradiologists who routinely reviewed neurological MRI studies from our department during the study period. These reports followed a consistent institutional reporting workflow and included assessment of brain and spinal cord lesion distribution, meningeal enhancement, and spinal cord pia mater enhancement. The extracted MRI variables were subsequently reviewed by the study neurologists for consistency with the predefined study categories. Baseline disease severity was assessed using the modified Rankin Scale (mRS), Clinical Assessment Scale for Autoimmune Encephalitis (CASE), and anti‐NMDAR Encephalitis One‐Year Functional Status (NEOS) score at admission. Follow‐up assessments were performed during the 1‐year follow‐up period after the initial hospitalization through outpatient visits or telephone interviews.

Antibody Identification

Serum and CSF samples were tested for GFAP‐IgG using a cell‐based assay in a certified clinical laboratory. For samples initially determined to be positive, additional dilution testing was performed according to the laboratory's routine clinical protocol. For CSF samples, antibody reactivity was assessed using predefined dilution levels, and the final reported titer was determined according to the observed fluorescence intensity at these dilution levels. Therefore, the reported CSF GFAP‐IgG titers represented standardized semi‐quantitative ordinal results rather than truly continuous endpoint titers. For the primary prediction model, CSF GFAP‐IgG titers were grouped into three categories to reduce sparse categories and improve model stability: low titer, 1:3.2 or 1:10; intermediate titer, 1:32; and high titer, ≥ 1:100. Positivity was defined as a CSF titer of at least 1:2 [9].

Statistical Analyses

Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median with interquartile range (IQR), as appropriate, and compared using Student's t‐test or the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages and compared using the chi‐squared test or Fisher's exact test, as appropriate. Variables with more than 20% missing values were excluded. Missing values in the remaining variables were handled using multiple imputation by chained equations with the mice package (version 3.16.0) in R, generating 20 imputed datasets. Analyses were performed separately in each imputed dataset, and estimates were pooled using Rubin's rules where appropriate.

Predictor selection was performed using elastic net regression within each imputed dataset under a nested cross‐validation framework. Predictor stability was assessed by repeated 5‐fold cross‐validation with 10 repeats. Final predictors were selected according to selection frequency, clinical interpretability, and availability in the early post‐admission period. A multivariable logistic regression model was then developed to estimate the risk of 1‐year recurrence, with regression coefficients pooled across imputations using Rubin's rules. To improve clinical interpretability, the effect estimate for CSF white blood cell count was reported per 50 × 106/L increase rather than per one‐unit increase.

Model discrimination was assessed using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC). Internal validation was performed using bootstrap resampling to obtain optimism‐corrected AUC estimates. Model calibration was evaluated using calibration plots and calibration slopes. Clinical utility was assessed using decision curve analysis. Accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, and precision were also calculated as supplementary performance metrics.

Additional analyses were performed to assess the robustness and interpretation of selected predictors. For CSF GFAP‐IgG titer, the distribution of the original reported titer levels was summarized according to 1‐year recurrence status. The overall categorical distribution was compared using Fisher's exact test, and ordinal differences between groups were assessed using the Mann–Whitney U test. To evaluate whether the model findings were sensitive to titer coding, we performed a sensitivity analysis in which the three‐category CSF GFAP‐IgG titer variable in the primary multivariable model was replaced by the original reported titer level coded as an ordinal semi‐quantitative variable. To evaluate the potential influence of CSF sampling time, time from disease onset to lumbar puncture was compared between groups, its correlation with CSF WBC count was assessed using Spearman correlation, and an additional sensitivity model was fitted by adding this interval to the final multivariable model. Because movement disorder subtypes were not mutually exclusive, each subtype was summarized and compared separately as a binary variable using Fisher's exact test. To address whether tremor alone accounted for the association between movement disorders and recurrence, we performed a sensitivity analysis replacing the composite movement disorder variable with tremor while retaining the other predictors. To evaluate the incremental contribution of CSF chloride, we compared the final model with a reduced model excluding CSF chloride.

For comparison, four additional machine‐learning models, including random forest, extreme gradient boosting, support vector machine, and elastic net, were developed and evaluated by repeated cross‐validation across the imputed datasets, with AUC as the primary metric. To improve interpretability, SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) analysis was performed for the extreme gradient boosting model. All analyses were conducted in R, and a two‐sided p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

The distribution of the original reported CSF GFAP‐IgG titer levels is shown in Table S1. Although the overall categorical distribution did not differ significantly between groups by Fisher's exact test, ordinal comparison showed a shift toward higher reported titer levels in patients with 1‐year recurrence. In sensitivity analysis, replacing the three‐category CSF GFAP‐IgG titer variable with the original reported ordinal titer level yielded directionally consistent results, with higher reported titer levels associated with increased odds of 1‐year recurrence (OR 1.913, 95% CI 1.204–3.038; p = 0.006; Table S2).

Baseline comorbidities, including abnormal thyroid function, thyroid disease, autoimmune disease, tumor, and Epstein–Barr virus infection, were comparable, as were the major clinical syndromes at presentation, including encephalitis, meningitis, myelitis, and optic neuritis. Among initial symptoms, meningeal irritation signs were less frequent in patients with recurrence (37.1% vs. 56.2%, p = 0.047), whereas movement disorders were more frequent (71.4% vs. 43.8%, p = 0.004). The distribution of movement disorder subtypes is summarized in Table S3. Because some patients had more than one subtype, these categories were not mutually exclusive. Visual impairment was more common in the recurrent group, although the difference did not reach statistical significance (28.6% vs. 14.9%, p = 0.063). Other presenting symptoms were not significantly different. Neuroimaging findings, including spinal cord parenchymal lesions, brain T2 lesion distribution, cerebral meningeal enhancement, and spinal cord pia mater enhancement, were similar between groups. Baseline mRS, CASE, and NEOS scores were likewise not significantly different. Notably, there were no significant differences in treatment strategies targeting GFAP‐A between the two groups (Table S4). Despite comparable treatment exposure, patients with recurrence had a less favorable clinical course, underscoring the need for early identification of individuals at increased risk of relapse.

Predictor Selection and Multivariable Model Development

To identify variables associated with 1‐year recurrence, candidate predictors were first screened across the 20 imputed datasets using elastic net regression within a nested cross‐validation framework. Predictor stability was assessed by repeated 5‐fold cross‐validation with 10 repeats, and variables were ranked according to their selection frequencies across resampling iterations. Final predictor selection was then refined according to selection stability, clinical interpretability, and availability early after admission. Based on this strategy, four variables were retained for the final multivariable logistic regression model: CSF white blood cell count (CSF_WBC), movement disorders, CSF chloride (CSF_CL), and CSF GFAP‐IgG titer category. Stability‐selection results are shown in Figure S1.

A nomogram incorporating these four predictors was subsequently developed to facilitate individualized estimation of recurrence risk (Figure 2). Each predictor was assigned a weighted point value proportional to its contribution in the final model, and the total score was translated into an estimated probability of recurrence within 1 year.

Nomogram for predicting the risk of 1‐year recurrence in patients with GFAP astrocytopathy.

Pooled multivariable logistic regression analysis across the 20 imputed datasets showed that higher CSF white blood cell count was independently associated with lower odds of recurrence within 1 year. When expressed per 50 × 106/L increase, the odds ratio was 0.669 (95% CI, 0.520–0.905; p = 0.006). Time from disease onset to lumbar puncture did not differ between patients with and without recurrence within 1 year (2.9 [1.7–4.0] vs. 2.7 [1.9–4.0] weeks; p = 0.845). This interval was not correlated with CSF WBC count (Spearman rho = 0.008; p = 0.924). In a sensitivity model additionally adjusted for time from disease onset to lumbar puncture, CSF WBC count remained inversely associated with recurrence within 1 year (OR 0.727 per 50 × 106/L increase, 95% CI 0.562–0.939; p = 0.015; Table S5). In contrast, movement disorders at presentation (OR 2.765, 95% CI 1.134–6.744; p = 0.025) and higher CSF GFAP‐IgG titer category (OR 5.079, 95% CI 1.411–18.288; p = 0.013) were associated with increased odds of recurrence. CSF chloride showed an inverse association with recurrence risk, but this did not reach statistical significance (OR 0.967, 95% CI 0.932–1.004; p = 0.077) (Table S6). A post hoc comparison with a reduced model excluding CSF chloride showed no meaningful change in model performance, suggesting limited incremental contribution of CSF chloride (Table S7).

Model Performance and Internal Validation

The final logistic regression model showed acceptable discrimination, with an optimism‐corrected AUC of 0.726 (Figure 3). Calibration analysis showed an apparent calibration slope of 0.810 and an optimism‐corrected calibration slope of 0.764, indicating acceptable but imperfect calibration. In particular, the optimism‐corrected calibration slope below 1.0 suggests some degree of overfitting (Figure 4). The clinical utility of the model was further examined by decision curve analysis. Across a clinically relevant range of threshold probabilities, the nomogram provided a higher net benefit than the default strategies of treating all patients as high risk or treating none as high risk, supporting its potential value for early risk stratification in routine practice (Figure 5).

Benchmarking Against Machine‐Learning Models

The performance of the logistic regression model was further compared with four machine‐learning approaches, including random forest, extreme gradient boosting, support vector machine, and elastic net. Logistic regression achieved the highest AUC (0.726), followed by random forest (0.706), extreme gradient boosting (0.683), support vector machine (0.635), and elastic net (0.591). Additional performance metrics are summarized in Table 2. Although support vector machine showed the highest accuracy (0.683) and specificity (0.724), logistic regression showed the highest sensitivity (0.772). None of the machine‐learning models surpassed logistic regression in terms of AUC, suggesting that the conventional logistic regression model provided the best overall discrimination in this cohort.

SHAP Analysis and Interpretation

To further examine feature contributions at the model level and whether they were directionally consistent with the primary logistic model, SHAP analysis was performed on the XGBoost model as an exploratory model‐interpretation analysis. The SHAP summary plot (Figure 6A) identified CSF WBC count as the most influential feature, followed by movement disorders, CSF chloride, and CSF GFAP‐IgG titer category. This ranking reflects the importance of these variables in predicting 1‐year recurrence in GFAP‐A, with CSF WBC count showing the strongest predictive power. The SHAP dependence plot for CSF WBC count (Figure 6B) revealed a predominantly inverse relationship with predicted recurrence risk. Lower values of CSF WBC contributed positively to the prediction of recurrence, while higher values tended to shift predictions toward a lower recurrence risk.

Discussion

In this cohort of 156 patients with CSF‐confirmed autoimmune GFAP astrocytopathy, 22.4% experienced recurrence within 1 year. Recurrence was not explained by baseline disability, neuroimaging burden, or treatment category alone. Instead, patients who relapsed showed a distinct early profile characterized by lower CSF white blood cell counts, more frequent movement disorders, and higher CSF GFAP‐IgG titer category. A four‐variable admission‐based model incorporating these routinely available markers showed acceptable internal performance and may provide a practical framework for early recurrence risk stratification.

Previous studies have shown that patients with GFAP‐A often respond well to corticosteroids during the acute phase, with partial or complete improvement reported in 77.1% of patients in a Chinese cohort and approximately 83% in a French cohort after high‐dose corticosteroid therapy [10, 11]. However, early clinical improvement does not necessarily indicate a monophasic course. Some patients subsequently experience relapse, steroid dependence, or repeated disease activity [1, 12], suggesting that short‐term treatment response alone may be insufficient for estimating long‐term recurrence risk. Most previous studies of GFAP‐A have focused on clinical phenotypes, MRI features, treatment response, and overall prognosis [1, 13, 14], whereas less attention has been paid to identifying patients at risk of relapse during the first hospitalization. In our cohort, patients with and without recurrence within 1 year were broadly similar in baseline disease severity, neuroimaging findings, and treatment exposure, but their 1‐year disease courses differed. This suggests that conventional baseline features may not be sufficient to identify recurrence risk on their own. Similar observations have been reported in other neuroimmune disorders, such as anti‐N‐methyl‐D‐aspartate receptor encephalitis and MOGAD [15, 16], in which relapse risk is not determined simply by acute‐phase severity or early imaging burden, but may be shaped by disease phenotype, persistent immune activity, treatment timing, and individual susceptibility. Because relapse may lead to renewed neurological worsening, longer exposure to immunotherapy, steroid‐related adverse effects, and greater follow‐up and treatment burden, identifying high‐risk patients during the initial hospitalization may help guide more tailored surveillance and long‐term management.

The inverse association between CSF white blood cell count and recurrence risk was unexpected. CSF pleocytosis is a common acute‐phase feature of GFAP‐A. Previous studies have shown that patients in the acute phase often have elevated CSF white blood cell counts, predominantly lymphocytic pleocytosis [3, 17]. This finding is generally interpreted as evidence of active inflammation within the central nervous system. However, in neuroimmune disorders, the intensity of acute cellular inflammation does not necessarily correspond to relapse propensity or long‐term outcome. For example, in a UK cohort of patients with multiple sclerosis, CSF lymphocytic pleocytosis was not associated with higher EDSS scores at follow‐up and did not predict worse long‐term disability [18]. In anti‐NMDAR encephalitis, elevated CSF white blood cell counts are also common, but CSF abnormalities were not significantly associated with clinical outcome [19]. These findings suggest that acute CSF inflammation may reflect ongoing CNS inflammatory activity at presentation, but may not directly predict the subsequent disease course. In our study, a higher CSF white blood cell count at onset may have primarily reflected a more evident acute inflammatory attack. Conversely, patients with a lower CSF cellular response may represent a different disease state in which recurrent activity is driven less by marked acute pleocytosis and more by persistent or reactivatable immune processes. This interpretation is also consistent with a recent two‐center Chinese GFAP‐A cohort, in which CSF white blood cell count was associated with functional improvement rather than 1‐year relapse, whereas CSF‐specific oligoclonal bands showed a stronger association with relapse risk [10]. A recent French study further reported that CSF lambda free light chains, but not kappa free light chains, were associated with clinical outcome as assessed by the modified Rankin Scale [20]. Although that study evaluated functional outcome rather than 1‐year recurrence, its findings are relevant because CSF lambda free light chains may reflect intrathecal immunoglobulin synthesis, whereas CSF WBC count mainly captures acute cellular pleocytosis. Importantly, the inverse association between CSF WBC count and 1‐year recurrence persisted after adjustment for time from disease onset to lumbar puncture, suggesting that the lower CSF WBC count in patients with recurrence within 1 year was unlikely to be explained simply by delayed CSF sampling. Together, these findings suggest that CSF WBC count may represent an important but partial marker of the inflammatory state in GFAP‐A, and that recurrence risk is unlikely to be explained by acute cellular pleocytosis alone. Therefore, the inverse association observed in our study should be regarded as hypothesis‐generating, rather than evidence that CSF inflammation itself is protective.

Overall, the four predictors retained in the final model may reflect complementary aspects of recurrence risk in GFAP‐A rather than repeated measures of the same inflammatory process. CSF WBC count may mainly reflect acute cellular inflammation, whereas CSF GFAP‐IgG titer may reflect the strength or persistence of GFAP‐related immune activity within the CSF. Because GFAP is an intracellular astrocytic cytoskeletal protein, whether GFAP‐IgG itself has direct pathogenic effects remains uncertain [10]. Therefore, a higher CSF GFAP‐IgG titer should not be interpreted simply as evidence that the antibody directly causes relapse; rather, it may serve as a marker of more active intrathecal immune response or greater antibody burden. This interpretation is consistent with current understanding of the immunopathology of GFAP‐A. Previous meningeal biopsy and histopathological studies have shown infiltration of CD4+ T cells, CD8+ T cells, and B cells in inflamed tissues, with a predominance of CD8+ T cells [21, 22]. Experimental models of GFAP‐related autoimmune CNS disease have also suggested that cytotoxic T cells targeting GFAP peptides can contribute to disease development [23]. These findings indicate that GFAP‐A is unlikely to be mediated solely by direct antibody effects, but may involve both cellular immunity and intrathecal antibody responses. In this context, lower CSF WBC count and higher CSF GFAP‐IgG titer in our study are not necessarily contradictory: the former may indicate a weaker acute cellular response, whereas the latter may indicate more active or persistent GFAP‐related immune activity. This framework helps reconcile the inverse association of CSF WBC count with recurrence and the positive association of CSF GFAP‐IgG titer category within the same prediction model.

Movement disorders provided another layer of clinical information. Previous studies have shown that movement disorders are not uncommon in GFAP‐A and often include ataxia, tremor, and myoclonus [1, 24]. Similarly, in autoimmune encephalitis and other antibody‐associated neurological diseases, movement disorders are generally regarded as manifestations of immune‐mediated motor dysfunction, potentially involving the cerebellum, brainstem, basal ganglia, or thalamo‐cortical circuits [25, 26]. Therefore, movement disorders in GFAP‐A may indicate more extensive or more complex neurological involvement, rather than an isolated symptom. In our study, movement disorders included tremor, ataxia/gait instability, myoclonus, dystonia, and other involuntary movements. Because individual subtypes were relatively sparse and sometimes overlapped within the same patient, the composite movement disorder variable may better reflect broader motor‐network involvement than a single movement disorder subtype. A sensitivity analysis using tremor alone yielded a directionally consistent but nonsignificant association, supporting the use of the broader composite movement disorder variable in the final model (Table S8). Notably, conventional MRI findings were broadly comparable between patients with and without recurrence within 1 year in our cohort, whereas movement disorders were more frequent in the recurrent group. This suggests that movement disorders may provide clinical information complementary to conventional MRI categories and may help identify patients with similar imaging profiles but different subsequent disease courses [26]. However, this finding should still be interpreted as a risk marker, rather than evidence that movement disorders themselves cause recurrence. By contrast, the role of CSF chloride in the model was weaker. A post hoc comparison with a reduced model excluding CSF chloride showed no meaningful change in model performance, supporting its interpretation as an auxiliary marker of changes in the CSF milieu rather than a central mechanism of relapse. Taken together, these variables suggest that recurrence risk in GFAP‐A may be related to a combination of acute cellular response, GFAP‐related immune activity, motor‐network involvement, and changes in the CSF milieu, rather than being determined by a single marker alone.

At the model level, our findings suggest that interpretability may be more important than algorithmic complexity in rare disease cohorts such as GFAP‐A. We compared logistic regression, random forest, XGBoost, support vector machine, and elastic net models, but none of the more complex machine‐learning approaches achieved a higher AUC than logistic regression. This finding is not unexpected. In a clinical cohort with a limited sample size, a modest number of recurrence events, and a small set of candidate predictors, the main challenge is often overfitting and limited reproducibility rather than insufficient model flexibility [26]. In this setting, a logistic model based on four early available variables is easier to interpret, reproduce, and translate into individualized risk estimation. The value of this model lies not in replacing clinical judgment, but in organizing early clinical and CSF information into a clearer risk stratification framework. The model showed acceptable discrimination, reasonable calibration, and potential net benefit across a range of clinically relevant thresholds [27]. Therefore, it may serve as an adjunctive tool for guiding follow‐up intensity and long‐term management discussions. SHAP analysis provided complementary interpretation, with feature effects that were generally consistent with those observed in the logistic regression model.

The present study should be interpreted in light of several limitations. First, the retrospective single‐center design may introduce selection bias and limits generalizability. Second, the number of recurrence events was modest, and although multiple imputation and bootstrap internal validation were used, predictor estimates may still be unstable. Third, the model has not been externally validated. Although bootstrap internal validation was performed, the optimism‐corrected calibration slope of 0.764 suggests some degree of overfitting, meaning that predicted risks may be overly extreme in new patient populations. Therefore, the model should be viewed as a preliminary risk stratification framework rather than a ready‐to‐implement clinical decision rule. External validation and, if necessary, model recalibration are required before clinical implementation. Fourth, treatment exposure was broadly compared between groups, but residual confounding related to treatment timing, duration, intensity, and maintenance strategy cannot be fully excluded. Finally, longitudinal immune markers, serial antibody titers, and follow‐up MRI data were not incorporated, limiting mechanistic interpretation. In addition, this study focused on recurrence within a predefined 1‐year window, and follow‐up beyond 1 year was not systematically available. Future multicenter studies with standardized treatment and follow‐up protocols are needed to validate this framework and clarify whether the identified predictors reflect distinct immunological phenotypes of relapse‐prone GFAP‐A.

Table S1: Distribution of original reported CSF GFAP‐IgG titers according to 1‐year recurrence status. Data are presented as n (%). The p value was calculated using Fisher's exact test for the overall distribution of original reported CSF GFAP‐IgG titer levels. When titers were treated as an ordinal semi‐quantitative variable, the Mann–Whitney U test showed a significant difference between groups (p = 0.018).

Table S2: Sensitivity analysis of CSF GFAP‐IgG titer coding in the multivariable model. The sensitivity model replaced the three‐category CSF GFAP‐IgG titer variable in the primary model with the original reported titer level coded as an ordinal semi‐quantitative variable. Original reported titers were coded as 1 = 1:3.2, 2 = 1:10, 3 = 1:32, 4 = 1:100, and 5 = 1:320. The odds ratio for titer represents the change in recurrence odds per one‐level increase in the original reported titer category.

Table S3: Distribution of movement disorder subtypes according to 1‐year recurrence status. Data are presented as n (%). Because movement disorder subtypes were not mutually exclusive, each subtype was compared separately as a binary variable. p values for movement disorder subtypes were calculated using Fisher's exact test. Any movement disorder was compared as a composite binary variable.

Table S4: Treatment strategies according to 1‐year recurrence status in patients with autoimmune glial fibrillary acidic protein astrocytopathy. Treatment exposure was compared between patients without and with recurrence within 1 year. No treatment variable differed significantly between groups. Data are presented as n (%). p values were calculated using the chi‐squared test or Fisher's exact test, as appropriate. GFAP‐A, autoimmune glial fibrillary acidic protein astrocytopathy; IVIG, intravenous immunoglobulin; PE/IA, plasma exchange/immunoadsorption.

Table S5: Sensitivity analysis adjusting for time from disease onset to lumbar puncture. The sensitivity model included the original four predictors and time from disease onset to lumbar puncture. CSF WBC count was modeled per 50 × 106/L increase, and time from disease onset to lumbar puncture was modeled per 1‐week increase. β coefficients, odds ratios, 95% confidence intervals, and p values were pooled across multiply imputed datasets.

Table S6: Multivariable logistic regression model for predictors of 1‐year recurrence in autoimmune glial fibrillary acidic protein astrocytopathy. The model included cerebrospinal fluid white blood cell count, movement disorders, cerebrospinal fluid chloride, and cerebrospinal fluid GFAP‐IgG titer category. β coefficients, odds ratios (OR), 95% confidence intervals (CI), and p values were pooled across multiply imputed datasets. CSF, cerebrospinal fluid; WBC, white blood cell; GFAP‐IgG, glial fibrillary acidic protein immunoglobulin G.

Table S7: Post hoc comparison of model performance with and without CSF chloride. The table shows the change in model performance after excluding CSF chloride from the four‐variable candidate model. Values were calculated as the performance of the reduced model without CSF chloride minus that of the model with CSF chloride, using the same imputed datasets and bootstrap procedure for this post hoc paired comparison.

Table S8: Sensitivity analysis replacing composite movement disorders with tremor alone. This sensitivity model replaced the composite movement disorder variable in the primary model with tremor alone, while retaining the other predictors. CSF WBC count was modeled per 50 × 106/L increase. β coefficients, odds ratios, 95% confidence intervals, and p values were pooled across multiply imputed datasets.

Figure S1: Stability‐selection results from elastic net regression. Bars show the selection frequency of candidate predictors across multiply imputed datasets and repeated cross‐validation. Variables are ranked in descending order of selection frequency; higher percentages indicate more stable inclusion during predictor screening.
