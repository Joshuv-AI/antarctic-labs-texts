An Enhanced Levenberg–Marquardt Method via Gram Reduction
Chengchang Liu, Luo Luo, John C.S. Lui

Abstract
This paper studied the problem of solving the system of nonlinear equations 𝐅 ⁡ ( 𝐱 ) = 𝟎 {\bf F}({{\bf x}})={\bf{0}} , where 𝐅 : ℝ d → ℝ d {\bf F}:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d} . We propose Gram-Reduced Levenberg–Marquardt method which updates the Gram matrix 𝐉 ​ ( ⋅ ) ⊤ ​ 𝐉 ​ ( ⋅ ) {\bf J}(\cdot)^{\top}{\bf J}(\cdot) in every m m iterations, where 𝐉 ⁡ ( ⋅ ) {\bf J}(\cdot) is the Jacobian of 𝐅 ⁡ ( ⋅ ) {\bf F}(\cdot) . Our method has a global convergence guarantee without relying on any step of line-search or solving sub-problems. We prove our method takes at most 𝒪 ⁡ ( m 2 + m − 0.5 ​ ϵ − 2.5 ) {\mathcal{O}}(m^{2}+m^{-0.5}\epsilon^{-2.5}) iterations to find an ϵ \epsilon -stationary point of 1 2 ​ ‖ 𝐅 ⁡ ( ⋅ ) ‖ 2 \frac{1}{2}\|{\bf F}(\cdot)\|^{2} , which leads to overall computation cost of 𝒪 ⁡ ( d 3 ​ ϵ − 1 + d 2 ​ ϵ − 2 ) {\mathcal{O}}(d^{3}\epsilon^{-1}+d^{2}\epsilon^{-2}) by taking m = Θ ⁡ ( ϵ − 1 ) m=\Theta(\epsilon^{-1}) . Our results are strictly better than the cost of 𝒪 ⁡ ( d 3 ​ ϵ − 2 ) {\mathcal{O}}(d^{3}\epsilon^{-2}) for existing Levenberg–Marquardt methods. We also show the proposed method enjoys local superlinear convergence rate under the non-degenerate assumption. We provide experiments on real-world applications in scientific computing and machine learning to validate the efficiency of the proposed methods.

Introduction

We consider solving the system of nonlinear equations

\displaystyle{\bf F}({\bf x})={\bf 0},

(1)

where
{\bf x}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and
{\bf F}({\bf x})\triangleq[F_{1}({\bf x}),\dots,F_{d}({\bf x})]^{\top}\!:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
with differentiable
F_{i}({{\bf x}}):{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}
for all
i\in[d]
.
This problem can also be reformulated by the nonlinear least-square problem:

\displaystyle\min_{{\bf x}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\phi({\bf x})\triangleq\frac{1}{2}\|{\bf F}({\bf x})\|^{2}.

(2)

Solving nonlinear equations is one of the most fundamental problems in scientific computing
[
36
,
52
]
.
It has wide applications in machine learning
[
13
,
8
,
4
,
29
]
, control system
[
7
]
, data assimilation
[
44
]
, and game theory
[
19
,
40
]
.

First-order methods are popular to solve nonlinear equations. Specifically, one can perform the gradient descent on problem (
2
), that is

\displaystyle\begin{split}{\bf x}_{t+1}={\bf x}_{t}-\eta\nabla\phi({\bf x}_{t})={\bf x}_{t}-\eta{\bf J}({\bf x}_{t})^{\top}{\bf F}({\bf x}_{t}),\end{split}

(3)

where
\eta>0
is the step-size,
\phi({\bf x}_{t})={\bf J}({\bf x}_{t})^{\top}{\bf F}({\bf x}_{t})
and
{\bf J}(\cdot)\triangleq[\nabla F_{1}(\cdot),\dots,\nabla F_{d}(\cdot)]^{\top}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d\times d}
is the Jacobian of
{\bf F}(\cdot)
.
The iteration scheme (
3
) takes
{\mathcal{O}}(d^{2})
flops in general.
By assuming the Lipschitz continuity on
\nabla\phi(\cdot)
, it requires
{\mathcal{O}}(\epsilon^{-2})
iterations to find an
\epsilon
-stationary point of
\phi(\cdot)
, then the overall computation cost is
{\mathcal{O}}(d^{2}\epsilon^{-2})
.
Gauss–Newton methods
[
5
,
39
]
consider the estimation
\nabla^{2}\phi(\cdot)\approx{\bf J}(\cdot)^{\top}{\bf J}(\cdot)
and establish the iteration

\displaystyle{\bf x}_{t+1}={\bf x}_{t}-\big({\bf J}({\bf x}_{t})^{\top}{\bf J}({\bf x}_{t})\big)^{\dagger}{\bf J}({{\bf x}}_{t})^{\top}{\bf F}({\bf x}_{t})

with local superlinear convergence, while such needs the flops of
{\mathcal{O}}(d^{3})
to compute the matrix (pseudo) inverse.
Recently,
Liu and Luo [30]
introduced Broyden family updates
[
41
,
28
]
to estimate
{\bf J}(\cdot)^{\top}{\bf J}(\cdot)
, which leads to the Quasi-Newton methods with local superlinear convergence and reduces the computation cost in per iteration to
{\mathcal{O}}(d^{2})
flops.
However, both Gauss–Newton method and its quasi-Newton variants lack global convergence guarantees.

In this paper, we are interested in Levenberg–Marquardt (LM) methods
[
26
,
34
]
, which iterates according to

\displaystyle{\bf x}_{t+1}={\bf x}_{t}-\big({\bf J}({\bf x}_{t})^{\top}{\bf J}({\bf x}_{t})+\lambda_{t}{\bf I}\big)^{-1}{\bf J}({\bf x}_{t})^{\top}{\bf F}({\bf x}_{t}),

(4)

where
\lambda_{t}>0
is the regularization parameter.
This method globalizes the Gauss–Newton iteration, and also maintains local superlinear convergence by properly choosing the regularization term
\lambda_{t}

[
47
,
18
]
.
However, the iteration scheme (
4
) takes
{\mathcal{O}}(d^{3})
flops since it contains matrix inversion like Gauss–Newton update.
Moreover, the upper bound on iteration numbers for existing global convergent LM methods are no better than
{\mathcal{O}}(\epsilon^{-2})

[
45
,
53
,
20
,
6
,
43
]
, which makes the total computation cost be
{\mathcal{O}}(d^{3}\epsilon^{-2})
.
Besides, these methods require the line-search step or solving trust-region sub-problem
[
50
]
to achieve a descent direction, which leads their implementation be complicated.
Recently,
Mishchenko [35]
proposed an adaptive LM method without any line-search step nor sub-problem solver, which determine the regularization term by

\displaystyle\lambda_{t}\propto\sqrt{\|{\bf J}({\bf x}_{t})^{\top}{\bf F}({\bf x}_{t})\|}\,.

(5)

This method can find an
\epsilon
-stationary within at most
\tilde{{\mathcal{O}}}(\epsilon^{-2.5})
iterations under the cubic-growth condition,
which is worse than line-search or trust-region based LM methods.
Furthermore, its overall computation cost
{\mathcal{O}}(d^{3}\epsilon^{-2.5})
is worse than classical LM methods, and the existence of its local superlinear convergence is unknown.
Build upon this, there raises a natural question that:

Can we design a computation efficient and globally convergent LM method with local superlinear convergence?

We give an affirmative answer to the above question by proposing the Gram-Reduced Levenberg–Marquardt (GRLM) method.
Instead of existing LM methods that compute the Gram matrix
{\bf J}({\bf x}_{t})^{\top}{\bf J}({\bf x}_{t})
at every iteration, our method only updates
{\bf J}(\cdot)^{\top}{\bf J}(\cdot)
at the snapshot point and reuse it in next
m
iterations.
The update of our method can be formulated by

\displaystyle\begin{split}\!\!{\bf x}_{t+1}\!=\!{\bf x}_{t}\!-\!\Big(\!{\bf J}\big({{\bf x}}_{\pi(t)}\big)\!^{\top}{\bf J}\big({{\bf x}}_{\pi(t)}\big)\!+\!\lambda_{t}{\bf I}\Big)^{-1}\!{\bf J}({\bf x}_{t})\!^{\top}\!{\bf F}({\bf x}_{t}),\end{split}

(6)

where
\pi(t)\triangleq m{\left\lfloor t/m\right\rfloor}
and
\lambda_{t}
is chosen according to equation (
5
).
Note that the scheme (
6
) only needs to compute the matrix
{\bf J}({{\bf x}}_{\pi(t)})^{\top}{\bf J}({{\bf x}}_{\pi(t)})
in the case of
t\equiv 0~({\rm mod}~m)
, which significantly reduces the time of computing the Gram matrix and leads to a better algorithmic complexity.
For the global behavior, our method takes at most overall computation cost of
{\mathcal{O}}(d^{3}\epsilon^{-1}+d^{2}\epsilon^{-2})
to find an
\epsilon
-stationary point under the cubic-growth condition, which improves the results of
{\mathcal{O}}(d^{3}\epsilon^{-2})
of existing LM methods.
For the local behavior, our method has the superlinear rate when the solution has non-degenerate Jacobian, which goes beyond the theory of
Mishchenko [35]
.
We summarize the main theoretical results of GRLM and related work in Table
1
.

Preliminaries

We use notation
\|\,\cdot\,\|
to present the Euclidean norm of a vector and spectral norm of a matrix, respectively.
We use notation
{\bf I}
to present the identity matrix.
We denote
\sigma_{\min}(\cdot)
as the smallest singular value of a matrix.
For the ease of presentation, we denote the Gram matrix of
{\bf F}(\cdot)
by

\displaystyle{\bf G}({\bf x})\triangleq{\bf J}({\bf x})^{\top}{\bf J}({\bf x})\succeq{\bf 0},

(7)

where
{\bf J}({{\bf x}})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d\times d}
is the Jacobian of
{\bf F}:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
at point
{{\bf x}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
.
We also define the approximate stationary point of the function
\phi(\cdot)=\frac{1}{2}\|{\bf F}(\cdot)\|^{2}
as follows.

Definition 1
.

We say
{\bf x}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
is an
\epsilon
-stationary point of
\phi(\cdot)
if it satisfies that
\|{\bf J}({\bf x})^{\top}{\bf F}({\bf x})\|\leq\epsilon.

We make the following standard assumptions on the Jacobian of
{\bf F}(\cdot)
.

Assumption 2
.

We assume the Jacobian of
{\bf F}(\cdot)
is bounded and Lipschitz continuous, i.e., we have
\|{\bf J}({\bf x})\|\leq L_{1}
and

\displaystyle\|{\bf J}({\bf x})-{\bf J}({\bf y})\|\leq L_{2}\|{\bf x}-{\bf y}\|

(8)

hold for all
{\bf x},{\bf y}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and some constants
L_{1},L_{2}\geq 0
.

Proposition 3
.

If the Jacobian
{\bf J}(\cdot)
satisfies Assumption
2
, then it holds that

\displaystyle\begin{split}\|{\bf F}({\bf x})-{\bf F}({\bf y})\|\leq L_{1}\|{\bf x}-{\bf y}\|~~~\text{and}~~~\left\|{\bf F}({\bf x})-{\bf F}({\bf y})-{\bf J}({\bf y})({\bf x}-{\bf y})\right\|\leq\frac{L_{2}}{2}\|{\bf x}-{\bf y}\|^{2}\end{split}

(9)

for all
{{\bf x}},{{\bf y}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
.

The following proposition means Assumption
2
leads to the Lipschitz continuity of
{\bf G}(\cdot)
.

Proposition 4
.

If the function
{\bf F}(\cdot)
satisfies Assumption
2
, we have

\displaystyle\!\!\!\|{\bf G}({\bf x})\|\!\leq\!L_{1}^{2}~~\text{and}~~\|{\bf G}({\bf y})\!-\!{\bf G}({\bf x})\|\!\leq\!2L_{1}L_{2}\|{\bf x}\!-\!{\bf y}\|

(10)

for all
{\bf x},{\bf y}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
.

3
Related Work

The idea of reusing second-information dates back to 1960s, where
Shamanskii [42]
presented a variant of Newton method that constructs a new Jacobian every
m
iterations and analyzed its local behavior.
Later, such idea has been generalized to different type of Newton methods
[
17
,
46
,
24
,
1
]
and to training the large language models
[
33
,
16
]
.
Particularly,
Fan [17]
proposed Shamanskii Levenberg–Marquardt method in the form of

\displaystyle{\bf x}_{t+1}={\bf x}_{t}-\left({\bf J}({\bf x}_{\pi(t)})^{\top}{\bf J}({\bf x}_{\pi(t)})+\mu_{t}\|{\bf F}({\bf x}_{\pi(t)})\|^{\alpha}{\bf I}\right)^{-1}{\bf J}({\bf x}_{\pi(t)})^{\top}{\bf F}({\bf x}_{t}),

where
\mu_{t}>0
and
\alpha\in[1,2]
,
with a fast local superlinear rate.
However, these methods do not have global theoretical advantage compared to traditional methods.

Recently,
Doikov et al. [14]
proposed the regularized Newton method with lazy Hessians for general minimization problem
\min_{{{\bf x}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\phi({{\bf x}})
, which iterates with

\displaystyle\!\!\!{\bf x}_{t+1}\!=\!{\bf x}_{t}\!-\!\big(\nabla^{2}\phi({\bf x}_{\pi(t)})\!+\!\sqrt{\!c\|\nabla\phi({\bf x}_{t})\|}\,{\bf I}\big)\!^{-1}\nabla\phi({\bf x}_{t})

(11)

for some
c>0
.
However, this method is not suitable to solve our system of nonlinear equations (or its nonlinear least-square formulation) in the following aspects:

•

The update (
11
) requires accessing the second-order information of the objective. In the view of nonlinear least-square formulation (
2
), we have

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}\phi({\bf x}_{\pi(t)})={\bf J}({\bf x}_{\pi(t)})^{\top}{\bf J}({\bf x}_{\pi(t)})+\sum_{i=1}^{d}F_{i}({\bf x}_{\pi(t)})\nabla^{2}F_{i}({\bf x}_{\pi(t)}).

Compared with the cost of LM methods mainly depends on
{\bf J}(\cdot)
, accessing the Hessians

\nabla^{2}F_{i}(\cdot),\dots,\nabla^{2}F_{d}(\cdot)
in above equation may be much more expensive.

•

The convergence guarantees of (
11
) require the convexity of
\phi(\cdot)
and Lipschitz continuity of
\nabla^{2}\phi(\cdot)
, while the function
\phi(\cdot)=\frac{1}{2}\left\|{\bf F}(\cdot)\right\|_{2}
in our problem (
2
) is non-convex in general and the popular setting of nonlinear equations (Assumption
2
) cannot guarantee the Lipschitz continuity on
\nabla\phi(\cdot)
nor
\nabla^{2}\phi(\cdot)
.

•

It is also notable to mention that
Doikov et al. [14]
also studied the cubic regularized Newton methods
[
37
]
with lazy Hessians for the general non-convex case and
Chen et al. [12]
studied the lazy extra Newton methods for the minimax problems, however, these algorithms also requires accessing the Hessian
\nabla^{2}\phi(\cdot)
and its analysis is based on the Lipschitz continuity of Hessian. Thus, their theory is not applicable for solving the system of nonlinear equations.

t=0,1,\dots T-1

3:

We propose the Gram-Reduced Levenberg–Marquardt (GRLM) method in Algorithm
1
.
Our GRLM method takes the Gram matrix
{\bf G}({\bf z}_{t})={\bf J}({\bf z}_{t})^{\top}{\bf J}({\bf z}_{t})
as the approximation to
\nabla^{2}\phi({\bf z}_{t})
, which avoid the exact
\nabla^{2}\phi(\cdot)
in regularized Newton with Lazy Hessians
[
14
]
.
Additionally, the setting of
{\bf{z}}_{t}
in GRLM does not require accessing
{\bf J}(\cdot)^{\top}{\bf J}(\cdot)
in every iterations, which makes the algorithm be more efficient than existing LM methods.

We then consider the convergence of our GRLM method.
For the ease of presentation, we denote

\displaystyle\lambda_{t}\triangleq\sqrt{c\|{\bf J}({\bf x}_{t})^{\top}{\bf F}({\bf x}_{t})\|}~~~~~\text{and}~~~~~r_{t}\triangleq\|{\bf x}_{t+1}-{\bf x}_{t}\|.

where the constant
c>0
follows the input of Algorithm
1
.

In contrast to the analysis of
Mishchenko [35]
that only lower bounds
r_{t}
by
\lambda_{t+1}
, the following lemma provides both lower bound and upper bound of
r_{t}
by
\lambda_{t}
.

Lemma 5
.

Under Assumption
2
, Algorithm
1
holds that

\displaystyle\frac{\lambda_{t}^{2}}{c(L_{1}^{2}+\lambda_{t})}\leq r_{t}\leq\frac{\lambda_{t}}{c}.

(12)

Compared with the ordinary LM method, our GRLM use
{\bf G}({\bf z}_{t})
instead of
{\bf G}({\bf x}_{t})
at each iteration, which leads to some additional error terms.
In the following lemma, we show such terms can be bounded by the distance between the current iteration point to the snapshot point due to the Lipschitz continuity of
{\bf G}(\cdot)
we have proved in Proposition
4
.

Lemma 6
.

Under Assumption
2
, Algorithm
1
holds that

\displaystyle\left\|{\bf G}({\bf x}_{t})({\bf x}_{t+1}-{\bf x}_{t})+{\bf J}({\bf x}_{t})^{\top}{\bf F}({\bf x}_{t})\right\|\leq\lambda_{t}r_{t}+2L_{1}L_{2}r_{t}\|{\bf z}_{t}-{\bf x}_{t}\|,

and

\displaystyle\begin{split}\left\langle{\bf J}({\bf x}_{t})^{\top}{\bf F}({\bf x}_{t})+{\bf G}({\bf x}_{t})({\bf x}_{t+1}-{\bf x}_{t}),{\bf x}_{t+1}-{\bf x}_{t}\right\rangle\leq-\lambda_{t}r_{t}^{2}+2L_{1}L_{2}r_{t}^{2}\|{\bf x}_{t}-{\bf z}_{t}\|.\end{split}

(13)

4.1
Global Convergence Analysis

We establish the global convergence for Algorithm
1
in this subsection.
We adopt the following cubic-growth assumption which has been well-studied by
Mishchenko [35]
.

Assumption 7
.

We assume the function
{\bf F}:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
satisfies that

\displaystyle\!\|{\bf F}({\bf y})\|^{2}\!\leq\!\|{\bf F}({\bf x})+{\bf J}({\bf x})({\bf y}-{\bf x})\|^{2}\!+\!M\|{\bf y}-{\bf x}\|^{3}

(14)

for all
{\bf x},{\bf y}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, where
M>0
is some constant.

The following lemma show that, by properly setting the regularization parameter
c>0
, we can guarantee the descent property of
\|{\bf F}({\bf x}_{t})\|
at the snapshot point
{{\bf x}}_{t}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
such that
t\equiv 0~({\rm mod}~m)
.

Lemma 8
.

Under Assumption
2
and
7
, if we run Algorithm
1
with
c=\max\{4L_{1}L_{2}m,M\}
, then it holds that

\displaystyle\|{\bf F}({\bf x}_{(k+1)m})\|^{2}\leq\|{\bf F}({\bf x}_{km})\|^{2}\leq\cdots\|{\bf F}({\bf x}_{0})\|^{2}

and

\displaystyle\|{\bf F}({\bf x}_{km})\|^{2}-\|{\bf F}({\bf x}_{(k+1)m})\|^{2}\geq\sum_{t={km}}^{(k+1)m-1}\frac{r_{t}^{2}\lambda_{t}}{6},

for all
k=0,1,\cdots
.

Now, we present the global convergence results of our GRLM (Algorithm
1
).

Theorem 9
.

Following the settings of Lemma
8
, Algorithm
1
takes at most

\displaystyle T={\mathcal{O}}(m^{2}+m^{-0.5}\epsilon^{-2.5})

iterations to find an
\epsilon
-stationary point of
\phi(\cdot)
, i.e., we have

\displaystyle\min_{t=0,\cdots,T-1}\|{\bf J}({\bf x}_{t})^{\top}{\bf F}({\bf x}_{t})\|\leq\epsilon.

Remark 10
.

Taking
m=1
, Algorithm
1
reduces to the ordinary Levernberg–Mardquardt method proposed
by
Mishchenko [35]
.
Compared with the iteration complexity of
{{\mathcal{O}}}(\epsilon^{-2.5}\log(1/\epsilon))
established in the previous work,
Theorem
1
provides an improved iteration complexity of
{\mathcal{O}}(\epsilon^{-2.5})
, which removes the logarithmic factor.
We achieve this by lower bounding
r_{t}
by
\lambda_{t}
rather than
\lambda_{t+1}
in the existing work and using a novel proof framework that divides the iterations according to the value of
\lambda_{t}
.

Recall that Algorithm
1
needs to compute a new
{\bf G}({\bf z}_{t})
in every
m
iterations. We use
K
to denote the numbers of the snapshot points, then Theorem
9
means

\displaystyle K=\left\lceil\frac{T}{m}\right\rceil={\mathcal{O}}(m+m^{-1.5}\epsilon^{-2.5}).

For reusing the Gram matrix
{\bf G}({\bf{z}}_{t})
and reducing the computation cost, we can implement Algorithm
1
by performing singular value decomposition on the Jacobian
at the snapshot point, which generally takes
{\mathcal{O}}(d^{3})
flops and results

\displaystyle{\bf J}({\bf z}_{t})={\bf U}({\bf z}_{t}){\bf\Sigma}({\bf z}_{t}){\bf V}({\bf z}_{t})^{\top},

where
{\bf U}({\bf z}_{t}),{\bf V}({\bf z}_{t})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d\times d}
are orthogonal and
{\bf\Sigma}({\bf z}_{t})
is diagonal.
Based on the SVD of
{\bf J}({\bf{z}}_{t})
, we can update
{\bf x}_{t}
according to

\displaystyle{\bf x}_{t+1}

\displaystyle={\bf x}_{t}-\left({\bf G}({\bf z}_{t})+\lambda_{t}{\bf I}\right)^{-1}{\bf J}({\bf x}_{t})^{\top}{\bf F}({\bf x}_{t})

\displaystyle={\bf x}_{t}-{\bf V}({\bf z}_{t})\big(({\bf\Sigma}({\bf z}_{t}))^{2}+\lambda_{t}{\bf I}\big)^{-1}{\bf V}({\bf z}_{t})^{\top}{\bf J}({\bf x}_{t})^{\top}{\bf F}({\bf x}_{t}),

which can be done in
{\mathcal{O}}(d^{2})
flops.

Thus, the total computation cost of Algorithm
1
is

\displaystyle\begin{split}\#\text{flops}={\mathcal{O}}(d^{3}K+d^{2}T)=\!{\mathcal{O}}(d^{3}m\!+\!d^{3}m^{-1.5}\epsilon^{-2.5}\!+\!d^{2}m^{2}\!+\!d^{2}m^{-0.5}\epsilon^{-2.5}).\end{split}

(15)

Now we desire to select an appropriate
m
to minimize the overall flops.
We let
m={\mathcal{O}}(d^{\alpha}\epsilon^{-\beta})
for some
\alpha,\beta\geq 0
and plug it into equation (
15
), then the total computation cost is

\displaystyle\#\text{flops}

\displaystyle={\mathcal{O}}\big(d^{3+\alpha}\epsilon^{-\beta}+d^{3-1.5\alpha}\epsilon^{-2.5+1.5\beta}+d^{2+2\alpha}\epsilon^{-2\beta}+d^{2-0.5\alpha}\epsilon^{-2.5+0.5\beta}\big)

\displaystyle\geq{\mathcal{O}}\Big(d^{3-0.25\alpha}\epsilon^{-1.25+0.25\beta}+d^{2+1.25\alpha}\epsilon^{-1.25-0.75\beta}\Big),

where the inequality is due to the AM–GM and the equality holds when
\alpha=0
and
\beta=1
.
Therefore, we should take
m=\Theta(\epsilon^{-1})
, which leads to
\#\text{flops}={\mathcal{O}}(d^{3}\epsilon^{-1}+d^{2}\epsilon^{-2})
.

We formally present above result in the following corollary.

Corollary 11
.

Following the setting of Lemma
8
, running Algorithm
1
with
m=\Theta(\epsilon^{-1})
can find an
\epsilon
-stationary point of
\phi(\cdot)
within
{\mathcal{O}}(d^{3}\epsilon^{-1}+d^{2}\epsilon^{-2})
flops.

As a comparison, the globally convergent LM method proposed by
Mishchenko [35]

requires

{\mathcal{O}}(d^{3}\epsilon^{-2.5}\log(1/\epsilon))
flops to find an
\epsilon
-stationary point of
\phi(\cdot)
, which is more expensive than our complexity of
{\mathcal{O}}(d^{3}\epsilon^{-1}+d^{2}\epsilon^{-2})
.
Our result is also sharper than the complexity of
{\mathcal{O}}(d^{3}\epsilon^{-2})
for other LM methods
[
45
,
53
,
20
,
6
,
43
]
.

Proposition 13

(

Under Assumption

6
Conclusion

In this paper, we have proposed Gram-Reduced Levenberg–Marquardt method (GRLM) for solving nonlinear equations.
GRLM enhances the behaviour of existing LM methods by reducing the computation times of the Gram matrices.
It is globally convergent with simple iteration scheme and cheap computation cost.
Furthermore, it exhibits the local superlinear convergence, which cannot be achieved by any of the first-order methods.
To the best of our knowledge, this is the first method for solving nonlinear equations that can achieve the best of the both worlds.

For the future work, it is interesting to design sketched, stochastic, incremental, and distributed variants of GRLM
[
51
,
49
,
11
,
54
,
32
]
.
It is also possible to incorporate the idea of super universal Newton methods
[
15
]
to make GRLM parameter-free.

&={\bf x}_{t}-{\bf x}^{*}-\left({\bf G}({\bf z}_{t})+\lambda_{t}{\bf I}\right)^{-1}{\bf J}({\bf x}_{t})^{\top}{\bf F}({\bf x}_{t})\\
&=\left({\bf G}({\bf z}_{t})+\lambda_{t}{\bf I}\right)^{-1}\left(({\bf G}({\bf z}_{t})+\lambda_{t}{\bf I})({\bf x}_{t}-{\bf x}^{*})-{\bf J}({\bf x}_{t})^{\top}{\bf F}({\bf x}_{t})\right)\\
&=\left({\bf G}({\bf z}_{t})+\lambda_{t}{\bf I}\right)^{-1}\left({\bf J}({\bf x}_{t})^{\top}({\bf J}({\bf x}_{t})({\bf x}_{t}-{\bf x}^{*})-{\bf F}({\bf x}_{t}))+({\bf G}({\bf z}_{t})-{\bf G}({\bf x}_{t}))({\bf x}_{t}-{\bf x}^{*})+\lambda_{t}({\bf x}_{t}-{\bf x}^{*}))\right),\end{split}

then it holds that

\displaystyle\|{\bf x}_{t+1}-{\bf x}^{*}\|

\displaystyle\overset{\eqref{eq:prop-G},\eqref{eq:Gregubound}}{\leq}\frac{2}{\mu^{2}}\left(\|{\bf J}({\bf x}_{t})\|\|{\bf J}({\bf x}_{t})({\bf x}_{t}-{\bf x}^{*})-{\bf F}({\bf x}_{t})\|+2L_{1}L_{2}\|{\bf z}_{t}-{\bf x}_{t}\|\|{\bf x}_{t}-{\bf x}^{*}\|+\lambda_{t}\|{\bf x}_{t}-{\bf x}^{*}\|\right)

\displaystyle~~\overset{\eqref{eq:boundFJ}}{\leq}\frac{L_{1}L_{2}}{\mu^{2}}\|{\bf x}_{t}-{\bf x}^{*}\|^{2}+\frac{2L_{1}L_{2}}{\mu^{2}}\|{\bf x}_{t}-{\bf x}^{*}\|\|{\bf z}_{t}-{\bf z}^{*}\|+\frac{2}{\mu^{2}}\lambda_{t}\|{\bf x}_{t}-{\bf x}^{*}\|

\displaystyle~~\overset{\eqref{eq:boundrlambda}}{\leq}\frac{L_{1}L_{2}}{\mu^{2}}\|{\bf x}_{t}-{\bf x}^{*}\|^{2}+\frac{2L_{1}c^{0.5}}{\mu^{2}}\|{\bf x}_{t}-{\bf x}^{*}\|^{1.5}+\frac{2L_{1}L_{2}}{\mu^{2}}\|{\bf z}_{t}-{\bf x}^{*}\|\|{\bf x}_{t}-{\bf x}^{*}\|

\displaystyle~~~=\alpha_{1}\|{\bf x}_{t}-{\bf x}^{*}\|^{2}+\alpha_{2}\|{\bf x}_{t}-{\bf x}^{*}\|^{1.5}+\beta\|{\bf z}_{t}-{\bf x}^{*}\|\|{\bf x}_{t}-{\bf x}^{*}\|.

Since we suppose
{\bf x}_{t},{\bf z}_{t}\in{\mathcal{S}}
, it holds that

\displaystyle\|{\bf x}_{t+1}-{\bf x}^{*}\|

\displaystyle=(\alpha_{1}\|{\bf x}_{t}-{\bf x}^{*}\|+\alpha_{2}\|{\bf x}_{t}-{\bf x}^{*}\|^{0.5}+\beta\|{\bf z}_{t}-{\bf x}^{*}\|)\|{\bf x}_{t}-{\bf x}^{*}\|

\displaystyle\overset{\eqref{eq:local-condi}}{\leq}\|{\bf x}_{t}-{\bf x}^{*}\|,

which means
{\bf x}_{t+1}\in{\mathcal{S}}
.
∎
