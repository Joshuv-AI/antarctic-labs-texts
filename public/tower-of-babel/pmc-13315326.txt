Development and validation of web-based prediction models for major low anterior resection syndrome at 3 and 6 months after sphincter-preserving surgery in patients with rectal cancer
Authors: Lin Sun, Xiaoshuang Zhang, Qiang Zhou, Jiacheng Wang, Minjing Shen, Yuzhen Ding, Zhiqiang Zhu, Jianbo Yang, Xi Wang, Juju Huang, Haonan Fang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo analyse the factors influencing major low anterior resection syndrome (LARS) at 3 months and 6 months after surgery in rectal cancer patients undergoing sphincter-preserving procedures, and separately develop and validate risk prediction models for the 3-month and 6-month postoperative periods.MethodsThis study enrolled patients who underwent radical sphincter-preserving surgery for rectal cancer at the First Affiliated Hospital of the University of Science and Technology of China between August 2017 and September 2024. The LARS scale was used to assess bowel function at 3 months and 6 months postoperatively. In total, 794 patients at 3 months postoperatively and 749 patients at 6 months postoperatively were included and randomly allocated to a training set and a validation set in a 7:3 ratio. Least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression and multivariable logistic regression were used to identify factors influencing major LARS at 3 months and 6 months postoperatively, and to separately develop risk prediction models for the two time points. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves, calibration curves, and clinical decision curve analysis were applied to evaluate the discrimination, calibration, and clinical applicability of the two models.ResultsMultivariable logistic regression demonstrated that preoperative chemotherapy, preoperative radiotherapy, and tumour distance from the anal verge were common risk factors associated with major LARS at 3 months and 6 months postoperatively in rectal cancer patients undergoing sphincter-preserving surgery. Anastomotic leakage was identified as a risk factor specific to 3 months postoperatively, whereas prophylactic stoma was a risk factor specific to 6 months postoperatively. Based on these findings, risk prediction models for major LARS at 3 months and 6 months postoperatively were developed, and interactive web-based calculators were developed using the RShiny platform, accessible at https://zhang13579.shinyapps.io/Postoperative_3-Month/ and https://zhang13579.shinyapps.io/Postoperative_6-Month/, respectively. Both models demonstrated good predictive performance in the training cohort and the validation cohort, as assessed by discrimination, calibration, and clinical applicability.ConclusionThe risk prediction models for major LARS at 3 months and 6 months postoperatively in rectal cancer patients undergoing sphincter-preserving procedures developed in this study demonstrated good predictive performance and can assist clinical healthcare professionals in identifying patients at high risk of major LARS.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00384-026-05133-7.

Introduction

Global cancer statistics showed that there were an estimated 1.9 million new cases and 904,000 deaths from colorectal cancer worldwide in 2022 [1]. In China, there were 517,100 new cases and 240,000 deaths from colorectal cancer in 2022 [2]. Rectal cancer accounts for approximately 60% of colorectal cancer cases, of which low and ultra-low rectal cancer account for 70–80% of all rectal cancers [3]. With the establishment and implementation of the total mesorectal excision theory for sphincter-preserving surgery, as well as advances in neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy and minimally invasive surgical techniques, sphincter-preserving surgery has been performed in 74.6% of patients with low rectal cancer. Compared with patients without sphincter preservation, these patients have a better quality of life and psychological status [4–6]. However, 35.9–49.7% of rectal cancer patients undergoing sphincter-preserving surgery develop low anterior resection syndrome (LARS) postoperatively, manifesting as bowel dysfunction, difficulty in stool evacuation, increased bowel movement frequency, urgency, and faecal incontinence [7–9].

Emmertsen et al. [10] developed the LARS scale in 2012 to assess bowel function in rectal cancer patients undergoing sphincter-preserving surgery. The scale has a total score of 0–42, with scores of 30–42 indicating major LARS [10]. Studies have shown that 44–69% of rectal cancer patients undergoing sphincter-preserving surgery develop major LARS postoperatively [11, 12]. Compared with minor LARS, patients with major LARS experience more severe symptoms, such as faecal incontinence and flatus incontinence, as well as problems including social avoidance, insomnia, anxiety, and depression [13–15].

To prevent the development of major LARS after sphincter-preserving surgery for rectal cancer, Harji et al. [16] designed a bowel function recovery programme initiated at the time of hospital discharge, consisting of six stages and lasting 1 year. This programme resulted in a reduction in the incidence of major LARS from 48% at baseline to 12% at 1 year postoperatively. The finding suggests that early postoperative intervention can effectively reduce the incidence of major LARS. Risk prediction models integrate multiple clinical variables to provide accurate and intuitive risk predictions, thereby assisting clinical healthcare professionals in identifying patients at high risk of developing major LARS after sphincter-preserving surgery for rectal cancer and enabling targeted interventions [17]. At present, most risk prediction models for major LARS following sphincter-preserving surgery for rectal cancer are presented as nomograms and primarily focus on patients beyond seven months postoperatively [18, 19]. Regarding major LARS within 6 months after surgery, Ho et al. [20] from Vietnam primarily investigated its incidence and found that the incidence of major LARS within 6 months postoperatively was higher than that at 1 year after surgery. Therefore, this study analysed the factors influencing major LARS at 3 months and 6 months postoperatively in rectal cancer patients undergoing sphincter-preserving surgery, to develop risk prediction models and web-based calculators that assist clinical healthcare professionals in identifying patients at high risk of major LARS within 6 months postoperatively and implementing early targeted interventions.

Methods

Study design and participants

This study is a retrospective study, and the research subjects are rectal cancer patients who underwent sphincter-preserving surgery at the First Affiliated Hospital of the University of Science and Technology of China between August 2017 and September 2024. The inclusion criteria included (1) pathologically diagnosed with rectal cancer; (2) laparoscopic-assisted sphincter-preserving surgery for rectal cancer was performed (with the anastomosis being achieved using a stapler); (3) the surgery was performed by the same colorectal sub-specialty surgical team from this hospital, and perioperative management was provided by the same nursing team; (4) aged ≥ 18 years; and (5) at least 3 months of anal function post-surgery. The exclusion criteria included (1) incomplete clinical data; (2) death during follow-up; (3) coexisting Crohn’s disease, ulcerative colitis, or familial adenomatous polyposis; (4) local tumour recurrence; (5) loss to follow-up; and (6) patients who died, were transferred to the ICU, or underwent a second surgery(excluding ileostomy) due to severe anastomotic leakage and other reasons.

The sample size for this study was estimated using logistic regression analysis, calculated to be 10 to 15 times the number of covariates [21]. In this study, 23 covariates were included at 3 months post-surgery, and at 6 months post-surgery, postoperative radiotherapy and chemotherapy were categorised into two groups (0–3 months and 4–6 months post-surgery), resulting in 25 covariates. Considering a 20% dropout rate, the minimum sample size was 288 cases at 3 months post-surgery and 313 cases at 6 months post-surgery. Following the inclusion and exclusion criteria, a total of 794 rectal cancer patients were included at 3 months post-surgery and 749 patients at 6 months post-surgery. To ensure consistency between the training and validation groups, this study used the “caret” package in R4.5.1 to randomly assign data from all rectal cancer patients with anal preservation to training and validation groups in a 7:3 ratio. In total, the 3-month post-surgery training group consisted of 556 patients and the validation group consisted of 238 patients, while the 6-month post-surgery training group consisted of 525 patients and the validation group consisted of 224 patients. This retrospective study was approved by the Medical Research Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of the University of Science and Technology of China (Ethics approval number: 2025-RE-109), with a waiver of patient informed consent. Major LARS assessment tool

The LARS scale, developed by Danish scholars Emmertsen et al. [10] in 2012, includes five items, which provide specific scores for flatus incontinence (0, 4, 7 points), faecal incontinence (0, 3, 3 points), bowel frequency (4, 2, 0, 5 points), tenesmus (0, 9, 11 points), and urgency (0, 11, 16 points). The total score is in the range of 0 to 42, with 0–20 points indicating no LARS, 21–29 points indicating minor LARS, and 30–42 points indicating major LARS.

Research variables

This study included 23 covariates, determined through a review of the literature and consultations with clinical staff, including (1) general demographic data: age, gender, residence, education level, occupation, and body mass index (BMI) and (2) clinical data: hypertension, diabetes, heart disease, cerebrovascular disease, tumour distance from the anus, anastomosis distance from the anus, preoperative chemotherapy, preoperative radiotherapy, tumour TNM staging, anastomotic leakage, prophylactic stoma (ileostomy), postoperative chemotherapy, postoperative radiotherapy, preoperative hypoalbuminemia, preoperative anemia, preoperative prealbumin, and preoperative NRS-2002 score.

In this study, 33 patients with anastomotic leakage in the 3-month postoperative group and 30 in the 6-month postoperative group improved following conservative treatment, including abdominal anti-infective therapy, whereas four patients in each group underwent secondary surgery with retention of the ileostomy after conservative treatment had failed. Among the 794 patients included in the 3-month postoperative group, 121 received preoperative chemoradiotherapy (CRT) and 38 received postoperative CRT. Among the 749 patients included in the 6-month postoperative group, 116 received preoperative CRT, 36 received postoperative CRT during the 0⁓3-month postoperative period, and 35 received postoperative CRT during the 4⁓6-month postoperative period. The CRT regimens varied among patients, and the main regimens were radiotherapy combined with capecitabine alone or with capecitabine plus oxaliplatin.

Data collection

The LARS scores of the patients were collected by the ward’s enterostomal therapist (ET). Since 2017, the ET in our department has received standardised training prior to telephone follow-up, using the Chinese version of the LARS scale to assess postoperative bowel function in rectal cancer patients who underwent sphincter-preserving surgery. During the follow-up, each item of the scale is asked in sequence to avoid leading statements, minimising recall bias to the greatest extent. The ET collected and recorded assessment data via telephone follow-up at 3 and 6 months postoperatively, within 3 days before and after these time points.

Two researchers independently collected patient covariate information through the hospital’s electronic medical record system. For information on postoperative chemotherapy and radiotherapy, if not available from the electronic medical record system, the researchers contacted the patients by phone to supplement the data. In this study, out of 1193 patients, 93 underwent telephone re-verification. Among the 794 patients included in the March model, 23 were re-verified; among the 749 patients included in the June model, 18 were re-verified; and 52 patients were re-verified in the excluded group. Once the data collection was completed, the two researchers cross-checked the data. Any discrepancies were reviewed by a third researcher, and the final results were determined through discussion. To ensure the accuracy of data collection and standardise the data collection format and methods, the three researchers received standardised training on the use of the electronic medical record database, data collection, and data entry prior to the collection process. The data were consistently entered into Excel 2021 software.

Statistical analysis

This study was analysed using SPSS 26.0 and R 4.5.1. Continuous variables with a normal distribution were expressed as the mean ± standard deviation, and intergroup comparisons were performed using the independent-samples t test. Continuous variables not conforming to a normal distribution were described as the median (interquartile range) [M(P25, P75)], and intergroup comparisons were conducted using the Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages, with intergroup comparisons performed using the χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test, while intergroup comparisons of ordinal data were conducted using the Mann-Whitney U test. Predictive variables were identified using least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression with tenfold cross-validation, which applies a penalised regression approach to shrink the coefficients of less important variables towards zero, thereby effectively addressing multicollinearity and demonstrating good performance in handling high-dimensional data and reducing overfitting. Independent variables selected by LASSO regression were included in a multivariable logistic regression model, and variables with P < 0.05 were used to construct the predictive model. The area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve (AUC) was used to assess the discriminative ability of the predictive model. Calibration curves were used to assess the calibration of the predictive model, with good agreement between the predicted and observed curves indicating good model calibration. Decision curve analysis (DCA) was used to evaluate the clinical applicability of the risk prediction model.

Results

Epidemiological characteristics of major LARS and comparison of baseline characteristics between the training and validation groups

Among all rectal cancer patients included in this study who underwent sphincter-preserving surgery, major LARS occurred in 236 cases (29.7%) at 3 months postoperatively and in 165 cases (22.0%) at 6 months postoperatively. In the training cohort, major LARS occurred in 161 cases (29.0%) at 3 months postoperatively and in 113 cases (21.5%) at 6 months postoperatively. In the validation cohort, major LARS occurred in 75 cases (31.5%) at 3 months postoperatively and in 52 cases (23.2%) at 6 months postoperatively. LASSO regression to identify influencing factors for major LARS

The dynamic trajectory plot of the variable regression coefficients in LASSO regression includes all candidate factors, with the degree of model compression increasing as the λ value increases, thereby resulting in the smallest number of candidate variables. The optimal tuning parameter λ was selected using tenfold cross-validation. To simplify the model, the study selected λ.1se as the optimal value (λ.1se = 0.0444 at 3 months post-surgery, λ.1se = 0.0524 at 6 months post-surgery, with corresponding −log(λ) values of 3.1136 and 2.9485, respectively). Multivariable logistic regression analysis to identify factors associated with major LARS

Major LARS was defined as the dependent variable (coded as 0 = no major LARS and 1 = major LARS), and variables selected by LASSO regression were entered into a multivariable logistic regression model. The analysis showed that preoperative chemotherapy, preoperative radiotherapy, anastomotic leakage, and a low tumour distance from the anal verge were risk factors for major LARS at 3 months postoperatively in rectal cancer patients undergoing sphincter-preserving surgery. Preoperative chemotherapy, preoperative radiotherapy, prophylactic stoma, and a low tumour distance from the anal verge were identified as independent risk factors for major LARS at 6 months postoperatively. Discussion

Maalouf et al. [15] found that major LARS has a substantial negative impact on patients’ social activities, sexual life, and sleep. Accordingly, it is crucial for healthcare professionals to identify patients at high risk of developing major LARS within 6 months after surgery and to implement early interventions. In this study, multivariable logistic regression identified four risk factors for major LARS at 3 months postoperatively, including preoperative chemotherapy, preoperative radiotherapy, anastomotic leakage, and tumour distance from the anal verge, and four risk factors for major LARS at 6 months postoperatively, including preoperative chemotherapy, preoperative radiotherapy, prophylactic stoma, and tumour distance from the anal verge. The risk prediction models for major LARS at 3 and 6 months postoperatively constructed using these factors showed good predictive performance, as indicated by the AUC, calibration curves, and decision curve analyses in both the training and validation cohorts, and were able to identify rectal cancer patients undergoing sphincter-preserving surgery who were at high risk of developing major LARS within 6 months after surgery.

This study found that a short tumour distance from the anal verge was a common risk factor for major LARS at both 3 and 6 months postoperatively in rectal cancer patients undergoing sphincter-preserving surgery, consistent with the findings of Yan et al. [19]. An excessively low tumour location may require the surgeon to resect the tumour more distally, resulting in a shorter residual rectum, impaired rectal compliance, and a sensation of urgency during defecation; moreover, a lower tumour location leads to a lower colorectal anastomosis, increasing the risk of anal sphincter injury and thereby impairing bowel control [22].

This study demonstrated that preoperative chemoradiotherapy was a risk factor for major LARS at both 3 and 6 months postoperatively in rectal cancer patients undergoing sphincter-preserving surgery. This may be attributed to the fact that chemotherapeutic agents can induce neuropathy of the pelvic floor nerves, impairing neural control of the anal sphincter and consequently affecting bowel function, while preoperative radiotherapy can cause ischaemic changes in the perirectal tissues, leading to tissue fibrosis and reduced bowel compliance, and may also damage rectal nerves or the anal sphincter, thereby resulting in defecatory dysfunction [23, 24].

This study demonstrated that anastomotic leakage was a risk factor for major LARS at 3 months postoperatively in rectal cancer patients undergoing sphincter-preserving surgery, consistent with the findings of Jutesten et al. [25]. The inflammatory response caused by anastomotic leakage can lead to perianastomotic infection and fibrotic proliferation of the rectal wall, resulting in anastomotic stenosis and reduced rectal wall compliance. Moreover, scar formation following anastomotic healing may impair the volume and motility of the neorectum, leading to postoperative bowel dysfunction [8, 26]. Therefore, it is suggested that healthcare professionals control perioperative blood glucose levels, ensure adequate nutrition, reduce intestinal fluid secretion, and perform reinforced anastomotic suturing intraoperatively to reduce the occurrence of anastomotic leakage [27]. Moreover, surgical technique constitutes an important factor influencing both the occurrence and severity of LARS, particularly when additional procedures, such as lateral lymph node dissection (LLND), are undertaken. Previous studies have indicated that LLND is associated with a higher risk of bowel, urinary, and sexual dysfunction, which may be related to injury of the pelvic autonomic nerves [28]. Furthermore, as LLND is a technically demanding procedure, its learning curve may further affect postoperative functional outcomes, with functional complications being more frequent during the initial stages of this learning curve [29]. This suggests that, in addition to patient and tumour characteristics, the surgeon’s experience may also be an important factor affecting postoperative LARS symptoms.

This study demonstrated that the retention of a prophylactic stoma was a risk factor for major LARS at 6 months postoperatively in rectal cancer patients undergoing sphincter-preserving surgery, consistent with the findings of Ma et al. [23]. In patients with a low anastomosis or to prevent postoperative complications such as anastomotic leakage, surgeons may construct a prophylactic stoma intraoperatively [30]. However, patients with a retained prophylactic stoma tended to experience more severe postoperative LARS, which may be explained by several possible mechanisms. First, the prolonged lack of pelvic floor muscle use during faecal diversion may lead to muscle atrophy and colorectal microbiota imbalance [19, 23, 30]. Second, patients who undergo preoperative radiotherapy and have an excessively low anastomotic level are more likely to retain a prophylactic stoma, and both factors have been shown consistently in this study and previous studies to contribute to the development of major LARS postoperatively [19, 23, 30]. Therefore, this study targets rectal cancer patients who undergo a temporary stoma, utilising a web-based calculator to predict the risk of severe LARS at 3 and 6 months after surgery, aiding clinical staff in decision-making about the likelihood of severe LARS in patients following sphincter-preserving surgery for rectal cancer, and providing focused postoperative follow-up and early rehabilitation interventions for high-risk patients. Clinically, patients with rectal cancer who undergo sphincter-preserving surgery and a temporary stoma typically undergo a second surgery 3 to 6 months after the initial operation [31, 32], wherein the small bowel stoma is closed, and normal anal function is restored. For such patients, healthcare professionals may use the web-based calculator to determine the timing of the stoma closure to avoid early restoration, which may still result in severe faecal incontinence due to low anterior resection syndrome, affecting quality of life and potentially requiring another stoma procedure.

By using the risk prediction models developed in this study, clinical healthcare professionals can identify rectal cancer patients undergoing sphincter-preserving surgery who are at high risk of developing major LARS within 6 months after surgery and provide guidance on interventions that combine Kegel exercises with warm-water sitz baths. These interventions enhance pelvic floor and perianal muscle training and improve local vascular function through warm-water stimulation, thereby relieving muscle spasm and improving bowel control [33, 34]. A staged intervention strategy as described by Harji et al. [16] may also be adopted. Traditional Chinese medicine may also be incorporated, for example by acupuncture at the Baliao and Zusanli acupoints to stimulate the sacral nerves and modulate anorectal pressure, thereby alleviating LARS symptoms and improving postoperative quality of life [35]. Given that LARS can be partially improved through long-term pelvic floor muscle training, the present predictive model may serve as a basis for guiding early rehabilitation interventions. For patients identified as high risk by the model, clinicians may consider initiating pelvic floor muscle training earlier and implementing more proactive postoperative management in conjunction with symptom monitoring.

This study also has several limitations. This was a retrospective study in which some patients’ radiotherapy and chemotherapy information could not be obtained from the hospital medical records system and therefore had to be supplemented by the researchers through telephone follow-up. In addition, radiotherapy and chemotherapy regimens varied among patients, resulting in a certain degree of bias in the study. Moreover, as this was a single-center retrospective study, the generalisability of the models remains to be validated. Additionally, certain confounding factors, such as smoking and peripheral vascular disease, were not included in the analysis. Future studies could include patients from different regions for external validation to assess the generalisability of the model and apply it in clinical practice to identify high-risk groups for major LARS within 6 months postoperatively, enabling early targeted interventions.

Conclusions

This study developed and validated two risk prediction models for major LARS within 6 months after surgery in rectal cancer patients undergoing sphincter-preserving surgery, and the results demonstrated that the models have good applicability. Healthcare professionals can apply these models to identify rectal cancer patients undergoing sphincter-preserving surgery who are at high risk of developing major LARS within 6 months after surgery, thereby enabling early targeted interventions.
