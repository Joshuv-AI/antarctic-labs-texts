Circulating senescence-associated secretory phenotype factors across the stages of type 1 diabetes in a cross-sectional cohort
Authors: Camille Préfontaine, Jasmine Pipella, Ian Heidinger, Nicole Alcasid, Peter J Thompson
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractType 1 diabetes (T1D) results from a chronic autoimmune disease that leads to pancreatic beta cell death and states of dysfunction such as senescence. Cellular senescence is a programmed stress response involving cell cycle arrest, apoptosis resistance and secretion of immunogenic molecules referred to as the senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP). Histologic evidence indicates the accumulation of senescent beta cells in T1D, however, there are no biomarkers to noninvasively detect senescent beta cells. Circulating SASP factors have been used as a biomarker for senescent cell accumulation in age-related diseases, but a similar approach has not been explored in T1D. Here, we measured a panel of 7 previously identified human islet-secreted SASP factors (GDF15, CXCL1, CXCL5, CXCL8, CCL20, IGFBP4 and TNFRSF10C) in a blinded cohort of pediatric and young adult plasma samples from TrialNet including autoantibody-negative controls, single autoantibody-positive, and clinical stages of T1D progression (n = 20 donors per group). SASP factor concentrations were higher in stages 1, 2 and 3 recent onset T1D donors versus controls and effectively discriminated stages 2 and 3 disease status. SASP factor concentration did not associate with the extent of beta cell dysfunction, autoantibody titre or donor age. Analysis of matched plasma and pancreas samples from an independent cohort of control donors supported a relationship between senescent beta cells and circulating SASP markers. These results suggest that senescent beta cell burden may be reflected by the circulating levels of specific islet-associated SASP factors and could represent a novel biomarker for senescence in T1D.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-24506-6.Subject terms: Type 1 diabetes, Senescence, Predictive markers

Introduction

Type 1 diabetes (T1D) results from a T cell-driven autoimmune process that leads to destruction of the majority of pancreatic beta cells, leading to insulin deficiency1. The natural history of the disease follows a clinically tractable course in genetically susceptible individuals, starting with unknown triggering event(s) that lead to activation of autoimmunity2. Stage 1 T1D is considered the earliest presymptomatic stage, where ≥ 2 autoantibodies are present. Stage 2 entails the development of glucose intolerance while stage 3 is clinical diagnosis of symptomatic T1D3. The utility of the staging model was instrumental in clinical trials that led to FDA approval of teplizumab, an anti-CD3 T cell immunotherapy that delays progression from stage 2 to 3 by a median time of 3 years4,5. Although autoantibodies and metabolic testing remains the gold-standard for staging T1D, the identification and implementation of additional biomarkers could refine predictions of the rate of progression, discriminate progressors from non-progressors and stratify patients for targeted interventions. Indeed, recent studies have shown that protein biomarkers in circulation can predict progression of autoimmunity and onset of T1D6 as well as individuals likely to experience partial T1D remission following insulin therapy7.

In addition to the established role of T cell-mediated autoimmunity in T1D, it is now generally accepted that beta cell stress contributes to disease development and progression8. Beta cells undergo a variety of stress responses during development of T1D, including unfolded protein response (UPR), type I interferon response and senescence9. Clinical trials targeting UPR and type I interferon response have shown promise for delaying the decline in C-peptide post-diagnosis10–13. Unlike UPR and interferon response which can lead to beta cell death, senescence is a stress response associated with resistance to cell death14. Accordingly, histological evidence from human donor pancreas has shown that senescent beta cells accumulate in autoantibody-positive (stage 1 and 2) and recent onset stage 3 T1D donors as compared with normoglycemic healthy controls15. Senescence also involves activation of a complex secretome that includes cytokines, chemokines, growth factors, extracellular matrix proteases, shed receptors and other immunogenic molecules referred to as the senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP)16. Preclinical studies in the nonobese diabetic (NOD) mouse model for T1D support senescence as a relevant therapeutic target for delaying progression of T1D from the presymptomatic to symptomatic stage15,17,18. However, in addition to identifying drug targets that mitigate the negative consequences of senescent beta cells, moving senescence-targeted approaches towards a clinical setting for delaying T1D will require clinically tractable and noninvasive biomarkers for accumulated senescent cells14.

The accumulation of senescent cells during aging has been strongly correlated with the levels of SASP factors in circulation19–23 and measuring SASP factor levels in plasma or serum samples has emerged as a novel approach for estimating the burden of senescence and predicting disease risk and outcomes21–26. Circulating SASP factors have also been measured in clinical trials as a biomarker to assess the efficacy of interventions targeting senescent cells26–29. Previous work has identified a unique collection of SASP factors secreted from human islets and beta cells undergoing senescence in culture models30,31, which could reach circulation and provide a biomarker for accumulated senescent beta cells in T1D. Our previous studies demonstrated that GDF15, CXCL1, CXCL8, CXCL5, CCL20, IGFBP4 and TNFRSF10C are upregulated transcriptionally and/or at the protein secretion level from human islets undergoing senescence in response to DNA damage in culture models30,32. Notably, secreted GDF15 was also found to be increased from isolated T1D donor islets containing senescent beta cells32. To determine whether this panel of islet SASP factors could be monitored in circulation during the development of T1D as a reflection of the accumulation of senescent cells in this disease, we performed a cross-sectional cohort study to explore utility and feasibility in collaboration with TrialNet. We measured SASP factors in a blinded age- and sex-matched cohort of pediatric and young adult biobanked plasma samples from participants in the TrialNet study group representing single autoantibody-positive (early autoimmunity), stages 1, 2 and 3 recent onset T1D that had stimulated C-peptide responses, as compared with healthy autoantibody-negative controls. We also examined relationships of SASP factor panel concentration to metabolic data, autoantibody titre, donor age and age at diagnosis of T1D. Several of the individual SASP markers as well as the total concentration of all seven SASP factors were higher in stages 2 and 3 donors compared to controls. By combining the most informative SASP markers, we refined the full panel to 4 markers that were higher in stages 1, 2 and 3 donors compared with autoantibody-negative controls. The refined 4-marker SASP panel had high sensitivity and specificity were strongly discriminative of stage 2 and 3 T1D status. Levels of these SASP factors were not predictive of measures of beta cell function in stage 2 and 3 donors and were not associated with autoantibody titre in the cohort. In addition, the SASP panel concentration was not associated with donor age over the pediatric to young adult age ranges represented in our study. Finally, we identified SASP factors associated with pediatric islets and confirmed a relationship between SASP markers in plasma and beta cells expressing senescence marker p21 in the pancreas of control donors using an independent cohort. Taken together these results support the feasibility and utility of monitoring a panel of circulating SASP factors in plasma samples in the context of T1D and suggest that SASP might be a useful biomarker for accumulated senescent cells in this disease.

Results

A cross-sectional cohort of plasma donor samples across the stages of T1D

To investigate whether SASP factors associated with senescence in human islets are detectable in circulation and change during the natural history of T1D, we obtained de-identified plasma donor samples from the TrialNet biorepository for an ancillary study. A total of 100 samples were obtained from control/autoantibody-negative (n = 20), early autoimmunity (single autoantibody-positive, n = 20), stage 1 T1D (≥ 2 autoantibodies, n = 20), stage 2 T1D (≥ 2 autoantibodies, dysglycemia), and stage 3 recent onset T1D with stimulated C-peptide responses (≥ 2 autoantibodies, clinically diagnosed T1D ranging from 133 to 437 days duration). Donor sample groups were matched for age and sex, as shown in Table 1. Samples were blinded prior to receipt and running assays and were unblinded after data were submitted to TrialNet.

Individual SASP markers and total SASP panel concentration increase during the stages of T1D

We performed Luminex multi-analyte bead assays to measure concentrations of a panel of 7 human islet/beta cell SASP factors including GDF15, CXCL1, CXCL5, CCL20, CXCL8, IGFBP4, and TNFRSF10C. These factors were previously identified from human islet and beta cell ex vivo models of senescence induced by DNA damage30,32. The panel was carefully selected on the basis of three criteria: (1) their consistent induction at the mRNA level from RNA-seq datasets on DNA damage induced senescence phenotypes from human islets30; (2) their increased secretion at the protein level during DNA damage mediated human islet senescence30,32; (3) their known association with the SASP of other types of senescent cells19,21. Importantly, we previously showed that GDF15 secretion is increased from T1D donor islets containing p21-expressing senescent beta cells32, underscoring the relevance of this marker to T1D. Hence, we reasoned our SASP panel would be an accurate reflection of the complexity of the senescent human islet secretome in T1D.

After unblinding samples for analyses, we first determined whether there were significant differences in individual SASP factor concentrations between control donors and any stage of T1D (Fig. 1A). SASP marker concentrations showed broad heterogeneity across donors in the same groups some samples being very low (2–10 pg/ml) to others at high concentrations (500–1000 pg/ml). GDF15, CXCL5 and IGFBP4 each showed significantly higher levels in stage 3 donors compared to controls (Fig. 1A). GDF15 and CCL20 showed significantly higher levels in stage 2 donors compared to controls (P < 0.005 and P < 0.05, respectively, Fig. 1A). Interestingly, CXCL5 was the only factor that was significantly higher in stage 1 donors compared to controls (P < 0.005, Fig. 1A). Thus, each of these 4 SASP factors in our panel showed a unique profile of differences between the stages 1–3 donors and controls. Notably, several factors were uninformative and did not show altered levels at any stage compared with controls, including TNFRSF10C, CXCL8 and CXCL1. Of these, TNFRSF10C was the most abundant factor across all donor groups and was not significantly different between controls and any stage of T1D (Fig. 1A). No factor on our panel showed a significantly higher level in the early autoimmunity (single autoantibody-positive) donors as compared with controls (Fig. 1A).

As SASP factors are typically co-secreted from senescent cells16, we next measured the total burden of SASP by adding concentrations from all seven factors in each donor to compare across the groups. This showed that the total 7-marker SASP panel concentration was significantly higher in stage 2 and stage 3 T1D donors as compared to the control donors (P < 0.05 and P < 0.05, respectively) (Fig. 1B). Reducing the panel to just the 4 informative markers that were significantly different at any disease stage compared with controls: GDF15, CXCL5, CCL20 and IGFBP4 showed higher concentrations in stages 1, 2 and 3 donors relative to controls (P < 0.05, P < 0.05 and P < 0.0005, respectively) (Fig. 1B) confirming they are the most informative in the total 7-marker panel. SASP factor concentrations for both the total 7-marker and 4-marker panels were also not different between male and female stage 2 and 3 donors (Fig. 1C) suggesting there was no sex-bias driving the differences in SASP factors. Pearson analysis showed that each of the 4 SASP marker concentrations was positively correlated to each other (Fig. 1D).

SASP panel concentration is strongly discriminative of stage 3 T1D

To determine how well the SASP panel concentration discriminates T1D disease stage from controls, we performed receiver operator characteristic (ROC) curve analyses (Fig. 2). Inclusion of all 7 markers showed reasonable discriminative value for stage 3 donor status with an ROC area under curve (AUC) = 0.75, CI = 0.60–0.91, P = 0.006 (Fig. 2A). However, using the 4-marker SASP panel showed much stronger accuracy for stage 3 donor status with high sensitivity and specificity, AUC = 0.96, CI = 0.92–1.00, P < 0.0001 (Fig. 2B). The full panel (Fig. 2C) and the 4-marker panel (Fig. 2D) gave comparable ROC AUC values for stage 2 donors: AUC = 0.76, CI = 0.61–0.91 P = 0.0045 and AUC = 0.78, CI = 0.63–0.91, P = 0.0029, respectively. However, only the 4-marker panel had acceptable discriminative value for stage 1 donors (AUC = 0.75, CI = 0.6–0.9, P = 0.0063) (Fig. 2E and F).

SASP panel concentration is not associated with the degree of residual beta cell function or autoantibody titres

SASP in age-related diseases is associated with tissue dysfunction16 and consistent with this we found higher levels of our 4-marker SASP panel in stage 1 and 2 donors coinciding with the transition to categorical dysglycemia (Fig. 1B). Thus, we explored how well SASP panel levels associated with the extent of beta cell dysfunction in the stage 2 donors and/or residual beta cell function in stage 3 donors. To do this, we examined clinical metabolic data obtained from TrialNet from stage 2 donor oral glucose tolerance tests (OGTTs) and stage 3 donors mixed-meal tolerance tests (MMTTs) and performed linear regression analysis with the 4-marker SASP panel concentrations (Fig. 3A and B). While SASP panel concentration showed a negative relationship with C-peptide area under the curve (AUC) as expected, it did not significantly account for the extent of variability in OGTT in the stage 2 donors (Fig. 3A). Similarly, stage 3 donor MMTT C-peptide AUC also showed a negative association with the 4-marker SASP concentration but did not reach significance (Fig. 3B). We also examined the relationship between autoantibody titre and SASP panel concentration using linear regression statistics with the following autoantibodies measured in all donors with ≥ 2 autoantibodies (stages 1, 2 and 3): GAD65, insulin (mIAA) and islet cell autoantigen IA-2 (ICA). Notably, autoantibody titre for these three major autoantibodies did not explain the 4-marker SASP panel concentration in stages 1, 2 and 3 donors (Fig. 3C, D and E).

SASP panel concentration is not associated with donor age in the trialnet cohort

Given that we had a range of donor ages from pediatric to adult (ages 9–35) and also T1D diagnosis date (stage 3 T1D) in the TrialNet cohort, we determined whether our SASP panel was associated with donor age and whether there were differences between the stage 3 T1D donors SASP factor levels and age at onset (Fig. 4). Variation in the 7-marker SASP or 4-marker SASP concentration was not explained by donor age across the cohort of all donors (n = 100 samples) (Fig. 4A and B). As T1D endotypes have been suggested based on age at diagnosis33,34, we also examined the relationships between SASP concentration and diagnosis age. We stratified the stage 3 T1D donors based on age at diagnosis, but found no significant differences between SASP factor concentrations in the younger age diagnosis donors (≤ 12 years of age, n = 6) compared with the older onset T1D donors (> 12 years of age, n = 14) (Fig. 4C). Consistent with this, age at diagnosis in stage 3 T1D samples did not explain variability in total 7-marker or 4-marker SASP panel concentrations as shown by linear regression statistics (Fig. 4D and E).

Comparison of pediatric and adult islet SASP profiles reveals donor heterogeneity

Our human islet SASP signature was determined from culture model studies using islets from young adult and middle-aged adult donors30. Since there may be differences in SASP in pediatric versus adult islets, secreted SASP markers relevant to pediatric individuals (0–18 years of age) may be missed. To circumvent these limitations and identify novel candidate SASP markers relevant to pediatric individuals, we used antibody arrays to screen secreted factors from islets of pediatric and middle-aged adult donors. Islets from a 5-year male old donor, a 16 year-old male donor, a 56 year-old male donor and a 57 year-old male donor were each induced into a senescence response using DNA strand break damage with bleomycin according to our established approach30, followed by collection of the conditioned media at day 5 post-drug washout to interrogate the SASP profile across 111 different cytokines, chemokines, proteases, growth factors and shed receptors, many of which are putative SASP factors16 (Supplementary Fig. S1). Validation of senescence induction by a combination of at least two independent assays, including either qRT-PCR or western blotting along with Luminex assay or ELISAs demonstrated dramatic induction of CDKN1A mRNA and elevation of p21 protein levels, along with elevated SASP gene expression and protein secretion of factors including GDF15, CXCL1, IGFBP4 and TNFRSF10C (Supplementary Fig. S1A-S1E). Antibody array profiling of the conditioned media from the islets of each donor showed that bleomycin-induced senescent islets had differences in their secretory profile compared to controls with 10 factors found either increased or in some cases decreased in senescent conditions compared to controls (Supplementary Fig. S1F). Interestingly, the 16 year-old donor islets had the broadest panel of SASP factors increased in secretion, including: GDF15, CXCL8, CXCL1, CXCL5, CCL20, MIF, MCP-1, Cystatin C, and VEGF (Supplementary Fig. S1G). Unfortunately, the antibody array lacked IGFBP4 and TNFRSF10C so these two SASP markers on our panel could not be surveyed. One of the two middle-aged adult donor islet preparations (UWHI357R) also showed several factors increased, including SERPINE1, Osteopontin, Lipocalin-2, CXCL8 and GDF15 (Supplementary Fig. S1G). The younger pediatric donor islet preparation (R593) and the other middle-aged adult donor (R591) had a limited SASP profile as GDF15 was the only SASP factor found to be increased and SERPINE1 and Osteopontin were found to be decreased from the senescent islets of these two donors (Supplementary Fig. S1G). Notably GDF15 was commonly increased in secretion in all preparations (Supplementary Fig. S1G and S1H). Taken together these data support the validity of our SASP panel composition for both pediatric and adult donors and suggest that islet SASP profiles exhibit donor heterogeneity.

SASP concentration relates to the frequency of p21+ senescent beta cells

Finally, we explored whether SASP concentration in plasma corresponded to the frequency of senescent beta cells in the pancreas. To do this, we took advantage of the heterogeneity in SASP panel concentration among control donors (Fig. 1), reasoning that donors with relatively high SASP concentration would have a higher frequency of senescent beta cells in their pancreas, as compared with those with low SASP concentrations. We measured SASP panel concentrations in an independent control cohort of plasma samples from normoglycemic control female and male donors (n = 20), ranging in age from pediatric to geriatric (4- to 73 years-old, Supplementary Table S2) obtained from the Alberta Diabetes Institute. We calculated 4-marker SASP panel concentrations and found a broad range of concentrations in this cohort spanning ~ 11,500 pg/ml as the highest concentration down to ~ 3,100 pg/ml as the lowest concentrations (Fig. 5A), which was higher than the highest concentration samples in the TrialNet cohort controls (Fig. 1B). Stratifying donors by age or sex showed that there were no significant differences among donors ≤ 18 years-old, 19–54 years-old, or ≥ 55 years-old (Fig. 5B), or between females and males (Fig. 5C). We next calculated average Z-scores for each of the 4 SASP panel markers to compare samples with relatively high SASP (SASPHigh) versus those with low SASP (SASPLow) (Fig. 5D). The top 5 SASPHigh donors were ages 70 (R499), 36 (R489), 32 (R488), 15 (R457) and 7 (R516) years old, whereas the bottom 5 SASPLow donors were 30 (R530), 29 (R537), 28 (R501), 27 (R535) and 18 (R521) years old. The 4-marker SASP panel concentration was higher in the SASPHigh versus SASPLow group, as expected (Fig. 5E) but there were no differences in average age between the groups (Fig. 5F). We next performed immunohistochemistry and confocal imaging for senescence marker p21 and beta cell marker insulin using matched pancreas sections obtained from the SASPHigh and SASPLow donors to quantify the frequency of senescent beta cells (p21+/Ins+) in manner blinded to the donor group. p21 was selected as a senescence marker because it is part of the senescence signature in residual beta cells of T1D donors15 and is upregulated by DNA damage and p53 signaling in human islets in association the secretion of SASP factors on our panel30. Remarkably, the average frequency of p21+ beta cells on a per islet basis was higher in the SASPHigh compared with SASPLow donor group islets (1.62% versus 5.98%, P < 0.0001, Fig. 5G and H). However, when we compared average p21+ beta cell frequency on a per donor basis SASPHigh and SASPLow donors, we found that the difference was not significant (P = 0.0694), due to two SASPHigh donors that had similar frequencies of p21+ beta cells to the five SASPLow donors (Fig. 5I). Plotting p21+ beta cell frequency for each donor and each islet indicated that the differences between the SASPHigh and SASPLow islets were driven by the donors R499, R488, and R489 which had on average 11.6%, 8.5% and 7.5% p21+ beta cells, respectively (Fig. 5J). In contrast, all the SASPLow donors and the SASPHigh donors R516 and R457 had on average ~ 1–2.1% p21+ beta cells (Fig. 5J). Together these data suggest the frequency of p21+ beta cells distinguishes SASPHigh from SASPLow control donors on an islet-population level, but not always at the level of individual SASPHigh donors. Evaluation of additional senescence markers will be required to identify differences in senescent beta cell accumulation between some SASPHigh and SASPLow donors.

Discussion

Immunohistochemistry studies from human donor pancreas showed that senescent beta cells are present in normoglycemic donors but accumulate at higher rates in single and multiple-autoantibody-positive donors with the highest burden found in T1D donors with residual C-peptide within 0–6 years post-diagnosis15. However, there are no non-invasive biomarkers for monitoring the accumulation of senescent cells in T1D, hampering efforts to track senescent cell burden during disease development. An increased burden of senescent cells in age-related diseases is reflected by higher levels of SASP factors in circulation19,21–25 and we hypothesized that this may also be applicable to T1D where senescent beta cells accumulate. In this study, we explored the hypothesis that senescent beta cell burden is reflected by higher levels of circulating islet-associated SASP factors during the stages of T1D. Previous work examined circulating immune cell cytokines and growth factors in plasma samples from T1D patients with the goal of understanding the autoimmune process35 but SASP factors produced from islets have not been studied. Here, we provide the first evidence corroborating histological studies on accumulated senescent beta cells in human T1D by showing that the aggregate concentration of a panel of 7 islet SASP factors was higher in plasma samples from stages 2 and 3 T1D donors relative to autoantibody-negative controls. While it is known that SASP factors often overlap with general immune inflammatory cytokines, our SASP marker panel was selected on the basis of the increased expression and/or secretion from senescent human islets in culture and our results did not show evidence of an overall general inflammatory process increasing across all stages of T1D. For example, while CXCL1 and CXCL8 are considered to be both canonical SASP factors16 and general inflammatory chemokines36, neither factor was increased at any T1D stage relative to controls. Rather, we observed distinct profiles for individual SASP markers across T1D stages. GDF15, CXCL5, CCL20 and IGFBP4 each showed different relationships to disease stage, with some factors being higher in stage 1 or 2 and others only higher in stage 3 donors as compared with controls. There was a wide range of concentrations for each SASP factor tested in all donors, which is consistent with heterogeneous senescent cell accumulation and the heterogeneity in SASP among individuals and over time37,38. Why these 4 SASP markers were strongly correlated and showed these relationships with disease stage, rather than the other 3 markers on our panel, remains to be determined. Interestingly, we found no differences in SASP marker concentrations between pre-stage 1 single autoantibody-positive (early autoimmunity) donor samples and controls, which could suggest that accumulation of senescent cells may be limited at this stage. Indeed, a high frequency of individuals are positive for a single autoantibody in the general population and the majority will not progress to stage 3 T1D39. We also observed no significant differences between males and females in the cohort, suggesting against sex bias of SASP. Together these data suggest that our panel of four circulating SASP factors follows the clinical development of T1D.

Previous studies measuring circulating SASP factors were conducted in the context of aging and age-related diseases19–26. In contrast, to our knowledge our SASP marker panel is the first to establish an association between circulating SASP factors and disease progression in a disease not associated with aging that frequently affects pediatric individuals. SASP marker concentration was not associated with donor age in the TrialNet cohort of pediatric to young adult donors in our study (ages 9–35, n = 100 total). This could be explained by the fact that our donor cohort was much younger than studies of age-related diseases where samples were collected from middle-aged to geriatric adults (50–80 s)19,21,24 and we could not directly compare pediatric and geriatric donors in our TrialNet donor cohort. Although commonly considered a pediatric disease, T1D can affect individuals at any age and age at diagnosis of T1D has been linked to differences in pathology, possibly reflecting distinct endo-types33. Individuals diagnosed ≤ 7 years of age typical have more aggressive autoimmunity and lower residual beta cell function and those ≥ 12 years of age have less aggressive autoimmunity and more residual beta cell function34. Although we had only a few young pediatric donors in our cohort, we found no differences in SASP factor concentrations in those diagnosed < 12 versus those ≥ 12, suggesting that SASP may be independent of age at onset.

How senescence relates to human islet beta cell function remains unclear. Metabolic phenotyping of large numbers of isolated human islets from elderly donors showed reduced glucose-stimulated insulin secretion as compared to younger adult donors40. On the other hand, other studies indicate improved glucose responsiveness in senescent human beta cells expressing the major senescence regulator cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitor p16INK4A41,42. In our study, SASP factor concentrations were not significantly associated with extent of residual beta cell function as suggested by the C-peptide AUC in MMTTs on stage 3 T1D donors and similarly no association was found with the extent of beta cell dysfunction as assessed by the OGTT C-peptide AUC in stage 2 donors. However, it should be noted that all stage 2 donors are classified as such on the basis of abnormal OGTT values already and we observed higher levels of both the 7-marker and 4-marker SASP panel concentrations in these donors, consistent with higher SASP relative to controls in the context of beta cell dysfunction at stage 2. While we observed no evidence of a significant relationship in our study, the association between SASP factor concentration and the extent of beta cell dysfunction requires investigation in larger study cohorts because we had a limited number of donors here. We also did not find evidence of relationships between autoantibody titres to GAD65, ICA or Insulin and SASP levels in stage 1, 2 and 3 donors. This result is plausible given that autoantibody titres are associated with risk and rate of progression to stage 3 T1D43 and the predicted extent of residual beta cell function44. The discordance between autoantibody titre and SASP levels further supports the notion that our panel is not a readout of general immune inflammation during disease progression in T1D.

To confirm the validity of our SASP panel and identify novel candidate factors, we screened > 100 SASP factors by antibody arrays, taking advantage of rare pediatric donor islet preparations as a comparison to young and middle-aged adult donor islet preparations. Our results support that SASP factors vary depending on the islet donor and donor age, as we found that a pediatric islet sample had a very different SASP factor profile as compared to the two adult donor islet samples. Identification of novel candidate islet SASP factors using this screening approach opens avenues for expanding our plasma SASP panel and improving its sensitivity and specificity. Using matched normoglycemic control donor plasma samples and pancreas sections, we also established a relationship between our SASP panel and p21+ beta cells. p21 was found in beta cells at higher frequencies in T1D donors compared with controls15, in live islets from an adult T1D donor compared to a control donor32, and is elevated in response to DNA damage in human islet beta cells ex vivo30,45. Thus, we surmised it would be suitable marker for quantifying beta cells experiencing DNA damage and senescence activation. Importantly, we identified control donors that showed relatively high and low SASP panel concentrations, and while all the SASPLow donors showed the expected low frequencies of p21+ beta cells, the SASPHigh donors showed heterogeneity, with 3 donors (ages 70, 32 and 36) showing a high burden of p21+ beta cells but 2 donors (pediatric ages 15 and 7) showing a low burden of p21+ beta cells. SASPHigh donors showed wide variation in age (ages 7, 16 and 70) and a high burden of p21+ beta cells were found in donors in their 30 s (R488 and R489), supporting the notion that the SASP concentration among our 4-marker panel is not strictly tied to age. However, the lack of high burden of p21+ beta cells in the pediatric SASPHigh donors was unexpected and suggests that additional markers may be required to stratify pediatric SASPHigh donors from adult SASPHigh donors. While our study provides an important proof-of-concept, additional studies across a larger number of matched plasma and pancreas samples from both control and T1D donors using additional senescence markers will be required to comprehensively characterize the association between plasma SASP markers and senescent beta cells in pancreas.

Limitations of study

Our study had some limitations. First, as a cross-sectional cohort study, we did not have longitudinal data to determine if any of the stage 1 or 2 donors ultimately progressed to stage 3 clinically diagnosed T1D. Second, we cannot establish from our assays on plasma that the measured SASP factors were released by islets/beta cells. Although our SASP markers were carefully selected from studies showing their secretion from senescent human islets in culture models30,32 and we demonstrated this using antibody arrays on our DNA damage senescence islet culture model with pediatric and adult islets, senescence is propagated via paracrine mechanisms through SASP16. This means that SASP factors could be secreted a variety of different types of cells in addition to beta cells during the development of T1D. While we have found no evidence for senescence signatures in alpha cells in human T1D45 additional cell types cannot be ruled out. It will be important to investigate whether other cell types undergo senescence in T1D and thus contribute to circulating SASP factor levels. Furthermore, although our initial results from normoglycemic control donors suggest a relationship between plasma SASP factor levels and p21+ senescent beta cells, a larger-scale determination of the concordance between senescent beta cell burden and plasma SASP factor concentration will be needed in future studies. These studies could employ a variety of different senescence markers such as p16INK4A, SASP proteins, and prosurvival markers and could identify senescent beta cell marker signatures relevant to pediatric individuals. Such studies are now possible thanks to efforts from the Network for Pancreas Organ Donors with Diabetes (nPOD)46. We also recognize that our chemical DNA damage senescence model, although useful, is not capable of reflecting the full spectrum of heterogeneity in senescent human beta cell populations in vivo. Thus, additional SASP factors being released in vivo may not be reflected in our culture model or on our SASP marker panel. Finally, we had a relatively limited number of donor samples in this blinded cross-sectional cohort study. Further expansion of the number of samples and validation in larger cohorts, as well as longitudinal samples from the same individuals could reveal additional variability and identify relationships between SASP and metabolic or immunological parameters that were not identified here.

In conclusion, we found that circulating levels of a panel of human islet-associated SASP factors were higher in a cross-sectional cohort of pediatric and young adult donors across the natural history of T1D and a refined 4-marker panel was discriminative of stage 1, 2 and 3 of T1D. We corroborated the utility of these plasma SASP markers by showing a relationship to p21+ beta cells in the pancreas from control donors. These data provide additional evidence to support that senescent cells accumulate during the development and progression of T1D from presymptomatic to symptomatic stages. Monitoring circulating SASP factors could also be useful in understanding the natural history of type 2 diabetes and metabolic syndrome, which involve gradual senescent cell accumulation in pancreas, adipose tissue and liver47–49. These findings pave the way for further investigations in larger cohorts and longitudinal studies in the same individuals to determine how circulating SASP markers change over time in T1D.

Methods

Human ethics

Ethical review and approval for all studies using de-identified biorepository samples and human donor islets was carried out by the University of Manitoba Health Research Ethics Board. These studies were determined to be exempt from ethical review and were approved under protocol HS25660. TrialNet donor samples were obtained from the NIDDK biorepository under Materials Transfer Agreement 59431-1. All methods were carried out in accordance with all guidelines and regulations.

Human donor islet culture

Human donor islets for research were provided by the Alberta Diabetes Institute (ADI) IsletCore at the University of Alberta in Edmonton, Canada (http://www.bcell.org/adi-isletcore.html). Islet isolation was approved by the Human Research Ethics Board at the University of Alberta (Pro00013094). All donors’ families gave informed consent for the use of pancreatic tissue in research. Islets for research were procured with the assistance of the Human Organ Procurement and Exchange (HOPE) program, Trillium Gift of Life Network (TGLN), and other Canadian organ procurement organizations. Human pancreatic islets were also provided by the NIDDK-funded Integrated Islet Distribution Program (IIDP) at City of Hope, NIH Grant # U24DK098085. Islet donor information is available in Supplementary Table S1. Islets were recovered for 24 h at 37 °C and 5% CO2, in islet media (RPMI-1640, 10% FBS, 5.5 mM D-glucose, 1X antibiotic-antimycotic (Gibco) prior to experiments. Senescence was induced by DNA double-strand break damage in human islets using our previously established protocol30 with exposure to 50 µM bleomycin sulfate in islet media for 48 h, followed by culture in islet media lacking bleomycin for an additional 4 or 5 days (termed ‘drug washout’) to permit SASP activation. Media was replaced with serum-free media 24 h prior to conditioned media collection for antibody arrays, Luminex assays or ELISA and harvest of islets for RNA isolation or protein extraction for western blotting on days 5 or 6 post-bleomycin washout.

Human plasma sample procurement and donor criteria

De-identified human plasma donor samples were obtained from the TrialNet sample bank managed by NIDDK as part of an ancillary peer-reviewed study. The study comprised a cross-section of biobanked plasma donor samples representing the spectrum of T1D development and onset (n = 100 samples in total). These included: control/autoantibody-negative (n = 20), early autoimmunity (single autoantibody-positive, n = 20), stage 1 T1D (> 2 autoantibodies, n = 20), stage 2 T1D (≥ 2 autoantibodies and dysglycemia based on oral glucose tolerance test, n = 20) and stage 3 recent onset T1D (≥ 2 autoantibodies, and within 133–450 days from diagnosis, n = 20). All donors and/or their legal guardians provided informed consent for their samples to be used for research purposes by TrialNet and through peer-reviewed ancillary studies. As part of the TrialNet TN01 pathway to prevention, the stage 1 and 2 donors had at least one first- or second-degree relative with clinically diagnosed T1D. Eighty-four out of 100 of the donors in our cohort including autoantibody-negative, stage 1, 2 or 3 T1D had SNP genotyping done by TrialNet using ImmunoChip or T1DExomChip to infer first and second-degree familial relationships50. All genetic relationships were then confirmed at clinical testing sites. Two of the stage 1 donors were identified as full siblings, whereas all other samples were from unrelated individuals. Donor groups were matched for age and sex. Full donor group clinical parameters are shown in Table 1. Samples were blinded to investigator (sample group identity was not known until after assays were run and data submitted to TrialNet). Upon receipt, samples were aliquoted and stored at −80 °C until Luminex assays were run. After running assays, all data were submitted to TrialNet, which then provided sample unblinding information and all additional clinical metabolic data for each donor, including: donor age, sex, diabetes duration (stage 3 group only), OGTT (stage 2 donors), MMTT (stage 3 donors) and autoantibody titres for stages 1, 2 and 3. These data were used to perform statistical analyses, as described below. All human donor plasma samples were free of common blood-borne human infectious disease pathogens.

Matched human plasma and pancreas donor tissue

We obtained de-identified human donor plasma samples and matched formalin-fixed paraffin embedded pancreas sections from a cohort of normoglycemic control donors from the Alberta Diabetes Institute (ADI) biospecimen repository (n = 20). Donors ranged in age from 4 to 73 years-old and included both males and females. Donor information is found in Supplementary Table S2.

Luminex assays

Luminex assays were performed as previously described, except using a custom 7-analyte panel specific for previously identified human islet SASP factors: GDF15, CXCL1, CXCL5, CXCL8, CCL20, IGFBP4, and TNFRSF10C30,32 (R&D Systems). Approximately 50 µl of plasma sample, islet conditioned media sample or 1:3 serially diluted protein standard cocktail was mixed with 1X diluted magnetic antibody bead cocktail and incubated on a plate-shaker overnight at 4 °C. All samples were run in duplicate. Beads were washed according to manufacturer instructions and incubated with a biotinylated antibody cocktail, followed by washes in 1X wash buffer and a streptavidin-RPE conjugate. Beads were resuspended in 125 µl of 1X wash buffer and the plate was run on a Bio-Plex 200 instrument (Bio-Rad), with a double-discriminator gate at 9,000–20,000 and 100 beads scored per region. Concentrations of each analyte in picograms per mililiter were determined from the average of the technical replicates interpolated from 5-parameter log standard curves generated for each analyte using the Bio-Plex Manager software, standard edition v.1.0. Any analyte concentrations below the limit of detection on the standard curve (typically 2–10 pg/ml) were considered undetected and given a concentration value of 0.

ELISA

ELISA was performed for GDF15 using the Human quantikine GDF15 ELISA kit, as previously32. The concentration of GDF15 was determined from standard curves using recombinant protein standards.

Antibody arrays

Human islet or EndoC-βH5 cell conditioned media was interrogated on the human proteome profiler XL cytokine antibody array (R&D Systems) to screen levels of 111 different cytokines, growth factors, shed receptors and ligands in the media. Total protein concentrations in the media were determined using the BCA protein assay (ThermoFisher) and equal amounts of total protein (approximately 1 µg) diluted in a 1 mL volume of binding buffer were incubated with membranes overnight at 4 °C according to product instructions. After washing membranes and application of biotinylated sandwich antibody, streptavidin-HRP was applied and membrane signal detected by enhanced chemiluminescence and X-ray film. Several exposures were taken to ensure signals were in lineage range. Duplicate spot intensities were quantified using Image J software and plotted to compare treatment conditions.

RNA isolation and qRT-PCR

RNA was extracted from human islets according to previous methods, using the TRIzol RNA microprep kit (ZymoResearch). RNA was DNAse I treated and eluted into sterile water. cDNA was synthesized from 180 ng of total RNA for each sample using the LUNA kit (New England BioLabs) and qPCR was performed using the LUNA universal qPCR master mix with SYBR green reagent. Data were normalized using the delta-delta Ct method with cyclophilin A (PPIA) as the housekeeping gene. Primer sequences are found in Supplementary Table S3.

Western blotting

Western blot on human islets was performed as previously30. Islets were lysed in ice-cold radioimmunoprecipitation buffer (ThermoFisher) containing 1% protease inhibitor cocktail and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (ThermoFisher). Protein was quantified using BCA assay (ThermoFisher) and approximately 7 µg of total protein was subject to SDS-PAGE on 4–12% Bolt Bis-Tris plus gradient gels (ThermoFisher). Proteins were transferred to nitrocellulose using semi-dry transfer on an iBlot 2 (ThermoFisher). Membranes were blocked for 1 h at 23 °C with 5% non-fat milk in Tris-buffered saline containing 0.1% Tween-20 (TBS-T). Membranes were the probed with primary antibodies (1:500-1:1000 anti-p21, cell signaling technology Cat# 2947) or vinculin (1:2000, cell signaling technology, Cat# 13901). After washing with TBS-T three times for 5 min each, membranes were probed with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies and detected using SuperSignal West pico ECL reagents (ThermoFisher) and exposure to X-ray film. Multiple exposures were taken to ensure signals remained in the linear range.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

IHC was performed on human pancreas sections as previously described45. Formalin-fixed paraffin embedded sections from normoglycemic control donors that were designated from their matched plasma samples as SASPHigh (n = 5 donors) or SASPLow (n = 5 donors) were de-waxed with successive washes in xylene, then graded ethanol and finally distilled water. Heat-mediated antigen retrieval was performed in citrate buffer pH 6.0 (Vector Labs) with a lab pressure cooker (TintoRetriever, BioSB) using the high setting for 15 min. Sections were washed in distilled water and permeabilized with Tris buffered saline (TBS: 25 mM Tris pH 7.6, 150 mM NaCl) containing 0.1% Triton-X-100 for 10 min at 23 °C. Sections were then incubated with primary antibodies rabbit anti-p21 (1:200, Cell Signaling Technology, 2947) and guinea pig anti-Insulin (1:2, DAKO, IR00261-2) overnight at 4 °C in antibody diluent (DAKO). Slides were washed with TBS with 0.1% tween-20 three times for 5 min each, followed by incubation with Alexa488- and Alexa594 conjugated secondary antibodies and mounted using ProLong Diamond Antifade mountant media (ThermoFisher). Images were acquired on a Zeiss LSM-700 confocal microscope. Ten randomly selected islets were imaged per donor, blinded to donor group, and scored for p21+/Ins+ cells as a percentage of the total Ins+ cell population in each islet.

Statistical analysis

We assessed data normality/lognormality using common approaches, including: D’Agostino and Pearson, Anderson-Darling, Shapiro-Wilk and Kolmogorov-Smirnov tests. Parametric one-way ANOVAs with Dunnett’s post-hoc multiple testing adjustment were used for normally distributed data but when any sample group(s) were found to fail the normality tests we performed nonparametric statistical testing using Kruskal-Wallis test with Dunn’s post-hoc multiple testing adjustment to compare SASP factor concentrations in the healthy control/autoantibody-negative group with each of the other groups. Results were considered significant at P < 0.05. Linear regression statistics were used to determine relationships between: SASP factor concentrations and metabolic testing data provided by TrialNet, including oral glucose tolerance test and mixed-meal tolerance test C-peptide AUC, autoantibody titres or donor age. For two sample group comparisons, significant differences between were computed with two-tailed T-tests or Mann-Whitney tests, with results significant at P < 0.05. All statistical testing was done using GraphPad Prism version 10.5.
