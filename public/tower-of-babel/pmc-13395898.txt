Tail-rattling in rodents: more than a threat display
Authors: Raffaele d’Isa, Piotr Bebas, Michael H. Parsons, Korneliusz Kurek, Rafal Stryjek
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
One of the most peculiar behaviors of rodents is tail-rattling, a rapid vibration of the tail which can produce a rattling sound when performed against a hard surface. Tail-rattling is widespread across rodents, having been observed in 32 species from 19 genera, including Mus and Rattus. Rodent tail-rattling has been known from the eighteenth century, when it was described as part of the defensive behavior of the crested porcupine. From the 1940s, comparative psychologist John Scott systematically studied tail-rattling in laboratory mice, making it widely popular among laboratory researchers as one of the main components of the mouse agonistic ethogram. Currently, tail-rattling is interpreted almost exclusively as a threat display. However, historically, tail-rattling has been described in a great variety of different contexts, including intraspecies agonism, antipredator behavior, male-to-female courtship, female proceptive behavior, caressing by a human caregiver, social isolation, home-cage change, footshock-induced fear and decisional conflict. This wide range of contexts indicates that tail-rattling is not just a sign of aggressiveness, but can be associated with a set of emotions comprising anger, fear, distress, uncertainty, sexual arousal and physical pleasure. Notably, tail-rattling is expressed by rodents in emotional contexts of both negative and positive affective value. In the present article, firstly we provide a historical overview of rodent tail-rattling observations, starting from porcupines and ending with mice, covering a period from the 1770s to 2026. We then discuss what emotional state tail-rattling may express. Subsequently, we evaluate the possible functions of rodent tail-rattling. Additionally, we present our explanatory model of tail-rattling, the discharge model. Finally, we propose future directions for rodent tail-rattling research, including the employment of tail-rattling as a marker of animal welfare in laboratory and zoo rodents.

Introduction

Several species of animals move their tails in response to emotional arousal. For example, dogs exhibit tail-wagging (the repetitive movement of the tail across the midsagittal/median plane) in response to emotional activation (Leonetti et al. 2024). In particular, dogs display a right-side wagging bias, regulated by the left hemisphere of the brain, for stimuli endowed with a positive emotional valence (for instance, when they encounter a familiar person), while they exhibit a left-biased wagging, mediated by the right hemisphere of the brain, for stimuli associated with a negative emotional valence (for example, when they encounter an unfamiliar, aggressive dog) (Quaranta et al. 2007; Gobbo et al. 2021; Ren et al. 2022). Among tail movements, a most peculiar one is tail-rattling, which is a rapid vibration of the tail that, when waving against a hard object, produces a succession of short, sharp sounds.

Tail-rattling is widespread across rodents, having been observed in 32 species from 19 genera, including the 10 hystricids from the Hystrix and Atherurus genera (Schianini et al. 2025; the common porcupine Hystrix cristata (Ewer 1968; Roze 2012), the African brush-tailed porcupine Atherurus africanus (Booth 1960; Wolff 1969), the Cape crested porcupine Hystrix africaeaustralis (Happold and Happold 2023) and 7 other hystricid species), the dusky-footed woodrat Neotoma fuscipes (Donat 1933; Vestal 1938; Linsdale and Tevis 1951; Wallen 1982; Dial 1987; Kelly 1990; Hamm et al. 2002), the big-eared woodrat Neotoma macrotis (Donat 1933), the Allegheny woodrat Neotoma magister (Poole 1940), the white-throated woodrat Neotoma albigula (Boggs 1974), the bushy-tailed woodrat Neotoma cinerea (Escherich 1981), the northern pygmy mouse Baiomys taylor (Quadagno and Banks 1970), the European water vole Arvicola amphibius1 (Frank 1956; Stoddart 1970; Potapov et al. 1999; Evsikov et al. 2006; Nazarova et al. 2021), the North American water vole Microtus richardsoni2 (Jannett and Jannett 1974), the black rat Rattus rattus (Ewer 1971; Estep et al. 1978), the Norway rat Rattus norvegicus (Ducommun 2012), the greater cane rat Thryonomys swinderianus (Ewer 1969), the Southern African vlei rat Otomys irroratus (Davis 1972), the green acouchi Myoprocta pratti (Morris 1962), the greater Egyptian gerbil Gerbillus pyramidum (Happold 1968), the Pacific jumping mouse Zapus trinotatus (Svihla and Dowell Svihla 1933), the woodland jumping mouse Napaeozapus insignis (Sheldon 1938), the Persian desert mouse Meriones persicus (Eibl-Eibesfeldt 1951), the house mouse Mus musculus (Uhrich 1938; Scott 1942, 1951, 1966; Vessey 1967; St. John 1973; Haber and Simmel 1976a, 1976b; Nyby 2001), the wood mouse Apodemus sylvaticus (Oldfield 1968), Tullberg's soft-furred mouse Praomys tullbergi (Happold 1978), Molina’s grass mouse Akodon molinae (Yunes and Castro-Vázquez 1990) and the four-striped grass mouse Rhabdomys pumilio (Camacho 2016)3.

At present, rodent tail-rattling is interpreted almost exclusively as a threat display and in biomedical research it is commonly employed as a behavioral marker of aggressiveness. However, historically, tail-rattling has been described in a great variety of different contexts, indicating that tail-rattling is not just a sign of aggressiveness, but can be associated with a much wider set of emotions comprising anger, fear, distress, uncertainty, sexual arousal and physical pleasure. Notably, these historical observations show that tail-rattling is expressed by rodents in emotional contexts of both negative and positive affective value, and that in rodents tail-rattling is more than just a threat display. This raises the necessity to re-interpret rodent tail-rattling based on the consideration of broader sources of documentation, including the historical literature.

In the present article, firstly we provide an extensive historical review of rodent tail-rattling observations, starting from porcupines and ending with mice. Then, considering the complete range of contexts in which tail-rattling is expressed by rodents, we discuss what emotional state tail-rattling may express. Subsequently, we evaluate the possible functions of rodent tail-rattling. Moreover, we provide our explanatory theory of tail-rattling, the discharge model, and we discuss the potential adaptiveness and evolutionary origin of tail-rattling. Finally, we propose future directions for rodent tail-rattling research.

Historical background: rodent tail-rattling from porcupines to laboratory mice

From the eighteenth century to the 1920s

Rodent tail-rattling was first observed in porcupines, which in response to danger rapidly shake their tail generating a rattling sound by clashing their tail quills. By the eighteenth century, the notion that porcupines perform tail-rattling had already become established knowledge, to the point that in 1771 it was included in the first edition of the Encyclopaedia Britannica. In this work, in reference to the common crested porcupine, we can read: “These quills the animals can erect or let down at pleasure; when irritated, he beats the ground with his hind-feet, erects his quills, shakes his tail, and makes a considerable rattling noise with his quills” (Smellie et al. 1771). Shortly after, the first edition of the British Royal Encyclopaedia confirmed the same account of the porcupine defensive behavior (Hall 1788).

In the nineteenth century, naturalists provided more detailed descriptions of porcupine tail-rattling. For instance, George Robert Waterhouse (1810–1888), from the Natural History Department4 of the British Museum in London, in the volume Rodentia of his A Natural History of the Mammalia, outlined the defensive display of the crested porcupine as follows: “When irritated, to defend itself it erects its formidable quills; and the long crest on the back of the neck is at the same time elevated. It emits a succession of short grunts, and, moreover, it makes a rattling noise with the tuft of hollow quills at the end of its tail, by rapidly vibrating that organ. Further, it will strike the ground with its feet, as do the Hares” (Waterhouse 1848). Consistent with previous knowledge, Waterhouse specified how this behavior is produced by the animals in the context of irritation and self-defense.

The parson naturalist John George Wood (1827–1889), in the volume Mammalia of his monumental The Illustrated Natural History, confirmed: “the Porcupine is not at all an aggressive animal, and seldom, if ever, makes an unprovoked attack. But if irritated or wounded, it becomes at once a very unpleasant antagonist, as it spreads out its bristles widely, and rapidly backs upon its opponent” […] “and even the preliminary rustle of the quills with which a Porcupine generally prepares every attack, is sufficient to make an ordinary horse flee in terror. The rustling sound is produced by a number of hollow quills which grow upon the Porcupine's tail, and which, when that member is agitated, clash against each other” (Wood 1859).

Charles Darwin (1809–1882), the father of theory of biological evolution by natural selection, in the milestone work The Expression of the Emotions in Man and Animals, proposed that emotions have a biological root which is shared by humans and non-human animals, that both human and non-human animal emotions are expressed by observable somatic and behavioral manifestations, and that different animals show species-specific behavioral patterns of emotion expression (Darwin 1872). In this work, Darwin included porcupine tail-rattling in his list of emotion-expressing behaviors of non-human animals. In particular, he presented porcupine tail-rattling as an example of expression of emotion through non-vocal production of sound. Darwin relayed how he had the chance to observe the behavior of a porcupine of the London Zoo when one day, as an experiment in the presence of the zoo superintendent Abraham Dee Bartlett (1812–1897), a living snake was introduced into the porcupine’s compartment. Based on his direct observations, Darwin reported that porcupines “vibrate their tails when angered” and “when the tail is rapidly shaken, these hollow quills strike against each other and produce, as I heard in the presence of Mr. Bartlett, a peculiar continuous sound” (Darwin 1872). Interestingly, Darwin formulated an explicit hypothesis on the adaptive value of porcupine tail-rattling. According to Darwin, porcupines would emit this behavior “to give warning to their enemy”, so that they “would thus escape being attacked”.

In 1926, the Smithsonian’s National Zoological Park in Washington organized a zoological expedition in East Africa, funded by the American entrepreneur Percy Chrysler (1875–1940), the founder of the automotive industry Chrysler Corporation. The British naturalist Arthur Loveridge (1891–1980), from the Museum of Comparative Zoology of Harvard University, who took part in the expedition and had the opportunity to observe a group of porcupines in Africa for two months, wrote in his report of the expedition: “To see a small porcupine in a rage is an amusing sight; to the accompaniment of an explosive noise he stamps his feet, shakes and erects his quills, and sets those in his tail rattling, making himself altogether unpleasant to handle” (Loveridge 1928).

From the 1930s to the 1990s

In the 1930s, tail-rattling was discovered in rats as well. Fae Donat Wood5 (1906–1992) from the University of California in Berkeley (California, USA), one of the world’s first female ethologists, reported tail-rattling in both free-living and captive woodrats of the Neotoma genus (Donat 1933). In particular, she observed tail-rattling in the wild by the free-living big-eared woodrat6 and in the laboratory by the captive dusky-footed woodrat. In 1930, Donat Wood collected dusky-footed woodrats from the wild and started keeping them in the laboratory, where she could study their behavior for years (Donat 1933). Remarkably, Donat Wood was also the first to achieve successful Neotoma breeding in captivity. Indeed, previous attempts to obtain breeding in caged woodrats had all failed. However, by adopting a more ethological and ecological approach, Donat Wood managed to succeed in what had never been accomplished before. In March 1932, Donat Wood began hosting woodrats in a more natural environment recreated in the laboratory: instead of housing a male–female pair in a cage, she kept the pair free in a spacious room (length: 5.2 m; width: 3 m; height: 2.7 m) with four windows and abundant nesting material on the floor (branches, sticks, twigs, leaves, bracken fern, grass, cotton and wood shavings), which the woodrats used, as typical of this species, to build their house (Donat 1933). The result of the construction efforts of the woodrats was a complex wooden structure consisting of “an elaborate collection of twigs of all sizes interwoven”, which contained “a deep, roundish nest with a small entrance hole at the top” that was composed of soft nesting material like shredded cotton, bits of fern and grass (Donat 1933). The presence in the room of sticks of various lengths and calibers strongly motivated nest-building behaviors: “To see a rat drag a heavy branch over four feet [1.2 m] long and an inch [2.5 cm] thick across the room was quite surprising, but many such were used in constructing the nest”. As further elements of environmental enrichment, considering the natural attraction of woodrats for shiny objects, Donat Wood left on the floor also some scraps of silk and pieces of bright tin, which were readily collected and stowed away by the rats. While such seminatural environments are now recognized as greatly useful for laboratory investigations of rodent social behavior (Hernández-Arteaga and Ågmo 2023), in the 1930s this method was particularly ahead of its time. This pioneering method allowed to obtain for the first time successful breeding in captive Neotoma rats, with pups being born in May 1932. Interestingly, in this seminatural environment Donat Wood could observe tail-rattling and she saw it in both sexes.

A few years later, the American zoologist Elden Hall Vestal (1914–1998), also from the University of California in Berkeley, confirmed Donat Wood’s observation regarding tail-rattling in free-living dusky-footed woodrats: “When late in the afternoon of February 22, 1935, I peered into an entrance of an inhabited wood rat house I heard a rapid clatter, as though the animal were striking its tail on dead leaves. This noise lasted about 3 s. It was repeated several times, and once or twice was followed immediately by a peculiar whine, that began relatively low in pitch and ascended the scale quickly, then ceased abruptly. On the following day, when I approached the house, at 5:45 P.M., I heard the same clatter. This time the whine was not heard” (Vestal 1938). Vestal explained that the clatter “is produced by the rapid vibration of the distal third of the tail upon a substrate” and consequently named this behavior “tail clattering”. As for the conditions triggering tail-rattling, Vestal reported that a free-living individual typically performed it when he approached and stood next to its house, without touching it. If Vestal hit the house, the occupant rat would respond with foot pattering and other tail-rattling. In nature, Vestal observed tail-rattling in response to humans or a skunk approaching the rat houses. In these cases, the rats were inside the houses, and likely detected the approaching intruders through olfactory and auditory cues. Upon detection of an intruder, the rats remained inside the house and performed the rattling from within the structure.

Remarkably, such a tail-rattling is as typical of this species as its tendency to build houses. In fact, tail-rattling is produced by dusky-footed woodrats so reliably in response to den invasion, that, by the late 1930s, it had become one of the most dependable indicators of occupancy of woodrat houses (Vestal 1938). Research from our century confirmed that tail-rattling is a superior index of dusky-footed woodrat ecological abundance than the counts of physical traces of dusky-footed woodrat houses (Hamm et al. 2002). Tail-rattling by the dusky-footed woodrats can be elicited by producing near their house alerting sounds, for example by imitating the call of a natural predator (the northern spotted owl), imitating tail-rattling by tapping a stick against a hard target, or imitating the sounds of the movements of an intruding animal by moving the brushes nearby (Hamm et al. 2002).

Vestal additionally studied a group of captive dusky-footed woodrats, that tail-rattled “when disturbed by foreign odors, sounds, and objects” (Vestal 1938). For instance, if Vestal went next to their cages, standing motionless a few feet away, the rats would begin sniffing intensely and, at a certain point, one would start tail-rattling. Soon, another would take up the tail-rattling, and then yet another, leading to a situation of group tail-rattling. However, the rattling was not produced as a chorus, but as a sequence, and almost never more than one rat at a time tail-rattled. Overall, Vestal’s observations led him to consider tail-rattling as an alarm signal: “I infer that it is a sound made instinctively by an individual for the benefit of the group. As soon as an approaching danger is detected by a wood rat, it gives an alarm clatter, which is relayed by other wood rats within "clatter distance" to still others within hearing. The effect is to put all the animals on the alert, in readiness to escape, should the danger come their way”. Another situation that repeatedly triggered the tail-rattling of the group of rats was a domestic cat (Felis catus) prowling near their cages.

Shortly after Vestal, the zoologist William Bebb Richardson (1912–2006) confirmed experimentally that dusky-footed woodrats perform tail-rattling when exposed to predators. In 1940, at the Arizonian biological station of the Archbold Expeditions,7 Richardson carried out staged encounters in which wild-caught dusky-footed woodrats were presented with snakes in an experimental cage (Richardson 1942). Typically, the woodrats reacted with a prolonged foot thumping (“a rapid raising of the hind feet approximately 1/4 inch off the ground and the kicking down of them in unison with a thump”), which lasted several minutes and was occasionally accompanied by “vibration of the tail”. Interestingly, conspecifics that could not see the snake nor the subject woodrat, but just hear the foot thumping and tail-rattling of the subject woodrat, became “alert, and on the watch for danger whatever it might be”, suggesting that the subject’s behavior worked as alarm signaling.

From 1934, the naturalist and wildlife artist Earl Lincoln Poole (1891–1972), from the Reading Public Museum in Reading (Pennsylvania, USA), studied the behavior of the Allegheny rat in the laboratory (Poole 1936; 1940). Between 1936 and 1940, Poole kept several Allegheny woodrats in large glass-fronted cages, where a series of behaviors could be observed in a practical manner without the need to open the cage lid, hence leaving the animals undisturbed. Regarding the tail, Poole described how it was used in several contexts. For instance, if the animal is quiet, during walking and running the tail “is ordinarily held in a fully extended and horizontal, or slightly upturned, position”. On the other hand, “during excitement, anxiety, or surprise, it is carried high”. Curiously, when the animal is standing in upright position to feed or fight, the tail may be worn on the back as “a brace”. Regarding tail-rattling, Poole reported: “Frequently, during excitement, it is lashed from side to side as a warning” (Poole 1940). Being a skilled artist, Poole also provided in this work sketches of the position of the tail during typical behaviors. In the same decade, tail-rattling was also first described in mice. The first to report mouse tail-rattling were the zoologists Arthur Svihla (1902–1996) and Ruth Dowell Svihla8 (1897–1974), a couple of married researchers from the Charles R. Conner Museum of the Washington State University in Pullman (Washington, USA). In the summer of 1931, the Svihlas collected wild Pacific jumping mice from the Olympic Peninsula of the State of Washington and brought them to the laboratory to study their behavior, observing them for about half a year. The Svihlas relayed that, once in the laboratory, the jumping mice were characterized by “high-strung nervous dispositions” and “on several occasions these rodents were seen to make a drumming noise by rapidly vibrating their tails against some resonant body such as the bottom of a tin can trap or shreds of paper used for nesting purposes” (Svihla and Dowell Svihla 1933). However, with time, the jumping mice seemed to become more relaxed. In autumn, perhaps as a result of a seasonal effect, the jumping mice became “more and more listless and drowsy, often spending days curled up in their characteristic hibernating position, bent over so that the head is between the hind legs and the long tail curled completely over the head and body” (Svihla and Dowell Svihla 1933). Few years later, the zoologist Carolyn Sheldon Murdock (1911–1973), the daughter of the famous nature conservationist and naturalist Charles Alexander Sheldon (1867–1928), confirmed the presence of tail-rattling even in a jumping mouse belonging to a different genus, the woodland jumping mouse, which she studied in Vermont from 1934 to 1937 (Sheldon 1938).

In laboratory mice, tail-rattling was first reported by the zoologist Jacob Uhrich (1909–1991), researcher at the University of Chicago (Illinois, USA) and subsequently Professor and Head of the Department of Biology of Trinity University in San Antonio (Texas, USA). While at the Whitman Laboratory of Zoology of the University of Chicago, Uhrich performed a groundbreaking study on the establishment of social hierarchies in mice, employing 300 male and approximately 150 female albino mice9 subdivided into 85 social groups, which he monitored daily over the course of months, starting in March 1935 and ending in April 1937, with 3 groups (composed of 17 mice) being evaluated for over 220 days (Uhrich 1937, 1938). Uhrich found that the most common social order among males was “exclusive domination of the group” by a single individual, and that social hierarchies could remain stable up to several months. In this study, Uhrich reported for a male mouse that, when a new mouse was introduced into its cage, “it lashed his tail” and fought with the introduced mouse (Uhrich 1938). However, Uhrich was more interested in investigating the formation and maintenance of social ranks rather than in describing the ethogram of the mouse agonistic behavior, so this note regarding tail-rattling remained quite incidental.

In the 1940s, tail-rattling in mice started to be studied with a greater focus. The first to underline the importance of tail-rattling in mice was the comparative psychologist John Paul Scott (1909–2000), who, starting from July 1940, performed experiments on the genetics of mouse social behavior at the Roscoe B. Jackson Laboratory at Bay Harbor (Scott 1940, 1942). In order to evaluate intermale social interactions, Scott performed an intruder test, in which an unfamiliar albino mouse was placed in the home-cage of a C57 black mouse or a C3H agouti mouse, and the behavior of the resident mouse in response to the arrival of the stranger was scored. Scott compiled a list of the main behaviors that mice exhibit during a social interaction, among which he included tail-rattling, that he described as “Twitching tail (may be rattled against cage)” (Scott 1942). Interestingly, Scott found that there was an effect of the genetic background of the mice, with remarkable strain differences in agonistic behavior (Scott 1942). Compared to C57 mice, C3H mice were more aggressive, showing more feints (sham charges), hair-fluffing (piloerection), jerky breathing and attacks. On the other hand, C57 exhibited greater social investigation, performing a greater number of non-hostile contacts with the newcomer. Subsequently, in order to provide a more complex environment for the study of mouse agonistic behavior, Scott created a large apparatus composed of a central square arena which was connected through alleys to four rectangular rooms, which were also interconnected between them through other alleys (Scott 1943; 1944a). A schematic of the apparatus is reproduced in Fig. 2. Using this testing apparatus, Scott explored the effect of the space available for escape (a large structure with multiple escape-pens versus a small box) on defensive behavior during fights between male C57 mice (Scott 1944b). In this study, Scott classified the mouse defensive ethogram into five behavioral stages, one of which included tail-rattling: “(a) fighting back, (b) running away, (c) defensive posture (standing on hind legs and holding front legs out toward aggressor) plus tail rattling, (d) defensive posture, squeaking when attacked and (e) lying on back with feet in air, squeaking when attacked”. Scott found that spatial restriction changed the defensive reaction. In particular, running away was more common in large pens, while the defensive posture, including tail-rattling, was more common in the small boxes. Additionally, Scott discovered that tail-rattling could be modulated by training (Scott 1946). If a mouse was hosted in the large apparatus for a week and was then exposed to an unfamiliar mouse that was held by the tail and dangled in front of it, in the first phase, the resident mouse tail-rattled and then attacked. In a later classification of the mouse ethogram during agonistic interactions, Scott listed the reactions that he considered as “emotional”, which included tail-rattling and hair-fluffing. Of these behaviors, Scott regarded tail-rattling as the most evident and practical to use as behavioral marker in experimental studies: “Of the types of reactions which appear to be emotional, the tail rattling is the one which can be best identified and described in detail. In extreme cases the mouse stands on its hind legs and appears to shiver from the tip of its nose to the tip of its tail, producing a lateral wriggling motion of its tail which is so rapid that if it happens to be near the side of the box a distinct rattling noise may be produced. In less extreme cases the tail only moves, the rest of the body remaining quiet. During an actual fight the tail is switched around wildly but never moved in this distinct and regular fashion” (Scott 1947). Scott confirmed his hypothesis about tail-rattling being an emotional reaction through an experiment in which he discovered that tail-rattling increased in a situation of decisional conflict. Basically, mice were previously exposed to both a weak inhibitory training (free interaction with a skilled fighter, which led to social defeat) and a weak stimulatory training (exposure to mice that were dangled in front of the subject mice, a situation in which the subject mice could attack but without the risk of losing the confrontation). Subsequently, in a social interaction test with a mouse dangled in front of them, the tail-rattling behavior of the double-trained group was greatly increased compared to the control group which was exposed to the stimulatory training alone, with the number of tail-rattles of the experimental mice being eight times that of the control mice (Scott 1947).

Following the path traced by Scott (Scott 1940, 1942), the zoologists Benson Earl Ginsburg (1918–2016) and Warder Clyde Allee (1885–1955), from the Whitman Laboratory of the University of Chicago, studied the ethogram of mouse agonistic interactions, recognizing seven main stages of agonism and identifying tail-rattling as one of these fundamental stages. According to Ginsburg and Allee, the seven stages are: 1) “mutual sniffing”; 2) “independent activity”: exploration of the cage, accompanied by fluffing of the hair and increase of the breathing rate; 3) “lashing of the tail”; 4) a “direct attack by one member of the pair” or an “attempt to bite back when sniffed at”; 5) a “series of hard and rapid attacks and counterattacks”; 6) “Intermittent chasing and biting”; 7) “submission”: the “chased mouse rears on its hind legs, draws one foreleg close to the other stiffly, remains motionless, and squeals when touched” (Ginsburg and Allee 1942).

The zoologist Elizabeth Ann Beeman (1916–1979), from the same Whitman Laboratory of the University of Chicago, extensively studied mouse tail-rattling in the 1940s. From 1941 to 1944, Beeman performed experiments on the effect of thiamine (vitamin B1) on the winning of staged dyadic fights for the establishment of social dominance in male mice (Beeman and Allee 1945). Among the behavioral variables for the assessment of the aggressive behavior of the mice in these social encounters, Beeman considered tail-rattling, hair-fluffing, mincing, chasing, attacking and fighting. According to Beeman, mincing is when a mouse is “taking many small steps in rapid succession near, or in circles around, his opponent. Such action may precede, accompany, or follow tail-lashing” (Beeman and Allee 1945). Her results showed that thiamine appeared to affect the physical capacity to win a fight, but not the aggressiveness, as thiamine-deficient mice continued to tail-rattle, chase and attack. This allowed alpha mice with B1 avitaminosis to keep their rank and maintain submission from their opponents, even if the thiamine-deficient mice were so weak that they would not have been able to force submission from their opponents through an actual fight. In the summer of 1946, Beeman was hosted at the Jackson Laboratory in Bar Harbor, where, under the supervision of John Paul Scott, she explored the effects of testosterone on aggressive behaviors, including tail-rattling. Once back in Chicago, she continued carrying out experiments on the same topic and in 1947 she reported in a single publication the results that she had obtained from both the Jackson Laboratory and the Whitman Laboratory (Beeman 1947). During these experiments, Beeman found that in normal mice there were important strain differences in tail-rattling. In particular, albino mice tail-rattled more than they attacked, while C57 mice attacked more than they tail-rattled. Moreover, the absolute number of tail-rattling episodes was greater in albino mice than in C57 mice. Regarding the effects of the testicular hormone, Beeman found that testosterone is crucial for tail-rattling. In mice lacking testosterone due to orchiectomy, the number of tail-rattles, attacks and fights dropped to zero. On the other hand, when orchiectomized mice were subsequently implanted with subcutaneous pellets of testosterone propionate, they recovered their aggressive behaviors and they exhibited amounts of tail-rattles, attacks and fights that were even higher than those of the normal unoperated mice. Even in the case of the testosterone-treated mice, albino mice tail-rattled more than C57 mice.

In the 1950s, tail-rattling was discovered in a series of other species, namely jirds, shrews and voles. At the beginning of the decade, the topic of rodent tail-rattling was addressed by the Austrian ethologist Irenäus Eibl-Eibesfeldt (1928–2018) from the Institut für Vergleichende Verhaltensforschung (Institute for Comparative Behavioral Research) in Altenberg near Vienna, the institute founded by the Austrian ethologist Konrad Lorenz (1903–1989) in 1949. In 1950 Eibl-Eibesfeldt devoted an article to the behavior of the house mouse and he included tail-rattling in the agonistic ethogram of the animal (Eibl-Eibesfeldt 1950). In line with Scott’s interpretation of tail-rattling as an emotional reaction (Scott 1947), Eibl-Eibesfeldt considered this behavior as an “expression of excitement"10 (Eibl-Eibesfeldt 1951). The year after his investigation of the house mouse, Eibl-Eibesfeldt performed a behavioral study of the Persian jird, a rodent native of the Middle East (Eibl-Eibesfeldt 1951). By keeping a group of these animals under his care for several months, he had the opportunity to observe in detail their behavior, both in their living cage and in a terrarium. In his report, Eibl-Eibesfeldt outlined a complete ethogram of the jird, considering a great variety of behavioral domains (including foraging, food hoarding, eating, digging, burrowing, nest-building, courtship, mating, reproduction, parental care, birth-to-adulthood development, social grooming, play fighting and agonistic behavior) and describing for the first time tail-rattling in this species. When describing social play, Eibl-Eibesfeldt noted that the vigor of play fighting could often simulate that of real fighting, but the animals were showing self-limitation of their attacks and some essential characteristics of real fighting were missing. Interestingly, tail-rattling was present in real fighting but not in play fighting. Similarly, in social grooming the tail of the groomer could have been slightly erected, but it was not rattled. Eibl-Eibesfeldt relayed the following description of social grooming in jirds: “The animals also groom each other uninvitedly. If one desert mouse licks another on the head, the other, if in the mood, turns its head toward its partner, offering its chin for grooming, and closes its eyes. Sometimes it lies on its back”11. In the same decade of the 1950s, tail-rattling was discovered in shrews, small mammals which are morphologically similar to rodents, but phylogenetically distant from rodents, as they belong to the order Eulipotyphla, whose most recent shared ancestor with Rodentia dates back to the Cretaceous period12. Considering the genetic distance between shrews and rodents, this shared behavioral trait could represent an interesting case of convergent evolution. Shrew tail-rattling was first reported by the Australian zoologist William Peter Crowcroft (1922–1996), at that time at the University of Oxford (UK). Starting from 1949, Crowcroft collected wild shrews (three species: common shrew, pygmy shrew and water shrew) from the Oxford University Estate at Wytham and kept them in the laboratory for detailed investigation of their behavior, which led him to the publication in 1955 of a work extensively characterizing ethologically-relevant shrew behaviors such as nest-building, burrowing (including tunnel construction, burrow closing and surface burrowing), agonistic behavior, social bond formation, mating, weaning and vocalization (Crowcroft 1955). As an example of social bond formation, Crowcroft relayed a case in which when five wild-caught common shrews (Sorex araneus) were housed in an environment featuring six covered boxes as sleeping places, two of them occupied one of the boxes and together they defended it against the other four shrews, which occupied individually four of the remaining five boxes. The presence of an empty box suggests that the two mice were associating willingly. Notably, in the section devoted to the aggressive behavior of the common shrew, Crowcroft described: “When shrews are highly excited tail-threshing may occur. It is best observed when two shrews engage in a screaming contest from opposite ends of a tunnel, perhaps because rearing-up is not possible in that position. Tail threshing is prominent in the fighting of the house-mouse where it always occurs in situations of conflicting drives. In both shrews and mice, it is often seen when an animal is held beneath the cupped hand” (Crowcroft 1955).

Shortly after, the German zoologist Fritz Frank (1914–1988), from the Institut für Grünlandfragen der Biologischen Bundesanstalt für Land- und Forstwirtschaft (Institute for Greenland Research of the Biological Federal Agency for Agriculture and Forestry) in Oldenburg (Germany), carried out behavioral observations of wild-caught water voles maintained in the laboratory. In a study on the scent-marking behavior of the European water vole, Frank described how in both an agonistic interaction with another male or an encounter with a female, the male water voles exhibit tail-rattling: “During combative confrontations with conspecifics, as well as when the male makes contact with a new female, the following can be observed: the aggressive part (during mating, i.e., the male) becomes visibly excited, the hair on his head and neck erects, and the tail is swung horizontally back and forth up to 45° to each side, so violently that it sweeps soil and peat bedding aside and makes banging sounds on the tin floor. At the same time, the flank glands begin to ooze profusely, and then the hind feet are alternately moved along the lateral glands with rapid scratching movements, similar to flank grooming, so that the inward-turned soles of the feet are moistened with glandular secretion”13 (Frank 1956). Frank noted that the flank glands of the water voles, which are arranged close to the hind legs in a horizontal row up to 2 cm long, “always become more active when the animal is excited”. He further specified that the activation of the flank glands (in response to an unknown male conspecific, a new potential sexual partner or even an unfamiliar human catching the vole) can be easily recognized by the sudden wetness and stickiness of the fur surrounding the flank glands. Interestingly, according to Frank’s observations, tail-rattling in water voles is performed in different contexts (agonism and courtship), but in both cases it is associated with secretion from the flank glands. This suggests that, in water voles, tail-rattling may have the additional function of increasing the efficiency of scent-marking. The basic process of scent-marking in water voles features repeated scratching of the flank glands with a hind paw and then multiple stomps of the paw on the ground to mark it (a behavior now known as scent-marking by foot-drumming or drum-marking). Tail-rattling during this process may contribute to spread the glandular secretion on the surrounding objects.

Still in the 1950s, Scott released, together with the Polish-American comparative psychologist Emil Fredericson (1922–1980), a milestone work entitled The Causes of Fighting in Mice and Rats (Scott and Fredericson 1951), which has become extremely influential and highly cited, with at present over 650 scientific citations14. Remarkably, Scott is the scientist that coined the phrase “agonistic behavior”, which is currently the standard terminology to refer to the behavioral domain of offensive and defensive social interactions. Scott first employed the phrase in a work published in 1950 with his research assistant Mary-‘Vesta Marston (1924–2024), one of the first female comparative psychologists, who three decades later would marry Scott and take the name of Mary-‘Vesta Marston-Scott (d’Isa 2025b). However, in this work (Scott and Marston 1950), the phrase agonistic behavior was just mentioned, without a definition. The following year, in the paper with Fredericson, Scott provided the first definition of agonistic behavior: a set of “patterns of behavior which may provide some measure of adjustment when two organisms come into conflict”, which includes pre-fighting behaviors, attack, counterattack, submission and escape (Scott and Fredericson 1951). Importantly, in this seminal 1951 work, tail-rattling is listed as one of the three pre-fighting behaviors (the other two being hair-fluffing and mincing). Tail-rattling is defined as follows: “The mouse rapidly twitches its tail from side to side, so that it takes on a wavy appearance. If the tail happens to strike against some hard object a loud rattling sound is produced. Both this and hair-fluffing appear to be involuntary behavior patterns, which other mice may learn to observe as warning signals, and occur in situations in which the mouse is hesitating between attack and escape reactions” (Scott and Fredericson 1951). An interpretation of the behavior is presented. Indeed, based on his previous findings in mice exposed to conflictual training (Scott 1947), Scott regarded that tail-rattling and hair-fluffing are expressed in situations of uncertainty due to decisional conflict, being “part of a mechanism which comes into effect when the animal is equally stimulated to escape and attack” (Scott and Fredericson 1951). Interestingly, Scott and Fredericson noted that the agonistic behavior of laboratory mice and laboratory rats was very similar, except that tail-rattling appeared to be absent in laboratory rats, as it had never been reported for these animals. Notably, in this work, the first graphical representation of tail-rattling is provided, in a diagram schematizing the environmental factors influencing it, which include social factors, biologically-determined instinctual reactions to possible predators or preys, and physical factors (such as space). In the 1960s, the Dutch behavioral geneticist Johannes Henricus Felix van Abeelen15 (1936–1998), from the Catholic University of Nijmegen (Netherlands), performed a behavioral characterization of different mouse genetic lines adopting ethological parameters, and tail-rattling was one of these parameters (van Abeelen 1964a, 1964b, 1965, 1966a, 1966b). In particular, according to van Abeelen, tail-rattling was part of the agonistic ethogram of male-male mouse interactions, which included also fixing (staring at the opponent), nosing, boxing, kicking, wrestling, biting, chasing, adopting a submissive posture and fleeing (van Abeelen, 1964a). Interestingly, van Abeelen reported that mice can display tail-rattling even in a non-social situation (van Abeelen 1965). When single male mice were placed for 15 min in an unfamiliar cage, at first they kept close to the walls, and they started wandering across the whole cage only after a few minutes. Moreover, in the novel cage, tail-rattling and hair-fluffing could be observed, even if the mouse was alone, suggesting a neophobic reaction to the unfamiliar environment.

In 1966, Scott published Agonistic Behavior of Mice and Rats: A Review, a follow-up to his 1951 paper. This article was also extraordinarily impactful and obtained over 570 citations16. In this work, Scott presented a table with the agonistic behaviors of the house mouse and the Norway rat. Again, tail-rattling was included in the agonistic ethogram of the mouse but not in that of the rat. Scott underlined how, at that time, tail-rattling in the Norway rat still had never been reported17. According to Scott, tail-rattling “is commonly heard and seen in mice which are hesitating before an attack” and “suggests a signal conveying a warning or threat, similar to growling in carnivores, but there is no evidence that other mice react to it in this way”. Basically, in this work Scott confirmed his previous hypothesis that mouse tail-rattling is a threat display, but in addition he noted that no empirical evidence had yet been found to confirm the idea that tail-rattling may actually reduce the likelihood of an attack from the receiver of the signal. Importantly, together with Scott’s previous review, this work strongly contributed to spreading knowledge of rodent tail-rattling in the scientific community. In fact, of all the works featuring rodent tail-rattling ever published, Scott’s 1951 and 1966 articles are the two most cited of all time.

In the 1970s, the scientific interest towards tail-rattling grew, to the point that the first scientific articles mentioning tail-rattling directly in their title were published. Six of such articles were published in this decade. The first article, released in 1972, used in the title the phrase “tail rattle” and was rapidly followed by another five articles which employed the phrase “tail rattling”.

The first of these six articles was by the comparative psychologist Morris Karl Morgret (1938–1997), from Washington State University in Pullman (Washington, USA). Morgret started his article by providing a simple and clear definition of the behavior: “Tail rattling (rapid shaking of the tail) is an unusual behavior associated with fighting in mice. If the tail happens to strike some object such as the side of the cage, a rattling sound is heard” (Morgret 1972). He then considered how little was known about the actual function of tail-rattling and underlined the necessity to investigate this issue more deeply.

The second article, and the first to use the phrase “tail rattling” in its title, was by the zoologist Richard Dennis St. John (1943–1981), from the Institute for Behavioral Genetics of the University of Colorado in Boulder (Colorado, USA), who in 1973 brought rodent tail-rattling on the pages of the prestigious journal Nature (St. John 1973). In this work, St. John designed a protocol capable of evoking tail-rattling in mice in the absence of exposure to a second animal (a non-social context). Basically, St. John found that if the mice were exposed to an unescapable sequence of electric shocks, they exhibited tail-rattling. The animals were placed in a box with a metal grid floor capable of releasing electric shocks, and they were exposed to four consecutive phases: a series of 10 low-intensity shocks every 20 s (phase I), a 120 s shock-free recovery period (phase II), a series of 10 higher-intensity shocks (phase III) and a final 120 s shock-free period (phase IV). Remarkably, tail-rattling was seen in all four phases. C57BL6 mice, for instance, showed the highest amount of tail-rattling in phase II, which was shock-free. This suggests that tail-rattling was not elicited by the actual shock, but rather by fear towards future possible shocks, associated with the impossibility to leave the box. Additionally, St. John found that shock-induced tail-rattling differed across five genetic strains of mice: C57BL/6, DBA/2, C3H/2, A and BALB/c. In phase I, BALB/c mice exhibited the highest level of tail-rattling of all strains, while C57BL/6 mice showed the lowest. The temporal distribution of tail-rattling also differed across strains. For example, BALB/c mice showed 98% of their total tail-rattling in phase I, 1% in phase II, 1% in phase III and 0% in phase IV. On the other hand, C57BL/6 mice showed a more uniform distribution of tail-rattling across phases: 29% in phase I, 35% in phase II, 23% in phase III and 13% in phase IV. The total tail-rattling across the four phases was higher for C57BL/6 mice than for BALB/c mice.

The same St. John also published the last article of this series (St. John 1978). In this study, St. John confirmed that the total shock-induced tail-rattling of BALB/c mice (sum of the four phases) was less than half than the tail-rattling of the C57BL/6 mice. At that time, it had been found that albino mice were more anxious, an effect possibly mediated by increased photophobia (hypersensitivity to light) due to the lack of the protective pigmentation in the eye (DeFries et al. 1966; van Abeelen and Kroes 1967; Owen et al. 1970), and a link between albinism and behavior had been hypothesized (Fuller 1967; DeFries 1969). To address this hypothesis, St. John decided to investigate whether the reduced tail-rattling was directly linked with albinism (St. John 1978). However, St. John managed to show that the observed reduction of tail-rattling actually was not dependent on albinism itself. The spontaneous occurrence of an albino mutation in an individual of the C57BL/6 line allowed for the creation of a new C57 line carrying the albino mutation (the C57BL/6J-cJ line). By employing crosses with this C57BL/6J-cJ line, St. John demonstrated that the albino condition itself could not be responsible for the decrease of tail-rattling seen in the albino BALB/c line. Indeed, through crossings, St. John was able to create albino mutants that tail-rattled three times as much as the pigmented C57BL/6 mice. Overall, the results of the behavioral testing of different crosses led St. John to hypothesize that the reduced propensity for tail-rattling in the BALB/c mice did not depend on the albino locus of the BALB/c line, but on a locus in linkage with the albinism locus of this line.

The third article was by the Czech neuropsychopharmacologist Miloslav Krsiak (1939–2016), from the Institute of Pharmacology of the Czechoslovak Academy of Sciences in Praha (Czechoslovakia18), who tested the effect of anxiolytic drugs on tail-rattling (Krsiak 1975). Krsiak found that low doses of diazepam, chlorpromazine and chlordiazepoxide decreased tail-rattling in male albino mice without reducing other aggressive behaviors. On the other hand, a high dose of imipramine was required to decrease tail-rattling, and this dose also reduced the other aggressive behaviors. Based on the fact that the sensitivity of tail-rattling to the relaxing effects of the drugs was higher than that of the other behavioral parameters, Krsiak proposed tail-rattling inhibition in aggressive mice as a new index to evaluate the tranquilizing activity of drugs.

Finally, the fourth and fifth articles were by the comparative psychologist Edward Clemens Simmel (1932–2006), from the Department of Psychology of Miami University in Oxford (Ohio, USA), and his pupil Sonja Haber (1951-living), who investigated the temporal relationship between tail-rattling and agonistic behavior in mice, in order to understand if this relationship was causal or simply coincidental. Haber and Simmel found that tail-rattling was emitted not only before fighting, but also after it. Moreover, the amount of tail-rattling performed in response to no behavior was much higher than that performed in response to any other behavioral category (social exploration, tail-rattling or fighting). This led them to challenge the idea that tail-rattling is a threat signal. Haber and Simmel presented their results in brief (Haber and Simmel 1976a) and more extensively (Haber and Simmel 1976b), in both cases employing the phrase “tail rattling” in the title. Notably, the previous year, Haber had devoted to this topic her entire thesis, which also contained “tail rattling” in the title (Haber 1975).

Between the 1960s and the 1970s, ethological investigations of non-model rodents expanded the list of known tail-rattling species, as well as the known contexts of the display of this behavior. Edwin Melvin Banks (1926–1985), Professor of Ecology, Ethology and Evolution at the University of Illinois at Urbana-Champaign (Illinois, USA), and his doctoral student David Quadagno (1941-living) were the first to relay tail-rattling in pygmy mice (Quadagno and Banks 1970). In the summer of 1967, Quadagno and Banks housed in the laboratory wild northern pygmy mice collected in Cochise County (Arizona, USA), and bred them to study their reproduction physiology and their behavior. Quadagno and Banks performed cross-fostering experiments, in which pygmy mice were raised by house mice, and then tested for their social reactions in staged encounters with conspecifics or heterospecifics. Interestingly, in the social behavior tests, it was seen that the pygmy mice reacted more sociably towards the foster species than towards their own. In particular, in dyadic encounters with conspecifics, in comparison to encounters with house mice, cross-fostered pygmy mice approached less, allogroomed less, escaped more and fought more. Moreover, cross-fostered pygmy mice tail-rattled at pygmy mice but not at house mice. Control in-fostered pygmy mice (raised by their biological mother) showed the opposite behavioral pattern, exhibiting less sociability and more aggressive behaviors in encounters with house mice than in encounters with pygmy mice. Moreover, in-fostered pygmy mice tail-rattled at house mice but not at pygmy mice. Basically, both cross-fostered and in-fostered pygmy mice displayed tail-rattling in response to the species that was unfamiliar to them.

The American zoologist Richard Marcy Davis, during his period of research in South Africa at the University of Pretoria (1970–1973), was the first to perform an extensive behavioral study of the Southern African vlei rat,19 featuring both field and laboratory observations (Davis 1972, 1973). Davis relayed that, when he staged social encounters between same-sex adult individuals, in the period of the initial contact, the rats typically showed tail-rattling: during the “periods of initial approach both animals come nose to nose in an elongate posture with much retreating, reapproaching, and tail shivering” (Davis 1972). At a certain point of the encounter, one rat generally approached aggressively the other and a fight occurred. At the end of the fight, one rat could be considered the dominant (the winner) and the other rat the submissive (which either fled or took the appeasement posture, consisting in lying on the back exposing the vulnerable abdomen). Interestingly, if one rat flees, tail-rattling may continue in this stage, but it is displayed by the dominant rat rather than the submissive. For this reason, Davis classified tail-rattling as an offensive threat, as opposed to defensive threats (such as the upright posture and the production of vocal chits), which are generally seen in the submissive individuals. The typical aggressive display of the dominant vlei rat features: a) approach in a low, elongate posture; b) with ears flattened against the head; c) while shivering the tail; d) then occasionally exposing a side to the opponent (the lateral offensive threat); e) finally, launching an attack. Nevertheless, Davis observed tail-rattling also in intersexual encounters (Davis 1972). In these encounters, the behavior was performed by the male, when cautiously approaching the female: “The male assumes the elongate posture when approaching the female and shows […] tail shivering. The female rises upright, "chits", and, if the male approaches too close, attempts to ward him off with the forelimbs”. Typically, the male persisted until he obtained the female’s tolerance, after which the two started what Davis defined as a “platonic relationship”, that is mere resting and sleeping huddled together, without mating. Davis never observed sexual activity in the captive vlei rats and no breeding occurred. Davis took into consideration the possibility that the male’s tail-rattling to the female could have served the function of a courtship display, but since the vlei rats did not exhibit copulation under laboratory conditions, he stated that any association between tail-rattling and mating remained unknown. Ontogenetically, tail-rattling appeared as early as postnatal day 9, and by postnatal day 12 it was fully coordinated (Davis 1972, 1973).

In the early 1970s, tail-rattling was reported for the first time for the black rat, also known as roof rat, by the British zoologist Dennis William Ewer (1913–2009), Professor at the University of Ghana in Accra (Ghana) from 1963. Although a British scientist in Africa, Ewer surely was not an outsider in this institution, as at that time almost the totality of the Department of Zoology of the University was British. For instance, in 1960, the British expatriates occupied over 80% of the university’s posts in Zoology (Baker 2013). This made it possible to bring much of the British zoological knowledge and ethological mindset to the University of Ghana. At that time, black rats were very common in Accra. Exploiting this opportunity, starting from 1967, Ewer performed a pioneering study of the behavior of the black rat by observing, from behind observation screens, free-living individuals that were attracted to the courtyard of the university through food baits (Ewer 1971). Ewer watched the rats almost every evening from August 1967 to June 1968, and again from September 1968 to June 1969, publishing the results of his observations in 1971. Notably, Ewer noticed tail-rattling behavior in female free-living black rats during mating. According to Ewer, when a female black rat becomes receptive, it runs a short distance and then stops. The male follows and mounts the female. After the first copulation episode, the “female again runs off and pauses. If the male does not follow, she may stand for a moment with her tail vibrating rapidly, then run on and again pause, or she may herself take the initiative: moving over to the male, she pushes against him and she may groom his head briefly or sniff his genitalia before running off and once more pausing”. It is interesting to note that, in these episodes, tail-rattling was performed by the female black rats not in an aversive situation but in an appetitive one, as can be understood by the fact that the females were not avoiding the males, but rather approaching and grooming them. Therefore, differently from the case of tail-rattling during agonistic interactions, in this context tail-rattling appears to be an expression of sexual arousal rather than of aggressiveness.

In the late 1970s, black rat tail-rattling was confirmed in laboratory-reared individuals by the team of the American comparative psychologist Donald Allen Dewsbury (1939–2025) at the Department of Psychology of the University of Florida in Gainesville (Florida, USA). The results of this study were presented at first by Daniel Quen Estep in his 145-pages PhD thesis (Estep 1975) and subsequently by the team in a scientific article (Estep et al. 1978). Notably, this study is the first quantitative description of the mating behavior of the black rat. The studied subjects were the laboratory-born offspring of wild black rats caught in Gainesville. Consistently with what reported by Ewer for free-living black rats, Dewsbury’s team observed that female black rats, prior to taking the lordotic posture which led to mounting by the male, frequently exhibited tail-rattling. In particular, the receptive females “often displayed a series of behavioral patterns including rapid ear wiggling, tail rattling, and approaches to the male, followed by rapid darting and stopping a few centimeters away. These patterns of behavior were observed on every test in which the females displayed lordosis when mounted and on no other tests” (Estep et al. 1978). In his PhD thesis, Estep relayed even more explicitly that these pre-lordosis behaviors (including tail-rattling) “were observed on every test in which the females were judged behaviorally receptive and were not observed in the few tests where the females were not behaviorally receptive” (Estep 1975). Basically, female tail-rattling was selectively associated with receptiveness and acceptance of the male. The females accepting the males (i.e., taking the lordosis posture) tail-rattled, while the females rejecting the males (i.e., not responding with the lordosis posture) did not. Hence, these observations strongly suggest that tail-rattling was performed by the female black rats as a proceptive behavior. Curiously, while darting and ear-wiggling are behaviors that can also be observed in the proceptive displays of female Norway rats, tail-rattling appears to be absent in the mating ethogram of the female Norway rat (Zipse et al. 2000).

Interestingly, rodent tail-rattling during mating is not limited only to the female. Indeed, in the 1960s, tail-rattling by the male courting the female was reported by the British ethologist Desmond Morris (1928–2026) for the green acouchi, a rodent from tropical South America (Morris 1962), soon followed by the American zoologist Devra Gail Kleiman (1942–2010) (Kleiman 1971). The tail of the green acouchi is very short, so when the animal is standing on its four limbs the tail does not touch the ground. During courtship, the male shows two typical tail movements: a tail-down and a tail-up vibration. The tail-down vibration is basically the green acouchi’s tail-rattling, only that the tail is too short to reach the ground, so it is unlikely to hit a substrate and create a sound. However, during this tail vibration, like in the tail-rattling of the rodents with longer tails, the tail is not erected. Additionally, the green acouchi can display a tail-up vibration, which is an even more conspicuous courtship signal performed with the tail erected. Moreover, mating-related tail-rattling was reported by Ewer for the greater cane rat, a Sub-Saharan African rodent also known as the grass cutter (Ewer 1969). In this species (in which the tail is long and thick), the male does not erect the tail, but directs it towards the female and lashes it from side to side. In the 1970s, female-induced tail-rattling was described in male dusky-footed woodrats. In 1976, the comparative psychologist Kurt Wallen (1944-living), from the University of California in Berkeley, caught dusky-footed woodrats and tested them in the laboratory in dyadic same-sex or opposite-sex social encounters (female-female, male-male and male–female). Wallen presented the results of these behavioral tests the following year in his PhD thesis (Wallen 1977) and published the associated article, which bears a title almost identical to that of his thesis, after another 5 years (Wallen 1982). In the opposite-sex social encounters, Wallen observed tail-rattling by the males exposed to the females. The tail-rattling was accompanied by an increase of foot-thumping behavior, as well as of defecation and urine marking, which led Wallen to interpret this tail-rattling by the males as a sign of “autonomic arousal” (Wallen 1982). Also in the 1970s, the American zoologist Kenneth Paul Dial (1953-living), at that time at California State University in Long Beach (California, USA), studied in the laboratory the copulatory behavior of the dusky-footed woodrat. The results of his observations were presented at first in his thesis (Dial 1978) and subsequently in the associated article, which was released only after almost 10 years (Dial 1987). Dial found that, in 23 of 24 cases, the mating behavior was initiated by the males. In particular, the males approached the females and tail-rattled. Subsequent to tail-rattling by the male, the female usually moved close and turned tail-first to the male. Then the female took the lordosis posture and copulation was observed.

From the 1980s, almost all research investigating rodent tail-rattling was performed on mice. A notable exception is tail-rattling in laboratory voles. In 1982 the Russian zoologist Galina Grigoryevna Nazarova (1956-living) established a laboratory colony of water voles at the Laboratory of Population Ecology and Animal Genetics of the Biological Institute of the Siberian Branch of the Academy of Sciences20 in Novosibirsk (Federal District of Siberia, Russia). A history of the water vole laboratory colony and of its employments in behavioral studies has been recently summarized by the founder of the colony herself (Nazarova 2026). In 1978 the Russian zoologist and animal geneticist Vadim Ivanovich Evsikov (1935–2016) was named Director of the Institute of Biology and the following year he founded the Laboratory of Population Ecology and Animal Genetics. In 1980 Nazarova joined the Laboratory and in 1982 Evsikov, who was not only the Director of the Institute but also the Head of the Laboratory, assigned to Nazarova the task of obtaining successful reproduction of voles in a vivarium and creating a colony of laboratory-reared voles, with the aim of performing laboratory research to understand the physiological and ethological mechanisms of water vole reproduction in nature. While during the first attempts the wild-caught water voles did not reproduce in captivity, after two years Nazarova managed to succeed and founded the first laboratory-bred colony of water voles in 1984. In 2009, after that Evsikov retired, Nazarova became the new Head of the Laboratory, which was renamed into Laboratory of Structure and Dynamics of Animal Populations. From the late 1990s, Nazarova, Evsikov and colleagues reported tail-rattling in laboratory water voles (Potapov et al. 1999; Evsikov et al. 2006; Nazarova et al. 2021), always in the context of intermale agonistic behavior and in particular performed by a dominant male towards a subordinate male (Potapov et al. 1999). The group hence interpreted this behavior as a threat display. Interestingly, the team found that the frequency of threat behaviors (tail-rattling and teeth chattering) was negatively correlated with the serum testosterone level, while being uncorrelated with body mass (Nazarova et al. 2021). Moreover, while direct aggression was correlated with the size of the litter in which the male vole was born and weaned (which determined the early rearing environment), no correlation was found between litter size and threat behaviors (Nazarova et al. 2021).

In the 1990s, the Finnish comparative psychologist Kenneth Sandnabba, from the Åbo Akademi University in Turku (Finland), studied the tail-rattling of female mice in two new contexts, both ethologically relevant: maternal aggression and predatory behavior. In a first study, Sandnabba reported tail-rattling by female mice towards a male intruder during the post-partum lactation period, in which the female was providing maternal care to newborn pups (Sandnabba 1993a). In a subsequent study, Sandnabba reported tail-rattling during predatory behavior in lactating female mice (Sandnabba 1995a). While mice are commonly seen as prey animals, they are also skilled predators of insects such as crickets (d’Isa 2025c). In the 1960s, the American comparative psychologist Karla Thomas Butler, from California State University, had developed a behavioral test to assess cricket-killing behavior in the laboratory (Thomas 1969; Butler 1973). Sandnabba employed the cricket-killing test to evaluate the predatory behavior of females from two genetic strains of Swiss albino mice, the Turku Aggressive (TA) and the Turku Non-Aggressive (TNA) strains, which had been selected for, respectively, high or low levels of isolation-induced intermale aggression (Sandnabba 1995a). By presenting lactating females with a cricket, Sandnabba found that TA females tail-rattled before chasing, attacking and consuming the cricket. Moreover, TA females, compared to TNA females, spent more time tail-rattling, chasing, attacking and consuming the cricket, and had a shorter latency to attack it. For intraspecies attacks, Sandnabba found that tail-rattling preceded an attack both in the case of maternal aggression in TA females (Sandnabba 1993a) and of intermale agonistic behavior in TA males (Sandnabba 1986). On the other hand, for interspecies attacks (predation on crickets), Sandnabba found that the predatory attacks of the TA females were preceded by tail-rattling (Sandnabba 1995a), while the predatory attacks of the TA males were not (Sandnabba 1993b, 1995b). Sandnabba hypothesized that this difference could have been due to the fact that the females were perceiving the cricket not simply as a prey but as a threat to the pups (Sandnabba 1995a).

In the same 1990s, the Italian ethologist Enrico Alleva (1953-living), Director of the Behavioural Pathophysiology Section of the Istituto Superiore di Sanità (the Italian National Institute of Health) in Rome (Italy), and his colleagues investigated mouse tail-rattling in a great number of studies (Maestripieri and Alleva 1990a, 1990b; Maestripieri et al. 1990; Aloe et al. 1990; Laviola et al. 1991; Bigi et al. 1992, 1993, 1994; Corridi et al. 1993; Petruzzi et al. 1995; Alleva et al. 1997; Fiore et al. 1998; Terranova et al. 1998; Rondinini et al. 1999). Among these studies, Alleva and his team explored how previous experiences can modulate tail-rattling responses. In particular, the team evaluated in dyadic encounters the social behavior of pairs of male albino Swiss mice that had previously been housed individually (for 24 days) and had either been made familiar with the odor of each other or not (Corridi et al. 1993). In the familiarization group, each mouse of the pair received daily in its home-cage bedding from the cage of the other mouse of the pair. On day 25, the social behavior test was carried out by placing together the two mice in a test cage that was not the home-cage of either of the two. Surprisingly, in the social behavior test, tail-rattling was significantly increased in the familiarization group compared to the no familiarization group. An effect of previous social encounter on tail-rattling was also seen. Indeed, when the social test was repeated daily across other 4 consecutive days, the tail-rattling of both groups significantly decreased compared to the first day, but the familiarization group continued to tail-rattle more than the no familiarization group.

At the end of the 1990s, Alleva’s team investigated the ontogeny of tail-rattling in mice (Terranova et al. 1998). Pairs of male CD-1 albino mice, initially unfamiliar, were placed together for 30-min social tests every 3rd day from postnatal day 23 to 47. Two housing conditions were employed: social housing or individual housing. Based on the fighting outcome in the social test, the mice were classified as dominant or subordinate. The results showed that dominant mice tail-rattled more than subordinates. The highest frequency of tail-rattling was seen in dominant individually-housed mice (which likely developed the highest territorial attitude), followed by the dominant socially-housed mice. In subordinate individually-housed mice tail-rattling was very little, while in subordinate socially-housed mice it was null across the whole testing period. For all three groups of mice that tail-rattled, the frequency of tail-rattling showed an inverted U-shaped profile, with a maximal peak at day 35, which is around the onset of puberty. On the other hand, the onset of tail-rattling varied across the groups. The dominant individually-housed mice were the earliest to show appreciable tail-rattling (at 29 days), followed by the dominant socially-housed mice (at 32 days) and the subordinate individually-housed mice (at 35 days). From day 38, tail-rattling started to decrease in all three groups, and at 47 days it was null in all groups.

The twenty-first century

In the new century (2000–2026), tail-rattling was used mainly as a behavioral marker of aggressiveness in mouse agonistic encounters (Sukhotina and Bespalov 2000; Van Loo et al. 2003; Lumley et al. 2004; Sato et al. 2004; Abrahams et al. 2005; Branchi et al. 2006; Gammie and Stevenson 2006; Landa et al. 2006; Kayasuga et al. 2007; Vekovischeva et al. 2007; Centenaro et al. 2008; Shaltiel et al. 2008; Koike et al. 2009; Ferragud et al. 2010; Patel et al. 2010; Godar et al. 2011; Umukoro et al. 2012; Hammamieh et al. 2012; Stein et al. 2013; Nosjean et al. 2015; Cressant et al. 2017; Nakata et al. 2018; Lee et al. 2019; de Munter et al. 2020; Mahadevia et al. 2021; Burn et al. 2021; Rigney et al. 2022; Kleshchev et al. 2022; Kukuia et al. 2022; Dorofeikova et al. 2023; Yang et al. 2023; Barcelos et al. 2024; Cheng et al. 2024; Gygax et al. 2024; Anderson et al. 2025; Li et al. 2025; Mañas-Ojeda et al. 2025; Mohapatra et al. 2025; Sznajder et al. 2025; Trives et al. 2025; Dwortz and Curley 2025; Bortolozzo-Gleich et al. 2025; Cohen and Ben-Shaul 2025; Tortorelli et al. 2025; Paterlini et al. 2026; Watt et al. 2026; Hertweck et al. 2026).

Nevertheless, in the past few decades, some papers have described mouse tail-rattling also in two contexts different from aggression: shock-induced fear and predator-induced fear. Interestingly, in these two contexts tail-rattling is not performed by an attacking animal. Moreover, in both contexts there is no animal to fight.

Shock-induced fear memory can be studied in the laboratory through two main paradigms: classical (or Pavlovian) learning and operant (or Skinnerian) learning. The first is a type of learning leading to the formation of an association between two stimuli, while the second features an association between a stimulus and a motor response (d’Isa et al. 2011). Two major behavioral tests for fear-mediated learning are the fear conditioning test (for Pavlovian learning) and the passive avoidance test (for Skinnerian learning). In the fear conditioning test, the mouse is placed in a chamber where a specific context (the visual pattern of the walls) or a specific cue (an auditory stimulus such as a tone, or a visual stimulus such as a light) are paired with the release of an electric shock (Shoji et al. 2014). In the test session, in which no shock is delivered, the animal is exposed to the same context or cue of the training and its behavior is scored. Freezing, the cessation of all body movements except those necessary for breathing, is the most commonly employed behavioral marker of conditioned fear (Müller and Fendt, 2023), with a higher level of freezing revealing a higher degree of fear conditioning memory. However, from the 1990s, tail-rattling in the fear conditioning test session has also been reported as a conditioned fear-related phenotype (Bach et al. 1995; Gerlai and McNamara 2000; Gerlai et al. 2002; Fitch et al. 2002; Barnes and Good 2005; Morellini and Schachner 2006; Wilson et al. 2011; Kawashita et al. 2014; Scarlata et al. 2019; Borkar et al. 2020; Le et al. 2024; Yamao and Matsui 2025). Notably, mice rattle their tail also in the post-shock phase of the training session (for example: Wilson et al. 2011; Miranda et al. 2025), but the fact that they rattle during the testing session (in which no shock is present), merely in response to the conditioned stimulus, indicates a conditioned tail-rattling response.

Fear-induced tail-rattling has been observed in the passive avoidance test as well. In this test, mice learn to avoid a chamber where they have previously received an electric shock (d’Isa et al. 2014). Basically, in the training session a mouse is released in a strongly lit chamber connected to a dark box. Being photophobic, the mouse tends to leave the lit chamber and enter the dark one. However, in the dark chamber it receives a footshock from the grid floor. In the test session, the mouse is placed again in the apparatus, but no shock is released. Mice that remember the previous experience remain in the lit chamber and avoid entering the dangerous dark box. In this testing session, tail-rattling by the mice has been reported21 (El-Ghundi et al. 2001; Morellini and Schachner 2006).

In addition to shock-induced fear, tail-rattling by mice has been found in the context of innate predator-induced fear responses. If mice are exposed to a looming visual stimulus above them (a small black disc expanding into a large disc), they exhibit a subsecond automatic fear reaction, which can be flight to the nest or freezing (Yilmaz and Meister 2013). The majority of the animals (60%) respond with escape, while the remaining mice freeze, but all mice show an immediate response. In the absence of a protective shelter, mice exhibit robust freezing (Carroll et al. 2025). Notably, the looming-induced fear reaction is not simply a response to a moving stimulus, as a receding disc (with the contour moving inwards rather than outwards) fails to evoke the subsecond fear response (Yilmaz and Meister 2013). Moreover, if the expanding disc is presented on the floor instead of on the ceiling, no tail-rattling or freezing are shown by the mice (Yilmaz and Meister 2013). The looming disc resembles the approach of a bird of prey swooping down from the sky and the automatic response to a looming stimulus is considered to have evolved as an antipredatory adaptation in response to aerial predators (Münch 2013; Wu and Zhang 2023). Notably, the looming-induced escape response is displayed also by mice that have never encountered an aerial predator in their whole life, which indicates the innate nature of this antipredatory reaction. Hence, this automatic response can be practically used in the laboratory to study fear-induced escape behavior and has become the basis of a widely employed behavioral test (Yilmaz and Meister 2013; Zhao et al. 2014; Wei et al. 2015;; Shang et al. 2015; De Franceschi et al. 2016; Huang et al. 2017; Salay et al. 2018; Zelikowsky et al. 2018a, 2018b; Robinson et al. 2019; Zhou et al. 2019; Koehler et al. 2019; Blok et al. 2020; Tseng et al. 2020; Yang et al., 2020; Lees et al. 2020; Kim et al. 2020; Daviu et al. 2020; Wang et al. 2021; Cai et al. 2021; Salay and Huberman 2021; Chen et al. 2022; Narushima et al. 2022; Lenzi et al. 2022; Bohne et al. 2022; Liu et al. 2022a, 2022b; Liu et al. 2022c; Lin et al. 2023; Wu et al. 2023; Bohl et al. 2023; Gonzalez et al. 2023; Solomon et al. 2023; Khalil et al. 2023; Kang et al. 2024; Yuan et al. 2024a, 2024b; Calanni et al. 2024; Lazzerini Ospri et al. 2024; Cheng et al. 2024; Lee et al. 2024; Bak et al. 2024; Carroll et al. 2025). In the looming disc test, mice are placed in a chamber containing a nest. The ceiling of the chamber can display a looming stimulus and the behaviors of the mice in response to the visual stimulus are recorded.

Starting from the late 2010s, tail-rattling has been reported in the looming disc test (Salay et al. 2018; Salay and Huberman 2021; Khalil et al. 2023; Lazzerini Ospri et al. 2024). Interestingly, the first group reporting tail-rattling in the looming disc test was also the first to investigate the neural substrates of tail-rattling (Salay et al. 2018). In particular, this research group from Stanford University (California, USA) found that an expanding disc on the ceiling, but not on the floor, induced c-Fos activation of the neurons of the ventral midline thalamus (vMT). Based on this finding, the researchers decided to clarify the role of the vMT through pharmacogenetic manipulations of this area’s function (Salay et al. 2018). Intriguingly, they discovered that vMT inactivation completely eliminated tail-rattling in the looming disc test, while activation of the vMT augmented tail-rattling compared to unstimulated mice. The vMT projects both to the medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC) and the basolateral amygdala (BLA). At this point, to identify the upstream neural substrate responsible for the defensive reaction induced by the looming stimulus, the group used an optogenetic approach in which blue light could be employed to selectively activate the vMT to mPFC pathway or the vMT to BLA pathway (Salay et al. 2018). When the vMT-mPFC pathway was activated in the looming disc test, 100% of the mice showed tail-rattling. On the other hand, activation of the vMT-BLA pathway did not affect tail-rattling. In a subsequent study, the same group studied the role of the ventral lateral geniculate nucleus (vLGN), a large retinorecipient thalamic structure, in tail-rattling (Salay and Huberman 2021). Chemogenetic inactivation of the GABAergic neurons of the vLGN annihilated tail-rattling in the looming disc test. Moreover, chemogenetic activation of the glutamatergic neurons of the vLGN projecting to the superior colliculus (SC) led to prolonged expression of tail-rattling in the looming disc test. Most notably, in the absence of any visual threat, when the mice were simply kept in their home-cage, activation of the glutamatergic neurons of the vLGN caused abundant tail-rattling, while control mice did not rattle at all.

In the 2020s, other groups shed light on the neural substrates of tail-rattling. Urocortin-3-expressing neurons of the perifornical area of the hypothalamus are activated in female mice displaying pup-directed attack, as well as in infanticidal males (Autry et al. 2021). Optogenetic activation of these hypothalamic urocortin-3-expressing neurons in virgin female mice presented with a pup in their home-cage led to pup-directed tail-rattling, while the same manipulation did not increase the aggressiveness of females towards an adult male in the resident-intruder test (Autry et al. 2021). Chemogenetic activation of the same neurons in virgin females caused pup neglect, as revealed by reduced pup retrieval (Autry et al. 2021). Conversely, optogenetic inhibition of these hypothalamic neurons in virgin males selectively reduced pup-directed attacks, without affecting aggressiveness directed towards an adult intruder (Autry et al. 2021).

The ventrolateral sector of the ventromedial hypothalamus (VMHvl), an area known for regulating intraspecies aggression in mice, has been found to be activated in male mice performing tail-rattling (Yang et al. 2023). Curiously, the same area is activated in male mice simply observing tail-rattling (Yang et al. 2023). Performing or observing an attack towards a conspecific also led to activation of the VMHvl. However, for the attacks, the increase of activity in the VMHvl neurons was greater in the performer than in the observer. On the other hand, for tail-rattling, the increase in neuronal activity was comparable between the performer and the observer. The activation of these observer neurons appeared to be specific to tail-rattling and attacking, as it was not seen for other social interactions such as sniffing or grooming.

More recently, the role of the intrinsically photosensitive retinal ganglion cells (ipRGCs) in tail-rattling has been explored (Lazzerini Ospri et al. 2024). The ipRGCs innervate the perihabenula (PHb), a nucleus of the dorsal thalamus which mediates the effect of light on mood and learning (LeGates et al. 2012; Fernandez et al. 2018). The PHb in turn provides input to the mPFC. In the looming disc test, ipRGC-ablated mice demonstrated normal image identification function, as escape to the shelter and freezing were displayed in a similar proportion to their littermate controls. On the other hand, ipRGC-ablated mice performed less tail-rattling, suggesting that the input from the ipRGCs to the PHb is crucial for the expression of this behavior.

Pituitary adenylate cyclase-activating polypeptide (PACAP)-expressing neurons in the lateral parabrachial nucleus (LPB) projecting to the dorsal raphe, which mediate panic-like behavioral and physiological responses, have also been linked to tail-rattling (Kang et al. 2024). In this study, injection of the panicogenic drug FG-7142 augmented the activity of these PACAP-expressing neurons of the LPB and caused a decrease of locomotor activity together with a strong cardiorespiratory activation. Chemogenetic inhibition of these neurons decreased both the behavioral and the autonomic panic-like responses of mice to a hypercapnic condition (10% CO2), suggesting that these neurons are necessary for the expression of panic (Kang et al. 2024). Conversely, optogenetic stimulation of these neurons, when the mice were simply placed in a glass cylindrical arena, immediately elicited reduction of locomotion and tail-rattling, which were accompanied by dramatic increases in breathing frequency and heart rate (Kang et al. 2024).

Finally, recent investigations have pointed out a role for subcortical astrocytes. In particular, a study from last year found that amygdalar astrocytes can mediate tail-rattling (Yamao and Matsui 2025). Photoactivation of the astrocytes in the amygdala led to effects comparable to a footshock in the fear conditioning test. Mice were placed in a fear conditioning chamber and received either a footshock or astrocytic photoactivation. After 6 min, the mice were reintroduced into the chamber and tested for conditioned fear. Notably, in both the group that had previously experienced the footshock and the group that had received the astrocytic photoactivation, an increase in freezing and in tail-rattling was observed. The amount of tail-rattling was comparable in the two groups.

Moreover, a study from this year found that optogenetic stimulation of the astrocytes of the periaqueductal gray of mice in an open arena produced tail-rattling (Barcelon et al. 2026). Both the number and the duration of tail-rattling episodes were increased during light-on epochs compared to light-off epochs. Such photostimulation additionally caused behaviors such as freezing, postural retreat, jumping and fleeing, suggesting an activation of defensive motor patterns. Shortly after, another study investigated the effects of optogenetic stimulation of the neurotensin (Nts)-expressing neurons of the periaqueductal gray that project to the ventral tegmental area (VTA) (Davis et al. 2026). This study found that activation of VTA-projecting Nts + neurons of the periaqueductal gray caused a significant increase of freezing and tail-rattling in mice placed in an empty cage. Interestingly, when this optogenetic stimulation was combined with administration of the dopamine receptor antagonist flupentixol to separate the activation of the periaqueductal gray neurons from the consequent dopaminergic activation of the VTA, it was seen that the mice still tail-rattled, indicating that the expression of tail-rattling is not dependent on VTA dopaminergic signaling.

What emotion does rodent tail-rattling express?

An emotion is a multicomponent response of an organism to an external or internal stimulus or situation, comprising experiential, physiological, somatic and behavioral components22. According to an evolutionary comparative approach to psychology and neuroscience, emotions have a biological basis and have an evolutionary origin (Bekoff 2000; Panksepp 2011, 2016; de Waal 2011; Anderson and Adolphs, 2014; LeDoux and Hoffmann, 2018; Paul et al. 2020; LeDoux 2021; van Heijst et al. 2025). According to this approach, launched by Charles Darwin with his seminal work The Expression of the Emotions in Man and Animals (Darwin 1872) and carried on by evolutionary comparative psychologists and neuroscientists, in addition to newly developed emotional capabilities, humans possess emotions that they have inherited from non-human ancestors, and a small subset of basic emotions are widely shared across mammals, including rodents (Bekoff 2000; Panksepp 2005, 2011, 2016; Watanabe and Kuczaj 2013; Anderson and Adolphs 2014; LeDoux and Hoffmann 2018; Paul and Mendel 2018, 2025; Paul et al. 2020; LeDoux 2021; Mota-Rojas et al. 2025). Such basic emotions comprise, among others, fear, anger, disgust, surprise, curiosity, sexual arousal, hunger, pain and joy. For a recent analysis of, for example, joy in non-human animals, see Nelson et al. 2023. In non-human animals, emotions are inferred from motor behaviors, posture, facial expressions, vocalizations and physiological parameters, such as heart rate, amount of salivation or number of fecal boluses defecated. Motor behaviors are generally the most conspicuous markers. However, with the progress of technology, scoring of facial expressions is becoming an increasingly important tool for emotion recognition in non-human animals (Florkiewicz 2025; Lazebnik and Florkiewicz 2025; Correia-Caeiro et al. 2025). For instance, systems for detection and quantification of pain through facial expressions have been developed for several non-human animal species (Evangelista et al. 2022), such as mice (Hohlbaum et al. 2020; McCoy et al. 2024; Onuma et al. 2024), rats (Klune et al. 2019; Arnold et al. 2023; Onuma et al. 2024), rabbits (Hampshire and Robertson 2015), cats (Evangelista et al. 2019), sheep (McLennan and Mahmoud 2019) and beef (Müller et al. 2019). In rats, distinct facial expressions are produced in response to pleasant or unpleasant tastes (Berridge 1991). Mice exhibit different facial patterns in aggressive versus fearful contexts (Defensor et al. 2012). Nadine Gogolla’s team at the Max Planck Institute of Neurobiology in Martinsried (Germany) recently showed, in a groundbreaking study, that in mice high-speed video recording analyzed by machine learning allows the identification of emotion-specific facial displays for a set of basic emotions, including pleasure, disgust, active fear (escape), passive fear (freezing), pain and malaise (Dolensek et al. 2020; comment: Girard and Bellone 2020).

Trying to understand what emotion underlies an instance of rodent tail-rattling is challenging. Superficially, it may seem that the emotional states of rodents are inaccessible black boxes. Nevertheless, a critical analysis of the contexts in which tail-rattling is produced, and of the associated behaviors, can actually allow an objective investigation of the possible emotions underlying tail-rattling. For example, when a rodent shows tail-rattling during mating, how can we know if the tail-rattling is due to fear, aggressiveness or sexual arousal? The answer mainly depends on the contextual behavior: if tail-rattling is associated with attacking behavior, then it is likely an expression of aggressiveness; if it is associated with retraction and avoidance behaviors, it is likely an expression of fear; if it is strictly associated with proceptive behavior and lordosis, then it is likely an expression of sexual arousal.

In the previous section (Sect. "Historical background: rodent tail-rattling from porcupines to laboratory mice"), we traced a history of the observations of rodent tail-rattling from the eighteenth century to present. In the present section, we will examine what currently available knowledge suggests regarding the possible emotional states underlying rodent tail-rattling. In order to understand the emotional meaning of tail-rattling, in the following sub-sections we first consider the affectively-relevant findings on tail-rattling by specific emotion, and we then propose a model that can explain the expression of tail-rattling across all the mentioned emotional contexts.

Aggressiveness

Anger is a negatively toned basic emotion, experienced as an aroused state of antagonism towards an animal or a situation perceived as aversive (Novaco 2017). Its intensity can range from low (irritation) to high (rage). Like in humans, in rodents anger is expressed by autonomic activation (increase of heart rate and respiratory frequency), distinctive facial pattens and behavior. However, as in rodents anger-related facial mimicry is subtle and as autonomic monitoring requires specific technological equipment, the most practical and commonly used operationalization of rodent anger is aggressive behavior, with the corresponding behavioral trait being aggressiveness. Aggressiveness is the propensity to threaten or actuate an attack towards a target (Abramson et al. 2025). In the laboratory, the most widely employed rodent test of aggressiveness is the resident-intruder test, in which an unfamiliar conspecific (the stimulus animal) is placed in the home-cage of a subject rodent (the resident) and the behavioral interaction between the two is scored (Koolhaas et al. 2013; Ruzza et al. 2015). As laboratory rodents (such as mice and rats) are territorial, the resident typically perceives the unfamiliar conspecific as an intruder, and rapidly shows aggressive behaviors (threats and attacks) towards it (Koolhaas et al. 2013; Ruzza et al. 2015). Interestingly, in this test the resident shows a peculiar aggressiveness-related facial display characterized by tightening of the orbital region of the face (narrowing of the eyes), flattening of the ears, swelling of the nose and swelling of the cheeks (Defensor et al. 2012; Ebbesen and Froemke 2021).

At present, the dominant view in the scientific literature is that tail-rattling is an expression of aggressiveness. However, according to us, equating tail-rattling to aggressiveness is wrong and arises from a bias that has mainly historical reasons. When rodent tail-rattling was first described, it was in porcupines, which displayed this behavior in a context of overt aggressiveness. Concerning their emotional state, the tail-rattling porcupines were described as “irritated” (Smellie et al. 1771; Hall 1788; Waterhouse 1848; Wood 1859), “angered” (Darwin 1872) or “in a rage” (Loveridge 1928). The evaluations of these early naturalists were grounded, as the tail-rattling observed in porcupines was part of a highly stereotyped behavioral sequence that started with quill erection and culminated with attacking behavior. There was a clear and very strong association between tail-rattling and attacking behavior.

Modern descriptions of porcupine agonistic behavior confirm both the context and the single behavioral components of the descriptions by the mentioned early naturalists. In particular, Emiliano Mori and colleagues outlined the agonistic ethogram of free-living crested porcupines during interspecies encounters with potential predators (hunting dogs), identifying four major components, which the authors classified as being characterized by increasing aggressiveness: 1) quill and crest erection; 2) tail-rattling; 3) hind foot stomping; 4) backward/sideways attack (Mori et al. 2014). In the attacks, porcupines are turned with their back towards the target and, with the quills erected, run backwards and laterally into their opponents, piercing them with the pointy quills23. Quill/crest erection was observed in 100% of the interspecies encounters and in 92% of the cases quill erection was accompanied by tail-rattling. In 55% of the cases with tail-rattling, the porcupines additionally showed foot stomping and in 100% of the foot-stomping cases an attack followed. Interestingly, tail-rattling appeared to be efficient as a defensive threat display. Indeed, when a porcupine encountered a single opponent (dyadic encounters), generally (in 86% of the cases) tail-rattling was sufficient to deter the opponent and the encounter did not lead to any injury for the porcupine. Only in 14% of the dyadic encounters, the porcupine escalated to foot stomping and attacking (with the porcupine receiving no injuries and the dog being wounded). On the other hand, when the porcupine was outnumbered by the opponents, in 100% of the cases tail-rattling was followed by foot stomping and attack. In these attacks, in 67% of the cases dogs were injured, while the porcupines were injured in 100% of the cases and killed in 33%. Encounters featuring more than one opponent were more dangerous for the porcupines and the porcupines increased their level of aggressiveness according to the overall offensive potential of the opponents. As, in the dyadic encounters, tail-rattling always repelled the opponent, the authors defined this defense as “acoustic aposematism”, in reference to the threatening function of the loud noise produced by the tail vibration. Importantly, porcupine tail-rattling was strongly associated with attacking (attacking was preceded by tail-rattling in 100% of the cases), which corroborates the hypothesis that porcupine tail-rattling is a form of aggressive signaling. Porcupines analogously exhibit tail-rattling in intraspecific agonistic events (Pigozzi 1997). Notably, tail-rattling is not part of the mating ethogram of porcupines, neither the male’s nor the female’s (Felicioli et al. 1997) and has never been found in any context other than agonism. Hence, in porcupines, tail-rattling appears indeed to be an expression of aggressiveness.

Nevertheless, while the interpretation of porcupine tail-rattling as an aggressive display is valid, the way that the porcupine model was superimposed on the laboratory mouse has been wrong and, since the laboratory mouse subsequently became the universal rodent model in experimental research, this mistake became universal as well. Mouse tail-rattling began to be described relatively late, over 160 years after that the description of porcupine tail-rattling had been included in the Encyclopaedia Britannica (Smellie et al. 1771). In particular, mouse tail-rattling was reported only from the 1930s, when Jacob Uhrich first observed it in the laboratory (Uhrich 1938), soon followed in the early 1940s by John Paul Scott (Scott 1942). Both Uhrich and Scott happened to report tail-rattling in the context of intraspecies aggressiveness, which primed subsequent research on this behavior. Uhrich noticed tail-rattling by chance and relayed it very incidentally (in a single sentence) within a work that focused on other behaviors. Scott, on the other hand, started observing tail-rattling by design in experimentally staged encounters between laboratory mice, and explicitly used tail-rattling as a behavioral parameter to evaluate social interactions. Notably, at that time, the best-known case of rodent tail-rattling was, by far, porcupine tail-rattling. When mouse tail-rattling had to be interpreted, porcupine tail-rattling inevitably served as a conceptual printing mold to obtain an explanation for the apparently bizarre tail-vibration of the mice.

Remarkably, at that time this behavior had no specific name in mice or rats and, when this behavior was performed by non-porcupine rodents, generic terms were employed to refer to it, such as vibrating (Svihla and Dowell Svihla, 1933), beating (Donat 1933), striking (Vestal 1938), clattering (Vestal 1938) or lashing (Uhrich 1938; Poole 1940; Ginsburg and Allee 1942; Beeman and Allee 1945). Scott was the first scientist that used the verb rattle (Scott 1942), which led to the noun tail-rattling (Beeman and Allee 1945; Scott 1946, 1947). Significantly, when Scott, Beeman and Allee chose to name this mouse behavior “tail-rattling”, such phrase was specifically employed for the porcupine’s behavior, and the verb rattle was commonly used to describe how the porcupine moved its tail. Consequently, this lexical choice further strengthened the idea of an analogous meaning for the mouse’s and the porcupine’s tail vibration. Starting from just 9 months after24 Scott’s first usage of the verb rattle, others began picking up this lexical choice (Ginsburg and Allee 1942: “rattled”; Beeman and Allee 1945: “rattled”, “tail rattle” and “tail-rattling”; Beeman 1947: “rattle”, “rattled”, “tail rattle” and “tail-rattling”; Warne 1947: “tail rattling”). Between the 1940s and the 1960s, Scott became an authority in the field of mouse agonistic behavior, releasing numerous works on the topic, including two extremely influential conceptual framework articles (Scott and Fredericson 1951; Scott 1966), in which “tail-rattling” was listed in the mouse aggressive ethogram. After Scott’s first review, “tail-rattling” became standard terminology for this behavior. Moreover, as the mouse became the most widely employed animal model in behavioral sciences (Voikar 2020; Moulin et al. 2021) and the two conceptual articles by Scott (which linked tail-rattling to agonism) became the most cited articles on tail-rattling of all times, the view that rodent tail-rattling is an expression of aggressiveness became the most common and widespread in the scientific community, remaining such until present. From the 1930s to the 2020s, a plethora of studies reported tail-rattling by mice in the agonistic context, typically when mice were exposed to unfamiliar conspecifics (see Sect. "Historical background: rodent tail-rattling from porcupines to laboratory mice"). As the other major rodent model, the Norway rat, rarely exhibits tail-rattling in laboratory contexts (but does show frequent tail-rattling in non-aversive extra-laboratory contexts; see Sect. "Caressing-induced pleasure"), this further corroborated the interpretative bias related to rodent tail-rattling.

The problem is that in mice, contrarily to what is seen in porcupines, the association with attacking behaviors is not so stringent. In porcupines, as we have described above, attacking behavior was preceded by tail-rattling in 100% of the cases (Mori et al 2014). In contrast, for male mice exposed to unfamiliar male conspecifics, analysis of the temporal relationship between tail-rattling and attacking found a very scarce association between the two, with tail-rattling preceding attack in just 6% of all the cases of tail-rattling, and tail-rattling following attack in 6% (Haber and Simmel 1976a, 1976b). In most cases, tail-rattling was associated with either no behavior or social investigation. Analogously, lactating female mice, exposed to intruders at 18–20 days post-partum, were highly aggressive, but attacks were rarely associated with tail-rattling (Parmigiani et al. 1988). In particular, the lactating females attacked 10 of 12 female intruders (83%), launching up to 50 attacks per encounter, but they exhibited tail-rattling no more than once per encounter, and the median number of tail-rattles was zero, meaning that at least 50% of the mice did not tail-rattle at all. Consistently, the lactating females attacked 11 of 12 male intruders (92%), launching up to 32 attacks per encounter, but again they exhibited tail-rattling no more than once per encounter, and the median number of tail-rattles was zero. Basically, in mice, attacking is often unpreceded by tail-rattling, and tail-rattling is often unfollowed by attacking. This raises doubts about the actual interpretation of tail-rattling.

While, currently, mouse tail-rattling is widely employed in laboratory testing as a marker of aggressiveness (see Sect. "The 21st century"), it is debatable whether the tail-rattling of mice during agonistic encounters is truly an expression of aggressiveness, rather than of some other emotional state. Indeed, Scott showed that, after acquiring experience in fighting, male mice increase (in comparison to the initial day of testing) their attacks against a mouse dangled in front of them, but they reduce tail-rattling (Scott 1946, 1947). Basically, aggressiveness increases, yet tail-rattling diminishes. Based on these results, Scott argued that “emotional reactions such as tail rattling and hair fluffing appear during a time of indecision”, when the mouse is not yet confident enough to attack. Hence, rather than expressing aggressiveness itself, mouse tail-rattling in the agonistic context would express a state of uncertainty arising from the inhibitory effect of fear on the drive to attack. With experience, the fear is reduced, as well as the hesitation to attack, and with it the tail-rattling. Moreover, by employing a different protocol, Scott showed that previous exposure to social defeat increased tail-rattling in mice when subsequently presented with a dangled (and hence inoffensive) mouse, in comparison to control mice that were never exposed to social defeat (Scott 1947). In this case, the inhibitory effect of fear is increased, and with it tail-rattling.

Furthermore, the connection between tail-rattling and aggressiveness is not selective, as tail-rattling can be observed in many non-agonistic contexts. Tail-rattling in non-agonistic contexts has been extremely underinvestigated compared to agonistic contexts, which has led to an observation bias. However, if we consider the full range of contexts in which rodents exhibit tail-rattling, through a more comprehensive evaluation of the scientific literature that takes into account also less-known and extra-laboratory sources, it appears clear that this behavior cannot be exclusively an expression of anger or aggressiveness. Indeed, in addition to aggressiveness, at least four other emotional contexts can be identified: fear (shock-induced or predator cue-induced), decisional uncertainty, sexual arousal and petting reward. We will examine these contexts in the following subsections.

Fear

Fear is a negatively toned emotion aroused by a perceived threat, involving an alarm reaction comprising a set of physiological changes which induce the organism to take a defensive action25. Autonomic signs of fear include increased heartbeat frequency, piloerection, muscular tension, urination and defecation. Behaviorally, fear can be expressed by defensive actions such as freezing (which reduces the chances of being visually or acoustically detected), passive avoidance (refraining from approaching the stimulus) or active avoidance (escaping from the stimulus). In rodent laboratory testing, the two major types of stimuli employed to induce fear are electric shocks and predator cues. Notably, both types of fear-inducing stimuli can elicit tail-rattling.

Richard Dennis St. John was the first to discover that exposure to a sequence of painful footshocks can induce tail-rattling in laboratory mice (St. John 1973, 1978). Notably, shock-induced tail-rattling is shown by the mice not simply in coincidence of the shock, but tens of seconds after the last shock and before a new shock, as if it was an expression of fear of a future shock. Moreover, subsequent studies have found that mice express tail-rattling in the fear conditioning paradigm (for example: Gerlai and McNamara 2000; Gerlai et al. 2002; Fitch et al. 2002; Wilson et al. 2011). In this paradigm, tail-rattling is seen not only in the training session, during which the mice receive the footshocks. Remarkably, if after a variable delay from the training, the mice are returned to the same fear conditioning chamber, even in total absence of new shocks, the mice, recognizing the chamber, will display again tail-rattling, as an expression of the conditioned fear to the context. It is important to note that in the context of fear conditioning no other animal is present in the chamber and actually no possible target to attack or threaten is present either. This strongly suggests that tail-rattling in the fear conditioning chamber is not a form of aggressiveness. The other behaviors which are displayed by mice in the shock chamber in addition to tail-rattling (such as reduced locomotion, freezing and increased defecation; for example, see: Chen et al. 1994; Dirks et al. 2001; Shoji et al. 2014) equally support the idea that shock-induced tail-rattling is an expression of fear rather than aggressiveness.

Another emblematic case where tail-rattling appears to be elicited by fear is tail-rattling induced by a looming disc, a stimulus which resembles an aerial predator approaching from above (Salay et al. 2018). This looming visual stimulus produces an escape-to-shelter reaction in the mice. In the shelter, mice produce abundant tail-rattling (Salay et al. 2018). The escape reaction, the absence of any attempt to bite or snap at the looming stimulus, and the fact that the tail-rattling is produced while hiding in the shelter (rather than in the unsheltered open space) all strongly suggest that looming-induced tail-rattling is an expression of fear, not of aggressiveness.

Still regarding fear-induced tail-rattling, rattling of the tail has been found in the context of female cricket-killing during the parenting phase. Kenneth Sandnabba discovered that, in TA mice, the females tail-rattled before attacking newly presented crickets (Sandnabba 1995a). Notably, this tail-rattling was present in the predatory ethogram of the female, but not in that of the male (Sandnabba 1993b, 1995b). Moreover, these females displayed tail-rattling to the cricket when they were in the lactation period and they had a litter of newborn pups. Additionally, females from this mouse strain showed tail-rattling also to male intruders, but only during lactation, not during gestation, when the pups were not born yet (Sandnabba 1993a). Altogether, these findings suggest that the female's tail-rattling to the cricket is not an expression of predatory aggressiveness, but rather of fear due to an animal which is perceived as a potential threat to the pups.

The display of tail-rattling in non-agonistic fear-related contexts may rise doubts regarding the actual nature of tail-rattling during agonistic situations. Is it possible that mouse tail-rattling in agonistic contexts is entirely due to fear rather than aggressiveness? The behavior of a mutant mouse strain, monoamine oxidase A knock-out (MAO A KO) mice, provides crucial insights that allow us to rule out this hypothesis. In the novel object exploration test, mice are exposed to an unfamiliar object to evaluate their responses to novelty. Healthy wildtype mice typically show attraction towards the novel object (neophilia), while anxiety-inducing manipulations lead to aversion for the novel object (neophobia). Object neophobia is seen, for example, in a mouse genetic model of anxiety which exhibits increased anxiety in the open-field test, in the elevated plus-maze, in the light–dark box and in the mirror chamber (Booher et al. 2021). Analogously, object neophobia has been observed in mice knockout for RICH2 (Rho-GTPase-activating Protein Interacting with Cdc-42-interacting Protein 4 Homolog 2), a RhoGAP synaptic protein (Sarowar et al. 2016, 2017).

When MAO A KO mice were exposed to a novel object, a black plastic cylinder (length: 8 cm; diameter: 3.5 cm), they showed a very peculiar phenotype: they approached the novel object, but to attack it (Godar et al. 2011). Notably, wildtype mice did not tail-rattle in this test, while the mutants did. Several facts suggest that the mutants were not tail-rattling to the object because of fear but rather because of aggressiveness. First, the tail rattling of the mutants was associated with approach (differently from the case of neophobia) and biting of the object, an overt aggressive behavior. Second, the novel object test was performed in three conditions (with different groups of mice): in a cage where the mice had been previously exposed to a day with a 15 min-session without the object (condition A), in a cage where the mice had been previously exposed to two days with a 15 min-session without the object (condition B), and in a fully familiar environment, the home-cage of the mice (condition C). If tail-rattling had been motivated by fear, then it should have decreased with familiarity. Instead, exactly the opposite was seen. In condition A, 33% of MAO A KO mice tail-rattled vs. 0% of the wildtype mice. On the other hand, in both environments with increased familiarity (conditions B and C), MAO A KO mice increased their tail-rattling frequency to 62.5% (while wildtype mice never tail-rattled). As environments with a higher familiarity are supposed to be associated with higher confidence and lower fear, it seems reasonable to hypothesize that the increase of tail-rattling in conditions B and C was likely due to a greater confidence in expressing aggressiveness rather than to fear. Finally, MAO A KO mice were assessed in fear tests and showed either no increase of fear or reduced fear (Godar et al. 2011). In particular, in the elevated plus maze, MAO A KO mice exhibited the same percentage of entries and duration of exploration in the open arms as the wildtype mice. In tests of predator-induced fear, such as exposure to predator (bobcat) urine or to an anaesthetized rat, MAO A KO mice displayed a significant reduction of fear-related behaviors. Hence, the mutant mice were not more fearful but less fearful, further supporting the idea that their increased tail-rattling in the novel object test was induced by aggressiveness rather than fear. It therefore appears that fear cannot substitute in toto aggressiveness as an explanation for tail-rattling in mouse agonistic contexts.

Analogously, chemogenetic activation of the ventromedial hypothalamus (VMH) of individually caged male mice caused tail-rattling towards novel objects that were placed in their home-cage, such as an inflated glove and a flipped (non-reflecting) mirror (Yang et al. 2017). Interestingly, with stimulation of the VMH in the empty home-cage, no significant increase in tail-rattling was observed, indicating that the stimulation-induced tail-rattling is target-directed. Notably, the rattling VMH-stimulated mice were approaching the objects rather than showing object avoidance. This suggests that fear alone cannot be an explanation for this this tail-rattling. In the case of the glove, VMH-stimulated mice even showed occasional biting attacks, indicating that their tail-rattling was associated with increased aggressiveness. When a reflecting mirror was placed in their cage, the group of the VMH-stimulated mice tail-rattled more than twice as much than towards the other objects (vs. inflated glove: > 40% of the mice; vs. flipped mirror: > 40%; vs. reflecting mirror: > 90%). Control non-stimulated mice never tail-rattled at any of the three objects. The reaction to the reflecting mirror further suggests that the tail-rattling of VMH-stimulated mice is due to increased aggressiveness towards intruders. Indeed, when females primed to be in estrus were placed in the home-cage of the resident male mice, the VMH-stimulated mice showed an aberrant aggressiveness towards the females, with more than 65% displaying tail-rattling and over 80% attacking the females. The fact that the tail-rattling was associated with attacking behaviors confirms that VMH-induced tail-rattling comprises an aggressive component. Control non-stimulated mice never attacked the females and never tail-rattled at them. Overall, the reaction of the VMH-stimulated mice to the novel objects does not seem to be explained by pure neophobia. However, the VMH-stimulated mice’s biting attacks against the glove were rare, and absent in the case of the mirrors. This suggests that their tail-rattling was not an expression of pure aggressiveness either. In sum, the VMH-stimulated mice’s tail-rattling to novel objects appears to reflect a combination of neophobia-induced hesitation and aggressiveness, rather than fear alone or aggressiveness alone.

Uncertainty

A very peculiar context in which tail-rattling has been observed is uncertainty in a situation of decisional conflict. Scott, who was the first to make this finding, noticed that if mice were exposed to both an inhibitory social training (leading to social defeat) and a weak stimulatory training (leading to social victory), and then underwent a social interaction test with a stimulus mouse dangled in front of them, they showed 8 times as much the tail-rattling shown by the control group, which had been exposed to the stimulatory training alone (Scott 1947). In the test, for the mice exposed to the conflicting training, the outcome of their interaction with the stimulus mouse was less certain, as a part of their previous experiences was successful and a part was unsuccessful. Such an uncertainty was associated with increased tail-rattling. This uncertainty-induced tail-rattling is related to fear-induced tail-rattling. Indeed, compared to the controls, what makes the double-trained mice hesitant between attack and escape, and makes them rattle, is that, having experienced social defeat in the past, in the test they fear a potential attack from the stimulus mouse. Based on this finding, Scott understood that tail-rattling could not be just an expression of aggressiveness, and he interpreted tail-rattling as an involuntary and reflex-like “emotional” reaction, like hair-fluffing, in opposition to the voluntary fighting behavior (Scott 1947, 1951). Ironically, since Scott described tail-rattling within the wider frame of agonistic behavior, his work was widely used to support the view that tail-rattling is just a sign of aggressiveness. However, this view was not that of Scott himself. In the conflicting training study, Scott observed the following chronology of agonistic behaviors: a) running away; b) nosing and grooming; c) hair-fluffing and tail-rattling; d) attacking. Since tail-rattling appeared after the running away phase and before the attacking phase, Scott considered it as an external emotional reaction appearing “during a time of indecision” between flight and attack (Scott 1947). Considering that the only difference between the experimental mice and the control mice was that the first group had experienced a social defeat session, during the tail-rattling phase the experimental mice’s inhibition in launching an attack very likely originated from the fear to receive counter-attacks in response. Notably, after the tail-rattling phase, the double-trained mice launched a considerably greater number of attacks towards the stimulus mouse, indicating that they were indeed more aggressive. However, in the initial tail-rattling phase, it appears that the increased aggressiveness of the experimental mice towards the stimulus mouse did not lead to an attack because it was inhibited by fear. Nevertheless, it is important to underline that, if the uncertainty hypothesis is correct, tail-rattling in the test session is not elicited by the fear of a specific target (in this case, the dangled mouse), but rather by a more aspecific tension originating from the inability, during a threatening situation, to actuate a threat-dealing action (e.g., flight or fight) which the animal is strongly driven to perform by the situation. In order to test Scott’s interpretation and verify if tail-rattling is selectively induced by uncertainty-induced discomfort rather than fear of a specific target, Scott’s experiment could be repeated with the addition of a third group of mice undergoing only the inhibitory training. These mice would have experienced exclusively social defeats in the training and would hence have a greater fear of the dangled mouse in the test. If the results show that the double-trained mice exhibit dramatic levels of tail-rattling, while the two single-trained groups do not (both the mice trained with victories only and the mice trained with defeats only), this would be a confirmation that the tail-rattling in the test is truly induced by the uncertainty and not by the fear of the dangled mouse itself, nor by the aggressiveness towards the dangled mouse per se. The uncertainty-induced tension, and the consequent tail-rattling, would in this case be the result of the combination of conflicting drives (fear driving to escape and aggressiveness driving to attack), with fear frustrating the drive to attack and with aggressiveness inhibiting the drive to escape.

Uncertainty induced by decisional conflict may also explain the tail-rattling observed by mice in the fear conditioning and in the passive avoidance test. In the fear conditioning, a tone or the chamber context may evoke memory of a previous footshock, and generate a strong motivational drive to escape, but flight is impossible to actuate, as the chamber is actually closed and unescapable. This leads to a conflict between what the animal would be driven to do and what it can actually do. Similarly, in the testing session of the passive avoidance, the mice would be motivated to leave the lit compartment due to their natural photophobia, but they refrain from entering the dark box because, after the first shock, they perceive the box as dangerous and hence fear leaving the lit compartment.

A recent study by the group of the behavioral neuroscientist Jonathan Paul Fadok at Tulane University in New Orleans (Louisiana, USA) provides experimental evidence supporting the idea that post-footshock tail-rattling is induced by uncertainty rather than simple fear of a new footshock (Le et al. 2024). In this study, the group employed the serial compound stimulus (SCS) paradigm, a recently developed Pavlovian fear conditioning procedure. In the SCS paradigm, during the training a footshock is paired with a SCS, which is a sequence of tone and white noise stimulus periods, and, in a subsequent testing session, the exposure to the SCS alone elicits a freezing-to-flight transition (Fadok et al. 2017; Totty et al. 2021; Matarazzo di Licosa et al. 2025). In order to distinguish the differential contributions of associative and non-associative mechanisms to the defensive reactions of mice, Fadok’s team used five experimental groups of mice in their study (Le et al. 2024): paired (5 pairings of a shock with a SCS featuring 10 pips of tone followed by 10 pips of white noise), paired reverse (5 pairings of a shock with a SCS featuring 10 pips of white noise followed by 10 pips of tone), unpaired (pseudorandom presentations of SCS and footshocks), unpaired reverse (pseudorandom presentations of reverse SCS and footshocks) and shock only (five shocks without any SCS). The training featured two consecutive days of conditioning. On the second day of conditioning, the paired group showed robust freezing during the tone period, switching to explosive jumping and darting behaviors during the white noise period. On the other hand, the unpaired group, as well as both the reverse SCS groups, exhibited less tone-induced freezing, and only rare jumping and darting during the white noise period. After the conditioning, extinction was carried out in two consecutive days. Notably, during the extinction the researchers found that the shock only and the unpaired groups displayed more tail-rattling during the SCS (both during the tone period and the white noise period) than the paired group. The fact that for the paired group the tone was announcing a certain footshock, while for the other groups it wasn’t, suggests that in this context tail-rattling was induced by uncertainty rather than by the fear of an upcoming footshock itself. This led Fadok’s group to interpret tail-rattling in this extinction paradigm as a non-associative behavioral response, which is suppressed when the SCS signals a threat in a predictable manner (Le et al. 2024).

Sexual arousal

Remarkably, rodent tail-rattling has not been reported only in aversive situations, but also in appetitive ones. The most important example is tail-rattling in the context of mating. In the 1930s, Fae Donat Wood, while keeping male and female dusky-footed woodrats together for breeding, was the first to observe tail-rattling in the context of mating (Donat 1933). Donat Wood noticed that the male woodrat showed interest towards the female woodrat and pursued her. While running about, normally, the woodrats were keeping their tails parallel to the ground at about 1 inch from it. However, when the female was chased by the male, her tail was kept higher, with the posterior half curved upwards, and now and then the female twitched the entire tail. On the other hand, the male, when placed in the breeding room with the female, performed vigorous tail-rattling, producing a peculiar drumming sound by repeatedly beating the tail on the ground. Donat Wood interpreted this as a “sexual behavior”. However, after that the male was removed and the female was alone with the pups that were born, on several occasions the female also produced this peculiar sound through tail-rattling. Donat Wood hence understood that this same behavior can be elicited by different contexts. In this second context, Donat Wood interpreted tail-rattling as a possible “nervous reaction to disturbances of various kind”, which are perceived by the female as alarming. It hence appears that in the first context, tail-rattling by the male was an appetitive reaction to the female. On the other hand, in the second context tail-rattling by the female was elicited by fear-inducing stimuli, possibly perceived as more salient by the mother due to her instinct to protect the newborns.

Subsequent laboratory behavioral testing by Kurt Wallen and Kenneth Paul Dial confirmed that male dusky-footed woodrats display tail-rattling when exposed to a female (Wallen 1977, 1982; Dial 1978, 1987). According to Dial, when presented with a female, males approach her and start tail-rattling. After the male’s tail-rattling, the female generally comes close to the male, turns into a tail-to-the-male position and adopts a lordosis posture, after which the male mounts the female and copulation occurs (Dial 1987). Notably, Wallen reported that the male tail-rattling to the females was accompanied by foot-drumming, defecation and urination (Wallen 1982). This led Wallen to interpret the male’s tail-rattling in this context as an expression of autonomic arousal induced by sexual excitement (Wallen 1982). Moreover, tail-rattling by the male to the female in the context of courtship has also been found in other rodent species, in particular the European water vole (Frank 1956), the green acouchi (Morris 1962) and the cane rat (Ewer 1969). Interestingly, in the European water vole, male tail-rattling to the female is accompanied by activation of the flank glands and consequent release of oily secretion on the fur surrounding the glands, which can be considered a sign of appetitive arousal in response to the female, as the same physiological response was observed also when the caregiver of the voles brought them food (Frank 1956). Additionally, in the black rat, females respond to the male’s courtship by tail-rattling (Ewer 1971; Estep et al. 1978). Importantly, this female tail-rattling is not an expression of fear, but rather of sexual arousal in response to the male, as it is always performed by the females before taking the lordosis posture, which is considered an expression of receptivity and acceptance of the male, and it is not performed by the females rejecting the male (Estep et al. 1978).

Caressing-induced pleasure

Finally, as another appetitive situation, there is extensive documentation by pet owners that pet rats (Rattus norvegicus) exhibit tail-rattling when they are being caressed by a familiar human with whom they have formed a strong affective bond26. Gentle caressing is rewarding to rats, as demonstrated by the conditioned place preference test, a standard test for the assessment of the rewarding properties of a stimulus (Nagaishi and Shiomi 2026). When pet rats are caressed by their caregivers, tail-rattling appears to express pleasure from tactile stimulation and a state of contentment. Ducommun’s guide to pet rats confirms tail movement in positive hedonic states: “Some rats seem to wag their tails when happy” (Ducommun 2012). Typically, during caressing-induced tail-rattling the rats keep their body still, maintaining it in contact with the human’s hand for the whole caressing period, often half-closing their eyes and lifting up their chin. Caresses on the head or on the dorsum are both effective in eliciting tail-rattling.

Remarkably, this type of tail-rattling induced by pleasurable interspecies social contact can be seen not just when rats interact with humans, but also with other non-human animals, typically other pets that live in the same house together with the pet rats. It is long known that co-living pet rats and pet dogs can form strong social bonds (d’Isa 2025a). During voluntary social contacts between such pets, rats may show tail-rattling. For instance, pet owner Annaliese Mazzuchelli released videos of her pet rats playfully interacting with her pet dog27. In one of these videos (Mazzuchelli-V2), a pet rat voluntarily approaches the dog and seeks physical contact with it, by slipping under the left forelimb of the lying dog. Shortly after this social contact, the rat begins tail-rattling, while keeping its dorsum against the chest of the dog. The rat remains in this position for 10 s, after which it slips out, it rears, it touches the dog’s snout with its snout, and then it leaves the dog. Few seconds after, the rat returns and makes repeated snout-snout contacts with the dog.

For pet mice, tail-rattling in appetitive situations has been relayed more rarely. However, there are reports of this behavior being displayed when the mice are in physical contact with a human with whom they have formed a bond28. As in the case of the pet rats, the pet mice are voluntarily seeking contact with the human in these contexts.

Our interpretation of the emotional state underlying tail-rattling: the discharge model

In sum, rodents show tail-rattling in a variety of different situations, associated with a different set of emotional states, including aggressiveness, fear, uncertainty-induced decisional conflict, sexual arousal and caressing-induced pleasure. Figure 5 summarizes the main emotional contexts inducing tail-rattling. How is it possible that tail-rattling is expressed in emotional contexts that are so different? Our answer is that tail-rattling is not associated with a specific emotion, but is rather an expression of a generic state of high emotional arousal, which is triggered as an involuntary automatic reaction by situations that strongly impact the affective state of the animal. Making a comparison with humans, the rodent tail-rattling could be considered as an analog of the human emotion-induced chills, which are often accompanied by piloerection and can occur in both pleasant contexts (such as the aesthetic chills in response to particularly captivating music pieces: Nusbaum et al. 2014; Bannister 2020; de Fleurian and Pearce 2021; Jain et al. 2023; Schoeller et al. 2024) and aversive contexts (such as fear chills: James 1894; Holtzclaw 1993; Krishnakumar and Schoeller 2021).Fig. 5Main emotional contexts inducing tail-rattling. Importantly, another fundamental feature appears to be shared across the different emotional contexts underlying rodent tail-rattling. What the observed contexts have in common is not just that they produce a high level of emotional activation. In these various contexts, there also appears to be a drive to perform an action that is unexpressed. This relates to the hypothesis proposed by Scott, according to which tail-rattling origins from a conflict between two opposite motivational drives. Remarkably, while Scott proposed this hypothesis to refer to a very specific context where there was a conflict between the actions motivated by fear and aggressiveness (flight vs. fight), actually Scott’s interpretation of tail-rattling can be generalized to all conflicting drives. Refining Scott’s hypothesis, we propose here a model that we name the discharge model of tail-rattling.

The principles of the discharge model of tail-rattling are illustrated in Fig. 6. Basically, rodents can be subjected to activatory drives, which induce action, and inhibitory drives, which induce inaction (Fig. 6, first row; being subject to an activatory drive is represented by the color red, while the inhibitory drive is represented by the color blue). When an individual is simultaneously subjected to an activatory and an inhibitory drive (a situation represented by the color purple), there can be two outcomes. Either the activatory drive prevails, and the animal displays locomotor activity (Fig. 6, second row), or the inhibitory drive prevails, and the animal remains in its place (Fig. 6, third row). When the inhibitory drive prevails, the drive to move cannot be expressed and is consequently discharged through tail-rattling. The discharge model leads to a slight re-interpretation of the reason for which Scott’s double-trained mice were hesitating and tail-rattling. According to Scott, the mice were hesitant between attacking and escaping, two actions. According to our model, the mice were instead undergoing a drive to attack (an action, prompted by aggressiveness), which was blocked by the fear of the target (an inhibitory drive), with the result that the mice remained on the spot and tail-rattled.

Notably, according to the discharge model, the conflicting drives explanation is not just valid in aversive situations (as in the fear vs. aggressiveness conflict proposed by Scott), but can also be applied to tail-rattling in appetitive contexts. Indeed, it is possible to imagine opposite drives even this situation. For instance, when a pet rodent is caressed or massaged by its caregiver, it may feel a drive to run about and jump for joy. However, the animal remains still, because it does not want to interrupt the rewarding petting. Hence, the motivational energy driving to move remains unexpressed and is discharged through tail-rattling. Similarly, when a male rodent tail-rattles during the initial phase of mating, this may be caused by the detection of a sexually arousing female, and a subsequent conflict arising from the fact that the sexual impulse to approach and mount the female is restrained by unfamiliarity with the female or by another source of hesitation, with the result that the animal remains still and tail-rattles. Indeed, considering altogether the different contexts in which rodents have been observed to rattle their tail, a high state of emotional arousal due to a motivational drive that remains untranslated into action appears to be the likeliest core emotional state underlying the expression of tail-rattling.

Our theory of tail-rattling does not imply that the usage of tail-rattling as a marker of mouse aggressiveness during agonistic encounters, which is currently the most widespread application of this phenotype across laboratories, should be discontinued. Mouse tail-rattling can and should be scored in agonistic encounters. However, when doing so, a set of issues should be kept in mind. First, tail-rattling and attacking behaviors are qualitatively different variables. Hence, they should not be summed to obtain a composite aggressiveness variable. Indeed, as we have seen, after manipulations such as exposure to a series of fight-winning experiences these two variables can dissociate, with attacks increasing and tail-rattling decreasing (Scott 1946, 1947). Second, while attacks reflect actuated aggressiveness, tail-rattling expresses unactuated aggressiveness. Third, while attacking behaviors are the purest behavioral markers of aggressiveness, in tail-rattling there is a mixture of aggressiveness together with an inhibitory drive (which can be, for instance, fear of the target or even, more simply, inaccessibility of the target). Finally, it should always be considered that tail-rattling is not specific to aggressive contexts, as it is observed in several other contexts. In fact, as we described in our discharge model, the origin of tail-rattling is not aggressiveness itself, but a more general and aspecific process. If these issues are kept in mind, tail-rattling remains an operatively useful variable to score in agonistic encounters, especially as a marker of unactuated aggressiveness, or of aggressiveness in the phase before it becomes actuated into an attack.

What is the function of rodent tail-rattling?

Is rodent tail-rattling a mere emotional expression, or does it additionally have an adaptive value? In other terms, can tail-rattling provide to the rattler an increase of its fitness, for which this behavior has been selected by biological evolution? If so, what adaptive function could tail-rattling possibly have? In the cases where tail-rattling appears to be non-adaptive, could it still have a function? If so, what could this function be? Up to now, several functions have been proposed for tail-rattling. In this section, we examine what the currently available knowledge about the contexts in which tail-rattling is performed suggests about the possible functions of tail-rattling. We then provide our hypotheses regarding the evolutionary origin of tail-rattling. Moreover, we subdivide tail-rattling into adaptive and non-adaptive. Finally, we explain why even non-adaptive tail-rattling can be functional.

Rodent tail-rattling may have multiple functions, depending on the context

The oldest proposed function for rodent tail-rattling is that of threat. Basically, in this view, tail-rattling is a warning signal emitted by the rattler to deter the receiver from launching an attack against the rattler. This is the functional interpretation given for the tail-rattling of the first rodent species in which this behavior was observed. For instance, in the 1830s, in an article entirely devoted to the porcupine by an anonymous naturalist simply known as “M”, we can read: “There is, however, still another sort of furniture peculiar to the tail of the porcupine, and bearing no very distant analogy to the apparatus which terminates that of the rattle-snake; it consists of a number of hollow, open, dry quills, elevated upon very slender stalks, which vibrate with every motion; these it clashes together when irritated, so as to produce a loud rustling noise, as a warning that farther aggression may be dangerous” (M, 1833). Analogously, according to George John Wood the porcupine’s rattling makes horses “flee in terror” (Wood 1859), while according to Charles Darwin the porcupines perform tail-rattling “to give warning to their enemy” so that the rattlers “would thus escape being attacked” (Darwin 1872).

Scott, who was the first to study systematically mouse tail-rattling, attributed to the tail-rattling of mice the same threat function that had been proposed for the tail-rattling of porcupines. According to Scott, “tail rattling is analogous to the warning sounds such as growling29 and spitting30” (Scott 1947). Scott suggested that “the process of positive and negative learning in male mice makes possible situations in which an animal can make no adaptive reactions” (Scott 1951). As a protection against this possibility, there is a mechanism which produces involuntary automatic external reactions which may act as “warning signals” to other animals (Scott, 1951). Importantly, according to Scott, tail-rattling “suggests a signal conveying a warning or threat, similar to growling in carnivores, but there is no evidence that other mice react to it in this way” (Scott 1966). In particular, “there was no evidence that inexperienced mice have any innate reaction to these behavior signals” (Scott 1947), but “other mice might learn to interpret this behavior as a warning” of a potential attack (Scott 1951), after that they have experienced tail-rattling followed by an attack from the rattler. Basically, according to Scott, tail-rattling has a communicative function, but this is not innate. The receiver’s reaction to the signal of the rattler is not instinctual, but learned through a previous association between the conspicuous stimulus (tail-rattling) and an aversive situation (being attacked). After this learning, tail-rattling can generate fear and hence work as a deterrent.

In the 1970s, Sonja Haber and Edward Clemens Simmel challenged Scott’s view that mouse tail-rattling has a threat function, or any communicative function at all (Haber 1975; Haber and Simmel 1976a, 1976b). According to Haber and Simmel, the answer to the question about what is the function of tail-rattling is the simplest of all: it has no function and it is exclusively an expression of stress. Haber and Simmel performed a series of observations in order to understand if the relationship between tail-rattling and agonistic behavior was causal or simply coincidental (Haber and Simmel 1976b). In particular, in dyadic social tests, they recorded the behaviors preceding and following an episode of fighting, as well as the ones preceding and following tail-rattling. Their results showed that, when mice tail-rattled in the test, the likelihood that the receivers responded with tail-rattling or fighting was exactly the same. Moreover, considering the cases of fighting, they were preceded and followed by an equal proportion of tail-rattling. No correlation between tail-rattling and fighting was found. Additionally, Haber and Simmel observed that mice also tail-rattled in non-social situations, in particular when mice were tested alone in a novel environment (an empty arena), when mice had their home-cages changed and cleaned, and when some mice were single-caged immediately after the weaning at 3 weeks of age (Haber and Simmel 1976b). Notably, in all these four conditions in which tail-rattling was observed, the mice were exposed to a source of stress (in the dyadic encounters: interaction with an unfamiliar individual; in the empty arena test: neophobia towards a novel unfamiliar environment; during cage change: removal of the familiar odor marks of the old cage; after weaning: maternal separation and social isolation). The fact that the tail-rattling in the social interactions was uncorrelated with fighting and that mice tail-rattled even when alone, led Haber and Simmel to believe that tail-rattling was not a threat signal and was not used for social communication. On the other hand, the fact that all the situations in which the mice tail-rattled were stressful, led Haber and Simmel to hypothesize that tail-rattling was actually an expression of stress, which was supported by their finding of an inverse correlation between tail-rattling and the count of leukocytes in the blood (considered a physiological marker of stress): “It appears then, that tail rattling is most likely an involuntary behavior of the mouse which is elicited by stressful stimuli, as suggested by our leukocyte measures. The threshold for tail rattling is affected by the type and intensity of the stimulation. It is the relationship between stress and fighting, rather than that between fighting and tail rattling, which is probably responsible for the frequent observation of this type of behavior in agonistic situations. Moreover, we have observed that tail rattling is not specific to agonistic behavior and the sequential analysis demonstrates that this form of response is not responded to by other mice in any communicative sense” (Haber and Simmel 1976b). Criticizing Scott’s functional interpretation, Haber and Simmel regarded that mouse tail-rattling was “an example of a misused phenotype” (Haber and Simmel 1976a), as it actually “appears to be incidental to agonistic behavior, serving neither a causal nor a communicative function” (Haber and Simmel 1976b).

Nevertheless, while Haber and Simmel were refuting Scott’s functional interpretation of tail-rattling, part of their results actually supported Scott’s view. In fact, Scott had clearly specified that he believed that, while tail-rattling itself is innate, the communicative function of tail-rattling is acquired through learning from previous experiences that have led to an association between tail-rattling and fighting. Haber and Simmel tested, in a dyadic social test with an unfamiliar mouse, a group of mice reared in pairs and a group reared in isolation (Haber and Simmel 1976b). Strikingly, in the social test the pair-reared group showed over three times as much the tail-rattling of the isolation-reared group. Since only the pair-reared group could have had the chance to learn a socially acquired communicative value of tail-rattling (for example, by associating the tail-rattling of another mouse with an attack from the rattler, or by learning to respond to tail-rattling with other tail-rattling), this finding actually supports Scott’s hypothesis.

If we enlarge our view to include also non-agonistic social contexts, we can find further evidence that tail-rattling may have a communicative function, but we can also understand that tail-rattling may be more than a simple threat display. As we have seen, rodent tail-rattling also occurs during sexual interactions. In at least four very different rodent species, the male tail-rattles at the female before mating (dusky-footed woodrat, European water vole, green acouchi and cane rat: Donat 1933; Frank 1956; Morris 1962; Ewer 1969; Kleiman 1971; Wallen 1977, 1982; Dial 1978, 1987). In this context, it can be hypothesized that tail-rattling has a courtship function. Moreover, in black rats, females display tail-rattling to males before mating with them (Ewer 1971; Estep et al. 1978). Generally, during black rat mating, a series of copulation acts is observed (Ewer 1971). After the first copulation act, the male and the female separate and groom themselves. Shortly after, the female appears to try to attract the attention of the male by running and stopping. If the male is unresponsive, the female may begin tail-rattling, then approach herself the male and start grooming him (Ewer 1971). Tail-rattling in female black rats exposed to a male is performed together with a series of proceptive behaviors (including ear wiggling, approaches to the male, and subsequent rapid darting and stopping a few centimeters away), which precede the receptive behavior, i.e., the lordosis behavior (Estep et al. 1978). It hence appears that female black rats employ tail-rattling as a proceptive signal towards the male, i.e., as a stimulus that encourages a sexual approach by the male.

Importantly, if we observe tail-rattling in the laboratory, it may be difficult to identify the function of this behavior, or it may even appear that this behavior has no function at all, due to the fact that the context of the laboratory is often oversimplified. However, if we consider the richness of the environmental and social contexts in which tail-rattling is performed in nature by free-living animals, it becomes easier to understand the possible functions of tail-rattling. As we have seen, in several laboratory paradigms (such as St. John’s test, cued fear conditioning, contextual fear conditioning and passive avoidance), mice tail-rattle after electric shocks, even if they are alone. Observed in the laboratory, this fear-mediated response may appear without scope. Nevertheless, if we consider the expression of fear-induced tail-rattling in the natural environment of the animals, then its function can become clearer. For instance, if a mouse feels menaced by the appearance of an unfamiliar conspecific or a terrestrial predator, and performs tail-rattling when its tail is under some dried leaves, the movement of the leaves and the sound of this movement may resemble the approach of another animal, and hence cause a moment of distraction in the opponent, which could provide to the rattler an advantage usable for a surprise attack or for escape. In this case, tail-rattling would be a form of behavioral deception, which is defined as performing a behavior that sends false information or withholds true information, in a way that is beneficial to the sender and detrimental to the receiver (d’Isa et al. 2024a). Tail-rattling would hence be an adaptive behavior, as it would enhance the rattler’s chances of survival in dangerous encounters. As another example, if we consider fear-induced tail-rattling to a predator performed when the rattler is in a social group of conspecifics, another possible function of tail-rattling may emerge. For instance, when mice or rats are in a group, if the individual closest to an approaching predator (such as a fox) detects the animal and, out of fear, rattles its tail, this tail-rattling may serve as an alarm signal for the mates of the rattler. In this situation, it may even be that the rattler is caught by the predator, but the more distant mates that received the rattler’s signal would have been alerted by the sound source in time for escape, and would have a greater probability of evading the predator. In this case, tail-rattling would not provide an advantage to the rattling individual but to its species. This alarm-signaling function of tail-rattling was first proposed in the 1930s by Donat Wood when commenting on the tail-rattling of dusky-footed woodrats, which according to her “may be a warning sound to spread alarm to other rats” (Donat 1933).

It hence appears that rodent tail-rattling may have multiple possible functions, depending on the context where a given species displays this behavior. In addition to considering the environmental and social richness of the natural context in which tail-rattling is performed in the wild, expanding the number of rodent species that we are considering can be equally helpful to recognize a possible adaptive function of tail-rattling. Indeed, a comparative approach to the study of animal behavior (Abramson 2023; d’Isa and Abramson 2023), in which a same behavior is compared across different species, can be useful to better understand the nature and function of that behavior. One scientist that was in the position to adopt a comparative approach to understand tail-rattling is Donald Ewer, who after having studied natural sciences at the University of Cambridge and having performed research at the University of Birmingham, moved to Africa in 1946, at first at the University of Natal31 in Pietermaritzburg (South Africa) and then at the University of Ghana in Accra (Ghana) (Baker 2013). This allowed Ewer to have knowledge of tail-rattling not only in laboratory mice, but also in more diverse rodents such as the porcupine, the cane rat and the black rat (Ewer 1968, 1969, 1971).

In fact, Ewer was the first to propose a complete theory of the function of rodent tail-rattling. In particular, Ewer noticed that rodent tail-rattling is performed in situations with completely different valence (such as agonism or mating) and this led him to hypothesize that, in a primitive stage of phylogenetic evolution, tail-rattling originally arose as a simple response to a high level of generic excitation, with no signaling function, but subsequently it was selected, species by species, for its adaptiveness in diverse contexts. Ewer effectively outlined this view in the early 1970s: “In many rodents tail vibration acts as a threat, as in the mouse and in a highly ritualized form in porcupines. In others it is used by the male in courtship […]. In order to account for these two conflicting uses of a similar signal, it seems necessary to postulate a primitive condition in which tail vibration had not acquired signal value but occurred in situations where the general level of excitation was high—as would be the case both in hostile encounters and in courtship. It could then have been elaborated independently a number of times either as a threat or as a courtship signal. The situation in R. rattus comes very close to this postulated phylogenetic starting point. Tail vibration has no detectable signal value and it occurs in situations associated either with agonistic encounters or with mating. These are, however, situations of frustration, rather than of conflict. In agonistic encounters tail vibration is performed not by the animal hesitating to attack, but by the victor whose opponent has vanished while in the sexual context it is shown not by the male, hesitating to approach a possibly aggressive female, but by the female, awaiting mounting. The rattus situation could obviously provide the starting point for ritualization of tail vibration into a copulation invitation signal from female to male” (Ewer 1971). Basically, according to Ewer, tail-rattling originated as a mere expression of emotional arousal, but it acquired an adaptive function due to the reactions of the other animals perceiving it. Up to present, this appears to be the most comprehensive and accurate functional explanation of tail-rattling.

In accordance with Ewer, we believe that, since in different rodent species tail-rattling appears to have different functions (for example, only courtship or only threat), it is likely that these species inherited this behavior from a species where the emission of tail-rattling was associated with a generic state of high arousal, and probably provided multiple adaptive advantages, depending on the context (for example, displaying tail-rattling both during courtship and as threat). Then, in specific descendant species, one type of context-specific tail-rattling could have been reinforced by selection (positive selection) over all the other contexts, while the emission of tail-rattling in the other contexts would have faced deselection (negative selection), likely as a way to increase the impact of the signal in one specific context. This process would have led to situations like that of the porcupine, which conspicuously rattles the tail (producing a loud sound through the clashing of specialized hard quills) as a threatening behavior but not during courtship, and to specular situations like that of rodent species which selectively vibrate the tail during courtship, such as the green acouchi, where males employ a tail-down vibration to court females and have additionally developed an even more conspicuous tail-up wagging as courtship signal (Morris 1962). The black rat would be an example of species that remained in the original situation of the ancestor, as it shows tail-rattling in both agonistic and mating contexts.

Considering the very high number of rodent species displaying tail-rattling (32), it seems unlikely that this situation could be the result of multiple and totally independent convergent evolution events. Rather, the degree with which tail-rattling is shared across rodent species suggests that this behavior could have been present in a common ancestor. Moreover, from the great genetic distance among the tail-rattling species (19 genera), it can be hypothesized that the ancestor from which these rodent species inherited the tail-rattling behavior must have been a particularly early rodent ancestor. Tail-rattling is present in rodent suborders as distant as Hystricomorpha (e.g., crested porcupines, green acouchis, greater cane rats) and Myomorpha (e.g., mice, rats, voles, gerbils, etc.). Since these suborders diverged from their common ancestor around 61.4 million years ago (Niimura et al. 2024), it is possible that tail-rattling is as old as this, if not even earlier.

Adaptiveness vs. functionality of tail-rattling: both adaptive and non-adaptive tail-rattling can be functional

A behavior is adaptive if it increases the performer’s fitness, that is its likelihood to survive or to procreate. On the other hand, a behavior is functional if it provides any sort of advantage to the performer. Consequently, all adaptive behaviors are also functional. Conversely, not all functional behaviors are necessarily adaptive. Is tail-rattling adaptive? Is it functional? Is it both adaptive and functional, or just functional?

It is possible to recognize four major contexts in which tail-rattling can be adaptive (Fig. 7). The adaptiveness of tail-rattling can be recognized by the behavior of the receiver of the signal. If tail-rattling is produced during agonism and the receiver flees, then tail-rattling can avoid fights which could cause injuries or death (Fig. 7, first row). For the rodent species displaying tail-rattling in one of the described contexts, tail-rattling is clearly adaptive, and consequently functional, with the function being, respectively, threat, male courtship, female proceptive signaling and alarm signaling. However, in many rodent species, including the common house mouse, often no apparent behavioral response is seen in the receiver. This raises doubts about the actual adaptiveness of tail-rattling in these species. Nevertheless, even in species where no reaction at all is displayed by the receiver (i.e., where tail-rattling is not a communication signal), or when tail-rattling is displayed in absence of a receiver, according to our discharge model it is still possible to identify a potential function for tail-rattling (i.e., an advantage for the performer): stress reduction. For example, we have seen that when mice are in the lit chamber of the passive avoidance, which induces photophobia (light-induced anxiety) in mice, and they must refrain from exiting the lit compartment because of possible electric shocks in the adjacent dark compartment, they rattle the tail. Remaining in the lit chamber leads to an increase of light-induced anxiety, which continues to motivate the animal to escape. The increasing drive to escape, remaining unexpressed, may cause further discomfort in the animal. In such a situation, discharging this motor drive through tail-rattling instead of through escape behavior, could reduce the drive and hence the discomfort. In this situation, tail-rattling would therefore have a stress-reduction or relieving function, which would be beneficial to the animal, even in the absence of communicative functions of tail-rattling.

Even in contexts associated with a positive affective value, non-communicative tail-rattling could have a stress avoidance effect. According to our discharge model, the pet rats which display tail-rattling when they are being caressed, emit this behavior as a discharge of the unexpressed motivational drive to run, which is inhibited by the rat in order to avoid losing the rewarding treatment. In this case, the rat’s tail-rattling may prevent possible stress coming from holding unexpressed the locomotor drive. However, it is important to underline that, even hypothesizing stress reduction as a possible function of non-communicative tail-rattling, the tail-rattling performed in these situations cannot be considered adaptive. For instance, when a caressed rat rattles its tail, this may indeed prevent the possible stress arising from the inhibition of the locomotor drive. Nevertheless, this isolated case of avoidance of a brief stress does not appear to have the potential to make a difference between the life and the death of the animal (i.e., an effect on the animal’s survival) or to increase its chances of reproduction. Even considering collectively the summed stress-reduction effect of all the tail-rattling episodes of a lifetime, it is still unlikely that tail-rattling could have any possible effect on the fitness of the animal, especially if we consider how rarely tail-rattling is performed by the species where tail-rattling does not have a clear communicative function. Therefore, in the species where tail-rattling is non-communicative, this behavior appears to be non-adaptive. However, even if tail-rattling does not increase the animal’s fitness, it provides an immediate benefit to the animal, and the behavior can hence be considered functional. Why non-adaptive tail-rattling cannot be considered a displacement behavior

Some features of non-adaptive tail-rattling (no effect on fitness, being performed apparently out of context, and stress reduction) may make it appear a displacement behavior. The first to propose the idea that tail-rattling could have originated from a displacement behavior was Ewer in the late 1960s (Ewer 1968). Nevertheless, we will explain why non-adaptive tail-rattling cannot be considered a displacement behavior and why it could rather be considered a vestigial behavior. Displacement behaviors and vestigial behaviors should not be confused.

Displacement behaviors are normally appropriate behaviors that, in response to stress or overarousal, are performed in an inappropriate context (Tinbergen 1939, 1940, 1952; Tinbergen and van Iersel 1947; Armstrong 1947, 1949a, 1949b, 1950; Bastock et al. 1953; Zeigler 1964; Delius 1967). More specifically, a “displacement activity is the performance of a behaviour-pattern, usually in an incomplete, eccentric, or imperfectly orientated form, out of the functional context of appetites and behaviour-patterns to which it is normally related, as a consequence of tension and the resultant deflexion” (Armstrong 1950). Basically, a behavior X is relocated from a context A, where that behavior is adaptive and functional, to a context B, where that behavior is non-adaptive and often dysfunctional. This behavior is defined “displacement32 behavior” because it is thought to be driven not by energy of its own but by displaced energy that belonged to a different action (behavior Y) that could not be expressed (Tinbergen 1952; Kortmulder 1998). An example is a male three-spined stickleback that, in the middle of the courtship towards a female, suddenly leaves the female, goes to the nest and starts displaying fanning behavior (which normally serves the function of ventilating the eggs), even if in the nest there are no eggs to ventilate, indicating a misplacement of parental behavior into a courtship context (Troisi 2002). Other examples of displacement behaviors are food deprivation-induced self-grooming in mice (Dietrich et al. 2015), threat-induced nose-licking in dogs (Pedretti et al. 2023) and stress-induced scratching in non-human primates (Troisi 2002).

On the other hand, vestigial behaviors are behaviors that were adaptive in an ancestor species and have become non-adaptive in a descendant species, becoming weaker as a result of a progressive negative selection (Rayner et al. 2022). An example is piloerection in humans, which has been inherited from an ancestral primate that had fur and for which piloerection in fear contexts had an adaptive value because it could deter opponents from attacking, by making the piloerected animal appear bigger. The concept of displacement is based on an intraspecies evaluation of behavior (the behavior in context A vs. the behavior in context B), while vestigiality is based on an interspecies evaluation (the behavior in the ancestor vs. the behavior in the descendant). From the point of view of the adaptiveness, both displacement and vestigial behaviors are non-adaptive (meaning that they do not increase the fitness of the animal). However, from the point of view of the functionality (i.e., providing any form of advantage to the animal), both displacement and vestigial behaviors can be dysfunctional, neutral or even functional. For instance, in some cases, both displacement and vestigial behavior are functional when they reduce the level of stress of the animal, even though these behaviors do not increase the chances of survival or reproduction of the animal.

From the scientific evidence that we have described up to here, we can understand that tail-rattling is an instinctual reaction and that this behavior is inherited. In species where tail-rattling is non-adaptive (non-communicative), it is not possible to identify a context A where the behavior is adaptive and a context B where the behavior is non-adaptive. It is hence not possible to say that tail-rattling is the result of a displacement, as there is no misplacement of an adaptive behavior into a non-adaptive context. Nevertheless, non-adaptive tail-rattling is inherited. If this behavior is not adaptive, and hence negatively selected by evolution, how is it possible that it is still inherited? The most reasonable answer is that non-adaptive tail-rattling has been inherited from an ancestor where tail-rattling was adaptive. In the descendant species, tail-rattling lost its adaptive value and started to undergo a negative selection. The reason for the persistence of this behavior is simply the slowness of the process of the negative selection. Interestingly, it has been noted how vestigial behaviors tend to persist longer than morphological traits which have lost their adaptiveness (Rayner et al. 2022). Therefore, in the species where at present tail-rattling is non-adaptive, this behavior persists, but as a vestigial behavior currently undergoing deselection. The fact that, in these species, tail-rattling is generally rarer, weaker and less conspicuous than in species where tail-rattling is adaptive supports the idea of the vestigiality of this behavior.

Remarkably, while non-adaptive tail-rattling cannot be considered a displacement behavior, it may share its same function. Indeed, the German ethologist and comparative psychologist Juan Delius (1936-living), a pupil of Tinbergen, proposed the idea that displacement behaviors originate from a potentially stressful over-arousal, and that their function is de-arousal (Delius 1967, 1970): “There is little doubt, however, that the behavioural situations leading to displacement, that is, conflict, frustration and thwarting, are effective in increasing arousal. I suggest that the occurrence of at least some displacement activities is the reflexion of a homeostatic process operating towards cancelling the arousal increment so generated, through the activation of an arousal inhibiting system” (Delius 1967). This system is defined by Delius as “a neural de-arousal system” (Delius 1967). Analogously, according to our discharge model, non-adaptive tail-rattling is functional to de-arousal, by reducing or preventing stress from the over-arousal which arises from the impossibility of actuating a motivational drive which is pushing the animal to perform an action that cannot be performed.

Future directions in tail-rattling research

As we have mentioned, observation of tail-rattling in the laboratory offers limited possibilities of scientific understanding of this behavior, as the standard context of the laboratory is oversimplified and it lacks most of the elements that in nature provide adaptive value to tail-rattling. On the other hand, studying tail-rattling in the wild is particularly difficult, as tail-rattling is a short-lasting behavior which is elicited by very particular circumstances, which occur in unpredictable moments. Differently from feeding and sleeping, tail-rattling is not a behavior that can be reliably observed daily and at easily predictable time-points. In order to solve this issue, two main research approaches can be adopted, one trying to make the laboratory more similar to nature and the other trying to make nature more similar to the laboratory.

The first approach is based on the use of seminatural environments. In this seminaturalistic approach, contrarily to conventional laboratory testing where the animals are evaluated in testing sessions of just few minutes, the behavior of the animals is monitored continuously 24 h per day. Moreover, the animals are assessed not in an external and unfamiliar testing space, but in their living space. Finally, the animals are not housed in standard home-cages, but in large rooms that comprise elements present in the natural environments of the hosted species and hence resemble its habitat in the wild: the seminatural environments. Notably, these seminatural environments promote the expression of a wider range of natural behaviors of the species. As an example, Donat Wood who pioneered the use of this setting in the 1930s, noticed that the dusky-footed woodrat would not breed in captivity if kept in breeding cages, but she managed to obtain successful breeding in captivity when she housed the animals in a seminatural environment (Donat 1933). Donat Wood could also observe tail-rattling in this environment, for example during the male’s approach to the female (Donat 1933). From the 2010s, the Norwegian behavioral neuroscientist Anders Ågmo, from the University of Tromsø (Norway), and his team have extensively used seminatural environments to study rat socio-sexual behaviors, strongly underlining the usefulness of this paradigm for rodent behavioral research (Chu and Ågmo 2014, 2015a, 2015b; Bergheim et al. 2015; Snoeren et al. 2015; Chu et al. 2015, 2017; Le Moëne and Ågmo 2018; Houwing et al. 2019; Le Moëne et al. 2019, 2020; Heinla et al. 2021; Sylte et al. 2021; Hernández-Arteaga and Ågmo 2023). Recently, researchers from the same team have employed seminatural environments also for the study of mouse social behavior (Le Moëne and Larsson 2024). By video-monitoring 24/7 the behavior of rodents hosted in seminatural environments, it would be possible to detect, over a long period of time, all the tail-rattling occurrences emerging from the spontaneous social interactions and the daily situations of the animals. The ethological relevance of seminatural environment testing can be further boosted if, in addition to domesticated rodent strains, wild-caught rodents are also employed as study subjects. By using wild-caught rodents for breeding, new wild-mimicking rodent strains can be created, such as the Warsaw Wild Captive Pisula Stryjek (WWCPS) rats (Stryjek and Pisula 2008). Indeed, the behavior of the rodent strains created by humans through extensive artificial selection can be distorted and not representative of the natural behavior of the species, while wild-mimicking rodents can model more faithfully the natural ethogram. Evident mismatches between the behavior of domesticated rodents and their wild counterparts are present (for example, see: Bolles and Woods 1964; Lockard 1968; Barnett and Smart 1975; Calisi and Bentley 2009; Stryjek and Modlinska 2016; Modlinska and Stryjek 2016; Stryjek et al. 2018, 2021a; Kondrakiewicz et al. 2019; Bárdos et al. 2024; more generally, for a review of the differences between the cognitive abilities of domesticated animals and their wild counterparts, see Bessa Ferreira et al. 2023). The case of tail-rattling is exemplar in this sense, as in a wild Rattus species (the black rat Rattus rattus) tail-rattling is present in both agonistic and appetitive contexts (Ewer 1971), while in the domesticated Rattus (Rattus norvegicus forma domestica, which is the species of the laboratory rat and the pet rats) it is present in appetitive contexts but absent (or rare) during agonism. In particular, there is extensive documentation by pet owners that pet rats exhibit tail-rattling when they are being caressed by a human with whom they have formed a strong affective bond, which is an appetitive situation. On the other hand, in laboratory rats exposed to intermale agonistic situations, tail-rattling responses have almost never been reported (differently from mice exposed to the same paradigm), indicating a surprising absence of agonistic tail-rattling (Scott and Fredericson 1951; Scott 1966). Occasionally, laboratory rats exposed to agonistic situations may display, instead of the rapid tail-rattling, a different tail behavior, which can be called tail-swishing, characterized by slow and ample arch movements of the tail from one side to the other. Agonistic tail-swishing has been observed in both female and male laboratory rats (female-female agonism: Adams and Boice 1983; male-male agonism: Farah Naquiah et al. 2016). However, up to present, agonistic tail-swishing remains a very rarely reported behavior in the laboratory rat.

The second approach is based on freely accessible (and exitable) observation chambers placed in nature (Stryjek et al. 2021a, 2021b, 2024a, 2024b, Parsons et al. 2023a, 2023b; Skorupska et al. 2024; Kurek et al. 2026). Through this approach it is possible to record behaviors: 1) which are spontaneous and have a high ethological relevance; 2) from rodents that are living in their natural habitat; 3) from rodents that are wild-born and have acquired all their life experiences in the wild; 4) from rodent species that are not found in laboratories; 5) with a long-term 24/7 monitoring. Importantly, this method is not limited to the study of individual behaviors and, when two or more animals are present in the chamber, intraspecies and interspecies social interactions can also be assessed. Such an approach allows observation of natural behaviors that are very difficult or impossible to evoke in the laboratory, and may even lead to discover previously unknown behaviors. An example is tail-belting (a thermoconservative behavior in which the tail is wrapped transversely around the back when the temperature is sub-zero), first described in the yellow-necked field mouse Apodemus flavicollis and in the black-striped field mouse Apodemus agrarius thanks to this recording method (Stryjek et al. 2021b, 2024b). Another example is deceptive dodging (a highly flexible escape behavior suggestive of tactical deception), first observed in black-striped field mice through the same method (d’Isa et al. 2024a). This naturalistic approach could also be useful to determine the effects of different natural environmental factors on tail-rattling. For instance, researchers could explore whether there is a seasonality in the propensity to tail-rattle (e.g., whether rodents tail-rattle with a different likelihood in spring compared to winter). Additionally, since most nocturnal rodents show moonlight avoidance (d’Isa 2025d), which suggests that they fear to roam out of the burrow in moon-lit nights, researchers could investigate if, during moon-lit nights, encounters with unfamiliar animals lead to an increased probability and/or duration of tail-rattling.

Another potentially helpful approach for tail-rattling research is the employment of robotic animals. The usefulness of robotic animals in rodent behavioral research has recently been highlighted (d’Isa 2025c). In classical social tests, a subject animal is exposed to a stimulus animal and the behavior of the subject animal is scored. Notably, if a robotic rodent is employed, its behavior can be totally under the control of the experimenter. It would hence be possible to make the stimulus animal produce tail-rattling at will. Moreover, the experimenter could decide all the parameters related to tail-rattling, including the duration, the intensity, the rhythm of the vibration and the number of rattling episodes in a given time period. The reactions of the subject animal to different types of types of tail-rattling could be studied, allowing researchers to dissect the communicative function of each feature of the rattling. Finally, instead of using pre-determined rattling programs, the tail-rattling of the stimulus animal could be adapted online by the experimenter during the testing session based on the tail-rattling response of the subject animal, as in a dialogue. An ethorobotic approach has already been employed to study a similar tail-behavior in squirrels. When California ground squirrels (Otospermophilus beecheyi) are exposed to a snake predator (such as the Western rattlesnake Crotalus oreganus or the gopher snake Pituophis catenifer), they show tail-flagging, a rapid and conspicuous waving of the erected tail (Hennessy et al. 1981; Swaisgood et al. 1999; Barbour and Clark 2012; Putman and Clark 2015). This snake-elicited tail-flagging has been interpreted as an alarm signal (Hennessy et al. 1981). An analogous tail-flagging has been reported for Eastern gray squirrels (Sciurus carolinensis) and chipmunks (Tamias striatus) in response to timber rattlesnakes (Crotalus horridus) (Clark 2005). A biomimetic robotic squirrel with a particularly realistic appearance, capable of tail-flagging and emitting alarm barks, has been used to study the reactions of free-living Eastern gray squirrels to these signals (Partan et al. 2010). The study found that the squirrels responded with alarm behaviors (such as tail-flagging, vocalization and tree ascension) to both the visual and the auditory unimodal signals. However, with multimodal signaling the responses were maximal. Compared to when the robotic squirrel was barking with the tail still, the situation where the robot barked while also tail-flagging elicited in the receivers considerably more tail-flagging and tree ascension responses.

Furthermore, for tail-rattling it would be useful to consider sources of information other than the laboratory and the field. In particular, pet owners can be a valuable additional source of information on tail-rattling. Indeed, humans co-living for years with an animal have incomparable possibilities of observing its behavior and can discover behaviors that are extremely unlikely to be observed in laboratory or field research. An exemplar case is rat eye-boggling, a rapid inward and outward vibration of the eye-balls that makes them appear pop in and out, which rats display during contentment-like states, for instance when being caressed by a caretaker (Neville et al. 2022; Borzello 2022; Schneidewind et al. 2024). Notably, in spite of the fact that rats have been extensively studied in laboratories worldwide, eye-boggling is virtually absent in laboratory reports. On the other hand, it is reported as a relatively common behavior by pet owners, and in a recent questionnaire owners of pet rats provided 8 on 10 as the most common rating for boggling frequency in their rats (Neville et al. 2022). In the case of tail-rattling, this behavior has surprisingly been observed in pet rats (Ducommun 2012; see also notes 26 and 27) but not in laboratory rats (Scott and Fredericson 1951; Scott 1966). Gathering information from pet owners could expand the known range of contexts in which rodents produce tail-rattling, helping us to shed more light on the nature of this behavior.

Another valid source of information regarding tail-rattling are zoos, which often host rodent species that come from distant areas of the world and are very rarely found elsewhere. Collecting information from zoo-housed animals could expand our knowledge about the range of rodent species that display tail-rattling. Moreover, in zoos tail-rattling could also be adopted as a behavioral marker for animal welfare. Indeed, if video cameras are placed in the enclosure of the animals and computer vision is instructed to automatically recognize aversive tail-rattling distinguishing it from appetitive tail-rattling, it would be possible to have a daily scoring of tail-rattling frequency. A certain threshold could be set and when the artificial intelligence program detects a frequency of aversive tail-rattling over that threshold, this would trigger an alarm sent to the caregivers, so that they could search for possible disturbances to the animals (for example, a machine next to the enclosure which is producing a disturbing sound) and remove them. Basically, setting tail-rattling as a behavioral parameter for artificial intelligence analysis could allow a machine learning-based program for automated welfare monitoring in zoo-housed rodents.

Remarkably, tail-rattling could become a welfare indicator not only in zoos, but also in laboratories, in particular in paradigms employing long-term continuous video-monitoring of the living space of the animals, as in home-cage behavioral monitoring and in seminatural environment monitoring. Indeed, the interest for the welfare of the animals with which humans interact has been progressively growing from the seventeenth century (for a history of the early animal welfare stances see Sect. 5.1 of d’Isa and Abramson 2025). In present-day scientific research, it has been strongly underlined how the maximation of animal welfare in behavioral testing, and the adoption of the so-called animal-friendly behavioral tests, is important both from an ethical point of view, which cannot accept avoidable suffering in the tested animals, and to ensure the achievement of valid scientific results (d’Isa and Gerlai 2023; d’Isa et al. 2024b). In fact, while an ethological approach in behavioral testing can help to maximize animal welfare (d’Isa and Gerlai 2023), the opposite is also true: maximation of animal welfare in behavioral testing can lead to obtain more ethologically-relevant findings.

An intriguing question is whether tail-rattling across different contexts is phenotypically always the same tail-rattling or not. An analysis of the kinesiological microstructure of tail-rattling across contexts could help to identify a set of context-specific subcomponents of the motor sequence. It may be discovered, for instance, that the tail-rattling produced in appetitive situations is slightly different from the tail-rattling in agonistic or fear-related contexts. If this is found, it would be possible to distinguish the different emotional contexts underlying tail-rattling by using these subcomponents as proxies. Additionally, the temporal relationships between the subcomponents could equally help to distinguish between different emotional contexts. For example, van Abeelen found that, in agonistic encounters, the tail-rattling of dominant and submissive mice shows a slight phenotypical difference: while the first is characterized by “a very rapid undulation of the tail”, the latter is “a stiff vibration” (van Abeelen 1965). Such a fine spatio-temporal analysis of the tail-rattling movement could be performed by examining videos obtained through high-speed cameras, that is cameras capable of recording at extremely high frame rates. Moreover, recent computer vision systems based on unsupervised machine learning pipelines, such as MoSeq, allow automatic identification of the modules of movement (the “syllables”), and classify behaviors based on the way that these syllables are flexibly placed into sequences (Lin et al. 2024; Ritter et al. 2025). Such motion sequencing methods could be employed to analyze vast amounts of recordings and identify different subtypes of tail-rattling.

Finally, modern neuroscience techniques can help to uncover the neural underpinnings of tail-rattling. Chemogenetic and optogenetic techniques can be used to selectively activate or inhibit specific brain areas and then observe the effect on tail-rattling. Actually, these techniques have already been employed successfully in studies assessing tail-rattling in mice, revealing that several neural structures play a key role in the production of tail-rattling, including the ventral thalamus (Salay et al. 2018), the prefrontal cortex (Salay et al. 2018), the lateral geniculate nucleus (Salay and Huberman 2021), the perifornical area of the hypothalamus (Autry et al. 2021), the ventromedial hypothalamus (Yang et al. 2017, 2023), the parabrachial nucleus (Kang et al. 2024), the melanopsin-containing ganglion cells of the retina (Lazzerini Ospri et al. 2024), the amygdala (Yamao and Matsui 2025) and the periaqueductal gray (Barcelon et al. 2026; Davis et al. 2026). In vivo brain electrophysiology in freely-moving rodents can also be a useful technique. Through a long-term monitoring that combines electrophysiological recording of brain activity with video recording of behavior (video-EEG), it would be possible to identify the brain activity before, during and after each tail-rattling event occurring over a long period of time, as is currently done for seizure events in mouse and rat models of epilepsy (for example: Baker et al. 2025; Liu and Gluckman 2026). Importantly, while wired electrophysiology features the presence of wires that could disturb the behavior of the studied subjects, implantable miniaturized telemetry systems now allow wireless long-term continuous electroencephalographic monitoring in small rodents (Zayachkivsky et al. 2015). Moreover, non-invasive wired electrophysiological recordings of brain activity with epidermal electrodes have recently been validated in both rats (Santangelo et al. 2018; Castoldi et al. 2018, 2020, 2021, 2022; d’Isa et al. 2020) and mice (Marenna et al. 2019, 2020, 2022). In the future, if technological improvements allow wireless non-invasive electrophysiological monitoring of brain activity through miniaturized skin-attachable sensors, it will be possible to record tail-rattling-associated brain activity with no disturbance at all to the rodents, in a way that would be fully respectful of the physical integrity and the welfare of the animals. This combination of non-invasiveness and wirelessness would at first be an improvement for research with laboratory rodents, but additionally it would possibly allow the employment of electrophysiological techniques even with zoo-housed rodents, providing unique insights into the cerebral functioning of rare rodent species.

In sum, up to present tail-rattling has been found in 32 rodent species from 19 genera, in a variety of contexts, ranging from agonism to mating. Several promising research approaches (including seminatural environments, observation chambers placed in nature, robotic animals and modern neuroscience techniques) can help us to expand our knowledge of the range of rodent species producing tail-rattling, of the number of different contexts eliciting this behavior, and of its neural substrates. Importantly, these research approaches can also be combined. For instance, robotic tail-rattling rodents could be placed in laboratory seminatural environments or even in observation chambers deployed in nature. Moreover, long-term electrophysiological monitoring could be performed while the animals are hosted in a seminatural environment comprising a rich social environment. Indeed, several research paradigms could be designed to deepen our understanding of a fascinating rodent behavior that has still many secrets to reveal.
