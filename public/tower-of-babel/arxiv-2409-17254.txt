Well-posedness of a first-order-in-time model for nonlinear acoustics with nonhomogeneous boundary conditions
Pascal Lehner

Abstract
We study well-posedness of a first-order-in-time model for nonlinear acoustics with nonhomogeneous boundary conditions in fractional Sobolev spaces. The analysis proceeds by first establishing well-posedness of an abstract parabolic-type semilinear evolution equation. These results are then applied to concrete operators and function spaces that capture the boundary conditions relevant for realistic modeling. Our approach is based on the spectral decomposition of a positive definite self-adjoint operator, with solution regularity characterized via the domains of its fractional powers. Employing Galerkin’s method and the Newton-Kantorovich theorem, we prove well-posedness for the abstract nonlinear system with possibly nonhomogeneous boundary data. The connection between (spectral) fractional powers of the Laplacian and fractional Sobolev spaces due to interpolation theory allows us to transfer these results to the nonlinear acoustic model under nonhomogeneous Dirichlet and Neumann boundary conditions, yielding fractional Sobolev regularity. For Hodge/Lions boundary conditions, we establish well-posedness with solutions in classical Sobolev spaces of integer order.

1.1
Motivation

The motivation to study system (
1
) under the various boundary conditions (
2a
) - (
2d
) stems from the results established in
[
11
]
. The present work can be viewed as a continuation of the research direction initiated in
[
11
]
,
where the authors study well-posedness of the coupled nonlinear PDE

\begin{split}\partial_{t}p+(1+\gamma)\nabla\cdot v-\zeta\Delta p+\epsilon_{1}p\nabla\cdot v+\epsilon_{2}\nabla p\cdot v&=p_{f}\\
\partial_{t}v+(1+\delta)\nabla p-\mu\Delta v-\frac{\epsilon_{3}}{2}\nabla(p^{2})+\frac{\epsilon_{4}}{2}\nabla(v\cdot v)&=v_{f}\\
p(\cdot,0)=p_{0},\quad v(\cdot,0)&=v_{0}\\
p=0,\quad v&=0\text{ on }\partial\Omega\end{split}

with
\epsilon_{1},...,\epsilon_{4}>0
, and
\gamma,\delta\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
sufficiently small given,
see
[
11
, Eq. (13)]
.
Equation (
1.1
) can be interpreted as a reduced model derived from the Navier–Stokes–Fourier system. It serves as an approximation for nonlinear acoustic wave propagation in a viscous fluid, capturing the interaction between local fluctuations in pressure
p
and the velocity field
v
, while not considering thermal and density variations. For a detailed derivation of (
1.1
) from fundamental physical principles, including the conservation of mass, momentum, and energy, as well as thermodynamic laws, we refer the reader to
[
11
, Section 2]
.

To place equation (
1.1
) in the context of wave equations and highlight its relevance to nonlinear acoustics, we note that it can be interpreted as a nonlinear, damped extension of the hyperbolic linear wave equation

\begin{split}\partial_{t}p+\nabla\cdot v=p_{f}\\
\partial_{t}v+\nabla p=v_{f}.\end{split}

Equation (
1.1
) extends this linear system by incorporating nonlinear terms and viscous dissipation, thereby enabling the modeling of high-intensity acoustic phenomena in viscous fluids.
In fact, the damping parameters

\zeta,\mu
are typically small in the context of nonlinear acoustics, so that the wave like behavior induced by the operator pair

(\nabla\cdot,\nabla)^{\top}

dominates over the diffusive effects introduced by the Laplacian
-\Delta

Moreover, as observed in
[
11
, Remark 2.4]
, setting
\gamma=\delta=0
in equation (
1.1
) yields a model that can be viewed as an alternative first-order-in-time formulation of Kuznetsov’s equation. The latter is given by

\begin{split}\partial_{tt}p-c^{2}\Delta p-b\partial_{t}\Delta p-\varepsilon\frac{B}{2A}\partial_{tt}(p^{2})-\varepsilon\partial_{tt}(v\cdot v)&=0\\
\partial_{t}v+\textasciiacute\nabla p&=0\\
p(\cdot,0)=p_{0},\quad\partial_{t}p(\cdot,0)=p_{1},\quad v(\cdot,0)&=v_{0}\\
p&=0\text{ on }\partial\Omega\end{split}

and originally derived in
[
16
]
.
Here

denotes the wave speed,
b
is a damping parameter,
\varepsilon
a small parameter and
\frac{B}{A}
is the parameter of nonlinearity.
Kuznetsov’s equation is widely used in nonlinear acoustics as a canonical model for high-intensity sound propagation. For a physical introduction to nonlinear acoustics, we refer to
[
8
]
, while a comprehensive mathematical overview can be found in the review article
[
12
]
.

In contrast to the model (
1

[

,
22

denotes the space of functions having a bounded and uniformly continuous derivative.

Interest in first-order formulations stems largely from their practical advantages in the design of efficient numerical methods, particularly in the context of adaptive space-time discretizations. In the case of the linear acoustics equation (
1.1
), effectiveness has already been demonstrated as a foundation for discontinuous Galerkin methods, see
[

24

To employ equation (
1.1

1.1
) under a variety of more general and physically relevant boundary conditions, rather than restricting the analysis to the commonly used homogeneous Dirichlet conditions. The specific choices of boundary conditions considered in this work ranging from (
2a

Before proceeding, we comment on the choice of the parameters used to derive (
1

1.1
). First, we set
\epsilon_{1}=\lambda,\epsilon_{2}=1,\epsilon_{3}=1,\epsilon_{3}=1,\epsilon_{4}=1

, cf.
[

. In
[
11
]
the parameters
\epsilon_{1},\dots,\epsilon_{4}
are treated as placeholders, allowing for flexibility in the coefficients of the nonlinear terms. However, in this work, we choose to simplify the model by avoiding additional parameters and instead focus on the core structure of the equation relevant to nonlinear acoustics.
Second, we set
\gamma=\delta=0
, since, as noted earlier, Kuznetsov’s equation can be recovered from equation (

are related to entropy contributions, which are typically neglected in models of nonlinear acoustics. While the analysis presented here could, in principle, be extended to the case

1.2
Contributions

First, this paper extends the results of
[
11
]
by proving existence of
H^{1+s}(\Omega)
regular solutions in space for

in the case of nonhomogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions, see Theorem

.
Compared to
[

]
, the approach presented here accommodates slightly less regular initial data

and right-hand sides

. An immediate consequence is that, for the limiting case

solutions that are unbounded are not necessarily excluded from a theoretic standpoint, since

only for

if

. To the author’s knowledge, fractional regularity results of this type have not been investigated for other equations in nonlinear acoustics.

Beyond the Dirichlet setting, this work also establishes well-posedness of equation (
1
) under nonhomogeneous Neumann and Hodge/Lions boundary conditions, see Theorems
3.2
,
3.3
, and
3.4
. The analysis of Hodge/Lions boundary conditions requires more delicate treatment, and in this case, we restrict to spatial dimension

and prove well-posedness for solutions with

regularity in space. To the best knowledge of the author, this is the first work to address Hodge/Lions boundary conditions in the context of nonlinear acoustics.

To obtain these results, we consider the more abstract formulations (
2
) and (
2
), which are semilinear parabolic evolution equations that have been extensively studied in the literature, see, for example,
[
17
,
20
]
.

For instance, well-posedness of (

) can be established using standard semigroup techniques, as presented in
[

,
26
]
. By applying classical semigroup theory, well-posedness of equation (

) follows under the condition

with

, see
[
26
, Section 8.3, Theorem 3.5]
. While there also exist results for less regular initial data, these typically require stronger assumptions on the right-hand side

, cf.
[
20
, Theorem 7.1.6]
, while in this work the right hand side can be any function in

, thereby including discontinuities in both space and time.

Alternatively, well-posedness of equation (

) can be shown via energy methods, as in
[
17
]
. However, the analysis in
[

]
only considers fractional Hölder regularity and does not address fractional Sobolev regularity. While fractional Sobolev regularity results are provided in
[
28
]
, those results are limited to the whole space

.
To the best knowledge of the author, a higher-order fractional Sobolev energy estimate on a bounded domain, combined with the specific type of nonlinearity considered here, has not yet been addressed in the literature.

1.3
Choice of boundary conditions

In the following, we briefly motivate our choice of Hodge/Lions boundary conditions for
v
instead of classical Neumann conditions on each component of

v
, as one might naturally impose for parabolic-type equations.

By Hodge/Lions boundary conditions we refer to the two boundary conditions involving the velocity
v
. In the Navier–Stokes literature, these are also known as Hodge, Lions, or Navier-slip boundary conditions, see, e.g.,
[
19
,
25
,
27
,
32
]
.
Physically, such conditions are often used to model friction along the boundary of the domain. The specific Hodge/Lions formulation considered here is a special case of slip boundary conditions. This choice is particularly natural for equation (
1
), which is derived from the Navier–Stokes–Fourier system for viscous, heat-conducting fluids, cf.
[
11
, Section 2]
.

If slip effects are not to be modeled, one may instead impose Dirichlet conditions on each component of
v
, leading to the classical no-slip condition
v=0
on
\partial\Omega
. See
[
19
]
for an overview of different boundary conditions used for the Navier–Stokes equations.
The present work therefore treats both slip and no-slip boundary conditions, while also allowing a choice between Dirichlet and Neumann conditions for

p
.

On the other hand, one could also consider classical Neumann boundary conditions on each component of
v
. However, the physical meaning of such conditions is questionable. Moreover, due to the fact that skew symmetry of
(\nabla\cdot,\nabla)^{\top}
is lost, classical Neumann boundary conditions on
v
do not fit into the framework that is used here. Therefore, the Hodge/Lions boundary conditions considered here are more natural for equation (
1
) as they provide differential operators and energy estimates in line with the Dirichet case.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. In order to treat the various boundary conditions in a unified way, we first consider a more abstract PDE in section
2
and prove its well-posedness in Theorems
2.2
and
2.3
. Then, we apply the results to equation (
1
) under the various boundary conditions (
2a
) to (
2d
) in section
3
.

Galerkin Approximation

To show existence of a solution, we use Galerkin’s method. That is, we approximate solutions to (

2.1.2
Energy estimates

For the energy estimates, we define the time dependent energy functional

\begin{split}\langle\partial_{t}u_{m},\phi\rangle_{V}=\langle\partial_{t}u_{m},\phi^{1}\rangle&=\langle f-\mathfrak{A}u_{m}-\mathfrak{B}[u_{m},{u_{*}}]-\mathfrak{B}[{u_{*}},u_{m}],\phi^{1}\rangle\\

\begin{split}\|\partial_{t}u_{m}\|_{V^{*}}&\leq\|\mathfrak{A}u_{m}\|_{V^{*}}+\|\mathfrak{B}[u,{u_{*}}]\|_{V^{*}}+\|\mathfrak{B}[{u_{*}},u]\|_{V^{*}}+\|f\|_{V^{*}}\\

\|u_{m}\|_{X}\leq C_{G}(\|f\|_{Y}+\|u_{m}(0)\|_{W})

.

2.1.3
Existence and uniqueness

Since
X
is Hilbert and
u_{m}
bounded in
X
, there exists a weakly convergent subsequence of
u_{m}
converging weakly to some
u\in X
.

Let
u_{m}
denote this subsequence. Then, we have for all
v\in L^{2}(0,T;V)\subset X

\begin{split}\langle f,v\rangle_{Y}&=\lim_{n\to\infty}\langle\partial_{t}u_{m}+\mathfrak{A}u_{m}+\mathfrak{B}[u_{m},{u_{*}}]+\mathfrak{B}[{u_{*}},u_{m}],v\rangle_{Y}\\
&=\langle\partial_{t}u+\mathfrak{A}u+\mathfrak{B}[u,{u_{*}}]+\mathfrak{B}[{u_{*}},u],v\rangle_{Y},\end{split}

since
\partial_{t},\ \mathfrak{A},\ \mathfrak{B}[\cdot,{u_{*}}],\ \mathfrak{B}[{u_{*}},\cdot]
are (weakly) continuous from
X
to
Y
, due to the boundedness of the operators shown at the beginning of the proof.

It is therefore left to show that
u(0)=g
holds, which we do by closely following the argument in
[
6
, Section 7.1.2 c]
.
First of all, since
X\hookrightarrow C([0,T];W)
, we know that imposing an initial condition as
u(0)
is sensible. Then by partial integration with respect to time in (
2.1.3
), we have on the one hand for some
v\in C^{1}([0,T];W)\subset L^{2}(0,T;V)
with
v(T)=0

-\langle\partial_{t}v,u\rangle_{Y}+\langle\mathfrak{A}u+\mathfrak{B}[u,{u_{*}}]+\mathfrak{B}[{u_{*}},u],v\rangle_{Y}=\langle f,v\rangle_{Y}+\langle u(0),v(0)\rangle_{V^{*}}

(25)

and on the other hand

\lim_{n\to\infty}-\langle\partial_{t}v,u_{m}\rangle_{Y}+\langle\mathfrak{A}u_{m}+\mathfrak{B}[u_{m},{u_{*}}]+\mathfrak{B}[{u_{*}},u_{m}],v\rangle_{Y}=\langle f,v\rangle_{Y}+\langle g,v(0)\rangle_{V^{*}}

(26)

since
u_{m}(0)\to g
weakly in
V
. Comparing both expression we see that due to arbitrariness of

, we obtain

.

Uniqueness of
u
follows by standard arguments, due to the linearity of (
2
), see
[
11
, Section 3.2.3]
for details.
∎

2.2
Contraction argument for the bilinear problem

In this section, we establish well-posedness of the nonlinear problem (
2
) using Theorem
2.1
.
For the proof, we use the following version of the Newton-Kantorovich theorem to treat the quadratic nonlinearity
\mathfrak{B}
with a contraction argument.

Lemma 2.1
.

(Newton-Kantorovich Theorem)

Let
X
and
Y
be Banach spaces and
\mathcal{F}:\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{F})\subseteq X\to Y
. Suppose that on an open convex set
D_{*}\subseteq\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{F})
,
\mathcal{F}
is Fréchet differentiable and
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
is Lipschitz continuous on
D_{*}
with Lipschitz constant
K

\|\mathcal{F}^{\prime}_{u_{1}}-\mathcal{F}^{\prime}_{u_{2}}\|_{\mathcal{L}(X,Y)}\leq K\|u_{1}-u_{2}\|_{X},\quad u_{1},\,u_{2}\in D_{*}.

(27)

For some
u_{*}\in D_{*}
, assume
\Gamma_{*}:={\mathcal{F}^{\prime-1}_{u_{*}}}
is defined on all of
\ Y
and

\beta K\|\Gamma_{*}\mathcal{F}(u_{*})\|_{X}\leq\frac{1}{2},\ \text{ where }\beta=\|\Gamma_{*}\|_{\mathcal{L}(Y,X)}.

(28)

Set

r^{\pm}=\frac{1}{\beta K}(1\pm\sqrt{1-2\beta K\|\Gamma_{*}\mathcal{F}(u_{*})\|_{X}})

(29)

and suppose that
B_{r^{-}}^{X}(u_{*})\subseteq D_{*}
.
Then, the Newton iterates

u^{(k+1)}=u^{(k)}-{\mathcal{F}^{\prime-1}_{u^{(k)}}}\mathcal{F}(u^{(k)}),\,k=0,1,2,\ldots

(30)

starting at
u^{(0)}:=u_{*}

are well-defined, lie in
B_{r^{-}}^{X}(u_{*})
and converge to a solution of
\mathcal{F}(u)=0
, which is unique in
B_{r^{+}}^{X}(u_{*})\cap D_{*}
.

A proof of Lemma
2.1
can be found in e.g.
[
14
,
13
]
. With this, for any given
T>0
we can establish well-posedness of the abstract system, provided the data are sufficiently small.

Theorem 2.2
.

Let
T>0
,
(f,g)\in L^{2}(0,T;D(\mathcal{A}^{\frac{1-\sigma}{2}})^{*})\times{D}(\mathcal{A}^{\frac{\sigma}{2}})
and Assumption
2.1
hold with
\sigma\in[0,1]
. Then, there exists a
r>0
so that if

\|(f,g)\|_{L^{2}(0,T;D(\mathcal{A}^{\frac{1-\sigma}{2}})^{*})\times{D}(\mathcal{A}^{\frac{\sigma}{2}})}<r,

there exists a unique solution
u\in X:=L^{2}(0,T;D(\mathcal{A}^{\frac{1+\sigma}{2}}))\cap H^{1}(0,T;D(\mathcal{A}^{\frac{1-\sigma}{2}})^{*})
of equation (
2
).

Proof.

The proof follows a similar argumentation as the proof of
[
11
, Theorem 3.2]
. For convenience of the reader, we restate it here. As in Theorem
2.1
, we use the short notations

Y:=L^{2}(0,T;D(\mathcal{A}^{\frac{1-\sigma}{2}})^{*})
,
U:=D(\mathcal{A}^{\frac{1+\sigma}{2}})
,
V:=D(\mathcal{A}^{\frac{1-\sigma}{2}})
and
W:={D}(\mathcal{A}^{\frac{\sigma}{2}})

We seek to apply Lemma
2.1
to the map
\mathcal{F}:X\to Y\times W
defined as

\mathcal{F}u:=(\partial_{t}u+\mathfrak{A}u+\mathfrak{B}[u,u],u(0))-(f,g).

(31)

We choose the open and convex set
D_{*}=U^{X}_{r_{G}}(0):=\{u\in X:\|u\|<r_{G}\}
with
r_{G}>0
from Theorem
2.1
so that (
2
) is well defined for all
{u_{*}}\in D_{*}
.

Then, by assumptions
2.1(iii)
(
11
) and (
16
) that yield boundedness of
\mathfrak{B}:X\times X\to Y

we have

\frac{\|\mathfrak{B}[h,h]\|_{Y}}{\|h\|_{X}}\leq\frac{(C_{\mathfrak{B}}^{2}C_{T}^{2}\|h\|_{X}^{2}\int_{0}^{T}\|h\|_{U}^{2}ds)^{\frac{1}{2}}}{\|h\|_{X}}\leq{C_{\mathfrak{B}}}C_{T}\|h\|_{X}\to 0\quad(\|h\|_{X}\to 0)

(32)

meaning that
\mathcal{F}
is Fréchet differentiable at each
{u_{*}}\in D_{*}
with

\mathcal{F}^{\prime}_{u_{*}}:\xi\mapsto(\partial_{t}\xi+\mathfrak{A}\xi,\xi(0))+(\mathfrak{B}[\xi,{u_{*}}]+\mathfrak{B}[u_{*},\xi],0),

(33)

so that
\mathcal{F}^{\prime-1}_{u_{*}}:Y\times W\to X
is well posed for
{u_{*}}\in D_{*}
by Theorem
2.1
.

By the same estimate for
\mathfrak{B}
, we have for
u_{1},u_{2}\in D_{*}
and

\|(\mathcal{F}^{\prime}_{u_{1}}-\mathcal{F}^{\prime}_{u_{2}})u\|_{Y}\leq\|\mathfrak{B}[u,u_{1}-u_{2}]+\mathfrak{B}[u_{1}-u_{2},u]\|_{Y}\leq 2C_{\mathfrak{B}}C_{T}\|u_{1}-u_{2}\|_{X}\|u\|_{X}

(34)

giving Lipschitz continuity of
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
with Lipschitz constant
K:=2C_{\mathfrak{B}}C_{T}
.

Next, we choose
{u_{*}}=0\in D_{*}
, in which case assumption (
28
) of Lemma
2.1
turns out to be

\beta K\|-(\partial_{t}\cdot+\mathfrak{A}\cdot,\cdot(0))^{-1}(f,g)\|_{X}\leq\frac{1}{2}.

(35)

The a priori estimate from Theorem
2.1
gives us

, so that

with

\beta K\|-(\partial_{t}\cdot+\mathfrak{A}\cdot,\cdot(0))^{-1}(f,g)\|_{X}\leq C_{G}^{2}K\|(f,g)\|_{Y\times W}<\frac{1}{2}.

∎

We note that the above theorem does not provide a global well-posedness result, as the choice of

, which can depend on

2.3
Nonhomogeneous boundary conditions

In this section, we demonstrate how the results of the previous section can be adapted to establish well-posedness of (
2
) in the presence of nonhomogeneous boundary conditions, using standard lifting arguments.

In the abstract formulation (
2
) and under Assumption
2.1
, no boundary conditions are explicitly imposed. However, when nonhomogeneous boundary conditions are incorporated into the operator formulation, assumption
2.1(ii)
may fail to hold. For instance, the symmetry of the linear operator

\mathcal{A}
may be lost, as occurs with the Laplace operator subject to Dirichlet or Neumann boundary conditions.

Therefore, the strategy in the following is to use extension properties of the self adjoint operator
\mathcal{A}
.
Then, for a nonhomogeneous boundary value problem, we look for a solution in the bigger space
\tilde{X}\supset X
such that
X\hookrightarrow\tilde{X}
and assume that for a
suitable boundary condition operator
\mathfrak{T}
defined on
\tilde{X}
, we have
\mathfrak{T}(u)=0
if
u\in X
. This means
X
represents the space of functions with homogeneous boundary conditions. Equation (
2
) can then be written as

\begin{split}\partial_{t}u+\tilde{\mathfrak{A}}u+\tilde{\mathfrak{B}}[u,u]&=f\\
u(0)&=g\\
\mathfrak{T}(u)&=h,\end{split}

where now
\tilde{\mathfrak{A}}:\tilde{X}\to\tilde{Y}
and
\tilde{\mathfrak{B}}:\tilde{X}\times\tilde{X}\to\tilde{Y}
.
To formalize this lifting argument, we make the following assumptions in the case of nonhomogeneous boundary conditions.

Assumption 2.2
.

Let Assumption
2.1
and Theorem
2.1
hold with
Y:=L^{2}(0,T;D(\mathcal{A}^{\frac{1-\sigma}{2}})^{*})
,
U:=D(\mathcal{A}^{\frac{1+\sigma}{2}})
,
V:=D(\mathcal{A}^{\frac{1-\sigma}{2}})
and
W:={D}(\mathcal{A}^{\frac{\sigma}{2}})
. Additionally, assume that

(i)

there are Banach spaces
\tilde{U},\tilde{V},\tilde{W}
such that
U\hookrightarrow\tilde{U}
,
V\hookrightarrow\tilde{V}
and
W\hookrightarrow\tilde{W}
and
U,V,W
are closed subspaces of
\tilde{U},\tilde{V},\tilde{W}
, respectively. Define

\tilde{X}:=L^{2}(0,T;\tilde{U})\cap H^{1}(0,T;{V}^{*}),\qquad\tilde{Y}:=L^{2}(0,T;V^{*}).

Moreover, the embedding
\tilde{X}\hookrightarrow C([0,T];\tilde{W})
holds.

(ii)

\mathfrak{T}:\tilde{X}\to\mathfrak{T}(\tilde{X})
is linear
and
u\in\tilde{X}
with
\mathfrak{T}(u)=0
implies
u\in X
.

(iii)

\tilde{\mathfrak{A}}=\tilde{\mathcal{A}}+\tilde{\mathscr{A}}:\tilde{X}\to\tilde{Y}
linear is well defined, where
\tilde{\mathcal{A}}:D(\tilde{\mathcal{A}})\subset H\to H
with
D(\mathcal{A})\hookrightarrow D(\tilde{\mathcal{A}})
and
\tilde{\mathscr{A}}:D(\tilde{\mathcal{A}})\subset H\to H
. Furthermore
\tilde{\mathcal{A}}\big|_{X}=\mathcal{A}
and
\tilde{\mathscr{A}}\big|_{X}=\mathscr{A}
, while
\tilde{\mathscr{A}}:\tilde{X}\to\tilde{Y}
is bounded.

\tilde{\mathfrak{B}}:\tilde{X}\times\tilde{X}\to\tilde{Y}
bilinear is well defined, bounded
and
\tilde{\mathfrak{B}}\big|_{X\times X}=\mathfrak{B}
.

(iv)

there exists a unique
\tilde{h}\in\tilde{X}
such that
\tilde{\mathcal{A}}\tilde{h}=0
and
\mathfrak{T}(\tilde{h})=h

with
\|\tilde{h}\|_{\tilde{X}}\leq c_{h}\|h\|_{X_{\mathfrak{T}}}
for some Banach space
X_{\mathfrak{T}}\subseteq\mathfrak{T}(\tilde{X})
.

We note that, in principle, it is possible to choose
X_{\mathfrak{T}}=\mathfrak{T}(\tilde{X})
. However, for the application to nonlinear acoustics discussed in Section
3
, we assume more regularity for
h
then
\mathfrak{T}(\tilde{X})
to avoid technical details that would otherwise arise.

Theorem 2.3
.

Let
T>0
and Assumptions
2.1
,
2.2
hold.
Let
f\in Y
,
g\in\tilde{W}
,
h\in X_{\mathfrak{T}}
with
\tilde{h}(0)-g\in W
. Then there exists a
\tilde{r}>0
such that if

\|f\|_{{Y}}+\|g\|_{\tilde{W}}+\|h\|_{X_{\mathfrak{T}}}<\tilde{r},

there exists a unique solution
u\in\tilde{X}
of equation (
2.3
).

Proof.

The main idea of the proof is to deduce a solution
u
of (
2.3
) as
u=u^{0}+\tilde{u}
with
u^{0}\in X
and
\tilde{u}\in\tilde{X}
.

1. We wish to obtain
\tilde{u}
as the unique solution of the linear problem

3.1
Preliminaries

We assume throughout this work that
\Omega
is a bounded, simply connected domain and has at least
C^{2,1}-
boundary.
In the following, we often write function spaces as
L^{2},\ H^{1},\ \dots
without specifying the domain. If we do not specify the domain, then by default these spaces have the domain
\Omega
. To note down vector valued function spaces, we use the notation
X^{j}:=X\times\dots\times X
,
j\in\mathbb{N},
with
X
a Banach space and equip these spaces with the component-wise norm, i.e.,
\|x\|_{X^{j}}=\sqrt{\|x_{1}\|_{X}^{2}+\dots+\|x_{j}\|^{2}_{X}}
. An element
x\in X^{j}
is often denoted as
x=(x_{1},...,x_{j})^{\top}
.
We use the following version of Hölder’s inequality

\displaystyle\|uvw\|_{L^{1}}\leq\|u\|_{L^{p}}\|v\|_{L^{q}}\|w\|_{L^{r}}\quad u\in L^{p},\ v\in L^{q},\ w\in L^{r},\quad 1=\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{q}+\frac{1}{r}

(42)

for
p,q,r\in[1,\infty)
, where
L^{p}
denote the usual Lebesgue spaces. For integrals of
u\in L^{1}
over
\Omega
, we use the shorthand notation
\int_{\Omega}u
, while for integrals over
\partial\Omega
, we write
\int_{\partial\Omega}udS
, where
dS
indicates the integration over a boundary.

With
H^{m}
we denote the usual Sobolev space of integrable functions with
m
-th order distributional derivatives in
L^{2}
. For
s>0
we denote by
H^{s}
the fractional order Sobolev spaces obtained by interpolation with the usual norm and seminorm.
For
u\in(H^{1})^{j}
we denote by
\nabla u\in(L^{2})^{j\times j}
the (weak) Jacobian matrix. If
u\in H^{1}_{0}
, which is defined as the completion of
C^{\infty}_{0}
in
H^{1}
, we choose
\|u\|_{H^{1}_{0}}=\|\nabla u\|_{L^{2}}=\sqrt{\langle\nabla u,\nabla u\rangle}
, where
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
denotes the
L^{2}
inner product. In this case, for smooth enough domains we have by Poincaré’s inequality
\|u\|_{L^{2}}\leq C_{P}\|\nabla u\|_{(L^{2})^{d}}
with constant
C_{P}>0
and space dimension
d\in\{2,3\}
.
As
H^{-s}
we denote the dual space of
H^{s}_{0}
.

Let
{C}:=\{u\in L^{2}:u(x)=c,\ c\in\mathbb{R}\}
be the closed subspace of constant functions of
L^{2}
. If
H
is a Hilbert space and
M\subset H
a subspace of
H
, we denote the quotient space as
H_{\diagup M}
.

3.1.1
Dirichlet Laplacian

The Dirichlet Laplacian
-\Delta_{D}:{D}(-\Delta_{D})\subset L^{2}\to L^{2}
can be obtained by a Friedrich’s extension, see
[
30
, Theorem 10.19]
. If we assume
\Omega
to be
C^{2}
-regular, we have
{D}(-\Delta_{D})=H^{2}\cap H^{1}_{0}
by elliptic regularity. Furthermore,
-\Delta_{D}
is self adjoint and has a spectral decomposition as required in assumption
2.1(ii)
for
\mathcal{A}
with
H=L^{2}
.

Moreover, the following characterization of the fractional domains of
-\Delta_{D}
for
s\in[0,2]
holds

{D}(-\Delta_{D}^{\frac{s}{2}})=\mathbb{H}^{s}:=\begin{cases}H^{s}\quad&0<s<\frac{1}{2},\\
H^{\frac{1}{2}}_{00}\quad&s=\frac{1}{2},\\
H^{s}_{0}\quad&\frac{1}{2}<s<1,\\
H^{s}\cap H^{1}_{0}\quad&1\leq s\leq 2,\end{cases}

(43)

see
[
7
, Theorem 3.1]
in combination with
[
3
, Proposition 4.1]
. The space denoted by
H^{\frac{1}{2}}_{00}
consists of all
u\in H^{\frac{1}{2}}
such that
\int_{\Omega}d^{-1}u^{2}<\infty
, where
d(x):\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
stands for the Euclidean distance from
x
to
\partial\Omega
. We define
\mathbb{H}^{-s}:=(\mathbb{H}^{s})^{*}
.

Note that
\mathbb{H}^{s}\hookrightarrow H^{s}
for
s\geq 0
with embedding constant
C_{\mathbb{H}}>0
, which follows from
[
2
, Proposition 2.2]
and the fact that
H^{1}_{0}\hookrightarrow H^{1}
.

3.1.2
Neumann Laplacian

The classical Neumann Laplacian
-\Delta_{N}:{D}(\Delta_{N})\subset L^{2}\to L^{2}
can be obtained by a Friedrich’s extension, see
[
30
, Theorem 10.20]
. If
\Omega
is assumed to be
C^{2}
-regular, we have

{D}(-\Delta_{N}):=\{u\in H^{2}:\mathcal{T}(\nabla u)\cdot n=0\},

(44)

where
\mathcal{T}:H^{s}\to H^{s-\frac{1}{2}}(\partial\Omega)
if
s>\frac{1}{2}
denotes the classical trace operator, see
[
6
, Chapter 5.5]
.

-\Delta_{N}
is also self adjoint and has almost a spectral decomposition as required in assumption
2.1(ii)
if

with the exception that the first eigenvalue vanishes, i.e.,
\lambda_{1}=0
. This can be overcome by considering
-\Delta_{N}:{D}(-\Delta_{N})_{\diagup{C}}\subset L^{2}_{\diagup{C}}\to L^{2}_{\diagup{C}}
, i.e., by factoring out constant functions. We write for this operator
-\Delta_{\tilde{N}}
with
{D}(-\Delta_{\tilde{N}})={D}(-\Delta_{N})_{\diagup{C}}
. Then, this Neumann Laplacian

does not have a vanishing eigenvalue, since the eigenspace corresponding to
\lambda_{1}
for
-\Delta_{N}
equals
C
.

Therefore, fractional powers of
-\Delta_{\tilde{N}}
can be defined as in the Dirichlet case, and we have for
s\in[0,2]

{D}(-\Delta_{\tilde{N}}^{\frac{s}{2}})=\mathbb{G}^{s}:=\begin{cases}\{u\in H^{s}:\int_{\Omega}u=0\}\quad&s<\frac{3}{2},\\
\{u\in H^{\frac{3}{2}}:\int_{\Omega}u=0,\ |\nabla u|\in H^{\frac{1}{2}}_{00}\}\quad&s=\frac{3}{2},\\
\{u\in H^{s}:\int_{\Omega}u=0,\ \mathcal{T}(\nabla u)\cdot n=0\}\quad&s>\frac{3}{2},\end{cases}

(45)

see
[
2
, Proposition 2.4]
for
s<1
and
[
15
, Remark 2.10]
for
s\geq 1
. Note that the condition
\int_{\Omega}u=0
is equivalent to factoring out constant functions, and we define
\mathbb{G}^{-s}:=(\mathbb{G}^{s})^{*}
.

3.1.3
Hodge/Lions Laplacian

For Hodge/Lions boundary conditions, we only cover the case
d=3
for simplicity, so that the vector cross product
\times
is a map
\times:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
. We consider a vector valued Laplacian acting on functions with
d=3
components fulfilling the Hodge/Lions boundary conditions as in
[
23
,
25
]
, denoted by
-\Delta_{M}
in the following. This operator can be constructed via a self adjoint Friedrich’s extension with domain

{D}(-\Delta_{M})=\{v\in H^{1}_{\perp}:\nabla\cdot v\in H^{1},\ \nabla\times(\nabla\times v)\in(L^{2})^{d},\ (\nabla\times v)\times n=0\text{ on }\partial\Omega\}

(46)

with

{D}(-\Delta_{M}^{\frac{1}{2}})=H^{1}_{\perp}:=\{v\in(L^{2})^{d}:\nabla\cdot v\in L^{2},\ \nabla\times v\in(L^{2})^{d},\ v\cdot n=0\text{ on }\partial\Omega\}

in case of a Lipschitz-regular domain
\Omega
, see
[
25
, Section 4]
. When

is assumed to be more regular, one can obtain more regular function spaces. This is shown in the next Lemma, justifying our notation

. Note that this construction of

and the representation of

is linked to the vector calculus formula
\Delta v=\nabla\nabla\cdot v-\nabla\times\nabla\times v
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
\Omega
be
C^{1,1}
-regular and
d=3
. Then for
a\in H^{\frac{1}{2}}(\partial\Omega)
and

the continuous embeddings

\begin{split}\{&v\in(L^{2})^{d}:\nabla\cdot v\in L^{2},\ \nabla\times v\in(L^{2})^{d},\ v\cdot n=a\text{ on }\partial\Omega\}\hookrightarrow(H^{1})^{d},\\
\{&v\in(L^{2})^{d}:\nabla\cdot v\in L^{2},\ \nabla\times v\in(L^{2})^{d},\ v\times n=b\text{ on }\partial\Omega\}\hookrightarrow(H^{1})^{d}.\end{split}

hold.
If
\Omega
is
C^{2,1}
-regular, then for
c\in H^{\frac{3}{2}}(\partial\Omega)
the continuous embedding

\{v\in(H^{1})^{d}:\nabla\cdot v\in H^{1},\ \nabla\times v\in(H^{1})^{d},\ v\cdot n=c\text{ on }\partial\Omega\}\hookrightarrow(H^{2})^{d}

(47)

holds,
leading to
{D}(-\Delta_{M})\hookrightarrow(H^{2})^{d}
.

Proof.

The embeddings (
3.1
), (
47
) can be deduced from
[
1
]
using
[
1
, Theorems 2.9, 2.12, Remark 2.14 and Corollary 2.15 with

]
.

To prove

note that for

with

we have
\nabla\cdot w=\nabla\cdot\nabla\times v=0\in L^{2}
(in a distributional sense),

and

) gives us

∎

4
Conclusion

To conclude, we have established well-posedness of (
1
) for the boundary condition configurations Dirichlet–Dirichlet, Dirichlet–Hodge/Lions, Neumann–Hodge/Lions, and Neumann–Dirichlet.
For cases involving Dirichlet or Neumann boundary conditions, we have obtained well-posedness under lower regularity assumptions on the data in dimensions two and three.
When Hodge/Lions boundary conditions are present, we restrict the results to dimension three and standard regularity.
Once the abstract framework from Section
2
is in place, well-posedness follows directly upon choosing appropriate domains for the operators.

All of the well-posedness results are established under the assumption that the data are sufficiently small. If the solution possesses enough regularity, the embedding in
16
is not required, and global well-posedness can be inferred, as shown in
[
11
]
for homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions. It is expected that these results carry over to the case of inhomogeneous boundary conditions as well.

There are several natural directions for future research.
A first step would be to compute numerical solutions of (
1
) and compare the behavior under the different boundary condition configurations.
Another question is how the present results extend to less regular domains, such as Lipschitz domains, which form the natural setting for finite element methods.
Less smooth boundaries imply less smooth domains of the Laplace operator, making it relevant in what sense the fractional domains of the Laplacian can be characterized by Sobolev spaces in analogy to e.g.
\mathbb{H}^{s}
.
Another open task in this direction would be to characterize the fractional domains of
-\Delta_{M}
for smooth and Lipschitz boundaries.

Other types of boundary conditions, such as absorbing boundary conditions, see
[
31
]
, are also of interest in nonlinear acoustics.
It remains an open question whether analogous boundary conditions can be formulated for equation (
1
).

Concerning the limit of vanishing damping coefficients

\zeta
and
\mu
in (
1
), note that, as seen in the energy estimate (
2.1.2
), the nonlinear terms must be controlled using the regularity provided by the symmetric operator
\mathcal{A}
. Consequently, for low regularity data, the controllability of the nonlinear terms relies on the Laplace operator. In contrast, if the initial data are sufficiently regular, well-posedness for the case
\zeta=\mu=0
can be established using hyperbolic methods. This scenario, among other aspects, is studied in the unfinished work
[
18
]
.
