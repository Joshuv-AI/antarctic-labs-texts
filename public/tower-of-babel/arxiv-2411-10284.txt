Optimal Capacity Modification for Stable Matchings with Ties Thanks: A preliminary version [ 31 ] of this work appeared in the proceedings of the 34th International Joint Conference on Artificial Intelligence (IJCAI) 2025
Keshav Ranjan, Meghana Nasre, Prajakta Nimbhorkar

Abstract
We consider the Hospitals/Residents ( HR ) problem in the presence of ties in hospital preferences. Among the three notions of stability, namely weak stability, strong stability, and super-stability, we focus on the notion of strong stability. Strong stability has many desirable properties, both theoretically and in practice; however, its existence is not guaranteed. In this paper, our objective is to optimally increase the quotas of hospitals to ensure that a strongly stable matching exists in the modified instance. We explore two natural optimization criteria: (i) minimizing the total capacity increase across all hospitals ( MinSum ) and (ii) minimizing the maximum capacity increase for any hospital ( MinMax ). We show that the MinSum problem admits a polynomial-time algorithm. We also establish an analog of the well-known rural hospitals theorem [Gale & Sotomayor, 1985; Roth, 1986], adapted to the MinSum augmentation setting. We consider a generalization of the MinSum problem in which each hospital incurs a cost per unit increase in its quota. We show that the cost version of the MinSum problem is NP-hard and inapproximable within any multiplicative factor, even if the costs are zero or one. For the MinSum objective with a set of forced edges, we give a polynomial-time algorithm. In contrast to the above results for the MinSum problem, we show that the MinMax problem is NP-hard. When hospital preference lists have ties of length at most ℓ + 1 \ell+1 , we give a polynomial-time algorithm that increases each hospital’s quota by at most ℓ \ell , ensuring the resulting instance admits a strongly stable matching. Moreover, among all such augmentations, our algorithm outputs the best strongly stable matching from the residents’ perspective.

Introduction

The Hospitals/Residents (
HR
) problem
[
19
,
32
]
is a many-to-one generalization of the classical stable marriage problem
[
15
]
. As the name suggests, the
HR
problem models the assignment of junior doctors (residents)
to hospitals where agents in both sets are allowed to rank acceptable agents from the other set in a preference order. The problem has been extensively investigated, as it has applications in a number of centralized matching schemes in many countries, including the National Resident Matching Program in the USA (
NRMP
), the Canadian Resident Matching Service (
CaRMS
), and the Scottish Foundation Allocation Scheme (
SFAS
), to name a few. In addition, the
HR
problem models several real-world applications, such as assigning children to schools
[
1

and students to undergraduate programs
[
5

, where agents need to be matched to programs, and both sets express preferences over each other.

We consider a generalization of the
HR
problem where
ties
are allowed in the preference lists. That is, agents can be indifferent between multiple agents from the other set. This problem is known as the Hospitals/Residents problem with ties (
HRT
).
Ties in preference lists play an important role in real-world matching applications. For instance, hospitals with many applicants often find it difficult to generate
strict preference lists and, within the framework of a centralized matching scheme, have expressed a desire to include ties in their preference lists
[
21
]
. In the context of college admissions, it is natural for colleges to place all students with equal scores in a single tie in their preference lists.

The classical notion of stability defined for strict preferences has been generalized in the literature for the case of ties, in three different ways — weak stability, strong stability and super-stability (see Definition
1.1
and the footnote therein). As indicated by the names, super-stability is the strongest notion, and weak stability is the weakest among the three. It is well-known that every instance of the
HRT
problem admits a weakly stable matching (and it can be obtained by breaking ties arbitrarily and computing a stable matching in the resulting strict-list instance); however, strong or super-stable matchings are not guaranteed to exist
[
22
]
.

The strongest notion of stability is super-stability. However, as noted in
[
20
]
, insisting on super-stability in practical scenarios can be overly restrictive and is less likely to be attainable. Moreover, in applications such as college admissions, students are naturally expected to
express strict preferences over colleges, even though colleges may place students with equal scores in a single tie

For instance, according to the equal treatment policy used in Chile and Hungary, it is not acceptable that a student is rejected from a college preferred by her, even though other students with the same score are admitted (see
[

Moreover, the same example illustrates that increasing hospital quotas alone may not help in obtaining an instance that admits a strongly stable matching. This happens because there are ties in residents’ preference lists, whereas quota augmentation is possible for hospitals only.

What if resident preference lists are strict and ties appear only in hospitals’ preferences? We call such instances

(Hospitals/Residents problem with ties on hospitals’ side only). There exist simple instances of

that do not admit a strongly stable matching; however, for any such instance, we can construct an

1.2
Our problems and contributions

Throughout the paper, unless stated otherwise, we assume that the given
HR-HT
instance
G=(\mathcal{R}\cup\mathcal{H},E)
does not admit a strongly stable matching. Deciding whether an
HRT
instance admits a strongly stable matching can be done in polynomial time using the algorithm by Irving
et al
.
[
20
]
(see Appendix

). Throughout this paper, the
augmented
instance
G^{\prime}
is the same as
G

q^{\prime}(h)\geq q(h)
for each
h
. Our first objective is to minimize the total increase in quotas across all hospitals, which we denote as the

MinSum-SS

Given an

G=(\mathcal{R}\cup\mathcal{H},E)

admits a strongly stable matching and the sum of the increase in quotas over all hospitals (that is,

Theorem 1.2

The
MinSum-SS
problem is solvable in polynomial time.

We establish an analog of the well-known rural hospitals theorem
[
16
,
33
]
for the
MinSum-SS
problem. We show that all strongly stable matchings across optimal augmented instances match the same set of residents. Furthermore, we prove that under-subscribed hospitals are filled to the same extent in every strongly stable matching across all optimal augmented instances. These results are stated in Section
2
as Theorem
2.5
and Corollary
1
.

Given the polynomial-time solution to the
MinSum-SS
problem, we consider the optimal total quota augmentation (if possible) required for matching a specified subset of edges
Q\subseteq E
in

(forced edges).
The

MinSum-SS-FE

Given an

G=(\mathcal{R}\cup\mathcal{H},E)

admits a strongly stable matching that includes all edges in

\sum_{h\in\mathcal{H}}(q^{\prime}(h)-q(h))

Theorem 1.3

The
MinSum-SS-FE
problem is solvable in polynomial time.

Next, we consider a generalization of the
MinSum-SS
problem, where increasing the quota of a hospital incurs a cost per unit increase in the quota. This is denoted as the

problem.

MinSum-Cost

Given an
HR-HT

G=(\mathcal{R}\cup\mathcal{H},E)
, where each hospital
h\in\mathcal{H}
has an associated cost
c(h)
for each unit increase in its quota, construct an augmented instance

such that

In contrast to the polynomial-time solvability of the

Theorem 1.4

The

Related Work

Capacity Modification:
Chen and Csáji
[
10
]
studied a problem similar to ours in the setting of strict preference lists, with the goal of augmenting hospital quotas so that the resulting instance admits a resident-perfect stable matching. They showed that, under the
MinMax
objective, the problem admits a polynomial-time algorithm. In contrast, perhaps surprisingly, the
MinMax
objective for strongly stable matching yields an NP-hardness result (Theorem
1.5
). They also consider the
MinSum
objective and show that the problem of obtaining an optimal augmented instance that admits a stable and resident-perfect matching under
MinSum
is NP-hard. This also implies that constructing an augmented instance in the
HR-HT
setting that admits a strongly stable, resident-perfect matching is NP-hard. However, without the resident-perfectness requirement, our result in Theorem
1.2
gives a polynomial-time algorithm.

Capacity modification to achieve specific objectives has attracted significant interest in recent years. Bobbio
et al
.
[
7
]
explored the complexity of determining the optimal variation (augmentation or reduction) of hospital quotas to achieve the best outcomes for residents, subject to stability and capacity variation constraints, and showed NP-hardness results. In a follow-up work, Bobbio
et al
.
[
6
]
developed a mixed integer linear program to address this issue, and provided a comprehensive set of tools for obtaining near-optimal solutions.
Gokhale
et al
.
[
18
]
considered the problem of modifying hospitals’ quotas to achieve two objectives – (i) to obtain a stable matching so as to match a given pair, and (ii) to stabilize a given matching, either by only augmenting or only reducing hospital quotas.
Afacan
et al
.
[
2
]
examined capacity design in the
HR
setting, to achieve a stable matching that is not Pareto-dominated by any other stable matching.

Kavitha and Nasre
[
25
]
and Kavitha
et al
.
[
24
]
addressed the capacity augmentation problem for
popular
matchings in the one-sided preference list setting (where every hospital is indifferent between its neighbors). It is known that a popular matching is not guaranteed to exist in this setting. Therefore, their objective was to optimally increase hospital quotas to create an instance that admits a popular matching. Although we consider a different setting (two-sided preference lists) and a different optimality notion—namely, strong stability—it is noteworthy that our results closely resemble those of Kavitha and Nasre
[

]
and Kavitha
et al
.

Strong Stability:
The notion of strong stability was first studied in the one-to-one setting (
i.e.
q(h)=1
for all
h\in\mathcal{H}
) for balanced, complete bipartite graphs by Irving
[

, where he gave an

[

]
extended the results in
[

to the general one-to-one setting (

[

[

showed that there exists a partial order with

[

showed that when edge weights are small, the maximum weight strongly stable matching problem can be solved in

if the maximum weight of an edge is

[

]
and by Boehmer and Heeger
[
8
]
.

Claim 2.2

Let

Proof

We proceed by contradiction. Suppose the claim fails,

Since

Now, let us consider a variant of the
MinSum-SS
problem where our goal is to determine the existence of an augmented instance that admits a resident-perfect strongly stable matching. Let us denote this problem by
MinSum-SS-RP
. Chen and Csáji
[
10
]
studied a special case of this problem called
MinSum Cap Stable and Perfect
problem. Given an
HR
instance (strict list), say
G
, and a budget
\ell
, the
MinSum Cap Stable and Perfect
problem asks whether it is possible to obtain an augmented instance
G^{\prime}
from
G
, only by increasing the quotas of some hospitals, such that
G^{\prime}
admits a resident-perfect stable matching, and the sum of the increase in quotas over all hospitals is at most
\ell
. They showed that this problem is NP-complete even for a very restricted case. Therefore, we conclude that

problem is NP-complete.

3
MINSUM-SS with Forced Edges

In this section, we consider the
MinSum-SS-FE
problem. Here, in addition to an
HR-HT
instance
G
, we are given a subset
Q\subseteq E
. Our goal in this problem is to determine whether there exists an augmented instance
G^{\prime}
that admits a strongly stable matching
M^{\prime}
with
Q\subseteq M^{\prime}
. Note that if
Q
contains two edges
(r,h_{1})
and
(r,h_{2})
for some resident
r
and distinct hospitals
h_{1}\neq h_{2}
, then no such augmented instance
G^{\prime}
exists. Therefore, we assume that for any two edges
(r_{1},h_{1}),(r_{2},h_{2})\in Q
, we have
r_{1}\neq r_{2}
.

We remark that such an augmented instance need not exist even when
|Q|=1
. For instance, consider an instance with one resident
r
and two hospitals
h_{1},h_{2}
where
r
prefers
h_{1}
over
h_{2}
. Let
Q=\{(r,h_{2})\}
. There is no way to augment the quotas of hospitals to get a strongly stable matching which contains
Q
. We show that the
MinSum-SS-FE
problem admits a polynomial-time algorithm.
Whenever an augmentation is possible, we output the optimally augmented instance.

3.1
Overall idea of our algorithm

First, we present the overall idea of our algorithm.
Our algorithm constructs a pruned graph by deleting certain edges—specifically, any edge whose inclusion in a matching, together with the forced edges
Q
, would introduce a strong blocking pair. The pruned graph may or may not admit a strongly stable matching. Hence, we use Algorithm
1
(for the
MinSum-SS
problem) to obtain an augmented instance. While the augmented instance admits a strongly stable matching, the matching along with the forced edges
Q
may not be strongly stable. To finally determine whether an augmentation is possible, we crucially use the analog of the rural hospitals theorem for the
MinSum-SS
problem, established as Theorem
2.5
and Corollary
1
.

For a hospital
h\in\mathcal{H}
, let
Q(h)=\{r\ |\ (r,h)\in Q\}
.
If such an augmented instance exists, then the quota of each hospital
h
must be at least
|Q(h)|
. Therefore, for each hospital
h
, without loss of generality, we assume
q(h)\geq|Q(h)|
.

3.2
Pruned instance

Let
(r,h)\in Q
. Suppose there exists a resident
r^{\prime}
such that
r^{\prime}\succeq_{h}r
. Then
r^{\prime}
cannot be matched to any
h^{\prime}
where
h\succ_{r^{\prime}}h^{\prime}
in a strongly stable matching
M
containing the edge
(r,h)
; otherwise,
(r^{\prime},h)
will block
M
. We call such a resident
r^{\prime}
a
distracting resident
for the forced edge
(r,h)\in Q
. Similarly, for the edge
(r,h)\in Q
, if there exists a hospital
h^{\prime}
such that
h^{\prime}\succ_{r}h
, then
h^{\prime}
cannot be matched to any resident
r^{\prime}
where
r\succeq_{h^{\prime}}r^{\prime}
in any strongly stable matching which contains the edge
(r,h)
. We call such a hospital
h^{\prime}
a
distracting hospital
for the forced edge
(r,h)
.
The notion of distracting agents is borrowed from
[
18
]
, where a special case of our problem is considered in the strict list setting with
|Q|=1
.
For each distracting resident/hospital, we remove any edge along which the distracting agent cannot be matched in any strongly stable matching containing the edge
(r,h)
. It is possible that the set of deleted edges may contain edges from
Q
. If it happens, then there exists an edge that needs to be deleted, but is also a forced edge. Hence, there does not exist an augmented instance
G^{\prime}
which admits a strongly stable matching containing the set
Q
, and therefore, we output “No augmentation possible to get a strongly stable matching containing Q”. In the remaining part of the section, we assume that the set of deleted edges does not contain any edge from
Q
.

Given the edge set
Q
of edges, we define a set
\mathcal{R}_{d}
of distracting residents and a set
\mathcal{H}_{d}
of distracting hospitals corresponding to all the edges in
Q
. Thus,

\mathcal{R}_{d}=\{r^{\prime}\ |\ r^{\prime}\succeq_{h}r\text{ for some }(r,h)\in Q\}
and
\mathcal{H}_{d}=\{h^{\prime}\ |\ h^{\prime}\succ_{r}h\text{ for some }(r,h)\in Q\}
. Next, we formally describe the pruning steps discussed above. We use the edge set
E_{d}
to keep track of deleted edges.

1.

Initialize
E_{d}=\emptyset
. For each edge
(r,h)\in Q
:

(a)

For each
r^{\prime}\in\mathcal{R}_{d}
, if
r^{\prime}
is distracting for edge
(r,h)
, then we delete all
h^{\prime}
from
Pref(
r^{\prime}
)
and
r^{\prime}
from
Pref(
h^{\prime}
)
such that
h\succ_{r^{\prime}}h^{\prime}
. Add all such
(r^{\prime},h^{\prime})
to
E_{d}
.

(b)

For each
h^{\prime}\in\mathcal{H}_{d}
, if
h^{\prime}
is distracting for edge
(r,h)
, then we delete all
r^{\prime}
from
Pref(
h^{\prime}
)
and
h^{\prime}
from
Pref(
r^{\prime}
)
such that
r\succeq_{h^{\prime}}r^{\prime}
. Add all such
(r^{\prime},h^{\prime})
to
E_{d}
.

2.

Let
Q_{\mathcal{R}}=\{r\ |\ (r,h)\in Q\}
.
Since every resident in
Q_{\mathcal{R}}
has a forced match, we delete all edges incident on
r\in Q_{\mathcal{R}}
. We include these deleted edges in
E_{d}
. Observe that after this step, the edges in
Q
are in
E_{d}
. To account for matching residents in
Q_{\mathcal{R}}
to their forced partners, we appropriately reduce the quotas of the corresponding hospitals.

We call this pruned instance
G_{p}
. We note that during this pruning process, some residents may become isolated, and the quotas of some hospitals may go down to zero. We denote the set of edges in
G_{p}
by
E_{p}
. That is,
E_{p}=E\setminus E_{d}
. Thus, the pruned instance
G_{p}=(\mathcal{R}\cup\mathcal{H},E_{p})
. For each hospital
h
, its quota in
G_{p}
is denoted by
q_{p}(h)
and
q_{p}(h)=q(h)-|Q(h)|
. It is easy to observe that both steps described above run in
O(m)
time. Therefore, the pruned graph
G_{p}
can be constructed in
O(m)
time.

3.3
Our algorithm

We now describe our algorithm. Observe that the pruned instance
G_{p}
may or may not admit a strongly stable matching. If
G_{p}
does not admit a strongly stable matching, we run Algorithm
1
for the
MinSum-SS
problem to obtain an augmented instance
G_{p}^{\prime}
that admits a strongly stable matching, say
M_{p}^{\prime}
. For the instance
G_{p}^{\prime}=(\mathcal{R}\cup\mathcal{H},E_{p})
, let
q^{\prime}_{p}(h)
denote the quota of hospital
h\in\mathcal{H}
. Even in the case when the pruned graph
G_{p}
itself admits a strongly stable matching, for convenience of notation we rename the instance
G_{p}
as
G_{p}^{\prime}
and a strongly stable matching in
G_{p}^{\prime}
as
M_{p}^{\prime}
. Although
M_{p}^{\prime}
is a strongly stable matching in
G_{p}^{\prime}
, for the matching
M_{p}^{\prime}\cup Q
to be a strongly stable matching, we need the following checks.

Step 1.

If there exists some
h\in\mathcal{H}_{d}
such that
|M_{p}^{\prime}(h)|<q(h)
(
i.e.

h
is under-subscribed with respect to its original quota), then we declare “No augmentation possible to get a strongly stable matching containing
Q
”. This step is justified in Lemma
3.3
.

Step 2.

Otherwise, we obtain a further augmented instance
G_{p}^{*}
and a matching
M_{p}^{*}
as described next. For a resident
r\in\mathcal{R}
, let
last(r)
denote the least-preferred hospital of
r
in the pruned instance
G_{p}
. Suppose
r^{\prime}\in\mathcal{R}_{d}
is a distracting resident for some edge
(r,h)
. This implies
r^{\prime}\succeq_{h}r
. We observe that for any augmented instance of
G
, no strongly stable matching containing the edge
(r,h)
can match the resident
r^{\prime}
to a hospital
h^{\prime}
such that
last(r^{\prime})\succ_{r^{\prime}}h^{\prime}
, otherwise
(r^{\prime},last(r^{\prime}))
blocks the resulting matching. For the same reason as above, no
r^{\prime}\in\mathcal{R}_{d}
can be left unmatched. Let
\mathcal{R}_{d}^{u}
denote the residents in
\mathcal{R}_{d}
that are left unmatched in the matching
M_{p}^{\prime}
. Then, for every
r^{\prime}\in\mathcal{R}_{d}^{u}
in the instance
G_{p}^{\prime}
, we match
r^{\prime}
to the hospital
last(r^{\prime})
to get the matching
M_{p}^{*}
and increase the quota of
last(r^{\prime})
by 1. We denote by
q^{*}_{p}(h)
the quota of every hospital in the instance
G_{p}^{*}
.

Step 3.

For each
(r,h)\in Q
we add
(r,h)
to
M_{p}^{*}
and increase the quota of
h
by 1. We also restore all the edges in
E_{d}
. With a slight abuse of notation, we call this instance
G_{p}^{*}
with quota
q_{p}^{*}(h)
for all
h\in\mathcal{H}
. We return
G_{p}^{*}
and the matching
M_{p}^{*}
.

This completes the description of our algorithm.

Running time of our algorithm.
Recall that the pruned graph
G_{p}
can be constructed in
O(m)
time. As noted in Section
2
, executing Algorithm
1
takes
O(m)
time to compute
G_{p}^{\prime}
and
M_{p}^{\prime}
. Constructing
M_{p}^{*}
from
M_{p}^{\prime}
also takes
O(m)
time. Thus, the overall running time of our algorithm is
O(m)
.

3.4
Correctness and optimality of our algorithm

We first prove the strong stability of the matching
M_{p}^{*}
output by our algorithm.

Claim 3.1

No edge in
E_{d}
blocks the matching
M_{p}^{*}
output at the end of
Step 3.

Proof

We first note that no edge in
Q
can be strongly blocking for

because

. Therefore, we only need to consider the edges in

. Observe that for each edge
(r^{\prime},h^{\prime})\in E_{d}\setminus Q
, either
r^{\prime}\in Q_{\mathcal{R}}
or
r^{\prime}\notin Q_{\mathcal{R}}
. We consider both of these cases below. Note also that each

is matched in

.

Case 1.

(r^{\prime},h^{\prime})\in E_{d}\setminus Q
and
r^{\prime}\in Q_{\mathcal{R}}
: Suppose

. It is easy to observe that if
\hat{h}\succ_{r^{\prime}}h^{\prime}
, then
(r^{\prime},h^{\prime})
cannot be a blocking pair with respect to

. Therefore, let us assume that
h^{\prime}\succ_{r^{\prime}}\hat{h}
. Then

is a distracting hospital for the edge
(r^{\prime},\hat{h})
. By construction, the pruned instance

does not contain any edge
(r^{\prime\prime},h^{\prime})
such that

. This implies

must be fully subscribed in

with respect to its augmented quota,

, and all residents matched to
h^{\prime}
are strictly better than

. Thus,
(r^{\prime},h^{\prime})
cannot be a blocking pair with respect to
M_{p}^{*}
.

Case 2.

(r^{\prime},h^{\prime})\in E_{d}\setminus Q
and
r^{\prime}\notin Q_{\mathcal{R}}
: In this case, at least one of the following holds: (i)

is a distracting resident, or (ii)
h^{\prime}
is a distracting hospital. If
r^{\prime}
is a distracting resident, say for an edge

, then
last(r^{\prime})\succ_{r^{\prime}}h^{\prime}
. This implies
(r^{\prime},h^{\prime})
cannot block

because

. Therefore, let us assume that
h^{\prime}
is a distracting hospital. Suppose

is one of the edges for which

is distracting. This implies
h^{\prime}\succ_{r}h
. Since
(r,h)\in M_{p}^{*}
and

is a strongly stable matching in

, the hospital
h^{\prime}
must be fully subscribed in
M_{p}^{*}
with respect to its augmented quota,
q_{p}^{*}(h^{\prime})\geq q_{p}(h^{\prime})
, and all residents matched to
h^{\prime}
are strictly better than
r^{\prime}
. Thus,
(r^{\prime},h^{\prime})
cannot be a blocking pair with respect to
M_{p}^{*}
.

Therefore, no edge in
E_{d}
blocks the matching
M_{p}^{*}
output at the end of
Step 3.
∎

Having proved that the matching
M_{p}^{*}
is strongly stable in the augmented instance
G_{p}^{*}
of

, we proceed to prove the optimality of
G_{p}^{*}
.

We first show, using Lemma

, that it suffices to start with the pruned instance

.

Lemma 3.2

Let
\widetilde{G}
be any augmented instance obtained from
G
such that
\widetilde{G}
admits a strongly stable matching
\widetilde{M}
where

. Then, the following hold:

(i)

\widetilde{M}\cap E_{d}=Q
.

(ii)

Moreover,
\widetilde{M}\setminus Q
is a strongly stable matching in some augmented instance of
G_{p}
.

Proof

Recall that
Q\subseteq E_{d}
. Therefore, we need to show that if there exists an edge
(r^{\prime},h^{\prime})\in E_{d}\setminus Q
, then
(r^{\prime},h^{\prime})\notin\widetilde{M}
. Recall the resident set

. The fact that

implies that if an edge
(r^{\prime},h^{\prime})\in E_{d}\setminus Q
such that

, then
(r^{\prime},h^{\prime})\notin\widetilde{M}
.

Now, let us assume that
(r^{\prime},h^{\prime})\in E_{d}\setminus Q
is an edge such that
r^{\prime}\notin Q_{\mathcal{R}}
. For any such edge
(r^{\prime},h^{\prime})
, at least one of the following holds: (i)
r^{\prime}
is a distracting resident, or (ii)
h^{\prime}
is a distracting hospital. Let us first assume that
r^{\prime}
is a distracting resident for some edge

. This implies

. Also, the fact that
(r^{\prime},h^{\prime})\in E_{d}\setminus Q
implies that

. Thus, if
(r^{\prime},h^{\prime})\in\widetilde{M}
, then
(r^{\prime},h)
blocks the matching

because

; a contradiction. Now, let us assume that

is a distracting hospital for some edge

. This implies that

. Also, since

, we have

. Since

, if
(r^{\prime},h^{\prime})\in\widetilde{M}
, the edge

blocks the matching

; a contradiction. This completes the first part of the proof.

First, we observe that

Lemma 4.1

If

admits a satisfying assignment, then there exists an instance

obtained from

Given a valid satisfying assignment for the instance

and a matching

as follows. For each variable

for all

Next, we show that the augmented instance

\displaystyle M_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ \bigcup_{p=1}^{\beta}\{(a_{p},v_{p})\}

\displaystyle M_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ \bigcup_{v_{p}\ :\ q(v_{p})>1}\{(b_{p}^{s},v_{p})\ |\ (b_{p}^{s},v_{p})\in E\text{ for some }s\}

\displaystyle M_{3}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ \bigcup_{v_{p}\ :\ q(v_{p})=1}\{(b_{p}^{s},w^{s})\ |\ (b_{p}^{s},w^{s})\in E\text{ for some }s\}

We now verify that the matching

•

We note that each resident is matched to at most one hospital. It is clear that each

. Therefore, if

, then

), and hence

. Thus, every

-resident and

We also note that the matching

In this section, we consider the

problem. We show that the
MinMax-SS
problem is NP-hard even for a very restricted setting.
For this, we consider a special case of this problem, where
q(h)=1
for all
h\in\mathcal{H}
and a budget
\ell=1
. We refer to this special case as the
1-or-2 Capacity-SS problem
, since the quota of each hospital in the augmented instance is restricted to either 1 or 2. Next, we show the hardness of
1-or-2 Capacity-SS problem
.

5.1
NP-hardness of
1-or-2 Capacity-SS problem

We prove the hardness of
1-or-2 Capacity-SS problem
by reducing from an instance of the
Monotone Not-All-Equal 3-SAT

\alpha
and
\beta
be two non-negative integers. The
Monotone Not-All-Equal 3-SAT
is a variant of the Boolean satisfiability problem where the input is a conjunction of clauses. Each clause is a disjunction of exactly three variables, and no variable appears in negated form. The goal is to determine whether there exists a truth assignment to the variables such that for each clause, at least one variable is set to true and at least one to false. This problem is known to be NP-complete
[
30
]
even when each variable appears in exactly four clauses
[
12
]
.

Single-peaked preferences originate from the study of elections, where preferences are typically assumed to be strict and complete. Preferences are called single-peaked if there is an arrangement of alternatives such that each voter’s preference graph has only one local maximum. In this graph, alternatives are on the
x
-axis, and scores assigned by voters are on the
y
-axis. Single-peaked preferences have also been considered for stable matchings
[
4
,
3
,
9
]
. Social choice literature has adapted these notions to incomplete preferences as well
[
9
,
14
]
. For the bipartite graph
G=(\mathcal{R}\cup\mathcal{H},E)
, providing a strict linear ordering over
\mathcal{R}
and
\mathcal{H}
separately suffices.
property.
We note that the preference list of a degree-2 vertex is always single-peaked with respect to any linear ordering, and the preference list of a degree-3 vertex is single-peaked with respect to the strict linear ordering if its least-preferred neighbor does not lie between its two other neighbors in that ordering. Now, we give the strict linear ordering of hospitals in
G
as follows. Let
V^{s}
denote the strict order
\langle v_{i}^{s},v_{j}^{s},v_{k}^{s}\rangle
and
W
to denote the strict order
\langle w^{1},w^{2},\ldots,w^{\alpha}\rangle
. Then the strict linear ordering of hospitals is given as
\langle V^{1},V^{2},\ldots,V^{\alpha},W\rangle
. It is trivial to observe that for any resident
r
, the preference list of
r
has a single peak over the above linear ordering. Thus, resident preferences are single-peaked.

Now, we show that the hospital preference lists are derived from a master list. We give a master list of residents as follows. Let
A^{s}
denote the strict order
\langle a_{i}^{s},a_{j}^{s},a_{k}^{s}\rangle
,
B
denote the tie containing all the
b

denote the tie
(d_{1}^{s},d_{2}^{s},d_{3}^{s})

\langle A^{1},A^{2},\ldots,A^{\alpha},B,D^{1},D^{2},\ldots,D^{\alpha}\rangle

Lemma

Conclusion

In this paper, we study the capacity augmentation problem for strongly stable matchings in the
HR-HT
setting. We investigate two natural optimization criteria,
MinSum
and
MinMax
.

The NP-hardness of the
MinMax-SS
problem naturally raises the question of whether a good approximation algorithm exists; we leave this as an open question. Our work considers only quota
augmentations
; the complementary problem of quota
reductions
, studied for strict preferences by Gokhale
et al
.
[
18
]
, remains largely unexplored in the presence of ties and is a natural next step. Finally, the
MinSum-Cost
inapproximability result relies on zero-one costs. It would be interesting to identify structural restrictions on the cost function or preference lists under which the problem becomes tractable. For instance, uniform costs reduce to
MinSum-SS
and are polynomial-time solvable.
