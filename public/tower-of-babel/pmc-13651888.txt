Citron kinase activity controls alternative splicing events that drive prostate cancer growth
Authors: Chitra Rawat, Nidhi Singh, Ujjwal R. Dahiya, Qiang Hu, Sarah Ng, Dingxiao Zhang, Shaun R. Stauffer, Amina Zoubeidi, Eva Corey, Jesse McKenney, Christopher J. Weight, Samuel C. Haywood, Song Liu, Tao Liu, Hannelore V. Heemers
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Prostate cancer (CaP) remains a major cause of cancer death in men, underscoring the need for mechanistically novel therapies. We previously identified the action of the mitotic kinase citron (CIT) kinase as a key driver of CaP progression and promising therapeutic target, but selective CIT inhibitors are not available. We therefore asked whether a better understanding of the action of CIT substrates, which execute CIT kinase action in CaP cells, can lead to novel approaches and/or alternative targets to indirectly interfere with CIT’s activity during CaP progression. In view of the enrichment in functions in alternative splicing among CIT substrates, we focus on the RNA splicing regulator THRAP3 as a representative substrate. Direct THRAP3 phosphorylation by CIT determined THRAP3’s RNA binding to CIT-controlled pre–messenger RNA targets. Cross-linking and immunoprecipitation sequencing identified a subset of CIT-dependent THRAP3-bound genes that were preferentially involved in cell proliferation. THRAP3 RNA binding was enriched in genes that undergo alternative splicing during CaP progression. CIT-dependent THRAP3-bound transcripts stimulated the growth of CaP cell lines, xenografts, patient-derived organoids, and patient-derived xenograft (PDX)–derived organoids, whereas CIT-independent THRAP3-bound transcripts from the same gene did not. An antisense oligo that blocks THRAP3’s recruitment to a CIT-dependent RNA binding site prevented the production of CIT- and THRAP3-dependent transcripts and inhibited the growth of cell lines and PDX-derived organoids representing different CaP stages and lineages but not of benign cells. Thus, CIT-mediated alternative splicing drives CaP progression, and its inhibition is a new therapeutic strategy in treatment-resistant CaP.

INTRODUCTION

Acquired resistance to the treatments for metastatic disease remains the major cause of the more than 36,000 American prostate cancer (CaP) deaths each year (1, 2). There is an urgent need for novel treatment options that are mechanistically different from current therapies and target biological processes that drive CaP progression. Recent phosphoproteomics studies using patient specimens and patient-derived xenograft (PDX) tissues that reflect clinical CaP progression have underscored deregulated action of kinases with implications for aggressive CaP behavior and treatment resistance (3–9). However, the kinases that control the emergence of the specific phosphomarks that are enriched during CaP progression remain mostly elusive. Moreover, a considerable fraction of human kinases is understudied, and their substrates, binding motifs, and manner of influencing CaP behavior are not completely understood. In addition, specific kinase inhibitors often are not available. These limitations have hindered the development of approaches to target the action of clinically relevant kinases to prevent CaP growth and progression.

We recently identified the poorly characterized Ser/Thr kinase citron kinase (CIT) as a pivotal determinant of CaP cell proliferation and a previously unrecognized driver of lethal CaP progression and treatment resistance (10). CIT was overexpressed in CaP and when overexpressed experimentally, CIT was tumorigenic, drove CaP growth, and overcame growth inhibition by the standard of care treatment enzalutamide. Conversely, silencing of CIT delayed CaP growth in vitro and in vivo, irrespective of androgen receptor (AR) status or disease stage without affecting nontransformed prostate cells and other benign cells—indicating a large therapeutic window to interfere with CIT action in CaP. CIT’s ability to induce CaP growth relied entirely on its kinase activity. A single point mutation that renders CIT kinase dead (11) was sufficient to blunt CaP cell proliferation, indicating an important role for phosphorylated CIT substrates in CaP growth (10). As these substrates remained unknown, we used mass spectrometry approaches in combination with phosphoenrichment strategies to uncover the CIT-dependent phosphomarks in its interactome. Unexpectedly, our data showed that 37% of the proposed CIT substrates function in alternative splicing (10). Validating these results, 50% of proteins were listed as components of the major spliceosome in the Spliceosome database (12). Reanalyzing CIT-dependent RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) data that we had generated from CaP cells that permit CIT silencing, we confirmed that CIT controls expression of close to 900 alternative transcripts. CIT-dependent transcript variants overlapped with those from genes for which alternative splicing is enriched during CaP progression and treatment resistance (10). Alternative splicing has been proposed as hallmark of aggressive CaP progression, and its regulation is long recognized to be tightly regulated by phosphorylation (13, 14). Our findings thus indicated that CIT mediates aggressive CaP behavior at least, in part, through phosphorylation of substrates that execute alternative splicing. As a selective CIT inhibitor that prevents in vivo CaP growth has proven difficult to develop (10, 15), we reasoned that interference with the activity of CIT’s phosphorylated substrates may lead to alternative therapeutic approaches, as CIT substrates mediate CIT’s action on CaP growth. Given the importance of CIT-dependent splicing events as targets for CIT substrates and their relevance during treatment-resistant CaP progression, specific targeting of the mechanism(s) that control these events may hold promise as an alternate therapeutic strategy in late-stage CaP.

Here, we focused on THRAP3, a representative CIT substrate that associates with the major spliceosome complex (12). We found that THRAP3 is directly phosphorylated by CIT and that CIT regulates a subset of THRAP3 RNA binding sites. CIT-dependent THRAP3-bound transcripts control the growth of CaP cell lines, xenografts, patient-derived organoids (PDOs), and PDX-derived organoids (PDXOs), whereas CIT-independent THRAP3-bound transcripts from the same genes did not. Antisense oligos (ASOs) that block THRAP3’s recruitment to its CIT-dependent RNA binding sites inhibit the growth of cell lines and PDXOs representing different CaP stages but not of benign cells. Our work thus provides new insights into how deregulated CIT action controls alternative splicing that drives CaP progression. It lays the foundation also for an entirely novel therapeutic strategy that restores a less aggressive splicing pattern to treat CaP that has acquired resistance to current therapies.

RESULTS

CIT’s kinase activity is critical for CaP growth and progression

We previously showed that transient expression of kinase-dead CIT [K126A, CIT-KD (11)] prevents the proliferation of CaP cells in vitro (10). However, CaP cell lines that stably and constitutively overexpress CIT-KD could not be established. Achieving CIT-KD levels that exceed endogenous CIT levels in transient transfections also proved very challenging, confirming the detrimental effects of abrogating CIT’s kinase function on CaP cell survival. Thus, to validate in established CaPs that CIT kinase activity is critical to progression, we instead used a cell line that allows for doxycycline-inducible CIT-KD overexpression in xenograft studies. Doxycycline treatment resulted in decreased viability of these cells, consistent with our previous results (10). Treating mice with doxycycline when engrafted CaPs reached 100 mm3 significantly delayed disease progression compared to animals receiving vehicle (Fig. 1A). That the doses of doxycycline that were used do not negatively affect CaP growth and cell proliferation has been validated before (10, 16).

This confirmation of CIT’s reliance on an intact kinase domain prompted us to further characterize CIT’s kinase action in CaP cells. We set out to define CIT’s consensus substrate motif. We performed in vitro kinase assays using a recombinant CIT kinase domain and peptide arrays containing 15-mer peptide sequences from human proteins and analyzed the phosphorylated 15-mer peptide sequences using PhosphoSitePlus tools to obtain a 15-mer consensus phosphorylation motif for CIT (fig. S1, A and B, Supplementary Text, and table S1). To independently verify this 15-mer, we reanalyzed phosphoproteomics data from a kinase substrate atlas for 303 human serine/threonine kinases, which included CIT, which was recently generated using in vitro phosphorylation assays on synthetic peptides (17). We examined CIT-dependent phosphorylated peptide data from the kinase substrate atlas using the same PhosphoSitePlus tools and the same 15-mer cutoff. Motifs from our 15-mer peptide array and from the kinase atlas were similar and reflect the preference for arginine residues at positions −5 and −3 relative to the phosphoacceptor site (P0) that has been noted for other members of the AGC family of kinases (fig. S1, B and C, Supplementary Results, and table S1) (18, 19). Moreover, via in vitro kinase assays using a recombinant CIT kinase domain and substrate peptides matching the ideal phosphomotif logo sequences, we confirmed CIT-dependent phosphorylation of the motifs from our screening and from the kinase substrate atlas screening. The CIT-dependent phosphorylation at these phosphomotifs reached the same extent as that of a 15-mer peptide derived for the positive control histone 3 (fig. S1D) (10). These results support our identification of a bona fide consensus phosphorylation motif for CIT. Next, we crossmatched the 149 protein sequences that contained CIT-dependent phosphomarks that we found in our prior mass spectrometry studies (10) against the CIT substrate motif that we had delineated. We found that 49% of the 15-mer spanning the predicted phosphoacceptor Ser or Thr contained at least seven amino acids matching those present in the logo (table S2). This congruence is consistent with observations that protein kinase substrate specificity is generally determined by a limited subset of residues surrounding the phosphoacceptor site. Synthetic peptides retaining only the most informative residues often preserve efficient phosphorylation [e.g., (20–22)]. Moreover, we found that 37 of these marks were significantly enriched [false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05; analysis of variance (ANOVA)] in phosphoproteome data from the specimens of patients with castration-resistant CaP (CRPC; Fig. 1B) (6), confirming the relevance of CIT’s kinase action in CaP progression and treatment resistance.

Spliceosome component THRAP3 is a direct CIT substrate

We sought to define further the importance of CIT-dependent phosphorylation of its substrates on CaP growth and progression. In view of the enrichment for functions related to alternative splicing in predicted CIT substrates (10), we focused on a representative substrate, THRAP3, which associates with the major spliceosome (12) and is well recognized to mediate alternative splicing (23, 24). We previously reported that THRAP3’s overall serine phosphorylation was decreased upon silencing of CIT in LNCaP and VCaP cells. Moreover, loss of THRAP3 mimicked the inhibitory effects on cell proliferation that we observed after CIT silencing (10). Similar to loss of CIT, loss of THRAP3 did not alter the viability of benign nonprostate human embryonic kidney 293T cells and transformed, but nonmalignant RWPE-1 prostate epithelial cells (25), despite similar decreases in THRAP3 levels in these cells compared to LNCaP cells following THRAP3 knockdown (fig. S2). Moreover, overexpression of THRAP3 overcame, at least in part, the inhibitory effects of CIT silencing on CaP viability, supporting the hypothesis that the effects of CIT on cell proliferation are mediated at least, in part, by THRAP3 (fig. S3A). THRAP3 overexpression in vehicle [dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO)]–treated control cells stimulated cell viability, again reminiscent of the impact of CIT overexpression (fig. S3A). RNA–immunoprecipitation (IP) (RIP) assays confirmed that THRAP3 but not CIT binds to representative pre-mRNAs for transcripts such as ESYT2 and FN1 that our previous rMATS analyses had returned as CIT dependent (10) but not the pre-mRNAs of the housekeeping genes β-actin and β-tubulin (fig. S3B). Review of our prior mass spectrometry data (10) suggested three CIT-dependent phosphosites in THRAP3, namely, S243, S320 and S379. In vitro kinase assays using recombinant CIT kinase domain and peptides that span these three putative phosphorylation sites confirmed phosphorylation at S243 and S379 but not S320 (Fig. 2A). These results suggest that the phosphorylation at S320 that was observed in mass spectrometry data may reflect indirect effects of CIT activity. Mutation of phosphoacceptor serine residues S243 to A243 (S243A) in the corresponding peptide completely prevented CIT-dependent phosphorylation at S243. A similar phosphodead mutation only partially decreased adenosine 5′-triphosphate (ATP) consumption for the S379 phosphomark [and increased its fold change (FC)] (Fig. 2A). In combination with the enriched phosphorylation of S243 in treatment-resistant patient specimens (Fig. 1B) (6), these results suggest that S243 is the major site for CIT kinase action in THRAP3.

CIT-induced THRAP3 phosphorylation stimulates CaP growth

We generated site-specific mutations that individually render S243 and S379 phosphodead (S243A and S379A) to determine the functional and cellular implications of CIT-dependent phosphorylation at these sites. Expression levels for these mutants were similar to those of wild-type THRAP3 (Fig. 2B). Ectopic expression of THRAP3-S243A in treatment-naïve LNCaP cells prevented the increase in cell viability that was found for wild-type THRAP3. Mutation at S379 did not alter increased viability observed for intact THRAP3, suggesting that phosphorylation of S243 controls the relevance of THRAP3 for CaP growth (Fig. 2C). Similar results were found for treatment-naïve VCaP cells and for C4-2 cells and 42FENZR cells, isogenic sublines from LNCaP that have failed one or more rounds of standard of care androgen deprivation therapy (ADT), and reflect AR-dependent CRPC and AR-independent ADT-resistant [neuroendocrine CaP (NEPC)] late-stage CaP phenotypes, respectively (Fig. 2D). To test whether these mutations have an additive effect on CaP cell viability, we also included expression constructs that encode a double phosphodead mutant of THRAP3 (S243A and S379A). The added mutation at S379 did not affect the growth-inhibitory effects of the S243A mutant, again verifying S243 as the dominant CIT-dependent phosphosite in THRAP3 (Fig. 2E). Ki67 staining assays confirmed increased cell proliferation with wild-type THRAP3 but not with the THRAP3 S243A or THRAP3 S243A and S379A mutants (Fig. 2F). Overall, these studies indicate a critical role for CIT-dependent direct phosphorylation of THRAP3 at S243 in CaP growth.

CIT controls THRAP3’s RNA binding

CIT-dependent phosphorylation controls THRAP3 nuclear localization

THRAP3 is a nuclear RNA binding protein that regulates the splicing of gene transcripts. Because THRAP3 phosphorylation can influence its cellular localization in other model systems (26), we next examined the effect of CIT-dependent phosphorylation at S243 and S379 on THRAP3’s cellular distribution. Immunofluorescence staining confirmed that CIT loss alters THRAP3 localization in LNCaP cells from nuclear to cytoplasmic (fig. S4). Moreover, immunofluorescence staining on LNCaP cells transfected with the THRAP3 phosphodead mutants showed that only the THRAP3 S243A mutation affected THRAP3’s localization in a manner similar to CIT loss (Fig. 2G). These findings suggested that CIT affects THRAP3 localization in a S243 phosphorylation–dependent manner and therefore may influence THRAP3’s ability to bind to RNA.

CIT-dependent phosphorylation controls THRAP3 RNA binding at a subset of genes

However, the extent of THRAP3’s transcriptome-wide RNA binding in CaP cells and its reliance on CIT remained unknown. Therefore, we developed a cross-linking and immunoprecipitation sequencing (CLIP-seq) assay using an antibody targeting THRAP3 and used it on LNCaP cells that allow for doxycycline-induced short hairpin RNA (shRNA)–mediated silencing of CIT. Data from input samples were used to reduce background noise while calling genes that are bound by THRAP3. Compared to input samples, upon THRAP3 IP, 3396 THRAP3-binding genes were enriched ≥1.5-fold (FDR < 0.05) in vehicle-treated cells. After doxycycline-mediated CIT silencing, 311 of these THRAP3-bound genes were affected (≥1.5 FC, FDR < 0.05) (Fig. 3A)—we refer to those genes as CIT-dependent. For the majority (n = 300) of these genes, THRAP3 binding was decreased after loss of CIT (Fig. 3A). For instance, the decreased set contained the FN1 pre-mRNA, confirming our previous findings of lower levels of an FN1 transcript after loss of CIT (10) and our current results that THRAP3 but not CIT binds FN1 pre-mRNA (fig. S3B). The ESYT2 sequence, which our prior rMATS analyses confirmed to yield a CIT-dependent transcript (10) and was THRAP3 bound (fig. S3B) was present in our CLIP-seq data but at an FC of 1.47, just below the applied cutoff of 1.5. That THRAP3 binds to these pre-mRNAs validates our CLIP-seq data. No difference in distribution of THRAP3 binding strength, which was measured as FC compared to input, was seen between CIT-dependent genes and other THRAP3-binding genes. Our technical validation of CLIP-seq data included RIP for THRAP3 at newly identified RNA binding sites such as carnitine palmitoyltransferase 1A (CPT1A), alpha-methylacyl-CoA-racemase (AMACR), and prune homolog 2 with BCH domain (PRUNE2) pre-mRNAs, where THRAP3 binding was reduced after doxycycline-mediated CIT silencing (Fig. 3B). Representative tracks are presented in Fig. 3C. That the decrease in THRAP3 RNA binding was due to CIT silencing and not related to the doxycyclin treatment was confirmed in LNCaP cells that stably express doxycycline-inducible nontargeting shRNAs. The latter cell line did not show reduced THRAP3 binding to representative pre-mRNAs after doxycycline treatment (fig. S5). THRAP3 silencing altered the expression of CIT-dependent transcripts to the same extent as CIT loss. Figure 3D shows results obtained for several CIT-dependent transcripts for AMACR and a CIT-dependent and a CIT-independent transcript for CPT1A in LNCaP and C4-2 cells. Notably, THRAP3 recruitment at CPT1A and AMACR pre-mRNAs decreased when S243 was rendered phosphodead (Fig. 3E). Moreover, overexpression of THRAP3 induced expression of CIT-dependent CPT1A and AMACR transcripts, which THRAP3 S243A mutant was unable to do (Fig. 3F). These results support that CIT’s effects on RNA binding are mediated by THRAP3 phosphorylation at S243.

CIT-dependent phosphorylation controls THRAP3-dependent splicing events

To better determine how the action of wild-type THRAP3 and THRAP3 S243A on alternative splicing differs in CaP cells, we performed overexpression studies with these THRAP3 versions in CaP cells. Figure 4A confirms significant overexpression of both wild-type THRAP3 and THRAP3 S243A compared to control-transfected cells. We then performed RNA-seq on RNA extracted from these cells and analyzed RNA-seq data from the three transfection groups for evidence of alternative splicing using rMATS, using an FDR < 0.05 as cutoff (10, 27). We considered the five major types of splicing events (Fig. 4B). Compared to cells expressing empty vector only, overexpression of wild-type THRAP3 altered 880 splicing events, most of which were skipped exon events, while THRAP3 S243A affected 928 splicing events, again with a preference for exon skipping (Fig. 4C). Because of the similar number of events affected by both wild-type and mutant THRAP3, we asked whether the events controlled by both THRAP3 versions could overlap. rMATS comparison between data from cells overexpressing the wild-type version versus the S243A mutant revealed 878 differentially spliced events, arguing against THRAP3 maintaining its activity after mutation at the S243 site. To verify this result, for each class of splicing events, we compared the overlap between events induced by wild-type THRAP3 and by THRAP3 S243A and, to facilitate interpretation of results, made this determination for both up- and down-regulated events. Because matching rMATS data based on exact splice sites is impractical, we performed these analyses using gene symbols. As shown in Fig. 4D, results from these studies confirmed limited overlap between events under the control of both THRAP3 versions. Numbers returned in Fig. 4D are a bit lower than number of splicing events in Fig. 4C, as a gene can undergo splicing at different sites. To examine the biological implications of the distinct splicing patterns that were induced by wild-type THRAP3 and by THRAP3 S243S, we performed pathway analyses on the genes whose splicing patterns were affected by wild-type THRAP3 (880 splicing events) and THRAP3 S243A (928 events), respectively. No significant results were found in hallmark gene sets analyses. Gene Ontology (GO) Biological Process (BP) (GO:BP) gene sets analyses, however, returned 10 gene sets for genes whose alternative splicing was controlled by wild-type THRAP3 and 6 gene sets for genes for which it was affected by THRAP3 S243A (Fig. 4E). No overlap was found between these gene sets, supporting that the distinct alternative splicing by wild-type THRAP3 and THRAP3 S243A affects different aspects of CaP cell biology.

CIT- and THRAP3-dependent transcripts preferentially stimulate CaP growth

CIT-dependent THRAP3-bound genes associate with cellular processes involved in cytokinesis

Using gene function overrepresentation analyses, we examined whether and how CIT-dependent and -independent THRAP3-bound transcripts differ in biological functions and impact on CaP cell biology. GO:BP enrichment (FDR < 0.25) analyses for CIT-dependent THRAP-bound genes from our CLIP-seq studies returned functions related to cell division (e.g., chromosome segregation, microtubule cytoskeleton organization involved in mitosis, and DNA replication), consistent with CIT function (Fig. 5A) (28). Figure S6 shows that overexpression of CIT-KD increased the number of multinucleated cells, and that overexpression of THRAP3 S243A has similar inhibitory effects on cytokinesis. In contrast, CIT-independent THRAP3-bound genes or “other” genes returned mostly functions that relate to RNA processing, location, and transport, consistent with THRAP3’s well-known roles in RNA binding and RNA processing (Fig. 5A and tables S3 and S4). These results indicated that CIT-dependent THRAP3-bound genes preferentially control cell proliferation. The Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB) hallmark gene sets analyses confirmed that CIT-dependent THRAP3-bound genes more strongly associated with proliferation than other THRAP3-recruiting genes (Fig. 5B).

THRAP3 and CIT- and THRAP3-dependent transcripts are involved in cell proliferation

These findings indicated that CIT phosphorylation of THRAP3 contributes to aggressive CaP progression. As the role of THRAP3 is understudied in CaP cells and the mechanisms by which THRAP3 contributes to CaP growth remain largely unknown, we performed RNA-seq on RNA from LNCaP cells that had been transfected with small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) targeting THRAP3 or nontargeting control siRNA. The efficacy of THRAP3 silencing is confirmed in Fig. 6A. Upon THRAP3 loss, expression of 108 genes changed at least twofold (P < 0.05), with 52 genes showing down-regulation and 56 genes down-regulation (Fig. 6B). We then performed gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA; FDR < 0.25) to determine which MSigDB gene sets relate most to the differential gene expression pattern we observed. Significantly associated hallmark gene sets included several related to cell proliferation (e.g., E2F targets, Myc targets, and G2-M checkpoint) (Fig. 6C), reminescent of our previous findings for CIT (10), and supported by the role for THRAP3 in CaP cell proliferation that we reported on [fig. S3A and (10)]. Several associated GO:BP gene sets related to viral genome reproduction and regulation of viral processes (Fig. 6D), consistent with literature reports that CIT is involved in HIV-1 budding (29) and our previous MSigDB results after CIT depletion in CaP cells (10). Given THRAP3’s well-known role in splicing regulation (23, 24), we also performed rMATS analyses on the RNA-seq data from cells transfected with THRAP3-targeting siRNAs versus control-transfected cells. These studies revealed alterations in almost 1400 alternative splicing events upon THRAP3 loss, most of which were skipped exon changes (Fig. 6E). These results indicate that the effect of THRAP3 on splicing is more pronounced than that on regulating gene expression in CaP cells.

In view of these findings, we examined also a representative splicing target for THRAP3 whose transcript generation is controlled by both THRAP3 and CIT, namely, CPT1A (Fig. 3, B and D). To better understand the impact of CPT1A (splicing) on CaP cell biology, we again performed RNA-seq studies using siRNAs that target all CPT1A transcripts in CaP cells and compared this to the effects of siRNAs that specially target the CIT/THRAP3-independent transcript variant 1 (v1) or the CIT/THRAP3-dependent transcript variant 2 (v2) (Fig. 3D) or those of nontargeting siRNA. Quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) assays confirmed the (a)specific knockouts of CPT1A transcript expression by these siRNA transfections (Fig. 6F). Global silencing of CPT1A transcripts led to differential expression of 229 genes (2FC, P < 0.05), while loss of CPT1A v1 altered expression of 286 genes (Fig. 6G). Silencing of the CIT- and THRAP3-dependent CPT1A v2 had the most pronounced effect with 899 genes changing expression (Fig. 6G). Results for GSEA hallmark gene set and GO:BP gene set analyses on CPT1A (transcript)–dependent differential gene expression are shown in Fig. 6 (H and I). Genes altered after loss of all CPT1A transcripts did not significantly overlap with any hallmark or GO:BP gene sets.

Because CIT-dependent splicing of CPT1A pre-mRNA depends on THRAP3, we then determined the overlap in THRAP3- and CPT1A (transcript)–dependent gene expression from our RNA studies. Focussing on genes whose expression was at least twofold up or down-regulated upon siRNA transfection, we noted most pronounced overlap in genes between THRAP3-dependent genes and those controlled by the CIT-dependent CPT1A v2 (Fig. 6J). This preferential gene overlap after THRAP3 and CPT1A v2 loss was reflected in gene set overlap. Five of seven hallmark gene sets returned after THRAP3 loss overlapped with those affected by CPT1A v2 loss (Fig. 6, C and H, and table S5), compared to only two for hallmark gene sets that were altered after CPT1A v1 depletion (Fig. 6, C and H, and table S6). Similarly, all six GO:BP gene sets affected by THRAP3 loss overlapped with CPT1A v2–dependent gene sets (Fig. 6, D and I, and table S7), but only 1 corresponded to CPT1A v1–controlled GO:BP gene sets (Fig 6, D and I). Together, these results support an important role for THRAP3 and the CIT-dependent transcripts under its control in CaP growth.

CIT- and THRAP3-dependent transcripts preferentially control CaP progression

Next, we assessed the clinical relevance of our CLIP-seq results further by examining the representation of THRAP3-bound genes in gene sets that are subjected to alternative splicing during CaP progression (13). THRAP3 RNA binding was enriched in genes that undergo alternative splicing in localized CaP tissues compared to benign prostate tissues and increased further in those that are alternatively spliced in CRPC and NEPC (Fig. 7A). Because of the relatively small size of these gene sets, it was difficult to ascertain whether enrichment for CIT-dependent THRAP3-bound genes differed from that of other THRAP3-bound genes. Our analyses of OncoSplicing (30) data confirmed enrichment of THRAP3-bound sequences in malignant versus benign prostate tissue (fig. S7). To experimentally determine whether CIT-(in)dependent transcripts differentially affect CaP cell proliferation, we had CIT-dependent and CIT-independent transcripts synthesized for ESYT2 [CIT-dependent = v1, long variant (10)] and CPT1A (CIT-dependent = v2) (Fig. 7B). Overexpression of CIT-dependent variants of CPT1A and ESYT2 preferentially enhanced the viability and increased the percentage of Ki67-positive LNCaP and C4-2 cells (Fig. 7, C and D). Moreover, xenograft studies using LNCaP cells with doxycycline-inducible expression of CPT1A v1 or v2 showed that induction of CPT1A v2 in established CaPs increases CaP growth (Fig. 7E), while in contrast, inducible expression of CPT1A v1 delayed in vivo CaP growth. We have shown before that doxycyclin treatment of xenografts from cells that express empty vector does not affect CaP growth (16). Furthermore, we noted similar effects in PDOs that we generated from ADT-naïve fresh CaP tissues and transduced with lentiviral vectors that encode CPT1A v1 or variant 2. Over 15 days, control organoids steadily increased in size, whereas those expressing CPT1A v1 decreased. Overexpression of CPT1A v2 again stimulated PDO growth (Fig. 7F). We noted similar effects in organoids from the CRPC LuCaP86.2 PDX model (31) that was derived from CRPC tissues obtained at warm autopsy (Fig. 7G). These findings confirm that CIT-dependent transcripts control CaP growth, suggesting that preventing the RNA binding events that control generation of these transcripts may be a therapeutic strategy in treatment-resistant CaP.

Overexpression of THRAP3 RNA binding sequences inhibit CaP growth

To further delineate the biological significance of THRAP3, we reanalyzed CLIP-seq data to retrieve and characterize THRAP3 RNA binding motif sequences. MEME motif analyses returned as significant two consensus motifs for the CIT-controlled THRAP3 RNA binding sites and four for the other THRAP3-binding sites. Although the motifs showed similarity overall, the most significantly enriched CIT-dependent motif present appeared to demonstrate less stringent sequence requirements at its 5′ end (Fig. 8A). These motifs also resembled a previously reported THRAP3 RNA binding motif obtained from osteosarcoma cells (32). To validate that THRAP3 binds these motifs, we designed a biotinylated RNA sequence that contains three copies of a 10-mer that is present in THRAP3 binding peak of the CPT1A pre-mRNA. Using this decoy RNA sequence, we performed streptavidin pull-down assays using LNCaP cell lysates. The biotinylated 3× THRAP3 binding-motif sequence but not a scrambled control sequence dose-dependently bound THRAP3 but not CIT or other nonspecific proteins [e.g., serum response factor (SRF) and lamin A/C] (Fig. 8B). THRAP3 S243A, which cannot be phosphorylated by CIT, bound this decoy RNA considerably less strongly (fig. S8). Moreover, overexpression of this specific RNA decoy decreased the expression of the CIT-dependent alternative CPT1A v2 (Fig. 8C), supporting that the decoy exerts on-target activity. We found that overexpression of this biotinylated probe also reduces the viability of CaP cells that represent different stages of CaP progression and treatment resistance (Fig. 8D) but not the transformed but benign RWPE-1 prostate epithelial cells (Fig. 8E). To verify whether these findings could be extrapolated to other THRAP3 binding-motifs, despite the overall high similarity among motifs (Fig. 8A), we examined a second and sequentially somewhat different motif (CCTGCTTCCC, corresponding to CIT-dependent motif #2 in Fig. 8A). This second motif is present in the pre-mRNA for RBM10, which is bound by THRAP3 in a CIT-dependent manner in our CLIP-seq data (Fig. 3). We confirmed that RBM10 transcript v2 expression depends on CIT and THRAP3 and found that wild-type THRAP3 but not THRAP3 S243A is robustly recruited to a biotinylated RNA sequence that contains three copies of this motif (fig. S9, A and B). Overexpression of this RNA decoy reduced expression of RBM10 v2 and inhibited viability of CaP cells (fig. S9, C and D).

The latter findings suggested that CIT-dependent THRAP3 RNA binding motifs can be exploited therapeutically using ASOs matching these sequences. This is an attractive possibility, as ASOs that target splicing events have already been Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approved for use in humans for genetic diseases, such as Duchenne muscular dystrophy and spinal muscular atrophy (33–35). We designed an ASO that is complementary to one copy of the CPT1A-derived CIT-dependent THRAP3 RNA binding site and consists of the 10–base pair (bp) THRAP3 RNA binding motif sequence flanked at its 5′ and 3′ end by 10 bps each that match the CPT1A pre-mRNA. We designed this ASO to have the same phosphorothioate backbone as FDA-approved drugs nusinersen and tofersen (36–38). We verified that the ASO does not affect THRAP3 expression (Fig. 9A). At 5 to 10 nM, this ASO decreased cell proliferation of ADT-naïve CaP cells (LNCaP), CRPC (C4-2), and NEPC cells (42FENZR, PC-3, and DU-145) but not the normal RWPE-1 cells (25, 39)—consistent with CaP-specific roles for CIT. The same doses of a scrambled ASO did not affect proliferation of these cell lines (Fig. 9, B and C). In trypan blue viability assays, the specific ASO decreased the percentage of live cells and increased the percentage of dead cells, suggesting that it mediates its effects, at least in part, by stimulating cell death (fig. S10). Furthermore, we found additive effects when low-dose ASO treatment was combined with enzalutamide or docetaxel, the standard of care ADT and chemotherapeutic approaches (40), respectively, in CRPC (fig. S11). Specific ASO treatment also reduced the growth of CRPC LuCaP 86.2 PDXOs (Fig. 9D) (31). Confirming on-target effects of the specific ASO, low-dose ASO treatment reduced CPT1A splicing to v2 (Fig. 9E). These results indicated that treatment with this specific ASO prevents THRAP3 RNA binding at CPT1A pre-mRNA, which we confirmed in RIP assays (Fig. 9F). Notably, treatments with these low doses of the CPT1A pre-mRNA–derived ASO were at least as effective in reducing cell viability as genetic and pharmacologic inhibition of CPT1A. siRNA-mediated silencing of the CIT- and THRAP3-dependent CPT1A v2 significantly decreased viability of CaP cells but not benign cells (fig. S12, A to C). In contrast, loss of CPT1A v1 showed only modest or no such inhibitory effects, whereas targeting all CPT1A expression did not significantly reduce cell viability (fig. S12, B and C). The CPT1A inhibitors etomoxir and perhexiline, neither of which inhibit CPT1A with complete selectivity (41, 42), dose-dependently decreased CaP cell viability (fig. S12D). These findings provide proof-of-concept evidence that ASO-mediated prevention of CIT-dependent THRAP3 RNA binding has therapeutic potential to inhibit the growth of advanced CaP (Fig. 9G).

DISCUSSION

CaP deaths are due to acquired resistance to the treatments for metastatic disease. The AR remains the main target for treatment of metastatic CaP (1, 43, 44). ADT interferes with androgen activation of AR and results in remission but eventually fails. The ensuing CRPC continues to rely on AR (1), and subsequent sequential administration of second- and higher-line ADT drugs can induce short-lived remission but does not cure CRPC. In a minority of patients with CRPC, these treatments foster epithelial plasticity that causes AR-indifferent lineages, often commonly referred to as NEPC, which are also incurable (45–47). Alternative CRPC treatment options include chemo-, immuno-, or radiotherapy, which lead to brief remissions but are not curative (40).

There is an urgent need to develop novel therapeutic approaches that target cellular mechanisms that differ from those that are bypassed by acquired resistance to current treatments. The strongest strategies inhibit processes that control aggressive CaP behavior and are relevant to CaP progression with sufficient CaP specificity to provide a suitable therapeutic window. The studies that we present here show that CIT-dependent THRAP3 RNA binding fulfills the criteria to serve as a target for such therapies.

CIT is a poorly characterized Ser/Thr AGC kinase that we recently found to be a driver of lethal CaP progression and treatment resistance (10). A key finding was that CIT’s kinase activity is critical to its oncogenic role. A fraction of CIT-interacting proteins that depend on CIT for phosphorylation were involved in the major spliceosome. This was consistent with CIT’s controls over a spectrum of alternative splicing events that are enriched during the progression from benign status to treatment-naive CaP to CRPC to NEPC (10). Alternative splicing has been reported as a hallmark of CaP aggressiveness and has long been recognized to be tightly controlled by phosphorylation (13, 14, 48). Because direct specific inhibition of CIT kinase activity is not feasible (10, 15) and CIT substrates convey its kinase action on cells, we reasoned that understanding how CIT substrates affect alternative splicing could lead to novel, more indirect strategies to target CIT for CaP treatments.

We focused on the representative CIT substrate THRAP3, a component of the major spliceosome (12), and demonstrated that THRAP3 silencing or overexpression mimics the inhibitory or stimulatory effects of similar manipulations of CIT on proliferation of CaP cells. Similar to CIT, loss of THRAP3 did not affect the growth of benign cells or nontransformed prostate epithelial cells. Moreover, we confirmed that THRAP3 is directly phosphorylated by CIT at two Ser residues, of which only Ser243 was relevant for THRAP3’s growth-stimulatory role. An intact Ser243 phosphomark was critical to retain THRAP3 in the nucleus, where alternative splicing takes place. These findings are consistent with phosphorylation of splicing factors regulating their nuclear import (49). In line with these observations, THRAP3 but not CIT was recruited to CIT-dependent splicing sites in a CIT- and CIT Ser243 phosphosite–dependent manner. The CIT- and THRAP3-dependent transcripts that we identified derived from genes that undergo alternative splicing during lethal CaP progression and preferentially controlled CaP cell proliferation. These results show that CIT mediates its control over aggressive CaP behavior, at least in part, by diversification of the CaP proteome. These results provide a fresh perspective on the molecular regulation of alternative splicing events that drive CaP progression. Although alternative splicing is implicated in CaP treatment resistance (13), the molecular basis for its generation and its biological consequences remain poorly understood and limited to a few events such as AR variants and REST splicing that have been studied in more depth (50–54) but did not occur in our data. A mechanism that coordinately controls alternative splicing relevant to treatment-resistant CaP by a central mediator, such as CIT (substrates), and whose reversal delays CaP progression, however, has not yet been reported.

Our findings provide novel insights into the role for THRAP3 in CaP. THRAP3 (also known as TRAP150) regulates RNA processing and pre-mRNA splicing, and, as part of the mediator complex, has also transcriptional coactivator activity (23, 24, 32, 55). The only literature report to date that addresses THRAP3’s role in CaP consists of a phosphoproteomics analysis that suggests a role of THRAP3 phosphorylation at S248 and S253 in CRPC growth (56). Our RNA-seq studies confirm the involvement of THRAP3 in several functions in cell division and cell proliferation and also link THRAP3 to viral genome action. Notably, both reflect cell functions that have been assigned to CIT before (10, 28, 29). Our RNA-seq and rMATS analyses thereof indicate also that THRAP3 may have a more pronounced role in control over alternative splicing compared to regulating gene expression in CaP. A pan-cancer clinical investigation analyzed TCGA and GTex datasets to compare THRAP3 RNA levels in cancer and normal tissues from different organs and found 19 human cancer types that show altered THRAP3 expression compared to nonmalignant counterparts (57). However, that study found that THRAP3 mRNA levels did not significantly differ between the CaP and normal prostate tissue, indicating that differential THRAP3 mRNA expression is not associated with CaP and that THRAP3 may exert its cancer-stimulating role via another mechanism in CaP. Supporting this possibility, in cBio portal data (58), analysis reveals less than 1% of CaP samples show altered THRAP3 mRNA expression or THRAP3 somatic mutations (none at S243, S320, or S379). However, THRAP3 action is well known to be controlled by its phosphorylation patterns. In CaP, THRAP3 has been reported as a coregulator for AR (56) whose phosphorylation at the abovementioned two Ser residues decreases in CRPC and controls differential THRAP3 protein interactions. Our findings that CIT-dependent phosphorylation at S243 mediates, at least in part, THRAP3’s role in CaP growth fits with such a mechanism of action. Phosphorylation at S243 was critical also for THRAP3 to bind CIT-dependent pre-mRNAs and THRAP3-binding RNA motifs. The S243A phosphomark has been implicated also in adipose tissue browning, insulin sensitivity, and glucose metabolism in mice (59, 60). Phosphorylation at THRAP3 S243 was enriched in phosphoproteomics data from the tissues of patients with CRPC (6), supporting the relevance of the S243 phosphosite to treatment-resistant CaP. Important questions remain regarding how THRAP3 exerts its impact on CIT-dependent alternative splicing. Our rMATS analyses on RNA-seq data from cells that overexpress wild-type THRAP3 or the S243A THRAP3 mutant demonstrated that CIT not being able to phosphorylate THRAP3 at S243 diminished the THRAP3-controlled splicing events while inducing also others. These results support that CIT-dependent THRAP3 phosphorylation is needed to maintain the CIT- and THRAP3-dependent splicing program and that lack of such control can deregulate splicing. These observations are consistent with phosphorylation of splicing factors controlling the emergence of specific splicing events (61). Does this mean that such control also relies on a specific protein interactome that is mediated by phosphorylation at THRAP3’s S243 residue? Could that interactome contain one or more of the other putative CIT substrates that we identified? Do all these substrates influence the same spectrum of alternative splicing events? This scenario may be unlikely given that we identified only ∼300 THRAP3- and CIT-dependent RNA binding sites here compared to close to 900 CIT-controlled transcripts in our prior rMATS analyses (10).

Our CLIP-seq approach allowed us to characterize genes that are THRAP3 bound in a CIT-dependent versus -independent manner, to validate the CIT and THRAP3 dependency of transcripts derived from these genes, and ultimately to define the impact of single transcripts on CaP cell proliferation and CaP progression. The results uncovered that overexpression of single CIT- and THRAP3-dependent transcript is sufficient to have significant growth stimulation in in vitro and in vivo studies on CaP model systems and patient-derived CaP models. The more pronounced effects of a CIT/THRAP3-dependent alternative transcript such as CPT1A v2 was independently confirmed in RNA-seq studies where silencing of this transcript had markedly stronger effects on CaP gene expression when compared to a CIT/THRAP3 independent transcript from the same gene. The CPT1A v2–dependent transcriptome also was more strongly associated with cell biology under the control of CIT and THRAP3. These findings put forward the CIT and THRAP3-(CPT1A) transcript variant signaling axis that we identified as a novel CaP-specific signaling mechanism during CaP progression and as a tractable target whose activity can be inhibited by preventing the defining THRAP3 RNA binding event. Our subsequent studies using decoy RNAs and ASO competing with or complementing these THRAP3 binding-motifs confirmed the overall feasibility of such an approach.

CPT1A is a mitochondrial outer membrane enzyme that controls the rate-limiting step of long-chain fatty acid β-oxidation (FAO). CPT1A supports CaP progression by enabling FAO-dependent metabolic adaptation, especially under androgen-deprived, hypoxic, and oxidative-stress conditions. CPT1A inhibition can sensitize CaP models to ADT (62, 63). Our RNA-seq studies provide more insights into how individual CPT1A transcripts and global CPT1A action influence CaP behavior. We found the CPT1A-derived approach as effective as genetic CPT1A (transcript) ablation, which is impractical to carry forward therapeutically, and the CPT1A inhibitors etomoxir and perhexiline, which are not selective for CPT1A and thus have off target effects (41, 42). In addition, the latter compounds target CPT1A’s catalytic activity, which is no longer present in CPT1A v2 (64). This highlights another advantage of the ASO approach, namely, the capacity of preventing the emergence of oncogenic variants that are otherwise pharmacologically undruggable (65, 66). ASOs may present therapeutic option of also for transcript targets that lack enzymatic activity or appropriate protein-protein interactions that can be pursued for drug discovery (67, 68).

Our findings provide a first opportunity to inhibit aspects of CIT action in CaP when a specific CIT inhibitor that inhibits CaP growth in vivo is not yet available (10, 15). Our work demonstrates CaP growth inhibition using ASOs based on CIT-dependent THRAP3-binding RNA motifs. Notably, splicing-related ASOs with similar backbone and formulation to the one that we have developed have already been FDA approved to treat human diseases such as Duchenne’s muscular dystrophy (34, 35), supporting safety and tolerability in humans, but have yet to be tested extensively in CaP. The ASO that we designed to prevent CIT-dependent THRAP3 RNA binding reverses CIT-dependent splicing, validating on target effects, and inhibits proliferation of CaP cells and PDXOs at low nanomolar doses, irrespective of AR status, treatment resistance, or CaP lineage. These results indicate that ASOs that target THRAP3 RNA binding may lead to a novel class of therapeutics to prevent CaP progression by alteration of tumor-promoting CIT activity. The ASO that prevents THRAP3 RNA binding does not show any overt off target antiproliferative effects on benign cells, even at higher doses. Furthermore, it decreased only the expression of the THRAP3- and CIT-dependent CPT1A transcript variant; the levels of the other CIT-independent CPT1A variant were not significantly altered. This lack of off-target effects is consistent with observations that transgenic mice with disrupted CIT action and humans with germline CIT alterations are viable (69–76), underscoring that strategies to target CIT action in patients with CaP will be tolerable. Targeting the generation of CIT-dependent alternative splicing patterns also may overcome current limitations to inhibiting alternative splicing in a cancer-specific manner. FDA-approved spliceosome inhibitors are not available, and the inhibitors that have been developed so far target core components of the spliceosome such as SRPK (13, 77), leading to broad cytotoxicity in benign cells. Despite these promising results, several unanswered questions will need to be addressed to move the ASO approach forward. Key questions relate to optimizing the kinetics of ASO effects on CaP cells and assessing practical treatment regimens. In addition, the ASO we developed here was derived from a candidate pre-mRNA approach that examined CPT1A splicing, which is only one of hundreds of THRAP3-dependent CIT-controlled splicing events that we identified.

Nonetheless, as evidenced by antiproliferative CaP effects after treatment with the RBM10-derived decoy, it is likely that the ASO approach that prevents THRAP3 RNA binding is more generally applicable. Our CLIP-seq studies may have identified target pre-mRNAs that can be pursued in such efforts. Although not all the splicing events and the related alternate transcripts that our previous rMATS analyses of CIT-dependent RNA-seq data and our current THRAP3 CLIP-seq studies uncovered have been studied in CaP, literature supports their relevance to (prostate) cancer progression. For instance, a longer ESYT2 variant [controlled by CIT in CaP (10)] is highly expressed in renal carcinoma and lung cancer (78). FN1 splicing has been associated with colon cancer, and targeting the splicing of its extracellular domain has been proposed as a therapeutic strategy (79, 80). Several alternative transcripts for AMACR, whose CaP-specific expression is exploited as a biomarker to detect CaP, have been reported in CaP, but their biological functions have not yet been elucidated (81, 82).

Together, we uncovered a novel signaling mechanism that bridges the deregulated action of a kinase that drives CaP progression and treatment resistance with an alternative splicing program that is also enriched in advanced CaP. Alterations in kinase activities and in the spectrum of alternate splice variants have both been recognized as mediators of aggressive CaP progression and treatment resistance but have proven very difficult to target for therapy in a specific and effective manner (3–9, 50), as we propose here.

Limitations of the study

Our work provides novel insights into kinase-controlled regulation of clinically relevant splicing during CaP progression and provides proof-of-principle evidence to pursue CIT-dependent RNA binding of splicing regulators for therapeutic intervention. However, we have studied only one CIT substrate that is relevant to alternative splicing. How the contribution of THRAP3-dependent splicing events compares to that of other CIT substrates will need to be determined. It will be important also to define the mutual exclusivity or overlap in substrates’ contributions and the enrichment during clinical CaP progression to the overall CIT-controlled splicing program to ensure that ultimately splice-switching ASOs target the splicing event(s) that are most relevant to lethal CaP progression. While our ASO exerts robust and CaP-selective growth-inhibitory effects, our models so far have consisted of in vitro cell lines and organoid systems ASO. Carrying ASOs forward for in vivo and clinical applicability will require optimizing ASO length, chemistry, formulation, and/or delivery methods (83).

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Human participants

PDO cultures were established from fresh CaP tissue that was obtained at radical prostatectomy with written consent following approval by the Cleveland Clinic IRB (IRB5923).

Animal subjects

Six- to 8-week-old male outbred homozygous nude (Foxn1nu/Foxn1nu) mice (strain 007850, the Jackson Laboratory, Farmington, CT, USA) were used in xenograft studies. LuCaP 86.2 PDX tissue was grafted in the flank of 6- to 8-week-old Fox Chase severe combined immunodeficient mice [J:NU(HOM), strain 236, Charles River Laboratories, Fairfield, NJ]. RIP assays

RIP was performed as described previously (86). Briefly, 10 million cells in 150-mm dishes were washed 2× with ice-cold PBS. A 10-ml PBS was added to the dishes, and the cells were ultraviolet (UV) cross-linked at 254 nm and 150 mJ/cm2 using Spectrolinker XL-1000 UV Crosslinker (Spectronics Corporation). Cross-linked cells were scraped and collected by centrifugation at ×1000g for 10 min at 4°C. Cells were resuspended in 6 ml of cell lysis buffer [10 mM tris (pH 7.4), 10 mM NaCl, 0.5% IGEPAL, 1× EDTA-free protease inhibitor cocktail, 1 mM dithiothreitol (DTT), RNAseOUT (200 U/ml)] and incubated on ice for 15 min. Cells were homogenized using a Dounce homogenizer (10 strokes with pestle A, 40 with pestle B) and centrifuged at ×1000g for 10 min at 4°C. The pellet was resuspended in 3 ml of nuclei resuspension buffer [50 mM Hepes-NaOH (pH 7), 10 mM MgCl2, 1× EDTA-free protease inhibitor cocktail, 1 mM DTT, RNAseOUT (200 U/ml)] and sonicated for 5 cycles 30′/30′ at low setting. A total of 200 U of DNase was added and incubated at 37°C for 30 min, at which time the reaction was stopped by adding 20 mM EDTA. Triton X-100 (1%), 0.1% sodium deoxycholate, 0.01% SDS, and 140 mM NaCl were added to the sample and the leftover nuclei resuspension buffer (complete nuclei resuspension buffer). Samples were centrifuged at ×20,000g for 10 min at 4°C, and the supernatant was collected. One percent of each sample was set aside as input sample. Antibodies or IgG isotype controls were added at 1 μg/mg of protein and rotated overnight at 4°C. Protein-G beads were equilibrated with complete nuclei resuspension buffer. The next morning, equilibrated beads were added and incubated for 2 hours on rotation at 4°C. Beads were washed six times with 1 ml of IP buffer [10 mM tris (pH 7.4), 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, 1 mM EGTA (pH 8), 1% Triton X-100, 0.5% IGEPAL, 1× EDTA-free protease inhibitor cocktail, 1 mM DTT, and RNAseOUT (200 U/ml)] using a magnetic stand. Beads were resuspended in 150 μl of Proteinase K buffer [1× NT-2 buffer, 1% SDS, and proteinase K (1.2 mg/ml), with NT-2 buffer: 250 mM tris (pH 7.4), 750 mM NaCl, 5 mM MgCL2, and 0.25% IGEPAL]. To the input sample, 7.5 μl of 20% SDS, 18 μl of Proteinase K, 1% input, and NT-2 buffer were added to make the total volume to 150 μl. IP and input samples were incubated at 55°C for 30 min with shaking. Samples were set in the magnetic stand, and the supernatant was transferred into fresh tubes. A total of 250 μl of NT-2 buffer was added, followed by 400 μl of phenol:chloroform:IAA (125:24:1) reagent, and the sample was mixed vigorously for 15 s. Samples were centrifuged at ×20,000g for 10 min at room temperature, and we collected 350 μl of the top aqueous phase in fresh tubes. A total of 400 μl of chloroform was added and mixed vigorously, and the sample was centrifuged at ×20,000g for 10 min at room temperature. We collected 300 μl of the top aqueous phase in fresh tubes and added 50 μl of 5 M ammonium acetate, 14 μl of 7.5 M LiCl, 1.25 μl of glycogen (20 mg/ml) and 850 μl of absolute ethanol. The sample was kept in the −80°C for 1 hour and then centrifuged at ×20,000g for 30 min at 4°C. The pellet was washed with 500 μl of cold 80% ethanol, followed by centrifugation at ×20,000g for 15 min at 4°C. The pellet was air dried and resuspended in 20 μl of nuclease-free water. The resuspension was subjected to qRT-PCR with primers that amplify the pre-mRNA sequence bound by THRAP3 as before (87).

Cross-linking and immunoprecipitation sequencing

LNCaP cells that allow for doxycycline-inducible expression of a CIT-targeting shRNA (10) were seeded at a density of 5 × 106 cells per dish in 15-cm dishes for 48 hours before treating with doxycycline (1 μg/ml) or vehicle (DMSO) for another 96 hours. After 96 hours, the dishes were washed with 1× PBS and cross-linked with UV exposure to 150 mJ/cm2 at 254 nm using a Stratalinker. Next, cells were lysed and THRAP3-IP’ed. A 100-ng RNA from each sample was sent to Roswell Park Comprehensive Cancer Center where the samples were processed for ribodepletion, library preparation, and sequencing. CLIP-seq data from biological duplicates have been deposited in Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) under accession number GSE310293. CLIP-seq findings were validated using primers for pre-mRNA transcripts via qRT-PCR. Primers are listed in table S8.

Bioinformatics analysis of CLIP-seq data

The CLIP-seq reads were mapped to the human genome and transcriptome using STAR (88), based on the human genome assembly GRCh38 and GENCODE (89) gene annotation version 39. The alignment results on genome coordination and transcriptome coordination were used to perform two types of analyses: (i) measurement of transcript abundance to determine THRAP3-bound RNA and (ii) motif analysis to detect the protein-RNA interaction sites. The RNA abundance analysis was done by first calculating the fragments or reads per million counts for each known gene using the RSEM algorithm (90), based on the alignment to the transcriptome. The counts quantitatively describe the association of protein-RNA association and were used for differential analysis using DESeq2 (91) to (i) identify the genes with significant binding events by comparing the IP and input libraries in control condition and (ii) to identify the differential binding between treatment conditions. The bound genes in each analysis were further examined using the clusterProfiler (92) tool against MSigDB (93) to find the associated biological functions and pathways. For the motif analysis, alignment with genomic coordination was used. The chromatin immunoprecipitation sequencing peak caller MACS version 3 (94, 95) was used, comparing the pileup of cutting sites of IP samples to the input sample, to call the enrichment sites. The 100-bp regions around 5′ ends of each read pair were used for building the pileup signals. Then, we filtered the enrichment sites by only keeping those on the bound genes from the above RNA abundance analysis. The RNA-protein interaction sites were analyzed with MEME (96) to identify de novo motifs over the top 1000 sites ranked by q values from MACS. The clinical relevance of THRAP3 RNA binding events was verified by overlapping THRAP3-bound genes with genes that undergo alternative splicing during CaP progression, for which data were retrieved and analyzed as before (10, 13, 97).

RNA-seq studies

For gene silencing experiments, LNCaP cells were transfected with siRNAs targeting THRAP3 or CPT1A (all transcripts, SmartPool) or CPT1A-v1 or CPT1A-v2 or nontargeting control siRNAs. Biological triplicates were performed for each treatment group. Cells were harvested 96 hours later, and RNA was isolated and column purified using RNeasy MinElute Cleanup kit (QIAGEN). RNA samples were processed for RNA-seq at Novogene. mRNA sequencing libraries were prepared using a polyadenylate enrichment approach. Sequencing (50 million reads per sample) was performed using paired-end 100-bp chemistry.

For overexpression experiments, LNCaP cells were transfected with an expression construct encoding wild-type THRAP3 or THRAP3-S243A mutant or empty vector (control). Biological triplicates were performed for each treatment group. Cells were harvested 96 hours later, and RNA was isolated and column purified using RNeasy MinElute Cleanup kit (QIAGEN). RNA samples were processed for RNA-seq at Genomics Shared Resources, Roswell Park Comprehensive Cancer Center, where the samples were processed for ribodepletion, library preparation, and sequencing (50 million reads per sample) as before (10).

RNA-seq data were deposited in GEO under GSE accession number GSE333837.

The sequencing reads from RNA-seq studies were mapped using STAR (version 2.7.10a) to the human transcriptome GENCODE version 39 on human genome assembly GRCh38. The fragments per million counts for each known gene were calculated by the RSEM (version 1.3.1) algorithm based on the alignment to transcriptome. To identify the differential expressed genes between pairs of transfection conditions, we used DESeq2 and applied adjusted P value cutoff of 0.05 and absolute FC cutoff of 2. To investigate the gene functions of differential expression, we performed GSEA with GSEA function in clusterProfiler (version 4.16.0) with q value cutoff of 0.5, using the FC as ranking and comparing against MSigDB gene sets for Hallmark and GO terms for BPs.

Alternative splicing events were quantified using rMATS (v4.3.0) based on the alignments from STAR and GENCODE annotation. Significant differential splicing events between conditions were identified using a cutoff of percent spliced in > 0.1 and an FDR < 0.05.

Pull-down assays using biotinylated THRAP3-binding RNA sequences

Two 30-mer biotinylated decoy RNAs containing three repeats of the 10-mer CIT-dependent THRAP3-binding RNA motifs—(i) from the CPT1A pre-mRNA (gene position: chr11:68,755,523 to 68,755,532) and (ii) RBM10 pre-mRNA (gene position: chrX:47,179,398 to 47,179,407)—with a 2′-O-methyl modification on the ribose of each nucleotide were designed and obtained from Integrated DNA Technologies. The decoy RNAs or their scrambled RNAs were transfected using Lipofectamine 2000 as before (10). RNA pull-down assays were performed to validate the binding of THRAP3 to the motifs identified from the CLIP-seq analysis. For this, LNCaP cells were seeded at a density of 5 × 106 cells per dish in 15-cm dishes. After 48 hours, the cells were collected and lysed using cell lysis buffer [10 mM tris (pH 7.4), 10 mM NaCl, 0.5% IGEPAL (NP-40), 1× EDTA-Free protease inhibitor cocktail, 1 mM DTT, and RNAseOUT (200 U/ml)] to obtain nuclear pellet, which was further lysed to extract nuclear lysate using nuclear lysis buffer [20 mM Hepes, 1.5 mM MgCl2, 420 mM NaCl, 0.2 mM EDTA, 25% glycerol, 1× EDTA-Free protease inhibitor cocktail, 1 mM DTT, and RNAseOUT (200 U/ml)], and protein concentration was quantified using Bradford assay. The nuclear lysate was divided into three parts and incubated on rotation with control (nuclease-free water), scramble, and decoy RNA. After 3 hours, equilibrated streptavidin beads were added to the samples and left on rotation overnight. Next morning, the beads were washed with IP buffer [10 mM tris (pH 7.4), 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, 1 mM EGTA (pH 8), 1% Triton X-100, 0.5% IGEPAL (NP-40), 1× EDTA-Free protease inhibitor cocktail, 1 mM DTT, and RNAseOUT (200 U/ml)], eluted, and run on 3 to 8% Tris-Acetate NuPAGE gel along with 1% input to probe for THRAP3 pulldown.

Sequences of the biotinylated RNA molecule and scramble matching control are included in table S8.

Subcutaneous CaP xenografting

Animal studies were conducted after approval by the Cleveland Clinic’s IACUC. Treatment was repeated every other day by replacing drinking water with fresh water supplemented with doxycycline or DMSO.

Statistical analyses

Except for CLIP-seq, RNA-seq, and xenograft studies, at least two independent experiments were performed. Data were presented as the mean ± SEM. To compare the difference between two groups, t test was used (SigmaPlot version 14.5). Differences among groups in xenograft studies were evaluated using two-way ANOVA tests (SigmaPlot version 14.5). Overlap in genes subject to alternative splicing during CaP progression was calculated using Fisher exact tests. All statistical tests are two tailed unless otherwise specified. Significance was set at <0.05. Statistical details and methodology of experiments can be found in figure legends. All the graphs were generated using either SigmaPlot version 14.5 or GraphPad Prism 11.0.0.
