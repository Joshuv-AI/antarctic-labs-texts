Timing of immune checkpoint inhibitor infusion predicts survival in advanced esophageal squamous cell carcinoma: A retrospective cohort study
Authors: Wenjing Wang, Chengyuan Fan, Li Shen, Ruijie Ni, Lisha Ye, Weimin Mao, Wei Hong, Xiaoling Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEsophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC) is a highly lethal malignancy for which immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) have improved outcomes, yet significant inter-patient variability in response persists. Emerging evidence suggests that circadian rhythms modulate anti-tumor immunity, but the impact of ICI infusion timing on ESCC prognosis remains unexplored.ObjectiveTo determine whether the timing of ICI infusion impacts survival outcomes in patients with advanced ESCC and to identify an optimal infusion time window.DesignA single-center, retrospective cohort study.MethodsWe included 339 patients with advanced ESCC who received ICI therapy at Zhejiang Cancer Hospital between June 2018 and September 2022. Infusion timing was examined using two metrics: (i) first infusion time, and (ii) average infusion time across the first three cycles. Restricted cubic spline models were applied to explore dose-response relationships with infusion time as a continuous variable. Patients were categorized as early (before 14:00) or late (at or after 14:00) for each metric. Survival outcomes were analyzed using univariate and multivariate Cox proportional hazards models and Kaplan-Meier methods. Sensitivity analyses included an alternative 13:30 cutoff and a subgroup analysis of patients receiving ICI monotherapy. The study was conducted in compliance with the STROBE guidelines.ResultsIn the primary analysis of first infusion time, each one-hour delay was associated with increased risks of disease progression (HR=1.049, 95% CI: 1.003–1.097, P=0.036) and mortality (HR=1.062, 95% CI: 1.014–1.112, P=0.010). Kaplan-Meier analysis showed significantly better median PFS (6.2 vs. 5.3 months, P=0.010) and OS (13.3 vs. 12.4 months, P=0.026) in the Early-first versus Late-first group. In the secondary analysis of average infusion time during the first three cycles, each one-hour delay increased progression risk (HR=1.073, 95% CI: 1.001–1.151, P=0.027) and mortality risk (HR=1.092, 95% CI: 1.017–1.172, P=0.015), with Kaplan-Meier analysis showing a trend toward improved median PFS (6.2 vs. 5.6 months, P=0.098) and OS (13.4 vs. 12.1 months, P=0.057) in the Early-3M versus Late-3M group. Restricted cubic spline analysis revealed a nearly linear dose-response relationship (nonlinearity P>0.05).ConclusionThe timing of ICIs infusions may be an independent prognostic factor for survival in advanced ESCC. Administration before 14:00 was associated with improved PFS and OS.Keywords: esophageal squamous cell carcinoma, immune checkpoint inhibitors, infusion timing, circadian rhythms

Introduction

Esophageal cancer is among the most lethal types of cancer worldwide, with consistently high incidence and mortality rates. Esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC) poses a considerable disease burden, particularly in China. 1 For a considerable duration, platinum-based and fluorouracil chemotherapy has been the prevailing initial treatment for patients diagnosed with advanced or metastatic esophageal cancer. Nevertheless, the efficacy of this approach has plateaued, and significant toxic side effects are frequently reported. In recent years, immunotherapy, particularly programmed death-1 (PD-1) inhibitors, has revolutionized the treatment paradigm for advanced esophageal cancer. The ESCORT-1st study, by comparing chemotherapy with or without camrelizumab, has set a new benchmark for the three-year overall survival rate (up to 25.6%) among existing first-line immunotherapy-combined-chemotherapy regimens. The incorporation of camrelizumab into first-line therapy has demonstrated significant improvements in overall survival (OS) and progression-free survival (PFS) in patients diagnosed with advanced ESCC. The long-tail effect of immunotherapy was evident in the sustained OS benefit observed after an additional 1.5 years of follow-up, with a discernible trend of increasing magnitude in the difference between the two groups' Kaplan-Meier curves over time. 2 Evidence from the ORIENT-15 and KEYNOTE-590 studies suggests that for patients diagnosed with ESCC and exhibiting a Combined Positive Score (CPS) score of ≥10, the five-year overall survival rate is significantly enhanced by 3.7-fold when compared with chemotherapy, with this benefit remaining consistent irrespective of PD-L1 expression levels. The utilization of immune checkpoint inhibitors, whether as a primary or secondary line of therapy, has been demonstrated to result in a substantial enhancement in both OS and PFS. This has offered new hope for patients with advanced cancer.3,4 However, a critical challenge in the clinical application of immunotherapy is the significant inter-patient variability in treatment response and survival outcomes. In addition to the well-established biomarkers, such as tumor programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) expression levels and tumor mutational burden, the identification of further modifiable factors with the aim of maximizing the efficacy of immunotherapy has become a current research priority.

The circadian timing system (CTS) exerts a regulatory influence on anti-tumor immune responses, modulating the tumor infiltration capacity of immune cells, including CD8+ T cells, macrophages, and dendritic cells. These cells, in turn, act as effectors, capable of mediating cancer cell killing. A substantial body of research has indicated that there is a significant diurnal fluctuation in circulating T lymphocyte subsets. 5 It is hypothesized that chronomodulated drug administration may substantially improve treatment efficacy and patient quality of life. In recent years, preliminary clinical evidence has begun to support this hypothesis. Retrospective studies on cancers such as melanoma and non-small cell lung cancer suggest that patients receiving immunotherapy earlier in the day (morning or before late afternoon) tend to achieve superior survival outcomes compared to those treated in the evening.6,7 However, in the context of esophageal cancer, a highly malignant tumor, the impact of infusion timing on immunotherapy efficacy remains unclear, with a distinct lack of dedicated exploration and research.

To address this critical issue, this study aimed to analyze the relationship between immunotherapy infusion timing and survival outcomes in patients with advanced esophageal cancer using real-world data. We conducted extensive sensitivity analyses across time points from 11:30 to 16:30 and identified 14:00 as the optimal cutoff based on statistical significance and clinical feasibility. To further evaluate the robustness of this threshold, we additionally employed 13:30 as an alternative cutoff and present the corresponding analysis results. This approach aims to provide novel, low-cost, and clinically actionable evidence for optimizing immunotherapy strategies in advanced esophageal cancer.

Materials and methods

Patients

This retrospective study included 339 patients with advanced esophageal cancer who received immune checkpoint inhibitor (ICI) therapy at Zhejiang Cancer Hospital between June 2018 and September 2022 (Figure 1). The primary inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) age 18 years or older; (2) histopathologically confirmed squamous cell carcinoma of the esophagus; (3) diagnosed as stage IIIB or IV advanced ESCC according to the 2019 American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) TNM staging system; (4) received at least one cycle of monotherapy or combination ICI treatment regimen; (5) presence of measurable lesions; (6) complete and precise records of the exact infusion time (to the minute) for the first immunotherapy cycle and at least the first three cycles; (7) Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group Performance Status (ECOG-PS) score of 0-2 (higher scores indicate greater functional impairment). Patients were excluded if they met any of the following criteria: (1) History of primary malignancy in other organs; (2) Presence of severe systemic disease or immunodeficiency; (3) Lack of tumor response assessment, infusion time records, or survival follow-up data. All enrolled patients were followed up until May 2025. This study protocol has been approved by the Ethics Committee of Zhejiang Cancer Hospital.

Flowchart of the study population. ICI, immune checkpoint inhibitor.

General information collection

The primary clinical parameters collected for all patients included the following: age, sex, alcohol consumption history, smoking history, ECOG performance status score, body mass index (BMI) level, recurrence status, tumor location, TNM staging, metastatic organs, immune checkpoint inhibitor treatment regimen, type of immune checkpoint inhibitor, and number of treatment lines, time of first and first three cycles of ICI infusion, and immune-related adverse events (irAEs). The TNM staging was evaluated in accordance with the 8th edition of the International Union Against Cancer (UICC) TNM Classification and the AJCC staging system. The clinical treatment responses of all patients following immune checkpoint inhibitor therapy were assessed in accordance with the Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST) version 1.1. The efficacy of the treatment was determined by radiological evaluation via computed tomography or magnetic resonance imaging, which yielded the following responses: complete response (CR), partial response (PR), stable disease (SD), or disease progression (PD). These responses were then correlated with clinical outcomes for progression-free survival (PFS) and overall survival (OS). The ICI treatment regimen was categorized as ICI monotherapy or combined ICI therapy. Combined ICI therapy included ICI plus chemotherapy (platinum-based doublet with paclitaxel or fluorouracil), ICI plus anti-angiogenic therapy, or ICI plus radiotherapy. The ICI agents used included Camrelizumab, Sintilimab, Pembrolizumab, Nivolumab, Tislelizumab, Toripalimab, and QL1604. The specific distribution of agents across treatment groups is provided in Table S2.

Efficacy assessment and follow-up

The primary endpoint was OS, defined as the time from initiation of immunotherapy to death, last follow-up, or data cutoff (May 31, 2025). The secondary endpoint was PFS, defined as the time from the initiation of immunotherapy to the first occurrence of disease progression or death. Patients without disease progression during follow-up were observed until the last follow-up date or data cutoff date, whichever occurred first.

Statistical analysis

Data collection and study population

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the baseline demographic and clinical pathological characteristics of the patients. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages, and intergroup comparisons were conducted using chi-squared or Fisher’s exact tests.

To explore the association between infusion timing and prognosis, we first treated the time of the first infusion and the average infusion time across the first three cycles as continuous variables. These were then incorporated into univariate Cox proportional hazards models to calculate hazard ratios and their 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Restricted cubic spline models were then used to fit the non-linear relationship with three nodes specified and the non-linear terms were tested for significance. Based on the indication that ‘later infusion times correlate with poorer prognosis', extensive cut-off sensitivity analyses were conducted, testing multiple time points from 11:30 to 16:30. To further characterize the biological gradient, we divided patients into quartiles based on infusion time and compared survival outcomes between the earliest (Q1) and latest (Q4) quartiles. These evaluated the strength and consistency of the association between the early and late infusion groups and PFS and OS at different cut-off points. Ultimately, 14:00 was selected as the primary cut-off point based on statistical significance and clinical feasibility. To evaluate the independent prognostic value of infusion timing, we constructed a multivariate Cox proportional hazards model, incorporating age, sex, PS score, number of treatment lines, stage, liver metastasis, lung metastasis and immune-related adverse events as covariates. To ensure the validity of the Cox model, we assessed the proportional hazards assumption via the Schoenfeld residual test. For the covariate that was found to violate this assumption during testing (number of treatment lines), a stratified Cox model was employed in the final model, and its compliance with the proportional hazards assumption was re-validated.

For categorical analyses, we defined two patient cohorts with explicit hierarchical designation to avoid analytical confusion. The primary analysis compared the Early-first cohort, whose initial infusion occurred before 14:00, against the Late-first cohort, whose initial infusion occurred at or after 14:00, based on the rationale that the first infusion captures the critical circadian window for initial immune priming and dendritic cell activation. As a secondary analysis, we compared the Early-3M cohort, whose average infusion time across the first three cycles fell before 14:00, against the Late-3M cohort, whose average time fell at or after 14:00, in order to evaluate sustained circadian exposure patterns.

All statistical analyses were performed using R (version 4.4.2), SPSS Statistics 27, and GraphPad Prism 9. The significance level was set at α=0.05, with all p-values being two-tailed, and P<0.05 considered statistically significant. The reporting of this study conforms to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) statement. A completed STROBE checklist is provided as Supplementary File 1.

Response and survival

Among the 339 patients with advanced esophageal squamous cell carcinoma who were treated with ICIs, the objective response rate (ORR) was 38.1%, and the disease control rate (DCR) was 82.6%. A PR occurred in 37.8% (128/339) of patients, an SD in 44.5% (151/339), and a PD in 17.4% (59/339). Among the 335 patients who received the ICI infusion between 8:00 and midnight during the first three cycles, the ORR was 38.2%, the DCR was 82.4%, the PR incidence was 37.5% (127/335), the SD incidence was 43.7% (148/335), and the PD incidence was 17.4% (59/335). Later infusion time is associated with increased mortality risk

Univariate Cox regression analysis revealed that the timing of the first infusion was associated with poorer survival outcomes. Each one-hour delay in the first infusion was associated with a 4.9% increase in the risk of disease progression (HR=1.049, 95% CI: 1.003-1.097, P=0.036) and a 6.2% increase in the risk of death (HR=1.062, 95% CI: 1.014-1.112, P=0.010). To further evaluate the biological gradient beyond the 14:00 cutoff, we compared extreme quartiles of first infusion time. Patients in the latest quartile (Q4) demonstrated a significantly higher risk of disease progression (HR=1.453, 95% CI: 1.054-2.005, P=0.023) and death (HR=1.641, 95% CI: 1.165-2.312, P=0.005) compared with the earliest quartile (Q1), with median PFS of 5.2 versus 6.0 months and median OS of 11.6 versus 15.1 months, respectively (Figure S2(a)-(b)). Similarly, each one-hour delay in average infusion time during the first three cycles was associated with a 7.3% increase in the risk of disease progression (HR=1.073, 95% CI: 1.001-1.151, P=0.027) and a 9.2% increase in the risk of death (HR=1.092, 95% CI: 1.017-1.172, P=0.015). Directionally consistent trends were observed for extreme quartiles of average infusion time (Figure S2(c)-(d)). Restricted cubic spline analysis revealed a nearly linear dose-response relationship between infusion timing and mortality risk (nonlinearity P value >0.05), which supports the interpretation of delayed infusion timing as a continuous risk factor (Figure S1).

To exclude confounding factors, we constructed a multivariate Cox model that was stratified by the number of treatment lines (a variable that violated the proportional hazards assumption). After adjusting for age, sex, PS score, staging, liver metastases, lung metastases, and irAEs, the time of first infusion remained an independent predictor of OS (HR=1.059, 95% CI: 1.011-1.111, P=0.017). Similarly, mean infusion time was independently associated with OS (HR=1.087, 95% CI: 1.010-1.169, P=0.027). However, in the multivariate model, the effect of infusion time on PFS was attenuated and did not demonstrate independent statistical significance (first: HR=1.046, 95% CI: 0.997-1.097, P=0.068; average: HR=1.048, 95% CI: 0.975-1.126, P=0.204). Predicting the optimal infusion timing threshold

To objectively determine clinically meaningful cutoff points, we conducted extensive sensitivity analyses of both the time of the first infusion and the average infusion time. The sensitivity analysis of the time of the first infusion revealed that its association with prognosis was consistently significant between 12:30 and 15:30. PFS remained significantly associated within this time window (all P<0.05), while OS was significantly associated between 12:30 and 15:00 (all P<0.05). At 14:00, both PFS and OS reached statistical significance (PFS: HR=1.343, P=0.014; OS: HR=1.344, P=0.019), with balanced effect sizes. Sensitivity analysis of mean infusion duration revealed a similar, albeit slightly delayed, pattern. The significant association with OS began at 12:30, while the significant association with PFS started at 13:30. The significant time window for both overlapped from 13:30 to 14:30. At 14:00, OS was significantly affected (HR=1.381, p=0.039), while PFS showed marginal significance (HR=1.300, p =0.082). 14:00 was the only time demonstrating significant effects on both PFS and OS in the first-dose infusion time analysis. It also revealed a clear OS benefit in the mean infusion time analysis and was centrally located within the shared significant window. Thus, it was identified as the optimal cutoff point. Administering ICI before 14:00 was significantly associated with improved PFS and OS

To further validate the aforementioned analysis, a comparison was made of survival outcomes in patients with ESCC receiving ICI infusion around 14:00. The Kaplan-Meier analysis revealed that patients in the Early-first group, defined as those who received their first infusion dose before 14:00, demonstrated significantly superior median PFS (6.2 vs. 5.3 months; P=0.010) and OS (13.3 vs. 12.4 months; P=0.026) compared with the Late-first group, defined as those who received their first infusion dose after 14:00 (Figure 4). Extending this to the average infusion time across the first three cycles, the Early-3M group showed a trend toward improved median PFS (6.2 vs. 5.6 months; P=0.098) and OS (13.4 vs. 12.1 months; P=0.057) relative to the Late-3M group (Figure S3). Collectively, these findings suggest that an early infusion pattern of ICI is associated with improved prognosis.

Sensitivity analyses addressing chemotherapy chronomodulation

To evaluate whether concurrent systemic therapy confounded the primary analysis, we performed three sensitivity analyses. Adding treatment regimen to the multivariate Cox model did not materially alter the effect estimates for first infusion time (OS: HR changed from 1.306 to 1.300; PFS: HR changed from 1.348 to 1.304). A formal interaction test indicated a significant interaction for OS (P=0.047), with the protective effect of early infusion being more pronounced in patients receiving ICI monotherapy. In the ICI-monotherapy subgroup (n=60), early first infusion remained significantly associated with improved OS (HR=0.512, 95% CI: 0.271–0.969, P=0.040) and PFS (HR=0.480, 95% CI: 0.260–0.885, P=0.019), with median OS of 15.8 vs. 9.1 months and median PFS of 4.1 vs. 2.4 months (Figure S4).

Administering ICI before 13:30 demonstrated a trend towards survival benefit but did not reach statistical significance

We further evaluated a 13:30 cutoff to comprehensively assess the optimal timing window. Early infusion before 13:30 improved first-dose PFS, but the first-dose OS comparison was only marginal. The average-time analyses also showed significant survival advantages. However, 14:00 was the only cutoff that achieved significance across all three metrics and both endpoints, making it the more robust threshold (Figure S5).

Discussion

This study provides the first systematic confirmation of a significant association between the timing of immunotherapy infusion and clinical prognosis in patients with advanced ESCC. Our findings suggest that with each additional hour of delay in the timing of immunotherapy infusion, the risk of disease progression and mortality increases accordingly. Furthermore, scheduling the infusion before 14:00 is independently associated with markedly improved PFS and OS. This association remains consistent in analyses examining the initial use of ICI drugs and ICI administration during the first three cycles.

A recent landmark randomized phase III trial by Zhang et al., published in Nature Medicine, demonstrated that advanced non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) patients receiving immunotherapy and chemotherapy before 15:00 showed a 60% reduction in the risk of disease progression or death and a 58% reduction in mortality risk compared to those treated after 15:00. This outcome was associated with enhanced anti-tumor CD8+ T-cell activity. 8 This prospective randomized evidence strongly corroborates our findings in ESCC and establishes circadian-modulated immunotherapy as a clinically actionable strategy. A previous dual-center, dual-continent investigation of NSCLC demonstrated significant improvements in median PFS (11.8 vs. 7.2 months, p<0.0001) and OS (33.0 vs. 19.5 months, p<0.0001) among patients who received immunotherapy combined with chemotherapy in the morning (before 11:30). 9 A prospective phase III clinical trial presented at the recent ASCO conference confirmed that patients with stage IIIC-IV NSCLC who received immunochemotherapy in the morning experienced nearly twice the PFS and OS benefits compared to those who received it in the evening. In melanoma studies, evening administration (after 16:30) of immune checkpoint inhibitors was associated with significantly reduced PFS and OS. 10 In the specific context of ESCC, a preliminary retrospective study of 62 patients documented improved prognosis for infusions administered before 13:00. 11 While both studies support the concept of chronomodulated immunotherapy in ESCC, several differences warrant consideration. Our cohort is substantially larger (339 vs. 62 patients), and the optimal cutoff identified in our analysis was 14:00 rather than 13:00. This discrepancy may reflect differences in patient demographics, treatment regimens, or institutional scheduling practices. Importantly, both studies converge on the afternoon as a critical transition window, supporting the biological plausibility of circadian-modulated ICI efficacy in ESCC. Furthermore, a meta-analysis of 13 solid tumor studies provides robust evidence supporting the “morning advantage,” showing that morning infusion significantly prolongs PFS (HR=0.51, p<0.00001) and OS (HR=0.50, p<0.00001). 12 Notably, the cutoff values defining “afternoon” varied considerably across these studies. The optimal infusion timing may differ depending on the type of cancer and the specifics of the treatment regimen.

Recent evidence suggests that the average infusion time over the first three cycles of therapy may carry prognostic weight comparable to or greater than the first infusion alone. 7 In our study, the first infusion time was designated as the primary analysis based on its biological relevance as the initial immune activation window, yet the average three-cycle time demonstrated parallel and significant associations. This concordance across both hierarchical analyses indicates that both the initial circadian context and sustained rhythmic exposure contribute to immunotherapy efficacy, and the robustness of the findings does not depend on the relative ranking of these metrics.

The immune response in mammals is precisely regulated by the endogenous circadian rhythm system. This regulation encompasses T-cell activation, migration to tumor tissues, and cytokine production. This influence permeates multiple critical stages of anti-tumor immunity. Research indicates that core clock genes, such as the BMAL1/CLOCK complex, regulate not only physiological rhythms but also directly influence immune cell function. For example, BMAL1 can bind directly to and regulate the expression of essential components of the T-cell receptor signaling pathway, thereby affecting T-cell activation and proliferation.13,14 Furthermore, studies have shown that during the circadian active phase (daytime in humans), dendritic cells exhibit increased migration to draining lymph nodes and enhanced antigen presentation. Concurrently, the circadian expression of co-stimulatory molecules (such as CD80 and CD86) on antigen-presenting cells collectively supports the notion that T-cell activation, migration, and effector functions exhibit circadian rhythms. 15 We hypothesize that the window before 14:00 coincides with the circadian active phase in humans, when dendritic cells naturally exhibit enhanced migration and antigen-presenting activity, and T cells are more responsive to activation signals. Synchronizing the initial infusion with this endogenous rhythm may therefore maximize the priming of anti-tumor immunity, rather than relying on an effect exclusive to a specific clock time. 15 This establishes optimal initial conditions for subsequent sustained anti-tumor immunity. Conversely, administration later in the circadian cycle may coincide with a gradually intensifying immunosuppressive environment, such as heightened regulatory T cell (Treg) function or increased immunosuppressive activity of myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), potentially partially offsetting ICI efficacy. Furthermore, anti-tumor immunity is a dynamic process requiring repeated activation and maintenance. 16 Administration during the rhythmic peak window over the first three cycles may, through repeated optimization of the T-cell activation environment, help maintain T-cell function, prevent exhaustion, and promote the formation of a more protective central memory T-cell pool, 17 thereby improving patients' PFS and long-term OS.

One finding worthy of further investigation is that infusion timing demonstrated more robust independent predictive value for OS in multivariate analyses, whereas its influence on PFS appeared diminished. This phenomenon may indicate that infusion timing affects the long-term benefits of immunotherapy. Infusion timing may promote the formation of central memory T cells by optimizing the activation of initial T cells. This provides more enduring tumor immune surveillance, or the long-term functional remodeling of the immune system. 18

Through continuous variable analysis, this study has revealed a clear dose-response relationship, significantly strengthening the possibility of a causal association between infusion timing and prognosis. We hypothesize that the early afternoon (before 14:00) corresponds to a peak window for anti-tumor immune responses in humans. During this time, infusion of ICIs may activate T cells more effectively, generating stronger anti-tumor effects.

In summary, our findings provide theoretical support for administering infusions before 14:00 to patients with advanced esophageal squamous cell carcinoma who are receiving ICI therapy. We acknowledge several limitations of our study. First, the retrospective design inherently limits our ability to fully exclude selection bias related to treatment timing. For example, patients with poorer performance status or more aggressive disease might have been scheduled for later infusion times due to bed availability or clinical workflow, which could confound the observed associations. Second, the effect sizes, while statistically significant, are modest (HRs approximately 1.05–1.09 per hour), and PFS did not retain independent significance in multivariate analysis. These findings should therefore be interpreted with appropriate caution and require prospective validation. Third, the study included only 25 female patients (7.4%), which markedly limits the statistical power for sex-specific subgroup analyses. Emerging evidence from the MEMOIR study suggests that sex-based differences may exist in susceptibility to time-of-day infusion effects among melanoma patients receiving immunotherapy. 6 Sex dimorphism in circadian rhythm regulation and immune response has been proposed as a potential modifier of chronotherapy efficacy. Future studies with more balanced gender representation are needed to clarify whether the optimal infusion timing window differs between sexes. Fourth, we lacked patient-derived biological samples, such as peripheral blood mononuclear cells, to directly assess circadian rhythm markers, e.g., expression of core clock genes, thereby precluding direct molecular validation of our hypothesis. Future prospective, multicenter studies with molecular analyses are needed. Our study paves the way for two key areas of future research. First, there is an urgent need for randomized controlled trials of esophageal cancer in which patients are randomly assigned to a “morning/early afternoon infusion group” or an “afternoon infusion group.” This will validate the causal effect of infusion timing on immunotherapy efficacy with the highest level of evidence. Second, translational research requires the systematic collection of serial biological samples from patients undergoing immunotherapy to investigate the links between infusion timing, circadian rhythms in immune cells, cytokine profiles, and tumor microenvironment alterations. These efforts will improve our mechanistic understanding and potentially guide the optimization of immunotherapy protocols.

Conclusion

Our findings suggest that the timing of ICI infusion may be an independent prognostic factor for survival in patients with advanced ESCC. Administration before 14:00 was associated with improved PFS and OS in this retrospective cohort.
