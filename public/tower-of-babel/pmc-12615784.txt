Predictive modeling of copper iodide properties using graph-theoretical descriptors
Authors: Hafiz Muhammad Fraz, Kashif Ali, Muhammad Faisal Nadeem, Nasir Ali, Fikadu Tesgera Tolasa
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Metal halides, inorganic compounds formed through the combination of metals and halogens, play a significant role in diverse applications. Copper iodide (CuI, Marshite) stands out as a particularly versatile metal halide with important uses in organic synthesis, semiconductor technology, catalytic processes, and atmospheric modification through cloud seeding. This study employs graph-theoretical approaches to analyze the structural properties of CuI, focusing on degree-based topological indices such as the Zagreb indices, Randić index, harmonic index, Sombor index, and atom-bond connectivity index. We develop regression models to establish relationships between these topological indices and key physicochemical properties of CuI, including heat of formation, molecular weight, and density. The study further presents comprehensive graphical representations through line plots, bar charts, violin plots, and heatmaps to visualize trends and correlations in these properties. Our computational approach provides valuable insights into the structure-property relationships of CuI, offering potential applications in material design and optimization for its various technological uses.

Introduction

Minerals constitute fundamental building blocks of biological systems, with inorganic compounds those devoid of carbon-hydrogen frameworks playing pivotal roles in aquatic ecosystems, particularly for piscine metabolic processes1. The extraction and stabilization of these mineral ores present significant materials science challenges, where topological analysis emerges as a powerful tool for characterizing structural stability in inorganic matrices2. Contemporary analytical techniques, including temperature-resolved biomass metal extraction coupled with plasma optical emission spectroscopy, have advanced our mineral characterization capabilities3. Among mineral classes, halide-dominated species typically colorless and water-soluble predominantly occur in xeric environments, exhibiting unique physicochemical properties4.

Topological indices, as molecular graph invariants, provide critical quantitative structure-property relationships (QSPRs) for chemical systems5. These metrics are systematically classified into degree-based6, neighborhood degree-based, distance-based7, and eccentricity-based variants, each revealing distinct aspects of molecular behavior8. Of particular utility in materials science are degree-based and distance-based indices, which correlate strongly with essential physicochemical parameters across diverse chemical systems.

Recent advances demonstrate the application of vertex degree-based topological indices in characterizing aluminophosphate entropy9,10, while M-polynomial approaches have successfully modeled \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Cu_2O$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$TiF_2$$\end{document} systems through Maple 15 computational visualization11. The nano-technology sector benefits from such analyses, particularly in gas-absorbent porous inorganic materials where Zagreb indices and Randic descriptors predict performance characteristics12. Nano-tubular architectures, crucial for nano-scale applications, have been extensively characterized through atom-bond connectivity indices for H-Naphthalenic and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$TUC_4$$\end{document} variants13, with complementary Zagreb-based analyses of zeolitic frameworks14.

The relationship between topological indices and the heat of formation of the hemihexaphyrazine (HHP) network has been explored using curve fitting models, revealing strong correlations (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\text {R}^2$$\end{document} \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\ge$$\end{document} 0.98) and highlighting the predictive reliability of reverse redefined Zagreb indices15. Similarly, graph theoretical techniques were applied to tetragonal zeolite farneseite (FAR), where bond-wise entropy analysis showed sodalite frameworks to possess higher entropies than farneseite, impacting phase transitions and structural transformations16. In the field of food chemistry, a QSPR-based model using topological indices was developed to predict the physicochemical properties of preservatives, with cubic regression providing the most accurate results, further validated using propionic acid as a case study17. For zeolite AWW, degree-based indices, entropies, and HOMO–LUMO gap measures were computed, and regression models established a strong relationship between entropy, spectral values, and potential energies of the framework18. Furthermore, chemical graph theory was applied to seventeen heart disease drugs through domination degree-based indices, and regression models confirmed their ability to predict physicochemical and ADMET properties, offering valuable insights for drug discovery and design19. Recent studies have demonstrated the growing relevance of topological indices in modeling and predicting physicochemical properties across diverse chemical structures. For instance, Siddique et al.20 applied curve fitting approaches to explore the heat of formation in hemihexaphyrazine, while Jacob et al.21 and Peter et al.22 investigated entropy-based indices for zeolite frameworks. Similarly, Gayathri and Roy23 employed regression models for comparative QSPR analysis of food preservatives, and Kuriachan and Parthiban24 introduced domination degree-based indices for evaluating ADMET properties of cardiovascular drugs. These contributions highlight the versatility of topological descriptors in bridging structural features with thermodynamic, biological, and functional properties of complex molecular systems.

The mineralogy of iodides reveals fascinating evolutionary pathways: while iron-bearing minerals are geochemically scarce, iodine compounds like silver iodide (1825) and copper iodide (marshite, 1854) have established significant technological footprints, unlike the elusive mercury iodide25. Copper iodide (CuI) demonstrates remarkable versatility, from sono-catalytic formaldehyde degradation in petrochemical applications26 to resistive random access memory (RRAM) devices leveraging its p-type semiconductor properties27–29. Structural studies confirm marshite’s predominant octahedral crystallization, forming isomorphic series with Ag/Cu systems30,31, with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\gamma$$\end{document}-CuI thin films exhibiting optimal performance at \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$190^{\circ }$$\end{document}C32. Comprehensive analyses of marshite’s electronic structure, vibrational modes, and mechanical properties33, along with its characteristic low aqueous solubility and redox-derived crystallization34, underscore its materials science importance.

The biomedical applications of CuI have gained particular prominence, demonstrating potent antiviral activity against feline calicivirus (FCV) through \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Cu^+$$\end{document} ion-mediated viral dormancy induction—a mechanism showing promise against SARS-CoV-235,36. In antimicrobial surface technologies, the iodide triad (CuI, AgI, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$PbI_2$$\end{document}) effectively inhibits microbial colonization, addressing critical challenges in biomedical materials37.

Preliminaries

In a chemical structure \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\phi$$\end{document}, atoms are bonded to each other through chemical bonds by sharing electrons, which accounts for the physicochemical properties and biological functions of such chemical structures. To numerically determine key physicochemical properties, the chemical structure is transformed into a molecular graph by representing atoms as vertices and chemical bonds as edges.

For a molecular graph \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\phi$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$V(\phi )$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$E(\phi )$$\end{document} denote the vertex set and edge set, respectively, where the number of edges incident to a vertex is called its degree (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$d_i$$\end{document}). An edge is termed a (1, 2)-edge if it connects vertices of degrees 1 and 2.

Gutman and Trinajstić, in 1972, established a relationship between total \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\pi$$\end{document}-electron energy and a topological invariant known as the Zagreb index, formulating two indices named the first and second Zagreb indices38, which were further studied by Cheng and Wu in39.

Definition 1

For a molecular graph \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\phi$$\end{document}, the first, second, and third Zagreb indices are defined as:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} M_1(\phi )= & \sum _{ij \in E(\phi )} (d_i + d_j) \\ M_2(\phi )= & \sum _{ij \in E(\phi )} (d_i \cdot d_j) \\ M_3(\phi )= & \sum _{ij \in E(\phi )} (d_i + d_j)^2 \end{aligned}$$\end{document}

In 1975, M. Randić proposed a degree-based topological index40, which was later generalized in41. The harmonic index was introduced by Fajtlowicz in 1987 while studying computer networks42.

Definition 2

The Randić and harmonic indices for a molecular graph \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\phi$$\end{document} are defined as:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} R(\phi )= & \sum _{ij \in E(\phi )} \frac{1}{\sqrt{d_i \cdot d_j}} \\ H(\phi )= & \sum _{ij \in E(\phi )} \frac{2}{d_i + d_j} \end{aligned}$$\end{document}

The concept of the Sombor index in chemical graph theory was introduced by Gutman43, with different chemical graph classifications presented in44. The atom-bond connectivity index was defined in45 and further studied in46.

Definition 3

For a molecular graph \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\phi$$\end{document}, the Sombor and atom-bond connectivity indices are defined as:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} So(\phi )= & \sum _{ij \in E(\phi )} \sqrt{d_i^2 + d_j^2} \\ ABC(\phi )= & \sum _{ij \in E(\phi )} \sqrt{\frac{d_i + d_j - 2}{d_i \cdot d_j}} \end{aligned}$$\end{document}

Comparison analysis of physicochemical properties and topological indices

This comprehensive analysis systematically investigates the relationship between molecular topological indices and physicochemical properties through multiple complementary approaches, revealing fundamental structure-property relationships that are both statistically robust and chemically meaningful. The consistent patterns observed across all analytical methods - including heatmaps, violin plots, linear plots, and tabular data - demonstrate a universal positive correlation between molecular size (n and m values) and all examined properties, including heat of formation (Hf), molecular weight (MW), and density (D). While this upward trend is universal, the growth rates exhibit significant variation among different index-property pairs, providing valuable insights into their underlying chemical behavior. The heat of formation associated with the second Zagreb index (Hf_M2) shows a 5% faster growth rate compared to Hf_M1, suggesting greater electronic sensitivity in the M2 index. Similarly, molecular weight linked to the third Zagreb index (MW_M3) increases 8.3% more rapidly than that associated with the Randić index (MW_R), indicating M3’s stronger dependence on molecular branching patterns. Most notably, the density related to the Sombor index (D_So) rises 12.5% faster than its harmonic index counterpart (D_H), implying that the Sombor index more effectively captures molecular packing efficiency. The symmetrical distributions in the violin plots confirm these trends are statistically significant and not measurement artifacts, while the heatmap’s color gradients provide intuitive visual confirmation of the quantitative progressions observed in the tabular data. These findings have important implications for predictive modeling in chemical design, as the established correlations enable more accurate property prediction for novel compounds. The quantified growth differentials offer practical guidance for selecting appropriate topological indices when targeting specific material properties, and provide validated relationships for quantitative structure-activity studies. The comprehensive agreement across multiple independent visualization and analysis methods strongly supports the robustness of these structure-property relationships, establishing a solid foundation for future work in developing more precise predictive models for specific chemical classes and applications in materials science.

This comprehensive investigation establishes robust quantitative relationships between topological indices and the physicochemical properties of copper iodide (CuI) through graph-theoretical analysis. Our results demonstrate systematic correlations between molecular descriptors (Zagreb indices, Randić index, harmonic index, Sombor index, and ABC index) and key material characteristics, validated across multiple analytical approaches.
