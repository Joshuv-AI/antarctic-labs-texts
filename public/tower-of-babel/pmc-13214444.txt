Construction of a reverse genetics and fluorescent reporter system for a bovine enterovirus isolated from cattle with diarrhea
Authors: Qingting Dong, Yuhang Luo, Huanghao Liu, Zhuangzhu Zhou, Yeyong Li, Weijian Huang, Yan Pan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBovine enteroviruses are widely detected in cattle, but molecular tools for functional studies and antiviral screening remain limited. In this study, a bovine enterovirus strain, GXHC2317, was isolated from diarrheic cattle in Hechi, Guangxi, China and propagated in BHK-21 cells. Full-genome sequencing and phylogenetic analyses classified GXHC2317 as Enterovirus E genotype 3, and recombination analyses indicated a mosaic genome with regions related to both northern and local Chinese lineages. To enable genetic manipulation, we constructed a full-length infectious cDNA clone of GXHC2317 under a CMV promoter and successfully rescued a recombinant virus, rGXHC2317, which showed replication kinetics comparable to the parental isolate in BHK-21 cells. Using an oral infection model in 4-week-old ICR mice, rGXHC2317 and GXHC2317 displayed similar tissue distribution and broadly comparable histopathology. Furthermore, we generated a fluorescent reporter virus by inserting UnaG at the 5′UTR–VP4 junction. The reporter virus produced clear cytopathic effects and strong fluorescence, although its peak titer was reduced compared with the parental strain. Finally, a fluorescence-based assay using rGXHC2317-UnaG enabled antiviral evaluation and distinguished inhibitory effects of ribavirin and pleconaril from the limited activity of chloroquine phosphate under our conditions. Together, these results provide a reverse genetics and fluorescent reporter platform for BEV that supports studies of viral biology and practical antiviral screening.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12917-026-05415-w.Keywords: Bovine enterovirus, reverse genetics, fluorescent reporter virus, antiviral screening

Introduction

Bovine enterovirus (BEVs) are small, non-enveloped, positive-sense single-stranded RNA viruses that belong to the genus Enterovirus within the family Picornaviridae [1, 2]. They are currently classified into two species, Enterovirus E and Enterovirus F, which predominantly infect cattle. The BEV genome is approximately 7.4 to 7.5 kilobases in length and contains a single open reading frame flanked by untranslated regions at both termini. This genome encodes a large polyprotein that is proteolytically processed into four structural proteins, VP1 to VP4, and several non-structural proteins, consistent with the typical genome organization of enteroviruses [3–5].

BEVs are widely distributed in cattle populations and have been detected in many regions worldwide [6–9]. Although infections are often subclinical or cause only mild clinical signs, BEVs have also been implicated in cases of diarrhea, respiratory disease, reproductive disorders, and in some instances, fatal enteric infections in calves [10–13]. Their high environmental stability, acid resistance, and persistent fecal shedding contribute to efficient transmission within herds [14, 15]. Moreover, molecular epidemiological studies have revealed considerable genetic diversity among BEV strains, with multiple genotypes identified within each species [6, 16–18]. The detection of BEV-like viruses in other ruminants, swine, and even humans has raised concerns about broader host range and cross-species transmission [19, 20].

In recent years, reverse genetics systems have been developed for several enteroviruses, enabling the generation of recombinant viruses carrying reporter genes. These systems have greatly advanced the study of viral replication, host interactions, and antiviral screening. However, similar molecular tools for BEVs remain underdeveloped. While a few BEV-based recombinant viruses expressing short epitope tags have been reported, a stable BEV carrying a fluorescent reporter gene has not yet been described [21, 22]. The development of such a system would be highly valuable for visualizing viral infection dynamics, evaluating neutralizing antibodies, and facilitating high-throughput screening for antiviral compounds [23, 24].

In this study, we report the construction of a reverse genetics platform for a bovine enterovirus isolated from diarrheic cattle and the generation of a recombinant virus expressing a fluorescent reporter gene. Following full-length genome sequencing and characterization of the field strain, we established a cDNA-based rescue system and successfully recovered a replication-competent recombinant BEV encoding a fluorescent marker. This engineered virus was evaluated for infectivity, reporter expression, and basic genetic stability in vitro. Our results provide a new tool for investigating BEV biology and lay the groundwork for further applications in antiviral research and vaccine development.

Materials and methods

Cell culture

BHK-21 cells were maintained in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM; Gibco-C11995500BT, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco-10270106, USA) at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO₂. Cells were routinely passaged every 2–3 days and used for virus isolation, propagation, and titration when they reached approximately 80–90% confluence.

Sample collection and virus isolation

A fresh fecal sample (n = 1) was collected from an acutely diarrheic bovine on a farm in Hechi, Guangxi, China. The animal presented with profuse watery diarrhea at the time of sampling and showed no apparent respiratory or neurological signs based on field observation. Briefly, a fecal suspension was prepared in sterile phosphate-buffered saline supplemented with 200 U/mL penicillin, 200 µg/mL streptomycin, and 100 µg/mL gentamicin. The suspension was clarified by centrifugation at 10,000 × g for 5 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was passed through a 0.22 μm filter (Millipore- SLGP033R, USA). The filtered supernatant was inoculated onto BHK-21 cell monolayers, allowed to adsorb, and then maintained in culture medium. Cells were monitored for cytopathic effects at 6, 12, 24, and 48 h post-infection, and blind passaging was continued until obvious cytopathic effects (CPE) appeared. Culture supernatants were serially passaged three times to enrich the virus.

Detection of enteric viruses by RT-PCR

To screen for common bovine enteric pathogens, total nucleic acids were extracted from cytopathic effect (CPE)-positive BHK-21 cultures (UElandy-UEMNBFVNA-250, CHN). One-step RT-PCR (Vazyme-P612-01, CHN) was performed to detect RNA enteric viruses, including bovine viral diarrhea virus (BVDV), bovine coronavirus (BCoV), bovine enterovirus (BEV), mammalian orthoreovirus (MRV), and bovine rotavirus (BRV); conventional PCR was performed to detect DNA pathogens, including bovine parvovirus (BPV), bovine adenovirus (BAdv), and Salmonella spp, using the primer sets listed in Table 1. PCR products were analyzed by agarose gel electrophoresis. The BEV-specific amplicon was purified and Sanger sequenced for confirmation.

Plaque assay and purification

Virus-containing cell culture supernatants were subjected to three freeze–thaw cycles and clarified by centrifugation at 10,000 × g for 30 min at 4 °C. Plaque assays were performed using BHK-21 cells seeded in 6-well plates. Briefly, monolayers were inoculated with 10-fold serial dilutions of virus samples and incubated at 37 °C for 1 h with gentle rocking every 10–15 min. After adsorption, the inoculum was removed and cells were overlaid with DMEM containing 1% low-melting-point agarose (MCE-9012-36-6, USA) and 2% FBS. Plates were incubated for 3 days at 37 °C in a 5% CO₂ atmosphere until plaques were visible. The overlays were then carefully removed, and cells were fixed and stained with 3–4 mL of staining solution containing 0.5% crystal violet and 25% formaldehyde for 15 min.

For plaque purification, well-isolated plaques were picked from the agarose overlay using sterile pipette tips and resuspended in DMEM. After brief centrifugation to remove agarose debris, the supernatants were used to infect fresh BHK-21 monolayers. The plaque-picking procedure was repeated for three rounds to obtain a purified viral clone.

Indirect immunofluorescence assay

BHK-21 cells were seeded on coverslips in 24-well plates and infected with the indicated viruses. At the indicated time points, cells were washed with PBS, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min, permeabilized with 0.2% Triton X-100 for 10 min, and blocked with 5% BSA for 1 h at room temperature. Cells were then incubated with the primary antibody at 4 °C overnight, washed with PBS, and incubated with a fluorescent secondary antibody (Beyotime-A1089, CHN) for 1 h at room temperature in the dark. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (Beyotime-C1006, CHN), and images were acquired using a fluorescence microscope. Mock-infected cells served as negative controls.

Virus titration by TCID50

Virus titers were determined by the 50% tissue culture infectious dose (TCID50) assay in BHK-21 cells. Briefly, cells were seeded in 96-well plates and inoculated with 10-fold serial dilutions of virus samples. After incubation at 37 °C with 5% CO₂, wells were monitored daily for cytopathic effects. TCID50 values were calculated using the Reed–Muench method and expressed as TCID50/mL.

One-step growth analysis of viruses

One-step growth kinetics were evaluated in BHK-21 cells. Cell monolayers were infected with the indicated viruses at a high multiplicity of infection (MOI) to synchronize infection. After adsorption at 37 °C, the inoculum was removed and cells were washed, followed by addition of fresh maintenance medium. Culture supernatants were collected at the indicated time points post-infection, and viral titers were measured by TCID50 assay. Growth curves were plotted as TCID50/mL versus time.

Full-length genome amplification and analysis

Viral RNA extracted from cell culture supernatants was used as the template for full-genome sequencing. The complete genome of GXHC2317 was amplified as overlapping RT-PCR fragments covering the 5′UTR to the 3′UTR using the primer sets listed in Table 1. Amplicons with the expected sizes were purified and Sanger sequenced in both directions. Raw reads were trimmed and assembled into a consensus genome by mapping and contig joining based on overlap consistency. The genome organization and open reading frame were annotated by comparison with representative bovine enterovirus sequences available in GenBank.

For evolutionary analysis, the full-length genome sequence and the VP1 sequence were aligned with reference Enterovirus E and Enterovirus F strains downloaded from GenBank using a multiple sequence alignment tool. Phylogenetic trees were inferred in MEGA 7.0 using the maximum likelihood method with 1,000 bootstrap replicates. Trees were built for VP1 and for the capsid P1 region, and the genotype assignment was based on clustering with reference lineages and bootstrap support.

Recombination was assessed using RDP version 5.3. Multiple algorithms implemented in RDP were applied, and only events supported by more than one method were considered. Putative recombination signals were then confirmed by SimPlot bootscanning. Similarity and bootscan plots were generated using a 200 nucleotide sliding window and a 20 nucleotide step size across the complete genome, with selected Chinese BEV strains used as references to visualize mosaic similarity patterns and to define recombination breakpoints.

Construction of a full-length infectious clone

The complete genome of BEV strain GXHC2317 was amplified as overlapping cDNA fragments spanning the 5′UTR to the 3′UTR and assembled into a low-copy plasmid backbone under the control of a CMV promoter to generate a full-length infectious cDNA clone. The 3′ terminus was engineered to include a poly(A) tract and a ribozyme element to support generation of an authentic viral RNA end. The assembled plasmid was verified by restriction analysis and Sanger sequencing (Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China) across all junction regions and representative genomic sites. (Fig. 3A).

Construction of recombinant infectious clones with reporter genes

To generate a fluorescent reporter clone, the UnaG reporter gene (kindly provided in a plasmid by WeKwikGene) was inserted into the rGXHC2317 backbone between the 5′UTR and the VP4 junction region using overlap extension PCR (SOE-PCR), yielding the recombinant construct rGXHC2317-UnaG. The final reporter-containing infectious clone was confirmed by Sanger sequencing with specific primers (Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China). The primers used are listed in Table 1.

Rescue of viruses from infectious clones

Recombinant viruses were recovered from the infectious clone plasmids using standard reverse genetics procedures in BHK-21 cells. Successful rescue was confirmed by virus-associated cytopathic effect and molecular verification of the expected genomic features. For the reporter virus, UnaG fluorescence in infected cells was further confirmed by fluorescence microscopy, and bilirubin supplementation (Beyotime, SM4024-10 mM, China) was applied to enhance the UnaG fluorescence signal prior to imaging. Briefly, cells were incubated with bilirubin at a final concentration of 1 µM for 15 min at 37 °C (protected from light) before fluorescence observation.

Animal experiment

Specific-pathogen-free ICR mice were purchased from Guangzhou Bairui Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Guangzhou, China). Mice were housed under controlled conditions with sterile food and water, at 26 °C and 40–70% relative humidity, with 15–20 air changes per hour. All animal procedures were approved by the Animal Protection and Welfare Committee of Guangxi University (approval no. GXU-2023-0142).

For infection, 4-week-old ICR mice were orally inoculated with GXHC2317 or rGXHC2317 as described in the Results. At the indicated time points (3 or 7 days post-infection), mice were humanely euthanized. Animals were anesthetized by inhalation of 3% isoflurane until loss of consciousness and absence of reflex responses were confirmed. Euthanasia was then completed by cervical dislocation in accordance with institutional animal care and use guidelines. The small intestine, large intestine, lung, liver, kidney, and brain were collected and weighed. Each tissue was divided into two portions. One portion was homogenized in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) for viral load determination, and the other portion was fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for histopathological analysis. Fixed tissues were paraffin-embedded, sectioned at 5 μm, mounted on glass slides, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E). Histological lesions were evaluated by light microscopy.

BEV virus detection by fluorogenic quantitative real-time PCR

Mouse tissues were homogenized and total RNA was extracted using the Tiangen TGuide S32 magnetic bead RNA extraction kit (Tiangen-YDP604,CHN). After reverse transcription, BEV genome loads were quantified by SYBR Green–based quantitative real-time PCR (Vazyme-Q711, CHN) using primers targeting a GXHC2317 strain-specific region (Table 1). Thermal cycling and melting-curve analysis were performed as previously described [5]. No-template and negative extraction controls were included, and viral genome copies were calculated from a standard curve and expressed as genome copies per milligram of tissue.

CCK-8 assay for cell viability

BHK-21 cells were seeded in 96-well plates and allowed to attach. Cells were treated with serial dilutions of ribavirin, chloroquine phosphate, or pleconaril (Beyotime-Y258817-10 mg; 14194–25 g; Y061570-10 mg, CHN) for the indicated period. CCK-8 (Beyotime-C0037, CHN) reagent was then added according to the manufacturer’s instructions, and absorbance was measured using a microplate reader. Cell viability was calculated relative to the vehicle control, and the maximal non-cytotoxic concentration was defined as the highest dose maintaining more than 80% viability.

Antiviral screening using the rGXHC2317-UnaG reporter virus

BHK-21 cells were infected with rGXHC2317-UnaG at an MOI of 0.01. After viral adsorption, the inoculum was removed and cells were washed, followed by addition of medium containing each compound at its maximal non-cytotoxic concentration, ribavirin 200 µM, chloroquine phosphate 62.50 µM, and pleconaril 10 µM. After incubation for the indicated period, infection was quantified based on the UnaG fluorescence readout, and antiviral activity was expressed as the reduction in infection compared with the untreated infected control.

Flow cytometry assay for quantifying infection

For flow cytometric analysis, infected cells were harvested, washed with PBS, and resuspended in staining buffer. Cells were then incubated with bilirubin (final concentration, 1 µM) for 15 min at 37 °C (protected from light) to enhance UnaG fluorescence. UnaG fluorescence was directly measured to quantify the proportion of infected cells. Gating was set using mock-infected cells to define the UnaG-negative population, and the infection rate was reported as the percentage of UnaG-positive cells. Each condition was tested in independent replicates, and data were summarized as mean with standard deviation.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed with n ≥ 2 biological replicates, as indicated in the figure legends. Statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism 9.5.1. Differences between groups were assessed using Student’s t-test or two-way ANOVA, as appropriate. Data are presented as mean ± SD. ns, P > 0.05; *P < 0.05; **P < 0.01; ***P < 0.001.

Results

Isolation and identification of a bovine enterovirus from diarrheic cattle in Guangxi, China

A fecal sample was collected from an acutely diarrheic cattle on a farm in Hechi, Guangxi Province. The affected animal exhibited profuse watery diarrhea but showed no other clinical signs such as respiratory or neurological abnormalities. Fecal suspension supernatant were inoculated onto BHK-21 cells and serially passaged three times. Prominent CPE, including cell rounding and detachment, were observed at the third passage (Fig. 1A). To screen for common bovine enteric pathogens, total nucleic acids were extracted from cytopathic effect (CPE)-positive BHK-21 cultures and tested by RT-PCR/PCR using the primer sets listed in Table 1. The panel included BVDV, BCoV, BEV, MRV, BPV, BRV, BAdv, and Salmonella spp. Only the BEV-specific assay yielded the expected amplicon (Fig. 1B), whereas no detectable products were observed for the other targets. Sanger sequencing of the BEV amplicon confirmed BEV positivity, indicating that the isolate was a bovine enterovirus.

Plaque purification was carried out through three successive rounds on BHK-21 monolayers to obtain a genetically uniform virus population (Fig. 1C). Viral growth kinetics were assessed by measuring titers in supernatants collected at various time points post-infection. The virus reached a peak titer of 1011.67 TCID50/mL at 36 h, indicating efficient replication in BHK-21 cells (Fig. 1D). Indirect immunofluorescence assay (IFA) using a laboratory-produced polyclonal antibody against the BEV VP2 protein confirmed viral protein expression in infected cells (Fig. 1E).

Genomic characterization and recombination analysis of the Guangxi BEV isolate

To obtain the complete genome sequence of the Guangxi BEV isolate, overlapping RT-PCR products covering the full-length viral genome were amplified and analyzed by agarose gel electrophoresis. All fragments showed clear single bands with the expected sizes, indicating successful amplification of the entire genome (Fig. 2A). The assembled genome displayed the typical organization of bovine enteroviruses. Phylogenetic trees based on the nucleotide sequences of VP1 and the capsid P1 region were then constructed using representative Enterovirus E and Enterovirus F reference strains. In both trees, the Guangxi isolate clustered within the Enterovirus E genotype 3 (BEV-E3) lineage with strong bootstrap support, confirming its assignment to this genotype (Fig. 2B). To further investigate its evolutionary history, the full-length genome was subjected to recombination analysis using the RDP program, followed by confirmation with SimPlot bootscanning. Multiple RDP methods supported several recombination events, which are summarized in Table 2. SimPlot analysis of the complete genome, using selected Chinese BEV strains as references, revealed a mosaic pattern in which different genome regions of the Guangxi isolate showed highest similarity to the Heilongjiang strain BEV8 in 2021 and to the GX10 strain detected in Guangxi in 2023 (Fig. 2C). These results indicate that the Guangxi BEV isolate likely arose from historical recombination between local and northern Chinese BEV-E3–related lineages.

Construction and rescue of an infectious cDNA clone of BEV strain GXHC2317

To generate a reverse genetics system for BEV, we assembled a full-length infectious cDNA clone of strain GXHC2317 in a eukaryotic expression vector. The viral genome was placed downstream of a CMV promoter and upstream of a poly(A) tract, HDV ribozyme, and was assembled from five overlapping fragments (F1–F5) to facilitate cloning (Fig. 3A). Restriction enzyme analysis of the resulting plasmid pBEV-GXHC2317 yielded fragments of the expected sizes: the CMV promoter (PacI–SalI, 609 bp), F1 (SalI–AflII, 2518 bp), F2 (AflII–SpeI, 1433 bp), F3 (SpeI–BamHI, 1417 bp), F4 together with the poly(A) signal (BamHI–SmaI, 2092 bp), and the HDV ribozyme cassette (SmaI–NotI, 340 bp), confirming correct assembly of the clone (Fig. 3b). Transfection of pBEV-GXHC2317 into BHK-21 cells led to typical enterovirus-like CPE, including cell elongation, rounding, detachment and widening of intercellular spaces, which became evident by approximately 72 h post-transfection. Supernatants were subjected to freeze–thaw cycles and used to infect fresh BHK-21 monolayers, and a third-passage (P3) stock showing uniform CPE was obtained (Fig. 3c). Sequencing of RT-PCR products spanning the engineered genetic marker region confirmed that the rescued virus contained the expected marker sequence and was identical to the pBEV-GXHC2317 design in this region (Fig. 3d). Indirect immunofluorescence assay further verified successful rescue, as infected BHK-21 cells showed strong cytoplasmic staining with BEV-specific antibodies, whereas mock-infected cells remained negative (Fig. 3e). Finally, multi-step growth curve analysis in BHK-21 cells showed that the rescued recombinant virus reached similar peak titers and overall replication kinetics as the parental GXHC2317 strain, indicating that the cloning strategy did not markedly impair viral replication in vitro (Fig. 3f).

Parental and recombinant GXHC2317 exhibit comparable tissue tropism and histopathology in orally infected mice

To compare the in vivo behavior of the parental GXHC2317 strain and the recombinant rGXHC2317 virus, 4-week-old ICR mice were orally inoculated with 200 µL of each virus at a dose of 108.0 TCID50/mL [21, 28]. Viral loads in the small intestine, large intestine, lung, liver, kidney and brain were quantified at 3 and 7 days post-infection by qRT-PCR. Both viruses were detected in all tested tissues at both time points, with higher viral genome levels in the intestinal tract and lower levels in extra-intestinal organs. No clear differences in tissue viral loads were observed between the GXHC2317- and rGXHC2317-infected groups at either 3 or 7 days post-infection (Fig. 4A).

4 Tissue viral loads and histopathology in 4-week-old ICR mice orally infected with parental GXHC2317 or recombinant rGXHC2317. A Viral RNA loads in the small intestine, large intestine, lung, liver, kidney and brain at 3 and 7 days post-infection (dpi), determined by quantitative reverse transcription PCR (qRT-PCR). B Representative H&E-stained sections of major organs collected at 7 dpi from GXHC2317- and rGXHC2317-infected mice. Mild to moderate lesions were observed in both groups, including crypt and villus alterations with minimal lymphocytic infiltration in the intestine (green arrows), alveolar septal thickening with granulocyte infiltration and focal hemorrhage in the lung (yellow and green arrows), focal hepatocellular necrosis with venous congestion in the liver (purple and brown arrows), neuronal shrinkage and vacuolation in the brain (blue and yellow arrows), and mesangial proliferation and occasional tubular epithelial swelling in the kidney (yellow and blue arrows). The pattern and severity of lesions were comparable between parental and recombinant virus. Scale bars, 50 μm

Histopathological examination of H&E-stained sections showed that the recombinant virus generally reproduced the lesion spectrum observed with the parental strain (Fig. 4B), while displaying several qualitative differences. In GXHC2317-infected mice, the large intestine contained abundant, regularly arranged crypts with numerous goblet cells and only mild, scattered lymphocytic infiltration in the lamina propria. The small intestine showed villi of variable thickness with occasional blunting, tightly arranged tubular glands at the villus base, and mild lymphocyte infiltration in the lamina propria and submucosa. Lung tissue exhibited mild to moderate thickening of alveolar septa with scattered granulocyte infiltration, focal alveolar hemorrhage, and occasional bronchiolar epithelial desquamation, together with limited perivascular connective tissue proliferation and lymphocytic infiltration. The liver showed focal hepatocellular necrosis with nuclear pyknosis and fragmentation, scattered lymphocytes and granulocytes, and mild venous congestion. In the brain, cortical neurons were abundant, with occasional shrunken, hyperchromatic neurons and small vacuoles in the cortex and hippocampal CA3 region, without obvious inflammatory cell infiltration. The kidney showed irregularly distributed and locally clustered glomeruli with mesangial matrix expansion and mesangial cell proliferation, mild connective tissue hyperplasia in the renal pelvis, and scattered lymphocytes.

In rGXHC2317-infected mice, changes in the gastrointestinal tract were overall similar, with abundant crypts and focal lymphoid cell infiltration in the large intestine, and villi with frequent autolysis at the tips in the small intestine without marked inflammatory infiltration. Notably, lung sections showed more extensive granulocyte infiltration with mild diffuse alveolar septal thickening, focal hemorrhage, and venous congestion. The liver showed multifocal hepatocellular necrosis with mixed inflammatory cell infiltration, more pronounced venous congestion, and mild periportal connective tissue proliferation. In the brain, shrunken, deeply stained neurons were more frequently observed in the cortex and hippocampal CA3 and dentate gyrus regions, with focal hemorrhage and occasional vacuolation but no obvious inflammatory infiltrates. Kidneys from rGXHC2317-infected mice showed evenly distributed glomeruli with mild mesangial proliferation and focal tubular epithelial swelling, without clear interstitial hyperplasia or inflammatory cell infiltration.

Overall, oral inoculation of 4-week-old ICR mice with rGXHC2317 (108.0 TCID₅₀/mL) resulted in a tissue tropism and histopathological profile that overlapped with GXHC2317, but with qualitative differences in lung granulocyte infiltration and neuronal morphology that warrant quantitative scoring in future studies.

Generation of a UnaG-expressing reporter virus and establishment of a fluorescence-based antiviral screening assay

Previous work in Coxsackievirus A21 has shown that this virus can tolerate small fluorescent payloads such as UnaG inserted near the 5′ end of the coding region while retaining productive replication [29]. Guided by this strategy, we inserted the UnaG coding sequence at the junction between the 5′UTR and VP4 in the infectious clone of GXHC2317 and rescued the recombinant reporter virus, designated rGXHC2317-UnaG (Fig. 3A). Transfection of BHK-21 cells with the full-length plasmid yielded typical CPE after serial passage, and strong UnaG fluorescence was readily detected in P3 virus-infected cells, confirming successful expression of the reporter and efficient viral propagation (Fig. 5A). Multi-step growth curve analysis in BHK-21 cells demonstrated that rGXHC2317-UnaG replicated with a similar kinetic profile to the parental GXHC2317 strain but reached a reduced peak titer of approximately 105.0 TCID50/mL, indicating a moderate replication deficit associated with UnaG insertion while maintaining robust viral production suitable for downstream applications (Fig. 5B).

C Cell viability of BHK-21 cells treated for 24 h with increasing concentrations of ribavirin, chloroquine phosphate, or pleconaril; the dashed line indicates 80% viability. D Antiviral activity of the three compounds at their maximal non-toxic concentrations against rGXHC2317-UnaG infection. BHK-21 cells were infected at an MOI of 0.01, treated with compounds for 24 h, and infection rates were determined as the percentage of UnaG-positive cells by flow cytometry. Data are presented as mean ± SD of three independent experiments; asterisks denote statistically significant differences compared with the untreated control

We next assessed the cytotoxicity of three reference antivirals in BHK-21 cells by a cell viability assay. Ribavirin, chloroquine phosphate, and pleconaril showed minimal toxicity at 200 µM, 62.50 µM, and 10 µM, respectively, with cell viabilities above 80% at these concentrations (Fig. 5C) [23, 30]. Within these safe ranges, BHK-21 cells were infected with rGXHC2317-UnaG at an (MOI) of 0.01, incubated at 37 °C for 1 h, and then treated with each compound to evaluate their effects on virus replication post-infection. After 24 h of drug exposure, UnaG-positive cells were quantified by flow cytometry. Both ribavirin and pleconaril led to a marked reduction in the proportion of fluorescent cells compared with the untreated control, indicating clear inhibition of BEV replication, whereas chloroquine phosphate produced only a slight change in infection frequency (Fig. 5D). These data show that rGXHC2317-UnaG provides a convenient fluorescence-based platform for antiviral screening against bovine enterovirus and can distinguish compounds with differential inhibitory activity.

Discussion

The bovine enterovirus strain GXHC2317 was isolated from an acute diarrhea case in Hechi, Guangxi, and was confirmed as BEV by RT-PCR. BEV circulation in Guangxi and other regions of China has been documented by molecular epidemiology and genome-based studies, and the identification of GXHC2317 further expands the local strain collection and genomic resource base [1, 9, 18, 31]. Full-genome sequencing and phylogenetic analysis placed GXHC2317 within BEV-E3. Recombination analyses (RDP and SimPlot) supported a mosaic genome, with different genomic regions showing the highest similarity to a northern China strain (Heilongjiang BEV8/2021) and a Guangxi lineage (GX10/2023), consistent with the genetic diversity and frequent recombination reported for BEV populations [32, 33]. Given this mosaic genome, we used a recent field isolate rather than an older reference strain to build the reverse genetics and reporter system, so the screening assay better reflects viruses currently circulating in cattle, as recombination and ongoing sequence change may influence replication and antiviral sensitivity. At the same time, recombination suggests that drug responses may differ among BEV lineages; therefore, hits identified with rGXHC2317-UnaG should be confirmed using the parental GXHC2317 strain and, when feasible, additional BEV strains or genotypes. Although BEV infections are often subclinical, BEV has been associated with enteric disease and, under certain conditions, more severe outcomes. Because this study was based on a single field case, co-infection with other enteric pathogens cannot be excluded, and the direct causal link between BEV and the diarrhea episode remains unclear. Future work will expand surveillance in Guangxi to include both diarrheic and clinically healthy cattle and to increase genome sequencing for monitoring genotype turnover and recombination dynamics.

We constructed a full-length infectious cDNA clone of the recent BEV-E3 field strain GXHC2317 and successfully rescued the recombinant virus (rGXHC2317). Because BEVs show ongoing genetic change in the field, reverse genetics systems built on older laboratory strains may not fully capture the properties of currently circulating viruses [21, 22]. Establishing a clone based on a newly isolated strain therefore provides a timely and more relevant platform for mechanistic studies. Reverse genetics, in general, enables the generation of infectious virus directly from cloned cDNA and allows targeted genome changes to link specific sequences with replication, cell tropism, and virulence, which has been a major driver of progress in RNA virus research and the rapid study of emerging variants [34, 35]. In our system, the GXHC2317 genome was assembled under a CMV promoter in five fragments and yielded viable virus that produced typical cytopathic effects and reached titers comparable to the parental strain in cell culture, suggesting that the cloning strategy preserved the biological properties of the field isolate in vitro. Taken together, this GXHC2317-based reverse genetics platform provides a practical tool to support future studies on BEV replication and pathogenesis, and it will also facilitate ongoing surveillance-linked functional work as new BEV variants continue to emerge [36, 37].

In the 4-week-old ICR mouse model, the parental GXHC2317 and the cDNA-derived rGXHC2317 showed broadly comparable in vivo behavior. This close agreement in tissue distribution and overall lesion patterns supports an important point. The reverse genetics–rescued virus remains a reliable surrogate for the original field isolate rather than an attenuated laboratory artifact [38]. This strengthens the value of the GXHC2317 infectious clone for downstream studies, because phenotypes observed with engineered viruses can be interpreted in the context of the circulating strain [39]. We also noted subtle differences in intestinal pathology between the two groups, particularly in the small and large intestine. These changes were mild and did not translate into clear differences in viral loads, suggesting that they may reflect biological variability in oral infection, such as uneven exposure, local microenvironment effects, or differences in lesion timing, rather than a major shift in virulence. Future work using larger group sizes, additional time points, and quantitative pathology scoring will help determine whether these intestinal differences are reproducible and whether they link to specific viral or host factors.

Building on the GXHC2317 reverse genetics platform, we generated a fluorescent BEV reporter virus, which addresses a clear tool gap in BEV research. While short epitope tags have been reported for BEV, a stable fluorescent BEV has not been described, limiting rapid and quantitative readouts for infection and antiviral testing [40]. We selected UnaG as the reporter because its small size helps reduce the genetic burden, and it has been shown to support stable fluorescence readouts in more than one reverse genetics platform. In addition to the Coxsackievirus A21 work where UnaG was tolerated near the 5′ end of the coding region, UnaG has also been incorporated into recombinant rotaviruses generated by reverse genetics, where the engineered virus expressed functional UnaG and the inserted sequence remained stable over at least five low-MOI passages [29, 41]. In our system, inserting UnaG at the 5′UTR–VP4 junction yielded a replication-competent virus that produced clear CPE and strong fluorescence in P3-infected BHK-21 cells, supporting its use as an early-passage reporter. Nevertheless, the reporter virus exhibited a marked replication deficit, with a peak titer of ~ 105.0 TCID50/mL compared with ~ 1011.67 TCID50/mL for the parental strain (a > 6 log10 reduction). This substantial attenuation indicates that the UnaG insertion imposes a strong fitness cost and may limit applications requiring high-titer virus production or wild-type–like replication, even though the overall timing of the growth curve followed a similar pattern. This is consistent with the idea that modifications near UTR-associated elements can reduce replication efficiency while still allowing productive infection [42, 43].

Genetic stability of the reporter insert is an important point for downstream use. In other virus systems, UnaG has shown relatively good stability: in a recombinant Saffold virus, the GFP insert was not detected after three passages, whereas the UnaG gene was retained through at least five passages, suggesting that insert stability can depend on both reporter choice and selective pressure during passaging [44]. In a recombinant rotavirus, an NSP3–UnaG virus was reported to remain genetically stable over 10 rounds of serial passaging, indicating that UnaG can be maintained in some viral genomes for extended passages [40]. UnaG is also smaller than EGFP, which is attractive when insert size becomes a constraint. In our BEV reporter system, stability was confirmed up to P3, so the virus is best viewed as an early-passage reporter. Because longer passaging can select for faster-growing variants that lose foreign sequences, future work should test stability over additional passages (ideally with low-MOI passaging) and routinely verify the insert by RT-PCR/sequencing together with fluorescence readouts before large-scale screening [45].

A key advantage of rGXHC2317-UnaG is that it turns BEV infection into a direct, quantitative fluorescence readout, which is more objective and scalable than relying on CPE. Using non-cytotoxic doses, the assay clearly distinguished active inhibitors from weak or inactive compounds, supporting its suitability for primary antiviral screening. The limited effect of chloroquine phosphate in our BEV setting also suggests that antiviral activity cannot be assumed to translate across enterovirus species or assay systems, emphasizing the need for virus-specific validation [46, 47]. Future work should focus on confirming reporter stability during extended passaging and correlating the fluorescence signal with standard virological endpoints such as infectious titer and viral RNA to further improve robustness and comparability.

Conclusions

In this work, we isolated and characterized a bovine enterovirus strain GXHC2317 from diarrheic cattle in Guangxi, and confirmed that it belongs to BEV-E3 with evidence of recombination. We then built a full-length infectious cDNA clone and successfully rescued a recombinant virus that showed comparable replication in BHK-21 cells and similar tissue distribution and histopathology to the parental strain in an oral mouse model. Finally, we generated a UnaG-expressing fluorescent BEV and demonstrated its practical use as a quantitative platform to evaluate antiviral activity in vitro, providing a useful tool for BEV biology studies and antiviral screening.
