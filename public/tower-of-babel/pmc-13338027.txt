Perceived versus measured drinking water quality in rural West Bengal, India: a comparative cross-sectional mixed-method case study
Authors: Sourav Biswas, Aparajita Chattopadhyay, Suchandra Chatterjee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAccess to safe drinking water remains a critical public health challenge in many parts of rural India. People’s perceptions of water quality, often based on sensory cues, may not align with laboratory evidence, leading to gaps in protective behaviour. This study examines the divergence between perceived and measured water quality in a southern district of West Bengal. A mixed-method study was conducted in four villages of North 24 Parganas. The villages were selected based on computed Water Quality Index based on government data, and the study followed a comparative cross-sectional design. Quantitative data were collected from 521 individuals across 120 households via a semi-structured questionnaire on background characteristics, water sources, water related perceptions treatment practices, and reported health outcomes. Household water samples were tested in a science laboratory to measure physicochemical and microbial parameters. Quantitative analysis included univariate and bivariate statistics and logistic regression, while 16 in-depth interviews explored water related perceptions, health experiences, and willingness to pay for safe water. Laboratory analysis showed that 64% of the households consumed poor-quality drinking water, with fluoride (76%) and arsenic (65%) exceeding permissible limits. A clear perception–measured gap was observed, confirmed by McNemar’s test (χ² = 12.25, p < 0.001). Misperception was higher among households with limited awareness of contamination, lack of treatment practices, and greater distance from water sources. Health risks were diverse, including self-reported gastrointestinal illnesses and respiratory symptoms. While many households expressed willingness to pay USD 0.16 to USD 3.20 per month for safe drinking water, affordability remained a barrier for many households. Introduction

Ensuring access to safe drinking water remains one of the most significant public health challenges in many parts of India. Although India has made substantial progress in expanding access to improved water sources, the safety of this water is far from guaranteed1. Recent national assessments have revealed widespread contamination of groundwater sources, even in areas previously categorised as having improved access2. In the Indo-Gangetic Plain, which spans over northern and eastern India, geogenic contamination by arsenic, iron, and fluoride are highly prevalent3–5. This region is also vulnerable to nitrate contamination from agricultural runoff, unlined sanitation systems, and inadequate waste disposal6–8. These contaminants are often undetectable without scientific testing and have been linked to serious health outcomes, including skin lesions and various cancers (especially of the skin, lungs, and bladder)9–12. Despite these risks, many communities continue to rely on sensory perception, such as taste, smell, and clarity, to judge water quality, a method that can dangerously misrepresent actual conditions. As Doria (2006) observes, consumers often judge water quality by taste, odour, and appearance, even though these may not reflect actual health risks13. Moreover, the mismatch between scientifically verified water contamination and user beliefs poses a significant public health challenge.

Extensive global and national literature has examined the role of perception in shaping household behaviour related to water safety. Studies from both high- and low-income countries suggest that risk perception has a significant influence on choices regarding water consumption and treatment. For instance, in the United States, households with negative perceptions of tap water are more likely to buy bottled water or use filtration devices, even if the water meets safety standards14. Similarly, in South Korea, Um et al. (2002) found that public scepticism about municipal water safety led to an increase in private averting behaviours15. In Low- and Middle-Income Countries (LMICs) like Sri Lanka, Nicaragua, and Nepal, negative perceptions often lead to proactive water treatment strategies such as boiling and filtering16–18. Similar perception-driven behaviours have also been documented in other arsenic-affected regions, including Bangladesh, China, and parts of Latin America, where limited awareness and reliance on sensory cues continue to influence household water-use decisions despite known contamination risks19–23. In this context, the relationship between perception and behaviour is particularly critical in arsenic-affected regions, where exposure pathways are complex and often not immediately perceptible. Evidence from South Asia, especially eastern India and Bangladesh, indicates that arsenic exposure occurs not only through contaminated groundwater but also through dietary intake, particularly rice and food products irrigated with arsenic-laden water24–27. A study by Ángel A. Carbonell-Barrachina and colleagues has shown that inorganic arsenic can accumulate in cooked rice, contributing substantially to overall dietary exposure, even when drinking water sources are improved28. Furthermore, cooking practices and the quality of water used during preparation can influence arsenic levels in rice, thereby affecting overall exposure29–31. In many cases, arsenic remains undetectable through taste, colour, or smell, which limits the effectiveness of sensory-based judgement of water safety. As a result, households may underestimate the associated health risks and continue unsafe consumption practices32,33.

Study in India by Jalan and Somanathan (2008) confirms that providing households with information about actual contamination in their drinking water significantly increases the likelihood of treatment behaviour35. These interventions have proven more effective than merely distributing free water purification tools, indicating that perception, particularly when informed by accurate data, can be a powerful catalyst for behavioural change. However, the effectiveness of such strategies depends on the presence of trust, education, and cultural openness to change, making context-specific understanding essential. More recently, Levêque and Burns (2017) applied structural equation modelling to show that prior knowledge, trust in public systems, and perceived control influence whether risk perception translates into preventive actions36. In Canada and Western Europe, studies have shown that aesthetic attributes (e.g., chlorine taste, odour, and colour) significantly affect the public’s trust in tap water and, in turn, their consumption behaviours13,37.

West Bengal, particularly the districts situated along the eastern banks of the Ganga River, is among the most severely affected regions in terms of groundwater arsenic contamination. The problem is not limited to arsenic; high levels of iron and fluoride also co-exist, sometimes masking arsenic’s taste or altering its appearance, complicating risk assessment based on sensory cues. Furthermore, nitrate from fertilizer runoff and bacterial contamination due to poor sanitation systems are additional health threats in the region38,39. Despite several public awareness campaigns, many residents still depend on shallow tube wells or hand pumps that are prone to contamination40,41. Households in rural areas of West Bengal often evaluate drinking water safety based on observable characteristics such as clarity, colour, and odour, even though harmful contaminants like arsenic, iron, and nitrate are typically undetectable through sensory assessment alone38. This over-reliance on sensory cues can create a false sense of security and delay the adoption of protective behaviours. Multiple studies emphasize that subjective perceptions of water safety, often shaped by social norms, and aesthetic cues can override empirical evidence, particularly in rural and peri-urban settings where formal risk communication is limited13,42. Factors such as age, education, and access to trusted information sources significantly influenced the perception of water-related risks43. Additionally, beliefs, norms, and perceptions disseminated through social networks, rather than formal authorities, significantly influenced residents’ risk assessments and water-use decisions44,45. As Rojas et al. (2013) note, people primarily judge water quality by its colour and appearance, using these as informal standards even for assessing raw water46.

While existing literature provides substantial insights into risk perception and water behaviour, several gaps remain unaddressed, particularly regarding how perception and reality interact in geochemically contaminated regions. Much of the existing research focuses on perceived risks or objective measurements in isolation, without exploring how the divergence between these two dimensions influences health behaviour. Moreover, fewer studies have integrated both qualitative and quantitative approaches to capture the nuanced motivations behind water use and treatment decisions. A more granular understanding of how geogenic contaminants, such as arsenic and fluoride, are perceived at the household level in endemic regions is missing. Do people view them as immediate threats or as distant, chronic risks? In addition, there is a lack of empirical evidence that simultaneously links laboratory-confirmed multi-parameter water quality assessments with household-level perceptions and behavioural responses in rural, groundwater-dependent settings. This study attempts to answer such questions. It offers primary data collected through semi-structured household interviews and laboratory testing of water sources, providing a holistic view of both perception and actual exposure. Unlike previous studies, this research integrates objective measurements of multiple contaminants (including arsenic, fluoride, and microbial indicators) with perception data to explicitly quantify the perception–measured gap and its implications for exposure and health risk. Specifically, the objectives of this study are to assess the perceptions of drinking water quality among households; to analyse how these perceptions compare with tested water parameters such as arsenic, fluoride, and bacterial presence; and to evaluate the implications of perception–measured gaps on health risk. By adopting a mixed-methods framework, this study provides a context-specific and integrated understanding of how misperception contributes to continued exposure, thereby addressing a critical gap in environmental health literature.

Data and methods

Study design

This study adopted a cross-sectional household survey design to assess perceptions of drinking water quality among residents and to compare these perceptions with laboratory-tested water parameters. In the absence of prior empirical estimates of the proportion of households with misperception regarding drinking water quality, the sample size was calculated using a proxy indicator reflecting exposure to unsafe water. According to published estimates, about 26 million people in West Bengal are at risk from arsenic in drinking water47, which corresponds to 28% of the state’s 2011 population. Study area and sample collection

For this study, North 24 Parganas district in the state of West Bengal was selected as the primary study area due to its long-standing and well-documented issue of arsenic contamination in groundwater, which poses a serious public health concern. According to groundwater monitoring data from the Central Ground Water Board (CGWB, 2019–2021), several blocks within North 24 Parganas report arsenic concentrations far exceeding the WHO guideline value of 0.01 mg/L and the Bureau of Indian Standards (BIS) permissible limit of 0.05 mg/L. In addition to arsenic, fluoride, and total dissolved solids (TDS) have also been reported in many locations, further contributing to poor water quality and associated health risks. This district spans over an area of 4,094 square kilometres and is geographically located between 22°11′6″ N to 23°15′2″ N latitude and 88°20′0″ E to 89°5′0″ E longitude. To evaluate groundwater quality in North 24 Parganas, the Water Quality Index (WQI) was computed for 36 monitoring sites using the method proposed by Brown et al. 197249. The WQI provides a single composite score that reflects the overall quality of water by integrating multiple physicochemical parameters. Based on the WQI scores, sites were categorised into different water quality grades. For the purpose of comparative analysis, four villages were selected for detailed investigation based on the calculated WQI scores: two with poor water quality and two with good quality. All villages were selected from a homogeneous geographical region with similar demographic and environmental characteristics, thereby reducing confounding factors and enhancing comparability. This selection strategy supports the comparative study design. A total of 521 individuals from 120 households across four villages, two with good groundwater quality (B and D villages) and two with poor quality (A and C), were surveyed. Each village contributed 30 households, ensuring balanced geographic and demographic representation. Although the estimated sample size was 511, the final survey covered 521 individuals.

To comprehensively assess environmental exposure, drinking water samples were collected from each surveyed household. For water sampling, 2 L of drinking water were obtained directly from the households’ drinking water storage containers. To ensure the long-term stability of the samples, two drops of nitric acid were added immediately after water collection. The samples were then stored in clean, sterilised containers and kept in a cool, dark place to preserve their properties. For microbial analysis, separate water samples were collected in sterile containers and stored at a low temperature until laboratory processing. This study used a variety of methods for water testing, as detailed in Appendix Table 1. Water parameters included coliform bacteria50,51, arsenic52, nitrate53, fluoride54, TDS55, electrical conductivity (EC)55, pH55, calcium56, magnesium56, total hardness56, chloride57, and dissolved oxygen58, while soil parameters included pH59, EC60, organic matter61, nitrogen62, phosphorus63, potassium64, calcium65, and magnesium65.

Households were included if they had resided in the village for at least five years, primarily relied on groundwater (e.g., tube wells or hand pumps), had an adult (≥ 18 years) who was willing to provide informed consent, and were available for both interviews and water sample collection. Information on children under 15 years was obtained from the mother or primary female caregiver. Households with temporary or seasonal residence were excluded to ensure reliability in long-term exposure assessment. Water parameters were tested in the laboratory between December 6, 2023, and May 25, 2024. Household data were collected from September 11, 2023, to February 6, 2024, over approximately five months. Methods

This paper is based on a mixed-methods research design integrating both quantitative and qualitative approaches to assess drinking water quality, public perception, health impacts, and household-level practices in selected villages of North 24 Parganas, West Bengal. Data were collected using a semi-structured household questionnaire capturing demographic details, water source information, perceptions of drinking water quality (in terms of taste, odour, colour, and clarity), household treatment practices, and health outcomes. Water samples were collected from household drinking sources and tested for physicochemical and microbial parameters, including arsenic, fluoride, nitrate, calcium, magnesium, chloride, TDS, EC, total hardness, and coliform bacteria. Laboratory testing was conducted according to BIS protocols. To calculate the WQI, we used the formula proposed by Brown et al.49 and adhered to the BIS for evaluating water properties and contaminants66. Appendix Table 2 provides an overview of the parameters and their permissible limits as defined by various countries and organizations67–69.

Calculation of the WQI

The WQI was calculated with data drawn from a government source, in three steps. First, a weight was assigned to each water quality parameter based on its standard value. These weights were calculated in such a way that their total equalled one. Second, a sub-index value was calculated for each parameter using its observed concentration, standard value, and ideal value (usually zero for most parameters, except pH). Finally, the overall WQI was calculated by combining all the sub-index values with their respective weights66,70,71. Based on the final WQI values and BIS standards, households were grouped into two categories: (i) poor-quality drinking water (WQI > 50) and (ii) good-quality drinking water (WQI ≤ 50). This classification was used to compare actual water quality with how households perceive it.

Perception–measured comparison

To evaluate perception vs. measured conditions, responses from 424 individuals aged 15 and above were compared with laboratory-measured water quality assessments. Perception-related questions were designed to capture subjective evaluations of water safety, taste, smell, and clarity. A comparative matrix (cross-tabulation) was then developed to assess alignment or mismatch between perceived and actual water quality status. A binary misperception variable was subsequently constructed, coded as 1 when perceived water quality did not match laboratory-tested quality and 0 when perception and laboratory results were concordant. To statistically assess whether discrepancies between perceived and actual water quality were systematic, McNemar’s paired test was applied. Furthermore, to identify factors associated with misperception, a multivariable logistic regression model was estimated using misperception as the outcome variable, adjusting for socio-demographic characteristics, household factors, access to drinking water sources, water treatment practices, and prior knowledge of water contamination.

Additionally, qualitative data were collected through 16 in-depth interviews, four from each village, including 12 females and 4 males, representing both exposure groups. These interviews explored perceptions of water quality, treatment practices, health effects, and willingness to pay for clean water.

The study surveyed a total of 521 individuals across four villages in North 24 Parganas. Appendix Table 3 presents the distribution of participants, which is relatively even across sites, with 129 from Village A (24.76%), 122 from Village B (23.42%), 130 from Village C (24.95%), and 140 from Village D (26.87%). The sample included 265 males (50.86%) and 256 females (49.14%). The largest proportion of participants were aged 35 to 49 years (128 participants, 24.57%), followed by 99 participants (19.00%) in the 15 to 24 age group and 94 participants (18.04%) aged 25 to 34 years. Participants aged 50 to 63 years accounted for 76 individuals (14.40%), while children aged 6 to 14 years accounted for 67 participants (12.86%). Children aged 0 to 5 years represented 30 participants (5.76%), and individuals aged 64 years and above accounted for 27 participants (5.18%).

Perception of drinking water quality

The perception of drinking water quality among the 424 participants aged 15 years and above provides important insights into how people judge the safety and acceptability of their water. Table 2 presents these findings based on responses to a semi-structured questionnaire. A large proportion of participants (58.25%) rated their drinking water quality as “Good,” while 19.1% believed it to be “Very Good.” On the other hand, 18.87% of participants felt that the water quality was “Bad,” and a smaller group (3.77%) perceived it as “Very Bad.”

When asked about the taste of their drinking water, 54.72% rated it as “Good” and 17.69% as “Very Good.” However, concerns were evident, as 21.46% said the taste was “Bad” and 6.13% said it was “Very Bad.” This suggests that more participants were dissatisfied with the taste of their water compared to its overall quality. A similar pattern was observed in how participants perceived the smell of their drinking water. While 58.02% found it “Good” and 19.34% “Very Good,” a significant number still described the odour as “Bad” (15.57%) or “Very Bad” (7.08%).

Regarding the colour or appearance of the water, responses were more mixed. While 22.88% perceived their water as “Very Clear” and 42.45% as “Somewhat Clear,” a notable 26.89% felt it was “Somewhat Cloudy,” and 7.78% thought it was “Very Cloudy.” These results reflect visible turbidity concerns, which can affect both confidence in water safety and actual use behaviour. In terms of awareness about drinking water safety, more than half of the participants (53.30%) felt “Somewhat Informed,” and 24.06% considered themselves “Well Informed.” However, a combined 22.64% reported low awareness, 16.04% said they were “Somewhat Badly Informed,” and 6.60% felt “Very Badly Informed.” This knowledge gap could contribute to underestimating risks, especially in areas with high levels of invisible contaminants like arsenic and fluoride.

Assessing population exposure to poor drinking water quality and the gap between perceived and actual drinking water quality

The physicochemical characteristics of drinking water across the selected villages (Appendix Table 4) indicate substantial spatial variation in water quality parameters. Elevated concentrations of key contaminants were observed across villages, with particularly high levels of arsenic and fluoride. In addition, higher values of physicochemical properties such as TDS, EC, and total hardness were observed in certain locations. For instance, mean arsenic concentrations were highest in Village C (0.76 ± 0.17 mg/L), followed by Village A (0.50 ± 0.25 mg/L), both substantially exceeding the permissible limit of 0.01 mg/L. Fluoride levels were also found elevated across all villages, with Village C (1.63 ± 0.21 mg/L) and Village A (1.50 ± 0.19 mg/L) exceeding the recommended limit of 1.0 mg/L. Similarly, TDS and EC values were markedly higher in Village C (577.90 ± 291.04 mg/L and 1001.54 ± 490.89 µS/cm, respectively), indicating poor water quality and possible mineral enrichment. Total hardness and calcium concentration were also found elevated, particularly in Village C, reflecting the geochemical composition of the aquifer. In contrast, relatively lower concentration of most water quality parameters were observed in Villages B and D.

The analysis of laboratory-tested water samples revealed a concerning level of exposure to various chemical and microbial contaminants among the surveyed population. Table 3 presents the proportion of individuals consuming drinking water that exceeds the BIS acceptable limits for several key water quality parameters, indicating potential health risks. Among the key contaminants and physiochemial parameters, fluoride contamination was the most widespread, affecting 75.62% (394 individuals), followed by elevated EC (> 300 µS/cm), which impacted 67.37% (351 individuals). High arsenic levels (> 0.01 mg/L) also pose a severe health risk, with 64.68% (337 individuals) exposed to arsenic-contaminated water. Calcium concentrations exceeding the BIS limit of 75 mg/L affected 65.45% (341 individuals). Nitrate levels above 45 mg/L impacted 47.41% (247 individuals). The presence of coliform bacteria, a strong indicator of microbial contamination, affected 41.07% (214 individuals). Additionally, 37.43% (195 individuals) were found to consume water with total hardness exceeding 300 mg/L. Higher levels of magnesium (> 30 mg/L) were found in the drinking water of 22.07% (115 individuals), which, when combined with sulphate, could have a laxative effect. Chloride concentrations exceeding 250 mg/L affected 13.63% (71 individuals), and 11.52% (60 individuals) who consumed water with TDS above 500 mg/L. Overall, 63.53% (331 individuals) were categorised as consuming poor-quality drinking water based on the WQI calculated using the Brown et al. (1972) method, following BIS cutoff standards. A comparison across different village types showed that the proportion of households exposed to contaminants is consistently higher in poor WQI villages (A and C) than in good WQI villages (B and D). For instance, arsenic contamination (> 0.01 mg/L) was reported among 90.73% of households in poor WQI villages compared to 38.93% in good WQI villages. Similarly, fluoride exposure (> 1 mg/L) was98.07% in poor WQI villages versus 53.44% in good WQI villages. Higher exposure levels in poor WQI villages werealso observed for nitrate (55.98% vs. 38.93%), coliform contamination (51.74% vs. 30.53%), EC (95.75% vs. 39.31%), calcium (76.45% vs. 54.58%), and total hardness (66.41% vs. 8.78%). Hence overall, poor drinking water quality was observed in 90.35% of households in poor WQI villages compared to 36.64% in good WQI villages.

In Fig. 2, a comparison of the perceived and measured drinking water quality revealed significant gaps between measured water quality and public perception of water. Among those who perceived their water quality as “very good,” 59.26% were actually consuming poor-quality water. Similarly, among participants who believed their water was “good,” 55.06% were unknowingly exposed to unsafe drinking water. The gap became even more pronounced among those who rated their water as “bad,” with 83.75% consuming poor-quality water, while of those who thought their water was “very bad,” 87.50% were indeed drinking contaminated water.

Perceived vs. measured drinking water quality assessment in the study area.

To statistically assess whether the observed discrepancies between perceived and laboratory-tested drinking water quality at the individual level were systematic, McNemar’s paired test was applied using pooled individual-level data (Table 5). The results indicated a statistically significant discordance between perception and measured water quality (χ² = 12.25, p < 0.001). The number of individuals exhibiting false safety perception, defined as perceiving drinking water as safe despite laboratory confirmation of unsafe quality, was markedly higher (n = 184) than those exhibiting false unsafe perception (n = 15). This asymmetry demonstrates that misperception was not random but was strongly skewed towards the under-recognition of unsafe drinking water. When interpreted alongside the case–control patterns presented in Table 6, these findings provided robust evidence of a pronounced perception–measured gap at the individual level.

As shown in the forest plot (Fig. 3), the multivariable logistic regression analysis identified key factors significantly associated with misperception of drinking water quality. Individuals residing ≥ 0.2 km from the drinking water source exhibited higher odds of misperception compared to those with water available within the dwelling (AOR: 1.79). Similarly, households not practicing water treatment were more likely to misperceive drinking water quality (AOR: 1.27). The forest plot further indicates that a lack of prior knowledge of water contamination was a strong predictor, with individuals having no prior knowledge showing substantially higher odds of misperception (AOR: 1.81). Religion also emerged as a significant determinant, with Muslims demonstrating higher odds of misperception compared to Hindus (AOR: 1.41). In contrast, higher education was associated with lower odds of misperception (AOR: 0.81).

Perception of water quality

Participants’ perceptions of water quality varied significantly across villages with different groundwater conditions. Participants from villages with poor water quality frequently expressed concerns regarding changes in clarity, taste, and visual appearance, particularly during the monsoon season. The accumulation of rainwater in courtyards and near hand pumps worsened the condition of available drinking water, highlighting the seasonality of water quality issues. A lady mentioned: “Every year, during the rainy season, water gets logged in the courtyard because of heavy rains. The hand pump water turns turbid and murky. Mosquitoes and flies are everywhere, and it becomes nearly impossible to get clean drinking water” (Female, 24 years, poor WQI village). Some participants also mentioned physical contaminants like sand and dirt in their drinking water, which made the water visibly unsafe. One participant stated, “Sometimes when we pump water, it comes out mixed with sand. We leave it to settle before using, but it’s not clean” (Female, 36 years, poor WQI village). Despite recognizing the contamination, families often had no alternative sources. As expressed by a male villager: “We know the water is not safe, but what can we do? There’s no piped water here, and buying bottled water is too costly for daily use” (Male, 30 years, poor WQI village). In contrast, in villages with good water quality, participants expressed satisfaction with their long-standing water sources, particularly tube wells that consistently delivered clean and rust-free water. “We’ve been using the same tubewell for more than 10 years. It still gives good water, no rust, no stains,” (Female, 38 years, good WQI village), said one participant. Another highlighted seasonal variation: “Most of the year, our hand pump water is clear and tastes fine. But during the rainy season, when water gets logged and reaches up to the courtyard, the tubewell water sometimes turns muddy and looks unclear” (Female, 32 years, poor WQI village).

These responses indicate that visible cues such as water clarity, colour, and taste play a major role in shaping perceptions of water safety, often overriding scientific or laboratory-based assessments.

Health effects of unsafe water

The interviews revealed a widespread belief in the link between poor water quality and various health outcomes, particularly among children. Several participants reported frequent gastrointestinal issues that they believed were triggered by consuming contaminated water. A woman noted, “In our family, children often suffer from vomiting and diarrhoea during the rainy season, and we believe it is due to the contaminated water” (Female, 28 years, poor WQI village). Similar accounts were given of respiratory symptoms following water consumption. “Children often suffer from fever, cold, and cough after drinking the water, especially during the rainy season when it becomes dirty” (Female, 27 years, poor WQI village), one participant shared.

Gastrointestinal disorders were frequently cited, with many villagers acknowledging recurring stomach ailments and loose motions. “Many people complain of stomach pain or loose motions after drinking this water. It doesn’t look clean, but we have no other source” (Male, 47 years, poor WQI village), said another participant. In addition to waterborne illnesses, vector-borne diseases also emerged as a concern. The combination of stagnant water and poor drainage contributed to an increase in mosquitoes and disease transmission during the monsoon. “Typhoid and malaria are common during the monsoon. The water gets mixed with drain water, and mosquitoes increase all around” (Male, 34 years, good WQI village), one male participant recounted.

Water treatment practices in the household

Household-level water treatment practices were inconsistent and largely shaped by the perceived condition of water or the occurrence of illness. Several families reported boiling or filtering water only when a family member fell ill or during times when visible contamination was present. A participant mentioned, “We boil water only when someone in the family falls ill” (Female, 28 years, poor WQI village). Another added, “We filter the water only during the rainy season, because it becomes dirtier than usual” (Female, 31 years, poor WQI village). However, some participants maintained regular filtration practices throughout the year. One noted, “We always use a filter at home, regardless of the season, to make sure our drinking water is clean” (Female, 26 years, good WQI village). The methods used for treatment varied, including boiling, heating, filtering through cloth or ceramic filters, and occasional use of alum or chlorine tablets. Many participants indicated that greater care was taken for children, with filtered or boiled water was prioritised for them, especially in rainy season.

Feedback on water quality improvements

Participants who adopted treatment methods like boiling or filtering reported noticeable improvements in water taste, appearance, and perceived safety. These changes were often used as proxies for cleanliness. “After boiling the water, it looks clearer and tastes better” (Female, 31 years, poor WQI village), said one female participant. “The salty taste is gone, and we feel the water is safer now” (Female, 35 years, good WQI village), another added. Participants described a reduction in illness episodes, particularly among children. “Filtered water is clean, and since using it, my children don’t fall sick as often” (Male, 29 years, good WQI village), said a male participant. Another noted, “Since we started filtering, we don’t visit the doctor as frequently” (Male, 34 years, good WQI village).

Participants also observed improvements in digestion, taste of cooked food, and reduced staining on utensils. In better water-quality villages, filtration still played a reassuring role. “Even though our water is usually fine, using a filter gives peace of mind” (Female, 31 years, poor WQI village), said one participant. While some mentioned minor dissatisfaction with altered taste, most participants were satisfied with filtration outcomes.

Willingness to pay and community support for safe water

When asked about contributing financially to a clean water supply, many participants expressed willingness to pay a nominal monthly amount. “We are ready to pay ₹30 per month regularly if the water stays clean and safe” (Male, 30 years, poor WQI village), stated one male participant. Another added, “If everyone in the village shares the cost, it will be easier for us and the expenses will be reduce” (Male, 47 years, poor WQI village). However, financial constraints were a barrier for some families. “My family doesn’t contribute because we have very low income and can’t afford it right now” (Female, 24 years, poor WQI village), shared a female participant. The notion that water is a basic right and should be provided by the government was also reflected in the responses. “We are willing to pay a small monthly fee, but the government should take responsibility to ensure safe water for all” (Male, 29 years, good WQI village), one participant argued.

Discussion

While prior research has examined water risk perception and behaviour, key gaps remain in understanding how perceived and measured water quality interact. Most studies consider these dimensions separately, with limited evidence on how their divergence shapes health behaviour or how households perceive contaminants such as arsenic and fluoride. This study addresses these gaps by integrating laboratory-tested water quality data with household perceptions and behaviours using a mixed-methods approach in rural North 24 Parganas, West Bengal. Combining laboratory-based WQI assessments of geogenic contaminants (arsenic, fluoride) with in-depth interviews offers a rich understanding of how perception influences behaviour. The study, based on a comparative cross-sectional design, has several important findings that need attention:

First: laboratory results showed that nearly two-thirds of the population consumed water above BIS safety limits, and a striking mismatch was observed between people’s perception and measured water quality. Many households reported their water as “good” even when contaminated, indicating a substantial perception–measured gap. Among those who perceived their water quality as “very good,” 59% were actually consuming poor-quality water, while 55% of those rating it as “good” were exposed to unsafe drinking water. The mismatch persisted even among those who perceived their water as unsafe, with 84% and 88% of participants who rated their water as “bad” and “very bad,” respectively, indeed consuming contaminated water. McNemar’s test indicates that misperception was systematic rather than random. The higher prevalence of false safety perception compared to false unsafe perception highlights a strong tendency toward under-recognition of unsafe drinking water72,73.

Second, the substantial variation in arsenic contamination across villages reflects the well-documented spatial heterogeneity of groundwater quality in alluvial aquifers of the Gangetic Plains, where localized hydrogeological and geochemical conditions govern arsenic mobilization. These differences likely arise from variations in aquifer composition, sediment characteristics, and groundwater flow dynamics across the study area. The elevated concentrations of arsenic, fluoride, electrical conductivity, and total hardness observed across villages in North 24 Parganas reflect the geochemical characteristics of groundwater in alluvial aquifers, where localized hydrogeological conditions influence contaminant mobilization. This spatial variability in arsenic contamination may also be influenced by proximity to river channels and paleo-channel deposits within the study area. Villages located closer to riverine systems are more likely to be underlain by younger alluvial sediments rich in fine particles and organic matter, which promote reducing conditions and enhance arsenic mobilization74,75. In contrast, villages situated relatively farther from river influence or within more sandy and oxidized aquifer zones may experience lower levels of contamination, helping to explain the observed differences across villages76,77. These patterns help explain the widespread exposure to unsafe drinking water observed in the study area. This discrepancy aligns with recent findings in industrial settings in West Bengal, where perceptions of water cleanliness do not align well with chemical testing results78. Such false reassurance often arises from water’s visual clarity, taste, and odour rather than recognising invisible risks. Importantly, only 23% of participants rated themselves as poorly informed, a knowledge gap similar to earlier work in Guwahati, where public understanding of contamination fell below 4%79. This lack of chemical awareness likely contributes to continued exposure.

Third, qualitative insights further deepened understanding by illuminating how contextual beliefs influence water-related behaviours. In high-exposure villages, participants commonly reported that heavy rainfall caused courtyard flooding, resulting in visibly muddy and turbid hand pump water during monsoon months. These seasonal changes shaped temporary perceptions of risk, yet year-round concern remained limited. Several females and males participants linked waterborne illness symptoms, particularly among children, to poor water quality, echoing earlier work that shows diarrhoeal illnesses are often underreported or misattributed in rural India80. In our study, participants frequently identified turbidity and visible particles, such as sand or cloudiness, as primary indicators of water safety, while awareness of chemical contaminants like arsenic and fluoride were notably low. Although the broader literature often indicate notes that sensory cues dominate risk perception in low-literacy or rural settings81,82, this study highlights that household hygiene practices and lifestyle factors also play a critical role in shaping both exposure and health outcomes83,84. Unsafe water storage practices, such as storing water in open or unclean containers, can lead to secondary microbial contamination even when the source water is relatively safe85,86. Similarly, poor sanitation conditions and inadequate hand hygiene may increase the risk of waterborne diseases, independent of source water quality87,88. Lifestyle factors, including irregular water treatment practices, reliance on untreated groundwater, and seasonal variations in water use, further contribute to the persistence of exposure89,90.

Fourth, while responses reveal a deep-seated awareness of the health effects of unsafe water, we found a mixed attitude towards water treatment and filtration processes. One of the primary concerns expressed by participants was the health effect of contaminated water. Many participants associated unsafe drinking water with waterborne diseases such as typhoid, malaria, dengue, diarrhoea, cholera, and elephantiasis. Additionally, they linked poor water quality to other illnesses like vomiting, fever, cough, headaches, gastric issues, and even tuberculosis. Beyond immediate infections, participants highlighted chronic conditions such as hypertension, diabetes, thyroid disorders, and asthma as potential long-term effects of consuming impure water. In the study area, water was primarily treated for children, while adults used treated water mainly during illness or when visible contamination was present. Treatment was more frequent during the rainy season when water contamination was more apparent. Feedback on water filtration interventions indicated a generally positive reception, with many participants noting improvements in taste, appearance, and perceived safety. Our findings show that treatment was often prioritised for children or sick family members, suggesting a partial understanding of the protective value of water treatment. Perceptions of improved taste, clarity, and reduced illness episodes post-treatment reinforced the perceived efficacy of filtration and boiling. However, such practices were not universally followed due to cost, time, and convenience constraints, a trend also documented in previous Indian studies91,92.

Fifth, regarding support for water filtration services, many villagers expressed interest in having local water centres to improve accessibility and even create job opportunities. Community-managed water centres were proposed as potential solutions, provided they ensured accessibility and employment opportunities. Concerns remained about the sustainability of such efforts without institutional and household-level support. Some participants remained uncertain about long-term adoption, stressing the importance of community engagement, family backing, and involvement of local leaders. There was also a strong call for increased community outreach and education on the long-term health and cost benefits of clean water. This behaviour reflected earlier studies by Brown and Clasen (2012) and Peletz et al. (2013), which emphasised the need for high adherence to water treatment protocols to achieve sustained health benefits93,94.

Sixth, willingness to pay for clean water varied. Many villagers expressed a readiness to contribute some money monthly, but others, especially from low-income households, felt that the responsibility should lie with the government. This sentiment reflects broader policy challenges in promoting community-managed water systems and user-fee models, particularly in contexts where public services are heavily subsidised92,95. Furthermore, household decision-making dynamics played a role, with females participants often deferred payment decisions to male members. This aligns with the findings of Francis et al. (2015), who highlighted how gender roles and intra-household power structures can hinder the sustainability of interventions92.

Seventh, most socio-demographic factors were not significantly associated with water quality exposure; however, religion and income emerged as key determinants, highlighting both economic inequality and religious disparities in access to safe water. Poorer households consistently face greater risks and lower access to safe water services96,97. While a higher proportion of Hindus are exposed to poor-quality water compared to Muslims; however, misperceptions of water quality were higher among Muslims. Experimental evidence from studies on risk message framing demonstrates that precise, transparent communication of uncertainty significantly improves public trust and supports behaviour change, particularly when people need to adopt protective actions98. Adopting such communication approaches is vital in regions where contamination risks are high but public perception remains low, as observed in our study region.

Limitation of the study

This study has some limitations. First, the cross-sectional design limits the ability to establish causal relationships between perceived and measured drinking water quality. Second, the study was conducted in selected villages of North 24 Parganas, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other geographical settings. Third, perception-related data were self-reported and may be subject to recall bias and social desirability bias. Additionally, information on children under 15 years of age was reported by mothers or primary caregivers rather than collected directly, which may introduce reporting bias. Although water samples were tested using standard protocols, temporal and seasonal variations in water quality could not be fully captured. Additionally, unmeasured factors such as household hygiene practices, risk awareness, and socio-cultural beliefs may influence perception, potentially affecting the observed perception–measured gap. Despite these limitations, the study provides valuable context-specific insights into the discrepancy between perceived and measured drinking water quality in rural settings.

Conclusion

Limited evidence exists that integrates perceived and laboratory-assessed drinking water quality in the context of West Bengal. The study provides context-specific evidence and contributes to the existing literature, offering implications for risk communication strategies and public health interventions. This study indicates a gap between perceived and measured drinking water quality in rural North 24 Parganas, West Bengal. Laboratory analyses revealed widespread contamination with arsenic, fluoride, and microbial pathogens, whereas household perceptions were largely based on sensory cues. This mismatch contributes to inadequate protective behaviours, irregular water treatment practices, and increased health risks. Although many households expressed willingness to pay for safe water, affordability and limited awareness of invisible contaminants remain major barriers. The findings underscore the importance of integrated interventions that combine regular water quality monitoring, community education on poor water quality related risks, and the provision of accessible, safe water solutions. Culturally sensitive and context-specific interventions- developed through meaningful community engagement can foster real behaviour change. Combining regular water testing, public disclosure of water quality results, and accessible treatment technologies may provide a robust framework to close the perception–measured gap and ensure sustainable and safe water access in rural regions of West Bengal.
