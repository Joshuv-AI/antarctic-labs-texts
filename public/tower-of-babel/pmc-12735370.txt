Predictive Ability of the Indian Diabetes Risk Score in the Evaluation of Diabetes Risk among Urban Adults of Raipur: A Cross-sectional Study
Authors: Ekta Krishna, Anjali Pal, Abhiruchi Galhotra, Arvind Shukla, Madhusudan Prasad Singh, Vijay Kumar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background:The Indian Diabetes Risk Score (IDRS) is a cost-effective and noninvasive screening tool that plays an important role in the early detection and risk-stratification of diabetes, and thus immediate referrals to health centers for further assessment and management of the individuals.Methods:The study involved 422 urban adults, aged 30 and older, who came under the field practice area of a tertiary hospital. Using a prevalidated questionnaire, data on sociodemographics, anthropometrics, physical measurement (like blood pressure), biochemical measurement (random blood sugar), and IDRS components were obtained. Univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses were performed to identify the predictors. The cut-off for the risk score for undiagnosed diabetes risk assessment was evaluated using the area under the curve (AUC) and the receiver-operating characteristic (ROC).Results:The average age of participants in this study is 46.1 years (±11.8). Out of the total participants, 50.7% were females, while 49.3% were males. According to IDRS, 20.3% of participants were found to be at high risk of diabetes, 56.2% at moderate risk, and 23.5% at low risk. Among the factors that significantly predicted increased diabetes risk according to IDRS were age, high salt consumption, female gender, and higher body mass index. With a 0.68 AUC, the IDRS exhibited an 86.6% sensitivity at a cut-off value of ≥30 and a 28.1% specificity. The study observed maximum sensitivity (60.6%) and specificity (69.5%) at a cut-off of 45, as further shown by evaluating Youden’s index (30.1%).Conclusions:With optimal performance at a cut-off of ≥45, the IDRS reasonably identified 76.5% of urban adults with moderate-to-high risk for diabetes. Integration of routine IDRS screening in primary healthcare in resource-limited settings, along with counseling on lifestyle modification, can increase its utility in risk stratification and referral.

INTRODUCTION

India currently has 90 million diagnosed cases of diabetes.[1] About half of adult diabetics in India are not even aware that they have this disease, according to the International Diabetes Federation, 2025 factsheet. India is the country with the second-highest number of diabetes cases after China.[2] If not well controlled, this progressive condition can cause substantial cardiovascular problems, retinopathy, nephropathy, and neuropathy. A study showed that screening two individuals with prediabetes and six with undiagnosed diabetes is necessary to identify one case, therefore, stressing the requirement for large resources.[3] Early diagnosis using cost-effective screening tools is crucial for managing the diabetes burden in India.

Noncommunicable diseases (NCDs) can be predicted using a variety of risk assessment techniques, such as the Framingham Risk Score, WHO/ISH risk charts, Community-Based Assessment Checklist, Indian Diabetes Risk Score (IDRS), and Finnish Diabetes Risk Score (FINDRISC). When compared with other methods, an analysis by Gupta et al.[4] showed that IDRS had a high sensitivity and specificity. Viswanathan Mohan developed the IDRS in 2005. It consists of four risk factor questionnaires, two of which are changeable (waist circumference and physical activity), and two of which are nonmodifiable (age and family history of diabetes). It has a specificity of 60.1% and a sensitivity of 72.5% when using a cut-off score of ≥60.[5] A survey in Kerala using IDRS found that 59.1% of participants scored over 60. Previous studies highlight the low prevalence of diabetes in those scoring below 60.[67]

Based on this, the objective of this study was to assess the prevalence of diabetes risk (low, moderate, and high) among adults living in urban Raipur using the Indian Diabetes Risk Score (IDRS) as well as to find out the predictors of high diabetes risk. Finally, to evaluate the diagnostic validity of the IDRS tool by analyzing its sensitivity, specificity, and discriminative ability.

METHODS AND MATERIALS

Study design, setting, and population

Over two months, from December 1, 2020, to January 31, 2021, a descriptive community-based cross-sectional study was conducted in the field practice area of a tertiary hospital in Raipur city. This area, comprising two wards, has an estimated population of around 26,525, divided into 17 smaller zones. Participants in the investigation were healthy people aged 30 and older who had resided in the area for at least six months before the study and gave their written consent. Participants with known diabetes and hypertension were excluded from this study to prioritize undiagnosed diabetes risk assessment in priority. This existing medical condition was verified through self-reports and, wherever possible, by going through medical records, with participants’ consent. Apart from this, those participants who were pregnant, acutely ill, or unable to understand the questionnaire had been excluded.

Sample size and sampling procedure

The sample size was computed using the IDRS high diabetes risk derived from an analysis by Muthuvelraj et al.[8] Therefore, the prevalence of 35.2% with a 95% confidence interval (CI) and a precision of 5% was used to calculate the sample size on Statulator, a web-based software (https://statulator.com/), to determine the sample size, which came out to be at least 350. A final sample size of 385 had been established after accounting for a 10% nonresponse rate. Simple random sampling was used for collecting 422 samples over two months. Rotating a bottle helped us to decide the starting street; subsequently, every other house was chosen. House members were chosen using the Kish selection grid.[9]

Data collection procedure and materials

A structured, prevalidated questionnaire was employed for data collection through face-to-face interviews. The questionnaire included the following sections: sociodemographic details; anthropometrics and physical measurements (weight, waist circumference, and blood pressure); biochemical measurements, the random blood sugar (RBS); and lastly, the IDRS scale, which comprises age, physical activity, abdominal obesity, as well as family history. IDRS ranges from 0 to 100, categorizing risk levels for diabetes, where a score of less than 30 indicates “low risk” for diabetes. Similarly, a score ranging between 30 and 59 indicates “medium risk” of diabetes, while a score of more than 60 indicates “high risk” of having diabetes. Anthropometric measurements, such as weight and waist circumference, were taken using an analog weighing machine and measuring tape. Physical measurement, like blood pressure, was measured using a standardized aneroid sphygmomanometer and a Littmann Classic II S.E. stethoscope. Biochemical measurement of random blood sugar (RBS) is done using a glucometer.

Statistical analysis

The cleaned and coded data on Excel were analyzed using Jamovi version 2.3.28 (www.jamovi.org/download.html). Mean as well as standard deviation employed for quantitative variables (like age), percentages for categorical variables like religion, occupation, and so on, were among the descriptive statistics. Univariate logistic regression identified variables with a P-value ≤0.2, which were subsequently included in multivariate logistic regression to determine adjusted odds ratios at 95% CIs for predictors of moderate-to-high diabetes risk score according to IDRS. Significance tests for all variables were conducted at a P-value threshold of <0.05 for multivariate analysis. A receiver-operating characteristic (ROC) curve is used to assess the discriminative ability of the IDRS. The criteria used in the study to evaluate discriminative ability are as follows: An area under the curve (AUC) of ≥0.7 indicates good discriminative ability, an AUC between 0.6 and 0.7 signifies satisfactory discriminative ability, and an AUC of less than 0.6 reflects unsatisfactory discriminative ability. Sensitivity, specificity, and Youden’s index were calculated to evaluate optimal cut-offs. Chi-square tests were used to check the associations between categorical variables (e.g., age, physical activity) and diabetes risk categories.

Ethical approval

The Institute Ethics Committee, AIIMS, Raipur, granted ethical clearance for this investigation with reference number 749 IEC-AIIMSRPR/2019, dated September 23, 2019. Written informed permission was obtained before the individuals were enrolled in the research. Throughout the research investigation, the information’s confidentiality was guaranteed.

RESULTS

The study comprised 422 participants from AIIMS Raipur’s urban field practice region who were at least 30 years old. The study participants’ mean age was 44.2 ± 11.9 years. Most participants (61.4%) were between the ages of 30 and 45; 88.6% were married; and 51.9% were part of extended families. Most people belonged to the Other Backwards Caste (OBC) (78.4%) and the Hindu religion (96.7%). Regarding socio-economic status, 57.1% belonged to the upper lower class, and 21.6% were in the lower middle class. The participant’s body mass index (BMI) showed that 41% were overweight (BMI >25 kg/m2). About 23.2% of participants noted hypertension. The prevalence of tobacco use and alcohol consumption was 57.1% and 17.8%, respectively [Table 1].

Risk of diabetes according to the IDRS of the participants

The IDRS of the participants indicates that 20.3% of them displayed high-risk scores of obtaining diabetes, 56.2% showed medium-risk scores, and 23.5% showed low-risk scores [Table 2]. The majority of participants (65.8%) under 35 years had a low risk of acquiring diabetes, while only 1.8% had a high risk, which was found statistically significant (P < 0.001). For all people aged 50 years or older, the risk of diabetes was found to be medium to high; this result was statistically significant (P < 0.001). Likewise, a substantial (P < 0.001) association was observed between the IDRS and a larger waist circumference. It was observed that 44.7% of individuals had a high risk of acquiring diabetes if their waist circumference was greater than 90 cm for females or 100 cm for males. However, it was discovered that the risk of diabetes was inversely related to physical activity levels, with 45.5% of people engaging in sedentary activities and 47.2% of those engaging in mild physical activity being at high risk of diabetes. This relationship was statistically significant (P < 0.001). Furthermore, this study found no significant association (P = 0.222) between a person’s chance of developing diabetes and their family history of the disease. However, compared to having no family history or only one parent having diabetes, having both parents with the disease seems to raise the risk.

Sensitivity and specificity analysis of IDRS at different cut-off values

In this study, the AUC for the IDRS was 0.68 (95% CI: 0.629–0.742), which suggests moderate discriminative ability. This means that the IDRS is somewhat effective in correctly classifying individuals at risk of diabetes, although there is room for improvement [Figure 1].

Different cut-offs of the Indian diabetic risk score for diagnosis of type 2 diabetes mellitus

The IDRS’s performance is further elucidated by examining the sensitivity as well as specificity values at different cut-off points [Table 4]. At a cut-off value of 30, the sensitivity was 86.6%, indicating a high proportion of true positives identified by the IDRS. At this cut-off, the negative predictive value was 82.5% along with the positive predictive value, which was 34.9%. However, the specificity was lower at 28.1%, indicating a relatively high false-positive rate, where individuals without diabetes risk were also classified as at risk by the IDRS.

DISCUSSION

A community-based cross-sectional study was conducted using the IDRS scale among urban adults aged 30 years and older to evaluate diabetes risk. Overall, this study showed that 75.6% of individuals exhibited a medium-to-high risk of acquiring diabetes. Specifically, 23.5% of study participants were found to have low risk, 56.2% had moderate risk, and 20.3% were identified as having a high diabetes risk score. In a nationwide study, 40.9% of people were categorized as high risk and 29.7% as moderate risk.[10] Similarly, a study conducted in Chandigarh by Dandona et al.[11] found that 47.8% of individuals exhibited moderate risk, while 48.7% demonstrated high risk. In a survey conducted by Arun et al.[12] in Lucknow, 67% were classified as moderate risk, 14.9% as high risk, and 17.4% as low risk. All these nationwide comparison of results indicates a large proportion of adults in India have a moderate-to-high risk of developing diabetes. However, various diabetes risk categorization across different states indicates that there is an influence of local dietary factors, cultural differences, geographical influence, and differing methodological criteria for screening individuals for diabetes risk.

Our study has identified age, gender, and elevated BMI as significant predictors for high diabetes risk score according to IDRS classification. Increasing age increases the likelihood of developing diabetes as insulin sensitivity and pancreatic function tend to decline with age.[1314]

Our study showed that males have a significantly lower likelihood of being classified as high diabetes risk according to IDRS when compared with females. A study conducted by Dandona et al.[11] in Chandigarh also showed similar study findings using the IDRS scale. Existing research has proven that females are protected due to estrogen effects before their menopause, subcutaneous fat deposition, and lifestyle choices, while males have more risk due to visceral fat deposition and risky health behaviors like alcohol consumption, tobacco use, and so on.[15] These contradictory results could be explained by the fact that other characteristics like health-seeking behavior in males are more compared to females. Similarly, males are more physically active due to their occupation and high literacy. Further explanation for our above study findings is that males with higher BMI were disproportionately excluded due to the study’s exclusion criteria (e.g., known diabetics or hypertension), thus diluting the risk profiles of male participants.

Our study recorded 41% participants to be overweight and also found BMI to be a significant predictor for high diabetes risk. These study findings are in alignment with NFHS-5 data, where the prevalence rate for obesity and overweight was recorded as 44.02% and 41.16% for males and females, respectively. While NFHS-4 data showed values for males and females as 37.71% and 36.14%, respectively.[16] This rising trend in BMI in the Indian population, which is historically known for an undernourished status, indicates rapid urbanization and dietary shifts from traditional food, where sedentary lifestyles, processed, fried, and fatty food consumption have become a new trend, especially in urban areas. Apart from it, reduced physical activity has become increasingly common in our nation.[17] Thus, the higher BMI and body weight, particularly around the abdomen, lead to insulin resistance and metabolic syndromes.[1418] Adding to these findings, waist circumference also served as a stronger predictor of diabetes than BMI, which is included as one of the main components of the IDRS measurement scale for diabetes risk.[1920]

Our study also showed that the impact of physical activity on diabetes risk, where participants with low or sedentary activity levels were seen to have high diabetes risk, highlighting the need to encourage an active lifestyle as a way to prevent diabetes.[21] The low physical activity level among urban populations living in slums could be explained by low mobility space, narrow roads, traditional restrictions on women going outside, better transportation facilities for short distances, and so on adds to their decreased physical activity level, and thus increases their risk of NCDs like diabetes.[17]

The IDRS demonstrated moderate predictive ability (AUC—0.68) among urban adults, which further strengthens its utility as a cost-effective screening tool. However, its performance varies regionally—in our study, it has shown sensitivity of 86.6% and specificity of 28.1% at a cut-off ≥30, which differs markedly from the original validation study, where IDRS showed 72.5% sensitivity and 95.1% specificity at a cut-off of ≥ 60.[22] In a study by Bhadoria et al.,[23] a cut-off score of 40 indicated 60.4% sensitivity along with 70.7% specificity. Additionally, the sensitivity and specificity from nationwide surveys conducted in five regions of India utilizing the IDRS as a diabetes risk assessment tool gave varied results.[24] On the above basis, we can conclude that IDRS has a reasonable but not exceptional predictive capacity. Though this tool is a cost-effective tool for resource-limited settings, there is a need for more research to confirm the best cut-off values for the IDRS in different geographical regions and also to know the potential advantages of using population-specific or region-specific cut-offs for IDRS in diabetes risk assessment. These variations could be influenced by population-specific factors like abdominal obesity prevalence, variation in physical activity reporting, and unaccounted risks (e.g., tobacco use, alcohol, etc.). These observations are supported by studies conducted in other Indian states.[242526] Our study findings further highlight the requirement to fill the knowledge gap by doing more research on the inclusion of lifestyle factors to enhance the utility of the IDRS scale across diverse populations. Furthermore, future interventional studies that assess the effectiveness of lifestyle changes,[27] such as diet and exercise programs, in lowering diabetes risk among high-risk individuals would be very beneficial to understand this topic in depth.

However, there are a few limitations of our study that should be noted. The cross-sectional study design always comes with a universal limitation the lack of establishment of temporality. This study is limited to the urban population. Better results could have been expected if this study had included the urban and rural populations. Social desirability bias also limits the generalizability of study findings.

This study has several strengths, which including its large sample size, inclusion of RBS testing, and findings supportive toward a region-specific cut-off for IDRS. Additionally, individuals who were identified as having a moderate risk of developing diabetes received appropriate counseling to encourage lifestyle changes aimed at reducing their risk. Those who scored 60 or higher were referred to an urban health center for proper diabetes management.

CONCLUSION

There is a high prevalence of diabetes risk (76.5% moderate-to-high risk) among urban adults in Raipur city, with age, female gender, more salt consumption, and elevated BMI emerging as significant predictors for high diabetes risk. So, it will be a pivotal step to include IDRS as a screening tool in resource-limited settings along with targeted counseling for lifestyle modification. In this study, IDRS demonstrated moderate utility for diabetes risk stratification, performing optimally at a cut-off ≥45, implying further research to evaluate IDRS against other tools such as FINDRISC and investigation of region-specific cut-offs to improve its utility in diverse groups.
