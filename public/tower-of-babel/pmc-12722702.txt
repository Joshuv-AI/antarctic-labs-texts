Rifaximin improves anxiety- and depression-like behaviors in rats by regulating NPY in the colon and mPFC
Authors: Qian Luo, Haonan Li, Yihe Wang, Dexiang Liu, Cyrus S. H. Ho
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Growing evidence implicates the brain-gut axis in depression pathogenesis, though the underlying mechanisms remain elusive. This study investigated the antidepressant potential of rifaximin, a non-absorbable antibiotic, and its mechanisms via the brain-gut axis in a rat chronic unpredictable mild stress (CUMS) model. We found that CUMS induced anxiety- and depression-like behaviors, impaired colonic endocrine cell function, and downregulated neuropeptide Y (NPY) expression in both the colon and medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC). CUMS also altered neuronal activation and disrupted key neurotransmitter (GABA, Glu, 5-HT) balance in the mPFC. Rifaximin treatment ameliorated these behavioral deficits, restored colonic endocrine function, and increased NPY levels in both the colon and mPFC. Furthermore, it normalized CUMS-induced alterations in neuronal activation and neurotransmitter balance. Crucially, functional knockdown of NPY in the mPFC not only reduced colonic NPY expression in control rats but also abolished the anxiolytic effects of rifaximin in CUMS-treated rats. In summary, this study suggests that in the CUMS model, rifaximin can play an anxiolytic and antidepressant effect, and its mechanism may be related to the rifaximin’s regulation on NPY mediated gut-brain axis between colon and mPFC.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-27826-9.

Introduction

Major depressive disorder (MDD) is the most common serious mental illness characterized by depressed mood or markedly diminished interest1. As a prevalent mental disorder and a major contributor to the global burden of disease, MDD affects approximately 350 million people worldwide2. Because of its great social value and economic importance, researchers around the world have been trying to find out the pathophysiological mechanism of MDD. After decades of research, researchers have proposed several potential mechanistic hypotheses for depression, including the neurotransmitter hypothesis3, the neural circuit hypothesis4,5, and the pro-inflammatory cytokine hypothesis6. However, these hypotheses cannot fully explain the pathogenesis of depression. In recent years, the brain-gut axis hypothesis has provided new possibilities for the exploration and treatment of the pathogenesis of depression.

The gut of mammals is a microbial organ, 90–95% of the total cells of which are microorganisms, and the metabolism, immune system, and signal transmission are all closely related with microbiota. Thus, the gut and gut microbiota can serve as a whole to respond to and influence other organs7. The brain-gut axis is the bidirectional message transformation pathway between brain and gut in mammals. Enteroendocrine cells (EECs) in the gastrointestinal tract release more than 20 signaling molecules that have important endocrine and metabolic functions and connect the brain and gut through several pathways including central nervous system, endocrine chemical signal system, immune regulation, and metabolic effects8,9. Among these molecules, neuropeptide Y (NPY) is a key neuropeptide secreted by EECs that plays a protective role in stress-related mental and behavioral abnormalities10,11. Over the past decade, accumulating evidence has linked the brain-gut axis to the pathophysiology of depression12,13. Depressed patients often have gut dysfunction, such as appetite disturbances, metabolic disturbances, functional gastrointestinal disorders, and gut microbiota abnormalities. However, the exact molecular mechanism involved is still largely unknown.

Rifaximin is a non-absorbable antibiotic that has a high safety profile due to its low systemic absorption14. Rifaximin has important antibacterial and anti-inflammatory effects in the colon, including reducing bacterial virulence15 and enhancing intestinal mucosal stability16. In addition, rifaximin appears to have unique prebiotic properties that promote the growth of beneficial bacteria such as Bifidobacteria and Lactobacilli. Our previous studies have shown that rifaximin can exert antidepressant effects by regulating short-chain fatty acids produced by intestinal flora, but whether rifaximin has a regulatory effect on the intestinal endocrine system is still unclear. Therefore, this study aims to further explore whether rifaximin participates in the anti-anxiety and anti-depressive effects through the EECs-NPY pathway, independent of short-chain fatty acids.

Therefore, we hypothesize that rifaximin alleviates anxiety- and depression-like behaviors in CUMS rats via the NPY-mediated gut-brain axis between the colon and the mPFC.

Materials and methods

Animals

Male Sprague Dawley rats (3 weeks old, 50 ± 2 g) were purchased from the experimental animal center of Shandong university (Jinan, China). All experimental protocols were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Shandong University (Approval number: ECSBMSSDU2022-2–83) and followed the guidelines of the National Institutes of Health guidelines for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and the ARRIVE guidelines (https://arriveguidelines.org). The sample size was chosen based on common practice established in prior publications17–19. All rats were housed under standard environmental conditions (22 ± 0.5℃, 50%±5% humidity, and a 12 h light/12 h dark cycle) and maintained with free access to a standard laboratory pellet diet and water. The rats were habituated to their new environment for a week, and changes in their sucrose preference before and after drug administration were recorded. The sucrose preferences in the first week were used as the baseline. After their acclimatization, the rats were divided randomly into different groups. The rats were 4 weeks old (puberty) at the start of the experiment, as this age is a critical period for the development of the prefrontal cortex, hippocampus and other emotional regulation centers in the brain, as well as the HPA axis20–24. It is particularly sensitive to stress exposure, which enables this study to more effectively simulate the interference of stress on neural development and thereby lead to the pathological process of depression.

Chronic unpredictable mild stress procedure

The CUMS procedure was carried out as previously described25. Rats in the CUMS and CUMS + R groups were randomly exposed to different stressors: cage tilting for 24 h, cold swimming for 3 min (at 0 ℃), water or food deprivation for 24 h, level shaking for 15 min, tail nip for 1 min (1 cm from the end of the tail), 45 °C heat stress for 5 min and inversion of the light/dark cycle for 24 h. These stressors were applied for 28 days, during which each stressor was applied 4 times. The rats were exposed to different stressors at random every day, making it impossible for the animals to predict the stimulus. The same stressor was not applied on consecutive days. The CON and CON + R group were undisturbed except for necessary procedures. All stressors were strictly determined and validated through a series of pilot studies. Throughout the procedure, rats were monitored closely for any immediate signs of excessive distress.

Drugs

Rifaximin was purchased from Yuanye Bio-Technology Co. Ltd (Shanghai, China). Rifaximin was mixed with 0.9% saline in high-speed vortex state. During the 4 weeks of CUMS procedures, rats in CUMS + R, CON + R, CUMS + R + IgG and CUMS + R + Ab groups were treated with rifaximin (150 mg/kg) by oral gavage once daily (at 16:00) for 4 weeks. The doses of rifaximin were chosen based on a previous study26. Rats in CON and CUMS groups were treated with 0.9% saline in the same volume at the same time. The procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Committee, Shandong University.

Behavioral tests

Sucrose preference test

The sucrose preference test (SPT) was conducted to assess anhedonia, a behavioral indicator reflecting diminished interest in rewarding stimuli. Prior to testing, baseline measurements were obtained during a habituation phase, wherein rats were acclimated to individual cages and provided with continuous access to two bottles, one containing a 1% sucrose solution and the other containing plain water, for 12 h. To ensure standardized conditions, rats were subjected to 12 h of food and water deprivation before the test session. During the formal assessment, rats were individually housed and allowed free choice between two bottles: one filled with 200 mL of 1% sucrose solution and the other with 200 mL of purified water, over a 12 h period. To mitigate potential side bias, the positions of the bottles were alternated after the first 6 h. Fluid consumption was quantified by measuring the pre- and post-test bottle weights. The sucrose preference value was calculated as follows: Sucrose intake (g)×100%/[sucrose intake (g) + water intake (g)].

Open field test

The open field test (OFT) was employed to evaluate exploratory behavior and anxiety-like responses in rats using an open-field device. The apparatus consisted of a square acrylic chamber (150 × 150 × 50 cm) with a floor subdivided into a 5 × 5 grid. Each rat was individually positioned at the center of the arena and permitted unrestricted exploration for a 5-min session. Locomotor activity (quantified by the number of grid crossings, defined as all four paws entering a new square), rearing frequency (both forelimbs elevated simultaneously), and time spent in the central zone were automatically recorded using video-tracking software (SMART 2.5, Spain). To minimize olfactory cues between trials, the apparatus was thoroughly cleaned with 75% ethanol prior to each test session.

Elevated plus maze

The elevated plus maze (EPM) apparatus consisted of four intersecting arms arranged in a cruciform configuration: two opposing open arms (50 × 10 cm) without walls and two enclosed arms (50 × 10 × 40 cm) with high side walls. Animal behavior was recorded using an overhead digital camera interfaced with automated video tracking software (SMART 2.5, Spain). For testing, each rat was carefully transferred from its home cage and positioned in the central platform (10 × 10 cm) facing an open arm. Behavioral parameters were automatically recorded for 5 min using the tracking system. Primary measures included: (1) total arm entries (sum of open and closed arm entries), reflecting general locomotor activity; and (2) anxiety indices calculated as both the percentage of open arm entries [open arm entries/(open + closed arm entries) × 100%] and percentage of time spent in open arms [open arm time/(open + closed arm time) × 100%]. Before each behavioral session, the device was sprayed with 75% ethanol to eliminate residual odors.

Standardized anesthesia and euthanasia

The rats were euthanized the next day after behavioral testing. Briefly, rats were deeply anesthetized with 10% pentobarbital (10 mL/kg; Merck KGaA, P3761) by intraperitoneal injection and euthanized by slowly perfused with 300 mL of phosphate buffer saline (PBS; Servicebio, G4202), followed by 300 mL of 4% paraformaldehyde (Servicebio, G1101). The brain and colon were then removed for subsequent detection.

Immunofluorescent analysis

After removement, the brain and colon tissues were immobilized in 4% paraformaldehyde for 72 h and dehydrated in 30% sucrose for 72 h, embedded in optimal cutting temperature compound, and sectioned into 10-µm-thick sections for staining. Tissues on slides were treated with 0.2% Triton X-100 for 10 min and blocked with 5% goat serum for 1 h at room temperature. The slide was incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies: Chromogranin B polyclonal antibody (CgB, Proteintech, 14968-1-AP, 1:200), CPE polyclonal antibody (Proteintech, 13710-1-AP, 1:200), Neuronal nuclei (E4M5P) mouse mAb (NeuN, Cell Signaling Technology, #94403, 1:200) and NPY polyclonal antibody (Proteintech, 12833-1-AP, 1:200). After the primary antibodies were rinsed with PBS, the tissues were covered with Alexa Fluor® 488- and Alexa Fluor® 594-conjugated fluorescence secondary antibodies and incubated in the dark at 37 °C for 60 min. After rinsing the secondary antibodies with PBS, 8 µL DAPI solution was added on the slides and were incubated in the dark for 3–5 min. The slides were observed with an Olympus BX53 fluorescence microscope equipped with a DP74 Microscope Digital Camera. Quantitative analysis of the immunofluorescence images was performed using ImageJ software. The number of EECs, CgB+ cells, CPE+ cells, and MFI of c-Fos, NPY1R, NPY were measured. For each animal, three non-overlapping fields of view from the mPFC or colon were randomly selected and captured at 10× or 20× magnification. All quantifications were performed by an investigator blinded to the experimental groups.

Neurotransmitter analysis

The concentrations of neurotransmitters in the colon and brain were analyzed using a Liquid chromatography/electrospray ionization-tandem mass spectrometry (LC/ESI-MS/MS) system as our previously described27. The stable isotope-labeled internal standards were used for quantification. mPFC were rapidly dissected on a pre-chilled plate according to the boundaries defined in the rat brain atlas of Paxinos and Watson. After dissection, tissues were immediately snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C until analysis to prevent post-mortem degradation of neurotransmitters. Weigh 50 mg of the sample into a 2 mL EP tube, accurately add 600 µL of 10% formic acid in methanol solution, add 50 mg of glass beads, put tubes into a high-throughput tissue grinder, grind at 55 Hz for 1 min, and then centrifuge at 12,000 rpm at 4 °C for 5 min. Take 100 µL of the supernatant, add 100 µL of the dual-isotope internal standard, vortex for 30 s, filter the supernatant with a 0.22 μm membrane, and the filtrate were used for LC-MS analysis.

The analysis conditions were as follows: HPLC column, Waters ACQUITY UPLC® BEH C18 column (100 mm × 2.1 mm i.d.; particle size, 1.8 μm); solvent system, water with 0.1% formic acid (A), acetonitrile with 0.1% formic acid (B); solvent gradients-10% B (0–1 min), 95% B (1–8 min), 95% B (8–9.5 min), finally 10% B (9.6–12 min); flow rate, 0.35 mL/min; temperature, 40 °C; injection volume, 2 µL.

The levels of neurotransmitters were detected using Metware (http://www.metware.cn/), the AB Sciex API 3000 system. The sample extracts were analysed with an AB Sciex API 3000 System, equipped with an ESI Turbo Ion-Spray interface, operating in positive and negative ion modes and controlled using the Analyst 1.6 software (AB Sciex). The key operating parameters were: Ion Spray Voltage: 5000 V; Source Temperature: 500 °C; Curtain Gas: 30 psi; Ion Source Gases 1 and 2: 50 psi. Data were acquired in Multiple Reaction Monitoring mode.

Stereotactic injection

Rats were anesthesia with 10% pentobarbital (10 mL/kg; Merck KGaA, P3761) before injection. Following cessation of movement, rats were fixed in prone position and NPY antibody (Ab) (12833-1-AP, Proteintech, China) or IgG were injected into the medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC) bilaterally with 1 µL per side using a 10-µL syringe. mPFC injections were targeted to: AP: −3.5 mm, ML: ±0.5 mm, and DV: −4 mm (relative to Bregma). The injection rate was 1 µL/10 min, and the needle was pulled out relaxedly after about 5 min of drug absorption. After both injections were complete, rats were placed on a warming pad until they regained normal color and resumed movement.

Enzyme‑linked immunosorbent assay

The concentrations of NPY in the colon and brain tissue were measured with validated specific enzyme‑linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) assays according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Elabscience, Wuhan, China). Each sample (100 µL) was added to ELISA plates in triplicate. A reference standard was used to establish the working curve, and the biotinylated antibody, HRP conjugate solution, substrate reagent and terminal solution were successively added according to the instructions. The NPY levels were measured by a microplate reader at 450 nm absorbance.

Western blot analysis

Total protein from colon and mPFC tissues were acquired using mixture of RIPA Lysis Buffer, PMSF Solution and Phosphatase inhibitor (mixing ratio: 100:1:1). The concentration of protein was determined using BCA protein assay (CoWin Biosciences, China). Each sample was loaded with 10 µg protein for Western blot. After separated, proteins were then transferred to 0.45 μm PVDF transfer membranes (Millipore, USA). The membranes were blocked with 10% skim milk for 1 h and incubated with primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight (NPY, 1:1000, 12833-1-AP, Proteintech, China). On the second day, anti-rabbit secondary antibodies were incubated at room temperature for 1 h. Finally, the membranes were covered with ECL luminol reagent and imaged using Chemiluminescence imaging system (Tanon-5200, Shanghai, China).

Statistical analysis

All the data in this paper are tested for normality using Shapiro-Wilk test. All data were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Two-tailed Student’s t-test was used for the comparison between two groups of normal distribution data. To determine the differences between various groups, two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was utilized. For multiple comparisons between various groups, one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post-hoc test was used for normal distribution data and Kruskal-Wallis test was used for non-normal distribution data. Pearson analysis was utilized to investigate correlations between different parameters and pearson correlation analysis was validated for linear relationships using scatter plots. Statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics 26.0 software. Statistical graphs are generated using GraphPad Prism 8 (GraphPad Software, USA). The analysis results were only further evaluated when a significant difference was observed. p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Rifaximin ameliorated CUMS-induced depressive and anxious behaviors

Schematic diagram of the experimental design was shown in Fig. 1A. The SPT was used to validate the CUMS model. Low sucrose ingestion reflects impaired reward responses and anhedonia. We found that CUMS-treated rats showed significantly decreased sucrose consumption (Fig. 1B; p = 0.004), and this effect could be prevented by rifaximin treatment. OFT was used to measure the spontaneous exploratory behavior in a novel environment. The results showed that the CUMS treatment led to a significant reduction in the total frequency of crossing (Fig. 1C; p<0.001), rearing (Fig. 1D; p<0.001) and the time spent in the central region (Fig. 1E; p = 0.004) compared with CON group, while rifaximin treated rats did not show a decrease in spontaneous movements. The elevated plus maze (EPM) was used to evaluate the anxiety response of rats. The results showed that the open arm time (Fig. 1F; p = 0.005) and open arm entrances (Fig. 1G; p = 0.009) of CUMS rats was significantly reduced, indicating that the rats were in a state of anxiety, while rifaximin treatment could alleviate the anxiety-like behavior caused by CUMS.

Rifaximin regulates the function of EECs and NPY expression in the colon

To evaluate the effect of rifaximin on EECs, we measured the number and the activation of EECs. CgB and CPE were used as marker of EECs. We found that the number of EECs were significantly decreased in the colon of CUMS rats (Fig. 2A and B, p = 0.042; Fig. 2C, p < 0.001), while rifaximin treatment could alleviate the negative effect on EECs (Fig. 2B, p = 0.023; Fig. 2C, p = 0.007). We also found that there was no significant difference in EECs number between the CON and CON + R group, indicating that rifaximin had no significant negative effect on EECs number. Next, we measured the activation of EECs by the expression of CPE, which was an exopeptidase crucial for the function of neuroendocrine cells by processing and sorting of many neuropeptides. The results of immunofluorescent analysis showed that Rifaximin led to increased CPE expression in EECs compared with CUMS group (Fig. 2D, p = 0.005), which suggests an enhanced processing and secretion of neuropeptides. Further, we measured the expression of NPY, which was the main production of EECs in colon (Fig. 2E). The NPY were decreased in CUMS treated rats compared with CON group (Fig. 2E, p < 0.001), while the expression of NPY in CUMS + R group were higher than CUMS group (Fig. 2E, p < 0.001). These results suggested that rifaximin protected against the EECs dysfunction caused by CUMS. This, in turn, may contribute to the observed reduced anxiety-like behavior in CUMS + R group.

Rifaximin regulates the excitability of mPFC neurons

Emerging evidence indicates that the gut-brain-axis plays a crucial role in many neuropsychiatric disorders28. And structural and functional changes induced by stress in the mPFC have been correlated with emotional disturbances in human and rodent29. We detected the neuronal activity in mPFC by measuring the expression of immediate early gene c-Fos. We subjected rats to immunohistochemistry for c-Fos in mPFC after CUMS treatment.

Colocalization of c-Fos and NEUN suggested c-Fos is mainly enriched in neurons nucleus. We found that CUMS and rifaximin did not have significantly effect on the number of prefrontal neurons, but CUMS could increase c-Fos expression in the mPFC (Fig. 3A-B, p < 0.001), which suggested the neurons were activated by chronic stress. And the increased c-Fos+ neurons were primarily distributed in the prelimbic cortex (PrL) subregion of the mPFC, which is closely related to anxiety and depression regulation29. On one hand, the PrL facilitates anxiety-related responses through its projections to the basolateral amygdala and the bed nucleus of the stria terminalis30. On the other hand, it contributes to the development of depressive states by integrating emotional and contextual information from brain regions such as the amygdala and hippocampus31.

Then we measured the NPY expression in mPFC and found that rifaximin prevented CUMS-induced NPY reduction in mPFC, which was similar to colonic NPY results (Fig. 3C, p < 0.001). NPY is active both in the periphery and brain, and it can cross the blood-brain barrier. To examine whether there was an interaction between colonic endocrine cell-derived NPY and prefrontal NPY, we used correlation analysis to analyze the interrelation of NPY expression in colon and brain (Fig. 3D, p < 0.001). The results show that colonic endocrine cell-derived NPY was positively correlated with the level of NPY in the mPFC.

NPY closely related to neural activity. Using pearson analysis, we found that the expression of c-Fos was negatively correlated with the level of NPY in the mPFC (Fig. 3E, p < 0.001), which suggested that the regulating effect of rifaximin on the excitability of mPFC neurons may be related to NPY. These results suggested that rifaximin may participate in the regulation of prefrontal neuron through regulating EECs’ function in colon.

Rifaximin regulates neurotransmitter expression in the mPFC

Given the established roles of multiple neurotransmitters in anxiety and depression, such as 5HT, dopamine and γ-Aminobutyric acid (GABA)32–34. We profiled the levels of key neurotransmitters in the mPFC, including GABA, L-Glutamine (Gln), L-Glutamic acid (Glu), Histamine (His), Serotonin hydrochloride (5HT) and Acetylcholine (ACH) in the mPFC. We found that CUMS treatment significantly decreased the expression of GABA (Fig. 4A, p = 0.012), Gln (Fig. 4B, p = 0.006) and 5HT (Fig. 4C, p = 0.001) in the mPFC, while rifaximin prevent the situation. We also found that rifaximin prevents Glu (Fig. 4D, p = 0.009) elevations caused by CUMS. Meanwhile, the results showed that rifaximin treatment upregulates GABA expression in the mPFC, while both CUMS and rifaximin treatment do not have significant effect on His and ACH (Fig. 4E and F). Overall, these results indicated that CUMS triggered disturbances in homeostasis of neurotransmitter systems, resulting impaired neurotransmission in the brain leads to anxiety and depression disorders in rats. And the application of rifaximin could reverse the homeostasis of neurotransmitter systems.

The robust anti-anxiety-like behavior observed in the OFT and EPM test (Fig. 1) may be mechanistically explained by the concomitant changes in key neurotransmitter systems, specifically, the significant elevation of GABA, Gln and 5HT levels, and reduce of Glu in the mPFC in rats.

Rifaximin alleviates anxiety-like behavior caused by CUMS through NPY

NPY has an impact on stress-related changes in emotional-affective behavior and stress coping. Therefore, we speculated that the anxiolytic and antidepressant effect of rifaximin may be related to the regulation of NPY in the mPFC. The results of correlation analysis showed that the expression of NPY in the mPFC was positively correlated with the crossing number, time spent in the central region and open arm entrances (Fig. 5A-C, p < 0.001). We further examined the expression of NPY1 receptor, which exerts anxiolytic effects by combining with NPY in the mPFC (Fig. 5D-E), the results showed that CUMS and rifaximin did not have significant effect on NPY1 receptor. These results indicated that rifaximin may protect against anxiety-like behavior by regulating prefrontal NPY.

In order to further explore the connection between NPY levels in the colon and mPFC, we injected NPY Ab into mPFC in Control rat to functionally knockdown NPY through competitive inhibition. At 3 days after injection, the brain and colon tissues were collected to detect the NPY level by immunofluorescence staining (Fig. 6A). First, to verify the efficacy of the central blockade, we measured NPY protein levels in the mPFC. We found that the NPY expression in mPFC were found to be decreased by approximately 92% in NPY Ab group compared with IgG group (Fig. 6B-C, p < 0.001), confirming successful local neutralization. Meanwhile, NPY knockdown in mPFC could also decreased NPY expression in colon by 73% (Fig. 6B-C, p < 0.001). Likewise, we observed that NPY Ab inhibited NPY protein in mPFC and colon by Western blot (Fig. 6D).

To test the role of NPY signaling in the treatment of CUMS with rifaximin more accurately, we investigated whether NPY in mPFC was a necessary and/or sufficient condition for rifaximin to improve the behaviors related to CUMS (Fig. 7A). The neutralizing antibodies were injected into mPFC in CUMS + R rats to block the effect of NPY, with IgG used as the negative control. The immunofluorescence results showed that the injection of NPY Antibody (Ab) successfully inhibited the expression of NPY in mPFC (Fig. 7B). Moreover, the results of the behavioral tests showed a clear reduction in the anxiety-like behavior of the CUMS + R rat that were injected with NPY Ab. This was indicated by a significant reduction in the total frequency of crossing (Fig. 7C; p = 0.015), time spent in the central region (Fig. 7D; p = 0.025), percent of time spent in the open arms (Fig. 7E; p = 0.006) and percent of entrances in the open arms (Fig. 7F; p = 0.014). These behavioral results infer that rifaximin improves the CUMS-related behavioral deficits by regulating the level of NPY in the mPFC.

These findings indicate that NPY-mediated brain-gut axis participated in rifaximin’s anxiolytic and antidepressant effects.

Discussion

In this study, we used chronic unpredictable stress to establish a depression model in rats to explore the mechanism of anti-depressant and anti-anxiety effects of rifaximin. We found that rifaximin can reverse the decrease in the number and activity of intestinal endocrine cells caused by CUMS and increase the NPY expression in the colon and the mPFC. NPY-mediated brain-gut axis may be one of the anxiolytic and antidepressant mechanisms of rifaximin.

Accumulating evidence suggests that the brain-gut axis may be involved in the pathogenesis and regulation of neurological and psychiatric disorders35,36. Although the gut microbiota is crucial to maintaining the homeostasis of the host, some important sensory and regulatory cells in the host should not be ignored. EECs, present throughout the gut epithelium, are key regulators of coordinated brain-gut-microbiota axis communication. On the one hand, EECs sense luminal microbial changes through microbial metabolites. On the other hand, they communicate with the host system through neuroendocrine molecules37. Therefore, EEC is considered to play an important role in neurological and psychiatric diseases. Rifaximin is a non-absorbable antibiotic possessing significant eubiotic properties. Rifaximin treatment enriches beneficial probiotics (e.g., Bifidobacteria, Lactobacilli) and modulates the gut microbiota composition38. Furthermore, oral rifaximin administration was reported to ameliorate chronic stress-induced intestinal inflammation and visceral hyperalgesia in rats by elevating the relative abundance of Lactobacillus species through ileal microbial population shifts. In our results, we found that CUMS treatment can reduce the number of EEC cells in the colon and decrease NPY in colon, which may be related to the decrease of NPY precursors processing enzyme CPE39. And we found that rifaximin could increase the expression of CPE after CUMS treatment. The effect of rifaximin on EEC cells may stem from its ability to reshape the intestinal microbial community and alter the pattern of microbial-derived signals.

Our behavioral tests found that rats exhibited depressive and anxious behavior after CUMS treatment, specifically manifested as decreased spontaneous movement and decreased exploratory behavior. Studies have shown that the prefrontal cortex exists in neural circuits related to depression and anxiety40. mPFC is exquisitely sensitive to stressful manipulations, a large body of literature has shown that stress states influence the activity of PFC neurons41,42. We found that total number estimation of c-Fos positive cells, indicating the neuronal activation upon stressful challenge, significantly increased in CUMS-treated rats compared with rifaximin groups. Holmes et al. also found that stress increases the expression of neural activity markers c-Fos43, which is consistent with our findings. However, there’s also study reported global PFC suppression in CUMS model in C57BL/6 mice44. This might be due to the differences in animal species, highlighting the importance of model selection when interpreting data related to stress-induced neuronal activity.

Neurotransmitters are endogenous signaling molecules released by neurons, facilitating the transmission of nerve impulses across synaptic clefts to ensure precise communication within neural circuits. This process enables effector organs to execute specialized physiological responses. Neurons secrete distinct neurotransmitter subtypes, which have been systematically categorized into several major systems, including cholinergic, glutamatergic, GABAergic, dopaminergic, serotonergic, histaminergic, and aminergic pathways45. Each of these systems plays a critical and unique role in regulating nervous system activity, thereby maintaining essential physiological processes and modulating behavioral functions. Stress can trigger disturbances in homeostasis of neurotransmitter systems, and resulting in impaired neurotransmission in the brain leads to different neurological disorders46. In this study, we found that CUMS induced GABA, Gln and 5HT decrease, and Glu increase in mPFC in rats, suggesting a disturbance in homeostasis of neurotransmitter systems. It is well-established that enhanced serotonergic transmission in the mPFC is associated with reduced anxiety47. Furthermore, GABA, as the primary inhibitory neurotransmitter, plays a crucial role in dampening neuronal excitability within circuits that process depression and anxiety48. And rifaximin could reverse these changes and restore neurotransmitter homeostasis in CUNS rats in this study. Therefore, we propose that rifaximin treatment alleviates anxiety-like behavior by potentiating both serotonergic and GABAergic neurotransmission in the mPFC, leading to a net inhibition of the stress-response circuitry.

NPY is the most abundant and widely distributed neuropeptide in the human brain, and has been shown to have a significant impact on brain activity49. Growing evidences supports a role for NPY as a protective neurochemical that mediates stress resilience50. Clinical investigations have revealed that the plasma and cerebrospinal fluid of depressed individuals contain decreased concentrations of NPY compared to healthy controls51,52. NPY deficiency is associated with an anxiogenic phenotype in rodents53, and highly anxious rats are more sensitive to the anxiolytic actions of NPY54. Our findings demonstrate a coordinated dysregulation of NPY in both the colon and mPFC following CUMS, which was reversed by rifaximin. This suggests the existence of a bidirectional NPY-mediated gut-brain circuit. On one hand, the brain-to-colon pathway likely involves stress-induced suppression of central NPY, which may lead to HPA axis hyperactivity55. The resultant increase in glucocorticoids can directly inhibit the synthesis and secretion of NPY from colonic enteroendocrine cells, contributing to gut dysfunction. On the other hand, the colon-to-brain pathway may operate via colonic NPY influencing the brain through vagal afferent signaling and by modulating local immune and barrier function56. A deficit in colonic NPY could impair these signaling routes, thereby failing to provide crucial homeostatic feedback to the brain and exacerbating anxiety-like states. The ability of rifaximin to concurrently restore NPY levels in colon and mPFC highlights its role in rectifying this bidirectional communication. Furthermore, our experimental data showing that mPFC NPY knockdown reduces colonic NPY provides direct causal evidence for the top-down arm of this axis. Thus, we propose that the anxiolytic effect of rifaximin is mediated, at least in part, by its action on this NPY-dependent gut-brain loop.

It is prudent to point out the limitations in this study. First, the study was conducted on male rats, ignoring the influence of gender in depression. Further studies are needed to explore the role of gender in the pathogenesis of depression. Further, behavioral assessments only detected anxiety/depressive-like behavior and did not rule out rifaximin’s effects on other behaviors (such as social interaction), making it impossible to fully attribute improvements specifically to anxiety and depression. Thirdly, changes in the expression of other NPY receptors (such as Y2R and Y5R) were not assessed, leaving the possibility that NPY may exert effects through non-Y1R pathways.

In conclusion, CUMS can cause depressive and anxious behaviors in rats, impair the function of intestinal endocrine cells, and lead to increased nerve excitability and decreased NPY content in the mPFC. Rifaximin can prevent anxiety- and depression-like behaviors caused by CUMS, and the mechanism may be related to the regulation of rifaximin on intestinal endocrine cell function and NPY secretion in the colon and the mPFC.
