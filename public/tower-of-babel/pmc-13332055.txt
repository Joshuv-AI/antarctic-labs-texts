Optimizing phosphorus and potassium fertilization enhances potato (Solanum tuberosum L.) yield and nutrient status in northwestern Ethiopia
Authors: Yohannes Gelaye, Dereje Ademe, Melkamu Alemayehu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPotato (Solanum tuberosum L.) productivity in northwestern Ethiopia is limited by poor soil fertility and nutrient imbalances, particularly deficiencies of phosphorus and potassium in Nitisols, resulting in suboptimal yield and tuber quality. This study aimed to evaluate the combined effects of different phosphorus and potassium fertilizer rates on potato yield, yield components, and nutrient accumulation under Nitisol conditions. A field experiment was conducted using three phosphorus rates (0, 34.5, and 69 kg P₂O₅ ha⁻¹) and four potassium rates (0, 100, 200, and 300 kg K₂O ha⁻¹) arranged in a factorial randomized complete block design with three replications. Yield components, total and marketable yields, and plant tissue phosphorus and potassium contents were measured and analyzed using analysis of variance. The interaction between phosphorus and potassium significantly affected major yield components, including marketable tuber number per hill, marketable tuber weight, marketable yield, and total tuber yield. The highest marketable tuber number (11.80) was obtained from 69 kg P₂O₅ ha⁻¹ combined with 300 kg K₂O ha⁻¹, whereas the maximum marketable yield (48.32 t ha⁻¹) and total tuber yield (49.14 t ha⁻¹) were achieved with 34.5 kg P₂O₅ ha⁻¹ combined with 200 kg K₂O ha⁻¹. Fertilizer application also enhanced phosphorus and potassium concentrations in both shoot and tuber tissues. The combined application of 34.5 kg P₂O₅ ha⁻¹ and 200 kg K₂O ha⁻¹ is recommended to improve potato productivity in Nitisols. Future studies should focus on multi-season validation, and integrated nutrient management strategies.Keywords: Potato nutrient content, Plant tissue analysis, Quality parameters, Yield componentsSubject terms: Physiology, Plant sciences

Introduction

Potato (Solanum tuberosum L.) is a globally important food crop that contributes to food security, income generation, and nutritional diversity, particularly in developing regions1. Owing to its high yield potential, short growth cycle, and superior nutritional composition, potato plays a pivotal role in strengthening global food and nutritional security2. Its agronomic flexibility and rapid biomass accumulation make it particularly valuable in developing regions, where it contributes to food availability, dietary diversification, and income generation under land- and resource-constrained conditions3. In sub-Saharan Africa, potato cultivation has expanded markedly in highland agro-ecosystems, where it enhances household income, livelihood resilience, and farming system sustainability. Despite its importance, potato productivity in Ethiopia remains far below its potential due largely to poor soil fertility and inadequate nutrient management. National tuber production has been projected at 921,403.9 t from 66,423.33 ha, with an average yield of only 13.76 t ha⁻¹ far lower than the 38 t ha⁻¹ typically achieved under experimental conditions. Similar yield gaps are evident at regional and zonal scales. In the Amhara regional state, potato production was estimated at 279,790.25 t from 18,185.40 ha, with a mean yield of 15 t ha⁻¹. Within the East Gojjam zone, production reached 65,080.09 t from 4,356.70 ha, corresponding to a productivity level of 14.4 t ha⁻¹4. These figures indicate a persistent disparity between attainable and realized yields, highlighting the need for improved soil fertility and nutrient management strategies.

Historically, fertilizer research and recommendations for potato production in Ethiopia have largely focused on nitrogen (N) and phosphorus (P), often applied using blanket recommendations irrespective of soil variability, crop demand, or agro-ecological context5,6.

Soil acidity limits potato productivity by reducing nutrient availability, disrupting uptake processes, and increasing toxic element solubility7. Moreover, soil pH-mediated interactions strongly influence the availability and efficiency of both phosphorus and potassium, further exacerbating yield limitations under suboptimal fertilization regimes8,9. Phosphorus supports energy transfer and root development, while potassium regulates physiological processes such as enzyme activation, osmotic balance, and assimilate transport. However, potassium nutrition has received comparatively little attention in Ethiopian potato production systems.

Compounding these challenges, most fertilizer recommendation frameworks do not adequately account for inherent soil physical and chemical properties or long-term soil fertility dynamics10. The study area, located in the Choke Mountains an important water tower of East Africa is characterized by steep topography, heavy rainfall, severe soil erosion, nutrient depletion, and declining soil organic matter11,12. Nutrient losses in East Africa, particularly in Ethiopia, have been estimated at 47–88 kg ha⁻¹ year on average, reaching up to 100 kg ha⁻¹ year on hilltops13. These losses are driven by a combination of landslides, overgrazing, soil attrition, and unsustainable land-use practices14. Under such conditions, reliance on cereal-based cropping systems and uniform fertilizer recommendations further accelerates soil degradation and yield stagnation.

Given the magnitude of nutrient depletion and the critical role of balanced fertilization, there is an urgent need to reassess fertilizer management strategies for potato production in Ethiopia. In particular, optimizing phosphorus and potassium inputs based on soil type and crop response is essential to enhance nutrient use efficiency, close yield gaps, and improve the sustainability of potato-based farming systems. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the response of potato to varying levels of phosphorus and potassium fertilization in the Nitisols of Northwestern Ethiopia, with the broader goal of generating site-specific evidence to inform improved fertilizer recommendations and future soil fertility research.

Materials and methods

Study area and experimental conditions

The field experiment was conducted during the 2018 main cropping season in the East Gojjam Zone of northwestern Ethiopia under rainfed conditions. The experimental site is geographically situated at 10°21′ N latitude and 37°43′ E longitude, at an elevation of approximately 2400 m above sea level. This highland agro-ecological zone is characterized by a unimodal rainfall pattern, with a mean annual precipitation of about 1300 mm, and a cool sub-humid climate with a mean annual temperature of 18.3 °C.

Soil characteristics

The experimental field is dominated by dark red clay soils classified as Nitisols, which are widespread in the Ethiopian highlands and known for their strong acidity and advanced weathering. Prior to planting, composite soil samples were collected from the surface layer (0–20 cm) and subjected to standard laboratory analyses to characterize baseline soil fertility status. The soil exhibited a moderately acidic reaction, with a pH of 5.5. Chemical analyses revealed generally low nutrient availability, with total nitrogen content of 0.09%, available phosphorus of 8.30 mg kg⁻¹, and exchangeable potassium of 0.135 mol kg⁻¹. The experimental soil was a moderately acidic, nutrient-deficient Nitisol typical of intensively cultivated Ethiopian highlands.

Experimental treatments, design, and procedures

Planting material

The potato cultivar ‘Belete’ was used as the experimental planting material. It was obtained from the Kimir Dingay Potato Marketing Center, Farta District, South Gondar Zone, Ethiopia. This cultivar is widely adapted to diverse agro-ecological conditions in Ethiopia and is recognized for its stable yield performance. ‘Belete’ was released in 2009 by the Holeta Agricultural Research Center, Ethiopia, and reaches physiological maturity within 110–120 days after planting, making it suitable for the main cropping season of the study area15,16.

Fertilizer sources and application procedures

Triple superphosphate (TSP) and potassium chloride were used as the respective sources of phosphorus and potassium. Phosphorus fertilizer was applied entirely at planting to ensure adequate availability during early root development and canopy establishment. Potassium fertilizer was applied in two splits, with half applied at planting and the remaining half applied 45 days after planting, in order to synchronize nutrient availability with periods of peak crop demand (Fig. 1).

Fertilization, management, harvesting, and traits measurement.

Nitrogen was applied uniformly across all treatments to eliminate confounding effects, with urea (46% N) supplied at 100 kg ha⁻¹ in two split doses half at planting and the rest top-dressed at 30 days after planting during the first earthing-up to ensure synchronized nutrient availability and maximize use efficiency.

Experimental treatments and design

TSP was applied at three rates: 0, 34.5, and 69 kg ha⁻¹, while potassium chloride (KCl) was applied at four rates: 0, 100, 200, and 300 kg ha⁻¹. Treatment levels were determined based on the nutrient contents of the respective fertilizers, assuming 46% P₂O₅ in TSP and 60% K₂O in KCl. These conversion factors were applied consistently throughout the study to ensure accurate nutrient rate calculations (Table 1).

Details of the treatment combination.

The experiment was laid out in a 3 × 4 factorial arrangement within a randomized complete block design, with three replications. Individual plots had a gross area of 11.25 m² (3.75 m × 3.00 m) and comprised five rows, each containing ten plants. Planting geometry followed recommended agronomic practices, with inter-row and intra-row spacings of 0.75 m and 0.30 m, respectively. Buffer zones of 0.50 m between plots and 1.00 m between blocks were maintained to minimize edge effects and treatment interference.

Crop management practices

All plots received uniform agronomic management throughout the growing season. Standard cultural practices, including weeding, cultivation, and ridging, were implemented in accordance with the recommendations of the Horticulture Division of the Amhara Agricultural Research Institute. Late blight (Phytophthora infestans), a major constraint to potato production in the region, was effectively managed (Fig. 1) through the application of Ridomil Gold® at 7-day intervals17.

Soil sampling and laboratory analysis

A representative composite soil sample was collected prior to planting from the experimental field at a depth of 0–30 cm using an auger (Fig. 2), following the diagonal (X) sampling method18. This approach ensured adequate spatial representation of the experimental area. Collected samples were air-dried under ambient laboratory conditions, gently ground, and passed through a 2 mm sieve to remove coarse fragments and obtain a uniform soil fraction for chemical analysis19. Standard analytical procedures were employed to determine baseline soil nutrient status. Total nitrogen was quantified using the Kjeldahl digestion and distillation method, available phosphorus was determined using the Olsen procedure, and exchangeable potassium was analyzed using the Morgan method. All laboratory analyses were conducted at the Debre Markos Soil and Plant Laboratory in accordance with established protocols20.

Soil sample collection and preparation.

Plant tissue sampling and nutrient analysis

Plant tissue sampling was conducted to assess phosphorus and potassium uptake in both vegetative and storage organs. For shoot analysis, approximately 200 g of healthy, non-necrotic tissue comprising leaves and stems was randomly collected from the net plot area of each treatment. Samples were composited per treatment and oven-dried at 65 °C until a constant weight was achieved to ensure moisture removal and sample stability.

At physiological maturity, tubers of all size classes were harvested from each plot. Tuber samples were composited according to treatment, and representative subsamples were thoroughly washed to remove adhering soil particles. The cleaned tubers were thinly sliced using a sharp knife and oven-dried at 70 °C until a constant weight was attained.

Dried shoot and tuber samples were subsequently submitted to the Debre Markos Soil and Plant Laboratory for nutrient analysis. Phosphorus and potassium concentrations in plant tissues were determined using the Olsen and Morgan analytical methods, respectively20.

Data collection

Yield and quality-related data were collected from the net plot area to ensure representative and unbiased measurements. Data collection was restricted to plants located in the central rows of each plot, while border rows were excluded to minimize edge and border effects that could confound treatment responses21.

Yield parameters

The following yield-related parameters were recorded to evaluate the effects of phosphorus and potassium fertilization on potato productivity. All measurements were obtained from five randomly selected plants within the net plot area, and mean values were used for statistical analysis.

Number of marketable tubers per hill (number): Marketable tubers were defined as those that were free from disease, mechanically uninjured, and had an individual tuber weight greater than 25 g22. The number of such tubers per hill was determined by direct counting.

Number of unmarketable tubers per hill (number): Unmarketable tubers included those that were diseased, mechanically injured, or weighed less than 25 g. These tubers were counted separately to quantify yield losses attributable to quality constraints.

Total number of tubers per hill (number): The total number of tubers per hill was calculated as the sum of marketable and unmarketable tubers.

Marketable tuber yield per hill (kg): Marketable tuber yield was determined by weighing all disease-free, mechanically uninjured tubers exceeding 25 g harvested from each selected hill22.

Unmarketable tuber yield per hill (kg): Unmarketable tuber yield was recorded as the combined weight of diseased, mechanically injured, and undersized tubers (< 25 g) per hill.

Total tuber yield per hill (kg): Total tuber yield was calculated as the sum of marketable and unmarketable tuber weights per hill.

Harvest index (%): The harvest index was calculated as the ratio of economic yield (tuber yield) to total biological yield, expressed as a percentage, using the standard formula described by 23.

Yield and quality assessment

All yield and quality parameters were recorded at physiological maturity, defined as the stage at which approximately 75% of the foliage had senesced7.

Harvesting and sampling were conducted from the net plot area to ensure representative yield estimation.

Marketable tuber yield (t ha-¹): Marketable yield comprised tubers that were free from disease, mechanically uninjured, and weighed more than 25 g. The cumulative weight of such tubers was recorded for each plot and subsequently converted to a hectare basis for analysis22,24.

Unmarketable tuber yield (t ha-¹): Unmarketable yield included tubers that were diseased, mechanically injured, or weighed less than 25 g. These tubers were weighed separately, and yields were expressed on a hectare basis.

Total tuber yield (t ha-¹): Total tuber yield was calculated as the sum of marketable and unmarketable tuber yields per plot and expressed on a hectare basis prior to statistical analysis.

Quality indicators

Tuber dry matter content (%): Dry matter content was determined using representative tuber samples of all size classes. Tubers were thoroughly washed to remove adhering soil, weighed to obtain fresh weight, thinly sliced, and oven-dried at 72 °C until a constant weight was achieved. Tuber dry matter percentage was calculated using the standard formula described by 25.

Tuber quality parameters

Tuber specific gravity (g cm-³): Tuber specific gravity was estimated using established dry matter–specific gravity conversion relationships. Based on the standard conversion approach, a potato tuber with a dry matter content of 21.21% corresponds to a specific gravity of 1.080 g cm⁻³22. Dry matter values obtained for each treatment were converted accordingly and used for subsequent quality analyses.

Total starch content (%): Total starch content was calculated as a function of tuber specific gravity using the following empirical relationship.

Starch (%) = 17.546 + 199.07× (specific gravity − 1.0988).

This equation was applied consistently across treatments to estimate starch concentration.

Total soluble solids (°Bx): Total soluble solids were determined using juice extracted from representative tubers sampled from each plot. Tubers were crushed using a mortar and pestle, and the resulting pulp was filtered through multiple layers of clean cloth to obtain clear juice24. The filtrate was placed on the prism of a calibrated refractometer, and total soluble solids were recorded in degrees Brix (°Bx). Mean values per treatment were used for statistical analysis.

Data analysis

All crop data were subjected to analysis of variance (ANOVA) using the Generalized Linear Model (GLM) procedure of the Statistical Analysis System (SAS), version 9.2. Treatment effects were considered statistically significant based on F-tests, and when significant differences were detected, treatment means were separated using the least significant difference (LSD) test at the 5% and 1% probability levels.

Number of unmarketable tubers per hill

Both phosphorus and potassium significantly affected unmarketable tuber number per hill at the 1% probability level (P ≤ 0.01) (Table 2). In addition, the interaction between phosphorus and potassium showed a highly significant effect at the 1% probability level (P ≤ 0.01) (Table 2). Among the treatment combinations, the highest number of unmarketable tubers per hill (1.40) was observed under the application of 34.5 kg P₂O₅ combined with 200 kg K₂O ha⁻¹. In contrast, the lowest mean value (0.37) was recorded under 0 kg P₂O₅ combined with 300 kg K₂O ha⁻¹.

This minimum value did not differ significantly from those obtained under 0 kg P₂O₅ combined with 0, 100, or 200 kg K₂O ha⁻¹; 34.5 kg P₂O₅ combined with 0–300 kg K₂O ha⁻¹; or 69 kg P₂O₅ combined with 100–300 kg K₂O ha⁻¹ (Table 3). These findings describe that specific nutrient combinations, rather than individual nutrient inputs, govern the formation of unmarketable tubers under the prevailing soil conditions.

Total number of tubers per hill

Total tuber number per hill was significantly influenced by phosphorus at the 1% probability level (P ≤ 0.01) (Table 2), while potassium alone did not have a significant effect. However, the interaction between phosphorus and potassium significantly affected total tuber number per hill at the 5% probability level (P ≤ 0.05) (Table 2). The highest total tuber number per hill (12.60) was achieved with the combined application of 69 kg P₂O₅ and 300 kg K₂O ha⁻¹, demonstrating a clear synergistic response. Conversely, the lowest total tuber number per hill (7.66) was recorded in the unfertilized control.

This lowest value was statistically comparable to those obtained from treatments receiving 0 kg P₂O₅ combined with 200–300 kg K₂O ha⁻¹; 34.5 kg P₂O₅ combined with 0, 100, or 300 kg K₂O ha⁻¹; and 69 kg P₂O₅ combined with 100 kg K₂O ha⁻¹ (Table 3). Overall, the results emphasize the importance of balanced phosphorus and potassium supply in maximizing tuber set under Nitisol conditions.

Marketable tuber weight per hill

Phosphorus and potassium both significantly affected marketable tuber weight per hill at the 1% probability level (P ≤ 0.01) (Table 2). The interaction between phosphorus and potassium also significantly influenced this parameter at the 5% probability level (P ≤ 0.05) (Table 2). Among the treatment combinations, the highest mean marketable tuber weight per hill (2.50 kg) was obtained from the application of 34.5 kg P₂O₅ combined with 200 kg K₂O ha⁻¹.

In contrast, the lowest marketable tuber weight per hill (1.03 kg) was recorded in the unfertilized control. This value did not differ significantly from those obtained under 0 kg P₂O₅ combined with 100, 200, or 300 kg K₂O ha⁻¹, as well as from 34.5 kg P₂O₅ combined with 0 kg K₂O ha⁻¹ (Table 3). These results indicate that moderate phosphorus supply combined with adequate potassium availability optimizes marketable tuber biomass under the study conditions.

Unmarketable tuber weight per hill

Unmarketable tuber weight per hill was significantly affected by potassium at the 1% probability level (P ≤ 0.01) (Table 2), whereas phosphorus alone did not show a significant effect. However, the interaction between phosphorus and potassium significantly influenced unmarketable tuber weight per hill at the 1% probability level (P ≤ 0.01) (Table 2). The maximum unmarketable tuber weight per hill (0.0366 kg) was recorded under the combined application of 69 kg P₂O₅ and 200 kg K₂O ha⁻¹.

This value was statistically comparable to that obtained from 34.5 kg P₂O₅ combined with 200 kg K₂O ha⁻¹. Conversely, the lowest unmarketable tuber weight per hill was observed under 69 kg P₂O₅ combined with 300 kg K₂O ha⁻¹, which was statistically similar to the values recorded under 34.5 kg P₂O₅ combined with 0–100 kg K₂O ha⁻¹ (Table 3). These findings suggest that excessive or imbalanced nutrient combinations may increase the proportion of unmarketable biomass, emphasizing the importance of optimized fertilizer management.

Total tuber weight per hill

Total tuber weight per hill was not significantly affected by the interaction between phosphorus and potassium fertilization; rather, the main effects (p < 0.05) of the individual nutrients significantly influenced it (Table 2). The highest mean total tuber weight per hill (2.53 kg) was recorded under the combined application of 34.5 kg P₂O₅ and 200 kg K₂O ha⁻¹.

In contrast, the lowest mean total tuber weight per hill (1.05 kg) was obtained from the unfertilized control. This value was statistically comparable to those observed under 0 kg P₂O₅ combined with 100, 200, or 300 kg K₂O ha⁻¹, as well as under 34.5 kg P₂O₅ applied without potassium fertilizer (Table 3).

Relative to the control treatment, the combined application of 34.5 kg P₂O₅ and 200 kg K₂O ha⁻¹ increased mean total tuber weight per hill by approximately 1.48-fold, highlighting the yield-enhancing effect of balanced phosphorus and potassium nutrition under Nitisol conditions.

Marketable tuber yield

Marketable tuber yield was highly significantly influenced by both the main effects and the interaction between phosphorus and potassium fertilization (p < 0.001) (Table 2). The highest marketable yield (48.32 t ha⁻¹) was obtained from the combined application of 34.5 kg P₂O₅ and 200 kg K₂O ha⁻¹. This yield was statistically comparable to that achieved under the application of 69 kg P₂O₅ combined with 200 kg K₂O ha⁻¹.

In contrast, the lowest marketable yield (20.47 t ha⁻¹) was recorded from the treatment receiving 34.5 kg P₂O₅ without potassium fertilizer. This value did not differ significantly from those obtained under 0 kg P₂O₅ at all potassium application levels, as well as from the treatment receiving 69 kg P₂O₅ without potassium fertilizer (Table 3). These results emphasize that potassium availability is a critical determinant of marketable yield response to phosphorus fertilization under the studied soil conditions.

Unmarketable tuber yield

Unmarketable tuber yield showed a highly significant response to phosphorus and potassium fertilization, being affected by both their individual contributions and their combined interaction (p < 0.001) (Table 2). The highest unmarketable yield (0.82 t ha⁻¹) was observed under the combined application of 34.5 kg P₂O₅ and 200 kg K₂O ha⁻¹.

This value was statistically similar to those obtained from treatments receiving 34.5 kg P₂O₅ combined with 300 kg K₂O ha⁻¹ and 69 kg P₂O₅ combined with 200 kg K₂O ha⁻¹. Conversely, the lowest unmarketable yield (0.34 t ha⁻¹) was recorded from the application of 34.5 kg P₂O₅ without potassium fertilizer, and this value did not differ significantly from those obtained under 0 kg P₂O₅ at all potassium levels or under 69 kg P₂O₅ without potassium fertilizer (Table 3).

Total tuber yield

Total tuber yield was highly significantly influenced by both the main effects and the interaction between phosphorus and potassium fertilization (p < 0.001) (Table 2). The highest total tuber yield (49.14 t ha⁻¹) was obtained from the combined application of 34.5 kg P₂O₅ and 200 kg K₂O ha⁻¹. This yield was statistically comparable to those achieved under 34.5 kg P₂O₅ combined with 300 kg K₂O ha⁻¹ and under 69 kg P₂O₅ combined with 200 kg K₂O ha⁻¹. In contrast, the lowest total tuber yield (20.81 t ha⁻¹) was recorded from the application of 34.5 kg P₂O₅ without potassium fertilizer. This value did not differ significantly from those obtained under 0 kg P₂O₅ across all potassium levels or under 69 kg P₂O₅ applied without potassium fertilizer (Table 3).

Relative to the lowest-yielding treatment, the combined application of 34.5 kg P₂O₅ and 200 kg K₂O ha⁻¹ increased total tuber yield by approximately 28.33 t ha⁻¹.

Harvest index

The harvest index was significantly influenced by the main effect of phosphorus fertilization (p < 0.05), whereas the effects of potassium fertilization and the phosphorus × potassium interaction were not statistically significant (Table 2). The highest harvest index value (0.884) was recorded under the application of 34.5 kg P₂O₅ ha⁻¹, which did not differ significantly from that obtained with 69 kg P₂O₅ ha⁻¹.

In contrast, the lowest harvest index (0.834) was recorded in the unfertilized control treatment (Table 4). These findings indicate that phosphorus availability plays a critical role in promoting biomass partitioning toward tuber production, independent of potassium supply, under the prevailing soil and environmental conditions.

Tuber dry matter content

Neither the interaction between phosphorus and potassium nor the main effects of the individual nutrients significantly influenced tuber dry matter content (p > 0.05) (Table 2). This illustrates that variations in phosphorus and potassium fertilization within the tested ranges did not alter tuber dry matter accumulation under the prevailing soil and environmental conditions.

Tuber specific gravity

Tuber specific gravity was significantly influenced by the main effect of potassium fertilization (p < 0.05), whereas neither the main effect of phosphorus nor the phosphorus × potassium interaction was statistically significant (Table 2). The highest specific gravity (1.1165 g cm⁻³) was recorded under the application of 200 kg K₂O ha⁻¹.

This value did not differ significantly from those obtained under the unfertilized control or the application of 300 kg K₂O ha⁻¹. Conversely, the lowest specific gravity (1.1067 g cm⁻³) was observed at 100 kg K₂O ha⁻¹, which was also statistically comparable to the control and 300 kg K₂O ha⁻¹ treatments (Table 4). These findings suggest a moderate potassium supply optimizes tuber density-related quality attributes in Nitisol soils.

Total starch content

Total starch content was significantly influenced by the main effect of potassium fertilization (p < 0.05), whereas neither the main effect of phosphorus nor the phosphorus × potassium interaction was statistically significant (Table 2). The highest starch content (21.10%) was recorded under the application of 200 kg K₂O ha⁻¹.

This value did not differ significantly from that obtained under the unfertilized potassium treatment (0 kg K₂O ha⁻¹). In contrast, the lowest starch content (19.15%) was observed at 100 kg K₂O ha⁻¹, which was statistically comparable to the values recorded under 0 and 300 kg K₂O ha⁻¹ (Table 4). These results indicate that moderate potassium supply optimizes starch accumulation in potato tubers under Nitisol conditions.

Total soluble solids

Total soluble solids were not significantly affected by phosphorus or potassium fertilization, either as individual main effects or through their interaction (Table 2). This indicates that variations in phosphorus and potassium application rates within the tested ranges did not influence soluble solid accumulation in potato tubers under the conditions of the present study.

Discussions

Phosphorus and potassium effects on potato yield components in Nitisols

Number of marketable tubers per hill

The observed response of marketable tuber number to the combined application of phosphorus and potassium can be attributed to the complementary physiological roles of these nutrients under nutrient-limited Nitisol conditions. Phosphorus and potassium jointly enhance canopy development, improve solar radiation interception, and stimulate key enzymatic processes involved in photosynthesis and assimilate partitioning. These effects collectively increase radiation use efficiency and promote favorable conditions for tuber initiation and development in potato crops grown on acidic, nutrient-deficient soils26. Phosphorus plays a critical role in energy transfer and meristematic activity, thereby supporting early stolon formation and tuber set, while potassium regulates enzymatic activation, osmotic balance, and translocation of photosynthates from source to sink tissues. The synergistic interaction between these nutrients is particularly important in Nitisols, where inherent nutrient limitations and soil acidity constrain crop growth and productivity. Consequently, balanced phosphorus and potassium supply can substantially enhance tuber initiation processes and increase the number of marketable tubers per hill.

Conversely, deficiencies in any of the primary macronutrients nitrogen, phosphorus, or potassium can severely restrict vegetative growth, disrupt physiological processes, and reduce tuber formation, potentially leading to stunted growth or complete crop failure under extreme conditions27. The present findings therefore illustrate the importance of integrated macronutrient management strategies to optimize potato yield components in acid soil environments.

Number of unmarketable tubers per hill

The variation in the number of unmarketable tubers per hill observed across fertilizer treatments can be explained by imbalances in nutrient supply resulting from both insufficient and excessive fertilizer application. Suboptimal fertilizer rates may stimulate tuber initiation without adequately supporting subsequent tuber bulking, leading to the formation of a higher proportion of undersized tubers. Conversely, excessive application of fertilizers can disrupt normal physiological processes, including assimilate partitioning and hormonal regulation, thereby increasing the incidence of unmarketable tubers28. Overapplication of macronutrients, particularly phosphorus and potassium, may interfere with tuber initiation and enlargement by promoting excessive vegetative growth at the expense of sink development. This imbalance reduces the efficiency of assimilate translocation to developing tubers and can result in a greater number of small, poorly developed tubers that fail to meet market standards29. Such responses highlight the importance of applying balanced and site-specific fertilizer rates to optimize tuber size distribution and minimize the proportion of unmarketable yield.

Total number of tubers per hill

The total number of tubers per hill is strongly influenced by the balance and availability of essential nutrients during the critical phases of tuber initiation and early bulking. Well-balanced fertilization has long been recognized as a key determinant of tuber set in potato, with both phosphorus and potassium playing central roles in regulating stolon development, assimilate supply, and sink establishment30. Phosphorus supports early root growth and energy transfer processes required for tuber initiation, while potassium enhances carbohydrate translocation and enzyme activity associated with tuber development.

In the present study, combined phosphorus and potassium application markedly increased total tuber number, indicating a synergistic interaction between these nutrients under Nitisol conditions. In particular, higher rates of phosphorus and potassium improved tuber initiation, setting, and bulking, thereby increasing both marketable and total tuber yields. Based on these responses, the combined application of 69 kg P₂O₅ and 300 kg K₂O ha⁻¹ emerged as an effective fertilization strategy for enhancing tuber number under the studied agro-ecological conditions.

These findings are consistent with previous reports demonstrating the importance of balanced phosphorus and potassium nutrition in maximizing tuber number and yield components in potato31. Together, the evidence reinforces the need for integrated nutrient management approaches that optimize nutrient supply during critical developmental stages to achieve sustained productivity gains.

Marketable tuber weight per hill

Marketable tuber weight per hill is largely governed by the efficiency of assimilate production and translocation from source tissues to developing tubers. Phosphorus and potassium play complementary roles in these processes, with phosphorus supporting energy metabolism and root function, and potassium facilitating carbohydrate synthesis, phloem loading, and translocation of photosynthates. Consequently, the combined application of phosphorus and potassium significantly enhanced tuber weight by improving nutrient uptake and sugar transport from leaves to tubers32,33.

Beyond their interactive effects, the individual application of phosphorus and potassium has also been shown to exert a strong influence on tuber weight. Adequate phosphorus availability promotes early plant establishment and efficient energy transfer, while potassium enhances enzymatic activity and osmoregulation, both of which contribute to increased tuber biomass accumulation34. Moreover, increased tuber weight in response to higher potassium supply may be attributed to improved vegetative growth, greater leaf area development, and enhanced photosynthetic capacity, which collectively increase assimilate availability for tuber bulking35. These findings highlight the importance of balanced phosphorus and potassium nutrition in maximizing marketable tuber weight, emphasize the role of potassium in sustaining vegetative vigor, and assimilate supply during critical tuber bulking stages.

Unmarketable tuber weight per hill

Increased unmarketable tuber weight per hill can be attributed not only to fertilizer mismanagement but also to deficiencies in overall crop husbandry. Inadequate site selection, improper land preparation, suboptimal planting practices, and poor field management can collectively impair tuber development and uniformity, thereby increasing the proportion of unmarketable tubers36. These effects are often exacerbated by interacting biotic, abiotic, and anthropogenic stressors, which together constrain physiological processes essential for optimal tuber growth and quality37. Nutrient mismanagement particularly the application of nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium beyond recommended rates has been shown to significantly increase the production of unmarketable tubers by disrupting normal growth regulation and assimilate partitioning38. Both excessive and insufficient macronutrient supply can induce nutrient imbalances that suppress tuber bulking, delay maturation, and reduce size uniformity. Furthermore, disproportionate application of major nutrients may antagonize the uptake of other essential elements, thereby limiting nutrient availability at critical growth stages and ultimately increasing the volume of unmarketable produce39.

Total tuber weight per hill

The observed increase in total tuber weight per hill can be attributed to incremental phosphorus and potassium fertilization up to an optimal threshold, beyond which these nutrients enhance plant growth and promote a greater supply of photosynthates for tuber development. Adequate phosphorus supports energy transfer and root activity, while potassium enhances photosynthetic efficiency and assimilate translocation, collectively facilitating successful crop growth and increased biomass allocation to tubers21,40.

However, contrasting reports state that the combined application of phosphorus and potassium may not always exert a significant interactive effect on total tuber weight per hill. In such cases, the individual (main) effects of these nutrients may still play a critical role by improving photosynthetic performance, activating key physiological and enzymatic functions, and regulating source–sink signaling pathways that govern tuber bulking7. These discrepancies revealed the strong influence of site-specific factors including soil fertility status, nutrient interactions, and environmental conditions on crop response, and they highlight the importance of context-specific fertilizer optimization rather than universal nutrient recommendations.

Marketable tuber yield

Optimizing phosphorus and potassium fertilization is central to achieving maximum marketable and total potato yield. Adequate supply of these nutrients enhances carbohydrate and protein accumulation in tubers, thereby improving both yield performance and quality attributes41. Balanced fertilization with nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium has been shown to exert a profound influence on nutrient composition, tuber development, and overall productivity of potato crops42. However, the magnitude and direction of potato yield responses to macronutrient application are highly context-dependent and vary with cultivar, soil physicochemical properties, topographic position, and agroecological conditions43. Both inorganic and organic sources of phosphorus and potassium can significantly influence potato yield by improving soil nutrient availability and plant nutrient uptake efficiency44,45. Numerous studies have emphasized that phosphorus and potassium nutrition markedly increases tuber diameter as well as marketable and total tuber yield when compared with unfertilized controls46.

Nevertheless, despite their complementary physiological roles, some reports confirm that phosphorus and potassium do not always exhibit a statistically significant interaction effect on marketable and total tuber yield, suggesting that their benefits may often arise primarily through independent rather than synergistic mechanisms47. These findings reinforce the importance of site-specific nutrient management strategies that account for soil fertility status and crop demand rather than relying solely on generalized fertilizer interaction models.

Unmarketable yield

Exceeding or falling below the recommended rates of phosphorus and potassium fertilization can increase the number and proportion of unmarketable tubers. Overapplication may disrupt tuber cellular organization, reduce size, and compromise weight, thereby elevating unmarketable tuber yield43. Beyond nutrient mismanagement, multiple factors including poor cultivar adaptation, suboptimal agronomic practices, inappropriate fertilization, and biotic stresses such as pests and diseases can collectively limit tuber development and quality, further contributing to increased numbers and yield of unmarketable tubers48. These findings show the importance of integrated nutrient and crop management strategies that optimize both yield quantity and tuber marketability.

Total tuber yield

Application of phosphorus and potassium at optimal rates exerts a significant positive effect on both marketable and total tuber yield. These nutrients enhance yield by promoting photosynthesis, phloem loading, assimilate translocation, and the synthesis of high-molecular-weight compounds within storage organs, thereby improving tuber development and biomass accumulation49.

In addition, adequate nutrient and moisture availability supports robust vegetative growth and facilitates optimal tuber set, which collectively influence overall tuber yield50. The observed yield increase with fertilizer application can also be attributed to expanded leaf area, which enhances photosynthate production, as well as increases in both tuber number and weight41. Importantly, the use of optimal fertilizer rates minimizes the proportion of unmarketable tubers, contributing to more economical and cost-effective production51,52. The significant phosphorus × potassium interaction observed in this study indicates a clear agronomic synergy between the two nutrients under Nitisol conditions. Phosphorus primarily enhances root development and tuber initiation; whereas potassium plays a critical role in photosynthesis, assimilate transport, and tuber bulking53. Consequently, the application of either nutrient alone cannot fully maximize potato yield because the physiological functions of one nutrient depend on the adequate supply of the other54. This finding highlights the importance of balanced P–K fertilization for improving nutrient use efficiency and achieving optimal potato productivity in Nitisol soils.

The findings of this study are consistent with several reports from Sub-Saharan Africa and comparable highland agro-ecologies, where balanced phosphorus and potassium fertilization significantly improved potato yield55,56. Similar synergistic responses to combined P and K application have been reported in Ethiopian Nitisols, Kenyan highland soils, and other tropical potato-growing regions, where nutrient deficiencies and soil acidity limit crop productivity57,58. These studies similarly attribute yield improvements to enhanced root development, efficient assimilate translocation, and improved tuber bulking under balanced nutrient supply. Therefore, the present results reinforce existing regional evidence that integrated P–K management is essential for sustainable potato production in nutrient-deficient tropical soils.

These results emphasize the critical role of precision nutrient management in achieving high yielding, high-quality potato production systems. Previous studies consistently show that potassium deficiency significantly lowers total tuber yield because potassium is essential for photosynthesis, carbohydrate transport, and overall plant growth59.

Harvest index

Phosphorus exerts a primary influence on the harvest index, reflecting the proportion of economic yield relative to total biological yield, because it is a fundamental component of nucleic acids and adenosine triphosphate (ATP), which drive energy-dependent physiological processes in potato60. Soil fertility is critical for achieving high-yielding, high-quality tubers, and effective nutrient management including the judicious use of biofertilizers directly impacts tuber grading and total yield61. Interestingly, reductions in harvest index with increasing nitrogen application are not necessarily associated with decreases in total root or tuber yield, highlighting that harvest index alone does not always correlate perfectly with overall productivity62. Compared to nitrogen and potassium, phosphorus accumulates to a relatively low extent in tubers, reflecting its preferential allocation to metabolic and structural functions in vegetative tissues63. These findings reveal the nuanced role of phosphorus in balancing source–sink dynamics and optimizing economic yield in potato production systems.

Responses of phosphorus and potassium on potato quality components in Nitisols

Dry matter content of tubers

Tuber dry matter content reflects the accumulation of starch, proteins, and mineral constituents, and is a key determinant of cooking quality and processing suitability in potatoes64. In the present study, neither the main effects of phosphorus and potassium nor their interaction significantly influenced tuber dry matter content46. Similarly, potassium application alone did not alter the percentage of dry matter65, and increasing phosphorus supply produced no significant changes in dry matter accumulation65. These results suggest that tuber dry matter content is governed by a complex interplay of environmental and agronomic factors, including intercepted solar radiation, soil temperature, moisture availability, and other cultural practices, which may mask the effects of macronutrient supplementation.

Contrary to these findings, several studies have reported that phosphorus fertilization can enhance tuber size and increase dry matter percentage by improving soil phosphorus availability and promoting assimilate partitioning to storage organs66. Additionally, varietal differences and soil characteristics can strongly influence dry matter accumulation, indicating that both genetic and edaphic factors interact with nutrient management to determine this quality trait67. Collectively, these observations highlight that while phosphorus and potassium are essential for growth and yield, their effect on tuber dry matter content may be context-specific and moderated by environmental and varietal factors. Many studies report that insufficient potassium reduces dry matter content in tubers, as potassium plays a key role in carbohydrate synthesis and storage68.

Specific gravity of tubers

Specific gravity is a critical quality parameter in potatoes, determining suitability for processing and culinary applications. Tubers with high specific gravity are preferred for frying, baking, mashing, and chipping, while low specific gravity potatoes are more suitable for boiling and canning22. In industrial processing, such as potato chip production, higher specific gravity is directly associated with greater product yield and reduced oil absorption, whereas low specific gravity compromises processing quality69. Specific gravity is also closely correlated with dry matter content, reflecting the concentration of starch and other solids within the tuber70.

In the present study, neither phosphorus nor potassium fertilization, nor their interaction, significantly affected tuber specific gravity71. These results suggest that, under the conditions of this experiment, tuber specific gravity is largely determined by inherent varietal traits, environmental factors, and physiological development, rather than by variations in macronutrient supply. The lack of response indicates that while phosphorus and potassium enhance overall growth and yield, their effect on processing-related quality traits such as specific gravity may be limited or context-dependent. Potassium-deficient plants tend to produce tubers with lower specific gravity, indicating reduced starch accumulation and poorer processing quality72.

Total starch content

Starch content is a key determinant of potato quality, particularly for processing and culinary applications. Optimal fertilization strategies, emphasizing lower nitrogen in combination with sufficient phosphorus and potassium, have been recommended to enhance tuber starch accumulation73. Moderate potassium application rates are generally associated with increased tuber starch content compared to low potassium treatments74. However, excessively high potassium levels may reduce starch and dry matter accumulation, likely due to nutrient imbalances that interfere with normal tuber metabolism75.

The chemical composition of potato tubers, including starch, can be enhanced with balanced nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium fertilization, highlighting the importance of integrated nutrient management for improving quality traits76. Nevertheless, environmental and growth conditions including light, temperature, and soil moisture exert a substantial influence on starch content, dry matter, and reducing sugar concentrations, often modulating the effects of fertilization77. Potassium, commonly applied as potassium chloride in potato production, affects several quality parameters beyond starch, including tuber size, specific gravity, susceptibility to blackspot bruising, post-cooking darkening, fry color, and storage performance78.

Starch constitutes the primary component of tuber dry matter in starchy cultivars. At elevated mineral fertilizer rates, however, some nutrients may accumulate in the soil without being fully absorbed by plants, disrupting mineral balance and reducing their effective availability for starch synthesis79.

Consequently, over-application of potassium can negatively affect both dry matter and starch content, underscoring the need for optimized, site-specific fertilizer management to achieve high-quality tubers80.

Total soluble solids

Although the present study did not detect significant changes in total soluble solids (TSS) in response to phosphorus or potassium fertilization, previous research has emphasized that potassium, alone or in combination with other nutrients, can significantly influence TSS, total acidity, and specific gravity in potato tubers81. Fertilized potatoes often exhibit higher TSS, greater specific gravity, and increased tuber firmness compared to unfertilized controls, reflecting improved physiological and biochemical status78. The accumulation of TSS in potato tubers is primarily determined by pectin content and the overall density of the storage organ, which in turn reflects the tuber’s ability to assimilate nutrients and convert sucrose into glucose and other soluble sugars82. While macronutrient supply is essential for supporting photosynthesis and carbohydrate translocation, the conversion of these assimilates into soluble solids appears to be influenced more strongly by intrinsic varietal traits, growth conditions, and post-harvest metabolism than by the levels of phosphorus and potassium applied in this study. These results suggest that optimizing tuber quality traits such as TSS may require integrated approaches that combine balanced fertilization with variety selection and environmental management.

Phosphorus content in potato shoots

Shoot phosphorus content is a critical indicator of nutrient status and overall plant health in potato crops. In this study, shoot phosphorus concentrations across all fertilized treatments ranged from 0.115 to 0.215%, which falls within the critical range of 0.1–0.5% required for optimal growth. Notably, the application of 69 kg P₂O₅ ha⁻¹ resulted in medium-level phosphorus accumulation in shoots, considered adequate for supporting crop development83. The observed increase in shoot phosphorus content with escalating phosphorus application reflects the plant’s capacity to uptake and assimilate available phosphorus from the soil. Phosphorus plays essential roles in energy transfer, nucleic acid synthesis, and enzymatic activation, all of which are critical for vegetative growth and tuber development. Categorically, shoot phosphorus is often classified as low (≈ 0.19%), medium (0.20–0.25%), high (0.26–0.28%), and very high (~ 0.28%)83, indicating that the fertilization rates applied in this study successfully elevated phosphorus levels to a range favorable for physiological function and yield potential. These findings emphasize the importance of phosphorus management in maintaining shoot nutrient sufficiency and optimizing potato productivity.

Phosphorus content in potato tubers

Phosphorus concentration in potato tubers is influenced by multiple factors, including tissue sampling methods, the type and age of sampled tissue, and varietal differences among cultivars84. Typically, tuber phosphorus content declines as the growing season progresses, reflecting the mobilization of phosphorus to support active vegetative growth and tuber bulking. For example, phosphorus levels can decrease from approximately 0.57% during early tuber development to around 0.16% at harvest85. This seasonal decline demonstrates the dynamic nature of nutrient partitioning within the plant, where phosphorus is preferentially allocated to metabolic processes, energy transfer, and enzymatic activities during critical growth stages. The observed tuber phosphorus concentrations indicate that adequate fertilization strategies are essential to maintain sufficient phosphorus availability throughout the growing season, thereby supporting both tuber development and final yield. These findings highlight the importance of timing and rate of phosphorus application in optimizing nutrient accumulation in storage organs.

Potassium concentrations in potato shoot tissue

Potassium is a key macronutrient that regulates numerous physiological processes in potato, including enzyme activation, osmoregulation, and assimilate translocation. The optimal potassium concentration in shoot tissue is generally considered to be between 2% and 5%. In this study, shoot potassium content exceeded the lower threshold in all treatments, including the control, which had a concentration of 2%, indicating that even without fertilization, baseline soil potassium supported minimal crop requirements. Overall, the observed values suggest that the potato crop exhibited low to medium potassium absorption under the experimental conditions86. For comparative categorization, shoot potassium contents are often classified as low (≈ 1.25%), medium (1.25–1.5%), high (1.5–1.65%), and very high (> 1.65%)87. These thresholds highlight that potassium uptake in the crop was sufficient to maintain physiological functions, although further increases in fertilizer application could potentially enhance vegetative growth, tuber bulking, and overall yield. The results emphasize the importance of monitoring shoot potassium concentrations to ensure optimal nutrient management and to avoid deficiencies or suboptimal uptake that could constrain productivity.

Potassium content in potato tubers

Potassium accumulation in potato tubers increased in response to higher rates of fertilizer application, reflecting the plant enhanced nutrient uptake under improved soil mineral availability. As phosphorus and potassium fertilization rates increased, the potassium content in tubers varied accordingly, indicating that nutrient supply directly influences tuber nutrient composition. Elevated potassium concentrations in both leaf and tuber tissues can be attributed to the crop’s efficient uptake mechanisms, supported by the availability of soluble potassium in the soil following fertilization88. Adequate potassium nutrition is essential for healthy plant development, optimized tuber growth, and the production of a greater number of marketable tubers.

For optimal yield and quality, tuber potassium concentrations are recommended to be within the range of 1.8–2.2%89. In this study, potassium content in tubers exceeded the upper limit of this range in all treatments, including the control, suggesting that the soil and applied fertilizers provided sufficient or even surplus potassium. These results pronounce the critical role of potassium management in ensuring both tuber nutrient sufficiency and enhanced crop performance, while also highlighting the potential for careful calibration of fertilizer rates to avoid excessive accumulation that may not further benefit yield or quality.

Conclusions

The present study demonstrates that the combined application of 34.5 kg P₂O₅ ha⁻¹ and 200 kg K₂O ha⁻¹ constitutes an optimal fertilization strategy for improving potato yield and tuber quality under the soil and environmental conditions of northwestern Ethiopia. The results also reveal the existence of significant potassium deficiency in the study area, a constraint that has remained largely unrecognized for several decades due to the longstanding assumption that potassium availability in Ethiopian soils is generally adequate. These findings highlight the need to reassess current fertilizer recommendations by incorporating site-specific nutrient limitations, particularly potassium, to support sustainable and high-productivity potato systems.

Although the conclusions are derived from experiments conducted under specific local conditions, the observed potassium limitation and crop response patterns are likely relevant to other potato-growing areas dominated by Nitisols with comparable climatic and management conditions. Therefore, while the exact fertilizer rates should be validated through location-specific trials, the study provides important evidence supporting the broader need to re-evaluate potassium management strategies across similar agro-ecological zones.
