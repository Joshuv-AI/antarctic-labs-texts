A low-energy effective Hamiltonian for Landau quasiparticles
Pierre-Louis Taillat, Hadrien Kurkjian

Abstract
We introduce a new renormalisation scheme to construct the Landau quasiparticles of Fermi fluids. The scheme relies on an energy cutoff Λ \Lambda which removes the quasi-resonant couplings, enabling the dressing of the particles into quasiparticles via a unitary transformation. The dynamics of the quasiparticles is then restricted to low-energy transitions and is fully determined by an effective Hamiltonian which unifies the Landau interaction function f f and the collision amplitude in a single amplitude 𝒜 \mathcal{A} regularized by Λ \Lambda . Our effective theory captures all the low-energy physics of Fermi fluids that support Landau quasiparticles, from the equation of state to the transport properties, both in the normal and in the superfluid phase. We apply it to an atomic Fermi gas with contact interaction to compute the speed of zero sound in function of the scattering length a a . We also recover the Gork’ov-Melik Barkhudarov correction to the superfluid gap and critical temperature as a direct consequence of the dressing of particles into Landau quasiparticles.



Introduction

Originally formulated as a phenomenological theory, Fermi liquid theory is based
on a quadratic action
Landau 1956
, in which fermionic quasiparticles are
described by a semiclassical density field
\delta n
fluctuating about the
Fermi sea and interacting through a static interaction function
f
.
The physical origin of quasiparticles is not elucidated; their existence is merely justified
by the heuristic assumption that the noninteracting states
can be adiabatically followed when interactions are switched on
Nozières and Pines 1966
.
When Landau’s semi-classical action becomes insufficient—for instance to describe the transport phenomena or the superfluid transition—additional phenomenological parameters are introduced
Pfitzner and Wölfle 1983
, such as a collision or pairing amplitude.
Microscopic approaches were later proposed
Landau 1959
;
Nozières and Luttinger 1962
;
Luttinger and Nozières 1962
to interpret the parameters of Landau’s theory in terms of two-particle correlation functions, which relies in particular on the introduction of a quasiparticle residue.

In a more modern perspective, Landau’s theory has been reinterpreted as a low-energy effective theory emerging from a renormalization process
Popov 1987a
;
Shankar 1994
;
Polchinski 1993
;
Chitov and Sénéchal 1995
;
Chitov and Sénéchal 1998
;
Dupuis 1998
. This moves beyond the phenomenological nature of the theory and provides it with a fundamental justification.
In the renormalization picture, the quasiparticle energies and interactions arise from the progressive integration of the high-energy degrees of freedom.
Although the renormalization group generates in principle a complete effective action for the quasiparticle field, in practice one follows Landau’s original formulation by concentrating on specific “interaction channels”, i.e., restrictions of the quasiparticle scattering processes to specific geometries
Delacrétaz et al. 2022
;
Ma and Lee 2024
.
The forward-scattering channel collects the collisions in which the scattering angle tends to zero; the pairing or Bardeen–Cooper–Schrieffer (BCS) channel describes head-on collisions, where the angle of incidence approaches
\pi
.
Restricting the attention to such channels is insufficient in three-dimensional (3D) Fermi liquids, where resonant collisions between quasiparticles of the Fermi surface are not limited to small momentum transfers nor to small center-of-mass momenta.
In 3D, the collision probability depends on two independent angles, in contrast to the static interaction function
f
, which depends only on the angle between the quasiparticle momenta
p
and
\textbf{p}^{\prime}
. In this respect,
the 3D case is fundamentally different from its 2D counterpart, where resonant collisions depend on a single angle and thus fall into either one of the two channels
Gran et al. 2023
;
Maki et al. 2025
.
Although effective-theory approaches are a natural starting point to derive the quasiparticle transport equation, and thus access the density and polarization response functions, they are often restricted to the collisionless regime, neglecting the ergodic processes contained in the collision integral. An effective theory that fully captures the Boltzmann equation of the Fermi liquid would then be particularly valuable, for instance to assess the corrections to transport properties beyond the Born–Markov approximation
Lin and Sauls 2022
.

In fact, a convincing low-energy effective theory should be able to describe, within a unified formalism, all low-energy phenomena, from the low-temperature thermodynamics to the hydrodynamic equations, in both the normal and superfluid phases (provided that superfluidity itself remains a low-energy phenomenon).
In this work, we construct an effective Hamiltonian that captures the full dynamics of Landau quasiparticles, and thereby the whole low-energy physics of fermionic fluids in which these quasiparticles are well defined. Our formalism relies on a unitary transformation that connects the quasiparticle states to the noninteracting Fock states; such unitary transformations are common in atomic physics
Cohen-Tannoudji et al. 1988
;
Van Vleck 1929
;
Schrieffer and Wolff 1966
when one applies a perturbation to multiplets of quasidegenerate energy levels.
Our construction of the quasiparticle states thus excludes from the dressing any quasidegenerate state in a narrow energy band of width
\Lambda
. The unitary operator
\exp(\hat{S})
of this dressing block-diagonalizes the Hamiltonian, thereby decoupling levels whose energy separation exceeds
\Lambda
. This is not the same as introducing a momentum cutoff
Popov 1987a
;
Shankar 1994
;
Polchinski 1993
;
Chitov and Sénéchal 1995
;
Chitov and Sénéchal 1998
;
Dupuis 1998
, and the renormalization group generated by infinitesimal variations of
\Lambda
is different.
To ensure that the physical quantities predicted by the effective theory are independent of
\Lambda
, the cutoff must be small compared to the Fermi energy
\epsilon_{\rm F}
yet remain large compared to the typical evolution frequencies of the fluid, such as the quasiparticle damping rate
\Gamma
in the normal phase, or the gap
\Delta
in the superfluid phase.
In Heisenberg picture, the transformation generated by
\hat{S}
relates particle operators to their quasiparticle counterparts; this allows us in particular to construct the quasiparticle annihilation operator
\hat{\gamma}

Luttinger and Nozières 1962
.

The adiabaticity condition usually invoked to justify the existence of the quasiparticles translates, in our formalism, into a condition of weak mixing between (significantly coupled) energy levels. Two quasiparticle states must be resonant at the scale
\Lambda
if and only if the corresponding particle states are resonant as well.
In a generic many-body system, this condition would restrict the theory to the lowest orders of perturbation theory. In a Fermi liquid, it remains valid all the way to the strongly interacting regime, and the dressed states, free from low-energy couplings, can be followed adiabatically. The conservation of a spectrum that vanishes linearly at the Fermi level thus appears as a necessary condition to the existence of Landau quasiparticles.

Our unitary transformation constructs the low-energy effective theory by a direct renormalisation of the underlying microscopic theory, without introducing emergent degrees of freedom.
The renormalisation group emerging from the infinitesimal generator
\hat{S}(\Lambda)-\hat{S}(\Lambda-\textrm{d}\Lambda)

Wegner 1994
;
Heidbrink and Uhrig 2002
acts on the full Hamiltonian. It is not restricted to a gradient expansion, nor to an expansion in powers of the quasiparticle field
\hat{\gamma}
.
Only after the renormalization procedure do we expand the Hamiltonian in powers of the fluctuations
\delta(\hat{\gamma}^{\dagger}\hat{\gamma})
of the density field about its expectation value in the quasiparticle Fermi sea (defined as the image of the non-interacting Fermi sea through the unitary transform). The effective Hamiltonian obtained in this way is not limited to specific interaction channels: its diagonal part in the Fock basis coincides with Landau’s semi-classical Hamiltonian, but its off-diagonal part contains the generic collision amplitude.
It allows us to derive the Boltzmann equation—including the collision integral—without leaving the effective picture,
i.e., without returning to particle Green’s functions and without using the quasiparticle residue.
The Born–Markov approximation can be used to truncate the Bogoliubov–Born–Green–Kirkwood–Yvon (BBGKY) hierarchy in the quasiparticle picture, even though the problem is strongly correlated in the particle picture.

Our construction of an effective Hamiltonian for Fermi fluids is motivated
by experimental considerations, particularly in the context of ultracold atomic gases.
The Fermi gas with contact interactions, long considered
as an academic model

;
Rainwater and Mohling 1976
;
Lipschitz and Pitaevskii 1981
,
can nowadays be prepared and manipulated with great flexibility using laser trapping techniques

;
Vogt et al. 2012
;
Trotzky et al. 2015
;
Li et al. 2024
;
Huang et al. 2025

At low temperature, its microscopic physics is fully characterized by the scattering
length
a

While it supports Landau quasiparticles only in the
weakly-interacting regime (
a\to 0
), the contact gas gives access to several observables whose dependence on

The Landau quasiparticles are then a
powerful, and likely inevitable, heuristic tool
to derive quantitatively predictions of mesurable quantities such as
the velocity and damping of zero sound

Rainwater and Mohling 1976
;
Taillat and H.

Vollhardt and Wolfle 1990

This article is divided into three sections.

In Section
I
, we construct the low-energy effective theory of Landau quasiparticles.
We introduce the unitary transformation that allows us to define the quasiparticle Fock states,
the operators acting on these states, and in particular their effective Hamiltonian.
We illustrate our formalism by applying it perturbatively to the Fermi gas with contact interactions.
We compute to second order in

the effective parameters of the theory: the quasiparticle energy,
the interaction functions, and the collision amplitude.
Finally, we return to a more general framework and derive the quasiparticle Boltzmann equation from the effective Hamiltonian.

In Section
II
, we study the density and spin responses of the contact Fermi gas.
In particular, we analyze the collisionless regime and compute the expansion of the zero-sound velocity
c_{0}

in powers of the interaction strength

A major result is the presence of a log-perturbative correction in the deviation

between this velocity and the Fermi velocity

,
which results into a prefactor

for the density mode and

We further investigate the role of collisions on zero sound and show that they lead to a collisional damping proportional to

where

This damping is universal in the sense that its dependence on
k_{\rm F}a
enters only through
\tau
.
Finally, we present a numerical method that solves the transport equation exactly throughout the crossover
from the hydrodynamic to the collisionless regime.

In Section

We express the zero-temperature order parameter
\Delta
and the critical temperature

in terms of a parameter

Partition of the Hilbert space and unitary transformation

\hat{N}_{\gamma}=\textrm{e}^{\hat{S}}\hat{N}\textrm{e}^{-\hat{S}}=\sum_{\alpha\sigma}\hat{\gamma}_{\alpha\sigma}^{\dagger}\hat{\gamma}_{\alpha\sigma}\quad\text{ with }\quad\hat{N}=\sum_{\alpha\sigma}\hat{a}_{\alpha\sigma}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{\alpha\sigma}

Projectors

Finally, in the quasiparticle picture, the projector onto an energy-shell becomes

\hat{P}_{\Lambda,\gamma}(E)=\textrm{e}^{\hat{S}}\hat{P}_{\Lambda}(E)\textrm{e}^{-\hat{S}}=\sum_{\{n_{\alpha\sigma}\}}\Pi_{\Lambda}(E-\hat{H}_{0,\gamma})|\{n_{\alpha\sigma}\}\rangle\langle\{n_{\alpha\sigma}\}|

and correspondingly

. The operators
\hat{P}_{\gamma}

{\left|\langle n|\hat{H}|n\rangle-\langle m|\hat{H}|m\rangle\right|}\ll\Lambda\iff{\left|{}_{0}\langle n|\hat{H}_{0}|n\rangle_{0}-{}_{0}\langle m|\hat{H}_{0}|m\rangle_{0}\right|}\ll\Lambda

I.1.5
Collision amplitudes

Contrarily to a widespread believe,
the effective description of the Fermi fluid
is not exhaustive if we restrict ourselves to the eigenenergy
\epsilon_{\alpha\sigma}

and interaction functions
f_{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}
defined above.
In fact these two quantities characterize only the diagonal elements
of
\hat{H}
, while
for many equilibrium and dynamical properties, a knowledge of the
off-diagonal elements is required:

\mathcal{A}_{i\to f}\equiv\langle f|\hat{H}|i\rangle

(38)

Even though it is restricted to quasi on-shell couplings (
\mathcal{A}_{i\to f}\neq 0\text{ only if }|E_{i}-E_{f}|\ll\Lambda
),
\hat{H}
can generate high-order collisions between quasiparticules. From Eq. (
16
), one can easily count that there are up to
n+1\leftrightarrow n+1
quasiparticle collisions if
\hat{V}
describes
2\leftrightarrow 2
particles collisions and
\hat{H}_{\rm eff}
is truncated to order
\hat{V}^{n}
.
However, we shall see that
2\leftrightarrow 2
quasiparticle collisions remain the more likely
if excited quasiparticles are confined to a low-energy shell about the Fermi
level.

Just like the interaction functions
f_{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}
, the
2\leftrightarrow 2
transitions amplitudes depend on the reference state
|\psi\rangle

in which we compute them. Let
|i\rangle=|\overline{\alpha\sigma},\overline{\beta\sigma^{\prime}},\gamma\sigma^{\prime},\delta\sigma,\psi\rangle

be a reference state in which we made sure that quasiparticles are absent in
\alpha\sigma,\beta\sigma^{\prime}
and present in
\gamma\sigma^{\prime},\delta\sigma
. Then let

|f\rangle=\hat{\gamma}_{\alpha\sigma}^{\dagger}\hat{\gamma}_{\beta\sigma^{\prime}}^{\dagger}\hat{\gamma}_{\gamma\sigma^{\prime}}\hat{\gamma}_{\delta\sigma}|i\rangle\qquad(\alpha\sigma,\beta\sigma^{\prime})\neq(\delta\sigma,\gamma\sigma^{\prime}),\,(\gamma\sigma^{\prime},\delta\sigma)

(39)

be the final state not proportional to
|i\rangle
. We then define
the collision amplitude
\mathcal{A}_{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}
between
\sigma
and
\sigma^{\prime}
quasiparticles through

\mathcal{A}_{i\to f}\equiv\frac{\mathcal{A}_{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}^{|i\rangle}(\alpha\beta|\gamma\delta)}{\mathcal{V}}

(40)

From the fermionic commutation relation and the hermiticity of the Hamiltonian, these amplitudes verify the relations

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}^{|i\rangle}(\delta\gamma|\beta\alpha)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}^{|i\rangle}(\alpha\beta|\gamma\delta)

(41)

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{\uparrow\uparrow}^{|i\rangle}(\beta\alpha|\gamma\delta)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{\uparrow\uparrow}^{|i\rangle}(\alpha\beta|\delta\gamma)=-\mathcal{A}_{\uparrow\uparrow}^{|i\rangle}(\alpha\beta|\gamma\delta)

(42)

In a spin-symmetric fluid, they verify in addition

\mathcal{A}_{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}^{|i\rangle}(\beta\alpha|\delta\gamma)=\mathcal{A}_{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}^{|i\rangle}(\alpha\beta|\gamma\delta)

(43)

Note that our van Vleck transformation a priori restricts
\mathcal{A}_{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}^{|i\rangle}(\alpha\beta|\gamma\delta)
to transitions
between quasi-degenerate states
|E_{f}-E_{i}|\ll\Lambda
. Comparing
E_{i}=\langle i|\hat{H}|i\rangle
and
E_{f}=\langle f|\hat{H}|f\rangle
,
this can be turned in the thermodynamic limit
4
4

4

One can show that

E_{{f}}-E_{{i}}=\epsilon_{\alpha\sigma}^{|i\rangle}+\epsilon_{\beta\sigma^{\prime}}^{|i\rangle}-\epsilon_{\gamma\sigma^{\prime}}^{|i\rangle}-\epsilon_{\delta\sigma^{\prime}}^{|i\rangle}+O\left(\frac{1}{\mathcal{V}}\right)
into a resonance condition on the energies (in
|i\rangle
) of the colliding quasiparticles:

\left|\epsilon_{\alpha\sigma}^{|i\rangle}+\epsilon_{\beta\sigma^{\prime}}^{|i\rangle}-\epsilon_{\gamma\sigma^{\prime}}^{|i\rangle}-\epsilon_{\delta\sigma}^{|i\rangle}\right|\ll\Lambda

(44)

I.1.6
Low-energy effective Hamiltonian in the vicinity of the quasiparticle Fermi sea

So far, we have described the matrix elements of

between arbitrary
quasiparticle states, noticing that even if we restrict to few-quasiparticle transitions

|\psi\rangle
.
This can be seen as a consequence of Eq. (

(at strong coupling) contains an infinite number of

One can however derive a tractable truncation of

\hat{\gamma}

|\{n_{\alpha\sigma}\}\rangle
and
|\{m_{\alpha\sigma}\}\rangle

One truncation plays a special role,
this is the one based on the quasiparticle Fermi sea:

\displaystyle|{\rm FS}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\textrm{e}^{\hat{S}}|{\rm FS}\rangle_{0}

(45)

\displaystyle E_{\rm FS}

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\langle{\rm FS}|\hat{H}{|{\rm FS}\rangle}

(46)

Note that

In the following,

and
\mathcal{A}_{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}\equiv\mathcal{A}_{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}^{|{\rm FS}\rangle}

As the main result of this section we write this truncation of the Hamiltonian
in the vicinity of the quasiparticle Fermi sea, including the collision
amplitudes between resonant states.
The truncation is written in terms of the fluctuations of the quasiparticle-hole operator,

\displaystyle\delta(\hat{\gamma}_{\alpha\sigma}^{\dagger}\hat{\gamma}_{\beta\sigma})

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\hat{\gamma}_{\alpha\sigma}^{\dagger}\hat{\gamma}_{\beta\sigma}-n_{\alpha\sigma}^{0}\delta_{\alpha\beta}

(47)

\displaystyle\delta\hat{n}_{\alpha\sigma}

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\delta(\hat{\gamma}_{\alpha\sigma}^{\dagger}\hat{\gamma}_{\alpha\sigma})

(48)

where

\alpha\sigma
. The fluctuation of the quasiparticle number
\delta\hat{n}

can be viewed as the quantum version of the classical field

(the semi-classical “number of quasiparticles”)

Restricting to terms quadratic in

\hat{H}=E_{\rm FS}+\sum_{\alpha\sigma}\epsilon_{\alpha\sigma}\delta\hat{n}_{\alpha\sigma}+\frac{1}{2\mathcal{V}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha\beta\gamma\delta\in\mathcal{D}\\
\sigma\sigma^{\prime}=\uparrow\downarrow\end{subarray}}\delta_{\alpha+\beta}^{\gamma+{\delta}}{\mathcal{B}}_{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}(\alpha\beta|\gamma{\delta})\delta(\gamma_{\alpha\sigma}^{\dagger}\gamma_{{\delta}\sigma})\delta(\gamma_{\beta\sigma^{\prime}}^{\dagger}\gamma_{\gamma\sigma^{\prime}})+O(\delta(\hat{\gamma}^{\dagger}\hat{\gamma})^{3})

The function

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{\uparrow\downarrow}(\alpha,\beta|\gamma,\delta)

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{\uparrow\downarrow}(\alpha,\beta|\gamma,\delta),\ \alpha\neq\delta

Conversely, the indistinguishability of the colliding
\sigma\sigma
quasiparticles leaves us some freedom in the choice of

5

5

The first constraint ensures that the particle-hole operators

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{\sigma\sigma}(\alpha,\beta|\alpha,\beta)

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{\sigma\sigma}(\alpha,\beta|\gamma,\delta)

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{\sigma\sigma}(\alpha,\beta|\gamma,\delta)-\mathcal{B}_{\sigma\sigma}(\beta,\alpha|\gamma,\delta)

We recover the usual semi-classical Hamiltonian of Fermi liquid theory

Finally, in fluids where the modes are indexed by a 3D wavevector

I.2.1

The kinetic and interaction Hamiltonian which describe our Fermi gas are:

I.3
Derivation of the Fermi liquid kinetic equations

Knowing the energy and transition amplitudes of the quasiparticle fluid,
we can now attempt to describe its dynamics by a kinetic equation.
We recall that Fermi systems at intermediate temperatures
T\approx T_{\rm F}

and strong interactions
do not obey a kinetic equation, as there is no separation of timescales
to break the BBGKY hierarchy.
By introducing the long-lived states
|\psi\rangle=\textrm{e}^{\hat{S}}|\psi\rangle_{0}
,
the quasiparticle description manages to overcome this limitation in the low temperature
limit
T\ll T_{\rm F}
, irrespectively of the interaction strength (as long as the quasiparticle picture
holds).

This section uses the effective Hamiltonian Eq. (
49
) to rigorously derive
the kinetic equation, and discuss its domain of validity.
In Sec.
I.3.1
, we consider the case of an homogeneous system
with an out-of-equilibrium quasiparticle distribution. We show that if the cloud of excited quasiparticles
is contained in a low-energy shell
\epsilon_{0}\ll\epsilon_{\rm F}
, one can treat the evolution of the quasiparticle
distribution in the Born-Markov approximation. This results in a nonlinear kinetic equation, from
which we extract the thermal lifetime of the quasiparticles.

In Sec.
I.3.2
and
I.3.3
, we study
transport phenomena, where the quasiparticle gas is excited by a
perturbation periodic in space and time, at frequency
\omega
and wavenumber
q
.
We assume that the corresponding energy scales are comparable and small compared to
\Lambda
:

v_{\rm F}q\ \approx\ \omega\ll\Lambda

(106)

In presence of the dynamical parameters
q
and
\omega
, there exists several ways to take the low temperature limit.
In Sec.
I.3.2
, we derive the collisional transport equation
in the limit

\frac{T}{T_{\rm F}}\to 0\text{ at fixed }v_{\rm F}q\tau,\ \omega\tau

(107)

where the mean collision time
\tau
scales, as
we shall see, as
1/T^{2}
. In this regime, all the lower bounds on
\Lambda
in Eq. (
6
)
are of order
T^{2}
:

v_{\rm F}q,\ \omega,\ \Gamma_{\rm typ}\approx T^{2}

(108)

Varying the parameter
\omega\tau
(after the limit
T\to 0
is taken),
this regime describes the crossover from hydrodynamic
\omega\tau\ll 1
to
collisionless transport
\omega\tau\gg 1
.

In Sec.
I.3.3
instead, we take the limit:

\frac{T}{T_{\rm F}}\to 0\text{ at fixed }v_{\rm F}q,\ \omega\ll\epsilon_{\rm F}

(109)

In this regime, the excitation energy
v_{\rm F}q

sets the high energy tail of the quasiparticle distribution
and the lower bound on
\Lambda
.
The collision integral vanishes as
(v_{\rm F}q/\epsilon_{\rm F})^{2}
, such that
transport is collisionless to leading order in
q
.

II.1.6
Low temperature factorization of the kernel

Among the fluids described by a Boltzmann equation, Fermi liquid have a remarkable property:
their collision kernel
\mathcal{N}(\textbf{p},\textbf{p}^{\prime})
can be factorized into a radial (or energy) dependance
and an angular dependence on

\alpha=(\widehat{\textbf{p},\textbf{p}^{\prime}}),\quad\cos\alpha=\cos\theta\cos\theta^{\prime}+\sin\theta\sin\theta^{\prime}\cos\phi

(178)

This a consequence of the restriction of both the collision probabilities and energy-conservation constraint
to the Fermi surface, such that the only remaining energy dependence in the kernel stems from the thermal
populations
n^{\rm eq}
.

We illustrate this decoupling in the calculation of
S_{\pm}
:

S_{\pm}(\textbf{p},\textbf{p}^{\prime})=\frac{(m^{*})^{2}T}{4\pi p_{\rm F}|\sin\frac{\alpha}{2}|}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}N_{y^{\prime},y+y_{2}-y^{\prime}}^{y_{2}}\textrm{d}y_{2}\int\frac{\textrm{d}\Omega_{2}}{2\pi}W_{S\pm}(\textbf{p},\textbf{p}_{2}|\textbf{p}^{\prime},\textbf{p}-\textbf{p}^{\prime}+\textbf{p}_{2})\delta\left(\cos\theta_{2}+\sin\frac{\alpha}{2}\right)\\
+O(T^{2})

(179)

with
N_{y_{1}y_{2}}^{y_{3}}=n(y_{1})n(y_{2})\bar{n}(y_{3})+\bar{n}(y_{1})\bar{n}(y_{2})n(y_{3})
and
n(y)=1/(1+\textrm{e}^{y})
. From the original expression (
166
), we have eliminated
\textbf{p}_{4}
using momentum conservation, and switched the radial integration from
p_{2}
to
y_{2}
using the relation, valid for a function
h(\textbf{p}_{2})
peaked about
p_{\rm F}
:

\int\frac{\textrm{d}^{3}\textbf{p}_{2}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\ h(\textbf{p}_{2})=\frac{m^{*}p_{\rm F}T}{(2\pi)^{2}}\int^{+\infty}_{-\infty}\textrm{d}y_{2}\int\frac{\textrm{d}\Omega_{2}}{2\pi}h(y_{2},\theta_{2},\phi_{2})+O(T)

(180)

where the solid angle
\textrm{d}\Omega_{2}=\sin{\theta_{2}}\textrm{d}\theta_{2}\textrm{d}\phi_{2}
locates
\textbf{p}_{2}
on the spherical frame of axis
\textbf{p}-\textbf{p}^{\prime}
,
as depicted by Fig.
5

\textbf{p}=\textbf{p}_{1}
,
\textbf{p}^{\prime}=\textbf{p}_{3}
). To leading order in
T
, the resonance
condition is

\epsilon_{\textbf{p}}+\epsilon_{\textbf{p}_{2}}-\epsilon_{\textbf{p}^{\prime}}-\epsilon_{\textbf{p}+\textbf{p}_{2}-\textbf{p}^{\prime}}=\frac{2p_{\rm F}^{2}\sin(\alpha/2)}{m^{*}}\left(\sin\frac{\alpha}{2}+\cos\theta_{2}\right)+O(T)

(181)

and allows us to integrate over
\theta_{2}
in Eq. (
179

over the energy coordinate
y_{2}
and the angle
\phi_{2}
.
The same calculation for

179

with
\textbf{p}_{3}
playing to role of
\textbf{p}_{2}

We thus obtain the factorized kernels

\displaystyle E_{\pm}(y,y^{\prime},\alpha)

\displaystyle\frac{(m^{\ast})^{2}T}{2\pi p_{\rm F}}\mathcal{S}(y,-y^{\prime})\Omega_{E\pm}(\alpha)+O(T^{2})

Here,

\mathcal{S}(y,y^{\prime})=\frac{y-y^{\prime}}{2}\frac{1}{\sinh{\frac{y-y^{\prime}}{2}}}\frac{\cosh{\frac{y}{2}}}{\cosh{\frac{y^{\prime}}{2}}}

(184)

The angular kernel
\Omega(\alpha)
follows from an azimuthal integration over
\phi

\displaystyle\Omega_{E\pm}(\alpha)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{\textrm{d}\phi}{2\pi}\frac{w_{E\pm}(\alpha,\phi)}{2|\text{cos}\frac{\alpha}{2}|}

(185)

\displaystyle\Omega_{S\pm}(\alpha)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{\textrm{d}\phi}{2\pi}\frac{w_{S\pm}(\alpha,\phi)}{2|\text{sin}\frac{\alpha}{2}|}

(186)

Changing the summation over
\textbf{p}^{\prime}
into integrals over
y^{\prime}
and
\theta^{\prime}
, we express the collision integral in Eq. (
172

I_{\pm}(y,\theta)=\frac{\textrm{i}}{\tau}g(y)\Bigg\{\overline{\Gamma}(y)\nu_{\pm}(y,\theta)\\
+\int\textrm{d}y^{\prime}\frac{\textrm{d}\Omega^{\prime}}{2\pi}\left(\mathcal{S}(y,-y^{\prime})\frac{\Omega_{\rm E\pm}(\alpha)}{\Omega_{\Gamma}}-2\mathcal{S}(y,y^{\prime})\frac{\Omega_{\rm S\pm}(\alpha)}{\Omega_{\Gamma}}\right)\nu_{\pm}(y^{\prime},\theta^{\prime})\Bigg\}

(187)

We have extracted a typical collision time

\frac{1}{\tau}=\frac{(m^{*})^{3}T^{2}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\left\langle\frac{W_{E+}(\theta,\phi)}{2\cos{\theta/2}}\right\rangle_{\theta,\phi}

(188)

where

\Gamma(\textbf{p})=\frac{1}{\tau}\overline{\Gamma}(y),\qquad\overline{\Gamma}(y)\equiv\pi^{2}+y^{2}

(189)

Note that this can also be deduced from the number conservation law
\Gamma(\textbf{p})=

(1/L^{3})

\sum_{\textbf{p}^{\prime}}S_{+}(\textbf{p},\textbf{p}^{\prime})=(1/\tau)\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\mathcal{S}(y,y^{\prime})\textrm{d}y^{\prime}\int_{0}^{\pi}\sin\alpha\textrm{d}\alpha\Omega_{S+}(\alpha)/\Omega_{\Gamma}

We conclude this subsection by giving a dimensionless

Assuming a periodic driving

Inserting the change of variable Eq. (

(191)

where

\omega_{0}={v_{\rm F}q}

(192)

is the typical excitation frequency, and

(193)

are the dimensionless symmetric and anti-symmetric Landau functions. The

II.2
Zero sound in the collisionless regime

Since the regime of hydrodynamic transport is covered by Ref.
Taillat and H.
Kurkjian 2025
, we concentrate here on the collisionless regime
\omega_{0}\tau\to+\infty
, where the collision integral can be treated as a perturbation of the transport equation.
We will perform an expansion of the quasiparticle distribution

\nu_{\pm}
in powers of
1/\omega_{0}\tau
:

\nu_{\pm}=\nu_{\pm}^{\rm cl}+\frac{\delta\nu_{\pm}}{\omega_{0}\tau}+O(\omega_{0}\tau)^{-2}.

(194)

II.2.1
Dispersion equation in the perfect collisionless regime (
\omega_{0}\tau=+\infty
)

Let us first compute the leading term
\nu_{\pm}^{\rm cl}
in the perfect collisionless regime limit
1/\omega_{0}\tau=0
.
The low-temperature transport equation (
191
) in this regime is

\left(c-\cos{\theta}\right)\ \nu_{\pm}(y,\theta)=-\cos{\theta}\left(1-\frac{1}{2}\int\textrm{d}y^{\prime}\frac{\textrm{d}\Omega^{\prime}}{2\pi}F^{\pm}(\alpha)g(y^{\prime})\ \nu_{\pm}(y^{\prime},\theta^{\prime})\right)

(195)

where
c=\omega/\omega_{0}
.
Since there is no explicit dependence on the energy variable
y
on the right-hand side, the collisionless distribution is energy-independent,
\nu^{\rm cl}_{\pm}=\nu^{\rm cl}_{\pm}(\theta)
.
To solve the remaining 1D integral equation, we project
\nu_{\pm}
and the interactions functions
F^{\pm}(\alpha)
onto the basis of Legendre polynomials

\displaystyle\nu_{\pm}(\theta)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{l=0}^{+\infty}\nu_{l}^{\pm}P_{l}(\cos\theta)

(196)

\displaystyle F^{\pm}(\alpha)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{l=0}^{+\infty}F_{l}^{\pm}P_{l}(\cos\alpha)

(197)

To lighten the notations, the subscript “cl” is implicit here and until Sec.
II.2.4
.
The integral equation folds onto a matrix equation
whose
l
-th component is given by

\nu_{l}^{\pm}(c)-\sum_{l^{\prime}}A_{ll^{\prime}}^{\pm}(c)\nu_{l^{\prime}}^{\pm}(c)+B_{l0}(c)=0

(198)

where we have introduced the matrices

\displaystyle B_{ll^{\prime}}(c)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int^{1}_{-1}\frac{\textrm{d}u}{2}P_{l}(u)\frac{u}{c-u}P_{l^{\prime}}(u)

(199)

\displaystyle A^{\pm}_{ll^{\prime}}(c)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle F^{\pm}_{l^{\prime}}B_{ll^{\prime}}(c)

(200)

This infinite-dimension linear system is solved by formally inverting the matrix
1-A
:

\vec{\nu}^{\pm}(c)=-\frac{1}{1-{A}^{\pm}(c)}\vec{B}_{0}(c)

(201)

where the source vector
\vec{B}_{0}=\left(B_{l0}\right)_{l\in\mathbb{N}}
is the consequence
of the external drive (recall that
\nu
is scaled to the drive intensity
U
). A phononic collective
modes occur when some component of the quasiparticle distribution
\nu
diverges
in response to the drive,
i.e.
when the matrix
1-A^{\pm}(c)
has a zero-energy
eigenvector. The dispersion equation on the reduced velocity
c_{0}=\omega_{\textbf{q}}/v_{\rm F}q
of the collective
modes is then

\textrm{det}\left(1-{A}^{\pm}(c_{0})\right)=0,

(202)

This dispersion equation can have several solutions, both real and complex.
However, when
F_{0}
is much larger than the other
F_{l}
’s, as in the case
of weakly-interacting Fermi gases and
3
He,
there is a dominant real solution,
traditionally called zero sound. Physically, this solution describes a longitudinal collisionless
phononic branch.

II.2.2
Log-perturbative expansion of the zero-sound velocity

We are now calcutating the zero-sound reduced frequency
c_{0}
in powers of
\overline{a}

in a weakly-interacting Fermi gas.
In equation (
198
) for
l>0
, the summation
over
l^{\prime}
is dominated by the term
l^{\prime}=0
(which contains the dominant coefficient
F_{0}
)
so that:

\nu_{l}^{\pm}=-B_{l0}(c)+F_{0}^{\pm}B_{l0}(c)\ \nu_{0}^{\pm}+O(\overline{a}),\quad\text{for }l\geq 1

(203)

Anticipating on the followings, we have estimated
B_{ll^{\prime}}=O(1/\overline{a})
.
Reinjecting in (
198
) for
l=0
, we eventually obtain
\bar{\chi}_{\pm}\equiv\nu_{0}^{\pm}
, which represents either the dimensionless density
\bar{\chi}_{\rho}
or polarization
\bar{\chi}_{\rm p}
response, depending on the
\pm
index:

\overline{\chi}_{\pm}(c)=-\frac{B_{00}(c)+\sum_{l^{\prime}>0}F^{\pm}_{l^{\prime}}B_{0,l^{\prime}}(c)B_{l^{\prime},0}}{1-F^{\pm}_{0}B_{0,0}(c)-\sum_{l^{\prime}}F^{\pm}_{0}F^{\pm}_{l^{\prime}}B_{0,l^{\prime}}(c)B_{l^{\prime},0}(c)}

(204)

The dispersion relation
1/\overline{\chi}(c_{0})=0
now reduces to:

1-F^{\pm}_{0}B_{0,0}(c_{0})-\sum_{l^{\prime}}F^{\pm}_{0}F^{\pm}_{l^{\prime}}B_{0,l^{\prime}}(c_{0})B_{l^{\prime},0}(c_{0})=0

(205)

Since we expect that
c_{0}-1
tends to zero exponentially as
|\overline{a}|\rightarrow 0
, we introduce the variable
\gamma=\ln{[(c_{0}-1)/2]}
. Contrarily to
c_{0}
,
\gamma
can be expanded in power of
\overline{a}
:

\gamma^{\pm}=\frac{\gamma_{0}^{\pm}}{\overline{a}}+\gamma_{1}^{\pm}+{O}(\overline{a})

(206)

The log-perturbative corrections to
\gamma
convert into a prefactor correction in
c_{0}-1
,
reminiscent of the Gork’ov Melik-Barkhudarov prefactor in the calculation of the superfluid critical temperature
Gor’kov and Melik-Barkhudarov 1958
.
When
c_{0}
tends to 1 exponentially, the functions
B_{ll^{\prime}}
have the following expansion

B_{00}(c_{0})=-1-\frac{1}{2}\gamma^{\pm}+O(\overline{a})\ \textrm{ and }\ B_{l0}(c_{0})=B_{0l}(c_{0})=\frac{\gamma^{\pm}}{2}+O(1)

(207)

By substituting the expansions of
\gamma
, of the Landau parameters
F^{\pm}_{l}
and of the functions
B_{ll^{\prime}}

into (
205
), and by restricting to terms of order
O(\overline{a})
, we obtain the following expressions of
\gamma_{0}^{\pm}
and
\gamma_{1}^{\pm}
:

\gamma_{0}^{\pm}=\mp\pi\ \textrm{ and }\ \gamma_{1}^{\pm}=-2+\frac{\pi^{2}}{2\overline{a}^{2}}\left(\sum_{l>0}F^{\pm}_{l}+\delta F_{0}^{\pm}\right)=\pm 4

(208)

where we have expanded
F_{0}^{\pm}
as:

F_{0}^{\pm}=\pm\frac{2\overline{a}}{\pi}+\delta F_{0}^{\pm},\quad\delta F_{0}^{\pm}=O(\bar{a}^{2})

(209)

We eventually recognize in
\gamma^{\pm}
the sum of the
F_{l}^{\pm}
that is the forward value (
\alpha=0
) of
F^{\pm}
.

\gamma^{\pm}=-\frac{2}{F^{\pm}(\alpha=0)}-2+O(\overline{a})

(210)

This shows that the Landau function
F(\alpha)
in the integral equation (
195
)
can be replaced (to leading and subleading order in
\overline{a}
) by its value in
\alpha=0
, that is for quasiparticles with
colinear momenta
\textbf{p}\parallel\textbf{p}^{\prime}
.
This is a consequence of the longitudinal nature of zero sound at weak-coupling:
the quasiparticle distribution
\nu_{\pm}(\theta)\propto\cos\theta/(c_{0}-\cos\theta)

is peaked about
\theta=0
, such that the quasiparticle momenta
p
are all nearly
colinear to
q
.

In short, the zero sound velocity (in units of
v_{\rm F}
) for the density mode is given by

c_{0}^{+}=1+2\textrm{e}^{4}\ \textrm{e}^{-\pi/\overline{a}},\qquad\overline{a}>0

(211)

and the velocity for the zero polarization mode is:

c_{0}^{-}=1+2\textrm{e}^{-4}\ \textrm{e}^{\pi/\overline{a}},\qquad\overline{a}<0

(212)

Second order corrections thus shift the density zero sound peak to higher velocities in the density-density response, increasing
c_{0}^{+}-1
by a factor
\textrm{exp}(6)\simeq 403
.
We then expect the resonance to be more easily observable than predicted by first-order approximations.
Since it exists only for
\overline{a}>0
, the density zero sound is observable in a Fermi gas only on the metastable branch. Experimental exploration of this metastable branch are restricted to
|\overline{a}|\lesssim 0.1
, where zero
sound is visible only at very low temperatures.

Conversely, the polarisation sound mode, which is observable on the ground branch at
\overline{a}<0
, is shifted closer to the continuum edge, with
c_{0}^{-}-1

reduced by a factor
\textrm{exp}(-2)\simeq 0.14
. This reduces the temperature range in which this zero sound mode is observable.

We have benchmarked these analytical results using a numerical solution of Eq. (
198
).
More details for the numerical evaluation are given in Appendix
B
.

Response function in the collisionless regime

Our discussion of zero sound so far has focused on reduced frequencies
c\approx c_{0}\approx 1

and polarisation-polarisation response.
Figs.

\textrm{Im}[\overline{\chi}_{\rho,\rm p}(c+i0^{+})]

In the attractive case
\overline{a}<0

\textrm{Im}[\overline{\chi}_{\rho}]
and
\textrm{Im}[\overline{\chi}_{\rm p}]
from the Lindhard response of an ideal gas (black curve).
Since a stable Fermi liquid regime exists in ultracold Fermi gases only for
\overline{a}<0

Conversely, in the repulsive case
\overline{a}>0
(blue curves), the deviations are increased to second-order.
This can be understood by comparing the first- and second-order approximation of the Landau parameters. For the leading coefficient,
F^{\pm}_{0}

\displaystyle\frac{F^{+(2)}_{0}}{F^{+(1)}_{0}}

\displaystyle\simeq

\displaystyle 1+1.143\ \overline{a}

\displaystyle\frac{F^{-(2)}_{0}}{F^{-(1)}_{0}}

\displaystyle\simeq

\displaystyle 1+0.130\ \ \overline{a}

(214)

Thus, for negative (resp. positive)
\overline{a}
, the second-order
F_{0}^{\pm}
is smaller (resp. larger) than its first-order counterpart both.
As a result, the effective interaction between quasiparticles is reduced (resp. increase), tending to restore (resp. remove) the behavior of an ideal gas.
Even though this is true both in the
density and polarisation channel, the effect is
\approx 10
times larger in the density channel.

In the density response (Fig.

c_{0}>1
; there remains also a secondary peak near the edge of the quasiparticle-quasihole continuum for
c\lesssim 1

This secondary peak visibly shrinks as second-order corrections push the zero sound resonance away from the continuum. In the attractive case, interactions tend to smoothen the sharp behavior at the continuum edge, and the density response
becomes a broad, featureless spectral function.

II.2.4

We now aim to include the collisional correction

Since
\nu_{\pm}^{\rm cl}
does not depend on energy, we perform an averaging to eliminate the dependence of the collision integral on
y
and
y^{\prime}
:

\int^{+\infty}_{-\infty}\textrm{d}y^{\prime}\ \mathcal{S}(y,\pm y^{\prime})=\overline{\Gamma}(y),\qquad\int^{+\infty}_{-\infty}\textrm{d}y\ g(y)\bar{\Gamma}(y)=\frac{4\pi^{2}}{3}

(217)

We then obtain the following equation for
\nu_{\pm}
:

\nu_{\pm}(\theta)=-\frac{\cos{\theta}}{c-\cos{\theta}}\left(1-\frac{F^{\pm}(0)}{2}\rho_{\pm}\right)-\frac{4\pi^{2}}{3}\frac{\textrm{i}}{\omega_{0}\tau}\frac{1}{c-\cos{\theta}}\left(\nu^{\rm cl}_{\pm}(\theta)+\int\frac{\textrm{d}\Omega^{\prime}}{2\pi}\ \ \mathcal{N}_{\pm}(\alpha)\ \nu^{\rm cl}_{\pm}(\theta^{\prime})\right)

(218)

where the angular collision kernel
\mathcal{N}_{\pm}(\alpha)
and its expansion in Legendre polynomials are given in Appendix
C
.

We then integrate over
\theta
so as to obtain
\rho_{\pm}
on the left-hand side.
In the collision integral,
\rho_{\pm}^{\rm cl}
can be replaced by the total density
\rho_{\pm}
, neglecting terms of order
1/(\omega_{0}\tau)^{2}
.
We thus obtain the following solution:

\rho_{\pm}(c)=\frac{-2B_{00}(c)}{1-F^{\pm}(0)B_{00}(c)+\frac{\textrm{i}}{\omega_{0}\tau}\frac{2\pi^{2}}{3}\frac{C_{\pm}(c)}{B_{00}(c)}}

(219)

where the collisional contribution
C_{\pm}
is given by:

C_{\pm}(c)=\int^{\pi}_{0}\textrm{d}\theta\ \frac{\sin{\theta}\cos{\theta}}{(c-\cos{\theta})^{2}}+\int\frac{\textrm{d}\Omega^{\prime}}{2\pi}\textrm{d}\theta\ \frac{\sin{\theta}\cos{\theta^{\prime}}}{(c-\cos{\theta})(c-\cos{\theta^{\prime}})}\mathcal{N}_{\pm}(\alpha)

(220)

Since these integrals are dominated by the vicinity of
\theta=0
and
\theta^{\prime}=0
, the collision kernel
\mathcal{N}_{\pm}(\alpha)
can be replaced by its value at
\alpha=0
.
This can again be interpreted as a consequence of the quasi-longitudinal nature of zero sound in the weak-interaction regime.
We thus arrive at

C_{\pm}(c)\simeq-B_{00}^{\prime}(c)\left(1+2(c-1)\gamma^{2}(c)\ \mathcal{N}_{\pm}(0)\right)

(221)

with
\gamma(c)=\ln{\frac{c-1}{2}}
.
A more general calculation of the function
C_{\pm}
is given in Appendix
C
.

To obtain the collisional correction to the zero-sound velocity, we now solve the equation

\frac{1}{\rho_{\pm}(z_{0}^{\pm})}=0,\quad\text{with }z_{0}^{\pm}=c_{0}^{\pm}+{\delta c_{0}^{\pm}}.

(222)

Expanding the denominator of
\rho_{\pm}
in powers of
O(1/\omega_{0}\tau)
, we finally extract the collisional correction to zero sound, which is purely imaginary:

\delta c_{0}^{\pm}=\frac{2\pi^{2}}{3}\frac{\textrm{i}}{\omega_{0}\tau}\frac{C_{\pm}(c_{0}^{\pm})}{B^{\prime}_{00}(c_{0}^{\pm})}=-\frac{2\pi^{2}}{3}\frac{\textrm{i}}{\omega_{0}\tau}+O(c_{0}-1).

(223)

This result describes the broadening of the zero-sound resonance in the response functions
\chi_{\rho}(c)
and
\chi_{p}(c)
,
or equivalently, its exponential damping in the time domain.
It is worth noting that
\mathrm{Im}(c_{0})
depends on the collision probability
W
only through the mean collision time
\tau
,
which makes the product
\omega_{0}\tau,\mathrm{Im}(c_{0})
universal in weakly interacting Fermi liquids.

In this sense, the damping of zero sound differs from that of hydrodynamic sound (first sound),
which is sensitive—via the shear viscosity
\eta
—to the angular dependence of
W
,
and therefore varies with
k_{\rm F}a
in a way that differs significantly from
\tau
.

II.3
Numerical solution in the collisionless to hydrodynamic crossover

Between the weakly collisional regime studied in Section
II.2
and the hydrodynamic regime treated in Ref.
Taillat and H.
Kurkjian 2025
, there exists a smooth transition as a function of
\omega_{0}\tau

Khalatnikov and Abrikosov 110
;
Egilsson and Pethick 1977
.
In the following, we develop a numerical method that allows us to solve the transport equation (
191
) in this intermediate regime.

II.3.1
Numerical method

In order to solve the transport equation (
191
), we project
\nu_{\pm}
onto basis of orthogonal polynomials:

\nu_{\pm}(y,\theta)=\sum_{n,l\in\mathbb{N}}\nu^{l}_{n{\pm}}P_{l}(\cos{\theta})Q_{n}(y)

(224)

where the
P_{l}
are the Legendre polynomials.
The orthogonal polynomials
Q_{n}
for the energy dependence
Repplinger et al. 2025

are defined by
Q_{0}=1
,
Q_{1}=y
and

\displaystyle{\int^{\infty}_{-\infty}\frac{\textrm{d}y}{4\cosh^{2}\frac{y}{2}}Q_{n}(y)Q_{m}(y)=\delta_{n,m}||Q_{n}||^{2}}

(225)

\displaystyle{yQ_{n}=Q_{n+1}+\xi_{n}Q_{n-1}\ \text{ with }\ \xi_{n}=\frac{||Q_{n}||^{2}}{||Q_{n-1}||^{2}}}

(226)

The decomposition over the
Q_{n}
allows for an exact treatment of the energy dependance, beyond the relaxation time approximations,
which limit the quasiparticle distribution to its
n=0
component.
The decomposed transport equation reads

\left(\frac{\omega}{\omega_{0}}+\frac{\textrm{i}}{\omega_{0}\tau}\mathcal{M}_{\pm}^{l}\right)\vec{\nu}_{\pm}^{l}-\frac{l}{2l-1}\overline{F}^{\pm}_{l-1}\vec{\nu}_{\pm}^{l-1}-\frac{l+1}{2l+3}\overline{F}^{\pm}_{l+1}\vec{\nu}_{\pm}^{l+1}=-\delta_{l,1}\vec{u}_{0}

(227)

where we have introduced a set of vectors and matrices

\vec{\nu}^{l}_{\pm}=\begin{pmatrix}\nu_{0\pm}^{l}\\
\nu_{1\pm}^{l}\\
\vdots\end{pmatrix},\qquad\vec{u}_{0}=\begin{pmatrix}1\\
0\\
\vdots\end{pmatrix},\qquad\mathcal{U}_{0}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0&\cdots\\
0&0&0&\cdots\\
\vdots\end{pmatrix}

(228)

\overline{F}^{\pm}_{l}=\left(1+\frac{F^{\pm}_{l}}{2l+1}\mathcal{U}_{0}\right)=\begin{pmatrix}1+\frac{F_{l}^{\pm}}{2l+1}&0&0&\cdots\\
0&1&0&\cdots\\
\vdots&&\ddots\\
0&\cdots&\cdots&1\end{pmatrix}

(229)

The collision time
\tau
has been defined (
188
). The infinite matrix
\left(\mathcal{M}^{l}_{\pm}\right)_{nn^{\prime}}
follows from the decomposition of the collision kernel
\mathcal{N}(\textbf{p},\textbf{p}^{\prime})
(see Eq. (
164
)) over the orthogonal basis; its
expression in terms of
W
is given in Ref.
Taillat and H.
Kurkjian 2025
.

We now present a numerical scheme to solve the transport equation (
227
) based on a backward recurrence on
l
. Assuming that at rank
l+1
, the component
\vec{\nu}^{l+1}
has been linearly expressed in terms of
\vec{\nu}^{l}
, we can propagate the linear relation backward in
l
:

\vec{\nu}^{l}=\mathcal{H}^{l}\vec{\nu}^{l-1}

(230)

where we omited the
\pm
index for convenience. Numerically, we introduce a truncation parameter
n_{\rm max}
and represent the infinite matrix
\mathcal{H}^{l}
by a complex
n_{\rm max}\times n_{\rm max}
matrix. Substituting this relation into equation (
227
) for
l>1
, we derive the following backward recurrence relation on
\mathcal{H}^{l}
.

\mathcal{H}^{l}=\frac{l}{2l-1}\left(\left(\frac{\omega}{\omega_{0}}+\frac{\textrm{i}}{\omega_{0}\tau}\mathcal{M}^{l}\right)-\frac{l+1}{2l+3}\overline{F}_{l+1}\mathcal{H}^{l+1}\right)^{-1}\overline{F}_{l-1}

(231)

To initialize the recurrence, we introduce a cutoff
l_{\rm max}
and we assume
\mathcal{H}^{l_{\rm max}+1}=0
.
At the end of the backward recurrence, we solve the remaining
2n_{\rm max}\times 2n_{\rm max}
coupled system on
\vec{\nu}^{0}
and
\vec{\nu}^{1}

\begin{cases}\left(\frac{\omega}{\omega_{0}}+\frac{\textrm{i}}{\omega_{0}\tau}\mathcal{M}^{0}_{+}\right)\vec{\nu}^{0}_{+}=\frac{1}{3}\overline{F}^{+}_{1}\vec{\nu}^{1}_{+}\\
\left(\frac{\omega}{\omega_{0}}+\frac{\textrm{i}}{\omega_{0}\tau}\mathcal{M}^{1}_{+}\right)\vec{\nu}^{1}_{+}-\overline{F}_{0}^{+}\vec{\nu}^{0}_{+}-\frac{2}{5}\overline{F}_{2}^{+}\mathcal{H}^{2}_{+}\vec{\nu}^{1}_{+}=-\vec{u}_{0}\end{cases}

(232)

Here,
\mathcal{H}^{2}_{+}
is computed recursively, starting from
l=l_{\rm max}
.
We choose the values of
l_{\rm max}
and
n_{\rm max}
based on a convergence analysis. Selecting cutoffs that are too low may lead to non-physical oscillations in the response functions.
We note that
l_{\rm max}
and
n_{\rm max}
depend on the regime of
\omega_{0}\tau
under study.
In the collisionless regime, we can restrict ourselves to small values of
n_{\rm max}
.
This confirms the observation made in the previous section: the energy dependence of
\rho_{\pm}
is contained in the collision term, which is subdominant.
Conversely, in the hydrodynamic regime, small values of
l_{\rm max}
suffice.
The conserved quantities are at
l=0
or
1
and the non-conserved quantities at
l\geq 2
decay as
(\omega_{0}\tau)^{l}

Repplinger et al. 2025
.

II.3.2
Anisotropic driving potential for the polarisation

The polarisation response to the isotropic drive introduced in Eq. (
137
) vanishes as
\omega_{0}\tau
in the hydrodynamic regime. This is because such a drive couples to a dissipative component (
\nu^{1}_{0-}
).
To make the diffusive mode of polarisation observable, one should rather couple the drive directly to the conserved quantity
n_{\uparrow}-n_{\downarrow}
, in the
l=0
channel.
To do so, we assume that the driving potential can be varied independently with
q
and
p
:

\hat{H}_{\rm ext}=\sum_{\textbf{p}\in\mathcal{D}}U_{-}(\textbf{p},\textbf{q})\left(\hat{\gamma}^{\dagger}_{\textbf{p}+\textbf{q}/2,\uparrow}\hat{\gamma}_{\textbf{p}-\textbf{q}/2,\uparrow}-\hat{\gamma}^{\dagger}_{\textbf{p}+\textbf{q}/2,\downarrow}\hat{\gamma}_{\textbf{p}-\textbf{q}/2,\downarrow}\right)

(233)

The dependence of the driving potential on
p
is irrelevant, so that we can write:

U_{-}(\textbf{p},\textbf{q})=U_{-}(\textbf{q})u(\theta)

(234)

This change of
\hat{H}_{\rm ext}
modifies the source term in the polarisation transport equation:

\left(\frac{\omega}{\omega_{0}}-\cos{\theta}\right)\nu_{-}(y,\theta)+\cos\theta\left[u(\theta)-\frac{1}{2}\int\textrm{d}y^{\prime}\frac{\textrm{d}\Omega^{\prime}}{2\pi}F^{-}(\alpha)g(y^{\prime})\nu_{-}(y^{\prime},\theta^{\prime})\right]=-\textrm{i}I(y,\theta)

(235)

To couple the drive directly to the polarisation fluctuations, the product
u(\theta)\cos{\theta}
should have a non-vanishing
l=0
component, which can be achieved with
u(\theta)=\cos{\theta}
for example.
For simplicity, we omit here the components
l\geq 1
whose contribution is negligible in the hydrodynamic limit,
i.e.
we assume
u(\theta)\cos{\theta}=1
.
The set of equations to be solved at the end of the backward recurrence is then:

\begin{cases}\left(\frac{\omega}{\omega_{0}}+\frac{\textrm{i}}{\omega_{0}\tau}\mathcal{M}^{0}_{-}\right)\vec{\nu}^{0}_{-}=\frac{1}{3}\overline{F}^{-}_{1}\vec{\nu}^{1}_{-}-\vec{u}_{0}\\
\left(\frac{\omega}{\omega_{0}}+\frac{\textrm{i}}{\omega_{0}\tau}\mathcal{M}^{1}_{-}\right)\vec{\nu}^{1}_{-}-\overline{F}_{0}^{-}\vec{\nu}^{0}_{-}-\frac{2}{5}\overline{F}_{2}^{-}\mathcal{H}^{2}_{-}\vec{\nu}^{1}_{-}=0\end{cases}

(236)

III
Superfluid pairing of Landau quasiparticles

In this section, we use the Landau quasiparticles, and their effective Hamiltonian Eq. (
49
),
to describe (in principle exactly) the superfluid phase from the superfluid instability down to
T=0
.
Our description is valid provided superfluidity remains a weak phenomenon in the sense that

\Delta,\ T_{c}\ll\epsilon_{\rm F}

(238)

where
\Delta
is the superfluid order parameter and
T_{c}
is the critical temperature.
Weak fermionic superfluids should then be viewed as condensates of quasiparticle pairs
Vollhardt and Wolfle 1990
,
schematically depicted by Fig.
11
; this is a substantial improvement from the pairs of bare particles
interacting via the bare interaction (as described by BCS theory), or even from the frequent picture
of bare particles interacting via a screened interaction.
The head-on collisions
\textbf{p},-\textbf{p}\to\textbf{p}^{\prime},-\textbf{p}^{\prime}
among quasiparticles, described by the amplitude
\mathcal{A}
,
favor the pairing instability and the appearance of a nonzero pairing field.
We shall see that
the pairing collision amplitude
\mathcal{A}(\textbf{p},-\textbf{p}|\textbf{p}^{\prime},-\textbf{p}^{\prime})
must exhibit a logarithmic
divergence as
\Lambda/\epsilon_{\rm F}\to 0
to ensure the existence of a superfluid phase.

Figure 11
:
Cooper pairs (dashed ellipse) in a Fermi superfluid are pairs of
\uparrow
and
\downarrow
Landau quasiparticles (red and blue clouds). To first order,
the spin-
\uparrow
quasiparticle can be seen as a cloud of spin-
\downarrow
particles (blue dots) surrounding the original spin-
\uparrow
particle (red dot).

In our approach, the compatibility of the quasiparticle picture with the existence of a superfluid ground
state is tied to the cutoff
\Lambda
. Exciting a few pairs at the Fermi level (from
e.g.
the Fermi sea
or the superfluid ground state) will change the energy of the interacting system by
\approx\Delta
.
The corresponding transition in the noninteracting fluid is conversely quasi-resonant.
Thus the weak crossing condition on the spectrum
of the Fermi liquid (Eq. (
4
)) is compatible with the existence of a pair binding energy as long as

\Delta,\ T_{c}\ll\Lambda

(239)

III.1
Pairing equation

We formulate an evolution
equation that captures the onset of quasiparticle pairing in the normal phase
Lin and Sauls 2022
, as the system approaches the critical temperature
T\rightarrow T_{\rm c}^{+}
.
This equation is to the quasiparticle pairing field
\hat{\gamma}_{\sigma}\hat{\gamma}_{\sigma^{\prime}}
what the transport equation is to
the density field
\hat{\gamma}^{\dagger}_{\sigma}\hat{\gamma}_{\sigma}
. Although pairing is in principle not restricted to the singlet spin wavefunction
(as in
e.g.
the A-phase of
3
He), we have in mind here the case of ultracold fermions, where
the interactions among opposite spin quasiparticles
\mathcal{A}_{\uparrow\downarrow}
dominateand favor the formation of
\uparrow\downarrow
pairs. We thus restrict to spin-singlet pairs; the corresponding quantum
pairing field in momentum space is

\hat{d}^{\textbf{q}}_{\textbf{p}}=\hat{\gamma}_{-\textbf{p}-\textbf{q}/2,\downarrow}\hat{\gamma}_{\textbf{p}-\textbf{q}/2,\uparrow}

(240)

This operator effectively annihilates a pair of
\textbf{p}\uparrow,-\textbf{p}\downarrow
quasiparticles with a center-of-mass momentum
q
.
By definition, its expectation value vanishes in an equilibrium state of the normal phase
\langle\hat{d}^{\textbf{q}}_{\textbf{p}}\rangle_{\rm eq}=0
for
T>T_{c}
.
However, fluctuations of
\hat{d}
are possible for example under the influence
of an external potential. The pair susceptibility, or pair response function,
then quantifies the magnitude of these fluctuations with respect to the drive intensity.
We are looking for a divergence of the pair susceptibility, that would signal that the normal phase becomes unstable, and the system undergoes a phase transition.

To compute the pair susceptibility, we introduce an external perturbation
\hat{H}_{\rm ext}
that couples directly to the pair field:

\hat{H}_{\rm ext}=\phi(-\textbf{q},t)\sum_{\textbf{p}}\left(\hat{d}^{-\textbf{q}}_{\textbf{p}}\right)^{\dagger}+\textrm{h.c.}

(241)

where the external pairing source oscillates at frequency
\omega
,
\phi(\textbf{q},t)=\phi(\textbf{q})\textrm{e}^{-\textrm{i}\omega t}
, causing
\hat{d}^{\textbf{q}}_{\textbf{p}}
to oscillate at frequency
\omega-2\mu
.
We expand the state of the system about a thermal quasiparticle state
\hat{\varrho}=\hat{\varrho}_{\rm eq}(T)+\delta\hat{\varrho}
(see
Eq. (
129
) for the definition of
\hat{\varrho}_{\rm eq}(T)
). Within linear response, the deviation from equilibrium is controlled by the drive intensity
\delta\hat{\varrho}=O(\phi/\epsilon_{\rm F})
.
We then evolve
\hat{d}^{\textbf{q}}_{\textbf{p}}
according to the Heisenberg equation of motion

i\partial_{t}\hat{d}^{\textbf{q}}_{\textbf{p}}=[\hat{d}^{\textbf{q}}_{\textbf{p}},\hat{H}+\hat{H}_{\rm ext}]

(242)

The derivation proceeds analogously to the derivation of the transport equation in Sec.
I.3
. The streaming term arises from the diagonal part of the Hamiltonian:

[\hat{d}^{\textbf{q}}_{\textbf{p}},\hat{H}_{2}+\hat{H}_{4}^{\rm d}+\hat{H}_{\rm ext}]=\left(\hat{\epsilon}_{\textbf{p}-\textbf{q}/2,\uparrow}+\hat{\epsilon}_{-\textbf{p}-\textbf{q}/2,\downarrow}\right)\hat{d}^{\textbf{q}}_{\textbf{p}}+(1-n^{\rm eq}_{\textbf{p}+\textbf{q}/2}-n^{\rm eq}_{\textbf{p}-\textbf{q}/2}){\phi}(\textbf{q})+O(\phi)^{2}

(243)

with
\hat{\epsilon}_{\textbf{p},\sigma}={\epsilon}_{\textbf{p}}
to leading order in
T/T_{\rm F}
.
In the quartic terms stemming from
\hat{H}_{4}^{\rm x}
, we inject the cumulant expansion Eq. (
119
) :

[\hat{d}^{\textbf{q}}_{\textbf{p}},\hat{H}_{4}^{\rm x}]=(1-n^{\rm eq}_{\textbf{p}+\textbf{q}/2}-n^{\rm eq}_{\textbf{p}-\textbf{q}/2})\frac{1}{L^{3}}\sum_{\textbf{p}^{\prime}}\mathcal{A}_{\uparrow\downarrow}\left(\textbf{p}-\frac{\textbf{q}}{2},-\textbf{p}-\frac{\textbf{q}}{2}|-\textbf{p}^{\prime}-\frac{\textbf{q}}{2},\textbf{p}^{\prime}-\frac{\textbf{q}}{2}\right)\hat{d}^{\textbf{q}}_{\textbf{p}^{\prime}}+\hat{J}_{\textbf{p}}

(244)

We have regrouped the quartic cumulants
(\hat{a}\hat{b}\hat{c}\hat{d})_{c}
in a collision integral
\hat{J}_{\textbf{p}}
which is negligible for the calculation of
T_{c}
. Note that the interaction between same-spin quasiparticles
\mathcal{A}_{\sigma\sigma}
contributes to
\hat{J}_{\textbf{p}}

but not to the partially contracted terms in Eq. (
244
). This is specific to the normal phase where the anomalous averages
\langle\hat{\gamma}_{\downarrow}\hat{\gamma}_{\uparrow}\rangle_{\rm eq}
vanish.
The pair transport equation of a Fermi liquid is then:

\begin{split}\left(\omega-\epsilon_{\textbf{p}-\textbf{q}/2}-\epsilon_{\textbf{p}+\textbf{q}/2}+2\mu\right)&\hat{d}^{\textbf{q}}_{\textbf{p}}=(\overline{n}^{\rm eq}_{\textbf{p}+\textbf{q}/2}-n^{\rm eq}_{\textbf{p}-\textbf{q}/2})\\
&\times\left\{\frac{1}{L^{3}}\sum_{\textbf{p}^{\prime}}\mathcal{A}_{\uparrow\downarrow}\left(\textbf{p}-\frac{\textbf{q}}{2},-\textbf{p}-\frac{\textbf{q}}{2}|-\textbf{p}^{\prime}-\frac{\textbf{q}}{2},\textbf{p}^{\prime}-\frac{\textbf{q}}{2}\right)\hat{d}^{\textbf{q}}_{\textbf{p}^{\prime}}+\phi(\textbf{q})\right\}\end{split}

(245)

III.2
Uniform pair susceptibility

The Thouless criterion defines
T_{c}
as the temperature at which the pair susceptibility acquires a singularity
for static and uniform perturbations, that is for
\omega=0
and
q=0
.
Restricting our pairing equation Eq. (
245
) first to
q=0
, we obtain:

\left(\omega-2(\epsilon_{\textbf{p}}-\mu)\right)d(\textbf{p})=(1-2n^{\rm eq}_{\textbf{p}})\left\{\frac{1}{L^{3}}\sum_{\textbf{p}^{\prime}}\mathcal{A}_{\uparrow\downarrow}(\textbf{p},-\textbf{p}|\textbf{p}^{\prime},-\textbf{p}^{\prime})\ d(\textbf{p}^{\prime})+{\phi}\right\}

(246)

When superfluidity occurs in a high partial wave,
d
has a non trivial dependence on the angle between
p
and a reference direction; we focus
here on s-wave pairing, for which the pairing function is isotropic
d(\textbf{p})=d(p)
.
The angular part of the integral equations is then a mere angular average of the
pairing amplitude

\overline{\mathcal{A}_{\uparrow\downarrow}}(p,p^{\prime},\Lambda)\equiv\frac{1}{2}\int^{\pi}_{0}\textrm{d}\alpha\ \sin{\alpha}\ \mathcal{A}_{\uparrow\downarrow}(\textbf{p},-\textbf{p}|\textbf{p}^{\prime},-\textbf{p}^{\prime})

(247)

where
\alpha=(\widehat{\textbf{p},\textbf{p}^{\prime}})
.
For the radial dependence, we introduce the following change of variable:

d(p)=\frac{1-2n^{\rm eq}_{\textbf{p}}}{\beta\left(\omega-2(\epsilon_{\textbf{p}}-\mu)\right)}D(y)=\frac{\text{tanh}(y/2)}{\beta\omega-2y}\ D(y),\qquad y=\beta\left(\epsilon_{\textbf{p}}-\mu\right)

(248)

with
\beta=1/T
. This reparametrization
may seem analogous to the change of variable

\delta n\to\nu
(see Eq. (
170
)) performed on the density field to focus on the low-energy region.
It extracts a prefactor that depends rapidly on energy from the unknown function
d
, and
we may expect
D
to be a smooth function of
y
.
However, the prefactor
\text{tanh}(y/2)/(\beta\omega-2y)
here does not vanish at large
y
. In consequence,
what restricts us to low energies is rather the finite energy width of the amplitude
\mathcal{A}
. As in Eq. (
77
), we must
then separate the unconstrained amplitude
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
from the low-energy projector
\Pi_{\Lambda}
:

\mathcal{A}_{\uparrow\downarrow}(\textbf{p},-\textbf{p}|\textbf{p}^{\prime},-\textbf{p}^{\prime})=\mathcal{A}^{\prime}_{\uparrow\downarrow}(\textbf{p},-\textbf{p}|\textbf{p}^{\prime},-\textbf{p}^{\prime})\ \Pi_{\Lambda}(2(\epsilon_{\textbf{p}^{\prime}}-\epsilon_{\textbf{p}}))

(249)

In presence of
\Pi_{\Lambda}
, we can now restrict the energy integrals to the low-energy region:

\frac{1}{L^{3}}\sum_{\textbf{p}^{\prime}}\Pi_{\Lambda}(2(\epsilon_{\textbf{p}^{\prime}}-\epsilon_{\textbf{p}}))\to\frac{m^{*}Tp_{\rm F}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\Pi_{\Lambda}\left(2T(y-y^{\prime})\right)\textrm{d}y^{\prime}\int_{0}^{\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\sin\alpha\textrm{d}\alpha\textrm{d}\phi

(250)

where
\phi
is an azimuthal angle locating
\textbf{p}^{\prime}
in a spherical frame of axis
p
. Up to corrections in
O(T/T_{\rm F})
, we can approximate the pairing amplitude
\mathcal{A}_{\uparrow\downarrow}
by its
value for
p=p^{\prime}=p_{\rm F}
. The integral equation focused on the low-energy region becomes

D(y)=\frac{m^{*}p_{\rm F}}{2\pi^{2}}\overline{\mathcal{A}_{\uparrow\downarrow}}(\Lambda)\int_{-\frac{\beta\Lambda}{2}+y}^{\frac{\beta\Lambda}{2}+y}\frac{\textrm{d}y^{\prime}}{\beta\omega-2y^{\prime}}\ D(y^{\prime})\tanh{\frac{y^{\prime}}{2}}+{\phi}+O\left(\frac{T}{T_{\rm F}}\right)

(251)

where
\overline{\mathcal{A}_{\uparrow\downarrow}}(\Lambda)=\overline{\mathcal{A}_{\uparrow\downarrow}}(p_{\rm F},p_{\rm F},\Lambda)
. The only remaining energy dependence on the
right-hand side is the integration interval
[-\frac{\beta\Lambda}{2}+y,\frac{\beta\Lambda}{2}+y]
whose centre is shifted from 0 by
y
. To leading order
in
1/\beta\Lambda
, we
can then approximate the pair field
D
by a constant

D(y)=D_{0}+O\left(\frac{y}{\beta\Lambda}\right)

(252)

The integral equation is now trivial, and yields the pair susceptibility

\chi_{\rm pair}(\omega)\equiv\frac{D_{0}(\omega)}{{\phi}}=\frac{1/\overline{\mathcal{A}_{\uparrow\downarrow}}(\Lambda)}{1/\overline{\mathcal{A}_{\uparrow\downarrow}}(\Lambda)+\frac{m^{*}p_{\rm F}}{2\pi^{2}}\mathcal{N}_{\Lambda}(\omega)}

(253)

with
\mathcal{N}_{\Lambda}(\omega)
defined as:

\mathcal{N}_{\Lambda}(\omega)=\int^{\beta\Lambda/2}_{-\beta\Lambda/2}\frac{\textrm{d}y^{\prime}}{2y^{\prime}-\beta\omega}\ \tanh{\frac{y^{\prime}}{2}}

(254)

The critical temperature can finally be determined by applying Thouless’ criterion to the pair susceptibility:

{\chi_{\rm pair}^{-1}(\omega=0,T=T_{c})}=0\iff\frac{1}{\overline{\mathcal{A}}_{\uparrow\downarrow}(\Lambda)}+\frac{m^{*}p_{\rm F}}{2\pi^{2}}\mathcal{N}_{\Lambda}(0,T_{c})=0

(255)

In the limit where
\beta\Lambda\gg 1
, the integral
\mathcal{N}_{\Lambda}(0)
diverges logarithmically

\mathcal{N}_{\Lambda}(0,T)=\ln{\left(\frac{\Lambda}{\pi T}\right)}+\gamma+O\left(\frac{T}{\Lambda}\right)

(256)

where
\gamma\approx 0.577
is the Euler-Mascheroni constant.
This divergence is compensated by a divergence of the s-wave pairing amplitude,
which we write generically as

\frac{1}{\overline{\mathcal{A}_{\uparrow\downarrow}}(\Lambda)}=-\frac{m^{*}p_{\rm F}}{2\pi^{2}}\left(\ln{\frac{\Lambda}{\epsilon_{\rm F}}}+\alpha_{\uparrow\downarrow}\right)+O\left(\frac{\Lambda}{\epsilon_{\rm F}}\right)

(257)

This expression of
1/{\mathcal{A}_{\uparrow\downarrow}}(\Lambda)
in the
s
-wave channel was postulated by Popov
Popov 1987b

and demonstrated (in 2D and for all Fourier component
m
of
\mathcal{A}_{\uparrow\downarrow}(\theta)
) by Chitov and Sénéchal
Chitov and Sénéchal 1995

using the renormalisation group flow.
We introduced here an effective parameter
{\alpha_{\uparrow\downarrow}}
of the low-energy theory, which we interpreted as the background
value of
1/\overline{\mathcal{A}_{\uparrow\downarrow}}
, over which the logarithmic divergence develops.
This parameter sets the critical temperature to

\frac{T_{\rm c}}{T_{\rm F}}=\frac{\textrm{e}^{\gamma}}{\pi}\ \textrm{e}^{-\alpha_{\uparrow\downarrow}}

(258)

This relation is valid generically in Fermi liquids subject to a weak superfluid instability.
It is non-perturbative and exact if the effective parameter
\alpha_{\uparrow\downarrow}
is known exactly rather than expanded in powers of the
interaction strength;
\alpha_{\uparrow\downarrow}
must however remain large and positive to maintain the validity of the quasiparticle picture,
through the inequality
T_{c}\ll T_{\rm F}
.

Remark that the pairing amplitude must be attractive,
\overline{\mathcal{A}_{\uparrow\downarrow}}<0
,
to trigger the superfluid transition.
It must also display a logarithmic divergence with
\Lambda
, which does not seem guaranteed,
for example if the bare potential vanishes for head-on collisions:
V(\textbf{p},-\textbf{p}|\textbf{p}^{\prime},-\textbf{p}^{\prime})=0
.
In this case, there is no divergence in the pair susceptibility (on the contrary, it is logarithmically suppressed with
\Lambda
),
i.e.
there is no superfluid phase.

Extending our low-energy effective theory further into the superfluid phase, we now calculate the order parameter at
T=0
,
through the gap equation:

\Delta(\textbf{p})=-\sum_{\textbf{p}^{\prime}}\mathcal{A}_{\uparrow\downarrow}(\textbf{p},-\textbf{p}|\textbf{p}^{\prime},-\textbf{p}^{\prime})\frac{\Delta(\textbf{p}^{\prime})}{2\sqrt{(\epsilon_{\textbf{p}^{\prime}}-\mu)^{2}+\Delta^{2}(\textbf{p}^{\prime})}}

(259)

Computing the integral restricted to the low-energy region, and assuming
the logarithmically divergent expression (
257
) of
\overline{\mathcal{A}_{\uparrow\downarrow}}

we obtain

\frac{\Delta}{\epsilon_{\rm F}}=\textrm{e}^{-\alpha_{\uparrow\downarrow}}

(260)

The ratio
\Delta/T_{c}=\pi/\textrm{e}^{\gamma}\simeq 1.764
found by BCS theory is thus
universal to all superfluids made of Landau quasiparticles
Popov 1987c
; it is well verified
in superfluid
3
He
Masuhara et al. 2000
, even though the fluid is strongly interacting (
F_{0}^{+}>10
). Deviations from
the BCS ratio (as
e.g.
in a unitary Fermi gas
Schirotzek et al. 2008
;
Ku et al. 2012
) may then be interpreted
as evidences of a non-Fermi liquid behavior.

III.3
Application to the contact Fermi gas: the Gor’kov-Melik-Barkhudarov correction to
T_{c}

We return to the Fermi gas with contact interactions. BCS theory describes pairing of particles
under the effect of the bare interactions, which provides a first approximation of the critical temperature:

\frac{T_{\rm c}^{\rm BCS}}{T_{\rm F}}=\frac{8\textrm{e}^{\gamma-2}}{\pi}\ \textrm{e}^{{\pi}/{2k_{\rm F}a}}

(261)

This perturbative expression is valid to leading order in
k_{\rm F}a
for
\text{ln}(T_{c}/T_{\rm F})
. Therefore, it
makes an uncontrolled error on
T_{c}/T_{\rm F}
.
To go beyond BCS approximation, Gor’kov and Melik-Barkhudarov
Gor’kov and Melik-Barkhudarov 1958
performed a second-order diagrammatic
calculation, in which they introduce in particular a dressed Green’s function
and an effective interaction.

The GMB correction is often understood
Heiselberg et al. 2000
;
Chen 2016
;
Pisani et al. 2018
as the result
of the screening of the pairing interactions among particles.
Our low-energy effective theory provides a simple and more general interpretation of the corrections to the BCS
gap and critical temperature as the result of the renormalisation of the particles into Landau quasiparticles.
In this picture, the GMB correction follows from a second order calculation of the effective parameters
of the theory, in particular of
\mathcal{A}_{\uparrow\downarrow}
.

Averaging expression (
94
) of
\mathcal{A}_{\uparrow\downarrow}
over
\theta_{13}=\alpha
and for
\theta_{12}=\pi
yields

\overline{\mathcal{A}_{\uparrow\downarrow}}(\Lambda)=g+g^{2}\frac{m^{*}p_{\rm F}}{2\pi^{2}}\left[\ln{\frac{\Lambda}{E_{\rm F}}}+\frac{7}{3}(1-2\ln{2})\right]+O(g^{3})

(262)

We may now identify the parameter
\alpha_{\uparrow\downarrow}
in the expansion of
1/\overline{\mathcal{A}_{\uparrow\downarrow}}(\Lambda)
:

\alpha_{\uparrow\downarrow}=-\frac{\pi}{2k_{\rm F}a}-\frac{7}{3}(\ln{2}-1)+O(k_{\rm F}a)

(263)

This pairing parameter is large and positive to leading order in
k_{\rm F}a<0
, which guarantees
the existence of a (weak) superfluid phase. It is however reduced by the second-order correction
which weakens superfluidity, and reduces the critical temperature:

{T^{\rm GMB}_{\rm c}}{}=\frac{\textrm{e}^{\gamma}}{\pi}\left(\frac{2}{\textrm{e}}\right)^{7/3}\textrm{e}^{\pi/2k_{\rm F}a}\ T_{\rm F}=\frac{T^{\rm BCS}_{c}}{(4\textrm{e})^{1/3}}

(264)

with
(4\textrm{e})^{1/3}\approx 2.2
. Corrections beyond GMB stemming from the third-order calculation of

\alpha_{\uparrow\downarrow}
are small,
i.e
of order
O(k_{\rm F}a)
, in both
T_{c}/T_{\rm F}
and
\ln(T_{c}/T_{\rm F})
.

Whereas the corrections to second order in
k_{\rm F}a
coming from the renormalisation of particles into quasiparticles
involve only the
\mathcal{A}_{\uparrow\downarrow}
collision amplitude, and can therefore be understood as a “screening” effect,
we note that this picture is not general and would fail to capture corrections to e.g. the effective mass to higher order in
k_{\rm F}a

or in more complex fermionic fluids.

Conclusion

Using a new renormalisation scheme, we have formulated an intuitive and controlled
construction of the Landau quasiparticles and the effective Hamiltonian
governing their dynamics.
Instead of the usual momentum cutoff, we introduce an energy cutoff

\Lambda
that separates resonant from off-resonant couplings.
In this framework, we interpret the quasiparticle annihilation operator
\hat{\gamma}

as the bare operator
\hat{a}
dressed only by the
off-resonant couplings. This dressing is implemented through a
unitary transformation, which becomes a Continuous Unitary Transformation (CUT) in the limit of infinitesimal variations of
\Lambda
.

To truncate the infinite series generated when expressing the Hamiltonian in terms
of
\hat{\gamma}
and
\hat{\gamma}^{\dagger}
, we introduced the fluctuations of the
density field
\delta(\hat{\gamma}^{\dagger}_{\alpha}\hat{\gamma}_{\beta})
around its Fermi-sea
expectation value.
Truncated to terms quadratic in these fluctuations, our effective Hamiltonian
contains the functional of Fermi liquid theory via the diagonal terms (
\alpha=\beta
).
Crucially, the same truncation also retains the full collision amplitude

\mathcal{A}_{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}(\textbf{p}_{1},\textbf{p}_{2}|\textbf{p}_{3},\textbf{p}_{4})
encoded in the off-diagonal terms
\alpha\neq\beta
.
This provides a single Hamiltonian describing both the interactions and the collisions
of Landau quasiparticles, unifying ingredients that are usually treated separately.
The interaction function
f
, the forward scattering amplitude
and the BCS pairing amplitude appear as different limits of a general amplitude
\mathcal{A}
regularized by
\Lambda
.

Armed with this effective Hamiltonian, we proposed a demonstration of the quasiparticle Boltzmann
equation exploiting the validity of the Born-Markov approximation in the quasiparticle picture.
We solved this Boltzmann equation exactly from the collisionless to the hydrodynamic regime
by decomposing the quasiparticle distribution on a basis of orthogonal functions.
Applying the effective picture to an atomic Fermi gas with contact interactions, we showed
how the use of Landau quasiparticles systematically improves the weak-coupling approximations,
in particular the RPA approximation on the speed of zero sound
c_{0}
,
and the BCS approximation on the superfluid gap and critical temperature.
In particular the celebrated Gork’ov-Melik Barkhudarov log-perturbative correction to
T_{c}

and
\Delta
emerges here as a direct manifestation of the quasiparticle dressing.

Extensions of this work could address the hydrodynamic
regime where a normal quasiparticle fluid and a quasiparticle condensate coexist.
The Boltzmann and pairing equations derived here in the normal phase are a natural
starting point for a microscopic derivation of the two-fluid
hydrodynamics of Fermi systems
Khalatnikov 2000
;
Vollhardt and Wolfle 1990
.
More generally, the present approach offers a versatile framework
for exploring transport, collective modes and pairing phenomena in a broad class of Fermi fluids.

Appendix C
Collision effects in the collisionless regime

In this appendix we present the calculation of the function
C_{\pm}
introduced (
220
):

C_{\pm}(c)=\int^{\pi}_{0}\mathrm{d}\theta\ \frac{\sin{\theta}\cos{\theta}}{(c-\cos{\theta})^{2}}+\int^{2\pi}_{0}\frac{\mathrm{d}\phi^{\prime}}{2\pi}\int^{\pi}_{0}\mathrm{d}\theta\mathrm{d}\theta^{\prime}\ \frac{\sin{\theta}\sin{\theta^{\prime}}\cos{\theta^{\prime}}}{(c-\cos{\theta})(c-\cos{\theta^{\prime}})}\mathcal{N}_{\pm}(\alpha)

(301)

with the angular collision kernel and its projection on Legendre polynomials given by:

\mathcal{N}_{\pm}(\alpha)=\frac{\Omega_{E\pm}(\alpha)-2\Omega_{S\pm}(\alpha)}{\Omega_{\Gamma}}=\sum_{l}\mathcal{N}_{\pm}^{l}(\alpha)P_{l}(\cos{\alpha})

(302)

We use the addition theorem:

\int^{2\pi}_{0}\frac{\mathrm{d}\phi^{\prime}}{2\pi}P_{l}(\cos(\alpha))=P_{l}(\cos{\theta})P_{l}(\cos{\theta^{\prime}})

(303)

This allows us to factorize the integrals over
u=\cos\theta
and
u^{\prime}=\cos\theta^{\prime}
in
C_{\pm}
:

C_{\pm}(c)=\int^{1}_{-1}\mathrm{d}u\ \frac{u}{(c-u)^{2}}+\sum_{l}\mathcal{N}_{\pm}^{l}\int^{1}_{-1}\mathrm{d}u\ \frac{P_{l}(u)}{c-u}\ \int^{1}_{-1}\mathrm{d}u^{\prime}\ \frac{u^{\prime}P_{l}(u^{\prime})}{c-u^{\prime}}

(304)

The different integrals are given by:

\int^{1}_{-1}\mathrm{d}u\ \frac{u}{(c-u)^{2}}=-B_{00}^{\prime}(c)

(305)

\int^{1}_{-1}\mathrm{d}u\ \frac{P_{l}(u)}{c-u}=2R_{l}(c)

(306)

\int^{1}_{-1}\mathrm{d}u^{\prime}\ \frac{u^{\prime}P_{l}(u^{\prime})}{c-u^{\prime}}=2cR_{l}(c)-2\delta_{l,0}

(307)

where we have introduced the Legendre functions of the second kind
Gradshteyn and Ryzhik 1994
. The contribution of collisions is therefore finally contained in the following formula:

C_{\pm}(c)=-B_{00}^{\prime}(c)+4\sum_{l}\mathcal{N}^{l}_{\pm}R_{l}(c)(cR_{l}(c)-\delta_{l,0})

(308)

In fact, this last formula is quite general, as the characteristics of the interactions are contained in the collision parameter
\mathcal{N}_{\pm}^{l}
.
We can now focus on the asymptotic behavior when
c
tends exponentially to
1
.
Note that:

\frac{R_{0}(c)(cR_{0}(c)-1)}{B_{00}^{\prime}(c)}\underset{\gamma\to-\infty}{\sim}-\gamma^{2}\textrm{e}^{\gamma}

(309)

Similarly, for
l>0
:

\frac{R_{l}^{2}(c)}{B_{00}^{\prime}(c)}\underset{\gamma\to-\infty}{\sim}-\gamma^{2}\textrm{e}^{\gamma}

(310)

In all cases, we can rewrite the function
C_{\pm}
in the limit where
c
tends exponentially to
1
as:

C_{\pm}(c)\simeq-B_{00}^{\prime}(c)\left(1+4{\gamma^{2}(c)\text{e}^{\gamma}}\ \mathcal{N}_{\pm}(0)\right)

(311)

where we have recognized that
\sum_{l}\mathcal{N}_{\pm}^{l}=\mathcal{N}_{\pm}(0)
.
