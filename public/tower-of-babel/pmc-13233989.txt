Road-Salt Induced Salinization of Urban Lakes and Wetlands: Implications for Monitoring
Authors: Madison E Silver, Isaac Armstrong, Erin K Cameron, Andrew S Medeiros
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRoad-salt-induced salinization can alter the biogeochemical properties of urban freshwaters, which are also vulnerable to multiple contemporary stressors (e.g., eutrophication, acidification). Therefore, it is important to characterize the degree of salinization and the relationship between chloride and other environmental parameters. Here, we investigate the salinization of urban lakes and wetlands in Halifax Regional Municipality, Nova Scotia, Canada, a lake-dense region that has experienced significant increases in chloride concentrations over the last few decades. We characterize recent lake salinization based on 2022–2024 monitoring data of 72 lakes and provide the first appraisal of wetland salinization in the region. Mixed effects models were used to determine the influence of (a) sampling location and timing on lake chloride concentrations, and (b) run-off input type, distance, and sample depth on wetland soil salinity. Overall, lakes and wetlands were found to be highly salinized. Chloride concentrations were significantly related to other salt constituents and metals, including Ba, Cu, Mn, and Sr. Chloride concentrations were significantly higher in lake deep-water samples than respective outlet samples (p < 0.001), and higher in spring than summer (p < 0.001), and this difference was more pronounced in high Cl− lakes. Measures of salinity were elevated in the deeper (10–20 cm) soil profile compared to the upper (0–10 cm) layer, suggesting possible downward movement of Na. Our research provides a better understanding of spatial and temporal trends in salinization, which helps inform monitoring strategies. Further research is needed to understand drivers of salinity in urban wetlands and the ecological impacts of chloride in highly salinized urban lakes.

Introduction

The widespread use of chloride-based deicing salts on roadways is the likely cause of numerous impacts observed in lakes and wetlands in northern latitudes (Hill and Sadowski 2016; Kaushal et al. 2018; Dugan et al. 2020). Road deicing salts may affect nutrient and metal cycling in soils by mobilizing metals, accelerating NaCl leaching, altering nitrogen and phosphorus ratios, altering soil structure, enhancing soil erosion, and reducing wetland filtration (Bäckström et al. 2004; Kim and Korestky 2013; van Dijk et al. 2015; Walker et al. 2021). Up to 5% of deicing salt weight can be composed of trace elements, including heavy metals (Michigan Department of Transportation 1993; Granato et al. 1995). The mobilization of metals can contaminate groundwater (Amrhein et al. 1992) and bioaccumulate in food webs (Bäckström et al. 2004). Increased salinity can impact vegetation richness, seedling density, and biomass (Walker et al. 2021). The ability of soils to buffer downstream lakes and rivers against chloride is variable, and most likely limited to low concentrations of salt (McGuire and Judd 2020). Freshwater systems in urban areas often have chloride concentrations above chronic water quality guidelines (Hill and Sadowski 2016). This can enhance mortality and decrease reproduction of freshwater biota (Arnott et al. 2020), which can cascade across higher trophic levels (Lind et al. 2018).

While chloride (Cl−) is naturally present in low concentrations in freshwater systems, elevated concentrations occur due to anthropogenic practices and are considered a reliable proxy for deicing salts (Dugan et al. 2017). However, the extent and magnitude of chloride contamination can vary due to environmental factors. The salinization of inland waters has been strongly correlated to impervious surface coverage (such as roads and sidewalks), urban development, and stormwater discharge (Todd and Kaltenecker 2012; Kaushal et al. 2018; Bermarija et al. 2023), with watersheds that have over 25% of area development being more likely to exceed chronic chloride guidelines (Scott et al. 2019). In areas where road salts can be attributed to a point-source, road salt impacts have been found to decrease with distance from roads (Eaton et al. 1999; Bäckstorm et al. 2004). In coastal areas, it can be more difficult to disentangle chloride inputs from road salt from the salt deposition from precipitation, and background concentrations of chloride can vary with distance from the ocean (Underwood et al. 1988). A recent overview of salinization effects of urban wetlands identified the need to assess the influence of salinization and variation in salt sources on urban wetland biogeochemistry (Kinsman-Costello et al. 2023).

In Canada, it is estimated that about 4 million tonnes of road deicing salt are used every winter, leading to the release of 3 million tonnes of chloride into the environment. In 1997–1998, Nova Scotia was found to have the highest loadings of chloride road salt per unit land area of all Canadian provinces (Environment Canada and Health Canada 2001). In Halifax, the capital of Nova Scotia, sodium chloride (NaCl) is primarily used for winter deicing due to its effectiveness, ease of use, and low cost. The Halifax Regional Municipality (HRM) has >1000 lakes which provide recreational and ecological services (Fig. 1; Halifax Regional Municipality 2025a); due to the high road density and loadings, these lakes are vulnerable to road-salt inputs. Regional increases in chloride and salinity since the beginning of road salt usage in 1958 have been well-documented (Gorham 1957; Watt et al. 1979; Clement et al. 2007; Clement and Gordon 2019). Chloride concentrations in twenty-three surveyed Halifax lakes increased by 172% between 1955 and 1977, presumably due to road deicing salts (Watt et al. 1979). Decadal synoptic water quality surveys showed a mean chloride increase of almost 200% between 1980 and 1991 and further significant increases from 1991 to 2011, particularly for lakes in developed watersheds (Clement and Gordon 2019). A recent study estimated chloride loading rates from areas with a “road” land-use in the Halifax region at 804–964 g/m2/year, and found that variation in chloride concentrations was significantly stormwater pipe density, percent urban cover, and road density (Bermarija et al. 2023).

Despite the wealth of monitoring data for HRM Lakes, it is still not well-understood how chloride co-varies with other limnological parameters, which is important as urban lakes increasingly experience multiple stressors (e.g., eutrophication, climate change, chemical pollution; Teurlincx et al. 2019). Lakes in Nova Scotia are particularly sensitive to acidification due to the lower buffering capacity of the regional bedrock (Clair et al. 2007), and lakes in the Halifax region have had significant increases in nutrients, metals, and dissolved organic content over the past 40 years, with lakes reporting metal concentrations over national aquatic guidelines (Doucet et al. 2023). Further, considerably less is known about the impacts of road salt on urban wetlands, which are viewed as assets that provide esthetic value and key habitat for urban wildlife (Manuel 2003) and act as buffers reducing pollution to lakes and ponds (Barten 1987). Here, we investigate the salinization of freshwater systems in the HRM by assessing lake water chemistry and surface organic soils from urban wetlands with the overall goals of (1) characterizing salinization of urban wetlands and freshwaters relative to environmental quality guidelines, (2) assessing how salinity variables co-vary with other environmental parameters, with a focus on metals, and (3) determining the influence of sampling strategies (e.g., seasonality, location) on measured values. Knowledge of how road salt influences urban freshwater chemistry is important for urban planning, and understanding how sampling decisions (time, location, distance, and depth) affect the values measured can inform management practices.

Methods

Study Area

The HRM has a humid, continental climate (Köppen Dfb), characterized by warm summers and mild, snowy winters. The 1991–2020 climate normals suggest an average daily temperature of −6.9 °C and a yearly precipitation of ~1400 mm (Environment and Climate Change Canada (ECCC) 2026). The regional bedrock includes Late Devonian granitoid rocks and the Cambrian/Early Ordovician Goldenville and Halifax Formations (White et al. 2008). The Halifax Formation is slate-dominated, and overlying lakes are exposed to acid rock drainage (the production of acid from the oxidation of sulfide-bearing minerals) and consequently are characterized by low pH and elevated metal concentrations (King and Hart 1990; White et al. 2008). As granite and shale bedrock offer poor buffering capacity, many lakes have also experienced anthropogenic acidification from atmospheric sulfate deposition (Clair et al. 2007).

The census population of the HRM is 544,834 (Statistics Canada 2026). The region includes both urban and rural areas, with the most developed areas in Dartmouth and in the Halifax Metro Area. The HRM contains many urban lakes and wetlands, which have been impacted by development for over two centuries (Manuel 2003; Dunnington et al. 2018; Hipwell et al. 2025) and which show considerable variability in lake area, depth, and watershed area, e.g., sites include fluvial lakes, large deep lakes, and small remnant bog-bordered wetlands (Manuel 2003; Bermarija et al. 2023).

Water Chemistry Data

Water quality data from 74 lakes were obtained from the Halifax LakeWatchers 2022–2024 dataset accessed through the Atlantic Datastream portal (10.25976/85pw-zj92; Halifax Regional Municipality 2025b; accessed November 2025). The LakeWatchers data were collected between 2022-04-25 and 2024-09-12 by staff and trained volunteers. Lakes were sampled twice a year (spring and summer) for 3 years. During each sampling event, a YSI meter was used to provide water column profiles of dissolved oxygen (DO), pH, temperature, and specific conductance. For water quality measures (dissolved chloride, unfiltered total phosphorus (TP), metals), two grab samples were taken from the deep basin of the lake, a surface sample and a bottom-water sample, and one grab sample was taken from the lake outlet. There was a difference in parameters analyzed in samples; e.g., metals were only studied in the deep-water basin bottom sample (and only in 2022), while dissolved chloride was only assessed in the deep-water surface sample and in the outlet sample (Kennedy 2024). Secchi disk measurements were taken at the deep-water basin sampling location.

We removed Porter’s Lake from our analysis as it is connected directly to the Atlantic Ocean and much of its water is highly saline due to marine influence. Of the remaining 73 lakes, 71 were measured consistently (twice a year, for 3 years). Another lake, Anderson Lake, was missing data for spring 2022, as the wrong Anderson Lake was sampled during that event. Thus, Anderson Lake is included in analyses using the 2023 and 2024 data but excluded from analyses relying on the 2022 data.

Soil Sampling

Soil sampling sites were selected using ArcGIS Pro 3.5.1 (ESRI 2021). Wetlands were chosen across the city, with control, roadside, and outfall sites being selected in the Halifax Metro Area (west and south), Cole Harbour, and Dartmouth regions (Fig. 1). The Nova Scotia Wetland Inventory (Nova Scotia Provincial Landscape Viewer 2022) was used to identify wetlands, and a dataset was provided by Halifax Water containing locations of stormwater outfalls. As well, road data was extracted from the Nova Scotia Roads, Rails, and Trails database (Government of Nova Scotia, 2022). Control sites were chosen by selecting wetlands that were at least 50 m from a roadway, while roadside sites were immediately adjacent to roads. To select outfall sites, wetland polygons and road lines were both buffered to 30 m, and outfall points were buffered to 5 m. The buffered outfall points, wetland polygons, and road lines were then intersected. Locations of sites that intersected (n = 9) were exported and assessed further for suitability. An initial search was completed in Google Earth Pro to ensure sites had not undergone development since the inventory was updated, and field site checks were completed between January and April 2022 to ensure suitability. Of the nine intersected sites, five were suitable for analysis.

Fifteen wetlands were sampled between May 24 and June 2, 2022 (Fig. 1). At each wetland, a 15 m transect was set up running into the wetland from (a) the stormwater drainage outflow pipe, (b) the wetland roadside edge, or (c) the wetland inflow (for control sites). A low-impact (L), outfall (O), and road (R) site was chosen across different regions of the city to identify differences. Organic soil samples were collected at 1 m, 5 m, and 15 m distances from the inflow using a 20 cm auger to 20 cm depth. At each distance, three replicate samples spaced 1 m apart were collected (Fig. 2). These soil samples were cut in the field using a serrated knife to separate into 0–10 cm and 10–20 cm depths, then stored in paper bags for transportation to the laboratory. Only organic soils were sampled to maintain the same soil type across all sites.

Diagram of wetland sampling methods. Samples were processed at St. Mary’s University, where they were thoroughly mixed, air dried for 48 h on newspaper at ~21 °C, then immediately crushed and sifted through a 2 mm sieve. This process followed specifications from the Nova Scotia Department of Agriculture Animal and Plant Laboratory (https://novascotia.ca/agri/programs-and-services/lab-services/animal-plant-lab/), where soil subsamples were sent for chemical analysis of exchangeable ion concentrations for Al3+ (kg/ha), Ca2+ (kg/ha), Cu2+ (ppm), K2O (ppm), Mg2+ (kg/ha), Mn2+ (ppm), Na+ (kg/ha), P2O5 (ppm), S (kg/ha), Zn2+ (ppm), using the Mehlich III Extractable Major and Trace Metal Ions method through Inductively Coupled Plasma Optical Emission spectroscopy (ICP-OES). Measurement of soil pH was done at Saint Mary’s University; briefly, 2.0 (±0.05) g of soil was placed in a labeled tube with 8 mL, and samples were agitated for 30 s every 5 min for 30 min (Kalra and Maynard 1991), then left to settle for 1 h. The resulting slurries were tested for pH using a ThermoScientific Orion Star A215 pH meter.

Statistical Analyses for Water Chemistry

Data were sorted and processed on R Studio Version 2024.09.0 + 375 (Posit Software 2024) using R Version 4.4.1 (R Core Team 2024), dplyr (Wickham et al. 2023), and ggplot2 (Wickham, 2016). Data under the limit of detection (LOD) were replaced using LOD/(sqrt(2)) (Croghan and Egeghy 2003), but variables where >25% of data points were under the LOD were excluded from analysis (see Table S1 for analysis LOD for variables). Variables retained from the dataset in the analysis were Al, Ba, Ca, Cl, Cu, Secchi disk depth, DO, Fe, Mg, Mn, K, Na, specific conductivity, Sr, water temperature, TP, depth, and pH. Variables that were excluded for being consistently under the LOD were Ag, As, B, Be, Bi, Cd, Co, Cr, Hg, Mo, Ni, Pb, Sb, Sn, Ti, Tl, U, V, Zn, and Escherichia coli concentrations.

We evaluated chloride concentrations, integrating data from all sampling seasons, years, and locations (lake deep water vs. outlet) to calculate the average chloride concentration for each lake compared to the Canadian Council of Ministers of the Environment (CCME) guideline for chronic chloride exposure for the protection of aquatic life of 120 mg Cl−/L (Canadian Council of Ministers of the Environment 2011). Prior to further analysis, all variables except pH were ln-transformed to meet assumptions of normality. We then used ANOVA to compare chloride concentrations between lake vulnerability categories as used by LakeWatchers. The lake vulnerability categories were defined by AECOM (2020) and designated lakes as High, Moderate, and Reference based on concerns including lake:watershed area ratio, land usage and zoning, public usage, drinking supply, areas of engineering concern, and existing monitoring programs. While there are three designated categories (High, Moderate, Reference), we only tested differences between High and Moderate, as the reference category only included three sites in our study lakes. We tested spatial autocorrelation of chloride concentrations using Moran’s I (function moran.test in package spdep; Pebesma and Bivand 2023).

To understand how chloride concentrations co-varied with other environmental parameters, we used a principal component analysis (PCA) to visualize the distribution of limnological variables (water chemistry, water temperature, depth, and Secchi depth) across lakes. This analysis used the environmental data from spring 2022, as this is the only sampling drive from which metals data are available. We also used ANOVA to test for differences in water chemistry between high and moderate vulnerability lakes based on PC 1 and PC 2 scores. The relationship between chloride concentrations and other environmental variables was further tested using generalized additive models (GAMs) on ln-transformed data (package mccv; Wood 2011). GAMs were used as they allow for non-linear (or non-monotonic) relationships.

To test for differences in chloride concentration between sampling year, season, and location, we used mixed effect models (function lmer in package lmerTest; Kuznetsova et al. 2017), which considered the response variable as a function of these three parameters and each lake as a random effect. We ran models using ln-transformed data. For parameters that were significant, we re-ran models using non-ln transformed data to assess the estimated difference in chloride, as this is relevant for informing sampling strategy and interpretation.

Statistical Analyses for Soil Samples

Soil samples measured in kg/ha were divided by 2 to convert to ppm. Technical replicates at each distance/depth combination were averaged. Soil salinity was quantified using the Sodium Adsorption Ratio (SAR), which was calculated as [Na]/sqrt(([Ca] + [Mg])/2); thus, a higher SAR could indicate effects of road salt usage as road salt increases Na and promotes leaching of Ca and Mg (Mason et al. 1999). SAR provides an estimate of exchangeable sodium in soil and was used because concentrations can be assessed relative to soil quality guidelines, where a SAR of 13 is used to designate high-salinity (sodic) soil (Richards 1954) and a SAR of 5 is the CCME guideline for agricultural, residential, and parkland soils (https://ccme.ca/en/chemical/201).

We used mixed effect models (function lmer in package lmerTest; Kuznetsova et al. 2017) to assess the influence of site type (Low Impact, Outfall, Road), distance (1 m, 5 m, and 15 m), and depth profile (0–10 cm vs. 10–20 cm) on the soil SAR, using each site as a random effect. We also tested variations in Na, Al, Zn, Ca, Mg, Mn, Fe, and Cu concentrations using the same mixed effect model approach, e.g., considering site type, depth, and distance, with site as a random effect. All continuous variables except for pH were ln + 1 transformed prior to analysis. As with the chloride analysis, we re-ran models for parameters that were identified as significant, using non-transformed data to better estimate the magnitude of difference in metal concentrations between sampling parameters. This was done for the purpose of informing the interpretation of results from future wetlands sampling.

Results

Chloride and Water Chemistry in Halifax Lakes

In the 72 lakes included in our dataset, sampled by the municipal water quality monitoring program, the average chloride concentrations ranged from 4.3 to 196.7 mg Cl−/L, with a median of 34.3 mg Cl−/L (Fig. 3a). Eight lakes met or exceeded the CCME long-term (chronic) toxicity guideline of 120 mg Cl−/L. Six of these lakes (Banook, MicMac, Russell, Oat Hill, Penhorn, and Governors) are currently considered high vulnerability lakes by the LakeWatchers program, while two (Lovett and Bayers) are considered moderate vulnerability. Overall, high vulnerability lakes had significantly higher chloride concentrations than moderate vulnerability lakes (ANOVA, R2 = 0.1737, p < 0.001; Fig. 3b). The reference lakes (designated by HRM) all had Cl− concentrations <15 mg/L. Lake chloride concentrations were highly spatially autocorrelated; using k = 7 (approximately 1/10 number of lakes sampled), Moran’s I = 0.2356 (p < 0.001) though we manually tested different iterations of k from 3 to 21 and all were significant at p < 0.001. Chloride concentrations were highest in lakes in developed areas, namely in Dartmouth and western Halifax.

GAMs showed that chloride was significantly correlated with other metrics of salinity and ions common in road salt (Mg, Na, Ca, K, all p < 2e-16, with 68-87% deviance explained). Chloride also explained high amounts of significant variation in Sr (p < 2e-16, 89.4% deviance explained) and Ba (p < 2e-16, 68.9% deviance explained). Chloride was weakly negatively related to Al (p < 0.001, 17.4% deviance explained) and Fe (p = 0.01, 8.93% deviance explained), and positively with pH (p < 0.001, 14.9% deviance explained). Chloride concentrations were not significantly related to DO, TP, lake depth, or surface water temperature, though they were positively related to Secchi disk measurements (p = 0.001, 14.1% deviance explained). Finally, chloride displayed interesting relationships with Mn (p < 6.05e-07, 41.0% deviance explained) and Cu (p < 0.001, 40.9% deviance explained); with Mn, the model showed low correlation for chloride values < 50 mg/L, and a positive relationship in sites >50 mg/L. Cu was highest in the lowest chloride sites, after which it declined and then showed a positive relationship with chloride concentrations >20 mg/L (Fig. 5).

Soil Salinity

SAR in soil sites ranged from 0.9 to 69.99 (Fig. 7a), and while there was considerable variation in the other soil parameters measured (Table 2), no variables except SAR exceeded their respective soil quality guidelines. The standard deviation of replicates collected from the outfall and those adjacent to roads had the highest variability of sodium and SAR (Fig. S2). The sites with the highest soil SAR were Low Impact 2 (mean SAR 20.1 ± 8.8, Spectacle Lake) and Road 3 (SAR = 37 ± 18, road, Black Lake), while multiple sites within the road and outfall categories had values that exceeded the SAR guideline for soil health (SAR > 13). These sites included Albro Lake and Russell Lake, which are included in the water chemistry data, where they have chloride concentrations >100 mg/L and are considered High Vulnerability lakes.

Neither input type nor distance from the input source were significant predictor of soil SAR; however, SAR values were significantly higher in the 10–20 cm depth than the 0–10 cm depth samples (p = 0.015*; Fig. 7b), though the magnitude of SAR difference was minimal (SAR difference = 1.26). Similarly, Na values were also significantly higher in the 10–20 cm depth profile (estimate = 21.6, p = 0.042). When assuming 2 nearest neighbors (k), mean SAR values were significantly spatially autocorrelated (Moran’s I = 0.42, p = 0.019) but not autocorrelated when k > 2 (for k = 3, Moran’s I = 0.08, p = 0.19); using k = 2 essentially linked sites within the designated study regions, this indicates significant regional patterns in soil SAR.

There were also significant relationships between Al, Zn, Mg, Fe, and Cu concentrations across site type, region, depth, and distance (estimated differences in predicted value are given in Table S3). Al was notably higher in outfall and road sites than in low-impact sites (p < 0.1), Fe was notably higher in outfall sites, road sites, and 5 m distances (p < 0.1), and significantly lower in the 10–20 cm depth interval (p < 0.05). Zn was also significantly higher in outfall sites (p < 0.05) and lower at the 10–20 cm depth interval (p < 0.05). Cu was significantly higher in outfall sites (p < 0.01) and lower further from the road (5 m distance, p < 0.05, 15 m distance, p < 0.001). Finally, Mg was significantly lower in outfall sites (p < 0.05) and notably higher at the 5 m distance than at 1 m (p < 0.1).

Discussion

Salinization of HRM Lake and Wetland Sites

Our first objective was to quantify current salinization in Halifax lakes and wetlands. From 2022 to 2024, eight of the study lakes exceeded the CCME guideline of 120 mg/L for long-term exposure. These lakes are predominantly located in urban areas, namely Dartmouth and western Halifax. Additionally, 46 sampled lakes had chloride concentrations above the regional background of 20 mg Cl−/L (Environment Canada & Health Canada 2001). These results are consistent with the 2006–2011 survey by Bermarija et al. (2023) and with the 1981–2017 data from Scott et al. (2019), where study lakes overlap. The vulnerability categories established by Halifax LakeWatchers were able to significantly predict lakes with higher chloride, but there were notable exceptions (e.g., Lovett Lake), suggesting that lake vulnerability should be assessed on a site-specific basis. Multiple studies suggest that chloride can impact aquatic biota at concentrations far below 120 mg/L. Arnott et al. (2020) found that chloride concentrations between 5 and 40 mg/L decreased reproduction and increased mortality of Daphnia, a keystone cladoceran grazer. In a bioassay study, Valleau et al. (2020) found a 14-day LC50 for Bosmina longirostris, another ubiquitous zooplankton grazer, of 24.3 mg Cl−/L in a soft-water medium, and found that reproduction was significantly impacted over 15 mg Cl−/L. Ginn et al. (2015) found increases in halophilous diatom flora in Halifax lakes with increasing conductivity, even though many of these lakes had chloride values < 120 mg Cl−/L, and often < 50 mg Cl−/L. Thus, more Halifax lakes may be vulnerable to the negative effects of salinization than suggested by the LakeWatchers vulnerability categories or CCME guidelines.

Out of the 15 wetlands surveyed, six had soil samples where the SAR values were > 13, and two sites had sampled SAR values > 20 in the sediments surveyed, which suggests extremely salinized soils. These highly salinized sites include wetlands feeding into Albro Lake, Spectacle Lake, and Russell Lake, which are all lakes providing important esthetic and recreational (public beaches, private docks) values and which are routinely stocked to support angling. An additional four sites had SAR values > 5, which is the CCME guideline for residential, agricultural, or parkland soils. This shows that city wetlands are likely affected by the input of road salt, necessitating the need for further wetland research to complement lake monitoring. The overall levels of watershed development, underlying geology, and/or coastal influence could all be influencing our results. The method with which we identified low-impact sites may not appropriately control road-salt inputs; for example, while the sampling location for Spectacle Lake was more than 50 m from a major roadway, it is in a developed watershed, and the lake itself is bordered by a parking lot. We suggest future research look at the influence of seasonality and sample during periods of flow to understand differences in stormwater, road spray, and snowmelt.

Road Salt Impacts on Water and Soil Chemistry

We examined how water and soil chemistry co-varied with chloride to understand how road salt may relate to other stressors and contaminants in HRM freshwaters. The water chemistry data show a clear distinction between the lakes with higher chloride and road-salt-related ions and lakes with high Al, Fe, low pH, and more color (low Secchi). This follows the natural geographic gradient in Halifax lakes, where lakes overlying Halifax Formation bedrock are exposed to acid rock drainage and subsequently have higher concentrations of Al and Fe (Clement et al. 2007). Less-developed HRM watersheds tend to be boggier, with more color and low pH due to humic substance input (Clement et al. 2007). Conversely, chloride and other major dissolved salts and minerals are higher in developed lakes following road density and urban influence (Bermarija et al. 2023). While our dataset showed no significant relationship between chloride and TP, despite the vulnerability of many HRM lakes to cultural eutrophication (Ginn et al. 2015), a more in-depth assessment of this relationship over more sampling events would be required. Doucet et al. (2023) suggest TP may be elevated in rural lakes due to septic system usage, so it may not co-vary with chloride as a proxy of urban development.

Chloride was strongly correlated with other salt constituents (e.g., Mg, K, Ca), which may be components of road salts (though HRM road salt is primarily NaCl) or easily displaced by chloride in roadside soils (Mason et al. 1999; Schuler and Relyea 2018). We also found that Sr and Ba concentrations were very strongly correlated with lake chloride. These metals are both trace constituents of road salts (Granato et al. 1995). While Ba and Sr may also be indicators of erosion, other metals/minerals associated with erosion but not as commonly with road salt (e.g., titanium, selenium, nickel, beryllium; Granato et al. 1995) were all under the detection limit, which suggests road salt usage may be inputting Sr2+ and Ba2+ into urban lakes. However, Sr2+ and Ba2+ are typically not of concern to aquatic organisms at the very low (<100 μg/L) concentrations reported here; our observed concentrations are orders of magnitude lower than suggested guidelines (e.g., 10.7 mg/L Sr, 13–18 mg/L Ba; McPherson et al. 2014; Golding et al. 2018).

There was a positive relationship between Mn (not typically a road salt constituent; Granato et al. 1995) and chloride, which was prominent in lakes above ~55 mg Cl−/L. The natural distribution of Mn in HRM lakes is higher in lakes overlying the Halifax Formation and is correlated with Al and Fe (Clement et al. 2007). However, this correlation was not observed in our data, suggesting factors outside natural geology are influencing Mn concentrations. Manganese is a component of unleaded gasoline in Canada (as methylcyclopentadienyl manganese tricarbonyl; Zayed et al. 2003), and studies have linked roadside snow and soil Mn concentrations to roadway proximity (Lytle et al. 1995; Loranger et al. 1996). As chloride will complex with Mn, road de-icers may mobilize Mn into freshwaters (Kennedy 2021).

Chloride and Cu also showed a significant non-linear relationship, suggesting that Cu may be naturally higher in rural, undeveloped lakes (perhaps due to underlying geology), but within lakes impacted by road salt (i.e., >20 mg Cl−/L), concentrations will increase with chloride. Cu can be a trace constituent of road salt (Granato et al. 1995), it may be mobilized from soils by chloride (Amrhein et al. 1992), or it can be a co-varying indicator of road density and motor vehicle traffic; Cui et al. (2010) found the highest copper source to urban lakes in Stockholm was from car brake linings.

In the wetland soils, Na+ and SAR were significantly higher in the deeper (10–20 cm) layer. Cunningham et al. (2008) found that the addition of road salt led to the leaching of elements through soil to lower depths. This differs from previous experimental research by Ke et al. (2013), who found that upper layers of soils (0–15 cm) had higher salt content than 15–30 cm in experimental garden plots treated with de-icing salt, and Werkmeister et al. (2018) who found slightly higher Na+ concentrations at the surface (0 cm) of prairie pothole wetland profiles compared to 10 and 20 cm of depth. This could mean that the salt had already been translocated into lower layers of the soil by the time of sampling. It is possible that longer soil profiles may be needed to characterize differences in soil chemistry between sites, as disturbances can alter soil element composition to (at least) a 1 m depth (Werkmeister et al. 2018). More intensive soil profiling might be needed to understand the long-term effects of road salt contamination on urban wetland chemistry. Al, Fe, Cu, and Zn were higher in the outfall and/or road sites than the control sites, which likely reflects urban metals from stormwater runoff or from stormwater pipes. This is shown strongly for Cu, where concentrations are highest within 1 m of the outfall. Interestingly, Mg2+ was negatively related to outfalls, which could suggest it is being displaced in roadside soils by heavy metals from stormwater runoff (as heavy metals can displace alkaline cations such as Ca2+ and Mg2+; Diatta et al. 2004).

Implications for Monitoring

Our mixed-model approach revealed spatial and temporal trends in measured chloride concentrations while accounting for lake-specific differences in water chemistry (e.g., lakes may have different baseline concentrations based on regional geology). Higher measured chloride concentrations in the spring align with the timing of road salt application, i.e., applied in the winter and washed into the lakes with the spring freshet (Scott et al. 2019). The magnitude of difference between spring and summer was higher in the model of only lakes exceeding the CCME guidelines than in the model of all lakes, likely as lakes receive road salt input that would have a temporal signal, contrasting less-impacted lakes which do not have the same seasonal variation in salt input, and where dissolved ions may be primarily sourced by natural weathering of rocks and of Atlantic Ocean inputs (Clement et al. 2007). Thus, measuring lakes in the spring might provide a better method of identifying lakes receiving road salt inputs. However, freshwater chloride concentrations have been observed to increase over all seasons, including summer (Todd and Kaltenecker 2012; Corsi et al. 2015). Monitoring of summer trends remains important. The difference between measuring in deep-water and outlet was low magnitude; while sampling from lake outlets might provide a slightly more conservative estimate, it may still be appropriate, e.g., as a cost- and time-saving measure allowing more lakes to be sampled.

The lack of a significant relationship between depth, distance, and measures of road-salt impact (primarily SAR, but also Na+) might initially suggest that sampling any location near an input would be appropriate for characterizing the soil salinity. However, there was considerable standard deviation in our measurements within each distance replicate. For SAR, five sites had standard deviations of over 6, while the median standard deviation in outfall and road sites was ~2. Similarly, some site/distance combinations had standard deviations of >100 ppm for Na+. The considerable variability in chemistry suggests heterogeneity in soil conditions, even at the small spatial scale of 1 m apart; while using replicates accounts for this, it may be worthwhile to note the properties at each site. For example, changes in elevation could impact run-off patterns.

Conclusions

We examined salinization and ecosystem risk in Halifax urban freshwaters, assessed how salinity variables co-varied with environmental parameters, and quantified the influence of sampling methods on measured values. Many urban freshwaters in Halifax are impacted by salinity, with eight of our 72 lakes consistently above the chronic exposure guideline for chloride. We found chloride varied with expected road salt constituents, but also with Ba, Cu, Sr (trace metal components of road salt), and with Mn, which may be mobilized from roadsides into freshwaters. Lake chloride measurements were higher in spring and in lake deep water, and this is more pronounced in high-chloride lakes, suggesting that temporal sampling can help highlight road-salt-impacted systems. Eight of the fifteen urban wetlands had SAR values over the soil health guideline, including a wetland not directly impacted by a major road or stormwater outfall. SAR was not significantly related to the type of input, though other metals were significantly higher near outfalls and roads, likely representing stormwater runoff or stormwater pipes. However, salinity measures of wetland soils were highly variable, even within replicates. Overall, we see that freshwaters in Halifax are highly salinized, and we suggest that future research focus on the impacts of road salt on the ecosystem services these lakes provide.
