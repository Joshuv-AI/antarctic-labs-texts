A Pedunculated Uvular Tumor Highlighting the Decline of Oropharyngeal Examination Skills
Authors: Ryo Ichibayashi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPedunculated uvular tumors, though rare, may cause airway symptoms when enlarged or elongated. This case highlights how diminished attention to basic oropharyngeal inspection in routine care can lead to missed diagnoses with potentially serious consequences. Reinforcing fundamental physical examination skills remains essential in emergency and general clinical practice.Keywords: incidental finding, missed diagnosis, oropharyngeal examination, pedunculated lesion, uvular tumor

A man in his 20s presented to the hospital with a sore throat, inspiratory difficulty, and fever. His medical history included childhood asthma and current treatment for atopic dermatitis. On physical examination, nasal obstruction, left cheek tenderness, posterior pharyngeal follicular lymphadenopathy, and anterior cervical lymphadenopathy were observed. Sinus computed tomography revealed no significant findings, and nasopharyngitis was diagnosed. A key clinical question in this situation is: What important oropharyngeal finding may be overlooked during routine examinations and potentially lead to airway symptoms?

During direct oropharyngeal inspection, an incidental pedunculated mass was noted on the uvula. Endoscopic evaluation demonstrated a spherical lesion approximately 8 mm in diameter. Before insufflation (Figure 1, Panel A), the mass rested posteriorly; after insufflation (Figure 1, Panel B), it elongated and shifted toward the airway. White arrows in Figure 1 indicate the lesion. This dynamic movement corresponded with the patient's symptoms of choking and dyspnea. Although the patient had been aware of the lesion for 5 years and had visited multiple clinics for upper respiratory symptoms, the mass had not been detected—likely due to limited oral examinations during the COVID‐19 pandemic, when diagnostic focus often shifted toward testing and imaging.

Endoscopic view of a pedunculated uvular tumor. Panel A shows the lesion at rest before insufflation. Panel B demonstrates the lesion elongated and displaced toward the airway due to insufflation. White arrows indicate the spherical mass (approximately 8 mm in diameter).

While most uvular lesions are benign, malignant entities have also been reported [1]. Therefore, persistent uvular masses require careful evaluation and histopathological confirmation. In this case, the patient was referred to another institution for resection; however, details of the final diagnosis and postoperative course are unavailable.

Pedunculated uvular tumors are rare, but enlargement or elongation may cause dysphagia or airway symptoms such as choking [2]. This case illustrates the potential risks of diagnostic oversight when routine physical examinations are incomplete or omitted. Recent literature has highlighted a steady decline in physicians' physical examination skills, especially in the oral cavity and oropharynx [3]. Factors include the widespread reliance on rapid diagnostic testing, evolving infection control practices, and dependence on imaging modalities.

This case underscores the enduring importance of fundamental bedside skills such as careful oropharyngeal inspection. Even common presentations, such as pharyngitis, may conceal clinically significant findings that affect patient safety and quality of care. Reinforcing these core skills among clinicians is essential to avoid diagnostic oversight, particularly for lesions with potential airway implications.
