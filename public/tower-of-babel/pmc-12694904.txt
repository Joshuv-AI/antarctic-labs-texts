Pharmacovigilance assessment of adverse events following yellow fever vaccination: Disproportionality analysis of VAERS reports, 2015–2025
Authors: Guojun Liang, Qiong Liu, Yang Song
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTYellow fever vaccination remains the most effective strategy for preventing infection in endemic and travel-associated settings, yet rare severe adverse events such as viscerotropic and neurotropic disease continue to raise safety concerns. Adverse event reports submitted to the Vaccine Adverse Event Reporting System (VAERS) between 2015 and 2025 were analyzed. Disproportionality algorithms were applied to evaluate reporting patterns, with subgroup analyses by age and sex. The most frequent events were local injection-site reactions and mild systemic symptoms, consistent with clinical trial findings. Severe outcomes, including yellow fever vaccine-associated viscerotropic and neurotropic disease, demonstrated strong disproportionality signals despite their rarity. Additional hypersensitivity-related and less traditional preferred terms were also detected across multiple methods. Female recipients showed higher frequencies of local reactions, while severe events were proportionally more common in older individuals. The analysis reaffirms the established safety profile of yellow fever vaccine while highlighting rare but clinically important severe events and emerging signals. Continued post-marketing surveillance and targeted risk communication remain essential to optimize safe vaccine use.

Introduction

Yellow fever is an acute viral hemorrhagic disease caused by an enveloped, single-stranded RNA virus of the genus Flavivirus. The disease remains endemic in large regions of sub-Saharan Africa and South America, where it circulates in both sylvatic and urban transmission cycles involving non-human primates and mosquito vectors such as Aedes aegypti and Haemagogus species.1,2 Although most infections present with a nonspecific febrile illness, a proportion of cases progress to a toxic phase marked by jaundice, bleeding diathesis, multiorgan failure, and high case-fatality rates. In the absence of specific antiviral therapy, vaccination coupled with sustained vector control constitutes the primary strategy for disease prevention and control.3,4

Live attenuated 17D-derived vaccines have been in continuous use for more than eight decades and remain the only licensed intervention offering effective, long-term protection against yellow fever. In the United States, vaccination is indicated for individuals aged ≥9 months who reside in or travel to areas with recognized transmission risk, as well as for laboratory workers handling potentially infectious materials. Under the International Health Regulations, proof of vaccination – documented via the International Certificate of Vaccination or Prophylaxis – is mandatory for entry into some countries, with validity commencing ten days after primary immunization and generally recognized for life.5–7 During the study period, two vaccines were used in the United States: YF-VAX, manufactured domestically from the 17D-204 substrain, and STAMARIL, produced in France from the same substrain and distributed internationally. Both are administered as a single subcutaneous dose, inducing durable immunity in most recipients.8

While the overall benefit – risk profile of yellow fever vaccines is favorable, rare but serious adverse events have been documented. These include severe allergic reactions, yellow fever vaccine-associated neurotropic disease (YEL-AND), and yellow fever vaccine-associated viscerotropic disease (YEL-AVD).9 Risk increases with advancing age, particularly in persons aged ≥60 y, and is highest in primary vaccine recipients.10 For anaphylaxis, the Brighton Collaboration case definition, remains the standard for surveillance-based classification, ensuring diagnostic consistency across studies.11 YEL-AND encompasses a spectrum of neurological syndromes, including meningoencephalitis, acute disseminated encephalomyelitis, and demyelinating neuropathies, typically arising within 30 d of vaccination. YEL-AVD presents as an acute multisystem illness clinically resembling wild-type yellow fever, often involving hepatic, renal, and coagulation dysfunction, and usually manifests within ten days post-vaccination. Although infrequent, YEL-AVD carries a high risk of severe morbidity and mortality.9

Post-marketing safety evaluation in the United States has relied heavily on the Vaccine Adverse Event Reporting System (VAERS). The national review covering 2007–2013 established baseline age-specific reporting rates for serious adverse events and confirmed an increased rate among older adults.11 Earlier VAERS analyses from 2000–2006 similarly documented the rarity of severe outcomes relative to doses distributed.12 Subsequent studies refined the clinical definitions for YEL-AND and YEL-AVD and identified their concentration in first-dose recipients.13 A targeted re-analysis of VAERS data from 1999–2018, focusing on anaphylaxis after YF-VAX, reported a rate of 14.6 cases per million distributed doses – higher than pooled estimates for many other vaccines – highlighting the importance of immediate recognition and treatment capacity at vaccination sites.14

Between 2015 and 2025, the U.S. yellow fever vaccination program underwent three distinct operational phases, each with implications for vaccine exposure and safety monitoring. The pre-shortage period maintained routine YF-VAX distribution through authorized providers. In mid-2017, manufacturing constraints led to depletion of the U.S. civilian YF-VAX supply, prompting the Food and Drug Administration and the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention to authorize STAMARIL under an Expanded Access Investigational New Drug protocol.15 This arrangement restricted administration to selected clinics and incorporated enhanced consent and follow-up procedures, potentially influencing the completeness and timeliness of adverse event reporting. The Expanded Access phase lasted until 2021, when YF-VAX returned to the civilian market amid the COVID-19 pandemic’s disruption of international travel and the subsequent gradual rebound in vaccine demand. These programmatic transitions created discrete eras with differing exposure profiles, providing a natural framework for temporal and product-specific safety analyses.

This study seeks to update the post-marketing safety profile of yellow fever vaccination in the United States by examining VAERS reports submitted between the first quarter of 2015 and the second quarter of 2025. VAERS is a spontaneous reporting system without stable exposure denominators, conventional incidence estimation is not feasible. International pharmacovigilance guidelines from the World Health Organization, the Council for International Organizations of Medical Sciences, and the European Medicines Agency identify disproportionality analysis as a central method for detecting potential safety signals in such systems. Serious outcomes associated with yellow fever vaccination are rare, and large analytical cohorts are difficult to assemble, which further supports the use of disproportionality analysis as an early signal detection tool. The adoption of this approach therefore aligns with the primary aim of the present study, which is the identification of reporting patterns that may indicate potential safety concerns. The primary objective is to estimate crude reporting rates for serious adverse events overall and for the predefined endpoints of anaphylaxis, YEL-AND, and YEL-AVD, applying standardized case definitions to enable direct comparison with prior national VAERS assessments. Rates will be stratified by age group, vaccine product, and program era, with denominators obtained from manufacturer distribution data or published counts for the STAMARIL Expanded Access period. Secondary objectives are to describe the clinical characteristics, timing, outcomes, and coadministration patterns of reported events, and to assess how programmatic changes influenced reporting trends. An additional aim is to compare observed age-specific patterns and clinical profiles with product labeling and published evidence, thereby evaluating the concordance between current guidance and recent post-marketing experience.

Methods

Study design and data source

This investigation was conducted as a retrospective review of adverse event reports submitted to the Vaccine Adverse Event Reporting System for yellow fever vaccines administered to United States civilians between the first quarter of 2015 and the second quarter of 2025. Reports were identified based on vaccine product codes corresponding to yellow fever vaccines, and all relevant manufacturer codes were included to ensure complete capture. Event coding followed the Medical Dictionary for Regulatory Activities (MedDRA) preferred terms as recorded in the database, and both primary and secondary symptom terms were retained for analysis.16 The database was downloaded in its raw format, and all processing and analysis were performed on the cleaned datasets. Because each report in VAERS is assigned a unique identification number and follow-up or corrected submissions are consolidated under that identifier as part of CDC’s routine data processing, the dataset available through CDC Wonder contains only the latest version of each report.

Case identification and manual review

Cases were screened for adverse events of special interest using predefined MedDRA term lists derived from prior yellow fever vaccine safety assessments. Reports were screened to confirm that each VAERS identification number represented a unique report, consistent with CDC’s consolidation of follow-up versions. No additional manual deduplication was undertaken, because VAERS releases only one consolidated version for each report.17 Independent adjudication was performed by two reviewers with clinical expertise, and disagreements were resolved through consensus discussion to maintain consistency with prior published VAERS analyses of yellow fever vaccine-related events.

Definitions of adverse events of special interest

Adverse events were classified according to established international consensus definitions to ensure comparability with prior safety assessments. Anaphylaxis was defined using Brighton Collaboration criteria, requiring acute onset and involvement of multiple organ systems with objective evidence of cardiovascular or respiratory compromise. Neurotropic disease included encephalitis, meningoencephalitis, and related central nervous system inflammatory conditions temporally associated with vaccination, confirmed by clinical features and, when available, supportive diagnostic testing. Viscerotropic disease was characterized by systemic febrile illness with multiorgan dysfunction following vaccination, consistent with definitions adopted by regulatory agencies. Reports were further categorized as serious or non-serious based on the United States Food and Drug Administration criteria, including outcomes of death, life-threatening illness, hospitalization or its prolongation, permanent disability, or congenital anomaly.

Variables, subgroup definitions, and time-to-onset grouping

Demographic variables included age, sex, and concomitant vaccine administration. Age was classified into seven categories, beginning with individuals younger than 18 y, followed by successive 10-y intervals up to 67 y, and a final group comprising those aged 68 y or older. Reports were stratified according to whether yellow fever vaccine had been administered alone or together with other vaccines, and deaths were examined separately as a predefined subgroup of interest. The interval from vaccination to symptom onset was organized into eight groups: 0 to 30 d, 31 to 60 d, 61 to 90 d, 91 to 120 d, 121 to 150 d, 151 to 180 d, 181 to 360 d, and more than 360 d. These intervals were chosen to enable evaluation of both acute and delayed onset events while remaining consistent with prior pharmacovigilance research.

Era definitions

To capture the influence of vaccine availability on reporting patterns, the study period was partitioned into three programmatic eras corresponding to changes in United States yellow fever vaccine supply. The first era, referred to as the pre-shortage period, extended from early 2015 through mid-2017 when YF-VAX was continuously available through routine distribution channels. The second era, beginning in mid-2017 and continuing through 2020, corresponded to the implementation of the STAMARIL Expanded Access program during which YF-VAX was unavailable and vaccination was restricted to a network of designated clinics. The third era commenced in 2021 and extended through mid-2025, representing the post-return to routine supply period when YF-VAX was reintroduced alongside other available products. This temporal segmentation provided a framework for assessing whether fluctuations in supply and distribution influenced both the volume and clinical characteristics of reported adverse events.

Statistical analysis

The primary analytical approach for signal detection was the calculation of reporting odds ratios (RORs) with associated 95% confidence intervals, comparing the proportional reporting of each adverse event term of interest for yellow fever vaccines versus all other vaccines in VAERS during the same period. Disproportionality was further assessed using three complementary methods: the information component (IC), with IC025 representing the lower bound of the 95% credibility interval; the proportional reporting ratio (PRR) with corresponding chi-square values; and the empirical Bayesian geometric mean (EBGM), with EBGM05 representing the lower bound.17–22 ROR was considered the primary determinant for signal identification, while concordance across multiple methods was used to strengthen the interpretation of findings. Sensitivity analyses included restriction to reports with documented first-dose status, reclassification of indeterminate cases after manual review, and product-specific stratification where manufacturer information was available.23,24 Analyses were performed for the overall dataset as well as for predefined subgroups according to age, sex, coadministration status, and fatal outcome.

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.4.1) with the packages epiR (version 2.0.66) for ROR and PRR calculations, openEBGM (version 0.8.0) for EBGM estimates, and vactools (version 0.4.1) for data extraction and cleaning. Data management was conducted using dplyr (version 1.1.4) and tidyr (version 1.3.1), and figures were generated using ggplot2 (version 3.5.1).

Ethical and data use considerations

The VAERS database is publicly available and fully de-identified. This secondary analysis involved no direct patient contact or interventions, and thus did not constitute human subjects research requiring institutional review board approval. All procedures adhered to VAERS data use policies and accepted public health research standards.

Results

Descriptive characteristics of reports

Between 2015 and mid-2025, a total of 1664 adverse event reports related to yellow fever vaccination were identified in VAERS. The annual distribution demonstrated pronounced fluctuation, with an initial peak in 2015 (216 reports) followed by a gradual decline that reached the lowest point in 2020 (74 reports). This nadir coincided with the COVID-19 pandemic and the constraints of the STAMARIL Expanded Access program, both of which substantially reduced vaccine administration (Figure 1). Reports increased steadily thereafter, reaching a secondary peak in 2023 (211 reports), likely reflecting the resumption of routine travel immunizations and the restored availability of YF-VAX. Regarding vaccine administration, 842 of 1644 cases (51.2%) involved yellow fever vaccine alone, while 802 (48.8%) were co-administered with other vaccines. Among 358 cases with brand information, YF-VAX represented 79.3%, STAMARIL 15.4%, and the remainder did not specify a product. In terms of temporal patterns, 1177 of 1230 reports (95.7%) occurred within 30 d post-vaccination, with only a small minority reported beyond this window. This distribution is consistent with current prescribing information, which notes immediate onset for anaphylaxis, onset within 1–30 d for yellow fever vaccine-associated neurotropic disease (YEL-AND), and onset typically within 1–10 d for viscerotropic disease (YEL-AVD).

Age-specific distributions are shown in Table 2. The proportion of serious cases increased with advancing age, reaching 19.4% in individuals aged 58–68 y and 16.2% in those ≥68 y, compared with 8.7% and 8.9% in younger adult groups. Notably, children and adolescents <18 y also demonstrated a higher proportion of serious outcomes (20.0%) despite representing a smaller cohort. Co-administration with other vaccines was more frequent in younger adults, whereas vaccination alone was predominant in older populations. Across all age groups, the majority of adverse events occurred within 30 d, underscoring the importance of vigilant monitoring in the early post-vaccination period. Disproportionality analysis of adverse events

Using ROR as the primary disproportionality metric, complemented by PRR, IC025, and EBGM05, the overall signal distribution is summarized in Table 3 and mapped by SOC hierarchy. Signals clustered most prominently in five domains: general disorders and administration-site conditions, skin and subcutaneous tissue disorders, hepatobiliary disorders, nervous system disorders, and infections and infestations. Within these, the most notable PT-level disproportionalities were consistent with the established safety profile of yellow fever vaccines. Yellow fever vaccine – associated viscerotropic disease (YEL-AVD) demonstrated a markedly elevated ROR, and yellow fever vaccine – associated neurotropic disease (YEL-AND) similarly showed strong disproportionality, together reflecting rare but severe outcomes. Within the nervous system category, encephalitis emerged as a robust signal, while hepatobiliary involvement was exemplified by jaundice. Common local adverse events included injection-site erythema and injection-site swelling, which were highly disproportional within general disorders. These findings are concordant with current product labeling, which highlights rare but serious viscerotropic and neurotropic events and frequent local and systemic reactions such as fever, headache, The prescribing information also notes that systemic manifestations such as fever, malaise, and headache typically emerge within the first week, supporting the reliability of this observation. Discussion

This study, which analyzed long-term data from the Vaccine Adverse Event Reporting System (VAERS), provides a systematic evaluation of adverse events following yellow fever vaccination, and the overall safety profile remains consistent with information in the current product label. The most frequently reported outcomes were local reactions at the injection site and mild systemic symptoms, consistent with results from clinical trials and prior post-marketing studies. At the same time, reports of yellow fever vaccine-associated viscerotropic disease and neurotropic disease were detected with notable disproportionality signals. Although infrequent, these events represent clinically significant outcomes that confirm the established profile of severe vaccine-related complications. Importantly, analysis at the preferred-term level revealed a limited number of signals not emphasized in the product label or in prior reviews. These signals were repeatedly identified across several analytical methods, indicating that they may represent emerging safety concerns rather than isolated statistical artifacts, and therefore warrant further verification.

Comparison with previous studies shows that the distribution of local and systemic reactions observed here corresponds closely to results from clinical trials and earlier post-marketing reports, while severe outcomes remain dominated by viscerotropic and neurotropic syndromes.25 In contrast with earlier pharmacovigilance summaries, this analysis highlighted certain hypersensitivity-related terms and a small number of less traditional preferred terms with relatively stronger signals, which may reflect differences in population characteristics, concomitant vaccinations, reporting behaviors, or analytical methods. It is also possible that the identification of such signals reflects greater sensitivity of disproportionality analyses over extended time periods, which increases the likelihood of capturing clinically relevant but previously under-recognized patterns.10 These differences may also relate to the heterogeneous health status and travel-related context of vaccine recipients. Fatal cases accounted for only a small fraction of reports, but the clustering of these outcomes in specific organ system categories underscores their clinical importance and is consistent with the warnings outlined in the prescribing information. The concentration of fatal cases in hepatic, neurologic, and multisystem failure categories mirrors the established pathophysiology of viscerotropic and neurotropic disease, providing further confirmation of biological plausibility.10

Subgroup analyses added further context for interpretation. Female recipients showed a higher frequency of local reactions, whereas male recipients demonstrated stronger signals for hypersensitivity-related terms such as urticaria. Some systemic symptoms appeared with relatively lower frequency in one sex, suggesting that these manifestations may occur less often after yellow fever vaccination than with other vaccines. The observed sex differences may plausibly reflect immunological mechanisms, as women often mount stronger innate and adaptive immune responses, while men may be more predisposed to IgE-mediated hypersensitivity, although behavioral and reporting differences could also contribute.26–28 In terms of age distribution, most adverse events were reported within 30 d post-vaccination, with younger individuals showing an earlier peak and older individuals demonstrating a more dispersed pattern.11 Severe adverse events were concentrated in the early post-vaccination period, consistent with the clinical timelines for viscerotropic disease, usually 1–10 d, and neurotropic disease, typically 1–30 d. The observation that severe events were proportionally more common in older recipients, while younger age groups contributed fewer but clinically relevant severe outcomes, supports the need for vigilant monitoring in elderly and other susceptible populations during the early post-vaccination window. This pattern is particularly relevant in clinical practice, as older age is a recognized risk factor for viscerotropic complications, yet younger age groups may still warrant targeted attention when vaccination is undertaken in the context of international travel or outbreak control.29

From a clinical and public health perspective, these findings reaffirm the importance of maintaining robust vaccine safety monitoring for yellow fever vaccine. Although severe adverse events such as viscerotropic and neurotropic disease are rare, their occurrence has profound implications for risk-benefit assessment, particularly in populations where the risk of yellow fever exposure is low. Clinicians should be aware of the characteristic time frames in which these outcomes are most likely to appear and provide clear risk communication to recipients, especially elderly individuals and those with underlying health conditions.30 Balanced communication that emphasizes both the rarity and the clinical seriousness of these outcomes is essential, since disproportionate attention to severe cases in public discourse can undermine vaccine confidence if not appropriately contextualized against the risks of wild-type yellow fever infection. Effective communication regarding both the rarity and potential seriousness of these adverse events is crucial to support informed decision-making and to maintain confidence in vaccination programs.31 The results also highlight the need for sustained post-marketing surveillance, not only to detect established signals but also to capture emerging patterns that may vary across geographic regions or demographic subgroups.

The strengths of our study lie in the large number of reports available in VAERS, the extended observation period, and the use of multiple disproportionality algorithms, which allowed for robust signal identification across different analytical frameworks. Stratification by sex and age further enhanced interpretability, providing clinically relevant insights into potential susceptibility factors. An additional consideration relates to the predefined endpoint of anaphylaxis. Although the search strategy encompassed all hypersensitivity-related preferred terms, no report fulfilled the preferred term used to designate anaphylaxis, and none demonstrated clinical features compatible with Brighton criteria. The absence of qualifying reports limits the ability to contextualize crude reporting rates for this endpoint or compare them directly with historical estimates. This observation is consistent with prior national evaluations documenting the extreme rarity of anaphylaxis following yellow fever vaccination. It also highlights limitations of spontaneous reporting systems, where heterogeneous coding or nonspecific allergic terminology may obscure retrospective identification of true anaphylactic reactions. Nevertheless, the inherent limitations of spontaneous reporting systems must be acknowledged. Underreporting and selective reporting are well-recognized issues that may distort the apparent frequency and distribution of adverse events, and the absence of denominator data prevents estimation of true incidence rates. The inability to verify causality at the individual report level also restricts the strength of conclusions, as confounding by concomitant medications, co-administered vaccines, and preexisting conditions cannot be excluded. Additionally, the passive nature of VAERS may lead to under-recognition of events with less obvious clinical manifestations, whereas serious outcomes are more likely to be reported, thereby influencing the observed distribution of adverse events. This asymmetry in reporting may exaggerate the prominence of rare severe events while simultaneously obscuring more common but clinically less dramatic reactions, a factor that should be considered when interpreting disproportionality results. In this context, the absence of reports meeting the predefined anaphylaxis endpoint warrants emphasis. Although several allergy-related preferred terms were present in the dataset, none demonstrated clinical features compatible with Brighton criteria for anaphylaxis, and none reached disproportionality thresholds across the analytical methods employed. This pattern aligns with the recognized rarity of confirmed anaphylaxis following yellow fever vaccination but also illustrates how nonspecific terminology or variability in diagnostic labeling within spontaneous reporting systems may obscure the identification of acute hypersensitivity reactions.

Future research should prioritize complementary approaches that extend beyond passive surveillance. Mechanistic studies are needed to clarify biological pathways underlying the observed sex differences in reporting patterns and to identify potential immunological or genetic determinants of susceptibility. Longitudinal cohort studies would help to establish long-term outcomes in individuals experiencing severe adverse events, particularly those with fatal or near-fatal manifestations, and could provide essential information to refine risk stratification strategies.28,32 Further refinement of statistical and pharmacovigilance methodologies is also necessary to improve detection of rare but clinically meaningful events, particularly in the context of vaccines administered to specific subpopulations such as travelers and military personnel. Integration of VAERS data with active surveillance systems such as the Vaccine Safety Datalink and international pharmacovigilance networks would enhance the ability to triangulate findings, validate signals, and strengthen causal inference. These combined approaches will contribute to a more comprehensive understanding of the safety profile of yellow fever vaccine and will guide strategies to optimize its use in populations where the benefits of immunization clearly outweigh the risks.

Conclusion

Analysis of VAERS reports from 2015 to 2025 indicates that the yellow fever vaccine maintains a safety profile consistent with labeling: local and mild systemic reactions predominate, while viscerotropic and neurotropic disease are rare but clinically important. Disproportionality methods identified a small number of candidate signals not emphasized in prior summaries, which should be interpreted cautiously given passive-surveillance constraints. Age- and sex-specific patterns and early time-to-onset support targeted vigilance in susceptible groups-particularly older recipients during the first weeks after vaccination. Continued monitoring, coupled with complementary epidemiologic studies, is warranted to validate signals and refine risk-benefit assessments for travelers.
