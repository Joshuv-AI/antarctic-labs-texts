The relationship between dietary inflammatory indices and male infertility: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Maryam Ranjbar Zahedani, Narzikul Makhmudov, Umida Sadikova, Zainab Shateri, Seyed Mojtaba Amiri, Mohadese Borazjani, Marzieh Mahmoodi, Mehran Nouri
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveInflammation is the body’s defense mechanism against harmful external stimuli, which can contribute to disorders such as infertility. The purpose of this research was to assess the relationship among the Dietary Inflammatory Index (DII), Empirical Dietary Inflammatory Pattern (EDIP), and Dietary Inflammation Score (DIS) in relation to the odds of infertility in Iranian men.ResultsWe observed higher odds of abnormal sperm concentration in both the second tertile (odds ratio (OR) = 3.198, 95% confidence interval (CI): 1.189–8.599) and the last tertile of DII (OR = 3.718, 95% CI: 1.373–10.046) when compared to the first tertile in the adjusted model. However, for other sperm quality parameters, no significant associations were found in the adjusted model (P > 0.05 for all). The findings of the present research indicated that following a pro-inflammatory dietary pattern, as reflected by a greater DII score, negatively impacts sperm quality parameters, including sperm concentration. Longitudinal studies are needed to better clarify the relationship between dietary inflammatory indices and male infertility.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13104-025-07554-z.

Introduction

Male infertility, defined as the inability to produce sufficient sperm to fertilize an oocyte, is a multifactorial condition influenced by inflammation, age, body composition, lifestyle behaviors (such as smoking, alcohol consumption, and physical activity), and diet [1–3]. Among these, diet plays a critical role in providing essential nutrients for growth, development, and reproductive function [1].

Growing evidence suggests that inflammation and oxidative stress are central mechanisms in the pathophysiology of male infertility. Inflammation, while a natural defense against harmful stimuli, can impair reproductive function and has been associated with disorders of the male reproductive tract [2]. It is also closely linked to oxidative stress, which contributes to structural and functional damage in male reproductive tissues [3].

Unhealthy dietary patterns can exacerbate inflammation and increase the risk of chronic disease. To capture the inflammatory potential of diet and lifestyle, indices such as the Empirical Dietary Inflammatory Pattern (EDIP), Lifestyle Inflammation Score (LIS), and Dietary Inflammatory Index (DII) have been developed [4–7]. The EDIP indicates the overall diet’s inflammatory potential as determined by various food groups [5], while the LIS is used to measure lifestyle components such as physical activity, body mass index (BMI), alcohol consumption, and smoking [6]. Higher scores on these indices reflect greater pro-inflammatory potential and have been associated with an increased risk of chronic disorders [4, 7].

However, no study has yet examined the relationship between these inflammation-related dietary indices and male infertility. Therefore, the present study aimed to investigate the associations of DII, Dietary Inflammation Score (DIS), and EDIP with the odds of infertility in Iranian men.

Methods

Research design

The current cross-sectional research was done at one of the infertility clinics in Isfahan province. Subjects were included if they met the criteria established in the study by Chiu et al. [8]. A total of 270 infertile men between 18 and 55 years old were chosen, taking into account sperm concentration with z = 1.96, d = 0.3, and s = 2.5. All subjects gave their agreement to participate by signing an informed consent statement. The exclusion criteria were defined as metabolic diseases (including kidney disease, cardiovascular disease, cancer, and diabetes), a history of urinary tract infection, genital surgery, anatomic disorders, azoospermia, depression, drug abuse, and hormone therapy [9]. Some details of this study have been previously published [10–13]. The research protocol received approval from the Isfahan University of Medical Sciences (IR.MUI.RESEARCH.REC.1397.232).

Sperm parameters

According to study protocols, semen samples were collected from subjects and allowed to liquefy at 37 °C before analysis. Then, four semen parameters were assessed in accordance with the World Health Organization (WHO) laboratory guidelines [14]: sperm morphology, sperm concentration, sperm volume, and total sperm motility [15].

Dietary assessment and food grouping

The research team engaged a trained dietitian to assess the participants’ dietary intakes using a food frequency questionnaire (FFQ) (168 items) [16]. An English version of the questionnaire used in this study has been uploaded as a Supplementary File. The researchers utilized standard measurement tools and a validated food album to help participants accurately report their food consumption. Subjects were requested to indicate their intake of each food item over the past year, categorizing their intake into four frequency options: annually, monthly, weekly, and daily. The research team used Ghafarpour’s method to convert gathered data to grams [17]. Then, each food item’s intake was determined by multiplying the daily frequency of intake by the portion size. Finally, the nutrient and energy intakes of the participants were assessed utilizing the Iranian-modified version of the Nutritionist IV software, and three dietary indices including DIS, DII, and EDIP were determined from the questionnaires.

DIS calculation

The dietary data obtained from the FFQ were utilized to compute the DIS. The DIS score comprises 19 food items from the FFQ questionnaire, excluding supplement consumption due to insufficient data. Finally, the DIS score was calculated for 18 food groups, such as nuts, added sugars, low-fat dairy, high-fat dairy, other vegetables, red and organ meats, processed meats, legumes, starchy vegetables, natural fruit juices, other fruits, deep orange or yellow vegetables and fruits, tomatoes, apples and berries, leafy greens and cruciferous vegetables, poultry, fish, other fats, refined grains, and tea. These food items were selected based on their influence on inflammatory biomarkers like interleukin (IL)-10, high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP), IL-8, and IL-6. A specific weight was defined based on its impact on inflammatory biomarkers to compute the score for each food item. The weight value of each food group is obtained by multiplying each one by its specific weight. The Z-score was subsequently utilized to standardize each food group, and the resulting values were aggregated to compute the participants' overall DIS [18, 19].

DII calculation

Pro-inflammatory factors like CRP, IL-6, IL-1β, tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), or anti-inflammatory biomarkers like IL-10 and IL-4 have been identified that could be negatively or positively affected by food components and nutrients. The DII was computed from dietary intake data collected through an FFQ questionnaire, with 45 nutrient components selected for their positive or negative effects on inflammatory biomarkers. Based on this, a +1 score is given for decreasing anti-inflammatory or increasing inflammatory factors, and a -1 score is given to components and nutrients that increase anti-inflammatory or decrease inflammatory factors, while a score of 0 is given if it does not affect any factors.

According to Iranian food culture, we used 30 food items instead of the original 45 to calculate the DII score because of the non-consumption of some foods by Iranians or lack of data on some items in the Iranian food database. These 30 items in calculating the Iranian DII score are fat, vitamin B12, cholesterol, saturated fat, trans fat, protein, carbohydrate, energy, iron, and anti-inflammatory factors including garlic, onion, tea, fiber, caffeine, magnesium, polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs), monounsaturated fatty acids (MUFAs), zinc, selenium, and vitamins B9, B6, B3, B2, B1, E, A, D, C, as well as β-carotene.

After calculating the energy-adjusted amount of nutrients by the residual method, the “standard global mean” was subtracted from each participant’s intake. Subsequently, the result was divided by the global standard deviation to compute the Z-score for each food item.

To achieve a normal distribution of our data, the scores were converted to centered percentiles. We then multiplied each participant’s score by the effect score associated with the corresponding food items. In our study, we employed the standard deviations, standard global mean, and food parameter effect scores provided by Shivappa et al. [20]. Men with high DII scores, calculated by accumulating all 30 foods’ DII scores, may consume more inflammatory food items, while those with lower DII scores may have a less inflammatory diet [21, 22].

EDIP calculation

According to the research conducted by Tabung et al. [5], the EDIP score is created by 18 food items and diagnostic biomarkers such as TNF-α, IL-6, and hs-CRP. The specific weight of each item is determined by its correlation with biomarker levels. The research team excluded three food items in calculating scores. Alcoholic beverages such as beer and wine are either uncommon or may go unreported among our participants due to their religious and cultural preferences; therefore, we decided to exclude these items. We also excluded low-energy beverages because we did not have these food items in our FFQ questionnaire. Thus, the EDIP score was calculated according to 15 items including tomatoes, low-energy and high-energy drinks (carbonated beverages with sugar, fruit punch drinks, and cola with sugar), refined grains (pasta, vermicelli, white bread, white rice, or biscuits), other vegetables (cucumber, zucchini, eggplant, cooked mushrooms, green pepper, mixed vegetables), other fish (fish or canned tuna), organ meat (chicken liver, calf, or beef), processed meat (sausage), and red meat (lamb or beef) as a pro-inflammatory group and dark yellow vegetables (squash or carrots), snacks (crackers or potato chips), pizza, fruit juice (cantaloupe juice, orange juice, apple juice, or other fruit juices), leafy green vegetables (lettuce, spinach, or cabbage), coffee, and tea as an anti-inflammatory group.

To calculate the score, we multiplied each item’s average daily intake by its assigned weight and subsequently summed all the weighted values. The total score was then divided by 1000 to adjust its magnitude. A greater score denotes a more pro-inflammatory diet according to both dietary inflammatory indices, whereas a lower score indicates the opposite [18, 23, 24].

Evaluation of stress, anxiety, and depression

The research team used a 21-item questionnaire known as the Depression, Anxiety, and Stress Scales (DASS-21) to assess stress, anxiety, and depression levels in all participants. We used Cronbach’s alpha coefficient to investigate the questionnaire’s reliability and validity, which was confirmed to be 0.84. Similar internal consistency coefficients have been reported in previous studies [25]. The questionnaire comprises seven questions for each of the three subscales: anxiety, stress, and depression. Each question provides four response options, with scores ranging from 3 (applicable to me very much or most of the time) to 0 (not applicable to me at all). The scores for each subscale were determined by analyzing the responses to these questions.

Assessment of other variables

To complete the demographic data, the participants’ medical history, details about cigarette use, supplement intake, weight (with 0.1 kg accuracy), height, and waist circumference (with 0.5 cm accuracy) were collected. BMI was computed in kilograms per square meter. To assess the physical activity levels of the participants, we employed the abbreviated version of the International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ) [26].

Statistical analysis

To analyze the data, SPSS software for Windows (version 26.0) was utilized. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered significant. The relationship between categorical variables was evaluated using the chi-square test, while the relationship between continuous variables was assessed through analysis of variance (ANOVA). The Kruskal-Wallis U test was applied to assess non-parametric continuous variables.

The relationship between the EDIP, DIS, and DII scores and sperm parameters—specifically, sperm abnormalities such as total sperm motility less than or equal to 40%, sperm concentration less than or equal to 15 (106/mL), and morphology of normal sperm equal to or greater than 4%—was evaluated using logistic regression. The regression analysis comprised two models. Model I was a crude model, while Model II was adjusted for several factors, including BMI (kg/m²), age (years), energy intake (kcal/day), marriage duration (years), physical activity (metabolic equivalent of task (MET) hours per day (MET.h/day)), stress, anxiety, and depression. It also accounted for cigarette history (no/yes) and the intake of vitamin and mineral supplements (no/yes).

Discussion

In this study, we evaluated the association between dietary inflammatory indices (EDIP, DIS, and DII) and the risk of infertility in 260 infertile adult men. Our findings indicate that greater adherence to a pro-inflammatory dietary pattern, reflected by higher DII scores, was associated with increased odds of male infertility. In the crude model, no significant associations were observed between any of the three indices and sperm quality parameters; however, in the adjusted model, only DII showed a significant association with abnormal sperm concentration. Additionally, men with higher DII scores tended to consume more red meat and refined grains, suggesting that adherence to a pro-inflammatory diet characterized by these foods may contribute to an elevated risk of infertility.

Inflammation and oxidative stress are key mechanisms linking diet to male infertility. Elevated levels of inflammatory markers such as TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1 have been reported in infertile men, and TNF-α, in particular, may contribute to sperm apoptosis and asthenozoospermia [27–29]. The impact of inflammation on reproduction may be attributed to two mechanisms: a direct detrimental effect on spermatozoa and/or functional and anatomical alterations in the male accessory glands [30]. Inflammation can also trigger oxidative stress, leading to structural and functional damage in male reproductive tissues [3, 30]. Moreover, unhealthy fats, especially trans fatty acids common in Western dietary patterns, may impair sperm metabolism through the downregulation of peroxisome proliferator-activated receptors (PPARs), which are nuclear receptors involved in sperm function [30]. Collectively, these pathways suggest that a pro-inflammatory diet may impair fertility by promoting oxidative stress, inflammation, and dysregulation of PPAR-mediated processes.

However, evidence of the association between EDIP, DIS, and DII indices is scarce [27]. Eslamian et al. performed two case-control studies involving 235 age-matched controls and 107 men with asthenozoospermia to explore the relationship between the risk of asthenozoospermia and nutrient patterns and dietary patterns. They reported that greater adherence to the Western pro-inflammatory dietary pattern was linked to increased odds of asthenozoospermia. In contrast, stronger adherence to a dietary pattern high in anti-inflammatory and antioxidant nutrients was linked to lower odds of asthenozoospermia [31, 32]. Ognjenlvic et al., in cross-sectional research, similarly demonstrated that following a Western dietary pattern is inversely linked to male reproductive health [33]. Nassan et al., also in a cross-sectional study, showed that adherence to a generally healthy dietary pattern is related to better fertility and semen quality [34]. Oostingh et al. showed a direct relationship between semen parameters and following a healthy dietary pattern in men with poor semen quality [35]. The research by Liu et al. revealed the negative impact of a Western diet, high carbohydrate intake, sugar-sweetened beverages, and sugary snacks on semen quality [36]. Moreover, adherence to the Western dietary pattern, described by an increased intake of red meat, high-fat dairy products, and refined grains, is related to pro-inflammatory status [29, 37, 38]. It has been shown that red meat consumption may be associated with increased oxidative stress and inflammation [39]. Papier et al. also reported the positive effect of increasing red meat consumption on increasing the level of inflammatory markers [40].

Strengths and limitations

The current research has several strengths, including its evaluation of the relationship between following a pro-inflammatory dietary pattern—represented by indicators such as the EDIP, DIS, and DII scores—and the risk of infertility among Iranian women for the first time. Additionally, a reliable and valid FFQ specifically designed for the Iranian population has been employed to evaluate dietary intake. However, one limitation of the present study is the use of an FFQ; as a common dietary assessment method, it is subject to recall bias and measurement error. Furthermore, the relatively small sample size of 260 men may limit the generalizability of our findings. Also, the cross-sectional design of the current research is one of the other main limitations, which does not allow the analysis of causal associations to the validity of the present study’s results. Some potential confounding factors, such as occupational exposures and other unmeasured variables, were not assessed in this study; therefore, residual confounding cannot be ruled out.

Conclusions

The findings of the current study indicated that following a pro-inflammatory dietary pattern, as reflected by a greater DII score, negatively impacts sperm quality parameters, including sperm concentration. This may be related to higher odds of infertility. However, it is proposed that more large prospective studies be performed to validate the findings of the current study, as well as provide a comprehensive and accurate assessment of the relationship between the risk of infertility and a pro-inflammatory diet.
