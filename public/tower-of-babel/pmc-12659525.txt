Patient reported barriers and facilitators of chronic kidney disease care in Sub-Saharan Africa: a mixed methods systematic review using the COM-B model
Authors: Habtamu Abera Areri, Leul Derbie Ketaw, Hailemariam Berhe, Moses Mwangi Gitonga, Samuel Kimani, Jacob Kariuki, Lisa Thompson, Rebecca Gary
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIn Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA), the prevalence of chronic kidney disease (CKD) is on the rise. Treatment for CKD in SSA is characterized by delayed initiation, early discontinuation, inadequate duration of therapy, and suboptimal follow-up. To our level of understanding, evidence on the barriers and facilitators of CKD care in SSA has not been comprehensively reported and is the focus of this mixed methods systematic review.MethodsThis convergent integrated Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) methodology for mixed systematic review followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses (PRISMA) 2020 guidelines. Six databases were utilized and included Medline, Ovid, Cumulative Nursing and Allied Health Literature (CINAHL), EMBASE, Web of Science, and Scopus for studies. Peer-reviewed articles published in the English language between 2000 and July 30, 2024, were included. Qualitative, quantitative, or mixed-method studies that reported health care, treatment barriers, and facilitators among patients with CKD residing in SSA were included. Two independent reviewers screened, appraised, and extracted data from included studies using a standardized JBI SUMARI data extraction form. A deductive data analysis approach was used to match the findings to the six components of the COM-B (capability, opportunity, motivation) behavioral change model.ResultsThe review identified 10,627 records, 155 of which were found to be eligible for full-text screening, and 28 of these articles met the inclusion criteria. The review included 15 quantitative and 13 qualitative studies with a total of 2,178 participants with ages ranging from 18 to 86 years. Fatigue and comorbidities were commonly discussed under capability barriers. Economic constraints, unavailability of drugs, care facility inaccessibility, and distance were commonly explained under opportunity barriers. Depression and uncertainty of the future were commonly discussed motivational barriers, whereas social support and a positive attitude towards CKD care were key facilitators of CKD care.ConclusionWe used a theory-informed approach to synthesize barriers and facilitators of CKD care and then mapped the identified barriers to behavioral change wheel interventions using the Taxonomy of Behavioral Change Technique. Education, training, enablement, environmental restructuring, and persuasion were commonly mapped to identify barriers. Further research is recommended to identify contextually appropriate interventions to address barriers and capitalize on facilitators.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12882-025-04602-5.Keywords: Dialysis, Chronic kidney disease, Adherence, Barriers, Facilitators, Behavioral change wheel, Sub-Saharan Africa

Background

The global prevalence of Chronic Kidney Disease (CKD) ranges from 8 to 16% with the majority of cases occurring in Low- and Middle Income Countries (LMICs) [1, 2]. CKD is a state of progressive irreversible loss of kidney function resulting in renal failure to maintain metabolic, fluid and electrolyte balance [3, 4]. With an increasing life expectancy in Africa, the prevalence of CKD is expected to dramatically rise over the next several decades [5]. In Africa, the pooled prevalence of CKD in the general population was 10.1% (ranged from 2% to 41%), while it was 14% in Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) countries [6, 7]. The high prevalence of CKD in SSA places it globally as one of the regions with the greatest burden [8]. Unlike developed countries, in SSA CKD is more common among younger and working populations, and the death rate is significantly higher due to the absence of early screening and treatment resources [7, 9, 10].

Findings from a review conducted on CKD patients in high income nations (United Kingdom, the USA and other European countries) and other studies from LMICs reported barriers associated with CKD care such as late referral, multiple co-morbidities, lack of education, low level of knowledge/awareness, communication problems and a shortage of CKD care teams. Furthermore, drug-drug interaction, hypertension, depression in CKD patients and economic challenges were cited as important barriers in CKD care [11, 12].

In most of SSA, high cost of treatment, lack of skilled providers, and limited facilities for renal therapy are thought to hinder CKD treatment, reducing quality of life, raising health care costs and utilization, and resulting in premature death [13]. Treatment (especially hemodialysis) for CKD in LMICs is characterized by delayed initiation, inadequate duration per session, frequent discontinuation and reduced frequency of sessions per week. In addition, the poor quality of services exhibited in LMICs are may be due to the shortage of, and in-adequately trained health professionals [14–17]. Overall, long standing issues of access and affordability (e.g., lack of health insurance coverage, unavailability of equipment, cost treatment, distance to care centers), lack of support, unemployment, limited formal education, health literacy and comorbidities remain key challenges to CKD care in the SSA [18]. All these factors contribute to the overwhelming burden for families and patients requiring CKD care [19]. However, no study has comprehensively synthesized and explained the barriers and facilitators of CKD care in the SSA region using a behavioral change model. We, therefore, comprehensively review and synthesize existing evidence on the barriers and facilitators of CKD care in SSA.

The COM-B model and CKD-care

It is well established that evidence-based strategies exist for the treatment of CKD and when implemented improve clinical outcomes. However, in SSA there remain gaps in the implementation of these strategies. The COM-B (capability, opportunity, motivation-behavior) framework is commonly used to identify barriers and facilitators of a behavior [20]. The COM-B/BCW model is commonly used for the synthesis of evidence in a systematic review of barriers and facilitators of different behaviors [21–27]. In addition, the Behavioral Change Wheel (BCW) is useful for identifying interventions that address the barriers and facilitators. Capability can be psychological (e.g., knowledge of CKD) or physical (e.g., absence of symptom), opportunity can be physical (e.g., resources) or social (e.g., social support) and motivation which could be reflective (e.g., trust in service) or automatic (e.g., emotional reactions) can either facilitate or prevent behavioral change [20] (see Figs. 1 and 2). BCW is a key tool for designing behavioral change interventions and it is a starting point for intervention development. The COM-B domains, which lie at the center of BCW, can be mapped onto the BCW intervention strategies and the Behavior Change Technique Taxonomy that are likely to be appropriate and effective in addressing barriers and capitalize facilitators of CKD care [20, 28]. To the best of our knowledge, there has been no systematic review on CKD care among CKD patients in SSA, and no studies have contextualized CKD care in the region using the COM-B model. This review was designed to answer (1) what are barriers and facilitators of CKD care among patients residing in SSA; and (2) what intervention strategies improve identified barriers and capitalize enablers of getting CKD care in patients residing in SSA. Therefore, this review aimed to synthesize evidence using both available quantitative and qualitative studies on barriers and facilitators of getting CKD care in SSA using the COM-B framework and to identify intervention strategies to improve CKD care using BCW. The mixed methods helped us to better comprehensively understand existing barriers and facilitators of CKD care in the region.

Methods

The Convergent integrated JBI methodology for a mixed methods systematic review (MMSR)was followed [29]. This approach was followed to get better understanding of barriers and facilitators of CKD care. The review was conducted according to our prior registered protocol (PROSPERO registration number: CRD42024583721). Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses (PRISMA) 2020 guidelines was used to present the findings of the review [30]. We also used the Enhancing Transparency in Reporting the Synthesis of Qualitative Research (ENTREQ) guidelines for reporting the findings [31].

Setting

The review included studies with any types of CKD patients residing in SSA countries.

Search strategy

Our search strategy located published studies using a three-step approach. Our initial search was limited to PubMed using key words. The text words contained in the titles and abstracts, and the index terms used to describe the articles, were used to develop a full search strategy for Medline, Ovid, Embase, CINAHL, Scopus, and Web of Sciences databases (see Supplement Table S3). The reference lists of all included articles were screened for additional articles that met our inclusion criteria. References of studies included in this review, or similar topics were used to locate additional records for review. Studies published in English language from 2000 to July 30, 2024, were included in this review. We decided to use this review period as there was no published systematic review on CKD care in the included study period. The search was conducted from August 1 to 30, 2024.

Types of studies

This systematic review included peer-reviewed published quantitative, qualitative or mixed method studies that included studies that addressed barriers and facilitators of CKD patients care residing in SSA.

Eligibility criteria

Sample

The review considered primary studies published in English with a report of CKD care in SSA or discussed barriers and facilitators of getting CKD care (on dialysis or medication, or other lifestyle recommendations). The review excluded acute kidney injuries, studies not describing CKD interventions such as medication(s), fluid and electrolyte assessment, symptom monitoring and other medical treatments for CKD care. All patients with CKD were included in our review.

Phenomena of interest

Studies that evaluated CKD care such as all types of dialysis, medical treatments, and lifestyle modifications.

Design

Studies published in peer review journals, survey, phenomenological study, qualitative descriptive studies, observational studies, qualitative descriptive studies and mixed method studies.

Evaluation/outcomes

The review included the following: primary or secondary outcomes of barriers and facilitators to get CKD care. The review included studies with primary outcomes of dialysis care, lifestyle recommendations, drug(s) treatment, and treatment experience whereas secondary outcomes such as treatment adherence and it related associated factors, complications, and quality of life. We excluded papers that did not focus on understanding barriers and facilitators of CKD care.

Research types

Quantitative and qualitative designs were included in our review.

Study selection

After the search strategy was finalized, identified citations were collated and uploaded into EndNote version 21.0, and duplicates were removed. Titles and abstracts were screened using eligibility criteria by two independent reviewers (HA & LD). Potentially relevant studies were retrieved in full and their citation details imported into the JBI System for the Unified Management, Assessment and Review of Information (JBI SUMARI) [32]. The full texts of selected citations were assessed in detail against the inclusion criteria by two independent reviewers (HA & LD). Reasons for exclusion of papers included those that did not meet the inclusion criteria and were recorded in the final PRISMA flow diagram. Any disagreements that arose during the screening and selection process were resolved through discussion and consensus between the primary reviewers (HA & LD). The results of the search and the study inclusion process were recorded and reported in full in the PRISMA flow diagram [30] (Fig. 3).

Prisma flow diagram of the search and study selection process [30]

Methodological quality

Two independent reviewers appraised all included studies (LD & HA). Quantitative (and a quantitative component of mixed methods) papers selected for retrieval were assessed by two independent reviewers at the study level for methodological validity before inclusion in the review using standardized critical appraisal instruments from JBI [33]. Qualitative papers (and qualitative components of mixed methods papers) selected for retrieval were assessed by two independent reviewers for methodological validity prior to inclusion in the review using the standardized critical appraisal instrument for cross-sectional and qualitative studies from JBI [34]. All studies (see Supplement Table S1), regardless of the results of their methodological quality, have undergone data extraction and synthesis.

Data extraction

Data extraction of both quantitative and qualitative studies were carried out by two independent reviewers (HA & LD) using JBI standardized data extraction tool in JBI SUMARI [35]. The extracted data included specific details about the study populations, methods, context, sample size, type of CKD cares (such as dialysis, drug(s) treatment and supportive care), and outcomes of significance to the review objective. Specifically, quantitative data were comprised of database outcomes of descriptive and/or inferential statistical tests. In addition, qualitative data was comprised of themes or subthemes with corresponding illustrations.

Data transformation

To facilitate data integration of both qualitative and quantitative studies, quantitative data were transformed into qualitized data using the JBI methodology for mixed methods systematic reviews [36]. This procedure involved transformation of quantitative data into textual descriptions or narrative interpretation to respond directly to the review question. The transformed textual descriptions were then assembled and pooled with the qualitative data extracted directly from qualitative studies (See Table 1). Then, authors undertook repeated, detailed examination of the assembled data to identify categories based on this review guiding model (COM-B) domains and its related six components.

Data synthesis and integration

A convergent integrated approach was used for data synthesis and integration according to the JBI methodology for Mixed Methods Systematic Review [29, 35]. HA and LD indecently read the result section of all included studies to determine its relevance in relation to the review questions. Then, data transformation process carried out by HA from quantitative data to qualitative data using JBI methodology. It involved assembling the qualitized data with the qualitative data to answer the review question. Then, assembled data were categorized and pooled together based on similarity in meaning to produce a set of integrated findings in the form of line of action statements. Finally, the findings of synthesis and integrated findings were configured under COM-B model domains (Capability, Opportunity and Motivation for behaviour). The barriers and facilitators of CKD care were mapped into the six sub-components of the COM-B Model Based on factors identified under each COM-B components intervention strategies based on BCW framework was proposed.

Barriers to CKD care

Physical capability

Fatigue and other bodily symptoms like restricted life and chronic physical exchaustion were commonly identified as physical capability barriers across five included studies [52, 55, 59, 60, 62]. Physical fatigue was the most common barrier affecting patients’ daily activity, social interaction and access to health care such as CKD care. For instance in one of the included studies, post-treatment fatigue interfered with family life and even relationships usually due to extreme tiredness [60]. Participants from included studies also complained that they were developing other medical conditions such as coagulation problem, treatment side effects, hypertension, lung diseases, gastritis and bone related symptoms which could further prevent them from accessing care and treatment [56, 57, 60, 62]. Comorbidites remain a major barrier for limiting access to CKD care as they causing limited physical capability [40, 42, 43, 45]. In our review, a higher grade of CKD has been also identified as a barrier to accessing care with higher grade associated with poorer access [42, 43, 62].

Psychological capability

Limited understanding of instruction given by healthcare providers to patients due to different reasons remain a capability barrier.It could be the instruction was not clearly communicated or CKD patinets did not recieved the information or thier level of understanding was limited [37, 47, 48]. This limited understanding particularly in regards to drug(s) is further complicated by multiple drug(s) prescriptions which could lead to non-compliance. In this review, non-compliance to prescribed medications was reported barrier for CKD care [42, 43, 48]. Besides, include pateients reported thaat limited knowledge of CKD care, management and symptoms as a barrier to CKD care [46, 63].

Physical opportunity

Economic challenges/financial constraints remained key barriers in almost half of the included papers [39, 40, 45, 51–53, 57, 59–63]. Unafforadability of medication(s) in the health facilities was discussed by CKD patients as another challenge in relation to physical opportunity [38, 44, 48, 50, 53, 54]. Our review also highlighted, unavailability/inacessibility of renal care providers as a common barrier to get CKD care [38, 40, 53, 54, 59, 62, 63]. CKD patients also commonly cited distance to CKD centers as a barrier for getting CKD care and treament [38, 40, 53, 54, 59, 62, 63]. Long geographic distances was further complicated by a lack of transportation to and from CKD care centers [50, 57, 59]. In addition, a lack of insurance coverage [38, 63], inadequate supplies [54] and limited attention from government [38] were reported as barriers associated with physical opportunity.

Social opportunity

Unemployment due to renal condition was a major social challenge for CKD care recipients [48, 56, 61, 62]. Restriction in social life and a lack of social support was the source of suffering and poor QOL [59–61]. In one study, for example, haemodialysis was seen as a hindrance to the desired way of life. Dependency on the haemodialysis machine resulted in patients feeling inferior and was associated with a loss of freedom [60]. Overall, a lack of social support was viewed as a major challenge in CKD care [62].

Reflective motivation

Unaffordable cost of medications was a reason for a lack of motivation to seek CKD care [48, 57, 62]. Family caregivers also had lower motivation to continue with treatment due to depleted resources for care and support. Inadequate attention from the government [38], and negative attitude towards diet advice (lifesyle recommondation) [47] were identified as motivational barriers in several studies. Participants also found healthcare providers were not motivated to provide the required care and treament for CKD patients [51].

Automatic motivation

Depression, anxiety, and a sense of despiration were mentioned as motivational barriers that patients encounter on a daily basis in the process of getting CKD care [41, 52, 59, 60]. A further motivational barriers in CKD care were tension, uncertainty, and worry they experienced in their daily lives due to the inaccessibility of renal care centers, the increasing expense of treatment, and their worsening health condition [41, 60, 61].

Facilitators of CKD care

Physical capability

Symptom alleviation after engaging in CKD care was reported as facilitator by CKD patients [57] and also served as a motivator of adherence to their treatment. CKD treatment was considered as a valuable means to decrease the burden of physical symptoms which made them uncomfortable and compromised their normal life. Improved health status and quality of life were also viewed as faciliatator of CKD care [57].

Pyschological capability

Patients who were informed about CKD care and treatment process or recieved education had better psychological capacity for utilizing avaiable CKD care in some studies [47, 57]. CKD patients with a higher educational level were more engaged in CKD care and related reommondations than those who were less educated [41, 49]. In one study, CKD patients with higher educational level and with better self-efficacy for self-management also had better CKD care outcomes such as carried out self-care recommendations and better engaged in CKD care and treatment programs. At the same time, better knowledge of CKD care was associated with enhanced treatment adherence [41]. Patients with CKD who had a more favorable view of their enhanced health status were also identified as a facilitator for CKD management [37].

Physical opportunity

Economical Support [62], existence of favourable government policy to support CKD care [63], availability of more renal centres [63] and health insurance [54] were discussed as facilitators in association with physical opportunity. CKD patients who received economic support from others were associated with better CKD care. The participants also stated that the economic support obtained played an irreplaceable role in the sustainability of their engagement in CKD care. Similarly, the kind of care they recieved from healthcare providers facilitated CKD care uptake [62].

Social opportunity

Social support obtained from family, friends or community members enabled CKD patients to recieve better CKD care in a sustainable way [37, 38, 49, 54, 57, 60, 62]. Supportive communication, sharing feelings, receiving counselling, and empathy from social groups/talking and sharing feelings and challenges with significant others and patients in similar condition were discussed by participants as a social opportunity of CKD care [53, 60–62]. Participants from one of the included studies commented as their CKD care uptake was facilitated by a positive relationship they had with their healthcare providers [62].

Reflective motivation

Participants discussed the need to adhere to treatment for the purpose of prolonging their lives and improving quality of life [57, 58]. Positive attitude towards CKD care was described as a motivator to participant in CKD care [40, 58, 62, 64]. In one of these studies, participants said they have an internal motivation of maintaining their good health, routine monitoring of their health status including strict follow up of medical recommendations [62]. Awareness of consequence of late CKD care was also discussed as motivator of CKD care [55]. Religiosity/faith in God was discussed as motivator of adherence to treatment. CKD patients religious belief also was a motivator to engage in CKD care [53, 62, 64]. In one study, having a positive relationship with healthcare providers enhanced their engagement in care [62]. Besides, trust in healthcare was discussed as motivator to engage in CKD care [57, 62].

Automatic motivation

CKD patients were more likely to follow their treatment regimen because they did not want to die. Fear of being removed from care [55], psychological and emotional support from clinicians [53] were discussed as motivators in engaging in CKD care.

Discussion

In this systematic review, we identified several important barriers and facilitators of CKD care among persons living with CKD in SSA using an established behavioral model [COM-B]. This is the the first application of this model to our knowledge. All factors identified in this study were placed into one of the six categories of the COM-B model. The COM-B model with the behavioral change wheel (BCW) is utilized in discussing our findings in light of potential future intervention functions (see Supplement Table S2) [20].

Capability barriers such as physical fatigue and other body symptoms were commonly identified. For instance, those who felt physical fatigue and had other body symptoms remained less involved in CKD care, which could be related to inadequate access and distance to care facilities. The distance factor could be addressed through environmental restructuring (i.e, decenteralizing renal care centers and increasing access) functions of BCWs. To overcome the shortage of renal care providers, SSA healthcare delivery system could use technologies (such as mobile apps) to manage routine symptoms. Patients with commorbidities (Hypertension and diabetes) were also shown to have inadequate CKD care. These patients are forced to take multiple drugs and follow different kinds of life style managements. Existence of multiple morbidity could also exacerbate symptoms and side effects, which all could lead to poor engagement in care. Hence, in such and other senarios attention should be given to education, persuasion and training to further enable CKD patients to enroll in an optimal level of CKD care. Our recommendation is in line with a systematic review conducted in developed countries [11] and a scoping review conducted in SSA [18]. CKD patients developed other medical conditions after starting CKD care and this prevented them from fully engaging in CKD care and other treatment recommendations [56, 57, 60, 62], which is consistent with our findings [11, 12].

Limited understanding of instructions given by healthcare providers and limited knowledge of CKD care were identified as barriers affecting CKD patients’ capabilities in seeking CKD care, which is consistent with a systematic review on CKD care in developed countries [11]. As a result of limited understanding of medication instructions, patients failed to compliance to treatment modalities, further complicating health status. As a result, CKD patients had to take multiple medications. Indeed, non-compliance to medication(s) remains a serious concern in CKD care. Besides, non-compliance to CKD care was affected by lack of transportation and cost of therapy [44, 50, 59]. The BCW intervention functions such as education, training, persuasion and enablement are recommonded to address limited understanding and/or knowledge of CKD care and its treatment. Above all, health care providers should give due emphasis on enhancing patients understanding once CKD is confrimed to improve patients’ compliance to its care. Our recommondation concurs with other studies [12, 65].

Opportunity barriers such as financial constraints, cost of medications, inaccessibility of CKD care, distance and transportation cost, unemployement and restricted social life [59–61] were identified as crucial barriers to CKD care. Our review corroborates other studies [12, 18, 65] conducted in SSA and other developing countries [12, 66] with respect to unaffordability, inadequate health care access and distance to facility. Participants who live in area with limited access to CKD care were forced to leave their family and move to areas with better access to get the recommended CKD care and support. In most cases, an advanced care centre wasn’t found in the area where patients live, so CKD patients were forced to live in a rental home, which also created further financial challenges. These factors can be addressed through physical and social environmental restructuring [20, 28]. Ensuring access to CKD care is an essential aspect of the environmental restructuring intervention function of BCW. Therefore, establishing adequate CKD care centers and a waiving/providing the care with reduced/affordable cost would benefit patients with CKD. To reduce the distance factor, common barriers across several studies, the healthcare system should restructure the physical environment, which includes decentralization of CKD care facilities and providers to the most peripheral areas. Our recommendation is in alignment with previous studies [12, 67–69]. Government and non-governmental organizations working to improve the quality of life of CKD patients need to pay adequate attention and ensure the availability of renal centers in areas that are easily accessible to patients. Regarding unemployment, our finding concurs with a scoping review conducted in Africa [18]. CKD patients faced restriction in social life and further suffering from lack of social support. Participants reported that lack of social support remains a major challenge in the process of CKD care although they expected financial, psycho-social, and moral supports [62].

Motivation is a key behavioral factor in BCW, and it improves one’s ability to maintain desired behavior [in our case, engaging in CKD care] [28]. The presence of depression, anxiety and despair were mentioned as motivational barriers. Patients with CKD struggle daily with such psychological issues as constant fear, depression, and despair, directly and indirectly associated with their medical conditions which is consistent with literature [11]. Our findings across several studies demonstrated that participants were experiencing tension, uncertainty, and stress or anxiety in their daily lives. These motivational barriers could be addressed by using education and persuasion function of BCWs. Thus, healthcare providers should screen for those barriers and should provide information on the health consequences of inadequate CKD care in a way that patients understand the benefits of engaging in CKD care programs. Routine counseling (as part of the persuasion function of BCW) will also encourage CKD patients to engage in CKD care. Our review showed that the cost of treatment remains expensive and unaffordable to many patients. These factors can be addressed by persuasion and enablement [in this case, a governmental subsidy], an intervention function of the BCW. Above all, healthcare providers should demonstrate effective therapeutic communication, build positive relationships with CKD patients and initate early referrals. Better communication and early referrals to better facility could reduces complixicity of the disease and enhance management of patients’ symptoms [70]

Strengths and limitations

Our review included both quantitative and qualitative studies to better understand barriers and facilitators of CKD care. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first systematic review that evaluated the barriers to and facilitators of CKD care of patients using the COM-B theoretical framework, pointing to possible intervention functions to improve CKD care in SSA. Despite identified strengths, this systematic review has several limitations. The identified barriers and facilitators were identified from CKD patients’ perspectives from some countries in SSA and not the entire region. The views of healthcare providers were not included. Another limitation of this review could be heterogenity of the included study designs. The inclusion of studies limited to the English language could not capture factors addressed in other languages. Although our search strategy was broad and comprehensive, it was limited to only peer-reviewed publications.

Recommendations for practice

The review identified different barriers across the three domain of COM-B model which could be addressed by using BCW intervention fuctions and Taxonomy of Behavioral Change Techniques. Enabling patients with chronic renal conditions to get optimal CKD care should be prioritized. Interventions to address CKD care should address the main barriers identified in this review and should include strategies to properly train renal care providers and ensure acceassble renal care centers with minimal cost of medication(s) and supplies. Availablity of adequately trained health care personnel in CKD care centers should receive emphasis from policy makers in SSA. Accessible trained CKD providers could be part of the solution for enhancing community awareness of CKD care, decentralizing renal care service, and provision of the service with minimal cost. Limited information about CKD and its care could be addressed by creating awareness by trained renal care providers and then ensured access to CKD care. Whenever possible, patients’ concerns should be addressed through education and training. There should be attention from the government regarding renal care subsidy like insurance coverage, as cost of care and medication(s) remain crucial barriers. Healthcare providers should establish a positive theraputic communication environment to enhance a trusting relationship between patients and providers. The renal care service should be decentralized to reduce the cost of travel and related expenses.SSA healthcare providers should leverage dignital technologies to reduce costs and overcome structural barriers. Patients with CKD are also expected to mobilize available community resources and become motivated to engage in CKD care and improve their well-being. Finally, addressing capability, opportunity and motivational barriers should be a continued and essential process.

Conclusions

Our review used theoritically informed approach to synthesize facilitators and barriers of CKD care. We used the Behavioral Change Wheel to map intervention to barriers of CKD care. The Taxonomy of Behavioural change Techniques helped describe the content and approaches of intervention to address the identified barriers. Training and education intervention functions of BCW were commonly mapped barriers across the three domain of COM-B model. Overall, training, education, enablement, environmental restructuring and persuasion intervention functions of BCW were mapped different barriers influencing CKD care in SSA. Further research is recommended to identify contextually appropriate interventions and behavioral change techniques which would be appropriate to address barriers and capitalize on facilitators.
