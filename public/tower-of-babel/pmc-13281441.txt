Gas6 restores microglial efferocytosis and limits neuroinflammation in neonatal hypoxic-ischemic encephalopathy by activating MerTK and the PI3K–Rac1 pathway
Authors: Yu Liu, Ayipaxiaguli Kasimu, Yuting Song, Jinke Liu, Chenqin Si, Qiuyi Chen, Yiyun Zhang, Lijian Xie, Danmei Chen, Bing Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimHypoxic-ischemic encephalopathy is a major cause of neonatal disability and mortality. Its core pathology involves extensive neuronal apoptosis and persistent inflammatory responses. Microglia play a crucial role in maintaining brain homeostasis and promoting injury repair by recognizing and clearing apoptotic neurons. However, the regulatory mechanisms underlying this process remain unclear.MethodThis study employed a co-culture model of apoptotic neurons, phagocytic function assays, cytokine analysis, transcriptome sequencing, Gas6 gene knockout and rescue experiments, combined with a mouse model of hypoxic-ischemic brain injury, to elucidate the role of microglia in the phagocytic process and the regulatory function of Gas6.ResultInjured neurons induced an early phase of pro-inflammatory activation and enhanced phagocytic capacity in microglia, followed by a shift towards an anti-inflammatory function. Transcriptome analysis suggested that co-culture with injured neurons activated pathways such as PI3K-AKT and NF-κB in microglia, concomitant with a significant upregulation of Gas6. Furthermore, we found that Gas6 deficiency significantly reduced the phosphorylation level of TAM receptors, leading to impaired downstream PI3K/AKT activation and a marked decrease in Rac1-GTP, thereby suppressing cytoskeletal rearrangement and phagocytic function. In parallel, Gas6-deficient microglia exhibited a sustained pro-inflammatory response, with both their efferocytic capacity and ability to regulate inflammation being significantly compromised. In vivo experiments showed that Gas6-KO mice displayed more severe neurological deficits, increased neuronal apoptosis, and stronger inflammatory responses after HIE. Supplementation with exogenous Gas6 elevated TAM receptor phosphorylation and the PI3K/AKT–Rac1 signaling pathway, partially restoring the phagocytic capacity of microglia.ConclusionThis study demonstrates the important role of the Gas6–TAM–PI3K/AKT–Rac1 signaling axis in modulating microglial efferocytic function and inflammatory state transition. It provides a potential therapeutic strategy for improving HIE prognosis by targeting the regulation of microglial phagocytosis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12964-026-02911-2.Keywords: Hypoxic-Ischemic Encephalopathy, Microglia, Efferocytosis, Growth arrest-specific protein 6, MERTK, PI3K/AKT signaling pathway, Neuroinflammation

Background

Hypoxic-ischemic encephalopathy (HIE) is one of the leading causes of neonatal death and long-term neurological sequelae [1]. Following cerebral ischemia and hypoxia, a complex cascade of events is triggered, ultimately leading to extensive programmed neuronal death [2–4]. The timely and efficient clearance of damaged neurons during HIE recovery is particularly critical [5, 6]. Within the recovery window, the phagocytic function mediated by microglia determines the success of inflammation suppression and tissue repair. Neuronal programmed cell death can occur in various forms, such as apoptosis, necroptosis, and pyroptosis [7, 8]. Among these, apoptosis is one of the predominant modes of neuronal death in HIE, especially within the ischemic penumbra [9, 10]. A hallmark of apoptotic cells is the maintenance of membrane integrity and the exposure of "eat-me" signals such as phosphatidylserine, enabling their recognition and clearance by professional phagocytes [11]. This process is known as efferocytosis [12]. In the central nervous system, microglia are the primary phagocytes responsible for efferocytosis [13–15]. They can sensitively recognize, engulf, and phagocytose apoptotic neurons [16], thereby preventing their progression to secondary necrosis and the consequent massive release of inflammatory factors. This is vital for maintaining brain microenvironmental homeostasis [17, 18]. Studies indicate that the efferocytic function of microglia is not static but is finely regulated [19]. Fully functional efferocytosis not only accomplishes debris clearance but also drives microglia to transition from a pro-inflammatory to a repair state, secreting cytokines such as IL-10 and TGF-β, thereby indirectly promoting tissue repair [20]. Conversely, dysregulated efferocytosis leads to the accumulation of apoptotic cells, exacerbates inflammatory responses, and induces secondary damage [21]. Thus, given the limited intrinsic repair capacity of neurons, targeted regulation of microglial efferocytic function—enhancing their phagocytic capacity and steering them toward an anti-inflammatory role—has emerged as a highly promising direction in HIE neuroprotective strategies [22]. Elucidating the key molecular mechanisms governing efferocytosis is of paramount significance for developing interventions to halt HIE progression and improve long-term outcomes in affected children.

Among the molecular regulators of efferocytosis, growth arrest-specific protein 6 (Gas6) has emerged as an important signaling molecule. Gas6 is a vitamin K-dependent secreted glycoprotein that serves as a ligand for the TAM receptor tyrosine kinase family, which includes Tyro3, Axl, and MerTK [23]. Binding of Gas6 to TAM receptors induces receptor phosphorylation, thereby activating downstream signaling pathways involved in cytoskeletal remodeling, cell survival, and immune regulation [24]. Gas6 can function as a bridging molecule by simultaneously binding phosphatidylserine exposed on apoptotic cells and TAM receptors expressed on phagocytes, thereby facilitating the recognition and engulfment of apoptotic targets [25]. Beyond promoting phagocytic uptake, activation of Gas6-TAM signaling has also been shown to suppress excessive inflammatory responses and promote immune homeostasis [22]. In the central nervous system, TAM receptors are expressed in microglia and have been implicated in the clearance of apoptotic neurons and maintenance of neural tissue integrity [26]. However, the specific contribution of Gas6-mediated TAM signaling to the regulation of microglial efferocytosis and inflammatory balance following hypoxic–ischemic brain injury remains incompletely understood.

In this study, we employed an in vitro co-culture model, phagocytosis assays, cytokine analysis, multi-omics sequencing, pathway enrichment analysis, and Gas6 gene knockout and rescue experiments to validate the functional changes in microglia following neuronal injury and the role of the key regulatory factor Gas6. Specifically, we established a glutamate-induced HT-22 neuronal injury model and performed co-culture with primary microglia. Experiments demonstrated that microglia could phagocytose apoptotic neurons (Acs) and exhibited a typical shift from a pro-inflammatory clearance state to an anti-inflammatory repair state upon stimulation. Flow cytometry further confirmed that the phagocytic activity of microglia toward Acs increased over time, reaching a plateau at 3–4 h. Transcriptomic analysis revealed significant reprogramming of the microglial expression profile under different conditions, with the co-culture group with Acs activating multiple pathways related to phagocytosis and inflammation regulation, including efferocytosis and PI3K-Akt. Furthermore, based on our established HIE mouse model, we found that Gas6 was significantly upregulated after hypoxic-ischemic injury and was primarily expressed by microglia. In vivo experiments showed that Gas6-knockout (Gas6−/−) mice exhibited more severe neurological deficits, increased neuronal apoptosis, and stronger inflammatory responses after HIE. Correspondingly, Gas6−/− microglia displayed reduced phagocytic capacity, upregulation of pro-inflammatory factors, downregulation of anti-inflammatory factors, and significantly decreased phosphorylation levels of TAM receptors. Supplementation with exogenous recombinant Gas6 reversed these defects. Moreover, Gas6 deficiency also impaired downstream PI3K/AKT activation and led to a marked decrease in Rac1-GTP, thereby suppressing cytoskeletal rearrangement and phagocytic function. Exogenous Gas6 supplementation restored TAM receptor phosphorylation and the PI3K/AKT–Rac1 signaling pathway, partially rescuing phagocytic function. Therefore, this study elucidates the dynamic biphasic response of microglia to injured neuron stimulation and provides new insights into systematically clarifies the central role of the Gas6-TAM axis in regulating microglial phagocytic function and inflammatory balance, providing an important theoretical foundation for microglia-targeted neuroprotective strategies.

Materials and methods

Mice

The C57BL/6 J mice and pups used in this study were purchased from Shanghai Jihui Laboratory Animal Breeding Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Gas6 knockout mice were obtained from GemPharmatech Co., Ltd. (Nanjing, Jiangsu, China). All animals were randomized in a standardized manner and housed in a specific pathogen-free (SPF) facility at Shanghai Public Health Clinical Center. The environmental conditions were maintained at a temperature of 22± 2 °C, relative humidity of 50%–60%, a 12-h light/dark cycle, with free access to food and water. Standard chow and water were provided ad libitum. Pups were nursed by their dams prior to weaning. All experimental procedures were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Shanghai Public Health Clinical Center (IACUC approval No. JYIEC-2020–25). The neonatal HIE model was established in postnatal day7 (P7) C57BL/6 J mouse pups, as previously described with modifications [27]. Briefly, after anesthesia with isoflurane, the right common carotid artery was permanently ligated with a 5–0 silk suture. Following a 1-h recovery period, the pups were placed in a hypoxic chamber maintained at 37 °C and exposed to a gas mixture of 8% oxygen and 92% nitrogen for 2.5 h to induce HIE. After the hypoxic exposure, the pups were returned to their dams. Control animals underwent only separation of the common carotid artery without ligation or hypoxia exposure. Upon completion of behavioral testing, mice were euthanized by decapitation under deep anesthesia on an ice-cold platform. For molecular analysis, dissected tissues were immediately snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and subsequently stored at − 80 °C until use. For histology, samples were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, followed by cryoprotection in graded sucrose solutions, and then processed for frozen sectioning.

Cell culture

Primary microglia were isolated from the brains of postnatal day 1–4 (P1–P4) C57 mouse pups following the protocol described by Du et al. [28]. The HT‑22 neuronal cell line was obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC; catalog No. CRL‑2900). Cells were cultured in DMEM medium (HyClone, SH30243.01) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco, 10,270,106) and 1% penicillin‑streptomycin (Hyclone, SV30010), and maintained at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO₂. For indirect co‑culture, a Transwell system (polycarbonate membrane, pore size 0.4 μm; Corning, 3450) was used. HT‑22 neurons were seeded in the upper chamber at a density of 2 × 104 cells per well, while microglia were plated in the lower chamber of a 24‑well plate at a density of 1 × 105 cells per well.

Recombinant Gas6 (rGas6) treatment

Recombinant Gas6 was administered via intracerebroventricular (i.c.v.) injection at 1 h after HIE induction. Mice received a dose of 20 μg/kg of rGas6 in a total volume of 4 μL. To maintain sustained central exposure, rGas6 was administered once every two days throughout the experimental period [29]. Control animals received an equal volume of vehicle (sterile phosphate-buffered saline [PBS] containing 0.1% bovine serum albumin) following the same schedule. For in vitro experiments, primary microglia were treated with rGas6 at a final concentration of 100 ng/mL. rGas6 was added to the culture medium at the initiation of co-culture or stimulation. Cells were then incubated for the indicated time periods. Control groups received an equal volume of vehicle (culture medium or PBS containing 0.1% BSA) under identical conditions.

Cell viability assay

For each experimental condition, cells were plated in 96-well plates at a concentration ranging from 5 × 103 to 10 × 103 cells per well. Viability was evaluated using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8; Beyotime, Shanghai, China; Catalog No. C0039), with absorbance readings obtained at 24-h intervals.

Lactate dehydrogenase activity assay

Cell injury was evaluated using an LDH assay kit (Beyotime, C0017). Briefly, culture supernatants were collected, centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 5 min at 4 °C to remove debris, and incubated with the working solution at 37 °C for 30 min. The reaction was terminated, and absorbance was measured at 450 nm. LDH release was calculated as (experimental group/control group) × 100%. All experiments were performed in at least three independent replicates.

Apoptosis assay

Apoptosis was assessed using the Annexin V, 633 Apoptosis Detection Kit (AD11, Dojindo) following the manufacturer's instructions. Cells were stained with Annexin V-633 and propidium iodide (PI), and the percentage of Annexin V⁺/PI⁺ cells was analyzed by flow cytometry (Beckman Gallios, Beckman Coulter, USA).

Microglial phenotypic analysis

Microglia were collected and subjected to Fc receptor blocking. Cells were then stained with APC-conjugated anti-CD86 (BioLegend, 159,215) for surface markers and PE-conjugated anti-CD206 (BioLegend,141,706) for intracellular markers. The proportions of CD86⁺ and CD206⁺ cells were analyzed by flow cytometry.

Transcriptome sequencing and bioinformatic analysis

Total RNA was extracted from microglia samples using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen). RNA quality and concentration were assessed using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific). Sequencing libraries were constructed from 3 μg of total RNA per sample following the manufacturer’s protocol and sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform to generate paired-end reads. Three independent biological replicates (derived from independent microglial isolations) were included for each experimental group. Raw sequencing reads were processed using fastp (v0.22.0) to remove adapter sequences and low-quality reads. The clean reads were aligned to the mouse reference genome (GRCm39) using HISAT2 (v2.1.0). Gene-level read counts were generated using HTSeq (v0.9.1), and gene expression levels were normalized as fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads (FPKM). Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using DESeq2 (v1.39.0) based on raw count data, with thresholds of |log₂ fold change|> 1 and adjusted P < 0.05 (Benjamini–Hochberg correction). Principal component analysis (PCA), sample correlation analysis, and hierarchical clustering were performed in R to evaluate the overall transcriptomic differences among samples. Functional enrichment analysis of DEGs was performed using clusterProfiler (v4.6.0) for Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analysis, with pathways satisfying adjusted P < 0.05 considered significantly enriched. Pathway activity inference was performed using the PROGENy algorithm to assess the regulatory states of major signaling pathways based on the expression of pathway-responsive genes, enabling comparison of pathway activities between experimental groups.

Western blot

Total proteins were extracted from mouse cortical tissues. After separation by 10% SDS-PAGE (Epizyme, PG211), proteins were transferred onto a PVDF membrane (Merck). The membrane was blocked with QuickBlock blocking buffer for 30 min, incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C, and then incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies at room temperature for 1 h. Protein bands were visualized by chemiluminescence. The levels of phosphorylated proteins were normalized to their corresponding total protein levels for quantitative analysis. Antibody information is provided in the Supplementary Materials.

Rac1 activation assay

Rac1 activation was assessed using an Active Rac1 Detection Kit (#8815, Cell Signaling Technology) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Microglia were harvested after co-culture with apoptotic neurons and lysed. GTP-bound Rac1 was selectively captured using GST-tagged PAK1-PBD fusion protein beads, washed, and eluted. Active GTP Rac-1 and Total Rac-1 in whole-cell lysates were analyzed by Western blotting. Band intensities were quantified using ImageJ and normalized to total Rac1 levels.

Behavioral assessments

Righting reflex test and negative geotaxis test

Pups were placed in a supine position on a flat surface, and the time required to completely turn over to a prone position with all four limbs touching the surface was recorded as the righting reflex latency [30]. In negative geotaxis test, Pups were placed head-down on a 45°-inclined textured surface. The latency to complete a 180° turn (head-up orientation) within 60 s was recorded [31].

Rotarod test

Motor coordination and endurance were assessed using a rotarod apparatus [32]. Prior to formal testing, mice underwent a 3-day acclimation training (twice daily) at a constant speed of 16 rpm. During the test, the latency to fall from the rod rotating at 16 rpm was recorded, with a maximum cut-off time of 300 s. Each mouse was tested three times, and the average value was taken as the final score.

Balance beam test

A narrow beam (120 cm long, 1 cm wide, 50 cm above the floor) was used to assess motor balance [33]. Mice underwent a 3-day pre-training period prior to formal testing. During the test, the time required for the mouse to traverse the beam from the start to the end point was recorded, and the number of hindlimb slips was counted.

Novel object recognition test

The experiment consisted of two phases [34]. In the habituation phase, mice were allowed to freely explore two identical objects placed in a square arena for 5 min. After a 1-h interval, during the test phase, one of the objects was replaced with a novel object, and exploration was allowed for another 5 min. The time spent exploring the novel and familiar objects was recorded. The preference index (PI) was calculated as: PI = time exploring novel object/(time exploring novel object + time exploring familiar object). The discrimination index (DI) was calculated as: DI = (time exploring novel object − time exploring familiar object)/total exploration time.

Catwalk gait analysis

Gait parameters were evaluated using the Catwalk automated gait analysis system [35]. Mice were acclimated to the testing environment for 30 min prior to the experiment. Each mouse was allowed to walk freely across the glass walkway, and three valid runs were recorded. Gait parameters were automatically extracted and analyzed using the built-in software.

Neurological deficit score

The neurological functional status was quantitatively assessed using the Neurological Deficit Score system proposed by María I. Ayuso et al. [31, 36], with scores ranging from 0 (normal neurological function) to 10 (most severe neurological deficit). The evaluation items included: state of consciousness, ocular signs, respiratory pattern, motor performance, and deep sensory reflexes.

Immunofluorescence

For cell staining, cells were seeded on poly-L-lysine-coated coverslips. After 12 h, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 20 min and permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100 for 1 h. Subsequently, samples were incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C, followed by incubation with corresponding fluorescent secondary antibodies at 37 °C for 1 h in the dark. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (Servicebio, G1012-10ML). After mounting, images were acquired using a laser scanning confocal microscope and fluorescence intensity was analyzed. Cellular identity was determined based on positive staining for cell type-specific markers.

For brain tissue immunostaining, frozen Sects. (20–30 μm thick) were collected from the ipsilateral cortex at the level of the dorsal hippocampus (approximately bregma -1.5 mm to -2.5 mm according to the mouse brain atlas of Paxinos and Franklin). Three consecutive sections per animal were analyzed. Sections were washed in PBS, blocked with 5% BSA for 1 h at room temperature, and incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. Appropriate fluorescent secondary antibodies were applied for 1 h at room temperature in the dark, and nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. Sections were mounted using anti-fade medium, and images were captured with a laser scanning confocal microscope under consistent settings. For quantification, three random fields per section were imaged, and fluorescence intensity or positive cell counts were analyzed using ImageJ software (NIH, USA). The average of all fields per animal was used for statistical analysis. Detailed antibody information is provided in the Supplementary Materials.

Phagocytosis assay

Microglia were pre-labeled with CMFDA (Thermo Fisher, C34554) and co-cultured with Far Red-labeled (Thermo Fisher, C34564) HT-22 neurons [37]. After co-culture, cells were collected and analyzed by flow cytometry. The proportion of CMFDA⁺/Far Red⁺ double-positive cells was quantified to calculate the phagocytic index.

ELISA

Cell culture supernatants were collected and analyzed for the concentrations of ARG-1, IL-6, IL-10, and TNF-α according to the manufacturer's instructions using commercially available ELISA kits (ARG-1: catalog No. JL13668; IL-6: catalog No. JL10484; IL-10: catalog No. JL20268; TNF-α: catalog No. JL20242). Absorbance was measured at a wavelength of 450 nm, and the cytokine concentrations were calculated based on the standard curve.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed with 3 (cellular assays) or 5 (animal studies) independent replicates. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation. Statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS 21 software (IBM, USA). Differences among groups were examined by one‑way ANOVA, and when a significant difference was detected, post‑hoc comparisons were performed using Tukey's HSD test. A p‑value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant (*p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001).

Result

Following co-culture with injured neurons, microglia exhibit a shift from a pro-inflammatory to an anti-inflammatory state

To investigate the response of microglia to neuronal injury, we first established an in vitro model of glutamate-induced neuronal apoptosis. HT-22 neurons were treated with 5 mM glutamate for 24 h to induce excitotoxicity, as confirmed by Annexin V/PI staining, CCK-8 assay, and LDH release assay (Supplementary Fig. 1). Primary microglia were isolated from neonatal C57BL/6 mouse brains(Supplementary Fig. 2) and co-cultured with glutamate-treated neurons at a 1:5 ratio immediately after two washes with pre-warmed PBS to completely remove residual glutamate [38]. A schematic timeline summarizing glutamate treatment, washing steps, co-culture initiation, and subsequent analyses is provided in Fig. 1A. Immunofluorescence staining performed after co-culture revealed co-localization between microglia and damaged neurons (Fig. 1B). After co-culturing microglia with differently treated neurons, we examined the expression of CD86 and CD206 on microglia (Fig. 1C-E). The results showed that healthy neurons did not alter the proportion of CD86⁺ or CD206⁺ microglial subpopulations, whereas co-culture with Acs led to a significant increase in both subpopulations. This finding suggests that microglia exhibit a heterogeneous and context-dependent activation pattern in response to injured neurons, rather than a uniform polarization toward a single phenotype. To further explore the temporal dynamics of this response, we measured the levels of inflammation-related cytokines in the culture supernatant at different time points (0 h, 3 h, 6 h, 12 h and 24 h after co-culture initiation) (Fig. 1F-I). During the early co-culture period (0 h-6 h), injured neurons significantly induced an increase in the pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-6 (Fig. 1F) and TNF-α(Fig. 1G). As the co-culture time extended, the levels of these two cytokines gradually declined. Meanwhile, the secretion of the anti-inflammatory markers IL-10 (Fig. 1H) and ARG1 (Fig. 1I) increased significantly between 6 and 24 h. Collectively, these results indicate a time-dependent shift in the overall inflammatory profile of microglia following stimulation by injured neurons. However, given that our data are derived from population-level analyses, they do not directly demonstrate phenotypic switching at the single-cell level. Instead, the observed changes may reflect dynamic alterations in the relative proportions or activation states of distinct microglial subpopulations.

The phagocytic activity of microglia toward injured neurons increases significantly over time, approaching a plateau around 3–4 h

To investigate the temporal dynamics of microglial phagocytic activity toward Acs, we established an in vitro co-culture system. Microglia and injured neurons were specifically labeled with CellTracker™ Green CMFDA and CellTrace™ Far Red, and co-cultured at a 1:5 ratio. Phagocytosis by microglia was then assessed via flow cytometry at various time points (0, 30 min, 1, 2, 3, and 4 h) (Fig. 1J-K and Supplement 3A) [39, 40]. The results demonstrated that phagocytic activity increased in a significant time-dependent manner. At the start of co-culture (0 h), no statistical difference in phagocytic capacity was observed between groups. With prolonged co-culture time, phagocytic activity in the MG + Acs group continued to increase (Supplement 3B), reaching 16.8%, 22.9%, and 41.7% at 1, 2, and 3 h, respectively. Subsequently, we analyzed the phagocytic index and its increase rate for both groups at different time points (Supplement 3C-F). Notably, the phagocytic index in the MG + Acs group increased by 54.8% from 2 to 3 h, whereas the increase rate declined markedly to 17.7% between 3 and 4 h. This pattern suggests that the rate of microglial phagocytic increase slowed during the later phase of co-culture and may indicate a tendency toward saturation. The factors contributing to this apparent slowing remain unclear. Possible explanations include a reduction in available target cells, temporary saturation of the phagocytic machinery, or metabolic constraints associated with sustained phagocytic activity.

Transcriptomic analysis revealed significant reprogramming of microglia during efferocytosis, along with differential expression of Gas6

To investigate the underlying mechanism, transcriptomic sequencing was performed on microglia cultured alone (MG group), microglia co-cultured with healthy neurons (MG + Neuron group), and microglia co-cultured with Acs (MG + Acs group). Transcriptomic profiling was performed using three biological replicates per group. Principal component analysis (PCA, Fig. 2A) and correlation analysis (Fig. 2C) showed good intra-group reproducibility among samples. However, the MG + Acs group exhibited significantly lower correlation with the other two groups, indicating that injured neurons could reshape the overall gene expression program of microglia. Cluster analysis (Fig. 2B) revealed that the MG + Acs group had the most distinct overall expression pattern compared to the other groups, with its corresponding region in the heatmap displaying a unique color distribution, suggesting extensive transcriptional reprogramming under this condition. To systematically evaluate the regulatory states of key signaling pathways under different conditions, pathway activity analysis was conducted on the MG + Neuron and MG + Acs groups using the PROGENy method. The results indicated significant differences between the two groups across multiple important signaling pathways (Fig. 2D), suggesting they might be in distinct functional states. Subsequently, focusing on key regulatory molecules among the differentially expressed genes, volcano plot analysis showed that the expression of Gas6 was significantly upregulated in the MG + Acs group (Fig. 2F). Meanwhile, KEGG enrichment analysis demonstrated significant activation of several pathways closely related to microglial phagocytosis and inflammatory regulation, such as PI3K-Akt and NF-κB (Fig. 2E). Therefore, we hypothesized that Gas6 expression might be induced and play an important role during the recognition and clearance of apoptotic neurons by microglia. We proceeded to further explore the specific function of Gas6 in both in vitro and in vivo models to elucidate its precise mechanism in microglial efferocytosis.

Gas6 deficiency exacerbates neurological deficits following HIE, leading to impaired microglial phagocytosis and reduced anti-inflammatory function

A HIE model was established in neonatal mice [37, 41], and behavioral tests were performed at different time points. Righting reflex and negative geotaxis assays [42, 43] were conducted from two days before to two days after modeling. Prior to induction (P5-P6), no statistically significant differences were observed among the three groups in these tests (Fig. 3A-B). On the day of modeling and during the following two days (P7-P9), pups in the Model group required significantly more time to complete both the righting reflex and negative geotaxis tasks compared with the NC and Sham groups. During the chronic phase, motor and sensory functions were evaluated using the balance beam test and a neurological deficit score [44, 45]. In the balance beam test, Model group mice traversed the beam faster than NC and Sham mice (Fig. 3C) and showed significantly fewer hindlimb slips during crossing (Fig. 3D). Neurological scoring revealed significantly higher deficit scores in the Model group relative to the NC and Sham groups (Fig. 3E). Immunofluorescence staining indicated markedly stronger Gas6 signal intensity in the Model group (Fig. 3F-G). Gas6 protein showed clear co-localization with the microglial marker Iba1, suggesting predominant expression in the cytoplasm of microglial cells (Fig. 3H-I) [46]. Western blot analysis further confirmed that Gas6 protein levels were significantly elevated in the Model group compared with the NC and Sham groups (Fig. 3J-K).

The HIE model was then replicated in Gas6−/− mice, followed by behavioral assessments, the timeline of behavioral assessments performed after HIE induction is summarized in. In the negative geotaxis (Fig. 4B) and righting reflex tests (Fig. 4C), no difference was observed between WT Sham and Gas6−/− Sham groups. Both WT Model and Gas6−/− Model groups exhibited significantly longer task-completion times compared with their respective sham groups; moreover, Gas6−/− Model mice performed worse than WT Model mice. In the balance-beam test, traversal time (Fig. 4D) and hindlimb slips (Fig. 4E) were evaluated. No differences were found between WT Sham and Gas6−/− Sham mice. Compared with WT Model mice, Gas6−/− Model mice showed significantly prolonged traversal time and increased hindlimb slips. Similarly, in the rotarod test (Fig. 4F-G), Gas6−/− Model mice displayed poorer grip strength scores and shorter latencies to fall. In the novel-object recognition test, discrimination index (Fig. 4H) and preference index (Fig. 4I) were assessed. Both indices were significantly lower in Gas6−/− Model mice compared with WT Sham and Gas6−/− Sham mice. Neurological deficit scoring further indicated more severe impairment in the Gas6−/− Model group (Fig. 4J). Additionally, gait parameters wee analyzed using the Catwalk system, including print length (Fig. 4K), print width (Fig. 4L), maximum contact area (Fig. 4M), and mean maximal pressure (Fig. 4N). Statistical analysis revealed that, compared with WT Model mice, Gas6−/− Model mice exhibited more pronounced locomotor deficits, particularly in the left forelimb and left hindlimb. Immunofluorescence analysis revealed a significant increase in neuronal apoptosis in the brains of Gas6−/− Model mice compared with Gas6−/− Sham mice (Fig. 5A–B). To evaluate the effect of post-injury Gas6 supplementation in vivo, recombinant Gas6 was administered to Gas6−/− Model mice following HIE induction, and neurological outcomes were subsequently assessed. Behavioral analyses demonstrated that rGas6 treatment significantly improved neurological performance, as evidenced by enhanced righting reflex (Fig. 5C), negative geotaxis (Fig. 5D), rotarod performance (Fig. 5E-F), and overall neurological scores (Fig. 5G). To further investigate the effects of rGas6 at the cellular level, primary microglia were isolated from neonatal Gas6−/− mouse brains and their phagocytic function was assessed by flow cytometry, a schematic diagram illustrating the experimental design is shown in Fig. 5H. The results showed that the phagocytic capacity of Gas6−/− microglia was significantly reduced relative to WT microglia (Fig. 5I-J). This functional impairment was rescued following supplementation with exogenous recombinant Gas6 protein. In addition, Gas6 deficiency led to an increased proportion of CD86⁺ microglia and a decreased proportion of CD206⁺ microglia (Fig. 5K-M). The exogenous addition of Gas6 restored the ratios of CD86⁺ and CD206⁺ cells to levels comparable to those in the WT group. Collectively, these findings suggest that Gas6 plays an important role in maintaining microglial phagocytic function, and its loss is associated with a shift toward a more pro-inflammatory phenotype.

Gas6 modulates microglial phagocytic function and inflammatory balance by promoting TAM receptor phosphorylation

Gas6 is a key ligand for the TAM receptor family (primarily Axl, MerTK, and Tyro3) [47] and typically transduces extracellular signals by binding to and activating these receptors [48]. In our experiments, we found that Gas6 exerts its effects by modulating the phosphorylation levels of TAM receptors without altering their total protein expression (Fig. 6A). Specifically, Gas6 knockout led to a significant reduction in TAM receptor phosphorylation, most notably in p‑MerTK, while supplementation with exogenous recombinant Gas6 restored phosphorylation levels in Gas6⁻/⁻ microglia co-cultured with Acs (Fig. 6B). These results indicate that Gas6 activates downstream signaling by promoting TAM receptors phosphorylation, with a relatively stronger phosphorylation signal observed for MerTK compared to other TAM receptors; however, this does not necessarily indicate functional dominance. We next examined the PI3K/AKT pathway, a key downstream signaling cascade of TAM receptors (Fig. 6C). In WT microglia co‑cultured with apoptotic neurons, levels of p‑PI3K and p‑AKT were significantly elevated compared to controls, indicating that intact Gas6 signaling effectively promotes PI3K/AKT pathway activation. In contrast, Gas6 knockout markedly reduced the phosphorylation of both PI3K and AKT, which was partially restored upon exogenous Gas6 supplementation. Further downstream, we evaluated the activation state of the effector molecule Rac1,which regulates cytoskeletal remodeling during phagocytosis (Fig. 6D). GTP Rac-1 levels were significantly lower in Gas6⁻/⁻ microglia after co-culture with apoptotic neurons compared with WT microglia, and exogenous Gas6 supplementation raised GTP Rac-1 levels toward those of the WT group. To investigate the role of Gas6 in regulating the inflammatory phenotype of microglia during efferocytosis, we measured the expression of inflammation‑related molecules in microglia under different treatments (Fig. 6E). Compared with WT microglia co-cultured with apoptotic neurons, Gas6⁻/⁻ microglia in the same setting showed significantly increased expression of the pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-6 and TNF-α, along with decreased expression of the anti-inflammatory cytokines IL-10 and TGF-β. Exogenous recombinant Gas6 supplementation reversed these changes, restoring cytokine expression profiles toward WT levels. We further quantified cytokine secretion in cell culture supernatants by ELISA at 0 h and 24 h after co-culture initiation (Fig. 6F-I). The 0 h time point represents the immediate post-induction baseline, defined as the moment immediately after apoptotic neurons were added to microglia, before detectable co-culture-induced responses occurred. Consistent with the intracellular protein expression data, Gas6⁻/⁻ microglia co-cultured with apoptotic neurons secreted significantly higher levels of IL-6 (Fig. 6F) and TNF-α (Fig. 6G), and significantly lower levels of IL-10 (Fig. 6H) and TGF-β (Fig. 6I) compared to WT microglia. Exogenous Gas6 supplementation restored the secretion of these cytokines to levels comparable to those observed in WT microglia. Together, these findings demonstrate that Gas6 promotes TAM receptor phosphorylation and activates the PI3K/AKT-Rac-1 signaling axis in microglia, thereby enhancing efferocytosis and promoting a shift toward an anti-inflammatory phenotype following interaction with apoptotic neurons.

Discussion

As the primary innate immune cells in the central nervous system, microglia play a critical role in responding to neural injury, regulating inflammation, and promoting tissue repair [49]. In brain injuries such as HIE, which are characterized by the coexistence of apoptosis and inflammation [50], microglia can exacerbate tissue damage through pro-inflammatory effects while also exerting protective functions by clearing apoptotic cells [41]. Therefore, elucidating the mechanisms by which microglia remove apoptotic cells is of significant importance for understanding the immune processes that follow brain injury [9, 42, 43].

In this study, we employed a glutamate-induced HT-22 injury model to investigate microglial responses to neuronal damage. While this model captures certain aspects of neuronal stress and apoptotic signaling relevant to microglial activation, HT-22 cells are an immortalized hippocampal neuronal cell line and do not fully recapitulate the cellular characteristics or developmental context of primary neonatal neurons, particularly under hypoxic–ischemic conditions. Therefore, in future studies using primary neonatal neurons or in vivo models will be necessary to validate the physiological relevance of these observations. In addition, under our experimental conditions, cell identity in the immunofluorescence analyses was primarily determined by the expression of specific markers (e.g., Iba1 for microglia, NeuN for neurons), as cellular morphology alone provided limited distinguishing features. This marker-based approach is widely used for identifying major cell types in situ; nevertheless, combining marker expression with additional structural or molecular markers would further strengthen cellular phenotyping. Future studies employing multiplex labeling or higher-resolution imaging will help validate and refine these observations. In vitro co‑culture experiments demonstrated that healthy neurons do not significantly activate microglia [40, 44]. In contrast, Acs induced marked alterations in microglial gene expression, involving changes in the levels of genes associated with inflammation, stress response, and cytoskeletal reorganization [45, 46]. This is consistent with the classical view that microglia primarily maintain brain homeostasis during later developmental stages [43, 44, 47, 48].Flow cytometry results revealed that Acs significantly increased the proportion of CD86⁺ and CD206⁺ microglia [51, 52]. Notably, the concurrent increase in CD86⁺ and CD206⁺ microglial populations observed in this study likely reflects a heterogeneous activation landscape rather than a uniform phenotypic transition. These changes may arise from dynamic shifts in the relative proportions or activation states of distinct microglial subpopulations in response to neuronal injury. Given that the present analyses were performed at the population level, the possibility of phenotype switching at the single-cell level cannot be directly resolved. Future studies employing single-cell approaches will be required to further dissect the dynamic state transitions of individual microglia under these conditions. Nevertheless, our findings clearly establish that injured neurons induce a shift in the overall balance of microglial inflammatory phenotypes at the population level. [53]. Moreover, we observed that the phagocytic activity of microglia exhibited a distinct time‑dependent pattern: it increased rapidly during the first 1–2 h of co‑culture and appeared to reach a plateau after approximately 3–4 h. The mechanisms underlying this apparent plateau remain unclear. Potential explanations may include a reduction in the number of available target cells, saturation of phagocytic vesicle formation, metabolic constraints, or negative-feedback regulatory processes triggered following successful phagocytosis [39]. These possibilities suggest that the observed plateau may arise from a combination of substrate availability and intrinsic cellular regulatory mechanisms. However, these mechanisms remain speculative and were not directly examined in the present study. Further investigations will therefore be required to clarify the basis of this temporal pattern.

To further explore the temporal dynamics of this response, we measured the levels of inflammatory cytokines in the culture supernatant at different time points. We found that injured neurons markedly elevated TNF‑α and IL‑6 levels during the early co‑culture period (0–6 h), and these pro‑inflammatory cytokines gradually declined as co‑culture continued. Meanwhile, the secretion of ARG1 and IL‑10 increased significantly between 6 and 24 h. Therefore, following activation by injured neurons, microglia undergo a functional transition from a pro‑inflammatory to an anti‑inflammatory state. Notably, our time-course analysis revealed a clear temporal relationship between Gas6 expression and microglial phenotypic transition. Gas6 levels began to increase at early time points following co-culture, peaked at the intermediate stage, and preceded the upregulation of anti-inflammatory markers such as IL-10 and Arg-1. This temporal pattern suggests that Gas6 induction occurs after the initial pro-inflammatory activation but before the establishment of a reparative phenotype, supporting a potential role for Gas6 in facilitating this transition. However, it should be noted that temporal association alone does not establish causality. Therefore, while our findings are consistent with a contributory role of Gas6 in promoting microglial phenotypic switching, further studies are required to determine the direct mechanistic link.

Findings from animal experiments further elucidate the potential pathological role and biological significance of Gas6 in hypoxic-ischemic encephalopathy. Behavioral observations during the acute phase revealed significant delays in righting reflex and negative geotaxis tests in neonatal mice following HIE induction [30, 47]. In the chronic phase, HIE mice exhibited prolonged traversal times and increased hindlimb slips in the balance beam test, reflecting a persistent decline in motor coordination [54]. Consistently elevated neurological deficit scores indicated sustained motor impairment and neurological dysfunction resulting from HIE. Interestingly, we observed a marked increase in Gas6 expression in the ipsilateral cortex following ischemic injury. Notably, although increased Gas6 expression in the injured cortex was accompanied by gradual improvement in behavioral outcomes, a direct correlation analysis was not performed in the present study [55]. Therefore, while these findings suggest a potential association between Gas6 upregulation and functional recovery, further quantitative analyses will be required to establish this relationship more definitively [56].

Notably, Western blot and immunofluorescence staining demonstrated a significant upregulation of Gas6 expression in the brains of HIE mice, with strong co-localization observed with the microglial marker IBA1. This suggests that the increased Gas6 originates primarily from activated microglia, providing an important clue to understanding its role in HIE. Although Gas6 expression was observed in Iba1-positive microglia in our model, the potential contribution of other neural cell types, including injured neurons, cannot be completely excluded. Previous studies have reported Gas6 expression in neuronal cells under stress conditions [57]. Therefore, it is possible that Gas6 activating microglial TAM receptors may originate from both microglia and surrounding injured neurons. However, the predominant co-localization of Gas6 with Iba1-positive cells in our immunofluorescence analysis suggests that microglia represent a major source of Gas6 in this context. Nevertheless, further cell-type–specific analyses, such as co-staining with neuronal markers or conditional cell-specific approaches, will be required to determine the relative contributions of different cell populations.

Simultaneously, transcriptomic sequencing revealed that Gas6 expression was markedly upregulated during microglial phagocytosis of injured neurons, accompanied by activation of pathways such as TAM receptors and NF-κB. Current research indicates that Gas6, a key ligand of the TAM receptor family [14], is typically elevated in response to tissue damage and increased cell death. It is regarded as an important signaling molecule that promotes phagocytosis, modulates inflammation, and maintains tissue homeostasis [35, 58, 59], thereby exerting neuroprotective effects [22, 58].To validate the function of Gas6, we replicated the HIE model in Gas6⁻/⁻ mice. The results showed that Gas6 deficiency significantly exacerbated acute behavioral deficits and led to more severe impairments in long-term motor coordination, grip strength, and cognitive function [59]. Immunofluorescence analysis indicated a marked increase in the number of Acs in the brains of HIE mice lacking Gas6, while in vitro functional assays further demonstrated a significant reduction in the ability of Gas6⁻/⁻ microglia to phagocytose injured neurons. Importantly, supplementation with exogenous recombinant Gas6 partially restored the phagocytic defect in Gas6⁻/⁻ microglia, confirming the crucial role of Gas6 in efferocytosis. Although our results show that Gas6 expression is elevated during microglial interaction with injured neurons and that Gas6 deficiency impairs phagocytosis, the causal relationship between Gas6 induction and efferocytosis remains to be fully defined. On the one hand, the ability of exogenous Gas6 to rescue the phagocytic defect in Gas6-deficient microglia supports a functional role for Gas6 as a regulator of efferocytosis. On the other hand, it remains possible that Gas6 expression is, at least in part, induced as a consequence of phagocytic activity itself. Therefore, Gas6 may function not only as an upstream modulator but also as part of a dynamic feedback mechanism associated with microglial activation and efferocytosis. Given the current experimental design, which does not directly uncouple phagocytic activity from Gas6 induction, this distinction cannot be conclusively resolved. Future studies employing pharmacological or genetic inhibition of efferocytosis, followed by assessment of Gas6 expression, will be required to clarify the directionality and mechanistic hierarchy of this relationship.

To explore the molecular mechanisms underlying the impaired phagocytic function observed in Gas6-deficient microglia, we next examined the activation status of the TAM receptor signaling pathway. Gas6 is a well-established ligand of the TAM receptor family and has been widely reported to regulate phagocytosis and inflammatory responses in innate immune cells [56]. Therefore, we investigated whether the functional defects observed in Gas6⁻/⁻ microglia were associated with altered TAM receptor activation. Further investigation revealed that Gas6 deficiency affected the overall phosphorylation level of TAM receptors without altering their total protein expression. Among the TAM receptors examined, p-MerTK showed a relatively greater reduction. Exogenous supplementation of recombinant Gas6 effectively restored its phosphorylation status. Since TAM receptor phosphorylation is a prerequisite for downstream signaling [60, 61], impaired receptor activation due to Gas6 deficiency was associated with significant inhibition of the PI3K/AKT pathway. Partial restoration of PI3K/AKT phosphorylation by exogenous Gas6 indicates that the Gas6–TAM signaling axis is essential for activating this pathway. Although MerTK phosphorylation exhibited a more prominent response to Gas6, given the known functional redundancy among TAM receptors, the current phosphorylation-based analysis does not allow us to definitively attribute the observed effects to a specific receptor subtype. Future studies employing receptor-specific inhibition or genetic approaches will be required to dissect the relative contributions and potential redundancy among TAM receptors in mediating Gas6-dependent efferocytosis.

As a key downstream effector of PI3K/AKT, Rac1 plays a vital role in regulating phagocytic cup formation, cytoskeletal remodeling, and microglial migration [22]. Our experimental results confirmed that Gas6 deficiency significantly reduced Rac1 activation, which was restored upon supplementation with recombinant Gas6. Moreover, Gas6 deficiency disrupted the inflammatory regulatory capacity of microglia. We observed that Gas6-KO microglia co-cultured with apoptotic cells exhibited significantly elevated levels of the pro-inflammatory cytokines TNF-α and IL-6, alongside markedly decreased levels of the anti-inflammatory cytokines IL-10 and TGF-β. These results indicate that Gas6 is involved in the phagocytosis-driven transition of microglia toward an anti-inflammatory phenotype. Under normal conditions, microglia that successfully phagocytose apoptotic cells shift toward a reparative state, promoting inflammation resolution. In the absence of Gas6, insufficient TAM receptor activation and impaired PI3K–Rac1 signaling hinder the effective clearance of apoptotic cells, thereby perpetuating a pro-inflammatory state.

In summary, Gas6 serves as a key regulator of microglial function. By promoting TAM receptor phosphorylation and driving the PI3K/AKT–Rac1 signaling pathway, it helps maintain both the phagocytic capacity and the inflammatory-modulatory function of microglia.

The core finding of this study is that Gas6 plays a crucial role in regulating microglial efferocytosis and inflammatory modulation. Injured neurons can trigger a rapid inflammatory response in microglia, which initially amplifies the damage response through pro-inflammatory and phagocytic activity. Subsequently, under the regulation of Gas6, microglia gradually transition toward an anti-inflammatory and reparative state, contributing to the resolution of inflammation and the restoration of tissue homeostasis. Therefore, as a critical node in modulating inflammatory status and tissue repair, Gas6 represents a potential therapeutic target for future interventions in HIE and other neuroinflammatory-related diseases.

Conclusion

This study systematically demonstrates that Gas6 is a pivotal molecule driving the efferocytosis and inflammatory regulation of microglia. Injured neurons can induce microglia to gradually transition from a pro-inflammatory, clearance-oriented state toward an anti-inflammatory, reparative state. In contrast, Gas6 deficiency leads to impaired TAM receptor phosphorylation, attenuated PI3K/AKT signaling, and insufficient Rac1 activation, thereby causing phagocytic dysfunction and sustained inflammation. Exogenous supplementation of Gas6 can partially restore the functional state of microglia and mitigate their inflammatory bias. In vivo experiments further validate the significant role of Gas6 in the acute injury response and long-term functional recovery following HIE. In summary, we identify Gas6 as a critical regulator of microglial efferocytosis and inflammatory balance. This finding provides a novel theoretical foundation for targeting microglia to enhance their inflammation-resolving and tissue-repair capabilities.
