Serum zonulin levels and their association with inflammatory and oxidative stress biomarkers in patients undergoing maintenance hemodialysis
Authors: Ali B Roomi, Hussein Jawad Kadhim, Abbas Khairi Fadhil, Ibrahim Abdullah Hamzah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMaintenance hemodialysis (HD) is accompanied by persistent inflammation, oxidative stress, and disturbances of the intestinal barrier.ObjectiveTo compare serum zonulin concentrations between patients receiving maintenance HD and healthy controls and to evaluate their associations with interleukin-6 (IL-6), malondialdehyde (MDA), and total antioxidant status (TAS).MethodsThis comparative cross-sectional study included 180 participants: 90 patients with end-stage renal disease receiving maintenance HD and 90 age- and sex-comparable healthy controls. Predialysis blood samples were analyzed for serum zonulin, IL-6, and MDA by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay and for TAS by a colorimetric assay. Group differences were evaluated using independent-samples t-tests, associations using Pearson's correlation, and adjusted relationships using multivariable linear regression.ResultsCompared with controls, the HD group had higher serum zonulin, IL-6, MDA, urea, creatinine, and phosphorus concentrations and lower TAS, albumin, calcium, and hemoglobin values (all P < 0.001). Within the HD group, zonulin correlated positively with IL-6 (r = 0.644, P < 0.001) and MDA (r = 0.782, P < 0.001) and inversely with TAS (r = −0.392, P < 0.001). After covariate adjustment, zonulin remained associated with IL-6 (B = 0.982, β = 0.577, P < 0.001), MDA (B = 36.76, β = 0.597, P < 0.001), and TAS (B = −0.018, β = −0.345, P = 0.021).ConclusionSerum zonulin was elevated in patients receiving maintenance HD and was independently associated with inflammatory and oxidative-stress biomarkers. These cross-sectional findings support an association but do not establish that altered intestinal permeability causes inflammatory-oxidative imbalance or that serum zonulin is ready for clinical use.Keywords: Hemodialysis, Zonulin, Intestinal permeability, Oxidative stress, Interleukin-6, End-stage renal disease, Gut-kidney axis

Introduction

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) is a major global health burden. In 2023, an estimated 788 million adults were living with CKD, and CKD accounted for approximately 1.48 million deaths, ranking ninth among causes of death worldwide.1 Progressive loss of kidney function may culminate in kidney failure, also termed end-stage renal disease (ESRD), for which kidney replacement therapy is required. Among patients receiving maintenance hemodialysis (HD), the burden extends beyond impaired filtration and includes persistent low-grade inflammation, oxidative imbalance, endothelial injury, metabolic disturbances, and immune dysfunction. Together, these abnormal findings are responsible for the cardiovascular morbidity and mortality seen in this patient population.2, 3, 4, 5 Despite advancements in dialysis techniques and supportive care, inflammation and oxidative stress are still important non-traditional risk factors for adverse events.4, 6. In addition, the process of hemodialysis itself might activate inflammation due to repetitive contact of blood with the dialysis membrane, complement activation, oxidative damage, endotoxin presence, and biocompatibility-induced immune response.7, 8 Interleukin-6 (IL-6) has special importance, since elevated levels of IL-6 in ESRD patients have been linked to chronic microinflammation, protein-energy wasting, endothelial dysfunction, cardiovascular diseases, and mortality.9, 10, 11, 12 Oxidative stress, which can be seen as an imbalance between the formation of reactive oxygen species and antioxidant protection, is yet another crucial factor of CKD.13, 14 Elevated lipoperoxidation, usually measured via MDA, along with decreased TAS, has been demonstrated in patients undergoing HD therapy.13, 14, 15, 16

It is possible that the gut-kidney axis could play a role in such systemic alterations. In patients with CKD, gut dysbiosis is accompanied by alteration in tight junctions and leaky gut. The resulting impairment in the barrier structure may enable transport of products from bacteria and gut microbiota into the blood, contributing to systemic inflammation.17, 18, 19, 20, 21 Uremic toxic load, oxidative stress, and immune dysfunction are all features of maintenance HD along with impairment of the gut barrier function, while alterations in the microbiota composition have been linked to inflammation, cardiovascular disease, protein energy wasting, and immune disorders.19, 21, 22, 23

Zonulin is involved in regulating the permeability of epithelial tight junctions and is commonly employed as an indirect biomarker for disruption of the intestinal barrier.24, 25 However, commercially available zonulin serum tests may measure zonulin-associated proteins other than pre-haptoglobin-2, and serum levels of the protein do not necessarily correlate with intestinal permeability.26, 27

Serum zonulin should therefore be interpreted as a putative, assay-dependent research marker rather than a definitive measure of gut permeability. Higher concentrations have been reported in inflammatory, immune-mediated, and metabolic disorders,28, 29 whereas studies in CKD and other renal diseases have produced mixed results regarding associations with renal dysfunction, systemic inflammation, endothelial impairment, and disease-specific outcomes.30, 31, 32, 33 Experimental and clinical evidence suggests that impaired intestinal barrier integrity may coexist with endotoxemia, inflammatory signaling, and oxidative stress.34 However, the relationship between serum zonulin and paired inflammatory, lipid-peroxidation, and antioxidant markers in maintenance HD remains insufficiently characterized. Accordingly, this study compared serum zonulin, IL-6, MDA, and TAS between patients receiving maintenance HD and healthy controls and evaluated whether serum zonulin was associated with IL-6, MDA, and TAS after adjustment for selected demographic and clinical covariates.

Materials and methods

Study design

This single-center comparative cross-sectional study included 180 participants: 90 adults with ESRD receiving maintenance HD and 90 healthy controls comparable in age and sex. Patients were recruited from the Hemodialysis Unit at Al-Hussein Teaching Hospital, Thi-Qar Health Directorate, Nasiriyah, Iraq. Blood samples from the HD group were collected immediately before a scheduled dialysis session to reduce the influence of intradialytic biochemical shifts on the biomarkers under investigation.

Participants and eligibility criteria

Eligible patients were adults receiving regular maintenance HD. Controls were assessed through medical history and were required to have no known CKD, inflammatory or autoimmune disease, liver disease, malignancy, or acute infection at the time of sampling. In both groups, individuals with active infection, chronic inflammatory or autoimmune disease, malignancy, severe hepatic dysfunction, or another major systemic illness likely to influence inflammatory or oxidative-stress biomarkers were excluded. The available study records did not preserve a more granular classification of the control recruitment source, such as hospital staff, community volunteers, or patients' relatives.

Blood sampling and serum preparation

Blood was collected under aseptic conditions. For the HD group, sampling was performed before the dialysis session. Approximately 10 mL of venous blood was collected into ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid-containing and plain tubes, according to the requirements of the planned laboratory tests. Blood in plain tubes was allowed to clot, and serum was separated by centrifugation at 3000 rpm for 10 min and aliquoted into sterile microtubes. Serum samples were stored at −20 °C until biochemical and immunological analysis, and repeated freeze-thaw cycles were minimized.

Laboratory measurements

Serum zonulin, IL-6, and MDA were measured using commercial enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay kits (Elabscience Biotechnology Inc., Houston, TX, USA) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Sandwich formats were used for zonulin and IL-6, whereas MDA was measured using a competitive format. Absorbance was read using a STAT FAX 4200 microplate reader (Awareness Technology Inc., USA). TAS was measured with an Elabscience colorimetric kit based on the reduction of 2,2′-azino-bis(3-ethylbenzothiazoline-6-sulfonic acid) radical cations by antioxidants in the sample. Routine biochemical variables were measured using BIOLABO reagents (BIOLABO SAS, Maizy, France): urea by an ultraviolet kinetic enzymatic method, creatinine by an enzymatic colorimetric method, albumin by the bromocresol green method, calcium by the o-cresolphthalein complexone method, and phosphorus by the ultraviolet phosphomolybdate method. Hemoglobin was measured using a Mindray BC-10 hematology analyzer (Mindray, China).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using JASP version 0.18.3. Distributional normality was assessed with the Shapiro-Wilk test, and continuous variables with approximately normal distributions were summarized as mean ± standard deviation. Between-group differences were evaluated using independent-samples t-tests for continuous variables and chi-square tests for categorical variables. Pearson's correlation coefficient (r) was retained from the original analysis.

The archived analysis output for this revision did not include diagnostic plots for linearity or influential observations, which limitation is stated in the Study Limitations section. The effect sizes obtained from Cohen's d were standardized between-group differences and were evaluated based on conventional cut-off values of 0.2, 0.5, and 0.8 for small, medium, and large effect sizes, respectively. Separate multivariable linear regression models for IL-6, MDA, and TAS were done within the HD group. The predictor variable used was the serum zonulin level. Demographic covariates included age and sex, while the clinical covariates included diabetes, hypertension, and smoking. Albumin, hemoglobin, and phosphorus were the indicators used for nutrition, hematology, and mineral metabolism, respectively.

Results are reported as unstandardized coefficients (B), standardized coefficients (β), 95% confidence intervals (CIs), and exact P values. Variance inflation factors (VIFs) below 5 were taken to indicate no material multicollinearity. Statistical significance was defined by a two-sided P value <0.05. No formal multiplicity correction was applied; therefore, effect sizes, CIs, and the consistency of related findings were considered alongside P values.

Results

Baseline characteristics of the study population

The study included 90 patients receiving maintenance HD and 90 controls. As shown in Table 1, mean age did not differ significantly between groups (P = 0.118), and the sex distribution was identical. Among patients receiving HD, 43 (47.8%) had diabetes mellitus and 33 (36.7%) had hypertension. Current smoking did not differ significantly between groups (P = 0.411). Diabetes, hypertension, and smoking were subsequently considered in the adjusted analyses because of their potential relationships with inflammatory and oxidative-stress biomarkers.

Discussion

The principal finding was that serum zonulin was substantially higher in patients receiving maintenance HD than in healthy controls and was associated with higher IL-6 and MDA and lower TAS. These relationships persisted after adjustment for measured demographic and clinical covariates. The results therefore identify a coherent inflammatory-oxidative pattern associated with circulating zonulin in this cohort, while the cross-sectional design precludes conclusions about temporal sequence or causality.

Inflammation is a persistent feature of ESRD and is associated with cardiovascular complications, protein-energy wasting, endothelial dysfunction, immune disturbance, and mortality. Consistent with this background, IL-6 was markedly higher in the HD group. Previous studies have linked circulating IL-6 and related inflammatory markers to cardiovascular risk and adverse outcomes in dialysis populations,9, 35, 36 including progression of the malnutrition-inflammation-atherosclerosis phenotype.37 The adjusted association between zonulin and IL-6 suggests that the two markers covary beyond the measured effects of age, sex, diabetes, hypertension, smoking, albumin, hemoglobin, and phosphorus; it does not establish that barrier dysfunction initiated inflammatory activation.

The HD subjects demonstrated significantly higher MDA levels and lower TAS values as well, reflecting greater lipid peroxidation and reduced aggregate antioxidant status seen in kidney failure.38, 39, 40 Importantly, zonulin remained positively associated with MDA and inversely associated with TAS after adjustment. The association was strongest for MDA, whereas the TAS model explained comparatively little variance. This difference indicates that the measured covariates and zonulin captured only a limited proportion of the determinants of antioxidant status and cautions against treating TAS as a downstream consequence of zonulin.

Serum zonulin was markedly elevated in the HD group. This finding is consistent with reports of altered intestinal barrier-related markers in CKD and other renal disorders29, 41, 42 and with evidence of intestinal permeability abnormalities and gut-kidney crosstalk in glomerular disease and dialysis populations.23, 43 However, serum zonulin is an indirect and assay-dependent marker. Commercial immunoassays may recognize zonulin-family or other related proteins, and serum concentrations can diverge from direct functional permeability measurements.24, 25, 26, 27 Accordingly, the present result should be interpreted as an elevation in the immunoreactivity measured by the specified assay rather than definitive proof of intestinal hyperpermeability.

Several nonexclusive explanations could underlie the observed pattern. Uremia, dietary restrictions, gut dysbiosis, inflammation, oxidative stress, and altered protein metabolism may influence epithelial tight junctions or circulating zonulin-related proteins. Recent studies in maintenance HD have associated gut dysbiosis with immune alterations, metabolic disturbances, and dialysis-related complications.44, 45, 46, 47 These mechanisms provide biological plausibility, but the present study did not measure microbiota composition, endotoxin, bacterial DNA, gut-derived uremic toxins, or direct intestinal permeability and therefore cannot distinguish among them.

Experimental CKD models have shown reduced expression of tight-junction components, including occludin and claudin-4, together with increased permeability to bacterial toxins.48 Such findings support a possible mechanistic framework for gut-barrier disruption in kidney disease. They do not, however, demonstrate that the higher serum zonulin measured in this human cohort arose from the same pathway.

The positive associations of zonulin with IL-6 and MDA and its inverse association with TAS constitute the novel contribution of this study. They are compatible with coordinated gut-barrier, inflammatory, and oxidative disturbances but also with reverse or shared causation. Systemic inflammation and oxidative stress may themselves impair tight-junction integrity, whereas dietary factors, comorbidity, medication exposure, nutritional status, residual kidney function, or reduced clearance of circulating proteins may influence both zonulin immunoreactivity and the other biomarkers.20, 34, 47, 49, 50 Residual confounding therefore remains a substantive consideration despite multivariable adjustment.

From a translational perspective, these findings are hypothesis-generating. Serum zonulin cannot yet be recommended for screening, diagnosis, prognosis, treatment selection, or monitoring in HD. Its incremental value over established inflammatory markers such as C-reactive protein or IL-6 was not tested, and C-reactive protein was not measured in this study. Prospective studies should determine temporal relationships, within-patient reproducibility, responsiveness to interventions, assay standardization, and whether zonulin improves discrimination, calibration, or clinical decision-making beyond conventional biomarkers before any clinical role is proposed.

Study limitations

Several limitations should be considered. First, the cross-sectional design does not establish temporal order, directionality, or causation and cannot exclude reverse causation. Second, intestinal permeability was represented by a single serum zonulin immunoassay. Given concerns regarding the molecular specificity of commercial assays and the influence of diet, systemic inflammation, comorbidity, and other circulating proteins, the results should not be equated with a direct functional permeability measurement. Third, dialysis vintage, delivered dialysis dose or Kt/V, vascular access type, session frequency, cause of ESRD, residual kidney function, body mass index, detailed nutritional status, dietary patterns, and medication exposure (including anti-inflammatory or immunosuppressive agents, phosphate binders, vitamin D analogs, and erythropoiesis-stimulating agents) were not systematically available for analysis. These omissions may have produced residual confounding. Fourth, control eligibility was based on the available health history and exclusion criteria; the specific recruitment source and systematic screening for subclinical inflammatory or metabolic disorders were not retained in sufficient detail. Fifth, although Pearson coefficients were retained from the original analysis, complete archived diagnostics for linearity and influential observations were unavailable during revision; these correlations should therefore be interpreted cautiously and verified from the raw data before final submission if those data are available. Sixth, the absence of C-reactive protein and of direct or complementary gut-barrier measures, including intestinal permeability tests, endotoxin, bacterial DNA, gut-derived uremic toxins, and microbiome profiling, limited biological and clinical interpretation. Finally, the single-center setting may restrict generalizability.

The study also has strengths. It included equally sized, age- and sex-comparable groups; standardized predialysis sampling; simultaneous assessment of a putative intestinal-barrier marker, an inflammatory cytokine, lipid peroxidation, and antioxidant status; and adjusted analyses with effect estimates and 95% CIs. These features improve the internal coherence of the observed associations but do not remove the limitations of biomarker specificity, unmeasured confounding, or the cross-sectional design.

Conclusion

Patients receiving maintenance HD had higher serum zonulin, IL-6, and MDA concentrations and lower TAS than healthy controls. Within the HD group, serum zonulin was independently associated with higher IL-6 and MDA and lower TAS after adjustment for selected demographic and clinical covariates. These findings support an association between circulating zonulin immunoreactivity and inflammatory-oxidative imbalance, but they neither establish intestinal hyperpermeability as the cause of that imbalance nor validate zonulin for clinical use. Multicentre longitudinal studies that include the measurement of permeability parameters, gut flora assessment, gut-produced toxins, dialysis-related parameters, drug exposure, and classic inflammatory markers are needed to establish the order of events and the predictive capacity of these biomarkers.
