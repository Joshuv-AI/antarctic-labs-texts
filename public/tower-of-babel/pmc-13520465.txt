Clinical outcomes of using 4 + 5 extensor compartment pedicled vascularized bone graft in Kienböck’s disease
Authors: Yavuz Önel, Cahit Ancar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study aimed to evaluate the mid-term clinical and radiological outcomes of a 4th–5th extensor compartment artery (4 + 5 ECA) pedicled vascularized distal radius bone graft in patients with Lichtman stage II and IIIa Kienböck’s disease, with a particular focus on differences between disease stages and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) evidence of lunate revascularization.MethodsA retrospective review was conducted on 30 patients (31 wrists) treated with a 4 + 5 ECA pedicled vascularized bone graft between 2022 and 2024. Nineteen wrists were classified as Lichtman stage II and 12 as stage IIIa. Clinical evaluation included visual analog scale (VAS) pain score, Mayo Wrist Score, wrist range of motion, and grip strength. Radiological assessment comprised plain radiographs and MRI, evaluating Lichtman stage progression, carpal height ratio, Stahl index, and MRI signal changes suggestive of revascularization. Preoperative and final follow-up results were compared within and between stage subgroups.ResultsThe mean follow-up duration was 27.5 months. In the entire cohort, VAS pain scores significantly decreased (6.1 to 2.6) and Mayo Wrist Scores significantly improved (36.4 to 68.4) (p < 0.05). Grip strength increased from 55.2% to 70.6% of the contralateral side. Clinical improvements were significantly greater in stage II patients than in stage IIIa patients. MRI findings consistent with lunate revascularization were observed in 21.1% of stage II wrists, whereas no clear revascularization was detected in stage IIIa wrists. Disease progression occurred in 29.0% of all cases, more frequently in stage IIIa wrists.ConclusionsThe 4 + 5 ECA pedicled vascularized bone graft is a safe and effective treatment for early-stage Kienböck’s disease, providing significant pain relief and functional improvement. Clinical and radiological outcomes are more favorable in Lichtman stage II patients, supporting early surgical intervention with appropriate indications.Keywords: Kienböck’s Disease, Vascularized Bone Grafts, Lunate Bone, Magnetic Resonance Imaging, Wrist Joint

Background

Kienböck’s disease is a wrist pathology characterized by avascular necrosis of the lunate bone located between the carpal bones. The disease most commonly occurs in individuals aged 20–40 who use their wrists intensively and repetitively. If left untreated, it can lead to permanent pain, limited range of motion, and degenerative osteoarthritis [1, 2]. Although mechanical and vascular factors such as trauma, repetitive microtraumas, radioulnar length difference (maturation disorder), lunate morphology, and vascular variations are thought to play a role in the etiology, the exact pathogenesis of the disease has not yet been fully elucidated [2].

Conventional wrist radiographs are the first-line imaging method in diagnosis, but their sensitivity is limited in the early stages. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) plays an important role in early diagnosis and post-treatment evaluation because it provides detailed information about bone marrow edema, necrotic areas and cartilage integrity in the lunate [3]. Treatment options for Kienböck’s disease range from conservative approaches and osteotomies aimed at correcting weight distribution to salvage surgical procedures such as proximal row carpectomy or wrist arthrodesis in advanced stages. Especially in young and active patients, vascularized bone grafting, aimed at restoring blood supply to the lunate bone and preventing its structural collapse, has become an important treatment option today [4]. Vascularized bone grafts prepared from the distal radius with a pedicle are commonly used in the treatment of lunate avascular necrosis because they do not require microvascular anastomosis and are anatomically close to the carpal bones [5, 6]. Distal radius vascularized bone graft based on 4 + 5 extensor compartment arteries (ECA), as described by Moran et al., has become a commonly preferred revascularization method in Lichtman stage II–III cases thanks to its long pedicle length and reliable circulation [5]. In the current literature, a reduction in pain and a significant improvement in grip strength are reported in patients who underwent 4 + 5 ECA pedicle graft application [5, 7]. However, it is noteworthy that most of these studies included a relatively small number of patients and the results obtained showed heterogeneity [7, 8].

This study evaluated the mid-term clinical and radiological outcomes of patients diagnosed with Lichtman stage II–IIIa Kienböck’s disease who underwent vascularized bone graft with 4 + 5 ECA pedicles. The aim was to separately examine stage II and stage IIIa subgroups to demonstrate lunate revascularization using MRI findings and to analyze the improvement in clinical parameters, considering possible differences between the stages.

Methods

This study was approved by the Non-Interventional Clinical Research Ethics Committee of Diyarbakır Gazi Yaşargil Training and Research Hospital, Health Sciences University, with decision number 44 dated 10.05.2024. Data from patients diagnosed with Kienböck disease who underwent 4 + 5 ECA pedicle vascularized bone graft surgery between June 2022 and January 2024 were retrospectively reviewed. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants for inclusion in the study and for the use of their data for scientific purposes.

Patients with a diagnosis of Kienböck disease confirmed by clinical examination and imaging, Lichtman stage II or IIIa disease, and no previous surgery on the affected wrist were included in the study. Patients with Lichtman stage IIIb–IV disease, advanced osteoarthritis, inflammatory arthritis, a follow-up duration of less than 18 months, or inability to maintain regular follow-up were excluded. In bilateral cases, each wrist was evaluated as an independent case.

All surgeries were performed by a single hand surgeon under pneumatic tourniquet and using a dorsal approach. The patient was positioned supine, and the affected upper extremity was positioned on a hand table. The joint was accessed via a longitudinal incision made between the 3rd and 4th extensor compartments on the dorsum of the wrist. The extensor tendons were carefully retracted, exposing the 4 + 5 ECA on the dorsal aspect of the distal radius. A corticocancellous bone graft of appropriate size and thickness to match the dimensions of the lunate was harvested from the distal radius over the pedicle containing these arteries (Fig. 1). A cortical window was created in the dorsum of the lunate, and necrotic and avascular bone tissue was carefully curetted and debrided. Debridement was continued with the aim of removing necrotic and avascular bone tissue and, whenever possible, reaching macroscopically viable, bleeding bone. In cases where macroscopically viable, bleeding bone could not be identified despite adequate debridement, further bone removal was avoided to preserve carpal structural integrity and mechanical stability. The prepared 4 + 5 ECA pedicled vascularized bone graft was then placed into the prepared bone bed, compressed in the appropriate position, and primary stability was achieved (Fig. 2). The capsule and extensor retinaculum were closed anatomically, and the skin was sutured. In the postoperative period, the wrist was immobilized in a neutral position with a short arm splint. After approximately 4–6 weeks, immobilization was discontinued, wrist movements were initiated, and a gradual strengthening exercise program was started.

Standard posteroanterior and lateral wrist radiographs and MRI scans were performed preoperatively and at the final follow-up visit. Radiographic evaluation included Lichtman staging, ulnar variance, carpal height ratio, and Stahl index measurements. MRI scans semi-quantitatively assessed signal changes in the lunate, bone marrow edema, and necrotic areas. Revascularization was defined as an increase in bone marrow signal intensity within the lunate on T1-weighted images and regression of bone marrow edema, manifested by decreased signal intensity, on fluid-sensitive T2-weighted images.

Clinically, pain intensity was scored using a 0–10 visual analog scale (VAS) [9]. The Mayo Wrist Score [10] was applied for functional evaluation. Wrist joint range of motion was measured using a goniometer, and flexion, extension, radial, and ulnar deviation angles were recorded. Grip strength was measured using a hand dynamometer, and the grip strength of the operated hand was calculated as a percentage of the contralateral hand’s grip strength.

Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation. The normality of the data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Student t-test was used to compare normally distributed data, Mann–Whitney U test for non-normally distributed data, and Wilcoxon signed-rank test for paired samples. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Lichtman stage II and stage IIIa subgroups were analyzed separately. While subgroups were compared within themselves in terms of preoperative and postoperative values, intergroup comparisons were made based on post-control measurements. Overall mean values for the entire cohort were reported, with particular emphasis on potential differences between stage subgroups.

In addition, a subgroup analysis was performed according to ulnar variance. Patients were categorized into two groups based on preoperative wrist radiographs: negative ulnar variance and neutral/positive ulnar variance. Postoperative clinical outcomes, including the Visual Analog Scale (VAS) pain score, Mayo Wrist Score, grip strength, radiographic disease progression, and MRI evidence of lunate revascularization, were compared between the two groups.

Results

A total of 30 patients (31 wrists) were included in the study; 19 patients were women and 11 were men. The mean age was 33.0 ± 9.8 years, ranging from 20 to 57 years. According to the preoperative Lichtman classification, 19 wrists (61.3%) were classified as stage II and 12 wrists (38.7%) as stage IIIa. The affected side was the right wrist in 20 cases and the left wrist in 11 cases. Of the stage II cases, 12 were in the right wrist and 7 in the left wrist; of the stage IIIa cases, 8 were in the right wrist and 4 in the left wrist. The mean follow-up period was 27.5 ± 5.6 months, ranging from 18 to 37 months (Table 1).

According to the study inclusion criteria, a minimum follow-up period of 18 months was required. Accordingly, two patients with follow-up durations of 6 and 12 months, respectively, were excluded from the study. In addition, two patients who had presented from another country for treatment were excluded because regular follow-up could not be maintained due to incorrect contact information. Thus, a total of four patients were excluded because they did not meet the minimum follow-up requirement or could not be followed regularly.

According to the latest control radiographs, out of a total of 31 wrists, 22 (71.0%) showed stable Lichtman stage, while 9 (29.0%) showed disease progression. When examining the stage subgroups, progression to a more advanced stage was observed in 4 out of 19 wrists (21.1%) in the stage II group, while stage progression occurred in 5 out of 12 wrists (41.7%) in the stage IIIa group. Among the wrists without radiographic disease progression, 15 of 19 wrists initially classified as Lichtman stage II and 7 of 12 wrists initially classified as stage IIIa maintained their initial stage throughout the follow-up period. No wrist demonstrated radiographic regression to an earlier Lichtman stage.

Of the wrists initially classified as stage II, two progressed to stage IIIa, one to stage IIIb, and one to stage IV. Of the wrists initially classified as stage IIIa, one progressed to stage IIIb, one to stage IIIc, and three to stage IV.

Significant MRI evidence of lunate revascularization was observed in a limited number of stage II wrists, whereas no significant revascularization was detected in the stage IIIa group. In the revascularized cases, regression of bone marrow edema and increased T1-weighted signal intensity were observed, with no subsequent progression of lunate collapse (Figs. 3 and 4). The carpal height ratio showed a limited decrease at the final follow-up, which was not statistically significant (p > 0.05) (Table 2 and 3).

A statistically significant reduction in VAS pain scores was observed in the overall series postoperatively (p < 0.05). Although this improvement was observed in both the stage II and stage IIIa groups, postoperative VAS scores were significantly lower in the stage II group than in the stage IIIa group (p < 0.05) (Table 2).

A statistically significant improvement in the Mayo Wrist Score was observed in the overall series postoperatively (p < 0.05). Although this improvement was observed in both stage groups, postoperative Mayo Wrist Scores were significantly higher in the stage II group than in the stage IIIa group (p < 0.05) (Table 2).

Although a limited increase in flexion–extension range of motion was observed postoperatively, the difference between preoperative and postoperative values was not statistically significant (p > 0.05) (Table 2).

Postoperative radial–ulnar deviation range of motion showed a slight increase; however, the difference from preoperative values was not statistically significant (p > 0.05) (Table 2).

Both stage groups demonstrated a limited postoperative increase in flexion–extension and radial–ulnar deviation range of motion, with no clinically significant difference observed between the groups (Table 2).

In both groups, the increases in postoperative range of motion remained clinically limited and did not reach statistical significance. However, the mean postoperative range of motion in the stage II group was systematically higher in all parameters compared to the stage IIIa group.

Subgroup analysis according to ulnar variance revealed no statistically significant differences between patients with negative ulnar variance and those with neutral or positive ulnar variance with respect to postoperative clinical outcomes, radiographic disease progression, or MRI evidence of lunate revascularization (Table 5).

A statistically significant improvement in grip strength was observed in the overall series postoperatively (p < 0.05). This improvement was more pronounced in the stage II group, which demonstrated significantly higher postoperative grip strength than the stage IIIa group (p < 0.05) (Table 2).

In the postoperative period, one patient developed a superficial surgical wound infection, and another developed a transient sensory deficit in the superficial radial nerve. Both complications resolved completely with conservative treatment. No fractures or significant donor site morbidity were observed in the distal radius region, the graft donor area, in any of the cases.

Of the 9 cases showing disease progression, 2 progressing from stage II to stage IIIa were placed under close clinical and radiological follow-up. Two of the remaining cases underwent lunate excision and scapho-capitate fusion during the follow-up period.

Discussion

In this study, successful mid-term results were obtained in early-stage Kienböck’s disease patients who underwent vascularized bone grafting with 4 + 5 ECA.

Patient selection plays a critical role in determining the optimal surgical treatment for Kienböck’s disease. Isolated 4 + 5 ECA pedicled vascularized bone grafting is the preferred joint-preserving procedure for patients with Lichtman stage II and selected stage IIIa disease, in whom the lunate is considered reconstructable and advanced radiocarpal degenerative changes are absent. The choice of this surgical technique is based primarily on the stage of the disease, preoperative radiographic and MRI findings, and the objective of preserving the native wrist anatomy while promoting the biological healing of the lunate. Alternative joint-preserving procedures, including radial shortening osteotomy, capitate shortening osteotomy, vascularized bone grafting combined with unloading procedures, and temporary radiocarpal spanning fixation, were not routinely performed in this patient cohort. Therefore, the present study was designed to evaluate the clinical and radiological outcomes of isolated 4 + 5 ECA pedicled vascularized bone grafting rather than to compare different joint-preserving surgical techniques.

In the postoperative period, all patients experienced a significant reduction in pain levels and a statistically significant improvement in wrist function scores. The mean VAS pain score decreased from 6.1 to 2.6, while the mean Mayo Wrist Score increased from 36.4 to 68.4. These clinical gains are consistent with results reported in the literature [11–17]. Levent et al. [11] and Aydemir et al. [12] reported significant clinical improvement in Kienböck patients after dorsal distal radius pedicle graft application. Similarly, in a recent study, Özdemir et al. [13] reported a decrease in VAS pain score from 6.5 to 2.4 and an increase in Mayo Wrist Score from 34 to 73.9. The pain and functional improvement rates obtained in our series are largely similar to these findings. However, the mean Mayo Wrist Score of 68.4 in our patients in the final evaluation is more modest compared to the results reported in some publications. For example, in the series of 21 patients by Byung S. Kim et al., the mean modified Mayo Wrist Score was reported as 87.9 [17]. Similarly, in the series of Özdemir et al., the mean final Mayo score was reported as 73.9 [13]. The lower mean value obtained in our study may be attributed to the inclusion of stage IIIa cases in the study and the more limited clinical gains obtained in these patients compared to stage II patients. Indeed, when the stage subgroups were evaluated separately, the mean postoperative Mayo Wrist Score was 72.0 in stage II patients, while it was 62.7 in the stage IIIa group. These findings suggest that the clinical gains that can be obtained with revascularized bone graft in more advanced stages of the disease may be relatively limited.

The literature suggests that vascularized bone grafting may provide favorable clinical outcomes, particularly in the early stages of Kienböck’s disease [4]. In their review, Kakar and Shin reported that the 4 + 5 ECA pedicled vascularized bone graft is a reliable treatment option, especially for patients with Lichtman stage II–IIIa Kienböck’s disease [6]. In our study, patients with Lichtman stage II disease achieved better clinical outcomes than those with stage IIIa disease. However, although MRI findings suggestive of revascularization were observed only in a limited number of stage II wrists, all patients demonstrated significant improvements in pain and functional outcomes. Therefore, the observed clinical improvement should not be attributed solely to MRI-confirmed revascularization. In addition to restoring vascularity, vascularized bone grafting may contribute to postoperative recovery through other biological and mechanical mechanisms, including debridement of necrotic bone, structural support provided by the graft, and stimulation of bone healing. Further prospective comparative studies incorporating advanced imaging modalities and larger patient cohorts are warranted to better clarify the relationship between radiological revascularization and clinical outcomes.

It is noteworthy that the rate of continued lunate collapse was higher in the stage IIIa group (41.7% in our series; 21.1% in the stage II group). Although this difference did not reach statistical significance, it suggests that the effectiveness of biological revascularization interventions may decrease in the later stages of the disease. Fujiwara et al. [18] reported functional improvements in vascularized radius grafts applied to stage III Kienböck patients for up to 10 years of follow-up, but showed that progressive degeneration could continue in some of the advanced stage cases. Therefore, the adequacy of revascularization alone in stage IIIa cases is discussed in the literature, and additional surgical strategies that can be applied together are suggested. Balancing joint loading with radial shortening osteotomy in patients with negative ulnar variance is among the suggested approaches [19]. In a recent study by Hong et al., it was reported that the combined application of radius wedge osteotomy and shortening in stage IIIa cases resulted in improvement in pain level and range of motion [20]. Although such combined approaches are promising, data on long-term results are still limited. In our series, it was observed that lunate collapse continued to progress in some of the stage IIIa cases (41.7%). Two of these patients underwent lunate excision and scapho-capitate fusion during the follow-up period, while salvage surgery was recommended for the other cases but was not performed by the end of the study period.

The radiological results obtained in our study are generally consistent with the literature. Although a limited decrease in the average carpal height ratio was observed in the postoperative period, this change was not statistically significant. Similarly, the change in the Stahl index remained minimal. These findings suggest that the application of vascularized bone graft contributes to the preservation of carpal geometry to a large extent in the short and medium term. Indeed, Kim et al. reported that no significant change in the postoperative carpal height ratio was detected in their series [17]. However, it is known that the success rates regarding the preservation of lunate height can vary between series. Moran et al. reported in their original study that clinical improvement was achieved in 92% of patients who underwent pedicled graft and that a significant increase in grip strength was obtained [5]. However, while the average grip strength in that series reached 89% of the contralateral hand, this rate remained at 70.6% in our entire series. It is thought that this difference may be due to the inclusion of stage IIIa cases in our study. In fact, while the average grip strength reached 75.5% in our stage II cases, this rate remained at 62.1% in the stage IIIa group. Similarly, in the 2024 study by Byung S. Kim et al., it was reported that the average grip strength reached 79.7% in all patients [17]. These data suggest that higher levels of hand grip strength can be recovered in patients treated surgically in the early stages, and that the disease stage is a determining factor in functional outcomes.

MRI findings that can be considered as direct indicators of lunate revascularization were detected in our study, especially in stage II cases. In the stage II group, 21% of the cases (4 wrists) showed significant signal changes and regression of bone marrow edema in postoperative MRI examinations, suggesting that lunate blood supply had improved. The absence of radiological progression in any of these cases during the follow-up period indicates that the vascularized graft was able to effectively restore lunate blood supply. In contrast, no significant signal changes were detected in post-graft MRI examinations in stage IIIa cases. It can be considered that in the presence of advanced sclerosis and partial collapse, it may be difficult to evaluate the vascularization of the graft within the lunate with MRI.

A noteworthy finding of the present study was the absence of MRI evidence of revascularization in patients with Lichtman stage IIIa disease despite significant improvements in pain and functional outcomes following surgery. This finding may be explained by the more advanced structural deterioration of the lunate and disruption of its vascular architecture in the later stages of the disease, which may reduce the potential for radiologically detectable revascularization. In addition, conventional MRI may not fully reflect the complex biological processes involved in postoperative healing. Therefore, the absence of MRI-confirmed revascularization should not be interpreted as treatment failure. The observed clinical improvement may also be attributable to other biological and mechanical effects of vascularized bone grafting, including structural support of the lunate, debridement of necrotic bone, and stimulation of bone healing. Nevertheless, the lack of radiological evidence of revascularization in all stage IIIa patients suggests that the biological response to vascularized bone grafting may be more limited in advanced disease stages, further emphasizing the importance of early surgical intervention in appropriately selected patients.

In the literature, the success rate of lunate revascularization following 4 + 5 ECA pedicled vascularized bone grafting has been reported to be approximately 71% [21]. In the review by Chojnowski et al., disease progression was observed in only 15% of patients during a mean follow-up period of 45 months, whereas revascularization and clinical improvement were achieved in the majority of cases treated with pedicled vascularized bone grafts [19]. In our series, although the mean follow-up period was shorter (approximately 27 months), radiographic disease progression was observed in 29% of the wrists. Despite this finding, significant improvements in pain, wrist function, and grip strength were achieved. These results indicate that symptomatic and functional recovery does not necessarily imply complete arrest of disease progression, particularly in patients with Lichtman stage IIIa disease. The discrepancy between clinical improvement and radiographic progression suggests that vascularized bone grafting may provide meaningful functional benefits through pain relief, improved wrist biomechanics, and stimulation of biological healing, even when structural progression of the lunate cannot be completely prevented. Therefore, clinical improvement should be interpreted independently of radiographic findings, and patients, particularly those with more advanced disease, should undergo careful long-term clinical and radiological follow-up. Nevertheless, the 71% stage stabilization rate reported after vascularized bone grafting remains noteworthy when compared with the natural course of untreated disease. Indeed, recent meta-analyses and long-term natural history studies have demonstrated that stage progression occurs in approximately 40% of conservatively managed Kienböck’s disease cases, whereas this rate decreases to approximately 17% in patients treated with vascularized bone grafts [16, 22, 23]. Collectively, these findings suggest that pedicled vascularized bone grafting may modify the natural course of the disease and slow the progression of lunate collapse, although it may not completely prevent radiographic progression in all patients. Furthermore, these observations underscore the importance of appropriate patient selection and early surgical intervention to maximize the potential benefits of vascularized bone grafting.

In the literature, it has been reported that the load on the lunate can be reduced by using temporary or permanent wrist supports in conjunction with vascularized bone graft application, and thus revascularization can be facilitated [24]. However, in our study, no additional fixation materials such as K-wire or radiocarpal spanning plate were used in any patient; stabilization was provided only with isolated 4 + 5 ECA pedicled vascularized bone graft. Therefore, the possible effects of internal fixation on disease stage progression could not be evaluated within the scope of the study. Future studies should investigate the effectiveness of combined surgical techniques in larger patient series and with longer follow-up periods.

This study has once again demonstrated that revascularization with pedicled distal radius graft is a safe method. No major complications occurred in our series. Only one patient experienced superficial surgical wound infection and one patient experienced transient sensory nerve irritation; these complications resolved completely with conservative treatment and did not require further surgical intervention. In particular, no fractures or instability developed in the distal radius region, which is the graft donor site.

In the original series by Moran et al., no serious complications were reported, and no donor site fractures were observed in any patient [5]. Similarly, Özdemir et al. [13] reported that only a few cases of minor complications were observed in their series of 23 patients, and all of these resolved with conservative methods. These findings show that 4 + 5 ECA-based vascularized bone graft surgery is a reliable method with a low complication rate in experienced hands. In addition, the reasonable operation time and the fact that it does not require microsurgical anastomosis are among the important advantages that increase the applicability of this technique [7].

Recent systematic reviews and meta-analyses suggest that both vascularized bone grafting and joint-unloading procedures, including radial shortening osteotomy and capitate shortening osteotomy, can provide satisfactory clinical outcomes in appropriately selected patients with early-stage Kienböck’s disease [25–27]. However, because the majority of the available evidence is derived from retrospective case series with considerable heterogeneity in patient selection, surgical indications, and outcome assessment, no joint-preserving procedure has been shown to be clearly superior to another. Consequently, current treatment algorithms recommend that the choice of surgical intervention should be individualized according to disease stage, ulnar variance, reconstructability of the lunate, carpal morphology, and surgeon experience [28]. In this context, the present study was not designed to compare different joint-preserving procedures but rather to evaluate the mid-term clinical and radiological outcomes of isolated 4 + 5 ECA pedicled vascularized bone grafting in selected patients with Lichtman stage II and IIIa disease. Future prospective comparative studies are warranted to better define the relative effectiveness and optimal indications of vascularized bone grafting compared with joint-unloading procedures.

Our study has several important limitations. First, its retrospective design and the absence of a control group treated with non-vascularized bone grafting or other surgical treatment modalities limit the strength of the conclusions that can be drawn from our findings. Although favorable clinical outcomes were observed following 4 + 5 ECA pedicled vascularized bone grafting, these findings should be interpreted as preliminary and require validation in future prospective comparative studies.

Second, the mean follow-up period of approximately 2.3 years is relatively short and does not provide comprehensive information regarding the long-term outcomes of vascularized bone grafting. Although previous studies have demonstrated that the benefits of revascularization procedures may be maintained beyond 5 years of follow-up [16, 18], degenerative changes may still progress over time in some patients. Therefore, the current follow-up duration is insufficient to adequately evaluate the long-term durability of pain relief, maintenance of grip strength, progression of lunate collapse, and the potential need for salvage procedures. Larger multicenter studies with longer follow-up periods are warranted to more accurately determine the long-term effectiveness and durability of this surgical technique.

Third, the limited number of patients, especially when divided into stage subgroups, led to some comparisons not reaching statistical significance. However, our findings obtained in the mid-term follow-up are consistent with trends reported in larger patient series and support the idea that 4 + 5 ECA pedicled vascularized bone graft application is an effective treatment option in early-stage Kienböck disease.

Fourth, postoperative MRI findings were not evaluated by a dedicated musculoskeletal radiologist, and no standardized MRI grading system was used. Consequently, the assessment of revascularization was based on qualitative rather than quantitative MRI findings, which may have limited the objectivity and reproducibility of the radiological evaluation.

Conclusions

In patients with early-stage Kienböck’s disease, 4 + 5 ECA pedicled vascularized distal radius grafting was associated with significant improvements in pain and wrist function, particularly in Lichtman stage II disease. Although favorable clinical outcomes were observed throughout the cohort, MRI evidence of revascularization was identified in only a limited number of patients, suggesting that postoperative recovery may involve mechanisms beyond radiological revascularization alone. The procedure appears to be a safe and practical surgical option because it does not require microsurgical anastomosis and was associated with a low complication rate in the present series. However, these findings should be interpreted in light of the retrospective study design and require confirmation in prospective comparative studies.
