Fate of post-blast residue in soils from an armed conflict zone determined by magnetic, microbiological, and ecotoxicological assessments
Authors: Kseniia M. Bondar, Anhelina Kyrychenko, Oleksandr Tashyrev, Ievgen Poliachenko, Volodymyr Bakhmutov, Anastasiia Sachko, Iryna Tsiupa, Volodymyr Karavan, Oleksandr Kruglov, Iryna Bida
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Weapon detonations generate substantial quantities of post-blast residue (PBR), which contributes iron-rich particles to soils. After deposition, these residues undergo redox transformations that produce bioavailable iron compounds, potentially increasing phytotoxic risks to agricultural crops. In the present study, soil contamination by detonation of military weapons was simulated by adding 1% of < 1 mm PBR to soil, and iron mobilization under waterlogged conditions was subsequently monitored. Scanning electron microscopy revealed two components in PBR: iron-bearing spherules and metallic fragments composed of an iron core with an oxidized surface layer. Magnetic analyses confirmed that waterlogging had a limited impact on PBR, with no significant changes in explosion-derived iron spherules; however, a decrease was observed in hematite-like phases associated with surface-corroded iron fragments. This decrease was linked to the interaction of these phases with metabolically active soil microorganisms. Following the addition of a carbon and energy source (potato) to PBR-contaminated soil, the abiotic reaction of Fe0 oxidation to Fe2+ significantly accelerated due to microbial synthesis of organic acids and protons (H+) and iron mobilization by microbial activity. This markedly affected the soft magnetic material (fine pedogenic magnetite and iron core of PBR fragments) as well as highly coercive phases (oxidized coating of iron fragments and lithogenic hematite). Phytotoxic effects were evaluated by assessing seed germination, seedling, and root growth in representative species planted during the period of elevated soluble iron concentrations. Increased iron accumulation in stressed seedlings was a key indicator of PBR toxicity. These laboratory-simulation results improve our understanding of the potential environmental transformation of war-related iron residues and their implications for soil sustainability, which require validation under field conditions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s10653-026-03497-x.

Introduction

The contamination of agricultural lands due to armed conflicts not only disrupts natural aquatic and terrestrial ecosystems but also poses a substantial threat to the diversity of bacterial communities, soil fertility and crop yield, with significant economic implications for agriculture (Gans et al., 2005; Zhu et al., 2020). Explosion of military weapons on battlefields results in the emission of iron- and aluminum-rich debris and fine particulates into the atmosphere, which are subsequently deposited on the ground as post-blast residue (PBR), enriching soils with various toxic substances (Broomandi et al., 2020; Tauqeer et al., 2021).

Previous studies from areas affected by the ongoing war in Ukraine indicate that military activities introduce both iron-rich particles and potentially toxic elements into soils. Thus, at artillery-impacted battlefield in northern Ukraine, increased magnetic susceptibility and saturation remanent magnetization of soil has been attributed to metallic iron and fine-grained magnetite particles (1–5 μm) released during munitions explosions (Bondar et al., 2025). Positive correlations between concentrations of As, Co, Cu, Fe, Zn, and magnetic parameters have been reported. However, these enrichments are so minor that they can only be reliably detected against weakly magnetic, heavy-metal-depleted soil backgrounds. Regional geochemical studies in the Kharkiv and Mykolaiv regions show that heavy-metal concentrations at battlefields generally remain close to background levels, with pronounced enrichments of Cd, Cu, and Zn restricted to localized sites, such as tank or helicopter explosion sites (Bonchkovski, 2025; Hlavayskii et al., 2026; Solokha et al. 2022). Similarly, studies in the Sumy and Chernihiv regions found no consistent enrichment of most heavy metals in bomb craters, although localized increases in Ba, Zr, and Mn were observed (Datsko et al., 2024).

In contrast, iron-rich residues are consistently associated with military explosions and occur widely at affected sites. Large quantities of metallic fragments, measuring from tens of centimeters to sub-millimeter size PBR, persist on battlefields as mechanical contaminants, where they interact with surrounding soil, gradually dissolving and transforming over time (Bonchkovskyi, 2025; Bondar et al., 2025; Hlavatskyi et al., 2025; Solokha et al. 2022). Therefore, Fe was selected as the primary toxicological focus because it represents a dominant and persistent component of PBR and can undergo redox-driven mobilization, potentially increasing its bioavailability and phytotoxicity. This focus does not exclude the environmental relevance of other potentially toxic elements but reflects the specific aim of assessing the transformation and ecological effects of the iron-rich fraction of PBR.

Iron oxides derived from PBR originating from explosive detonations and heavy artillery use constitute a significant yet underexplored subgroup of anthropogenic Fe materials. The effects of these oxides on soil biochemical processes, microbial diversity, and phytotoxicity are poorly documented, creating a gap in our understanding of post-conflict environmental recovery.

Fe-containing materials serve many roles in soil remediation and plant growth stimulation. Fe2O3 and Fe3O4 nanoparticles can regulate plant growth, enhance resistance, alleviate stress, and improve overall plant resilience (Hou et al., 2024). Despite the positive effects of Fe materials on plants and soil, their potential toxicity and the environmental impact associated with their use in agriculture remain a serious concern because of controversial findings; this necessitates a comprehensive investigation of these materials to mitigate risks to human health through food chain transfer (Hou et al., 2024; Lee et al., 2010; Shah et al., 2022).

Understanding the transformation pathways of iron compounds in soils is essential because iron minerals play a critical role in regulating the mobility, bioavailability, and retention of nutrients and trace metals, including contaminants. Iron undergoes rapid redox cycling Fe(II) ↔ Fe(III) in response to environmental fluctuations such as changes in soil moisture, oxygenation, and organic matter decomposition. These transformations influence sorption processes, pH buffering, and the stabilization or release of toxic elements in both natural and disturbed settings (Cornell & Schwertmann, 2003; Voegelin et al., 2021).

Microorganisms play a crucial role as primary drivers of iron cycling. Iron-reducing and iron-oxidizing bacteria mediate mineral dissolution, precipitation, and phase transformations; these metabolic processes are usually faster and more selective than abiotic reactions. Their metabolic activity leads to the production of magnetite, ferrihydrite, and other nano- and micro-scale Fe oxides, which significantly alter soil magnetic properties and influence biogeochemical cycling (Emerson et al., 2010; Kappler & Straub, 2005). In contaminated soils, microbial pathways can either immobilize potentially toxic metals by co-precipitation with iron minerals or remobilize them during reductive dissolution, creating environmental risks (Borch et al., 2010; Zachara et al., 2001). Moreover, experimental studies have confirmed that soil microorganisms can mobilize insoluble Fe(II) and Fe(III) compounds by forming iron-organic chelates with microbial exometabolites (organic acids) (Govorukha & Tashyrev, 2016).

The success of vegetation establishment and the remediation of degraded lands are primarily determined by the ability of seeds to germinate in the presence of pollutants (Lefèvre et al., 2009; Mushtaq, 2011). Most existing studies have focused on the toxic effects of heavy metals in soil, highlighting their risks to plant growth, crop productivity, and human health (Angon et al., 2024; Shetty et al., 2025). Essential micronutrients such as Fe, with an indispensable role in plant metabolism, may also become highly toxic at elevated concentrations (Sevik et al., 2025; Tao et al., 2023; Tombuloglu et al., 2022, 2024). The phytotoxicity of iron is context-dependent and has been insufficiently studied in soils affected by military activities. In particular, the behavior and ecological impact of iron derived from PBR, a widespread but poorly characterized contaminant in war zones, remain largely unexplored, especially in fertile chernozem soils of eastern Ukraine. Predicting the involvement of iron compounds in biogeochemical cycles and assessing the potential risks of iron contamination to plants are important for understanding the long-term environmental impacts of war-related iron residues.

This study reports laboratory experiments on artificial mixtures of the most common soil originating from the current war zone in eastern Ukraine and PBR obtained as a magnetic extract from surface deposits at an unexploded ordnance (UXO) destruction site. The study was designed as a simulation of contamination expected in current frontline lands experiencing long-term heavy shelling and bombing. The study aimed to conduct a stepwise magnetic, microbiological, and ecotoxicological assessment of soils enriched with PBR by addressing the following specific questions: what are the mineralogical and morphological components of PBR? Is soluble iron mobilized from PBR under waterlogged soil conditions due to the metabolic activity of the soil microbiome, and what proportion of this iron can be specifically attributed to PBR? What are the main drivers controlling the transformation of iron into bioavailable, mobilized forms in contaminated soil? Does iron derived from PBR exhibit phytotoxic effects on seed germination and early seedling development in chernozem soils?

Materials and methods

Study design

This study was conducted in three consecutive stages: (1) laboratory simulation of environmental conditions and sample preparation; (2) magnetic assessment of mineral transformation; and (3) evaluation of phytotoxic effects through a seed germination experiment. This stepwise design allowed us to trace PBR transformation in soil from its initial contamination to its biological impact.

In the first stage, realistic conditions in PBR-contaminated soil from a battlefield in eastern Ukraine were simulated in the laboratory. A dedicated experiment was conducted to validate the hypothesis of microbial mobilization of iron from PBR. The following activities were performed: (a) extraction of PBR from surface deposits at a long-operating UXO destruction site and it`s chemical and micromorphological characterization; (b) preparation of PBR-contaminated soil by mixing PBR with control—natural soil from the Kharkiv region—at concentrations expected under real battlefield conditions; (c) exposure of both control and PBR-contaminated soils to waterlogging to reproduce seasonal soil saturation, with time-lapse measurements of metabolic parameters of soil microbiome activity in soil, such as pH, Eh (redox potential), optical density (D) of growing bacteria in soil water, total carbon content (Ctotal) in soil water, soil gas phase analysis (H2, O2, N2, CH4, and CO2) and concentrations of Fe(II) and Fe(III) soluble compounds in the liquid phase of soil extracts; (d) enhancement of the conditions in step (c) by adding a carbon and energy source (potato) to PBR-contaminated soil to further validate the hypothesis of iron mobilization from PBR through microbial activity. This stage aimed to produce samples for further characterization of environmentally relevant PBR transformations in war-affected soils and to determine the period of maximum Fe(II) and Fe(III) compound mobilization, thereby defining the optimal timing for subsequent biological testing.

The second stage of the study focused on the magnetic monitoring of mineralogical transformations. Magnetic measurements were conducted to quantify changes in soil magnetic properties following the addition of PBR and its subsequent degradation under waterlogged conditions. This approach enabled us to quantitatively assess contamination effects and track iron-bearing phase transformations.

The third stage involved evaluation of the phytotoxic effects of PBR-contaminated soil using a plant-based bioassay. Representative monocotyledonous and dicotyledonous species were used as test organisms to examine plant responses to the contaminated soil. This stage aimed to determine whether mobilized iron, derived from PBR, induces toxic effects on early plant development.

The methodological details of each stage are described below.

Description of samples

PBR (sample PBR in Table 2) was obtained as a magnetic extract from surface samples of sandy deposits collected at the UXO destruction site Stari Petrivtsi, located in the Kyiv region (N50.630, E30.364). This site has been operational since 2013, and multiple controlled detonations of various types of ammunition have been conducted at this location (Bondar et al., 2025). A magnetic extract was prepared by dispersing the abovementioned sieved material in water and separating the magnetic fraction using a neodymium magnet. The resulting magnetic extract constituted 1% (w/w) of the surface deposits. Therefore, to replicate the original contamination level, the Chernik Phaeozem soil was mixed with PBR to achieve a concentration of 1% (w/w) (sample S+PBR in Table 2), ensuring a consistent concentration of metal contaminants across the different experimental phases.

To simulate the microbial transformation of control soil and PBR-enriched soil under anaerobic conditions representative of seasonal waterlogging due to factors such as intensive snowmelt and intensive summer rainfall, a special experiment was designed consisting of 3 variants (Fig. 1):Fig. 1Experimental scheme depicting the simulation of microbial mobilization of ferrous insoluble compounds under waterlogging conditions: electrodes: 1—pH, 2—Eh, 3—gas holder, 4—tube for sampling the liquid phase; 5—soil, 6—PBR, post-blast residue, 7—potato

Variant 1 (control): natural soil (200 g) and 50 mL water were added to a container (Fig. 1a). Input: sample S; output: sample S-W.

Variant 2: 2 g PBR was added to 200 g natural soil and 50 mL water (Fig. 1b). Input: sample S+PBR; output: sample S+PBR-W.

Variant 3: A mixture of soil (200 g), water (50 mL), PBR (2 g), and potatoes (20 g, size: 0.2 cm) was placed in a container. The potatoes served as a carbon and energy source for promoting microbial metabolism in the model system. Output: sample S+PBR+p-W.

To prevent soil moisture loss, the containers were sealed with lids. The lids were fitted with electrodes (1 and 2) for measuring soil pH and Eh, respectively, and a gas holder (3)—a vessel with a sealed rubber gasket on the top; the collected gas was sampled with a syringe and needle and used for subsequent gas chromatographic analysis. Soil solution was withdrawn using a syringe through a tube (4) for the analytical determination of Fe(II) and Fe(II) soluble compounds and for measuring biomass growth (optical density of soil water). The cultivation period was 20 days at 20 °C.

Electron microscopy investigation of PBR

Chemical composition analysis and micromorphological characterization of PBR particles were conducted in the Laboratory of Electron Microscopy, Microanalysis and X-Ray Diffraction at the University of Warsaw. A Zeiss Sigma 500 VP (Jena, Germany) scanning electron microscope equipped with an energy-dispersive X-ray spectrometer XFlash 6I10 (Bruker, Germany) was used for this purpose. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and energy-dispersive X-ray spectrometry (EDX) were performed on both powdered PBR material and polished cross-sections prepared from the PBR material embedded in epoxy resin.

Measurements of Fe(II) and Fe(III) contents

The concentrations of soluble Fe(II) and Fe(III) compounds were measured by a colorimetric method (Sandell, 1959) using the photoelectric colorimeter KPK-2PM (JSC “Zagorsk Optical and Mechanical Plant,” Sergiyev Posad, Russia).

The concentration of Fe(II) was measured using o-phenanthroline. For this purpose, 1.5 mL of the supernatant was taken and 0.75 ml of a 0.25% o-phenanthroline solution was added. The presence of Fe(II) was indicated by the formation of a red Fe(II)—o-phenanthroline complex. The Fe(II) concentration was determined at λ = 490 nm and an optical path length of 0.5 cm.

The concentration of Fe(III) was determined based on the formation of red-colored Fe(III)–thiocyanate complexes with potassium thiocyanate (KSCN) in an acidic medium. For this, 1.5 mL of the culture fluid was taken, followed by the addition of 0.25 mL of 1.5 M KSCN solution and 0.75 mL of concentrated HCl. The Fe(III) concentration was determined at λ = 490 nm using an optical path length of 1.0 cm.

Assessment of the metabolic activity of the soil microbiome

Microbial synthesis of chelators of iron-oxidized insoluble compounds was evaluated by measuring Ctotal using the permanganate titration method (Suslova et al., 2014). Microbial growth in soil water was determined based on the increase in the optical density (D) value estimated by the colorimeter KPK-2PM at 540 nm wavelength with an optical path length of 0.5 cm (Mira and Yeh, 2022). Metabolic activity was assessed based on the composition of the gas phase using gas chromatograph LKhM-8MD (Drugov & Berezkin, 1991). Two steel columns were used: one (I) for analyzing H2, O2, N2, and CH4 and the other (II) for analyzing CO2. Column parameters were as follows: column I: l = 3 m, d = 3 mm, sorbent 13X (NaX); column II: l = 2 m, d = 3 mm, sorbent Porapak-Q. The following chromatographic conditions were maintained: column temperature, + 60 °C; evaporator temperature, + 75 °C; detector temperature, − 60 °C; detector current, − 50 mA; carrier gas, argon; gas flow rate, 30 mL/min. The content of H2, O2, N2, and CO2 gases (%) was calculated from the area of their peaks.

pH and Eh were determined by potentiometry (Tashyrev & Prekrasna, 2014) and calorimetry using sodium resazurinate as a redox indicator (Wagner et al., 2019), respectively. Potentiometric measurements were performed using the pH–150 MA pH/millivoltmeter and measuring electrodes (Antech, Gomel, Belarus). A porous glass electrode (ESC-10603/4) was used for pH measurements, while a platinum electrode (EPV-1) and a chlorine silver reference electrode (EVL-1M3) were used to measure Eh.

Magnetic analyses

Magnetic analyses were conducted at the Institute of Geophysics PAS in Warsaw to characterize magnetic mineralogy, grain size distribution, and concentration in the samples. Six sub-samples were prepared from each sample and analyzed.

Volume magnetic susceptibility k was measured on samples packed into 8 cm3 cubic plastic boxes using the MFK1-FA Kappabridge instrument (AGICO, Brno, Czech Republic); operating frequencies: 976 and 15,616 Hz, magnetic field amplitude: 200 A/m. The mass-specific magnetic susceptibility χ was calculated from the sample’s dry weight. Frequency dependence of magnetic susceptibility (kfd) was determined from the k values measured at the two frequencies and corrected for the apparatus ratio (0.8), as recommended by Hrouda (2011). kfd was used as a proxy of the relative proportions of superparamagnetic (SP) and multidomain (MD) fractions in the total magnetic susceptibility signal (Dearing et al., 1997).

Artificial 2 cm cubic monoliths were prepared from soil samples to investigate anhysteretic remanent magnetization (ARM) and saturation isothermal remanence (Mrs). Monoliths were formed by pouring the moist material into a cubic-shaped plastic box and allowing it to dry. ARM was imparted by a DC bias field of 100 µT and a maximum alternating field of 100 mT. Mrs was acquired in a field of 1 T using an MMPM10 Pulse Magnetizer (Magnetic Measurements, UK). Remanence was measured with a spinner magnetometer (JR6A, Agico, Brno) with a sensitivity of 2 × 10–6 A/m. ARM susceptibility (χARM) was calculated as mass-normalized ARM per unit bias field.

Isothermal remanent magnetization (IRM) for selected samples was induced in a stepwise manner and measured up to a maximum field of 1 T using the MicroMag AGM 2900 system (Princeton Measurement Corporation, Princeton, NJ, USA). The obtained IRM acquisition curves were further processed with MAX UnMix software (Maxbauer et al., 2016) to identify the individual components contributing to remanence. The following parameters were estimated for each sample: the relative contribution to the total IRM (% contr.); the field at which half of the saturation IRM was acquired (Bh, expressed in log-units and mT); and the width of the coercivity distribution, defined as one standard deviation of the logarithmic distribution and expressed by the dispersion parameter (DP) (Kruiver et al., 2001).

The ratios χARM/χ and Mrs/χ were utilized as relative measures of the concentration of remanence carriers. High values indicate relative enrichment, whereas low values, in our case, correspond to the predominance of SP particles.

Magnetic mineralogy was derived from Curie temperature (Tc) and phase transformation observed during the measurement of the thermal dependence of magnetic susceptibility k(T). It was measured using the KLY-5 Kappabridge instrument with a CS-L cryostat and a CS-3 furnace (AGICO, Czech Republic) during warming of the samples from liquid nitrogen temperature to room temperature and during heating of the samples to 700 °C.

Soil phytotoxicity assessment

Soil phytotoxicity was assessed by seed germination and early seedling growth assays. The following four plant species were used as bioindicators: a monocotyledonous species, i.e., Triticum aestivum L. (cv. Zymoyarka), and three dicotyledonous species from the family Brassicaceae: Sinapis alba L., Raphanus sativus L. (cv. Saxa Polana), and Lepidium sativum L. (cv. Rzeżucha ogrodowa). All seeds were used without surface sterilization and were soaked in distilled water for 3 h prior to sowing.

Phytotoxicity of PBR-contaminated soil was assessed based on physiological (seed germination) and morphological (seedling root and shoot length) parameters (ISO 11269-2:2012; ISTA, 1999; Leitgib et al., 2007; US Epa, 1996). Seed germination tests were conducted in Petri dishes (100 × 15 mm) lined with sterile filter paper and filled with the respective soil variants. Twenty seeds were placed on the soil surface in each dish, moistened with 6 mL of distilled water, and covered with a layer of filter paper. The dishes were sealed with parafilm to prevent moisture loss and incubated in the dark at 20–25 °C for 96 h. After incubation, the number of germinated seeds was recorded, and germination percentage was calculated as the percentage of germinated seeds relative to the total number of seeds.

Seedling growth was assessed using two approaches for exposure: (i) whole-soil exposure according to ISO 11269-2:2012 and (ii) soil–water extract exposure based on root elongation bioassay; this strategy allowed comparison of protocols known to produce different responses in metal-contaminated soils (Leitgib et al., 2007).

For the whole-soil exposure protocol, Petri dishes (d = 10 cm) were filled with control soil (S) and soil containing 1% (w/w) of PBR (S+PBR). Both soils were moistened to field capacity with deionized water to simulate waterlogging conditions and pre-incubated for 8 days at 22 °C. Subsequently, 6–13 seeds were placed on the soil surface and covered with sterile filter paper.

For the soil–water extract exposure protocol, aqueous extracts were prepared at a soil-to-water ratio of 1:4 (w/v). The suspension was shaken for 120 min and filtered through ashless filter paper. Next, 10 mL of the filtrate was added to Petri dishes lined with sterile filter paper, and 20 seeds were placed on the surface.

In both assays, seedlings were incubated in the dark at 20–25 °C for 96 h. Root and shoot lengths were measured using a digital caliper. Each treatment was performed in three independent replicates (n = 3). Growth inhibition was expressed as the percentage difference between the mean root or shoot length in the control and treatment groups, with negative values indicating growth stimulation.

After measurement, seedlings were rinsed with tap water, washed twice with distilled water, and separated into shoots and roots. Plant material was oven-dried at 55 °C to constant weight and used for iron content analysis.

Measurements of Fe content in plants

Dried plant samples were grounded using a laboratory mill and decomposed with concentrated nitric acid in a Multiwave 5000 reaction platform. The digestion was performed in two stages: 15 min of heating to 200 °C, followed by a 15-min hold at 200 °C. Fe concentration was determined using a C-115 M flame atomic absorption spectrometer (NVP “Akademprylad” LLP, Sumy, Ukraine) in an acetylene-air flame.

Results

Microscopic characterization of PBR

SEM images of PBR powder samples revealed the presence of angular metal fragments, mineral dust particles, and spherical particles ranging in size from 1 to 100 μm (Fig. 2). The spherules were uniformly distributed within the field of view, and many of them adhered to the surfaces of larger particles (Fig. 2b). This may explain the presence of non-magnetic dust in the magnetic extract. Observations of iron fragments and spherules in polished sections showed the presence of a metal core consisting of Fe (up to 100% (w/w)), coated with a layer of iron oxide at the surface (Fig. 2c, d). This finding indicates that both metal fragments and spherules predominantly exhibit a core–shell structure; moreover, in many cases, intergrowth structures of oxides inside the core were observed.Fig. 2SEM images and selected EDX spectra of the PBR powder material (a, b) and polished section (c, d)

SEM images and selected EDX spectra of the PBR powder material (a, b) and polished section (c, d)

According to EDX results, the spherules showed a low proportion of elements other than iron and oxygen (FeO ≈ 67–80% (w/w)) and contained heavy metals such as Cu, Sn, Pb, Cr, Mn, and Zn (Fig. 2b). However, spherules with a dominant composition of Sb and Pb were also observed (Fig. 2a, spectrum 1). The iron fragments also contained W, Mo, Al, Cr, V, Pb, Sb, Ti, and Sn. The presence of small Pt peaks (Fig. 2c, d) in the spectrograms could be attributed to platinum sputtering of polished sections during sample preparation.

Thus, we found that the main components of PBR are iron-containing particles, including surface-oxidized (rusty) metal fragments and spherical particles of varying diameter.

Indicators of metabolic activity of soil microbiome and mobilization of insoluble Fe(II) and Fe(III) compounds

Variants 1 (S) and 2 (S+PBR) showed low metabolic activity of the natural soil microbiome. Over the course of 12 days, O2 concentration gradually decreased from 21 to 3–4%, while CO2 concentration slightly increased to 7–8% by the end of the experiment (Fig. 3a). H2 content remained low, ranging from 2 to 5% throughout the experiment (Fig. 3b). Low metabolic activity was also confirmed by low concentrations of soluble organic compounds (Ctotal: 80–160 mg/L), a slight increase in biomass (D up to 0.24 optical density units), a slight decrease in pH (from 7.8 to 7.0), and positive Eh values (+ 360 to +100 mV) (Fig. 3a–d). Therefore, the concentration of soluble Fe(II) and Fe(III) compounds remained low and did not exceed 50–85 mg/L (Fig. 3j, h).

The addition of an organic substrate (potato) in Variant 3 (S+PBR+p) stimulated intense microbial metabolic activity, which was accompanied by elevated concentrations of Fe(II) and Fe(III) compounds in the soil solution. On day 12, the maximum concentrations of Fe(II) and Fe(III) reached 960 and 420 mg/L, respectively. Microbial activity rapidly depleted O2, sharply decreased Eh (from + 420 to − 205 mV) and pH (from 7.8 to 4.2), and increased the concentration of H2 and CO2 (up to 58% and 72%, respectively). The increase in the concentration of acidic soluble compounds (chelators for mobilizing insoluble oxidized iron) was confirmed by the increase in Ctotal from 140 to 1200 mg/L and a simultaneous decrease in pH.

Magnetic properties and mineralogy

Natural Chernik Phaeozem soil (sample S) exhibited a magnetic susceptibility value of 78.7 ± 3.3∙10–8 m3kg−1, which is typical for Ukrainian chernozem-like soil (Jeleńska et al., 2004; Gorka Kostrubiec et al., 2016). The addition of 1 w% PBR to soil (sample S+PBR) increased χ by 27–46%, up to 126 ± 19.1∙10–8 m3kg−1. Discussion

Evidence of PBR transformation in soil based on magnetic analyses

PBR, collected from surface deposits at UXO destruction sites and battlefields, comprises detonation-derived ferromagnetic particles that can significantly increase soil magnetic susceptibility and obscure the pedogenic magnetic signal (Bondar et al., 2025). SEM–EDX analyses revealed that PBR is predominantly composed of iron-bearing particles, including surface-oxidized (rusted) metallic fragments and spherical particles of varying diameters. Iron fragments are introduced into the soil during explosive events, when metallic casings are violently fragmented and dispersed (Clausen et al., 2018; Jenkins et al., 2006). These particles typically exhibit a core–shell structure, consisting of a metallic iron core surrounded by an oxidized outer layer composed mainly of iron oxyhydroxides. Additionally, spherical particles (spherules), ranging from 1 to 100 μm in diameter, are formed from molten iron-bearing material during detonation and represent a characteristic component of PBR. Both particle types may contain trace and potentially toxic metals, with their elemental composition reflecting that of the corresponding munition residues (Yüksel et al., 2025).

In terrestrial ecosystems, the redistribution of iron is most prominently observed in early spring, during snowmelt and subsequent waterlogging, when limited oxygen diffusion promotes anaerobic microbial activity and enhances iron redox cycling (Colombo et al., 2014; Kappler et al., 2021). Waterlogging induces reducing conditions even at low soil temperatures (+ 4 °C to 12 °C) (Kronberg et al., 2024), leading to enhanced dissolution of Fe oxides and increased concentrations of dissolved iron in porewater (Barczok et al., 2023). In chernozem soils, seasonal waterlogging following snowmelt generally persists from several days up to 2–3 weeks and may exceed 20 days, depending on hydrometeorological factors and soil drainage properties (Panasiuk, 2003). Moreover, in clay-rich chernozem-like soils, reductive dissolution of iron may occur even during mild winters if the waterlogging state persists for more than 1–2 weeks (Kronberg et al., 2024). Thus, the 20-day duration adopted in the laboratory waterlogging experiment may be considered a realistic proxy for early spring conditions typical of such environments.

The addition of 1 wt. % PBR to soil—consistent with concentrations observed at the UXO destruction site—increased χ by ~30–50%. Subsequent exposure to waterlogging predominantly affected the pedogenic magnetic phase, as evidenced by a comparable decrease in χ in both control and PBR-contaminated soils. According to Maher (1998), even minor losses (< 0.1%) of ultrafine-grained (< ~20 nm) ferrimagnetic particles can drastically reduce χ.

The dissolution of fine-grained SP pedogenic magnetite relatively increased the contribution from coarse PBR-derived magnetic grains, as evidenced by a substantial decrease in χfd in S+PBR-W and, more markedly, in S+PBR+p-W. In the latter case, intensified microbial activity likely induced extensive depletion of pedogenic ferrimagnetic phases, thereby causing PBR-derived particles to dominate the magnetic signal.

This interpretation is further supported by an approximately twofold decrease in χARM, reflecting significant dissolution of small single-domain (SD) pedogenic magnetic grains after 20 days of waterlogging. Moreover, a notable reduction in Mrs in contaminated soils (S+PBR-W compared to S+PBR) indicates that PBR components were also affected under these conditions.

Thermomagnetic analysis revealed that PBR contains magnetite and metallic iron. The Hopkinson peak, a characteristic of MD magnetite, appeared in all bulk PBR-contaminated soil samples (S+PBR, S+PBR-W, and S+PBR+p-W), confirming that this magnetite phase is stable to waterlogging.

Coercivity spectra analysis based on the unmixing of the IRM acquisition curves provided significant insights on the magnetic phases present in PBR. Iron fragments (PBRcr) were composed of soft (Bh < 27 mT) and hard (Bh = 97–163 mT) phases, corresponding to iron core and oxidized coating, respectively, while spherules (PBRfn) showed an intermediate coercivity range (Bh = 47–55 mT). Waterlogging slightly reduced the highly coercive phase, which could be attributed to microbial dissolution of highly coercive oxyhydroxides (e.g., hematite and goethite) of PBRcr coating, as the highly coercive phase of the control soil coercivity spectrum (lithogenic hematite) remained unchanged after waterlogging. In aerobic environments, ferromagnetic iron oxides of PBRcr are relatively stable; however, under anaerobic or waterlogged conditions, soil microorganisms promote the reduction of insoluble Fe(III) oxides to soluble Fe(II) forms (Sahrawat, 2004). Following the addition of the organic substrate (variant 3), the microbial-driven dissolution of soft phase, both pedogenic and PBRcr, revealed dominance of the PBRfn (spherules)-related intermediate coercivity phase, namely coarse-grained MD magnetite.

Thus, the findings of this study indicate that iron fragments may decompose progressively under environmental conditions, whereas spherical particles remain comparatively stable. Iron fragment degradation enriches soil with potentially toxic elements present within alloy phases, including Pb, Sb, Cr, and W.

Magnetic measurements demonstrated that explosion-derived spherules, predominantly composed of coarse magnetite, were remarkably resilient to waterlogging conditions. The addition of an organic substrate to PBR-contaminated soil did not induce measurable decomposition of the fine PBR component. This observation aligns with the relative stability of industrial fly ash particles in natural environments, including peat bogs, where anthropogenic magnetic particles derived from centuries-old industrial activity continue to produce a strong magnetic signal (e.g., Michczynski et al., 2022; Oldfield et al., 2015; Thompson & Oldfield, 1986).

The results of this study, however, offer only a hypothetical basis for predicting the long-term fate and field behavior of PBR in war-affected soils, and therefore warrant further validation.

The integral pathways of interaction between the soil microbiome and iron compounds

The studied chernozem-like soil (S) is characterized by a relatively high total iron content (27,283–31,097 mg kg−1), with the majority of iron occurring in poorly soluble mineral forms, including Fe(III) oxides, hydroxides, and silicate-bound phases. Under deep anaerobic, low-potential conditions, soil microorganisms mobilize mostly native iron, with a small contribution from PBR-derived phases, at least during the early stages of incubation (Kappler et al., 2021). Our results showed that microbial activity under oxygen-limited conditions promotes the transformation of insoluble Fe(II) and Fe(III) oxides to soluble, mobile Fe2+ and Fe3+ cations as well as soluble complexes (e.g., H3C–COO–Fe2+–OOCCH3) creating a transient phase of elevated iron mobility.

Microorganisms cannot directly utilize metallic iron because the redox potential (Eh) of the Fe0 oxidation reaction is more negative than that of the lowest-potential hydrogen fermentation reaction (Pourbaix, 1963). Thus, the reaction Fe0 + 3H2O = Fe(OH)3 + 3H+ + 3e has an Eh of − 473 mV, whereas the hydrogen fermentation reaction (C6H12O6 + 2H2O = 2CH3COOH + 4H2 + 2CO2) shows a 60 mV higher Eh (− 14 mV). Therefore, Fe0 oxidation is exclusively a chemical, abiotic process in which Fe0 is oxidized to insoluble compounds, while water-derived protons are reduced to H2. Hydrogen synthesis is a marker of deeply anaerobic metabolism and the associated synthesis of iron chelators (organic acids in particular (Hovorukha, et al., 2021)). In the presence of O2, a dense layer of insoluble Fe(II) and Fe(III) compounds forms on the surface of metallic iron due to the precipitation of Fe2+ and Fe3+ cations at slightly acidic and neutral pH values. This layer blocks protons’ access to the metallic iron surface and significantly inhibits the oxidation of Fe0.

Microorganisms in soil drive the anaerobic low-potential degradation of organic polymers (proteins and polysaccharides) (Chen et al., 2020). This process also generates organic acids (including acetate) and protons (e.g., CH3COOH = CH3COO− + H+). Organic acids serve as strong chelators of metals, facilitating the mobilization of otherwise insoluble Fe(II) and Fe(III) precipitates. The increase in proton content dissolves Fe(II) and Fe(III) precipitates (e.g., Fe2O3 + 6H+ = 2Fe2+ + 3H2O). Consequently, precipitates of oxidized iron are removed from the surfaces of iron particles, substantially accelerating the abiotic oxidation of Fe0 to Fe2+. Fe0 oxidation is further accelerated by acidification (protons from organic acids) and by the removal of iron chelates from Fe0 particle surfaces due to mass exchange through vigorous gas release during anaerobic fermentation.

A high concentration of acidic chelating metabolites promoted the dissolution of iron oxides and hydroxides on the surface of metallic iron, while a high concentration of protons accelerated the abiotic oxidation of metallic iron by protons of water: Fe0 + 2H+ = Fe2+ + H2. Thus, in ecosystems, metabolically active soil microbiomes significantly accelerate the abiotic, chemical oxidation of Fe0, thereby reducing the mass of PBR and, correspondingly, its magnetization.

Our laboratory-scale results demonstrate the capacity of the soil microbiome to mediate rapid and efficient iron mobilization within a relatively short period (8–20 days), even under closed, non-flow-through conditions. In natural environments, characterized by long time scales with active mass exchange processes and recurrent waterlogging, the contribution of these processes to the incorporation of iron compounds into biogeochemical cycles, particularly during iron accumulation by plants, is expected to be substantially greater.

Clear visual evidence of complex redox transformations during abiotic and biotic iron oxidation and mobilization processes is presented in the experiment described in Supplementary Material. The obtained data confirmed that, in the presence of water, Fe0 is slowly oxidized by water protons to the Fe2+ cation. Atmospheric oxygen then oxidizes Fe2+ to insoluble oxides (Fe2O3 and Fe3O4), which block proton access to iron. These oxides clearly inhibit the Fe0 oxidation process. Under biotic conditions, metabolically active microorganisms ferment organic substrates and synthesize acidic products (organic acids) and gases (H2 and CO2). Protons and organic radicals of acidic products (e.g., H+ and CH3COO−) dissolve iron oxides, thereby facilitating the oxidation of Fe0 by protons. Explosive gas synthesis leads to active mass exchange and effective removal of Fe(II) and Fe(III) compounds from the surface of metallic iron. In addition, the concentration of protons increases during anaerobic fermentation, substantially accelerating the abiotic reaction of oxidation of Fe0 to Fe2+. Thus, in ecosystems, metabolically active microorganisms can function as biocatalysts, enhancing the oxidation of Fe⁰ by protons of microbial origin and promoting the mobilization of oxidized iron products.

Phytotoxicity of PBR

Under anaerobic conditions, the microbial transformation of PBR progressively creates a reducing environment, with subsequent iron mobilization into its more soluble and biologically available Fe2+ forms. The 8-day pre-incubation (fermentation) of chernozem prior to phytotoxicity assessment was introduced to simulate environmentally relevant soil conditions associated with the early stages of spring waterlogging (Colombo et al., 2014; Kappler et al., 2021). The resulting increase in Fe2⁺ may induce species-specific physiological responses and affect iron uptake and distribution in plants.

The differences in Fe accumulation observed among species indicate different strategies of iron homeostasis. Fe concentrations in the shoots of L. sativum and R. sativus were lower in S+PBR soil than in the control (S), suggesting the involvement of regulatory mechanisms that limit iron translocation from roots to shoots when external Fe2+ availability increases, possibly through the regulation of iron uptake systems such as IRT1 and FRO2 (Martín-Barranco et al., 2020; Spielmann et al., 2023). In contrast, S. alba accumulated more Fe in shoots, consistent with the higher metal accumulation capacity reported for some Brassicaceae species (Li et al., 2023; Sági-Kazár et al., 2021). T. aestivum displayed an excluder-type response, characterized by substantial iron accumulation in roots (up to 30- to 50-fold higher than that in shoots) and limited translocation to above-ground tissues (shoots). This protective response involves immobilization of excess Fe in the root apoplast, complexation with organic ligands (such as citrate and nicotianamine), and sequestration in vacuoles, thus reducing the risk of toxicity in photosynthetically active tissues (Chao & Chao, 2022; Leskova et al., 2025).

PBR contamination did not significantly affect seed germination under either whole-soil or soil–water extract exposure conditions. The germination percentage remained high (> 95%) in wheat, radish, and garden cress, while mustard showed a slightly lower value (87–89%) without significant differences between treatments. These results agree with previous studies showing that seed germination is relatively insensitive to iron oxides and metal-containing particles under a wide range of exposure conditions (Di Salvatore et al., 2008; Shah, et al., 2022; Tang et al., 2012).

In contrast, early seedling growth was more sensitive to soil conditions, particularly when directly exposed to contaminated soil. The negligible toxicity observed in soil–water extracts indicates that the phytotoxic effects cannot be explained solely by the concentration of dissolved iron in the bulk soil solution. Although other factors associated with PBR-contaminated soil, including microbial metabolites, direct contact of roots with PBR particles, and non-iron (trace) elements present in the PBR, may also contribute to plant responses, our results suggest that bioavailable Fe2+ is the most plausible explanation for the phytotoxic response observed in this study. SEM–EDX analysis showed that the PBR consisted predominantly of iron oxides, whereas the accompanying non-iron elements were detected only in minor amounts and were unlikely to account for the observed biological effects.

During soil incubation, microbial activity promoted the reduction of Fe(III) to Fe(II). This was accompanied by the decline in Eh and the increase in Fe2+ concentration, while pH changed only slightly. These results indicate that iron mobilization was the major geochemical process under the experimental conditions. Therefore, phytotoxicity is more likely associated with processes occurring at the soil-root interface, where localized microbial Fe(III) reduction, Fe2+ formation, and microscale redox gradients develop, rather than with the chemistry of the bulk soil solution alone (Colombo et al., 2014; Li et al., 2023).

The different responses of the tested species agree with previous reports on plant tolerance to iron excess. Dicotyledonous species, particularly from the Brassicaceae family, are generally more sensitive during early developmental stages, when root tissues are directly exposed to elevated Fe2⁺ concentrations. In contrast, T. aestivum shows higher tolerance, likely due to more efficient regulation of ion homeostasis and oxidative stress (Kabir et al., 2016; Sági-Kazár et al., 2021; Zahra et al., 2021; Harish et al., 2023).

In the present laboratory-based study, the observed growth inhibition (18–22%) coincided with the period of most intensive microbial iron mobilization (days 8–21), suggesting that phytotoxic effects are linked to the transient increase in bioavailable Fe2⁺ rather than total iron content. This supports the concept of a temporary defined “window of phytotoxicity,” during which elevated Fe2⁺ availability induces sub-lethal stress in sensitive plant species.

Notably, the degree of growth inhibition did not correspond to the amount of iron accumulated in plant tissues. Iron accumulation in plant tissues did not reflect the degree of phytotoxicity. L. sativum and R. sativus showed the strongest growth inhibition, although Fe concentrations in their shoots were lower than in S. alba. Conversely, wheat accumulated large amounts of Fe in roots without detectable inhibition of root or shoot growth. These results indicate that tissue Fe concentration alone is not a reliable indicator of PBR phytotoxicity. Plant tolerance therefore depends not only on the amount of accumulated iron, but also on its distribution within the plant and the efficiency of iron homeostasis. Early phytotoxic effects therefore appear to be more closely related to Fe2⁺ availability in the rhizosphere than to the final Fe concentration measured in plant tissues.

Conclusions

The fate of PBR in chernozem soil was investigated through a controlled laboratory experiment simulating waterlogging, a key process driving the transformation of iron compounds in soil iron cycling pathways. PBR consists of iron-containing particles, including metal fragments and spherical particles of < 100 μm. Iron fragments have a metallic core and a rusty coating composed of highly coercive hydroxides such as hematite and goethite. O2 deficiency and reducing conditions decompose the rusty coating and expose the iron core to chemical oxidation. This process is facilitated by activated soil microbial metabolism, which promotes the rapid incorporation (mobilization) of oxidized insoluble iron compounds into biogeochemical cycles. Spherical particles of PBR, containing coarse-grained MD magnetite, exhibit a characteristic coercivity range of Bh (47–55 mT) and remain stable under the soil fermentation condition; their magnetic properties become predominant only after active microbial treatment or long-term waterlogging exposure. These findings indicate that waterlogging selectively dissolves pedogenic magnetite and oxidized iron fragments, whereas PBR-derived MD magnetite spherules remain highly resistant. These processes cause a progressive shift in magnetic properties toward dominance by stable, explosion-derived magnetic particles.

The phytotoxic effects observed in this study are most likely associated with the microbial mobilization of iron into bioavailable forms, particularly Fe(II), under oxygen-limited (low potential) conditions during soil incubation. This microbial-driven increase in soluble iron induces moderate, species-dependent phytotoxicity in chernozem soils, primarily affecting early seedling growth rather than germination.

The results of the present study provide laboratory-scale evidence for the importance of considering microbial metabolic activity, soil redox conditions, and plant species-specific responses when assessing ecological risks in war-affected agricultural systems. However, since the extent and persistence of these processes remain uncertain, field-based investigations are necessary to validate the observed transformations and determine their long-term environmental significance for soil and ecosystem health.
