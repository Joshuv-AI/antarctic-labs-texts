Mechanism and kinetics study of MG1113, an anti–tissue factor pathway inhibitor antibody promoting hemostasis in hemophilia
Authors: Heechun Kwak, Hyun-Ja Nam, Sung Ho Hwang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTargeting tissue factor pathway inhibitor (TFPI) is an emerging hemostatic rebalancing strategy for hemophilia. MG1113 is a humanized monoclonal antibody designed to specifically bind the Kunitz domain (KD)2 of TFPI.ObjectivesThis study elucidated the detailed pharmacologic mechanism of action and the preclinical hemostatic efficacy of MG1113.MethodsIn vivo efficacy was evaluated using an acquired hemophilia A (HA) cynomolgus monkey model. In vitro chromogenic assays, factor (F)Xa generation, and clot waveform analysis were performed in human factor–deficient plasmas to assess the dynamics of coagulation. Additionally, a cell-based system using EA.hy926 endothelial cells was established to examine the dual neutralization of TFPI isoforms.ResultsIn acquired HA monkeys, a single dose of MG1113 (3 mg/kg) reduced free TFPI levels by 98.8%, shortened the modified prothrombin time and restored thrombin peak height to ∼78% of normal levels. Mechanistically, MG1113 successfully prevented TFPI-mediated inhibition of the extrinsic tenase complex—specifically in the presence of FXa—despite targeting only KD2. MG1113 restored the FXa amplification loop and simultaneously neutralized both plasma-derived TFPI-α and cell-surface TFPI-β. However, despite sufficiently restoring FXa generation, clot waveform analysis revealed that MG1113 exhibited a broader, less intense, and more prolonged coagulation profile compared with the rapid burst characteristic of factor replacement therapy.ConclusionMG1113 effectively restores hemostasis by blocking the TFPI/FXa interaction. By systematically lowering the threshold for tissue factor–mediated initiation, MG1113 facilitates physiological restoration of hemostasis. This mechanistic profile—combined with gradual clot kinetics and the advantages of a monoclonal antibody—makes MG1113 suited for routine prophylaxis.

Introduction

Hemophilia is a congenital bleeding disorder characterized by deficiencies in specific coagulation factors—factor (F)VIII in hemophilia A (HA) and FIX in hemophilia B (HB) [1,2]. These deficiencies disrupt the intrinsic tenase complex (FVIIIa/FIXa), resulting in impaired thrombin generation and a failure to maintain adequate hemostasis. While factor replacement therapy remains a primary treatment modality, it is limited by the treatment burden of frequent intravenous infusions and the development of neutralizing antibodies (NAbs; inhibitors) against FVIII or FIX. Patients who develop inhibitors require bypassing agents, such as activated prothrombin complex concentrates or recombinant activated FVII, which often exhibit shorter half-lives and less consistent clinical responses compared with factor replacement [3].

The therapeutic landscape for hemophilia with inhibitors has evolved with the introduction of nonfactor therapies [2]. Emicizumab, a bispecific antibody mimicking activated FVIII function, has established a new paradigm by providing continuous hemostatic protection regardless of inhibitor status and reducing the treatment burden through subcutaneous administration [4]. The hemostatic rebalancing approach—targeting natural anticoagulants to enhance procoagulant potential—has emerged as another strategy. This includes small-interfering RNA targeting antithrombin and monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) targeting the tissue factor pathway inhibitor (TFPI), such as concizumab and marstacimab, which are currently in late-stage clinical development or have recently received regulatory approval [2,[5], [6], [7]].

TFPI exists in 2 major isoforms, TFPI-α and TFPI-β, which are distributed differently throughout the vascular system [8,9]. TFPI-α is bound to the endothelial cell surface, circulates as a soluble form in plasma, and is stored within platelets for release upon activation. In contrast, TFPI-β is a glycosylphosphatidylinositol-anchored protein tethered to the surface of endothelial cells. Structurally, TFPI-α contains 3 Kunitz-type domains (KD; KD1, KD2, and KD3) and a basic C-terminal region, while TFPI-β lacks the KD3 and the C-terminal tail [8,9]. The KD2 is specifically responsible for binding and inhibiting activated FX (FXa), whereas the KD1 inhibits the FVIIa/tissue factor (TF) complex, extrinsic tenase [8,10]. Furthermore, the KD3 of TFPI-α specifically interacts with protein S (PS), which acts as a cofactor to markedly enhance the inhibition of FXa by KD2 [11]. The inhibition of FVIIa/TF complex by KD1 is significantly amplified upon the formation of a quaternary complex (FVIIa/TF/FXa/TFPI). Within this assembly, TFPI achieves optimal inhibition by simultaneously occupying the active sites of both FXa and FVIIa via KD2 and KD1, respectively [9,12].

MG1113 is a humanized mAb designed to specifically bind the KD2 of TFPI [13]. By blocking the interaction between TFPI and FXa, MG1113 prevents the formation of the inhibitory quaternary complex (TF/FVIIa/FXa/TFPI), thereby maintaining the activity of both FXa and the TF/FVIIa/FXa complex. Previous studies in plasma-based systems and HA-induced rabbits have suggested that neutralizing TFPI can enhance thrombin generation in FVIII- or FIX-deficient environments [13]. However, the precise molecular mechanisms by which MG1113 modulates the extrinsic pathway, especially in the presence of various TFPI isoforms and cofactors such as PS, require further investigation in more physiologically relevant models. Determining whether MG1113 can effectively counteract this cofactor-enhanced inhibition is crucial for defining its therapeutic profile.

In this study, we investigated the hemostatic efficacy and the detailed mechanism of action of MG1113 using a nonhuman primate (NHP) model and various in vitro systems. An acquired HA model was established in cynomolgus monkeys by administering anti-FVIII NAbs to mimic the clinical phenotype of patients with inhibitors. Using this NHP model, we assessed the in vivo efficacy of MG1113 on plasma clotting parameters, thrombin generation, and target neutralization. Furthermore, we conducted mechanistic assays to evaluate how MG1113 influences the extrinsic tenase complex and the FXa-mediated feedback loop, as well as its ability to neutralize both TFPI-α and TFPI-β. Finally, we used clot waveform analysis (CWA) to compare the kinetics of clot formation induced by MG1113 with those of factor replacement therapy. These investigations aim to provide a comprehensive understanding of the mechanistic characteristics of TFPI-targeted therapy in hemophilia.

Methods

Efficacy study in acquired HA monkey model

Study design and plasma preparation

The blood sampling and administration schedules for the efficacy study in cynomolgus monkeys are illustrated in Supplementary Figure 1. The animal study was conducted at Genia (Gyeonggi-do, South Korea). All animal experiments complied with the Animal Research: Reporting of In Vivo Experiments (ARRIVE) guidelines, and the study protocol was approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of ORIENT (Approval Nos. ORIENT-IACUC-18013 and 18027). Male cynomolgus monkeys (40-44 months old; 2.7-3.2 kg) were used for the study.

To obtain baseline values, blood samples were collected from normal monkeys immediately prior to the administration of sheep anti-human FVIII antibody (100,000 BU/head; PROLYTIX). Blood was drawn into sodium citrate tubes (BD) and centrifuged at 2000 × g for 15 minutes at 4 °C to obtain plasma. One hour after the antibody administration, blood samples were collected to confirm the inhibitor effect. Immediately following this sampling, vehicle (saline) or MG1113 (3 mg/kg) was administered intravenously. A final blood sample was collected 2 hours posttreatment. All plasma samples were stored at −75 ± 5 °C until analysis. Thrombin generation assay (TGA) and free TFPI ELISA were performed as previously described [13,14].

Activated partial thromboplastin time

The activated partial thromboplastin time (aPTT) was measured using a STart 4 hemostasis analyzer (Stago) prewarmed to 37 °C. A steel ball was dispensed into each cuvette chamber, followed by 50 μL of Actin FSL (Siemens). After installing the cuvette into the analyzer, it was incubated at 37 °C for 60 seconds. Plasma (50 μL) was then added to each chamber and incubated for 3 minutes at 37 °C. Coagulation was initiated by the addition of 50 μL of 25 mM CaCl2 (Siemens) prewarmed to 37 °C, and the clotting time was recorded.

Modified prothrombin time

Standard prothrombin time assays use high TF concentrations that bypass the intrinsic pathway, making them insensitive to clotting factor deficiencies in hemophilic plasma. In this study, we used a modified prothrombin time (mPT) assay with highly diluted TF to limit the initial trigger. This approach intentionally prolongs the baseline clotting time, allowing for the amplification of the coagulation cascade where the intrinsic pathway’s contribution becomes relevant. This provides a more sensitive detection of the procoagulant effect of anti-TFPI agents in FVIII-deficient conditions [13,[15], [16], [17]]. The mPT assay was performed using a STart 4 hemostasis analyzer prewarmed to 37 °C. Thromborel S (Siemens) was diluted 1:50,000 with 25 mM CaCl2 (Siemens) and kept cold prior to use. A steel ball was placed into each cuvette chamber, and 50 μL of plasma was added. The cuvette was installed in the analyzer and incubated at 37 °C for 60 seconds. Clotting was initiated by adding 100 μL of the diluted Thromborel S to each chamber, and the clotting time was measured.

TFPI neutralization assay in the extrinsic tenase complex

The ability of MG1113 to neutralize TFPI inhibition within the extrinsic tenase complex was evaluated under 4 different conditions involving varying combinations of coagulation factors as follows: (i) TF/FVIIa, (ii) TF/FVIIa+PS, (iii) TF/FVIIa/FXa, and (iv) TF/FVIIa/FXa+PS.

Assays were performed in 96-well plates (Corning) using an assay buffer composed of 25 mM HEPES, 175 mM NaCl, 5 mg/mL bovine serum albumin (BSA), and 3 mM CaCl2 (pH 7.5), and the total assay volume was 200 μL/well. Recombinant human (rh)FVIIa (Novo Nordisk), rhFXa (Enzyme Research Laboratories), rhTFPI (Creative Biomart), rhPS (Enzyme Research Laboratories), Dade Innovin (source of TF; Siemens), rivaroxaban (Santa Cruz Biotechnology), and SPECTROZYME FVIIa (Sekisui Diagnostics) were diluted in the assay buffer. In general, MG1113, anti-TFPI KD1 antibody (positive control; Sekisui Diagnostics), or human immunoglobulin (hIg)G (isotype control; Thermo Fisher Scientific) were serially diluted and incubated with rhTFPI for 15 minutes at 37 °C. Concurrently, an enzyme complex mixture containing rhFVIIa, Dade Innovin, and appropriate factors (rhFXa and/or rhPS) was added to the antibody-TFPI mixture and incubated for 15 minutes at 37 °C. For assays containing rhFXa, rivaroxaban was added to a final concentration of 10 μg/mL prior to substrate addition to inhibit FXa activity. The reaction was initiated by adding the chromogenic substrate SPECTROZYME FVIIa to a final concentration of 500 μM. Absorbance was measured at 405 nm (reference at 650 nm) using a microplate reader (VersaMax; Molecular devices) after incubation at 37 °C. Neutralization of TFPI-mediated inhibition of FVII activation

The assay was performed in 96-well plates with a final reaction volume of 200 μL. All reagents were diluted in an assay buffer consisting of 20 mM HEPES, 150 mM NaCl, 1 mg/mL BSA, and 5 mM CaCl2 (pH 7.4). Unless otherwise noted, the concentrations described further represent the final concentrations in the assay.

First, rhTFPI (50 nM) was preincubated with varying concentrations of MG1113 or hIgG (6.25-200 nM) for 15 minutes at 37 °C. To initiate the reaction, FVII (50 nM) and FXa (10 nM) were added to the mixture and incubated for 2 hours at 37 °C. Following this incubation, rivaroxaban (10 μg/mL) was added to inhibit FXa activity and prevent interference with the substrate signal. The chromogenic substrate SPECTROZYME FVIIa was then added to a concentration of 500 μM, and the mixture was incubated for an additional 2 hours at 37 °C. Absorbance was measured at 405 nm using a microplate reader, with the reference absorbance measured at 650 nm subtracted from the readings. The IC50 value of MG1113 was calculated through 4-parameter fitting.

Neutralization of TFPI-mediated inhibition of prothrombinase activity

The assay was conducted in 96-well plates with a final reaction volume of 200 μL, excluding the stop buffer. All reagents were diluted in an assay buffer composed of 20 mM HEPES, 150 mM NaCl, 5 mM CaCl2, 1 mg/mL BSA, and 0.1% PEG-8000 (pH 7.4). Unless otherwise specified, the concentrations indicated further refer to the final concentrations in the assay.

First, rhTFPI (40 nM) was incubated with varying concentrations of MG1113 or hIgG (1.25-160 nM) for 30 minutes at 37 °C. Subsequently, rhFXa was added to a concentration of 10 mIU/mL, and the mixture was incubated for an additional 30 minutes at 37 °C. To initiate the reaction, a substrate mixture containing 100 μM phospholipid-TGT (Rossix), 20 mU/mL FVa (PROLYTIX), 150 nM FII (Enzyme Research Laboratories), and 500 μM S-2238 (Chromogenix) was added to the wells. After incubation for 15 minutes at 37 °C, the reaction was terminated by the addition of 50 μL of stop buffer (20% acetic acid) per well. The absorbance was measured at 405 nm using a microplate reader. The IC50 value of MG1113 was calculated through 4-parameter fitting.

Factor Xa generation and clotting turbidity assays

To reconstitute the experimental conditions, rhFVIII (Baxter) was serially diluted in FVIII-deficient plasma to concentrations ranging from 0.0039 to 16 IU/mL. For antibody treatment groups, MG1113 or control hIgG was diluted in FVIII-deficient plasma to concentrations of 0.039 to 160 nM; 70 μL of each plasma mixture were dispensed into a 96-well plate and incubated at room temperature for 20 minutes. Experiments involving FIX were performed using an identical protocol, substituting rhFIX (Pfizer) and FIX-deficient plasma for their FVIII counterparts.

Relipidated rhTF (Sekisui Diagnostics) was diluted to 5 ng/mL using 25 mM CaCl2 and stored at 4 °C until use. A trigger solution was prepared by mixing 12 μL of the diluted rhTF, 8,136 μL of prewarmed (37 °C) 25 mM CaCl2, and 252 μL of 10 mM S-2765 (Werfen). The reaction was initiated by adding 140 μL of this trigger solution to each well containing the plasma samples. The change in absorbance was monitored at 405 nm every minute for 80 minutes at 37 °C using a microplate reader.

For the clotting turbidity analysis, the assay was performed under identical conditions, with the exception that distilled water was used in place of the chromogenic substrate S-2765 in the trigger solution. Clot formation was monitored by measuring the increase in turbidity at 671 nm (recorded as absorbance). Cell-based ELISA for detection of cell-surface TFPI-β

To evaluate the cell-surface expression of TFPI-β, a cell-based ELISA was performed using EA.hy926 cells (CRL-2922; ATCC). The cells were seeded into 96-well culture plates at a density of 2 × 104 cells/well (200 μL/well) 1 day prior to the assay. On the day of the experiment, the culture medium was removed, and the cells were washed 3 times with 1× phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). For primary antibody binding, mouse IgG (Invitrogen), anti-TFPI KD1 antibody (ADG4903; Sekisui Diagnostics), and anti-TFPI KD3 antibody (LS-C195698; LifeSpan Biosciences) were serially diluted in 1× PBS to concentrations ranging from 0 to 100 nM. The diluted antibodies were added to the plates (100 μL/well) and incubated at 2 to 8 °C for 1 hour. After removing the primary antibodies, the cells were washed 3 times with 1× PBS. Subsequently, 100 μL of horseradish peroxidase–conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG (Novex), diluted 1:2000 in blocking buffer (1% BSA in 1× PBS), was added to each well. The plates were incubated at 2 to 8 °C for an additional 1 hour. Following washes, 100 μL of TMB microwell peroxidase substrate (SeraCare) was added to each well and incubated at room temperature for 30 minutes. The reaction was terminated by the addition of 100 μL/well of 1 N H2SO4. The absorbance was measured at 450 and 650 nm using a microplate reader. The final binding activity was determined by subtracting the background absorbance at 650 nm from the signal absorbance at 450 nm.

Cell-based TGA

EA.hy926 cells expressing cell-surface TFPI-β were seeded into 96-well culture plates at a density of 2 × 104 cells/well 1 day prior to the assay. On the day of the assay, the culture medium was removed, and the cells were washed twice with 1× PBS. Factor VIII–deficient plasma (6.5 mL) was mixed with 195 μL of the chromogenic substrate S-2238 (10 mM; Werfen). The plasma-substrate mixture (103 μL/well) was added to the washed EA.hy926 cells. Subsequently, 50 μL of MG1113 or hIgG, serially diluted to various concentrations (0.78-200 nM) in distilled water, was added to the wells. The plate was incubated at 37 °C for 15 minutes to allow antibody binding. Coagulation was initiated by the addition of 50 μL of prewarmed (37 °C) 60 mM CaCl2 solution to each well. Thrombin generation was monitored by measuring the absorbance at 405 nm every minute for 90 minutes at 37 °C using a microplate reader.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis and graph generation were performed using GraphPad Prism (version 10.6.1; GraphPad Software). All data were assumed to follow a normal distribution and were presented as the mean along with the corresponding SEM. The significance level (α) was set at 0.05.

Results

Establishment of an acquired HA monkey model and evaluation of the hemostatic efficacy of MG1113

To evaluate the in vivo efficacy of MG1113, an acquired HA monkey model was established by administering anti-FVIII NAbs to cynomolgus monkeys. The model phenotype was validated by analyzing plasma collected before and 1 hour after NAb administration (Supplementary Figure 1). At baseline, the mean aPTT, mPT, and thrombin peak height were 24.4 seconds, 189.7 seconds, and 237.5 nM, respectively (Figure 1A–C). Following NAb administration, the mean aPTT and mPT were significantly prolonged to 62.2 seconds (2.5-fold) and 287.7 seconds (1.5-fold), respectively (Figure 1A, B). Concurrently, the mean thrombin peak height significantly decreased to 68.3 nM, representing 28.8% of the normal level (Figure 1C). Subsequent treatment with either vehicle or 3 mg/kg MG1113 was performed 1 hour after NAb administration (Supplementary Figure 1). Plasma samples were collected 2 hours posttreatment to assess hemostatic changes. No significant difference in aPTT was observed between the vehicle- and MG1113-treated groups, indicating that the NAb-induced HA phenotype remained stable throughout the evaluation period (Figure 1D). In contrast, mPT was significantly shortened in the MG1113-treated group compared with that of the vehicle group (231.6 vs 303.6 seconds) (Figure 1E). Furthermore, the thrombin peak height significantly increased to 176.1 nM (vs 65.1 nM in vehicle), recovering to ∼74% of normal levels (Figure 1F). This functional recovery coincided with a marked reduction in free TFPI-α concentration to 1.2% of baseline (Figure 1G). These findings indicate that the observed shortening of mPT and the restoration of thrombin peak height were attributable to the TFPI-inhibitory function of MG1113.

Restoration of extrinsic tenase activity by MG1113 via inhibition of TFPI

The previous study demonstrated the efficacy of MG1113 in plasma from persons with hemophilia and in an acquired HA rabbit model, attributing the effect to the neutralization of TFPI’s KD2-mediated FXa inhibition [13]. Therefore, it is likely that the efficacy observed in the current acquired HA monkey model is similarly driven by this mechanism.

Expanding on these findings, we sought to investigate whether MG1113, which specifically targets KD2, could also neutralize TFPI's other key inhibitory function: the inhibition of the extrinsic tenase (TF/FVIIa complex) mediated by KD1. Consistent with previous reports [18], TFPI inhibited extrinsic tenase activity more potently in the presence of FXa and/or PS (Supplementary Figure 2).

We evaluated the effect of MG1113 under 4 conditions: TF/FVIIa, TF/FVIIa+PS, TF/FVIIa/FXa, and TF/FVIIa/FXa+PS. To distinguish extrinsic tenase activity, a FVIIa-specific substrate was used, and rivaroxaban was added to block nonspecific FXa activity. In the absence of FXa (TF/FVIIa and TF/FVIIa+PS), TFPI reduced the activity of extrinsic tenase by 32.5% and 19.1%, respectively (Figure 2A, B). MG1113 restored activity to 43.6% and 32.1% at the highest concentration, respectively, whereas the KD1-specific antibody restored activity to 75.9% and 48.3%, respectively. Conversely, in the presence of FXa (TF/FVIIa/FXa and TF/FVIIa/FXa+PS), TFPI reduced the activity of extrinsic tenase by 0.667% and 3.83%, respectively (Figure 2C, D). MG1113 significantly restored activity to 89.0% and 91.8%, respectively, at the highest concentration. In these conditions, the KD1-specific antibody showed negligible recovery (5.07% and 18.4%, respectively). Promotion of FXa amplification feedback loops

The restoration of extrinsic tenase activity by MG1113 is expected to enhance FX activation. The FXa can subsequently activate FVII and, in complex with FVa (prothrombinase), activate prothrombin (FII). We investigated whether MG1113 could neutralize TFPI inhibition in these feedback loops. MG1113 restored FVII activation by FXa in a concentration-dependent manner by neutralizing TFPI, and the IC50 value of MG1113 against 50 nM TFPI was measured to be ∼27.7 nM (R2 = 0.9813) (Figure 3A). Similarly, MG1113 concentration dependently restored prothrombin activation by the prothrombinase complex, and the IC50 value of MG1113 against 40 nM TFPI was measured to be ∼17.0 nM (R2 = 0.9835) (Figure 3B). MG1113 enhances FXa generation and induces clot formation in FVIII- and FIX-deficient plasmas

To verify FXa amplification, FXa generation was induced in FVIII- or FIX-deficient plasmas using TF and CaCl2 and monitored using an FXa-specific substrate. In FVIII-deficient plasma, both rhFVIII and MG1113 increased FXa generation in a concentration-dependent manner (Figure 4A–F; Supplementary Figure 3A–C). Notably, MG1113 treatment significantly shortened the time to peak (TTP), whereas rhFVIII did not affect TTP (Figure 4D). While the FXa peak height continued to increase with rhFVIII, it tended to saturate with MG1113 (Figure 4E). The total FXa generation (area under the curve [AUC]) with 10 nM MG1113 was comparable with that of 1 IU/mL rhFVIII (Figure 4F). Similar trends were observed in FIX-deficient plasma treated with rhFIX or MG1113 (Figure 4G–L; Supplementary Figure 3D–F); MG1113 distinctly shortened the TTP compared with rhFIX (Figure 4J). However, unlike in FVIII-deficient plasma, MG1113 induced a robust FXa peak height in FIX-deficient plasma (Figure 4K). Similarly, in FIX-deficient plasma, 2.5 nM MG1113 achieved a TTP comparable with that of 1 IU/mL rhFIX, with a peak height ∼19% of the control (Figure 5J–L). The waveform was again lower and broader, with a similar onset time to rhFIX, but the endpoint was later (Figure 5J). These results indicate that MG1113 induces plasma coagulation with a lower intensity than factor replacement therapy and takes longer to complete coagulation.

To further investigate whether this distinct profile is due to the low hemostatic ability of MG1113, we compared the CWA of MG1113 with those of varying concentrations of factor replacements. Notably, when rhFVIII or rhFIX concentrations were reduced to levels that produced peak heights similar to that of MG1113 (eg, ≤0.0156 IU/mL), the initiation of coagulation was substantially delayed. In contrast, MG1113 initiated clot formation significantly earlier despite having a similarly attenuated peak height (Supplementary Figure 4). These data suggest that MG1113 does not merely exhibit weak coagulation but rather possesses a distinct kinetic profile characterized by early initiation followed by a gradual and prolonged propagation phase.

Discussion

In this study, we demonstrated the hemostatic efficacy of MG1113 in an acquired HA monkey model. A single dose of MG1113 (3 mg/kg) shortened the mPT and restored thrombin peak height to ∼78% of normal levels (Figure 1). Notably, free TFPI levels were reduced by 98.8%, indicating that MG1113 effectively binds to and neutralizes TFPI. This marked reduction suggests high target specificity and confirms that the drug operates via its intended mechanism in vivo. These findings demonstrate that MG1113 effectively restores the extrinsic pathway, which is naturally inhibited by TFPI, thereby compensating for the functional defects of the intrinsic pathway.

The key mechanism underlying the efficacy of MG1113 involves blocking the interaction between TFPI and FXa. For TFPI to inhibit the extrinsic tenase complex, it must first bind to FXa to form a quaternary inhibitory complex (TF/FVIIa/FXa/TFPI) [8,12]. In this context, FXa acts as an essential bridge that allows TFPI to access and inhibit the extrinsic tenase. Our data showed that MG1113 failed to block TFPI inhibitory activity in the absence of FXa but effectively neutralized it when FXa was present. This confirms that MG1113 binds to the KD2 of TFPI, physically preventing its interaction with FXa and thereby blocking the pathway by which TFPI inhibits the extrinsic tenase. Furthermore, we confirmed that MG1113 counteracts the cofactor effect of PS, which typically enhances TFPI inhibition [11,18]. Collectively, these results provide mechanistic evidence that MG1113 promotes hemostasis by relieving the TFPI-mediated inhibition of the coagulation initiation phase.

There are 2 findings from the FXa generation assays. First, a difference in FXa production was observed between FVIII-deficient and FIX-deficient plasma treated with MG1113. Factor IX–deficient plasma exhibited higher peak heights and a larger AUC compared with FVIII-deficient plasma at the same MG1113 concentration (Figure 4). The distinct reactivity between FVIII- and FIX-deficient plasmas likely reflects their different roles in the intrinsic tenase complex, where FVIIIa is an essential cofactor, and FIXa is the catalytic enzyme. Given that FVIIIa amplifies FIXa activity by several orders of magnitude [20], FIX-deficient plasma—which retains normal FVIII levels—would be expected to exhibit a more robust FXa generation response. Additionally, variations in baseline TFPI concentrations among commercial deficient plasmas may also contribute to this discrepancy. While these results suggest that FXa is produced more rapidly and in greater quantities by MG1113 in FIX-deficient conditions, they do not necessarily imply superior clinical efficacy of MG1113 in persons with HB over those with HA. Indeed, clinical data for other anti-TFPI antibodies have not shown significant differences in efficacy between persons with HA and those with HB [6,7]. Next, a minor second peak appeared after the primary FXa generation peak (Figure 4; Supplementary Figure 3A–F). While the exact source of this second peak is not definitively clear from current data, it could represent additional FXa generated by a positive feedback loop or thrombin generated by prothrombinase. This is because S-2765, the substrate used in the FXa generation assay, is known to be a substrate specific to FXa, but it was confirmed to also show low reactivity with thrombin (Supplementary Figure 5).

Notably, the extent of FXa generation restoration did not fully correlate with the actual clotting kinetics. Although MG1113 enhanced FXa generation to levels exceeding those achieved by full factor replacement (1 IU/mL) (Figure 4), CWA revealed that the speed and strength of clot formation remained delayed or were weaker compared with factor replacement therapy (Figure 5). This indicates that while enhancing the extrinsic pathway can fully restore the initiation trigger, it cannot fully substitute for the rapid and robust propagation phase normally driven by the intrinsic tenase complex. This aligns with the in vivo results, where thrombin generation was not fully restored despite a 98.8% reduction in free TFPI (Figure 1), further demonstrating that while TFPI inhibition can induce sustained thrombin generation, it does not replicate the characteristic thrombin burst of the intrinsic pathway.

Consequently, the hemostatic profile of MG1113 is fundamentally distinct from that of factor replacement therapies. Importantly, our comparative CWA analyses (Supplementary Figure 4) directly counter the possibility that this profile merely reflects a low overall hemostatic potential of MG1113. When factor replacements were diluted to produce an attenuated peak equivalent to that of MG1113, they exhibited a substantially delayed onset of coagulation. In contrast, MG1113 maintained a rapid initiation of clot formation despite its lower peak velocity. This early onset aligns with its mechanism of action: rather than inducing an immediate, high-intensity coagulation burst suitable for on-demand treatment, neutralizing TFPI with MG1113 appears to systematically lower the threshold for TF-mediated coagulation initiation. This physiological restoration of the hemostatic balance, coupled with the clinical advantages inherent to mAbs—such as the feasibility of subcutaneous administration and a reduced risk of immunogenicity—and the broader, less intense clot kinetics observed in our CWA, provides a conceptual framework for maintaining steady basal hemostasis. Therefore, we propose that MG1113 is mechanistically and pharmacologically well-suited for routine prophylactic therapy rather than on-demand treatment for acute bleeding.

This study has several limitations. First, in the acquired hemophilia monkey model used in this study, although the administration of a high dose of anti-human FVIII antibody reduced thrombin peak height by ∼71%, this level biochemically corresponds to a moderate-to-severe hemophilic phenotype [21]. This incomplete neutralization is likely due to limitations in interspecies crossreactivity. However, despite this technical limitation, the model remains relevant pathophysiologically. Notably, the model demonstrated a significant prolongation of aPTT, consistent with established NHP models of hemophilia [22]. Furthermore, according to a recent study [23], peak thrombin levels of <49% compared with healthy individuals are closely associated with a severe clinical bleeding phenotype (annual bleeding rate ≥ 5). Therefore, despite the incomplete biochemical inhibition of FVIII, the thrombin generation profile and prolonged aPTT of our NHP model fall well within the range of a severe bleeding phenotype, confirming its suitability as a proper model for evaluating the hemostatic efficacy of MG1113.

Second, we used calcium and TF as initiators to induce coagulation in plasma-based assays (Figures 4 and 5). While this setup is optimized for evaluating the modulatory effects of MG1113 on the extrinsic pathway, it may not fully capture the activity of recombinant factors directly associated with the intrinsic pathway. Nevertheless, according to the cell-based model of coagulation, the extrinsic tenase complex is considered the primary physiological trigger for hemostasis [12]. Therefore, we believe that the comparative evaluation conducted in this study provides a reasonable reflection of physiological conditions.

Third, due to the use of pooled platelet-poor plasma, the potential influence of platelets on MG1113 activity could not be assessed. Platelets store TFPI-α (accounting for ∼8%-10% of total blood TFPI) and release it upon activation by stimuli such as thrombin [8]. Consequently, the absence of platelets in our experimental system might affect the overall evaluation of MG1113’s neutralizing capacity. Because of this difference between the in vitro assay and the in vivo environment, further studies using platelet-rich plasma or whole blood models are needed to better understand the comprehensive efficacy of MG1113.

Furthermore, it should be noted that this study represents a preclinical investigation primarily focused on elucidating the pharmacological mechanism of MG1113. Because our in vitro assays—including the comparative observations in HA and HB conditions—relied on commercially sourced deficient plasmas and specific animal models, they may not fully capture the complexity of human pathophysiology. Therefore, further investigations using actual patient-derived samples will be essential in future studies to validate these mechanistic findings and translate them into a broader clinical context.

In conclusion, our findings demonstrate that MG1113 effectively restores hemostasis by specifically neutralizing the TFPI-mediated inhibition of the extrinsic tenase complex. Based on these mechanistic characteristics and the clinical advantages inherent to mAbs, MG1113 appears more suitable as routine prophylactic therapy rather than an on-demand treatment for acute bleeding. Its profile suggests it can maintain a patient’s hemostatic baseline above a certain threshold, thereby potentially reducing the frequency of spontaneous bleeding. Future studies should focus on the safety of concomitant administration, specifically investigating potential interactions when additional coagulation factors or bypassing agents are required to treat breakthrough bleeding in patients already receiving MG1113 prophylaxis.
