The expansion of drug-coated balloon (DCB) technology has transformed the treatment of coronary artery disease beyond its original scope for in-stent restenosis (ISR). As the “leave-nothing-behind” strategy continues to gain traction, DCB is increasingly used in de novo lesions, small-vessel disease, bifurcations, and in patient populations in whom avoiding an additional metallic layer is particularly attractive. Yet despite a growing adoption, the interpretation of clinical outcomes following DCB treatment remains challenging in contemporary real-world practice, where patient complexity, procedural variability, and follow-up strategies may substantially influence observed results.1

In this issue of JACC: Asia, Lu et al2 provide important prospective multicenter data evaluating the AGENT paclitaxel-coated balloon in 500 predominantly Chinese patients treated across 9 centers in Taiwan, Hong Kong, and Singapore. The registry included both ISR and de novo coronary lesions in small vessels, the 2 major indications for DCB worldwide. At 12 months’ follow-up, major adverse cardiac events occurred in 13.9%, cardiac mortality in 1.6%, and myocardial infarction in 2.0% of the patients.2 Notably, there were no cases of acute/subacute vessel occlusion or safety signals during the 1-year follow-up.2

The importance of these data extends beyond the performance of a single device. East Asian populations frequently present with characteristics that are particularly relevant to DCB therapy, including smaller vessels, diffuse and multivessel coronary disease, and higher rates of diabetes. Indeed, 58% of patients in this registry had diabetes, and 62.6% had multivessel disease, creating a pragmatic and clinically relevant population in which to evaluate contemporary DCB performance. The study therefore fills an important regional evidence gap while providing insights that are likely applicable beyond East Asia.3,4

Several aspects of this registry deserve recognition. First, this study reflects a generally unselected all-comer cohort, in contrast to a narrowly defined trial population.2 Second, the included lesions were long (on average, >22 mm), substantially longer than those observed in randomized studies addressing the role of DCB in small vessels and ISR.2 Third, DCBs were applied as state-of-the-art, with a 1:1 balloon-to-vessel ratio, a prolonged inflation time, and a relatively low bailout stenting rate.2,5 Overall, these findings reinforce the growing body of evidence supporting the safety and feasibility of DCB-based strategies in routine clinical practice.

Yet the most thought-provoking observation from this study is not the overall event rate, but rather the remarkable divergence in outcomes according to follow-up strategy. Patients undergoing protocol-directed surveillance angiography experienced a substantially higher 12-month major adverse cardiovascular event rate than those managed without routine angiographic follow-up (26.9% [47 of 175] vs 4.9% [15 of 305], P < 0.001).2 This difference was driven almost entirely by repeat revascularizations rather than by hard clinical endpoints such as death or myocardial infarction.2 Such findings remind us that in coronary intervention, outcomes are not only determined by biology and device performance, they are also influenced by how actively we search for abnormalities and how we respond when they are detected. This is particularly true by acknowledging that in >80% of cases, target lesion revascularization was performed in a nonsignificant lesion by quantitative coronary angiography (specifically, >80% of lesions had a percent diameter stenosis of <70%), and the actual number of symptomatic patients is unknown.2 These findings raise an important question: when does angiographic restenosis represent true treatment failure?

Recent consensus recommendations from the Drug-Coated Balloon Academic Research Consortium have sought to address precisely this issue by emphasizing clinically or physiologically indicated revascularization rather than angiography-driven intervention alone.5 The findings reported by Lu et al2 provide real-world evidence supporting this evolving framework. They suggest that routine surveillance angiography may substantially influence event rates, particularly when intermediate angiographic abnormalities are interpreted without physiological confirmation or clear evidence of ischemia. This is notably true if the operators are not highly confident with this technology, because other studies have not shown a detrimental effect of surveillance angiography on clinical outcomes.6 We believe that angiographic surveillance in DCB studies should be reserved for randomized trials with mechanistic endpoints.

The registry also highlights the importance of adequate training for DCB angioplasty and paves the way for opportunities in future investigations. Technical success in this study was lower in de novo lesions (86%), and late lumen loss (LLL) was as high as 0.17 mm in this population, significantly higher than the one described in a registry conducted in Japan using the same device, which, despite a slightly distinct population, achieved a higher rate of technical success.2,7 This argument is not dissimilar if we compare the performance of Magic Touch sirolimus-DCB in 2 studies, both assessed with independent core laboratory analyses: in TRANSFORM I8 (TReAtmeNt of Small Coronary Vessels: MagicTouch Sirolimus Coated Balloon) LLL was 0.32 ± 0.47 mm, and 0.09 ± 0.34 mm in FASICO NATIVES (FAtebenefratelli SIrolimus COated [FASICO] NATIVES registry),9 depicting different experience and dedication to lesion preparation: despite a similar reference vessel diameter, acute lumen gain was 0.57 ± 0.37 in the former and 0.91 ± 0.33 mm in the latter study.

These findings reinforce the importance of meticulous lesion preparation, appropriate vessel sizing, and optimization of the final angiographic result. Increasing use of intravascular imaging may further improve procedural precision, particularly as DCB therapy expands into more complex lesion subsets. The ULTIMATE III (Intravascular Ultrasound Versus Angiography-Guided Drug-Coated Balloon) study showed how intravascular imaging in guiding DCB interventions was able to enhance lesion selection, improve procedural outcomes, and reduce LLL in patients with de novo coronary lesions.10

Taken together, the study by Lu et al2 provides important real-world data on the AGENT paclitaxel-coated balloon in East Asian patients and underscores a fundamental challenge in contemporary coronary interventions. These findings remind us that the apparent success or failure of a DCB strategy depends not solely on the technology deployed, but also on the framework through which outcomes are evaluated (Figure 1).
