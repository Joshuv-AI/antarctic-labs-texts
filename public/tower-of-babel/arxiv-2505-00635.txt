SOMA: a novel sampler for exchangeable variables
Yifei Xiong 1 , Nianqiao Phyllis Ju 1

Abstract
The problem of sampling exchangeable random variables arises in many Bayesian inference tasks, especially in data imputation given a privatized summary statistics. These permutation-invariant joint distributions often have dependency structures that make sampling challenging. Component-wise sampling strategies, such as Metropolis-within-Gibbs, can mix slowly because they consider only comparing a proposed point with one component at a time. In this work, we propose a novel Single-Offer-Multiple-Attempts (SOMA) sampler that is tailored to sampling permutation invariant distributions. The core intuition of SOMA is that a proposed point unsuitable to replace one component might still be a good candidate to replace some other component in the joint distribution. SOMA first makes a singer offer, and then simultaneously considers attempts to replace each component of the current state with the single offer, before making the final acceptance or rejection decision. We provide an acceptance lower bound of SOMA and, using a coupling method, derive the convergence rate upper bound of SOMA in the two-dimensional case. We validate theoretical findings with numerical simulations, including a demonstration on differentially private Bayesian linear regression.



1
Introduction

Exchangability and permutation invariance arise in various problems in statistics and machine learning, such as de Finetti’s theorem
(
Diaconis and Freedman, 1978
)
, distributional symmetry
(
Austern and Orbanz, 2022
)
, graphons for exchangeable graphs
(
Caron and Fox, 2017
;
Crane and Dempsey, 2018
)
, permutation-invariant neural networks
(
Lee et al., 2019
;
Bloem-Reddy et al., 2020
;
Kimura et al., 2024
)
.
The joint distribution of exchangeable random variables is invariant to permutations of the components.
An important motivating example of our work is data imputation given noisy observations
(
Ju et al., 2022
)
, especially when the noise process satisfies differential privacy
(
Dwork, 2006
)
.
When missing or latent data are treated as random variables, they can often be viewed as exchangeable, and sampling from their conditional distributions allows us to complete the dataset for inference.

While exchangeability has deep theoretical foundations, for example, de Finetti’s theorem, Aldous–Hoover representations, etc., there’s relatively less work on how to construct or sample from general exchangeable distributions in practice, especially beyond simple or tractable cases.

To sample from distributions with intractable normalizing constants, we typically resort to Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) methods.
The Gibbs sampler
(
Gelfand and Smith, 1990
)
is a popular MCMC algorithm to sample multivariate distributions.
It updates the components in turn by sampling from its distribution conditional on the current values of the remaining components in the vectors.
The Gibbs sampler is useful when the target distribution has tractable full conditional distributions when the joint distribution is not.
When the full conditionals are intractable,
one often uses the Metropolis-within-Gibbs sampler (MwG)
(
Gilks et al., 1995
)
, which keeps the one-component-at-a-time structure of Gibbs but uses Metropolis-Hastings moves to approximate the conditional updates.
A common Metropolis kernel for MwG has been the independent Metropolis-Hastings (IMH)
(
Tierney, 1994
;
Liu, 1996
;
Lee and Neal, 2018
)
sampler. We refer to using IMH inside of MwG as independent-Metropolis-within-Gibbs (IMwG).
While IMwG is suitable for sampling from multivariate distributions, it is not designed specifically to sample exchangeable variables.

To the best of our knowledge, there has not been a sampler exploits the symmetry of permutation invariant distributions in its design.
In this work, we propose a novel Single-Offer-Multiple-Attempts (SOMA) sample leverages permutation invariance.
Here is the core intuition of SOMA.
During component-wise updates of IMwG,
when a proposed point is unlikely to replace one component, it might still be likely to replace another component.
Simultaneously considering multiple proposals might speed up the convergence of the sampler without adding significant computation cost.
Unlike IMwG, SOMA transforms the IMH proposal into a state-dependent Metropolis kernel, and thus makes it more effective than conventional Gibbs style one-at-a-time updates.
Our experiments indicate that as the number of components increases or as posterior correlation grows, SOMA remains highly efficient in avoiding stagnation, and results in better exploration of the state space than IMwG.

We also analyze the convergence rate of SOMA using the coupling method, and establish geometric ergodicity of SOMA for dimension 2.
Theoretical analysis of the convergence rate of component-wise accept–reject-based Markov chains remains a challenging area
(
Brown and Jones, 2022
;
Qin and Jones, 2022
;
Qin et al., 2023
)
, specially for dimensions higher than 2.
Since our proof uses a coupling design, we believe that extending it to higher dimensions for a general permutation invariant distribution is challenging and would require stronger assumptions than what we currently use.
We use numerical experiments to compare SOMA with IMwG up to 100 components (dimension
\approx 400
).

The remainder of this paper is structured as follows: In Section
2
, we give examples of permutation invariant distributions and adapt MwG and IMH samplers to this setting.
We present the SOMA algorithm in Section
3
and analyze its acceptance probability and convergence rate in Section
4
. Experimental results are presented in Section
5
.

2
Problem formulation and challenges

2.1
Permutation invariant distributions

Consider the problem of drawing samples from a multivariate target distribution on
\mathbb{X}^{n}
with density
\pi(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
.
In this work, we focus on densities
\pi
exhibiting the following permutation invariance property.

Assumption 1

(Permutation invariance)
.

For any permutation
\sigma
of the group
\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
, we have

\pi(x_{\sigma(1)},x_{\sigma(2)},\ldots,x_{\sigma(n)})=\pi(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}).

In other words,
(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})\sim\pi
is an exchangeable sequence of random variables.

Exchangability arises in various contexts of statistics. We give three illustrative examples here.

Example 1

(Bernoulli-Laplace diffusion model
(
Diaconis and Shahshahani, 1987
)
)
.

Let
X_{i}\overset{iid}{\sim}\mathrm{Bernoulli}(p)
. Given the constraint
\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}=s
, the conditional distribution of
X\mid\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}=s
is permutation invariant, and the density is

\pi\left(x\mid\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}=s\right)=\frac{1}{|\{x:\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}=s\}|}\quad\text{for }\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}=s.

(1)

Example 2

(Data imputation given noisy summary statistic
(
Ju et al., 2022
)
)
.

Consider a confidential dataset
x=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})^{\top}
consisting of
n
data points. Suppose the data analysts can only observe a noisy summary statistic
s_{\mathrm{dp}}=s(x)+z
, where
s(x)
is a summary statistic, and
z
is the added noise. During data imputation, we can assume
x_{i}\overset{iid}{\sim}f(\cdot)
and the observation model is
s_{\mathrm{dp}}\sim\eta(\cdot\mid s(x))
.
The posterior density of
x
given
s_{\mathrm{dp}}
is thus given by

\pi(x\mid s_{\mathrm{dp}})\propto\eta(s_{\mathrm{dp}}\mid x)\prod_{i=1}^{n}f(x_{i}).

(2)

When the summary statistic
s(x)
is invariant to the ordering of the components (e.g., the mean, median, or covariance), the data augmentation posterior
\pi(x\mid s_{\mathrm{dp}})
is permutation invariant.

Example 3

(Bayesian PCA
(
Bishop, 1998
)
)
.

Consider a dataset
v=(v_{1},\ldots,v_{m})^{\top}
that each
v_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
follows a conditional Gaussian distribution
p(v_{i}\mid u_{i})=\mathcal{N}(\Theta u_{i}+\mu,\sigma^{2}I_{d})
, where
I_{d}
is the
d
-dimensional identity matrix,
\Theta=(\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{n})
is a
d\times n
matrix, and
u_{i}
is a
n
-dimensional latent variable with prior
\mathcal{N}(0,I_{n})
. Let
C=\sigma^{2}I_{d}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\theta_{i}\theta_{i}^{\top}
and
S
be the sample covariance matrix of
v
. Then the conditional density of
\Theta
given the other parameters is
p(\Theta\mid\mu,\sigma^{2},v)\propto\exp[-m(\ln|C|+\mathrm{Tr}(C^{-1}S))/2]
, which is invariant to permutation of the columns
\theta_{i}
.

2.2
Gibbs, Metropolis, and Metropolis-within-Gibbs

When direct sampling from the joint distribution is difficult, a Markov chain Monte Carlo strategy is the Gibbs sampler
(
Geman and Geman, 1984
;
Gelfand and Smith, 1990
)
.
Gibbs sampling is ubiquitous in Bayesian modeling and it is particularly useful when one can sample from the full conditional distributions

\pi_{i}(x_{i}\mid x_{-i})=\frac{\pi(x_{i},x_{-i})}{\int\pi(x_{i},x_{-i})\ \mathrm{d}x_{i}}\propto\pi(x_{i},x_{-i}).

(3)

Here
x_{-i}
includes all elements of
x
except
x_{i}
, i.e.,
x_{-i}=(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{i-1},x_{i+1},\ldots,x_{n}).

Note that permutation invariance allows us to write the conditional as
\pi(x_{i}\mid x_{-i})
instead of
\pi_{i}(x_{i}\mid x_{-i}).

Gibbs samplers entails iterative updates according to
x_{i}\sim\pi(\cdot\mid x_{-i}).

There are two main update rules for the Gibbs sampler
(
Andrieu, 2016
)
: systematic-scan (SysScan, also called deterministic-scan) and random-scan (RanScan). The former method sequentially updates all components once at a time with a fixed schedule
i=1,2,\ldots,n
, and the latter randomly selects a component
i
to perform the conditional update in each iteration.

In many practical scenarios (e.g., Example
2
), the full conditional distribution
\pi(x_{i}\mid x_{-i})
is intractable and hence direct sampling is difficult.
In that case, we must resort to approximated Gibbs samplers
(
Qin et al., 2023
)
such as the Metropolis-within-Gibbs sampler
(
Gilks et al., 1995
)
, which uses a Metropolis-Hastings kernel to approximate each condition distribution.

A significant drawback of general-purpose, off-the-shelf methods is that they do not attempt to exploit the symmetry of permutation invariant distributions.
One strategy to exploit permutation invariance is to first restrict sampling from the space of ordered sequences
\{x_{1}\leq x_{2}\leq x_{3}\leq\ldots\leq x_{n}\}
and then obtain samples from the full space by permuting indices in the ordered sequences.
As illustrated in
Figure

1
, it is unclear whether sampling from the constrained space is simpler.
At the same time, accept-reject type of MCMC sampling from such a constrained space is challenging; see
Lan and Kang, 2023
for a review on sampling from spaces with constraints like
c(x)=0
and
c(x)\geq 0
.

Figure 1
:
Comparison of a permutation invariant density in the full space
\mathbb{R}^{2}
(left) and in the constrained space
\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:x_{1}\leq x_{2}\}
(right).

Another implication of permutation invariance is that marginal distributions of
\pi
are identical.

Lemma 1

(Marginal equivalence)
.

If the density
\pi(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
is permutation invariant, then the marginal distributions
\pi(x_{i})
are identical for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
.

The inherent symmetry from permutation invariance suggests a certain homogeneity among the coordinates.
As a result, it is reasonable to use the same Metropolis kernel in each Gibbs step.
In particular,
we can use independent Metropolis-Hastings
(
Tierney, 1994
;
Liu, 1996
;
Lee and Neal, 2018
;
Wang, 2022
)
, making the entire algorithm IMH within Gibbs (IMwG).
In general, IMH uses proposal distributions that are independent of the current state.
In the context of IMwG, when updating the
i
-th component, IMH proposes a new state
y\sim q(\cdot)
from
\mathbb{X}
, independent of the current state
x_{i}
.
Replacing
x_{i}
by
y
with probability

\alpha^{\mathrm{IMwG}}_{i}(y,x_{i}\mid x_{-i})=\min\left(1,\frac{\pi(y\mid x_{-i})q(x_{i})}{\pi(x_{i}\mid x_{-i})q(y)}\right)=\min\left(1,\frac{\pi(y,x_{-i})q(x_{i})}{\pi(x_{i},x_{-i})q(y)}\right)

(4)

leaves the (conditional) target distribution
\pi(x_{i}\mid x_{-i})
invariant.
If accepted, then the new state
x^{\prime}=[x_{-i},y]
is the result of replacing the
i
-th component of
x
by
y
and keeping all other components.
We will compare our proposed method with IMwG, under both RanScan and SysScan schedules.
We refer to these two baselines as Ran-IMwG and Sys-IMwG, respectively.

3
SOMA: A Single-Offer-Multiple-Attempts sampler

We describe our novel Single-Offer-Multiple-Attempts (SOMA) sampler in this section.
SOMA is motivated by the following observation about IMwG. When a proposed value
y
to replace
x_{i}
is rejected, it can still be a favorable candidate to swap out a different component
x_{j}
.
In this sense, plain IMwG is not using the proposed samples efficiently.

Unlike the IMwG strategy of comparing
y\in\mathbb{X}
with one component
x_{i}
at a time,
SOMA simultaneously considers a single proposed state
y
as a candidate to replace multiple components.
With a sample
y
, SOMA first randomly choose a component
1\leq i\leq n
with probability proportional to

w_{i}(y,x)=\frac{\pi([x_{-i},y])}{q(y)\prod_{j=1,j\neq i}^{n}q(x_{j})},\quad\text{for }i=1,2,\ldots,n.

(5)

In other words, the probability of choosing the
i
-th component is
\mathbb{P}(I=i\mid y,x)=w_{i}(y,x)/\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}(y,x)\right)
, and we write the normalizing constant as
W(y,x)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}(y,x).
This completes the first stage of SOMA, which chooses an index
i
based on the current state
x\in\mathbb{X}^{n}
and a proposed component
y
.
In the second stage, SOMA attempts to swap out
x_{i}
and replace it by
y
with probability

\alpha_{i}^{\mathrm{SOMA}}(y,x)=\min\left\{1,\frac{W(y,x)}{W(y,x)+w_{0}(y,x)-w_{i}(y,x)}\right\},

(6)

where

w_{0}(y,x)=\frac{\pi(x)}{\prod_{j=1}^{n}q(x_{j})},

(7)

is defined analogously to (
5
).

Algorithm 1
.

SOMA sampler with target distribution
\pi
.

Input
: proposal kernel
q(\cdot)
, number of SOMA iteration
T

Initialization
: set initial value of
x=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})^{\top}
.

For
i=1
to
i=T

Stage 1:

Propose
y\sim q(\cdot)

Set weight
w_{0}=\frac{\pi(x)}{\prod_{j=1}^{n}q(x_{j})}
,
w_{i}=\frac{\pi([x_{-i},y])}{q(y)\prod_{j=1,j\neq i}^{n}q(x_{j})}
and
W=\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}

Select random index
I\in\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
with probability
\mathbb{P}(I=i)=\frac{w_{i}}{W}

Stage 2:

Replace
x_{I}
with
y
using probability
\alpha=\min\left\{1,\frac{W}{W+w_{0}-w_{I}}\right\}

Update state
x^{(t)}\leftarrow(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})^{\top}

Output:
SOMA samples
\{x^{(t)}\}_{t=1}^{T}

While IMwG exploits the symmetry of permutation invariant distributions by using the same independent proposal for each component, SOMA has pushed this idea further.
SOMA recognizes that an unfavorable proposal for
x_{j}
might be a favorable proposal for some other
x_{i}
and it seeks to find such an index
i
by comparing all the weights given in (
5
).

The advantage of SOMA lies in its efficient use of the ‘single offer’ from the proposal distribution
q
.
Stage 1 of SOMA chooses a favorable component
i
among the multiple choices to swap out
x_{i}
by
y
.
Then Stage 2 compares the current state
x
with the proposed state
x^{\prime}=[x_{-i},y]
.
The acceptance probability (
6
) accounts for the fact that
x^{\prime}
is selected from multiple attempts and is not a direct comparison like (
4
).
We summarize SOMA in Algorithm
1
and show that it is reversible with respect to the target
\pi
in Theorem
2
. The proof is deferred to Section
B.1
.

Theorem 2

(Reversibility)
.

Assume
\pi
is permutation invariant. Stage 1 of Algorithm
1
generates a proposal kernel, which we denote by
K^{\mathrm{SOMA}}(x^{\prime}\mid x)
.
Let
d(x,x^{\prime})
be the minimum number of components in which
x
and
x^{\prime}
differ, up to permutation of indices, i.e.,

d(x,x^{\prime})=\min_{\sigma\in S_{n}}\|x_{\sigma}-x^{\prime}\|_{0},

where we use
x_{\sigma}
to represent the vector obtained from permitting indices from
x
.
We must have
K^{\mathrm{SOMA}}(x^{\prime}\mid x)=0
for all
d(x,x^{\prime})>1
. When
d(x,x^{\prime})=1
, suppose they only differ on the
i
-th entry, i.e.,
x_{i}^{\prime}=y
and
x_{j}^{\prime}=x_{j}
for all
j\neq i
, then we must have

K^{\mathrm{SOMA}}(x^{\prime}\mid x)=q(y){w_{i}(y,x)}/{W(y,x)}.

Associating this proposal kernel with acceptance probability (
6
) in Stage 2 leads to a Markov transition kernel
P_{\mathrm{SOMA}}
that is reversible with respect to
\pi
.

Remark 1
.

From Theorem
2
, we see that SOMA transforms the independent proposal
q
into a proposal kernel
K^{\mathrm{SOMA}}
that is dependent on the current state. This distinguishes SOMA from IMH.
The proposal kernel
K^{\mathrm{SOMA}}
is also different in nature from common MCMC algorithms such as random walk Metropolis, Hamiltonian Monte Carlo, and Langevin Monte Carlo.
SOMA does not require gradient information or a step-size.

Remark 2
.

We should also distinguish the SOMA sampler from the Ran-IMwG and Sys-IMwG samplers.
To begin with, SOMA is not a Gibbs sampler - it is a Metropolis-Hastings type algorithm, consisting of a proposal step and an accept/reject step. Instead, Stage 1 of SOMA constructs a Metropolis kernel
K^{\mathrm{SOMA}}
by converting the IMH proposal into a dependent one.
Second, although SOMA and Ran-IMwG randomly pick an index
i
to perform a swap move, Ran-IMwG uses fixed weights independent of the current state, while SOMA uses weights (
5
) that adapt to the current state.
We can see SOMA as a multiproposal MCMC
(
Pozza and Zanella, 2025
)
method, and Ran-IMwG is the ‘single proposal’ counterpart that SOMA should compare with.
More discussions are in Section
A
.

Remark 3
.

Multiple-try Metropolis (MTM)
(
Liu et al., 2000
)
also entails multiple evaluations in one Metropolis iteration. In particular, the Multiple-try Metropolis Independent sampler (MTM-IS)
(
Yang and Liu, 2023
)
bears a resemblance to SOMA.
MTM-IS generates multiple samples
\{y_{1},\ldots,y_{n}\}
from an independent proposal
q
, selects one index from them, and generates ‘balancing trials’ to satisfy reversibility. On the other hand, SOMA only requires one sample
y
from
q
. The multiple attempts for SOMA are created by comparing
y
with each component in the current state
x
. SOMA would require much less computation and memory cost than MTM-IS.

4
Convergence of SOMA

We study the convergence characteristics of SOMA in this section, by focusing on its convergence rate and comparison with Ran-IMwG and Sys-IMwG.

Definition 3

(Geometric ergodicity)
.

A Markov chain with transition kernel
P(\cdot\mid\cdot)
invariant to
\pi
is geometric ergodic in total variation distance if the following inequality holds for some function
C
, constant
r\in(0,1)
and
\pi
-almost all
x
:

\|P^{t}(\cdot\mid x)-\pi(\cdot)\|_{\mathrm{TV}}\leq C(x)r^{t},

(8)

where the left-hand side is the total variation distance between the target distribution and the distribution of the Markov chain at iteration
t
when it started at
x
.

The constant
r\in(0,1)
gives the convergence rate: the smaller
r
is, the faster the convergence.

In Section
4.1
, we first examine the acceptance probabilities of the three algorithms, which play a crucial role in determining convergence efficiency.
In Section
4.2
, we derive an upper bound on the convergence rate. In Section
A.2
, we provide implementation strategies for SOMA and compare its computational cost with other methods.

4.1
Acceptance rate

For accept-reject-based Markov chains, a small acceptance probability is a clear signal for slow convergence
(
Brown and Jones, 2022
)
.
For component-wise Markov chains, slow convergence of the Metropolis kernels makes the overall Metropolis within Gibbs sampler mix slowly
(
Qin et al., 2023
)
.

First, we analyze the coordinate-wise acceptance probability of the three algorithms. This is the probability of accepting a proposed move conditioning on the proposal point as well as the chosen coordinate for a swap move.

Theorem 4

(Coordinate-wise acceptance)
.

Let
\alpha_{i}(y,x)
denote the acceptance probability of replacing the
i
-th component of
x\in\mathbb{X}^{n}
with
y\in\mathbb{X}
, given that index
i
has been selected for the update.
For the SOMA sampler and the IMwG samplers, the corresponding Metropolis acceptance probability satisfies

\alpha^{\mathrm{IMwG}}_{i}(y,x)=\min\left\{1,\frac{w_{i}(y,x)}{w_{0}(y,x)}\right\}\leq\alpha_{i}^{\mathrm{SOMA}}(y,x).

(9)

The complete proof is in Section
B.2
. Theorem
4
indicates that, if all three methods choose to update the same component
i
, SOMA always has an acceptance probability at least as high as the other IMwG methods.

Theorem 5
.

Let
P_{\mathrm{SOMA}}
and
P_{\mathrm{RAN}}
be the Markov transition kernels associated with SOMA and Ran-IMwG. We must have, for
\pi
-almost all
x,x^{\prime}
with
d(x,x^{\prime})=1
,

P_{\mathrm{SOMA}}(x^{\prime}\mid x)\leq nP_{\mathrm{RAN}}(x^{\prime}\mid x).

(10)

Suppose
x
and
x^{\prime}
differs on component
i
and with out loss of generality assume
i=1
. Then, starting from the right-hand side,

\displaystyle n\cdot P_{\mathrm{RAN}}(x^{\prime}\mid x)

\displaystyle=n\cdot\frac{1}{n}q(x^{\prime}_{1})\alpha_{1}^{\mathrm{IMwG}}(x^{\prime}_{1},x)=n\cdot\frac{1}{n}q(x^{\prime}_{1})\min\left(\frac{w_{1}}{w_{0}},1\right)

\displaystyle=q(x^{\prime}_{1})\cdot w_{1}\cdot\min\left(\frac{1}{w_{1}},\frac{1}{w_{0}}\right)

\displaystyle\geq q(x^{\prime}_{1})\cdot w_{1}\cdot\min\left(\frac{1}{w_{1}+\sum_{i=2}^{n}w_{i}},\frac{1}{w_{0}+\sum_{i=2}^{n}w_{i}}\right)

\displaystyle=q(x^{\prime}_{1})\cdot\frac{w_{1}}{w_{1}+\sum_{i=2}^{n}w_{i}}\min\left(1,\frac{w_{1}+\sum_{i=2}^{n}w_{i}}{w_{0}+\sum_{i=2}^{n}w_{i}}\right)=P_{\mathrm{SOMA}}(x^{\prime}\mid x).

∎
Theorem

5
indicates an ordering between the asymptotic variances of the samplers. We will discuss this more in Section
A.2
.
Theorem

5
can also be seen as a consequence of
Pozza and Zanella, 2025
, and we include this alternative proof in Section
A.1
.

Next, we analyze the overall acceptance probabilities.
Let
A(x)
be the probability of accepting a move from
x
.
For SOMA and Ran-IMwG, the coordinate
i
is chosen randomly and we have

\displaystyle A^{\mathrm{SOMA}}(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{X}}q(y)\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\frac{w_{i}(y,x)}{W(y,x)}\right)\cdot\alpha_{i}^{\mathrm{SOMA}}(y,x)\mathrm{d}y,

(11)

\displaystyle A^{\mathrm{RAN}}(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{X}}q(y)\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)\cdot\alpha_{i}^{\mathrm{IMwG}}(y,x)\mathrm{d}y.

(12)

(
11
) and (
12
) reiterate Remark
2
that Ran-IMwG uses fixed weights while SOMA adaptively decides which component to update.
Sys-IMwG has a predetermined update order, and we write the probability of accepting a move to change the
i
-th component as

A_{i}^{\mathrm{SYS}}(x)=\int_{\mathbb{X}}q(y)\alpha^{\mathrm{IMwG}}_{i}(y,x)\mathrm{d}y.

(13)

We establish in
Theorem

6
that, from each current state
x
, SOMA has a higher acceptance probability than RanScan.

Theorem 6

(Comparison of acceptance probabilities)
.

For any
x\in\mathbb{X}^{n}
, we have:

A^{\mathrm{SOMA}}(x)\geq A^{\mathrm{RAN}}(x).

(14)

Corollary 7
.

Let
P_{\mathrm{RAN}}
be the Markov transition kernels associated with Ran-IMwG, then for all
t
,
the total variation distance in (
8
) satisfied

\|P^{t}_{\mathrm{RAN}}(\cdot\mid x)-\pi(\cdot)\|_{\mathrm{TV}}\geq\left(1-A^{\mathrm{RAN}}(x)\right)^{t}\geq\left(1-A^{\mathrm{SOMA}}(x)\right)^{t}.

(15)

Corollary
7
follows
Theorem

6
and
Brown and Jones, 2022
. It suggests that if
A^{\mathrm{SOMA}}(x)\to 0
(e.g., as
n\to\infty
), then the lower bound on the convergence rates for both SOMA and Ran-IMwG approaches 1, indicating slow convergence. However, if
A^{\mathrm{SOMA}}(x)
remains bounded away from zero, Ran-IMwG may still suffer from poor convergence, while SOMA can potentially avoid this issue by maintaining a higher acceptance probability.

To establish lower bounds for the overall acceptance probability
A(x)
for general
n
,
we make the following assumption about the proposal kernel
q
and the target distribution
\pi
.

Assumption 2

(Bounded ratio of weights)
.

For all indices
1\leq i,j\leq n
, the ratio between the weights
w_{i}(y,x)
and
w_{j}(y,x)
is bounded, i.e., there exists a constant
M>1
such that

\frac{w_{i}(y,x)}{w_{j}(y,x)}\leq M\quad\forall\quad 1\leq i,j\leq n,x\in\mathbb{X}^{n},y\in\mathbb{X}.

(16)

In other words, the proposal distribution
q
and target distribution
\pi
satisfies

\frac{\pi(y,x_{-i})q(x_{i})}{\pi(y,x_{-j})q(x_{j})}\leq M\quad\forall\quad 1\leq i,j\leq n,x\in\mathbb{X}^{n},y\in\mathbb{X}.

(17)

A sufficient condition for Assumption

to hold is for the weights to be bounded, i.e., there exist constants
M_{2}>M_{1}>0
, such that

for all

Bounded weights have been a common assumption in Monte Carlo methods. For example, for single-component IMH updates (i.e.,

for all

is required to ensure geometric ergodicity
(

;
Wang, 2022
;
Yang and Liu, 2023
)
.

We give instances where Assumption

Definition 8

(
\epsilon
-
differential privacy
(
\epsilon

(

)
)
.

We say a randomized algorithm with conditional density function

satisfies if

(i.e.,

where the parameter

is called the

.

Let’s consider data imputation after observing summary statistics through an
\epsilon
-DP noise.
Revisiting Example

4.2
Upper bound of the convergence rate

In this section, we use coupling-based techniques to analyze the convergence rate of the algorithms.
Our proof leverages the coupling lemma. It entails constructing two chains on a common probability space such that they eventually meet and remain coupled.

Theorem 10

(Coupling inequality
(
Rosenthal, 1995
)
)
.

Let
\{(\Phi^{(t)},\tilde{\Phi}^{(t)})\}_{t=0}^{\infty}
be a pair of Markov chains, each marginally associated with an irreducible transition kernel
P
and stationary distribution
\pi
. Suppose that
(i). if
\Phi^{(t_{0})}=\tilde{\Phi}^{(t_{0})}
for some
t_{0}
, then
\Phi^{(t)}=\tilde{\Phi}^{(t)}
for all
t>t_{0}
; and
(ii).
\tilde{\Phi}^{(0)}\sim\pi
.
Then

\|P^{t}(\cdot\mid x)-\pi(\cdot)\|_{\mathrm{TV}}\leq\mathbb{P}(\tau>t),\quad\text{where meeting time }\tau=\min\{t:\ \Phi^{(t)}=\tilde{\Phi}^{(t)}\}.

(20)

To utilize (
20
), we must construct such a faithful coupling.
We create a coupling of SOMA in the two stages: (1) use a common proposed state
y
and select a common index
I
using a maximal coupling of Multinomial random variables
(

;
Jacob, 2020
)
; (2) accept and reject the proposed states with a common random number. We summarize the coupling kernel in Algorithm

.

The proof constructs a transition matrix of the between-chain distance

Remark 4
.

The analysis we provide in Theorem

has focused on

It is natural to ask whether Theorem

In fact, a qualitative comparison between multiple-component Gibbs samplers would also be challenging when

Appendix A
SOMA as a multi-proposal MCMC sampler

In Section
3
, we mention that SOMA can be viewed as a special case of multi-proposal MCMC methods. We provide a more detailed discussion here.

A.1
Alternative proof of Theorem
5

SOMA simultaneously considers
n
proposed states
[x_{-1},y]
,
[x_{-2},y],\ldots,[x_{-n},y]
, with (unnormalized) weights
w_{i}(y,x).

In this view, SOMA is indeed a multi-proposal MCMC method.
The Markov transition kernel of SOMA
P_{\mathrm{SOMA}}(\cdot\mid x)
is a special case of
P^{(K)}(\cdot\mid x)
of
Pozza and Zanella, 2025
with
K=n
.
SOMA has a special way of constructing the multiple proposals and a weight function to choose among the proposed states.

In SOMA, the ‘marginal proposal’ for each component
i
is in fact the IMH proposal
q(\cdot)
. Let

Q_{i}(x^{\prime}\mid x)=\begin{cases}q(x^{\prime}_{i})&\text{if }x^{\prime}_{j}=x_{j}\quad\forall j\not=i\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

denote the IMH proposal kernel for the
i
-th component, defined on the full state space
\mathbb{X}^{n}.

Then
\widetilde{Q}(\cdot\mid x)=\frac{1}{n}Q_{i}(\cdot\mid x)

is the overall proposal kernel for Ran-IMwG: it proposes an index
i
uniformly and proposes a new from
\mathbb{X}
.
We denote the Markov transition kernel associated with proposal
\widetilde{Q}
and
acceptance probability (
4
) by
\widetilde{P}.

It coincides with the Markov transition kernel of Ran-IMwG, denoted
P_{\mathrm{RAN}}(\cdot\mid x).

In other words, Ran-IMwG is the ‘single proposal’ kernel that we should compare SOMA with.
Pozza and Zanella, 2025
[Theorem 1] states that

P^{(n)}(A\setminus\{x\}\mid x)\leq n\widetilde{P}(A\setminus\{x\}\mid x)

for
\pi
-almost all
x
and for all measurable
A\subset\mathbb{X}
.
In the context of SOMA and Ran-IMwG, this is

P_{\mathrm{SOMA}}(A\setminus\{x\}\mid x)\leq nP_{\mathrm{RAN}}(A\setminus\{x\}\mid x),

which is equivalent to (
10
) in Theorem
5
.

A.2
Asymptotic variance of SOMA and computation cost

A consequence of Theorem
5
is that, a
serial
implementation of SOMA would not be competitive against Ran-IMwG in terms of statistical efficiency once we consider their computation costs.

We can measure efficiency with the asymptotic variance in a central limit theorem for an MCMC estimator
(
Jones, 2004
)
: given a (measurable) test function
h
and a kernel
P
invariant to
\pi
, our estimand is
\mathbb{E}_{\pi}(h(X))
and its variance is
\mathrm{Var}_{\pi}(h(X))<\infty
. Using MCMC samples drawn from
P
, we denote the asymptotic variance by
\mathrm{Var}(h,P)

(
Neal, 2004
)
.

Corollary 13
.

For any function
h
integrable with respect to
\pi
, we have

\mathrm{Var}(h,P_{\mathrm{RAN}})\leq n\mathrm{Var}(h,P_{\mathrm{SOMA}})+(n-1)\mathrm{Var}_{\pi}(h).

(26)

This result follows from Theorem
5
and
Andrieu et al., 2018
.
∎

Since RanScan involves only
{w_{i}}/{w_{0}}
for a specific
i
but SOMA requires
\{w_{j}\}_{0\leq j\leq n}
, the cost of SOMA could be
\mathcal{O}(n)
that of RanScan with a serial implementation. When
nT
iterations of RanScan has the same runtime with
T
iterations of SOMA, and these estimators have variances
(nT)^{-1}\mathrm{Var}(h,P_{\mathrm{RAN}})
and
T^{-1}\mathrm{Var}(h,P_{\mathrm{SOMA}})
respectively. Equation (
26
) suggests
nT
iterations of RanScan are asymptotically more efficient than
T
iterations of SOMA, implemented serially.

Luckily, computing the weights in parallel can reduce the runtime cost of SOMA.
Oftentimes, the target distribution
\pi
has the structure to make this parallel/vectorized implementation feasible.
SOMA can thus have a computational complexity comparable to Ran-IMwG and Sys-IMwG under moderate values of
n
, using a vectorized implementation. The costs would have the same order under a parallel implementation.
These allow one to harness the efficiency gained from SOMA’s design.
We give Example
5
, which continues with the perturbed summary statistics privacy mechanism in Examples
2
and
4
.

Example 5

(Record additive privacy mechanisms)
.

Privacy mechanisms that add data-independent noise to a query of the form
s(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}t(x_{i})
have densities

\eta(s_{\mathrm{dp}}\mid x)=g(s_{\mathrm{dp}},\sum_{i=1}^{n}t_{i}(x_{i}))
for tractable and known functions
g
and
t
.

Ju et al., 2022
coined the term ‘record additivity’ to describe how
\eta(s_{\mathrm{dp}}\mid x)
depends on an additive summary of
x
, and connects this property with implementation efficiency of data augmentation methods.
With the widely used Laplace mechanism
s_{\mathrm{dp}}\sim\mathrm{Laplace}(s(x),{\Delta}/{\epsilon})
and the weights
w_{i}=\eta(s_{\mathrm{dp}}\mid x_{-i},y)
in Example
4
, we should have

We can see that the proposal consists of two steps: proposing a new entry

Without loss of generality, let’s assume

To verify detailed balance, we should derive the ‘backward proposal kernel’
K(x\mid x^{\prime})
. In this proposal kernel, the current state is
x^{\prime}
, the proposed point is

, the index

is selected according to weights

defined in (

) & (

up to shuffling indices, in terms of having equal density under

\displaystyle w_{1}(x_{1},x^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi([x^{\prime}_{-1},x_{1}])}{q(x_{1})\prod_{j=2}^{n}q(x_{j}^{\prime})}=\frac{\pi(x)}{\prod_{j=1}^{n}q(x_{j})}=w_{0}(y,x),

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi([x^{\prime}_{-k},x_{1}])}{q(x_{1})q(y)\prod_{2\leq j\leq n,j\not=k}q(x^{\prime}_{j})}=\frac{\pi([x_{-k},y])}{q(y)\prod_{1\leq j\leq n,j\not=k}q(x_{j})}=w_{k}(y,x),\quad\text{for }k\geq 2.

Using these weights, the backward proposal kernel density is

(28)

Plugging (

Without loss of generality, assume

\displaystyle\quad P_{\mathrm{SOMA-coupled}}\left(d(x^{\prime},\tilde{x}^{\prime})=d(x,\tilde{x})-1\mid x,\tilde{x}\right)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{X}}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}^{c}}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{I}^{c}}\mathbb{P}(I=i,\tilde{I}=j\mid x,\tilde{x},y)\cdot\min(\alpha_{i},\tilde{\alpha}_{j};x,\tilde{x},y)q(y)\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle\geq\frac{n}{n+M-1}\cdot\mathbb{P}(I\in\mathcal{I}^{c},\tilde{I}\in\mathcal{I}^{c}\mid x,\tilde{x},y)

\displaystyle=\frac{n}{n+M-1}\cdot\min\left(\frac{\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}^{c}}w_{i}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}},\frac{\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}^{c}}\tilde{w_{i}}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\tilde{w}_{i}}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{n}{n+M-1}\cdot\frac{d(x,\tilde{x})}{d(x,\tilde{x})+M(n-d(x,\tilde{x}))}.

The first inequality uses Theorem
9
.
The second lower bound is achieved when

for all

and all

. We also note that the two index sets have cardinality
d(x,\tilde{x})
and
n-d(x,\tilde{x})
respectively.

Similarly, for the coupled RanScan-IMwG kernel, we have

P_{\mathrm{Ran-coupled}}\left(d(x^{\prime},\tilde{x}^{\prime})=d(x,\tilde{x})-1\mid x,\tilde{x}\right)\geq\inf_{i\in\mathcal{I}^{c}}\left[\alpha_{i}^{\mathrm{IMwG}}(x,y)\right]\cdot\mathbb{P}(I=\tilde{I}\in\mathcal{I}^{c})=\frac{1}{M}\cdot\frac{d(x,\tilde{x})}{n}.

Define
d_{t}=d(x^{(t)},\tilde{x}^{(t)})
as the number of differing components at time
t
. The sequence
\mathcal{D}:=\{d_{t}\}_{t=0}^{\infty}
forms a Markov chain with discrete state space
\{0,1,\dots,n\}
. The meeting time of the two chains is

\tau=\min\{t:d_{t}=0\}.

For the chain
\mathcal{D}
, let
P_{d}=(p_{i,j})_{n\times n}
denote its transition matrix, where
p_{i,j}:=\mathbb{P}(d_{t+1}=j\mid d_{t}=i)
. For our proposed coupling method in Algorithm 2, the matrix
P_{d}
for the case
n=2
is given by:

P_{d}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\
p_{10}&p_{11}&p_{12}\\
0&p_{21}&p_{22}\end{pmatrix}.

(49)

Our strategy is to find upper bound for
p_{12}
and lower bounds for
p_{10}
and
p_{21}
.These bounds lead to a kernel
Q_{d}
that stochastically dominate
P_{d}
:

Q_{d}:=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\
q_{10}\leq p_{10}&q_{11}&q_{12}\geq p_{12}\\
0&q_{21}\leq p_{21}&q_{22}\geq p_{22}\end{pmatrix}.

(50)

The absorbing time of
Q
is an upper bound on the absorbing time of
P
, which is the meeting time of our coupled Markov chains.

Convergence rate of SOMA.

Next, we find the lower bounds for
p_{10}
and
p_{21}
.
Based on Lemma
11
with
n=2
, we have

p_{10}\geq\frac{2}{2+M-1}\frac{1}{1+M}=\frac{2}{(M+1)^{2}},

and

p_{21}\geq\frac{2}{2+M-1}\frac{2}{2}=\frac{2}{M+1}.

For the upper bound of
p_{12}
, Lemma
15
gives
p_{12}\leq\frac{M(M-1)}{(M+1)^{2}}
.

We define the matrix
Q_{d}
with row sums equal to 1, representing the stochastic dominance of
P_{d}
:

Q_{d}:=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\
\frac{2}{(M+1)^{2}}&\frac{3M-1}{(M+1)^{2}}&\frac{M(M-1)}{(M+1)^{2}}\\
0&\frac{2}{M+1}&\frac{M-1}{M+1}\end{pmatrix}.

(51)

For
Q_{d}
, the largest eigenvalue is
\lambda_{1}=1
and the second largest eigenvalue is:

\lambda_{2}=\frac{M^{2}+3M-2+\sqrt{M^{4}+2M^{3}+9M^{2}-8M}}{2(1+M)^{2}}.

(52)

Thus, we have
\mathbb{P}(\tau>t)\leq C\lambda_{2}^{t}
, providing the upper bound for the convergence rate of the SOMA algorithm.

Convergence rate of RanScan-IMwG.
Let

P_{d}^{\mathrm{RAN}}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\
p_{10}^{\mathrm{RAN}}&p_{11}^{\mathrm{RAN}}&p_{12}^{\mathrm{RAN}}\\
0&p_{21}^{\mathrm{RAN}}&p_{22}^{\mathrm{RAN}}\end{pmatrix}.

(53)

denote its transition matrix.
Based on Lemma
11
with
n=2
, we have

p_{10}^{\mathrm{RAN}}\geq\frac{1}{2M},\quad p_{21}^{\mathrm{RAN}}\geq\frac{1}{M},

and

p_{12}^{\mathrm{RAN}}\leq\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{P}(\alpha<\xi<\tilde{\alpha}\ \mathrm{or}\ \tilde{\alpha}<\xi<\alpha)\leq\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\frac{1}{M}\right).

Thus, we define the corresponding stochastic dominance matrix as

Q_{d}^{\mathrm{RAN}}:=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\
\frac{1}{2M}&\frac{1}{2}&\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{2M}\\
0&\frac{1}{M}&1-\frac{1}{M}\end{pmatrix},

(54)

The second largest eigenvalue of
Q_{d}^{\mathrm{RAN}}
is then

\lambda_{2}^{\mathrm{RAN}}=\frac{3M-2+\sqrt{M^{2}+4M-4}}{4M}.

(55)

Convergence rate of SysScan-IMwG.
Since this algorithm updates components in a fixed order, the indices chosen for updating in the two coupled chains are always identical. For
n=2
, we view every two consecutive iterations as one overall update and denote the new time index by
h=2t
. Let

P_{h}^{\mathrm{SYS}}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\
p_{10}^{\mathrm{SYS}}&p_{11}^{\mathrm{SYS}}&p_{12}^{\mathrm{SYS}}\\
p_{20}^{\mathrm{SYS}}&p_{21}^{\mathrm{SYS}}&p_{22}^{\mathrm{SYS}}\end{pmatrix}

(56)

be the transition matrix for the chain
\{d_{h}\}_{h=0}^{\infty}
. In this case, we have the following inequalities:

\displaystyle p_{10}^{\mathrm{SYS}}

\displaystyle\geq\min\{\alpha,\tilde{\alpha}\}\min\{\alpha,\tilde{\alpha}\}\geq\frac{1}{M^{2}},

\displaystyle p_{20}^{\mathrm{SYS}}

\displaystyle\geq\min\{\alpha,\tilde{\alpha}\}\min\{\alpha,\tilde{\alpha}\}\geq\frac{1}{M^{2}},

and

\displaystyle p_{12}^{\mathrm{SYS}}\leq\min\{1-\alpha,1-\tilde{\alpha}\}\min\{1-\alpha,1-\tilde{\alpha}\}\geq\left(1-\frac{1}{M}\right)^{2},

\displaystyle p_{22}^{\mathrm{SYS}}\leq\min\{1-\alpha,1-\tilde{\alpha}\}\min\{1-\alpha,1-\tilde{\alpha}\}\geq\left(1-\frac{1}{M}\right)^{2}.

Accordingly, the stochastic dominance matrix is defined as

Q_{h}^{\mathrm{SYS}}:=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\
\frac{1}{M^{2}}&1-\frac{1}{M^{2}}-\left(1-\frac{1}{M}\right)^{2}&\left(1-\frac{1}{M}\right)^{2}\\
\frac{1}{M^{2}}&1-\frac{1}{M^{2}}-\left(1-\frac{1}{M}\right)^{2}&\left(1-\frac{1}{M}\right)^{2}\end{pmatrix}.

(57)

The second largest eigenvalue of
Q_{h}^{\mathrm{SYS}}
is

\lambda_{2}^{\mathrm{SYS}}=\frac{(M+1)(M-1)}{M^{2}}.

Since the updates are performed every two iterations, we have

\mathbb{P}(\tau>2t)\leq C\left(\lambda_{2}^{\mathrm{SYS}}\right)^{t}
,
or equivalently,

\mathbb{P}(\tau>t)\leq C\left(\sqrt{\lambda_{2}^{\mathrm{SYS}}}\right)^{t}
.
Thus, the convergence rate satisfies

r_{\mathrm{SYS}}\leq\frac{\sqrt{(M+1)(M-1)}}{M}.

(58)

This completes the proof.
