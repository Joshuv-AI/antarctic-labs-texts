Starch-modified date palm leaf fibers for eco-friendly epoxy biocomposites: microstructural, structural, and thermal characterization
Authors: Abbes Ouaar, Hamida Boussehel, Youcef Djebloun, Djamel Ghernaout, Ahmed Belaadi, Maroua Chettah, Herbert Mukalazi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Growing environmental concerns are driving the search for sustainable alternatives to synthetic materials. This study investigates the effect of a chemical-free, starch-based treatment on date palm leaf fibers (DPFs) for use in fully biodegradable epoxy biocomposites. The treatment consists of mechanical cleaning, water washing, and immersion in an aqueous starch solution, with no chemical reagents. SEM and AFM analyses confirm effective removal of surface waxes, lignin, and hemicellulose, along with significant fiber bundle fibrillation. Surface amplitude (Rq) decreased by 37%, from 2.59 nm to 2.39 nm. XRD analysis reveals an increase in the Crystallinity Index from 41.93% to 44.72%, with a crystal size of 4.33 nm. FTIR analysis further confirms purification of the cellulosic structure. TGA results show improved thermal stability of starch-treated fibers (STFs) between 250 °C and 350 °C, indicating reduced hemicellulose and cellulose degradation. These findings demonstrate that starch treatment is an effective, eco-friendly surface modification that enhances interfacial adhesion and supports the development of high-performance biodegradable biocomposites.

Introduction

The growing environmental problems caused by petroleum-based synthetic materials are forcing us to reconsider the materials we use. Lignocellulosic fibers from agricultural waste are now used as structural reinforcement in sustainable biocomposites1. Natural-source-based composites have become promising substitutes for synthetic petrochemical-based counterparts2. Natural-source-based composites have become promising substitutes for synthetic petrochemical-based counterparts, as recent studies demonstrate that hybrid natural fiber composites can achieve superior mechanical performance and energy absorption, even outperforming conventional carbon fiber systems in high-performance applications3.

So far, thermoplastic starch (TPS) and lignocellulosic fibers are the most common materials used to make such eco-friendly “green” materials. Low cost, abundance, and renewability are the factors that lead to the deployment of these two types of materials2.

Date palm leaf fibers (DPFs) are a promising renewable resource because they are widely available in dry and semi-dry regions where date farming is a major part of the economy4. DPFs, a sustainable and biodegradable resource, have attracted significant interest for their potential to reduce environmental impact across several key industries, including building and construction, automotive, and packaging5. Recent optimization studies have shown that alkaline treatment conditions, particularly NaOH concentration, significantly enhance the mechanical properties of DPFs, leading to improved tensile performance and confirming their suitability for engineering applications6. Alkali-treated bagasse fiber-reinforced epoxy and polyester composites show greater performance, with epoxy being stronger and polyester being more stable in heat and UV light. This makes them great for outdoor use that is good for the environment7. The DPFs from the trunk and leaf exhibit considerable mechanical strength and modulus, comparable to many natural fibers (NFs) and higher than those of Epoxy-glass fibers and many aluminum alloys8. Wide varieties of both thermoset and thermoplastic polymers have been modified by reinforcing with DPFs derived from leaves and trunks. DPFs represent a promising alternative to synthetic fibers in polymer composite industries for both advanced structural and semi structural applications8.

Prospective applications of polymer/DPFs include environmentally sustainable, lightweight, naturally sourced materials that provide excellent acoustic and thermal insulation and are cost-effective9. Applications of polymer/DPF composites include constructing air-conditioning tunnels, building projects, producing automotive components such as dashboard panels, and using them in the home furnishings sector9. The chemical composition and structure of DPF fibers may be influenced by climatic exposure, age, and degradation10. Water is the primary chemical constituent of a living date palm (DP) tree. However, the cell walls of dry DP cells primarily comprise cellulose (CL), hemicellulose (HCL), and lignin (LG), with lesser quantities of extractives, protein, starch, and inorganic substances10.

All published studies reported varied results regarding the lignocellulosic composition of different parts of palm trees, including CL, HCL, and LG, primarily due to soil variability and the climatic conditions under which DPs were cultivated (Alotaibi et al., 2019). Despite the benefits of NFs, they also have specific disadvantages, including inadequate water and fire resistance, weak fiber-matrix adhesion, and reduced durability, particularly in industrial applications. Consequently, several procedures, including mechanical and physicochemical pretreatments of raw fibers, have been proposed to modify or enhance their structural and surface properties. These procedures are often applied to composite materials11.

The hydrophilic nature of cellulosic materials, together with capillary action, leads to water absorption when samples are immersed, thereby increasing the dimensions of the composite. This causes fiber swelling, thereby increasing the composite’s thickness swelling. The fiber moisture content ranges from 5% to 15%. Moisture induces dimensional changes in composites, thereby affecting their mechanical properties. During the manufacture of thermoset-based composites, moisture leads to void formation and weakens fiber-matrix adhesion, thereby diminishing mechanical properties12. Interfacial adhesion of NFs as reinforcement in polymeric composites is a critical factor in composite design13.

In all the studies described above, palm fibers undergo chemical treatment before being used as reinforcement in polymers to ensure optimal adhesion between the fibers and the polymer matrix14. This chemical treatment makes the final product prohibitively expensive, largely because fiber production is limited and only specific tree portions are used for fiber, which together hinder the material’s commercialization14. Researchers15 extended this work to NF epoxy composites, including jute, hemp, flax, and their mixtures, after extended water-immersion aging. They observed significant changes in dynamic mechanical properties, with storage and loss moduli increasing by as much as 763% and 590%, respectively, after aging. These changes correlated tribologically with higher friction coefficients and increased wear rates, indicating severe deterioration in the mechanical performance of NF composites due to water absorption15. Scientists16 employed statistical modeling to quantify the correlations among fiber content, contact temperature, and wear performance. They reported that fiber reinforcement mitigates frictional heat by improving thermal dissipation; however, excessive loading increases wear due to greater surface interaction.

Recent investigations indicate that the inclusion of seashell powder as a filler in flax–epoxy composites markedly improve their mechanical properties, with maximal enhancements noted at moderate filler concentrations. This addition strengthens the fiber–matrix bond, reduces water absorption, and maintains thermal stability, demonstrating the potential of such sustainable composites in high-performance engineering applications17,18.

Starch is a natural biopolymer derived from traditional botanical sources such as potatoes, rice, and corn, as well as from unconventional sources like the roots, skins, and pits of vegetables and fruits, many of which are typically discarded. Therefore, starch is abundant and can add value to what is commonly considered “waste”19. The hydroxyl groups of starch can interact with the functional groups of NFs. Starch, a primary storage polysaccharide in plants, has been identified as a potential substitute for non-biodegradable synthetic thermoplastics and their composites. PVA biofilms reinforced with agricultural waste (BHP) show improved mechanical properties, good interfacial bonding, and enhanced biodegradability, demonstrating strong potential as sustainable packaging materials (Senthil Maharaj20.

Advances in TPS-based materials offer a superior alternative to reduce or eliminate reliance on petroleum-derived polymers. The advantages of TPS include its abundance, renewability, biodegradability, biocompatibility, and low cost compared with other thermoplastic biopolymers21. PLA biofilms fortified with Sechium edule peel powder exhibit superior mechanical qualities, consistent structure, and better biodegradability, indicating significant promise for sustainable packaging and associated applications22.

This research uses Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FTIR), X-ray Diffraction (XRD), Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM), and Atomic Force Microscopy (AFM) to examine temporal changes in the microstructure of DPFs. The study compares untreated fibers (UTFs) with fibers integrated into starch-based systems. It also examines environmentally sustainable techniques for altering fibers without chemical interventions. This work aims to transform a substantial amount of agricultural waste into viable technical products, thereby fostering economic development and facilitating future growth. This objective is achieved by enhancing the compatibility and adhesion between DPFs and starch, thereby improving the biocomposite’s cohesion and strength, as well as its moisture resistance. In this context, the combined microstructural, structural, and thermal characterization adopted in this work is directly linked to composite design requirements. Microstructural analyses (SEM/AFM) clarify how surface roughness, fibrillation, and impurity removal can promote fibre–matrix bonding during composite manufacturing, whereas structural (XRD/FTIR) and thermal (TGA) measurements provide insight into stiffness, moisture sensitivity, and processing stability of the fibres within epoxy biocomposites.

The characterization strategy adopted in this study was deliberately designed as a multi-scale, complementary approach. Microstructural analysis (SEM and AFM) was conducted to assess surface morphology, fibrillation, and the removal of non-cellulosic impurities factors that directly govern interfacial bonding quality during composite manufacturing. Crystallographic analysis (XRD and FTIR) was performed to quantify changes in cellulose crystallinity, as a higher Crystallinity Index is associated with improved stiffness, reduced moisture absorption, and stronger hydrogen bonding. Thermal analysis (TGA) was included to evaluate fiber stability during epoxy curing, where premature degradation would compromise the final mechanical performance of the bio composite.

Experimental procedures

Materials

The date palm leaf fibers (DPFs) used in this study were collected from the Biskra region in southeastern Algeria, which is renowned for large-scale date palm cultivation. The diameter of the fiber bundles ranged from 0.2 mm to 0.5 mm. Initially, the raw DPFs were thoroughly cleaned to remove dust and other surface contaminants, then rinsed with cold distilled water. Subsequently, the fibers were immersed in a suspension containing 100 g of starch and 1 L of distilled water (DW) at room temperature for 30 min and manually mixed using a silicone spatula to ensure uniform coating. The starch-treated fibers (STFs) were then rinsed with DW to remove excess, non-adhered starch and dried in a forced-air oven (Nabertherm, Germany) at 80 °C for 24 h to ensure complete moisture removal. Both STFs and untreated fibers (UTFs) were used for the subsequent analyses.

Property characterization

Atomic force microscopy (AFM)

AFM measurements were performed with a tiny tip affixed to a cantilever, positioned near the surfaces of both untreated and treated palm leaf fiber samples. Analyses were performed using an A100 AFM (A.P.E. Research, Nanotechnology, Italy). The goal was to delineate the nanoscale surface topography and roughness and to assess the effects of the chemical treatment.

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) analysis

A Thermo Scientific™ Prisma E system (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., USA) was used for SEM. This work aimed to reveal morphological changes resulting from starch treatment, particularly in impurities, surface roughness, and microstructural defects.

Thermal analysis

Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) of cellulosic fibers was performed using the SDT Q600 (TA Instruments, USA). The analysis was conducted in alumina crucibles under a nitrogen atmosphere, with a heating rate of 10 °C·min⁻¹ and a temperature range of 30 °C to 1000 °C. Sample weights ranged from 7 to 12 mg.

X-ray diffraction analysis (XRD)

Using Cu Kα radiation, a Rigaku MiniFlex 600 diffractometer (Rigaku Holdings Corporation, Japan) was used to obtain the XRD pattern of the fibers. The tube current was 15 mA, and the acceleration voltage was 40 kV. The instrument settings were a scan speed of 0.020°·min⁻¹ and a 2θ range of 5–60°.

Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy

The date palm leaf fibers, both treated and untreated, were examined by FTIR. Measurements were performed using an Agilent Cary 630 FTIR spectrophotometer (Agilent Technologies, Molecular Spectroscopy, Delaware, USA). The FTIR spectra of the materials were obtained over the range of 4000–400 cm⁻¹ at a resolution of 4 cm⁻¹. This technique enabled a comprehensive analysis of vibrational frequencies, thereby aiding in the identification of chemical alterations that occurred during the treatment.

Results

Atomic force microscopy (AFM) analysis

AFM examination revealed distinct morphological differences between UTFs and STFs. The UTF has a rougher surface, with a maximum height variation of approximately 0.87 μm (Fig. 4b). This uneven surface is generally associated with non-cellulosic extractives, including waxes, pectin (PC), and lignin (LG) (Farag et al., 2025).

Assessing surface roughness parameters provides a precise characterization of the material’s topography. The root-mean-square roughness (Rq = 2.592 nm) quantifies the total deviation of the surface from the ideally smooth plane. The average roughness (Ra = 2.188 nm) provides a general assessment of the mean elevation of surface imperfections. The maximum roughness height (Rz = 7.441 nm) is significantly higher, indicating considerable waviness and height variations. The skewness value (Rsk = −0.2341) quantifies the symmetry of the peaks and valleys on the surface, indicating a uniform profile. The kurtosis value (Rku = 2.551), which is higher than the normal value, quantifies the surface’s sharper, more pronounced characteristics, indicating the presence of considerable asperities (Fig. 4d). This result was confirmed by23.

The STF exhibits a smoother and more uniform surface morphology, with a reduced height range of 0.55 μm (Fig. 4a), approximately a 37% decrease in surface amplitude. This transition to a more even topography indicates that the starch-based treatment successfully modifies the fiber surface. The treatment appears to facilitate the removal of amorphous surface impurities, resulting in a cleaner fiber structure. The surface roughness parameters provide a comprehensive picture of the material’s topographical features. Ra (1.841 nm) is the average height of the surface’s bumps and dips. Rq (2.387 nm) is the root mean square deviation of the surface profile from a perfectly smooth plane. The apparent Rz (16.28 nm) indicates a highly wavy surface. Rsk (0.06331) indicates that peaks and valleys are nearly evenly spaced, suggesting a fairly uniform surface texture. The high Rku suggests sharp features that could be crucial for applications sensitive to surface protrusions or depressions. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) analysis

SEM analysis provides comprehensive insight into the microstructural evolution of DPFs after starch treatment (Fig. 5).

(Fig. 5b) shows that untreated DPF consists of large, uneven bundles covered with non-cellulosic impurities such as LG, waxes, and PC26 These impurities obscure the underlying cellular structure and create an uneven fiber surface.

By contrast, the STF (Fig. 5a) shows pronounced morphological changes, most notably fibrillation. Fibrillation occurs when the main fiber bundle breaks into smaller microfibrils. CL polymers are organized into fibrillar structures surrounded by HCL and LG. LG serves as a natural adhesive, encapsulating and uniting the CL and HCL constituents. This interconnected network provides mechanical strength, structural stability, and improved resistance to chemical and biological degradation27.

The structural changes observed in starch-treated samples significantly affect the composite’s performance. When the fiber breaks down into microfibrils, its effective surface area increases, improving compatibility with polymer matrices.

Surface porosity: By removing amorphous components such as LG and HCL, micro-pores form within the fiber structure. This increased porosity makes the surface more reactive for matrix bonding, even though it may reduce the fiber diameter. The enhanced crystallinity, together with the extraction of LG and HCL to obtain purer CL, leads to superior thermal characteristics, as CL generally has higher heat stability than LG and HCL28.

Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA)

The thermal degradation characteristics and stability of UTFs and STFs were assessed by TGA over a temperature range of 30 to 1000 °C (Fig. 6).

Fig. 6Thermogravimetric Analysis (TGA) of palm fiber.

Thermogravimetric Analysis (TGA) of palm fiber.

The TGA curves show how weight loss (WL) changes with temperature. They also show that DPFs breaks down in multiple stages when heated, both with and without starch treatment.

The thermogram profile of the fiber shows three degradation stages. These stages represent the degradation of different parts of the fibers, each in turn. Stage I spans from 30 °C to 239.5 °C, during which the fibers lose approximately 2.7–5.7% of their weight. Comparable results were reported by (29 a), with an initial WL of approximately 5–10%. This is largely because water is physically absorbed, and the moisture trapped in the fiber cell walls evaporates.

The system enters the main degradation zone when the temperature rises to 250–400 °C (Stage II). In this zone, the HCL and amorphous CL components of the DPFs break down due to heat, which causing the greatest WL (about 60.35% −70%), comparable to the thermal degradation behavior reported by (Raza et al., 2022), was observed at 282 °C and 383 °C. A significant mass loss (Raza et al., 2022), estimated at around 60–70% of the initial weight, is ascribed to the heat decomposition of hemicellulose, cellulose, lignin, and pectin in the fibers30. These results align with those documented by31, who noted a weight reduction of approximately 62% at about 475 °C.

As the temperature rises from 435 °C to 840 °C in Stage III, the degradation rate slows, with WL ranging from 27.24% to 30.48%. This occurs because LG was carbonized at a high temperature (Wu et al., 2018). The ultimate residual mass is 3.47% for UTFs at a maximum temperature (Tmax) of 850 °C, whereas it diminishes to 1.86% for STFs at a Tmax of 861.70 °C. Table 2 summarizes the results.

These findings demonstrate that augmenting CI and CS enhances mechanical properties and promotes better interfacial bonding in polymer composite applications39. The improved CI enhances mechanical characteristics owing to its organized CL structure and robust intra-molecular hydrogen bonding40,41. The reduction of non-cellulosic materials promotes a more regular alignment of cellulose chains and may favor mechanical interlocking with the matrix42. Dypsis baronii leaf fibers exhibit high cellulose content, good thermal stability, and enhanced interfacial adhesion, making them effective sustainable reinforcements with potential in structural composites and corrosion-resistant applications (Shanmuga et al., 2025).

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that date palm leaf fibers (DPFs) can serve as a sustainable reinforcing agent for advanced biocomposites without conventional chemical modifications.In contrast to these conventional alkali or acid surface treatments, which require concentrated NaOH/HCl solutions, multiple rinsing/neutralization steps and generate saline effluents, our starch-based route uses only water and a bio-based, food-grade polysaccharide, without any hazardous synthetic reagents, making it a more sustainable and effectively chemical-free alternative for modifying date palm fibers.The starch treatment produced clear improvements in surface morphology, fibrillation, crystallinity, and thermal stability, confirming its effectiveness as an eco-friendly modification route for DPFs.SEM and AFM analyses showed surface refinement and increased reactivity, XRD and FTIR confirmed a more ordered cellulose-rich structure, and TGA revealed improved stability in the main degradation range.These findings support the potential use of starch-treated DPFs in fully biodegradable epoxy biocomposites for applications such as automotive, construction, and packaging.Future work should include detailed mechanical testing, including tensile, flexural, and impact behavior, as well as evaluation under industrial processing conditions.Overall, this integrated microstructural–structural–thermal approach ensures that the proposed starch-treated DPFs are not only environmentally attractive but also technically compatible with the interfacial bonding and processing conditions required in practical epoxy biocomposite applications.
