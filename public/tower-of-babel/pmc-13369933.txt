Integrative taxonomic and phytopharmacological analysis of ethnobotanical Acanthaceae species from Manipur, India
Authors: Khuraijam Jyoti, Lushita Phairembam, Nameirakpam Rikesh Mangang, Shimrata Sharma Laimayum, Salam Shyamchandra Singh, Hmingremhlua Sailo, Laldinfeli Ralte, Naorem Santa Singh, Moirangthem Rakesh Singh, Yengkhom Tunginba Singh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Nongmangkha is one of the several medicinal plants traditionally used by the Meitei community of Manipur, India, for treating respiratory and infectious diseases. However, taxonomic confusion exists among three commonly used species— Phlogacanthus thyrsiformis (Roxb. ex Hardw.) Mabb, Phlogacanthus pubinervius T. Anderson, and Justicia adhatoda L.—limiting their safe and effective utilization. Three chloroplast gene- rbcl, rpoC1 and trnH-psbA were used as DNA barcodes to identify the plants then, the sequences obtained were used for secondary structure prediction in RNAfold web server to discriminate among the three plants. The HPTLC revealed chemical fingerprints of the studied species which helped in taxonomic authentication of plants, while GC–MS and FTIR analysis of the extracts showed presence of several bioactive compounds and functional groups of the compounds respectively. Elemental analysis using XRF detected 19 elements in P. pubinervius indicating higher chemical diversity, while J. adhatoda and P. thyrsiformis contained 15 elements each. The plant extracts examined demonstrated significant antimicrobial activity against Staphylococcus epidermidis, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Epidermophyton floccosum, and Fusarium oxysporum. To further explore their potential, molecular docking studies were carried out using the key bioactive compounds, which revealed promising interactions with Metallo-β-lactamase—suggesting their potential as effective antimicrobial agents. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first report to provide comparative insights into the three studied samples at anatomical, chemical, and molecular levels, paving the way toward accurate identification.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-46775-5.

Introduction

Taxonomic confusion remains a major challenge in medicinal plant research, despite the immense therapeutic value of plant resources1. The presence of homonyms and synonyms, along with close morphological similarity among taxa, often leads to ambiguity in species identification and nomenclature. Reliance on morphological characters alone is particularly problematic, as these traits are influenced by environmental factors and are therefore prone to error2. Such misidentification can result in overlapping chemical profiles and medicinal claims, ultimately generating uncertainty in botanical identity. Accurate identification and correct nomenclature are thus essential for reliable genus delimitation and species delineation, especially for medicinal plants88,89. Identification with the help of distinguishable anatomical features have also played an important role in taxonomy and in determination of phylogenetic relationships3, 4. However, in some cases, maybe due to the convergent evolution, same anatomical traits can occur in a distantly related taxa which can lead to misidentification of species5. Another drawback is lack of standardized terminologies for the interpretation of anatomical traits which lead to confusion among the taxonomists6. Identification of species with the help of morphological and anatomical characters are robust when combined with molecular data7. Hence, an integrative approach is required for identification and delineation of species8.

Medicinal plants have long been integral to the culture and traditions of Manipur, serving not only as remedies for various diseases but also playing a key role in traditional rituals, healing practices, and folklore90. They are deeply rooted in the region’s heritage, reflecting the knowledge passed down through generations9. Among different medicinal plants, Nongmangkha is a widely recognized plant of ethnobotanical significance for the Meitei community in Manipur90. There are four types of Nongmangkha commonly used in Manipur, namely Justicia adhatoda, Phlogacanthus thyrsiformis, Phlogacanthus pubinervius, Phlogacanthus jenkinsii, all of which belong to the Acanthaceae family. The family consists of 225 genera and approximately 3000 species. Various members of this family have remarkable medicinal uses in different parts of the world since they possess anti-oxidant, anti-microbial, insecticidal and hepatoprotective properties10, 11. At present, there exists a chaotic state of nomenclature of Nongmangkha species. For example, P. jenkinsii and P. pubinervius look similar and both are regarded as Nongmangkha. Also, there is an ambiguity in the nomenclature of P. pubinervius (Nongmangkha) as some authors name it P. thyrsiformis (Phlogacanthus pubinervius – eFlora of India)12. Thus, need of the hour is proper identification of Nongmangkha species with the help of molecular markers and chemical fingerprinting.

J. adhatoda L. is widely distributed in South Asia and Indo-China. In Manipur, it is locally known as Nongmagkha angouba (Justicia adhatoda-eFlora of India). The plant is shrub, evergreen having large lance-shaped leaves arranged in an opposite phyllotaxy. The corolla is broad and white, with a curved upper lip and a lower lip adorned with purple streaked markings13. The leaves decoction is used in herbal remedy for treating colds, coughs, whooping coughs, chronic bronchitis, asthma, and as a sedative expectorant, antispasmodic, and anthelmintic14.

P. thyrsiformis (Roxb. ex Hadw.) Mabb distributes in North Eastern India, upper Gangetic plain, sub-tropical Himalayas, Bengal and Bihar. In Manipur, it is locally known as Nongmagkha angangba15. The plant is shrub, large lance-shaped leaves, flowers are orange-red, tubular, 2-lipped, hairy and borne in thryses. (Flowers of India, PlantaeDB). It is an edible plant, and its flowers are commonly used in various recipes across northeastern India. In traditional medicine, the aqueous extract of its flowers is utilized for treating kidney disorders16.

P. pubinervius T. Anderson locally known as Nongmangkha, is distributed in Bhutan, North-east India and West Bengal. The plant is shrub, leaves are broadly elliptic, small, subsessile flowers in axillary cyme, corolla orange-yellow, strongly curved, shortly pubescent, wide mouth; tube inflated from base; upper lip longer than lower (Phlogacanthus pubinervius – eFlora of India). Both leaves and inflorescences are consumed as food and also has medicinal properties15. Currently, there is no proper documentation of its uses due to uncertainty in the nomenclature as most the authors consider this species as P. thyrsiformis leading to confusion in its identification and records17.

Parallel to taxonomic challenges, plants are known to contain a variety of phytochemicals (flavonoid, alkaloids and terpenes etc.) with great potential to cure several illnesses indicating that they can act as therapeutic agents18. Due to the adverse effects of synthetic drugs, herbal medicines which are the natural products have become an interesting subject to the scientific community91. But taking the crude extract or decoction orally is not recommended because it may contain toxic components that might harm our body19. Thus, proper characterization and identification of phytochemical constituents is necessary as it can predict the pharmacological activity of plants such as antibacterial, antifungal, anticancer, anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties. It is also important for the downstream targeted isolation process of compounds to enhance further investigations20, 21. GC–MS is one of the widely used techniques for screening phytochemicals as it is a fast and a reliable technique to identify the volatile compounds from the plant extracts20, 22. HPTLC also aids in the characterization of medicinal plants by generating distinct chemical fingerprints23, 24. Another novel technique to characterize phytochemical constituents by identifying functional groups such as alkanes, alcohol, alkyl halides, alcohols, phenols, aldehydes, ketone, esters etc., is Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FTIR). In addition, elemental analysis of medicinal plants with the help of X-Ray Fluorescence (XRF) is essential for quality control, safety, therapeutic efficiency as well as to corroborate the scientific validation of its traditional uses25, 26. Moreover, identification of drug like compounds for its target protein is the next step after the screening of bioactive compounds27. In this, computer aided tools can be utilized for drug-discovery approaches from the plant natural products, as they detects how the ligand-receptor binds anticipating the working of the drug model28.

Precise identification with the help of molecular markers is very important especially for medicinal plants to ensure their safe utilization as well as for conservation strategies29. For systematic plant identification, DNA barcoding is widely used as it give quick as well as efficient results and also, evolutionary relationships can be inferred by reconstructing the phylogenetic tree30. This method can be employed during non-flowering season unlike morphological identification done by taxonomy experts31. The method utilizes standardized DNA sequences either coding or non-coding, either from the nuclear genome such as Internal Transcribed Spacer (ITS1 or ITS2) or chloroplast genome such as ribulose bisphosphate carboxylase large (rbcl), maturase (matK), trnH-psbA, rpoB, rpoC1 etc. followed by amplification, sequencing a DNA region, then comparing it to a reference database like NCBI of known species32. The efficiency of these barcodes depends on several factors such as specificity, universality, quality of the sequence and success rate of amplification33. Thus, the establishment of a single ideal barcode for plants is a matter of debate22.

Despite the benefits of all three species, the extensive mechanistic insights from research at the molecular and genetic levels as well as the screening of phytochemicals for antimicrobial activity are still being elucidated.

Thus, in our present study we focus on exploring the characterization and comparative analysis of phytochemicals constituents of J. adhatoda, P. thyrsiformis, P. pubinervius, their antimicrobial activities and genetic variation to evaluate the evolutionary relationship among them. This study will provide unique characteristic features at structural, chemical and molecular levels of the three taxonomically challenging species of Nongmangkha, facilitating their proper delineation as well as delimitation and to provide scientific validation of its traditional uses. This is the first report to present a comparative analysis of different types of Nongmangkha found in Manipur.

Materials and method

Plant collection and identification

The three Nongmangkha species— Justicia adhatoda Phlogacanthus thyrsiformis and Phlogacanthus pubinervius—were collected during their flowering season, with the first two species gathered from Kakching district, Manipur, India, and Phlogacanthus pubinervius from the Manipur University campus, Imphal, Manipur, India during January and February 2025 (Fig. 1). The identification and authenticity of the collected plant species were confirmed by Department of Life Sciences (Botany), Manipur University. The voucher specimen of all 3 species was later deposited in the herbarium of the Department of Life Sciences (Botany), Manipur University. Microscopic analysis

Transverse section (TS) of young stem of P. thyrsiformis, P. pubinervius, and J. adhatoda, stained with safranin, was studied using NIKON ECLIPS E200 Upright Microscope.

Genomic DNA isolation

Total genomic DNA was isolated using the standard CTAB method92. The integrity and quality of the DNA were assessed by standard agarose gel electrophoresis and spectrophotometry.

PCR amplification and sequencing

The isolated gDNA was amplified using the barcoding primers listed in Table 2. PCR amplification was carried out in a 25 µl reaction mixture using 2X EmeraldAmp MAX PCR Master Mix, 100 ng/ul each of forward & reverse primers, 5 ng of genomic DNA in a thermal cycler (Proflex PCR system, Thermo Fischer, USA). The conditions of the PCR followed an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 5 min, followed by 35 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 1 min, annealing at 55 °C for 1 min, extension at 72 °C for 1 min and a final extension at 72 °C for 10 min. Bioinformatics analysis & secondary structure predictions

The sequences were submitted in Basic Local Alignment Tool (BLAST) for sequence similarity search. Species identification was carried out using Nucleotide BLAST, based on the highest similarity score and the lowest E-value and then sequences were submitted to NCBI database. Using MEGA 12, the sequences were translated into protein and multiple sequence alignment was performed using Clustal Omega. Secondary structure prediction based on MFE was performed using the three chloroplast genes sequences in RNAfold Web. Cluster analysis was also done and tree was drawn using MEGA 12 (Fig. S1a–c).

HPTLC

The methanolic plant extract (0.5 mg/mL) was sonicated for 5 min and centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 5 min after which 30 mg of activated charcoal was added to each sample. The samples were incubated for 30 min at RT and then centrifuged at 15,000 rpm for 10 min. Chromatography was performed on Merck HPTLC Silica Gel 60 F254 plates (20 × 10 cm). The sample application was made using a Linomat 5 applicator equipped with a Hamilton syringe. Samples were applied as bands of 8 mm length at volumes of 2µL, 4µL, 6µL, and 8 µL, alongside the 2µL caffeine standard34. The chromatogram was developed in a Twin Trough Chamber (20 × 10 cm) saturated with a solvent mixture of toluene: chloroform: ethanol (4:4:1, v/v/v) for 20 min. The plate was allowed to develop to a distance of 70 mm, air-dried, then it was examined under UV at 254 nm (R254)35. After air drying, the developed plates were derivatized using 10% methanolic sulfuric acid and dried in a 100 °C oven for 3 min. Final visualization and fingerprinting were performed by scanning the plates under UV at 360 nm (R360)36.

Sample preparation & GC–MS analysis

The plant material (leaves) was air-dried at room temperature and then ground into a fine powder. 30 g of the powder were extracted with 300 ml of methanol using a Soxhlet apparatus for 30 cycles. The extract was then concentrated using a rotary evaporator at 50 °C and the resulting crude extract was stored at 4 °C for future use. GC–MS analysis of the methanolic leaf extract of three species of Acanthaceae family were conducted using an Agilent 7890B GC system coupled with an Agilent 5977A MSD37. Detected compounds were identified by comparing their mass spectra with those in the NIST20.1 library (2020). Compounds were then identified based on the retention times and mass spectra with already known compounds in the NIST library (C:\Database\NIST20.1).

Anti-microbial

The antimicrobial activities of the methanolic extracts from three Acanthaceae species were tested against two bacterial strains and two fungal strains: Staphylococcus epidermidis, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Epidermophyton floccosum, and Fusarium oxysporum at different concentrations (25, 50, and 100 mg/ml). Ciprofloxacin was used as the positive control for bacterial strains, and Itraconazole was used as the positive control for fungal strains.

Fourier transformed infrared spectroscopy (FTIR)

Functional groups present in the samples were analysed using a PerkinElmer L1600300 Spectrum Two LiTa FT-IR, covering a spectral range from 400 to 4000 cm⁻1, following the procedure adopted by Wulandari et al.38 with some modifications.

XRF

The analysis was conducted using a Bruker S1 Titan 800 portable X-ray Fluorescence (pXRF) instrument at the Department of Earth Sciences, Manipur University. Samples were first powdered using an agate mortar and then sieved through an ASTM 230 mesh. Approximately 4 g of the powdered sample were placed into a pXRF sample cup, with one side covered using a 3 µm prolene film. Each sample was analyzed in triplicate at different positions to ensure accuracy, and the final result was reported as the average of the three measurements. Data were expressed in terms of weight percentage or parts per million (ppm). The analysis was carried out using the Geoexploration Oxide 3-phase mode, with a measurement time of 60 s per phase.

Molecular docking

Bioactive compounds were selected from the three plant species based on the results from GC–MS analysis. The identified compounds were further validated and supplemented through comprehensive literature searches to ensure their bioactivity. The chemical structures of the selected compounds were retrieved from public databases such as PubChem and ChEMBL. Molecular docking studies were performed to assess the binding strength and interaction profiles of selected bioactive ligands with the Metallo-β-lactamase 1 (NDM-1) enzyme, an important target for inhibiting β-lactamase activity.

Ligand preparation

Ligand preparation was carried out based on Lipinski Rule of Five to ensure favourable drug like properties39. The chemical structures of the selected compounds were retrieved from public databases such as PubChem and ChEMBL.

Retrieval of target protein

The three-dimensional structure of the target protein, Metallo-β-lactamase 1 (NDM-1), an essential enzyme implicated in antibiotic resistance through its β-lactamase activity, was retrieved from the Protein Data Bank (PDB ID: 4EY2)40.

Docking analysis

The prepared target protein structure was further analysed using AutoDock Vina, which facilitated molecular docking simulations to predict the binding affinities and interactions between the selected ligands and the active site of NDM-1. This computational approach enabled the identification of potential inhibitors that could effectively bind and inhibit the enzyme’s function41.

The HPTLC method was employed to establish a comparative fingerprint of phytoconstituents present in J. adhatoda, P. thyrsiformis and P. pubinervius (Table 7). The HPTLC profile at two different wavelengths and the Rf values are depicted in Figs. 6, 7 respectively. The tracks Y1–Y4, W1–W4, and R1–R4 represent replicates of the same extract applied in increasing volumes (from 2 to 8 µL). The highest number of bands were observed in P. pubinervius and lowest in P. thyrsiformis. The banding patterns were significantly different among the three species thus, creating a unique HPTLC fingerprint profile for each species. Rf value of 0.06 was obtained in P. thyrsiformis and P. pubinervius while Rf value of 0.35 was obtained in J. adhatoda and P. pubinervius. Nine bioactive compounds were identified from J. adhatoda, 8 from P. thrysiformis, and 10 from P. pubinervius. The major compound in J. adhatoda was found to be phytol since it shows highest peak area, whereas in P. thrysiformis and P. pubinervius, γ-stigmasterol shows the highest peak area. Discussion

The concordant evidence from morphological traits, species-specific chemical fingerprints, and multilocus DNA barcoding unequivocally resolves the taxonomic ambiguity between P. pubinervius and P. thyrsiformis, which has persisted due to inconsistent nomenclature.

The present study highlights the importance of proper identification employing different strategies such as morphological and microscopic studies, DNA barcoding and chemical fingerprinting. Microscopic study of P. thrysiformis, P. pubinervius and J. adhatoda help in identification to some extent in our current study. However, more-in-depth studies such as chemical fingerprinting by HPTLC, evaluation of phytoconstituents by GC–MS, DNA barcoding at molecular level are required for accurate identification, as the specific anatomical features observed are not sufficient to clearly differentiate among the three species. Our integrative framework—combining DNA barcoding with chemical fingerprinting—resolves this ambiguity and provides unambiguous diagnostic features for each species, thereby correcting probable taxonomic misassignments in previous literature.

Medicinal plants contain a mixture of phytochemicals that contribute to their overall efficacy93. HPTLC fingerprints of individual species aid in the accurate identification of medicinally important plants. Abdallah et al.72 developed an HPTLC fingerprinting method for four Artemisia species, integrated with comparative profiling using GC–MS and NIR spectroscopy. By comparing HPTLC fingerprints, adulterated herbal products can be detected, highlighting HPTLC as a reliable method for evaluating herbal product quality94,95. Kripasana and Xavier73 reported the phytochemical constituents of P. thyrsiformis and P. pubinervius using GC–MS. However, their findings contradict ours because they appear to have referred to P. thyrsiformis as P. pubinervius and vice versa. In our present study, phytol is the principal component in J. adhatoda but it is also found in other two samples. It is known to possess antimicrobial activity and abundantly found in nature as it is the metabolic by product of chlorophyll74. The presence of phytol and neophytadiene in P. thyrsiformis and J. adhatoda was reported by Ningthoujam et al.96, which corroborates our findings. However, in their study as well, P. pubinervius appears to have been mistakenly referred to as P. thyrsiformis. This highlights the importance of precise taxonomic identification through traditional experts, scientific validation and molecular tools to prevent misidentification and facilitate reliable phytochemical studies. P. thyrsiformis and P. pubinervius showed the presence of γ-stigmasterol. Stigmasterol and its derivatives have been isolated from many plants due to their pharmacological and biological significance, including anti-osteoarthritic activity, cytotoxicity, anti-hypercholesterolemic activity, anti-tumor effects, hypoglycemic activity, antioxidant and antimutagenic properties, anti-inflammatory effects, and central nervous system (CNS) activities, as well as their influence on thyroid function75, 76. Some compounds found in all samples remain unidentified, therefore, further characterization and isolation are needed to investigate their pharmacological activities. Among the three plant species studied, P. pubinervius is used more frequently in Manipur followed by J. adhatoda. This widespread use is likely linked to the fact that it contains a greater number of bioactive compounds, making it more valuable or effective in traditional or therapeutic uses.

Our study aimed to compare P. thrysiformis, P. pubinervius and J. adhatoda at the molecular level for accurate identification. The sequences obtained for the three species were submitted to NBCI BankIt to fill a significant gap in the DNA barcode reference databases of these particular species. DNA barcoding provide insights into the genetic importance of various medicinal plants77. Using standard barcodes, this technique quick and efficient process that provides precise species discrimination78, 79. This study is the first report to identify all three Nongmangkha types in Manipur by DNA barcoding with the help of rbcl, rpoC1 and trnH-psbA gene. Reference11 conducted a study to evaluate the potential of three plastid DNA regions- matK, trnH-psbA, and rbcL as DNA barcodes for the identification of a medicinal plant belonging to Acanthaceae family and revealed trnH-psbA as an effective DNA barcode. Several authors have reported the use of ITS2 and matK as efficient barcodes to authenticate herbal medicines80, 81. However, in our study PCR amplification using ITS2 and matK primer were successful but the sequencing results were not convincing. Thus, for the discrimination of these three medicinal plants of Acanthaceae, chloroplast genes rbcl, rpoC1 and trnH-psbA were used for barcoding due to its good quality sequencing results and 100% success rate of PCR amplification.

The secondary structure predictions using rbcl, rpoC1 and trnH-psbA for the three samples showed significant differences. The loops number, position, size, double helices number, were distinct among the three samples for each gene. Moreover, the shape as well as the stem loop distribution were different. The secondary structure predictions using three chloroplast genes (i.e., rbcl, rpoC1 and trnH-psbA) showed significant variations among P. thrysiformis, P. pubinervius and J. adhatoda. Our study predicted the secondary structure employing three chloroplast genes based on the minimum free energy (MFE) method. The MFE method of secondary structure prediction is a dynamic programming algorithm that finds all possible RNA folding to select the one with the lowest energy value indicating the most stable form in equilibrium conditions82. The results showed that structural differences reflect underlying sequence divergence and their uniqueness in the secondary structure. Thus, it aids in comparative sequence analysis as well as in developing species specific RNA molecule77, 83. The unique structural features of these structures can be molecular fingerprints of particular species and utilized in taxonomic classification and identification of medicinal plants2.

Various functional groups in medicinal plants such as aldehydes, alkenes, amines, amides, alcohols, phenols, aromatics, carboxylic acids, esters, ethers etc., can be analysed by using FTIR. It helps in the identification of the bioactive compounds, its structure as well as role. It can be concluded that there are no significant differences in the peaks of FTIR spectra (Fig. 10) indicating the presence of similar functional groups in J. adhatoda, P. thrysiformis and P. pubinervius.

The XRF results revealed greater elemental diversity observed in P. pubinervius may be associated with its higher medicinal potential. Hossen et al.84 studied the elemental composition in J. adhatoda using the PIXE technique however, Cr and Fe were not detected in their study. Seal and Tapan97 reported the presence of the heavy metals Pb and Cr in very low amount in P. thyrsiformis but Pb is not detected in our case. The presence of different elements in plants reflects both genetic factors and agroclimatic conditions85. The growing global challenge of antimicrobial resistance (AMR) has significantly compromised the effectiveness of many conventional antibiotics, leading to an increase in treatment failures and posing a serious public health threat worldwide (World Health Organization, 2020). As a result, there has been renewed enthusiasm in exploring plant-based alternatives, particularly due to the rich diversity of bioactive compounds found in natural products, which possess proven antibacterial, antifungal, and antiviral properties102, 104. GC–MS analysis revealed the presence of several bioactive compounds in the tested plant extracts, which may be responsible for the observed antimicrobial activities. Previous reports have documented the antibacterial activity of P. thyrsiformis-identified as P. pubinervius in our current study—against Salmonella typhimurium and S. enterica105, as well as Bacillus cereus, Vibrio cholerae, and Staphylococcus aureus96. Consistent with these findings, our study demonstrated that P. pubinervius exhibited the most potent broad-spectrum antimicrobial activity among the examined plant species. It displayed dose-dependent antibacterial effects against Staphylococcus epidermidis, a pathogen associated with nosocomial infections106, and Pseudomonas aeruginosa, known to cause urinary tract and respiratory infections22, along with antifungal activity against Epidermophyton floccosum, a dermatophyte responsible for infections such as tinea cruris, tinea pedis, and tinea corporis107.

Justicia adhatoda has been previously reported to possess antibacterial activity against Klebsiella pneumoniae, Escherichia coli, Streptococcus pyogenes, and Serratia marcescens, in addition to antifungal activity against Aspergillus flavus, Candida albicans, and Cryptococcus neoformans113. While in our study, both J. adhatoda and P. thyrsiformis exhibited no inhibitory activity against P. aeruginosa, they did display antifungal activity against Fusarium oxysporum, a major wilt pathogen103, and E. floccosum, respectively.

The antimicrobial efficacy of the plant extracts was further supported by their minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) values, which ranged from 0.781 to 6.25 mg/mL. Notably, both J. adhatoda and P. thyrsiformis exhibited strong antibacterial activity against S. epidermidis, with the lowest MIC value recorded at 0.781 mg/mL. However, antifungal activity across all extracts was comparatively moderate, with MIC values ranging from 3.125 to 6.25 mg/mL.

The observed antimicrobial potential is likely attributed to the presence of known bioactive compounds identified in the GC–MS analysis, including Betaine42, 43, 1-Benzyl-1,2,3-triazole44, Tetra-decanoic acid45, and 2,6-Dimethoxyphenol60, all of which have demonstrated antimicrobial properties in previous studies. The antimicrobial activity reported herein not only validates the traditional use of these medicinal plants but also highlights the importance of ethnobotanical knowledge in guiding the discovery of novel antimicrobial agents.

Molecular docking has become indispensable tool in recent biomedical and pharmaceutical research86. It remains a crucial component of drug discovery, facilitating rapid in-silico prediction and identification of promising drug candidates, thereby reducing both time & cost87. In our study, we aimed to explore the selective bioactive compounds from the three species- P. thyrsiformis, J. adhatoda and P. pubinervius with Metallo-β-lactamase 1 (NDM-1) enzyme using molecular docking analysis to assess their efficacy as β-lactamase inhibitors.

Molecular docking studies focused on the Metallo-β-lactamase 1 (NDM-1) enzyme were performed utilizing a selection of bioactive ligands to assess their efficacy as β-lactamase inhibitors. Among the evaluated samples compounds identified in P. pubinervius, 2,6,10-Dodecatrien-1-ol, 3,7,11-trimethyl-, acetate (E, E), demonstrated the strongest binding affinity, with the lowest free binding energy of (–8.1 kcal/mol). It also displayed antimicrobial activity, suggesting a stable and meaningful interaction within the enzyme’s active site. Additionally, it showed a favourable ligand efficiency (–0.91) and a high drug score (0.89), although its predicted inhibition constant (KI) was moderate at 35.66 nM. Beta-D Glucosyloxyazoxymethane identified in both P. thyrsiformis and P. pubinervius demonstrated promising outcomes with a binding energy of –7.5 kcal/mol and a minimal KI of 19.9 nM, indicating its strong affinity for the zinc-rich catalytic pocket108.

Other compounds, such as Phenol, 2,6-dimethoxy from P. pubinervius and 1-Benzyl-1,2,3-triazole from J. adhatoda, also showed stable binding interactions with energies of –6.6 kcal/mol and –6.4 kcal/mol, respectively, and ligand efficiencies of –0.87 each, reinforcing their potential as inhibitors. In contrast, Betaine and Tetradecanoic acid from J. adhatoda exhibited weak interactions, with high inhibition constants (> 120 nM) and low binding energies, indicating limited affinity for NDM-1’s active site. Generally, a docking binding free energy below –5 kcal/mol suggests strong binding affinity and is considered promising for drug development109.The docking analysis suggests that various ligands, notably beta-D Glucosyloxyazoxymethane and 2,6,10-Dodecatrien-1-ol derivatives, may function as effective NDM-1 inhibitors and could provide a foundation for the optimization and advancement of new agents aimed at β-lactamase-mediated multidrug resistance110.

Metallo-β-lactamase 1 (NDM-1), encoded by the blaNDM-1 gene, is a therapeutically relevant enzyme that imparts substantial resistance to many β-lactam antibiotics, including carbapenems, rendering it a critical focus for antimicrobial resistance (AMR) research. The crystal structure of NDM-1 (PDB ID: 4EY2) provides a high-resolution model (1.90 Å) with a clearly defined active site coordinated by two zinc ions crucial for catalytic activity111. The structural clarity and the existence of a co-crystallized hydrolyzed ligand render 4EY2 an exemplary candidate for structure-based drug design and molecular docking investigations. The conserved metal-binding pocket facilitates efficient virtual screening of small compounds and prospective inhibitors that may enhance the efficiency of β-lactam antibiotics. NDM-1 is a pivotal molecular target for developing new β-lactamase inhibitors due to its significant role in multidrug resistance and global spread through mobile genetic elements112.

Conclusion

In this study, we adopted a comprehensive and integrative approach to the identification and characterization of three medicinal plants, belonging to Acanthaceae- Phlogacanthus thyrsiformis, Phlogacanthus pubinervius, and Justicia adhatoda, combining anatomical, molecular, and chemical aspects. With the help of DNA barcoding, RNA secondary structure prediction, chemical fingerprinting, functional group identification, antimicrobial assays, elemental profiling, and molecular docking, we were able to discriminate the three species and demonstrate their therapeutic potential. P. pubinervius is distinguished by clear morphological features, higher chemical diversity, and distinct genetic signatures, resolving its long-standing taxonomic ambiguity with P. thyrsiformis. Also, the strong binding affinity (–8.1 kcal/mol) of 2,6,10-dodecatrien-1-ol, 3,7,11-trimethyl-, acetate (E,E) supports its prioritization for future drug discovery studies. However, several bioactive compounds detected through GC–MS analysis remain unidentified, suggesting in-depth phytoconstituents investigations by employing gold standard techniques such as X-ray crystallography & Nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR). From a practical perspective, the generated molecular barcodes and chemical fingerprints offer reliable tools for authentication, quality control, and prevention of misidentification or adulteration of Nongmangkha in traditional medicine and herbal markets. Furthermore, the observed antimicrobial activity and favorable ligand–target interactions of selected phytocompounds highlight the pharmacological potential of these species and provide a scientific basis for their traditional therapeutic use. Overall, this integrative approach establishes a reproducible model for accurate identification and pharmacological validation of taxonomically challenging medicinal plants. Moreover, further research is essential to evaluate their pharmacological efficacy, optimize bioactive compound extraction, and support the development of novel drug formulations based on these traditionally used medicinal plants. The findings of our study will pave a way for esteemed researchers in authentication and identification of medicinally important plants belonging to Acanthaceae.
