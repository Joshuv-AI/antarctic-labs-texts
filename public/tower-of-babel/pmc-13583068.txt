Effects of damaged astrocytes on DNA damage and repair in human neurons: Implications for Alzheimer's disease
Authors: Starr Welty, Michael Bagnell, Fulin Ma, Karl Herrup, Arthur Samuel Levine
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundStudies suggest a strong association between astrocytes, neuronal DNA damage, elevated amyloid-β, and brain degeneration in Alzheimer's disease (AD).ObjectiveThis study aimed to show whether astrocytes with damaged DNA affect human neuronal progenitor cells (NPCs) or differentiated neurons in close proximity, dependent on astrocytic APOE allele expression.MethodsImmortalized human astrocytes (hTERT) expressing APOE were treated with etoposide to induce DNA damage and co-cultured in a transwell system with human NPCs or differentiated neurons. We used western blotting and immunostaining to evaluate the DNA damage response of the NPCs and neurons.ResultsUndamaged NPCs showed increased DNA damage when co-cultured with damaged astrocytes. The astrocytic APOE genotype had little to no effect on the transcellular damage response. NPCs overexpressing the amyloid-β protein precursor responded more robustly when co-cultured with damaged astrocytes. Differentiated neurons showed no significant changes in their DNA damage response to damaged astrocytes.ConclusionsThis study is the first to demonstrate that astrocytic DNA damage may contribute to early stages of neuronal pathology in AD by inducing a DNA damage response in vulnerable neuronal populations.Keywords: Alzheimer's disease, amyloid, astrocytes, DNA damage, DNA repair, etoposide, neurodegenerative disease, neurons, oxidative stress

Introduction

Alzheimer's disease (AD) is the fifth leading cause of death in the United States, and its prevalence is predicted to rise. 1 While sporadic late onset AD is characterized by cognitive and memory deficits, the sequence of events that triggers the development of AD pathology, e.g., amyloid-β (Aβ) plaques and tau tangles, is not well characterized. Recent research has focused on understanding how genetics, environment, and social risk factors contribute to the development of AD decades before the onset of symptoms. 2 Important studies highlight how excessive oxidative damage in brain regions associated with the progressive pathology of AD, and the early activation of astrocytes, may contribute to the neuronal degradation characteristic of AD.3–7 DNA is a major target of this oxidative damage. Persistent, unrepaired DNA double strand breaks (DSBs) are characteristic of neurodegenerative diseases, and studies have shown the accumulation of DSBs in astrocytes, neurons, and oligodendrocytes of postmortem brain samples of AD patients early in disease progression.8–12 Other research has shown deficits in the DNA damage response (DDR) associated with AD pathology, including decreased homologous recombination (HR) and transcription-coupled homologous recombination (TC-HR) repair-associated proteins and their recruitment in AD patient samples and mouse models.11,13,14 Our previous research shows directly that the accumulation of DNA DSBs in human neuronal progenitors (NPCs) leads to the overproduction of Aβ40 and Aβ42 and to potentially further increases in neuronal DSBs. 14 Here we focus on the relationship between astrocytes and neurons, and the consequences of DNA DSBs in astrocytes- a topic that has not been well studied.

Astrocytes provide essential functions in neuronal development and maintenance including regulation of neurotransmitters and modulation of synapses.15–22 In AD and other neurodegenerative diseases, astrocytes become reactive, increasing their size and number, and producing pro-inflammatory cytokines which could exacerbate neuronal damage7,23–27. Astrocytes are also involved in the clearance of Aβ from the brain, but in AD this process becomes impaired.28,29 This contributes to the accumulation of Aβ, furthering inflammation, reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, Aβ-induced DNA damage, and neuronal damage.30–32 While numerous studies correlate Aβ, pathological tau, and cognitive impairment associated with AD, including single-cell transcriptomic atlases profiling specific brain regions,33,34 research has demonstrated that activated astrocytes are an essential upstream event that triggers Aβ, leading to pathologic tau accumulation. 7 Thus we have chosen to focus on astrocytes as one of the primary contributors towards the development of AD.

Among their supporting roles in the brain, astrocytes are a major source of the lipid carrier protein, apolipoprotein E (APOE). The APOE gene exists in three allelic forms (ε2, ε3, and ε4) where ε4 is considered a major heritable risk factor of AD, ε3 is considered neutral, and ε2 is moderately protective. In individuals with the ε4 variant, astrocytes have a reduced ability to clear Aβ from the brain, leading to the accumulation of Aβ plaques,28,29 potentially contributing to further activation of astrocytes, oxidative damage, and DSBs. Since astrocyte reactivity is an essential component of the Aβ/pathogenic tau cascade, it is important to understand how astrocyte DNA damage induces or contributes to oxidative DNA damage in NPCs and terminally differentiated neurons.

In this study we profile the human astrocyte DDR using etoposide treatment to induce the accumulation of DNA DSBs by quantifying expression of multiple DDR associated proteins. We then investigate the effect of astrocytes on the early NPC and neuronal DDR by quantifying γH2AX expression. We provide evidence that astrocyte APOE does not affect the wild type (wt) NPC or differentiated neuronal DDR. However, NPCs that overexpress the amyloid-β protein precursor (AβPP), e.g., ReN GA2 NPCs, demonstrate significant induction of the DDR when co-cultured with etoposide treated astrocytes, regardless of astrocytic APOE expression. These results indicate that damaged astrocytes induce the DDR in bystander NPCs, especially in the presence of neuronal Aβ40 and Aβ42. These data provide essential information on the first stages of AD pathology and how oxidative DNA damage, both in astrocytes and NPCs, may affect the early progression of AD.

Methods

hTERT astrocyte cell culture and stable cell line generation

The immortalized human telomerase reverse transcriptase (hTERT) astrocyte cell line was purchased from Applied Biological Materials (T0281). These astrocytes were immortalized via transduction with recombinant lentiviruses carrying the hTERT gene, enabling them to express the telomerase enzyme, bypass replicative senescence, and divide indefinitely. Astrocytes were cultured in flasks and dishes precoated with 0.1% Matrigel (Fisher Scientific, CB40230) with Prigrow IV medium (ABM, TM004) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Fisher Scientific, MT35010CV), 10 ng/mL human recombinant epidermal growth factor protein (hEGF) (PeproTech, AF-100-15), 1% L-glutamine (Fisher Scientific, 25030081), and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Thermo Fisher, 15140122). Serum deprivation was accomplished by culturing cells without FBS for 72 h. To generate stable 3 x HA-tagged APOE ε2, ε3, and ε4 hTERT astrocyte lines, lentiviral vectors were designed utilizing VectorBuilder. VectorBuilder vector ID numbers are as follows: VB230106-1250yev (pLV[Exp]-Puro-CMV > HA/HA/HA/{APOE E2}), VB230106-1254knb (pLV[Exp]-Puro-CMV > HA/HA/HA/{APOE E3}), VB230106-1257ycu (pLV[Exp]-Puro-CMV > HA/HA/HA/{APOE E4}). Cells were cultured for 2 days and then incubated with lentiviral vector for 48 h. After transduction, stable expressing cells were selected using 5 μg/mL puromycin (Millipore Sigma, P8833) added to media for 10 days, and APOE over-expression verified via western blotting and immunostaining.

REN VM cell culture and neuronal differentiation

The human ReN VM wt and REN VM GA2 immortalized neuronal progenitor cell lines were purchased from Millipore Sigma (SCC008; SC008FAD4 respectively). The ReN VM GA2 cell line leads to overexpression of AβPP, altered subcellular localization of AβPP, and increased Aβ42 generation, and were generated as previously described. 35 We cultured NPCs in 2D 1% Matrigel coated T75 flasks or 6-well plates (Fisher Scientific, 354230) in proliferation media: Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium/Nutrient Mixture F-12 (Thermo Fisher, 10565042) supplemented with heparin (Sigma-Aldrich, H3393), B-27 (Gibco, 17504044), human fibroblast growth factor basic protein (bFGF) (Fisher Scientific, GF003AF100UG), and human recombinant epidermal growth factor protein (hEGF) (Millipore Sigma, GF144) at 37°C, 5% CO2 as previously described. 36 For differentiation into neurons, NPCs were passaged at 500,000 cells/well in 2D 1% Matrigel coated 6-well plates or Mattek dishes (Thermo Fisher, NC9862238) in proliferation media for 24 h at 37°C, 5% CO2. Proliferation media was then exchanged for differentiation media: Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium/Nutrient Mixture F-12 and B-27, with ½ media changes every 3 days for 16–20 days.

Primary astrocyte culture and preparation of conditioned media

Astrocytes were obtained from P2-P5 C57BL/6J mice. Brain tissues were dissected to dissociate the cortices. Cortices were cut into small pieces using forceps and digested with 0.25% trypsin-EDTA (Sigma) for 45 min at 37°C. The tissue was then washed in DMEM containing 10% FBS to inactivate the trypsin, and a pipette was used to separate the tissue. Cell suspension was then plated on poly-L-lysine-coated T75 flasks in DMEM, high glucose, 10% FBS, and 1% pen/strep (10,000 U/ml; ThermoFisher Scientific). After two weeks, pure astrocytes were obtained by shaking the flasks at 37°C for 4 h and discarding the supernatant and all loosened cells. The remaining adherent cells (predominantly astrocytes) were trypsinized and rinsed before replating in T75 flasks. After 2 days astrocytes were harvested by trypsinization, rinsed, pelleted and plated on coverslips in a 24-well plate at a final density of 50,000 cells per well. Cultures were maintained in DMEM medium without FBS for 48 h prior to use. KU-60019 conditioned medium was prepared as described previously 37 by incubating astrocytes in Neurobasal medium containing 1 µM KU-60019 (KM) for 48 h. In the control group, astrocytes were exposed to Neurobasal medium without KU-60019 (CM). After two days, the conditioned medium was centrifuged at 2000 g to remove cells and debris, then used within 24 h.

Primary neuronal culture

Embryonic cortical neurons were isolated following standard procedures as described previously. 38 Cortices were collected from embryonic day 16.5 (E16.5) C57BL/6J mouse embryos then digested in trypsin for 10 min in 37°C. Cells were cultured in Neurobasal medium (ThermoFisher Scientific) supplemented with 2% B27 (ThermoFisher Scientific), 1% Glutamax (ThermoFisher Scientific), and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (10,000 U/ml; Thermo Fisher Scientific), in 37°C, 5% CO2. Neurons were plated at a final density of 8500 cells/cm 2 on poly-L-lysine-coated glass coverslips (ThermoFisher Scientific). For histological studies, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA, P6148-500G; Sigma-Aldrich) for 15 min. Cells were stored in 0.1% PFA for long term storage.

siAPOE knockdown in hTERT astrocytes

Dharmacon ON-TARGET plus SMART pool human siAPOE (Cat# L-006470-00-0005) and control non-targeting siRNA (Cat# D-001810-01-03) were resuspended to 20 μM in 1 x siRNA buffer (DharmaFECT, T-2001-01). hTERT astrocytes were passaged at 300,000 cells/mL in a transwell system (outlined below) or on 0.1% Matrigel coated 6-well plates. 24 h prior to experimental treatment, siAPOE or control siRNA was transfected using DharmaFECT transfection reagent 1 (Horizon, T-2001-01) in serum-free/antibiotic-free medium. Cells were incubated for a minimum of 24 h at 37°C, 5% CO2 prior to continuation of experimental procedures. APOE knockdown was verified using confocal imaging and western blotting.

Transwell system and DNA damage assays

For the transwell system, hTERT astrocytes were passaged at 300,000 cells/mL on 0.4 μm pore size PET cell culture inserts (Fisher Scientific, 08-771) in 6-well plates and allowed to grow for 24 h at 37°C, 5% CO2. For all other experiments, astrocytes were passaged onto 0.1% Matrigel coated 6-well plates or Mattek dishes. Unless otherwise specified, astrocytes were serum starved for 72 h, then treated with experimental conditions in fresh media with serum. ReN wt and GA2 NPCs were passaged at 500,000–700,000 cells/mL on 2D 1% Matrigel coated 6-well plates or Mattek dishes and allowed to grow for 24 h at 37°C, 5% CO2. Neurons were differentiated as outlined above. For etoposide treatments, cells were treated with indicated concentrations of etoposide or 0.02% DMSO control for 4 h at 37°C, 5% CO2. Cells were then washed with 1 X PBS, the media was replaced, and the cells were allowed to recover for noted time courses at 37°C, 5% CO2. For the transwell system, PET cell culture inserts containing astrocytes were added on top of 6-wells containing ReN wt or GA2 cells during recovery time courses. Etoposide was re-suspended in 100% DMSO at 50 mM stock concentration prior to dilution.

Immunostaining

hTERT astrocytes, ReN wt NPCs or neurons, and ReN GA2 NPCs or neurons were grown on 2D 0.1% or 1% Matrigel coated 12 mm diameter coverslips in 6-well plates or Mattek dishes for immunostaining. Coverslips and dishes were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min at room temperature. For permeabilization, 0.2% Triton was used for 10 min at room temperature. Cells were blocked with 10% goat serum for 1 h at room temperature. For primary antibody staining, coverslips and dishes were incubated with primary antibody in 10% goat serum overnight at 4°C in a humidity chamber. Coverslips and dishes were incubated with 1:1000 DAPI and secondary antibody Alexa Fluor 694/555 goat anti-mouse or -rabbit immunoglobulin G conjugate (Invitrogen) in 10% goat serum for 1 h at room temperature. Coverslips and dishes were then mounted with ProLong Diamond Antifade Mountant (Fisher Scientific, P36961) onto microscope slides. Primary antibodies used were anti-GFAP (1:1000, Fisher Scientific, 01-670-261), anti-GFAP (1:400, Sigma, G3893), anti-MAP2 (1:1000, Millipore, MAB378), anti-MAP2 (1:2500, Abcam, ab5392), anti-synapsin-1 (1:1000, Millipore, AB1543), and anti-Ki67 (1:1000, Fisher Scientific, PA5-19462). For immunocytochemistry in neurons, after fixation and washing with PBS, cells were permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100 in PBS (PBST). Cells were blocked for 1 h at room temperature with PBST containing 5% donkey serum, then incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibody in PBST. The next day, cells were incubated with secondary antibody diluted at 1:500 in PBST for 1 h at room temperature, followed by staining with 1:500 DAPI for 5 min. The coverslips were then mounted in Hydromount. Fluorescence images were captured with a Nikon Eclipse Ti2 confocal laser scanning microscope (Nikon Instruments, Inc.).

Neutral comet assay

The neutral comet assay was performed using the Comet Assay Kit (Abcam, ab238544) as described previously. 36 After treatment, hTERT astrocytes, ReN wt NPCs, and ReN GA2 NPCs were trypsinized, and the cell pellets were prepared in comet agarose on slides as per manufacturer's protocol. The slides were subjected to electrophoresis in TBE Electrophoresis Solution for 40 min at approximately 35 V on ice, then incubated with Vista Green DNA dye. Images were obtained with a Nikon Eclipse Ti2 confocal laser scanning microscope (Nikon Instruments, Inc.) using the FITC filter. Approximately 3–6 images (5–20 cells per image) were randomly taken, and the % tail DNA (100 x Tail DNA intensity/Cell DNA intensity) of 30 cells was calculated using the OpenComet plugin of ImageJ analysis software ver. 2.0.0.

Lysate preparation and western blot

hTERT Astrocytes, ReN wt NPCs or 16-18-day neurons, and ReN GA2 NPCs or 16–18-day neurons were grown on 2D 0.1% or 1% Matrigel coated 6-well plates or in transwell systems and treated as described in the DNA damage assays. After treatments and recovery time courses, media was removed and spun at 15,000 g for 10 min at 4°C to pellet dead cells and debris. Supernatant was then removed. For APOE detection, 1 mL of supernatant was concentrated using Amicon Ultra centrifugal filters (Millipore Sigma, UFC501096). 25 μL of concentrated supernatant was combined with 4% β-mercaptoethanol in 4 X Non-Reducing LDS Sample Buffer (Thermo Scientific, 84788) and used in western blotting. Cell lysates were prepared with 100 μL of ice cold RIPA lysis buffer per sample (25 mM Tris HCL pH 7.6, 150 mM NaCl, 1% NP-40, 1% sodium deoxycholate, 0.1% SDS) with 1 X protease inhibitor (Sigma-Aldrich, 04693116001), phosphatase inhibitors (1:100 dilution) (Millipore Sigma, P0044, P5724) and 25–30 U benzonase (Sigma-Aldrich, 70746-3). Total protein concentrations of the lysates were determined by Bicinchoninic Acid (BCA) assays (Thermo Scientific, 23227). Samples were prepared for western blot using a lysate dilution that would yield 25–40 µg total protein (NPCs) or 20 µg total protein (differentiated cells) with 4% β-mercaptoethanol in 2 X Novex Tris-Glycine SDS Sample Buffer (Life Technologies, LC2676). Samples were heated to 70°C for 10 min, then run on 4%-20% or 6% Novex Wedge Well Tris-Glycine mini gels (Thermo Fisher, XP04205BOX) in 1 X Tris/Glycine/SDS running buffer (Thermo Fisher, BP13414) at 80 V for 10 min, then 100 V for 2 h. The protein was transferred to a nitrocellulose membrane using the Invitrogen Power Blotter System (Thermo Fisher, PB0012) and 80% pre-made transfer buffer stock (5.85 g glycine, 11.6 g Tris, 0.375 g SDS, milliQ water to 1 L) with 20% ethanol at a constant 25 V for 45 min. For ATM and pATM, protein was transferred to PVDF membrane activated in methanol using 80% pre-made transfer buffer stock (144 g glycine, 30.3 g Tris base, milliQ water to 1 L) with 20% methanol at a constant 80 mA for 24 h in 4°C on ice. Blots were stained with Ponceau (5% acetic acid, 0.1% Ponceau stain) for five minutes to verify total protein transfer. Blots were then blocked in TBST with 5% milk or 5% BSA for one hour, and immunoblotted overnight at 4°C using the following antibodies at a 1:1000 dilution in 5% milk or 5% BSA and TBST: anti-γH2AX (Abcam, ab26350), anti-GFAP (Thermo Scientific, PA110004), anti-ATM (2C1) (Thermo Scientific, MA1-23152), anti-Ku80 (Abcam, ab80592), anti-RAD52 (Santa Cruz, sc-365341), anti-H3 (Abcam, ab1791), anti-HA (Millipore Sigma, 11867423001), anti-APOE [Full Length (FL)] (Thermo Scientific, TA805358); at 1:50000 dilution in 5% milk and TBST: anti-pATM (phospho S1981) (Abcam, ab81292); and at 1:10000 dilution in 5% milk and TBST: anti-β-Actin (Sigma-Aldrich, A5441), anti-vinculin (Thermo Scientific, 700062), and anti-GAPDH (Sigma-Aldrich, G9545). Blots were probed with appropriate secondary antibodies (anti-Mouse IgG Horseradish Peroxidase HRP, anti-Rabbit IgG HRP, or anti-Chicken IgY HRP) in a 1:10000 dilution in 5% milk and TBST for 1 h at room temperature. Blots were imaged using ECL substrate or SuperSignal West Femto Maximum Sensitivity Substrate in the Bio-Rad ChemiDoc. Bands were quantified utilizing ImageJ analysis software ver. 2.0.0.

Measurement of cell viability

Cell viability of hTERT astrocytes, ReN wt NPCs, and ReN GA2 NPCs was assessed using a LIVE/DEAD cell double staining kit (Millipore Sigma, 05441-1KT-F) following the manufacturer's instructions. This enabled the simultaneous fluorescence staining of viable and dead cells via calcein-AM and propidium iodide (PI) dyes, which stain living and dead cells, respectively. Briefly, cells seeded in 0.1% Matrigel coated Mattek dishes were treated with experimental conditions. Cells were allowed to recover for 24 h at 37°C, 5% CO2. Media was removed and cells were incubated in fresh media containing calcein-AM, PI, and Hoechst live-cell DNA stain at 37°C, 5% CO2 for 30 min. Fluorescence images were captured with a Nikon Eclipse Ti2 confocal laser scanning microscope (Nikon Instruments, Inc.).

Measurement of cellular proliferation and cytotoxicity

Cellular proliferation and cytotoxicity of hTERT astrocytes was measured using a ViaLight plus kit (Fisher Scientific, LT07-221) following the manufacturer's instructions. This enabled the measurement of ATP present in metabolically active cells. Briefly, cells seeded in 0.1% Matrigel coated white-walled clear bottom 96-well plates were treated with experimental conditions. Cell Lysis Reagent was added to extract ATP from cells followed by the addition of ATP monitoring reagent to generate a luminescent signal. Luminescence was read using a BioTek SynergyNeo2 model spectrometer. Experiments were performed in triplicate.

Image analysis and fluorescence intensity quantification

Fiji software was used to quantify the cellular MAP2 intensity, dendritic length and synapsin-I puncta. For cellular fluorescence intensity, freehand line tool was used to select the outline of a cell body with MAP2 staining as counterstained ROI. An average of three background regions with the same area as a given cell body stain was used as a control for background staining and subtracted from each image. Raw Integrated Density was used as a measurement for the cellular intensity. The density of synapsin-I puncta was quantified along a segment of primary dendrite at the length between 20-100 µm and normalized to 100 µm.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as a mean ± SEM. Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism version 9.0.0. Differences between means were assessed by Students T-test, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), or two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Tukey's multiple comparisons test. All experiments were completed with a minimum of two to three replicates unless indicated in the figure legends. p< 0.05 was considered significant.

Results

Serum starvation of astrocytes reduces baseline γH2AX expression

Previous studies have shown that astrocytes exhibit a reactive profile when cultured in media containing high concentrations of serum (FBS), 39 while astrocytes cultured in serum-free media have a genetic profile more closely aligned to in vivo “unreactive” astrocytes.40–43 To determine the optimal conditions for our experiments, we performed serum starvation on hTERT wt astrocytes over 24, 48, and 72 h with or without subsequent etoposide treatment (Supplemental Figure 1). Using γH2AX as a marker of DSBs, we found that 72-h serum starvation significantly reduced baseline DSBs and rendered the cells less responsive to etoposide treatment compared to unstarved controls (Supplemental Figure 1B, D). We saw no effect on GFAP expression regardless of serum starvation treatment (Supplemental Figure 1C, D). These results demonstrate a reduction in astrocytic baseline γH2AX expression with 72-h serum starvation, leading to reduced confounding effects upon experiments. Therefore, we used the 72-h serum starvation protocol for all experiments outlined in this study.

Human astrocyte DDR profile after DSB induction and recovery

The human astrocyte response to DNA DSBs is not well documented. We therefore performed a series of baseline studies by inducing DNA DSBs in serum-starved hTERT astrocytes with etoposide and probed western blots with antibodies for DSB repair pathway associated proteins (Figure 1). Topoisomerase II resolves DNA tangles and supercoils that may occur during DNA transcription and replication by generating transient DSBs and then re-ligating the broken DNA strands. 44 Etoposide induces the accumulation of DNA DSBs by binding DNA at topoisomerase II-DNA cleavage sites and inhibiting the re-ligation process. 45 After 72 h of serum starvation, astrocytes were incubated with 10 μM etoposide for 4 h and then allowed to recover for 0, 2, 4, 8, and 24 h. We probed cell lysates with antibodies specific for the Non-Homologous End Joining (NHEJ) pathway associated protein Ku80; the Homologous Recombination (HR) pathway associated proteins ATM, pATM, γH2AX; and the Single-Strand Annealing (SSA)/Transcription-Coupled Homologous Recombination (TC-HR) pathway associated protein RAD52. We also probed lysates for the astrocyte specific marker glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) (Figure 1A-E and Supplemental Figure 2A-E).

hTERT astrocyte DDR profile after etoposide induced DSBs. Western blot of endogenous (A) γH2AX expression, (B) endogenous pATM and total ATM expression, (C) endogenous Ku80 expression, (D) endogenous RAD52 expression, and (E) endogenous GFAP expression in hTERT astrocytes with 72-h serum starvation, treated with and without 5 µM etoposide for 4 h and allowed to recover for 24 h. Fold change in endogenous protein levels compares controls to treatment samples relative to loading controls (Vinculin or GAPDH). Error bars represent means ± SEM of three separate experiments, and the p values were determined using two-way ANOVA and Tukey's multiple comparisons test. *p < 0.05.

After DSBs occur, the HR-associated protein ATM is recruited to the damage site where it is auto-phosphorylated followed by phosphorylation of the histone variant H2AX at serine 139 to generate γH2AX.46,47 We found that total ATM expression did not change regardless of treatment or recovery time. However, pATM and γH2AX expression levels increased following etoposide damage, indicating the creation of DNA DSBs. Although typically γH2AX expression reduces over time, indicating DSB resolution, γH2AX expression remained high through 24 h following etoposide treatment, with pATM expression peaking at 8 h and substantially reducing at 24 h (Figure 1A, B and Supplemental Figure 2A, B). Ku80 and RAD52 expression levels did not significantly change with or without etoposide treatment (Figure 1C, D and Supplemental Figure 2C, D). GFAP expression levels did not significantly change with or without etoposide treatment (Figure 1E and Supplemental Figure 2E). The comet assay showed no significant tail moments at 24 h following treatment with 5 μM etoposide for 4 h, indicating resolved DNA DSBs (Supplemental Figure 3A). These differences were not due to cell viability as a toxicity assay using the ViaLight plus kit (a measure of ATP levels) revealed no significant difference in astrocyte survival with increasing doses of etoposide (0.5–20.0 μM, for 4 h followed by a 24-h recovery; Supplemental Figure 3B).

Undamaged NPCs increase γH2AX expression in response to etoposide treated astrocytes

Astrocytic reactivity is one of the first events in the cascade leading toward the progression of AD. 7 We therefore asked whether astrocytes would support or inhibit the neuronal DDR after etoposide treatment. To confirm that DNA DSBs are accumulated and that etoposide induces the DDR in hTERT wt astrocytes, ReN NPCs, and differentiated ReN neurons, we immunostained cells for γH2AX 24 h after a 4-h treatment with 5 μM etoposide (Figure 2). For these experiments we utilized wt astrocytes, and ReN GA2 NPCs as well as differentiated GA2 neurons which overexpress AβPP. Nuclei were scored as positive if they had >20 γH2AX foci. Astrocytes, ReN GA2 NPCs, and ReN GA2 neurons showed nearly 100% γH2AX foci expression at 0 h after treatment, indicating induction of the DDR following etoposide induced accumulation of DSBs. 24 h after etoposide treatment, astrocyte γH2AX foci were over 50% resolved (Figure 2A, B). However, we noticed that a large number of ReN GA2 NPCs died before fixation in the 24-h etoposide recovery group (data not shown). The NPCs that remained showed high γH2AX foci expression, indicating that the accumulated DSBs had not yet been repaired (Figure 2A, C). Differentiated ReN GA2 neurons showed nearly 100% γH2AX foci resolution 24 h after etoposide treatment, indicating DNA damage repair (Figure 2A, D).

(A) Representative images of 72-h serum starved astrocytes, GA2 NPCs, and 16–20 day differentiated GA2 neurons that were treated with and without 5 µM etoposide for 4 h and allowed to recover for 0 h and 24 h. Cells were fixed and immunostained with antibodies for γH2AX (green & insert) and, in the case of differentiated GA2 neurons, MAP2 (red). Nuclei with γH2AX foci >21 represent DNA damage positive cells in (B) astrocytes, (C) GA2 NPCs, and (D) GA2 neurons. Scale bar = 100 µm. Error bars represent means ± SEM of three separate experiments, and the p values were determined using two-way ANOVA and Tukey's multiple comparisons test. *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, ****p < 0.0001.

To investigate whether astrocytes would support or inhibit the neuronal DDR, we used a co-culture transwell system with 0.4 μm pore size. hTERT wt, ε2, ε3, or ε4 astrocytes were cultured in the insert, whereas ReN NPCs or 16–20 day differentiated neurons (wt and GA2) were cultured in the lower compartment (Figure 3A). Astrocytes and ReN wt NPCs or neurons were initially prepared and maintained separately. Astrocytes were seeded in the insert in 6-well plates with 3.5 mL of astrocyte media, serum starved for 72 h, and followed by a 4-h treatment with 5 μM etoposide. ReN wt NPCs or neurons were seeded on 1% Matrigel coated 6-well plates in 2 mL proliferation media (neurons were differentiated for 16–20 days via removal of growth factors), then treated with 5 μM etoposide for 4 h. The cell cultures were washed, combined in the transwell system, then allowed to recover in 3.5 mL proliferation or differentiation media for 24 h (Figure 3A). The 24-h recovery time period allowed sufficient time for the astrocytes to produce and secrete APOE into the co-culture media. In these experiments, no-transwell (NO-TW) refers to wells where ReN wt/GA2 NPCs or neurons were cultured and treated but were not combined with astrocyte-containing transwells for the 24-h recovery period. These were used as controls as the NPCs or neurons were not exposed to astrocyte derived APOE or any other astrocyte secreted factors at any point during the experiment. We used 5 μM etoposide in these studies because 10 μM etoposide led to a significant loss of NPCs after 24-h recovery, necessitating a reduction in etoposide concentration.

We probed western blots of the ReN NPC and neuronal cell lysates with antibodies specific for γH2AX (Figure 3 and Supplemental Figure 4). We found that in these transwell experiments, when APOE overexpressing astrocytes were pre-treated with etoposide, untreated ReN wt NPCs showed significantly increased expression of γH2AX compared to controls when all APOE allele data were pooled (Figure 3B). When each APOE allele was analyzed separately, untreated ReN wt NPCs showed significantly increased expression of γH2AX when astrocytes overexpressing APOE ε4 were treated with etoposide (Figure 3C and Supplemental Figure 4A). Untreated APOE overexpressing astrocytes had no effect on ReN wt NPC γH2AX expression, regardless of ReN wt NPC treatment status (Figure 3B-D and Supplemental Figure 4A). Differentiated ReN wt neurons showed no difference in γH2AX expression regardless of astrocyte APOE allele or etoposide treatment status (Figure 3E and Supplemental Figure 4B).

These results together indicate that the DNA of ReN wt NPCs is damaged by diffusible factors released from etoposide treated astrocytes, and that this effect is greatest when the astrocytes carry an APOE ε4 genotype.

Neuronal Aβ does not negatively affect the astrocytic DDR and the support of neuronal DDR

ReN GA2 NPCs overproduce Aβ40 and Aβ42 in response to the accumulation of DSBs. 36 We therefore asked whether astrocytic induction of neuronal DNA damage was affected by the presence of enhanced levels of Aβ produced in the target neurons. We exposed ReN GA2 NPCs or 16–20 day differentiated GA2 neurons to hTERT astrocytes cultured in the upper chamber of the transwell, with and without etoposide treatment. We probed western blots of the GA2 cell lysates with antibodies specific for γH2AX (Figure 4 and Supplemental Figure 5). We found that etoposide treated astrocytes significantly increased γH2AX expression of the lower chamber ReN GA2 NPCs compared to no-transwell controls when all APOE allele data were pooled (Figure 4A). This effect was even stronger than that seen with wt ReN NPCs (Figure 3B). The specific APOE allele carried by the etoposide treated astrocytes (ε2, ε3, ε4) made no significant difference in ReN GA2 NPC γH2AX expression (Figure 4B and Supplemental Figure 5A). While there was a slight trend for APOE expression to dampen baseline γH2AX expression in the ReN GA2 NPCs, the effect was not statistically significant (Figure 4A-C and Supplemental Figure 5A). Fully differentiated ReN GA2 neurons showed no difference in γH2AX expression regardless of astrocyte presence or treatment status (Figure 4D and Supplemental Figure 5B). These results indicate that while baseline DNA damage is higher in differentiated ReN GA2 neurons than their NPC precursors, the impact of bystander astrocytes is stronger on the DNA integrity of ReN GA2 NPCs than their differentiated counterparts. In either state, Aβ over-producing ReN GA2 cells are more vulnerable than the wild type cells.

Damaged astrocytes affect the neuronal DDR, but not via APOE

To determine whether APOE secreted by the etoposide treated astrocytes was responsible, in whole or in part, for the increase in ReN GA2 NPC γH2AX seen in Figure 4A&B, we used siRNA to knock down endogenous APOE in the hTERT wt astrocytes (Figure 5 and Supplemental Figure 6). Western blot and immunostaining confirmed APOE KD in the astrocytes (Supplemental Figure 6A, B). APOE knockdown (KD) hTERT astrocytes were then cultured in the insert of the transwell system while ReN wt or GA2 NPCs were cultured in the lower compartment (Figure 5A). We treated astrocytes with 5 μM etoposide for 4 h. We then probed lysates from ReN wt NPC and GA2 NPC samples with antibodies specific for γH2AX. We found γH2AX expression in either ReN wt or GA2 NPC was not significantly different regardless of APOE allele status of the astrocytes in the transwell. Nonetheless, γH2AX expression in untreated ReN wt NPCs increased in transwells containing etoposide treated wt astrocytes or APOE KD astrocytes when compared to no-transwell controls (Figure 5B and Supplemental Figure 7). Similarly, untreated ReN GA2 NPC γH2AX expression increased in transwells containing etoposide treated APOE KD astrocytes compared to no-transwell controls (Figure 5C and Supplemental Figure 7). These results indicate that etoposide treated astrocytes, independent of their level of APOE expression, enhance γH2AX expression, and thus DNA damage, in ReN wt NPCs or GA2 NPCs.

Primary cultured mouse astrocytes respond to DNA damage by releasing neurotoxic substances

In order to further confirm our findings, we obtained conditioned medium (CM) from cultures of astrocytes harvested from the P5 mouse cortex (see Methods, Supplemental Figure 8). In these experiments, DNA damage was induced by inhibiting DNA repair with the ataxia-telangiectasia mutated (ATM) kinase inhibitor, KU-60019 (1 µM), applied for 48 h. The conditioned medium from untreated- (CM) or KU-treated (KM) astrocyte cultures was collected and used to substitute the medium of naïve mouse neuronal cultures at concentrations ranging from 10% to 20%. Application of 10% CM did little to alter either dendritic structure or synaptic density, while 10% KM treatment led to significant decreases in both (Figure 6A, C, D). Further increases in the concentration of KM led to little additional damage (Figure 6).

Discussion

Our results demonstrate that astrocytes in which the DNA has been damaged produce a bystander effect that induces DNA damage in nearby neuronal cells, evidenced by increases in γH2AX expression. It has been well documented that activated astrocytes release pro-inflammatory cytokines, chemokines, and ROS which can contribute to neuroinflammation,48,49 damaging NPCs and neurons in close proximity. However, no other studies have explored how astrocytic DNA DSBs affect the DNA of nearby neurons or neuronal precursors and their DDR. Our research identifies a potential early cascade of events that could contribute to the development of AD pathology decades before the onset of cognitive impairment. The precise identity of the molecules involved in this phenomenon remains unknown.

Since our previous studies revealed a direct link between oxidative neuronal DNA damage, DNA DSBs, and increased neuronal Aβ production, 36 we therefore asked whether astrocytes, which demonstrate an AD-associated reactive profile in single cell transcriptomic data sets, 33 might also play a role in this sequence of events. We first found that hTERT astrocytes treated with etoposide increased pATM and γH2AX expression, indicating a robust DDR in reaction to the etoposide-induced accumulation of DSBs, while NHEJ-associated Ku80, TC-HR/SSA-associated RAD52, and total ATM levels remained unchanged. Total GFAP expression also remained unchanged (Figure 1A-E). We also tested whether astrocytic derived APOE affected the human NPC or neuronal DDR in an allele specific manner using the transwell co-culture system. Wild type, APOE ε4, and APOE KD astrocyte genotypes all contributed to an increase in ReN wt NPC γH2AX expression after astrocytes were treated with etoposide (Figures 3B, 3C, 5B) whereas APOE ε2 or ε3 genotypes did not. The induced DNA damage, indicated by this increased γH2AX expression, was more pronounced in the untreated ReN GA2 NPCs which hyper-secrete Aβ40 and Aβ42, and was seen with astrocytes of all genotypes (Figures 4A, 4B, 5C). Neither ReN wt nor GA2 differentiated neurons increased γH2AX expression when co-cultured with etoposide treated astrocytes of any kind (Figures 3E and 4D). These results suggest that APOE does not affect the ReN NPC or neuronal DDR regardless of allelic expression. We propose that the hyper-secretion of Aβ40 and Aβ42, especially in response to DNA damage, as noted in our previous research,, 36 significantly contributes to the vulnerability of these cells to the astrocytic factors (Figure 7). More investigation is necessary in order to illuminate the specific factors that astrocytes secrete in response to DNA damage. Understanding how these factors are uptaken by NPCs and how they dysregulate the NPC DDR may offer an opportunity for therapeutics, treatment to inhibit NPC and neuronal loss, and potential AD prevention.

ReN wt NPC and GA2 NPC γH2AX expression. Results of co-culture transwell experiments from Figures 3–5 where black arrows indicate changes in ReN wt and GA2 NPC γH2AX protein expression and lines indicate no changes in NPC γH2AX expression when etoposide damaged or undamaged NPCs were cultured with etoposide damaged or undamaged astrocytes.

Increased γH2AX expression in untreated ReN GA2 NPCs in the transwell system with etoposide treated astrocytes indicates that damaged astrocytes secrete factors that damage the DNA of bystander neuronal cells (Figures 4A, 4B, 5C). In regards to ReN wt NPCs, only wt, APOE ε4, and APOE KD astrocytes treated with etoposide induced increased γH2AX expression in the NPCs (Figures 3B, 3C, 5B). Etoposide treated astrocytes of all APOE genotypes (including KD and wt) induced increased γH2AX expression in the ReN GA2 NPCs (Figures 4A, 4B, 5C). By contrast, terminally differentiated ReN wt or GA2 neurons were resistant to the damaged astrocyte effect (Figures 3E, 4D). The wt astrocytes express an extremely low amount of endogenous APOE, with siAPOE knockdown cells expressing even lower endogenous APOE (Supplemental Figure 6A, B). Overexpression of APOE itself, regardless of allele type, did not significantly affect γH2AX expression in NPCs (wt or GA2). Therefore, the increase in ReN GA2 NPC γH2AX expression in response to etoposide treated astrocytes could be attributed to either 1: reduced secreted APOE from the astrocytes or 2: increased Aβ40 and Aβ42 secretion from the ReN GA2 NPCs, or both. This implies that overexpressed APOE provides protection for vulnerable populations of neuronal progenitors similar to ReN GA2 NPCs, though not in an allele specific manner. Our findings also further implicate a significant effect of Aβ40 and Aβ42 upon the NPC DDR as it dramatically increases NPC DNA damage in response to astrocytic DNA damage in co-culture.

In a functional DSB repair system, the majority of γH2AX foci resolve within 24 h, indicating efficient repair. A decrease in γH2AX expression can indicate either a reduced initial response to DSBs, or enhanced repair. Residual yH2AX foci can indicate lethal DSB lesions. 50 In our study which quantified both γH2AX expression and the number of foci, the slight reduction seen in etoposide treated ReN wt NPC, ReN GA2 NPC, and neuronal GA2 γH2AX expression in the transwell system with untreated astrocytes indicates that these cells may provide either DDR enhancing or inhibiting factors to damaged NPCs and neurons. The trends towards reduced DNA damage in ReN cells (particularly GA2) exposed to untreated astrocytes overexpressing APOE hints at a modest APOE protective effect.51,52 While these differences did not reach statistical significance, such a finding would be in line with work from other cell types in knockout mice and argue for the pursuit of additional experiments on the potential DNA protective effect of APOE in neurons (Figures 3D, 3E, 4C, 4D).

The response of neurons to astrocytic DNA damage is not solely reserved to increased γH2AX expression. As evidenced in our study, ATM inhibition induced DNA damage in astrocytes, which elicited a response in primary cultured neurons treated with their conditioned medium (Figure 6). Such treatment led to a reduction in both dendrite length and density with a 1:5 dilution of the astrocyte conditioned medium (Figure 6A-D). While untreated astrocyte conditioned media alone negatively affects neuronal cultures at high concentrations (>50%, Figure 6B, C), this detrimental effect on neuronal viability is likely due to the absence of neuron specific nutrients in astrocyte media. Importantly, the significant loss of dendritic spines, especially in the cortex and hippocampus of AD patients, contributes to the synaptic dysfunction associated with AD pathology. 53

Results from this study indicate that vulnerable NPCs such as ReN GA2 NPCs, with mutations that promote Aβ40 and Aβ42 hyper-secretion, are much more sensitive to etoposide induced DSBs, leading to residual γH2AX foci retention, incomplete repair, and cell loss (Figures 2A, 2C, 4A, 4B). Our data reveal that vulnerable NPCs also respond to astrocytic DNA damage by activating their own DDR, indicated by increased expression of γH2AX (Figure 4A, B). More research, such as the use of iPSC-derived astrocytes, is required to illuminate the complex interactions in human astrocyte-NPC DDR dynamics, including those involving the hyper secretion of Aβ40 and Aβ42 as an NPC response to DSBs. Failure to efficiently repair DSBs in NPCs contributes to defects in the remaining neuronal progenitor pool, leading to dysfunctional differentiated neurons and potentially contributing to a negative feedback loop of early AD pathology. Identifying and protecting vulnerable populations of NPCs from neurotoxic factors produced by damaged astrocytes could be a pathway towards successful AD prevention.
