Potential probiotic Lactiplantibacillus plantarum strains alleviate TNF-α by regulating ADAM17 protein and ameliorate gut integrity through tight junction protein expression in in vitro model
Authors: M Bidyarani Devi, Anupam Bhattacharya, Arun Kumar, Chingtham Thanil Singh, Santanu Das, Hridip Kumar Sarma, Ashis K Mukherjee, Mojibur R Khan
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLactiplantibacillus species are extensively studied for their ability to regulate host immune responses and functional therapeutic potentials. Nevertheless, there is a lack of understanding on the mechanisms of interactions with the hosts during immunoregulatory activities.MethodsTwo Lactiplantibacillus plantarum strains MKMB01 and MKMB02 were tested for probiotic potential following Indian Council of Medical Research (ICMR) guidelines. Human colorectal adenocarcinoma cells such as HT-29, caco-2, and human monocytic cell THP-1 were also used to study the potential of MKMB01 and MKMB02 in regulating the host immune response when challenged with enteric pathogen Salmonella enterica typhimurium. Cells were pre-treated with MKMB01 and MKMB02 for 4 h and then stimulated with Salmonella. qRT-PCR and ELISA were used to analyze the genes and protein expression. Confocal microscopy and field emission scanning electron microscopy (FESEM) were used to visualize the effects. An Agilent Seahorse XF analyzer was used to determine real-time mitochondrial functioning.ResultsBoth probiotic strains could defend against Salmonella by maintaining gut integrity via expressing tight junction proteins (TJPs), MUC-2, and toll-like receptors (TLRs) negative regulators such as single Ig IL-1-related receptor (SIGIRR), toll-interacting protein (Tollip), interleukin-1 receptor-associated kinase (IRAK)-M, A20, and anti-inflammatory transforming growth factor-β and interleukin-10. Both strains also downregulated the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines/chemokines interleukin-1β, monocyte chemoattractant protein (MCP)-1, tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), interleukin 6, and nitric oxide (NO). Moreover, TNF-α sheddase protein, a disintegrin and metalloproteinase domain 17 (ADAM17), and its regulator iRhom2 were downregulated by both strains. Moreover, the bacteria also ameliorated Salmonella-induced mitochondrial dysfunction by restoring bioenergetic profiles, such as non-mitochondrial respiration, spare respiratory capacity (SRC), basal respiration, adenosine triphosphate (ATP) production, and maximal respiration.ConclusionsMKMB01 and MKMB02 can reduce pathogen-induced gut-associated disorders and therefore should be further explored for their probiotic potential.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12964-024-01900-7.Keywords: Probiotics, Anti-inflammatory, Immunomodulatory, Oxidative stress, Gut integrity, ADAM17 and iRhom2

Introduction

The gut microbiota is responsible for important functions in the body, including regulation of the immune system and metabolic balance, the breakdown of dietary fibers, and defense against harmful pathogens. Lactiplantibacillus/Lactobacillus species are among the indigenous microorganisms found in the gastrointestinal tract. They have been studied globally for their probiotic properties in maintaining gut integrity through immunomodulatory functions and competitive exclusion of pathogens [1]. Lactobacillus rhamnosus GG, which has been extensively used for scientific research and clinical applications has also been reported to attenuate Salmonella infection by reducing tumor necrosis factor α (TNF-α) and myeloperoxidase, and maintaining IL-10 levels in specific pathogen-free (SPF) C57BL/6 mice colon [1]. In the gastrointestinal tract, Salmonella spp. has been linked to salmonellosis, causing both systemic and localized infections through the transduction of various pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β, IL-6, IL-8, and TNF-α in macrophages and intestinal epithelial cells [2–5].

The World Health Organization (WHO) defines probiotics as “live microorganisms that, when administered in adequate amounts, confer a health benefit on the host” [6]. Probiotics play a crucial role in regulating immune responses, defending against pathogens, and reducing pathogen-induced inflammation [7]. Some of the most frequently used probiotics are from the genera Lactiplantibacillus, Bifidobacterium, Enterococcus, Streptococcus, Saccharomyces, and Bacillus [8].Escherichia coli Nissle 1917 and specific strains of Pediococcus, Leuconostoc, and Lactococcus have also been used as probiotics [9]. Among the genera, various strains of Lactiplantibacillus have been studied worldwide for their beneficial effects against different gut-associated disorders in vitro and in vivo [7, 10–13].

Salmonella infection-induced intestinal inflammation escalating the risk of inflammatory bowel diseases (IBDs) by regulating multiple pro-inflammatory signaling pathways including TNF-α pathways [14]. TNF-α is a pleiotropic pro-inflammatory cytokine and adipokine involved in aberrant regulation, associated with inflammation and the pathogenesis of several diseases [15]. TNF-α is released as pro-TNF-α during inflammation in two components (transmembrane TNF-α, mTNF-α and soluble TNF-α, sTNF-α) by macrophages/monocytes, lymphocytes, and intestinal epithelial cells (IECs). Pro-TNF-α undergoes proteolytic cleavage by a disintegrin and metalloprotease 17 (ADAM17), also known as tumor necrosis factor-α-converting enzyme (TACE), releasing ectodomain (sTNF-α), which is commonly known as TNF-α [16]. ADAM17 also regulates the expression of genes by various transmembrane protein cleavages that release ectodomain from membrane-bound pro-proteins.

Cytokine release by the cells is induced by lipopolysaccharide (LPS), present on the outer membrane of Salmonella. It is considered the major virulence factor responsible for colonization, motility, and invasion [17]. LPS induces intestinal inflammation by activating Toll-like receptors (TLRs) signaling [18]. Recognition of LPS by the TLRs activates the nuclear factors κB (NF-κB) pathway that produces inflammatory cytokines/chemokines [18]. While the intestinal mucus layer and tight junction proteins (TJPs) act as an integral physiologic and mechanical barrier against Salmonella infection [13], disruption of the mucus layer and TJPs damages the gut barriers and compromises gut integrity. Aberrant gut integrity permits infiltration of the luminal content into the intestinal mucosa, activating various inflammation and circulatory impairments, leading to sepsis. On the other hand, various in vitro studies have demonstrated that IECs such as caco-2 and HT-29 cell lines are critical in vitro models for selecting active probiotic strains based on their functional properties [25–27]. THP-1 cells have also been used as a model for human monocytes [19].

This study determines the probiotic potential of MKMB01 and MKMB02 in inhibiting Salmonella-induced inflammation in vitro. Both MKMB01 and MKMB02 reduced TNF-α by regulating ADAM17 expression and minimizing the proteolytic cleavage of pro-TNF-α that reduces the release of active soluble TNF-α into the extracellular domain. In addition, Lactiplantibacillus strains prevented the loss of gut integrity by maintaining the expression of mucin-2 and TJPs.

Materials and methods

Bacterial strains and cell culture conditions

L. plantarum MKMB01 was isolated from fermented fruit beverage (Atingba) of Leimaram, (24.7157° N, 93.7854° E) Manipur, India, which is prepared traditionally by the Chiru tribe. L. plantarum MKMB02 was isolated from curd prepared by the Nepali community of Serou (24.2674° N, 93.8755° E), Manipur, India. These two Lactiplantibacillus strains were isolated using De Man, Rogosa, and Sharpe (MRS) agar (Himedia, India). The pure cultures were cryopreserved at -80 °C with 20% (v/v) glycerol as the cryoprotectant. The strains were morphologically characterized and identified based on full-length 16S rRNA genes. The BLAST tool was used to compare the 16S rRNA gene sequences of the two bacterial isolates with L. plantarum type strains (accession nos. MKMB01-OQ629987 and MKMB02-OQ629988) in the NCBI database.

The pathogenic strains, Salmonella enterica typhimurium MTCC 98, E. coli K12 ATCC 29,425, Klebsiella pneumoniae MTCC 109, and Staphylococcus aureus MTCC 3160 were cultured on nutrient (NA) agar (Himedia, India) and Listeria monocytogenes MTCC 657, Yersinia enterocolitica ATCC 23,715 and Enterococcus faecalis ATCC 47,077 were cultured using biosafety level II (BSL-II) on brain heart infusion (BHI) agar (Himedia, India). Before the experiments, the bacteria were cultured overnight and adjusted to the appropriate cell density (CFU/mL). The cultures were then washed three times with phosphate buffered saline (PBS). Handling of microorganisms was done following appropriate procedures and protocols given by the Institutional Biosafety Committee (IBSC), IASST.

Human colorectal adenocarcinoma (caco-2) and HT-29 cells were maintained in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium (DMEM) with 10% FBS and 1% pen/step and human leukemia monocytic (THP-1) cells in Roswell Park Memorial Institute (RPMI) 1640 at 37 °C and 5% CO2. The media were changed every alternative day until the desired confluency was achieved. To understand the downstream biokinetic relation of immune cells to IEC responsiveness to stressors, cell line-derived IEC caco-2, and macrophage-like THP-1 cells were co-cultured to mimic intestinal conditions with reference to the in vitro co-culture model described earlier by Kampfer et. al., 2017 [20]. To induce differentiation of THP-1 cells into macrophage-like cells, the cells were grown overnight in a medium containing 0.1 µM of phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA) (P8139, Merck, Germany). Caco-2 and HT-29 cells were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC), USA, and THP-1 cells were from the National Centre for Cell Science (NCCS), Pune, India.

For co-culturing caco-2 and THP-1 cells, the caco-2 cells were seeded on transwell inserts with 0.4 µm2 pore size (CLS3450, Corning, USA) and THP-1 cells in the basolateral compartment.

Tolerance to gastrointestinal conditions

Tolerance to gastrointestinal stress was tested as described previously [21] with some modifications. Briefly, MKMB01 and MKMB02 were cultured at 37 °C, and when the cell density reached 1 × 109 CFU per/mL, bacteria were centrifuged and washed three times with PBS. The bacterial cells were re-suspended in PBS of different pHs [3 mg/mL pepsin (9001756, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) at pH 2; 1 mg/mL pancreatin (P1625, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) at pH 8; and 2% oxgall (212820, Becton, Dickinson (BD), USA) solution at pH 7.4]. Culture in pH 7.4 without treatment was used as control. The pH of PBS was adjusted using 2 M HCl and 2 M NaOH. Resistance was assessed as cell viability in log10 CFU/mL, evaluated after incubating anaerobically (Whitley A35 anaerobic Workstation, UK) for periods of 0 and 3 hours with pepsin, and 0 and 4 hours with pancreatin and oxgall.

Antibiotic test

The antibiotic susceptibility of MKMB01 and MKMB02 was determined by following previously described methods with some modifications [22, 23]. Briefly, a 15 mL MRS agar-containing plate was overlayed with 5 mL of soft agar (1% agar) containing 100 µL of 109 CFU/mL. The plates were kept at room temperature until solidified. Antibiotic discs (HX022, Himedia, India) containing (ampicillin (10 µg), chloramphenicol (25 µg), penicillin-G (1 unit), streptomycin (10 µg), sulphatriad (300 µg), and tetracycline (25 µg) were then placed on the agar and incubated at 37ºC. After 24 hours of incubation, inhibition zones were measured and assessed according to manufacturer’s instructions.

Antimicrobial test

Antimicrobial activity of MKMB01 and MKMB02 were tested as previously described [24] against pathogenic strains S. typhimurium, E. coli K12, K. pneumoniae, S. aureus, L. monocytogenes, Y. enterolytica, and E. faecalis with some modifications. In summary, cultures grown overnight were subjected to centrifugation at a speed of 10,000 rpm for a duration of 10 minutes and the supernatant was collected. The antimicrobial activity was tested using the well-diffusion method. A volume of 1 × 109 CFU/mL of the pathogenic strains was mixed with soft agar (1%, v/v) and overlayed onto the appropriate agar plates for incubation. After the plates were solidified, 100 µL of the bacterial supernatant was added to the punctured area and incubated at 37ºC for 24 hours. Growth-free inhibition zones were recorded around the wells and assessed according to the Clinical & Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) guidelines.

Auto-aggregation and hydrophobicity assay

Auto-aggregation and hydrophobicity assays of MKMB01 and MKMB02 were performed using the previously described method with some modifications [25]. Auto-aggregation was monitored after 24 hours of incubation of the bacteria in a glass tube. The cultures were mixed by vortexing and kept at 37 °C. After 4 hours, absorbance was measured at 660 nm (Basic Eppendrof Biospectrophotometer, Germany), and the auto-aggregation percentage (AAg%) was measured.

The adhesion of cells to hexadecane droplets was used to determine the hydrophobicity of the bacteria strains. Bacterial cultures were grown overnight in MRS agar, washed, and collected by centrifugation. Bacterial pellets were further re-suspended at a concentration of 1 × 109 CFU/mL in 5 mL of Ringer solution (96724, Sigma-Aldrich, USA). The percentages of hydrophobicity were calculated after measuring absorbance at 600 nm for the lower phase after 30 min of mixing with an equal volume of bacterial suspension and n-hexadecane.

Antioxidant activity assay

The antioxidant activity of bacterial cells and cell lysates was evaluated according to a previously described method [26]. The bacteria were cultured to 1 × 109 CFU/mL and washed thrice with PBS. Washed bacteria were sonicated for 3 minutes at 30 kHz and then transferred to an ice bath for 1 minute. The process was repeated twice. After sonication, the cell lysates were collected by centrifugation at 4 °C. PBS was used as the control. Absorbance was recorded at 517 nm using a UV/VIS spectrophotometer (Multiskan Go, Thermo Scientific, USA). Antioxidant activity % was calculated using the formula:

Antioxidant activity % = [(Control − Sample)/Control] × 100.

Adhesion to human colorectal adenocarcinoma HT-29 cells

Adhesions of MKMB01 and MKMB02 were analyzed using HT-29 cells following the previously reported method [27]. Solutions of 1 × 109 CFU/mL of bacteria in DMEM with 10% FBS without antibiotics was added to the HT-29 cells after they were washed three times, and the solutions were then incubated at 37 ℃ for 4 hours with 5% CO2. The treated cells were then washed thrice with PBS to remove unadhered bacteria. After trypsinization, the bacterial cells were plated over MRS agar. Bacterial cell density was recorded after incubating for 48 hours at 37 ℃. The prepared bacteria at 1 × 109 CFU/mL was used as the control.

Field Emission Scanning Electron Microscopy (FESEM)

Qualitative assessment of adhesion and biofilm production of MKMB01 and MKMB02 was studied using a scanning electron microscope following the previously described method with slight modification [28, 29]. For the adhesion assay, cells were grown on sterile coverslips until confluency. After washing thrice with PBS, 1 × 109 CFU/mL of bacteria in DMEM with 10% FBS without antibiotic was added into wells and further incubated at 37 ℃ for 1 hour with 5% CO2. The colonized HT-29 cells on coverslips were washed thrice and then fixed using 4% paraformaldehyde solution for 10 minutes. Cells were dehydrated with an ascending ethanol gradient, air-dried, and coated with gold particles.

To analyze biofilm production by MKMB01 and MKMB02, 48 hours-grown bacterial slides were washed twice with PBS to remove the medium. Bacterial cells were then fixed using 4% paraformaldehyde solution for 10 minutes, dehydrated in an ascending gradient of ethanol, air-dried, placed on an SEM stub, and coated with gold particles. The images of bacteria MKMB01 and MKMB02 that had adhered to the HT-29 cells and biofilm production were taken with a Sigma Field Emission Scanning Electron Microscope (FESEM) (Zeiss SIGMA VP-FESEM, Zeiss, Germany).

Transepithelial Electrical Resistance (TEER) assay

The TEER assay is a quantitative technique to measure the integrity of tight junction dynamics with in vitro models. It was performed as described previously [30]. Briefly, caco-2 cells were grown on polyester transwell inserts (Corning, Germany) with a pore size of 0.4 µm2. The confluent cells were treated with MKMB01 and MKMB02 for 4 hours at 1 × 109 CFU/mL, followed by treatment with Salmonella (1 × 109 CFU/mL) for 6 hours. The resistance across each cell monolayer was periodically measured using an ERS-2 voltometer (Millicell-Merck, Germany) for 6 hours after Salmonella treatment. Cells without treatment were used as controls.

Determination of extracellular cytokines from treated cells

Cytokines released by treated cells (caco-2, HT-29, or THP-1 or co-cultured THP-1 and caco-2) after Salmonella treatment were monitored in cell supernatants. For the treatment, caco-2, HT-29, and THP-1 cells were grown in 6-well culture plates (CC7682-7506, Eppendorf CytoOne® Plate, India) and maintained until confluent. After confluency was reached, cells were pre-treated with MKMB01 and MKMB02 for 4 hours at 1 × 109 CFU/mL and then stimulated with Salmonella at a concentration of 1 × 109 CFU/mL for 6 hours. After the treatment, cell-free supernatants were collected and analyzed for the release of cytokine.

Caco-2 cells were grown on transwell inserts for the co-culture until confluency according to a previously described method [20]. Before the experiment, THP-1 cells were differentiated overnight using PMA at a concentration of 0.1 µM. The differentiated cells were detached and plated onto the basolateral compartment at a density of 1 × 106 cells/well. After 1 hour of re-attachment, MKMB01 and MKMB02 were added to the caco-2 cells, which were then incubated for 4 hours, and treated with Salmonella for 6 hours. The cell-free supernatant was collected from the transwell inserts and from the basolateral compartments of the co-cultured plates to analyze the release of cytokines in response to Salmonella-treatment. Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay kits [KHC3011 (TNF-α), and BMS213-2 (IL-6) Invitrogen, USA] were used for the analyses.

Nitric Oxide (NO) assay

NO is a diatomic free radical that has a very brief lifespan in biological systems and is released by several epithelial and immune cells during inflammation. The Griess reagent was used to measure NO concentrations in the supernatants of treated cells (G7921, Invitrogen, USA) [31] at 0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7 and 8 hours post-stimulation by Salmonella. The Griess reagent is comprised of a 1% solution of sulfanilamide in 5% phosphoric acid and 0.1% naphthyl ethylenediamine dihydrochloride in deionized water. The concentration of NO was monitored by blending an equal amount of Griess reagent and supernatant. The ELISA reader measured light absorption at 548 nm, and the calibration curve was plotted according to the manufactures’s protocol. Cells without treatment were used as controls.

Flow cytometry-based analysis of apoptosis using annexin V and propidium iodide

Apoptosis of caco-2 cells was assayed using the Annexin V: propidium iodide (PI) method with some modifications [32]. All untreated and treated caco-2 cells were resuspended in Annexin V-FITC (A13199, Invitrogen, USA) and propidium iodide (25535164, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) containing binding buffer. After washing three times with PBS and kept at room temperature for 30 minutes, they were analyzed with a flow cytometer (BD). Unstained and Annexin V or propidium iodide-stained cells were used as controls.

Determination of intracellular Reactive Oxygen Species (ROS) level

The intracellular ROS level was analyzed using dichloro-dihydro-fluorescein diacetate (DCFDA) as described earlier [33]. For the study, caco-2 cells were cultured in 6-well plates until reaching confluency. All bacterial cultures were prepared at a 1 × 109 CFU/mL concentration. MKMB01 and MKMB02 were used in the treatment of the confluent cells for 4 hours and then stimulated with Salmonella for 8 hours. Cells without treatment were used as the control. After 30 minutes of incubation with DCFDA (287810, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) (10 µM), cells were collected and analyzed using a flow cytometer (BD Bioscience, USA).

Confocal microscopic analysis

The potential of MKMB01 and MKMB02 in maintaining gut integrity with Salmonella treatment was analyzed using confocal microscopy. Caco-2 cells were seeded onto chamber slides (PEZGS0816, Merck, Germany) and pre-treated with MKMB01 and MKMB02 for 4 hours. Slides were then treated with Salmonella for 6 hours at a concentration of 1 × 109 CFU/mL. To analyze the expression of ADAM17, caco-2, and THP-1 cells were co-cultured and treated with MKMB01 and MKMB02 for 4 hours and then stimulated with Salmonella for 6 hours. Cells were stained following the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, after the treatment, PBS-washed cells were fixed using 4% paraformaldehyde solution for 15 minutes, permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 (9036195, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) for 10 minutes, and blocked with 2% BSA pre-prepared using PBS. After 1 hour, the cells are then incubated with primary antibody at room temperature for 3 hours. Cells were then incubated at room temperature with Alexa fluor-conjugated secondary antibody for 1 hour, which was followed by a 5-minutes incubation with ′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) at room temperature. Images were captured using a confocal microscope (Leica DMi, Germany).

Cell mito stress assay

The mitochondrial function of cells treated with MKMB01 and MKMB02 probiotics was performed using the Cell Mito Stress Test kit (Agilent Technologies, USA), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Before the experiment, ≈ 5 × 104 cells were cultured using a seahorse 24-well cell culture microplate. After 90–95% confluency was achieved, cells were pre-treated with approx. 2 × 107 CFU of MKMB01 and MKMB02 for 4 hours, which was followed by Salmonella treatment (≈ 2 × 107 CFU). After treatment, the media was replaced with an assay medium (RPMI, 2 mM glutamine, 10 mM glucose, and 1 mM pyruvate, pH 7.4). Before the assay, the sensor cartridge was hydrated with an Agilent Seahorse XF calibrant. It was loaded with oligomycin (1 µM / 56 µL), FCCP (1 µM / 62 µL), and rotenone/antimycin (0.5 µM / 69 µL) in the respective port as described in the manufacturer’s protocol. Mitochondrial function was measured directly from the cell’s oxygen consumption rate (OCR). Cells without treatment were used as controls.

Total RNA isolation and cDNA synthesis

Caco-2 cells, HT-29 cells, and cells co-cultured with THP-1 were pre-treated with MKMB01 and MKMB02 for 4 hours, before being treated with Salmonella for 6 hours and collected. RNA was isolated using Trizol (15596026, Invitrogen, USA) and quantified by measuring light absorbance at OD260/280 using the nanodrop technique (Thermo Scientific, USA). Samples with a ratio of 1.8-2.0 were used for cDNA synthesis. A Verso cDNA Synthesis kit (AB1453B, Invitrogen, USA) was used for cDNA synthesis and the product was kept at -80˚C until further use. All sample preparations for the qRT-PCR were carried out using Eppendrof epMotion 5073 (Germany).

Real-Time (RT)-PCR analysis

RT-PCR was executed in the CFX96 RT-PCR system (Bio-Rad, USA). Each PCR reaction contained 10 µL SYBR Green (1725124, Bio-Rad, USA), 1 µL cDNA template, 0.4 µL of 10 µM primer and 8.2 µL nuclease free water (NFW) in a total reaction volume of 20 µL. PCR reactions were performed according to the annealing temperature of each primer pair. β-actin expression was used to normalize in each sample, and the ΔCt of each sample was calculated (CT target − CT reference). Untreated control culture cells were used for calibration, and the ΔΔCt method [34] was used to calculate the difference between treated and controls. Finally, the gene expression data is presented as the relative fold change to the negative control group after normalization with an endogenous reference gene (β-actin). Each RT-PCR reaction was performed in triplicate. All primers used in the qRT-PCR are listed in Supplementary Table 6.

Whole genome sequencing and assembly

Whole genome sequencing of the bacterial strains was performed by a high throughput Illumina MiSeq sequencing platform (in-house). Sequencing libraries were prepared using the Illumina DNA Prep Kit (20060059 & 20060060, Illumina, Inc., USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, high-quality DNA was first fragmented using bead-linked transposomes. Index adapters (Set A 20091654, Illumina UD Indexes, Illumina, Inc., USA) were then attached to the fragmented DNA, and sequenced using MiSeq V2 reagent kits (300 cycles) with the 2 × 150 bp paired end method (MS1022002, Illumina Inc., USA).

The sequenced data was then processed to generate FASTQ files. The reads were assessed for quality by analyzing factors such as the distribution of quality scores, average base content per read, GC distribution, presence of PCR amplification issues, overrepresented sequences, and the presence of adapters. After conducting a thorough quality analysis, the sequences were trimmed to retain only the high-quality reads suitable for further analysis. The adapter removal process was carried out using fastq mcf (v- 1.04.803). To assess the alignment percentages, the processed reads were aligned against the genomes of viruses, bacteria, and archaea using the Burrows-Wheeler Aligner (BWA) software (version 0.7.12) [35]. De-novo assembly was performed using a spades assembler (v1.2) [36], and the assembled scaffolds were subjected to additional downstream analysis. To identify the ribosomal genes (rps), the assembled sequence underwent screening using the rMLST approach [37].

Gene prediction and functional annotation of the genome

The genome was annotated using RASTk [37] and Prokka [38] to get a comprehensive insight into the list of genes in the genomes of L. plantarum MKMBSO1 and MKMBSO2. Briefly, ARAGORN [38], Prodigal [39], and GeneMarkS-2+ [40] annotated the genes associated with tRNA, rRNA, and protein-coding sequences. Protein-coding genes were categorized based on domain-based search tools like HMMER and Pfam [41, 42], and the TIGRFAMs [43] databases. The annotated genes were subjected to comparisons with a cluster of orthologous groups. The functional annotation of these groups was carried out using eggNOG [44], KEGG pathways, and SMART & PFAM domains to gain an understanding of the complex biological functions. BlastKOALA [45] was also used to annotate genes based on the KEGG database. Genes were selected and analyzed based on their functional properties related to probiotic attributes, including the ability to respond to stress, adhere to host cells, tolerate acidic environments, and resist bile.

Identification of secondary metabolites and biosynthetic gene clusters

To identify the secondary metabolite biosynthesis gene clusters in the genome, Antismash [46] was used. This software uses a pipeline that detects gene clusters by analyzing signatures of secondary metabolites and conducts the comparative analysis using secondary metabolism clusters of the orthologous groups. In addition, the genome was assessed for its potential to produce bacteriocins using the BAGEL database [47]. The program ranked the features and identified multiple possible bacteriocin gene clusters within the genome.

Statistical analysis

Every experiment was performed in triplicate, with three replicates each time. Data is presented as the mean ± standard error of the mean (S.E.M.). Statistical significance was calculated using Student’s t-test within SigmaPlot software. The levels of statistical significance were determined at p ≤ 0.05 (denoted by *), p ≤ 0.01(denoted by **), and p ≤ 0.001 (denoted by ***). For all pair-wise multiple-group comparisons, the significant differences were first analyzed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by the Student-Newman-Keuls method within SigmaPlot software.

Results

Basic probiotic test

Tolerance to pepsin

Tolerance of MKMB01 and MKMB02 to gastrointestinal conditions such as pepsin, pancreatin, and ox gall (bile) was determined by measuring viable cell counts, represented in log10 CFU/mL in Supplementary Fig. 1a, b, and c. Compared to the control (PBS, pH 7.4), the viability of MKMB01 and MKMB02 decreased significantly after 3 hours of treatment with pepsin (PBS, pH 2) but retained their viability to 90.10% ± 1.07 (p ≤ 0.001) and 82.95% ± 0.69 (p ≤ 0.001) respectively (Supplementary Fig. 1a). After exposure to pancreatin (pH 8, 1 mg/mL pancreatin) and 2% (w/v) bile salt, oxgall (pH 7.4) for 4 hours, both of the bacterial strains retained their viability with no significant differences as compared to the control (pH 7.4) (Supplementary Fig. 1b and c). These results indicate that the MKMB01 and MKMB02 strains can thrive in harsh gastrointestinal conditions with high viability percentages (Supplementary Table 1). Representative images of the colony forming units (CFU) on agar plates are presented in Supplementary Fig. 2A, B, C, D, and E.

Antibiotic susceptibility and antimicrobial activity

The bacterial strains MKMB01 and MKMB02 were tested for antibiotic susceptibility against common antibiotics using Hexa G plus antibiotic discs (Himedia, India) (Supplementary Fig. 3A and B) containing ampicillin (AMP = 10 µg), chloramphenicol (C = 25 µg), penicillin (P = 1 unit), streptomycin (S = 10 µg), sulphatriad (S3 = 300 µg), and tetracyclin (TE = 25 µg). As presented in Supplementary Table 2, MKMB01 and MKMB02 are resistant to streptomycin, but they are moderately susceptible to other antibiotics. Images of the culture plates are shown in Supplementary Fig. 3A and B.

The antimicrobial properties of both strains were also tested using culture supernatants against pathogens such as E. coli K12, K. pneumoniae, L. monocytogenes, S. typhimurium, S. aureus, and E. faecalis. MRS broth was used as the control, and no antimicrobial activity was observed. The interpretation of the antibiotic and antimicrobial susceptibility test results follow the guidelines recommended by the Clinical & Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI); i.e., zone of inhibition (ZOI) ≤ 14 mm as resistant (R), 15–19 mm as intermediate, and ≥ 20 mm as susceptible [48, 49]. Antibiotic susceptibility testing was carried out on Mueller–Hinton agar. Based on the results, MKMB01 and MKMB02 possess low antimicrobial activity, with the highest activity against K. pneumoniae (18.66 ± 0.8 and 18 ± 0) and S. aureus (18.66 ± 0.8, and 18.33 ± 0.6). The lowest activity was observed in E. faecalis (15 ± 0 and 15.66 ± 0.6) (Supplementary Fig. 3C-I and Supplementary Table 3).

Additional probiotic properties

Surface hydrophobicity and auto-aggregation ability

Surface hydrophobicity and auto-aggregation were evaluated with regards to the adhesion ability in the gut (Supplementary Fig. 1d and e). Our study demonstrated that MKMB01 (52.5%, p ≤ 0.001) has a higher surface hydrophobicity than MKMB02 (32.3%%V, p ≤ 0.001). Both MKMB01 and MKMB02 strains possess medium auto-aggregation ability (68.7%; p ≤ 0.001 and 42.1%; p ≤ 0.001, respectively) (Supplementary Fig. 1e) (Reference range: High is ≥ 70% AA, Medium is 20–70% AA, and Low is ≤ 20% AA; [25]).

Adhesion to HT-29 cells

Adhesion to the epithelia of the gut lining is essential for exerting the beneficial effects of probiotics. The adhesion ability of the MKMB01 and MKMB02 strains was evaluated by incubating with a monolayer mucin-producing colonic HT-29 cell line at 1 × 109 CFU/mL for 4 hours. Both stains demonstrated high adhesion capacity to the monolayer; however, MKMB01 had a higher adhesion ability than MKMB02 (54.5% vs. 23.8%; p ≤ 0.001 and p ≤ 0.001, respectively) (Supplementary Fig. 1F).

MKMB01 and MKMB02 adhesion to Ht-29 cells (Supplementary Fig. 4A, a, b, and c; and B, a, b, and c) was visualized using field emission scanning electron microscopy (FESEM). We found that both L. plantarum strains (MKMB01 and MKMB02) adhered to HT-29 cells.

The formation of biofilms was also monitored using FESEM. We observed that MKMB01 and MKMB02 strains formed biofilms on glass coverslips in MRS broth when cultured 48 hours at 37 °C (Supplementary Fig. 4 C, a, b, and c; and D a, b, and c. The scale bars in a, b, and c denote 20 μm/5KX, 6 μm/10KX, and 2 μm/30KX, respectively).

Antioxidant activity

Intracellular antioxidant activity was evaluated using cellular extracts of the two probiotic bacteria MKMB01 and MKMB02 (Supplementary Fig. 5). MKMB01 possessed DPPH radical scavenging potential of 29.42%± 0.73 (p ≤ 0.001), whereas MKMB02 of about 26.84 ± 0.47 (p ≤ 0.001).

Incubation with MKMB01 and MKMB02 before Salmonella treatment in HT-29 cells increased the MUC-2 gene expression by ≈ 2 folds (p ≤ 0.001) and 1 fold (p ≤ 0.003), compared to cells treated with Salmonella alone, which showed ≈ 1 fold decrease in MUC-2 gene expression (p ≤ 0.005).

We also monitored the integrity of the intestinal barrier by evaluating the TEER of MKMB01 and MKMB02 strains in Salmonella-challenged caco-2 cells. MKMB01 and MKMB02 reduced the expression of IL-1β and inflammatory chemokine MCP-1 in Salmonella-induced caco-2 cells

IL-1β is an essential inflammatory cytokine mediator of inflammatory responses involved in various gut-associated disorders. With Salmonella treatment, IL-1β mRNA expression increased by ≈ 2.3 folds (P ≤ 0.001) compared to the untreated control. Following pre-treatment of MKMB01 and MKMB02 strains for 4 hours prior to Salmonella treatment, the IL-1β mRNA level was reduced ≈ 2.2 folds (P ≤ 0.001) and 1.7 folds (P ≤ 0.001), respectively ((Fig. 2B).

On the other hand, pre-treatment with MKMB01 and MKMB02 also reduced the expression of MCP-1 by ≈ 0.75 fold (p ≤ 0.001) and by ≈ 0.42 fold (p ≤ 0.002), respectively, which was elevated by ≈ 0.96 fold (p ≤ 0.001) when stimulated with Salmonella, thus minimizing the Salmonella-induced inflammatory responses in caco-2 cells.

Lactiplantibacillus strains MKMB01 and MKMB02 modulate the expression of TLR-negative regulators in caco-2 cells

In the literature, Salmonella infection is reported to trigger the expression of TLR-4, playing an essential role in activating host innate and adaptive immunity. Therefore, we investigated the potential of MKMB01 and MKMB02 to modulate the expression of TLR-negative regulators. Surprisingly, treatment with Salmonella alone did not alter the expression of TLR-negative regulators, such as toll-interacting protein (Tollip) and TNF-α-induced protein 3 (TNFAIP3) (also known as A20) (Fig. 2Eand G). Nevertheless, MKMB01 and MKMB02 pre-treatment increased the expression of Tollip significantly (Fig. 2E) by ≈ 0.9 fold (p ≤ 0.004) and ≈ 1.02 fold (p ≤ 0.002), respectively. Similarly, A20, which is a critical negative regulator of NF-κB, increased significantly (Fig. 2G) by ≈ 0.69 fold (p ≤ 0.045) and ≈ 0.53 fold (p ≤ 0.043)), respectively.

On the other hand, single Ig IL-1-related receptor (SIGIRR), also known as toll/interleukin-1 receptor 8 (TIR8) was reduced by ≈ 0.4 fold after Salmonella treatment (Fig. 2D). This effect was altered with MKMB01 and MKMB02 pre-treatment (Fig. 2D) which increases the expression of SIGIRR by ≈ 1.71 folds (p ≤ 0.001) and 1.5 folds (p ≤ 0.001), respectively. In contrast, the expression of interleukin-1 receptor-associated kinase (IRAK)-M showed no changes with either treatment (Fig. 2F).

MKMB01 and MKMB02 regulate the expression of anti-inflammatory cytokines TGF-β and IL-10

Releasing high levels of TGF-β and IL-10 suppresses pathological immune responses and promotes tolerance in the gut. Therefore, we tested MKMB01 and MKMB02 for their potential to regulate TGF-β and IL-10 expression in Salmonella-stimulated caco-2 cells. Compared to untreated control and Salmonella-treated cells, mRNA expression of IL-10 was reduced by ≈ 0.4 fold (p ≤ 0.043), but no significant change was seen in the expression of TGF-β (Fig. 2H and I). Nevertheless, pre-treatment of MKMB01 and MKMB02 for 4 hours before the Salmonella infection increased the expression of IL-10 (Fig. 2H) by ≈ 1.8 folds (p ≤ 0.001) and 1.5 folds (p ≤ 0.001), respectively, compared to the untreated control and cells treated with Salmonella alone. Similarly, pre-treatment of MKMB01 and MKMB02 increased the expression of TGF-β to ≈ 1 fold (p ≤ 0.001) and 1.5 folds (p ≤ 0.001), compared to Salmonella treated cells (Fig. 2I).

Effect of MKMB01 and MKMB02 on the expression of proinflammatory cytokines TNF-α and IL-6

TNF-α and IL-6 play crucial roles in immune responses and antigen presentation. To analyze the potential of MKMB01 and MKMB02 in reducing the expression of proinflammatory cytokines at transcriptional and protein levels, PMA-differentiated THP-1 cells were co-cultured with caco-2 cells and pre-treated with MKMB01 and MKMB02, which were then infected with Salmonella for 6 hours. Pre-treatment of MKMB01 and MKMB02 decreased the mRNA expression of TNF-α (Fig. 2J) by ≈ 1.45 folds (p ≤ 0.001) and 1.04 folds (p ≤ 0.001), respectively, compared to Salmonella treatment alone, which increased by 0.89 fold (p ≤ 0.001) compared to the control. Similarly, with MKMB01 and MKMB02 pre-treatment, the mRNA expression of IL-6 (Fig. 2K) decreased by ≈ 0.42 fold (p ≤ 0.007) and 0.33 fold (p ≤ 0.014), respectively, compared to cells treated with Salmonella alone, which increased the expression by ≈ 0.2 fold (p ≤ 0.04), compared to control.

We also assessed the release of soluble TNF-α and IL-6 into the culture media in PMA-differentiated THP-1 cells co-cultured with caco-2 cells (to depict intestinal conditions). We found that treatment with Salmonella increased the release of soluble TNF-α (Fig. 2L) to a concentration of 900.64 ± 31.26 pg/mL (p ≤ 0.001), compared to the control (19.66 ± 2.93 pg/mL). MKMB01 and MKMB02 pre-treatment; however, reduced the release of soluble TNF-α to 304.60 ± 39.02 pg/mL (p ≤ 0.001) and 483.74 ± 35.74 pg/mL (p ≤ 0.001), respectively. Interestingly, the release of IL-6 (Fig. 2N) into the culture media reached only up to 20.99 ± 1.77 pg/mL (p ≤ 0.001) with Salmonella treatment, compared to control (3.01 ± 0.61 pg/mL). With MKMB01 and MKMB02 treatments, the concentration of IL-6 was reduced to 3.03 ± 0.5 pg/mL (p ≤ 0.001) and 2.85 ± 0.73 pg/mL (p ≤ 0.001), respectively, compared to THP-1 cells with Salmonella-treatment.

We also tested for TNF-α and IL-6 release using caco-2 and HT-29 cells, but found that caco-2 cells do not produce either of the cytokines. HT-29 released lower concentrations of TNF-α and IL-6 (Figs. M and O), compared to THP-1 cells. With Salmonella treatment, the concentration of TNF-α in the culture media increased to 394.34 ± 17.00 pg/mL (p ≤ 0.001), compared to the control (1.45 ± 0.86 pg/mL). MKMB01 and MKMB02 treatments reduced the concentrations to 190.83 ± 1.38 pg/mL (p ≤ 0.001) and 194.28 ± 2.76 pg/mL (p ≤ 0.001), respectively. With Salmonella treatment, the concentration of IL-6 increased to 8.63 ± 1.07 pg/mL (p ≤ 0.002), compared to the control (0.19 ± 0.00 pg/mL. Following MKMB01 and MKMB02 treatments; however, the concentrations were reduced to 1.10 ± 0.32 pg/mL (p ≤ 0.002) and 2.10 ± 0.45 pg/mL (p ≤ 0.002).

MKMB01 and MKMB02 affect the release of proinflammatory cytokines by regulating the expression of ADAM17 protein

We studied the effects of MKMB01 and MKMB02 in regulating the expression of inflammatory regulators and adaptors (ADAM10/ADAM17) using RT-PCR followed by immunocytochemistry. Even though ADAM10 and ADAM17 formed with similar sequences and structures, only ADAM17 was expressed (Fig. 2P and Q). Salmonella treatment increased the mRNA expression of ADAM17 by ≈ 0.61 fold (p ≤ 0.001) compared to control which was reduced by MKMB01 and MKMB02 treatment by ≈ 0.80 fold (p ≤ 0.001) and 0.60 fold (p ≤ 0.001), respectively. Therefore, we proceeded to examine the expression of the ADAM17 protein. Similar to the mRNA results, the expression of the ADAM17 protein increased with Salmonella infection by ≈ 2.8 folds (p ≤ 0.001) compared to the control (Fig. 2T (a, b, c, and d) and U). With MKMB01 and MKMB02 pre-treatment, the expression of ADAM17 was reduced by ≈ 1.87 folds (p ≤ 0.001) and 1.53 folds (p ≤ 0.001), compared to Salmonella treatment alone. Thus, MKMB01 appeared to have a greater potential to reduce the release of soluble TNF-α and its adaptor protein ADAM17.

ADAM17 is an enzyme that requires proteolytic cleavage for activation and trafficking by rhomboid proteins (iRhoms) (i.e., iRhom1 and 2). The qRT-PCR results indicated that pre-treatment of MKMB01 and MKMB02 significantly decreased the expression of iRhom2 (≈ 1.26 folds, p ≤ 0.001 and 0.83 fold, p ≤ 0.001, respectively) compared to Salmonella treatment, which increased the expression by ≈ 1.52 folds (p ≤ 0.001), compared to the control (Fig. 2S). Nevertheless, we found no significant change in the expression of iRhom1 in any treatments (Fig. 2R).

No significant changes were observed in pre-apoptotic cells.

Discussion

Lactiplantibacillus belongs to the phylum Firmicutes and is “generally recognized as safe” (GRAS) [50]. Over the past few decades, the identification and characterization of Lactiplantibacillus has been extended, and the search for novel strains exerting health benefits termed “probiotics” has escalated. Survival of the orally ingested probiotics in the unfavorable gastrointestinal conditions is necessary to exert the beneficial effects. Lactiplantibacillus strains are generally resistant to low pH [51] and sustained in bile salts [52].

In our study, L. plantarum (both MKMB01 and MKMB02) was resistant to harsh gastrointestinal conditions. These results were correlated with genomic data obtained from WGS (Table 1). On the other hand, susceptibility to antibiotics and lack of transferable antibiotic-resistance genes are important criteria for probiotic selection [53]. Broadly, two antibiotic groups such as the inhibitors of cell-wall synthesis (ampicillin and penicillin) and inhibitors of protein synthesis (chloramphenicol, streptomycin, sulphatraid, and tetracycline) are recommended, based on sensitivity testing [53].

Our results indicated that both Lactiplantibacillus strains were resistant to streptomycin, have low sensitivity to sulphatraid, and have the highest sensitivity to ampicillin. Similarly, MKMB01 and MKMB02 possess low to moderate antimicrobial activities to some potentially harmful bacteria acting as natural antagonists. In addition, the adhesion of Lactiplantibacillus has been claimed to be essential for exerting its beneficial probiotic effect in the large intestine. Several Lactiplantibacillus stains have been reported for their adhesion potentials similar to our results. In addition, adhesion-related genes in MKMB01 and MKMB02 reflected their adhesion potential (Table 1). On the other hand, biofilm formation by Lactiplantibacillus is considered beneficial and a potential property to promote colonization in the intestinal mucosa, thereby competing for adherence with pathogenic bacteria [54]. Exopolysaccharides (EPS) produced during biofilm formation can also inhibit biofilm formation by various pathogenic bacteria [55–57].

Recent scientific advances have revealed many mechanisms by which probiotics exert health-promoting effects in humans and laboratory animals [58]. Our study evaluated the interactions of MKMB01 and MKMB02 with human colonic cells caco-2 and HT-29. We observed that MKMB01 and MKMB02 promote gut integrity by expressing MUC-2 and TJPs. The intestinal barrier is distinctively divided into a physical barrier (the mucus layer), a tightly packed epithelial layer, and the lamina propria that act as a barrier for the immune system [59]. The mucus layer, the first barrier, prevents direct interactions between colonic epithelial cells and intestinal bacteria. A dysfunctioning mucus barrier can result in excessive stimulation of intestinal epithelial cells and the mucosal immune system. As a consequence, this can lead to the development of gut-associated disorders, including ulcerative colitis (UC).

In our study, we found that MKMB01 and MKMB02 treatments enhanced the expression of MUC-2 genes. Commensals like Lactiplantibacillus spp. have been reported to interact with mucin-producing goblet cells and promote the release of MUC-2. Commensal microorganisms in the gut attach and utilize the MUC-2 glycans as a source of energy, aided by lectin-type adhesions and glycan-degrading enzymes [60]. The metabolites produced by adhered bacteria induce the transcription of the MUC2 gene by binding with activator protein 1 (AP-1) [61]. The effect of probiotics on MUC-2 mRNA expression has been well-documented in the literature [62], and the ability of probiotics to induce mucin production is vital for maintaining gut integrity.

The mechanical barrier (the epithelial layer) prevents direct contact of systemic components with luminal contents and antigens. A dysfunctioning epithelial layer caused by the degradation of TJPs can affect host immunity and lead to a leaky gut condition. The leaky gut is associated with gut-induced inflammation and disorders, which are precursors to various diseases. In recent decades, the influence of Lactiplantibacillus spp. in regulating TJPs has been well studied [63–65]. In particular, Lactiplantibacillus treatment led to the upregulation of TJP expression, to maintain the intestinal barrier disrupted by Salmonella. Increased expression or abundance of TJPs is associated with epithelial barrier protection, and reduced epithelial permeability [66]. We observed that the prior treatment of MKMB01 and MKMB02 reduced the destruction of TJPs during the invasion of Salmonella in caco-2 cells, compared to Salmonella treatment alone. Our study depicts the potential use of MKMB01 and MKMB02 in maintaining the intestinal barrier function and maintaining the gut integrity from invasive pathogens like Salmonella. For decades, in vitro and in vivo studies of Lactiplantibacillus spp. have demonstrated their positive effects on intestinal barrier integrity and the ability to reverse the effects of barrier dysfunction [67–70].

The TEER assay, an accepted quantitative technique that measures integrity dynamics in vitro, showed the potential of MKMB01 and MKMB02 to maintain gut integrity. Lactiplantibacillus maintains TEER measurements against barrier antagonists such as pathogens or chemical agents [67–70]. TJPs, the TEER assay, and MUC-2 expression results indicated that L. plantarum stains MKMB01 and MKMB02 maintained gut integrity in response to Salmonella. Prior treatment of Lactiplantibacillus might have prevented precluding Salmonella invasion and the subsequent disruption to the barrier integrity.

The presence of microbes is recognized by toll-like receptors (TLRs) expressed by epithelial and immune cells. TLRs are essential for the mucosal host defense against gram-negative bacteria. In particular, TLRs recognize several microbial ligands such as TLR2 for lipoproteins, TLR3 for dsRNA, TLR4 for lipopolysaccharide, TLR7 and TLR8 for ssRNA, and TLR9 for unmethylated CpG motifs in DNA [71]. In the case of Salmonella, in association with CD14 and myeloid differentiation protein 2 (MD2), the LPS is recognized through TLR4. Expression of TLR4 induces innate immune responses, recruiting immune cells such as macrophages and dendritic cells, which initiate inflammatory cytokine production and an oxidative burst [72].

TLR4 expression recruits adaptor proteins such as MyD88, TRIF-related adaptor molecules (TRAM), and MyD88-adapter-like protein (Mal). The signaling through TLR4 and its adaptors induces the release of cytokines such as IL-6, IL-12, and TNF-α, which are regarded as pro-inflammatory. These cytokines are then involved with inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS) for NO release by macrophages. This key inflammatory cytokine activates the caspase pathway, leading to cell apoptosis [72, 73]. Generally, TLR expression induces antimicrobial pathways, such as releasing antimicrobial peptides (AMPs) and ROS from mucosal immune cells for pathogen elimination [71]. The overexpression of TLR; however, prolongs inflammation in the gut, and can induce chronic perforations. TLR4 has been reported to be important for protecting against Salmonella infection [74]. Nevertheless, Salmonella-induced TLR4 signaling pathways and its associated receptors are yet to be understood in terms of controlling Salmonella infections and the related immune responses. In this regard, probiotic strains MKMB01 and MKMB02 regulated Salmonella infections and their associated TLR4-induced inflammatory downstream pathways of mucosal immune system.

In the study, we found that pre-treatment of MKMB01 and MKMB02 upregulated negative regulators of TLR4, such as A20 (inhibitor of NF-κB activation and TNF-mediated apoptosis), SIGIRR, and Tollip. Still, no significant difference was observed with IRAK-M expression. As a result, TLR4-mediated inflammatory pathways and responses were reduced to maintain immune balance in Salmonella-induced caco-2 cells. A20 regulates TLR4 responsiveness to LPS and limits the activation of NF-κB through the mediated pathway, where deficiency of A20 causes lethal inflammation [75].

Similarly, stimulation of intestinal cells with LPS (TLR ligand) induces hyporesponsiveness through SIGIRR, Tollip, and IRAK-M upregulation, reducing the release of TLR-mediated inflammatory cytokines [73, 75–78]. Surprisingly, IRAK-M expression in MKMB01 and MKMB02 pre-treated caco-2 cells was not significantly different from Salmonella-treated caco-2 cells. From the results, we can deduce that MKMB01 and MKMB02 pre-treatment before Salmonella infection in caco-2 cells prevents TLR4 overstimulation by regulating TLR4 negative regulators. (Figs. 2D, E, F, and G). The upregulation of these negative regulators inhibits TLR4-triggered NF-κB-TNF-α signaling pathways, minimizing further Salmonella-induced inflammation.

The binding of LPS and recognition of TLR4 components initiates a downstream signaling cascade associated with the release of cytokines like IL-6, IL-1β, and MCP-1 through NF-κB and mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) pathways [79]. IL-1β is an essential mediator of the inflammatory response that activates monocytes, initiating downstream inflammatory signaling pathways involved in cell death. IL-1β has been reported to be secreted by the cells before the release of the cell death marker enzyme, viz. lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), suggesting the liability for cell death with IL-1β release [42]. In our study, pre-treatment with the probiotic strains MKMB01 and MKMB02 reduced the transcription of IL-1β stimulated by Salmonella. The decrease in the expression of IL-1β with probiotic treatment can also be correlated with the results of viability of caco-2 cells in the apoptosis assay (Fig. 3A-E) as the release of IL-1β is highly associated with cell death.

Alternatively, MCP-1 is a chemokine that plays a critical role in controlling the movement and infiltration of monocytes/macrophages [80]. Uncontrolled release of the MCP-1 chemokine may result in the recruitment of monocytes at the inflamed site, exaggerating the severity of inflammation. As shown in Fig. 2C, a significant elevation in MCP-1 mRNA expression in caco-2 cells was detected when cells were exposed to Salmonella. Nevertheless, pre-incubation of caco-2 cells with L. plantarum MKMB01 and MKMB02 for 4 hours resulted in an attenuation of MCP-1 mRNA expression in Salmonella-stimulated cells, indicating the potential anti-inflammatory effect of the probiotics (Fig. 2C). The presence of immunoregulatory genes such as dltB, dltD, and dltC in MKMB01 and MKMB02 may explain the regulatory expression of several inflammatory and anti-inflammatory cytokines/chemokines in human cell lines with MKMB01 and MKMB02 treatment.

Previous reports have shown the effect of Salmonella in inducing the expression of cytokines like TNF-α and IL-6 by macrophages. Interestingly, the expression of these pro-inflammatory cytokines can be reduced with L. plantarum stains MKMB01 and MKMB02 treatment. We observed that administering probiotics before Salmonella treatment can decrease the release of inflammatory cytokines by stimulated macrophages, indicating an overall anti-inflammatory effect. Moreover, we found that the downregulation of TNF-α and IL-6 expression was linked to the downregulation of ADAM17 (also known as TNF-alpha-converting enzyme, TACE). MKMB01 and MKMB02 decreased the expression of ADAM17 at transcriptional and translational levels. Our findings reveal a novel mechanism that facilitates the release of Salmonella-induced TNF-α and IL-6 through probiotic treatment.

We also analyzed the expression of another adaptor (ADAM10), which can regulate the release of TNF-α. Nevertheless, the expression of ADAM10 was not significantly changed in any of the treatments, compared to the untreated control; hence, the release of soluble TNF-α was not associated with ADAM10 expression. Thus, the downregulation of ADAM17 might have a direct impact on soluble TNF-α release, thereby influencing the post-transcriptional modification of cytokines. This result opens up a new research area for investigating the regulation of ADAM17 and the mode of interaction between the host and potential probiotics.

Another factor that could mask the expression of ADAM17 are the pseudoproteases iRhom1 and iRhom2, which are essential for maturation and transportation. Therefore, we also monitored the expression of ADAM17-binding components iRhom1 and iRhom2 in THP-1 cells. We observed that iRhom2, but not iRhom1, was upregulated in THP-1 cells infected with Salmonella, but the expression decreased with MKMB01 and MKMB02 pre-treatment. Therefore, the mature form of ADAM17 was reduced significantly, as reflected by the amount of soluble TNF-α released into the culture medium. In contrast, iRhom1 that was not upregulated considerably (or downregulated) in any of the treatments, indicating that Salmonella-induced ADAM17 expression was not regulated by iRhom1. The different activities of the two iRhoms has been suggested to be due to variations in their trafficking mechanism or affinity, or the stability of their complex formations or susceptibility with ADAM17 [81].

We also studied the potential of MKMB01 and MKMB02 in reducing the ROS generated by Salmonella in caco-2 cells. ROS mediates various modifications in signaling molecule intermediates, disrupting normal cellular metabolism and immune-signaling pathways. During the inflammation/translocation of microbes, the host cells use nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate hydrogen (NADPH) oxidase to reduce oxygen to superoxide. This superoxide directly damages the host cells and can also form hydrogen peroxide. These ROS can react with biological molecules to generate reactive hydroxyl radicals. These highly reactive radicals damage the protein structure and oxidises DNA, leading to cellular death. Therefore, increased ROS during inflammation may cause systemic inflammation if not controlled. We observed that MKMB01 has more potential to reduce the generated ROS by Salmonella infection, compared to MKMB02.

We further analyzed the genomic content of MKMB01 and MKMB02 and found that several anti-oxidizing genes such as msrA, msrB, npr, nfrA1, trxA, and trxB were present. These may help to remove the ROS, and prevent the inactivation (oxidation) of antioxidant enzymes. We also analyzed the potential of MKMB01 and MKMB02 in reducing mitochondrial dysfunction using mitochondrial inhibitors and monitoring the real-time changes to mitochondrial respiration using several different parameters (i.e., non-mitochondrial respiration, basal respiration, maximal respiration, ATP production, and SRC). Consumption of oxygen by cellular enzymes other than those in the mitochondria is referred to as non-mitochondrial respiration. Basal respiration represents the energetic demand under basal respiration, whereas maximal respiration represents the maximum rate of respiration achieved by cells when oxidizing substrates under a physiological energy demand. SRC indicates cell wellness, referring to the mitochondrial maximum capacity in response to energy requirements for producing extra ATP [82]. A decrease in maximal and basal respiration indicates mitochondrial dysfunction [83], as seen in the cells after receiving Salmonella treatment [84]. MKMB01 and MKMB02 pre-treatment reversed the effect of mitochondrial dysfunction by maintaining basal and maximal respiration (Fig. 3M and N). As shown in Fig. 3L, non-mitochondrial respiration was reduced significantly with Salmonella infection but the effect was ameliorated with MKMB01 and MKMB02 pre-treatment.

We also observed that ATP production was increased following the pre-treatment with MKMB01 and MKMB02 in caco-2 cells before being infected with Salmonella (Fig. 3P). Moreover, after the Salmonella infection, the SRC was reduced significantly (p ≤ 0.001), compared to untreated caco-2 cells, but it increased significantly with MKMB01 and MKMB02 pre-treatment, compared to Salmonella infection. Thus, treatment with MKMB01 and MKMB02 increased SRC significantly, compared to untreated caco-2 cells. These results illustrate the potential of MKMB01 and MKMB02 to increase the ATP production when cells are subjected to stress or energy demands to regulate cellular functions. This helps the cells to overcome stress and produce more ATP when needed, indicating the potential of cells and the ability to withstand harsh conditions and maintain mitochondrial dysfunction, and increase cellular survival. These results can also be incorporated with WGS data, which shows the presence of genes related to stress responses (Table 1). Overall, the quantifiable changes in the mitochondrial factors reveal the changes that occur in mitochondrial cellular respiration when the cells were exposed to Salmonella.

Conclusion

L. plantarum strains MKMB01 and MKMB02 possess the potential to regulate inflammatory responses upregulated in response to Salmonella treatment. MKMB01 and MKMB02 also restored TLR-induced inflammation by regulating innate immune responses and modulating the expression of TLR-negative regulators. Further, MKMB01 and MKMB02 regulated Salmonella-induced TNF-α release by downregulating the expression of metalloprotease ADAM17 and its adaptor iRhom2. These activities point to the molecular mechanisms that are involved. In addition, MKMB01 and MKMB02 exerted antioxidant activity by quenching ROS and restoring mitochondrial functions that were disrupted by Salmonella treatment in caco-2 cells. The two bacterial strains show promise and should be further explored for development as probiotics.
