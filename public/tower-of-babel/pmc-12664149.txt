Is there a difference in the postoperative quality of recovery and analgesia between erector spinae and serratus anterior plane blocks following modified radical mastectomy: a randomized controlled study
Authors: Mehtap Gürler Balta, Vildan Kölükçü, Ahmet Tuğrul Şahin, Ali Genç, Mustafa Süren, İsmail Okan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe postoperative recovery quality of patients may be associated with the efficacy of perioperative analgesia management. The effects of erector spinae plane block (ESPB) and serratus anterior plane block (SAPB) on the quality of recovery following breast cancer surgery have not been clearly established. The objective of the present study is to assess the quality of recovery and analgesic efficiency of ESPB and SAPB as postoperative analgesia methods in modified radical mastectomy surgeries.MethodsA total of 68 female patients undergoing modified radical mastectomy were randomly separated into two groups. The main outcome of this randomized, double-blind prospective study was the 40-item global quality of recovery (QoR-40) questionnaire score, collected at 24 and 48 h postoperatively. Other measures of interest included postoperative numerical rating scale (NRS), cumulative opioid consumption, time to first analgesic request, demographic data, hemodynamic variables and postoperative complications such as nausea, vomiting and dizziness.ResultsIn the postoperative period after 24 h, the global QoR-40 scores were found as 185.5 (range:150–199) in the ESPB group and 186 (range:158–198) in the SAPB group (p = 0.304). The median time to first analgesic request was 645 min (range:30–740) in the ESPB group and 640 min (range:30–700) in the SAPB group, without any statistical variation between the groups (p = 0.216). Cumulative opioid consumption did not significantly differ between the two groups (p = 0.785). Likewise, no significant difference was detected between the ESPB and SAPB groups in mean arterial pressure, heart rate, SpO₂ levels or complication rates.ConclusionPreoperative administration of ESPB and SAPB in breast cancer surgery improved the quality of recovery and effectively reduced analgesic requirement. Future studies should investigate how local anesthetic type and volume affect postoperative recovery and analgesia.Trial registrationClinicalTrials.gov ID: NCT04296188 on 03/03/2020.

Introduction

Although breast cancer is the most prevalent cancer type worldwide, it comprises a much smaller fraction of cancer-related deaths due to advancements in treatment modalities [1]. The majority of new breast cancer cases are diagnosed locally or regionally before metastasis occurs, and more than 90% of these are treated surgically [2]. Postmastectomy pain has different sources [3]. Targeted pain management focuses on improving quality of life and reducing opioid use [3]. In recent years, the use of ultrasound-guided multimodal anesthesia methods in perioperative analgesia has enabled higher success rates, fewer complications and improved postoperative analgesic efficacy [4].

Ultrasound-guided erector spinae plane block (ESPB) and serratus anterior plane block (SAPB) are effective regional anesthesia techniques for postoperative analgesia in modified radical mastectomy (MRM). SAPB, first defined by Blanco et al., consists of the injection of local anesthetic into the fascial plane between the serratus anterior and latissimus dorsi muscles, which effectively targets the lateral cutaneous branches of the thoracic intercostal nerves [5, 6]. ESPB, on the other hand, was initially described by Forero et al. in 2016 and entails injecting local anesthetic into the fascial plane between the erector spinae muscle and the transverse processes of the vertebrae [7]. Many studies have reported that both blocks improve postoperative pain management and reduce opioid consumption compared to control groups [8–11]. The comparison between ESPB and SAPB in terms of analgesic efficacy has revealed that both techniques provide effective analgesia following thoracic or abdominal surgery; however, their effects on recovery scores may be different [12, 13]. Nevertheless, the comparison of the effects of ESPB and SAPB on quality of recovery following breast cancer surgery has not been clearly established in the literature. Although both blocks target similar dermatomal areas, they differ in terms of application techniques, anatomical spread patterns, and potential sympathetic block effects. Therefore, there may be clinically significant differences in their effects on the quality of patient recovery. In our current study, we aimed to perform a multidimensional assessment of recovery between these two commonly used block techniques using QoR-40 scores. Studies comparing these two blocks may yield different results with respect to the quality of recovery, depending on factors such as the type of surgery, patient demographics and the time of block administration. The postoperative quality of recovery (QoR-40) score is a global measure used to assess the recovery status after anesthesia and surgery [14]. This score consists of 40 questions encompassing five domains including patients’ psychological support, emotional state, physical comfort, physical independence in carrying out daily activities and pain intensity. In numerous surgical settings, the QoR-40 score has been demonstrated as a valid and precise tool for measuring the dynamic and multifaceted process of postoperative recovery. Additional studies are needed to clarify the effects of different surgical approaches on the quality of recovery. To our knowledge, this is one of the first trials to apply the QoR-40 scale comparatively between ESPB and SAPB in breast cancer surgery.

The purpose of this study is to compare the effects of ESP and SAP blocks on the postoperative quality of recovery using the QoR-40 score after MRM surgery. Furthermore, it seeks to evaluate the variations in total opioid consumption, the time of first analgesic administration, postoperative NRS, complications, and intraoperative hemodynamic variables.

Materials and methods

This study was conducted as a prospective, randomized controlled trial in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki after securing permission from the Institutional Ethics Committee of Tokat Gaziosmanpaşa University Faculty of Medicine (19-KAEK-253). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to their inclusion in the study. This study was prepared in accordance with the CONSORT (Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials) guidelines, which aim to ensure transparent and complete reporting of clinical trials. It included 68 patients undergoing MRM surgery between February 2020 and August 2022 at a tertiary care center. Using a computer-generated randomization program, the patients were allocated into two groups at random: ESPB and SAPB. Before the surgery, the patients were given information regarding the study by an anesthesiologist not involved in the study, and informed consent in writing was received from all participants. As this study was structured as a double blinded trial, both the patients and the observer collecting the data were blinded to interventions administered to patients.

The inclusion criteria were patients classified as ASA I, II, or III who were planned to receive elective unilateral modified radical mastectomy surgery, did not use steroids or regularly use systemic analgesic medications (e.g., NSAIDs, opioids, or gabapentinoids) for chronic pain management and consented to take part in the study. The reason for excluding patients on routine systemic corticosteroids was to minimize confounding effects on postoperative analgesic requirements and quality of recovery scores, as corticosteroids can modulate inflammatory and pain responses, potentially biasing the outcome measures The criteria for exclusion were patients with infection at the injection site, those who did not agree to join in the study, individuals with central or peripheral neurological disorders, those with coagulopathies, or receiving anticoagulant drugs, uncooperative patients, individuals with a history of allergic reactions to any of the drugs administered in the study and those with recurrent breast cancer.

A Stimuplex® A100 needle (0.80 × 100 mm [21G × 4’’]; B Braun, Pennsylvania, USA) and a Hitachi Noblus Ultrasound System, equipped with a high-frequency linear probe (Noblus; Hitachi Aloka Medical Ltd., Kashiwa-shi, Chiba, Japan) were used in the administration of the blocks.

Erector spinae plane block group (ESPB group): All ESP blocks were performed while patients were awake, prior to induction of general anesthesia. The lateral decubitus position was chosen in accordance with our institutional protocol to ensure patient comfort and optimal ultrasound guidance. Before commencing the surgery, the patients were arranged in the lateral decubitus position with the surgical side facing upwards. After ensuring the sterilization of the surgical site, the spinous process of the fourth thoracic vertebra was identified using a linear ultrasound probe, and by shifting 3 cm laterally, the transverse process of the T4 vertebra and the erector spinae muscle were visualized. The intervention site was confirmed by injecting a test dose of 1–2 mL saline solution between the deep fascia of the erector spinae muscle and the transverse process of the vertebra using a 100-mm sonovisible nerve block needle with an in-plane technique from caudal to cranial. Following this, an erector spinae plane block was performed using 30 mL of 0.25% bupivacaine. The choice of 0.25% bupivacaine was guided by institutional safety protocols, aiming to balance adequate analgesia with reduced risk of local anesthetic systemic toxicity. Although higher concentrations such as 0.375–0.5% may extend block duration, previous studies have demonstrated that 0.25% bupivacaine is effective for postoperative pain control in breast surgery when administered as part of a multimodal analgesic regimen. Based on previous randomized studies in patients undergoing modified radical mastectomy, 30 mL of 0.25% bupivacaine was selected to ensure sufficient fascial plane spread and effective analgesia.

Serratus plan block group (SAPB group): Prior to commencing the surgery, the patients were positioned in the lateral decubitus position with the surgical side facing upwards, and the upper arm was adjusted to expose the axillary region. After skin sterilization, a linear ultrasound probe was used to visualize the latissimus dorsi and serratus muscles along the mid-axillary line, in a longitudinal parasagittal position at the 4th and 5th rib levels. Then, a 100-mm sonovisible nerve block needle was advanced from caudal to cranial between the 4th rib and the serratus anterior muscle using an in-plane technique. After assuring the absence of blood or air by performing aspiration, a test dose of 1–2 mL saline solution was administered. Upon the confirmation of the intervention site, 30 mL 0.25% bupivacaine was administered between the two muscles.

All patients were taken to the operating room, where they were placed under continuous monitoring. Intubation was then performed following muscle relaxation induced by intravenous propofol (2 mg/kg), fentanyl (1 mcg/kg) and rocuronium (0.6 mg/kg). Anesthesia maintenance was ensured with 40% oxygen and 4–7% desflurane, adjusted to achieve a minimum alveolar concentration of 1. In cases where the heart rate (HR) or mean arterial pressure (MAP) elevated by more than 20% compared to the pre-induction baseline during the surgery, an intravenous dose of 1 µg/kg fentanyl was injected, and the total amount of fentanyl given during the surgery was recorded. After the completion of the surgery, anesthesia maintenance was stopped, and sugammadex at a dose of 2 mg/kg was used to reverse the residual block. During the intraoperative period, 10 mg/kg paracetamol and 0.05 mg/kg morphine were given for postoperative analgesia. All patients were extubated when the train of four ratio reached 0.9. The duration of the surgery was noted.

In the postoperative recovery room, an intravenous patient-controlled analgesia (PCA) device was connected. A solution containing 2 mg/ml tramadol was prepared for PCA. PCA was initiated with a bolus dose of 20 mg, a 10-minute lockout time, no basal infusion, and a maximum dose of 600 mg over 24 h. The same dosing regimen was also continued during the ward follow-up.

The 11-point NRS, where 0 indicates no pain and 10 represents the worst imaginable pain, was used to assess pain intensity. The patients were asked to numerically express their perceived pain intensity.

The pain intensity was assessed using NRS at 30 min and at 1, 2, 6, 12 and 24 h following postoperative extubation by an anesthesiologist who was not involved in the procedure. In patients with NRS ≥ 4, 0.03 mg/kg morphine was administered as rescue analgesia, and their NRS was reevaluated after 30 min; if the score remained ≥ 4, the same dose was repeated. The QoR-40 questionnaire was administered at 24 and 48 h after surgery by an investigator blinded to group allocation. The total tramadol and morphine consumption was measured.

MAP, HR and oxygen saturation (SpO2) values of patients were recorded before induction and at 30 min intervals following induction throughout the surgery. In the postoperative period, MAP and HR were noted at 30 min and 1, 2, 6, 12 and 24 h after extubation. Furthermore, possible adverse effects, such as vomiting, dry mouth, headache, dizziness, dyspepsia, rash and nausea, were also recorded. The patients were asked to complete the QoR-40 questionnaire at 24 and 48 h postoperatively.

Statistical analysis

All data gathered in the study were statistically analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics v27 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables were reported as mean ± standard deviation for normally distributed data, and as median (minimum–maximum) for data not conforming to a normal distribution. Categorical variables were displayed as percentage (%).

Between group comparisons were carried out with the Mann-Whitney U test in non-normally distributed data and the independent sample t test in normally distributed continuous data. The Wilcoxon test was applied for repeated measurements taken over time. Categorical data were compared using the Fisher exact test. p < 0.05 was regarded statistically significant in all analyses.

Physical comfort

In the ESPB group, the median physical comfort score was 54 (range: 38–60) on postoperative day 1 and 55.5 (range: 40–60) on day 2, whereas in the SAPB group, it was 55.5 (range: 30–60) and 55.5 (range: 33–60), respectively. Physical comfort levels remained statistically similar both within and between the groups on either day. (p > 0.05).

Emotional status

On the postoperative first day, the median emotional state score was recorded to be 42 (range: 35–45) in the ESPB group and 43.5 (range: 21–45) in the SAPB group (p = 0.196). The second day scores were 43.5 (range: 35–45) and 44 (range: 30–45), respectively, with no statistical difference between the groups (p = 0.453). Within-group comparisons revealed no significant differences in emotional status scores on either postoperative day (p > 0.05).

Physical independence

The median physical independence score in the ESPB group was 22.5 (range: 12–25) on postoperative day 1 and remained unchanged at 22.5 (range: 15–25) on the second day, whereas in the SAPB group, it was 23.5 (range: 18–25) on both days (p > 0.05).

Psychological support

Both groups showed similar psychological support scores. Although the median of psychological support score on the postoperative day 1 was 35 in both groups, the range was 25–35 in the ESPB group and 30–35 in the SAPB group (p = 0.254). The median score remained the same on the second day at 35, with a range of 29–35 in the ESPB group and 33–35 in the SAPB group (p = 0.196). On both days, no statistically significant differences were found within the groups (p > 0.05).

Pain

The median pain score was measured to be 33 (range: 25–35) in the ESPB group and 32 (range: 27–35) in the SAPB group on the first postoperative day (p = 0.178). On the second day, it was 33 (range: 26–35) in the ESPB group, 32 (range: 27–35), with no statistical significance between the groups (p = 0.172). Within-group differences were observed to be insignificant on both days (p > 0.05).

These results show that no significant variation was detected between the ESPB and SAPB groups in terms of the QoR-40 scores and its subdomains.

Hemodynamic variables

There were no statistically significant differences detected between the ESPB and SAPB groups in terms of MAP, HR and SpO2 values.

The comparison of MAP values measured before induction, at induction, and intraoperatively at 30, 60, 90, 120, 150 and 180 min revealed no significant variations between the two groups (p > 0.05) (Fig. 2).

Discussion

The reason we did not include a paravertebral block or a control group in our study is that such comparisons have already been made in the literature, and both ESPB and SAPB are less invasive alternatives that are commonly preferred in clinical practice. In this regard, our study aimed to directly contribute to anesthesiologists who are faced with the decision of which technique to prefer in daily practice. In our prospective randomized clinical study, the efficacy of ESPB compared to SAPB in patients who underwent MRM surgery was investigated. Postoperative recovery is a complex process depending on patient, surgical and anesthetic factors. In assessing the efficacy of the anesthetic procedure, the impact of patient-centered outcomes has recently been embraced [15]. Although postoperative lower pain scores are valuable outcomes, they may not be perceived as a better recovery experience by patients when accompanied by other debilitating side effects. In our study in which patients received MRM with ultrasound-guided SAPB and ESPB, the scores of the global QoR-40 and all its subdomains, used to assess postoperative recovery, demonstrated clinically meaningful and comparable improvement in both blocks. The probability of patients being pain-free (NRS < 4) was similar in the SAPB and ESPB groups. There were insignificant differences between the groups with regard to first analgesic request and total opioid consumption.

The QoR-40 score is recognized as an internationally valid measure to assess patients’ quality of recovery after surgery; however, there is a scarcity of comparative studies focusing on early postoperative recovery quality between ESP and SAP blocks after breast surgery [16]. While Kamiya et al. applied the QoR-40 scale to PECS blocks, comparative data for ESPB and SAPB are lacking. Our study therefore provides one of the first QoR-40-based evaluations of these plane blocks in breast cancer surgery. Although there is no direct study in the literature comparing ESPB and SAPB in terms of recovery quality following breast surgery, Yao et al. examined the 40-item QoR-40 questionnaire scores at 24 h postoperatively in patients who received SAPB. Their results aligned with those of our study’s SAPB group and yielded high QoR-40 scores [9]. Studies conducted in recent years have examined the comparative efficacy of ESPB and SAPB in managing postoperative pain and improving quality of recovery following thoracic surgical procedures [9, 17, 18]. Both ESPB and SAPB have been associated with better postoperative outcomes relative to control groups, encompassing higher QoR scores, as well as reduced pain levels and opioid consumption [12, 13]. Some studies in the literature indicated that ESPB showed superior postoperative outcomes compared to the SAPB regarding QoR scores, analgesia efficacy and lower incidence of complications following minimally invasive thoracic procedures [13]. However, there are also studies reporting that there was no significant differences in QoR scores [19]. Recent investigations focusing the comparative analgesic efficacy of ultrasound-guided ESPB and SAPB in patients undergoing MRM have reported differing results [6, 20–26]. Some studies have reported that ultrasound-guided ESPB provides longer analgesia and lower pain scores compared to SAPB [9, 23, 27–30]. However, some have found the opposite, suggesting that SAPB is superior [22, 26, 31] and others have demonstrated that both blocks offer similar analgesic efficacy and safety in radical mastectomy procedures [20]. In our study, no significant differences were detected between the ESPB and SAPB groups in terms of NRS scores. Similar findings were reported by Ahuja et al. [6], who conducted a randomized controlled trial directly comparing the analgesic efficacy of ESPB and SAPB in 80 female patients. Their study found that both blocks provided effective analgesia, with similar times to first analgesic request and comparable analgesic efficacy between groups, aligning with our results [6]. The volume of local anesthetic is a critical determinant of block distribution and efficacy. Ahuja et al. administered 30 mL of ropivacaine, whereas our study employed 30 mL of 0.25% bupivacaine. Although the total volume was identical, pharmacological differences between the agents—particularly in terms of potency and duration of action—may have contributed to the observed variations in analgesic duration. Similarly, Wang et al. stated that both ESPB and SAPB exhibited comparable analgesic efficacy in patients undergoing RM [20]. Wang et al. evaluated patients undergoing radical mastectomy rather than modified radical mastectomy, which represents a broader surgical extent and a distinct pain profile. Therefore, direct comparison with our findings should be made cautiously. The difference in surgical scope limits the direct comparability between the two studies.In a study conducted on 56 women, Wagih et al. found no significant differences between SAPB and ESPB regarding analgesic consumption, pain scores and patient satisfaction after MRM surgery, with neither group reporting any complications or major side effects [21]. Their results were consistent with ours.In contrast to Wagih et al. who achieved continuous analgesia through catheter infusion following block administration, our study employed a single-shot injection technique. This methodological difference may account for the comparable—but not superior—analgesic outcomes observed in our results. Specifically, Wagih et al. used 20 mL of bupivacaine combined with 8 mg of dexmedetomidine and placed a catheter for continuous infusion, a factor likely to prolong the duration of analgesia relative to our single-injection approach. But et al. indicated that both SAPB and ESPB were as effective as paravertebral block in terms of intraoperative analgesia [25]. But et al. reported superior analgesia with serratus anterior plane block (SAPB) and paravertebral block compared with erector spinae plane block (ESPB) when using a smaller anesthetic volume (20 mL). In contrast, our findings demonstrated comparable efficacy between SAPB and ESPB, suggesting that a larger anesthetic volume may enhance the effectiveness of ESPB. The results of But et al. may therefore reflect the influence of injection volume on the spread and performance of the ESPB.Studies have shown that SAPB not only reduces postoperative pain scores but also diminishes the incidence of opioid-associated side effects like nausea and sedation, thereby leading to improved patient satisfaction and functional recovery [32, 33]. SAPB and ESPB are generally regarded as safe procedures, with rare occurrences of complications like hemodynamic instability or local anesthetic systemic toxicity [34].

SAPB has been demonstrated to decrease acute postoperative pain, opioid usage, and side effects like nausea and vomiting relative to control groups [9, 35, 36]. Both ESPB and SAPB techniques exhibited a comparable incidence of postoperative nausea and vomiting, reflecting similar safety profiles [29, 34]. SAPB technique provides high patient satisfaction with minimal side effects, strengthening its role as a viable analgesic option in breast surgery [37]. Our results revealed a comparable frequency of side effects between the two techniques.

There are certain limitations in our study that should be acknowledged, including the absence of a control group, its single-center design, and small sample size. Another limitation of our study is the lack of preoperative baseline QoR-40 measurements. We acknowledge that assessing each patient’s delta QoR score (postoperative minus baseline) could have provided a more individualized insight into the recovery process. However, due to logistical and procedural constraints in the clinical workflow, it was not feasible to obtain reliable baseline QoR-40 scores preoperatively. We have therefore relied on postoperative comparisons between groups, as has been done in several other similar studies. This limitation should be addressed in future trials by incorporating baseline measurements to better capture the net improvement in recovery. Additionally, the effect of local anesthetic on the dermatomal block was not examined in the study. Further multicenter studies with larger populations are needed to confirm and broaden our results.

Conclusion

Erector spinae plane block and serratus anterior plane block are both effective techniques in the management of pain after modified radical mastectomy. They can be applied based on patient-specific needs and anesthetist preference. Both techniques have emerged as viable alternatives to conventional analgesic procedures in managing postoperative pain, a critical factor in improving recovery outcomes. In conclusion, both ultrasound-guided SAPB and ESPB play a vital role in enhancing life quality of patients undergoing modified radical mastectomy by providing effective analgesia and minimizing the need for systemic opioids. Both ESPB and SAPB provided effective postoperative analgesia and similar quality of recovery. However, future studies should explore the impact of anesthetic type and volume, as these factors may significantly influence block performance.
