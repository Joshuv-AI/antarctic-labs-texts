International Classification of Disease based Injury Severity Score (ICISS): a comparison of methodologies applied to linked data from New South Wales, Australia
Authors: Janneke Berecki-Gisolf, Ehsan Rezaei-Darzi, D Tharanga Fernando, Angelo DElia
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe International Classification of Disease Injury Severity Score (ICISS) provides an efficient method to determine injury severity in hospitalised injury patients. Injury severity metrics are of particular interest for the tracking of road transport injury rates and trends. The aims of this study were to calculate ICISS using linked morbidity and mortality datasets and to compare predictive ability of various methods and metrics.MethodsThis was a retrospective analysis of Admitted Patient Data Collection records from New South Wales, Australia, linked with mortality data. Using a split sample approach, design data (2008–2014; n=1 035 174 periods of care) was used to derive survival risk ratios and calculate various ICISS scales based on in-hospital death and 3-month death. These scales were applied to testing data (2015–2017; n=575 306). Logistic regression modelling was used to determine model discrimination and calibration.ResultsThere were 12 347 (1.19%) in-hospital deaths and 29 275 (2.83%) 3-month deaths in the design data. Model discrimination ranged from acceptable to excellent (area under the curve 0.75–0.88). Serious injury (ICISS≤0.941) rates in the testing data varied, with a range of 10%–31% depending on the methodology. The ‘worst injury’ ICISS was always superior to ‘multiplicative injury’ ICISS in model discrimination and calibration.ConclusionsIn-hospital death and 3-month death were used to generate ICISS; the former is recommended for settings with a focus on short-term threat to life, such as in trauma care settings. The 3-month death approach is recommended for outcomes beyond immediate clinical care, such as injury compensation schemes.Keywords: Design, Severity Scales, Outcome of Injury

The results of this study provide various ICISS metrics; it is recommended that the ICISS based on death in hospital is best used in settings with a focus on short-term threat to life, such as trauma care settings, whereas the ICISS based on death within 3 months is recommended for use in settings with longer-term outcome trajectories, such as injury compensation schemes.

Introduction

Road safety has been a focus area for public health and injury prevention, globally, since the 1999 World Disaster Report.1 This led to the launch of the decade of action in 2011, which used five pillars to guide the implementation of good practices: road safety management, safer roads and mobility, safer vehicles, safer road users, and post-crash response.2 3 Although there have been some country-level successes in this decade, the number of road deaths globally has plateaued.4 In order to address the road injury problem, it is crucial to monitor road transport injuries and fatalities, and use this information in the evaluation of road safety programmes.

Injuries can range from mild (without need for medical attention) to fatal. For effective monitoring of road transport injuries over time, and for comparing injury trends per road user type and by region, consistent injury indicators need to be used. Routinely collected health data are widely used for tracking and monitoring road transport (and other) injury trends. Re-use of datasets such as hospital separations records for injury surveillance is relatively time-effective and cost-effective. Furthermore, there is no self-report bias, reliance on police reports or compensable status, and data capture and completeness levels are likely to be steady over time. Conversely, police reporting has been shown to underestimate road transport serious injury rates, especially for cyclist and motorcyclist injuries.5,8 Injury claims databases, which are another source of administrative data related to injuries, are limited to compensated injuries, which (depending on the jurisdiction and injury compensation scheme) may also under-represent cyclist and motorcyclist injuries.

Hospital admission has been used as threshold indicator for injury incidence; however, this is not ideal as hospitalisation can also be influenced by factors other than severity.9 For example, theatre availability, distance from patient’s residence, payment systems, and patient preference can all influence hospitalisation. Therefore, injury-specific severity metrics have been developed: these generally use health records with coded injury diagnoses, and base injury severity on the survivability of the injury.10

Traditionally, the Injury Severity Score (ISS)11 has been used as measure of injury severity; the ISS relies on consensus driven estimates of injury severity, based on threat to life. A newer, entirely data-derived alternative to gauge injury severity is the ICISS (ICD-based Injury Severity Score). Similar to the ISS, the ICISS is also applied to coded health data to determine threat to life. To generate the ICISS, survival risk ratios (SRRs) are derived by calculating survival proportions for each injury diagnosis code. In cases where there are several injuries recorded in the same hospital admission, SRRs can be multiplied (multiplicative method) or alternatively, the SRR of the most severe injury can be selected (worst injury method).12 13 ICD-9 codes were first directly mapped to mortality in a study by Osler et al.14 Both ISS and ICISS are directly or indirectly based on injury survivability, which is a limitation, as injury survivability has improved over time.15

In Australia, the ICD-10 (and also the Australian modification: the ICD-10-AM) was first used to determine its performance in predicting threat to life in a study by Stephenson et al; it was found to be a reasonable method and potentially well suited to monitor time trends in serious injury rates.16 The ICD-10-AM based ICISS was applied to the Victorian State Trauma Registry and compared with AIS based mortality predictions in a study by Willis et al, and found to be a useful alternative.17 Several Australasian road transport injury studies have used the ICD-10-AM based ICISS.18,20 The study by McDonald et al further supports the utility of ICD-10-AM for tracking severe injury in road transport, as the comparison method, police-reported severity, was again reported to be a poor predictor of threat to life.20

The ICISS is empirically derived by calculating survival proportions for each injury diagnosis code. As survivability of injuries can differ by place and change over time (ideally, survivability improves over time with improved care), ICISS needs to be local and current to achieve an accurate estimation of serious injury rates. To track severe injuries due to road transport in the state of New South Wales (NSW), this study is designed to derive and test ICD-10 based ICISS metrics. To improve performance, ICISS is derived using linked death data.21 22 Prehospital deaths are included in the analysis: these are injury-related mortality data of those who died without making it to hospital. Including prehospital deaths has been recommended, to determine injury mechanism and contributing factors and inform care processes.23 In a study including prehospital deaths in derivation of ICISS, it has been shown that including death data and comorbidity in the ICISS improves the measures of concordance and calibration.22 Therefore, the aims of this study are: (1) to provide ICISS measures for NSW, Australia, in particular for application to road transport, using injury-related hospital admissions linked with mortality data; and (2) to evaluate predictive ability and compare the performance of the various methods. Various methods are tested and compared in terms of accuracy, such as the worst injury versus all injuries (multiplicative method); the calculation and testing of ICISS are carried out on all-cause injury data.

Methods

This study was a retrospective analysis of NSW hospital data linked with mortality data: injury-related admissions were selected and followed up within subsequent hospital transfers to determine in-hospital death and within linked mortality data to determine death within 3 months. We calculated the SRRs (also termed diagnosis-specific survival probabilities) for each injury-related diagnosis code in the data; these were ICD-10-AM coded. Hospital admissions were grouped into periods of care (POCs). Mortality outcomes included in-hospital death as well as 3-month death, determined from linked death data. ‘Prehospital deaths’, that is, injury deaths without preceding injury-related hospital admission, were also included in the SRR calculations. The available data were split into two cohorts: a design cohort for calculating SRRs, and a testing cohort for evaluating the newly generated ICISS and its permutations (such as the worst-injury method vs multiplicative method; and in-hospital death vs 3-month death).

Data sources

The morbidity and mortality data for this study were NSW Admitted Patient Data Collection (APDC) from the NSW Ministry of Health; the Cause of Death—Unit Record Files (CODURF); and the Registry of Births, Deaths and Marriages (RBDM). The CODURF data (up to 31 December 2017) constitutes cause of death, while the RBDM data (up to 31 December 2019) does not include coded diagnosis data but was used to supplement data on fact of death. The CODURF and RBDM death datasets were not perfectly matched: 4088/248059 (1.6%) CODURF cases were unmatched by person identifier and death date but only 18/428059 (0.007%) cases were unmatched by person identifier. Person-level, state-wide inpatient hospital records were probabilistically linked with death records by the Centre Health Record Linkage at the NSW Ministry of Health.

Sample

Hospital records with an injury-related ICD-10-AM code (in the range of S00-T98) were selected. Cases were limited to residents of NSW, Australia. Only those with sex recorded as male or female were included, to avoid small cell counts and therefore to preserve data confidentiality. Cases from the first of January 2008 to the end of December 2014 were allocated to the design cohort; cases from the first of January 2015 to the end of December 2017 were allocated to the testing cohort. Hospital records were grouped as POCs, as described in the next section, and matched with the relevant records in death datasets.

Periods of care

POCs were defined as periods of hospital stay initiated by an index admission and including subsequent, related transfers and changes in care type. Index injury admissions were identified as records with an injury (ICD-10-AM codes in the range of S00-T98) as the first-occurring, or principal, diagnosis codes. Subsequent, related hospital admission records, which could include inward transfers from other hospitals and changes in care type within the same hospital, were identified and grouped together with the index injury admission, and collapsed into POCs. POCs map the patient’s journey through the hospital system, considering all contiguous episodes, their dates, recorded admission and separation modes and care types. POCs included subsequent injury admissions that immediately followed the previous admissions (within a day); that is, transfers and statistical separations were kept. In constructing POCs, transfers were identified: episodes with overlapping dates, nested episodes where one occurs ‘nested’ within the time frame of another episode, serially occurring episodes, same-day episodes and sequential episodes with a change in episode type were accounted for in the grouping. Where two injury related POCs were ≤1 day apart, the latter POC was excluded. POCs starting with a statistical separation or transfer episode were also excluded. All non-injury POCs were excluded.

Prehospital injury deaths

Prehospital injury deaths were deaths due to an injury, without a time-matched recorded hospital admission: that is, the person died at the scene. These cases were included in the SRR calculations. Injury deaths were selected as deaths with an injury (ICD-10: S00-T89) recorded in any of the 20 Record Axis Data (RACS) fields as well a recorded external cause of death (ICD-10-AM: V00-Y84) in the underlying cause of death data field. Prehospital injury deaths were selected as injury deaths that occurred between 1 January 2008 and 31 December 2014 due to an injury. If the death occurred within 90 days of an injury-related hospital discharge, the death was considered most likely to be an outcome of the hospital-admitted injury and was therefore excluded from the prehospital injury deaths.

Mortality outcomes

Death outcomes were calculated using POC hospital admission data. Deaths that were recorded in the hospital admissions in a POC were selected as in-hospital deaths. Deaths that were recorded in either of the linked mortality datasets (RBDM and CODURF) were selected as 3-month deaths if they occurred within 3 months of the index admission date of the POC.

Developed SRRs and ICISS

In the design cohort, all injury-related ICD-10-AM codes of episodes in each POC were used to calculate SRRs. ICD-10-AM codes can be very detailed, containing up to five characters. For the purpose of this study, to avoid unnecessary detail and to limit the inclusion of specific, detailed diagnoses with low cell counts, diagnosis codes truncated to a maximum of four characters were used to generate developed SRRs. Prehospital injury deaths in the design cohort were included in the SRR calculation. Injury deaths that did not occur within 90 days of an injury admission were defined as prehospital death. For each diagnosis code, SRRs were calculated by dividing the total number of surviving cases by the total number of cases: the SRR was therefore the surviving proportion, per injury code.14 These calculations were repeated after stratification of the data by age groups (0–14; 15–24; 25–64; 65–79; 80+ years).

Using the SRRs that were developed in the design cohort, ICISS was calculated in the design and the testing cohorts. ICISS calculations were based on the index admission diagnosis codes. The full-length index diagnosis codes were applied to generate existing and developed ICISS. ICISS was calculated using several different methods: (1) using the ‘worst injury’ versus the ‘multiplicative’ method; (2) using age-stratified versus all ages SRRs; (3) using SRRs based on in-hospital mortality versus using SRRs based on 3-month death. The original SRRs calculated for Australia16 were also applied, for comparison purposes. Finally, to limit the effect of SRRs that were calculated on very rare injuries (yielding potentially unreliable estimates), a ‘conservative estimate’ was generated by omitting SRRs based on injury diagnosis codes with <5 cases in the design data. Finally, ICISS was classified as serious injury where ICISS≤0.941.24 25

Statistical analysis

The various ICISS associated with each of the mortality outcomes were modelled using logistic regression in the testing cohort. Model discrimination and calibration were used to check the performance of the ICISS. The area under curve (AUC) was used to assess the discrimination. An AUC in the range of 0.7–<0.8 was considered acceptable; AUC in the range of 0.8–<0.9 was considered excellent and AUC of ≥0.9 was considered outstanding.26 Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness of fit test was used to assess the model calibration, along with plots of the observed versus predicted probabilities of the death outcomes.

Patient and public involvement

Patients or the public were not involved in the design or interpretation of this study.

Results

The design cohort consisted of 1 035 174 POCs, by 801 432 people, for the period 2008–2014. In this 7-year period, there were 12 347 (1.19%) in-hospital deaths and 29 275 (2.83%) deaths that occurred within 3 months of admission (table 1). Some of the 3-month deaths have been double counted, in cases where a death falls within the 3-month window of more than one period of care of one individual. There were 27 504 3-month deaths without double-counting. The testing cohort consisted of 575 306 POCs, by 428 621 people, for the period of 2015–2017. In this 3-year period, there were 5456 (0.95%) hospital deaths and 15 226 (2.65%) deaths that occurred within 3 months of admission (table 1). There were 14 026 3-month deaths without double-counting related to patients with multiple POCs. There was inconsistency in linked hospital and mortality data: 3.18% and 2.64% of in-hospital deaths recorded in the hospital data were not recorded as deaths within 3 months in the Design and Test data, respectively. However, when considering all deaths (not limited to deaths that occurred within 3 months of hospitalisation), inconsistency reduced to 0.65% and 0.35% for design and test data, respectively.

Overall, calculated serious injury rates were consistently higher in the testing cohort than in the design cohort. The serious injury rates that were calculated based on ICISS varied considerably for the various methodologies. The ICISS based on in-hospital deaths resulted in lower rates of serious injury compared with the ICISS based on 3-month death. ICISS calculated using the ‘multiplicative method’ had higher rates of serious injury compared with the ICISS calculated using the ‘worst injury’ method; this held true for each of the three groups, in both design and test cohorts. Serious injury rates derived from the ‘age-group specific’ ICISS were higher than those resulting from the all-age ICISS; this held true for most of the in-hospital deaths results but not consistently for the 3-month deaths results. In the ICISS for 3-month death, using the multiplicative method, the age-group specific ICISS resulted in the higher rates of serious injury than the all-age ICISS.

The AUC ranged from 0.752 to 0.880; this range is classified as acceptable to excellent. The original Australian ICISS resulted in excellent model discrimination, when applied to linked hospital data with in-hospital death as outcome. When applied to hospital data with linked death data to determine 3-month death, model discrimination was acceptable. The model discrimination was inferior to the new indices based on in-hospital deaths, but superior to the new indices based on 3-month deaths; however, the former is a better suited comparison. Notably, the original ICISS was based on unlinked hospital data and therefore the comparability of the various methods is of limited value.

Generally, the AUC indicates that in terms of model discrimination, the ‘worst injury’ ICISS was always superior to the ‘multiplicative injury’ ICISS. The results for the ‘conservative’ method, which does not use SRRs that were calculated from <5 cases, were almost identical to the ‘all SRRs’ method, in terms of model discrimination and calibration (Hosmer-Lemeshow statistics). In addition, the age group specific ICISS consistently performed better than general ICISS with regards to model discrimination, as shown in the increased AUC. The AUC for the ICISS based on 3-month death were superior to those ICISS based on in-hospital death outcomes however the difference was inconsistent and marginal in size in terms of model discrimination (AUC).

Discussion

This study provides newly calculated ICISS for injury hospitalisations in NSW, Australia. Using a split sample method, the new ICISS was based on a design dataset and the subsequent validation was based on a testing dataset; both consisted of linked hospital admission and mortality data. Serious injury rates were found to vary within the testing data, with a range of 10%–31%, depending on the ICISS methodology that was applied. Model discrimination ranged from acceptable to excellent, in the newly calculated scores as well as the original Australian ICISS, which was applied for comparison. Model discrimination was poorer when testing the 3-month death outcomes than the hospital death outcomes, with areas under the curve below 0.800 for the general ICISS (although still in the acceptable range). Model calibration was poor in all models.

ICISS validation studies generally report on mortality rates, predictive power, model discrimination and model calibration, without necessarily presenting the rates of serious injury, based on the ICISS cut-off of ≤0.941.24 25 Australian studies using a similar linked-data approach, and presenting serious injury trends, are scarce, and some of these are focused on paediatric populations only, which have adjusted cut-off levels for serious injury.27 Overall, the 11%–14% serious injury rates reported in the current study, using the original Australian SRRs and ‘worst injury’ method, are similar to the 12.5% serious injury reported by Fernando et al for a comparable hospitalised injury sample in the state of Victoria.28 In their study, the original Australian SRRs were mapped to the data and the ‘worst injury’ method was used to calculate ICISS in the Victorian study.

The utility of the ICISS as measure of serious injury has been reported by many.12,1416 17 27 29 30 While the advantages, such as ease of use in large, administrative datasets, have been well established, some drawbacks of ICISS have also been noted. Foremost, the multiplicity of methodologies for ICISS have been stated as a weakness; in a systematic review of injury severity scoring tools, Tohira et al described the predictive performance of the ICISS as ‘unstable’.31 Willis et al also noted the dependence of ICISS performance on a range of factors, such as the types of datasets to which it is applied; the version of the ICD that is employed; the methods for estimating and implementing SRRs; and the covariates that are included in subsequent modelling.17 Indeed, a wide range of methods is available for calculating ICISS: in terms of the data source for the SRRs, which can be locally derived from the data or mapped using existing SRRs; in terms of mortality outcomes, which can range from in-hospital death, to 30-day or 90-day mortality, based on hospital data or linked mortality data; and in terms of the formula used for calculating ICISS, using the ‘worst injury’ or ‘multiplicative’ method. ‘Worst injury’ has been reported to provide superior estimates by some12 13 17 32 whereas others have reported better estimates using the multiplicative method.14 27 33 The choice of methods has a pronounced effect on the results. This has been confirmed in the current study using NSW data; furthermore, this has been extended to demonstrate the effect of the methodology not only on the validation but also on the reported prevalence of serious injury in the study population.

In determining which ICISS methodology is most suited, we should consider not only the performance of ICISS in terms of validation, but also its purpose and the setting. Regarding the former, the current study findings support the use of the ‘worst injury’ method, and the use of age-stratified SRRs. A conservative approach, utilising only SRRs that were calculated based on ≥5 cases, seems prudent, although there was marginal to no effect of this on the validation metrics. This is intuitive, as the design dataset is of the same origin as the testing dataset. Injury types that were extremely rare in the design data were also likely to be extremely rare in the Testing data and therefore were unlikely to make a significant contribution to the validation metrics. Regarding the purpose for which the ICISS is used, we need to be mindful that use of in-hospital deaths versus the use of linked data to establish 3-month deaths provide very different estimates of serious injury rates. Rather than trying to establish one approach to be superior to the other, these can be viewed as addressing different purposes. The in-hospital death method is likely to capture mostly deaths that are directly related to the injury: therefore, this method has a high injury specificity. Conversely, the 3-month death method may also capture deaths due to unrelated causes postdischarge, and therefore has lower injury specificity. However, some delayed injury deaths that occurred after hospital discharge will be captured using this method: compared with the in-hospital death method, it has a higher sensitivity. Viewing it as such may help to determine which method is best suited for a particular purpose. For example, injury compensation claims research may be best served using the 3-month death method, as compensation generally covers the recovery period beyond hospital discharge, and longer-term outcomes need to be accounted for. For hospital-based trauma care purposes, the in-hospital death method may be better suited for understanding the proximal impact of clinical care, and simply to gauge relative injury severity at outset.

A final note on the choice of methodology, is that when comparing serious injury rates between jurisdictions and tracking serious injury rates over time, it is essential to: (1) ensure that datasets are similar in design and version of ICD-10 coding; (2) consistently use the same mortality outcome measure, such as in-hospital death or 3-month death. Even in-hospital death can be established in different ways: using the index admission only, or using the discharge status of the final readmission for the same injury.16 34 (3) Apply the same sets of SRRs, using the mapping method, to all datasets, to ensure a fair comparison; and (4) use consistent ICISS methodology: either ‘worst injury’ or the multiplicative method.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths in terms of methodological approach. The use of linked data has added value by enabling the grouping of hospital admission records into POCs, which allows for detection of in-hospital deaths that occurred after transfer or readmission. Furthermore, linkage to mortality data has enabled the identification of death within 3 months of the injury; using this linked data methodology, it was possible to compare these outcomes in terms of the resulting SRRs and ICISS validity. Inclusion of prehospital deaths further contributed to completeness of the SRRs and more accurate assessment of threat to life in this study.22 23 Finally, using a split sample approach, the newly derived SRRs were tested on a separate testing dataset, further contributing to the robustness of the methods.

This study also has limitations which need to be acknowledged. First, the SRRs produced in this study are primarily intended for local use, in-hospital data from NSW, Australia. The locally derived indices are a strength, as they are ideally suited for the time and place, but also a limitation, as they cannot be applied to data from other jurisdictions without a preceding validation. However, there is no reason to suppose that the conceptual findings, such as those resulting from comparisons between the various methods and outcomes, are not generalisable beyond the local setting. It is, however, recommended that the SRRs derived in this study are applied and tested for accuracy in other jurisdictions, and compared with other local SRRs, for better understanding of local differences in threat-to-life associated with injury. It is also recommended that the derived SRR are used as relative, not absolute, measures of injury severity. Although there was reasonable correspondence between mean observed and expected survival, the model calibration was poor, with underestimated risks as demonstrated in the figures.35 The SRRs presented in this study should therefore be used as relative injury severity measures, not as absolute measures of threat to life: this relates to the model calibration, which was poor in this study, but also the expected change over time in injury survivability.15 Absolute risk estimates will be further compromised when the current SRRs are applied to new data with a different patient survival profile. Finally, comorbidities were not included in the analyses in this study. It would be useful in future studies to include comorbidity, as this has been reported to improve predictions.36 Pre-existing medical conditions are associated with mortality, in patients with mild to moderately severe injuries.37 Future studies to capture the effects of comorbidity on injury severity metrics are recommended; in doing so, newly developed Australian injury comorbidity indices can be used,38 in place of the Charlson comorbidity index, which was reported in 1987 and not designed specifically for injury hospitalisation samples.39

In conclusion, this study presents newly derived SRRs and subsequent validation of the ICISS for the state of NSW, Australia. Based on linked data, both in-hospital death and 3-month death were used to generate and test the ICISS; the mortality outcome had a profound effect on the calculated prevalence of severe injury. The former approach, using in-hospital death, is recommended for settings requiring high injury specificity, and a focus on short-term threat to life, such as in trauma care settings. The latter approach, using 3-month death, has higher sensitivity and is recommended for use in settings with outcome trajectories that span beyond the immediate clinical care, such as injury compensation schemes. Overall, the ‘worst injury’ method was found to perform better than the ‘multiplicative method, and the ICISS based on age-stratified SRRs performed better than the ICISS based on all-age SRRs.
