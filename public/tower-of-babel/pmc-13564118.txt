From cyberbullying to criminal liability: Digital forensic markers in cases of suspected abetment to suicide
Authors: Nurzhan Saulen, Almas Kanatovich Kanatov, Zhauhar Kenesbaevna Kozhantayeva, Orynbassar Tlepbergenov, Dinara Osmanova
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCyberbullying that escalates into a victim's suicide poses significant challenges at the intersection of criminal law, causation analysis, and digital forensics. This article aims to examine how four jurisdictions - Kazakhstan, the United States, Germany, and India - conceptualise and assign criminal liability for suspected abetment to suicide conducted through online harassment, and to identify the evidentiary thresholds that shape prosecutorial decision-making.Methodscomprise comparative doctrinal analysis of legislation and published judgments, supplemented by structured document analysis of official materials and digital-evidence standards. The document was the unit of analysis. The source corpus included statutes and amendments, reported judicial decisions, official prosecutorial and institutional materials, and standards or guidance. Sources were compared across legal basis, prohibited conduct, mens rea, causation, victim autonomy, procedural status, and the evidentiary functions of digital records.Resultsshow substantial divergence: Kazakhstan expressly criminalises online forms of driving, inducing, or assisting suicide; India retains a statutory abetment offence but applies a demanding test of intent and proximity; the United States relies on state-specific offences or manslaughter; Germany generally precludes homicide attribution where suicide reflects autonomous decision-making. The reviewed authorities show that attributed communications, call records, and complete timelines can bear on authorship, knowledge, sequence, autonomy, and causation, but their legal effect depends on the applicable offence.Conclusionsemphasise that rigorous digital-evidence procedures and harmonised investigative standards are essential for fair, reproducible adjudication of cyber-induced suicides, while preserving due-process and free-expression safeguards. Practically, legislators should define prohibited conduct, culpability, causal proximity, vulnerability, and autonomy safeguards; investigators should document source, lawful authority, acquisition, timestamps, integrity checks, analysis, and every transfer so that digital evidence can be assessed across borders.Keywords: Cyberbullying, Abetment to suicide, Digital evidence, Digital forensics, Chain of custody, Comparative law, Social media harassment

Introduction

In the age of pervasive connectivity, words can kill. For this article, cyberbullying is understood, following Smith et al., as intentional harmful conduct carried out through electronic communication, repeated over time against a person who cannot easily defend himself or herself[ [1], p. 376]. Empirical research reports an association between cyberbullying victimisation and suicide-related outcomes [2], while cases such as Carter have brought the separate question of criminal attribution before courts [3]. When a bullied teenager or a blackmailed victim dies by suicide, society and legal systems face a haunting question: should the instigators be held criminally liable for abetment to suicide? This question straddles sensitive fault lines between free will and culpability, between speech and crime. On one hand, suicide is an act of the victim; on the other, the role of extreme bullying in eroding a victim's will to live cannot be ignored [2,3] (see Table 1).

High-profile cases across jurisdictions have underscored this dilemma. In Massachusetts (USA), 18-year-old Conrad Roy's 2014 death led to an unprecedented manslaughter conviction of his girlfriend Michelle Carter, who had barraged him with text messages encouraging him to follow through on his suicidal thoughts [3]. Kazakhstan addresses the digital setting in Article 105(2)(4): use of telecommunications networks or information-technology facilities, including the Internet, is an aggravated form of the offence [4]. Germany takes a different starting point but does not exclude liability in every case. Assistance or encouragement is generally not attributed as homicide where the person made and carried out a freely responsible decision to die. Liability may nevertheless arise where the decision was impaired by immaturity, acute mental disorder, coercion, deception, or comparable pressure and the defendant exercised control over the fatal course of events. A person who performs the fatal act may also fall within the homicide provisions, including section 216 StGB where the killing follows the victim's express and serious request [[5], [6], [7], [8], [9]]. India now addresses abetment of suicide under sections 45 and 108 of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023; earlier conduct remains governed by the saved provisions of the Indian Penal Code [10].

The comparative legal landscape is thus varied and evolving. Kazakhstan explicitly criminalises “Driving to Suicide” in Article 105 of its Criminal Code, encompassing threats, cruel treatment or systematic humiliation that causes a person to attempt or die by suicide, as well as inducing or assisting suicide [4]. Aggravating circumstances - notably use of telecommunications networks or information-technology facilities, including the Internet, or conduct against a minor - increase the penalties [4]. India's current regime is in sections 45 and 108 of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023 (BNS), which define abetment and punish abetment of suicide by up to ten years' imprisonment and a fine; section 358 preserves liabilities and proceedings arising under the earlier Indian Penal Code [10]. Indian courts require explicit instigation or intentional aid, or a sufficiently proximate course of conduct from which an intention to incite suicide can reasonably be inferred; mere harassment does not suffice [11,12]. The United States lacks a single federal offence on inducing suicide; state approaches vary. In Massachusetts, Carter affirmed that verbal acts communicated by text and telephone can constitute wanton or reckless conduct when the facts establish causation [3]. German doctrine instead uses free responsibility as a central rule of attribution. Where the person retains control over the final act and the decision is autonomous, assistance is generally not treated as homicide; where the decision is not freely responsible and a third party intentionally controls the process, homicide liability through indirect perpetration may arise [[6], [7], [8]].

The four jurisdictions were selected purposively, not as a representative sample of world law. Kazakhstan supplies an express offence with an Internet aggravator [4]; the United States supplies state-specific routes, illustrated here by Massachusetts manslaughter [3]; India combines a statutory abetment offence with a high judicial threshold [[10], [11], [12]]; and Germany places free responsibility and control at the centre of attribution [[5], [6], [7], [8], [9]]. The comparison is therefore one of contrasting legal models. Countries that do not separately criminalise suicide-related conduct fall outside the sample, which limits generalisation.

While laws set the stage, digital forensics provides the proof. In cyberbullying-linked suicides, the perpetrator's actions leave an electronic trail - a series of digital footprints that investigators must carefully follow and preserve. Text messages, chat logs, social media postings, emails, search histories, even video evidence can all serve as digital forensic markers of the suspect's role in instigating or aggravating the victim's suicidal crisis [20]. These cases test the capabilities of forensic experts: can they recover deleted WhatsApp messages that contained the fatal taunts? Can they tie an anonymous social media account to the accused? Can they prove that a victim's final ominous status update or a cryptic suicide note on their phone was induced by months of online abuse? The integrity and admissibility of such digital evidence are paramount. Failure to properly authenticate a screenshot or to maintain chain-of-custody for a seized smartphone can derail a prosecution and deny justice in these emotionally charged cases [21].

This article adopts an interdisciplinary approach - drawing on substantive criminal law, procedure, and forensic science - to examine how cyberbullying transitions from an online phenomenon into courtroom evidence and criminal liability. We first set out the comparative doctrinal design, source selection, and common analytical dimensions; a separate Digital-forensic context section summarises the evidence-preservation standards used as an analytical benchmark. Next, in Results, we present a comparative legal analysis grounded in real cases from Kazakhstan, the U.S., Germany, and India, highlighting how each jurisdiction's legal framework addresses the concept of incitement or assistance in suicide, especially via digital communications. Within each jurisdiction, the Results present the statutory framework and a small set of illustrative authorities before a separate cross-jurisdictional comparison. In the Discussion, we critically analyse these findings: What are the challenges in proving a causal link between online harassment and suicide beyond a reasonable doubt? How do different legal cultures balance free speech and personal autonomy against the urge to punish those whose hateful conduct had lethal consequences? We also explore the practical difficulties investigators face, such as jurisdictional hurdles in obtaining data from foreign-based tech companies, and the ethical questions around accessing a deceased victim's digital accounts. We also note the procedural status of current legislative proposals. Finally, our Conclusion underscores the necessity of clear laws and robust forensic methodologies to ensure that when cyberbullying does lead to irreparable tragedy, the digital cries for help and the accused's digital abuses can be translated into credible evidence in court. The convergence of effective digital forensic investigation with well-crafted legal frameworks will determine whether justice can be done in these profoundly tragic cases.

Digital-forensic context

Digital-evidence handling is assessed against standards and guidance issued by the International Organization for Standardization and the International Electrotechnical Commission (ISO/IEC), the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST), and the Scientific Working Group on Digital Evidence (SWGDE).

Digital evidence identification: The first step in these investigations is to identify all potential sources of digital evidence related to the interactions between the victim and the suspected offender. Investigators typically create an evidence inventory spanning personal devices (smartphones, laptops, tablets), online accounts (social media profiles, messaging apps, email services), and other digital services (cloud storage, blogs, gaming or forum accounts) of both the victim and the suspect. In the context of suspected abetment to suicide, the most probative evidence is often the communication trail between victim and accused. For example, in the Carter case, critical evidence included dozens of SMS texts and call logs between Michelle Carter and Conrad Roy in the days and hours leading up to his death [3]. Such leads direct investigators to specific platforms (e.g., WhatsApp, Facebook, Instagram, VKontakte, SMS records) to target for evidence collection.

Collection and preservation: Once identified, digital evidence sources must be collected in a forensically sound manner. This involves creating exact copies of data (for instance, imaging a phone or computer drive, or capturing social media content via platform data export tools or legal requests) while avoiding any alteration of the original data. Standard practice is guided by International Organization for Standardization and International Electrotechnical Commission (ISO/IEC) 27037:2012, which addresses identification, collection, acquisition, and preservation of digital evidence [21]. According to ISO/IEC 27037, it is imperative to preserve the integrity and authenticity of digital evidence; this means the data should remain unmodified from the point of collection, or if any changes occur, they must be documented [21]. In practical terms, digital evidence first responders use write-blocking hardware and specialised software tools to image devices and to hash data before and after collection, ensuring that later analysis can be shown to reflect the original evidence [21]. For cloud-based or server-held data, investigators typically issue preservation orders and legal requests, including mutual legal assistance (MLA) requests where required, to service providers to obtain the records.

For provider-held records, preservation and production are separate tasks. A preservation request seeks to prevent deletion while the competent authority obtains the process required for disclosure. The request should identify the account or subscriber, provider, relevant period, categories of content and non-content data, and the legal authority relied upon. The record should also distinguish material supplied by a user, material acquired from a device, and material produced by a provider. Those sources may corroborate one another, but they do not carry the same provenance or establish authorship by themselves.

Collected evidence must be stored securely to prevent tampering. Typical practice is to use evidence preservation facilities such as locked evidence rooms or secure digital repositories. For digital files, this often means copying them onto write-once media or secure forensic servers, and computing hash values (e.g., SHA-256) to detect any alteration. Chain-of-custody documentation begins at the moment of collection and continues through every hand-off or analysis step [[21], [22], [23], [24], [25], [26], [27]]. A chain-of-custody record is a log of who has accessed or possessed the evidence, at what times, and for what purpose [26]. It often takes the form of a form or series of forms accompanying the evidence bag or container. According to Scientific Working Group on Digital Evidence (SWGDE) best practices, chain-of-custody entries should be contemporaneous with collection and transfer and include a unique evidence identifier, the date and time, and each person taking possession[19, §§12-14]. This record is vital in court to establish that digital evidence has not been tampered with and is the same as that originally collected.

Forensic analysis techniques: After preservation, investigators employ various forensic tools and techniques to extract and analyse content relevant to the case. Mobile device forensics is often central, given that much cyberbullying occurs via smartphones. Investigators create a forensic image of the phone's storage (or perform a logical acquisition if physical imaging is not possible) and use validated tools to retrieve messages, call logs, contacts, social media application data, browser history, and images [20]. In the context of abetment to suicide, particular attention is paid to conversational evidence - e.g., SMS threads, WhatsApp or Telegram chats between victim and suspect, comments on the victim's social media posts that might show public shaming, and any digital suicide notes that could bear on the suspect's influence. In Carter, forensic analysis revealed not only the text messages Carter sent Roy, but also her later admissions to friends, including that she had told him to return to the truck [3]. Digital evidence may also be recovered from application databases or backups when a user has deleted the visible message, subject to the device, application, acquisition method, and validation of the tool used [20,27].

It is also essential to correlate digital evidence with the timeline of the victim's decline and eventual suicide. Investigators construct a chronology: for instance, what was the victim's last communication? Did they receive a threatening message on the same day they died by suicide? Victim device forensics can reveal whether the victim searched online for suicide methods after an argument, or saved screenshots of bullying messages. The importance of chronology is illustrated by BGH 4 StR 81/23, where the court assessed an 8-h telephone interaction together with earlier threats, the victim's severe depression and emotional dependence, and the events immediately preceding the suicide attempt [8].

Standards and validation: Throughout the digital evidence handling process, adherence to international standards and accepted best practices helps ensure that findings can be scrutinised. The functions are distinct: ISO/IEC 27037 addresses identification, collection, acquisition, and preservation; ISO/IEC 27041 addresses suitability and adequacy of investigative methods; ISO/IEC 27042 addresses analysis and interpretation; and ISO/IEC 27043 sets incident-investigation principles and processes [[21], [22], [23], [24]]. National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) Special Publication (SP) 800-86 offers a complementary four-phase model for forensic work in incident response [25], while current SWGDE guidance emphasises scoping, data integrity, hashing, contemporaneous documentation, and chain of custody [26,27]. An investigator must also validate forensic tools using known test data and maintain audit logs of the analysis conducted. If a chat log is presented as evidence of incitement, the defence may ask whether it was fabricated or altered. The answer lies in source documentation, hash-based integrity checks, chain of custody, reproducible analysis, and testimony explaining how the data were obtained and interpreted [[21], [22], [23], [24], [25], [26], [27]].

Interpretation requires the same discipline as acquisition. Isolated screenshots may omit earlier messages, replies, edits, reactions, or the identity of the device from which an account was accessed. Analysts should preserve the complete available exchange, document time-zone conversion and clock drift, distinguish platform timestamps from device display times, and record the steps used to attribute an account or message. Where a tool recovers deleted material, the report should identify the acquisition type, tool and version, relevant settings, validation information, and the limitations of the recovered artefact [[20], [21], [22], [23], [24], [25], [26], [27]].

In summary, the forensic sequence explains what information may be available and how its reliability can be tested. The legal analysis asks what each jurisdiction requires; the standards-informed analysis asks what attributed content, timing, metadata, and continuity documentation can contribute to proof of those requirements.

Methods

Study design: This research uses comparative doctrinal analysis, supplemented by structured document analysis, of publicly available legal and institutional materials. The document was the unit of analysis. The source corpus comprised statutes and amendments, reported court decisions, official prosecutorial or institutional materials, and standards or guidance issued by ISO/IEC, NIST, and SWGDE. Materials were identified in English, Russian, and German through official legislation and court portals, institutional websites, and targeted legal searches. The review included materials available up to 5 August 2026; the status of the cited legislation and pending proposals was verified as of that date. Jurisdictions were selected purposively to contrast an express offence with an Internet aggravator (Kazakhstan), state-specific manslaughter or assistance routes (United States), a statutory abetment offence applied under a high inferential threshold (India), and an autonomy-centred attribution model (Germany). Documents were compared across legal basis, prohibited conduct, mens rea, causation, victim autonomy, procedural status, and the evidentiary functions of digital records. A small set of illustrative judicial decisions and reported cases was selected within each jurisdiction for doctrinal relevance and procedural clarity. ISO/IEC, NIST, and SWGDE materials supplied normative benchmarks for evidence handling and interpretation [[20], [21], [22], [23], [24], [25], [26], [27]].

In summary, our methodological approach combines doctrinal comparison with a standards-informed evidential analysis. The standards define the information needed to authenticate and interpret digital records. This analytical separation distinguishes the legal elements that must be proved from the evidentiary functions that attributed content, timing, metadata, and continuity records may perform.

Results

Legal frameworks and selected cases by jurisdiction

Kazakhstan: criminalising online “driving to suicide”

Article 105 of Kazakhstan's Criminal Code now covers three forms of conduct: driving a person to suicide or an attempt through threats, cruel treatment or systematic humiliation; inducing suicide; and assisting suicide. The basic offence carries restriction of liberty or imprisonment for up to three years. Article 105(2)(4) raises the maximum to five years where telecommunications networks or information-technology facilities, including the Internet, are used. Article 105(3) prescribes imprisonment for five to nine years where the conduct is committed against a minor [4]. The present formulation was introduced by Law No. 72-VIII and took effect on 16 June 2024; the Supreme Court's current interpretive guidance addresses causation and fault [13,14].

The 2024 reform also added Article 313-1 on suicide propaganda. That provision concerns deliberate dissemination intended to induce suicide among an indeterminate audience where the elements of Article 105 are absent. The Supreme Court accordingly distinguishes victim-specific threats, cruel treatment, systematic humiliation, inducement or assistance under Article 105 from propaganda directed at an undefined group under Article 313-1 [4,14]. This distinction is especially relevant to open online groups: liability cannot be inferred from a label attached to the content; the intended audience and the proved mode of influence must be identified.

Published figures cited by Rakymbayeva record 51 registered offences under Article 105 in 2010, 63 in 2015, and 47 in 2022[23, p. 253]. These are national registration figures, not counts of prosecutions or convictions, and they pre-date the June 2024 amendment. They therefore indicate the number of offences entered in criminal statistics, not the number of cases proved in court. Separately, the Akmola Regional Prosecutor's Office reported in October 2024 that one case had been referred to Kokshetau City Court [15]; the release gives no final judgment. Keeping the data types separate avoids treating registered offences, referrals, and convictions as interchangeable.

Contemporary reporting on Kazakhstan's response to online ‘death communities’ recorded 63 cases of children's involvement at the beginning of 2017, the blocking of harmful groups, and 44 criminal cases opened during 2016 after 16 child deaths attributed by officials to such groups [16]. The report does not identify final judgments, case-specific digital exhibits, or a connection between those Kazakhstan proceedings and the prosecution of Philipp Budeikin in Russia. It is therefore used as contextual reporting rather than as a conviction count or proof of a joint investigation.

An official release issued by the Akmola Regional Prosecutor's Office on 14 October 2024 reports that Kokshetau prosecutors reopened an investigation into a woman's death by suicide. According to the release, investigators attributed the death to seven years of physical and psychological abuse by her cohabiting partner. The conduct was charged under Articles 105(2)(1) and 110(2)(1), and the case was sent to Kokshetau City Court [15]. The release documents a prosecution-stage decision only. It does not report a conviction, a final judicial finding, or the use of any particular item of digital evidence.

Analysis: Kazakhstan's framework should not be reduced to strict liability or a simple “but for” test. Paragraph 29 of the Supreme Court's Normative Resolution No. 1 makes a causal link between the defendant's conduct and the suicide or attempt a mandatory condition of Article 105 liability. For the form based on threats, cruel treatment or systematic humiliation, the conduct is intentional, while the actor's attitude to the resulting suicide or attempt is negligent. If the actor intends to cause death through the victim's suicide and desires that result, the conduct is classified as intentional killing under Article 99 [14]. The resolution treats humiliation as systematic where it occurs on three or more occasions. Use of the Internet changes the applicable penalty under Article 105(2)(4); it does not remove the need to prove conduct, fault, and a causal link. Digital records are therefore relevant to authorship, repetition, timing, knowledge of vulnerability, targeting, and the relationship between the communication and the suicide or attempt.

United States: patchwork approaches and a landmark case

United States law remains state-specific. There is no single federal offence that generally criminalises encouraging suicide, and state provisions differ in scope. Massachusetts prosecuted Carter under common-law involuntary manslaughter; Minnesota's statute prohibited intentionally advising, encouraging, or assisting suicide, but Melchert-Dinkel narrowed its speech-based terms on constitutional grounds [3,17]. Pending Massachusetts reform is treated separately from current law [18].

The case of Commonwealth v. Michelle Carter in Massachusetts became a defining moment in American jurisprudence on this issue. In July 2014, 18-year-old Conrad Roy died by suicide via carbon monoxide inhalation in his truck. Investigation revealed that his long-distance girlfriend, 17-year-old Michelle Carter, had through dozens of text messages encouraged him to go through with it and, critically, during the act, told him over the phone to “get back in” the carbon monoxide-filled truck when he had second thoughts [3]. Massachusetts had no specific offence at the time; S.3027 remained pending as of 5 August 2026 [18]. The local district attorney charged Carter with involuntary manslaughter, a common-law crime for causing death by wanton or reckless conduct. In 2017, the trial judge convicted Carter of involuntary manslaughter as a youthful offender; the Supreme Judicial Court of Massachusetts affirmed the judgment in 2019 [3]. The court held that after Roy had stepped out of the truck in panic, Carter's insistence that he return to the toxic environment, followed by her failure to seek help, constituted wanton and reckless conduct causing death[3, pp. 361-370]. Digital evidence was indispensable: text messages, call records, and Carter's admissions to friends supplied the sequence, her knowledge of Roy's condition, and corroboration of the fatal telephone instruction [3].

In State v. Melchert-Dinkel, the Minnesota Supreme Court reviewed online exchanges in which the defendant adopted false identities and gave two vulnerable persons detailed information about suicide methods. The court held that “assists” could cover targeted words that enabled a particular person's act, but severed “advises” and “encourages” because those terms swept too broadly across protected expression. It remanded because the trial court had not made findings under the narrowed assistance provision[26, slip op. pp. 16-21]. The case supplies a second U.S. model: a specific statute can reach targeted digital assistance, but liability cannot rest on offensive advocacy alone.

Analysis: Carter shows that Massachusetts common-law manslaughter can reach exceptional facts where knowledge, causal sequence, and wanton or reckless conduct are proved [3]. Melchert-Dinkel also shows the constitutional limit: a law may punish targeted assistance without criminalising broad advice or encouragement [17]. Any targeted reform must define the prohibited conduct, required mental state, causal proximity, and safeguards for protected expression and autonomous decisions; S.3027 remains a proposal rather than current law [18].

India: abetment of suicide and the high bar of instigation

India's current offence is found in section 108 of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023 (BNS), read with the definition of abetment in section 45. Section 108 applies where a person dies by suicide and the accused has abetted that act; the maximum penalty is ten years' imprisonment and a fine. Section 45 covers instigation, participation in a conspiracy followed by an act or illegal omission, and intentional aid by act or illegal omission. The BNS entered into force on 1 July 2024. For earlier conduct, section 358 preserves the operation, liabilities, and proceedings arising under the Indian Penal Code, including IPC sections 306 and 107 [10]. IPC authorities therefore remain relevant to the corresponding BNS provisions, but the temporal regime must be identified.

The judicial standard is demanding but is not confined to an express instruction to die. Ude Singh v. State of Haryana states that abetment may involve direct or indirect incitement. Mens rea may be inferred from an intentional and continuous course of conduct that leaves the victim perceiving no other option, provided the acts are sufficiently proximate and the inference rests on the accused's actual conduct and surrounding circumstances. The Court upheld the adult appellants' convictions on evidence of sustained, targeted humiliation and a proximate final incident; proceedings against an appellant who had been sixteen were terminated under juvenile protections[11, paras. 11.1-11.2, 15-16.1, 20-24, 26]. Nipun Aneja v. State of Uttar Pradesh marks the limiting side: the Supreme Court quashed section 306 proceedings because workplace pressure, a transfer decision, and a group meeting did not establish intentional and proximate instigation by the individual accused[12, paras. 20-27].

Analysis: Indian courts require evidence of explicit instigation or intentional aid, or a sufficiently proximate course of conduct from which an intention to incite suicide can reasonably be inferred; mere harassment or the victim's subjective sense of having no alternative does not, without more, establish abetment [11,12]. Digital messages may show continuity, targeting, knowledge, and proximity, but they do not displace proof of mens rea. The result is a high threshold without a categorical requirement for an express command.

Germany: suicide and the primacy of self-determination

German doctrine uses free responsibility as the starting point for homicide attribution. The Federal Constitutional Court treats the decision to end one's life as part of personal autonomy and requires that it be based on an informed and free will[5, paras. 203, 208-212, 232, 240-244]. Where a person retains control over the final act and the decision is autonomous, assistance is generally not treated as homicide. The position changes where immaturity, acute mental disorder, coercion, deception, or comparable pressure defeats free responsibility and a third party intentionally controls the fatal process; section 216 StGB separately covers killing on an express and serious request [[5], [6], [7], [8], [9]].

The Federal Court of Justice's decision in BGH 5 StR 132/18 marks the autonomy side of the boundary. Two elderly women decided independently to die, obtained medical advice, and themselves drank the prepared medication. The court upheld the doctor's acquittal on homicide counts because their decisions were freely responsible and they retained control over the life-ending acts[6, paras. 16-25].

BGH 4 StR 81/23 demonstrates the narrower route to liability. The defendant knew that the 77-year-old victim had severe depression and had become emotionally dependent on him. During a telephone call lasting more than 8 h, he repeatedly humiliated her, urged her to kill herself, and threatened further violence. The victim attempted suicide and survived. The Federal Court of Justice dismissed the defendant's appeal, leaving in force the conviction for attempted manslaughter by indirect perpetration. It held that the decision could be treated as non-autonomous and that the defendant sought to direct the process from a distance[8, paras. 3-20, 24-28]. This was an abusive-relationship case involving telephone communication, not a social-media cyberbullying death; it is used as a close remote-pressure analogue.

These decisions explain why the German position is neither categorical immunity nor a general offence of encouragement. The questions are whether the decision was freely responsible, who controlled the final act, and whether the defendant intended to use an impaired victim as an instrument against herself [[6], [7], [8]].

The German approach does not make digital evidence irrelevant; it changes what the evidence must prove. Complete communications may establish duration, threats, knowledge of depression or dependence, the victim's expressed resistance or doubt, and the defendant's efforts to control timing and method. The medium is not a separate ground of liability. Its legal significance lies in what the record shows about free responsibility, control, and the elements of any independent offences such as coercion, stalking, insult, or unlawful disclosure.

In the United States, the medium is not itself an element, but Carter and Melchert-Dinkel show how digital communications may supply the conduct, knowledge, and sequence [3,17]. India's offence applies regardless of medium; the record must still support intent and proximity [[10], [11], [12]]. Germany likewise attaches significance to what a communication proves about pressure, knowledge, free responsibility, and control rather than to the medium alone [8].

Discussion

The comparative results reveal a common attribution problem: when may another person's conduct be treated as legally relevant to a suicide or attempt? Kazakhstan addresses specified conduct through an express offence; Massachusetts and Minnesota use distinct common-law and statutory routes; India applies a demanding abetment test; and Germany distinguishes autonomous self-directed action from conduct that defeats free responsibility. The discussion considers causal inference, autonomy, cross-border evidence, and practical reforms.

Causal inference across legal tests: The Results identified jurisdiction-specific causation rules; the analytical task is to separate four propositions: attribution (who controlled the account or device); contextual meaning (the complete exchange rather than isolated wording); temporal sequence (including escalation and the final interaction); and competing explanations (prior history and intervening events). No marker is sufficient alone, and temporal proximity is not itself proof of causation.

The same record may therefore have different legal effects. In Carter, call history, messages, and admissions supported recklessness and causation at a defined moment [3]. Under BNS sections 45 and 108, comparable material still must support intentional instigation or aid and a proximate connection; Ude Singh permits inference from a targeted course of conduct, whereas Nipun Aneja shows why harassment without demonstrable mens rea is insufficient [[10], [11], [12]]. Digital forensics can reduce factual uncertainty, but it does not lower the substantive burden of proof.

Victim autonomy as a comparative legal filter: Victim autonomy is not a binary attribution of responsibility. German doctrine asks whether the decision remained free and responsible and whether the person retained control of the final act [[5], [6], [7], [8]]. Carter did not treat Roy as devoid of agency throughout: the critical interval began when he left the truck and Carter directed him to return, knowing the lethal conditions[3, pp. 366-370]. Minnesota similarly confines “assistance” to targeted conduct that enables suicide, leaving general advocacy outside the offence[26, slip op. pp. 16-21].

Kazakhstan's Article 105 contains no equivalent autonomy safe harbour, but dependency, repeated humiliation, targeted persuasion, and an independent decision remain relevant to causation and fault [4,14]. India applies a different safeguard: Ude Singh allows intent to be inferred from an intentional and proximate course of conduct, while Nipun Aneja cautions that the victim's distress or perception alone cannot establish the accused's mens rea [11,12]. Across all four models, the legally relevant questions are the accused's culpability, the degree of interference with decision-making, and the proximity between conduct and death.

Cross-border acquisition and the Budeikin case: Public reporting confirms that Philipp Budeikin was investigated and prosecuted in Russia and that the Tobolsk District Court sentenced him in July 2017 to three years and four months' imprisonment for conduct involving ‘death groups’ on VKontakte [28]. The Kazakhstan source used here records separate domestic measures in 2016-2017, including identification of children involved in online ‘death communities’, blocking of groups, and the opening of criminal cases [16]. Neither source reports a Kazakhstan-Russia mutual legal assistance request, extradition, joint investigative team, or transfer of digital evidence in Budeikin's prosecution. The case demonstrates the transnational circulation of the phenomenon, but it is not treated as proof of a documented bilateral operation. Mutual legal assistance treaties (MLATs) provide the formal basis for one state to request evidentiary or coercive acts from another. At the relevant time, assistance between Kazakhstan and Russia in criminal matters was available under the 1993 Minsk Convention. Articles 5-8 regulate transmission, content, and execution: competent authorities communicate through designated channels; assistance may include service of documents, searches, seizure and transfer of evidence, expert examination, questioning, tracing, prosecution, and extradition; a request must identify the case, persons, requested act, relevant facts, and legal characterisation; and the requested authority executes it under its own law and returns the resulting materials [29]. Applied to platform evidence, a request should specify the account, provider, relevant period, data sought, legal authority, and preservation requirements. The public sources on Budeikin do not establish that this mechanism was invoked.

Privacy is also an issue: after a suicide, accessing the victim's accounts can engage privacy and data-protection rules, particularly where messages contain third-party personal data. Serious-crime procedures may permit access, but the authority, scope, and handling of the material still require documentation. Access to private messages should therefore be justified under the applicable procedure and limited to what the investigation requires.

Chain of custody across borders: If digital evidence is collected abroad, its admissibility or weight may be challenged where the receiving authority cannot evaluate how it was obtained and handled. Harmonised procedures matter because a receiving court or laboratory must be able to understand the source, lawful authority, acquisition method, integrity checks, analysis, and every transfer. ISO/IEC 27037, 27041 and 27042 and SWGDE documentation requirements provide a common vocabulary for that assessment [[21], [22], [23], [24],26,27]. Compliance does not make evidence automatically admissible, but it makes foreign work transparent, reproducible, and open to challenge. This documented transparency is the practical basis on which authorities can scrutinise and trust evidence produced in another jurisdiction.

Legal reforms: Kazakhstan enacted Law No. 72-VIII, which revised Article 105 and added Article 313-1 on suicide propaganda, effective 16 June 2024 [4,13,14]. Massachusetts S.3027, a draft concerning harassment resulting in serious bodily injury or death, was referred to Senate Ways and Means on 2 April 2026 and remained pending on 5 August 2026 [18]. It is discussed as a proposal, not as current criminal law. In India, the BNS replaced the IPC for post-1 July 2024 conduct while preserving earlier liabilities and proceedings [10].

Recommendations: Drawing on the comparative insights, we propose:

-Where policymakers consider a targeted offence of malicious suicide encouragement, the provision should define the required purpose or knowledge, the relevance of victim vulnerability, causal proximity, and the boundary between punishable pressure and protected assistance. Germany demonstrates why a broad prohibition requires careful constitutional scrutiny and a safeguard for genuinely autonomous decisions [5]. Kazakhstan's separate treatment of victim-specific conduct under Article 105 and indefinite-audience propaganda under Article 313-1 supplies a different drafting model [4,14].

Dual use of digital markers: The same markers have two distinct uses. After a death, attributed content, timestamps, escalation patterns, account continuity, and preservation metadata help a court evaluate intent, causation, and authenticity. In a live case, they may support proportionate triage by a platform, school, clinician, or safeguarding authority before harm occurs. A single phrase should not be treated as a risk score: intervention requires context, pattern, corroboration, and safeguards against false positives and unnecessary disclosure. If a preventive alert prompts action, any evidential record should be preserved lawfully and with documented integrity. Prevention and prosecution therefore share an evidential substrate but serve different purposes.

Youth procedure and sociocultural context: Age affected procedure and disposition in two reviewed authorities but does not supply a uniform substantive test. Carter was prosecuted as a youthful offender, and the Supreme Judicial Court recorded that the trial judge had considered her age and maturity when evaluating whether her conduct was wanton or reckless[3, pp. 369-370]. In Ude Singh, juvenile protections terminated proceedings against a sixteen-year-old appellant while the adult convictions remained[11, paras. 11.1-11.2, 26]. Cultural context may nevertheless affect how distress, responsibility, and suicide-related conduct are understood and reported. Cross-cultural scholarship cautions that social determinants are mediated by cultural meanings and calls for fine-grained analysis rather than broad national generalisations[ [30], pp. 3, 9]. Suicide-related stigma may also encourage concealment and deter help-seeking[ [31], pp. 4-5]. In an evidentiary setting, those mechanisms may limit the communications and contextual information available to investigators. The available legal sources do not establish that culture or stigma changed charging or adjudication in the selected cases, and they do not support country-level claims about ‘Eastern,’ ‘Western,’ or ‘collectivist’ cultures. Culture and stigma are therefore treated as case-specific contextual factors, not as national explanations for legal outcomes.

Limitations: This analysis focuses on reported decisions, statutory texts, official materials, and selected publicly reported investigations. It is affected by litigation, publication, and reporting bias: unreported matters, cases resolved without charge, inaccessible files, and differences in language and database coverage are underrepresented. The four jurisdictions form a purposive, maximum-variation sample and do not represent the prevalence of legal models worldwide. The findings are confined to what can be established from publicly accessible legal and institutional documents; unpublished factual material and evidentiary detail may therefore be underrepresented. Pending legislation is reported only as a status snapshot dated 5 August 2026. The study therefore supports doctrinal comparison and standards-informed evidential analysis, not causal claims about national culture, deterrence, youth offending, or the frequency with which any digital marker changes an outcome.

Conclusion

This comparison shows that liability for suicide-related online conduct turns on jurisdiction-specific rules of culpability, causation, and autonomy rather than on the digital medium alone. Kazakhstan uses an express offence and Internet aggravator [4,14]; Massachusetts and Minnesota supply distinct common-law and statutory routes [3,17]; India requires intentional and proximate abetment [[10], [11], [12]]; and Germany centres attribution on free responsibility and control [[5], [6], [7], [8], [9]]. These models are not interchangeable, and the available public record does not support a claim of a uniform global trend.

Digital records nevertheless perform common evidential functions. Complete communications, account attribution, timing, metadata, acquisition records, and continuity documentation can test knowledge, targeting, sequence, free responsibility, and causal proximity. They cannot substitute for the elements of an offence or establish causation merely because a message preceded a death or attempt [3,8,11,12,14,17,[20], [21], [22], [23], [24], [25], [26], [27]]. Their value lies in reducing uncertainty about who communicated, what was said in context, when the relevant conduct occurred, and how the material was preserved and interpreted.

The comparative findings also caution against collapsing moral blame into legal causation. A complete digital record may establish sustained humiliation or explicit encouragement, yet each legal system still asks a different question: whether a specified course of conduct caused the result, whether the accused intended or recklessly created the relevant risk, whether instigation can be inferred from a proximate pattern, or whether the person's decision remained freely responsible. The evidentiary method must therefore remain element-led. Investigators should collect enough context to test both the prosecution hypothesis and plausible alternative explanations, while courts should resist treating a single phrase, a screenshot, or temporal proximity as conclusive.

The same discipline is necessary when evidence crosses borders. Prompt preservation may prevent loss, but preservation alone does not confer authority to disclose, prove authorship, or establish continuity. Requests, provider productions, device acquisitions, analytical steps, and transfers should form one reviewable record. Common ISO/IEC, NIST, and SWGDE concepts cannot displace national rules of procedure, but they can make the work intelligible to a foreign court and allow the defence to test it [[20], [21], [22], [23], [24], [25], [26], [27],29]. That transparency is central to both cooperation and fairness.

Two immediate priorities follow. First, policymakers who adopt or revise suicide-related offences should define the prohibited conduct, required culpability, causal proximity, treatment of vulnerability, and safeguards for protected expression and autonomous decision-making. Second, investigative authorities should require prompt lawful preservation and a documented, standards-aligned chain from acquisition through analysis and any cross-border transfer, so that the same record can be independently tested by every receiving authority [[20], [21], [22], [23], [24], [25], [26], [27],29].
