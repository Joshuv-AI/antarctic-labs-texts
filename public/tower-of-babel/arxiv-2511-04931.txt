Intersection numbers of the natural embedding of the twisted triality hexagon 𝖳 ⁡ ( q 3 , q ) \mathsf{T}(q^{3},q) in 𝖯𝖦 ⁡ ( 7 , q 3 ) \mathsf{PG}(7,q^{3}) Journal: Discrete Mathematics
Sebastian Petit, Geertrui Van de Voorde

Abstract
In this paper, we study and characterise the natural embedding of the twisted triality hexagon 𝖳 ⁡ ( q 3 , q ) \mathsf{T}(q^{3},q) in 𝖯𝖦 ⁡ ( 7 , q 3 ) \mathsf{PG}(7,q^{3}) . We begin by describing the possible intersections of subspaces of 𝖯𝖦 ⁡ ( 7 , q 3 ) \mathsf{PG}(7,q^{3}) with 𝖳 ⁡ ( q 3 , q ) \mathsf{T}(q^{3},q) . Then, we provide conditions on a set of lines ℒ \mathcal{L} which ensures that ℒ \mathcal{L} forms the line set of a naturally embedded twisted triality hexagon. This work follows up on similar results for the split Cayley hexagon by J. A. Thas and H. Van Maldeghem (2008) and F. Ihringer (2014).

Introduction

Generalised hexagons
, and the overarching
generalised polygons
, were introduced in 1959 by Jacques Tits
[
10
]
.
Since then, these highly symmetrical structures have been studied intensely for their intriguing properties.
The monograph
[
11

In the finite case, Feit and Higman have shown that thick generalised

[

Almost by definition, generalised triangles are equivalent to

Similarly, generalised quadrangles have their own extensive literature.

We focus on generalised hexagons.
Up to duality, only two classes of finite thick generalised hexagons are known: the

of order
(q,q)
and the

of order
(q^{3},q)

[

This paper contributes to this study by characterising the natural embedding of the twisted triality hexagon
\mathsf{T}(q^{3},q)
in
\text{PG}(7,q^{3})
. In accordance with
[

]
and
[

]
we focus on the set of lines of the generalised hexagon.
After some introduction (Section

As a crucial tool, we use items (i) and (ii) from the main result of

Background

2.1
Generalised hexagons

In this section we highlight some of the basic properties and notation concerning generalised hexagons which will be used later. More details can be found in
[
11
]
.

Definition 3
.

A weak generalised hexagon is a point-line geometry
(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{L},\mathtt{I})
satisfying:

1.

There exists no
k
-gon (as a subgeometry) for
2\leq k<6
.

2.

Every two elements of
\mathcal{P}\cup\mathcal{L}
are contained in a hexagon.

A thick generalised hexagon is a weak generalised hexagon with the additional property:

1.

There exists a heptagon (as a subgeometry).

Remark 3
.

Property (GH3) is equivalent to the following, see for instance
[
11
, Lemma 1.3.2]
:

1.

All points and lines are incident with at least 3 lines or points.

The
incidence graph
of a point-line geometry is the bipartite graph with as vertices the points and the lines and an edge between vertices
x
and
y
if and only if
x
and
y
are incident.
A weak generalised hexagon can also be defined using its incidence graph:

Corollary 1
.

(See e.g.
[
11
, Theorem 1.5.10]
)
Let
\Gamma
be a point-line geometry with at least two points and the properties that:

1.

every point is incident with at least two lines;

2.

every line is incident with at least two points.

Then,
\Gamma
is a weak generalised hexagon if and only if its incidence graph has diameter 6 and girth
12
.

The
distance
,
\delta(x,y)
, between two elements
x
and
y
of a weak generalised hexagon
\Gamma
is the distance inherited from its incidence graph. Two points, resp. lines, are
opposite
if they are at the furthest possible distance, i.e. 6.

The property that there are no quadrangles implies that for any two points
x
and
y
at distance 4 from each other, there is a unique point, which we will denote by
x\bowtie y
, collinear with both.

The following useful observation follows directly from the definition of a weak generalised polygon.

Corollary 2
.

(See e.g.
[
11
, Theorem 1.3.5]
)
Let
x
be a point and
L
be a line of a weak generalised hexagon
\Gamma
. Then, there is a unique point on
L
closest (i.e., at smallest distance) to
x
.

The weak generalised hexagons that we consider have an order
(s,t)
such that every line is incident with
s+1
points and every point is incident with
t+1
lines for some integers
s,t\geq 1
.

Remark 4
.

It is well-known (see for example
[
11
, Corollary 1.5.3]
) that every thick finite generalised hexagon has an order.
Some restrictions on possible orders are known (
[
1
]
and
[
2
]
).

Corollary 3
.

(See e.g.
[
11
, Lemma 1.5.4]
)
Let
\Gamma
be a finite weak generalised hexagon of order
(s,t)
. Then,

|\mathcal{P}|=(1+s)(1+st+s^{2}t^{2})
and
|\mathcal{L}|=(1+t)(1+st+s^{2}t^{2})
.

As mentioned before, up to duality, only two classes of finite thick generalised hexagons are known: the
split Cayley hexagons
of order
(q,q)
and the
twisted triality hexagons
of order
(q^{3},q)
. We focus on this second class although the split Cayley hexagons will still play an important role.

Definition 4
.

A subgeometry
(\mathcal{P}^{\prime},\mathcal{L}^{\prime},\mathtt{I}^{\prime})
of a weak generalised hexagon forming a weak generalised hexagon is called a
subhexagon
. In general, a point of a subgeometry is called
ideal
if all lines through that point are contained in the subgeometry. Dually, we call a line of a subgeometry
full
if all points on that line are contained in the subgeometry. If all points are ideal we call the subgeometry itself ideal and likewise we call a subgeometry full if all of its lines are full.

Remark 5
.

Since we will only be working with weak generalised hexagons which have an order, we can restate the definitions of ideal and full as follows:
Let
\Gamma
be a weak generalised hexagon of order
(s,t)
and let
\Gamma^{\prime}
be a subhexagon of order
(s^{\prime},t^{\prime})
of
\Gamma
. Then
\Gamma^{\prime}
is ideal precisely if
t=t^{\prime}
and
\Gamma^{\prime}
is full precisely when
s=s^{\prime}
.

Remark 6
.

It is well known that
[
11
, Section 1.9.8]
the split Cayley hexagon
\mathsf{H}(q)
contains ideal subhexagons of order
(1,q)
. We even have the following property:
any two opposite points of
\mathsf{H}(q)
are contained in a
(1,q)
subhexagon.
Likewise, the following is true in the twisted triality hexagon
\mathsf{T}(q^{3},q)
:
any two opposite points are contained in an ideal split Cayley subhexagon.

Corollary 4
.

Let
\Gamma
be a generalised hexagon and let
\Gamma^{\prime}
be a subhexagon of
\Gamma
.
Let
x
and
y
be two non opposite elements of
\Gamma
. Then, the shortest path between
x
and
y
is contained in
\Gamma^{\prime}
.

Proof.

Assume that the shortest path between
x
and
y
is different in
\Gamma
and
\Gamma^{\prime}
. Combining these paths implies the existence of a
k
-gon with
2\leq k<6
in
\Gamma
which is not possible.
∎

Definition 5
.

Let
\Gamma=(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{L},\mathtt{I})
be a generalised hexagon and let

be either a point or a line in
\Gamma
. We define:

\displaystyle\Gamma_{i}(x)

\displaystyle:=\{y\in\mathcal{P}\cup\mathcal{L}:\delta(x,y)=i\},

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\leq i}(x)

\displaystyle:=\{y\in\mathcal{P}\cup\mathcal{L}:\delta(x,y)\leq i\}

where
\delta(x,y)
is the distance between

and

inherited from the incidence graph.

Remark 7
.

For convenience we will use the sets
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{L}
as maps from sets and geometric structures to the subsets just containing points and lines, respectively, of the hexagon.
With this in mind,
\mathcal{P}(\Gamma_{\leq i}(x))
is the set of point of the hexagon at distance at most
i
from
x
.
To simplify even further, we introduce the following abbreviations:

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{\leq i}(x):=\mathcal{P}(\Gamma_{\leq i}(x)),

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\leq i}(x):=\mathcal{L}(\Gamma_{\leq i}(x)),

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{i}(x):=\mathcal{P}(\Gamma_{i}(x)),

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{i}(x):=\mathcal{L}(\Gamma_{i}(x)).

The following two observations will be helpful:

Corollary 5
.

Let
U
and
V
be two subsets of points of
\mathsf{PG}(7,q^{3})
. Then,

\mathcal{P}(U\cap V)=\mathcal{P}(U)\cap\mathcal{P}(V).

Corollary 6
.

Let
U
and
V
be subspaces of
\mathsf{PG}(7,q^{3})
. If
\mathcal{P}(U)
contains points spanning
V
, then
V
is a subspace of
U
.

Let
\Gamma
be a weak generalised hexagon of order
(s,t)
.
Let
L
and
M
be two opposite lines of
\Gamma
and let
x_{L}
be an arbitrary point on
L
. By Lemma
2
, there exists a unique point
x_{M}
on
M
at distance
4
from
x_{L}
. The path we find from
x_{L}
to
x_{M}
goes through the point
x:=x_{L}\bowtie x_{M}
. Since
x_{L}
was arbitrary and there are no
k
-gons for
k\in\{2,3,4,5\}
, we find
s+1
such points
in between

L
and
M
.
We call this set,
\mathcal{P}_{3}(L)\cap\mathcal{P}_{3}(M)
, the
distance-3-trace
,
T(L,M)
, of
L
and
M
.
Dually we define the
distance-3-trace
,
T(x,y)
, of two opposite points

and

to be the set
\mathcal{L}_{3}(x)\cap\mathcal{L}_{3}(y)
.

Definition 6
.

We call a line
L

distance-
3
-regular
if for any two lines
M
and
N
opposite
L
, the distance-3-traces
T(L,M)
and
T(L,N)
are either the same or have at most one point in common.
Dually, a point

is
distance-
3
-regular
if for any two points

and
z
opposite
x
, the distance-3-traces
T(x,y)
and
T(x,z)
are either the same or have at most one point in common.
We say that a weak generalised hexagon
\Gamma
is
distance-
3
-regular
if all points and all lines of
\Gamma
are distance-
3
-regular.

It is easy to see from our construction that the following is true:

Corollary 7
.

Let
L
and
M
be two opposite lines of a weak generalised hexagon
\Gamma
. Then, all points of
T(L,M)
are mutually opposite.
Dually, for any two opposite points

and

of a weak generalised hexagon
\Gamma
, all lines of

are mutually opposite.

Remark 8
.

It is well known that the twisted triality hexagon
\mathsf{T}(q^{3},q)
is distance-
3
-regular (
[
5
]
,
[
11
]
).
This justifies the following definition:

Definition 7
.

Let
L
and
M
be two opposite lines of a twisted triality hexagon
\mathsf{T}(q^{3},q)
.

We define
R(L,M)
to be the set of
q+1

\bigcap_{x\in T(L,M)}\mathcal{L}_{3}(x)
and call this the

defined by

.

Remark 9
.

For the natural embedding of
\mathsf{T}(q^{3},q)
in
\mathsf{PG}(7,q^{3})
, we will later see that

is contained in
\langle L,M\rangle
and forms a subset of one generator set of a hyperbolic quadric
\mathsf{Q}^{+}(3,q^{3})
, but a priori this does not need to be true.

Lemma 2
.

Let
L
and
M
be two opposite lines of a twisted triality hexagon
\mathsf{T}(q^{3},q)
. Let
N
be a line which is opposite all lines of
R(L,M)
. Then, every point of
T(L,M)
is at distance 5 from
N
.

Proof.

Let
x
be a point of
T(L,M)
. All
q+1
lines of
R(L,M)
intersect a line through
x
and since they are all mutually opposite (Corollary

) no two of them can intersect the same line.
Hence, for each of the
q+1
lines through
x
, there is a unique line of
R(L,M)
intersecting it. This implies that

, which must be opposite all lines of
R(L,M)
, can not intersect any of the lines through

.
∎

Lemma 3
.

(See also
[
4
]
for the dual statement)
Let
\mathcal{S}
be a set of mutually opposite lines in a weak generalised hexagon
\Gamma=(\mathcal{L},\mathcal{P},\mathtt{I})
of order
(s,t)
. Then,
|\mathcal{S}|\leq st^{2}+1
.

Proof.

Let
L
be a line of
\mathcal{S}
and let
x
be a point on
L
.
We define the following map:

\pi:\mathcal{S}\setminus\{L\}\to\mathcal{L}_{\leq 3}(x)\setminus\mathcal{L}_{\leq 2}(L):M\to\pi(M)

where
\pi(M)
is the unique line intersecting

and at distance

from

(Corollary

We now show that
\pi
is injective.
Assume that
\pi(M_{1})=\pi(M_{2})=:N
, i.e.

intersects
M_{1}
and

. Since all lines of
\mathcal{S}
are mutually opposite, this is only possible if

.
The statement now follows from

|\mathcal{L}_{\leq 3}(x)\setminus\mathcal{L}_{\leq 2}(L)|=(t+1)(st+1)-(s+1)t-1=st^{2}.

∎

Lemma 4
.

Let
\mathcal{S}
be a set of
q^{3}+1
mutually opposite lines of a finite split Cayley hexagon
\mathsf{H}(q)
. Then, every line of
\mathsf{H}(q)=(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{L})
intersects a line in
\mathcal{S}
.

Proof.

Since all lines of
\mathcal{S}
are mutually opposite, there are no lines of
\mathsf{H}(q)
intersecting two lines of
\mathcal{S}
.
We can therefore easily count all lines intersecting a line of
\mathcal{S}
:

|\mathcal{S}|(q+1)q+|\mathcal{S}|=(q^{3}+1)(q+1)q+q^{3}+1=q^{5}+q^{4}+q^{3}+q^{2}+q+1=|\mathcal{L}|.

∎

Lemma 5
.

Let
\Gamma=(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{L},\mathtt{I})
be a finite generalised hexagon
\Gamma
of order
(s,t)
. Let
\Gamma^{\prime}=(\mathcal{P}^{\prime},\mathcal{L}^{\prime},\mathtt{I}^{\prime})
be an (ideal) subhexagon of order
(k,t)
.
If
k^{2}t=s
, then every line of
\Gamma
intersects a line in
\Gamma^{\prime}
.

Proof.

For every line in
\Gamma^{\prime}
, there are
(s+1)t
lines of
\Gamma
intersecting it. The subhexagon
\Gamma^{\prime}
contains
(k+1)t
of these lines. This implies that for every line
L
of
\Gamma^{\prime}
there are
(s-k)t
lines of
\Gamma
not in
\Gamma^{\prime}
which intersect
L
. Each line of
\Gamma
intersecting two lines of
\Gamma^{\prime}
is contained in
\Gamma^{\prime}
(Corollary
4
).
We can count all lines of
\Gamma
intersecting a line of
\Gamma^{\prime}
:

\displaystyle|\mathcal{L}^{\prime}|\cdot(s-k)t+|\mathcal{L}^{\prime}|

\displaystyle=|\mathcal{L}^{\prime}|\cdot((s-k)t+1)

\displaystyle=(t+1)(k^{2}t^{2}+kt+1)(st-kt+1)

\displaystyle=(t+1)(k^{2}st^{3}+kst^{2}+st-k^{3}t^{3}-k^{2}t^{2}-kt+k^{2}t^{2}+kt+1)

\displaystyle=(t+1)(s^{2}t^{2}+st+1)

\displaystyle=|\mathcal{L}|.

∎

.

Let
\Gamma^{\prime}
be a subhexagon of order
(1,q)
of a split Cayley hexagon
\mathsf{H}(q)
. Then, every line of
\mathsf{H}(q)
intersects a line of
\Gamma^{\prime}
.

Corollary 9
.

Let
\mathsf{H}(q)
be a split Cayley subhexagon of a twisted triality hexagon
\mathsf{T}(q^{3},q)
. Then, every line of
\mathsf{T}(q^{3},q)

We now consider the standard embedding of a twisted triality hexagon

We start from a hyperbolic quadric
\mathsf{Q}^{+}(7,\mathbb{K})=:\mathsf{Q}
in
\mathsf{PG}(7,\mathbb{K})
.

The points on
\mathsf{Q}
have homogeneous coordinates satisfying the following equation:

x_{0}x_{4}+x_{1}x_{5}+x_{2}x_{6}+x_{3}x_{7}=0.

Since
\mathsf{Q}
is determined by a quadratic equation, we have the following property which will be useful later:

Lemma 6

Let

be a line of
\mathsf{PG}(7,\mathbb{K})
then

contains

or

points of

The set of

-spaces in

is partitioned into two classes such that any two

-spaces from the same subclass meet in an odd dimensional subspace and any two
3
-spaces from different classes meet in an even dimensional subspace. Without loss of generality we call elements of one subclass
3
-spaces and elements of the second subclass
3^{\prime}
-spaces.
We say that a
3
-space and a
3^{\prime}
-space are incident if they meet in a plane.
Interestingly, both the
3
-spaces and the
3^{\prime}
-space behave the same as the class of points and hence can be coordinatised in the same way.

We use the following trilinear form
\mathcal{T}:V\times V\times V\to\mathbb{K}
, where

is an eight-dimensional vector space over
\mathbb{K}
, to establish these coordinates:

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}(x,y,z)=\,

\displaystyle\begin{vmatrix}x_{0}&x_{1}&x_{2}\\
y_{0}&y_{1}&y_{2}\\
z_{0}&z_{1}&z_{2}\end{vmatrix}+\begin{vmatrix}x_{4}&x_{5}&x_{6}\\
y_{4}&y_{5}&y_{6}\\

\displaystyle+x_{3}(z_{0}y_{4}+z_{1}y_{5}+z_{2}y_{6})+x_{7}(y_{0}z_{4}+y_{1}z_{5}+y_{2}z_{6})

\displaystyle+y_{3}(x_{0}z_{4}+x_{1}z_{5}+x_{2}z_{6})+y_{7}(z_{0}x_{4}+z_{1}x_{5}+z_{2}x_{6})

\displaystyle+z_{3}(y_{0}x_{4}+y_{1}x_{5}+y_{2}x_{6})+z_{7}(x_{0}y_{4}+x_{1}y_{5}+x_{2}y_{6})

\displaystyle-x_{3}y_{3}z_{3}-x_{7}y_{7}z_{7}.

A point
x
now belongs to the
3
-space

if and only if
\mathcal{T}(x,y,z^{\prime})
vanishes as a function of

. Similarly, a point

belongs to the

-space

if and only if

vanishes as a function of

. A

-space

and a

-space

are incident if and only if

is vanishes as a function of

.

The

-spaces with their coordinates, will further be referred to as 1-points,

The original points will be referred to as

Proof of Theorem
2
.

In Section
3
, we have shown that the lines
\mathcal{L}^{\mathsf{T}}
of a naturally embedded twisted triality hexagon
\mathsf{T}(q^{3},q)
in
\mathsf{PG}(7,q^{3})
satisfy the properties (Pt), (Pl), (Sd), (4d), (4d’), (5d), (6d) and (To).
∎

Proof of Theorem
3
.

In Section
4
, we have shown that any set of lines satisfying (Pt), (Pl) and (Sd) contain no triangles and no quadrangles.
In Lemma
36
, we have shown that if this set also satisfies property (4d’) we can have no pentagons.
Using property (Pt) and the fact that the lines are full, we now count the number of lines in
\mathcal{L}
.
Starting from a point
x
, there are
q+1
lines of
\mathcal{L}
through
x
. Since there are no triangles, we find through each of the points on these lines,
q
more lines of
\mathcal{L}
. This gives us
(q+1)q^{4}
lines of
\mathcal{L}
at distance
3
from
x
. Since there can be no quadrangles, we can also easily count the number of points on lines of
\mathcal{L}
at distance
4
from
x
to be
(q+1)q^{7}
. Finally since there are no pentagons, the number of lines of
\mathcal{L}
at distance
5
from
x
must be
(q+1)q^{8}
.
This gives us a lower bound of

(q+1)+(q+1)q^{4}+(q+1)q^{8}=q^{9}+q^{8}+q^{5}+q^{4}+q+1

for the number of lines in
\mathcal{L}
.
Property (To) implies we precisely meet this bound. It follows that all lines of
\mathcal{L}
are at distance at most
5
from
x
. Since
x
was arbitrary, it follows that the maximal distance between any line of
\mathcal{L}
and any point on a line of
\mathcal{L}
is at most
5
.

This implies that the maximum length of a path between two vertices in the incidence graph is
6
.
Furthermore, by considering a point
x
and a line
L
at distance 5, we find a path
