Coin Turning Games on Partially Ordered Sets
M. Ishikawa, T. Ohmoto, H. Tagawa, Y. Takayama

Abstract
. A finite impartial game is a two-player game in which the players take turns making moves and the game ends after finitely many moves. In this paper, we study a class of finite impartial games introduced by H. Lenstra, which we call coin turning games. We focus on two typical classes of coin turning games, namely the order ideal games and the rulers, distinguished by their choices of turning sets. For several posets arising from enumerative combinatorics, we determine the Sprague-Grundy functions. In particular, we determine the Sprague-Grundy function of the order ideal game on the ASM poset 𝑨 n \boldsymbol{A}_{n} , introduced by J. Striker in connection with the alternating sign matrices.

1.
Introduction

A coin turning game is a finite impartial game, that is, a type of combinatorial game.
It is played by two players who alternately make moves, and the game terminates after finitely many moves.
H. Lenstra
[
7

studied coin turning games on an arbitrary poset
X

\mathscr{T}

X

).
In this paper, we investigate poset games on several posets arising in combinatorial studies (see, e.g.,
[
14
]
),
including the poset

of all subspaces of the
n

\mathbb{F}_{q}
,
the poset
\Pi_{n}
of set partitions.
Of particular importance is the poset

(1.1)

\boldsymbol{A}_{n}=\left\{(x,y,z)\in\mathbb{Z}^{3}\,\middle|\,x,y,z\geq 0\textrm{ and }x+y+z\leq n-2\right\},

which arises as the set of join-irreducible elements of the distributive lattice of alternating sign matrices of size

(see
[
3
,
6
,
8
,
9
,
10
,

This poset was introduced by J. Striker
[
15

The partial order on

(1.2)

x_{1}\geq x_{2},\,y_{1}\geq y_{2},\,z_{1}\leq z_{2}\text{ and }x_{1}+y_{1}+z_{1}\geq x_{2}+y_{2}+z_{2}.

A poset game

Common choices of

Our goal is to obtain explicit formulas for the Sprague-Grundy functions

One of the main objectives of this paper is to determine the Sprague-Grundy function of the order ideal game on

Let

For each

(1.3)

2.1.
Basic notions of finite impartial games

Any game considered in this paper is played by two players,
and the players alternatively make a move.
The winner is the player who makes the last move.
An
impartial game
is a game satisfying the following conditions (see
[
1
,
12
]
):

(a)

The possible moves from each position are completely determined by that position
(that is, there are no
chance moves
such as rolling dice or shuffling cards).

(b)

Both players have complete information about the game state at every position.

(c)

Both players have the same set of available moves from each position.

(d)

From any given position, only finitely many positions are reachable, and no position can appear twice during a play.

Definition 2.1

(
[
2
,
4
,
7
,
11
,
12
]
)
.

A game is a pair
(\mathcal{P},N)
,
where
\mathcal{P}
is the set of all possible positions
and
N:\mathcal{P}\to 2^{\mathcal{P}}
is a map that assigns to each position
P\in\mathcal{P}

the set of all possible positions
P^{\prime}
that can be reached from
P
in a single move.
If
P^{\prime}\in N(P)
,
we say
P^{\prime}
is an
option
of the current position
P
,
and we write
P\rightarrow P^{\prime}
.
If
N(P)=\emptyset
,
then
P
is called an
ending position
.
We denote by
\epsilon
the set of all ending positions.
The game determined by the pair
(\mathcal{P},N)
is denoted by
\mathscr{A}(\mathcal{P},N)
.

Starting from a position
{P}_{0}\in\mathcal{P}
,
the game is played by two players who alternatively make moves,

{P}_{0}\rightarrow{P}_{1}\rightarrow{P}_{2}\rightarrow\cdots\rightarrow{P}_{m},

where at each position
P_{i}
, the player in turn chooses an option
P_{i+1}\in N(P_{i})
.
If
{P}_{m}\in\epsilon
,
we call such a sequence a
play
(or a
game sequence
)
with the
starting position

P_{0}

and the
ending position

P_{m}
,
and we say that its
length
is
m
.
Throughout this paper, we assume that every play has finite length.
The player who makes the last move
P_{m-1}\to P_{m}
is called the
winner
,
and the opposing player is called the
looser
.
For a position
P\in\mathcal{P}
, let
\ell\left({P}\right)
denote the maximum length among all plays starting from
P
;
this number is called the
length
of the position
P
.
It follows immediately that, if
P^{\prime}
is an option of
P
, then
\ell\left({P^{\prime}}\right)<\ell\left({P}\right)
.

For any proper subset
T\subsetneq\mathbb{N}
,
where
\mathbb{N}
denotes the set of non-negative integers, we define

\operatorname{mex}\left(T\right)=\min\left(\mathbb{N}\setminus T\right)
,
and call
\operatorname{mex}\left(T\right)
the
minimal-excluded number
of
T
.
In particular,
\operatorname{mex}(\emptyset)=0
.
The Sprague-Grundy function is defined inductively using the
\operatorname{mex}
operator; see
[
5
,
13
]
for details.

Definition 2.2

(Sprague-Grundy function
[
5
,
13
]
)
.

Let
\mathscr{A}=\mathscr{A}(\mathcal{P},N)
be a finite impartial game.
The
Sprague-Grundy value
(or
Grundy value
for short)
{g}_{\mathscr{A}}(P)\in\mathbb{N}

of a position
P\in\mathcal{P}
is defined inductively by the following conditions:

(i)

{g}_{\mathscr{A}}(P)=0

if
P
is an ending position (equivalently,
\ell(P)=0
);

(ii)

{g}_{\mathscr{A}}(P)=\operatorname{mex}\left\{{g}_{\mathscr{A}}(P^{\prime})\,\middle|\,P^{\prime}\in N(P)\right\}

if
\ell(P)>0
.

This inductive definition determines a function
g_{\mathscr{A}}:\mathcal{P}\to\mathbb{N}
which we call the
Sprague-Grundy function
(or
Grundy function
for short) of
\mathscr{A}
.
For brevity, we will often write
g(P)
in place of
g_{\mathscr{A}}(P)
.

2.2.
Combined game

Given two finite impartial games
\mathscr{A}_{1}
and
\mathscr{A}_{2}
,
we give the definition of the combined game
\mathscr{A}_{1}+\mathscr{A}_{2}

in which, on each turn, a player can either make a move in the first game, while leaving the second game untouched,
or make a move in the second game, while leaving the first game untouched.
The precise definition is given below.

Definition 2.3
.

Let
\mathscr{A}_{1}=\mathscr{A}(\mathcal{P}_{1},N_{1})
and
\mathscr{A}_{2}=\mathscr{A}(\mathcal{P}_{2},N_{2})
be finite impartial games.
Define the set
\mathcal{P}
of positions to be the Cartesian product
\mathcal{P}=\mathcal{P}_{1}\times\mathcal{P}_{2}
.
For a position
P=(P_{1},P_{2})\in\mathcal{P}
, we define its set of options by

(2.1)

N(P)=\{(P_{1}^{\prime},P_{2}),(P_{1},P_{2}^{\prime})\ |\ P_{1}^{\prime}\in N_{1}(P_{1}),P_{2}^{\prime}\in N_{2}(P_{2})\}.

We call the game
\mathscr{A}=(\mathcal{P},N)
the
combined game
of
\mathscr{A}_{1}
and
\mathscr{A}_{2}
,
and denote it by
\mathscr{A}=\mathscr{A}_{1}+\mathscr{A}_{2}
.

Definition 2.4

(
[
4
,
7
,
11
]
)
.

If
a
and
b
are non-negative integers,
the
NIM-sum

a\overset{*}{+}b
of
a
and
b
is defined inductively by

(2.2)

a\overset{*}{+}b:=\operatorname{mex}\bigl(\{a^{\prime}\overset{*}{+}b\mid 0\leq a^{\prime}<a\}\cup\{a\overset{*}{+}b^{\prime}\mid 0\leq b^{\prime}<b\}\bigr).

This inductive definition determines a binary operation

\overset{*}{+}\colon\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{N}
,
(a,b)\mapsto a\overset{*}{+}b
,
which is also referred to as
NIM-addition
.
Let
A
be a finite subset of
\mathbb{N}
.
We denote the NIM-sum over the set
A

by
{{\sum}^{*}}_{a\in A}a
.
In particular, the NIM-sum over the empty set is
0
, which follows immediately from the definition (
2.2
).

We summarize the useful properties of NIM-addition in the Appendix
A
.

If
\epsilon_{1}
and
\epsilon_{2}
denote the set of the ending positions of
\mathscr{A}_{1}
and
\mathscr{A}_{2}
, respectively,
then it is immediate that
\epsilon=\epsilon_{1}\times\epsilon_{2}
is the set of ending positions of the combined game
\mathscr{A}=\mathscr{A}_{1}+\mathscr{A}_{2}
.
Furthermore, for any position
P=(P_{1},P_{2})\in\mathcal{P}
,
it follows directly from the definition that
\ell\left({P}\right)=\ell\left({P_{1},P_{2}}\right)=\ell\left({P_{1}}\right)+\ell\left({P_{2}}\right)
.

Theorem 2.1

(
[
7
]
)
.

Let
\mathscr{A}_{1}=\mathscr{A}(\mathcal{P}_{1},N_{1})
and
\mathscr{A}_{2}=\mathscr{A}(\mathcal{P}_{2},N_{2})
be finite impartial games
with Grundy functions
g_{\mathscr{A}_{1}}
and
g_{\mathscr{A}_{2}}
, respectively.
For the combined game
\mathscr{A}=\mathscr{A}_{1}+\mathscr{A}_{2}=\mathscr{A}(\mathcal{P},N)
,
the Grundy function is given by

(2.3)

g_{\mathscr{A}_{1}+\mathscr{A}_{2}}(P_{1},P_{2})=g_{\mathscr{A}_{1}}(P_{1})\overset{*}{+}g_{\mathscr{A}_{2}}(P_{2})

for all
(P_{1},P_{2})\in\mathcal{P}=\mathcal{P}_{1}\times\mathcal{P}_{2}
.

We proceed by induction on
\ell\left({P_{1},P_{2}}\right)
.
If
\ell\left({P_{1},P_{2}}\right)=0
, then
(P_{1},P_{2})\in\epsilon
.
Hence we have
P_{1}\in\epsilon_{1}
and
P_{2}\in\epsilon_{2}
which implies

g_{\mathscr{A}}(P_{1},P_{2})=0=0\overset{*}{+}0=g_{\mathscr{A}_{1}}(P_{1})\overset{*}{+}g_{\mathscr{A}_{2}}(P_{2}).

Now let
\ell\left({P_{1},P_{2}}\right)>0
and assume that

g_{\mathscr{A}}(\overline{P_{1}},\overline{P_{2}})=g_{\mathscr{A}_{1}}(\overline{P_{1}})\overset{*}{+}g_{\mathscr{A}_{2}}(\overline{P_{2}})

holds for any pair
(\overline{P_{1}},\overline{P_{2}})
with
\ell\left({\overline{P_{1}},\overline{P_{2}}}\right)<\ell\left({P_{1},P_{2}}\right)
.
By the definition (
2.1
) of combined game and the Grundy value, we have

\displaystyle g_{\mathscr{A}}(P_{1},P_{2})

\displaystyle=\operatorname{mex}\{g_{\mathscr{A}}(P_{1}^{\prime},P_{2}),g_{\mathscr{A}}(P_{1},P_{2}^{\prime})\ |\ P_{1}^{\prime}\in N(P_{1}),P_{2}^{\prime}\in N(P_{2})\}.

Since
\ell\left({P_{1}^{\prime},P_{2}}\right)<\ell\left({P_{1},P_{2}}\right)
and
\ell\left({P_{1},P_{2}^{\prime}}\right)<\ell\left({P_{1},P_{2}}\right)
,
the induction hypothesis yields

\displaystyle g_{\mathscr{A}}(P_{1},P_{2})=\operatorname{mex}\{g_{\mathscr{A}_{1}}(P^{\prime}_{1})\overset{*}{+}g_{\mathscr{A}_{2}}(P_{2}),

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad g_{\mathscr{A}_{1}}(P_{1})\overset{*}{+}g_{\mathscr{A}_{2}}(P_{2}^{\prime})\ |\ P_{1}^{\prime}\in N(P_{1}),P_{2}^{\prime}\in N(P_{2})\}.

Since

\operatorname{mex}\{g_{\mathscr{A}_{1}}(P^{\prime}_{1})\ |\ P_{1}^{\prime}\in N(P_{1})\}=g_{\mathscr{A}_{1}}(P_{1})
and

\operatorname{mex}\{g_{\mathscr{A}_{2}}(P^{\prime}_{2})\ |\ P_{2}^{\prime}\in N(P_{2})\}=g_{\mathscr{A}_{2}}(P_{2})

by (
2.2
),
we obtain

g_{\mathscr{A}}(P_{1},P_{2})=g_{\mathscr{A}_{1}}(P_{1})\overset{*}{+}g_{\mathscr{A}_{2}}(P_{2})

by Lemma
A.2
.
∎

3.1.
Preliminaries on Posets

A
partially ordered set
(or
poset
, for short)
P

is a set equipped with a binary relation, denoted by
\leq
,
satisfying the following axioms for all
x,y,z\in P
:
(P1)

x\leq x
,
(P2)

x=y
if
x\leq y
and
y\leq x
,
(P3)

x\leq z
if
x\leq y
and
y\leq z
.
The relation
\leq
is called the
partial order
on
P
.
We write
x<y

if
x\leq y
and
x\neq y
.
We may also denote the poset by
(P,{\leq}_{P})
(or simply
(P,\leq)
)
to emphasize the underlying order relation.
The axioms (P1), (P2) and (P3) are called

reflectivity
,
antisymmetry
and
transitivity
, respectively.
Two elements
x,y\in P
are said to be
comparable

if either
x\leq y
or
y\leq x
; otherwise they are
incomparable
.

A
chain
(or
totally ordered set
)
is a poset in which any two elements are comparable.
An
antichain

is a subset
A
of a poset
P
such that
any two distinct elements of
A
are incomparable.
If
x<y
and no element
u\in P
satisfies
x<u<y
,
we say that
y

covers

x

(or
x
is
covered
by
y
),
and write
y\gtrdot x
(or
x\lessdot y
).

We say that
P
has a minimum element
\hat{0}
if there exists an element
\hat{0}\in P
such that
t\geq\hat{0}
for all
t\in P
.
Similarly,
P
has a maximum element
\hat{1}
if there exists
\hat{1}\in P
such that
t\leq\hat{1}
for all
t\in P
.

If
P
and
Q
are posets,
then the
direct (or cartesian) product
of
P
and
Q
is the poset
P\times Q

on the set
\left\{(s,t)\,\middle|\,s\in P\text{ and }t\in Q\right\}

with order relation defined by
(s,t)\leq(s^{\prime},t^{\prime})
in
P\times Q
if and only if
s\leq s^{\prime}
in
P
and
t\leq t^{\prime}
in
Q
.

Let
(P,{\leq}_{P})
and
(Q,{\leq}_{Q})
be posets.
We say that a map
\varphi\colon P\rightarrow Q
is
order-preserving

if
\varphi(s)\,{\leq}_{Q}\,\varphi(t)

for any
s,t\in P
such that
s\,{\leq}_{P}\,t
.
Further more, if
\varphi
is bijective and the inverse map is order-preserving,
then we say that
\varphi
is
order isomorphism
,

P
and
Q
are
order isomorphic
,
denoted
P\cong Q
.
If
C
is a chain with
|C|=|P|
,
then an order preserving bijection
\sigma:P\to C
is called a
linear extension
of
P
.

By an
induced subposet
of a poset
P
,
we mean a subset
Q
of
P

equipped with the partial order inherited from
P
; that is,
for
s,t\in Q
, we have
s\leq t
in
Q
if and only if
s\leq t
in
P
.
Then we say that the subset
Q
of
P
has the
induced order
.
Unless stated otherwise, the term
subposet
will always mean an induced subposet.

A
chain
of a poset
P
is a subposet
C\subseteq P
that is totally ordered.
A chain
C
is called
maximal

if it is not properly contained in any larger chain of
P
.
The
length
of a chain
C
is defined by
\ell(C)=|C|-1
.
A poset
P
is called
graded of rank
n

if every maximal chain of
P
has the same length
n
.
In this case there exists a
rank function

\rho\colon P\rightarrow\left\{0,1,...,n\right\}

such that
\rho(x)=0
for every minimal element
x\in P
,
and
\rho(y)=\rho(x)+1

whenever
y
covers
x
.
If
\rho(x)=i
,
then we say that
x
has rank
i
.

An
order ideal
of a poset
P
is a subset
I\subseteq P

such that if
t\in I
and
s\leq t
, then
s\in I
.
The collection of all order ideals of
P
, partially ordered by inclusion, forms a poset denoted by
J(P)
.
If
P
is finite, there is a bijection between the set of order ideals of
P

and the set of antichains of
P
.
Given an antichain
A\subseteq P
,
the corresponding order ideal is

\left\{x\in P\,\middle|\,x\leq a\textrm{ for some }a\in A\right\}
,
which is called the order ideal
generated
by
A
.
In particular, for
x\in P

we write
\Lambda_{x}=\left\{t\in P\,\middle|\,t\leq x\right\}
,
and call
\Lambda_{x}
the
principal order ideal
generated by
x
.

We also introduce a special type of subposet called an interval.
For elements
x,y\in P
with
x\leq y
,
the
closed interval

[x,y]:=\left\{t\in P\,\middle|\,x\leq t\leq y\right\}

is the induced subposet of
P

consisting of all elements lying between
x
and
y
.

Definition 3.1

(
[
14
]
)
.

(1)

For
n\in\mathbb{P}
,
where
\mathbb{P}
denotes the set of positive integers,
let

.
With the usual order,

It is called the

(2)

For

let

denote the set of all positive divisors of

,

We call

If

4.
Sprague-Grundy Theory of Coin Turning Games

In this section, we describe a finite impartial game induced from the poset structure of a given poset; see
[
7
,
11
]
.
The key theorem in this section is Theorem

,
which provides a method to compute the Grundy functions for games of this type,
and frequently used in the rest of the paper.

We also introduce the symmetric difference of two sets.
Let
X
be a set,
and let
A,B\subseteq X
.
We define the set

A\ominus B:=\left(A\cup B\right)\setminus\left(A\cap B\right)
,
and call it the

of

and

.

4.1.
Coin Turning Games

Throughout this section, we fix a finite poset
X

and a family
\mathscr{T}
of subsets of
X

which satisfies

(
\sharp
)  each
T\in\mathscr{T}
has a (unique) maximum element.

For each
T\in\mathscr{T}
,
we denote by
m_{T}
the unique maximal element of
T
.
For
x\in X
,
we define
\mathscr{T}_{x}=\{T\in\mathscr{T}\mid m_{T}=x\}
,
and for a subset
P\subseteq X
,
we define
\mathscr{T}_{P}=\{T\in\mathscr{T}\mid m_{T}\in P\}
.

Definition 4.1

(Coin Turning Game
[
7
,
11
]
)
.

Let
X
be a finite poset,
and
\mathscr{T}
a family of subsets of
X

which satisfies the condition (
\sharp
).
We define a game

\mathscr{A}

by the following rule:

(a)

The set of positions is
\mathcal{P}={2}^{X}
,
the collection of all subsets of
X
.

(b)

Given a position
P\in\mathcal{P}
,
the player in turn can choose a subset
T\in\mathscr{T}_{P}

and make the move
P\to P\ominus T
,

(c)

A position
P
is an ending position if
\mathscr{T}_{P}=\emptyset
.
Equivalently, the set of ending positions is

\epsilon=\left\{P\in\mathcal{P}\,\middle|\,{m}_{T}\notin P\textrm{ for any }T\in\mathscr{T}\right\}
.

We call this game the
(X,\mathscr{T})
-game
(or simply
\mathscr{T}
-game
),
and we denote it by
\mathscr{A}=\mathscr{G}(X,\mathscr{T})
.

We refer to games of this type as
(finite) coin turning game
.
Let
D:=\left\{m_{T}\,\middle|\,T\in\mathscr{T}\right\}
and
E:=X\setminus D
.
Then a position
P\in\mathcal{P}
is an ending positions if and only if there is no element
P\cap D=\emptyset
,
that is, if and only if
P\subseteq E
.
Consequently,
\epsilon=2^{E}
.

We will see that no position can occur more than once during a play of the game.
In particular, from any given position only finitely many positions are reachable.
To establish this fact, we prove the following proposition.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
X
be a finite poset and let
\mathscr{T}
be a family of subsets of
X
satisfying condition
(\sharp)
.
Fix a linear extension
\tau:X\to C
, where
C=[n]
is the chain with
n=|X|
.
Let
\rho:C\to\mathbb{N}
be the rank function of
C
,
defined by
\rho(i)=i-1
for
i\in[n]
.
Define a map
F:\mathcal{P}=2^{X}\to\mathbb{N}
by

F(P)=\sum_{x\in P}2^{\rho(\tau(x))}.

Thus
F
assigns to each position
P\in\mathcal{P}
a non-negative integer.
Then,
for any position
P
and any option
P^{\prime}\in N(P)
, we have
F(P^{\prime})<F(P)
.

If
P^{\prime}\in N(P)
, then there exists
T\in\mathscr{T}
such that its unique maximal element
x=m_{T}\in P
satisfies
P^{\prime}=P\ominus T
.
In particular, we have
x\in P
, but
x\not\in P^{\prime}
.
Moreover, for any element
y\in P^{\prime}\setminus P
,
condition (
\sharp
) implies that
y<x
.
Since
\tau
is a linear extension, this yields
\rho(\tau(y))<\rho(\tau(x))
.
Consequently, in passing from
P
to
P^{\prime}
,
the term
2^{\rho(\tau(x))}
is removed from the sum defining
F(P)
,
while any newly added terms correspond to strictly smaller powers of
2
.
Hence we conclude that
F(P^{\prime})<F(P)
.
∎This proposition means that this function
F(P)
strictly decreases at each move and therefore never remains constant or increases.
Consequently, no position can appear more than once during a play, and every play terminates after finitely many moves.

The reason we call the
(X,\mathscr{T})
-game a coin-turning game is as follows.
Imagine a coin is placed on each element of
X
, and each coin shows either heads or tails.
For a position
P\in\mathcal{P}
, the coin at a vertex
x\in X
shows heads if
x\in P
, and shows tails otherwise.
On a player’s turn, the player chooses a set
T\in\mathscr{T}_{P}

and flips all coins on the vertices in
T
.
If a player cannot find any
T\in\mathscr{T}_{P}
, then that player loses.
From this perspective, we refer to the poset
X
as the
board
, and to each subset

T\in\mathscr{T}
as a
turning set
.

4.2.
The Fundamental Theorem of Coin Turning Games

We call the following as the fundamental theorem of coin turning game.
It plays a key role in our paper.

Theorem 4.1

(Fundamental Theorem of Coin Turning Games
[
7
]
)
.

Let
X
be a finite poset,
and let
\mathscr{T}
be a family of subsets of
X
satisfying condition (
\sharp
).
Define a map

g_{X,\mathscr{T}}:X\rightarrow\mathbb{N}

inductively by

(4.1a)

g_{X,\mathscr{T}}(x):=\operatorname{mex}\Bigg\{\underset{t\in T\setminus\{x\}}{\,{\sum}^{*}}g_{X,\mathscr{T}}(t)\,\Bigg|\,T\in\mathscr{T}_{x}\Bigg\}.

Recall that
{{\sum}^{*}}_{t\in\emptyset}t=0
.
For any subset
P\subseteq X
, we set

(4.1b)

g_{X,\mathscr{T}}\left(P\right)=\underset{x\in P}{\,\,{\sum}^{*}}\,g_{X,\mathscr{T}}(x).

Then the map
g_{X,\mathscr{T}}:2^{X}\to\mathbb{N}
agrees with the Grundy function
g_{\mathscr{A}}
of the game
\mathscr{A}=\mathscr{G}(X,\mathscr{T})
.
When no confusion can arise, we write
g_{\mathscr{T}}
or simply
g
in place of
g_{X,\mathscr{T}}
.

We proceed by induction on the length
\ell(P)
.

If
\ell(P)=0
then
P\subseteq E
, so
\mathscr{T}_{P}=\emptyset
.
Hence
g_{X,\mathscr{T}}(x)=\operatorname{mex}(\emptyset)=0
for all
x\in P
,
and therefore
g_{X,\mathscr{T}}(x)=\underset{x\in P}{{\sum}^{*}}g_{X,\mathscr{T}}(x)=0
,
which agrees with the Grundy value of an ending position.

Now assume
\ell(P)>0
.
By definition,

g_{X,\mathscr{T}}(x)=\operatorname{mex}\biggl(\biggl\{\underset{t\in T\setminus\{x\}}{{\sum}^{*}}g_{X,\mathscr{T}}(t)\,\bigg|\,T\in\mathscr{T}_{x}\biggr\}\biggr).

Using the standard property of the
\operatorname{mex}
operator with Nim addition
(cf. (
A.1
)),
we see that the right-hand side of (
4.1a
) equals

\displaystyle\underset{x\in P}{{\sum}^{*}}g_{X,\mathscr{T}}(x)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{mex}\biggl(\bigcup_{x\in P}\bigcup_{T\in\mathscr{T}_{x}}\biggl\{\underset{y\in P\setminus\{x\}}{{\sum}^{*}}g_{X,\mathscr{T}}(y)\overset{*}{+}\underset{t\in T\setminus\{x\}}{{\sum}^{*}}g_{X,\mathscr{T}}(t)\biggr\}\biggr)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{mex}\biggl(\bigcup_{T\in\mathscr{T}_{P}}\biggl\{\underset{y\in P\setminus\{m_{T}\}}{{\sum}^{*}}g_{X,\mathscr{T}}(y)\overset{*}{+}\underset{t\in T\setminus\{m_{T}\}}{{\sum}^{*}}g_{X,\mathscr{T}}(t)\biggr\}\biggr).

If
y\in P\cap T
then
g_{X,\mathscr{T}}(y)
is cancelled by the same term in the second sum.
Hence we obtain

\underset{x\in P}{{\sum}^{*}}g_{X,\mathscr{T}}(x)=\operatorname{mex}\biggl\{\underset{y\in P\ominus T}{{\sum}^{*}}g_{X,\mathscr{T}}(y)\mid T\in\mathscr{T}_{P}\biggr\}.

Since
\ell(P\ominus T)<\ell(P)
,
we have

\underset{y\in P\ominus T}{{\sum}^{*}}g_{X,\mathscr{T}}(y)=g_{X,\mathscr{T}}(P\ominus T)

by induction hypothesis.
This shows that
\underset{x\in P}{{\sum}^{*}}g_{X,\mathscr{T}}(x)
is the Grundy value of
P

from Definition
2.2

(ii)
and Definition
4.1

(b)
.
This completes the proof.
∎

4.3.
The Product Theorem for Coin Turning Games

Let
\mathscr{A}_{1}=\mathscr{G}(X_{1},\mathscr{T}_{1})
and
\mathscr{A}_{2}=\mathscr{G}(X_{2},\mathscr{T}_{2})
be coin turning Games, where

X_{1}
and
X_{2}
are finite posets and
\mathscr{T}_{1}
and
\mathscr{T}_{2}
are their families of turning sets, respectively.
Let
X=X_{1}\times X_{2}
be the direct product poset.
Define

\mathscr{T}=\{T_{1}\times T_{2}\,|\,T_{1}\in\mathscr{T}_{1},\,T_{2}\in\mathscr{T}_{2}\}.

Then, for each
T_{1}\times T_{2}\in\mathscr{T}
, the element
(m_{T_{1}},m_{T_{2}})
is the unique maximum element of
T_{1}\times T_{2}
.
Hence the family
\mathscr{T}
satisfies condition (
\sharp
).

Theorem 4.2

(Product Theorem for Coin Turning Games
[
7
]
)
.

Then the Grundy function
g_{X,\mathscr{T}}
is given by

(4.2a)

g_{X,\mathscr{T}}(x_{1},x_{2})=g_{X_{1},\mathscr{T}_{1}}(x_{1})\overset{*}{\times}g_{X_{2},\mathscr{T}_{2}}(x_{2})

for all
(x_{1},x_{2})\in X
.
Consequently, for arbitrary subsets
P_{1}\subseteq X_{1}
and
P_{2}\subseteq X_{2}
,
we have

(4.2b)

g_{X,\mathscr{T}}(P_{1}\times P_{2})=\underset{(x_{1},x_{2})\in P_{1}\times P_{2}}{{\sum}^{*}}g_{X,\mathscr{T}}(x_{1},x_{2}).

This follows from the Sprague-Grundy theorem for sums of impartial games,
together with the definition of Nim multiplication as the Grundy value of the Cartesian product of two positions.
This product theorem will be used for divisor posets (see Theorem
5.2
)
and for the poset of set partitions (see Theorem
6.1
,
(ii)
).

4.4.
Two Typical Classes of Coin Turning Games

As described above,
a coin turning game

depends not only on the underlying poset

but also on the choice of the turning set
\mathscr{T}
.
We first present three typical choices for the turning set.

Definition 4.2
.

Let

be a poset.

(i)

If we take

as the turning set,
then the game
\mathscr{G}\left(X,\mathscr{T}\kern-4.0pt\mathscr{T}\right)
is called the

on

.

(ii)

If we take the turning set to be

the family of all principal order ideals of
X
,
of all principal order ideals of
X
as the turning set,
then the game
\mathscr{G}(X,\mathscr{O})
is called the

on
X
.

(iii)

If we take the turning set to be

\mathscr{I}=\left\{[x,y]\,\middle|\,x,y\in X,\,x\leq y\right\}

the family of all intervals of

, then the game
\mathscr{G}(X,\mathscr{I})
is called the

In what follows, cases
(ii)
and
(iii)
constitute the primary objects of study,
since the problem is usually straightforward in case
(i)
.
Accordingly, the main objective of this paper is
to compute the Sprague-Grundy functions of the coin turning games

in cases
(ii)
and
(iii)
.
Among these, the case

is substantially more challenging.

The following proposition is elementary and an immediate consequence of the definitions together with Theorem

but extremely useful for simplifying the computation of Grundy values by exploiting symmetries of posets.
It will be used to prove Lemma

and Lemma

for the ASM posets.

Proposition 4.2
.

Let
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
be finite posets, and let
\mathscr{T}_{1}
and
\mathscr{T}_{2}
be families of turning sets on
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
, respectively.
Suppose that
f:X_{1}\to X_{2}
is an order-preserving bijection such that

(4.3)

\mathscr{T}_{2}=\{f\left(T\right)\mid T\in\mathscr{T}_{1}\}.

Then the Grundy functions satisfy

g_{\mathscr{T}_{1}}\left(x\right)=g_{\mathscr{T}_{2}}\left(f(x)\right)

for all
x\in X_{1}
.
Hence
g_{\mathscr{T}_{1}}\left(P\right)=g_{\mathscr{T}_{2}}\left(f(P)\right)
for any subset
P\subseteq X_{1}
.

4.5.
Order Ideal Games

In the order ideal game,
we obtain the following lemma as a direct application of Theorem
4.1
.
Although this lemma is simple, it plays a crucial role in §
5.4

in the proof of Theorem
1.1

concerning order ideal games on the ASM posets.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
X
be a finite poset.
In the order ideal game on
X
,
the Grundy function

g_{\mathscr{O}}:X\to\mathbb{N}

is determined by

(4.4)

g_{\mathscr{O}}\left(x\right)=\begin{cases}0&\textrm{if }\left|\left\{t\in{\Lambda}_{x}\setminus\{x\}\,\middle|\,g_{\mathscr{O}}\left(t\right)=1\right\}\right|\text{ is odd},\\
1&\textrm{if }\left|\left\{t\in{\Lambda}_{x}\setminus\{x\}\,\middle|\,g_{\mathscr{O}}\left(t\right)=1\right\}\right|\text{ is even.}\end{cases}

Thus
g_{\mathscr{O}}\left(x\right)
takes value only
0
or
1

for each
x\in X
.
Consequently, for any position
P\subset X
,
the Grundy value

g_{\mathscr{O}}\left(P\right)

is also restricted to
0
or
1
.

For each position of the form
P=\{x\}
,
there is exactly one available move, namely
\{x\}\to{\Lambda}_{x}\setminus\{x\}
.
Therefore, (
4.1a
) of
Theorem
4.1
immediately yields the formula (
4.4
).
The second claim follows directly from this equation.
∎

Corollary 4.1
.

Let
X
be a graded poset with a unique minimum element
\hat{0}
,
and let
\rho:X\to\mathbb{N}
denote its rank function.
Then the Grundy function of the order ideal game
\mathscr{G}\left(X,\mathscr{O}\right)
is given by

(4.5)

g_{\mathscr{O}}(x)=\begin{cases}1,&\text{ if $x=\hat{0}$,}\\
0,&\text{ otherwise,}\end{cases}

for all
x\in X
.

We proceed by induction on the rank
\rho(x)
.
If
\rho(x)=0
, that is,
x=\hat{0}
, then
\Lambda_{x}\setminus\{x\}
is empty, and hence

g(x)=\operatorname{mex}\{0\}=1
.

Suppose now that
\rho(x)>0
.
By the induction hypothesis,

\hat{0}
is the only element in
\Lambda_{x}\setminus\{x\}
whose Grundy value equals
1
.
Therefore, equation (
4.4
) in Lemma
4.1
yields the desired conclusion.
This completes the proof.
∎Hence, if the poset
X
is graded and has
\hat{0}
,
then the Grundy function has a particularly simple form, as described above.
The ASM poset
\boldsymbol{A}_{n}
is graded but has multiple minimal elements,
and therefore determining the Grundy function for the order ideal game on
\boldsymbol{A}_{n}

becomes a very interesting and nontrivial problem.

5.
Sprague-Grundy Analysis of Coin Turning Games

The purpose of this section is to determine the Grundy functions of several coin turning games.

Let
a,b\in\mathbb{N}
.
The operation
a\oplus b
is performed by writing
a
and
b
in binary and then adding the digits without carrying; e.g.,

5\oplus 9=(101)_{2}\oplus(1001)_{2}=(1100)_{2}=12
.
It is well-known that
(\mathbb{N},\oplus)
forms an abelian group of exponent
2
.
In Theorem
A.1
, we prove that the NIM-sum
a\overset{*}{+}b
defined in Definition
2.4
coincides with
a\oplus b
.

Definition 5.1
.

Let
i
be a nonnegative integer.
For a positive integer
x
,
we denote by
\gamma_{i}\left({x}\right)
the
i
th digit in the binary expansion of
x
.
In other words we have
x=\sum_{i\geq 0}\gamma_{i}\left({x}\right)2^{i}
where
\gamma_{i}\left({x}\right)=0\text{ or }1
.
We further use the notation

d_{\max}\left({x}\right)=\max\{i\in\mathbb{N}\,|\,\gamma_{i}\left({x}\right)\neq 0\}
and

d_{\min}\left({x}\right)=\min\{i\in\mathbb{N}\,|\,\gamma_{i}\left({x}\right)\neq 0\}
.
For a positive integer
x
, let
\mathscr{R}\left({x}\right)=2^{d_{\min}\left({x}\right)}
.
The sequence
d_{\min}\left({x}\right)
for
x\in\mathbb{P}
is well known as the
ruler sequence
.
By a slight abuse of language, we also refer to the sequence
\mathscr{R}\left({x}\right)

5.1.
Rulers on Finite Chains

In this subsection we begin with the simplest case,

, the

The Grundy function for the order ideal game on
C
is given by Corollary

is graded and has a minimum element

Hence, in this subsection we describe the Grundy function of the ruler on

Although this case is well known,

The key ingredient is the following lemma,
which characterizes the ruler sequence via the minimum excluded value.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let

which satisfies
f(1)=1
, and

(5.1)

f(n)=\operatorname{mex}\Bigl\{\overset{n-1}{\underset{k=m}{\,\,\,{\sum}^{*}}}f(k)\,|\,m=1,2,\dots,n\Bigr\}

for
n>1
.
Then we have
f(n)=\mathscr{R}\left({n}\right)
.

We give a proof of this lemma in Appendix
B
.
This lemma will also be used in §
5.2
and §
5.3
.
Hence we are in the position to state the main theorem of this section.

Theorem 5.1

(
[
11
]
)
.

Let
n
be a positive integer,
and let
C=[n]
be the
n
-element chain with the rank function
\rho(x)=x-1
.
Then the Grundy function of the ruler on
C
is given by

(5.2)

g(x)=\mathscr{R}\left({x}\right)=\mathscr{R}\left({\rho(x)+1}\right)

.

The proof of this theorem reduces to Theorem
4.1
together with the above lemma.
Let

denote the Grundy function of the ruler

.

If
x=1\in C=[n]
is the minimum element of

there is no element less than

.

In this subsection, we focus on the ASM poset

\mathscr{T}=\mathscr{O}:=\left\{{\Lambda}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\in J\left(\boldsymbol{A}_{n}\right)\,\middle|\,\boldsymbol{x}\in\boldsymbol{A}_{n}\right\}

Now,
we denote

\mathbb{Y}_{n}:=\{(r,s)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}\mid 0\leq s\leq r\leq n-2\},

and define a map

\pi\colon\boldsymbol{A}_{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{Y}_{n},\quad\left(x,y,z\right)\mapsto\left(n-2-(x+y),z\right).

We write the coordinate projections of
\pi
as

\pi_{1}\left(x,y,z\right)=\rho(x,y,z)=n-2-(x+y)

and
\pi_{2}\left(x,y,z\right)=z
.
The following lemma then shows that the Grundy value
g\left(\boldsymbol{x}\right)

depends only on the pair
\left(\pi_{1}\left(\boldsymbol{x}\right),\pi_{2}\left(\boldsymbol{x}\right)\right)
for
\boldsymbol{x}=(x,y,z)\in\boldsymbol{A}_{n}
.

Lemma 5.4
.

Let
n
be a positive integer.
In the order ideal game

\mathscr{G}\left(\boldsymbol{A}_{n},\mathscr{O}\right)
,
the Grundy value
g\left(\boldsymbol{x}\right)
depends only on the pair
\left(\pi_{1}(\boldsymbol{x}),\pi_{2}(\boldsymbol{x})\right)

Let
\boldsymbol{x}_{1}=\left(x_{1},y_{1},z_{1}\right)

\boldsymbol{x}_{2}=\left(x_{2},y_{2},z_{2}\right)

\boldsymbol{A}_{n}

If

x_{1}+y_{1}=x_{2}+y_{2}

z_{1}=z_{2}
,

\Lambda_{\boldsymbol{x}_{1}}
and
\Lambda_{\boldsymbol{x}_{2}}
are order isomorphic.
Indeed, setting
l=x_{2}-x_{1}
,
the map

F\colon\Lambda_{\boldsymbol{x}_{1}}\rightarrow\Lambda_{\boldsymbol{x}_{2}},\qquad F\left(x,y,z\right)=\left(x+l,y-l,z\right)

is a well-defined order-preserving bijection.
Let
X_{i}=\Lambda_{\boldsymbol{x}_{i}}
and
\mathscr{O}_{i}=\{\Lambda_{\boldsymbol{y}}\in\mathscr{O}\mid\boldsymbol{y}\in X_{i}\}
for
i=1,2
.
Then the restricted games
\mathscr{G}\left(X_{1},\mathscr{O}_{1}\right)
and
\mathscr{G}\left(X_{2},\mathscr{O}_{2}\right)

satisfy condition (
4.3
) of Proposition
4.2
.
Therefore, we have
g(\boldsymbol{y})=g\left(F(\boldsymbol{y})\right)
for any
\boldsymbol{y}\in X_{1}
