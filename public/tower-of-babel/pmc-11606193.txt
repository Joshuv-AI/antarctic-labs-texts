Using the adaptive action method to tackle wicked problems in rural faculty development
Authors: Cheri Bethune, Shabnam Asghari, Andrew Graham, Alexandria Tobin, Wendy Graham
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractMedical educators face wicked (complex) problems. Few faculty development (FD) programs exist specifically designed for rural physicians. Memorial University created the 6for6 program, a longitudinal FD program in foundational research skills for rural physicians. This work explores the use of the Adaptive Action Method in tackling wicked problems in rural faculty development.Keywords: Rural research, Wicked problems, Adaptive action, Faculty development

Introduction

Medical educators have long faced complex challenges requiring a multifaceted approach, and the COVID-19 pandemic has only exacerbated these issues [1]. Rural health research is particularly overlooked, leaving rural physicians struggling to meet the needs of their communities. Rural populations have many unique challenges compared to their urban counterparts, such as limited access to healthcare facilities and services due to geographic isolation, cultural and social norms, transportation barriers, an aging population, and physician shortages [2–4]. Few faculty development (FD) programs exist specifically designed for rural physicians [5]. This is why researchers at Memorial University of Newfoundland, Canada, created the 6for6 program - a comprehensive longitudinal FD program in research skills specifically tailored for highly distributed rural physicians. This program offers a structured curriculum, mentorship opportunities, and research support over six sessions. The 6for6 program has seen great success, as described by previous publications [6–8]; however, alumni still report challenges and barriers in engaging in research relevant to their communities [1].

Rural physicians face several intractable, or ‘wicked,’ problems in their practice populations. These wicked issues are complex, and often, finding a solution seems elusive [9]. While there are alternative terms, ‘wicked’ originates from the social planning field with recent uses in medical education to describe complex and challenging problems [10, 11]. An effective strategy for tackling these problems is the Adaptive Action Method (AAM) [12]. This method involves three main questions: What? So What? And Now What?

“What?” encourages dialogue between stakeholders to recognize the power of diverse perspectives when dealing with intractable issues;

“So what?” acknowledges the importance of the problem and identifies the affected parties;

Finally, “Now what?” focuses on taking action by assessing and applying the solutions and strategies presented through the iterative AAM process.

By asking these questions, identifying diverse perspectives, acknowledging wicked problems as patterns, and taking wise actions based on consequences, rural physicians can gain greater understanding and work toward addressing their intractable challenges [13].

The AAM was highlighted in Stewart Mennin’s article ‘Ten Global Challenges in Medical Education: Wicked Issues and Options for Action’ as a strategy to tackle wicked problems in medical education [11]. This powerful tool encourages creativity through an iterative cycle of evaluation that allows for adaptations to be made as new challenges arise, which leads to increased creativity in the spirit of continuous Quality Improvement (QI). By employing the AAM, the 6for6 program has been developed, instructed, and improved as a unique rural faculty development program - designed specifically to respond to these complex issues [14]. We will outline utilizing the AAM in three different contexts to demonstrate its broad applicability.

The first wicked problem: how can rural physicians gain research capability?

The birth of a research skills program designed for rural clinicians

A Rural physician is at the ‘coalface’ of healthcare in their community - uniquely positioned as a problem solver and advocate for issues related to the health of their local community. A ‘go-to’ person. This role requires a rural physician to demonstrate leadership in identifying and addressing healthcare challenges by seeking local solutions. This leadership can demand research skills. So, what research skills are necessary for a rural family physician, and how can they best attain them?

The what? (what is the problem)

A challenge of many healthcare problems lies in their complexity. This complexity involves many players (stakeholders); levels of government and bureaucracy, the local players within the community, and distance and geography. Navigating this is challenging, and the problem(s) often appear insoluble. The Faculty of Medicine at Memorial University is committed to training physicians for its widely distributed rural population. One of the “wicked” problems we identified was how to support busy clinicians in attaining relevant research skills to better equip them to address the unique challenges they face in serving their rural communities.

So what? (how important or crucial is the problem?)

Rural physicians seldom have access to faculty development programs that engage them in meaningful research. The challenges at the individual level include time, knowledge and skills, and financial barriers. At the institutional level, a commitment to addressing the educational needs of distributed faculty beyond their role as clinical teachers is not a priority. At the community level, engagement with local stakeholders, health services administration, and various levels of bureaucracy is challenging. This complexity is clearly a wicked problem.

“Many family doctors in rural places feel isolated and intimidated. This program was very expressly about giving those family doctors the confidence to know they could address a question and answer it in a way that made sense for their community,” 6for6 Program Lead [14].

The now what? (what possible action(s) can be taken to address the problem?)

We conducted a national survey of primary care research directors (a nominal group) to identify fundamental research skills, expose some barriers, and suggest pedagogical strategies to address this gap. A more focused needs assessment of rural physician scholars helped identify their barriers to research engagement, including time, clinical workload, geographical distance, support, and skills. A program model and curriculum were conceptualized using adult learning and participatory action [5, 14].

The proposed pilot project was vetted by the Dean (a former rural clinician researcher) as a major stakeholder. The concepts and approach resonated with his philosophy of supporting and sustaining rural generalists. Three years of core funding was initially obtained [15], which has since become an annual operational grant from Memorial University’s Faculty of Medicine. To date, there have been 44 rural participants over ten years who have completed the 6for6 program.

“It really was a program about demystifying how to ask questions. About how to get answers. About {challenging} the idea that research is hard. And it’s fundamentally a participatory-action research project, responding to the realities of family doctors in rural and remote places,” 6for6 Program Lead [14].

The 6for6 program emerged iteratively over several years with effective feedback from participants who shaped the program content and process to better align with their learning needs. Through the guiding questions of “what,” “so what,” and “now what,” 6for6 has been continuously improved and adapted. For example, the COVID-19 pandemic required the 6for6 team to quickly pivot to an online-only delivery format for the 2020–2021 cohort [1]. By applying the AAM as a problem-solving method, the 6for6 team was able to break down the task into manageable pieces and identify the necessary steps to initiate the transition. In this case, our “what” became how could the 6for6 team deliver the program online, while retaining the essential qualities of in-person instruction? “So what” identified the importance of maintaining the essence of the program to ensure that online participants receive the same level of research competency as previous cohorts. Some important considerations were the participant’s ability to provide peer feedback, connect with their mentor, librarian, and program coordinator, and engage with other participants [1]. Finally, “now what” prompted the development of a stepwise plan to address the problem. This included an environmental scan to determine the best approach to deliver online content, internal and external consultations with education design experts, subject-matter experts, and 6for6 alumni, as well as various other methods. The online model was a success, which we have used to inform further modifications to the 6for6 program delivery model [1]. Thus, the AAM has continued to serve as an effective method to adapt our program in the face of challenges.

The second wicked problem: how to narrow (refine or clarify) a research question?

The 6for6 program is designed to empower rural clinicians with the knowledge, skills, and confidence to pursue their research questions. Novice researchers often struggle to solidify their research question. Specifically, novice researchers in rural settings face significantly more barriers than their urban counterparts, and they often investigate complex, challenging issues that have long plagued their rural communities, making this task particularly difficult. However, solidifying a research question provides foundational clarity and direction to guide their work, making it imperative for their success. The 6for6 process applies the AAM to each participant’s research question to address this issue. This aids participants in clarifying the problem, its importance, and the next steps. We have found these three questions useful in coaching the participants to refine their research question and articulate the importance of the problem.

The what? (what is the problem)

Participants often come into the 6for6 program with a passionate but vague research concept, driven by the problems they observe in their rural practice. They know a lot about the topic and know there are many complex and intertwined aspects. 6for6 was created to help solve this problem by developing rural and remote family physicians’ research competency, defined as the knowledge, attitudes, and skills required to complete their own research project [6].

A 6for6 participant brought the following issue to his 6for6 peers: A group of concerned citizens have gathered in the town hall in our small community with a common concern of the health of their well water. There have been emerging rumours that the well water supply in this region has had higher levels of arsenic than acceptable for health and safety. This environmental concern has been translated into ‘health problems’ in the community, such as increased incidence of bladder cancer. The community anxiety is high. How can I, as the local family physician, help address this fundamental problem in my rural community? [16]

So what? (how important or crucial is the problem?)

6for6 participants are supported to recognize the value and extent of their research interests. Who and what does this research impact? What stakeholders are at play? This brainstorming helps participants understand that there are multiple variables they can study to answer their research question. Within each identified variable are various quantitative and qualitative investigation methods.

Not only does contaminated water affect the individual’s health, but it also increases the demand on an already strained healthcare system. This also affects governments in the way of finding solutions – do you pay for the fix now, or pay via worse health outcomes for the citizens? The implications of this issue are broad.

The now what? (what possible action(s) can be taken to address the problem?)

At this point, the novice researcher has identified the true scope of their idea and will then utilize the “now what?” to help hone their question and focus on a specific way to investigate it. Here, participants look at each idea they came up with in the “so what?” and carefully examine the feasibility of each. What barriers exist that may prevent one of the ideas from being used? The idea here is for participants to envision how to move forward with their project. Rather than staying stuck by the complexity, ideas are analysed by peers and faculty using specific, measurable, achievable, relevant, and time-bound (SMART) criteria and then refined. The “now what?” also involves 6for6’s “secret sauce,” which involves vital collaborations with other researchers, librarians, and experts who help form the participant’s research team.

The Community wells were tested, some were identified as unsafe, and various levels of government and scientists were engaged with the local community in offering remediation and alternative water sources. This community action has been recognized as having an impact beyond the local community to other settings where arsenic and other natural contaminants have threatened the safety of the local water supply, and broader subsequent implications for more rural community well safety have surfaced. [16]

Narrowing down a research question in and of itself is a wicked problem that is helped with using the AAM. The AAM provides structure by evaluating each topic systematically, thus enabling researchers to avoid an overwhelming cycle of ideas and solutions. Additionally, the AAM encourages researchers to appraise their work and consider how their results might help others in similar contexts.

The third wicked problem: rural recruitment and retention

The AAM is not limited to program development or refining research questions. The 6for6 team has taken the initiative to share our success with the AAM at numerous national and international conferences to demonstrate to attendees how to apply the method to their own wicked problems. Most recently, the 6for6 team presented a faculty development workshop at the 30th Annual Rural & Remote Medicine Conference on tackling wicked problems such as rural recruitment and retention using the AAM. This final example demonstrates the AAM’s broad applicability in empowering rural physicians to tackle other vexing problems.

The what? (what is the problem?)

The 6for6 team has presented the AAM as an interactive faculty development workshop to encourage the attendees to think about their wicked problem and then use the method to work through their problem. After identifying their "what," attendees discuss why the problem matters with their peers to identify their next steps. The most common “what” that has arisen through this exercise is the wicked problem of recruitment and retention in the face of critical physician shortages. Rural physician recruitment is a major challenge that can result in many additional wicked problems. Not only does low recruitment and retention affect the quality of care provided to rural communities, but it also diminishes the amount and quality of rural research being conducted [17]. If there are no rural physicians with research capacity, less relevant rural research can be done.

An attendee at the Society of Rural Physicians meeting is struggling with the wicked challenge of rural doctor shortages. With no other care options for people, effectively managing the workload is a challenge. There are not enough providers to get it all done.

So what? (how important or crucial is the problem?)

Rural physicians are often confronted with challenges and barriers that their urban counterparts are not, such as professional and geographical isolation. This can exacerbate issues such as physician burnout and increased wait times, leading to worse health outcomes for those living in rural communities. Rural and remote citizens deserve prompt and efficient care, and providing doctors to serve these communities is socially accountable. The AAM provides an avenue for rural physicians to take steps toward addressing their wicked problems.

Burnout leads to attrition. Physician attrition is a major problem in rural areas, leading to orphan patients, overrun ER departments, and critical hospital closures [18]. The recruitment of new physicians is impacted [19].

A rural physician struggling with burnout due to physician shortages can apply the AAM to identify new strategies they may not have considered. By asking “so what,” physicians are prompted to acknowledge the problem and consider the affected parties before devising solutions. This method ensures that the physician examines their problem from different perspectives. For example, what stakeholders are involved and can any assist with addressing a portion of the problem? This could include coworkers, the health provider, professional organizations, and other healthcare professionals such as nurse practitioners, physician assistants, social workers, and physiotherapists, among others. The AAM is a framework that inspires and encourages new ideas.

The now what? (what possible action(s) can be taken to address the problem?)

One of the goals of the 6for6 team has been to share the AAM with other rural and remote physicians. By presenting the AAM at national and international conferences to encourage attendees to apply it to their wicked problems, we start a dialogue amongst the workshop attendees to discuss their challenges and solutions. Often, their challenges are similar, and attendees are able to offer their peers insightful and practical solutions from their own work. Furthermore, the boost of morale gained from the knowledge that they are not alone in their struggle, and that many of the issues they face are shared issues, is crucial. Success with these workshops has helped numerous attendees approach their wicked problems with a new mindset.

Expanding provider options could provide a solution to rural physician burnout. Peers brainstormed how physician assistants could fit into our health system to ease the burden of care on rural providers.

In the case of rural physician burnout, workshop attendees have had several valuable suggestions. For example, the physician may defer some of their duties to other members of their team, such as a physician assistant, as appropriate. If the physician is struggling with administrative burden, they could reach out to their employer to request additional support. Alternatively, the physician could contact their health authority to inquire about locum coverage. Many attendees have direct experience from what has helped them in their daily practice, as physician shortages and burnout are pervasive issues. The workshop setting provides an opportunity for the attendees, guided by the AAM, to share their knowledge with other rural physicians who may not have considered the solutions presented.

Conclusions

Rural health research is notoriously difficult due to barriers such as professional and geographical isolation. Additionally, many rural physicians lack the skills, competencies, and resources to conduct research in their communities despite being optimally positioned to investigate these issues [1, 5–8]. The 6for6 program provides the framework for rural physicians to begin their research journey; however, it does not solve the numerous ‘wicked’ problems rural physicians face in their daily practice.

By using three separate examples, this article aims to demonstrate the broad applicability and benefit of employing the AAM to tackle systems-level challenges in rural health care, as described by Mennin [11]. By utilizing the AAM to continuously improve the 6for6 program, refine participants’ research questions, and examine complex health issues and possible solutions, we illustrate the utility of the AAM in addressing several ‘wicked’ rural health challenges.

We (the 6for6 team) face challenges in our longitudinal FD program for rural researchers as we face the ‘wicked’ challenge of sustainability of rural health research following our foundational program. Upon completion of their program or project, rural physicians lose momentum in the face of ongoing rural resource challenges. We are engaging with an AAM approach to clarify the challenges leading to research disengagement and are exploring some solutions to rural research capacity building. One of our solutions is to integrate the 6for6 alumni into the program once they have finished their formal training. For example, by inviting them as guest speakers, mentors, and scientific planning committee members for subsequent cohorts, we ensure they remain engaged in rural research [20].
