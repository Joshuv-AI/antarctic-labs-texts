Taking the eye-tracker out to dinner: characterizing spatial attention biases during an everyday behavior using computer vision
Authors: S Ladouce, C R Gillebert
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractVisual exploration during everyday tasks reveals attentional processes and offers promising avenues for clinical assessment. In this study, we examined whether the spatial attention bias induced by the presence of a mobile phone during a routine activity, eating dinner, can be effectively captured using wearable sensors that record gaze and body orientation. In a within-subject design, participants ate spaghetti while their mobile phone was either absent or placed on the left or right side of their tray. Our analyses focused on deviations in gaze and body orientation from the center of the plate and fixations on target objects automatically extracted through computer vision. Phone placement shifted gaze toward its location, producing a clear lateralization throughout the meal: without a phone, gaze centered normally; with a phone, participants fixated more on nearby objects and less on those opposite. These results demonstrate that wearable eye-tracking can detect spatial attention biases in natural behavior. Integrating computer vision enabled automatic contextualization of gaze data, allowing for the extraction of meaningful features related to specific elements of the visual environment. This scalable, non-invasive, and ecologically valid approach holds promise for assessing attentional dynamics in real-world context.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-24264-5.Keywords: Eye-tracking, Spatial attention, Ecological validity, Wearable sensors, Computer vision, Everyday behaviorSubject terms: Diagnostics, Drug safety, Liver diseases, Risk factors, Digestive signs and symptoms, Liver, Liver diseases

Introduction

Spatial attention is crucial for filtering and efficiently retrieving relevant information from the continuous and rich visual stream encountered in everyday life. This cognitive mechanism enables individuals to prioritize specific regions of space, guiding perception and actions within cluttered and dynamic environments1. By orienting our bodies, head, and eyes toward pertinent locations or objects while ignoring irrelevant ones, spatial attention optimizes cognitive processing, enhancing task performance2. Research consistently indicates that spatial attention exhibits systematic biases rather than uniform distribution across the visual field. Traditionally, these biases have been studied using laboratory paradigms such as visual search, line bisection, and stimulus detection tasks, revealing fundamental mechanisms governing how attention is deployed through task performance measures such as detection rates and reaction times3,4.

Although task performance measures have long been used to infer attentional deployment, the advent of eye-tracking technology has enabled more direct and fine-grained observation of gaze behavior5,6. Eye-tracking allows researchers to quantify overt spatial biases through metrics such as fixation durations, gaze shifts, and spatial distributions of visual exploration. For instance, in free-viewing paradigms, participants consistently exhibit greater fixation density toward specific spatial locations, typically those that are visually salient or contextually informative, revealing attentional preferences and cognitive strategies7,8. A substantial body of research has shown that spatial attention is not uniformly distributed across the visual field but instead exhibits systematic biases. One such example is the horizontal bias, a tendency to scan scenes more extensively along the horizontal axis, which has been attributed to both biomechanical constraints and culturally shaped viewing habits9,10. Another well-documented effect is the central bias, where fixations tend to cluster near the center of visual displays, often due to display composition, default motor tendencies, or framing effects11–14. Additionally, gaze patterns are shaped by object-context associations, with attention directed toward semantically meaningful or expected locations based on learned scene structure15,16.

Collectively, these biases reflect the influence of low-level salience, task relevance, learned priors, and environmental structure in guiding attention across both structured and naturalistic viewing. Understanding how such biases emerge in controlled settings provides a critical foundation for investigating how they may be preserved or altered in real-world environments. Importantly, gaze-based measures have also proven sensitive for detecting spatial attention deficits following neurological impairments, often surpassing the resolution of traditional paper-and-pencil neuropsychological assessments17.

Until recently, eye-tracking research remained confined to controlled laboratory settings, raising questions about whether findings obtained in controlled, static environments generalize to everyday life situations18. Real-world settings are continually shaping attentional allocation to ongoing behavior, task demands, and environmental factors19. Laboratory experiments, which often isolate attention within highly controlled and simplified scenarios, might only partially capture the complexity of naturalistic behavior20. Consequently, questions have been raised regarding whether findings derived from traditional laboratory tasks can be generalized to explain attentional processes occurring in daily life. Indeed, it has been argued that some of the attentional phenomena observed in laboratory tasks may reflect idiosyncrasies of experimental protocols or constraints rather than genuine aspects of everyday attention21. Addressing these limitations has become increasingly feasible with the advent of portable sensors, such as wearable eye-tracking glasses and motion-sensing devices, enabling researchers to explore attention beyond the laboratory22,23.

Within the rich attentional landscape of everyday environments, smartphones represent a particularly personally relevant and attention-capturing object due to their habitual use and potential for cognitive and physiological engagement24. With billions of users globally25, smartphones continuously engage individuals cognitively and emotionally, attracting involuntary attention even when irrelevant to current tasks26,27. Empirical studies indicate that the mere presence of smartphones (i.e., even turned off or placed face down) significantly impairs cognitive performance in a range of standardized cognitive assessment tasks28–30. Notably, individual differences in habitual smartphone use can further enhance spatial biases towards these devices31. These findings have been interpreted in the context of the limited pool of attentional resources theory32,33, explaining that attentional resources are partially allocated to the device’s presence, reflecting implicit monitoring and attention oriented toward the smartphone. This aligns with broader research on attentional capture, which shows that stimuli associated with reward or habitual engagement elicit automatic orienting responses34. Collectively, these findings position smartphones as uniquely powerful ecological stimuli that can be used for experimentally manipulating spatial biases in naturalistic contexts.

To examine how distractors influence attention in real-world settings, researchers increasingly rely on portable eye-tracking devices to capture sensitive markers of attention during natural activities. Wearable eye-tracking has opened new avenues for capturing gaze dynamics during everyday activities such as walking35,36 and social interactions37. Unlike traditional setups that constrain movement, portable eye-tracking provides a first-person, egocentric perspective closely mirroring participants’ real-world visual experiences. Early studies employing these methods have highlighted differences in attentional patterns between laboratory and natural settings, emphasizing the influence of contextual factors and ongoing behaviors38,39. By concurrently capturing gaze and bodily movements, wearable systems can further elucidate the close coupling between attention, perception, and action36,40.

The present study aimed to capitalize on wearable eye-tracking technology to capture overt spatial attention biases induced by the lateral placement (left, right, or absent) of participants’ smartphones during natural everyday behavior (see Fig. 1). Specifically, we examined horizontal gaze eccentricity as an index of lateral spatial bias, which has proven sensitive for identifying spatial attentional biases induced experimentally or following neurological damage17,41,42. We additionally contrasted gaze and body orientation metrics during an ecologically valid meal-eating task with those recorded in a tablet-based free-viewing task, exploring how the smartphone’s presence influences attentional allocation across contexts differing in ecological realism19,43. This within-subject comparison provides a direct test of whether attentional effects observed in the lab generalize to everyday behavior.

One key challenge in applying eye-tracking to real-world environments is the difficulty of accurately identifying gaze targets in a dynamic, three-dimensional space. In computerized paradigms, the location of visual stimuli is controlled and registered at all times, facilitating the extraction of gaze features related to the exploration of objects of interest44. In contrast, obtaining information about the visual exploration of natural stimuli embedded in a three-dimensional environment presents methodological challenges45. Previous works have relied on manual annotations and segmentation of the gaze data46 which are time-consuming and prone to human errors, or placing markers near objects of interest to track their positions, contaminating the visual environment with visually salient distractors. To circumvent these issues, we implemented computer vision algorithms to automatically detect objects in the visual environment and contextualize gaze data47,48.

Building upon this evidence and methodology, the present study evaluated whether wearable eye-tracking technology can capture overt spatial attention biases induced by the presence of a salient distractor during an everyday behavior. We hypothesized that the smartphone’s lateral placement would systematically bias visual attention toward its location, as reflected by a lateral shift in gaze eccentricity and increased exploration of nearby objects, across both a free-viewing tablet task and a naturalistic meal. By integrating wearable eye-tracking with object-recognition techniques, this study offers a generalizable approach for analyzing spatial attention in naturalistic environments, a method with broader applicability for ecological cognition research. Furthermore, the study aimed to determine whether smartphone-induced attentional biases observed in laboratory experiments extend to naturalistic contexts, thereby addressing a key open question regarding the ecological validity of lab-based findings. Understanding how salient distractors such as smartphones bias attention in everyday contexts may inform the design of attention-sensitive technologies or interventions aimed at minimizing distraction in learning or safety–critical environments.

Results

Free viewing tablet task

Gaze eccentricity

To examine whether the phone’s lateral placement influenced the spatial distribution of gaze during the free-viewing tablet task, we analyzed the proportion of gaze samples falling within left, central, and right eccentricity bins. A significant main effect of eccentricity bin was found [F(1.15, 26.59) = 1608.70, p < 0.001, = 0.98], indicating that gaze distribution differed substantially across the three spatial bins. There was no main effect of phone location on the proportion of gaze samples falling within the eccentricity bins [F(2, 46) = 29.09, p = 1.00, = 4.773], as all gaze samples fell within the defined eccentricity range across all phone position conditions. A significant interaction was observed between phone location and eccentricity bin [F(1.79, 41.27) = 223.15, p < 0.001, = 0.90].

Post-hoc paired-sample t-tests revealed a significant centralization of gaze in the phone-absent condition. A greater proportion of gaze samples fell within the central eccentricity bin in the absent condition compared to both the left [t(23) = 15.03, p < 0.001, d = 3.58] and right [t(23) = 12.92, p < 0.001, d = 3.08] phone conditions. This pattern reflects the higher kurtosis of the gaze eccentricity distribution in the absent condition, with gaze more tightly clustered near the center (see Fig. 2).

When a phone was present, gaze shifted toward its location. A significantly greater proportion of gaze samples fell within the left eccentricity bin when the phone was on the left side, compared to both the absent [t(23) = 19.03, p < 0.001, d = 4.53] and right phone conditions [t(23) = 22.73, p < 0.001, d = 5.41]. Similarly, more gaze samples fell within the right eccentricity bin when the phone was on the right side, relative to both the absent [t(23) = 16.63, p < 0.001, d = 3.96] and left phone conditions [t(23) = 20.62, p < 0.001, d = 4.91].

Interestingly, the phone-absent condition showed slightly more gaze directed to the left side than the right-phone condition [t(23) = 3.70, p < 0.01, d = 0.88], and more gaze to the right side than the left-phone condition [t(23) = 3.99, p < 0.001, d = 0.95]. This result, in which the phone-absent condition shows relatively more gaze to both left and right sides compared to the opposing phone-present conditions, reflects two interacting factors: (1) the increased centralization of gaze in the absent condition, resulting in reduced proportions in both peripheral bins; and (2) the lateralized attentional pull of the phone in the present conditions, which suppresses exploration of the opposite side.

Taken together, these results indicate that the presence and lateral placement of a phone reliably biased gaze toward the phone’s side, while reducing peripheral gaze to the opposite side.

Proportion of fixation time on objects of interest

To assess whether the phone’s position affected object-based attention in the free-viewing tablet task. we analyzed the proportion of fixation time on predefined areas of interest: the phone, plate, lateral objects, and background.

Phone: There was no significant difference observed in the proportion of time the phone was fixated between the left and right phone position conditions [t(1,23) = 1.46, p > 0.05, d = 0.30].

Plate: Phone position had a significant effect on the proportion of time spent fixating on the plate [F(2,46) = 46.08, p < 0.001, = 0.67]. Post-hoc comparisons revealed that the plate was fixated significantly more when the phone was absent compared to when it was placed on the left [t(23) = 8.00, p < 0.001, d = 2.16] or right [t(23) = 8.59, p < 0.001, d = 2.32] side. No difference was observed in plate fixation between the left and right phone conditions [t(23) = 0.59, p > 0.05, d = 0.16].

Lateral objects: Phone position had a main effect on the proportion of fixation time on lateral objects [F(2,46) = 13.92, p < 0.001, = 0.38]. Post-hoc tests revealed that the proportion of fixations on the lateral objects close to the phone was higher compared to those on the objects that were on the opposite side [t(1,23) = 5.2, p < 0.001, d = 1.49] as well as to these lateral objects when the phone was absent [t(1,23) = 3.27, p < 0.01, d = 0.93]. No difference was found between the proportion of time the lateral objects opposite to the phone were explored as compared to the same objects when the phone was absent [t(1,23) = 1.95, p > 0.05, d = 0.56].

Background: There was no significant effect of phone position on the proportion of time spent fixating on the background [F(2,46) = 0.32,p > 0.05, = 0.01].

Meal eating

Gaze eccentricity

We next examined whether phone location biased gaze direction during naturalistic meal consumption by comparing the distribution of gaze samples across left, central, and right eccentricity bins.

There was no main effect of phone location on the proportion of gaze samples falling within the predefined eccentricity bins [F(2, 46) = 0.05, p = 1.00, = 0], as all gaze samples across conditions fell within the defined eccentricity range. However, there was a significant main effect of eccentricity bin [F(1.27, 29.25) = 656.58, p < 0.001, = 0.96], indicating that gaze distribution varied substantially across spatial bins. Moreover, a significant interaction was observed between phone location and eccentricity bin [F(2.82, 65.82) = 40.89, p < 0.001, = 0.64].

Post-hoc paired-sample t-tests showed that a greater proportion of gaze samples fell within the central bin in the absent condition compared to both the left [t(23) = 31.26, p < 0.001, d = 8.57] and right [t(23) = 31.50, p < 0.001, d = 8.64] phone conditions. This reflects a higher kurtosis in the gaze eccentricity distribution when no phone was present (see Fig. 3).

More notably, a higher proportion of gaze samples fell within the left eccentricity bin when the phone was placed on the left side of the tray compared to both the absent [t(23) = 8.33, p < 0.001, d = 1.86] and right phone positions [t(23) = 9.20, p < 0.001, d = 2.05]. No significant difference was found in the proportion of gaze samples within the left bin between the absent and right phone conditions [t(23) = 0.87, p = 1.00, d = 0.19].

Conversely, a greater proportion of gaze samples fell within the right eccentricity bin when the phone was placed on the right side of the tray, compared to both the absent [t(23) = 7.13, p < 0.001, d = 1.59] and left phone positions [t(23) = 8.43, p < 0.001, d = 1.88]. No difference was found between the absent and left phone conditions for the right eccentricity bin [t(23) = 1.30, p = 1.00, d = 0.29].

Taken together, these findings indicate that the presence of a phone on either side of the tray significantly biased gaze distribution toward the corresponding side during the meal-eating task.

Proportion of fixation time on objects of interest

We also analyzed the proportion of time participants spent fixating on key objects of interest during meals (i.e., the phone, plate, lateral objects, and background) as a function of phone position.

Phone: For the meal eating, there was no significant difference observed in the proportion of time the phone was fixated between the left and right phone position conditions [t(1,23) = 0.34, p > 0.05, d = 0.07].

Plate: No significant effect of phone position was observed on the proportion of fixation time on the plate [F(2, 46) = 0.32, p > 0.05, = 0.01].

Background: Similarly, there was no significant effect of phone position on the proportion of time spent fixating on the background [F(2, 46) = 0.75, p > 0.05, = 0.03].

Lateral objects: Phone position significantly influenced the proportion of fixation time on lateral objects [F(1.54, 35.51) = 9.44, p < 0.001, = 0.29]. Participants fixated more on objects located on the same side as the phone compared to those on the opposite side [t(23) = 4.28, p < 0.001, d = 1.24], and also more than when the phone was absent [t(23) = 2.79, p < 0.05, d = 0.81]. No significant difference was found between the proportion of fixations on lateral objects opposite the phone and the same objects in the phone-absent condition [t(23) = 1.48, p > 0.05, d = 0.43].

Head and torso orientation

To determine whether spatial attention shifts during meal eating were accompanied by overt body movements, we examined head and torso orientation across conditions, focusing on whether participants physically turned toward the phone’s location.

Head orientation: A significant main effect of eccentricity bin on head orientation measures distribution was observed [F(1.19, 27.42) = 176.23, p < 0.001, = 0.88], indicating that head orientation was not uniformly distributed across the visual field. Post-hoc t-tests showed that the proportion of head orientation toward the central field was significantly greater than toward the left [t(23) = 12.42, p < 0.001, d = 2.39] and right [t(23) = 18.40, p < 0.001, d = 3.54] fields. There was no main effect of phone position on the distribution of head yaw orientation [F(2, 46) = 0.00, p = 1.00, = 0]. In all meal-eating conditions, head rotation measures fell entirely within the range defined by the three eccentricity bins. The interaction between phone position and eccentricity bin was not significant [F(1.94, 44.64) = 0.22, p > 0.05, = 0.01].

Torso orientation: A main effect of eccentricity bin was observed [F(2, 46) = 870.20, p < 0.001, = 0.97], with a significantly higher proportion of torso orientation directed toward the central field compared to both the left [t(23) = 36.50, p < 0.001, d = 7.14] and right [t(23) = 35.73, p < 0.001, d = 6.99] fields. Again, no main effect of phone position was found on the distribution of torso yaw orientation [F(2, 46) = 0.00, p = 1.00, = 0]. Torso rotation values also fell entirely within the predefined eccentricity range across all conditions. The interaction between phone position and eccentricity bin was not significant [F(2.06, 47.49) = 1.29, p > 0.05, = 0.05].

To further interpret the absence of condition-level effects in head and torso orientation, we examined inter-individual variability. For torso orientation, participants showed highly stable and centered alignment across conditions: on average, 97.35% of samples (SD = 4, range = 83–100%) fell within the central orientation field, with only two participants exhibiting noticeable deviations in one condition, none of which were aligned with phone position.

For head orientation, a greater degree of individual variability was observed. On average, 77% of samples (SD = 11, range = 57–91%) fell within the central bin, and while some participants occasionally turned their heads more laterally, these shifts did not show a consistent relationship with phone location. These findings suggest that, in the context of a naturalistic eating task, body orientation remains mostly stable, and that the observed lateral gaze shifts occurred primarily via eye movements rather than head or torso reorientation.

Behavioral measures

We evaluated whether phone presence influenced meal-related behavior by analyzing total eating duration across the three phone conditions. We also assessed whether hunger or time since last meal were associated with eating duration. No significant effect of mobile phone presence or position was observed on the time taken to eat the meal [F(2, 46) = 1.23, p > 0.05, = 0.05]. Additionally, neither subjective hunger ratings nor the reported time since the last meal were significantly correlated with eating duration across meal eating and tablet sessions [r(71) = –0.15, p > 0.05; r(71) = –0.02, p > 0.05, respectively].

Questionnaires results

Finally, we explored whether individual differences in smartphone use—captured via self-reported screen time and PUMP scores—were associated with gaze behavior, particularly the proportion of time spent fixating the phone in each condition. Participants reported an average daily smartphone usage of 1 h and 48 min (SD = 34 min). Reported phone usage time was positively correlated with the proportion of time the phone was fixated during meals [rS (23) = 0.41, p < 0.05]. This association was not observed in the tablet condition, where the proportion of fixation time was not significantly related to reported phone use [rS (23) = 0.16, p > 0.05].

The average total score on the Problematic Mobile Phone Use (PUMP) scale was 35 (SD = 5.6) out of a possible maximum of 100. PUMP scores were positively correlated with the proportion of time the phone was fixated during the meal-eating condition [rS(23) = 0.45, p < 0.05], but not in the tablet condition [rS(23) = 0.11, p > 0.05].

Discussion

The main objective of this study was to assess the validity and sensitivity of wearable gaze-tracking and motion-sensing devices in capturing over spatial attention biases during an everyday behavior. To this end, 24 participants were equipped with portable eye-tracking glasses and an IMU belt while engaging in either an ecologically valid task (i.e., eating a meal) or a less naturalistic, free-viewing task presented on a tablet. Spatial attention bias was experimentally induced by manipulating the presence and lateral position of a visually and emotionally salient object: the participant’s own smartphone. These manipulations were implemented in both the meal-eating and tablet-based tasks. While the tablet condition was not designed for direct comparison, it provided a structured reference point to contextualize gaze and attention metrics observed in the meal eating condition.

Our results demonstrate that a spatial attention bias can be induced by the presence and position of a smartphone in the visual environment. Gaze eccentricity, defined as the horizontal displacement of gaze from the center of the tray, was sensitive to the phone’s location in both conditions. Object-related gaze features, such as the proportion of fixation time and number of fixations on lateral objects, similarly reflected this bias as participants directed more attention to objects positioned on the same side as the phone. Notably, the relationship between fixation time on the phone and self-reported smartphone use was only found to be significant during the meal-eating condition. This suggests that gaze data collected in naturalistic contexts may provide higher sensitivity to individual differences.

The observed lateralized gaze patterns demonstrate that spatial attention is modulated by emotionally salient, everyday objects, even in the absence of explicit task demands. This aligns with existing literature on exogenous attention capture49 and offers an important real-world extension of prior findings. In particular, our data offer new insight into the mere presence effect of smartphones. Ito and Kawahara31 previously found that target detection in a visual search task was faster when targets appeared on the same side as a visible smartphone, especially in individuals with higher habitual smartphone use. While they concluded that phones impose a general attentional load regardless of spatial location, they also observed spatial bias effects in heavy users, highlighting the role of individual usage patterns in attentional capture. The authors also compared performance when a notepad was present and found that target detection was slower in the phone condition, indicating a mere presence effect. Notably, this distraction occurred regardless of target location, suggesting that the phone imposed a general cognitive load. Based on these behavioral data, the authors concluded that attentional resources were broadly taxed by the need to filter out the distracting presence of the phone. In our study, the reported gaze features help to clarify this ambiguity: across both paradigms, the phone’s presence induced clear lateralized shifts in gaze, supporting the idea that the phone imposes not only a general cognitive load but also a spatially localized attentional bias. This supports a refinement of the “mere presence” account, indicating that distraction from personally salient objects is not purely diffuse but can include spatial components even when no task-relevant targets are displaced.

Our findings also extend observations from Koessmeier and Büttner50, who manipulated smartphone presence during cognitive testing and reading tasks with eye-tracking glasses. The authors questioned the strength of the smartphone’s mere presence effect, as participants’ performance to the tasks and their spatial attention were not affected by the presence of the smartphone during the tasks. Although they reported no performance or gaze changes during the tasks, they did find that attention was drawn to the smartphone during breaks. This suggests that attentional effects of smartphone presence may be context-dependent, i.e. emerging more readily when attentional demands are low and participants are free to distribute their attentional resources spontaneously as opposed to following task instructions. Our results corroborate and extend this view, highlighting the critical role of task context in modulating attentional capture. In structured tasks with explicit goals, participants may engage top-down attentional control that limits the influence of exogenous distractors51. In contrast, naturalistic, self-paced behaviors such as eating allow attentional biases to emerge more readily, as attentional allocation is less constrained by task instructions. This context-sensitive modulation suggests that task structure is a key gating factor in attentional capture and should be explicitly incorporated into theoretical models of attention in real-world settings.

Indeed, we report that within the frame of a habitual behavior such as eating a meal, which can be considered an active although not highly demanding behavior in terms of sensory and cognitive processing of visual information, smartphone’s presence affected spatial attention. In contrast to experimental tasks that require participants’ full attention, many everyday life behaviors are performed while attention is directed toward exogenous (i.e., ambient sound52,), endogenous (e.g., mind-wandering53,), or even no information at all (mind-blanking,54). This discrepancy between structured experimental tasks and natural behaviors underscores the need for ecologically valid investigations of spatial attention.

Furthermore, Yarbus55 demonstrated that explicit task instructions induce top-down gaze strategies toward the sampling of task-relevant regions. By contrast, in undirected, natural behaviors attention can drift toward non-task-related objects, revealing individual biases. For individuals without problematic smartphone habits, attentional control might be sufficient to override distraction in task-based contexts, whereas such control might not be spontaneously engaged during passive, everyday behavior. Thus, measuring spatial biases during natural activity may yield greater diagnostic sensitivity.

Exploratory analyses further revealed that gaze behavior was not consistently associated participants between the tablet and meal-eating conditions. Specifically, correlations between conditions in the proportion of time spent fixating the phone, and in gaze allocation to the phone side or the opposite side, were not statistically significant (see Supplementary Materials). These findings suggest that spatial attention biases are not stable across contexts, but may instead dynamically adapt to task structure and environmental demands.

Interestingly, the relationship between the proportion of time the phone was fixated during the meal and both self-reported phone use and PUMP scale scores suggests that gaze measures of spatial bias in everyday life could serve as clinical and behavioral diagnostics. This potential is underscored by the fact that our sample was relatively homogeneous in habitual phone use, yet significant correlation still emerged. Eye-tracking metrics may therefore be especially sensitive for detecting systematic biases in individuals with stimulus-related addictions56.

Our findings highlight the utility of gaze eccentricity and object-based gaze metrics for capturing spatial attention biases in everyday behaviors. Portable eye-tracking has been previously used to detect neglect-like patterns during functional tasks, such as tea-making57, but these studies required labor-intensive manual annotation of gaze data. Our approach automates the contextualization of gaze data and may facilitate clinical applications of gaze-based spatial bias metrics for diagnostic purposes. Indeed, fixation asymmetries58 and gaze distribution metrics59 have proven sensitive in detecting neglect in stroke patients. By minimizing the burden of manual coding and enhancing ecological validity, this method represents a scalable tool for both research and clinical assessment of spatial attention.

Although the tablet-based free-viewing task provided a useful control, its narrower display (covering only ~ 80% visual angle of the real-world scene) and series of static 10-s images punctuated by fixation crosses stand in stark contrast to the wide, continuous, and interactive meal visual environment. The discrete trial structure of the tablet condition likely amplified bottom-up saliency of the phone. As noted in prior research, visual exploration of a novel visual scene is initially governed by bottom-up processes and gradually shifts toward top-down strategies60. At the onset of each trial, visual exploration was reset to its former stimulus-driven influences, which may explain the comparatively higher proportion of fixation time on the smartphone observed in the tablet condition. Moreover, to effectively guide action-oriented movements, attention is directed towards task-relevant locations within the visual environment40. The affordances of utensils and food, provided visual-motor targets for action61,62, which likely tied gaze behavior more directly to functional demands and may have reduced sensitivity to the distractor.

Body orientation metrics (yaw of head and torso) did not exhibit a lateralization effect. Prior work has demonstrated that asymmetries in head and trunk orientation can reflect spatial biases63,64, particularly in patients with neglect65,66. In our study, the physical layout of the meal eating and free-viewing tasks were not particularly conducive of movement: all objects were within the participant’s immediate field of view and within arm’s reach, limiting the need for bodily reorientation67. Future studies should assess body orientation metrics in larger-scale, more dynamic environments that require movement or locomotion68,69, where attentional shifts may be more strongly coupled to gross motor behaviors and whole-body orientation.

Finally, the meal-eating task offered limited behavioral outcomes, meal duration being the only metric quantified. Although phone presence induced a spatial bias, it did not alter meal duration. Embedding goal-relevant performance metrics in naturalistic tasks (e.g., driving, navigation, or meal preparation) would yield a more complete picture of how attentional biases affect real-world performance70. Additionally, combining wearable methods with mobile neuroimaging could reveal the neural correlates of visual processing in natural environments45. Future studies could also examine how gaze behavior changes over time during naturalistic activities, especially in relation to motor actions. Segmenting periods of active versus passive engagement may provide deeper insight into how visuomotor dynamics shape attention in real-world contexts.

While the use of participants’ own smartphones enhanced ecological validity, the absence of a control condition involving a neutral, non-phone object limits our ability to determine whether the observed attentional bias is specific to the smartphone itself. Although the correlations with habitual phone use suggest that the effect is—at least partly- related to smartphone salience or relevance, future studies are needed to disentangle phone-specific effects from those elicited by other lateral objects with varying degrees of visual or emotional salience.

Despite these limitations, our study demonstrates the feasibility and utility of wearable eye-tracking combined with computer-vision contextualization for capturing subtle spatial attention shifts during everyday behavior. These results validate mobile sensing as a tool for naturalistic attention research and challenge the assumption that laboratory findings always translate directly to real-world contexts71.

By revealing context-sensitive effects of attentional capture, this work reinforces the theoretical importance of studying attention as it unfolds in unconstrained, goal-directed behavior. The methodological innovations introduced here provide a scalable path for integrating real-world attention measures into research and practice. This method holds promise for advancing the study of perception and attention beyond the laboratory, offering broad applicability across various behaviors, environments, and populations, including individuals with developmental (e.g., ADHD) or acquired (e.g., post-stroke) attentional impairments. The sensitivity and ecological validity of these real-world measures may support the development of novel diagnostic and rehabilitative tools in cognitive and clinical neuroscience. Future work taking advantage of these methods will bridge the gap between laboratory research and the complex attentional demands of everyday life behaviors.

Methods

Participants

Twenty-five right-handed individuals took part in this study (14 female; mean age 24.6 years). One dataset was excluded due to a synchronization issue during one of the session, and a replacement participant was recruited to maintain the sample size. All participants had normal or corrected-to-normal vision, no motor impairments, and no reported medical or neurological conditions. They were recruited through an online platform advertising research at the Faculty of Psychology and Educational Sciences of the KU Leuven. Prior to the first session, participants were fully informed about the study protocol and asked to report any dietary restrictions or allergies. The participants provided informed consent and were informed about data usage. The participants were explicitly informed of their right to withdraw from the study at any time without needing to provide justification. Upon completion of the final session, participants received a meal voucher redeemable at the university cafeteria as compensation. This study was carried out in line with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Approval was granted by the Social and Societal Ethics Committee (SMEC) of KU Leuven [Approval number: G-2024-7628-R2(MIN)].

Given the novelty of our paradigm and absence of directly comparable studies to support a formal a priori power analysis, we conducted a post hoc power analysis using the effect size observed in our primary dependent measure (gaze eccentricity) during the meal-eating condition. The observed effect size was large (η2ₚ = 0.64, f ≈ 1.33). Power simulations indicated that a sample size of 13 participants would have been sufficient to detect this effect with 95% power (α = 0.05). Our final sample size of 24 participants therefore provided excellent power (> 0.99) to detect spatial attention biases in this within-subject design.

Experimental design

The aim of this study was to assess the feasibility of capturing measures of spatial bias during everyday activities. Eating a meal was selected as an appropriate scenario because it is a natural, routine behavior occurring daily. Following common spatial attention paradigms, spatial bias was introduced by placing a salient object, the participant’s mobile phone, 20 cm to either the left or right side of a food tray positioned directly in front of them. This design enhanced ecological validity by incorporating a familiar object into a realistic context, thus maximizing the potential for spatial bias due to the high emotional valence associated with personal smartphones72. Indeed, it has become common practice for individuals to keep their smartphones within close proximity at all times, such as during meals, social interactions, or work-related tasks, underscoring their ubiquity50. Several studies have documented that individuals check their phones frequently and often mindlessly throughout the day29. While ongoing research continues to explore the broader impacts of mobile phone omnipresence, in the current study, the phone served primarily as a stimulus to induce spatial attention bias. The lateralized phone conditions (left, right) were compared against a control condition where no phone was present (absent condition).

Visual exploration data collected during the three real-world eating conditions (conducted over three sessions on separate days) were also contextualized using a laboratory-based free-viewing task. In this task, participants viewed static images depicting food trays containing various dishes, with the same experimental manipulation applied (phone on the left, right, or absent). This controlled, non-immersive setting served as a reference point for interpreting gaze behavior observed in the real-world conditions. Contrasting continuous, interactive behavior with the passive viewing of static stimuli allowed for the exploration of how visual attention may differ between naturalistic and artificial contexts.

Protocol

Upon arrival, participants were greeted by the experimenter, provided with written briefing information about the study, and asked to fill out consent forms. While participants reviewed these documents, the experimenter brought a plate of spaghetti pre-ordered from the university eatery. Spaghetti was selected for this study due to its visual homogeneity (i.e., lack of distinct segments) and compatibility with most dietary preferences.

After providing informed consent, participants reported the time elapsed since their last meal and rated their subjective hunger on a 1-to-10 Likert scale. They were then fitted with eye-tracking glasses and instructed on how to place a motion-sensing belt at the infrasternal angle (inferior end of the sternum). Correct belt placement was verified by the experimenter. Exactly 250 g of cooked spaghetti (either vegan or meat-based sauce included) were weighed and served to ensure consistency across sessions.

Participants were seated in an ergonomic chair, adjusted for comfort and to maintain a consistent viewing distance (~ 50 cm) from their eyes to the center of the plate (or tablet in the tablet session). The eye-tracker was calibrated using a one-point calibration procedure, during which participants fixated on a target placed slightly below eye level at a distance of 70 cm. This calibration point was consistent with the visual angle and depth experienced while looking down at the tray during a meal.

Depending on the experimental condition during the meal-eating sessions, participants’ phones were either placed behind them on a separate table (absent condition) or positioned face-up 15 cm to the left or right of the food tray. Phone placement locations were standardized using discreet pencil marks on the table surface. Participants were instructed to activate flight mode and disable notifications for the duration of the meal. The experimenter verified that eye-tracking and motion sensor data were properly streamed via a third-party synchronization platform (Lab Streaming Layer,73).

For the tablet session, participants were instructed to freely explore images presented on a tablet. During meal sessions, participants received no specific instructions other than informing the experimenter when they finished eating. Throughout all sessions, the experimenter sat facing away from participants at the opposite end of the room, and ambient restaurant noise was played in the background to enhance immersion and reduce participants’ self-consciousness. On average, participants finished eating in 5 min and 16 s (range: 3′37′′–8′06′′).

Due to the nature of the meal task, each participant completed three sessions, scheduled on separate days within a maximum interval of one week between sessions, conducted between 11:30 AM and 1:30 PM. Session order was counterbalanced across participants. The free-viewing tablet task was consistently scheduled as a fourth, final session to prevent repetitive exposure to food tray stimuli and mobile phone-related questionnaires from influencing subsequent meal sessions. Each session concluded with a debriefing and scheduling of the subsequent session.

Materials

Eye-tracking

Eye-tracking

Participants’ gaze was recorded using Tobii Pro Glasses 3 eye-tracking glasses (Tobii, Stockholm, Sweden). The glasses were fitted individually to ensure participant comfort by adjusting the nose pads and were secured with soft lanyards. Participants sat in ergonomic chairs adjusted for consistent viewing distances (~ 50 cm) from their eyes to the center of the plate or tablet, depending on the session. The eye-tracking system used four sensors (two per eye) employing corneal reflection, dark pupil, and stereo geometry approaches to track eye movements at a 100 Hz sampling rate. Additionally, integrated gyroscope and accelerometer sensors recorded head movements at 100 Hz. Both gaze and head motion data were streamed through the Lab Streaming Layer (LSL) synchronization framework73. The glasses also featured a front-facing camera recording first-person perspective footage at 25 frames per second (resolution: 1920 × 1080 pixels, field of view: 95° horizontal/63° vertical). These recordings were stored internally alongside gaze and head motion data, as the video stream was not compatible with the LSL framework.

2.Motion sensing

Motion sensing

A wearable motion-sensing module (EmotiBit, OpenBCI, New York) incorporating a 16-bit Inertial Measurement Unit (BMI160, Bosch) and a digital geomagnetic sensor (BMM150, Bosch) was used to record torso movements. The module was securely strapped at the infrasternal angle (inferior end of the sternum) using an extensible elastic belt (see Fig. 1A). Accelerometers, gyroscopes, and magnetometers within the module measured linear acceleration, angular velocity, and magnetic field strength respectively, across three axes (X, Y, Z) at 25 Hz. Motion data were streamed wirelessly to the LSL synchronization platform. The gyroscope data along the X-axis were specifically used to derive torso yaw (sideways rotation) movements. Similarly, head yaw rotations were obtained from the eye-tracking glasses’ integrated gyroscope sensors at 100 Hz.

3.Free-viewing tablet paradigm

Free-viewing tablet paradigm

A series of food tray photographs was presented on a 21.5-inch tablet (Cintiq 22, Wacom, Saitama, Japan). The stimuli comprised 12 unique dishes centrally positioned on food trays surrounded by standard utensils (fork, knife, spoon), a glass of water, and a pepper grinder. Dishes were selected based on visual symmetry and uniformity (e.g., soup, rice salad). Each dish was photographed in three conditions: mobile phone positioned on the tray’s left side, right side, or absent. Additionally, each photograph had a horizontally flipped counterpart to control for object lateralization, resulting in a total of 72 unique stimuli (see Fig. 4). The stimulus presentation was implemented using PsychoPy74. The code and stimuli are available via an open-access repository [https://github.com/sladouce/Spaghetti_Eye-tracking]. Each stimulus was preceded by a fixation cross presented at the screen center for one second, followed by the stimulus itself displayed for ten seconds. Stimulus presentation software transmitted timestamped event markers through the LSL system at stimulus onset and fixation cross appearance, labeling phone position conditions (left, absent, right) and image orientation (original or flipped). These markers facilitated the analysis of gaze and body movement data related to different phone positions and the influence of proximal objects (utensils, grinder, glass) on visual exploration. The presentation order of the stimuli was randomized separately for each participant at the start of the task, and an output file logging the individualized sequence and questionnaire responses was saved at the end of the free-viewing task session. To reduce peripheral distractions, tablet edges and supporting surfaces were covered with a white cloth. Following the free-viewing task, participants completed the computerized Problematic Mobile Phone Use (PUMP) scale75 and reported their daily average phone usage based on screen-time summaries.

(A) Each stimulus consists of a food tray upon which a plate, a glass of water, a pepper grinder, and cutlery are placed. Moreover, each plate was photographed with a phone on the left or right of the plate, or absent. Each of these three photographs were flipped horizontally to control for lateralization of objects surrounding the plate. (B) The aforementioned six stimuli were produced with 12 unique plates. 4.Problematic Mobile Phone Use (PUMP) scale.

Problematic Mobile Phone Use (PUMP) scale.

The PUMP is a 20-item questionnaire assessing problematic mobile phone use, validated for internal consistency and convergent validity75. Items reflect DSM-5 substance use disorder criteria, rated on a 5-point Likert scale (1 to 5). Total scores range from 20 to 100, where higher scores indicate more problematic phone use. Typically, scores below 50 suggest mild usage, while scores above 70 indicate excessive and potentially problematic use76.

Data processing

Multimodal data alignment

Multimodal data alignment

The data streams from the eye-tracking glasses (gaze and head motion) and the motion-sensing belt (torso motion) were synchronized using the Lab Streaming Layer (LSL,73) framework and saved into an Extensible Data Format (.xdf) file. The scene video recordings were stored internally on the eye-tracking system, along with synchronized gaze and head motion data. Because scene video could not be streamed via LSL, the recordings from the eye-tracking system (including gaze, head motion, and scene video) and all other data streams had to be initiated sequentially by the experimenter. This manual initiation introduced an initial offset, compounded by an additional arbitrary delay arising from the startup of the multi-stream LSL recording process. As a result, the synchronized multimodal data stored in .xdf files were shorter and temporally offset relative to the data captured directly by the eye-tracking system.

To align these multimodal data streams accurately, we first zero-padded the onset of the LSL-based gaze data to match the length of the internally stored gaze data. We then computed a cross-correlation between these two gaze signals (using relative x-axis gaze positions) to identify the lag associated with the highest correlation, indicating optimal alignment. This identified lag was used to shift the LSL-based data accordingly. If, following realignment, the adjusted signal began after time zero, additional zero-padding was applied to the onset of the LSL data. Similarly, if the realigned data length differed from the internally stored template data, zero-padding was added to the end of the data stream. While this zero-padding ensured precise temporal alignment across data streams, it had no practical consequences for the extraction of gaze and body motion features. Indeed, the zero-padding only affected brief periods at the start or end of recordings, when the task had either not yet begun or had already concluded.

2.Segmentation of gaze events through REMoDNAV

Segmentation of gaze events through REMoDNAV

Gaze data were segmented into fixation and saccade events using the velocity-based REMoDNaV algorithm77. In order to relate the scale of gaze dynamics mapped onto the scene recordings to actual eye displacement in the real-world, a visual angle conversion factor of 0.0587 degrees per pixel was calculated. This pixel to visual angle degrees conversion considered the eye-tracker’s field of view (120 cm), the viewing distance (50 cm), and the horizontal resolution of the scene recording (1920 pixels), as implemented in the formula below.

Continuous gaze data sampled at 100 Hz were smoothed using a Savitzky-Golay filter with a 0.05-s window. Default parameters for REMoDNaV were otherwise applied. An output file detailing event types, onset times, and durations was generated.

3.Gaze data contextualization through computer vision

Gaze data contextualization through computer vision

The first-person view video recordings were processed frame-by-frame to detect objects using a YOLOv8 model implemented through the Ultralytics Python library. A custom-trained model was specifically developed for this study. Training data comprised 150 annotated images, including 36 images used in the tablet-based free-viewing task and 114 screenshots extracted from pre-test video recordings at various time points. Each image was manually annotated by the experimenter, indicating the presence and location of five objects of interest (phone, plate, glass, grinder, and target) using bounding boxes. The dataset used for model training is publicly available at https://github.com/sladouce/Spaghetti_Eye-tracking]. The training images were extracted from pre-tests sessions with the experimenter as subject and were therefore not included in the dataset used in the present study.

The YOLOv8 model was trained for 300 epochs using a batch size of 16 within the Ultralytics framework. The training utilized the default optimizer, with initial and final learning rates set to 0.01, momentum at 0.937, and weight decay at 0.0005. A learning rate warm-up was applied during the first three epochs. Loss function coefficients were adjusted to 7.5 for bounding box regression, 0.5 for object classification, and 1.5 for distribution focal loss. Model validation was performed on a held-out subset after each epoch, with detailed performance metrics provided in Fig. 5.

For each video frame, gaze coordinates were cross-referenced with detected bounding boxes to determine whether gaze points intersected with identified object regions. The resulting annotations indicating object-specific gaze intersections for each frame were recorded and saved as CSV files for subsequent analysis.

4.Extraction of gaze eccentricity measure

Extraction of gaze eccentricity measure

A gaze eccentricity measure was computed as the horizontal (x-axis) distance between the continuous gaze coordinates and the center of the plate at each time point. The plate center, detected frame-by-frame via computer vision, served as a central reference point, as it was consistently positioned directly in front of the participants with other objects symmetrically arranged around it. Because the gaze data sampling rate (100 Hz) exceeded the frame rate of the scene recordings (25 fps), each gaze point was matched to the most recent available plate position.

The distribution of gaze eccentricity was calculated across the entire meal for each dataset. In the meal-eating conditions, 99.9% of gaze samples (SD = 0.01) fell within ± 500 eccentricity units, corresponding to approximately ± 47.5 degrees of visual angle around the plate center. For the tablet condition, all gaze samples across datasets similarly fell within this ± 47.5-degree range.

The left and right fields extended from − 47.5° to − 10° and from 10° to 47.5°, respectively. For the tablet condition, where the plate appeared 20% smaller, the central field limits were adjusted to ± 8 degrees accordingly. For each condition (phone left, phone right, phone absent), gaze eccentricity distributions were computed by aggregating gaze samples across all corresponding trials within that condition.

To assess the effect of phone position on spatial attention, gaze eccentricity distributions from both the meal-eating and tablet conditions were binned into left, central, and right fields. A 3 × 3 repeated-measures ANOVA was conducted with phone position (left, absent, right) and eccentricity bin (left, central, right) as within-subject factors. This allowed us to compare how gaze distribution varied with phone placement in both real-world and laboratory-like settings.

5.Extraction of object-related gaze features

Extraction of object-related gaze features

Following segmentation of the continuous gaze data into fixation and saccade events, and object detection in the scene video via computer vision, each fixation was assigned to a specific object of interest—plate, glass, grinder, or phone—if the gaze coordinates fell within the corresponding bounding box. During the meal-eating condition, participants were free to interact with the environment, often moving objects such as bringing the glass to their mouth while gazing toward the phone. When gaze coordinates overlapped with multiple object bounding boxes, a priority rule was applied to resolve ambiguity, assigning fixations in the following order: phone > glass > grinder > plate. Fixations that did not fall within any object bounding box were labelled as background.

To account for variability in meal duration across participants, object-related fixation metrics (total fixation time and number of fixations) were normalized by dividing each by the meal duration. From these normalized metrics, we extracted the proportion of time spent fixating on each object and the number of fixations per minute.

For the tablet-based free-viewing condition, average object-related gaze features were computed for each phone position (left, absent, right) for the phone, plate, and background categories. For lateral objects—the glass and the grinder—fixation features were analyzed based on their position relative to the phone (same side, opposite side, or phone absent) to assess the effect of phone proximity on visual attention.

To investigate how phone position influenced visual exploration of different environmental elements, we compared the proportion of fixation time allocated to each object across phone position conditions. The visual scene consistently included four primary objects of interest: plate, phone, glass, and grinder. Fixations outside these areas (e.g., on the table, wall, or tray) were categorized as background. Although we also extracted the number of fixations per minute, this measure showed partial multicollinearity with fixation duration and yielded similar results. Therefore, only the proportion of fixation time is reported in the main text; full analyses of fixation count metrics are provided in the Supplementary Materials.

6.Extraction of head and torso motion features

Extraction of head and torso motion features

Inertial measurement unit (IMU) data from the torso and head sensors were processed to extract and compare yaw rotation signals across phone position conditions. Baseline correction was subsequently applied by subtracting the median yaw value recorded during the first three seconds of the trial which was a period during which participants were sitting still and fixated the central target right in front of them. The resulting time series represented the continuous head and torso yaw orientation throughout the meal.

Orientation distributions were then computed by binning the yaw angles into 100 bins spanning from − 50° to 50°. As in the gaze eccentricity analysis, these distributions were grouped into three lateral visual fields: left (− 50° to − 10°), central (− 10° to 10°), and right (10° to 50°). Due to the static nature of the free-viewing tablet task, in which participants remained seated with minimal movement, head and torso orientation data were analyzed only for the meal-eating condition.

Statistical analyses

A series of repeated-measures analyses of variance (ANOVAs) were conducted to examine the effects of phone position (left, absent, right) on gaze eccentricity, object-related visual exploration, head and torso orientation, and behavioral measures such as meal duration. However, the analysis of the proportion of time the phone was fixated was restricted to the left and right phone position conditions. In the absent condition, the phone was not present in the visual scene, resulting in uniformly zero values. This lack of variance violates ANOVA assumptions and would distort sphericity estimates. Therefore, a paired-samples t-test was conducted for this specific measure. Relationships between gaze features, behavioral metrics, and questionnaire scores were assessed using regression analyses. To control for multiple comparisons, Holm corrections were applied to all post hoc paired-sample t-tests used to follow up on significant main effects and interactions observed in the repeated-measures ANOVAs.
