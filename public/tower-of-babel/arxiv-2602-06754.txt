A Unified Framework for LLM Watermarks
Thibaud Gloaguen, Robin Staab, Nikola Jovanović, Martin Vechev

Abstract
LLM watermarks allow tracing AI-generated texts by inserting a detectable signal into their generated content. Recent works have proposed a wide range of watermarking algorithms, each with distinct designs, usually built using a bottom-up approach. Crucially, there is no general and principled formulation for LLM watermarking. In this work, we show that most existing and widely used watermarking schemes can in fact be derived from a principled constrained optimization problem. Our formulation unifies existing watermarking methods and explicitly reveals the constraints that each method optimizes. In particular, it highlights an understudied quality-diversity-power trade-off. At the same time, our framework also provides a principled approach for designing novel watermarking schemes tailored to specific requirements. For instance, it allows us to directly use perplexity as a proxy for quality, and derive new schemes that are optimal with respect to this constraint. Our experimental evaluation validates our framework: watermarking schemes derived from a given constraint consistently maximize detection power with respect to that constraint.

Introduction

Large Language Model (LLM) watermarking algorithms modify the sampling procedure of the underlying LLM to introduce a detectable signal in the generated text
[
18
]
.
Importantly, prior watermarking works have proposed a range of seemingly unrelated approaches
[
18
,
8
,
1
,
20
,
6
]
for how to modify the sampling procedure.
Usually, given a specific motivation (e.g., distortion-freeness or power), they first propose a new watermark design, prove that it satisfies some (theoretical) properties, and experimentally validate it.
As a result, it becomes difficult to compare existing watermarking schemes at a fundamental level, relate them to one another, or design new methods in a principled manner.

This work:

In this work, we address this issue by introducing a new unified framework for LLM watermarking.
In particular, we show that the construction of a watermarking algorithm can be phrased as a constrained optimization problem: the goal is to maximize the detectability of the inserted watermark signal, while limiting its impact on the quality of the generated text.
By formalizing and analyzing this optimization problem, we find that a number of popular schemes (e.g., Red-Green watermarks, AAR/KTH, SynthID) can be formulated as solutions to our optimization problem when instantiated with specific constraints.

Importantly, the explicit nature of the constraints in our framework further allows us to better understand and relate existing schemes and provides a principled approach to the design of new, more powerful ones.
As we show in our empirical evaluation, our framework provides a natural and practical way to design and instantiate powerful schemes tailored to specific constraints such as quality preservation.
Lastly, our framework allows us to theoretically identify and experimentally validate a previously understudied fundamental trade-off in LLM watermarks: quality-diversity-power, i.e., the trade-off between the quality of the watermarked text, the output diversity under the watermarking scheme, and the overall watermarking signal strength.

Background and Related Work

We now introduce the necessary background on LLM watermarks, including all relevant related work.
We defer specific details of each watermarking scheme to
Appendix

B
.

LLM Watermarks

The goal of LLM watermarks is to insert a detectable signal in the text generated by the LLM.
At each generation step, watermarks use a hash of the previously generated context to seed a pseudorandom number generator and attribute a pseudorandom score to each token in the vocabulary.
We call this the
hashing
mechanism. Using those sampled pseudorandom scores, the
sampling mechanism
modifies the original next-token probability distribution provided by the model into a watermarked next-token probability distribution.
The next token is then sampled according to the watermarked distribution.
Then, given a text, the
watermark detector
reconstructs the pseudorandom score for each token in the text and computes a p-value.
The p-value represents the probability of observing such a result or a more extreme one under the assumption that the sequence was sampled independently of the scores.
In other words, if the text is human-generated, the respective scores are random and the p-value will be high: the text is not watermarked.
By contrast, it is statistically unlikely that many tokens have consistently high scores, allowing us to deduce that such texts are watermarked.

Importantly, the detector should be
model-free
, i.e., computing the p-value should not require access to the model and should be computationally efficient.
Indeed, most practical scenarios assume that detection is run at internet scale, where computational efficiency is required.

Watermark Sampling Mechanisms

Prior works have proposed a variety of sampling mechanisms, trying to balance power with impact on quality.
Popular approaches include
Kirchenbauer et al. 2023
, which biases the logits of the next-token distribution according to binary scores,
Dathathri et al. 2024
leverages tournament sampling to favor higher scoring tokens,
Aaronson 2023
deterministically samples the highest scoring token according to the logarithm of the next-token probability distribution and a Gumbel score,
Kuditipudi et al. 2024
,
Christ et al. 2024
use inverse-transform sampling, and
Wu et al. 2023
,
Chen et al. 2025
reweight the next-token distribution according to the scores’ ranking.
While those sampling mechanisms are seemingly unrelated, our work shows that most of them can be derived from a unified family of constrained optimization problems (
Section

4.2
).

Optimal Watermark Design

Several prior works have attempted to design LLM watermarks, or some of their components, from a theoretical perspective.
For the hashing mechanism,
Hou et al. 2024
,
Ren et al. 2024
,
Kodama et al. 2025
propose using a semantic-aware hash to increase the watermark detectability after paraphrasing, and
Zhou et al. 2024
,
Shen et al.
improve the resilience of the hashing mechanism against spoofing attacks.

Huang et al. 2023
,
Li et al. 2025b
,
Li et al. 2025a
study the watermark detector to find the shape of the optimal detector with respect to the test power.

More closely related to our work,
Wouters 2023
,
Tsur et al. 2025b
study the sampling mechanism that consists of an exponential tilting of the next-token probability distribution with binary scores (a similar setting to
Kirchenbauer et al. 2023
) and find a closed-form formula to compute the watermarked distribution that maximizes power under a log-likelihood constraint.

Gloaguen et al. 2025b
frames Red-Green watermarks as a constrained optimization problem to derive a watermarking rule for diffusion language models.

Tsur et al. 2025a
frame watermarking as an optimal transport problem, from which they derive distortion-free schemes that deterministically (according to the pseudorandom scores and
p
) sample each token, recovering the watermarking scheme from
Aaronson 2023
.
Lastly,
He et al. 2024
jointly optimize the detector and the sampling mechanism, yielding a powerful yet non-robust watermark.
Based on their theoretical insights, they instantiate a distortion-free scheme that introduces dependence between the score and the next-token distribution.

Unlike our approach, existing theoretical frameworks focus solely on finding optimal components for specific LLM watermarks and therefore are not capable of capturing and extending a wide range of existing watermarking schemes.

Setting

We recall the main components of watermarks (
Figure

1
, left): the hashing mechanism to compute pseudorandom scores, the sampling mechanism that, given the next-token probability distribution, computes a watermarked probability distribution, and the detector that, given a text, assesses whether it is watermarked.
Our goal is to design a principled sampling mechanism, given a fixed hashing mechanism and a fixed detector. Let
\Sigma
be the finite vocabulary,
p\in\Delta(\Sigma)
be the next-token probability distribution,
G
the random variable representing the pseudorandom scores with
\mathbb{G}\subset\mathbb{R}^{|\Sigma|}
as support, and
g\sim G
a sample from
G
computed from the context via the hashing mechanism.
At every generation step, we want to find a watermarked probability distribution
q(g)
such that later, without knowledge of
p
, we can distinguish whether a sequence of tokens was sampled from
p
or
q(g)
.

Optimization Objective

A priori, finding such a distribution
q(g)
is a complex task.
If our detection can depend on
p
and
q(g)
, the Neyman–Pearson lemma provides a formula for the best possible detector.
Hence, finding
q(g)
can be simplified to maximizing the power of this optimal detector.
However, in practice, we want model-free detection (
Section

2
), i.e., the detector should depend on neither
p
nor
q(g)
.
From
He et al. 2024
, we know that an optimal model-free detector is intractable.
In the literature, popular schemes often use the sum of individual token scores as their detection statistic.
This suggests that, at each generation step, to maximize the power of the detector one should sample the next-token with the highest possible score.
In our notation, this corresponds to solving

q^{*}(g)=\argmax_{q\in\Delta(\Sigma)}\mathbb{E}_{\Omega\sim q}[g_{\Omega}]\coloneq\argmax_{q\in\Delta(\Sigma)}g\cdot q.

(1)

Solving
Equation

1
for all
g\in\mathbb{G}
is then equivalent
to solving for all
q:\mathbb{G}\rightarrow\Delta(\Sigma)
,

q^{*}=\argmax_{q:\mathbb{G}\rightarrow\Delta(\Sigma)}\mathbb{E}_{G}[G\cdot q(G)].

(2)

Adding Constraints

Yet, purely optimizing
Equation

2
gives a trivial solution: for all
g
,
q^{*}(g)
corresponds to greedy sampling according to the scores
g
.
This is clearly undesirable, as such a watermark would ignore and thus heavily distort the original next-token distribution
p
, leading to low-quality generations.
We therefore introduce a constraint to
Equation

2
that controls how distortionary the watermark can be.
With this, our watermark framework becomes

\displaystyle q^{*}=

\displaystyle\argmax_{q:\mathbb{G}\rightarrow\Delta(\Sigma)}\quad\mathbb{E}_{G}[G\cdot q(G)]

(3)

\displaystyle\text{s.t. }\forall g\in\mathbb{G},\quad D\left(q(g),\mathbb{E}_{G}[q(G)],p\right)\leq\varepsilon.

The constraint
D\colon\Delta(\Sigma)^{3}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
allows to capture both distortion given a fixed
g
through
q(g)
, and distortions in expectation through
\mathbb{E}_{G}[q(G)]
.
For instance, we can choose as
D
a KL constraint
\text{KL}(q(g)||p)
or a distortion-free constraint
\text{KL}(\mathbb{E}_{G}[q(G)]||p)
.
With our framework, as we show in
Section

4
, designing a watermarking sampling mechanism now becomes equivalent to choosing a meaningful metric
D
and then solving
Equation

3
.

Hard and Soft Constraints

We define
D
such that it depends on both
q(g)
and
\mathbb{E}_{G}[q(G)]
.
We consider imposing a constraint on
q(g)
as a
hard constraint
, as the watermark cannot significantly distort the probability distribution given a fixed
g
.
Likewise, imposing a constraint on
\mathbb{E}_{G}[q(G)]
is a
soft constraint
.
A single instantiation of
q(g)
can heavily distort the distribution, but on average, the constraint holds.
This means that, in practice, there are two different kinds of distortions when it comes to watermarking.
We find in
Section

5.3
that soft-constraint watermarks are more powerful and better preserve quality, but exhibit very low diversity.
In particular, we show in
Theorem

D.5
that (optimal) watermarks with only soft constraints are necessarily deterministic (i.e., given a fixed prompt, the watermarked LLM always samples the same text).
In contrast, hard constraints tend to better preserve diversity at the cost of quality.
Interestingly, although the separation of hard and soft constraints is not strictly necessary in our framework, we found that all existing watermarking schemes from
Section

4.1
can be expressed using only one of the two.
This explicit separation provides a novel and more principled perspective on the watermark quality-diversity-power trade-off.

Penalized Formulation

In practice, solving the constrained optimization problem from
Equation

3
at each generation step can prove to be too costly.
Hence, for practical reasons, most schemes from
Section

4
actually correspond to the penalized version of the optimization problem,

\displaystyle\argmax_{q:\mathbb{G}\rightarrow\Delta(\Sigma)}\mathbb{E}_{G}\!\left[G\!\cdot\!q(G)-\frac{1}{\delta}D\left(q(G),\mathbb{E}_{G}[q(G)],p\right)\right]\!.

(4)

With the penalized version, assuming the existence of an interior point, the shape of the optimal solution
q^{*}
is the same as the constrained one: for all
\varepsilon>0
, there exists
\delta\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
such that
Equation

3
and
Equation

4
have the same solution.
Hence, the penalized version corresponds to parametrizing the scheme with
\delta
instead of
\varepsilon
.
For clarity and consistency, in
Section

4
when we instantiate a watermark sampling mechanism, we parametrize it by
\varepsilon
if we solve the constrained formulation (
Equation

3
) in practice, and by
\delta
if we solve the penalized formulation (
Equation

4
).

Capturing Existing Watermarking Sampling Mechanisms

Red-Green Watermarks

One of the most prominent schemes in the literature is the Red-Green watermark (KGW) from
Kirchenbauer et al. 2023
.
At each step
t
, the Red-Green watermark pseudo-randomly partitions the vocabulary into a set of
\gamma|\Sigma|
green tokens and the red tokens.
Then, it adds a constant
\delta>0
to the logits of tokens in the green set, effectively boosting the probability of green tokens.
The detector thresholds the number of green tokens in a text to determine whether it is watermarked.

To capture Red-Green watermarks, we let the entries of
G
be correlated Bernoulli random variables, ensuring it has exactly
\gamma|\Sigma|
ones.
Then, we use the following constraint,

\forall g\in\mathbb{G},\,D_{\text{R-G}}(q(g),\mathbb{E}_{G}[q(G)],p):=\text{KL}(q(g)||p).

(5)

Given
\delta>0
as the penalization constant, we show in
Theorem

D.1
that the solution to our optimization problem from
Equation

4
is given by

\forall g\in\mathbb{G},\,q_{\text{R-G}}(g)\propto p\exp(\delta g).

(6)

In the logit space, we have, up to an additive constant,

\forall g\in\mathbb{G},\,\log q_{\text{R-G}}(g)=\log p+\delta g.

(7)

This expression exactly matches the Red-Green watermark algorithm described above: if a token
u\in\Sigma
is green (
g_{u}=1
), we add
\delta
to its logit.
Hence, we have shown that Red-Green watermarks can be naturally derived from our framework as solutions to
Equation

4
using a (hard) KL constraint, and we find in practice (
Section

5.1
) that Red-Green watermarks are indeed Pareto-optimal with respect to the detectability–KL constraint trade-off.

At the same time, because Red-Green watermarks only use a hard constraint, i.e., the constraint depends only on
q(g)
and not on the distribution of
G
.
Hence, as we show in
Theorem

D.1
,
Equation

6
is the solution of
Equation

4
with the constraints from
Equation

5
for any distribution of
G
.
This allows defining new watermarking schemes that we evaluate in
Section

C.2
.

AAR and KTH Watermarks

Both
Aaronson 2023
and
Kuditipudi et al. 2024
(EXP-edit) used the Gumbel-max trick to build a distortion-free watermark.
They associate with each token i.i.d. Gumbel scores and sample the next token as the argmax of the Gumbel scores and the token probability.
In expectation over the Gumbel scores, such sampling is distributed exactly as the original probability.

To capture AAR, we use a soft-constraint with the entries of
G
being i.i.d. Gumbel random variables, for all
g\in\mathbb{G}
,

D_{\text{AAR}}(q(g),\mathbb{E}_{G}[q(G)],p):=\text{KL}(\mathbb{E}_{G}[q(G)]||p).

(8)

Given
\varepsilon=0
(i.e., enforcing distortion-freeness),
Theorem

D.3
gives us the following solution

\forall u\in\Sigma,q_{\text{AAR}}(g)_{u}:=\mathbbm{1}\{u=\argmax_{v\in\Sigma}(g_{v}+\log p_{v})\}.

(9)

This is exactly the AAR/KTH watermark, which our framework recovers when enforcing the distortion-free constraint with Gumbel scores
G
.
This also shows that, when using Gumbel scores with sum-based detectors, AAR/KTH are the most powerful distortion-free schemes.

Importantly, with
Equation

8
and
\varepsilon>0
, our framework extends AAR/KTH to a (more powerful) distortionary variant.
Indeed, we show in
Theorem

D.3
that, when using
D_{\text{AAR}}
, we obtain the following sampling mechanism:

q_{\text{AAR}}(g)_{u}:=\mathbbm{1}\left\{u=\argmax_{v\in\Sigma}\left(g_{v}+\frac{1}{1+\delta}\log p_{v}\right)\right\},

(10)

where
u\in\Sigma
and
\delta\geq 0
.
We evaluate this extension in
Section

5
, and find it to be a powerful distortionary scheme.

Our framework also generalizes the AAR/KTH mechanism to any distribution of
G
.
For instance, when using a log-normal distribution for
G
, it directly corresponds to the HeavyWater scheme
[
25
]
, originally derived using optimal transport.
Indeed, in
Theorem

D.3
, we find that the resulting sampling mechanisms for any
G
are always pseudo-deterministic but lack a closed form: they require solving
|\Sigma|
equations that depend on the distribution of
G
.
With an equality constraint (
\varepsilon=0
), this is exactly the optimal transport problem introduced in
Tsur et al. 2025a
.
Hence, setting
\varepsilon\geq 0
also extends the schemes from
Tsur et al. 2025a
with a distortionary version.

SynthID

Another prominent watermarking scheme is SynthID
[
8
]
, which uses
m
-layer tournament sampling as a watermark sampling mechanism.

To capture the base
m=1
layer tournament, we use i.i.d. Bernoulli scores
G
and a
\chi^{2}
distance as a constraint,

D_{\chi^{2}}(q(g),\mathbb{E}_{G}[q(G)],p):=\sum_{u\in\Sigma}\frac{\left(q(g)_{u}-p_{u}\right)^{2}}{p_{u}}.

(11)

Given
\varepsilon
small enough,
Theorem

D.8
shows that the solution is given by

q_{\text{1 layer}}(g):=p(1+(g-p\cdot g)).

(12)

This corresponds to the vectorized 1-layer tournament sampling formula derived in
Dathathri et al. 2024
.
To capture the
m
-layer tournament, we show in
Theorem

D.8
that it directly corresponds to chaining the optimization problem
m
times, each time using the
\chi^{2}
constraint with respect to the previous solution.
This formulation intuitively offers a new perspective on why increasing the number of tournament layers
m
improves the watermark strength: at each layer of the tournament, we reapply the
\chi^{2}
watermark.

\chi^{2}
Watermark

With
Equation

11
, we can also extend the 1-layer tournament beyond the SynthID formulation.
In particular, with arbitrary
\varepsilon\geq 0
and given any
g\in\mathbb{G}
, we find in
Theorem

D.6
that the sampling mechanism corresponding to the
\chi^{2}
constraint is given by

\forall u\in\Sigma,q^{*}(g)_{u}=p_{u}[1+\delta(g_{u}+\mu)]_{+}

(13)

where
\mu\in\mathbb{R}
is chosen to satisfy the simplex constraint,
\delta\geq 0
, and
[\,\cdot\,]_{+}
is the positive part function.
This offers a new approach to distortionary 1-layer tournament sampling.
In SynthID, greater power can be obtained at the cost of increased distortion by increasing the number of candidates per tournament.
However, this only allows power to be increased in discrete steps.
By contrast, in
Equation

13
, increasing
\delta
increases power while trading off distortion in a continuous manner.
We evaluate this scheme using binomial scores in
Section

5
.

4.2
Designing Optimal Watermarking Schemes Given Specific Constraints

Next we show how to design optimal watermarking sampling mechanisms using our proposed framework with respect to specific constraints.
As long as computing the solution to
Equation

3
is efficient, one can choose any constraints on the simplex as
D
, e.g., the total variation distance or the Hellinger distance.
To highlight the practicality of our framework, we propose two new schemes, one hard- and one soft-constrained, where the constraints specifically target the watermarked text quality.
We find in
Section

5.2
that those new schemes indeed have a superior detectability-quality trade-off than prior works.

Perplexity As a Distance

The per-token perplexity (PPL) is defined as the exponential of the information entropy.
It measures how surprising it is that a given token has been sampled.
The perplexity of a sequence of tokens is the average of the individual token perplexities.
In the broader NLP literature, and in the watermarking field in particular, it has been used as a proxy to measure the quality impact of a watermark
[
18
,
8
]
.
Notably, our framework allows us to directly use the perplexity (measured according to the original model probability
p
) as a constraint when designing watermarking sampling mechanisms.
Specifically, we introduce both a hard-constraint and a soft-constraint variant, for all
g\in\mathbb{G}
,

\displaystyle D_{\text{Hard PPL}}:=(p-q(g))\cdot\log p

(14)

\displaystyle D_{\text{Soft PPL}}:=(p-\mathbb{E}_{G}[q(G)])\cdot\log p.

(15)

Hard PPL Constraint

Given
\varepsilon\geq 0
, finding the watermark sampling mechanism using the hard PPL constraint is equivalent to solving the following optimization problem

\displaystyle q^{*}=

\displaystyle\argmax_{q:\mathbb{G}\rightarrow\Delta(\Sigma)}\quad\mathbb{E}_{G}[G\cdot q(G)]

(16)

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}\,\forall g\in\mathbb{G},D_{\text{Hard PPL}}\left(q(g),\mathbb{E}_{G}[q(G)],p\right)\leq\varepsilon.

This is a linear programming problem that, while no closed-form solution exists, can be solved efficiently using standard solvers.
In particular, given
g\in\mathbb{G}
, the optimal distribution
q^{*}(g)
is almost deterministic: the optimal solution is the barycenter of two Dirac distributions and hence of support at most
2
.
This result is intuitive: if one wants to minimize the PPL of a sequence of tokens, one simply has to do greedy sampling.
An intuitive way to solve the optimization problem in
Equation

16
is to order the tokens according to their probability
p
.
There exists a rank
r>0
such that deterministically sampling any token below this rank satisfies the constraint.
If the maximum of
G
is reached in this subset, we sample this token.
Otherwise, find the best combination of a token below and a token above the threshold that maximizes the power.
We evaluate this scheme in
Section

5
, and find that it is a powerful scheme with high diversity.

Soft PPL Constraint

With the soft PPL constraint, we show in
Theorem

D.5
that, because we are only constraining
\mathbb{E}_{G}[q(G)]
, the solution
q^{*}(G)
is a Dirac distribution.
Specifically, we show in
Theorem

D.9
that we have

\exists\lambda\!\in\mathbb{R},\forall u\!\in\Sigma,q^{*}\!(g)_{u}=\!\argmax_{v\in\Sigma}(g_{v}\!+\!\lambda(\varepsilon)\log p_{v}),

(17)

where
\lambda(\varepsilon)
is the unique solution to
\mathbb{E}_{G}[q^{*}(G)\cdot\log p]=p\cdot\log p-\varepsilon
.
Interestingly, when
G
is composed of i.i.d. Gumbel random variables and
\varepsilon=0
, then
\lambda(0)=1
, which is exactly the AAR/KTH sampling mechanism.
While in general, the feasible set of distortion-free constrained watermarks is contained in the set of soft PPL constrained watermarks, with i.i.d. Gumbel random variables the solution coincides.
For other distributions, however, there is no closed-form solution for
\lambda(\varepsilon)
, requiring a root-finding algorithm to approximate
\lambda(\varepsilon)
.
While this makes the watermark itself more expensive, our framework remains consistent: Our evaluation in
Section

5
shows that this scheme outperforms all other schemes in the detectability-quality trade-off.

5.3
Impact on Diversity

Next, we investigate how different watermarking methods affect output diversity.
Although prior work
[
13
]
suggested that watermarks, especially distortion-free schemes, may reduce diversity, this effect has not been rigorously evaluated.
We propose a new experimental protocol to quantify the impact of watermarking on LLM output diversity.
Our results show that soft-constrained watermarks substantially reduce diversity, whereas hard-constrained watermarks are able to retain it to a larger extent.

Measuring Watermark Diversity

As in
Section

5.2
, we use
Llama3.1-8B
with temperature
0.7
.
The effect of a watermark on diversity, as noted in
Gloaguen et al. 2025a
, is to reduce the number of unique outputs given a fixed prompt.
This means that, in order to measure the watermark’s impact on diversity, we need to sample the model multiple times using the same prompt.
If the watermark negatively impacts diversity, we expect a higher number of repetitions within the generated answer sequences.
In the limit, with AAR/KTH, given a fixed prompt the sampling is deterministic and all the generated sentences are exactly the same: the watermark exhibits low diversity.

We therefore propose the following experimental setup.
Given a dataset of prompts
\{x_{i}\}_{i}
, for each prompt, we sample the model
N
times, computing the self-BLEU score
[
2
]
for that prompt.
Self-BLEU scores are between
0
and
1
and measure the n-gram repetition between texts.
In our setting, a high self-BLEU score means that, for a given prompt, the multiple generations have very similar n-grams.
To smooth out the effect of the prompt itself (some prompts might naturally lead to low-diversity text), we then average all self-BLEU scores.
In practice, we use
100
prompts from the ELI5 dataset and sample the model
N\coloneq 100
times per prompt, yielding a total of
10{,}000
samples.
For each sample, we generate
200
tokens, using the trigram self-BLEU score with uniform weights.

Watermarks Diversity-Quality Trade-off

Figure

4
shows the impact on diversity and quality of different watermarks given a fixed TPR@1.
In particular, as predicted by our framework, we find that there is a negative correlation between diversity and quality: given a fixed power, some schemes prioritize diversity over quality and vice versa.
In particular, soft-constrained schemes (AAR/KTH and PPL (Soft)) have particularly low diversity (i.e., high self-BLEU scores) but also the highest quality.
This confirms the theoretical derivation from
Section

3
: at a fixed power, there is a fundamental trade-off between watermark quality and diversity.
More generally, this suggests that to measure the impact of distortion-free watermarks it is also necessary to evaluate the watermark behavior under repetition of the same prompt (a similar finding to
Wu et al. 2025
), and not only assume that distortion-free implies no actual distortion.

6
Conclusion and Limitations

Our work shows that most existing watermarks can be seen as instances of a unified constrained optimization problem.
As confirmed by our evaluation, our framework makes three contributions:
(i) it provides a principled way to interpret prior watermarks,
(ii) it allows us to practically instantiate powerful new schemes tailored to specific watermarking requirements,
and lastly (iii) it provides a novel perspective on the quality-diversity-power trade-off in watermarks.

Limitations

Our work makes two key assumptions when constructing the optimization problem.
First, we restrict the optimization to the token level rather than the sequence level.
Second, we deliberately optimize only the watermark sampling mechanism and do not jointly optimize the detector.
While these assumptions make the optimization tractable and enable us to capture and extend a wide range of watermarking schemes, there may exist jointly optimized approaches that achieve better overall watermarking performance.
Lastly, we note that there exists a range of watermarking algorithms (such as fully cryptographic watermarks) that are not captured by our framework.

Impact Statement

This paper presents a unified framework for LLM watermarking, which allows us to better understand existing schemes and design new ones.
Better-understood and better-tuned watermarks could support provenance, auditing, and mitigation of large-scale AI-generated text misuse.
Nonetheless, while LLM watermarks in general, and our framework in particular, are designed to mitigate false positives, they should not be used as sole evidence in major decisions.

\mathrm{KL}(q(g)\|p)=\sum_{u\in\Sigma}q(g)_{u}\log\frac{q(g)_{u}}{p_{u}}
with the convention
0\log 0=0
.

Define, for
\delta\geq 0
,

q_{\delta}(g)_{u}\;:=\;\frac{p_{u}e^{\delta g_{u}}}{Z_{g}(\delta)},\qquad Z_{g}(\delta):=\sum_{v\in\Sigma}p_{v}e^{\delta g_{v}},

and let

M(g):=\arg\max_{u\in\Sigma}g_{u},\qquad\varepsilon_{\max}(g):=-\log\Big(\sum_{u\in M(g)}p_{u}\Big).

Then:

1.

If
0<\varepsilon<\varepsilon_{\max}(g)
, there exists a
unique

\delta\in(0,\infty)
such that
\mathrm{KL}(q_{\delta}(g)\|p)=\varepsilon
. Moreover, the optimizer is unique and equals
q^{*}(g)=q_{\delta}(g)
, i.e.

q^{*}(g)\propto p\,e^{\delta g}.

2.

If
\varepsilon\geq\varepsilon_{\max}(g)
, the distribution

q_{\infty}(g)_{u}:=\frac{p_{u}\mathbf{1}\{u\in M(g)\}}{\sum_{v\in M(g)}p_{v}}

is optimal.

Proof.

Fix
g\in\mathbb{G}
. The problem is a convex optimization problem maximizing a linear function over a convex set (the KL-divergence ball). We introduce a Lagrange multiplier
\lambda\geq 0
for the inequality constraint
\mathrm{KL}(q(g)\|p)-\varepsilon\leq 0
and a multiplier
\nu\in\mathbb{R}
for the simplex constraint
\sum_{u}q(g)_{u}=1
. The Lagrangian is:

\mathcal{L}(q(g),\lambda,\nu)=-\sum_{u\in\Sigma}q(g)_{u}g_{u}+\lambda\left(\sum_{u\in\Sigma}q(g)_{u}\log\frac{q(g)_{u}}{p_{u}}-\varepsilon\right)+\nu\left(\sum_{u\in\Sigma}q(g)_{u}-1\right).

Note that we minimize
-\mathcal{L}
to maximize the objective. Taking the derivative with respect to
q(g)_{u}
and setting it to zero:

\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial q(g)_{u}}=-g_{u}+\lambda\left(\log\frac{q(g)_{u}}{p_{u}}+1\right)+\nu=0.

Rearranging for
q(g)_{u}
:

\lambda\log\frac{q(g)_{u}}{p_{u}}=g_{u}-\nu-\lambda\implies q(g)_{u}=p_{u}\exp\left(\frac{g_{u}-\nu-\lambda}{\lambda}\right).

(18)

If
\lambda=0
, equation
Equation

18
implies
g_{u}=\nu
for all
u
where
q(g)_{u}>0
, which contradicts the assumption that
g
is non-constant. Thus, we must have
\lambda>0
. Let
\delta\coloneqq 1/\lambda\in(0,\infty)
. Absorbing the normalization constants into a factor
1/Z_{g}(\delta)
, we obtain the form:

q_{\delta}(g)_{u}=\frac{p_{u}e^{\delta g_{u}}}{Z_{g}(\delta)},\quad\text{where }Z_{g}(\delta)=\sum_{v\in\Sigma}p_{v}e^{\delta g_{v}}.

By the Karush-Kuhn-Tucker (KKT) conditions, specifically complementary slackness, we require
\lambda(\mathrm{KL}(q_{\delta}(g)\|p)-\varepsilon)=0
. Since
\lambda>0
, we must have
\mathrm{KL}(q_{\delta}(g)\|p)=\varepsilon
, unless the constraint is inactive at the global maximum of the objective over the simplex (Case 2).

Let
\psi_{g}(\delta)\coloneqq\mathrm{KL}(q_{\delta}(g)\|p)
. We analyze the behavior of
\psi_{g}(\delta)
:

•

As
\delta\to 0
,
q_{\delta}(g)\to p
, so
\psi_{g}(0)=0
.

•

As
\delta\to\infty
, the mass of
q_{\delta}(g)
concentrates on the set
M(g)=\argmax_{u}g_{u}
. Specifically,
q_{\delta}(g)\to q_{\infty}(g)
, where
q_{\infty}(g)_{u}=\frac{p_{u}\mathbf{1}\{u\in M(g)\}}{\sum_{v\in M(g)}p_{v}}
.

•

The limit of the KL divergence is:

\lim_{\delta\to\infty}\psi_{g}(\delta)=\sum_{u\in M(g)}\frac{p_{u}}{\sum_{v\in M(g)}p_{v}}\log\left(\frac{p_{u}/\sum_{v\in M(g)}p_{v}}{p_{u}}\right)=-\log\left(\sum_{v\in M(g)}p_{v}\right)=\varepsilon_{\max}(g).

•

The derivative is
\psi_{g}^{\prime}(\delta)=\delta\operatorname{Var}_{U\sim q_{\delta}(g)}(g_{U})
. Since
g
is non-constant, the variance is positive, so
\psi_{g}(\delta)
is strictly increasing.

Case 1:
0<\varepsilon<\varepsilon_{\max}(g)
.

Since
\psi_{g}(0)=0
,
\lim_{\delta\to\infty}\psi_{g}(\delta)=\varepsilon_{\max}(g)
, and
\psi_{g}
is continuous and strictly increasing, there exists a unique
\delta\in(0,\infty)
such that
\psi_{g}(\delta)=\varepsilon
. This
\delta
satisfies the KKT conditions, making
q_{\delta}(g)
the unique optimizer.

Case 2:
\varepsilon\geq\varepsilon_{\max}(g)
.

The unconstrained maximum of the linear function
g\cdot q(g)
over the probability simplex
\Delta(\Sigma)
occurs at the vertices corresponding to
M(g)
. Among distributions supported on
M(g)
,
q_{\infty}(g)
minimizes the KL divergence to
p
(it is the projection of
p
onto the face defined by
M(g)
). Since
\mathrm{KL}(q_{\infty}(g)\|p)=\varepsilon_{\max}(g)\leq\varepsilon
,
q_{\infty}(g)
is feasible. Since it achieves the global maximum of the objective function
g\cdot q(g)
, it is optimal.
∎

Theorem D.2
.

Let
p\in\Delta(\Sigma)
have full support, let
g\in\mathbb{G}
be non-constant, and let
\delta>0
. Consider

\displaystyle q^{*}(g)\in\argmax_{q(g)\in\Delta(\Sigma)}\quad

\displaystyle g\cdot q(g)-\frac{1}{\delta}\mathrm{KL}(q(g)\|p),

We then have,

q^{*}(g)\propto p\,e^{\delta g}.

Proof.

Fix
g\in\mathbb{G}
. We formulate the Lagrangian
\mathcal{L}(q(g),\nu)
for the optimization problem, introducing a multiplier
\nu
for the normalization constraint
\sum_{u}q(g)_{u}=1
:

\mathcal{L}(q(g),\nu)=\sum_{u\in\Sigma}q(g)_{u}g_{u}-\frac{1}{\delta}\sum_{u\in\Sigma}q(g)_{u}\ln\frac{q(g)_{u}}{p_{u}}-\nu\left(\sum_{u\in\Sigma}q(g)_{u}-1\right).

The first-order optimality condition with respect to
q(g)_{u}
is given by setting the derivative to zero:

\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial q(g)_{u}}=g_{u}-\frac{1}{\delta}\left(\ln\frac{q(g)_{u}}{p_{u}}+1\right)-\nu=0.

Rearranging the terms yields:

\ln\frac{q(g)_{u}}{p_{u}}=\delta g_{u}-(1+\delta\nu)\implies q(g)_{u}=p_{u}e^{\delta g_{u}}e^{-(1+\delta\nu)}.

Since
e^{-(1+\delta\nu)}
is a constant scaling factor independent of
u
(determined by the constraint
\sum_{u}q(g)_{u}=1
), we conclude that

q^{*}(g)_{u}\propto p_{u}e^{\delta g_{u}}.

∎

D.2
AAR/KTH Watermarks

Here we formalize and then prove the different results established with respect to AAR/KTH watermarks.
We first show the shape of the optimal solution when using soft-KL constraints with any score distribution (
Theorem

D.3
), and then establish the extended watermark mechanism of AAR/KTH (
Section

4.1
) when using a Gumbel distribution (
Theorems

D.3
and
D.4
).
Lastly, we show that for soft-constrained watermarks with a continuous score distribution, the optimal solution is deterministic (
Theorem

D.5
), which theoretically demonstrates the lack of diversity in soft-constrained schemes observed in
Section

5.3
.

Theorem D.3
.

Let
p\in\Delta(\Sigma)
have full support, let
G
be a random vector with support
\mathbb{G}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{|\Sigma|}
and i.i.d. coordinates from a law
\mathcal{L}
, and let
\varepsilon\geq 0
. Consider the optimization problem

\displaystyle q^{*}\in\argmax_{q:\mathbb{G}\to\Delta(\Sigma)}\quad

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[G\cdot q(G)\big]

(19)

subject to

\displaystyle D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(\mathbb{E}[q(G)]\|p\big)\leq\varepsilon,

(20)

where
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(r\|p)=\sum_{u\in\Sigma}r_{u}\log\frac{r_{u}}{p_{u}}
.

Then there exists a vector
\tau\in\mathbb{R}^{|\Sigma|}
and a measurable tie-breaking function
T:\mathbb{G}\times\Sigma\to[0,1]
such that an optimal solution is given by

q^{*}(G)_{u}\;=\;\begin{cases}1&\text{if }\{u\}=M(G,\tau),\\
T_{u}(G)&\text{if }u\in M(G,\tau)\text{ and }|M(G,\tau)|>1,\\
0&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

(21)

where
M(G,\tau)
denotes the set of maximizing indices:

M(G,\tau)=\argmax_{v\in\Sigma}\big(G_{v}+\tau_{v}\big).

The tie-breaking weights satisfy
\sum_{u\in M(G,\tau)}T_{u}(G)=1
for all
G
, and
T,\tau
are chosen such that
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\mathbb{E}[q^{*}(G)]\|p)=\varepsilon
(unless
\tau=0
is feasible).

In particular, if
G
has i.i.d. standard Gumbel coordinates, there exists
\alpha\in[0,1]
such that an optimal solution is given by

\forall u\in\Sigma,\qquad q^{*}(G)_{u}\;=\;\mathbbm{1}\Big\{u\in\arg\max_{v\in\Sigma}\big(G_{v}+\alpha\log p_{v}\big)\Big\},

and
\alpha
is chosen so that
\mathrm{KL}(\mathbb{E}[q^{*}(G)]\|p)=\varepsilon
if feasible, else
\alpha=0
.

Proof.

Let
d=|\Sigma|
and write indices as
u\in\Sigma
.
Set

r\;=\;\mathbb{E}[q(G)]\in\Delta(\Sigma).

We can rewrite
Equation

19
as

\displaystyle\max_{q,r}\quad

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[G\cdot q(G)\big]

(22)

s.t.

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[q(G)]=r,

(23)

\displaystyle\mathrm{KL}(r\|p)\leq\varepsilon,

(24)

\displaystyle q(G)\in\Delta(\Sigma)\quad\text{for almost every $G$.}

(25)

The feasible set is convex and admits a Slater point, namely
q(G)\equiv p
(so
r=p
and
\mathrm{KL}(p\|p)=0<\varepsilon
), hence strong duality and KKT conditions hold.

Shape of an optimizer.

Introduce
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
as dual variable for
Equation

23
and
\mu\geq 0
for
Equation

24
. The Lagrangian is

\mathcal{L}(q,r,\lambda,\mu)=\mathbb{E}\big[G\cdot q(G)\big]+\lambda\cdot(\mathbb{E}[q(G)]-r)-\mu\big(\mathrm{KL}(r\|p)-\varepsilon\big),

which we rewrite as

\mathcal{L}(q,r,\lambda,\mu)=\mathbb{E}\big[(G+\lambda)\cdot q(G)\big]-\lambda\cdot r-\mu\big(\mathrm{KL}(r\|p)-\varepsilon\big).

(26)

For fixed
(\lambda,\mu,r)
, maximizing
Equation

26
over
q
subject to
Equation

25
separates over
G
:

\sup_{q}\mathcal{L}(q,r,\lambda,\mu)=-\lambda\cdot r-\mu\big(\mathrm{KL}(r\|p)-\varepsilon\big)+\mathbb{E}\Big[\sup_{q(G)\in\Delta(\Sigma)}(G+\lambda)\cdot q(G)\Big].

(27)

For any
g\in\mathbb{G}
,

\sup_{q\in\Delta(\Sigma)}(g+\lambda)\cdot q=\max_{v\in\Sigma}(g_{v}+\lambda_{v}),

and the supremum is attained by any
q
supported on the maximizers.

Let
(\lambda^{*},\mu^{*})
be an optimal dual pair and define

M(G,\lambda^{*})\;=\;\arg\max_{v\in\Sigma}\big(G_{v}+\lambda^{*}_{v}\big).

By strong duality, there exists a primal optimizer
q^{*}
that attains the supremum in
Equation

27
for
(\lambda^{*},\mu^{*})
. Thus, for almost every
G
,
q^{*}(G)
is supported on
M(G,\lambda^{*})
.

Choose a measurable tie-breaking function
T:\mathbb{G}\times\Sigma\to[0,1]
such that

T_{u}(G)=0\quad\text{if }u\notin M(G,\lambda^{*}),\qquad\sum_{u\in M(G,\lambda^{*})}T_{u}(G)=1,

and, whenever
M(G,\lambda^{*})=\{u\}
is a singleton, we set
T_{u}(G)=1
. Then, with
\tau=\lambda^{*}
,

q^{*}(G)_{u}\;=\;\begin{cases}1&\text{if }\{u\}=M(G,\tau),\\
T_{u}(G)&\text{if }u\in M(G,\tau)\text{ and }|M(G,\tau)|>1,\\
0&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

which is exactly the form in
Equation

21
.

Let
q^{0}(G)_{u}\propto\mathbbm{1}\Big\{u\in\arg\max_{v\in\Sigma}G_{v}\Big\}
a solution of the unconstrained problem, and
r^{0}=\mathbb{E}[q^{0}(G)]
.
If
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(r^{0}\|p)\leq\varepsilon
, then
q^{0}
is feasible for
Equation

19
and, being optimal for the unconstrained problem, is also optimal for the constrained one; we may take
\tau=0
.
Otherwise
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(r^{0}\|p)>\varepsilon
, so the KL constraint is active at any optimum by KKT/complementary slackness. Thus there exists an optimal pair
(q^{*},r^{*})
with
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(r^{*}\|p)=\varepsilon
, and by the previous paragraph
q^{*}
has the form
Equation

21
for some
\tau
and measurable tie-breaking rule
T
. This proves the first part of the theorem.

Gumbel case.

Assume now that
G
has i.i.d. standard Gumbel coordinates. For any
\tau\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, define

q^{\tau}(G)_{u}\;=\;\mathbbm{1}\Big\{u=\arg\max_{v\in\Sigma}\big(G_{v}+\tau_{v}\big)\Big\},\qquad r(\tau)_{u}\;=\;\mathbb{E}[q^{\tau}(G)_{u}].

(28)

The Gumbel-max trick gives

r(\tau)_{u}\;=\;\frac{e^{\tau_{u}}}{\sum_{v\in\Sigma}e^{\tau_{v}}},\qquad u\in\Sigma.

(29)

Fix
\tau
and write
Z_{v}=\tau_{v}+G_{v}
. For each realization of
G
,

\max_{v}Z_{v}=\sum_{u\in\Sigma}Z_{u}\,q^{\tau}(G)_{u}=G\cdot q^{\tau}(G)+\tau\cdot q^{\tau}(G).

Taking expectations and denoting
r=r(\tau)
,

\mathbb{E}\big[G\cdot q^{\tau}(G)\big]=\mathbb{E}\big[\max_{v}Z_{v}\big]-\tau\cdot r.

(30)

From the computation of the distribution of
Z^{*}=\max_{v}Z_{v}
,

\mathbb{E}\big[\max_{v}Z_{v}\big]=\gamma+\log\Big(\sum_{v\in\Sigma}e^{\tau_{v}}\Big),

(31)

where
\gamma
is the Euler-Mascheroni constant. Combining
Equation

30
and
Equation

31
,

\mathbb{E}\big[G\cdot q^{\tau}(G)\big]=\gamma+\log\Big(\sum_{v}e^{\tau_{v}}\Big)-\tau\cdot r.

(32)

Yet, we have

\tau\cdot r=\sum_{u}r_{u}\log r_{u}+\log\Big(\sum_{v\in\Sigma}e^{\tau_{v}}\Big).

(33)

Substituting
Equation

33
into
Equation

32
yields

\mathbb{E}\big[G\cdot q^{\tau}(G)\big]=\gamma-\sum_{u\in\Sigma}r_{u}\log r_{u}=\gamma+H(r),

where
H(r):=-\sum_{u}r_{u}\log r_{u}
is the Shannon entropy. Thus, in the Gumbel case, optimizing
Equation

19
over
q
is equivalent to optimizing over
r=\mathbb{E}[q(G)]
:

\displaystyle\max_{r\in\Delta(\Sigma)}\quad

\displaystyle H(r)

(34)

s.t.

\displaystyle\mathrm{KL}(r\|p)\leq\varepsilon.

(35)

Introduce multipliers
\lambda\geq 0
for
Equation

35
and
\beta\in\mathbb{R}
for
\sum_{u}r_{u}=1
. The Lagrangian is

\mathcal{J}(r,\lambda,\beta)=-\sum_{u}r_{u}\log r_{u}-\lambda\left(\sum_{u}r_{u}\log\frac{r_{u}}{p_{u}}-\varepsilon\right)+\beta\left(\sum_{u}r_{u}-1\right).

(36)

Assuming
r_{u}>0
, stationarity in
r_{u}
gives

-(\log r_{u}+1)-\lambda\big(\log(r_{u}/p_{u})+1\big)+\beta=0,\qquad u\in\Sigma.

Thus

r_{u}=e^{C}p_{u}^{\alpha},

where

\alpha:=\frac{\lambda}{1+\lambda}\in[0,1),\qquad C:=\frac{\beta-(1+\lambda)}{1+\lambda}.

(37)

Normalization
\sum_{u}r_{u}=1
fixes
e^{C}
, and we obtain the family

r_{u}^{*}(\alpha)=\frac{p_{u}^{\alpha}}{\sum_{v\in\Sigma}p_{v}^{\alpha}},\qquad\alpha\in[0,1).

(38)

Note that
r^{*}(0)
is the uniform distribution on
\Sigma
, and
r^{*}(1)=p
. The map
\alpha\mapsto\mathrm{KL}(r^{*}(\alpha)\|p)
is continuous, with

\mathrm{KL}\big(r^{*}(0)\|p\big)=\mathrm{KL}(\mathrm{Unif}\|p),\qquad\mathrm{KL}\big(r^{*}(1)\|p\big)=0.

Hence:

•

If
\varepsilon\geq\mathrm{KL}(\mathrm{Unif}\|p)
, the KL constraint is slack and the unconstrained maximizer of
H(r)
is the uniform distribution
r^{*}(0)
, corresponding to
\alpha=0
.

•

If
0\leq\varepsilon\leq\mathrm{KL}(\mathrm{Unif}\|p)
, there exists (by continuity) some
\alpha\in[0,1]
such that

\mathrm{KL}\big(r^{*}(\alpha)\|p\big)=\varepsilon,

and the constraint is active at the optimum.

For any optimal solution
r^{*}
of
Equation

34
–
Equation

35
, the softmax bijection
Equation

29
allows us to realize
r^{*}
as
r(\tau)
for some
\tau
. From
Equation

38
, we have

\log r_{u}^{*}(\alpha)=\alpha\log p_{u}+\text{const},\qquad u\in\Sigma.

Hence, we see that
\tau_{u}
can be chosen of the form

\tau_{u}=\alpha\log p_{u}+c,\qquad u\in\Sigma,

for some constant
c\in\mathbb{R}
. Adding a constant to all coordinates of
\tau
does not change the argmax in
Equation

28
, so without loss of generality we may take
c=0
, that is,

\tau_{u}=\alpha\log p_{u},\qquad u\in\Sigma.

Combining this with
Equation

28
yields

q^{*}(G)_{u}=\mathbbm{1}\Big\{u=\arg\max_{v\in\Sigma}\big(G_{v}+\alpha\log p_{v}\big)\Big\},

for some
\alpha\in[0,1]
chosen as above.
This completes the proof.
∎

Theorem D.4
.

Let
p\in\Delta(\Sigma)
have full support, let
G
be a random vector with support
\mathbb{G}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{|\Sigma|}
and i.i.d. coordinates from a Gumbel distribution, and let
\delta>0
. Consider

\displaystyle q^{*}\in\argmax_{q:\mathbb{G}\to\Delta(\Sigma)}\quad

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[G\cdot q(G)\big]-\frac{1}{\delta}\mathrm{KL}\big(\mathbb{E}[q(G)]\|p\big),

where
\mathrm{KL}(r\|p)=\sum_{u\in\Sigma}r_{u}\log\frac{r_{u}}{p_{u}}
.
Then, we have

\forall u\in\Sigma,\qquad q^{*}(G)_{u}\;=\;\mathbbm{1}\Big\{u\in\arg\max_{v\in\Sigma}\big(G_{v}+\frac{1}{1+\delta}\log p_{v}\big)\Big\}.

Proof.

The proof is similar to that of
Theorem

D.3
.
In
Equation

36
, instead of being a Lagrangian variable,
\lambda
is explicitly given as
\lambda=\frac{1}{\delta}
.
This then gives, in
Equation

37
,
\alpha=\frac{1}{1+\delta}
.
Thus, we have that,

\forall u\in\Sigma,\qquad q^{*}(G)_{u}\;=\;\mathbbm{1}\Big\{u\in\arg\max_{v\in\Sigma}\big(G_{v}+\frac{1}{1+\delta}\log p_{v}\big)\Big\},

which completes the proof.
∎

Theorem D.5
.

Let
p\in\Delta(\Sigma)
, let
G
be a random vector with support
\mathbb{G}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{|\Sigma|}
and i.i.d. coordinates from a continuous law
\mathcal{L}
with finite first-order moment, and let
\varepsilon\geq 0
. Consider

\displaystyle q^{*}\in\argmax_{q:\mathbb{G}\to\Delta(\Sigma)}\quad

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[G\cdot q(G)\big]

subject to

\displaystyle D\big(\mathbb{E}[q(G)],p\big)\leq\varepsilon,

where
D:\Delta(\Sigma)\times\Delta(\Sigma)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
.
Then, if the problem is feasible, there exists an optimal solution with support of size
1
.

Proof.

Let
q
be any optimal solution.
Let
X\in\Sigma
be a random variable corresponding to the sampled token, and define

\forall g\in\mathbb{G},\ \forall u\in\Sigma,\quad\mathbb{P}[X=u\mid G=g]=q_{u}(g).

Then the objective rewrites as

\mathbb{E}[G\cdot q(G)]=\mathbb{E}[G_{X}],

and the average output distribution is the marginal of
X
,

r(q)_{u}\coloneqq\mathbb{E}[q_{u}(G)]=\mathbb{P}[X=u],\quad u\in\Sigma.

The law of
G
is atomless since its coordinates are i.i.d. from a continuous law. By the Dvoretsky-Wald-Wolfowitz purification theorem
[
9
]
applied to
g\mapsto q(g)
and the finite family of integrable functions

(g,u)\mapsto g_{u}\quad\text{and}\quad(g,u)\mapsto\mathbf{1}\{u=v\},\ v\in\Sigma,

there exists a measurable function
f:\mathbb{G}\to\Sigma
such that, with
X^{\prime}=f(G)
,

\mathbb{E}[G_{X^{\prime}}]=\mathbb{E}[G_{X}]\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbb{P}[X^{\prime}=u]=\mathbb{P}[X=u],\ \forall u\in\Sigma.

Define
q^{\prime}:\mathbb{G}\to\Delta(\Sigma)
by
q^{\prime}(g)=e_{f(g)}
, so that
q^{\prime}(G)
has support of size
1
almost surely. Then

\mathbb{E}[G\cdot q^{\prime}(G)]=\mathbb{E}[G_{X^{\prime}}]=\mathbb{E}[G_{X}]=\mathbb{E}[G\cdot q(G)],

and

\mathbb{E}[q^{\prime}(G)]_{u}=\mathbb{P}[X^{\prime}=u]=\mathbb{P}[X=u]=\mathbb{E}[q(G)]_{u},\quad u\in\Sigma.

Hence
D(\mathbb{E}[q^{\prime}(G)],p)=D(\mathbb{E}[q(G)],p)\leq\varepsilon
, so
q^{\prime}
is feasible and optimal. This proves the existence of an optimal solution with support of size
1
.
∎

D.3
Chi2 Watermarks

Here we formalize and then prove the various results established for the one-layer SynthID and
\chi^{2}
watermark.
In particular, we first establish the general watermark sampling mechanism for the
\chi^{2}
watermark in
Theorem

D.6
, and show in
Theorem

D.7
how it reduces to the 1-layer SynthID tournament in some specific cases.

Theorem D.6
.

Let
p\in\Delta(\Sigma)
have full support, let
g\in\mathbb{G}
, and let
\varepsilon>0
. Consider

\displaystyle q^{*}(g)\in\argmax_{q(g)\in\Delta(\Sigma)}\quad

\displaystyle g\cdot q(g)

subject to

\displaystyle\sum_{u\in\Sigma}\frac{(q(g)_{u}-p_{u})^{2}}{p_{u}}\leq\varepsilon.

Then there exists
\delta,\mu\in\mathbb{R}
such that,

\forall u\in\Sigma,q^{*}(g)_{u}=p_{u}[1+\delta(g_{u}+\mu)]_{+}

where
[x]_{+}\coloneq\max(0,x)
,
\mu
is chosen such that
\sum_{u\in\Sigma}q^{*}(g)_{u}=1
, and
\delta
is chosen such that
\sum_{u\in\Sigma}\frac{(q^{*}(g)_{u}-p_{u})^{2}}{p_{u}}=\varepsilon
if feasible, else the greedy solution is feasible.

Proof.

We introduce a Lagrange multiplier
\lambda\geq 0
for the inequality constraint
\sum_{u\in\Sigma}\frac{(q_{u}-p_{u})^{2}}{p_{u}}=\varepsilon
, a multiplier
\mu\in\mathbb{R}
for the simplex constraint
\sum_{u}q_{u}=1
and
\nu\in\mathbb{R}^{|\Sigma|}_{+}
for the positivity constraints.
The Lagrangian is:

\mathcal{L}(q,\lambda,\mu)=-\sum_{u\in\Sigma}q_{u}g_{u}+\lambda\left(\sum_{u\in\Sigma}\frac{(q_{u}-p_{u})^{2}}{p_{u}}-\varepsilon\right)-\mu\left(\sum_{u\in\Sigma}q_{u}-1\right)-\sum_{u\in\Sigma}\nu_{u}q_{u}.

Note that we minimize
-\mathcal{L}
to maximize the objective. Taking the derivative with respect to
q_{u}
and setting it to zero:

\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial q_{u}}=-g_{u}+2\lambda\frac{(q_{u}-p_{u})}{p_{u}}-\mu-\nu_{u}=0.

Rearranging for
q_{u}
:
