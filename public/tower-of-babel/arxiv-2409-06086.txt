A Database of Continued Fractions of Polynomial Type
Henri Cohen

Abstract
We describe a database of 1925 continued fractions with polynomial coefficients, of which more than 1600 are new, both for interesting constants and for transcendental functions, and provide the database inside the T e X source of the paper. Look in particular at the section “Table of Contents”.

The Database in Detail

I will now describe the precise format of the database of continued fractions.
It is intended to be used by
Pari/GP
, but of course since the format
is transparent it can be used with any other software through an appropriate
interface.

2.1
Computer Representation of Continued Fractions

Let
(a(n))_{n\geq 0}
and
(b(n))_{n\geq 0}
be two sequences of complex numbers.
The continued fraction
(a,b)
is the formal expression

(a,b)=a(0)+\dfrac{b(0)}{a(1)+\dfrac{b(1)}{a(2)+\dfrac{b(2)}{a(3)+\ddots}}}

written more compactly as
(a,b)=a(0)+b(0)/(a(1)+b(1)/(a(2)+\cdots))
.
Note that this notation is different from that used by other authors
such as
[
Cuyt
]
. I do not claim that my notation
is better but simply that it is the one used in the present paper.
We will always assume that
b(n)\neq 0
for all
n
(otherwise the CF
terminates), and that
a(n)=0
only for a finite number of
n
. We write as
usual formally
p(n)/q(n)=a(0)+b(0)/(a(1)+b(1)/(a(2)+\cdots+b(n-1)/a(n)))
.

Definition 2.1

A CF
((a(n))_{n\geq 0},(b(n))_{n\geq 0})
is said to have

polynomial coefficients
if there exists a
period

T
such
that for every
j
with
0\leq j<T
, for
n
sufficiently large both

a(nT+j)
and
b(nT+j)
are rational functions of
n
with

rational
coefficients.

The name
polynomial
is justified since it is clear that
any CF with coefficients in
\mathbb{Q}(n)
is equivalent (i.e., a CF with the
same
p(n)/q(n)
) to one with coefficients in
\mathbb{Z}[n]
.

In practice
T
will be equal to
1
or
2
, so for
n
sufficiently large
either
a(n)
and
b(n)
are rational functions, or
a(2n)
,
a(2n+1)
,

b(2n)
, and
b(2n+1)
are rational functions.

It is evidently easy to
contract
a CF of period 2 to a CF of period
1, but the point of keeping a period 2 CF is that often its coefficients
are simple, while those of the contracted CF are complicated.

Note that (of course excluding finite and periodic CFs) most explicit CFs
occurring in the literature have polynomial coefficients,
with the notable exception of
q
-continued fractions such as Ramanujan’s

R(q)=q^{1/5}/(1+q/(1+q^{2}/(1+q^{3}/(1+\cdots))))\;,

which are fascinating but will not be considered here.

Let
(a,b)=((a(n))_{n\geq 0},(b(n))_{n\geq 0})
be a continued fraction with
polynomial coefficients. If
a
(similarly
b
) has period
1
, i.e., if

a(n)
is a polynomial for
n\geq n_{0}
for some
n_{0}
, we will represent
a

as the vector

A=[a(0),a(1),\dotsc,a(n_{0}-1),a(n)]\;,

where for

i\leq n_{0}-1
the
a(i)
do not depend on
n
, while
a(n)
is a polynomial
(exceptionally also a rational function) in the reserved variable
n
. Note
that the variable
n
is
reserved
for this purpose and cannot be used
elsewhere. It would perhaps have been more logical to use the variable
x

instead, but the variable
n
is almost universally used in texts so I have
chosen to use it.

Note also that the representation is not unique, for instance
we also have
A=[a(0),a(1),\dotsc,a(n_{0}-1),a(n_{0}),a(n)]
.

For instance, the continued fraction

\log(2)=3/(5-9\cdot 1^{2}/(15-9\cdot 2^{2}/(25-9\cdot 3^{2}/(35-9\cdot 4^{2}-\cdots))))

given above is represented by

[[0,10*n-5],[3,-9*n
ˆ
2]]

If
A
has period
2
, i.e., if
a(2n)
and
a(2n+1)
are possibly different
polynomials for
n
large, we will represent
A
as a vector of
2
-component
vectors

A=[[a(0),a(1)],\dotsc,[a(2m_{0}-2),a(2m_{0}-1)],[a(2n),a(2n+1)]]\;.

For instance, for the continued fraction

\pi=4-4/(3+9/(5+4/(7+25/(9+16/(11+49/(13+36/(15+\cdots)))))))\;,

A
has period 1 but
B
has period 2, so we can represent the CF by

[[4,2*n+1],[[-4,9],[(2*n)
ˆ
2,(2*n+3)
ˆ
2]]]

Note that in this case
all
the components of
B
must be two-component
vectors. For instance the above could
not
be written as

[[4,2*n+1],[-4,[(2*n+1)
ˆ
2,(2*n)
ˆ
2]]]

2.2
The Database Formats

First, a warning. We reserve the following variable names which must never
be used in working with the CFs of this file:

1.

“n”, which is universally used to denote the CF index.

2.

“d”, which denotes the radicand of a quadratic extension, see below
the explanation for
v[3][1]
.

3.

“x” which stands for
1/n
and “y” which stands for
1/n^{1/2}

in the asymptotic expansions
A
, see the explanation for
v[3][4]
.

There are three kinds of inputs in the file. By far the most important ones are
of course the CFs themselves, which are also the most numerous.

\bullet

Format of the Continued Fractions Entries

Such an entry, let’s call it
v
, has eight components, which we now
describe. To make the explanation clearer, we use the following example:

[(z)->cosh(Pi*z),[[1,1/2,4*n^2-8*n+(2*z^2+5)],
[2*z^2,-4*n^4+8*n^3+(-4*z^2-6)*n^2+(4*z^2+2)*n+(-z^2-1/4)]],
[1,[0,1,0,1],(z)->z^2*cosh(Pi*z),
1-1/2*z^2*x+(1/6*z^4-1/6*z^2-1/12)*x^2+(-1/24*z^4+1/6*z^2+1/12)*x^3+O(x^4)],
[[1,4*z^2,[1/2-I*z,1;1/2+I*z,1;3/2,-2],1],
[1,-4*z^2,[-1/2,2;1/2-I*z,-1;1/2+I*z,-1],1]],
[],"6.6.5",["AP->6.6.11","APD->6.6.6"],""];

1.

v[1]
is a closure giving the limit of the CF. Note
that in CFs involving variables, the CF may converge to that limit only
in a certain range of the variables, but we have not given that (we may
in a future version).

2.

v[2]
is the
[a(n),b(n)]
of the CF in the format
explained above, with one important difference: consider for instance the
entry 5.7.1. beginning with:

[k->zeta(k),[0,n^k+(n-1)^k],[1,-n^(2*k)]]

Giving this to
GP
would make it choke (give an error message) because

n
ˆ
k
is not a valid
GP
input when
k
is a variable.
Thus we replace it in the file by

[k->zeta(k),[k->[0,n^k+(n-1)^k],k->[1,-n^(2*k)]]]

which is a valid input since the closure is not evaluated.

3.

v[3]
is itself a 4-component vector
[d,[F,E,D,P],C,A]

giving the exact speed of convergence of the CF, including a few terms of
its asymptotic expansion. If
L
denotes the value of the CF and
p(n)/q(n)

are the partial quotients, this notation means that

L-\dfrac{p(n)}{q(n)}=\dfrac{C}{n!^{F}E^{n}e^{\sqrt{Dn}}n^{P}}A\;,

with the following conventions:

(a)

v[3][1]
is a number or a polynomial (very often 1) with the
following meaning: often, the speed of convergence and the asymptotic
expansions involve elements of a quadratic extension (of
\mathbb{Q}
or of

\mathbb{Q}[z]
for instance). To avoid using square roots, we set
v[3][1]

to the radicand, and we use the specific letter
d
to indicate the
square root of
v[3][1]
. Thus, we must avoid the letter
d
in
variables of CFs. In the running example, we have
v[3][1]=1
so
no quadratic extension.

(b)

v[3][2]
gives the speed of convergence in the above FEDP format,
using
d
if necessary. The running example has

v[3][2]=[0,1,0,1]
, meaning that we are in case
P^{+}
with

P=1
. In some rare cases, the speed does not make any sense, in which
case we set
v[3][2]=0
. In some other rare
cases, the CF does not converge for real values of the variables (which
is implicitly what is assumed), in which case we set

v[3][2]=[0,0,0,0]
.

(c)

v[3][3]
gives the constant
C
such that

S-p(n)/q(n)\sim C/[FEDP]
, or
0
if unknown or has not been computed.
Note that
C
is always given as a closure, first because in some cases
(as for the CF itself) it is not possible to do otherwise in
GP
,
and second because writing
C=()->Pi
is both much clearer to read
than
C=3.1415...
and also allows to have
C
to any accuracy.

(d)

v[3][4]
gives the asympotic expansion
A=1+...
, such that

S-p(n)/q(n)=(C/[FEDP])A
, or
0
if unknown or not computed.
The reserved letter
x
means
1/n
and the reserved letter
y

means
1/n^{1/2}
.

Thus, the above example says that

\cosh(\pi z)-\dfrac{p(n)}{q(n)}=\dfrac{z^{2}\cosh(\pi z)}{n}\left(1-\dfrac{z^{2}/2}{n}+\dfrac{z^{4}/6-z^{2}/2-1/12}{n^{2}}+\cdots\right)

4.

In a large number of cases, the CF or its inverse corresponds to a more
or less explicit hypergeometric series
S
given in a special format
explained below. Thus,
v[4]
is set to
0
if neither CF nor
its inverse has such a series, to
S
if only the CF has a series,
to the two-component vector
[S,T]
if both have a series (with
T

the series for the inverse), and finally the two-component vector

[[],T]
if only the inverse has a series.

These series are given in the following format: they are all of the form

S=u+\sum_{n\geq 0}h(n)\prod_{1\leq i\leq g}(a_{i})_{n}^{e_{i}}w^{n}\;,

where
h
is a rational function,
e_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}
, and as usual
(a_{i})_{n}
denotes
the rising Pochhammer symbol
\Gamma(a_{i}+n)/\Gamma(a_{i})
(note that the series
always starts at
n=0
). This is represented by

S=[u,h(n),[a_1,e_1;a_2,e_2;...a_g,e_g],w]

(when
g=0
, the empty matrix is simply set to
1
).
Note that for convergence, we always have

\sum_{1\leq i\leq g}e_{i}\leq 0
. Thus, the above example says that

\displaystyle\cosh(\pi z)

\displaystyle=1+4z^{2}\sum_{n\geq 0}\dfrac{(1/2-iz)_{n}(1/2+iz)_{n}}{(3/2)_{n}^{2}}\text{\quad and}

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{\cosh(\pi z)}

\displaystyle=1-4z^{2}\sum_{n\geq 1}\dfrac{(-1/2)_{n}^{2}}{(1/2-iz)_{n}(1/2+iz)_{n}}

The fact that the series begins at
n=0
has been chosen for uniformity,
but often leads to slightly more cumbersome formulas, for instance
instead of
\zeta(2)=\sum_{n\geq 1}1/n^{2}
we write instead

\zeta(2)=\sum_{n\geq 0}1/(n+1)^{2}
, hence represented by

[0,1/(n+1)
ˆ
2,1,1]
.

5.

v[5]
is used for testing. When empty, it means that
the default values for the variables used in the CF can be used
(presently
2/3
,
3/4
,
1/2
,
2
for closures with arity less or equal to

4
). If it is a vector of values, these are taken instead of the default
values. For instance for a closure of arity
2
,
v[5]=[2/3,-1]

means that one can take the default value
2/3
for the first variable,
but probably cannot use
3/4
for the second, but
-1
instead.
Other values of
v[5]
have specific meanings which may change:

•

v[5]=1
: Bernoulli type sequence, test expansion.

•

v[5]=2
: Jacobi type sequence, only test expansion.

•

v[5]=3
: Jacobi type sequence, test expansion and numerics, (2,1/2).

•

v[5]=4
: Change z into 1/z, multiply by z, test expansion.

•

v[5]=5
: Choose
z=2i
, multiply by
-i
, test.

Note that in the present version,
v[5]=4
and
v[5]=5
occur
each for a single CF.

6.

v[6]
is the
T
e
X
label used for the CF, so as to be
able to find it easily. It is very useful to write a trivial little
script to find conversely the given entry of the big vector
V

corresponding to a given label. Note that the labels are rather arbitrary
(but of course unique) since they were given after discovery of new CFs,
and more or less in increasing order.

7.

v[7]
is a 2-component vector [A1,AD1] whose entries are strings
describing the behavior of the CF under Apéry acceleration: A1 is
for the Apéry diagonal process, and AD1 for the Apéry dual. The meanings
of the strings are as follows: “NO” if Apéry is not applicable,
“BAcomp” if it is but the Bauer–Muir method introduces complicated
denominators, “AP→label” (“APSI→label” if followed by a simplification)
and “APD→label” if Apéry leads to the corresponding labeled CF,
(“APD→SELF” it is self-dual), “APcomp” and “APDcomp” if the result is
too complicated to be included in the encyclopedia so with no label,
and a possible additional “sim” if similar to.

8.

v[8]
is a (usually empty) string giving any additional information.

\bullet

Format of Parametrized Continued Fractions

Many CFs are part of families of inequivalent CFs depending on one or more
parameters. These families are included in the file in a different format,
since the initial values of
a(n)
and
b(n)
are not given. We have
only given such families for CFs of period 1.

The corresponding vector
v
has now only five components, and again
to make the explanation clearer we give an example:

[[()->Pi,2*k+2,(2*n-1)*(2*n+2*k-4*u-1)]
[1,[0,-1,0,k+1]],"5.3.3",[],"u<=k/2"];

1.

The first component
v[1]
is a three-component
vector:
v[1][1]
is the closure giving the limit
S
, and
v[1][2]

and
v[1][3]
are polynomials giving
a(n)
and
b(n)
for
n

sufficiently large. This means that for suitable initial values
a(0),a(1),\dotsc
, and
b(0),b(1),\dotsc
, and for given nonnegative integers
k
and

u
, the corresponding CF converges to
S
. Equivalently, the CF

[[v[1][2]],v[1][3]]
converges to a limit of the form
(aS+b)/(cS+d)

with
a
,
b
,
c
,
d
integral and
ad-bc\neq 0
. For instance, choosing

u=0
and
k=0,1,2
, or
u=1
and
k=2
, the above parametrized CF says that
the limits of the CFs

\displaystyle 2+1/(2+1/(2+9/(2+25/(2+49/(2+81/(2+\cdots))))))\;,

\displaystyle 4-1/(4+3/(4+15/(4+35/(4+63/(4+99/(4+\cdots))))))\;,

\displaystyle 6-3/(6+5/(6+21/(6+45/(6+77/(6+117/(6+\cdots))))))\;,\text{\quad and}

\displaystyle 6+1/(6+1/(6+9/(6+25/(6+49/(6+81/(6+\cdots))))))

are all of the form
(A\pi+B)/(C\pi+D)
with
AD-BC\neq 0
and

A
,
B
,
C
, and
D
in
\mathbb{Z}
.

2.

v[2]
contains the information on the speed of convergence
in the shortened format
[d,[F,E,D,P]]
, since the constant
C

and the asymptotic expansion
A
vary in a parametric family so
are not given.

3.

v[3]
is the label where the parametric CF occurs (same
as
v[5]
for ordinary CFs).

4.

v[4]
is used for testing, but for now is the empty vector.

5.

v[5]
is a string giving some additional information.

Note that for integer
k
, the CF
(a(n+k),b(n+k))
is essentially the
same as the CF
(a(n),b(n))
, so is of course not considered as a new CF,
so this trivial parametrization is not included.

\bullet

Format of Definitions

In some of the CFs, it is necessary to add some function definitions,
either because the corresponding functions do not exist in
Pari/GP

(for example
bernfrac(k)
and
eulerfrac(k)
exist, but not

tanfrac(k)
for the tangent numbers), or simply because the existing
expression is cumbersome. The command
V=readvec("file.gp");
will have two
effects: first, it defines the function so that it can be used in the CFs,
and second it is kept as an element of
V
as a closure (but without
its name). Thus, if you do not know the meaning of a function,
typing
?functionname
will give you an explanation or a formula for it.

More Details

Here is in more detail the different types of vectors
v
that we can
encounter, with examples.

1.

Definitions: as explained above. These are
closures
. Example:

R1(k,n)=(n^k*(2*n+1)+(n-1)^k*(2*n-3))/(2*n-1);

2.

Parametrized continued fractions: as explained above. These are

vectors
with
five
components. Example:

[[()->2^(1/3),7*n-5+k+v+4*u,-4*(n+u)*(3*n+v-2)],
[1,[0,4/3,0,u+(5-v)/3+2*k]],"5.2.3",[],""];

3.

Ordinary CF with
a
or
b
expressed as a closure: These are

vectors

v
with
eight
components such that
v[2][1]

or
v[2][2]
is a closure. Example:

[k->zeta(k),[k->[0,n^k+(n-1)^k],k->[1,-n^(2*k)]],[1,[0,1,0,k-1],
k->1/(k-1),1-((k-1)/2)*x+sum(m=1,4,binomial(k+2*m-2,2*m)*
bernfrac(2*m)*x^(2*m),O(x^10))],[0,(n,k)->1/(n+1)^k,1,1],
[3],"5.7.1",["BAcomp","BAcomp"],""];

4.

Ordinary CF with the last component of
a
and
b
a closure. Example:

[()->1,[[0,n->f(n)],[n->f(n+1)+1]],[0,0,0,0],
0,[],"6.1.1",["NO","NO"],""];

5.

Ordinary CF of period 2 or more: These are vectors
v
with eight
components such that the first (hence any) component of
v[2][1]
or

v[2][2]
is a vector. Example:

[()->2^(1/3),[[[0,5],[2*n,12*n+6]],[[6,-1/3],[-(n+1)*(3*n+2),
-n*(3*n+1)]]],[2,[0,(1+d)^2,0,0],()->2^(1/3)*3^(1/2)/(1+sqrt(2))^2,
1+(9*d/8)*x+(-9*d/8+81/64)*x^2+(67105*d/41472-81/32)*x^3+O(x^4)],
0,[],"5.2.10",["NO","NO"],""];

6.

Ordinary CF of period 1 with no closures. Example:

[()->3^(1/2),[[3/2,(2*n+1)^2],[2,-n^2*(n+2)^2]],[3,[0,(2+d)^2,0,0],
()->2*sqrt(3)/(2+sqrt(3))^3,1+d*x+(3/2-3*d/2)*x^2+(3*d-9/2)*x^3
+(45/4-27*d/4)*x^4+O(x^5)],0,[],"5.2.1",
["NO","NO"],"Infinitely contractible"];

A second example with Apéry information and series for the inverse:

[()->2^(1/3),[[1/2,7*n-5],[1,-12*n^2+8*n]],[1,[0,4/3,0,5/3],
()->2^(8/3)/gamma(1/3),1-55/9*x+170/3*x^2-1579490/2187*x^3
+230596751/19683*x^4+O(x^5)],[[],[0,2,[-2/3,1;1,-1],3/4]],
[],"5.2.3",["APSI->5.2.12","APD->SELF"],""];

where the string “APSI→5.2.12” indicates that after
simplification, Apéry leads to label 5.2.12, and “APD→SELF”
that it is self-dual for Apéry. The series entry says that

2^{-1/3}=2\sum_{n\geq 0}((-2/3)_{n}/n!)(3/4)^{n}
,
which is simply the special case
x=3/4
of the expansion of
2(1-x)^{2/3}
.

Here is a little script which outputs a number from 1 to 6 giving the type
of a vector
v
:

/* Find the type number of an entry in the file */

typevec(v)=
{ my(tv=type(v),lv=#v,a,b);
if(tv=="t_CLOSURE",return(1));
if(tv!="t_VEC"||(lv!=5&&lv!=8),error("incorrect entry"));
if(lv==5,return(2));
[a,b]=v[2];
if(type(a)=="t_CLOSURE"||type(b)=="t_CLOSURE",return(3));
if(type(a)!="t_VEC"||type(b)!="t_VEC",error("incorrect entry 2"));
if(type(a[#a])=="t_CLOSURE"||type(b[#b])=="t_CLOSURE",return(4));
if(type(a[1])=="t_VEC"||type(b[1])=="t_VEC",return(5));
return(6); }

Warnings.
Evidently in such a large database essentially put together
“by hand” it is unavoidable that there are typographical
errors. But apart from this, two warnings are necessary.

1.

First, the speed of convergence has been obtained
using heuristic methods, although I believe that the results are correct
(in particular, for CFs for
functions
, the indicated
speed of convergence often depends on the range of the variables). In
addition, the constant
C
has been obtained using linear dependence
algorithms (typically the
lindep
function of
Pari/GP
): it should
be correct, but I have not tried to prove anything.

2.

Second, many of the new CFs have been obtained rigorously from existing
ones, for instance by Bauer–Muir–Apéry type methods, but many others
have been obtained “à la Ramanujan”, in particular not rigorously,
although I am quite confident of their correctness.
Thus it is absolutely impossible for me to give the origin, and
a fortiori the proofs, of the CFs in the database.

Evidently I would more than welcome corrections and/or additions. Since
each CF has a label number, please refer to it when sending comments.
Ideally, this file should be put on a web site which could be regularly
updated by users, but I do not have the necessary knowledge to do this.

2.4
Beautifying the Continued Fractions

From a given CF in the database format, it is not difficult to create a
“beautified” version which is more human-readable, and I advise the reader
to do so. In fact, the author has done so and added a large quantity of
additional information, thus creating a 676 page book, see
[
Coh3
]
.
In particular, some
CFs may not be understandable without this additional information, but I
believe that the present file is sufficiently useful as is.

Here is an example of beautification. Entries

and
V[1191]

are (newlines inserted for better readability):

[[()->exp(Pi*z/2),4*k+2,4*n^2-4*n+(z^2+1)],

3
Some Remarks and Questions on the Database

In compiling and computing this database of continued fractions, a number
of sporadic questions have arisen. Here are a few:

1.

The CF given as an example for
3^{1/2}
(label 5.2.1) has the
curious property of being infinitely contractible: usually, computing
the contraction of a CF of bidegree
(d_{1},d_{2})
gives a CF of larger
bidegree. For the present CF of bidegree
(2,4)
, the contractions
become considerably more complicated, but can always be simplified (i.e.,
to a CF with the same partial quotients) to bidegree
(2,4)
.
First, I do not know how to prove this, and second, I do not know how to
characterize such CFs.

2.

The parametric families, especially those for periods of degree
2

and
3
such as
\zeta(2)
and
\zeta(3)
, have been obtained by searches.
In many cases they are probably not complete, but in some cases there are so
many that it would be desirable to classify them, which I have not always
succeeded in doing. See
[
Coh1
]
for detailed discussions of this.

3.

In the case of logarithmic convergence, I do not know of
any reasonable method to compute the constant
C
such that

S-p(n)/q(n)\sim C/\log(n)
, except when the CF is completely explicit,
or when it is the specialization of a CF with more variables (for instance,
this is how I found that the constant
C
for 5.4.27 is equal to

\pi^{3}/8
and that of 5.5.10 is
\pi^{4}/28
).

4.

When the convergence is precisely
S-p(n)/q(n)\sim C/n^{k}
with integral

k
, I do not know of a systematic way to compute the asymptotic expansion

A
to more than
k-1
terms, except when the CF comes from a sum of
explicit rational functions. In many cases, this is due to the presence of
more complicated terms in the asymptotic expansion, typically involving

\log(n)
. In all other convergence cases (except of
course the logarithmic case), including
C/n^{k}
when
k\notin\mathbb{Z}
, there is
an algorithmic way to compute
A
.

5.

Since there are so many CFs for the functions
\beta(z)
and
\beta_{1}(z)
,
it is slightly surprising that there are no interesting ones for

\beta_{2}(z)=-\beta_{1}^{\prime}(z)
; nonetheless, I have given a couple of (uninteresting)
ones.

6.

Although
I_{\nu}(z)
is an even function of
z
(up to a power of
z
),
the CF 6.22.6 does not seem to define an even function. This implies
that there does not seem to exist a corresponding CF for
J_{\nu}(z)
(not
using complex numbers), and consequently that there does not seem to
exist analogs for
J
of the CFs 5.12.10 and 5.12.11 for
I
.

7.

There exist many CFs involving the Bessel functions
J_{\nu}(z)
,

I_{\nu}(z)
, and
K_{\nu}(z)
, as well as

H_{\nu}(z)=J_{\nu}(z)\pm iY_{\nu}(z)
. Are there interesting ones involving

Y_{\nu}(z)
alone ?

8.

Using Apéry, it is not difficult to find a parametric family of CFs
for
\cosh(\pi z/3)
, see 6.6.9. On the other hand, the very similar
CF 6.6.20 for
\sinh(\pi z/3)/(z\sqrt{3}/2)
does not accelerate
nicely, so I have been unable to find a corresponding parametric family.

9.

The CFs 5.11.91 for
\Gamma(1/3)^{2}/\Gamma(2/3)
and
5.11.129 for
\Gamma(2/3)^{2}/\Gamma(1/3)
are extremely similar and
have the same origin. They can both be Apéry accelerated giving
complicated formulas, but the former has a very simple Apéry dual
5.11.96, while the latter has a very complicated Apéry dual which
therefore we have not given. Is there an analogue of 5.11.96 for

\Gamma(2/3)^{2}/\Gamma(1/3)
?

10.

Similarly, there is a beautiful rapidly convergent CF
5.11.140 as well as a very simple hypergeometric series for

\Gamma(2/3)^{2}/\Gamma(1/3)
, but apparently nothing of the sort for

\Gamma(1/3)^{2}/\Gamma(2/3)
.
