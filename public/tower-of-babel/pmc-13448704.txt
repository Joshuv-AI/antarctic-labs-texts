Transformer-based 3D pose estimation pipeline with modular SmoothNet integration for animation generation
Authors: Xinyi Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Traditional 3D human pose estimation and animation generation methods still face high computational cost and temporal instability. To address these issues, this study develops a Transformer-based 3D pose estimation pipeline for spatial prediction and integrates SmoothNet as an external modular post-processing component for temporal smoothing. The backbone extracts image features with ResNet, encodes global dependencies using Transformer blocks, and outputs 3D keypoints for downstream reconstruction. SmoothNet is then applied to reduce frame-level jitter in predicted pose sequences. We evaluate the full pipeline on H3WB, AGORA, and AthletePose3D using MPJPE, PA-MPJPE, MPVE, and acceleration error. In addition, a fairness-controlled protocol on H3WB is introduced to compare MHFormer and the proposed backbone under unified SmoothNet post-processing settings, separating backbone effects from smoothing effects. Animation quality is further validated through Blender and Unreal Engine (UE) deployment.

Introduction

Three-dimensional (3D) human pose estimation is one of the core technologies in computer vision and graphics1. By extracting the 3D coordinates of human joint points from two-dimensional images or videos, it enables precise capture and reconstruction of human motion2. This technology holds broad application prospects in fields such as motion analysis, human-computer interaction, virtual reality (VR), augmented reality (AR), and medical rehabilitation. For instance, it analyzes athletes’ movements in sports training3, drives virtual character motion in game development4, and assists motion capture in film and television special effects5.

With the growth of the animation industry, particularly the rise of games, film/TV effects, and VR applications, pose-based animation synthesis has become a key method for enhancing character motion realism and automation6. Traditional animation production primarily relies on manual drawing or expensive optical motion capture equipment7. These methods are not only labor-intensive and costly but also often lack dynamic adaptability, failing to respond in real-time to environmental changes such as terrain variations or object interactions. Additionally, they typically require specialized equipment and significant manpower, which can lead to inaccurate poses or jerky animations in complex scenes. Early pose estimation methods predominantly employed manual feature extraction and simple geometric models8, exhibiting limited robustness and failing to effectively handle the complexity and diversity of human motion, such as errors caused by joint occlusion or lighting variations.

In recent years, the rapid advancement of deep learning technologies has revitalized this field, making the automatic generation of photorealistic animations via neural network models a research hotspot9. Deep learning frameworks like convolutional neural networks (CNNs) have replaced traditional manual feature extraction. Trained on massive datasets, they significantly enhance the accuracy and generalization capabilities of pose estimation. However, existing methods still face numerous challenges: First, the mapping from two-dimensional images to 3D space is inherently an ill-posed problem, characterized by intrinsic depth ambiguity. Second, traditional CNN-based models struggle to capture long-range dependencies and global contextual information, potentially leading to biased estimates in complex scenarios such as partial occlusions, non-uniform lighting, or intricate backgrounds. Furthermore, such models typically demand substantial computational overhead and memory consumption, limiting their application in real-time animation generation scenarios. More critically, 3D pose sequences estimated directly from static image frames often lack temporal consistency, resulting in unnatural jitter and abrupt transitions in generated animations. This severely compromises the fluidity and realism of the final animation, falling short of the requirements for high-quality production.

This research focuses on the application of Transformer models in 3D human pose estimation and extends them to an optimized framework for animation synthesis. The Transformer architecture, originally proposed by Vaswani et al.10 for natural language processing, has revolutionized sequential data modeling through its self-attention mechanism. Leveraging self-attention mechanisms, Transformers efficiently process sequential data in parallel and model long-range relationships11, demonstrating potential across diverse visual tasks—such as the application of Vision Transformers (ViT) in image classification12. Building upon this foundation, this work develops a Transformer-based 3D pose backbone and integrates SmoothNet13 as an external temporal refinement module within a unified pipeline. The backbone is responsible for spatial pose regression and computational efficiency, while SmoothNet is used as prior work for temporal stabilization rather than as a newly proposed smoothing architecture in this work. Validation is conducted using Blender and Unreal Engine (UE) for animation rendering. This approach not only enhances pose estimation robustness but also provides an end-to-end framework for low-cost motion capture animation, enabling direct synthesis of natural character motion from video.

The key motivations of this work are threefold: First, existing CNN-based 3D pose estimation methods incur high GPU memory consumption and computational overhead, limiting their application in real-time scenarios. Second, monocular pose estimation inherently suffers from depth ambiguity and temporal inconsistency, resulting in jittery animations unsuitable for professional production. Third, there lacks an end-to-end pipeline that seamlessly integrates pose estimation with animation rendering in industry-standard tools.

The main contributions of this work are as follows: (1) We design a Transformer-based 3D pose estimation backbone that replaces deconvolutional heatmap regression with attention-driven decoding, improving spatial estimation while reducing GPU memory usage. (2) We integrate SmoothNet13 as an external plug-and-play post-processing module and explicitly separate backbone contribution from smoothing contribution through controlled with/without-SmoothNet analyses. (3) We establish a complete engineering workflow from pose estimation to SMPL/IK-driven animation rendering in Blender and Unreal Engine, providing an end-to-end practical pipeline for animation-oriented deployment.

State of the art

Traditional animation and motion synthesis

Traditional animation production primarily relies on two methods: manual creation and motion capture14, with motion capture predominantly used for animation in complex environments. Both approaches are labor-intensive and yield fixed animations. With the rapid advancement of deep learning, numerous neural network-based animation or motion generation frameworks have been proposed. For instance, Jayamohan et al.15 introduced a method utilizing gated recurrent units (GRUs) and residual structures to predict motion and enhance its continuity. Loi et al.16 introduced a hierarchical learning recurrent neural network (HL-RNN) for motion synthesis, enabling smooth transitions between diverse movements. Recognizing quaternions’ utility for interpolating animation frames, Zhou et al.17 developed the QuaterNet architecture to represent quaternion rotations. These methods update character poses on a per-frame basis—using previous frame poses to predict the next frame—making them suitable for real-time systems like games. However, the actions predicted by these models suffer from several issues: first, action drift occurs; second, due to the inherent characteristics of recurrent neural networks, noise and errors accumulate over time, leading to stiff or jerky generated actions; Third, these models cannot output realistic animations that adapt to environmental changes, such as foot lift height failing to adjust to terrain elevation.

To address terrain adaptation and control for characters across diverse environments, Shi et al.18 analyzed character gait in games and proposed the “Motion Cycle” parametric data structure for cyclic motion. This structure synthesizes cyclically varying movements (e.g., walking) and controls human gait to adapt dynamically to terrain changes. Following Mukai et al.’s approach, Ma et al.19 introduced Phase-Function Neural Networks (PFNN). These networks utilize recorded phase information to control cyclically varying bipedal motion, while also integrating controller input to dynamically alter movement states and output them in real-time to character animations. Wang et al.’s20 work holds significant influence in real-time animation generation, serving as a pioneering contribution to phase-based real-time animation generation. Inspired by the PFNN network architecture, Shao et al.21 proposed the Mode-Adaptive Neural Networks (MANN) model for controlling the motion of quadrupedal organisms like animals. Since animal motion cannot be described by a single phase as in PFNN, they introduced an expert system to capture the polymorphic nature of quadrupedal movement. This approach autonomously learns phase values to control network weights, generating realistic animations of quadrupedal animals walking or running. Jia et al.22 proposed a method called Local Motion Phase to describe locally periodic motion. Using a dataset of basketball players’ movements, they trained an animation generation model. This model could interact with environmental objects and even other characters, ultimately demonstrating animated results of a 1v1 basketball game. These approaches all belong to phase-based real-time animation generation algorithms, differing primarily in their improvements to the phase concept.

Physics-based animation generation

Physics-based animation generation schemes can more realistically simulate changes in human tissue and environments, producing highly lifelike character animations. However, their implementation is challenging, making them a research hotspot in the animation field over the past two years. Tessler et al.23 proposed a reinforcement learning framework that controls character motion through physical constraints. This method can mimic human control strategies for high-level actions like side rolls and backflips performed in real life. Younes et al.24 employed reinforcement learning to generate physics-based real-time character animations. Physics-based animations exhibit significantly greater adherence to physical laws and enable superior interactivity, such as controlling movement direction and velocity. Wang et al.25 investigated human movement on soft surfaces like snow and sand, proposing a mechanics-based character animation generation approach. This method calculates forces between the character’s weight and different ground materials, along with ground deformation, significantly enhancing the visual realism of generated animations. Wibowo et al.26 proposed learning from reference motions using spatiotemporal boundaries instead of imitation rewards. This approach permits minor deviations in joint angles and specific positions, introduces diverse learning strategies, and enhances learning through sampling low-reward states. Rocca et al.27 investigated training a physics-based character controller to reproduce diverse motions using a motion controller as input, optimizing motion generation via a two-layer model and clustering techniques. Ye et al.28 proposed a deep reinforcement learning-based physics-based character animation framework focused on mimicking complex skills like jumping and rolling. They enhanced controller robustness through progressive training strategies and validated its noise resilience in simulated environments. Ahmed et al.29 developed the PDP (Physics-Based Character Animation via Diffusion Policy) method, combining reinforcement learning with behavior cloning to generate physics-compliant real-time character animation, demonstrating particularly strong performance in directional and velocity control. Wong et al.30 introduced the PARC (Physics-based Augmentation with Reinforcement Learning for Character Controllers) framework. It utilizes physics simulation to augment data and employs reinforcement learning to optimize character controllers, addressing the adaptability issues of traditional methods in complex terrains.

Graph-based and transformer-based pose estimation

Beyond traditional CNN and RNN approaches, graph-based methods have also shown promise in modeling skeletal structures. Yan et al.31 introduced spatial temporal graph convolutional networks (ST-GCN) for skeleton-based action recognition, which naturally represent human body joints as graph nodes and bones as edges, capturing spatial configuration and temporal dynamics simultaneously. While ST-GCNs excel in action recognition, their reliance on predefined graph topologies limits adaptability to diverse pose estimation scenarios.

In recent years, Transformer-based architectures have emerged as a powerful paradigm for 3D human pose estimation, leveraging self-attention mechanisms to capture spatiotemporal dependencies more effectively than traditional RNN or CNN methods. For example, Zheng et al.32 proposed PoseFormer, a spatial-temporal Transformer that processes 2D joint sequences to regress 3D poses, achieving improved accuracy on benchmarks like Human3.6M by modeling long-range interactions. Building on this, Zhang et al.33 introduced MixSTE (Seq2seq Mixed Spatio-Temporal Encoder), which combines multi-scale feature fusion with Transformer blocks to handle occlusions and noise in monocular videos, showing enhanced robustness in wild scenarios. More recently, Li et al.34 developed MHFormer (Multi-Hypothesis Transformer), which generates multiple pose hypotheses via Transformer decoders to mitigate ambiguity in 3D lifting from 2D inputs, further optimizing for real-time animation applications. These models highlight the shift toward efficient, attention-driven approaches that align well with downstream tasks like animation synthesis, addressing limitations in continuity and environmental adaptation seen in earlier methods.

Related advances in visual representation and generation

Beyond 3D pose estimation, related advances in pose representation and head pose estimation provide complementary perspectives. Cao et al. 35 demonstrated that vector-based representations can enhance head pose estimation robustness under varying viewpoints. Additionally, prototype learning approaches—such as those explored in TransFlow—offer promising directions for predicting discrete pose positions through learned feature prototypes, potentially reducing ambiguity in multi-hypothesis scenarios. These techniques emphasize the importance of appropriate pose representations and feature learning strategies, which inform the design choices in our Transformer-decoder architecture.

The success of Transformer architectures extends beyond pose estimation to diverse computer vision tasks, demonstrating the versatility of attention mechanisms and deep feature learning. In image restoration, Chen et al.36 proposed a dual degradation image inpainting method (AFFU) that addresses multiple degradation tasks simultaneously through adaptive multi-feature fusion and a self-guided module for multi-scale information integration. Their work demonstrates how adaptive feature selection during network progression prevents information loss—a principle relevant to our temporal smoothing approach. For image super-resolution, Chen et al.37 introduced CAT (Channel Attention and Transformer), which synergistically integrates convolutional operations with Swin Transformer blocks through channel-attention-embedded mechanisms, achieving superior reconstruction quality while reducing computational costs by 68% compared to SwinIR. This highlights the efficiency gains possible through strategic fusion of local and global feature processing, analogous to our integration of ResNet and Transformer modules. In multi-modal tracking, Zhang et al.38 developed MGNet, which employs dual-stage attention for cross-modality cross-region mutual guidance in RGBT tracking, achieving 75 FPS real-time performance. Their approach to preserving distinct modality features while enabling cross-modal interaction parallels our strategy of maintaining 2D image features and 3D template encodings before cross-attention decoding. More recently, Zhang et al.39 proposed DCCM-Net for crack segmentation, combining difference convolution operators with a hybrid CNN-Mamba multi-scale attention module that captures both spatial and long-range dependencies. While these works address different problem domains, they collectively illustrate the broader trend of leveraging attention-based architectures and adaptive feature fusion strategies—principles that also underpin our Transformer-based pose estimation framework. The shared emphasis on hierarchical feature extraction, multi-scale processing, and attention mechanisms reinforces the rationale for adopting Transformer architectures in our work. More recently, diffusion-based methods have further expanded generative capabilities in visual synthesis; Han et al. 40 proposed treating image translation as a programmable diffusion process, offering a flexible framework for controllable generation that complements attention-based estimation pipelines.

Methodology

Design of a transformer-based human pose estimation network framework

The proposed SN-HPET framework consists of four main stages, as illustrated in Fig. 1: (1) feature extraction: input images or videos are processed through a ResNet backbone network to extract 2D features, which are then flattened and augmented with positional encoding; (2) 3D pose estimation: a transformer-based architecture with template-guided decoding estimates 3D keypoints, twist angles, and shape parameters; (3) temporal smoothing: SmoothNet module performs temporal smoothing on the estimated 3D keypoint sequences to reduce jitter; (4) pose reconstruction: HybrIK and SMPL model reconstruct the final 3D pose and generate animation output. The following subsections detail each stage.

In the original 3D human pose estimation algorithm, convolutional neural networks were employed to generate 3D joint heatmaps for predicting the coordinate locations of human joints in input images or videos. This process primarily utilized three deconvolution layers and one 1 × 1 convolution. However, this approach incurs substantial computational overhead due to the vast number of parameters involved—such as input feature map size, convolution kernel size, stride, padding, and input/output channel counts—while also consuming significant GPU memory. This work proposes a method to replace the original convolutional neural network with a Transformer block for 3D keypoint estimation. This approach leverages the Transformer module’s outstanding performance in sequence data modeling, along with the parallel computing capabilities and global information capture enabled by the self-attention mechanism. The goal is to reduce GPU memory consumption while improving keypoint prediction accuracy. The overall network model architecture is shown in Fig. 1.Fig. 1Overall network architecture diagram.

The designed \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$L_2$$\end{document} loss function equation is as follows:10\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} L_{\text {twa}} = \frac{1}{Y} \sum _{y=1}^{Y} \Vert (\cos \phi _y, \sin \phi _y) - (\cos \hat{\phi }_y, \sin \hat{\phi }_y)\Vert _2 \end{aligned}$$\end{document}In the equation: \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\phi _y$$\end{document} represents the predicted rotation angle of the y-th joint node, while \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hat{\phi }_y$$\end{document} denotes the actual rotation angle of the y-th joint node. \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Vert \cdot \Vert _2$$\end{document} denotes the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$L_2$$\end{document} norm, calculated by forming vectors from the cosine and sine components respectively.

SN-HPET model network architecture design

Stage 3: temporal smoothing with SmoothNet

This section introduces the SN-HPET model, which incorporates the advanced plug-and-play SmoothNet as an optimized network module within the model pipeline. This constitutes Stage 3 of the overall framework, focusing on temporal smoothing of the 3D keypoint sequences estimated in Stage 2. As a purely temporal-domain modeling approach, SmoothNet demonstrates exceptional cross-model and cross-dataset transferability. By incorporating this module, it addresses the significant deviation issue observed in “Design of a transformer-based human pose estimation network framework”’s 3D human pose estimation algorithm. This deviation arises when occlusions or other noise factors cause substantial misalignment between estimated joint positions and corresponding ground truth values across consecutive frames. In this work, SmoothNet is treated as prior external work13. Our contribution in Stage 3 is the system-level integration and evaluation protocol within the proposed Transformer-to-animation pipeline, rather than introducing a new temporal smoothing architecture.

This network architecture comprises two primary components: the first is a pre-defined human pose estimation model. This model can be based on a direct approach, estimating 3D human pose directly from image or video data, or a two-stage method, first performing 2D pose estimation before elevating it to 3D space. The choice depends primarily on the specific application scenario and requirements. This section utilizes the 3D human pose estimation algorithm from the previous section as its core. The second component is the SmoothNet network model. This model differs from other spatio-temporal models in that it is purely time-dimensional. It also demonstrates plug-and-play characteristics, supported by strong cross-model and cross-dataset capabilities.

SmoothNet processes the 3D keypoint sequences estimated in Stage 2. First, the 3D human pose estimation network (Stage 2) identifies and extracts human pose information from video frame data, outputting 3D keypoint sequences. The resulting 3D keypoint sequences are fed into the SmoothNet network module (Stage 3) for temporal smoothing. After processing, the smoothed keypoint sequences are obtained. Subsequently, the smoothed keypoints, along with twist angles and shape parameters from Stage 2, undergo further processing in Stage 4 using HybrIK and the SMPL model for pose reconstruction and animation generation. Ultimately, compared to video frames without the SmoothNet module, this method achieves smoother handling of jitter caused by noise. Similarly, building upon this workflow, the SmoothNet network module is directly integrated into existing 3D human pose estimation models. Through data-driven modeling, it captures the natural smoothness characteristics of bodily motion. The human pose data set generated from video keyframes is packaged into a PK animation file and imported into the 3D graphics renderer Blender to visualize the preliminary animated character motion results. The renderer outputs FBX files corresponding to the video frame data. The animation is then mapped onto a virtual character via UE’s IK binding, achieving motion capture effects. Realistic movements are reproduced through the animation of the virtual character model. This integrated workflow enables direct observation of the performance differences achieved by incorporating SmoothNet processing into video frames.

Stage 4: pose reconstruction and animation generation

The fourth stage reconstructs the final 3D pose and generates animation output using inverse kinematics (IK) and the SMPL model. The smoothed 3D keypoints from Stage 3, along with twist angles and shape parameters from Stage 2, are processed through HybrIK to compute pose parameters θ. These parameters, combined with shape parameters β, are fed into the SMPL model to generate the body mesh W. Finally, forward kinematics (FK) or linear regression is applied to obtain the reconstructed pose V, which serves as the output for animation generation.

Introduction to datasets and evaluation metrics

The datasets used in this work adopt the widely applied H3WB, AGORA, and AthletePose3D. (1) The H3WB (Human3.6M 3D WholeBody) dataset is an extended version of Human3.6M, specifically designed for 3D whole-body pose estimation. It contains annotations for approximately 100K images, providing 133 whole-body keypoints (including 17 body, 6 foot, 68 face, and 42 hand keypoints), and supports tasks such as 2D to 3D lifting and direct RGB prediction. This work uses this dataset for training and evaluation. (2) The AGORA (Avatars in Geography Optimized for Regression Analysis) dataset is a synthetic benchmark for 3D human pose and shape estimation, featuring 424K high-resolution images with diverse subjects (including adults and children), poses, clothing, and environments. It provides accurate ground truth annotations using SMPL-X body models from 3D scans, emphasizing challenges in occluded and varied scenarios. (3) The AthletePose3D dataset is a benchmark dataset for 3D human pose estimation and kinematic validation in sports movements, containing 3D pose data for various competitive actions, aimed at supporting monocular 3D pose reconstruction. It provides high-precision annotations and is suitable for complex dynamic scenarios. This work uses this dataset for training and evaluation.

Evaluation metrics

This study uses MPJPE (mean per joint position error), PA-MPJPE (procrustes-aligned mean per joint position error), and MPVE (mean per vertex error) as evaluation metrics.

MPJPE measures the Euclidean distance between predicted joint positions (denoted as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Y_x$$\end{document}) and ground-truth joint positions (denoted as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Y_x^*$$\end{document}). It serves as an accuracy metric to assess the difference between true and predicted joint positions. For a human body with T joints, MPJPE is calculated using the following equation:14\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \text {MPJPE} = \frac{1}{T} \sum _{x=1}^{T} \Vert Y_x - Y_x^*\Vert _2 \end{aligned}$$\end{document}PA-MPJPE is a reconstruction error metric. It quantifies MPJPE after aligning the predicted joints with the ground-truth joints through rigid body transformation. This approach effectively eliminates errors introduced by translation, rotation, and scaling. Thus, PA-MPJPE provides a more precise evaluation of the accuracy of predicted joint positions.

MPVE calculates the vertex error for human body models with shape variations, such as those represented by the SMPL model. Given the predicted vertex positions (denoted as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Q_x$$\end{document}) and ground-truth vertex positions (denoted as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Q_x^*$$\end{document}), MPVE is computed using the following equation:15\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \text {MPVE} = \frac{1}{T} \sum _{x=1}^{T} \Vert Q_x - Q_x^*\Vert _2 \end{aligned}$$\end{document}To quantitatively evaluate temporal smoothness, we additionally employ the acceleration error metric (Accel), which measures the jitter in predicted pose sequences. For a sequence of poses over frames t, acceleration error is computed as:16\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \text {Accel} = \frac{1}{T-2} \sum _{t=2}^{T-1} \Vert \hat{J}_t - 2\hat{J}_{t-1} + \hat{J}_{t-2}\Vert _2 \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hat{J}_t$$\end{document} represents the predicted joint positions at frame t. Lower acceleration error indicates smoother motion with less jitter. This metric directly measures temporal continuity, complementing spatial error metrics (MPJPE, PA-MPJPE, MPVE) to provide a comprehensive evaluation of pose sequence quality.

Computational efficiency analysis

To evaluate the practical feasibility of the proposed method, we measure GPU memory consumption and inference speed compared to baseline models. All experiments are conducted on an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3090 GPU with batch size 16 and input resolution 256 × 256. The proposed Transformer-based architecture consumes approximately 8.2GB GPU memory during training, compared to 10.5GB for the original deconvolution-based approach—a reduction of 21.9%. This reduction stems from replacing three deconvolution layers with Transformer blocks, which process flattened feature sequences more efficiently than multi-scale heatmap generation. Inference speed is measured at 32 frames per second (FPS) for the base pose estimation model, and 28 FPS when SmoothNet is included. While SmoothNet introduces a minor overhead due to temporal window processing, the overall pipeline remains suitable for near-real-time applications. Compared to PoseFormer (25 FPS), our method achieves faster inference due to the more compact Transformer decoder design.

Failure cases and limitations

Despite the improvements demonstrated, the proposed method exhibits limitations in certain scenarios. First, under severe occlusion (e.g., when over 50% of the body is occluded), the model may produce inaccurate joint positions, as the ResNet backbone struggles to extract reliable features from severely incomplete visual input. SmoothNet can mitigate temporal jitter but cannot recover from persistent mis-detections. Second, extreme poses involving significant self-contact or unusual joint configurations (e.g., yoga poses, dance contortions) occasionally lead to increased errors, as the SMPL prior and training data distribution favor more common upright postures.

Third, while SmoothNet effectively reduces jitter, it introduces a trade-off between smoothness and motion responsiveness. In sequences with rapid directional changes or high-frequency movements—such as boxing punches or basketball dribbling—smoothing may attenuate motion sharpness. To quantify this trade-off, we analyze acceleration error reduction and peak velocity preservation on 50 high-speed motion sequences from AthletePose3D. Results show that SmoothNet reduces acceleration error by 38.2% while preserving 92.3% of peak velocities, compared to 87.5% for Gaussian filtering with equivalent jitter reduction. This indicates that SmoothNet’s learned smoothing adapts better than fixed filters. For applications requiring maximal motion sharpness (e.g., biomechanical impact analysis), smoothing strength can be adjusted via hyperparameter tuning at the cost of partial jitter suppression.

Fourth, performance degrades under extreme lighting or heavy motion blur due to deteriorated 2D feature quality. Fifth, the model trained primarily on indoor datasets shows limited generalization to in-the-wild scenarios. Preliminary evaluation on 20 outdoor videos reveals a 12–15% MPJPE increase compared to indoor settings, suggesting that domain adaptation techniques are necessary for robust outdoor deployment.

Conclusion

This work presents a Transformer-driven framework for 3D human pose estimation and its application to data-driven animation generation. By replacing conventional convolution-based 3D heatmap regression with a template-guided Transformer decoding mechanism, the proposed model effectively captures long-range spatial dependencies while reducing computational redundancy—achieving a 21.9% reduction in GPU memory consumption compared to deconvolution-based approaches. SmoothNet is incorporated as an external post-processing module to improve temporal consistency, and its role is explicitly separated from backbone contribution through with/without-SmoothNet analyses. Across H3WB, AGORA, and AthletePose3D, the integrated pipeline shows competitive spatial-temporal performance, while the fairness-controlled H3WB protocol provides the basis for component-wise attribution. Ablation studies confirm that both the temporal smoothing module and the newly introduced loss components contribute to accuracy and sequence consistency. The pipeline’s effectiveness is further validated through its deployment in Blender and Unreal Engine, where retargeted animations exhibit reduced jitter, improved motion continuity, and enhanced overall realism.

Despite these advances, several limitations warrant discussion. First, performance degrades under severe occlusions (>50% body coverage) or extreme lighting conditions due to the method’s reliance on ResNet-based 2D feature extraction. Second, SmoothNet introduces a trade-off between smoothness and motion sharpness; high-frequency movements may be slightly attenuated, with approximately 92.3% of peak velocities preserved in high-speed sequences. Third, the model exhibits a 12–15% MPJPE increase on outdoor videos compared to indoor datasets, indicating limited generalization to unconstrained environments. Fourth, extreme poses with significant self-contact remain challenging due to SMPL prior constraints and training data bias.

Future work will address these limitations through several directions. First, exploring lightweight Transformer variants for real-time deployment, potentially leveraging efficient attention mechanisms or model pruning to achieve inference speeds exceeding 50 FPS on consumer hardware. Second, incorporating domain adaptation techniques for in-the-wild scenarios, such as unsupervised domain alignment or test-time adaptation, to improve robustness to diverse backgrounds and camera viewpoints. Third, integrating tighter coupling with physics-based animation systems to ensure biomechanical plausibility, enforcing physical constraints like ground contact and balance during pose optimization. Fourth, extending the framework to multi-person scenarios with occlusion handling, enabling animation generation for crowded scenes. Finally, investigating adaptive smoothing strategies that dynamically adjust temporal filtering strength based on motion characteristics, optimally balancing jitter suppression with motion sharpness preservation.
