Rheumatic Heart Disease in Pregnancy in Sub-Saharan Africa: A Case Series Emphasizing Gaps in Access to Definitive Care
Authors: Pearson Mwemezi, Gissela B Nyakunga, Alex F Lwiza, Simon C Peter, Hendrina Lazarus, Hellen Kyilyosudu, Beatrice Kabuka, Abid M Sadiq
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRheumatic heart disease (RHD) remains a major cause of maternal cardiovascular morbidity and mortality in low-resource settings, particularly among women of reproductive age. Physiological changes during pregnancy can unmask previously undiagnosed disease or precipitate decompensation in those with established valvular lesions. We present a case series of three women with RHD during pregnancy and the early postpartum period, highlighting the burden, clinical presentation, and management challenge in a resource-limited setting. All patients presented with advanced disease, including severe mitral stenosis, atrial fibrillation, and heart failure, with one case complicated by multivalvular involvement and reduced left ventricular function. Management focused on hemodynamic stabilization using diuretics, rate control, anticoagulation, and multidisciplinary care. Definitive interventions such as percutaneous balloon mitral valvotomy or valve replacement were indicated but deferred due to limited availability and financial constraints. All three mothers were clinically stabilized and remained alive at short-term follow-up, although one preterm neonate died from respiratory distress syndrome and Case 3 required readmission because of medication nonadherence. This series underscores the persistent burden of RHD in pregnancy in sub-Saharan Africa and highlights critical gaps in access to definitive cardiac interventions and anticoagulation monitoring. Strengthening health systems, improving access to specialized cardiac care, and implementing preventive strategies are essential to reduce morbidity and mortality associated with RHD in pregnancy in resource-limited settings.Keywords: rheumatic heart disease, pregnancy, mitral stenosis, cardio-obstetric care, anticoagulation, valve intervention, sub-Saharan Africa, case report

Introduction

Rheumatic heart disease (RHD) remains one of the leading causes of acquired valvular heart disease among women of reproductive age in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), with sub-Saharan Africa continuing to bear a disproportionate share of the global burden despite substantial reductions in high-income settings.1,2 In endemic regions, RHD remains a major contributor to maternal cardiovascular morbidity and mortality because many women present with advanced valvular disease during their reproductive years, often after years of limited access to early diagnosis, secondary prophylaxis, and specialist cardiac care.3,4 Recent evidence from sub-Saharan Africa further demonstrates that pregnant women with RHD frequently experience heart failure, atrial arrhythmias, preterm delivery, and maternal death, while only a minority undergo definitive valve intervention during pregnancy or the early postpartum period. 5

Pregnancy represents a period of marked hemodynamic stress that can unmask previously undiagnosed RHD or precipitate clinical deterioration in women with established valvular disease.6,7 Physiological increases in plasma volume, heart rate, stroke volume, and cardiac output substantially increase transmitral flow across stenotic valves, making women with moderate-to-severe mitral stenosis particularly susceptible to pulmonary edema, heart failure, atrial fibrillation, thromboembolism, and adverse fetal outcomes, including preterm birth and low birth weight.3,8 Women with multivalvular disease or impaired ventricular function face an even greater risk of maternal and perinatal complications throughout pregnancy and the early postpartum period.9-11

Contemporary international guidelines advocate early risk stratification, multidisciplinary pregnancy-heart team care, individualized anticoagulation strategies, and timely definitive intervention, including percutaneous balloon mitral commissurotomy (PBMC) for suitable patients with severe symptomatic mitral stenosis.6,7 However, translating these recommendations into routine practice remains challenging in many LMICs because of delayed diagnosis, shortages of specialized multidisciplinary services, and limited availability of catheter-based and surgical valve interventions.1,2 Inadequate anticoagulation monitoring and financial barriers further contribute to delays in definitive care in many endemic regions. 4 A recent scoping review similarly identified inequitable access to specialist care, valve intervention, anticoagulation services, and coordinated multidisciplinary management as major contributors to disparities in maternal outcomes among pregnant women with RHD worldwide. 12

These challenges are particularly evident in East Africa, where women commonly present with advanced rheumatic valve disease after prolonged periods of untreated or undertreated illness.13,14 In Tanzania, access to PBMC, valve replacement surgery, and specialized cardio-obstetric services remains limited, often necessitating prolonged medical therapy and referral to distant tertiary cardiac centers. 15 Consequently, clinicians are frequently required to balance evidence-based recommendations with pragmatic management strategies dictated by available resources.

Although previous studies have described maternal and fetal outcomes associated with RHD during pregnancy, relatively few have specifically examined the practical barriers to delivering guideline-recommended definitive care in resource-limited settings. This case series describes three women with advanced rheumatic valvular heart disease managed during pregnancy and the early postpartum period at a tertiary referral center in Tanzania. By highlighting multidisciplinary management alongside barriers to definitive valve intervention, anticoagulation monitoring, and referral pathways, these cases illustrate the real-world challenges of implementing evidence-based care for pregnant women with RHD in sub-Saharan Africa.

The reporting of this case series conforms to the CARE (CAse REport) Statement: Consensus-based Clinical Case Reporting Guideline and was prepared in accordance with the EQUATOR Network recommendations 16 (Supplementary File 1). This study is a descriptive case series of three patients with rheumatic heart disease managed during pregnancy and the early postpartum period at a tertiary referral center. Clinical information was obtained from contemporaneous medical records, including patient history, physical examination, laboratory investigations, electrocardiography, echocardiography, treatment, and follow-up data. No inferential statistical analyses, confidence intervals, hypothesis testing, regression analyses, survival analyses, risk prediction models, or other analytical modeling techniques were performed because the objective was to provide descriptive clinical evidence rather than statistical inference.

Case Presentation

Case 1

A 33-year-old, gravida 8, para 7, woman presented at approximately 26–28 weeks of gestation by dates with progressive dyspnea that had worsened over the preceding two days after a month of intermittent exertional breathlessness (NYHA III to IV). She reported orthopnea, palpitations, generalized body swelling, reduced urine output, and a dry cough. She denied prior chronic medical illness but reported using herbal medications in the weeks before presentation. On examination, she was alert and oriented with a blood pressure of 108/64 mmHg, an irregular pulse of 114 beats per minute, a respiratory rate of 19 breaths per minute, and an oxygen saturation of 98% on room air. Cardiovascular examination revealed a displaced apex beat and a mid-diastolic murmur at the mitral area. Jugular venous distension, bilateral basal crackles, and peripheral pitting edema were present.

Laboratory evaluation demonstrated white blood count (WBC) 9.49 × 109/L, hemoglobin 10.8 g/dL, and platelets 207 × 109/L. Renal function showed serum creatinine of 105 µmol/L with estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) of 56 mL/min/1.73 m2. Electrolytes revealed sodium 128.3 mmol/L and potassium 3.84 mmol/L. Coagulation testing showed prothrombin time 25.1 seconds with international normalized ratio (INR) 2.52. Malaria and venereal disease research laboratory testing were negative. Chest radiography demonstrated marked cardiomegaly with bilateral pleural effusions and pulmonary congestion (Figure 1A). Electrocardiography revealed atrial fibrillation with an irregular ventricular response (Figure 2A). Transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated biatrial enlargement and a thickened, restricted mitral valve, with a mitral valve area of approximately 1.3 cm2 and elevated transmitral gradients (peak and mean gradients of 26 and 14 mmHg, respectively, on serial measurements) (Figure 3A and B). Mild–moderate mitral regurgitation (Figure 3C) was present along with moderate tricuspid regurgitation (velocity 3.19 m/s) and mild aortic regurgitation. Left ventricular systolic function was preserved with left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) ≈55%, and no intracardiac thrombus was identified.

The multidisciplinary obstetric and cardiology teams initiated treatment aimed at stabilizing maternal hemodynamics while maintaining fetal safety. Fluid intake was restricted to approximately 1 liter per day, and intravenous furosemide 40 mg three times daily was administered with close monitoring of urine output and electrolytes. Rate control for atrial fibrillation was achieved with carvedilol 6.25 mg twice daily. Because of the high thromboembolic risk associated with rheumatic mitral stenosis and atrial fibrillation, anticoagulation was initiated with enoxaparin during hospitalization with a plan for transition to warfarin 5 mg once daily after delivery for long-term anticoagulation.

The patient improved clinically with resolution of pulmonary congestion and stabilization of hemodynamics. Given the financial constraints and limited access to definitive cardiac intervention, valve replacement was deferred, and outpatient cardiology follow-up was arranged. Following multidisciplinary management involving the cardiology, obstetric, anesthesiology, and neonatal teams, she developed spontaneous preterm labor at 34+3 weeks’ gestation and delivered a 1.2 kg male infant vaginally. The neonate had Apgar scores of 3 and 5 at 1 and 5 minutes, respectively, required admission to the neonatal intensive care unit, and died two days later from respiratory distress syndrome. The mother remained hemodynamically stable during the immediate postpartum period and was transitioned from enoxaparin to warfarin for long-term anticoagulation. At follow-up, she remained on conservative medical therapy while awaiting mitral valve replacement because of financial constraints.

Case 2

A 33-year-old, gravida 5 para 4, woman presented at approximately 27–29 weeks of gestation with worsening respiratory symptoms and progressive bilateral lower-limb swelling over five days. She reported a two-year history of productive cough that had recently worsened and described progressive dyspnea that had advanced from exertional breathlessness to dyspnea at rest. Additional symptoms included chest tightness, palpitations, intermittent fevers, night sweats, and two days of blood-streaked sputum.

On admission she appeared mildly dyspneic with blood pressure 90/56 mmHg, pulse 115 beats per minute, respiratory rate 26 breaths per minute, and oxygen saturation 94% on room air, later improving to 99%. Conjunctival pallor and bilateral pitting edema were present. Lung examination revealed reduced bilateral air entry with basal crackles, while cardiac auscultation demonstrated a grade 3 diastolic murmur at the mitral area. Obstetric ultrasound confirmed a viable intrauterine pregnancy with appropriate fetal heart rate and amniotic fluid volume.

Laboratory testing showed WBC 13.15 × 109/L, hemoglobin 9.5 g/dL, mean corpuscular volume 88.6 fL, and platelets 239 × 109/L. The erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) was 80 mm/hr on admission and decreased to 39 mm/hr at discharge. Renal function tests revealed serum creatinine 47 µmol/L, eGFR 142 mL/min/1.73 m2, and urea 3.4 mmol/L. Electrolytes were sodium 143 mmol/L and potassium 4.6 mmol/L. Liver enzymes showed aspartate aminotransferase 34.1 U/L and alanine aminotransferase 30.4 U/L, and coagulation parameters were prothrombin time 10.6 seconds with INR 1.0. Sputum testing for tuberculosis using GeneXpert was negative, and cultures showed no growth. Chest radiography demonstrated increased bronchovascular markings and mixed interstitial infiltrates in the lower zones with mild pleural effusion, consistent with pulmonary edema (Figure 1B). Electrocardiography showed sinus tachycardia with T-wave inversion in the anterior precordial leads (Figure 2B).

Transthoracic echocardiography revealed severe rheumatic mitral stenosis, with a mitral valve area of approximately 0.7 cm2 and mean transmitral gradient of 18 mmHg, along with a Wilkins score of 7 (Figure 3D–F. The left atrium was markedly dilated, but left ventricular systolic function was preserved with LVEF 66%, and right ventricular function was normal with tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion (TAPSE) 18 mm.

The patient was treated with empirical intravenous ceftriaxone 1 g twice daily for five days because pneumonia was considered in the initial differential diagnosis and with aggressive diuresis using intravenous furosemide 40 mg three times daily, later transitioned to oral furosemide 40 mg twice daily. For rate control and heart failure management, she was started on carvedilol 3.125 mg twice daily. Serial fetal monitoring was performed throughout hospitalization. Although balloon mitral valvuloplasty was discussed as a potential pregnancy-preserving intervention, financial limitations and resource constraints precluded immediate intervention, and the multidisciplinary team elected to continue medical optimization with referral for possible intervention postpartum.

Her clinical status improved significantly with treatment, with resolution of edema and improvement in dyspnea. She was discharged hemodynamically stable with a referral for advanced cardiac care. Following multidisciplinary management involving the cardiology, obstetric, anesthesiology, and neonatal teams, she subsequently delivered vaginally at 37+2 weeks’ gestation to a 1.8-kg female infant with Apgar scores of 7 and 9 at 1 and 5 minutes, respectively. Both the immediate postpartum and neonatal courses were uneventful. At follow-up, the mother remained on conservative medical therapy while awaiting mitral valve intervention because of financial constraints, and the infant remained healthy.

Case 3

A 29-year-old para 5 woman presented in the early postpartum period approximately 12 days after spontaneous vaginal delivery at 37–39 weeks of gestation. She delivered a 2.1-kg infant with an Apgar score of 8 at 1 minute. During pregnancy, she had experienced progressive dyspnea that worsened markedly after delivery and was accompanied by orthopnea, paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnea, productive cough, palpitations, oliguria, and bilateral lower-limb swelling. At the referring facility, she received two units of packed red blood cells for severe postpartum anemia before being referred to our center for further management.

On arrival she appeared ill with moderate pallor and bilateral pitting edema extending to the knees. Vital signs included blood pressure 98/67 mmHg, heart rate 118 beats per minute with an irregularly irregular rhythm, respiratory rate 18 breaths per minute, and oxygen saturation 98% on nasal prongs. Cardiac examination revealed a displaced apex beat, gallop rhythm, a pansystolic murmur at the mitral area, and a rough systolic murmur at the aortic area. Jugular venous distension and bilateral basal crepitations were also present.

Laboratory evaluation demonstrated hemoglobin 7.4 g/dL, WBC 10.35 × 109/L, and platelets 246 × 109/L. Renal function showed serum creatinine at 93 µmol/L, with electrolytes sodium at 138 mmol/L and potassium at 2.84 mmol/L. Inflammatory markers included C-reactiveprotein 58.4 mg/L and ESR 80 mm/hr, while troponin measured 0.087 ng/mL. Chest radiography revealed marked cardiomegaly with diffuse interstitial pulmonary edema and a small right pleural effusion (Figure 1C). Electrocardiography demonstrated atrial fibrillation with rapid ventricular response, with rates reaching 168 beats per minute (Figure 2C).

Transthoracic echocardiography showed severe rheumatic multivalvular disease, including severe mitral stenosis with a mitral valve area of approximately 0.5 cm2 and a mean gradient of 18 mmHg (Figures 3G and 3H), moderate mitral regurgitation, and severe aortic stenosis with an aortic valve area of approximately 0.5 cm2 accompanied by moderate aortic regurgitation (Figure 3I). Both atria were markedly dilated, right ventricular function was mildly reduced (TAPSE 15 mm), and left ventricular systolic function was reduced with LVEF 39%.

Management focused on stabilizing heart failure and controlling atrial fibrillation in the postpartum period. She received intravenous furosemide, initially 20 mg twice daily, which was escalated according to clinical response. Rate control was achieved with carvedilol, later transitioned to bisoprolol 10 mg once daily. Guideline-directed heart-failure therapy was initiated with enalapril 2.5 mg once daily, spironolactone 12.5 mg once daily, and dapagliflozin 10 mg once daily. Dapagliflozin was initiated after counseling the patient to discontinue breastfeeding because of the limited safety data regarding sodium-glucose cotransporter-2 inhibitors during lactation. Anticoagulation for atrial fibrillation and rheumatic valvular disease was started with warfarin 5 mg once daily, and potassium chloride 20 mmol every eight hours was administered to correct hypokalemia. Iron supplementation with ferrous sulfate 200 mg once daily was also prescribed.

Her clinical status improved with medical optimization, and she was discharged home with cardiology follow-up and referral for double-valve replacement surgery. During her postpartum admission, management was coordinated by the cardiology, obstetric, anesthesiology, nursing, and cardiac surgery referral teams, with optimization of heart failure therapy, anticoagulation, correction of anemia and electrolyte abnormalities, and counseling regarding definitive surgical intervention. However, she was readmitted two weeks later after discontinuing her medications because of financial constraints, requiring further medical optimization and adherence counseling before discharge. At subsequent follow-up, she remained on conservative medical therapy while awaiting double-valve replacement because of financial constraints, while the infant remained healthy.

Discussion

All three cases demonstrate classical RHD complications in pregnancy: symptomatic severe mitral stenosis (Cases 1–3), atrial fibrillation with rapid ventricular response (Cases 1 & 3), marked left- and right-sided valvular involvement (Case 3), heart failure physiology and cardiomegaly on chest imaging, and anemia or inflammatory markers that commonly coexist in LMIC presentations. Management in each instance prioritized maternal hemodynamic stabilization, fetal surveillance, and referral for definitive valve intervention where feasible. The international guidelines agree that pregnancy in women with severe, symptomatic mitral stenosis (especially valve area ≤1.5 cm2 or mean gradient elevated despite medical therapy) warrants early multidisciplinary evaluation and consideration of PBMC as the preferred definitive, pregnancy-preserving intervention if anatomy is favorable and the center has experience.3,8-10 Cases 1 and 2 had valve areas (≈1.3 cm2 and 0.7 cm2, respectively) consistent with symptomatic severe mitral stenosis; PBMC would be guideline-indicated where available. Case 3 illustrates greater complexity (severe mitral stenosis plus severe aortic stenosis and reduced LVEF), where percutaneous options are unlikely to address the full lesion set and cardiac surgery (often after pregnancy) may be required.

Key medical priorities in symptomatic RHD during pregnancy are (1) treating congestion (judicious diuresis), (2) controlling rate in atrial fibrillation, (3) optimizing oxygenation and anemia, (4) thromboprophylaxis for atrial fibrillation or severe mitral stenosis, and (5) continuous fetal monitoring during acute management.6,7,9,11 Loop diuretics are effective and generally safe in pregnancy when required for congestion; fluid restriction and careful electrolyte monitoring are necessary. Beta-blockers (e.g., metoprolol, bisoprolol, and carvedilol) are commonly used for rate control; avoid drugs with clear fetal risk profiles unless indicated. Severe mitral stenosis with atrial fibrillation confers high thromboembolic risk. Guidelines recommend anticoagulation tailored to gestational age, lesion type, and valve prosthesis status: low molecular weight heparin (LMWH) (weight-adjusted, with anti-Xa monitoring where available) is commonly used in pregnancy, but for women with mechanical valves some guidance still supports vitamin K antagonists in the 2nd/3rd trimester with careful counseling due to maternal thrombotic risk versus fetal warfarin embryopathy risk in the 1st trimester. In settings lacking reliable anti-Xa or INR monitoring, clinicians face difficult trade-offs, highlighted in our cases where enoxaparin was bridged to warfarin postpartum.6,7,17

Practical note from the cases: where routine laboratory monitoring (anti-Xa for LMWH or frequent INR for warfarin) is inconsistently available, clinicians should document the monitoring plan, use weight-based LMWH dosing, and prioritize early referral to centers able to support monitoring or provide definitive intervention.

PBMC has the strongest evidence base as the pregnancy-preserving intervention for symptomatic, pliable mitral stenosis and is associated with improved maternal hemodynamics and favorable fetal outcomes when performed by experienced operators with fetal shielding and minimized fluoroscopy exposure.10,11 Randomized data are lacking, but large case series and systematic reviews support safety and efficacy. For women with favorable valve morphology (low Wilkins score) in whom medical therapy fails, PBMC should be offered irrespective of gestational age if feasible and is generally preferred when intervention cannot be deferred.

Barriers in resource-limited settings include lack of catheterization labs, trained operators, and cost, factors that explain why Cases 1 and 2 were managed conservatively and referred. When PBMC is not available, optimizing medical therapy and arranging timely referral (or planned early delivery in extreme cases with fetal viability) is a pragmatic compromise.

Complex multivalvular involvement with hemodynamic compromise (Case 3: severe mitral stenosis and severe aortic stenosis with reduced LVEF) often requires valve replacement(s). Cardiac surgery during pregnancy is high risk but may be lifesaving when medical and percutaneous options are exhausted; it is ideally performed in a center with cardiac surgery and obstetric critical-care support. When surgery is required postpartum, the timing should balance maternal recovery and neonatal considerations.6,7,10,11

Anticoagulation in RHD with atrial fibrillation or prosthetic valves is one of the most challenging areas in pregnancy medicine. Recent reviews underscore that (1) for native valve mitral stenosis with atrial fibrillation, therapeutic LMWH is acceptable with anti-Xa monitoring when available and is often used until delivery, with transition plans for delivery and postpartum care6,7; and (2) for mechanical prosthetic valves, no anticoagulant strategy is risk-free. Continued vitamin K antagonists (warfarin) protect the mother but increase fetal teratogenic risk in the first trimester; dose-adjusted LMWH with anti-Xa monitoring carries higher maternal valve thrombosis risk if monitoring is inadequate. Local resource constraints (absent anti-Xa) substantially increase the risk of either thrombosis or fetal harm. 17

Clinical implication from the series: in settings where monitoring is unreliable, shared decision-making with the patient and family, plus early referral to centers with capability for monitoring or mechanical valve management, is essential, and documentation and backup emergency plans (e.g., for valve thrombosis) should be in place.

The three cases highlight several modifiable health-system gaps that extend beyond individual patient management and have important public health implications for sub-Saharan Africa. First, strengthening antenatal cardiovascular screening, including early cardiac auscultation, timely echocardiography for women with suggestive symptoms or murmurs, and standardized referral pathways to specialist cardio-obstetric services, could facilitate earlier diagnosis before the onset of advanced heart failure or life-threatening complications.6,7,9 Second, improving access to definitive interventions, including PBMC and valve surgery through regional cardiac centers, streamlined referral networks, and financial protection mechanisms, would reduce delays in definitive treatment that were evident in all three cases.1,2,15 Third, strengthening anticoagulation services by expanding access to point-of-care INR testing, ensuring a reliable supply of anticoagulant medications, establishing standardized anticoagulation monitoring protocols, and providing structured patient education and adherence support could reduce thromboembolic complications among women with rheumatic valvular disease and atrial fibrillation.7,12,17 Preventive strategies should also remain a priority through early diagnosis of acute rheumatic fever, implementation of secondary benzathine penicillin prophylaxis to prevent disease progression, and improved transition of adolescents and young women with RHD into specialized reproductive healthcare services.1,4,9 Finally, integrated multidisciplinary cardio-obstetric care involving cardiologists, obstetricians, anesthesiologists, nurses, neonatologists, and cardiac surgeons should be strengthened through standardized management protocols and regular multidisciplinary case discussions to optimize maternal and neonatal outcomes in resource-limited settings.6,7,9,12

This article recommends that (1) early multidisciplinary triage for any pregnant woman with suspected moderate-severe RHD or new arrhythmia be done by involving cardiology, obstetrics, anesthesia, and neonatology early;6,7 (2) use medical stabilization aggressively while arranging definitive options and document fetal monitoring and timing of planned delivery; (3) evaluate suitability for PBMC and refer urgently if PBMC is available, and when not available, arrange prioritized referral to a higher-level center;6,7,9-11 (4) anticoagulation, especially if atrial fibrillation/severe mitral stenosis is present, start therapeutic anticoagulation per guideline algorithms, and if monitoring is limited, favor strategies that minimize maternal risk and document an escalation plan;14,17 and (5) for policy advocacy, it is suggested to compile local outcome data to inform health-system prioritization and funding.

This case series has several important limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, it describes only three patients managed at a single tertiary referral center, limiting the generalizability of the observations to other populations and healthcare settings. Second, as an observational case series, it is intended to describe clinical presentations and management experiences rather than establish causal relationships or evaluate treatment effectiveness. Third, the follow-up period was relatively short, precluding assessment of long-term maternal cardiovascular outcomes, durability of medical management, subsequent definitive valve interventions, and longer-term neonatal outcomes. Fourth, management decisions were influenced by substantial resource constraints, including limited availability of PBMC, cardiac surgery, anticoagulation monitoring, and financial barriers to care, which may differ considerably from practice in well-resourced settings. Finally, advanced investigations, including routine transesophageal echocardiography, cardiac magnetic resonance imaging, and serial biomarker assessment, were not routinely available and therefore could not be incorporated into patient evaluation. Nevertheless, these cases accurately reflect the real-world challenges encountered in managing rheumatic heart disease during pregnancy in many resource-limited regions of sub-Saharan Africa and highlight important gaps in access to definitive cardiac care that warrant further investigation through larger prospective multicenter studies.

Future research should focus on generating high-quality evidence to address the health-system gaps highlighted by these cases. Prospective multicenter registries across sub-Saharan Africa are needed to better characterize maternal and fetal outcomes, determine the proportion of women eligible for and receiving definitive valve interventions, and identify factors associated with delayed referral and adverse outcomes.5,9 Implementation research should evaluate pragmatic strategies to strengthen referral pathways, improve affordability and timely access to PBMC and valve surgery, and optimize anticoagulation monitoring and adherence in resource-limited settings.1,2,12 In addition, studies evaluating integrated cardio-obstetric care models, including dedicated maternal-cardiac clinics, standardized multidisciplinary management protocols, and tele-cardiology referral networks, may identify scalable approaches to improving access to specialist care and reducing preventable maternal and neonatal complications.6,7,9 Finally, health economic evaluations are needed to determine the cost-effectiveness of expanding definitive valve interventions, anticoagulation monitoring infrastructure, and comprehensive RHD care programs in endemic regions to inform health policy and resource allocation.1,2

Conclusion

RHD in pregnancy remains a high-risk, high-impact condition in endemic, resource-limited settings. International guidelines provide clear pathways (multidisciplinary pregnancy-heart teams, PBMC for suitable mitral stenosis, and careful anticoagulation), but implementation requires sustained investment in procedural capacity, anticoagulation monitoring infrastructure, financial protection mechanisms, and primary prevention. In contexts like ours, safe, pragmatic care blends guideline-directed medical stabilization with expedited referral to centers capable of PBMC or valve surgery, alongside public-health measures to prevent RHD progression in young women.
