A core stemness-associated module reveals PLK1, NUF2, KIF23, CDCA8, TOP2A, CENPF, AURKA, and ASPM as key genes in rectal cancer
Authors: Biao Yao, Dan Yang, Chengfeng Fu, Song Deng, Lifeng Yang, Lei Tian
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRectal cancer (RC) presents significant clinical challenges due to a high risk of recurrence and therapy resistance, which are largely driven by cancer stem cells (CSCs). To address the critical need for RC-specific CSC biomarkers and targets, this study aimed to leverage single-cell transcriptomics and high-dimensional co-expression network analysis (hdWGCNA) to define a core stemness-associated signature and identify potential therapeutic vulnerabilities.Materials and methodsWe built a single-cell atlas (GSE199726) and applied hdWGCNA to the CSC-like compartment, then validated findings across TCGA–READ and GEO GSE90627 cohorts. Pathway/immune features were assessed by clusterProfiler, GSEA, CIBERSORT, and ESTIMATE. Drug candidates were nominated by Enrichr/DSigDB and molecular docking. Wet-lab assays included qRT–PCR (SW620/Caco-2 vs HIEC-6), PLK1 knockdown (si-PLK1#1/#2), wound healing, transwell, and CCK-8 at different time courses (0/24/48/72 h).ResultsOur analysis identified a CSC-enriched subpopulation from the single-cell atlas of RC, from which hdWGCNA derived four co-expression modules. This yielded an eight-gene panel (PLK1, NUF2, KIF23, CDCA8, TOP2A, CENPF, AURKA, ASPM) that was consistently upregulated in tumors across TCGA and GEO cohorts, correlated with G2/M checkpoint and E2F/MYC pathways, and associated with an immune-altered microenvironment. Crucially, this yielded an eight-gene panel that demonstrated exceptional diagnostic accuracy (AUC > 0.8) and significant prognostic value. In vitro, all eight genes were elevated in cancer cells; si-PLK1 efficiently reduced PLK1 expression and curtailed migration, invasion, and proliferation across time.DiscussionBy integrating single-cell atlasing with hdWGCNA, we identified an eight-gene rectal CSC signature that captures a G2/M-stemness program, which generalized across cohorts and was functionally substantiated through PLK1 knockdown in vitro. This tractable vulnerability, anchored by kinases PLK1/AURKA, provides a compact biomarker readout for risk stratification and preclinical therapeutic screening, warranting further in vivo and clinical validation.ConclusionsAn eight-gene CSC signature captures a stemness-linked G2/M program that generalizes across cohorts, relates to the microenvironment, and is therapeutically tractable via kinase targeting, providing a compact readout for risk stratification and preclinical screening.

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) remains a major contributor to the global cancer burden. In 2020, there were more than 1.9 million new CRC cases and approximately 930,000 deaths worldwide, underscoring a sustained need for improved prevention and treatment strategies [1, 2]. Among CRC cases, rectal cancer (RC) accounts for over 35% of all new CRC diagnoses and deaths, thereby playing a key role in the high incidence and mortality of gastrointestinal malignancies [3]. Disturbingly, the incidence of CRC among young adults—who are not currently covered by routine screening guidelines—has nearly doubled over the past decades, making CRC the leading cause of cancer-related death in young adult men [4]. Notably, RC is a major contributor to this worrying trend: by the end of the current decade, a 124% increase in RC patients under the age of 35 is anticipated [5]. Although neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy, total mesorectal excision, and molecularly guided systemic therapy have improved outcomes, 5-year relative survival for distant-stage disease hovers near 15%, reflecting persistent therapeutic resistance and frequent recurrence [6–8].

The cancer stem cell (CSC) paradigm provides a key framework to explain CRC aggressiveness, heterogeneity, and treatment resistance: a small stem-like subpopulation with self-renewal and multilineage potential sustains tumor growth, drives recurrence and metastasis, and diminishes therapeutic efficacy via immune evasion [9]. At the marker level, CD44, CD133, and EpCAM are repeatedly used for identification and functional studies of CRC/rectal CSCs; notably, CD44 splice isoforms correlate with invasion, metastasis, and poor prognosis and thus have prognostic and therapeutic relevance [10]. In parallel, multiple integrative and clinical studies indicate that high EpCAM expression is associated with worse overall and disease-free survival, especially in gastrointestinal tumors [11]. These observations are consistent with a broader conceptual synthesis of cancer immune evasion mechanisms that reinforce resistance and relapse [12]. However, the field still lacks systematic approaches to identify core gene regulatory programs driving the CSC state in rectal cancer beyond these conventional markers, and how such programs may inform novel biomarker discovery and therapeutic targeting remains poorly explored.

The advent of single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) enables lineage- and state-resolved analysis of tumor ecosystems, allowing more precise delineation of CSC subpopulations and their stemness programs [13]. Empirically, Lin et al. developed a robust stemness signature from multi-center scRNA-seq cohorts that identifies CSC populations and associates with patient outcomes, highlighting the clinical utility of single-cell-defined stemness features [14]. Contemporary multimodal single-cell/spatial studies have also charted the cellular ecology and plasticity trajectories across primary CRC and metastases, supporting a network-driven route to translational biomarkers and targets [15, 16]. However, the high sparsity and noise of scRNA-seq challenge conventional co-expression methods [17]. High-dimensional weighted gene co-expression network analysis (hdWGCNA)—through metacell/pseudobulk construction and robust module inference—improves network analysis under sparsity and facilitates the discovery of state-resolved co-expression modules and hub genes [18]. This study aims to systematically decipher the core molecular programs of RC stem cells and explore their potential clinical translational value. By integrating single-cell transcriptomic profiling with high-dimensional gene co-expression network analysis, we successfully identified a key gene module closely associated with stem cell properties and constructed a highly discriminative biomarker combination based on this module. This work offers a novel perspective on understanding RC stem cell heterogeneity. The identified genetic signatures not only provide potential tools for disease risk stratification but also point the way toward developing therapeutic strategies targeting this stem cell subpopulation.

Materials and methods

Data sources

Only de-identified public data sets were used; no additional IRB approval was required.

scRNA-seq: GEO GSE199726; n = 2 RC tumor specimens included for atlas construction and downstream analyses.

TCGA cohort (training set): TCGA–READ via UCSC Xena (GDC Data Release 40); retained primary solid tumor (code 01) and solid tissue normal (code 11); n = 164 tumors and n = 10 adjacent normal tissues.

External validation set: GEO GSE90627; n = 32 RC tumors and n = 96 adjacent normal tissues for independent validation.

Filtering, dimensionality reduction, and clustering of scRNA-seq data

We processed scRNA-seq data in Seurat, performing cell-level quality control by excluding cells with < 200 or > 10,000 detected genes and retaining 12,329 cells for analysis [19]. Normalization and variance stabilization were conducted with SCTransform, followed by principal component analysis (PCA) [20]. Batch effects were corrected in PCA space using Harmony with sample identity as the covariate. UMAP was computed on batch-corrected PCs for visualization and graph construction; a Euclidean KNN graph was built with FindNeighbors using the top 50 PCs, and communities were identified with FindClusters (resolution = 0.2). Cell types were annotated using canonical markers curated from the CellMarker database and the literature.

Differential expression analysis across cell subpopulations

Using SCTransform-normalized and Harmony-corrected data, differential expression between each cluster and all remaining cells was performed in Seurat with FindAllMarkers (two-sided Wilcoxon rank-sum test), specifying logfc.threshold = 0.25, min.pct = 0.30, and only.pos = TRUE to identify cluster-specific upregulated markers. Multiple testing was controlled by Bonferroni correction (reporting P_val_adj), and genes were ranked/visualized with avg_log2FC. The resulting marker sets informed cell-type annotation and downstream functional enrichment.

Functional annotation of cell clusters

Cluster-specific upregulated genes were functionally annotated using clusterProfiler [21].

High-dimensional WGCNA and selection of CSC-related genes

Using hdWGCNA, we imported the Seurat/SCTransform object corrected by Harmony and extracted the CSC cluster expression matrix; thereafter, metacells were constructed to mitigate single-cell sparsity (genes retained if detected in ≥ 5% of cells). A signed co-expression network was built by selecting the soft-threshold β = 8 based on scale-free topology fit, followed by computation of the adjacency matrix and TOM. Modules were identified via dynamic tree cutting and merging, yielding four modules (M1–M4). Genes were prioritized within each module by module eigengene (ME)–CSC trait correlation and intramodular connectivity (kWithin/kIM), together with eigengene-based connectivity (kME); hub genes were defined and forwarded to downstream differential analysis and functional validation.

Differential expression analysis

Using gene-level raw counts from TCGA–READ, we created a DESeqDataSet in DESeq2 with the design ~ condition. Lowly expressed genes were prefiltered. Standard DESeq2 steps were applied to estimate size factors and dispersions [22], followed by Wald tests and Benjamini–Hochberg multiple-testing correction (reporting padj). Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were defined by |log2FC|≥ 1 and padj < 0.01 [23].

Protein–protein interaction (PPI) network and hub gene prioritization

The intersection gene set was mapped to STRING (Homo sapiens), restricting evidence to experiments, databases, and co-expression, with a combined score ≥ 0.70 to define high-confidence interactions. Isolated nodes were removed, and the largest connected component was retained; the weighted edge table (combined score) was exported and imported into Cytoscape 3.8.0 for visualization and analysis. Node centrality was scored with cytoHubba using six algorithms—MCC (Maximal Clique Centrality), MNC (Maximum Neighborhood Component), Degree, EPC (Edge Percolated Component), Closeness, and Radiality—selecting the top 10 nodes per algorithm. The intersection of these top-ranked sets was designated as candidate hub genes.

Immune infiltration analysis

Using bulk transcriptomes, immune cell proportions were inferred with CIBERSORT employing the LM22 signature (sourced from CIBERSORTx); quantile normalization was disabled for RNA-seq (quantile = FALSE), with permutations set to the default (perm = 100) [24]. Group differences in immune infiltration between tumors and adjacent normal tissues were assessed by the Wilcoxon rank-sum test with Benjamini–Hochberg correction for multiple testing. Spearman correlations quantified associations between candidate biomarkers and the estimated fractions of each immune cell type. In parallel, ESTIMATE was applied to compute StromalScore, ImmuneScore, and ESTIMATEScore; tumor-adjacent normal differences were tested by Wilcoxon, and Spearman correlations were calculated between biomarker expression and these scores.

Gene set enrichment analysis

A genome-wide list ranked by the DESeq2 Wald statistic was analyzed using pre-ranked GSEA in GSEA v4.2.2 with MSigDB HALLMARK (human) and 1,000 gene set permutations. NES and P were reported; significance was defined as P < 0.05. For biomarker-specific analyses, samples were dichotomized at the median expression and phenotype-based GSEA was performed with identical gene sets and permutation settings.

Drug prediction and molecular docking

Candidate drugs were prioritized using Enrichr against DSigDB, ranked by adjusted p values; entries that were clearly cell-line-specific or non-drug compounds were excluded, and only candidates with P < 0.05 were advanced. Receptor structures were obtained via UniProt cross-references to PDB, preferring X-ray models with higher resolution (smaller Å) and constructs covering the binding site; among multiple entries, ligand-bound or pocket-defined conformations were prioritized. Small-molecule 3D structures were downloaded from PubChem and energy-minimized in ChemBioOffice. Receptor/ligand preparation was performed in PyMOL and AutoDockTools (removal of waters/heteroatoms, hydrogenation, charge assignment, PDBQT conversion); the docking grid encompassed the known active pocket (e.g., ATP site for kinases). Docking and scoring were carried out with the AutoDockTools/AutoDock suite; hits were retained if binding energy < − 5.0 kcal/mol, and poses were visually validated in PyMOL with key hydrogen-bond/interaction distances < 3.5 Å.

Cell culture and transfection

Human normal intestinal epithelial cells HIEC-6 (CRL-3266, ATCC, Manassas, VA, USA) and human colorectal cancer cell lines SW620 (TCHu101, National Collection of Authenticated Cell Cultures/Shanghai Cell Bank, Shanghai, China) and Caco-2 (SCSP-5027, Shanghai Cell Bank, Shanghai, China) were used. STR identification has been carried out on the cells, and the outcome of mycoplasma detection shows negativity. Cells were cultured in DMEM (PM150210, Procell, Wuhan, China) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (164,210, Procell) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (Procell) under standard conditions (37 °C, 5% CO₂, saturated humidity) in a tri-gas incubator (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Cells were passaged at 70–80% confluence with trypsin–EDTA and were routinely tested to be mycoplasma-free.

For gene silencing, small interfering RNAs targeting PLK1 (si-PLK1) and a non-targeting negative control siRNA were obtained from GenePharma (Shanghai, China). The sequences were: si-PLK1#1, 5′-UAUUCAUUCUUCUUGAUCCGG-3′; si-PLK1#2, 5′-UUCUGUACAAUUCAUAUGGUG-3′. Transfections were performed in SW620 and Caco-2 cells using Lipofectamine 3000 (11,668,027, Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) following the manufacturer’s instructions.

Cell viability assay

Following siRNA transfection, SW620 and Caco-2 cells were seeded at 2 × 103 cells/well in 96-well plates and cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin–streptomycin at 37 °C, 5% CO₂, saturated humidity for 48 h. Cell viability was measured using CCK-8 (C0037, Beyotime) by adding 10 μL/well and incubating 4 h; absorbance at 450 nm was recorded on a microplate reader (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Viability was calculated as (OD450(sample)–OD450(blank))/(OD450(siNC)–OD450(blank)) × 100%. Each condition included ≥ 3 technical replicates and was repeated in ≥ 3 independent experiments.

Quantitative reverse transcription PCR (qRT–PCR)

Total RNA was extracted from cells using TRIzol (15596026CN, Invitrogen, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. RNA concentration and purity were assessed on a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). cDNA was synthesized with the Reverse Transcription Kit (D7178S, Beyotime, Shanghai, China). qPCR was performed using SYBR Green qPCR Mix (D7260, Beyotime) on an Applied Biosystems thermal cycler/real-time PCR system (Applied Biosystems, USA).

The cycling program was: 95 °C for 10 min, then 30 cycles of 95 °C for 15 s, 60 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 1 min, followed by a final extension at 72 °C for 5 min. Relative expression was calculated using the 2⁻ΔΔCt method with GAPDH as the internal control. Primer sequences are listed in Table 1. All reactions were run with technical triplicates and included no-template controls. Primers utilized in this study

Wound-healing assay

Cells were seeded in 6-well plates (2 mL complete medium per well) and maintained at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO₂ until a confluent monolayer was established. A straight scratch was made across each well using a sterile 200-µL pipette tip. Wells were rinsed twice with PBS to remove dislodged cells and then overlaid with serum-free DMEM. Images of the wound area were captured at 0 and 48 h under an inverted microscope. The wound-healing rate was defined as the percentage reduction in the average wound width at 48 h relative to the initial width. Each condition was assayed in triplicate in at least three independent experiments.

Cell invasion assay

Cell invasion was assessed using 24-well transwell inserts pre-coated with Matrigel (pore size 8 µm, 3422, Corning, Corning, NY, USA; C0372, Beyotime, Shanghai, China). After siRNA transfection, SW620 and Caco-2 cells were suspended in serum-free DMEM and seeded into the upper chamber (200 µL/well), while the lower chamber contained 700 µL DMEM + 10% FBS as chemoattractant. Following 48 h incubation at 37 °C, non-invaded cells on the upper surface were gently removed with a cotton swab. Inserts were rinsed with PBS, and invaded cells on the underside were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (P0099, Beyotime) and stained with 0.1% crystal violet (C0121, Beyotime) for 30 min. Images were captured under an inverted microscope (DP27, Olympus, Japan) and invading cells were counted in three random fields per insert. Each condition was tested in triplicate inserts and repeated in ≥ 3 independent experiments.

Flow cytometry

Cells from SW620 and Caco-2 lines that were transfected with either PLK1-specific siRNA or si-NC were harvested, washed with PBS, and then resuspended in 195 μL of annexin-V FITC (BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA) containing 5 μL of propidium iodide (PI), following the provided protocol. After a 10-min incubation period in the dark at room temperature, flow cytometry was employed for analysis. The results were assessed using Lysis software (EPICS-XL, Ramsey, Minnesota, USA).

Statistical analysis

Analyses were performed in R and GraphPad Prism. Data are presented as mean ± s.d. from ≥ 3 independent experiments with ≥ 3 technical replicates unless stated. Two-group comparisons used two-tailed Student’s t tests. Multiple Testing measurements were assessed by one/two-way ANOVA. Adjusted p values are reported where applicable. All tests were two-sided with α = 0.05. The data with p < 0.05 were considered as statistically significant.

Experimental validation in colorectal cell models

To validate the eight biomarkers identified in silico (PLK1, NUF2, KIF23, CDCA8, TOP2A, CENPF, AURKA, ASPM), qRT–PCR showed higher mRNA levels for all eight genes in colorectal cancer cells (SW620, Caco-2) vs normal intestinal epithelial cells (HIEC-6) (Fig. 10A). Given its established role as a master regulator of the cell cycle and a driver of malignancy across diverse cancers [25], PLK1 was selected for further experimental validation. We then transfected si-PLK1 into SW620 and Caco-2; qPCR confirmed efficient knockdown, and si-PLK1#1—which achieved greater suppression—was used for subsequent assays (Fig. 10B). Functionally, si-PLK1#1 significantly reduced migration in wound-healing assays and invasion in Matrigel transwells relative to siNC control (Fig. 10C, D). Consistently, CCK-8 time-course assays (0, 24, 48, 72 h) revealed attenuated proliferation/viability after PLK1 silencing in both cell lines, with significant differences emerging from 24 h onward (Fig. 10E; two-way ANOVA with post-hoc tests, P < 0.05). Finally, we assessed apoptosis levels using flow cytometry. Discussion

This work integrates single-cell atlasing and hdWGCNA with multi-cohort validation to delineate a stemness-linked G2/M program in RC. From the scRNA-seq atlas we resolved a CSC-like compartment, built a signed co-expression network, and prioritized an eight-gene signature (PLK1, NUF2, KIF23, CDCA8, TOP2A, CENPF, AURKA, ASPM) by eigengene traits and intramodular connectivity. The panel generalized across TCGA–READ and an external GEO cohort, tracked with E2F/MYC and G2/M hallmark activity, and associated with stromal/immune features inferred by CIBERSORT and ESTIMATE. Drug-repositioning and docking further nominated kinase targets within this axis (notably PLK1/AURKA). Orthogonal wet-lab assays then substantiated the computational inferences: the eight genes were elevated in CRC cells vs normal intestinal epithelial cells, PLK1 knockdown was efficient, and perturbation curtailed migration, invasion, and proliferation in vitro. In summary, this study represents the first integration of single-cell transcriptomic profiling with hdWGCNA, successfully identifying and validating an eight-gene mitotic-stem cell signature associated with RC stem cell properties. This provides experimentally validated candidate biomarkers and target resources for advancing the biology of RC stem cells.

The eight genes identified in this study are mostly key nodes of mitosis/spindle and mitosis checkpoints. Recent evidence shows that they are closely related to the stemness of tumors. ASPM is increasingly recognized as a cross-cancer hub of stemness regulation linked to aggressive phenotypes [26]; in colorectal settings, reports indicate that ASPM promotes β-catenin nuclear translocation and enhances migration/invasion [27]. Beyond its canonical mitotic roles, AURKA sustains CSC-like states and post-anti-EGFR resistance via the AURKA–YAP1 axis in CRC; AURKA inhibition reverses this phenotype [28], and organoid data suggest selective vulnerability in ARID1A-mutant CRC [29]. As a core member of the Survivin-containing chromosomal passenger complex, CDCA8 has been shown (e.g., 2022 data in prostate cancer) to be overexpressed, to promote proliferation, and—upon silencing—to suppress growth and induce apoptosis, consistent with a stemness–proliferation coupling role [30]. CENPF has recently been implicated in CRC metastasis via USP4-mediated deubiquitination and protein stabilization, underscoring a position at the interface of cell-cycle control, motility, and stemness [31]. KIF23 participates in late mitosis/cytokinesis; 2021 work in CRC cells and clinical specimens demonstrated that KIF23 enhances proliferation and migration and correlates with poor prognosis—functionally coherent with maintenance of “stem-like” programs [32]. As a core component of the NDC80 kinetochore complex, NUF2 is upregulated across cancers (multi-omics 2024) and linked to stemness [33]; in prostate cancer, NUF2 emerges as a principal driver of proliferation/stemness [34]. PLK1, a master kinase at G2/M, is elevated in CRC and associated with metastasis, stemness, and therapeutic resistance; its inhibition curtails migration/invasion and downregulates stemness pathways [35, 36]. Finally, TOP2A has been tied to CRC stemness and oxaliplatin resistance: UPF1-dependent activation/phosphorylation of TOP2A sustains stemness and confers resistance [37]. Collectively, while these mitotic regulators have been individually implicated in stemness pathways, our integrated eight-gene signature captures their coordinated expression as a unified biological program that demonstrates independent prognostic value beyond individual markers. This composite biomarker not only reinforces the fundamental link between cell division machinery and cancer stemness, but may also refine existing molecular stratification systems by quantifying the activity of this core proliferative–stemness axis in rectal tumors.

Our deconvolution results indicate that the eight-gene index is significantly associated with infiltration of multiple immune subsets, while tumor samples display markedly lower ImmuneScore/ESTIMATEScore, consistent with a more immune-cold microenvironment. This observation accords with contemporary models in which high proliferation/spindle defects, chromosomal instability (CIN), cGAS–STING rewiring, immune escape: sustained CIN can shift innate immunity from acute activation toward chronic suppression, overall reducing T-cell infiltration and response to immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) [38, 39]. At the gene level, several recent studies converge on druggable crosstalk between the hubs and immune checkpoints: AURKA signaling inter-relates with PD-L1 and may be pharmacologically tractable (AURKA inhibition can modulate PD-L1 and augment effector cytotoxicity or form positive feedback) [40, 41], PLK1 inhibition shows potential synergy with immunotherapy [42], while TOP2A correlates positively (pan-cancer) with PD-L1 and macrophage infiltration [43]; CDCA8 associates with CD8 +/CD4 + T-cell infiltration [44]; KIF23 links to checkpoint expression/immune responses in gastrointestinal tumors [45]; and NUF2 correlates with multiple immune subsets and predicted ICB responsiveness [46]. Altogether, we posit that the ASPM–AURKA–CDCA8–CENPF–KIF23–NUF2–PLK1–TOP2A network may promote an immune-excluded phenotype in RC—potentially via CIN-driven myeloid/T-cell axis remodeling—and thereby influence immunotherapy sensitivity. These hypotheses are testable using spatial transcriptomics, functional perturbations, and rational combinations pairing cell-cycle inhibitors with ICB.

Guided by enrichment and docking, Lucanthone emerged as a candidate acting on the G2/M–stemness axis through AURKA and PLK1 (predicted binding energies ≈ − 6.5 and − 6.6 kcal/mol, with hydrogen-bond distances in plausible ranges). Over the past 3–5 years, Lucanthone has been characterized as a brain-penetrant autophagy/lysosomal inhibitor that reduces stem-like cells and synergizes with temozolomide in glioma models [47]; it has also been repeatedly reported to inhibit APE1, a DNA-repair factor, suggesting potential to sensitize chemo-/radiotherapy [48]. Clinically relevant comparators exist on the target side: AURKA inhibition reverses anti-EGFR resistance/CSC-like conversion in CRC [28], while the PLK1 inhibitor onvansertib has shown acceptable safety and activity in phase II trials for KRAS-mutant mCRC [49]. Despite these convergences, we emphasize that there is no current in vivo/clinical evidence that Lucanthone directly inhibits AURKA/PLK1 or exerts antitumor efficacy in CRC. Given its dual mechanistic profile (autophagy/stemness and DNA-repair interference) alongside our docking-based feasibility, a pragmatic next step is to test Lucanthone in RC models characterized by AURKA/PLK1 high expression with stem-like/immune-cold features, and to explore combinations with AURKA/PLK1 inhibitors or cytotoxic/radiation regimens.

This study has several limitations that warrant attention. First, the association between the eight-gene signature and the immune rejection phenotype we identified is primarily based on bioinformatics inference and correlation analysis. While consistent with biological logic, it has not yet established a direct causal relationship. Future research will combine spatial multi-omics technologies with genetic perturbation experiments to directly validate the regulatory role of this gene program in the spatial distribution of immune cells while preserving the integrity of the tumor microenvironment. Second, although we have conducted in-depth functional validation of PLK1, the biological functions of other core genes in the signature remain incompletely elucidated. Subsequent studies will progressively perform systematic loss-of-function and gain-of-function experiments on these genes to comprehensively decipher the specific contributions of each member in maintaining tumor stem cell properties. Third, computational drug prediction results require experimental validation. Upon obtaining Lucanthone and related compounds, we plan to immediately conduct in vitro enzyme activity inhibition assays and cellular-level efficacy assessments to confirm their inhibitory effects on PLK1/AURKA and antitumor activity. Fourth, the analysis in this study primarily relies on retrospective public databases. To validate the generalizability of our findings, we are currently establishing a prospective cohort of rectal cancer patients at our center. This cohort will be used to independently validate the prognostic predictive value of the eight-gene signature. Finally, the current study is primarily based on in vitro experimental evidence. We have initiated the establishment of patient-derived organoid models and humanized mouse xenograft models. We will evaluate the therapeutic potential of targeting this gene network within these more clinically relevant systems, providing more compelling preclinical evidence for future clinical translation.

Conclusion

This study systematically identified a mitotic-stem cell signature in RC comprising eight core genes (PLK1, NUF2, KIF23, CDCA8, TOP2A, CENPF, AURKA, ASPM) through the integration of single-cell transcriptomic profiling and high-dimensional co-expression network analysis. This signature demonstrates stable diagnostic and prognostic value across multiple independent cohorts, with its expression pattern significantly correlated with G2/M checkpoint activation and immune microenvironment remodeling. In vitro experiments confirm its specific overexpression in colorectal cancer cells, where core member PLK1 plays a pivotal role in regulating cell migration, invasion, and proliferation. The analytical framework established in this study provides novel methodological support for deciphering the biology of RC stem cells. The identified gene signature lays the molecular foundation for developing precision stratification and therapeutic strategies targeting this distinct tumor subgroup.
