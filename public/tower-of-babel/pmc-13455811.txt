Prognostic Value of Platelet-Associated Biomarkers in Nasopharyngeal Carcinoma Patients Received IMRT: A Retrospective Study
Authors: Yanming Jiang, Jialing Ma, Shiting Huang, Xinbin Pan, Xiaodong Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeThis study examines the prognostic significance of platelet-related biomarkers for overall survival (OS) in nasopharyngeal carcinoma (NPC) using Cox proportional hazards and random survival forest (RSF) models, and compares the two modes.MethodsIn total, 825 patients with confirmed nasopharyngeal carcinoma were included in this study, and a systematic analysis of their prognostic risk factors was conducted. The patient dataset was divided into training and validation datasets at a ratio of 7:3. A Cox model and RSF model were constructed using clinical data, platelet distribution width (PDW), the ratio of platelet distribution width to platelet count (PDW/P), mean platelet volume (MPV), mean platelet volume‒platelet count ratio (MPV/P) and follow-up survival data. The performances of the two prediction models were evaluated.ResultsMultivariate analysis revealed that age (P<0.001, hazard ratios (HR) =1.76, 95% confidence intervals (CI):1.28—2.42), T stage (T3: HR 2.81, 95% CI:1.62—4.83, p<0.001; T4: HR 4.10, 95% CI:1.97—8.52, p<0.001), PDW/P (P= 0.027, HR=1.82, 95% CI:1.07—3.22), and EBV-DNA level (P=0.016, HR=1.51, 95% CI:1.08—2.11) were independent prognostic factors for OS. The RSF model revealed that T stage, stage, the PDW/P and PDW had a notable impact on the model. The Log rank test results showed that when the risk score threshold of the RSF model was 39.63, it had efficacy in differentiating patient groups with significant differences in prognosis. The C-index of the training cohort for the COX model and the RSF model were 0.70 (95% CI: 0.663–0.737) and 0.71 (95% CI, 0.69–0.73) respectively, and 0.62 (95% CI, 0.553–0.681), 0.68 (95% CI:0.62–0.74) in the validation data. Moreover, the decision curve analysis demonstrated that the RSF model provided greater net benefit than the Cox proportional hazards model did.ConclusionThe PDW/P ratio was an independent prognostic factor for OS. The RSF model demonstrated high reliability in predicting clinical outcomes for patients with NPC.

Introduction

Nasopharyngeal carcinoma (NPC) is a common, malignant type of head and neck cancer, and it is an epithelial cancer that originates from the nasopharynx epithelium.1 Compared with other types of cancer, nasopharyngeal carcinoma has an obvious geographical distribution. In 2022, global cancer statistics estimated that 120,416 cases of NPC occurred worldwide, with 73,476 deaths. It ranked 23rd among all newly diagnosed cancers and 21st among total cancer-related deaths.2 In addition, nasopharyngeal cancer is prevalent in East Asia and Southeast Asia, especially in southern China.3 The incidence of NPC is higher in men than in women, and the ratio of men to women with NPC was approximately 2.5:1 in China in 2015.4

Advances in diagnostic imaging techniques, the widespread use of intensity-modulated radiotherapy (IMRT), and the refinement of chemotherapy protocols have markedly increased the survival rates of NPC patients.3 Approximately 90% of early-stage nasopharyngeal carcinoma patients can achieve complete cure through IMRT alone.5 Nonetheless, owing to the concealed anatomical location of the nasopharynx and unusual initial disease symptoms, 70% of patients with nasopharyngeal carcinoma are diagnosed with stage III or IV disease at their first consultation.6 Moreover, up to 30% of nasopharyngeal carcinoma patients with the same TNM stage experience disease progression despite receiving similar treatments as those that respond positively to treatment. Approximately 20–30% of patients with advanced nasopharyngeal carcinoma continue to encounter obstacles in treatment, mainly because of recurrence or metastasis.7 Therefore, there is a clinical need for reliable biomarkers that can accurately predict the prognosis of patients with NPC and for individualized risk stratification.

In addition to being crucial in the pathophysiology of haemostasis and thrombosis, platelets are involved in the development and metastasis of tumours.8 The physiological aspects of platelets are influenced by cancer cells, and activated platelets contribute to every stage of cancer development, promoting tumour growth, blood vessel development, metastasis, and thrombosis associated with cancer.9 Normal platelet counts are maintained via an equilibrium between platelet generation and depletion. Newer platelets are larger and more active than older platelets because they store more particles and receptors. They are more likely to aggregate and be stimulated to release chemical mediators.10 This means that platelet volume reflects parameters, such as the mean platelet volume (MPV), platelet distribution width (PDW), mean platelet volume‒platelet count ratio (MPV/P), platelet distribution width‒platelet count ratio (PDW/P) and plateletcrit (PCT), reflect platelet activation. Increasing evidence suggests a link between high platelet counts and unfavorable outcomes in different cancer types.11–13 It has been confirmed that the platelet count and platelet-lymphocyte ratio (PLR) are important prognostic indicators in NPC.14 Previous studies have shown that in NPC patients, the higher the T-stage, the lower the MPV/P ratio. Conversely, the more advanced the T-stage, the greater the value of PDW.15 In addition, studies have confirmed that the platelet-to-albumin ratio is an independent prognostic factor in NPC patients.16 However, the ability of platelet volume parameters to predict survival in patients with NPC remains unclear. Therefore, we conducted the current study to evaluate the predictive value of the platelet volume index in patients with NPC.

Materials and Methods

Patients and Treatments

Data from locally advanced nasopharyngeal carcinoma (LA-NPC) patients at the Union for International Cancer Control (UICC; 8th edition) who had stage III or IVA disease at the Affiliated Tumour Hospital of Guangxi Medical University between January 2013 and December 2016 were analysed. Patients with a history of other malignancies and incomplete clinicopathologic data were excluded. Additionally, NPC patients with concurrent infections, thrombosis, or blood disorders and those taking medications that affect platelets were excluded (Figure 1). The clinical data collected included age, sex, chemotherapy, clinical stage, radiotherapy dose, pretreatment plasma Epstein-Barr virus DNA copy number, pretreatment platelet (PLT) count, platelet count, mean platelet volume (MPV), and platelet distribution width (PDW), which were calculated for each patient. MPV/P refers to the ratio of the mean platelet volume to the platelet count, and PDW/P refers to the ratio of the platelet distribution width to the platelet count. All patients received concurrent chemoradiotherapy (CCRT) with or without induction chemotherapy(IC). All patients received IMRT at a total of 70.06 to 74.58 Gy given in 31 to 33 fractions to the primary lesion, 60 to 72.64 Gy given in 30 to 33 fractions to the metastatic lymph node volume, 60 to 62 Gy given in 30 to 31 fractions to the high-risk clinical target volume, and 54 to 55.8 Gy given in 30 to 31 fractions to the low-risk clinical target volume. All patients received radiotherapy once a day, 5 times per week. The IC regimens included the GP (gemcitabine 1,000 mg/m2 + cisplatin 80mg/m2), TP (docetaxel 75 mg/m2+cisplatin 75 mg/m2) and TPF (docetaxel 60mg/m2 + cisplatin 60mg/m2+ 5-fluorouracil 3,000 mg/m2), every 21days before concurrent chemoradiotherapy (CCRT), at least two cycles.Figure 1Flowchart of patient selection and exclusion criteria.A flowchart of patient selection for nasopharyngeal carcinoma study from 2013 to 2016.

Flowchart of patient selection and exclusion criteria.

Patient Follow-up

Patients were monitored every 3 months for the first 2 years after the end of treatment. Follow-up assessments were performed every 6 months for 3 to 5 years after treatment. After five years, an annual review was conducted.

Statistical Analysis

The primary endpoint of this study was OS, defined as the interval from the date of diagnosis to death or the most recent follow-up. The optimal cut-off values for the PDW, MPV, PDW/P, MPV/P and PCT for the prediction of OS were determined on the basis of Youden’s index and were calculated by MedCalc software (MedCalc Software Ltd, Ostend, Belgium; https://www.medcalc.org; 2021).

Continuous variables were presented as the mean±SD or median (as appropriate). Statistical analysis was performed using the Mann–Whitney U-test or the Kruskal–Wallis test. Categorical variables are presented as numbers and proportions. Statistical analyses were performed using the χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test. Univariate and multivariate regression analyses were conducted utilizing the Cox proportional hazards model. A two-tailed approach was used for all P value calculations, with statistical significance defined as P < 0.05. IBM SPSS Statistics was used for statistical analysis, version 27.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The patient dataset was divided into a training dataset and a validation dataset at a 7:3 ratio, and Cox univariate analysis was employed to screen out potential prognostic factors affecting OS. Variables with P<0.05 in the univariate analysis were entered into the Cox multivariate analysis, and variables with P<0.05 in the multivariate analysis were entered into the Cox model. A survival prediction model was constructed utilizing the random survival forest (RSF) model in the training set. The accuracy of the predictive model was evaluated by constructing time-dependent receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves. A risk score to indicate patient prognosis was calculated, presenting that the higher risk score was correlated with a worse prognosis, the risk score cut-off point was determined by maximum selection rank statistics. The Kaplan‒Meier method was used to estimate survival distributions. Comparisons of OS were performed using the Log rank test. ROC curves and calibration curves were used to evaluate and compare the sensitivity and specificity of the Cox model and RSF model. In this study, the predictive performance of the Cox model and the RSF model was compared using the net reclassification improvement (NRI) index, the integrated discrimination improvement (IDI) index, and decision curve analysis (DCA). R version 4.4.0 was used for data processing.

For confirming the nomogram’s precision within the validation group, both the C-index and the calibration gradient were utilized, the C-index was 0.62 (95% CI, 0.553–0.681). Random Survival Forest Model

The training dataset was used to construct the RSF model. A total of 1000 decision trees were generated from the replacement bootstrap samples. Four candidate variables (mtry) were randomly selected from the 12 variables for branch partitioning. Branches continue to split until no fewer than 15 deaths (node size) occurred in each terminal branch.

As the tree count increased, the rate of prediction errors varied, and the significance of these variables shifted in the RSF predictive model (Figure 5). Variable importance (VIMP) values were generated for all the features used to grow trees was also generated after the complete construction of 1000 trees, and a higher VIMP values indicated that the variable was important for the model to accurately predict OS. As shown in Figure 5, the four leading factors that had a notable impact on the model included T-stage, stage, PDW/P, and PDW. The variable importance metric derived from the RSF model was used to rank the predictive contribution of each variable to the outcome. It is crucial to note that this measure reflects association and predictive utility within the model framework, not causal influence. Causal inference would require specific study designs and methodologies to account for confounding.Figure 5Results of the random survival forest.Two plots showing error rate by number of trees and variable importance rankings.

Comparing the RSF and Cox Models

Each patient was scored using the nomogram and the RSF model. Based on the predetermined cut-off point in the RSF model and the median risk score in the Cox model, patients were classified into high- and low-risk groups, respectively, after which corresponding survival curves were generated (Figures 9, 10 and 11, 12). Both models effectively distinguished between patients with poor prognosis and those with favourable prognosis, with significantly shorter OS in the poor prognosis cohort than in the favourable prognosis group. DCA was performed to compare the clinical utility of the two models, which indicated a greater net benefit of the RSF model relative to the Cox model (Figure 13). Discussion

Clinical prediction models are widely used in current research, and correct model selection is fundamental.17 The Cox model, the most widely used model,18 is associated with cancer survival and effectively identifies risk factors with simple and easy methods. However, because electronic patient files bring more information, a model that can be easily automated is needed. A good example is the RSF. The RSF model offers two key advantages over Cox regression. First, RSF can readily handle high-dimensional data, while Cox regression is constrained by the linearity and proportional hazards assumptions.19 Second, RSF is capable of capturing complex nonlinear relationships between predictors and outcomes, whereas Cox regression is generally limited to linear associationsNotably, the key predictors identified by the two models differed: the Cox model highlighted age, T-stage, PDW/P, and EBV-DNA, while the RSF model emphasized T-stage, stage, PDW/P, and PDW. These differences reflect the ability of machine learning methods like RSF to model intricate variable interactions and capture complex biological relationships more effectively.

Studies have verified that tumor-induced coagulation dysfunction is intimately connected to the occurrence and development of cancer.20,21 Earlier research has indicated that the combination of the MPV/P and PDW can serve as a significant laboratory marker for distinguishing NPC patients from those with benign nasopharyngeal tumours and healthy individuals.15 This study is the first to reveal the correlations among platelet-related parameters and the prognosis of patients with NPC using an RSF model. This study revealed that the PDW/P strongly correlated with the clinical prognosis of NPC patients. The MPV signifies the activation of platelets, whereas the PDW reflects changes in the volume of platelets. These are frequently employed to differentiate between thrombocytopenia characterized by low yield and severe destruction.22 Many studies have investigated the MPV in individuals with cancer and its relationship with survival. The latest systematic review,23 which investigated the possible association between MPV and 21,034 individuals diagnosed with various forms of cancer, revealed that most studies on gastric, endometrial, and lung cancer patients had increased MPV levels, whereas patients with renal cell carcinoma and gallbladder cancer had reduced MPV. Moreover, gastrointestinal cancer patients with increased MPV24 and reduced MPV25 were found to have poor prognoses, suggesting an unclear relationship. The same conclusion has also been reached in colon cancer studies.26,27 Notably, a recent meta-analysis of lung cancer patients revealed no correlation between the MPV and overall survival.28 In the present study, the MPV was closely associated with the T-stage (P<0.001) and stage (P=0.003); however, there was no relationship with prognosis. These divergent findings suggest distinct effects of various organs, cancer types, or cancer stages on the MPV, necessitating further research with more extensive sample sizes and extended follow-up periods to clarify the precise function of the MPV in cancer development.

According to the univariate analysis, PDW may be a predictor of OS, and the P value was close to 0.05. In the RSF model, the top four variables that significantly influenced the model were T-stage, stage, the PDW/P and PDW. PDW is a haematological indicator that reflects the degree of variation in platelet volume. Compared with the MPV, the PDW is a more specific marker of platelet activation since it does not increase during simple platelet swelling.29 Recent studies have shown that a high PDW is a poor prognostic factor in patients with oesophageal carcinoma,30 laryngeal cancer,31 and gastric cancer.32

Our findings revealed that PDW was correlated with T-stage. Research has shown that elevated PDW is associated with tumour invasion depth.30 A meta-analysis also confirmed that PDW was an effective indicator of cancer prognosis.33 Platelets can protect tumour cells from immune-mediated elimination within the circulatory system, promote tumour cell arrest within the vasculature and affect tumour cell survival, thereby supporting the establishment of secondary lesions.34 Research has confirmed that inflammation plays an important role in the occurrence and development of cancer.35 Inflammation leads to the production of relevant cytokines in the tumour microenvironment, such as IL-1, IL-8, IL-6, IL-18, IL-12, TNF, and IFN-γ, which promote the abnormal maturation of bone marrow cells and megakaryocytes, resulting in increased production and release of premature platelets of different natures and sizes in the cardiovascular system.36 These morphological alterations may lead to increased heterogeneity in platelet size, consequently resulting in elevated PDW values. The PDW/P has been proven to be a good prognostic indicator for many diseases, such as papillary thyroid carcinoma37 and breast cancer.38 In the random forest prognostic model, the PDW/P was also revealed to be an important prognostic indicator. A high PDW/P indicates high platelet activity and relatively low or low platelet count. A significant increase in PDW indicates that the bone marrow is stimulated by strong inflammation to accelerate the production of large numbers of immature and highly reactive platelets. Activated platelets can prevent tumour cells from being lysed, release growth factors, and promote tumour growth and metastasis. However, the platelet was relatively low or low, suggesting that the tumour-related hypercoagulable state, microvascular lesions, and extensive involvement of platelets in the metastasis process result in a platelet consumption rate far exceeding the rate of platelet generation. Moreover, tumour infiltration into the bone marrow and chemotherapy damage the haematopoietic capacity of the bone marrow, which cannot effectively respond to stimulation to generate sufficient platelets. Therefore, a high PDW/P indicates high platelet activity and a low platelet count, indicating that the prognosis of patients with NPC is poor. Pretreatment plasma EBV-DNA levels are a prognostic indicator of OS.39,40 One study revealed that 4000 copies/mL was a significant prognostic factor for OS, which is consistent with the results of the present study.41,42 It has been confirmed that IC before CCRT provides survival benefits for LA-NPC patients,43 and IC combined with CCRT is strongly recommended for LA-NPC patients according to the NCCN and CSCO guidelines.44,45 However, in our research, we found that the survival of patients who received induction chemotherapy did not differ from that of patients who did not, which is related to the following factors: 1) different induction chemotherapy regimens; 2) different induction chemotherapy cycles; and 3) the administration of adjuvant chemotherapy after the end of radiotherapy in some patients. PCT reflects the platelet mass per unit volume, which is obtained by multiplying the PLT count and MPV.46 Recent studies have shown that PCT levels are lower in lung cancer patients than in healthy controls,47 but are higher in papillary thyroid carcinoma patients than in healthy controls.48 Several studies have confirmed that PCT is an important factor affecting the prognosis of some types of cancer, such as early-stage breast cancer,49 and cervical cancer.50 In this study, univariate analysis revealed that PCT was associated with the prognosis of NPC patients, but multivariate analysis revealed that PCT was not an independent prognostic factor. The exploration of PCT in NPC is still very limited, and more relevant studies are needed to clarify the prognostic value of PCT in NPC.

In our research, the RSF model showed strong performance in the training datasets, with a C-index of 0.71 (95% CI, 0.69–0.73), and 0.68 (95% CI:0.62–0.74) in the validation data, which outperformed the Cox model. The net benefit (NB), which can be visualized in DCA, is a simple summary metric used to quantify the clinical practicability of predictive models and their ability to inform decision-making.51 When multiple prognostic or diagnostic scoring tools are available, plotting the net benefits of these different instruments on a single graph enables a visual comparison of the clinical utility advantages offered by a novel scoring system relative to existing tools or established clinical practice strategies. For a new scoring system to be accepted, it should demonstrate specific merits: for instance, exhibiting superior net benefit at certain risk thresholds, maintaining equivalent net benefit while improving computational efficiency, or achieving value enhancement through more cost-effective and less invasive diagnostic testing modalities.52 As depicted in Figure 13, DCA was employed to evaluate the clinical utility of each model. The analysis plots the probability threshold for OS risk on the X-axis against the net benefit of model application on the Y-axis. The DCA results demonstrated a consistently higher net benefit for the RSF model compared to the Cox model. In addition, the NRI and IDI are also employed to assess model performance. The RSF model achieved higher predictive accuracy than the Cox model (NRI =0.196, P<0.01; IDI =0.443, P<0.01). The key advantage of the random forest algorithm lies in its ability to handle large amounts of high-dimensional data, even when dealing with missing data. This model can maintain a high level of accuracy and has robust generalization capabilities, allowing for maximum utilization of clinical data while minimizing the loss of information during traditional model construction.

In the past few years, the quantity of studies focusing on the use of machine learning algorithms to assist in clinical decision-making for patients with NPC has increased. These studies have played crucial roles in predicting NPC risk, diagnosis, and prognosis.53,54 In this study, the use platelet-related indicators for predicting the prognosis of NPC patients was investigated and an RSF model was developed using a machine learning algorithm. The results showed that this model had satisfactory prediction efficiency, demonstrating that RSF maybe could offer a new approach for constructing models that align with personalized diagnosis and treatment concepts. This can aid clinicians in making treatment decisions or designing clinical trials.

However, this investigation has several limitations. First, the study was conducted at a single center, and retrospective studies may introduce bias in data selection and analysis. Second, DCA offers important insights into the clinical utility of a prognostic model in a target population, however, it cannot replace the external validation of the new model in different validation cohorts. Assessment of external validity remains a crucial step in ensuring the generalizability of model performance to diverse patient populations and should always precede the widespread clinical application of any prediction model or clinical decision algorithm.52 The model was constructed using data from a single-institution patient cohort, and therefore requires rigorous external validation. Third, the high importance of a variable in our RSF model indicates its utility for improving predictive accuracy but does not imply it is a direct cause of the outcome. Unmeasured confounders may underlie the observed associations. Therefore, our findings should be interpreted as identifying robust predictive markers rather than establishing causal pathways. In addition, the dynamic changes in platelet volumetric parameters over time, along with their potential to be affected by various clinical conditions, represent further complexities that were not entirely addressed in the current analysis. Future research should incorporate dynamic assessments and additional data collection, along with prospective multicenter trials, to validate these preliminary findings, thereby addressing the limitations of the proposed prognostic model and improving its generalizability and robustness. Therefore, we emphasize that the urgent next step is to validate our model in large-scale, multi-center, prospective cohorts to confirm its generalizability and clinical utility before any potential application. Although recent studies have shown that immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) have shown good efficacy in recurrent or metastatic NPC,55–57 the datas of OS in LA-NPC are immature and still in the exploratory stage.58 If there is an important change in the standard of care for LA-NPC in the future, it should be considered that the change in treatment regimen will affect the performance of the model.

Conclusion

In this study, we explored the relationships between platelet-associated biomarkers and OS rates in NPC patients. We propose the PDW/P as an innovative biological indicator for predicting OS in LA-NPC patients, The PDW/P may be a suitable prognostic predictor with broad application prospects because of its a noninvasive nature, easy accessibility and cost-effectiveness. Additionally, we established and validated a Cox proportional hazards model and RSF model to predict clinical outcomes. In conclusion, our research indicates that the proposed model may potentially serve as a tool for predicting the prognosis of nasopharyngeal carcinoma. However, its application in clinical settings requires further validation in external populations.
