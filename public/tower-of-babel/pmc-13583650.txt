Testicular Schistosomiasis Mimicking Testicular Tumour: A Case Report
Authors: Auwal Idris Kabuga, Rufai Yunusa, Ahmad Hamza, Nura Yusuf, Musbahu Yusuf, Muhammad Yaseen Hassan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Schistosomiasis is a neglected tropical disease, with the highest burden in Sub-Saharan Africa, predominantly due to Schistosoma haematobium. Testicular involvement is rare; fewer than 30 cases have been reported in the literature to date, though sporadic reports continue to emerge from endemic areas. We report the case of a 34-year-old school teacher from Kano, Nigeria, with a history of freshwater exposure and childhood schistosomiasis treated with praziquantel. He presented with a two-year history of a firm, progressively enlarging left hemi-scrotal mass. Clinical examination and scrotal ultrasound were both suggestive of a testicular tumour. The patient underwent left radical orchidectomy. Histopathological examination, independently verified by three pathologists, revealed calcified Schistosoma ova within a densely fibrotic, granulomatous background, with no evidence of malignancy. This case underscores testicular schistosomiasis as an important differential diagnosis for testicular masses in endemic regions, and highlights the central role of histopathology in preventing unnecessary orchidectomy.

Introduction

Schistosomiasis, caused by trematode parasites of the genus Schistosoma, is a neglected tropical disease endemic in more than 78 countries worldwide.1 Prevalence is highest in Sub-Saharan Africa, where more than 250 million people require preventive treatment.2 Schistosoma haematobium, the principal agent of urogenital schistosomiasis, typically involves the bladder and ureters. Testicular schistosomiasis (TS) remains a rare ectopic manifestation, with fewer than 30 cases documented in a 2022 systematic review.3

In endemic regions such as Nigeria, where local prevalence exceeds 20% in some communities,4,5 TS often presents as a painless, unilateral scrotal mass resulting from granulomatous inflammation and fibrosis around trapped Schistosoma eggs. This closely mimics testicular germ cell tumours, the most common malignancy in young men (peak incidence 29-37 years).6 This leads to frequent misdiagnosis and radical orchidectomy. The diagnostic overlap is compounded in resource-limited settings, where tumour markers, cross-sectional imaging, and parasitological confirmation may be unavailable. This underscores the relevance of histopathology for a definitive diagnosis.3

We report a case of TS in a young man from Kano, Nigeria, initially managed as a suspected testicular malignancy, in whom histology following orchidectomy confirmed schistosomiasis.

Case report

A 34-year-old schoolteacher was referred to our clinic with a two-year history of painless left hemi-scrotal swelling, initially insidious in onset, with rapid growth over the two months preceding presentation. He reported a history of regular freshwater exposure and had received praziquantel treatment for schistosomiasis in childhood. He had been married for two years, with no conception and evaluated for primary infertility. He reported no erectile or ejaculatory dysfunction. There was no haematuria, weight loss, history of trauma, or symptoms suggestive of tuberculosis. HIV serology was non-reactive.

Scrotal examination revealed a left hemi-scrotal swelling with normal overlying skin and no hydrocele. The right testis was unremarkable, measuring 3.5 x 2.0 cm, while the left testis was enlarged, measuring 7 x 5 cm, non-tender, hard, irregular, and multi-nodular. No inguinal or palpable lymphadenopathy was identified. A clinical diagnosis of left testicular tumour was made.

Scrotal ultrasound showed a normal right testis and an enlarged, distorted left testis with heterogeneous parenchymal echotexture and a 5 x 4.1 cm hypoechoic lesion in the upper pole. There was no hepatosplenomegaly, ascites, or para-aortic or pelvic lymphadenopathy. Scrotal Doppler ultrasound, CT, MRI, and tumour markers were not performed. Urinalysis showed numerous pus cells; urine microscopy, culture, and sensitivity showed mixed growth of Escherichia coli and Staphylococcus aureus sensitive to ceftriaxone, levofloxacin and ciprofloxacin. No ova were identified. Stool microscopy was unremarkable, while seminal fluid analysis showed azoospermia. The packed cell volume was 33%. The eosinophil count was 4%, within the normal reference range for our laboratory. Total white cell count and differentials were unremarkable.

He underwent left radical orchidectomy and was discharged 72 hours later without complication.

Histopathological examination revealed effacement of the normal testicular architecture, with extensive replacement of the parenchyma by densely hyalinized fibrocollagenous stroma. Within this stroma were numerous granulomas composed of lymphocytes, histiocytes, and foreign body-type giant cells surrounding several calcified Schistosoma ova. No viable (non-calcified) ova were identified, and no areas of necrosis or vasculitis were seen. There was no evidence of malignancy. Discussion

Testicular schistosomiasis is a rare ectopic manifestation of Schistosoma haematobium infection, most often reported from endemic areas of Sub-Saharan Africa, including Nigeria.3,7 Following skin or mucosal penetration by cercariae in freshwater, adult worms mature in the mesenteric and vesical venous plexuses, where females deposit eggs. Eggs that fail to reach the bladder lumen become trapped in surrounding tissue, where they stimulate a granulomatous host response driven by lymphocyte-derived fibrogenic cytokines, leading to fibrosis at the sites. Typically affected organs are bladder, ureters, seminal vesicles, and cervix.2,5 Testicular involvement is thought to occur when eggs are carried to the testis through the pampiniform venous plexus and associated venous collaterals, rather than by arterial spread.8 Badmus et al. proposed that portal hypertension might increase testicular egg deposition via portosystemic venous collaterals, with a possible predilection for the left side owing to the drainage pattern of the left gonadal vein into the left renal vein; however, this remains a hypothesis based on a single case series and has not been confirmed in larger studies.8

Our patient's history of documented childhood schistosomiasis, treated with praziquantel, followed by adult presentation with ectopic testicular disease, raises the possibility of either reinfection in a hyperendemic area or incomplete parasitological clearance with ongoing egg deposition.

This case illustrates the classic diagnostic dilemma in which TS mimics a testicular tumour. Both conditions present as painless scrotal masses in young men from overlapping demographics, typically adolescents and men aged 15-35 years.6 Both can produce a firm, nodular testis on examination and a heterogeneous, hypoechoic lesion on ultrasound. Normal tumour markers do not reliably exclude malignancy, since a proportion of germ cell tumours are non-secretory, and in our patient these markers were readily available, narrowing the preoperative diagnostic options. Unlike urinary or bladder schistosomiasis, TS lacks a reliable non-invasive marker: urine microscopy for ova is frequently negative because of ectopic egg deposition away from the urinary tract, as was the case here, and eosinophilia is inconsistently present.

Point-of-care Schistosoma antigen tests, where available, may add supportive evidence but are not yet routinely accessible in many endemic settings. In practice, therefore, a definitive preoperative diagnosis of TS is rarely achievable, and histopathology remains the diagnostic standard.3,9

Several previous Nigerian reports illustrate the range of clinical presentations of TS and reinforce the geographic breadth of this diagnostic challenge. Dauda and Rafindadi described a case from Zaria, northern Nigeria, in which testicular schistosomiasis simulated malignancy in a manner closely paralleling our patient's presentation.10 Badmus et al. reported a similar case from Ibadan, south-western Nigeria, in which orchidectomy was performed for a presumed tumour.8 Ekenze et al. and Oguntunde et al. each described paediatric cases from Enugu and Lagos, respectively, in which TS mimicked malignancy and hydrocele.9,11 Adisa et al. reported a case of S. mansoni-related testicular involvement from the Jos Plateau presenting with primary infertility rather than a mass.4 Taken together with our case from Kano, these reports span most of Nigeria's major geopolitical zones, suggesting that TS is likely under-recognised rather than truly confined to fewer than 30 cases worldwide; publication bias and limited access to histopathology in many endemic settings probably contribute to substantial underreporting.3

Our patient's two years of primary infertility raises the possibility that schistosomal granulomatous involvement of the testis and epididymis, rather than an unrelated cause, contributed to impaired spermatogenesis, as has been described in other testicular schistosomiasis cases with infertility.4 If this is the case, the affected testis may already have had reduced functional reserve prior to orchidectomy; however, this does not lessen the importance of avoiding unnecessary loss of testicular tissue in men who wish to preserve fertility.

Equivocal testicular lesions may benefit from frozen-section assessment to support testis-sparing surgery. Where unavailable, clinical suspicion based on schistosomiasis exposure should inform patient counselling and management. Multidisciplinary collaboration may help balance malignancy exclusion with organ and fertility preservation. In endemic areas, clinicians should consider testicular schistosomiasis in compatible testicular masses.

Similar diagnostic difficulty has also been reported outside Nigeria, for example in a case from Egypt in which testicular schistosomiasis was likewise identified only on histology after surgery for a suspected mass. This indicates that this diagnostic pitfall is not unique to any single country but reflects the broader challenge of recognising TS wherever S. haematobium is endemic.12

Conclusion

Testicular schistosomiasis, though rare, is an important differential diagnosis for testicular masses in patients from endemic regions, particularly those with a history of freshwater exposure or prior schistosomiasis. Clinicians practising in such settings should maintain a high index of suspicion and, where feasible, use intraoperative frozen section or a period of close surveillance to avoid unnecessary orchidectomy. Ultimately, histopathological examination remains the diagnostic gold standard, and its judicious use can spare patients the physical and fertility-related consequences of unwarranted radical surgery.
