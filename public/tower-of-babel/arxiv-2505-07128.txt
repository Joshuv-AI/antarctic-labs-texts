Well-Posed Geometric Boundary data in General Relativity, II: Dirichlet boundary data
Zhongshan An and Michael T. Anderson

Abstract
. In this second work in a series, we prove the local-in-time well-posedness of the IBVP for the vacuum Einstein equations with Dirichlet boundary data on a finite timelike boundary, provided the Brown-York stress tensor of the boundary is a Lorentz metric of the same sign as the induced Lorentz metric on the boundary. This is a convexity-type assumption which is an exact analog of a similar result in the Riemannian setting. This assumption on the (extrinsic) Brown-York tensor cannot be dropped in general.



1.
Introduction

This work is the second in a series on the initial boundary value problem (IBVP) in general relativity. As in Part I,
[
2
]
, let
(M,g)
be a globally hyperbolic
vacuum solution of the Einstein equations

(1.1)

\mathrm{Ric}_{g}=0,

on a finite domain
M\simeq I\times S
, where the Cauchy surface
S
is a compact
n
-manifold with boundary
\partial S=\Sigma
and
{\mathcal{C}}=I\times\Sigma
is a
timelike boundary
{\mathcal{C}}
of
M
. Consider the space
\mathbb{E}
of all such solutions; this is also known as the covariant pre-phase space on a domain with
finite boundary, cf.
[
14
]
and references therein. The quotient space
{\mathcal{E}}=\mathbb{E}/{\rm Diff}_{0}(M)
of
\mathbb{E}
by the group of diffeomorphisms
\varphi
of
M

fixing
S
and
{\mathcal{C}}
is the moduli space of vacuum solutions, closely related to the covariant phase space of vacuum Einstein metrics.

We seek a characterization or effective description of
{\mathcal{E}}
in terms of initial data on
S
and boundary data on
{\mathcal{C}}
. Thus, let
\mathcal{I}_{0}
denote the space of
initial data, i.e. pairs
(\gamma_{S},\kappa)
consisting of a Riemannian metric
\gamma_{S}
on
S
and a symmetric bilinear form
\kappa
on
S
, satisfying the vacuum Einstein
constraint equations, (discussed in detail later). In this work, we choose the space of boundary data

{\mathcal{B}}={\mathcal{B}}_{Dir}=Met({\mathcal{C}})

to be the space of Dirichlet boundary data, consisting of globally hyperbolic Lorentz metrics
\gamma_{{\mathcal{C}}}
on
{\mathcal{C}}
. Thus, we seek an accurate correspondence

(1.2)

\begin{split}&\Phi:{\mathcal{E}}\rightarrow\mathcal{I}_{0}\times_{c}{\mathcal{B}},\\
&\Phi(g)=(g_{S},K,g_{{\mathcal{C}}}),\\
\end{split}

where
g_{S}
is the metric on
S
induced by
g
,
K
is the second fundamental form of
S\subset(M,g)
and
g_{{\mathcal{C}}}
is the metric on
{\mathcal{C}}
induced
by
g
. The subscript
c
in (
1.2
) denotes the compatibility conditions between data in
\mathcal{I}_{0}
and
{\mathcal{B}}
, discussed in detail in §2.4. The (local) well-posedness
for the IBVP for vacuum Einstein metrics with Dirichlet boundary data basically requires that the map
\Phi
is a bijection, so that solutions
g
of (
1.1
)
exist and are uniquely determined by their initial and boundary data. This existence result is understood to be local-in-time to the future (or past) of
S
.

Consider first the simpler Euclidean or Riemannian situation where
g
is a Riemannian vacuum Einstein metric on a domain
M
with
boundary
{\partial M}
and with induced boundary metric
g_{{\partial M}}
. Roughly speaking, this corresponds to studying the space of time-independent or stationary
solutions of the vacuum equations (
1.1
). Now, strictly speaking, here the Dirichlet BVP is never elliptic or well-posed; in fact the Hamiltonian constraint
(or Gauss equation) is an obstruction to ellipticity. Briefly, to see this, the Hamiltonian constraint or Gauss equation for Riemannian Einstein metrics (
1.1
)
gives

(1.3)

|A|^{2}-H^{2}+R_{g_{{\partial M}}}=0,

on
{\partial M}
, where
A
is the second fundamental form of
{\partial M}
in
(M,g)
,
H=\mathrm{tr}A
is the mean curvature of
{\partial M}
and
R_{g_{{\partial M}}}
is the scalar curvature
of
({\partial M},g_{{\partial M}})
. Ellipticity of the equations (
1.1
), in any suitable gauge, would imply that solutions
g
on
M
are as smooth as the smoothness of the
boundary metric
g_{{\partial M}}
, i.e. if
g_{{\partial M}}
is
C^{k,\alpha}
, then
g
itself must be
C^{k,\alpha}
. (Similar statements hold with respect to Sobolev topologies).
However,
g\in C^{k,\alpha}
implies
A,H\in C^{k-1,\alpha}
so that (
1.3
) implies
R_{{\partial M}}\in C^{k-1,\alpha}
; this is clearly false for generic
C^{k,\alpha}
boundary metrics.
This shows that in function spaces with a given finite degree of differentiability, the Einstein equation (
1.1
) is not solvable for generic Dirichlet
boundary data.

Similar arguments apply in the Lorentzian case, where the Hamiltonian constraint implies there are no boundary stable energy estimates, cf.
[
8
]
,
in any gauge. The boundary problem is thus not maximally dissipative in any gauge, calling into question the well-posedness.

There are in fact numerous examples where both existence and uniqueness break down for Dirichlet boundary data. This occurs in both
Riemannian and Lorentzian settings in regions where
A=0
, cf.
[
5
]
. More interesting examples have recently been discussed in detail in

[
9
]
, cf. also §7 for further discussion.

Nevertheless, there is an important class of Euclidean (or stationary Lorentzian) vacuum solutions for which Dirichlet boundary data is effectively
elliptic and so well-posed. Thus if the symmetric form

(1.4)

\Pi=Hg_{{\mathcal{C}}}-A,

is positive (or negative) definite on
{\partial M}
, i.e. of the same signature (up to sign) as the induced metric
g_{{\partial M}}
on
{\partial M}
, then Dirichlet boundary
data are effectively elliptic, if one allows for loss-of-dervative behavior, cf.
[
6
]
,
[
7
]
,
[
22
]
. In this case, the map (similar to
\Phi
above) from bulk
solutions
g
to Dirichlet boundary data
g_{{\partial M}}
is a smooth tame Fredholm map in
C^{\infty}
near
(M,g)
; this guarantees existence and uniqueness up to a finite
dimensional indeterminacy (even in spaces with finite differentiability). A key idea in the proof of such ellipticity, originating in Nash’s work on the isometric
embedding problem of Riemannian metrics into
\mathbb{R}^{N}
(and the associated Nash inverse function theorem) is the form of the variation of the boundary
metric
g_{{\partial M}}
under infinitesimal deformations (diffeomorphisms) normal to the boundary; cf. §2.5 for further discussion.

The form
\Pi
arises naturally from a physics perspective. It is the momentum conjugate to the metric
g_{{\partial M}}
for the Euclidean Einstein-Hilbert
action with Gibbons-Hawking-York boundary term

\int_{M}R_{g}dv_{g}+2\int_{{\partial M}}H_{g}dv_{g_{{\partial M}}}.

It is also the Brown-York stress-energy tensor for Dirichlet boundary data on
{\partial M}
. Note that in dimension 3, the definiteness of
\Pi
is
equivalent to the definiteness of the second fundamental form
A
, i.e. the convexity (or concavity) of
{\partial M}
in
(M,g)
. In higher dimensions
the condition
\Pi>0
is a weaker version of the convexity condition
A>0
.

Turning to the Lorentzian setting of interest, the Lorentzian analog of the definiteness of
\Pi
on
{\partial M}
is the statement that
\Pi
has
the same signature, up to overall sign, of the induced Lorentz metric
g_{{\mathcal{C}}}
on
{\mathcal{C}}
. Throughout much of this paper, we then
make the following analogous convexity-type assumption:

Convexity Assumption
(*). The symmetric form
\Pi=Hg_{{\mathcal{C}}}-A
in (
1.4
) is non-degenerate on
{\mathcal{C}}
, with signature
(1,n-1)
, so induces a
Lorentz metric on
{\mathcal{C}}
. We assume in addition that
\Pi(\partial_{t},\partial_{t})<0
, (cf. Remark
2.1
).

To illustrate on a simple example, let
S
be any compact smooth domain in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
n\geq 3
, with smooth convex boundary
\partial S=\Sigma
. Let
M=\mathbb{R}\times S

be the timelike product over
S
in Minkowski space
(\mathbb{R}^{1,n},g_{0})
. Let
\nu_{{\mathcal{C}}}
be the (spacelike) unit outward normal to
{\mathcal{C}}
in
M\subset\mathbb{R}^{1,n}
. Since

A=\tfrac{1}{2}{\mathcal{L}}_{\nu_{{\mathcal{C}}}}g_{0},

it is easy to see that
A(\partial_{t},\partial_{t})=0
but
A
is positive definite,
A>0
in the spatial directions
S
. It follows easily that
\Pi
is a Lorentz metric on

{\mathcal{C}}
of the same signature as
g_{{\mathcal{C}}}
. Thus, all such configurations satisfy Assumption
(*)
. Notice that this fails for
n=2
where
\Pi
for the timelike
product is degenerate in the spatial direction. Suitable perturbations of the product in outward directions will however satisfy
(*)
.

In general, the Assumption
(*)
is an extrinsic condition on the metric
g
at the boundary
{\mathcal{C}}
and so cannot be determined from the induced metric

g_{{\mathcal{C}}}
alone. It is an open condition, so is preserved under small perturbations, and does not depend explicitly or directly on the initial data on a Cauchy slice
S
.
It is also gauge-invariant, in fact invariant under general diffeomorphisms
\varphi:M\rightarrow M
mapping
{\mathcal{C}}\rightarrow{\mathcal{C}}
, but not necessarily preserving the Cauchy surface

S
. Note also that it holds for natural far-field or near-infinity boundaries in asymptotically Minkowski or asymptotically AdS spacetimes.

Let
{\mathcal{E}}^{*}
denote the space of smooth (
C^{\infty}
) vacuum Einstein metrics
g
on
M
satisfying the Assumption (*) at the boundary
{\mathcal{C}}
.
Clearly
{\mathcal{E}}^{*}
is an open subset of
{\mathcal{E}}
. Similarly, as above
\mathcal{I}_{0}
and
Met({\mathcal{C}})
denote the space of
C^{\infty}
vacuum initial data and boundary data.

The following result implies the local well-posedness of the IBVP for the Einstein equations with Dirichlet boundary data satisfying
Assumption (*). It is the first such well-posedness result with respect to any geometric boundary data for the Einstein equations.

Theorem 1.1
.

The space
{\mathcal{E}}^{*}
is a smooth Frechet manifold and the map

(1.5)

\begin{split}&\Phi:{\mathcal{E}}^{*}\rightarrow\mathcal{I}_{0}\times_{c}Met({\mathcal{C}}),\\
&\Phi(g)=(g_{S},K,g_{{\mathcal{C}}}),\\
\end{split}

is a smooth open embedding into the Frechet manifold
\mathcal{I}_{0}\times_{c}Met({\mathcal{C}})
. Thus
{\rm Im}\Phi
is an open set in

\mathcal{I}_{0}\times_{c}Met({\mathcal{C}})
and
\Phi
is a smooth diffeomorphism onto its image.

As noted above, Theorem
1.1
is a local-in-time result. Solutions with given initial and Dirichlet boundary data exist for a definite positive
time
t_{0}>0
, but it is not asserted that if the boundary metric
\gamma_{{\mathcal{C}}}
exists for all time
t
, a solution
g
with this boundary data will also
exist. An understanding of the global or long-time behavior is a different issue, which requires a good understanding of the local-in-time behavior
to make any initial progress.

Although stated in the smooth (
C^{\infty})
context, Theorem
1.1
also holds with respect to Sobolev-type spaces
H^{s}
with a finite
number of derivatives, cf. Remark
6.2
.

Theorem
1.1
stands in striking contrast with the main result in
[
2
]
, where it was shown that the IBVP for conformal/mean curvature
boundary data

([g_{{\mathcal{C}}}],H_{{\mathcal{C}}})

is never well-posed per se. It is necessary to add on the extra data of the corner angle
\alpha
between
S
and
{\mathcal{C}}
at the corner
\Sigma
to
obtain uniqueness of solutions, even at the linearized level. Here for Dirichlet boundary data, such extra data is not necessary, under
Assumption
(*)
. In fact, the corner angle
\alpha
is uniquely determined by the initial data on
S
and Dirichlet boundary data on
{\mathcal{C}}
.
This suggests that the well-posedness of the IBVP for geometric boundary data depends in a subtle way on
the nature of the geometric boundary data.

We note that Theorem
1.1
holds in any dimension and for vacuum solutions with any cosmological constant
\Lambda\in\mathbb{R}
. The
statements and proofs of the results to follow require only minor and essentially obvious modifications when
\Lambda\neq 0
. We will not consider
here the coupling to other matter field equations.

In general it is not easy to understand the image of
\Phi
. There is no simple intrinsic characterization of the Assumption (*), i.e. whether
(*)
holds or
not cannot usually be determined in terms of Dirichlet boundary data alone.

Consider for instance the simpler situation in
2+1
dimensions: all Lorentz vacuum Einstein metrics in
2+1
dimensions are
flat (or constant curvature if
\Lambda\neq 0
). Solving the Dirichlet BVP for such metrics is thus the same as finding isometric embeddings
of a timelike cylinder

(I\times S^{1},\gamma)\subset\mathbb{R}^{1,2},

(bounding a compact solid cylinder in
\mathbb{R}^{1,2}
), with prescribed Lorentz metric
\gamma
into Minkowski
\mathbb{R}^{1,2}
. This is the Lorentzian analog of
the well-known isometric embedding (or immersion) problem of surfaces in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. The classical solution of the Weyl embedding problem by Nirenberg
and Pogorelov gives the existence of a unique solution in the Euclidean setting when the Gauss curvature satisfies
K_{\gamma}>0
. It is easy to see that this
is exactly the condition that
-\Pi
is a positive definite form, i.e. the Riemannian version of Assumption
(*)
. Of course this is an intrinsic condition on the
boundary metric
\gamma
, by Gauss’ Theorema Egregium.

In the Lorentzian
2+1
setting, Assumption (*) implies the intrinsic condition,

(1.6)

K_{\gamma}>0.

However, (
1.6
) is not sufficient to imply Assumption
(*)
. This is due to the fact that symmetric forms
\Pi
as in (
1.4
), although diagonalizable with
respect to Riemannian (positive definite) metrics, are not necessarily diagonalizable with respect to metrics of Lorentz signature. Thus there are timelike surfaces in

\mathbb{R}^{1,2}
with positive Gauss curvature for which Assumption
(*)
does not hold, cf. §7 for further discussion. On the other hand, if
\Pi
, (i.e.
A
), is
diagonalizable with respect to
\gamma
, then (
1.6
) does imply Assumption
(*)
.

In the Euclidean setting, very little is known about a general theory for isometric embedding or immersion of closed surfaces in
\mathbb{R}^{3}

beyond the convex case
K_{\gamma}>0
(or
K_{\gamma}\geq 0
), cf.
[
13
]
,
[
20
]
. Once the Gauss curvature is allowed to change sign, many complicated
behaviors may occur. Even the local isometric embedding for surfaces in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
remains an open problem in general. By the remarks above, all
of this holds for Dirichlet boundary data for Riemannian vacuum Einstein metrics in
3
dimensions. It seems likely that similar vast complications hold for
Lorentzian vacuum Einstein metrics in
2+1
dimensions, cf. also §7 for further discussion. It would of course be interesting to explore this further.

As indicated above, the proof of the well-posedness the the IBVP for Dirichlet boundary data does not proceed by using a suitable gauge reduction to
a hyperbolic IBVP for which one can apply a standard, existing theory of well-posedness; in fact we believe that such an approach is not feasible. Instead,
due to essential loss-of-derivative issues associated with Dirichlet boundary data, we use the Nash-Moser implicit function theorem in Frechet spaces
to prove that well-posedness at the linear level implies well-posedness at the nonlinear level. This requires of course a considerable detailed analysis of the
linearized problem.

A brief summary of the contents of the paper is as follows. In §2, we introduce preliminary material needed for the work to follow. Due to the use of
Nash-Moser, this section is quite extensive, in particular with regard to the exact choices of spaces involved as well as compatibility conditions at the
corner
\Sigma
. In §3, we derive the basic equations for the boundary data not part of the Dirichlet boundary data and prove localized existence theorems
(Theorems
3.7
and
3.10
) for both modified boundary data and Dirichlet boundary data. In §4, perhaps the most important section, we derive
the apriori energy estimates for the linear problem which are crucial in order to be able to apply the nonlinear Nash-Moser theory. These energy estimates are
then globalized (in space) in §5, leading to the use of the Nash-Moser theory and proof of Theorem
1.1
in §6. In §7, we conclude with
several remarks, including a discussion of the
2+1
dimensional case and discuss examples where well-posedness
breaks down. Finally in the Appendix we prove several (less important) technical results not given in detail in the main text.

2.
Initial Material

In this section, we describe the overall approach and detail the rather extensive preliminary material needed for the proof of Theorem
1.1
.

Let
S
be a compact, connected and oriented
n
-manifold with boundary
\partial S=\Sigma
; here
\Sigma
may or may not be connected. Let
M=I\times S
,
where
I=[0,1]
is parametrized by a time function
t:M\rightarrow I
. Let
S_{t}=\{t\}\times S
; this will be identified with
S_{0}
upon introduction of local coordinates.
The choice of time interval
[0,1]
is for convenience; the value
1
could be replaced by any other fixed constant. We consider Lorentz metrics
g
on
M

which are globally hyperbolic in the sense of manifolds with boundary, where the boundary
{\mathcal{C}}=I\times\Sigma
is timelike with respect to the metric
g
; the
induced metric
g_{{\mathcal{C}}}
is thus a globally hyperbolic metric on
{\mathcal{C}}
with closed Cauchy surface
\Sigma
.

Let
\nu_{S}
denote the future timelike unit normal vector field to
S\subset(M,g)
and similarly let
\nu_{{\mathcal{C}}}
denote the (spacelike)
outward unit normal to
{\mathcal{C}}\subset(M,g)
. Then

K=\tfrac{1}{2}{\mathcal{L}}_{\nu_{S}}g|_{S}\ {\rm and}\ A=\tfrac{1}{2}{\mathcal{L}}_{\nu_{{\mathcal{C}}}}g|_{{\mathcal{C}}}.

In local coordinates
x^{\alpha}=(t,x^{a})
near the corner
\Sigma
,
x^{0}=t\geq 0
is a defining function for
S=\{t=0\}
, while
x^{1}\leq 0
is chosen
to be a defining function for
{\mathcal{C}}=\{x^{1}=0\}
. The coordinates
x^{\alpha},\alpha=2,\cdots,n
are local coordinates of the corner
\Sigma
itself.
As usual, Greek letters
\alpha,\beta
denote spacetime indices
0,\dots,n
, Unless otherwise indicated, Roman letters denote spacelike indices
1,\dots,n

and capital Roman letters denote corner indices
2,\dots,n
.

As noted in the Introduction, in the (elliptic) case of Riemannian metrics, it is well-known that there is a loss-of-derivatives problem for vacuum
Einstein metrics with Dirichlet boundary data. This is even more severe in the case of Lorentz metrics and implies that one cannot expect to
approach the well-posedness of the IBVP by standard methods. In this section, we develop the necessary preliminary results needed to apply
the Nash-Moser implicit function theorem.

Remark 2.1
.

Recall that Assumption
(*)
requires that the symmetric form
\Pi=Hg_{{\mathcal{C}}}-A
induce a Lorentz metric on
{\mathcal{C}}
of the same type as

g_{{\mathcal{C}}}
. Let
C_{-\Pi}
denote the future timelike cone of
-\Pi
and
C_{g_{{\mathcal{C}}}}
denote the future timelike cone of
g_{{\mathcal{C}}}
at any point

p\in{\mathcal{C}}
. Then Assumption
(*)
requires

C_{-\Pi}\cap C_{g_{{\mathcal{C}}}}\neq 0.

We will always assume that

(2.1)

\partial_{t}\in C_{-\Pi}\cap C_{g_{{\mathcal{C}}}},

(with respect to some local coordinates near
{\mathcal{C}}
).

2.1.
The basic mappings, spaces and norms.

To begin, we must choose a suitable function space. There are various possibilities, but we work with the spaces naturally associated to
energy estimates for wave-type equations. Thus let
D
denote a domain given as either
M
,
S
,
{\mathcal{C}}
,
\Sigma
or the corresponding
t
-level
sets
S_{t}
,
\Sigma_{t}
, or the corresponding
t
-sublevel sets,
M_{t}
,
{\mathcal{C}}_{t}
; (
M_{t}=\{p\in M:t(p)\leq t\}
). Define the Sobolev
H^{s}
norm on
functions on
D
by

||v||_{H^{s}(D)}^{2}=\int_{D}|\partial_{D}^{s}v|^{2}dv_{D},

where
\partial_{D}^{s}
consists of all partial coordinate derivatives tangent to
D
of order
\leq s
. In place of coordinate derivatives, one may use the
components of covariant derivatives of
v
up to order
s
, given a (background) Riemannian metric
g_{D}
on
D
. The volume form
dv_{D}
is also
induced from the metric
g_{D}
.

Define the stronger
\bar{H}^{s}
norm by including all space-time derivatives up to order
s
, so that

||v||_{\bar{H}^{s}(D)}^{2}=\int_{D}|\partial_{M}^{s}v|^{2}dv_{D},

where
\partial_{M}
denotes partial derivatives along all coordinates of
M
at
D
.

Finally, for the Cauchy slices
S_{t}
, define the boundary stable
H^{s}
norm on
S_{t}
by

||v||_{\bar{\mathcal{H}}^{s}(S_{t})}^{2}=||v||_{\bar{H}^{s}(S_{t})}^{2}+||v||_{\bar{H}^{s}({\mathcal{C}}_{t})}^{2}.

We will use the function space

(2.2)

{\mathcal{N}}^{s}(M)=C^{0}(I,\bar{H}^{s}(S),

for functions on
M=M_{1}
. With the usual
C^{0}
norm in
I
, the space
{\mathcal{N}}^{s}
, with associated norm
||\cdot||_{{\mathcal{N}}^{s}}
is a
separable Banach space. The normed topology (
2.2
) is commonly used for the space of solutions of wave-type equations.

Note that

H^{s+1}(M)\subset{\mathcal{N}}^{s}(M)\subset H^{s}(M).

Remark 2.2
.

We will frequently use the well-known Sobolev trace theorem, which states that the restriction
f\rightarrow f|_{{\mathcal{C}}}
has the
associated bound

(2.3)

||f|_{\mathcal{C}}||_{H^{s-1/2}({\mathcal{C}})}\leq C||f||_{H^{s}(M)}.

Similarly,

(2.4)

||f|_{\Sigma_{t}}||_{H^{s-1/2}(\Sigma_{t})}\leq C||f||_{H^{s}(S_{t})}\ {\rm and}\ ||f|_{\Sigma_{t}}||_{H^{s-1/2}(\Sigma_{t})}\leq C||f||_{H^{s}({\mathcal{C}}_{t})}.

Here
C
is a constant depending only on the metric
g
. In addition, we always assume
s>\frac{n+1}{2}+2
, so that by the Sobolev
embedding theorem, metrics
g\in Met(M)
are at least
C^{2,\alpha}
. We note also the following Cauchy-Schwarz type inequality to be used
in §4:

(2.5)

\int_{\Sigma}f_{1}f_{2}\leq||f_{1}||_{H^{1/2}(\Sigma)}||f_{2}||_{H^{-1/2}(\Sigma)}.

We refer to
[
3
]
for these results.

Let
Met^{s}(M)
be the space of globally hyperbolic Lorentz metrics
g
on
M
, with coefficients
g_{\alpha\beta}\in{\mathcal{N}}^{s}
. The initial data
of
g
is given by the pair
(g_{S},K)
consisting the metric
g_{S}
induced on the initial Cauchy surface
S=S_{0}
and the
second fundamental form or extrinsic curvature
K
of
(S,g_{S})\subset(M,g)
. This pair takes its values in the space

\mathcal{I}^{s}=Met^{s}(S)\times(S^{2}(M))^{s-1}(S)
of all initial data
(\gamma,\kappa)
, with the
H^{s}(S)\times H^{s-1}(S)
topology.

For this work, the space of boundary data is

{\mathcal{B}}^{s-1/2}=Met^{s-1/2}({\mathcal{C}}),

of globally hyperbolic Lorentz metrics on the boundary
{\mathcal{C}}
. Again, the bulk metric
g
induces a boundary metric
g_{{\mathcal{C}}}\in{\mathcal{B}}^{s-1/2}
.

We consider the map

(2.6)

\Phi:Met(M)\rightarrow[S^{2}(M)\times\mathcal{I}(S)\times{\mathcal{B}}]_{c}:={\mathcal{T}},

\Phi(g)=(\mathrm{Ric}_{g},g_{S},K,g_{{\mathcal{C}}}),

where
(g_{S},K)
and
g_{{\mathcal{C}}}
are the initial and Dirichlet boundary data induced by
g
. The subscript
c
denotes the compatibility
conditions between the initial and boundary data at the corner
\Sigma
; these are discussed in detail in §2.3. For simplicity, we have dropped
here the notation for the topologies, indexed by
s
.

This is, roughly speaking, the equation we wish to solve and prove well-posedness for the IBVP, compare with (
1.2
).

Now, as is well-known, the mapping
\Phi
in (
2.6
) is not surjective, i.e. the equation
\Phi(g)=\tau
is not solvable for general

\tau\in{\mathcal{T}}
. This is due to the constraint equations, coupling the bulk term
\mathrm{Ric}(\nu_{S},\cdot)
in the (timelike) direction
\nu_{S}
normal
to
S
with the initial data
(g_{S},K)
.

To describe this in more detail, first extend
\Phi
by adding on data for the normal vector
\nu_{S}
of
S\subset M
. Let
\widehat{\mathcal{T}}=[{\mathcal{T}}\times\mathbb{V}_{S}^{\prime}]_{c}

where
\mathbb{V}_{S}^{\prime}
is the space of vector fields
\nu
along
S
nowhere tangent to
S
. We then consider the extended map

(2.7)

\widehat{\Phi}:Met(M)\rightarrow\widehat{\mathcal{T}},

\Phi(g)=(\mathrm{Ric}_{g},g_{S},K,\nu_{S},g_{{\mathcal{C}}}),

where
\nu_{S}
is the future unit timelike normal to
S\subset(M,g)
.

The Hamiltonian and momentum constraint equations, (or equivalently the Gauss and Gauss-Codazzi equations) along
S
are given by:

(2.8)

|K|^{2}-H^{2}-R_{g_{S}}-R_{g}+2\mathrm{Ric}_{g}(\nu_{S},\nu_{S})=0,

(2.9)

{\rm div}_{g_{S}}(K-Hg_{S})-\mathrm{Ric}_{g}(\nu_{S},\cdot)=0.

Here
H=\mathrm{tr}_{g_{S}}K
is the mean curvature of
S
and
R_{g_{S}}
is the scalar curvature of
g_{S}
. These equations are identities on the domain

Met(M)
but induce non-trivial equations on the target
\widehat{\mathcal{T}}
. To see this, note that

-R_{g}+2\mathrm{Ric}_{g}(\nu_{S},\nu_{S})=-\mathrm{tr}_{g_{S}}\mathrm{Ric}_{g}+3\mathrm{Ric}_{g}(\nu_{S},\nu_{S}),

and the latter is well-defined on
\widehat{\mathcal{T}}
. The rest of the terms in (
2.8
)-(
2.9
) are already defined on
\widehat{\mathcal{T}}
. Thus, let

\widehat{\mathcal{T}}_{0}\subset\widehat{\mathcal{T}}
be the subset satisfying (
2.8
)-(
2.9
), so that

(2.10)

\widehat{\Phi}:Met^{s}(M)\rightarrow\widehat{\mathcal{T}}_{0}.

Note that there are also such constraint equations along the boundary
{\mathcal{C}}
. However, the second fundamental form
A
of
{\mathcal{C}}\subset(M,g)
does
not appear as boundary data, so there are no meaningful constraint equations to impose, cf. §3.1 however.

2.2.
Gauge fixing

A clearer understanding of the image of
\Phi
or
\widehat{\Phi}
above requires an understanding the action of the gauge group on the domain and target
spaces. Thus, consider the gauge group
G=\mathrm{Diff}_{0}(M)
of diffeomorphisms
\varphi:M\rightarrow M
which restrict to the identity on
S\cup{\mathcal{C}}
. The initial and boundary
data are invariant under the action of
\mathrm{Diff}_{0}(M)
, while in the bulk, the action
(\varphi,\mathrm{Ric})\rightarrow\varphi^{*}(\mathrm{Ric})
is not invariant in general, unless one is on-shell,
i.e. on the space
{\mathcal{E}}
of vacuum solutions.

The choice of gauge is described by a choice of vector field
V
on
M
, leading to the reduced Einstein equations

(2.11)

Q:=\mathrm{Ric}_{g}+\delta^{*}V=0.

Geometrically, perhaps the most natural choice of gauge is the choice of (generalized) harmonic or wave coordinates

(2.12)

V_{g}=\Box_{g}x^{\alpha}\partial_{x^{\alpha}}.

This gauge will be used throughout the paper. It is well-known that the gauge (
2.12
) can be naturally globalized to all of
M
, cf.
[
2
]
for
instance.

The gauged version of the map
\Phi
is then given by

(2.13)

\Phi^{H}:Met(M)\rightarrow{\mathcal{T}}^{H},

\Phi^{H}(g)=\big((\mathrm{Ric}_{g}+\delta_{g}^{*}V_{g}),(g_{S},K,\nu_{S},V|_{S}),g_{{\mathcal{C}}},V|_{{\mathcal{C}}}\big),

where
V|_{S}
and
V|_{{\mathcal{C}}}
are the restrictions of
V_{g}
to
S
and
{\mathcal{C}}
respectively and
{\mathcal{T}}^{H}=[\widehat{\mathcal{T}}_{0}\times\mathbb{V}_{S}\times\mathbb{V}_{{\mathcal{C}}}]_{c}
; we
refer again to §2.3 for a detailed description of the target space
{\mathcal{T}}^{H}
, cf. (
2.49
).

The overall method of proof of well-posedness is to prove that
\Phi^{H}
in (
2.13
) is a tame Fredholm map with a tame approximate inverse at the
linearized level and to then apply the Nash implicit function theorem in Frechet spaces. Thus, most of the analysis to follow deals with the study of the linearization

(2.14)

D\Phi_{g}^{H}:T_{g}Met(M)\rightarrow T_{\tau}({\mathcal{T}}^{H}),

where
\tau=\Phi^{H}(g)
. Actually the understanding of the map
D\Phi_{g}^{H}
will be developed through the study of the modified map
D\widetilde{\Phi}^{H}
introduced in
§2.5 below.

The bulk term in the linearization
D\Phi_{g}^{H}
is given by

(2.15)

L(h)=\mathrm{Ric}^{\prime}_{h}+\delta^{*}V^{\prime}_{h}+(\delta^{*})^{\prime}_{h}V.

The linearization of the gauge field
V=V_{g}
is given by

(2.16)

V^{\prime}_{h}=\beta_{g}h-\langle D^{2}x^{\alpha},h\rangle\partial_{x^{\alpha}},

where
\beta_{g}=\delta_{g}+\frac{1}{2}d\mathrm{tr}_{g}h
is the Bianchi operator.

The equation
L(h)=F
then has the form

(2.17)

L(h)=-\tfrac{1}{2}D^{*}Dh+S(\partial h)=F,

where
S
is a first order linear operator on
h
and
D^{*}D
is the rough wave operator on symmetric bilinear forms.
Of course all the coefficients of the derivatives in (
2.17
) depend on the background metric
g
at which the linearization is formed.

The equation (
2.17
) may be written in local coordinates in the form

L(h)=-\tfrac{1}{2}(\Box_{g}h_{\alpha\beta})dx^{\alpha}dx^{\beta}+P_{\alpha\beta}(\partial h)dx^{\alpha}dx^{\beta}=F_{\alpha\beta}dx^{\alpha}dx^{\beta},

where
P(\partial h)
involves
h
and its first derivatives. In the localized context discussed in §2.4, the coefficients of
P
are
all of order
\varepsilon
,
P(\partial h)=\varepsilon(\partial h)
. Since
L
is uncoupled at leading order, it may be written as a coupled system of scalar
wave equations

(2.18)

(L(h))_{\alpha\beta}=-\tfrac{1}{2}\Box_{g}(h_{\alpha\beta})+P_{\alpha\beta}(\partial h)=F_{\alpha\beta}.

In later sections, we will repeatedly need to solve linear wave equations of the form (
2.18
), i.e.

(2.19)

-\tfrac{1}{2}\Box_{g}v+p(\partial v)=\varphi,

for given
\varphi
. Here
v
may be either scalar valued or vector-valued. Although solvability of the linear equation
or system (
2.19
) holds more generally, we will only need to actually solve such systems in
C^{\infty}
, so with
g
,
p
and

\varphi
in
C^{\infty}
.

The next Lemma is well-known and will be used repeatedly in §3 and §4.

Lemma 2.3
.

For either Dirichlet or Sommerfeld boundary data, the
C^{\infty}
system (
2.19
) is solvable for any smooth
\varphi
, with standard energy estimates.

Proof.

The existence and uniqueness of solutions to (
2.19
) in Sobolev spaces, with given initial data and with either Dirichlet or
Sommerfeld boundary data is standard, cf.
[
11
]
,
[
19
]
. We assume implicitly here that the initial data and boundary data for
v
satisfy the
requisite compatibility conditions at the corner
\Sigma
, cf. §2.3. Energy estimates for such equations are also standard; for later reference,
we state the details here.

The strong or boundary stable energy estimate with Dirichlet boundary data states that any (smooth) solution of (
2.19
) satisfies
the bound

(2.20)

||v||_{\bar{\mathcal{H}}^{s}(S_{t})}^{2}\leq C[||v||_{H^{s}(S_{0})}^{2}+||\partial_{t}v||_{H^{s-1}(S_{0})}^{2}+||v||_{H^{s}({\mathcal{C}}_{t})}^{2}+||\varphi||_{H^{s-1}(M_{t})}^{2}],

where
C
is a constant depending only on
g
and the coefficients of
p
. Note the difference in the stronger and weaker norms on the left
and right of (
2.20
). We recall that
{\mathcal{C}}_{t}=\{p\in{\mathcal{C}}:t(p)\leq t\}
and similarly for
M_{t}
.

There is a similar estimate for Sommerfeld boundary data, where the boundary data is given by

b(v)=(\partial_{t}+\nu_{{\mathcal{C}}})(v).

In this case, one has

(2.21)

||v||_{\bar{\mathcal{H}}^{s}(S_{t})}^{2}\leq C[||v||_{H^{s}(S_{0})}^{2}+||\partial_{t}v||_{H^{s-1}(S_{0})}^{2}+||b(v)||_{H^{s-1}({\mathcal{C}}_{t})}^{2}+||\varphi||_{H^{s-1}(M_{t})}^{2}].

Similar results hold for
b(v)=(a\partial_{t}+b\nu_{{\mathcal{C}}})(v)
provided
a>0
,
b>0
. For both (
2.20
) and (
2.21
), we assume

t\in I=[0,1]
.

∎

Remark 2.4
.

One also has existence and uniqueness for smooth solutions of the equation (
2.19
) with given Neumann boundary data
b(v)=\nu_{{\mathcal{C}}}(v)
.
However, in this case, there is no effective energy estimate as in (
2.20
) or (
2.21
). Instead there is such an estimate but with a loss
of derivative - or more precisely a loss of half-a-derivative, i.e.

(2.22)

|v||_{\bar{\mathcal{H}}^{s}(S_{t})}^{2}\leq C[||v||_{H^{s}(S_{0})}^{2}+||\partial_{t}v||_{H^{s-1}(S_{0})}^{2}+||\nu_{{\mathcal{C}}}(v)||_{H^{s-1/2}({\mathcal{C}}_{t})}^{2}+||\varphi||_{H^{s-1}(M_{t})}^{2}].

We refer to
[
17
, Ch.8]
,
[
21
]
for further details. In some situations, the bound on
||\nu_{{\mathcal{C}}}(v)||_{H^{s-1/2}({\mathcal{C}}_{t})}
can be improved to a stronger

H^{s}
bound. However, it cannot be improved to an effective
H^{s}
bound as in (
2.20
) or (
2.21
).

We next discuss the gauge term
V
and its linearization
V^{\prime}_{h}
. Consider the equation

(2.23)

\Phi^{H}(g)=\mathrm{Ric}_{g}+\delta_{g}^{*}V=Q,

where
Q
is given data in the target data space
{\mathcal{T}}^{H}
. We will need the following results on the gauge field from
[
2
]
.
For completeness we state the full result but refer to
[
2
, [Lemmas 3.2-3.3]
for the proof.

Lemma 2.5
.

The gauge field
V
satisfies the equation

(2.24)

-\tfrac{1}{2}(\Box V+\mathrm{Ric}_{g}(V))=\beta(Q).

on
M
, where
\Box
denotes the vector wave operator
\Box=-D^{*}D
acting on vector fields. The initial data
V|_{S}
,
\partial_{t}V|_{S}
and
boundary data
V|_{{\mathcal{C}}}
are determined by the target data in
{\mathcal{T}}^{H}
. Thus such data uniquely determine
V
.

Similarly, for the linearization
L(h)=F
as in (
2.17
), one has

(2.25)

-\tfrac{1}{2}(\Box_{g}V^{\prime}_{h}+\mathrm{Ric}_{g}(V^{\prime}_{h}))+\tfrac{1}{2}\nabla_{V}V^{\prime}_{h}=\beta F+\beta^{\prime}_{h}\mathrm{Ric}+O_{2,1}(V,h)),

where
O_{2,1}(V,h)
is second order in
V
, first order in
h
and vanishes if
V=0
.

The initial data
V^{\prime}_{h}|_{S}
,
\partial_{t}V^{\prime}_{h}|_{S}
and boundary data
V^{\prime}_{h}|_{{\mathcal{C}}}
are determined by the target space data
in
T({\mathcal{T}}^{H})
.

Again following
[
2
]
, the gauge variation
V^{\prime}_{h}
is decomposed as

(2.26)

V^{\prime}_{h}=V^{\prime}_{F}+W^{\prime}_{h},

where
V^{\prime}_{F}=V^{\prime}_{F}({\mathcal{T}})
is the vector field satisfying

(2.27)

-\tfrac{1}{2}[\Box V^{\prime}_{F}+\mathrm{Ric}(V^{\prime}_{F})]=\beta F,

with
(V^{\prime}_{F})|_{S}=(V^{\prime}_{h})|_{S}
,
(\partial_{t}V^{\prime}_{F})|_{S}=(\partial_{t}V^{\prime}_{h})|_{S}
along
S
and
(V^{\prime}_{F})|_{{\mathcal{C}}}=V^{\prime}_{h}|_{{\mathcal{C}}}
along
{\mathcal{C}}
. Thus

V^{\prime}_{F}
is uniquely determined by the target space data
T({\mathcal{T}}^{H})
(so is independent of
h
). The remaining component

W^{\prime}_{h}
then satisfies

(2.28)

-\tfrac{1}{2}[\Box W^{\prime}_{h}+\mathrm{Ric}(W^{\prime}_{h})]=\beta^{\prime}_{h}\mathrm{Ric}+O_{2,1}(V,h)),

with
W^{\prime}_{h}|_{S}=\partial_{t}W^{\prime}_{h}|_{S}=W^{\prime}_{h}|_{{\mathcal{C}}}=0
.

2.3.
Geometry at the corner

Next we discuss the compatibility conditions on the initial and boundary data at the corner
\Sigma
, and the corresponding structure of the metric
at
\Sigma
.

Any smooth metric
g
on
M
induces initial data on
S
and boundary data on
{\mathcal{C}}
. The smoothness of
g
imposes compatibility
conditions on these sets of data. Conversely, the compatibility conditions on the (abstract or target) initial and boundary data are the conditions
that such smooth data extend to a smooth metric on
M
.

In more detail, let
{\mathcal{U}}
denote the uncoupled space of target data, so that

{\mathcal{U}}=Sym^{2}(M)\times\mathcal{I}\times\mathbb{V}_{S}^{\prime}\times\mathbb{V}_{S}\times{\mathcal{B}}\times\mathbb{V}_{{\mathcal{C}}}.

This is a product space, where
S^{2}(M)
is the space of symmetric forms
Q
with the
H^{s-2}
topology, (cf. (
2.23
)),
\mathcal{I}
is the space of
initial data
(\gamma_{S},\kappa)
with the
H^{s}(S)\times H^{s-1}(S)
topology,
\mathbb{V}^{\prime}_{S}
is the space of
H^{s}
vector fields along
S
nowhere tangent to
S
,

\mathbb{V}_{S}
is the space of
H^{s-1}
vector fields
V_{S}
along
S
,
{\mathcal{B}}
is the space of boundary data
\gamma_{{\mathcal{C}}}
with the
H^{s-1/2}
topology as in §2.1
while
\mathbb{V}_{{\mathcal{C}}}
is the space of
H^{s-3/2}
vector fields
V_{{\mathcal{C}}}
along
{\mathcal{C}}
. By construction,
{\mathcal{U}}
is a separable Hilbert space.

The metric
g
induces data on
{\mathcal{U}}
through the map
\Phi^{H}
. The two equations for
g
, namely the bulk equation
\mathrm{Ric}_{g}+\delta^{*}V_{g}=Q
in (
2.23
)
and the definition of the gauge field
V_{g}
in (
2.12
), as well as definition of the normal vector
\nu_{S}
in (
2.34
), determine compatibility
conditions for
g
at the corner
\Sigma
. These need to be expressed in terms of equations for the data in
{\mathcal{U}}
. The gauged target space

{\mathcal{U}}_{c}=[\mathcal{I}\times\mathbb{V}_{S}^{\prime}\times\mathbb{V}_{S}\times{\mathcal{B}}\times\mathbb{V}_{{\mathcal{C}}}]_{c}\subset{\mathcal{U}}

is then the subspace satisfying these compatibility conditions. The compatibility conditions will be expressed as the zero-locus of a (large) collection of
functions on
{\mathcal{U}}
, ordered according to the degree of differentiability.

We note that these compatibility conditions are invariant under smooth diffeomorphisms
\varphi
which
map
\varphi:S\rightarrow S
,
\varphi:{\mathcal{C}}\rightarrow{\mathcal{C}}
and
\varphi:\Sigma\rightarrow\Sigma
. Due to this, we may assume without loss of generality that the metric
\gamma_{{\mathcal{C}}}
on
{\mathcal{C}}

is in Gaussian (or Fermi) geodesic coordinates, i.e.

\gamma_{{\mathcal{C}}}=-dt^{2}+\gamma_{\Sigma_{t}}

where
\gamma_{\Sigma_{t}}
is a curve of metrics on
\Sigma_{t}
. Similarly, we may assume
\gamma_{S}
has the form

\gamma_{S}=dx_{1}^{2}+\gamma_{\Sigma_{x^{1}}},

where
\gamma_{\Sigma_{x^{1}}}
is a curve of metrics on the level sets
\Sigma_{x^{1}}
of
x^{1}:S\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
. These assumptions simplify some of the discussion to follow.

We begin with the space
\mathcal{I}\times{\mathcal{B}}
of initial data
(\gamma_{S},\kappa)
and boundary data
\gamma_{{\mathcal{C}}}
. The
C^{0}
compatibility of this data requires that

(2.29)

(\gamma_{S}-\gamma_{{\mathcal{C}}})|_{\Sigma}=0.

This is the only
C^{0}
compatibility condition between initial and boundary data
\mathcal{I}\times{\mathcal{B}}
in
{\mathcal{U}}
. It follows that the full ambient metric
g
is determined
at the corner, except possibly for the angle term
\alpha=\langle\nu_{S},\nu_{{\mathcal{C}}}\rangle
between
S
and
{\mathcal{C}}
; here
\nu_{S}
is the future unit normal to
S
in

(M,g)
and
\nu_{{\mathcal{C}}}
is the outward unit normal to
{\mathcal{C}}
in
(M,g)
. From the geodesic normalizations above, it is easy to see
that
\partial_{t}
is the future unit normal of
\Sigma\subset({\mathcal{C}},\gamma_{{\mathcal{C}}})
and
\partial_{x^{1}}=N
is the unit outward normal of
\Sigma\subset(S,\gamma_{S})
.
Also,

\alpha=\langle\nu_{S},\nu_{{\mathcal{C}}}\rangle=-\langle\partial_{t},\partial_{x^{1}}\rangle=-g_{01},

and

(2.30)

\partial_{t}=\sqrt{1+\alpha^{2}}\nu_{S}-\alpha\partial_{x^{1}}.

The following Lemma shows that
\alpha
is in fact determined by the initial and boundary data under Assumption
(*)
, so that

\alpha=\alpha(\gamma_{S},\kappa,\gamma_{{\mathcal{C}}}),

is well-defined as a function on
\mathcal{I}\times{\mathcal{B}}
and hence on
{\mathcal{U}}
via the projection
{\mathcal{U}}\rightarrow\mathcal{I}\times{\mathcal{B}}
.

Lemma 2.7
.

For any
C^{1}
metric
g
, one has the relation

(2.31)

H_{\Sigma}^{{\mathcal{C}}}=\sqrt{1+\alpha^{2}}\mathrm{tr}_{\Sigma}\kappa-\alpha H_{\Sigma}^{S},

where
H_{\Sigma}^{S}
and
H_{\Sigma}^{{\mathcal{C}}}
are the mean curvatures of
\Sigma
in
(S,\gamma_{S})
and
({\mathcal{C}},\gamma_{{\mathcal{C}}})
respectively.
Consequently, under Assumption (*),
\alpha
is determined by the data
(\gamma_{S},\kappa,\gamma_{{\mathcal{C}}})
.

Proof.

The formula (
2.31
) follows directly by taking the trace of the covariant derivative of
\partial_{t}
in (
2.30
).
Since all the terms
H_{\Sigma}^{S}
,
H_{\Sigma}^{{\mathcal{C}}}
and
\mathrm{tr}_{\Sigma}\kappa
are determined by the initial and boundary data, it follows
that
\alpha
is uniquely determined, up to a sign choice, by the initial and boundary data, provided one of
\mathrm{tr}_{\Sigma}K\neq 0
or
H_{\Sigma}^{S}\neq 0
.
However, if both are zero, then
tr_{\Sigma}A_{{\mathcal{C}}}=0
. This is impossible by Assumption (*).

Given a fixed value for the initial and boundary data, and so for
H_{\Sigma}^{{\mathcal{C}}},\mathrm{tr}_{\Sigma}\kappa,H_{\Sigma}^{S}
, the (generically) two values
\alpha_{\pm}
of
\alpha

satisfying (
2.31
) are mapped to each other by the sign or orientation change
\nu_{S}\rightarrow-\nu_{S}
. As will be seen later from the proof of
Theorem
1.1
, this orientation change, corresponding to future or past evolutions, gives rise to solutions
(M^{\pm},g^{\pm})
which
are isometric under an orientation reversing diffeomorphism, cf. Remark
7.3
for concrete examples. Thus fixing the choice of orientation
fixes the angle
\alpha
.

∎

We note that without Assumption (*), so if both terms
\mathrm{tr}_{\Sigma}K
,
H_{\Sigma}^{S}
above vanish, perhaps even in a small open set in
\Sigma
,
then
\alpha
remains undetermined; this may reflect a lack of well-posedness/uniqueness, as in
[
2
]
, when Assumption
(*)
is dropped.

This shows that the ambient metric
g_{\alpha\beta}
is uniquely determined along
\Sigma
by
(\gamma_{S},\kappa,\gamma_{{\mathcal{C}}})
. The
C^{1}
compatibility condition is then given by

(2.32)

{\mathcal{L}}_{\partial_{0}}\gamma_{{\mathcal{C}}}|_{\Sigma}-\sqrt{1+\alpha^{2}}\kappa|_{\Sigma}+\alpha B_{\Sigma}=0,

where
B_{\Sigma}={\mathcal{L}}_{\partial_{1}}\gamma_{S}|_{\Sigma}
is (twice) the second fundamental form of
\Sigma\subset(S,\gamma_{S})
. Note that the
\gamma_{\Sigma}
trace of
(
2.32
) is equivalent to (
2.31
). We write this in local coordinates as

(2.33)

\partial_{0}\gamma_{AB}-\chi_{1}(\partial\gamma_{S},\kappa,\partial g_{\mathcal{C}})=0.

Here and in the following,
\chi,\zeta,\xi
will denote general smooth functions of the jets of the indicated data in
{\mathcal{U}}
at
\Sigma
, with the subscript indicating
the leading order of derivatives. The equation (
2.33
) implies that the full second fundamental form of
\Sigma\subset({\mathcal{C}},\gamma_{{\mathcal{C}}})
is determined by its
trace, i.e. by
H_{\Sigma}^{{\mathcal{C}}}
, given
(\gamma_{S},\kappa)
. In the following, the specific form of
\chi,\zeta,\xi
may change from line to line, but without a change in the notation.

Remark 2.8
.

The discussion above shows that the
C^{1}
jet of the metric

at
\Sigma
is determined by the initial and boundary data of

in
{\mathcal{U}}
and

and

,

Next we discuss the compatibility conditions on the remaining initial and boundary data
\nu=\nu_{S}
and
V_{S}
,
V_{{\mathcal{C}}}
. First for
\nu
,
we have

(2.34)

\nu_{S}=-(|\nabla t|)^{-1}\nabla t=-(|\nabla t|)^{-1}g^{0\alpha}\partial_{\alpha},\ |\nabla t|=\sqrt{-g^{00}}.

At
\Sigma
, this gives the

(2.35)

\nu-(1+\alpha^{2})^{-1/2}(\partial_{t}-\alpha\partial_{x^{1}})=0,

\nu-\chi_{0}(\alpha)=0.

This couples the
C^{0}
behavior of

. There are no

.

Next we discuss the gauge field

. The data

and

condition

(2.36)

(V_{S}-V_{{\mathcal{C}}})|_{\Sigma}=0.

This is the only compatibility condition for

itself; it ensures that

and

extend to a smooth vector field on

, when

and
V_{{\mathcal{C}}}
are smooth vector fields on

and

respectively.

Next, the gauge field
V
couples to the other data
(\gamma_{S},\kappa,\nu,\gamma_{{\mathcal{C}}})
by the definition (

), which we rewrite in the form

(2.37)

V^{\alpha}=\Box_{g}x^{\alpha}=\partial_{\mu}g^{\mu\alpha}+\tfrac{1}{2}g^{\alpha\mu}g^{\rho\sigma}\partial_{\mu}g_{\rho\sigma}.

Setting
\alpha=1
gives (since
g^{11}=1
along
S
)

\begin{split}V^{1}&=\partial_{0}g^{01}+\tfrac{1}{2}g^{10}\partial_{0}g_{11}+g^{01}g^{01}\partial_{0}g_{01}+\tfrac{1}{2}g^{10}g^{AB}\partial_{0}g_{AB}+g^{01}\partial_{1}g_{01}+\tfrac{1}{2}g^{AB}\partial_{1}g_{AB}\\

Notice that

so this is not a compatibility equation. Instead, this equation determines
\partial_{0}g_{01}
(equivalent to
\partial_{0}g^{01}

up to determined terms):

\partial_{0}g_{01}=\chi_{1}(V^{1},\partial\gamma_{S},\kappa,\partial\nu,\partial\gamma_{\mathcal{C}}).

It follows that all the first derivatives
\partial_{\rho}g_{\alpha\beta}
are determined by the data
(V^{1},\partial\gamma_{S},\kappa,\partial\nu,\partial\gamma_{\mathcal{C}})
at
\Sigma
, and hence the components

are uniquely determined and satisfy the compatibility condition:

(2.38)

V^{a}=\chi_{1}^{a}(V^{1},\partial\gamma_{S},\kappa,\partial\nu,\partial\gamma_{\mathcal{C}}),\ \ a=0,2,\dots,n.

Let
{\mathcal{U}}_{c}^{1}\subset{\mathcal{U}}
be the subspace satisfying the first order compatibility conditions above. The data in
{\mathcal{U}}_{c}^{1}

determines the full 1-jet of
g_{\alpha\beta}
at
\Sigma
. For simplicity of notation, we suppress here the index
s
denoting the
H^{s}
differentiability

We will need the Lemma below and those to follow for the non-linear analysis in §6.

Lemma 2.9
.

For
s
sufficiently large (depending only on the dimension

{\mathcal{U}}_{c}^{1}
is a closed Hilbert submanifold of
{\mathcal{U}}
.

Proof.

Define the following map

W^{1}:{\mathcal{U}}\rightarrow Sym(\Sigma)^{2}\times\mathbb{V}^{\prime}(\Sigma)\times\mathbb{V}(\Sigma)\times\mathbb{V}_{{\mathcal{C}}}(\Sigma),

W^{1}(\gamma_{S},\kappa,\nu_{S},V_{S},\gamma_{{\mathcal{C}}},V_{{\mathcal{C}}})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}(\gamma_{S}-\gamma_{{\mathcal{C}}})|_{\Sigma}\\
\partial_{t}\gamma_{{\mathcal{C}}}-\chi(\partial\gamma_{S},\kappa,\partial g_{\mathcal{C}})\\
\nu-\chi(\alpha)\\
(V_{{\mathcal{C}}}-V_{S})|_{\Sigma}\\
V_{S}^{a}-\chi^{a}(V_{S}^{1},\partial\gamma_{S},\kappa,\partial\nu,\partial\gamma_{\mathcal{C}})\\
\end{array}\right.

In the above, all data on the right are evaluated at
\Sigma
; also
\mathbb{V}_{{\mathcal{C}}}(\Sigma)
denotes vector fields at
\Sigma
tangent to
{\mathcal{C}}
.
By definition, the inverse image
W^{-1}({\bf 0})={\mathcal{U}}_{c}^{1}
.

We claim that
W^{1}
is a submersion at
{\mathcal{U}}_{c}^{1}
, i.e. the derivative
DW^{1}
is everywhere surjective on
{\mathcal{U}}_{c}^{1}
. To see this, consider

(0,0,\nu^{\prime},V_{S}^{\prime},\gamma_{{\mathcal{C}}}^{\prime},V_{{\mathcal{C}}}^{\prime})\in T({\mathcal{U}})

\begin{split}&DW^{1}(0,0,\nu^{\prime},V_{S}^{\prime},\gamma_{{\mathcal{C}}}^{\prime},V_{{\mathcal{C}}}^{\prime})=\begin{cases}-\gamma^{\prime}_{{\mathcal{C}}}\\
\partial_{t}(\gamma_{{\mathcal{C}}}^{\prime})-\chi(\alpha^{\prime})\\
\nu^{\prime}-\chi(\alpha^{\prime})\\
V_{S}^{\prime}-V_{{\mathcal{C}}}^{\prime}\\
(V_{S})^{a})^{\prime}-\chi^{a}((V_{S}^{1})^{\prime},\nu_{S}^{\prime},\gamma_{{\mathcal{C}}}^{\prime})\\
\end{cases}\end{split}

Again, all data on the right are evaluated at
\Sigma
. Using the triangular structure, it is easy to see this map is surjective. First
\gamma_{{\mathcal{C}}}^{\prime}
may be prescribed
arbitrarily. Next,
H^{\prime}_{{\mathcal{C}}}

The kernel

is thus a closed subspace of a Hilbert space, and so has a closed complement. It then follows from the implicit function theorem for

{\mathcal{U}}
.

This compatibility equation couples the second order jet of

in the

Next we discuss the compatibility conditions on

\begin{split}0&=-\tfrac{1}{2}\partial_{0}\partial_{0}g_{0a}+g^{01}\partial_{1}\partial_{0}g_{0a}+\tfrac{1}{2}\partial_{1}^{2}g_{0a}+P_{0a}(\partial g)-Q_{0a}\\

(If

(2.45)

Lemma 2.10
.

For

sufficiently large (depending only on the dimension

(1)

(2.47)

2.4.
Localization

As usual with hyperbolic PDE problems, the main existence results will first be proved locally, in regions where the constant coefficient
approximation is effective. Such local solutions will then be assembled together, by an essentially standard process, via rescaling and a partition of unity,
The discussion in this section is similar to that in
[
2
]
, to which we refer for some details, with one important exception noted below.

Given a metric
g
on
M
as in §1, the localization at a point
p\in\Sigma
is a (small) neighborhood
U\subset M
of
p
, diffeomorphic via
a local chart to a Minkowski corner

\mathbf{R}=\{(t=x^{0},x^{1},\dots,x^{n}):t\geq 0,x^{1}\leq 0\}

with
S\cap U\subset\{t=0\}
,
{\mathcal{C}}\cap U\subset\{x^{1}=0\}
and
x^{\alpha}(p)=0
.

In such a metrically small neighborhood
U
, as usual we renormalize the metric and coordinates simultaneously by rescaling; thus
for
\lambda
small, set

\widetilde{g}=\lambda^{-2}g,\ \ \widetilde{x}^{\alpha}=\lambda^{-1}x^{\alpha}

so that
\partial_{\widetilde{x}^{\alpha}}=\lambda\partial_{x^{\alpha}}
and

(2.51)

\widetilde{g}(\partial_{\widetilde{x}^{\alpha}},\partial_{\widetilde{x}^{\beta}})|_{\widetilde{x}}=g(\partial_{x^{\alpha}},\partial_{x^{\beta}})|_{\lambda x}.

The equation (
2.51
) holds in the same way for any variation
h=\frac{d}{ds}(g+sh)|_{s=0}
of
g
. Note that while the components

g_{\alpha\beta}
of
g
are invariant under such a rescaling, all higher derivatives become small:

(2.52)

\partial_{\widetilde{x}^{\mu}}^{k}\widetilde{g}_{\alpha\beta}=\lambda^{k}\partial_{x^{\mu}}^{k}g_{\alpha\beta}=O(\lambda^{k}).

Thus the coefficients are close to constant functions in the rescaled chart,

(2.53)

||\widetilde{g}-g_{\alpha_{0}}||_{C^{\infty}(U)}\leq\varepsilon=\varepsilon(\lambda,g),

where
g_{\alpha_{0}}
is a flat (constant coefficent) Minkowski metric. This is the frozen coefficient approximation.

The
\lambda\rightarrow 0
limit blow-up metric
g_{\alpha_{0}}
is the Minkowski metric, given in adapted coordinates by

g_{\alpha_{0}}=-dt^{2}-\alpha_{0}dtdx^{1}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}(dx^{i})^{2}.

Here
\alpha_{0}=\alpha(p)
is the value of the corner angle at
p
.

It is important to note here however that these limit Minkowski metrics do
not
satisfy Assumption (*). Thus, while
\lambda
is small, the
limit
\lambda\rightarrow 0
is never taken in this work. We only consider metrics
g
, and the linearizations at such
g
, which satisfy (*).

For later use, we record how the data in
{\mathcal{T}}^{H}
and the Sobolev norms behave under rescaling. Thus, while
\mathrm{Ric}_{\widetilde{g}}=\mathrm{Ric}_{g}
as abstract
symmetric bilinear forms, in local coordinates one has

(\mathrm{Ric}_{\widetilde{g}})_{\widetilde{\alpha}\widetilde{\beta}}=\lambda^{2}(\mathrm{Ric}_{g})_{\alpha\beta},

(K_{\widetilde{g}})_{\widetilde{\alpha}\widetilde{\beta}}=\lambda(K_{g})_{\alpha\beta},

(\widetilde{\nu}_{S})^{\widetilde{\alpha}}=\nu_{S}^{\alpha}

(\widetilde{V})^{\widetilde{\alpha}}=\lambda V^{\alpha}.

Similarly, for
D\subset S
or
D\subset{\mathcal{C}}
of dimension
n
,

||f||_{H_{\widetilde{g}}^{s}(D)}^{2}=\lambda^{n-2s}||f||_{H_{g}^{s}(D)}^{2},

||f||_{{\mathcal{N}}_{\widetilde{g}}^{s}(M)}^{2}=\lambda^{n-2s}||f||_{{\mathcal{N}}_{g}^{s}(M)}^{2}.

Also as in Part I, we will always assume that
U
is embedded in a larger region
\widetilde{U}
, so

U\subset\widetilde{U},

with
\widetilde{U}
still covered by the adapted coordinates
(t,x^{i})
, with
t\geq 0
and
x^{1}\leq 0
in
\widetilde{U}
so that the initial surface
S
, boundary

{\mathcal{C}}
and corner
\Sigma
in
\widetilde{U}
are an extension of the corresponding domains in
U
. We also assume (
2.53
) still holds in
\widetilde{U}
.
All target data in
\widehat{\mathcal{T}}
and later
{\mathcal{T}}^{H}
given in
U
is extended off
U
to be of compact support in
\widetilde{U}
away
from
S\cap U
and
{\mathcal{C}}\cap U
. In particular, all target data vanishes in a neighborhood of the full timelike boundary of
\widetilde{U}
and in
a neighborhood of the initial slice
\{t=0\}
away from
{\mathcal{C}}\cap U
and
S\cap U
respectively. The same statements hold for variations of the
target data, i.e. in
T(\widehat{\mathcal{T}})
or
T({\mathcal{T}}^{H})
.

For later reference, we note that for the linear systems of wave equations on
\widetilde{U}
appearing in §3 and §4, the finite propagation speed
property implies that solutions
h
of the equations also have compact support in
\widetilde{U}
away from
S\cap U
and
{\mathcal{C}}\cap U
, for some
definite (possibly small) time
t>0
.

2.5.
Modified boundary data.

Based on prior work and analogies with the Riemannian setting, it is not to be expected that well-posedness of the Dirichlet IBVP can be proved directly.
As noted in §1, some extra hypothesis such as Assumption
(*)
must be invoked.

Instead, we proceed indirectly, based on the analogous procedure used in the elliptic or Riemannian case. As noted in the Introduction,
this procedure originates in the work of Nash on isometric embeddings of Riemannian metrics into
\mathbb{R}^{N}
.

Given
g
, consider arbitrary variations
h=\frac{d}{dt}(g+th)
of
g
. Let
h^{\mathsf{T}}
denote the restriction of
h
to the boundary
{\mathcal{C}}
,
so
h^{\mathsf{T}}
gives the variation of the induced metric
g_{{\mathcal{C}}}
on the boundary. Consider the equivalence relation

(2.54)

h^{\mathsf{T}}\sim h^{\mathsf{T}}+fA,

for any function
f
. The relation (
2.54
) is well-defined only if
A
never vanishes; this is ensured by Assumption
(*)
. Also, for
g
or
h

in
{\mathcal{N}}^{s}(M)
, the Sobolev trace theorem (
2.3
) gives
g_{{\mathcal{C}}},h^{\mathsf{T}}\in H^{s-1/2}({\mathcal{C}})
, while generically
A\in H^{s-3/2}({\mathcal{C}})
which is not
contained in
H^{s-1/2}({\mathcal{C}})
. Thus (strictly speaking) the relation (
2.54
) is not defined on function spaces having only finite differentiability.
For this reason, we assume henceforth that
g\in C^{\infty}(M)
. In this case, (
2.54
) is well-defined.

Let
[h]_{A}
denote equivalence class of
h^{\mathsf{T}}
in (
2.54
). The representative of
[h]_{A}
is chosen to be
h_{A}
given by

(2.55)

\langle h_{A},A\rangle=0,

pointwise.

Let
\nu=\nu_{{\mathcal{C}}}
be the unit normal vector field to
{\mathcal{C}}
in
M
. Note that

fA=[\delta^{*}(f\nu)]^{\mathsf{T}}=\tfrac{1}{2}[{\mathcal{L}}_{(f\nu)}g]^{\mathsf{T}}.

Thus, let
X
be a smooth extension of
f\nu
to a vector field on
M
and let
h=\delta^{*}X
. Then at
{\mathcal{C}}
,

(2.56)

h^{\mathsf{T}}=(\delta^{*}X)^{\mathsf{T}}=fA,\ \ h(\nu,\cdot)=(\delta^{*}X)(\nu,\cdot)=df.

Even though well-known, it is worth emphasizing the following two points here.

\bullet
At the boundary
{\mathcal{C}}
, the deformation
\delta^{*}X
is algebraic in
f
in boundary tangential directions, but is a first order
derivative operator on
f
in normal directions.

\bullet
The deformation
\delta^{*}X
corresponds to an infinitesimal displacement of the boundary
{\mathcal{C}}
in the normal
direction
f\nu
of the background
(M,g)
. Thus, such deformations move the boundary
{\mathcal{C}}
within a fixed background metric;
see Remark
2.14
for a more detailed discussion of the nonlinear setting, as opposed to the linearized setting here.

Although the deformation
h=\delta^{*}X
is perhaps the most natural extension of the boundary deformation
fA
of
h^{\mathsf{T}}
, it is important to
note that this deformation
\delta^{*}X
will
not
be used until the later parts of the main analysis; it only appears in the proof of Theorem
3.10

relating the modified boundary data (discussed next) with the Dirichlet boundary data. Instead, the extension of the deformation
fA
of
g_{{\mathcal{C}}}

to a
h
deformation of
g
on
M
is done locally in coordinates without involving any components of the form
dx^{1}\cdot dx^{\alpha}
,
cf. Remark
3.8
below.

Having reduced the Dirichlet data on
{\mathcal{C}}

(using the fact that the initial data on
\Sigma_{0}
vanish). The last term here is again bounded by
K
by the previous
arguments, so that

(4.41)

D\leq\int_{{\mathcal{C}}_{t}}\langle\delta^{*}(h(\nu)^{\mathsf{T}}),\dot{f}\widehat{\Pi}\rangle+cK.

To handle this term, we use the standard formula for variation
A^{\prime}_{h}
of the second fundamental form:
A=\frac{1}{2}{\mathcal{L}}_{\nu}g
, so
that
A^{\prime}_{h}=\frac{1}{2}{\mathcal{L}}_{\nu}h+{\mathcal{L}}_{\nu^{\prime}_{h}}g
. A standard further computation gives the formula

(4.42)

2A^{\prime}_{h}=\nabla_{\nu}h^{\mathsf{T}}-2\delta^{*}h(\nu)^{\mathsf{T}}-h_{\nu\nu}A.

Note here that
A
is not renormalized, so that we are working with
A
and not
\widehat{A}
.
As above, the zero order term in
h
will be ignored. Substituting the first term on the right in (
4.42
) into (
4.41
) and using
(
4.12
) leads to

|\int_{{\mathcal{C}}_{t}}\langle\nabla_{\nu}h^{\mathsf{T}},\dot{f}\widehat{\Pi}\rangle|\leq C[||h_{A}||_{\bar{H}^{1}({\mathcal{C}}_{t})}^{2}+||f||_{\bar{H}^{1}({\mathcal{C}}_{t})}^{2}+\Lambda^{2}\int_{{\mathcal{C}}_{t}}(\dot{f})^{2}]\leq cK.

Thus we have shown so far that

(4.43)

D\leq-\int_{{\mathcal{C}}_{t}}\langle A^{\prime}_{h},\dot{f}\widehat{\Pi}\rangle+cK.

At this point, we return to the formulas (
3.5
)-(
3.6
). On the right hand side of (
3.6
), we have the term
-\langle A^{\prime}_{h},\Pi\rangle=\langle A^{\prime}_{h},A\rangle-H^{\prime}_{h}H_{{\mathcal{C}}}
. In contrast to the right side of (
3.5
) however, here we have
\widehat{\Pi}=\lambda^{-1}\Pi
in place of
\Pi
with
\lambda^{-1}>>1
.
Using (
3.5
), write then

(4.44)

-\int_{{\mathcal{C}}_{t}}\dot{f}\langle A^{\prime}_{h},\widehat{\Pi}\rangle dv_{g_{{\mathcal{C}}}}=\tfrac{1}{2}\lambda^{-1}\int_{{\mathcal{C}}_{t}}\dot{f}\widetilde{\Box}_{{\mathcal{C}}}fdv_{g_{{\mathcal{C}}}}+\dot{f}\lambda^{-1}R,

where
R
consists of the remaining (lower order) terms in (
3.5
), analysed later.

Recall that
\widetilde{\Box}_{{\mathcal{C}}}
is the wave operator with respect to the Lorentz metric
\zeta:=\widehat{\Pi}
and
\zeta(\partial_{t},\partial_{t})<0
. Let
\sqrt{-g_{\zeta}}
be
the volume density of
\zeta
, and write
\sqrt{-g_{{\mathcal{C}}}}=\omega\sqrt{-g_{\zeta}}
, where
\omega>0
and
\sqrt{-g_{{\mathcal{C}}}}
is the volume density of
g_{{\mathcal{C}}}
.

This gives

-\int_{{\mathcal{C}}_{t}}\dot{f}\langle A^{\prime}_{h},\widehat{\Pi}\rangle dv_{g_{{\mathcal{C}}}}=\tfrac{1}{2}\lambda^{-1}\int_{{\mathcal{C}}_{t}}\omega\dot{f}\Box_{\zeta}fdv_{g_{\zeta}}+\dot{f}\lambda^{-1}R.

The term
\int_{{\mathcal{C}}_{t}}\dot{f}\Box_{\zeta}fdv_{g_{\zeta}}
is just the term used to compute the
H^{1}
energy of
f
along
{\mathcal{C}}
. Thus, applying
the divergence theorem (as in the usual proof for scalar energy estimates) gives

(4.45)

\lambda^{-1}\int_{{\mathcal{C}}_{t}}\omega\dot{f}\Box_{\zeta}fdv_{g_{\zeta}}=-\tfrac{1}{2}\lambda^{-1}\int_{\Sigma_{t}}(|\dot{f}|^{2}+|d_{S}f|^{2})dv_{\Sigma_{t}}-\lambda^{-1}\int_{{\mathcal{C}}_{t}}\dot{f}\langle d\ln\omega,df\rangle_{\zeta}dv_{g_{{\mathcal{C}}}}+\dot{f}\lambda^{-1}R.

Now the main point is that the first term on the right is negative, giving the negative term on the right side of (
4.37
). Next, for the
d\ln\omega
term, we have

|d\ln\omega|\sim\lambda
, since
\partial(\widehat{\Pi})=O(\lambda)
. So here the
\lambda
terms cancel, leaving the usual
H^{1}
energy of
f
, which may be easily absorbed into the
previous large negative term.

The proof is then completed by estimating the last
\dot{f}\lambda^{-1}R
term. Here
R
consists of the terms
q(\partial f)
in (
3.5
) and the terms in
(
3.6
) besides the
\langle A^{\prime}_{h},\Pi\rangle
term. A brief inspection shows that the terms in
\lambda^{-1}\dot{f}q(\partial f)
are all bounded by
||f||_{H^{1}({\mathcal{C}})}^{2}
,
and so these terms may be absorbed as above.

For the terms in (
3.6
), beginning with
\dot{f}\lambda^{-1}R^{\prime}_{h_{A}}
, we have

|\int_{{\mathcal{C}}_{t}}\dot{f}\lambda^{-1}R^{\prime}_{h_{A}}|\leq\delta\lambda^{-1}||f||_{H^{1}({\mathcal{C}}_{t})}^{2}+\delta^{-1}\lambda^{-1}||h_{A}||_{H^{2}({\mathcal{C}}_{t})}^{2}.

The first term here is small compared with the
\lambda^{-1}
term in (
4.45
) and so again may be absorbed, while the second term is of the form

\delta^{-1}\lambda^{-1}J_{{\mathcal{T}}}
. (The same analysis holds when
F\neq 0
and for the initial data). Next the terms involving
A^{2}
,
\mathrm{Ric}
are
O(\lambda^{2})
so
these contribute terms controlled by
J_{\lambda}
.

Next we come to the gauge terms:
\delta^{*}V^{\prime}_{h}
and
(\delta^{*})^{\prime}_{h}V
. The normal derivatives
\delta^{*}V^{\prime}_{h}(\nu,\nu)
are controlled by the Dirichlet boundary data

V^{\prime}_{{\mathcal{C}}}
as in (
4.10
) and so are bounded by
J_{\lambda}
. Since
V=O(\lambda)
, the same is true for
(\delta^{*})^{\prime}_{h}V
as well as for the remaining trace
terms in (
3.6
).

This completes the proof of Lemma
4.4
and so also completes the proof of Proposition
4.2
.

∎

Combining the results above leads then quite easily to the following main estimate on
h(\nu)^{\Sigma}
.

Proposition 4.5
.

For
(\widetilde{U},g)
as in Theorem
4.1
, the term
h(\nu)^{\Sigma}
satisfies the estimate

(4.46)

\max_{t\in[0,1]}||h(\nu)^{\Sigma}||_{\bar{H}^{s}(S_{t})}^{2}\leq C\lambda^{-1}[||h_{A}||_{H^{s+1}({\mathcal{C}})}^{2}+||\eta||_{H^{s}({\mathcal{C}})}^{2}+||V^{\prime}_{{\mathcal{C}}}||_{H^{s}({\mathcal{C}})}^{2}]+\lambda^{2}||h||_{H^{s}(\widetilde{U})}^{2},

provided the bulk data
F
, the initial data for
h
and the initial data for the gauge field
V^{\prime}_{h}
vanish at
S
.

Proof.

We first prove the result for
s=1
. By combining the results above, one obtains the estimate

(4.47)

\max_{t\in 0,1}E_{t}^{\nu}+c\lambda^{-1}||f||_{H^{1}({\mathcal{C}})}^{2}\leq\delta\Lambda\max_{t\in[0,1]}E_{t}^{\nu}+\delta^{-1}cJ_{\lambda}+c\lambda^{-1}J_{{\mathcal{T}}}.

Recall that
\Lambda
is a fixed
C^{3}
bound for
g
. Choose
\delta
sufficiently small, depending on
\Lambda
, so that the coefficient
\delta\Lambda<\frac{1}{2}
.
The term
\max_{t\in[0,1]}E_{t}^{\nu}
on the right in (
4.47
) may be absorbed into the left on (
4.47
), which then gives

(4.48)

\begin{split}\max_{t\in[0,1]}||h(\nu)^{\Sigma}||_{\bar{H}^{1}(S_{t})}^{2}+\lambda^{-1}||f||_{H^{1}({\mathcal{C}})}^{2}&\leq C\lambda^{-1}[||h_{A}||_{H^{2}({\mathcal{C}})}^{2}+||\eta_{h}||_{H^{1}({\mathcal{C}})}^{2}+||V^{\prime}_{{\mathcal{C}}}||_{H^{1}({\mathcal{C}})}^{2}]\\
&+c\lambda^{2}||h||_{H^{1}(\widetilde{U})}^{2}+c\lambda^{2}||h||_{H^{1}({\mathcal{C}})}^{2}.\\
\end{split}

The first
\lambda^{2}
term over
\widetilde{U}
comes from the bulk
P(\partial h)
term. Regarding the last
\lambda^{2}
term on the right over
{\mathcal{C}}
, a simple inspection shows that this term
comes only from the original
f
estimate in (
4.12
) and in the Sommerfeld boundary data estimate (
4.13
). For the
f
estimate, the
h
terms appear on the
right of (
3.6
). These terms are exactly the terms analysed in Lemma
4.4
from (

) onwards, but now with

in place of

, i.e. without
the

term. These then are easily absorbed into the other terms of (

) as previously. Similarly, for the Sommerfeld boundary data, the
h
terms appear
only as

terms, so at lower order, and so again are easily absorbed.

Thus, (
4.48
) may be written as

(4.49)

\max_{t\in[0,1]}||h(\nu)^{\Sigma}||_{\bar{H}^{1}(S_{t})}^{2}\leq C\lambda^{-1}[||h_{A}||_{H^{2}({\mathcal{C}})}^{2}+||\eta_{h}||_{H^{1}({\mathcal{C}})}^{2}+||V^{\prime}_{{\mathcal{C}}}||_{H^{1}({\mathcal{C}})}^{2}]+\lambda^{2}||h||_{H^{1}(\widetilde{U})}^{2},

which proves (
4.46
) with
s=1
.

Taking derivatives of (
4.16
) and commuting in the usual way for such energy estimates, cf.
[
11
]
,
[
19
]
, then gives the higher order estimates
(
4.46
).

∎

The estimate (
4.46
) is now an effective improvement and replacement for (
4.15
). Summing up the previous estimates
(
4.8
)-(
4.14
) together with (
4.46
) replacing (

), by choosing
\lambda
sufficiently small depending on

, one may absorb

|h||_{H^{s}(\widetilde{U})}\leq\max_{t\in[0,1]}|h||_{\bar{H}^{s}(S_{t})}
, giving then

(4.50)

\max_{t\in[0,1]}||h||_{\bar{H}^{s}(S_{t})}^{2}\leq C\lambda^{-1}[||h_{A}||_{H^{s+1}}^{2}+||\eta_{h}||_{H^{s}}^{2}+||V^{\prime}_{{\mathcal{C}}}||_{H^{s}({\mathcal{C}})}^{2}].

In the discussion above, we have assumed that

, and that the initial data vanish. By linearity, and by the remarks given previously regarding the

and the initial data, it is then easy to see that (

) gives (

) when this vanishing assumption is dropped. This

.

∎

.

5.
Linear Analysis II

In this section, we prove the global versions of the results of §3 and §4, both for the modified boundary data and for Dirichlet boundary data spaces.
This is done by patching together local solutions to obtain global in space solutions.

The first main result proves existence and uniqueness for the modified boundary data in the linearized setting.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
(M,g)
be a smooth globally hyperbolic Lorentzian manifold,
M\simeq I\times S
, with compact Cauchy surface
S
having compact boundary

\partial S=\Sigma
and with timelike boundary
({\mathcal{C}},g_{{\mathcal{C}}})
. Suppose
g
satisfies the convexity Assumption
(*)
at
{\mathcal{C}}
. Then for any smooth

\widetilde{\tau}^{\prime}\in T_{\widetilde{\tau}}(\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}^{H})
, the equation

(5.1)

D\widetilde{\Phi}_{g}^{H}(h)=\widetilde{\tau}^{\prime}

has a unique
C^{\infty}
solution
h
. The constructed solution
h
depends smoothly on the data
\widetilde{\tau}^{\prime}
.

Moreover, any
h
satisfying (
5.1
) satisfies the tame energy estimate

(5.2)

\max_{t\in[0,1]}||h||_{\bar{H}^{s}(S_{t})}\leq C\lambda^{-1}||D\widetilde{\Phi}_{g}^{H}(h)||_{H^{s}(\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}^{H})}.

Proof.

Given the previous local results, the proof of Theorem
5.1
is essentially standard. We provide the details below,
following the proof of the analogous result in
[
2
]
.

Given
(M,g)
as in Theorem
5.1
, first choose an open cover
\{U_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
of the corner
\Sigma
, where each
U_{i}
is sufficiently
small so that the local existence result Theorem
3.7
holds for each
U_{i}
. Also, choose an open set
U_{0}
in the interior
M\setminus{\mathcal{C}}

such that
(\cup_{i=1}^{N}U_{i})\cup U_{0}
also covers a tubular neighborhood of the initial surface, i.e.
S\times[0,t_{0}]
for some time
t_{0}>0
.

Let
\{\widetilde{U}_{i}\}_{i=0}^{N}
be a thickening of the cover
\{U_{i}\}_{i=0}^{N}
, so that
U_{i}\subset\widetilde{U}_{i}
, as described in §2.4. Let

\rho_{i}
be a partition of unity subordinate to the cover
\{\widetilde{U}_{i}\}_{i=0}^{N}
, so that
{\rm supp}\rho_{i}\subset\widetilde{U}_{i}
,
\rho_{i}=1
on

U_{i}
and
\sum_{i}\rho_{i}=1
. Let

\widetilde{\tau}^{\prime}=\big(F,(\gamma^{\prime},\kappa^{\prime},\nu^{\prime},V_{S}^{\prime})_{S},(h_{A},\eta,V_{{\mathcal{C}}}^{\prime})_{{\mathcal{C}}})\big),

be arbitrary
C^{\infty}
data in
T(\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}^{H})
on
M
. Then the data
\rho_{i}\tau^{\prime}
has compact support in the sense of §2.1 in
\widetilde{U}_{i}

and by Theorem
3.7
there exists a solution
h_{i}
in
\widetilde{U}_{i}
satisfying

(5.3)

D\widetilde{\Phi}^{H}_{g}(h_{i})=\rho_{i}\widetilde{\tau}^{\prime}.

(Here
\widetilde{\tau}^{\prime}
is rescaled by
\lambda
as in §2.4, but the notation will not reflect this). Moreover, as noted at the end of §2.4, by the finite propagation
speed property, the solution
h_{i}
has compact support in the sense of §2.4 for
t\in[0,t_{i}]
for some
t_{i}>0
. Thus,
h_{i}
extends smoothly as the
zero solution on
M_{t_{i}}\setminus\widetilde{U}_{i}
.

Similarly, in the interior region
U_{0}\subset\widetilde{U}_{0}
, let
h_{0}
be the solution to the Cauchy problem

L(h_{0})=\rho_{0}F\ \mbox{ in }\widetilde{U}_{0},\ \ (g_{S},K,\nu_{S},V_{g})^{\prime}_{h}=\rho_{0}(\gamma^{\prime},\kappa^{\prime},\nu^{\prime},V_{S}^{\prime})\ \mbox{ on }S_{0}.

Then again
h_{0}
has compact support in
\widetilde{U}_{0}
for some time interval
[0,t_{0}]
and as above extends to
M_{t_{0}}
. We relabel so that

t_{0}
is a common time interval for all
h_{i}
,
i\geq 0
.

The sum

(5.4)

h=\sum_{i=0}^{N}h_{i},

is thus well-defined on
M_{t_{0}}
and by linearity

(5.5)

D\widetilde{\Phi}_{g}^{H}(h)=\widetilde{\tau}^{\prime}.

(For this global solution, the rescaling above is of course removed). By the local energy estimate in Theorem
4.2
, the solution
h_{i}
of (
5.3
) is
unique, and hence
h
is the unique smooth solution (
5.5
), since if
h^{\prime}
is any other solution of (
5.5
), one may apply the local decomposition
(
5.4
) in the same way. Moreover, the partition of unity property shows that (
4.2
) gives (
5.2
).

We note that the various
H^{s}
norms in (
4.2
) scale in different ways with
\lambda
, but the factor
\lambda^{-1}
is the dominant factor. The smoothness
statement follows from that in Theorem
3.7
.

∎

Next we pass from the modified boundary data
(h_{A},\eta)
to the case of interest, Dirichlet boundary data. The analog of Theorem
5.1

also holds in this setting, with a slight further loss of derivative in the target space. As in Theorem
3.10
, we restrict to vacuum Einstein metrics
g
.

Theorem 5.2
.

Let
(M,g)
be a smooth globally hyperbolic vacuum Einstein metric in harmonic gauge,
M\simeq I\times S
, with compact Cauchy surface
S
having
compact boundary
\partial S=\Sigma
and with timelike boundary
({\mathcal{C}},g_{{\mathcal{C}}})
. Suppose
g
satisfies the convexity Assumption
(*)
at
{\mathcal{C}}
. Then for any
smooth
\tau^{\prime}\in T_{\tau}({\mathcal{T}}^{H})
, the equation

(5.6)

D\Phi_{g}^{H}(h)=\tau^{\prime}

has a unique
C^{\infty}
solution
h
. The constructed solution
h
depends smoothly on the data
\tau^{\prime}
.

Moreover, any
h
satisfying (
5.6
) satisfies the tame energy estimate

(5.7)

\max_{t\in[0,1]}||h||_{\bar{H}^{s}(S_{t})}\leq C\lambda^{-1}||D\Phi_{g}^{H}(h)||_{H^{s+1}({\mathcal{T}}^{H})}.

Proof.

Theorem
5.1
gives the existence and tame energy estimates for the modified linearized boundary data
([h]_{A},\eta)
. We show
how this gives existence and tame energy estimates for the linearized Dirichlet boundary data
h^{\mathsf{T}}
in a manner analogous to the passage from
Theorem
3.7
to Theorem
3.10
.

As in the proof of Theorem
3.10
, given
\tau^{\prime}\in T({\mathcal{T}}^{H})
, let
\widetilde{\tau}^{\prime}
equal
\tau^{\prime}
in all terms, except the boundary data for
\widetilde{\tau}^{\prime}
is
given by
(\gamma^{\prime}_{A},\eta^{\prime})
in place of
\gamma^{\prime}
from
\tau^{\prime}
, where
\gamma^{\prime}=\gamma^{\prime}_{A}+f^{\prime}A
and
\eta^{\prime}
is uniquely constructed from the corner information of
\tau^{\prime}
.

Let
\widetilde{h}
be the unique solution to
D\widetilde{\Phi}_{g}^{H}(\widetilde{h})=\widetilde{\tau}^{\prime}
given by Theorem
5.1
. The estimate (
5.2
) then gives

\max_{t\in[0,1]}||\widetilde{h}||_{\bar{H}^{s}(S_{t})}\leq C\lambda^{-1}||\widetilde{\tau}^{\prime}||_{H^{s}(\widetilde{T}^{H})}\leq C\lambda^{-1}||\tau^{\prime}||_{H^{s}(T^{H})}.

A solution to (
5.6
) can then be constructed by setting
h=\widetilde{h}+\delta^{*}X
, where
X
is given by the solution of (
3.32
). As before, the
Dirichlet boundary data of
X
on
{\mathcal{C}}
is given by
(f^{\prime}-\widetilde{f})\nu
, where
f^{\prime}
is part of the data
\tau^{\prime}
and
\widetilde{f}
is part of the solution
\widetilde{h}
. By
the Dirichlet energy estimate (
2.20
),

\max_{t\in[0,1]}||X||_{\bar{H}^{s+1}(S_{t})}\leq C(||f^{\prime}||_{H^{s+1}({\mathcal{C}})}+||\widetilde{f}||_{H^{s+1}({\mathcal{C}})})\leq C||\tau^{\prime}||_{H^{s+1}(T^{H})}.

For the last inequality, we have applied the observation from the proof in Proposition
4.5
that the Dirichlet data
||\widetilde{f}||_{H^{s+1}({\mathcal{C}})}
is
bounded by
\widetilde{\tau}^{\prime}
(and also by
\tau^{\prime}
). Thus we have

\max_{t\in[0,1]}||h||_{\bar{H}^{s}(S_{t})}\leq C(\max_{t\in[0,1]}||\widetilde{h}||_{\bar{H}^{s}(S_{t})}+\max_{t\in[0,1]}||X||_{\bar{H}^{s+1}(S_{t})})\leq C\lambda^{-1}||\tau^{\prime}||_{H^{s+1}(T^{H})}.

It remains to show uniqueness of solutions to (
5.6
), so that any solution must equal to the solution constructed in Theorem
3.10
and hence
satisfies the energy estimate above. Suppose
\widehat{h}
solves

D\Phi^{H}_{g}(\widehat{h})=0.

Let
Y
be the vector field such that

(5.8)

V^{\prime}_{\delta^{*}Y}=0\ \mbox{ in }U,\ \ Y=\partial_{t}Y=0\mbox{ on }S,\ \ Y^{\intercal}=0,\ \ \eta_{\widehat{h}+\delta^{*}Y}=0\ \mbox{ on }{\mathcal{C}}.

It is easy to check that this system is well-posed and admits a unique smooth solution. Furthermore, the tensor
\widehat{h}+\delta^{*}Y
is now a zero solution
for
D\widetilde{\Phi}_{g}^{H}
, i.e.
D\widetilde{\Phi}_{g}^{H}(\widehat{h}+\delta^{*}Y)=0
. It then follows from the energy estimate (
5.2
) that
\widehat{h}=-\delta^{*}Y
. Since
h
has zero
Dirichlet data, we also have
(\delta^{*}Y)^{\mathsf{T}}=-h^{\mathsf{T}}=0
, which further implies (using Assumption
(*)
) that the normal component
Y^{1}=\langle Y,\nu_{\mathcal{C}}\rangle
of

Y
must vanish. Combining this with (
5.8
), we obtain
Y=0
in
U
and hence
\widehat{h}=0
. This completes the proof of uniqueness.

∎

6.
Nash-Moser theory

The analysis in §5 shows that the linearized IBVP with Dirichlet boundary data is well-posed and has tame energy estimates on-shell, if one allows
for a loss of derivatives for the boundary data. This is the setting of the Nash-Moser implicit function theorem, which we now apply to prove Theorem
1.1
.

Before starting the proof, we need the following result. Let

{\mathcal{T}}^{H}_{0}=\{(0,(\gamma,\kappa),\nu,0,\gamma_{{\mathcal{C}}},0)\}\subset{\mathcal{T}}^{H},

so
Q
and the initial and boundary data of the gauge field in
{\mathcal{T}}^{H}
are set to zero.

Lemma 6.1
.

{\mathcal{T}}^{H}_{0}
is a smooth submanifold of
{\mathcal{T}}^{H}
.

Proof.

The proof is similar to that of Proposition
2.11
. We work with
H^{s}
spaces of finite differentiability but will neglect this in the notation.
Consider then the map

Z:{\mathcal{T}}^{H}\rightarrow Sym^{2}(M)\times\mathbb{V}(S)\times\mathbb{V}({\mathcal{C}}),

(6.1)

\begin{split}&Z(Q,\gamma_{S},\kappa,V_{S},\nu_{S},\gamma_{{\mathcal{C}}},V_{{\mathcal{C}}})=\begin{cases}Q\\
V_{S}\\
V_{{\mathcal{C}}}.\\
\end{cases}\end{split}

As before, it suffices to prove that
Z
is a submersion, i.e. the derivative
DZ
is surjective, on
{\mathcal{T}}_{0}^{H}
. Recall from Proposition
2.11
that

T({\mathcal{T}}^{H})=KerDC,

where
C
is the constraint map in (
2.48
). To see that arbitrary
(Q^{\prime},V^{\prime}_{S},V^{\prime}_{{\mathcal{C}}})
may be chosen within
KerDC
, it suffices then to show that
the usual, non-gauged constraint operator

(6.2)

\begin{split}&C(\gamma_{S},\kappa)=\begin{cases}|\kappa|^{2}-(\mathrm{tr}_{\gamma_{S}}\kappa)^{2}-R_{\gamma_{S}}\\
{\rm div_{\gamma_{S}}}\kappa-d_{S}(\mathrm{tr}_{\gamma_{S}}\kappa)\\
\end{cases}\end{split}

is a submersion. Now if
DC
in (
6.2
) is not surjective, choose a vector field
X
which is
L^{2}(S)
orthogonal to
{\rm Im}\,DC
. Integrating by parts gives then

0=\int_{S}\langle DC(\gamma_{S}^{\prime},\kappa^{\prime}),X\rangle=\int_{S}\langle(\gamma_{S}^{\prime},\kappa^{\prime}),DC^{*}X\rangle+\int_{\Sigma}\beta(\gamma_{S}^{\prime},\kappa^{\prime})(X),

where the boundary term
\beta(\gamma_{S}^{\prime},\kappa^{\prime})
consists of zero and first order differential operators on
X
at
\Sigma
, with coefficents depending on
(\gamma_{S}^{\prime},\kappa^{\prime})
.
Since
(\gamma^{\prime},\kappa^{\prime})
may be chosen arbitrarily, this implies first that
DC^{*}X=0

Concluding Remarks

We conclude this work with several remarks concerning the breakdown of the well-posedness in Theorem
1.1
when Assumption
(*)
is
dropped. First however we discuss some of the difficulties that occur even in the simple case of
2+1
dimensions.

To begin, recall from the discussion in §1 that a vacuum metric
g
is Ricci-flat and so flat in
2+1
dimensions. Assuming the domain
M\simeq I\times S

is also simply connected, the developing map gives an immersion

(7.1)

(M,g)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{1,2}.

It is easy to see that
F
is an embedding, so
M
may be viewed as a domain in Minkowski space
\mathbb{R}^{1,2}
with timelike boundary
{\mathcal{C}}
. The Dirichlet
IBVP in
2+1
dimensions is thus closely related to the problem of existence and uniqueness of isometric embedding of timelike surfaces
({\mathcal{C}},\gamma)
into

\mathbb{R}^{1,2}
.

Remark 7.1
.

There is a major difference in the behavior of the extrinsic curvature between surfaces in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
and timelike surfaces in
\mathbb{R}^{1,2}
. For a surface in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
,
the second fundamental form operator
A
(aka Weingarten map) is self-adjoint with respect to the induced Riemannian metric
\gamma
on the surface, and so

A
may be diagonalized by an orthonormal basis with respect to
\gamma
, giving the principal curvatures. Consequently, for instance
K>0
implies

H=\mathrm{tr}_{\gamma}A>0
.

However, this is not the case for Lorentz metrics;
A
may or may not be diagonalizable with respect to
\gamma
, cf.
[
16
]

for a general discussion. For instance, there are examples of Lorentz metrics on
{\mathcal{C}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{1,2}
with
K_{\gamma}>0
but
H=0

(timelike minimal surfaces).

We note the following:

Lemma 7.2
.

In
2+1
dimensions, the Assumption
(*)
implies that the Gauss curvature
K_{g_{{\mathcal{C}}}}
is positive:

(7.2)

K_{g_{{\mathcal{C}}}}>0.

Proof.

Choose an orthonormal basis
\{e_{a}\}
for
g_{{\mathcal{C}}}
. The matrix of
g_{{\mathcal{C}}}
in this basis is the identity
I
so the matrix
\Pi_{g_{{\mathcal{C}}}}
of
\Pi
in
this basis is
HI-A_{g_{{\mathcal{C}}}}
, where
A_{g_{{\mathcal{C}}}}
the matrix of
A
. Then

det\Pi_{g_{{\mathcal{C}}}}=det(HI-A_{g_{{\mathcal{C}}}})=H^{2}-(\mathrm{tr}A_{g_{{\mathcal{C}}}})H+detA_{g_{{\mathcal{C}}}}=detA_{g_{{\mathcal{C}}}}.

The Assumption
(*)
implies the matrix
\Pi_{g_{{\mathcal{C}}}}
is positive definite, so that
det\Pi_{g_{{\mathcal{C}}}}>0
. The result then follows from Gauss’ Theorema
Egregium.

∎

Note that the same proof using the Gauss equation (Hamiltonian constraint) in place of the Theorema Egregium gives the following. For

Ric-\frac{R}{2}g+\Lambda g=0

with
\Lambda=-1
or
\Lambda=+1
, (anti-deSitter or de Sitter spacetimes), Assumption
(*)
implies

respectively.

However, the converse of Lemma
7.2
, i.e. whether Assumption
(*)
follows from (

of timelike surfaces

in

but where Assumption
(*)
fails, cf. again
[

]
for example.

If

that

implies Assumption
(*)

dimensions, it is not clear whether Assumption

Remark 7.3
.

We include here a remark regarding the orientation discussion in Lemma
2.7
. Let

be an embedded timelike surface in

\bullet

\bullet
Next we discuss the more non-trivial examples presented in

\int_{{\mathcal{C}}_{t}}\langle h(\nu)^{\Sigma},\nabla_{\partial_{t}}X\rangle\leq\delta\int_{{\mathcal{C}}_{t}}|h(\nu)^{\Sigma}|^{2}+\delta^{-1}\int_{{\mathcal{C}}_{t}}|\nabla_{\partial_{t}}X|^{2}.

For the first term here,

\delta\int_{{\mathcal{C}}_{t}}|h(\nu)^{\Sigma}|^{2}\leq\delta\max_{t\in[0,1]}\int_{\Sigma_{t}}|h(\nu)^{\Sigma}|^{2}\leq\delta\max_{t\in[0,1]}E_{t}^{\nu}.

This gives (
8.2
), using the Sobolev trace inequality (
2.4
) for
\Sigma_{t}\subset{\mathcal{C}}
.

The estimate (
8.3
) then follows easily, using again the Sobolev trace inequality, as does (
8.4
) via the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality,
Sobolev trace inequality and (
4.9
).

∎

Lemma 8.2
.

Set
B=A+H\gamma
and note that
|B|_{C^{1}}\leq\lambda\Lambda
.Then

2\int_{{\mathcal{C}}_{t}}\langle\nabla_{\partial_{t}}h(\nu)^{\Sigma},[Bh(\nu)^{\mathsf{T}}]^{\Sigma}\rangle=\frac{d}{dt}\int_{{\mathcal{C}}_{t}}\langle h(\nu)^{\Sigma},[Bh(\nu)]^{\Sigma}\rangle-\int_{{\mathcal{C}}_{t}}\langle h(\nu)^{\Sigma},[(\nabla_{\partial_{t}}B)h(\nu)]^{\Sigma}\rangle,

so that

(8.5)

\max_{t\in[0,1]}|\int_{{\mathcal{C}}_{t}}\langle\nabla_{\partial_{t}}h(\nu)^{\Sigma},[Bh(\nu)^{\mathsf{T}}]^{\Sigma}\rangle|\leq\lambda\Lambda\max_{t\in[0,1]}E_{t}^{\nu}.

Proof.

We have

\int_{{\mathcal{C}}_{t}}\langle\nabla_{\partial_{t}}h(\nu)^{\Sigma},Bh(\nu)^{\Sigma}\rangle=\frac{d}{dt}\int_{{\mathcal{C}}_{t}}\langle h(\nu)^{\Sigma},Bh(\nu)^{\Sigma}\rangle-\int_{{\mathcal{C}}_{t}}\langle h(\nu)^{\Sigma},\nabla_{\partial_{t}}Bh(\nu)^{\Sigma}\rangle=

\frac{d}{dt}\int_{{\mathcal{C}}_{t}}\langle h(\nu)^{\Sigma},Bh(\nu)^{\Sigma}\rangle-\int_{{\mathcal{C}}_{t}}\langle h(\nu)^{\Sigma},B\nabla_{\partial_{t}}h(\nu)^{\Sigma}\rangle-\int_{{\mathcal{C}}_{t}}\langle h(\nu)^{\Sigma},(\nabla_{\partial_{t}}B)h(\nu)^{\Sigma}\rangle=

\frac{d}{dt}\int_{{\mathcal{C}}_{t}}\langle h(\nu)^{\Sigma},B)h(\nu)^{\Sigma}\rangle-\int_{{\mathcal{C}}_{t}}\langle\nabla_{\partial_{t}}h(\nu)^{\Sigma},Bh(\nu)^{\Sigma}\rangle-\int_{{\mathcal{C}}_{t}}\langle h(\nu)^{\Sigma},(\nabla_{\partial_{t}}B)h(\nu)^{\Sigma}\rangle.

This gives the first statement. For the second statement, proceed in the same way as in Lemma
8.1
.

∎
