Pro-V : An Efficient Pro gram Generation Multi-Agent System for Automatic RTL V erification
Yujie Zhao 5 , Zhijing Wu 5 , Hejia Zhang 5 , Zhongming Yu 5 , Wentao Ni 5 , Chia-Tung Ho 2 , Haoxing Ren 2 , Jishen Zhao 5

Abstract
LLM-assisted hardware verification is gaining substantial attention due to its potential to significantly reduce the cost and effort of crafting effective testbenches. It also serves as a critical enabler for LLM-aided end-to-end hardware language design. However, existing current LLMs often struggle with Register Transfer Level (RTL) code generation, resulting in testbenches that exhibit functional errors in Hardware Description Languages (HDL) logic. Motivated by the strong performance of LLMs in Python code generation under inference-time sampling strategies, and their promising capabilities as judge agents, we propose Pro-V a fully program generation multi-agent system for robust RTL verification. Pro-V incorporates an efficient best-of-n iterative sampling strategy to enhance the correctness of generated testbenches. Moreover, it introduces an LLM-as-a-judge aid validation framework featuring an automated prompt generation pipeline. By converting rule-based static analysis from the compiler into natural language through in-context learning, this pipeline enables LLMs to assist the compiler in determining whether verification failures stem from errors in the RTL design or the testbench. Pro-V attains a verification accuracy of 87.17% on golden RTL implementations and 76.28% on RTL mutants. Our code is open-sourced at https://github.com/stable-lab/Pro-V .

II
Background

II-A

Testbench for Hardware Validation

Testbench-based dynamic verification is a widely adopted approach, offering greater flexibility and scalability than formal methods
[
11
]
. The standard testbench consists of three main components:
The Stimulus Generator produces input signals to drive the emulator.
The functional model executes the design’s intended behavior based on these inputs and generates reference outputs.
The Validator compares the RTL output with the reference and generates reports that include
functionality pass rate
or
coverage
. The functionality pass rate is computed by the checkpoints-based or waveform-based match point.

To support these workflows, a variety of mature program-based testbench workflows have been widely adopted for RTL verification
[
5
,
6
,
4
]
, many of which support mainstream Verilog simulators
[
6
,
5
]
. For example, Verilator
[
5
]
is a cycle-accurate, high-performance simulator that translates Verilog or synthesizable SystemVerilog code into optimized C++. This executable faithfully emulates the timing and logic behavior of the original hardware design, enabling extremely efficient simulation for large-scale or long-duration workloads. This architecture allows any reference signal expressible in C++-compatible form to be seamlessly integrated into the simulation workflow. In addition, cocotb
[
6
]
is a widely used Python-based co-simulation library that enables testbench development, interfacing directly with HDL simulators. Both of them support writing testbenches in a high-level language, leveraging the program language’s expressiveness to facilitate the easier construction of complex verification scenarios.

II-B

Language Disparity in LLM Code Generation Capabilities

LLMs such as Claude 3.5
[
12
]
and GPT-4o
[
13
]
exhibit significantly stronger performance on Python code generation compared to Verilog, largely due to the closed-source nature and limited availability of Verilog datasets. As a result, these models have been predominantly trained on Python, leading to an imbalance in their coding proficiency across different languages. The HumanEval benchmark
[
14
]
has emerged as a standard for evaluating Python code generation, while VerilogEval
[
15
]
closely mirrors its task format and complexity in the hardware domain. For example, GPT-4o achieves a 90.2% pass rate on HumanEval but drops to 51.26% on VerilogEval. Similarly, Claude 3.5 attains 100% accuracy on HumanEval yet only 72.4% on VerilogEval.

Limitations 2: Inefficient and ineffective Inference Scaling Loop.

Two primary strategies for inference scaling are commonly distinguished: tree-based methods
[
18
,
19
]
and line-based methods
[
20
]
. Tree-based inference scaling methods explore a diverse set of potential reasoning paths simultaneously, often employing search algorithms like Best-of-N. In contrast, linear scaling approaches typically focus on iteratively refining a single line in a chained manner to progressively enhance the quality of the output. Prior approaches such as CorrectBench
[
9
]
adopt a linear scaling where only
one
new testbench is generated in each iteration, incorporating a self-check mechanism to extend the reasoning chain and improve LLM performance. Specifically, CorrectBench generates one testbench per iteration and employs a “corrector” that prompts the LLM to verify its own output. However, this generation strategy lacks output diversity. Moreover, the corrector in CorrectBench triggers the LLM to generate up to 20 RTL implementations per testbench, resulting in high computational cost.

Limitations 3: Compiler-solely Validation Design.
A critical challenge in automated testbench generation for hardware workflows lies in the potential unreliability of the generated testbenches. Specifically, an incorrect testbench will produce erroneous reference signals that deviate from the intended functionality, thereby misleading the compiler into generating incorrect validation reports. This can result in both false positives and false negatives during the verification process. As a result, compiler-only validation mechanisms lack the robustness and reliability necessary for accurate RTL verification. To mitigate this limitation, agent-based intervention is essential to enhance the trustworthiness of the validation pipeline.

III
Opportunities and Motivations for LLM-aided RTL Verification

Drawing upon the identified three limitations of
the SOTA automated testbench generation workflows discussed in Sec.
II-C
, we distill our insights and identify potential opportunities to develop an effective system for automatic RTL verification.

Opportunity 1: End-to-End Python Generation via Multi-Agent Systems for Automatic RTL Verification.
Given the availability of robust software compilers optimized for hardware verification, as discussed in Section
II-A
, and the significantly stronger performance of LLMs in generating Python code compared to RTL, as outlined in Section
II-B
, it is both feasible and highly advantageous to design an agent system in which the LLM is only required to generate Python code while bypassing direct RTL code generation. This approach leverages LLM strengths and offers substantial potential for improving reliability, efficiency, and scalability in automated hardware verification.

Opportunity 2: Inference-Time Strategy for Scalable Testbench Optimization.

The absence of effective inference-time strategies in existing frameworks, such as CorrectBench
[
9
]
, reveals a key opportunity for enhancing testbench quality through systematic exploration. Leveraging the inherent stochasticity of LLMs via best-of-n sampling enables broader exploration of the solution space, while incorporating a judge score mechanism provides a principled way to filter and select high-quality candidates. This opens a new opportunity for designing scalable and efficient inference-time pipelines that amplify LLM reasoning, significantly improving functional correctness and coverage.

Opportunity 3: LLM-as-a-Judge Aided Validation.

Existing validation approaches predominantly rely on compiler-based checks, which focus heavily on the functional correctness of the Python code alone. However, LLMs inherently struggle with accurately translating RTL problem descriptions into Python, often leading to subtle implementation mismatches. By augmenting the prompt with natural language form descriptions of compiler feedback, and both the Python-based testbench and the corresponding DUT, the LLM can perform more effective in-context learning. This enriched context enables the LLM to act as a more reliable judge, identifying misalignments between the testbench and the DUT and providing corrective guidance to improve validation accuracy.

Pro-V Design

Motivated by the potential opportunities from Sec.

, we develop
Pro-V
, a complete multi-agent program generation system for hardware verification. Notably, our
Pro-V
instantiates the first opportunity outlined in Sec.
III

IV-A

Simplified Python-Based Testbench Generation Pipeline.

We present a simplified pipeline to support an end-to-end Python-based testbench generation system for hardware verification. As described in Sec.
II-A
, the Stimulus Generator produces input signals. The stimulus agent generates Python code with a predefined tail (
Stimuli_Gen.py
), which is executed to produce a list of input dictionaries stored in
Input_signal.json
. Simultaneously, the functional agent generates Python code with a handcrafted tail (
sim_generator.py
) that reads
Input_signal.json
and generates corresponding reference outputs saved in
Reference_signal.json
. Finally, a static code generation module (
sim_main_gen.py
) reads the JSON files and generates
sim_main.cpp
, where each stimulus is compiled into a C++ code block that applies the input, executes the DUT, and compares the output with the reference signal.

IV-B
2
Judge-aided Self-Improvement

Given the demonstrated effectiveness of inference-time scaling approaches, we propose a judge-guided best-of-N searching mechanism to enhance solution quality. This mechanism employs an LLM-as-a-Judge module to evaluate the functional alignment between filtered stimulus candidates and their corresponding specifications, as stored in ‘Reference_signal_candidates.json‘.

The search process operates as follows: For each functional model
M_{i}\in\mathcal{M}
, the Judge agent first performs candidate selection by identifying the most promising Python implementation
M_{i^{*}}
from the available candidates. Subsequently, the judge conducts alignment verification to assess potential mismatches between the selected candidate and its corresponding signal relative to the given functional description.

The termination condition is satisfied when either: (1) the best candidate demonstrates full alignment with the specification, or (2) the maximum iteration limit is reached. Upon meeting the termination condition, the corresponding Program Emulator is returned as the final solution.

In cases where misalignment is detected, the system initiates refinement by forwarding the mismatch information alongside
M_{i^{*}}
to the Refine Agent
\mathcal{A}_{refine}
. This agent subsequently generates a new set of
N
refined functional model candidates, and the evaluation loop continues until either perfect alignment is achieved or the predefined iteration budget is exhausted.

LLM-as-a-judge aided RTL Validator

Fig. 5

One demo of Judge aided Validation mechanism. Some details are omitted to save space.

LLM-as-a-Judge Assisted Validation.

Motivated by the practical reality that engineers often intervene to identify whether a failure originates from the DUT or an incorrectly crafted testbench, we introduce an LLM-as-a-Judge aided validation mechanism, illustrated in Fig.
5
. Unlike traditional validator workflows, which rely solely on compiler-reported results to determine correctness (left side of Fig.
5
), our framework integrates a Judge Agent to prevent misleading failure reports caused by erroneous testbenches.

To support this process, we modify the compiler to automatically generate enhanced diagnostic reports with natural language descriptions, which are significantly more interpretable for LLMs compared to raw compiler stack traces. As illustrated in Fig.
5
, the Judge-aided Validator follows a multi-stage validation workflow:

Stage 1:
The compiler validates the RTL DUT using the generated Python-based testbench as the traditional flow.

Stage 2:
If the validation fails, the enhanced compiler generates a structured report highlighting signal-level mismatches with the corresponding scenario description. The LLM-based Judge Agent then performs root cause analysis to determine whether the failure stems from the DUT or the Python functional model.

Stage 3:
Based on the Judge Agent’s diagnosis, the system either confirms alignment if the Python model is correct or automatically refines the Python functional model if a misalignment is detected. This loop iteratively continues until the refined testbench aligns with the DUT behavior.

Implication and advantages of the proposed template.

1. This mechanism introduces the Judge Agent into the validation process, enabling it to intervene and prevent erroneous testbenches from generating misleading reports that falsely implicate correct RTL designs. As a result, the overall robustness and reliability of the hardware auto-verification system are significantly enhanced.

2. In practical applications, testbenches are typically generated for a specific DUT, making it both feasible and advantageous to leverage DUT information to enhance the verification process. By augmenting the prompt with DUT and compiler feedback, the model can perform in-context learning to capture domain-specific patterns and improve testbench generation. As shown in Fig.
5
, the LLM is able to infer RTL-specific conventions, such as width-based signal assignments—for instance,
reg[4:1] q = 4’b1000
corresponds to
q4 = 1, q3 = 0, q2 = 0, q1 = 0
—which are often misinterpreted under Python’s 0-based indexing.

VI
Conclusion

In this work, we present
Pro-V
, an automated Python-based Program Generation Multi-Agent System that addresses critical limitations in current LLM-based RTL verification approaches. Our system enhances the state-of-the-art workflow by introducing inference-time scaling through dual sampling of functional models and input signals, coupled with a self-improving mechanism via best-of-n selection strategy to boost functional correctness and coverage. A key innovation is our integration of LLM-as-a-judge into the validation process, where rule-based static analysis converts compiler-stack information into detailed natural-language analysis for enhanced prompting. Through comprehensive experimental evaluation,
Pro-V
demonstrates substantial improvements in RTL verification tasks, achieving 87.17% verification accuracy on golden RTL implementations and 76.28% on RTL mutants, representing significant advances over state-of-the-art baselines (78.85% and 55.77%, respectively). With the orthogonal enhancement of our LLM-as-a-judge mechanism,
Pro-V
reaches up to 89.7% accuracy, establishing a new benchmark for automated hardware verification systems and paving the way for more reliable and efficient RTL design validation.
