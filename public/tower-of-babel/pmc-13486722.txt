Multiobjective blood pump impeller optimization with three response surface methods and prototype stator casting for an integrated motor pump
Authors: Reza Sahebi-Kuzehkanan, Hanieh Niroomand-Oscuii, Habib Badri Ghavifekr, Farzan Ghalichi, Zahra Sahebi-Kuzehkanan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCardiovascular diseases are a leading cause of mortality worldwide; rotary blood pumps are a key therapy. Prior optimization studies often treated few geometric variables with single-objective formulations. We address multi-conflicting objectives under a comprehensive parameterization: fifteen blade and flow-path design variables, sampled by a Design of Experiments plan generating 290 Computational fluid dynamics cases validated against the Food and Drug Administration benchmark pump. Kriging, Genetic Aggregation, and Neural Networks were trained as response-surface surrogates, enabling a head-to-head comparison of Response Surface Method techniques. A Multi-Objective Genetic Algorithm simultaneously maximized outlet pressure and minimized mean scalar shear stress. Three Pareto-optimal designs were examined for global and local sensitivities. The Neural-Network surrogate provided the best fidelity for optimization; the most-optimized design achieved a 23% increase in outlet pressure and a 26% reduction in mSS relative to the baseline. To bridge simulation and hardware, an Fe3O4 cast stator was also rapidly prototyped. These results demonstrate a complete pipeline from Design of Experiments based surrogate modeling and multiobjective optimization to prototype realization for integrated blood pump drives.Keywords: Blood pump, Computational fluid dynamics, Optimization, Response surface methodology, Multi-Objective Genetic AlgorithmSubject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

Cardiovascular diseases stand as the primary cause of global mortality, annually claiming nearly 18 million lives1. While heart transplantation has represented the forefront of advanced treatments for such diseases, emerging technologies have ushered in the utilization of ventricular assist devices2. Indeed, centrifugal pumps currently serve as integral components in various medical, biological, and microfluidic applications.

Centrifugal pumps featuring hydrodynamic and magnetic levitation are widely employed in biological applications. Relative to axial pumps, they yield higher head and reduced flow rates. Furthermore, they operate at lower rotational speeds for equivalent outputs and exhibit superior flow estimation of motor power3. Consequently, these pumps are highly suitable for clinical applications. Nevertheless, diminishing pump dimensions while effectively mitigating blood trauma represent significant and formidable challenges.

In centrifugal blood pumps, elevated rotational speeds engender excessive shear stress in areas such as the impeller-housing gap and pump outlet. Red blood cells subjected to momentary high shear stress or persistent low shear stress undergo hemolysis. The ensuing increase in free hemoglobin culminates in blood toxicity and can compromise other organs like the kidneys by impeding excretion4. Thus, assessing hemolysis assumes paramount importance in device design, and myriad studies have probed blood damage to yield diverse predictive models5.

It merits acknowledgment that right and left heart conditions differ, and appropriate pump designs have also been carried out for the right heart6. Although the current study primarily focuses on optimizing the design for left ventricular support, findings suggest that, with reduced rotational speeds, the proposed pump might be applicable to both ventricles. However, further investigations are necessary to validate this adaptability and assess its effectiveness under right ventricular conditions.

Nowadays, computational fluid dynamics (CFD) and advanced design software tools play a pivotal role in designing pumps and optimizing flow dynamics within them. Numerous studies employing these tools have delved into the impact of geometric alterations on the performance of centrifugal pumps. Findings suggest that modifications such as steeper height profile7, backward blade8, minimized blade clearance gap9, diminished blade counts9, reduced impeller’s inlet angle10, increased blade angle (in streight blades)11, curved blade profile12, and elevated blade height13 contribute to mitigating shear stress and minimizing hemolysis. Notably, research indicates that shear stress is lower in 8-blade impellers compared to those with 16 blades14, and 6-blade impellers exhibit lower shear stress than their 5, 7, and 8-blade counterparts11. Although, in another research a decrease in blade height and an increase in clearance have been correlated with reduced shear stress15. Based on these findings, it can be concluded that the optimal number of blades, blade height, and blade clearance may vary depending on the specific geometry and operating conditions of the pump. This highlights the importance of tailoring the design parameters to the unique requirements of each application to achieve the desired balance between performance, efficiency, and hemolysis reduction.

Another critical aspect in assessing the performance of blood pumps is the generated head, serving as a metric for pump efficiency and its capability to surmount resistance. Thus, ensuring the pump’s ability to meet requirements such as flow rate and requisite pressure necessitates maintaining an appropriate head. Previous investigations have demonstrated that augmenting blade height and minimizing volute clearance can increase head15. Additionally, straight blades manifest superior flow rate and head compared to alternative types8,16. Moreover, increasing the blade angle can elevate pressure head and hydraulic efficiency11.

In recent years, there has been a pronounced focus on pump optimization. To this end, a diverse array of variables including short blades number of compound impeller, the relative position of blade radial inlet, blade circumferential bias degree, deflection angle17, impeller outlet width and outer diameter, blade wrap angle18, impeller rotation direction, hub diameter, volumetric flow, pressure on the suction side, required pump head, blade thickness19, impeller inlet and outlet angles20, number of vanes, inlet beta shroud, exit beta shroud, hub inlet blade draft, Rake angle, and the impeller’s rotational speed21 have been scrutinized. While industrial pumps traditionally prioritize objectives such as enhanced pump efficiency, hydraulic or volumetric efficiency21, minimized cavitation19, and improved suction performance20, blood pumps emphasize achieving appropriate head and minimizing hemolysis, with hemolysis mitigation being a paramount objective18.

In the study conducted by Tian et al., it was observed that the workload associated with the orthogonal method is lower compared to that of permutation and combination methods17. Additionally, Non-dominated Sorting Genetic Algorithm-II22, Reverse design19, and Response Surface Methodology20,23,24 have been employed. Multi-objective optimization investigations have been undertaken, and those objectives may potentially conflict with each other, such as achieving high efficiency while minimizing cavitation18–21,25.

Ideally, blood pumps achieve high head at low speed and shear stress, alongside a compact size. One geometric optimization study demonstrated a 14% efficiency increase and doubled hemolysis reduction compared to the HVAD pump at 3–6 L/min; however, at 1–3 L/min, efficiency dropped by 30% and the hemolysis index nearly tripled18. Another study on downsizing implantable pumps reported no post-implantation hemolysis, thrombosis, or thermal tissue damage25.

Gaps in prior research studies.

Previous pump optimization studies have typically focused on a limited number of geometric parameters, restricting the understanding of complex interactions that affect pump performance. Consequently, sensitivity analyses in these works rarely capture the full influence of all relevant variables. Furthermore, prior research has generally considered single or closely aligned objectives, leaving the challenge of conflicting optimization goals largely unaddressed. Methodologically, there is a scarcity of comparative studies assessing multiple Response Surface Methodologies (RSMs); most investigations adopt a single approach without cross-evaluation. Additionally, earlier studies often relied on direct optimization, which becomes computationally prohibitive for high-dimensional problems, whereas RSM utilizes meta-modeling to handle such complexity more efficiently.

This gap is profoundly evident in biological pumps, where balancing hydraulic efficiency with biological factors (e.g., hemolysis) is crucial. Due to computational costs, existing literature largely restricts this design space. To the best of our knowledge, comprehensive studies simultaneously optimizing a high-dimensional design space (comprising 15 geometric parameters) remain scarce, particularly those incorporating critical yet often overlooked variables like position-dependent blade thickness for medical applications.

Innovations and contributions of this study.

The present work makes a meaningful contribution to the literature by addressing key limitations in the multi-objective optimization of medical devices. A central novelty is the simultaneous analysis and optimization of a high-dimensional design space comprising 15 geometric parameters. This comprehensive approach clarifies how intricate geometric changes affect outlet pressure and mean scalar shear stress, illuminating coupled effects among parameters and providing practical guidance for future designs.

Furthermore, unlike prior studies relying on single response models, this research integrates and comparatively evaluates three advanced RSM approaches—Kriging, Genetic Aggregation, and Neural Networks—newly applied to multi-objective pump design. This comparative evaluation identifies the most suitable surrogate for highly complex problems and deepens the understanding of modeling techniques in biomedical pump optimization.

Another innovation is employing a multi-objective genetic algorithm (MOGA) to optimize two conflicting goals: maximizing outlet pressure while minimizing mean scalar shear stress. Combined with the evaluated RSMs, this approach efficiently navigates a strongly coupled parameter space that would be computationally prohibitive for direct optimization.

Overall, the results advance both impeller optimization and the broader design of biomedical devices. Additionally, building on our previous report26, which assembled an integrated pump-motor prototype, this study designed and fabricated a nonstandard stator via polyurethane-ferrite casting to expedite motor fabrication.

Section “Result and discussion” presents the results from the response surface method, multi-objective optimization, and computational fluid dynamics simulations. Finally, section “Conclusion” is dedicated to the conclusion.

Materials and methods

Optimization process

In the first step, an impeller was designed at the operating point. Then, 15 design variables were selected as input parameters, while two were chosen as output parameters (pressure and mean scalar shear stress) to serve as optimization targets.

Next, the initial model was simulated, followed by the simulation of 290 experiments to create a dataset for the response surface method (RSM) design. These experiments were conducted using the Central Composite Design (CCD) method.

Then, kriging, genetic aggregation, and neural networks were used to obtain response surface functions. In the next step, each response surface function should be optimized multi-objectively using the genetic algorithm. At the end, optimization results of each method will be compared with the initial model and the best-optimized model is to be selected. Figure 1 shows the flow chart of optimization process.

Establish dataset

In building surrogate models for costly functions or experimental datasets, the pre-experimental design phase is essential in selecting the initial sampling points. As a result, a major focus of research is on achieving optimal space coverage while minimizing the number of samples. Traditional sampling methods for computational experiments include Monte Carlo Sampling (MCS), Latin Hypercube Sampling (LHS), and Central Composite Design (CCD). Among these methods, CCD is particularly popular27, since it provides a central point with sets of axial and factorial points arranged along the axes of the selected input parameters and across the diagonals of the parameter space.

In this work, a total of 290 distinct configurations of impeller parameters were produced through the CCD approach, within the predetermined range of impeller parameter variations. These sets were then modeled and simulated, creating a detailed database containing impeller parameters, pressure values generated, and the mean scalar shear stress.

For the current rotary pump simulation, the frozen-rotor technique was employed to conduct steady-state simulations. This approach allows for capturing the flow characteristics in the rotating domain by approximating the transient behavior as a steady-state solution, effectively reproducing a reliable time-averaged flow field with far lower computational cost than a full transient analysis. Our response surface optimization required 290 CFD evaluations; performing the same number of transient runs would be computationally prohibitive. Numerous validated studies on rotary pumps and ventricular assist devices have widely adopted this methodology, demonstrating that the steady-state approach offers sufficient accuracy for design optimization. While transient CFD is capable of resolving instantaneous instabilities, pressure spikes, and peak shear, it remains impractical for large-scale parametric studies9,10,28. Therefore, the steady-state model provides the best balance between fidelity and computational feasibility for the present work.

At high shear rates, blood behaves as a Newtonian fluid, especially for the shear rates found in heart pumps. Blood was thus modeled here as a Newtonian fluid with a viscosity of 0.0035 Pa·s and a density of 1050 kg/m329,30.In this study, blood was modeled as a Newtonian fluid, which is a standard and widely accepted simplification in the literature for blood pump optimization. While non-Newtonian effects (increased viscosity at low shear rates) are significant for predicting thrombosis and flow stasis, they have a negligible impact on hemolysis, which occurs primarily in high-shear regions where blood behaves Newtonians. Given the primary focus on hemolysis and the computational requirement of 290 CFD simulations, the Newtonian assumption remains a reliable approach for comparing and ranking different design candidates.

Turbulence modelling

According to our group’s previous studies and based on the directly calculated Reynolds number under the operating conditions of the present pump, the Reynolds number exceeds , confirming that the internal flow is fully turbulent, so appropriate turbulence models should be employed for the simulation. The nature of the flow, available simulation time, and the type of problem to be solved determine the choice of the turbulence model. The type of problem dictates which model would be best for an application, as no single model is always superior to another28,31. Among the most popular two-equation turbulence models is the k-ω model extension called Menter’s SST k-ω model. Several tested research works have proved its efficiency and reliability. In this analysis, turbulent flow simulation was done by using the k-ω SST model32,33.

Calculation of hemolysis index

Red blood cells (RBCs) experience different levels of shear stress as they travel through the Left Ventricular Assist Device (LVAD). In this study, the empirical power-law model proposed by Giersiepen et al.9 was utilized to estimate flow-induced hemolysis. This specific formulation was selected over other alternatives primarily due to its widespread adoption in computational studies of rotary blood pumps and cardiovascular devices, which facilitates direct benchmarking against existing literature29. Furthermore, while different hemolysis models may predict varying absolute values of blood damage, the relative comparison between different geometrical designs is well-preserved29. Given that the primary objective of the present work is shape optimization, capturing these relative improvements accurately is of paramount importance, making the Giersiepen model highly suitable for comparing design iterations. This model establishes a power-law relationship between the magnitude of shear stress and the exposure time of RBCs to that stress.

In above equation ∆t is exposure time and HI is (%) of the blood-hemolysis index (which represents the percentage of free hemoglobin to all hemoglobin in blood) and τ is scalar shear stress, which include viscous and Reynolds stress tensor.

In this simulation, the Eulerian approach is used to measure hemolysis.In the Eulerian approach, rather than explicitly integrating exposure time along individual fluid pathlines, the time accumulation of damage is handled implicitly by solving a steady convective scalar transport equation. Specifically, we employed a well-established linearization technique34 where a linearized damage index is advected across the computational domain using a shear-dependent source term, allowing the final Hemolysis Index to be recovered without direct time tracking.

Numerical solution

Numerical simulations have become very helpful in studying complex engineering problems due to the time-consuming and costliness of practical tests. Ansys CFX 21 was used for simulations in this study. Its powerful ability in pump flow simulation makes it one of the popular choices for Ventricular Assist Device (VAD) design and analysis15,35–38.

Generation of the grid

The fluid domain was meshed with a hybrid approach: a structured grid in the impeller to resolve the blades, and an unstructured linear tetrahedral mesh in the volute to capture the casing’s complex flow. A total of 2.71million elements were selected based on mesh independence calculations, with a percent error of in contrast to a more refined mesh for the pressure at the impeller outlet. Figure 3 presents the mesh independence verification. To ensure numerical accuracy and stability, strict structural mesh quality was maintained across the domain. The computational grid achieved an average Orthogonal Quality of 0.77 and an average Skewness of 0.23. Crucially, statistical evaluation showed that 98% of the mesh elements maintained an Orthogonal Quality greater than 0.45 and a Skewness lower than 0.54, guaranteeing highly reliable spatial discretization. Additionally, the near-wall mesh resolution was finely controlled to ensure across all solid boundaries, which is essential for accurately resolving the viscous sublayer.

Boundary conditions

In this study, boundary conditions were pressure and volume flow rates at the inlet and outlet, respectively. The outlet flow rate was prescribed as 5 L/min, while the inlet pressure was specified as 40 mmHg, which corresponds to the average intraventricular pressure in the left ventricle. The Multi-Reference Frame (MRF) approach was adopted for the simulation31, assuming all walls as a no-slip boundary condition. The simulation was done at a design point of 2500 rpm with a flow rate of 5 L/min.

Near Wall treatment

Wall effects are generally treated by wall functions, where empirical equations are used to provide the proper boundary conditions near the walls without resolving the boundary layer completely. This, in turn, enables one to model steep shear gradients near the walls with coarser mesh. In CFX, the wall function approach is compatible with all turbulence models.

In this context, an automatic wall boundary treatment adopted in CFX provides a smooth shift from the low-Reynolds-number approach to the grid-density-based method that achieves a balance between computational cost and accuracy. The presented approach was thus used here to treat the wall effect by reducing computational demands, keeping the results accurate in this respect39,40.

Convergence criterion

Convergence was enforced at a residual threshold of 1 × 10⁻⁴ while monitoring the outlet-averaged pressure. A pilot run showed stabilization within ~ 300 iterations with fluctuations < 1 mmHg; therefore, 300 iterations were adopted for all subsequent cases. The pressure level in this pilot does not represent the pump’s operating pressure, it was chosen solely to facilitate numerical convergence and validation. Subsequent simulations were executed at the specified rotational speeds under clinically relevant conditions to achieve VAD-appropriate outlet pressures within the physiological arterial range (~ 85–130 mmHg). Validation

The FDA benchmark pump was developed to provide a standardized, rigorous framework for validating computational fluid dynamics (CFD) methods applied to medical devices15. It is important to emphasize that in our previous studies26,41,42, we directly simulated and experimentally evaluated the FDA benchmark pump itself. Those prior works explicitly demonstrated that the exact computational framework employed in the current study, comprising the identical mesh topology principles, turbulence modeling, and solver settings, yielded results in excellent quantitative agreement with the experimental data of the benchmark pump. Specifically, our numerical framework demonstrated strong agreement with the FDA’s experimental velocity data, with relative deviations for the average velocity profile falling within the range of 8% to 16%, and a deviation of 17% observed for the peak velocity.

While the specific geometry of the newly proposed pump differs from the FDA model, the underlying fluid kinematics and complex flow features, such as rotating reference frame effects, boundary layer development under high shear stress, and centrifugal pressure rise, are fundamentally similar. By successfully validating our specific solver settings on the FDA pump in our own prior research, we have established a robust and proven foundation. This provides high confidence in the framework’s capability to accurately capture the flow physics of the proposed geometry under the targeted physiological operating conditions.

Response surface methodology

Response surfaces are functions of varying nature that describe the relationship between input and output parameters, allowing the estimation of output values without the execution of a complete solution. A number of algorithms are available to generate response surfaces. These include genetic aggregation, kriging, and neural networks. The methods are described in the following subsections.

Genetic aggregation

Genetic aggregation

This technique utilizes an iterative genetic algorithm to find the optimal response surface along with its relevant configurations. During the response generation phase, the top-performing surfaces are selected and combined. This “aggregation” occurs at different response levels, leading to an improvement in the final response surface’s accuracy and quality27,43.

Genetic aggregation employs a genetic algorithm to select the optimal response surface. This algorithm generates populations comprising various response surfaces that are evaluated in parallel27. To maintain diversity among response surfaces, the genetic algorithm eliminates a portion of any overrepresented response surface types within the population. When predicted values differ, the population incorporates metamodels with similar prediction accuracy, thereby increasing the likelihood of error elimination43.

Kriging

Kriging

Kriging is a meta-modeling algorithm and a non-parametric interpolation model that enhances response surface quality. This approach interpolates the Design of Experiments (DoE) points, and due to its non-parametric nature, the training points are utilized both to estimate unknown parameters and to predict the response at new locations. This multidimensional interpolation technique was initially developed for computer experiments with deterministic error characteristics44. Its functionality is based on the combination of polynomial modeling and the determination of local standard deviation27,45–48.

One method to increase accuracy is to define an active learning function to add new points. Kriging is powerful because it can estimate the interpolation error. This predictor is built using an initial set of simulation data points. Then, based on the estimated error, it determines where new simulation data is needed to reduce the error in subsequent iterations49.

Neural network

Neural network

Neural networks are methods inspired by the structure of the human brain. To approximate a function, a network is created with three layers: input, hidden, and output, where the layers are interconnected through weighted links27. Each processing element in an Artificial Neural Network (ANN) is called a neuron50, and the mapping relationship between layers is referred to as the transfer function51. Between the hidden and the input layer, an activation function is used, while between the output and the hidden layer, a linear function is used52.

This approach uses a few design points for estimation, and its performance improves as the number of available points increases, as does the number of intermediate cells27. The training procedure of an ANN is a search for the optimal connection weights that minimize the error function between the network’s output and the target values in the training dataset50.

The key benefit of this approach lies in its ability to process information with characteristics such as non-linearity, high parallel processing, and the capacity to learn and adapt50, which are used to obtain behavior similar to the binary electrical signals of the brain. This function is continuous and differentiable27.

In one comparative study, the quality of responses obtained from kriging, neural network, and genetic aggregation methods were investigated. Standard deviations of the responses were compared with calculated values at the design points. Kriging and GA results were found to be acceptable27. The choice between these methods often depends on the specific problem, the amount and nature of available data, computational resources, and the desired balance between accuracy, interpretability, and computational efficiency.

Kriging often works well when dealing with smooth, low-dimensional, and continuous problems. This is due to its ability to estimate the internal error and make refinement points27,45. It is appropriate in cases of nonlinear response-a limitation of genetic aggregation. It allows a flexible choice for various options regarding the correlation function44. On the other side, it is computationally costly with large datasets and problems in high dimensions44.

Genetic aggregation enforces the aggregation of several metamodels, possibly considering strengths from different approaches and can be more robust and accurate in some cases compared to a single metamodel. Still, it might be computationally expensive and complex to implement.

The Neural Network is very flexible, models complex nonlinear relationships, and works well for high-dimensional problems27. For Neural Networks, large quantities of data are necessary to achieve the best performance. However, they can have noisy responses where there are a large number of input parameters27. Again, limited control over the algorithm and the fact that its nature might make interpretation from it very difficult should be noted. In general, its performance depends on its component models, and this method can also be computationally expensive.

Multi-objective optimization

Multi-objective optimization involves the simultaneous optimization of numerous goals by considering non-dominated solutions, where improving a certain objective results in at least one other objective being compromised. Many optimization problems have objectives that conflict with each other, therefore, making it impossible to find one solution that optimizes all the objectives simultaneously52. This leads to a set of optimal solutions known as the Pareto front, which is comprised of non-dominated solutions. Solution (a) is better than solution (b) in case (a) has improved at least one of the objectives without deterioration regarding the remaining objectives in respect to (b)50.

Objective Function Selection: Available power-law models use sub-linear power (0.66 < α < 0.785) for hemolysis prediction and super-linear power (1.991 < β < 2.416) for scalar shear stress. As a result, the impact of scalar shear stress on the hemolysis index (HI) is more significant than that of residence time. Any factor that reduces scalar shear stress is likely to lower the HI, provided it does not increase residence time by more than τ(−β/α) times. τ is the ratio of scalar shear stress in the new design to that in the basic design50.

Compared to single-objective optimization, multi-objective optimization avoids challenges associated with weight factor selection and provides more realistic results53. Given the above considerations, it can be concluded that scalar shear stress can be a significant factor in determining the likelihood of hemolysis. Therefore, in this study, the mean scalar shear stress is considered as one of the optimization objectives, while the outlet pressure is selected as the other objective in the optimization process.

To ascertain whether this deterministic fit in Kriging and Genetic Aggregation inherently translates to robust predictive capability or merely manifests severe mathematical overfitting, it is scientifically imperative to evaluate the models against out-of-sample data. Initially, addressing the dataset dimensionality: according to established literature55, a Central Composite Design (CCD) accommodating 15 parameters mandates a minimum of 287 experimental runs. Given that 290 discrete experiments were executed in this study, the premise of data scarcity is definitively invalidated. Consequently, the exact nodal coincidence observed in Kriging and Genetic Aggregation during the training phase strongly indicates “memorization” of the training set (exact interpolation) rather than the abstraction of underlying phenomenological trends.

To explicitly quantify the models’ generalization limits and provide clear diagnostic values, a 10-fold cross-validation protocol was executed. In this rigorous resampling procedure, the dataset is randomly partitioned into 10 mutually exclusive subsets (folds). The predictive model is iteratively trained on 9 folds (90%) and subsequently validated against the remaining independent hold-out set (10%). Due to software constraints in directly plotting out-of-sample scatter outputs, the exact mathematical limits of dispersion are encapsulated via the cross-validation RMSEand values, effectively capturing the identical behavioral trends.

The Cross-Validation columns delineated in Table 3 unequivocally resolve the initial paradox and expose the underlying structural fragility of the interpolative models. When subjected to out-of-sample test cohorts, both Kriging and Genetic Aggregation experience a drastic degradation in predictive performance. For instance, in forecasting Outlet pressure, the Kriging model’s precipitously drops from 1.000 to 0.712, accompanied by a substantial surge in RMSEfrom near-zero to 2753.50. This pronounced statistical deterioration, frequently termed variance collapse, serves as irrefutable empirical evidence of overfitting.

Conversely, the Neural Network architecture exhibits exceptional structural stability. For the “Mean SSS” parameter, its training (0.832) and CV (0.801) demonstrate a high degree of concordance, with the RMSEvalues remaining highly consistent (7.51 in training vs. 8.17 in CV). A comparative evaluation of the training and cross-validation outcomes clearly indicates that the Neural Network successfully circumvents over-parameterization; it delineates highly non-linear, resilient decision boundaries that generalize remarkably well to unobserved domains.

Therefore, based on these comprehensive diagnostic analytics, it is concluded that while Kriging and Genetic Aggregation function as optimal exact interpolators for predefined nodal coordinates, they are intrinsically susceptible to severe overfitting. Due to its empirically validated capacity for robust generalization, the Neural Network emerges as the most reliable response surface methodology for this investigation.

Local sensitivity

Local Sensitivity is an analysis technique where the impact of changes in a specific parameter on the system output is evaluated while other parameters are kept constant. In this analysis, one parameter is varied at a specific design point, and its effect on the output is observed. This is under the assumption that the system is at a steady-state condition, in which small changes in one parameter can have specific effects on the output of the system, such as outlet pressure or mean scalar shear stress. This method is highly useful for identifying critical parameters, as some parameters may have a major impact on the output while others have negligible effects.

Based on this approach, Figs. 5 and 6 illustrate the local sensitivity of output parameters to input parameters. As a general trend across all models, parameters such as suction diameter, outlet width, and a few others positively affect the increase in pressure. The effects of the remaining parameters vary depending on the method, being positive, negative, or close to zero.

Specifically, the curves associated with Outlet Width and Outlet Angle, particularly in the Kriging model (orange), have steep slopes. This indicates that these are the most critical parameters affecting outlet pressure, implying that even small changes in these variables will have a substantial effect on the output. Furthermore, Outlet Width significantly impacts mean scalar shear stress, whereas the other parameters do not show a significant effect on it. Suction Diameter and Axial Extension appear to be important but with lesser magnitude than Outlet Width and Outlet Angle. Conversely, parameters related to thickness (Thickness leading edge at hub and shroud) and blade angles have a minimal impact, as their sensitivity curves do not exhibit significant changes.

When comparing the different modeling methods, one can observe that the Kriging and Genetic Aggregation models are more sensitive at the examination point than the Neural Network model. For these former models, even minor changes in input data can lead to radical changes in the output, indicating a higher inherent sensitivity to input variations.

However, local sensitivity alone cannot study parameter interactions, since it alters only one parameter at a time while keeping the others fixed. While it is well-suited for initial single-parameter effect estimates, a global sensitivity analysis, where all parameters are altered simultaneously, would be required to study complex interactions or non-linear dependencies among the parameters.

Suction diameter

Suction diameter

The suction diameter is a highly influential parameter. Its sensitivity is positive for the Kriging and Neural Network (NN) models but near-zero for the Genetic Aggregation (GA) model. This variation highlights how different mathematical architectures interpret the highly non-linear design space differently. Physically, the suction diameter’s effect is governed by the inlet velocity triangle rather than simple cross-sectional area relations. Changing this diameter simultaneously alters the inlet meridional () and peripheral () velocities. The positive sensitivity (Kriging/NN) indicates an optimized relative flow angle () that reduces shock losses and increases outlet pressure. Conversely, the near-zero sensitivity (GA) reflects an aerodynamic balance point where the variations in and offset each other, causing negligible changes in hydraulic performance.

For all, the effect is negative and significant on the mean scalar shear stress. Increased suction diameter reduces the average fluid velocity and turbulent kinetic energy that can reduce mean scalar shear stress. Increased suction diameter permits smoother fluid passage along the impeller entry walls and thus reduces mean scalar shear stress.

Outlet width

Outlet width

In all the models, increasing the width of the outlet raises the pressure of the outlet. According to Bernoulli’s equation, an increased width of the outlet decreases the velocity of the outlet, and if this decrease is well converted into static pressure, the pressure at the outlet becomes higher. Besides, increasing the width of the outlet gives a chance for the orderly escape of fluid, minimizing losses of energy and keeping outlet pressure high.

For the all, the mean scalar shear stress sensitivity is negative and high. Increased outlet width decreases the outlet velocity and velocity gradients near the walls. The more organized and smoother the fluid exit, the smaller the turbulent fluctuations can be, which in turn reduces mean scalar shear stress.

Inlet angle

Inlet angle

The sensitivity of the inlet angle parameter regarding outlet pressure is positive for Genetic Aggregation, although the magnitude is insignificant. The sensitivity of the same variable is negative for the Neural Network. Centrifugal pumps typically have a inlet angle; as this angle nears zero, the flow enters more in parallel, which has been observed to result in better outlet pressure.

For mean scalar shear stress (SSS), the sensitivity is zero for Genetic Aggregation and the Neural Network, but positive for Kriging.

Outlet angle

Outlet angle

This parameter’s sensitivity regarding outlet pressure is negative for Genetic Aggregation and the Neural Network, with Genetic Aggregation exhibiting a higher magnitude. However, it is positive for Kriging. Increasing the outlet angle can misalign the flow direction, potentially increasing losses; thus, it cannot be conclusively stated that increasing the outlet angle will universally decrease or increase pressure.

Regarding mean SSS, the sensitivity is negative for Kriging and Genetic Aggregation, but positive for the Neural Network.

Axial extension

Axial extension

This parameter exhibits positive sensitivity for Kriging and the Neural Network concerning outlet pressure, while Genetic Aggregation shows zero sensitivity. Across all models, the overall sensitivity magnitude, whether positive or negative, remains relatively low.

For mean SSS, the sensitivity is negative across all models, with high sensitivity observed in Kriging and Genetic Aggregation. This suggests that increasing this parameter effectively reduces the mean SSS.

Blade thickness at the hub and shroud at the leading edge and trailing edge

Blade thickness at the hub and shroud at the leading edge and trailing edge

For these parameters, the thickness at the leading edge (LE) of both the hub and the shroud exerts only a minor effect on the outlet pressure. Conversely, the thickness at the trailing edge (TE) is highly influential. Across every model, increasing the shroud TE thickness significantly boosts the outlet pressure. The hub TE thickness exhibits the same positive trend across all models, although its numerical impact is smaller than that of the shroud.

The sensitivity of mean SSS is negative for all models, indicating that increasing blade thickness reduces mean SSS. This reduction is attributed to the larger impact area of thicker blades, which eliminates sharp points where the fluid initially interacts with the blade. Additionally, increased thickness promotes a uniform flow distribution across the blade passage, thereby reducing mean SSS.

Consequently, these findings explicitly highlight the critical role of blade thickness in managing the trade-off between hydraulic performance and hemocompatibility; specifically, strategic thickness variations (such as at the trailing edge) can synergistically enhance the outlet pressure while simultaneously mitigating blood damage.

Blade angle leading edge

Blade angle leading edge

In both Genetic Aggregation and the Neural Network, the sensitivity of the LE blade angle concerning outlet pressure is negative; increasing the blade inlet angle reduces the outlet pressure. This pressure drop can be attributed to the fluid entering the blade with greater deviation (incidence) as the inlet angle increases. This makes it more difficult for the fluid to follow the blade geometry seamlessly, ultimately reducing the outlet pressure.

Broader implications and discussion:

The results obtained in this research contribute significantly to the existing literature by proving the practical feasibility of Neural Networks over other RSM methods such as Genetic Aggregation and Kriging. Genetic Aggregation and Kriging had presented steady statistical performance, while Neural Network with its flexibility and higher precision for data verification turned out more useful in real life. These will not only help in moving the research forward in pump optimization but also set a kind of benchmark for future research studies involving engineering applications of multi-objective optimization.

The results obtained in this study have wide applicability in the real world, particularly in the design of blood pumps and other medical devices. An optimized design of the impeller with the neural network yielded an increase of 23% in outlet pressure and a reduction of 26% in mean scalar shear stress. In addition, the optimized configuration resulted in approximately an 11% rise in static pressure, contributing to a more favorable overall pressure field. These improvements are especially critical in blood pumps, where minimizing shear stress is crucial to reduce hemolysis and ensure safe operation. Moreover, smoother pressure distribution and reduced energy losses will result in longer blade life, enhanced pump stability, and better energy efficiency, which are very important for battery-operated devices used in medical applications.

Sensitivity analysis indicated that the shroud-side outlet angle and hub-side inlet angle have the greatest influence on outlet pressure, while the inlet angle and outlet width are the dominant factors affecting mean scalar shear stress; blade thickness at the hub and shroud at trailing edge was also more influential than at the leading edge, and increasing it at both the leading edge and trailing edge reduced shear stress.

The broader impacts involve how this research will be taken forward into future engineering practices: The multi-objective optimization framework established in this study guides the development of pumps not limited to medical applications, but also in aerospace and energy sectors. Moreover, the capability of considering conflicting objectives simultaneously supplies the real tool for practical challenge involvement in complex engineering systems.

Further on, the methodologies and the outcome of this study provide great research in the field of problems over highly nonlinear multidimensional optimization. In conclusion, embedding advanced RSM methodologies along with sensitivity analysis using various wide-ranging parameters offers substantial backbone information for future researchers on more such studies. In addition, the realism of operating conditions factor to accomplish the optimization process for certain factors was given the importance it deserved by this study. Finally, the paper has contributed to developing new aspects within pump optimization and a versatile framework for its application to optimizing a broad range of complex systems at all levels of industries.

Study limitations and future directions

The ferrite filled polyurethane stator enabled rapid tooling, complex shape realization, and low speed system integration. However, the material exhibited porosity and limited mechanical strength relative to laminated steels, and more importantly its low saturation flux density (typical of ferrites) led to early magnetic saturation, preventing attainment of the required torque at the desired speed. This outcome is consistent with the intrinsic limitation in saturation flux density of ferrite based, polymer bonded composites.

The ferrite-filled polyurethane stator enabled rapid tooling, complex shape realization, and low-speed system integration. However, the material exhibited porosity and limited mechanical strength relative to laminated steels. More importantly, while traditional laminated silicon steels typically offer a high saturation flux density in the range of 1.5-2 T64, the ferrite-based polymer composite exhibits a significantly lower saturation flux density (typically below 0.5T65. This intrinsic limitation led to early magnetic saturation, reducing the overall magnetic loading and preventing the attainment of the required torque at the desired high speeds. This outcome is consistent with the well-known magnetic limitations of ferrite-based, polymer-bonded composites.

While the primary focus of this study is the hydrodynamic optimization of the pump, the thermal management of the integrated motor-pump remains a critical consideration for hemocompatibility. Heat dissipation from the stator into the blood flow is a known risk factor for thermally induced thrombosis and protein denaturation. The proposed cast stator presents unique thermal characteristics in this regard. Its inherently lower thermal conductivity compared to traditional laminated steel may act as a thermal insulator, potentially protecting the blood from rapid heating. However, this simultaneously restricts heat dissipation from the internal coils to the surroundings. Future multidisciplinary studies incorporating coupled thermo-fluid (conjugate heat transfer) analyses are essential to evaluate the localized temperature distribution and optimize the thermal management of the cast stator design.

Conclusion

In this study, a data-driven multi-objective optimization framework was proposed to improve the hydraulic and hemocompatibility performance of a centrifugal blood pump impeller. Although Kriging and Genetic Aggregation performed slightly better during the initial fitting stage, subsequent validation revealed overfitting and poor generalization to unseen designs. In contrast, the Neural Network provided the most reliable surrogate behavior, with the smallest prediction errors for candidate geometries. Sensitivity analysis showed that the shroud-side outlet angle and hub-side inlet angle dominate outlet pressure, while the inlet angle, outlet width, and blade thickness near the trailing edge govern mean scalar shear stress. Compared with the initial design, the Neural Network-optimized impeller achieved about 23% higher outlet pressure, 26% lower mean scalar shear stress, and an approximately 11% increase in static pressure, leading to a more uniform pressure field and improved hydraulic stability.
