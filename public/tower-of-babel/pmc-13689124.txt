Background

Despite global efforts to reduce the burden of HIV/AIDS, sub‑Saharan Africa continues to bear the highest proportion of new infections and HIV‑related morbidity worldwide, making HIV testing a central component of prevention and treatment strategies [1]. Timely HIV testing is essential for identifying individuals living with HIV, facilitating access to antiretroviral therapy and ensuring early initiation of treatment, and advancing progress toward the UNAIDS 95‑95‑95 targets [2]. Nevertheless, HIV testing uptake remains suboptimal in many settings, including Ghana [2]. National survey data indicate that although Ghana has made progress in expanding access to HIV testing services, important gaps persist. About half of women of reproductive age have ever tested for HIV (approximately 54% in 2022), with lower levels observed in rural areas (39%) and among men (25%), signaling ongoing challenges in achieving universal knowledge of HIV status [3–5].

Gender disparities in HIV testing uptake are well documented in Ghana and beyond. Studies show that women usually report higher HIV testing uptake than men, a pattern attributed to routine testing during antenatal care and greater contact with health services whereas men’s lower engagement reflects sociocultural barriers and limited health service access [3, 4]. Wealth, educational attainment and place of residence are important determinant of HIV testing uptake [3, 6, 7]. Evidence shows that individuals with higher socioeconomic advantages are more likely to undergo HIV testing, whereas those with lower advantages are less likely to be tested [3, 5–7]. In sub‑Saharan Africa, these disparities extend beyond individual-level factors, as structural determinants—such as access to health information, availability of health infrastructure, and rural-urban inequalities—also contribute to unequal HIV testing [5].

Understanding how gender and socioeconomic factors influence HIV testing is essential for informing targeted public health interventions in Ghana. While traditional regression methods have identified key determinants of HIV testing, they may have limitations when analysing sparse subgroups and the complex hierarchical structures national survey data [8–11]. A Bayesian analytical framework offers many advantages by incorporating uncertainty, prior information, and multilevel effects, producing more stable and interpretable estimates of inequalities in HIV testing uptake while providing nuanced insights into which populations are most underserved. This study aims to examine how gender and socioeconomic inequalities in HIV testing vary across communities in Ghana, offering evidence to guide interventions and policies that promote equitable HIV testing coverage.

Method

Study area and data source

This study was conducted in Ghana, located in Western Africa. Ghana shares boundaries in the north with Burkina Faso, in the east with Togo, in the west with Côte d’Ivoire, and in the south with the Gulf of Guinea. The data was extracted from the 2022 Ghana Demographic and Health Survey data (GDHS), particularly from Men and Women files. The 2022 GDHS is a nationally representative household survey that collects detailed information on sociodemographic characteristics, HIV, and other important health indicators using standard questionnaires spanning between October 2022 and February 2023, targeting women aged 15–49 years and men 15–59 years [12, 13].

Study design

The 2022 GDHS is a cross-sectional study, which uses the 2021 Population and Housing Census updated frame prepared by the Ghana Statistical Service (GSS). The survey used a stratified two-stage cluster sampling design to produce nationally representative estimates for urban and rural areas across the 16 regions [12, 13].

Sampling strategy and study population

The GDHS employs a stratified two-stage cluster sampling design based on the most recent national population and housing census [12, 13].

In the first stage, enumeration areas (EAs), also referred to as clusters, were selected using probability proportional to size (PPS) sampling from the national sampling frame. The country was stratified by region and place of residence (urban/rural) to ensure representativeness.

In the second stage, a fixed number of households were systematically selected from each cluster following a household listing exercise. For this study, men and women aged 15–59 years with complete data on HIV testing status (v781) and all covariate were included (Fig. 1). The final analytical sample comprised 22,058 respondents.

Study variables

Outcome variable

The outcome variable was HIV test uptake, defined as whether an individual (man or woman) had ever been tested for HIV preceding the survey. Consistent with the DHS definition, HIV testing uptake was coded as a binary variable, with respondents who reported ever having been tested for HIV coded as 1and those who had never been tested coded as 0. The prevalence of HIV testing uptake was defined as the proportion of respondents who reported ever having been tested for HIV [14].

Explanatory variables

Based on previous studies selected explanatory variables were considered [3, 5]. These variables include; age-group, gender, educational level, employment status, occupation, marital status, wealth index, region, and residence. Meanwhile, variables such as the place where respondent last HIV test was taken and year of most recent HIV test were used only to estimate the prevalence of HIV testing uptake.

Data analysis

Descriptive analysis

The analysis was conducted in two stages using Stata 16.1. In the first stage, the prevalence of HIV testing uptake was estimated using sampling weights following the procedure described by Elkasabi, Ren and Pullum [15]. This approach adjusted for unequal probabilities of selection. The complex survey design, including clustering and stratification, was accounted for using survey (svy) commands. Variables capturing the location and year of the respondent’s most recent HIV test were not included in the Bayesian regression model because of substantial missing data and were used only to describe temporal trends in HIV testing uptake in stage two (Fig. 1). Although, complete-case analysis may have introduced selection bias if excluded respondents differed systematically from those included, trends were assessed using survey-weighted proportions and line graphs. While difference across groups with complete data were evaluated using chi-square test at 5% significance level.

Bayesian hierarchical analysis

Because survey estimation relies on design-based variance estimation, Bayesian estimation is model-based and derives uncertainty from the posterior distribution via Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) simulation [16]. Given the nested structure of the GDHS data, a Bayesian multilevel logistic regression model was fitted to examine factors associated with HIV testing uptake.

Because the Bayesian estimation procedures available in Stata 16.1 do not support survey weight or the svy prefix, the Bayesian multilevel models were estimated without incorporating survey weights. However, clustering (v001) in the DHS sampling design was accommodated through cluster-level random intercepts. Weakly informative N(0, 1002) priors were assigned to model parameters to improve estimation stability without imposing strong prior assumptions. The model was estimated using MCMC simulation with the Gibbs random-walk Metropolis–Hastings (MH) algorithm. We ran 25,000 iterations per chain (specifically 2 chains), with the first 5000 iterations discarded as burn-in to ensure convergence. The remaining 20,000 draws were used for posterior inference. A fixed random seed (1234) was used to ensure reproducibility.

Model convergence was assessed using standard diagnostic tools, including Gelman-Rubin statistic (Rc), trace, density, and autocorrelation plots, acceptance rate, Monte Carlo standard error (MCSE) and effective sample size (ESS). The estimates are reported as adjusted odds ratios (aORs) with 95% credible intervals (CrI). Evidence of association was inferred when the 95% CrI did not include the null value (aOR = 1). We also employed a survey-weighted multilevel generalised linear model with a logit link function as a sensitivity analysis to assess the robustness of the primary findings. In this model, adjusted odds ratios (aORs) their corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CI) are reported.

Concentration index

The concentration index (CI) measures socioeconomic-related inequality of health variable such as HIV testing uptake [17]. This study used the Erreygers normalization concentration index (ECI) to assess socioeconomic inequality in HIV testing uptake because it accounts for the bounded nature of the binary outcome and satisfies the mirror property [18]. The “conindex” command was used to plot the Concentration Curve (CC) and estimate the ECIs. Although the standard CI ranges from − 1 and + 1, it is not appropriate for binary health outcomes because it may produce bias estimates of socioeconomic inequality [19]. HIV testing uptake is a binary outcome, with “0” indicating no HIV testing and “1” indicating that HIV testing has occurred. The CC plots the cumulative percentage of HIV testing uptake against cumulative proportion of the population ranked by wealth (from poorest to richest). The 45˚ line represents the line of equality, where the same cumulative percentage of HIV testing uptake is observed across the population. The CC indicates a pro-poor distribution when the curve lies above the line of equality, and a pro-rich when it lies below the line of equality [20]. A negative value of the ECI indicates that HIV testing is disproportionately concentrated among the poor, whereas a positive value indicates concentration among the rich. Prevalence of HIV testing uptake by facility type

The prevalence of HIV testing uptake increased gradually over time, with a more pronounced rise observed between 2020 and 2022 (Fig. 5). Gender disparities in HIV testing uptake and associate factors

Table 2 shows statistically significant association between socioeconomic and HIV testing uptake in the overall sample and in the gender-stratified analyses (p < 0.001). Women reported substantially higher uptake (57.4%, 95% CI: 55.9–58.7) compared with men (26.9%, 95% CI: 25.3–28.6). The uptake of HIV testing significantly increased with age, peaking among respondents aged 30–34 years (66.9%, 95% CI: 64.5–69.2). Across all age categories, the prevalence of HIV testing uptake was higher among women than men. Specifically, among those aged 25–29 years, uptake was 72.5% (95% CI: 69.9–74.9) among women compared with 30.8% (95% CI: 27.2–34.7) among men. Similarly, among respondents aged 30–34 years, uptake was 77.3% (95% CI: 74.6–79.7) for women against 39.5% (95% CI: 35.1–44.0) for men. Additionally, urban residence had higher HIV testing uptake (52.0%, 95% CI: 50.5–53.5) than rural residence (42.0%, 95% CI: 40.2–43.8). However, gender disparity persists in both settings. In urban areas, uptake was 60.6% (95% CI: 58.7–62.5) among women compared with 32.8% (95% CI: 30.4–35.4) among men. The corresponding prevalence in rural areas were 53.0% (95% CI: 50.9–55.1) and 19.8% (95% CI: 17.8–22.0), respectively (Table 2).

Gender disparities of HIV testing uptake and associate factors

Likewise, there was a statistically significant association between educational level and HIV testing uptake. Individuals with higher education had the highest uptake of 72.0% (95% CI: 69.2–74.6). Specifically, women with higher education reported the highest prevalence of HIV testing uptake (79.4%, 95% CI: 75.6–82.7) compared with 60.8% (95% CI: 55.8–65.5) among men. Conversely, uptake was noticeably lower among respondents with no formal education, particularly among men (10.5%, 95% CI: 8.4–13.2). Also, employment status was significantly associated with HIV testing, those employed reported higher uptake 52.5% (95% CI: 51.1–53.8) compared with 30.8% (95% CI: 29.1–32.6) of those who are unemployed. Within both categories, women consistently had higher uptake than men. Similarly, HIV testing uptake increased across wealth quintiles. Among the rich/richest group, uptake was 56.6% (95% CI: 55.1–58.1), compared with 36.7% (95% CI: 34.9–38.5) among the poorest/poor. The disparities persisted across all wealth categories, with men consistently reporting lower uptake. Significant regional variation was also observed. Testing uptake was relatively higher in Greater Accra (54.2%, 95% CI: 51.3–57.0) and Eastern (55.0%, 95% CI: 51.7–58.4) regions, and lower in Northern (31.9%, 95% CI: 26.9–37.4) and Savannah (25.1%, 95% CI: 20.2–30.6) regions. Across the 16 regions, women demonstrated higher HIV testing uptake than men (Table 2).

Bayesian analysis of factors associated with HIV testing uptake

Table 3 presents the adjusted odds ratios (aORs) and 95% credible intervals (CrIs) from the Bayesian multilevel logistic regression models for the pooled sample and the gender-stratified models. Overall, men had significantly lower odds of HIV testing uptake than women (aOR = 0.17; 95% CrI: 0.14, 0.19). Compared with adolescents aged 15–19 years, individuals aged 25–29 years (aOR = 6.17; 95% CrI: 4.63, 7.83) and 30–34 years (aOR = 6.73; 95% CrI: 5.72, 8.46) had higher odds of HIV testing in the pooled model. Similar associations were observed in the gender-stratified analyses. Among men, those aged 25–29 years (aOR = 22.74; 95% CrI: 5.30, 54.32) and 30–34 years (aOR = 29.35; 95% CrI: 7.32, 76.57) had substantially higher odds of HIV testing than those aged 15–19 years. Likewise, among women, those aged 25–29 years (aOR = 11.01; 95% CrI: 6.82, 18.60) and 30–34 years (aOR = 12.50; 95% CrI: 5.49, 23.14) also had higher odds of HIV testing uptake than adolescents aged 15–19 years.

Individuals residing in rural areas had lower odds of HIV testing than those in urban areas in the pooled model (aOR = 0.80; 95% CrI: 0.54, 0.98). Although, a similar association was in the gender-specific models, the credible intervals included one, indicating no statistically significant association (Table 3). Compared with individuals with secondary education, those with no formal education had lower odds of HIV testing in the pooled model (aOR = 0.39; 95% CrI: 0.36, 0.44). Similar associations were observed among men (aOR = 0.31; 95% CrI: 0.18, 0.48) and women (aOR = 0.41; 95% CrI: 0.37, 0.46). In contrast, individuals with higher education had roughly three times the odds of HIV testing in the pooled model (aOR = 3.06; 95% CrI: 2.54–4.01). The association was stronger among (aOR = 3.92; 95% CrI: 2.30, 7.08) than among women (aOR = 2.17; 95% CrI: 1.48, 2.91). Marital status was also associated with HIV testing uptake. Compared with married individuals, those who had never married had lower odds of HIV testing in the pooled model (aOR = 0.22; 95% CrI: 0.18, 0.27). A similar pattern was observed in the gender-specific analyses, particularly among women (aOR = 0.17; 95% CrI: 0.12, 0.22). Individuals in the rich/richest wealth quintiles had higher odds of HIV testing than those in the poorest/poor wealth quintiles in the pooled model (aOR = 1.38; 95% CrI: 1.24–1.53). Similar associations were observed among men (aOR = 1.82; 95% CrI: 1.58, 2.14) and women (aOR = 1.62; 95% CrI: 1.35, 2.15).

The Bayesian multilevel indicated residual community-level variation in HIV testing uptake. After adjustment for individual-level covariates, the estimated community-level variance was 1.76 (95% CrI: 0.20, 3.80) in the pooled model, 2.51 (95% CrI: 0.16, 5.76) among men, and 1.82 (95% CrI: 0.33, 3.78) among women. The corresponding intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC) were 34.6%, 43.2%, and 35.6%, respectively. This indicate that a substantial proportion of variation in HIV testing uptake remained attributable to differences between communities after accounting for individual-level characteristics. The MCMC acceptance rates ranged from 24.9% to 36.4% across all the models, which falls within the recommended range of approximately 20% to 50% for MH [11, 25, 26]. This suggest adequate proposal efficiency when considered alongside Gelman-Rubin satisfactory convergence statistics and ESS [11, 26, 27]. Gelman-Rubin statistics value close 1 (Rc < 1.1), indicate good convergence of MCMC chains [28]. The Gelman-Rubin statistics ranges from 1.00 to 1.08 for women, 1.00–1.16 for men, and 1.00–1.20 for all gender (SI Table S2), suggesting a moderate convergence [29, 30]. An Rc higher than 1.1 can occur for several reasons, including strong correlations between the intercept and other parameters such as group-level random effect [28], poor initialization of chains (thus; if chains start from different values) [31], the choice of parameterization which affects convergence in hierarchical models [32, 33], and weak identifiability (e.g. sparse data or unbalanced groups, resulting to wide or multi-modal posterior for the intercept) [34]. However, our analysis with 25,000 iterations had many Rc close to 1.0, with maximum Rc only arising from men (Rc = 1.16 among 25–29 years) and all gender models (Rc = 1.20 among rural residents), exceeding the 1.1 (SI Table S2). The ESS mirrors how many independent samples the MCMC has effectively drawn. The ESS values greater than 200 are usually adequate for stable posterior estimates [11]. Most groups in this study had ESS beyond 200, the ESS ranges from 242.64 to 2335.63 (all gender), 182.69–3173.96 (men), and 127.50–3817.00 for women (SI Table S2). Groups that showed < 200 ESS, suggests unreliable estimates requiring more sampling or better turning, while groups with ESS > 200 suggest good estimates. Visual inspection of the trace, posterior density and histogram plots suggested substantial sampling behaviour and good mixing between chains, while autocorrelation plots show relatively slow decay, indicating residual dependence across all models (SI Figures S1-S3). Despite these, fewer ESS and Rc were below the threshold, suggesting the need for longer chains, further sampling and diagnostics.

The sensitivity analysis revealed similar estimates of HIV testing uptake, suggesting consistency of findings in the Bayesian model (SI Table S1). The large adjusted odds ratios observed, particularly men reflect substantial differences in HIV testing uptake across categories rather than numerical instability.

Socioeconomic inequalities across wealth of HIV testing uptake

Residence also played a key role in explaining inequality. Rural residence reduced socioeconomic inequality in HIV testing uptake by 10.6% in the pooled sample, indicating rural residents were disproportionately represented by poorer households. In some cases, contributions exceeded 100%, reflecting strong opposing effects across subgroups and the additive nature of the decomposition approach. Consequently, this may occur because positive and negative contributions offset one another. Values greater than 100% or 1000%, indicated that a covariate exerts strong effect than the overall measured inequality but is partially offset by other variables acting in the opposite direction. The contribution among men and women exceeded 100%, highlighting persistent rural—urban disparities in HIV testing uptake. However, percentage contributions exceeding 100% should not be interpreted literally as explaining more than all observed inequality but rather as reflecting offsetting contributions from other determinants. Education emerges as another contributing factor. Individuals with no formal education reduced socioeconomic inequality in HIV testing uptake by 5.8%, reflecting a higher concentration among poorer households. However, unemployment showed divergent patterns by sex. Among men, unemployment substantially reduced inequality, while among women it increased inequality by 5.4%, indicating concentration among wealthier households. Extreme values observed in some subgroups reflect strong opposing contributions within decomposition framework.

Discussion

This study employed a Bayesian hierarchical logistic approach and decomposition analysis to examine gender and socioeconomic inequalities of HIV testing uptake. Sparse data in the GDHS is one of the essential components that this method captured, revealing the true influence of factors on HIV testing [9, 11]. This study found that only 48% of respondents have ever been tested for HIV, indicating substantial gaps in testing coverage. HIV testing uptake was more pronounced in women (57.4%) than men (26.9%). This highlights the need for targeted interventions to increase awareness and access to HIV testing, particularly among populations that are currently underserved. The high rate of HIV testing at public health facilities observed in this present study highlights their central role in service delivery. Although private-sector and home-based contributions were less to overall uptake, evidence suggests that home-based HIV testing is feasible, acceptable, and effective in increasing testing uptake and coverage, particularly among underserved populations [35, 36]. Therefore, scaling up home-based and community-based approaches could complement facility-based services and improve access to HIV testing among populations less likely to utilise formal health facilities [35, 37].

The Bayesian multilevel analysis revealed that gender, age, residence, education, employment status, marital status, and wealth were statistically significant predictors of HIV testing uptake. Substantial community-level variation persisted after controlling for individual-level factors, highlighting the importance of both individual and contextual determinants in shaping HIV testing behaviors. Notably, men were significantly less likely to undergo HIV testing compared with women. This is consistent with previous studies conducted in sub-Saharan Africa and Ghana, which have reported lower HIV testing uptake among men, often attributed to gender norms, lower health-seeking behavior, and limited contact with health services [4, 38–41]. In Ghana, women have greater opportunities to test through antenatal care services and prevention of mother-to-child transmission (PMTCT) programs, which integrate routine HIV testing into maternal health services. This increases women’s likelihood of HIV testing compared with men. This may be explained by men’s poor health-seeking behaviors, as they often have limited contact with the healthcare system [42]. Nonetheless, the lower odds of HIV testing observed among men in this study may reflects barriers identified in the literature, including stigma, perceived low risk of HIV infection, concerns about confidentiality with healthcare workers, and financial costs, which collectively contribute to lower testing uptake [4, 38].

Another important predictor of HIV testing uptake in this study was age. Individuals aged 25–34 years were significantly more likely to test for HIV than adolescents aged 15–19 years. This finding aligns with previous research in sub-Saharan Africa showing that HIV testing uptake increases with age due to greater exposure to sexual health information, higher perceived risk of infection, and increased interaction with healthcare services [43, 44]. Younger adolescents may face additional barriers such as stigma, concern about confidentiality, and limited autonomy in accessing HIV testing services, which may reduce their likelihood of testing.

Educational attainment also played a significant role in HIV testing uptake among both men and women. Individuals with higher education had higher odds of HIV testing compared with those with lower levels of education, consistent with patterns observed in both men and women in this study. This highlights the important role of education in promoting HIV testing uptake. Education improves health literacy, awareness of HIV prevention strategies, and understanding of the benefits of early diagnosis and treatment [4, 40, 43, 45]. Previous studies in Ghana and other parts of sub-Saharan Africa have similarly demonstrated that higher educational attainment is associated with increased HIV testing uptake [4, 6, 42, 45, 46]. Educated individuals are more likely to access health information through media and public health campaigns, which can positively influence health-seeking behaviors [6, 45].

Furthermore, individuals residing in rural areas were less likely to test for HIV compared with urban residents. This finding reflects persistent rural-urban disparities in access to healthcare services. Although men were less likely to test for HIV than women in this study, the pattern highlights broader gender-and residence-related inequalities in access to HIV testing services. These findings are consistent with earlier studies reporting rural–urban disparities in healthcare access in many low- and middle-income countries [3, 39, 45, 47, 48]. This suggest that rural populations may face barriers such as limited availability of healthcare facilities, transportation barriers, and lower exposure to HIV awareness campaigns. Rural-urban disparities in HIV testing uptake, particular among women of reproductive age, have been widely documented [3]. These structural barriers could be associated with lower testing uptake in rural areas than urban areas.

Socioeconomic status was also a significant determinant of HIV testing uptake. Individuals from wealthier households were more likely to test for HIV compared with those from poorer households. Notably, men from wealthier households had higher odds of HIV testing compared with those in the poorest wealth quintile, with a similar pattern observed among women. This finding reflects broader socioeconomic inequalities in healthcare services access and health information. Wealthier individuals may have better access to transportation, healthcare facilities, and health-related information, which facilitate HIV testing uptake. Similar socioeconomic disparities in HIV testing have been reported in previous studies across sub-Saharan Africa [43, 47].

Likewise, the multilevel analysis revealed considerable community-level variation in HIV testing uptake, indicating that contextual factors beyond individual characteristics could influence testing behaviour. Community-level factors such as prevailing social norms, availability of testing facilities, HIV awareness programs, and local health infrastructure likely contribute to these differences. Similar findings from multilevel studies in sub-Saharan Africa underscore the important role of community-level factors in shaping HIV service utilisation [49].

This study therefore suggested that large odds ratios, especially men should therefore be interpreted as indicating strong relative associations between age and HIV testing rather than unusually large absolute increases in testing probability or stable estiamtes.

The decomposition analysis further revealed that HIV testing uptake was disproportionately concentrated among wealthier populations, indicating a pro-rich inequality. This suggests that individuals from higher socioeconomic backgrounds are more likely to access HIV testing services [5, 7]. The decomposition results showed that age, education, and residence were the main contributors to this inequality, with higher education and various age groups contributing positively to the pro-rich distribution of HIV testing. In particular, these factors increased the observed socioeconomic-related inequality by being more concentrated among wealthier households.

In contrast, men, rural residents, never married, ever married, unemployed men, and lower educational attainment contributed negatively to the observed inequality. These inequalities in HIV testing uptake are likely driven by structural determinants surrounded within both the healthcare system and broader social context. They also reflect lower testing uptake among disadvantaged populations, who are disproportionately represented in poorer households. Lower uptake among rural populations may be attributed to limited availability and accessibility of HIV testing services, distances to health facilities, and weaker outreach programme [42, 48]. These barriers are compounded by the greater concentration of healthcare resources and HIV testing services in urban areas. Similar rural–urban disparities in HIV testing have been reported in Ghana and attributed to inequities in the distribution of health services and access to care [3]. In addition, lower testing among men than women may be influenced by prevailing gender norms and reduced engagement with routine healthcare services [50]. Furthermore, HIV-related stigma remains an important structural and social barrier that discourages individuals from seeking testing and care, particularly among men and populations living in rural and underserved communities [42, 51, 52].

These findings highlight persistent socioeconomic and structural inequalities in HIV testing uptake. Addressing these disparities will require targeted public health interventions that improve access to HIV testing services among disadvantaged populations. Expanding community-based HIV testing programs, increasing outreach services in rural areas, and strengthening health education initiatives among populations with low educational attainment may help reduce inequalities and improve early HIV diagnosis.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths. First, the analysis employed a Bayesian multilevel modeling approach, which handled sparse subgroups data and examine individual level determinants of HIV testing uptake while accounting for clustering within communities. Second, the use of nationally representative survey data enhances the generalizability of the findings. Third, the study incorporated decomposition analysis of the Erreygers concentration index, which provided additional insight into the major drivers of socioeconomic inequalities in HIV testing uptake. However, some limitations worth acknowledging. The study relied on cross-sectional data, which limits the ability to establish causal relationships between the explanatory variables and HIV testing uptake. In addition, HIV testing status was self-reported, which may have introduced recall bias and affected the estimated prevalence of HIV testing uptake in Ghana. Furthermore, temporal trends in HIV testing were reconstructed retrospectively from participants’ reports of the most recent HIV test. Consequently, these trends may be subject to recall error and should be interpreted cautiously, as they do not represent prospectively collected longitudinal data. Although the diagnostic statistics and plots indicate moderate stability and convergence, results from this study should again be interpreted with caution due to evidence of likely unstable estimates. Furthermore, potentially relevant contextual factors, such as community-level HIV awareness programmes or cultural norms, were not available in the dataset and therefore could not be included in the analysis. Further studies, should apply more advanced methods to examine factors associated with HIV testing uptake in Ghana, including contextual factors that the present study could not address.

Policy implications

The findings highlight the need for targeted interventions to improve equitable access to HIV testing services, particularly among men, rural populations, individuals with low educational attainment, and those from poorer households. Intensifying community-based and mobile HIV testing services, especially in underserved rural areas, could help reduce geographic barriers to testing. The distribution of HIV self-test kits in the country is commendable. However, policy implementation should ensure adequate linkage to counselling services, as individuals who tested HIV-positive through self-testing may experience psychological distress and may not subsequently seek counselling. This includes ensuring direct and timely access to professional support and guidance, including confirmatory testing, prompt linkage to treatment or care, and effective follow-up support from community health workers. In addition, public health programs should strengthen health education and awareness campaigns to improve HIV-related knowledge among populations with lower levels of education. Interventions that encourage men engagement in HIV testing, such as workplace testing programs and community outreach initiatives, may also help address the observed gender disparities in HIV testing uptake. Collectively, these strategies could reduce socioeconomic inequalities and improve early detection of HIV.

Conclusion

This study provides insightful evidence on gender and socioeconomic inequalities in HIV testing uptake in Ghana. Overall, less than 50% of Ghanaians had ever tested for HIV, with testing among men below 30%. Gender, age, education, residence, and socioeconomic status were associated with HIV testing uptake, with community-level variation persisting after adjusting for individual-level factors. HIV testing uptake was disproportionately concentrated among wealthier groups, highlighting persistent socioeconomic inequalities in access to testing services. This study suggested equity-focused HIV testing strategies to address both individual and structural barriers to testing, particularly among men, rural populations, and socioeconomically disadvantaged groups. To reduce these disparities, targeted interventions—such as male-focused outreach programmes, rural mobile testing services, and integration with non-HIV services are recommended to improve HIV testing uptake in Ghana.
