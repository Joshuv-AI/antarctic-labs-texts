Restriction-modification systems and prophages drive genomic diversification in Priestia megaterium
Authors: Miao Wang, Chuyang Shao, Juan Ignacio Vílchez, Danxia He, Lixian Chen, Jin-Lin Zhang, Huiming Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBacteria are frequently stressed by bacteriophages. Priestia megaterium is a bacterium of both agricultural and biotechnological importance, yet it has been unclear how the interplay between phage-imposed stress and bacterial immunity shapes the genome evolution and stability in P. megaterium. Through comparative genomics and methylome profiling, herein we show that restriction-modification (RM) systems are the dominant and ubiquitous barrier to lysogeny in P. megaterium. Intact prophages disproportionately carry anti-restriction genes, including multiple DNA methyltransferases and anti-restriction proteins, revealing the molecular strategies required to overcome the strong RM-mediated selective pressure inherent to this species. Prophage-derived genes are significantly enriched within the unique pangenome fraction and include auxiliary metabolic genes absent in host genomes. Moreover, prophage content strongly predicts phylogenetic structure in P. megaterium. Together, our findings demonstrate how defensive constraints, phage counter-adaptations, and horizontal acquisition jointly drive genomic diversification of P. megaterium.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13062-026-00841-0.

In natural environments, bacteria are frequently stressed by bacteriophages. Temperate phages can integrate into host chromosomes or persist as plasmids, establishing lysogeny and thereby altering host genomes [1]. Priestia megaterium (previously known as Bacillus megaterium) is a Gram-positive bacterium found in diverse habitats such as plant rhizosphere and human intestine, bearing dual agricultural and industrial importance due to its plant-beneficial traits and advantages as a cell factory for biotechnological applications [2–6]. It has been unclear how the interplay between phage-imposed stress and bacterial immunity shapes the genome evolution and stability in P. megaterium. Through comparative genomics of 183 strains and methylome analysis of co-isolated strains with divergent phage histories, herein we show how specific defensive constraints, phage counter-adaptations, and horizontal acquisition jointly drive genomic diversification of P. megaterium.

We started with a comparative genomic analysis of 183 P. megaterium strains (Supplementary Dataset 1). Prophage sequences were identified in 120 strains as 215 distinct regions, exhibiting considerable size diversity ranging from 1.6 kb to 239.4 kb, and included 10 intact, potentially excisable prophages (Supplementary Dataset 1). Phylogenetic classification indicated that prophage sequences from the Schatzie and Lilbeanie genera were the most prevalent within the P. megaterium genomes (Fig. 1a). Meanwhile, 1,479 defense genes belonging to 77 distinct types of anti-phage systems were identified across all genomes (Fig. 1b), with every strain encoding at least one defense system type. Notably, restriction-modification (RM) systems were the dominant and universal defensive strategy, present in all 183 strains (Fig. 1b). The central role of RM systems in defense suggests they exert strong selective pressure on phages. Consistent with this, the prophage count negatively correlated with total number of defense genes (Spearman ρ = −0.18, p = 0.04), driven primarily by RM systems (Spearman ρ = −0.23, p = 0.01), supporting that RM systems are the principal barrier to lysogeny in P. megaterium (Fig. S1a, S1b).

To establish a mechanistic link between RM systems and phage resistance, we performed a focused analysis of two strains, TG1-E1 and YC4-R4, which were co-isolated by our laboratory from the same ecological niche [7, 8]. Genome analysis revealed that TG1-E1 harbors two integrated prophage regions, while YC4-R4 is prophage-free (Supplementary Dataset 1). A detailed comparison of their defense repertoires revealed a critical difference. YC4-R4 encodes a functional M.EacI/EacI RM system, which catalyzes 6mA methylation and targets the DNA sequence “GGATC” [9], whereas TG1-E1 lacks this or other intact RM systems (Supplementary Dataset 2). Examination of global methylation patterns via 6mA dot blot and Single‑Molecule Real‑Time (SMRT) sequencing revealed 18,882 6mA methylation sites in YC4-R4, which was a more than tenfold increase over the 1,085 sites found in TG1-E1 (Fig. 1c and d). Motif analysis confirmed that the “GGATC” sequence was the dominant methylation target in YC4-R4, accounting for 50.0% (9,447) of all its 6mA sites (Supplementary Dataset 2, Fig. S2). Crucially, the prophage regions integrated in TG1-E1 contained 252 unmethylated “GGATC” sequences (Supplementary Dataset 2). This provides a direct molecular explanation for the observed phenotypic difference, that is, the functional M.EacI/EacI system in YC4-R4 would recognize and cleave invading phage DNA at these unprotected sites, thereby preventing lysogeny. These findings showcased how the presence or absence of a single, specific RM system can dictate the outcome of phage infection and shape the prophage repertoire of a strain.

In response to this pervasive and potent host defense, successful prophages exhibit evolved molecular countermeasures, as shown by the strong positive correlation between the intactness of prophage genomes and the presence of prophage-encoded genes dedicated to neutralizing RM systems (Fig. 1e). Particularly, 80% of the identified intact prophages encoded anti-RM genes, compared to < 16.2% of incomplete prophage regions. These counter-defenses include phage-encoded DNA methyltransferases, which modify the phage genome to mimic host methylation patterns and evade restriction, and anti-restriction proteins (Fig. 1f). For instance, an intact prophage in strain Bi82 encodes three distinct methyltransferases, including M1.NcuI, M.NgoAXII and M1.BspACI [10–12], illustrating a strategy of deploying multiple modifications to ensure protection against a variety of host restriction activities. Similarly, strain MER_65 encodes the anti-restriction protein ArdA, a DNA mimic that binds to and inhibits Type I restriction endonucleases [13]. These adaptations highlight the intense evolutionary pressure exerted by RM systems and demonstrate that achieving stable lysogeny in P. megaterium often requires phages to actively disarm this primary line of host immunity.

Beyond this antagonistic arms race, our analysis reveals that prophages contribute substantially to the functional and genetic diversity of P. megaterium, transitioning from invaders to integrated sources of genomic innovation. Among the 7306 protein coding DNA sequences (CDS) in the prophage regions, 3,746 were complete and showed a significant enrichment (39.8% vs. 23.5% in the non-prophage genome) for genes of “Unknown Function (S)” (Fig. 2a), positioning prophages as a major reservoir of novel genetic material. Among genes with predicted functions, we identified 97 auxiliary metabolic genes (AMGs) residing within 41 prophage regions (Supplementary Dataset 1). These AMGs are involved in central metabolic pathways including coenzyme, amino acid, and carbohydrate metabolism (Fig. S3). Importantly, 28 of these AMGs had no homologous sequences in the non-prophage genome of their respective host strains, indicating that prophages can provide unique metabolic capabilities not inherent to the P. megaterium genetic backbone, such as specialized roles in stress-related nucleotide metabolism (Fig. 2b).

Pangenome analysis confirmed that P. megaterium possesses an open pangenome (Fig. 2c), indicative of high genetic flux and ongoing horizontal gene transfer. The pangenome consisted of 165,615 core genes (16.2%), 840,671 accessory genes (82.2%), and 16,197 unique genes (1.6%) (Fig. S4). A total of 6,834 prophage-derived CDS were mapped to the pangenome. These CDS were disproportionately enriched within the unique gene fraction (2,880 genes; 17.78%), compared to the accessory (3,894 genes; 0.46%) and core (60 genes; 0.04%) fractions (Fig. 2d and e and Supplementary Dataset 1). This strong bias demonstrates that prophage integrations are a primary source of strain-specific genetic material. Furthermore, a Mantel test revealed a strong correlation (r = 0.8997, p = 0.001) between the genetic distance based on prophage content and the overall phylogenetic distance between strains (Fig. 2f). This result demonstrates that the gain and loss of prophages is not a random accessory feature but a major evolutionary force driving strain-level diversification and phylogenetic structure within P. megaterium.

In summary, our study reveals RM systems as the universal and dominant barrier to prophage integration in P. megaterium, whereas successful prophages disproportionately carry anti-restriction genes, including multiple DNA methyltransferases and anti-restriction proteins, thereby revealing the molecular strategies required to overcome the strong RM-mediated selective pressure inherent to P. megaterium. Prophage-derived genes are significantly enriched within the unique pangenome fraction and include auxiliary metabolic genes absent in host genomes. Moreover, prophage content strongly predicts phylogenetic structure in P. megaterium. These findings collectively demonstrate how defensive constraints, phage counter-adaptations, and horizontal acquisition jointly drive genomic diversification of a bacterium with agricultural and industrial importance.
