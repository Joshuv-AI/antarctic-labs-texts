A protocol for a randomized controlled trial of photobiomodulation for management of temporomandibular disorder pain in Adults
Authors: Selenia Rubio, Patricia Cabrera, Rory Reever, Cesar Migliorati, Richard Ohrbach, Frank C. Gibson, Zhigang Li, Angela Mickle, Rosalynn R.Z. Conic, Roger B. Fillingim, Margarete Ribeiro Dasilva
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Evidence-based treatments for painful temporomandibular disorders (TMD) are lacking. The most common treatments for painful TMDs, intraoral appliances and pain medication, provide suboptimal pain control, often leading to treatment-limiting adverse effects. Photobiomodulation therapy (PBM) shows substantial potential for the management of pain in people with a TMD; however, its efficacy for TMD pain reduction has not been rigorously tested. We will conduct a double-blind, randomized, placebo-controlled clinical trial of multimodal PBM for TMD pain. This single-site trial will randomize 130 participants, aged 18 years and older, with chronic painful TMD. Individuals will complete a detailed medical history to confirm eligibility criteria, followed by a clinical exam to confirm TMD case status according to the Diagnostic Criteria for TMD (DC/TMD). Eligible participants will be randomized to either active or sham PBM treatment. Participants will complete eight treatment visits scheduled 2–5 days apart. This will be followed by a post-intervention visit that will include symptom questionnaires, TMD exam, pressure pain threshold (PPT) measures, and blood draw. A 6-month follow-up visit will include a TMD exam, questionnaires, and pressure pain threshold measures. Analyses will determine intervention effects on the primary outcome (pain intensity) and multiple secondary outcomes and will examine whether changes in inflammation and pain sensitivity are associated with the intervention response.Clinicaltrials.gov identifierNCT05916235

Introduction

Temporomandibular disorders (TMDs) are a set of complex musculoskeletal disorders often characterized by pain and limited jaw function. Painful TMDs affect 5–12 % of the population [1], incurring significant healthcare costs and significantly reducing quality of life (QOL). Despite their public health impact, evidence-based treatments for painful TMDs are lacking. The most common treatments, such as intraoral appliances and pain medication, provide suboptimal pain control and often lead to treatment-limiting adverse effects [[2], [3], [4]]. Therefore, the development of new, safe, and effective therapies is crucial to improving the QOL for patients with these painful conditions.

Recent years have witnessed a burgeoning interest in the therapeutic potential of photobiomodulation (PBM) for treating pain, including low-level laser therapy (LLLT) and Light Emitting Diodes (LED). Briefly, both LLLT (laser) and LED light stimulate photoreceptors in the target tissue(s), activating secondary mediators (e.g., adenosine triphosphate (ATP), cyclic adenosine monophosphate (cAMP), and nitric oxide (NO), thereby influencing multiple biological processes, including gene expression, cell signaling, cellular metabolism, and cytokine release [5].

Therapeutic PBM devices are often manufactured to emit similar wavelengths in the red or near-infrared spectrum; however, these two modalities differ in important ways. First, laser-based systems are usually higher power, with smaller beams, resulting in higher intensity (irradiance) and a stronger direct effect on nociceptive and analgesic pathways compared to LEDs [[6], [7], [8]]. In contrast, LEDs emit lower power light over a larger area, and this lower irradiance and lower dose produce less penetration but a greater effect on reducing inflammation and increasing blood and lymphatic flow [5]. Thus, while these two forms of PBM share similarities, important differences render them potentially highly complementary in the treatment of musculoskeletal pain. Hence, our protocol will use both laser and light-emitting diodes.

PBM therapy has been demonstrated to confer several benefits on muscle tissues, including: prevention of post-exercise muscle damage, reduction in delayed onset muscle soreness (DOMS), increased workload capacity of muscle, improved fatigue resistance, and faster muscle recovery [9,10]. These benefits to muscle function could potentially improve TMD-related muscle pain. Another mechanism implicated in TMD pain is inflammation. Indeed, increased circulating levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as interleukin1β (IL-1β), IL-6, tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), and monocyte chemoattractant protein-1 (MCP-1) have been observed in participants with TMD, including both myalgia and arthralgia [11,12]. PBM therapy has been shown to decrease the release of several cytokines (e.g., IL-6, TNF-α, MCP-1, etc.) [[13], [14], [15], [16], [17]], which could, in turn, reduce TMD pain and associated hyperalgesia. Interestingly, PBM has been shown to reduce peripheral nociceptive drive [[18], [19], [20]] and to decrease the expression of pro-nociceptive proteins such as TRPV1, CGRP, and substance-P following trigeminal nerve injury [18].[NO_PRINTED_FORM]

As detailed in a recent meta-analysis [19], current evidence supporting the efficacy of PBM for TMD pain is of low quality for several reasons. First, most previous studies used single-wavelength PBM treatment parameters, which limits the ability to target multiple pathophysiological mechanisms. Second, few studies applied validated approaches to the TMD case definition, resulting in considerable heterogeneity and poor replicability of participant populations. Finally, previous trials failed to include carefully masked placebo conditions [19,20]. Therefore, we will conduct a double-blind, randomized, placebo-controlled trial to address these shortcomings. We propose to rigorously test a mixed modality (i.e., comprised of both laser and LED light sources and including different wavelengths, power densities, and energy densities) PBM protocol to treat chronic painful TMD, targeting several potential pathophysiological pathways implicated in TMD pain. We hypothesize that, compared to sham PBM, active PBM will significantly reduce TMD pain, and the analgesic effects of PBM will be associated with reductions in mechanical pain sensitivity and systemic inflammation.

Materials and methods

Study design

In this single-site, Phase II, double-blind, placebo-controlled, randomized trial, participants will receive either active PBM or sham PBM. The same PBM device will be used for both groups; however, it includes a sham setting that emits no therapeutic light. The trial was registered at Clinicaltrials.gov (identifier NCT05916235) prior to participant enrollment. This protocol includes all the elements of the SPIRIT (Standard Protocol Items: Recommendations for Interventional Trials) Checklist [21]. The study was approved by the University of Florida Institutional Review Board (IRB# 202300986) in 2023. After completing a pre-screening computer-assisted telephone interview (CATI), individuals meeting the initial eligibility criteria will provide informed consent at an in-person visit (V0) at the University of Florida, where TMD status will be confirmed via clinical examination using the Diagnostic Criteria for Temporomandibular Disorder (DC/TMD) [22,23]. Pressure pain threshold (PPT) and inflammatory markers will also be measured at V0 to establish a baseline for each participant.

Enrolled participants will complete an electronic Daily Symptom Diary (DSD) for at least one week before the first intervention visit (V1). At V1, those participants who reported an average pain score ≥30 on the DSD will be randomized to receive either active or sham PBM treatment across eight sessions over three to four weeks. Blood draws, PPT, and DC/TMD assessment will be repeated at visits V5, V9, and V10 (approximately six months after V9). DSDs will continue during treatment, for one week prior to the one- and three-month follow-up visits after V9, and again one week before V10. The final visit, V10, will assess the long-term efficacy of PBM.

All data will be entered into REDCap, a secure, HIPAA-compliant electronic data capture system with built-in validation rules. The study coordinator and research staff will conduct ongoing monitoring to ensure data completeness and accuracy. Data oversight and protocol adherence will be managed internally by the PI, study coordinator, and biostatistician in accordance with the study's Quality Management Plan. The biostatistician will perform routine data checks and generate queries to resolve discrepancies.

Given the low-risk nature of the intervention and the use of objective outcome measures, a formal Data Monitoring Committee or Adjudication Committee is not required. No interim analyses are planned, and no formal stopping guidelines have been established. Adverse events will be assessed by the PI and reported in accordance with IRB and institutional policies. There are no formal provisions for ancillary or post-trial care. If a participant experiences harm related to study participation, the PI will assess the event and arrange appropriate medical care if needed. No additional compensation will be provided beyond what is required by institutional policy.

Safety oversight and reporting procedures follow International Conference on Harmonization (ICH) Guideline E2A for Clinical Safety Data Management. Adverse events (AEs) are graded as mild, moderate, or severe and assessed for relatedness to the PBM intervention as unrelated, unlikely, possible, probable, or definite. Serious adverse events (SAEs) are defined as any event that results in death, is life-threatening, requires hospitalization or prolongation of hospitalization, results in significant disability, or represents a congenital anomaly or other important medical event requiring intervention to prevent such outcomes. SAEs related to study procedures are reported to the sponsor within 24 h for deaths or life-threatening events and within five working days for all other SAEs. Expected AEs include mild and transient effects such as warmth, redness, itching, or dryness at the treatment site. The study may be temporarily halted if treatment-related SAEs occur, pending review by the principal investigator and oversight team.

While no independent auditing is scheduled, the trial may be audited by the IRB or the FDA, as applicable, particularly due to the use of a device intervention. Any important protocol modifications (e.g., changes to eligibility criteria, outcomes, or analyses) will be submitted to the IRB for approval and communicated to study staff and, when applicable, participants. Updates will also be made to ClinicalTrials.gov and reflected in future publications.

Access to the final trial dataset will be limited to the principal investigator, study biostatistician, and designated members of the research team. There are no contractual agreements that restrict investigator access to the final dataset. Trial results will be submitted for publication in peer-reviewed journals and reported on ClinicalTrials.gov in accordance with NIH policy. There are no publication restrictions. Data sharing will be available upon reasonable request to the PI after study completion, as outlined in the study's protocol.

Specific aims and hypothesis

The primary specific aim is to determine the efficacy of multimodal PBM vs. placebo for reducing pain immediately following the intervention. We hypothesize that compared to placebo, the multimodal PBM intervention will significantly reduce TMD pain immediately following the intervention.

The secondary specific aim is to investigate the effectiveness of PBM in reducing pain intensity and interference related to TMD 6 months after completion of the intervention. We hypothesize that compared to the placebo condition, participants who receive multimodal PBM will report lower pain intensity and interference up to 6 months after completion of the intervention.

The tertiary or exploratory aim is to investigate multiple outcomes of PBM therapy. To assess whether PBM increases jaw function, the Standard Movement Task (SMT) from the DC/TMD will be used to evaluate participants’ jaw range of motion and pain upon movement using a numeric rating scale (0–100). Outcomes will be measured by comparing pain intensity and range of motion for unassisted and assisted mouth opening between intervention and placebo groups at V9, controlling for baseline (V0), with assessments at V0, V5, V9, and V10 over a 220-day period. To determine if PBM reduces the inflammatory response, levels of circulating cytokines will be measured at V1, V5, and V9 using the Luminex Discovery Assay kits, with cortisol measured as a control. Cytokine levels at V5 and V9 will be compared to baseline (V1) between groups, over a 4–6 week period. The extent to which PBM reduces mechanical pain sensitivity will be evaluated using Pressure Pain Threshold (PPT) measurements via algometer on three cranial and two extracranial sites, comparing V5 and V9 with V0, between groups. PPT assessments will occur at V0, V5, V9, and V10 across 220 days. Lastly, to explore if PBM-induced changes in inflammation and pain sensitivity are associated with reductions in clinical pain, changes in cytokine levels and PPT after intervention will be compared to reductions in clinical pain recorded in daily diaries, the DC/TMD exam, and the PEG. Change scores will be calculated from V0 or V1 to V9, with analyses expected over a 4–8 week time frame.

Participants

This single-site trial will randomize 130 participants, aged 18 years and older, with chronic painful TMD recruited via posted flyers, social media, and referrals from local clinicians.

Individuals are excluded if they have a diagnosis of rheumathoid arthritis, fibromyalgia with predominant pain outside of the orofacial region, psychiatric hospitalization within one year of CATI, uncontrolled diabetes or seizures, treatments initiated within the last 30 days prior to screening or baseline visit (e.g., injections, occlusal appliances, or new pain medications), hypersensitivity to light therapy, are currently enrolled in another interventional study, or are currently pregnant or lactating.

Due to the unknown effects of PBM on pregnancy, female participants of childbearing potential will undergo urine pregnancy tests at Visit 0 and all intervention visits. Participants who become pregnant before randomization will be withdrawn from the study. If pregnancy occurs after randomization, the intervention will be discontinued, and the participant will be referred for appropriate care. With participant consent, data collection will continue for safety monitoring purposes until the pregnancy outcome is determined. Any outcome other than a normal birth or elective abortion of a healthy fetus will be reported as a serious adverse event (SAE).

Randomization

Participants (n = 130) will be randomized 1:1 to active or sham PBM (n = 65 per group). The placebo condition involves probe application without emitting light from the probes. A permuted block randomization will be used with random block sizes of 4 and 8 and with sex and baseline pain level [moderate (30–59) vs. severe (60–100)] treated as stratification factors. The study statistician will maintain the randomization code list and will be the only team member unblinded to treatment allocation. This ensures the integrity of the study blinding while allowing accurate data analysis. For participants deemed eligible at Visit 1 (V1), a randomization code will be generated in REDCap, and it will be entered into the laser randomization device. This code will determine whether the device will deliver active PBM (laser and LED) or sham PBM (no light emission) treatment.

Blinding/placebo management

To ensure masking, the THOR® LX2.3 laser device uses a pre-programmed Randomizing Switch Box containing numeric codes that correspond to either active or placebo output, unknown to study staff. Participants wear THOR protective goggles with internal LED light that obscures visual laser cues. Interventionists also wear THOR protective goggles that block the laser's red light, also keeping them blinded to treatment status. The randomization code will be maintained solely by the biostatistician. Investigators, coordinators and interventionist will remain blinded throughout data collection. Unblinding will occur only if knowledge of group assignment is required to address participant safety. If oversight bodies request unblinded data during the study, the unblinded biostatistician will generate the report.

Interventions

Photobiomodulation therapy intervention

All participants (n = 130) will be randomized to receive either active or sham PBM (n = 65 per group). All procedures will be performed using the THOR® LX2.3 Photobiomodulation System (THOR Photomedicine Ltd., Chesham, UK). The device is preprogrammed to operate in either active or sham mode according to the randomization code. Participants assigned to the active PBM group will receive the full multimodal PBM protocol integrating both high-irradiance laser and low-irradiance LED light delivered in a standardized sequence across sessions. Participants in the sham PBM group will undergo the same procedures using the identical probes, sequence, and duration; however, the device will operate in placebo mode with no light emission, preserving the blinding of both participants and interventionists. Each participant will complete eight intervention sessions over three to four weeks. Sessions will be spaced 2–5 days apart, and each will last approximately 30 min.

Interventionist training

PBM interventionist training

All interventionists will complete manufacturer-provided training to ensure safe and consistent device use. Training requires reviewing the Manual of Procedures (MOP), watching the training video, and completing three supervised practice sessions. Each interventionist must demonstrate accurate probe placement and delivery under the supervision of the Principal Investigator (PI) or Co-Investigator (Co-I). Annual retraining will be conducted to prevent protocol drift.

DC/TMD exam training

The PI and consultant (Dr. Richard Ohrbach), both calibrated DC/TMD examiners, will train study staff in the standardized clinical exam. Training consists of two phases:

Phase 1: includes instruction on palpation calibration (0.5 kg and 1 kg), anatomical landmark identification, mouth opening measurements, and adherence to the DC/TMD exam sequence. Staff will also be trained to complete the DC/TMD form in REDCap, including the required documentation sequence.

Phase 2: involves certification through practice exams on 10 individuals (5 controls and 5 participants with painful TMDs). The examiner must achieve 100 % concordance in TMD diagnostic classification with the PI or designated expert. Additional cases will be evaluated if needed to meet this threshold.

Primary outcome

Daily Symptom Diary (DSD)

Participants will complete the DSD electronically each evening from Visit 0 through Visit 9, and for one week prior to 1-month, 3-month, and 6-month follow-up (V10). The diary captures pain intensity (0–100 numeric rating scale [NRS]), pain density throughout the day (0–100 %), fatigue, sleep, and somatic symptoms. V9 is our primary endpoint visit.

Secondary outcomes

Pain, enjoyment, general activity (PEG) questionnaire

The PEG is a 3-item self-report scale (0–10) assessing average pain over the past week, and pain interference with enjoyment of life and general activity [24]. It will be administered at V0, V5, V9, 1- and 3-month follow-up, and V10.

Adherence to these procedures will be supported by scheduling the next visit during each session and emailing participants a summary of all visit dates and times. A personalized visit window is calculated for each participant; any deviations will be reported to the IRB. Study staff will monitor completion of Daily Symptom Diaries (DSDs), and participants who do not comply despite follow-up may be withdrawn from the study. If a participant is withdrawn, efforts will be made to complete a withdrawal visit that includes all Visit 9 procedures to collect endpoint data and minimize missing outcomes.

Tertiary/exploratory outcomes

Jaw functional limitation scale (JFLS)

The JFLS assesses jaw function across three domains: mastication, jaw mobility, and verbal/emotional expression [25]. [NO_PRINTED_FORM]The JFLS will be administered at V0, V5 (prior to intervention delivery), V9, and V10. Its psychometric properties make it suitable for monitoring functional outcomes in TMD populations [25].

TMD examination

Trained and calibrated study staff will perform a TMD examination at the Baseline Visit (V0), V5, V9, and V10 based on the (DC/TMD) [22,23]. The exam includes palpation of bilateral masticatory muscles (temporalis, masseter) and TMJs. To be eligible, participants must report jaw/facial pain modified by jaw function and meet DC/TMD criteria for myalgia or myalgia with arthralgia.

Pressure pain threshold (PPT) measurements

PPT will be assessed at V0, V5, V9, and V10 bilaterally over the temporalis, masseter, and trapezius muscles, the TMJs, and the lateral epicondyles with a pressure algometer. Three consistent trials (<30 kPa (kPA) difference), or up to five attempts, will be recorded. The mean of the two closest values will be used. Stimulus intensity will be increased at ∼30 kPA/sec until the participant reports pain, with a cutoff pressure of 600 kPA for all sites.

Long-term analgesic effectiveness of PBM

To evaluate PBM's long-term analgesic effects, DSD pain intensity scores from the week before V10 will be compared between intervention groups, controlling for baseline (V1) DSD pain intensity scores.

Blood assays

A trained phlebotomist will obtain up to 40 mL of blood by venipuncture at V1, V5, V9, and V10. Three whole blood tubes will be processed to obtain plasma. Plasma for cytokine and cortisol analyses will be stored at −80 °C. Circulating cytokine/chemokine levels (TNF-alpha, IL-6, IL-8/CXCL8, IL-10, CCL2/JE/MCP-1, IL-1beta/IL-1F2, and IL-1RA/IL-1F3 in plasma will be measured using a validated assay following the manufacturer's guidelines (Luminex Discovery Assay kit). Samples will be analyzed in duplicates to evaluate intersample differences. Cortisol levels will also be measured by immunoassay using the Cortisol Parameter Assay kit and used as a control variable.

Pain medication use

Participants who have taken daily prescription or over-the-counter (OTC) pain medication for ≥30 days prior to screening will be encouraged to maintain their regimen throughout the study and record each dose in the DSD. All pain medications recorded will be categorized as daily or episodic. Following randomization (V1), pain medication use recorded for the week prior to V9 will be compared between groups, controlling for pre-V1 use.

Sample size and statistical power

For the primary endpoint (average daily pain), we assume the standardized effect size is −0.55 based on findings reported in a recent meta-analysis [19]. We plan to randomize 130 participants (65/group). Assuming a 20 % attrition rate, we will have 104 subjects (52/group) for analysis at the end of the study. The statistical power with a two-sided type I error rate of 0.05 is 80 % based on a two-sample t-test. A 30 % reduction in pain has been reported as the minimally clinically significant effect [26]. Assuming an average pre-intervention pain rating of 5/10, and a standard deviation of 2.5 (as in our pilot study), a 30 % reduction in pain would be equal to a standardized mean difference of 0.6. Thus, we have more than adequate power for detecting a clinically significant reduction in pain. Also, given our power to detect a medium effect size, we have sufficient power to detect changes that are of practical significance for all secondary and exploratory outcomes.

Statistical analysis plan

Intent-to-treat (ITT) analysis will be performed for all analyses. The ITT sample includes all randomized participants regardless of study completion. A per-protocol (PP) analysis will also be considered complementary to the ITT analysis. The PP sample includes those who were compliant with the protocol (i.e., completed at least 75 % of treatment visits).

Data transformation: The distributions of all variables will be examined before analysis. Transformations such as natural-logarithm or Box-Cox transformations will be applied to continuous variables as needed to comply with normality assumptions. All binary variables will be coded 1/0 to indicate the two categories, and categorical variables will be converted into dummy variables. Generalized linear models or generalized linear mixed effects models (LMM) will be used for non-continuous outcomes.

Covariate adjustment: Since the randomization will be stratified by sex and baseline pain level [moderate (30–59) vs. severe (60–100)], those two variables will always be adjusted in all regression models to match the design so that the advantages of stratification can be achieved in the analysis results. We may not need to adjust for any other potential confounders because of the randomized design; however, we will examine the distributions of potential confounders such as race/ethnicity, age, and psychological factors assessed via questionnaire at V0 (See Table 3 for details on covariates.). Those with significantly different distributions between the two arms will be adjusted in the regression models. We will also adjust for those variables (if any) that are associated with the outcome variable in the regression models to increase the efficiency of the statistical estimation for the treatment effects.

Primary Objective: We hypothesize that compared to placebo, the PBM intervention will significantly reduce TMD pain. To evaluate the change in TMD-related pain immediately after PBM/placebo intervention, the dependent variable will be the 0–100 average daily pain ratings from the DSD. Ratings averaged over one week prior to V1 will serve as baseline; those averaged over the week before V9 will be the endpoint. A two-sample T-test will be used to compare the two intervention groups at V9 in terms of the primary endpoint. A linear regression model will test the association between average daily pain at V9 and intervention group (PBM vs placebo), adjusting for baseline pain, controlling for potential confounders (e.g., demographic variables, psychological factors). In addition, LMM will be used as the primary approach to handle repeated measures as it can provide valid estimates if data are missing at random. An alternative to LMM is Generalized Estimating Equations (GEE) to analyze the repeated measurements in relation to the intervention groups. GEE requires data missing completely at random. If there are no or very few missing data, GEE will be used as the primary method for the longitudinal data analysis.

Secondary Hypotheses: We hypothesize that, compared to placebo, participants receiving active PBM will have more favorable secondary endpoints 6 months after completion of the intervention, including: a) lower pain intensity & interference collected by the PEG questionnaire; b) lower jaw functional disability measured by the JFLS questionnaire; and c) lower pain ratings from the DSD for the week prior to V10.

For PEG scores, a linear regression model will be used to test the intervention effect at 1, 3, and 6 months separately, where baseline PEG scores and potential confounders (e.g., demographic variables, psychological factors, intervention expectations) will be adjusted. LMM and GEE (if needed) will assess the overall long-term effects by analyzing the measurements at 1, 3, and 6 months in the same model. Similar analyses will be performed using the JFLS score as the dependent variable to assess intervention effects on jaw functional disability and for DSD pain intensity ratings averaged over the week prior to the 6-month follow-up visit.

Exploratory hypothesis: We hypothesize that multimodal PBM will increase pressure pain thresholds and decrease levels of circulating pro-inflammatory cytokines; and PBM-induced reductions in mechanical pain sensitivity and inflammation measured midway through and immediately after intervention will predict greater improvements in clinical pain immediately following PBM. For this hypothesis, V1 cytokine levels will be treated as the baseline variable. The V5 cytokine levels will be treated as midpoint, and V9 cytokine levels will be treated as the endpoint. A linear regression model will test the level of cytokines at V1, V5, and V9 separately (PBM vs placebo), and potential confounders (e.g., demographic variables, psychological factors, intervention expectations) will be controlled in the model. We will use potential outcomes (PO, i.e., counterfactual-outcomes) mediation analysis approach to estimate and test whether changes in inflammation and PPT measured at V5 (halfway through intervention) mediate the effect of the intervention condition on average daily pain at V9. Mediation analyses that use mediator variables ascertained at intermediate time points support stronger causal inferences. However, we will also consider changes in PPT and inflammation from V0 and V1, respectively, to V9, as we recognize that intervention effects on these potential mediators may not fully emerge until the full dose of the intervention has been delivered. PO mediation analysis is more flexible than traditional mediation analysis approaches in terms of allowing interaction between the independent variable and mediators and non-linear indirect effects. In the mediation model, the intervention is the independent variable, inflammation and PPT will be the mediators, and the outcome variable is average daily pain from the DSD. Baseline values and potential confounders will be adjusted in the mediation model.

Missing data and sensitivity analysis

We will test for differences in baseline characteristics between participants lost to follow-up versus those who are not using the t-test and Fisher's exact test. Missing data patterns will also be evaluated, including the frequency and percentage of those missing for each variable and the distribution of the number of variables missing. In addition, data collected to the point of lost to follow-up will be compared to the data of those who complete the study to examine possible missing data mechanisms, e.g., missing completely at random, missing at random, or missing not at random. While data may not be missing completely at random, it may be reasonable to assume that data are missing at random. If this is the case, LMM or Generalized Linear Mixed Models (GLMM) approaches can generate valid results. Multiple model-based imputation methods will be applied to account for the missing data, if needed.

Discussion

This study aims to investigate the effect of multimodal PBM, compared to placebo, on chronic TMD pain. We will explore both the immediate and long-term effects of PBM treatment, including reductions in pain intensity, improvements in jaw function and movement, and corresponding changes in plasma cytokine levels and pressure pain threshold. We hypothesize that PBM will improve TMD-related pain and function, thereby offering a non-invasive treatment alternative that avoids the negative systemic side effects associated with conventional medications such as opioids and NSAIDs [3,4].

To our knowledge, this is the first research study to employ a rigorously placebo-controlled multimodal PBM approach to address chronic TMD pain, combining different light therapy techniques to specifically target musculoskeletal and inflammatory factors. This protocol will employ a double-blind, placebo-controlled design to enhance rigor and reproducibility. By investigating whether changes in inflammation and pain sensitivity correlate with clinical pain reduction, this study will offer insights into the mechanisms whereby PBM exerts its therapeutic effects.

Recent systematic reviews have reinforced the potential of PBM in TMD management [27,28]. Carvalho et al. conducted a randomized, triple-blinded, placebo-controlled clinical trial involving 145 participants, demonstrating that PBM significantly reduced orofacial and cervical skull pain and increased maximum mouth opening immediately after treatment [29]. Furthermore, the recent systematic review by Díaz et al. [28] supported the efficacy of PBM in reducing TMD pain and improving jaw function across diverse patient populations and study protocols, further supporting the rationale for this study [30]. Collectively, these findings highlight PBM's efficacy as a conservative, non-invasive therapy for TMD, offering a favorable safety profile compared to pharmacologic treatments that often carry systemic side effects or other non-pharmacological therapies such as occlusal splints that offer limited short-term efficacy [[29], [30], [31]].

Photobiomodulation (PBM) has been shown to alleviate pain and inflammation through several synergistic mechanisms, including improved cellular metabolism and local blood flow and reduced oxidative stress [19]. PBM further modulates immune responses by shifting macrophage polarization from the pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype to the anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype [[14], [15], [16]]. Additionally, PBM downregulates COX-2 and prostaglandin E2 (PGE2), which are key components of the inflammatory cascade [19]. PBM also stabilizes nerve membranes, enhances endogenous opioid release, and improves lymphatic drainage. In patients with painful temporomandibular disorders (TMD), these mechanisms have been shown to significantly reduce pain and improve function, as confirmed in recent clinical trials and systematic reviews [[26], [27], [28],[32], [33], [34], [35]].

Building on promising evidence of PBM's efficacy, this study will evaluate its immediate and long-term efficacy in managing chronic TMD pain and related symptoms. By tracking outcomes at multiple intervals following treatment, we aim to provide a comprehensive understanding of PBM's sustained benefits and its potential as a safe, effective, and non-invasive therapy for chronic painful TMD.
