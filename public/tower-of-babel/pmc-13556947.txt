Associations of herpes zoster disease perceptions, altruism and family harmony with herpes zoster vaccination uptake among older adults in Shenzhen, China
Authors: He Cao, Liwen Ding, Hongbiao Chen, Kechun Zhang, Yuan Fang, Lizhen Liu, Weijun Peng, Shaojian Xu, Zixin Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTHerpes zoster (HZ) poses a substantial health burden among older adults, yet HZ vaccination coverage remains low in mainland China. This study examined the associations between HZ vaccination uptake and factors at both individual and interpersonal levels. This was a cross-sectional survey conducted in Shenzhen, China, between August and October 2024. A total of 1,556 Shenzhen residents aged ≥60 y with established administrative health records were recruited through multistage random sampling. Multivariable multilevel logistic regression models were fitted (level 1: individual participants; level 2: community health centers). Among the participants, 8.0% received a HZ vaccination. After adjusting for background characteristics that were significantly associated with HZ vaccination uptake in the literature and this study, better understanding (coherence) (AOR: 1.15, 95%CI: 1.07, 1.24) was associated with higher HZ vaccination uptake. Being tired of receiving repeated vaccination (vaccination fatigue) (AOR: 0.79, 95%CI: 0.62, 0.99) was correlated with lower HZ vaccination uptake. There is a strong need to improve HZ vaccination coverage among older adults in China. These findings identified potentially modifiable psychosocial factors that warrant evaluation in prospective or intervention studies.

Background

Primary varicella zoster virus (VZV) infection, also known as chickenpox, commonly occurs in childhood.1 After primary infection, the virus remains inactive in the body. A decline in VZV-specific cell-mediated immunity, commonly caused by increasing age and immunosuppressive conditions, could lead to reactivation of the virus.2 Once reactivated, VZV can cause herpes zoster (HZ) or shingles, which is characterized by a painful, unilateral rash along dermatomes.3 One of the most common serious complications of HZ is postherpetic neuralgia (PHN), which is defined as persistent pain lasting for more than 90 d after onset of rash or after cutaneous healing.2,3 Approximately 10–20% of HZ patients develop PHN, with the risk increasing with age.4–6 In mainland China, the pooled incidence of HZ was 11.69/1000 person-years in those aged 60 or above, which was higher than in other age groups.7 Among people with HZ in mainland China, 12.6% of them developed PHN.7,8 It is apparent that HZ is a health threat for older adults both internationally and locally.

HZ vaccination has been proven to be an effective preventive strategy for reducing the incidence and severity of HZ and its complications, with strong evidence supporting its safety and efficacy.9,10 Two HZ vaccines are available for older adults, including the live attenuated zoster vaccine (Zostavax) and the recombinant zoster vaccine (Shingrix).11,12 Receiving a single dose of Zostavax or two doses of Shingrix could significantly reduce the risk of HZ and PHN, with a duration of protection up to 10 y.13,14 Therefore, health authorities in the United States started to recommend HZ vaccination to adults aged 50 y or above in 2017.15 In mainland China, Shingrix was licensed for adults aged 50 y or above in 2019, while Zostavax was approved in 2023 for adults aged over 40 y.16 At the time when this study was conducted, adults aged 50 y or above in mainland China were recommended to receive two doses of Shingrix, with an interval of 2–6 months. However, HZ vaccination was not yet covered by the Basic Health Insurance in mainland China, and the cost to receive two doses of Shingrix is CNY3,000–4,000 (US$413–551).

Previous studies observed a low coverage of HZ vaccination among Chinese older adults (10.3% in mainland China and 5.5% in Hong Kong),17,18 which was much lower than the corresponding coverage in the United States (36%).19 Among unvaccinated older adults in China, 7.7–16.6% intended to receive a self-financed HZ vaccination, whereas 37.3–72.3% were willing to receive a fully-subsidized HZ vaccination provided by the government.18,20 Some studies have explored factors associated with behavioral intention or actual uptake of HZ vaccination among Chinese older adults.17,18,20–25 However, these factors were limited to socio-demographic characteristics (e.g., age, sex assigned at birth, and socio-economic status), accessibility to HZ vaccination, and knowledge and perceptions related to HZ vaccination (e.g., perceived vaccine efficacy, concerns about side-effects and cost).17,18,20–25

This study applied a partial socioecological model by focusing on factors associated with HZ vaccination uptake among older adults at the individual and interpersonal levels.26–29 At the individual level, this study considered illness representations of HZ, vaccine fatigue, and attitudes toward aging. Illness representations refer to how individuals think about a disease, and such concept has been increasing used to explain health behaviors including vaccination uptake.30 A health threat (e.g., HZ infection) would trigger the cognitive representation regulating the objective threat and the emotional representation regulating emotions. Illness representations are modifiable through health promotion, and they may be related to coping strategies to deal with the threat (e.g., adopt a health behavior).31 Previous studies found that illness representations were significantly correlated with uptake of mpox, pneumococcal, coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19), and human papillomavirus (HPV) vaccination.32–34 Vaccine fatigue refers to people’s inaction toward instructions or recommendations for vaccination due to perceived burden and burnout.35 The COVID-19 pandemic has aggravated vaccine fatigue mainly due to the rapidly changing guidelines and the need for repeated vaccination.36 Previous studies reported that 54.3% of Chinese older adults had vaccine fatigue, which was associated with lower motivation to receive COVID-19 vaccination booster doses and seasonal influenza vaccination.37 Attitudes toward aging, individual perceptions and feelings about the aging process, also merit attention.38 Older adults face not only biological declines but also psychosocial challenges such as age-related discrimination.39 In this context, their personal attitudes toward aging could influence both physical and mental health.40–42 Positive attitudes toward aging are associated with preventive health behaviors, including a balanced diet, physical activity, active intention to seek health care, and high medication adherence.43–45 However, little is known about whether attitudes toward aging would be associated with HZ vaccination uptake among Chinese older adults.

At the interpersonal level, we considered altruism and family harmony. An altruistic behavior is defined as a behavior that is motivated by a desire to benefit someone other than oneself.46 A potentially useful way to reduce vaccine hesitancy is to trigger altruism and altruistic behaviors.47 Relatively few studies examined the association between altruistic factors and vaccination uptake. One survey showed that altruism could influence the decision to receive a seasonal influenza vaccination.48 Another national survey among older adults in Italy suggested that altruism was associated with greater trust of the national vaccination plan and higher uptake rates of seasonal influenza and COVID-19 vaccination.49 Furthermore, a behavioral experiment showed that providing vaccination messages targeting altruism could increase vaccination intention.47 Recent data showed that most older adults in China were living with other family members, such as their spouse, children and grandchildren.50,51 It is possible that a desire to benefit other family members may be associated with them to receive a HZ vaccination. Family harmony, a core value in Chinese traditional culture, emphasizes peaceful, cooperative, and supportive family relationships. Numerous studies in China have shown that family harmony was negatively associated with the occurrence of health compromising behaviors (e.g., smoking and drinking among youth).52,53 Some studies suggested that in cultures valuing family harmony, older adults may emphasize the contribution of vaccinations to family health.54,55 However, no study has tested the associations of altruism and family harmony with HZ vaccination uptake.

To address these gaps, this study examined the HZ vaccination uptake among adults aged 60 and above in Shenzhen, China. This study also investigated associations between HZ vaccination uptake and factors at both individual (illness representations, vaccine fatigue and attitudes toward aging) and interpersonal levels (altruism and family harmony) under the socioecological model. We hypothesize these individual and interpersonal variables are associated with HZ vaccination uptake in this age group.

Materials and methods

Study design

Between August and October 2024, two surveys using identical sampling strategies were conducted among parents of children aged 0–15 y and people aged 60 y or above, respectively. Details of these surveys were reported in published studies.56–58 This study was based on the aforementioned survey targeting people aged 60 y or above. At the time when this study was conducted, HZ vaccination was available for people aged 60 y or above. HZ vaccination was not covered by the Basic Medical Insurance in China. The cost to receive two doses of HZ vaccine was between CNY3,000 (US$413) and CNY4,000 (US$551). Shenzhen, the study site, is one of the most developed cities and a special economic zone in mainland China. The population size of the city was 17 million in 2021.

Participants and data collection

The eligibility criteria of the participants in the original survey were reported in a published study.58 To be specific, participants were Shenzhen residents aged 60 y or above with established administrative health records in community health centers. These records are routinely established for Chinese residents who have lived in the same locality for more than six months. Information related to sociodemographic characteristics, children development indicators, pregnancy and childbirth (for women only), chronic disease management and vaccination history is included in the administrative health records.

Details of the sampling method (i.e., multistage random sampling) used in this study were reported in published studies.56–58 First, 10 community health centers were randomly selected from all 926 centers in Shenzhen in the first stage. Second, 200 residents aged 60 y or older were randomly sampled from the administrative health records maintained by each selected center. Prospective participants were contacted by center staff via telephone at different timeslots during weekdays and weekends. The staff briefed prospective participants about the study and invited them to complete a face-to-face interview at the community health center. In the community health centers, participants provided written informed consent, and completed a 30-minute interview in either Mandarin or Cantonese. After completion of the interview, each participant received a CNY25 (US$3.5) cash coupon as a token of appreciation. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Longhua Center for Disease Control and Prevention (reference: 2024008).

Sample size planning

The sample size planning of the original survey was also reported in a published study.58 The target sample size was 1,500. Based on the HZ vaccination uptake rate reported by a previous study, we assumed that the HZ vaccination uptake rate was about 10% among our participants. To illustrate the statistical power, if 5% to 8% of the participants without a facilitating condition (the reference group) had received a HZ vaccination, the sample size for this secondary analysis (1,500) was sufficient to identify an odds ratio as small as 1.62 when comparing participants with and without a facilitating condition, with a power of 0.80 and an alpha of 0.05 (PASS 11.0, NCSS, LLC).

Measurements

Development of the questionnaire

We reported the details of questionnaire development in a published study.58 The questionnaire was developed by a panel of experts specialized in public health, vaccination behaviors and health psychology, workers of Centers for Disease Control and Prevention and older adults. We purposively recruited 10 residents aged 60–84 y to test the readability and length of the questionnaire. The pilot testing results suggested that the questions were easy to understand, and the duration of interview was acceptable. The panel finalized the questionnaire based on minor suggestions made by the participants.

Background characteristics and HZ vaccination uptake

The interviews collected participants’ socio-demographic characteristics (e.g., age, sex assigned at birth, education level, marital status, employment status, income, and living arrangement), lifestyle (smoking and binge drinking in the past year), presence of chronic conditions, and history of confirmed severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus-2 (SARS-CoV-2) infection and HZ. Participants were asked whether they had received a HZ vaccination during their lifetime. In addition, seasonal influenza vaccination uptake in the 2023/24 season and the number of doses of COVID-19 vaccination they had received were collected.

Independent variables at the individual level: illness representations, vaccine fatigue, and attitudes toward aging

We followed the approaches used in previous studies and used the Brief Illness Perception Questionnaire (B-IPQ) to ask the participants to imagine a hypothetical scenario of contracting HZ.59–62 The term “disease” was specified as “HZ,” and open-ended questions were excluded.32 The B-IPQ used in our study consisted of eight items assessed on a scale of 0 to 10, including: (1) consequences (“How much does HZ affect your life?”), (2) timeline (“How long do you think your HZ will continue?”), (3) personal control (“How much control do you feel you have over your HZ?”), (4) treatment control (“How much do you think your treatment can help your HZ?”), (5) identity (“How much do you experience symptoms from your HZ?”), (6) concern (“How concerned are you about your HZ?”), (7) coherence (“How well do you feel you understand your HZ?”), and (8) emotions (“How much does your HZ affect you emotionally? e.g., does it make you angry, scared, upset or depressed?”).63 Higher scores for consequences, timeline, identity, concern, and emotions represent more negative illness perceptions, while higher scores for personal control, treatment control, and coherence indicate more positive perceptions. Vaccine fatigue was measured by a validated question: “You are tired of receiving repeated vaccination” (response categories: 1 = disagree, 2 = neutral, 3 = agree), with higher scores indicating greater vaccine fatigue.37 Attitudes toward aging were assessed using the Physical Change Subscale of the validated Chinese version of the 24-item Attitudes to Aging Questionnaire (AAQ).64 The Physical Change Subscale has 8 items (example items include “Growing old is easier than I thought”). Each item is rated on a 5-point Likert scale (from 1 = strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree). Higher scores on this subscale reflect more positive attitudes toward aging. The subscale demonstrated high internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.77 in this study) and validity across diverse older populations.

Independent variables at the interpersonal level: altruistic attitudes and family harmony

Altruism was measured using the validated Chinese version of the Elder Care Research Center Altruism Scale (five items, Cronbach’s alpha of 0.69 in this study).65 Family harmony was measured using the validated Chinese version of the 5-item Family Harmony Scale (FHS).66 The FHS measures five key aspects of family harmony, including effective communication, conflict resolution, mutual tolerance, family identity, and quality time spent with family. Each item was rated on a 5-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 = strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree. The FHS has shown good internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.96 in this study) and robust psychometric properties, making it appropriate for use in large-scale epidemiological studies. Higher scores of these scales indicated greater altruism and better family harmony.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize all variables included in the study. The reliability of the scales was examined using Cronbach’s alpha. HZ vaccination uptake was analyzed as the dependent variable. We applied multilevel logistic regression models (level 1: individual participants; level 2: community health centers) to analyze factors associated with the dependent variable. Random intercept models were used to allow the intercept of the regression model to vary across community health centers, which could account for intra-correlated nested data. Multilevel logistic regression models are commonly used in studies with similar sampling methods.67–69 A summary multivariable multilevel logistic regression model (level 1: individual participants; level 2: community health centers) that considered all individual and interpersonal-level variables as candidates was then fitted, after adjusting for background characteristics that were that were associated with HZ vaccination uptake among people in China17,18,20,21 and significant variables in Table 3 (i.e., age group, sex assigned at birth, income and education levels, seasonal influenza vaccination uptake in the 2023/24 flu season, living with spouse, and history of herpes zoster). Results were expressed as crude odds ratios (ORs), adjusted odds ratios (AORs) and their 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Subgroup analysis were conducted to investigate associations between B-IPQ items and HZ vaccination uptake among participants with and without a history of HZ infection. We performed all statistical analyses with R version 4.2.3 and considered two-sided p values < 0.05 as statistically significant.

Results

Background characteristics

The research team approached 2,000 people aged 60 y or above during the study period. Among them, 74 had migrated to other cities at the time of this study, 370 refused to participate in the study due to lack of time and other tangible reasons, and 1,556 completed the face-to-face interviews. The majority of these participants were aged 60–70 y (76.4%), female (61.4%), married (90.1%), and had a monthly income less than CNY5,000 (US$690) (55.4%). Only 15.8% of them attended college or university. About 10% of the participants reported smoking (9.7%) or binge drinking (9.1%) in the last year. About two-thirds of the participants had at least one chronic condition (65.8%), and 70.2% and 11.1% reported a history of confirmed SARS-CoV-2 and HZ infection, respectively. Subgroup analysis showed that more concerns (concern) (AOR: 1.08, 95%CI: 1.01, 1.16, p = .01) and better understanding (coherence) (AOR: 1.15, 95%CI: 1.08, 1.23, p < .001) were associated with higher HZ vaccination uptake among participants without a history of HZ. However, the associations between all BIPQ domains and HZ vaccination uptake were not statistically significant among people with a history of HZ (Table S2 of Appendix 1).

Discussion

To our knowledge, this study is one of the first studies to investigate the associations of HZ disease perceptions, vaccine fatigue, attitudes toward aging, altruism and family harmony with HZ vaccination uptake among older adults. It extended the application of the socio-ecological model. The findings provided an updated estimate of HZ vaccination coverage among older adults and generated some hypotheses for potential intervention strategies that warrant evaluation in future studies. Another strength of this study was a relatively large study sample recruited through multistage random sampling.

Only 8% of the older adults had ever received a HZ vaccination. Such coverage was comparable to other Chinese studies (10.3% in mainland China and 5.5% in Hong Kong),17,18 but was much lower than that among older adults in the United States (36%).19 Mathematical modeling suggested that achieving a HZ vaccination coverage of at least 40% in adults aged 50 y or above could considerably reduce public health burdens caused by HZ in this age group.70 Given the very low coverage, the current vaccine delivery model is less likely to create a significant public health benefit. Further studies are warranted to identify and evaluate strategies for improving HZ vaccination coverage.

History of HZ was associated with higher HZ vaccination uptake. Previous experience of HZ were associated with greater perceived threat of HZ, which could explain greater motivation to receive vaccination to prevent HZ recurrence.71 In line with previous findings, receiving a seasonal influenza vaccination was associated with higher HZ vaccination uptake.18 Future studies may test whether promoting and implementing HZ vaccination together with seasonal influenza vaccination would be useful to increase HZ vaccination uptake among older adults. Higher monthly income level was correlated with higher HZ vaccination uptake. Given the high cost of HZ vaccination in China, it is more affordable for older adults with a higher income level. The majority of the participants were living with their spouses. Such a living arrangement was negatively correlated with HZ vaccination uptake. Previous studies suggested that if one spouse had concerns about the vaccination or vaccine hesitancy, it might influence the other’s decision to take up vaccines.72

Our findings identified potentially modifiable psychosocial factors that warrant evaluation in prospective or intervention studies. Similar to previous findings, older adults who had a better understanding of HZ (coherence) were more likely to report HZ vaccination uptake.17 Previous studies showed that better knowledge of an infectious disease was correlated with higher motivation to receive vaccination. Future prospective or intervention studies may test whether enhancing knowledge is helpful to increase HZ vaccination uptake. About one third of our participants agreed that they were tired of receiving repeated vaccination. Such perception was associated with lower uptake of HZ vaccination. During the COVID-19 pandemic, older adults in China were recommended to receive repeated COVID-19 vaccination (e.g., primary series, first and second booster dose). Such repeated vaccination has been reported to be associated with vaccine fatigue and vaccine hesitancy.73 Future prospective or intervention studies may test whether making a clarification that the purpose of HZ vaccination is different from COVID-19 vaccination (e.g., against different pathogens) is helpful to promote HZ vaccination uptake.

Subgroup analysis showed that people with a history of HZ infection had higher levels of personal and treatment control, concern, and coherence compared with those without such history. It is possible that people with such history of infection answered these questions based on their actual experience. Concerns and coherence were correlated with higher HZ vaccination uptake in those without a history of HZ infection. None of the BIPQ domains was significantly associated with HZ vaccination uptake among people with a history of HZ infection. However, given the small sample size of the subgroup with a history of infection (n = 173), we were not able to draw a conclusion whether the association between illness representation and HZ vaccination uptake was different between older adults with and without a history of HZ infection.

This study had several limitations. First, this study included older adults with and without a history of HZ infection. Although HZ can recur, participants with a history of HZ infection may have different illness perceptions compared to those without such history. In this study, we measured illness perceptions by asking the participants to imagine a hypothetical scenario of contracting HZ. It was possible that people with a history of HZ infection answered these questions based on their actual experiences. Second, the sample size of participants with a history of HZ infection was small. We could not draw a conclusion whether the associations between B-IPQ items and HZ vaccination uptake were different between participants with and without a history of HZ infection. Third, HZ vaccination uptake was self-reported. Recall bias may exist. History of other vaccination uptake (e.g., seasonal influenza and COVID-19 vaccination) was entirely self-reported without validation. It was also possible that participants would over-report HZ vaccination and other vaccination uptake due to social desirability. Fourth, using a case-control study design by recruiting older adults based on their HZ vaccination uptake would be better to investigate factors associated with HZ vaccination uptake. Fifth, this study focused on community-dwelling older adults, and did not include older inpatients or residents of residential care homes. Therefore, the findings may not be generalized to all older adults in Shenzhen. Sixth, we did not collect information from older adults who refused to join the study. It is possible that participants and refusals would have different characteristics. Self-selection bias may exist. Seventh, this study only applied a partial socioecological model instead of the full model. Future studies may explore the associations between factors at organizational, community and policy levels and HZ vaccination uptake. Moreover, due to differences in healthcare system, vaccination delivery models, health literacy and cultural factors, the findings might not be applicable to other cities in China. Shenzhen is one of the most developed cities in mainland China. It is expected the performance of its healthcare system and residents’ health literacy would be higher than less developed regions of China. Therefore, more older residents in Shenzhen might intend to receive an HZ vaccination compared with their counterparts living in less developed regions. Furthermore, causality could not be established as this was a cross-sectional survey.

Conclusion

Given the very low coverage, there is a strong need to improve HZ vaccination uptake among older adults in China. Disease perceptions related to HZ and vaccine fatigue were associated with HZ vaccination uptake. These findings identified potentially modifiable psychosocial factors that warrant evaluation in prospective or intervention studies.
