Full-endoscopic decompression for thoracic ossification of the ligamentum flavum in professional pitchers: surgical technique, outcomes, and return to sport. Illustrative cases
Authors: Seung Hoon Lee, Seung Jin Choi, Christoph P. Hofstetter, Phil Bohlen, Roshini Ramkumar, Joonho Byun, Chang Hwa Ham, Eun-Sang Kim, Joo Han Kim, Woo-Keun Kwon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUNDThoracic ossification of the ligamentum flavum (T-OLF) is an important cause of progressive thoracic myelopathy and may be particularly debilitating in professional baseball pitchers, in whom surgical treatment must restore neural function while preserving the spinal biomechanics required for elite pitching. Although open laminectomy has traditionally been the standard treatment, extensive muscle dissection and disruption of posterior elements may compromise postoperative stability and return to play.OBSERVATIONSThe authors applied a refined full-endoscopic interlaminar decompression technique for single-level and multilevel T-OLF in professional pitchers. The key surgical strategy involved thinning the ossified lesion to a paper-thin shell and elevating it around a lateral hinge point, thereby minimizing direct vertical compression on the vulnerable thoracic spinal cord. When transient intraoperative neurophysiological monitoring deterioration occurred, staged decompression was performed to reduce neurological risk.LESSONSBoth patients showed marked neurological recovery and returned to professional pitching within 6 months, without evidence of biomechanical compromise. These findings suggest that full-endoscopic decompression, by preserving the posterior tension band and paraspinal musculature, may represent a viable primary surgical option for T-OLF in high-demand athletes requiring preservation of spinal mobility and kinetic chain function. https://thejns.org/doi/10.3171/CASE26537

Thoracic ossification of the ligamentum flavum (T-OLF) is recognized as a primary etiology of progressive thoracic myelopathy, a condition characterized by chronic spinal cord compression that often leads to irreversible neurological deficits if left untreated.1,2 Because of the unique anatomical constraints of the thoracic spine, including a relatively narrow spinal canal and a precarious watershed blood supply, even minor space-occupying lesions can result in significant clinical symptoms.3,4

Traditionally, open decompressive laminectomy, with or without concomitant instrumented fusion, has served as the gold standard for management. However, these conventional approaches necessitate extensive dissection of the paraspinal musculature and the removal of critical posterior bony elements, such as the spinous processes and laminae.5 Such structural disruption frequently leads to postlaminectomy instability, chronic axial back pain, and potential kyphotic deformity, all of which contribute to a protracted and often incomplete recovery.4 While supplemental instrumented fixation and fusion can mitigate potential instability, these procedures inherently restrict the range of motion and flexibility of the thoracic spine.

In high-performance individuals, particularly professional baseball pitchers, the requirements for surgical success extend beyond simple neurological decompression to the preservation of complex biomechanical functions. The thoracic spine serves as a vital link in the “kinetic chain,” facilitating the extreme axial rotation and high-velocity deceleration necessary for elite-level pitching.6 Any compromise in the structural integrity of the posterior tension band or atrophy of the multifidus muscles can disrupt this chain, leading to decreased performance or compensatory injuries in the shoulder and elbow. Recently, full-endoscopic spine surgery (FESS) has emerged as a transformative, minimally invasive alternative. By utilizing a targeted interlaminar approach, FESS minimizes iatrogenic tissue trauma and preserves the midline structures while ensuring definitive decompression of the neural elements.7–10 In this report, we describe a refined ­full-endoscopic interlaminar technique, focusing on the “hinge technique” and its successful clinical application in professional athletes.

Illustrative Cases

Case 1

A 31-year-old professional baseball pitcher presented with a 6-month history of bilateral sole paresthesia (leg pain visual analog scale [VAS] score 7) and intermittent lower extremity weakness. Physical examination revealed subjective weakness and equivocal deep tendon reflexes. The patient was a high-performance athlete.

MRI and CT confirmed T-OLF at the T10–11 level (Fig. 1). Although traditional laminectomy with instrumentation was considered for stabilizing the thoracolumbar junction, the patient strongly requested a minimally invasive approach to preserve muscle function for his career. Given the risk of myelopathy progression, FESS was planned. The ossification of the ligamentum flavum (OLF) mass was successfully removed in an en bloc fashion without dural injury. Postoperative CT and MRI confirmed the successful removal of the OLF with full ­decompression of the spinal cord (Fig. 2). By postoperative day (POD) 1, sensory and motor functions were fully restored. The 6-day hospitalization was not due to a procedure-related complication; the patient remained neurologically stable postoperatively and was discharged on POD 6 according to his preference and discharge planning. By 3 months postoperatively, he returned to 70% intensity running with a leg pain VAS score of 2. At 6 months, he returned to full competition (leg pain VAS score 3, Oswestry Disability Index [ODI] score 4), with minor fluctuations in soreness attributed to increased training intensity. Case 2

A 31-year-old professional baseball pitcher presented with a 2-week history of numbness in the soles and big toes. Despite a perfect modified Japanese Orthopaedic Association (mJOA) score (17/17) at rest, symptoms were notably exacerbated during the high-torque pitching motion. The paresthesia initially began on the left side and progressed bilaterally.

CT and MRI revealed severe spinal cord compression due to multilevel OLF at T9–10 and T10–11 of two segments (Fig. 3). Preoperative CT was carefully reviewed for radiological findings suggestive of dural involvement, including the tram-track sign, comma sign, banner cloud sign, and double-layer sign.11,12 No definite CT evidence of dural ossification was identified. Given the multilevel involvement (T9–10, T10–11) and the extreme severity of ossification with suspected dural adhesions, we planned a staged surgery in two separate steps, addressing each level one by one. This strategy was specifically chosen to prioritize the neurological safety of the professional athlete and to minimize the cumulative surgical stress on the thoracic spinal cord. By dividing the procedure, we could maintain precise focus on the delicate dissection required for each highly adherent OLF mass while monitoring for any transient neurophysiological changes.

In the first session, the OLF of T9–10 level was found to be extremely mature and firmly fused with the superior articular process (SAP) of T10. During decompression, immediately after cutting the SAP of T10, the intraoperative neurophysiological monitoring (IONM) team reported a significant decrease in left lower extremity motor evoked potential (MEP) amplitudes. No concomitant somatosensory evoked potential (SSEP) deterioration or abnormal spontaneous electromyographic activity was observed. Surgical manipulation was temporarily paused, and the operative field was reassessed. The MEP amplitudes gradually recovered, with complete recovery in the abductor hallucis channel and partial recovery in the flexor hallucis longus and vastus lateralis channels. No further IONM deterioration was observed through wound closure. Severe epidural bleeding at the laminectomy site was controlled with meticulous hemostasis using multiple hemostatic agents, and a drain was placed. The second session was conducted 3 days later, targeting the T10–11 level via a right-sided approach. The interval was selected pragmatically rather than according to a fixed protocol. Before surgery, we considered completing both levels in a single session if the first-level decompression proceeded safely and efficiently. However, because the T9–10 decompression alone required approximately 3 hours and was accompanied by a transient MEP warning, we elected not to proceed immediately to the second level. The day after the first-stage procedure was dedicated to postoperative recovery and neurological observation, and an additional day was allotted to confirm clinical stability before the second-stage decompression. Similar to the previous level, the OLF exhibited severe dural adhesion and was fused with the T11 SAP. Leveraging the hinge technique and careful 1-mm Kerrison punching, we successfully removed the second OLF mass. Throughout this session, IONM signals remained stable, and complete bilateral decompression was confirmed through the unilateral corridor. Also, postoperative CT and MRI confirmed successful removal of both T9–10 and T10–11 OLFs, resulting in very good spinal cord decompression. No new postoperative neurological deficit occurred after the IONM event (Fig. 3).

At the 1-month follow-up, the patient reported significant improvement in the preexisting pedal numbness, with only minor residual symptoms at the distal toes. The calf and thigh paresthesia were successfully resolved. At the 3-month follow-up, he reported significant improvement (ODI score 1), despite mild reactive soreness (leg pain VAS score 3) during his rehabilitation pitching program, and at 6 months after surgery, he was pitching in elite-level competition (Table 1). Surgical Technique

The surgical procedure is performed with the patient under general anesthesia, positioned prone on a Wilson frame. The spine is maintained in a flexed position to naturally widen the interlaminar window and facilitate a more direct corridor to the thoracic spinal canal. To mitigate the risk of catastrophic spinal cord injury in the narrow thoracic critical zone, continuous IONM, including MEPs and SSEPs, is mandatory to assess real-time cord function throughout the decompression process.5,13

Under the guidance of anteroposterior and lateral C-arm fluoroscopy, the target interlaminar level is identified. A 1-cm skin incision is made, and a working tube for the endoscope is precisely docked onto the inferior margin of the cranial lamina. We utilize a 15° endoscope (iLESSYS Delta, joimax GmbH) featuring a large 6.0-mm working channel and a 10-mm outer diameter; this specialized equipment provides the necessary maneuverability and high-definition visualization required for the delicate task of thoracic decompression.

Following the initial approach, meticulous soft tissue dissection is performed using bipolar radiofrequency cautery, endoscopic punches, and graspers to clearly expose the thoracic lamina and the junctional boundaries of the OLF. A high-speed diamond burr is then introduced to perform a precise laminotomy. The critical surgical sequence begins with thinning the lamina cortex from the cranial to the caudal side. The burr is used to create a paper-thin bony margin at the attachment sites of the OLF. Once the bone is sufficiently thinned, a fine hook is used to perform meticulous dissection, confirming the separation between the ossified mass and the underlying epidural space.

The contralateral margin of the OLF is similarly thinned and then cut from the contralateral medical facet. During this process, the ipsilateral margin—typically fused with the SAP—is also thinned as much as possible using the diamond burr, but it is intentionally left intact to serve as a strategic hinge. This hinge technique is the cornerstone of the procedure’s safety profile. Once the three margins (cranial, caudal, and contralateral) are completely freed, a curette or a dedicated lifting instrument is inserted under the already cut contralateral edge. The OLF mass is then gently elevated toward the lateral hinge until the thin bony bridge fractures, allowing the entire ossified complex to be mobilized away from the spinal cord. This pivot-based elevation prevents the application of direct vertical or downward pressure on the fragile thoracic cord.

After the OLF is mobilized, a hook is used to thoroughly detach any remaining adhesions between the ventral surface of the OLF and the underlying dura mater. This process is significantly facilitated by the hydrostatic “liquid dissection” effect of the continuous irrigation fluid, which helps create a safe cleavage plane even in the presence of firm adhesions. Finally, the residual ossified fragments are removed with pituitary forceps. The completion of decompression is confirmed by observing the restoration of rhythmic dural pulsations and the absence of any residual compression under high-magnification endoscopic visualization (Fig. 4). Meticulous hemostasis is maintained throughout the procedure to ensure a clear operative field, and the wound is closed with standard suturing or topical skin adhesives (Video 1). Discussion

The management of T-OLF in young professional athletes presents a unique and formidable clinical challenge. In this specific population, the surgical objective must extend far beyond simple neurological recovery to encompass complete biomechanical restoration of the spinal column.14,15 To our knowledge, this is the first report specifically focusing on full-endoscopic decompression for T-OLF in competitive professional elite athletes. The novelty of these cases lies in the application of a muscle-preserving and posterior tension band–­sparing decompression strategy in professional baseball pitchers. As highlighted in our case illustrations, a high-performance pitcher requires a decompression strategy that addresses severe cord compression without the morbidity associated with spinal fusion or extensive muscle stripping. The full-endoscopic interlaminar approach satisfies these rigorous requirements by providing a targeted, ultra-minimally invasive corridor that reaches the pathology with surgical precision.10 In particular, the hinge technique described in our technical note is vital for ensuring cord safety; by meticulously freeing three margins and fracturing the OLF toward a lateral pivot, we significantly mitigate the risk of iatrogenic cord injury, a concern that is paramount within the narrow and precarious thoracic critical zone.

From the perspective of pitching biomechanics, maintaining both thoracic mobility and structural stability is essential for elite performance. As analyzed by Thompson et al.,6 efficient power generation in professional pitchers relies heavily on the kinetic chain and the segmental separation between the pelvis and the trunk, often referred to as the “X-factor” stretch. During the late cocking and acceleration phases of pitching, the thoracic spine is subjected to extreme axial rotation and repetitive tensile stress.14–16 A notable observation in our case series is the potential correlation between the repetitive, high-torque rotational forces of pitching and the specific localization of the OLF. In case 2, the multilevel involvement (T9–10, T10–11) showed a pattern in which the ossification appeared on opposite sides or adjacent segments, potentially reflecting the asymmetric stress distribution during the pitching motion. Interestingly, even in case 1, where only a single level was symptomatic and surgically addressed, early-stage OLF was identified on the contralateral side of the adjacent segment.

This leads to a compelling hypothesis: the extreme, unidirectional rotational torque inherent in professional pitching may serve as a chronic mechanical stimulus that accelerates ligamentous ossification. We speculate that the focal stress concentrated at the thoracolumbar junction during the late cocking and acceleration phases may predispose these elite athletes to segment-specific OLF. While this unidirectional rotation theory requires further validation through larger pitcher cohort studies and biomechanical modeling, it underscores the necessity of early screening and proactive management in this high-risk group.

Our report highlights that FESS is an ideal solution for maintaining the structural integrity of the kinetic chain. For this population, preserving the posterior tension band and midline structures is not merely an advantage but a prerequisite for maintaining the X-factor stretch, which is the vital segmental separation between the pelvis and trunk necessary for generating pitching velocity. By avoiding disruption of the posterior tension band, we allow athletes to return to their premorbid pitching mechanics. Future research focusing on longitudinal data from professional pitcher cohorts will be essential to definitively establish the causal link between specific pitching mechanics and the development of T-OLF.

Furthermore, the management of multilevel T-OLF with extreme ossification, as demonstrated in our second case, underscores the necessity of a meticulous, staged surgical protocol. Although preoperative CT did not show definite signs of dural ossification, dense dural adhesion was encountered intraoperatively. This distinction is important because the absence of radiological dural ossification does not exclude firm adhesion between mature OLF and the dura. Therefore, the ossified mass should be thinned and mobilized carefully rather than forcibly detached.

The thoracic spinal cord is notoriously vulnerable due to its watershed blood supply and the limited epidural space available for manipulation.7,9 A significant IONM warning during endoscopic thoracic decompression should prompt a stepwise response. In our practice, this includes immediate cessation of mechanical manipulation, withdrawal of instruments from the spinal canal, reduction of irrigation pressure, reassessment of anesthetic and hemodynamic factors when available, and observation for signal recovery. If signals fail to recover, are accompanied by SSEP deterioration or abnormal electromyography activity, or continue to worsen, the procedure should be aborted, staged, or converted to open decompression depending on the degree of residual compression, surgical visibility, and neurological risk.

By electing a staged approach, addressing complex multilevel pathologies in separate sessions, we successfully mitigated the cumulative risks of spinal cord edema and reperfusion injury that can occur during prolonged thoracic maneuvers. The use of a full-endoscopic system provides an additional safety layer through its hydrostatic properties; the continuous saline irrigation creates a liquid interface that assists in the blunt dissection of firm dural adhesions, which are common in mature OLF lesions.17

This report has several limitations. First, it includes only 2 cases, and the findings cannot be generalized to all patients with T-OLF or to all elite athletes. Second, the follow-up duration, particularly the 6-month follow-up in one case, is insufficient to confirm durable neurological recovery, sustained return to sport, or absence of OLF progression. Professional pitchers return to repetitive asymmetric thoracic rotation, extension, and tensile loading after surgery; therefore, residual or adjacent-level OLF may progress even after successful decompression of the symptomatic level.18 Third, although preservation of the posterior musculature and tension band is biomechanically appealing in professional pitchers, this advantage was inferred from the surgical approach and sport-specific functional demands rather than directly measured using postoperative muscle imaging, strength testing, or motion analysis.

Overall, this FESS protocol, enhanced by high-definition visualization and a tiered surgical plan, provides a robust framework for tackling the most challenging T-OLF pathologies. For the elite athletic population, in whom even a minor permanent neurological deficit or a decrease in spinal flexibility would be career ending, the combination of definitive decompression and maximal structural preservation offered by the endoscopic approach should be considered the preferred surgical standard. Future long-term studies will be beneficial to further validate the durability of these clinical outcomes in high-impact professional sports.

Observations

In these 2 professional baseball pitchers, full-endoscopic interlaminar decompression achieved adequate decompression of symptomatic T-OLF while preserving the posterior musculo-ligamentous structures. Both patients showed neurological improvement and returned to professional pitching within the available follow-up. In the multilevel case, the staged approach was selected after prolonged first-level decompression and a transient MEP warning, and no new postoperative neurological deficit occurred.

Lessons

Full-endoscopic interlaminar decompression utilizing the hinge technique offers definitive spinal cord decompression while preserving the posterior musculo-ligamentous structures essential for elite athletic performance. In professional pitchers with T-OLF, this approach enabled complete neurological recovery and successful return to competition without sacrificing thoracic mobility. For young, high-demand athletes in whom biomechanical integrity is as critical as neurological outcome, FESS should be considered the preferred surgical standard. Larger prospective studies are warranted to validate these findings and to elucidate the potential role of repetitive rotational stress in the pathogenesis of T-OLF in this population.
