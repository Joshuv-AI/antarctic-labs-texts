Electronic energy levels and optical spectra of trivalent lanthanide ions in water – Ce(III), Pr(III), Nd(III), Sm(III), Eu(III), Gd(III), Tb(III), Dy(III), Ho(III), Er(III), Tm(III), and Yb(III)
Authors: Villads R. M. Nielsen, Thomas Just Sørensen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Chemistry is—in essence—our collective understanding and language describing the electronic structure of each element in the periodic table. For the f-block ions, Dieke diagrams have formed the basis when describing electronic structure. Dieke reported numerical values of the electronic energy levels in his book from 1968, and Carnall reported multiplet energies for the lanthanide(III) ions in water in a series of papers from the same year. The experimental methods have improved since, and in this work, we present a new Dieke diagram for the trivalent lanthanide ions in water. With optical spectroscopy, in particular luminescence spectroscopy at cryogenic temperatures, the electronic energy levels are determined using fitting, and several levels reported by Dieke and Carnall are found to be artifacts. Further, the new methods allow both determination of speciation and description of electronics states and lines. This is in contrast to the bands and multiplets considered by Carnall, who was restricted to multiplets by using room temperature absorption spectroscopy. To conclude, the ground state manifold of each luminescent lanthanide is described, and a new benchmark for lanthanide(III) electronic structure is now available for use across the fields of chemistry.

Introduction

Since the early reports on the electronic structure of trivalent lanthanide ions—Ln(III) ions—by among others Reed, Dieke, and Carnall1–5, these have found applications in lighting6–8, as amplifiers in optical telecommunication9, in magnets for wind turbines and electric motors10, as MRI contrast agents11,12, and in highly sensitive bioassays13,14. Recently the unique magnetic and optical properties Ln(III) ions have been explored in scintillators15–17, as model compounds for actinides8,18, for fluorescence guided surgery and in radiotherapy19,20, for optical thermometers21,22, in circular polarized luminescence23–25, and as photoredox catalysis26–29. These many applications of lanthanide ions are built through our understanding of their distinct electronic energy levels, that is, the unique electronic structures found in the lanthanide series with the 4fN and 4fN-15d1 electron configurations30.

Considering Ln(III) ions in materials and in solution, the 4fN electron configurations are unique, as the valence electrons are shielded by xenon core electrons, which result in almost pure atomic states, with only small contributions from bonding and small perturbations from the surrounding crystal field. These properties are unique to the Ln(III) ions, and we have been refining the experimental tools available to interrogate the electronic structure of these ions over the last decade31–36. The result is this report of the experimentally determined electronic energy levels of the Ce(III), Pr(III), Nd(III), Sm(III), Eu(III), Gd(III), Tb(III), Dy(III), Ho(III), Er(III), Tm(III), and Yb(III) ions in deuterated water.

The electronic energy levels of a Ln(III) ion are grouped in multiplets which are observed as bands in the optical spectra of Ln(III) ions. For the multiplets we adopt the Russel-Saunders term symbols (2S+1)LJ as names. The individual electronic states of the Ln(III) ions may be observed as individual lines in some experiments1,36–39, but are most often reported as multiplets2,40. As free ions, the electronic structure of Ln(III) ions are described by the multiplets that consist of degenerate electronic states. In condensed phases, the crystal field makes electronic states non-degenerate for ions with integer J and Kramers doublets for ions with ½-interger J1,4,30. In 1963, Dieke reported the full collection of observed energy levels of the Ln(III) ions doped at 1% in LaCl35. They were observing bands and assigned each multiplet to a Russel-Saunders term symbol2. The assignment of the experimentally observed multiplets was a result of manually counting of lines and bands in the crystalline samples available, the compounds are listed below41. The result of the counting was summarized in a diagram, which we now call the Dieke diagram. The assignment in the Dieke diagram, and the energies reported by Carnall for multiplets, has been the golden standard when it comes to electronic structure in the f-block2. This is to an extent where multiplets are referred to as main ‘levels’ and where the actual electronic states have been demoted to ‘sublevels’42–44. Note, Dieke and Reed were using the correct nomenclature for lines, transitions, and describing the individual electronic states in their seminal work1,2,4,45,46.

The Dieke diagram has since then been extended47–49, and based on computational results the 4fN multiplets have been extended all the way up to 200,000 cm–150. However, at higher energy (≥20,000 cm–1) the Russel-Saunders coupling scheme becomes poor due to J-J mixing2,51, and the mixed configurations, such as 4fN5d1 and more diffuse charge transfer states, become important at high energy (≥35,000–100,000 cm–1)52,53. Thus, we need to update the model and experimental data, the latter we provide here.

The barycenters of the multiplets in a Ln(III) ion—in a 4fN configuration—are reported to be similar in solution, complexes, and solids. Thus, the Dieke diagram from the 1960s has been used as a reference for the energy and nature of the Ln(III) multiplets across chemistry, physics, and materials science1–5,54–59. The experiments are difficult and reports of high-quality optical spectra of Ln(III) ions are rare30,60–62, particularly in water where the luminescence is quenched63,64. Most common are spectra of Eu(III) and Tb(III) ions, which are the most emissive Ln(III) ions, as explained by the energy gap law32,33,65. Here, we report all the spectra in deuterated water and have included all experimental data as Supporting Information. We report the spectra in deuterated water to increase the quantum yield and the spectral resolution. The absorption and luminescence spectra of the Ln(III) ions ion deuterated water were analyzed and based on this analysis a new revised Dieke diagram of the [Ln(III)(D2O)n]3+ ions was created, see Fig. 1. With modern equipment and methodology, we resolve speciation and report high resolution spectra that allows us to assign both the barycenters of multiplets and—where data allows—the energy of the actual electronic states. The results are described in detail for each Ln(III) ion, where thermal population of states is also considered. Results

The samples of Ln(III) ions in deuterated water were prepared by dissolving the corresponding triflate salts (Ln(OTf)3) to obtain a concentration of 50 mM with 100 mM DClO4. The acidity provided by the perchloric acid is needed to ensure that the pure aqua ions are studied, the low pH precludes the formation of hydroxides69. Both triflate and perchlorate have previously been shown not to bind to Ln(III) ions at significantly higher concentrations than used here. Thus, the Ln(III) ions in these samples are the deuterated aqua ions [Ln(D2O)8/9]3+35,70. The spectral shapes of the deuterated aqua ions and the non-deuterated aqua ions are generally accepted to be identical, despite the change in lifetime and the resulting emission intensity. Finding and fitting 4fN → 4fN and inter-configurational transitions

The energy levels were extracted by fitting the optical spectra. The spectra recorded at 77 K were used to remove thermally excited states and reduce thermal broadening. At higher energies the 77 K excitation spectra are not available and absorbance were used. Above 30,000 cm–1 intense and broad absorption bands are observed for most of the Ln(III) aqua ions, often with less intense broad bands on the red side of these bands. Figure 3a shows these bands for Dy(III). The broad absorbance begins at ~35,000 cm–1 with a peak center at 42,400 cm–1, while the strong bands occur at >47,000 cm–1. Similar plots for the entire series can be found in Figs. S1, S2. The intense transitions shown in green have properties that match those characteristics of the inter-configurational 4fN → 4fN-15d1 transitions52,53. The broad transitions shown in light green are significantly weaker and are at lower energies. These cannot be confidently assigned as 4fN → 4fN-15d1 transitions nor can they be classified as a 4fN → 4fN transitions, but broad, weak bands are often seen for charge transfer transitions. As this remains to be verified, we simply classify them as inter-configurational transitions. To assign the 4fN → 4fN transitions and extract the energy levels, the inter-configurational transitions are accounted for through quadratic splined background subtraction of the absorption data, see in the supporting information Section S2 for details. Figure 3b shows the fitting of some 4fN → 4fN transitions of the emission spectrum in Dy(III). Prior to fitting, the emission and excitation spectra have been corrected as discussed in the supporting information Section S5–S6. The fits are performed using a sum of line functions taking the number of possible levels in the terms into account and considering the thermal populations as specified in the methods section, see Section S6. For Dy(III) three bands are observed in the 20,700 cm–1 band at RT, while only two band are observed at 77 K. Similarly, four and three bands are observed at 17,300 cm–1 at RT and 77 K respectively. The additional intensity at the high energy shoulders is assigned to arise from thermally excited levels within the emissive multiplet of Dy(III). Thus, the 77 K data can be fitted with Voigt functions and the electronic energy levels determined. Details of all fits can be found in Figs. On speciation

When we report on the energy diagram of the trivalent lanthanides in deuterated water, it must be declared that the molecular structure is not isotropic throughout the series. Several studies agree that the light Ln(III) ions are 9-coordinated and the heavy Ln(III) ions are 8-coordinated36,68,71–74. However, when the coordination number change from 9 to 8 is not known and the presence of more than one species is possible in the middle of the series. Indeed, we found that Eu(III), Gd(III), and Tb(III) display concentration dependent emission spectra, which indicate the presence of multiple emissive species in solution. The full speciation analysis is provided and discussed in the supporting information Section S5. No spectral variance was identified for the other Ln(III) ions.

While results would indicate that the shift in coordination number could occur between Eu(III) and Tb(III)71–74, the effect is concentration dependent which is not obvious for a change in coordination number. In the following we must conclude that the data for Eu(III), Gd(III), and Tb(III) likely arise from more than a single species, while the data from rest of the series is from either [Ln(D2O)9]3+ or [Ln(D2O)8]3+. We note that the differences are small and we do not consider them further in this work, however, when detailed studies of lanthanide complexes are done in solution their concentration dependent speciation must be documented.

As some lanthanide aqua ions have concentration dependent spectra, a single concentration was chosen to limit the parameter space. While Eu(III) and Tb(III) can be measured at 1 mM, the other lanthanides(III) required a sample concentration of 50 mM Ln(III). The exception was Ce(III), which displays a significantly higher molar absorptivity, and had to be diluted to 0.25 mM to ensure that the absorbance was below 0.1 across the spectrum.

Cerium(III)

Ce(III) is a 4f1 system with 14 electronic states in 7 Kramers doublets. The spectra of the Ce(III) ion in deuterated water are shown in Fig. 3c. The emission and excitation spectra measured at 77 K are fitted with a sum of Gaussian functions and the results are provided in Table S1. Details are provided in the supporting information Sections S6.1 and S7.1.

Absorbtion arise from the 2F5/2 multiplet that has 3 Kramers doublets.

Emission is observed from a configuration that is assumed to be a 5d1 configuration. The lowest energy level of this configuration was determined to be at 32,750 cm–1. The assignment is based on the observations that (1) the extinction coefficient is two orders of magnitude larger than typical f-f transitions, (2) the width of the peaks are one to two orders of magnitude larger, and (3) the Stokes’ shift was found to both be large and vary with temperature. They are determined to be 2900 cm–1 at RT and 4700 cm–1 at 77 K.

In our data, the 2 multiplets, 2F5/2 and 2F7/2 containing 14 electronic states in 7 Kramers doublets can be accounted for and assigned. However, the individual Kramers doublets within 2F5/2 and 2F7/2 cannot be resolved. Two bands at higher energy are observed in the excitation spectrum at 77 K, while three are observed at RT. Many additional bands are observed in the absorbance spectrum. The nature of these is unknown.

Absorption arises from the 7F0 multiplet with 1 electronic state. The next multiplet 7F1 is populated with less than 1 % of the 7F0 population below 100 K (−173 °C). However, at RT, the three states in 7F1 are populated corresponding to 34% of the 7F0 population. This results in a significant room temperature absorbance from the 7F1 states as shown in Fig. 5b. Furthermore, the 7F2 multiplet is populated more than 2 % of the 7F0 population at RT and some lines from this multiplet is also observed. Note that the paramagnetic properties of Eu(III) arise from the thermally populated 7F1 and 7F2 multiplets.

Emission is observed from the 5D0 multiplet at 17,270 cm–1 with 1 electronic state. The next multiplet 5D1 is populated with less than 1 % of the 5D0 populations below 560 K (287 °C). While luminescence is well known to occur from 5D1, and even 5D2, this can be due to relatively slow non-radiative relaxation pathways from 5D1 and not a thermal equilibrium75. In a system such as water or even deuterated water with efficient relaxation pathways emission from 5D1 is therefore not expected and indeed not observed in any substantial amount63,64, see Fig. 5b.

In our data 18 multiplets containing 135 electronic states were accounted for and assigned. In the excitation spectra all lines are narrow and represent f-f transitions in the entire range from 0 to 40,000 cm–1. In the absorption spectrum the f-f transitions are convoluted with bands with broad features above 33,000 cm–1. The broad bands are assumed to arise from a inter-configuration transitions. These do not lead to emission from the 5D0 term. Comparing our results to those reported by Carnall55, we find that the barycenter of all bands that we can assign match well. However, Carnall assigns many more term symbols than we find in our data. Consulting Fig. 5b, it is apparent that the peaks Carnall include in his list are only found in the room temperature data. Thus, Carnall’s list include bands that arise from the thermally excited 7F1 and 7F2 multiplets. Most notably are the 7F1 → 5G6 and 7F2 → 5G6 absorption bands at 26,620 and 26,300 cm–1. These Carnall miss-assigned as 7F0 → 5G4 and 7F0 → 5G2. This becomes obvious in the 77 K excitation spectra, where no bands are found in this range.

Discussion

The revised Dieke diagram of the Ln(III) ions in deuterated water

Figure 1 shows the experimentally determined Dieke diagram for the Ln(III) ions in water from 0 to 42,000 cm–1, and the electronic energy levels are compiled in Tables S1–S12. Some levels have been removed from the diagram, and the precision has been markedly increased. An important distinction has been made since the reports by Dieke and Carnall1–5. Today we have fully realized that the Russel-Saunders terms describe multiplets of states30, and that we must consider the individual electronic states to describe the properties and electronic structure of a Ln(III) ion. Describing the terms is not enough, and the data herein describes the actual electronic states/Kramers levels.

The lowest energy multiplets for the Ln(III) ions in deuterated water are shown in Fig. 9b. These multiplets include the ground state. Multiplets from 0 to 600 cm–1 are included in Fig. 9b, as states within this energy range will be populated with 5% relative to the ground state population at room temperature. For Eu(III) there are two multiplets within this energy range. For all other Ln(III) ions there is only a single multiplet. The width of the luminescence band that corresponds to each level is shown with a red box, and the resolved lines are shown as a black horizontal lines. For Nd(III), Pr(III), Sm(III), Eu(III), Gd(III) and Yb(III) it was possible to assign all electronic states within this range. For Tb(III), Dy(III), Ho(III), Er(III), and Tm(III) significant crystal field splitting was observed, however the lowest energy terms contain too many electronic states for these to be confidently assigned at 77 K. For Ce(III) it was not possible to describe the lowest energy levels, as the line width is significantly broadened by the 5d1 character of the emissive state.

In the revised Dieke diagram (Fig. 1), the assignment of term symbols for the energy levels throughout the Ln(III) series is based on the results of Carnall54–57. In the work by Carnall the determined barycenters of the absorption bands of each Ln(III) were fitted to semi-empirical models within the Russel-Saunders approximation. These models include the calculation of all term energies, many of which are not observed experimentally. This was noted by the original authors1–5, and we find that this approach has provided results that generally align with our observations at lower energies (below ~20,000 cm–1). Thus, in this range we have used the Russel-Saunders term symbols to name the multiplets in the revised Dieke diagram. However, the term symbols should be considered as names only at higher energies (above ~20,000 cm–1), where this method proves less accurate. Theoretically, the Russel Saunders term symbols become an increasingly poor descriptor at higher energies. Wybourne reported calculations based on the intermediate coupling scheme on the free Sm(III) and Dy(III) ions to determine the validity of the Russel Saunders term symbols when describing multiplets in the Ln(III) electronic energy manifold51,76. For Sm(III), all low energy multiplets described by the 6HJ terms (all below 5000 cm–1) have more than 95% 6H character, while the emissive multiplet described by the 4G5/2 term symbol (at 18,000 cm–1) only has 48% 4G character. For, Dy(III) the multiplets described with 6HJ terms (all below 11,000 cm–1) have more than 90% 6H character, while the emissive multiplet described with the 4F9/2 term symbol (at 21,000 cm–1) only has 62 % 4F character. Finally, the significantly higher energy Dy(III) multiplet described by the 4G5/2 term symbol (reported at 31,000 cm–1 for the free ion), only has 38% 4G character51,76.

Experimentally, the Russel-Saunders multiplets are often too close in energy to differentiate whether multiple observed levels are actual different terms or if they are simply the same term split by the crystal field splitting. For example, the Nd(III) absorption band between 21,000 and 22,400 cm–1 according to Carnall contains 4 Russel-Saunders multiplets: 2K15/2 at 21,000 cm–1, 2G9/2 at 21,300 cm–1, (2D, 2P)3/2 at 21,300 cm–1, and 4G11/2 and at 21,650 cm–1. This results in a group of states with 21 Kramers levels within 650 cm–1. In contrast, the 5 Kramers levels in the lowest energy multiplet is split by 400 cm–1. A complete experimental determination of these 21 Kramers levels will be very difficult to achieve.

Combining the onset of state-mixing and the experimental difficulties it becomes apparent that one should be careful to assign Russel Saunders term symbols at high energies. Below approximately 20,000 cm–1, however, the Russel-Saunders approximation describes the electron states of the Ln(III) ions well. Above 20,000 cm–1 the 4f states begin to mix, and somewhere between 30,000 cm–1 and 50,000 cm–1 mixed-configuration 5d4f states become dominating. These states manifest as broad and strong absorption bands in the spectra, resembling those observed in Ce(III). Although several of these bands have been previously assigned as 4fN → 4fN transitions by Carnall1–5, The excitation spectra reveal that they do not lead to 4f–4f emission. This behavior was confirmed for Sm(III), Eu(III), Tb(III), and Dy(III). The bands were also observed in Nd(III), Ho(III), Er(III), Tm(III), and Yb(III), but here the excitation spectra could not be recorded due to technical restrictions. The 5d14fN-1 states have been well characterized in Ln:LiYF₄ (Ln = Ce, Pr, Nd),⁵² where they exhibit crystal field dependencies similar to d-block elements. In LiYF₄, the onset of 4fN-15d1 mixed-configuration states occurs at 32,500 cm⁻¹ for Ce(III) and 47,500 cm⁻¹ for Nd(III). In the absorbance spectra of Ln(III) ions in deuterated water, we distinguish two types of inter-configurational transitions experimentally. Strong broad bands at high energies, which we assign to 4fN → 5d14fN-1 transitions, and weak and very broad bands at slightly lower energies, which we refer to simply as inter-configurational transitions. The less intense transitions are clearly observed for Pr(III), Tb(III), Dy(III), and Yb(III). It may be argued that these can also be observed for Nd(III), Sm(III), Ho(III), Er(III), and Tm(III), however, here they are too weak and convoluted with 4fN → 4fN transitions to be assigned. The nature of these less intense transitions between 30,000 and 45,000 cm⁻¹ remains speculative, however the width could indicate a charge transfer transition. In Fig. 10, we present an updated Dieke diagram that include the onset of mixed-configuration states. The determinations of the onsets are shown in Figs. In summary, by using modern instrumentation we have revised the Dieke diagram for Ln(III) ions dissolved in deuterated water. We provide an updated list of energies for the multiplets described by Russel-Saunders term symbols across the series of naturally occurring Ln(III) ions. In addition, we resolve and report the energy of some electronic states, thus demonstrating the importance of considering electronic states rather than multiplets in lanthanide coordination chemistry and lanthanide luminescence.

We revised the Dieke diagram in two ways. First, we determined the barycenters at a higher precision than what was previously reported. This is a direct consequence of resolving the energy with 77 K using emission and excitation spectra. This is most notable for Nd(III), Tb(III), Dy(III), Ho(III), and Tm(III), which have large crystal field splittings in the lowest energy multiplet. The new barycenter results from a correction that removes the thermally populated levels. Thus, the low temperature data shifts the band positions to higher energies than what is observed at room temperature. Second, we remove lines from thermally populated higher energy multiplets. In Eu(III), the multiplets described by the 7F1 and 7F2 term symbols are populated to a degree high enough for absorption bands to appear at room temperature. Carnall has several of these bands in his list of Russel-Saunders term energies for Eu(III).

In conclusion, we have determined the electronic structure of the Ln(III) ions in deuterated water. We have removed errors, increased precision, and moved to a description of states rather than multiplets.

Methods

Preparation of samples

For the lanthanide(III) ions, the dry triflate salt (Ln(CF3SO3)3 or Ln(OTf)3, 98%, Strem Chemicals) was dissolved in 3 mL of D2O (Eurisotop, 99.90%D) with 100 mM of DClO4. A mass of Ln(OTf)3 was added to obtain a solution with a concentration of 50 mM. For Gd(OTf)3 this was 90.7 ± 0.4 mg in 3 mL D2O. Each sample was sonicated at 100 % at 60 °C for 60 min prior to measurements with an Emmi-30HC EMAG technologies sonicator.

Optical spectroscopy

All room temperature experiments were performed on the solutions transferred to 10 mm quartz cuvettes from Starna Scientific (23/Q/10). All 77 K experiments were performed on snap frozen solutions in 7″ NMR tubes from Bruker (Inside diameter = 4.21 mm, wall thickness 0.38 mm). The solutions where snap frozen by dipping the tubes quickly in liquid nitrogen, where they were kept for the entire measurement time.

Absorption spectra

Absorption spectra were recorded on a Cary 5000 double-beam absorption spectrometer from Agilent Technologies. The measurements were done from 1800 to 200 nm with a step size of 1 nm and an integration time of 0.5 s and a slit size of 2.5 nm. Each measurement took 14 min. For each measurement, the instrument zero/baseline was determined using D2O with 100 mM of DClO4. The absorbance data is corrected with two correction procedures. (1) Vibrational overtones from H2O impurities are significant above 1200 nm and were corrected by background subtraction of linearly scaled measurements of the Gd(III) sample, which has no electronic transitions in this range. (2) The entire range was manually background subtracted using a cubic interpolation of selected background points. The raw data and the two background subtraction procedures are shown in Fig. S2.

Emission spectra

Three different detector setups were used to capture the entire emission range from 300 to 1700 nm. Emission below 500 nm is recorded on a PTI QuantaMaster 8075 instrument from Horiba Scientific using a xenon lamp for excitation. Unless specified otherwise, the slit size corresponds to a spectral resolution of 0.5 nm. In this range the emission intensity was corrected for wavelength-dependent detector sensitivity using a factory provided correction file. Emission above 500 nm is recorded on a custom build spectrometer77, where the samples are excited by a supercontinuum laser (NKT SuperK Fianium FIU-15) that is coupled to a tunable band-pass filter (NKT LLTF Contrast VIS/SWIR HP8). In the range between 500 and 950 nm a 750B grating and a PyLoN detector were used for measurements, and a 1200B grating and the NIRvana detector were used for measurements above 950 nm. In both ranges a default slit size of 10 µm was used resulting in a spectral resolution of 0.4 nm. In many cases, however, the brightness was too low above 950 nm. In these cases, a spectral resolution of 4 nm was used. This is explicitly described where relevant. In these two ranges the emission intensity was corrected for wavelength-dependent detector sensitivity using a NIST traceable lamp (StellarNet Inc. SL1-CAL halogen lamp) measured for each range. In cases where multiple detector setups were needed to collect the full emission range, the spectra were stitched together by normalization to the area of a band measured in both ranges. Additional discussion and details for recording and corrections of emission spectra are provided in the supporting information, see supporting information Section S3.

Excitation spectra

Two different excitation sources and detector setups were used to capture the entire excitation range. Excitation below 500 nm is recorded on the PTI QuantaMaster 8075 instrument from Horiba Scientific using a xenon lamp for excitation. Unless specified otherwise, the slit size corresponds to a spectral resolution of 0.5 nm. In this range the excitation intensity was corrected by the measured flux of excitation source. Excitation above 500 nm were done on the custom build spectrometer77, where the samples are excited by a supercontinuum laser (NKT SuperK Fianium FIU-15) that is coupled to a tunable band-pass filter (NKT LLTF Contrast VIS/SWIR HP8). The spectral resolution is between 1.5 and 2 nm as determined by the width of the laser source39. In this range above 500 nm the excitation intensity is corrected by the measured flux of the excitation source. Additional discussion and details for recording and corrections of excitation spectra are provided in the supporting information, see supporting information Section S4.

Line fitting

The spectra of the Ln(III) aqua ions were fitted with functions to extract the positions of energy levels. Voigt functions were used to fit all spectra as this function is found to encapsulate various origins of line broadening of Ln(III) aqua ions in solution. Each set of spectra was fitted using a sum of line functions selected through manual inspection of the spectra, taking into account thermal contributions from excited states. The line shapes used were either Voigt or Gaussian functions, with the choice of function manually determined for each set of spectra. The analytical fitting procedure is similar to what was reported in earlier work34,39. The emission and excitation spectra at 77 K were used as the primary source of data to fit, but in some ranges this data is not possible to obtain and in these cases the absorbance spectra at RT were used instead. Thermally excited states are typically not significant at 77 K, but in some cases they are. In these cases, the fit models are expanded to include thermal contributions by fitting each band with an additional set of lines with a fixed energy difference defined by the energy difference to the thermally excited levels.

Inter-configuration transitions have not been assigned and fitted with the same rigor as was possible for 4fN → 4fN transitions. Measurements in water or deuterated water does not allow for the complete capture of such transitions and the assignment of mixed configuration states was done manually. The 4fN-15d1 states are assigned to begin at the point the absorbance is above 0.1. The onset of mixed-configuration with lower absorbance intensity at lower energy is determined from manual inspection of the onset. Both onsets are arbitrarily chosen and should therefore be considered only as rough estimates.

Details for each individual Ln(III) ions are provided in the supporting information Sections S6 and S7.
