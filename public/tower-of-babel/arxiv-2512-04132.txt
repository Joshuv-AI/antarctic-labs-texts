A Fresh Look at Bivariate Binomial Distributions Journal: Electronic Notes in Theoretical Informatics and Computer Science Volume: 5 Email: bart@cs.ru.nl
Bart Jacobs myemail

Abstract
Binomial distributions capture the probabilities of ‘heads’ outcomes when a (biased) coin is tossed multiple times. The coin may be identified with a distribution on the two-element set { 0 , 1 } \{0,1\} , where the 1 1 outcome corresponds to ‘head’. One can also toss two separate coins, with different biases, in parallel and record the outcomes. This paper investigates a slightly different ‘bivariate’ binomial distribution, where the two coins are dependent (also called: entangled, or entwined): the two-coin is a distribution on the product { 0 , 1 } × { 0 , 1 } \{0,1\}\times\{0,1\} . This bivariate binomial exists in the literature, with complicated formulations. Here we use the language of category theory to give a new succint formulation. This paper investigates, also in categorically inspired form, basic properties of these bivariate distributions, including their mean, variance and covariance, and their behaviour under convolution and under updating, in Laplace’s rule of succession. Furthermore, it is shown how Expectation Maximisation works for these bivariate binomials, so that mixtures of bivariate binomials can be recognised in data. This paper concentrates on the bivariate case, but the binomial distributions may be generalised to the multivariate case, with multiple dimensions, in a straightforward manner.



keywords

Probability theory, bivariate binomial distribution, convolution
Laplace’s rule of Succession, Expectation Maximisation

1
Introduction

A very basic physical model of a random phenomenon is the tossing (or
‘flipping’ or ‘throwing’) of a coin. The possible outcomes are ‘head’
and ‘tail’, or ‘
1
’ and ‘
0
’ in this paper. The coin may have a bias

r\in[0,1]
, which we incorporate in a ‘flip’ distribution on the set
of outcomes
\{0,1\}
. Using ‘ket’ notation
|{\kern 1.00006pt}-{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle
it is written
as:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\mathsl{flip}(r)&=&r\big|{\kern 1.00006pt}1{\kern 1.00006pt}\big\rangle+(1-r)\big|{\kern 1.00006pt}0{\kern 1.00006pt}\big\rangle.\end{array}

(1)

This says that the probability of outcome
1
is
r
and the
probability of outcome
0
is
1-r
.

Figure 1:
Plots of an ordinary binomial distribution (20 tosses with
bias
\frac{7}{10}
) on the left, and a bivariate binomial
distribution on the right, with 10 tosses of a two-coin

\frac{3}{8}\big|{\kern 1.00006pt}0,0{\kern 1.00006pt}\big\rangle+\frac{5}{12}\big|{\kern 1.00006pt}0,1{\kern 1.00006pt}\big\rangle+\frac{1}{12}\big|{\kern 1.00006pt}1,0{\kern 1.00006pt}\big\rangle+\frac{1}{8}\big|{\kern 1.00006pt}1,1{\kern 1.00006pt}\big\rangle
.

Repeating such coin flipping
K\in\mathbb{N}
times, still with a bias
r\in[0,1]
, yields the binomial distribution on the set
\{0,1,\ldots,K\}

where a number
n
in the set gets the probability that
n
out of
these
K
flips are head. Again using ket notation this
binomial distribution is written as:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\mathsl{bn}[K](r)&=&\displaystyle\sum_{0\leq n\leq K}\,\binom{K}{n}\cdot r^{n}\cdot(1-r)^{K-n}\,\big|{\kern 1.00006pt}n{\kern 1.00006pt}\big\rangle.\end{array}

(2)

The probabilities add up to one by the Binomial Theorem. On
the left in Figure
1
one sees the plot of such a
binomial distribution with
K=20
tosses of a coin with bias
r=\frac{7}{10}
. Via the bias this typical (discrete) bell shape can be
shifted to the left or right.

On the right in Figure
1
we see a plot of a bivariate
binomial distribution. It has a bell shape in two dimensions. It does
not arise from a single coin distribution
\mathsl{flip}(r)
on
\{0,1\}
but
from what we call a two-coin distribution on

\{0,1\}\times\{0,1\}
. Such a two-coin may arise as a tensor product

\mathsl{flip}(r)\otimes\mathsl{flip}(s)
of two separate coins, but in general it is
more than such a product. It may incorporate the important feature of
‘joint’ distributions on product spaces, namely that the whole
distribution does not coincide with the product of its marginals. In
that case the joint distribution is called dependent, entangled, or
entwined.

A possible physical model of such a two-coin distribution on

\{0,1\}\times\{0,1\}=\{(0,0),(0,1),(1,0),(1,1)\}
is given by a
tetrahedron, as on the side, also known as a triangular pyramid. Its
four faces can be labeled
00
,
01
,
10
and
11
. Such a
tetrahedron can be tossed, where the outcome is the label on the face
on which the tetrahedron lands. One could imagine that the weight
distribution inside the tetrahedron is such that the outcomes are
determined by a general two-coin distribution:

r_{1}\big|{\kern 1.00006pt}0,0{\kern 1.00006pt}\big\rangle+r_{2}\big|{\kern 1.00006pt}0,1{\kern 1.00006pt}\big\rangle+r_{3}\big|{\kern 1.00006pt}1,0{\kern 1.00006pt}\big\rangle+r_{4}\big|{\kern 1.00006pt}1,1{\kern 1.00006pt}\big\rangle\qquad\mbox{where}\qquad r_{1}+r_{2}+r_{3}+r_{4}=1.

This paper formulates this bivariate binomial distribution, for such a
two-coin, using some basic category theory, namely the functoriality
of sending a set
X
to the set
\mathcal{D}(X)
of distributions on
X
. For
a number
K\in\mathbb{N}
of tosses, it produces a distribution on the
product set
\{0,1,\ldots,K\}\times\{0,1,\ldots,K\}
— as on the
right in Figure
1
where
K=10
. This bivariate
binomial is introduced as a (functorial) pushforward of a multinomial
distribution along a ‘marginal heads’ function, see
Section
3
for the mathematical
details. Intuitively, one throws the tetrahedron
K
-many times and
one separately counts the number of
1
’s in the first
a
and in the
second
b
position in the label
ab
written on the faces that the
tetrahedron lands on (in
K
-many tosses). We focus on a

2
-dimensional extension of the binomial distribution. One can also
extend to
N
-dimensional form, via a ‘hedron’ object with
2^{N}
sides
and labels from
\{0,1\}^{N}
. It gives a distribution on

\{0,1,\ldots,K\}^{N}
when one counts the
1
’s in each of the
N

positions
1,\ldots,N
of the (down-facing) labels, after multiple
tosses.

The (ordinary) binomial distribution (
2
) can be seen
as discrete version of the widely used Gaussian (normal) distribution.
Gaussians often occur in
N
-dimensional form. The
N
-dimensional
discrete version, however, is less familiar. In Section
6
we
show how to recognise (mixtures of) bivariate binomials in (multisets
of) data items. This technique may have practical value and could
increase the popularity of bivariate binomial distributions.

This paper starts by describing the relevant background information,
on multisets and distributions, tensor products and convolutions of
distributions, channels and their inversions, and on binomial and
multinomial distributions. Section
3
then
introduces bivariate binomial distributions, via functoriality, and
establishes some basic properties with respect to marginalisation and
also closure under convolution. Subsequently, Section
4

determines the mean, variance and covariance of bivariate
binomials. It turns out that they can be expressed, respectively, as

K
times the mean, variance and covariance of the underlying two-coin
distribution, where
K
is the number of tosses.
Section
5
briefly discusses Laplace’s rule of
succession, which is in generally used to describe the expected
outcome after an update. Here it is formulated in general terms, as a
mean of a dagger (channel), and more specifically for bivariate
distributions, with Dirichlet and Poisson distributions as
priors. Section
6
gives an illustration of how bivariate
binomials can be recognised in suitable data multisets.

2
Preliminaries

2.1
Multisets, distributions and random variables

A
multiset
is like a subset, except that its elements may occur
multiple times. Alternatively, a multiset is like a list in which the
order of the elements does not matter. For instance, an urn filled
with three red, two green and five blue balls forms a multiset. We
write it using ket notation as
3|{\kern 1.00006pt}R{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle+2|{\kern 1.00006pt}G{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle+5|{\kern 1.00006pt}B{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle
, where
the ket brackets
|{\kern 1.00006pt}-{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle
serve to separate the elements
R,G,B
for
the three colours, from their multiplicities
3,2,5
. Also a draw of
multiple balls from such an urn forms a multiset. In general, multiple
data items, as used for learning (see Section
6
) are
naturally described as a multiset.

For an arbitrary set
X
we shall write
\mathcal{M}(X)
for the set of
(finite) multisets with elements from
X
. A multiset

\varphi\in\mathcal{M}(X)
may be described in two equivalent ways, namely
via kets as a formal sum
\varphi=n_{1}|{\kern 1.00006pt}x_{1}{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle+\cdots+n_{K}|{\kern 1.00006pt}x_{K}{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle=\sum_{i}n_{i}|{\kern 1.00006pt}x_{i}{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle
, with elements
x_{i}\in X
and associated multiplicities
n_{i}\in\mathbb{N}
. Alternatively, we may
describe a multiset
\varphi\in\mathcal{M}(X)
as a function
\varphi\colon X\rightarrow\mathbb{N}
with finite support, where
\mathrm{supp}(\varphi)=\{x\in X\;|\;\varphi(x)\neq 0\}
. We can combine these notations and
write
\varphi=\sum_{x}\varphi(x)|{\kern 1.00006pt}x{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle
, where the summands with
multiplicity zero are omitted.

The size
\|\varphi\|\in\mathbb{N}
of a multiset
\varphi\in\mathcal{M}(X)
is the
total number of its elements, including multiplicities. Thus,

\|\varphi\|=\sum_{x}\varphi(x)
. The abovementioned urn
\upsilon=|{\kern 1.00006pt}R{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle+2|{\kern 1.00006pt}G{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle+5|{\kern 1.00006pt}B{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle
has size
\|\upsilon\|=10
. There
is one multiset in
\mathcal{M}(X)
of size zero, namely the empty multiset,
written as
\mathbf{0}\in\mathcal{M}(X)
. As a function, it satisfies
\mathbf{0}(x)=0
, for each
x\in X
, giving
x
multiplicity
0
. We write

\mathcal{M}[K](X)\subseteq\mathcal{M}(X)
for the subset of multisets of
size
K\in\mathbb{N}
.

The mapping
X\mapsto\mathcal{M}(X)
forms a monad on the category of
sets. We only use functoriality: a function
f\colon X\rightarrow Y
yields another function
\mathcal{M}(f)\colon\mathcal{M}(X)\rightarrow\mathcal{M}(Y)

via the following two equivalent definitions, in ket form on the left,
and in function form on the right.

\begin{array}[]{rclcrcl}\mathcal{M}(f)\Big(\sum_{i}n_{i}\big|{\kern 1.00006pt}x_{i}{\kern 1.00006pt}\big\rangle\Big)&=&\sum_{i}n_{i}\big|{\kern 1.00006pt}f(x_{i}){\kern 1.00006pt}\big\rangle&&\mathcal{M}(f)(\varphi)(y)&=&\displaystyle\sum_{x\in f^{-1}(y)}\,\varphi(x).\end{array}

(3)

We shall especially use functoriality for a projection
function
\pi_{1}\colon X\times Y\rightarrow X
. Then

\mathcal{M}(\pi_{1})\colon\mathcal{M}(X\times Y)\rightarrow\mathcal{M}(X)
performs
marginalisation.

One more observation is that
\mathcal{M}(X)
is the free commutative monoid
on the set
X
. The monoid structure is given by pointwise addition of
multisets:
\big(\varphi+\psi\big)(x)=\varphi(x)+\psi(x)
, with
the everywhere zero multset
\mathbf{0}\in\mathcal{M}(X)
as neutral element.
Functions
\mathcal{M}(f)
preserve this monoid structure.

We turn to distributions: a (finite discrete probability) distribution

r_{1}|{\kern 1.00006pt}x_{1}{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle+\cdots+r_{K}|{\kern 1.00006pt}x_{K}{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle
is written as a multiset,
except that the multiplicities
r_{i}
are now probabilities from the
unit interval
[0,1]
that add up to one:
\sum_{i}r_{i}=1
. We
shall write
\mathcal{D}(X)
for the set of distributions with elements from

X
. A distribution
\omega\in\mathcal{D}(X)
may be written in ket form, as
we just did, but also as a function
\omega\colon X\rightarrow[0,1]
with finite support
\mathrm{supp}(\omega)=\{x\in X\;|\;\omega(x)\neq 0\}
and with
\sum_{x}\omega(x)=1
. When
X
is a finite
set, we say that
\omega\in\mathcal{D}(X)
has full support when

\mathrm{supp}(\omega)=X
, that is, when
\omega(x)>0
for each
x\in X
. The operation
\mathcal{D}
is also a monad on the category of
sets. Functoriality works as for
\mathcal{M}
, in (
3
), in
particular with projections
\mathcal{D}(\pi_{i})
for marginalisation.

Each non-empty multiset
\varphi\in\mathcal{M}(X)
can be turned into a
distribution
\mathsl{Flrn}(\varphi)\in\mathcal{D}(X)
, where
\mathsl{Flrn}
stands for
frequentist learning. This involves learning by counting:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\mathsl{Flrn}(\varphi)&\coloneqq&\displaystyle\sum_{x\in\mathrm{supp}(\varphi)}\,\frac{\varphi(x)}{\|\varphi\|}\,\big|{\kern 1.00006pt}x{\kern 1.00006pt}\big\rangle.\end{array}

(4)

For instance, for the above urn
\upsilon=3|{\kern 1.00006pt}R{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle+2|{\kern 1.00006pt}G{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle+5|{\kern 1.00006pt}B{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle
we get
\mathsl{Flrn}(\upsilon)=\frac{3}{10}|{\kern 1.00006pt}R{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle+\frac{1}{5}|{\kern 1.00006pt}G{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle+\frac{1}{2}|{\kern 1.00006pt}B{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle
. It captures the
probabilities of drawing a ball of a particular colour from the urn.

A random variable is a pair
(\omega,p)
where
\omega\in\mathcal{D}(X)
is
a distribution and
p\colon X\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
an ‘observable’. The
validity (or expected value) is:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\omega\models p&\,\coloneqq&\displaystyle\sum_{x\in X}\,\omega(x)\cdot p(x).\end{array}

(5)

2.2
Products and convolutions of distributions

For two distributions
\omega\in\mathcal{D}(X)
and
\rho\in\mathcal{D}(Y)
one can
form the (parallel) product distribution
\omega\otimes\rho\in\mathcal{D}(X\times Y)
, via
\big(\omega\otimes\rho\big)(x,y)=\omega(x)\cdot\rho(y)
. Marginalisation of such a product yields
the components:

\begin{array}[]{rclcrcl}\mathcal{D}(\pi_{1})\big(\omega\otimes\rho\big)&=&\omega&\qquad\mbox{and}&\mathcal{D}(\pi_{2})\big(\omega\otimes\rho\big)&=&\rho.\end{array}

In general, for a ‘joint’ distribution
\tau\in\mathcal{D}(X\times Y)
, on a product space, one does
not
have
\tau=\mathcal{D}(\pi_{1})(\tau)\otimes\mathcal{D}(\pi_{2})(\tau)
. If this equation
does hold,
\tau
is called independent, non-entangled, or
non-entwined.

We write
\boldsymbol{2}=\{0,1\}
. A distribution
\tau\in\mathcal{D}(\boldsymbol{2}\times\boldsymbol{2})
, say
\tau=r_{1}|{\kern 1.00006pt}0,0{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle+r_{2}|{\kern 1.00006pt}0,1{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle+r_{3}|{\kern 1.00006pt}1,0{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle+r_{4}|{\kern 1.00006pt}1,1{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle
with
r_{1}+r_{2}+r_{3}+r_{4}=1
, is non-entwined if and only if
r_{1}\cdot r_{4}=r_{2}\cdot r_{3}
.

Tensors of distributions are used for a special sum of distributions,
called convolution. Let
M=(M,+,0)
be a commutative monoid and let

\omega,\rho\in\mathcal{D}(M)
be two distributions on the underlying set

M
. Then we can form their convolution
\omega+\rho\in\mathcal{D}(M)
as,
via functoriality of
\mathcal{D}
, applied to the addition
+\colon M\times M\rightarrow M
, as in:

\begin{array}[]{rcccl}\omega+\rho&\coloneqq&\mathcal{D}(+)\Big(\omega\otimes\rho\Big)&=&\displaystyle\sum_{x,y\in M}\,\omega(x)\cdot\rho(y)\,\big|{\kern 1.00006pt}x+y{\kern 1.00006pt}\big\rangle.\end{array}

(6)

This turns
\mathcal{D}(M)
into a commutative monoid, with

1|{\kern 1.00006pt}0{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle\in\mathcal{D}(M)
as unit, for
0\in M
. It is not hard to see
that if
f\colon M\rightarrow M^{\prime}
is a homomorphism of monoids, then
so is
\mathcal{D}(f)\colon\mathcal{D}(M)\rightarrow\mathcal{D}(M^{\prime})
.

In the sequel we shall encounter distributions on
\{0,1\}
or more
generally on
\{0,\ldots,K\}
. By considering these sets as subsets of

\mathbb{N}
, with its additive commutative monoid structure, we can apply
the convolution definition (
6
) to such
distributions. Commutative monoids are closed under products
\times
,
so that
\mathbb{N}^{2}=\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N}
is also a commutative monoid, via
component-wise sums and zeros. Also, as we have seen, the set

\mathcal{M}(X)
of multisets over a set
X
is a commutative monoid, so that
convolution can be used for distributions on multisets. This applies
to multinomial distributions, see Subsection
2.4
.

2.4
Binomial and multinomial distributions

We have seen the binomial distribution
\mathsl{bn}[K](r)\in\mathcal{D}\big(\{0,\ldots,K\}\big)
in Equation (
2
), for

K
-many tosses of a coin with bias
r\in[0,1]
. A single coin toss
is given by the flip distribution
\mathsl{flip}(r)
in (
1
).

Lemma 2.1
.

(1)

The binomial distribution is an iterated
convolution of the flip distribution:

\begin{array}[]{rcccl}\mathsl{bn}[K](r)&=&K\cdot\mathsl{flip}(r)&=&\underbrace{\mathsl{flip}(r)+\cdots+\mathsl{flip}(r)}_{K\text{ times}}.\end{array}

Hence, binomial distributions are closed under
convolution:
\mathsl{bn}[K](r)+\mathsl{bn}[L](r)=\mathsl{bn}[K+L](r)
.

(2)

The mean and variance of the binomial
distribution are given by:

\begin{array}[]{rclcrcl}\mathrm{mean}\Big(\mathsl{bn}[K](r)\Big)&=&K\cdot r&\qquad\mbox{and}&\mathrm{var}\Big(\mathsl{bn}[K](r)\Big)&=&K\cdot(K-1)\cdot r.\end{array}

[formula]

We turn to the multinomial distribution. We can view a distribution

\omega\in\mathcal{D}(X)
as an abstract urn, where
X
is the set of colours
and
\omega(x)\in[0,1]
gives the probability of drawing a ball of
colour
x\in X
. We are interested in drawing multiple balls, in
‘multinomial mode’, with replacement. A draw of
K
-many balls from
the urn
\omega
can be identified with a
K
-sized multiset

\varphi\in\mathcal{M}[K](X)
. The multinomial distribution

\mathsl{mn}[K](\omega)\in\mathcal{D}\big(\mathcal{M}[K](X)\big)
assigns
probabilites to such draws of size
K
. It is defined as:

\begin{array}[]{rclcrcl}\mathsl{mn}[K](\omega)&\coloneqq&\displaystyle\sum_{\varphi\in\mathcal{M}[K](X)}\,({\kern 1.0pt}\varphi{\kern 1.0pt})\cdot\prod_{x\in\mathrm{supp}(\omega)}\omega(x)^{\varphi(x)}\,\big|{\kern 1.00006pt}\varphi{\kern 1.00006pt}\big\rangle&\quad\mbox{where}&({\kern 1.0pt}\varphi{\kern 1.0pt})&\coloneqq&\displaystyle\frac{\|\varphi\|!}{\prod_{x}\varphi(x)!}.\end{array}

(9)

The precise form of this multinomial distribution does not
matter so much at this stage; interested readers may
consult
[
9
,
11
]
. We make the following facts
explicit. The first two items are standard. For details about the
third item, see
e.g.

[
13
, Prop. 7.2]

or
[
14
]
.

Lemma 2.2
.

Consider the multinomial distribution (
9
)
for
\omega\in\mathcal{D}(X)
and
K\in\mathbb{N}
.

(1)

The binomial and multinomial
distribution can be identified when
X=\boldsymbol{2}=\{0,1\}
,
via the isomorphisms
\mathsl{flip}\colon[0,1]\mathrel{\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\raisebox{1.50694pt}{$\scriptstyle\cong$}}}{{\raisebox{0.0pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{$\rightarrow$}}}}}\mathcal{D}(\boldsymbol{2})
and
\{0,\ldots,K\}\mathrel{\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\raisebox{1.50694pt}{$\scriptstyle\cong$}}}{{\raisebox{0.0pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{$\rightarrow$}}}}}\mathcal{M}[K](\boldsymbol{2})
, via
n\mapsto n|{\kern 1.00006pt}1{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle+(K-n)|{\kern 1.00006pt}0{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle
, in:

\textstyle{[0,1]\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\mathsl{bn}[K]}

\sim

\textstyle{\mathcal{D}\big(\{0,\ldots,K\}\big)\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}

\sim

\textstyle{\mathcal{D}(\boldsymbol{2})\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\mathsl{mn}[K]}
\textstyle{\mathcal{D}\big(\mathcal{M}[K](\boldsymbol{2})\big)}

(2)

Multinomials are closed under
convolution:
\mathsl{mn}[K](\omega)+\mathsl{mn}[L](\omega)=\mathsl{mn}[K+L](\omega)
.

(3)

For elements
y,z\in X
with

y\neq z
,

\begin{array}[b]{rcl}\displaystyle\sum_{\varphi\in\mathcal{M}[K](X)}\,\mathsl{mn}[K](\omega)(\varphi)\cdot\varphi(y)&=&K\cdot\omega(y)\\[10.00002pt]
\displaystyle\sum_{\varphi\in\mathcal{M}[K](X)}\,\mathsl{mn}[K](\omega)(\varphi)\cdot\varphi(y)\cdot\varphi(z)&=&K\cdot(K-1)\cdot\omega(y)\cdot\omega(z).\\[10.00002pt]
\displaystyle\sum_{\varphi\in\mathcal{M}[K](X)}\,\mathsl{mn}[K](\omega)(\varphi)\cdot\varphi(y)^{2}&=&K\cdot(K-1)\cdot\omega(y)^{2}\,+\,K\cdot\omega(y).\end{array}

[formula]

3
Introducing bivariate distributions

This section introduces the main distribution of this paper, namely
the bivariate binomial distribution. It also describes some of its
most basic properties. Our definition exploits functoriality of
distributions
\mathcal{D}
, via a marginal heads function that is defined
first. This definition is compared to explicit formulations in the
literature in Remark
3.5
below.

Definition 3.1
.

Fix a number
K\in\mathbb{N}
and consider the following ‘marginal heads’
function:

\textstyle{\mathcal{M}[K]\big(\boldsymbol{2}\times\boldsymbol{2}\big)\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\mathsl{heads}}
\textstyle{\{0,1,\ldots,K\}\times\{0,1,\ldots,K\},}

defined as pair of marginals evaluated at
1\in\boldsymbol{2}
, in:

\begin{array}[]{rcccl}\mathsl{heads}(\varphi)&\coloneqq&\big(\,\varphi(1,0)+\varphi(1,1),\,\varphi(0,1)+\varphi(1,1)\,\big)&=&\big(\,\mathcal{M}(\pi_{1})(\varphi)(1),\,\mathcal{M}(\pi_{2})(\varphi)(1)\,\big).\end{array}

The last line makes clear that the multiplicities of
1
,
that is of ‘head’, in the marginal mulitsets are counted by this
function
\mathsl{heads}
.

More generally, for a ‘dimension’
N\geq 1
one can define:

\textstyle{\mathcal{M}[K]\big(\boldsymbol{2}^{N}\big)\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\mathsl{heads}}
\textstyle{\{0,\ldots,K\}^{N}}
\textstyle{\varphi\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{\big(\,\mathcal{M}(\pi_{1})(\varphi)(1),\ldots,\mathcal{M}(\pi_{N})(\varphi)(1)\,\big)}

(10)

We shall need the following combinatorial result about the marginal
heads function.

Lemma 3.2
.

For
K\geq 0
and
N\geq 1
and numbers
\vec{n}=n_{1},\ldots,n_{N}\in\{0,\ldots,K\}
one has:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\displaystyle\sum_{\varphi\in\mathcal{M}[K](\boldsymbol{2}^{N}),\,\mathsl{heads}(\varphi)=\vec{n}}\,({\kern 1.0pt}\varphi{\kern 1.0pt})&\,=&\displaystyle\prod_{1\leq i\leq N}\,\binom{K}{n_{i}}.\end{array}

We do the proof for
N=2
and use Vandermonde’s formula, see

e.g.

[
6
]
: for numbers
B,G\in\mathbb{N}
and
K\leq B+G
,

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\displaystyle\binom{B+G}{K}&=&\displaystyle\sum_{b\leq B,\,g\leq G,\,b+g=K}\,\binom{B}{b}\cdot\binom{G}{g}.\end{array}

(11)

Now, for a multiset

\varphi\in\mathcal{M}[K]\big(\boldsymbol{2}\times\boldsymbol{2}\big)
we use new
variables
i,j
below, where
i=\varphi(1,1)
and
j=\varphi(0,1)
.

\begin{array}[b]{rcl}\lx@intercol\lx@add@cssclass{ltx_eqn_lefteqn}$\displaystyle\sum_{\varphi\in\mathcal{M}[K](\boldsymbol{2}\times\boldsymbol{2}),\,\mathsl{heads}(\varphi)=(n_{1},n_{2})}\,({\kern 1.0pt}\varphi{\kern 1.0pt})$\mbox{}\hfil\lx@intercol\\
&=&\displaystyle\sum_{\varphi\in\mathcal{M}[K](\boldsymbol{2}\times\boldsymbol{2}),\,\varphi(1,0)+\varphi(1,1)=n_{1},\,\varphi(0,1)+\varphi(1,1)=n_{2}}\frac{K!}{\varphi(0,0)!\cdot\varphi(1,0)!\cdot\varphi(0,1)!\cdot\varphi(1,1)!}\\[10.00002pt]
&=&\displaystyle\sum_{i\leq n_{1},\,j\leq K-n_{1},\,i+j=n_{2}}\,\frac{K!}{(K-n_{1}-j)!\cdot(n_{1}-i)!\cdot j!\cdot i!}\\[8.00003pt]
&=&\displaystyle\sum_{i\leq n_{1},\,j\leq K-n_{1},\,i+j=n_{2}}\,\frac{K!}{n_{1}!\cdot(K-n_{1})!}\cdot\frac{n_{1}!}{i!\cdot(n_{1}-i)!}\cdot\frac{(K-n_{1})!}{j!\cdot(K-n_{1}-j)!}\\[11.99998pt]
&=&\displaystyle\binom{K}{n_{1}}\cdot\sum_{i\leq n_{1},\,j\leq K-n_{1},\,i+j=n_{2}}\,\binom{n_{1}}{i}\cdot\binom{K-i}{j}\hskip 2.0pt\;\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{VandermondeBinaryEqn}}}{{=}}}\;\hskip 2.0pt\displaystyle\binom{K}{n_{1}}\cdot\binom{K}{n_{2}}.\end{array}

[formula]

Definition 3.3
.

For a ‘two-coin’ distribution
\gamma\in\mathcal{D}\big(\boldsymbol{2}\times\boldsymbol{2}\big)
and a ‘toss number’
K
the
bivariate
binomial
distribution is defined as pushforward along the marginal heads
function, of a multinomial distribution:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\mathsl{bvbn}[K](\gamma)&\coloneqq&\mathcal{D}\big(\mathsl{heads}\big)\Big(\mathsl{mn}[K](\gamma)\Big)\,\in\,\mathcal{D}\Big(\{0,1,\ldots,K\}\times\{0,1,\ldots,K\}\Big)\\
&=&\displaystyle\sum_{0\leq n_{1},n_{2}\leq K}\;\;\sum_{\varphi\in\mathcal{M}[K](\boldsymbol{2}\times\boldsymbol{2}),\,\mathsl{heads}(\varphi)=(n_{1},n_{2})}\,\mathsl{mn}[K](\gamma)(\varphi)\,\big|{\kern 1.00006pt}n_{1},n_{2}{\kern 1.00006pt}\big\rangle.\end{array}

(12)

The
N
-dimensional version of this binomial distribution is
obtained by first forming the multinomial distribution

\mathsl{mn}[K](\gamma)\in\mathcal{D}\big(\mathcal{M}[K]\big(\boldsymbol{2}^{N}\big)\big)
, for an
N
-coin

\gamma\in\mathcal{D}\big(\boldsymbol{2}^{N}\big)
, and then pushing along the

N
-dimensional marginal heads function (
10
).

one finds the
following expression, translated into ket form:

\begin{array}[]{l}\displaystyle\sum_{0\leq k,l\leq K}\;\sum_{\max(0,k+l-K)\leq i\leq\min(k,l)}\;\frac{K!}{i!\cdot(k-i)!\cdot(l-i)!\cdot(K-k-l+i)!}\\[-3.99994pt]
\hskip 150.00023pt\cdot\gamma(0,0)^{i}\cdot\gamma(0,0)^{k-i}\cdot\gamma(0,0)^{l-i}\cdot\gamma(0,0)^{K-k-l+i}\,\left|\vrule height=8.5pt,depth=4.5pt,width=0.0pt{\kern 1.00006pt}\right.{\kern-1.99997pt}k,l{\kern-1.99997pt}\left.\vrule height=8.5pt,depth=4.5pt,width=0.0pt{\kern 0.0pt}\right>\end{array}

(13)

One recognises elements of the multinomial
distribution (
9
) in this formulation, but it is
hard to follow what is going on. In fact, this
formulation (
13
) is subtly different, from the one
that we use in (
12
), since it does not count
heads

/
ones
but it counts
tails
/
zeros
, in each
coordinate separately.

Multi-dimensional (multivariate) versions of the
formula (
13
) become even harder, see
e.g.
[
1
,
17
]
. Here, the
multi-dimensional form is obtained in a straightforward manner: the
definition (
12
) still works for an
N
-coin

\gamma\in\mathcal{D}\big(\boldsymbol{2}^{N}\big)
with the marginal heads
function (
10
) used in
N
-ary form.

(2)

We can also give a more explicit
formulation of our functorial definition (
12
) of the
bivariate binomial, in the style of (
13
), via the
following characterisation of the inverse image.

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\lx@intercol\lx@add@cssclass{ltx_eqn_lefteqn}$\displaystyle\big\{\varphi\in\mathcal{M}[K]\big(\boldsymbol{2}\times\boldsymbol{2}\big)\;\big|\;\mathsl{heads}(\varphi)=(n_{1},n_{2})\big\}$\mbox{}\hfil\lx@intercol\\[1.99997pt]
&=&\begin{cases}\begin{array}[]{l}\big\{(K-n_{2}-i)\big|{\kern 1.00006pt}0,0{\kern 1.00006pt}\big\rangle+(n_{2}-n_{1}+i)\big|{\kern 1.00006pt}0,1{\kern 1.00006pt}\big\rangle+i\big|{\kern 1.00006pt}1,0{\kern 1.00006pt}\big\rangle+(n_{1}-i)\big|{\kern 1.00006pt}1,1{\kern 1.00006pt}\big\rangle\\
\qquad\qquad\Big|\;0\leq i\leq\min(n_{1},K-n_{2})\big\}\end{array}&\mbox{if }n_{1}\leq n_{2}\\[13.99995pt]
\begin{array}[]{l}\big\{(K-n_{1}-i)\big|{\kern 1.00006pt}0,0{\kern 1.00006pt}\big\rangle+i\big|{\kern 1.00006pt}0,1{\kern 1.00006pt}\big\rangle+(n_{1}-n_{2}+i)\big|{\kern 1.00006pt}1,0{\kern 1.00006pt}\big\rangle+(n_{2}-i)\big|{\kern 1.00006pt}1,1{\kern 1.00006pt}\big\rangle\\
\qquad\qquad\Big|\;0\leq i\leq\min(n_{2},K-n_{1})\big\}\end{array}&\mbox{if }n_{2}<n_{1}.\end{cases}\end{array}

(14)

We first collect some basic, standard properties about the bivariate
binomial distribution, in particular in relation to the ordinary
binomial distribution. The definition (
12
) of the bivariate
binomial distribution via functoriality allows equational reasoning
in proofs. The details are relegated to the appendix.

Lemma 3.6
.

Let a number
K\in\mathbb{N}
and a two-coin distribution

\gamma\in\mathcal{D}\big(\boldsymbol{2}\times\boldsymbol{2}\big)
be given. For
clarity we use different notation for the various projections:

\textstyle{\boldsymbol{2}\times\boldsymbol{2}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\pi_{1}}
\scriptstyle{\pi_{2}}
\textstyle{\boldsymbol{2}}
\textstyle{\{0,\ldots,K\}\times\{0,\ldots,K\}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\Pi_{1}}
\scriptstyle{\Pi_{2}}
\textstyle{\{0,\ldots K\}}

(15)

We then write
\gamma_{1}\coloneqq\mathcal{D}(\pi_{1})(\gamma)

and
\gamma_{2}\coloneqq\mathcal{D}(\pi_{2})(\gamma)
in
\mathcal{D}(\boldsymbol{2})

for the two marginal coins. They are determined by the ‘heads’
probabilities
\gamma_{1}(1),\gamma_{2}(1)\in[0,1]
, via the
isomorphism
\mathcal{D}(\boldsymbol{2})\cong[0,1]
.

(1)

The marginals of the
bivariate binomial distribution are the (ordinary) binomials
of the marginals of the two-coin distribution:

\begin{array}[]{rclcrcl}\mathcal{D}\big(\Pi_{1}\big)\Big(\mathsl{bvbn}[K](\gamma)\Big)&=&\mathsl{bn}[K]\Big(\gamma_{1}(1)\Big)&&\mathcal{D}\big(\Pi_{2}\big)\Big(\mathsl{bvbn}[K](\gamma)\Big)&=&\mathsl{bn}[K]\Big(\gamma_{2}(1)\Big).\end{array}

(2)

When
\gamma
is
non-entwined, that is, when
\gamma=\gamma_{1}\otimes\gamma_{2}
,
then
\mathsl{bvbn}[K](\gamma)
is also non-entwined, with:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\mathsl{bvbn}[K]\Big(\gamma_{1}\otimes\gamma_{2}\Big)&=&\mathsl{bn}[K]\Big(\gamma_{1}(1)\Big)\otimes\mathsl{bn}[K]\Big(\gamma_{2}(1)\Big).\end{array}

This second result confirms that the notion of bivariate
binomial is a well-defined extension of the ordinary binomial: if the
two-coin distribution
\gamma
is a tensor product of two coins, the
bivariate binomial is a tensor product of the two (marginal,
ordinary) binomial distributions.

There is one more well-behavedness property that we add, namely
closure under convolution, as we have seen for binomial and
multinomial distributions, in Lemmas
2.1

and
2.2
. Again, the proofs are in the appendix.

Proposition 3.7
.

Let
\gamma\in\mathcal{D}\big(\boldsymbol{2}\times\boldsymbol{2}\big)
be given,
with numbers
K,L\in\mathbb{N}
. Bivariate binomials are closed under
convolution, as expressed on the left below.

\begin{array}[]{rclcrcccl}\mathsl{bvbn}[K](\gamma)\,+\,\mathsl{bvbn}[L](\gamma)&=&\mathsl{bvbn}[K+L](\gamma)&&\mathsl{bvbn}[K](\gamma)&=&K\cdot\gamma&=&\gamma+\cdots+\gamma.\end{array}

Thus, the bivariate binomial is a
K
-fold convolution of
the two-coin distribution
\gamma
, as on the right.
\square

4
Mean, variance and covariance

This section describes common statistical characteristics of the
bivariate binomial distribution, namely the mean, variance and
covariance. It turns out that these values are
K
-times the values
for the underlying two-coin distributions. This gives nice
formulas. The proofs require some work.

First, for a distribution
\omega\in\mathcal{D}\big(\mathbb{R}^{N}\big)
, for some
number
N\geq 1
, the mean is described as the tuple in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
given
by the validities of the projections
\pi_{i}\colon\mathbb{R}^{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, viewed as observables. This gives the formula:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\mathrm{mean}(\omega)&\coloneqq&\big(\,\omega\models\pi_{1},\,\ldots,\,\omega\models\pi_{N}\,\big).\end{array}

(16)

Lemma 4.1
.

For
K\in\mathbb{N}
and
\gamma\in\mathcal{D}\big(\boldsymbol{2}\times\boldsymbol{2}\big)
,

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\mathrm{mean}\Big(\mathsl{bvbn}[K](\gamma)\Big)&=&K\cdot\mathrm{mean}\big(\gamma\big)\;\in\;\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}.\end{array}

An abstract argument uses that the mean ommutes with convolutions, in
combination with
Proposition
3.7
. But
one can also proceed in a more concrete manner. First, the mean of

\gamma\in\mathcal{D}\big(\boldsymbol{2}\times\boldsymbol{2}\big)
can be expressed
in terms of
\gamma
’s marginals
\gamma_{1}\coloneqq\mathcal{D}(\pi_{1})(\gamma),\gamma_{2}\coloneqq\mathcal{D}(\pi_{2})(\gamma)\in\mathcal{D}(\boldsymbol{2})
, namely as:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\mathrm{mean}\big(\gamma\big)\hskip 2.0pt\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{MeanEqn}}}{{=}}}\hskip 2.0pt\big(\,\omega\models\pi_{1},\,\omega\models\pi_{2}\,\big)&=&\displaystyle\left(\sum_{b_{1},b_{2}\in\boldsymbol{2}}\,\gamma(b_{1},b_{2})\cdot b_{1},\,\sum_{b_{1},b_{2}\in\boldsymbol{2}}\,\gamma(b_{1},b_{2})\cdot b_{2}\right)\\[10.00002pt]
&=&\displaystyle\left(\sum_{b_{2}\in\boldsymbol{2}}\,\gamma(1,b_{2}),\,\sum_{b_{1}\in\boldsymbol{2}}\,\gamma(b_{1},1)\right)\hskip 2.0pt=\hskip 2.0pt\Big(\,\gamma_{1}(1),\,\gamma_{2})(1)\,\Big).\end{array}

[formula]

Next, using the projection notation from (
15
),

\begin{array}[b]{rcl}\mathrm{mean}\Big(\mathsl{bvbn}[K](\gamma)\Big)&\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{MeanEqn}}}{{=}}}&\big(\,\mathsl{bvbn}[K](\gamma)\models\Pi_{1},\,\mathsl{bvbn}[K](\gamma)\models\Pi_{2}\,\big)\\[1.99997pt]
&=&\displaystyle\left(\sum_{n_{1},n_{2}\in\mathbb{N}}\,\mathsl{bvbn}[K](\gamma)(n_{1},n_{2})\cdot n_{1},\,\sum_{n_{1},n_{2}\in\mathbb{N}}\,\mathsl{bvbn}[K](\gamma)(n_{1},n_{2})\cdot n_{2}\right)\\[10.00002pt]
&=&\displaystyle\left(\sum_{n_{1}\in\mathbb{N}}\,\mathcal{D}(\Pi_{1})\Big(\mathsl{bvbn}[K](\gamma)\Big)(n_{1})\cdot n_{1},\,\sum_{n_{2}\in\mathbb{N}}\,\mathcal{D}(\Pi_{2})\Big(\mathsl{bvbn}[K](\gamma)\Big)(n_{2})\cdot n_{2}\right)\\[10.00002pt]
&=&\displaystyle\left(\sum_{n_{1}\in\mathbb{N}}\,\mathsl{bn}[K]\big(\gamma_{1}(1)\big)(n_{1})\cdot n_{1},\,\sum_{n_{2}\in\mathbb{N}}\,\mathsl{bn}[K]\big(\gamma_{2}(1)\big)(n_{2})\cdot n_{2}\right)\quad\mbox{by Lemma\hbox to0.0pt{~\ref{BivBinMarginalLem}~\eqref{BivBinMarginalLemProj}\hss}}\\[10.00002pt]
&=&\Big(\,\mathrm{mean}\big(\mathsl{bn}[K]\big(\gamma_{1}(1)\big)\big),\,\mathrm{mean}\big(\mathsl{bn}[K]\big(\gamma_{2}(1)\big)\big)\,\Big)\\[3.99994pt]
&=&\Big(\,K\cdot\gamma_{1}(1),\,K\cdot\gamma_{2}(1)\,\Big)\qquad\mbox{by Lemma~\ref{BinomialLem}~\eqref{BinomialLemMeanVar}}\\[1.99997pt]
&=&K\cdot\Big(\,\gamma_{1}(1),\,\gamma_{2})(1)\,\Big)\;\;\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(*)}}{{=}}}\;\;\mathrm{mean}\big(\gamma\big).\end{array}

[formula]

Analogous results exist for the variance and covariance of
the bivariate binomial. We first recall the definitions,
for a distribution
\omega\in\mathcal{D}(X)
and two observables
p,q\colon X\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
on the same set:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\mathrm{var}\big(\omega,p)&=&\Big(\omega\models p\mathrel{\&}p\Big)-\Big(\omega\models p\Big)^{2}\\[3.99994pt]
\mathrm{cov}\big(\omega,p,q)&=&\Big(\omega\models p\mathrel{\&}q\Big)-\Big(\omega\models p\Big)\cdot\Big(\omega\models q\Big).\end{array}

(17)

The conjunction
\mathrel{\&}
of observables is given by
point-wise multiplication. When the underlying set
X
is (a subset
of)
\mathbb{R}^{N}
one typically computes these variances and covariances
for the projections. That will be done below.

Theorem 4.2
.

For
K\in\mathbb{N}
and
\gamma\in\mathcal{D}\big(\boldsymbol{2}\times\boldsymbol{2}\big)

one has, for
i,j\in\{1,2\}
,

\begin{array}[]{rclcrcl}\mathrm{var}\Big(\mathsl{bvbn}[K](\gamma),\Pi_{i}\Big)&=&K\cdot\mathrm{var}(\gamma,\pi_{i})\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad\mathrm{cov}\Big(\mathsl{bvbn}[K](\gamma),\Pi_{i},\Pi_{j}\Big)&=&K\cdot\mathrm{cov}(\gamma,\pi_{i},\pi_{j}).\end{array}

We do the case for
i=1
. First,

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\mathrm{var}\Big(\gamma,\pi_{1}\Big)\hskip 2.0pt\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{VarCovarEqn}}}{{=}}}\hskip 2.0pt\Big(\gamma\models\pi_{1}\mathrel{\&}\pi_{1}\Big)\,-\,\Big(\gamma\models\pi_{1}\Big)^{2}&=&\displaystyle\left(\sum_{b_{1},b_{2}\in\boldsymbol{2}}\,\gamma(b_{1},b_{2})\cdot b_{1}\cdot b_{1}\right)\,-\,\left(\sum_{b_{1},b_{2}\in\boldsymbol{2}}\,\gamma(b_{1},b_{2})\cdot b_{1}\right)^{2}\\[10.00002pt]
&=&\gamma(1,0)+\gamma(1,1)\,-\,\Big(\gamma(1,0)+\gamma(1,1)\Big)^{2}.\end{array}

We turn to the bivariate binomial and make use of
Lemma
2.2
(
3
).

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\mathrm{var}\Big(\mathsl{bvbn}[K](\gamma),\Pi_{i}\Big)&=&\Big(\mathsl{bvbn}[K](\gamma)\models\Pi_{1}\mathrel{\&}\Pi_{1}\Big)\,-\,\Big(\mathsl{bvbn}[K](\gamma)\models\Pi_{1}\Big)^{2}\\[1.99997pt]
&=&\displaystyle\left(\sum_{n_{1},n_{2}\in\mathbb{N}}\,\mathsl{bvbn}[K](\gamma)(n_{1},n_{2})\cdot n_{1}\cdot n_{1}\right)\,-\,\left(\sum_{n_{1},n_{2}\in\mathbb{N}}\,\mathsl{bvbn}[K](\gamma)(n_{1},n_{2})\cdot n_{1}\right)^{2}\\[10.00002pt]
&=&\displaystyle\left(\sum_{\varphi\in\mathcal{M}[K](\boldsymbol{2}\times\boldsymbol{2})}\,\mathsl{mn}[K](\gamma)(\varphi)\cdot\big(\varphi(1,0)+\varphi(1,1)\big)^{2}\right)\\
&&\qquad\displaystyle-\,\left(\sum_{\varphi\in\mathcal{M}[K](\boldsymbol{2}\times\boldsymbol{2})}\,\mathsl{mn}[K](\gamma)(\varphi)\cdot\big(\varphi(0,1)+\varphi(1,1)\big)\right)^{2}\\[11.99998pt]
&=&K\cdot(K-1)\cdot\gamma(1,0)^{2}+K\cdot\gamma(1,0)\,+\,2\cdot K\cdot(K-1)\cdot\gamma(1,0)\cdot\gamma(1,1)\\
&&\qquad+\,K\cdot(K-1)\cdot\gamma(1,1)^{2}+K\cdot\gamma(1,1)-\Big(K\cdot\gamma(1,0)+K\cdot\gamma(1,1)\Big)^{2}\\[1.99997pt]
&=&K\cdot\Big(-\gamma(1,0)^{2}+\gamma(1,0)-2\cdot\gamma(1,0)\cdot\gamma(1,1)-\gamma(1,1)^{2}+\gamma(1,1)\Big)\\[1.99997pt]
&=&K\cdot\Big(\gamma(1,0)+\gamma(1,1)-\big(\gamma(1,0)+\gamma(1,1)\big)^{2}\Big)\\[1.99997pt]
&=&K\cdot\mathrm{var}\Big(\gamma,\pi_{1}\Big)\quad\mbox{as shown above.}\end{array}

For covariance, the cases where
i=j
are covered by the previous
point, since covariance with equal observables is variance. We do the
case
i=1,j=2
, which gives the same outcome as for
i=2,j=1
. We
first look at the covariance of the two-coin distribution
\gamma
.

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\mathrm{cov}\Big(\gamma,\pi_{1},\pi_{2}\Big)&\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{VarCovarEqn}}}{{=}}}&\Big(\gamma\models\pi_{1}\mathrel{\&}\pi_{2}\Big)\,-\,\Big(\gamma\models\pi_{1}\Big)\cdot\Big(\gamma\models\pi_{2}\Big)\\[1.99997pt]
&=&\displaystyle\left(\sum_{b_{1},b_{2}\in\boldsymbol{2}}\,\gamma(b_{1},b_{2})\cdot b_{1}\cdot b_{2}\right)\,-\,\left(\sum_{b_{1},b_{2}\in\boldsymbol{2}}\,\gamma(b_{1},b_{2})\cdot b_{1}\right)\cdot\left(\sum_{b_{1},b_{2}\in\boldsymbol{2}}\,\gamma(b_{1},b_{2})\cdot b_{2}\right)\\[10.00002pt]
&=&\gamma(1,1)\,-\,\Big(\gamma(1,0)+\gamma(1,1)\Big)\cdot\Big(\gamma(0,1)+\gamma(1,1)\Big).\end{array}

Next, again by Lemma
2.2
(
3
):

\hskip-10.00002pt\begin{array}[b]{rcl}\lx@intercol\lx@add@cssclass{ltx_eqn_lefteqn}$\displaystyle\mathrm{cov}\Big(\mathsl{bvbn}[K](\gamma),\Pi_{1},\Pi_{2}\Big)$\mbox{}\hfil\lx@intercol\\[1.99997pt]
&=&\Big(\mathsl{bvbn}[K](\gamma)\models\Pi_{1}\mathrel{\&}\Pi_{2}\Big)\,-\,\Big(\mathsl{bvbn}[K](\gamma)\models\Pi_{1}\Big)\cdot\Big(\mathsl{bvbn}[K](\gamma)\models\Pi_{2}\Big)\\[1.99997pt]
&=&\displaystyle\left(\sum_{n_{1},n_{2}\in\mathbb{N}}\,\mathsl{bvbn}[K](\gamma)(n_{1},n_{2})\cdot n_{1}\cdot n_{2}\right)\\
&&\quad\displaystyle-\,\left(\sum_{n_{1},n_{2}\in\mathbb{N}}\,\mathsl{bvbn}[K](\gamma)(n_{1},n_{2})\cdot n_{1}\right)\cdot\hbox to0.0pt{$\displaystyle\left(\sum_{n_{1},n_{2}\in\mathbb{N}}\,\mathsl{bvbn}[K](\gamma)(n_{1},n_{2})\cdot n_{2}\right)$\hss}\\[10.00002pt]
&=&\displaystyle\left(\sum_{\varphi\in\mathcal{M}[K](\boldsymbol{2}\times\boldsymbol{2})}\,\mathsl{mn}[K](\gamma)(\varphi)\cdot\big(\varphi(1,0)+\varphi(1,1)\big)\cdot\big(\varphi(0,1)+\varphi(1,1)\big)\right)\\
&&\quad-\,\Big(K\cdot\gamma(1,0)+K\cdot\gamma(1,1)\Big)\cdot\Big(K\cdot\gamma(0,1)+K\cdot\gamma(1,1)\Big)\\[1.99997pt]
&=&K\cdot(K-1)\cdot\Big(\gamma(1,0)\cdot\gamma(0,1)+\gamma(1,0)\cdot\gamma(1,1)+\gamma(1,1)\cdot\gamma(0,1)\\
&&\quad+\,\gamma(1,1)^{2}\Big)+K\cdot\gamma(1,1)\,-\,K^{2}\cdot\Big(\gamma(1,0)+\gamma(1,1)\Big)\cdot\hbox to0.0pt{$\Big(\gamma(0,1)+\gamma(1,1)\Big)$\hss}\\[1.99997pt]
&=&K\cdot\Big(\gamma(1,1)\,-\,\big(\gamma(1,0)+\gamma(1,1)\big)\cdot\big(\gamma(0,1)+\gamma(1,1)\big)\Big)\\[1.99997pt]
&=&\mathrm{cov}\Big(\gamma,\pi_{1},\pi_{2}\Big),\quad\mbox{see before.}\end{array}

[formula]

We conclude with an observation which will be useful for
Expectation Maximisation in Section
6
.

Fact 1
.

Suppose we have a distribution
\sigma\in\mathcal{D}\Big(\{0,\ldots,K\}\times\{0,\ldots,K\}\Big)
, for some
given
K\in\mathbb{N}
. We know that
\sigma
is a bivariate binomial
distribution, of the form
\sigma=\mathsl{bvbn}[K](\gamma)
but we
do not know
\gamma\in\mathcal{D}\big(\boldsymbol{2}\times\boldsymbol{2}\big)
. We
can compute the mean and the covariance of
\sigma
. Via
Proposition
4.1
and Theorem
4.2

we can then obtain the probabilities of
\gamma
, and thus
\gamma

itself, in the following way.

•

\gamma(1,1)=\frac{1}{K}\cdot\Big(\mathrm{cov}\big(\sigma,\Pi_{1},\Pi_{2}\big)-\big(\sigma\models\Pi_{1}\big)\cdot\big(\sigma\models\Pi_{2}\big)\Big)
;

•

\gamma(1,0)=\frac{1}{K}\cdot\big(\sigma\models\Pi_{1}\big)-\gamma(1,1)
;

•

\gamma(0,1)=\frac{1}{K}\cdot\big(\sigma\models\Pi_{2}\big)-\gamma(1,1)
;

•

\gamma(0,0)=1-\gamma(1,0)-\gamma(0,1)-\gamma(1,1)
.

Thus, when the draw size
K
is given, a bivariate
binomial distribution is entirely determined by its mean and covariance,
since its mean is given by the pair of validities
(\,\mathsl{bvbn}[K](\gamma)\models\Pi_{1},\,\mathsl{bvbn}[K](\gamma)\models\Pi_{2}\,)
, see (
16
).

5
Laplace’s rule of succession

Laplace’s rule of succession provides an answer to what was originally
formulated as the sunrise problem: suppose I have seen the sun rise in

n
successive days, what is the probability that it will rise
tomorrow? The answer according to Laplace’s rule is

\frac{n+1}{n+2}
. It will go to one as
n
goes to infinity.

The binomial distribution plays a role in a systematic answer
formulation of the rule of succession. Hence the question arises: is
there a role for the bivariate binomial too? In order to answer
this question we first provide a general, modern interpretation of the
rule of succession. It is generally understood as providing the expected
outcome after an update, see
e.g.

[
16
]

or
[
2
]
. We reformulate this as: the rule of
succession calculates the
mean of a dagger
.

The relevant calculations involve continuous distributions as priors,
such as the Beta and Dirichlet distributions. We have to assume that
the reader is reasonably familiar with these distributions and with
the associated results. Therefore, this section only gives a sketch of
the situation at hand, from a structural angle. We start by recalling,
without proof, basic facts about the Beta distribution on
[0,1]
and
about the Dirichlet distribution on discrete distributions

\mathcal{D}(X)
. These continuous distributions are typically used for the
bias parameter
r\in[0,1]
of a flip distribution
\mathsl{flip}(r)
and for
the distribution parameter
\omega\in\mathcal{D}(X)
of a multinomial
distribution
\mathsl{mn}[K](\omega)
. The results below are
standard. The formulation of the conjugate prior property in terms of
daggers is in line with
[
8
]
.

.

6
Expectation Maximisation for bivariate binomials

Expectation Maximisation is an unsupervised machine learning technique
for classifying data in a finite number of classes/features. The
classification takes place by finding a ‘mixture’ distribution that
assigns propertions (in the form of probabilities) to the different
classification features. This section gives an impression how
bivariate distributions — in the formulation of this paper — may
be useful in concrete applications in machine learning. Below we use
the channel-based analysis of Expectation Maximisation developed
in
[
12

14

and demonstrate how it works in a
concrete example. It avoids explicit updates of parameters, as happens
in common formulations in Expectation Maximisation. However,
implicitly, a two-coin distribution parameter is recomputed in each
iteration via the steps described in Fact
1
.

7
Concluding remarks

This paper reformulates bivariate binomial distributions in a concise
manner, by exploiting the functoriality of the mapping
X\mapsto\mathcal{D}(X)
, sending a set
X
to the set of probability distributions on

X
. It demonstrates the clarifying power of the categorical approach
to probability theory by (re)describing some of the basic properties
of bivariate binomials, for instance via (dagger) channels. It also
shows that this categorical formulation makes it possible to prove
basic properties via equational reasoning. The paper concentrates on
two dimensions, for simplicity, but the extension to multiple
dimensions is straightforward.

The multivariate binomial distribution be understood as a discrete
version of the widely used multidimensional Gaussian distribution. By
the famous theorem of De Moivre-Laplace every binomial distributions
approximate a Gaussian distribution. This is a special case of the
Central Limit Theorem. The approximation also works for multivariate
binomials, see

(of Lemma

(1)

We do only the first equation, using twice the Binomial
Theorem. For
0\leq n_{1}\leq K
we have:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\mathcal{D}\big(\Pi_{1}\big)\Big(\mathsl{bvbn}[K](\gamma)\Big)(n_{1})&=&\displaystyle\sum_{0\leq n_{2}\leq K}\,\mathcal{D}\big(\mathsl{heads}\big)\Big(\mathsl{mn}[K](\gamma)\Big)(n_{1},n_{2})\\[10.00002pt]
&=&\displaystyle\sum_{0\leq n_{2}\leq K}\;\sum_{\varphi\in\mathcal{M}[K](\boldsymbol{2}\times\boldsymbol{2}),\,\mathsl{heads}(\varphi)=(n_{1},n_{2})}\,\mathsl{mn}[K](\gamma)(\varphi)\\[13.99995pt]
&=&\displaystyle\sum_{\varphi\in\mathcal{M}[K](\boldsymbol{2}\times\boldsymbol{2}),\,\varphi(1,0)+\varphi(1,1)=n_{1}}\,({\kern 1.0pt}\varphi{\kern 1.0pt})\cdot\prod_{i,j\in\boldsymbol{2}}\,\gamma(i,j)^{\varphi(i,j)}\\[11.99998pt]
&=&\displaystyle\sum_{0\leq i\leq n_{1}}\,\sum_{0\leq j\leq K-n_{1}}\,\frac{K!}{(K-n_{1}-j)!\cdot j!\cdot(n_{1}-i)!\cdot i!}\\
&&\qquad\cdot\,\gamma(0,0)^{K-n_{1}-j}\cdot\gamma(0,1)^{j}\cdot\gamma(1,0)^{n_{1}-i}\cdot\gamma(1,1)^{i}\\
&=&\displaystyle\sum_{0\leq i\leq n_{1}}\,\sum_{0\leq j\leq K-n_{1}}\,\frac{K!}{n_{1}!\cdot(K-n_{1})!}\cdot\frac{n_{1}!}{i!\cdot(n_{1}-i)!}\cdot\frac{(K-n_{1})!}{j!\cdot(K-n_{1}-j)!}\\
&&\qquad\cdot\,\gamma(0,0)^{K-n_{1}-j}\cdot\gamma(0,1)^{j}\cdot\gamma(1,0)^{n_{1}-i}\cdot\gamma(1,1)^{i}\\[1.99997pt]
&=&\displaystyle\binom{K}{n_{1}}\cdot\big(\gamma(1,0)+\gamma(1,1)\big)^{n_{1}}\cdot\big(\gamma(0,0)+\gamma(0,1)\big)^{K-n_{1}}\\[8.00003pt]
&=&\displaystyle\binom{K}{n_{1}}\cdot\gamma_{1}(1)^{n_{1}}\cdot\big(1-\gamma_{1}(1)\big)^{K-n_{1}}\hskip 2.0pt=\hskip 2.0pt\mathsl{bn}[K]\Big(\gamma_{1}(1)\Big)(n_{1}).\end{array}
