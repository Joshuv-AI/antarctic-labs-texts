Network analysis of the relationship between anxiety, depression and resilience in patients with pancreatic cancer and gender differences
Authors: Haimu Cheng, Xiaomin Zhuang, Yamin Zhao, Qixin He, Hua Zhang, Yuanxia Han
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPancreatic cancer patients demonstrate elevated vulnerability to depression and anxiety relative to other gastrointestinal malignancies, attributable to the disease’s dismal prognosis and substantial symptom burden. However, symptom-level relationships among anxiety, depression, and resilience in pancreatic cancer remain insufficiently understood. Therefore, this study employed network analysis to investigate the interrelationships among anxiety, depression, and psychological resilience in the population. Additionally, a comparative analysis of the network structure was conducted to explore gender differences. Ultimately, this study aims to provide theory-driven intervention strategies and practical guidelines for supporting the mental health of patients diagnosed with pancreatic cancer.MethodThis cross-sectional study enrolled 759 patients with pancreatic cancer from Guangzhou, China, between June 2024 and July 2025. Anxiety, depression, and resilience were assessed using the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS) and Brief Resilience Scale (BRS). A regularized partial correlation network was constructed based on the survey data, and the bridge expected influence index was applied to quantify the bridge components. We also compared the network structures of male and female samples to explore sex differences.ResultsThe prevalence rates of anxiety and depression were 24.2% and 30.3%, respectively. A7 “Panic” (0.903) was the core symptom in the anxiety, depression, and resilience symptom networks. R1 “Bounce back quickly” (-3.342) was the core bridge symptom connecting the three network clusters. A comparison of male and female networks indicated that the overall network structure exhibited no significant sex differences; however, the global strength of the female network was significantly higher. The strongest positive associations remained consistent across the male and female networks (R1 “Bounce back quickly” and R3 “Recover from stress quickly”). A7 “Panic” emerged as a shared core symptom in both male and female networks.Conclusion“Panic” was identified as the central symptom in the anxiety–depression–resilience symptom networks, while “Bounce back quickly” served as the key bridge node connecting these three symptom clusters. These findings provide potential symptom-level targets for future psychological assessments and intervention research. Additionally, there were significant differences in global strength between male and female networks, with gender-specific strategies recommended for each.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s40359-026-05266-y.Keywords: Pancreatic cancer, Anxiety, Depression, Resilience, Network analysis

Introduction

The rising incidence of cancer has brought associated psychiatric comorbidities, such as anxiety and depression, into sharper focus. Recent epidemiological data indicate that the prevalence of depression and anxiety in patients with cancer is 23.4% and 19.9%, respectively, which is approximately fourfold higher than that in the general population [1, 2].

Pancreatic cancer (PC) is a highly fatal malignancy with a dismal prognosis, with an overall 5-year survival rate of only 10% [3]. Its global incidence continues to increase; however, therapeutic options remain limited, primarily because the disease is frequently diagnosed at an advanced stage. It is projected that by 2030, PC will become the second leading cause of cancer-related death worldwide [4]. For affected individuals, receiving the diagnosis and undergoing treatment constitutes an intensely stressful experience, predisposing them to sustained negative psychological sequelae, including emotional distress, depression, anxiety, sleep disturbances, fatigue, and impaired quality of life [5, 6].

Studies have indicated that, following a diagnosis of PC, 15% to 31.4% of patients experience clinically relevant anxiety or depressive symptoms [7–10]. Notably, the proportion of patients with PC suffering from these conditions is significantly higher than that observed in patients with other gastrointestinal tumors [11, 12]. Furthermore, the presence of psychiatric symptoms prior to diagnosis has been associated with worse overall survival [7]. The progressive psychological burden in this population represents a prevalent and serious clinical challenge that, if inadequately managed, may contribute to a poorer quality of life and clinical outcomes.

Resilience is conceptualized as self-belief in one’s capacity to successfully recover from adversity [13]. In the context of cancer, it refers to an individual’s protective traits or personal characteristics, which are considered modifiable and can facilitate successful adaptation to illness [14]. Previous research has suggested that psychological resilience encompasses the interaction between intrinsic resilience characteristics and external environmental factors [15]. Individuals with higher resilience levels are better protected against developing mental health disorders. Multiple studies have examined the beneficial role of resilience in alleviating anxiety and depressive symptoms [16], improving quality of life [17], and enhancing health outcomes among patients with cancer [18].

Although numerous studies have explored the associations between resilience, anxiety, and depression, they have predominantly relied on comparisons based on total scores. This approach overlooks the distinct components within these psychological constructs and obscures their intricate relationships. Network analysis is a statistical methodology used to map the complex connections among individual symptoms of mental disorders [19]. In contrast to traditional correlation-based methods, network analysis enables the visualization of sophisticated interactions between psychological constructs at a more granular and symptom-specific level. Furthermore, previous research has explored gender disparities in psychological distress, including anxiety and depression. For instance, Clark et al. [20] reported a significantly higher prevalence of depression among men with PC than among women (34.0% vs. 22.6%). Zhou et al. [21] demonstrated that women with colorectal cancer experience more severe psychological distress and greater impairments in social functioning than men, despite a stronger propensity to employ active coping strategies.

Prior research on patients with PC has predominantly focused on investigating the prevalence of psychological and symptomatic burdens, such as anxiety, depression, and fatigue, and analyzing their impact on treatment adherence and postoperative outcomes [7, 8, 10, 22, 23]. To date, limited attention has been devoted to the potential of resilience to alleviate anxiety and depression in this specific population. Moreover, no study has employed network analysis to elucidate sex differences in symptom-level interactions among anxiety, depression, and psychological resilience within the PC population. Therefore, it is essential to construct and compare sex-specific networks.

Therefore, the present study pursued three primary objectives: (1) to investigate the prevalence of anxiety and depression following PC diagnosis; (2) to identify the central and bridge symptoms within the anxiety-depression-resilience network; and (3) to compare these network structures by sex. By employing network analysis, this study aims to identify critical therapeutic targets for ameliorating psychological distress, thereby providing an empirical framework to guide the design of targeted sex-specific psycho-oncological interventions.

Methods

Study design and participants

Using a convenience sampling method, we recruited eligible patients from the pancreatic tumor outpatient clinic and inpatient wards of a large specialized oncology hospital in Guangzhou. The recruitment period was from June 2024 to July 2025.

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) age ≥ 18 years; (2) preliminary diagnosis of a pancreatic tumor based on imaging examinations; (3) voluntary participation with signed informed consent; and (4) ability to cooperate during the survey, comprehend the questionnaire content, and independently complete the assessment.

The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) diagnosis of an organic mental disorder; (2) presence of severe physical illness or adverse drug reactions; and (3) experiencing significantly stressful events in the past year.

Sampling

The required sample size was calculated using the standard formula for cross-sectional studies: n = Zα2(1-P)P/δ2, where α = 0.05, Zα = 1.96, and the allowable relative error was set at 15%, yielding an absolute error of δ = 0.15P. Based on a reported prevalence of psychological symptom burden of 30% among patients with PC [8], the required initial sample size was 398. To account for an anticipated 20% rate of invalid or incomplete questionnaires, the minimum target sample size was increased to 478 participants. To further ensure the stability and robustness of the network analysis, we expanded the data collection and ultimately enrolled 759 participants.

Data collection

This survey was conducted via the Wenjuanxing online survey platform (https://www.wjx.cn), the most popular online service providing questionnaires designed and distributed in China. The questionnaire included the research introduction, purpose, and informed consent forms. Participants could access the questionnaire by scanning a WeChat QR code or by clicking a website link. The participants first agreed to participate and then completed all the questions. The questionnaire took approximately 15 min to complete. During the data collection period, 804 questionnaires were collected, of which 45 were excluded due to incomplete responses, logical inconsistencies, or duplicate submissions, yielding a final analytical sample of 759 valid responses.

Ethics statement

In this study, all researchers obtained informed consent from the participants before completing the questionnaire survey, and the participants subsequently signed written informed consent forms. This study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. This study was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of Sun Yat-sen University Cancer Center (Approval No. B2023-629-01).

Measures

Demographic information

In accordance with research requirements, we developed a demographic information form that included items related to demographic and work-related characteristics, such as age, gender, marital status, educational attainment, employment status, place of residence, and economic status.

Anxiety and depression

The Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS), developed by Zigmond and Snaith [24] in 1983, is a widely used 14-item self-report instrument designed to screen for anxiety and depression in non-psychiatric medical populations. It comprises two subscales — seven items each for anxiety (HADS-A) and depression (HADS-D) — with each item rated on a 4-point Likert scale (0–3 points) reflecting symptom frequency over the past month. Total subscale scores range from 0 to 21 points, with established clinical cut-offs: ‌normal‌ (0–7), ‌mild‌ (8–10), ‌moderate‌ (11–14), and ‌severe‌ (15–21). The Chinese version, translated and validated by Ye et al. [25], demonstrated strong reliability and construct validity for use in general hospital settings. In the present study, Cronbach’s α was 0.787, confirming its reliability in this specific context.

Resilience

The Brief Resilience Scale (BRS) is a six-item measure developed by Smith et al. [26] designed to assess the capacity for stress recovery, a construct closely aligned with psychological resilience. The scale was adapted into Chinese by Fu et al. [27]. The scale employs a 5-point Likert format ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree), consisting of three positively worded items (items 1, 3, and 5) and three reverse-scored items (items 2, 4, and 6), with higher scores indicating greater resilience. Positively worded items were scored from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree), while reverse worded items were scored from 5 (strongly disagree) to 1 (strongly agree). Previous research has established its reliability and construct validity. In this study, the scale achieved a Cronbach’s α of 0.744.

Statistical analyses

First, the data were analyzed using SPSS version 26.0. Descriptive statistics were computed for sample characteristics: continuous variables were described using the mean and standard deviation (SD), and categorical variables were described using frequency and percentage. A t-test was used to compare the statistically significant differences in depression, anxiety, and resilience scores between the male and female groups.

Network analysis was conducted using R version 4.5.2. The qgraph package was employed to visualize the network structure of anxiety, depression, and resilience among patients, and to explore core symptoms within the network. A Gaussian graphical model (GGM) was fitted to the data, and the network model was constructed based on Spearman’s correlation. It should be clarified that the HADS and BRS scales both generate ordinal categorical data. The rationales for selecting Spearman’s correlation are as follows: (1) the study had an adequate sample size, and the data showed no severe skewness; (2) polychoric correlation, which is suitable for ordinal categorical data, was also considered in the preliminary analysis, and the validation results indicated that the variable association trends were highly consistent between the two correlation analyses; and (3) considering the sparsity of the network model and the interpretability of the results, Spearman’s correlation is more practically applicable in the context of this study.

A stable and clear network model was built using the least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) algorithm combined with the Extended Bayesian Information Criterion (EBIC) [28, 29], where the parameter value for EBIC was set to 0.5. The Fruchterman-Reingold algorithm was used to determine the node positions within the network. The edges between nodes represent partial correlation relationships between symptoms, with positive correlations depicted by blue edges and negative correlations by red edges. The thicker the edge, the stronger the correlation between the two symptoms; conversely, the thinner the edge, the weaker the correlation between the two symptoms. Node centrality, which quantifies the relative importance of a node within a network architecture, is conventionally evaluated using four widely adopted indicators: strength, betweenness centrality, closeness centrality, and expected influence (EI). The extant literature has established that EI exhibits superior performance over conventional strength-based measures in identifying central nodes within networks characterized by negative edges [30, 31]. In light of the negative correlations observed among the nodes in the present network, we consistently employed the EI to quantify the central symptoms. To pinpoint bridge nodes, we further utilized the Bridge Expected Influence (BEI) index. Centrality metrics were computed using the qgraph package, whereas bridge nodes were detected using the networktools package.

Network stability, accuracy, and difference tests were performed using the bootnet package. Correlation stability (CS) coefficients for edge weights and centrality indices were estimated using a case-dropping bootstrap (1,000 samples). CS coefficients > 0.25 indicate acceptable stability, with values > 0.5 considered preferable [32]. Edge weight accuracy was examined using non-parametric bootstrapping (1,000 samples), with narrower 95% confidence interval (CI) indicating greater precision. The significance of the differences between node strength centrality values and edge weights was evaluated using bootstrap difference tests (1,000 samples; α = 0.05).

We employed the Network Comparison Test (NCT) package in R to compare the network structures between male and female patients diagnosed with PC. Following the NCT framework, we conducted three complementary invariance tests: (a) network structure invariance test, (b) global network strength invariance test, and (c) local invariance test, specifically examining differences in edge weights and node centrality indices [33]. Initially, the absolute values of each edge weight were compared across 1,000 permutation iterations, with resulting P-values adjusted using the Holm-Bonferroni procedure to control for multiple comparisons. Then, differences in edge values were compared by subtracting the edge weights from the network model in pairs. Finally, independent t-tests were performed to examine the statistically significant differences between male and female participants.

Results

Sample characteristics

This study enrolled 759 patients with PC, including 383 men (50.5%) and 376 women (49.5%). The mean age was 55.7 ± 13.2 years. 710 participants were married (93.5%), 36 were unmarried (4.8%), and 13 were divorced or widowed (1.7%). Regarding employment status, 455 were not employed (59.9%), while 304 were employed (40.1%). In terms of educational level, 186 held a junior college degree or above (24.5%), whereas the remaining 573 had completed senior high school or lower (75.5%). Concerning place of residence, the majority lived in urban areas: 540 resided in cities (71.1%) and 219 in rural areas (28.9%). Regarding annual family income, 377 reported earnings exceeding 150,000 RMB (49.7%), 238 reported earnings between 60,000 and 150,000 RMB (31.3%), and 144 reported earnings below 60,000 RMB (19.0%). Network structure

As shown in Fig. 1, the network was closely interconnected and comprised 115 non-zero edges. Within the cluster of anxiety symptom, the strongest association weight was observed between A7 “Panic” and A5 “Frightened butterflies in stomach” (r = 0.274). In the cluster of depression symptom, the strongest association weight was observed between D1 “Still enjoy the same” and D2 “See the humor in things” (r = 0.243). Within the cluster of resilience symptom, R1 “Bounce back quickly” and R3 “Recover from stress quickly” showed the strongest connection (r = 0.393). Between the anxiety and depression clusters, the strongest edge was identified between A4 “Relaxed” and D7 “Enjoy something” (r = 0.113). The strongest edge between anxiety and resilience clusters linked A4 “Relaxed” and R2 “Hard to bear stress” (r = -0.075). Between the depression and resilience clusters, the strongest edge connected D4 “Slowed down” and R5 “Come through difficulties easily” (r = -0.076). Figure S1 presents the results of the accuracy examination of the edge weights. The black line represents the average edge weight estimated via bootstrapping, the red line depicts the edge weight from the study sample, and the grey area indicates the 95% CI. The relatively narrow 95% CI for the edge weights in this study suggest the acceptable precision of the estimated weights. Bootstrap difference tests for the edge weights are shown in Fig. S2. Network structure of gender

To investigate sex differences in network structure, separate network analyses were conducted for male and female participants (Fig. 3). Both networks comprised 190 possible edges, with 90 edges exhibiting non-zero weights in males and 101 in females. The edge weight matrices for the male and female networks are provided in Table S3. The three strongest connections showed partial concordance across sexes: R1 “Bounce back quickly” – R3 “Recover from stress quickly” (men: r = 0.347; women: r = 0.386) and R2 “Hard to bear stress” – R4 “Hard to snap back” (men: r = 0.277; women: r = 0.348) ranked among the top three associations in both networks. The strongest negative edge in males occurred between D5 “Lost interest” and R5 “Come through difficulties easily” (r = -0.106), whereas in females, the strongest negative edge was observed between A4 “Relaxed” and R4 “Hard to snap back” (r = -0.126).

Accuracy and stability of gender

Accuracy testing revealed relatively narrow 95% confidence intervals for edge weights in both male and female networks, indicating acceptable precision. Network comparison tests for gender

No statistically significant differences were observed in the overall network structure between males and females (M = 0.19, P = 0.15) (Fig. S8). However, global strength was significantly higher in the female network than in the male network (female: 8.18, male: 6.53; S = 1.65, P = 0.02) (Fig. S9). Edge strength tests identified nine edges with significant sex differences (Table S4). Significant differences were also observed in the edge strength centrality (Table S5).

Discussion

The prevalence of anxiety and depression among patients with PC was 24.2% and 30.3%, respectively. These estimates exceed those reported by Davis et al. [7] (19.4% and 14%), Janda et al. [9] (15% and 15%), and Akizuki et al. [10] (16.5% and 16.5%), yet align with the findings by Dengsø et al. [8] (20.4% and 31.4%). These discrepancies may reflect variations in sample characteristics, assessment instruments, and diagnostic thresholds employed in the studies.

In the anxiety-depression clusters, a positive association was observed between A4 “Relaxed” and D7 “Enjoy something”. This suggests that a relaxed state may enhance patients with PC to engage in activities, potentially alleviating depressive symptoms. Within the anxiety-resilience clusters, A4 “Relaxed” was negatively associated with R2 “Hard to bear stress”. Stress theory posits that exposure to stressful events triggers physiological responses (e.g., palpitations, sweating, gastrointestinal discomfort, and restlessness) that collectively impair the capacity for relaxation [34]. PC is characterized by aggressive biology, poor prognosis, and limited survival rates. Following diagnosis, patients typically experience greater psychological distress than those with other malignancies, compromising their capacity for relaxation [12]. In the depression-resilience clusters, a negative correlation was identified between D4 “Slowed down” and R5 “Come through difficulties easily”. This negative association indicates that psychomotor retardation depletes psychological resources in patients with PC, undermining their coping efficacy and impeding their ability to overcome difficulties.

Centrality analyses indicated that A7 “Panic” was the most central symptom in the network. Worry and panic are prominent psychological responses following cancer diagnosis, potentially driven by disease progression uncertainty, inadequate social support, and financial burden. Previous studies have demonstrated that patients with PC experience heightened uncertainty regarding disease progression [35], substantial caregiver burden [36, 37], and increased financial strain [38] relative to patients with other malignancies. Fear of cancer progression (FoP), defined as “fear, worry, or concern about cancer that returns or progresses,” represents a central construct in psycho-oncology [39]. Although FoP does not constitute a formal psychiatric diagnosis, it represents a clinically significant anxiety syndrome in patients with cancer [40]. Extant research has documented significant positive associations between FoP, anxiety, and depression [41, 42]. Collectively, a PC diagnosis increases vulnerability to anxiety and depressive symptoms. Furthermore, worries and panic related to disease progression exacerbate symptom severity. Thus, addressing worries and fear of progression represents a critical therapeutic target for alleviating anxiety and depression in this population.

Bridge centrality analyses revealed that R1 “Bounce back quickly” exhibited the highest BEI absolute value. This suggests that, for patients with PC, rapid recovery capacity may function as a key transdiagnostic mechanism connecting and potentially buffering anxiety and depression. Notably, the negative sign precisely reflects the essential role of “bounce back quickly” in buffering against anxiety and depression rather than exacerbating them. The capacity for rapid recovery from adversity reflects the ability to restore psychological equilibrium and maintain positive functioning. This finding aligns with the conceptualization of resilience as a dynamic process characterized by the capacity to maintain or restore homeostasis and positive adaptation following adversity [43]. The treatment trajectory following PC diagnosis exposes patients to substantial uncertainty, physical symptoms, and psychological burden. Patients capable of rapidly restoring psychological equilibrium may more effectively interrupt the propagation of anxiety (e.g., worry and fear) and depressive (e.g., helplessness) symptoms.

In the gender comparison network, the strongest positive associations remained consistent across the male and female networks, indicating that psychological resilience, as a cross-gender protective psychological trait, exhibits a highly stable core structure when confronting major physical trauma [44]. However, in the female network, the association between R1 “Bounce back quickly” and R3 “Recover from stress quickly” was notably stronger than that in the male network. This finding suggests that when coping with the extreme stressor of a PC diagnosis, female patients exhibit a tighter coupling between the “emotional elasticity” and “stress compensation” mechanisms of their psychological resilience than male patients. Research on gender differences in stress coping strategies has indicated that women tend to adopt ruminative emotion regulation strategies when facing stress, whereas men are more inclined toward problem-focused coping, distraction, or avoidance [45]. Consequently, the strong connection between R1 and R3 in the female network may represent a gender-specific “psychological defence loop.” This finding also echoes the conclusions of Yang et al. [46], who proposed that patients with PC can be categorized into three psychological subgroups, with gender being one of the key determinants distinguishing these subgroups. Furthermore, A7 “Panic” emerged as a shared core symptom in the anxiety, depression, and resilience networks for both male and female patients. This underscores that PC, characterized by its insidious onset, extremely poor prognosis, and high mortality rate, confronts patients with an “existential threat” and “sense of loss of control” at the moment of diagnosis [47]. Regardless of sex, the acute panic triggered by a PC diagnosis dominates the emotional network, subsequently propagating downstream to elicit widespread anxiety and depressive symptoms.

Furthermore, comparing the male and female networks revealed no significant differences in the overall structure or core nodes, suggesting that the core targets for psychological interventions in patients with PC are universally applicable across genders. However, the female network exhibited a significantly higher global strength. Within the framework of network analysis, a higher global strength reflects a tighter overall coupling among nodes. This implies that psychological symptoms in female patients are more prone to a “cascade effect”, in which the exacerbation of a single symptom can rapidly propagate throughout the entire symptom network and accelerate the depletion of psychological resilience. Therefore, psychosocial interventions for female patients should prioritize early identification and systematic disruption to prevent the resonant deterioration of tightly interconnected symptom clusters.

These findings have important clinical implications for mitigating psychological distress in patients with PC. First, the core element “Panic” suggests that cognitive-behavioral group interventions may be particularly effective [48]. Specifically, cognitive-behavioral therapy (CBT) can alleviate patients’ panic through the following therapeutic pathways: (1) cognitive restructuring enables patients to identify and rectify catastrophic thoughts and irrational beliefs concerning disease progression and prognostic outcomes; (2) emotion regulation strategies (e.g., progressive muscle relaxation and mindfulness meditation) help relieve panic-related somatic tension and distress; (3) behavioral activation promotes gradual re-engagement in valued and meaningful activities, thereby interrupting the vicious cycle of social avoidance and functional impairment driven by panic; and (4) exposure therapy systematically guides patients to confront fear-provoking situations associated with their illness (e.g., medical examinations, treatment discussions), thus attenuating conditioned fear responses [49]. Systematic reviews have demonstrated that CBT significantly attenuates anxiety and depressive symptoms while enhancing quality of life in patients with cancer, relative to control conditions [50].

Then, the prominent bridge role of “Bounce back quickly” capacity suggests that Attention and Interpretation Therapy (AIT) may enhance this specific resilience component. AIT is a structured group-based psychological intervention, the core mechanisms of which include: (1) attention training, delivered through mindfulness meditation and focused-attention exercises, facilitates the redirection of attention away from disease-related negative stimuli while fostering enhanced awareness and acceptance of present-moment experiences; (2) cognitive bias modification, which targets the identification and modification of negative interpretation tendencies regarding disease-related information (e.g., misattributing somatic discomfort as unequivocal signals of cancer progression), thereby cultivating a more flexible and neutral cognitive style; and (3) self-efficacy enhancement is promoted through the establishment of incremental therapeutic goals, systematic documentation of mastery experiences, and the provision of mutual support within the group, collectively reinforcing patients’ confidence in their capacity to navigate disease-related challenges. Ding et al. [51] have demonstrated that, among twelve distinct psychological interventions, AIT emerged as the optimal approach for enhancing psychological resilience in patients with cancer. Furthermore, randomized controlled trials conducted among postoperative colon cancer patients have corroborated that AIT effectively elevates resilience levels while concomitantly reducing cancer-related fatigue and negative affect [52].

The last, given the stronger symptom associations and more tightly interconnected social networks observed among female patients, gender-differentiated psychological interventions should be implemented, with particular emphasis on the early identification and systematic intervention for women. Interpersonal Psychotherapy (IPT) represents a particularly suitable intervention option for female patients with PC. Systematic review evidence has demonstrated that IPT effectively reduces depressive symptom in patients with breast cancer while concurrently improving interpersonal emotion regulation capacities and perceived social support [53]. Additionally, supportive-expressive group therapy and peer support programs facilitate the provision of secure emotional expression spaces and peer support networks, thereby effectively promoting social support and assisting patients in navigating existential concerns [54].

Limitations

This study had several limitations. First, the cross-sectional design precluded the inference of causal relationships among resilience, anxiety, and depression. Therefore, longitudinal investigations and dynamic network analyses are required to elucidate these associations. Second, node redundancy was not formally evaluated in this study. Although the symptoms were chosen based on well-established clinical scales and prior empirical evidence, statistical redundancy among nodes could compromise the interpretation of the network structure. Future studies should systematically screen for node redundancy using procedures such as the Goldbricker algorithm to further corroborate network robustness. Third, the sample was recruited from a single medical center in Southern China, potentially limiting the generalizability of our findings. Fourth, the study population comprised patients with newly diagnosed PC for whom definitive cancer staging and treatment plans had not yet been established. As a result, most clinical characteristics were either incomplete or imprecise and were therefore not reported in the paper. Future research should aim to obtain and supplement these data from post-treatment patient cohorts. Finally, this study focused primarily on the associations between symptom-level anxiety and depression and the bounce-back component of resilience. Future research should explore the interplay of other resilience dimensions (e.g., social support) within the psychological network of patients with PC to develop more comprehensive resilience-based interventions.

Conclusion

This study utilized network analysis to explore the interrelationships among resilience, anxiety, and depression among patients with PC and their differences between men and women. Our findings identified “Panic” as the central symptom within the anxiety-depression-resilience symptom networks, and “Bounce back quickly” as the key bridge node connecting these three symptom clusters. These findings provide potential symptom-level targets for future psychological assessments and intervention research. Additionally, there were significant differences in global strength between male and female networks, with gender-specific strategies recommended for each.
