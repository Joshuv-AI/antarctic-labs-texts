Video-Based Peer Support and Exclusive Breastfeeding and Maternal Self-Efficacy
Authors: Kris Yuet Wan Lok, Caroline Hoi Lam Ip, Ying Dai, Yuen Fan Chan, Martha Sin Lam Choi, Christine Lam, Sze Ki Wong, Lai Fong Ho, Pui Hing Chau, Yan-Shing Chang, Debra Bick
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Key PointsQuestionDoes the use of peer support by video call, compared with standard care, increase exclusive breastfeeding rates and maternal breastfeeding self-efficacy scores during the first 6 months post partum?FindingsIn this randomized clinical trial involving 442 first-time mothers, technology-based breastfeeding peer support did not increase the exclusive breastfeeding rate at 6 months, but it resulted in a significantly higher exclusive breastfeeding rate at 2 months and a significantly higher breastfeeding self-efficacy at 4 months compared with standard care.MeaningThese findings suggest that integrating video call peer support into existing programs may improve maternal and infant outcomes by increasing breastfeeding rates and duration, as well as enhancing maternal confidence.

Introduction

Exclusive breastfeeding is the globally recommended standard for infant feeding for the first 6 months of life, supported by its well-documented benefits for both infant and maternal health.1,2,3,4 Despite these benefits, global rates of exclusive breastfeeding remain suboptimal, with many mothers discontinuing within the first few weeks post partum.4 A key determinant of breastfeeding success is maternal self-efficacy, defined as a mother’s confidence in her ability to breastfeed, which has been consistently associated with the initiation duration and exclusivity of breastfeeding.5 Interventions that enhance maternal self-efficacy may therefore play a critical role in promoting sustained breastfeeding.5

Peer support has emerged as a promising strategy to improve breastfeeding outcomes, particularly when delivered by trained volunteers with personal breastfeeding experience.6,7,8 Systematic reviews confirm the efficacy of in-person and telephone-based peer support. However, their implementation faces distinct challenges. In-person support can be resource intensive and geographically constrained, while telephone support, though accessible, lacks the visible cues critical for assessing latch, infant positioning, and breast health.

Video call–based peer support offers a potential synthesis, providing the visual interaction necessary for practical, personalized assistance with accessibility and flexibility of remote delivery. It represents a scalable model that can reach mothers in their home environment, overcoming barriers of distance, mobility, and, as highlighted during the COVID-19 pandemic, infection control.9,10

To our knowledge, few studies have rigorously evaluated the effectiveness of structured video call–based peer support program on exclusive breastfeeding and maternal self-efficacy, particularly among first-time mothers with low breastfeeding confidence. This study aimed to address this evidence gap by conducting a multicenter, randomized clinical trial to evaluate the impact of a structured video call peer support intervention on exclusive breastfeeding and maternal breastfeeding self-efficacy during the first 6 months post partum.

Methods

Design

We conducted a multicenter, 2-arm, randomized clinical trial from January 31, 2021, to June 30, 2024. First-time mothers were recruited from the postnatal wards of 4 public hospitals in Hong Kong. Eligibility criteria included primiparity, 18 years or older, intention to breastfeed (defined as the mother’s stated plan to provide any breast milk to her infant), and low breastfeeding self-efficacy (defined as a baseline score of <46 on the Breastfeeding Self-Efficacy Scale–Short Form [BSES-SF]). Exclusion criteria were applied if their infant (1) had a gestational age less than 37 weeks, (2) had an Apgar score below 8 at 5 minutes, (3) had a birth weight less than 2500 g, (4) was admitted to the special care infant unit for more than 48 hours after birth, (5) was admitted to the neonatal intensive care unit at any time after birth, or (6) had severe medical conditions or congenital malformations. The primary analysis was conducted from July 1 to 31, 2024, with final data analysis completed by December 31, 2025.

This study followed the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) reporting guideline and was registered at ClinicalTrials.org. Ethics approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Board of the University of Hong Kong, Hospital Authority Hong Kong West Cluster, and the Central Institutional Review Board. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. The study protocol (Supplement 1) was previously published.11

Randomization and Masking

A research assistant (M.S.L.C.) screened and recruited participants in the postnatal wards of the 4 public hospitals. An independent researcher (Y.F.C.) generated the randomization sequence using simple randomization in Stata software, version 16 (StataCorp LLC). Participants were assigned in blocks of 4, at a 1:1 ratio, to either the intervention group (who received home-based peer support) or the control group (who received standard care). Treatment allocation was concealed from the researcher who performed the randomization. This was an open-labeled trial. Due to the nature of the counseling intervention, it was not possible to blind the participants or the peer supporters. Outcome assessors (the research assistants conducting the follow-up questionnaires) were also not blinded to the treatment allocation, as the same research team managed participant scheduling and outcome assessment.

Usual Care

The standard postpartum hospital stay was 3 days at all sites, with access to lactation consultants as needed. Prior to discharge, all participants received a standard breastfeeding information leaflet produced by the Hong Kong Department of Health. Within the first week after discharge, a routine postnatal checkup was provided for all mothers at a government Maternal and Child Health Centre. All participants in both the intervention and control groups received identical schedules of research outcome assessments conducted via telephone or online survey at 1, 2, 4, and 6 months post partum.

Intervention

Participants in the intervention group received usual postnatal care plus a structured peer support program delivered via video calls. The intervention protocol, including the timing and number of sessions, was informed by a meta-analysis of layperson-based interventions in promoting exclusive breastfeeding12 and consultations with community-based lactation experts. The goal was to design a feasible, scalable intervention that provided support at critical transition points in the early postpartum period.

The protocol stipulated a minimum of 2 sessions. The first session was scheduled for approximately 10 days post partum. This timing was chosen to provide support shortly after the standard 1-week routine postnatal check-up, when early challenges (eg, latching difficulties, perceived insufficient milk supply) are common and immediate professional follow-up may be limited. The second session at 1 month post partum, coincided with the end of the traditional month of confinement (ie, a traditional Chinese postpartum practice where a new mother stays indoors and follows a strict regimen of rest and specific nutrition for a month to recover from childbirth and replenish her health) and a common transition where mothers may return to work or resume more household responsibilities, which can disrupt breastfeeding routines. Each session followed a structured, 30-minute protocol focused on active listening, confidence reinforcement, collaborative problem-solving for specific challenges, and guidance to local resources. An additional session was available on request for ongoing or emergent issues. Peer supporters were recruited and trained by the Natural Parenting Network, a nonprofit breastfeeding support organization. Eligible criteria for peer supporters required at least 4 months of personal breastfeeding experience, completion of a minimum of 16 hours of classroom training with 2 supervised practicums, and a passing score on a final examination. The curriculum covered essential topics: (1) the importance of breastfeeding, (2) ensuring a good start to breastfeeding, (3) assisting mothers with breastfeeding, (4) communication skills, (5) common breastfeeding problems, (6) maternal diet and hygiene, (7) maternal illness and needs, and (8) local support resources and the role of peer counselors. Additionally, all peer supporters received supplementary training from a lactation consultant midwife focused on delivering support effectively via video call. Adherence to the intervention protocol was as follows: 142 participants in the intervention group (63.4%) received the first session, 120 (53.6%) received the second, and 1 received an extra session. Common reasons for nonadherence, reported by participants, including feeling too busy or feeling that breastfeeding was well-established. Fidelity checks confirmed that 92% of conducted sessions adhered to the core protocol components.

Data Collection

At the time of recruitment, demographic data (including maternal age, educational attainment, marital status, employment, infant feeding intention, and baseline breastfeeding self-efficacy) were collected via a self-reported questionnaire. Obstetric and neonatal medical data were extracted from medical records. For participants in the intervention group, a research nurse conducted additional telephone follow-up using a predesigned questionnaire to assess infant feeding status and experiences with peer support.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was the proportion of infants who were exclusively breastfed at 6 months post partum, as this is the duration recommended by the World Health Organization (WHO). This outcome was assessed as part of a comprehensive set of feeding status measures taken at 1, 2, 4, and 6 months. Following the WHO definition for cross-sectional surveys, exclusive breastfeeding was defined as the infant receiving only breastmilk (including expressed milk) in the 24 hours preceding the interview, allowing for the exception of medicines, oral rehydration solution, and vitamins, drops, and/or syrups.13 This definition captures feeding practices at the time of each assessment, allowing for the possibility that mothers could transition between feeding states (eg, from supplementing to exclusive breastfeeding) during the study period. Feeding status was classified using a pre-established system that included questions on the introduction of solids, other fluids, and the cessation of breast milk feeding. This classification system has been used and reported extensively in previous breastfeeding studies.14

Secondary outcomes included the prevalence of exclusive breastfeeding at 1, 2, and 4 months post partum; the prevalence of any breastfeeding at all time points; and maternal psychosocial measures, including breastfeeding self-efficacy, depressive symptoms, and infant feeding attitudes. Breastfeeding self-efficacy was assessed at baseline and at 2 and 4 months post partum using the Chinese version of the 14-item BSES-SF.15,16 This instrument uses a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (not at all confident) to 5 (always confident). Total scores range from 14 to 70, with higher scores indicating greater breastfeeding self-efficacy. Based on the validated Hong Kong Chinese version, a cutoff score of 46 (after rounding up to the nearest integer) was used to indicate low self-efficacy.16

Maternal depressive symptoms were evaluated at baseline and 1 and 2 months post partum using the Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale (EPDS).17 The EPDS is a 10-item self-report scale where each item is scored from 0 to 3, yielding a total score from 0 to 30. Higher scores indicate more depressive symptoms. A standard cutoff score of 13 or greater was used to indicate a high risk of postnatal depression.

Infant feeding attitudes were assessed at baseline and 2 and at 4 months post partum using the Iowa Infant Feeding Attitude Scale (IIFAS).18 The IIFAS is a 17-item instrument that measures maternal attitudes toward infant feeding. Items are scored on a 5-point Likert scale, producing a total score between 17 and 85; higher scores indicate a more favorable attitude toward breastfeeding.18

In addition to these primary and secondary outcome measures, data on weaning and return to work were collected when applicable. Return-to-work data included the timing of return, weekly working hours, and details about expressing breast milk at the workplace. Weaning data included the infant’s age at weaning and the reasons for weaning.

Statistical Analysis

The primary hypothesis was that the intervention would increase the proportion of infants exclusively breastfed at 6 months post partum. Sample size estimation was calculated to detect an increase of 15 percentage points (from 26.3% to 41.3%)18 with 80% power (α = .05), requiring 442 participants after accounting for 30% attrition.

Descriptive statistics summarize baseline characteristics. Between-group differences in breastfeeding rates were assessed with χ2 tests. Adjusted odds ratios (AORs) for exclusive and any breastfeeding at each time point were estimated using multivariable logistic regression, controlling for study site, maternal educational attainment, postpartum employment, breastfeeding intention, partner’s infant feeding preference, professional support, and delivery mode.

Generalized estimating equations (GEE) with an exchangeable working correlation structure assessed longitudinal trajectories of exclusive and any breastfeeding rates, adjusting for covariates and within-participant correlation. All models used robust standard errors and included study site fixed effects to account for within-site clustering; cluster-robust methods were not used due to the limited number of sites (n = 4).

Maternal psychosocial outcomes (EPDS, BSES-SF, and IIFAS scores) were analyzed longitudinally using linear GEE. Cross-sectional mean differences at each time point were estimated using adjusted linear regression. Two prespecified post hoc analyses examined associations between program engagement and breastfeeding outcomes (eMethods 1 and 2 in Supplement 2).

All analyses were conducted based on intention to treat (ITT). Analyses for each outcome were conducted using all available data at the relevant time point. All analyses were conducted using Stata, version 16 and R, version 4.5.2 (R Project for Statistical Computing). All statistical tests were 2 sided, with P < .05 indicating statistical significance.

Results

A total of 1468 participants were assessed for eligibility between November 5, 2021, and July 31, 2023, of whom 442 mother-infant dyads (mean [SD] maternal age, 32.4 [4.0] years) were randomized to the intervention (n = 224) or control (n = 218) group. The mean (SD) maternal age was 32.4 (4.2) years in the intervention group and 32.3 (3.9 years) in the control group. Baseline maternal and infant characteristics were comparable between the 2 groups, with no statistically significant differences in demographic characteristics, infant feeding preferences, or other baseline measures (Table 1).

Of the 1468 individuals initially assessed, 680 did not meet the inclusion criteria, 340 declined to participate, and 6 refused without providing a reason (Figure). All 442 randomized participants were included in the primary ITT analysis. Fourteen participants (10 in the intervention group and 4 in the control group) provided no outcome data at any follow-up and were considered lost to follow-up. Follow-up rates for breastfeeding outcomes were high at all assessment points. At the 6-month follow-up, 192 women (85.7%) in the intervention group and 197 women (90.4%) in the control group completed the final assessment, resulting in a total of 53 who did not complete.

Depressive Symptoms (EPDS)

No statistically significant between-group differences in EPDS scores were observed at any postpartum time point in either the overall GEE model (group effect: β = 0.28 [95% CI, −0.52 to 1.08]; P = .49) or in cross-sectional analyses at baseline (β = 0.31 [95% CI, −0.52 to 1.13]; P = .46), 1 month (β = 0.37 [95% CI, −0.56 to 1.29]; P = .44), or 2 months (β = 0.11 [95% CI, −0.81 to 1.03]; P = .82) post partum (Table 4). EPDS scores did not change significantly over time in either group (β per month = −0.15 [95% CI, −0.51 to 0.20]; P = .40), and there was no significant group × time interaction (β = −0.07 [95% CI, −0.58 to 0.44]; P = .78).

Breastfeeding Self-Efficacy (BSES-SF)

Baseline BSES-SF scores were similar between the intervention (mean [SD], 37.23 [6.61]) and control (mean [SD], 36.92 [6.60]) groups. In the overall GEE model, BSES-SF scores increased significantly over time (β per month = 0.69 [95% CI, 0.15-1.23]; P = .01), and the rate of increase was significantly greater in the intervention group than in the control group (time × intervention interaction: β = 1.01 [95% CI, 0.21-1.81]; P = .01), indicating a diverging trajectory of self-efficacy over time. The overall group effect across all time points was not statistically significant (β = 0.21 [95% CI, −1.05 to 1.46]; P = .75).

Cross-sectional analyses at individual time points showed no significant between-group difference at 2 months (β = 2.18 [95% CI, −0.36 to 4.71]; P = .09). By 4 months post partum, however, the intervention group had significantly higher BSES-SF scores than the control group (mean [SD], 45.03 [12.87] vs 40.66 [11.30]; adjusted β = 4.65 [95% CI, 1.60-7.70]; P = .003), indicating a clinically meaningful and statistically significant improvement in breastfeeding self-efficacy (Table 4).

Infant Feeding Attitudes (IIFAS)

Baseline IIFAS scores were comparable between groups (mean [SD], 60.10 [5.14] in the intervention group vs 60.36 [6.08] in the control group). In the overall GEE model, IIFAS scores declined significantly over time (β = −0.37 [95% CI, −0.61 to −0.13] per month; P = .002), with no significant group × time interaction (β = 0.16 [95% CI, −0.19 to 0.52]; P = .37) and no significant overall group effect (β = 0.20 [95% CI, −0.78 to 1.17]; P = .69). Cross-sectional analyses showed no significant between-group differences at 2 months (β = 1.05 [95% CI −0.27 to 2.37]; P = .12) or 4 months (β = 0.85 [95% CI, −0.82 to 2.53]; P = .32) (Table 4).

Additional Analyses

The results of the mediation analysis are provided in eTable 1 in Supplement 2; the post hoc analysis of intervention session completion, eTable 2 in Supplement 2; and the dose-response analysis, eTable 3 in Supplement 2. Post hoc analyses within the intervention group suggested a dose-response association, with participants who completed more peer support sessions showing significantly higher breastfeeding rates and self-efficacy scores. Furthermore, a formal dose-response analysis demonstrated a clear gradient, where receiving both early intervention sessions was associated with the highest odds of exclusive breastfeeding at 2 and 4 months.

Discussion

In this randomized clinical trial, a video call–based peer support intervention significantly increased the rate of exclusive breastfeeding at 2 months post partum. The effect, an absolute percentage point increase of 8.0 (AOR, 1.10 [95% CI, 1.01-1.19]; P = .03), suggests the support effectively helped mothers remediate early supplementation. This validates a scalable, hybrid support model aligned with WHO goals. However, the intervention’s effect on the primary outcome—exclusive breastfeeding at 6 months post partum—was not sustained, and no significant effects were observed on any breastfeeding or maternal depressive symptoms. The attenuation of benefit may relate to waning intervention intensity and the evolving challenges of later infancy, such as returning to work, which the support was not specifically designed to address.

A key mechanism for early breastfeeding success was a significant, sustained improvement in maternal breastfeeding self-efficacy, a known covariate associated with breastfeeding duration. This suggests the intervention empowered mothers with practical skills and confidence.19 Future strategies should investigate optimized timing and intensity of support to prolong the positive effect in exclusivity.

The success of the intervention in improving exclusive breastfeeding at 2 months is a finding of both clinical and public health importance. The early postpartum period is a critical window during which mothers face substantial challenges to exclusive breastfeeding, including perceived insufficient milk supply, latch difficulties, and fatigue. Our results demonstrate that timely, video-based support from trained peers can help mothers navigate these challenges. The public health impact of this finding is to validate a scalable, hybrid modality that merges the visual, practical advantages of in-person support with the accessibility of remote delivery.

The intervention’s effectiveness at 2 months suggests that scalable peer support programs, as implemented in this study, can be a powerful tool to establish exclusivity during its most vulnerable phase. The significant effect observed specifically at 2 months, rather than showing a sustained decline from birth, reflects our assessment of feeding practices in the 24 hours prior to each survey. This definition, aligned with WHO guidelines, captures the dynamic reality of infant feeding. It allows for the critical scenario wherein a mother, after early challenges lead to formula use, may—with timely support—overcome those hurdles and re-establish exclusive breastfeeding. The intervention’s effect at 2 months, therefore, likely represents a successful remediation of early supplementation, demonstrating that the peer support was effective not merely in preventing the introduction of formula but in actively helping mothers discontinue it to achieve exclusivity. This finding underscores the value of problem-solving support in reclaiming exclusive breastfeeding as a public health goal.

Furthermore, the high and comparable rates of any breastfeeding in both groups throughout the study suggest the intervention’s primary effect was to help mothers maintain a higher standard of breastfeeding (exclusivity) during the initial high-risk period. Achieving sustained exclusivity to 6 months likely requires more intensive, prolonged, or differently timed intervention strategies that address the complex logistical, social, and physiological barriers that emerge after the immediate postpartum period. Future research should investigate the optimal dose and timing of support to extend the positive effect of exclusivity observed at 2 months. The absence of an effect on any breastfeeding is consistent with other studies.9,20 The high baseline rates in both groups (exceeding 90% at 1 month), likely created a ceiling effect, leaving little room for improvement. Similarly, the lack of a significant effect on maternal depressive symptoms is consistent with other trials focused on feeding support rather than broad mental health intervention,21 suggesting that impacting clinical depressive symptoms may require a more targeted psychological approach.

These findings have important implications for postpartum care. While targeted support interventions can enhance exclusive breastfeeding in the early months, their effect appears time-limited unless reinforced. Achieving sustained breastfeeding outcomes likely requires multilevel strategies, including ongoing support from health care professionals, family and community engagement, workplace accommodations, and policies that extend beyond the initial postpartum phase.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be considered. First, this was an open-label trial. Due to the nature of the counseling intervention, it was not possible to blind the participants or the peer supporters. Furthermore, the research assistants conducting the outcome assessments were not blinded to group allocation. This lack of blinding could have introduced expectation bias, as participants aware of receiving support may have been more motivated to report positive outcomes, and assessors may have been influenced by knowledge of group assignments. As no widely accepted sham comparison exists for breastfeeding support interventions, this limitation is inherent to the intervention type. Second, the generalizability of our findings may be limited, as mothers who were more determined to breastfeed or who faced fewer initial challenges may have been more likely to participate. Third, self-reported breastfeeding practices are susceptible to social desirability bias, which could have led to an overestimation of breastfeeding rates. Fourth, the pragmatic nature of the intervention, which refers to its implementation in a clinical setting and reliance on clinical adherence, resulted in lower than anticipated session attendance; 63.4% of the intervention group completed the first call and 53.6% completed the second. Common reasons for nonadherence reported by participants included feeling too busy or feeling that breastfeeding was well-established. This reflects that the challenges of implementing such support may have attenuated the observed intervention effect. Finally, the timing of the first support session (scheduled for approximately 10 days post partum) may have been too late for mothers who experience immediate, significant challenges after discharge, potentially limiting the intervention’s impact for this subgroup.

Conclusions

In this randomized clinical trial, a structured video call–based peer support intervention did not significantly increase the rate of exclusive breastfeeding at 6 months post partum, but it did increase the rate at 2 months post partum. This early success represents a critical achievement in helping mothers establish exclusive breastfeeding—a key covariate associated with longer-term breastfeeding duration. The intervention also provided a lasting benefit by improving maternal breastfeeding self-efficacy. These findings inform public health policy, indicating that integrating accessible peer support into standard postnatal care is a viable strategy to improve early exclusive breastfeeding rates. Future implementation should focus on optimizing such programs to provide more adaptable and sustained support, aiming to maintain this critical practice through the first 6 months of life.
