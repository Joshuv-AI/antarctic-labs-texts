Machine learning based prediction of the performance and emission characteristics of CRDI diesel engine using diethyl ether and carbon nanotube additives with Spirulina platensis as a third-generation biofuel
Authors: Venkata Ramana Menda, Rakesh Kumar Tota, Joga Rao Bikkavolu, H. Ravi, Gandhi Pullagura, S. M. Dasharath, Debabrata Barik, Milon Selvam Dennison, Ayyar Dinesh, Saravanan Rajendran
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Alternative fuels are required to provide the world’s energy demands due to excessive fossil fuel use, harmful petrol emissions, environmental pollution, growing demand, rising costs, and fossil fuel degradation. The additives are utilized in biodiesel-diesel blends because they partially meet the physicochemical and thermal properties, resulting in improved combustion, performance, and reduced emissions. This research aims to enhance performance metrics and minimize emissions by incorporating diethyl ether (DEE) and single walled carbon nanotube (CNT) nanoparticles (NPs) into a Spirulina platensis (SP) microalgae-based biodiesel-diesel blend at various injection pressures (IPs). The prepared blends are homogeneous and consistent, as analyzed by characterization using FTIR, XRD, FESEM, EDX, and Raman spectrum analysis. The prepared samples are tested on a two-cylinder, four-stroke, Common Rail Direct Injection (CRDI) diesel engine. The findings revealed that the Brake Thermal Efficiency (BTE) is improved by 15% while the Brake Specific Fuel Consumption (BSFC), Hydrocarbon (HC), Carbon Monoxide (CO), Nitrogen Oxide (NOx), and smoke opacity are reduced by 13.5, 20.7, 39.5, 20.6, and 9.7%, respectively, for the TF + CNT50 sample at higher IP and Brake Mean Effective Pressure (BMEP). The fuel sample TF + CNT50 was shown to be more sustainable and suitable for use in multi-cylinder, CRDI diesel engines without engine modifications. Furthermore, Machine Learning (ML) methods such as Support Vector Regression (SVR), Random Forest (RF), and Decision Tree (DT) are used for accurately predicting engine performance and emission characteristics by analyzing the correlations between input and output data. Simulating these interactions improves engine design and reduces experimentation costs.

Introduction

The higher thermal efficiency, higher load-bearing ability, and durability of compression ignition (CI) engines are significant characteristics to consider in various sectors, including industries, transportation, and power generation. The rising need for transportation, new strategic initiatives, and an expanding automotive sector have all contributed to increased fuel consumption. Right now, fossil-based fuel resources, particularly in transportation, account for around 80% of fossil fuels used. The International Energy Agency (IEA) predicts that the crude oil demand will reach 99 million barrels per day by 2035, with an anticipated yearly growth of 2.1% of coal reserves1,2. Petroleum-based fuel combustion emits hazardous pollutants that have a substantial negative impact on the environment and humans. Due to fuel scarcity and environmental concerns, the pursuit of alternative fuels has expedited. CI engines are used to generate energy from petroleum fuels and release significant amounts of hazardous pollutants. Numerous qualitative studies have been conducted to enhance performance characteristics and minimize pollutants3.

The synthesis of free fatty oils to yield a maximum amount of alternative fuels derived from renewable sources has grown significantly in response to growing energy needs and the numerous challenges associated with fossil fuels. These challenges include environmental damage, climate change, and the depletion of petroleum supplies. Several investigations are being performed to develop an alternate fuel source that promotes sustainability over the long run. Biodiesel is a popular choice due to its unique properties. These include biodegradability, non-toxicity, renewability, and the ability to release less pollutants4.

Researchers discovered that the biodiesel is a potential and sustainable energy source that emits fewer harmful substances from its exhaust than diesel, with the exception of NOx5,6. Biofuel is often regarded as the superior alternative to fossil-based diesel fuel. Biomass produces biofuels. Plant-based and plant-derived substances, such as agricultural products and trees, grasses, wood and wood residues, aquatic species, animal waste, municipal wastes, and other residual materials, are the principal sources of the feedstock for biomass. Because of numerous capacities, microalgae are a viable feedstock for preparing methyl ester. Algal oil production may yield 9000–57,000 L of biofuel per acre, rather than 2000–2500 L of jatropha biodiesel. India generates between 450 and 500 million tons of biomass per year. India now consumes just 32% of biomass turned into primary energy. According to Energy Alternatives India, 63 million hectares of wasteland are being cultivated for jatropha, making it one of the world’s largest jatropha growing locations. The remaining barren ground is used for algae production. The algae can cultivate in salt water, wastewater, and freshwater. Microalgae produce 1,368,000 L of oil per acre, whereas jatropha generates just 1892 L per acre7,8.

According to Amaro et al.9, microalgae absorb huge quantities of Carbon Dioxide (CO2) and supply around 40–50% of the oxygen in the environment, which helps in sustaining biological life on Earth. The alleged benefits of using microalgae as a biofuel feedstock are that they grow fast, absorb more CO2 from the air, can grow in saltwater and marine environments, and wastewater, and oil production is approximately 20 times better than terrestrial seeds.

A recent investigation utilized microalgae biodiesel in CRDI engines, offering a wide range of novel ways. Arunprasad et al.10 used a 20% microalgae biodiesel-diesel mix, and ruthenium oxides to increase CI engine performance and minimize emissions. Tayari and Abedi et al.11 used biofuel blends derived from Chlorella vulgaris microalgae and hydrogen gas in CI engines to minimize CO, HC, and CO2 emissions while improving performance. Many investigations are carried out using nano-assisted fuel blends to enhance the engine performance and reduce emission parameters because they have the potential to show a significant impact on combustion parameters12–15. The addition of NPs shows enhanced spray jet momentum, which further results in superior secondary atomization as shown in Fig. 1a. Though the metallic NPs show better performance, combustion, and reduced pollutants, they emit toxic emissions into the environment as combustion residues, which reflect on severe health hazards to humankind. Thus, the non-metallic organic materials like GNPs, CNTs, and GQDs are used in recent investigations due to their non-toxic nature in the exhaust, higher thermal conductivity, and superior heat transfer capabilities. Many scholars used CNTs as a catalyst in biodiesel-diesel blends to expedite combustion processes and thus lead to improving performance parameters and mitigating emissions2. Moreover, these NPs possess catalytic activity during combustion and decompose into CO2 at elevated temperatures (> 650℃) as the possible chemical reaction is depicted in Fig. 1b16. In a study, Alenezi et al.17 explored that the addition of CNTs in Biodiesel-diesel mix reduced BSFC, CO, HC, and NOx when compared to diesel fuel. In another investigation, Rajpoot et al.18 concluded that the dispersion of CNTs in three generations of biodiesel-diesel blends resulted in improved performance and reduced emission parameters except NOx emissions.

Researchers explored oxygenated alcohol additives in Biodiesel-diesel blends on a CI engine. The higher-level alcohol DEE was found to be potentially out of numerous alcohol additives due to various benefits, including greater volatility, less viscosity, higher cetane number, superior calorific value, more oxygen content, and non-corrosive nature compared to other alcohols19–21. In a study, Sakthivadivel et al.22 stated that the DEE, ethanol-enriched alcohol, is used as an additive along with neem-based biodiesel, and BTE is improved by 7.25, and BSFC is reduced by 6.7%. Many scholars stated that the alcohol additives improve the evaporation and auto-ignition qualities, while NPs possess catalytic activity which improves performance, and their superior heat transfer capabilities reduce cylinder temperature, thereby decreasing NOx and other emissions12,23. The inclusion of non-metallic NPs and higher level oxygenated alcohols is found to be more popular in recent investigations since the small quantities of those additives in biodiesel-diesel blends enhance physio-chemical properties and result in improved performance and reduced emissions in CI engines24.

A few researchers identified various strategies to improve the engine performance and lower the emissions, which include varying engine input parameters such as injection timing, IP, and compression ratio. Out of these strategies, varying IP is found to be a more beneficial way of improving the engine’s overall performance over a wide range of viscous fuels/fuel blends since it is easy to vary the IP25,26. High viscous fuels may not be easily formed into fine particles and have difficulty in evaporation and atomization, which leads to poor performance, where the increasing IP strategy shows benefit for effective combustion. In a study, Rao et al.25 Reported that the addition of NPs in yellow oleander based B20 fuel sample at higher IP (250 bar) resulted in improving performance (BTE is increased by 12.31%, and BSFC is decreased by 22.85%), and reduced emission parameters (CO, UHC, NOx, and smoke are decreased by 15.38, 23.2, 12.5, and 5%). In another study, Mendonca S. et al.27 concluded that the inclusion of Al2O3 NPs (at a concentration of 25, 50, and 75 mg/l) in simarouba oil-based biodiesel-diesel mixture resulted in enhanced performance and lowered emission parameters under higher IP (250 bar).

Modern developments in predictive modelling and optimization techniques for CI engines have heavily relied on ML approaches to estimate performance and emission parameters. While traditional statistical models provide useful insights, they frequently assume linearity and require predefined functional associations between variables, which may not accurately capture the complex, nonlinear behavior of engine performance and emissions characteristics as impacted by biodiesel mixtures and nanotechnology. ML algorithms, on the other hand, can simulate complex, multidimensional relationships without making explicit assumptions about data distribution. Machine learning techniques are used to evaluate the overall performance of engines and the emissions characteristics of different fuels. For example, Yıldırım et al.28 examined the predictive efficacy of SVR and ANN techniques with regard to engine reaction to various hydrogen gas fuels. With a mean difference of 53.70%, they discovered that ANN had a lower overall Mean Absolute Percentage Error (MAPE) than SVR. In another study, Krishnasamy et al.29 trained an RF Regressor on 324 data points from experiments, resulting in an R2 score of 0.997 and an 85:15 training-testing split. The model accurately forecasted engine performance, and Lagrangian optimization was used to identify the ideal input parameters: 12.48 Nm torque, 8.29 L/min biogas flow, 72.8% methane, and 68.3 °C intake temperature, which maximized BTE and reduced emissions. In another investigation, Sanjeevannavar et al.30 studied many ML methods, including XGBoost, RF, Decision Tree, and Linear Regression, for predicting engine performance. XGBoost beat other models with R2 = 0.999, RMSE = 0.540, and MAE = 0.292, indicating greater accuracy. In a similar study, Yang et al.31 investigated the prediction capabilities of ANN, SVR, and RF for forecasting Indicated Mean Effective Pressure (IMEP) utilizing input parameters such as spark timing, engine speed, and load. ANN and SVR performed well, with R2 indicating performance with actual engine data.

Novelty of the current investigation

The current study aims to determine the performance and emission parameters of a twin cylinder, 4-stroke, CRDI diesel engine using Ternary Fuel (TF) (10% of algae-based biodiesel blended in 80% of diesel with 10% of DEE). The SP. microalgae is used to prepare biodiesel using the chemical transesterification process. During the initial stage of experimentation, the CRDI CI engine is run with diesel fuel and validated with Diesel R-F simulation software. In the further stage, the nano-dispersed (at a quantity of 25, 50, and 75 mg/l) TF samples are used on the CRDI CI engine under variable IPs (500, 600, and 700) to test the performance and emission characteristics. Till now, no research has been conducted using CNTs and DEE additives, including biodiesel-diesel samples on a CRDI diesel engine to improve performance and mitigate emission parameters. Therefore, the present study aims to focus on the suitability and sustainability of nano-assisted TF blends on the CRDI diesel engine to determine the performance and emission characteristics. Further, this study extends to validating ML models through comparative analysis using SVR, RF, and DT in accurately forecasting the engine performance and emission parameters by analyzing relationships between input and output parameters from experimental data. This integrated approach not only provides a deeper understanding of NPs and DEE-assisted biodiesel performance and emission parameters but also establishes a scalable methodology for optimizing alternative fuels with CNTs and DEE using data-driven tools. Ultimately, this research contributes to the development of cleaner, more efficient diesel engine technologies, offering a promising pathway toward sustainable and intelligent fuel systems.

Materials and methodology

In recent years, microalgae have become a more popular feedstock for biodiesel preparation. Many scholars discovered that microalgae are freely available in the environment and contain a good amount of oil. SP has 60% of protein and 10 to 25% lipid content by weight. The SP microalgae dried powder is supplied by a local vendor in India. It is an autotrophic single-celled microorganism from blue- blue-green algae species that can grow in both salt and fresh water. The solvents methanol (99.5%), DEE (99%), n-heptane (99%), White colour KOH pellets (85%), and surfactant QPAN 80) used in the current study are provided by Molekula BioKemix Limited, Hyderabad, India.

Oil extraction and biodiesel production

Algae oil is washed using petroleum ether with reflux in glassware. Oil extraction is carried out by the Soxhlet apparatus. The dried algae powder, 0.25 kg, was taken into the Soxhlet apparatus with petroleum ether. The cycle was started for 48 h at 60 °C. Around 60 ml of oil is extracted by this method. The extracted oil was heated using a rotating evaporator at 70 °C, at which petroleum ether is evaporated. The purified oil is then used for the transesterification process2.

In the transesterification process, 250 ml of methanol is mixed with 18 g of Potassium hydroxide KoH in a 2 neck round bottom flask, which is placed in the heating mantle. The heater is set at 65 °C and the stirrer speed is maintained at 200 rpm for preparing the methanol and KOH solution homogeneous. After 20 min, the purified oil (1000 ml) is slowly mixed in the solution and heated at 65 °C, and 200 rpm for 2 h. Then, the oil containing long chain molecules are broken into glycerol and short chain esters. Glycerol is separated from the esters using separating funnel. The separated ester is washed several times and dried. The maximum biodiesel yield is found to be 97.2% using the below Eq. (1)19. Preparation of fuel blends

The first sample is Diesel fuel, which is taken as the reference fuel and termed as D100. The second sample is B20, which contains 20% of Biodiesel (from SP microalgae) and 80% of diesel. The third sample is TF, which is a combination of 80% of diesel, 10% of SP microalgae biodiesel, and 10% of DEE. The remaining samples are TF + CNT25, TF + CNT50, and TF + CNT75, in which CNTs at a dosage of 25, 50, and 75 mg/l are dispersed in the TF sample. To prepare the nano assisted TF samples, initially, the NPs are functionalized with surfactant (QPAN80) at different dosages, such as 1:1, 1:2, 1:3, 1:4, and 1:5 in the presence of hexane solvent using probe sonicator (Hielscher ultrasonicator, at 40 kHz, and 160 W) for 30 min each. After sonification, these test samples are allowed to dry for 24 h. The best results were obtained with 1:4 NPs to surfactant ratio due to homogeneous dispersion. The same optimum ratio is followed to prepare TF + CNT25, TF + CNT50, and TF + CNT75 samples employing an ultrasonicator for 10–15 min. The prepared samples are tested for physio-chemical properties, and the results are given in Table 1. These samples are tested on twin cylinder, CRDI, direct injection, CI enigne for four times to reduce the errors causing in the measurements. The specifications of CNTs are given in Table 2.

Characterization of carbon nanotubes and biodiesel blends

FTIR spectroscopy was observed between 4000 and 500 cm− 1 and was used to identify the functional groups of the CNTs. Figure 3a. displays the synthetic CNTs’ FTIR spectra following full acetone drying. The presence of distinctive vibrations of the hydroxyl (O–H) group was established by the wide band peak at 3443 cm− 1. The stretching band (–C==O) of the spectrum was visible due to the abrupt bend at wave number 1726 cm− 1. The asymmetric stretching mode of -CH3 is represented by the absorption peaks at 2935 cm− 1, whereas the symmetric stretching vibrations of –CH2 are ascribed to the peaks at 2858 cm− 1. Therefore, the existence of several functional groups, including –C==O, –CH2, –CH3, and OH group, in the synthesized CNTs is confirmed by the FTIR data19. Each of these functional groups has the potential to contribute to adhesion. The adhesion characteristics of the CNTs and algae biodiesel mixes may be influenced by each of these functional groups. The X-ray diffractometer was used to assess the crystalline phase and structural morphology of the synthesised CNTs using 1.54060 Å Cu-Kα radiation with nickel chromator in the 2θ range from 0° to 90°32. Using Scherer’s formula, the average crystalline size was determined to be 35 nm. The synthetic CNTs’ X-ray pattern is shown in Fig. 3b. The Joint Committee of Powder Diffraction Standards, or JCPDS, is used to index each peak in the diffraction pattern. The crystalline nature of CNTs with the lattice plane of (002) and (100) was validated by the significant peak at 2θ = 26.3. The cylindrical shape of graphite implanted to form a nest-like structure is indicated by the presence of nanotubes, which was verified by FESEM.

Experimental setup

The decrease the exhaust gas temperature, a calorimeter is attached to the exhaust system. A temperature sensor, pressure sensor, in-cylinder pressure transducer, an engine load sensor with DAQ, and an eddy current dynamometer are used to collect the data from the experiment during engine testing. An engine software is installed on the PC to analyze the tested data. A multi gas analyzer (MARS Technologies Inc., MN-05), and a smoke meter (model: SM-05) are used to measure the exhaust gases and smoke. The technical details of the engine are provided in Table 3.

Machine learning predicting tools

ML techniques have become increasingly valuable in modelling and predicting complex nonlinear relationships in internal combustion engine systems. For selecting a suitable machine learning model, the regression technique must align with the complexity of the input–output relationships. In this study, Support Vector Regression (SVR), Random Forest (RF), and Decision Tree (DT) algorithms were implemented to predict engine performance and emission characteristics using experimental data. The SVR model employed a radial basis function (RBF) kernel, as it effectively captures nonlinear interactions. Hyperparameters were optimized through a grid search, where the penalty parameter C was varied between 1 and 100 and the kernel coefficient γ between 0.01 and 0.1. For the RF model, 100 decision trees were used, and the maximum depth was adjusted in the range of 10–30 to balance prediction accuracy and avoid overfitting. To ensure reliability in training and testing, the dataset was randomly partitioned into 80% training and 20% testing subsets, and 5-fold cross-validation was applied during model training for hyperparameter optimization and validation. The target outputs included BSFC in kg/kWh, BTE in %, CO% emissions, HC in ppm emissions, NOx in ppm, and Smoke Opacity (or Smoke Density). Each of these output variables was modelled individually using all three ML techniques to assess their predictive capabilities and generalization performance. The input features considered in the modelling process were BMEP (bar), IP (bar), and Fuel blends, which significantly influence the combustion and emission behaviour of engines. Experimental data were collected and subjected to preprocessing steps, including standardization (to zero mean and unit variance) and normalization (scaling to a fixed range, typically 0 to 1) to ensure feature uniformity and improve model convergence. The dataset of experiment details is given in Table 6. The dataset was then divided into training and testing subsets. Each ML model was trained on the processed data, optimized using cross-validation, and evaluated based on statistical performance metrics such as the coefficient of determination (R2), Mean Absolute Error (MAE), and Mean Squared Error (MSE), as defined in Eqs. (4)–(6). The preferred hyperparameters were chosen based on their ability to consistently deliver high R2 values and low error metrics across validation folds. Once finalized, these hyperparameters were used to retrain each model on the full training dataset before final evaluation against the independent test set (20%). Results and discussions

Performance characteristics

In the present study, the performance and emission parameters of a diesel engine are evaluated with D100, B20, TF, TF + CNT25, TF + CNT50, and TF + CNT75 samples under an injection pressure of 700 bar, which are indicated in Table 6. The optimized results are obtained with higher IPs for the TF + CNT50 out of all the remaining blends that are discussed below.

Brake thermal efficiency (BTE)

The effect of BTE vs. BMEP for the tested fuel samples at higher IP (at 700 bar) is shown in Fig. 7a. The BTE values depend on various factors such as engine load, calorific value, viscosity, density, and IP, etc. The increase in IP increases the cylinder pressure and temperature, resulting in better air-fuel mixing, easy evaporation and atomization, and complete combustion36. Thereby, increasing BTE. The BTE values are found to be 31.9, 30.6, 32.8, 33.5, 35.2, and 34.2% for D100, B20, TF, TF + CNT25, TF + CNT50, and TF + CNT75 fuel mixtures, respectively, at increased IP. The BTE is decreased for the B20 blend due to the lower calorific value. While the BTE value for the TF sample is increased due to lower viscosity with the addition of DEE, and higher energy content. Whereas, the fuel sample TF + CNT50 results in enhanced BTE by 15% when compared to diesel at higher IP and BMEP. Therefore, the nano-assisted TF samples result in higher BTE due to increased calorific value and catalytic activity of NPs. The higher CNT NPs concentration decreases the BTE value due to improper dispersion, which causes sedimentation and agglomeration. These results are in line with the previous results revealed by Zhang et al.37. In a recent study, Suresh and Babu38 reported that the addition of CNTs in TF results in improved BTE by 8.02% which is attributed to higher energy level, catalytic effect of NPs, and increased IP.

Brake specific fuel consumption (BSFC)

The BSFC values specify the quality of engine efficiency by fuel consumed per unit brake power produced. The variation of BSFC values with reference to the BMEP for various fuel samples under higher IP is illustrated in Fig. 7b. The addition of DEE, CNTs, and higher IP results in decreased BSFC values, which in turn enhances atomization, combustion, and BTE39. The BSFC values are found to be 0.318, 0.34, 0.31, 0.309, 0.294, and 0.305 for D100, B20, TF, TF + CNT25, TF + CNT50, and TF + CNT75 samples under an IP of 700 bar and higher BEMP, respectively. The BSFC value is decreased by 13.5% for the TF + CNT50 sample at higher IP when correlated to the B20 sample, which is attributed to the correct proportion of DEE and CNT in the B20 sample, improved evaporation and atomization, better spray characteristics, increased heat transfer capabilities, higher surface to volume ratio, and greater thermal conductivity of NPs. Thus, resulting in enhanced combustion, higher BTE, and lower BSFC values. In an investigation, Saikia et al.34 explored the inclusion of TiO2 NPs in jatropha biodiesel and tested it on a CRDI diesel engine under variable IP and Injection Timing (IT). The results revealed that the increased IP, IT, and inclusion of NPs in biodiesel decreased BSFC by 4.02% due to better heat transfer capacity of NPs, higher surface to volume ratio, and oxygen content.

Emission parameters

Hydrocarbon (HC)

Partial or incomplete combustion in the combustion chamber leads to the production of unburnt HC emissions. Figure 8a presents the HC emissions vs. BMEP for various tested fuel samples at higher IP. It is observed from the figure that as the load increases, BMEP increases, and the air-fuel mixture induced is also increased, resulting in incomplete combustion at rich zones. HC emissions are found to be low for the TF + CNT50 sample compared to other NPs included in the TF sample due to higher IP and cylinder temperature, which led to complete combustion. On the other hand, the catalytic activity and heat transfer abilities of NPs lead to complete combustion. Further, HC emissions are decreased for the TF sample compared to the B20 sample, which is attributed to density, viscosity, oxygen availability, fuel atomization, shorter ignition delay, and rapid combustion25. Moreover, the HC emissions are found to be less for B20 as compared to D100, which is owing to the O2 molecules emitted by the biodiesel sample. The HC emissions are found to be 47.3, 46.5, 42.5, 39.4, 37.5, and 38.2 ppm for D100, B20, TF, TF + CNT25, TF + CNT50, and TF + CNT75 mixtures, respectively, at higher IP. The highest HC pollutant is reduced by 20.7% for the TF + CNT50 sample at an IP of 700 bar as compared to D100. In a similar article, Soudagar et al.40 concluded that the addition of Graphene Oxide (GO) NPs in Pongamia Pinnata biodiesel-diesel blends with 5% of DEE results in reduced HC emissions by 70.3% at higher IP (900 bar) which is ascribed to higher cetane number, lower viscosity and density, secondary fuel atomization, and micro explosion.

Carbon monoxide (CO)

Improper/abnormal combustion may lead to CO emission, which is a very common effluent. The effect of CO emissions with respect to BMEP for the tested fuel samples at higher IP is shown in Fig. 8b. It is clear from the graph that the CO emissions increase as BMEP increases. The CO emissions are converted into CO2 at a higher cylinder temperature. The CO emissions are found to be decreased with B20 and the inclusion of DEE in the B20 sample, which is due to the excess oxygen level in the fuel samples. Moreover, the nano-assisted TF sample further reduces the CO emissions due to improved rate of reaction and combustion properties with the addition of CNTs. The combustion process is improved by DEE due to its evaporation capacity. The CO emissions are observed to be 0.124, 0.11, 0.10, 0.094, 0.075, and 0.086% for D100, B20, TF, TF + CNT25, TF + CNT50, and TF + CNT75 fuel mixtures, respectively, at higher BMEP and IP. The maximum reduction in CO emissions is observed to be 39.5% for the TF + CNT50 sample when correlated to the D100 sample. In a similar study, Rao et al. (2024)25 concluded that the addition of CNTs at a dosage of 60 ppm in Yellow Oleander Biodiesel-diesel blends results in decreased CO emissions by 15.6% when compared to diesel at elevated IPs, which is due to the improved combustion properties with NPs and higher IP. In a similar investigation, Srinidhi et al.41 concluded that the inclusion of nickel oxide NPs in neem-based biodiesel-diesel sample reduced HC values by 71.4% at elevated IPs, which is due to secondary atomization, and catalytic activity of NP.

Nitrogen oxides (NOx)

NOx is emitted due to higher cylinder temperature and oxygen level in the fuel sample. Figure 9a shows the variation of NOx emissions with reference to the BMEP at higher IP. It is evident from the figure that the NOx emissions increase with a rise in BMEP. NOx emissions are found to be high for the B20 mix, which is owing to higher oxygen content and complete combustion. Whereas, NOx is reduced for the TF sample due to the quenching effect and evaporation rate of DEE. Moreover, the addition of CNTs in TF samples lowers the NOx values, which is owing to the greater heat absorption capacity of NP and the cooling effect of DEE. The NOx values are noticed to be 905, 920, 812, 792, and 730 for D100, B20, TF, TF + CNT25, TF + CNT50, and TF + CNT75 samples, respectively, at higher IPs. The NOx emissions are reduced by 20.6% for the TF + CNT50 sample over the B20 sample due to the synergistic effect of DEE and CNTs. In a similar study, Rao et al.2 stated that the inclusion of CNTs at a quantity of 50 mg/l in B20 blend results in decreased NOx values by 12.5% when correlated to diesel, which is ascribed to the higher thermal conductivity of nano additives and heat-absorbing capacity.

Smoke opacity

Smoke opacity is emitted due to a lack of oxygen in the combustion zone, a rich air-fuel mixture, poor evaporation of fuel, and incomplete combustion39. Higher IPs improve the spray properties, which decreases the fuel droplet size, thereby improving atomization and leading to better combustion. Figure 9b presents the variation of smoke opacity vs. BMEP for the tested fuel samples at higher IPs. The smoke opacity values are found to be 34.8, 33.9, 33.5, 32.8, 31.4, and 32% for D100, B20, TF, TF + CNT25, TF + CNT50, and TF + CNT75 samples, respectively, at higher IPs. The smoke emissions are lowered by 9.7% when compared to the D100 sample at higher IP, which confirms the fine particles spray in the combustion zone and leads to better atomization and comprehensive combustion. These results are well connected to the study carried out by Suresh and Babu38, which confirms that the addition of CNTs in the TF sample results in reduced smoke opacity by 3%.

Conclusions

The current investigation aims to determine the performance and emission parameters of a CRDI diesel engine using CNT nano additives dispersed in the TF sample under different IPs. The biodiesel yields 97.2% which is produced from SP Algae. A 20% of biodiesel is mixed with 80% of diesel. Then, TF is prepared by mixing 10% of biodiesel, 80% of diesel, and 10% of DEE. Finally, the CNTs at a dosage of 25, 50, and 75 mg/l are dispersed in the TF sample with the aid of a Hielscher ultrasonicator. These prepared samples are tested on a twin cylinder, 4-stroke, CRDI, diesel engine under variable IPs. The optimized results are observed at an IP of 700 bar, and the same are discussed above. Based on the above inferences, the following conclusions are given:

The physio-chemical properties of fuel samples are on par with the ASTM standards. The prepared samples are found to be stable and homogeneous which are evidenced by characterization (FTIR, XRD, FESEM, EDX, and Raman spectrum analysis).Moreover, the performance parameters are improved, such as BTE is increased by 15%, and BSFC is reduced by 13.5% respectively for the TF + CNT50 sample than the B20 sample at higher BMEP, and IP which is ascribed to fuel spray characteristics, increased calorific value, better evaporation, shorter ignition delay, and catalytic activity of NPs.Further, the emission characteristics including HC, CO, NOx, and Smoke are observed to be decreased by 20.7, 39.5, 20.6, and 9.7% for TF + CNT50 mix at higher load, and IP as correlated to D100 sample which is due to greater oxygen content, higher energy content, and higher surface to volume ratio of NPs. While NOx values are decreased for the TF + CNT50 sample by 20.6% when compared to B20 at higher IP, which is attributed to the heat absorption capacity of NPs, and the quenching and evaporative cooling effect of DEE.The RF accurately predicted the output parameters at higher IPs than the SVR and DT, as indicated by high R2 values of BTE and SFC, having 0.9737 and 0.9878, and emission-related predictions for CO, HC, CO₂, NOₓ, and smoke are consistently above 0.98, with lower RMSE and MAPE values for both performance and emissions.This approach accurately predicts engine efficiency and emission parameters for nano-assisted fuel samples in IC engines, saving time and money.
