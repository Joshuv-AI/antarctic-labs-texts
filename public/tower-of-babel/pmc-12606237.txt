Exploring the predictive power of iTBS induced prefrontal excitability using concurrent TBS/fNIRS
Authors: Rebecca L D Kan, Linsen Li, Teng Liu, Xue Pang, Chenxi Tan, Bella B B Zhang, Minxia Jin, Penny P Qin, Adam W L Xia, Xiaoyun Guo, Jack Jiaqi Zhang, Georg S Kranz
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe effect of a single session intermittent theta-burst stimulation (iTBS) on dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC) function remains uncertain. Additionally, the clinical relevance of predicting DLPFC function through iTBS-induced DLPFC excitability has not been investigated. To address these gaps, concurrent iTBS/functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS) was employed. Thirty healthy participants were recruited in this randomized, sham-controlled, crossover study. Working memory performance, a core function of the DLPFC, was assessed using 3-back task before and at five subsequent timepoints following iTBS. Prefrontal hemoglobin concentrations were measured concurrently by fNIRS throughout the whole procedure. No significant differences in post-stimulation 3-back task performance were observed between active and sham stimulation. Nevertheless, a significant decrease in iTBS-induced oxygen-hemoglobin (HbO) change was observed in the active group compared to the sham group. However, iTBS-induced HbO changes did not significantly predict working memory performance at any timepoint. These results reveal that a single iTBS session of the left DLPFC results in significant immediate cortical excitability changes. However, these changes do not translate into improved working memory compared to sham stimulation and iTBS-induced prefrontal excitability does not appear to predict subsequent working memory performance following a single session iTBS. Nevertheless, the potential predictive power of iTBS-induced prefrontal excitability in symptom changes of rTMS for depression is still worthy of an investigation given that such symptom changes are thought to result from rTMS effects. Introduction

The n-back task is one of the most commonly used working memory paradigms that reliably induces dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC) activation1. Hoy and colleagues examined the effect of a single-session intermittent theta-burst stimulation (iTBS) of the left DLPFC on subsequent n-back task performance at three timepoints (0, 20 and 40 min post-iTBS). Their findings revealed an enhancement in working memory capacity, peaking approximately 20 min after iTBS when compared to sham stimulation2. Moreover, this improved task performance was accompanied by increased parietal neural excitability, as measured by electroencephalography (EEG)2. This finding is interesting as it informs about the temporal specificity of the effects of left DLPFC iTBS. However, results by Hoy and colleagues are in contrast to two other studies that failed to find a memory-enhancing effect by single-session iTBS beyond practice effects3,4. This discrepancy underscores the need for further replication studies that clarify the impact of a single session iTBS on working memory performance. Moreover, to our knowledge, no study has examined so far whether iTBS-induced immediate prefrontal excitability can predict post-stimulation working memory performance. Such investigation would be fundamental to establish the predictive potential of prefrontal excitability for prognostic means in psychiatry.

Corticomotor excitability in response to transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS) of the primary motor cortex is an established prognostic biomarker of motor recovery following neurological impairment5,6. However, in psychiatric disorders, non-motor regions of the frontal cortex are of greater relevance. The DLPFC in particular, is considered the primary target region for therapeutic repetitive TMS (rTMS) in psychiatry7. Despite its importance, TMS-induced cortical excitability of the DLPFC has been understudied, largely due to the lack of a reliable and convenient tool for simultaneous measurement. Concurrent TMS/functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS) presents an ideal approach to measure TMS-induced immediate prefrontal excitability. The hemoglobin concentration change measured by fNIRS reflects neural excitability following the neurovascular coupling (NVC) phenomenon. While fNIRS does not provide a direct measure of neural activation, it is a reliable and widely used method for assessing brain activity. The concurrent TMS/fNIRS method is particularly suitable because the far-infrared light used in fNIRS does not interfere with the magnetic field generated by TMS. In previous concurrent TBS/fNIRS studies8,9, we validated changes in prefrontal hemoglobin oxygenation (HbO) as a reliable neurophysiological marker assessing the effects of iTBS over DLPFC. However, the potential of concurrent TBS/fNIRS for predicting mental functions and behavior remains to be shown10.

Here, we applied concurrent iTBS/fNIRS to the left DLPFC to test whether single-session iTBS increases n-back task performance and its associated prefrontal response, as well as whether iTBS-induced prefrontal excitability can predict such performance. We hypothesized that: (1) the stimulation significantly improves working memory performance and associated hemodynamic changes (i.e., HbO), as compared to sham stimulation; and that (2) iTBS-induced immediate prefrontal excitability (e.g., increased HbO levels) predicts subsequent working memory performance.

Results

Characteristics of participants

Thirty healthy participants were recruited for this randomized, cross-over experiment. None of them had undergone TMS prior to this study. Demographic characteristics are presented in Table 1. All working memory performance data (active: 30; sham: 30) were included in the analysis. However, some fNIRS data were excluded due to lower frequency noise or poor signal quality (active: 5; sham: 5). Consequently, 50 sets of hemoglobin concentration data (active: 25; sham: 25) and 60 sets of behavioral data (active: 30; sham: 30) were included in the final analysis.

Can prefrontal excitability induced by a single-session iTBS predict working memory performance?

Simple linear regression analyses were conducted to assess whether iTBS induced HbO change predicted 3-back performance after the stimulation. However, no significant effects were observed at any timepoint in either group.

Discussion

In the current study, we examined the memory-enhancing effects of a single session iTBS in healthy individuals and the potential ability of iTBS induced immediate prefrontal excitability to predict working memory performance. We observed that (1) active iTBS led to a significant immediate brain excitability change compared to sham stimulation. However, this did not result in a better working memory performance compared to sham iTBS, or any significant task-related neurophysiological change after the stimulation; (2) the HbO change during iTBS did not predict working memory performance after the stimulation.

Different from our hypothesis 1, no memory enhancement effects or cognitive-related hemodynamic effects of a single-session iTBS were observed in our study. Instead, we observed an improvement in the 3-back task performance over time in both groups which can be attributed to practice effects. This is consistent with previous research demonstrating practice effects in both iTBS and sham stimulation groups11. For true iTBS effects, studies have shown that while continuous TBS may suppress practice effects, iTBS may not significantly modulate it12. This may explain the non-significant difference between active and sham groups in our study. Furthermore, our experiment exclusively employed the 3-back task, which may have limited our chance to observe an effect. That is, we may have missed stimulation effects on working memory performance that would have otherwise revealed itself in a less difficult version of the n-back task (i.e., 2-back). Indeed, previous studies indicate that single-session iTBS effects are related to the cognitive load, such that iTBS was shown to enhance performance in the 2-back task but not in the 3-back task2,12,13. However, we decided to limit the task to the 3-back version given our previous pilot measurements that indicated strong ceiling effects with the 2-back version in healthy participants. Another possible explanation is the “enhancement ceiling effect” proposed by Hoy and colleagues14. This concept suggests that in healthy individuals, particularly young, well-educated participants, rTMS may not produce significant improvements in cognitive performance because these individuals are already performing at or near their maximum potential. As a result, there is limited room for observable enhancement, and the effects of rTMS may be less pronounced due to this performance ceiling.

Concerning the stimulation effects on task-related hemoglobin concentration changes, our findings differ from previous studies, which reported changes in electrophysiological markers even in absence of behavioral changes3,15. We observed no significant group differences in task-related hemodynamic responses after stimulation. This discrepancy may be due to our experimental design, which involved changes in the positioning of participants after the stimulation, necessitating the use of individual phase baselines to calculate task-related hemoglobin changes instead of utilizing the overall baseline.

In our study, contrary to our hypothesis, a significant decrease in prefrontal HbO was observed during active iTBS compared to both the pre-stimulation n-back task and sham iTBS. This finding differs from the conventional prefrontal hemodynamic change (i.e., increased HbO) typically observed during cognitive tasks or after several pulses of TMS16,17. One possible explanation for this discrepancy is the use of subthreshold stimulation intensity (80% of the resting motor thresholds, rMT). By definition, subthreshold stimulation does not induce action potentials but modulates the neuronal response threshold by influencing synaptic activity, which in turn requires energy consumption18. This energy demand may lead to a decrease in HbO and an increase in HbR, as observed in our study. This could explain the observed reduction in prefrontal HbO during active iTBS compared to the pre-stimulation n-back task. Another possible explanation is that iTBS induces an immediate reduction in metabolic activity within the stimulated region, as suggested by previous studies19,20. Additionally, prior research has shown that TBS appears to influence both oximetric measures and autonomic-dependent responses21. The reduction in metabolic activity and the modulation of related responses could account for the decreased HbO observed in our study. Notably, the pattern of increased HbR and decreased HbO during TBS was also observed in an independent cohort from another concurrent iTBS/fNIRS study conducted by our group9.

Regarding hypothesis 2, our results revealed that immediate TMS-induced prefrontal excitability failed to predict subsequent working memory performance. One possible explanation is the small sample size, which may have resulted in low statistical power, limiting the ability to detect a true effect. Another explanation relates to our initial finding that a single-session iTBS did not improve working memory performance compared to sham stimulation. Instead, the observed improvements in working memory performance were likely due to practice effect. This suggests that while iTBS may alter cortical excitability, these changes do not necessarily result in a change of working memory performance. Nevertheless, despite the lack of a predictive relationship in this context, the potential role of iTBS-induced prefrontal excitability in clinical applications, such as predicting treatment response in rTMS for depression, remains an important area for future investigation. Unlike cognitive performance, which may be influenced by multiple factors such as practice effects and ceiling effects, changes in depressive symptoms may be more directly linked to the cumulative neuromodulatory effects of TMS. Future studies with larger sample sizes and longitudinal designs are needed to further explore this relationship.

There are several limitations in this study. Firstly, the absence of baseline hemoglobin measurements before iTBS and before the first post-iTBS n-back task may have contributed to high variability in hemoglobin concentration changes during these two conditions. Secondly, participants’ position change—lying down during iTBS and sitting during the n-back tasks—(see methods section) necessitated the use of individual baselines before each stimulus rather than an overall baseline, potentially affecting the consistency of measurements. Thirdly, the exclusive use of the 3-back task in our experiment may have prevented us from observing iTBS memory enhancement effects. Furthermore, the included participants were all college students with an average length of education over 17 years. This demographic characteristic may have resulted in more pronounced practice effects in the n-back task, limiting the generalizability of our results to other populations. Lastly, the sham method adopted in our study (rotating the coil), combined with the cross-over design, may inadvertently reveal the actual stimulation condition, potentially introducing bias into our results.

In summary, our study shows that a single iTBS session applied to the left DLPFC can induce immediate hemodynamic changes but does not result in relevant changes in behavioral performance or subsequent task-related neurophysiological changes when compared to sham stimulation. Additionally, our study indicates that iTBS-induced prefrontal excitability did not predict working memory performance after the stimulation. In the future, iTBS-induced prefrontal excitability should be investigated as a potential predictor of prefrontal function in studies with larger sample sizes.

Methods

Participants

Thirty healthy participants, who met TMS inclusion criteria as determined by a safety screening questionnaire by Rossi et al., updated in 2011, were recruited for this study22. Further inclusion criteria were right-handedness and an age between 18 and 60 years. Exclusion criteria were a current or past diagnosis of any neurological or psychiatric disorder, any serious medical conditions, seizure, or pregnancy. Written informed consent was obtained after explaining the study including all of its procedures to participants. Ethical approval was obtained by the Human Subjects Ethics Sub-Committee, Institutional Review Board, The Hong Kong Polytechnic University (HSEARS20200120005-03) and the study was performed according to the Declaration of Helsinki.

Study design

A randomized, double-blinded, repeated-measures cross-over design was utilized. Each participant attended two sessions, separated by at least 7 days23 (see Fig. 2b). The sequence of stimulation conditions (active and sham iTBS) was counterbalanced and pseudo-randomized across participants using a computerized randomization method. Working memory was assessed using 3-back task, and was performed before the stimulation, immediately after the stimulation, and at 10, 20, 30, and 40 min after the stimulation respectively (six timepoints in total). Each 3-back task lasted for two minutes, leaving an 8-min resting period in between tasks. Hemoglobin concentrations were measured concurrently by fNIRS during the whole procedure (see Fig. 2a). The entire process was performed in a quiet room. Individual resting motor thresholds (rMT), were determined prior to the experiment, defined as the intensity to elicit a motor evoked potential (MEP) with peak-to-peak amplitude ≥ 0.05 mV in 5 out of 10 trials in the contralateral first dorsal interosseous (FDI) muscle. Throughout the procedure, participants sat in a chair, which was reclined during the stimulation period to minimize displacement of the coil.

Theta burst stimulation

iTBS was applied using a 70-mm diameter figure-eight coil (Cool-B65) and a transcranial magnetic stimulator (MagPro X100, MagVenture, Denmark). We followed the original iTBS protocol as described by Huang et al.24: 3-pulse bursts with 50 Hz intra-burst frequency for 20 trains, each train containing 10 bursts delivered at 5 Hz and followed by an 8-s rest (600 pulses in total). A TMS-neuronavigator system (Localite GmBH, Bonn, Germany) was used to identify the left DLPFC with MNI coordinates (x = − 38, y = + 44, z = + 26)25. An intensity of 80% individual rMT was applied2. For sham iTBS, the coil was rotated 90 degrees with the lateral side of the coil placed over the target26.

Working memory: 3-back task

The letters A to J were pseudo-randomly presented on a screen for 2000 ms each. Participants were required to press ‘m’ if the presented letter was the same as the letter presented 3 trials earlier whereas they were required to press ‘n’ if the presented letter was not the same as the letter presented 3 trials earlier (See Fig. 2c). Each task block consisted of 60 trials with 25% targets4, hence, each task block lasted for 2 min27. If participants pressed the button more than once, the reaction time recorded was based on the last button press4. Before each 3-back task, the experimenter placed the computer in front of the participant and provided a verbal countdown (“3, 2, 1”) to signal the start of the tasks.

Concurrent iTBS-fNIRS configuration

We used a frequency-domain fNIRS system (OxiTs; ISS Inc, Champaign, Illinois, USA) to monitor absolute hemoglobin concentration changes including oxy-hemoglobin (HbO) and deoxy-hemoglobin (HbR) during the entire assessment (see Fig. 1a): A rubber sensor consisting of 8 emitters was placed at the left DLPFC, navigated by the neuronavigator system, and fixed at the target site using a sterile head bandage. The TMS coil was put on top of the rubber sensor with the coil’s center positioned above the midpoint of the rubber sensor in between the detector and emitters. Coil and rubber sensor were separated by a 3D printed polylactic acid “bridge” as the fNIRS signal is sensitive to the pressure exerted by the TMS coil. The applied intensity was adjusted accordingly given the increased coil to scalp distance. For more details, see Kan et al.9.

Outcome measures

Behavioral outcomes

Working memory performance was quantified using d prime (d′), a discriminability index measuring the ability to correctly identify targets and to minimize false alarms in n-back tasks28. d′ is calculated with the formula: d′ = ZHit–ZFA, where Hit represents the hit rate (proportion of hits when a signal is present: hits/(hits + misses)), while FA represents the false-alarm rate (proportion of false alarms when a signal is absent: false alarms/(false alarms + correct negative))28. We also measured reaction time (RT), including mean hitRT (targeted trials) and mean negRT (correct rejection RT of nontargeted trials) for hits only where responses occurred prior to onset of the next stimulus.

Neuroimaging outcomes

Hemodynamic changes (ΔHb), including oxgen-hemoglobin (ΔHbO) and deoxgen-hemoglobin change (ΔHbR), in the left DLPFC during iTBS (Tstimulation) as well as during 3-back tasks at each phase (Tpre, T0, T1, T2, T3, T4) were assessed. Hb changes during each 3-back task were calculated by subtracting the mean of 2 min of resting hemoglobin values before each 3-back task but omitting the last 10-s immediately before the task to eliminate the effect of preparation. Hemoglobin changes during iTBS were determined by subtracting at least 10 s of absolute baseline data collected before stimulation. Similarly, ΔHb during the T0 task was calculated by subtracting the absolute baseline data obtained before T0 (Information on the mean baseline duration for calculating hemoglobin changes during the iTBS and T0 task can be found in Supplementary 1.1).

Statistical analysis

To test Hypothesis 1, a generalized linear mixed-effects model was conducted using group (active and sham groups) and timepoint (Tpre, T0, T10, T20, T30, T40) as fixed factors, participants as random factor, and n-back performance (i.e. d’, RT) as well as hemoglobin changes (i.e. ΔHbO, ΔHbR) as outcomes. Notably, when analyzing hemoglobin changes, an additional timepoint (Tstimulation) was included as a fixed timepoint factor (seven timepoints in total). The initial analysis focused on the main effects of group and timepoint, as well as their interaction. For these overall effects, the significance level was set at p < 0.05. For post hoc pairwise comparisons, the significance level was adjusted and was set at 0.05/5 = 0.001 to account for multiple comparisons regarding post-stimulation 3-back task performance and associated hemoglobin changes between the two groups. Additionally, group differences in iTBS-induced hemoglobin changes were examined separately, regardless of whether the main effect of group was observed. The significance level for iTBS-induced hemoglobin change between the active and sham groups was set at p < 0.05.

To test Hypothesis 2, Linear regression analysis was conducted to predict working memory performance using iTBS induced prefrontal excitability (i.e., ΔHbO, ΔHbR during iTBS). Prior to the linear regression analysis, potential outliers were identified as data points falling outside the range of ± 2 standard deviations from the mean (for details, see Supplementary 2.1). To account for multiple regression analyses associating post-stimulation 3-back task performance with iTBS-induced hemoglobin changes, the significance level was adjusted to 0.05/5 = 0.001.
