PIFC-SABiLSTM: physics-inspired feature constraint with self-attention bidirectional LSTM for interpretable fault diagnosis of rolling bearings
Authors: Wanjun Qin, Jie Ma, Qiao Peng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Rolling bearings are critical components of rotating machinery. The operational status of these bearings directly impacts the stability and safety of the entire system. However, conventional data-driven models frequently demonstrate deficiencies in generalization and robustness when detecting early, subtle fault features. The present paper puts forth a novel approach to fault diagnosis, namely PIFC-SABiLSTM (Physics-Inspired Feature Constraint with Self-Attention BiLSTM). PIFC-SABiLSTM is a data-driven model that incorporates physics-inspired feature constraints with a self-attention mechanism and a bidirectional long short-term memory (BiLSTM) network. This integration aims to address the limitations of conventional data-driven models, particularly their poor generalization and limited robustness. This approach is predicated on the transformation of the statistical patterns of rolling bearing vibration characteristics during steady-state operation into differentiable and physically feasible domain constraints. A Physics-Inspired Feature Constraint (PIFC) layer is constructed, where a projection operator guides the learning process of the network to conform to actual vibration physics and suppress the generation of non-physical features. This approach effectively decouples and fuses multi-scale time-frequency features by integrating the global dependency modeling capability of self-attention mechanisms for periodic impact patterns with the bidirectional temporal capture capability of Bidirectional Long Short-Term Memory (BiLSTM) for transient events. The experimental findings on the MFPT (Mechanical Faults Prevention Technology) bearing dataset demonstrate that the proposed method achieves 98.70% accuracy and an F1 score exceeding 98%, thereby significantly outperforming existing mainstream methods. This research proposes a novel “data-mechanism” synergistic optimization approach for high-precision, intelligent fault diagnosis in complex industrial environments. The approach demonstrates significant practical value for engineering applications.

Introduction

Rolling bearings are critical components widely used in rotating machinery. The operational status of bearings directly influences the reliability, safety, and service life of entire equipment systems. In the context of complex operating conditions, such as those encountered in wind power generation1, aircraft engines2, and high-speed locomotives3, bearings subjected to prolonged variable loads and high speeds are more vulnerable to localized damage, including fatigue spalling, cracks, and wear. If not promptly and accurately diagnosed, these early and subtle faults can trigger chain reactions leading to equipment performance degradation and catastrophic failures, causing significant economic losses and safety hazards. Consequently, investigating efficient and intelligent fault diagnosis technologies for rolling bearings is of substantial significance from both theoretical and engineering standpoints for achieving predictive maintenance and intelligent operation and maintenance in modern industrial equipment.

Conventional fault diagnosis techniques principally depend on the manual extraction of time-frequency domain characteristics (e.g., Fourier transforms, wavelet packet decomposition, and envelope spectrum analysis4–6. These are then combined with threshold discrimination or with classical classifiers such as Support Vector Machines (SVM) and Random Forest (RF) for decision-making7–9. While these approaches do offer a certain degree of interpretability, they remain heavily dependent on expert judgment and reveal limited capability for feature extraction when confronted with non-linear, non-stationary signals. This makes them unsuitable for complex variable operating conditions.

However, extant research in this area is not without its limitations. Purely data-driven methods based on Transformers, Self-Attention, BiLSTMs, and the like, while achieving high diagnostic accuracy, exhibit limited generalization capabilities and lack mechanism-based explanations when encountering new operating conditions or equipment. Current physics-based methodologies (e.g., PINN) predominantly depend on accurate partial differential equations or dynamic models. However, in the context of rotating machinery fault diagnosis, factors such as contact nonlinearity, time-varying stiffness, and complex loading render accurate modeling exceptionally challenging, thereby limiting the direct application of PINN. The majority of studies are based on single sensors or single-modality data, which limits the ability to fully leverage multi-source heterogeneous information for physics-guided cross-modal fusion.

In order to address the aforementioned challenges, this paper proposes a novel fusion framework for rolling bearing vibration fault diagnosis—PIFC-SABiLSTM (Physics-Inspired Feature Constraint with Self-Attention-BiLSTM). The fundamental innovation of this method is rooted in its approach, which diverges from the conventional reliance on explicit differential equations. Instead, it extracts statistical characteristics from a substantial corpus of real-world data collected during the operational phase of rolling bearings. These characteristics are then structured into soft constraints and embedded into the deep learning training process via a PIFC layer. This strategy preserves the “mechanism-guided” essence of PINN while overcoming the practical bottleneck of unknown equations. On the basis of this foundation, a dual-path temporal decoder is developed, comprising a self-attention mechanism and a BiLSTM network. The former captures periodic impact features through global dependency modeling, enabling adaptive focus on critical frequency components related to faults. The latter extracts transient event evolution patterns via bidirectional temporal learning, fully characterizing the dynamic processes before and after fault occurrence. The two mechanisms achieve fine-grained decoupling and deep integration in the feature space, forming a collaborative optimization paradigm where “data-driven approaches take precedence while physical laws provide supplementary guidance.” This enhancement leads to a substantial improvement in the model’s robustness, generalization capability, and interpretability.

It is important to note that Physics-Informed and Physics-Inspired methods are related but not identical. Physics-Informed methods typically incorporate explicit governing equations, physical laws, or residual constraints directly into the optimization objective or network architecture, thereby ensuring that the learning process is regulated by known physical formulations. In contrast, Physics-Inspired methods do not necessitate explicit equations, but rather employ physical intuition, empirical statistical regularities, or mechanism-motivated prior constraints to guide representation learning. In this study, the proposed PIFC mechanism is constructed from the statistical characteristics of healthy-bearing features rather than from explicit dynamic equations or PDE residuals. Consequently, the proposed method is more appropriately categorized as Physics-Inspired rather than strictly Physics-Informed.

The primary contributions of this paper are threefold:

A physics-inspired feature constraint mechanism is proposed that does not require precise dynamical equations. This mechanism innovatively transforms statistical patterns into differentiable soft constraints.

A dual-path fusion architecture is designed that integrates Self-Attention and BiLSTM. This architecture achieves effective decoupling and collaborative modeling of multi-scale spatiotemporal features.

The proposed method’s significant robustness advantages under complex conditions are demonstrated through systematic experimental validation. These conditions include strong noise, small samples, and class imbalance.

Related Work

In recent years, the rapid advancement of deep learning technologies has provided novel approaches for rolling bearing fault diagnosis. Transformers and their core mechanism, self-attention, have emerged as a research hotspot in time-series signal processing due to their significant advantage in capturing long-range dependencies. Ding et al.10 designed specialized tokenizer and encoder modules to extract features from the time-frequency representation of vibration signals. The proposed model was a transformer-based time-frequency feature extraction model, which showed to achieve end-to-end learning for the purpose of bearing fault diagnosis. This approach demonstrated a substantial improvement in performance when compared to conventional Neural Networks (CNNs) and Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs) in terms of capturing long-range dependencies. Hou et al.11 proposed a multi-feature parallel fusion encoder combined with an enhanced Transformer. Multi-head self-attention was employed to extract local and global features. A cross-flipping decoder was designed to enhance feature coupling, and explicit FFT feature extraction was integrated to develop the Diagnosisformer model. This approach yielded diagnostic accuracies ranging from 99.84% to 99.85%, as evaluated using standard benchmark datasets. Pei et al.12 proposed a multi-layer attention stacking strategy, integrating a multi-head self-attention encoder with positional encoding alongside a CNN classifier to design a Transformer-based convolutional network. This approach demonstrated to exhibit exceptional stability and noise resistance in the context of rotating machinery fault diagnosis.

Bidirectional Long Short-Term Memory (BiLSTM) networks offer distinct advantages in time series modeling by concurrently processing forward and backward time sequences. Zhao et al.13 performed multi-scale feature extraction through convolutional layers of varying depths, utilizing BiLSTM layers to capture bidirectional temporal correlations of multi-scale spatio-temporal features. The introduction of an attention mechanism was undertaken to assign greater weights to key spatio-temporal features. The Multi-Scale CNN-BiLSTM with Attention Mechanism (MSCNN-BiLSTM-AM) network was constructed. This approach yielded an average accuracy of 99.89% across four distinct datasets and attained an accuracy of 88.27% by utilizing a mere 10% of the training data in the context of multi-scenario transfer learning. Song et al.14 integrated raw data with temporal features as model inputs, constructing a one-dimensional convolutional neural network. The researchers introduced an efficient channel attention mechanism to extract fault features and employed an improved particle swarm optimization algorithm to automatically optimize CNN-BiLSTM network hyperparameters, such as the learning rate and batch size. This approach was developed to design a source-domain diagnostic model, thereby facilitating the transfer of diagnosis across a range of operating conditions. The utilization of 100% samples resulted in an accuracy rate ranging from 99.88% to 99.92%. Following the implementation of transfer learning with a mere 10% of the training samples, the accuracy metric exhibited a range between 86.53% and 90.12%. Bian et al.15 employed a deep CNN with a wide first-layer convolution kernel (64 × 1) to capture short-time-frequency features analogous to Short-Time Fourier Transform (STFT). The utilization of BiLSTM was employed to extract global temporal dependencies and bidirectional features, with the objective of optimizing feature space distances through the implementation of a contrastive loss function. The construction of the Wide Deep CNN-BiLSTM (WDCNN-BiLSTM) model was based on a Siamese network architecture. This approach integrates metric learning with few-shot learning, attaining an accuracy of 83.47% with a mere 90 training samples while sustaining robust performance under noise interference with signal-to-noise ratios as low as −4 dB.

However, it should be noted that the aforementioned purely data-driven methods have inherent limitations in terms of generalization capability, interpretability, and data requirements. In order to address these shortcomings, Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs) and Physics-Inspired Feature Constraint (PIFC) methods have emerged. By incorporating prior knowledge, such as bearing dynamics models, fault feature frequencies, and degenerative physical laws, into neural networks, these approaches facilitate an organic integration of data-driven and mechanism-driven methods. Shen et al.16 incorporated bearing fault feature frequencies (Ball Pass Frequency of Inner race (BPFI), Ball Pass Frequency of Outer race (BPFO), Ball Spin Frequency (BSF), Fundamental Train Frequency(FTF)) and a physics-based threshold model into the training process. A customized data-physics-driven loss function that penalized physically inconsistent predictions was employed to design a physics-informed deep convolutional neural network. In comparison with purely data-driven methodologies, this approach led to a reduction in misclassifications that violate physical knowledge by over 50%, thereby significantly lowering the false alarm rate. Ni et al.17 generated structure-dynamics-dominated features through a physics-mode-dominant generation layer based on cepstral index filtering. They designed a domain transformation layer for variable-speed conditions and employed a parallel dual-channel residual learning architecture for automatic fault feature extraction. The Physics-Informed Residual Network (PIResNet) was constructed by directly embedding physical laws—such as modal characteristics of bearing vibration systems, cepstrum index filtering, and fault feature frequencies—into the network architecture. This approach demonstrated a remarkable degree of efficacy in non-stationary conditions, effectively addressing the dual challenges posed by sparse data and complex operating conditions in industrial applications.

Yucesan and Viana18 embedded a reduced-order physical model for bearing fatigue damage accumulation within an RNN unit structure. By modeling grease degradation mechanisms through neural networks and integrating a bearing fatigue damage accumulation model (L10 life equation) with data-driven learning, they constructed a hybrid physical-information neural network. This approach achieved wind turbine-specific re-lubrication interval optimization, fully leveraging the respective strengths of the physical model in ensuring predictions comply with fatigue laws and the data-driven component in learning complex degradation processes. Sun et al.19 encoded domain knowledge related to fault diagnosis into multimodal data (1D vibration signals and 2D thermal images) as discriminative information. The Physically-Inspired Multimodal Machine Learning (PMFN) method was proposed as a means of embedding representational patterns of rotating machinery faults within multimodal data by utilizing attention mechanisms. This approach provides a framework for constructing adaptive correlations across diverse sensing modalities by leveraging physical knowledge. The systematic incorporation of physical knowledge into machine learning models serves to bridge the disparity in physical meaning between multimodal fault information. This approach endows machine learning models with both robust data fitting capabilities and physical stability, thereby demonstrating superior reliability and robustness compared to purely data-driven methods in industrial gearboxes and bearing-rotor systems. Qin et al.20 embedded the bearing dynamics model directly into a neural network architecture. To this end, a physics-constrained boundary loss function was designed to determine parameter ranges and accelerate network convergence. A truth loss was constructed using physical principles to evaluate spectral differences between simulated and actual data. The proposed methodology entailed the utilization of physical laws as constraints to identify dynamics model parameters. This approach involved the implementation of an Inverse Physics-Informed Neural Network (Inverse PINN), in conjunction with digital twin technology, to ensure the generation of data that maintained physical consistency. The generation of high-quality bearing fault samples under various operating conditions and failure modes was successful, offering a novel approach to addressing data imbalance issues.

The utilization of physical information and physics-inspired methodologies confers considerable advantages over the employment of exclusively data-driven methodologies. The incorporation of physical prior knowledge facilitates effective diagnostics, even in scenarios involving limited samples or even zero samples, thereby substantially reducing reliance on substantial labeled data sets. The universality of physical laws across diverse operating conditions and systems enables enhanced generalization of models to scenarios that extend beyond the training set. The explicit causal relationships and mechanism explanations provided by physical models ensure the traceability and verifiability of diagnostic outcomes, thereby enhancing their acceptance and trust among engineers. Physical constraints guarantee that predictions adhere to the established laws of physics, precluding the generation of physically implausible outputs. These models effectively integrate physical simulation data and experimental data of varying fidelity, enabling the construction of more comprehensive and robust diagnostic models.

In addition to the aforementioned approaches, recent studies have further demonstrated that advanced artificial intelligence methods from associated industrial domains can provide valuable insights for intelligent fault diagnosis. Within the domain of industrial defect detection, knowledge distillation and domain adaptation techniques have been increasingly explored to enhance model efficiency and cross-domain generalisation. Wen et al.21 proposed a triple semantic-aware knowledge distillation (TSKD) network for industrial defect detection, which incorporates three refinements: dual-relation distillation (DRD), decoupled expert distillation (DED) and cross-response distillation (CRD) to achieve comprehensive transfer of industrial semantic knowledge. In the field of online industrial inspection, Wen et al.22 developed a lightweight rotated network for online pipeline weld defect detection in magnetic flux leakage (MFL) inspection systems. This study demonstrated that efficient model design can achieve robust real-time defect detection when deployed in industrial sites. Furthermore, Wen et al.23 introduced a wavelet-guided spatial–frequency prior network for robust defect detection in vision-based measurement systems. This study showed that incorporating wavelet-based prior information into feature extraction can enhance detection robustness. In the area of domain-adaptive object detection, Wen et al.24 proposed the FGPro framework that explores fine-grained visual–text feature alignment with prompt tuning for domain-adaptive object detection. The FGPro framework contains three elaborated levels: domain-adaptive prompts with a relation encoder, bidirectional cross-modal attention, and prompt-guided cross-domain regularization. This framework achieves notable improvements, including + 2.8% AP50 in industrial scenarios.

In the domain of intelligent fault diagnosis, recent research has emphasised the use of adaptive and attention-based deep architectures. Liang et al.25 designed a time–frequency spectral feature enhancement module (TFSFEM). The TFSFEM employs learnable weight parameters to perform quadratic convolution nonlinear transformation on the wavelet time–frequency map of the signal, thereby enhancing its ability to extract higher-order fault features. Moreover, a convolutional multi-scale attention fusion module (CMSAFM) has been developed. The CMSAFM introduces efficient multi-scale attention, which achieves precise focusing on key features through grouped feature interactions and adaptive weight allocation. The effectiveness and superiority of the model were validated using the self-built engineering application datasets and the PU dataset. Guo et al.26 developed a dual-channel feature aggregation network (DCNet) with an attention mechanism for wind turbine fault diagnosis under variable speed operating conditions, demonstrating that attention-guided dual-channel feature fusion can effectively extract discriminative features from vibration signals. Additionally, Liang et al.27 proposed an attention-augmented separable residual network (ASResnet) for enhanced fault diagnosis of rolling bearings, which employs separable convolution to reduce network parameters and a convolutional block attention module (CBAM) to focus on key feature regions, achieving improved diagnostic accuracy and reduced training time on both the CWRU bearing dataset and real engineering datasets. These studies collectively indicate that current research is evolving towards more adaptive, lightweight, and knowledge-guided intelligent diagnosis frameworks, providing important methodological references for the present work. Guo et al.28 proposed a time–frequency ensemble network (TFNet) to fully exploit multi-domain discriminative features under variable speed conditions. Experiments conducted on the WT-Planetary gearbox dataset, the XJTUSuprgear variable-speed gearbox dataset and the CWRU Bearing dataset demonstrate that the model exhibits high diagnostic accuracy and strong generalisation capabilities.

Method design and model architecture

In the context of high-noise environments, the operation of rolling bearings is often compromised by the superposition of high-frequency interference and background noise, which hinders the extraction of early and subtle fault features. Conventional deep learning models, devoid of physical prior constraints, are susceptible to local optima or overfitting. To address these challenges, this paper proposes the PIFC-SABiLSTM framework. The central tenet of this approach entails the dynamic calibration of the feature representations of Self-Attention and BiLSTM through PIFC, thereby facilitating a profound integration of “data-driven” and “mechanism-guided” methodologies. Adhering to the tenets of “feature decoupling—physical constraints—multi-scale fusion,” this framework establishes a novel paradigm for fault diagnosis that is both highly interpretable and robust.

The overall process proceeds as follows: The input data is segmented into fragments and fed into multi-scale spatio-temporal features. The data undergoes standardization via Z-score normalization and Min-Max normalization, followed by processing through Self-Attention and BiLSTM. Subsequently, the PIFC layer performs dynamic calibration, and after category weight training, the output is directed to classification decision-making. The PIFC layer functions as an intermediary between “physical prior knowledge and data learning,” thereby facilitating a profound integration of mechanism and learning.

The present study proposes a novel approach to calibrating the feature representations of Self-Attention and BiLSTM through the PIFC layer. This dynamic calibration process is hypothesized to result in a synergistic optimization of time-frequency and temporal features. Self-attention adaptively focuses on fault-related key frequency components by computing attention weights between sequence elements. BiLSTM captures bidirectional temporal dependencies in fault evolution through its forward-backward recurrent structure. This establishes a collaborative paradigm, which is defined by “feature decoupling—physical constraints—multi-scale fusion.” This collaborative paradigm enables closed-loop optimization of mechanisms and data.

Construction of a multi-scale time-frequency feature system

To address the non-stationary and multi-frequency coupling characteristics of vibration signals, this paper proposes a multi-scale time-frequency feature system that achieves a balance between information richness and engineering interpretability. This system serves as an input for the PIFC-SABiLSTM model. The system under consideration comprises 25 feature dimensions, which are further categorized into three distinct types:

1) Time-domain characteristics (10 dimensions): A consideration of the statistical properties of signal amplitude is necessitated, including the mean, the variance, the peak, the peak-to-peak, the skewness, the kurtosis, the waveform factor, the pulse factor, the margin factor, and the energy.

2) Frequency-domain characteristics (8 dimensions): It is imperative to capture spectral distribution characteristics, encompassing the following: spectral centroid, spectral broadening, mean square frequency, frequency variance, energy concentration, harmonic ratio, total harmonic distortion ratio, and dominant frequency band energy proportion.

3) the time-frequency domain features (7 dimensions): The integration of joint time-frequency information, encompassing energy entropy across the first three layers of wavelet packet decomposition, maximum amplitude and its corresponding frequency within the Hilbert envelope spectrum, and instantaneous amplitude standard deviation, is imperative.

This provides a robust foundation for the constraints observed in the PIFC layer. This enables the synergistic optimization of both the “physically feasible domain” and “feature representation.”

Physics-inspired feature constraint layer

Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs) are capable of achieving mechanism embedding through the incorporation of partial differential equation residual terms. However, in the context of rotating machinery fault diagnosis, the establishment of precise dynamic equations poses a significant challenge, thereby limiting their direct application. To address this, the present study proposes PIFC, whose theoretical foundation originates from the statistical patterns of vibration characteristics in rolling bearings under steady-state operating conditions. During steady-state operation, the vibration signals of healthy bearings can be regarded as stationary random processes, exhibiting remarkable stability in the distribution of their key features30.

In light of the aforementioned observations, this paper adopts the statistical center \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\mu\:}_{ref}=-0.23$$\end{document} and dispersion \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\sigma\:}_{ref}=0.27$$\end{document} of the normal state characteristics as the reference benchmark for the “physically feasible domain.” In this context, “physical” does not refer to explicit mechanical equations but rather to statistical physics, where the characteristic distribution of the normal state exhibits inherent stability and centrality. This stability is determined by the bearing’s healthy condition and thus serves as prior knowledge to guide model learning. In the event that the features learned by the model deviate from this baseline, it is indicative of potential faults or abnormal operating conditions. The implementation of a physical constraint loss function effectively mitigates the risks associated with overfitting and the generation of non-physical features.

The input feature sequence is defined as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:Z=[{z}_{1},{z}_{2},\dots\:,{z}_{T}]\in\:{R}^{d\times\:T}$$\end{document}, where d = 25 denotes the feature dimension and T represents the time step. The region that is both physically feasible and well-defined is delineated by the following equation:3\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\begin{array}{c}{{\Omega\:}}_{phys}=\{z\in\:{R}^{d}\mid\:\parallel\:{\mu\:\left(z\right)-{\mu\:}_{ref}\parallel\:}_{2}\le\:{\epsilon\:}_{\mu\:},{\parallel\sigma\:\left(z\right)-{\sigma\:}_{ref}\parallel}_{2}\le\:{\epsilon\:}_{\sigma\:}\end{array}$$\end{document}

In the formula, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mu\:\left(z\right)=\frac{1}{T}\sum\:_{t=1}^{T}{z}_{t}$$\end{document} represents the mean of the feature sequence, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\sigma\:\left(z\right)=\sqrt{\frac{1}{T-1}\sum\:_{t-1}^{T}{({z}_{t}-\mu\:)}^{2}}$$\end{document} is the standard deviation. These represent the mean and standard deviation vectors of the feature sequence, respectively. \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\mu\:}_{ref}=-0.23$$\end{document} is the reference mean for the normal state feature (derived from the normalized C3 samples in the training set), and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\sigma\:}_{ref}=0.27$$\end{document} is the reference standard deviation. \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\epsilon\:}_{\mu\:}=0.3$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\epsilon\:}_{\sigma\:}=0.1$$\end{document} serve as tolerance thresholds, controlling the degree of relaxation for mean and standard deviation constraints, respectively.

The PIFC layer maps latent features onto the physically feasible region via a differentiable projection operator \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{P}_{{\Omega\:}}:{R}^{d}\rightarrow{{\Omega\:}}_{phys}$$\end{document}, with its update process implemented through gradient descent:4\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\begin{array}{c}{P}_{{\Omega\:}}\left(z\right)=z-{\nabla\:}_{z}{L}_{phys}\end{array}$$\end{document}

The physical constraint loss function is:5\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\begin{array}{c}{L}_{phys}={\lambda\:}_{p}\cdot\:\left(E\left[{\Vert\:\mu\:\left(z\right)-{\mu\:}_{ref}\Vert\:}^{2}\right]+E\left[{\Vert\:\sigma\:\left(z\right)-{\sigma\:}_{ref}\Vert\:}^{2}\right]\right)\end{array}$$\end{document}

In the formula, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\mu\:}_{ref}=-0.23$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\sigma\:}_{ref}=0.27$$\end{document} and, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\lambda\:}_{p}$$\end{document}=0.0005 serves as the constraint strength coefficient. This design facilitates the network’s autonomous optimization of feature distributions during training, thereby suppressing the generation of abnormal amplitudes or outlier features. This ensures that outputs adhere to physical laws. This enhancement significantly improves the model’s robustness and generalization capabilities in high-noise environments.

The PIFC layer does not impose rigid constraints; rather, it guides network learning through the loss function, allowing for a certain degree of deviation (controlled by \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\epsilon\:}_{\mu\:}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\epsilon\:}_{\sigma\:}$$\end{document}). This soft-constraint mechanism has been demonstrated to preserve data-driven flexibility, thereby enabling the learning of feature representations for a variety of fault modes. Concurrently, it incorporates the stability of physical prior knowledge, ensuring that feature distributions do not deviate excessively from normal states. This constitutes one of the core innovations of the proposed method. During the training phase, when the features extracted by the network deviate from the statistical center of the normal state, the physical constraint loss, denoted by \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{L}_{phys}$$\end{document}, increases. Gradient descent is a process that automatically adjusts network parameters, thereby guiding the distribution of features toward the physically feasible domain. This approach fosters a dynamic equilibrium between “data-driven” and “mechanism-guided” learning.

To avoid information leakage, the reference statistics \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\mu\:}_{ref}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\sigma\:}_{ref}$$\end{document} are computed only from the normal samples in the training set after stratified train-test splitting, rather than from the full dataset.

Feature extraction and decoupling

Fault features in vibration signals manifest multi-scale periodicity. The Self-Attention mechanism is designed to adaptively capture global dependencies by computing attention weights between elements within the sequence. It is posited that the input features \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:X$$\end{document}are contained within the space \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{R}^{d\times\:T}$$\end{document}. The Self-Attention computation is carried out as follows:6\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\begin{array}{c}Attention\left(A,K,V\right)=softmax\left(\frac{Q{K}^{T}}{\sqrt{{d}_{k}}}\right)V\end{array}$$\end{document}

In this equation, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:Q={W}_{q}Z,K={W}_{k}Z\:\mathrm{a}\mathrm{n}\mathrm{d}\:V={W}_{v}Z$$\end{document} denote the query, key, and value matrices, respectively. \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{W}_{q},{W}_{k}{,W}_{v}\in\:{R}^{d\times\:d}$$\end{document} denote learnable weights, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{d}_{k}$$\end{document} is the scaling factor to stabilize gradients. The attention weight \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:softmax\left(\frac{Q{K}^{T}}{\sqrt{{d}_{k}}}\right)$$\end{document} quantifies the correlation between features at disparate positions in the sequence, thereby enabling the network to automatically prioritize key frequency components associated with faults.

This paper employs a multi-head attention mechanism (h = 5) to capture complex feature correlations across different representation subspaces by computing multiple sets of attention weights in parallel. This approach achieves cross-channel decoupling of local damage features.

The Self-Attention mechanism offers several advantages over traditional convolutional operations. It can directly model feature dependencies at arbitrary distances without requiring multi-layer stacking. Attention weights are dynamically computed based on the input, providing greater adaptability to different fault patterns. Attention weights for all positions in a sequence can be computed simultaneously, resulting in high training efficiency.

In the context of fault diagnosis, the bidirectional modeling capability of BiLSTM is evident in the gradual evolution of faults. Preliminary indicators may manifest hours or even days prior to the occurrence of the fault, thereby enabling the backward LSTM to retrace these initial features from the moment of fault initiation. Concurrently, the forward LSTM model captures the ensuing impacts of the fault, including secondary losses and resonance frequency shifts. The integration of bidirectional information facilitates the model’s comprehensive understanding of the entire lifecycle evolution patterns of faults.

The integration of these components enables the model to perform two functions: “global localization,” in which the Self-Attention component identifies the time window in which faults occur, and “local refinement,” in which the BiLSTM component captures the dynamic evolution of faults. This integration allows the model to effectively represent complex and variable fault patterns.

Loss function design and optimisation objectives

In order to achieve a balance between high-precision fault diagnosis and physical consistency, this paper designs an optimization objective based on a dual loss function. The total loss function is defined as:9\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\begin{array}{c}{L}_{total}={\lambda\:}_{1}{L}_{CE}+{\lambda\:}_{2}{L}_{phys}\end{array}$$\end{document}

In this equation, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{L}_{CE}$$\end{document} denotes the cross-entropy loss, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{L}_{phys}$$\end{document} represents the physical heuristic feature constraint loss, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\lambda\:}_{1}=1.0$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\lambda\:}_{2}=0.0005$$\end{document} serve as balancing coefficients, achieving a favorable equilibrium between classification accuracy and preservation of physical prior knowledge.

The weighted cross-entropy loss \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{L}_{CE}$$\end{document} is employed to mitigate sample imbalance, formulated as:10\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\begin{array}{c}{L}_{CE}=-\frac{1}{N}\sum\:_{i=1}^{N}{w}_{{y}_{i}}\mathrm{log}p\left({y}_{i}|{X}_{i}\right)\end{array}$$\end{document}

Where N denotes the total number of samples, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{y}_{i}$$\end{document} represents the true label of the i-th sample, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:p\left({y}_{i}|{X}_{i}\right)$$\end{document} signifies the model’s predicted probability. The weight \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{w}_{k}=\frac{N}{K\cdot\:{N}_{K}}\:\left(11\right)$$\end{document} is determined by the number of samples \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{N}_{k}$$\end{document} in category k, with K being the total number of categories. This strategy involves the allocation of greater weights to minority classes, thereby effectively mitigating the dominance of majority classes during the training process and enhancing the model’s capacity to recognize rare fault types.

The primary parameter settings were determined based on data-driven statistics, architectural compatibility, and empirical stability. Specifically, µ and σ were computed from the training set for Z-score normalization, while \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\mu\:}_{ref}$$\end{document} and\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\:{\sigma\:}_{ref}$$\end{document} were derived from the normalized C3 samples in the training set to characterise the physically feasible region. The physical constraint coefficient \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\lambda\:}_{p}$$\end{document} was set to 0.0005 to ensure that the physical prior serves as a soft regulariser without overwhelming the classification loss. The number of attention heads was set to h = 5 because the input feature dimension is 25, allowing an even partition of the feature space. In addition, two stacked BiLSTM layers were adopted to balance temporal feature extraction capability and model complexity.

In summary, the multi-scale feature system provides a priori support for physical feasible domain constraints. Self-Attention and BiLSTM, on the other hand, focus on decoupling spatio-temporal features and modeling temporal evolution, achieving spatio-temporal domain complementarity. The PIFC layer functions as a central processing unit, converting statistical a priori information into physical constraints, thereby ensuring a highly robust solution for strong noise environments.

Experimental verification and fault diagnosis analysis

The chapter’s objective is to validate the effectiveness of the proposed PIFC-SABiLSTM framework in rolling bearing vibration fault diagnosis. To this end, systematic experiments are conducted using the publicly available MFPT bearing dataset. The experiments illustrate inner ring and outer ring faults, as shown in Fig. 7. The proposed method is evaluated for its diagnostic performance under complex operating conditions by developing multi-scale time-frequency feature inputs and combining ablation experiments with comparisons against multiple advanced models. The experimental framework is designed to assess the model’s capacity to identify early-stage, subtle fault characteristics, its resilience to variations, and its generalizability in contexts where data is imbalanced.

The findings indicate that the full model exhibits superior performance in terms of accuracy and F1 score when compared to other variants, underscoring the significance of the synergistic interaction among Self-Attention, BiLSTM, and the physical constraint layer in enhancing diagnostic performance.

Subsequent to the elimination of the PIFC layer, the model’s accuracy exhibited a decline of 3.46%, accompanied by an augmentation in training fluctuations, as illustrated in Fig. 9. This finding suggests that the physical prior exerts a suppressive effect on the generation of non-physical features. The PIFC layer essentially functions as a soft regularization mechanism. Rather than relying on precise differential equations, it employs the empirically feasible domain derived from long-term operational data statistics as prior knowledge to guide the learning process. This “statistics-over-equations” approach successfully overcomes the bottleneck of traditional PINNs, which struggle to model mechanical systems, offering a novel pathway for embedding physical information in complex industrial scenario.

Instead, it utilizes the empirically derived feasible domain from long-term operational data as prior knowledge to guide the learning process. This approach of “substituting equations with statistics” successfully overcomes the bottleneck of traditional PINNs struggling to model mechanical systems, providing a new pathway for embedding physical information in complex industrial scenarios.

The accuracy for each category are compared in Fig. 10:

As demonstrated in Fig. 10, the PIFC-SABiLSTM model exhibits optimal performance, with a recognition accuracy of 98.70%, while the model lacking PIFC indicates a slight decline (95.39%). This finding serves to substantiate the hypothesis that the PIFC layer exerts a substantial inhibitory effect on misclassifications, achieved through the strategic constriction of the center of feature distribution. It is noteworthy that the accuracy of the model without BiLSTM exhibited a substantial decline, thereby underscoring the critical role of bidirectional temporal dependency modeling in feature extraction. The model lacking PIFC demonstrated a more modest decline in accuracy; however, it exhibited considerably elevated levels of training loss in comparison to the PIFC-SABiLSTM model (as illustrated in Fig. 9). This finding suggests that the primary function of the PIFC layer is to stabilize the training process and enhance model robustness through soft regularization mechanisms. This is in contrast to the direct boosting of feature expression capabilities. This robustness is particularly critical under conditions involving small sample sizes.

Conclusion

The present study addresses the challenges of extracting early-stage fault features and the insufficient generalization capability and poor robustness of models for rolling bearings in rotating machinery. The proposed framework is a deep sequence fusion diagnostic framework—PIFC-SABiLSTM—based on physics-inspired heuristic feature constraints. This approach diverges from the conventional PINN by eschewing the utilization of precise dynamic equations. Instead, it embeds empirical statistical patterns from rolling bearings under steady-state operation as physical prior knowledge into the network training process, thereby establishing a collaborative optimization mechanism centered on data-driven learning supplemented by physical principles. This design achieves an organic integration of mechanism knowledge and deep learning without requiring explicit modeling of system dynamics. Consequently, this design significantly enhances the model’s robustness and interpretability under complex operating conditions.

The experimental findings on the MFPT bearing dataset revealed that the proposed method achieved an accuracy rate of 98.70% for identifying three typical operating conditions (normal, inner ring failure, and outer ring failure), with F1 scores exceeding 98% across all cases. Its performance significantly outperformed mainstream methods such as Support Vector Machines (SVM), Random Forests, CNN-LSTM, and Self-Attention. This advantage is attributable to the PIFC layer’s proactive suppression of non-physical feature generation and noise interference through physical constraints. Concurrently, the architecture integrating Self-Attention and BiLSTM achieves precise decoupling and collaborative modeling of periodic impacts and transient times. By substituting partial differential equations with statistical patterns, it overcomes the bottleneck of traditional PINNs, which struggle to model complex mechanical systems without precise dynamic equations.

While the method outlined in this paper has yielded notable outcomes on the MFPT dataset, further improvements are needed for practical industrial applications.

Firstly, the cross-condition generalization capability is examined. The present PIFC layer’s physical feasible domain reference is predicated on statistical patterns that are particular to specific cross-conditions. However, these patterns may prove ineffective in scenarios encompassing diverse loads, speeds, or equipment. The exploration of adaptive physical constraint mechanisms is crucial for the dynamic adjustment of reference benchmarks through online or transfer learning. This, in turn, enhances the performance of cross-load and cross-device generalization.

Secondly, the concept of real-time optimization is introduced. Existing models incorporating self-attention and bidirectional long-short-term memory (BiLSTM) structures have substantial parameter counts, rendering them unsuitable for high-frequency sampling diagnostics. Subsequent approaches should employ model compression or lightweight designs to reduce computational complexity while maintaining diagnostic accuracy, enabling real-time online detection on edge devices.

Thirdly, multi-sensor fusion is employed. Single vibration signals are inadequate for the comprehensive characterization of bearing degradation mechanisms. The integration of multi-modal data, including vibration, temperature, and acoustic emission, has been demonstrated to yield more comprehensive fault features. This, in turn, facilitates the development of more sophisticated fault diagnosis systems.

The proposed PIFC-SABiLSTM framework presents a novel paradigm for intelligent diagnosis of complex industrial systems through “data-mechanism” co-optimization. It is of considerable theoretical and engineering significance in advancing the deep application of physical information neural networks in mechanical engineering. Subsequent research endeavors will extend these findings, with the objective of developing more generalized, efficient, and reliable intelligent operation and maintenance systems.
