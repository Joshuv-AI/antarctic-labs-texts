Postbiotics as microbial-derived therapeutics for wound healing disorders: from molecular mechanisms to future application
Authors: Miroslav Dinić, Tammy Gonzalez, Melissa Caridad Hernandez, Dušan Radojević, Lauren Fernandez, Nataša Golić, Irena Pastar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractSkin microbiota emerged as an important factor in modulating all aspects of the wound healing process, with the potential utilization of residing microbes as biological therapeutics. Commensal or probiotic bacteria represent a first layer of the cutaneous barrier protection, inhibiting detrimental effects of pathogens and modulating cutaneous immune response. In this review, we highlight the latest findings on pro-healing ability of a new subclass of probiotic-derived products named postbiotics, defined as preparations of inanimate microorganisms and/or their components that confer a health benefit on the host. By mimicking the beneficial effect of live probiotics, postbiotics have the ability to restore healthy skin microbiome through inhibition of pathogen colonization and biofilm formation. Postbiotics also regulate skin epithelial barrier function and cutaneous immune response to suppress intracellular pathogen invasion. In order to effectively restore barrier breach, postbiotics stimulate multiple cellular components of the cutaneous wound healing process. This review describes molecular and cellular aspects of host-postbiotics interactions during cutaneous wound healing and provides a current viewpoint of therapeutic potential and advantages of postbiotics application for treatment of cutaneous wound healing disorders. In the era of rising antimicrobial resistance and epidemic proportions of wound healing disorders, probiotic-based therapeutics offer safe, effective and yet underutilized solutions for unmet clinical need.Keywords: Postbiotics, Commensal bacteria, Dysbiosis, Epithelial barrier, Keratinocytes, Chronic wounds

Dysbiosis as a key hallmark of wound healing disorders

Healthy skin microbiota is composed of different aerobic and anaerobic bacteria, fungi, and viruses which form a protective microbial layer on the skin surface providing defense against pathogens and shaping cutaneous immune response [1]. Commensal skin microbiota promote several components of the host defense including production of skin antimicrobials, activation of innate immune response, and recruitment of immune cells promoting skin reparatory abilities [2]. Understanding the interplay between the skin microbiota and immune system has received attraction in the past two decades and remains crucial for development of effective treatment strategies of common and challenging to treat wound healing disorders [1, 3].

Chronic non-healing wounds, such as diabetic foot ulcers (DFUs), venous leg ulcers (VLUs), and pressure ulcers are classified as injuries that failed to re-epithelialize and reestablish cutaneous structural integrity in a timely manner [4, 5]. Despite the epidemic of chronic wounds affecting nearly 2.5% of the total population in the United States alone [6], and astronomical costs up to $35.8 billion annually [7], the field is still faced with limitations in therapeutic strategies. The management of chronic wounds primarily involves debridement approaches to remove non-viable tissue and topical antimicrobials and wound dressings for prevention of infection. However, the overall cure rate remains below 50% with a high rate of recurrence [8]. The significant mortality rate of chronic wounds is comparable to cancer [9], emphasizing the need for development of more effective interventions.

Non-healing wounds are colonized with complex microbial communities, composed of aerobic and anaerobic pathogens, and biofilm formation [2, 10], imposing the risk of wound infection. Chronic wound pathogens also carry antimicrobial resistance genes (AMR) and form multi-species and multi-kingdom biofilms [4, 10, 11], thereby diminishing the efficacy of antimicrobial therapies [2, 12]. Antimicrobial resistance is a major challenge for clinicians when managing cutaneous infections associated with dysbiosis, as it often renders conventional treatments ineffective [13, 14]. This breeds a favorable environment for multi-drug resistant bacteria, including common pathogens Staphylococcus aureus [15] and Pseudomonas aeruginosa, to further inhibit wound healing [16, 17]. In addition to extracellular bacterial colonization, persistent wound dysbiosis is also associated with intracellular S. aureus niche, known to evade host immune defense, further amplifying delayed healing [18, 19]. The multiple strategies employed by intracellular S. aureus to invade and hide in host cells is of notable clinical importance, as it allows for higher antimicrobial resistance compared to extracellular strains and may cause recurrent wound colonization [20, 21]. In addition to S. aureus and P. aeruginosa, fastidious anaerobic bacteria including Anaerococcus, Finegoldia, Peptoniphilus, Prevotella and Porphyromonas genus contribute to wound chronicity [4, 22, 23], and largely remain unexplored for therapeutic targeting. Given the rising prevalence of antimicrobial resistance, it is of importance that clinicians adopt a multifaceted approach to treatment targeting wound pathogens [14], with the unmet need for novel therapies tailored to prevent wound infection without imposing the risk of AMR.

Additional chronic inflammatory skin disorders such as psoriasis, rosacea, atopic dermatitis (AD), and acne, share certain pathological signatures with non-healing wounds including epidermal barrier disruption and persistent inflammation [24–27]. Furthermore, these conditions are associated with the distinct microbial dysbiosis, implying the need for tailored probiotic-based interventions [28–31]. In AD, increase colonization by S. aureus strongly correlates with disease severity and chronicity [32, 33] emphasizing additional level of similarity with the chronic wound dysbiosis. These insights broaden the potential scope of postbiotic-based therapeutics: by restoring microbiome balance and reinforcing the barrier function, postbiotics could not only aid chronic wound healing but may also ameliorate chronic inflammatory skin conditions.

This review highlights a therapeutic potential of beneficial bacteria and molecular aspects of host-microbe interactions in treatment of wound healing disorders, emphasizing impactful yet unexplored utilization of postbiotic therapies.

Postbiotics as alternative therapeutics

Probiotics, beneficial microbes, are defined as live microorganisms that confer health benefits to their host when consumed/applied in adequate amounts [34]. Both systemic (oral) and topical application of probiotics has demonstrated effectiveness in promoting wound healing in humans and animal models [35, 36], as previously reviewed in detail [37, 38]. Some of the healing properties of probiotic strains include modulating the immune response, promoting angiogenesis and overall enhanced tissue repair [37, 38]. However, targeting complex chronic wound dysbiosis with live commensal bacteria carries a risk for patients due to tissue immune suppression and multiple co-morbidities [39]. Therefore, postbiotics emerged as a safe alternative strategy. The term postbiotic was defined by the International Scientific Association for Probiotics and Prebiotics (ISAPP) as a “preparation of inanimate microorganisms and/or their components that confers a health benefit on the host” [40, 41]. Effective postbiotics contain inactivated microbial cells or cell components, with or without metabolites, that support observed health benefits [42]. Postbiotics can be generated with or without metabolites and soluble factors, secreted by commensal bacteria or released after bacterial lysis mimicking the beneficial effect of live probiotics [43, 44]. Postbiotics include microbial cell wall fragments, biomolecules, and both intracellular and secreted metabolites - short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), antioxidants, enzymes, (lipo)peptides, polyphosphates, polysaccharides, and tryptophan metabolites. Together, they promote tissue repair while avoiding the risks of using live probiotic microorganisms. Postbiotics can be generated from a single strain or a mix of probiotics by disruption of the bacterial cell wall by applying a combination of at least two treatments including mechanical, chemical or enzymatic lysis to release intracellular metabolites and/or cell wall components in the form of fragments (Fig. 1). In addition, secreted postbiotics could be collected from cell free supernatants [43, 45]. The benefits of probiotics include stability, feasibility of large-scale production, and ability to generate off-shelf products.

Postbiotics are produced through various methods involving the inactivation and lysis of live probiotic microorganisms. During or after microbial cell lysis, bioactive compounds such as cell wall fragments, intracellular metabolites, and biomolecules are released, contributing to the healing-promoting properties of the final postbiotic preparation

Application of postbiotics for wound healing disorders gained special attention after the study which confirmed that oral supplementation with human commensal L. reuteri in the form of sterile microbial lysate improved wound healing [46]. This postbiotic demonstrated the ability to boost systemic oxytocin levels and decrease corticosterone levels in mice in the same manner as live bacteria resulting in enhanced wound closure [46]. Beside wound healing, the antimicrobial and anti-inflammatory effects of postbiotics are particularly noteworthy in inflammatory skin diseases like AD where postbiotics act by suppressing pathogenic bacteria that facilitate immune dysregulation and increased intestinal permeability, which worsen symptoms of AD. Additionally, postbiotics help regulate the abnormal Th2 immune response associated with this condition [47–49]. Moreover, postbiotics could induce reparative processes and help in various skin conditions including acne, seborrheic dermatitis, and UVB-induced skin damage [50, 51]. However, their clinical potential remains limited by the lack of standardized formulations, quantified active components, and robust comparative studies, underscoring the urgent need for rigorous standardization and mechanistic validation in future applications.

To date, many of studies showed various beneficial effects of postbiotics including bacterial fragments or bacteria-free supernatants including: (i) prevention of wound dysbiosis and suppression of wound biofilm formation, (ii) promotion of epithelial barrier function, (iii) stimulation of keratinocytes migration and wound epithelialization, (iv) and regulation of immune response (Fig. 2) which we outline in detail.

Postbiotics as preventive and treatment measure for wound infection

The host-commensal microbiome crosstalk plays a critical role in cutaneous wound healing by preventing infection during the repair process. Once the barrier is breached microbes from the environment or skin surface encounter the wound leading to colonization, where microorganisms adhere and persist on the wound surface. When colonizing microbes begin to proliferate, they can form biofilms and dominate the wound environment, still localized mainly at the superficial level. However, when microbial invasion extends into deeper tissue, it marks a beginning of clinical infection, often accompanied by inflammation, tissue damage, and prolonged wound healing process [2]. In contrast to pathogenic bacteria, which usually form harmful biofilms and drive infection by invading deeper tissue layers, commensal bacteria typically remain restricted to the surface colonization stage. At this level, they enhance host protection by preventing pathogen adhesion, displacing pathogens from damaged tissue, and activating cutaneous immune response, primarily through the stimulation of antimicrobial peptides (AMPs) [38, 52]. For instance, skin commensal Staphylococcus epidermidis activates resident immune surveillance cells - γδ T cells, and induces expression of the innate immune effector perforin-2 expression in human skin, enhancing the ability of skin cells to kill intracellular pathogens and thus contributing to protective cutaneous immune responses [52].

Multiple studies show that the beneficial effects of commensal bacteria can be mimicked by topical application of their postbiotics, without the need for live bacteria. Studies have shown that postbiotics from various Lactobacillus species can protect human keratinocytes from S. aureus and P. aeruginosa infection and promote wound healing. For instance, both lysate and cell-free supernatants of Lactobacillus rhamnosus GG were able to inhibit S. aureus adhesion to keratinocytes through competitive exclusion. However, only the lysate was effective in displacing S. aureus that had already adhered [53]. Moreover, the authors demonstrated that the pilus protein SpaC from L. rhamnosus GG is responsible for the dose-dependent inhibition of S. aureus adhesion to keratinocytes, resulting in improved keratinocyte survival following bacterial exposure. This anti-adhesive effect was further enhanced by several moonlighting proteins, such as enolase, triosephosphate isomerase, and elongation factor Tu, identified in the most active lysate fraction [54].

In vivo studies in rodents also support these findings. For example, a protein-rich fraction from L. plantarum USM8613 inhibited S. aureus growth and biofilm formation, while simultaneously promoting wound healing by upregulating genes involved in skin innate immune response, particularly β-defensin 2 [55]. Moreover, metabolites from L. acidophilus applied 1 h after infection with P. aeruginosa reduced pathogen burden within 24 h, and reduced plasma IL-6 and MCP-1 levels promoting wound healing [56].

Chronic tissue colonization could initiate intracellular invasion with pathogens like S. aureus associated with suppression of AMPs and activation of the AIM2 inflammasome, mechanisms that drive impaired wound healing [18]. Uptake of S. aureus into host phagosomes is induced by the bacteria’s fibronectin-binding proteins (FnBps), and phenotypic switching in some strains can develop accessory gene regulator (agr)-deficient colonies. These colonies, also known as “small colony variants,” have down regulated virulence genes and slow metabolisms, which allow them to remain dormant in host cells for weeks to months at a time [57]. Additionally, intracellular survival of S. aureus in phagocytic cells such as polymorphonuclear neutrophils is partly due to the action of SarA, a global regulator of several virulence factors. These factors enable the bacteria to employ evasion mechanisms, such as being taken up into large vacuoles called macropinosomes, which provide protection by preventing fusion with lysosomes and subsequent destruction [58]. This highlights the importance of AMP induction as a key defense against intracellular bacterial persistence. Recent studies have demonstrated that postbiotics contained in lysate obtained from various Lactobacillus species can effectively reduce intracellular accumulation of S. aureus in both immortalized and patient-derived keratinocytes [59]. This study showed that treating S. aureus-infected keratinocytes with a L. curvatus lysate led to reduction in intracellular S. aureus counts, on the order of a 10-fold compared to untreated infected cells, observed within 6–24 h post-infection [59]. This is of clinical relevance, as intracellular S. aureus can be found in a myriad of cutaneous disorders, including DFUs [60], AD [57, 60], and epidermolysis bullosa [57] which could all potentially benefit from treatment with postbiotics capable of targeting intracellular bacteria. These antimicrobial effects of the postbiotic against intracellular S. aureus were mediated by a robust upregulation of host genes encoding AMPs, including β-defensins, S100 family proteins, angiogenin, and perforin-2. However, the detailed involvement of these AMPs in the context of postbiotic treatment still needs to be fully elucidated. Notably, similar AMP induction was observed in a human ex vivo wound model treated with the same lysate from L. curvatus BGMK2-41, with the similar induction of AMP expression including cathelicidin (CAMP, encoding LL-37). LL-37, in particular, is an AMP with broad antimicrobial activity that also facilitates wound healing, it promotes keratinocyte migration and proliferation and stimulates angiogenesis [61]. In fact, neutralizing LL-37 by antibodies in skin organ cultures was shown to significantly inhibit re-epithelialization, implicating LL-37 as a key factor in wound closure. Consistently, chronic wound tissues often show deficient LL-37 levels in the non-healing epithelium [61]. Thus, postbiotic-driven restoration of AMP levels in conjunction with elimination of the intracellular S. aureus can overcome the immunosuppression in chronic wounds [62] and may also prove effectiveness against other emerging intracellular pathogens relevant for a variety of skin diseases.

Effects of postbiotics on cutaneous barrier integrity

Cutaneous barrier integrity relies on a multi-layered defense system. The first component is the stratum corneum, the surface layer of terminally differentiated keratinocytes (corneocytes) embedded in a lipid-rich matrix, which provides a physical and chemical barrier [63]. Beneath this, in the viable epidermis, cell–cell junctions, particularly tight junction proteins, seal the intercellular spaces of the epidermis, preventing excessive water loss and blocking entry of pathogens. This tight junction network includes transmembrane proteins such as claudins and occludin, along with intracellular scaffold proteins like zonula occludens-1 (ZO-1) that link the junctional complexes to the actin cytoskeleton [63, 64]. Maintaining these structures is crucial for skin barrier function [65].

Tight junction proteins are often targeted by pathogens colonizing the wound. For example, P. aeruginosa and Acinetobacter baumannii-inducible microRNAs such as miR-146a and miR-106b, expressed in host wound-edge tissue, have been shown to silence ZO-1 and ZO-2, leading to compromised tight junction function and increased skin permeability [66]. A disrupted barrier then facilitates deeper microbial invasion and sustains inflammation, creating a vicious cycle of delayed healing.

Recent studies demonstrated that lysate of L. curvatus BGMK2-41 strain can strengthen the epithelial barrier by upregulating ZO-1 and occludin proteins, both in vitro and in ex vivo human skin models [59], revealing promising potential of postbiotics to restore tight junction proteins network. Also, lysates from multiple probiotic strains, especially Bifidobacterium longum and L. rhamnosus GG, dose-dependently improved keratinocyte barrier function by increasing transepithelial electrical resistance (TEER) and expression of ZO-1, occludin, claudin-1 [67]. Effects were observed at concentrations ranging from 104 to 108 CFU/mL. Furthermore, Toll-like receptors 2 (TLR2) neutralization abolished the barrier-enhancing effects of B. longum, but not L. rhamnosus GG, indicating that B. longum acts via a TLR2-dependent pathway, while L. rhamnosus GG uses a TLR2-independent mechanism. This demonstrates strain-specific mechanisms in modulating tight junction integrity [67]. Therefore, given their central role in maintaining skin barrier function and their vulnerability to pathogenic bacteria, tight junction proteins represent attractive targets for postbiotics aimed at restoring barrier integrity and promoting wound healing.

Postbiotics promote epithelialization

In acute physiological wounds, tissue injury prompts keratinocytes to reorganize their cytoskeleton and modulate the expression of cell surface receptors, processes essential for keratinocyte migration required to close the wound gap [68]. In contrast, these mechanisms are impaired in chronic wounds, where keratinocyte migration is often dysfunctional despite continued proliferation, leading to delayed or incomplete wound healing [69]. Re-epithelialization is co-regulated by signaling cues from skin commensal bacteria that colonize the wound surface, further coordinating the cellular activities necessary for effective tissue repair [2]. Both in vitro and in vivo studies have demonstrated the beneficial effects of various Lactobacillus species and their derivatives in promoting wound re-epithelialization and modulating key cellular responses involved in cutaneous wound healing.

Multiple in vitro studies using keratinocytes scratch assays have demonstrated that lysates from various Lactobacillus species can enhance wound closure. Notably, lysates from L. rhamnosus GG and L. reuteri ATCC55730 significantly promoted re-epithelialization, with L. rhamnosus GG exhibiting the most pronounced effect [53]. Further analysis revealed that L. rhamnosus GG induced upregulation of the chemokine CXCL2 and its receptor CXCR2, both of which are critically involved in orchestrating cellular migration and the wound healing response [70].

In addition to well-characterized effects of lysates obtained from commercial probiotics such as L. rhamnosus GG, bacterial strains isolated from artisanal dairy products have demonstrated comparable effects on wound healing. Specifically, lysates prepared from L. plantarum SGL07 and L. salivarius SGL19 were shown to promote re-epithelialization by enhancing keratinocyte migration in HaCaT cell line [71]. Enhancement of wound closure was also observed both in keratinocyte scratch assay and in relevant human skin ex vivo wound model after application of lysate from L. curvatus BGMK2-41 strain. Gene expression analysis pointed to upregulation of promigratory factors in keratinocytes such as Keratin 6 (KRT-6) and Forkhead Box N1 (FOXN1), involved in initiation of migration and regulation of reepithelialization [59].

In vivo studies further support the findings from in vitro experiments. For instance, extracts from L. reuteri have been shown to accelerate wound healing in mice, likely through the induction of matrix metallopeptidase 1 (MMP1), a key enzyme known for improving tissue repair [72, 73]. MMPs play essential roles throughout all phases of wound healing, although their effects can vary. For example, MMP1 is typically upregulated in healing wounds and supports tissue remodeling, whereas MMP9 is often associated with impaired healing and is elevated in chronic, non-healing wounds [69].

While effective re-epithelialization is crucial for wound healing, its dysregulation can promote excessive fibroblast activity, triggering fibrosis and hypertrophic scar formation [74]. Recent studies have shown that postbiotics may help mitigate these adverse outcomes in a scleroderma mouse model. Cell-free supernatants from probiotic lactobacilli were found to significantly reduce the expression of Collagen I/III and alpha-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA), as well as limit fibroblast proliferation, key factors involved in hypertrophic scarring [75]. These effects were attributed to bacterial extracellular vesicles (EVs) contained in supernatant, which are nano-sized membrane vesicles released by bacteria, carry diverse bioactive molecules that play a role in cell-free intercellular signaling and may offer therapeutic potential in modulating the wound healing [76]. This novel metabolite delivery system highlights the remarkable capacity of probiotic EVs in shaping immune responses and advancing wound healing [77]. Interestingly, a study utilizing a combination of Lactobacillus strains (BioK) further demonstrated that postbiotic-derived lactic acid contributed to the downregulation of Nox-4, α-SMA, and Collagen I expression via the TGF-β/Smad signaling pathway, an essential pathway involved in fibroblast-to-myofibroblast differentiation and extracellular matrix production. This finding highlights lactic acid as one of the active agents in reducing fibrosis and scar formation through modulation of profibrotic signaling [78].

Immunomodulation capability of postbiotics

Rapid colonization by skin or environmental microorganisms upon acute wounding triggers the recruitment of immune cells such as neutrophils and macrophages to the site of injury. These cells play a critical role in initiating the inflammatory phase of wound healing process by releasing a range of pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α) and signaling molecules essential for tissue repair [79]. However, in the chronic wound environment, the inflammatory response fails to resolve properly, resulting in a prolonged low-grade inflammation. This is partly due to the imbalance of pro- and anti-inflammatory cellular responses [5, 80] and the lack of neutrophils at the wound edge caused by impaired immune cell recruitment to the site of barrier breach [81].

Postbiotics have shown promise in modulating cutaneous immune responses, potentially helping to restore balance and promote more efficient wound healing. Topical application of postbiotics has been shown to regulate cytokine production, affecting both pre-stored cytokines in keratinocytes and those released by activated immune cells [59]. In a human ex vivo wound model, treatment with postbiotics increased IL-10 levels, a cytokine with potent anti-inflammatory properties, without significantly altering pro-inflammatory IL-1β expression, suggesting a potential role in restoring anti-inflammatory signaling [59].

Comprehensive in vitro and in vivo studies have recently identified Reuterin (3-hydroxypropionaldehyde), an anti-inflammatory compound present in EVs released from L. reuteri, as a key modulator of immune responses. Reuterin has been shown to significantly upregulate the production of IL-10, while simultaneously reducing pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 in murine macrophages [77]. Furthermore, the internalization of L. reuteri-derived EVs by macrophages not only attenuates inflammation but also promotes polarization of macrophages toward M2-like phenotypes, crucial for resolving inflammation during the healing process. In vivo application of EV-enriched supernatants in wounded mice further demonstrated enhanced wound repair, underscoring the potential of EVs as a therapeutic approach to modulate inflammation and accelerate wound healing.

Molecular recognition of postbiotics and interactions with host receptors during wound healing

The interaction between bacterial structural macromolecules and secreted metabolites with host tissues requires close engagement with cellular immune and non-immune receptors. The immune receptor classes involved in recognizing microbial structures typically exhibit broad specificity for conserved molecular motifs or patterns known as pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) [82]. Among these, TLRs are the most extensively characterized. TLRs are expressed on keratinocytes as well as various immune cells involved in wound repair, highlighting their central role in cutaneous immune responses during barrier repair [68, 83]. The ligands for TLRs are found in bacterial lysates and extracts, including components of bacterial cell walls, peptidoglycan fragments, muropeptides, lipoteichoic acid, lipopeptides, exopolysaccharides, and bacterial DNA released during bacterial lysis [82].

Additionally, probiotic extracts contain effector soluble molecules released from probiotic bacteria. These compounds can be divided into two main categories: (i) metabolic products/by-products of bacterial metabolic pathways (SCFAs, polyphosphates, GABA, tryptophan metabolites, etc.) or (ii) soluble proteinaceous compounds (peptides, enzymes, etc.) capable to interact with both immune and various non-immune host receptors (G-protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs), tyrosine kinases receptors, nuclear hormone receptors, etc.) involved in processes such as cell proliferation, migration, and tissue regeneration (Fig. 3).

The role of bacterial structural macromolecules in wound healing

Peptidoglycan (PGN)

Fragments of PGN, as the most abundant component of bacterial cell walls, are recognized by TLR2 after bacterial autolysis. Its activation requires dimerization with TLR6 and the presence of CD14 co-receptor to trigger immune response [84]. Experiments conducted on intestinal epithelial Caco-2 cells showed that PGN derived from L. plantarum 1.0386 strain could protect against lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-induced increase of paracellular permeability and disruption of tight junctions [85]. This was also the case with HaCaT keratinocytes where application of Lactobacillus lysates enriched with PGN fragments increased epithelial resistance and barrier functionality [67]. Moreover, PGNs from different Lactobacillus strains inhibit LPS-induced release of pro-inflammatory cytokines from murine macrophages which are essential element for resolving inflammation in final step of wound closure [86].

Lipoteichoic acid (LTA)

As the second most abundant component of the bacterial cell wall, LTA is recognized by TLR2 and its activation requires heterodimerization with TLR6 and the presence of CD14 and CD36 co-receptors [84]. LTA purified from S. epidermidis showed the ability to inhibit Cutibacterium acnes-induced expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-6 and TNF-α in keratinocytes. Mechanistically, authors found that LTA activated TLR2 to induce miR-143, while miR-143, in turn, directly targeted 3′ UTR of TLR2 downregulated its expression leading to suppressed inflammation in the skin [87]. LTA was also identified as an active component of the pro-healing effect of L. plantarum GMNL-6 and L. paracasei GMNL-653. TGF-β-induced fibrosis was diminished in vitro by LTA application through a decreased level of α-SMA and Smad2 phosphorylation [88].

Exopolysaccharides (EPS)

Surface polysaccharides are loosely associated with the bacterial cell wall and could be most likely recognized by the hosts TLR4 and C-type lectin receptor (CLR) [84]. With unique composition, EPS are usually found among probiotic lactobacilli responsible for their beneficial effects though their anti-inflammatory and antioxidative properties [89, 90]. Besides anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties of EPS, dermal wound healing activity has also been reported. EPS-Ca6, a polymer isolated and purified from Lactobacillus sp. Ca6 strain, showed potential to accelerate wound closure in a rat wound model by promoting extracellular matrix remodeling. This was achieved through upregulation of hydroxyproline synthesis, the major component of tissue collagen responsible for the wound closure [91]. Since the prolonged inflammatory phase in chronic wounds results in excessive degradation of the extracellular matrix [5], postbiotic stimulation of collagen matrix synthesis would be highly beneficial in promoting healing.

Bacterial DNA (CpG-DNA)

Unmethylated CpG sequences abundant in the bacterial genome can activate immune response via TLR9 signaling [84]. Its stimulatory effect is observed during wound healing. Application of synthetic CpG-DNA accelerated wound closure in TLR9-dependent manner through promotion of macrophages chemotaxis to the wound site and production of VEGF [92]. In term of probiotic CpG-DNA, there is lack of data considering its role in cutaneous wound healing, however systemic treatment of rats with combination of L. rhamnosus GG and B. longum BB536 showed both immunomodulatory activity and intestinal barrier improvement in ovalbumin-sensitized rats via induction of TLR9. Probiotic DNA was identified as an active component of this probiotic mixture capable of exhibiting the same effects as the live bacteria [93].

The role of bacterial metabolites on tissue repair and wound healing

Polyphosphates (PPs)

Polymers of phosphate residues are usually synthesized and accumulated in bacteria as a response to unfavorable nutritional conditions. Although there is lack of evidence of PPs direct effect on acceleration of cutaneous wound healing and keratinocyte migration, the data about their potential of repairing intestinal epithelial barrier encourages their application for skin barrier restoration [94]. First, bacterial PPs are capable to attenuate LPS-induced M1 macrophage polarization and favor the upregulation of M2 characteristics of macrophages which is important for resolving tissue injuries independent of their etiology [95]. Additionally, PPs inhibit LPS-induced CXCL10 chemokine release. This effect was potentiated by application of different TLR ligands including TLR2-PG and TLR9-CpG-DNA which implies that postbiotics enriched with TLR agonists and PPs could have synergistic effects [95]. Finally, there is evidence that PPs improve the wound closure of intestinal epithelial cells in vitro, by enhancing extracellular signal-regulated kinase (ERK) phosphorylation and consequently platelets activation [96].

γ-aminobutyric acid (GABA)

GABA is the major inhibitory neurotransmitter in mammals, also produced by commensal bacteria with probiotic lactobacilli representing the most important group of GABA producers [97]. GABA is very often identified as the key molecule of postbiotics action conferring various beneficial effects on the host including immunomodulation [98] and barrier restoration [97]. In line with expectations, topical application of GABA accelerated the barrier recovery after tissue disruption via activation of GABAA, but not GABAB receptors [99]. It also promoted M2 phenotype of macrophages by preventing pro-inflammatory cytokine production [100], highlighting healing potential of bacterial supernatants enriched with GABA.

Short chain fatty acids (SCFA)

SCFA are well-known metabolites produced by commensal bacteria during fermentation of fibers and usually include acetate, propionate, and butyrate. Although SCFA represents the most studied gut bacteria metabolites with numerous beneficial effects, their role in wound healing especially after topical application is still largely unexplored [101]. It is known that butyrate facilitates polarization of macrophages towards M2 phenotype and enhances their migration and wound-healing properties [102]. Butyrate also has ability to reprogram keratinocyte metabolism to induce mitochondria-dependent epidermal differentiation which is deregulated in chronic wounds characterized by suppression of keratinocyte differentiation markers KRT(Keratin)1 and KRT10 [69, 103]. Although, expression of G protein-coupled receptors (GPCR) recognizing SCFA is low in keratinocytes, it has been demonstrated that butyrate influences gene expression in keratinocytes indirectly via histone acetylation [104]. As a highlight, oral application of butyrate exhibits remarkable effects on the skin by strengthening epidermal barrier integrity through stimulation of production of key structural components, integrin, filaggrin, KRT1 and KRT10, ultimately limiting allergen sensitization in an animal model of inflammatory skin condition atopic dermatitis [103].

Tryptophan metabolites

Recent studies have shown that skin-resident bacteria can promote epithelialization and barrier repair by enhancing the expression of genes related to epidermal differentiation. This process occurs through the activation of the Aryl Hydrocarbon Receptor (AHR) signaling pathway [105, 106]. The AHR pathway is a ligand-dependent transcription factor acting as an environmental sensor that responds to a variety of environmental stimuli, including pollutants, toxins, and natural compounds, but it can also respond to endogenous signals like tryptophan metabolites produced by skin commensals [105, 107]. The clear link between skin commensal microbes and the AHR pathway suggests that postbiotics could be explored as a potential preventative or therapeutic approach for treating epidermal barrier defects. Different probiotic species have been shown to produce various tryptophan metabolites, which are key activators of the AHR [108]. For example, tryptophan metabolite, indole-3-pyruvate has been found to protect keratinocytes from UVB radiation, suppressing pro-inflammatory cytokine production and inhibiting cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2) activation. In vivo studies demonstrated that indole-3-pyruvate could prevent UVB-induced skin damage in HR-1 hairless mice [109]. Additionally, 6-formylindolo[3,2-b]carbazole (FICZ), a derivative of indole-3-pyruvate produced by microbial activity and also naturally expressed in human skin [110], has been shown to enhance keratinocyte migration and promote wound healing through the mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK/ERK) pathway in multiple mouse models [111]. These observations highlight the potential of postbiotics enriched with AHR-activating tryptophan metabolites to amplify and drive the beneficial effects of commensal microbes.

Soluble proteinaceous compounds

In depth genomic analysis of probiotic strain sequencing database have predicted a broad group of proteins, including secreted and soluble peptides and enzymes with potential regulatory effects on human cells [112, 113]. A notable study confirmed probiogenomics’ healing capability of soluble probiotic proteins in EVs produced by L. casei DSM20011 and L. plantarum NCIMB8826 to promote cell migration and reduce inflammation, resulting in improved wound closure in a mouse full-thickness wound model [98]. A high number of proteins were detected in EVs with the most common proteins being p40 and p75 muramidases, which are the first probiotic derived proteins that demonstrated the beneficial effects of promoting epithelial homeostasis through specific signaling pathways [114]. Moreover, specific peptides conjugated with a lipid moiety, like lipopeptide 78 isolated from skin commensal S. epidermidis, have been shown to activate TLR2 to induce β-catenin phosphorylation, which translocated into nuclei to bind to PPARγ reducing the expression of TLR3-mediated inflammatory response and resulting in accelerated wound healing in normal and diabetic mice [115]. Beside the potential of postbiotics to influence host healing mechanisms, postbiotics could protect the cutaneous barrier from pathogens by modulating their virulence. For example, autoinducing peptides produced by another skin commensal S. hominis have shown capability to inhibit the agr quorum sensing system of S. aureus [116] and protect against skin injury and wound infection.

Closing remarks

This review summarizes the current knowledge and future directions on postbiotics as an emerging promising therapeutic agent, particularly for cutaneous wound healing disorders, due to their multifaceted mechanisms of action and favorable safety profile. While the importance of commensal and probiotic bacteria in maintaining cutaneous homeostasis is well-established [2, 59], bacterial metabolites, secreted products and probiotic lysates represent a largely untapped therapeutic resource.

Unlike traditional antimicrobials, postbiotics exert both direct and indirect antimicrobial effects without contributing significantly to the global concern of antimicrobial resistance, particularly relevant in the field of chronic wound management [13, 14]. Postbiotics inhibit the growth of pathogenic microorganisms either directly through antimicrobials including organic acids and bacteriocins, or indirectly, by interfering with pathogen colonization, which is essential for chronic wounds predisposed to high risk of infection. Furthermore, patient population affected by chronic wounds is characterized by multiple comorbidities, including diabetes, venous or arterial insufficiency, advanced age and limited mobility [5, 69] predisposing them to higher risk of both tissue and systemic infections with multi-drug-resistant bacteria. This further emphasizes a significant advantage of postbiotics due to minimal risk of promoting antimicrobial resistance. Moreover, because postbiotics are derived from bacterial strains that are sensitive to antibiotics and do not engage in vertical gene transfer, they do not contribute to the propagation of resistance genes. The non-cytotoxic nature, biocompatibility, and minimal systemic absorption, especially when applied topically, adds to postbiotics’ favorable profile (Table 1). In contrast to traditional antibiotics or wound antiseptics, postbiotics have not been associated with side effects, making them a safer alternative for long-term or prophylactic use, which is particularly attractive yet unexplored for patients at the high risk for wound healing disorders. Considering a harsh highly proteolytic chronic wound environment [5, 69] future studies should identify optimal topical formulation of postbiotics for sustained delivery. Additionally, postbiotics promote tissue repair by enhancing collagen synthesis, accelerating re-epithelialization, and restoring the skin barrier, while modulating local immune cell milieu. In depth longitudinal studies utilizing established preclinical models [117] and randomized clinical trials, including control arms with the widely used antimicrobial treatments and dressing, are warranted for in depth understanding of postbiotic mode of action, and their impact on specific phases of the dynamic wound healing process.

Accumulated evidence underscores the molecular and cellular mechanisms by which postbiotics facilitate wound repair, advocating for the next steps in required for their integration into therapeutic pipelines for cutaneous conditions associated with dysbiosis. Considering their safety profile, pro-healing properties, coupled with the low costs and off-the-shelf availability postbiotics represent a compelling but underutilized solution to an escalating clinical challenge and epidemic proportions of wound healing disorders. There is a pressing need for in depth translational studies and clinical trials to validate current findings and pave the way for next-generation wound care strategies grounded in evidence on beneficial effects of microbiome-derived postbiotics.
