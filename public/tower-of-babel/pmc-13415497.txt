ABSTRACT
IntroductionRare bone and mineral conditions (RBMCs) are diverse disorders involving skeletal fragility, altered bone metabolism, and multisystemic complications. Improved pediatric care has increased survival, creating a growing population of adolescents and young adults requiring specialized support. Yet, transition from pediatric to adult services remains fragmented and underdeveloped across RBMCs. This review aimed to map existing literature on transition care for young people with RBMCs and to identify priorities, barriers, and gaps to guide future research and practice.MethodsWithin the ERN BOND framework, a scoping review was conducted following PRISMA-ScR guidelines. MEDLINE, EMBASE, and SCOPUS were searched (July–August 2025) for studies addressing transition from pediatric to adult care in RBMCs. All study designs were eligible. Screening and data extraction were performed by multiple reviewers using Covidence, and findings were summarized descriptively.ResultsOf 1706 records identified, 34 met inclusion criteria. Most studies originated from Europe and North America and focused on osteogenesis imperfecta or X-linked hypophosphatemia. Expert opinion and consensus papers dominated the evidence base, while empirical research remained limited. Across RBMCs, transition processes were consistently described as suboptimal, with poor coordination, limited adult multidisciplinary expertise, unclear pathways, and inadequate patient preparation. Patients, parents, and healthcare professionals highlighted priorities such as continuity of care, structured planning, patient empowerment, psychosocial support, and improved communication between pediatric and adult teams. Barriers included scarcity of adult specialists, organizational fragmentation, and insufficient training. Recommendations emphasized early planning, readiness assessment, multidisciplinary collaboration, and support for self-management.ConclusionEvidence on transition care in RBMCs is sparse, largely expert-driven, and concentrated in a few conditions. Nonetheless, consistent themes underscore the need for structured, coordinated, and patient-centered transition pathways. Strengthening adult expertise, ensuring continuity of care, and enhancing psychosocial support are essential to improve outcomes. Further longitudinal and implementation research is urgently needed to inform evidence-based transitional care models for RBMCs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12325-026-03632-1.

Key Summary Points

Transitional care for rare bone and mineral conditions is consistently reported as fragmented, poorly coordinated, and unsatisfactory across conditions and settings.The evidence base is dominated by expert opinion, with limited empirical research evaluating transition models, outcomes, or implementation strategies.Patients, parents, and healthcare professionals share common priorities—continuity of care, structured planning, empowerment, communication, and psychosocial/vocational support—yet these are rarely implemented in practice.Major barriers include lack of adult multidisciplinary expertise, organizational instability, insufficient funding, and reluctance to leave trusted pediatric providers.Sustainable, person-centered transition pathways require early planning, multidisciplinary coordination, validated tools, and system level investment, alongside more rigorous research to guide practice.

Introduction

Rare bone and mineral conditions (RBMCs) encompass a heterogeneous group of disorders characterized by skeletal fragility, short stature, abnormal bone and mineral metabolism, and multisystemic involvement [1]. These include, among others, calcium-phosphate imbalances, growth and bone mineralization disturbances, and a diverse group of over 770 different skeletal dysplasias [2, 3], which are often associated with susceptibility to fractures, structural bone deformities, and related comorbidities [1].

Advances in pediatric care have significantly improved the life expectancy and quality of life of affected individuals, resulting in a growing population of adolescents and young adults who require ongoing specialized care into adulthood [4, 5]. However, the transition from pediatrics to adult care remains a critical and often underdeveloped phase in the continuum of care [6, 7].

Transition care refers to the intentional and planned pathway of adolescents and young adults with chronic health conditions who transition from pediatric to adult care practices [8]. Unlike transfer care, which is a one-time, formal event in which responsibility for a patient's care is transferred from one provider or setting to another, for example from a pediatrician to an internist, transition care is a broader, more gradual process that prepares the patient for this transfer through a series of coordinated steps and support services over a period of time, addressing medical, psychosocial, developmental, and vocational needs, among others [9, 10].

In the context of RBMCs, this process is particularly complex due to the rarity and severity of the conditions, the need for multidisciplinary management, and the limited availability of adult specialists, which is often scattered between different specialties [11–13]. Without structured transition protocols, young adults are at risk of losing access to trusted, accessible, and efficient healthcare, which can lead to treatment interruption, prolonged loss to follow-up, and worsening of health outcomes [12, 14]. Recent efforts have been made to address these gaps. The European Calcified Tissue Society (ECTS) Rare Bone Disease Action Group conducted a Delphi consensus process to develop recommendations for transition care in RBMCs. Their findings emphasized the importance of early planning, patient empowerment, structured coordination, and multidisciplinary collaboration to ensure seamless transition and sustained care quality [15].

Notwithstanding the growing interest in improving health outcomes among young adults [11, 12, 16], ongoing challenges persist due to inadequate transition care and a lack of programming, causing significant uncertainty and distress among patients, their families, and healthcare providers [7, 12, 14]. Therefore, within the framework of the ERN BOND (European Reference Network for Rare Bone Diseases [17]), the aim of this study was to examine the existing body of literature on the transition of care from pediatric to adult life in RBMCs. Accordingly, to map and synthesize current literature in the field and identify priorities, barriers and key gaps in knowledge and practice to guide future research and practice.

Methods

To synthesize existing evidence on the transition from childhood to adulthood care in RBMCs and identify any gaps in the literature, a scoping review was conducted. Scoping reviews allow for a relatively broad formulation of research questions [18], thereby granting the mapping of published evidence on topics on which available information is scarcer, such as transitional care in RBMCs.

RBMC was defined as the diagnoses in the international nosology of genetic skeletal disorders [2] and in the classification of rare bone disorders-particularly primary bone dysplasias-in Orphanet (https://www.orpha.net).

A systematic literature search was performed by a senior librarian (TMH) at The University of Oslo Library of Medicine and Science, in the following databases: MEDLINE, EMBASE, and SCOPUS. These databases were selected from the university library’s database collection as they focused on areas such as medicine, social sciences, and humanities to provide a comprehensive overview of the current body of literature. The systematic search was followed by a peer review with two librarians and took place between July and August 2025.

Combined with Boolean operators “and” and “or”, MeSH terms and free-text keywords, comprising synonyms and terms closely related to “rare bone and mineral conditions” (as a group and specific to each disease) and to “transition to adult care”, were used as search terms. Supplementary Table S1 provides a more detailed overview of the terms that were included and the searches conducted in the databases.

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

Publications to be included had to be available in full text and published in one of the following languages: English, Scandinavian language, Portuguese, Spanish, French, or German. However, there were no geographical restrictions on where publications were published, meaning that all countries were included. There were also no restrictions on the year of publication, that is, all literature published to date was considered (last search on August 1, 2025).

Only studies involving human participants were included, all designs were allowed; specifically, descriptive or randomized controlled trials, meta-analyses, systematic reviews, case reports, and guidelines. This search also included articles on the development and implementation of guidelines, as clinical guidelines can be considered the result of scientific research [19].

The studies had to address the transition from pediatric to adult care of people with RBMCs. In other words, publications that focused solely on biomechanics, genetics, or other similar areas not relevant to the focus of this research and that did not address transition care were not included in this review. Broader transition literature in chronic pediatric conditions where not included.

Data Screening and Extraction

All search results were uploaded to the Covidence software [20], a web-based tool for managing decisions to include or exclude references in the systematic review process and for data extraction. The search results were organized according to a Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) flow diagram [21]. Figure 1 shows the screening process from the initial search to the final list of studies included in the review. Titles and abstracts were independently screened by two authors (SC and LSJ) after removing duplicates, and full-text versions of the selected abstracts were retrieved. Articles whose full texts could not be obtained through interlibrary loan or direct contact with the authors were excluded. The retrieved full-text articles were reviewed by the same two authors and by LLW and were included if they fulfilled the inclusion criteria. Additionally, reference lists of all included studies were examined to identify further relevant publications. Disagreements encountered during the screening process were resolved through discussion with the research team until consensus was reached. Furthermore, whenever there were doubts about the classification of the disease, the Orphanet web platform, an encyclopedia of rare diseases, was used [22].Fig. 1Flowchart of the literature search and the inclusion process for transitional care in rare bone and mineral conditions

Data extraction was performed by SC, LSJ, LLW, and JH using a predefined model customized by the reviewers in the Covidence software. The template included descriptors for the main author(s) last name(s), year of publication, country, name of the RBMC(s), study design, study aim, study sample, and main findings relevant to the current scoping review (Table S2).

Ethical Approval

This article is based on previously conducted studies and does not contain any new studies with human participants or animals performed by any of the authors.

Data Analysis

The results were synthesized and presented in table and figure form, accompanied by a descriptive summary. The quantitative data were analyzed using descriptive statistics with the aid of Microsoft Excel (Microsoft 365 version).

A total of 1706 records were identified in the literature search after removing duplicates. Overall, after screening titles and abstracts, 83 remained for full text review, and 34 studies met the final inclusion criteria. Assessment of Transitional Care Processes, Priorities, Barriers, and Strategies in RBMCs

All the studies that assessed transition care experiences reported unsatisfactory transfer processes [23–29]. Fredwall et al. evaluated the practices in the transition process of individuals with achondroplasia through an exploratory survey with representatives of patient advocacy groups; 50% of respondents considered the transition experience to adult services poor or very poor, and none considered it good or very good [23]. In another study carried out in a MO-expertise center in the Netherlands, in which patients' experience and satisfaction with the transfer were assessed using the ‘On Your Own Feet-Transfer Experience Scale’, the transfer process was generally considered unsatisfactory, with a low average satisfaction score [24].

In the study by Dogba et al., a transition program from a pediatric orthopedic hospital for adult care of young adults with OI in Canada was evaluated, including the perspectives of different stakeholders (namely patients, parents, healthcare professionals, social workers, and administrative staff) and the authors concluded that the main weaknesses of the program reported by the different stakeholders were the discontinuation of certain transition activities and the successive organizational changes [25]. Lack of clarity in participant transition was also reported by young adults with Ol in the UK when interviewed about the transition processes from pediatric to adult care, as observed in the study led by Hill et al. in the BOOST Brittle Bones Before Birth program [26]. Furthermore, in an international survey on the transition process reported by patients with Gaucher disease type 1 (most patients were from the Netherlands, the United Kingdom, and the United States), only one-third of patients were aware of transition clinics [27].

Lankhorst et al. and Huurneman et al., both studies conducted in the Netherlands, evaluated other non-medical domains, namely education and employment support, during the transition to adulthood in young adults with tULRD, and found that almost all young adults reported difficulties in finding a suitable academic career or employment [28, 29]. However, most young adults were convinced that they were suited for almost any study or job, but their teachers and potential employers were more reserved [28]. Additionally, patients, parents, and healthcare professionals suggested to include methods offering specific training programs to practice work-related tasks, prepare for job interviews, and provide psychosocial support to increase self-confidence during the transition care process [29].

Few RBMC studies have explicitly assessed the priorities and barriers in transition care, limited to MO, Ollier disease, Maffucci syndrome, OI, achondroplasia, Gaucher disease type 1, and mucopolysaccharidosis [12, 23, 27, 30, 31].

The common priorities in transition care identified by patients, parents, and healthcare professionals in MO, Ollier disease, and Maffucci syndrome were the coordination and continuity of care, transition planning, patient empowerment and communication, digital health records, and social and practical support [12]. In OI, the priorities reported by parents were the implementation of transition care plans that leverage the strengths and capabilities of each family, balancing immediate health protection with long-term well-being, providing space and time for families to express their concerns about vulnerability, risk and uncertainty, and minimizing sources of uncertainty by ensuring continuity of care [30].

When assessing the main barriers in transition care, according to representatives of advocacy groups for patients with achondroplasia, the fundamental barriers identified in an exploratory survey were the limited availability of adult multidisciplinary teams, the lower level of experience in achondroplasia management in adult services compared to pediatric services, and a lack of interest or resistance among some individuals with achondroplasia to engage with adult care, which consequently leads to a loss of follow-up during the transition to adult services [23]. In Gaucher disease type 1, the main challenges in the transition care process reported by healthcare professionals included lack of expertise, limited funding, and difficulty in coordinating care between different specialties [27].

An international study that used case studies as examples for exchanging experiences on transition care for patients with mucopolysaccharidosis, involved different metabolic disease centers in Germany, Spain, Russia, and the UK. Common challenges identified by the healthcare professionals in the transition care processes included attachment to pediatricians and patients' reluctance to attend appointments with unfamiliar team members, as well as complex clinical symptoms such as immunodeficiency, respiratory infections, speech and hearing loss, visual impairment, multiple dysostosis, cardiac and respiratory dysfunction, hepatomegaly, and pain [31]. The authors also highlighted some important strategies including provision of psychological support during the transition of patients with complex symptoms to further reassure the patients/parents throughout the process, and the extending time required to accommodate the different clinical needs. Considering that the transition time to different specialized adult care units may vary, the transfer period should be smooth, with coordinated management of all aspects of medical care [31].

In addition, sufficient time should be allowed for patients to understand their responsibilities in the adult care setting, and patient education should be provided [31, 32] to facilitate the empowerment of young adults [27]. Furthermore, patients benefit from a period of overlap where they are given the opportunity to visit the adult facilities and meet the care team before transferring from pediatric care, as well as the possibility of attending appointments, or at least part of them, without their parents’ presence [27, 31].

Self-Management in Transitional Care for RBMCs

Two studies specifically explored (using semi-structured interviews) self-management and transition care in RBMCs, particularly in achondroplasia, OI, SEMD, SEDT, and acromicric/ geleophysic dysplasia [33, 34].

Michalovic and colleagues studied the self-management needs of young adults with OI and found that, even without adult‑care follow‑up, participants actively managed their symptoms by using anti‑inflammatory medication for pain, relying on a wheelchair for long distances, taking calcium supplements, exercising to strengthen bones and muscles, and practicing yoga for back pain, in addition to using family, friends, and community resources to obtain support and live the independent life they desired [33].

In the study conducted by Ricks et al., aiming to understand which resources young adults with short stature and skeletal dysplasia use to support their independence during the transition to adulthood, the authors identified four main categories of independence: prioritizing self-sufficiency, accommodating tension (between wanting to be self-sufficient and acknowledging the obstacles that can keep young adults from fully achieving it), identifying obstacles, and incorporating facilitators. Overall, they concluded that independence is, above all, a balance between self-sufficiency and acceptance of necessary assistance, and that healthcare professionals should promote and facilitate connections with others who have similar experiences and with patient support organizations [34].

Transition Timing and Assessment of Transition Readiness

Some studies have specified the initiation of the transition care by age, at 12 years of age (XLH) [11, 16, 35], or by an age range (in mid- to late childhood, achondroplasia) [36], such as between 16 and 18 years of age in OI [37] and mucopolysaccharidosis [38], while others have been less age-specific and have considered other parameters related to the level of maturity of the young person and emotional readiness (RBMCs in general [15, 39]), the degree of disability, and the capacity of the adult care sector (OI, achondroplasia, MO [40], and XLH [41]). In contrast, Casado et al., in their expert opinion study on physicians' experience in transitioning patients with XLH, found that there was no consensus that the age of 12 was an appropriate and feasible point to begin the transition care [11]. Furthermore, they pointed out that the age of transition may be associated with the adolescent's gender, since physical maturation is achieved 2–3 years earlier in girls, which may also be accompanied by greater psychological maturity, making transition at a younger age more feasible than in boys [11].

Nevertheless, the grounds for successful transition care for patients with XLH, according to Dahir et al., should be laid before adolescence, ideally once patients begin to understand their condition [16], and parents should be informed about the transition care process at the time the metabolic bone disease is diagnosed [11].

For OI, achondroplasia, and MO, the transition care should preferably happen, as reported by Koumakis and Cormier, during a period of clinical, psychological, and social stability [40]. Also, Couce et al. recommend that the transition process in mucopolysaccharidosis should start when the disease is stable, i.e., when there are no significant therapeutic changes, while for patients with a very short life expectancy, not undergoing the transition may be considered following consent of the family and healthcare professionals [38].

Assessing transition readiness, using validated scales to determine if adolescents are ready to start their medical care in adult specialties, was deemed important by 73.0% of healthcare professionals in the expert opinion survey conducted by Casado et al. [11]. This includes the Transition Readiness Assessment Questionnaire (TRAQ) 5.0 (containing 20 items across 5 subscales: medication, attendance at appointments, monitoring of health problems, communication with healthcare professionals, and ability to manage activities of daily living), where each item is scored using a Likert scale from 1 to 5 (1 being minimum autonomy and 5 being maximum autonomy) [42]. In a study of expert opinion on recommendations for XLH transition care, Munns et al. suggest that validated transition questionnaires, such as the TRAQ, can be administered 2 years prior to transition, followed by assessments every 1—2 years until transition. However, validated and translated local versions of TRAQ may need to be developed for use in specific local contexts [43].

Continuous and Coordinated Care and a Multidisciplinary Team

All expert opinion/consensus and reviews highlighted the importance of ensuring continuous access to care provided by an experienced multidisciplinary team as a fundamental principle in the transition care of RBMCs [11, 15, 16, 35–51, 53]. Furthermore, the transition process requires active communication between members of the pediatric and adult healthcare teams, along with a proactive approach from the patient and family, as indicated by Dahir et al. and Shapiro et al. regarding the preparation of transition care for young adults with XLH and OI, respectively [16, 37]. Similarly, a study aimed at summarizing and informing about transitional care practices in rare skeletal dysplasias, such as OI, achondroplasia, and MO, concluded that the referring pediatrician or orthopedic specialist should be responsible for preparing patients for the transition phase and that the success of the transition depends on appropriate timing and progressive transfer involving both pediatricians and adult physicians [40].

Furthermore, in their consensus study on the transition from pediatric to adult care in patients with mucopolysaccharidosis, Couce et al. noted that the pediatrician is responsible for preparing the discharge report, which should include, at least, the patient's age, weight, height, type of mucopolysaccharidosis, genetic diagnosis, surgical history, relevant disease complications, and current medications [38]. Accordingly, Kubota et al. recommend that transition documents for patients with XLH also include disease and treatment history, XLH complications, and information on the onset of XLH symptoms in adulthood, next to advocacy group support [41].

In the evidence-based guidelines led by Haffner et al., which involved healthcare professionals and patient advocacy groups on recommendations for transitional care in XLH, the recommendations highlighted that, at the time of transfer to adult services, a complete orthopedic evaluation (clinical and radiographic) should be performed to allow for the identification of any residual deformity and facilitate appropriate follow-up arrangements. Also, a list of measurements was mentioned, including height, weight, examination of intermalleolar and intercondylar distance, presence of rickets, search for osteomalacia, pseudofractures, osteoarthritis and enthesopathy, pain, stiffness and fatigue, neurological examination, musculoskeletal function, orthopedic and dental examination, renal ultrasonography, fundoscopy and brain MRI, hearing test, levels of calcium, phosphate, 25(OH) vitamin D, parathyroid hormone, and creatinine, and blood pressure [35].

Additionally, to ensure a smooth transfer of medical records, treatment plans, and necessary care, a transition coordinator can play a key role in the transition process [39]. According to Couce et al. and Cormier-Daire et al., addressing transition care in mucopolysaccharidosis and achondroplasia, respectively, a coordinator for the transition process possesses the knowledge and skills necessary to manage the specific RBMC, ensures that the necessary care is provided, and integrates the various departments involved in this management, a role typically filled by an internist or physician [38, 44].

On the other hand, Shapiro et al. noted that, when a formal team may not be available during the transition care for individuals with OI, they themselves must take an active role and become their own health coordinator, collaborating with their pediatricians to identify suitable adult specialists, facilitate communication between specialists, and gather relevant medical records [37]. Nevertheless, all studies advocate that continuous access to care should be supported by a multidisciplinary team. However, only a few studies specified which members to include [16, 35, 37, 49, 50].

The review of Ross et al., on transition care in OI, XLH, and hypophosphatasia, stated that nurses and other medical staff members can also increase the competency of the multidisciplinary team when they are comfortable interacting with this population from both a pediatric and adult perspective. This also included social workers, and administrative staff assigned to coordinate the logistically complex medical care of these patients [50].

Both the literature review conducted by Rauch et al. and the consensus study by Shapiro et al. on the transition from pediatric to adult-oriented healthcare for individuals with OI included family physicians, orthopedists, physiatrists or physical therapists, occupational therapists, nutritionists, and counselors or social workers as members of the multidisciplinary team [37, 49].

In their expert opinion study on recommendations for transitional care in XLH, Dahir et al. listed physicians, physiotherapists, surgeons, psychologists, dentists, and social workers as members of the multidisciplinary team [16], while Haffner et al. extended the team to also include representatives of patient groups [35].

Empowerment of the Young Person and Self-Management

Several studies advocate for the involvement of all stakeholders (i.e., the young person, parents, healthcare professionals, social workers, patient advocacy groups) for the success of transition care in RBMCs [15, 38, 40, 42], as well as the incorporation of non-medical care support (e.g., educational and vocational aspects) aimed at an individualized care transition [15, 37–40, 42, 45–47, 50].

Further recommendations include engaging and empowering adolescents to take responsibility for their own care as they transition into adulthood, through psychological and educational resources [15, 16, 38, 40]. In a study of expert opinion on recommendations for transitional care in XLH, the authors claim that emancipation from parental involvement does not only depend on psychosocial resources but also on guidance for parents on how to empower their children to take responsibility for their condition as they grow and mature. Moreover, as access to specialists may be limited by geographical location, it is helpful to teach patients to advocate for their own health care [16]. Along the same lines, a systematic review of the existing literature on transitional care in OI concludes that comprehensive education is essential for adult autonomy and individual empowerment. This includes education about the disease, development of self-care skills, and fostering a support network and mentorship (i.e., patient associations connection) [39]. Moreover, it is helpful to provide genetic counseling, psychosexual well-being (with language carefully chosen to include all aspects of relationships, gender, sexual health, and sexual orientation), and management of pregnancy, as pointed out by Cormier-Daire et al., Koumakis et al., and Ross et al. regarding transition care in achondroplasia, OI, MO, XLH, and hypophosphatasia, respectively [40, 44, 50].

To support the young adults with achondroplasia in engaging with their condition and taking more responsibility from their parents during the transition from pediatric to adult care, Fredwall and colleagues developed a self-assessment guide with a checklist to assist in identifying and recognizing the main and red-flag symptoms, the actions to be taken to manage the symptoms, and the necessary referrals to specialists. The checklist includes information for routine monitoring of blood pressure, hearing, pain, weight, discussion of the need for adaptative aids, as well as psychosocial aspects of care [36].

Transfer Tools and Funding Support

In addition, to facilitate the transition from pediatric healthcare to adult healthcare, an expert opinion study on transitional care in OI suggested the creation of a transfer tool developed by a task force. A task force composed not only of healthcare professionals, but also of social workers, administrative staff, and patients, offering a clinically meaningful and person-centered transfer tool that can, in turn, help improve the transition experience for adolescents with OI [47]. Inspired by existing transfer tools, such as the ‘MyHealth Passport/SickKids, Good2Go Program’ [52], the authors reviewed the available literature on transfer tools to develop the “OI Transfer Summary” tool, which includes 11 sections: (1) contact information; (2) general information; (3) psychosocial characteristics; (4) general medical information; (4) family history; (5) medical diagnoses and history; (6) current prescribed medication(s); (7) recent laboratory results, x-rays, and so forth; (8) medical equipment, orthotics, and assistive devices; (9) functional capabilities and independence level; (10) follow-up requirements/other professionals and community services; and (11) general concerns [47]. Similarly, following the work of Carrier et al. [46], Jeong and colleagues developed the ‘OI MyHealth Passport’ transfer tool, involving both patients and healthcare professionals in its development. The authors argued that access to resources that aid in the transition to adult services, such as transfer tools, would make the adolescents with OI better prepared to manage their care in adulthood [53].

Finally, to ensure continuity and uninterrupted transition care for all young adults with RBMCs, securing funding for resources is considered necessary, as recommended in a consensus study on transitional care conducted by a group of European experts in RBMC, which included healthcare professionals and patient advocacy groups from the ECTS [15]. However, in healthcare systems with limited resources, there may be specific challenges, such as funding constraints and/or a lack of formal reimbursement for transitional care coordination functions. This necessitates the consideration of digital health use solutions (i.e., telehealth), as they can enable more equitable care (in terms of costs and travel) [50], as well as the gradual integration and adaptation of essential care components which can serve as alternative solutions to improve feasibility and support transition care processes [15].

Discussion

This is the first scoping review synthesizing transition care literature across RBMCs. Although the review focuses on transitional care, many of the included studies primarily evaluated the transfer phase. Moreover, the studies were conducted in healthcare systems that differed substantially in organizational structures, policies, and funding models. This limited the ability to directly compare the roles of service providers, the resources available, or the transitions between levels of care within and between countries. The findings should therefore be interpreted with these contextual differences in mind.

In this context, and despite the multisystemic nature of these conditions and the high risk of complications in adulthood [54, 55], the evidence base to support structured transition from pediatric to adult services remains limited, fragmented, and unevenly distributed across diagnoses. The 34 included studies, published over more than a decade, reflect a field still in its early stages of conceptualization, with most publications focusing on a small subset of RBMCs and relying heavily on expert opinion rather than empirical data. Consistent with broader transition literature in chronic conditions [56–58], the studies included here depicted transition as a vulnerable period characterized by discontinuity of care, uncertainty, and unmet needs for young people with RBMCs. It is limited representation from low- and middle-income settings. This restricts the applicability of current recommendations to health systems with different structures, resources, and pathways of care.

An important contextual observation is that the transition-care literature in RBMCs is relatively recent, with the earliest publications appearing in 2012. This timing reflects broader advances in rare disease diagnosis and management over the past two decades [50, 59]. Improvements in genetic testing, early detection, multidisciplinary pediatric care, and the introduction of targeted therapies have significantly increased survival and long-term prognosis for many rare diseases, including skeletal dysplasias and metabolic bone disorders [60]. The emergence of RBMC-specific transition publications after 2012 reflects a structural change in the natural history of these disorders: as more young people survive into adulthood, health systems are now confronted with the need to develop structured, evidence-based transition pathways that did not exist when adult services rarely encountered these conditions [4, 5].

The majority of the included studies consisted of expert opinions and consensus documents, with relatively few empirical studies and almost no longitudinal or interventional study designs [24, 25], underscoring the absence of robust empirical research in this field. While these publications offer valuable clinical insights, they cannot substitute for systematic evaluation of transition models, patient outcomes, or implementation strategies. Only a minority of studies used qualitative methodologies to explore lived experiences [23, 26–29], and even fewer examined transition processes from the perspective of adult-care providers [24, 25], despite their central role in ensuring continuity of care.

This pattern is similar to other rare disease fields, where expert consensus often precedes formal evaluation of models of care [61]. Within RBMCs, the literature is strongly skewed towards OI, XLH, achondroplasia, mucopolysaccharidosis, and MO, while literature on other rarer RBM conditions (e.g., Ollier disease, etc.) appears sporadically.

This imbalance reflects both the rarity of these conditions and the absence of dedicated transition structures [11, 15, 50]. It also means that, for most RBMCs, clinicians must extrapolate from evidence generated in a few better-studied conditions or from generic transition frameworks, such as the ‘Six Core Elements’ of transition developed by Got Transition [62], rather than relying on guidelines specific to RBMC diagnosis.

The large number of recommendation papers, compared to the small number of empirical studies, suggest that transition care in RBMCs has not yet reached a mature implementation phase. In many areas of rare diseases, the publication of recommendations often preludes the development, pilot implementation, and evaluation of structured transition programs, reflecting a phase in which clinical experience is available, but real-world implementation remains limited [61, 63]. This pattern is clearly visible in RBMCs, in which, although expert groups have articulated detailed guidance for conditions such as OI, XLH, achondroplasia, mucopolysaccharidosis, and MO, there is little evidence that these recommendations have been systematically operationalized.

Moreover, the concentration of recommendations in only a handful of RBMCs highlights a deeper structural gap. For the many understudied RBMCs, the evidence base remains too limited to support the development of formal recommendations. Instead, clinicians and families often rely on small case series, single-center experiences, or informal knowledge shared within expert networks. This reflects a broader challenge in rare diseases, where low prevalence, heterogeneous phenotypes, and limited research infrastructure make it difficult to accumulate the critical mass of evidence needed to move from descriptive studies to consensus guidelines and, ultimately, to implementation and evaluation [56, 64]. As a result, transition care for many RBMCs remains in an early developmental stage.

Key Findings Across Transition Processes in RBMCs

Across studies assessing transition experiences, the transfer from pediatric to adult care was perceived as unsatisfactory by patients, families, and healthcare professionals. Surveys and qualitative studies with patients and parents have reported poor or very poor transition experiences, lack of information about what adult care would entail, and low awareness of transition clinics or structured pathways [23–26]. Healthcare professionals echoed these concerns, citing discontinuity of care, limited expertise in adult services, and insufficient communication between pediatric and adult teams [12, 25, 27]. These challenges mirror those described in other chronic pediatric conditions [63, 65], but are amplified in RBMCs by the multisystemic nature of the disorders, the need for coordinated orthopedic and metabolic follow-up, and the scarcity of adult providers with relevant expertise [50, 54, 56].

A recurring theme in RBMCs was discontinuity of multidisciplinary care. Pediatric services often provide coordinated, specialist multidisciplinary input, whereas adult services tend to be more fragmented, with less disease-specific expertise [23, 25, 27]. This pattern is supported by broader research showing that young people with complex conditions are more vulnerable to receiving fragmented care in adulthood, especially due to the absence of designated responsible physicians or transition coordinators [14, 56, 66].

Non-medical domains such as education, employment, and social participation were only explicitly addressed in a minority of studies, despite evidence that young adults with RBMCs face substantial challenges in these areas [67]. Studies involving tULRD, for example, highlighted significant barriers to vocational participation [28], while research in OI and skeletal dysplasias emphasized the importance of psychosocial support, self-management skills, and opportunities to build independence [40]. This aligns with wider literature showing that chronic conditions and disabilities can adversely affect vocational trajectories, and that structured vocational and psychosocial support during transition improves participation and quality of life [6, 8, 68], reinforcing the need for transition models that extend beyond medical care to cover broader social and developmental needs.

Self-Management, Independence, and Empowerment

Only a small number of studies have specifically examined self-management and independence, yet they have revealed that young adults with RBMCs actively develop strategies to manage symptoms and daily functioning, and draw on family, community, and peer support to sustain independence, in the absence of structured adult care follow-up [33, 34].

These findings suggest that young people possess substantial adaptive capacity, but that healthcare systems often fail to support or build upon these strengths. Further, transition care is not merely a transfer of services, but a developmental process that involves acquiring self-management skills, autonomy, and health literacy [69]. Therefore, empowerment, peer support, connection to patient advocacy groups, and shared decision-making are important facilitators of independence, highlighting the value of integrating psychosocial and educational resources into transition planning.

Multidisciplinary Care, Timing, and Structured Processes

Despite the heterogeneity of RBMCs, the recommendations identified across expert opinion and review articles were remarkably consistent. Early preparation, individualized planning, and assessment of transition readiness were widely endorsed [11, 15, 16, 40, 50], although the appropriate age to initiate transition remains debated, with some proposing age thresholds [11, 16, 35, 37, 38], while others emphasize developmental maturity, disease stability, and the capacity of the adult care sector [15, 39–41]. This reflects the broader recognition in all transition processes, that rigid age cut-offs are problematic and that transition timing should consider cognitive, emotional, and social readiness, in addition to maturity age [63, 69].

Nevertheless, validated tools to assess transition readiness, such as the TRAQ, are widely recommended in generic transition frameworks [70]. In RBMCs, experts suggested that such tools could be adapted to these conditions [42, 43], but no studies reported systematic use of readiness measures in these populations. This represents a missed opportunity to structure preparation, tailor interventions, and monitor progress.

Multidisciplinary teams with expertise in RBMCs were consistently described as essential, typically including physicians (pediatric and adult specialists), related healthcare professionals (e.g., physiotherapists, occupational therapists, psychologists), and, in some cases, social workers and administrative staff. Like other chronic and rare conditions [56, 66, 71], the presence of a transition coordinator or designated professional was highlighted as an essential facilitator for continuity, coordination of complex care, and navigation across services [11, 15, 16, 40, 50]. However, few RBMC studies provided empirical data on how such roles are implemented or evaluated in practice.

Stakeholder Involvement and Social Support Networks

Importantly, several studies stressed the need to involve all stakeholders, including patients, families, healthcare professionals, social workers, and patient advocacy groups, in the design and implementation of transition pathways. This aligns with broader principles of person-centered care and reflects the complex nature of RBMCs [72].

In this scoping review, patient advocacy groups were particularly visible in expert consensus [15, 35] and surveys capturing perceived priorities and barriers [23, 26]. Their involvement helps to highlight issues that might be overlooked in clinician-centric models, such as perceived lack of adult expertise, resistance or fear of adult services, and the psychosocial burden of transition. The role of advocacy organizations is increasingly recognized in the broader context of rare disease care, not only as source of peer support and information, but also as partnership in designing and evaluating services [73].

However, participation of patients, families, and advocacy groups in empirical transition research remains limited, especially in study design, governance, and evaluation. There is a need to move from consultation to genuine co-creation, where stakeholders help shape research questions, outcomes, and models of transition care.

Implications for Practice and Policy

The findings of this scoping review underscore the urgency of developing structured multidisciplinary transition pathways tailored to the needs of young people with RBMCs. Early preparation, readiness assessment, and individualized planning should be standard components of care. Transition coordinators can play a pivotal role in ensuring continuity, particularly in health systems where adult expertise in RBMCs is limited. Finally, integrating psychosocial, educational, and vocational support is essential to address the broader developmental challenges faced by this population.

At the policy level, sustainable funding mechanisms and organizational structures are needed to support transition coordination, multidisciplinary teams, and dedicated adult services for RBMCs. National or regional guidelines, aligned with broader transition frameworks but adapted for RBMCs, could provide a foundation for consistent and equitable care. Engaging patient advocacy groups and all stakeholders in guideline development, healthcare service design, and evaluation is critical to ensuring that models of care are feasible, acceptable, and responsive to real-world needs.

Strengths and Limitations of this Scoping Review

This scoping review has several strengths. It systematically mapped the literature across a wide range of RBMCs, study designs, and stakeholder perspectives, providing a broad overview of current knowledge and gaps. It also synthesized findings in relation to established transition principles, situating RBMC-specific evidence within the wider field of youth-to-adult healthcare transition.

However, limitations should be acknowledged. The underlying evidence is heterogeneous and dominated by expert opinion rather than empirical data, which constrains the strength of inferences. The concentration of studies in high-income settings limits generalizability to other contexts. As per scoping review methodology, a formal quality assessment of the individual studies was not performed, although scoping reviews typically do not require a formal quality assessment [79, 80]. Lastly, the rapidly evolving nature of both RBMC care and transition frameworks means that new evidence may have emerged after the end of the search period.

Conclusion

Transition care for young people with RBMCs remains an underdeveloped and understudied area, despite the lifelong nature of these conditions and the complexity of their management. Existing evidence, though limited, is remarkably consistent in highlighting dissatisfaction with current transition processes, limited adult-care expertise, and substantial unmet psychosocial and vocational needs. Although expert consensus provides valuable guidance, there is an urgent need for empirical research (co-produced with stakeholders, to develop, implement, and evaluate structured transition models tailored to RBMCs), validated tools, and systemic interventions to support effective, equitable, and person-centered transition pathways. Strengthening transition care for RBMCs is essential to improving long-term outcomes and ensuring continuity of care across the life course.
