Stabilizing Transformer Training Through Consensus
Shyam Venkatasubramanian, Sean Moushegian, Michael Lin, Mir Park, Ankit Singhal, Connor Lee

Abstract
Standard attention-based transformers are known to exhibit instability under learning rate overspecification during training, particularly at high learning rates. While various methods have been proposed to improve resilience to such overspecification by modifying the optimization procedure, fundamental architectural innovations to this end remain underexplored. In this work, we illustrate that the consensus mechanism, a drop-in replacement for attention, stabilizes transformer training across a wider effective range of learning rates. We formulate consensus as a graphical model and provide extensive empirical analysis demonstrating improved stability across learning rate sweeps on text, DNA, and protein modalities. We further propose a hybrid consensus-attention framework that preserves performance while improving stability. We provide theoretical analysis characterizing the properties of consensus.

Introduction

The literature on machine learning and language modeling is rich with results linking model performance to scale
(
17
)
, reinforcing an impetus to increase model size. While the visible challenges of scaling are the
heavy
constraints of securing compute and data, equally important are the subtler and more
delicate
challenges of balancing interdependent hyperparameters within a narrow region of optimality across many orders of magnitude in model size.

In this work, we consider the latter
delicate
challenge via the lens of learning rate robustness in transformer training. For large pretraining runs, sensitivity to high learning rates is a major practical concern: training instability can manifest as explicit numerical overflows or loss curves that subtly plateau above the attainable optimum, which degrades model performance
(
31
)
. Fixing such runs can require expensive restarts from earlier checkpoints at reduced learning rates
(
18
)
. To mitigate failures, practitioners often tune learning rates on smaller models and rely on heuristic methods
(
12
)
to extrapolate suitable hyperparameters to larger models. Alternatively, practitioners perform limited training runs on larger models
(
46
)
to ablate the learning rate, though this quickly becomes expensive at scale.

Attention-based transformers have been shown to exhibit training instability at high learning rates
(
45
;
47
;
18
)
. Recent empirical studies further suggest that a key cause of this instability is concentrated in the attention mechanism itself: the spectra
\sigma(W_{q}^{\top}W_{k})
of the key and query matrices become ill-conditioned as the learning rate is increased
(
32
)
. As the transformer backbone and attention mechanism are increasingly used beyond text—for DNA, proteins, and other modalities—developing alternative mechanisms more robust to high learning rates is of practical interest.

Much of the work on mitigating instability at high learning rates has focused on optimizers. For example, it has been shown stochastic gradient descent (SGD) performs worse for transformers than the widely used AdamW
(
21
;
25
)
, with evidence this gap is related to block-wise heterogeneity in the Hessian
(
48
)
as well as the
\ell^{\infty}
geometry of the loss
(
44
)
. Learning rate warmup using a narrow window of maximum learning rates has been shown necessary for stable large-scale training
(
18
;
42
)
. Regularization of the parameter norm (e.g.,
\ell_{2}
-regularization) has been shown to stabilize training by bounding the parameters in a well-behaved neighborhood of the loss landscape
(
38
)
. Other methods, such as momentum and coordinate-wise effective step size scaling
(
25
)
, refine optimizers and improve convergence across learning rates.

In contrast, we shift our focus from the optimization procedure to architecture. Unbounded
magnitude
of hidden embeddings can destabilize training, and adding LayerNorm to architectures can mitigate this issue
(
20
)
. In a similar vein, we posit that training can be destabilized by a layer exhibiting unbounded
frequency
, where highly-related sequence positions exhibit wildly different representations. We hypothesize that a simple architectural regularizer, like LayerNorm, can stabilize training.

To this end, we rigorously study the
consensus
mechanism, a drop-in replacement for attention proposed in
36
. The inputs to consensus are a sequence of embeddings pertaining to a
consensus graph
, whose edges connect related sequence positions. Consensus interchanges information between these positions via the consensus graph, and finally outputs transformed embeddings. In this paper, we show that this interchange is equivalent to a low-pass filter on the frequency of embeddings. Like attention, consensus admits
self-consensus
, which relates an input sequence to itself, and
cross-consensus
, which incorporates auxiliary context; both can be extended to multi-head formulations. Through empirical results across text, DNA, and proteins, we show that consensus tolerates a wider range of learning rates than attention, with the difference most pronounced in the high learning rate regime. Finally, we present a hybrid consensus-attention architecture demonstrating how consensus can be integrated into transformers to improve stability while preserving attention-level performance.

This paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we provide relevant background material. In Section
3
, we outline the consensus mechanism. In Section
4
, we analyze relevant theoretical properties of consensus. In Section
5
, we present empirical results benchmarking hybrid consensus-attention and consensus-based transformers versus attention-based transformers on text, DNA, and proteins. We conclude the paper in Section
6
. In the Appendix, we provide proofs and derivations, additional empirical results, architecture and training details, and sample generations.

1.1
Graph-Based Models

Numerous datasets comprise multiple features of distinct types, which are statistically related. Some features (e.g., adjacent pixels in an image) are strongly related, while others (e.g., opposite-corner pixels) are related weakly. Representing features as nodes and dependencies as weighted edges, one can construct a graph that captures the data’s joint dependency structure. These inter-feature dependencies may arise from causality, physical proximity, or correlation under the data-generating distribution. Prior works have incorporated graphs into generative, regression, and encoding models, as well as regularization methods
(
24
)
.

Bayesian networks
(
16
)
and Markov random fields
(
41
;
5
;
23
)
are widely studied probabilistic generative models
(
27
)
. Given a known and fixed feature-dependency graph, these methods learn a joint distribution over the features. In some data types, dependencies are naturally hierarchical—one feature influences others, which in turn influence additional features—in which case a directed acyclic graph is an appropriate representation. In other settings, pairwise interactions are symmetric, and undirected graphs provide a better model. Accordingly, Bayesian networks and Markov random fields parameterize distributions on directed and undirected dependency graphs, respectively, enabling sampling and inference.

Beyond generative modeling, graph-based models also have applications in nonparametric regression. When a learned or known graph with weighted adjacency matrix
W\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
and diagonal degree matrix
D\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
marks the similarity of the data, the nonparametric regression method of Laplacian smoothing can be used for denoising. Let
X\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
be a noised version of
Y\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
, where
X_{i}=Y_{i}+\epsilon_{i}
with
\epsilon_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(0_{d},\sigma^{2}I_{d})
.
Observing only
X
, Laplacian smoothing
(
35
;
13
)
approximates the unobserved clean

by
\hat{Y}
. For sufficiently small

\displaystyle\hat{Y}

\displaystyle=\argmin_{Y}\big(\|X-Y\|^{2}+\rho Y^{\top}LY\big)

\displaystyle=(I+\rho L)^{-1}X\approx(I-\rho L)X

\displaystyle=(I-\rho D)X+\rho WX,

(1)

where
L=D-W
is the graph Laplacian. In this formulation, Laplacian smoothing is a

, as it continuously propagates information between neighboring nodes in a graphical structure
(
9
;
29
;
6
;
43
)
. Researchers have shown that existing mechanisms asymptotically behave as a consensus algorithm
(
1
)
.

Graphical methods have additionally been incorporated into encoding models. For data with a known dependency structure, graph convolutional networks
(
22
)
extract latent representations. Given a weighted graph with adjacency matrix

and diagonal degree matrix

2.1
Energy and Smoothing

For a connected, directed, and weighted graph

A

\mathcal{E}(u)=\frac{1}{2}\sum\nolimits_{i,j}W_{i,j}(u_{i}-u_{j})^{2}=u^{\top}Lu.

Moreover, the gradient of the energy is given by:

\displaystyle\nabla\mathcal{E}(u)

\displaystyle=\left[\sum\nolimits_{j}(W_{i,j}+W_{j,i})(u_{i}-u_{j})\right]_{i}

\displaystyle=(L+L^{\top})u=2L_{\text{sym}}u.

(6)

We note self-connections in

Consensus Mechanism

In this section, we formalize the

36

The consensus mechanism is a drop-in replacement for attention in transformers that uses the update of Eq. (
7

(V,E)
with

, consensus takes as input a vector of source embeddings

corresponding to nodes

, the source embedding

is mapped to

, and for each edge

, the pair of source embeddings

is mapped to a

R^{(i,j)}
. We perform a gradient update step to decrease the energy
\mathcal{E}(u)

\mathcal{E}(u)
is given by:

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(u)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\{i,j\}\in E}(u^{(i)}-u^{(j)})^{\top}R^{(i,j)}(u^{(i)}-u^{(j)}),

(14)

where

5.2
Estimation of Max Learning Rate From the Hessian

Using the terminal checkpoints from Section
5.1
, we estimate a maximum stable learning rate from the loss Hessian encountered during the optimizer updates. For training step
t
, let
\theta_{t}
denote the model parameters,
u_{t}
be the optimizer step direction at step
t
, and
\alpha
be the learning rate, so that
\theta_{t+1}=\theta_{t}+\alpha u_{t}
. A second-order Taylor expansion for the batch loss
L_{t}
around
\theta_{t}
gives us:

\displaystyle L_{t}(\theta_{t+1})-L_{t}(\theta_{t})\leq

(17)

\displaystyle\qquad\alpha\langle\nabla L_{t},u_{t}\rangle+\alpha^{2}\frac{\langle u_{t},\nabla^{2}L_{t}u_{t}\rangle}{2}+\alpha^{3}O(\|u_{t}\|^{3}).

When the step direction is a descent direction for the batch loss, we have that
\langle\nabla L_{t},u_{t}\rangle<0
, and if the local curvature along the step is positive, then
\langle u_{t},\nabla^{2}L_{t}u_{t}\rangle>0
. If these conditions hold, we may define:

\displaystyle\alpha_{\max}(t)

\displaystyle=-2\langle\nabla L_{t},u_{t}\rangle\,/\,\langle u_{t},\nabla^{2}L_{t}u_{t}\rangle,

(18)

where Eq. (
17
) has negative RHS, implying batch loss drops up to second order when
\alpha<\alpha_{\max}(t)
. If
\langle\nabla L_{t},u_{t}\rangle>0
, we define
\alpha_{\max}=0
. Otherwise, if
\langle u_{t},\nabla^{2}L_{t}u_{t}\rangle<0
, then
\alpha_{\max}=\infty
. In the case of SGD
u_{t}=-\nabla L_{t}(\theta_{t})
, we have:

\alpha_{\max}(t)=\big[2\|\nabla L_{t}\|^{2}\big]\,/\,\big[\nabla L_{t}^{\top}\nabla^{2}L_{t}\,\nabla L_{t}\big],

(19)

which implies
\alpha_{\max}(t)\geq 2/\|\nabla^{2}L_{t}\|_{2}
. Therefore the standard
(
2
)
SGD stability bound of
\|\nabla^{2}L_{t}\|_{2}\leq C
and
\alpha<2/C
implies
\alpha<\alpha_{\max}(t)
.

Conclusion

In this paper, we reformulated consensus using graph spectral theory, relating information propagation to algebraic connectivity and deriving bounds for graph classes relevant to sequential data. Across text, DNA, and proteins, we found that consensus was more robust to learning rate overspecification than attention, and that a hybrid consensus-attention architecture preserved attention-level performance while inheriting the stability benefits of consensus at scale. We further connected these empirical findings to effective step sizes by estimating a directional maximum stable learning rate from the loss Hessian along optimizer update directions. We note that consensus requires a choice of graph connectivity that is not always available
a priori
, motivating future work on adaptive or learned graph construction.

Impact Statement

This paper improves the training stability of transformers by increasing robustness to learning-rate overspecification. By reducing the need for extensive hyperparameter tuning and mitigating training instabilities, the method may lower the practical cost and expertise required to train large generative models, which can benefit engineering applications but may also broaden access to capable generative modeling in ways that raise familiar dual-use concerns.

Appendix A
Additional Theoretical Background

We extend the scalar-valued graph-signal formulation in Section
2.3
of the main text to the vector-valued setting used by consensus. For completeness, we summarize the corresponding block-matrix construction and notation below.

A.1
Vector-Valued Signals

We construct a block adjacency matrix
W\in\mathbb{R}^{Nd\times Nd}
as:

W=\begin{bmatrix}W_{0,0}&\cdots&W_{0,N-1}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
W_{N-1,0}&\cdots&W_{N-1,N-1}\end{bmatrix},

(21)

where
W_{i,j}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
is positive definite (
W_{i,j}\succ 0
) when nodes
i
and
j
share an edge, and
W_{i,j}=0_{d\times d}
otherwise. We define the
per-node out-degree
matrices:

D_{i}\;=\;\sum\nolimits_{j\neq i}W_{i,j},

(22)

and assemble the (block-diagonal) degree matrix
D\in\mathbb{R}^{Nd\times Nd}
as:

D=\begin{bmatrix}D_{0}&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&\cdots&D_{N-1}\end{bmatrix}.

(23)

We then construct the (generally non-symmetric) Laplacian
L=D-W\in\mathbb{R}^{Nd\times Nd}
and symmetrize it as
L_{\text{sym}}=\tfrac{1}{2}(L+L^{\top})
. Analogous to the scalar-valued case,
L_{\text{sym}}
has
d
zero eigenvalues,
\lambda_{0}=\lambda_{1}=\cdots=\lambda_{d-1}=0
, with corresponding eigenvectors given by block-permutations of:

v_{0}=\mathrm{cat}(1_{d},0_{d},\cdots,0_{d})\in\mathbb{R}^{Nd}.

(24)

The consensus update is given by Eq. (
7
), where indexing and summation are interpreted over the
d\times d
block structure.

Proof of Remark
4.1
.

The resulting graph Laplacian
L
is a matrix whose columns are populated by the cyclic permutations of the vector,
\Pi_{N}^{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
, where
\Pi_{N}^{w}
is given by:

\displaystyle\Pi_{N}^{w}=\begin{bmatrix}2w&\smash{\underbrace{\begin{matrix}-1&\cdots&-1\end{matrix}}_{w}}&\smash{\underbrace{\begin{matrix}0&\cdots&0\end{matrix}}_{N-2w-1}}&\smash{\underbrace{\begin{matrix}-1&\cdots&-1\end{matrix}}_{w}}\end{bmatrix}^{\top}.

Following
(
34
)
, we calculate the eigenvalues of this symmetric circulant matrix. Of note, we see that for any
k_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
, the following is an eigenvector for
\lambda_{1}
:

\displaystyle\phi_{k}^{N,k_{0}}=\cos\left(\frac{2\pi(k+k_{0})}{N}\right),\quad\text{for:}\quad 0\leq k<N.

(25)

∎

Proof of Remark
4.2
.

We leverage a comparison to the Laplacian of
C^{w}_{2N}=(V,E)
. Let
L
and
\overline{L}
be the graph Laplacians of
P^{w}_{N}
and
C^{w}_{2N}
respectively. Let
V_{1}=\{0,...,N-1\}
and
V_{2}=\{N,...,2N-1\}
such that
V=V_{1}\sqcup V_{2}
. Let
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
be the induced subgraphs of
V_{1}
and
V_{2}
respectively with edge sets
E_{1}
and
E_{2}
, and note that
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
are isomorphic to
P^{w}_{N}
. Define the following 2 to 1 projection
\pi:V\to V_{1}
:

\displaystyle\pi(k)=\begin{cases}k&\text{if}\ 0\leq k\leq N-1\\
2N-1-k&\text{if}\ N\leq k\leq 2N-1.\end{cases}

(26)

For a function
v
on
V_{1}
, let
\overline{v}
be the following extension of
v
on all of
V
given by
\overline{v}_{k}=v_{\pi(k)}
. Thus we have
\|\overline{v}\|^{2}=2\|v\|^{2}
, and
\overline{v}\perp\mathbf{1}_{2N}
if and only if
v\perp\mathbf{1}_{N}
. Subsequently, we have that:

\displaystyle\langle\overline{v},\overline{L}\overline{v}\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum\nolimits_{\{i,j\}\in E}(\overline{v}_{i}-\overline{v}_{j})^{2}

(27)

\displaystyle=\sum\nolimits_{\{i,j\}\in E_{1}}(\overline{v}_{i}-\overline{v}_{j})^{2}+\sum\nolimits_{\{i,j\}\in E_{2}}(\overline{v}_{i}-\overline{v}_{j})^{2}+\sum\nolimits_{\{i,j\}\in E\setminus(E_{1}\cup E_{2})}(\overline{v}_{i}-\overline{v}_{j})^{2}

(28)

\displaystyle=2\sum\nolimits_{\{i,j\}\in E_{1}}(v_{i}-v_{j})^{2}+\sum\nolimits_{\{i,j\}\in E\setminus(E_{1}\cup E_{2})}(v_{\pi(i)}-v_{\pi(j)})^{2}.

(29)

To upper bound the second term, we rely upon the assumption that
w<N/2
and note that the projection
\pi
satisfies
\{\pi(i),\pi(j)\}\in E_{1}
whenever
\pi(i)\neq\pi(j)
and
\{i,j\}\in E
. We also note that the second sum is indexed by edges that cross
V_{1}
and
V_{2}
, i.e., in each term, exactly one of
i,j
is in
V_{1}
and the other index in
V_{2}
. Given
k,l\in E_{1}
, there’s at most 2 cross-edges that project to to
\{k,l\}
under
\pi
. Hence, after this double counting, we obtain:

\displaystyle\langle\overline{v},\overline{L}\overline{v}\rangle

\displaystyle\leq 2\sum\nolimits_{\{i,j\}\in E_{1}}(v_{i}-v_{j})^{2}+2\sum\nolimits_{\{i,j\}\in E_{1}}(v_{i}-v_{j})^{2}\leq 4\langle v,Lv\rangle.

(30)

Next, since the second term of Eq. (
29
) is non-negative, we automatically get
2\langle v,Lv\rangle\leq\langle\overline{v},\overline{L}\overline{v}\rangle
and thus:

2\braket{v,Lv}\leq\braket{\overline{v},\overline{L}\overline{v}}\leq 4\braket{v,Lv}.

(31)

Using Eq. (
31
) and the fact that
\|\overline{v}\|^{2}=2\|v\|^{2}
, we have that:

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}(C^{R}_{2N})\leq\frac{\langle\overline{v},\overline{L}\overline{v}\rangle}{\|\overline{v}\|^{2}}\leq 2\frac{\langle v,Lv\rangle}{\|v\|^{2}},

(32)

by the variational characterization of
\lambda_{1}
. Minimizing the RHS over all
v\perp\mathbf{1}
yields:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\lambda_{1}(C^{R}_{2N})\leq\lambda_{1}(P^{w}_{N}).

(33)

Consider the eigenfunction
\phi=\phi^{2N,\tfrac{1}{2}}
of
\bar{L}
defined in Eq. (
25
) corresponding to eigenvalue
\lambda_{1}(C_{2N}^{w})
on
C_{2N}^{w}
. A simple computation shows that
\phi_{k}=\phi_{\pi(k)}
and hence
\phi=\bar{\gamma}
for some function (not necessarily an eigenfunction)
\gamma
on
G_{1}
. Thus:

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}(P^{w}_{N})\leq\frac{\langle\gamma,L\gamma\rangle}{\|\gamma\|^{2}}\leq\frac{1}{2}\frac{\langle\phi,\overline{L}\phi\rangle}{\|\phi\|^{2}/2}=\lambda_{1}(C^{w}_{2N}).

(34)

Appendix C
Variants of the Consensus Mechanism

In this section, we formalize the cross-consensus, multi-headed self-consensus, and multi-headed cross-consensus mechanisms described in the main text. We further outline an adaptation of rotary positional embeddings (RoPE) for consensus.

Across all algorithms, we define the following functions. For
\Psi\in\mathbb{R}^{\Omega\times\omega}
, we define
\text{RS}_{\Omega,\omega}:\mathbb{R}^{\Omega\omega}\mapsto\mathbb{R}^{\Omega\times\omega}
(reshape) and
\text{RN}:\mathbb{R}^{\Omega\times\omega}\mapsto\mathbb{R}^{\Omega\times\omega}
(row-norm) by:

\displaystyle\left[\text{RS}_{\Omega,\omega}(\Psi)\right]_{i,j}

\displaystyle=\Psi_{i\omega+j},\quad\quad\quad\left[\text{RN}(\Psi)\right]_{i,j}=\begin{cases}{\Psi_{i,j}}/{\|\Psi_{i,:}\|}&\text{ if }\|\Psi_{i,:}\|>0,\\
\Psi_{i,j}&\text{ else}.\end{cases}

(36)

We define
\sigma^{+}(\cdot)
to be the softplus activation function, and denote SCWM and CCWM as the Self-Consensus Weight Matrix (Algorithm
2
) and the Cross-Consensus Weight Matrix (Algorithm
3
), respectively. We note that
b_{s}
cancels in the consensus update; as such, practitioners may omit it from Algorithm
1
without affecting computation.

C.1
Cross-Consensus

In multimodal applications (e.g., the protein setting in Section
5
), we seek to imbue information from an auxiliary
context
modality into the
source
modality. Let the source embeddings be
y=[y^{(0)},\dots,y^{(N-1)}]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times d}
, and let the context embeddings be
c=[c^{(0)},\dots,c^{(J-1)}]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{J\times d}
. We outline cross-consensus, a drop-in replacement for cross-attention, which performs a consensus-style update on a bipartite graph.

Let
V
and

be node sets with
|V|=N
and
|V^{\prime}|=J

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(u,v)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{(i,j)\in E_{+}}\big(u^{(i)}-v^{(j)}\big)^{\top}R^{(i,j)}\big(u^{(i)}-v^{(j)}\big),

(i,j)\in E_{+}

C.3
Rotary Positional Embeddings for Consensus

We describe rotary positional embeddings (RoPE)
(
37
)
for self and cross-consensus. In settings where the node set has a natural positional order (e.g., window-path graphs), we inject position information by
rotating
the projected node embeddings before forming edge-wise disagreements in the consensus update. Suppose
d
is even and fix a base
B>0
. For a position index
p\in\mathbb{N}
, we define the angle vector
\bm{\theta}(p)\in\mathbb{R}^{d/2}
by:

\displaystyle\bm{\theta}(p)

\displaystyle=[\theta(p)_{0},\ldots,\theta(p)_{(d/2)-1}]^{\top},\qquad\theta(p)_{k}=p\,B^{-2k/d},\ \forall k\in\{0,\ldots,(d/2)-1\}.

(41)

Suppose
\cos(\bm{\theta}(p))
and
\sin(\bm{\theta}(p))
denotes the elementwise cosine and sine of
\bm{\theta}(p)
, repeated to length
d
(so the Hadamard products below are well-defined). We further define the fixed permutation-sign matrix:
\smash{\mathcal{P}=\Big[\begin{smallmatrix}\mathbf{0}_{d/2\times d/2}&-\mathbf{I}_{d/2}\\
\mathbf{I}_{d/2}&\mathbf{0}_{d/2\times d/2}\end{smallmatrix}\Big]}
, which swaps the two
d/2
-length halves and applies a sign flip.

RoPE for Self-Consensus.

For each node
i\in V
, after Line
4
of Algorithm
1
, we rotate the projected embedding as:

\displaystyle\tilde{u}^{(i)}\;\coloneqq\;u^{(i)}\odot\cos(\bm{\theta}(i))\;+\;(\mathcal{P}u^{(i)})\odot\sin(\bm{\theta}(i)).

(42)

Then, for every
(i,j)\in E
, the gradient update forms edge disagreements using
\tilde{u}^{(i)}-\tilde{u}^{(j)}
in place of
u^{(i)}-u^{(j)}
.

RoPE for Cross-Consensus.

For each source node
i\in V
and context node
j\in V^{\prime}
, after Lines
4
and
7
of Algorithm
4
, we rotate the projected embeddings as:

\displaystyle\tilde{u}^{(i)}\;\coloneqq\;u^{(i)}\odot\cos(\bm{\theta}(i))\;+\;(\mathcal{P}u^{(i)})\odot\sin(\bm{\theta}(i)),

(43)

\displaystyle\tilde{v}^{(j)}\;\coloneqq\;v^{(j)}\odot\cos(\bm{\theta}(j))\;+\;(\mathcal{P}v^{(j)})\odot\sin(\bm{\theta}(j)).

(44)

Then, for every
(i,j)\in E^{+}
, the gradient update forms edge disagreements using
\tilde{u}^{(i)}-\tilde{v}^{(j)}
in place of
u^{(i)}-v^{(j)}
.
