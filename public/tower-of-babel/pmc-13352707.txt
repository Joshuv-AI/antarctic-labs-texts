Genome-wide analysis identifies LliNAC80 as a potential key regulator of abiotic stress resilience in endangered mangrove Lumnitzera littorea
Authors: Min Li, Wenxi Huang, Chumiao Liang, Jing Ou, Xiang Jin, Yuchen Yang, Ying Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLumnitzera littorea (Jack) Voigt is a true mangrove species of important ecological and medicinal values; however, it is highly sensitive to environmental fluctuations and is currently endangered in China. Thus, elucidating the molecular mechanisms underlying its stress tolerance is essential not only for its conservation, but also for identifying valuable genetic resources to improve plant resilience to stresses. NAC transcription factors represent a major family involved in regulating plant responses to abiotic stressors. In this study, a total of 84 NAC genes were identified in the L. littorea genome, which were categorized into 16 subfamilies. These genes were unevenly distributed across the 12 chromosomes, with dispersed duplication identified as the primary driver for the expansion of L. littorea NAC (LliNAC) gene family. Comparative transcriptome analysis revealed that 34 LliNAC genes were up-regulated in L. littorea in response to cold stress. Among these, LliNAC80 emerged as a key regulator of the transcriptional remodeling of genes associated with chilling sensing, antioxidation, and osmoprotection. Furthermore, overexpression of LliNAC80 substantially enhanced the tolerance of Arabidopsis thaliana seedlings to low temperature, hyper-salinity, and osmotic stress. These findings highlight the functional importance of LliNAC genes in environmental adaptation of L. littorea, and identify LliNAC80 as a promising candidate for future bioengineering-based improvement of stress resilience in mangroves and crops.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-026-08995-9.Keywords: endangered mangrove, NAC gene family, abiotic stress resilience, transcriptional regulation, genetic improvement

Introduction

Mangrove forests are vital for coastal protection, atmospheric carbon sequestration, and biodiversity conservation in tropical and subtropical wetlands [1]. Despite their importance, these ecosystems have faced severe loss and degradation over the past 50 years due to the combined pressure of climate change and human activities [2], leading to a decline in functional diversity and ecosystem services. Lumnitzera littorea (Jack) Voigt, a true mangrove species of the family Combretaceae, holds significant ecological and medicinal values [3, 4]. Its bark, twigs, leaves, and fruits are rich in bioactive compounds, including macrolides, polyketones, flavonoids, and phenolic glycosides, which are used across Asia for anti-angiogenesis, anti-inflammatory treatments, dermatology, leishmaniasis, and hepatoprotection [5–7]. While common in Southeast Asia and northern Australia, L. littorea in China is restricted to small, fragmented patches in Hainan Island [8]. These local populations further suffer from low reproductive capacity and limited genetic diversity, undermining their poor evolutionary potential and resilience to environmental fluctuations [9–11]. L. littorea was designated a State First-class Protection Tree Species in China in 2021 (https://www.forestry.gov.cn/c/www/lczc/10746.jhtml), and the preservation and restoration of this species is now a matter of extreme urgency.

Reforestation is an efficient strategy for restoring the population of rare species. Recently, significant efforts have been dedicated to the artificial breeding and replanting of L. littorea seedlings in coastal areas. However, this species is highly sensitive to environmental shift, including soil salinity, water availability, and ambient temperature [3, 12]. These abiotic stressors can negatively impact the growth, development and survival of L. littorea, ultimately undermining reforestation success [13]. For instance, fourteen L. littorea trees were introduced to Hainan Dongzhai Harbor National Nature Reserve in 2006 for ex-situ conservation; however, the entire cohort perished during the extreme cold of the 2008 Chinese winter storm [14]. Thus, a comprehensive understanding of the mechanisms underlying stress tolerance of L. littorea is essential for identifying candidate targets for future genetic improvement of its stress resilience.

NAC (NAM, ATAF1/2, and CUC2) transcription factor family is a large group of plant-specific proteins that regulate various aspects of plant growth, development, and responses to stress, particularly to drought, salinity and temperature fluctuations [15, 16]. For instance, SNAC1 (STRESS-RESPONSIVE NAC 1) has been identified as a key regulator of stress tolerance across multiple species, including rice (Oryza sativa) [17], barley (Hordeum vulgare) [18], cotton (Gossypium hirsutum) [19], maize (Zea mays) [20], banana (Musa acuminata) [21], and oat (Avena sativa) [22]. In rice, overexpression of SNAC1 increases sensitivity to abscisic acid (ABA) and triggers stomatal closure, thereby enhancing drought resistance [23]. Similarly, loss-of-function of the CaNAC064 gene renders pepper plants (Capsicum annuum) more susceptible to low-temperature stress, while its overexpression in Arabidopsis thaliana confers significant cold tolerance [24]. Furthermore, NAC20 has been shown to positively regulate cold resistance by activating the DREB/CBF-COR pathway and reducing reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation, lipid peroxidation, and cell membrane leakage [25, 26]. These findings underscore the potential key roles of NAC genes in modulating plant responses to abiotic stresses.

With the increasing availability of whole-genome sequences in recent years, the genomic features and functions of numerous transcription factor families have been characterized across various mangrove species [3, 27–29]. These findings not only unravel the molecular mechanisms underlying mangrove stress tolerance and adaptation, but also provide valuable genetic resources for developing stress-resilient plants. For instance, the overexpression of MpNAC1 from the mangrove associate Millettia pinnata substantially improves salt and drought tolerance in A. thaliana and rice, highlighting the potential of NAC genes for the genetic engineering of plant resistance [30]. However, the NAC gene family remains poorly understood in true mangrove species, particularly in the endangered L. littorea.

To address this gap, we performed a genome-wide identification of the NAC gene family in L. littorea, providing a comprehensive characterization of its genomic features, phylogenetic relationships, and evolutionary history. Furthermore, comparative transcriptome analysis and heterologous expression validation were conducted to investigate the biological functions of NAC genes in response to various abiotic stresses. Our findings lay a foundation for future bioengineering efforts aimed at enhancing the tolerance of both mangrove plants and crops to global climate change.

Materials and methods

Identification of NAC family members in the L. littorea genome

The complete genome, protein sequences, and associated annotation files for L. littorea were retrieved from NCBI (GCA_024138015.1) [31], while the A. thaliana genome was obtained from the TAIR database (https://www.arabidopsis.org/). The Hidden Markov Model (HMM) profile for the NAM domain (PF02365) was downloaded from the Pfam (https://pfam.xfam.org/) and used for identifying the LliNAC proteins using HMMER (version 3.2.1) with a default E-value cutoff of 10− 5. Candidate LliNAC proteins were further validated for conserved structural domains using SMART (http://smart.embl-heidelberg.de/), and the sequences with incomplete domains were excluded from further analysis.

Chromosomal coordinates of the LliNAC genes were extracted from the L. littorea genome annotations [31]. LliNAC genes were renamed according to their physical positions and visualized using TBtools [32]. Duplication events and synteny of LliNAC genes were analyzed using MCScanX [33] with default parameters, and the results were visualized using the Circle Gene View and Advanced Circos modules in TBtools. Finally, cis-regulatory elements (CREs) were screened within the promoter regions (2000 bp upstream from the transcription start site) of LliNAC genes using PlantCARE [34].

Molecular property and structural characterization of LliNAC proteins

ProtParam of the ExPASy (http://web.expasy.org/protparam/) was employed to determine the primary physical and chemical properties of each LIiNAC gene, including the number of amino acids (AAs), molecular weight (MW), theoretical isoelectric points (pI), and grand average hydropathy (GRAVY). Subcellular localization of LliNAC proteins was predicted using the BUSCA web server (http://busca.biocomp.unibo.it/) [35]. Conserved motifs within the LliNAC proteins were identified using the MEME suite (version 5.1.0, http://meme-suite.org/tools/meme) with the maximum number of motifs set to 8. The results were subsequently visualized using TBtools.

Phylogenetic analysis and protein interaction network

For phylogenetic analysis, NAC protein sequences from A. thaliana (Table S1) were retrieved from the TAIR database (https://www.arabidopsis.org/). Multiple sequence alignment of NAC proteins from L. littorea and A. thaliana were performed using MEGA-X [36]. A phylogenetic tree was constructed using the Neighbor-Joining (NJ) method with 1000 bootstrap replicates and visualized via ChiPlot (https://www.chiplot.online/). Orthologous relationship between L. littorea and A. thaliana NAC proteins were identified using BLASTP, and a putative protein-protein interaction network for LliNAC proteins was constructed based on the established interaction network of A. thaliana in the STRING database (https://string-db.org/).

Expression analysis of LliNAC genes across tissues via reverse transcription quantitative PCR

Roots, stems, leaves, flowers, and fruits were collected from three L. littorea plants within a natural population at the Tielu Bay Mangrove Natural Reserve (Sanya, Hainan, China). Total RNA was extracted from each tissue sample using the EASYspin Plus Complex Plant RNA Kit (Aidlab Biotechnologies., Ltd, Beijing, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The isolated RNA was subsequently reverse-transcribed into cDNA. The expression levels of LliNAC genes in these tissues were quantified via reverse transcription quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR) using the PerfectStart® Green qPCR SuperMix kit (TransGen Biotech, Beijing, China). Gene-specific primers were designed using Primer 3.0 [37], and sequences are listed in the Table S2. All experiments were performed with three biological replicates. The thermal cycling conditions consisted of 40 cycles of denaturation at 94 °C for 5 s, annealing at 55 °C for 15 s, and extension at 72 °C for 10 s. The USB1 gene, a widely recognized “housekeeping” gene in plants, was used as the internal reference [3, 38, 39], and relative expression levels of the examined genes were normalized using the 2−∆∆CT method [40]. Statistical significance was determined by one-way ANOVA tests with a p-value cut-off of 0.05.

Transcriptome analysis identifies key LliNAC genes associated with cold stress response

To explore the responses of LliNAC genes to cold stress, we utilized the RNA-seq data from our previous study [41], involving L. littorea seedlings grown under control condition (CK; 25 °C day/ 23 °C night) and cold stress (8 °C day/5 °C night). Data pre-processing, read alignment, transcriptional abundance inference, and differential expression analysis were conducted following previously established procedures. LliNAC genes significantly up- and down-regulated upon cold stress were determined using the same log2fold-change and p-value thresholds as in the earlier study. Subsequently, Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) [42] was performed to identify gene modules associated with chilling response. For modules showing significant correlation (p-value < 0.05), we conducted Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis and identified hub genes based on their connectivity and relevance to the cold response. The resulting co-expression network was visualized using Cytoscape [43].

For expression validation, mature L. littorea seeds were harvested from Tielu harbor, Sanya, Hainan, China. The plant materials were formally identified by Cairong Zhong, and leaf voucher specimens were deposited in the Plant Herbarium of Hainan Normal University (accession number: 2018_Ll01–2018_Ll06). Seeds were germinated and grown for one year. Three seedlings were maintained under CK condition (25 °C day/ 23 °C night), while another three were subjected to cold stress (8 °C day/5 °C night) for 48 h. Total RNA was extracted from the leaves of each individual plant. RT-qPCR was conducted to validate the expression profiles of four differentially expressed LliNAC genes (LliNAC17, 49, 76 and 80) under CK and cold stress conditions, following the aforementioned protocols.

Subcellular localization of LliNAC80 protein

To determine the subcellular localization of LliNAC80 protein, the full-length coding sequence (CDS) of LliNAC80 was amplified using specific primer pairs (Table S2). The resulting PCR fragment was purified, inserted into the BsaI/Eco31I site of the pBWA(V)BS expression vector, and created a C-terminal fusion with the Green Fluorescent Protein (GFP) reporter, following the Golden Gate assembly protocol [44]. The recombinant construct (35S:LliNAC80-GFP) and an empty vector control (35S:GFP) were transformed into Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain GV3101 via the heat-shock method. Confirmed transformants were cultured in LB medium supplemented with kanamycin at 28 °C until reaching an OD600 of 0.6–0.8. Cells were harvested by centrifugation, resuspended in infiltration buffer, and incubated in the dark for 2 h. The suspension was then infiltrated into the abaxial surfaces of Australian tobacco (Nicotiana benthamiana) leaves. Following infiltration, the plants were grown under a 16-h light/8-h dark cycle at 25 °C for 48 h. Fluorescence signals were visualized using a Nikon C2 + confocal laser-scanning microscope (Nikon Instruments Inc., Tokyo, Japan).

Functional validation of LliNAC80 under abiotic stress

A. tumefaciens strain GV3101 harboring the LliNAC80-pBWA(V)BS construct was introduced into A. thaliana using the floral dip method [45]. Ten independent transgenic lines were obtained through hygromycin resistance screening, and homozygous T3 progenies from three lines (OE1, OE2 and OE3) were selected for further experiments. LliNAC80 overexpression in these lines was confirmed via RT-qPCR, using the A. thaliana USB1 gene (AT5G51170) as the internal control (Table S2).

Seeds from these three T3 lines were pooled, sterilized and divided into three experimental groups. The first two groups were sown on 1/2 MS medium supplemented with varying concentrations of D-mannitol (0, 100, 200, and 400 mM) or NaCl (0, 50, 100, and 150 mM) to stimulate osmotic and high salinity stress, respectively. These plates were maintained at 22 °C with 40% − 50% humidity and a 16 h light/8 h dark photoperiod. The third group was sown on 1/2 MS medium and exposed to different temperatures (4, 10, 20, and 30 °C) to evaluate the germination under thermal stress. Each treatment utilized 30 seeds per line (WT and OE-LliNAC80) as biological replicates, with germination rates recorded daily for eight days. Subsequently, 20 uniform seedlings at the 2–4 leaf stage were transferred to square Petri dishes and subjected to the same stress conditions to evaluate their root elongation. For each condition, statistical significance for germination rates and root lengths between WT and the OE-LliNAC80 line was determined using Student’s t-test in IBM SPSS Statistics (IBM Corp., NY, USA).

Results

Identification, chromosomal distribution and collinearity of LliNAC genes

A total of 84 NAC genes were identified in the L. littorea genome, and designated LliNAC1 through LliNAC84 based on their chromosomal positions (Fig. 1A). Chromosome (Chr) 11 hosted the largest number of LliNAC genes (16), followed by Chr 2 (10), and Chr 6 and 10 (8 each), while Chr 7 contained the fewest (3). The encoded proteins ranged from 139 to 1053 AAs in length, with MW between 15.216 and 115.79 KDa and PI spanning 4.49 to 9.67 (Table S3). The majority of LliNAC proteins (77) were predicted to be nuclear-localized. However, LliNAC33, LliNAC61 and LliNAC67 were suggested to reside in the chloroplast, while LliNAC38, LliNAC65, LliNAC27 and LliNAC14 were localized to the chloroplast outer membrane, plasma membrane, endomembrane system, and extracellular space, respectively (Table S3). The grand average of hydropathicity for all LliNAC proteins was below 0, mirroring their hydrophilic nature.

Collinearity analysis was conducted to investigate the expansion mechanisms of the LliNAC family. We identified 19 collinear gene pairs involving 33 LliNAC genes (Fig. 1B). Notably, all but two of these pairs were located on different chromosomes, indicating that segmental or dispersed duplication was the primary driver of the expansion of LliNAC gene family.

Phylogenetic relationships and protein interaction network of LliNAC proteins

A phylogenetic tree was constructed using protein sequences from 84 LliNACs and 94 A. thaliana NAC genes to explore their evolutionary relationships (Fig. 1C). The 178 genes were classified into 17 subfamilies. The TIP subfamily consisted of the highest number of NAC genes (11), followed by NAM (9), ONAC003 (8), and OsNAC7 (8). Notably, while the subfamily ANAC001 in A. thaliana includes six NAC proteins, no LliNAC genes were assigned to this branch. This suggests that certain LliNAC genes may have undergone evolutionary divergence or were lost in L. littorea.

A protein-protein interaction network for LliNAC proteins was constructed using the A. thaliana network as a reference. The result showed that a single A. thaliana NAC protein can correspond to one or more L. littorea homologs. Thirty-one LliNAC proteins (Fig. 1D, Table S4) were identified as potential key nodes within the network [46]. Furthermore, LliNAC proteins were found to interact with other transcription factors, including ABF4, CCR1, CCR2, CYP84, and CYP73. These results suggest that a regulatory cascade involving LliNAC proteins and these transcription factors may be essential to their biological functions.

LliNAC gene structure, conserved protein motifs, and promoter cis-regulatory elements

The NAM domain was detected in 83 of the 84 LliNAC proteins (Fig. 2A, Table S5). Absence of this domain in LliNAC84 suggests a potential loss of DNA binding functionality. Within the NAM domain, five subdomains (A-E) were further characterized (Figure S1), consistent with observations in other plant species [47]. Specific structural variations were observed: LliNAC50 and 81 lacked Subdomain A and B, while LliNAC75 and 84 lacked Subdomain D and E (Figure S1), which may affect their dimerization and nuclear localization. In contrast, the absence of the less-conserved Subdomain E in LliNAC20 suggests that it may possess a specific functional role distinct from other LliNAC proteins. Motif analysis identified eight conserved motifs within these subdomains (Fig. 2B): Motif 3 and 8 were located in Subdomain A; Motif 4 in Subdomain B; Motif 2, 5, 6 and 7 in Subdomain E; and Motif 1 in Subdomain C and D.

A total of 31 CREs were identified in the promoter regions of LliNAC genes, categorized into three functional groups (Fig. 2C, Table S6). The first group is primarily involved in the regulation of plant growth and development, including meristem expression, palisade mesophyll cell differentiation, and light responsiveness. Elements associated with light and circadian control were the most abundant, with each LliNAC promoter containing one to seven such CREs. The second group is related to abiotic and biotic stress responses, including those involved in anaerobic induction (found in 75 LliNAC genes), low-temperature responsiveness (33 genes), drought induction (34 genes), and defense/stress responsiveness (31 genes). These LliNAC genes may contribute to the adaptation of L. littorea to coastal intertidal habitats. The third group includes elements responsive to ABA, auxin, gibberellin (GA), salicylic acid (SA) and methyl jasmonate (MeJA), suggesting that these genes participate in phytohormone-mediated signaling pathways.

Tissue-specific expression profiles of LliNAC genes

To investigate the functional divergence of the 84 LliNAC genes, their expression patterns were analyzed across various tissues (roots, stems, leaves, flowers, and fruits) using RT-qPCR. The profiling revealed ample tissue-specific expression across the gene family (Fig. 3). A majority of LliNAC genes exhibited high expression levels in flowers and fruits. In contrast, only two genes, LliNAC48 and LliNAC72, were predominantly expressed in roots. Approximately one-third of the LliNAC genes showed high transcript abundance in leaves and stems. These results underscore the functional specialization of LliNAC genes throughout the development of L. littorea.

Functional characterization of LliNAC80 in abiotic stress resilience of L. littorea

Transcriptome analysis revealed that 36 out of the 84 LliNAC genes were differentially expressed in response to cold stress; of these, 34 genes were up-regulated and two were down-regulated (Fig. 4A). The expression levels of four differentially expressed genes (LliNAC17, 49, 76, and 80) were verified via RT-qPCR, and the results showed a high degree of consistency with the RNA-seq (Fig. 4B). WGCNA identified LliNAC80 as a hub gene within a module significantly and positively correlated with low temperature exposure (Correlation coefficient = 0.968; p-value = 1.51e-3; Fig. 4C). GO enrichment analysis indicated that genes within the module primarily participate in the regulation of kinesin complex, calcium-mediated phospholipid bind, DNA repair, cell redox homeostasis, and trehalose biosynthesis (Fig. 4D). These processes are likely critical for mitigating the deleterious effects of cold stress on L. littorea.

We further took a deep look into the functional significance of LliNAC80 for abiotic stress resilience of L. littorea. Multiple sequence alignment of NAM domains of LliNAC80 with stress-related NAC proteins from A. thaliana, rice, common wheat (Triticum aestivum), and soybean (Glycine max) (UniProt ID or GenBank accession numbers in Table S7) revealed that Subdomain A, C and D of LliNAC80 are highly conserved with minimal gaps or substitutions, likely ensuring its proper dimerization and DNA binding activity. In contrast, the divergence observed in Subdomains B and E may contribute to the functional specificity of LliNAC80 (Figure S2). Fluorescence signals of the GFP-fused protein (35S:LliNAC80-GFP) in tobacco leaves showed that LliNAC80 protein localizes to the nucleus (Fig. 5A). This is consistent with our in silico predictions using BUSCA and observations in other plant species, such as barley [18] and pitaya (Hylocereus undatus) [48]. Subsequently, A. thaliana transgenic lines overexpressing LliNAC80 (OE-LliNAC80) were generated, with the overexpression of LliNAC80 validated by both PCR (Fig. 5B) and RT-qPCR (Fig. 5C).

Under control conditions (20 °C), the germination rate of OE-LliNAC80 seeds reached 96%, significantly higher than the 68% observed in the wild type (WT) (Fig. 5D). While low temperatures reduced germination in all lines, OE-LliNAC80 seeds exhibited a significantly slower rate of decline than WT, indicating enhanced cold tolerance. Similarly, the primary root length of OE-LliNAC80 lines was greater than that of WT at both 20 °C and 10 °C; however, no significant differences were observed under extreme cold (4 °C) (Fig. 5D and E). Furthermore, OE-LliNAC80 lines outperformed WT under heat stress (30 °C) in both seed germination and root growth.

Overexpression of LliNAC80 also improved tolerance to salinity and osmotic stress in A. thaliana. Under NaCl treatment, the germination rate of the transgenic seeds was substantially higher than that of WT (Fig. 5F), and roots of the transgenic seedlings were significantly longer when NaCl concentration reached or exceeded 100 mmol/L (Fig. 5F and G). Similarly, OE-LliNAC80 lines maintained higher germination rates under D-mannitol-induced osmotic stress (Fig. 5H). While root growth was comparable between genotypes at 100 and 400 mmol/L D-mannitol, the transgenic lines exhibited shorter roots than WT specifically at a concentration of 200 mmol/L (Fig. 5H and I).

In summary, our results suggest that LliNAC80 acts as a candidate key transcriptional regulator in L. littorea, potentially modulating Ca2+-dependent cold sensing, DNA damage repair, ROS scavenging and osmotic adjustment to mitigate cold-induced damage (Fig. 6). Additionally, LliNAC80 mediates auxin degradation to reallocate energy from developmental processes to stress responses, thereby prioritizing survival over growth in stressful environments.

Discussion

Tropical intertidal swamps are characterized by high fluctuations in soil moisture, salinity, and ambient temperature, which severely limit the growth and survival of mangrove plants. Long-term adaptive evolution has provided mangroves with morphological and physio-biochemical solutions to cope with these challenges [49]. Understanding the molecular basis of these adaptive mechanisms can not only assist in the conservation of mangrove communities, but also provide valuable genetic resources for improving stress resilience in crops. The NAC family is a large group of transcription factors that play crucial roles in plant growth, development, and abiotic stress responses [20, 50–53]. In this study, we identified 84 LliNAC genes in the L. littorea genome, which were classified into 16 subgroups. This classification is largely consistent with those of A. thaliana and rice [51], reflecting the high evolutionary conservation of NAC proteins in plants. Gene duplication is a fundamental event and a primary driver of adaptation to changing environments [25]. For instance, whole genome duplication (WGD) was found to be a key contributor to the expansion of the WRKY family in Avicennia marina [28]. However, no recent WGD has been observed in L. littorea, except for the gamma paleohexaploidy event shared by all core eudicots [31]. Instead, the expansion of LliNAC genes in L. littorea occurred primarily via dispersed duplication. Notably, the number of NAC members in L. littorea is lower than in other woody plants, such as apple (Malus domestica; 180 genes) [54], Chinese white pear (Pyrus bretschneideri; 183 genes) [55], Betula pendula (114 genes) [56], and poplar (Populus trichocarpa; 163 genes) [52], yet higher than in jujube (55 genes) [47]. This is consistent with the observation by Xie et al. [31] that over two-thirds of gene families in L. littorea have significantly contracted compared to its terrestrial relatives. Such gene family contraction has been suggested as an adaptive strategy for mangrove plants to acclimate to unstable environments, as a smaller gene set may enhance the stability of gene regulatory networks [57].

Despite a decline in gene family members, our study demonstrates the biological significance of LliNAC genes in L. littorea’s response to abiotic stress. Notably, 34 LliNAC genes were significantly up-regulated in L. littorea seedlings under low-temperature exposure (Fig. 4A). Among these, LliNAC80 was identified as a candidate key regulator of cold-responsive gene sets (Fig. 4C). Overexpression of LliNAC80 in A. thaliana successfully alleviated the negative impacts of temperature drops on germination and growth (Fig. 5D and E). Functional enrichment analysis further suggested a putative role of LliNAC80 in modulating the expression of genes involved in Ca2+-mediated phospholipid bind, DNA repair, cell redox homeostasis, and trehalose biosynthesis (Fig. 4C and D). In plants, cold stress alters plasma membrane fluidity and rigidity, triggering a Ca2+ influx. This elevated cytosolic Ca2+ concentration acts as a second messenger, recruiting phospholipid-binding proteins to the membrane surface to activate downstream signaling pathways [58, 59]. Our previous research indicated that under cold stress, Ca2+ signaling likely down-regulates protein phosphorylation in L. littorea, potentially promoting the dephosphorylation of ICE1. This, in turn, releases the transcriptional inhibition of CBF3, enhancing tolerance to low temperatures [41]. Furthermore, cold stress induces photoinhibition and ROS accumulation in L. littorea leaves [12]. LliNAC80-mediated up-regulation of cell redox homeostasis and DNA repair likely improve plant’s ability to neutralize excess ROS and mitigate oxidative damage to genetic materials.

Beyond cold tolerance, LliNAC80 overexpression also enhanced A. thaliana’s resilience to salinity and osmotic stress. Compared to WT, OE-LliNAC80 transgenic lines exhibited significantly higher germination rates under both NaCl and D-mannitol treatments (Fig. 5F and H). Moreover, OE-LliNAC80 seedlings showed superior growth compared to WT, particularly when exposed to high concentrations of NaCl and D-mannitol (Fig. 5G and I). These findings highlight the key regulatory role of LliNAC80 in the transcriptional remodeling of L. littorea and suggest its potential as a promising candidate for the bioengineering-based improvement of stress resilience in both mangrove trees and crops. Despite these insights, there is a primary limitation in the current study. The findings are largely derived from in silico bioinformatic investigations. Further functional validation, such as physiological assays or dual-luciferase reporter assay, is required to experimentally verify the direct regulatory targets and the molecular mechanisms underlying LliNAC80-mediated stress responses in L. littorea.

CREs function as “molecular switches” that interact with transcription factors to dictate the timing, location, and magnitude of gene expression [60]. Genetic manipulation of CREs allows for the fine-tuning of gene transcription without altering the coding sequence or protein properties, offering a precise and flexible strategy for plant trait improvement [61]. In the promoter region of LliNAC80, we identified 25 CREs primarily involved in responsiveness to abiotic stress-related phytohormones, such as ABA, SA, and MeJA (Fig. 2C). These elements represent targets for manipulating LliNAC80 expression via insertion or deletion. For instance, in wheat (Triticum aestivum), the insertion of two MYB binding sites into the TaNAC071-A promoter significantly increased its expression, thereby enhancing drought tolerance [62]. While mangrove research currently lacks a robust genome-editing system for CRE modification, future advancements in genetic engineering will be vital to overcoming this limitation and bolstering mangrove resilience to climate fluctuations.
