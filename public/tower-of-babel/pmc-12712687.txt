Harnessing biomaterials for tuning inflammation: A neutrophil-centric perspective
Authors: Qi-Hui Wang, Wen-Zhi Song, Chun-Yu Han, Shuang Yang, Sheng-Rui Gao, Yi-Cen Ai, Dezhou Wang, Yu-Tong Liu, Wan-Zhong Yin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Inflammation is crucial in combating infection, tissue repair, and immune regulation. However, dysregulated inflammatory responses induce and exacerbate numerous systemic diseases, even posing life-threatening risks. Neutrophils, the most abundant white blood cells in the bloodstream, are vital to the innate immune system. In recent years, neutrophils have been discovered to exhibit phenotypic heterogeneity and functional plasticity, capable of regulating various biological processes and impacting human diseases. Furthermore, neutrophils and their derivatives can serve as natural sources for biomaterials to control inflammatory responses. In this timely review, we begin by outlining the complex functions of neutrophils in inflammatory responses. Subsequently, we summarize the design strategies for neutrophil-based biomaterials. Afterward, we list and analyze the advancements in neutrophil-based anti-inflammatory biomaterials. Finally, we explore the challenges and prospects in this area. This review aims to provide a comprehensive understanding of harnessing neutrophils as a therapeutic target.

Introduction

Recently, researchers have developed numerous diagnostic and therapeutic platforms based on neutrophils. These platforms are spatiotemporally controllable and efficient, offering new strategies to manage inflammation. Given these advancements, this review provides a timely summary of the field. This review aims to provide a comprehensive overview of harnessing neutrophils as a therapeutic target.

An inflammatory response is a biological process initiated by the body's immune system in response to harmful stimuli, such as pathogens, damaged cells, or irritants [1,2]. A moderate inflammatory response can protect the host from harm caused by exogenous or endogenous pathogens, which is crucial for restoring internal homeostasis. However, an uncontrolled inflammatory response or one that fails to subside promptly can damage tissues, leading to a variety of diseases such as sepsis, systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), cardiovascular disorders, and even becoming life-threatening (Scheme 1A) [[3], [4], [5]].Scheme 1(A) Pathogenic and protective activities of neutrophils. Neutrophils serve as the first line of the innate immune response, executing rapid and diverse functions to combat invading pathogens and thereby safeguard the body's health [6]. Neutrophils constitute 40 %–70 % of human white blood cells, and the human bone marrow generates approximately 100–200 billion neutrophils daily [7]. The latest research, employing isotope labeling techniques, has precisely discovered that neutrophils possess a relatively short lifespan with a half-life (0.79 ± 0.25 days; 19 h) and a marrow transit time (5.80 ± 0.42 days) [8]. Traditionally, it was believed that neutrophils eliminate pathogens through phagocytosis, intracellular degradation, degranulation, antimicrobial peptides, reactive oxygen species (ROS), and neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) (Scheme 1B) [9]. However, the heterogeneity and functionality of neutrophils are far more complex than initially thought, particularly in inflammatory conditions, leading to the onset and progression of various diseases [10,11].

Beyond their well-established roles in phagocytosis and pathogen clearance, neutrophils exhibit a sophisticated arsenal of effector mechanisms. One prominent feature is the formation of NETs [12], which can occur through two distinct pathways: suicidal NETosis, a lytic process involving chromatin decondensation and nuclear membrane disruption, and vital NETosis, which allows for NET release without immediate cell death, enabling continued antimicrobial functions. Neutrophils also express a range of pattern recognition receptors, including Toll-like receptor 9, which senses bacterial CpG DNA and amplifies pro-inflammatory responses [13]. Adhesion and migration are critically regulated by integrins through bidirectional signaling: inside-out signaling primes integrins for high-affinity ligand binding, while outside-in signaling, triggered by ligand engagement, reinforces adhesion and promotes cytoskeletal reorganization. Furthermore, the high-affinity Fc gamma receptor I (FcγRI or CD64), upregulated in inflammatory conditions, enhances antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity and phagocytosis [14]. Degranulation, another key function, involves the regulated release of antimicrobial proteins from different granule subsets—azurophilic, specific, gelatinase, and secretory vesicles—each with distinct cargo and mobilization kinetics [15]. Under certain conditions, neutrophils can undergo inflammatory cell death pathways such as pyroptosis, mediated by gasdermin D cleavage and pore formation, which amplifies inflammation [16]. Intriguingly, neutrophils also contribute to the resolution of inflammation through the release of specialized pro-resolving mediators, neutrophil-derived extracellular vesicles, and nanoparticles, which can modulate immune responses and promote tissue repair. This functional diversity underscores the neutrophil's dual role as both a defender and a regulator of inflammation, presenting a rich repertoire of targets for biomaterial-based interventions.

Beyond their classical roles in host defense and inflammation, neutrophils are increasingly recognized as active contributors to tissue repair and resolution of inflammation. Through the release of growth factors, cytokines, and specialized pro-resolving mediators, neutrophils facilitate angiogenesis, extracellular matrix remodeling, and epithelial regeneration [17]. They also engage in crosstalk with macrophages and other stromal cells to promote wound healing and tissue homeostasis [18]. This reparative function highlights the dual nature of neutrophils—not merely as agents of tissue damage but also as orchestrators of recovery—thereby expanding the therapeutic potential of neutrophil-centric biomaterials in regenerative medicine.

In addition to their functional plasticity, neutrophils exhibit remarkable phenotypic heterogeneity, which is increasingly recognized as a critical determinant of their diverse roles in health and disease [7]. Under different pathological contexts, neutrophils can adopt distinct subsets—such as N1/N2 polarization in tumors, low-density granulocytes in autoimmune diseases, and aged or reverse-migrated populations in resolving inflammation [19]. These subsets differ in surface receptor expression, cytokine secretion, and functional outputs such as NETosis, phagocytosis, and immunomodulation capacity. Understanding this heterogeneity is essential for the rational design of neutrophil-targeted therapies, as it enables the selective targeting of pathogenic subsets while sparing or even enhancing protective ones. Biomaterials that can distinguish between neutrophil subpopulations based on specific markers or functional states hold great promise for achieving precise immunomodulation in complex inflammatory diseases.

In this review, we first outline the key and diverse roles of neutrophils in the inflammatory response. Subsequently, we introduce the design strategies for neutrophil-based biomaterials. We then summarize the latest advancements in neutrophil-delivered therapeutics. Finally, we provide perspectives on the development of next generation of neutrophil-based biomaterials. This review aims to offer a reference for the development of advanced anti-inflammatory biomaterials.

The complex functions of neutrophils in inflammatory responses

Milestone events in the discovery and development of neutrophils

Leukocytes were first identified in 1843 by Gabriel Andrai and William Addison [20]. Nevertheless, it was not until 1879 that Paul Ehrlich initially distinguished eosinophils, neutrophils, basophils, and lymphocytes through coal tar dyes for staining purposes [21]. Chemokines activate downstream signaling pathways by binding to their respective receptors on neutrophils and inducing their migration. Between 1910 and 1987, four human neutrophil chemokines were gradually identified [22]. In 1985, Karl Welte purified a cytokine that plays a crucial role in regulating granulocyte proliferation, differentiation, and mobilization from the bone marrow, known as Granulocyte Colony-Stimulating Factor (G-CSF) [23]. In 1994, Timothy A. Springer proposed that neutrophil recruitment follows a three-step model: the migration of neutrophils and monocytes from the vascular system is regulated by at least three distinct molecular signals—selectins, chemokines, and integrins. However, in recent years, the classic leukocyte recruitment cascade has been questioned due to its inapplicability to all organs [24]. Between 1972 and 2004, researchers identified various forms of neutrophil death. Similar to other cell types, neutrophils undergo different modes of death, including apoptosis, pyroptosis, and ferroptosis [25]. In recent years, researchers have discovered new types of cell death such as pyroptosis and cuproptosis, but whether neutrophils can undergo these forms of death remains to be investigated [26]. Additionally, in 2004, Volker Brinkmann discovered a unique form of neutrophil death termed “NETosis” describing the phenomenon where neutrophils enhance innate immunity by releasing NETs, a structure that accompanies neutrophil death [27]. However, not all releases of NETs result in the subsequent death of neutrophils. In addition to undergoing various forms of death, neutrophils can also undergo reverse migration, a process in which they are guided back to the bone marrow , where they undergo apoptosis and are subsequently cleared by macrophages [28,29]. With the emergence of various advanced technologies, the heterogeneity of neutrophils has been gradually uncovered. In 2009, Fridlender demonstrated that transforming growth factor-beta (TGF-β) can alter the phenotype of neutrophils in tumors, classifying them into N1 and N2 types, which exhibit anti-tumor and pro-tumor characteristics, respectively [30]. In 2012, Pillay discovered a unique phenotype of human neutrophils possessing immunosuppressive capabilities during acute inflammation [31]. Subsequent research revealed that neutrophils in sepsis exhibit strongly inhibited chemotaxis. These studies laid the foundation for extensive research into immunosuppressive neutrophils [32]. Receptors and their downstream signaling pathways in neutrophil

The complex functions and heterogeneity of neutrophils are primarily mediated through a variety of cell surface receptors [33]. The G-protein-coupled receptor (GPCR) is a seven-transmembrane protein receptor that comprises a G-protein binding region within the cytoplasm and a ligand binding region outside the cell [34]. Neutrophils migrate to target sites through a process controlled by chemotaxis, relying on cell surface receptors to sense the gradient of chemoattractants. The majority of neutrophil chemoattractant receptors are GPCRs, which can activate the chemotactic migration of neutrophils [35]. The attachment of chemoattractants to GPCR triggers the pertussis toxin-sensitive heterotrimeric G proteins belonging to the Gαi/o family, initiating signaling to subsequent pathways [36]. Complement component 5a (C5a) mediates its effects through two GPCRs: C5a receptor 1 (C5aR1)-[Cluster of Differentiation 88 (CD88)] and C5a receptor 2 (C5aR2)-[C5L2 (C5a-like receptor 2), GPR77 (G protein-coupled receptor 77)] [37]. C5a induces a conformational change in C5aR1, thereby triggering its binding to G proteins [38]. The activated G proteins subsequently dissociate into α and βγ subunits [39], which go on to activate various signaling cascades involving phospholipase D (PLD), Protein kinase C (PKC), and Phosphatidylinositol-3 kinase (PI3K). Additionally, an increase in intracellular calcium levels activates both the mitogen-activated protein kinase/extracellular regulated protein kinases (MAPK/ERK) and nuclear factor kappa-B (NF-κB) signaling pathways [22]. It is important to note that NF-κB activation also initiates a critical negative feedback loop by upregulating the transcription of its inhibitor, IκB-α. This mechanism is essential for the self-limitation of inflammation and the transition to the resolution phase. These pathways lead to the release of granules, production of ROS, and activation of transcription factors, further promoting the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines, co-stimulatory molecules, and complement components. The Leukotriene B4 Type 1 Receptor (BLT1) is a GPCR specifically targeted by Leukotriene B4 (LTB4) [40]. The neutrophil chemotaxis mediated by BLT1 requires the activation of both PI3K and ERK pathways [41]. Furthermore, it has been demonstrated that LTB4 can upregulate the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines associated with atherosclerosis, primarily through the activation of c-Jun N-terminal kinase (JNK), ERK, and NF-κB pathways [42]. Neutrophils are generally believed to have a limited expression of chemokine receptors, predominantly comprising the Cysteine X Cysteine (CXC) group [43]. CXCR2 and CXCR4 serve as the primary receptors for CXC motif chemokine ligand 8 (CXCL8) and CXC motif chemokine ligand 12 (CXCL12), respectively [44]. The expression of C-X-C Motif Chemokine Receptor 2 (CXCR2) is upregulated and plays a crucial role in driving inflammatory diseases characterized by neutrophil infiltration and activation [45]. CXCR2 activates various G protein-induced downstream signaling cascades, exerting diverse biological effects [46]. Among these, Phospholipase C/Protein kinase C (PLC/PKC) influences cellular functions [47], MAPK/p38 and Ras/ERK promote cell proliferation and survival [48], Janus Kinase 2/Signal Transducer and Activator of Transcription 3 (JAK2/STAT3) is involved in cell migration and proliferation, and PI3K/Akt (Protein Kinase B) induces cell migration [49]. Human neutrophils express the formyl peptide receptors (FPR) 1 and FPR2, with FPR1 specifically and rapidly binding to fMLF, while FPR2 binds fMLF with low affinity [50]. FPR1 primarily regulates chemokine-induced neutrophil chemotaxis [51]. The FPR1 receptor is continuously expressed on the surface of resting neutrophils and is rapidly upregulated in response to various lipopolysaccharides (LPS), unmethylated CpG oligodeoxynucleotides, and TNF-α. The Toll-like receptor (TLR) family serves as the primary receptors for neutrophils to recognize damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) and pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) [52]. Activation of TLRs initiates a diverse array of signaling pathways, including both MyD88-dependent and MyD88-independent (TRIF-dependent) pathways, which involve NFκB and MAPK signaling cascades [53]. These pathways facilitate the release of various pro-inflammatory cytokines by neutrophils. During bacterial infections, TLR3 and TLR4 specifically utilize the TRIF-dependent pathway [54]. This pathway activates interferon regulatory factors (IRFs), particularly IRF3, driving the production of Type I interferons, which regulate neutrophil diversity and function within the inflammatory response [55]. Upon bacterial entry into the organism, TLR4 on the neutrophil surface detects LPS and mediates both early and late activation of the MAPK and NF-κB pathways through MyD88 (Myeloid Differentiation Primary response 88) and CD14 (Cluster of Differentiation 14). Conversely, the PI3K signaling pathway negatively regulates TLR signaling to prevent an excessive immune response [52]. Among the Fragment crystallizable gamma receptors (FcγRs) [56], FcγRIIa and FcγRIIIb are predominantly expressed on human neutrophils. The binding of immune complexes (IC) to FcγRIIa can trigger processes such as neutrophil degranulation, ROS production, inflammatory mediator release, and NET formation [57]. Compared to FcγRIIa, FcγRIIIb is more effective in inducing actin polymerization, β1 integrin activation, and NET formation [58]. Specifically, FcγRIIIb clustering activates spleen tyrosine kinase (Syk) and Transforming Growth Factor-β-Activated Kinase 1 (TAK1) [59], thereby activating the MAPK/MEK/ERK signaling pathway. MAPK signaling activates NADPH (Nicotinamide Adenine dinucleotide Phosphate, reduced form) oxidase, leading to ROS production and promoting NETs formation [58]. The activation signal of FcγRIIa is transmitted by the immunoreceptor tyrosine-based activation motif (ITAM), which is subsequently phosphorylated by Src-family kinase and serves as a docking site for Syk [60]. This initiates multiple signaling cascades, including MAPK, PI3K, and phospholipase C (PLC)-γ, ultimately inducing neutrophil activation, calcium mobilization, chemokine release, ROS production, and migration [61]. In neutrophils, tyrosine kinases respond to a variety of extracellular stimuli (including bacterial products, chemokines, and inflammatory mediators) to regulate complex signaling networks. The receptor tyrosine kinase (RTK) mesenchymal-epithelial transition factor (c-MET) has been identified as a crucial target for neutrophil function during inflammatory responses [62]. Inhibition of HGF (Hepatocyte Growth Factor)/MET signaling and c-MET has emerged as an effective strategy to mitigate uncontrolled neutrophil-mediated inflammation. Non-receptor tyrosine kinases (non-RTKs), such as Src and Syk, play their roles by coordinating the effects of FcγR and adhesion receptors on the neutrophil surface [63]. Src kinases (e.g., Lyn and Hck) are pivotal in integrin-mediated neutrophil activation, including phagocytic functions and pro-inflammatory responses. Additionally, Src kinases are involved in GPCR-mediated signaling, regulating neutrophil function by activating downstream pathways such as PI3K, MAPK, and NF-κB [64].

Understanding these receptor-mediated signaling pathways not only elucidates the molecular basis of neutrophil function but also provides a rational foundation for designing neutrophil-centric biomaterials. For instance, the high expression of CXCR2 on activated neutrophils at inflammatory sites can be exploited for targeted drug delivery. Biomaterials functionalized with CXCR2 ligands (e.g., CXCL8) can enhance site-specific accumulation, improve therapeutic efficacy, while minimize off-target effects. Similarly, FcγRs, which mediate phagocytosis and immune complex recognition, offer another strategic target. Nanoparticles coated with IgG or immune complexes can be selectively internalized by neutrophils via FcγRIIa/FcγRIIIb, facilitating intracellular drug release or modulation of neutrophil activation. Furthermore, formyl peptide receptors (FPR1/2) and complement receptors (e.g., CR3) have been leveraged to guide neutrophil-hitchhiking systems or neutrophil membrane-camouflaged nanocarriers to inflamed tissues. By mimicking natural ligands or opsonins, these biomaterials harness the innate tropism and signaling cascades of neutrophils, enabling precise control over inflammatory responses. Therefore, strategically targeting neutrophil surface receptors is essential. This approach forms the foundation for designing next-generation anti-inflammatory biomaterials.

The role of neutrophils in inflammatory diseases

1)Acute Inflammatory and Infectious Diseases:

1)Acute Inflammatory and Infectious Diseases:

Acute Inflammatory and Infectious Diseases:

Sepsis represents an uncontrolled immune reaction to infection, resulting in impairment of organ function [67]. Neutrophils serve as the first-line of defense against pathogen invasion. However, during sepsis, their lifespan is prolonged and their migratory capacity is impaired, confining them within blood vessels. Through the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines, ROS, and NETs, they induce severe vascular inflammation, ultimately leading to endothelial dysfunction [68]. Particularly, the release of NETs shifts the endothelium from an anti-inflammatory and anti-coagulant phenotype to a pro-inflammatory and procoagulant one [69]. Therefore, inhibiting NET release represents a promising strategy for the treatment of sepsis.

Necrotizing fasciitis is an aggressive and localized bacterial infection that affects the subcutaneous tissues and fascia [70]. Following bacterial invasion of the subcutaneous tissue, the pathogens proliferate and release exotoxins, which induce platelet-leukocyte aggregation and block capillaries, severely impeding the steady arrival of neutrophils at the site of infection [71]. Additionally, Streptococcus pyogenes secretes the proteases streptococcal C5a peptidase A (ScpA) and ScpC, which degrade neutrophil chemotactic factors, thereby disrupting the recruitment of neutrophils to the infected area.

Coronavirus Disease 2019 (COVID-19) is a highly infectious respiratory illness caused by the Severe Acute Respiratory Syndrome Coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2). In the context of COVID-19, the excessive activation of neutrophils is implicated in the onset of cytokine storms, the release of DAMPs, and the formation of thrombi. Elderly individuals, with compromised neutrophil function manifesting as impaired phagocytosis, reduced ROS production, diminished cytokine secretion, and altered chemotactic abilities, are rendered more susceptible to such infections [72]. The low-density neutrophil (LDN) subpopulation associated with COVID-19 exhibits immunosuppressive properties, potentially leading to impaired lymphocyte responses during acute COVID-19 [73]. These LDNs are prone to undergoing NETosis during the disease course, which is closely linked to vascular obstruction and COVID-19-related acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS). Emerging evidence suggests that targeting specific pro-inflammatory cytokines, some of which are abundantly produced by activated neutrophils (such as IL-1 and IL-6), holds investigational promise for mitigating severe COVID-19 immunopathology [74].2)Metabolic and Autoimmune Diseases:

2)Metabolic and Autoimmune Diseases:

Metabolic and Autoimmune Diseases:

Gout is a metabolic disorder caused by hyperuricemia, characterized by the deposition of monosodium urate (MSU) crystals in joints, leading to self-limiting inflammation. MSU crystals directly activate neutrophils to induce inflammation by downregulating the expression of two inhibitory receptors: Myeloid Inhibitory C-type Lectin (MICL) and Signal Inhibitory Receptor on Leukocytes-1 (SIRL-1) [75]. However, NETs can promote the resolution of gouty inflammation by accumulating MSU crystals, degrading cytokines and chemokines, and inhibiting the recruitment and activation of neutrophils [76].

Diabetes is a chronic condition marked by hyperglycemia, resulting from an absolute or relative deficiency in insulin secretion and impaired insulin utilization. The role of neutrophils in diabetes primarily manifests in diabetic complications, such as diabetic nephropathy, diabetic retinopathy, and diabetic foot ulcers [77]. For instance, the pathogenesis of diabetic foot ulcers involves vascular pathology, wherein hyperglycemia can trigger thrombo-inflammatory responses through the release of neutrophil and platelet-derived particles [78].

SLE is a chronic, diffuse connective tissue disease primarily caused by abnormal activation of the immune system, which leads to the attack on the body's own tissues. Patients with SLE exhibit a wide range of vascular dysfunctions, with atherosclerosis being the most emblematic. In SLE, neutrophils contribute to vascular endothelial cell damage and impair vascular repair through enhanced secretion of Type I Interferon (IFN-I), production of ROS, and release of inflammatory cytokines [79]. Moreover, the LDN subset within the neutrophil population plays a crucial role. The three-stage model of neutrophil-mediated vascular inflammation provides a mechanistic foundation for understanding SLE [80].

Rheumatoid Arthritis (RA) is a condition characterized by synovitis as its pathological basis, which can ultimately lead to joint deformities. Neutrophils are a significant source of the enzymes peptidylarginine deiminase type (PAD)-2 and PAD4, which are crucial for the citrullination of proteins [81]. Antibodies against citrullinated proteins (ACPA) can trigger preclinical RA. Furthermore, the inflammatory environment in the synovium can promote the formation of NETs and neutrophil degranulation, further contributing to joint damage. Myeloperoxidase (MPO) also generates ROS byproducts, thereby exacerbating oxidative stress within the joint [82].3)Chronic Inflammatory Diseases:

3)Chronic Inflammatory Diseases:

Chronic Inflammatory Diseases:

IBD is a chronic idiopathic inflammatory condition of the intestine that encompasses ulcerative colitis (UC) and Crohn's disease (CD). Neutrophils play a dual role in the pathophysiology of IBD [83]. The infiltration of neutrophils into the intestinal mucosa is a hallmark of active IBD [84]. On one hand, neutrophils are involved in clearing invading microbes; on the other hand, during chronic inflammation, the extensive infiltration of neutrophils can create a pro-inflammatory intestinal microenvironment through degranulation [85]. Targeting the neutrophil-microbiota crosstalk presents a promising therapeutic strategy for IBD. However, due to the complex functions of neutrophils in IBD, further comprehensive research is required to elucidate their precise role [86].

Design strategies for neutrophil-based anti-inflammatory biomaterials

Engineered neutrophil

Engineered neutrophil therapy represents an innovative treatment modality that harnesses genetic engineering and other advanced technologies to modify these cells, transforming them into a sophisticated platform for the diagnosis and treatment of inflammatory conditions [87,88]. This strategy typically encompasses the following steps: 1) Extraction and Purification of Neutrophils: Currently, techniques such as gradient centrifugation and magnetic bead sorting are predominantly employed to isolate and purify neutrophils. 2) Cell Modification: Neutrophils are engineered using gene engineering and other technologies to enhance their functionalities or endow them with new properties (such as enhanced targeting to specific inflammatory sites via surface receptor overexpression, enhanced resistance to apoptosis to prolong their functional activity, or the ability to produce and deliver therapeutic agents like anti-inflammatory cytokines, enzymes, or loaded nanoparticles). 3) Safety and Efficacy Testing: Prior to in vivo administration, comprehensive assessments, including compatibility and safety testing, are conducted to evaluate the therapy's efficacy and safety profile. 4) Infusion and Treatment: Engineered neutrophils are administered intravenously to animal models. Engineered neutrophils leverage their inherent targeting abilities and biological safety. Collectively, these attributes establish engineered neutrophils as a promising new-generation platform for diagnosing and treating inflammatory diseases. Neutrophils offer significant advantages over other immune cells due to their abundant and straightforward extraction methods. However, it is important to note that gradient centrifugation faces challenges related to purity, while magnetic bead sorting is susceptible to cell contamination [89,90]. Consequently, there is a pressing need to develop novel extraction and purification techniques tailored to neutrophils. Furthermore, the short lifespan of neutrophils (approximately 18 h) presents a challenge for maintaining viability and preventing apoptosis during the extraction and engineering process. To address this, the engineering of neutrophils must be efficient and rapid, followed by timely administration to patients to prevent apoptosis and homing to the bone marrow. Nevertheless, researchers can also exploit the homing characteristics of apoptotic neutrophils to develop a natural bone marrow-targeted delivery system [91].

Neutrophil-mediated drug delivery system

Living neutrophils and neutrophil-derived vesicles serve as excellent carriers for drugs or other therapeutic agents due to their inherent targeting capabilities, high drug-loading efficiency, and good biocompatibility. For drug delivery using live neutrophils, the drugs can be loaded through the neutrophils' uptake mechanisms and released upon neutrophil death or NET formation [92,93]. However, this method is limited by the relatively low uptake efficiency of neutrophils and the inevitable impact of the cargo on the neutrophils, which may even lead to premature cell death. In recent years, bioorthogonal membrane hitchhiking technologies have been proven to have fewer cytotoxic effects and higher drug-loading rates, but this technology may cause damage to the cell membrane [94].

Neutrophil-derived vesicles possess similar characteristics to cell membranes, including surface proteins and functions, and have advantages such as easy preservation, operation, and low toxicity compared to live cells [95]. However, the application of vesicles still faces some related challenges, including poor in vivo stability, complex composition, manufacturing challenges, and the standardization of extracellular Vehicles (EVs) formulations [96]. The future development and improvement of vesicle-based biomaterials mainly focus on the following aspects: simplifying and standardizing the synthesis process, artificially modifying vesicles, and developing new technologies to improve the production efficiency and performance of vesicles.

Biomimetic neutrophil

Traditional biomimetic neutrophil biomaterials employ strategies such as the use of cell membranes or other biocompatible materials to disguise the biomaterials, aiming to enhance their targeting and therapeutic efficacy [97]. These biomimetic cells typically possess the following features: 1) Cell membrane camouflage: To evade immune system recognition, extend circulation time in the blood, and enhance targeting; 2) Intelligent drug release: These biomaterials can control drug release in response to environmental stimuli (such as pH, temperature, enzyme activity, etc.), thus releasing drugs specifically in the inflammatory microenvironment; 3) Multifunctionality: The biomimetic cells can simultaneously carry various therapeutic molecules, such as antibiotics, gene therapy agents, immunomodulators, etc., to achieve combined therapy; 4) Biocompatibility and degradability: These particles are usually made of biocompatible materials that can be safely degraded in the body, reducing side effects. However, there are still challenges in converting this technology into clinical applications, including issues related to large-scale production, stability, safety, and efficacy verification. The future development and improvement of these biomaterials will focus on the following aspects: Utilizing cell membranes from different sources to endow biomaterials with various functions, optimizing the steps of membrane extraction and fusion to enhance the stability and functionality of the biomaterials in the body [98]. In addition, researchers have developed in situ self-assembled nanofiber networks based on biomimetic NETs to achieve efficient bacterial capture capabilities similar to NETs [99]. Future researchers may design advanced biomaterials with functions similar to those of neutrophils, such as phagocytosis, ROS production, inflammatory chemotaxis, and homing.

Neutrophil-targeted biomaterials

With the advancement of research into the biology, functions, and heterogeneity of neutrophils, researchers have recognized the potential therapeutic benefits of modulating the immune regulation and anti-infection capabilities of neutrophils for inflammatory diseases. Consequently, numerous preclinical studies have proposed various strategies, such as targeting neutrophil recruitment to inflammatory microenvironments, inhibiting neutrophil pro-inflammatory actions, promoting apoptosis of activated neutrophils, and enhancing the anti-infection capacity of neutrophils [100]. Although many studies targeting neutrophils have shown promising therapeutic effects, the specificity of current neutrophil-targeting biomaterials needs to be strengthened, and the design of the next generation of targeted materials should focus on the unique targets of neutrophils [101]. In addition, given the complex role of neutrophils in inflammatory diseases, it is important to be mindful of the potential side effects that interventions targeting neutrophils may bring.

Neutrophil-based monitoring platforms

Due to their role as the first line of defense against pathogens, the development of neutrophil-based imaging and monitoring systems is crucial for understanding the onset and progression of diseases. Thanks to the advancing research on neutrophils, researchers have developed biomaterials that can specifically label these cells, enabling effective monitoring of certain inflammatory diseases [102,103]. However, this field still faces several challenges: 1) Limited imaging depth: Imaging deep tissues remains difficult. 2) Signal interference: The complex environment within the body can interfere with imaging signals. 3) Potential cellular harm: Biomaterials may impair the function of neutrophils. Moreover, while neutrophils play a key role in the early stages of inflammatory diseases, the effectiveness of neutrophil-based imaging in other stages of inflammation is questionable. The advances in neutrophil-based anti-inflammatory biomaterials

In this section, we will highlight the key design principles and mechanistic insights of neutrophil-based biomaterials by discussing selected representative examples, rather than providing an exhaustive list of all reported systems. This approach aims to elucidate the overarching strategies that can be harnessed for future biomaterial design.

Engineered neutrophil

As outlined in Section 3.1, engineered neutrophils represent a promising platform for targeted anti-inflammatory therapy. A key principle in engineering neutrophils is leveraging their innate chemotaxis to achieve targeted drug delivery to inflammatory sites. This is exemplified by Zhou et al., who created a neutrophil-based “fire extinguisher” system (preclinical study) [104]. The core concept here is not merely the specific inhibitors used, but the strategy of employing the neutrophil as a guided vehicle for liposomal cargo. This approach capitalizes on the cell's natural ability to navigate to inflammation, ensuring the localized release of therapeutic agents (in this case, into macrophages) to suppress critical pro-inflammatory pathways like NF-κB and STING. This highlights the principle of “hijacking native cellular tropism” for precision therapy. In a similar vein, Che et al. demonstrated that neutrophils could effectively ferry nanocarriers to sites of inflammation, underscoring the versatility of using whole cells as guided biological vehicles (preclinical study) [105].

Neutrophil-mediated drug delivery system

Living neutrophil as drug delivery system

A key principle is harnessing the innate biological tropism of living neutrophils for targeted drug delivery. This approach capitalizes on the natural ability of neutrophils to migrate to inflammatory sites. A representative strategy involves engineering nanocarriers that are efficiently loaded into or onto circulating neutrophils in vivo. For instance, one study demonstrated the principle of “voluntary opsonization,” where liposomes were designed to rapidly adsorb complement proteins, leading to their selective uptake by activated neutrophils via complement receptors (preclinical study). This cell-state-dependent targeting ensures that the drug-laden neutrophils are recruited specifically to acute inflammatory lesions, effectively transforming the body's own immune cells into targeted delivery vehicles [106].

Neutrophil extracellular vesicle drug delivery system

A prominent principle is the functionalization of neutrophil-derived vesicles to enhance their targeting and therapeutic efficacy. In a preclinical study, Jiang et al. demonstrated this by engineering neutrophil exosomes with Prussian blue nanoparticles via click chemistry [107]. The significant point is the methodology of surface-modifying vesicles to create a hybrid biomaterial that mimics apoptotic neutrophils. This principle allows for precise homing to inflammatory sites and macrophages, promoting tissue repair processes such as angiogenesis, as shown in myocardial infarction models. It underscores the potential of “designer vesicles” with augmented functionalities.

Similarly, the principle of biomimetic packaging is exemplified by loading therapeutics into neutrophil membrane vesicles. This strategy has been applied to create inhalable nanomedicines for treating lung inflammation, where the neutrophil membrane enhances lung accumulation and targeted drug release, significantly improving therapeutic outcomes while minimizing systemic exposure. For instance, Meng and colleagues developed an inhalable NanoDEX nanomedicine (preclinical study), which comprises cholesterol-engineered neutrophil membrane vesicles loaded with DEX, aimed at enhancing COVID-19 treatment and managing complications (Fig. 1A) [108]. Compared to the free DEX, the novel DEX nano inhalation formulation, NanoDEX, showed a more than 14-fold increase in pulmonary enrichment (Fig. 1B). Beyond simple drug delivery, a sophisticated extension of this principle involves engineering vesicles to modulate cellular metabolism in target cells. One study showed that neutrophil membrane vesicles internalized by macrophages can replenish metabolites, reprogramming them from a pro-inflammatory to a pro-regenerative phenotype, which was further integrated into a “Trojan horse” scaffold for spatiotemporal control of nerve regeneration (in vitro study) [109]. Furthermore, engineered neutrophil nanovesicles have been designed to enhance the clearance of pathological debris by other immune cells, such as promoting myelin phagocytosis by microglia, demonstrating the potential to amplify endogenous repair mechanisms in diseases like multiple sclerosis (preclinical study) [110].

Biomimetic neutrophil

This section will illustrate key design principles of biomimetic neutrophil platforms through representative examples, focusing on the conceptual innovation rather than exhaustive experimental details.

Neutrophil membrane-modified biomaterial-synthesis system

Biomimetic strategies can transform neutrophils into in vivo synthetic factories or leverage their natural apoptotic processes. One pioneering principle is the use of neutrophil membranes to guide bioorthogonal catalysis directly to inflammatory sites. For instance, Qu's research team designed a system where neutrophil membrane-coated, chiral palladium catalysts performed asymmetric synthesis in vivo (preclinical study) [111]. This approach exemplifies how biomimetic targeting can be harnessed for the localized production of chiral drugs (e.g., ibuprofen) at disease sites, a principle that could revolutionize targeted prodrug activation.

Another principle involves mimicking natural clearance pathways. Instead of transplanting whole apoptotic cells, Bao et al. engineered neutrophil apoptotic bodies (eNABs) loaded with a therapeutic agent (preclinical study) [112]. These eNABs inherited the targeting capability of apoptotic neutrophils and promoted the resolution of inflammation by driving macrophages toward an anti-inflammatory phenotype, significantly improving outcomes in myocardial infarction. This demonstrates the principle of designing biomaterials that exploit endogenous immune signaling pathways for tissue repair.

Neutrophil membrane-modified treatment platforms

The core principle of using neutrophil membranes is to bestow nanotherapeutics with innate targeting capabilities towards inflamed tissues. This concept has been applied across diverse diseases. For rheumatoid arthritis, a platform co-delivering an anti-inflammatory agent (Leonurine) and catalase (CAT) demonstrated the principle of multi-component microenvironment remodeling (preclinical study) [113]. The neutrophil membrane provided joint targeting, while CAT simultaneously degraded ROS and alleviated hypoxia, creating a feedback loop for enhanced therapy.

The principle of biomimetic camouflage is powerfully illustrated by platforms that cloak nanotherapeutics in neutrophil membranes. Chen's research team developed a nanomedicine disguised with neutrophil membranes for treating ischemic stroke (preclinical study) [116]. The neutrophil membrane coating provided innate tropism to inflamed brain tissue, while a ROS-responsive core enabled stimulus-triggered release of the neuroprotective drug FTY720. Further expanding the scope, biomimetic membranes have been used to create “nanotoxoid” vaccines against antibiotic-resistant Acinetobacter baumannii (preclinical study) [115]. The neutrophil membrane coating safely presented bacterial antigens, effectively mobilizing both innate and adaptive immunity. This demonstrates the principle of using biomimetic vesicles as versatile antigen-presentation platforms for difficult-to-treat infections.

Other innovative platforms highlight additional design principles. One system combined neutrophil membrane targeting with an energy-generating core (derived from microalgae) to counteract metabolic failure in stroke, illustrating the principle of metabolic support alongside drug delivery (preclinical study) [114]. Another platform utilized a metal-organic framework (ZIF-8) core for efficient gene silencing, demonstrating the principle of combining biomimetic targeting with potent cytosolic delivery of nucleic acids (preclinical study) [117].

NETs biomimetic biomaterials

Moving beyond membrane coatings, a key design principle is the synthetic recapitulation of neutrophil effector functions, such as the formation of NETs. Jiang's team developed a system where small molecules self-assemble into nanofibers in vivo upon encountering the inflammatory microenvironment (preclinical study) [99]. This synthetic NET-mimetic network physically entraps bacteria, demonstrating a potent, mechanisminspired antimicrobial strategy that does not rely on traditional antibiotics. This highlights the principle of creating “synthetic innate immunity” through responsive biomaterials.

Neutrophil membrane based scavengers

A sophisticated application of biomimicry is the creation of cellular “decoys” or “scavengers”. Li's group designed neutrophil membrane-coated nanoparticles that function as neutralization decoys (NDs) for spinal cord injury (Fig. 3) (preclinical study) [119]. The principle here is receptor-mediated interception: the decoys display the same receptors as activated neutrophils, competitively binding and neutralizing inflammatory cytokines, thereby disrupting the feed-forward loop of inflammation. Neutrophil-targeted biomaterial

Pioneering biomaterials for targeting neutrophil precisely

This section illustrates key principles for achieving precise neutrophil targeting, using representative examples to highlight different strategic approaches. This includes exploiting neutrophil phenotypic plasticity for therapeutic programming. A fundamental strategy involves harnessing the functional plasticity of neutrophils. For instance, Liu et al. demonstrated that preconditioning neutrophils to an N2-like phenotype (e.g., via TGF-β) yields a conditioned medium that, when incorporated into hydrogels, promotes vascularization in ischemic diseases (preclinical study) [120]. This exemplifies how guiding neutrophil polarization can create biomaterials that actively regulate the inflammatory microenvironment for tissue repair.

Engineering Nanoparticle ‘Identity' for Selective Neutrophil Uptake. The intrinsic physicochemical properties of nanoparticles dictate their interactions with neutrophils. Brenner and colleagues established that protein nanoparticles with a defined, aggregated state are preferentially recognized and taken up by neutrophils in inflamed lungs, a process mediated by complement proteins C3 and C5 (preclinical study) [101]. This work provides a crucial design rule: engineering supramolecular protein arrangements can confer neutrophil tropism, a principle successfully applied to synthetic liposomes by functionalizing them with aggregation-prone proteins.

Targeting Activation-Specific Surface Markers. Achieving specificity for pathologically activated neutrophils is a central goal. Stavrou's group pioneered this by targeting neutrophil elastase (NE), a protein uniquely exposed on the surface of activated neutrophils (preclinical study) [121]. They developed a heteromultivalent nanoparticle functionalized with an NE-binding peptide and an anti-Ly6G antibody fragment. This approach exemplifies the principle of using ligands against activation-specific markers to distinguish disease-driving neutrophils from their resting counterparts, minimizing off-target effects.

Clear activated neutrophils

A pivotal therapeutic strategy is the specific clearance of overactive neutrophils to resolve inflammation. Innovative biomaterials are being designed to signal macrophages for neutrophil removal, mimicking or enhancing natural clearance pathways.

Hijacking Phagocytic Signaling for Neutrophil Clearance. One approach involves deceiving the body's own clearance mechanisms. Wang et al. designed a biomimetic nanoparticle (PC@PLGA) that co-opts the “eat-me” signal of calreticulin (preclinical study) [122]. Decorated with P-selectin for targeting and exogenous calreticulin, these particles bind to activated neutrophils and present an artificial “senescence” signal, tricking macrophages into initiating programmed cell removal (PrCR). Boosts neutrophil antibacterial activity

Enhancing the innate antibacterial functions of neutrophils represents a powerful strategy against resistant infections. Biomaterials can achieve this by simultaneously disarming bacterial defenses and re-energizing neutrophil attacks.

Multi-mechanistic Sensitization of Bacteria to Host Defenses. Zhu et al. designed a sophisticated nanoparticle (Hb-Naf@RBCM NP) that performs a coordinated “multi-hit” attack on S. aureus (preclinical study) [124]. It delivers an antibiotic (nafcillin) to inhibit bacterial antioxidant pigment (STX) synthesis, uses hemoglobin to deplete bacterial protective H2S, and employs a red blood cell membrane coating that disrupts bacterial membranes. Furthermore, by providing oxygen, it rejuvenates neutrophil oxidative burst in hypoxic infection sites. This multi-pronged approach effectively sensitizes bacteria to neutrophil killing across multiple preclinical infection models.

Restoring Neutrophil Metabolism and Function. Sepsis can paralyze neutrophil function. Gong's team addressed this by developing nanoparticles that directly deliver NAD⁺ into neutrophils, bypassing cellular uptake limitations to replenish their energy and redox cofactors (preclinical study) [125]. This metabolic support reinvigorated neutrophil antimicrobial activity without inducing excessive inflammation, highlighting the principle of targeting cell metabolism to restore immune competence.

Modifying the Inflammatory Microenvironment to Reactivate Immunity. In chronic biofilm infections, Wang's team created a microneedle patch (FGO@MN) that releases iron ions in response to the biofilm microenvironment (preclinical study) [126]. The iron both reactivates dormant neutrophils and induces lethal iron overload in bacteria, demonstrating how biomaterials that manipulate the local biochemical milieu can tip the balance in favor of the host immune system.

Monitoring platforms for neutrophils

As discussed in Section 3.5, neutrophil-based imaging platforms face challenges such as limited depth and signal interference. Representative studies have tackled these issues through innovative bioengineering strategies. The core principle demonstrated here is the use of native neutrophil trafficking to achieve site-specific accumulation of imaging agents. Critically, the nanoengineering process preserved the cells' intrinsic functions, allowing them to migrate to pneumonic sites. Beyond targeting and uptake, the performance of the imaging agent itself is paramount. Addressing the common limitations of short half-life and low brightness in chemiluminescence, Pu's group introduced a novel chemiluminescent scaffold (BAPD) with intramolecular hydrogen bonding (preclinical study) [129]. The fundamental advance here lies in the molecular engineering of the probe core to significantly improve its photophysical properties, resulting in a 21-fold increase in half-life and an 8.2-fold boost in brightness. This platform, further tailored with neutrophil elastase (NE)-responsive elements, facilitated high-fidelity, longitudinal imaging of neutrophil activity in models of peritonitis and psoriasis.

Despite the promising preclinical advances illustrated by these examples, it is important to note that the majority of neutrophil-based biomaterials remain in the experimental stage. Very few have progressed to clinical trials, and those that have are often evaluated in oncology rather than inflammatory diseases. Key challenges include scalability, regulatory classification, and reproducibility of cell-based products. Future efforts must focus on bridging this translational gap through standardized manufacturing, rigorous safety profiling, and early-phase clinical trials.

Conclusions and perspectives

Cell-based biomaterials have emerged as a natural source for advanced biomaterials of the future, thanks to their inherent targeting capabilities, biocompatibility, and degradability. This review aims to summarize the complex role of neutrophils in inflammation and provide insights for the continuous development of neutrophil-based inflammation-regulating biomaterials. Sections 3, 4 detail a diverse array of neutrophil-centric biomaterial strategies. These include engineered neutrophils, neutrophil-mediated drug delivery systems, biomimetic neutrophils, neutrophil-targeted biomaterials, and monitoring platforms. The advances in neutrophil-based anti-inflammatory biomaterials.

In terms of therapeutic efficacy, biomimetic strategies, particularly those utilizing neutrophil membrane coatings (e.g., NRN for stroke, Leo@CAT@NM-Lipo for RA), often demonstrate superior targeted accumulation and potent anti-inflammatory effects in preclinical models by leveraging innate tropism. Neutrophil-mediated living cell delivery systems (e.g., Lip@MH for cytokine storm) excel in leveraging active chemotaxis for deep tissue targeting but face challenges regarding carrier cell viability and functional consistency. Engineered neutrophil-derived exosomes (e.g., uPB-Exo) show promise due to their multifunctionality and reduced immunogenicity, yet their precise mechanisms and contributions from specific neutrophil subpopulations require further elucidation.

Regarding safety profiles, biomimetic nanoparticles and extracellular vesicles generally exhibit favorable biocompatibility and reduced risks of acute immune reactions compared to the administration of living engineered cells. However, long-term fate, potential off-target effects, and the immunogenicity of membrane proteins or synthetic components (e.g., Pd catalysts, ZIF-8) warrant thorough investigation. Strategies employing live neutrophils, while highly specific, carry inherent risks such as unintended activation or premature cell death, potentially leading to drug leakage or exacerbated inflammation.

The scalability and translational potential of these platforms vary significantly. Biomimetic nanovesicles and synthetic NET-mimetics benefit from relatively standardized production processes, enhancing their manufacturability. In contrast, the large-scale generation, purification, and genetic modification of primary neutrophils for therapeutic use remain formidable hurdles due to their short lifespan and sensitivity to ex vivo manipulation. Neutrophil-targeted nanoparticles (e.g., PC@PLGA) offer a promising “off-the-shelf” alternative but require optimization of targeting ligand stability and specificity for human applications.

From a clinical translation standpoint, the journey of neutrophil-based biomaterials from bench to bedside is still in its early stages. While the preclinical landscape is rich and rapidly expanding, their progression into clinical trials remains limited. To date, most advancements are confined to animal models, and few neutrophil-inspired nanotherapies have entered Phase I/II clinical trials, often for oncology rather than inflammatory diseases. Key regulatory hurdles include defining the biological classification of these complex products—whether as cell therapies, drug-device combinations, or novel biologics—which directly impacts the approval pathway. Scaling up production presents another layer of challenge, particularly for systems relying on primary human neutrophils. Ensuring batch-to-batch consistency, maintaining sterility, and achieving good manufacturing practice compliance for cell-derived components (e.g., neutrophil membranes, extracellular vesicles) are non-trivial and costly endeavors. Future efforts must prioritize establishing robust, scalable manufacturing protocols, conducting detailed toxicological and immunogenicity studies in relevant disease models, and initiating well-designed early-phase clinical trials to evaluate the safety and preliminary efficacy of these promising platforms in humans.

However, despite the development of numerous promising neutrophil-centric anti-inflammatory materials, the field still faces many challenges. Looking ahead, novel biomaterial synthesis methods, in-depth exploration of the complex interactions of neutrophil-based advanced materials in inflammation, non-genetic engineering modifications of neutrophils, screening of new targets, and accelerated clinical translation are all opportunities for the future (Fig. 8). Current techniques for neutrophil isolation and purification are time-consuming and prone to contamination, leading to significant changes in neutrophils during the isolation process and affecting experimental results [130]. The complex role of neutrophil-based biomaterials has not been fully explored, mainly due to the multifunctionality and heterogeneity of neutrophils. Spatial transcriptomics, high content screening and assay for transposase-accessible chromatin with high-throughput sequencing play a crucial role in comprehensively understanding neutrophils [[131], [132], [133]]. Genetic engineering techniques are difficult to apply to neutrophil modification due to their short lifespan. Therefore, it is necessary to fully utilize other rapid and efficient non-genetic engineering methods to process and modify neutrophils, such as Neutrophil-targeted nanoparticles, exosomes and drugs [134]. For the screening of new targets, the integration of multi-omics analysis and artificial intelligence can identify new effective therapeutic targets [135,136]. For clinical translation, a significant bottleneck in biomaterials conversion is the difference between commonly used rodent models and human tissues and organs. While the preclinical landscape for neutrophil-based biomaterials is rich and rapidly expanding, their progression into clinical trials remains limited. Future work must prioritize several key steps. These include establishing robust GMP-compliant manufacturing protocols, conducting detailed toxicological and immunogenicity studies, and initiating well-designed early-phase clinical trials. These efforts will help evaluate the safety and preliminary efficacy of these promising platforms in humans. While large mammals can relatively accurately represent human tissues, conducting experiments on these animals is expensive and subject to strict ethical considerations. Advances in organoid engineering may solve this problem [137,138]. Additionally, accelerating the development and translation of biomaterials requires the joint efforts of scientists, entrepreneurs, governments, patients, and healthcare professionals [139,140].
