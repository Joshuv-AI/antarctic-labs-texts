Contained transverse colonic perforation caused by an ingested fish bone presenting as chronic abdominal pain: a case report
Authors: Gnanakanesh Rajeeth, Kulasegaran Srijeyaharish, Kanthasamy Umasankar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction and importance:Foreign body ingestion is common; however, gastrointestinal perforation is rare and often poses a diagnostic challenge, particularly when symptoms are chronic and nonspecific. Colonic perforation due to ingested foreign bodies is uncommon, with the transverse colon being an especially rare site due to its mobility and wide lumen.Case presentation:We report the case of a 19-year-old male who presented with central abdominal pain and intermittent diarrhea for 1 month, followed by acute worsening of abdominal pain, fever, nausea, and vomiting over the proceeding 3 days. Clinical examination revealed features of peritonitis. Laboratory investigations demonstrated leukocytosis and markedly elevated inflammatory markers. Ultrasonography and contrast-enhanced computed tomography revealed a localized intra-abdominal abscess containing a linear hyperdense structure suggestive of a foreign body. Emergency exploratory laparotomy identified a large concealed abscess cavity containing a 3-cm fish bone, associated with a sealed perforation of the transverse colon. Segmental transverse colectomy with en bloc abscess excision and side-to-side stapled anastomosis was performed. The patient recovered uneventfully and was discharged on postoperative day 5.Clinical discussion:Foreign body–induced colonic perforation may present insidiously due to localized containment of contamination, resulting in delayed diagnosis. The absence of pneumoperitoneum and lack of a clear ingestion history further contribute to diagnostic difficulty. Cross-sectional imaging plays a crucial role, and timely surgical intervention is essential to prevent morbidity.Conclusion:Ingested foreign bodies should be considered in young patients presenting with chronic abdominal pain and localized intra-abdominal abscesses, even in the absence of a clear history of ingestion, as delayed recognition may result in concealed gastrointestinal perforation.

Introduction

Foreign body ingestion is frequently encountered in clinical practice, particularly among children and young adults. The vast majority of ingested foreign bodies pass through the gastrointestinal tract without complication[1,2]. Colonic perforation secondary to foreign body ingestion is rare, with the sigmoid colon and rectum being the most frequently affected sites[3,4]. Perforation of the transverse colon is particularly unusual due to its mobility and wide lumen, which reduce the likelihood of impaction[4,5]. Clinical presentation may be atypical, especially when the perforation is contained, leading to chronic or misleading symptoms and delayed diagnosis[6–8]. We report a rare case of concealed transverse colonic perforation caused by an ingested fish bone, presenting as chronic abdominal pain and diarrhea. This case has been reported in accordance with the SCARE criteria[9].

Case presentation

A previously healthy 19-year-old man presented with central abdominal pain and intermittent loose stools for the past month. Over the preceding three days, his abdominal pain worsened and was associated with fever, nausea, and intermittent vomiting. He had no history of foreign body ingestion, trauma, prior abdominal surgery, or medication use. Urine output and bowel habits were normal.

On examination, the patient was febrile, with a pulse rate of 90 beats per minute, a respiratory rate of 23 breaths per minute, and a blood pressure of 110/70 mmHg. Abdominal examination revealed generalized tenderness with guarding and rigidity, most prominent in the central abdomen.

Laboratory investigations showed leukocytosis (white blood cell count 16 000/mm3), hemoglobin of 12 g/dL, and elevated inflammatory markers (CRP: 130 mg/L, ESR: 22 mm/h). Renal and liver function tests were within normal limits.

Ultrasonography demonstrated a localized hypoechoic collection in the paracolic region, adjacent to the small bowel, with a linear echogenic structure suggestive of a foreign body. Contrast-enhanced computed tomography revealed a heterogeneously enhancing fluid collection with an irregular wall in the epigastric region, just inferior to the gastric antrum.

It contained a linear hyperdense structure consistent with a foreign body (Fig. 1). No pneumoperitoneum was identified. Figure 1.Contrast-enhanced CT scan demonstrating a localized intra-abdominal abscess with a linear hyperdense structure suggestive of a foreign body (arrow).

In view of clinical peritonitis and radiological findings, the patient underwent an emergency exploratory laparotomy.

Intra-operatively, a large concealed abscess cavity was identified, adherent to the inferior aspect of the greater curvature of the stomach and the transverse colon (Fig. 2). On opening the cavity, purulent material was drained, and a 3-cm fish bone was retrieved (Fig. 3). A sealed perforation of the transverse colon, communicating with the abscess cavity, was identified. En bloc resection of the affected segment of transverse colon along with the abscess cavity was performed, followed by restoration of bowel continuity using a side-to-side stapled colo-colic anastomosis. Thorough peritoneal lavage was carried out and drains were placed.

The postoperative course was uneventful. The patient resumed oral intake gradually and was discharged on postoperative day 5. At follow-up, he remained asymptomatic.

Discussion

Foreign body ingestion is generally benign; however, sharp objects, such as fish bones, carry a significantly higher risk of gastrointestinal perforation[1,2]. Fish bones are the most commonly implicated foreign bodies due to their sharp edges and radiolucent nature, which may delay diagnosis[6,8].

Colonic perforation caused by ingested foreign bodies is rare, accounting for only a small proportion of reported cases[3,4]. Although the transverse colon is relatively mobile, transient angulation at the hepatic and splenic flexures, combined with localized luminal narrowing and peristaltic dynamics, may predispose the colon to impaction of sharp foreign bodies such as fish bones[4,5]. In the present case, perforation was contained, resulting in localized abscess formation rather than generalized peritonitis, thereby explaining the chronic symptoms and the absence of pneumoperitoneum[6–8].

Diagnosis is often delayed due to nonspecific clinical features and the absence of a clear history of foreign body ingestion, a phenomenon well documented in the literature[8,10]. Computed tomography plays a pivotal role in diagnosis, allowing accurate identification of foreign bodies, abscess formation, and associated inflammatory changes[6,7].

Surgical intervention remains the definitive treatment for complicated cases involving perforation and abscess formation[4,11]. In cases of contained perforation with localized contamination, segmental colonic resection with primary anastomosis is considered a safe and effective surgical option in hemodynamically stable patients, as demonstrated in this case and supported by previous reports[4].

Conclusion

Contained colonic perforation due to ingested foreign bodies may present with chronic, nonspecific abdominal symptoms and can be easily overlooked. A high index of suspicion, careful radiological assessment, and timely surgical intervention are essential to prevent morbidity[6–8]. Foreign body ingestion should be considered even in the absence of a clear ingestion history, particularly in young patients presenting with unexplained intra-abdominal abscesses[10,11].
