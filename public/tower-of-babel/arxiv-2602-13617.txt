1.
Introduction

A permutation
(\pi_{1},\dots,\pi_{n})
of
\{1,\dots,n\}\eqqcolon[n]

is said to be
3
AP-free
if no indices
i<j<k
satisfying

\pi_{i}+\pi_{k}=2\pi_{j}
exist. We write
\theta(n)
for the number of

3
AP-free permutations of
[n]
.
The study of
3
AP-free permutations of
[n]

dates back to at least 1973, when Entringer and Jackson
[
EJ73
]

asked if all permutations of
[n]
contain a
3
AP
in the
American Mathematical Monthly
problems section.
In the solutions, Simmons
[
Sim75
]

gave values of
\theta(n)
for
1\leq n\leq 20
(some incorrect);
the initial values are
(\theta(1),\theta(2),\dots)=(1,2,4,10,20,48,104,282,496,1066,2460,\dots)
.

Shortly after, Davis, Entringer, Graham, and Simmons
[
DEGS77
]

investigated
3
AP-free permutations of
[n]
,
as well as
k
AP-free permutations of
\mathbf{N}
and
\mathbf{Z}
.
They proved the relations

\theta(2n)\geq 2\theta(n)^{2}\quad\text{and}\quad\theta(2n+1)\geq\theta(n+1)\theta(n)\quad\text{for $n\geq 1$,}

as well as the bounds

2^{n-1}\leq\theta(n)\leq\Bigl\lfloor{n+1\over 2}\Bigr\rfloor!\,\Bigl\lceil{n+1\over 2}\Bigr\rceil!\quad\text{for $n\geq 1$;}

they also showed that

\theta(2^{k})>{1\over 2}c^{2^{k}}
for
k\geq 4
where
c=(2\theta(16))^{1/16}\approx 2.248
,
giving an improved exponential lower bound for
\theta(n)
along powers of two.

After some time, these bounds were improved by
Sharma
[
Sha09
]
, who proved the relation

\theta(n)\leq 21\theta\Bigl(\Bigl\lfloor{n\over 2}\Bigr\rfloor\Bigr)\,\theta\Bigl(\Bigl\lceil{n\over 2}\Bigr\rceil\Bigr)\quad\text{for $n\geq 3$,}

as well as the exponential lower and upper bounds

{n2^{n}\over 10}\leq\theta(n)\leq{2.7^{n}\over 21}\quad\text{for $n\geq 11$.}

LeSaulnier and Vijay
[
LV11
]
then
improved the lower bound to
\theta(n)\geq{1\over 2}c^{n}
for
n\geq 8
,
where
c=(2\theta(10))^{1/10}\approx 2.152
.

Nonetheless, much remains mysterious about
\theta(n)
;
for example, it is not known whether

\theta(n)\leq\theta(n+1)
for all
n
, despite this appearing rather obvious
for small values of
n
.
In 2004, Landman and Robertson
[
LR04
]
asked in their book

Ramsey Theory on the Integers
if
\lim_{n\to\infty}\theta(n)^{1/n}
exists.
Later in 2017, Correll and Ho
[
CH17
]
presented a dynamic programming algorithm
that enabled them to compute values of
\theta(n)
up to
n=90
. Since then,
values of
\theta(n)
up to

(see OEIS
A003407

\displaystyle\bigl(2\theta(160)\bigr)^{1/160}\approx 2.20499

\displaystyle\leq\limsup_{n\to\infty}\theta(n)^{1/n}<2.32721\approx\bigl(21\theta(128)\bigr)^{1/128}.

2.
The proof

The idea is to consider subsequences of
(\theta(n)^{1/n})_{n\geq 1}

of the form

\bigl(b_{t}(m)\bigr)_{t\geq 0}\coloneqq\bigl(\theta(m2^{t})^{1/(m2^{t})}\bigr)_{t\geq 0}.

(1)

We will show that these subsequences converge for each
m\geq 1
,
then we will pick two of these subsequences and use known values of
\theta(n)

to prove that they converge to different limits. First, we need two lemmas:

Lemma 1

(
[
DEGS77
]
)
.

We have
2\theta(k)^{2}\leq\theta(2k)
for
k\geq 1
.

Proof.

Given two
3
AP-free permutations
(a_{1},\dots,a_{k})
and

(b_{1},\dots,b_{k})
of
[k]
,
both

(2a_{1},\dots,2a_{k},2b_{1}-1,\dots,2b_{k}-1)\;\;\text{and}\;\;(2b_{1}-1,\dots,2b_{k}-1,2a_{1},\dots,2a_{k})

are
3
AP-free permutations of
(1,\dots,2k)
.
∎

Lemma 2

(
[
Sha09
]
, Theorem 2.8)
.

For
n\geq 3
, we have
\theta(n)\leq 21\theta(\lceil n/2\rceil)\theta(\lfloor n/2\rfloor)
.
In particular, for
k\geq 1
, we have
\theta(2k)\leq 21\theta(k)^{2}
.

These lemmas imply that

2\theta(k)^{2}\leq\theta(2k)\leq 21\theta(k)^{2}\quad\text{for $k\geq 1$,}

(2)

so taking
(2k)
-th roots gives

2^{1/(2k)}\theta(k)^{1/k}\leq\theta(2k)^{1/(2k)}\leq 21^{1/(2k)}\theta(k)^{1/k}\quad\text{for $k\geq 1$.}

(3)

Setting
k=m2^{t}
with
m\geq 1
and
t\geq 0
thus gives

2^{1/(m2^{t+1})}b_{t}(m)\leq b_{t+1}(m)\leq 21^{1/(m2^{t+1})}b_{t}(m).

(4)

In particular, since
2^{1/(m2^{t+1})}>1
, the sequence
(b_{t}(m))_{t\geq 0}

is strictly increasing.
Repeatedly applying the bound on the right in (
4
) yields

b_{t}(m)\leq b_{0}(m)\cdot 21^{\sum_{j=1}^{t}{1\over m2^{j}}}\leq b_{0}(m)\cdot 21^{1/m},

(5)

so
(b_{t}(m))_{t\geq 0}
is bounded above. Thus
(b_{t}(m))_{t\geq 0}

converges to some limit
L_{m}
.

Now let us bound
L_{m}
. Applying (
4
) with
t+r
in place of
t
, we get

2^{1/(m2^{t+r+1})}b_{t+r}(m)\leq b_{t+r+1}(m)\leq 21^{1/(m2^{t+r+1})}b_{t+r}(m).

(6)

Iterating the left inequality
k
times gives

b_{t+k}(m)\geq b_{t}(m)\prod_{r=1}^{k}2^{1/(m2^{t+r})}\geq 2^{(1-2^{-k})/(m2^{t})}b_{t}(m).

(7)

Similarly, iterating the right inequality gives

b_{t+k}\leq 21^{(1-2^{-k})/(m2^{t})}b_{t}(m).

Sending
k\to\infty
, we find that

\bigl(2\theta(m2^{t})\bigr)^{1/(m2^{t})}\leq L_{m}\leq\bigl(21\theta(m2^{t})\bigr)^{1/(m2^{t})}

(8)

for all
m\geq 1
and
t\geq 0
.

To complete the proof, we show that
L_{1}\neq L_{75}
.
Indeed, since
64=1\cdot 2^{6}
and
75=75\cdot 2^{0}
, (
8
) implies that

L_{1}\geq(2\theta(64))^{1/64}
and
L_{75}\leq(21\theta(75))^{1/75}
.
Since
\theta(64)=39911512393313043466768
and
\theta(75)=30235147387260979648843264

(see
[
CH17
]
or OEIS
A003407
for
\theta(n)
values),
we have
L_{1}\geq(2\theta(64))^{1/64}\approx 2.27953231299

and
L_{75}\leq(21\theta(75))^{1/75}\approx 2.27703523933
.
Thus
L_{1}\neq L_{75}
, and we are done.

3.
Discussion

As a consequence of this result,
3
AP-free permutations provide an
interesting and rare example of a combinatorial object that appears
(empirically) to be increasing (that is, writing
a_{n}
for the number of objects of
size
n
, data suggests that
a_{n}\leq a_{n+1}
for all
n
),
with reasonably close bounds
c^{n}\leq a_{n}\leq C^{n}
for some constants
1<c<C
,
where the growth rate
\lim_{n\to\infty}a_{n}^{1/n}
does not exist.

Why does this happen?
A common way to prove that such limits exist, though not the only way, is as follows:
Many combinatorial objects are such that you can combine smaller objects
to form bigger ones; this often leads to bounds such as
a_{m}a_{n}\leq a_{m+n}
.
We can then apply Fekete’s lemma to show convergence. However, while we can
combine two
3
AP-free permutations of order
n
to get a
3
AP-free permutation
of order
2n
(thus implying the convergence of
\theta(2^{n})^{1/2^{n}}
),
there does not appear to be any simple way to combine
3
AP-free
permutations of different sizes to obtain a new
3
AP-free permutation.

Using the ideas of this paper, we have the following separation,
which is the largest gap we can prove from the data we have
(namely
\theta(n)
values up to
n=200
):
We have
L_{1}\geq(2\theta(128))^{1/128}\approx 2.28484
and

L_{81}\leq(21\theta(162))^{1/162}\approx 2.23760
, so

\displaystyle 2.20<\liminf_{n\to\infty}\theta(n)^{1/n}<2.23760<2.28484<\limsup_{n\to\infty}\theta(n)^{1/n}<2.33.
