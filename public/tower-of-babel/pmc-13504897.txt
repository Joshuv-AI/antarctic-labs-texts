Burden, unmet healthcare and associated disabilities among people with prevalent stroke in tea-tribe communities of Cachar district, Assam state, Northeast India: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Vivek K Nambiar, Vivek Verma, Subham Nunia, Mazumder M Jubair, Karthika Rani, Shashank Jaiswal, Nishant Agrawal, Divyasree Chandran, Amit Pashi, Pradip Pashi, Manoj Ghosh, Masuma Khanam, Rahul Das, Sanjoy Kumar Singha, Mita Das, Aishwarya Chakraborty, Nabarun Chakraborty, Shubhadeep Roychoudhury, Shatabhisha Chakraborty Roychoudhury, Tarun B Sukai, Dilip Nath, Denny John
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractBackgroundTea-tribe communities in Assam face a substantial and under-recognised burden of stroke, shaped by socioeconomic marginalisation, limited access to formal healthcare and distinct cultural practices.ObjectiveWe estimated the prevalence of physician-diagnosed stroke, described associated functional and psychological disabilities, characterised gaps in diagnosis and care, and developed a logistic regression-based classification score for prevalent stroke among tea-tribe adults in Cachar district, Assam, Northeast India.MethodsWe conducted a community-based cross-sectional door-to-door survey (July 2024–August 2025) across five tea estates among permanent tea-tribe residents aged ≥18 years. Trained community health workers collected data on sociodemographics, vascular risk factors, healthcare utilisation and poststroke functional and psychological outcomes using a structured questionnaire, the Modified Rankin Scale and a symptom inventory. Stroke was identified through community symptom screening and confirmed by teleconsultation with a senior neurologist, supported by neuroimaging where available. A multivariable logistic regression model with stepwise selection identified factors associated with prevalent stroke and regression coefficients were converted into an additive point-based classification score. Internal validation used 1000 bootstrap samples and a 70:30 train–test split. The model performance was assessed using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve and Brier score.ResultsAmong 3818 participants, 164 had a history of stroke (prevalence 4.3%), with a higher prevalence in older adults, men, unemployed or semiskilled or skilled workers and households below the poverty line. Hypertension was the strongest risk factor and its combination with daily tea containing added salt was associated with markedly higher odds of stroke. Alcohol consumption, smoking and previous heart disease were also associated with stroke. Stroke survivors had high functional and psychological burdens and fewer than half reached a health facility within 4.5 hours or underwent CT/MRI. The final model showed good discrimination (area under the curve 0.84) and calibration (Brier score 0.037). The derived classification score (0–19 points) identified a highly associated group (scores >13) with a stroke prevalence of 14.5%.ConclusionsThe tea-tribe community in Assam faces a high stroke burden, substantial disability and major gaps in timely diagnosis and care. A simple, internally validated classification score based on demographic, socioeconomic and vascular factors may help target community screening and prevention but requires external validation and cautious interpretation given the cross-sectional design and self-reported exposures.Keywords: Stroke, Epidemiology, Cross-Sectional Studies, PUBLIC HEALTH

Introduction

Stroke is a major cause of global morbidity, mortality and disability, mainly impacting developing countries, which will bear most of its burden in the future.1 In India, stroke risk varies between urban and rural areas.2–4 A 2024 analysis of 5 population-based stroke registries showed a pooled incidence of 138.1 per 100 000 and an age-standardised rate of 103.4 overall, with higher rates in males (125.7) than females (80.8).5

There are no estimates of stroke prevalence among tribal populations in India who make up almost 10% of the total population of India.6 The only reported study among tribals about risk factors and stroke outcomes in the tribal community was conducted in 2010, with significant differences observed between tribals and non-tribals.6

Assam, a northeastern Indian state, produces nearly 49% of India’s tea.7 The multiethnic ‘Tea Tribes’ in Assam, descended from tea garden workers, face vulnerabilities due to poor socioeconomic conditions, illiteracy, cultural factors, overcrowding and unhygienic living conditions, making them prone to various diseases and malnutrition.8–10 Studies show high rates of hypertension and tobacco use among tribal groups, worsened by low health literacy and limited access to healthcare.11–13 Cultural practices such as excessive salt intake and unregulated alcohol and tobacco use also increase vascular and cardiovascular risks.11–19

The State Government has not carried out any census of tea-tribe population in Assam State; however, the total population of tea tribes is estimated to be around 20% of Assam’s population (approximately 65 lakh based on Census 2011).20 These tea tribes are mainly located in the districts of Upper Assam (Charaideo, Dibrugarh, Golaghat, Jorhat, Sivasagar and Tinsukia districts), the Northern Brahmaputra belt (Kokrajhar, Nagaon, Sonitpur and Udalguri districts) and the Barak Valley (Cachar, Hailakandi and Karimganj districts), where there is a high concentration of tea gardens.

In Cachar, Assam, access to healthcare for tea tribes remains challenging.21 Many tea garden workers rely on local providers or traditional healers because formal services are limited in remote areas. The district’s inadequate healthcare, limited number of hospitals and staff shortages restrict access, leading to diagnostic delays, fewer treatments and a higher disease burden. Poor living conditions also worsen health. Despite government programmes such as Ayushman Bharat, poor implementation, low awareness and logistical barriers hinder the programme’s impact, especially among tea tribes with limited awareness. NGOs have sought to improve socioeconomic status, healthcare access and skills, but resource constraints and remoteness have impeded progress. A previous study examined morbidity in four districts but not Cachar’s tea tribes.22 This study gathered data on stroke prevalence, causes and disabilities in Cachar’s tea community and used a stepwise multivariable logistic regression to identify stroke-related factors for a classification model for prevalent stroke to guide targeted interventions, strengthen health systems and promote equitable stroke care for tribal populations nationwide.

Methods

Study design and setting

A cross-sectional study was conducted from July 2024 to August 2025 across five tea estates—Roskandi, Borjalenga, Silcoorie and Pallorbond—located within the Tapang, Chota-Jaleng and Lakhipur blocks in the Cachar district (online supplemental figure 1). The study comprised an intensive door-to-door household survey of households with individuals 18 years and above and those who provided consent, to collect data, ensuring priority-satisfactory coverage as elements of the community-based cross-sectional study design. This study was conducted as part of household-level screening; hence, clustering of risk factors within households at the time of screening was not followed.

Participant selection and recruitment

The study population consisted of adults who were permanent inhabitants (having available housing in the tea-estate premises) of the chosen tea estates and were aged 18 years or older. A household listing was prepared from estate records, and all occupied residential units (2012 households) within the estates were approached in a systematic door-to-door manner. Trained field workers visited each household, introduced the study and screened all usual residents for eligibility. Individuals were eligible if they met the age and residence criteria, were present during the visit, and were able to understand and respond to the questionnaire; those who declined participation or were in a serious condition and unable to communicate were excluded. For eligible residents not at home at the first visit, up to two repeat visits were attempted at different times of day to minimise non-response. Written (or witnessed thumb-print) informed consent was obtained before the interview, and every consenting eligible household member was administered the structured questionnaire by the field worker in the local language. Among eligible adults approached under these criteria, all provided consent and completed the survey, reflecting the confined nature of tea-estate housing and the longstanding relationships between field teams and estate management.

Sample size estimation

To attain a power of 90% and a significance of 5% for detecting a difference of 0.8% using a two-sided exact test, the sample size was calculated to be 3054, based on a reported prevalence of 1.6% for stroke in Assam.23 Considering a 20% non-response rate, the required minimum sample size for the study was calculated to be 3818.

Predictor variables

A structured questionnaire was used to collect sociodemographic data, including age, gender and economic status. Hospital admission status, time from stroke onset to admission, neuroimaging (CT/MRI) results if performed, current medication status and physiotherapy use were all recorded to evaluate treatment access and usage. The supervisor verified these details by reviewing medical records. Risk factors (tea with salt, tobacco, alcohol) were assessed at the time of stroke onset, not at the survey.

Exposure variables

Clinical risk factors included self-reported parameters such as diabetes, the habit of drinking tea with white salt, chewing tobacco, smoking, alcohol intake and any existing heart condition. Salted tea consumption is a common socioeconomic and cultural practice among tea-estate workers, potentially reflecting dietary patterns.24 Systolic and diastolic blood pressure were assessed using a portable sphygmomanometer.

Stroke identification

The treating doctor and the field workers at the tele-stroke clinic at Assam University underwent training along with pilot data collection (of 10 samples each) before final data collection. A refresher training was conducted by the central tele-stroke team (hub centre) from the Department of Neurology, Amrita Institute of Medical Sciences and Research Centre, Kochi. All patients with stroke were referred to a tele-stroke clinic at Assam University and were confirmed via teleconsultation by a senior neurologist (VAN) based at the hub centre.

Stroke cases were identified as acute neurological deficits of vascular origin lasting more than 24 hours or resulting in death, confirmed by clinical diagnosis and neuroimaging, in accordance with the WHO definition.25 In community surveys, all persons with limb weakness, facial weakness, unilateral sensory symptoms and unilateral visual impairment at less than three lasting for more than 24 hours were considered as stroke.26 The clinical diagnosis was confirmed by a stroke specialist at the hub centre after seeing all details, including the type of presentation, imaging (where available) and follow-up.

Transient ischaemic attacks and stroke mimics were excluded based on the neurologist’s assessment. We were not able to reliably differentiate ischaemic from haemorrhagic stroke in all participants and therefore analysed stroke as a composite outcome; time since index stroke was not collected in a standardised form and is not reported. Online supplemental figure 2 provides the stroke ascertainment algorithm.

Primary and secondary outcomes

The primary outcomes include the prevalence of stroke through self-report and verification of records.

Poststroke functional assessment was conducted using the Modified Rankin Scale (mRS) as part of the data collection questionnaire (online supplemental file 1). Functional disability was assessed using ‘no disability’, ‘single’, ‘two’ or ‘three or more’ with questions evaluating challenges with walking alone, bathing, dressing, getting out of bed, working and being bedridden.

Additionally, psychological impact (pain, memory issues, mood problems, numbness, depression and tightness) and psychological impact score (single, two, three or more) were assessed as part of the questionnaire (online supplemental file 1). These were not based on validated psychiatric instruments and should be interpreted as self-reported symptoms rather than clinical diagnoses.

The risk factors at the time of stroke, such as drinking tea with salt, chewing tobacco, smoking and alcohol intake, as well as any lifestyle changes subsequently considered, were recorded as part of the data collection (online supplemental file 1).

The secondary outcomes include poststroke hospitalisation utilisation, including financial sources of treatment.

Adjusted ORs with 95% CIs and corresponding scores for the association between each predictor and stroke prevalence were obtained, along with performance metrics of scores for each of these predictors were also assessed for a classification model for prevalent stroke.

Data collection and quality

Trained field staff collected data, and field supervisors, conversant with the local language, reviewed completed forms every day to ensure the quality of the data and 5% of families were reinterviewed at random for validation. If data were incomplete on a particular day, then field staff were instructed to collect complete data the next day. Data entry was performed in REDCap.

Statistical analysis

For continuous variables, means and SDs were displayed, whereas frequencies and proportions were provided for categorical variables. χ2 tests for categorical variables and t-tests for continuous variables were used in bivariate analyses to investigate relationships between sociodemographic characteristics and clinical outcomes. Multivariable logistic regression model was constructed to identify independent predictors of stroke prevalence. Poststroke functional impairment was also examined by evaluating treatment access gaps, including non-adherence to medication, delayed admission, lack of neuroimaging and lack of physiotherapy. Utilising the distribution of mRS categories and reported activity limits, functional disability was examined. The poststroke burden, in terms of management, functionality and psychological status, was compared with the unexposed group across all levels.

All ORs were adjusted for the set of included predictors. A significance threshold of p<0.05 was applied. Before including them in the logistic regression model, we assessed the variance inflation factor (VIF) for multicollinearity among predictors. A stepwise forward elimination technique was used to remove predictors with the highest p values, leaving only significant predictors (p<0.05) and clinically relevant ones.

Model development

A multivariable logistic regression model was developed using predictor variables to identify stroke-related factors, including occupation status, alcohol consumption, tea with salt, hypertension status, poverty status, gender and age group.

Model performance

The model’s discrimination was assessed using area under the curve (AUC) and calibration plots comparing predicted odds of prevalent stroke. Receiver operating characteristic curve (ROC curves) were developed for each stepwise logistic regression level. Overall accuracy was measured using the Brier score, which calculates the mean squared error between predictions and observed outcomes. Decision curve analysis evaluated the clinical applicability and net benefit of utilising the model for stroke prevalence.

Model validation

The internal validation of the predictive accuracy of the model’s scores was performed using 1000 bootstrap samples to estimate CIs and assess model potential. The internal validation using a train–test split was performed by splitting the entire dataset into a training dataset (using 70% of the sample (n=2670)).

Development of the classification tool for prevalent stroke

A point-based classification score was developed using the adjusted ORs (AORs) obtained from the final multivariable logistic regression model. Following the approach described by Sullivan et al, the AORs were converted to regression coefficients using the natural logarithm (β=ln(AOR)).27 To create an additive and clinically applicable scoring system, the smallest statistically significant regression coefficient (β=0.419 for alcohol consumption) was selected as the scaling constant. Each regression coefficient was divided by this constant and rounded to the nearest integer to derive the corresponding association points. Variables with AORs that were not statistically significant (smoking and heart disease) were excluded from the final score.

The assigned scores were as follows: age 45–59 years=4 points, age ≥60 years = 5 points, male sex=2 points, unemployed=2 points, semiskilled/skilled occupation=2 points, below poverty line (BPL) family=3 points, taking salt with tea without hypertension=4 points, hypertension without taking salt with tea=6 points, combined taking salt with tea and hypertension=6 points and alcohol consumption=1 point. The total score ranged from 0 to 19, with higher scores indicating a greater association of prevalent stroke.

The discriminative ability of the score was examined by cross-tabulating total association scores with observed stroke prevalence. Based on the observed increase in stroke frequency across score categories, a threshold of ≥13 points was selected to classify individuals at high association for stroke prevalence. Classification categories were defined as low (0–9), moderate (10–12), high (13–16) and very high (≥17).

Trained staff visited households, confirmed willingness, explained the study’s purpose, assured confidentiality, answered questions, obtained consent and conducted interviews at participants’ convenience. All analyses were performed using SAS University Edition (SAS Campus Drive).

Reporting guidelines

The methods and results of this study were reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines for cross-sectional studies and the Transparent Reporting of a multivariable prediction model for Individual Prognosis or Diagnosis (TRIPOD) checklist for risk prediction models.28 29

Patient and public involvement

Community health workers involved in data collection are members of the local community with an understanding of the local language and customs. During the training on the use of the tool, the community health workers were encouraged to provide inputs to ensure the questionnaire reflects community needs and requirements in terms of understanding the questions mentioned. Additionally, this study is funded by the national research bodies, namely the Indian Council of Medical Research and the Indian Council of Social Science Research.

Three members of the author team (AP, PP and SN) belong to the tea-tribe community. Online supplemental file 3 provides details of the study team and reflexivity.

Results

The majority of the study population were from Roskandi (n=2001), followed by Borjgalenga (n=878), Silcoorie (n=509) and Pallorbond (n=423). Of the 3818 participants, 164 individuals (4.3%) were identified as having stroke (table 1). About half of the participants (79 patients, 48%) reportedly underwent CT/MRI. Stroke prevalence increased with age. Higher prevalence of stroke was observed among males and families BPL. Hypertension was the highest risk factor, with the interaction of salt-tea consumption and hypertension showing a synergistic effect (table 1). Only 50% of individuals reached a health facility for treatment within 4.5 hours. Online supplemental figure 3 provides the STROBE flow chart for cross-sectional studies.

Online supplemental figure 4 compares the ROC curves of the discriminatory performance between the training and testing datasets. Both curves display similar shapes and run closely together, indicating consistent model behaviour across training and unseen data. The training ROC curve (solid blue line) and the test ROC curve (dashed red line) rise steeply toward the upper-left corner, indicating high sensitivity and low false-positive rates. The close alignment between the two curves suggests that the model has good generalisation ability and is not overfit to the training data. Overall, the model performs comparably well on both datasets, demonstrating robust classification accuracy and potential. The final multivariable logistic regression model for prevalent stroke demonstrated excellent discrimination, with an AUC of 0.8473 (95% CI approximately 0.82 to 0.87) (figure 2).

ROC curve for the selected logistic model. ROC, receiver operating characteristic.

Online supplemental figure 5 provides the ROC curves comparing multivariable logistic regression models for prevalent stroke with and without tobacco chewing as a predictor. Both models showed excellent discrimination (AUC 0.8451 for the model including tobacco and 0.8473 for the model without tobacco) with nearly superimposed ROC curves, indicating that tobacco chewing contributed minimal additional classification value beyond other covariates. The ROC curves rose steeply towards the upper-left corner, reflecting high sensitivity at acceptable false-positive rates and suggesting that the model can distinguish stroke from non-stroke cases with good accuracy in this population.

In an extended model that additionally included tobacco-related variables, the overall discrimination was very similar to the main model (online supplemental file 2). The area under the ROC curve (AUC) was 0.845 (SE 0.014; 95% CI 0.818 to 0.872) for the model including tobacco, compared with 0.847 (SE 0.014; 95% CI 0.820 to 0.875) for the model without tobacco (online supplemental file 2). The contrast in AUC between the two models was −0.002 (95% CI −0.007 to 0.002; p=0.33), indicating no statistically or clinically meaningful improvement in discrimination with the addition of tobacco variables.

Older age groups showed significantly increased odds of the outcome, with ORs from 4.7 to 8.1 and all CIs excluding 1 (table 4). Male gender also increased odds (OR=2.34, 95% CI 1.59 to 3.43), consistent across models. Those who were unemployed or semiskilled/skilled workers reported increased odds of 2.39 and 2.25, respectively. Those BPL had about twice the odds of the outcome as those above it. High odds were observed for hypertension without tea with salt and for hypertension alone, compared with tea with salt, but with wide CIs indicating lower precision. Tea with salt was not significantly associated with higher odds compared with neither hypertension nor tea with salt. Alcohol use was linked to increased odds (OR=1.52, 95% CI 1.08 to 2.40).

The multivariable model yielded a Brier score of 0.037 (0 optimal), indicating strong performance, with optimal sensitivity and specificity for use in community settings for stroke prevalence among adults in the target population.

Decision curve analysis suggests that using the classification model offered slightly higher net benefit than ‘treat all’ or ‘treat none’ strategies for threshold probabilities between approximately 5% and 20%; outside this range, the net benefit was similar (online supplemental figure 6).

Calibration assessment using deciles of the classification model showed close agreement between observed and predicted stroke probabilities across most of the association spectrum, with only modest deviation at the highest predicted probabilities. The solid line shows observed stroke probability across deciles of association, and the dashed line represents perfect calibration (45° line) (online supplemental figure 7). These findings are consistent with the low Brier score (0.037), supporting good overall model accuracy and reliability.

A total of 3818 participants were included in the classification score analysis, of whom 164 (4.3%) had a history of stroke. The point-based classification score ranged from 0 to 19, with progressively increasing stroke prevalence across higher score categories. Participants with scores between 0 and 9 had a very low observed stroke association of 0.71% (16/2257) (table 5). The association increased to 5.10% (48/942) among those with scores of 10–12, indicating a moderate-associated group. A substantial increase in stroke prevalence was observed among participants with scores of 13–16, where 14.48% (87/601) had experienced a stroke. Participants with scores of 17 or higher demonstrated the highest observed stroke prevalence of 72.22% (13/18). Individuals with a classification score of ≥13 exhibited approximately a threefold higher observed stroke prevalence than those with scores of 10–12 and more than a 20-fold higher prevalence than those with scores below 10 (table 5), supporting the proposed cut-off for identifying individuals at high association of stroke in community settings.

Discussion

The prevalence of stroke among Assam tea tribes was high (4.3%) and markedly higher than national estimates from community-based rural studies, which range from 1.5% to 2.0%, 3 13 30 and higher than the reported prevalence of 1.6% in Assam overall.23 This suggests that tea-tribe communities bear a greater burden of stroke than both rural and urban cohorts, despite having a relatively younger working-age population, underscoring a dual burden of epidemiological and social vulnerability in this historically marginalised group.

Identifying age, male sex and hypertension as predictive correlates is in line with the established national and international pattern of increasing prevalence of stroke with advancing age of male participants.31 32 A fivefold odds of prevalent stroke was observed for those already age-adjusted between 45 and 59 years, and a near 10-fold odds of prevalent stroke for those aged ≥60 years; the studies further corroborated age, male sex and hypertension as correlates of stroke reported in previous incidence and prevalence studies in Northeast India and the National Stroke Registry Programme.5 11 Our study shows that tea-tribe individuals tend to have increased case fatality and later access to care, with high categorically congruent association emerging from recent work completed in Gadchiroli and the tribal state of Jharkhand, indicating more than 80% of stroke deaths occurred before or during hospitalisation due in large part to limited access to care.11 13

One of the most notable associations from the study was the interaction between ‘tea with salt’ consumption and hypertension, showing a 12-fold to 14-fold higher odds of prevalent stroke. However, there were wide CIs and a modest number of stroke events in some exposure data, emphasising imprecision. There is no global evidence that moderate non-sweetened tea consumption generates neuroprotection for brain health, but ‘tea with salt’ is a cultural variation consumed by tea tribes in the region, perhaps increasing sodium intake along with endothelial dysfunction. Other culturally derived dietary experiences have been reported among tea garden workers, with even higher prevalence (over 25%) in both cohorts and among young adults from other districts of the state.33 34 These results indicate the importance of appropriate public health messaging that would ultimately discourage sodium consumption in tea rather than discouraging tea specifically.35

Belonging to a household BPL almost doubled the odds of stroke. Economic deprivation has been linked to delays in hospitalisation, poor adherence to treatment and rehabilitation, as studies at a national level have indicated a positive relationship between low Human Development Index and stroke burden.32 Alcohol intake and smoking as added behavioural risk factors further increase the risk of stroke, especially within the contexts of poverty and poor access to healthcare. Tobacco chewing was negatively associated with stroke, showing a protective association. This association is likely to be explained by residual confounding, reverse causality, or selection bias (eg, cessation from stroke), and should not be interpreted as evidence of benefit, and requires further investigation with more detailed exposure measurement.

Alarmingly high proportions of people experienced disability from stroke, with over 80% of survivors reporting some disability and over 60% of survivors experiencing three or more disabilities. Proportions are higher than in urban Indian studies, where it may be more likely that access to rehabilitation is improved.3 The current study also highlighted a lack of diagnostic access, with only 48.2% of participants undergoing CT/MRI scans. Other rural studies have noted similar patterns of diagnostic access, with most rural/tribal stroke sufferers not accessing neuroimaging or physiotherapy follow-up on time.11 13 Low awareness of Ayushman Bharat or any other state-based health insurance schemes, coupled with contextual barriers to accessing care, was a common theme identified in a previous report from Assam and across Northeast India.19 22

The multivariable logistic regression model had an AUC of approximately 0.84–0.85, demonstrating excellent discrimination for prevalent stroke in this community-based sample. ROC curves comparing models with and without tobacco chewing overlapped almost completely, and the AUCs were nearly identical (0.8451 vs 0.8473), indicating that tobacco chewing, while epidemiologically interesting, contributed little incremental classification value beyond the other covariates. These findings reinforce our cautious interpretation of the apparently protective association with tobacco chewing and support the view that it should not be prioritised as a key variable in operational screening tools until confirmed in longitudinal cohorts.35 Although both smoking and tobacco chewing were highly prevalent in this population, incorporating tobacco variables into the multivariable classification model did not materially improve its ability to discriminate between adults with and without prevalent stroke. The AUC remained essentially unchanged when tobacco measures were added, and the ROC contrast test was non-significant, suggesting that in this setting the incremental predictive value of tobacco exposure, over and above age, sex, hypertension, salt-tea use, poverty and alcohol, is limited. This does not imply that tobacco is unimportant for vascular risk, but rather that, given the very high background prevalence of tobacco use and the dominant effect of hypertension and salt-tea consumption, tobacco may contribute less to association stratification at the individual level than expected in other populations.36

The low Brier score (0.037) and visually good calibration across association deciles suggest that the model’s absolute probability estimates are reasonably well calibrated, which is crucial for communication and decision-curve evaluation.37 Decision curve analysis indicated a modest net clinical benefit of using the model to guide association-based strategies compared with ‘treat all’ or ‘treat none’ approaches for threshold probabilities in the 5%–20% range, supporting its potential use for targeted community screening and counselling in tea-tribe populations. Likewise, the logistic regression model we constructed to develop classification score for stroke prevalence demonstrated reasonably good predictive performance and potential. An initial assessment of collinearity diagnostics showed that all predictors had VIFs below the cut-off of 10, indicating no significant multicollinearity among the independent variables. Stepwise model selection identified those predicted to be most important for stroke: age, gender, interaction of exposure (tea with salt) and disease (hypertension), and alcohol and tobacco chewing were most significantly associated with the outcome.

Additionally, the predictors examined accounted for a reasonable amount of variation in attrition outcomes. The model’s potential suggests it is less likely to be overfit and is expected to perform similarly across data from other settings. In general, the data indicated that logistic regression is an effective and interpretable method for identifying determinants of stroke and other associated factors. While the relationships were generally consistent across the full and training samples, this suggests they are stable.

By integrating socioeconomic deprivation and culturally embedded practices into a multivariable classification score, this study extends existing stroke classification tools—which were largely developed in urban, non-tribal cohorts—to a marginalised tea-tribe population in Northeast India, and may support more equitable classification-based screening and prevention. The stroke classification score showed a steep gradient in observed prevalence, from less than 1% in the lowest category to more than 70% in the highest, indicating meaningful separation of low-associated and high-associated individuals with stroke prevalence and aligning with recommendations that classification models provide clear stratification for clinical or public health decision-making.

The study’s strengths include a door-to-door survey across five tea estates, ensuring all participants were part of the local tea-tribe population in Cachar. Community-based surveying minimised selection bias and provided a more accurate stroke prevalence estimate. Data were gathered by trained field staff, with daily oversight to ensure quality. To ensure agreement, a second interviewer visited 5% of households to assess data accuracy. REDCap was used for data entry to protect data integrity and minimise transcription and data handling mistakes. This study is the first study to comprehensively assess stroke prevalence, risk factors and disability outcomes in the tea-tribe community in Assam, a population with significant health disparities and limited access to care. The study had the added feature of assessing tea consumption with added salt, a culturally specific practice nearly exclusive to tea-tribe individuals, and found an interactive effect of salt combined with hypertension, further strengthening the strong association of stroke. This culturally relevant association contributes to the public health literature. Multivariable logistic regression and classification model evidenced excellent discrimination (AUC=0.83–0.84). Rigorous internal validation was conducted by bootstrapping the logistic regression model with 1000 samples, which validated model stability and robustness, reduced overfitting, and enabled translation to clinical and screening contexts. The assessment addressed more than just stroke prevalence but also included functional disability (mRS) as well as indicators of psychological outcomes (ie, depression, memory loss and mood changes) to address a comprehensive assessment of post-stroke burden which impacts physical and mental well-being after having experienced a stroke. The study’s methodological strengths included a large sample size, a high participation rate and internal validation and bootstrapped logistic regression models. The study adhered to the STROBE guidelines for cross-sectional studies and, on model development, the TRIPOD criteria, in an effort to promote scientific transparency, adherence to requisite reporting practices, and the reproducibility of the study.28 29

Our modelling strategy adhered to key elements of contemporary guidance for prognostic model development, including the TRIPOD recommendations, by prespecifying clinically plausible predictors, using multivariable logistic regression and undertaking internal validation with bootstrap resampling and a train–test split.29 The resulting classification model demonstrated good discrimination (AUC 0.84) and a low Brier score, broadly comparable to established cardiovascular risk prediction models such as Framingham-type and QRISK-type scores, which typically report c-statistics in the 0.70–0.80 range with low Brier scores.38–40 Calibration was acceptable across most classification strata supporting its potential utility as an exploratory tool for classification in this setting.

Causation is limited by the cross-sectional design as well as by some variables being self-reported (eg, tea with salt tobacco and alcohol) at the time of stroke onset not at the survey thus subject to recall bias; hence, the results should be viewed with some caution.41 We acknowledge the potential for residual confounding about interaction between ‘tea with salt‘ consumption and hypertension particularly from overall dietary sodium intake occupational factors socioeconomic status hypertension severity and duration and differential access to healthcare which were not fully captured in our dataset. Transient ischaemic attacks and stroke mimics were excluded based on the neurologist’s assessment. We were not able to reliably differentiate ischaemic from haemorrhagic stroke in all participants and therefore analysed stroke as a composite outcome. We were unable to systematically capture time since the index stroke and neuroimaging was unavailable or incomplete for many participants so we could not reliably distinguish ischaemic from haemorrhagic events and subtype-specific patterns could not be explored. This was due to challenges that many of the study participants and/or caregivers mentioned that due to the previous flood in the area many lost their CT/MRI films and hence this could not be presented during the neurologist tele-consultation. As imaging or detailed clinical records were unavailable, we acknowledge that misclassification is possible and could bias associations towards or away from the null. Additionally, we did not use validated instruments such as the PHQ-9 or HADS for psychological outcomes and this may lead to misclassification and potentially conservative estimates of psychological burden. The use of stepwise selection in logistic regression has important limitations including the risk of overfitting and model instability. Although the ROC and calibration plots suggest good discrimination and reasonable agreement between associated and observed classification, the combination of stepwise variable selection and a relatively small number of stroke events means that the model should still be viewed as exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than as a definitive clinical classification-scoring tool. As we had used stepwise selection in a dataset with relatively few stroke events, there remains a risk of overfitting and instability of regression coefficients; therefore, model estimates should be interpreted cautiously and require independent confirmation. Finally, tobacco exposure was assessed using simple self-report categories, which may not capture duration and intensity; this may partly explain why adding tobacco variables did not improve model discrimination despite the biological plausibility of tobacco as a vascular risk factor. The absence of clear improvement in model performance with tobacco variables, together with the unexpected inverse association observed for tobacco chewing, should be interpreted cautiously. Residual confounding, misclassification of exposure and survival bias are all plausible in this marginalised setting and longitudinal studies will be needed to better characterise tobacco-related association for stroke prevalence among tea-tribe communities.

Although internal validation suggested good performance, the use of stepwise selection in a dataset with a modest number of stroke events raises concerns about overfitting and coefficient instability, as noted in methodological evaluations of stepwise logistic regression. In keeping with current guidance that classification models should undergo rigorous external validation before use in practice, our score should be considered exploratory and hypothesis-generating and its performance should be externally validated in other tea-tribe and tribal populations in India and similar low-resource settings.

As the classification score relies on variables that can be captured through structured interviews and basic blood pressure measurement, it is potentially implementable by community health workers as part of opportunistic screening and our decision-curve analysis suggests modest net clinical benefit for classification thresholds relevant to referral decisions in low-resource stroke services. The study underscores the need for community-based hypertension screening and dietary education to reduce salt intake which informs our understanding of health disparities related to stroke and stroke-related diseases among the tea-tribe population. Incorporating cultural adaptations of stroke awareness programmes and mobile neuroimaging services may further help bridge the diagnostic and treatment gaps within this socially, culturally and politically historical population.

Conclusions

This work presents one of the most thorough assessments of stroke burden among Assam’s tea tribes, unveiling unique sociocultural and clinical factors that contribute to limited access to healthcare. Furthermore, the relationship between salt-tea consumption and the development of hypertension is a new, culturally specific, potentially modifiable exposure (tea with added salt) that warrants confirmation in longitudinal studies. Despite the unexpected inverse association observed with tobacco chewing, the totality of evidence still supports tobacco control as a critical vascular prevention strategy. The classification model for stroke prevalence could be used to screen for change and ultimately to reduce inequalities in stroke outcomes for marginalised populations in India.
