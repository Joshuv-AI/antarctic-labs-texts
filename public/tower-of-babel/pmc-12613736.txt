Contrasting organizational responses to femicide in Mexico’s public health crisis
Authors: Veronica Valencia Gonzalez
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFemicide—the gender-motivated killing of women—remains an urgent public health and human rights crisis in Latin America. In Mexico, legal reforms have established formal mechanisms for prevention and response, yet implementation remains fragmented, particularly in regions marked by structural violence and institutional distrust. This study examines how femicide is conceptualized and addressed by both formal institutions and grassroots organizations in two distinct contexts: Mexico City and rural Michoacán.MethodsDrawing on 64 in-depth interviews and participant observations conducted between 2022 and 2024, this qualitative study employs a comparative case study design to explore how divergent organizational frameworks, political conditions, and cultural logics shape femicide response. Thematic analysis was used to identify patterns in discourse, action, and collaboration across formal and community-based actors.ResultsFindings reveal two fundamentally different logics of femicide response. Formal institutions emphasize legal harmonization, training protocols, and policy compliance metrics—reflecting a technocratic model of prevention aligned with bureaucratic governance. Grassroots actors, by contrast, center relational care, symbolic resistance, and immediate community mobilization. These differences are not merely operational, but epistemic: institutional actors often devalue lived experience and emotional labor, while grassroots actors articulate survivor-defined safety, cultural legitimacy, and trust as central to prevention. In rural and high-impunity regions, community-led responses often function as the only reliable form of protection and accountability.ConclusionsFemicide prevention frameworks in Mexico must move beyond symbolic gestures of inclusion and begin to reckon with the structural exclusion of grassroots knowledge and labor. Meaningful response requires a shift in power and priorities—one that values community knowledge, centers survivor-defined metrics of safety and trust, and explores models of shared governance in contexts where institutional systems are distrusted or absent. In such settings, grassroots responses are not peripheral—they are essential.Keywords: Femicide, Gender-based violence, Grassroots response, Mexico, Structural violence, Epistemic injustice, Violence prevention, Rural communities, Institutional trust

Introduction

Over the past three decades, Mexico has witnessed an alarming rise in femicide—the gender-motivated killing of women—which has become a defining crisis of gender-based violence in the region. The term gained national and international prominence in the 1990 s following the murders of hundreds of women in Ciudad Juárez, where widespread impunity and state inaction exposed deep structural misogyny and galvanized feminist movements across Latin America [1–3]. Scholars such as Marcela Lagarde y de los Ríos [4], reframed these killings as feminicidio, a term that highlights not only the violence itself but the state’s complicity through inaction, negligence, and impunity.

In the years since, Mexico has enacted a series of legal and institutional reforms aimed at addressing violence against women, including the 2007 General Law on Women’s Access to a Life Free of Violence [5]. These efforts have emphasized formal mechanisms such as legal codification, inter-institutional coordination, and standardized training [6–8]. Yet implementation has been highly uneven—especially in rural and marginalized regions—raising critical questions about the limits of formal institutional approaches to addressing femicide. Recent studies have similarly highlighted how formal prevention frameworks often fail to integrate community-driven efforts, especially in areas where state legitimacy is fragile or contested [9, 10]. These limitations are especially troubling given Mexico’s commitments under the 2030 Agenda for Sustainable Development, including SDG 5.2 (eliminating all forms of violence against women and girls) and SDG 16 (promoting peaceful and inclusive societies with access to justice). Yet in many under-resourced and high-impunity settings, such goals remain aspirational amid persistent institutional fragility and gendered impunity [11–13].

Michoacán, a state marked by high levels of organized violence, military intervention, and institutional precarity, exemplifies the challenges of formal response in contexts of structural vulnerability. In 2022, the state reported among the highest femicide rates in the country, alongside widespread displacement, impunity, and mistrust in public institutions [14, 15]. In such environments, femicide is not merely an interpersonal crime but a manifestation of intersecting harms: state abandonment, patriarchal control, and systemic disposability of certain lives. These dynamics reflect broader public health critiques of fragmented systems of GBV response that fail to meaningfully include affected populations in the design and governance of interventions [14, 16].

Amid this crisis, grassroots actors1—often survivors, families of victims, and local women’s collectives—have stepped into the void, responding with memorials, protests, and mutual aid. These responses diverge sharply from formal institutional strategies. For the purposes of this study, formal organizational actors2 refer to government-affiliated institutions and formally recognized non-governmental organizations (NGOs) that operate within Mexico’s national or regional prevention systems. These state actors often prioritize measurable outputs and procedural compliance, grassroots actors emphasize emotional visibility, symbolic justice, and community solidarity. For many, placing a cross, organizing a march, or naming the dead is not ancillary—it is the response.

This article examines how femicide is understood and addressed by both formal institutions and grassroots organizations in two contrasting contexts: Mexico City and Michoacán. Rather than evaluating effectiveness, the analysis centers on the divergent ways femicide is conceptualized, rendered visible, and acted upon. Drawing on 64 in-depth interviews and field observations conducted between 2022 and 2024, the article traces how institutional mandates, political geographies, and cultural logics shape both action and inaction in the face of femicide. In doing so, it contributes to ongoing debates in feminist, sociolegal, and public health scholarship on how to address structural violence in contexts marked by fragility, inequality, and resistance [17].

Theoretical frameworks

This study is grounded in feminist and decolonial critiques of institutional power, particularly those that interrogate how state and non-state actors construct legitimacy, urgency, and authority in responding to gender-based violence [17; 18, 8]. While prior scholarship has documented the uneven implementation of gender violence policy in Latin America [3, 13], fewer studies have explored the epistemic tensions that arise when informal, community-based responses emerge as alternatives in contexts of institutional failure. Drawing on Fricker’s [18] concept of epistemic injustice, this article examines how survivor and community knowledge is often excluded or devalued within formal prevention frameworks, even as grassroots actors perform critical public health functions in high-risk environments [19].

In addition, this work builds on Janice Gallagher’s [20] notion of bootstrap justice, which theorizes how marginalized communities in Latin America often engage in autonomous forms of claim-making when legal remedies are inaccessible or ineffective. These frameworks help illuminate how grassroots actors not only respond to state abandonment, but also generate alternative systems of accountability, memorialization, and protection. This study contributes to these literatures by analyzing the interactional dynamics between formal and informal actors in two distinct Mexican contexts, rural Michoacán and Mexico City, highlighting how competing logics of legitimacy and urgency shape the architecture of femicide response. By centering survivor-defined action and locally embedded knowledge systems, this article reframes femicide prevention as a contested site of structural, emotional, and political negotiation.

Methodology

Study origin and aim

This study draws from qualitative data originally collected for a broader dissertation project on intimate partner violence (IPV) and gender-based violence in rural Michoacán, Mexico. Although the initial research focused on IPV, femicide emerged as a recurring and urgent theme in participant narratives, institutional discourse, and public mobilization. Survivors, community members, and organizational actors invoked femicide not only as an extreme form of violence but also as a symbol of institutional abandonment and collective grief. This article offers a focused analysis of that subset of the data to examine how formal and grassroots organizations understand, respond to, and frame femicide in two distinct geographic and institutional contexts.

Research design and epistemological foundations

The study is grounded in a constructivist epistemology and draws from feminist, decolonial, and community-engaged methodological traditions. Constructivism informed the analytic premise that meanings of femicide, justice, and state legitimacy are not fixed, but socially produced and contextually situated. A feminist methodological orientation foregrounded power asymmetries in both the phenomena under study and the research process itself—attending to how structural inequalities shape survivors’ pathways, as well as researcher-participant dynamics [20–22]. The decolonial dimension of the study entailed not only a critique of Western-centric policy frameworks but also an effort to recognize and validate alternative knowledge systems—particularly the experiential and community-based epistemologies that circulate in contexts of state neglect [17, 23, 24].

Community-engaged principles shaped every phase of the research, from the selection of field sites to the development of interview protocols and the interpretation of findings. Rather than treating participants as passive informants, the study adopted dialogic and iterative strategies that prioritized participant meaning-making. This included allowing survivors and grassroots actors to define what justice meant to them, even when their definitions conflicted with institutional or international standards. These epistemological commitments supported a trauma-informed, context-sensitive approach that aligns with best practices in public health research on gender-based violence [25, 26] especially in under-resourced and high-risk settings where formal data are limited and institutional trust is low.

Comparative case study design

A comparative case study design was employed to examine femicide-related responses across two socio-politically distinct contexts: Michoacán and Mexico City. Michoacán was selected for its rural character, limited institutional infrastructure, and elevated levels of violence and displacement. Mexico City was chosen for its policy visibility, institutional density, and role as a national reference point for gender-based violence interventions. This design allowed for the analysis of how femicide is framed and addressed under varying conditions of state presence, organizational capacity, and community trust.

The case comparison was not intended to assess effectiveness in a conventional sense, but rather to explore how divergent organizational logics and sociopolitical contexts shape discourses, strategies, and tensions in the field of femicide response. The unit of analysis was not only the institution or organization but also the symbolic and practical frameworks through which femicide is understood and contested. This approach aligns with analytic goals in sociolegal studies and feminist criminology, where meaning-making, legitimacy, and institutional discourse are central to understanding how responses to violence are constructed [27, 28]. While Frías [28] focuses on the plural and often contradictory justice logics surrounding femicide in Mexico, Bui and Morash [27] underscore how institutional responses are shaped by culturally embedded understandings of gender, legitimacy, and violence, particularly within marginalized communities.

Data collection

Fieldwork was conducted between 2022 and 2024 and included 64 semi-structured interviews and participant observation. These methods are well-established in public health and violence prevention research for their capacity to capture lived experience, institutional processes, and social context [29, 30]. Interviews were conducted in Spanish and ranged from 45 min to three hours, with an average duration of approximately 90 min. A semi-structured interview guide was used to ensure consistency while allowing for the emergence of context-specific themes. The guide was designed to elicit narratives about institutional responses, justice-seeking pathways, barriers to support, and perceived legitimacy of state and community-based actors.

Participants were recruited through purposive and snowball sampling to capture variation across geography, institutional affiliation, gender identity, and proximity to violence. Interviewees included survivors, rural residents, healthcare providers, legal advocates, social workers, grassroots organizers, and personnel from formal organizations. For analytical clarity, formal organizational actors are defined as government-affiliated institutions or legally registered non-governmental organizations (NGOs) that operate within national or regional prevention systems. Among the community-based participants, 31 individuals were interviewed across three municipalities in Michoacán—Morelia, Pátzcuaro, and Zamora—and in Mexico City. Among formal actors, 33 individuals were interviewed across a range of governmental and NGO institutions.

Participant observation was also conducted at six public events centered on femicide and gender-based violence: three in Michoacán and three in Mexico City. These included marches, vigils, and protests. Fieldnotes documented messaging, symbolic representations of femicide, interactions between activists and state actors, and the spatial dynamics of collective resistance. These observations helped contextualize the narratives shared in interviews and offered insight into the performative and emotional dimensions of public discourse on femicide.

Data analysis

Thematic analysis was conducted using an inductive, iterative approach based on Braun and Clarke’s [31] framework. Transcripts and fieldnotes were closely read and initially coded for emergent themes related to organizational framing, survivor navigation, and expressions of legitimacy. Early codes included “symbolic state response,” “institutional versus emotional knowledge,” “justice as care,” and “relational trust.” As femicide emerged as a central analytic category, the codebook was refined to explore tensions in urgency, framing, and action between grassroots and institutional actors.

A second-cycle coding was used to refine emergent patterns into higher-order thematic categories. These categories were then compared across the two case sites to identify both convergences and divergences in how femicide was framed and acted upon. Particular attention was paid to how meaning was constructed around key terms such as “justice,” “response,” and “state,” and how these meanings varied by organizational context. This approach allowed for the tracing of both symbolic and procedural logics and how these were expressed differently across institutional and grassroots settings.

Coding was conducted using ATLAS.ti qualitative analysis software. Analytic memos were developed throughout to document evolving interpretations, methodological tensions, and positional reflections. Comparative analysis was employed to identify shared challenges and context-specific patterns across the two field sites. This included examining the ways in which different actors invoked femicide, the logics underpinning their interventions, and the material and symbolic resources mobilized in each setting.

To strengthen analytic rigor, multiple strategies were employed to address common concerns about validity and trustworthiness in qualitative research. Data triangulation across interviews, observations, and documents helped verify findings. Peer debriefing through qualitative working groups and continuous member-checking with community-based actors enabled refinement of emergent themes and ensured alignment with local realities. These practices align with grounded theory and feminist standards for transparency, reflexivity, and methodological rigor [20, 30].

Researcher positionality and reflexivity

The study was conducted by a survivor-researcher with familial and cultural ties to Michoacán. These relationships facilitated access to rural communities and shaped the ethical stance of the research. The researcher’s professional background includes domestic violence crisis intervention work and internships with UN Women Mexico, INMUJERES, and SEIMUJER—providing insight into both community-level and bureaucratic responses to gender-based violence. These experiences enabled an understanding of how institutional logics interact with survivor needs, and how gaps in the system are navigated on the ground.

As a survivor of intimate partner violence, the researcher brings personal insight into the affective, material, and institutional dimensions of harm. This positionality was not incidental, but integral to the research design, shaping interview practices, analytic decisions, and ethical commitments. Reflexive practices included the use of analytic memos to track emotional responses, moments of resonance, and shifting interpretations. Power dynamics were explicitly managed—particularly in interviews with institutional actors—while trauma-informed techniques were employed in interviews involving survivors or discussions of loss. This layered perspective—researcher, advocate, survivor—enhanced both the analytical depth and ethical rigor of the study.

Ethics approval

This study received ethical approval from the Institutional Review Board at the University of California, Irvine (Protocol #728). Verbal informed consent was obtained from all participants. Confidentiality measures included pseudonyms, data de-identification, and secure storage. Participants were informed of their right to pause or withdraw from the study at any time. Trauma-sensitive interviewing techniques were employed, and referral information for local support services was offered at the conclusion of each interview.

Findings

This section presents three interrelated themes that illustrate how formal institutions and grassroots organizations conceptualize, enact, and legitimize responses to femicide. Drawing from interviews and field observations conducted in Michoacán and Mexico City, the findings reveal contrasting logics concerning urgency, survivor support, and institutional authority. These divergences reflect not only practical and operational differences, but also deeper epistemic tensions—rooted in conflicting values around knowledge production, legitimacy, and accountability amid persistent gender-based violence. These themes echo earlier critiques of carceral feminism and state-centered logics of protection, and they reflect the plural and affective justice strategies that often arise in contexts marked by institutional fragility. Contrasting logics: institutional metrics vs. Embodied response

Formal institutions frequently framed their approach to femicide as a technical and procedural challenge. Officials described their primary tasks as developing protocols, promoting interagency coordination, and aligning prevention frameworks with international mandates. A Mexico City official explained, “Of course we measure progress by the number of femicide, but we also measure progress by gradual measures, by how many municipalities adopt the guidelines and how many officers complete the module on gender-based violence. Not just by the numbers of femicide.” This institutional logic relied on metrics, standardized training modules, and top-down policy implementation. It conceptualized prevention through a bureaucratic lens, privileging system-building over immediacy. While this logic offered a vision of sustainability and technical rigor, it often sidelined the emotional and temporal urgency that femicide generates in communities.

In contrast, grassroots actors—particularly in Michoacán—understood femicide as a public emergency demanding visible, emotional, and collective response. Community organizers, mothers of victims, and youth activists spoke of painting murals, organizing vigils, and chanting victims’ names not only as acts of mourning but as resistance and deterrence. One aunt, whose niece was killed in rural Michoacán, recalled: “Not one person from the big organizations came. But the town walked with my sister and family. That was the first time we felt seen—even if the case is still unsolved.”

This clash between institutional metrics and community mourning reflects an epistemic disjuncture over what constitutes “meaningful” action. While formal actors emphasized policy timelines and legal compliance, grassroots participants insisted that urgency and presence were themselves forms of justice. This theme resonates with critiques of carceral feminisms that privilege state responses while marginalizing lived experience and embodied knowledge [32, 33].

Divergent pathways to protection: accessibility, trust, and survival

Participants described significant disparities in access to protection, particularly when facing imminent risk of femicide. Survivors from rural regions recounted being turned away from police stations, asked for burdensome documentation, or blamed for their own victimization. One survivor shared, “They told me they did not handle those kinds of cases there.” Others reported that institutions only responded after a death had occurred, reinforcing a sense that protection was reactive rather than preventive. This reactive posture produced a sense of abandonment and reinforced survivors’ mistrust in formal systems.

In these gaps, informal networks became lifelines. Community-based collectives, often composed of survivors and family members of victims, offered accompaniment, temporary housing, and emotional support. These efforts were nimble, relational, and based on belief rather than bureaucratic proof. A grassroots organizer stated: “We do not wait for permission. If a woman says she’s in danger, we act. Because sometimes there’s no second chance.”

Despite their effectiveness, these responses remained invisible or unacknowledged by formal institutions. Their work was unpaid, emotionally taxing, and excluded from prevention frameworks. Yet they were widely described by participants as the most trustworthy, accessible forms of support—raising critical questions about who is deemed a “legitimate” provider of care and protection. These findings reinforce the limitations of institutional approaches grounded in bureaucratic metrics and point to the importance of relational autonomy and survival rationality [23]. Grassroots responses embody a logic of care and immediacy that institutions struggle to replicate.

Epistemic tensions and fragmented collaboration

The final theme concerns the strained and often asymmetrical relationships between formal and grassroots actors. While many formal representatives acknowledged the symbolic value of community efforts, they frequently described them as “emotional,” “useful,” or “awareness-raising”—terms that positioned grassroots actions as ancillary to “real” policy work. Conversely, grassroots organizers spoke of feeling dismissed, tokenized, or used for institutional legitimacy without genuine collaboration. One activist explained: “They take our words and put them in reports. But they do not walk with us. They do not mourn with us.”

Barriers to collaboration included differences in timelines, definitions of risk, and institutional tolerance for dissent. Grassroots actors worked immediately and emotionally, while institutions prioritized planning cycles, risk management, and professional tone. As a grassroots coordinator put it: “They want us to be calm, professional, and neutral. But we are angry. We have lost people.”

Despite these divides, many participants—particularly from grassroots groups—expressed interest in deeper collaboration, including shared funding, cross-training, and co-designed strategies. However, they emphasized that true partnership would require power-sharing, not just symbolic inclusion. As one community organizer summarized, “We are building trust and supporting victims’ families while they’re writing reports about laws and policies.” This theme underscores broader feminist and decolonial critiques of how legitimacy is conferred and withheld in institutional spaces [34, 35]. The epistemic tensions between institutional authority and community-rooted knowledge are not merely procedural—they reflect a broader conflict over what counts as evidence, care, and justice.

Discussion

This study contributes to a growing body of feminist public health literature that conceptualizes femicide not as an isolated act of interpersonal violence but as the outcome of structural and institutional conditions—patriarchy, impunity, and systemic neglect—that create and sustain gendered vulnerabilities. By comparing formal and grassroots organizational responses to femicide in Mexico City and Michoacán, this study reveals not only differing strategies but fundamentally distinct epistemologies, with significant implications for violence prevention frameworks, public health practice, and survivor wellbeing.

The findings reaffirm and extend calls to frame femicide as a public health crisis shaped by intersecting social determinants of violence, including poverty, geographic exclusion, institutional distrust, and gendered impunity [6, 14, 15, 36]. Formal actors—particularly those tied to government bureaucracies or international initiatives—tended to approach prevention through measurable outputs and institutional protocols. These included standardized trainings, protocol dissemination, adoption of municipal plans, and periodic monitoring. While such activities fulfill procedural mandates and align with international accountability metrics, they often failed to engage with the immediacy, grief, and urgency described by survivors and advocates on the ground. This disconnect underscores critiques within feminist public health literature about the technocratic reduction of harm to managerial practices that obscure structural root causes [15, 37].

Conversely, grassroots actors articulated a distinct logic of prevention rooted in relational care, community visibility, and emotional solidarity. Many described actions like public vigils, collective mourning, feminist murals, accompaniment, and public denunciation as central to their work—not as symbolic gestures but as interventions in and of themselves. These practices echo what Villalón [38] describes as “collective memory mobilizations,” which serve both affective and political functions. They resist the social erasure of victims, demand recognition, and foster localized models of justice. These grassroots strategies reflect core principles of community resilience theory [39], functioning as informal but essential components of the public health response in contexts where state systems are absent, ineffective, or distrusted.

Importantly, these divergent logics of response—technocratic institutionalism versus embodied community care—are not simply operational or tactical. They are epistemic. As Fricker [18] theorizes, epistemic injustice emerges when certain forms of knowledge—especially those rooted in lived experience, emotion, and localized expertise—are systematically devalued. In our study, community advocates described being consulted without influence, praised without power, and referenced without real collaboration. These patterns mirror broader critiques within feminist public health and sociolegal scholarship of tokenistic inclusion and the hierarchical privileging of institutional expertise over survivor knowledge [40, 41].

These exclusions have tangible consequences. Across both Mexico City and Michoacán, survivors and advocates described situations where institutions failed to act even when femicide risk indicators were evident—especially in rural, Indigenous, or otherwise marginalized areas. Geographic isolation, linguistic barriers, fear of retaliation, and deeply entrenched institutional distrust rendered legal mechanisms like alert systems or protection orders functionally inaccessible. In contrast, informal networks mobilized quickly, flexibly, and relationally—offering shelter, accompaniment, information, and collective validation. These findings align with public health literature emphasizing survivor-defined metrics of success, culturally grounded prevention strategies, and community-led service provision in high-impunity settings [14, 15, 37, 42, 43].

Crucially, the comparative design of this study reveals that these differences cannot simply be attributed to regional disparities in institutional strength or resource availability. Rather, they stem from fundamentally divergent assumptions about what counts as prevention, who is best positioned to deliver it, and what timeliness and effectiveness look like in contexts of gendered risk. Formal actors viewed femicide as a legal and bureaucratic challenge requiring long-term structural reform. Grassroots actors, by contrast, treated it as an urgent public emergency demanding immediate and embodied responses.

These findings underscore the need to critically evaluate the disjuncture between formal global commitments—such as those outlined in the SDGs—and the realities of implementation in under-resourced, high-risk areas [11–13]. To improve prevention frameworks, particularly in fragile, rural, or distrustful institutional settings, public health and policy interventions must directly engage these epistemic divides. Following Menjívar and Walsh [Menjívar C, Walsh SD], interventions must be co-produced with those most affected, grounded in local realities, and oriented toward justice as well as safety. Public health literature from Latin America emphasizes the promise of trust-based, participatory models and local knowledge integration as essential components of effective gender-based violence prevention [14, 42, 43].

Femicide in Mexico—particularly in rural and high-impunity regions—cannot be addressed through technocratic institutionalism alone. It demands a fundamental reimagining of whose knowledge counts, whose voices are heard, and what prevention looks like when care, urgency, and justice are defined from the ground up. While this study is grounded in the Mexican context, the structural and epistemic tensions identified here—between institutional technocratic responses and grassroots, relational approaches—may resonate across other postcolonial, high-impunity settings. In contexts where institutional legitimacy is contested and state capacity uneven, grassroots actors often emerge as central to public health and justice efforts, offering insights that may inform cross-contextual feminist and public health scholarship.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, its qualitative design and focus on two specific sites—Mexico City and rural Michoacán—limit the generalizability of findings to other regions of Mexico or Latin America. While these cases provide insight into divergent organizational responses, they do not encompass the full spectrum of approaches or sociopolitical conditions across the country.

Second, although the study includes a broad range of institutional and grassroots actors, it does not capture the perspectives of state-level decision-makers or federal agencies involved in femicide prevention. These absences may limit insight into top-down implementation logics and national coordination mechanisms.

Third, while participant observation allowed for embedded understanding, the research was temporally bounded (2022–2024) and may not fully capture evolving dynamics—particularly in high-conflict regions with frequent shifts in political leadership and public security.

Fourth, the study is also shaped by the researcher’s positionality as both an external scholar and a collaborator with grassroots actors. While this dual position enabled trust and depth in community-based spaces, it may also have introduced interpretive biases or shaped which actors were willing to participate. Reflexive memoing and triangulation were used to mitigate these influences.

Finally, given the sensitivity of the topic, interviews were conducted with a strong emphasis on minimizing retraumatization and respecting participant boundaries. As a result, some lines of inquiry—particularly regarding specific femicide cases or institutional misconduct—were not pursued in depth. These ethical constraints were intentional and grounded in trauma-informed protocols designed to prioritize participant safety, emotional wellbeing, and consent, in accordance with institutional review standards.

Policy implications

Preventing femicide in Mexico requires more than procedural compliance—it demands a shift in how institutions conceptualize violence, measure effectiveness, and engage with communities. While national legislation such as the General Law on Women’s Access to a Life Free of Violence (LGAMVLV) provides formal infrastructure, this study reveals a persistent disconnection between institutional strategies and the lived realities of those most affected. Particularly in rural and high-impunity contexts, institutional responses are frequently experienced as slow, symbolic, or absent. In contrast, grassroots networks operate with immediacy, flexibility, and cultural fluency—qualities that institutional frameworks have yet to meaningfully integrate.

One central implication is the need to formalize the role of grassroots actors within national prevention systems. Community-based groups—often led by survivors and victims’ families—are already performing essential public health functions, including accompaniment, crisis intervention, and visibility efforts. Yet, they remain under-resourced and structurally excluded. Institutional partnerships must move beyond tokenistic consultations toward shared governance models that grant grassroots actors decision-making power over prevention design, implementation, and evaluation. This requires not only recognition, but also direct funding mechanisms, capacity-building, and protections for those at the frontlines of femicide response.

Second, metrics for evaluating femicide prevention must be reoriented toward survivor-defined indicators of success. Current frameworks prioritize quantifiable outputs—such as number of trainings or municipalities adopting protocols—but these often obscure critical outcomes such as survivor trust, perceived safety, or successful informal interventions. Integrating qualitative and participatory evaluation tools—such as narrative-based assessments and community-led monitoring—can help capture the complexity of prevention efforts in under-resourced and high-risk settings.

Third, this study highlights the need for emergency response protocols tailored to rural and high-impunity regions, co-developed with grassroots actors. These protocols should include mechanisms for temporary shelter, accompaniment, emergency relocation, and legal navigation—services that are already being informally provided but without institutional support. Crisis intervention funds should be made available directly to community collectives and local organizations capable of rapid mobilization. In high-femicide contexts, speed and proximity save lives.

Fourth, sustainable prevention requires a redistribution of epistemic and material power. Collaborative spaces such as roundtables or co-hosted campaigns are often limited by underlying hierarchies. Institutional actors must be trained to understand grassroots knowledge not as anecdotal or emotional, but as critical expertise shaped by proximity to harm. Cross-training programs—where public officials and community-based advocates learn from each other—can build mutual understanding and reduce the epistemic barriers that inhibit effective collaboration.

Finally, policy reform must acknowledge that femicide is both a structural and an intersectional crisis. Prevention strategies must be grounded in an understanding of how gendered violence is compounded by poverty, Indigenous identity, rural isolation, and institutional distrust. The socio-ecological model remains a useful tool but must be adapted to reflect conditions where state presence is fragmented and legitimacy is in crisis. In such contexts, relational infrastructure—the web of trust, accompaniment, and shared mourning built by communities—functions as the true frontline of prevention. Policies that invest in and learn from these infrastructures will be better equipped to address not only femicide, but the broader ecosystem of violence that sustains it.

As Mexico remains formally committed to global frameworks like the Sustainable Development Goals, specifically SDG 5.2 (eliminating violence against women) and SDG 16 (access to justice and strong institutions) [11, 44], this study reveals how implementation gaps persist, especially in regions marked by high impunity. Aligning these commitments with community-grounded strategies is critical to realizing their goals [12].

Conclusion

Addressing femicide in Mexico requires more than institutional reform or legal harmonization—it demands a fundamental transformation in how femicide is conceptualized, whose knowledge is validated, and which forms of response are prioritized. This study has shown that responses to femicide unfold along two distinct but often disconnected paths: a formal, bureaucratically structured system grounded in law, metrics, and institutional mandates, and a grassroots system rooted in emotional urgency, symbolic resistance, and relational care.

While both systems purport to reduce gender-based violence, they operate through divergent logics and occupy different positions of legitimacy, visibility, and power. Formal institutions tend to frame femicide through a lens of procedural governance—focusing on codified definitions, training metrics, and measurable outputs. This approach privileges technocratic solutions and often overlooks the lived realities of survivors, especially in rural or high-impunity regions. Grassroots actors, by contrast, engage femicide as a moral and communal crisis. Their practices are shaped by the immediacy of loss, the need for public recognition, and the imperative to create safety when institutional support is absent or inaccessible.

Importantly, grassroots responses are not peripheral—they are often the primary or only form of intervention available to at-risk women and their communities. Yet these efforts remain structurally excluded from formal prevention frameworks, dismissed as emotional or unsustainable despite their relational depth and cultural legitimacy. This exclusion reflects enduring epistemic hierarchies that marginalize survivor knowledge, community-based strategies, and non-institutional forms of care. As this study and others suggest, these dynamics are not simply gaps in coordination—they represent fundamental differences in how violence, urgency, and justice are defined.

Reimagining femicide response as a public health and structural issue requires confronting these divides. It calls for shared governance models that redistribute power and resources, and for institutional humility that makes space for community leadership. Rather than measuring success solely through protocols implemented or municipalities reached, systems must begin to account for survivor-defined safety, trust, and belonging. These are not auxiliary concerns—they are central to any meaningful response to femicide.

Although focused on Mexico, these findings may hold relevance for other Global South contexts grappling with fragmented state presence, gendered impunity, and epistemic marginalization. The divergence between institutional and grassroots logics of prevention, as well as the exclusion of survivor expertise, are not unique to this setting and warrant broader comparative attention in violence prevention efforts globally.

Ultimately, this study affirms that building more just, effective, and culturally grounded responses to femicide will require elevating the work already being done in marginalized communities—not as a stopgap, but as a model. Recognizing grassroots actors as co-architects of femicide response is not only a matter of inclusion—it is a prerequisite for structural transformation. If femicide is to be addressed as the public emergency it is, then prevention must begin with those closest to the harm—and with the forms of care and resistance they have long practiced in the absence of the state.

Ethics approval and consent to participate

This study received ethical approval from the Institutional Review Board at the University of California, Irvine (Protocol #728) and was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. Verbal informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to data collection. Confidentiality measures included pseudonyms, data de-identification, and secure storage. Participants were informed of their right to pause or withdraw from the study at any time. Trauma-sensitive interviewing techniques were employed, and referral information for local support services was offered at the conclusion of each interview.
