Background

Reanalysis – the process of re-examining a person’s existing genomic data based on new knowledge or technology – has the potential to greatly increase diagnostic yield for people living with a genetic, rare or undiagnosed condition [1]; a recent systematic review showed that 10% of as-yet-undiagnosed patients received a diagnosis when their data was reanalysed at just two years post-test [2]. The potential diagnostic benefit of reanalysis is expected to increase as more genomes are sequenced and knowledge of gene-disease relationships expands; variants previously considered non-diagnostic may later be recognised as clinically relevant. Consequently, some have suggested that all undiagnosed cases should be routinely reanalysed [3, 4] and some professional bodies have issued recommendations [5, 6].Despite these recommendations, which are now over six years old [5], routine reanalysis has not been widely implemented. This is due to several factors, such as the lack of available funding allocated to analysis beyond the original test and haphazard pathways for triggering reanalysis that are often dependent on patients booking following up appointments or returning with new symptoms [7]. However, one of the largest barriers is the labour-intensive nature of doing this reanalysis manually [8].

In Australia, genomic testing is typically provided through clinical genetics services within the public healthcare system, often following referral from a specialist. With diagnostic genomic sequencing (such as exome or genome sequencing) analysis generally involves filtering based on the patient’s clinical presentation, as well as on variant properties, to identify potentially causative variants. If a genetic diagnosis is not found during the initial test, reanalysis of genomic data may occur, but this is not routinely embedded within clinical care pathways and is often triggered haphazardly by clinician review, new clinical information, or research initiatives. Consequently, access to reanalysis is variable and dependent on local resources, funding arrangements, and workforce capacity. The introduction of automation into genomic medicine could help address the current lack of capacity to routinely reanalyse large volumes of genome data. Interviews with clinical genetics and laboratory staff across Australia indicated that automating reanalysis could help to streamline processes, reduce variation in analysis across patients and patient groups, and optimise resource use [1]. Using technical advancements to routinely reanalyse genomic data could increase efficiency and accuracy, subsequently providing an opportunity to increase the availability (and frequency) of reanalysis to all people living with an undiagnosed condition [9].

These kinds of automation tools are currently being developed, trialled and validated [10] and are set to revolutionise our capacity to reanalyse genomic data from people living with an undiagnosed condition, both retrospectively and moving forward. However, as with most new technologies, these bring with them additional ethical and practical challenges that require consideration. Some of these relate to the potential overlap, conflation with, and confusion about the involvement of artificial intelligence (AI) (or lack thereof) in the use of machines to assist processes.

Artificial intelligence (AI) refers broadly to computational systems designed to perform tasks that typically require human intelligence, such as learning from data, pattern recognition, and decision-making. In healthcare, this commonly involves machine learning (ML) approaches that use large datasets to train algorithms capable of identifying patterns and generating predictions. In the context of genomic medicine, ML models may analyse large genomic datasets to identify variants associated with disease or to predict diagnoses based on genomic and clinical data [11]. For example, ML approaches may be used to predict a diagnosis from an individual patient’s genomic data, to identify potential genetic causes of disease in people living with an undiagnosed condition using combined genomic and clinical information, or to predict phenotypic outcomes from genotypic variation [9].

Automation does not (necessarily) involve AI, yet it still raises important questions about bias, transparency and accountability, risk to data security, and the subsequent impact on trust. Despite this, the public’s perspectives on these ethical issues are yet to be examined. To address this, we aimed to qualitatively explore the preferences and values of Australians living with – or caring for a person with – a genetic, rare or undiagnosed condition towards automated reanalysis.

Methods

Research paradigm and author positionality

The authors adopted a pragmatist orientation for this study. The authors’ beliefs were that automation has potential benefits for genomic medicine – including clinicians, patients, health systems and wider society – but that the surrounding ethical issues such as consent models need to be explored carefully as the field evolves.

The author team comprises researchers and clinicians working in Australia in bioethics, social science, and clinical genomics. FL is a genetic counsellor and qualitative researcher whose work focuses on the ethical and social implications of genomic technologies. DV is a social scientist with a background in genetic counselling who researches ethical issues relating to genetic testing and genomics. ZS is a clinical geneticist whose work focuses on the implementation of genomic medicine in healthcare systems. EK and SN are researchers working in the field of genomics and genomic medicine. All authors have an academic interest in the use of automation and AI in genomic medicine, including potential benefits and ethical challenges.

Recruitment

People living with – or caring for a person with – a genetic, rare or undiagnosed condition over the age of 18, and currently residing in Australia were targeted for recruitment.

Participants were recruited via two main avenues: (1) advertisements distributed through local and national rare disease networks’ and support groups’ newsletters and mailing lists; and (2) invitations to people living with – or caring for a person with – a genetic, rare or undiagnosed condition participating in existing research projects (Australian Genomics Health Alliance [12], Australian Undiagnosed Disease Network (UDN-Aus) [13] and GeneSTART [14]) who had given consent to be recontacted for other research purposes. Those who were invited via the advertisements provided their contact details and availabilities via an online consent form. Research participants from the other programs were invited to participate in a focus group via direct email invitation and then asked to complete the online form if they wished to participate.

Participants were sent a AUD$75 voucher as remuneration for their time at focus group completion. Recruitment continued until all avenues were exhausted.

Data collection and analysis

Prior to the focus groups, participants were asked to watch a 4-minute video [15] to provide them with background information about the topics that would be discussed (defining DNA and genomic sequencing, outlining how reanalysis of genomic sequencing data might be automated, and describing how AI might be used in such reanalysis). Focus groups explored participants’ preferences and values regarding key characteristics of automated reanalysis of genomic data. The focus group guide is included as Supplementary Material.

The 1.5-hour focus groups were facilitated by DV with support from FL and SN and conducted via Zoom. Both DV and FL are skilled qualitative researchers with experience in focus group methodology and training in genetic counselling. SN was trained by FL. One of the participants was previously known to the researchers.

Focus groups were recorded and transcribed. Transcripts were analysed by DV, SN and EK using inductive content analysis (ICA), whereby content categories are generated from the data, rather than predetermined [16]. ICA was chosen to allow patterns to emerge directly from participants’ accounts. ICA is a flexible approach well suited to exploring perspectives in areas where existing theoretical frameworks or prior empirical evidence are limited. Coding continued iteratively until all data relevant to the research question had been coded into categories and subcategories. A subset of transcripts were co-coded and findings were discussed by the study team to ensure rigour. Data analysis was managed using NVivo [17].

Content categories

Highlighting the need for automated reanalysis

Participants discussed many enduring challenges with the current system of genomic medicine as a way of highlighting the need for automated genomic reanalysis. They raised concerns relating to the high burden the existing system of genomic medicine places on families, describing a combination of administrative demands, difficulties navigating healthcare systems, emotional strain associated with prolonged uncertainty, and the additional pressures experienced by families with communication or access barriers.

“I think it’s really hard when parents are really busy, or they’re from a CALD [culturally and linguistically diverse] background, or they have an [intellectual disability] themselves, and just the general person puts their faith in the medical system until they have reason to doubt it, so they would presume that [reanalysis is] happening, and then they’re surprised when they’re not.” [Participant 1, Carer].

They recognised both the burden that families of children with genetic conditions already face due to the nature of the conditions, and the added burden that needing to trigger reanalysis may present.

“…most of these families who have got kids with genetic conditions are so time-poor, and you’re often sleep-deprived, you’re dealing with behaviour, you might be dealing with seizures, and just each day you’re in survival mode, for then [sic] to try and think about everything else on top of that…” [Participant 1, Carer].

Participants discussed the lack of access to medical appointments as a major issue for automated reanalysis. This related to long waiting lists to see specialists, including clinical geneticists and genetic counsellors and could be exacerbated by geographical location.

“I think geneticists as well probably aren’t the most accessible people in the hospital…” [Participant 12, Carer].

“…from what I’ve just learnt in the last six to 12 months is that everything’s in a catchment area, so we don’t get seen by our [major city] network, it’ll actually be done at our local hospital, so that’s why I’m on a waitlist.” [Participant 14, Carer].

“…apparently they can’t make appointments more than two years in advance. In fact, I think they can’t make them more than one year in advance, which is ridiculous. If your dentist can do it, and your car people can do it, you should be able to do it at the hospitals.” [Participant 1, Carer].

Participants discussed the challenge of ensuring the patient remains the focus of genomic investigations and stressed the importance of maintaining this focus in the face of reanalysis. They highlighted the lack of written information, contact, or follow up from clinicians and research teams as problematic.

“When we’ve got people that were tested that we know have the gene now interested in having babies, and saying to me, “[P3], what’s the name of the gene?” I can’t tell them, and I can’t find the researcher, and I can’t find the paper…” [Participant 3, Person living with a genetic, rare or undiagnosed condition].

“No one followed up to make sure I didn’t drive off that [bridge] after they diagnosed my child with a life-threatening condition.” [Participant 1, Carer].

Participants also suggested some form of prioritisation or triaging may occur in genomic testing (and therefore reanalysis), noting that they had observed substantial differences in the time taken for different patients to receive genomic results. Participants speculated that this variation might reflect differences in clinical urgency, the severity of a condition, or the availability of specialist services, although the basis for such prioritisation was often unclear to them. Some participants questioned whether these differences reflected equitable access to genomic testing.

“I feel like there is some sort of prioritisation already happening, in the sense that we got our diagnosis and exome sequencing done in a couple of weeks, and other people have told me it’s taken five to six months to get results.” [Participant 12, Carer].

Benefits of automated reanalysis

Focus group participants identified many benefits of automating reanalysis. Participants recognised that implementing automation would facilitate cheaper and faster analysis, enabling testing for more people living with a genetic, rare or undiagnosed condition.

“…technology changes and sometimes it’s more efficient, it’s quicker, it might be cheaper and you’ll be able to run more tests if it’s going through automation.” [Participant 13, Person living with a genetic, rare or undiagnosed condition and carer].

Furthermore, participants assumed that automation would likely also improve the accuracy of genomic testing.

“You’ve got the system finding the results, and then you’ve got it being double checked by the scientists. So, I mean, I don’t know how you could try and be any more accurate than that really.” [Participant 10, Carer].

Reducing the burden for families through improved health outcomes and knowledge about the future was seen as an important advantage of automation.

“…if you were getting more timely reanalysis which could lead to improved treatment and better management of your health in the future then that lessens the burden overall.” [Participant 23, Person living with a genetic, rare or undiagnosed condition].

Additionally, participants anticipated that the automation in genomic medicine was likely to reduce costs for the healthcare system through earlier diagnosis and intervention.

“…to do it and get diagnosed and therefore save the money in the future, when down the track, especially as people get older and more problems arise from whatever the conditions, it’s going to cost the government a hell of a lot more money.” [Participant 22, Person living with a genetic, rare or undiagnosed condition].

Participants also foresaw knowing that a diagnosis was still being sought for them could lead to improvements in peace of mind for people living with a genetic, rare or undiagnosed condition and their families who had access to automated reanalysis.

“I think it has a potential to provide a lot of reassurance to families. Certainly in our situation, a lot of that worry about what the future holds and the admin that we’ve experienced as carers to someone with disability, just having that run in the background is one less thing to have to worry about.” [Participant 11, Carer].

Limited knowledge and misunderstandings about the current process of reanalysis

Focus group discussions unveiled several misunderstandings about how reanalysis currently does, or should, happen. Several participants expressed that they assumed reanalysis would require a new biological sample. Others assumed that currently there was no process for reanalysis unless initiated by the patient.

Additionally, some believed that reanalysis could only occur overseas, and that it would be expensive to facilitate. Others again assumed that the process of reanalysis as it currently occurs was already automated.

Systemic challenges

Participants foresaw some challenges with implementing automation in genomic medicine. These included systemic challenges (such as staff capacity and funding) and the potential limitations of available computing power.

Implications for insurance

Some participants raised concerns for the impact of the results from automated reanalysis on insurance eligibility.

Concerns for privacy and data security

Participants expressed concerns for privacy and security as a result of automated reanalysis. These concerns related to the potential for government to access the data, and the risk of data breaches if data was moved overseas.

Frequency of automated analysis

Participants were undecided regarding the frequency of automated reanalysis. Some participants suggested 6-monthly and 12-monthly intervals for reanalysis to occur because patients were used to waiting, and there was limited benefit to reanalysing the genomic data any more frequently. However, others suggested that this might not be frequent enough, and in fact, waiting a year for a diagnosis may be too long. Conversely, some participants expressed concern about the potential waste of resources if automated reanalysis occurred more frequently than is necessary.

How to trigger automated reanalysis

Participants felt that the health professional with which the patient has an existing relationship should be the one to facilitate reanalysis. Some suggested that integrating reanalysis into the existing system of review appointments may help to streamline its implementation. Irrespective of how reanalysis occurs, participants emphasised that access to reanalysis should be equitable. These views often arose in discussions about perceived prioritisation or variation in access to genomic services, with participants expressing concern that factors such as geography, service availability, or a family’s capacity to advocate for themselves could influence whether reanalysis occurred.

Involvement of artificial intelligence in automated reanalysis

Focus group participants were asked to consider the role of AI in genomic reanalysis. Participants highlighted the need for transparency in any reanalysis process that involved the use of AI. Participants also expressed a desire for some human involvement in the process as a safeguard measure against inaccuracies and enhance trust.

Returning results to the family

Focus groups discussed how results of automated reanalysis should be returned to patients and their families. Some participants suggested sending out an initial letter informing the patient of the result and offering an appointment with their health professional. Others felt that patients and families should be asked how they wished to receive their results prior to the results becoming available. Specifically, participants wanted a person, rather than a machine, to be responsible for delivering the findings of automated reanalysis. Participants agreed that the results of automated reanalysis should be returned to patients by someone with expertise in genetics, particularly if they were already familiar with the family.

When asked which results should be returned to patients, participants felt that they had a right to receive all results, including negative findings and variants of uncertain significance (VUSs). Irrespective of the type of result, focus group participants agreed that counselling at the time of results disclosure and follow up were essential to support people living with a genetic, rare or undiagnosed condition and their families.

Funding considerations

Participants were asked to discuss how they thought automated reanalysis should be funded. Many expressed concerns regarding costs for families if automated reanalysis was self-funded. However, others recognised that automated reanalysis may somewhat duplicate the analysis, leading to an unnecessary use of resources. Other potential funding models proposed included having automated reanalysis fully publicly funded, partially subsidised, and having out-of-pocket costs means-tested.

Discussion

Advancements in genomic medicine have increasingly highlighted the importance of reanalysis of genomic data to improve diagnostic outcomes over time. Our focus group study reveals strong support among people living with a genetic, rare or undiagnosed condition and their carers for the automation of genomic reanalysis, emphasising its potential to reduce both practical and psychological burdens associated with manual processes. Participants underscored the need for transparent communication and continued human oversight to build trust in automated systems. However, widespread misunderstandings about reanalysis, concerns around data privacy, and practical considerations with return of results remain significant barriers that must be addressed to ensure ethical and effective implementation.

The case for automated genomic reanalysis

Although our focus groups were not explicitly designed to explore experiences with the current system of genomic reanalysis, participants frequently raised concerns about its limitations. These discussions underscore a growing consensus around the need for automated genomic reanalysis to reduce the burden on both families and healthcare systems. In our focus groups, participants described multiple forms of burden associated with navigating genomic testing pathways, including administrative and logistical demands, emotional strain, and the ongoing responsibility of coordinating care and information-seeking. Such experiences echo findings in rare disease research describing the substantial practical and emotional labour undertaken by families navigating the diagnostic odyssey [18]. Previous research – our own and others – has similarly shown strong support among clinical genetics and laboratory staff across Australia for the automation of reanalysis processes [1, 19]. However, significant challenges remain, including logistical, ethical, and technical barriers to implementation [8].

Some of these challenges may be mitigated through better education and training for clinicians and laboratory staff involved in genomic medicine, alongside dedicated funding structures, and the strategic embedding of ‘project champions’ to guide implementation [1]. Importantly, our findings reveal that people caring for someone living with a genetic, rare or undiagnosed condition – and those living with such a condition themselves – bring a unique perspective to these discussions. For many, the mere imagination of their data being routinely and automatically reviewed offers psychological relief. It removes the perceived obligation to initiate reanalysis themselves, reducing their mental load and fostering a sense of ongoing support.

Addressing misunderstandings and ethical obligations

Since 2020, the Australian government has funded two cycles of genomic reanalysis for children under the age of 15 following clinical genomic sequencing, with each reanalysis occurring at least 18 months after the initial test [20]. Outside of these funded cycles, reanalysis is not routinely embedded within Australian clinical genomics pathways and may instead be triggered by clinician review, new clinical information, or research initiatives. As a result, access to reanalysis can vary between services and patient groups, and the process is not always transparent to patients and families. Against this backdrop, our study uncovered a wide range of misconceptions about how reanalysis works, ranging from assumptions that a new biological sample is required, to beliefs that reanalysis is already fully automated or solely patient-initiated. These misunderstandings highlight the need to strengthen communication between patients and health professionals at the outset of genomic testing and throughout any subsequent reanalysis. They also underscore the importance of standardising reanalysis practices across services. Automated reanalysis systems may contribute to this by providing more consistent and routine approaches to identifying when genomic data should be reanalysed.

Some participants expressed concern that automated reanalysis might waste resources if performed too frequently. These comments reflect broader uncertainties about how automated systems would operate in practice, including the extent to which automation could reduce workload given the ongoing need for human oversight and clinical recontact. In this context, informed consent must go beyond simple disclosure and actively address misconceptions. It must also include discussion of the roles and responsibilities around recontact, as recommended by both the American College of Medical Genetics (ACMG) and the European Society of Human Genetics (ESHG) [21, 22]. These guidelines advocate for shared responsibility across the clinical team, laboratory, and the patient and their family. Our findings, however, suggest that current practices fall short of these ideals.

Concerns around data privacy and security further complicate the landscape. Training machine learning models requires large genomic datasets, increasing the risk of data breaches or misuse [9]. These concerns echo prior findings from our own work, which identified privacy and data misuse as persistent anxieties within the Australian public – particularly in the context of AI in genomic medicine [23, 24]. These issues warrant clear and explicit discussion with people living with a genetic, rare or undiagnosed condition and their carers, especially when introducing automation into clinical pathways.

Practical implementation: workforce challenges and psychosocial support

Focus group participants voiced diverse preferences for the frequency of reanalysis, with no consensus evident. However, for many, even a one-year delay in potential diagnosis was considered too long. Additionally, participants wanted all results from reanalysis returned to them, and by a person rather than a machine. Participants were also sensitive to the emotional and psychological impact of receiving new findings – often years after the initial testing – highlighting the importance of psychosocial support at the point of recontact. Participant preferences integrated a strong desire for volume of information, flexibility of delivery, and intensive health professional support, collectively raising concerns for impact on genomics workforce capacity in this space.

Genetic health professionals also recognise the ethical weight of recontact, given its implications for patients’ access to clinically relevant information, the potential impact on health management, and the responsibilities placed on clinicians and laboratories to identify and communicate new findings [19].

While the ESHG advises that recontact decisions should be guided by patient interests and prior agreements, they recommend disclosure only of findings with clinical or personal utility [22]. Current ACMG guidance also frames recontacting patients as a minimum ethical standard, but only when medically relevant findings arise [21]. Genetic health professionals also recognise the ethical weight of recontact; it has implications for patients’ access to clinically relevant information, the potential impact on health management, and the responsibilities placed on clinicians and laboratories to identify and communicate new findings [19]. There exists here a tension between the types of reanalysis results to be returned to patients (specifically, whether negative or uncertain findings are routinely disclosed) and whether there is workforce capacity to appropriately communicate with and support families in the delivery of such results.

Nevertheless, sustainability remains a key concern. As the ESHG has noted, recontact systems must be resourced appropriately to ensure long-term viability [22]. A recent survey of Australian genetic health professionals confirms this challenge, identifying workforce capacity as the greatest barrier to reanalysis implementation [8]. These issues support a move toward centralised, automated reanalysis systems that can better facilitate ongoing communication with patients.

Limitations

Our sample included substantially more carers than people living with a genetic, rare or undiagnosed condition themselves. While this may appear imbalanced, it reflects the realities of rare disease care; it is much more common for people with rare disease accessing genomic sequencing to be children which means that their family will be providing consent on their behalf. In situations where the person living with the rare disease is an adult, family members (particularly parents) still may take on primary responsibility for navigating healthcare systems, coordinating appointments, and engaging with research opportunities. As a result, carers may be more likely to participate in studies concerning genomic testing and reanalysis. Their perspectives are therefore highly relevant to understanding how automated reanalysis may affect families managing rare conditions. Nevertheless, future research should continue to prioritise the inclusion of people living with genetic conditions themselves to ensure their perspectives are adequately represented. This study focused specifically on the perspectives of people living with a genetic, rare or undiagnosed condition and their carers. While these voices are critical for understanding the lived experience of genomic testing and reanalysis, other stakeholder perspectives, including those of the broader public, policymakers, and healthcare system decision-makers, are also important to consider. In particular, questions around resource allocation, funding models, and prioritisation within publicly funded healthcare systems will require engagement with a wider range of stakeholders.

Conclusion

Our findings suggest that people living, with and affected by rare, undiagnosed, or genetic conditions believe in the potential for benefit from integrating automated genomic reanalysis into the healthcare system. This appears to be based on both the time and resources it is likely to save as well as the psychological relief it may provide to carers and people living with a genetic, rare or undiagnosed condition. However, successful implementation hinges not just on technological readiness, but also on development of thoughtful governance models, comprehensive informed consent processes, transparent data practices, and sustained human oversight.

While AI systems for use in genomic reanalysis are still maturing [2], they are advancing rapidly [25, 26]. Genomic medicine is uniquely positioned to benefit from these tools, given the data-rich, pattern-dependent nature of genomic datasets and the reliance of variant interpretation on recognising patterns across large-scale genomic and clinical data [9]. Yet, our findings suggest that as we move toward greater automation, careful attention must be paid to issues of equity, accountability, and trust. Embedding these considerations into the design, governance, and delivery of automated reanalysis systems will be essential to realising their full potential in ethically responsible ways.
