Toward personalized medicine in AD/ADRD through genetic-exposome dementia risk assessments
Authors: Paulina Tolosa-Tort, Meri Okorie, Aadrita Chatterjee, Ana I Boeriu, Rakshya U Sharma, Shea J Andrews
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGenetic-Exposome Dementia Risk Assessments (GEDRA) integrate genomic and environmental risk factors into actionable, patient-centered tools for Alzheimer’s disease and related dementias (AD/ADRD). By enabling midlife risk stratification before biomarker or cognitive changes, GEDRA supports personalized medicine through targeted screening, tailored interventions, treatment guidance, and proactive planning. Strategic investment in GEDRA will advance responsible risk communication and translate precision prevention strategies into equitable, clinically meaningful practice.Subject terms: Biomarkers, Diseases, Health care, Medical research, Neurology, Neuroscience

Introduction

Alzheimer’s disease (AD) and Alzheimer’s disease-related dementias (ADRD) are a global health challenge due to their rising global prevalence and socioeconomic impact1. Their complexity and heterogeneity continue to pose obstacles in both research and clinical care, particularly in efforts to intervene early2–6. Timely risk identification is critical: growing evidence shows that early diagnosis and preventive strategies can extend healthy years of life and reduce disease burden—even among individuals with high genetic susceptibility7. In this context, accurate risk assessment before the onset of irreversible neurodegeneration is critical8. A growing body of evidence highlights the value of incorporating genomic information into complex disease risk models to improve personalized risk assessment, screening, and prevention9. This underscores the need for a comprehensive, integrated approach to AD/ADRD risk assessment, incorporating both genetic and modifiable factors to refine prevention and treatment efforts. Genetic-Exposome Dementia Risk Assessments (GEDRA), which integrate both genetic and non-genetic risk factors, represent a novel framework for personalized dementia risk assessment (Box 1). In this review, we outline the core components of GEDRA, discuss challenges to its implementation, and highlight its potential clinical and personal utility for precision dementia prevention.

Current advances and limitations in Alzheimer’s disease management

AD/ADRD is a growing public health crisis, with AD/ADRD expected to be among the leading causes of death globally and the 5th leading cause of years of life lost by 204010. Globally, dementia cases are expected to rise to 131.5 million by 2050, with much of this increase driven by low- and middle-income countries, with global costs reaching $16.9 trillion1. In the U.S., its prevalence is projected to double by 2060 to 13.8 million, with the total economic burden exceeding $1.4 trillion11. Despite these dire projections, we are now entering a new era in the clinical management of AD/ADRD with the availability of amyloid, tau, and neurodegeneration (A/T/N) blood-based biomarkers, anti-amyloid immunotherapies, and the success of multi-domain lifestyle intervention trials for prevention.

A/T/N plasma biomarkers show fair-to-good predictive performance and become abnormal years before clinical symptoms, offering opportunities for earlier diagnosis in primary care settings12,13. However, they only indicate disease once pathology has begun, may overdiagnose older adults, and cannot identify who is most likely to be biologically vulnerable14. Amyloid-β-targeting monoclonal antibodies, while slowing disease progression, are limited by costs, substantial medical infrastructure demands, and reduced benefits in patients with mixed pathology15,16. Lifestyle interventions targeting modifiable risk factors have demonstrated cognitive and health benefits in older adults at risk of dementia17,18; however, growing evidence suggests that midlife represents the most critical window for optimizing brain health and reducing dementia incidence in aging populations19,20.

With A/T/N biomarkers only indicating when pathology begins and anti-amyloid-β monoclonal antibodies only approved for use in the “early AD stage,” where AD pathology is present alongside clinical symptoms, there is an urgent need for improved risk assessment to facilitate personalized screening and prevention in midlife before A/T/N biomarkers become elevated (Fig. 1).

Genetic-exposome dementia risk assessments

Individuals are increasingly seeking to understand their personal risk of AD/ADRD, particularly as prevention strategies and disease-modifying treatments advance21,22. As a complex multifactorial disease, dementia risk is influenced by genetic and non-genetic risk factors23. Heritability estimates suggest that genetics account for 40–60% of the variation in AD risk24–28. Rare, high-penetrance monogenic mutations in APP, PSEN1, and PSEN2 are responsible for autosomal dominant forms of AD (ADAD)24–26. Sporadic AD has a strong polygenic component, the APOE*ε4 allele is the most influential common variant, and genome-wide association studies (GWAS) have identified more than 80 additional risk loci with smaller effects29,30. Beyond genetics, approximately 45% of dementia cases are attributable to 14 modifiable risk factors that can potentially be targeted through lifestyle and clinical interventions to reduce risk7. Taken together, these findings underscore the need for personalized risk assessments that integrate both genetic and modifiable factors.

Genomic-Informed Risk Assessments are a novel tool that directly addresses this need by integrating multiple sources of risk, including monogenic variants, polygenic risk scores, family history, and clinical risk factors31. Additionally, they provide care guidelines and lifestyle recommendations to both patients and providers, with the goal of improving risk communication and uptake of preventive interventions9,31. The eMERGE network is already piloting genomic-informed risk assessments for multiple diseases, underscoring the feasibility of such approaches for AD/ADRD9,31.

(GEDRA, pronounced jed-rah) extend the GIRA framework by integrating ADAD mutations, APOE, polygenic risk scores (PRS), family history, and polyexposure scores (PXS) into personalized dementia risk assessments. GEDRA operationalizes emerging diagnostic frameworks for AD/ADRD, including the probabilistic model by capturing the full spectrum of genetic and non-genetic risk factors that shape disease heterogeneity, and the tridimensional framework by linking etiology (genetics and the exposome) with biomarkers of pathology and neuroanatomical–clinical features2,32. Critically, GEDRA enables risk stratification to begin in midlife, well before A/T/N biomarker and cognitive changes become detectable. Midlife risk assessment that combines genetic and clinical risk factors can guide precision prevention by prioritizing patients for biomarker screening, tailoring interventions targeting modifiable risk factors, informing treatment response to disease-modifying therapies, and supporting proactive advanced planning (Fig. 1).

In the following sections, we examine the core components of a GEDRA, evaluating both their individual contributions to risk prediction and their integration into a comprehensive framework. We highlight the evidence supporting each component, how they interact, and the challenges surrounding their measurement, interpretation, implementation, and applicability across diverse populations. Finally, we outline a framework for incorporating genomic-informed risk assessment into AD care, showing how GEDRA can be leveraged alongside emerging blood-based biomarkers and disease-modifying therapies to optimize early intervention, support personalized prevention, and enable personal decision making.

Core components of the GEDRA Framework

Autosomal Dominant Alzheimer’s Disease Mutations: Beyond determinism

Autosomal Dominant Alzheimer’s Disease (ADAD) represents a distinct, monogenic form of AD with a clear inheritance pattern and early onset. While ADAD accounts for less than 1% of all AD cases, symptoms typically manifest between the ages of 30 and 5033,34. For GEDRA, ADAD mutations represent the highest genetic risk category in which a single mutation is both necessary and sufficient to cause the full spectrum of AD pathobiology. However, disclosure of ADAD mutations must account for modifying genetic and non-genetic factors that influence clinical progression.

ADAD follows an autosomal dominant inheritance pattern, where a single mutation in one of APP, PSEN1, or PSEN2 almost invariably leads to disease onset33,34. To date, over 250 distinct pathogenic variants have been identified across these genes33,34. These mutations shift APP processing towards the amyloidogenic pathway, with APP mutations resulting in overproduction of amyloid-β (Aβ), whereas PSEN1 and PSEN2 mutations impair γ-secretase processivity, elevating the Aβ42:Aβ40 ratio and promoting accumulation of aggregation-prone Aβ4235,36. ADAD mutations produce a clinical-pathobiological trajectory that closely follows the deterministic amyloid hypothesis, with early and widespread Aβ deposition across the neocortex, followed by overlapping tau pathology and subsequent neurodegeneration in temporoparietal and medial temporal regions. Clinically, this manifests as an amnestic-predominant syndrome with symptom onset typically between ages 30 and 50 and an average disease duration of 10 years33,34.

Despite this deterministic nature, phenotypic expression—particularly in the age of onset—shows considerable variability. In the Dominantly Inherited Alzheimer Network cohort, age at symptom onset has a standard deviation of ~9 years, with the mutation type/genetic variant explaining just over 50% of the variance in individual onset timing (Fig. 2)34. Notably, even among individuals with identical mutations in the same family, age of onset can vary by more than a decade33,34. The nearly 50% of unexplained variance in ADAD age of onset reflects the influence of additional stochastic factors beyond the primary genetic determinants.

Notably, rare protective variants have been reported that may delay disease onset in otherwise fully penetrant ADAD mutations. In the PSEN1-E280A Colombian kindred, a woman homozygous for the APOE Christchurch variant remained cognitively unimpaired for almost three decades beyond the expected onset age, despite widespread amyloid deposition37. A similar, though less pronounced delay has been observed in heterozygous APOE*ε3 Christchurch carriers, although this observation remains under active investigation38,39. In another case, an individual heterozygous for the RELN H3447R variant showed preserved cognition despite a high amyloid plaque burden, attributed to limited entorhinal tau pathology40 Likewise, a presenilin 2 p.Asn141Ile carrier, remained cognitively intact more than 20 years past the typical age of onset age, again with extensive amyloid pathology but reduced tau accumulation; in this case, MAPT haplotype variants and occupational heat exposure were proposed as modifiers41.

Beyond rare variants, common genetic modifiers that influence age of onset and rate of cognitive decline in familial and early onset forms have been identified among disease-associated common variants from late-onset Alzheimer’s disease (LOAD) cohorts42–44. APOE genotype modulates disease progression in ADAD, with the ε4 allele accelerating cognitive decline and biomarker changes, and ε2 associated with delayed onset and attenuation of neurodegeneration-related effects45–48. Non-genetic factors like higher education, cognitive engagement, and vascular health have been also shown to contribute to delayed symptom onset despite similar pathology47.

Together, these findings shift our understanding of ADAD from a purely deterministic framework to one in which mutations almost invariably guarantee disease onset, but genetic and non-genetic factors shape timing, severity, and presentation. The growing availability of biobanks with large-scale sequencing creates opportunities to systematically identify additional resilient carriers and the factors underlying their protection49. The substantial variability in age at onset highlights the need for personalized risk assessments that can generate actionable insights to inform proactive, delay-oriented interventions.

APOE genotype: semi-dominant inheritance in sporadic AD

APOE is the largest common genetic risk factor for sporadic AD due to the high population frequency and large effect size of its risk-defining allele. APOE exists in three common isoforms (ε2, ε3, and ε4) with global prevalences of 7%, 79%, and 14% respectively50. The APOE*ε2 allele is the strongest protective common allele against AD, while the ε4 allele increases risk in a dose-dependent manner—individuals carrying one ε4 allele have a 3–4-fold higher risk, while homozygotes face a 9–15-fold increased risk50–52. However, the impact of APOE*ε4 on AD risk varies by ancestry, being strongest in individuals of East Asian descent, intermediate in those of European descent, and attenuated in individuals of African and Hispanic descent53.

Although the APOE*ε4 allele is strongly associated with AD, its inheritance pattern differs from the autosomal dominant transmission seen in ADAD mutations. Instead, it follows a semi-dominant inheritance pattern, with risk depending on whether individuals are heterozygous or homozygous for the APOE*ε4 allele54. The lifetime risk of dementia by age 85 varies by APOE genotype, with estimates of approximately 59% for individuals with two copies of the APOE ε4 allele, 48% for those with one copy, and 39% for those with no copies, with divergence in risk becoming apparent at approximately age 70 years52,55. APOE*ε4 homozygotes begin to show biomarker evidence of AD pathology around age 55, and by age 65, most carriers have abnormal CSF amyloid levels and positive amyloid brain scans56. Symptom onset also occurs 7–10 years earlier in APOE*ε4 homozygotes compared with APOE*ε3 homozygotes, with the predictability similar to that of ADAD mutation carriers56.

The clinico-pathobiological features of AD dementia also differ markedly by APOE genotype2. Carriers of the APOE*ε4 allele typically show earlier and greater amyloid deposition with a frontal predominance, more severe cerebral amyloid angiopathy with capillary involvement, medial temporal–focused tau pathology, and an amnestic clinical profile characterized by disproportionate memory impairment. By contrast, non-carriers more often exhibit parietal-predominant amyloid deposition, less severe CAA, greater frontal and parietal tau pathology with associated cortical atrophy, and a clinical phenotype marked by deficits in executive, visuospatial, and language functions.

Despite the high lifetime risk and predictable sequence of biomarker and clinical changes associated with the APOE*ε4 allele, APOE is generally regarded as a risk factor, rather than causative, for sporadic AD. Not all APOE*ε4 carriers develop AD, and among those who do, the age of symptom onset varies considerably54. This variability aligns with the concept of cognitive reserve, whereby non-genetic and genetic protective and risk factors affect the brain’s ability to withstand neuropathological damage through compensatory mechanisms57. Importantly, unfavorable clinical risk profiles can attenuate the protective effects of APOE*ε2 and exacerbate the APOE*ε4 risk effect, whereas favorable clinical risk profiles mitigate the impact of APOE*ε4 on AD risk58.

While APOE has the largest effect size among common genetic variants for sporadic AD, recent whole exome- and genome-sequencing studies have also identified an intermediate class of rare variants with effect sizes comparable to APOE. These include loss-of-function or missense variants in genes such as TREM2, SORL1, and ABCA759. Although these variants occur at low population frequencies, at the individual level, these variants can substantially elevate AD risk and influence age at symptom onset. Conceptually, these variants occupy a position between APOE and fully penetrant ADAD mutations, combining large individual effects with incomplete penetrance. Their inclusion in GEDRA is particularly valuable because of their mechanistic specificity, as they implicate defined biological pathways and genes for which targeted or gene-informed therapies may become available, similar to emerging approaches for APOE. As sequencing becomes more accessible and clinical interpretation frameworks mature, GEDRA is designed to incorporate such variants dynamically, allowing disease-specific genetic contributors to be updated and expanded over time.

Together, these findings suggest that APOE warrants separate consideration from ADAD mutations and polygenic risk scores in GEDRA. The high prevalence of APOE*ε4, strong dose-dependent effects on risk, and influence on biomarker and clinical trajectories underscore its central role in sporadic AD. At the same time, incomplete penetrance highlights opportunities to mitigate risk through modifiable lifestyle and clinical factors, reinforcing the importance of integrating APOE genotype into prevention-focused, personalized risk assessments.

Polygenic risk scores: from single genes to polygenic burden

Most AD-associated genetic variants identified by genome-wide association studies (GWAS) are common variants with modest effect sizes that do not determine the disease but increase susceptibility24. Polygenic risk scores (PRS) quantify an individual’s genetic liability for a trait or disease by aggregating the effects of multiple SNPs, weighted by their GWAS-derived effect size estimates. PRS are increasingly used to stratify individuals at elevated risk for complex diseases, with the extreme right tail of the PRS distribution identifying up to 20 times more individuals at a given risk level than rare monogenic mutations, despite conferring comparable absolute risk60. These findings highlight the potential of PRS to capture at-risk individuals who would not be identified through monogenic mutations or APOE genotype.

Interest in polygenic risk scores for Alzheimer’s disease (AD-PRS) is rapidly growing. In clinically adjudicated cases, AD-PRS demonstrates good predictive performance, with an area under the curve (AUC) of ~0.74, improving to ~0.84 in pathologically confirmed cases and controls61. Moreover, individuals in the highest quintile of an AD-PRS have a threefold higher risk of dementia compared to the general population62. Beyond dementia risk, AD-PRS show stronger associations with downstream biomarkers such as plasma glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP), CSF tau, and clinical diagnosis, compared with Aβ-specific measures in blood, CSF, or neuroimaging63,64. This suggests that AD-PRS captures non-amyloid pathways contributing to AD pathogenesis, particularly in APOE*ε4-unrelated AD dementia.

The most accurate PRS models exclude the APOE region and treat APOE genotypes as separate predictors, underscoring that APOE and polygenic burden represent distinct but complementary sources of risk65–67. AD-PRS retain their predictive accuracy among pathologically confirmed APOE*ε3 homozygotes and APOE*ε4-negative individuals, particularly aged 70 and over68,69. In APOE*ε4 carriers, the higher AD-PRS exacerbates AD risk, accelerates age at onset by about five years, and exacerbates cognitive decline70–72. These findings highlight a particularly vulnerable subgroup with combined genetic burden.

Despite their promise, the clinical translation of AD-PRS is hampered by limited transportability across ancestrally diverse and admixed populations. Because most GWAS have been conducted in European ancestry cohorts, PRS derived from them often have poor transportability to ancestrally diverse and admixed populations due to differences in linkage disequilibrium, minor allele frequency, and SNP effect sizes73,74. Implementing such models prematurely risks exacerbating existing disparities in AD diagnosis and care75. Ongoing efforts aim to address this by expanding GWAS in underrepresented populations, developing cross-ancestry PRS models, and calibrating PRS distributions according to individualized ancestry76–79. AD-PRS that explicitly incorporate ancestry and population-specific genetic architecture already show improved performance in diverse cohorts compared with single-ancestry European-based models76,80,81.

Nevertheless, methodological diversity further complicates translation. More than 30 methods for deriving PRS have been developed, with little guidance on which are best suited for predicting AD across diverse populations82. Early PRS approaches relied on LD clumping and p-value thresholding to select SNPs for constructing a PRS, whereas newer methods incorporate Bayesian shrinkage of effect sizes, restrict variants to only those with functional validation or in specific biological pathways, integrate multiple traits, and include rare variants82–85. While advances represent important progress, their improvements are often modest, and model performance remains highly trait- and data-dependent9,67,82,86–88. Consequently, systematic benchmarking is needed to establish analytical and clinical validity and clarify which models are most appropriate for AD risk prediction across populations.

Altogether, substantial progress has been made in developing AD-PRS that complements ADAD mutations and APOE in personalized medicine frameworks. However, their use remains largely confined to research settings until analytical and clinical validity are firmly established. Equally important, systematic evaluation of their clinical and personal utility—both for patients and for healthcare providers—is needed to determine their real-world impact and guide responsible implementation.

Family history: from shared inheritance to risk prediction

Family history quantifies the number of relatives diagnosed with dementia and reflects both inherited genetic liability and shared environmental exposures. While PRS are valuable for disease prediction, integrating family history can improve accuracy for complex diseases, particularly in diverse populations and when models account not only for the presence but the type (parental vs sibling) of affected relatives89. Even after adjusting for clinical covariates, family history contributes independent predictive value, suggesting that family history captures additional variability beyond both known genetic and non-genetic risk factors89.

In AD, a parental family history (PFH) of dementia is independently associated with increased risk90. Individuals with a PFH of dementia are also more likely to carry the APOE*e4 allele compared to those without and to have higher PRS91,92. In addition, midlife risk factors such as hypertension, hypercholesterolemia, diabetes, obesity, smoking, and depressive symptoms are more prevalent among individuals with a family history of dementia91. Taken together, family history captures not only the contribution of rare high-penetrance variants but also the cumulative burden of common risk alleles and shared environmental exposures.

Traditional AD risk prediction studies and clinical assessments typically focus only on immediate family members and close relatives93. However, the degree of risk increases with the number and closeness of affected relatives (Fig. 3). Having one or more first-degree relatives (FDRs) significantly elevates risk, with further increases when second-degree relatives or even third-degree relatives are also affected, particularly when multiple relatives are affected93. Additionally, sex-specific effects have been observed with maternal family history associated with higher AD risk and elevated amyloid burden compared to paternal family history, possibly due to mitochondrial or X chromosome effects90,94,95.

To systematize these patterns, scoring tools such as the modified Goldman Score can be used to classify individuals according to the number of affective relatives, inheritance patterns, and age of onset96–98. New approaches also incorporate detailed family history data, such as genealogy records and linked death certificate data, to provide more personalized and accurate risk estimates for AD. Even when detailed family history is unavailable, these methods can still be applied broadly by using estimates rather than requiring exact counts of affected relatives93. While detailed genealogical and registry-based data can improve precision, most variance in AD risk is explained by the presence of affected FDRs, suggesting that simple counts of FDR may be sufficient for clinical implementation of GEDRA.

In sum, family history remains a powerful predictor of AD risk that complements ADAD mutations, APOE, and PRS. Its value lies in capturing residual variability not fully explained by genetics or shared environment. Importantly, a family history of dementia may also serve as the catalyst for individuals to pursue genetic testing and seek a clearer understanding of their personal risk following a recent parental diagnosis.

Polyexposure scores: actionability in AD risk assessment

Polyexposure scores (PXS) estimate an individual’s likelihood of developing AD based on weighted composites of environmental exposures58,99,100. These exposures are often conceptualized within the exposome, encompassing both the personal exposome, which includes lifestyle, clinical, and behavioral exposures, as well as the broader external exposome such as the social and built environments101–103. Unlike PRS, PXS incorporate modifiable factors, making them valuable not only for risk stratification but also for guiding personalized behavioral and lifestyle interventions to reduce dementia risk. Within the GEDRA framework, integrating PXS alongside ADAD mutations, APOE, PRS, and family history allows both precise stratification of biological risk and targeted guidance on how individuals can actively reduce their vulnerability through modifiable pathways.

Within the exposome, modifiable risk factors for dementia encompass behavioral, environmental, socio-cultural, and clinical variables that can be altered through intervention to influence disease risk and outcomes7. Up to 45% of dementia cases could be prevented or delayed by addressing 14 such risk factors with strong evidence of being causally associated with dementia7. Unlike genetic risk factors that predominantly influence AD pathogenesis via amyloid and tau pathology, modifiable risk factors act through more diverse mechanisms, including cerebrovascular health, neuroinflammation, and cognitive reserve (Fig. 4). PXS builds on this by integrating multiple modifiable risk factors into a single composite index, with model composition and weights varying depending on methodologies and training data. PXS demonstrate moderate predictive ability for dementia, with AUCs ranging from 0.58 to 0.76104. Importantly, they moderate genetic susceptibility to dementia, with favorable risk profiles attenuating the risk conferred by the APOE*ε4 genotype or high PRS58.

The FINGER and US-POINTER multidomain lifestyle intervention trials show that targeting diet, exercise, cognitive training, social engagement, and cardiovascular health monitoring can improve risk profiles and cognitive outcomes among high-risk individuals18,105. Furthermore, the SMARRT and MYB trials suggest that personalizing interventions to each person’s risk factor profile may yield greater benefits, since both the risks and the motivation to address them vary widely across individuals17,106. Individual risk reduction can translate to population declines in dementia incidence and likely contributes to the secular decline in dementia incidence observed in several high-income countries, where rising educational attainment and reduced smoking have paralleled decreases in age-specific incidence rates107,108.

Although lifestyle and behavioral changes are increasingly recognized as effective strategies for dementia risk reduction, the clinical uptake of PXS to guide prevention has been limited109. More than 50 PXS models have been developed, yet many lack external validation and show reduced accuracy outside their development cohorts110,111, particularly in populations of non-European descent112–114. This limited portability reflects both methodological issues and population differences in the association of risk factors with dementia pathogenesis104,115. Sex further modifies PXS performance, influencing not only the distribution of risk factors but also the effectiveness of lifestyle interventions116. Critically, if PXS are to inform interventions, the included risk factors must be grounded in causal evidence to ensure that predictions are clinically meaningful and actionable. Looking ahead, expanding PXS beyond traditional modifiable risk factors to incorporate the broader exposome and social determinants of health could improve predictive accuracy while also highlighting systemic barriers to that shape the feasibility of behavioral change.

Altogether, PXS brings actionability to GEDRA. By translating established modifiable risk factors into composite measures, and reinforced by the success of multidomain lifestyle interventions, PXS provide a foundation for personalized, prevention-focused dementia risk assessment and a pathway toward clinical implementation.

Establishing personal and clinical utility of GEDRA

GEDRA that integrates genetic and modifiable risk factors would serve as a practical entry point for midlife risk assessment, for example following a recent parental dementia diagnosis or subjective cognitive concerns (Fig. 5). After a baseline GEDRA profiling, patients could be triaged to pathway-specific next steps. Those with high genetic liability (e.g., APOE*ε4/ε4 or high AD-PRS) might begin earlier and more frequent biomarker surveillance, including A/T/N plasma biomarkers and cognitive screening. Individuals with normal biomarkers but unfavorable clinical profiles could receive an intensified lifestyle counseling, modeled on interventions such as US-POINTER (diet, exercise, cognitive training, and cardiovascular monitoring). Abnormal blood-based biomarkers could trigger referral to specialty care for confirmatory testing (amyloid and/or tau PET or CSF) and MRI, consideration of eligibility for disease-modifying therapies or trials, and tailored monitoring schedules. Genomic profiles would also inform treatment selection and safety monitoring as response and adverse-event risks may differ across genetic subgroups. Throughout this pathway, timely risk knowledge would enable proactive personal, legal, and financial planning while cognition is intact.

GEDRA thus provides a foundation for precision medicine in AD/ADRD, translating population-level evidence into personalized screening, prevention, and treatment decisions. Realizing this vision will require prospective studies testing how genetic-exposome risk profiles influence biomarker trajectories, efficacy of multi-domain interventions, predict treatment response, and overcome implementation barriers.

Guiding personalized biomarker surveillance

GEDRA can guide personalized A/T/N biomarker screening schedules. Genetic and clinical risk factors are not only associated with abnormal A/T/N biomarker profiles but also with differences in the timing of A/T/N positivity117,118. This suggests that individuals identified as high risk by GEDRA may experience earlier or accelerated biomarker changes. GEDRA-derived risk profiles could therefore be used to identify patients most likely to benefit from earlier or more frequent biomarker monitoring. Blood-based A/T/N biomarkers, particularly plasma Aβ42/Aβ40 ratios and p-tau217, become abnormal approximately a decade prior to symptom onset and thus serve as important adjuncts to the GEDRA framework by providing early biological evidence of AD-related pathology13. Longitudinal measurement of these biomarkers can then be integrated with GEDRA risk profiles to enable dynamic, patient-specific predictions of individual clinical trajectories over time.

Beyond screening, biomarkers provide sensitive endpoints for testing the efficacy of multidomain prevention interventions in individuals identified as high-risk by GEDRA. Prior multidomain prevention trials have primarily relied on cognitive outcomes, which may be insufficiently sensitive when interventions are initiated earlier in midlife17,105,106,119. Biomarkers, by contrast, can detect early changes in disease biology and provide evidence that multidomain interventions are altering disease trajectories117. Incorporating biomarkers as endpoints in behavioral interventions operationalizes the sequential use of GEDRA: genomic risk tools enable early stratification, personalized interventions are delivered, and biomarkers are used to monitor both biological efficacy and refine future risk prediction.

Personalizing prevention to promote brain health

GEDRA can strengthen lifestyle interventions to promote brain health by motivating and supporting behavioral change. Personalized estimates of genetic liability for dementia, presented alongside blood-based biomarkers and cognitive testing, can shift perceptions of perceived risk and promote a positive attitude towards behavioral change120,121. Genetic testing on its own, however, is unlikely to lead to behavioral change. When combined with PXS that highlight specific modifiable risk factors and alongside lifestyle coaching, patients receive clear, actionable targets for reducing dementia risk120,121. Evidence that genetic risk assessments motivate behavioral change remains limited, but early findings are encouraging. Studies of APOE disclosure suggest that APOE*ε4 carriers adopt changes in diet, medication use, and advance planning122. Importantly, prevention trials provide emerging evidence that individuals with elevated genetic liability may derive the greatest benefit from interventions. In FINGER and J-MINT, lifestyle interventions had a greater effect on cognitive outcomes in participants with a higher AD-PRS or APOE*ε4 alleles, and in FINGER, on amyloid deposition123–126.

These findings underscore the importance of integrating GEDRA into prevention frameworks, not only to motivate behavior change but also to stratify participants, optimize intervention efficacy, and ultimately personalize dementia prevention.

Precision medicine: matching patients to treatments

As therapeutic developments in AD accelerate, genetic risk profiles are increasingly recognized as key modifiers of treatment selection and response. ADAD mutation and APOE*ε4 carriers have more typical amyloid-driven cognitive impairment, suggesting that carriers may preferentially respond to anti-amyloid therapies2. However, results to date have been mixed. In the DIAN-TU trial, treatment of ADAD mutation carriers with gantenerumab and solanezumab reduced amyloid load, confirming target engagement, but the long-term clinical effects remain uncertain and under active investigation2,127,128. In contrast, trials of lecanemab, aducanumab, solanezumab, and donanemab show modestly greater efficacy in APOE*ε4 carriers relative to non-carriers129. At the same time, APOE*ε4 status confers increased risk of treatment-related adverse events, particularly amyloid-related imaging abnormalities (ARIA)129. These dual influences on both efficacy and safety highlight the importance of incorporating genetic profiling into treatment decisions.

Beyond amyloid, genomic risk profiles could inform treatment selection for emerging therapies. APOE remains a central focus of drug development, with approaches ranging from gene therapy and antisense oligonucleotides to lipid-modulating interventions130,131. Other genetic targets, such as TREM2, have been pursued in microglia-focused trials, though with limited success to date130. Looking forward, AD-PRS that are stratified by biological pathway may provide additional insights into treatment selection, particularly as interventions shift toward multimodal strategies that extend beyond amyloid132,133. Incorporating therapeutic considerations into GEDRA for clinical decision-making for AD therapies could enable more precise matching of patients to therapies, improving both efficacy and safety profiles.

Development and future implementation of GEDRA

Implementing GEDRA will likely require an iterative, staged approach that supports early clinical use while enabling progressively more sophisticated integration of risk domains over time. Initial implementations could draw on genomic risk reporting frameworks being evaluated in the Genomic Medicine at Veterans Affairs (GenoVA) Study and the eMERGE consortium’s Genomic Informed Risk Assessment (GIRA)9,31,134,135. Both GenoVA and eMERGE prioritize interpretability and actionability by using dichotomized and hierarchically structured risk designations. In eMERGE’s GIRA, for example, pathogenic variants, polygenic risk scores, and family history are evaluated sequentially using a predefined hierarchy to determine overall high-risk status, while preserving transparency about each component. An analogous approach may guide initial development and implementation of GEDRA. Evidence from linear burden models—using indicator variables for the presence or absence of individual risk factors—suggests an additive relationship between cumulative risk-factor burden and dementia risk136. However, because penetrance and effect size differ substantially across GEDRA components and components may interact, additive burden models provide a pragmatic starting point for early integration and communication, but will require further development to better reflect relative weighting and interaction effects.

More comprehensive integrated models, including modeling of gene–gene and gene–environment interactions, are still being developed and will require robust validation across diverse populations before clinical deployment137. Subsequent refinements may incorporate stratified risk estimation by age, sex, and ancestry. However, such stratification will depend on the availability of large, ancestrally diverse datasets that ensure adequately powered analyses, accurate calibration, and equitable performance. As evidence accumulates across populations, the structure and weighting of GEDRA will evolve accordingly. Integration of GEDRA within health systems could also enable evaluation of machine learning models that leverage electronic health record–derived information, including longitudinal measures of biomarkers of AD pathology and cognitive screening, to support development of personalized dynamic risk prediction models138. However, increasing model complexity must be balanced against interpretability, transparency, and equitable clinical implementation. GEDRA is therefore envisioned as a flexible framework that can progressively incorporate methodological advances while maintaining clinical usability and responsible translation.

Expanding GEDRA to Alzheimer’s disease-related dementias

The proposed GEDRA framework is anchored in AD, reflecting the comparatively advanced understanding of AD genetic architecture that has enabled increasingly robust polygenic risk modeling. In contrast, genetic discovery of other ADRD, including dementia with Lewy bodies and frontotemporal dementia, remains more limited to pathogenic variants due to smaller GWAS datasets. Extending GEDRA to other dementias could therefore begin by incorporating pathogenic variants for FTD (e.g., C9orf72) and DLB (SNCA). As larger and more diverse cohorts become available, disease-specific polygenic architectures for ADRD can be incorporated into GEDRA alongside established pathogenic variants.

Clinical, behavioral, and environmental risk factors in the broader exposome have been studied extensively in relation to AD and cognitive decline, and many are also likely to influence risk across ADRD, particularly through shared pathways such as cerebrovascular injury, neuroinflammation, metabolic dysfunction, and cognitive reserve [ref]. Accordingly, within GEDRA, PXS provide a flexible risk assessment framework aimed at identifying individuals at elevated risk of dementia broadly, rather than assigning a specific underlying pathology. This framing is particularly relevant given the high prevalence of mixed neuropathologies in older adults, where multiple etiologic processes often contribute to clinical dementia. As genetic and exposome knowledge expands across neurodegenerative disorders, GEDRA can be updated to incorporate disease and pathway-specific risk components that improve etiologic specificity while maintaining its primary role in broad dementia risk stratification.

Disclosure of genetic results

Predictive genetic testing for ADAD mutations has been available for decades, typically offered only to individuals with symptoms or a family history suggestive of autosomal dominant inheritance139,140. Uptake has historically been low, limited by the absence of effective treatments and concerns about stigma or psychological distress associated with positive results140. Similarly, APOE susceptibility testing has not been widely implemented in clinical practice140. This landscape is now changing with the approval of anti-amyloid therapies and the success of multidomain lifestyle interventions, which create meaningful opportunities for earlier intervention. APOE pharmacogenetic testing is also now recommended prior to anti-amyloid therapy treatment initiation to identify patients at elevated risk of ARIA140. While concerns about psychological harm remain, disclosure studies show that revealing APOE genotype or ADAD mutation status has minimal long-term adverse effects122,141,142.

Standards for disclosing PRS are less mature and pose greater challenges than single-variant9,135. PRS models are complex, and without appropriate counseling, patients may struggle to interpret their results121. Low genetic risk may lead to false reassurance, while high or changing risk categories may create confusion as models are updated with new genetic discoveries121. Concerns about psychological distress remain, although current evidence suggests no sustained adverse effects; however, the evidence base is still limited121. These challenges underscore the need for careful communication, ongoing support, standardized disclosure protocols, and regulatory oversight.

As GEDRA moves toward clinical translation, careful attention to disclosure will be essential. Patient-facing materials should be co-designed with stakeholders to ensure information is clear, contextualized, actionable, references appropriate ancestry-matched populations, and specifies meaningful time horizons (e.g., 10-year vs lifetime risk). Results must be linked to practical next steps—such as eligibility for prevention programs, biomarker monitoring, or lifestyle interventions—and presented using visual aids, layered explanations, and culturally tailored framing to accommodate diverse levels of health literacy and numeracy. Embedding GEDRA within a structured, stepwise continuum of care—with follow-up visits and primary-care integration—can ensure that disclosure supports prevention and planning rather than leaving patients to interpret results in isolation. Achieving this will require expanding disclosure capacity beyond genetic counselors through education of other healthcare professionals and, potentially, AI-enabled tools to support scalable, patient-centered implementation143.

Equity, access, and structural barriers

Dementia disproportionality affects marginalized groups, with Black and Hispanic communities experiencing higher prevalence than non-Hispanic White communities in the U.S144. Globally, low- and middle-income countries (LMIC) are projected to bear the greatest increases in incidence and cost145. Without equitable implementation, GEDRA could exacerbate existing health disparities.

Although the costs of genomic technologies have fallen–under USD $1000 for WGS and USD $50 for genotyping arrays–financial barriers, limited infrastructure, and shortages of trained genetic counselors continue to restrict access, especially in low-resource settings and LMIC146. Beyond cost and capacity, uptake is influenced by cultural and psychosocial factors, including limited awareness of genetics, stigma surrounding heritable disease, paternalistic counseling practices, and religious or cultural beliefs that shape the acceptability of testing146. Even where testing is available, differences in health literacy and perceptions of agency can influence whether individuals understand and act on risk information146.

Social determinants of health and broader structural factors will have critical implications for the real-world implementation of GEDRA, influencing feasibility, accessibility, and acceptability across diverse healthcare systems and populations. Equitable implementation will therefore require not only rigorous validation but also adaptation to social context and health-system capacity.

Conclusion

GEDRAs hold significant promise as tools to integrate genetic, clinical, and lifestyle factors into formats that are interpretable, clinically meaningful, and personally relevant. By contextualizing complex risk information, they can empower individuals and families to make informed decisions, while also equipping clinicians with a structured framework for guiding discussions around prevention, monitoring, and care. Looking ahead, the development of GEDRAs will require parallel innovation in both methodology and implementation. Beyond improving predictive accuracy, research must evaluate how reports are communicated, how they influence patient and provider decision-making, and how they can be adapted across diverse populations and health systems. With advances in AD/ADRD plasma biomarkers, lifestyle interventions, and disease-modifying therapies, now is a critical moment to invest in GEDRA as part of an integrated precision medicine framework. Doing so will ensure that risk information is not only accurate, personalized, and ethically delivered, but also actionable–heralding a new era for the clinical management of AD/ADRD.
