Response surface guided design of lipid-polymer hybrid nanoparticles for dual delivery of rosuvastatin and ezetimibe
Authors: Nadica Vanova Nakjinova, Maja Simonoska Crcarevska, Hrisanta Godzo, Katerina Brezovska, Nikola Jovanovikj, Lina Livrinska, Elena Stefova Mitrovska, Anita Grozdanov, Monika Stojanovska Pecova, Kristina Mladenovska
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFixed-dose formulations of rosuvastatin and ezetimibe loaded lipid-polymer hybrid nanoparticles were developed, employing a combination of polymer and lipid components designed to achieve a potential core-shell-like structure through nanoprecipitation method. For building structure, poly-(DL)-lactic-co-glycolic acid, acid-or ester-terminated, and soy phosphatidylcholine combined either with 1,2-dioleoyl-3-trimethylammonium-propane chloride or 1,2-distearoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphoethanolamine as lipids were used along with α-hydro-ω-hydroxypoly(oxyethylene)poly(oxypropylene)poly(oxyethylene) block copolymer and polyoxyethylene (20) sorbitan monooleate as stabilizers. The effects of independent variables (lipid quantity and type, polymer type and total drugs quantity) on the response variables (particle size, polydispersity index, zeta potential, total drug loading and rosuvastatin to ezetimibe ratio) were evaluated using response surface methodology by Design-Expert® software. Four optimal formulations were developed, with acid-terminated polymer and 1,2-dioleoyl-3-trimethylammonium-propane chloride, minimized particle size (229–240 nm) and polydispersity index (0.150–0.237), maximized total drugs loading (up to 7.44%) and neutral zeta potential (within ± 10 mV), having successfully loaded rosuvastatin and ezetimibe in ratio of 1, 1.5, 2 and 2.5, with potential for its modulation based on lipid to polymer ratio. Optimal formulations with suitable biopharmaceutical and physicochemical properties for oral administration were obtained, showing sustained drugs release over 36 h (in pH 6.8 and 7.4) and stable colloidal behavior over three-month storage. SEM revealed spherical morphology and size consistent with DLS measurements.FTIR, DSC and TGA confirmed nanoparticles stability and gave valuable insight into the drug-excipient interactions. XRPD analysis confirmed drugs encapsulation in an amorphous state. The optimized formulations may serve as a promising platform for future development in hyperlipidemia therapy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s11671-026-04553-2.Keywords: Lipid-polymer hybrid nanoparticles, Rosuvastatin, Ezetimibe, Fixed-dose combination

Introduction

Hyperlipidemia is at the root of the development and progression of the global primary causes of morbidity and mortality, i.e. cardiovascular diseases (CVDs). Reduction of low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) in hyperlipidemia is the main aim the drugs tend to achieve. It is estimated that with reduction of each mmol/L LDL-C the overall CVD risk is reduced by 22% [1]. Such reductions in CVD rates are often achieved through intensive treatment regimens with hydroxy-methyl-glutaryl coenzyme A (HMG-CoA) reductase inhibitors (i.e. statins). Given the well-documented side effects associated with high-intensity statin use [2], achieving these targets remains a significant challenge. Fixed-dose combinations (FDCs) of statin and other hypolipidemic drugs in hyperlipidemia might help in overcoming this challenge by offering a reduced risk of adverse reactions and greater efficacy relative to higher dose monotherapy, improved medication concordance due to reduced dosing and simplified regimens, and lower overall costs [3].

Clinical studies have demonstrated that the FDC of 10–40 mg rosuvastatin (ROS) and 10 mg ezetimibe (EZE) can lower LDL-C levels by up to 75%, irrespective of the baseline LDL-C levels, while maintaining a favorable safety profile across a wide range of patients with hypercholesterolemia, including those at high CVD risk or with established atherosclerotic CVD. Additional benefits such as improvement in carbohydrate metabolism were also observed [4–6], all owing to the complementary mechanisms of action that synergistically enhance LDL-C reduction, firstly, by inhibiting the cholesterol synthesis through inhibition of HMG-CoA reductase by ROS and secondly, by inhibiting intestinal cholesterol absorption through the primary target of EZE i.e. Nieman Pick C1 like 1 protein (NPC1L1). However, despite the improved benefit/risk ratio of this FDC, several obstacles still need to be overcome, indicating the need to target these drugs to the liver after oral administration. Namely, aside from its action on HMG-CoA reductase located primarily in the liver, ROS can also impair cholesterol synthesis in other tissues by interfering with additional metabolic pathways, leading to gastrointestinal disturbances, liver injury (elevated liver transaminases), renal failure, severe myopathy, elevated creatinine kinase of greater than ten times the upper limit of normal and rhabdomyolysis, and in some cases, neuropathies. Rare adverse effects might include diabetes mellitus, hematuria, proteinuria, and hypersensitivity [7–10]. On the other side, EZE undergoes extensive first pass metabolism in the liver, being metabolized to EZE-glucuronide, equally potent metabolite. It also undergoes extensive hepatic recirculation process with repeated delivery back to the intestines where it is reabsorbed, leading to prolonged cholesterol lowering activity [11–13]. Recently, it has been found that its target NPC1L1, localized at apical surface of the intestinal lumen, resides also in the canalicular membrane of hepatocytes, having a direct role in regulating biliary cholesterol excretion and hepatic/blood cholesterol levels [14].

These insights, along with the need of targeting ROS to HMG-CoA in the hepatocytes, draw to the main idea of the study, to encapsulate the FDC of ROS and EZE into the advanced delivery platform of biocompatible lipid-polymer hybrid nanoparticles (LPHNPs) for oral administration and liver targeting. Among many benefits, the core-shell resembling structure of these new generation delivery platforms offers possibility for (simultaneous) loading of both hydrophilic and lipophilic drugs. In addition, the polymeric core region enclosed by a lipid layer mimicking apolipoprotein structures provides high loading efficacy and sustained release of drug, enhancing stability and liver targeting of these delivery systems [15–17].

To the best of authors’ knowledge, there have been no reports on formulating LPHNPs containing a FDC of ROS and EZE so far. For building the hybrid structure in this study, poly-(DL)-lactic-co-glycolic acid (PLGA) as a polymeric part of the carrier was used, having in regard its excellent biocompatibility and biodegradability [18]. Given that the encapsulation efficiency and degradation rate (i.e. drugs release profile) can vary depending on the hydrophilicity of the PLGA type, specifically between ester- and acid-terminated forms, the potential of both types, with a fixed 50:50 lactic to glycolic acid ratio, was separately evaluated. Based on literature evidences [18–20], acid-terminated PLGA-COOH exhibits greater water uptake and earlier onset of bulk erosion than ester-terminated analogue of comparable Mw and lactide to glycolide ratio, providing a predictable, erosion-controlled release window (appropriate for small-molecule payloads), while avoiding excessively prolonged polymer residence. In contrast, ester-terminated PLGA, being more hydrophobic and slower-eroding, would be preferable if more extended release and weaker interfacial charge are desired. In addition, the amorphous PLGA matrix supports molecular dispersion of the drugs, limiting large crystalline domains and enabling reproducible loading and release. However, these inherent properties of PLGA can sometimes be a disadvantage, having in regard the possibility for rapid clearance by the immune system [that limits circulation time of PLGA nanoparticles (NPs)], lack of optimal control over dug release and prevention of specific interactions with the cells (affecting the drug bioavailability at the site of action). To overcome these limitations, surface engineering of PLGA-based NPs have long been a focus in drug delivery research, even prior to the emergence of LPHNPs as a distinct class of nanocarriers. Various materials have been used, such as polyethylene glycol and polyethyleneimine (for improving circulation time and enhancing water-soluble drug entrapment), chitosan (for providing a positive charge and a hydrophilic surface for mucoadhesivity and more prolonged and high cumulative drug release), and gelatin (for specific applications) [21–26].

Various lipids, ranging from synthetic lipids to natural cell membrane-derived vesicles, have been also employed for surface modification, depending on the surface chemistry, synthesis method, and intended biomedical application [27–29]. For forming the hybrid structure of the NPs in the current study, in addition to the PLGA polymer, soy phosphatidylcholine (lecithin) was used, combined either with 1,2-dioleoyl-3-trimethylammonium-propane chloride (DOTAP) as cationic lipid or 1,2-distearoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphoethanolamine (DSPE) as zwitterion phospholipid, all of them being biodegradable and used in other types of lipid containing nanocarriers for oral administration where their advantages compared to conventional therapy has been proven [30–34]. Lecithin incorporated into PLGA NPs for surface modification may improve their colloidal stability through hydrophobic interactions, control drug release, enable targeted delivery and improve NPs penetration while mimicking biological membranes [35]. DOTAP and DSPE may also improve drug delivery and targeting, DOTAP by inducing a positive surface charge and promoting interaction with negatively charged cell membranes, while DSPE by forming a more stable, biocompatible surface [36].

Literature data comparing biopharmaceutical and physicochemical properties of LPHNPs built of different PLGA types (acid-terminated vs. ester-terminated) in combination with either DOTAP or DSPE (cationic vs. neutral lipid) are scarce. This gap is especially evident in the context of FDC formulations involving drugs with different solubility such as ROS and EZE. To the best of our knowledge, beyond the commonly employed PLGA-DSPE pairing in the synthesis of LPHNPs, the specific combination of acid-terminated PLGA with the cationic lipid-DOTAP has not been previously reported or thoroughly examined, particularly not in the above-mentioned context. Taking all these into consideration, the current work was designed to fabricate and optimize ROS and EZE-loaded LPHNPs by using a combination of these lipids and types of PLGA, with particle size distribution and surface charge suitable for specific delivery to the liver and high drugs loading and ROS to EZE ratio simulating the ratio in the conventional formulations, as quality attributes (QAs).

Optimization was conducted with suitable design of experiments (DoE) using Design expert® software and response surface modeling (RSM). As a structured alternative to one-factor-at-a-time (OFAT) testing, DoE was used to enable simultaneous evaluation of dominant variables affecting QAs and their interactions, improve precision and efficiency in the optimization process, while with RSM, predictive models were developed to map responses and identify the optimal region (or “design space”) that meets the predefined targets for the QAs. In the subsequent phase, the model was validated by manufacturing the predicted optimal formulations, performing comprehensive physicochemical and biopharmaceutical characterization, and confirming that the experimental results fell within the confidence interval (CI) of the predicted responses with adequate prediction error, thereby contributing to a deeper understanding of these nanostructures as carriers of FDC drugs.

Beyond serving as a dual-drug carrier, the LPHNPs were designed to achieve a polymer-lipid association indicative of a core-shell-like arrangement. DoE-guided optimization of the polymer to lipid ratio and ROS to EZE ratio, coupled with a straightforward nanoprecipitation process, enabled reproducible modulation of physicochemical properties, release kinetics, and stability, supported by complementary characterization.

Materials and methods

Materials

For the preparation of the LPHNPs, PLGAs with a molar ratio of 50:50 and viscosities of 0.4 dL/g (ester-terminated) and 0.2 dL/g (acid-terminated) were kindly donated by Corbion (Netherlands). Lecithin, along with DSPE or DOTAP, intended for the formation of lipid fraction of the NPs, were kindly provided by Lipoid GmbH (Germany). α-Hydro-ω-hydroxypoly(oxyethylene)poly(oxypropylene)poly(oxyethylene)block copolymer (poloxamer 188) and polyoxyethylene (20) sorbitan monooleate (polysorbate 80), used as stabilisers, were purchased from BTC (Germany) and CRODA (England), respectively. Ethanol 96% (v/v), used in water phase during synthesis, was purchased from Merck (Germany). ROS as a calcium salt and EZE were kindly donated from DSM Sinochem Pharmaceuticals (India) and Hetero Labs Limited (India), accordingly. Acetonitrile (ACN), used as an organic solvent in the synthesis process and analytical procedure (a part of the mobile phase and diluent) and formic acid, utilised also as a part of the mobile phase in the analytical procedure, were purchased from Merck (Germany). Water (highly purified), used for particles preparation and analysis, was obtained using TKA-LAB Reinstwasser system (Niederelbert, Germany). All the chemicals used were of analytical grade.

Methods

Preparation of LPHNPs

ACN solution of both drugs EZE and ROS at a 1:1 (w/w) with 75 mg PLGA was prepared and added drop-wise at 0.5 mL/min (total volume of 10 mL) into the 4% (w/w) hydroethanolic lipid dispersion (total volume of 40 mL), pre-heated at 65 °C [above the lipid’s gel to liquid transition temperature (Tm)] [37–40], under continuous sonication using a titanium micro tip probe attached to an ultrasonic homogenizer (BioLogics 300 VT, BioLogics Inc., USA), operated with amplitude power of 30%, and temperature control of 40 ± 5 °C. Dispersions were then sonicated for additional 5 min at same temperature control and then left mixing on magnet stirrer (VARIO MAG Multipoint, USA) at speed of 350 rpm and ambient temperature, allowing self-assembly of NPs to occur, with subsequent ACN evaporation. Poloxamer 188, as a bi-functional block copolymer surfactant in 1:1 weight ratio to polymer (75:75 mg), and 60 mg polysorbate 80 were used as a LPHNPs stabilisers in the hydroethanolic phase. Separation of LPHNPs was performed through initial vacuum mechanical filtration on 2–3 μm pore size cellulose filter (for large or aggregated particles removal), with subsequent centrifugal ultrafiltration (13000 rpm, 10 min on MiniSpin® plus; Eppendorf, Germany) using 3 kDa cut-off filters (Amicon®, Merck, Germany) for removal of non-encapsulated drugs. The LPHNPs retained on 3 kDa cut-off filters were washed with highly purified water and subsequently dried to obtain them in powder form.

DoE and optimization of ROS and EZE-loaded LPHNPs formulations

To model the influence of independent variables on dependent responses, and afterwards to optimize the LPHNPs with FDC of ROS and EZE, Design-Expert® software (V8, trial, Stat-Ease Inc., Minneapolis, MN, USA) was used. A total of 40 experimental runs were generated using a central composite design, varying four independent variables at two levels [low (− 1) and high (+ 1)], with two replicates at the center points and one replicate at each factorial and axial point. Run order was fully randomized to minimize systematic bias and account for potential experimental variability. Four independent formulation variables were included: the quantity of lipid (A) and total drug content (B) as numeric variables, and the type of lipid (C) and type of PLGA (D) as categorical variables. The coded and actual values of the formulation variables are presented in Table 1, while the design matrix of experimental runs is presented in Table 2. No blocking was applied, as all experiments were performed under uniform laboratory conditions. Z-average particle size (PS), polydispersity index (PDI), zeta potential (ZP), total drug loading (DL) and ROS to EZE ratio were followed as dependent variables.

Design matrix of experimental runs with actual values of independent variables

Based on the results from the DoE and established mathematical models, subsequent multi-response optimization was carried out using the numerical optimization tool in Design-Expert®. It was conducted using desirability functions to identify formulations that best satisfied predefined criteria. The optimization goals were defined as follows: the ROS to EZE ratio was set as the target parameter (evaluated at 1, 1.5, 2, and 2.5 in separate runs; importance = 5), PS and PDI were minimized (importance = 3), total DL was maximized (importance = 3), and ZP was maintained within ± 10 mV (importance = 3). The desirability “weight” (shape) parameter was kept at the default value of 1 for all responses, while the “importance” parameter (scale 1–5) was used to prioritize the ROS to EZE ratio as a target over the secondary responses. The same set of constraints and importance levels were applied across all optimization runs, with only the ROS to EZE target ratio value varied between them. Predicted optimal LPHNPs formulations were then prepared by the previously described nanoprecipitation method and subsequently characterized for their physicochemical and biopharmaceutical properties using the methodologies detailed below.

Physicochemical characterization of formulations of ROS and EZE-loaded LPHNPs

a. Particle size, polydispersity index and zeta potential

a. Particle size, polydispersity index and zeta potential

PS, PDI and ZP were determined by dynamic light scattering (DLS) method using a Zetasizer Nano-ZS100 (Malvern Instruments, UK) at 25 °C on wet dispersions. Wet dispersions were prepared by dispersing the dried LPHNPs (~ 0.3 mg) for 1 min in 1.5 mL phosphate buffer saline (PBS) with low molarity 0.0001 M (рH 7.4), using titanium micro tip probe attached to an ultrasonic homogenizer (BioLogics 300 VT, BioLogics Inc., USA) and amplitude power control of 30%. Aliquots of the samples were transferred to disposable folded capillary cells and the measurements were performed with temperature stabilization time of 120 s. Medium had a viscosity of 0.8894 cP, a scattering angle of 173 ° and a dielectric constant of 78.5 (at 25 °C). Measurements were performed in triplicate and each measurement comprised multiple automatically optimized sub-runs to ensure accuracy and reproducibility. Results are reported as mean ± SD (n = 3).

b. Encapsulation efficacy, drug loading

b. Encapsulation efficacy, drug loading

Encapsulation efficiency (EE) and drug loading (DL) expressed in %, were determined as described below (Eq. 1 and Eq. 2). Non-encapsulated drugs were quantified in the filtrate obtained after centrifugal ultrafiltration using 3 kDa cut-off filters, employing a validated and purpose-developed RP-HPLC method [41].

For assessing the formulation’s ability to incorporate a drug, the DoE was primarily focused on optimizing the total DL, expressed as a sum of DL values of both drugs. Although not included as a response variable in the original design matrix, EE was also measured for each drug to assess process efficiency. Each measurement was performed in triplicate and presented as mean value (%) ± SD.

c. ROS to EZE ratio

c. ROS to EZE ratio

ROS to EZE ratio was included as a response in the DoE and further as a primary optimization metric due to its relevance to dose strength and pharmacological balance. ROS to EZE ratio was calculated in triplicate (as described in Eq. 3) using each of the triplicate measurements of DL and is presented as mean value (%) ± SD.

d. Morphological characterization

d. Morphological characterization

Morphological characterization was performed on optimal ROS and EZE-loaded LPHNPs formulations using scanning electron microscopy (SEM), with acceleration voltage of 10 kV on JSM-IT200 (JEOL Ltd., Japan). The samples were prepared out of fraction of LPHNPs retained on 3 kDa cut-off filters, washed three cycles with highly purified water, then re-suspended and air-dried on glass plates at ambient temperature (~ 1.5 mg particles). Small fractions of dried samples of optimal drugs-loaded LPHNPs were set on adhesive carbon tape placed on stub and then sputter-coated using gold for better conductivity.

e. Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR)

e. Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR)

For identification of interactions between the ingredients in the formulation and stability of the drugs during preparation procedure, FTIR was used. FTIR spectra of all raw pharmaceutical ingredients, optimal LPHNPs formulations, placebo LPHNPs and physical mixture of all formulation ingredients prepared by dry mixing, were recorded in the region 4000–400 cm− 1 using ATR module with diamond crystal and pressure tower, on Nicolet iS50 FTIR spectrometer (Thermo Scientific, USA). An amount of 2–5 mg was used per sample.

f. Differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) and thermogravimetric analysis (TGA)

f. Differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) and thermogravimetric analysis (TGA)

DSC and TGA were used to gain valuable insight into the thermal behavior and thermal robustness of the formulations and possible ingredients’ interactions. DSC was performed on instrument Netzsch DSC 204 F1 Phoenix (Germany) and TGA on Netzsch TG 209 F1 Iris (Germany). Measurements were performed on each individual formulation ingredient, physical mixture of all formulation ingredients, placebo LPHNPs and optimal ROS and EZE-loaded LPHNPs. An amount of 2–5 mg was used per sample, and the analyses were conducted under a dynamic nitrogen flow of 30 mL/min, with a scanning rate of 10 °C/min, over a temperature range of 25–250 °C.

g. X-Ray powder diffraction (XRPD) analysis

g. X-Ray powder diffraction (XRPD) analysis

To identify the nature of the solid state of ROS and EZE in optimal formulations of LPHNPs, XRPD analysis was performed to compare the crystalline structure of the pure drugs with that of the drugs-loaded LPHNPs and placebo LPHNPs, based on the 2θ values of their characteristic peaks. Flat-plate geometry method on Rigaku MiniFlex 600 C (Japan) was used, equipped with D/teX Ultra2 high-speed detector, using the following set parameters: CuKα radiation at 40 kV/15 mA, scan rate of 4 °/min in the 3–50 ° (2θ) range.

Biopharmaceutical characterization of ROS and EZE-loaded LPHNPs formulations

a. In vitro release studies and kinetic modeling

a. In vitro release studies and kinetic modeling

To rank the formulations in respect to drugs release kinetics, the release of ROS and EZE from the optimal LPHNPs was followed over a 36-hour period in vitro, in PBSs having pH 6.8 as regulatory accepted medium [42] and 7.4 (to simulate the intercellular pH of hepatocytes [43]), using the most commonly employed dialysis method [44–46]. The dispersions of ROS and EZE-loaded LPHNPs in PBSs (5 mg/mL) containing 0.3% (w/w) polysorbate 80 were placed in membrane bags (Membra-cel® dialysis tubing, MWCO 7000 regenerated cellulose, diameter 22 mm, Serva, Germany). Polysorbate 80 was added for maintaining sink conditions due to the poor solubility of the drugs in aqueous media, especially of EZE [47]. Membrane bags with dispersions of LPHNPs (3 mL) were placed in glass vessels containing 140 mL of the same buffer media The in vitro release experiments were executed under sink conditions (which according to European Pharmacopeia are met when the dissolution medium volume is at least three times the volume needed to dissolve the drugs dose at saturation), in shaking water bath (Haake SWB 20, Fisher Scientific, Germany) at 37 ± 0.5 °C using 100 rpm. Aliquots of 2 mL were withdrawn at predetermined time intervals (1, 6, 12, 24, and 36 h) and an equal volume of fresh buffer was added immediately to maintain a constant volume throughout the study. Quantification of ROS and EZE from each sampling point was performed using the same validated RP-HPLC method mentioned above [41]. The amount of ROS and EZE released over time was expressed as cumulative percentage of the initial amount reported as mean (%) ± SD from three independent measurements (n = 3). All in vitro drug release data were integrated into different kinetic equations to elucidate the mechanisms of drug release from each optimal formulation at each pH. The release data of each drug both from pH 6.8 and 7.4 were fitted to zero-order (Eq. 4), first-order (Eq. 5), Higuchi (Eq. 6), Hixon-Crowell (Eq. 7), Korsmeyer-Peppas (Eq. 8) and Peppas-Sahlin model (Eq. 9). The best suited model was found by evaluating the R2 values, where ideal correlation with a specific model corresponds to R2 = 1 [48, 49]. Modeling of the dissolution data was performed using the DDsolver (Microsoft Excel add-in program, version 1).

Hold-time stability assessment of optimal formulations of ROS and EZE-loaded LPHNPs

To obtain an information about consistency and potential tendency of optimal ROS and EZE-loaded LPHNPs formulations for aggregation, sedimentation or precipitation upon storage and handling, with an aim to anticipate the formulation’s behavior in biological compartments post-administration and conditions between synthesis and subsequent formulation processing steps, an adequate hold-time study was performed. Optimal formulations stored as refrigerated dispersions in highly purified water were kept at 2–8 °C for a period of 3 months in sealed borosilicate glass vials, leaving one-third of the total vial volume left as air headspace. Afterwards, the LPHNPs with ROS and EZE were separated on 3 kDa cut-off filters by centrifugal ultrafiltration (13000 rpm, 10 min on MiniSpin® plus; Eppendorf, Germany) and dried, and the colloidal stability indicating parameters in respect to initial values were determined. PS, PDI and ZP were determined using the same method as described above, while the total DL and ROS to EZE ratio were calculated through the quantification of the non-encapsulated (i.e. leaked) drugs in the filtrate obtained after separation of the NPs. All analyses were carried out on samples withdrawn after three months, each analysis was performed in triplicate (n = 3) and it is presented with the corresponding mean ± (SD) values.

The predicted residual sum of squares (PRESS) values were consistent with the magnitude of each response and together with the corresponding predicted R2 (Q2) values, indicate that the models possess acceptable predictive ability for formulation screening and design space exploration. Although models exhibited modest R2 values (< 0.5), their adjusted and predicted R2 values were in reasonable agreement (ΔR2 < 0.2), suggesting no substantial overfitting and adequate predictive reliability for exploratory optimization. Furthermore, the lack-of-fit test was not significant for all responses except ZP, confirming that the fitted models appropriately describe the data within experimental error. The significant lack of fit observed for ZP is most likely attributable to unmodeled curvature or inherent experimental variability rather than systematic bias. Adequate precision values exceeded the threshold of 4 for all responses, indicating sufficient signal-to-noise ratios and confirming that the models can be reliably used to navigate the design space. Overall, these statistical and diagnostic indicators demonstrate that the response surface models are valid and suitable for interpreting variable’s effects and guiding formulation optimization.

Effect of formulation variables on PS and PDI

The series of LPHNPs loaded with ROS and EZE generated from the DoE resulted in values of PS within a range of 136.06 ± 3.59 to 581.33 ± 65.91 nm and PDI from 0.080 ± 0.02 to 0.523 ± 0.04 (see Table S1 in Supplementary Information). The impact of examined variables on PS considering the actual values of the factors is outlined by Eq. 10 (for LPHNPs prepared with DOTAP) and Eq. 11 (for LPHNPs prepared with DSPE).

When analyzing the association of the investigated independent variables with the PS using one-way ANOVA, the interactions of AC and BC (where A is lipid quantity, B is total drugs quantity and C is type of lipid) proved as statistically significant (p < 0.05). When DOTAP as a lipid type was used, at constant drugs quantity and PLGA type, the LPHNPs manifested a decrease in PS over tested range of lipid quantity. In contrast, when DSPE was used, an increase in its quantity led to a corresponding increase in PS, as shown in Fig. 1.

Considering the association of the investigated independent variables with the PDI, the type of the lipid (C) was statistically significant independent variable that affected the PS distribution (p < 0.05). The slight differences between the coefficients (Eq. 13 and Eq. 15 compared to Eq. 12 and Eq. 14) indicate that larger PDI values (related to wider PS distribution) are associated with usage of DSPE as a type of lipid, with even larger increase of PDI when PLGA was ester terminated. The relatively smaller PS and lower PDI observed with DOTAP-modified formulations are probably attributed not only to the favorable interaction of DOTAP with PLGA and shorter hydrocarbon chain length compared to DSPE, but also to DOTAP-induced alterations within the lipid fraction of the NPs. These changes likely enhance electrostatic interactions in the polar head region, promoting tighter packing and reducing the bilayer curvature radius, which in turn contributes to smaller PS [50]. The latter results in reduced impact on PDI i.e. narrower size distribution of LPHNPs, irrespective of the increase in drugs load and lipid quantity, as illustrated at Fig. 2. Overall, it can be said that the cationic lipid over the tested range provided better control on LPHNPs size distribution, increasing their robustness.

Effect of formulation variables on ZP

Series of drugs-loaded LPHNPs derived from the DoE experiments had a ZP between − 20.23 ± 3.39 mV and 29.83 ± 0.98 mV, with most of the formulations being within the range |10 mV| (see Table S1 in Supplementary Information). The model that described the effect of formulation variable on ZP (see Table 3) indicated that the variables A, C, interaction AC and A2 have statistically significant influence on ZP (p < 0.05). The impact of examined variables on ZP of LPHNPs considering actual values when DOTAP was used as a type of lipid is delineated by Eq. 16 and in case of DSPE by Eq. 17.

Based on the coefficients value, both lipids at low quantities led to formation of drugs-loaded LPHNPs with lower or more negative ZP. DSPE showed more pronounced impact, leading to formation of LPHNPs with lower ZP values at lower tested quantities compared to DOTAP. However, with increase in the quantity of both lipids, increase in ZP (to more positive values) over the tested range was observed, with non-linear association, as showed at Fig. 3, where the 3D plot illustrates the U-shaped quadratic response of ZP to the effect of total drugs and lipid quantity for drugs-loaded LPHNPs formed of combination of DOTAP/PLGA-acid terminated.

Effect of total drugs and lipid quantity on ZP

The observed influence of lipid, both of its type and quantity on ZP might indicate that the lipid fraction is probably positioned as a layer near the surface of the LPHNPs, where it interacts at the interface and modulates the particles’ behavior. The intrinsic charge properties of both DOTAP and DSPE (cationic vs. neutral/anionic) further explain the curvature and ZP shifts, where although showing similar trend, DOTAP provides more robust effect on ZP within the tested quantity range.

Effect of formulation variables on DL

To better reflect the overall loading capacity of the synthesized LPHNPs, DL in this study is reported as total DL representing the combined quantity of both drugs. The DL in the series of drugs-loaded LPHNPs generated by the DoE ranged from 1.47 ± 0.20% to 13.53 ± 0.80% (see Table S1 in Supplementary Information). As shown in Table 3, the effect of formulation variables on total DL as a dependent variable was best described by the RSM reduced quadratic model, in which total drugs quantity (B), type of lipid (C) and B2 had statistically significant influence on this response (p < 0.05). The impact of the examined variables on DL, in terms of actual values, for the LPHNPs formulations based on combination of DOTAP/PLGA-acid terminated, DSPE/PLGA-acid terminated, DOTAP/PLGA-ester terminated and DSPE/PLGA-ester is delineated by the Eqs. 18, 19, 20 and 21, respectively.

The effect of total drugs and lipid quantity on DL of LPHNPs formulations containing combination of DOTAP/PLGA-acid terminated is presented in Fig. 4, where it can be seen that with increase in DOTAP quantity, the total DL increases, irrespectively of the type of PLGA used. However, as 3D contour plot shows also, the increase of the total DL followed the increase in the lipid quantity to certain point, above which it decreased. Having the equations coefficients in regard, one can see that the increase in total DL was lower in formulations based on DSPE compared to DOTAP, especially when the LPHNPs were formed with PLGA-acid terminated (Eq. 19) as a polymer type. The observed effect of DSPE is probably due to the weaker electrostatic interactions with the polymer, often crucial for obtaining high DL [51].

Effect of total drugs and lipid quantity on DL

Although not included as response in the DoE, the EEs of ROS and EZE were also separately determined. The minimum and maximum EE for ROS in the experimental formulations of drugs-loaded LPHNPs was 8.18% and 85.68% (run 12 and 32, respectively), while for EZE, minimum EE of 14.62% (run 17) and maximum EE of 95.40% (run 14) was obtained (see Table S1 in Supplementary Information). EE for ROS increased with increase in the lipid quantity, with the effect being more pronounced when DOTAP was used as a part of the lipid fraction. The trend was linear up to a certain point after which further increase in total DL or total drug quantity added (i.e. quantity of ROS) was followed by a reduction in its EE. For EZE, lipid type and quantity [in combination with total drug (i.e. EZE) quantity increase] produced only a modest increase in its EE, compared to ROS, with no clear decrease observed in the tested range.

Effect of formulation variables on ROS to EZE ratio

In the current study, the ROS to EZE ratio was evaluated as a dependent variable, since variations in the formulation are expected to alter the encapsulation of both drugs and their localization within the LPHNPs structure, further affecting their release profile. In the series of drugs-loaded LPHNPs derived by the DoE, a ROS to EZE ratio within 0.3 ± 0.02 and 3.5 ± 0.00 was obtained (see Table S1 in Supplementary Information), depending linearly on the total drugs and lipid quantity, as shown in Fig. 5 where this dependence for the LPHNPs formulations formed of combination of DOTAP/PLGA-acid terminated is presented.

Effect of total drugs and lipid quantity on ROS to EZE ratio

Total drugs quantity (B) was statistically significant variable (p < 0.05). Its impact on the ROS to EZE ratio in terms of actual values is expressed by Eq. 22 for the formulations of drugs-loaded LPHNPs formed of DOTAP/PLGA-acid terminated, while for the LPHNPs formulations based on DSPE/PLGA-acid terminated, DOTAP/PLGA-ester terminated and DSPE/PLGA-ester terminated, the same is delineated by Eqs. 23, 24 and 25, respectively. As Eq. 23 shows, the effect of DOTAP on the ROS to EZE ratio was slightly more prominent, especially when it was combined with PLGA-ester terminated as a polymer.

The predicted and experimentally obtained results (averaged of three samples) showed good agreement, falling well within the 95% CI of the mean values. Surface morphology

In Fig. 6 are presented the SEM images of the optimized formulations of ROS and EZE-loaded LPHNPs, revealing a “cluster of grapes” like morphology, with uniform and spherical shape of the closely distributed particles within the clusters. The measured size on the particles arranged within the clusters was in good correlation with the hydrodynamic diameter (PS) measured by DLS method. Similar clustering structures have been reported in the literature and often attributed to stabilizers detachment with drying and ionic-related clustering [52, 53].

Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy

Depicted ATR-FTIR spectra of raw materials and drugs, optimal ROS and EZE-loaded LPHNPs, three placebo formulations (one prepared for the optimal formulations 1 and 2 and two separate ones for the formulations 3 and 4) and a physical mixture of the excipients with both drugs are shown in Fig. 7 (formulation 2) and in Figs. S34–S36 in the Supplementary Information for other three formulations.

FTIR spectra of ROS, EZE, physical mixture, optimal drugs-loaded and placebo LPHNPs (formulation 2)

As shown in Fig. 7 and also in Figs. S34–S36 in the Supplementary Information, in the spectra of the LPHNPs loaded with ROS and EZE, several low-intensity bands were observed in the 1500–1650 cm⁻¹ region, originating from both drugs. In the spectra of EZE, a band at 1511 cm⁻¹ can be ascribed to C = C stretching in the benzene ring [47], while in the spectra of ROS, the band observed at 1551 cm⁻¹ depicted to the stretching of C = O group [54]. The remaining vibrational modes in the spectra of the drugs and physical mixture (i.e. bands at 3266.51 cm⁻¹ for O-H stretching, 1359.84 cm⁻¹ for C-F stretching, 1102.34 cm⁻¹ for C-N stretching, 1727.28 cm⁻¹ for C = O stretching from β-lactam ring, and 2916.42 cm⁻¹ for C-H stretching in case of EZE and characteristic broad band at 3372 cm⁻¹ attributed to carboxylic OH group and bands at 1381 cm⁻¹ and 1153 cm⁻¹ related to the asymmetric and symmetric stretching of sulfoxide group in case of ROS) are mostly overlapped by the particle matrix itself, which can be probably ascribed to the positioning of the drugs within the interior of the particles, indicative for effective encapsulation and positioning of ROS and EZE within the particles, as concluded in other studies were attenuation/disappearance of characteristic drug bands in loaded NPs or weak visibility of the specific bands compared to the pure drugs was evident [55, 56].

When comparing the spectra of optimal formulations of ROS and EZE-loaded LPHNPs with the spectrum of physical mixture of all ingredients (see Fig. 7 and Figs. S34–S36 in the Supplementary Information), one can see the difference in the region 3400 –3200 cm⁻¹. Sharper and more intense peak in the spectrum of the physical mixture, specific for O-H and N-H bonds, is not visible in the spectra of optimal formulations of drugs-loaded LPHNPs, indicating possible formation of new hydrogen bonds in drugs-loaded LPHNPs. Those hydrogen bonds involve amino (-NH2) or hydroxyl (-OH) groups, which results in less free (-NH2) or hydroxyl (-OH) groups in the LPHNPs. Such interactions (hydrogen bonding of type N-H···O or O-H···N) were expected to occur during synthesis of the drugs-loaded LPHNPs. Although less likely, the observed broadening in this region may also result from ionic interactions involving acidic and basic groups present in the components (e.g. carboxylic acids and amines). During synthesis, proton transfer can lead to ionization of these groups, which may contribute to the extension or broadening of vibrational modes in this region.

Nevertheless, the most dominant vibrational modes in the spectra of optimal formulations of drugs-loaded and placebo LPHNPs (see Fig. 8) arise from the lipid-DOTAP and poloxamer 188, particularly in the following regions: around 1700 cm⁻¹ (attributed to carbonyl stretching, predominantly from the lipid layer), 2800–3000 cm⁻¹ and 950–970 cm⁻¹ corresponding to the trimethylammonium group, indicating the presence of DOTAP [57]. The slight shift and intensity in the region 1700 –1500 cm⁻¹ (C = O stretching/amide region) in the spectra of LPHNPs suggest ester or amide interactions with possible occurrence of chemical or strong hydrogen bonding between the lipid and polymer within the NPs structure. In addition, the presence of a strong vibrational mode in the 1000–1100 cm⁻¹ region in all optimal formulations can be attributed to the C-O-C stretching of the ester bonds in the polyoxyethylene (PEO) and polyoxypropylene (PPO) blocks of poloxamer 188, consistent with its IR spectrum characterization in earlier studies [58]. This could indicate that poloxamer 188 resides on the surface of the particles, where it likely acts as a stabilizing agent. One can also see that non-specific O-H (~ 3400 cm⁻¹) and C-O-C (1100–1200 cm⁻¹) stretching vibrations appear in all optimal formulations. The C = O stretching peak at ~ 1735 cm⁻¹, characteristic for stretching vibrations of ester bonds in polysorbate 80, and the bands near 1700 cm⁻¹ and within 1000–1200 cm⁻¹, attributed to carbonyl and phosphate groups present in lecithin, also appear in the spectra of formulations. It could indicate that besides poloxamer 188 and DOTAP, both polysorbate 80 and lecithin are present on the particle surface, as part of the layered lipid fraction.

FTIR spectra of optimal drugs-loaded LPHNPs formulation 2, placebo and individual excipients

Previous studies have reported coating and stabilization of PLGA NPs using similar surfactants/stabilizers [59–61], and similar stabilizers have been also applied for modification of lipid-containing NPs [62, 63]. While polymer-core/lipid-shell arrangements have been suggested in LPHNPs of similar composition prepared by nanoprecipitation, based on indirect evidence such as PS and surface composition depiction by FTIR and TEM [34, 64–66], direct structural confirmation was not obtained in the present study. Our FTIR and additional characterization data suggest interactions consistent with such an architecture, but the internal structure of the LPHNPs should be considered proposed rather than confirmed. Techniques like small-angle X-ray, small-angle neutron scattering, confocal microscopy or cryo-TEM can be further applied to confirm the characterization.

The thermograms of the physical mixture, placebo and drugs-loaded LPHNPs were similar, with one sharp endothermic peak at 53.3 °C, 49.1 °C and 48 °C in the thermograms of placebo LPHNPs F1-3, placebo LPHNPs F4 and the physical mixture, respectively, while in the DSC thermograms of optimal formulations of drugs-loaded LPHNPs (1, 2, 3 and 4), endothermic peaks were visible at 52.5 °C, 52.3 °C, 53.3 °C and 52.6 °C, respectively. The similar thermal behavior of optimal LPHNPs formulations was expected, having in consideration that same quantities of lecithin and PLGA were used for their preparation, being higher than the quantity of DOTAP, which was added in different quantities during preparation of the optimal formulations. Shifted endothermic peaks to higher temperature observed in placebo and drugs-loaded LPHNPs thermograms compared to the thermogram of the physical mixture and the non-significant difference between DSC thermograms of placebo and drugs-loaded LPHNPs, suggest that the NPs’ matrix contributes to the thermal stabilization. This indicates that the synthesis process, likely enhances the stability of the ROS and EZE-loaded LPHNPs.

The physical mixture exhibited earliest degradation onset, broad mass-loss profile (a main DTG peak within 230–235 °C) and greatest cumulative loss within the tested range, indicative of the lowest thermal robustness. ROS and EZE-loaded LPHNPs formulation 1 proved as the most thermally robust among the optimal formulations exhibiting slowest mass-loss rate and later transition temperatures.

X-Ray Powder Diffraction analysis (XRPD)

XRPD patterns of each optimal formulation of ROS and EZE-loaded LPHNPs compared to the pure ROS and EZE and placebo LPHNPs (one prepared for the optimal formulations 1 and 2 and two separate ones for the formulations 3 and 4) are shown in Fig. 14. EZE used in our formulations complies mostly with crystalline form B, which is characterized by 2θ at 18.7, 19.5, 23.0, 23.5 and 24.6 ° and additionally at 8.0, 15.9, 20.7, 21.9 and 25.5 ° [69]. ROS incorporated into the formulations was primarily amorphous, with minor crystalline remnants detectable at 6.7, 10.6, 17.4, 20.1, and 23.5 °. The difractograms of the placebo LPHNPs and drugs-loaded LPHNPs were similar, confirming an amorphous matrix. Compared to the corresponding placebo, the drugs-loaded LPHNPs show a small shift of the amorphous halo and a subtle change in its breadth. Such changes can be attributable to minor alterations in the polymer/lipid matrix upon co-encapsulation of ROS and EZE. Absence of distinct diffraction peaks in the difractograms of drugs-loaded LPHNPs indicate that the drugs are incorporated in either a molecular dispersed or amorphous state. In addition, one can suppose that the incorporation of the drugs did not change the inherent amorphous nature of the LPHNPs. These findings are consistent with the DSC analysis, which showed no endothermic peaks at the characteristic melting points of the crystalline drugs (165.1 °C for EZE and 226.1 °C for ROS, see Fig. S37 in Supplementary Information), being similar to literature reported values [70, 71], indicating successful amorphization in all optimal drugs-loaded LPHNP formulations.

In vitro release profile and kinetic modeling

Optimal LPHNPs were compared regarding the cumulative release over time of ROS and EZE at pH 6.8 and 7.4. The cumulative release results are presented in Fig. 15. All optimal formulations showed prolonged release over the tested 36 h in both media. The cumulative ROS release within the predefined time frame was slower in the medium with pH 7.4, ranging from 51.62% (optimal formulation 4) to 87.11% (optimal formulation 3) in pH 6.8 and from 29.20% (optimal formulation 2) to 49.87% (optimal formulation 1) in pH 7.4. For EZE, relatively slower release was observed in comparison to ROS and similar in both tested media, ranging from 27.34% (optimal formulation 4) to 84.47% (optimal formulation 2) in pH 6.8 and from 17.85% (optimal formulation 4) to complete release from optimal formulation 2 in pH 7.4. The generally slower release of EZE is probably attributed to its lower solubility and lipophilic nature or its deeper entrapment in the NPs [47].

Cumulative drugs release (%) from optimal ROS and EZE-loaded LPHNPs formulations (n = 3, mean (%) ± SD; SD bars not visible where they fall within the symbol)

When comparing the drug release profiles of all optimal formulations in both tested media, formulation 4 consistently showed the slowest release of both drugs, having ROS to EZE ratio of 2.5 and prepared from highest DOTAP quantity. This finding points to the importance of the lipid fraction not only for the drugs entrapment efficacy, but also for their release from the LPHNPs. Namely, the commonly reported phenomenon of initial burst release from NPs within the first few hours was not observed in the optimal formulations of drugs-loaded LPHNPs, which showed significant release after 12th hour, with relatively sustained release afterwards, indicative for successful drugs entrapment within the structure of the LPHNPs.

Various kinetic models were applied to the in vitro dissolution patterns of ROS and EZE from the optimal LPHNPs (see Table S3, Supplementary Information) and best fitted models with proposed release mechanisms for each drug from each formulation at each pH are presented in Table 5. ROS exhibited a consistent and predictable release profile across all formulations, with best fits observed for the Higuchi, Korsmeyer-Peppas, and mostly Peppas-Sahlin models (R2 > 0.96) in both pH media (see Figs. S44–S47 in the Supplementary Information), indicating a primarily diffusion-controlled release mechanism with Fickian (k1 > k2, k2 often negative or small and n values around 0.4–0.6) or slightly anomalous (combined mechanism) characteristics. In contrast, EZE displayed more variable release, with generally weaker fits to zero and first-order models, particularly at pH 6.8. Nonetheless, the Peppas-Sahlin model provided the best overall description of EZE release (see Figs. S48–S51 in the Supplementary Information) highlighting the involvement of both Fickian diffusion and possible polymer relaxation or erosion processes (k2 values vary in sign and magnitude) [72, 73].

Discussion

Starting from the premise that LPHNP-based systems represent a promising platform for advancing oral drug delivery and improving access to more effective and patient-friendly treatment options, in the current study this platform was used for development of FDC of the two hypolipidemic drugs ROS and EZE. For the synthesis of the ROS and EZE-loaded LPHNPs, the one-step nanoprecipitation method was used, proven as a suitable, efficient, and reproducible, and being recognized also by researchers in the field as amendable to scale-up [74, 75]. It is widely regarded as the “gold standard” for NPs synthesis, often yielding small, narrowly distributed particles with high DL and good physicochemical and biopharmaceutical properties [76, 77]. These properties are largely influenced by the solubility characteristics of the encapsulated drugs and by the selection of lipid and polymer components, as evidenced by the results of this study also. PLGA (as ester and acid-terminated) was employed in the current study as a biodegradable polymer, providing structural integrity and enabling controlled drug release. To enhance interactions with negatively charged mucosal surfaces, the NPs were modified with lecithin in combination with either the cationic lipid DOTAP or the zwitterion/neutral lipid DSPE as part of the lipid fraction, expecting that these structural modifications and the respective combination will improve mucoadhesion and facilitate cellular uptake, ultimately enhancing the oral bioavailability of the drugs.

For optimization of the ROS and EZE-loaded LPHNPs formulation, sequential experimental strategy was successfully applied, which effectively minimized the risk of working within a limited or poorly prioritized design space. Initially, OFAT experiments were performed that helped in identifying the critical formulation parameters and defining their operational ranges. Afterwards, the effects of critical formulation variables i.e. type of PLGA (ester- and acid-terminated), quantity and type of lipids (DOTAP and DSPE), and total drugs quantity (ROS and EZE) on the key biopharmaceutical and physicochemical characteristics of the LPHNPs for controlled and targeted drugs delivery after oral administration were evaluated, investigating their relationships and identifying statistically significant interactions among the key formulation factors. OFAT experiments supported the optimization of other supplementary parameters not included in the final DoE as well, such as the organic-to-aqueous volumetric phase ratio and the quantity of lecithin, polysorbate 80 [78–80] and of poloxamer 188 as well [data not published]. Amplitude power control of 30% was used to avoid excessive heating and foaming, which was observed when the power was closer to the limit (50%). The amplitude power, drip rate and overall time of sonication were fixed such as to obtain best balance between the droplet breakup influencing PS and PDI, solvent evaporation rate and the thermal control. During nanoprecipitation, the hydroethanolic phase was maintained above each lipid’s Tm to ensure a fluid liquid-crystalline state. Operating above Tm enhances lateral lipid mobility and interfacial adsorption, allowing lipids to rapidly self-assemble around the precipitating polymer core. This reduces coating defects, aggregation, and polydispersity, resulting in reproducible LPHNPs. This approach is supported by several studies [37–40] in which for preparation of LPHNPs by nanoprecipitation, the lipid-aqueous phase was kept above the Tm to obtain a homogeneous liquid-crystalline phase and to promote lipid-polymer self-assembly, potentially in a core-shell structure. Maintaining the temperature above the lipid phase-transition point was recommended as a standard practice for achieving fluid bilayers with higher mobility and permeability, which is directly relevant to the formation of LPHNPs containing lipid shell layer.

Through description with adequate mathematical models, the DoE provided valuable insights into the complexity of the prepared LPHNPs formulations and facilitated the identification of the variables’ values that would yield formulations with desired or optimal therapeutic effect. When the desired individual characteristics were combined to obtain formulations with specific ROS to EZE ratio (1, 1.5, 2 and 2.5) and maximized DL, minimized PS and PDI, and ZP within |10 mV|, four optimal formulations of ROS and EZE-loaded LPHNPs composed of PLGA acid-terminated as a polymer fraction, and DOTAP and lecithin as a lipid fraction along with stabilizers polysorbate 80 and poloxamer 188 were selected, all showing sustained drugs release.

PS was selected as a QA for oral delivery of NPs. Studies investigating the effect of NPs size on intestinal cellular uptake and transport have consistently shown that smaller particles, with size below 300 nm, are ideal for oral drug delivery since both enterocytes and M cells preferentially internalize them, leading to efficient intestinal transport [81, 82]. Compared to a previous study that reported larger PS (~ 900 nm) for polymeric NPs loaded with the same FDC of ROS and EZE [83], the analysis of the optimized formulations of LPHNPs developed in the current study revealed significantly smaller particles and narrower PS distribution, with PS ranging from 229.33 ± 15.15 to 240.23 ± 5.76 nm and PDI from 0.150 ± 0.04 to 0.237 ± 0.01. The significant formulation variables influencing PS were shown to be the total drugs quantity and lipid type as well as interaction between the lipid type and quantity, while for the PDI the lipid type was significant (p < 0.05). The relatively smaller PS and lower PDI observed with DOTAP-modified formulations can be attributed not only to its favorable interaction with PLGA and shorter hydrocarbon chain length compared to DSPE, but also to DOTAP-induced alterations within the lipid fraction. These changes likely enhance electrostatic interactions in the polar head region, promoting tighter packing and reducing the lipid/surfactants layer curvature radius, which in turn contributes to smaller PS [50, 84, 85].

ZP was also considered as a QA of the formulations and important from two aspects, targeting drugs delivery to the liver after oral administration and ensuring colloidal stability, having in regard that one of the key identifiers of physically stable NPs is their repulsion degree [86]. However, in interpreting the ZP values, not only repulsion degree (reflecting the prevention of particles aggregation and depending mainly on the electrostatic, steric, entropic or Van der Waals forces), but also the complementary role of steric effects, particularly those mediated by surfactants, should be considered [87–89]. Considering the literature data, although NPs with ZP values within ± 30 mV are generally considered stable [90, 91], for the specific purpose of hepatocellular uptake, particles with relatively neutral ZP values within ± 10 mV are considered even more suitable [92]. This is supported by the evidence indicating that positively charged NPs tend to preferentially accumulate in the kidneys, whereas neutral or negatively charged are more likely to be localized in the liver [93]. Additionally, there are reports showing that NPs with ZP within ± 20 mV still maintain physical stability, primarily due to the presence of adsorbed non-ionic surfactants that provide steric stabilization. Taking this into consideration, the ZP was targeted at ± 10 mV, indicating an approach designed to balance liver-targeted delivery and steric stabilization. At physiological pH (7.4), near-neutral NPs are expected to permeate the intestinal mucus barrier more effectively than highly charged NPs, prolonging circulation time, and enhancing hepatocyte accumulation [94, 95]. Near-neutral charge is also advantageous for oral delivery, as it reduces nonspecific mucoadhesion, minimizes interactions with bile salts and proteins, mitigates cationic-lipid-related cytotoxicity, and improves colloidal robustness across pH and ionic-strength variations [96, 97]. Since near-neutral ZP implies weaker electrostatic repulsion and potential aggregation, stability was primarily ensured via poloxamer 188 and polysorbate 80, which according to the experimental data probably intercalate at the NPs interface and provide a steric/hydration barrier through presenting the PEO chains to water. By adjusting the content of DOTAP to modulate ZP, the steric stabilization strategy rendered the near-neutral charge both intentional and appropriate.

Analysis of optimized ROS and EZE-loaded LPHNPs in the study revealed that ZP within a desired near neutral range was obtained (-0.28 ± 0.08 mV to 7.64 ± 0.47 mV). Lipid quantity and type and interactions of these two variables showed a statistically significant influence (p < 0.05) on the ZP. Positioning of the cationic lipid DOTAP close to the LPHNPs surface (as depicted by FTIR analysis), forming the lipid fraction, can explain the positive ZP and the highest ZP value in the optimal LPHNPs formulation 4 (7.64 ± 0.47 mV), which is formulated from the highest quantity of DOTAP compared to the other three optimal formulations. Overall, the relatively neutral ZP values, along with the demonstrated colloidal hold-time stability and low PDI values observed for the optimized LPHNPs, contribute to a more nuanced understanding of the role of ZP values compared to steric stabilization and confirm the importance of evaluating both electrostatic and steric stabilization mechanisms in determining NPs stability.

DL is also a QA in the design and development of NP-based drug delivery systems and was therefore included as a dependent variable in the DoE. Compared to EE, which signifies the proportion of drugs successfully encapsulated, reflecting the process efficiency, DL provides a more meaningful measure of a formulation practical and therapeutic potential. A formulation may exhibit high EE yet have low DL if the drug content relative to the carrier is minimal, leading to a need for large amounts of carrier to deliver a therapeutic dose. Higher DL enables efficient drug delivery within a minimal carrier volume, improving pharmacokinetics, patient compliance, and reducing toxicity risks. The nonlinear relationship between the EE and DL was also evident in our study, as the experimental series as well as optimized formulations with the highest total DL did not exhibit the highest EE. For increasing both the EE and loading in the LPHNPs of the purely soluble ROS and especially EZE i.e. to ultimately support the formulations performance and clinical relevance, in the current study stabilizing agents such as polysorbate 80 or poloxamer 188 were included, previously shown as enhancers of the physical stability of NPs and facilitators of drug solubilization [98]. The appropriate amounts of polysorbate 80 and poloxamer 188, titrated in manner of providing higher EEs and DLs, were determined in OFAT experiments. For example, the increase in polysorbate 80 from 0.25 mg/mL to 1.5 mg/mL improved the EE and indirectly the DL of EZE by more than 40%, while improving also the PS and PDI, thus confirming its stabilization role. Analysis of optimized ROS and EZE-loaded LPHNPs revealed a total DL within 5.88 ± 0.03–7.44 ± 0.02%, where expectedly maximum total DL was observed in case of optimal formulation 4 formed of highest total drugs and DOTAP quantity. In addition to the significant linear influence of drugs quantity on their loading, the type of lipid used largely affected the DL capacity as well. However, with increase in drugs quantity, the DL decreased and this nonlinear relationship can be attributed to the saturation of the lipid matrix with drugs at some point. This phenomena was also identified in previous studies [99], in which lipid type and quantity were identified as key formulation variables affecting DL. Overall, the achieved total DL in the optimized LPHNPs is sufficiently high to allow their incorporation in pharmaceutically acceptable quantities into final solid dosage form for oral delivery, such as tablets, or capsules. This supports their potential for further development into scalable and patient-friendly oral delivery systems.

Besides DL, importantly, maintaining the optimal drugs ratio within the formulation is crucial to preserve the synergistic therapeutic effects of the FDC drugs. Our experimental data included this QA of synthetized LPHNPs that balances efficacy, safety, and patient usability. In conventional, commercially available dosage forms for oral administration, ROS and EZE are present in non-equimolar ratio (1:2–4:1), being clinically and regulatory optimal, and offering additive LDL-C reduction without added toxicity. EZE is fixed at 10 mg due to its plateau effect, while ROS is titrated from 5 to 40 mg in order to meet the LDL-C targets, although some studies show that even 10 mg EZE combined with 10 mg ROS can match the efficacy of higher-dose ROS monotherapy, depending on the baseline LDL-C levels [100–103]. In an attempt to achieve ROS to EZE ratio of 1, 1.5, 2 and 2.5 in the optimized drugs-loaded LPHNPs, a ratio within 1.27 ± 0.005–2.32 ± 0.03 was obtained, being consistent with the 95% CI for the mean values for that response. Consistently with the previous findings that highlights the importance of lipid composition on DL [99], the DoE in the current study indicated that the lipid type and quantity, aside from the statistically significant influence of total drugs quantity (p < 0.05), play a crucial role in the DL of ROS, considering that a higher drugs ratio corresponds to increased ROS loading. This can be attributed to the distinct physicochemical properties of the two drugs i.e. more hydrophilic nature of ROS (LogP = 0.13) compared to EZE (LogP = 4.52) [104, 70] and to the hypothesis that ROS is dominantly entrapped between the lipid-polymer contact layer, while EZE in the hydrophobic polymeric fraction. Overall, by successfully modulating the type and amount of polymer and lipid components forming the LPHNPs complex matrices, targeted ROS to EZE ratios were achieved. This not only reflects a rational and well-controlled design strategy but also supports further development of formulations into clinically relevant FDC therapies.

The successful encapsulation of ROS and EZE within the particles was supported by FTIR spectroscopy, revealing the differences in the bands within the 1500–1650 cm⁻¹ region and absence of the most specific peaks for each of the drugs in the spectra of optimal LPHNPs. Although the spectral characteristics pointed to presence of mainly new hydrogen bonds within the LPHNPs (probably occurred during synthesis), the formation of either covalent bonds during synthesis cannot be fully excluded. Amines, alcohols, and acidic groups may participate in the formation of amide, ester, or ether bonds. These types of chemical interactions could also lead to observed changes in the region around 1700 cm⁻¹, associated with C = O stretching of ester and amide bonds. Due to the indirect influence on the environment surrounding nearby -OH or -NH groups, such interactions would also affect the 3400 –3200 cm⁻¹ region, as observed in the spectra of the optimized formulations. Although it is not clear which stabilizer is more prominently present on the surface of the NPs, based solely on the FTIR spectra, it is evident that the lipid fraction (composed of DOTAP and lecithin) along with the stabilizers (poloxamer 188/polysorbate 80) is localized near to the particle surface, likely forming the lipid/surfactant shell around the polymeric core. The region 1100–1200 cm⁻¹, associated with C-O-C stretching vibrations from each of the constituents of lipid fraction, and bands around 1700 cm⁻¹ and 1000–1200 cm⁻¹, originating from the stretching vibrations characteristic for the carbonyl and phosphate groups of lecithin, suggest arrangement of lipids as a shell around the polymeric core, which is consistent with earlier studies in which LPHNPs of similar composition by nanoprecipitation were prepared [34, 64–66]. In addition, the presence of a surface hydrophilic layer formed of poloxamer 188 and polysorbate 80 most likely contributes to prevention of particle aggregation, consistent with the observed colloidal hold-time stability of the optimized formulations.

DSC and TGA analyses confirmed the enhanced solid-state thermal robustness of LPHNPs compared to the physical mixture of all ingredients in both the drugs-loaded and placebo LPHNPs due to the present interactions between the excipients forming the NPs structure and the drugs, which expectedly resulted in reduction of the thermal decomposition and shift of the endothermic peak to higher temperature. DSC revealed one characteristic melting peak for each sample and absence of crystalline characteristic melting peaks of EZE and ROS in the optimal drugs-loaded LPHNPs formulations (~ 165.1 °C for EZE and ~ 226.1 °C). This finding together with the XRPD patterns confirmed encapsulation of ROS and EZE either in a molecular dispersed or amorphous state. Having in regard that both ROS and EZE are recognized as BCS class II drugs (EZE as drug with poor aqueous solubility and ROS, despite its salt form and pH-dependent ionization, as solubility-limited in the intestinal pH range [105–107]), loss of crystallinity is desired outcome, since apparent solubility and dissolution rate of the drugs are increased by eliminating lattice energy, enabling supersaturation (“spring”) and faster ingress into the absorptive pathway [108–111]. The hybrid design of NPs may prevent the recrystallization and sustain supersaturation (“parachute”), while improving wetting and diffusional transport of drugs. Together, these aspects are expected to enhance oral bioavailability and support hepatocyte-directed exposure versus crystalline drugs alone or in FDC.

In addition to the positive influence on the total DL and NPs stability, it was expected that the interactions between the lipids and polymer forming the NPs structure and the drugs would further modulate the release behavior of the drugs in the gastrointestinal tract. PLGA matrices are often associated with an initial burst release [112]. Studies have shown that this effect can be mitigated with slight modifications such as strengthening drug-carrier interactions through pH adjusting during preparation, or by modifying the surface of the PLGA, as evidenced in our study. The sustained release of both ROS and EZE and the absence of initial burst release from the optimized LPHNPs formulations observed in the study can be attributed not only to the drugs’ physicochemical properties and their interactions within the lipid-polymer matrix, but also to the surface modification of the polymer with lipids and the presence of poloxamer 188/polysorbate 80 intercalating on the surface of the LPHNPs. The presence of poloxamer 188 and other surfactants on the NPs surface has been associated with increased particle rigidity, which can further modulate drug release by slowing diffusion and altering the release profile [113]. Notably, EZE, the more lipophilic compound, also having slower release profile, is primarily encapsulated within the central hydrophobic domain of the LPHNPs formed of PLGA, which slows the molecule pathway to the surface upon release. This consideration is consistent with the results from the DoE, where the lipid quantity had dominant influence on ROS DL and had less impact on EZE DL. For ROS, all optimal formulations exhibited more consistent, dominantly diffusion-driven release, while for EZE more variable release profile was observed, governed by mixed mechanisms involving both diffusion and matrix relaxation. The release of both drugs was generally best described by the Peppas-Sahlin mathematical model, and was also evaluated in relation to the kinetic exponent (n) from the Korsmeyer-Peppas model, a key model in interpreting anomalous drug release governed additively by diffusion and polymer relaxation as drug release mechanisms [114]. Similar release mechanism for drugs incorporated in LPHNPs were observed in other studies [15, 115, 116]. Despite having the highest DL and greatest ROS to EZE ratio, the optimized LPHNPs formulation 4 generally exhibited slowest release of both drugs. This might be attributed to the highest DOTAP content, which likely reinforces the lipid fraction and increases its thickness, thereby acting as a stronger barrier to drugs diffusion. In addition to the barrier effect of the lipid fraction, the dissolution rate may also be influenced by the diffusion gradient driven by the total DL within the particles, which can modulate the diffusion mechanism. Given that the LPHNPs optimal formulation 1 prepared of lowest amount of DOTAP and drugs exhibited a relatively slower release profile for both ROS and EZE and yet similar to the release profile of both drugs from the LPHNPs optimal formulation 4, prepared of highest amount of DOTAP and drugs, it is likely that the lower total DL in the optimal formulation 1 resulted in a lower diffusion gradient, thereby sustaining the release of both drugs [114]. Тhe differences in K0 (zero-order constant) and KH (Higuchi constant) between optimal formulations 1 and 4, together with the fitted model indices, further highlight the distinctions in their dominant drug release mechanisms. Formulation 1 has higher K0, while formulation 4 a higher KH constant, which leads to conclusion that the formulation 4 aligns better to Higuchi model, consistent with diffusion-controlled release. These differences are likely due to the difference in DOTAP content and total DL, despite that both formulations have relatively comparable sustained release profiles for both drugs at pH 6.8 and 7.4 (as standard dissolution environments) and similar PS.

Overall findings highlight the ability of the LPHNPs platform to modulate drug release kinetics based on drugs characteristics and their localization within the formed NPs, reinforcing its potential as a versatile system for the tailored delivery of small molecules. Although in vitro testing conditions ensured valuable insights into the release behavior, future evaluation in biorelevant FaSSIF and FeSSIF media, mimicking the gastrointestinal conditions can support the in vitro-in vivo correlation.

Optimal formulations of ROS and EZE-loaded LPHNPs upon three months storage expectedly showed slight shift of the ZP toward more positive values, compared to initial results. The observed shift can be probably attributed to the increased exposure of cationic DOTAP on the particle surface due to the possible gradual loss of poloxamer 188 and polysorbate 80 from the surface of the LPHNPs. It is evident by the values of PS and PDI, which are closer to their initial values, that agglomeration did not take place upon storage, which can be probably ascribed to the presence of repulsive forces originating from the more exposed cationic lipid on the surface of LPHNPs. The drug leakage observed during storage is consistent with expected colloidal behavior and does not compromise LPHNPs integrity. The preserved drugs ratio indicates no preferential release, degradation, or alteration of therapeutic balance, and supports the stability of the dual-drug encapsulation. While not reflective of the final oral dosage form (i.e. shelf-life), the colloidal stability study offers valuable insights into the intermediate formulation robustness, in vivo performance upon administration, and serves as an information for hold-time stability during processing.

Conclusion

This research presents a robust and promising platform for development of next-generation targeted and personalized drug delivery systems, utilizing FDCs of drugs in LPHNPs, with the flexibility to modulate release kinetics based on the properties of the drugs. RSM design was successfully applied to optimize formulations of ROS and EZE-loaded LPHNPs, formed of DOTAP/lecithin and acid-terminated PLGA along with poloxamer 188 and polysorbate 80 as stabilisers, and synthetized by simple nanoprecipitation method. Comprehensive physicochemical characterization of the optimized formulations showed that spherical and uniform particles, closely distributed within a “cluster of grapes” like morphology were synthetized, with relatively high total DL, ROS to EZE ratio resembling the ratio in conventional commercial drugs, and PS, PDI and ZP suitable for efficient drugs delivery after oral administration. Amorphization of the drugs within the LPHNPs was confirmed using appropriate analytical techniques, providing deeper insight into drug-excipient and excipient-excipient interactions, their distribution within the NPs structure, and the colloidal stability of the drugs-loaded LPHNPs during storage. All optimal formulations with a PLGA acid-terminated polymer and DOTAP as a lipid type showed sustained release of both drugs over tested time frame at both tested dissolution media with pH 6.8 and 7.4, driven mostly by diffusion and matrix relaxation mechanisms. These findings highlight the potential of LPHNPs to overcome formulation challenges associated with poorly soluble drugs and pave the way for more effective therapeutic strategies. Further in vivo studies of optimal formulations are needed to evaluate the pharmacokinetic performance of the developed ROS and EZE-loaded LPHNPs, with focus on their biodistribution, bioavailability at the site of action (i.e. cellular uptake in hepatocytes) and off-target safety/toxicity. Furthermore, their benefit/risk profile should be confirmed in comparison with the conventional formulations of FDC of ROS and EZE.
