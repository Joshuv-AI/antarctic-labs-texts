GlyphWeaver: Unlocking Glyph Design Creativity with Uniform Glyph DSL and AI
Can Liu, Shiwei Chen, Zhibang Jiang, Yong Wang

Abstract
Expressive glyph visualizations provide a powerful and versatile means to represent complex multivariate data through compact visual encodings, but creating custom glyphs remains challenging due to the gap between design creativity and technical implementation. We present GlyphWeaver, a novel interactive system to enable an easy creation of expressive glyph visualizations. Our system comprises three key components: a glyph domain-specific language (GDSL), a GDSL operation management mechanism, and a multimodal interaction interface. The GDSL is a hierarchical container model, where each container is independent and composable, providing a rigorous yet practical foundation for complex glyph visualizations. The operation management mechanism restricts modifications of the GDSL to atomic operations, making it accessible without requiring direct coding. The multimodal interaction interface enables direct manipulation, natural language commands, and parameter adjustments. A multimodal large language model acts as a translator, converting these inputs into GDSL operations. GlyphWeaver significantly lowers the barrier for designers, who often do not have extensive programming skills, to create sophisticated glyph visualizations. A case study and user interviews with 13 participants confirm its substantial gains in design efficiency and effectiveness of producing creative glyph visualizations.

3
Overview

We propose GlyphWeaver to enhance the accessibility and expressiveness of the design and creation of glyph visualizations through three core components (Fig.
1
): the GDSL provides a structured representation, the operation management mechanism simplifies editing and ensures consistency, and the multimodal interaction interface enables users to conveniently articulate their design intentions.

•

Glyph Domain-Specific Language (GDSL).

The GDSL is a container-based and structure-driven representation for glyph visualizations.
At its core, GDSL treats every visual element as part of a container hierarchy, where containers can hold primitives, repeated elements, or composite assemblies.
This approach allows users to build complex glyphs by nesting and composing containers.

•

GDSL Operation Management Mechanism.

Although GDSL is powerful and expressive, directly authoring its code can be challenging for designers.
To address this, we introduce an operation management mechanism that restricts modifications of GDSL instances to five atomic operations.
While each operation is simple and well-defined, they can be composed to construct complex glyph visualizations.
Such a mechanism makes user intentions consistently translated into these operations, reducing the learning curve.

•

Multimodal Interaction Interface.

GlyphWeaver supports multimodal interaction:
direct manipulation via the Figma interface, natural language (NL) input through a dialogue panel, and parameter tuning through interactive controls.
Leveraging an MLLM, the framework translates these inputs into precise GDSL operations.

4.1
Containers

We analyzed 39 award-winning visualizations from
Information is Beautiful
and 11 D3 visualization examples, totaling 50 cases. Through statistical and structural analysis, we found that these visualizations can be structurally represented using three types of containers: first, basic containers, which construct single visual elements; second, repeater containers, which duplicate elements in polar or Cartesian coordinates while applying parameter variations, such as color or scaling, corresponding to data encoding; third, compositor containers, which spatially arrange heterogeneous elements, for example, overlaying or placing one element on top of another.
All visualizations employed basic and repeater containers, while 64% also used compositors.
Across the 233 recorded container operations, we observed 89 basic (Encode), 105 repeater (Repeat), and 39 compositor (Composite) operations.
Based on these observations, the three container types in GDSL enable the representation of a wide variety of glyph visualization structures.

•

Basic containers

encapsulate basic elements (polygons, lines, images, and text).
A basic container maintains an independent coordinate system.
It enables precise control over visual attributes such as position, scale, rotation, and color, making it a fundamental building block in glyph design.
Basic containers typically encode data in the form of a dictionary, where each data value is directly mapped to the visual attributes of an element.
For example, a number value can be mapped to the width and height of a rectangle.

•

Repeater containers

replicate a unit multiple times using either the Cartesian or polar coordinate systems.
Cartesian coordinates are for grid-based and linear arrangements, and polar coordinates are for circular arrangements.
A repeater container contains a child container and defines both the repetition count and arrangement pattern, replicating the child container into multiple instances according to this specified arrangement.
Repeater containers encode a list of data values, where each item is mapped to an instance of its child container.
For example, if a petal is rotated and duplicated 12 times to form a flower, and different petals are expected to have varying sizes, each entry in the data array would contain a unique scale value.

•

Compositor containers

integrate multiple different child containers.
Compositor containers also support both Cartesian and polar coordinate systems.
Unlike repeater containers that manage duplications of a child container, compositor containers coordinate the positioning of different child containers.
Compositor containers typically use dictionary-structured data, where different keys correspond to different child containers.

Spatial layout of containers.

The positions of containers are influenced by their inner coordinate system, the transformation relative to the parent, and the spatial relationship between sibling containers.

4.2
Hierarchical Container Relationships

GDSL employs a hierarchical container system that enables nested structure while maintaining manageable complexity.

Cognitive alignment.

A hierarchical structure inherently aligns with users’ cognitive processes.
Users typically commence with basic visual marks and progressively develop complexity by applying repetition and composition.
Complex visual structures can be derived through the orchestration of three container types.
This hierarchical architectural framework not only facilitates extensibility but also promotes the development of reusable components that can be leveraged in subsequent design iterations.

Nested construction.

The construction process in GDSL leverages this hierarchical approach through a nesting mechanism.
Basic containers serve as the foundation, which can then be duplicated to form repeater containers, while multiple different containers can be combined into a compositor container.
This process can continue recursively, where a composing container can be a child container of a repeater container, facilitating the construction of increasingly sophisticated structures.

Complexity management.

To manage the potential complexity that arises from such hierarchical nesting, GDSL implements several control mechanisms.
Despite increasing structural complexity, the system maintains manageability by encapsulating logic at each hierarchical level.
Each container operates within its independent coordinate system, preventing spatial transformations from interfering across different levels.
Additionally, GDSL maintains independent data management at each hierarchical level, preserving a clear data flow throughout the structure.
Each level also maintains a separate management system that governs parameter mapping rules specific to its scope.
This hierarchical and modular approach allows GDSL to maintain clarity and control while enabling users to create complex visualizations through the simple principles of element composition and repetition.

5
GDSL Operation Management Mechanism

To enable controllable editing of GDSL instances,
we propose a GDSL operation management mechanism.
It employs concise and semantically clear operations to ensure structural validity and enhance the editing accuracy and consistency.
This module consists of:
(1) a set of well-defined atomic operations, which serve as the fundamental building blocks for GDSL editing; and
(2) an MLLM-powered operation translator, which translates user intentions into valid atomic operations.

5.1
Atomic Operations

In GDSL, glyph construction is achieved through five fundamental operations, grouped into two core categories: container creation and container modification. These operations are designed as indivisible semantic units, ensuring completeness and composability in glyph design. Any valid glyph can be realized by first creating a structural skeleton and then modifying its parameters or binding data.

•

Creating basic containers.

Basic containers encapsulate basic elements, including polygons, lines, rectangles, circles, and text.
The operation
CreateBasic
defines the visual attributes for the basic element (e.g., position, size, color), along with a coordinate system specification that includes an origin point and transformations (rotation, translation, and scaling).

•

Creating repeater containers.

The
CreateRepeat
operation replicates existing content to generate a new container.

CreateRepeat
requires specifying the repeating unit’s ID (an existing container), the repetition count, the coordinate type, and the repetition category (uniform, stacked, or flexible).
An example is illustrated in
Figure 4
, where a container of four repeated circles is generated.

Figure 4
:
Creating repeater container replicates a basic container according to a polar coordinate system.

•

Creating compositor containers.

CreateCompositor
operation combines multiple existing units into a composite container. Parameters include the information to be combined and potential spatial relationships between components. For each spatial relationship, the source and target IDs, the type of spatial dependency (e.g., top, bottom, left, right), and the spatial distance must be specified. For example, positioning container A above container B with a distance of 5 units would be expressed as:

{source ID = "container A"
,

target ID = "container B"
,

spatial type = "top"
,

distance =
\{x:0,y:5\}\}
.

•

Modifying parameters.

The operation
ModifyParams
alters the parameters of a target container.
This operation requires providing the target container, visual attribute, and new value.
For example, changing the repetition count of a repeater container (with id “repeat”), the parameters are:

{target_ID = "repeat"

•

Encoding data.

The operation
EncodeData

This operation adjusts the parameters of target elements in batches.
Unlike the

For example, as shown in

5.2
MLLM-Powered Translator

To ensure diverse user inputs are translated into GDSL operations in a controllable yet flexible way, GlyphWeaver uses a four-stage translation strategy. First, it constrains the parsing scope, clearly bounding model outputs. Within this controlled space, users can interactively adjust parameters to align results with their intent. The system then supports hierarchical parsing for complex designs, decomposing multi-level structures step by step. Finally, GlyphWeaver provides suggestive feedback for irrelevant inputs, ensuring even off-topic commands are redirected productively.

•

Constrained Parsing Scope.

The translator first enforces a restricted output space: the MLLM is explicitly instructed to generate only five supported atomic operations (defined in
subsection 5.1
).
This scope prevents unsupported or overly broad outputs and guarantees predictability.
When user instructions are underspecified but still valid, GlyphWeaver supplements them with default values. For instance, the input
“rotate and replicate the object six times”
is interpreted as six rotations with a
60^{\circ}
each, forming a circular pattern.

•

Interactive Parameter Adjustment.

Even within constrained operations, automatically inferred parameters may not always align with user intent. GlyphWeaver therefore provides interactive adjustment: the system displays parameters (e.g., angle, scale, repetition count), and users can refine them directly via ambiguity widgets (see
subsection 6.2
).
This setup allows users to correct any errors in machine-generated results.

•

Hierarchical Parsing for Complex Designs.

For inputs describing multi-level or composite structures, GlyphWeaver applies hierarchical parsing. The translator first identifies higher-level structural patterns, then recursively analyzes their constituent components. By decomposing complex designs into manageable layers, the system limits the translator’s focus to one container structure at one step, which enhances controllability.

•

Suggestive Feedback for Irrelevant Inputs.

Finally, when the input does not correspond to any GDSL operation, the system shifts into a tolerant feedback mode.
Instead of failing silently, GlyphWeaver provides helpful suggestions.
For example, if the input is
“what day is it today”
, the system recognizes its irrelevance and returns feedback such as
“try ‘replicate the shape five times’ instead.”

When no real-world data is available, GlyphWeaver provides two ways to supply data: math expressions or direct data provision.

•

Math Expressions.

The synthetic data can be provided by natural language descriptions (e.g.,
“a dataset with an increasing trend”
or
“random values between 10 and 20”
). The translator then translates these descriptions into mathematical expressions.
GlyphWeaver supports two variables, index (representing the sequence number of each data point) and random (a random number generator), together with basic arithmetic operations (e.g.,
+
,
-
,
\times
, and
/
).
By composing them, the LLM can generate a wide range of mathematical expressions, such as (index * 5 + random()) for a sequence with a linear trend and random variation.

•

Direct Data Provision.

Beyond the expression-based generation, GlyphWeaver also supports directly binding user-provided or updated lists of values.
For example, when creating a group of text elements using a repeater container, a user can bind a predefined list of labels, e.g., the names of USA states, to the text attributes of these text elements.
Leveraging the capabilities of MLLMs, users may either request the system to
“change the text into three different U.S. state names”
or explicitly specify the list by saying
“change the text to California, Arizona, Texas”
.
This allows for a straightforward and controllable data binding process.

6.1
Direct Manipulation Interface

We integrate GlyphWeaver with Figma via the Figma API to leverage Figma’s direct manipulation interface.
Users can select graphics directly on the Figma canvas, and GlyphWeaver monitors these selection changes in real time.
When the selection is updated, GlyphWeaver prompts the user in the interface:
“You have selected [object]. Would you like to start the parsing process?”
The selected graphics are then converted to SVG format and, upon user confirmation, parsed into GDSL containers.

•

Basic Container Creation
.
For selected elements, GlyphWeaver first creates
basic containers
in the GDSL. Each basic container encapsulates a standalone graphical element, including text, rectangles, circles, paths, or grouped elements treated as a single SVG unit.

•

Repeater Container Creation.

We examine whether any of these basic containers form repeating patterns. Candidate repeated elements are identified using a multimodal large language model (MLLM), followed by a rule-based pipeline that infers the geometric transformations relating these elements. This pipeline analyzes the spatial coordinates of repeated elements and organizes them into a hierarchical framework of 2D affine transformations.
The space of affine transformations comprises translation, rotation, scaling, and their combinations (shearing is rarely encountered in visual design tools and is therefore excluded). Accordingly, we focus on translations, rotations around a fixed center, and scalings, either overall (
s
) or along a single direction (
s_{x},s_{y}
). To align with user intent, we adopt a simple-to-complex inference strategy: transformations are fitted in order of increasing complexity, translations first, then single-center rotations, and finally uniform or non-uniform scalings if necessary.
Based on the inferred parameters, we construct
repeater containers
in either Cartesian or polar coordinates, associating scaling parameters with the corresponding data encoding. Elements for which no valid transformation can be inferred remain in their basic containers.

•

Compositor Container Creation.

If the selection contains more than one container, we create a
compositor container
to group them.
We position these containers based on their coordinates.

6.2
Dialogue Panel

Users can input natural language (NL) commands in the dialogue panel.
Each NL command is processed by the translator (e.g., GPT-4o), which takes as input the current GDSL, other existing GDSLs on the canvas, and the user’s NL input.
The MLLM first classifies the instruction into one of four operations (except for creating a basic container) and then extracts the relevant parameters required for execution.

•

Modify Parameter.

Users can modify the parameters of a container by setting a single value on a visual attribute.
For example, users may say,
“Change the circle’s fill to blue.”

The system identifies the target element ID and the specified attribute, and then executes the corresponding operation accordingly.

•

Encode Data.
User can provide a list of values, or specify an expression.
Users may instruct the MLLM to generate a data list or a data expression. For instance,
“Randomize petal sizes between 1 and 1.5.”
prompts the model to generate a data expression.

•

Create Compositor Containers.

Users can integrate existing DSL instances from the canvas into the current GDSL, specifying the spatial relationship.
For example,
“Add a triangle above the square”
or
“Place a circle 50 units above the rectangle.”

The system determines the referenced element, placement rule (e.g., “top”), and the horizontal or vertical offset before applying the composition.

•

Create Repeat Containers.

Users can construct repeater containers by giving NL commands that describe repetition patterns.
For instance, as illustrated in
Figure 6
, a user may say,
“Vertically duplicate it twice”
to create a repeater container.
The system determines the target element, repetition count, scaling options, and the parameters for translation or rotation.

When user instructions are ambiguous, the system suggests valid examples, such as
“Specify the element and color, e.g., ‘Change the circle’s fill to blue.”

By leveraging an LLM to translate natural language into precise GDSL operations, GlyphWeaver supports flexible and expressive user interactions.

Ambiguity Widgets.

Ambiguity widgets

GlyphWeaver embeds ambiguity widgets within the dialogue panel’s textual feedback, enabling direct parameter adjustment by users.
Users can modify:

•

Target element selection.
When creating containers or updating parameters, GlyphWeaver identifies target containers by their IDs.
Ambiguity widgets present a list of valid IDs from the current GDSL, allowing users to revise the selected target element.

•

Numerical values.
For parameters expressed as numeric values (e.g., repetition counts, angles, translation distances, or coordinates), users can edit these values directly via the widgets.

•

Color selection.
Users can refine color attributes by selecting or adjusting colors interactively.

•

Data expressions and arrays.
More generally, parameters represented as strings, including generated data expressions and data arrays, are editable as free-form string input.

For example, as shown in

Adjustments made through ambiguity widgets are immediately reflected in both the GDSL representation and the preview panel, providing instant feedback for interactive refinement.

6.3
Preview Panel

GlyphWeaver provides a preview panel that is synchronized with the current GDSL instance.
The preview panel updates the rendered output as users apply operations to edit the instance.

The Preview panel acts as a renderer for GlyphWeaver, where the DSL is instantiated layer by layer according to the rules of repetition, composition, spatial relationships, and data mappings.
The basic elements are represented using SVG primitives such as
<rect>
,
<circle>
, and
<path>
(for polygons and lines), while images are rendered using
<rect>
with texture filters (
<filter>
).
Repeating and compositional structures are expanded into corresponding
<g>
(group) elements, each with appropriate transformation parameters. The instantiation process unfolds the hierarchical GDSL structure in a top-down manner,
recursively expanding Repeater Containers based on their parameters.
As a result, the rendering time complexity is proportional to the total number of instantiated SVG groups, which is determined by the product of repetition counts across all nested levels in the GDSL. Each repeated structure maps directly to an SVG
<g>
element, so the overall complexity matches that of standard SVG rendering. While the actual rendering time may be slightly higher due to the overhead of recursively expanding the GDSL structure, the asymptotic complexity remains unchanged.
These groups and basic elements are then rendered in SVG, as shown in
Figure 6
.

Once the user is satisfied with the rendered result, clicking the Apply button will send the corresponding SVG result onto the Figma canvas.
This ensures bidirectional synchronization among the GDSL instance, the SVG representations in GlyphWeaver interface, and the graphics in the Figma canvas, with no loss of information.
The rendered result synchronized to the Figma canvas is cached on our server via unique identifiers.
In subsequent sessions, users can reference and reuse previous GDSL instances in current canvas.
For example, if a user previously created a blue flower and later draws a set of lines, the composition algorithm can associate the blue flower with these lines, such as
“put a blue flower on top of each line”
. This compositional flexibility allows users to produce complex and visually engaging results.

6.4
System Latency and Performance

GlyphWeaver consists of several stages: exporting selected graphics from Figma to SVG, transferring the SVG data to GlyphWeaver, parsing natural language and images via the online MLLM, and rendering the glyph visualization in the preview panel.
The time cost for exporting selected graphics from the Figma canvas is negligible, typically below 100 ms.
Similarly, SVG transfer from Figma to GlyphWeaver involves only local data operations and incurs no significant delay.
The primary latency occurs during the communication with the cloud MLLM, which handles visualization parsing and intent interpretation.
On average, the natural language parsing requests exhibit latencies under 3 seconds, while the response time of the requests involving image analysis is within 5 seconds.
Although this constitutes the dominant time cost in the interaction loop, it remains acceptable for interactive design tasks.
The glyph visualization rendering time is also negligible, as the preview panel employs a lightweight SVG-based rendering engine that ensures real-time responsiveness.
In summary, GlyphWeaver achieves low-latency,
with negligible time costs for all pipeline stages except the communication with the cloud MLLM service.

8
Discussion

8.1
Expressiveness and Flexibility

Our multimodal glyph authoring system demonstrates significant potential in bridging the gap between user intent and formal glyph specifications.
The system’s core strength lies in its ability to interpret diverse input modalities (NL instructions and direct manipulation) and translate them into coherent GDSL operations.
We found that the GDSL successfully covers a wide range of existing glyph visualizations, validating its expressiveness and flexibility.
Notably, our system supports nested data structures beyond simple tabular data, enabling the handling of more complex and diverse data visualization requirements.
However, the GDSL currently lacks support for data mapping requiring additional computational processes. For instance, while our system enables users to directly map nodes to coordinates using x and y data, it does not yet accommodate the automatic computation of node positions (e.g., x and y coordinates) through algorithms such as force-directed layouts. This limitation arises from the expansive scope of layout algorithms, which presents significant challenges in providing comprehensive support for every possible method. Consequently, our system is presently restricted to offering direct data mapping functionalities.

8.2
Code Generation from GDSL

GDSL is a structured, formalized visualization design language, distinguished by its ease of conversion into executable code.
Currently, we have implemented a D3-based GDSL rendering module for the preview panel.
This module enables flexible visualization of various results by specifying the container ID, attributes, and bound data.
After completing the design, users can update data via the data-binding function to directly generate diverse visualization outcomes.
However, the current rendering functionality is embedded within the GDSL tool and does not yet support independent code module output.
In the future, we plan to develop an independent code conversion module to generate deployable D3.js code for each GDSL instance. Unlike the existing embedded implementation, this module will utilize a templated code generator to systematically map GDSL’s structured representation and data-binding rules to multiple programming languages or libraries (e.g., Python, JavaScript, or its D3.js library). For instance, the code generator can parse GDSL definitions to produce D3.js data-binding and rendering logic, enabling seamless integration between design and deployment while offering users a more flexible development experience.

8.3
Data Binding and Constant Visual Attribute

Our data-binding approach introduces a critical distinction between data-driven and constant visual elements, where some components dynamically reflect the underlying data while others remain static across glyph instances.
This separation, explicitly encoded in the GDSL, enhances design precision and reusability, allowing users to create glyphs that balance adaptability with consistency, a capability less pronounced in systems that failed to distinguish between these element types. This design choice supports iterative refinement, as users can tweak data-driven aspects without disrupting the glyph’s structural integrity.
By exposing the underlying data and mappings to users, the system fosters transparency, empowering them to verify and adjust bindings directly.
The openness encourages experimentation and ensures that users retain control over the data-to-visual translation.
This offers a significant advantage over fully automated systems that often obscure such mappings.
However, as dataset complexity increases, this transparency could become a double-edged sword: while it provides clarity for small-scale designs, it may overwhelm users with intricate nested structures, suggesting a need for scalable abstraction mechanisms. Balancing automation with user oversight remains a key challenge, as excessive reliance on manual adjustments could undermine the system’s efficiency.

8.4
Role of Large Language Models

The MLLM translates NL instructions into DSL operations and maintains state consistency throughout the design process.
By leveraging MLLMs as state managers, our system can effectively track and reconcile changes across multiple input modalities, ensuring that modifications remain coherent and consistent with the underlying GDSL representation.
Our approach constrains MLLMs’ outputs to a structured format and validates them against the DSL grammar, which not only maintains system reliability but also ensures that all operations preserve the semantic integrity of the design.
The user confirmation step in this process serves not only as a safeguard against potential misinterpretations but also as a mechanism for users to understand and verify the system’s state transitions.
This transparency in state management helps users maintain a clear mental model of their design while benefiting from the automated capabilities of the system.
We particularly emphasize the collaborative process between humans and MLLMs.
This collaboration ensures controllability in the human-machine interaction design process, achieving a more accurate and effective design workflow.

9
Conclusion and Future Work

GlyphWeaver tackles the critical challenges of designing expressive glyph visualizations.
A key innovation is the introduction of the GDSL.
The GDSL, together with MLLMs, enables an intelligent glyph design system.
GlyphWeaver significantly reduces the complexity and time required for glyph design, making advanced visualization techniques accessible to a wider audience, including non-experts.
Our contributions advance both the theoretical understanding of glyph-based visualization and the development of practical tools that empower users to create customized and expressive visualizations without extensive programming expertise.
By introducing a structured, expressive, and flexible DSL, our work paves the way for more intuitive, adaptable, and efficient glyph design processes.
In the future, we plan to develop a data management layer for computing derived data. Many effective glyph visualizations require transformations of raw data before mapping to visual attributes.
Integrating these advanced data management capabilities directly into GlyphWeaver would transform it into a comprehensive environment for the entire visualization workflow, from data ingestion and transformation to final expressive representation.
