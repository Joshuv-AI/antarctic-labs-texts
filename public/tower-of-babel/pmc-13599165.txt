PLGA-loaded puerarin attenuates arsenic-induced liver injury through modulation of ferroptosis, oxidative stress, inflammation, and apoptotic signaling
Authors: Shaza A Alyamani, Badriah A Hifni, Nasser S Alqahtani, Ekramy M Elmorsy, Ayat B Al-Ghafari, Huda A Al Doghaither, Wajid Ali Chatha, Gehad E Elshopakey
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundArsenic (AR) is an environmental and mining occupational toxicant with reported hepatotoxic effects. Ferroptosis has recently emerged as a critical mechanism involved in arsenic-induced hepatotoxicity. This study investigated the hepatoprotective effects of puerarin (PU) and its PLGA-loaded nanoparticle formulation (PU-PLGA) against arsenic-induced liver injury in rats, with particular emphasis on ferroptosis pathways modulation.MethodsSixty adult male Sprague–Dawley rats were randomly assigned into six groups: control, PU, PU-PLGA, arsenic (AR), AR + PU, and AR + PU-PLGA. Subacute hepatotoxicity was induced by oral administration of arsenic (10 mg/kg) for 14 days. Oxidative stress, ferroptosis-related biomarkers, inflammatory mediators, apoptotic markers, fibrotic indicators, DNA damage, histopathological alterations, and ultrastructural changes were evaluated.ResultsArsenic exposure induced marked hepatic injury characterized by Fe²⁺ accumulation, increased lipid peroxidation markers (MDA and 4-HNE), upregulation of ACSL4, and suppression of GPX4 and SLC7A11, indicating enhanced ferroptotic activity. These alterations were accompanied by oxidative stress, inflammatory activation (NF-κB, TNF-α, IL-6, IL-1β, and COX-2), apoptotic signaling (Bax and caspase-3), DNA damage (8-OHdG and DNA fragmentation), and fibrotic responses (TGF-β and collagen I). Treatment with PU and PU-PLGA significantly attenuated arsenic-induced hepatic injury by reducing iron accumulation and lipid peroxidation, restoring GPX4 and SLC7A11 expression, and suppressing ACSL4 upregulation. Notably, PU-PLGA demonstrated greater efficacy than free PU in modulating ferroptosis-related biomarkers, restoring antioxidant and survival signaling (Nrf2, p-AKT, and Bcl-2), and improving hepatic histopathological and ultrastructural integrity.ConclusionP PU-PLGA nanoparticles exerted potent hepatoprotective effects against arsenic-induced liver injury, associated with modulation of ferroptosis-related pathways, oxidative stress, inflammatory responses, apoptosis-associated signaling, and profibrotic alterations. These findings highlight PU-PLGA as a promising nanotherapeutic strategy for arsenic-associated hepatotoxicity, although further mechanistic and translational validation is warranted.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s40360-026-01221-0.Keywords: Puerarin, PLGA nanoparticles, Arsenic-induced hepatotoxicity, Oxidative stress, Ferroptosis, Nrf2 signaling pathway

Background

Arsenic (AR) is a ubiquitous environmental toxicant originating from both natural and anthropogenic sources. Natural sources include sulfide mineral deposits, volcanic emissions, and the progressive weathering of crustal rocks, whereas anthropogenic contributions arise predominantly from pesticide usage, mining activities, and metal smelting processes, particularly in copper and nickel industries [1, 2]. AR exists in multiple chemical forms depending on its binding partners; inorganic arsenic species are formed through interactions with oxygen, chlorine, or sulfur, while organic forms arise from associations with carbon and hydrogen moieties [3]. A critical toxicological concern is that most arsenic compounds are colorless, odorless, and tasteless, which significantly hinders their detection in environmental matrices such as water, food, and air, thereby increasing the likelihood of chronic, unrecognized exposure [4].

Chronic exposure to AR has been strongly associated with multi-organ toxicity, with the liver representing a primary target due to its central role in xenobiotic metabolism and detoxification [5]. Emerging evidence has identified ferroptosis as a pivotal mechanism underlying AR-induced hepatotoxicity, characterized by iron-dependent lipid peroxidation and collapse of antioxidant defense systems [6, 7]. This process is initiated by excessive generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and disruption of intracellular redox homeostasis, leading to oxidative damage to lipids, proteins, and DNA within hepatocytes [8–10]. Biomarkers such as malondialdehyde (MDA), 4-hydroxynonenal (4-HNE), and 8-hydroxy-2′-deoxyguanosine (8-OHdG) are widely utilized indicators of oxidative stress and molecular damage [11, 12].

In addition, AR-induced mitochondrial dysfunction plays a critical role in amplifying oxidative stress through enhanced ROS production and impaired bioenergetics, thereby exacerbating hepatocellular injury [13]. Concomitantly, arsenic activates inflammatory signaling cascades, particularly through the nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB) pathway, resulting in elevated levels of pro-inflammatory mediators including tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), interleukin-6 (IL-6), interleukin-1 beta (IL-1β), and cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2) [14, 15]. These mediators contribute to the progression of hepatic injury and fibrosis, partly via activation of transforming growth factor-beta (TGF-β) signaling pathways [16]. Furthermore, AR exposure induces multiple programmed cell death pathways, including apoptosis and ferroptosis, through dysregulation of key molecular regulators such as BAX, caspase-3, BCL-2, AKT, acyl-CoA synthetase long-chain family member 4 (ACSL4), transferrin receptor 1 (TFR1), solute carrier family 7 member 11 (SLC7A11), and glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4) [6, 17].

Given that oxidative stress and inflammation are central drivers of AR-induced hepatotoxicity, therapeutic strategies targeting these interconnected pathways are of considerable interest. Natural bioactive compounds have garnered significant interest owing to their diverse pharmacological activities and favorable safety profiles as potential medicinal agents. A substantial body of research indicates that diverse natural products can efficiently regulate oxidative stress, inflammation, apoptosis, ferroptosis, and fibrotic signaling pathways in a synergistic manner to mitigate tissue damage and the advancement of chronic diseases. Moreover, natural compounds have shown significant potential in preventing hepatic, pulmonary, cardiovascular, metabolic, and neurodegenerative disorders by addressing multiple interconnected pathogenic mechanisms rather than a singular molecular target [18–23].

Puerarin (PU), a naturally occurring isoflavone, exhibits robust antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and cytoprotective activities [24, 25]. It has been demonstrated to attenuate hepatotoxicity through modulation of key signaling pathways, including NF-κB and nuclear factor erythroid 2–related factor 2 (Nrf2) [26]. Additionally, PU suppresses apoptosis, preserves mitochondrial function, and enhances endogenous antioxidant defense mechanisms [27, 28]. Recent studies further suggest that PU may modulate ferroptosis by regulating lipid peroxidation and redox-sensitive enzymes, thereby expanding its therapeutic relevance [29].

Despite these promising pharmacological properties, the clinical translation of PU is significantly limited by poor aqueous solubility, low chemical stability, rapid metabolic degradation, and consequently reduced oral bioavailability [30, 31]. These pharmacokinetic constraints markedly restrict its therapeutic efficacy, particularly under conditions characterized by heightened oxidative stress and inflammatory burden.

To address these limitations, poly(lactic-co-glycolic acid) (PLGA)-based nanoparticulate systems have emerged as an advanced drug delivery platform capable of enhancing the pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic profiles of bioactive compounds [32]. Encapsulation of PU within PLGA nanoparticles has been shown to improve its solubility, stability, cellular uptake, and systemic bioavailability compared to its free form [33]. Moreover, this nanoformulation provides protection against premature degradation, enhances gastrointestinal absorption, and facilitates targeted delivery to hepatic tissues [34]. Such nano-enabled delivery systems are expected to potentiate the antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and anti-apoptotic effects of PU at the cellular and molecular levels [35].

Accordingly, the present study was designed to mechanistically evaluate the hepatoprotective efficacy of PLGA-loaded puerarin (PU-PLGA) against arsenic-induced liver injury in a rat model, with particular emphasis on the integrated and simultaneous modulation of oxidative stress, inflammatory responses, apoptosis, and ferroptosis-related pathways. Unlike previous studies that have largely examined these mechanisms in isolation or focused on conventional antioxidant effects, the current work provides a multi-dimensional assessment. To the best of our knowledge, this study represents the first report to demonstrate the synergistic regulation of ferroptosis and apoptosis by a nanoformulated puerarin delivery system in arsenic-induced hepatotoxicity.

Methods

Preparation and physicochemical characterization of puerarin -loaded PLGA nanoparticles (PU-PLGA)

Puerarin-loaded PLGA nanoparticles (PU-PLGA) were prepared using a modified nanoprecipitation–emulsification solvent evaporation method based on L Zhang, C Duan, S Feng, B Zhao, H Li, X Zhang, Y Zhou and Z Qin [36]. Briefly, 100 mg of puerarin and 200 mg of PLGA were dissolved in 3 mL acetone to form the organic phase. In parallel, an aqueous phase consisting of 1.5% (w/v) polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) was prepared and maintained in an ice bath (4 °C) to enhance emulsion stability and limit premature solvent diffusion. The organic phase was slowly injected (dropwise, ~ 1 mL/min) into the aqueous phase under continuous magnetic stirring (800 rpm).

A coarse emulsion was generated using an Ultra-Turrax T25 homogenizer at 15,000 rpm for 5 min, followed by probe sonication (Bandelin Sonopuls HD 3200) at 80% amplitude, 10 s pulse/10 s pause cycles for 3–5 min to obtain nanosized particles. The nanosuspension was stirred at room temperature for 3 h to allow complete acetone evaporation, yielding stabilized PU-PLGA nanoparticles. The formulation was stored at 4 °C until further analysis.

Particle size (Z-average), polydispersity index (PDI), and zeta potential were measured using a Zetasizer Nano ZS. All measurements were performed in triplicate at 25 °C following appropriate dilution with deionized water to avoid multiple scattering effects. Morphology was examined using transmission electron microscopy (TEM; JEOL 2100, 160 kV).

Entrapment efficiency and drug loading

The entrapment efficiency (EE) and drug loading (DL) of PU-PLGA nanoparticles were evaluated using ultrafiltration centrifugation. The resulting samples were then quantified using HPLC [37]. In brief, 0.4 mL of PU-PLGA suspension was loaded into a 0.3 kDa ultrafiltration tube and centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 30 min at 4 °C. Afterwards, 0.3 mL of the resulting filtrate was taken and diluted to a final volume of 1 mL using the mobile phase. HPLC analysis was subsequently performed to determine the levels of free drug and total drug encapsulated within the nanoparticles. EE and DL were calculated using the following equations: EE (%) = (W₁ − W₂)/W₁ × 100; DL (%) = (W₁ − W₂)/M × 100. Where W₁ is total drug, W₂ is free drug, and M is nanoparticle mass.

Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR)

FTIR analysis was conducted to evaluate drug–polymer interactions. Samples (pure PU, PLGA, PU-PLGA) were prepared using KBr pellet method under standardized pressure and scanned over 4000–400 cm⁻¹. Peak shifts, intensity changes, and disappearance of characteristic bands were interpreted as indicators of molecular interactions and successful encapsulation.

In vitro drug release study

The in vitro release profile of PU from PU-PLGA nanoparticles was evaluated using a dialysis bag diffusion method. A known amount of nanoparticle suspension was placed into a dialysis membrane with a molecular weight cut-off of 12–14 kDa. The membrane was then immersed in 50 mL of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4) containing 0.5% Tween 80. The system was maintained at 37 ± 0.5 °C under continuous shaking at 100 rpm. At predetermined time intervals, 1 mL of release medium was withdrawn and replaced with an equal volume of fresh medium. Samples were withdrawn at predetermined intervals and analyzed by HPLC. Release kinetics were further analyzed using zero-order, first-order, Higuchi, and Korsmeyer–Peppas models to elucidate the release mechanism.

Experimental animals and study design

The study utilized sixty adult male Albino Sprague Dawley rats weighing between 190 and 230 g, which were obtained from Medical Experimental Research Center (MERC), Mansoura University, Egypt. Male animals were selected to minimize potential variability associated with hormonal fluctuations during the estrous cycle, which may influence metabolic, oxidative stress, and inflammatory responses. All rats were regularly assessed for their general health status. They were maintained in disinfected plastic cages under controlled laboratory conditions. The environmental temperature was kept constant at 25 °C. Relative humidity was standardized at 45% to ensure stable housing conditions. A 12-hour light/dark cycle was applied to support normal circadian rhythms. Food and water were provided freely throughout the study. All animals were acclimated to laboratory conditions for two weeks prior to experimental procedures. All experiments were performed following the OECD 420 (2001) guidelines governing acute oral toxicity studies in rodents. All procedures involving animals were carried out under international ethical standards, ensuring minimal stress and appropriate humane care.

The animals were allocated at random into six groups of ten rats each. Sample size (n = 10/group) was determined based on power analysis (G*Power 3.1), assuming an effect size (f = 0.4), α = 0.05, and power (1 − β) = 0.80, consistent with previous hepatotoxicity models. This sample size is sufficient to detect biologically relevant differences while minimizing animal use.

Group I functioned as the control and received corn oil orally. Group II (PU) was orally administered Puerarin (100 mg/kg body weight). Rats in Group II were treated with free Puerarin (PU) at 100 mg/kg body weight, while Group III received an equal dose of PU incorporated into PLGA nanoparticles (PU-PLGA) through oral gavage. Prior studies have confirmed that these treatment schedules are safe when administered daily to rats with demonstrated hepatoprotective effect [38]. PU-PLGA dose equivalence was maintained to ensure direct comparison of formulation-dependent effects. In Group IV, sub-acute liver toxicity was induced by oral administration of arsenic (AR) at a dose of 10 mg/kg body weight daily. This treatment was carried out for 14 days, as previously described by T Alkully, MF El-Refaei and EA Abdallah [39]to induce reproducible subacute hepatotoxicity without excessive mortality. Rats in Groups V and VI were administered AR alongside either crude PU or its PLGA nanoparticles formulation (PU-PLGA) at the previously indicated doses. Treatments were administered once daily for 14 days. A standardized dosing interval (30 min) between AR and treatments was applied to avoid pharmacokinetic interference. This sample size was selected in accordance with earlier hepatoprotective research to provide 80% power with a 95% confidence level, also accounting for anticipated variability and effect size.

Serum biochemical and lipid profile evaluation

The rats (n = 10) were subjected to a 10-hour fasting period before euthanasia. Isoflurane was used to induce anesthesia until loss of reflex responses, after which euthanasia was performed using 5% isoflurane in oxygen for 5 min. The procedure was considered complete upon confirmation of death through the absence of heartbeat and respiration. Blood was collected from the retro-orbital venous sinus and subsequently allowed to clot at room temperature. The separated serum was obtained by centrifugation at 3,000 × g for 10 min. It was then divided into aliquots. The samples were stored at − 80 °C to preserve their integrity. Serum biochemical analyses were performed using ELISA kits supplied by Bio-Med Diagnostic (Egypt), following the recommended procedures. The biochemical panel consisted of total protein (TP; Cat. No. MET-5001), albumin (ALB; Cat. No. MET-5017), aspartate aminotransferase (AST; Cat. No. MET-5127), alanine aminotransferase (ALT; Cat. No. MET-5123), and alkaline phosphatase (ALP; Cat. No. MET-5135). Serum globulin levels were calculated by subtracting albumin from total protein. Commercial ELISA kits (Bio-Med Diagnostic, Egypt) were used to determine serum triglycerides, total cholesterol, high-density lipoprotein, and low-density lipoprotein. The respective kit catalog numbers were MET-5027, MET-5025, MET-5029, and MET-5031. Very low-density lipoprotein levels were calculated by dividing triglycerides by 5. All biochemical assays were performed in duplicate with internal quality controls to ensure assay reliability. ELISA kits were used following manufacturer instructions. Calibration curves and intra-/inter-assay variability were monitored.

Tissue collection and homogenization

The liver tissue was carefully harvested and flushed with ice-cold 1.15% KCl solution to wash away any remaining blood. Tissue portions allocated for biochemical and molecular assays were kept at − 20 °C for storage. Tissue portions intended for histological and ultrastructural evaluation were fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin. Meanwhile, liver samples were homogenized in 50 mmol/L Tris–HCl buffer adjusted to pH 7.4. This was done to obtain a uniform tissue suspension suitable for analysis. The homogenate was then processed to ensure proper sample preparation. Subsequently, it was centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C. Finally, the clear supernatant fractions were carefully collected for further assays. To preserve enzyme activity and molecular stability, the extracts were collected and stored at − 80 °C.

Assessment of intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) production

ROS production within cells was determined using a fluorometric kit (Green; MyBioSource, USA; Cat. No. MBS2540517). A single-cell suspension was prepared from liver tissues and exposed to DCFH-DA at 37 °C for 30 min while protected from light. The incubation was followed by washing with serum-free medium to remove unbound probe, and the cells were subsequently resuspended for analysis. A fluorescence microplate reader was used to detect the signal at an excitation wavelength of 488 nm and an emission wavelength of 525 nm. Fluorescence values were normalized to protein content to account for variability in cell number.

Hepatic DNA damage assessment by ELISA

Hepatic oxidative DNA damage was evaluated by measuring 8-hydroxy-2′-deoxyguanosine (8-OHdG) levels. This was performed using a commercial ELISA kit (MyBioSource, Cat. No. MBS165204). Tissue homogenates were applied to antibody-coated wells and incubated with standards and detection reagents. The extent of DNA oxidative damage was determined through a colorimetric reaction. Measurements were taken at 450 nm, and 8-OHdG concentrations were derived from a standard curve. Hepatic apoptotic DNA fragmentation was quantified using an ELISA kit supplied by MyBioSource (Cat. No. MBS270898).The assay involved incubating samples in antibody-coated wells. A detection antibody was then added, followed by an enzyme conjugate. Finally, the substrate was added in sequence. The colorimetric signal was read using a microplate reader, and hepatic DNA fragmentation levels were normalized to the control group. Both oxidative DNA damage (8-OHdG) and apoptotic DNA fragmentation assays were validated using standard curves and run in triplicate to ensure reproducibility.

Assessment of hepatic inflammation, fibrosis, and COX-2/NF-κB signaling

Hepatic concentrations of tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α; AEES00516), interleukin-1 beta (IL-1β; AEES02613), and interleukin-6 (IL-6; RTES00015) were measured using rat-specific ELISA kits from Assay Genie. Hepatic transforming growth factor-beta (TGF-β; MyBioSource, Cat. No. MBS160117) and collagen type I (COL1; MyBioSource, Cat. No. MBS262647) levels were further determined using ELISA kits. Nuclear factor kappa B was quantified using a commercial kit (NF-κB; MyBioSource, Cat. No. MBS453975) in isolated nuclear fraction. Nuclear fractions were prepared using a Nuclear Extraction Kit (Abcam, Cambridge, UK; Cat. No. ab113474) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2; MyBioSource, Cat. No. MBS266603) was measured using rat-specific ELISA kits. All assays were performed strictly in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. Normalization based on total protein content was applied to ensure the precision of the obtained results. Samples were run in triplicate to ensure consistent and reproducible results. The VersaMax microplate reader measured absorbance at 450 nm, and concentrations were calculated according to the standard curves supplied with each kit. All ELISA measurements were normalized to total protein concentration and expressed per mg protein.

Measurement of hepatic apoptosis and survival signaling proteins by ELISA kits

Quantification of hepatic apoptotic proteins, including Bax (Bcl-2–associated X protein), Bcl-2 (B-cell lymphoma-2), and Caspase-3, was performed using rat-specific ELISA kits from MyBioSource. The respective proteins were measured using Cat. No. MBS2512405, MBS2515143, and MBS763727. Moreover, liver concentrations of p-AKT (phosphorylated Protein Kinase B) were measured using a Rat p-AKT ELISA Kit (Cat. No. MBS1600201). All procedures were executed in accordance with the manufacturers’ instructions to maintain accuracy and consistency of the results. To improve comparability between samples, normalization was based on total protein content. A microplate reader was used to record absorbance values, and sample concentrations were calculated using the corresponding standard curves supplied with the kits.

Assessment of ferroptosis, oxidative stress, and NRF2 in liver tissue

Levels of ferroptosis-associated hepatic markers such as glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4), 4-hydroxynonenal (4-HNE), solute carrier family 7 member 11 (SLC7A11), acyl-CoA synthetase long-chain family member 4 (ACSL4), transferrin receptor 1 (TFR1), and ferrous iron (Fe²⁺) were measured. These Key ferroptosis markers (GPX4, ACSL4, SLC7A11, TFR1, Fe²⁺) were selected based on established roles in lipid peroxidation and iron metabolism, ensuring mechanistic relevance. These assessments were performed using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits and colorimetric methods. ELISA kits from MyBioSource (Cat. Nos. MBS7606779, MBS8820647, MBS7204860, and MBS265236) were used to measure 4-HNE, ACSL4, SLC7A11, and TFR1, respectively. The quantification of GPX4 was carried out using an ELISA kit from Biomatik (Cat. No. EKU12535), and results were validated in some samples using a RedDot Biotech kit (Cat. No. RD-GPX4-Ra). Fe²⁺ levels in hepatic tissue were quantified using an Abcam colorimetric iron assay kit (Cat. No. ab83366). All tests were executed strictly in line with the manufacturers’ instructions. A microplate reader was used to measure absorbance, and concentrations were calculated from kit-provided standard curves. Malondialdehyde (MDA), an index of lipid peroxidation, was quantified using a TBARS assay kit from BioDiagnostic (Cat. No. MD 2529). The analysis followed the manufacturer’s instructions, and readings were obtained spectrophotometrically at 532 nm.

Hepatic levels of nuclear factor erythroid 2–related factor 2 (NRF2) were quantitatively assessed in isolated hepatic nuclear fractions using a rat-specific enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA). Nuclear fractions were prepared using a Nuclear Extraction Kit (Abcam, Cambridge, UK; Cat. No. ab113474) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Nuclear NRF2 levels were subsequently quantified using a rat NRF2 ELISA kit (MyBioSource, San Diego, CA, USA; Cat. No. MBS1602926). All experimental procedures were carried out strictly according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Measurements were performed spectrophotometrically at a wavelength of 450 nm. Each sample was analyzed in triplicate to ensure accuracy and reproducibility of the results.

Extraction of total RNA and gene expression analysis by qRT-PCR in liver tissue

Total RNA from liver tissues was extracted following homogenization in 1 mL QIAzol Lysis Reagent using a Tissuelyser II. Chloroform was added to induce phase separation, and the mixture was centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 15 min. The resulting aqueous phase containing RNA was then carefully retrieved. The RNA was subsequently precipitated using isopropanol and then recovered by centrifugation at 12,000 × g for 10 min before further examination. The isolated RNA was resuspended in DNase/RNase-free water, and its concentration and purity were measured using spectrophotometric methods. cDNA was generated from the extracted RNA using the iScript™ cDNA Synthesis Kit as recommended by the manufacturer. qRT-PCR was then performed utilizing iTaq Universal SYBR Green Supermix (Bio-Rad, 172–5121) for amplification and detection. Gene-specific primers for ferroptosis-related markers were employed, as listed in Table 1. The primers were resuspended in nuclease-free water to ensure stability before being used in the experiments. The amplification step of real-time PCR was conducted using the Rotor-Gene Q system (Qiagen). The procedure commenced with denaturation at 95 °C for 3 min, followed by 40 successive amplification cycles. Every amplification cycle consisted of a 15-second denaturation step at 95 °C and an annealing phase at 58–60 °C for 30 s, depending on the primers used. Each cycle ended with a 30-second extension step carried out at 72 °C. Amplification specificity was validated through melt curve analysis performed from 65 °C to 95 °C, and gene expression was analyzed using the 2^–ΔΔCt method [40]. Amplification efficiency (90–110%) and specificity (single melt peak) were confirmed. The expression data were normalized using β-Actin as the housekeeping gene. Each assay was run in triplicate, with no-template controls used to ensure specificity.

Histological processing and semi-quantitative assessment of liver injury

The harvested tissues were preserved by fixation in 10% formalin (20:1, v/v) for a period of 72 h prior to histological processing. The samples were dehydrated using graded ethanol, cleared with xylene, and then infiltrated with molten paraffin for embedding. Tissue sections were cut using a rotary microtome, then dehydrated through graded alcohols and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E). The prepared histological sections were carefully examined under a light microscope. Hepatic histopathological alterations were assessed semi-quantitatively following the AASLD criteria (Table S1) [41]. Detailed evaluations were performed on three rats from each group. Each slide included three liver sections. Each section was analyzed in four randomly selected microscopic fields at 400× magnification. This resulted in a total of 12 fields evaluated per animal. The severity of hepatic lesions was scored using a semi-quantitative scale ranging from 0 (no lesion) to 3 (severe lesion), and the mean score for each animal was calculated from all examined fields. Blinded assessment was performed by an independent histopathologist to reduce observer bias.

Assessment of hepatic ultrastructure

The specimens were primarily fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde solution (0.1 M phosphate buffer) at 4 °C for 24 h. The tissues were washed after fixation and then subjected to post-fixation in 1% osmium tetroxide for 2 h to preserve ultrastructural integrity. The samples were dehydrated through a graded ethanol series ranging from 50% to 100%. This was followed by clearing with acetone. Finally, the tissues were infiltrated with epoxy resin prior to embedding. The liver specimens were sectioned into 60–70 nm ultrathin slices with an ultramicrotome and contrasted with uranyl acetate and lead citrate. The sections were subsequently attached to copper grids to improve conductivity for electron microscopy analysis. Transmission electron microscopy imaging was performed using a JEOL 2100 system (Japan), operated at 160 kV accelerating voltage. At least 5–7 fields per sample were analyzed to ensure representative ultrastructural evaluation.

Complementary in vitro ferroptosis challenge and rescue experiments

Isolation and culture of primary rat hepatocytes

Primary hepatocytes were isolated from adult male rats using a two-step collagenase perfusion method modified from Seglen (1976). Briefly, under anesthesia, the portal vein was cannulated and the liver was perfused in situ at 37 °C, first with Ca²⁺/Mg²⁺-free Hanks’ balanced salt solution containing 0.5 mM EDTA for 5 min, followed by Ca²⁺-containing buffer supplemented with 0.05% (w/v; 500 µg/mL) collagenase type IV for approximately 6–10 min. The digested liver was excised, gently dispersed in culture medium, and filtered through a 100-µm sterile nylon mesh. Hepatocytes were collected by low-speed centrifugation, washed, and resuspended in complete culture medium. Cell viability was determined by trypan blue exclusion, and preparations with viability ≥ 85% were used for subsequent experiments. Cells were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO₂.

Experiment design

Lipotoxic injury was induced by exposure to 250 µM palmitic acid (PA) for 24 h. Cells were assigned to eight groups: control, PA, PA + LUT, PA + LUT-LNPs, PA + empty LNPs, PA + ferrostatin-1 (Fer-1), PA + RSL3, and PA + LUT-LNPs + RSL3. LUT was used at 20 µM, and LUT-LNPs were administered at an equivalent LUT concentration, whereas empty LNPs were matched to the lipid content of LUT-LNPs. Fer-1 was used at 2 µM as a ferroptosis inhibitor, while RSL3 was used at 1 µM during the final 8 h of incubation as a pharmacological inhibitor of GPX4. LUT, LUT-LNPs, empty LNPs, or Fer-1 were added 2 h before PA exposure.

Assessment of cytotoxicity by LDH release

Cellular injury was assessed by measuring lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) release into the culture medium using the Pierce™ LDH Cytotoxicity Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Rockford, IL, USA; Cat. No. 88953) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Following treatment, culture supernatants were collected, and LDH activity was determined colorimetrically at 490 nm with background correction at 680 nm. LDH release was calculated relative to the maximum LDH activity obtained from completely lysed cells and expressed as percentage cytotoxicity.

Flow-cytometric assessment of lipid peroxidation

Lipid reactive oxygen species were assessed using BODIPY™ 581/591 C11 (Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat. No. D3861). Following treatment, hepatocytes were incubated with 2 µM C11-BODIPY 581/591 for 30 min at 37 °C in the dark, washed twice with PBS, and immediately analyzed by flow cytometry. A single consistent gate defining the hepatocyte population was applied to all samples. Lipid peroxidation was quantified as the mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) of oxidized C11-BODIPY in the green fluorescence channel following excitation at 488 nm and expressed relative to the untreated control.

ELISA assessment of ferroptosis-associated markers and lipid peroxidation

Cellular GPX4, SLC7A11, and 4-hydroxynonenal (4-HNE) were determined in hepatocyte lysates using commercially available ELISA kits. GPX4 was measured using the Rat Glutathione Peroxidase 4/GPX4 ELISA Kit (Novus Biologicals, Bio-Techne; Cat. No. NBP3-54325), SLC7A11 using the FineTest® Rat SLC7A11 ELISA Kit (Wuhan Fine Biotech, Wuhan, China; Cat. No. ER1709), and 4-HNE-protein adducts using the OxiSelect™ HNE Adduct Competitive ELISA Kit (Cell Biolabs, San Diego, CA, USA; Cat. No. STA-838). All assays were performed according to the manufacturers’ instructions. Analyte concentrations were calculated from the corresponding standard curves, normalized to the total protein concentration of each cell lysate, and expressed relative to the untreated control.

Statistical analysis

Microsoft Excel was used for initial data processing. Normality was tested using the Shapiro–Wilk test, and Levene’s test was employed to assess data normality and homogeneity prior to parametric testing. Differences between groups were analyzed using one-way ANOVA in SAS (2012; Proc ANOVA), followed by Tukey’s HSD test for pairwise comparisons. Results are presented as mean ± SEM, with p < 0.05 regarded as statistically significant. Data visualization was performed using GraphPad Prism 9.0 (GraphPad Software, USA) to produce clear and accurate figures and graphs.

Results

Morphological and physicochemical properties of PU-PLGA

The PU-PLGA nanoparticles were spherical and non-aggregated, as shown in Fig. 1A. Their sizes varied from 46 to 65 nm, demonstrating a uniform nanoparticle distribution (Fig. 2B). Figure 1C demonstrates that PU-PLGA exhibited a mean hydrodynamic size of 69 nm and a PDI of 0.127, reflecting good dispersion uniformity. According to Fig. 1D, the PU-PLGA nanoparticles showed a zeta potential of − 35.88 mV, suggesting strong colloidal stability. The entrapment efficiency (EE) of PU-PLGA nanoparticles was determined to be 84.46 ± 1.73%, indicating efficient incorporation of the drug within the PLGA polymeric matrix. The drug loading (DL) was found to be 8.52 ± 0.85%, suggesting a moderate loading capacity consistent with PLGA-based nanoparticle formulations prepared via the solvent evaporation technique.

Effect of FTIR analysis on PU-PLGA nanoparticle formation

FTIR spectroscopy was employed to confirm the successful incorporation of PU into the PLGA nanoparticle system and to assess possible interactions between the drug and polymer. The spectrum of free PU exhibited characteristic peaks at 3415 cm⁻¹ (O–H stretching), 2960–2875 cm⁻¹ (C–H stretching), and 1718 cm⁻¹ (C = O stretching), confirming its structural features. In addition, aromatic vibrations were observed at 1588 and 1518 cm⁻¹, along with C–O related bands at 1265 cm⁻¹ and 1015 cm⁻¹. PLGA showed its typical strong ester carbonyl peak at 1762 cm⁻¹, together with C–O–C stretching vibrations at 1195 cm⁻¹ and 1092 cm⁻¹, confirming the integrity of the polymer backbone. In the PU-PLGA nanoparticle formulation, the characteristic peaks of PU were still detectable but with reduced intensity and slight shifts. The O–H stretching band shifted from 3415 to 3388 cm⁻¹, while the carbonyl region showed a shift from 1718 to 1710 cm⁻¹, indicating possible hydrogen bonding interactions. Meanwhile, the PLGA ester peak remained prominent at approximately 1758 cm⁻¹, suggesting that the polymer structure was preserved after nanoparticle formation. Importantly, no new peaks were observed in the nanoparticle spectrum, indicating the absence of chemical bonding between PU and PLGA (Fig. 2).

Comparative in vitro release profile of PU and PU-PLGA nanoparticles

The free PU drug exhibited a rapid release behavior, where approximately 22% was released at the initial time point, followed by a sharp increase reaching nearly 96% within 24 h. This indicates an immediate and burst-type release characteristic of the unencapsulated drug. In contrast, PU-PLGA nanoparticles demonstrated a markedly sustained release profile. Only about 8% of the drug was released initially, suggesting minimal burst release due to surface-associated drug. The release then proceeded gradually over time, reaching approximately 52% after 12 h and continuing in a controlled manner up to 91% over 72 h. This sustained pattern reflects the role of the PLGA polymer matrix in modulating drug diffusion and enabling prolonged release behavior (Fig. 3).

Liver histopathology showed that both crude PU and PU-PLGA groups exhibited normal hepatic morphology without evidence of structural damage. Liver sections revealed well-preserved central veins along with a maintained lobular architecture. Both free PU and PU-PLGA given with AR notably improved the histological features of the liver, minimizing damage and restoring near-normal hepatocyte and hepatic architecture (Figs. 9E–F). Histopathological assessment revealed that AR administration led to substantial hepatic damage, evidenced by elevated tissue injury scores. Significant reductions in histopathological scores were observed following treatment with free PU or PU-PLGA, and the PLGA nanoparticles formulation demonstrated the strongest improvement in liver histology (p < 0.0001; Fig. 9G). A significant difference was observed between the PU and PU-PLGA-treated groups in hepatic damage scores (P = 0.015; Fig. 9G).

Discussion

The present study shows that AR-induced liver injury was associated with coordinated alterations in oxidative stress, ferroptosis-related pathways, inflammation, apoptosis-associated signaling, and early fibrotic remodeling, suggesting substantial interplay among these pathological processes rather than their occurrence as isolated events. Biochemically, AR exposure resulted in marked elevation of ALT, AST, and ALP, indicating loss of hepatocellular membrane integrity, accompanied by reduced total protein, albumin, and globulin, reflecting impaired hepatic synthetic capacity. Concomitant dyslipidemia (↑TC, TG, LDL, VLDL; ↓HDL) further indicates mitochondrial dysfunction and disrupted lipid handling, which are hallmarks of arsenic hepatotoxicity [42, 43]. These lipid alterations further support hepatic metabolic dysfunction and may be associated with mitochondrial impairment and disturbed lipid metabolism [44].

Conversely, administration of PU-PLGA effectively normalized these biochemical alterations and the lipid profile, suggesting restoration of hepatocellular integrity and metabolic function. Importantly, the nanoformulation consistently outperformed free PU, demonstrating greater biological efficacy under the conditions of the present study. Although PLGA-based delivery may enhance drug stability, exposure, and tissue delivery, pharmacokinetic parameters and hepatic PU concentrations were not directly assessed; therefore, the superior effects of PU-PLGA cannot be definitively attributed to enhanced systemic exposure or hepatic accumulation. However, the differences observed for certain biochemical parameters and HDL were relatively small and remained within the range of normal biological variability. This observation may reflect differences in the sensitivity and temporal recovery patterns of individual physiological markers, as biochemical indices do not necessarily respond uniformly to therapeutic intervention. These findings align with previous reports linking puerarin to lipid metabolic regulation and antioxidant activity, while extending them by demonstrating greater efficacy of the PLGA formulation in this experimental model [24, 45].

The present findings demonstrate that AR exposure induces pronounced oxidative stress accompanied by substantial oxidative DNA damage. These results agree with earlier studies showing that AR disrupts the mitochondrial electron transport chain, causing ROS overproduction that can promote lipid peroxidation and DNA damage [44, 46]. Oxidative stress may also contribute to the development of ferroptosis-associated alterations rather than representing solely a downstream consequence of arsenic toxicity. AR elevated hepatic iron levels and upregulated TFR1 and ACSL4 while suppressing the SLC7A11/GPX4 axis, producing a molecular profile consistent with increased susceptibility to iron-dependent lipid peroxidation and impairment of glutathione-dependent antioxidant defenses [47, 48]. Growing evidence highlights the involvement of ferroptosis in heavy metal–induced organ toxicity [48, 49]. The present results align with recent studies highlighting the protective role of the SLC7A11/GPX4 axis against iron-dependent lipid oxidative injury in hepatocytes [50]. Nevertheless, because ferroptosis was not functionally validated using lipid ROS-specific probes or pharmacological ferroptosis inhibitors, these findings support ferroptosis-associated pathway involvement rather than definitive confirmation of ferroptotic cell death.

PU-PLGA markedly attenuated these alterations, significantly reducing ROS, lipid peroxidation, and DNA damage. These changes were accompanied by increased nuclear NRF2 abundance, measured specifically in isolated hepatic nuclear fractions, together with restoration of SLC7A11 and GPX4, suggesting engagement of NRF2-associated antioxidant responses [51, 52]. Because a nuclear-to-cytoplasmic NRF2 ratio was not determined, however, these findings should not be interpreted as direct demonstration of NRF2 nuclear translocation. Restoration of glutathione-related antioxidant defenses and suppression of lipid peroxide accumulation may contribute to the observed attenuation of ferroptosis-associated alterations [53]. Similarly, PU-PLGA markedly reduced TFR1 and ACSL4 levels and decreased hepatic iron accumulation. Collectively, these changes indicate that PU-PLGA counteracted multiple molecular and biochemical features associated with AR-induced ferroptotic susceptibility rather than definitively demonstrating inhibition of ferroptotic cell death. A recent study in RAW264.7 macrophages similarly demonstrated that PU modulates glutathione, arachidonic acid, and tryptophan metabolism and alters ferroptosis-associated proteins, including ACSL4 and GPX4, changes associated with reduced lipid peroxidation and strengthened cellular antioxidant capacity [25]. These observations provide biological support for, but do not establish, a causal anti-ferroptotic mechanism underlying the hepatoprotective effects observed in the present study.

Inflammatory signaling was closely associated with oxidative stress and ferroptosis-related alterations in this model, suggesting potential reciprocal interactions among these processes. AR exposure increased hepatic NF-κB levels together with TNF-α, IL-6, IL-1β, and COX-2, a pattern consistent with enhanced inflammatory signaling that may further amplify oxidative damage and cellular injury [54]. NF-κB signaling can promote the expression of inflammatory mediators that enhance oxidative stress and promote immune cell infiltration, thereby aggravating hepatic injury [54, 55]. Increased COX-2 levels also suggest enhanced inflammatory activity and potentially increased prostaglandin synthesis, contributing to the progression of inflammation and liver dysfunction [56]. The present results agree with previous studies showing that AR modulates redox-sensitive inflammatory pathways, including NF-κB signaling, providing a potential link between oxidative stress and inflammation [57, 58]. PU-PLGA significantly reduced NF-κB levels and downstream inflammatory mediators, consistent with attenuation of this redox–inflammatory response. Mechanistically, this effect could involve modulation of NF-κB nuclear translocation, as previously reported for puerarin; however, NF-κB nuclear translocation or phosphorylation was not directly assessed in the present study [27]. The greater anti-inflammatory efficacy of PU-PLGA relative to free PU demonstrates a formulation-associated therapeutic advantage under the present experimental conditions. Although sustained release and enhanced bioavailability represent plausible advantages of PLGA-based delivery, these mechanisms were not directly evaluated in the present study [30].

Apoptosis and tissue remodeling are essential physiological processes that are tightly regulated under normal conditions but can be markedly disrupted following toxic exposures such as AR. In the present study, AR exposure was associated with significant alterations in apoptosis-related signaling, as indicated by increased hepatic BAX and caspase-3 levels together with decreased BCL2 and p-AKT levels. This molecular profile is consistent with enhanced intrinsic apoptosis-associated signaling; however, it does not independently establish apoptotic cell death. BAX/BCL2 imbalance can favor mitochondrial outer membrane permeabilization, cytochrome c release, and subsequent caspase activation [59, 60]. Nevertheless, TUNEL staining and cleaved caspase-3 assessment were not performed; therefore, definitive activation of the mitochondrial apoptotic cascade cannot be concluded from the present findings. The observed reduction in p-AKT is also consistent with attenuation of AKT phosphorylation-associated survival signaling. However, because p-AKT was quantified independently by ELISA and total AKT was not measured, the p-AKT/total AKT ratio could not be determined, precluding definitive conclusions regarding AKT activation status. Conversely, PU-PLGA increased BCL2 and p-AKT levels while reducing BAX and caspase-3, suggesting attenuation of apoptosis-associated signaling and restoration of a molecular profile favoring cell survival [26]. Collectively, these findings support a potential association between modulation of AKT-related survival signaling and the hepatoprotective effects of PU-PLGA, although confirmation using total AKT, the p-AKT/total AKT ratio, and complementary apoptosis-specific approaches are warranted.

Furthermore, AR induced changes consistent with early fibrotic remodeling, as indicated by increased TGF-β and collagen type I, suggesting enhanced profibrotic signaling and extracellular matrix accumulation [61, 62]. Likewise, the reduction in hepatic TGF-β together with decreased collagen type I following PU-PLGA treatment indicates attenuation of these profibrotic alterations. This suggests a potential role of PU-PLGA in limiting early processes associated with the progression of liver fibrosis. Previous studies have reported that PU exerts antifibrotic effects in the liver through downregulation of TGF-β and collagen type I [63]. Such effects have been associated with reduced hepatic stellate cell activity and extracellular matrix accumulation through modulation of TGF-β/ERK1/2 signaling [63]. However, because hepatic stellate cell activation and ERK1/2 signaling were not directly assessed in the present study, these mechanisms should be considered biologically plausible rather than directly demonstrated.

The histopathological and TEM observations were in agreement with the biochemical and molecular data. AR-treated rats showed significant hepatocellular degeneration, vacuolization, sinusoidal congestion, and inflammatory infiltration, reflecting marked liver damage. Ultrastructural examination by TEM revealed swollen mitochondria with disrupted cristae and nuclear alterations. These findings are consistent with substantial mitochondrial and subcellular injury but, in the absence of cell death-specific morphological or functional assays, should not be considered direct evidence of a particular regulated cell death pathway. These alterations were markedly improved following PU administration, with PU-PLGA showing greater preservation of hepatic histoarchitecture and mitochondrial ultrastructure than free PU. Collectively, these morphological findings support the biochemical and molecular evidence of greater hepatoprotection afforded by the nanoformulation under the present experimental conditions.

Collectively, the findings support an integrated mechanistic framework rather than establishing a definitive causal sequence. AR-induced ROS generation and mitochondrial dysfunction were accompanied by iron accumulation, suppression of the SLC7A11/GPX4 antioxidant axis, and enhanced lipid peroxidation, forming a molecular and biochemical profile consistent with increased ferroptosis-associated susceptibility. These alterations occurred concurrently with increased NF-κB and inflammatory mediators, changes in apoptosis-associated proteins, and early profibrotic responses, suggesting substantial crosstalk among oxidative, ferroptosis-related, inflammatory, apoptosis-associated, and fibrotic processes. PU-PLGA counteracted this pathological profile at multiple levels: (i) increased nuclear NRF2 abundance was accompanied by restoration of antioxidant and ferroptosis-related defenses, including SLC7A11 and GPX4; (ii) reduced NF-κB and inflammatory mediators indicated attenuation of inflammatory signaling; (iii) increased p-AKT and BCL2 together with reduced BAX and caspase-3 were consistent with modulation of survival and apoptosis-associated signaling; and (iv) reduced TGF-β and collagen type I indicated attenuation of early profibrotic remodeling. These associations should not be interpreted as definitive evidence of NRF2 activation, NF-κB inhibition, PI3K/AKT pathway activation, prevention of apoptosis, or direct inhibition of ferroptotic cell death because pathway-specific functional validation was not performed. Nevertheless, the coordinated modulation of these interconnected processes provides a plausible mechanistic basis for the greater hepatoprotective efficacy observed with PU-PLGA compared with free puerarin (Fig. 13).

Arsenic (AR) Arsenic (AR) exposure was associated with ferroptosis-related alterations characterized by Fe²⁺ accumulation, enhanced lipid peroxidation (MDA and 4-HNE), upregulation of ACSL4, and suppression of GPX4 and SLC7A11. These alterations were accompanied by increased oxidative stress, inflammatory responses, apoptosis-associated signaling, DNA damage, and profibrotic changes, together with impaired antioxidant and survival-associated signaling. Treatment with puerarin (PU) mitigated these pathological alterations, whereas PU-loaded PLGA nanoparticles (PU-PLGA) exerted greater protective effects by more effectively modulating ferroptosis-related markers, restoring redox homeostasis, increasing nuclear NRF2 and p-AKT/BCL2 levels, reducing inflammatory mediators and apoptosis-associated signaling, and attenuating DNA damage and profibrotic alterations, resulting in improved hepatic structural and functional integrity. This study, while comprehensive, has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the work primarily relied on ELISA-based protein quantification, and additional validation using Western blotting and immunohistochemistry would further strengthen the mechanistic findings. Moreover, direct functional assessment of ferroptosis using lipid ROS-specific probes such as BODIPY-C11 or ferroptosis inhibitors such as ferrostatin-1 was not performed, limiting definitive confirmation of ferroptotic cell death. Accordingly, the observed changes in Fe²⁺, lipid peroxidation, ACSL4, GPX4, and SLC7A11 should be interpreted as evidence of ferroptosis-associated alterations rather than definitive demonstration of ferroptotic cell death. Although ferroptosis has been increasingly implicated in arsenic-induced hepatotoxicity, the novelty of the present study lies in demonstrating the enhanced hepatoprotective efficacy of PU-PLGA nanoparticles and their association with modulation of ferroptosis-related pathways, particularly GPX4/SLC7A11 signaling. Similarly, although apoptosis-associated signaling was evaluated through Bax, Bcl-2, and caspase-3, TUNEL staining and cleaved caspase-3 assessment were not performed. Because TUNEL detects DNA strand breaks that are not entirely specific to apoptosis, particularly under conditions involving substantial oxidative DNA damage and other forms of regulated cell death, its inclusion would have provided complementary rather than independently definitive evidence of apoptosis. Nevertheless, the absence of TUNEL and cleaved caspase-3 limits definitive attribution of the observed cellular injury to apoptosis, and the present findings should therefore be interpreted as modulation of apoptosis-associated signaling. Furthermore, although NRF2 was quantified specifically in isolated hepatic nuclear fractions rather than as total cellular NRF2, nuclear/cytoplasmic fractionation followed by Western blotting was not performed. Thus, the observed changes in nuclear NRF2 abundance provide evidence consistent with modulation of NRF2 nuclear localization but do not provide a direct nuclear-to-cytoplasmic translocation ratio. Likewise, p-AKT was quantified by a phospho-specific ELISA, whereas total AKT was not independently measured; consequently, the p-AKT/total AKT ratio could not be determined, limiting definitive interpretation of AKT activation status. Western blot assessment of NF-κB activation status was also not conducted; therefore, the pathway-related findings should be interpreted in conjunction with the consistent changes observed in inflammatory mediators. In addition, pharmacokinetic and bioavailability comparisons between PU and PU-PLGA were not directly evaluated, restricting mechanistic interpretation of the superior efficacy of the nanoformulation. The absence of PLGA-alone and AR + PLGA control groups also precluded complete separation of potential carrier-related effects from those attributable to encapsulated PU. Future studies incorporating these controls and pharmacokinetic analyses are warranted to delineate the individual contributions of PLGA and PU. Furthermore, the study was conducted in a single experimental model and included only male rats, limiting generalizability across models and sexes. Future investigations incorporating additional preclinical models and both male and female animals are warranted. Finally, long-term outcomes, including progression to fibrosis or cirrhosis, were not investigated and require further evaluation.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that AR exposure induces significant hepatotoxicity associated with coordinated alterations in oxidative stress, DNA damage, inflammation, profibrotic responses, apoptosis-associated signaling, and ferroptosis-related pathways, rather than through isolated pathological events. These alterations were consistently associated with significant histological and ultrastructural injury, corroborating the extent of hepatic damage. The toxic consequences were markedly ameliorated by PU, supporting its multifaceted hepatoprotective effects. PU-PLGA exhibited greater efficacy than free PU, as indicated by improved normalization of biochemical indices, restoration of redox balance, modulation of ferroptosis-related markers, and preservation of hepatic architecture. Although enhanced stability, bioavailability, sustained release, and cellular uptake represent plausible advantages of PLGA-based delivery, these mechanisms were not directly evaluated and therefore cannot be considered established explanations for the superior efficacy of PU-PLGA. The hepatoprotective effects of PU-PLGA were associated with increased nuclear NRF2 abundance, reduced NF-κB and inflammatory mediators, restoration of the SLC7A11/GPX4 antioxidant axis, and modulation of p-AKT/BCL2/BAX/caspase-3 signaling. These findings support coordinated modulation of antioxidant, inflammatory, ferroptosis-related, and apoptosis-associated pathways rather than definitive activation or inhibition of individual signaling pathways or cell death mechanisms. Collectively, these findings identify PU-PLGA as a promising hepatoprotective nanoformulation against AR-induced liver injury. Additional functional mechanistic, pharmacokinetic, safety, and translational studies are required to substantiate its therapeutic relevance.
