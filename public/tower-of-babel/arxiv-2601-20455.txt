Existence and selection of solutions in the energy-variational framework with applications in fluid dynamics 0 0 footnotetext: Keywords: global existence, selection criteria, energy-variational solutions, abstract evolution equations, time discretization, Euler–Korteweg system, binormal curvature flow, vortex filament equation. 0 0 footnotetext: MSC 2020: 35A01, 35A15, 35D99, 35Q35, 76B47, 76N10
Thomas Eiter Robert Lasarzik Marcel Śliwiński

Abstract
We provide a novel existence result for energy-variational solutions to a general class of evolutionary partial differential equations. Compared to previous works on this solution concept, the generalization is mainly twofold: a relaxation of the assumptions on the regularity weight and the admissibility of energies with merely linear growth. We apply the abstract theory to the Euler–Korteweg system and to the equation for binormal curvature flow, which serve as examples that require the first and second generalization, respectively. Moreover, we discuss criteria that are suitable for the selection of particular energy-variational solutions in the possibly multi-valued solution set.



1
Introduction

Ideas on weak solution concepts for partial differential equations (PDEs) can be traced back to Lagrange’s study of the wave equation in 1760
[
35
]
,
and the seminal work by Leray
[
41
]
on weak solutions to the Navier–Stokes equations
was the first such theory developed in a functional analytic framework.
Nowadays, even further generalized notions of solutions have become common in
mathematical research on PDEs,
in particular in fluid mechanics.
For the Euler equations, there are different solvability frameworks
that allow for establishing existence results.
The first global existence result for the incompressible Euler equations
is due to DiPerna and Majda
[
18
]
, who used generalized Young measures
to describe the limits of approximate solutions
in terms of
measure-valued solutions
.
A different concept was introduced by Pierre-Louis Lions
[
42
, Sec. 4.4]

in the form of
dissipative solutions
, based on a variational inequality, the relative energy inequality.
These concepts have been further developed and applied to more involved equations.
For instance, there are results concerning the
existence of measure-valued solutions to the
compressible isentropic Euler system
[
11
]
, the Euler–Korteweg system and
the Euler–Korteweg–Poisson system
[
25
]
, as well
as dissipative solutions to the Ericksen–Leslie equations
[
37
]
and a model for
magnetohydrodynamics
[
4
]
.
In this article, we focus on the concept of

energy-variational solutions
.
As has been shown before, it is suitable to derive general existence results for large classes of
systems of conservation laws
[
20
]
,
including the Euler equations for incompressible and compressible fluids,
of more general damped Hamiltonian systems
[
1
]
,
and of models for visco-elasto-plasticity
[
22
]
.

Consider two Banach spaces
\mathbbm{Y}
and
\mathbbm{V}
with a dense and compact embedding
\mathbbm{Y}\hookrightarrow\mathbbm{V}
,
and an abstract evolutionary problem of the form

\displaystyle\partial_{t}U(t)+A(t,U(t))

\displaystyle=0\qquad

\displaystyle\text{in }\mathbbm{Y}^{*}\text{ for }t\in(0,T),

(1.1a)

\displaystyle U(0,\cdot)

\displaystyle=U_{0}\qquad

\displaystyle\text{in }\mathbbm{V}^{*},

(1.1b)

where
U:[0,T]\rightarrow\mathbbm{V}^{*}
is the unknown state variable and
A
is a (time-dependent) operator.
We assume the existence of a convex energy
{\mathcal{E}}
and a dissipation potential
\Psi

such that any smooth solution to (
1.1
) satisfies the energy-dissipation balance

\mathcal{E}(U)\Big|^{t}_{s}+\int_{s}^{t}\Psi(\tau,U(\tau))\differential{\tau}=0

(1.2)

for
s,t\in[0,T]
with
s<t
.
An energy-variational solution consists of the state variable
U
and
a second variable
E\colon[0,T]\to[0,\infty)
such that
{\mathcal{E}}(U)\leq E
and
the variational inequality

[E-\langle U,\Phi\rangle]\Big|^{t}_{s}+\int_{s}^{t}\langle U,\partial_{t}\Phi\rangle-\langle A(\tau,U),\Phi\rangle+\Psi(\tau,U)+\mathcal{K}(\Phi)[\mathcal{E}(U)-E]\differential{\tau}\leq 0

(1.3)

holds for all suitable test functions
\Phi\in\mathcal{C}^{1}([0,T];\mathbbm{Y})

and almost all
s<t\in[0,T]
, including
s=0
with
U(0)=U_{0}
.
Here, the
regularity weight

\mathcal{K}
is chosen in a way
that ensures the weak* lower semicontinuity
of the left-hand side of (
1.3
)
with respect to the natural topology.
The energy-variational inequality (
1.3
)
can be seen as the sum of an energy-dissipation inequality, with
{\mathcal{E}}(U)
replaced with
E
,
with a the weak formulation of (
1.1
)
and an additional error term
that is the product of the regularity weight
\mathcal{K}
with the energy defect, defined as the
difference of the physical energy
\mathcal{E}
and the auxiliary
variable
E
.
In particular, any weak solution
U
that satisfies the energy-dissipation inequality
can be identified with an energy-variational solution
(U,E)
by setting
E={\mathcal{E}}(U)
.
This also motivates to choose
\mathcal{K}
minimal in a certain sense
since then (
1.3
) is close to a weak formulation of (
1.1
).

Besides the existence results for general systems,
this novel framework has also been used to identify singular
limits for fluid equations
[
39
,
22
]
.
or the limit of solutions to an implementable numerical
schemes for the Ericksen–Leslie equations
[
36
]
.
Here, the
energy-variational solution for one choice of the regularity weight could
be shown to imply the existence of a measure-valued
solution, whereas another choice provided the convergence of solutions to a
structure-inheriting finite-element scheme.
Energy-variational solutions further come along with a relative-energy inequality,
that can be used to derive a weak-strong uniqueness principle
[
20
,
1
]

or to quantify the long-time behavior
[
21
]

of energy-variational solutions in certain settings.
Moreover, in a recent preprint
[
40
]
, it was shown that
energy-variational framework can also be applied to
the geometric PDE for a
Navier–Stokes two-phase flow model, and to the system of polyconvex elasticity.
The energy-variational solutions were shown to be
stronger in comparison to the available varifold solutions
and Young measure-valued solutions, while significantly
reducing the degrees of freedom of the solution concept.

While a generalization of the solvability concept may yield a framework
that simplifies to show existence results,
it enhances the non-uniqueness of solutions.
Starting with the seminal work by De Lellis and Székelyhidi
in the context of the incompressible Euler equations
[
17
]
,
the non-uniqueness of solutions was shown for the compressible Euler equations
[
13
]
, the Euler–Korteweg system
[
19
]
, the
Navier–Stokes equations
[
12
]
and other systems, using convex integration.
Moreover, the existence of multiple Leray–Hopf solutions to the Navier–Stokes equations
was shown for a particular external forcing
[
2
]
, based on a
linear stability analysis around a special vortex
solution.
This approach was recently adapted for the unforced case
by developing a computer-assisted proof of the relevant
instability
[
30
]
.

In order to counteract this inherent non-uniqueness in fluid dynamics,
one can reduce the number of solutions by
introducing suitable selection criteria,
for instance, by physically motivated principles.
This approach goes back to the seminal work by Dafermos
[
16
]
,
on the selection of weak solutions to hyperbolic conservation laws
by maximizing the entropy dissipation rate.
In the context of the Euler equations,
there are selection criteria that single out solutions with maximal dissipation
[
9
,
10
,
38
,
24
]
,
with minimal acceleration
[
49
]
,
with maximal turbulence
[
34
]
,
or subject to a least-action
principle
[
27
]
.
In
[
39
]
it was shown that energy-variational solutions
are also suitable to implement certain selection criteria in the context of incompressible fluids.
It was shown that one can select a solution with vanishing energy defect at finitely many prescribed points in time.
Alternatively, the selection by minimization of a strictly convex integral functional is not
only unique but also depends continuously on the data in certain scenarios.

One main novelty of the article at hand is a new existence result
for energy-variational solutions to the evolution equation (
1.1
)
by generalizing previous works in several directions.
We relax the assumptions on the
underlying space as well as on the energy
{\mathcal{E}}
,
for which we merely assume a linear growth condition,
in contrast to superlinear growth assumed in
[
1
]
.
Moreover, we reduce the assumptions from
[
1
,
20
]

on the regularity weight
\mathcal{K}
from convexity to mere
weak* lower semicontinuity.
This allows for the inclusion of lower order terms in
A

that can be treated by compactness and strong convergence.
For the existence proof,
we follow
[
20
,
1
,
22
]

and construct solutions based on a minimizing-movements scheme reminiscent of the
usual Jordan–Kinderlehrer–Otto scheme for gradient flows
[
44
]
.
More precisely, the
n
-th iterate
U^{n}
is determined from the previous iterate
U^{n-1}
by

\mathcal{E}(U^{n})+\tau\Psi^{n}(U^{n})-\mathcal{E}(U^{n-1})-\langle U^{n}-U^{n-1},\Phi\rangle-\tau\langle A^{n}(U^{n}),\Phi\rangle\leq 0

(1.4)

for all suitable test functions
\Phi
.
Here
\Psi^{n}
and
A^{n}
are certain approximations of
\Psi
and
A
.
The scheme (
1.4
) corresponds to
a fully implicit discretization of a weak formulation of (
1.1
)
combined with a discrete energy-dissipation inequality.
We construct the iterate
U^{n}
by minimizing the left-hand side of (
1.4
)
after taking the supremum over all admissible
\Phi
.
To ensure a convex-concave structure sufficient for its solvability,
we suitably restrict the class of test functions
\Phi
in terms of
another regularity weight
\tilde{\mathcal{K}}
.
This second regularity weight is only used on the discrete level,
and its effect disappears in the time-continuous limit.
In particular, the choice of
\tilde{\mathcal{K}}
can be coarser than
\mathcal{K}
in a certain sense
without having an impact on the definition of solutions through (
1.3
).

Another main contribution of the present article is the development of different
selection criteria in this general setting of energy-variational solutions.
We first show that for any countable, ordered sequence of time points in
[0,T]
,
we can construct an energy-variational solution with
vanishing energy defect at those times.
This is an extension of a similar result from
[
39
]
.
Moreover, we adapt a criterion from
[
24
]
, which was used in the context of dissipative solutions to the Euler equations.
We show that for any initial data there exists an energy-variational solution with an arbitrarily small energy defect,
that is, for all
\epsilon>0
there exists an energy-variational solution
(U,E)
such that

\mathcal{E}(U)\leq E\leq\mathcal{E}(U)+\epsilon\quad\text{in }[0,T].

This means in a certain way
that one can find energy-variational solutions that are arbitrarily close
to a possible weak solution.
As a third criterion, we show that we can select an energy-variational solution that minimizes a given functional among all solutions.
Under additional strict convexity assumptions,
this minimizer is even unique.
A typical example for such a functional would be the
L^{1}(0,T)
-norm of
E

in order to minimize the effect of the auxiliary variable
and to maximize the energy dissipation in a certain sense.
Similarly, measure-valued solutions with maximal turbulence
have been identified by maximization of concave functionals in
[
34
]
.
A selection process by successively minimizing a countable family of convex functionals
was used in
[
9
,
10
,
24
]

to identify solutions to the Euler equations with maximal dissipation.
Here, the exact energy functionals are not specified
while the selected solution strongly depends on this choice.
In particular, it remains open which choice would lead to a physically reasonable solution.

We exemplify the advantages of the newly developed framework
by concrete PDEs from fluid mechanics.
At first, we consider the Euler–Korteweg system
for inviscid compressible flow with capillary effects.
The local-in-time existence of strong solutions to this system was established
in
[
8
]
.
We refer to
[
5
]

for a global-in-time existence result for small data.
Similarly to the Euler equations,
the Euler–Korteweg equations admit non-unique weak solutions for certain initial data
[
19
]
,
whereas weak solutions coincide with the unique strong solution if the latter exists
[
26
]
.
By realizing the Euler–Korteweg system in the setting of energy-variational solutions, this work establishes the first solvability framework
that gives the global-in-time existence for general initial data.
Here, we benefit from the consideration of
two different regularity weights
\mathcal{K}
and
\tilde{\mathcal{K}}
.
Since the energy functional associated with the Euler–Korteweg system
contains gradient terms,
the natural
a priori
bounds yield strong compactness
that can be exploited when passing with the discretized solution to the weak* limit.
In contrast, to ensure existence of the discretized solution constructed by (
1.4
),
the necessary convexity requires a larger regularity weight

\tilde{\mathcal{K}}
.

The second example
illustrates the advantage
of allowing for a more general functional framework and
for energies that are homogeneous of degree one.
Such features are usually necessary to treat geometric PDEs,
and we establish an energy-variational framework for the equations for the binormal curvature flow,
which models the evolution of vortex filaments.
While the quality of the existence results for such geometric PDEs is similar
to the Euler and Euler–Korteweg equations,
the employed concepts are very different.
For instance, for the binormal curvature flow, there are results
on the existence of smooth solutions locally in time
[
6
]
,
on the global existence of generalized solutions
[
32
, Thm. 1]
,
and on weak-strong uniqueness
[
32
, Thm. 2]
,
and the state of the art for mean-curvature flow is similar
[
29
]
.
However, generalized solutions in this area differ
from the concepts in fluid dynamics, as
varifold solutions
are usually considered.
Roughly speaking, these describe the
limits of evolving manifolds and their tangent spaces.
Therefore, the energy-variational framework presented here may be considered the
first overarching solution concept that combines the two areas of classical fluid dynamics and geometric PDEs.
Since this allows for the consideration of existence results
and selection criteria in a unified context,
the energy-variational structure might also be beneficial
for studying the connection
between fluid flows and the movement of vortex filaments
[
31
]

and the related singular limits.
This, however, remains part of future research.

In Section

2
Energy-variational solutions in an abstract setting

For the introduction of the concept of energy-variational solutions
to the abstract evolution equation (
1.1
),
we first collect general assumptions on the relevant functionals and operators.
We derive a series of basic properties of energy-variational solutions.
Subsequently, we address the main results in this framework:
a general existence result in Subsection
2.4
,
and suitable criteria
to select specific candidates within the solution set
in Subsection
2.5
.

2.1
Assumptions

In order to carry out the analysis of the abstract evolution equation (
1.1
), we introduce the following set of structural and technical assumptions:

(A1)

\mathbbm{Y},\mathbbm{V}
are separable Banach spaces
with a dense and compact embedding
\mathbbm{Y}\hookrightarrow\mathbbm{V}
.

(A2)

The energy functional
\mathcal{E}:\mathbbm{V}^{*}\rightarrow[0,\infty]
is convex and weakly* lower semicontinuous, and it is coercive in the sense that there are
\alpha,\beta\geq 0
with

\forall U\in\mathbbm{V}^{*}:\quad{\mathcal{E}}(U)\geq\alpha\|U\|_{\mathbbm{V}^{*}}-\beta.

(2.1)

(A3)

The dissipation potential
\Psi:[0,T]\times\mathbbm{V}^{*}\rightarrow\mathbbm{R}\cup\{+\infty\}

and the operator
A:[0,T]\times(\text{dom}\,{\mathcal{E}}\cap\text{dom}\,\Psi)\rightarrow\mathbbm{Y}^{*}
, where we set
\dom\Psi:=\bigcap_{t\in[0,T]}\dom\Psi(t,\cdot)
,
are both measurable with respect to the first variable and satisfy the equality

\forall\Phi\in\mathbbm{Y}\cap\dom\partial{\mathcal{E}}^{*}\quad\exists\zeta\in\partial{\mathcal{E}}^{*}(\Phi)\cap\dom\Psi\quad\forall t\in[0,T]:\quad\langle A(t,\zeta),\Phi\rangle=\Psi(t,\zeta),

(2.2)

(A4)

There exists a regularity weight
\mathcal{K}:\mathbbm{Y}\rightarrow[0,\infty)
that is continuous
and maps bounded sets to bounded sets,
and such that the mapping

U\mapsto\Psi(t,U)-\langle A(t,U),\Phi\rangle+\mathcal{K}(\Phi)\mathcal{E}(U)

is weakly* lower semicontinuous on
\text{dom}\,{\mathcal{E}}\cap\text{dom}\,\Psi
for all
\Phi\in\mathbbm{Y}
and a.a.
t\in[0,T]
.

(A5)

There exists an auxiliary regularity weight
\tilde{\mathcal{K}}:\mathbbm{Y}\rightarrow[0,\infty)
that is convex, continuous, and maps bounded sets to bounded sets. Moreover, the mapping

U\mapsto\Psi(t,U)-\langle A(t,U),\Phi\rangle+\tilde{\mathcal{K}}(\Phi)\mathcal{E}(U)

is convex and weakly* lower semicontinuous on
\text{dom}\,{\mathcal{E}}\cap\text{dom}\,\Psi
for all
\Phi\in\mathbbm{Y}
and a.a.
t\in[0,T]
. Furthermore, for all
\Phi\in\mathbbm{Y}
there exists a constant
c(\Phi)\geq 0
such that

\Psi(t,U)-\langle A(t,U),\Phi\rangle+\tilde{\mathcal{K}}(\Phi)\mathcal{E}(U)\geq-c(\Phi)({\mathcal{E}}(U)+1)

(2.3)

for all
U\in\mathbbm{V}^{*}
and a.a.
t\in(0,T)
. Moreover, for
\Phi\in\mathbbm{Y}
with
\|\Phi\|_{\mathbbm{Y}}\leq R
, where
R>0
, the constant
c(\Phi)
only depends on
R
and is idenpendent of
\Phi
.

The space
\mathbbm{Y}
introduced in
(A1)
serves as the class of test functions, and the dual space
\mathbbm{V}^{*}
of
\mathbbm{V}
is the state space. Observe that the dense embedding
\mathbbm{Y}\hookrightarrow\mathbbm{V}
implies the (dense) embedding
\mathbbm{V}^{*}\hookrightarrow\mathbbm{Y}^{*}
,
and the compactness of one of the embeddings implies
the compactness of the other by Schauder’s theorem.
Moreover, separability ensures that bounded sets in
\mathbbm{V}^{*}
are sequentially weakly* compact
by the sequential version of the Banach–Alaoglu theorem.

The convexity and the lower semicontinuity from
(A2)

imply weak* lower semicontinuity of the energy functional
{\mathcal{E}}
,
and the coercivity assumption ensures that
the sublevel sets of
{\mathcal{E}}
are sequentially weakly* compact by the Banach–Alaoglu theorem.
This allows to conclude the existence of convergent subsequences from
a priori
bounds.

The assumption
(A3)

ensures that the dissipation potential
\Psi

is consistent with the operator
A
.
Indeed, consider the regular case where we have the Fenchel equivalence, so that
\zeta\in\partial{\mathcal{E}}^{*}(\Phi)

if and only if
\Phi\in\partial{\mathcal{E}}(\zeta)
.
Then we can formally test (
1.1a
)
with an element
\Phi(t)\in\partial{\mathcal{E}}(U(t))
,
and obtain

0=\big\langle\partial_{t}U(t)+A(t,U(t)),\Phi(t)\big\rangle=\frac{\differential}{\differential t}{\mathcal{E}}(U(t))+\Psi(t,U(t)),

which gives the energy-dissipation law (
1.2
).

Note that for the intersections
\mathbbm{Y}\cap\dom\partial{\mathcal{E}}^{*}
and
\partial{\mathcal{E}}^{*}(\Phi)\cap\dom\Psi

we tacitly use the natural embeddings
\mathbbm{V}\hookrightarrow\mathbbm{V}^{**}

and
\mathbbm{V}^{*}\hookrightarrow\mathbbm{V}^{***}
.
More precisely,
\Phi\in\mathbbm{Y}\cap\dom\partial{\mathcal{E}}^{*}
means that

\Phi\in\mathbbm{Y}\hookrightarrow\mathbbm{V}
and that the
natural representative of
\Phi
in
\mathbbm{V}^{**}
is an element of
\dom\partial{\mathcal{E}}^{*}
.
Similarly,
\partial{\mathcal{E}}^{*}(\Phi)\cap\dom(\Psi)
has to be understood.

The regularity weights
\mathcal{K}
and
\tilde{\mathcal{K}}

from
(A4)
and
(A5)

are fundamental for our approach, but play different roles:
The weak* lower semicontinuity from assumption
(A4)
ensures that the solution
concept is stable under weak* convergence.
In particular, it ensures that solutions can be approximated within the weak* topology in
\mathbbm{V}^{*}
,
where convergence can be derived from a piori bounds.
The regularity weight
\tilde{\mathcal{K}}
is of a more technical nature.
The convexity from
(A5)
ensures that the approximate solutions can be constructed
by solving certain saddle-point problems.
If solutions are approximated differently, for instance, by a space-time discretization
or a vanishing-viscosity approach, the assumption
(A5)
may be obsolete.

The lower bound (
2.3
) is needed for technical reasons.
For instance, as
\Psi
does not have a sign in general,
it ensures that the energy-dissipation inequality ensures integrability of
\Psi
,
see Proposition
2.7
below.
Instead of using the energy,
one can also derive an affine lower bound of the left-hand side of (
2.3
),
which would be natural if
A
contains terms linear in
\Phi
.
By the coercivity estimate (
2.1
), such a bound implies (
2.3
).

The assumption that
\mathcal{K}
and
\tilde{\mathcal{K}}
map bounded sets to bounded sets
is merely technical.
It is trivially satisfied by all examples presented later
since both functionals
are always dominated by a multiple of the norm
\|\cdot\|_{\mathbbm{Y}}
in these cases.

At first sight, the assumption
(A4)
seems redundant
since one can simply choose
\mathcal{K}=\tilde{\mathcal{K}}
if
(A5)
is satisfied.
However, as
\mathcal{K}
appears in the energy-variational formulation (
1.3
) (compare also Definition
2.1
below),
it measures to a certain extent how far the energy-variational solution deviates from a weak solution.
This motivates choosing
\mathcal{K}
as small as possible.
The possibility of using two different regularity weights is one of the novelties of this paper,
and it has recently been adapted to treat a visco-elasto-plasticity model
[
22
]
,
while in other contexts, they coincide
[
1
,
20
]
.

2.2
Definition of energy-variational solutions

Now we can formally introduce energy-variational solutions to
the abstract problem (
1.1
).

Definition 2.1
.

Let
U_{0}\in\mathbbm{V}^{*}
with
{\mathcal{E}}(U_{0})<\infty
.
The pair
(U,E)
with

U\in L^{\infty}_{w^{*}}(0,T;\mathbbm{V}^{*})\cap\mathrm{BV}([0,T];\mathbbm{Y}^{*}),\qquad E\in\mathrm{BV}([0,T])

is called an
energy-variational solution
to (
1.1
) if
\mathcal{E}(U(t))\leq E(t)
for almost all
t\in(0,T)
and if

[E-\langle U,\Phi\rangle]\Big|^{t}_{s}+\int_{s}^{t}\langle U,\partial_{t}\Phi\rangle-\langle A(\tau,U),\Phi\rangle+\Psi(\tau,U)+\mathcal{K}(\Phi)[\mathcal{E}(U)-E]\differential{\tau}\leq 0

(2.4)

for all
\Phi\in\mathcal{C}^{1}([0,T];\mathbbm{Y}\cap\dom\partial{\mathcal{E}}^{*})
and almost all
s<t\in[0,T]
, including
s=0
for which
U(0)=U_{0}
.

Remark 2.2
.

If
(U,E)
is an energy-variational solution with
E(t)={\mathcal{E}}(U(t))
,
then (
2.4
) simplifies to

{\mathcal{E}}(U)\Big|^{t}_{s}+\int_{s}^{t}\Psi(\tau,U)\,\mathrm{d}\tau-\langle U,\Phi\rangle\Big|^{t}_{s}+\int_{s}^{t}\langle U,\partial_{t}\Phi\rangle-\langle A(\tau,U),\Phi\rangle\,\mathrm{d}{\tau}\leq 0,

which is the combination of an energy-dissipation inequality
with a weak formulation of (
1.1b
).
In particular, any weak solution satisfying the energy-dissipation balance
is an energy-variational solution.

Remark 2.3
.

In Definition
2.1

we only consider test functions taking values in
\mathbbm{Y}\cap\dom\partial{\mathcal{E}}^{*}
.
Here we tacitly use the embedding
\mathbbm{Y}\hookrightarrow\mathbbm{V}\hookrightarrow\mathbbm{V}^{**}
, and
the intersection has to be understood as in (
2.2
).
We see in Proposition
2.6
below that
the domain
\dom\partial{\mathcal{E}}^{*}
contains at least a ball around zero,
so that the class of test functions is sufficiently rich.
We further show if
{\mathcal{E}}
has superlinear growth, then we even have
\dom{\mathcal{E}}=\dom\partial{\mathcal{E}}^{*}=\mathbbm{V}^{**}
,
and test functions from the whole linear space

\mathcal{C}^{1}([0,T];\mathbbm{Y}\cap\dom\partial{\mathcal{E}}^{*})

are admissible.

Remark 2.4
.

Since we do not assume separability of the space
\mathbbm{V}^{*}
,
Pettis’s theorem that identifies weak and strong measurability (see
[
46
, Theorem 1.34]
for instance) does not apply in general.
Therefore, it is necessary to consider solutions in a Bochner space
of weakly* measurable functions, namely
L^{\infty}_{w*}([0,T];\mathbbm{V}^{*})

since it can be identified with the dual space of
L^{1}([0,T];\mathbbm{V})
in this general setting,
compare
[
45
, Theorem 6.14]
.

Remark 2.5
.

Observe that we do not specify an initial value for
E
in Definition
2.1
,
which allows for certain flexibility and features like the semiflow property, see Proposition
2.10
below.
However, in the main existence result from Theorem
2.13
,
we show that the choice
E(0+)={\mathcal{E}}(U_{0})
is indeed possible,
so that the energy defect vanishes initially.

2.3
General properties of energy-variational solutions

We collect a number of properties, which follow from the definition of energy-variational solutions.
Let the assumptions
(A1)
–
(A5)
be fulfilled for all the following propositions.

First of all, we show that
\dom\partial{\mathcal{E}}^{*}
contains a neighborhood of zero.
In particular, the class of test functions
in the energy-variational formulation (
2.4
)
is not too small and
contains at least a ball around zero.

Proposition 2.6
.

The convex conjugate
{\mathcal{E}}^{*}
of
{\mathcal{E}}
satisfies

{\mathcal{E}}^{*}\leq\iota_{\overline{B}_{\alpha}}+\beta
,
where
\alpha,\beta\geq 0
are the constants from
(A2)
and
B_{\alpha}=\{\eta\in\mathbbm{V}^{**}\mid\|\eta\|_{\mathbbm{V}^{**}}<\alpha\}
.
In particular, it holds
\overline{B}_{\alpha}\subset\dom{\mathcal{E}}^{*}

and
B_{\alpha}\subset\dom\partial{\mathcal{E}}^{*}
.
Moreover, if
{\mathcal{E}}
has superlinear growth,
that is,

\lim_{\|U\|_{V^{*}}\to\infty}\frac{{\mathcal{E}}(U)}{\|U\|_{V^{*}}}=\infty,

(2.5)

then
\dom{\mathcal{E}}^{*}=\dom\partial{\mathcal{E}}^{*}=\mathbbm{V}^{**}
.

Proof.

From the definition of the convex conjugate and from
(A2)
,
we infer

{\mathcal{E}}^{*}(\eta)\leq\sup_{U\in\mathbbm{V}^{*}}\langle U,\eta\rangle-\alpha\|U\|_{V^{*}}+\beta\leq\sup_{U\in\mathbbm{V}^{*}}\big[\|\eta\|_{V^{**}}-\alpha\big]\|U\|_{V^{*}}+\beta=\iota_{B_{\alpha}}(\eta)+\beta.

This further implies
{\mathcal{E}}^{*}(\eta)<\infty
for
\eta\in\overline{B}_{\alpha}
, that is,
\dom{\mathcal{E}}^{*}\subset\overline{B}_{\alpha}
,
which also yields
\dom\partial{\mathcal{E}}^{*}\subset B_{\alpha}

as
\dom\partial{\mathcal{E}}^{*}
is dense in
\dom{\mathcal{E}}^{*}
and convex.

Now let
{\mathcal{E}}
satisfy (
2.5
).
Then for every
\alpha>0
there exists
\beta>0
such that (
2.1
) holds.
Hence, by the previous argument we have
B_{\alpha}\subset\dom\partial{\mathcal{E}}^{*}\subset\dom{\mathcal{E}}^{*}

for any
\alpha>0
, so that
\dom\partial{\mathcal{E}}^{*}=\dom{\mathcal{E}}^{*}=\mathbbm{V}^{**}
.
∎

We can now show that the function class specified in Definition
2.1

is natural for energy-variational solutions,
and that we can derive associated
a priori
bounds.

Proposition 2.7
.

Let
(U,E)
be an energy-variational solution with
E(0+)\leq M
.
Then there exists a constant
C(M)>0
, independent of
(U,E)
, such that

\norm{U}_{L^{\infty}_{w^{*}}(0,T;\mathbbm{V}^{*})}+\norm{U}_{\mathrm{BV}([0,T];\mathbbm{Y}^{*})}+\norm{E}_{\mathrm{BV}([0,T])}+\norm{\Psi(\cdot,U)}_{L^{1}(0,T)}\leq C(M).

(2.6)

Proof.

Plugging
\Phi=0
into the inequality (
2.4
), we obtain

\displaystyle E\Big|_{s}^{t}+\int_{s}^{t}\Psi(\tau,U)\differential{\tau}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{s}^{t}\mathcal{K}(0)[E(\tau)-{\mathcal{E}}(U)]\differential{\tau}\leq\int_{s}^{t}\mathcal{K}(0)E(\tau)\differential{\tau}

(2.7)

for almost all
0\leq s\leq t\leq T
.
Since
\Psi(\tau,U)\geq-c(0)({\mathcal{E}}(U)+1)-\tilde{\mathcal{K}}(0){\mathcal{E}}(U)
by assumption
(A5)
, inequality (
2.7
) this yields

E\Big|_{s}^{t}\leq\int_{s}^{t}[\mathcal{K}(0)+c(0)+\tilde{\mathcal{K}}(0)]E+c(0)\differential{\tau}.

We can apply Gronwall’s inequality to obtain

\|E\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T)}\leq C
.

Next, we show that
\|E\|_{\mathrm{BV}([0,T])}\leq C
.
Let us define
h:[0,T]\to\mathbbm{R}
as

h(t):=E(t)+\int_{0}^{t}\Psi(\tau,U)+\mathcal{K}(0)[\mathcal{E}(U)-E]\differential{\tau}.

Due to (
2.7
),

h
is non-increasing, so that
|h|_{\mathrm{TV}([0,T])}=|h(0)-h(T)|\leq h(0)=E(0)
.
We further define
g=h-E
.
On the one hand, with the inequality (
2.7
) we estimate
g
from above as

\displaystyle g(t)=\int_{0}^{t}\Psi(\tau,U)+\mathcal{K}(0)[{\mathcal{E}}(U)-E]\differential{\tau}\leq-E(t)+E(0)\leq 2\|E\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T)}\leq C.

(2.8)

On the other hand, by assumption
(A5)
we can estimate
\tilde{g}(\tau):=\Psi(\tau,U(\tau))+\mathcal{K}(0)[\mathcal{E}(U(\tau))-E(\tau)]
from below by

\displaystyle\tilde{g}(\tau)

\displaystyle=\Psi(\tau,U(\tau))+\mathcal{K}(0)[{\mathcal{E}}(U(\tau))-E(\tau)]

\displaystyle\geq-c(0)({\mathcal{E}}(U(\tau))+1)+\mathcal{K}(0)[{\mathcal{E}}(U(\tau))-E(\tau)]-\tilde{\mathcal{K}}(0){\mathcal{E}}(U(\tau))\geq C_{1}

due to
\|E\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T)}\leq C
.
Hence,
\tilde{g}\in L^{1}(0,T)
, and
g
is absolutely continuous, and as such
g\in\mathrm{BV}([0,T])
.
We further conclude
\|g\|_{\mathrm{BV}([0,T])}\leq C
,
and
E=h-g
implies

\|E\|_{\mathrm{BV}([0,T])}\leq C
.
Moreover, the asserted bound on
\Psi(\cdot,U)
follows from the bounds on
\tilde{g}
.

It remains to show the bounds on
U
.
From
{\mathcal{E}}(U(t))\leq E(t)
, we conclude
\|{\mathcal{E}}(U)\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T)}\leq C
. Combined with coercivity of
{\mathcal{E}}
(assumption
(A2)
), this implies
\|U\|_{L^{\infty}_{w^{*}}(0,T;\mathbbm{V}^{*})}\leq C
.
Moreover, rewriting the energy-variational inequality (
2.4
) and adding and subtracting the term
\tilde{\mathcal{K}}(\Phi){\mathcal{E}}(U)
, we get

\displaystyle-\langle U(t)-U(s),\Phi\rangle

\displaystyle\leq-\int_{s}^{t}\Psi(\tau,U)-\langle A(\tau,U),\Phi\rangle+\tilde{\mathcal{K}}(\Phi){\mathcal{E}}(U)\differential{\tau}

\displaystyle\qquad-(E(t)-E(s))+\int_{s}^{t}[\tilde{\mathcal{K}}(\Phi)-\mathcal{K}(\Phi)]{\mathcal{E}}(U)+\mathcal{K}(\Phi)E\differential{\tau}

for any
\Phi\in\mathbbm{Y}\cap\dom\partial{\mathcal{E}}^{*}
and a.a.
s<t
,
s,t\in[0,T]
.
By Proposition
2.6
, this includes all
\Phi\in\mathbbm{Y}
with

\|\Phi\|_{\mathbbm{Y}}<\alpha
, where
\alpha\geq 0
is the constant from
(A2)
.
By assumption
(A5)
, it holds

\inf_{\|\Phi\|_{\mathbbm{Y}}<\alpha}[\Psi(t,U)-\langle A(t,U),\Phi\rangle+\tilde{\mathcal{K}}(\Phi){\mathcal{E}}(U)]\geq-c({\mathcal{E}}(U)+1)).

Using the identity
\|U\|_{\mathbbm{V}^{*}}=\alpha^{-1}\sup_{\|\Phi\|_{\mathbbm{V}}<\alpha}\langle U,\Phi\rangle

and the assumption that
\mathcal{K}
and
\tilde{\mathcal{K}}
map bounded sets to bounded sets,
we can estimate

\displaystyle\sup_{0=t_{0}<t_{1}<\ldots<t_{N}=T}\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}\|U(t_{i+1})-U(t_{i})\|_{\mathbbm{Y}^{*}}\leq\int_{0}^{T}c({\mathcal{E}}(U)+1)+cE\differential{\tau}+\|E\|_{\mathrm{BV}[0,T]}\leq C.

Together with
\|U\|_{L^{\infty}_{w^{*}}(0,T;\mathbbm{V}^{*})}\leq C
,
this implies
\|U\|_{\mathrm{BV}([0,T];\mathbbm{Y}^{*})}\leq C
and completes the proof.
∎

Next we show that the energy-variational formulation (
2.4
),
which holds pointwise a.e. in
[0,T]
,
can be replaced with a pointwise inequality.
If
{\mathcal{E}}
has superlinear growth, without loss of generality,
we may assume that
U
is weakly* continuous in
\mathbbm{V}^{*}
(and thus, in
\mathbbm{Y}^{*}
as well).

Proposition 2.8
.

Let
U\in L^{\infty}_{w^{*}}(0,T;\mathbbm{V}^{*})\cap\mathrm{BV}([0,T];\mathbbm{Y}^{*})
,

E\in\mathrm{BV}([0,T])
and
\Phi\in\mathcal{C}^{1}([0,T];\mathbbm{Y})
.
Then (
2.4
) holds for a.a.
s<t\in[0,T]
if and only if

[E-\langle U,\Phi\rangle]\Big|^{t+}_{s-}+\int_{s}^{t}\langle U,\partial_{t}\Phi\rangle-\langle A(\tau,U),\Phi\rangle+\Psi(\tau,U)+\mathcal{K}(\Phi)[\mathcal{E}(U)-E]\differential{\tau}\leq 0

(2.9)

holds for all
s\leq t\in[0,T]
.
Moreover, if
(U,E)
is an energy-variational solution as in Definition
2.4
,
and if
{\mathcal{E}}
satisfies (
2.5
),
then there exists
\tilde{U}\in\mathcal{C}_{w*}([0,T];\mathbbm{V}^{*})
such that
U=\tilde{U}
almost everywhere on
[0,T]
.

Proof.

The assumptions on
U
,
E
,
\Phi
imply

[t\mapsto E(t)-\langle U(t),\Phi(t)\rangle]\in\mathrm{BV}([0,T])
.
One-sided limits of this mapping exist.
On the one hand, passing to such limits in (
2.4
) yields (
2.9
).
On the other hand, as these limits coincide with the function values a.e., (
2.9
) implies (
2.4
).

In particular, if
(U,E)
is an energy-variational solution, it satisfies (
2.9
).
Assuming superlinear growth of
{\mathcal{E}}
, we have
\dom{\mathcal{E}}^{*}=\dom\partial{\mathcal{E}}^{*}=\mathbbm{V}^{**}
by Proposition
2.6
,
so that the we can take arbitrary test functions in
\Phi\in\mathcal{C}^{1}([0,T];\mathbbm{Y})
in (
2.9
).
Choosing
t=s
and
\Phi\in\mathbbm{Y}
independent of time, yields

[E-\langle U,\Phi\rangle]\Big|^{t+}_{t-}\leq 0\quad\text{for all}\;t\in(0,T).

From a standard variational argument (compare
[
20
, Lemma 2.10]
),
it follows that
U(t+)=U(t-)
in
\mathbbm{Y}^{*}
for all
t\in(0,T)
.
Therefore, the function
\tilde{U}
defined by
\tilde{U}(t):=U(t+)
in
\mathbbm{Y}^{*}

satisfies
\tilde{U}\in\mathcal{C}_{w^{*}}([0,T];\mathbbm{Y}^{*})
,
and as
U\in\mathrm{BV}([0,T];\mathbbm{Y}^{*})
, we have that

U=\tilde{U}
a.e. in
[0,T]
.
Moreover, for
\{t_{n}\}\subset[0,T]
with
t_{n}\to t\in[0,T]
, we have

\|\tilde{U}(t_{n})\|_{\mathbbm{V}^{*}}\leq\|U\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;\mathbbm{V}^{*})}
, so that
there exists a (not relabeled) subsequence with

\tilde{U}(t_{n})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle*}}{{\rightharpoonup}}\tilde{U}_{t}
in
\mathbbm{V}^{*}

for some element
\tilde{U}_{t}\in\mathbbm{V}^{*}
.
Since we also have
\tilde{U}(t_{n})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle*}}{{\rightharpoonup}}\tilde{U}(t)
in
\mathbbm{Y}^{*}
, we infer
\tilde{U}_{t}=\tilde{U}(t)
due to the dense embedding
\mathbbm{Y}\hookrightarrow\mathbbm{V}
,
so that
\tilde{U}(t_{n})\to\tilde{U}(t)
in
\mathbbm{V}^{*}
. In conclusion, we obtain
\tilde{U}\in\mathcal{C}_{w^{*}}([0,T];\mathbbm{V}^{*})
.
∎

The following result shows that one-sided limits of
U
exist in the weak* topology of
\mathbbm{V}^{*}
.
Moreover, a vanishing energy defect at a certain time
yields right-continuity of the energy in this point.

Proposition 2.9
.

At each point
t_{0}\in[0,T]
,
the one-sided weak* limits

U(t_{0}+):=\lim_{t\searrow t_{0}}U(t)
and
U(t_{0}-):=\lim_{t\nearrow t_{0}}U(t)

exist in
\mathbbm{V}^{*}
.
Moreover, if
t_{0}\in[0,T]
such that
{\mathcal{E}}(U(t_{0}+))=E(t_{0}+)
, then
\lim_{t\searrow t_{0}}{\mathcal{E}}(U(t))={\mathcal{E}}(U(t_{0}+))
.

Proof.

Firstly, since
U\in\mathrm{BV}([0,T];\mathbbm{Y}^{*})
, the one-sided limit
U(t_{0}+)
and
U(t_{0}-)

exists in
\mathbbm{Y}^{*}
,
and by
U\in L^{\infty}(0,T;\mathbbm{V}^{*})
, the same argument as in the proof of Proposition
2.8
yields their existence in
\mathbbm{V}^{*}
.

As
E\geq{\mathcal{E}}(U)
and as
{\mathcal{E}}
is weakly* lower semicontinuous, we further observe that

\displaystyle E(t_{0}+)=\lim_{t\searrow t_{0}}E(t)\geq\limsup_{t\searrow t_{0}}{\mathcal{E}}(U(t))\geq\liminf_{t\searrow t_{0}}{\mathcal{E}}(U(t))\geq{\mathcal{E}}(\lim_{t\searrow t_{0}}U(t))\geq{\mathcal{E}}(U(t_{0}+)).

If the first and the last term in this chain of inequalities coincide,
we conclude that
{\mathcal{E}}(U(t))\to{\mathcal{E}}(U(t_{0}+))
as
t\searrow t_{0}
.
∎

Another useful characteristic of energy-variational solutions
is the following semi-flow property.

Proposition 2.10
.

Energy-variational solutions possess the semi-flow property:
If
0<t_{0}<T
and
(U_{1},E_{1})
is a solution on
[0,t_{0}]
and
(U_{2},E_{2})
is a solution on
[t_{0},T]

such that
U_{1}(t_{0}-)=U_{2}(t_{0}+)
in
\mathbbm{Y}^{*}
and
E_{2}(t_{0}+)\leq E_{1}(t_{0}-)
, then the concatenation

(U(t),E(t)):=\begin{cases}(U_{1}(t),E_{1}(t))\quad\text{if }t\in[0,t_{0}),\\
(U_{2}(t),E_{2}(t))\quad\text{if }t\in[t_{0},T],\end{cases}

is a solution. If
(U,E)
is an energy-variational solution on
[0,T]
, its restriction
to a subinterval
[t_{0},t_{1}]\subset[0,T]
is an energy-variational solution on
[t_{0},t_{1}]

with initial value
U(t_{0}+)
.

Proof.

Let
0<t_{0}<T
and let
(U,E)
be defined as above. Then
(U,E)
satisfies the variational inequality (
2.4
). Indeed, since the variational inequality (
2.4
) is formulated in an integral form, we decompose the integral over
[0,T]
as two integrals over
(0,t_{0})
and over
(t_{0},T)
. On the interval
[0,t_{0})
the inequality holds for
(U_{1},E_{1})
, and on
[t_{0},T]
it holds for
(U_{2},E_{2})
by assumption, with test functions that are suitably restricted.
Therefore, the variational inequality (
2.4
) also holds for the concatenated pair
(U,E)
on
[0,T]
. Moreover, due to weak* lower semicontinuity of
{\mathcal{E}}
, it holds

\mathcal{E}(U(t_{0}+))=\mathcal{E}(U_{2}(t_{0}+))\leq\liminf_{t_{n}\searrow t_{0}}\mathcal{E}(U_{2}(t_{n}))\leq\liminf_{t_{n}\searrow t_{0}}E_{2}(t_{n})=E_{2}(t_{0}+)=E(t_{0}+).

Thus the concatenation
(U,E)
is also an energy-variational solution in the sense of the Definition
2.1
.

Furthermore, if
(U,E)
is a solution on
[0,T]
, then its restriction to any subinterval satisfies the same energy-variational inequality on any interval
[s,t]\subset[0,T]
.
∎

An advantage of energy-variational solutions compared to weak solutions
is the structure of the solution set.
As the first feature, we show its convexity in the case that
\mathcal{K}
and
\tilde{\mathcal{K}}
coincide.

Proposition 2.11
.

Let
M\in[{\mathcal{E}}(U_{0}),\infty]
.
If the regularity weights
\mathcal{K},\tilde{\mathcal{K}}
are such that
\mathcal{K}=\tilde{\mathcal{K}}
, then the solution set
\mathcal{S}(U_{0},M)
defined by

\mathcal{S}(U_{0},M):=\big\{(U,E)\;\text{is an energy-variational solution with initial data}\;U_{0}\text{ and }E(0+)\leq M\big\}

(2.10)

is convex.

Proof.

The conditions
{\mathcal{E}}(U)\leq E
and
E(0+)\leq M
are preserved under convex combinations,
and if
\mathcal{K}=\tilde{\mathcal{K}}
, then
the left-hand side of energy-variational formulation (
2.4
)
is convex as a function of
(U,E)
by
(A5)
.
This shows convexity of
\mathcal{S}(U_{0},M)
.
∎

We next show the sequential weak* compactness of the solution set.
For this property, the weak* lower semicontinuity induced by
the regularity weight
\mathcal{K}
from
(A4)
is crucial.

Proposition 2.12
.

Let
U_{0}\in\mathbbm{V}^{*}
and
M\in[{\mathcal{E}}(U_{0}),\infty)
.
Then the set
\mathcal{S}(U_{0},M)
, defined in (
2.10
), is sequentially weakly* compact in the space
L^{\infty}_{w^{*}}(0,T;\mathbbm{V}^{*})\cap\mathrm{BV}([0,T];\mathbbm{Y}^{*})\times\mathrm{BV}([0,T])
.

Proof.

Let
\{(U^{n},E^{n})\}_{n\in\mathbbm{N}}\subset\mathcal{S}(U_{0},M)
.
From Proposition
2.7

we obtain the uniform bounds

\|U^{n}\|_{L^{\infty}_{w^{*}}(0,T;\mathbbm{V}^{*})\cap\mathrm{BV}([0,T];\mathbbm{Y}^{*})}\leq c,\quad\|E^{n}\|_{\mathrm{BV}([0,T])}\leq c

for some
c>0
.
From the sequential Banach–Alaoglu theorem we infer that there exist subsequences (not relabeled), such that
U^{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle*}}{{\rightharpoonup}}U
in
L^{\infty}_{w^{*}}(0,T;\mathbbm{V}^{*})\cap\mathrm{BV}([0,T];\mathbbm{Y}^{*})
and
E^{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle*}}{{\rightharpoonup}}E
in
\mathrm{BV}([0,T])
. For each
n\in\mathbbm{N},s<t\in[0,T],\Phi\in\mathcal{C}^{1}([0,T];\mathbbm{Y})
it holds

[E^{n}-\langle U^{n},\Phi\rangle]\Big|^{t}_{s}+\int_{s}^{t}\langle U^{n},\partial_{t}\Phi\rangle-\langle A(\tau,U^{n}),\Phi\rangle+\Psi(\tau,U^{n})+\mathcal{K}(\Phi)[\mathcal{E}(U^{n})-E^{n}]\differential{\tau}\leq 0.

(2.11)

Due to a generalization of Helly’s selection theorem
(see
[
43
, Theorem B.5.10]
), we have
U^{n}(t)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle*}}{{\rightharpoonup}}U(t)
in
\mathbbm{Y}^{*}

and
E^{n}(t)\rightarrow E(t)
a.e. on
[0,T]
.
Arguing similarly as in the proof of Proposition
2.8
,
we conclude
U^{n}(t)\rightharpoonup U(t)
in
\mathbbm{V}^{*}
.
Moreover, from the compact embedding
\mathrm{BV}([0,T])\hookrightarrow L^{1}(0,T)
, the convergence
E^{n}\rightarrow E
in
L^{1}(0,T)
follows.
These convergence suffice to pass to the limit in all terms in (
2.11
)
where
(U^{n},E^{n})
appears in a linear way.
For the remaining terms,
we use Fatou’s lemma together with
the lower semicontinuity property from
(A4)

to conclude

\displaystyle\liminf_{n\to\infty}\int_{s}^{t}-\langle A(\tau,U^{n}),\Phi\rangle+\Psi(\tau,U^{n})+\mathcal{K}(\Phi)\mathcal{E}(U^{n})\differential{\tau}

\displaystyle\geq\int_{s}^{t}-\langle A(\tau,U),\Phi\rangle+\Psi(\tau,U)+\mathcal{K}(\Phi)\mathcal{E}(U)\differential{\tau}.

Note that the application of Fatou’s lemma was justified
as (
2.3
) induces an integrable lower bound
for the integrand,
compare also estimate (
2.27
) in the proof of Theorem
2.13
below.
In conclusion,
we can thus pass to the limit inferior on the left-hand side of (
2.11
),
which yields (
2.4
).
Moreover, passing to the limit inferior in the condition
{\mathcal{E}}(U^{n})\leq E^{n}
a.e. in
[0,T]

yields
{\mathcal{E}}(U^{n})\leq E^{n}
a.e. in
[0,T]
.
Thus the limiting pair
(U,E)
is an energy-variational solution in the sense of Definition
2.1
.
∎

2.4
Existence of energy-variational solutions

We now come to the first main result of this article,
which states the global-in-time existence of energy-variational solutions
to the abstract problem (
1.1
).

Theorem 2.13

(Existence of energy-variational solutions)
.

Let the assumptions
(A1)
-
(A5)
be satisfied. For every
U_{0}\in\operatorname{dom}{\mathcal{E}}
, there exists an energy-variational solution to (
1.1
) in the sense of Definition
2.1
, which satisfies
E(0+)=\mathcal{E}(U_{0})
.

The proof of Theorem
2.13
is divided into two parts. Firstly, we establish the existence of solutions at the time-discrete level. Secondly, we show convergence of these discretized solutions to an energy-variational solution.

2.4.1
Construction of approximate solutions

As the first step towards a proof of Theorem
2.13
, we construct approximate solutions by a time-discrete scheme,
consisting of solving a saddle-point problem in each time step.

Fix
N\in\mathbbm{N}
such that
N>\tilde{\mathcal{K}}(0)
and set
\tau=T/N
. In order to obtain an equidistant discretization, we set
t^{n}=\tau n
for
n\in\{0,1,\ldots,N\}
. We define the class of test functions as

\mathbbm{Y}_{\tau}:=\{\Phi\in\mathbbm{Y}\cap\dom\partial{\mathcal{E}}^{*}\;|\;\tilde{\mathcal{K}}(\Phi)\leq 1/\tau\},

and we set

\displaystyle A^{n}(U)

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{\tau}\int_{t^{n-1}}^{t^{n}}A(t,U)\differential{t},

\displaystyle\qquad\Psi^{n}(U)

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{\tau}\int_{t^{n-1}}^{t^{n}}\Psi(t,U)\differential{t},

for
U\in\mathbbm{V}^{*}
. We define the function
\mathcal{F}^{n}:\mathbbm{V}^{*}\times\mathbbm{Y}_{\tau}\rightarrow(-\infty,\infty]
as

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}^{n}(U|\Phi)

\displaystyle=(\mathcal{E}(U)+\tau\Psi^{n}(U)-\mathcal{E}(U^{n-1}))-\langle U-U^{n-1},\Phi\rangle-\tau\langle A^{n}(U),\Phi\rangle,

(2.12)

where we set
\mathcal{F}^{n}(U|\Phi)=\infty
if
U\notin\dom{\mathcal{E}}
.

We defined
U^{n}\in\mathbbm{V}^{*}
,
n\in\{0,1,\ldots,N\}
iteratively by the following minimization scheme:

\displaystyle\begin{split}U^{0}&=U_{0},\\
U^{n}&=\argmin_{U\in\mathbbm{V}^{*}}\sup_{\Phi\in\mathbbm{Y}_{\tau}}\mathcal{F}^{n}(U|\Phi).\end{split}

(2.13)

The proof of existence of solutions
U^{n}
is preceded by the following auxilliary lemma.

Lemma 2.14

(
\mathcal{H}
is proper)
.

Let
\mathcal{H}^{n}:\mathbbm{V}^{*}\rightarrow\mathbbm{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
be defined as

\mathcal{H}^{n}(U):=\sup_{\Phi\in\mathbbm{Y}_{\tau}}\mathcal{F}^{n}(U|\Phi).

Then
\mathcal{H}
is a convex, weakly* lower semicontinuous and proper functional such that

\inf_{U\in\mathbbm{V}^{*}}\mathcal{H}^{n}(U)\leq 0.

Proof.

Let
\Phi\in\mathbbm{Y}_{\tau}
be arbitrary.
By the energy-balance assumption
(A3)
,
there exists
\hat{U}\in\partial{\mathcal{E}}^{*}(\Phi)\cap\dom\Psi^{n}

such that
\langle A^{n}(\hat{U}),\Phi\rangle=\Psi^{n}(\hat{U})
. Then it holds

\inf_{U\in\mathbbm{V}^{*}}\mathcal{F}^{n}(U|\Phi)\leq\mathcal{F}^{n}(\hat{U}|\Phi)=\mathcal{E}(\hat{U})-\mathcal{E}(U^{n-1})-\langle\hat{U}-U^{n-1},\Phi\rangle\leq 0.

Since
\Phi
was arbitrary,
the claimed inequality follows if

\displaystyle\inf_{U\in\mathbbm{V}^{*}}\sup_{\Phi\in\mathbbm{Y}_{\tau}}\mathcal{F}^{n}(U|\Phi)=\sup_{\Phi\in\mathbbm{Y}_{\tau}}\inf_{U\in\mathbbm{V}^{*}}\mathcal{F}^{n}(U|\Phi).

(2.14)

We observe that the function
\mathcal{F}^{n}(U|\Phi)
is concave with respect to
\Phi
, since it is linear in
\Phi
and defined on the convex subset
\mathbbm{Y}_{\tau}
. Moreover, it is continuous with respect to
\Phi
. To show convexity in
U
, we rewrite
\mathcal{F}^{n}
as

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}^{n}(U|\Phi)

\displaystyle=(1-\tau\tilde{\mathcal{K}}(\Phi))[\mathcal{E}(U)-\mathcal{E}(U^{n-1})]-\langle U-U^{n-1},\Phi\rangle

(2.15)

\displaystyle\phantom{=}+\tau\Psi^{n}(U)-\tau\langle A^{n}(U),\Phi\rangle+\tilde{\mathcal{K}}(\Phi)[\mathcal{E}(U)-{\mathcal{E}}(U^{n-1})].

Since
{\mathcal{E}}
is convex, since
1-\tau\tilde{\mathcal{K}}(\Phi)\geq 0
, and since the second line in the above expression is convex thanks to the assumption
(A5)
, we conclude that
\mathcal{F}^{n}
is convex with respect to
U
. Similarly, we see that
\mathcal{F}^{n}
is weakly* lower semicontinuous with respect to
U
.
In particular, this implies that
\mathcal{H}
is convex and weakly* lower semicontinuous
as the supremum of functionals with these properties.
Moreover, we conclude that sublevel sets of the function
\mathcal{F}^{n}(\cdot|0)
are weakly* closed.
They are also bounded
due to the coercivity assumption on
{\mathcal{E}}
from
(A2)
.
Therefore, we can apply
[
7
, Theorem 2.130]
, and we conclude the equality (
2.14
),
which finishes the proof.
∎

We can now show the existence of solutions to the minimization problems from (
2.13
).

Lemma 2.15

(Existence of a discrete minimizer)
.

Let
N\in\mathbbm{N}
with
\tilde{\mathcal{K}}(0)<N
.
Then there exists a solution
(U^{0},\dots,U^{N})
to the minimization problem (
2.13
),
Moreover, for
n=1,\dots,N
, the minimizer
U^{n}
satisfies

\sup_{\Phi\in\mathbbm{Y}_{\tau}}\mathcal{F}^{n}(U^{n}|\Phi)\leq 0.

(2.16)

Proof.

The minimization problem (
2.13
) is equivalent to finding a unique minimizer
U^{n}
of the functional
\mathcal{H}^{n}
from Lemma
2.14
for
n=1,\dots,N
.
Moreover, these minimizers directly satisfy (
2.16
).
Since
\mathcal{H}
is convex and weakly* lower semicontinuous by Lemma
2.14
,
level sets of
H
are sequentially closed in the weak* topology. Moreover, since
\mathcal{H}
is coercive, which follows from
(A2)
,
level sets are also bounded in
\mathbbm{V}^{*}
.
The Banach–Alaoglu Theorem implies that level sets are sequentially compact with respect to the weak* topology.
Since
\mathcal{H}
is also convex by Lemma
2.14
,
the existence of a solution to the minimization problem (
2.13
)
follows from
[
47
, Prop. 1.48]
.
∎

From the solution
(U^{0},\dots,U^{N})

to the minimization problem (
2.13
),
we now introduce approximate solutions for the energy-variational solution
(U,E)
.
We define piecewise constant prolongations
\overline{U}^{N}\colon[0,T]\to\mathbbm{V}^{*}

together with the corresponding energy
\overline{E}^{N}\colon[0,T]\to[0,\infty)
as

\overline{U}^{N}(t)=\begin{cases}U^{0}=U_{0}\quad&\text{for}\quad t=0,\\
U^{n}\quad&\text{for}\quad t\in(t^{n-1},t^{n}],\end{cases}\qquad\quad\overline{E}^{N}(t)=\begin{cases}\mathcal{E}(U_{0})\quad&\text{for}\quad t=0,\\
\mathcal{E}(U^{n})\quad&\text{for}\quad t\in(t^{n-1},t^{n}].\end{cases}

(2.17)

To obtain a variational problem satisfied by
(\overline{U}^{N},\overline{E}^{N})
,
we introduce piecewise constant and piecewise linear approximations of test functions.
For functions
\chi\in\mathcal{C}([0,T];X)
where
X
is
\mathbbm{R}
or
\mathbbm{Y}
, let us define

\displaystyle\overline{\chi}^{N}(t)=\begin{cases}\chi(0)\quad&\text{for}\quad t=0\\
\chi(t^{n})\quad&\text{for}\quad t\in(t^{n-1},t^{n}],\end{cases}\qquad\quad\underline{\chi}^{N}(t)=\begin{cases}\chi(t^{n-1})\quad&\text{for}\quad t\in[t^{n-1},t^{n}),\\
\chi(T)\quad&\text{for}\quad t=T,\end{cases}

and

\hat{\chi}^{N}(t)=\frac{\chi(t^{n})-\chi(t^{n-1})}{\tau}(t-t^{n-1})+\chi(t^{n-1})\quad\text{for}\quad t\in[t^{n-1},t^{n}],

for all
n\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
.

Lemma 2.16
.

For each
N\in\mathbbm{N}
, the approximate solution
(\overline{U}^{N},\overline{E}^{N})
satisfies

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}-\partial_{t}\hat{\phi}^{N}\Big[\overline{E}^{N}-\langle\overline{U}^{N},\underline{\Phi}^{N}\rangle\Big]+\overline{\phi}^{N}\langle\overline{U}^{N},\partial_{t}\hat{\Phi}^{N}\rangle+\underline{\phi}^{N}\Big[\Psi(t,\overline{U}^{N})

\displaystyle-\langle A(t,\overline{U}^{N}),\underline{\Phi}^{N}\rangle\Big]\differential{t}

(2.18)

\displaystyle\leq\phi(0)\big[\mathcal{E}(U_{0})-\langle U_{0},\Phi(0)\rangle\big]

for all
\Phi\in\mathcal{C}^{1}([0,T];\mathbbm{Y}_{\tau})

and

\phi\in\mathcal{C}^{1}_{c}([0,T))
such that
\phi\geq 0
.

Proof.

By Lemma
2.15
, we have the inequality

\mathcal{F}(U^{n},\Phi)=\mathcal{E}(U^{n})+\tau\Psi^{n}(U^{n})-\mathcal{E}(U^{n-1})-\langle U^{n}-U^{n-1},\Phi\rangle-\tau\langle A^{n}(U^{n}),\Phi\rangle\leq 0

(2.19)

for all
\Phi\in\mathbbm{Y}_{\tau}
.
For
\Phi\in\mathcal{C}^{1}([0,T];\mathbbm{Y}_{\tau})

and
\phi\in\mathcal{C}^{1}_{c}([0,T))
with
\phi\geq 0
,
we define
\phi^{n}:=\phi(t^{n})
and
\Phi^{n}=\Phi(t^{n})
.
In order to obtain (
2.18
) from the discrete formulation (
2.19
) for each
n\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
, we use
\Phi=\Phi^{n-1}
in (
2.19
),
multiply with
\phi^{n-1}\geq 0
, and sum over
n=0,\dots,N
to obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{N}\Big[\phi^{n-1}

\displaystyle\left[E^{n}-E^{n-1}+\tau\Psi^{n}(U^{n})\right]-\phi^{n-1}\langle U^{n}-U^{n-1},\Phi^{n-1}\rangle-\tau\phi^{n-1}\langle A^{n}(U^{n}),\Phi^{n-1}\rangle\Big]\leq 0.

(2.20)

Performing discrete integration by parts and dividing by
\tau>0
, we obtain

\displaystyle-\sum_{n=1}^{N}\left(\frac{\phi^{n}-\phi^{n-1}}{\tau}\right)E^{n}+\phi^{n-1}\Psi^{n}(U^{n})+\frac{\phi^{n}-\phi^{n-1}}{\tau}\langle U^{n},\Phi^{n-1}\rangle-\phi^{n}\langle U^{n},\frac{\Phi^{n}-\Phi^{n-1}}{\tau}\rangle

\displaystyle-\sum_{n=1}^{N}\phi^{n-1}\langle A^{n}(U^{n}),\Phi^{n-1}\rangle-\phi^{0}\left(E^{0}-\langle U^{0},\Phi^{0}\rangle\right)

\displaystyle\leq 0,

where we used that
\phi^{N}=\phi(T)=0
.
Inserting the definition of the prolongations, we arrive at (
2.18
).
∎

2.4.2
Convergence of approximate solutions

We now derive convergence properties
for the approximate solutions
\{(\overline{U}^{N},E^{N})\}_{N\in\mathbbm{N}}
,
which will finally allow us to pass to the limit in (
2.18
).

Lemma 2.17

(Convergence of prolongations)
.

There exist elements

U\in L^{\infty}_{w^{*}}(0,T;\mathbbm{V}^{*})\cap\mathrm{BV}([0,T];\mathbbm{Y}^{*}),\qquad E\in\mathrm{BV}([0,T])

such that the sequence
\{(\overline{U}^{N},\overline{E}^{N})\}_{N\in\mathbbm{N}}
of approximate solutions, defined in (
2.17
),
satisfies the following convergences (up to a subsequence):

\displaystyle\overline{U}^{N}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle*}}{{\rightharpoonup}}U\quad

\displaystyle\text{in }L^{\infty}_{w^{*}}(0,T;\mathbbm{V}^{*})\cap\mathrm{BV}([0,T];\mathbbm{Y}^{*}),

(2.21)

\displaystyle\overline{U}^{N}(t)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle*}}{{\rightharpoonup}}U(t)\quad

\displaystyle\text{in }\mathbbm{V}^{*}\text{ for all~}t\in[0,T],

(2.22)

\displaystyle\overline{E}^{N}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle*}}{{\rightharpoonup}}E\quad

\displaystyle\text{in }\mathrm{BV}([0,T]),

(2.23)

\displaystyle\overline{E}^{N}

\displaystyle\rightarrow{E}\quad

\displaystyle\text{strongly in }L^{1}(0,T),

(2.24)

\displaystyle\overline{E}^{N}(t)

\displaystyle\rightarrow{E}(t)\quad

\displaystyle\text{for all }t\in[0,T].

(2.25)

Proof.

To first derive suitable bounds on
(\overline{U}^{N},\overline{E}^{N})
,
we make use of the discrete inequality (
2.20
)
derived in the previous proof.
Let
k,\ell\in\{0,\dots,N\}
with
k<\ell
.
Using
\phi
with
\phi^{n-1}=1
for
k+1\leq n\leq\ell

and
\phi^{n-1}=0
else, we obtain

E^{\ell}-E^{k}+\sum_{n=k+1}^{\ell}\Big[\tau\Psi^{n}(U^{n})-\langle U^{n}-U^{n-1},\Phi^{n-1}\rangle-\tau\langle A^{n}(U^{n}),\Phi^{n-1}\rangle\Big]\leq 0.

Starting from this inequality, we can now proceed in the same way as in the proof
of Proposition
2.7

to derive a uniform bound of the form

\|\overline{E}^{N}\|_{\mathrm{BV}([0,T])}+\|\overline{U}^{N}\|_{L^{\infty}_{w^{*}}(0,T;\mathbbm{V}^{*})}+\|\overline{U}^{N}\|_{\mathrm{BV}([0,T];\mathbbm{Y}^{*})}\leq C
.
By the sequential form of the Banach–Alaoglu theorem,
we conclude the existence of limit functions
U
and
E

such that, up to a subsequence, (
2.21
) and (
2.23
) follow.
The compact embedding
\mathrm{BV}([0,T])\hookrightarrow L^{1}(0,T)
(e.g., see
[
23
, Theorem 5.5]
)
provides the convergence (
2.24
),
and (
2.25
) follows from Helly’s selection theorem.
To show (
2.22
),
we use the generalized Helly’s selection theorem
from
[
43
, Theorem B.5.10]

to obtain a (not relabeled) subsequence such that

\overline{U}^{N}(t)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle*}}{{\rightharpoonup}}U(t)
in
\mathbbm{Y}^{*}
for all
t\in[0,T]
.
Combining this convergence with the boundedness of
\{\overline{U}^{N}\}_{N\in\mathbbm{N}}

in
L^{\infty}_{w^{*}}(0,T;\mathbbm{V}^{*})
,
we can argue as in the proof of Proposition
2.8

that we even have
\overline{U}^{N}(t)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle*}}{{\rightharpoonup}}U(t)
in
\mathbbm{V}^{*}
,
which is (
2.22
).
∎

As the final step, we pass to the limit
N\to\infty
in (
2.18
)
and show
that
(U,E)
is an energy-variational solution.
The lower semicontinuity of the regularity weight
\mathcal{K}
from
(A4)

ensures that this limit passage towards the formulation (
2.4
) is possible.

Proof of Theorem
2.13
.

First of all, note that by the weak* lower semicontinuity of
{\mathcal{E}}

and the convergence properties (
2.24
)
and (
2.22
), we have

{\mathcal{E}}(U(t))\leq E(t)
for all
t\in[0,T]
.
Moreover, one readily shows that the test functions satisfy

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\hat{\Phi}^{N}

\displaystyle\rightarrow\partial_{t}\Phi,\quad

\displaystyle\underline{\Phi}^{N}

\displaystyle\rightarrow\Phi,\quad

\displaystyle\nabla\underline{\Phi}^{N}

\displaystyle\rightarrow\nabla\Phi\quad

\displaystyle\text{in }\mathbbm{Y}\text{ uniformly in }[0,T],

(2.26)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\hat{\phi}^{N}

\displaystyle\rightarrow\partial_{t}\phi,\quad

\displaystyle\overline{\phi}^{N}

\displaystyle\rightarrow\phi,\quad

\displaystyle\underline{\phi}^{N}

\displaystyle\rightarrow\phi\quad

\displaystyle\text{uniformly in }[0,T],

as
N\to\infty
.
Fix
\Phi\in\mathcal{C}^{1}([0,T];\mathbbm{Y}\cap\dom\partial{\mathcal{E}}^{*})

and

\phi\in\mathcal{C}^{1}_{c}([0,T))
such that
\phi\geq 0
.
Then
\Phi\in\mathcal{C}^{1}([0,T];\mathbbm{Y}_{\tau})
for all
\tau>0
sufficiently large.
To pass to the limit inferior in (
2.18
),
we examine each term in the inequality separately, according to its structure. For the terms where
(\overline{U}^{N},\overline{E}^{N})
appears linearly, we can directly pass to the limit by (
2.21
), (
2.23
), (
2.24
) and (
2.26
) to obtain

\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\int_{0}^{T}-\partial_{t}\hat{\phi}^{N}\left[\overline{E}^{N}-\langle\overline{U}^{N},\underline{\Phi}^{N}\rangle\right]+\overline{\phi}^{N}\langle\overline{U}^{N},\partial_{t}\hat{\Phi}^{N}\rangle\differential{t}=\int_{0}^{T}-\partial_{t}\phi\left[E-\langle U,\Phi\rangle\right]+\phi\langle U,\partial_{t}\Phi\rangle\differential{t}.

For the remaining
N
-dependent terms on the left-hand side of (
2.18
),
we make use of the lower semicontinuity induced by the regularity weight
\mathcal{K}

from
(A4)
. We first write

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\underline{\phi}^{N}\Big({\Psi}(t,\overline{U}^{N})-\langle A(t,\overline{U}^{N}),\underline{\Phi}^{N}\rangle\Big)\differential{t}

\displaystyle\quad=\int_{0}^{T}\underline{\phi}^{N}\Big({\Psi}(t,\overline{U}^{N})-\langle A(t,\overline{U}^{N}),\underline{\Phi}^{N}\rangle+\mathcal{K}(\underline{\Phi}^{N}){\mathcal{E}}(\overline{U}^{N})\Big)\differential{t}-\int_{0}^{T}\underline{\phi}^{N}\mathcal{K}(\underline{\Phi}^{N})\overline{E}^{N}\differential{t}.

The last term is linear in
\overline{E}^{N}
and we can pass to the limit by (
2.24
) using the continuity of
\mathcal{K}
.
For the other integral, observe that the integrand has an integrable lower bound since

\displaystyle\underline{\phi}^{N}\Big({\Psi}(t,\overline{U}^{N})-\langle A(t,\overline{U}^{N}),\underline{\Phi}^{N}\rangle+\mathcal{K}(\underline{\Phi}^{N}){\mathcal{E}}(\overline{U}^{N})\Big)

(2.27)

\displaystyle\geq-\|\phi\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T)}\Big(c(\underline{\Phi}^{N})({\mathcal{E}}(\overline{U}^{N})+1)+\big|\mathcal{K}(\underline{\Phi}^{N})-\tilde{\mathcal{K}}(\underline{\Phi}^{N})\big|\mathcal{E}(\overline{U}^{N})\Big)

by
(A5)
, where

c(\underline{\Phi}^{N})
,
\mathcal{K}(\underline{\Phi}^{N})
and
\tilde{\mathcal{K}}(\underline{\Phi}^{N})

can be estimated by a constant only depending
on an upper bound of
\|\Phi\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;\mathbbm{Y})}
.
Therefore, we can apply Fatou’s lemma to estimate

\displaystyle\liminf_{N\rightarrow\infty}

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\underline{\phi}^{N}\left(\Psi(t,\overline{U}^{N})-\langle A(t,\overline{U}^{N}),\underline{\Phi}^{N}\rangle+\mathcal{K}(\underline{\Phi}^{N}){\mathcal{E}}(\overline{U}^{N})\right)\differential{t}

\displaystyle\geq\int_{0}^{T}\liminf_{N\rightarrow\infty}\underline{\phi}^{N}\left(\Psi(t,\overline{U}^{N})-\langle A(t,\overline{U}^{N}),\underline{\Phi}^{N}\rangle+\mathcal{K}(\underline{\Phi}^{N}){\mathcal{E}}(\overline{U}^{N})\right)\differential{t}.

From the weak* convergence (
2.22
) pointwise in
[0,T]

and the weak* lower semicontinuity assumed in
(A4)
,
we further conclude

\displaystyle\liminf_{N\rightarrow\infty}\underline{\phi}^{N}\left(\Psi(t,\overline{U}^{N})-\langle A(t,\overline{U}^{N}),\underline{\Phi}^{N}\rangle+\mathcal{K}(\Phi^{N}){\mathcal{E}}(\overline{U}^{N})\right)\geq\phi\big(\Psi(t,U)-\langle A(t,U),\Phi\rangle+\mathcal{K}(\Phi)\mathcal{E}(U)\big)

in
[0,T]
, where we also use (
2.26
).
Combining the all these convergence results, we finally conclude
from (
2.18
)
that the limiting pair
(U,E)
satisfies the inequality

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}-\partial_{t}\phi\Big[E-\langle U,\Phi\rangle\Big]+\phi\Big[\langle U,\partial_{t}\Phi\rangle+\phi\big(\Psi(t,U)-\langle A(t,U),\Phi\rangle

\displaystyle+\mathcal{K}(\Phi)\big[{\mathcal{E}}(U)-E\big]\Big]\differential{t}

\displaystyle\leq\phi(0)\big(\mathcal{E}(U_{0})-\langle U_{0},\Phi(0)\rangle\big).

Since
\phi\in\mathcal{C}^{1}_{c}([0,T))
such that
\phi\geq 0
,
is arbitrary,
this inequality is equivalent to
the energy-variational inequality (
2.4
)
for a.a.
s<t\in[0,T]
, including
s=0
with

E(0+)-\langle U(0+),\Phi(0)\rangle=\mathcal{E}(U_{0})-\langle U_{0},\Phi(0)\rangle
,
see
[
20
, Lemma 2.5]
.
In particular, it holds
E(0+)={\mathcal{E}}(U_{0})
.
Since
\Phi\in\mathcal{C}^{1}([0,T];\mathbbm{Y}\cap\dom\partial{\mathcal{E}}^{*})
is arbitrary,
we have shown that the limit pair
(U,E)
is an energy-variational solution in the sense of Definition
2.1
with
E(0+)={\mathcal{E}}(U_{0})
.
∎

2.5
Selection criteria

While the existence of energy-variational solutions is ensured by Theorem
2.13
,
the solution set can multi-valued in general.
This motivates to introduce a additional admissibility criteria
to select solutions with certain properties.
In what follows, we assume that the assumptions
(A1)
-
(A5)
are fulfilled, so that the solution set is nonempty
due to the existence result from Theorem
2.13
.

We start with the following criterion that selects an energy-variational solution
(U,E)
in such a way that the energy defect
E-{\mathcal{E}}(U)
vanishes at countably many
selected points in time.
Note that the occurring point evaluations make sense, compare Proposition
2.8
.
A similar criterion with finitely many points was introduced in
[
39
, Prop. 6]
.

Theorem 2.18
.

Let
\{t_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbbm{N}}\subset[0,T]
be a sequence of time points such that
t_{i}<t_{i+1}
for all
i\in\mathbbm{N}
. Then there exists an energy-variational solution
(U,E)
with
{\mathcal{E}}(U(t_{i}))=E(t_{i})
for all
i\in\mathbbm{N}
.

Proof.

We construct the solution inductively. Without loss of generality, let
t_{0}=0
. Let
i\in\{0,1,\ldots\}
. For
t_{0}=0
the existence of such a solution follows from Theorem
2.13
, because
E(0+)={\mathcal{E}}(U_{0})
. Assume that there exists an energy-variational solution
(U^{n},E^{n})
in
[0,t_{i+1}]
such that
{\mathcal{E}}(U^{n}(t_{i}))=E^{n}(t_{i})
for all
i\leq n
. Next, consider
U^{n}(t_{i+1})
with
t_{i+1}>t_{i}
. Theorem
2.13
provides the existence of a solution
(\bar{U},\bar{E})
, such that
\bar{U}(t_{i+1})=U^{n}(t_{i+1})
and
{\mathcal{E}}(\bar{U}(t_{i+1}))=E^{n}(t_{i+1})
. Then the concatenation

(U^{n+1},E^{n+1}):=\begin{cases}(U^{n},E^{n})\quad&\text{if}\quad t<t_{i+1},\\
(\bar{U},\bar{E})\quad&\text{if}\quad t\geq t_{i+1},\end{cases}

is again an energy-variational solution due to Proposition
2.10
and fulfills
{\mathcal{E}}(U^{n+1}(t_{i}))=E^{n+1}(t_{i})
for all
i\leq n+1
.
∎

Although the previous selection criterion yields a solution with vanishing energy defect
at specified time points, this defect could still become large between such points.
The following result shows that one can also select an energy-variational solution
in such a way that the energy defect is smaller than any prescribed constant.
In particular, it shows that there are energy-variational solutions
that are close to weak solutions.
An analogous property was shown for dissipative weak solutions the Euler system in
[
24
, Theorem 3.1]
.

Theorem 2.19
.

For all
\epsilon>0
there exists an energy-variational solution
(U,E)
such that

{\mathcal{E}}(U(t))\leq E(t)\leq{\mathcal{E}}(U(t))+\epsilon.

Proof.

Let
(U,E)
be an energy-variational solution satisfying Theorem
2.13
. Then, let
t_{\delta}(U,E):=\sup\{t>0\;|\;E(s)\leq{\mathcal{E}}(U(s))+\delta\;\forall s\leq t\}
. To see that
t_{\delta}(U,E)>0
, let us suppose the opposite, that
t_{\delta}(U)=0
. Then, for all
t>0
, there exists
s\in(0,T)
such that
E(s)>{\mathcal{E}}(U(s))+\delta
. Let
s\searrow 0_{+}
. Then
E(s)-{\mathcal{E}}(U(s))>\delta>0
implies that
\lim_{s\to 0_{+}}E(s)-\liminf_{s\to 0_{+}}{\mathcal{E}}(U(s))\geq\delta>0
. However,
E\in\mathrm{BV}([0,T])
, and
\liminf_{s\to 0_{+}}{\mathcal{E}}(U(s))={\mathcal{E}}(U_{0})
by the same argument as in Proposition
2.9
. Therefore we get
E(0_{+})-{\mathcal{E}}(U_{0})>0
, which contradicts the initial condition. This shows that
t_{\delta}(U,E)>0
. Next, let us define an ordering such that
(U,E)\ll(V,F)
if and only if
t_{\delta}(U,E)\leq t_{\delta}(V,F)
and
(U,E)=(V,F)
on
[0,t_{\delta}(U,E)]
. The solution set
\mathcal{S}(U_{0})
endowed with the relation
\ll
is a partially ordered set. Let

(U^{1},E^{1})\ll(U^{2},E^{2})\ll\ldots\ll(U^{n},E^{n}),\quad(U^{i},E^{i})\in\mathcal{S}(U_{0})\quad\forall i=1,2,\ldots

be an ordered chain. By Proposition
2.12
, there exists a subsequence
(U^{n},E^{n})
such that
U^{n}\rightharpoonup\tilde{U}
in
L^{\infty}_{w^{*}}(0,T;\mathbbm{V}^{*})\cap\mathrm{BV}([0,T];\mathbbm{Y}^{*})
and
E^{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle*}}{{\rightharpoonup}}\tilde{E}
in
\mathrm{BV}([0,T])
. It holds that
(\tilde{U}(t),\tilde{E}(t))=(U^{n}(t),E^{n}(t))
for all
t<t_{\delta}(U^{n},E^{n})
, and for all
n=1,2,\ldots
. Now, either
t_{\delta}(\tilde{U},\tilde{E})=T
, meaning that
(\tilde{U},\tilde{E})
is an upper bound for the chain, or
t_{\delta}(\tilde{U},\tilde{E})<T
and we construct a concatenation

(U,E)(t,\cdot)=\begin{cases}(\tilde{U},\tilde{E})(t,\cdot),&\text{for }0\leq t\leq t_{\delta}(\tilde{U},\tilde{E}),\\
(\hat{U},\hat{E})(t-\tilde{T},\cdot),&\text{for }t>t_{\delta}(\tilde{U},\tilde{E}).\end{cases}

where
(\hat{U},\hat{E})
is an energy-variational solution starting at time
t_{\delta}(\tilde{U},\tilde{E})
, with initial condition
U(t_{\delta}(\tilde{U},\tilde{E}))
,
E(t_{\delta}(\tilde{U},\tilde{E}))
. Such constructed
(U,E)
is an upper bound for the chain. By Zorn’s lemma we conclude that there exists a solution in
\mathcal{S}(U_{0})
which is maximal with respect to relation
\ll
.
Either this solution, let us call it
(U,E)
, satisfies
t_{\delta}(U,E)=T
, or
t_{\delta}(U,E)<T
. The first case ends the proof, but in the latter case we can build another solution
(\tilde{U},\tilde{E})
, by concatenation as we did just above. The new solution would satisfy
(U,E)\ll(\tilde{U},\tilde{E})
, therefore contradicting the claim that
(U,E)
with
t_{\delta}(U,E)<T
was maximal.
∎

While the previous two results focused on properties of the energy defect as a criterion for selection,
one may also select solutions by minimizing or maximizing
certain functionals.
Here we make use of the structural properties of the solution set,
established in Proposition
2.11
and Proposition
2.12
.

Theorem 2.20
.

Let
U_{0}\in\dom{\mathcal{E}}
, set
X:=L^{\infty}_{w^{*}}(0,T;\mathbbm{V}^{*})\cap\mathrm{BV}([0,T];\mathbbm{Y}^{*})\times\mathrm{BV}([0,T])
, and let

\mathcal{I}\colon X\to(-\infty,\infty]

be a weakly* lower semicontinuous functional.
Then there exists an energy-variational solution
(U,E)
that minimizes the functional among all energy-variational solutions with initial value
U_{0}
and with
E(0+)={\mathcal{E}}(U_{0})
,
that is,

\mathcal{I}(U,E)=\min\big\{\mathcal{I}(V,F)\mid(V,F)\in\mathcal{S}(U_{0},{\mathcal{E}}(U_{0}))\big\}.

If
\mathcal{K}=\tilde{\mathcal{K}}
and if
\mathcal{I}
is strictly convex, then this minimizer is unique.

Proof.

The set
\mathcal{S}(U_{0},{\mathcal{E}}(U_{0}))
is non-empty by Theorem
2.13
,
and it is sequentially weakly* compact by Proposition
2.12
.
Therefore, any infimizing sequence has a limit in
S(U_{0},{\mathcal{E}}(U_{0}))
,
and by weak* lower semicontinuity of
\mathcal{I}
,
this limit is the asserted minimizer.
Moreover, if
\mathcal{K}=\tilde{\mathcal{K}}
,
then
S(U_{0},{\mathcal{E}}(U_{0}))
is convex by Proposition
2.11
,
and the strict convexity of
\mathcal{I}
implies the uniqueness of
the minimizer.
∎

The standard example would be given by an integral functional

\mathcal{I}(U,E)=\int_{0}^{T}j(t,U(t),E(t))\differential{t}

for a suitable function
j\colon[0,T]\times\mathbbm{V}^{*}\times[0,\infty)

such that
j(t,\cdot,\cdot)
is weakly* lower semicontinuous.
A simple choice would be to take
j(t,U,E)=E
or
j(t,U,E)={\mathcal{E}}(U)
,
which would lead to the minimization of the auxiliary energy variable
E

or the mechanical energy
{\mathcal{E}}(U)

in the
L^{1}(0,T)
-norm.
By minimizing the energy, we maximize the dissipation,
so that these solutions might be considered maximally dissipative in a certain sense.
Of course, combinations of these properties, like
j(t,U,E)={\mathcal{E}}(U)+E
, are possible.

A related choice that takes a different viewpoint would be the consideration
of
j(t,U,E)={\mathcal{E}}(U)-E
, the (negative) energy defect.
As seen in the existence proof in Subsection
2.4
,
the difference between
E
and
{\mathcal{E}}(U)
is due to the lack of strong convergence.
Therefore, the energy defect quantifies to some extent the
the oscillations and concentrations that make the difference between strong and weak convergence,
which may be regarded as a measure for turbulence in our fluid dynamical applications.
Minimizing the integral functional over
j(t,U,E)={\mathcal{E}}(U)-E

thus yields an energy-variational solution with maximal turbulence,
which adapts the concept from
[
34
]
,
where maximally turbulent measure-valued solutions to the Euler equations were introduced.

Since the functional
\mathcal{I}
takes into account global information in
[0,T]
,
minimal energy-variational solutions with respect to
\mathcal{I}

need no longer satisfy the semi-flow property established in Proposition
2.10
.
However, in
[
9
,
10
]
,
a semi-flow selection was established for dissipative weak solutions to the Euler equations
by successively minimizing functionals of the form

\mathcal{I}(U,E)=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda t}F(U(t),E(t))\differential{t}

for different
\lambda\in\mathbbm{R}
and a certain function
F
.
Therefore, it might be possible to transfer this approach to the current setting
of energy-variational solutions.

3
The Euler–Korteweg system

In this section we provide an example for a system for which existence of energy-variational solutions can be shown by applying Theorem
2.13
,
and which is not treatable with the existence theory developed before in
[
20
,
1
]
,
namely the Euler–Korteweg system.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbbm{R}^{d}
,
d\in\mathbbm{N}
, be a bounded Lipschitz domain and
T>0
.
We consider the Euler–Korteweg system
with a constant capillary coefficient and an adiabatic pressure law,
which reads

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho+\diiv m

\displaystyle=0\quad

\displaystyle\text{in}\;\Omega\times(0,T),

(3.1a)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}m+\diiv\left(\frac{m\otimes m}{\rho}\right)+\nabla(\rho^{\gamma})

\displaystyle=\rho\nabla(\Delta\rho)\quad

\displaystyle\text{in}\;\Omega\times(0,T),

(3.1b)

\displaystyle(\rho(0,\cdot),m(0,\cdot))

\displaystyle=(\rho_{0},m_{0})\quad

\displaystyle\text{in}\;\Omega.

(3.1c)

Here,
\rho\colon(0,T)\times\Omega\to[0,\infty)
and
m\colon(0,T)\times\Omega\to\mathbbm{R}^{d}

denote the density and the momentum of the fluid.
For simplicity, we have set all physical constants equal to
1
.
The system is equipped with impermeability boundary conditions

\displaystyle m\cdot n

\displaystyle=0{}=\nabla\rho\cdot n\quad\text{on}\quad(0,T)\times\partial\Omega.

(3.1d)

Note that the momentum equation (
3.1b
) can also be written as

\partial_{t}m+\diiv\left(\frac{m\otimes m}{\rho}\right)+\nabla(\rho^{\gamma})=\diiv\mathbbm{K}\quad\text{ in }\Omega\times(0,T)

(3.2)

with the Korteweg stress tensor

\mathbbm{K}=\Big(\diiv(\rho\nabla\rho)-\frac{1}{2}|\nabla\rho|^{2}\Big)\mathbbm{I}_{d}-\nabla\rho\otimes\nabla\rho
.

3.1
Energy-variational framework

To introduce a setting of energy-variational solutions to the Euler–Korteweg system,
we define the total energy

\mathcal{E}(\rho,m)=\int_{\Omega}\eta(\rho(x),m(x))+\frac{|\nabla\rho(x)|^{2}}{2}\differential{x},

(3.3)

where

\eta:\mathbbm{R}\times\mathbbm{R}^{d}\rightarrow[0,\infty]
is given by

\eta(\rho,m)=\begin{cases}\frac{|m|^{2}}{2\rho}+\frac{\rho^{\gamma}}{\gamma-1}\quad&\text{if}\quad\rho>0,\\
0\quad&\text{if}\quad(\rho,m)=(0,0),\\
\infty\quad&\text{else}.\end{cases}

We further define

\mathcal{V}=\{\varphi\in\mathcal{C}^{2}(\overline{\Omega};\mathbbm{R}^{d}):\varphi\cdot n=0\text{ on }\partial\Omega\}.

(3.4)

Then the regularity weight
\mathcal{K}\colon\mathcal{V}\to[0,\infty)
, which appears in the energy-variational formulation,
is chosen as

\mathcal{K}(\varphi)=2\|(\nabla\varphi)_{\mathrm{sym},-}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}+\max\{(\gamma-1,1)\}\|(\diiv\varphi)_{-}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}.

(3.5)

With these notation,
we can introduce energy-variational solutions to (
3.1
) by adapting
Definition
2.1
as follows.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
(\rho_{0},m_{0})\in L^{1}(\Omega)\times L^{1}(\Omega;\mathbbm{R}^{3})
such that
{\mathcal{E}}(\rho_{0},m_{0})<\infty
.
A triple
(\rho,m,E)\in L^{\infty}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega)\cap L^{\gamma}(\Omega))\times L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{\frac{2\gamma}{\gamma-1}}(\Omega;\mathbbm{R}^{d}))\times\mathrm{BV}([0,T])
is called an energy-variational solution to the Euler–Korteweg system (
3.1
) if
\mathcal{E}(\rho(t),m(t))\leq E(t)
for a.e.
t\in(0,T)
and if

\displaystyle\left[E-\int_{\Omega}\rho\,\psi+m\cdot\,\varphi\differential{x}\right]\bigg|_{s}^{t}

\displaystyle+\int_{s}^{t}\int_{\Omega}\rho\,\partial_{t}\psi+m\cdot\nabla\psi+m\cdot\,\partial_{t}\varphi+\left(\frac{m\otimes m}{\rho}+\rho^{\gamma}\mathbbm{I}_{d}\right):\nabla\varphi\differential{x}\differential{\tau}

(3.6)

\displaystyle+\int_{s}^{t}\int_{\Omega}\rho\nabla\rho\cdot\nabla(\diiv\varphi)+\frac{1}{2}|\nabla\rho|^{2}\diiv\varphi+\nabla\rho\otimes\nabla\rho:\nabla\varphi\differential{x}\differential{\tau}

\displaystyle+\int_{s}^{t}\mathcal{K}(\varphi)(\mathcal{E}(\rho,m)-E)\differential{\tau}\leq 0

holds for all test functions
\psi\in\mathcal{C}^{1}([0,T];\mathcal{C}^{1}(\Omega))

and
\varphi\in\mathcal{C}^{1}([0,T];\mathcal{V})
,
and for a.e.
s<t
, including
s=0
with
\rho(0)=\rho_{0},\,m(0)=m_{0}
.

One readily sees that (
3.6
) is derived
by adding the energy inequality
E\big|^{t}_{s}\leq 0

and the term with the regularity weight
to a weak formulation of (
3.1
),
which can be directly derived using the representation (
3.2
) of the momentum equation and integration by parts,
where we include the boundary conditions (
3.1d
) in a weak sense.

We apply the previously developed theory to show
the existence of energy-variational solutions to the Euler–Korteweg system.

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbbm{R}^{d}
be a bounded domain with
\mathcal{C}^{1,1}
-boundary.
Then for all initial data
(\rho_{0},m_{0})\in L^{1}(\Omega)\times L^{1}(\Omega;\mathbbm{R}^{3})
such that
\mathcal{E}(\rho_{0},m_{0})<\infty
, the system (
3.1
) admits an energy-variational solution as in Definition
3.1
, with
{\mathcal{E}}(\rho_{0},m_{0})=E(0+)
.

In order to obtain solutions by using the abstract framework introduced Section
2
,
we encode the mean value of the density
\rho
in the functional framework.
Observe that this mean value is invariant during the evolution.
Indeed, setting
s=0
,
\phi=0
and
\psi\equiv\alpha
for some constant
\alpha\in\mathbbm{R}
,
we obtain

E(t)-\alpha\int_{\Omega}\rho(t,x)\differential{x}\leq E(0)-\alpha\int_{\Omega}\rho_{0}(x)\differential{x}

for a.a.
t\in[0,T]
.
As
\alpha\in\mathbbm{R}
is arbitrary,
this implies that
\int_{\Omega}\rho\differential{x}
is constant in time.

The constraint to mean-free functions will be used for finding a regularity weight
\tilde{\mathcal{K}}

suitable to satisfy assumption
(A5)
.
In order to keep the linear structure of the spaces,
for a given initial value
\rho_{0}
, we set

\overline{\rho}:=\frac{1}{|\Omega|}\int_{\Omega}\rho_{0}(x)\differential{x},\qquad h(t,x):=\rho(t,x)-\overline{\rho},

and we show existence of solutions in the form
(\rho,m)=(h+\overline{\rho},m)
,
where
h
has vanishing mean value.

We thus consider state variables
(h,m)
and test functions
(\psi,\varphi)
in the spaces

\begin{split}\mathbbm{V}^{*}=\left(H_{(0)}^{1}(\Omega)\cap L^{\gamma}(\Omega)\right)\times L^{\frac{2\gamma}{\gamma-1}}(\Omega;\mathbbm{R}^{d}),\qquad\mathbbm{Y}=\mathcal{C}^{1}_{(0)}(\overline{\Omega})\times\mathcal{V},\end{split}

(3.7)

with
\mathcal{V}
from (
3.4
).
Here
H_{(0)}^{1}(\Omega)
and
\mathcal{C}^{1}_{(0)}(\overline{\Omega})

denote the subspaces of
H^{1}(\Omega)
and
\mathcal{C}^{1}(\overline{\Omega})

of elements with vanishing mean.
Observe that the state space
\mathbbm{V}^{*}
is the dual space of the reflexive and separable space
\mathbbm{V}=\big((H_{(0)}^{1}(\Omega))^{*}+L^{\frac{\gamma}{\gamma-1}}(\Omega)\big)\times L^{\frac{2\gamma}{\gamma+1}}(\Omega;\mathbbm{R}^{d})
.

The energy functional
\overline{{\mathcal{E}}}\colon\mathbbm{V}^{*}\to[0,\infty]
is given by

\overline{{\mathcal{E}}}(h,m):={\mathcal{E}}(h+\overline{\rho},m)=\int_{\Omega}\eta(h(x)+\overline{\rho},m(x))+\frac{|\nabla h(x)|^{2}}{2}\differential{x}

(3.8)

with
{\mathcal{E}}
from (
3.3
).
In this system the dissipation potential vanishes, that is,
\Psi\equiv 0
,
and the
operator
A\colon\dom\overline{{\mathcal{E}}}\to\mathbbm{Y}^{*}
is given by

\displaystyle\langle A(h,m),(\psi,\varphi)\rangle

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}-m\cdot\nabla\psi-\left(\frac{m\otimes m}{h+\overline{\rho}}+(h+\overline{\rho})^{\gamma}\mathbbm{I}_{d}\right):\nabla\varphi\differential{x}

(3.9)

\displaystyle+\int_{\Omega}-(h+\overline{\rho})\nabla h\cdot\nabla(\diiv\varphi)-\frac{1}{2}|\nabla h|^{2}\;\diiv\varphi-\nabla h\otimes\nabla h:\nabla\varphi\differential{x}.

Additional to the regularity weight (
3.5
),
which yields sufficient lower semicontinuity properties in the sense of assumption
(A4)
,
we need a larger regularity weight

\tilde{\mathcal{K}}:\mathcal{V}\rightarrow\mathbbm{R}

given by

\tilde{\mathcal{K}}(\varphi)=2\|(\nabla\varphi)_{\mathrm{sym},-}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}+\max\{(\gamma-1,3)\}\|(\diiv\varphi)_{-}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}+2c_{P}\|\nabla\diiv\varphi\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)},

(3.10)

in order to satisfy the convexity assumption
(A5)
.
Here,
c_{P}>0
denotes the smallest constant such that the Poincaré inequality

\forall h\in H^{1}_{(0)}(\Omega):\quad\int_{\Omega}|h|^{2}\differential{x}\leq c_{P}^{2}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla h|^{2}\differential{x}

(3.11)

is satisfied.

3.2
Existence of energy-variational solutions

To prove Theorem
3.2
, we show that
the assumptions
(A1)
–
(A5)
are satisfied,
so that we can apply the general result from Theorem
2.13
.
We first collect properties of the energy functional.

Lemma 3.3

(Properties of
{\mathcal{E}}
)
.

For
\overline{\rho}\geq 0
, let the functional
\overline{{\mathcal{E}}}\colon\mathbbm{V}^{*}\to[0,\infty]
be defined by (
3.8
).
is convex and weakly* lower semicontinuous
and there exists
c>0
such that

{\mathcal{E}}(\rho,m):=\overline{{\mathcal{E}}}(\rho-\overline{\rho},m)\geq c\Big(\|m\|_{L^{\frac{2\gamma}{\gamma+1}}(\Omega)}^{\frac{2\gamma}{\gamma+1}}+\|\rho\|^{\gamma}_{L^{\gamma}(\Omega)}+\|\nabla\rho\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\Big)

(3.12)

for all
(\rho,m)
such that
(h,m)=(\rho-\overline{\rho},m)\in\mathbbm{V}^{*}
.
In particular,
\overline{{\mathcal{E}}}
is coercive in the sense of (
2.1
), has superlinear growth in the sense of (
2.5
)
and satisfies

\dom\partial\overline{{\mathcal{E}}}^{*}=\dom\overline{{\mathcal{E}}}^{*}=\mathbbm{V}^{**}=\mathbbm{V}
.

Proof.

The functionals
{\mathcal{E}}
and
\overline{{\mathcal{E}}}
are both convex and weakly lower semicontinuous
as integral functionals with nonnegative convex integrand.
Since
\mathbbm{V}
is reflexive, this also implies weak* lower semicontinuity.
To show (
3.12
), let
q=\frac{\gamma+1}{\gamma}
with
q^{\prime}=\gamma+1
.
If
\rho=h+\overline{\rho}>0
, then Young’s inequality implies

\displaystyle{\mathcal{E}}(\rho,m)

\displaystyle-\frac{\|\rho\|^{\gamma}_{L^{\gamma}(\Omega)}}{2(\gamma-1)}-\frac{1}{2}\|\nabla\rho\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}=\int_{\Omega}\frac{|m|^{2}}{2\rho}+\frac{\rho^{\gamma}}{2(\gamma-1)}\differential{x}=\int_{\Omega}\Big(\frac{|m|^{2}}{2\rho}\Big)^{\frac{\gamma}{\gamma+1}q}+\Big(\frac{\rho}{(2(\gamma-1))^{\frac{1}{\gamma}}}\Big)^{\frac{\gamma}{\gamma+1}q^{\prime}}\differential{x}

\displaystyle\geq c(\gamma)\int_{\Omega}\Big(\frac{|m|^{2}}{2\rho}\,\frac{\rho}{(2(\gamma-1))^{\frac{1}{\gamma}}}\Big)^{\frac{\gamma}{\gamma+1}}\differential{x}=c(\gamma)\int_{\Omega}|m|^{\frac{2\gamma}{\gamma+1}}\differential{x}=c(\gamma)\|m\|_{L^{\frac{2\gamma}{\gamma+1}}(\Omega)}^{\frac{2\gamma}{\gamma+1}}

for some constant
c(\gamma)>0
.
This shows (
3.12
) for
\rho>0
.
The other cases can be treated similarly or are trivial.
In particular,
{\mathcal{E}}
satisfies (
2.1
) and (
2.5
) and, in accordance with Proposition

,
we conclude
\dom\overline{{\mathcal{E}}}^{*}=\mathbbm{V}^{**}=\mathbbm{V}
by reflexivity of
\mathbbm{V}
.
∎

In the next lemma, we consider weak solutions to the problem

\displaystyle\Delta\rho+\frac{\gamma}{\gamma-1}|\rho|^{\gamma-2}\rho+\lambda

\displaystyle=g

\displaystyle\text{in }\Omega,

\displaystyle n\cdot\nabla\rho

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\text{on }\partial\Omega,

with the constraint
\lambda\in\partial\mathcal{G}(\rho)
, where

\mathcal{G}\colon H^{1}(\Omega)\cap L^{\gamma}(\Omega)\to[0,\infty],\qquad\mathcal{G}(\rho)=\int_{\Omega}\iota_{[0,\infty)}\differential{x}.

(3.13)

This is a step towards identifying elements of the subdifferential of the energy
\overline{{\mathcal{E}}}
,
which is necessary for checking the validity of the assumption
(A3)
.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbbm{R}^{d}
be a bounded domain with
\mathcal{C}^{1,1}
-boundary,
and let

g\in C(\overline{\Omega})
. Then there exists a unique
\rho\in H^{1}(\Omega)\cap L^{\gamma}(\Omega),\rho\geq 0
such that

\int_{\Omega}\nabla\rho\cdot\nabla\varphi+\Big(\frac{\gamma}{\gamma-1}\rho^{\gamma-1}+\lambda\Big)\varphi\differential{x}=\int_{\Omega}g\varphi\differential{x}

(3.14)

for all
\varphi\in H^{1}(\Omega)\cap L^{\gamma}(\Omega)
,
where
\lambda\in\partial\mathcal{G}(\rho)
with
\mathcal{G}
from (
3.13
)
Moreover,

\lambda\in L^{p}(\Omega)
and
\rho\in W^{2,p}
for any
1\leq p<\infty
,
and
\lambda(x)\in\partial\iota_{[0,\infty)}(\rho(x))
for a.a.
x\in\Omega
.

Proof.

The weak formulation (
3.14
) can be regarded as the Euler–Lagrange equations
for the functional
\mathcal{D}=\mathcal{F}+\mathcal{G}
with

\mathcal{F}(\rho)=\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\rho|^{2}+\frac{1}{\gamma-1}\rho^{\gamma}-g\rho\differential{x}.

Since
\mathcal{D}
is strictly convex, proper and lower semicontinuous,
it has a unique minimizer
\rho\in H^{1}(\Omega)\cap L^{\gamma}(\Omega)
,
which yields the existence of a unique weak solution to (
3.14
).

To show the increased integrability,
we use the Yosida approximation for
\iota_{[0,\infty)}(\rho)
, defined as

\displaystyle f_{\epsilon}(\rho)=\inf_{\eta\in\mathbbm{R}}\{\frac{1}{2\epsilon}|\eta-\rho|^{2}+\iota_{[0,\infty)}(\rho)\}=\inf_{\eta\in\mathbbm{R},\eta\geq 0}\{\frac{1}{2\epsilon}|\eta-\rho|^{2}\}=\begin{cases}0\quad&\text{if }\rho\geq 0,\\
\frac{1}{2\epsilon}|\rho|^{2}\quad&\text{if }\rho<0.\end{cases}

We first consider a regularized version of the problem, given by the weak formulation

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\nabla\rho_{\epsilon}\cdot\nabla\varphi+\Big(\frac{\gamma}{\gamma-1}|\rho_{\epsilon}|^{\gamma-2}\rho_{\epsilon}+f^{\prime}_{\epsilon}(\rho_{\epsilon})\Big)\varphi\differential{x}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}g\varphi\differential{x}

(3.15)

for all
\varphi\in H^{1}(\Omega)\cap L^{\gamma}(\Omega)
.
From
[
48
, Theorem 4.7]
it follows that there exists
\rho_{\epsilon}\in H^{1}(\Omega)\cap L^{\infty}(\Omega)
that satisfies (
3.15
).
To obtain
a priori
estimates on
f^{\prime}_{\epsilon}(\rho_{\epsilon})
, we test the equation (
3.15
) with
f^{\prime}_{\epsilon}|f^{\prime}_{\epsilon}|^{p-2}
. Since

f^{\prime}_{\epsilon}(\rho(x))=\begin{cases}0,&\text{if }\rho(x)\geq 0,\\[5.69054pt]
\frac{\rho(x)}{\epsilon},&\text{if }\rho(x)<0,\end{cases}

we see that

is a viable test function. After a short calculation, we obtain

\begin{split}\int_{\Omega}\Big[(p-1)|f^{\prime}_{\epsilon}(\rho_{\epsilon})|^{p-2}f^{{}^{\prime\prime}}_{\epsilon}(\rho_{\epsilon})\Big]|\nabla\rho_{\epsilon}|^{2}+\Big(\frac{\gamma}{\gamma+1}|\rho_{\epsilon}|^{\gamma-2}\rho_{\epsilon}&+f^{\prime}_{\epsilon}(\rho_{\epsilon})\Big)|f^{\prime}_{\epsilon}(\rho_{\epsilon})|^{p-2}f^{\prime}_{\epsilon}(\rho_{\epsilon})\differential{x}\\
&=\int_{\Omega}g\,|f^{\prime}_{\epsilon}(\rho_{\epsilon})|^{p-2}f^{\prime}_{\epsilon}(\rho_{\epsilon})\differential{x}.\end{split}

Due to

and

all terms on the left-hand side are nonnegative.
Omitting the first and the second term, we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}|f^{\prime}_{\epsilon}(\rho_{\epsilon})|^{p}\differential{x}\leq\int_{\Omega}g\,|f^{\prime}_{\epsilon}(\rho_{\epsilon})|^{p-2}f^{\prime}_{\epsilon}(\rho_{\epsilon})\differential{x}\leq\frac{1}{p}\|g\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}^{p}+\frac{p-1}{p}\|f^{\prime}_{\epsilon}(\rho_{\epsilon})\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}^{p}

by Hölder’s and Young’s inequality,
which implies the uniform bound

\displaystyle\|f^{\prime}_{\epsilon}(\rho_{\epsilon})\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}^{p}

\displaystyle\leq\|g\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}^{p}.

(3.16)

To derive uniform estimates of
\rho_{\epsilon}
in

first consider

.
We test (

This yields

for

and

by Hölder’s and Young’s inequalities.
Again, all terms on the left-hand side are nonnegative,
and we can omit the first and the third one.
Choosing

sufficiently small reveals

for some

.
As

To derive

By the uniform bounds (

we can pass to a subsequence such that

in

Moreover, by (

Letting

4
Binormal curvature flow

In this section, we apply the general theory to the binormal curvature flow,
which describes the motion of a curve in the three-dimensional space
in the direction of the binormal vector, at a speed proportional to the curvature.
Consider a closed curve with (time-dependent) arc-length parametrization
\gamma:[0,T]\times\mathbbm{R}\to\mathbbm{R}^{3}
,
that is,
|\partial_{s}\gamma(t,s)|=1

and
\gamma(t,s+L)=\gamma(t,s)
for
(t,s)\in[0,T]\times\mathbbm{R}
,
where
L>0
is the length of the curve.
The curve evolves according to binormal curvature flow
if
\gamma
is subject to

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\gamma=\partial_{s}\gamma\times\partial_{ss}\gamma\quad\text{ in }[0,T]\times\mathbbm{R}.

(4.1)

The binormal curvature flow describes the asymptotic evolution of vortex rings according to the incompressible Euler equations.
We refer to
[
32
]
and
[
31
]
for
more details on this connection,
and an introduction into the general topic
and the associated generalized solution concepts.
By studying the binormal curvature flow in terms of energy-variational solutions,
we treat them in the same solution framework as the Euler equations,
compare
[
20
]
.
Such a combination of both systems can be beneficial,
for instance, for investigating the singular limit.

We note that the solution framework introduced below is less regular than the one considered by Jerrad and Smets
[
32
]
.
Firstly, we consider general divergence-free measures in the context of energy-varatiational solutions, which constitute a convex superset of the integral 1-currents used in
[
32
]
.
In other words, in the proposed framework we lose a certain level of regularity of the solution, especially the
1
-rectifiability of the curves.
Secondly, we have the restriction on the class of test functions
since the energy functional has only linear growth,
compare Remark
2.3
.
Omitting this restriction, we further show that the concept of energy-variational solutions is selective enough to retain a weak-strong uniqueness principle in the same spirit as in
[
32
]
.

4.1
Energy-variational framework

To derive a suitable energy-variational formulation of the binormal curvature flow (
4.1
),
we first consider a weak formulation that was first introduced in
[
33
]
.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
\gamma\colon[0,T]\times\mathbbm{T}^{1}\to\mathbbm{R}^{3}
be a smooth solution to (
4.1
),
where
\mathbbm{T}^{1}:=\mathbbm{R}/\ell\mathbbm{Z}
for some
\ell>0
.
Then for all
X\in\mathcal{C}^{1}([0,T];\mathcal{C}^{2}(\mathbbm{R}^{3};\mathbbm{R}^{3}))
it holds

\Big[\int_{\gamma_{\sigma}}X\cdot\tau_{\gamma_{\sigma}}\,\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{1}\Big]\Big|_{\sigma=0}^{t}=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\gamma_{\sigma}}\partial_{t}X\cdot\tau_{\gamma_{\sigma}}\,\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{1}\,\mathrm{d}\sigma-\int_{\gamma_{\sigma}}\nabla(\nabla\times X):\tau_{\gamma_{\sigma}}\otimes\tau_{\gamma_{\sigma}}\,\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{1}\,\mathrm{d}\sigma

(4.2)

for all
t\in[0,T]
, where

\gamma_{\sigma}:=\gamma(\sigma,\cdot)
for
\sigma\in[0,T]
and
\tau_{\gamma_{\sigma}}
is the oriented tangent vector at
\gamma_{\sigma}
.

Proof.

The result follows directly from
[
32
, Lemma 1]
.
∎

Since (
4.2
) only involves first-order spatial derivatives of
\gamma
in terms of the tangent vector
\tau_{t}
,
this identity may serve as the basis of a weak formulation of (
4.1
),
compare
[
33
,
32
]
.
Moreover, a straightforward generalization of (
4.2
) is to
consider regular vector measures
\mu\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbbm{R}^{3};\mathbbm{R}^{3})
instead of curves.
For those, the tangent field
\tau_{\mu}=\frac{\differential\mu}{\differential|\mu|}

is defined as the Radon–Nikodym derivative of
\mu
with respect to
|\mu|
,
and the weak form (
4.2
) is replaced with

\displaystyle\Big[\int_{\mathbbm{R}^{3}}X\cdot\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{\sigma}\Big]\Big|_{\sigma=0}^{t}=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbbm{R}^{3}}\partial_{t}X\cdot\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{\sigma}\,\mathrm{d}\sigma-\nabla(\nabla\times X):\tau_{\mu_{\sigma}}\otimes\tau_{\mu_{\sigma}}\,\mathrm{d}|\mu_{\sigma}|\,\mathrm{d}\sigma\,

(4.3)

for
X\in\mathcal{C}^{1}([0,T];\mathcal{C}^{2}(\mathbbm{R}^{3};\mathbbm{R}^{3}))
,
where
\mu_{\sigma}=\mu(\sigma)\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbbm{R}^{3};\mathbbm{R}^{3})
.
Observe that a curve pameterized by
\gamma\colon\mathbbm{T}^{1}\to\mathbbm{R}^{3}
can be identified with the Radon measure
\mu=\tau_{\gamma}\mathcal{H}^{1}\lfloor_{M}
,
where
M=\gamma(\mathbbm{T}^{1})
,
that is,

\forall X\in\mathcal{C}_{0}(\mathbbm{R}^{3};\mathbbm{R}^{3}):\ \int_{\mathbbm{R}^{3}}X\cdot\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{\gamma}=\int_{M}X\cdot\tau_{\gamma}\,\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{1}=\int_{\mathbbm{T}^{1}}X(\gamma_{t}(s))\cdot\partial_{s}\gamma_{t}(s)\,\mathrm{d}s.

(4.4)

In virtue of this identification, the formuation (
4.3
) is consistent with (
4.2
)
in the following sense.

Lemma 4.2

(Consistency with smooth solutions)
.

Let
\gamma\colon[0,T]\times\mathbbm{T}^{1}\to\mathbbm{R}^{3}
be a smooth solution to (
4.1
).
Set
M_{t}=\gamma_{t}(\mathbbm{T}^{1})
for
t\in[0,T]
,
and define the measure
\mu_{\gamma_{t}}=\tau_{\gamma_{t}}\mathcal{H}^{1}\lfloor_{M_{t}}\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbbm{R}^{3};\mathbbm{R}^{3})
.
Then
\mu_{\gamma}
fulfills (
4.3
)
for all
X\in\mathcal{C}^{1}([0,T];\mathcal{C}_{c}^{2}(\mathbbm{R}^{3};\mathbbm{R}^{3}))
and
t\in[0,T]
.

Proof.

This follows directly from Lemma
4.1
and the previous considerations.
∎

The existence of weak solutions defined through (
4.2
) or (
4.3
) is unknown,
which motivated the study of a more generalized solution concept in
[
32
]

based on the notion of varifolds.
We follow here a different direction and introduce energy-variational solutions
by adapting Definition
2.1
to the current framework.

To obtain a Banach space that is suitable for our analysis,
it is natural to consider divergence-free measures
\mu\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbbm{R}^{3};\mathbbm{R}^{3})
.
This class is the weak* closure of the linear span
of the set of closed oriented curves, if we identify them with measures in the sense of (
4.4
).
Moreover, an oriented curve is closed if and only if
the integral over any gradient field along the curve vanishes,
which means that the associated measure is divergence free.

As the underlying space, we consider the closure of all smooth and compactly supported
divergence-free vector fields, given by

\mathbbm{V}:=\overline{\{\varphi\in\mathcal{C}_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbbm{R}^{3};\mathbbm{R}^{3})\mid\diiv\varphi=0\}}^{\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{C}_{0}(\mathbbm{R}^{3};\mathbbm{R}^{3})}}\subset\mathcal{C}_{0}(\mathbbm{R}^{3};\mathbbm{R}^{3}).

Then the state space is given by the class of divergence-free Radon measures.

Proposition 4.3
.

The dual space of
\mathbbm{V}
can be identified with

\mathbbm{V}^{*}=\{\mu\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbbm{R}^{3};\mathbbm{R}^{3})\mid\diiv\mu=0\text{ in the sense of distributions}\}.

Proof.

One the one hand, it is clear that any
\mu\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbbm{R}^{3};\mathbbm{R}^{3})
with
\diiv\mu=0

defines a linear functional on
\mathbbm{V}
through
\varphi\mapsto\int_{\mathbbm{R}^{3}}\varphi\cdot\differential{\mu}
.
On the other hand, let
\ell\in\mathbbm{V}^{*}
, and define the class of the gradient fields by
\mathbbm{G}=\{\nabla\psi\mid\psi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbbm{R}^{3})\}
. Since
\mathbbm{G}\cap\mathbbm{V}=\{0\}
,
the Hahn–Banach theorem guarantees the existence of an extension
\tilde{\ell}\in\mathcal{\mathcal{C}}_{0}(\mathbbm{R}^{3};\mathbbm{R}^{3})^{*}
such that
\tilde{\ell}(\varphi+\xi)=\ell(\varphi)

for all
\varphi\in\mathbbm{V}
and
\xi\in\mathbbm{G}
.
By the Riesz representation theorem,
\tilde{\ell}
is given by a measure
\mu
,
and we conclude that
\mu
is divergence-free.
∎

On
\mathbbm{V}^{*}
we define the energy

\mathcal{E}:\mathbbm{V}^{*}\to[0,\infty]
via

\mathcal{E}(\mu):=\int_{\mathbbm{R}^{3}}\,\mathrm{d}|\mu|=\|\mu\|_{\mathcal{M}(\mathbbm{R}^{3};\mathbbm{R}^{3})},

(4.5)

and we have
\dom{\mathcal{E}}=\mathbbm{V}^{*}
.
The space
\mathbbm{Y}
of test functions is given by

\mathbbm{Y}:=\{\varphi\in\mathcal{C}_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbbm{R}^{3};\mathbbm{R}^{3})\mid\diiv\varphi=0\}

The dissipation potential is trivial, that is,
\Psi\equiv 0
, and
the operator
A:\dom{\mathcal{E}}\to\mathbbm{Y}^{*}
is given by

\displaystyle\langle A(\mu),\varphi\rangle:=\int_{\mathbbm{R}^{3}}\nabla(\nabla\times\mathbf{\varphi}):\tau_{\mu}\otimes\tau_{\mu}\,\mathrm{d}|\mu|\,,

(4.6)

with tangent field
\tau_{\mu}=\frac{\,\mathrm{d}\mu}{\,\mathrm{d}|\mu|}
.
Now, we are in the position to transfer the general definition of energy-variational solutions (Definition
2.1
) to the current context.
As is shown below, a suitable regularity weight to ensure existence is given by

\mathcal{K}(\mathbf{\varphi}):=3\|(\nabla(\nabla\times\mathbf{\varphi}))_{\mathrm{sym}}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbbm{R}^{3};\mathbbm{R}^{3\times 3})}.

(4.7)

Definition 4.4
.

Let
\mu_{0}\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbbm{R}^{3};\mathbbm{R}^{3})
with
\diiv\mu_{0}=0
.
We call
(\mu,E)\in L^{\infty}_{w^{*}}(0,T;\mathcal{M}(\mathbbm{R}^{3};\mathbbm{R}^{3}))\times\mathrm{BV}([0,T])
an energy-variational solution to the binormal curvature flow
with initial value
\mu_{0}
if
E\geq{\mathcal{E}}(\mu)
a.e. in
(0,T)
and if

\displaystyle\begin{split}\left[E-\int_{\mathbbm{R}^{3}}\mathbf{\varphi}\cdot\,\mathrm{d}\mu\right]\Big|_{s}^{t}+\int_{s}^{t}\int_{\mathbbm{R}^{3}}\partial_{t}\mathbf{\varphi}\cdot\,\mathrm{d}\mu-\int_{\mathbbm{R}^{3}}\nabla(\nabla\times\mathbf{\varphi}):\tau_{\mu}\otimes\tau_{\mu}\,\mathrm{d}|\mu|\,\mathrm{d}\tau\\
+\int_{s}^{t}3\|(\nabla(\nabla\times\mathbf{\varphi}))_{\mathrm{sym}}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbbm{R}^{3};\mathbbm{R}^{3\times 3})}\left[\int_{\mathbbm{R}^{3}}\,\mathrm{d}|\mu|-E\right]\,\mathrm{d}\tau\leq 0\end{split}

(4.8)

for all
\mathbf{\varphi}\in\mathcal{C}^{1}([0,T];\mathcal{C}^{2}_{0}(\mathbbm{R}^{3};\mathbbm{R}^{3}))
with
\diiv\varphi=0
and

|\varphi|\leq 1
,
and for a.e.
0\leq s<t\leq T
, including
s=0
with
\mu(0)=\mu_{0}
.

Remark 4.5
.

We only consider test functions
\varphi
with
|\varphi|\leq 1
in Definition
4.4
.
This constraint corresponds to the restriction in Definition
2.1

that the test functions take values in
\mathbbm{Y}\cap\dom\partial{\mathcal{E}}^{*}
,
see Lemma
4.7
below.
Further note that the formulation (
4.8
) is invariant under addition of gradient fields
\nabla\psi
to the test functions
since
\mu
is divergence-free and
\nabla\times\nabla\psi=0
,
so that a larger class of functions would be admissible.
For instance, one may consider compactly supported test functions

\varphi\in\mathcal{C}^{1}([0,T];\mathcal{C}^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbbm{R}^{3};\mathbbm{R}^{3}))

with Helmholtz decomposition
\varphi=\nabla\times\chi+\nabla\psi

such that
|\nabla\times\chi|\leq 1
.
Note that the Helmholtz decomposition is indeed available for
\varphi\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbbm{R}^{3};\mathbbm{R}^{3})
,
and one can construct
\chi\in\mathcal{C}_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbbm{R}^{3};\mathbbm{R}^{3})
and
\psi\in\mathcal{C}_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbbm{R}^{3})
by the vector potentials

\chi(x)=\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{\mathbbm{R}^{3}}\frac{\nabla\times\varphi(y)}{|x-y|}\differential{y},\qquad\psi(x)=-\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{\mathbbm{R}^{3}}\frac{\diiv\varphi(y)}{|x-y|}\differential{y}.

4.2
Existence of energy-variational solutions

The abstract theory developed before leads to the following existence theorem.

Theorem 4.6
.

Let
\mu_{0}\in\mathbbm{V}^{*}
such that
{\mathcal{E}}(\mu_{0})<\infty
. Then there exists an energy-variational solution in the sense of Definition
4.4
with
E(0)=\int_{\mathbbm{R}^{3}}\,\mathrm{d}|\mu_{0}|
.

In order to apply the general result from Theorem
2.13
,
we verify necessary assumptions
(A1)
–
(A5)

in the following two lemmata.

Lemma 4.7

(Properties of the energy functional)
.

Let the functional
\mathcal{E}:\mathbbm{V}^{*}\to[0,\infty]
be defined by (
4.5
).
Then
{\mathcal{E}}
is convex and weakly* lower semicontinuous and trivially satisfies

{\mathcal{E}}(\mu)\geq\|\mu\|_{\mathbbm{V}^{*}}
.
Moreover, for all
\xi\in\mathbbm{V}^{**}
it holds

{\mathcal{E}}^{*}(\xi)=\begin{cases}0&\text{if }\|\xi\|_{\mathbbm{V}^{**}}\leq 1,\\
\infty&\text{else.}\end{cases}

(4.9)

In particular,
\dom{\mathcal{E}}^{*}=\dom\partial{\mathcal{E}}^{*}
,
and

0\in\partial{\mathcal{E}}^{*}(\xi)
for all
\xi\in\dom\partial{\mathcal{E}}^{*}
,
so that
for any
\varphi\in\mathbbm{Y}\cap\dom\partial{\mathcal{E}}^{*}
there exists an element

\mu\in\partial{\mathcal{E}}^{*}(\mathbf{\varphi})
(namely
\mu=0
) such that

\langle A(\mu),\varphi\rangle=0
.

Proof.

As
{\mathcal{E}}=\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{M}(\mathbbm{R}^{3};\mathbbm{R}^{3})}
on the weakly* closed subspace
\mathbbm{V}^{*}
,
the convexity and weak* lower semicontinuity follow directly,
and the coercivity estimate is trivial.
The identification of
{\mathcal{E}}^{*}

is a standard example from calculus of variations,
and (
4.9
) directly implies the remaining assertions.
∎

To show that the choice of the regularity weight
\mathcal{K}
as in (
4.7
)
is suitable, we use the following convexity result.

Lemma 4.8
.

Let
\mathbbm{M}\in\mathbbm{R}^{d\times d}_{\mathrm{sym}}
,
d\in\mathbbm{N}
, be a symmetric matrix,
and let
\lambda_{m},\lambda_{M}\in\mathbbm{R}
be the smallest and the largest eigenvalue of
\mathbbm{M}
, respectively.
For
\gamma\in\mathbbm{R}
consider the function

f\colon\mathbbm{R}^{d}\to\mathbbm{R},\quad f(\xi)=\gamma|\xi|+\frac{\mathbbm{M}\xi\cdot\xi}{|\xi|},

where we set
f(0)=0
.
Then
f
is convex if and only if
\gamma\geq\lambda_{M}-2\lambda_{m}
.

Proof.

We follow the proof of
[
15
, Theorem 1.2]
,
where a similar function on the space
\mathbbm{R}^{2\times 2}
was considered.
To study convexity, we first compute the second-order derivatives of
f
.
As
f
is continuous, it suffices to consider
\xi\neq 0
.
We have

\displaystyle\partial_{j}f(\xi)

\displaystyle=\gamma\frac{\xi_{j}}{|\xi|}+\frac{2(\mathbbm{M}\xi)_{j}}{|\xi|}-\frac{(\mathbbm{M}\xi\cdot\xi)\xi_{j}}{|\xi|^{3}},

\displaystyle\partial_{j}\partial_{k}f(\xi)

\displaystyle=\gamma\frac{\delta_{jk}}{|\xi|}-\gamma\frac{\xi_{j}\xi_{k}}{|\xi|^{3}}+\frac{2\mathbbm{M}_{jk}}{|\xi|}-\frac{4(\mathbbm{M}\xi)_{j}\xi_{k}+(\mathbbm{M}\xi\cdot\xi)\delta_{jk}}{|\xi|^{3}}+\frac{3(\mathbbm{M}\xi\cdot\xi)\xi_{j}\xi_{k}}{|\xi|^{5}}.

Hence,
f
is convex if and only if for all
\xi\neq 0
and
\mu\in\mathbbm{R}^{d}
the quantity

\displaystyle\sum_{j,k=1}^{d}\partial_{j}\partial_{k}f(\xi)\mu_{j}\mu_{k}

\displaystyle=\gamma\Big(\frac{|\mu|^{2}}{|\xi|}-\frac{(\xi\cdot\mu)^{2}}{|\xi|^{2}}\Big)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{|\xi|^{5}}\Big(2|\xi|^{4}(\mathbbm{M}\mu\cdot\mu)-4|\xi|^{2}(\mathbbm{M}\xi\cdot\mu)(\xi\cdot\mu)-|\xi|^{2}(\mathbbm{M}\xi\cdot\xi)|\mu|^{2}+3(\mathbbm{M}\xi\cdot\xi)(\xi\cdot\mu)^{2}\Big)

is nonnegative.
As
\nabla^{2}f
is homogeneous of degree
-1
, it suffices to consider
|\xi|=1
.
We decompose
\mu=\alpha\xi+\beta\eta
with
s,t\in\mathbbm{R}
and
|\eta|=1
such that
\xi\cdot\eta=0
.
Then it holds

\displaystyle|\mu|^{2}=\alpha^{2}+\beta^{2},\quad\xi\cdot\mu=\alpha,\quad\mathbbm{M}\xi\cdot\mu=\alpha(\mathbbm{M}\xi\cdot\xi)+\beta(\mathbbm{M}\xi\cdot\eta),

\displaystyle M\mu\cdot\mu=\alpha^{2}(\mathbbm{M}\xi\cdot\xi)+2\alpha\beta(\mathbbm{M}\xi\cdot\eta)+\beta^{2}(\mathbbm{M}\eta\cdot\eta),

and we have

\sum_{j,k=1}^{d}\partial_{j}\partial_{k}f(\xi)\mu_{j}\mu_{k}=\beta^{2}\big(\gamma+2\mathbbm{M}\eta\cdot\eta-\mathbbm{M}\xi\cdot\xi\big).

Therefore,
f
is convex if and only if

\gamma+2\inf_{|\eta|=1}\mathbbm{M}\eta\cdot\eta-\sup_{|\xi|=1}\mathbbm{M}\xi\cdot\xi\geq 0.

As
\mathbbm{M}
is symmetric, this corresponds to the asserted characterization.
∎

Lemma 4.9

(Regularity weight)
.

For every
\varphi\in\mathbbm{Y}
, the mapping
\mu\mapsto-\langle A(\mu),\mathbf{\varphi}\rangle+\mathcal{K}(\mathbf{\varphi}){\mathcal{E}}(\mu)

is nonnegative, convex and weakly* lower semicontinuous in
\mathbbm{V}^{*}
.

Proof.

We rewrite the mapping as

\displaystyle\mu\mapsto\int_{\mathbbm{R}^{3}}-\nabla(\nabla\times\mathbf{\varphi}):\tau_{\mu}\otimes\tau_{\mu}+3\|(\nabla(\nabla\times\mathbf{\varphi}))_{\mathrm{sym}}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbbm{R}^{3};\mathbbm{R}^{3\times 3})}\,\mathrm{d}|\mu|=\int_{\mathbbm{R}^{3}}f(x,\tau_{\mu}(x))\,\mathrm{d}|\mu|(x)

with

f(x,\xi):=-\frac{[\nabla(\nabla\times\varphi)(x)]_{\mathrm{sym}}:\xi\otimes\xi}{|\xi|}+3\|(\nabla(\nabla\times\varphi))_{\mathrm{sym}}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbbm{R}^{3};\mathbbm{R}^{3\times 3})}|\xi|.

For this identity, we use that
|\tau_{\mu}|=1
and that
\tau_{\mu}\otimes\tau_{\mu}
is symmetric.
The function
f
is continuous, and it is
1
-homogeneous with respect to
\xi
.
Moreover,
f(x,\cdot)
is convex by Lemma
4.8
.
By a version of Reshetnyak’s semicontinuity theorem,
see
[
3
, Proposition 2.37]
for instance,
this implies the asserted convexity and weak* lower semicontinuity.
The nonnegativity trivial.
∎

We can now conclude the existence of energy-variational solutions to the binormal curvature flow
from Theorem
2.13
.

Proof of Theorem
4.6
.

In order to apply the general existence theorem (Theorem
2.13
) for this setting,
we verify the assumptions
(A1)
–
(A5)
.
These follow from the previous lemmata with the choices
\Psi=0
and
\mathcal{K}=\tilde{\mathcal{K}}
.
∎

4.3
Weak-strong uniqueness principle

Although the concept of energy-variational solutions
seems to be less restrictive than the generalized solutions from
[
32
,
31
]
, we can still adapt the for weak-strong uniqueness from
[
32
, Theorem 2]
to our setting, making it a reasonable concept.
However, we have to slightly modify the definition of energy-variational solutions
by omitting the norm restriction on the admissible test functions
that appears in Definition
4.4
.

Recall the identification of a curve
\gamma

with a measure
\mu_{\gamma}
from (
4.4
).

Theorem 4.10

(Weak-strong uniqueness)
.

Let
L>0
and
\gamma:[0,T]\times\mathbbm{T}^{1}\rightarrow\mathbbm{R}^{3}
denote a smooth classical solution of the binormal curvature flow without self-intersection,
that is, the arc-length parameterization
\gamma_{t}:=\gamma(t,\cdot)
solves (
4.1
) and is injective for any
t\in[0,T]
.
Let
(\mu,E)
be an energy-variational solution to the binormal curvature flow on
[0,T]

with
E(0+)={\mathcal{E}}(\mu_{0})
and
such that (
4.8
) holds
for all
\varphi\in\mathcal{C}^{1}([0,T];\mathcal{C}^{2}_{c}(\mathbbm{R}^{3};\mathbbm{R}^{3}))
and for a.e.
0\leq s<t\leq T
.
If the initial values of
\gamma
and
\mu
coincide
in the sense that
\mu_{\gamma_{0}}=\mu_{0}
,
then
\mu_{\gamma_{t}}=\mu_{t}
for a.a
t\in[0,T]
.

To show this result, we strongly follow
the proof of
[
32
, Theorem 2]
. We only need to adapt the concluding argument a little.
As one ingredient,
we use the following elementary result
on divergence-free vector measures
supported on a curve.

Lemma 4.11
.

Let
M\subset\mathbbm{R}^{3}
be a closed, connected
\mathcal{C}^{1}
-curve
with arc-length parametrization
\gamma:\mathbbm{T}^{1}\to M
.
Let

\mu=\theta\,\tau_{\gamma}\,\mathcal{H}^{1}\lfloor_{M}

with
\theta\in L^{1}(M)
. If
\operatorname{div}\mu=0
in
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\mathbbm{R}^{3})
, then
\theta
is constant a.e. on
M
.

Proof.

For any
\varphi\in\mathcal{C}_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbbm{R}^{3})
the assumption
\diiv\mu=0
yields

0=\langle\operatorname{div}\mu,\varphi\rangle=-\langle\mu,\nabla\varphi\rangle=-\int_{M}\theta(x)\,\tau_{\gamma}(x)\cdot\nabla\varphi(x)\,\differential\mathcal{H}^{1}(x).

With the arc-length parameter
s\in\mathbbm{T}^{1}
, such that
x=\gamma(s)
and
\differential\mathcal{H}^{1}(x)=\differential s
, this gives

0=-\int_{\mathbbm{T}^{1}}\theta(\gamma(s))\,\frac{\differential}{\differential s}\big(\varphi(\gamma(s))\big)\,\differential s=\int_{\mathbbm{T}^{1}}\frac{\differential}{\differential s}\theta(\gamma(s))\,\varphi(\gamma(s))\,\differential s

by integration by parts.
Thus
(\theta\circ\gamma)^{\prime}=0
in
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\mathbbm{T}^{1})
, whence
\theta
is constant a.e. on
M
.
∎

We further introduce some tools from
[
32
]
.
For
\gamma=(\gamma_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
as in Theorem
4.10
,
we set

r\equiv r(\gamma):=\frac{1}{2}\min_{t\in[0,T]}\min\left(\left\|\partial_{ss}\gamma_{t}\right\|_{\infty}^{-1},r_{s}(t)\right)>0.

Here, the security radius
r_{s}(t)
,
t\in[0,T]
, is defined as the largest positive real number with the property that every point
x\in\mathbbm{R}^{3}
with
\operatorname{dist}\left(x,\gamma(t)\right)<r_{s}(t)
has a unique closest point
P_{t}(x)
on
\gamma_{t}
.
Indeed, such a point always exists and it holds
r(t)>0
as
\gamma_{t}\in\mathcal{C}^{1}(\mathbbm{R})

has no self-intersections.
Define the vector field
X_{\gamma}
on
\mathbbm{R}^{3}\times[0,T]
by

X_{\gamma}(t,x)=f\left(\operatorname{dist}\left(x,\gamma_{t}\right)^{2}\right)\tau_{t}\left(P_{t}(x)\right),

(4.10)

where
\tau_{t}
is the oriented unit tangent vector along
\gamma_{t}
and

f\left(d^{2}\right)=\begin{cases}\left(1-(d/r)^{2}\right)^{3}&\text{ for }0\leq d^{2}\leq r^{2},\\
0&\text{ for }d^{2}\geq r^{2}.\end{cases}

The derivation of the following proposition is technical and due to
[
32
, Proposition 4]
.

Proposition 4.12
.

Let
\gamma
be as in Theorem
4.10
, and let
X_{\gamma}
be given by (
4.10
).
For any
\xi_{0}\in\mathbbm{S}^{2}\subset\mathbbm{R}^{3}
, the estimate

\left|\partial_{t}X_{\gamma}\cdot\xi_{0}-\nabla\left(\nabla\times X_{\gamma}\right):\left(\xi_{0}\otimes\xi_{0}\right)\right|\leq K\left(1-X_{\gamma}\cdot\xi_{0}\right)

holds on
[0,T^{*}]\times\mathbbm{R}^{3}
, where

K:=\frac{54}{r^{2}}+14\left\|\partial_{sss}\gamma\right\|_{L^{\infty}\left([0,T^{*}]\times\mathbbm{T}^{1}\right)}.

Proof.

See
[
32
, Proposition 4]
.
∎

Now we have provided all the necessary ingredients to
deduce Theorem
4.10
.

Proof of Theorem
4.10
.

For
\varphi=X_{\gamma}
as defined in (
4.10
),
we obtain from (
4.8
) that

\displaystyle\left[E-{\mathcal{E}}(\mu)+\int_{\mathbbm{R}^{3}}\left(1-X_{\gamma}\cdot\frac{\,\mathrm{d}\mu}{\,\mathrm{d}|\mu|}\right)\,\mathrm{d}|\mu|\right]\Big|_{0}^{t}+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbbm{R}^{3}}\partial_{t}X_{\gamma}\cdot\frac{\,\mathrm{d}\mu}{\,\mathrm{d}|\mu|}-\nabla(\nabla\times X_{\gamma}):\frac{\,\mathrm{d}\mu}{\,\mathrm{d}|\mu|}\otimes\frac{\,\mathrm{d}\mu}{\,\mathrm{d}|\mu|}\,\mathrm{d}|\mu|\,\mathrm{d}\sigma

\displaystyle+\int_{s}^{t}\|(\nabla(\nabla\times X_{\gamma}))_{\mathrm{sym},+}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbbm{R}^{3};\mathbbm{R}^{3\times 3})}\left[\int_{\mathbbm{R}^{3}}\,\mathrm{d}|\mu|-E\right]\,\mathrm{d}\sigma\leq 0\,.

Invoking Proposition
4.12
, we infer the inequality
