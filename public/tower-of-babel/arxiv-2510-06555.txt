Twisted locality-preserving automorphisms, anomaly index, and generalized Lieb-Schultz-Mattis theorems with anti-unitary symmetries
Ruizhi Liu, Jinmin Yi, Liujun Zou

Abstract
Symmetries and their anomalies are powerful tools to understand quantum matter. In this work, for quantum spin chains, we define twisted locality-preserving automorphisms and their Gross-Nesme-Vogts-Werner indices, which provide a unified framework to describe both unitary and anti-unitary symmetries, on-site and non-on-site symmetries, and internal and translation symmetries. For a symmetry G G with actions given by twisted locality-preserving automorphisms, we give a microscopic definition of its anomaly index, which is an element in H φ 3 ​ ( G , U ⁡ ( 1 ) ) H^{3}_{\varphi}(G;U(1)) , where the subscript φ \varphi means that anti-unitary elements of G G act on U ⁡ ( 1 ) U(1) by complex conjugation. We show that an anomalous symmetry leads to multiple Lieb-Schultz-Matttis-type theorems. In particular, any state with an anomalous symmetry must either have long-range correlation or violate the entanglement area law. Based on this theorem, we further deduce that any state with an anomalous symmetry must have long-range entanglement, and any Hamiltonian that has an anomalous symmetry cannot have a unique gapped symmetric ground state, as long as the interactions in the Hamiltonian decay fast enough as the range of the interaction increases. For Hamiltonians with only two-spin interactions, the last theorem holds if the interactions decay faster than 1 / r 2 1/r^{2} , where r r is the distance between the two interacting spins. We demonstrate these general theorems in various concrete examples.



I
Introduction

One of the central tasks of quantum many-body physics is to characterize and classify quantum phases of matter. Symmetry is a key ingredient in this endeavor. The most familiar example where symmetry plays a crucial role is spontaneous symmetry breaking (SSB), where the ground states fail to respect the symmetry of the underlying Hamiltonian. However, the use of symmetry in modern quantum many-body physics extends far beyond SSB. In particular, it is by now well established that the anomalies associated with symmetries provide powerful, non-perturbative constraints on the correlation, entanglement and energy spectrum of quantum many-body systems
Hooft 1980
;
Chen et al. 2013
;
Wen 2013
.

A paradigmatic example of such a constraint is given by the Lieb–Schultz–Mattis (LSM) theorems
Lieb et al. 1961
;
Oshikawa 2000
;
Hastings 2004
. These fundamental theorems have become a cornerstone in our understanding of strongly correlated systems, with wide-ranging implications for quantum criticality, quantum spin liquids, and symmetry-protected topological (SPT) phases. Recently, the LSM constraints have been interpreted from various perspectives and generalized to different contexts
Cheng et al. 2016
;
Po et al. 2017
;
Jian et al. 2018
;
Cho et al. 2017
;
Watanabe 2018
;
Metlitski and Thorngren 2018
;
Cheng 2019
;
Kobayashi et al. 2019
;
Ogata and Tasaki 2019
;
Else and Thorngren 2020
;
Jiang et al. 2021
;
Yao and Oshikawa 2021
;
Ogata et al. 2021
;
Aksoy et al. 2021
;
Ye et al. 2022
;
Ma and Wang 2023
;
Cheng and Seiberg 2023
;
Kawabata et al. 2024
;
Aksoy et al. 2024
;
Seifnashri 2024
;
Zhou et al. 2023
;
Kapustin and Sopenko 2024
;
Garre Rubio et al. 2024
;
Liu et al. 2024
;
Pace et al. 2025
. Furthermore, these constraints are identified as a key ingredient to study the classification of quantum phases of matter in a lattice system
Zou et al. 2021
;
Ye et al. 2022
;
Ye and Zou 2024
;
Liu and Ye 2025
.

In addition to symmetry and its associated anomaly, another crucial ingredient of quantum many-body physics is locality, which sharply distinguishes a quantum many-body system from ordinary quantum mechanical systems. The assumption of locality-preserving dynamics is essential for the validity of LSM-type theorems, and the action of symmetries must preserve a notion of spatial locality. Mathematically, this requirement is captured by the framework of quantum cellular automata (QCA) or locality-preserving automorphisms (LPA)
Gross et al. 2012
;
Ranard et al. 2022
;
Kapustin and Sopenko 2024
;
Tu et al. 2025
. These structures formalize the idea that time evolutions and symmetry transformations act on quantum operators in a way compatible with locality.

However, the standard definitions of QCA and LPA are limited to unitary symmetries. Importantly, they exclude anti-unitary symmetries, such as time reversal, which play a central role in many physical systems. To extend the LSM-type constraints to include such cases, it is worthwhile generalizing the framework of QCA and LPA to accommodate anti-unitary symmetry actions. In this work, we introduce twisted versions of QCA and LPA in quantum spin chains, which incorporate anti-unitary symmetries into the locality-preserving paradigm. Building on this formalism, we define a corresponding anomaly index from the twisted symmetry action and establish new LSM-type constraints. Our results provide a unified perspective on how both unitary and anti-unitary symmetries enforce nontrivial constraints on quantum matter, thereby broadening the reach of the LSM constraints.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. In Sec.

Review of the operator algebra formalism

Our discussion relies on the formalism of operator algebra, which can conveniently deal with both finite-size and infinite-size systems. In this section, we briefly review the background and terminology of this formalism that are necessary to formulate and prove our results. Interested readers can find more detailed reviews in, for example, Appendix A of Ref.

II.1
Algebras of local and quasi-local operators

We first introduce some general background of the algebras of local operators and quasi-local operators. Readers are referred to Refs.
Bratteli and Robinson 2013a
;
Bratteli and Robinson 2013b
;
Naaijkens 2017
;
Landsman 2017

Throughout this subsection, we work on lattices of general spatial dimension
d
, i.e., for an infinite-size system our lattice is
\Lambda\simeq\mathbb{Z}^{d}
unless otherwise specified. We assume that our on-site Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{k}

\Lambda

Liu et al. 2024

Nevertheless, the Heisenberg picture of quantum mechanics, which focuses on operators rather than states, is still applicable even to systems with infinitely many degrees of freedom, such as spin systems on infinite lattices. In this formalism, we start with the notion of
local operators
. Given a
finite subset

\Gamma\subset\Lambda

\mathcal{H}_{\Gamma}:=\bigotimes_{k\in\Gamma}\mathcal{H}_{k}

Now we introduce a norm on the algebra
\mathscr{A}_{\Gamma}

A\in\mathscr{A}_{\Gamma}

\displaystyle||A||:=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}|\psi\rangle\in\mathcal{H}_{\Gamma},\\
\langle\psi|\psi\rangle=1\end{subarray}}|A|\psi\rangle|,

(II.1)

where

Given any local operator
A\in\mathscr{A}_{\Gamma}
, if
\Gamma^{\prime}
is another finite subset containing
\Gamma
(i.e.,
\Gamma\subset\Gamma^{\prime}
), then there is a natural way to extend

to a local operator supported on
\Gamma^{\prime}

\displaystyle\tilde{A}=A\bigotimes_{k\in\Gamma^{\prime}\setminus\Gamma}I_{k},

(II.2)

where
\tilde{A}
is the extension of

\Gamma^{\prime}
and

is the identity operator on
\mathcal{H}_{k}

acts as an identity outside
\Gamma
.
It is often convenient to identify these two operators. One can easily check that
||\tilde{A}||=||A||
, so this identification is unambiguous for norms.

We then make the following definition:

Definition II.1
.

The algebra of local operators

\displaystyle\mathscr{A}^{l}:=\bigcup_{\Gamma\subset\Lambda,|\Gamma|<\infty}\mathscr{A}_{\Gamma}

(II.3)

with the above identification
\tilde{A}\sim A
. We also define
\mathscr{A}_{\emptyset}=0
.

Here

means the cardinality of
\Gamma
, and we write
|\Gamma|<\infty

\Gamma
is a finite set. More explicitly,
A\in\mathscr{A}^{l}
if there is a finite subset
\Gamma
such that
A\in\mathscr{A}_{\Gamma}
(this

).

One crucial property of
\mathscr{A}^{l}
is locality. Given
A_{i}\in\mathscr{A}_{\Gamma_{i}},i=1,2

\Gamma_{1}\cap\Gamma_{2}=\emptyset
, then

\displaystyle[A_{1},A_{2}]=0.

(II.4)

We remark that
A_{1,2}
in above equation really mean their extensions on some
\Gamma
that contains
\Gamma_{1}\cup\Gamma_{2}
.

Although the above definition of the local operator algebra
\mathscr{A}^{l}
is clear and physically motivated, it is often insufficient to only work with

. To see it, consider a time evolution generated by a local Hamiltonian

Definition II.2
.

The quasi-local operator algebra

II.2
Quantum cellular automata and locality-preserving automorphisms

After introducing the basic notions of operator algebras, our next goal is to define a proper notion of symmetry action on local operators. This symmetry action is required to preserve certain notion of locality. In this subsection, we focus on unitary symmetries. The discussion will be generalized to include both unitary and anti-unitary symmetries in Sec.
III
.

Given a symmetry group
G
, it is natural to define the symmetry action as a homomorphism
G\to\mathrm{Aut}(\mathscr{A}^{l})
, where
\mathrm{Aut}(\mathscr{A}^{l})
is the automorphism group of the local operator algebra
\mathscr{A}^{l}
, which is defined below.

Definition II.4
.

We say that
\alpha:\mathscr{A}^{l}\to\mathscr{A}^{l}
is an automorphism of
\mathscr{A}^{l}
, if

1.

\alpha(A+B)=\alpha(A)+\alpha(B)

2.

\alpha(AB)=\alpha(A)\alpha(B)

3.

\alpha(A^{\dagger})=\alpha(A)^{\dagger}

4.

\alpha(\lambda A)=\lambda\alpha(A),\forall\,\lambda\in\mathbb{C}

5.

\alpha
is invertible.

All automorphisms of
\mathscr{A}^{l}
form a group under
finite
compositions, denoted by
\mathrm{Aut}(\mathscr{A}^{l})

There is a special subgroup of
\mathrm{Aut}(\mathscr{A}^{l})
in the literature called quantum cellular automata
Arrighi 2019
;
Farrelly 2020
, which will be useful to describe symmetry actions.

Definition II.5
.

A quantum cellular automaton (QCA) is an automorphism
\alpha
of
\mathscr{A}^{l}
such that for a local operator
A\in\mathscr{A}_{X}
(where
X
is a finite subset),
\alpha(A)\in\mathscr{A}_{B(X,r_{\alpha})}
, where
r_{\alpha}>0
does not depend on
A
.

Here
B(\Gamma,r_{\alpha}):=\{x\in\Lambda|d(x,\Gamma)\leqslant r_{\alpha}\}
, where
d
is the distance on lattice and
0\leqslant r_{\alpha}<\infty
does not depend on
A
. According to this definition, QCA preserves the locality of an operator. Moreover, QCA form a group under finite compositions, which is denoted by
\mathcal{G}^{\mathrm{QCA}}
.

It is useful to look at a special example of QCA, which will also be discussed later.

Example II.1
.

One particular example of QCA is a (finite-depth unitary) circuit. For simplicity, we describe it in one spatial dimension (1d). Let
\{P_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}

\displaystyle\alpha=\prod_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{Ad}_{U_{k}}

(II.10)

where

A circuit is a finite composition of BPU’s (these BPU’s may be defined for different partitions). The group of all circuits are denoted by
\mathcal{G}^{cir}
. Later we will see that not every QCA is a circuit.

As explained in the last subsection, one needs to consider not only QCA’s. Instead, the automorphism group

An automorphism

. This terminology might be confusing, since usually we assume that the time evolutions are differentiable in time (i.e., the Hamiltonian is continuous in time), but
\mathcal{G}^{loc}
includes time evolutions that are not differentiable in time.

II.3
States, GNS construction and factors

After introducing the notions of local and quasi-local operator algebras, QCA and LPA, now we introduce the notions of states, Gelfand-Naimark-Segal construction and von Neumann factors in this subsection. These concepts are also necessary for formulating and proving our results.

A state
\psi
of the
C^{*}
-algebra
\mathscr{A}^{ql}
is defined to be a nonzero linear functional
\psi:\mathscr{A}^{ql}\to\mathbb{C}
which is

1.

Positive, i.e.,
\psi(A^{\dagger}A)\geqslant 0,\,\forall\,A\in\mathscr{A}^{ql}
.

2.

Normalized, i.e.,
\psi(I)=1
, where
I
is the identity operator in
\mathscr{A}^{ql}
.

Given two quantum states
\psi_{0},\psi_{1}
and a real number
t\in[0,1]
, one can construct another state

\displaystyle\psi_{t}=t\psi_{1}+(1-t)\psi_{0}.

(II.19)

It is easy to check that
\psi_{t}
is positive and normalized, thus indeed being a valid state. A state
\psi
is said to be mixed if there exists
\psi_{0},\psi_{1}
and
0<t<1
such that
\psi=\psi_{t}
. A state is pure if it is not mixed.

For any state
\psi
, there is a representation of
\mathscr{A}^{ql}
associated with it, which is given by the so-called Gelfand-Naimark-Segal (GNS) construction. The data of this representation is encapsulated in the GNS triple
(\mathcal{H}_{\psi},\pi_{\psi},|\psi\rangle)
, where
\mathcal{H}_{\psi}
is a Hilbert space,
\pi_{\psi}:\mathscr{A}^{ql}\to\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}_{\psi})
is a
*
-homomorphism and
|\psi\rangle
is a vector state which represents the abstract state
\psi
on
\mathcal{H}_{\psi}
, in the sense that

\displaystyle\psi(A)=\langle\psi|\pi_{\psi}(A)|\psi\rangle,\forall A\in\mathscr{A}^{ql}.

(II.20)

It can be shown that the GNS triple is unique up to unitary equivalence (see, for example, Ref.
Naaijkens 2017
). If two representations
\pi_{1}
and
\pi_{2}
are unitarily equivalent, then we write
\pi_{1}\simeq\pi_{2}
. A GNS representation of
\psi
is irreducible if and only if the state
\psi
is pure
Naaijkens 2017
.

Next, we give a brief introduction to von Neumann factors and factor states, which are special states that are of physical interest.
Familiarity with von Neumann algebras would be helpful, but it is not necessary for the following discussions. See, e.g., the Appendix A of Ref.
Liu et al. 2024
for an introduction to von Neumann algebra for working physicists.

We begin with the definition of factor representations
Kadison and Ringrose 1997
;
Landsman 2017
.

Definition II.7
.

Let
\pi:\mathscr{A}^{ql}\to\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
be a
*
-representation of
\mathscr{A}^{ql}
. It is called irreducible if the commutant

\displaystyle\pi(\mathscr{A}^{ql})^{\prime}:=\{x\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})|[x,\pi(\mathscr{A}^{ql})]=0\}

(II.21)

equals
\mathbb{C}\cdot\mathrm{id}_{\mathcal{H}}
. It is called a factor (also known as primary) representation if

\displaystyle\pi(\mathscr{A}^{ql})^{\prime}\cap(\pi(\mathscr{A}^{ql})^{\prime})^{\prime}=\mathbb{C}\cdot\mathrm{id}_{\mathcal{H}}

(II.22)

A state
\psi
of
\mathscr{A}^{ql}
is called a factor state if its GNS representation is a factor representation.

The physical importance of factor states is recognized by the following profound theorem.

Theorem II.1

(Theorem 2.6.10 of Ref.
Bratteli and Robinson 2013a
)
.

A state
\psi
on
\mathscr{A}^{ql}
is clustering iff it is a factor state.

Here clustering means that the connected correlation function of any two local operators decays to zero (no matter how fast) when the distance between these two local operators is taken to infinity. The above theorem is elegant because it connects the clustering property, which is an important and intuitive concept related to the locality structure of the states, with the properties of the GNS representation of the state, which appears to be much more abstract.

Mathematically, there is a classification of factors into three types (see Refs.
Bratteli and Robinson 2013a
;
Landsman 2017
for details about this classification). The only relevant one for us will be type-I factors.

Definition II.8
.

Let
\pi
be a factor representation of
\mathscr{A}^{ql}
. It is called type-I if the double commutant

\displaystyle\pi^{\prime\prime}(\mathscr{A}^{ql}):=(\pi(\mathscr{A}^{ql})^{\prime})^{\prime}\simeq\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{K})

(II.23)

for some Hilbert space
\mathcal{K}
. Similarly, a state
\psi
is called type-I if its GNS representation is type-I.

Remark II.1
.

Given a state
\psi
(not necessarily a factor), the double commutant associated to
\psi
will be denoted by
\mathscr{M}_{\psi}
. It turns out this algebra
\mathscr{M}_{\psi}
is a so-called von Neumann algebra, and we will call it as the von Neumann algebra associated with
\psi
.

The reason we need type-I states is that they are particularly well-behaved. More concretely,

Lemma II.1

(Lemmas A.5, A.7 of Ref.
Liu et al. 2024
)
.

Let
\psi
be a type-I factor and
\mathscr{M}_{\psi}
be the double commutant algebra. Then

•

\psi=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{k}\psi_{k}
, where
0\leqslant\lambda_{k}\leqslant 1
with
\sum_{k}\lambda_{k}=1
and
\psi_{k}
are pure states such that
\psi_{i}\simeq\psi_{j}
for any
i,j
. In particular, all pure states are type-I.

•

All
*
-automorphisms of
\mathscr{M}_{\psi}
are inner, i.e., they are given by
\mathrm{Ad}_{U}
for some
U\in\mathscr{M}_{\psi}
.

These two properties will be extremely useful when proving a root lemma, Lemma
V.1
, based on which all our results are derived.

When is a factor state of type-I? Sometimes the definition can be awkward to use and an alternative criterion might be useful. Ref.
Liu et al. 2024
provides a useful and convenient way (see Proposition A.10 therein):

Lemma II.2

(Proposition A.10 of Ref
Liu et al. 2024
)
.

Let
\psi
be a factor state of a 1d spin chain satisfying the area law of entanglement entropy
3
3

3

Given a state
\psi
in 1d and a finite interval
[a,b]
, the restriction
\psi|_{[a,b]}
can be represented by a density matrix
\rho
. The state
\psi
satisfies the area law if the entanglement entropy
S:=-{\rm tr}(\rho\log\rho)<D
for some
D>0
that is independent of
a
and
b
.
, then
\psi
must be of type-I.

We finish this subsection by briefly discussing the relations between two states. Given two states, we are often interested in how different they are, which can be characterized by the following concepts.

Definition II.9
.

Let
\psi,\rho
be two states of
\mathscr{A}^{ql}
, they are called quasi-equivalent if there is a
*
-isomorphism
f:\mathscr{M}_{\psi}\to\mathscr{M}_{\rho}
such that

\displaystyle f(\pi_{\psi}(a))=\pi_{\rho}(a),\forall a\in\mathscr{A}^{ql}.

(II.24)

We write
\psi\sim\rho
for quasi-equivalence.

They are called disjoint if their GNS representation contains no equivalent subrepresentations.

In general, two states can be neither quasi-equivalent nor disjoint, but the situation significantly simplifies for factor states. In fact,

Lemma II.3

(Corollary 8.22 of Ref.
Landsman 2017
)
.

Two factor states are either disjoint or quasi-equivalent.

II.4
Split property

To state and prove our results, the next ingredient is the split property of factor states. Recall that we always have the factorization of the algebra,
\mathscr{A}^{ql}\simeq\mathscr{A}^{ql}_{<0}\otimes\mathscr{A}^{ql}_{\geqslant 0}
, where
\mathscr{A}^{ql}_{<0}
(resp.
\mathscr{A}^{ql}_{\geqslant 0}
) is the algebra of quasi-local operators supported on the negative half chain,i.e.,
(-\infty,0)
(resp. non-negative half chain, i.e.,
[0,\infty)
). It is natural to ask if we always have similar factorization for states. The answer is generally no, because there can be quantum entanglement. To make factorization of states more precise, we define the following important split property.

Definition II.10
.

A factor state
\psi
of
\mathscr{A}^{ql}
splits or has the split property if there exist states
\psi_{<0}
and
\psi_{\geqslant 0}
for
\mathscr{A}^{ql}_{<0}
and
\mathscr{A}^{ql}_{\geqslant 0}
respectively, such that
\psi\sim\psi_{<0}\otimes\psi_{\geqslant 0}
(note it is quasi-equivalence here).

Intuitively speaking, if a state splits, then there is limited amount of quantum entanglement between the left half and right half of the underlying quantum spin chain.

Remark II.2
.

As shown in Corollary A.4 of Ref.
Liu et al. 2024
, if a state
\psi
satisfies the split property, i.e.,
\psi\sim\psi_{<0}\otimes\psi_{\geqslant 0}
, then for any finite integer
n
,
\psi\sim\psi_{<n}\otimes\psi_{\geqslant n}
. Namely, if a state splits at any site, then it splits at all sites.

For 1d lattice system, it turns out the entanglement area law implies the split property for factor states.

Lemma II.4

(Theorem 1.5 in Ref.
Matsui 2013
)
.

For a 1d lattice system, if a factor state
\psi
satisfies entanglement area law, then it splits.

We remark that the original statement of Theorem 1.5 in Ref.
Matsui 2013
does not assume that
\psi
is a factor state, but its proof actually assumes it.

II.5
Anti-unitary equivalence

In this paper, we will disuss both unitary and anti-unitary symmetries. The treatment of unitary symmetries is described before, which is given by a homomorphism from the symmetry group
G
to the group of LPA,
\mathcal{G}^{lp}
. The consequences of such symmetries are also throughly explored in Ref.
Liu et al. 2024
. To discuss anti-unitary symmetries, below we first define anti-unitary equivalence of representations (see Appendix
A
for more details on anti-linear maps and complex conjugations). Our terminology follows Ref.
Swanson 2020
.

Definition II.11
.

Let
(\mathcal{H}_{1},\mathcal{H}_{2})
be two Hilbert spaces with inner products
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{1}
and
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{2}
, respectively. An anti-linear map
\tilde{U}:\mathcal{H}_{1}\to\mathcal{H}_{2}
is called anti-unitary if

\displaystyle\langle\tilde{U}a,\tilde{U}b\rangle_{2}=\overline{\langle a,b\rangle_{1}},\forall\,a,b,\in\mathcal{H}_{1}

(II.25)

where
\bar{\cdot}
means complex conjugation. Let
(\pi_{1},\mathcal{H}_{1})
and
(\pi_{2},\mathcal{H}_{2})
be representations of
\mathscr{A}^{ql}
and
\bar{\mathscr{A}}^{ql}
respectively, where
\bar{\mathscr{A}}^{ql}
is the complex conjugate of
\mathscr{A}^{ql}
(see Definition
A.2
for its definition). These representations are anti-unitarily equivalent if there is anti-unitary map
\tilde{U}:\mathcal{H}_{1}\to\mathcal{H}_{2}
such that

\displaystyle\tilde{U}\pi_{1}(A)\tilde{U}^{-1}=\pi_{2}(A)

(II.26)

where
A
is regarded as an element in
\mathscr{A}^{ql}
(resp.
\bar{\mathscr{A}}^{ql}
) on LHS (resp. RHS).

Remark II.3
.

Sometimes,
\mathcal{H}_{1}
can be identified with
\mathcal{H}_{2}
. In this case,
\tilde{U}
is simply an anti-unitary operator on
\mathcal{H}_{1}
.

Now, we state a key lemma, which is a generalization of Theorem 2.2 of Ref.
Ogata 2019
.

Lemma II.5
.

Let
\psi
be a factor state of
\mathscr{A}^{ql}
and
\tilde{\alpha}\in\widetilde{\mathrm{Aut}}(\mathscr{A}^{ql})
, then the following two conditions are equivalent.

1.

There is a quasi-equivalence between
\psi
and
\psi_{\tilde{\alpha}}
, where
\psi_{\tilde{\alpha}}
is defined by
4
4

4

Note that
\psi\circ\tilde{\alpha}
is not a state of
\mathscr{A}^{ql}
if
\tilde{\alpha}
is anti-linear.

\displaystyle\psi_{\tilde{\alpha}}(A):=\begin{cases}\psi(\tilde{\alpha}(A)),\quad\text{for linear $\tilde{\alpha}$};\\
\psi(\tilde{\alpha}(A)^{\dagger}),\quad\text{for anti-linear $\tilde{\alpha}$.}\end{cases}

(II.27)

2.

For any
\epsilon>0
, there is a finite subset
\Gamma_{\epsilon}
of the lattice, such that

\displaystyle|\psi(A)-\psi_{\tilde{\alpha}}(A)|<\epsilon||A||,\quad\forall\,A\in\mathscr{A}^{ql}_{\Gamma^{c}_{\epsilon}}

(II.28)

where
\Gamma_{\epsilon}^{c}
means the complement of
\Gamma_{\epsilon}
.

Remark II.4
.

In the language of usual quantum mechanics, the condition
\psi=\psi_{\tilde{\alpha}}
means that the state
\psi
is invariant under the operation
\tilde{\alpha}
.

Remark II.5
.

If
\psi
is a pure state with
\psi_{\tilde{\alpha}}=\psi
and
\tilde{\alpha}
is of order 2 (i.e.,
\tilde{\alpha}^{2}=1
), then above lemma reduces to Theorem 2.2 of Ref.
Ogata 2019
. This lemma can also be viewed as an anti-unitary version of Proposition 3.2.8 of Ref.
Naaijkens 2017
.

As an important corollary, we have

Corollary II.1
.

Let
\psi
and
\psi_{\tilde{\alpha}}
satisfy either assumptions in Lemma
II.5
. Then there exists an operator
\tilde{U}
on
\mathcal{H}_{\psi}
such that

\displaystyle\tilde{U}\pi_{\psi}(a)\tilde{U}^{-1}=\pi_{\psi}(\tilde{\alpha}(a)),\forall\,a

(II.29)

where
\tilde{U}
is unitary (resp. anti-unitary) if
\tilde{\alpha}
is linear (resp. anti-linear).

The proofs to Lemma
II.5
and Corollary
II.1
are left to Appendix
A.4
.

II.6
Hamiltonians and ground states

The above discussions are mostly about operators and states, without referring to any Hamiltonian. In this subsection, we introduce Hamiltonians, ground states and locally unique gapped ground states.

In quantum mechanics with finitely many degrees of freedom, Hamiltonians are operators in the Hilbert space. In infinite-size systems, however, the notion of the total Hilbert space does not make sense, and the Hamiltonians are also not operators. Instead, Hamiltonians are defined by their commutators with operators. Specifically, the Hamiltonians of our interest, which are deembed admissible Hamiltonians, are defined as follows.

Definition II.12
.

In a 1D lattice system
\Lambda=\mathbb{Z}
where sites are labeled by
i
, the Hamiltonian
\delta_{H}
is admissible if
\delta_{H}=\sum_{Z:|Z|\leqslant k}[h_{Z},\bullet]
with
h_{Z}
’s (i.e., Hamiltonian terms supported on
Z
) satisfying

\displaystyle\begin{split}\sup_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}||h_{i}||&<B,\\
\sup_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}Z:|Z|\leqslant k,Z\owns i\\
\mathrm{diam}(Z)=r\end{subarray}}||h_{Z}||&<\frac{J}{r^{\mathfrak{a}}},\,{\rm with\ }\mathfrak{a}>2,\end{split}

(II.30)

where
\mathrm{diam}(Z):=\max_{x,y\in Z}d(x,y)
, k is an integer,
J
and
B
are positive constants and
h_{i}
is a one-body potential at site
i
.

In the above,
\delta_{H}
gives the commutator between the Hamiltonian and a local operator. The first condition in Eq. (
II.30
) means that the single-body terms in the Hamiltonian are always bounded, and the second condition means that the interaction strengths decay fast enough when the ranges of the interactions increase. In particular, if the Hamiltonian consists of only 2-body interactions, then the 2-body interaction decays faster than
1/r^{2}
, where
r
is the distance between the two interacting spins.

Given the notion of Hamiltonians, now we define ground states in the operator algebra formalism.

Definition II.13
.

A state
\psi
is said to be the ground state of the Hamiltonian
\delta_{H}
if

\displaystyle\psi(A^{\dagger}\delta_{H}(A))\geqslant 0,\forall\,A\in\mathscr{A}^{l}

(II.31)

In the language of ordinary quantum mechanics, this condition means that applying any operator to the ground state cannot decrease the expectation value of the Hamiltonian. In infinite-size systems, the ground states defined above should be viewed as the “lowest-energy” states within a superselection sector.

A locally unique gapped ground state, which may simply be called a gapped ground state, is defined as follows. Physically, a locally unique gapped ground state is a gapped ground state in a superselection sector.

Definition II.14
.

A ground state
\psi
of Hamiltonian
\delta_{H}
is a locally unique gapped ground state if there is a
\gamma>0
such that

\displaystyle\psi(A^{\dagger}\delta_{H}A)\geqslant\gamma\psi(A^{\dagger}A)

(II.32)

for any
A\in\mathscr{A}^{l}
with
\psi(A)=0
. The energy gap
\Delta
is the largest possible
\gamma
satisfying the above inequality.

Some important properties of locally unique gapped ground states of an admissible Hamiltonian are proved in Ref.
Liu et al. 2024
. In particular, it is shown there in that locally unique gapped ground states of an admissible Hamiltonian satisfies the area law of entanglement entropy (see Theorem IV.1 therein and Theorem 4.4 of Ref.
Ukai 2025
), they are pure (see Theorem A.7 therein) and also satisfy the split property (see Corollary F.1 therein).

III
Twisted locality-preserving automorphisms, their GNVW index, and symmetry actions

After reviewing the operator algebra formalism in Sec.
II
, in this section, we discuss a twisted version of QCA and LPA to describe anti-unitary symmetries such as time reversal.

First, we define twisted automorphisms, which are generalizations of the automorphisms in Definition
II.4
to include anti-linear operations.

Definition III.1

(Twisted automorphisms)
.

A map
\alpha:\mathscr{A}^{ql}\to\mathscr{A}^{ql}
is called a twisted automorphism of
\mathscr{A}^{ql}
if the followings are true:

1.

\alpha(A+B)=\alpha(A)+\alpha(B)

2.

\alpha(AB)=\alpha(A)\alpha(B)

3.

\alpha(A^{\dagger})=\alpha(A)^{\dagger}

4.

\alpha(\lambda A)=\lambda\alpha(A),
or
\alpha(\lambda A)=\overline{\lambda}\alpha(A)
,
\forall\,\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
, where
\bar{\lambda}
means the complex conjugate of
\lambda
.

5.

\alpha
is invertible.

If
\alpha(\lambda A)=\lambda\alpha(A)
, we say that
\alpha
is linear. Otherwise, it is said to be anti-linear. Twisted automorphisms form a group under finite compositions, denoted by
\widetilde{\mathrm{Aut}}(\mathscr{A}^{ql})
.

Remark III.1
.

It is important to note that the
\dagger
(more precisely, the
*
-operation) on
\mathscr{A}^{ql}
is not a twisted automorphism because it violates the property (2) above.

There are multiple ways to characterize twisted automorphisms, which should be familiar from the discussion of time reversal symmetries in quantum mechanics. First, let
\alpha\in\widetilde{\mathrm{Aut}}(\mathscr{A}^{ql})
and
\beta\in\mathrm{Aut}(\mathscr{A}^{ql})
. One can verify that
\alpha\beta\alpha^{-1}\in\mathrm{Aut}(\mathscr{A}^{ql})
. As a consequence,
\mathrm{Aut}(\mathscr{A}^{ql})
is a normal subgroup of
\widetilde{\mathrm{Aut}}(\mathscr{A}^{ql})
.
The quotient group
\widetilde{\mathrm{Aut}}(\mathscr{A}^{ql})/\mathrm{Aut}(\mathscr{A}^{ql})=\mathbb{Z}_{2}
, because
\alpha^{2}\in\mathrm{Aut}(\mathscr{A}^{ql})
is linear for any
\alpha\in\widetilde{\mathrm{Aut}}(\mathscr{A}^{ql})
. Therefore, there is a short exact sequence

\displaystyle 1\rightarrow\mathcal{G}^{lp}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle i}}{{\hookrightarrow}}\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{lp}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\phi}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mathbb{Z}_{2}\rightarrow 1,

(III.1)

where
i
denotes the inclusion map and
\phi
denotes the quotient map. For
\tilde{\alpha}\in\widetilde{\mathrm{Aut}}(\mathscr{A}^{ql})
, it is linear if
\phi(\tilde{\alpha})=0
and anti-linear if
\phi(\tilde{\alpha})=1
.

In fact,
\widetilde{\mathrm{Aut}}(\mathscr{A}^{ql})
is a semi-direct product, i.e.,

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathrm{Aut}}(\mathscr{A}^{ql})\simeq\mathrm{Aut}(\mathscr{A}^{ql})\rtimes\mathbb{Z}_{2}

(III.2)

To see it, note that for each site
n\in\mathbb{Z}
one can choose a complex conjugation operator
\Theta_{n}
, which is an anti-linear operator satisfying
\Theta_{n}^{2}=1
. Writing
K_{n}:=\mathrm{Ad}_{\Theta_{n}}
, one can formally define (see Appendix
A
for more detail)

\displaystyle K:=\prod_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}K_{n}

(III.3)

Obviously,
K\in\widetilde{\mathrm{Aut}}(\mathscr{A}^{ql})
and
K^{2}=1
, so it generates a
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
subgroup of
\widetilde{\mathrm{Aut}}(\mathscr{A}^{ql})
. Note that
\alpha:=\tilde{\alpha}K
is a linear automorphism. Thus, any element
\tilde{\alpha}\in\widetilde{\mathrm{Aut}}(\mathscr{A}^{ql})
can be written as

\displaystyle\tilde{\alpha}=\alpha K

(III.4)

for some
\alpha\in\mathrm{Aut}(\mathscr{A}^{ql})
and
K
defined in Eq. (
III.3
). We stress that
K
defined above is not unique, because
\Theta_{n}
is not unique. However, we will show that all our main results are valid for all choices of
K
.

Following the last section, we define twisted locality-preserving automorphisms as follows.

Definition III.2
.

Let
\alpha\in\widetilde{\mathrm{Aut}}(\mathscr{A}^{ql})
. It is called a twisted locality-preserving automorphism if for any local operator
A\in\mathscr{A}_{X}
and
r>0
, there exists a local operator
B\in\mathscr{A}_{B(X,r)}
and a non-negative decreasing function
f_{\alpha}(r)\searrow 0
, such that

\displaystyle||\alpha(A)-B||<f_{\alpha}(r)||A||

(III.5)

Especially,
f_{\alpha}
is the same for all
A\in\mathscr{A}_{X}
. The group of twisted LPA is denoted by
\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{lp}
. If there exists a positive number
R
such that
f_{\alpha}(r)=0,\,\forall\,r>R
, then we say that
\alpha
is a twisted quantum cellular automaton. The smallest possible choice of
R
is called the radius of the twisted QCA
\alpha
, denoted by
r_{\alpha}
. The group of twisted QCA is denoted by
\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{\mathrm{QCA}}
.

From the above definition, it is readily verified that
K
defined in Eq. (
III.3
) is a twisted QCA and therefore a twisted LPA.

Following a similar discussion as that on
\widetilde{\mathrm{Aut}}(\mathscr{A}^{ql})
, we have the following semi-direct product decomposition

\displaystyle\begin{split}\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{lp}&\simeq\mathcal{G}^{lp}\rtimes\mathbb{Z}_{2}\\
\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{\mathrm{QCA}}&\simeq\mathcal{G}^{\mathrm{QCA}}\rtimes\mathbb{Z}_{2}\end{split}

(III.6)

Note that the anti-unitary map
K\in\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{\mathrm{QCA}}\subset\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{lp}
and
K^{2}=1
, so it generates above
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
factor.

One can define the GNVW index for an element in
\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{lp}
as follows. If
\tilde{\alpha}\in\mathcal{G}^{lp}
(i.e., it is linear), then
\mathrm{ind}(\tilde{\alpha})
is already defined in Eq. (
II.12
). On the other hand, if
\tilde{\alpha}
is anti-linear, according to the above discussion, we can write
\tilde{\alpha}=\alpha K
. Then we define the GNVW index of
\tilde{\alpha}
as
\mathrm{ind}(\tilde{\alpha}):=\mathrm{ind}(\alpha)
. It is important to show that this index is a well-defined homomorphism
\mathrm{ind}:\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{lp}\to\mathbb{Z}[\{\log p_{j}\}_{j\in J}]
. In particular, because there can be different
\alpha
’s and
K
’s such that
\tilde{\alpha}=\alpha K
, we must show that this index does not depend on how we decompose
\tilde{\alpha}
into
\alpha K
. The proof that this index is well-defined will be presented in Appendix
B
.

With the above preparations, we can now discuss symmetry actions for both unitary and anti-unitary symmetries. Suppose that the symmetry group is
G
. By a
G
symmetry action, we mean a group homomorphism

\tilde{\alpha}:G\to\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{lp}
. Note that there is an induced map

\displaystyle\varphi:G\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\tilde{\alpha}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{lp}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\phi}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mathbb{Z}_{2}.

(III.7)

For a group element
g\in\mathcal{G}
, we say that it is unitary if
\varphi(g)=0
and anti-unitary if
\varphi(g)=1
. Notice, strictly speaking, whether the symmetry is unitary or anti-unitary does not only depend on
g
itself, but also on the symmetry action
\tilde{\alpha}
.

We end this section by discussing how the multiplication and associativity work for the symmetry actions described by twisted LPA. Since
\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{lp}=\mathcal{G}^{lp}\rtimes\mathbb{Z}_{2}
, for any
g\in G
we have

\displaystyle\tilde{\alpha}(g)=\alpha(g)\theta(g)

(III.8)

where
\alpha(g)\in\mathcal{G}^{lp}
and

\displaystyle\theta(g):=\begin{cases}1,\varphi(g)=0\\
K,\varphi(g)=1\end{cases}

(III.9)

By assumption, as a homomorphism,
\tilde{\alpha}
satisfies
\tilde{\alpha}(g)\tilde{\alpha}(h)=\tilde{\alpha}(gh)
, which gives the following composition rules for
\alpha(g)
and
\theta(g)
:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\alpha(g)(\theta(g)\triangleright\alpha(h))&=\alpha(gh)\\
\theta(g)\theta(h)&=\theta(gh)\end{split}

(III.10)

where
\theta(g)\triangleright\alpha(h):=\theta(g)\alpha(h)\theta(g)^{-1}
.

IV
Anomaly index

After introducing twisted LPA, we can now define an anomaly index for a symmetry action
\tilde{\alpha}:G\to\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{lp}
. If this anomaly index takes a nontrivial value (in the sense to be introduced), then we say that the symmetry action is anomalous. Otherwise, the symmetry action is said to be non-anomalous or anomaly free. In essence, the anomaly characterizes the interplay between locality and the symmetry. Later, we will formulate various LSM-type constraints due to an anomalous symmetry.

IV.1
General definition

Our construction of the anomaly index is inspired by Ref.
Kapustin and Sopenko 2024
. Ref.
Kapustin and Sopenko 2024
focuses on unitary symmetries described by LPA, and we extend the notion of the anomaly index to both unitary and anti-unitary symmetries described by general twisted LPA.

To construct the anomaly index, we define several new objects:

1.

\mathscr{A}^{ql}_{<0}
: Algebra of quasi-local operators supported on negative half-chain, i.e.,
(-\infty,-1]
.

2.

\mathscr{A}^{ql}_{\geqslant 0}
: Algebra of quasi-local operators supported on non-negative half-chain, i.e.,
[0,\infty)
.

3.

\mathcal{G}^{lp}_{0}
: The subgroup of
\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{lp}
generated by elements of the form of
\mathrm{Ad}_{U}
, where
U
is a quasi-local unitary operators.

4.

\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{lp}_{<0}
: Twisted locality-preserving automorphisms of
\mathscr{A}^{ql}_{<0}
only, acting trivially on
\mathscr{A}^{ql}_{\geqslant 0}
.

5.

\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{lp}_{\geqslant 0}
: Twisted locality-preserving automorphisms of
\mathscr{A}^{ql}_{\geqslant 0}
only, acting trivially on
\mathscr{A}^{ql}_{<0}
.

6.

\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{lp}_{+}
(resp.
\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{lp}_{-}
) is the group generated by
\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{lp}_{\geqslant 0}\mathcal{G}^{lp}_{0}
(resp.
\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{lp}_{<0}\mathcal{G}^{lp}_{0}
).

Note that we always have

\displaystyle\mathscr{A}^{ql}\simeq\mathscr{A}^{ql}_{<0}\otimes\mathscr{A}^{ql}_{\geqslant 0}

(IV.1)

Moreover,
\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{lp}
has a natural group action on
\mathcal{G}_{0}
by conjugation. Concretely, for any
\tilde{\alpha}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{lp}
and
U\in\mathcal{U}^{ql}
, we have

\displaystyle\tilde{\alpha}\triangleright\mathrm{Ad}_{U}:=\tilde{\alpha}\circ\mathrm{Ad}_{U}\circ\tilde{\alpha}^{-1}=\mathrm{Ad}_{\tilde{\alpha}(U)}\in\mathcal{G}^{lp}_{0}

(IV.2)

One must be careful that
\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{lp}_{<0}
and
\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{lp}_{\geqslant 0}
are not a subgroup of
\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{lp}
. One might think that given an anti-linear
\tilde{\alpha}_{\geqslant 0}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{lp}_{\geqslant 0}
, one can always obtain a twisted automorphism of
\mathscr{A}^{ql}\simeq\mathscr{A}^{ql}_{<0}\otimes\mathscr{A}^{ql}_{\geqslant 0}
by considering
\mathrm{id}_{\mathscr{A}^{ql}_{<0}}\otimes\tilde{\alpha}_{\geqslant 0}
. However,
\mathrm{id}_{\mathscr{A}^{ql}_{<0}}\otimes\tilde{\alpha}_{\geqslant 0}
is neither linear nor anti-linear on
\mathscr{A}^{ql}
(see Appendix
A
for more details), hence it is not an element of
\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{lp}
. However, let
\tilde{\alpha}_{<0}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{lp}_{<0}
be another anti-linear element, one can show that
\tilde{\alpha}_{<0}\otimes\tilde{\alpha}_{\geqslant 0}
is anti-linear on
\mathscr{A}^{ql}
and thus an element of
\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{lp}
.

Below we give the general definition of the anomaly index, which relies on the following two propositions that are proved in Appendix
C.1
.

Proposition IV.1
.

Let
\tilde{\alpha}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{lp}
. Then
\tilde{\alpha}
has a vanishing GNVW index if and only if it admits a decomposition

\displaystyle\tilde{\alpha}=(\tilde{\alpha}_{<0}\otimes\mathrm{id})\alpha_{0}(\mathrm{id}\otimes\tilde{\alpha}_{\geqslant 0})

(IV.3)

where
\tilde{\alpha}_{<0}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{lp}_{<0}
,
\alpha_{0}\in\mathcal{G}_{0}^{lp}
and
\tilde{\alpha}_{\geqslant 0}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{lp}_{\geqslant 0}
. Moreover,
\tilde{\alpha}_{\geqslant 0}
and
\tilde{\alpha}_{<0}
are anti-linear on
\mathscr{A}^{ql}_{\geqslant 0}
and
\mathscr{A}^{ql}_{<0}
, respectively, if and only if
\tilde{\alpha}
is anti-linear. For notational simplicity, below we write
\mathrm{id}\otimes\tilde{\alpha}_{\geqslant 0}
as
\tilde{\alpha}_{\geqslant 0}
and
\tilde{\alpha}_{<0}\otimes\mathrm{id}
as
\tilde{\alpha}_{<0}
.

For
\tilde{\alpha}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{lp}
with a vanishing GNVW index, such a decomposition is not unique. The following result characterizes the relation between different decompositions.

Proposition IV.2
.

Let
\tilde{\alpha}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{lp}
with a vanishing GNVW index and suppose that it decomposes as

\displaystyle\tilde{\alpha}_{<0}\alpha_{0}\tilde{\alpha}_{\geqslant 0}=\tilde{\alpha}=\tilde{\beta}_{<0}\beta_{0}\tilde{\beta}_{\geqslant 0}.

(IV.4)

Then there exists a quasi-local unitary
V
such that

\displaystyle\tilde{\beta}_{\geqslant 0}=\mathrm{Ad}_{V}\circ\tilde{\alpha}_{\geqslant 0}.

(IV.5)

Especially,
\mathrm{Ad}_{V}
is only supported on the non-negative half chain.

With these results, one can construct the anomaly index as follows.

We first consider a simpler case, where
\tilde{\alpha}:G\to\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{lp}
is a symmetry action with
\mathrm{ind}(\tilde{\alpha}(g))=0
for all
g\in G
. The more general case will be discussed later. By Proposition
IV.1
, we have

\displaystyle\tilde{\alpha}(g)=\tilde{\alpha}_{<0}(g)\alpha_{0}(g)\tilde{\alpha}_{\geqslant 0}(g)

(IV.6)

Since
\tilde{\alpha}
is a group homomorphism, we have
\tilde{\alpha}(g)\tilde{\alpha}(h)=\tilde{\alpha}(gh)
. So one obtains two different decompositions of
\tilde{\alpha}(gh)
:

\displaystyle\tilde{\alpha}_{<0}(g)\tilde{\alpha}_{<0}(h)\beta_{0}\tilde{\alpha}_{\geqslant 0}(g)\tilde{\alpha}_{\geqslant 0}(h)=\tilde{\alpha}_{<0}(gh)\alpha_{0}(gh)\tilde{\alpha}_{\geqslant 0}(gh)

(IV.7)

where
\beta_{0}:=(\tilde{\alpha}_{<0}^{-1}(h)\triangleright\alpha_{0}(g))(\tilde{\alpha}_{\geqslant 0}(g)\triangleright\alpha_{0}(h))\in\mathcal{G}^{lp}_{0}
. Then according to Proposition
IV.2
, there exists a quasi-local unitary
V(g,h)
such that

\displaystyle\tilde{\alpha}_{\geqslant 0}(g)\tilde{\alpha}_{\geqslant 0}(h)=\mathrm{Ad}_{V(g,h)}\tilde{\alpha}_{\geqslant 0}(gh)

(IV.8)

Next, one uses the fact that associativity demands that
(\tilde{\alpha}_{\geqslant 0}(g)\tilde{\alpha}_{\geqslant 0}(h))\tilde{\alpha}_{\geqslant 0}(k)=\tilde{\alpha}_{\geqslant 0}(g)(\tilde{\alpha}_{\geqslant 0}(h)\tilde{\alpha}_{\geqslant 0}(k))
, from which one deduces that

\displaystyle\mathrm{Ad}_{\omega(g,h,k)}=1

(IV.9)

where

\displaystyle\omega(g,h,k):=V(g,h)V(gh,k)V(g,hk)^{-1}(\tilde{\alpha}_{\geqslant 0}(g)\triangleright V(h,k))^{-1}

(IV.10)

So
\omega(g,h,k)\in\mathrm{U}(1)
.

Moreover, by a brute force calculation (see Appendix
C.2
), one sees that
\omega(g,h,k)
satisfies a twisted 3-cocycle equation:

\displaystyle(\tilde{\delta}^{(4)}\omega)(g,h,k,l)=\frac{(\theta(g)\triangleright\omega(h,k,l))\omega(g,h,k)\omega(g,hk,l)}{\omega(gh,k,l)\omega(g,h,kl)}=1,

(IV.11)

where
\theta(g)\triangleright\omega(h,k,l)=\omega(h,k,l)
if
g
is unitary, and
\theta(g)\triangleright\omega(h,k,l)=\overline{\omega(h,k,l)}
if
g
is anti-unitary.

Also, note that
V(g,h)
in Eq. (
IV.8
) has an instrinsic phase ambiguity, because
\mathrm{Ad}_{V(g,h)}=\mathrm{Ad}_{V(g,h)/\lambda(g,h)}
for any
\lambda(g,h)\in\mathrm{U}(1)
. By replacing
V(g,h)
with
V(g,h)/\lambda(g,h)
, according to Eq. (
IV.10
),
\omega(g,h,k)
changes according to

\displaystyle\omega(g,h,k)\rightarrow\omega(g,h,k)(\tilde{\delta}^{(3)}\lambda)(g,h,k)

(IV.12)

with

\displaystyle(\tilde{\delta}^{(3)}\lambda)(g,h,k)=\frac{(\theta(g)\triangleright\lambda(h,k))\lambda(g,hk)}{\lambda(g,h)\lambda(gh,k)},

(IV.13)

Therefore, one can define a twisted cohomology group
\mathrm{H}^{3}_{\varphi}(G;\mathrm{U}(1))
:

\displaystyle\mathrm{H}^{3}_{\varphi}(G;\mathrm{U}(1))=\ker(\tilde{\delta}^{(4)})/{\rm Im}(\tilde{\delta}^{(3)}),

(IV.14)

where a unitary
g\in G
does not act on the
\mathrm{U}(1)
coefficient, while an anti-unitary
g\in G
acts on the coefficient
\mathrm{U}(1)
by complex conjugation.

Now we can define the anomaly index.

Definition IV.1
.

Given a
G
symmetry action
\tilde{\alpha}:G\rightarrow\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{lp}
where
\tilde{\alpha}(g)
has a vanishing GNVW index for all
g\in G
, its anomaly index is defined as the element in
\mathrm{H}^{3}_{\varphi}(G;\mathrm{U}(1))
, which
\omega(g,h,k)
in Eq. (
IV.10
) corresponds to.

The validity of this definition is ensured by the following theorem, which is proved in Appendix
C
.

Theorem IV.1
.

The phase factor
\omega:G^{3}\to\mathrm{U}(1)
defined in Eq. (
IV.10
) is a twisted 3-cocycle, and its corresponding cohomology class in
\mathrm{H}^{3}_{\varphi}(G;\mathrm{U}(1))
is independent of the choice of decomposition
\tilde{\alpha}_{\geqslant 0}
in Eq. (
IV.6
) and the site at which the decomposition is carried out.

Next, we turn to the more general case where some symmetry actions may be described by a twisted LPA with a nonzero GNVW index. Concretely, suppose that the image of symmetry action
\tilde{\alpha}:G\to\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{lp}
has nontrivial GNVW index, then
\tilde{\alpha}
and Eq. (
III.6
) induce the following map

\displaystyle\tau:G\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\tilde{\alpha}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{lp}\longrightarrow\text{generalized translation}

(IV.15)

So one can stack another copy of the system with the original system, on which
G
acts as
\tau(g)^{-1}
. Thus, on the composite system, the symmetry acts as
\tilde{\alpha}(g)\otimes\tau(g)^{-1}
, which has a vanishing GNVW index. This reduces the current situation to the earlier one, and the anomaly index of
\tilde{\alpha}
is defined to be the anomaly index of
\tilde{\alpha}\otimes\tau^{-1}
.

Note that
\tau(g)^{-1}
above is linear if
g
is a unitary symmetry, and
\tau(g)^{-1}
is anti-linear if
g
is an anti-unitary symmetry. One might want to demand that
\tau(g)^{-1}
should always be the ordinary linear translation operation, even if
g
is anti-unitary. However, if one does this, when
g
is an anti-unitary symmetry,
\tilde{\alpha}(g)\otimes\tau(g)^{-1}
is neither linear nor anti-linear on the composite operator algebra of the composite system, and the discussion above does not apply anymore. Therefore, we always demand that
\tau(g)^{-1}
should be anti-linear (resp. linear) if
g
is an anti-unitary (resp. unitary) symmetry. More precisely, denoting by
\tau_{0}(g)
the ordinary linear translation operation, then
\tau(g)=\tau_{0}(g)\theta(g)
, with
\theta(g)
given in Eq. (
III.9
).

From the above definition of the anomaly index, one can see that, roughly speaking, the anomaly characterizes the interplay between the local operator algebra and the symmetry action. For example, if the symmetry acts in a completely local, on-site fashion, then we can take
V(g,h)=1
in Eq. (
IV.8
). According to Eq. (
IV.10
),
\omega(g,h,k)=1
. Therefore, an on-site symmetry is anomaly free.

IV.2
Example:
G=G_{0}\times\mathbb{Z}

Now we illustrate the concept of the anomaly index in a very important example. In this example, we compute the mixed anomaly between the lattice translation symmetry and an on-site symmetry
G_{0}
(which can include anti-unitary symmetry actions), i.e.,
G=G_{0}\times\mathbb{Z}
. Here the lattice translation symmetry is the ordinary one, which is unitary, but
G_{0}
can contain unitary and anti-unitary symmetries. The calculation is very similar to Example 3.2 of Ref.
Kapustin and Sopenko 2024
, except that Ref.
Kapustin and Sopenko 2024
focuses on unitary symmetries but we treat unitary and anti-unitary symmetries on equal footing. The result is that the anomaly of the
G
symmetry is in one-to-one correspondence with the class of projective representation under
G_{0}
, which the degrees of freedom in each unit cell carry. If these degrees of freedom carry a linear representation under
G_{0}
, then the
G
symmetry is anomaly free.

Concretely, for any
g\in G_{0}
and
n\in\mathbb{Z}
, the symmetry action under consideration is formally given by

\displaystyle\tilde{\alpha}(g,n)=\tau_{0}^{n}\prod_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}\mathrm{Ad}_{\rho_{j}^{-1}(g)}\theta(g)

(IV.16)

where
\tau_{0}
is the generator of translation,
\theta(g)
is given in Eq. (
III.9
), and
\rho_{j}
is the on-site action of
G_{0}
at site
j
with a projective phase
\eta\in\mathrm{H}_{\varphi}^{2}(G_{0};\mathrm{U}(1))
, i.e.,
\rho_{j}(g)\left(\theta_{j}(g)\triangleright\rho_{j}(g^{\prime})\right)=\eta(g,g^{\prime})\rho_{j}(gg^{\prime})
for
g,g^{\prime}\in G_{0}
, where
\theta_{j}(g)=1
if
g
is unitary, and
\theta_{j}(g)=K_{j}
if
g
is anti-unitary.
5
5

5

At the
j
-th site, the
G_{0}
symmetry maps the state
|\psi_{j}\rangle
to
\rho_{j}(g)\Theta_{j}^{\varphi(g)}|\psi_{j}\rangle
, and its action on an operator
O_{j}
at this site is
O_{j}\rightarrow\mathrm{Ad}_{\rho_{j}^{-1}(g)}\theta_{g}(O_{j})
. Note the inverse in the adjoint action of the symmetry on the operator.
We assume that
\rho_{j}
’s are the same for all
j\in\mathbb{Z}
, otherwise it is not compatible with the translation symmetry.

Physically,
\tilde{\alpha}(g,n)
is a composition of translating by
n
unit cells and an on-site symmetry action, where the degrees of freedom in each unit cell form a projective representation under
G_{0}
that is described by
\eta
. For example, when
G_{0}
is the usual order-2 time reversal symmetry, i.e.,
G_{0}=\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{T}
, we have
\mathrm{H}^{2}_{\varphi}(\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{T},\mathrm{U}(1))=\mathbb{Z}_{2}
. Denoting the generator of
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{T}
by
T
and the identity element by
e
, the two elements in
\mathrm{H}^{2}_{\varphi}(\mathbb{Z}_{2},\mathrm{U}(1))
are characterized by
\eta(T,T)\eta(e,e)=\pm 1
. The case with
\eta(T,T)\eta(e,e)=1
(resp.
\eta(T,T)\eta(e,e)=-1
) corresponds to the case where the on-site degrees of freedom are Kramers singlets (resp. Kramers doublets).

The anomaly index takes value in
\mathrm{H}^{3}_{\varphi}(G_{0}\times\mathbb{Z};\mathrm{U}(1))
. By the Künneth formula (see, e.g., Chapter 3 of Ref.
Hatcher et al. 2002
),

\displaystyle\mathrm{H}^{3}_{\varphi}(G_{0}\times\mathbb{Z};\mathrm{U}(1)_{\varphi})=\mathrm{H}^{3}_{\varphi}(G_{0};\mathrm{U}(1))\oplus\mathrm{H}^{2}_{\varphi}(G_{0};\mathrm{U}(1)).

(IV.17)

The first part,
\mathrm{H}^{3}_{\varphi}(G_{0};\mathrm{U}(1))
, characterizes the anomaly of the
G_{0}
symmetry, since it originates purely from
G_{0}
and is independent of the translation symmetry. Because
G_{0}
is on-site, this part must vanish for the symmetry action under consideration, and the classification of the anomalies in the present case is given by the second part,
\mathrm{H}^{2}_{\varphi}(G_{0};\mathrm{U}(1))
. For this part, we note that, in the the Künneth formula, the map from
\mathrm{H}^{3}_{\varphi}(G_{0}\times\mathbb{Z};\mathrm{U}(1))\to\mathrm{H}^{2}_{\varphi}(G_{0};\mathrm{U}(1))
is given by the slant product (also known as the loop transgression in the mathematical literature). Explicitly, the slant product maps a twisted 3-cocycle
\omega((g,n),(g^{\prime},n^{\prime}),(g^{\prime\prime},n^{\prime\prime}))
to a 2-cocycle

\displaystyle(\omega/[1])(g,g^{\prime})=\frac{\omega((e,1),(g,0),(g^{\prime},0))\omega((g,0),(g^{\prime},0),(e,1))}{\omega((g,0),(e,1),(g^{\prime},0))}

(IV.18)

where
\omega/[1]
denotes the 2-cocycle resulted from the map, and

is the identity element in
G_{0}
. One can verify that
\omega/[1]
indeed satisfies the twisted 2-cocycle equation:

\displaystyle\left(\tilde{\delta}^{(3)}(\omega/[1])\right)(g,h,k)=\frac{(g\triangleright(\omega/[1])(h,k))(\omega/[1])(g,hk)}{(\omega/[1])(g,h)(\omega/[1])(gh,k)}=1.

(IV.19)

In general, the slant product is just a homomorphism from
\mathrm{H}^{3}_{\varphi}(G_{0}\times\mathbb{Z};\mathrm{U}(1))
to
\mathrm{H}^{2}_{\varphi}(G_{0};\mathrm{U}(1))
, but not necessarily a bijective map. In the present case, because
G_{0}
is on-site, we will see that it becomes a bijective map in the sense below.

V
Generalized Lieb-Schultz-Mattis theorems for infinite systems with anti-unitary symmetry

After defining the anomaly of a symmetry in Sec.
IV
, in this and the next sections, we explore the consequences of an anomalous symmetry. In this section, we focus on the consequences on infinite-size systems, where notions like locality are most cleanly defined. In the next section, we discuss the consequences of an anomalous symmetry on large but finite systems, which are physically relevant.

V.1
The root lemma and its corollaries

All our theorems are rooted in the following lemma, which can be viewed as a generalized Lieb-Schultz-Mattis (LSM) theorem.

Lemma V.1
.

Let
\psi
be a type-I factor state satisfying the split property, and
\tilde{\alpha}:G\to\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{lp}
be a
G
-symmetry action with anomaly index
\omega\in\mathrm{H}_{\varphi}^{3}(G;\mathrm{U}(1))
. If
\psi
is symmetric under
G
-symmetry, i.e.,
\psi=\psi_{\tilde{\alpha}(g)}
for all
g\in G
, then
\omega
vanishes.

The proof of this root lemma is presented in Appendix
D
. Here we derive some important corollaries from this lemma.

Corollary V.1
.

Let
\psi
be a (not necessarily pure) state satisfying the clustering property and the area law of entanglement entropy, and
\tilde{\alpha}:G\to\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{lp}
be a
G
symmetry action with anomaly index
\omega\in\mathrm{H}^{3}_{\varphi}(G;\mathrm{U}(1))
. If
\psi
is symmetric under
G
, i.e.,
\psi=\psi_{\tilde{\alpha}(g)}
for all
g\in G
, then
\omega
vanishes.

This corollary is a powerful and elegant theorem that concerns the interplay between correlation, entanglement and symmetry.

Proof of Corollary
V.1
.

Because
\psi
satisfies the clustering property, according to Theorem
II.1
,
\psi
is a factor state. Because
\psi
satisfies the entanglement area law, according to Lemma
II.2
and Lemma
II.4
, it is a type-I factor state that satisfies the split property. Then by the root lemma (Lemma
V.1
),
\omega
vanishes.

∎

Another corollary from the root lemma is as follows.

Corollary V.2
.

Let
\tilde{\alpha}:G\to\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{lp}
be a
G
symmetry action with anomaly index
\omega\in\mathrm{H}^{3}_{\varphi}(G;\mathrm{U}(1))
. If there exists a
G
-symmetric state pure
\psi
that satisfies the split property, then
\omega
vanishes.

When the symmetry is unitary, this corollary reduces to Remark 4.1 of Ref.
Kapustin and Sopenko 2024
, which is one of the main results of Ref.
Kapustin and Sopenko 2024
. Here we extend this result to anti-unitary symmetries. Comparing Corollary
V.1
and Corollary
V.2
, the former is often easier to use, because its statement only involves conditions like the clustering property and entanglement area law, which are relatively easy to check, thanks to the vast results in the previous literature. On the other hand, checking whether a state is pure often requires more effort, so Corollary
V.2
is relatively more difficult to apply.

Proof of Corollary
V.2
.

Because
\psi
is pure, according to Lemma
II.1
, it is a type-I factor state. Then by the root lemma (Lemma
V.1
),
\omega
vanishes.

∎

V.2
The more standard LSM theorems:

Constraints on the spectrum and symmetry-enforced long-range entanglement

Lemma
V.1
, Corollary
V.1
and Corollary
V.2
can all be viewed as some generalized versions of the LSM theorem. Using these theorems, we can deduce the more standard LSM theorems very easily.

First, we show that an anomalous symmetry imposes nontrivial constraints on the energy spectrum of the Hamiltonian.

Theorem V.1
.

Let
\tilde{\alpha}:G\to\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{lp}
be a symmetry action with a nontrivial anomaly index
\omega\in\mathrm{H}^{3}_{\varphi}(G;\mathrm{U}(1))
and
H
be an admissible
G
-symmetric Hamiltonian. Then
H
does not admit a
G
-symmetric locally unique gapped ground state.

This theorem extends Theorem V.1 of Ref.
Liu et al. 2024
to systems with anti-unitary symmetries. There are multiple ways to prove this theorem. For example, we can prove it by using Corollary
V.1
. First, we note that the locally unique gapped ground states of an admissible Hamiltonian satisfy the clustering property (see Theorem 2.8 of Ref.
Hastings and Koma 2006
). Second, it is known that the locally unique gapped ground states of an admissible Hamiltonian satisfy the entanglement area law (see Theorem 4.4 of Ref.
Ukai 2025
and Theorem IV.1 of Ref.
Liu et al. 2024
). Therefore, according to Corollary
V.1
, an admissible Hamiltonian with an anomalous symmetry cannot have a
G
-symmetric locally unique gapped ground states.

Another version of the LSM theorem, which shows that an anomalous symmetry forces a system to be long-range entangled, can also be derived.

Theorem V.2
.

Let
\tilde{\alpha}:G\to\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{lp}
be an anomalous
G
symmetry action, i.e., its anomaly index is nontrivial. Let
\psi
be a
G
-symmetric state, i.e.,
\psi=\psi_{\tilde{\alpha}(g)}
for all
g\in G
. Then
\psi
is long-range entangled.

In this paper, we define long-range entangled (LRE) states and short-range entangled (SRE) states following Ref.
Liu et al. 2024
. In an infinite-size system, a state is considered as an SRE state if it can be disentangled (i.e., converted to a product state) by a finite-time evolution generated by an almost local Hamiltonian, otherwise it is an LRE state. Here an almost local Hamiltonian is a Hamiltonian that can contain non-local interactions, but as the spatial sizes of these interactions increase, their magnitudes decay faster than any polynomial function.

Theorem
V.2
provides powerful constraints on the entanglement structure of a state invariant under an anomalous symmetry, and it implies that systems with anomalous symmetries are natural platforms to look for LRE states. As discussed in Ref.
Liu et al. 2024
, because we define SRE and LRE states based on time evolutions generated by almost local Hamiltonians, rather than finite-depth quantum circuits or time evolutions generated by strictly local Hamiltonians, the symmetry-enforced long-range entanglement proved here is a very strong statement. Moreover, our results actually also imply that an anomalous symmetry is not comptible with an invertible state, which, when being stacked with another state, becomes an SRE state.

Theorem
V.2
can also be proved by using any of Lemma
V.1
, Corollary
V.1
and Corollary
V.2
. For example, to prove Theorem
V.2
based on Lemma
V.1
, we first note that an SRE is a pure state (see, for example, footnote 43 of Ref.
Liu et al. 2024
), so it is a type-I factor state. Second, as shown in Lemma 4.2 in Ref.
Kapustin et al. 2021
, an SRE state satisfies the area law of entanglement. So according to Lemma
II.4
, an SRE state satisfies the split property. Then Lemma
V.1
shows that an SRE state is not compatible with an anomalous symmetry.

VI
Consequences for finite-size systems

Our analysis above applies to infinite-size quantum spin chains. One may wonder how the main theorems apply to realistic physical systems, which, though large, are always finite. For the cases with only unitary symmetries, this question is systematically addressed in the last appendix of Ref.
Liu et al. 2024
. Below we will describe the finite-size versions of Theorem
V.1
and Theorem
V.2
, which generalizes the results in Ref.
Liu et al. 2024
to cases with anti-unitary symmetries.

VI.1
Setup for thermodynamic limits

To begin, we will give a sharp definition of the thermodynamic limits of a squence of finite-size states, automorphisms and Hamiltonians.

Let
\Lambda\simeq\mathbb{Z}
be the infinite lattice and fix an increasing sequence of finite subsets,

\displaystyle\Gamma_{1}\subseteq\Gamma_{2}\dots\subseteq\Lambda,\quad|\Gamma_{L}|<\infty

(VI.1)

indexed by an integer
L
, which can be thought of as the size of a finite system. We assume each
\Gamma_{L}
is connected and it eventually exhausts
\Lambda
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\bigcup_{L=1}^{\infty}\Gamma_{L}=\Lambda

(VI.2)

Let
\psi_{L}:\mathscr{A}_{\Gamma_{L}}\to\mathbb{C}
be a sequence of quantum states. we formally extend them to be a state of
\mathscr{A}^{ql}
as follows. Let
\Omega
be a fixed pure product state of
\mathscr{A}^{ql}
(which models the environment), then
\Omega_{L}:=\Omega|_{\Gamma_{L}^{c}}
is another pure product state on
\mathscr{A}_{\Gamma_{L}^{c}}
,where
\Omega|_{\Gamma_{L^{c}}}
is the restriction of
\Omega
on
\Gamma_{L^{c}}
. We define
\tilde{\psi}_{L}:=\psi_{L}\otimes\Omega_{L}
, which is a state on
\mathscr{A}^{ql}
. Formalizing this idea, we define a sequence of finite-size states as follows.

Definition VI.1
.

Fix a pure product state
\Omega:\mathscr{A}^{ql}\to\mathbb{C}
, a sequence of finite-size states
\{\tilde{\psi}_{L}\}_{L=1,2,\dots}
means

\displaystyle\tilde{\psi}_{L}:=\psi_{L}\otimes\Omega|_{\Gamma_{L}^{c}}

(VI.3)

where
\psi_{L}
is a state of
\mathscr{A}_{\Gamma_{L}}
.

Having a sequence of states, a natural task is to define the notion of convergence. In the following, we will only use the so-called
weak-
*
convergence
of sequence of states, which physically means that the expectation values of local observables converge in the thermodynamic limit.

Definition VI.2

(weak-
*
convergence of states)
.

Let
\{\tilde{\psi}_{L}\}
be a sequence of states of
\mathscr{A}^{ql}
defined as above. We say that this sequence of states weak-
*
converges to a state
\psi
in the infinite-size system if for any
\epsilon>0
and
A\in\mathscr{A}^{l}
, there exists a constant
L_{\epsilon,A}
such that
\forall\,L>L_{\epsilon,A}
, we have

\displaystyle|\tilde{\psi}_{L}(A)-\psi(A)|<\epsilon||A||

(VI.4)

In this case, we say that
\psi
is the weak-
*
limit of
\psi_{L}
as
L\to\infty
and write
\lim_{L\to\infty}\psi_{L}=\psi
.

In other words, weak-
*
convergence means for any fixed finite region
\Gamma
, the reduced density matrix of
\psi
on
\Gamma
converges in the trace norm. This equivalent characterization does not depend on the choice of
\Omega
(i.e. the environment).

Given a sequence of states, its weak-
*
limit may not exist. However, as discussed in Ref.
Liu et al. 2024
, the celebrated Banach-Alaoglu-Bourbaki theorem ensures that one can always find a weak-
*
convergent subsequence.

It will also be useful to define the notion of convergence for Hamiltonians and automorphisms. Let
\tilde{\alpha}_{L}
be a twisted
*
-automorphism of
\mathscr{A}_{\Gamma_{L}}
, and we formally extend it to
\mathscr{A}^{ql}
by viewing it as
\tilde{\alpha}_{L}\otimes\mathrm{id}_{\Gamma_{L}^{c}}
if it is linear, and as
\tilde{\alpha}_{L}\otimes K_{\Gamma_{L}^{c}}
if it is anti-linear, where
K_{\Gamma_{L^{c}}}=\prod_{j\in\Gamma_{L^{c}}}K_{j}
. Again, by abusing notations, we will write
\tilde{\alpha}_{L}
rather than
\tilde{\alpha}_{L}\otimes\mathrm{id}_{\Gamma_{L}^{c}}
or
\tilde{\alpha}_{L}\otimes K_{\Gamma_{L}^{c}}
. Therefore, we have a natural notion of finite-size sequence of twisted automorphisms (and similarly, sequence of finite-size Hamiltonians).

Definition VI.3
.

A sequence of finite-size twisted
*
-automorphisms on
\mathscr{A}^{ql}

\{\tilde{\alpha}_{L}\}_{L=1,2,\cdots}
means
\{\tilde{\alpha}_{L}\otimes\theta(\tilde{\alpha}_{L})\}_{L=1,2,\dots}
, where each
\tilde{\alpha}_{L}
is a twisted
*
-automorphism on
\mathscr{A}_{\Gamma_{L}}
, and
\theta(\tilde{\alpha}_{L})=\mathrm{id}_{\Gamma_{L}^{c}}
if
\tilde{\alpha}_{L}
is linear, while
\theta(\tilde{\alpha}_{L})=K_{\Gamma_{L}^{c}}=\prod_{j\in\Gamma_{L}^{c}}K_{j}
if
\tilde{\alpha}_{L}
is anti-linear. Similarly, a sequence of finite-size Hamiltonians
\{H_{L}\}_{L=1,2,\dots}
means
\{H_{L}\otimes\mathrm{id}_{\Gamma^{c}_{L}}\}_{L=1,2,\dots}
, where each
H_{L}
is a self-adjoint operator on
\Gamma_{L}
.

However, merely a sequence does not buy us much. We now define the strong convergence of such finite-size sequences of twisted
*
-automorphisms and finite-size Hamiltonians.

Definition VI.4
.

Let
\tilde{\alpha}_{L}\in\widetilde{\mathrm{Aut}}(\mathscr{A}_{\Gamma_{L}})
, and consider the sequence
\{\tilde{\alpha}_{L}\}_{L\in\mathbb{N}}
.
If for any fixed
A\in\mathscr{A}^{l}
and
\epsilon>0
, there exists
\tilde{\alpha}\in\widetilde{\mathrm{Aut}}(\mathscr{A}^{ql})
and
L_{\epsilon,A}>0
, such that for any
L>L_{\epsilon,A}
,

\displaystyle||\tilde{\alpha}_{L}(A)-\tilde{\alpha}(A)||<\epsilon||A||

(VI.5)

Then we say
\{\tilde{\alpha}_{L}\}
strongly converges to
\tilde{\alpha}
as
L\to\infty
and write
\lim_{L\to\infty}\tilde{\alpha}_{L}=\tilde{\alpha}
.

Similarly, if for any fixed
A\in\mathscr{A}^{\ell}
and
\epsilon>0
, there exists a densely defined derivation
\delta_{H}
and
L_{\epsilon,A}>0
, such that for any
L>L_{\epsilon,A}
,

\displaystyle||\delta_{H}(A)-[H_{L},A]||<\epsilon||A||

(VI.6)

Then we say
\mathrm{ad}_{H_{L}}:=[H_{L},\cdot]
strongly converges to
\delta_{H}
and write
\lim_{L\to\infty}\mathrm{ad}_{H_{L}}=\delta_{H}
. We say
H_{L}
is admissible if
H
is admissible.

The following lemma shows that these notions of convergences fit nicely.

Lemma VI.1

(Lemma G.1 of Ref.
Liu et al. 2024
)
.

Consider sequences of finite-size states
\{\psi_{L}\}_{L=1,2,\dots}
, finite-size
*
-automorphisms
\{\tilde{\alpha}_{L}\}_{L=1,2,\dots}
and finite-size Hamiltonians
\{H_{L}\}_{L=1,2,\dots}
. Assume that

\displaystyle\begin{split}\lim_{L\to\infty}\psi_{L}&=\psi\\
\lim_{L\to\infty}\tilde{\alpha}_{L}&=\tilde{\alpha}\\
\lim_{L\to\infty}[H_{L},\cdot]&=\delta_{H}\end{split}

(VI.7)

Then

1.

\lim_{L\to\infty}\psi_{L}(A)=\psi(A),\quad\forall\,A\in\mathscr{A}^{ql}
.

2.

\lim_{L\to\infty}\psi_{L\tilde{\alpha}_{L}}=\psi_{\tilde{\alpha}}
.

3.

\lim_{L\to\infty}\psi_{L}(B\,\mathrm{ad}_{H_{L}}(A))=\psi(B\,\delta_{H}(A)),\quad\forall\,A\in D(\delta_{H},)\,B\in\mathscr{A}^{ql}
.

where
D(\delta_{H})\supseteq\mathscr{A}^{\ell}
is the domain of
\delta_{H}
.

This lemma generalizes Lemma G.1 of Ref.
Liu and Ye 2025
to include anti-linear automorphisms. The proof of this lemma is straightforward and very similar to the proof of Lemma G.1 in
Liu et al. 2024
, so we do not reproduce it here.

VI.2
Consequence on the spectrum

Now we arrive at the core of this section, i.e., the finite-size versions of Theorem
V.1
and Theorem
V.2
.

Theorem VI.1
.

Let
\alpha_{L}:G\to\widetilde{\mathrm{Aut}}(\mathscr{A}_{\Gamma_{L}})
be a strongly convergent sequence of symmetry actions and
H_{L}
be a strongly convergent sequence of Hamiltonians. If

1.

\tilde{\alpha}:=\lim_{L\to\infty}\tilde{\alpha}_{L}
is a symmetry action described by (twisted) LPA.

2.

\delta_{H}:=\lim_{L\to\infty}\mathrm{ad}_{H_{L}}
is an admissible Hamiltonian.

3.

H_{L}
is invariant under
\tilde{\alpha}_{L}(g)
for all
L
and
g\in G
.

4.

H_{L}
has a unique gapped ground state with uniformly lower bounded energy gap
\Delta_{L}\geqslant\Delta>0
.

Then
\tilde{\alpha}
has trivial anomaly index.

This theorem, where the symmetry can contain both unitary and anti-unitary symmetries, is a generalization of Theorem G.3 in Ref.
Liu et al. 2024
, which only discusses unitary symmetries. The same proof as in Ref.
Liu et al. 2024
applies here, once the LPA discussed in Ref.
Liu et al. 2024
are properly replaced by their twisted versions. For this reason, we do not reproduce the full details here.

As a corollary, we have the finite-size version of Theorem
V.1
.

Corollary VI.1
.

If a sequence of finite-size spin chains have admissible Hamiltonians
H_{L}
which are symmetric under an anomalous symmetry, then
H_{L}
cannot have a unique gapped ground state for sufficiently large
L
.

Note that the maximal size of such systems that allows a unique gapped ground state depends on the details of
H_{L}
and
\alpha_{L}
, and is hence non-universal.

Remark VI.1
.

Although we focus on open boundary conditions in above discussion, our Theorem
VI.1
and Corollary
VI.1
hold valid for systems with periodic boundary conditions as well (see Appendix G.5 of Ref.
Liu et al. 2024
for detailed discussion on the periodic boundary condition).

VI.3
Symmetry-enforced long-range entanglement in finite-size systems

Next, we turn to symmetry-enforced long-range entanglement, i.e., any state invariant under an anomalous symmetry must be long-range entangled, as stated in Theorem
V.2
.

We will present the finite-size version of symmetry-enforced long-range entanglement, which is much more nontrivial and stronger than Theorem
V.2
. In this context, we say that a sequence of finite but large systems is SRE if each of them can be disentangled by a time evolution generated by an almost local Hamiltonian over a duration that does not diverge as the system size goes to infinity, otherwise it is LRE. Then we have the following finite-size version of Theorem
V.2
.

Theorem VI.2
.

Let
\tilde{\alpha}_{L}
be an operation on a system with size
L
, and suppose that
\tilde{\alpha}_{L}
converges to an anomalous symmetry
\tilde{\alpha}:G\to\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{lp}
as
L\rightarrow\infty
. Let
\{|\psi_{L}\rangle\}
be a sequence of SRE states defined on a system of size
L
. Then
|\psi_{L}\rangle
is symmetric under
\alpha_{L}
for at most finitely many
L
.

This theorem extends Theorem VI. 2 in Ref.
Liu et al. 2024
, which only considers unitary anomalous symmetries, to situations that can include anti-unitary anomalous symmetries. Its proof is also very similar to that of Theorem VI. 2 in Ref.
Liu et al. 2024
. To prove this theorem, we first note that such a sequence of states must have a subsequence that has a well-defined thermodynamic limit, due to the Banach-Alaoglu-Bourbaki theorem. This thermodynamic limit can be shown to satisfy the clustering property and entanglement area law (see the proof of Theorem G.5 in Ref.
Liu et al. 2024
). Hence a symmetric SRE can be ruled out by the powerful Corollary
V.1
.

Remark VI.2
.

In the above theorem, we do not need to assume that this sequence of finite-size states converge to an infinite-size version of SRE state, and this is why this theorem is so nontrivial and strong. In fact, it is currently not known if our definition on SRE sequences ensures that its weak-
*
limit (if exists) is an infinite-size version of SRE state, since the convergence of states does not imply the convergence of disentanglers even if we restrict ourselves to a subsequence.

VII
Applications

After proving our main theorems, we present some representative applications of them, where anti-unitary symmetries play an important role.

VII.1
Quantum spin chains with Dyzaloshinski-Moriya interactions

We first consider a quantum spin chain with a Dyzaloshinski-Moriya (DM) interaction, whose Hamiltonian is

\displaystyle H=\sum_{i,j}\left(J_{ij}\vec{S}_{i}\cdot\vec{S}_{j}+\vec{D}_{ij}\cdot(\vec{S}_{i}\times\vec{S}_{j})\right)

(VII.1)

where
\vec{S}_{i}
is the spin operator at the site
i
, and
J_{ij}
and
\vec{D}_{ij}
are coupling constants that are assumed to decay faster than
\left({\rm dist}(i,j)\right)^{-2}
. In this Hamiltonian, the first Heisenberg term preserves the
SO(3)
spin rotational symmetry and the
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{T}
time reversal symmetry that takes
\vec{S}_{i}\rightarrow-\vec{S}_{i}
. The second term is the DM interaction arising from spin-orbit coupling, which breaks the
SO(3)
symmetry but preserves the
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{T}
symmetry. It is further assumed that this Hamiltonian is translation invariant. So this Hamiltonian has a
G=\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{T}\times\mathbb{Z}
symmetry.

As demonstrated in Sec.
IV.2
, this
G
symmetry is anomaly free if the local moments are Kramers singlets, and it is anomalous if the local moments are Kramers doublets. In the latter case, we can conclude that the Hamiltonian cannot have a unique gapped symmetric ground state, according to Theorem
V.1
and Corollary
VI.1
, and any state invariant under the
G
symmetry must be long-range entangled, according to Theorem
V.2
and Theorem
VI.2
. Moreover, by Corollary
V.1
, a
G
-symmetric state in such a system cannot satisfy both the clustering property and entanglement area law.

VII.2
Chiral spin chains with an anti-unitary translation symmetry

In Ref.
Yao et al. 2024
, a class of models for spin-
S
chiral spin chains are studied, with the Hamiltonian

\displaystyle H=\sum_{i\alpha}J_{\alpha}S^{\alpha}_{i}S^{\alpha}_{i+1}+\sum_{i\alpha\beta\gamma}(-1)^{i}K_{\alpha\beta\gamma}\epsilon_{\alpha\beta\gamma}S^{\alpha}_{i}S^{\beta}_{i+1}S^{\gamma}_{i+2}

(VII.2)

For general values of
J_{\alpha}
and
K_{\alpha\beta\gamma}
, this model does not have the full
SO(3)
spin rotational symmetry. Instead, it has a unitary
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{x}\times\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{z}
symmetry, which can be formally generated by
X=\mathrm{Ad}_{R^{x}}
and
Z=\mathrm{Ad}_{R^{z}}
, with

\displaystyle R^{x}=\prod_{i}e^{i\pi S_{i}^{x}},\quad R^{z}=\prod_{i}e^{i\pi S_{i}^{z}}.

(VII.3)

Interestingly, there is also an anti-unitary translation symmetry
\mathbb{Z}^{T}
, which is generated by
T_{2}=T_{1}T
, where
T_{1}
is the ordinary linear translation, and
T_{2}
is the the usual time reversal action for spins, under which
\vec{S}_{i}\rightarrow-\vec{S}_{i}
.
As the combination of
T_{1}
and
T
,
T_{2}
acts on the spin operators as

\displaystyle T_{2}(\vec{S}_{i})=-\vec{S}_{i+1}.

(VII.4)

Based on twisted boundary condition and robustness of spectrum, Ref.
Yao et al. 2024
argues that such models cannot have a unique symmetric gapped ground state if
S\in\mathbb{Z}+\frac{1}{2}
. Below we show that the
G=\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{x}\times\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{z}\times\mathbb{Z}^{T}
symmetry has a nontrivial (resp. trivial) anomaly index if
S\in\mathbb{Z}+\frac{1}{2}
(resp.
S\in\mathbb{Z}
). Then we will comment on the consequences of the anomaly.

The simplest way to obtain the anomaly index in the present case is to note that
G
is a subgroup of
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{x}\times\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{z}\times\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{T}\times\mathbb{Z}
, where
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{T}
is viewed as the usual time reversal symmetry with generator
T
, and
\mathbb{Z}
is viewed as the ordinary linear translation symmetry with generator
T_{1}
. There is a natural inclusion map
i:G\to\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{x}\times\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{z}\times\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{T}\times\mathbb{Z}
. Specifically, denote the group element
(R^{x})^{x}(R^{z})^{z}T_{2}^{n}\in G
by
(x,z,n)
, where
x\in\{0,1\}
,
z\in\{0,1\}
and
n\in\mathbb{Z}
. Then under the map
i
it becomes
i[(x,z,n)]=(x,z,n,n):=(R^{x})^{x}(R^{z})^{z}T^{t}T_{1}^{n}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{x}\times\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{z}\times\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{T}\times\mathbb{Z}
. Suppose that the system originally has the
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{x}\times\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{z}\times\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{T}\times\mathbb{Z}
symmetry, which is later broken to the
G
symmetry. According to Eq. (
IV.23
), if
S\in\mathbb{Z}
, the anomaly index of the virtual original
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{x}\times\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{z}\times\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{T}\times\mathbb{Z}
is already trivial, so the anomaly of the
G
symmetry, which is its pullback induced by the map
i
, is also trivial. On the other hand, if
S\in\mathbb{Z}+\frac{1}{2}
, the anomaly index of this virtual original
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{x}\times\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{z}\times\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{T}\times\mathbb{Z}
symmetry would be

\displaystyle\omega_{0}((x_{1},z_{1},t_{1},n_{1}),(x_{2},z_{2},t_{2},n_{2}),(x_{3},z_{3},t_{3},n_{3}))=(-1)^{(x_{1}z_{2}+t_{1}t_{2})n_{3}}.

(VII.5)

Therefore, when
S\in\mathbb{Z}+\frac{1}{2}
, the anomaly index of the
G
symmetry, which is the pullback of the above
\omega_{0}
induced by the inclusion map
i
, is

\displaystyle\begin{split}\omega((x_{1},z_{1},n_{1}),(x_{2},z_{2},n_{2}),(x_{3},z_{3},n_{3}))&=(i^{*}\omega_{0})((x_{1},z_{1},n_{1}),(x_{2},z_{2},n_{2}),(x_{3},z_{3},n_{3}))\\
&=\omega_{0}(i[(x_{1},z_{1},n_{1})],i[(x_{2},z_{2},n_{2})],i[(x_{3},z_{3},n_{3})])\\
&=(-1)^{(x_{1}z_{2}+n_{1}n_{2})n_{3}}\end{split}

(VII.6)

One can check that this
\omega
indeed satisfies the 3-cocycle equation, Eq. (
IV.11
). Moreover, it is shown in Appendix
E
that Eq. (
VII.6
) indeed represents a nontrivial element in
H_{\varphi}^{3}(G;\mathrm{U}(1))
.

Therefore, when
S\in\mathbb{Z}+\frac{1}{2}
we can apply our theorems to extract some physical consequences. In particular, we can conclude that not only the Hamiltonian Eq. (
VII.2
) but also its long-range interacting versions satisfying the admissible condition Eq. (
II.30
) cannot have a unique gapped symmetric ground state, according to Theorem
V.1
and Corollary
VI.1
, and any state invariant under the
G
symmetry must be long-range entangled, according to Theorem
V.2
and Theorem
VI.2
. Moreover, by Corollary
V.1
, a
G
-symmetric state in such a system cannot satisfy both the clustering property and entanglement area law.

VIII
Discussion

In this work, we extend the previous discussions on the anomalies of unitary symmetries and their consequences to systems that can have anti-unitary symmetries, by introducing twisted locality-preserving automorphisms, which describe unitary and anti-unitary symmetries in a unified fashion. We find that anomalous symmetries have important implications on the correlation, entanglement, and energy spectra of the quantum spin chains, as summarized by various generalized LSM-like theorems. These general results are demonstrated in various concrete examples.

We remark that our results apply to a very broad class of systems, whose symmetry can include both unitary and anti-unitary symmetries, on-site or non-on-site symmetries, internal and translation symmetries. The Hamiltonian of the system can be either local or non-local, as long as it satisfies Eq. (
II.30
). Moreover, we derive results for both infinite-size quantum spin chains and sequences of large but finite-size quantum spin chains.

It is important to note that our discussions of symmetries and their anomalies are entirely formulated using microscopic, Hamiltonian-independent data of a lattice system, and we do not assume any description of the system in terms of any effective theory, such as a quantum field theory. In many of the previous studies, certain assumptions about the low-energy and large-distance physics of the quantum many-body system are assumed, which often lack rigorous microscopic justification. We believe that the ultimate understanding of quantum matter should be formulated using theoretical frameworks that have the same spirit as the one in this paper, which describes the universal properties of a quantum many-body system in terms of its microscopic data.

Below we comment on some open questions for future studies.

•

So far our framework does not capture point-group symmetry, because such a symmetry can map a local operator to another one with a support very far away from the original one, which cannot be realized by a twisted locality-preserving automorphism. It is useful to extend our framework to also include these symmetries.

•

Our discussion focuses on quantum spin chains. It is interesting to generalize our results to fermionic systems and bosonic systems where the dimension of each local Hilbert space is infinite.

•

The overarching goal is to have a unified framework to describe the symmetries and anomalies for all quantum many-body systems, including systems in higher dimensions, systems living in the continuum, etc.

•

We derive nontrivial constraints on the system due to an anomalous symmetry. However, even if a symmetry is not anomalous, there can still be nontrivial constraint if the system is restricted to a specific symmetry sector. For example, if a system has a
U(1)\times\mathbb{Z}
symmetry, where
U(1)
is on-site and
\mathbb{Z}
is translation, then this symmetry is anomaly free. However, as the original LSM theorem states, as long as the filling factor is not an integer, there are still nontrival constraints due to this
U(1)\times\mathbb{Z}
symmetry
Lieb et al. 1961
. A more recent example concerns a system with only translation symmetry, which is anomaly free, and it is demonstrated in Ref.
Gioia and Wang 2022
that a translation symmetric system with a nonzero momentum must be long-range entangled.
6
6

6

The notion of long-range entanglement discussed in Ref.
Gioia and Wang 2022
is different from the one discussed in the present paper.
It is interesting to incorporate these examples in a unified framework.

•

In Ref.
Zou et al. 2021
, a hypothesis of emergibility was proposed, which conjectures that the landscape of quantum phases of matter that can emerge in a lattice system is determined by the symmetry and anomaly of the lattice system. This hypothesis was then applied to carry out the classifications of various quantum phases of matter
Ye et al. 2022
;
Ye and Zou 2024
;
Liu and Ye 2025
. It is useful to have a microscopic justification of this hypothesis.

Anti-linear maps and complex conjugation

Anti-linear maps and complex conjugations are extensively used in this paper. In this appendix, we provide a brief review of these concepts (see Refs.

A.1
Complex conjugation of vector spaces

To deal with anti-linear maps, an important idea is to reduce anti-linear maps to linear maps. This way of thinking should be familiar from the treatment of anti-unitary symmetries in ordinary quantum mechanics, but here we present a more formal discussion that is useful for some of our purposes.

First, we introduce the complex conjugate of a vector space
V
over
\mathbb{C}
.

Definition A.1

(Complex conjugate vector space)
.

Given a vector space
V
over
\mathbb{C}
, its complex conjugate,
\bar{V}
, is another vector space over
\mathbb{C}
, which has

1.

the same underlying set as
V
. In other words, if
v\in V
, then
v\in\bar{V}
.

2.

the same addition as
V
, i.e.,
V
and
\bar{V}
are identical as Abelian groups.

3.

a different scalar multiplication defined as
\lambda\star v:=\bar{\lambda}v
, where we have used the scalar multiplication of
V
on the right hand side.

From this point of view, an anti-linear operator
T
on
V
is nothing but a
\mathbb{C}
-linear
map
T:V\to\bar{V}
. To see it, note that for such a
\mathbb{C}
-linear map
T:V\to\bar{V}
,

\displaystyle T(\lambda v)=\lambda\star T(v)=\bar{\lambda}T(v),

(A.1)

hence
T
can be viewed as an anti-linear map on
V
in the usual sense. Alternatively, an anti-linear operator can also be viewed as a linear map
\bar{T}:\bar{V}\to V
, defined by

\displaystyle\bar{T}(\lambda\star v):=\lambda T(v).

(A.2)

An anti-linear operator
T
on
V
is called a complex conjugation (also known as a real structure)
7
7

7

What we really mean by
T^{2}=\mathrm{id}_{V}
is that
T\bar{T}=\mathrm{id}_{\bar{V}}
and
\bar{T}T=\mathrm{id}_{V}
.
if
T^{2}=\mathrm{id}_{V}
. In the case of a finite dimensional vector space, such operators always exist. One way to explicitly construct a complex conjugation is to choose a complete set of orthonormal basis and demand that an anti-linear map takes each of these basis states to itself. It can be verified that such an anti-linear map is a complex conjugation. However, just as there is no natural choice for the complete set of orthonomal basis, there is also no natural choice for the complex conjugation operator. Physically, this means that the “complex conjugation operator” is always basis-dependent.
More generally, we have

Corollary A.1
.

Taking complex conjugate
\bar{\cdot}
is a functor on the category of finite-dimensional complex vector spaces
\textbf{Vec}_{\mathbb{C}}
. Furthermore, this functor is monoidal (i.e., it preserves tensor product).

Proof.

To check the functoriality, we have to show that for any map
f:V\to W
between objects of
\textbf{Vec}_{\mathbb{C}}
, there is an induced map
\bar{f}:\bar{V}\to\bar{W}
. This map can be constructed as follows,

\displaystyle\bar{f}(v)

\displaystyle:=f(v),\quad\forall v\in V

(A.3)

Obviously
\bar{f}
defined above preserves additions. For scalar multiplication, we have

\displaystyle\begin{split}\bar{f}(\lambda\star v)&=\bar{f}(\bar{\lambda}v)\\
&=f(\bar{\lambda}v)\\
&=\bar{\lambda}f(v)\\
&=\lambda\star\bar{f}(v)\end{split}

(A.4)

Hence
\bar{f}:\bar{V}\to\bar{W}
is a linear map induced by
f:V\to W
. Moreover, it is easy to see
\bar{\mathrm{id}_{V}}=\mathrm{id}_{\bar{V}}
and
\overline{f\circ g}=\bar{f}\circ\bar{g}
for linear maps
f:V\to W
and
g:U\to V
. So
\bar{\cdot}
is indeed a functor.

Next, we check that it is monoidal. More precisely, we show there is a natural isomorphism

\displaystyle\overline{V\otimes W}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sim}}{{\longrightarrow}}\bar{V}\otimes\bar{W}.

(A.5)

The natural isomorphism is constructed as follows. Since
\overline{V\otimes W}
and
\bar{V}\otimes\bar{W}
are both generated by elements of the form
v\otimes w
, we only have to define the isomorphism on these generators:

\displaystyle F(v\otimes w)=v\otimes w

(A.6)

Obviously
F
extends additively. The only thing to check is scalar multiplication. Note

\displaystyle\begin{split}F(\lambda\star(v\otimes w))&=F(\bar{\lambda}(v\otimes w))\\
&=F((\lambda\star v)\otimes w)\\
&=(\lambda\star v)\otimes w\\
&=v\otimes(\lambda\star w)\end{split}

(A.7)

So
F
extends to a linear map between
\overline{V\otimes W}
and
\bar{V}\otimes\bar{W}
. It is obvious that
F
is injective and surjective. Hence it is an isomorphism.
∎

The above statement has an important consequence.

Corollary A.2
.

Let
T_{k},\,k=1,2
be anti-linear operators on
V_{k},\,k=1,2
, then
T_{1}\otimes T_{2}
is an anti-linear operator on
V_{1}\otimes V_{2}
.

Proof.

By assumption, we have
T_{k}:\bar{V}_{k}\to V_{k}
, so

\displaystyle T_{1}\otimes T_{2}:\bar{V}_{1}\otimes\bar{V}_{2}\to V_{1}\otimes V_{2}

(A.8)

On the other hand, we know that
F:\overline{V\otimes W}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sim}}{{\longrightarrow}}\bar{V}\otimes\bar{W}
. So
(T_{1}\otimes T_{2})\circ F:\overline{V_{1}\otimes V_{2}}\to V_{1}\otimes V_{2}
is an anti-linear operator on
V_{1}\otimes V_{2}
. In practice, we often omit
F
because it is a natural isomorphism and say
T_{1}\otimes T_{2}
is an anti-linear operator on
V_{1}\otimes V_{2}
.
∎

A further remark is

Remark A.1
.

Just like

. So we identify them below.

. If we define

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathcal{L}_{n}&:=\alpha(\mathcal{B}_{n})\cap\mathcal{C}_{n},\\
\mathcal{R}_{n}&:=\alpha(\mathcal{B}_{n})\cap\mathcal{C}_{n+1},\end{split}

(B.3)

then, obviously, we have
\mathcal{L}_{n}\otimes\mathcal{R}_{n-1}\subset\mathcal{C}_{n}
. Moreover, it turns out that the reverse inclusion is also true.

Lemma B.1

(Theorem 4.1 of Ref.
Ranard et al. 2022
)
.

We have the following tensor-factorization of algebras

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathcal{C}_{n}&:=\mathscr{A}_{2n-1}\otimes\mathscr{A}_{2n}=\mathcal{L}_{n}\otimes\mathcal{R}_{n-1},\\
\mathcal{B}_{n}&:=\mathscr{A}_{2n}\otimes\mathscr{A}_{2n+1}=\alpha^{-1}(\mathcal{L}_{n})\otimes\alpha^{-1}(\mathcal{R}_{n}).\end{split}

(B.4)

Based on this lemma, we can dfine the GNVW index of a QCA.

Definition B.1

(GNVW index of QCA)
.

For a nearest-neighbor QCA
\alpha
of a spin chain, its index is defined as
12
12

12

Our definition includes a minus sign compared to Ref.
Ranard et al. 2022
.

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathrm{ind}(\alpha)&:=\frac{1}{2}(\log(\dim(\mathcal{R}_{n}))-\log(\dim(\mathscr{A}_{2n+1})))\\
&=\frac{1}{2}(\log(\dim(\mathscr{A}_{2n}))-\log(\dim(\mathcal{L}_{n})))\end{split}

(B.5)

The second equality is due to
\dim(\mathscr{A}_{2n-1}\otimes\mathscr{A}_{2n})=\dim(\mathcal{L}_{n}\otimes\mathcal{R}_{n-1})
. The GNVW index takes value in
\mathbb{Z}[\{\log(p_{j})\}_{j\in J}]
where
p_{j}
’s are prime divisors of
D
, the dimension of local Hilbert space, as explained in Eq. (
II.12
). It can be shown that
\mathrm{ind}:\mathcal{G}^{\mathrm{QCA}}\to\mathbb{Z}[\{\log(p_{j})\}_{j\in J}]
is a group homomorphism
Gross et al. 2012
.

As an example, the GNVW index of block-paritioned unitaries (BPU, see Eq. (
II.10
)) vanishes. To see this, one can re-group the lattice such that this BPU acts in an on-site way. In this case,
\alpha(\mathscr{A}_{k})=\mathscr{A}_{k}\,,\,\forall k\in\mathbb{Z}
, so
\mathcal{L}_{n}=\mathscr{A}_{2n}
. As a result,

\displaystyle\mathrm{ind}(\alpha)=\frac{1}{2}(\log(\dim(\mathscr{A}_{2n}))-\log(\dim(\mathcal{L}_{n})))=0

(B.6)

Recall that finite-depth quantum circuits are finite compositions of BPU’s, since
\mathrm{ind}
is a group homomorphism, they must have a vanishing GNVW index as well.

In the case of shift
\tau
(i.e., translation by
+1
),
\mathcal{L}_{n}=\mathbb{C}
, so

\displaystyle\mathrm{ind}(\tau)=\frac{1}{2}\log(\dim(\mathscr{A}_{2n}))=\log(D)

(B.7)

where
D=\dim\mathcal{H}_{2n}
.

We briefly comment on generalizing above definition to
\mathcal{G}^{lp}
. In Ref.
Ranard et al. 2022
, it is proved that any LPA can be well approximated by a sequence of QCA, i.e., for each
\alpha\in\mathcal{G}^{lp}
, there exists a sequence
\{\beta_{n}\}_{n=1,2,...}
such that

\displaystyle\alpha=\lim_{n\to\infty}\beta_{n}

(B.8)

so one can define

\displaystyle\mathrm{ind}(\alpha):=\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathrm{ind}(\beta_{n})

(B.9)

It can be shown that
\mathrm{ind}(\alpha)
is well-defined, i.e., it does not depend on the choice of
\beta_{n}
(see Ref.
Ranard et al. 2022
for more information).

B.2
GNVW index for twisted QCA and twisted LPA

With the above background, in this sub-section, we generalize the notion of GNVW index to
\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{\mathrm{QCA}}
and
\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{lp}
. We will focus on
\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{\mathrm{QCA}}
first and then extend the discussion to
\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{lp}
.

As noted in the main text (see Eq. (
III.6
)), for any anti-linear
\tilde{\alpha}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{\mathrm{QCA}}
, we always have

\displaystyle\tilde{\alpha}=\alpha K,\quad\alpha\in\mathcal{G}^{\mathrm{QCA}}.

(B.10)

We define the GNVW index of
\tilde{\alpha}
as follows.

Definition B.2

(GNVW index for
\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{\mathrm{QCA}}
)
.

Let
\tilde{\alpha}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{\mathrm{QCA}}
, we define the GNVW index to be

\displaystyle\mathrm{ind}(\tilde{\alpha}):=\begin{cases}\mathrm{ind}(\tilde{\alpha}),\quad\tilde{\alpha}\,\text{is linear}\\
\mathrm{ind}(\alpha),\quad\tilde{\alpha}\,\text{is anti-linear}\\
\end{cases}

(B.11)

In the second case, we have used
\tilde{\alpha}=\alpha K
for some
\alpha\in\mathcal{G}^{\mathrm{QCA}}
.

We have to show this definition is well-defined (i.e., it does not depend on how we choose
\alpha
and
K
for a given
\tilde{\alpha}
) and it gives a group homomorphism from
\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{\mathrm{QCA}}\to\mathbb{Z}[\{\log(p_{j})\}_{j\in J}]
(we only know that it is homomorphism from
\mathcal{G}^{\mathrm{QCA}}\to\mathbb{Z}[\{\log(p_{j})\}_{j\in J}]
so far).

To proceed, we need the following lemma.

Lemma B.2
.

For
K
defined in Eq. (
III.3
), we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{ind}(K\alpha K^{-1})=\mathrm{ind}(\alpha)

(B.12)

for any
\alpha\in\mathcal{G}^{\mathrm{QCA}}
.

Proof.

Without loss of generality, we assume that
\alpha
is a nearest-neighbor QCA by re-grouping the lattice. We also adapt the notations introduced in Eq. (
B.2
) and (
B.3
).

Let us recall that
K
is actually a linear map
K:\bar{\mathscr{A}}^{ql}\to\mathscr{A}^{ql}
, so by
K\alpha K^{-1}
we really mean
K\bar{\alpha}K^{-1}
, where
\bar{\alpha}:\bar{\mathscr{A}}^{ql}\to\bar{\mathscr{A}}^{ql}
is induced by
\alpha
. Since
K
is on-site, we have
K^{-1}(\mathscr{A}_{n})=\bar{\mathscr{A}}_{n}
for each
n\in\mathbb{Z}
. Moreover,
K\bar{\alpha}K^{-1}
is also nearest-neighbor QCA. By the definition of GNVW index, we consider

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathcal{L}_{n}^{\prime}&:=K\bar{\alpha}K^{-1}(\mathcal{B}_{n})\cap\mathcal{C}_{n}=K\bar{\alpha}(\bar{\mathcal{B}}_{n})\cap\mathcal{C}_{n}\\
\mathcal{R}_{n}^{\prime}&:=K\bar{\alpha}K^{-1}(\mathcal{B}_{n})\cap\mathcal{C}_{n+1}=K\bar{\alpha}(\bar{\mathcal{B}}_{n})\cap\mathcal{C}_{n+1}\end{split}

(B.13)

We only have to show
\dim(\mathcal{L}_{n}^{\prime})=\dim(\mathcal{L}_{n}),\,\forall\,n\in\mathbb{Z}
. To see it, note that

\displaystyle K(\mathcal{L}_{n}^{\prime})=\bar{\alpha}(\bar{B}_{n})\cap\bar{\mathcal{C}}_{n}=\bar{\mathcal{L}}_{n}

(B.14)

So we have
\dim(\mathcal{L}_{n}^{\prime})=\dim(\bar{\mathcal{L}}_{n})=\dim(\mathcal{L}_{n})
. As a consequence, we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{ind}(K\alpha K^{-1})=\frac{1}{2}(\log(\dim(\mathscr{A}_{2n})-\log(\dim(\mathcal{L}^{\prime}_{n}))=\mathrm{ind}(\alpha)

(B.15)

∎

Now we are ready to show that
\mathrm{ind}
is well-defined.

Theorem B.1
.

The GNVW index map defined in Definition
B.2
is well-defined, i.e., it does not depend on the choice of
\alpha
and
K
for a given
\tilde{\alpha}
.

Proof.

Suppose we choose a different complex conjugation denoted by
K^{\prime}
. Since
K^{\prime}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{\mathrm{QCA}}
is anti-linear, there exists
\eta\in\mathcal{G}^{\mathrm{QCA}}
such that

\displaystyle K^{\prime}=\eta K

(B.16)

On the other hand,
(K^{\prime})^{2}=1
by assumption. So we have

\displaystyle 0=\mathrm{ind}((K^{\prime})^{2})=\mathrm{ind}(\eta K\eta K)=\mathrm{ind}(\eta)+\mathrm{ind}(K\eta K^{-1})

(B.17)

where we have used that
\mathrm{ind}
is a homomorphism on
\mathcal{G}^{\mathrm{QCA}}
and
K=K^{-1}
.
By Lemma
B.2
, we conclude that

\displaystyle\mathrm{ind}(\eta)=0

(B.18)

By the structure theorem of QCA, this means
\eta
is a circuit.
So the choice of complex conjugation is unique up to a circuit.

Now let
\tilde{\alpha}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{\mathrm{QCA}}
be anti-linear. Suppose
\alpha^{\prime}K^{\prime}
is its another decomposition. That is,

\displaystyle\tilde{\alpha}=\alpha K=\alpha^{\prime}K^{\prime}=\alpha\eta K

(B.19)

To show that the Definition
B.2
is well-defined, we have to show
\mathrm{ind}(\alpha^{\prime})=\mathrm{ind}(\alpha\eta)=\mathrm{ind}(\alpha)
. This is indeed true because
\eta\in\mathcal{G}^{\mathrm{QCA}}
and
\mathrm{ind}(\eta)=0
.

Therefore, Definition
B.2
is well-defined.
∎

Now we are ready to show that
\mathrm{ind}
is a homomorphism from
\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{\mathrm{QCA}}
to
\mathbb{Z}[\{\log(p_{j})\}_{j\in J}]
.

Theorem B.2
.

The GNVW index map in Definition
B.2
is a group homomorphism from
\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{\mathrm{QCA}}
to
\mathbb{Z}[\{\log(p_{j})\}_{j\in J}]
.

Proof.

There are three nontrivial cases. All automorphisms with
\tilde{\cdot}
will be anti-linear below.

First, let us consider
\alpha\tilde{\beta}
, where
\alpha\in\mathcal{G}^{\mathrm{QCA}}
and
\beta\in\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{\mathrm{QCA}}
. We need to show

\displaystyle\mathrm{ind}(\alpha\tilde{\beta})=\mathrm{ind}(\alpha)+\mathrm{ind}(\tilde{\beta})

(B.20)

By writing
\tilde{\beta}=\beta K
for some
\beta\in\mathcal{G}^{\mathrm{QCA}}
, one gets

\displaystyle\mathrm{ind}(\alpha\tilde{\beta})=\mathrm{ind}(\alpha\beta K)=\mathrm{ind}(\alpha\beta)

(B.21)

and the desired result follows since
\mathrm{ind}(\alpha\beta)=\mathrm{ind}(\alpha)+\mathrm{ind}(\beta)
.

Secondly, let us consider
\tilde{\alpha}\beta
. Writing
\tilde{\alpha}=\alpha K
, we have

\displaystyle\tilde{\alpha}\beta=\alpha K\beta=\alpha(K\beta K^{-1})K.

(B.22)

So, by definition

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathrm{ind}(\tilde{\alpha}\beta)&=\mathrm{ind}(\alpha(K\beta K^{-1}))\\
&=\mathrm{ind}(\alpha)+\mathrm{ind}(K\beta K^{-1})\\
&=\mathrm{ind}(\alpha)+\mathrm{ind}(\beta)\\
&=\mathrm{ind}(\tilde{\alpha})+\mathrm{ind}(\beta).\end{split}

(B.23)

In the third equality we have used Lemma
B.2
.

The last case
\tilde{\alpha}\tilde{\beta}
can be treated in the same way as the second case.
∎

The above discussion gives a definition of the GNVW index of a twisted QCA, and shows that this definition is well-defined and it is a homomorphism from
\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{\mathrm{QCA}}
to
\mathbb{Z}[\{\log(p_{j})\}_{j\in J}]
.

The index theory can be constructed similarly for
\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{lp}
. The only difference is that in Theorem
B.1
, the statement that “the choice of complex conjugation is unique up to a circuit” should be replaced by “the choice of complex conjugation is unique to a time evolution generated by some local Hamiltonian”.

Appendix C
Anomaly index

In this appendix, we present the proofs of various aspects of the anomaly index, which ensures that the anomaly index defined in the main text is a valid concept.

C.1
Decomposition of
\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{lp}

In this subsection, we establish Proposition
IV.1
. To this end, we first review a few lemmas about decomposition in
\mathcal{G}^{lp}
from Ref.
Kapustin and Sopenko 2024
. Proposition
IV.1
is the generalization of these lemmas to
\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{lp}
.

Lemma C.1

(Lemma 2.1 of Ref.
Kapustin and Sopenko 2024
)
.

Let
\alpha\in\mathcal{G}^{lp}
with vanishing GNVW index, then

\displaystyle\alpha=\alpha_{<0}\alpha_{0}\alpha_{\geqslant 0}

(C.1)

where
\alpha_{<0}\in\mathcal{G}^{lp}_{<0}
,
\alpha_{0}\in\mathcal{G}^{lp}_{0}
and
\alpha_{\geqslant 0}\in\mathcal{G}^{lp}_{\geqslant 0}
. The converse is also true.

Let us explain the intuition behind the above result. Given the decomposition Eq. (
IV.1
), one may want to restrict the symmetry action
\alpha
to each half chain, which gives the
\alpha_{\geqslant 0}
and
\alpha_{<0}
parts. However, generically
\alpha
can expand the support of an operator. If an operator is supported on one of the two half chains, under
\alpha
it will generically acquire some support on the other half chain. But
\alpha_{\geqslant 0}
and
\alpha_{<0}
cannot achieve this, so the
\alpha_{0}
part is also expected.

Lemma C.2

(Lemma 2.2 of Ref.
Kapustin and Sopenko 2024
)
.

Suppose
\alpha\in\mathcal{G}^{lp}
has a vanishing GNVW index and it admits two different decomposition

\displaystyle\alpha=\alpha_{<0}\alpha_{0}\alpha_{\geqslant 0}=\alpha^{\prime}_{<0}\alpha^{\prime}_{0}\alpha^{\prime}_{\geqslant 0}

(C.2)

then
\alpha_{<0}(\alpha^{\prime}_{<0})^{-1}\in\mathcal{G}^{lp}_{0}
and
\alpha_{\geqslant 0}(\alpha^{\prime}_{\geqslant 0})^{-1}\in\mathcal{G}^{lp}_{0}
.

Now we turn to
\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{lp}
. Note that one can always fix
K
(which is chosen to be on-site, as in Eq. (
III.3
)), so that for any anti-linear
\tilde{\alpha}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{lp}
, we have

\displaystyle\tilde{\alpha}=\alpha K,\quad\alpha\in\mathcal{G}^{lp}

(C.3)

Assuming
\mathrm{ind}(\tilde{\alpha})=\mathrm{ind}(\alpha)=0
, the following propositions are true.

Proposition C.1
.

Let
\tilde{\alpha}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{lp}
with vanishing GNVW index. Then it admits a decomposition

\displaystyle\tilde{\alpha}=\tilde{\alpha}_{<0}\alpha_{0}\tilde{\alpha}_{\geqslant 0}

(C.4)

where
\tilde{\alpha}_{<0}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{lp}_{<0}
,
\alpha_{0}\in\mathcal{G}_{0}^{lp}
and
\tilde{\alpha}_{\geqslant 0}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{lp}_{\geqslant 0}
.
Moreover,
\tilde{\alpha}_{\geqslant 0}
(resp.
\tilde{\alpha}_{<0}
) is anti-linear on
\mathscr{A}^{ql}_{\geqslant 0}
(resp.
\mathscr{A}^{ql}_{<0}
) if and only if
\tilde{\alpha}
is anti-linear. The converse is also true.

Proof.

The case where
\tilde{\alpha}
is linear is addressed by Lemma
C.1
. In the following, we assume
\tilde{\alpha}
is anti-linear.
By Lemma
C.1
, we have

\displaystyle\alpha=\alpha_{<0}\alpha^{\prime}_{0}\alpha_{\geqslant 0}

(C.5)

Since
K
is on-site, one can define

\displaystyle\begin{split}K_{\geqslant 0}&:=\prod_{n\geqslant 0}K_{n}\\
K_{<0}&:=\prod_{n<0}K_{n}\end{split}

(C.6)

Then one defines

\displaystyle\begin{split}\tilde{\alpha}_{\geqslant 0}&:=\alpha_{\geqslant 0}K_{\geqslant 0}\\
\alpha_{0}&:=K_{<0}\triangleright\alpha_{0}^{\prime}=K_{<0}^{-1}\alpha_{0}^{\prime}K_{<0}\\
\tilde{\alpha}_{<0}&:=\alpha_{<0}K_{<0}\end{split}

(C.7)

Using that
K_{<0}\alpha_{\geqslant 0}=K_{<0}\alpha_{\geqslant 0}K_{<0}^{-1}K_{<0}=\alpha_{\geqslant 0}K_{<0}
, one can see that
\tilde{\alpha}=\tilde{\alpha}_{<0}\alpha_{0}\tilde{\alpha}_{\geqslant 0}
. This establishes the decomposition and the (anti-)linearity of
\tilde{\alpha}_{\geqslant 0}
and
\tilde{\alpha}_{<0}
.

Conversely, if
\tilde{\alpha}
is anti-linear of the form of Eq. (
C.4
), then we have

\displaystyle\tilde{\alpha}\circ K=(\tilde{\alpha}_{<0}\circ K_{<0})\circ(K_{<0}^{-1}\alpha_{0}K_{<0})\circ(\tilde{\alpha}_{\geqslant 0}\circ K_{\geqslant 0})

(C.8)

Note that
\tilde{\alpha}\circ K
,
K_{<0}^{-1}\alpha_{0}K_{<0}
,
\tilde{\alpha}_{<0}\circ K_{<0}
and
\tilde{\alpha}_{\geqslant 0}\circ K_{\geqslant 0}
are all linear LPA’s. Thus
\mathrm{ind}(\tilde{\alpha})=\mathrm{ind}(\tilde{\alpha}\circ K)=0
is ensured by Lemma
C.1
.

∎

The second proposition characterizes the relation between different decompositions of
\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{lp}
. We state a stronger version and prove it here, which will be used later.

Proposition C.2
.

Suppose
\tilde{\alpha}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{lp}
has a vanishing GNVW index and it can be decomposed as

\displaystyle\tilde{\alpha}_{<0}\alpha_{0}\tilde{\alpha}_{\geqslant 0}=\tilde{\alpha}=\tilde{\beta}_{<n}\beta_{n}\tilde{\beta}_{\geqslant n}

(C.9)

where
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geqslant 0}
is some lattice site and
\beta_{n}\in\mathcal{G}_{0}^{lp}
.
Then there exists a quasi-local unitary
V
supported on the non-negative half chain, such that

\displaystyle\tilde{\beta}_{\geqslant n}=\mathrm{Ad}_{V}\circ\tilde{\alpha}_{\geqslant 0}

(C.10)

if
\tilde{\alpha}
is linear. If
\tilde{\alpha}
is anti-linear, then there exists a quasi-local unitary
V
supported on the non-negative half chain, such that

\displaystyle\tilde{\beta}_{\geqslant n}=\mathrm{Ad}_{V}\circ K_{[0,n)}\tilde{\alpha}_{\geqslant 0},

(C.11)

where
K_{[0,n)}=\prod_{j=0}^{n-1}K_{j}
.

A similar statement holds if
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{<0}
, with
K_{[0,n)}
above replaced by
K_{[n,0)}=\prod_{j=n}^{-1}K_{j}
.

Proof.

Without loss of generality, we assume
n\geqslant 0
. Then

\beta_{n}=\tilde{\beta}_{<n}^{-1}\tilde{\alpha}_{<0}\alpha_{0}\tilde{\alpha}_{\geqslant 0}\tilde{\beta}_{\geqslant n}^{-1}=\left(\tilde{\beta}_{<n}^{-1}\tilde{\alpha}_{<0}\tilde{\alpha}_{\geqslant 0}\tilde{\beta}_{\geqslant n}^{-1}\right)\left((\tilde{\alpha}_{\geqslant 0}\tilde{\beta}_{\geqslant n}^{-1})^{-1}\alpha_{0}\tilde{\alpha}_{\geqslant 0}\tilde{\beta}_{\geqslant n}^{-1}\right)\in\mathcal{G}_{0}^{lp}.

Since
(\tilde{\alpha}_{\geqslant 0}\tilde{\beta}_{\geqslant n}^{-1})^{-1}\alpha_{0}\tilde{\alpha}_{\geqslant 0}\tilde{\beta}_{\geqslant n}^{-1}\in\mathcal{G}_{0}^{lp}
, we get that
\tilde{\beta}^{-1}_{<n}\tilde{\alpha}_{<0}\tilde{\alpha}_{\geqslant 0}\tilde{\beta}_{\geqslant n}^{-1}\in\mathcal{G}^{lp}_{0}
. Thus, according to Lemma 3.1 of Ref.
Lance 1968
, for any
\epsilon>0
, there exists
R_{\epsilon}>0
such that for any
A\in\mathscr{A}^{ql}_{[R_{\epsilon},\infty)}
, we have

\displaystyle||\tilde{\beta}^{-1}_{<n}\tilde{\alpha}_{<0}\tilde{\alpha}_{\geqslant 0}\tilde{\beta}_{\geqslant n}^{-1}(A)-A||=||\tilde{\alpha}_{\geqslant 0}\tilde{\beta}_{\geqslant n}^{-1}(A)-A||<\epsilon||A||

(C.12)

where we have assumed
R_{\epsilon}>n
, which is always possible.

If
\tilde{\alpha}
is linear on
\mathscr{A}^{ql}
, then
\tilde{\beta}^{-1}_{<n}
,
\tilde{\alpha}_{<0}
,
\tilde{\alpha}_{\geqslant 0}
and
\tilde{\beta}^{-1}_{\geqslant n}
are all linear on the algrebras of their respective supports. Using Lemma 3.1 of Ref.
Lance 1968
again, we conclude that there is a quasi-local unitary operator
V
(supported on non-negative half chain) such that

\displaystyle\tilde{\beta}_{\geqslant n}=\mathrm{Ad}_{V}\circ\tilde{\alpha}_{\geqslant 0}.

(C.13)

On the other hand, if
\tilde{\alpha}
is anti-linear on
\mathscr{A}^{ql}
, then
\tilde{\alpha}_{\geqslant 0}
is anti-linear on
\mathscr{A}^{ql}_{[0,\infty)}
and
\tilde{\beta}^{-1}_{\geqslant n}
is anti-linear on
\mathscr{A}^{ql}_{[n,\infty)}
. So
K_{[0,n)}\tilde{\alpha}_{\geqslant 0}\tilde{\beta}^{-1}_{\geqslant n}
is linear on
\mathscr{A}^{ql}_{\geqslant 0}
. Moreover, because
R_{\epsilon}>n
by assumption,

\displaystyle||K_{[0,n)}\tilde{\alpha}_{\geqslant 0}\tilde{\beta}_{\geqslant n}^{-1}(A)-A||=||\tilde{\alpha}_{\geqslant 0}\tilde{\beta}_{\geqslant n}^{-1}(A)-A||<\epsilon||A||

(C.14)

Now we can use Lemma 3.1 of Ref.
Lance 1968
again, and conclude that there is a quasi-local unitary operator
V
(supported on the non-negative half chain) such that

\displaystyle\tilde{\beta}_{\geqslant n}=\mathrm{Ad}_{V}\circ K_{[0,n)}\tilde{\alpha}_{\geqslant 0}.

(C.15)

∎

A special case of the above proposition is when
n=0
. In this case,
K_{[0,0)}
should be viewed as identity, and this proposition reduces to Proposition
IV.2
in the main text.

C.2
Twisted 3-cocycle condition

We now check that
\omega
defined in Eq. (
IV.10
) is indeed a twisted 3-cocycle, and thus prove the first part of Theorem
IV.1
.

To simplify the notations, we will denote
\tilde{\beta}_{g}:=\tilde{\alpha}_{\geqslant 0}(g)
in this section. Then, by using the definition Eq. (
IV.10
) repeatedly, we find that the cocycle condition follows from

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\omega(gh,k,l)\omega(g,h,kl)(\mathrm{Ad}_{V(g,h)}\circ\tilde{\beta}_{gh})(V(k,l))\tilde{\beta}_{g}(V(h,kl))V(g,hkl)\\
=&V(g,h)\omega(gh,k,l)\tilde{\beta}_{gh}(V(k,l))V(g,h)^{-1}\omega(g,h,kl)\tilde{\beta}_{g}(V(h,kl))V(g,hkl)\\
=&V(g,h)\omega(gh,k,l)\tilde{\beta}_{gh}(V(k,l))V(gh,kl)\\
=&V(g,h)V(gh,k)V(ghk,l)\\
=&\omega(g,h,k)\tilde{\beta}_{g}(V(h,k))V(g,hk)V(ghk,l)\\
=&\omega(g,h,k)\tilde{\beta}_{g}(V(h,k))\omega(g,hk,l)\tilde{\beta}_{g}(V(hk,l))V(g,hkl)\\
=&\omega(g,h,k)\omega(g,hk,l)\tilde{\beta}_{g}(\omega(h,k,l)\tilde{\beta}_{h}(V(k,l))V(h,kl))V(g,hkl)\\
=&\omega(g,h,k)\omega(g,hk,l)(g\triangleright\omega(h,k,l))(\mathrm{Ad}_{V(g,h)}\circ\tilde{\beta}_{gh})(V(k,l))\tilde{\beta}_{g}(V(h,kl))V(g,hkl)\end{split}

(C.16)

where
g\triangleright\omega(h,k,l)=\omega(h,k,l)
if
g
is linear, and
g\triangleright\omega(h,k,l)=\overline{\omega(h,k,l)}
if
\tilde{\alpha}(g)
is anti-linear.

C.3
Independence of all artifical choices

We first show that anomaly index is independent of decomposition we use in Eq. (
III.8
).

Proof of the independence of decomposition.

To simplify the notations, we denote
\tilde{\alpha}_{\geqslant 0}
by
\tilde{\beta}_{g}
.

By Proposition
IV.2
, different choices of
\beta_{g}
are related by adjoint action of
U_{g}\in\mathcal{U}^{ql}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\tilde{\beta}_{g}^{\prime}=\mathrm{Ad}_{U_{g}}\tilde{\beta}_{g}

(C.17)

From

\displaystyle\begin{split}\tilde{\beta}_{g}\tilde{\beta}_{h}&=\mathrm{Ad}_{V(g,h)}\tilde{\beta}_{gh}\\
\tilde{\beta}^{\prime}_{g}\tilde{\beta}^{\prime}_{h}&=\mathrm{Ad}_{V^{\prime}(g,h)}\tilde{\beta}^{\prime}_{gh}\end{split}

(C.18)

One can solve

\displaystyle V^{\prime}(g,h)=U_{g}\tilde{\beta}_{g}(U_{h})V(g,h)U_{gh}^{-1}

(C.19)

up to a phase factor
\eta(g,h)\in\mathrm{U}(1)
. The effect of this phase factor is to shift
\omega
by a 3-coboundary
\tilde{\delta}\eta
, so we will not be concerned about this ambiguity.

We have

\displaystyle\begin{split}V^{\prime}(gh,k)&=U_{gh}\tilde{\beta}_{gh}(U_{k})V(gh,k)U_{ghk}^{-1}\\
V^{\prime}(g,hk)^{-1}&=U_{ghk}V(g,hk)^{-1}\tilde{\beta}_{g}(U_{hk})^{-1}U_{g}^{-1}\\
\tilde{\beta}^{\prime}_{g}(V^{\prime}(h,k))^{-1}&=U_{g}\tilde{\beta}_{g}(U_{hk})\tilde{\beta}_{g}(V(h,k))^{-1}\tilde{\beta}_{g}\tilde{\beta}_{h}(U_{k})^{-1}\tilde{\beta}_{g}(U_{h})^{-1}U_{g}^{-1}\end{split}

(C.20)

Now using
\tilde{\beta}_{gh}=\mathrm{Ad}_{V(g,h)}\tilde{\beta}_{g}\tilde{\beta}_{h}
in
V^{\prime}(gh,k)
, we obtain

Appendix D
Proof of Lemma
V.1

In this section, we utilize some powerful tools to show Lemma
V.1
, which is a generalization of Lemma E.5 of Ref.
Liu et al. 2024
. Lemma
V.1
is restated below.

Lemma D.1
.

Let
\tilde{\alpha}:G\to\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{lp}
be a symmetry action and
\psi
be a type-I factor state that satisfies the split property. If
\psi=\psi_{\tilde{\alpha}(g)},\forall g\in G
(i.e.,
\psi
is invariant under
G
), then the anomaly index
\tilde{\omega}\in\mathrm{H}^{3}_{\varphi}(G,\mathrm{U}(1))
vanishes.

Proof.

Recall that for a general factor state, we say that
\psi
splits if
\psi\sim\psi_{<0}\otimes\psi_{\geqslant 0}
, where
\sim
means quasi-equivalence. We then have

\displaystyle\psi_{<0}\otimes\psi_{\geqslant 0}\sim\psi=\psi_{\tilde{\alpha}(g)}\sim(\psi_{<0\tilde{\alpha}_{<0}(g)})\otimes(\psi_{\geqslant 0\tilde{\alpha}_{\geqslant 0}(g)})

(D.1)

One then deduces that
\psi_{\geqslant 0}\sim\psi_{\geqslant 0\tilde{\alpha}_{\geqslant 0}(g)}
. So
\psi\sim\psi_{<0}\otimes\psi_{\geqslant 0}\sim\psi_{<0}\otimes\psi_{\geqslant 0\tilde{\alpha}_{\geqslant 0}(g)}\sim\psi_{\tilde{\alpha}_{\geqslant 0}(g)}
.

For now, we assume
\tilde{\alpha}(g)
is linear, and the anti-linear case will be treated shortly. In this case,
\psi_{\tilde{\alpha}_{\geqslant 0}(g)}=\psi\circ\tilde{\alpha}_{\geqslant 0}(g)
.
Let
(\mathcal{H}_{\psi},\pi_{\psi},|\psi\rangle)
be the GNS triple of
\psi
. Then the GNS representation of
\psi\circ\tilde{\alpha}_{\geqslant 0}(g)
is given by
\pi_{\psi}\circ\tilde{\alpha}_{\geqslant 0}(g)
acting on
\mathcal{H}_{\psi}
.
Because
\psi\sim\psi\circ\tilde{\alpha}_{\geqslant 0}(g)
, by Definition
II.9
, there exists a
*
-isomorphism
F_{g}:\mathscr{M}_{\psi}\to\mathscr{M}_{\psi\circ\tilde{\alpha}_{\geqslant 0}(g)}
such that

\displaystyle F_{g}(\pi_{\psi}(a))=\pi_{\psi}([\tilde{\alpha}_{\geqslant 0}(g)](a)),\forall a\in\mathscr{A}^{ql}.

(D.2)

Moreover,
\pi_{\psi}(\mathscr{A}^{ql})^{\prime}=\pi_{\psi}(\tilde{\alpha}(g)_{\geqslant 0}\mathscr{A}^{ql})^{\prime}
since
\tilde{\alpha}(g)_{\geqslant 0}
is a
*
-automorphism. This in turn means
\mathscr{M}_{\psi}=\mathscr{M}_{\psi\circ\tilde{\alpha}_{\geqslant 0}(g)}
as an abstract von Neumann algebra. Thus,
F_{g}
can be thought of as a
*
-automorphism on
\mathscr{M}_{\psi}
. By assumption,
\mathscr{M}_{\psi}
is a type-I factor acting on
\mathcal{H}_{\psi}
, which means
\mathscr{M}_{\psi}=\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})\otimes 1_{\mathcal{K}}
with
\mathcal{H}
and
\mathcal{K}
two other Hilbert spaces such that
\mathcal{H}_{\psi}=\mathcal{H}\otimes\mathcal{K}
. Now using Lemma
II.1
, which means there exists a map
\tilde{U}_{g}\in\mathscr{M}_{\psi}
such that

\displaystyle\pi_{\psi}(\tilde{\alpha}_{\geqslant 0}(a))=\tilde{U}(g)\pi_{\psi}(a)\tilde{U}(g)^{-1}

(D.3)

If
\tilde{\alpha}(g)
is anti-linear instead,
\psi\sim\psi_{\tilde{\alpha}_{\geqslant 0}(g)}
in fact means
\bar{\psi}\simeq\psi\circ\tilde{\alpha}_{\geqslant 0}(g)
as states of
\bar{\mathscr{A}}^{ql}
. The map
F_{g}
still can be constructed as
*
-automorphism of
\bar{\mathscr{M}}_{\psi}
, which is again given by a unitary operator
\tilde{U}(g)\in\bar{\mathscr{M}}_{\psi}
. However, below we regard
\tilde{U}(g)
as an anti-linear map
\tilde{U}(g):\mathcal{H}_{\psi}\to\bar{\mathcal{H}}_{\psi}
.

In summary, we have shown that
there exists an operator
\tilde{U}(g)
, such that

\displaystyle\pi_{\psi_{\tilde{\alpha}_{\geqslant 0}(g)}}(A)=\tilde{U}(g)\pi_{\psi}(A)\tilde{U}(g)^{-1},\forall A\in\mathscr{A}^{ql}.

(D.4)

where
\tilde{U}
is unitary (resp. anti-unitary) if
\tilde{\alpha}
is linear (resp. anti-linear).

According to Eq. (
IV.8
), we have

\displaystyle\pi_{\psi}(\mathrm{Ad}_{V(g,h)}A)=\pi_{\psi}(\tilde{\alpha}_{\geqslant 0}(g)\tilde{\alpha}_{\geqslant 0}(h)\tilde{\alpha}_{\geqslant 0}(gh)^{-1}A)=\pi_{\psi_{\tilde{\alpha}_{\geqslant 0}(g)\tilde{\alpha}_{\geqslant 0}(h)}\tilde{\alpha}_{\geqslant 0}(gh)^{-1}}(A),\forall A\in\mathscr{A}^{ql}.

(D.5)

By Eq. (
D.4
),

\displaystyle\pi_{\psi}(V(g,h))\pi_{\psi}(A)\pi_{\psi}(V(g,h))^{-1}=\tilde{U}(g)\tilde{U}(h)\tilde{U}(gh)^{-1}\pi_{\psi}(A)\tilde{U}(gh)\tilde{U}(h)^{-1}\tilde{U}(g)^{-1},\forall A\in\mathscr{A}^{ql}.

(D.6)

We emphasize that
\tilde{U}(g)
does not need to be a homomorphism.

The above Eq. (

\displaystyle\eta(g,h):=\pi_{\psi}(V(g,h))^{-1}\tilde{U}(g)\tilde{U}(h)\tilde{U}(gh)^{-1}

II.7

\displaystyle\begin{split}\pi(V(g,h))&=\eta(g,h)^{-1}\tilde{U}(g)\tilde{U}(h)\tilde{U}(gh)^{-1}\\

where all

Combining the above equation with Eq. (

\displaystyle\omega(g,h,k)=(\tilde{\delta}^{(3)}\eta)(g,h,k)
