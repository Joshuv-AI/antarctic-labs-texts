MentorCollab : Selective Large-to-Small Inference-Time Guidance for Efficient Reasoning
Haojin Wang, Yike Wang, Shangbin Feng, Hannaneh Hajishirzi Yulia Tsvetkov

Abstract
Large reasoning models (LRMs) achieve strong performance by producing long chains of thought, but their inference costs are high and often generate redundant reasoning. Small language models (SLMs) are far more efficient, yet struggle on multi-step reasoning tasks. A natural idea is to let a large model guide a small one at inference time as a mentor, yet existing collaboration methods often promote imitation, resulting in verbose reasoning without consistent error correction. We propose MentorCollab , an inference-time collaboration method in which an LRM selectively and sparsely guides an SLM, rather than taking over generation. At randomly sampled token positions, we probe for divergences between the two models and use a lightweight verifier to decide whether the SLM should follow a short lookahead segment from its mentor or continue on its own. Across 15 SLM--LRM pairs and 3 domains (math reasoning, general knowledge, and commonsense reasoning), our method improves performance in 12 settings, with average gains of 3.0% and up to 8.0%, while adopting only having 18.4% tokens generated by the expensive mentor model on average. We find that short segments and selective probing are sufficient for effective collaboration. Our results show that selective inference-time guidance restores large-model reasoning ability without substantial inference overhead. 1 1 1 Code available at github.com/haojinw0027/Mentorcollab .



1
Introduction

Figure 1:

Example of
MentorCollab
on a query from the MATH
(
13
)
dataset.
MentorCollab
proposes mentoring LRM reasoning segments and verifies them before adoption, enabling the small model to correct its initial mistake while avoiding the redundant reasoning seen in prior collaboration methods. Tokens generated from the mentoring LRM is highlighted in red in the figure.

Large language models trained with long chains of thought now achieve strong performance on a wide range of reasoning tasks, from mathematics to commonsense question answering
(
40
;
11
;
26
;
24
)
.
These reasoning-specialized models are effective, but they are also expensive to run and prone to generating redundant or reflective reasoning, leading to high inference cost and overthinking
(
32
;
21
,
inter alia
)
.
In contrast, small language models (SLMs;
\leq
8B parameters) are far cheaper and faster to deploy, but they typically struggle on multi-step reasoning problems.
In this paper, we propose a method to recover much of the reasoning ability of large models while retaining the efficiency of small ones.

A natural idea is to let a large model help a small one at inference time, using the large model as a teacher that provides guidance when needed.
Recent work has explored such
model collaboration
under different levels of access and interaction, including text-level, logits-level, weights-level, and API-level approaches
(
7
)
.
Logits- or weights-level methods require joint training or low-level access, making them costly and hard to deploy, whereas API-level collaboration is lightweight, practical, and largely training-free.
Most existing API-level approaches either route each query to a single model
(
14
;
2
)
or adopt a generator–mentor setup in which a stronger model intermittently takes over generation or injects tokens into the output of a weaker one.
For example, Nudging
(
6
)
injects mentor tokens when the generator is uncertain.
While effective in some settings, when the teacher is a reasoning-specialized large model (LRM), these strategies tend to push the small model to imitate verbose chain-of-thought patterns—often introducing reflective cues such as
Wait.
—resulting in longer reasoning traces and higher cost without reliably correcting underlying errors; other switching methods
(
38
;
3
)
exhibit similar behavior by promoting large-model mimicry rather than targeted error correction.

These observations suggest that effective collaboration should not aim for imitation.
Instead, the large model should act as a
mentor
: it should provide brief, targeted cues that steer the small model only when such guidance is likely to help, while leaving the small model in control of generation.
In other words, we seek
selective inference-time guidance
rather than full takeover.

In this work, we introduce
MentorCollab
(Figure
1
), an inference-time collaboration that instantiates this idea. We refer to the SLM as the
generator
and the LRM as the
mentor
.
Rather than injecting mentor tokens directly or switching decoding to the mentor,
MentorCollab
randomly samples token positions during generation to probe for divergences between the two models.
When a divergence is detected, a lightweight verifier decides whether the generator should follow a short lookahead segment from the mentor or continue along its own trajectory.
This design keeps the generator in charge, while allowing the mentor to provide concise guidance at critical points.

We consider two variants of this approach.

MentorCollab-Free
uses the generator itself, via prompting, as the verifier, while
MentorCollab-MLP
employs a small MLP-based verifier trained once per generator and reused across different mentors.
Unlike training-based collaboration methods that require extensive joint optimization for each model pair and shared tokenizers
(
28
)
,
MentorCollab-MLP
introduces minimal overhead and remains flexible across heterogeneous models.

We evaluate
MentorCollab
across 15 generator–mentor pairs and three domains: math reasoning, general knowledge, and commonsense reasoning.

MentorCollab
improves accuracy in 12 out of 15 settings, achieving an average gain of 3.0% and peak improvements of up to 8.0%, while having only 18.4% of mentor tokens in the final generation on average.
Further analysis shows that effective collaboration does not require long reasoning traces or frequent mentor intervention: short lookahead segments (4-8 tokens) and moderate probing frequency are sufficient.

Overall, our results show that selective inference-time guidance can recover much of the reasoning ability of large models at a fraction of the cost.
This suggests a practical path toward efficient reasoning systems that combine the strengths of large and small language models without requiring additional training or low-level access.

2
Methodology

2.1
Problem Setting

Consider a SLM
\mathcal{G}
and a LRM
\mathcal{M}
.
Let
\mathbf{x}=[x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}]\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{n}
denote the input token sequence.
Given
\mathbf{x}
,
MentorCollab
aims to generate a continuation
[x_{n+1},\ldots,x_{n+K}]
through collaborative inference between
\mathcal{G}
and
\mathcal{M}
.
At each generation step
t

(n<t\leq n+K)
, we define:

•

x_{t}^{\mathcal{G}}
be the token generated by
\mathcal{G}
,

•

x_{t}^{\mathcal{M}}
be the token generated by
\mathcal{M}
if consulted,

•

x_{t}
be the final token selected by
MentorCollab
.

Given this setup, we next describe how
MentorCollab
dynamically coordinates the generator and mentor during inference time.

Figure 2:

Overview of
MentorCollab
. At randomly sampled token positions, if the generator token and the mentor token disagree, we prompt two models to produce a short future segment respectively. A verifier then selects which segment to follow, and generation proceeds from the selected continuation.

2.2
MentorCollab

MentorCollab
(
fig.

2
) is a lightweight test-time collaboration framework that allows a generator
\mathcal{G}
to selectively incorporate guidance from a mentor
\mathcal{M}
during decoding.
The framework operates in three stages—
decision
,
consultation
, and
verification
—which we describe in detail below.

Decision

To decide whether to consult the mentor,
MentorCollab
independently samples

\displaystyle B_{t}\sim\mathrm{Bernoulli}(\rho),\rho\in(0,1),

where
\rho
is the decision proportion hyperparameter, and queries mentor model
\mathcal{M}
iff
B_{t}=1
.
When a query occurs (
B_{t}=1
), both
\mathcal{G}
and
\mathcal{M}
propose their next tokens
x_{t}^{\mathcal{G}}
and
x_{t}^{\mathcal{M}}
.
Following implementations from
6
, such comparison is performed at whitespace boundaries, effectively comparing at the word level.
We define a disagreement indicator
D_{t}
as follows:

\displaystyle D_{t}=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }x_{t}^{\mathcal{G}}\neq x_{t}^{\mathcal{M}},\\
0&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

and
MentorCollab
proceeds to the next stage of consultation only when
D_{t}=1
.

Consultation

Once disagreement is detected (
D_{t}=1
),
MentorCollab
prompts both
\mathcal{G}
and
\mathcal{M}
to produce short future segment
\mathbf{s}
.
For each model
m\in\{\mathcal{G},\mathcal{M}\}
, we define its proposed segment as:

\displaystyle\mathbf{s}^{m}=[x_{t}^{m},x_{t+1}^{m},\ldots,x_{t+L-1}^{m}],

where
L
is the length of the segment.

Verification

Given the proposed segments
\mathbf{s}^{\mathcal{G}}
and
\mathbf{s}^{\mathcal{M}}
,
MentorCollab
employs a lightweight verifier
\mathcal{V}
to select between them.
We consider two verifier variants:
MentorCollab-Free
and
MentorCollab-MLP
.

MentorCollab-Free
We directly use the generator
\mathcal{G}
itself as the verifier
\mathcal{V}_{\text{free}}
through a verification prompt.
Given the two candidate segments, we append a short query asking which segment should be chosen (e.g.,
“Now I will choose the next sequence that could lead to the correct answer. (Option A or B?)”
), without revealing which model produced which segment.
The verifier
\mathcal{V}_{\text{free}}
outputs a choice (“A” or “B”), which we map to a decision
\hat{m}_{t}\in\{\mathcal{G},\mathcal{M}\}
indicating which segment to follow.

MentorCollab-MLP
Instead of directly treating
\mathcal{G}
as the verifier, we train a lightweight multi-layer perceptron (MLP) verifier
\mathcal{V}_{\text{train}}
.
To learn a stable verifier, we formalize the training of
\mathcal{V}_{\text{train}}
as a supervised binary classification problem.
Running
MentorCollab-Free
over a training split
\mathcal{D}_{\text{train}}
with model pair
(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{M})
, we obtain a dataset
\mathcal{T}=\{(\mathbf{h}_{i}^{\mathcal{G}},\hat{m}_{i})\}_{i=1}^{N}
, where
\mathbf{h}_{i}^{\mathcal{G}}
is the last-layer hidden state of
\mathcal{G}
at the verification index
i
and
\hat{m}_{i}\in\{\mathcal{G},\mathcal{M}\}
is the decision which leads to the correct continuation.
We curate
\mathcal{T}
for samples where the generator
\mathcal{G}
fails but
MentorCollab-Free
succeeds by choosing the correct segment.
The verifier
\mathcal{V}_{\text{train}}
is then formalized as
f_{\theta}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\{0,1\}
, where
d
is the dimension of the hidden state.
The verifier
f_{\theta}
outputs
f_{\theta}(\mathbf{h}_{i}^{\mathcal{G}})
interpreted as
\mathbb{P}
.
We train
\theta
by minimizing the empirical binary cross-entropy loss:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \mathcal{L}(\theta)&=-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\Bigl[\hat{m}_{i}\log f_{\theta}(\mathbf{h}_{i}^{\mathcal{G}})\\[2.5pt]
&\qquad+(1-\hat{m}_{i})\log\bigl(1-f_{\theta}(\mathbf{h}_{i}^{\mathcal{G}})\bigr)\Bigr].\end{aligned}

using mini-batch Adam
16
and validation-based early stopping.
At test time, the learned verifier
f_{\theta}
replaces the heuristic decision rule by selecting the mentor branch whenever
f_{\theta}(\mathbf{h}_{i}^{\mathcal{G}})>0.5
.
We give a detailed description of the verifier training in
appendix

B
.

3
Experiments

3.1
Experiment Setup

Generators and Mentors

We select three small base models (Llama3.1-8B
34
, Gemma3-4B-PT
33
, and Qwen3-8B-Base
41
) and two small instruct models (Llama3.2-3B-Instruct and Qwen3-1.7B) from diverse model families and sizes as the generators.
we select three representative LRMs: Qwen3-14B and Qwen3-32B from the Qwen3 family
41
, and DeepSeek-R1-Distilled-Llama-70B from the R1 distilled family
11
.

Hyperparameters

To balance inference cost and generation quality, we conduct our main experiments with a decision proportion of
\rho=25\%
and a segment size of
L=16
.
As SLMs typically produce concise reasoning traces, we cap the maximum generation length at 512 tokens across all experiments and adopt greedy decoding.

Domains and Datasets

We evaluate
MentorCollab
across three domains: MATH
(
13
)
for mathematical reasoning, SuperGPQA
(
35
)
for general knowledge, and Com
2
-hard-Intervention
(
39
)
for commonsense reasoning.
MATH contains competition-level mathematics problems, and we use its official test split.
SuperGPQA covers graduate-level knowledge across 285 different disciplines; we randomly sample 500 test examples.
Com
2
-hard-Intervention targets challenging commonsense reasoning via causal interventions, and we evaluate on 241 single-answer instances.
For
MentorCollab-MLP
, the verifier is trained on the MATH training split for mathematical reasoning.
For general knowledge and commonsense reasoning, it is trained on a shared set of 4,000 examples sampled from SuperGPQA.
We use 4-shot prompts for MATH, 5-shot for SuperGPQA, and zero-shot for Com
2
-hard-Intervention, with all prompt templates provided in
appendix

C
.

Baselines

We compare
MentorCollab
with five baselines, either with or without training.

•

Average Decoding
, which average the top-5 token distributions of two models at each token position and select the token with the highest probability in the averaged distribution, following
6
.

•

Nudging

(
6
)
, which hands over generation to the mentor whenever the generator’s next-token probability falls below the threshold
\gamma=0.40
.

•

CoSD

(
38
)
, which employs a rule-based verification module in which the generator hands over to the mentor when its next-token probability falls below
\alpha=0.50
and the mentor’s next-token probability exceeds
\beta=0.50
multiplied by the probability of generator token.

•

R-Stitch

(
3
)
, which uses distribution entropy as a proxy signal to decide when to switch between models. It switches to mentor when the entropy of generator exceeds the threshold
\tau
, and switches back to the generator when its entropy falls below
\tau
.
We set the hyperparameters of
\tau
to 0.03 as suggested in the paper.

•

Co-LLM

(
28
)
, which trains a single layer to classify the hidden state of the generator. We evaluate Co-LLM only on three generator–mentor pairs with compatible tokenizers and only on the MATH domain, since the method depends on tokenizer compatibility and supervised training on required ground-truth training sequences, which significantly increases evaluation cost and limits scalability across diverse model pairs.

3.2
Results

We report the results of
MentorCollab
on multiple generator–mentor model pairs across different domains in
table

1
.
Under the short token budget in our setting, mentor LRMs frequently collapse mid-reasoning, consistent with prior empirical observations
(
30
)
.
Prior collaboration methods align generators with mentors at the token level, often inheriting verbose or over-extended reasoning.
In contrast,
MentorCollab
performs segment-level selection, incorporating mentor guidance only when it corrects the generator’s initial errors.
Additional analysis over averaged results are presented in
section

A.1
.
Across three domains and mentor configurations, our selected small generators outperform their single-model baselines in 12 out of 15 cases, typically improving by 3–8 absolute points.
In contrast, prior collaboration baselines rarely yield more than 1–2 points and often underperform the generator itself.
We further compare
MentorCollab-MLP
with Co-LLM on the MATH domain (
table

2
). The results show that
MentorCollab-MLP
achieves consistent performance improvements, while avoiding the need for expensive collaboration-specific training.

Furthermore,
MentorCollab
achieves these gains with incorporated token usage comparable to other baselines, as shown in
fig.

3
.
By injecting only 10–30% of the mentor’s tokens,
MentorCollab
improves the small model’s performance by selectively injecting short mentor reasoning segments that inspire the generator’s own inference, rather than overwhelming it with full mentor trajectories.
Overall, the findings suggest that
MentorCollab
provides a stable and efficient way to collaborate small-scale models with LRMs, offering a new perspective on the complementarity of these two model types.

4
Analysis

4.1
Decision Proportion
\rho

In this section, we further examine how varying the decision proportion
\rho
affects the effectiveness of
MentorCollab
.

Figure

4
presents results on MATH and SuperGPQA under different values of
\rho
.
On MATH, the performance of
MentorCollab-MLP
consistently improves as
\rho
increases, suggesting that more frequent incorporation of the mentor’s reasoning benefits the generator model.
In contrast, on SuperGPQA, larger
\rho
does not necessarily yield higher accuracy, and performance can decline when
\rho
becomes too large.
As in
table

1
, we empirically find

In contrast,

Fusion-based methods fall into two categories: token-level distribution fusion and criterion-based model switching during generation.

6
Conclusion

We propose
MentorCollab
, an inference-time, API-level model collaboration framework that treats LRMs as mentors and SLMs as generators.
Instead of pushing generators to imitate LRMs as in prior work
(
17
;
10
)
,
MentorCollab
steers the SLM only when such guidance is likely to be beneficial and achieves cost-efficiency via lightweight, reusable verifiers that bypass computationally intensive joint optimization.
Across diverse generator–mentor pairs,
MentorCollab
yields consistent improvements of 3–8 absolute points while introducing as few as 10% of mentor tokens under a short-token budget, making it suitable for scenarios where concise reasoning is desired.
Further analyses show that
MentorCollab
is robust to segment length, decision proportion, and verifier configuration, and generalizes across domains and model families.
These findings also provide empirical support for the existence of forking paths in neural text generation
(
1
;
36
)
.
We expect future work to build upon
MentorCollab
by developing more efficient verifiers, further advancing scalable and robust model collaborations.

Limitations

Our study could be extended in the following ways.
First,
MentorCollab
largely relies on random positions to initiate mentor collaboration.
A more precise criterion for detecting intervention needs would further decrease the computational overhead associated with dual-model inference.
Second, it remains unclear why high-likelihood generator predictions are bypassed in certain contexts, warranting future analysis of internal representations to uncover the underlying causes.
Finally, our study focuses on a dual-model setting with a single generator and mentor, and extending
MentorCollab
to multi-mentor collaboration setup is left as future work.
We hope this work motivates further exploration of more fine-grained and computationally efficient paradigms for model collaborations.

Ethics Statement

We use only public benchmarks and do not collect user data; nonetheless, the approach inherits biases of underlying LMs and may amplify mentor biases through selective segment adoption, and it increases data exposure at deployment due to multi-model querying; thus any real-world use should include bias audits, content filtering, and strict data-retention controls.

B.2
Hyperparameters

The verifier is implemented as a lightweight MLP that takes the generator’s hidden state at the decision point as input and predicts which branch to follow.
Specifically, the MLP consists of three hidden layers with widths
\{2d,d,d/2\}
, where
d
denotes the hidden size of the generator model.
Each hidden layer is followed by Batch Normalization and ReLU activation, with a dropout rate of 0.1 applied throughout.
The final layer is a linear projection to a scalar, followed by a sigmoid function to produce a probability score.
During inference, scores above 0.5 indicate selecting the mentor-guided branch, while lower scores correspond to retaining the generator’s own continuation.
