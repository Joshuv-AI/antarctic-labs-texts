Home‑Based Nutritional Management After Pediatric Liver Transplantation: Dilemmas and Needs from Dual Perspectives
Authors: Jing Yang, HuaFen Wang, FangYan Lu, Li Dong, XuYe Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AimTo explore the dilemmas and needs of home-based nutritional management for pediatric liver transplant recipients from the dual perspectives of caregivers and medical providers.DesignA descriptive qualitative study using traditional content analysis.MethodsUsing purposive sampling, semi-structured in-depth interviews were conducted from December 2025 to January 2026 with caregivers of pediatric LT recipients, as well as physicians, nurses, and dietitians from a tertiary hospital in Hangzhou. Traditional content analysis was employed for data analysis.ResultsA total of 20 participants were enrolled (11 caregivers, 6 nurses, 2 physicians, and 1 dietitian). Four core themes were identified. Cognitive dilemmas included caregivers’ superficial understanding of nutrition-rehabilitation associations, poor transmission of authoritative information, and misleading non-professional sources. Behavioral dilemmas comprised imbalance between risk avoidance and nutritional adequacy, lack of nutritional monitoring, and declining adherence. Structural dilemmas involved fragmentation of multidisciplinary collaboration, socioeconomic and family constraints, and disconnected out-of-hospital communication. Systematic support needs encompassed follow-up and monitoring, standardized nutritional training, and peer and social support.ConclusionHome nutritional management for pediatric LT recipients faces cognitive, behavioral, and structural dilemmas: caregivers show shallow knowledge, conservative practices, and poor adherence; systems suffer from fragmented teamwork, poor out of hospital communication, and socioeconomic barriers. Both sides desire smart platforms, diversified follow-up, standardized training, and social support. We recommend a hospital-home collaborative model integrating smart monitoring, phased follow-up, multidisciplinary cooperation, and social resources to enhance nutritional outcomes and quality of life.

Background

Liver transplantation (LT) has become an effective treatment for saving the lives of children with acute or chronic liver failure. Its main indications include liver failure caused by biliary atresia, inherited metabolic diseases, and primary liver malignancies. The annual number of pediatric LT in China exceeds 1000 cases, ranking first globally in total surgical volume.1 The 5-year and 10-year postoperative survival rates have reached 93.1% and 91.8%, respectively.2 With the continuous refinement of LT techniques, postoperative survival rates have significantly improved, and the evaluation indicators of medical outcomes have shifted from mere survival rates to quality of life. In the early period after pediatric LT, children face multiple challenges simultaneously, including surgical trauma stress, immunosuppressive therapy, and the transition from enteral nutrition to oral feeding. Given that these children are in a critical stage of growth and development, nutritional status is not only an important guarantee for surgical success but also a core determinant of long-term quality of life, immune reconstitution, and neurocognitive development.3 Nevertheless, the problem of malnutrition in children after LT remains prominent. Studies have shown that the incidence of malnutrition in children after LT at 1, 3, and 6 months post-discharge is as high as 66.0%, 54.9%, and 53.5%, respectively, and it takes 6 to 12 months for postoperative nutritional status to return to normal levels.4,5 Home-based nutritional management after discharge is a key link affecting the long-term prognosis of these children and urgently requires attention.

Currently, most relevant studies, both domestic and international, have focused on perioperative nutritional management in pediatric LT, primarily addressing nutritional risk assessment, nutritional demand analysis, and in hospital support strategies.6 In contrast, systematic research on home‑based nutritional management after discharge remains relatively scarce. However, the post‑discharge period is a critical phase when children are removed from professional inhospital support and rely entirely on caregivers to implement nutritional plans. During this stage, caregivers must independently handle complex issues such as feeding difficulties, nutritional monitoring, use of specialized formulas, and complementary food introduction. Their cognitive level, access to information, implementation conditions, and psychological status directly affect the children’s nutritional outcomes.4

Although some studies have noted the impact of caregivers’ lack of nutritional knowledge on children’s nutritional status,4,7 most existing research adopts a single perspective design,7 examining the issue either solely from the caregivers’ or solely from the medical providers’ viewpoint, with few studies integrating both perspectives within a unified framework. This single‑perspective approach has important limitations that constrain our understanding of home‑based nutritional management in several ways: First, it fails to reveal the discrepancies in how caregivers and medical providers interpret nutritional information. For instance, instructions given by healthcare professionals may be based on clinical standards, but caregivers constrained by their own cognitive background or daily experience may understand the same advice differently, leading to implementation errors. Second, it cannot capture the mismatched expectations regarding needs: caregivers tend to prioritize daily feasibility, whereas providers may emphasize biochemical targets. Such latent divergence, if unrecognized, undermines the relevance and effectiveness of interventions. Third, a single‑lens design overlooks the attenuation and distortion that occur during information transfer; it cannot systematically illustrate how medical orders are received, translated, and acted upon, nor the behavioral barriers encountered in real‑life execution. Fourth, it is inadequate to fully expose systemic obstacles such as fragmented multidisciplinary collaboration, disconnected out of hospital communication, and socioeconomic constraints. These blind spots directly impede the scientific development and effective implementation of a continuous nutritional management model, rendering many existing intervention strategies disconnected from the real world home context. Given that qualitative research is well suited to exploring the subjective experiences and interactive relationships of diverse stakeholders, and can uncover deep cognitive and behavioral logics that quantitative methods may miss,8 this study adopts a dual‑perspective qualitative design to fill the above research gaps and to provide empirical evidence for building a hospital‑home collaborative continuous nutritional management approach.

This study adopted a dual‑perspective qualitative design with two specific objectives: (1) a comparative objective to identify and contrast the similarities and differences between caregivers’ and healthcare providers’ perceptions regarding nutritional information interpretation, implementation barriers, and support expectations; and (2) an integrative objective to synthesize the convergent and divergent viewpoints from both groups, thereby constructing a comprehensive framework for a hospital-home collaborative continuous nutritional management model. By clarifying both the points of alignment and divergence between the two stakeholder groups, this study aims to provide empirical evidence for developing family centered, integrated nutritional management pathways that improve children’s growth outcomes and reduce family caregiving burden.

Methods

Design

A qualitative descriptive approach was employed to explore the dilemmas and needs of home-based nutritional management for pediatric LT recipients from the dual perspectives of caregivers and medical providers. This method effectively captures uninterpreted phenomena while maintaining rigor.9 The design enabled rich, contextualized data collection through semi-structured interviews with participants, which is particularly valuable for understudied clinical contexts.10 Consistent with this methodological approach, the primary researcher carefully positioned herself within the research design, considered her past clinical experiences in pediatric LT, and documented her assumptions and preconceived notions. The Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Studies (COREQ) guidelines were also applied.11

Sampling

Purposive sampling was used to select caregivers of pediatric patients who had undergone LT and were discharged after recovery, as well as healthcare professionals from the LT center of a tertiary hospital in Hangzhou between December 2025 and January 2026 as interview participants. The authors confirm that all organs were donated voluntarily with written informed consent, and that these procedures were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Istanbul. The exclusion criterion for healthcare professionals was: ① inability to complete the interview due to any reason during the study period. Sample Size

The sample size was determined based on the principle of information saturation. Saturation was operationally defined as the point at which three consecutive interviews yielded no new codes relevant to the research questions and the existing thematic framework adequately explained the phenomena under study.13 During the concurrent process of data collection and analysis, the research team met weekly to review emerging codes and assess whether new themes continued to emerge.

Saturation was assessed separately for the caregiver and healthcare professional groups. For the caregiver group, saturation of the four core themes, information and cognition disconnects, execution and feedback gaps, system and coordination disconnects, and shared priorities for system redesign, was achieved after interviews with 9 caregivers.14 For the healthcare professional group (physicians, nurses, and dietitians), saturation of the same themes was achieved after interviews with 7 providers. To verify the stability of saturation, two additional caregivers and two additional healthcare professionals were interviewed after preliminary saturation was reached, confirming that no new themes or subthemes were generated. All four confirmation interviews were included in the final analysis.

The sampling process followed a maximum variation strategy. In the caregiver group, diversity was emphasized in terms of age, gender, educational level, and relationship with the child. In the healthcare professional group, differences were emphasized with respect to professional background (physician, nurse, dietitian), years of work experience, and professional title, in order to obtain rich and varied perspectives.

Definitions of Key Concepts

Nutritional management encompasses a set of practices including feeding implementation (eg, formula preparation, complementary food introduction, dietary modification), nutritional monitoring, caregiver education, and decision‑making regarding dietary adjustments Monitoring refers specifically to the objective tracking of children’s growth indicators (height, weight, and body mass index) and, where applicable, dietary intake documentation. Follow-up refers to scheduled post-discharge contacts between healthcare providers and families, conducted for nutritional assessment, reinforcement of guidance, and problem-solving. These terms are used consistently throughout the manuscript to ensure conceptual clarity.15

Data Collection

Semi-structured interviews were used for data collection. The interviewer was the first author of this study, who had a background in pediatric LT nursing and had received systematic training in qualitative research methods. The interviewer had no prior contact or therapeutic relationship with any participant to minimize potential influence on participant expression due to power dynamics.

During the study period, the researcher screened potential participants according to the inclusion criteria. For caregivers, after initial contact by the attending physician, eligible participants were introduced to the study purpose before discharge or during outpatient follow-up visits. For healthcare professionals (physicians, nurses, and dietitians), eligible participants were recruited directly from the LT center based on the inclusion criteria, with assistance from the department head and nursing leadership. All potential participants who expressed interest were provided with detailed information about the study.

Interviews were conducted in a quiet and comfortable conference room in the ward area. Before each interview, the researcher clearly explained the study purpose, methods, and confidentiality principles to the participant, and obtained permission to audio-record the entire interview. During the interviews, the researcher asked flexible follow-up questions based on participant responses and paid close attention to non-verbal information such as facial expressions, tone of voice, and body language, which were annotated in the transcripts using parentheses (eg, “sigh”, “hesitation”, “raised tone”) to supplement the verbal data. Each interview lasted approximately 20–40 minutes, with the principle of ensuring that participants felt they had fully expressed themselves, without mandatory time limits. Data Analysis

Within 24 hours after each interview, the researcher transcribed the audio recordings verbatim into electronic text and re-checked the accuracy of the content. NVivo 11 (QSR) supported data management. During the transcription process, identifiable information such as participants’ names was anonymized to protect participant privacy. Given the limited existing research on this topic and the lack of a pre-existing theoretical framework, this study adopted an inductive traditional content analysis approach16 to analyze the interview data. This method does not presuppose a theoretical framework but rather extracts and refines themes from the data through repeated reading and coding of the text. In the participant coding, “P” represented caregivers of pediatric patients, and “D” represented medical professionals.

Two researchers (the first author and a graduate student with experience in qualitative research) independently performed the coding analysis. Both had received systematic training in qualitative research methods. After independent coding, the two researchers compared their coding results and discussed any discrepancies. If consensus could not be reached, a third member of the research team was invited to participate in the discussion until an agreement was achieved. Subsequently, the data were systematically organized, synthesized, and summarized. Member checking was conducted by sharing the summarized findings with participants to ensure alignment with their original responses.

The specific analysis steps were as follows. First, through repeated reading, the researchers read the interview texts repeatedly and carefully until they fully grasped the overall content of the data and entered a state of in-depth understanding. Second, extraction of meaning units and open coding was conducted: the text was analyzed line by line, important statements were marked, content elements closely related to the core research questions were extracted, and preliminary codes were formed. Third, categorization and theme refinement were performed by systematically comparing and synthesizing similar or related codes to gradually form themes and subthemes. Fourth, definition of themes and selection of quotations were carried out: the final themes and subthemes were defined, and representative quotations were selected from the original interview data as supporting examples.

To systematically integrate data from both caregiver and provider groups, we employed a comparative thematic analysis approach.17 After independently developing separate thematic frameworks for caregivers and providers, we constructed a joint display matrix that juxtaposed themes from both groups across three pre‑identified analytical domains: cognitive understanding, behavioral execution, and systemic barriers. This matrix facilitated a side‑by‑side comparison, enabling us to systematically identify: (a) convergent themes – issues where both groups expressed similar views; (b) divergent themes – areas of disagreement or mismatched expectations; and (c) unique themes – issues raised exclusively by one group. Following this comparative matrix analysis, we synthesized the findings across groups to develop an integrated conceptual model. All matrix comparisons were independently performed by two researchers, with discrepancies resolved through team discussion.

Ethics and Informed Consent

This study strictly adhered to the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Review Committee of the participating hospital (20251368). All participants (Caregivers of the children and Healthcare professionals) were informed of the study purpose, methods, potential risks and benefits, as well as their right to voluntary participation and withdrawal at any time prior to enrollment. The researcher assured participants that all interview content would be used solely for this study and that personal identifying information would be kept strictly confidential. All participants signed informed consent forms before participating in the interviews, which included authorization for the publication of anonymized responses and direct quotes in this study. Interview recordings and transcribed texts were accessible only to the research team, and the raw data were destroyed upon completion of the study.

Reflexivity and Rigor

To ensure the authenticity and reliability of the research findings, this study maintained rigor throughout the research process and engaged in continuous reflexive practice. The interviewer, as the first author, had a background in pediatric LT nursing, which facilitated the establishment of trust with participants and enabled in-depth exploration of the research questions. However, clinical experience also posed potential biases, such as presuppositions about specific issues or selective attention. To minimize such biases, the interviewer wrote research memos prior to the interviews to document initial presuppositions regarding the research questions, maintained an open attitude during the interviews to avoid leading questions, and recorded reflective notes promptly after each interview to review the potential influence of personal tendencies on data collection. During the data analysis phase, two researchers independently coded the data and held regular research team meetings to review the coding and theme development processes, ensuring that the analysis results emerged from the data themselves rather than from the researchers’ subjective presuppositions.

Regarding rigor, this study ensured research quality from four dimensions: credibility, transferability, dependability, and confirmability. The researcher’s relevant clinical background helped establish trust with participants and facilitated in-depth information gathering. The research team performed cross-validation of coding and theme development, and confirmed the accuracy of key information with participants at the end of each interview. Detailed descriptions of participant characteristics and the provision of rich raw quotations in the results section facilitated readers’ assessment of the applicability of the research findings. The research process remained transparent, with original materials such as interview recordings, transcripts, and coding records retained for audit. The interview participants covered caregivers, physicians, nurses, and dietitians, ensuring cross-validation of information from multiple perspectives. Through continuous self-reflection and team supervision, the study strived to ensure that the research findings authentically and objectively reflected the experiences and perspectives of the participants.

Through an integrated analysis of the interview data from the dual perspectives of caregivers and healthcare professionals, four core themes were identified. Rather than categorizing dilemmas by whether they originate from caregivers or systems which risks oversimplifying multidimensional data we adopted a relational classification that captures the interactions between stakeholder groups across key care interfaces. The four themes are: Cognitive Information Disconnects (where gaps in information transmission and comprehension occur between providers and families), Execution–Feedback Gaps (where behavioral monitoring and adjustment mechanisms fail due to insufficient feedback), System Coordination Disconnects (where fragmented teamwork and communication breakdowns undermine continuity), and Shared Priorities for System Redesign (where both groups converge on solutions). This relational framework directly reflects our joint display matrix analysis (Data Collection) and clarifies how dual‑perspective integration enriches understanding beyond single‑lens designs. In presenting each theme, we explicitly indicate convergent, divergent, or unique perspectives between the two groups.

Cognitive–Information Disconnects

Caregivers’ Superficial Understanding of the Association Between Nutrition and Recovery

We found that most caregivers failed to establish a deep connection between their child’s nutritional status and long-term recovery. They also lacked awareness of the need to continuously monitor growth and development indicators. As time since discharge increased and professional guidance weakened, caregivers often equated good nutrition simply with the absence of symptoms. One mother stated: As long as my baby doesn’t have a fever or diarrhea, I think it’s fine. I haven’t really paid much attention to whether the nutrition is good or not. (P6)

As long as my baby doesn’t have a fever or diarrhea, I think it’s fine. I haven’t really paid much attention to whether the nutrition is good or not. (P6)

Caregivers attributed postoperative growth retardation to the disease itself rather than linking it to nutritional management. Several mothers expressed: I know about malnutrition, but they said the LT surgery would take care of it (P7).

I know about malnutrition, but they said the LT surgery would take care of it (P7).

A father also noted: I’m not sure if my baby is malnourished. She just looks a bit small, and I don’t know if it will affect her in the future (P10).

I’m not sure if my baby is malnourished. She just looks a bit small, and I don’t know if it will affect her in the future (P10).

This cognitive bias weakened the initiative and motivation for home-based nutritional management. A LT nurse confirmed: I’ve noticed many parents think that if their child isn’t sick, then nutrition must be fine. This misconception is quite common (D3).

I’ve noticed many parents think that if their child isn’t sick, then nutrition must be fine. This misconception is quite common (D3).

While both groups converged on recognizing caregivers’ superficial understanding, their interpretations diverged: providers framed it as a health literacy deficit, whereas caregivers themselves saw “no symptoms” as a natural proxy for “good recovery”.

Ineffective Transmission of Authoritative Information and Barriers to Comprehension

In discharge guidance, discrepancies in information conveyed by different healthcare providers made it difficult for caregivers to accurately implement nutritional plans. Physicians differed in their opinions regarding the duration of specialized medium-chain triglyceride (MCT) formula use and determinations of feeding contraindications. This resulted in a lack of standardized discharge guidance. Several caregivers mentioned: How long should MCT formula be given? One doctor said one month, another said two weeks. We’re so confused (P3).

How long should MCT formula be given? One doctor said one month, another said two weeks. We’re so confused (P3).

A LT center nurse added: Clinically, many infants with chylous leakage require MCT formula, which is recommended for one month. However, due to the high cost and inconvenient access, implementation is not consistent. (D6)

Clinically, many infants with chylous leakage require MCT formula, which is recommended for one month. However, due to the high cost and inconvenient access, implementation is not consistent. (D6)

Furthermore, caregivers had limited medical knowledge and struggled to understand professional terminology. This led to attenuation and distortion of information at the receiving end. A dietitian noted: We repeatedly tell them that as long as it’s tolerated, anything can be eaten. But after returning home, they still tell each other, ‘this can’t be eaten, that can’t be eaten’. (D7)

We repeatedly tell them that as long as it’s tolerated, anything can be eaten. But after returning home, they still tell each other, ‘this can’t be eaten, that can’t be eaten’. (D7)

A marked divergence emerged here: providers believed their instructions were sufficiently clear, whereas caregivers reported persistent confusion stemming from contradictory messages across different physicians.

Dominance and Misguidance of Non-Professional Information Sources

After discharge, caregivers’ immediate connection with the medical team was severed. This forced them to turn to non-authoritative channels such as patient support groups, Douyin, and Xiaohongshu for answers. One mother admitted: When I encounter feeding problems, I usually look for information on Douyin or Xiaohongshu, but I can’t fully trust what I find online (P5).

When I encounter feeding problems, I usually look for information on Douyin or Xiaohongshu, but I can’t fully trust what I find online (P5).

These channels provided predominantly anecdotal experiences, which caregivers found difficult to assess for applicability. This often led to cognitive anxiety and decision-making dilemmas. Another mother expressed her confusion: Most of the time, I ask questions in patient support groups, but every child is different, and I rarely get the right answer (P6).

Most of the time, I ask questions in patient support groups, but every child is different, and I rarely get the right answer (P6).

More concerning, anecdotal experiences shared among patients often contradicted professional guidance, further amplifying misunderstandings. One mother’s experience was particularly illustrative: Someone told me that beef and pork should not be eaten, so even though my baby is 12 months old, I only give him porridge and formula because I’m afraid of causing problems. (P4)

Someone told me that beef and pork should not be eaten, so even though my baby is 12 months old, I only give him porridge and formula because I’m afraid of causing problems. (P4)

A physician also observed this phenomenon: We gave very clear instructions at discharge, but parents go back home, hear something different in the support groups, and start adjusting feeding practices on their own. By the time they come for a follow-up, the problems are already evident. (D8)

We gave very clear instructions at discharge, but parents go back home, hear something different in the support groups, and start adjusting feeding practices on their own. By the time they come for a follow-up, the problems are already evident. (D8)

Both groups acknowledged the negative influence of non-professional sources, yet they diverged in framing: caregivers turned to these channels to fill a guidance void, while providers viewed them primarily as adherence barriers.

Execution–Feedback Gaps

Imbalance Between Risk Avoidance and Nutritional Adequacy

Most caregivers reported difficulty balancing the need to ensure adequate nutrition while avoiding feeding intolerance. They feared both that insufficient nutrition might affect growth and that improper feeding might induce complications. Over half of the caregivers exhibited pronounced overly conservative tendencies. One mother stated: He has poor digestion. I’m afraid giving him too much will cause diarrhea, so even though he’s one year old now, I only dare to give him porridge (P2).

He has poor digestion. I’m afraid giving him too much will cause diarrhea, so even though he’s one year old now, I only dare to give him porridge (P2).

In the context of information overload and lack of professional guidance, caregivers’ tendency toward risk avoidance was continuously reinforced. This led them into an overly conservative state. Another father noted: We’ve been home for six months and only give him formula because once he had diarrhea after eating fruit, and since then I’ve been afraid to introduce solid foods. (P8)

We’ve been home for six months and only give him formula because once he had diarrhea after eating fruit, and since then I’ve been afraid to introduce solid foods. (P8)

Multiple caregivers mentioned that this defensive strategy manifested as delayed introduction of solid foods, monotonous dietary structures, and strict restriction of food types. These measures appeared to mitigate short-term risks but ultimately compromised dietary diversity and nutritional balance. One mother expressed confusion about feeding combinations: I give him three meals a day like adults eat, and between meals, I let him eat whenever he wants. I’m not really sure how to put it all together (P6).

I give him three meals a day like adults eat, and between meals, I let him eat whenever he wants. I’m not really sure how to put it all together (P6).

Nurses also noted during interviews that the phenomenon of caregivers overly restricting food due to fear of feeding risks was common in clinical practice. There was convergent recognition of conservative feeding practices, but caregivers and providers framed it differently: caregivers viewed their approach as prudent precaution, whereas providers interpreted it as excessive anxiety driven by misinformation.

Lack of Nutritional Monitoring Behaviors

In the absence of systematic guidance and effective follow-up support, the vast majority of caregivers relied on subjective judgment rather than objective monitoring to assess their child’s nutritional status. One mother noted: I look at my baby—if he seems energetic, I assume he’s recovering well. No one told me I should be measuring height and weight (P1).

I look at my baby—if he seems energetic, I assume he’s recovering well. No one told me I should be measuring height and weight (P1).

Caregivers evaluated recovery based on superficial indicators such as good energy levels and absence of illness, overlooking the value of objective monitoring. Over two-thirds of caregivers reported never or rarely measuring their child’s height. Another mother’s approach was typical: We weigh him, but we’ve never measured his height. I don’t know if there’s malnutrition (P3).

We weigh him, but we’ve never measured his height. I don’t know if there’s malnutrition (P3).

Even when monitoring awareness existed, it was limited to a single indicator—weight—making comprehensive assessment difficult. Another mother’s feeding philosophy reflected a lack of monitoring awareness: I just think that if my child eats more, he’ll get more nutrition. I give him whatever he likes to eat (P2).

I just think that if my child eats more, he’ll get more nutrition. I give him whatever he likes to eat (P2).

This monitoring blind spot allowed nutritional issues to remain hidden for extended periods. By the time abnormalities such as growth retardation became apparent, opportunities for timely intervention were often missed. A specialized nutrition nurse also observed this phenomenon: Many parents rely purely on their instincts to judge whether their child is growing well; they never measure height or weight (D5).

Many parents rely purely on their instincts to judge whether their child is growing well; they never measure height or weight (D5).

A clear divergence was evident: providers assumed that growth monitoring was a routine practice, whereas caregivers reported they had never received explicit instruction on how or why to measure these indicators.

Decline in Adherence and Implementation Deviation

As time since discharge increased and dynamic assessment mechanisms were absent, caregivers’ adherence showed a declining trend. Their execution of precise measurement standards in individualized plans gradually slackened. This compromised the effective implementation of nutritional interventions. The head nurse of the LT center noted: Caregivers’ cooperation shows a clear declining trend with time since discharge. In the first three months post-surgery, adherence remains relatively good, but after six months, it becomes arbitrary. (D1)

Caregivers’ cooperation shows a clear declining trend with time since discharge. In the first three months post-surgery, adherence remains relatively good, but after six months, it becomes arbitrary. (D1)

Most caregivers struggled to achieve the precise measurement standards required by professionals. A specialized nutrition nurse pointed out: Many parents have generally low adherence to dietary records. It’s difficult for them to maintain daily detailed records of their child’s dietary intake, especially breastfeeding amounts, which are particularly challenging to estimate. (D5)

Many parents have generally low adherence to dietary records. It’s difficult for them to maintain daily detailed records of their child’s dietary intake, especially breastfeeding amounts, which are particularly challenging to estimate. (D5)

Such meticulous management behaviors as dietary recording and intake estimation were difficult to sustain, causing nutritional management to gradually devolve toward a coarse approach. A transplant center physician also observed: Parents’ dietary management mostly takes a coarse approach. It’s challenging to achieve the professional requirement of precise measurement to the gram (D8).

Parents’ dietary management mostly takes a coarse approach. It’s challenging to achieve the professional requirement of precise measurement to the gram (D8).

Caregivers’ adherence continuously declined both temporally and in terms of precision, creating a significant gap between actual practice and individualized plan requirements. While both sides observed this adherence decline, they attributed it differently: providers tended to blame caregiver complacency, whereas caregiver accounts pointed more toward a progressive loss of feedback and guidance over time.

System–Coordination Disconnects

Fragmentation of Multidisciplinary Collaboration

In the clinical practice of nutritional management for pediatric LT patients, multidisciplinary collaboration was characterized by role absence and fragmented coordination. This made it difficult to achieve continuous care from hospital to home. First, the role of the dietitian was limited to inpatient consultations and became completely absent after discharge. The head nurse of the transplant center noted: Currently, it’s mostly a consultation model. We lack a dedicated dietitian to develop personalized nutritional management plans, and after discharge, dietitians are not involved in nutritional management. (D1)

Currently, it’s mostly a consultation model. We lack a dedicated dietitian to develop personalized nutritional management plans, and after discharge, dietitians are not involved in nutritional management. (D1)

A dietitian also emphasized: Dietitians should routinely take the lead in determining children’s daily nutritional targets, but in reality, this role is absent (D7).

Dietitians should routinely take the lead in determining children’s daily nutritional targets, but in reality, this role is absent (D7).

Nurses’ follow-up functions were similarly limited, focusing primarily on routine condition monitoring without specialized, systematic support for nutritional management. A transplant center nurse noted: Nurses play a crucial coordinating role in the team, but current follow-up content mainly focuses on routine condition monitoring; nutrition is not specifically addressed. (D2)

Nurses play a crucial coordinating role in the team, but current follow-up content mainly focuses on routine condition monitoring; nutrition is not specifically addressed. (D2)

This role absence and fragmented coordination directly affected caregivers, leaving them in a situation where they had nowhere to turn for help. Multiple caregivers expressed helplessness: At discharge, the doctor told us how to feed the child, but when we encounter problems at home, we don’t know whom to ask. We simply can’t reach the doctors or dietitians. (P3)

At discharge, the doctor told us how to feed the child, but when we encounter problems at home, we don’t know whom to ask. We simply can’t reach the doctors or dietitians. (P3)

Thus, multidisciplinary collaboration fractured during the transition from hospital to home, making it difficult to achieve a closed-loop system of continuous care. Thus, multidisciplinary collaboration fractured precisely during the hospital-to-home transition, but this breakdown manifested differently across perspectives: providers emphasized role fragmentation within the system, whereas caregivers experienced it primarily as communication inaccessibility once they returned home.

Constraints of Socioeconomic and Family Conditions

The effective implementation of professional plans was constrained by multiple structural factors, including economic conditions, geographic environment, and family support. The interplay of these factors placed some families in challenging implementation situations. Economic pressures forced some families to forgo MCT formula. One caregiver expressed helplessness: The specialized formula is too expensive. We couldn’t afford it, so we switched to goat’s milk formula (P6).

The specialized formula is too expensive. We couldn’t afford it, so we switched to goat’s milk formula (P6).

Geographic limitations further exacerbated implementation difficulties. Another caregiver noted: We live in a rural area; it’s inconvenient to buy things. There’s a market only every three days (P9).

We live in a rural area; it’s inconvenient to buy things. There’s a market only every three days (P9).

Grandparent-led care made precise management difficult to implement. A dietitian observed: With some children being raised by grandparents, what you tell them doesn’t seem to matter. The parents are away working, but the actual practices still follow the old ways. (D7)

With some children being raised by grandparents, what you tell them doesn’t seem to matter. The parents are away working, but the actual practices still follow the old ways. (D7)

The combination of these multiple factors meant that even when caregivers were willing to implement plans, they faced significant challenges in doing so, making precise nutritional management difficult to achieve. Caregivers identified financial and geographic barriers as primary and immediate obstacles to implementation, whereas providers, while acknowledging these factors, did not assign them the same level of priority in their clinical reasoning.

Disconnection of Post-Discharge Medical Communication

After returning home, caregivers experienced a sudden loss of the readily accessible medical support they had in the hospital. This left them with only periodic outpatient follow-up visits as a channel for addressing nutritional concerns. Several mothers articulated this contrast: When we were in the hospital, we could directly ask doctors or nurses whenever we had questions. But after going home, there’s no way to ask—we have to wait until the follow-up appointment to talk to the doctor. (P1)

When we were in the hospital, we could directly ask doctors or nurses whenever we had questions. But after going home, there’s no way to ask—we have to wait until the follow-up appointment to talk to the doctor. (P1)

There was a temporal misalignment between feeding concerns arising at home and periodic clinic visits, making timely resolution difficult. Even when some families maintained contact with physicians via WeChat, the lack of institutional support made such communication unsustainable. One mother expressed her hope: Doctors are very busy; sometimes they respond slowly on WeChat. It would be great if there were a dedicated nutrition specialist available (P11).

Doctors are very busy; sometimes they respond slowly on WeChat. It would be great if there were a dedicated nutrition specialist available (P11).

A LT physician acknowledged: Currently, some families do add the attending physician on WeChat, and I do respond, but the response speed isn’t that fast (D8).

Currently, some families do add the attending physician on WeChat, and I do respond, but the response speed isn’t that fast (D8).

A salient divergence emerged: caregivers prioritized communication disconnection as the single greatest barrier to home-based management, whereas providers were more concerned with teamwork fragmentation—a gap that reflected fundamentally different vantage points on the same care continuum.

Shared Priorities for System Redesign

Follow-Up and Monitoring Needs

Both caregivers and healthcare providers expressed a need to utilize intelligent tools to optimize follow-up management. They hoped that digital platforms could help bridge the gap in continuous care. Regarding follow-up formats, both parties anticipated a hybrid model combining offline and online approaches. Healthcare providers proposed a staged follow-up strategy, emphasizing the integration of in-person clinic guidance in the early postoperative period with ongoing online follow-up in later stages. One nurse noted: In the first month after surgery, weekly in-person clinic follow-ups can be conducted. Later stages can use online formats, so parents don’t have to come to the hospital frequently. (D3)

In the first month after surgery, weekly in-person clinic follow-ups can be conducted. Later stages can use online formats, so parents don’t have to come to the hospital frequently. (D3)

Caregivers, in turn, expected a regular and reasonable follow-up frequency. One caregiver expressed: Follow-up visits every half month or month would be good—letting the doctor check the child’s nutritional status feels appropriate (P9).

Follow-up visits every half month or month would be good—letting the doctor check the child’s nutritional status feels appropriate (P9).

Regarding follow-up content, both parties hoped that follow-ups would focus specifically on nutritional management, including dietary adjustments, interpretation of growth indicators, and feeding-related questions. In terms of platform functionality, both parties envisioned intelligent platforms capable of data recording, automated assessment, health education push notifications, and online consultation. One caregiver expressed the desire for: A nutrition chart on the phone listing what to eat at each stage (P5).

A nutrition chart on the phone listing what to eat at each stage (P5).

Another hoped the platform would combine authority with interactivity: It would be great to have a mini-program with official dietary guidance where you could leave messages if you have questions (P11).

It would be great to have a mini-program with official dietary guidance where you could leave messages if you have questions (P11).

Additionally, platforms were expected to have early warning and proactive intervention capabilities. One healthcare provider noted: I hope that when a child’s nutritional status begins shifting from good to malnourished, we can identify and intervene early (D9).

I hope that when a child’s nutritional status begins shifting from good to malnourished, we can identify and intervene early (D9).

The goal, as envisioned by both sides, was to achieve integration between family-initiated self-monitoring and provider-led remote assessment—closing the loop that had been missing in current practice.

Standardized Nutrition Training Needs

Healthcare providers indicated that clinical nutrition management requires mastery of core skills such as energy calculation, specialized formula use, and nutritional risk assessment. They also noted the need for continuous knowledge updates to address children’s individual needs. However, providers reported that current training often tends to be superficial, lacking effective integration between assessment and application. A LT center nurse’s comment captured a key concern: Training must include an assessment component; it can’t just be a formality. Theoretical exams and practical skills tests are needed to evaluate learning outcomes. (D6)

Training must include an assessment component; it can’t just be a formality. Theoretical exams and practical skills tests are needed to evaluate learning outcomes. (D6)

Another nurse emphasized the need for precision in training content: Regular training sessions should be organized, covering the latest concepts in nutritional management, especially highly individualized knowledge like energy calculation. (D4)

Regular training sessions should be organized, covering the latest concepts in nutritional management, especially highly individualized knowledge like energy calculation. (D4)

A LT physician further underscored the scope of training: Both theoretical knowledge and practical skills should be covered, particularly specialized training in nutritional management for patients with complex needs (D8).

Both theoretical knowledge and practical skills should be covered, particularly specialized training in nutritional management for patients with complex needs (D8).

These insights collectively suggested that developing a practical nutrition training system integrating instruction, competency assessment, and clinical application had become a pressing need to enhance the precision and consistency of nutrition management across the care continuum.

Both caregivers and healthcare providers recognized the important role of peer support and social welfare organizations in alleviating caregiver stress and improving care quality. Caregivers desired the establishment of experience-sharing platforms to facilitate horizontal knowledge exchange. Relationships Among the Four Themes: A Preliminary Conceptual Model

The four themes identified above are linked through a bidirectional causal structure, informed by the Health Belief Model and the Continuity of Care Framework (elaborated in the Discussion). Cognitive–information disconnects (Theme 1) serve as the cognitive precursor: inconsistent guidance and non-professional information shape caregivers’ understanding, which in turn drives execution–feedback gaps (Theme 2). This is manifested specifically in conservative feeding practices, neglect of growth monitoring, and declining adherence over time. These behavioral patterns, however, are sustained by system–coordination disconnects (Theme 3), particularly fragmented multidisciplinary roles and disrupted post-discharge communication, which deprive caregivers of the corrective feedback needed to adjust their behaviors. Socioeconomic constraints, embedded within Theme 3, act as an amplifying factor that disproportionately affects families with fewer resources. The resulting vicious cycle, moving from information distortion to behavioral maladaptation to system reinforcement and then back to worsening cognitive distortion, can only be interrupted by targeted interventions at each level. Notably, caregivers and providers perceived different points of entry into this cycle: caregivers experienced it primarily through daily behavioral struggles at home, whereas providers observed it through adherence decline and clinical outcomes. Both perspectives are necessary to capture the full dynamics of the cycle and to design interventions that address both ends of the care continuum. Theme 4 (shared priorities) represents the convergent pathway through which both caregivers and providers envision breaking this cycle. This relational framework provides a theoretical basis for designing integrated, multi-level interventions (Figure 1). It should be noted, however, that these relationships are inferred from participants’ accounts and do not constitute direct evidence of causal progression. Discussion

This study identified that cognitive dissonance between caregivers and healthcare professionals constitutes the primary trigger for inadequate home-based nutritional management. This manifests as caregivers’ superficial understanding of nutritional value, insufficient homogenization of authoritative information transmission, and predominant misinformation from informal sources. These three elements progressively interact, forming a vicious cycle of “cognitive bias—information misinformation—decision-making confusion”. Caregivers commonly equated “good nutrition” with “absence of abnormal symptoms”, believing that “no fever, no diarrhea” signified satisfactory recovery, failing to establish an effective connection between nutritional support and the child’s long-term rehabilitation. This finding aligns with Liu et al,7 who reported that caregivers generally lack a systematic understanding of the importance of post-transplant nutrition. However, this study further revealed that caregivers’ cognitive limitations extend beyond a simple lack of knowledge; more critically, they fail to comprehend the role of nutritional management in supporting long-term outcomes such as immune reconstitution, neurocognitive development, and overall survival quality. This “blind spot regarding long-term value” results in the persistent marginalization of nutritional management in home care.18

Caregivers’ conservative feeding practices and monitoring neglect are explicable through the Health Belief Model.19 In this study, they tended to view malnutrition as a minor, transient problem rather than an urgent threat. Within the HBM framework, this perception weakens the impetus for proactive nutritional management. At the same time, they perceived feeding as fraught with obstacles: formula preparation was cumbersome, complementary feeding guidelines felt ambiguous, and financial constraints were real. Perceived barriers consistently emerge as the strongest negative correlate of feeding behavior. The combination of low severity perception and high barriers sets up a self-reinforcing loop: if the problem does not seem serious and the solution seems too difficult, monitoring becomes an expendable activity. This is not irrationality, but a predictable response to the conditions caregivers face. What we observed amounts to breakdowns on all three fronts. Guidance was inconsistent across providers, roles were fragmented, and communication all but ceased after discharge. Relational continuity is not merely one dimension among three; it underpins the others. Once that relationship attenuates, as it inevitably does when outpatient visits are spaced months apart, informational and managerial coherence erode with it. Caregivers are left to reconcile conflicting advice on their own. The failure of home-based nutritional management, then, is not a story of caregiver deficiency, but one of structural gaps in the provider–family interface.

Insufficient homogenization of authoritative information transmission exacerbates this cognitive dissonance. In this study, significant discrepancies existed among different physicians regarding the duration of MCT milk formula use and contraindications for feeding, leaving caregivers in a state of confusion after receiving contradictory information. McLaren-Hedwards et al,20 in interviews with transplant clinicians, also identified the lack of consistent nutritional recommendations as a key barrier to implementing nutritional interventions. Trigui et al21 noted the absence of long-term dietary guidelines specifically for post-liver transplantation, highlighting the lack of standardized pathways for clinical nutritional guidance. When authoritative information fails to provide consistent output, caregivers turn to informal sources such as patient support groups and social media platforms like TikTok, where the mixed and professionally unverified information further amplifies cognitive biases and decision-making risks.22 Notably, this disconnect was asymmetrically perceived: providers viewed caregivers’ confusion as a knowledge deficit, while caregivers attributed it to inconsistent guidance—a perceptual gap invisible to single-perspective designs.

Beyond these cognitive challenges, caregivers’ behavioral patterns further compound the difficulties in home-based nutritional management. This study found that caregivers’ feeding decision-making phase exhibited a conservative tendency, the process monitoring phase showed subjective complacency, and the sustained implementation phase displayed a pattern of attenuation. These factors collectively exacerbate the imbalance in home-based nutritional management for pediatric patients, aligning closely with findings from research on behavioral dilemmas in home care following pediatric LT.23 Overly conservative feeding represents a defensive stress reaction by caregivers to perceived feeding risks, stemming from excessive anxiety regarding postoperative feeding complications and a risk-avoidance mindset. This is a common behavioral manifestation of post-trauma caregiving stress.24 In this study, once a child experienced adverse events such as diarrhea or vomiting, caregivers adopted a generalized avoidance strategy, solidifying isolated incidents into long-term prohibitions. This was expressed through delayed introduction of complementary foods, restricted food variety, and maintenance of a monotonous diet. Although this approach mitigates the immediate risk of feeding intolerance in the short term, it comes at the long-term cost of dietary diversity and nutritional balance, thereby exacerbating the child’s malnutrition and delaying growth and development. This conclusion is consistent with findings from domestic studies on feeding behaviors of caregivers of pediatric LT recipients.7

The subjective nature of monitoring practices further undermines effective nutritional management. The vast majority of caregivers relied solely on subjective feelings to judge their child’s nutritional recovery, failing to conduct regular monitoring of objective growth indicators such as height and weight. Consequently, nutritional problems were often identified only after the optimal window for intervention had passed. Caregivers not only lack a routine awareness of nutritional monitoring but also lack simple, user-friendly standardized monitoring tools and practical guidance. The absence of a continuous post-discharge nutritional monitoring system represents a common shortcoming in home-based management following pediatric transplantation both domestically and internationally.25 Additionally, caregiver adherence remained relatively good within the first three months post-transplant but gradually showed a trend of casualness and laxity as time since discharge increased. This suggests that intervention points for continuity of care should not be concentrated solely in the early post-discharge period but should be dynamically scheduled and reinforced in stages based on patterns of declining adherence, aligning with the changing patterns of adherence in post-transplant family care.26 Providers perceived these behaviors as non-compliance, whereas the data suggest they are predictable responses to feedback deprivation—indicating that intervention should target monitoring structures, not caregiver motivation alone.

The persistence of these cognitive and behavioral dilemmas, however, cannot be fully understood without examining the deeper structural issues that sustain them. This study found that fragmented multidisciplinary collaboration, disconnected post-discharge communication, and socioeconomic constraints interact, making it challenging to correct individual behaviors due to systemic barriers. First, multidisciplinary collaboration mechanisms exhibit a disconnected pattern characterized by in-hospital involvement but post-discharge absence. Dietitians participate only through consultations during hospitalization and are completely absent after discharge. Sun et al27 confirmed that multidisciplinary nutritional management helps improve nutritional status and promote recovery in transplant patients. Second, the disconnection in post-discharge communication between healthcare providers and families exacerbates management difficulties. After discharge, caregivers’ immediate connection with the medical team is severed, leaving scheduled outpatient visits as the only channel for addressing concerns. This model of intermittent guidance creates a significant temporal mismatch with the real-time challenges faced by caregivers during the home care period. Socioeconomic constraints place disadvantaged families in a situation where execution becomes impossible: the high cost of specialized formula forces some families to switch to regular formula on their own, while difficulties in procuring supplies in rural areas further diminish dietary diversity. Wadhwani et al,28 regarding intervention strategies for social adversity in pediatric LT recipients, emphasized the need to refine the mechanisms by which risk factors impact outcomes to improve post-transplant prognosis for children from socioeconomically disadvantaged backgrounds. Providers focused on fragmented teamwork, while caregivers prioritized communication disconnection a divergence suggesting that internal coordination and external connectivity must be addressed simultaneously.

Based on these findings, we recommend the following strategies, each directly targeting a specific dilemma identified in the data: as supported by Zhang et al,24 who demonstrated that “Internet+”-based continuous nursing improved nutritional status and adherence in pediatric LT recipients, and by emerging evidence from other digital health interventions in pediatric transplant populations.29,30 Given these systemic barriers, there is an urgent clinical need to construct a hospital-community-family triadic linkage model for continuity of nutritional management.31 It is recommended to establish an intelligent nutritional management platform integrating functions such as nutritional monitoring, online follow-up, and educational training. This platform should define clear timelines, such as weekly follow-ups during the first month post-discharge and bi-weekly follow-ups from one to six months, to overcome the spatial and temporal limitations of post-discharge communication and facilitate the broader accessibility of professional nutritional care, thereby optimizing the implementation effectiveness of continuity of nutritional management models.32 Concurrently, social welfare organizations should be engaged to provide assistance with specialized formula costs for economically disadvantaged families, alleviating structural socioeconomic pressure through targeted support.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the research sample was derived from a single center, with all participants recruited from the same transplant center. The regional distribution, medical resources, and follow-up management model possess unique characteristics, thereby limiting the generalizability of the study findings. Second, the interview window was confined to the first six months post-discharge, preventing the tracking of changes in caregivers’ and healthcare professionals’ perceptions and behaviors regarding nutritional management beyond six months. This limitation makes it difficult to capture the dynamic evolution of home-based nutritional management over time following discharge. Third, this study only adopted the perspectives of caregivers and healthcare professionals, without incorporating the viewpoints of the pediatric patients themselves (where age-appropriate). Fourth, only one dietitian was included in the study, which may not fully reflect the diversity of perspectives within the dietitian professional group. Future research should expand the sample scope by conducting multicenter studies and incorporating multiple stakeholder perspectives to enhance the representativeness and generalizability of the findings. Additionally, longitudinal follow-up studies could be conducted to further validate the conclusions and explore the dynamic patterns of home-based nutritional management.

Conclusion

This qualitative study explored dilemmas and needs in home-based nutritional management for pediatric liver transplant recipients from the perspectives of caregivers and healthcare providers. Four core themes were identified: cognitive–information disconnects, execution–feedback gaps, system–coordination disconnects, and shared priorities for system redesign. Through comparative analysis, we found that while both groups converged on recognizing similar challenges, they diverged significantly in causal attribution providers often framed issues as caregiver knowledge deficits or non-compliance, whereas caregivers attributed difficulties to inconsistent guidance, inaccessible professional support, and socioeconomic constraints. These themes are interrelated: cognitive deficits drive behavioral maladaptation, which is sustained by system-level failures that in turn reinforce cognitive distortions. Based on the shared priorities expressed by both groups, smarter follow-up systems, standardized guidance, and peer support represent promising directions for future intervention development. However, given the exploratory, single-center nature of this study, implementation research across diverse settings is needed to validate the feasibility and effectiveness of these approaches.
