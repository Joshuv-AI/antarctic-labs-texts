Larmor radiation as a witness to the Unruh effect
Atsushi Higuchi, George E. A. Matsas, Daniel A. T. Vanzella, Robert Bingham, João P. B. Brito, Luís C. B. Crispino, Gianluca Gregori, Georgios Vacalis

Abstract
We discuss the emission of radiation from general sources in quantum scalar, electromagnetic and gravitational fields using the Rindler coordinate frame, which is suitable for uniformly accelerated observers, in the Minkowski vacuum. In particular, we point out that, to recover, from the point of view of uniformly accelerated observers in the interaction picture, the usual Larmor radiation, which is independent of the choice of the vacuum state, it is necessary to incorporate the Unruh effect assuming the Minkowski vacuum. Thus, the observation of classical Larmor radiation in the Minkowski vacuum could be seen as vindicating the Unruh effect in the sense that it is not correctly recovered in the uniformly accelerated frame unless the Unruh effect is taken into account.



On a sabbatical leave.

I
Introduction

Quantum mechanics (QM) and special relativity (SR), each in their own way, have profoundly changed our understanding of Nature. While classic determinism had to
give way to the inherent uncertainties of quantum superpositions, space and time were eventually understood as just parts of a more fundamental stage, the spacetime,
on which mass-energy equivalence opened the way for “matter” to be no longer conserved.
In view of these shifts in the paradigm separately promoted by QM and SR, it should not
come as a surprise that their combination, into the successful framework of quantum field
theory (QFT), would lead to novel effects which defy our intuition. The relative (hence, non-fundamental) nature of the quantum particle
concept is possibly one of the most underappreciated revelations of QFT. It probably finds
its utmost realization in the Unruh effect
[
1
]
,
according to which the usual vacuum state of a QFT in
Minkowski spacetime—i.e. the state that inertial observers describe as the absence
of real particles—is “seen” as a thermal bath of particles by uniformly accelerated
observers. This effect is closely related to the Hawking effect, which leads to the evaporation
of black holes
[
2
,
3
]
.

Despite the rigorous derivations of the Unruh effect in QFT using
different approaches (see Refs.
[
4
,
5
,
6
,
7
]
), its reality (or consequences) are
frequently put into question (see, e.g., Ref.
[
8
,
9
,
10
,
11
,
12
,
13
,
14
]
) — not rarely due to misconceptions about its
precise meaning.
These debates have motivated several proposals for observing
signatures of the acceleration radiation in the laboratory (see, e.g.,
Refs.
[

,
16
,

,

,

,

,

,

24
,
25
]
and references therein).
In considering these proposals, it is important to keep in mind that the
Unruh effect is
not
an additional ingredient which is assumed on top of
QFT in the inertial frame; this is an effect that must be taken into account
when interpreting standard inertial-frame QFT results from the perspective of
uniformly accelerated observers. In other words, without the Unruh thermal
bath in the uniformly accelerated frame, uniformly accelerated observers
would not be able to explain the phenomena that inertial observers in the usual vacuum
successfully describe with the standard QFT.
The Unruh effect in QFT is analogous to

inertial
forces, also known as
fictitious forces
, in Newtonian mechanics:
one needs to take the inertial forces into account in the non-inertial frame in order to correctly describe the motion
of particles which inertial observers successfully describe using Newton’s laws
without these “extra” forces. Similarly, one needs to take the Unruh effect into account to use QFT in a uniformly accelerated frame in order
to correctly describe the results of QFT in an intertial frame.
This analogy clarifies what constitutes an
“observation” of the Unruh effect in
any
inertial-lab-based experiment:
it means measuring an effect in the inertial frame which uniformly accelerated
observers can only account for by using the Unruh thermal bath.

The main purpose of this paper is to explain further the fact
that usual
classical
radiation (as measured
in inertial frames), such as Larmor radiation
[

,
27
]
, can be considered as such an observation of the Unruh effect. This has
already been pointed out in the literature with particular sources and

,

; here,
we present a general proof valid not only for the electromagnetic field with general charge distributions but also for the
scalar and graviton fields.

Building on the observation of Unruh and Wald that the emission of a quantum in the inertial
frame corresponds to either the emission or absorption of a quantum to or from the Unruh
thermal bath in the uniformly accelerated frame
[
36
]
, we show
here
that, at first order in perturbation theory,
the
interaction probability

(i.e. the sum of emission and absorption probabilities) of a classical source
according to uniformly accelerated observers, in the presence of the Unruh thermal bath,
gives exactly the emission probability needed to reproduce classical radiation
from the inertial perspective. The quantum nature of the Unruh effect is reflected in the fact that this agreement
is only possible by assuming that radiation is made of
quanta
, whose
energy
E
and frequency
\nu
are related by Planck’s formula,
E=h\nu
,
with
h
being Planck’s constant.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. In Sec.

, we expand the
massless Klein-Gordon scalar field using the Unruh modes and their complex conjugates, which are eigenfunctions of the boost Killing vector field and are
positive- and negative-frequency solutions with respect to inertial time translations—and,

according to inertial observers. In Sec.

which are eigenfunctions of the boost Killing vector field with support in a Rindler wedge—hence, related to quantization in the uniformly accelerated frame.
Then, in Sec.

P_{\rm em}^{(\rm M)}
for a classical source to emit a quantum of the field, from the
inertial-frame perspective, and (ii) the probability
P_{\rm int}^{(\rm R)}
for the same classical source to absorb or emit
a quantum from or to the Unruh thermal bath, from the uniformly accelerated perspective.
These two quantities turn out to be exactly the same, provided the source is completely
contained in the Rindler wedge (so that it can be fully described in the uniformly
accelerated frame).
Sec.
V
is devoted to extending the result
in Sec.
IV

to the electromagnetic and graviton fields. Finally, in Sec.
VI

present our final considerations and discussion. In Appendix
A

is found in the Heisenberg picture in

formula is reproduced in the
Fulling vacuum state as well in the interaction picture. We adopt
the metric signature
(+,-,-,-)

\hbar=c=1

II
The Unruh modes as the boost eigenfunctions

We begin by considering the Unruh effect
for the free massless scalar field.
This quantum field is naturally expanded in terms of the momentum eigenstates
as
[
37
]

\displaystyle\widehat{\phi}(x)=\int\frac{d^{3}\mathbf{k}}{(2\pi)^{3}2k}\left[f^{\mathbf{k}}(x)\,\hat{a}_{\mathbf{k}}+\overline{f^{\mathbf{k}}(x)}\,\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k}}\right]\,,

(1)

with
x=(t,\mathbf{x})=(t,z,\mathbf{x}_{\perp})
,

\mathbf{k}=(k_{z},\mathbf{k}_{\perp})
, and
k=\|\mathbf{k}\|
,
where

\displaystyle f^{\mathbf{k}}(x)=e^{-ikt+i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{x}}=e^{-ikt+ik_{z}z+i\mathbf{k}_{\perp}\cdot\mathbf{x}_{\perp}}\,.

(2)

The annihilation and creation operators,
\hat{a}_{\mathbf{k}}

and
\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k}}
, satisfy

\displaystyle[\hat{a}_{\mathbf{k}},\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}]=(2\pi)^{3}2k\,\delta^{(3)}(\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{k}^{\prime})\,,

(3)

with all other commutators among them vanishing. The Minkowski vacuum,

\ket{0_{\mathrm{M}}}
, is defined by

\displaystyle\hat{a}_{\mathbf{k}}\ket{0_{\mathrm{M}}}=0\,\ \textrm{for all}\ \mathbf{k}\,.

(4)

The Unruh modes, introduced in Unruh’s original paper
[
1
]
, can be characterized as the superpositions of
f^{\mathbf{k}}(x)
that are eigenfunctions of the boost
transformation in the
z
-direction.
To see this,
we first define the rapidity in the
z
-direction by

\displaystyle\vartheta

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2a}\ln\frac{k+k_{z}}{k-k_{z}}\,,

(5)

where
a\,(>0)
and
\vartheta
have the dimensions of acceleration and time,
respectively.
Then,

\displaystyle k

\displaystyle=k_{\perp}\cosh a\vartheta\,,

(6)

\displaystyle k_{z}

\displaystyle=k_{\perp}\sinh a\vartheta\,,

(7)

where

k_{\perp}=\|\mathbf{k}_{\perp}\|
.
The commutator (
3
) can be written as

\displaystyle[\hat{a}_{\mathbf{k}},\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}]=16\pi^{3}a^{-1}\delta(\vartheta-\vartheta^{\prime})\delta^{(2)}(\mathbf{k}_{\perp}-\mathbf{k}^{\prime}_{\perp})\,,

(8)

where
\vartheta^{\prime}
is the rapidity for
\mathbf{k}^{\prime}
, and the measure for the integration
over
\mathbf{k}
can be written as

\displaystyle\frac{d^{3}\mathbf{k}}{(2\pi)^{3}2k}=\frac{a}{16\pi^{3}}d\vartheta\,d^{2}\mathbf{k}_{\perp}\,.

(9)

Now, we define the Unruh modes by
[
38
,
39
]

\displaystyle u^{(\omega,\mathbf{k}_{\perp})}(x)

\displaystyle=a\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-i\omega\vartheta}f^{\mathbf{k}}(x)\,d\vartheta\,,

(10)

which can be inverted as

\displaystyle f^{\mathbf{k}}(x)=\frac{1}{2\pi a}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{i\omega\vartheta}u^{(\omega,\mathbf{k}_{\perp})}(x)\,d\omega\,.

(11)

(See Ref.
[
40
]
for a similar formula for the Unruh modes for the spinor field.)
Under the boost transformation parametrized by
\beta
,

\displaystyle(t,z)

\displaystyle\mapsto(t\cosh a\beta-z\sinh a\beta,-t\sinh a\beta+z\cosh a\beta)\,,

(12)

we have
f^{\mathbf{k}}(x)\mapsto f^{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}(x)
, where

\mathbf{k}^{\prime}_{\perp}=\mathbf{k}_{\perp}\quad{\rm and}\quad k^{\prime}_{z}=k_{\perp}\sinh[a(\vartheta+\beta)].

This transformation can be undone in the integral in Eq. (
10
)
by letting
\vartheta\mapsto\vartheta-\beta
. Thus, under the boost
transformation (
12
) the Unruh modes transform as

u^{(\omega,\mathbf{k}_{\perp})}(x)\mapsto e^{i\omega\beta}u^{(\omega,\mathbf{k}_{\perp})}(x)
.
That is, the Unurh modes are indeed eigenfunctions of the boost transformation
in the
z
-direction.

It is clear from Eqs. (
10
) and (
11
)
that the Unruh modes form a (generalized) basis for the space of positive-frequency
solutions to the Klein-Gordon equation. Hence, one can expand the quantum
field
\widehat{\phi}(x)
in terms of the Unruh modes:

\widehat{\phi}(x)=\int\frac{d^{2}\mathbf{k}_{\perp}}{16\pi^{3}a}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\omega\left[u^{(\omega,\mathbf{k}_{\perp})}(x)\,\hat{b}_{(\omega,\mathbf{k}_{\perp})}+\overline{u^{(\omega,\mathbf{k}_{\perp})}(x)}\,\hat{b}^{\dagger}_{(\omega,\mathbf{k}_{\perp})}\right].

(13)

Substituting Eq. (
11
) into Eq. (
1
)
with Eq. (
9
)
and identifying the coefficients of
u^{(\omega,\mathbf{k}_{\perp})}(x)
as

\hat{b}_{(\omega,\mathbf{k}_{\perp})}
in Eq. (
13
), we find

\displaystyle\hat{b}_{(\omega,\mathbf{k}_{\perp})}=\frac{a}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{i\omega\vartheta}\hat{a}_{\mathbf{k}}\,d\vartheta\,.

(14)

Then, we find from Eq. (
8
)

\displaystyle[\hat{b}_{(\omega,\mathbf{k}_{\perp})},\hat{b}^{\dagger}_{(\omega^{\prime},\mathbf{k}^{\prime}_{\perp})}]=8\pi^{2}a\,\delta(\omega-\omega^{\prime})\delta^{(2)}(\mathbf{k}_{\perp}-\mathbf{k}^{\prime}_{\perp})\,.

(15)

Since the operators
\hat{b}_{(\omega,\mathbf{k}_{\perp})}
are superpositions
of
\hat{a}_{\mathbf{k}}
, the Minkowski vacuum state is annihilated by them:

\displaystyle\hat{b}_{(\omega,\mathbf{k}_{\perp})}\ket{0_{\mathrm{M}}}=0\,\ \textrm{for all}\ \omega\ \textrm{and}\ \mathbf{k}_{\perp}\,.

(16)

III
The Unruh modes in Rindler coordinates

In this section we show that the
definition (
10
) of the
Unruh modes agrees with the standard definition as linear combinations of
the Rindler modes
[
41
]
. Then we review some aspects of the
Unruh effect. First we define the Rindler coordinates and Rindler wedges.
Right Rindler coordinates,
\tau
and
\xi
, are defined by the relations,

\displaystyle t

\displaystyle=a^{-1}e^{a\xi}\sinh a\tau\,,

(17)

\displaystyle z

\displaystyle=a^{-1}e^{a\xi}\cosh a\tau\,.

(18)

The constant
a
is the proper acceleration of the worldline defined by

\xi=0
and
\mathbf{x}_{\perp}=\mathrm{constant}
. These coordinates,
together with
\mathbf{x}_{\perp}
, cover the right Rindler wedge defined by

z>|t|
. Left Rindler coordinates,
\tilde{\tau}
and
\tilde{\xi}
,
are defined by

\displaystyle t

\displaystyle=a^{-1}e^{a\tilde{\xi}}\sinh a\tilde{\tau}\,,

(19)

\displaystyle z

\displaystyle=-a^{-1}e^{a\tilde{\xi}}\cosh a\tilde{\tau}\,.

(20)

These coordinates, together with
\mathbf{x}_{\perp}
, cover the left Rindler
wedge defined by
z<-|t|
.

The Unruh modes (
10
) can be expressed in Rindler coordinates given by
Eqs. (
17
)–(
20
),
using Eqs. (
6
) and (
7
), as

\displaystyle u^{(\omega,\mathbf{k}_{\perp})}(x)

\displaystyle=ae^{-i\omega\tau}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-i\omega s+i(k_{\perp}/a)\exp({a\xi})\sinh as+i\mathbf{k}_{\perp}\cdot\mathbf{x}_{\perp}}\,ds\,,

(21)

in the right Rindler wedge, and

\displaystyle u^{(\omega,\mathbf{k}_{\perp})}(x)

\displaystyle=ae^{i\omega\tilde{\tau}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-i\omega s-i(k_{\perp}/a)\exp({a\tilde{\xi}})\sinh as+i\mathbf{k}_{\perp}\cdot\mathbf{x}_{\perp}}\,ds\,,

(22)

in the left Rindler wedge. Using the formula
[
42
, Eq. 10.32.7]

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-i\omega s}e^{ix\sinh as}\,ds=\frac{2}{a}e^{\pi\omega/2a}K_{i\omega/a}(x)\ \textrm{for}\ x>0\,,

(23)

and its complex conjugate, where
K_{\nu}(x)
is the modified Bessel function
of the second kind, and recalling that
K_{\nu}(x)=K_{-\nu}(x)
, we find

\displaystyle u^{(\omega,\mathbf{k}_{\perp})}(x)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}2e^{\pi\omega/2a}K_{i\omega/a}\left(\frac{k_{\perp}}{a}e^{a\xi}\right)e^{-i\omega\tau+i\mathbf{k}_{\perp}\cdot\mathbf{x}_{\perp}}\,,&\textrm{in the right Rindler wedge},\\
2e^{-\pi\omega/2a}K_{i\omega/a}\left(\frac{k_{\perp}}{a}e^{a\tilde{\xi}}\right)e^{i\omega\tilde{\tau}+i\mathbf{k}_{\perp}\cdot\mathbf{x}_{\perp}}\,,&\textrm{in the left Rindler wedge}.\end{cases}

(24)

This equation is valid for all real values of
\omega
.
Now, we define the right and left Rindler modes,

v^{(\mathrm{R}:\omega,\mathbf{k}_{\perp})}(x)
and

v^{(\mathrm{L}:\omega,\mathbf{k}_{\perp})}(x)
, respectively, for
\omega>0
by

\displaystyle v^{(\mathrm{R}:\omega,\mathbf{k}_{\perp})}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{u^{(\omega,\mathbf{k}_{\perp})}(x)-e^{-\pi\omega/a}\overline{u^{(-\omega,-\mathbf{k}_{\perp})}(x)}}{\sqrt{1-e^{-2\pi\omega/a}}}\,,

(25)

\displaystyle v^{(\mathrm{L}:\omega,\mathbf{k}_{\perp})}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{u^{(-\omega,\mathbf{k}_{\perp})}(x)-e^{-\pi\omega/a}\overline{u^{(\omega,-\mathbf{k}_{\perp})}(x)}}{\sqrt{1-e^{-2\pi\omega/a}}}\,.

(26)

Then,

\displaystyle v^{(\mathrm{R}:\omega,\mathbf{k}_{\perp})}(x)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\sqrt{8\sinh(\pi\omega/a)}K_{i\omega/a}\left(\frac{k_{\perp}}{a}e^{a\xi}\right)e^{-i\omega\tau+i\mathbf{k}_{\perp}\cdot\mathbf{x}_{\perp}}\,,&\textrm{in the right Rindler wedge},\\
0\,,&\textrm{in the left Rindler wedge},\end{cases}

(27)

\displaystyle v^{(\mathrm{L}:\omega,\mathbf{k}_{\perp})}(x)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}0\,,&\textrm{in the right Rindler wedge},\\
\sqrt{8\sinh(\pi\omega/a)}K_{i\omega/a}\left(\frac{k_{\perp}}{a}e^{a\tilde{\xi}}\right)e^{-i\omega\tilde{\tau}+i\mathbf{k}_{\perp}\cdot\mathbf{x}_{\perp}}\,,&\textrm{in the left Rindler wedge}.\end{cases}

(28)

The right Rindler modes are positive-frequency with respect to the Killing
vector field
\partial/\partial\tau
, whereas the left Rindler modes are positive-frequency
with respect to the Killing vector field
\partial/\partial\tilde{\tau}
.
The Unruh modes can be expressed in terms of the Rindler modes by inverting
the relations (
25
) and (
26
) for
\omega>0

as

\displaystyle u^{(\omega,\mathbf{k}_{\perp})}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{v^{(\mathrm{R}:\omega,\mathbf{k}_{\perp})}(x)+e^{-\pi\omega/a}\overline{v^{(\mathrm{L}:\omega,-\mathbf{k}_{\perp})}(x)}}{\sqrt{1-e^{-2\pi\omega/a}}}\,,

(29)

\displaystyle u^{(-\omega,\mathbf{k}_{\perp})}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{v^{(\mathrm{L}:\omega,\mathbf{k}_{\perp})}(x)+e^{-\pi\omega/a}\overline{v^{(\mathrm{R}:\omega,-\mathbf{k}_{\perp})}(x)}}{\sqrt{1-e^{-2\pi\omega/a}}}\,.

(30)

The scalar field
\widehat{\phi}(x)
can be written as

\displaystyle\widehat{\phi}(x)=\widehat{\phi}_{\mathrm{R}}(x)+\widehat{\phi}_{\mathrm{L}}(x)\,,

(31)

where

\widehat{\phi}_{\mathrm{R}}(x)=\int\frac{d^{2}\mathbf{k}_{\perp}}{16\pi^{3}a}\int_{0}^{\infty}d\omega\left[v^{(\mathrm{R}:\omega,\mathbf{k}_{\perp})}(x)\hat{b}_{(\mathrm{R}:\omega,\mathbf{k}_{\perp})}+\overline{v^{(\mathrm{R}:\omega,\mathbf{k}_{\perp})}(x)}\hat{b}^{\dagger}_{(\mathrm{R}:\omega,\mathbf{k}_{\perp})}\right]\,,

(32)

and similarly for
\widehat{\phi}_{\mathrm{L}}(x)
. By substituting
Eqs. (
29
) and (
30
) into
Eq. (
13
) and comparing the resulting
expression with Eq. (
32
), the annihilation
operators
\hat{b}_{(\mathrm{R}:\omega,\mathbf{k}_{\perp})}
and

\hat{b}_{(\mathrm{L}:\omega,\mathbf{k}_{\perp})}
can be expressed as

\displaystyle\hat{b}_{(\mathrm{R}:\omega,\mathbf{k}_{\perp})}

\displaystyle=\frac{\hat{b}_{(\omega,\mathbf{k}_{\perp})}-e^{-\pi\omega/a}\hat{b}^{\dagger}_{(-\omega,-\mathbf{k}_{\perp})}}{\sqrt{1-e^{-2\pi\omega/a}}}\,,

(33)

\displaystyle\hat{b}_{(\mathrm{L}:\omega,\mathbf{k}_{\perp})}

\displaystyle=\frac{\hat{b}_{(-\omega,\mathbf{k}_{\perp})}-e^{-\pi\omega/a}\hat{b}^{\dagger}_{(\omega,-\mathbf{k}_{\perp})}}{\sqrt{1-e^{-2\pi\omega/a}}}\,.

(34)

All right Rindler operators,
\{\hat{b}_{(\mathrm{R}:\omega,\mathbf{k}_{\perp})},\hat{b}_{(\mathrm{R}:\omega,\mathbf{k}_{\perp})}^{\dagger}\}
,
commute with all left Rindler operators,

\{\hat{b}_{(\mathrm{L}:\omega,\mathbf{k}_{\perp})},\hat{b}_{(\mathrm{L}:\omega,\mathbf{k}_{\perp})}^{\dagger}\}
,
and these operators satisfy

\displaystyle[\hat{b}_{(\mathrm{R}:|\omega|,\mathbf{k}_{\perp})},\hat{b}^{\dagger}_{(\mathrm{R}:|\omega^{\prime}|,\mathbf{k}^{\prime}_{\perp})}]

\displaystyle=[\hat{b}_{(\mathrm{L}:|\omega|,\mathbf{k}_{\perp})},\hat{b}^{\dagger}_{(\mathrm{L}:|\omega^{\prime}|,\mathbf{k}^{\prime}_{\perp})}]

\displaystyle=8\pi^{2}a\,\delta(|\omega|-|\omega^{\prime}|)\delta^{(2)}(\mathbf{k}_{\perp}-\mathbf{k}^{\prime}_{\perp})\,.

(35)

The Fulling vacuum,
\ket{0_{\mathrm{F}}}
, is defined by

\displaystyle\hat{b}_{(\mathrm{R}:\omega,\mathbf{k}_{\perp})}\ket{0_{\mathrm{F}}}=\hat{b}_{(\mathrm{L}:\omega,\mathbf{k}_{\perp})}\ket{0_{\mathrm{F}}}=0\,.

(36)

Thus, there are no “Rindler particles”, i.e. particles created by the operators
\hat{b}_{(\mathrm{R}:\omega,\mathbf{k}_{\perp})}^{\dagger}

or
\hat{b}_{(\mathrm{L}:\omega,\mathbf{k}_{\perp})}^{\dagger}
, in this state.
Then, we find, from Eq. (
33
),

\displaystyle\bra{0_{\mathrm{M}}}\hat{b}^{\dagger}_{(\mathrm{R}:\omega,\mathbf{k}_{\perp})}\hat{b}_{(\mathrm{R}:\omega^{\prime},\mathbf{k}_{\perp}^{\prime})}\ket{0_{\mathrm{M}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{8\pi^{2}a}{e^{2\pi\omega/a}-1}

\displaystyle\times\delta(\omega-\omega^{\prime})\delta^{(2)}(\mathbf{k}_{\perp}-\mathbf{k}_{\perp}^{\prime})\,.

Now, let us define the operator
\hat{A}
by

\displaystyle\hat{A}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{8\pi^{2}a}}\int_{0}^{\infty}d\omega\int d^{2}\mathbf{k}_{\perp}F(\omega,\mathbf{k}_{\perp})\hat{b}_{(\mathrm{R}:\omega,\mathbf{k}_{\perp})}\,.

(38)

where
F(\omega,\mathbf{k}_{\perp})
is a continuous and
square-integrable function. Then

\displaystyle[\hat{A},\hat{A}^{\dagger}]=\int_{0}^{\infty}d\omega\int d^{2}\mathbf{k}_{\perp}\,|F(\omega,\mathbf{k}_{\perp})|^{2}\,,

(39)

and

\displaystyle\bra{0_{\mathrm{M}}}\hat{A}^{\dagger}\hat{A}\ket{0_{\mathrm{M}}}=\int_{0}^{\infty}d\omega\int d^{2}\mathbf{k}_{\perp}\,\frac{|F(\omega,\mathbf{k}_{\perp})|^{2}}{e^{2\pi\omega/a}-1}\,.

(40)

Thus, the expected number of the “Rindler particles” with given Rindler energy
(i.e. the energy associated with the boost Killing vector field

\partial/\partial\tau
) is given by the Bose-Einstein distribution
function with temperature
a/2\pi
.
This is a manifestation of the well-known Unruh effect:
the Minkowski vacuum,
\ket{0_{\mathrm{M}}}
,
is a thermal state with temperature
a/2\pi

with respect to the energy corresponding to the boost Killing
vector field,
\partial/\partial\tau
, i.e. the Rindler energy, in the right Rindler wedge.

IV
Radiation by a classical source through the Unruh effect

In the Heisenberg picture, analysis of radiation from a classical source
does not depend on the quantum state because it reduces to solving the
classical field equation (see Appendix
A
). Thus, the radiation can be discussed in the
purely classical context, as is well known. However, perhaps surprisingly,
to analyze such a process of radiation in the interaction picture in the
Rindler frame, it is crucial to take the Unruh effect into account as
the present authors have emphasized in the context of the uniformly
accelerated source. In this section we explain this fact for
a general classical source
1
1

1

In fact, traces of the Unruh effect
can be found in the classical field by decomposing it in terms of the
Rindler modes
[
43
]
.
.

The classical source is introduced by adding the following term in the
Lagrangian density:

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\textrm{int}}(x)=j(x)\widehat{\phi}(x)\,,

(41)

with the corresponding interaction action,

\displaystyle\widehat{S}_{\textrm{int}}=\int\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\textrm{int}}(x)\,d^{4}x\,.

(42)

Then, in the interaction picture, this interaction adds to the final
state the following one-particle part:

\displaystyle\ket{1\mathrm{p}}

\displaystyle=i\int j(x)\widehat{\phi}(x)\ket{0_{\mathrm{M}}}\,d^{4}x

(43)

\displaystyle=i\int\frac{d^{3}\mathbf{k}}{(2\pi)^{3}2k}\tilde{\jmath}(k)\hat{a}_{\mathbf{k}}^{\dagger}\ket{0_{\mathrm{M}}}\,,

(44)

where
\tilde{\jmath}(k)

is the
4
-dimensional Fourier transform of
j(x)
:

\displaystyle\tilde{\jmath}(k)=\int j(x)e^{ikt-i{\bf k}\cdot{\bf x}}\,d^{4}x\,.

(45)

The final state to first order is

\displaystyle\ket{f}

\displaystyle=\left(1+i\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{for}}\right)\ket{0_{\mathrm{M}}}+\ket{1\mathrm{p}}\,,

(46)

where the forward-scattering amplitude
\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{for}}
satisfies

\displaystyle 2\,\mathrm{Im}\,\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{for}}=\braket{1\mathrm{p}|1\mathrm{p}}\,,

(47)

so that
\braket{f|f}=1
to first order.

The emission probability can be found as an integral over
\mathbf{k}
:

\displaystyle P_{\mathrm{em}}^{(\mathrm{M})}

\displaystyle=\braket{1\mathrm{p}|1\mathrm{p}}

\displaystyle=\int\frac{d^{3}\mathbf{k}}{(2\pi)^{3}2k}|\tilde{\jmath}(k)|^{2}\,.

(48)

The Minkowski particle number operator is defined as

\displaystyle\widehat{N}

\displaystyle=\int\frac{d^{3}\mathbf{k}}{(2\pi)^{3}2k}\hat{a}_{\mathbf{k}}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{\mathbf{k}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{8\pi^{2}a}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\omega\int d^{2}\mathbf{k}_{\perp}\hat{b}^{\dagger}_{(\omega,\mathbf{k}_{\perp})}\hat{b}_{(\omega,\mathbf{k}_{\perp})}\,.

(49)

The first expression for
\widehat{N}
can be used to show that

\bra{f}\widehat{N}\ket{f}=\braket{1\mathrm{p}|1\mathrm{p}}=P_{\mathrm{em}}^{(\mathrm{M})}
. That is,
the one-particle emission probability is equal to the expected Minkowski-particle number in the final state at first order in perturbation theory.
Equation (
49
) can be regarded as a classical
result in the following sense: If we Fourier-transform the energy density
of the emitted wave, divide each
\mathbf{k}
-component by
k
and integrate
the result over
\mathbf{k}
, then we obtain this expression
[
43
]
.

Now, if the classical source
j(x)
has support only in the right
Rindler wedge, then the same process can be described as follows.
The field
\widehat{\phi}(x)
restricted to the right Rindler wedge is the field

\widehat{\phi}_{\mathrm{R}}(x)
given by Eq. (
32
).
Recall that the Minkowski vacuum is seen as a thermal bath of
temperature
a/2\pi
with respect to the energy associated with the
Killing vector field
\partial/\partial\tau
. We define the
emission and absorption amplitudes by

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}^{(\omega,\mathbf{k}_{\perp})}_{\mathrm{em}}

\displaystyle=\bra{0_{\mathrm{F}}}\hat{b}_{(\mathrm{R}:\omega,\mathbf{k}_{\perp})}\widehat{S}_{\textrm{int}}\ket{0_{\mathrm{F}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int j(x)\overline{v^{(\mathrm{R}:\omega,\mathbf{k}_{\perp})}(x)}\sqrt{-g}\,d^{4}x\,,

(50)

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}^{(\omega,\mathbf{k}_{\perp})}_{\mathrm{abs}}

\displaystyle=\bra{0_{\mathrm{F}}}\widehat{S}_{\textrm{int}}\hat{b}_{(\mathrm{R}:\omega,\mathbf{k}_{\perp})}^{\dagger}\ket{0_{\mathrm{F}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int j(x)v^{(\mathrm{R}:\omega,\mathbf{k}_{\perp})}(x)\sqrt{-g}\,d^{4}x\,,

(51)

respectively, where
g
is the determinant of the metric
g_{\mu\nu}
in Minkowski
(and Rindler) spacetime. If the initial state was the Fulling vacuum, then the
emission probability would be found using the expansion (
32
)
of the field
\widehat{\phi}_{\mathrm{R}}
and the commutation relation (
15
),
with
\hat{b}_{(\omega,\mathbf{k}_{\perp})}
replaced by
\hat{b}_{(R:\omega,\mathbf{k}_{\perp})}
:

\displaystyle P^{(\mathrm{R},0)}_{\textrm{em}}

\displaystyle=\left\|\widehat{S}_{\textrm{int}}\ket{0_{\mathrm{F}}}\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{8\pi^{2}a}\int_{0}^{\infty}d\omega\int d^{2}\mathbf{k}_{\perp}\left|\mathcal{A}^{(\omega,\mathbf{k}_{\perp})}_{\mathrm{em}}\right|^{2}\,.

(52)

Since the Minkowski vacuum is the thermal state of temperature
a/2\pi
with respect to the
Rindler energy, the interaction probability for the Minkowski vacuum is

\displaystyle P_{\textrm{int}}^{(\mathrm{R})}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{8\pi^{2}a}\int_{0}^{\infty}d\omega\int d^{2}\mathbf{k}_{\perp}\left(\frac{\left|\mathcal{A}^{(\omega,\mathbf{k}_{\perp})}_{\mathrm{em}}\right|^{2}}{1-e^{-2\pi\omega/a}}+\frac{\left|\mathcal{A}^{(\omega,\mathbf{k}_{\perp})}_{\mathrm{abs}}\right|^{2}}{e^{2\pi\omega/a}-1}\right)\,.

(53)

The first and second terms in the integrand above represent the
(spontaneous and induced) emission
[with
(1-e^{-2\pi\omega/a})^{-1}=1+(e^{2\pi\omega/a}-1)^{-1}
]
and absorption, respectively.

Now, as Unruh and Wald have shown
[
36
]
, both the
emission and absorption of a particle in the thermal bath of temperature

a/2\pi
in the Rindler wedge are seen as emission of a particle in Minkowski spacetime.
Hence, we expect that

P^{(\mathrm{R})}_{\mathrm{int}}=P^{(\mathrm{M})}_{\mathrm{em}}
.
To demonstrate this equality, we use the expansion of

\widehat{\phi}(x)
in terms of the Unruh modes, i.e. Eq. (
13
), in Eq. (
43
).
Thus, we find

\displaystyle\ket{1\mathrm{p}}

\displaystyle=\frac{i}{16\pi^{3}a}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\omega\int d^{2}\mathbf{k}_{\perp}\,\tilde{\jmath}^{\;(\mathrm{R})}(\omega,\mathbf{k}_{\perp})\hat{b}_{(\omega,\mathbf{k}_{\perp})}^{\dagger}\ket{0_{\mathrm{M}}}\,,

(54)

where

\displaystyle\tilde{\jmath}^{\;(\mathrm{R})}(\omega,\mathbf{k}_{\perp})

\displaystyle=\int j(x)\overline{u^{(\omega,\mathbf{k}_{\perp})}(x)}\sqrt{-g}\,d^{4}x\,.

(55)

Since the classical source
j(x)
has support only in the right Rindler
wedge by assumption, we have from Eqs. (
29
)
and (
30
),

\displaystyle\tilde{\jmath}^{\;(\mathrm{R})}(\omega,\mathbf{k}_{\perp})

\displaystyle=\frac{2\pi}{\sqrt{1-e^{-2\pi\omega/a}}}\mathcal{A}^{(\omega,\mathbf{k}_{\perp})}_{\mathrm{em}}\,,

(56)

\displaystyle\tilde{\jmath}^{\;(\mathrm{R})}(-\omega,\mathbf{k}_{\perp})

\displaystyle=\frac{2\pi}{\sqrt{e^{2\pi\omega/a}-1}}\mathcal{A}^{(\omega,-\mathbf{k}_{\perp})}_{\mathrm{abs}}\,,

(57)

with
\omega>0
.
Thus, we find

\displaystyle\ket{1\mathrm{p}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{i}{8\pi^{2}a}\int_{0}^{\infty}d\omega\int d^{2}\mathbf{k}_{\perp}\left[\frac{\mathcal{A}^{(\omega,\mathbf{k}_{\perp})}_{\mathrm{em}}}{\sqrt{1-e^{-2\pi\omega}}}\hat{b}_{(\omega,\mathbf{k}_{\perp})}^{\dagger}\right.

(58)

\displaystyle+\left.\frac{\mathcal{A}^{(\omega,-\mathbf{k}_{\perp})}_{\mathrm{abs}}}{\sqrt{e^{2\pi\omega}-1}}\hat{b}_{(-\omega,\mathbf{k}_{\perp})}^{\dagger}\right]\ket{0_{\mathrm{M}}}\,.

Then, we have
\braket{1\mathrm{p}|1\mathrm{p}}=P^{(\mathrm{R})}_{\mathrm{int}}

by Eq. (
15
), where
P^{(\mathrm{R})}_{\mathrm{int}}
is
given by Eq. (
53
). Thus, we indeed find

P_{\mathrm{int}}^{(\mathrm{R})}=P^{(\mathrm{M})}_{\mathrm{em}}

given by Eq. (
48
).

The electromagnetic and gravitational cases

In this section we briefly discuss the electromagnetic and gravitational
fields coupled to a classical source and show that the results for the
massless scalar field presented in the previous sections hold for these
fields as well. This will be the generalization of some results for the
uniformly accelerated sources coupled to the electromagnetic
field
[
33
]
and gravitational field
[
35
]

(see also Ref.
[
34
]
).

V.1
The electromagnetic case

The Lagrangian density with the interaction term between the quantum
field and a classical current is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{int}}=\sqrt{-g}\left[-\frac{1}{4}\widehat{F}_{\mu\nu}\widehat{F}^{\mu\nu}-j^{\mu}(x)\widehat{A}_{\mu}(x)\right]\,,

(59)

where
\widehat{F}_{\mu\nu}=\nabla_{\mu}\widehat{A}_{\nu}-\nabla_{\nu}\widehat{A}_{\mu}
.
We define the anti-symmetric tensors
\epsilon^{(\parallel)}_{\mu\nu}

and
\epsilon^{(\perp)}_{\mu\nu}
by

\displaystyle\epsilon^{(\parallel)}_{tz}=-\epsilon^{(\parallel)}_{zt}=1\,,

(60)

\displaystyle\epsilon^{(\perp)}_{xy}=-\epsilon^{(\perp)}_{yx}=1\,,

(61)

with all other components vanishing. In Rindler coordinates we have

\displaystyle\epsilon^{(\parallel)}_{\tau\xi}=-\epsilon^{(\parallel)}_{\xi\tau}=e^{2a\xi}\,,

(62)

with all other components vanishing.

Two physical modes with momentum
\mathbf{k}
can be chosen as

\displaystyle A_{\mu}^{(\mathrm{I}:\mathbf{k})}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k_{\perp}}\epsilon^{(\parallel)}_{\mu\nu}\nabla^{\nu}f^{\mathbf{k}}(x)\,,

(63)

\displaystyle A_{\mu}^{(\mathrm{I\!I}:\mathbf{k})}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k_{\perp}}\epsilon^{(\perp)}_{\mu\nu}\nabla^{\nu}f^{\mathbf{k}}(x)\,.

(64)

These modes are transverse,
\nabla^{\mu}A_{\mu}^{(\mathrm{P}:\mathbf{k})}=0
, and satisfy the orthonormality condition
for the transverse modes,

\displaystyle-i\int_{\Sigma}\overline{A^{(\mathrm{P}^{\prime}:\mathbf{k}^{\prime})\mu}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\leftrightarrow}}{{\nabla}}_{\nu}A^{(\mathrm{P}:\mathbf{k})}_{\mu}\,d\Sigma^{\nu}=(2\pi)^{3}2k\delta^{\mathrm{P^{\prime}P}}\delta^{(3)}(\mathbf{k}^{\prime}-\mathbf{k})\,,

(65)

where
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\leftrightarrow}}{{\nabla}}_{\nu}=\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\leftarrow}}{{\nabla}}_{\nu}-\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rightarrow}}{{\nabla}}_{\nu}
and
where
\Sigma
is a
t
=constant Cauchy surface.
Then, the quantum electromagnetic field
\widehat{A}_{\mu}(x)
with
complete gauge fixing is

\displaystyle\widehat{A}_{\mu}(x)=\int\frac{d^{3}\mathbf{k}}{(2\pi)^{3}2k}\sum_{\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{I},\mathrm{I\!I}}\left[A_{\mu}^{(\mathrm{P}:\mathbf{k})}(x)\,\hat{a}_{(\mathrm{P}:\mathbf{k})}+\overline{A_{\mu}^{(\mathrm{P}:\mathbf{k})}(x)}\,\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{(\mathrm{P}:\mathbf{k})}\right]\,,

(66)

where the operators
\hat{a}_{(\mathrm{P}:\mathbf{k})}

and
\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{(\mathrm{P}:\mathbf{k})}
,

\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{I},\mathrm{I\!I}
, satisfy

\displaystyle\left[\hat{a}_{(\mathrm{P}:\mathbf{k})},\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{(\mathrm{P}^{\prime}:\mathbf{k}^{\prime})}\right]=(2\pi)^{3}\,2k\,\delta_{\mathrm{PP^{\prime}}}\,\delta^{(3)}(\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{k}^{\prime})\,,

(67)

with all other commutators among them vanishing, which is a consequence
of the orthonormality condition (
65
). The Minkowski vacuum
state
\ket{0_{\mathrm{M}}}
is annihilated by

\hat{a}_{(\mathrm{P}:\mathbf{k})}
, i.e.
\hat{a}_{(\mathrm{P}:\mathbf{k})}\ket{0_{\mathrm{M}}}=0

for all
\mathrm{P}
and
\mathbf{k}
.

The one-photon state which the classical current
j^{\mu}(x)
generates is

\displaystyle\ket{1\mathrm{p}}

\displaystyle=-i\int j^{\mu}(x)\widehat{A}_{\mu}(x)\ket{0_{\mathrm{M}}}d^{4}x

\displaystyle=-\int\frac{d^{3}\mathbf{k}}{(2\pi)^{2}2k}\tilde{\jmath}^{\,\mu}(k)k_{\perp}^{-1}\left[\epsilon^{(\parallel)}_{\mu\nu}k^{\nu}\hat{a}_{(\mathrm{I}:\mathbf{k})}^{\dagger}+\epsilon^{(\perp)}_{\mu\nu}k^{\nu}\hat{a}_{(\mathrm{I\!I}:\mathbf{k})}^{\dagger}\right]\ket{0_{\mathrm{M}}}\,,

(68)

where
\tilde{\jmath}^{\,\mu}(k)
is the Fourier transform of

j^{\,\mu}(x)
defined in the same way as
\tilde{\jmath}(k)
is defined
from
j(x)
in Eq. (
45
).
Then, the emission probability is

\displaystyle P_{\mathrm{em}}^{(\mathrm{M})}

\displaystyle=\braket{1\mathrm{p}|1\mathrm{p}}

\displaystyle=\int\frac{d^{3}\mathbf{k}}{(2\pi)^{3}2k}\overline{\tilde{\jmath}^{\,\mu}(k)}k_{\perp}^{-2}\left[\epsilon_{\mu\alpha}^{(\parallel)}k^{\alpha}\epsilon_{\nu\beta}^{(\parallel)}k^{\beta}+\epsilon_{\mu\alpha}^{(\perp)}k^{\alpha}\epsilon_{\nu\beta}^{(\perp)}k^{\beta}\right]\tilde{\jmath}^{\,\nu}(k)\,.

(69)

One can verify the following formula (for
k_{\perp}\neq 0
), e.g., in the
Lorentz frame with
k^{t}=k^{x}=k_{\perp}
and
k^{z}=k^{y}=0
:

\displaystyle\epsilon_{\mu\alpha}^{(\parallel)}k^{\alpha}\epsilon_{\nu\beta}^{(\parallel)}k^{\beta}+\epsilon_{\mu\alpha}^{(\perp)}k^{\alpha}\epsilon_{\nu\beta}^{(\perp)}k^{\beta}

\displaystyle=-k_{\perp}^{2}g_{\mu\nu}+\frac{1}{2}(k_{\mu}\check{k}_{\nu}+k_{\nu}\check{k}_{\mu})\,,

(70)

where the vector
\check{k}^{\mu}
is obtained by multiplying the

x
- and
y
-components of
k^{\mu}
by
-1
.
Since
k_{\mu}\tilde{\jmath}^{\,\mu}(k)=0
, we find

\displaystyle\braket{1\mathrm{p}|1\mathrm{p}}

\displaystyle=-\int\frac{d^{3}\mathbf{k}}{(2\pi)^{3}2k}\overline{\tilde{\jmath}^{\,\mu}(k)}\tilde{\jmath}_{\mu}(k)\,,

(71)

as is well known.

We define the Rindler modes by

\displaystyle A_{\mu}^{(\mathrm{RI}:\omega,\mathbf{k}_{\perp})}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k_{\perp}}\epsilon^{(\parallel)}_{\mu\lambda}\nabla^{\lambda}v^{(\mathrm{R}:\omega,\mathbf{k}_{\perp})}(x)\,,

(72)

\displaystyle A_{\mu}^{(\mathrm{RI\!I}:\omega,\mathbf{k}_{\perp})}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k_{\perp}}\epsilon^{(\perp)}_{\mu\lambda}\nabla^{\lambda}v^{(\mathrm{R}:\omega,\mathbf{k}_{\perp})}(x)\,.

(73)

Since the differential operators
\epsilon^{(\parallel)}_{\mu\lambda}\nabla^{\lambda}

and
\epsilon^{(\perp)}_{\mu\lambda}\nabla^{\lambda}
commute with the operations
for defining the Unruh and Rindler modes from the modes
f^{\mathbf{k}}(x)
,
the relationship between the right Rindler modes

A_{\mu}^{(\mathrm{RP}:\omega,\mathbf{k}_{\perp})}(x)
,

\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{I},\mathrm{I\!I}
,
and the Minkowski modes
A_{\mu}^{(\mathrm{P}:\mathbf{k})}(x)
is
exactly the same as that between
v^{(\mathrm{R}:\omega,\mathbf{k}_{\perp})}(x)

and
f^{\mathbf{k}}(x)
. Hence, the quantum field
\widehat{A}_{\mu}(x)

in the right Rindler wedge can be expanded in the same way as in the
scalar case (
32
):

\displaystyle\widehat{A}_{\mathrm{R}\,\mu}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\frac{d^{2}\mathbf{k}_{\perp}}{16\pi^{3}a}\int_{0}^{\infty}d\omega\sum_{\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{I},\mathrm{I\!I}}\left[A_{\mu}^{(\mathrm{RP}:\omega,\mathbf{k}_{\perp})}(x)\,\hat{b}_{(\mathrm{RP}:\omega,\mathbf{k}_{\perp})}\right.

(74)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\left.\overline{A_{\mu}^{(\mathrm{RP}:\omega,\mathbf{k}_{\perp})}(x)}\,\hat{b}^{\dagger}_{(\mathrm{RP}:\omega,\mathbf{k}_{\perp})}\right]\,,

where the operators
\hat{b}_{(\mathrm{RP}:\omega,\mathbf{k}_{\perp})}

and
\hat{b}^{\dagger}_{(\mathrm{RP}:\omega,\mathbf{k}_{\perp})}

satisfy the commutation relations

\displaystyle\left[\hat{b}_{(\mathrm{RP}:\omega,\mathbf{k}_{\perp})},\hat{b}^{\dagger}_{(\mathrm{RP^{\prime}}:\omega^{\prime},\mathbf{k}_{\perp}^{\prime})}\right]=8\pi^{2}a\,\delta_{\mathrm{PP^{\prime}}}\delta(\omega-\omega^{\prime})\delta^{(2)}(\mathbf{k}_{\perp}-\mathbf{k}_{\perp}^{\prime})\,,

(75)

with all other commutators among them vanishing. Then, exactly as
in the scalar case, we find

\displaystyle\braket{1\mathrm{p}|1\mathrm{p}}

\displaystyle=\int\frac{d^{2}\mathbf{k}_{\perp}}{8\pi^{2}a}\int_{0}^{\infty}d\omega\sum_{\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{I},\mathrm{I\!I}}\left(\frac{\left|\mathcal{A}^{(\mathrm{P}:\omega,\mathbf{k}_{\perp})}_{\mathrm{em}}\right|^{2}}{1-e^{-2\pi\omega/a}}+\frac{\left|\mathcal{A}^{(\mathrm{P}:\omega,\mathbf{k}_{\perp})}_{\mathrm{abs}}\right|^{2}}{e^{2\pi\omega/a}-1}\right)\,,

(76)

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}^{(\mathrm{P}:\omega,\mathbf{k}_{\perp})}_{\mathrm{em}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi}\int j^{\mu}(x)\overline{A_{\mu}^{(\mathrm{RP}:\omega,\mathbf{k}_{\perp})}(x)}\sqrt{-g}\,d^{4}x\,,

(77)

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}^{(\mathrm{P}:\omega,\mathbf{k}_{\perp})}_{\mathrm{abs}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi}\int j^{\mu}(x)A^{(\mathrm{RP}:\omega,\mathbf{k}_{\perp})}_{\mu}(x)\sqrt{-g}\,d^{4}x\,,

(78)

with the same interpretation of Eq. (
76
) in terms of
the Unruh effect as in the scalar case.

V.2
The gravitational case

The Lagrangian density
\mathcal{L}_{\textrm{EH}}^{(2)}
for the linearized
gravitational field coupled to a classical stress-energy tensor is

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{-g}}\mathcal{L}_{\textrm{EH}}^{(2)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\nabla_{\alpha}h_{\mu\nu}\nabla^{\alpha}h^{\mu\nu}-\nabla_{\alpha}h_{\beta\mu}\nabla^{\beta}h^{\alpha\mu}

(79)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\left(\nabla_{\alpha}h^{\mu\alpha}-\frac{1}{2}\nabla^{\mu}h\right)\nabla_{\mu}h+\kappa T^{\mu\nu}h_{\mu\nu},

where
h=h^{\alpha}{}_{\alpha}
and
\kappa=\sqrt{8\pi G}
. Two physical modes
with momentum
\mathbf{k}
can be chosen as

h^{(\mathrm{I}:\mathbf{k})}_{\mu\nu}(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left[g_{\mu\nu}+2q_{\mu\nu}(\mathbf{k}_{\bot})\right]f^{\mathbf{k}}(x)\,,

(80)

where

q_{\mu\nu}(\mathbf{k}_{\bot})=\begin{cases}-g_{\mu\nu}-{k_{\mu}k_{\nu}}/{k_{\perp}^{2}}\,,&\textrm{if}\ \mu,\nu=x\ \textrm{or}\ y,\\
0\,,&\textrm{otherwise}\,,\end{cases}

(81)

and

\displaystyle h^{(\mathrm{I\!I}:\mathbf{k})}_{\mu\nu}(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}k_{\perp}^{2}}\left[\epsilon^{(\parallel)}_{\mu\alpha}\epsilon^{(\perp)}_{\nu\beta}+\epsilon^{(\parallel)}_{\nu\alpha}\epsilon^{(\perp)}_{\mu\beta}\right]\nabla^{\alpha}\nabla^{\beta}f^{\mathbf{k}}(x)\,.

(82)

These modes satisfy the de Donder condition,

\displaystyle\nabla^{\mu}h_{\mu\nu}^{(\mathrm{P}:\mathbf{k})}-\frac{1}{2}\nabla_{\nu}h^{(\mathrm{P}:\mathbf{k})}=0\,,

(83)

and the normalization condition for the modes satisfying Eq. (
83
),

\displaystyle i\int\left[\overline{h^{(\mathrm{P}^{\prime}:\mathbf{k}^{\prime})\mu\nu}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\leftrightarrow}}{{\nabla}}_{\alpha}h^{(\mathrm{P}:\mathbf{k})}_{\mu\nu}-\frac{1}{2}\overline{h^{(\mathrm{P}^{\prime}:\mathbf{k}^{\prime})}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\leftrightarrow}}{{\nabla}}_{\alpha}h^{(\mathrm{P}:\mathbf{k})}\right]d\Sigma^{\alpha}

\displaystyle=(2\pi)^{3}2k\,\delta^{\mathrm{P^{\prime}P}}\delta^{(3)}(\mathbf{k}^{\prime}-\mathbf{k})\,.

(84)

Then, the emission probability from the classical stress-energy tensor

T^{\mu\nu}(x)
is

\displaystyle\braket{1\mathrm{p}|1\mathrm{p}}

\displaystyle=\int\frac{d^{3}\mathbf{k}}{(2\pi)^{3}2k}\sum_{\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{I},\mathrm{I\!I}}\left|\mathcal{A}^{(\mathrm{P}:\mathbf{k})}\right|^{2}\,,

(85)

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}^{(\mathrm{P}:\mathbf{k})}=\kappa\int T^{\mu\nu}(x)\overline{h^{(\mathrm{P}:\mathbf{k})}_{\mu\nu}(x)}\sqrt{-g}\,d^{4}x\,.

(86)

Define the Fourier transform of
T^{\mu\nu}(x)
by

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}^{\mu\nu}(k)=\int T^{\mu\nu}(x)e^{ik\cdot x}d^{4}x\,.

(87)

(Note that
\sqrt{-g}=1
here.)
Then,

\displaystyle\braket{1\mathrm{p}|1\mathrm{p}}=\kappa^{2}\int\frac{d^{3}\mathbf{k}}{(2\pi)^{3}2k}\overline{\mathcal{T}^{\mu\nu}(k)}S_{\mu\nu\lambda\sigma}(k)\mathcal{T}^{\lambda\sigma}(k)\,,

(88)

where

\displaystyle S_{\mu\nu\lambda\sigma}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}(g_{\mu\nu}+2q_{\mu\nu})(g_{\lambda\sigma}+2q_{\lambda\sigma})

(89)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\frac{2}{k_{\perp}^{4}}\epsilon^{(\parallel)}_{\mu\alpha}k^{\alpha}\epsilon^{(\perp)}_{\nu\beta}k^{\beta}\epsilon^{(\parallel)}_{\lambda\gamma}k^{\gamma}\epsilon^{(\perp)}_{\sigma\delta}k^{\delta}\,.

By working in the Lorentz frame where
k^{z}=k^{y}=0
so that

k^{t}=k^{x}=k_{\perp}
(with the assumption that
k_{\perp}\neq 0
) and using

k_{\mu}\mathcal{T}^{\mu\nu}(k)=0
, we find

\displaystyle\overline{\mathcal{T}^{\mu\nu}}(k)S_{\mu\nu\lambda\sigma}(k)\mathcal{T}^{\lambda\sigma}(k)

\displaystyle=\overline{\mathcal{T}^{\mu\nu}(k)}\mathcal{T}_{\mu\nu}(k)-\frac{1}{2}\overline{\mathcal{T}(k)}\mathcal{T}(k)\,,

(90)

where
\mathcal{T}(k)=\mathcal{T}^{\mu}{}_{\mu}(k)
.
Thus, the emission probability is

\displaystyle\braket{1\mathrm{p}|1\mathrm{p}}=\kappa^{2}\int\frac{d^{3}\mathbf{k}}{(2\pi)^{3}2k}\left[\mathcal{T}^{\mu\nu}(k)\mathcal{T}_{\mu\nu}(k)-\frac{1}{2}\overline{\mathcal{T}}(k)\mathcal{T}(k)\right]\,,

(91)

as is well known.

The physical right Rindler modes can be chosen as

\displaystyle h^{(\mathrm{IR}:\omega,\mathbf{k}_{\perp})}_{\mu\nu}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left[g_{\mu\nu}+2q_{\mu\nu}(\mathbf{k}_{\bot})\right]v^{(\mathrm{R}:\omega,\mathbf{k}_{\perp})}(x)\,,

(92)

\displaystyle h^{(\mathrm{I\!IR}:\omega,\mathbf{k}_{\perp})}_{\mu\nu}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}k_{\perp}^{2}}\left[\epsilon^{(\parallel)}_{\mu\alpha}\epsilon^{(\perp)}_{\nu\beta}+\epsilon^{(\parallel)}_{\nu\alpha}\epsilon^{(\perp)}_{\mu\beta}\right]\nabla^{\alpha}\nabla^{\beta}v^{(\mathrm{R}:\omega,\mathbf{k}_{\perp})}(x)\,.

(93)

The modes
h^{(\mathrm{I\!IR}:\omega,\mathbf{k}_{\perp})}_{\mu\nu}(x)

were found in Ref.
[
44
]
up to a normalization factor
whereas the modes
h_{\mu\nu}^{(\mathrm{IR}:\omega,\mathbf{k}_{\perp})}(x)

were obtained in Ref.
[
35
]
by a gauge transformation from
the modes given in Ref.
[
44
]
up to a normalization factor.
The tensors in Eq. (
92
) and the differential operator
in Eq. (
93
) commute with the operations for defining
the Unruh and Rindler modes from the Minkowski modes. Hence, the relationship
between the description of the emission process in Minkowski spacetime and
the interaction of the classical source with the thermal bath is exactly
the same as in the scalar case. Thus, just like in the scalar case, if we
define the emission and absorption amplitudes in the right Rindler wedge as

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}^{(\mathrm{P}:\omega,\mathbf{k}_{\perp})}_{\mathrm{em}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\kappa}{2\pi}\int T^{\mu\nu}(x)\overline{h_{\mu\nu}^{(\mathrm{RP}:\omega,\mathbf{k}_{\perp})}(x)}\sqrt{-g}\,d^{4}x\,,

(94)

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}^{(\mathrm{P}:\omega,\mathbf{k}_{\perp})}_{\mathrm{abs}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\kappa}{2\pi}\int T^{\mu\nu}(x)h^{(\mathrm{RP}:\omega,\mathbf{k}_{\perp})}_{\mu\nu}(x)\sqrt{-g}\,d^{4}x\,,

(95)

respectively,
then Eq. (
76
) for
\braket{1\mathrm{p}|1\mathrm{p}}

holds as in the electromagnetic case together with its interpretation in terms of
the Unruh effect.

VI
Discussion

We have shown that what uniformly accelerated observers interpret as
interaction of an
arbitrary
charge distribution with
the Unruh thermal bath is interpreted by inertial observers as emission of radiation by the same charge distribution.
All calculations were performed in first-order perturbation theory,
which means that the inertial-frame result corresponds to
classical

radiation—once one accepts that the latter consists of
quanta
of
energy satisfying Planck’s energy-frequency relation. This result, obtained for the scalar, electromagnetic,
and graviton fields, corroborates the claim that classical
radiation observed in the inertial frame can already be considered
as an “observation” of the Unruh effect
in the same sense that, in Newtonian mechanics,
the planetary motion in the inertial
frame can be considered to be providing evidence of the inertial forces, e.g., the centrifugal force,
in the rotating frame.
This observation has already been made
in the literature for the case of point-like sources
in specific trajectories
[
28
,
29
,
30
,
31
,
32
,
33
,
34
,
35
]
. Here, the observation is extended to arbitrary currents
for the scalar, electromagnetic, and gravitational fields.
In our view, the analysis presented here proves, beyond any
doubt, that classical radiation seen by inertial observers,
with the only extra assumption that it is constituted by
quanta

of energy, is a testimony
to the existence of the Unruh thermal bath in the uniformly
accelerated
frame. Those who dispute this observation
would have to reproduce the radiation from the classical source
in a uniformly accelerated frame without using the Unruh effect.

Appendix A
Radiation in the Heisenberg picture

In this Appendix we
discuss radiation of massless scalar field in
4
dimensions from a
classical source in the Heisenberg picture. This confirms that the radiation
formula from a classical source does not depend on the quantum state.

The field equation for the Heisenberg operator for a massless scalar field,
\widehat{\phi}_{\mathrm{H}}(x)
, with a classical source term
j(x)
is

\displaystyle\Box\widehat{\phi}_{\mathrm{H}}(x)=j(x)\,,

(96)

where we assume
j(x)
to be compactly supported.
The retarded Green’s function
G_{R}(x,x^{\prime})
satisfying

\displaystyle\Box_{x}G_{R}(x,x^{\prime})=\delta^{(4)}(x-x^{\prime})\,,

(97)

is given by

\displaystyle G_{R}(x,x^{\prime})=i\theta(t-t^{\prime})\int\frac{d^{3}\mathbf{k}}{(2\pi)^{3}2k}\left[e^{-ik\cdot(x-x^{\prime})}-e^{ik\cdot(x-x^{\prime})}\right]\,,

(98)

where
\theta
is the Heaviside step function.

Let
\widehat{\phi}(x)
be the quantum field without the source
j(x)

that agrees with

\widehat{\phi}_{\mathrm{H}}(x)
in the past of the source. Then
the field
\widehat{\phi}_{\mathrm{H}}(x)
at a time in the future of the source is

\displaystyle\widehat{\phi}_{\mathrm{H}}(x)

\displaystyle=\widehat{\phi}(x)+\phi^{(\mathrm{cl})}(x)\,,

(99)

where

\displaystyle\phi^{(\mathrm{cl})}(x)

\displaystyle=\int G_{R}(x,x^{\prime})j(x^{\prime})\,d^{4}x^{\prime}

\displaystyle=i\int\frac{d^{3}\mathbf{k}}{(2\pi)^{3}2k}\left[\tilde{\jmath}(k)e^{-ik\cdot x}-\overline{\tilde{\jmath}(k)}e^{ik\cdot x}\right]\,,

(100)

where
\tilde{\jmath}(k)
is the Fourier transform of
j(x)
defined by Eq. (
45
).
Here we have used the fact that the source
j(x)
is real.
Thus, if we expand the fields
\widehat{\phi}_{\mathrm{H}}(x)
and
\widehat{\phi}(x)
as

\displaystyle\widehat{\phi}_{\mathrm{H}}(x)

\displaystyle=\int\frac{d^{3}\mathbf{k}}{(2\pi)^{3}2k}\left[\hat{a}_{\mathbf{k}}^{\mathrm{H}}e^{-ik\cdot x}+\hat{a}_{\mathbf{k}}^{\mathrm{H}}e^{ik\cdot x}\right]\,,

(101)

\displaystyle\widehat{\phi}(x)

\displaystyle=\int\frac{d^{3}\mathbf{k}}{(2\pi)^{3}2k}\left[\hat{a}_{\mathbf{k}}e^{-ik\cdot x}+\hat{a}_{\mathbf{k}}e^{ik\cdot x}\right]\,,

(102)

in the future of the source, then we have

\displaystyle\hat{a}^{\mathrm{H}}_{\mathbf{k}}=\hat{a}_{\mathbf{k}}+i\tilde{\jmath}(k)\,.

(103)

We assume that the Heisenberg state
\ket{\mathrm{H}}
satisfies

\bra{\mathrm{H}}a_{\mathbf{k}}\ket{\mathrm{H}}=0
for all
\mathbf{k}
.
The initial and final number operators,
\widehat{N}^{(i)}
and
\widehat{N}^{(f)}
, are defined by

\displaystyle\widehat{N}^{(i)}

\displaystyle=\int\frac{d^{3}\mathbf{k}}{(2\pi)^{3}2k}\hat{a}_{\mathbf{k}}^{\dagger}a_{\mathbf{k}}\,,

(104)

\displaystyle\widehat{N}^{(f)}

\displaystyle=\int\frac{d^{3}\mathbf{k}}{(2\pi)^{3}2k}\hat{a}_{\mathbf{k}}^{\mathrm{H}\dagger}a_{\mathbf{k}}^{\mathrm{H}}\,.

(105)

Then, the initial and final particle numbers,

\bra{\mathrm{H}}\widehat{N}^{(i)}\ket{\mathrm{H}}
and
\bra{\mathrm{H}}\widehat{N}^{(f)}\ket{\mathrm{H}}
, are ill-defined in general, but their difference,
i.e. the increase in the particle number, is well-defined and state independent. It is given by

\displaystyle\Delta N

\displaystyle=\bra{\mathrm{H}}\widehat{N}^{(f)}\ket{\mathrm{H}}-\bra{\mathrm{H}}\widehat{N}^{(i)}\ket{\mathrm{H}}

\displaystyle=\int\frac{d^{3}\mathrm{k}}{(2\pi)^{3}2k}|\tilde{\jmath}(k)|^{2}\,.

(106)

Thus, the increase in the particle number is independent of the state and agrees with
the classical result.

Appendix B
Emission from a classical source in the Fulling vacuum

In this Appendix we analyze the emission process from a classical source in the Fulling vacuum state in the interaction picture. We find that the classical
result is reproduced differently compared to the case with the Minkowski vacuum
state.

The final state to first order in perturbation theory is

\displaystyle\ket{f}

\displaystyle=\left(1+i\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{for}}\right)\ket{0_{\mathrm{F}}}+\ket{1\mathrm{p}^{(\mathrm{R})}}\,,

(107)

1

\displaystyle\ket{1\mathrm{p}^{(\mathrm{R})}}=\frac{i}{8\pi^{2}a}\int_{0}^{\infty}d\omega\int d^{2}\mathbf{k_{\perp}}\mathcal{A}^{(\omega,\mathbf{k}_{\perp})}_{\mathrm{em}}\hat{b}^{\dagger}_{(\mathrm{R}:\omega,\mathbf{k}_{\perp})}\ket{0_{\mathrm{F}}}\,,

(108)

\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{for}}

\displaystyle 2\,\mathrm{Im}\,\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{for}}=\braket{1\mathrm{p}^{(\mathrm{R})}|1\mathrm{p}^{(\mathrm{R})}}\,.

(109)

[See Eq. (
46
) for the case with the Minkowski vacuum.]
We find

\displaystyle\braket{1\mathrm{p}^{(\mathrm{R})}|1\mathrm{p}^{(\mathrm{R})}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{8\pi^{2}a}\int_{0}^{\infty}d\omega\int d^{2}\mathbf{k_{\perp}}\left|\mathcal{A}^{(\omega,\mathbf{k}_{\perp})}_{\mathrm{em}}\right|^{2}\,.

(110)

The right Rindler particle number operator is

\displaystyle\widehat{N}^{(\mathrm{R})}=\int_{0}^{\infty}d\omega\int d^{2}\mathbf{k}_{\perp}\hat{b}^{\dagger}_{(\mathrm{R}:\omega,\mathbf{k}_{\perp})}\hat{b}_{(\mathrm{R}:\omega,\mathbf{k}_{\perp})}\,,

(111)

\langle 1\mathrm{p}^{(\mathrm{R})}|\widehat{N}^{(R)}|1\mathrm{p}^{(\mathrm{R})}\rangle=\langle 1\mathrm{p}^{(\mathrm{R})}|1\mathrm{p}^{(\mathrm{R})}\rangle

\langle 1\mathrm{p}^{(\mathrm{R})}|\widehat{N}^{(L)}|1\mathrm{p}^{(\mathrm{R})}\rangle=0

\displaystyle\braket{f|\widehat{N}^{(\mathrm{R)})}|f}

\displaystyle\braket{f|\widehat{N}^{(\mathrm{L)})}|f}

(113)
