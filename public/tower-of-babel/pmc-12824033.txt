Quantitative validation of data-driven motion correction for brain PET using phantom with motion generator system
Authors: Yuto Kamitaka, Muneyuki Sakata, Keiichi Oda, Akie Katsuki, Hirofumi Kawakami, Kei Wagatsuma, Masato Kobayashi, Kenji Ishii
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHead motion during brain positron emission tomography (PET) degrades image quality and quantitative accuracy. Therefore, a data-driven motion correction (MC) method utilizing ultrafast list-mode reconstruction technology has been proposed and shown to considerably improve image quality. However, reproducing accurate actual motions and motion-free images using clinical data alone remains challenging. This study aimed to quantitatively evaluate data-driven MC using a brain phantom for known tracer distributions and a custom-made motion generator system for variable known motions.MethodsHoffman 3D brain phantom was filled with 20 and 3 MBq of [18F]fluoro-2-deoxy-D-glucose (FDG) to simulate high- and low-radioactivity conditions corresponding to brain FDG PET and amyloid PET acquisitions, respectively. Two separate phantom measurements were performed accordingly. Motion simulation was conducted using a custom-designed motion generator, incorporating 15° and 30° rotations about the z-axis, 3° and 6° rotations about the x-axis, and 5 mm and 10 mm translations along the z-axis in the PET image coordinates. The data-driven MC was applied with frame durations of 1, 2, 5, 10, and 20 s for motion estimation. The estimated motions were compared with the motions measured using an external optical tracker system. %contrast and gray matter coefficient of variation (CV%) were calculated from the motion-corrected PET images.ResultsThe motion generator system successfully reproduced the designed motions. Motion estimation remained stable under high-radioactivity condition but showed reduced stability under low-radioactivity condition, particularly with shorter frame durations. Under both conditions, longer frame durations led to underestimation of continuous motion. The data-driven MC improved %contrast and gray matter CV% across all conditions, with shorter frame durations providing better correction for quick or continuous motions. However, shorter frame durations increased statistical noise, especially under low-radioactivity condition.ConclusionThe data-driven MC effectively improved the quality of motion-affected PET images under both high- and low-radioactivity conditions, indicating its broad applicability. However, correction accuracy deteriorated under the lower-radioactivity condition.Keywords: Head motion, Data-driven motion correction, Image quality, [18F]FDG, Amyloid PET, Dementia, Alzheimer’s disease

Introduction

Brain positron emission tomography/computed tomography (PET/CT) is a minimally invasive imaging modality that facilitates both visual and quantitative evaluation of brain metabolism, amyloid-β deposition, and tau aggregation as core biomarkers for dementia diagnosis [1–3]. The recent approval and clinical implementation of Alzheimer’s disease (AD)-modifying drugs, such as lecanemab and donanemab, across various countries have increased the demand for brain PET scans. This modality employs radiopharmaceuticals, including [18F]fluoro-2-deoxy-D-glucose ([18F]FDG), [18F]florbetapir, [18F]flutemetamol, and [18F]florbetaben, which are essential for the early and differential diagnosis of dementia [1].

According to the guidelines of the Japanese Society of Nuclear Medicine (JSNM) and the Society of Nuclear Medicine and Molecular Imaging (SNMMI)/European Association of Nuclear Medicine (EANM), these FDG and amyloid scans require an acquisition time of 10–30 min [4–6]. Findings from the Japanese Alzheimer’s Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (J-ADNI) study indicated that approximately 80% of participants, including cognitively normal individuals, exhibited head motion during a 30-min FDG scan [7]. Furthermore, mild head motion was observed, even during shorter 10-min acquisitions [8]. Such head motion significantly degrades image quality and compromises the quantitative accuracy of PET data [7, 9–11]. Head motion causes image blurring and introduces errors in attenuation and scatter corrections because of the misalignment between CT and PET images [12, 13]. Owing to significant improvements in the spatial resolution of PET/CT through advancements in hardware and software [14–17], motion artifacts have become increasingly pronounced. The administration of AD-modifying drugs requires evidence of positive amyloid biomarkers (amyloid PET or cerebrospinal fluid test) [18, 19]. Although amyloid PET is typically assessed visually as positive or negative in clinical practice, quantitative measures, such as the standard uptake value ratio (SUVR) [20] or centiloid scale [21], have become more prevalent in clinical research and drug development for AD. For instance, quantitative amyloid PET is increasingly used in both longitudinal and cross-sectional evaluations to monitor treatment response [22]. Therefore, minimizing the influence of head motion, which degrades the PET image quality and quantitative accuracy, remains a critical challenge for achieving accurate brain PET imaging.

Several methods of motion correction (MC) have been proposed. A typical hardware-based approach involves tracking markers attached to the head using external devices, such as Polaris® [12, 13, 23, 24]. Accurate reflection of motion direction and magnitude measured by external devices in PET coordinates requires precise calibration to align the external device coordinates with those of the PET/CT system [23]. This approach increases the burden on both subjects and staff [25]. Another approach involves frame-based methods, where imaging data are divided into multi-minute frames, each reconstructed separately and subsequently aligned [7, 26, 27]. However, the images used for alignment contain motion-related errors owing to inaccurate attenuation and scatter correction [12, 13].

A data-driven MC method utilizing ultrafast list-mode reconstruction technology has been developed [11, 28]. This method divides the list-mode data into short temporal frames ranging from less than a second to several seconds, and the motions are estimated using images of the short temporal frames [11, 29]. Subsequently, the estimated motions are corrected by adjusting the list-mode data, followed by image reconstruction using the same parameters as those in ordinal clinical conditions. This approach eliminates motion-related errors in attenuation and scatter corrections. Although data-driven MC has been increasingly reported [11, 27, 30], most evaluations have been limited to clinical images. Evaluations using clinical images are practical; however, they rely on relative improvements in qualitative and quantitative image attributes, as accurately reproducing absolute motions and motion-free images from clinical data alone is challenging. A study using artificial simulated motions applied to clinically measured list-mode data evaluated registration accuracy under short frame durations [29]; however, the MC performance with real measured motions should be quantitatively verified. Therefore, this study aims to quantitatively validate data-driven MC using phantom measurements with predefined motions and known tracer distributions. Our custom-made motion generator system realized more variations in the designed motions compared to the study by Odagiri et al. [31], and the phantom’s radioactivity concentrations were configured to reflect the clinical brain FDG and amyloid PET imaging conditions.

Materials and methods

Motion generator system

Figure 1 shows the motion generator system, comprising three stepper motors controlled by a microcontroller and nonmetallic components fabricated using a 3D printing system. Part of this system was designed using computer-aided design (CAD) software FreeCAD ver. 0.19 (https://www.freecad.org) and printed using Guider 2 (FLASHFORGE, Hangzhou, Zhejiang, China). For the simulation of head motion, the system could rotate about the x- and z-axes and translation along the z-axis in the PET image coordinates. These are most commonly experienced head movements in clinical situations. The motion ranges were defined as 9° rotation about the x-axis, 360° rotation about the z-axis, and 30 mm translation along the z-axis. It should be noted that while the rotational axis about the z-axis ((a) in Fig. 1) coincides with the z-axis in the PET image coordinates, the rotational axis about the x-axis ((b) in Fig. 1) was parallel, but does not coincide with the x-axis in the PET image coordinates.

Motion generator system for simulation head motion. The system enables rotations about the z- (a) and x-axes (b) of the PET image coordinates and translation along the z-axis (c). The marker for optical tracker was attached to the top center of the phantom cylinder

Data acquisitions

Two phantom measurements were performed on different days, using a Hoffman 3D brain phantom (Data Spectrum Corporation, Hillsborough, NC, USA) and a motion generator system. All PET data for the phantom were acquired using a Discovery MI PET/CT system (GE HealthCare, Milwaukee, WI, USA), a PET scanner comprising a lutetium-yttrium oxyorthosilicate (LYSO) silicon photomultiplier (SiPM) PET instrument with a 64-slice CT scanner [17].

[18F]FDG was used for radioactivity measurements, and the activities at the start of the acquisition were 20 and 3 MBq, which were defined as the high- and low-radioactivity conditions, respectively. The activities were determined by considering the estimated activities according to standard protocols used in clinical studies. The brain radioactivity level 30 min after the injection of 185 MBq [18F]FDG was estimated to be 20 MBq [4, 5, 32, 33]. Similarly, 3 MBq at the start of acquisition corresponded to the estimated lowest brain radioactivity level among the protocols with amyloid PET tracers and represented the estimated brain radioactivity levels 50 min after the injection of 555 MBq of [11C]PiB and 90 min after the injection of 185 MBq of [18F]flutemetamol [4, 5].

The motions simulated in this study were 15° and 30° rotation about the z-axis (head rotation) (phases (2) and (3)), 3° and 6° rotation about the x-axis (chin down) (phases (4) and (5)), 5 mm and 10 mm parallel translation along the z-axis (phases (6) and (7)) and 30° continuous rotation about the z-axis from side to side at 1°/s and 2°/s speed (phases (8) and (9)). Table 1 lists the order of the simulated motion and scan duration for each phase. For the rotations about the z- and x-axes and the translation along the z-axis (phases (2)–(7)), the phase was first started in the original posture and subsequently moved to the designed posture for 60 s. The acquisition continued in this posture, and the phantom was returned to its original posture at the end of the phase. Continuous rotation about the z-axis enabled movement from the start to the end of the motion phase. The scan duration per phase was adjusted, considering radioactive decay, to achieve the same number of counts in each phase and eliminate differences in image quality owing to differences in the number of counts. Because the phantom may not move exactly as programmed, the real positions of the phantom were simultaneously observed with Polaris Vicra (Northern Digital Incorporated, Waterloo, ON, Canada), an optical tracker used to evaluate motion estimation. The markers for the Polaris were attached to the top center of the phantom cylinder.

Motion correction and image reconstruction

The PET data were motion corrected, and the images were reconstructed on a workstation running the PET Duetto reconstruction toolbox using lmDuetto ver. 2.17 (GE HealthCare) for MATLAB 2020b (The MathWorks Inc., Natick, MA, USA), based on the implementation described in prior research [11, 29]. The frame durations for the motion estimations were 1, 2, 5, 10, and 20 s. The images for motion estimation were reconstructed from the list-mode data using the maximum likelihood expectation maximization method with four iterations, without applying attenuation or scatter correction. For the motion estimations, the reconstructed images were aligned to the image of the first 120 s using rigid registration implemented as the method of “Realign” in SPM12 (the Wellcome Centre for Human Neuroimaging, University College London, UK). The motions measured by Polaris and those estimated from the alignment of the images were compared. The data for list-mode events, attenuation correction factor, scanner sensitivity factor, and estimated scatter contribution were corrected according to the estimated motions [11]. The images used for the analysis were fully reconstructed using three-dimensional ordered-subset expectation maximization (3D-OS-EM) with time-of-flight (TOF) (4 iterations, 16 subsets) and a 4 mm full width at half maximum (FWHM) Gaussian post-filter, from the data with and without motion corrections. The parameters of the reconstructed images were 128 × 128 matrix, 71 slices, 25.6 cm FOV, and 2.0 × 2.0 × 2.79 mm/voxel.

Discussion

A data-driven MC provides robustness against head motions, and its ability must be evaluated through the experiments with the known motions and the known distributions. In this study, we quantitatively evaluated the MC using a custom-made motion generator system and a Hoffman 3D brain phantom. The experimental settings were designed for variations in head motion, and the conditions reflected clinical brain FDG and amyloid PET imaging.

Minor discrepancies between the designed motions, which were specified as rotation amounts of the stepper motors, and the motions measured by Polaris were observed. Minor rotations or translations other than the designed motions, such as the small translations (Tx and Ty in Figs. 3 and 4) in the phases of the rotations about the z-axis (phases (2), (3), (8), and (9)), were observed. The discrepancies and errors can be attributed to the backlash in the gear system of the motion mechanisms, bending of the nonmetal materials, and misalignment of the rotation axis during the rotation of the heavy (6.5 kg) phantoms using nonmetal material support parts and gear systems. Despite these discrepancies and errors, the system demonstrated high motion reproducibility, enabling valid comparisons across varying radioactivity concentrations.

In addition, the discrepancies in the translations (Ty and Tz in Figs. 3 and 4) in the phases of rotation about the x-axis (phases (4) and (5)) were owing to the difference in the origins of the measured and estimated motions. The origin of the motion measured by Polaris was at the markers of the original posture attached to the top center of the phantom, and the origin of the motion estimated by MC was at the origin of the PET image coordinates. Because the locations and orientations of the two origins were manually calibrated to have only a z-axis offset, the differences in their origins did not theoretically affect the measurements and estimations in the other phases (rotations about the z-axis and translations along the z-axis). However, the enhancements of the minor translations other than the designed motions, such as those attributed to the misalignment of the rotational axis, were observed in the measured translations by Polaris.

Excluding these discrepancies, the motions measured by Polaris and those estimated by the MC showed general agreement. However, underestimations of peak rotation angles during 30° continuous rotations were observed with longer frame durations for motion estimation in both the high- and low-radioactivity conditions. The stability of the estimated motions decreased with shorter frames and lower activity conditions.

In the evaluations based on the parameters calculated from the reconstructed images, the gray matter CV% of the reference phase was higher in the low-radioactivity condition than in the high-radioactivity condition. The %contrasts values in the reference phase were similar; however, higher CV% in both gray and white matter regions of the Hoffman phantom led to slightly lower %contrast under low-radioactivity conditions than in the high-radioactivity conditions.

MC improved %contrast and gray matter CV% to levels comparable to those of the reference phase under both high- and low-radioactivity conditions. However, slight differences were found in the results depending on the frame duration and radioactivity concentration. When using MC, shorter frame durations for motion estimation allow for the correction of finer motions, which results in fewer counts per frame, potentially leading to unstable interframe motion estimations. Conversely, longer frame durations ensure sufficient counts per frame, but may include large intra-frame motions, resulting in the degradation of motion-corrected images. Prior research, which only considered interframe motions, suggested that the accuracies of the registrations depend on the true and scattered counts per frame, and 4 × 105 true and scattered counts per frame provide sufficient levels of accurate registration [29]. While the scanners used were different from those used in prior research, this count level was achieved with 1.8 s and 11.5 s in high- and low-radioactivity conditions, respectively, in the present study. The experimental results demonstrated that motion estimation at 1 s/frame would be sufficient in the high-radioactivity condition, and when considering the combinations of %contrast and gray matter CV%, 5 s/frame would be the best choice in the low-radioactivity condition. Considering the combined effects of inter- and intra-frame motion, these results are generally consistent with the findings of prior research [29]. Additionally, the differences in the results across frame durations were minimal, with motion estimation at 1–5 s/frame yielding similar and acceptable results under both high- and low-radioactivity conditions. Note that, in this study, motion estimation frames were defined based on frame durations to evaluate the effects of duration on motions at a fixed speed. Count-based frame definitions for motion estimation, such as 4 × 105 true and scattered counts per frame, would yield more optimized results in clinical situations, as proposed in prior research [29]. Count-based frame definitions are adaptable for radioactive decay during measurements, and these optimum frame definitions may vary depending on the physical performance of the PET/CT device.

Spangler-Bickell et al. reported that applying motion correction to clinical images without motion did not affect the image quality [11]. Consistent with these findings, the phantom tests in this study demonstrated similar results, indicating that MC can be applied universally to all datasets, and not solely to those containing motion artifacts.

Regarding computational time, the reconstruction of each short frame for motion estimation was proportional to the number of counts in the frame, but was much faster than the duration of each frame [28]. Thus, the frame reconstructions and motion estimations were completed within several minutes in all cases of this study. Therefore, the frame durations for motion estimation did not make much difference in the total computational time for motion estimation [29]. The most time-consuming process was the full reconstruction with MC using the estimated motions. While the main part (such as 3D-OS-EM) of the reconstruction was independent of the frame duration for motion estimation, shorter frames for motion estimation had disadvantages compared to longer ones in the process of calculating the scanner sensitivity corrections. However, the computational time could be reduced by the optimized implementation, such as grouping the short frames based on thresholds for the magnitude of estimated motions.

The present study has several limitations. The current study simulated head motions as rotations about a single axis or translations along a single axis, whereas in actual clinical examinations, head motions occur as a combination of multi-axis rotations and translations, which were beyond the scope of this study. Furthermore, the maximum velocities of head motion simulated in this study were relatively slow (2°/s and 0.2 mm/s). In the clinical setting, sudden and large motions may occur at speeds that exceed these limits.

Conclusions

This study evaluated data-driven MC using a motion generator system and the Hoffman 3D brain phantom. The experimental results with variable settings indicated that shorter frame durations effectively captured rapid or continuous motions but frequently produced unstable estimations of postures under low-radioactivity conditions. Conversely, longer frame durations stabilized motion estimation but resulted in incomplete correction of rapid or continuous motions. Overall, the data-driven MC effectively improved the quality of motion-included images to a level comparable to motion-free images under both high- and low-radioactivity conditions. However, optimization based on the device’s physical performance is necessary. This supports the effectiveness and robustness of data-driven MC.
