Computer‐Aided Sperm Analysis Protocol for Evaluating Sperm Motility in Japanese Medaka
Authors: Miwa Kuroyanagi, Satoshi Ansai, Keigo Okamoto, Ai Kato, Touko Yamazaki, Yasuhiro Kamei, Eiji Watanabe, Kiyoshi Naruse, Yukiko Ogino
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTChanges in sperm motility can serve as an early indicator of reproductive effects caused by environmental chemicals or genetic perturbations. However, sperm motility is highly sensitive to external factors such as osmolarity, ionic composition, and the timing of measurement after activation, making it challenging to obtain consistent and reproducible measurements. Here, we present a standardized protocol for assessing sperm motility in Japanese medaka ( Oryzias latipes ) using a sperm motility analysis system (SMAS), an application for computer‐aided sperm motility analysis (CASA). This protocol details the procedures for sperm collection, activation, and quantitative motility assessment, with particular focus on changes in the percentage of motile sperm post activation and the effects of sperm cryopreservation. We demonstrate time‐dependent declines in sperm motility and velocity, and highlight the importance of early post‐activation measurements to accurately capture peak motility. Notably, cryopreservation significantly accelerated the decline in sperm motility rate without affecting the initial proportion of motile sperm. To enable reliable comparisons among experimental groups, we recommend standardizing the initiation time after sperm activation by using CASA, and show that measurements should be initiated within 1 min after activation to obtain consistent and reliable data. This standardized SMAS‐based protocol provides a robust and reproducible framework for sperm motility analysis in medaka and will be valuable not only for studies in reproductive biology, toxicology, and environmental risk assessment but also for applied research, such as breeding of aquacultural fishes.

Background and Features

Sperm motility is a sensitive and integrative endpoint that reflects multiple cellular processes, including mitochondrial function, flagellar structure, and ion channel signaling. Therefore, alterations in sperm motility can serve as an early indicator of reproductive toxicity induced by environmental chemicals and genetic alterations, representing a phenotypic endpoint at the interface of environmental toxicology and reproductive biology (Kollar et al. 2021; Ribeiro et al. 2023; Ogino et al. 2023). However, sperm motility is highly sensitive to external factors such as osmolarity, ionic composition, and the timing of measurement after activation, which can result in considerable variability depending on experimental conditions.

In many freshwater teleosts, sperm motility is activated upon exposure to a low‐osmolarity environment such as freshwater, although the optimal ionic conditions for activation and motility maintenance are species‐specific. Because the ionic composition of freshwater is highly variable, the use of an isotonic saline medium (e.g., Ringer's or equivalent balanced salt formulations) allows for highly reproducible analyses. In masu salmon ( Oncorhynchus masou ), isotonic salt solutions have been shown to prolong the duration of sperm motility compared with distilled water, indicating the benefit of controlled media for reproducible functional assessment (Ohta et al. 1986). In medaka and zebrafish, sperm motility is commonly analyzed using defined balanced salt solutions (BSSs), including species‐specific formulations or Hank's balanced salt solution (HBSS), to provide stable osmotic and ionic conditions and to minimize variability associated with freshwater activation (Kollar et al. 2021; Closs et al. 2022; Sayyari et al. 2024).

Medaka sperm motility is known to be initiated in HBSS across a wide range of osmolarities between 25 and 686 mOsm/kg (Yang and Tiersch 2009), suggesting that sperm motility can be initiated in BSS. Consistently, BSS is routinely used in protocols such as those of the National BioResource Project (NBRP). Medaka sperm has been shown to retain motility for extended periods at low temperatures (e.g., 4°C), indicating that low temperature helps maintain sperm in a stable state after activation (Yang and Tiersch 2009). This property suggests that maintaining sperm at low temperature can help preserve the initial motility state and improve reproducibility during analysis. However, during handling, sperm suspensions inevitably return to room temperature between loading into the chamber and measurement, and variations in handling time during this interval may affect the results.

Even under controlled ionic conditions, sperm motility remains highly sensitive to variability in the interval between activation and measurement, resulting in substantial operator‐dependent variation. Therefore, computer‐aided sperm motility analysis (CASA) has been utilized to objectively and reproducibly quantify sperm motility while minimizing operator‐dependent variability (Kime et al. 2001; Yang and Tiersch 2009; Kollar et al. 2021; Closs et al. 2022; Sayyari et al. 2024). In this study, we evaluated the effects of time after activation and cryopreservation on medaka sperm motility following suspension in BSS using a sperm motility analysis system (SMAS) to establish a stable and reproducible measurement protocol.

Data Storage and File Structure

The output data are organized into two main folders as follows: the “ResultData (SMAS3)” folder, containing summary statistics, per‐sperm motility parameters, and raw tracking data (x–y coordinates), and the “Image” folder, containing recorded videos and processed image data.

Cryopreservation of Sperm and Its Use in SMAS

If sperm cryopreservation is required, the sperm suspension is aspirated into 10‐μL glass capillaries using a mouth‐operated glass capillary suction tube and transferred into cryotubes. The cryotube caps are loosely closed, and the cryotubes are placed in a 15‐mL tube standing on dry ice and pre‐incubated for 20 min. After incubation, the caps are tightly closed, and the cryotubes are transferred to a Dewar flask containing liquid nitrogen (Kinoshita et al. 2009). Cryopreserved sperm remain capable of fertilization and are widely used in medaka, particularly for the maintenance and management of genetic stocks (Taniguchi et al. 2006).

Anticipated Results

Methods: Time‐Dependent Changes in Sperm Motility

To examine the duration of continuous sperm motility after activation, we quantified sperm motility at multiple time points following dilution of the sperm suspension in BSS. The initial time point was defined as described in Section 3.4.2. Sperm motility was assessed at 1, 3, 5, and 10 min after the initial time point. To ensure accurate evaluation of time‐dependent changes, the elapsed time after the initial time point was recorded for each measurement using a timer. In addition, sperm motility dynamics were compared between fresh and cryopreserved sperms measured at 1, 3, and 5 min after the same time point as used for fresh samples (Figure 4). The sperm motility was markedly decreased at 10 min after the initial time point in fresh sperm. Based on this result, we determined that measurements within 5 min are sufficient to assess the effects of cryopreservation on sperm motility; therefore, motility at 10 min was not assessed in cryopreserved sperm. A total of five wild‐type medaka ( O. latipes , OK‐Cab strain, ID:MT830) provided by the NBRP Medaka were used in this study.

Methods: Statistical Analysis

The total sperm number was estimated by SMAS based on the detected sperm concentration in the chamber, taking into account the dilution factor (1:10) and the total volume of the original sperm suspension in cryopreserved extender (66 μL per testis). The obtained data were analyzed using linear mixed‐effects models to account for repeated measurements within individuals. Given that this study aimed to establish a standardized measurement protocol, a relatively small sample size (n = 5) was employed, and the use of a linear mixed‐effects model allowed us to appropriately account for repeated measurements within individuals and inter‐individual variability. Time after activation (seconds), sperm condition (fresh or cryopreserved), and their interaction were included as fixed effects, and individual males were included as a random effect to account for inter‐individual variability. Time after activation was treated as a continuous variable to estimate the rate of change over time. Statistical analyses were performed in R using the lme4 package. The fixed‐effect estimates and associated statistics are summarized in Table 2.

Results

Based on the mixed‐effects model analysis using the elapsed time after activation (Table 2), the motile sperm rate showed a significant time‐dependent decline in fresh sperm (slope = −0.088% s−1, p < 0.001), indicating a progressive reduction in sperm motility following activation (Figure 4a). Although no significant difference in motile sperm rate was detected between fresh and cryopreserved sperm at the initial time point (p = 0.99263), a significant interaction between time after activation and sperm condition was observed. Cryopreservation increased the rate of motility decline by 0.063637% s−1 relative to fresh sperm (β = −0.063637, p = 0.001). The decline rate for cryopreserved sperm was obtained by summing the main effect of time and the time × condition interaction term (−0.088% s−1 + −0.064% s−1), yielding a total decline rate of −0.152% s−1. Model estimates indicated a decline rate of −0.088% s−1 for fresh sperm and −0.152% s−1 for cryopreserved sperm, corresponding to an approximately 1.7‐fold faster loss of motility in cryopreserved sperm. Inter‐individual variation was accounted for by including individual males as a random effect in the model.

Using the mixed‐effects modeling framework, we next analyzed time‐dependent changes in the total number of sperms detected after activation (Table 2). The total sperm number significantly decreased over time (slope = −0.07761 s−1, p < 0.001), indicating a gradual loss of detectable sperms during the observation period (Figure 4b). In contrast to sperm motility, cryopreservation had a significant negative main effect on the total sperm number at the initial time point (1 min after activation), with an average reduction of 21.8 sperms compared with fresh sperm (β = −21.82251, p = 0.005). However, no significant interaction between time after activation and cryopreservation was detected (p = 0.168). These results indicate that cryopreservation reduces the initial number of detectable sperms but does not alter the rate of sperm loss over time.

We also analyzed the time‐dependent change in average speed of sperms after activation (Table 2). The average speed significantly decreased over time in fresh sperm (slope = −0.027661 μm s−1, p < 0.001). Cryopreserved sperm exhibited a significantly lower average speed at the initial time point (1 min after activation), with a reduction of 11.77 μm s−1 compared with fresh sperm (β = −11.769688, p < 0.001). No significant interaction between time after activation and cryopreservation was detected (p = 0.897158), indicating that the rate of speed decline over time did not differ between fresh and cryopreserved sperm (Figure 4c).

Discussion

In this study, we quantitatively characterized time‐dependent changes in sperm motility after activation and demonstrated that cryopreservation primarily affects the stability of sperm motility over time rather than the initial proportion of motile sperms. Although the motile sperm rate at 1 min after activation did not differ significantly between fresh and cryopreserved sperm, cryopreserved sperm exhibited a markedly accelerated decline in motility as time progressed. This finding indicates that cryopreservation compromises the ability of the sperm to sustain motility following activation, rather than impairing the initiation of motility.

The use of precise time measurements combined with mixed‐effects modeling, in which time after activation was treated as a continuous variable, enabled us to quantify the rate of motility loss with high resolution. The estimated decline in motility was substantially steeper in cryopreserved sperm than in fresh sperm, highlighting a pronounced reduction in motility stability caused by cryopreservation. Notably, this dynamic effect contrasted with the pattern observed for the total number of sperms, where cryopreservation significantly reduced the initial sperm count but did not alter the rate of decline over time. This divergence suggests that the effects of cryopreservation vary among the measured sperm parameters.

In addition to these effects on the motility rate and sperm number, cryopreservation reduced the initial swimming speed of sperms. Cryopreserved sperm exhibited a markedly lower average velocity immediately after activation compared with fresh sperm, whereas the subsequent rate of speed decline over time did not differ between conditions. Thus, cryopreservation affected the initial swimming speed but did not alter the temporal pattern of speed reduction after activation.

Our results further reveal a pronounced time‐dependent decline in sperm motility even in fresh sperm following activation. Although fresh sperm maintained a high motile sperm rate of nearly 90% at 1 min after activation, motility progressively decreased to approximately 80% at 3 min, 70% at 5 min, and less than 50% by 10 min. These findings indicate that sperm motility is inherently transient after activation, even under non‐cryopreserved conditions, and that the timing of motility assessment critically influences the measured values. Based on this rapid decline, assessing sperm motility within 1 min after activation appears to be the most appropriate window for evaluating the intrinsic motility capacity of fresh sperm. Beyond this time point, motility measurements increasingly reflect post‐activation deterioration rather than baseline sperm quality. The observed decrease in the total number of detected sperms over time coincided with a decline in swimming velocity, which became apparent by 3 min after activation. However, this reduction is unlikely to be attributable to exclusion of slow‐moving sperm by the SMAS detection algorithm, as the parameter settings were configured to detect sperms across a broad range of swimming speeds. Instead, the decrease in detected sperm number may be partly explained by the slight focal drift during continuous observation, whereby some sperms gradually moved out of the focal plane, particularly as motility decreased.

In salmonid fishes such as Atlantic salmon, cryopreservation generally results in reduced sperm motility compared with fresh sperm immediately after activation (Dziewulska et al. 2011). In medaka, however, motility did not differ at the early time point but declined rapidly thereafter in cryopreserved sperm (Figure 4a and Table 2). These differences may be associated with species‐specific reproductive strategies and sperm physiological properties.

Together, these results emphasize the importance of strict temporal control in sperm motility assays and highlight that even short delays after activation can lead to substantial underestimation of sperm performance. These findings underscore that comparisons of sperm motility across studies are highly sensitive to post‐activation timing, underscoring the need for standardized measurement protocols. The standardized protocol enables the reproducible and systematic comparisons of reproductive toxicity caused by environmental factors and the reproductive effects of genetic alterations in medaka. Furthermore, these findings will be valuable for establishing reliable protocols for sperm cryopreservation and quality control in aquaculture fishes.

Conclusions

Sperm motility declined rapidly after activation even in fresh samples, demonstrating that post‐activation timing is a critical determinant of SMAS measurements and should be strictly standardized. Accordingly, SMAS measurements should ideally be initiated within 1 min after activation to obtain consistent and reliable data. Independently, cryopreservation reduced initial swimming speed and the number of detectable sperm and accelerated the loss of motility over time, while not affecting the initial proportion of motile sperm. These findings indicate that cryopreservation impairs both the magnitude and stability of sperm motility.
