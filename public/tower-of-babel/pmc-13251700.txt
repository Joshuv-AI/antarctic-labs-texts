Cerebellar structure is abnormal in schizophrenia and deviates from bipolar disorder
Authors: Mahmoud Rashidi, Jayden Morales, Zachary Brodnick, Vince D Calhoun, Robert Christian Wolf, Bryon A Mueller, Caroline Demro, Scott R Sponheim, Jessica A Turner
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•A cerebellar structural network distinguished schizophrenia from bipolar disorder.•Network included vermis VIIIa, bilateral Crus I, and right lobule IX.•Schizophrenia showed reduced gray matter in negatively loaded cerebellar regions.•Gray matter volume and network loadings correlated with cognitive performance.•Diagnosis predicted cerebellar network loadings independent of cognition.Keywords: Source-based morphometry, Cerebellum, Gray matter, Schizophrenia, Bipolar disorder, MRI

Introduction

Recent advances in neuroimaging have enabled the detection of subtle brain structural variations, extending investigative focus beyond well-studied cortical regions to include previously underexamined structures such as the cerebellum. Long regarded as peripheral to psychiatric nosology, the cerebellum has increasingly been recognized as a meaningful contributor to the neural systems that shape cognitive and affective processes (Adamaszek et al., 2017, Buckner, 2013, McAfee et al., 2022, Moberget et al., 2014, Schmahmann, 2004, Strick et al., 2009). The cerebellum—conceptualized as a neural hub (Kawabata et al., 2022)—coordinates cognitive, emotional, and motor processes disrupted in psychosis, a role supported by its dense connectivity with both cortical and subcortical regions (McAfee et al., 2022). This is complemented by the Cognitive Dysmetria Model of psychosis (Andreasen et al., 1998, Andreasen and Pierson, 2008), where evidence points to cerebellar abnormalities as a meaningful contributor to the development of psychiatric illnesses—including schizophrenia (SZ) and bipolar disorder (BD) (Johnson et al., 2015, Moberget et al., 2018, Monkul et al., 2008, Womer et al., 2009).

Despite extensive genomic, physiological, and neuroimaging research in SZ and BD, there is little reliable evidence of differences in localized brain morphometry between these disorders (Consortium, 2013, Garcia-Rizo et al., 2016, Goldsmith et al., 2016). Some networks and structural variations appear to be shared across disorders, while others reflect disorder-specific deviations (Cheon et al., 2022, Goodkind et al., 2015, Hirjak et al., 2019b, Opel et al., 2020). As the proposed dichotomous relationship between the two disorders has increasingly been challenged (d’Albis and Houenou, 2015), it remains unclear whether they are best conceptualized as existing along a psychosis continuum or as distinct clinical entities (Dines et al., 2024, Sorella et al., 2019).

Various neuroimaging methods have been employed to examine structural abnormalities in psychiatric conditions. Conventional approaches, such as voxel-based morphometry (VBM) and region-of-interest (ROI) analyses, are univariate in nature, examining voxel-wise differences across subjects (Ashburner and Friston, 2000, Mechelli et al., 2005). Such univariate techniques are limited in their ability to capture patterns of interrelationships in structural variation across brain regions. Contemporary research has introduced source-based morphometry (SBM), an alternative that addresses some limitations of univariate approaches. SBM is a data-driven, network-oriented approach that follows the same preprocessing pipeline as VBM. This multivariate technique employs independent component analysis (ICA) to integrate voxel-wise information and identify spatially covarying gray matter (GM) “networks” (Gupta et al., 2014, Xu et al., 2009). A key strength of SBM is its ability to preserve spatial relationships across distributed brain regions by modeling voxel interdependencies rather than treating them independently (Gupta et al., 2019, Lacomba-Arnau et al., 2025, Xu et al., 2009).

Comparative studies examining SZ and BD have shown that SBM detects a greater number of altered neural structures compared with healthy controls (HC) (Kašpárek et al., 2009, Lapomarda et al., 2021, Singh et al., 2022). This is particularly relevant for studies targeting the cerebellum, as SBM can capture finer subregional features while preserving structural integrity. SBM also separates and filters artifact components such as motion or scanner effects, thereby improving sensitivity and reducing the need for multiple statistical test corrections (Chen et al., 2014). A foundational SBM study by Xu et al. demonstrated the method’s ability to identify distributed GM networks that differentiate SZ patients from HC in a large sample (Xu et al., 2009). They observed five networks that showed significantly higher GM concentration in HC than SZ patients, spanning temporal, thalamic, frontal, parietal, and occipital regions. These observations have been supported by subsequent meta-analytic studies (van Erp et al., 2016, Vita et al., 2012). Although morphological studies have reported cerebellar involvement in SZ or BD, findings remain largely inconsistent (Laidi et al., 2019, Moberget et al., 2018, Moussa-Tooks et al., 2022), and few studies have systematically examined cerebellar subregions. Consequently, the interrelationships among spatially covarying cerebellar regions remain poorly characterized.

A recent comparative study by Rootes-Murdy et al. examined GM concentration and volume in SZ, BD, and HC reported cerebellar gray matter reductions in SZ and BD compared to HC (Rootes-Murdy et al., 2022). They found cerebellar subregion differences in vermal areas, Crus I, and Crus II. In a study of BD participants and HC, Lapomarda et al. reported reduced GM concentration in BD within a parietal-occipital and cerebellar network, consisting of the cerebellar tonsil and culmen (Lapomarda et al., 2021). However, similar studies examining GM alterations in SZ and BD reported cerebellar changes in clinical groups but did not parse out cerebellar substructures (Gupta et al., 2014, Sorella et al., 2019), which is warranted given the complex nature of cerebellar functional connectivity.

The primary goal of the present study was to identify distinct cerebellar structural variations between individuals with SZ, bipolar disorder with psychotic features (BDwP), and HC using SBM. We hypothesized that certain cerebellar networks would exhibit shared alterations across disorders, consistent with transdiagnostic mechanisms, whereas others would demonstrate disorder-specific deviations corresponding to distinct cognitive and clinical profiles.

Methods and materials

Participants

This study was conducted as a part of the Human Connectome Project for Psychosis (P-HCP), collected at the University of Minnesota (Demro et al., 2021). In brief, participants with a diagnosis must meet the DSM-5 diagnostic criteria for SZ or BDwP. Participants were adults over the age of 18 and screened for eligibility prior to any data collection. After data curation and preprocessing as described below, this study comprised 85 individuals diagnosed with SZ, 36 individuals diagnosed with BDwP, and 47 HC. None of the SZ patients were diagnosed with schizoaffective bipolar or schizoaffective depressive types. MRI data acquisition

Neuroimaging scanning was conducted at the Center for Magnetic Resonance Research at the University of Minnesota in accordance with Human Connectome Project protocols (Harms et al., 2018). Scanning protocols and preprocessing guidelines have been previously reported (Demro et al., 2021). In summary, T1-weighted images for each participant were collected using a 3-Tesla Siemens Prisma scanner using a 32-channel head coil with the following parameters: voxel size = 0.8 mm3, field of view = 240 × 256 mm, flip angle = 8°, repetition time = 2500 ms.

Four resting-state fMRI scans were acquired, each lasting 6.5 min (488 volumes), collected as two runs per session. For each resting-state pair, scans were acquired with anterior-to-posterior phase encoding followed by posterior-to-anterior phase encoding. During scanning, participants were instructed to keep their eyes open, fixate on a cross, remain awake, and not engage in any specific thoughts. Images were acquired at 2-mm isotropic resolution with the following parameters: repetition time (TR) = 800 ms, echo time (TE) = 37 ms, and flip angle = 52°. For this study, only the resting-state fMRI scan acquired closest in time to the T1-weighted image was used to calculate framewise displacement (FD), which was included as a covariate in the statistical models to account for head motion artifacts (Power et al., 2012).

Behavioral data acquisition

Cognition was evaluated by the Brief Assessment of Cognition in Schizophrenia (BACS) (Keefe et al., 2004, Keefe et al., 2008). This battery includes subtests assessing verbal memory, working memory, motor speed, verbal fluency, attention/processing speed, and executive function. Scores are standardized by age and gender to produce a cognition composite score.

MRI data preprocessing

The Automatic Cerebellum Anatomical Parcellation using U-Net with Locally Constrained Optimization (ACAPULCO) algorithm was used to isolate the cerebellum in the T1-wighted images (Han et al., 2020). Next, the Spatially Unbiased Infratentorial Template (SUIT) toolbox (Diedrichsen, 2006) was used to normalize the cerebellum into the SUIT space and segment the cerebellum into gray and white matter, using Statistical Parametric Mapping (SPM), version 12 (https://www.fil.ion.ucl.ac.uk/spm/). The segmented gray matter images were modulated using the Jacobian determinants derived from spatial normalization, thereby preserving regional tissue volumes. Consequently, the resulting gray matter maps reflect local gray matter volume rather than tissue concentration (intensity). All structural MRI data underwent rigorous quality assurance procedures. In addition to the automated quality control outputs provided by the ENIGMA Cerebellum Volumetrics Pipeline, each subject’s cerebellar segmentation and spatial normalization were visually inspected. This inspection specifically assessed the accuracy of cerebellar isolation, registration quality, and completeness of coverage of all cerebellar regions, including the vermis and inferior cerebellar structures. Seven participants were excluded due to improper isolation (3 scans), insufficient segmentation (1 scan), or image quality (3 scans). SUIT and ACAPULCO were used via a pipeline established by the ENIGMA-Ataxia initiative (Harding et al., 2021). Images were smoothed using a 3-mm full-width at half-maximum (FWHM) Gaussian kernel.

Total intracranial volume (TIV) was not included as a covariate in the primary SBM analysis. This is because SBM is an ICA-based approach that identifies spatially covarying patterns of gray matter variation across subjects. The resulting subject loadings reflect the expression of distributed covariance networks rather than absolute regional or global brain volume measures. Therefore, global scaling factors such as TIV are not typically treated as confounds in SBM, in contrast to voxel-based or surface-based morphometric analyses where voxel-wise intensity or vertex-wise thickness is directly modeled.”.

Data analysis

Methodological details on SBM can be found elsewhere (Xu et al., 2009). In brief, the SBM module of the Group ICA for fMRI Toolbox (GIFT; https://trendscenter.org/software/gift/) was used. Estimation of the appropriate number of independent components to capture the variability in the dataset was performed using a minimum description length (MDL) method (Li et al., 2007, Rissanen, 1978) and was determined to be 30. MDL is an information-theoretic approach to model selection. It formalizes the idea that the best model is the one that provides the shortest overall description of the data, balancing goodness of fit with model complexity. In practice, MDL minimizes the total code length required to encode both the model itself and the data given the model. This discourages overfitting by penalizing overly complex models that do not substantially improve explanatory power. Thus, MDL selects models that achieve an optimal trade-off between simplicity and accuracy, often aligning closely with principles such as Occam’s razor. We implemented the decomposition within the independent vector analysis with Gaussian–Laplace priors (IVA-GL) framework even though only a single dataset was analyzed. In the single-dataset case, IVA-GL is mathematically equivalent to conventional ICA, but it provides a unified estimation framework that preserves subject-specific variability, enables direct extension to multi-dataset analyses, and ensures methodological consistency across studies. The ICASSO algorithm was used to run the ICA decomposition 100 times to ensure the reliability and stability of the evaluated components (Himberg et al., 2004).

Univariate general linear models (GLM) with group affiliation (SZ, BDwP, or HC) as independent variable, ICA loading coefficients as dependent variables, and age and gender as covariates were used. Bonferroni correction at α = 0.05 was used to adjust the p values for multiple comparisons. FD was calculated to quantify head motion using the method implemented in fMRIPrep (11). The mean FD from the resting-state fMRI acquired in the same session as the structural scan was included as a covariate in a statistical model to control for head motion.

Thresholding and neuroanatomical characterization of a selected independent component (IC) from a spatial brain map was obtained via an in-house MATLAB script. We thresholded the IC map at a specified z-score, identified significant clusters within the thresholded map, and extracted peak coordinates for each cluster. We then mapped these peaks to cerebellar anatomical labels using the SUIT atlas and generated both a tabular output of peak coordinates with their corresponding anatomical labels and a flatmap visualization of the thresholded IC on the cerebellar surface. For visualization of the spatial maps of SBM components, voxel-wise z-scores were thresholded at |z| > 2.5. This threshold was applied solely for display purposes to highlight the most prominent spatial patterns. All statistical analyses, including estimation of component structure and group comparisons, were performed on the full, unthresholded component maps without applying any spatial threshold. For creating the masks for the voxel-based morphometry, a |z| > 2.5 was used which is commonly adopted in the neuroimaging literature as a conservative yet still descriptive cutoff to balance sensitivity and specificity.

To determine whether positive or negative loading coefficients of the cerebellar structural network contributed to group differences, we generated separate binary masks for positive (z > 2.5) and negative (z < −2.5) coefficients. These masks were then applied to the preprocessed images to calculate the GM volume within the masked cerebellar areas.

We conducted a mediation causal analysis to test whether cognition (measured by BACS) mediated the relationship between group affiliation (SZ, BDwP, or HC) and loading coefficients. The analysis controlled for age and gender. We used the mediation package in R (Team, 2021) to quantify the average causal mediated effect (ACME), average direct effect (ADE), and total effects via nonparametric bootstrap sampling.

Visualization of MRI images were done by FSLeyes (1.12.6; FMRIB, Oxford, UK, https://git.fmrib.ox.ac.uk/fsl/fsleyes/fsleyes/), MATLAB, and SPM12. Statistical analyses were performed using R and SPSS (IBM Corp., 2016).

Results

Group differences in independent component loadings

One IC showed statistically significant difference across groups, F(2, 161) = 7.46, p = 0.024 (Bonferroni-corrected), η2p = 0.085. Estimated marginal means indicated that the SZ group showed higher loading coefficient values (mean ± standard error: 0.296 ± 0.102) compared with the BDwP group (−0.192 ± 0.165) and the HC group (−0.296 ± 0.128). Bonferroni-corrected post-hoc comparisons revealed that IC values were significantly higher in the SZ group than in both BDwP (p = 0.041) and HC (p = 0.001) groups. The BDwP and HC groups did not significantly differ (p = 1.00). See Fig. 1A for descriptive means. The positive (z > 2.5) loading coefficients of this structural network predominantly comprised vermis VIIIa, bilateral Crus I, and right IX. See Fig. 1B and C for anatomical details and Supplementary Table 1 for anatomical coordinates. The negative (z < −2.5) loading coefficients of this structural network predominantly comprised bilateral Crus I and bilateral IX. Robustness to demographic covariates and motion

To ensure that the significant group difference in this structural network was not driven by head motion artifacts, a univariate GLM controlling for age, gender, as well as frame-wise displacement was conducted. The group difference remained significant, F(2, 153) = 4.09, p = 0.019, η2p = 0.05. To further ensure that the difference across groups is not driven by age and gender differences across groups, a subset of participants was randomly selected with no significant difference in age or gender, all ps > 0.48. See Supplementary Table 3 for details. The group differences remained significant, F(2, 113) = 3.22, p = 0.04, η2p = 0.06.

Negative-loading regions drive group differences and relate to cognition

The cerebellar regions within the mask derived from positive loading coefficients showed no significant group differences, F(2, 165) = 0.84, p = 0.43, suggesting that these regions did not contribute to the observed GM volume differences across groups within this structural network. In contrast, regions within the mask derived from negative loading coefficients exhibited significant group differences, F(2, 165) = 3.72, p = 0.026, indicating that these regions primarily drove the group-related effects. Specifically, the SZ group showed lower GM volume (0.59 ± 0.01) compared to the BDwP (0.63 ± 0.02) and HC (0.64 ± 0.02) groups. Post-hoc comparisons confirmed significant differences between SZ and BDwP (p = 0.05) and between SZ and HC (p = 0.02), but not between BDwP and HC (p = 0.85; see Fig. 2A).

Associations between cognition, GM volume, and network loadings

Two-tailed Pearson correlations indicated a significant association between the BACS composite scores and GM volume in voxels with a loading coefficient of z < −2.5 (r = 0.27, p < 0.001; see Fig. 2B), as well as the BACS composite scores and loading coefficients of the structural network (r = −0.29, p < 0.001; see Fig. 2C). See Supplementary Table 4 for the correlation of the loading coefficients of the structural network and BACS subscores. As a follow-up, we examined the correlation between BACS composite scores and GM volume in voxels with a loading coefficient of z < −2.5 for each diagnostic group. Only the SZ group showed a significant correlation (r = 0.253, p = 0.020). See Supplementary Table 5 for details.

Cognition did not mediate group differences in network loadings

A causal mediation analysis tested whether cognition (BACS composite score) mediated the relationship between group (SZ, BDwP, or HC) and the loading coefficients, controlling for age and gender. Estimates were obtained using nonparametric bootstrap sampling and 95% confidence intervals. The indirect effect was small and not statistically significant (ACME = −0.027, 95% CI = −0.14 to 0.07), indicating no reliable mediation via cognition. The direct effect of group on loading coefficients remained significant after accounting for cognition (ADE = −0.42, 95% CI = −0.77 to −0.03), and the total effect was also significant (Total = −0.445, 95% CI = −0.75 to −0.05). The proportion of the total effect explained by the indirect path was approximately 6.0% (−0.027/−0.445 ≈ 0.060), but because the ACME was not significant this should not be interpreted as evidence of mediation. In other words, cognitive performance did not significantly mediate group differences in the loading coefficients; significant group differences remained after accounting for cognition, suggesting additional non-cognitive mechanisms. Additional analyses examining individual cognitive subdomains including verbal memory, working memory, motor speed, verbal fluency, attention/processing speed, and executive function yielded no significant indirect effects. These results are presented in Supplementary Table 6.

Discussion

The present study applied SBM to characterize cerebellar GM structural networks across individuals with SZ, BDwP, and HC. One IC significantly differed across groups, with individuals with SZ demonstrating higher loading coefficients relative to both BDwP and HC, reflecting a stronger expression of GM covariance pattern. The BDwP group did not significantly differ from HC. Group effects were primarily driven by negative loading coefficients within this cerebellar structural network. Consistent with these findings, VBM analyses indicated that, compared to BDwP and HC, the SZ group exhibited reduced GM volume within this network, predominantly localized to bilateral Crus I and bilateral lobule IX.

Our findings of reduced bilateral Crus I volumes in SZ corroborate results from a large-scale mega-analysis (Wolfers et al., 2018) as well as more recent studies by He et al. (He et al., 2019) and Rootes-Murdy et al. (Rootes-Murdy et al., 2022). A growing body of literature implicates Crus I in higher-order cognitive processes, including verbal working memory, divided attention, and active maintenance (Bègue et al., 2024, King et al., 2019). Clinically significant deficits within these cognitive domains have been linked to manifestations evinced in psychotic disorders (Andreasen et al., 1998, He et al., 2019, Hirjak et al., 2019a, Moussa-Tooks et al., 2022, Wolf et al., 2020, Wolfers et al., 2018). However, causal mediation analyses indicated that cognition did not significantly mediate the relationship between group membership and structural network loading coefficients. Notably, group differences in network expression remained significant after accounting for cognitive performance, suggesting that cerebellar structural alterations in SZ are not fully explained by cognitive impairment alone and may reflect additional illness-related mechanisms.

Turning to the lower bilateral lobule IX volume observed in SZ, the voxel-based mega-analysis by Moberget et al. (Moberget et al., 2018) likewise reported significant group effects (HC > SZ), indicating reduced volumes in bilateral lobule IX. Moberget et al. further noted that lower GM volume was most pronounced in posterior cerebellar regions. Consistent with these findings, smaller-scale studies have also identified GM deficits in lobule IX, particularly in the left hemisphere (Rasser et al., 2010). Taken together, these results underscore the relevance of lobule IX to SZ neurobiology, a conclusion further supported by evidence of altered functional connectivity involving lobule IX in SZ cohorts (Feng et al., 2022, Kim et al., 2020, Wang et al., 2014).

Unlike SZ, BDwP did not show significant group differences relative to HC. These findings contrast with a limited number of multivariate studies reporting cerebellar structural differences between BD and HC groups (Lapomarda et al., 2021, Rootes-Murdy et al., 2022, Sorella et al., 2019). Notably, however, none of these studies specifically examined individuals with BDwP. One possible explanation for these discrepant findings is offered by Kahn, who proposed that aberrant brain maturation precedes the onset of psychosis (Kahn, 2020). This perspective is supported by a recent large-scale meta-analysis of over 192 studies, which demonstrated that age of onset in SZ spectrum disorders occurs earlier than bipolar-related disorders (Solmi et al., 2022). Such developmental differences may help explain why significant GM reductions were observed in SZ but not in BDwP. Khan further emphasized the distinction between chronological age and the relative age of brain maturation (Kahn, 2020). Using brain-age estimation approaches (Franke et al., 2012, Franke et al., 2010), prior work has shown that individuals with SZ exhibit higher estimated brain ages compared to those with BD (Hajek et al., 2019). This finding aligns with the well-established notion that advancing brain age is associated with GM reductions and other age-related neurobiological changes (Blinkouskaya and Weickenmeier, 2021, Lee and Kim, 2022). Consistent with this framework, the SZ group in the present sample had a higher mean age, which may have contributed to the observed group differences.

Cognitive performance has been linked to GM volume in both the cerebrum and cerebellum (de Chastelaine et al., 2023, Van Rossem et al., 2025). Consistent with this research, cognitive performance via BACS scores were positively correlated with GM volume, with the association in the SZ group being the most pronounced. Likewise, negative correlations were seen between BACS performance and individual subject IC loading coefficients. However, when examining the mediating role cognition may have, there were no significant indirect effects between diagnosis and GM patterns. With that said, when accounting for cognition, there was significance in both direct effects of diagnosis and GM pattern as well as total model effect. These results suggest that another cognition-modulating system may be involved, explaining GM differences.

The finding that cerebellar GMV correlated with BACS performance only in the schizophrenia group may reflect several non-mutually exclusive mechanisms. First, this pattern may be driven by differences in variability and range restriction across groups, with SZ showing greater heterogeneity and impairment in both brain structure and cognition, thereby increasing sensitivity to detect brain–behavior associations, whereas controls exhibit restricted cognitive variance and BDwP may be more heterogeneous or state-dependent. Second, it may indicate some degree of disorder-specific involvement of cerebellar–cortical circuitry in schizophrenia, consistent with models of dysconnectivity and “cognitive dysmetria,” in which cerebellar integrity is more tightly linked to global cognitive functioning. Finally, statistical factors such as sample size and heterogeneity across diagnostic categories may further contribute to the absence of detectable associations in BD and control groups.

As previously mentioned, SBM provides a multivariate approach when investigating GM morphometry. Future cerebellar studies would benefit from an increased use of multivariate approaches as they allow enhanced retention of inherent structural integrity and covariance. In parallel with this, the present study also benefited by utilizing SUIT space parcellation of the cerebellum (Diedrichsen, 2006), allowing for the detection of cerebellar subregion GM alterations that are often overlooked or grouped together with other regions. Previous SBM analyses in SZ have included the cerebellum as part of the overall analysis, rather than as a separated dataset (Castro et al., 2016, Gupta et al., 2019, Rootes-Murdy et al., 2022, Silva et al., 2024). Those studies have consistently implicated the cerebellum as a critical finding in SZ. However, those studies have tended to find only a few, large cerebellar components that can include inferior cerebrum, while the analyses we have done are constrained to the cerebellum, and are able to identify more nuanced patterns of gray matter covariation within the cerebellum as a result. Thus, by capitalizing on both techniques, refined examination cerebellar contributions can be made as well as potentially addressing inconsistencies in literature.

The importance of these findings lies in their contribution to further delineating the neurobiology of SZ and BD. Ongoing debate persists regarding whether these disorders are dimensionally distinct or exist along a shared neurobiological spectrum (d’Albis and Houenou, 2015, Dines et al., 2024, Sorella et al., 2019). In this study, SBM enabled a data-driven identification of disorder-specific differences that may shape divergent psychopathological trajectories. Moreover, these results provide additional evidence supporting the role of the posterior cerebellum in complex, heterogeneous psychiatric disorders. By leveraging analytic methodologies that examine cerebellar subdivisions, this approach allows for a more nuanced investigation of cerebellar contributions to psychopathology. Taken together, these findings may inform future neuromodulation strategies, suggesting that greater emphasis on posterior cerebellar regions could contribute to symptom amelioration.

There were several limitations in this study. While strengths include a multivariate approach, and accounting for covariates like age, gender, and FD, other covariates such as medication use and treatment history were not accounted for. Studies have shown that when accounting for clinical interventions, those who were undergoing treatment reported higher preservation of grey matter compared to unmedicated individuals (McDonald, 2015, Vita et al., 2015). Additionally, the present study was cross sectional in design. By examining the longitudinal patterns of GM, further inferences can be made about the role of the cerebellum in cognitive related disorders. A limitation of this study is the lack of a formal sensitivity analysis across different model orders. Although the MDL criterion provides a data-driven estimate of optimal dimensionality, ICA solutions may still vary with the number of components due to splitting or merging effects. Future work could assess the stability of the identified components across modest perturbations around the MDL estimate to further evaluate robustness. A major limitation of the present study is that potential confounding variables known to influence cerebellar gray matter volume, including education duration, illness duration, and antipsychotic medication dosage, were not included as covariates in the statistical model. This was primarily due to incomplete availability of these measures across all participants. Consequently, their potential influence on the observed group differences cannot be fully excluded and should be considered when interpreting the findings. The present study used the BACS scores which may not fully capture cerebellar-specific cognitive processes. Although the BACS provides a broad evaluation of global cognition, it may be less sensitive to domains more closely linked to cerebellar function, such as timing, coordination of cognitive processes, and certain aspects of executive control. Future studies would benefit from incorporating cerebellar-targeted cognitive tasks to more precisely assess the relationship between cerebellar structural alterations and cognition. Other limitations of the study include the incomplete availability of medication data across participants, which limits the ability to assess potential effects of psychotropic treatment on cerebellar morphology. In addition, information on psychiatric comorbidities (e.g., OCD, ASD, ADHD) was not systematically collected, which may reduce the specificity of the findings with respect to primary diagnostic categories. Finally, group differences in age, sex distribution, and sample size may have influenced the results; although these variables were included as covariates, residual confounding effects cannot be fully excluded.

Conclusion

Findings from the present study indicate that cerebellar GM alterations are diagnostically specific to schizophrenia as compared to bipolar disorder with a history of psychosis. Moreover, cognitive performance did not significantly mediate group differences in cerebellar GM, suggesting that the observed structural alterations are largely independent of global cognitive impairment. Future studies incorporating a broader range of neurocognitive measures may help clarify whether cognition is truly unrelated to group differences in cerebellar structure. Collectively, these findings underscore the utility of multivariate analytical frameworks for investigating abnormalities in cerebellar morphology and their relationships to clinical profiles across psychotic disorders.
