A two-stage robust non-intrusive load monitoring method against unknown waveform distortions
Authors: Wenlong Guo, Qingquan Luo, Tao Yu, Xiaolei Hu, Yufeng Wu, Minhang Liang, Zhenning Pan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNon-intrusive load monitoring (NILM), an anticipated technology in smart grids, has recently provided innovative solutions for energy savings and electrical safety. However, existing event-based NILM methods often ignore the impact of voltage fluctuations, line losses, and variations in load impedance characteristics during waveform extraction. It causes various distortions in the extracted waveforms that are difficult to collect beforehand, significantly reducing the performance of the load identification model. Therefore, we analyze waveform distortion as multi-source noise interference and propose a two-stage robust non-intrusive load identification method against unknown waveform distortions. The proposed method includes a general denoising module based on a dual-mapping mechanism and a robust load identification model based on non-local residual shrinkage modules. It achieves robust load identification under unknown noise interference by first cyclic denoising distorted waveforms and then enhancing the model’s ability to capture key features. Experiments on three public datasets and one private dataset show that the proposed method maintains the best performance even under low signal-to-noise ratios. The denoising module can also be cascaded with other identification models in a plug-and-play manner to improve robustness. This is particularly desirable for edge-deployed in residential e-bike charging safety monitoring, campus dormitory prohibited-appliance detection, and office energy management.Keywords: Load identification, Unknown waveform distortion, Robustness, Feature masking, Non-local residual shrinkageSubject terms: Energy science and technology, Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

By integrating advanced measurement, communication, and control technologies, smart grids can fully exploit the value of data in various applications1. In recent years, non-intrusive load monitoring (NILM) has become an indispensable bridge between smart grids and end-users. It was first proposed by Hart to monitor appliance-level energy consumption information of householders by analyzing aggregated data only2. Currently, NILM has been applied in many fields, such as energy management3, ambient assisted living4, and electrical accident prevention5.

Event-based NILM has garnered significant attention due to its low computational resource requirements, short response time, and ability to identify multiple appliances6–8. It involves three stages: event detection, waveform extraction, and load identification. Event detection captures the moment of appliance status change by monitoring variations in aggregated data. In waveform extraction, the voltage phases of the stable aggregated data before and after the event are first aligned. Then, the current waveform of a single appliance is extracted by subtracting the two data segments. Finally, load identification maps the waveform to the type of appliance.

Existing event-based load identification research centers on event detection and identification methods9,10. The former focuses on detecting event moments more accurately, while the latter seeks to improve identification performance. However, few studies have discussed the waveform extraction stage and analyzed its impact on subsequent identification. Prior waveform extraction methods generally depend on three basic assumptions: the linear superposition of current6, constant grid voltage7, and constant appliance impedance characteristics8. Therefore, the waveforms of a single appliance can be extracted by simply subtracting the aggregated data before and after the event.

Nevertheless, satisfying the above assumptions in practical applications is difficult for the following reasons. For the first assumption, the total current is not a simple linear sum of all appliance currents due to circuit losses11. In addition, interactions between fluctuations in the sampling frequency and the grid frequency make it difficult to align the waveforms before and after the event for point-by-point subtraction12. For the second assumption, the grid voltage is not constant due to inherent operational fluctuations and the fluctuations caused by appliance state transitions13,14. For the third assumption, the impedance characteristics of type III appliances with continuously changing operating states are not constant15. As the above assumptions cannot be satisfied, the waveform extraction stage is susceptible to multiple interferences, causing distortions in the extracted waveforms.

Seriously, the diversity of the distortions increases as the variety of appliances grows16. This diversity is difficult to avoid, even though distorted waveforms have been collected from the known appliance combinations (i.e., all appliances operating simultaneously on the household bus). For instance, frequent on-off or mode switching causes current fluctuations with variable amplitudes17. The diverse combinations of operating modes in multi-mode appliances produce various current superposition effects, complicating waveform distortion18. Furthermore, unknown combinations of appliances with more varied operating modes may cause additional interference in waveform extraction19, exacerbating the diversity of distortions. In practical applications, collecting the distorted waveforms of the target appliances in advance from all appliance combinations is impossible. Based on this, we define unknown waveform distortion as unpredictable nonlinear disturbances caused by appliance combinations, grid interference, user behavior, and other real-world factors. These distortions are non-stationary, difficult to model analytically. Therefore, the unknown distortions violate the machine learning assumption that training and test data are independently and identically distributed, potentially leading to a decline in identification accuracy20.

In recent years, numerous load identification methods have been proposed. Deep learning-based methods, such as temporal convolutional networks7, long short-term memory networks21, and convolutional neural networks10,22, have become dominant due to their superior performance. However, most load identification methods are tested on datasets where only a single appliance is running or a few appliances are running together with slight distortions. This is incompatible with real-world complex appliance usage and may lead to overly optimistic load identification performance6. Some researchers have considered sampling noise in raw waveforms and have used simple methods such as Gaussian sequence convolution10, mean filters7, high-brightness encoding23, and adjusting model parameters24 to mitigate its impact. But these methods show poor robustness when handling waveform distortions far more complex than sampling noise. Only a few studies have examined waveform distortions from known appliance combinations. For example, Mukaroh et al.25 use a generative adversarial network to create aggregate distorted waveforms of known appliance combinations. Wu et al.26 mitigate waveform distortion by separating target load features in known appliance combinations using spectrogram differences before and after the event. Nevertheless, these solutions lack generalizability to unknown waveform distortions, limiting their practicality.

To specifically analyze the waveform distortions, we divide the distorted waveforms into two parts: noise and clean waveforms. Inspired by the field of artificial intelligence, load identification performance can be improved by eliminating waveform noise and enhancing the model’s robustness to distorted waveforms. Deep learning-based noise-robust methods have been widely researched in speech recognition27 and seismic data processing28. However, they typically rely on large amounts of paired real noise and clean data for training, applying them directly to NILM is challenging. This is because waveform distortions are closely related to appliances’ operating conditions and overlap the clean waveform in frequency, which is unique to NILM. Due to the diversity of distortions and the cost of data collection, it is difficult to collect a large number of distorted waveforms in advance. Therefore, we aim to utilize only known clean waveforms to denoise distorted waveforms and improve the robustness of the identification model. In practice, the nonstationary noise ε arises from several common factors. (i) Switching high-power appliances on or off can cause a noticeable voltage shift between the pre- and post-event steady states. This shift introduces a voltage-related term, ε1 (corresponding to ∆U). Its effect is evident in Fig. 1(d), which shows a marked amplitude deviation after the switching event. (ii) Aging wiring, long feeders, and contact resistance increase circuit losses. As a result, the aggregated current deviates from the assumed linear superposition, giving rise to ε2 (associated with line losses). Since such circuit-related losses are inherent in practical systems, their influence typically manifests as subtle waveform deviations and small-amplitude fluctuations, which can be observed across all subfigures in Fig. 1. (iii) In real-world deployments, background loads may change their operating states within the event window. Continuously varying appliances (e.g., air conditioners, computers, and other power-electronic loads) may exhibit different current waveforms before and after the event. These background variations are incorporated into the extracted switching waveform and often dominate the observed distortion, leading to ε3 (associated with changes in appliance impedance). This phenomenon is illustrated in Fig. 1(a)-(c), where waveform discrepancies cannot be solely attributed to simple voltage shifts or line losses. Because these factors depend on appliance combinations and operating conditions, the resulting distortions are diverse and cannot be fully characterized in advance.

Consequently, it is impractical to collect distorted waveforms that cover all possible appliance combinations. In this paper, we use the term “unknown” in a practical sense. Specifically, “unknown waveform distortions” refer to real distorted waveforms extracted from appliance combinations and operating conditions that are not observed during training. Therefore, the corresponding distortion component ε is unseen during training.

Denoising methods

Although methods for handling waveform distortion are lacking in NILM, there is an increasing demand for denoising in speech signal processing. Many effective denoising methods have been developed to reduce background noise and enhance signal quality. Traditional denoising methods are only effective in certain noise environments and for steady-state signals29, making them less suitable for non-stationary noise. Recently, deep learning-based methods have outperformed traditional methods. These methods typically use an encoder-mask module-decoder framework to optimize the mask module for different denoising tasks. Models such as end-to-end denoising networks30, time-frequency mask-based deep neural networks31, and transformer-based denoising networks32 have demonstrated superior performance. Additionally, certain speech separation techniques33,34, based on principles similar to those of denoising, have also proven effective.

Despite these methods are designed primarily for speech signal processing, they show potential for addressing waveform distortions. However, speech signals exhibit distinct time- and frequency-domain characteristics that differ from noise, while clean appliance waveforms in NILM lack such clear distinctions. These methods are intended to restore various clean speech signals, requiring extensive real distorted data for training to enhance model generalization and practical performance. Therefore, adjustments are needed to better align these methods with NILM characteristics.

This section presents the proposed two-stage robust load identification method. The first stage employs a general lightweight denoising module to improve the recognizability of distorted waveform y by reducing the noise ε. As shown in the upper part of Fig. 2, we train a denoising module using well-labeled synthetic data generated through a training strategy based on clean appliance-level waveforms x, and generalize it to unknown distorted waveforms. In the second stage, feature extraction with adaptive soft thresholding and the classification components together form a robust identification model, enhancing the identification process by capturing key features.

General denoising module

Existing event-based load identification methods rely on observed waveform patterns, which leads to inaccuracies when identifying distorted waveforms. To address this problem, we propose a general denoising module. The key idea is a dual-mapping mechanism: from distorted waveform to estimated noise , and from denoised waveform (obtained as ) to clean waveform . The former captures the invariant clean features of the target appliance, while the latter fine-tunes the denoised waveform. Unlike speech-denoising methods, our method estimates noise by masking invariant clean features and indirectly restores the clean waveform. Therefore, the proposed denoising module requires only a small amount of synthetic data for model training, even when facing unknown noise in complex appliance combinations. Additionally, we employ a cyclic denoising strategy that allows the lightweight model to achieve denoising performance comparable to large speech-denoising models. Robust identification model based on non-local residual shrinkage module

The denoising module effectively reduces noise, providing cleaner inputs for the second stage. Inspired by the concept of soft thresholding in neural networks35, we propose a robust identification model, the non-local residual shrinkage neural network (NLRS-Net). Our model can capture key features of the appliance and remove noisy features. This capability allows the model to adapt to more complex waveform patterns, enhancing identification robustness. Firstly, it uses a 1D-Conv followed by n + 1 stacked NLRS for feature extraction. The extracted key features are then fed into a two-layer linear classifier to get the appliance label. The NLRS module employs a dual attention mechanism and soft thresholding to capture key features. This enables it to effectively ignore distortion effects, improving appliance identification accuracy. As shown in Fig. 4, the NLRS is built on a residual network. An adaptive threshold network calculates the threshold, followed by soft thresholding to filter the residual network’s output.

Structure of the non-local residual shrinkage module. It consists of a two-layer 1D-Conv residual block and an adaptive threshold module. The adaptive threshold module combines spatial attention and channel attention to compute channel-wise thresholds, which are used in the subsequent soft-thresholding operation for feature shrinkage.

The residual network includes two identical 1D-Convs and a skip connection, which further extract distorted waveform features. Each layer is followed by batch normalization to accelerate training and prevent overfitting.

The adaptive threshold network first computes the feature vector s(x) with spatial attention characteristics using the spatial attention module. The spatial attention module extracts features through 1D-Conv and compresses them along the channel dimension, generating a one-dimensional feature vector of length L. It then combines positional information from all load features to construct an attention vector using the softmax function, enhancing correlations between positions. The vector is multiplied by the output of the 1D-Conv and fed into the channel attention mechanism, optimizing the effectiveness of threshold evaluation.

Furthermore, we assess the impact on the threshold of all feature values within a single channel by performing absolute value operation and global average pooling on the feature channels. Then two linear layers are used for adaptive threshold learning, generating a set of coefficients nc ranging between 0 and 1 via the sigmoid function. After calculating the threshold, we apply soft thresholding to the output features of the second 1D-Conv. It converts noise features to a range close to zero.

Training strategy

To guide the denoising module in learning the feature masking strategy mentioned earlier, we propose a training strategy based on synthetic data to train using only known appliance-level waveforms. As shown in the upper right part of Fig. 2, we add two types of random noise with a specified SNR to the clean waveform: Gaussian and random walk noise36. For the denoising module, the noise type is variable during training. The training strategy encourages the module to focus on masking low-fluctuation clean waveform features in synthetic waveforms. It is not intended to fit any real distortion distribution. As a result, the denoising module can achieve robust performance and generalize to real unknown distortions caused by appliance combinations that are not observed during training. Experimental settings

Datasets and data processing

To validate the proposed method, four datasets are used for our experiments: LIT38, private dataset, PLAID39, and WHITED40. The first two datasets are used to evaluate the model’s robustness in identifying unknown distorted waveforms. The synthetic subset in the LIT dataset includes 81 appliance combinations of 26 different appliances. It contains 30 s of aggregated measurements sampled at 15.36 kHz. The subset precisely records the switching timestamps of each appliance, allowing up to 8 appliances to operate simultaneously. It includes complex and diverse waveform distortions due to different appliance combinations, making it applicable to our work. The private dataset measures the aggregate data using a smart plug with high-frequency sampling. The data is transmitted in real-time via serial communication to a computer for storage. The proposed measurement is shown in Fig. 5, operating under the 220 V, 50 Hz grid conditions and sampled at 7.5 kHz in Guizhou, China. Background appliances operate on the power strip before the event to simulate a complex real-world environment. The target appliance is then connected to collect aggregate current and voltage data. Finally, individual measurements of 12 different appliances are collected over 7 s intervals. Referring to the experimental setup of LIT, the private dataset also includes 60 different appliance combinations. Additionally, following the experimental setups of existing load identification studies6,7,23, we use the PLAID and WHITED datasets to evaluate the performance of the proposed NLRS-Net in identifying clean appliance-level waveforms.

The measurement setup of the private dataset. (a) Sampling scenario for the appliance combination containing three appliances. (b) Sampling topology for different appliance combinations.

In data processing, we directly extract waveforms by subtracting the steady-state aggregate current before and after events based on the timestamps recorded in the LIT and private datasets. The LIT dataset includes 20 randomly selected cycles for each appliance combination, totaling 1520 extracted waveforms and 500 appliance-level waveforms. The private dataset includes 30 samples of randomly selected cycles for each appliance combination, totaling 1800 extracted waveforms and 360 appliance-level waveforms. Due to the similar external characteristics and usage of some appliances, we regard the LED Lamp, LED Panel, Phone Charger Asus, Phone Charger Alcatel, and Laptop Lenovo collectively as Power Electronic Devices (PED), the Incandescent Lamp and Resistor as the Lamp, and retain only the F1 operating mode of the HairDryer Eleganza. Finally, we downsample the voltage and current of all datasets using polynomial interpolation to 150 sample points as the model input6,7,23.

Experimental setup for appliance-level waveform identification

To demonstrate that the proposed identification model achieves the same level of clean appliance-level waveform identification as representative studies, 10-fold cross-validation is performed in the PLAID and WHITED datasets. We compare NLRS-Net to other recent advanced methods: adaptive weighted recurrence graph (AWRG)6, learnable recurrent graph (LRG)7, and color-encoded V-I trajectory (V-I)23. Their experimental settings are the same as those of their original papers except for the differences in the training and testing sets. Both the training and testing sets consist of clean appliance-level waveforms. The proposed NLRS-Net uses an initial learning rate of 0.01, which is then multiplied by 0.9 for every 10 epochs. The total number of training epochs is 300, and the batch size is 256. The number of NILRS modules is set to 3.

Experimental setup for robustness evaluation

To verify the robustness of the proposed two-stage method for unknown distorted waveforms, experiments are designed on both the LIT and private datasets. It consists of three parts of the experiment. All experiments use unknown distorted waveforms extracted from different appliance combinations as the testing set, which are only used for testing. In each experiment, all competing methods and the proposed method are trained under the same training conditions specified for that experiment and evaluated on the same test set, ensuring a controlled and fair comparison.

Experiment 1 (one-stage, only IM): Basic robustness evaluation. Here, “basic” denotes that the evaluation focuses solely on the inherent robustness of the identification model (IM) architectures under clean-training conditions, without applying synthetic-data training strategies (TS) or denoising modules (DM). All identification models (including competing and proposed models) are trained on clean appliance-level waveforms and evaluated on the aforementioned test set. This experiment serves as a reference setting for Experiment 2.

Experiment 2: Two-stage robustness evaluation. We generate the training set using the proposed training strategy. It is then used to individually train the denoising module and the identification model. All identification models are retrained using the same TS-generated training set in this experiment. During the testing phase, we implement a two-stage testing process: (a) (one-stage, IM + TS) The identification model is tested directly using the test set to observe performance changes due to variations in the training data introduced by the training strategy. (b) (two-stage, DM + IM+TS) The identification model is cascaded with the denoising module to assess how the denoising module reduces the impact of waveform distortions on identification performance. Here, IM + TS and DM + IM+TS are applied in the same manner to each identification model, so that (a) isolates the effect of TS and (b) quantifies the additional benefit brought by DM under controlled conditions.

Experiment 3: Robustness evaluation for different levels of distortion. It aims to observe the performance variation of different methods in various distortions. Building on Experiment 2, we group the extracted waveforms according to SNR. Given that waveform features are almost completely lost at SNR 0 dB, we group these waveforms as 0 dB. Finally, different groups are tested using the identification model cascaded with the denoising module.

In the above experiments, the proposed method uses the stochastic gradient descent (SGD) optimizer combined with a cyclic learning rate scheduler for training. The specific strategy involves multiplying the current maximum learning rate by 0.9 after every 10 epochs, gradually reducing it from 0.02 to 0.001. To avoid overfitting, an early stopping strategy is implemented. If the model’s performance on the validation set does not improve for 20 consecutive epochs after training for 50 epochs, the training is stopped. The validation set comprises 20% of the data split from the training set. All testing is performed by calculating the average performance across 10 iterations for a more convincing result.

In the denoising module, the 1D-Conv parameters for the encoder and decoder are (1,32,3,1) and (32,1,3,1), respectively. The padding mode is set to 1. The parameters of intra-NLRS and inter-NLRS are (32,32,3,1) and (150,150,3,1), respectively. The parameter of the left branch linear layer is (150,150). The denoising iteration T is set to 2.

Experimental setup for evaluating the denoising module

This experiment evaluates the denoising methods from two aspects: (a) denoising performance; (b) performance improvement for load identification. The first reflects the model’s ability to remove noise from unknown distorted waveforms, while the second assesses its impact on non-intrusive load identification. These experiments are conducted on LIT and private datasets. We compare the proposed denoising module with well-established denoising methods of speech denoising: TSTNN32, Conv-TASNET33, and DTLN31. We use the proposed training strategy to generate a synthetic training set, while the test set comprises real-world distorted waveforms extracted from various appliance combinations. The following are the specific experimental procedures.

Evaluation of denoising performance: First, all denoising models are trained using the synthetic training set. The experimental settings for the comparison models follow those in the original paper. Since test samples with an SNR above 20 dB have minimal impact on the identification model, we focus only on samples with an SNR below 20 dB in this experiment. These samples are further divided into two segments: SNR < 10 dB and 10 dB ≤ SNR < 20 dB, to better evaluate the model’s denoising performance at different distortion levels.

Evaluation of improvements in identification: Building upon the above experiments, we further train the proposed robust identification model using the synthetic training set. Next, the above two-stage testing process is applied to the test set. Additionally, we use the identification performance of Experiment 1 in robustness evaluation as a baseline.

Experimental setup for ablation experiments

To verify the effectiveness of the proposed NLRS structure, we ablate the spatial attention network and the adaptive thresholding network to construct different identification models, Ours/CW and Ours/RBU. Subsequently, we evaluate their performance on the appliance-level waveforms in the PLAID and WHITED datasets and conduct a basic robustness evaluation using the LIT dataset.

Results of two-stage robustness evaluation (one-stage, IM + TS): Table 2 shows the direct test results of the identification model in Experiment 2. In the LIT dataset, 5.86% of the extracted waveforms are completely distorted (SNR 0 dB) and 5.85% are severely distorted (0 dB < SNR 5 dB), introducing more noise than clean waveforms. It results in the key features being blurred and difficult to identify. Consequently, the NLRS-Net and LRG show minor improvements. Nevertheless, even with the training strategy applied, the best-performing competing method remains 1.25% and 2.91% lower than the proposed method in terms of ACC on the LIT and private datasets, respectively. Meanwhile, AWRG and V-I, which have lower performance, exhibit more significant gains. For instance, AWRG shows a 9.34% increase in F1-macro compared to the baseline. This improvement is due to the training strategy, which enhances data diversity and helps the model learn more waveform patterns. However, severe distortions still cannot be fully handled. It necessitates further denoising of waveforms to enhance their quality for improved identification.

Results of two-stage robustness evaluation (two-stage, DM + IM + TS): Experiment 2 (b) confirms that the denoising module improves the recognizability of distorted waveforms by reducing noise, alleviating identifying problems caused by severe distortions. As shown in Table 2, the proposed method achieves 95.85% accuracy on the LIT dataset and 98.88% on the private dataset. This corresponds to an overall improvement of 2.69% and 3.65% over the single-stage baseline on the LIT and private datasets, respectively. Although the F1-macro on the LIT dataset is slightly lower than that on LRG, the proposed method still excels in other performance metrics, demonstrating greater robustness. It is worth noting that the proposed denoising module is not only effective for our method but also boosts the performance of LRG and AWRG. The F1-macro of AWRG improves by 19.24% compared to the baseline and further improves by 9.9% compared to (a). It indicates that the denoising module has a degree of generality and benefits different methods. However, V-I does not perform well. The reason is that the V-I trajectory in Liu et al.23 relies on the reactive currents. It is hard to distinguish among V-I trajectories of different appliances in the LIT dataset, as most appliances have low reactive currents. Additionally, the denoising module aims to reduce noise in the complete current, leading to less pronounced reactive power characteristics of the denoised waveforms.

The detailed robustness improvement of the identification models can be analyzed through the F1-macro for each appliance. Figure 6 presents the F1-macro gain due to the denoising module and training strategy. It lists the five appliances with the most significant gains of different methods. The proposed method achieves a gain in robustness of over 10% for most appliances compared to the baseline. Additionally, the denoising module can enhance the model’s robustness beyond the training strategy for most appliances. This is because the denoising module nonlinearly corrects waveform distortions to improve the waveform’s recognizability, as shown in Fig. 7. According to the SNR metric, the denoising module improves the quality of distorted waveforms by approximately 112%, increasing the SNR from 7.18 dB to 15.2 dB. With the high diversity of unknown waveform distortions, it is difficult to remove the noise completely. Therefore, we enhance the identification model’s ability to capture key features to better identify denoised waveforms. Notably, in AWRG and LRG, the improvement of the HairDryer Parlux - Fan1 is limited when only training with the training strategy. However, cascading the denoising module significantly improves performance. It highlights the importance of the denoising module for improving model robustness.

In contrast, for Microwave Standby and Air Heater Nilko, the F1-macro gains are lower after cascading the denoising module compared to training with the strategy alone. Analysis of the waveforms reveals that the denoised waveforms of these appliances are too similar to the waveforms of other appliances, causing confusion and misclassification. This is because appliances with similar external characteristics but different labels lead to the problem of label noise20. It is a common challenge in load identification. Nonetheless, the denoising module still improves the performance of the different methods for most appliances and stabilizes it at a higher level than the baseline.

Further analysis reveals the effect of denoising iterations on both denoising performance and identification accuracy. As shown in Fig. 8, we track the changes in waveform SNR and accuracy on the LIT dataset with increasing iterations. When the number of iterations exceeds two, the improvement in both SNR and accuracy diminishes, and accuracy starts to decline after the third iteration. Balancing performance gains and computational cost, we choose two iterations for all two-stage experiments. Additionally, since the identification accuracy of our method on the private dataset reaches nearly 99% after the second denoising iteration, further iterations offer limited improvements. This confirms that the two-iteration denoising process proposed in this paper is effective across different real-world scenarios. Considering the balance between performance and computational cost, we recommend using a fixed two-iteration denoising process for practical applications.

Discussion and conclusion

To address the decline in performance caused by unknown waveform distortions in practical NILM, we provide a two-stage robust load identification method against unknown waveform distortions. In the first stage, it improves the waveform recognizability by removing noise from the distorted waveforms with a denoising module. In the second stage, it employs NLRS-Net to precisely capture the key features of the denoised waveforms to achieve robust load identification. Unlike existing methods, the proposed method does not require the prior collection of unknown distorted waveforms in complex electricity scenarios with multiple appliances. Experimental results demonstrate consistent and substantial improvements over representative methods. The key findings are summarized as follows. (1) The proposed two-stage framework achieves 95.85% and 98.88% accuracy on the LIT and private datasets, respectively, under unknown waveform distortions. Compared with representative methods, it improves the F1-macro by up to 40.9% and 42.44% on the two datasets. (2) The general denoising module enhances robustness across different models. For example, it increases the F1-macro of AWRG on the LIT dataset from 65.83% to 85.07%. Under low-SNR conditions (SNR < 10 dB), the module increases the waveform SNR by 147% and the SISNR by 309%. (3) The proposed method achieves 100% accuracy at SNR values above 12 dB, whereas other methods require more than 20 dB. Additionally, NLRS-Net contains only 103k parameters and requires 9 × 10⁻⁴ s per inference, demonstrating its suitability for real-time edge deployment.

Despite its promising performance, several limitations should be noted. First, the framework is designed for end-to-end modeling of one-dimensional current waveforms and may require architectural modifications to integrate with image-based or multimodal NILM approaches. Second, although validated on multiple datasets with complex appliance combinations, its long-term robustness under continuous real-world deployment with evolving usage patterns warrants further investigation. Third, extreme distortions that obscure key steady-state characteristics may still lead to classification ambiguity.

To address these limitations, future work will focus on three directions. First, we will explore advanced image-based techniques to further enhance the denoising module. Second, we will further validate robustness through edge deployment in more complex real-world scenarios, including (i) household e-bike charging safety monitoring for fire-risk prevention, (ii) dormitory monitoring of prohibited appliances for electrical safety, and (iii) office energy management of typical equipment. In these scenarios, the aggregated current often comprises diverse and complex appliance combinations, making waveform extraction more susceptible to unknown distortions. Finally, we will investigate additional discriminative feature-learning strategies to better distinguish appliances with highly similar waveform characteristics and to enhance robustness under extreme distortions.
