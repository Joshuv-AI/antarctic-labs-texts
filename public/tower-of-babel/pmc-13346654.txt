Damage evolution and wellbore stability model of coal seams under the coupled effects of fluctuating pressure and hydration
Authors: Wenbao Zhai, Chao Fang, Qingfeng Guo, Qing Zhao, Zixuan Yang, Qian Wang, Hui Zhang, Jintao An, Kunhong Lv
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDeep coalbed methane horizontal wells frequently undergo cyclic fluctuating pressures induced by drill string/casing movement during hole conditioning and casing running. Under the combined effects of long-term drilling-fluid soaking-induced weakening and pressure disturbances, the borehole wall is prone to progressive damage evolution and instability, yet existing models struggle to quantitatively capture this synergistic behavior. To address this gap, this study systematically investigates the fatigue-damage characteristics of coal under varying confining pressures, loading rates, and loading gradients using a true-triaxial cyclic loading-unloading test system. The results show that cyclic disturbances significantly alter the elastic modulus, peak strength, and failure mode of coal; higher loading rates and steeper loading gradients accelerate damage accumulation, whereas increased confining pressure enhances the load-bearing capacity of coal. Meanwhile, drilling-fluid soaking causes the elastic modulus and compressive strength to continuously decrease with time, and the two types of damage effects can be uniformly characterized using a damage-variable framework. Furthermore, an analytical model is developed for steady-state fluctuating pressure comprising three components-static shear force, viscous drag, and inertial force-and is coupled with the damage variable to establish a total damage model for coal under the combined action of drilling-fluid weakening and fluctuating pressure. By incorporating the Mohr-Coulomb criterion and a single-weak-plane criterion, a wellbore stability analysis method is formulated. Model validation indicates that, as the damage variable increases, the collapse-density and fracture-density windows shrink markedly, leading to a significant deterioration in wellbore stability. A case study of Well X in the western Ordos Basin demonstrates that the proposed model can accurately explain the pump pressure build-up and wellbore pack-off/bridging encountered during casing running at a depth of 4218 m, and reveals that an excessively high casing running speed increases instantaneous fluctuating pressure and triggers the propagation of borehole-wall damage. Finally, recommendations are proposed for optimizing the drilling-fluid density range and controlling casing running speed, providing theoretical support and engineering guidance for safe drilling, managed-pressure cementing, and casing deployment in deep coalbed methane wells.Keywords: Coal rock, True triaxial cyclic loading and unloading, Damage variable, Fluctuating pressure, HydrationSubject terms: Energy science and technology, Engineering

Introduction

Deep coal-rock gas horizontal wells play a pivotal role in China’s efforts to increase reserves and production of unconventional natural gas. However, wellbore instability has long remained a critical technical bottleneck that constrains drilling safety and completion quality. Coal and coal-bearing rocks are inherently characterized by low strength, highly developed fracture systems, pronounced heterogeneity, and strong sensitivity to drilling fluids. Their mechanical properties undergo sustained deterioration under the combined effects of drilling-fluid immersion, hydration-induced softening, and capillary adsorption. During wiper trips and casing-running operations, swab and surge pressures generated by drill string reciprocation subject the wellbore to frequent cyclic loading and unloading, thereby promoting the initiation, propagation, and progressive coalescence of microcracks within the coal-rock matrix1,2. Damage evolution driven jointly by drilling-fluid weakening and fluctuating pressure makes the wellbore highly susceptible to complications such as borehole shrinkage, pipe sticking, pump-pressure increase, and even borehole burial events. Conventional wellbore stability design relies primarily on static triaxial mechanical parameters and single-event failure criteria, which are insufficient to capture the progressive damage accumulation arising from the coupled effects of cyclic loading and fluid invasion/erosion3. Consequently, such approaches cannot meet the demand for accurate wellbore stability prediction in deep coal-rock gas wells. Therefore, establishing a stability analysis framework capable of describing the full process of “drilling-fluid weakening-pressure disturbance-damage evolution-wellbore instability” is of great significance for enhancing safe drilling capability and reducing the incidence of complex downhole accidents in deep coal-rock gas development4,5.

In terms of fluctuating pressure, overseas research on swab/surge pressure during tripping operations started relatively early and has led to transient-dynamics-based prediction models that can accurately capture how drill string velocity, acceleration, and drilling-fluid rheology affect pressure peaks. However, such models typically rely heavily on downhole real-time monitoring data, which limits their practicality for widespread field deployment. To improve engineering applicability, some studies have simplified these approaches into steady-state or quasi-steady-state models, in which the fluctuating pressure is decomposed into three components-static shear force, viscous resistance, and inertial force-so that pressure amplitudes can be estimated using measurable parameters, making them more suitable for rapid on-site evaluation6,7. Regarding drilling-fluid-induced weakening, extensive studies have been conducted worldwide on the triaxial mechanical behavior of shale under soaking conditions, whereas comparatively limited attention has been paid to coal rocks with well-developed fractures and complex structures8,9. Coal rocks contain pervasive cleats and exhibit strong pore connectivity; consequently, drilling fluids can more readily invade under the combined action of hydraulic pressure differential and capillary forces, thereby accelerating microcrack growth. Their mechanical degradation patterns differ markedly from those of shale, and existing weakening models cannot be directly transferred4. With respect to cyclic loading, previous studies have shown that rocks under repeated loading exhibit fatigue-related responses such as reductions in elastic modulus, decreases in peak strength, and changes in hysteresis behavior10,11. Nevertheless, most investigations have focused on uniaxial or conventional triaxial conditions, with insufficient consideration of the true three-dimensional in-situ stress environment and the staged cyclic disturbances encountered downhole12–14. Although current wellbore stability models have widely adopted transversely isotropic elasticity, the Mohr-Coulomb criterion, and single-weak-plane failure criteria for stability evaluation, most of them still take static mechanical parameters as inputs and lack a damage-evolution-driven mechanism for updating these parameters8. A few studies have introduced damage mechanics concepts, but they are commonly based on monotonic test data and therefore cannot capture the full fatigue-damage process of coal rocks under fluctuating pressure4,8. Moreover, damage variables have not yet been explicitly linked to the evolution of collapse and fracture pressures in a clear mathematical form, and systematic research on how the wellbore stability mud-weight window dynamically changes with damage accumulation remains scarce. From an engineering perspective, current field decisions on wiper-trip and casing-running risks are largely based on experience or single-factor assessments15. A quantitative, predictive, and optimizable theoretical framework is still lacking, which prevents the provision of clear guidance for mud-weight adjustment, casing-running speed control, and managed-pressure cementing operations.

In summary, existing studies still exhibit three key limitations. First, there is a lack of a unified damage characterization framework that can simultaneously describe time-dependent drilling-fluid weakening and periodic pressure disturbances. Second, cyclic loading-unloading test data that replicate pressure variations during tripping, wiper trips, and casing running under true triaxial stress conditions are still scarce, which constrains the establishment of a quantitative relationship between fluctuating pressure and coal-rock fatigue damage. Third, current wellbore stability models have not yet achieved deep coupling with damage variables, making it difficult to accurately predict how collapse and fracture pressures evolve with damage progression16. To address these gaps, this study focuses on wellbore instability during the typical wiper-trip and casing-running stages in deep coal-rock gas wells. First, true triaxial cyclic loading-unloading experiments are conducted to elucidate the effects of loading rate, loading gradient, and confining pressure on hysteresis characteristics, elastic modulus degradation, and strength evolution of coal rocks. Subsequently, a drilling-fluid weakening damage model and a fluctuating-pressure-induced damage model are developed, and a unified formulation of the total damage variable is proposed. The damage variable is then incorporated into the Mohr-Coulomb criterion and the single-weak-plane criterion to establish a damage-degradation-informed wellbore stability analysis method. Finally, the proposed method is validated using casing-running field conditions from Well X in the western Ordos Basin, where the instability mechanism at a depth of 4218 m is interpreted and optimization recommendations are provided for both mud weight and casing-running speed. The outcomes of this work provide theoretical tools and practical guidance for safe drilling, managed-pressure cementing, and completion operations in deep coal-rock gas wells.

Experimental scheme and result analysis

Experimental scheme

Experimental materials

The coal-rock cores used in the experiments were prepared by wire cutting to a uniform size of 50 mm × 50 mm × 100 mm, yielding a total of 30 groups of specimens. The coal-rock specimen used in the experiment is shown in Fig. 1. To ensure comparability of the test results, core selection followed consistency criteria in terms of density, integrity, and acoustic wave velocity. Macroscopic structural observations indicate that the samples are mainly composed of bright coal and semi-bright coal; some specimens contain semi-dull and dull coal, featuring relatively high inorganic mineral content that is distributed in banded patterns. The coal-rock is naturally characterized by pre-existing microcracks and cleats, exhibiting pronounced brittleness and anisotropy. These structural features provide inherent pathways for fatigue damage induced by cyclic loading.

Experimental device

The experiments were conducted using the SPAX-2000 true triaxial rock testing system, as shown in Fig. 2. This system is equipped with three independent loading units, enabling precise control of the maximum, intermediate, and minimum principal stresses, thereby reproducing loading conditions consistent with the three-dimensional in-situ stress environment downhole. The apparatus integrates high-precision force-displacement sensors, a closed-loop servo control system, and an automated data acquisition module, which together allow real-time recording of stress, strain, and failure characteristics throughout cyclic loading. The main technical specifications include a maximum normal load of 2000 kN and a maximum lateral load of 800 kN, with a force-control accuracy of ≤ 0.05 kN and a displacement resolution of ≤ 0.01 mm. These capabilities satisfy the requirements for investigating cyclic loading-unloading behavior and fatigue damage evolution in coal-rock specimens.

Experimental method

This experimental program was designed to simulate the periodic fluctuating pressure induced by drill string movement during wiper-trip and casing-running operations. True triaxial cyclic loading-unloading was adopted to reproduce the repeated stress disturbances experienced by the surrounding rock around the wellbore. The test procedure consisted of three stages:

Application of initial confining stresses. Stresses were simultaneously applied in the x-, y-, and z-directions at a loading rate of 0.10–0.20 MPa/s. Experimental result analysis

Characteristics of stress-strain curve

The representative stress-strain curves obtained from the cyclic loading-unloading tests are shown in Fig. 4. The curves exhibit a clear three-stage deformation behavior, including the compaction stage, elastic stage, and failure stage. Under cyclic loading, multiple hysteresis loops can also be observed, indicating the accumulation of irreversible plastic deformation and progressive fatigue damage in the coal-rock specimens.

Stress-strain curve.

(1) Compaction stage. During the initial loading, the pre-existing pores and microcracks in the coal rock are progressively compacted. Strain increases faster than stress, resulting in a concave-upward curve. Owing to the high crack density of coal rock, this stage is relatively short.

(2) Elastic stage. After pore compaction is largely completed, the specimen enters a quasi-linear deformation regime, where the stress-strain relationship is approximately linear. This stage reflects the elastic response of the coal-rock matrix and constitutes the longest portion of the overall loading process.

(3) Failure stage and hysteresis behavior. At higher stress levels, cracks begin to initiate, propagate, and coalesce, ultimately triggering macroscopic failure. Under cyclic loading, multiple hysteresis loops appear on the stress-strain curves, indicating the accumulation of irreversible plastic strain. The loops are relatively uniformly distributed, suggesting that damage progressively accumulates under periodic disturbances. Moreover, the good connectivity between curves at successive loading levels implies that the specimens retain a certain degree of structural integrity prior to fatigue failure.

The presence of hysteresis loops indicates that: (1) coal rock behaves as a distinctly non-ideal elastoplastic material, in which recoverable (elastic) and unrecoverable (plastic) deformation coexist during cyclic loading; (2) as the number of cycles increases, the accumulation of plastic strain drives an increase in the damage variable, providing key mechanical evidence for accelerated wellbore degradation under fluctuating-pressure conditions.

Mechanical characteristics

After cyclic loading, the peak strength and elastic modulus exhibit systematic variations with loading rate, loading gradient, and confining pressure. The mechanical parameters obtained after cyclic loading, including peak strength and elastic modulus, are summarized in Table 2. These results show that the post-cyclic mechanical response of coal rock varies systematically with loading rate, loading gradient, and confining pressure. An increased loading gradient implies a larger pressure-fluctuation amplitude at each level, which intensifies stress concentration and promotes earlier crack growth. The results show that peak strength increases with loading gradient, whereas the elastic modulus decreases, indicating stiffness degradation and accelerated damage accumulation in the coal rock. A higher loading rate causes the specimen to experience a larger stress increment within a shorter time, producing an evident “rate effect” in strength, i.e., an increase in peak strength. Meanwhile, rapid loading induces stronger crack disturbances, leading to a slight reduction in elastic modulus. An increase in confining pressure significantly suppresses crack propagation, enabling the specimen to sustain higher failure stresses; accordingly, peak strength increases approximately linearly with confining pressure. Higher confining pressure also steepens the linear segment of the stress-strain curve, resulting in a larger elastic modulus and reflecting enhanced overall stiffness. Overall, loading rate and loading gradient represent the intensity of fluctuating-pressure disturbance, while confining pressure represents the constraint level of the wellbore stress environment. Their combined effects govern the fatigue damage evolution and failure behavior of coal rock, providing an important physical basis for the subsequent development of a damage-variable model.

Fluctuating pressure calculation model

The transient model can capture rapid time-dependent pressure variations with high fidelity, but it requires real-time downhole velocity data. In contrast, the steady-state model relies on parameters that are easier to obtain and has stronger engineering applicability, making it suitable for rapid field calculations17,18. Given that real-time pipe running/pulling velocity is often unavailable and considering the operational characteristics of deep coal-rock gas horizontal wells, a steady-state fluctuating-pressure model is the more appropriate choice. It allows simple and efficient calculations using experimentally obtained rock properties (e.g., uniaxial compressive strength and elastic modulus) and can meet the need for fast response in field operations. For most deep wells and coal-rock gas wells, the steady-state model provides a better balance between accuracy and operability.

Damage variable characterization

Under the coupled effects of drilling-fluid soaking-induced weakening and fluctuating-pressure disturbance, damage accumulates in the wellbore surrounding rock. The associated mechanical degradation is mainly manifested as a reduction in elastic modulus and a decrease in peak strength. Accordingly, this study develops a drilling-fluid-weakening-based damage variable model and a fluctuating-pressure-induced damage variable model, and further integrates them into a unified total damage variable.

Wellbore stability calculation process

After accounting for formation damage induced by drilling-fluid weakening and fluctuating-pressure disturbance, wellbore stability evaluation must be re-performed on the basis of updated mechanical properties. The complete workflow of the damage-coupled wellbore stability analysis is shown in Fig. 5 and summarized as follows. First, the initial boundary conditions at the borehole wall are determined from the well trajectory parameters, in-situ stresses, pore pressure, and mud density. Next, using the drilling-fluid rheological properties together with the pipe running/pulling velocity and acceleration, the steady-state fluctuating pressure is calculated and superimposed as an additional disturbance load onto the wellbore internal pressure. Then, according to the soaking duration and cyclic-loading conditions, the drilling-fluid weakening damage factor Dt and the fluctuating-pressure damage factor Df are evaluated, and the total damage variable D is obtained through the adopted coupling relationship. Based on the effective stress concept, the damage variable is introduced to correct both elastic parameters and strength parameters, such that the elastic modulus, peak strength, tensile strength, cohesion, etc. reflect their degraded states after damage. With the updated mechanical parameters, the Lekhnitskii-Aadnoy analytical method is employed to compute the radial, tangential, and axial stresses at the borehole wall, and the three principal stresses are further determined. The Mohr-Coulomb criterion and the single-weak-plane criterion are then used to evaluate the potential for shear failure, while the tensile-strength criterion is used to assess the potential for tensile fracturing. By iteratively adjusting the mud density, the collapse mud density corresponding to the shear-failure limit and the fracture mud density corresponding to the tensile-failure limit are obtained, respectively. The interval between these two densities defines the safe mud-weight window. Finally, by incorporating the variation of D with depth or operational parameters (e.g., pipe running speed, soaking time), the dynamic evolution of the mud-weight window under different damage levels can be established. This enables identification of high-risk intervals, sensitive parameter ranges, and impending instability trends, thereby providing quantitative guidance for field mud-weight management, casing-running speed optimization, and managed-pressure operations.

Discussion

Model verification

The input parameters are as follows: well depth of 3000 m; circumferential angle around the borehole (120°); well deviation angle (88°); static Poisson’s ratio (0.24); tensile strength (2 MPa); matrix internal friction angle (25°); matrix cohesion (18 MPa); weak-plane internal friction angle (16°); weak-plane cohesion (12 MPa); weak-plane dip angle (20°); weak-plane azimuth (120°); porosity (5%); Biot coefficient (0.8); wellbore-wall permeability coefficient (0.2); equivalent pore-pressure density of the formation (1.15 g/cm³); wellbore fluid-column pressure (56 MPa); angle between the weak plane and the maximum principal stress (45°); and angle between the matrix failure plane and the maximum principal stress (30°).

All these parameters jointly govern the stress distribution, pressure fluctuations, and damage level of the wellbore surrounding rock during drilling, thereby controlling wellbore stability. Using the above inputs provides the necessary data support for subsequent fluctuating-pressure calculation and damage analysis, and offers a theoretical basis for wellbore stability assessment. The interaction between drilling-fluid-induced weakening and cyclic-loading-induced fatigue damage deserves further investigation. At the microscale, drilling-fluid invasion into coal cleats and microfractures can weaken intergranular bonding, promote matrix softening, and reduce the resistance to crack initiation and propagation. As a result, coal rock that has experienced prior fluid-induced weakening may exhibit a higher fatigue-damage accumulation rate under the same cyclic pressure disturbance. In the present study, this nonlinear interaction was not explicitly introduced into the calculation model, because the available tests were designed to characterize soaking-induced weakening and cyclic-loading-induced damage separately. Quantitative calibration of such an interaction would require additional true-triaxial cyclic loading tests on coal specimens after different controlled soaking durations. This will be an important focus of future work.

Case analysis

Taking Well X in the western Ordos Basin as an example, the well exhibited pronounced downhole pressure fluctuations during the running of the gas-zone casing. Abnormal phenomena such as the back-calculated pressure rising from 5 MPa to 8.6 MPa and a slow decrease in pump pressure collectively indicate that microcrack propagation and localized structural weakening had already occurred at the borehole wall. Sustained reverse pressure and cyclic pressure disturbances reduced the load-bearing capacity of the wellbore, which was particularly evident in the 4218 m interval where high in-situ stresses were superimposed. Stress concentration induced by pressure fluctuations triggered localized fracturing and spalling of the surrounding rock into the annulus, further impairing the mud’s cuttings-carrying capacity and promoting annular blockage, ultimately leading to borehole burial (a stuck point) at this depth. This process demonstrates that, under deep-well conditions, fluctuating-pressure variations constitute a key controlling factor that triggers wellbore instability and borehole burial events.

Relevant parameters of Well X were collected, including drilling-fluid properties, drilling-fluid soaking time, coal-rock mechanical parameters, casing size parameters, and well trajectory parameters. The coal-rock mechanical parameters were obtained from triaxial mechanical tests after drilling-fluid immersion and true triaxial cyclic loading-unloading tests. The specific input parameters are listed in Table 6. The parameters related to casing movement were determined from the field operation record. In the horizontal section, the average operation time was approximately 10 min per casing joint, including hoisting, make-up, and running-in operations. The length of a single casing joint was 11 m. Because hoisting and make-up do not contribute to the downward movement of the casing, the effective running time was assumed to be 0.8 min per joint after subtracting an average hoisting and make-up time of 9.2 min. Accordingly, the representative casing-running speed in the gas-bearing interval was calculated as 0.208 m/s and used as the base-case speed in the fluctuating-pressure calculation. Other operational and formation parameters, including drilling-fluid density, rheological parameters, casing geometry, soaking time, well depth, deviation angle, and azimuth, were kept fixed as listed in Table 6 unless otherwise stated. This assumption allows the influence of casing-running speed and mud density on fluctuating pressure and the mud-weight window to be evaluated independently.

Assuming other conditions remain unchanged, the mud density in the well was varied to analyze its influence on fluctuating pressure, as illustrated in Fig. 9. Taking a casing-running speed of 0.2 m/s as an example, when mud density increases from 1.0 g/cm³ to 1.7 g/cm³, the calculated fluctuating pressure increases from 3.66 MPa to 3.72 MPa. This indicates that mud density has a relatively minor effect on the steady-state fluctuating pressure within this density range. It should be noted that the above result represents an engineering estimate based on the steady-state velocity assumption. In the 4218 m interval, where the borehole wall had already experienced drilling-fluid-induced weakening and cyclic-pressure disturbance, local annular narrowing caused by borehole shrinkage, cuttings accumulation, casing eccentricity, or coal-rock spalling may have increased the local annular fluid velocity and additional pressure loss. Therefore, the actual instantaneous fluctuating pressure in this interval could be higher than the calculated steady-state value. This potential underestimation does not change the main conclusion that excessive casing-running speed increases instability risk, but it suggests that the recommended speed limit should be implemented conservatively in high-risk intervals.

Variation pattern of pressure fluctuations.

Based on the above finding that mud density has only a minor influence on fluctuating pressure, several measures are recommended to effectively enhance wellbore stability. First, the casing-running speed must be strictly controlled to avoid sudden increases in fluctuating pressure caused by rapid and continuous pipe movement, which may trigger wellbore instability or fracturing. Second, the inhibitive and sealing performance of the drilling fluid should be strengthened, particularly by improving its low-filtration and plugging characteristics, so as to reduce drilling-fluid invasion into cleats and microfractures and shorten the open-hole exposure time. This can mitigate coal-rock softening, fracture propagation, and strength degradation, thereby maintaining the load-bearing capacity of the borehole. Finally, it is recommended to set pressure alarm thresholds at the wellhead or via PWD monitoring. Once abnormal pressure fluctuations are detected, the running speed should be reduced or circulation should be stopped in time to prevent sudden wellbore instability under severe pressure variations. Implementing these measures will help ensure wellbore integrity and reduce the risk of wellbore collapse or lost-circulation incidents caused by improper operations.

Using the proposed casing-running fluctuating-pressure wellbore stability model, the variation of the mud-weight window with casing-running speed and mud density was obtained (Fig. 10). At the field mud density of 1.53 g/cm³, the mud-weight window shrinks as the casing-running speed increases. Moreover, when the field casing-running speed is 0.208 m/s, the mud-weight window also narrows with increasing mud density. Model calculations indicate that, at a mud density of 1.53 g/cm³, the mud-weight window ranges from 1.355 to 2.425 g/cm³, corresponding to a casing-running speed of 0.169 m/s. Considering a 5% safety margin, the recommended casing-running speed range is 0.161–0.177 m/s. The field average running speed of 0.208 m/s exceeds this recommended range, which is consistent with the observed pump-pressure increase and borehole burial event. Considering the possible local amplification of annular velocity and pressure loss in the weakened 4218 m interval, the instantaneous pressure disturbance may have been greater than the steady-state estimate. Therefore, the calculated speed range should be regarded as a conservative operational threshold for field control rather than an exact transient limit. Reasonable control of casing-running speed is thus an important measure for reducing fluctuating-pressure-induced wellbore instability risk.

Based on the above analysis, the following measures are recommended to address wellbore instability induced by fluctuating pressure during casing running. First, the casing-running speed should be strictly controlled within 0.161–0.177 m/s. In particular, within the weak interval at approximately 4218 m, the speed limit should be set to ≤ 0.161 m/s, and any instantaneous acceleration must be strictly avoided to prevent excessive fluctuating pressure caused by overly rapid pipe movement and the resulting wellbore instability. Second, managed-pressure cementing (MPC) is recommended. By continuously monitoring running speed, annular pressure, and downhole equivalent circulating density (ECD), a real-time “speed-pressure” relationship can be established. This curve can be used to identify abnormal conditions and to promptly adjust operational parameters, thereby maintaining wellbore stability and optimizing drilling-fluid management. On this basis, provided that sufficient circulation is ensured, the mud density may be temporarily reduced from 1.53 g/cm³ for a short period, while maintaining continuous fluid replenishment and operating under the prescribed speed limit, in order to control both wellbore stability and pressure variation. If abnormal annular pressure behavior or cuttings anomalies occur, the mud density should be immediately restored to the original 1.53 g/cm³, so as to ensure smooth casing-running operations and prevent formation fracturing or wellbore collapse. These integrated measures can effectively mitigate the adverse impact of fluctuating pressure on wellbore stability and reduce the risk of instability caused by improper operational control.

Conclusions

This study addresses the coupled effects of drilling-fluid-induced weakening and fluctuating-pressure disturbance that commonly compromise wellbore stability during wiper trips and casing running in deep coal-rock gas horizontal wells. A systematic experimental program was conducted, including triaxial compression tests after drilling-fluid immersion and true triaxial cyclic loading-unloading tests. Based on these results, a fully coupled evaluation framework linking steady-state fluctuating pressure, damage variables, and wellbore stability was established, enabling damage-mechanics-based prediction of wellbore stability. The proposed framework, for the first time, integrates “time-dependent drilling-fluid weakening,” “periodic pressure disturbance,” and a “three-dimensional stress state” into a unified scheme. It provides a quantitative description of wellbore damage evolution and offers an innovative theoretical approach for investigating fluctuating-pressure mechanisms and designing safe casing-running operations in deep coal-rock gas wells.

In addition, the present total damage formulation treats drilling-fluid-induced weakening and fluctuating-pressure-induced fatigue damage using a first-order superposition relationship. Although this treatment provides a practical engineering framework based on the available experimental data, the possible nonlinear interaction between these two mechanisms—particularly the acceleration of fatigue damage by prior coal-rock weakening—still requires further experimental verification.
