Motion-based baby schema in infant gait: inversion effect and the influence of maternal experience
Authors: Yuki Shimizu, Sayaka Kitada
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Research on the baby schema has predominantly focused on static facial or bodily proportions, yet Lorenz’s original concept also highlighted clumsy infant movements as potential cues eliciting caregiving responses. To investigate whether motion-based infantile features trigger baby schema reactions, three groups of adults (mothers, female students, male students) were presented with point-light displays (PLDs) depicting an infant, a preschooler, and an adult walking, shown side-by-side in both upright and inverted orientations. Participants evaluated each pair for relative cuteness, desire to help, clumsiness, and familiarity. The results indicated that infant gait reliably elicits core baby schema responses across groups, particularly for clumsiness and helping desire. Inverting infant-based pairings significantly reduced perceptions of cuteness and the desire to help, supporting the notion that upright orientation facilitates the holistic processing of dynamic infantile cues. Mothers generally exhibited responses similar to male and female students, yet they reported heightened baby schema responses in some conditions. Further analyses demonstrated that the age of their own child modulated baby schema perceptions, indicating that real-world caregiving experience may enhance sensitivity to infantile motion. Overall, these findings extend baby schema theory beyond static cues to dynamic gait-based features, illustrating how orientation and parenting experience jointly shape adult reactions to clumsy infant movements and underscoring the broader developmental and practical significance of motion-driven caregiving triggers.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-24782-2.

Introduction

Baby schema (Kinderschema) refers to a set of infantile physical characteristics, such as a relatively large head, high forehead, large eyes, and a small nose and mouth, that are commonly perceived as cute. Originally proposed by Lorenz1, this conceptual framework positions these morphological characteristics as biological mechanisms that trigger parental affection and protective behaviors1–3. Numerous empirical studies have demonstrated that baby schema cues often evoke positive emotional responses (e.g., enhanced perceptions of cuteness) and heighten attentional or caregiving motivations4–8. Such effects appear robust across diverse cultural contexts9,10, and are even observed when juvenile features are present in animal species5,11,12.

Baby schema perceptions from infant motions

While the majority of empirical research on baby schema has focused on static facial proportions, Lorenz’s original concept also encompasses the clumsy movement characteristics of infants1. In particular, the gait of infants is marked by features such as asymmetrical limb movements, short stride length, instability in gait rhythm, unsteady interlimb coordination, and a stooped posture13–16. These characteristics may serve as potent baby schema cues because they clearly signal immaturity and potential vulnerability.

A recent empirical investigation by Kitada and Shimizu17 demonstrated that infant gait can elicit baby schema perceptions. In their study, participants, comprising two- to five-year-olds and adults, were shown walking videos—converted into point-light displays (PLDs)—of an infant, a preschooler, and an adult. They measured participants’ attention biases towards each PLD, revealing two principal findings: (1) individuals exhibit attention bias to infant gait, and (2) this attention bias does not appear to manifest in children as young as two to five years old. The clumsy movements of infants may serve as an adaptive function by attracting the attention of surrounding adults, thereby potentially safeguarding infants from harm. However, as Kitada and Shimizu17 focused exclusively on assessing visual attention bias towards infant gait, it remains uncertain whether these clumsy motor patterns also evoke core baby schema responses, such as heightened perceptions of cuteness or increased caregiving motivation.

Inversion effect and baby schema

The face inversion effect is a robust phenomenon in which recognition and discrimination deteriorate considerably when faces are turned upside-down, largely because upright orientation supports holistic or configural processing18–20. Kuraguchi and Nittono21 explored the applicability of the face inversion effect to judgments of infant cuteness. Their research encompassed two experimental tasks: a rating task, in which participants assessed high- and low-cuteness infant faces for cuteness or beauty, and a two-alternative forced-choice (2AFC) task, in which participants chose a cuter or more beautiful face from a pair. No inversion effect was detected in the rating task; however, the 2 AFC task revealed a significant inversion effect as participants’ accuracy in identifying the cuter (or more beautiful) face diminished when the images were inverted. These results indicate that, while infant cuteness may largely rely on fundamental baby schema features, detailed comparisons require holistic face processing, which is disrupted by inversion.

A parallel concept, the motion inversion effect (MIE), occurs when the recognition and processing of biological motions, such as PLDs, are substantially impaired by inverting the stimuli22–24. Under typical upright orientation, observers easily perceive intentions, emotions, and identities from biological motions25–27, but inverting the display forces a more piecemeal analysis that interferes with recognizing coherent movement. Extending this concept to infant motion could ascertain whether infantile movement patterns, such as unsteady walking, depend on upright orientation to evoke perceptions of baby schema. Unlike faces—which we occasionally encounter from an upside-down perspective (e.g., lying down)—we rarely see inverted walking in everyday life. Hence, we predict that baby schema responses derived from gait are especially susceptible to orientation-based disruptions.

Examining the baby schema in conjunction with the MIE addresses two significant research gaps. First, it extends the conceptualization of baby schema beyond mere facial characteristics by emphasizing the role of motor behavior in prompting caregiver engagement. Second, it enhances the understanding of the motion inversion effect by assessing whether specialized mechanisms that facilitate the perception of upright biological motion also increase sensitivity to infantile movement cues.

Sex differences and parental status in baby schema perceptions

A substantial body of evidence indicates that men and women generally respond in similar ways to infant stimuli, indicating that the reactions to infantile characteristics are intrinsic to humans. However, subtle sex differences can arise under certain conditions. For instance, women tend to devote more visual attention to infant faces than do men28, which is consistent with hormonal explanations involving endogenous testosterone and estradiol29. Women also display greater sensitivity to minor variations in cuteness30–32, exhibit increased caregiving motivation when viewing infants with pronounced baby schema33, and show elevated activation in reward-related brain regions34. By contrast, other investigations demonstrate that both sexes typically regard “babyish” features as appealing35,36 and manifest positive implicit responses to infant cues37.

Parental status appears to further modulate these reactions, although its impact is not straightforward. Mothers commonly report and exhibit more pronounced emotional or behavioral responses to infant stimuli, as evidenced by subjective ratings and the corresponding neural measures32,33. Non-parents also respond favorably to infant faces but seem to be less attuned to subtle emotional signals32. However, another study did not find any significant differences between parents and non-parents37. Overall, the literature underscores a broad, evolutionarily rooted responsiveness to infant characteristics across adults while highlighting the nuanced role of sex and childcare experience in shaping the precise nature of these responses.

The current study

The present investigation aims to enhance our understanding of baby schema by focusing on motion-based rather than exclusively static cues. To achieve this, point-light displays (PLDs) depicting the walking patterns of an infant, a toddler, and an adult, as developed by Kitada and Shimizu17, were employed in this study. While their original paradigm focused on identifying which PLD most strongly captured participants’ visual attention, our approach directly asked observers to assess which display appeared cuter, more in need of help, clumsier, and more familiar, thereby allowing for a more explicit examination of baby schema perceptions. We included perceived familiarity—alongside traditional baby schema indices (cuteness, clumsiness, and the desire to help)—because prior evidence indicates that differences between mothers’ and non-mothers’ responses to infant faces are attributable to their differential familiarity with the infant-face category38.

We furthermore examined the inversion effect, comparing upright and inverted presentations, to probe the perceptual and cognitive processes that underlie cuteness perception in motion. Whereas previous research21 employed a two-alternative forced-choice (2 AFC) task in which participants simply chose the cuter of two faces, we asked participants to indicate how much cuter one figure was than the other by moving a slider on a continuous scale from − 100 to + 100. This continuous measure allowed observers to report finer gradations in their perceptions. Finally, by recruiting mothers, female students, and male students (both student groups with no children), we elucidated potential sex differences and the role of caregiving experience in shaping responses to dynamic infant cues.

Importantly, while we used the same PLD stimuli as Kitada and Shimizu17, the current study represents a conceptually and methodologically distinct investigation. Conceptually, we extend beyond the previous study’s emphasis on initial attentional capture to explore subsequent stages of the baby schema cascade, specifically focusing on affective appraisals of cuteness and the desire to help, as well as perceived clumsiness and familiarity. Methodologically, we introduced an upright versus inverted presentation to examine inversion effects in dynamic motion processing, and recruited a novel sample comprising mothers alongside female and male students to explore the influence of gender and caregiving experience. These innovative elements allow us to address theoretical questions that were previously unanswerable within the original eye-tracking paradigm, thereby ensuring that the current study constitutes as an independent contribution rather than a mere extension of prior work.

This research design yields several advantages. First, it extends baby schema studies beyond static facial features, highlighting how movement patterns evoke baby schema perceptions. Second, examining the inversion effect in a motion-based context parallels longstanding inquiries in face perception research, thereby enhancing theoretical insight into the role of configural processing in dynamic settings. Third, comparing distinct participant groups clarifies how biological sex and prior caregiving experiences collectively modulate responses to infant motions, with implications not only for evolutionary models of parental investment, but also for applied domains such as child welfare and early intervention.

Method

Participants

A total of 177 individuals participated in this study, which was divided into three distinct groups. The first group consisted of 55 mothers (Mage = 40.09 years, SDage = 4.63, range = 31–51) whose children were aged 2–6 years (Mage = 4 years 11 months, SDage = 13.19 months, range = 2 years 2 months–6 years 5 months). The second and third groups consisted of 60 female university students (Mage = 21.25 years, SDage = 2.17, range = 18–30) and 62 male university students (Mage = 21.18 years, SDage = 2.13, range = 19–27). One additional university participant who reported their gender as “other” was excluded from the analyses. In addition, two mothers who discontinued the survey midway and one student who completed the survey in less than five minutes were excluded, and a ResponseID and IP address check confirmed that no duplicate entries remained; the counts reported above refer to the final screened sample.

The mothers were recruited from the Waseda University Child Lab’s data pool, and university students were recruited via the Waseda University’s student participant recruitment website. All university participants reported having no prior experience of raising children. Participation was part of a broader study involving multiple tasks, and each session was compensated with 1,000 Japanese yen (approximately USD 7). An a priori power analysis using G*Power 3.139, designed to detect differences among the three groups under the assumptions of a medium effect size (f = 0.25), α = 0.05, and desired power (1 – β) = 0.80, suggested a minimum total sample of 159. Based on these analyses, we concluded that the sample size was appropriate for this study.

Stimuli

We reused the exact PLD video files from Kitada and Shimizu17, which depicted three walkers: an infant (12 months old, just beginning to walk), a preschooler (5 years old), and an adult (woman in her 20 s). PLDs were adopted because they preserve the kinematic signature of walking while removing potential confounds such as facial and physical characteristics. This isolation allowed us to test whether motion patterns alone elicit perceptions of baby schema. Extensive research shows that PLDs convey age27, emotion25, gender40, direction26, action content41, and familiarity42. The findings indicate that PLDs maintain ecological significance despite their visually minimalistic nature.

Each walker was filmed by crossing a screen at 240 fps using a digital camera. Eleven body points (head top, collarbones, elbows, wrists, lower back, knees, and ankles) were tracked, and the Mathematica software was used to generate the PLDs. Following the procedure in Kitada and Shimizu17 (Study 2), infant and preschooler PLDs were resized to match the dimensions of the adult stimuli. This standardization eliminated overall body size as a confounding variable, thereby focusing observers’ judgments on the movement itself rather than scale. The videos were presented in three pairwise contrasts (Infant vs. Adult, Infant vs. Preschooler, and Preschooler vs. Adult), each consisting of two side-by-side, five-second looping clips. Procedure

All sessions were conducted online using Qualtrics. Participants were presented with three pairs of PLD videos: Infant versus Adult, Infant versus Preschooler, and Preschooler versus Adult, in both inverted and upright orientations, resulting in a total of six trials. Each pair was displayed as a loop of a five-second clip, repeated until the participants provided their responses. Approximately half of the participants were initially presented with the inverted version, whereas the remaining participants were first shown the upright version. Within each subgroup, approximately half of the participants viewed PLD videos in which the walker approached from the left, and the other half viewed videos in which the walker approached from the right. After viewing each pair, participants were asked to rate four dimensions: which display was perceived cuter (cuteness), which elicited a stronger desire to help (helping desire), which appeared clumsier (clumsiness), and which felt more familiar (familiarity). This was done using a slider scale ranging from –100 (indicating the left video was significantly higher on a given attribute) to + 100 (indicating the right video was significantly higher). This score was later re-coded so that ratings in the direction of the younger display were always positive (+ 1 to + 100) and ratings in the direction of the older were negative (− 1 to − 100). Upon completion of the first three inverted or upright trials, the participants were required to respond to an open-ended question asking what they believed the videos were depicting.

Data analysis

For each pair and rating scale, positive scores (+ 1 to + 100) indicate that the younger walker received higher ratings, whereas negative scores (–1 to –100) indicate that the older walker received higher ratings. A score of 0 denotes no perceived difference (neutral point), and the absolute distance from zero reflects the magnitude of that difference. All analyses were conducted in R (R Core Team, 2024). To determine whether each Group × Pair × Orientation mean rating differed significantly from zero, we performed separate one-sample t-tests for the ratings of cuteness, helping desire, clumsiness, and familiarity. We then fitted separate linear mixed-effects models (LMMs) using lme443 and lmerTest44. LMMs were adopted to accommodate the repeated-measures structure in which each participant rated multiple pairs, allowing random effects to capture individual-level variance. In each model, Group (mother, female student, male student) served as a between-participant factor, and Pair (Infant vs. Adult, Infant vs. Preschooler, Preschooler vs. Adult) and Orientation (upright vs. inverted) were within-participant factors. Random intercepts for participant were included to capture individual-level variance. We assessed model significance via Type III ANOVA with Satterthwaite’s approximation of degrees of freedom. When interactions were significant, post-hoc tests clarified the nature of these effects.

We further fit an additional set of LMMs in which Clumsiness and Orientation were the fixed effects to investigate how the perception of motion-based clumsiness contributes to cuteness, helping desire, and familiarity ratings, and whether orientation modifies that relationship. Additionally, to examine whether maternal responses varied as a function of their own child’s age, we conducted a separate analysis restricted to the mother group, fitting an LMM that included Pair, Orientation, and Child Age (in months) as fixed effects.

Responses to the open-ended question for the first three trials were classified from two distinct viewpoints: (1) whether the participant explicitly mentioned “walking” and (2) whether the participant referred to age or age-related differences in the videos. For each viewpoint, the inter-coder agreement was 99.4%, and discrepancies were resolved through discussion. The final coded data for each viewpoint were then analyzed using a 2 (Orientation) × 2 (Response) chi-square test.

Ethics statement

This study was approved by the ethics committee of Waseda University (No. 2024-526), and all procedures adhered to the guidelines and regulations provided by the ethics committees. The participants first completed an informed consent form, and only those who provided consent were able to proceed to the questionnaire.

The LMM for helping desire revealed significant main effects of Pair, F(2, 870) = 32.27, p < 0.0001, and Orientation, F(1, 870) = 47.49, p < 0.001, as well as a significant Pair × Orientation interaction, F(2, 870) = 7.97, p < 0.0001, and a Group × Pair interaction, F(4, 870) = 2.95, p = 0.020. Follow-up orientation-based comparisons indicated that Infant versus Adult was rated significantly higher upright (M = 38.70, SD = 43.57) than inverted (M = 3.03, SD = 57.91), t(870) = 7.12, p < 0.0001, and that Infant versus Preschooler showed a smaller but still significant orientation difference between upright (M = 35.79, SD = 43.43) and inversed (M = 19.60, SD = 51.11), t(870) = 3.22, p = 0.001. Preschooler versus Adult, however, did not demonstrate a significant orientation effect, t(870) = 1.64, p = 0.102. Therefore, the inversion-based reduction in helping desire appears largely confined to pairings involving an infant, whereas the preschooler–adult context remains stable. Post-hoc analysis revealed that group effect was not significant in the Infant versus Adult and the Preschooler versus Adult conditions, F(2, 725.88) = 0.156, p = 0.856, F(2, 725.88) = 2.22, p = 0.109, respectively, while it was significant in Infant versus Preschooler condition, F(2, 725.88) = 3.49, p = 0.031. Subsequent pairwise comparisons indicated that mothers scored significantly higher than male students, t(726) = 2.41, p = 0.048, whereas the difference between mothers and female students, t(726) = 2.18, p = 0.088, and between male students and female students, t(726) = 0.22, p = 1.000, were not significant.

The analysis of clumsiness showed significant main effects of Pair, F(2, 870) = 43.43, p < 0.0001, and Orientation, F(1, 870) = 5.85, p = 0.016, together with a significant Pair × Orientation interaction, F(2, 870) = 6.39, p = 0.002. Pairwise orientation comparisons revealed that Infant versus Adult was perceived as significantly clumsier for younger walker when upright (M = 42.59, SD = 45.01) than inverted (M = 27.34, SD = 64.61), t(870) = 2.95, p = 0.003. Infant versus Preschooler also showed that the upright score (M = 39.56, SD = 42.83) was higher than inversed score (M = 25.33, SD = 56.67), t(870) = 2.75, p = 0.006. In contrast, Preschooler versus Adult did not exhibit a significant orientation effect, t(870) = 1.52, p = 0.129. Therefore, similar to the results of cuteness, the inversion-based reduction in clumsiness appears only for pairings involving an infant.

Familiarity had no significant effects. Neither the main effect nor interaction was statistically significant.

Effects of clumsiness perception on cuteness, helping, and familiarity ratings

The LMM including Clumsiness and Orientation as predictors showed a significant main effect of Clumsiness on cuteness, F(1, 341.02) = 5.29, p = 0.022, helping desire, F(1, 343.83) = 53.08, p < 0.0001, and familiarity, F(1, 349.92) = 15.24, p < 0.001. However, the Clumsiness × Orientation interaction was not significant for any of the three dimensions, ps > 0.15. These results suggest that stronger clumsiness perceptions lead to greater subjective baby schema responses, regardless of orientation.

Effects of child age on maternal responses

The final set of analyses focused on the mother group, incorporating Child Age (in months) as a continuous covariate. Only helping desire demonstrated a significant Child Age × Pair interaction, F(2, 265) = 3.16, p = 0.044, despite there being no main effect of Child Age. In slope-based follow-up, only the Preschooler versus Adult comparison produced a significant positive slope (0.880, 95% CI [0.017, 1.742]), indicating that each additional month of child age raised relative rating of helping desire to preschooler by approximately 0.88 points. By contrast, neither Infant versus Adult nor Infant versus Preschooler changed significantly. No effects of child age emerged for cuteness, clumsiness, or familiarity (Fs < 2.59, ps > 0.077), underscoring that helping desire in the preschooler–adult context is uniquely sensitive to maternal experience.

Open-ended responses

A chi-square test examining whether participants mentioned “walking” in the upright versus inverted condition was significant, χ2(1,N = 177) = 32.32, p < 0.001. Specifically, in the upright condition, 82.22% of participants mentioned walking while in the inverted condition only 39.08% mentioned walking. By contrast, a second chi-square test comparing age-related references was not significant (14.44% upright vs. 20.69% inverted), χ2(1,N = 177) = 1.19, p = 0.275. Thus, inversion substantially reduced participants’ tendency to identify the motion as “walking,” but did not significantly affect whether they noted age differences.

Discussion

This study substantiates the notion that baby schema encompasses not only static morphological characteristics but also infant movements, thereby corroborating Lorenz’s1 early proposition that clumsy motor patterns can serve as significant cues. By assessing direct responses to the baby schema in terms of perceived cuteness, desire to help, clumsiness, and familiarity, the present study expands upon Kitada and Shimizu’s17 earlier finding of a visual attention bias toward infant gait. Furthermore, by recruiting three distinct groups—mothers, female students, and male students—we explored how sex and caregiving experiences may modulate motion-based baby schema responses.

This broader approach revealed several key patterns. First, infant gait reliably elicits core baby schema perceptions across groups, particularly for clumsiness and helping desire. Because our PLDs excluded facial and body-shape cues, cuteness was less salient except among mothers. Meanwhile, helping desire remained robust even without explicit infant facial or bodily features, reinforcing that clumsy motion alone can evoke a caregiving impulse. Consistent with this interpretation, few participants spontaneously mentioned age (14.44% upright; 20.69% inverted), yet helping desire remained robust, indicating that motion alone can elicit baby schema responses without explicit age reasoning. Second, orientation-related disruptions in perceiving infant gait were observed, mirroring earlier findings on face inversion effects on infants’ cuteness21. In particular, inversion diminished cuteness, helping desire, and clumsiness to infant gait for infant versus adult and infant versus preschooler contrasts, indicating the disruption of holistic or configural processing mechanisms essential for perceiving infantile features. The open-ended responses further support this interpretation: participants were far less likely to identify the motion as “walking” when inverted (39.08%) than when upright (82.22%), indicating that inversion specifically obscures the kinematic cues of clumsy infant gait. Nevertheless, one-sample t-test results indicated that clumsiness and helping desire remained significantly above neutral point even in inverted infant conditions, suggesting that baby schema responses persist to some extent even without upright cues. In contrast, preschooler–adult combinations were only marginally affected by orientation, implying that once developmental contrasts became less stark, motion inversion exerted a weaker influence on baby schema responses. Additionally, we found that perceived clumsiness itself was significantly associated with higher ratings of cuteness, helping desire, and familiarity across both upright and inverted conditions, suggesting that once observers detect “immaturity” in movement, it strongly drives baby schema responses, regardless of orientation.

Third, the lack of differences between male and female students in their responses to clumsy infant movements suggests that men and women may respond similarly to motion-based baby schema cues, consistent with prior work indicating broadly comparable reactions to infant features across sexes35–37. Some studies have shown that subtle sex differences arise when stimuli are overtly infantile or highly exaggerated28, indicating that contextual or stimulus-specific factors may be critical in detecting gender-related variations. Crucially, the inclusion of mothers in our study revealed a more nuanced pattern than a simple male–female comparison would suggest. Only mothers consistently perceived cuteness from infant gait PLDs. Moreover, the infant–preschooler pairing yielded a group main effect in which mothers scored significantly higher than male students. This divergence suggests that maternal experience may heighten sensitivity to developmental contrasts just beyond infancy. Furthermore, among the mothers themselves, those with older children showed heightened desire to help when comparing a preschooler to an adult, supporting the notion that extended caregiving experience sharpens one’s responsiveness to more advanced childlike cues. This pattern not only adds to the evidence that parenting history can refine baby schema responses but also underscores how nuanced developmental contrasts can be critical in revealing mother-specific effects. Collectively, these data suggest that caregiving experience dynamically adjusts the thresholds for perceiving a "need for help." Specifically, mothers identified the preschooler as vulnerable—a developmental stage characterized by high mobility yet limited autonomy—whereas non-parents did not. This developmental specificity aligns with neural evidence indicating that postpartum adaptations enhance maternal vigilance to child distress cues beyond infancy45,46.

Lastly, familiarity appeared unaffected by either orientation or group factors, perhaps because participants perceived movement patterns in ways that were less directly tied to infantile or adult cues. Familiarity might arise from two possible sources: recognizing the movement as childlike and therefore distinct or perceiving it as reminiscent of one’s own typical gait. Either way, the present findings indicate that familiarity does not necessarily fluctuate alongside baby schema responses such as cuteness or helping desire.

In summary, these findings underscore the important role played by motion-based cues and orientation in shaping baby schema responses. Upright presentations of infants’ clumsy movements trigger pronounced increases in baby schema responses. Moreover, maternal characteristics interact selectively with the child’s apparent developmental level, indicating that the baby schema’s potency partly depends on real-world parenting experiences and the specific mismatch between infantile features and more mature forms. By documenting how these motion-centered infant cues evoke both universally positive reactions and mother-specific patterns, this study advances our understanding of baby schema beyond static facial proportions and bridges gaps in the literature on sex differences and caregiving experience.

Several limitations warrant attention along with directions for future research. First, although PLDs appear to successfully highlight the distinctive kinematics of infant movement, employing more naturalistic stimuli that also preserve bodily attributes and contextual information may yield richer insights. Accordingly, we expect partial generalizability to real-world caregiving contexts, with clumsy infant gait eliciting helping motivation when visible, but the strength of this effect varying with concurrent facial/body and auditory cues and situational demands. Future work should therefore replicate the present design using silhouette or full-video stimuli to verify whether the motion-based cuteness effect endures under more ecologically valid conditions. Second, the present design compared mothers with male and female university students as non-parent groups; expanding it to include fathers and participants from diverse cultural backgrounds might uncover subtler or more extensive variations in responses to clumsy movements. Third, the current study relied solely on subjective ratings, future research would benefit from combining these perceptual judgments with measures of attention (e.g., eye tracking) and neural indicators (e.g., EEG or fMRI) to capture more comprehensively how observers register and respond to infantile motion cues. Additionally, incorporating an overt post-task age-categorization test—requiring observers to label each PLD as “infant”, “preschooler”, or “adult”—would allow future research to disentangle baby-schema responses that arise implicitly from those that co-occur with conscious age recognition.

Conclusion

Taken together, our results demonstrate that clumsy infant movements can meaningfully evoke baby schema perceptions in adults, even absent overt infant facial or body-shape cues. Upright orientation amplifies perceptions of infant gait, particularly for clumsiness and helping desire, whereas inversion partially disrupts these responses—though some degree of baby schema reaction persists even upside down. Mothers alone consistently perceived infant gait as cute and exhibited heightened helping responses in certain infant–preschooler comparisons, suggesting that extended caregiving experience enhances sensitivity to developmental nuances. These findings broaden the traditional focus on static infant features to underscore how motion and orientation jointly shape baby schema outcomes, highlighting a compelling avenue for future research involving naturalistic gait cues, attention-based measures, and neural indicators.
