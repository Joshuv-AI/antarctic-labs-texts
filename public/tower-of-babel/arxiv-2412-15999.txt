Abstract
. We consider a sequence of Hawkes processes whose excitation measures may depend on the generation, and study its scaling limits in the near-unstable limiting regime. The limiting random measures, characterized via a nonlinear convolutional equation, form a family parameterized by a pair consisting of a locally finite measure and a geometrically infinitely divisible probability distribution on the positive real line. These measures can be interpreted as generalizations of the Feller diffusion and fractional Feller (CIR) processes, but also allow for a "driving noise" associated with general Lévy-type operators of order at most 1 1 , including fractional derivatives of any order α > 0 \alpha>0 (formally corresponding to possibly negative Hurst parameters).

60F17, 60G55, 60G57, 45D05

1.
Introduction

Hawkes point processes were introduced in

[
25
,
24
]
as models for self-exciting
stochastic phenomena. Their fundamental property is that new points
are generated at a rate that depends on the number and locations of
existing points via a function known as the excitation intensity.
Initially used as models for seismic events, Hawkes processes have
since found numerous applications in various disciplines ranging from
epidemiology and criminology, to genetics, neuroscience,
economics and finance (see the survey
[
37
]
and its
references).

1.1.
Limiting theory of Hawkes processes - an overview of the
literature

The investigation into the limiting theory of Hawkes processes began
almost immediately after their introduction. A central limit theorem
(as
t\to\infty
) for Hawkes processes whose kernels admit a finite
first moment was already established in
[
23
]
(this paper
also introduced the cluster representation we use in the current paper). We
start with a brief survey of existing pertinent results split
into two classes, based on the scaling regime.

In the first class, the total mass
a=\int_{0}^{\infty}\phi(t)\,dt
of
the kernel
\phi
is kept constant, while time, space and other
parameters are scaled. One of the earliest results here was provided by

[
2
]
, where a functional central limit theorem (FCLT)
with convergence to a scaled Brownian motion was established
under a finiteness assumption on the
1/2
-th moment of the kernel.
Later,
[
19
]
introduced a framework where the background
(immigrant) intensity is taken to infinity, but only space is scaled
to compensate. Under the assumption that the kernel is exponential, it
is shown there that the limiting process is no longer Brownian but
only Gaussian with a non-Markovian covariance function. More recently,
a FCLT for marked Hawkes processes and associated shot noises was
established in
[
27
]
. We also mention a recent preprint

[
28
]
by the same authors where FCLTs or parallel negative
results are established for Hawkes processes in several stability
regions defined by the values of the total mass and the first moment
of the kernel
\phi
.

The second class of results features the "nearly unstable" scaling regime,
introduced in
[
30
]
, which is also utilized in the present work.
In this regime, both time and space are scaled in a non-Brownian manner,
while the total mass of the kernel is sent to
1
, the stability threshold
constant. Assuming that the kernel has a finite first moment, these authors
establish a functional scaling limit theorem for the integrated intensity
process with the Feller (CIR) diffusion, a non-Gaussian process, as the
limit. In the follow-up paper
[
31
]
, the requirement for a finite
first moment is relaxed to the finiteness of some moment above
1/2
, and
the limiting Feller diffusion is replaced by a fractional Feller (CIR)
process, which is neither Markovian nor a semimartingale. The mode of
convergence obtained in
[
31
]
was strengthened in

[
26
]
under the same assumptions on the kernel. These authors
show that the intensity processes themselves converge in the Skorokhod
topology, and not only their integrals, as in
[
31
]
.

1.2.
Our contributions

The goal of this paper is to add to the
existing literature by extending the aforementioned results in several
directions. Firstly, we consider nonhomogeneous Hawkes processes, i.e.,
generalizations of Hawkes processes where the kernel is allowed to vary
from generation to generation (see
[
15
]
or
[
39
]

for a related model).
This not only provides additional modeling flexibility, but also unlocks a
wider range of possible limiting objects. Additionally, we allow the
kernels themselves to serve as scaling parameters in that they may depend
on the scaling parameter
n
. Compared to the existing results, these two
extensions can be interpreted as a transition from scaled sums of
i.i.d. sequences to sums of (triangular) arrays of independent random
variables in classical probability theory. Continuing this analogy, the
class of our limiting objects now includes not only the analogues of stable
distributions (fractional Feller (CIR) processes), but also the analogues
of infinitely divisible distributions (termed Feller random measures in
this paper).

Another direction in which we broaden all existing results is that we do
not impose any conditions on the integrability of the kernels; we do not
even require them to be functions in
\mathcal{L}^{1}[0,\infty)
but permit them to be
general finite measures on
[0,\infty)
. This allows us, in particular, to expand
the analysis of
[
30
,
31
]
down to and below the critical

1/2
-moment threshold imposed in the existing literature. In this regime,
the limiting objects are no longer necessarily (integrals of) stochastic
processes; they can now be located only in the space of locally finite random
measures. Hence, we cannot talk about convergence in Skorokhod’s
J_{1}
, or
any related topology, but instead work with the vague topology this space
is naturally endowed with. On the other hand, we show that the limiting
random measures are almost surely atom-free; vague convergence in that case
implies weak convergence of cumulative distribution processes in the
topology of locally uniform (and therefore locally
J_{1}
) convergence.

The level of generality of our framework and the appearance of limiting
objects beyond the class of stochastic processes render the tools of
stochastic analysis and martingale theory, standard in the Hawkes-process
literature, less effective. This is further exacerbated by the
nonhomogeneity of our model; the generation-dependence of the excitation
kernel makes it challenging to express the conditional intensity process in
a convenient form without sacrificing finite dimensionality. Consequently,
we are led to the cluster representation of the Hawkes process (similar to

[
12
]
) and the related cascade of relationships among the Laplace
functionals associated with a sequence of auxiliary point processes. Here,
the process indexed by
m
represents the progeny of an individual of
generation
m-1
. The crux of the argument then rests on obtaining tight
joint estimates for processes of different indices.
This results in a
convergence theorem that provides scaling constants, gives sufficient
conditions under which the scaling limit exists, and characterizes its
Laplace functional in terms of the unique solution to a nonlinear
convolutional Riccati equation.
The central condition on the array of excitation kernels takes the flavor
of classical convergence of triangular arrays and is given in two equivalent
formulations. Moreover, three alternative natural and easily verifiable
conditions for the main theorem to hold are provided and discussed.

We also show that all possible limits of sequences of scaled
Hawkes processes in our setting are
completely described by two measure-valued parameters: a locally finite
measure
\mu
and a
probability measure
\rho
on
[0,\infty)
. While
\mu
can be any locally finite
measure on
[0,\infty)
, the class of possible
\rho
turns out to
coincide with the
class of
geometrically infinitely divisible distributions
. Such
distributions admit a theory somewhat parallel to that of
infinitely divisible distributions (see, e.g.,
[
36
]
) and
the appearance of a particular
\rho
in our limit can be directly related
to the characteristics of the array of the excitation measures, especially
in the cases of
geometrically stable
distributions, i.e.,
distributions in the Mittag-Leffler family.

The name
Feller random measures
is chosen for our limiting objects
because, as shown in
[
30
]
and
[
32
]
, their densities
are given by a Feller diffusion when
\rho
is the exponential distribution
and a rough (fractional) Feller process when
\rho
is the Mittag-Leffler
distribution with index above
1/2
. The third section of the paper is devoted
to studying the distributional properties of Feller random measures, such as
moments, infinite divisibility and (non) existence of densities in
special cases.

1.3.
Connections with fractional Brownian motion and rough
volatility models

One of the motivations for this work comes from the role
Hawkes processes have in financial modeling. Their self-exciting nature is
particularly well-suited for capturing the dependence of market buy and
sell orders on past orders (see, e.g.,
[
4
]
for an
overview). A phenomenon well-explained by such models is the observed
"roughness" (see
[
7
]
and
[
20
]
) of market
volatility. Indeed, the fractional Feller (CIR) process that appears in the
results of
[
31
]
- and corresponds to the squared volatility -
can be informally thought of as a continuous stochastic process "driven" by
a fractional Brownian motion (fBM). The value of the Hurst parameter
H\in(0,1)
of this fBM, used to describe the degree of "roughness" of the
volatility process, has been the subject of several empirical studies.
Early estimates gave
H\in(1/2,1)
(
[
7
]
) whereas two decades
later the consensus shifted towards
H\in(0,1/2)
(see
[
20
]
,

[
6
]
and
[
17
]
). Many of the later estimates
put
H
very close to
0
, suggesting that
H=0
might be the "true" value
(see
[
16
]
,
[
5
]
).

Even though there is no universally accepted way to define a fractional
Brownian motion with
H=0
either as a stochastic process or as a random
measure/field, several authors have proposed models that could play such a
role in one sense or another. These include the multifractal random walks
(see
[
3
]
) and various Gaussian random fields with a
logarithmic kernel (see
[
18
]
,
[
41
]
, and

[
22
]
for a sample of different approaches). Our framework allows
us not only to define generalized fractional Feller (CIR) processes
corresponding to values of the Hurst parameter
H
in the interval

(-1/2,1/2]
, but also to encompass a much wider range of driving noises
beyond the one-dimensional fractional family. Moreover, we only require a
single passage to the limit, and do not define a limiting process for
H>0

first, and then pass it to a (second) limit
H\to 0
, as is often done in
the literature mentioned above. While a full analysis is left for future
research, and it is difficult to give a formal definition of the notion of
a driving noise for random measures, we do note that the form of the
covariance kernel that we obtain in subsection
3.2.3
below suggests
the log-correlated class (see the survey
[
10
]
) in the case

H=0
.

1.4.
Organization of the paper

Following this introduction, Section 2
introduces the necessary background and notation, and states the main theorem
together with several weaker sufficient conditions for its validity. Section 3
is devoted to the study of various properties of the limiting Feller random
measures. All proofs are deferred to the appendix, which is organized into four
subsections.
Subsection

A.1
provides proofs related to alternative conditions for the main theorem, Subsection
A.2
develops results on convolutional Riccati
equations, and Subsection
A.3
contains additional
estimates related to Hawkes processes. Finally, Section
A.4
combines these
ingredients to establish the main theorem through several auxiliary results.

1.5.
Notation and conventions

Let
I
be either
[0,\infty)
,
(0,\infty)
, or
[0,T]
for some
T\geq 0
.

\mathcal{S}^{\infty}(I)
denotes the family of all Borel measurable functions on
I
such
that
\left\lvert f\right\rvert_{\mathcal{S}^{\infty}(I)}:=\sup_{t\in I}\left\lvert f(t)\right\rvert<\infty
, while

\mathcal{S}^{\infty}_{\mathrm{loc}}[0,\infty)
consists of all functions
f:[0,\infty)\to{\mathbb{R}}
such that
f|_{[0,T]}\in\mathcal{S}^{\infty}[0,T]
for each
T\geq 0
.

\mathcal{B}(I)
denotes the set of Borel subsets of
I
, and
\mathcal{M}(I)
denotes the
set of all positive Borel measures on
I
. The measurable structure on

\mathcal{M}(I)
is generated by the evaluation maps
\mu\mapsto\mu(A)
, with
A\in\mathcal{B}(I)
. The sets of finite measures, measures that are finite on
bounded sets, and probability measures on
I
are denoted by
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{f}}(I)
,

\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{lf}}(I)
, and
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{p}}(I)
, respectively. The total mass of

\mu\in\mathcal{M}_{\cdot}(I)
is denoted by
\lvert\mu\rvert
.

For
\mu\in\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{lf}}[0,\infty)
,
\hat{\mu}
denotes its Laplace transform
\hat{\mu}(\lambda)=\int_{[0,\infty)}e^{-\lambda t}\,\mu(dt)
. The convolution of
f\in\mathcal{S}^{\infty}_{\mathrm{loc}}[0,\infty)
and

\mu\in\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{lf}}[0,\infty)
, defined by
\int_{[0,\cdot]}f(\cdot-s)\,\mu(ds)
, is
denoted by
f*\mu
.

We recall that the
vague topology
on
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{lf}}(I)
, with convergence
denoted by
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle v}}{{\longrightarrow}}
, is the coarsest topology on
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{lf}}(I)
such that the
map
\mu\mapsto\int f\,d\mu
is continuous for each continuous function

f:I\to{\mathbb{R}}
with compact support. The
weak topology
on the
space
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{f}}[0,\infty)
, with convergence denoted by
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle w}}{{\longrightarrow}}
, is defined
similarly, but with the requirement of a compact support relaxed to
boundedness of the test functions
f
.

Random elements taking values in
\mathcal{M}(I)
are called
random
measures
, and random measures with values in
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{lf}}(I)
are said to be

locally finite
.
For a sequence of locally finite random measures
\{\xi_{n}\}_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
, we say that

\xi_{n}

converges to
\xi
in distribution
, and write
\xi_{n}\Rightarrow\xi
, if
\xi_{n}
converges weakly to
\xi
when interpreted as
a sequence of random elements in
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{lf}}(I)
equipped with the vague
topology. We refer the reader to
[
33
, Section 4.1, p. 111]
for a
textbook treatment and proofs of properties of the vague and weak
topologies used in the paper.

\lceil\cdot\rceil
denotes the ceiling function, i.e.,
\lceil x\rceil
is the
smallest integer greater than or equal to
x
.

2.
A convergence theorem for nonhomogeneous Hawkes processes

Before we present our main result, we introduce the
notation and adopt one of several similar not entirely equivalent
frameworks for Hawkes processes found in the literature. For background
information on random measures and point processes, we refer the reader to
standard texts such as
[
8
,
9
]
or
[
33
]
.

2.1.
Hawkes processes

A locally finite random measure
N
on
[0,\infty)
is called a

point process
if

N(A)\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
for every bounded measurable set
A\subseteq[0,\infty)
.
Each point process
N
admits a
sequence
\{T_{k}\}_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}
of
[0,\infty]
-valued random variables,
called the
points of
N
, such that
T_{0}\leq T_{1}\leq\dots
and

T_{k}\to\infty
, a.s., and

\displaystyle N=\textstyle\sum_{k}\delta_{T_{k}},

where
\delta_{t}
denotes the Dirac measure at
t
, and
the sum is taken only over
k
with
T_{k}<\infty
;
equivalently,

\delta_{+\infty}
is identified with the zero measure on
[0,\infty)
.
Since
\int f(t)\,N(dt)=\textstyle\sum_{k}f(T_{k})
, a.s., whenever
both sides are well defined,
we frequently use the shorthand

\displaystyle\textstyle\sum_{T\in N}f(T):=\int f(t)\,N(dt).

Recall that for
\mu\in\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{lf}}[0,\infty)
, the
Poisson process with
intensity measure
\mu
is the unique point process
P
such that 1)

P(A)
is Poisson distributed with mean
\mu(A)
for each bounded

A\in\mathcal{B}([0,\infty))
, and 2)
P(A_{1})
, …,
P(A_{n})
are independent
random variables whenever
A_{1},\dots,A_{n}
are measurable, bounded and
disjoint.

The definition of a standard Hawkes process involves two components: the
background arrival rate and the excitation function. It will be convenient
for our later analysis to separate the two and first construct a class of
processes without any background intensity, but started, instead, from a
single point (progenitor) at time
t=0
. In our nonhomogeneous setting, its
distribution is determined by two parameters: a constant
a\in(0,1)
and a
sequence
\pi=\{{\pi}^{m}\}_{m\in{\mathbb{N}}}
of
excitation profiles
, i.e.,
probability measures on
(0,\infty)
. Together, they form the sequence

\{a{\pi}^{m}\}_{m\in{\mathbb{N}}}
of
excitation measures
. To connect this
formulation with the standard notation, observe that when
\pi^{m}
is
absolutely continuous, we can define the excitation function
\phi^{m}

(associated with the rate at which the points in generation
m-1
produce
offspring in generation
m
) by
a\pi^{m}(dt)=\phi^{m}(t)\,dt
.

More precisely, the
nonhomogeneous single-progenitor Hawkes process

\tilde{H}
with parameters
a
and
\pi=\{{\pi}^{m}\}_{m\in{\mathbb{N}}}
is defined by

(2.1)

\displaystyle\tilde{H}:=\cup_{m\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}}{\tilde{H}}^{m},

where the sequence
\{\tilde{H}^{m}\}_{m\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}}
of "generations" is built
from a double sequence
{\tilde{P}}^{m}(k)
,
m\in{\mathbb{N}}
,
k\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}

of independent Poisson processes, where
{\tilde{P}}^{m}(k)
has intensity

{a\pi}^{m}
, for each
k\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
.
The zero-th generation
\tilde{H}^{0}

is simply the Dirac mass
\delta_{0}
at 0, i.e., a deterministic point process
with a single point at
0
, representing the lone progenitor.
Once the first
m
generations
{\tilde{H}}^{0},\dots,{\tilde{H}}^{m-1}
,

m\in{\mathbb{N}}
, have
been constructed, we set

(2.2)

\displaystyle{\tilde{H}}^{m}:=\bigcup_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}}\left\{{T}^{m-1}(k)+S\,:\,S\in{\tilde{P}}^{m-1}(k)\right\}

where
\{{T}^{m-1}(k)\}_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}}
denotes the
point sequence of
{\tilde{H}}^{m-1}
.
In keeping with the convention introduced above, the first union
is taken over
k
such that
{T}^{m-1}(k)<\infty
.

In the sequel,
we often identify a point process with its (random) point set.
Moreover, we abuse the notation and write, for example,

{\tilde{P}}^{m-1}(T)
for the
Poisson process
{\tilde{P}}^{m-1}(k)

whose index
k
is such that
T=T^{m-1}(k)
.
This way, (
2.2
) takes a more legible
form

{\tilde{H}}^{m}=\bigcup_{T\in{\tilde{H}}^{m-1}}\left(T+{\tilde{P}}^{m-1}(T)\right).

The Hawkes process is defined as a superposition of independent
single-progenitor Hawkes processes, started at points of an
underlying Poisson process. More precisely, in addition to the
parameters
a
and
\pi=\{{\pi}^{m}\}_{m\in{\mathbb{N}}}
of a single-progenitor process,
let a
background intensity
measure
\mu\in\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{lf}}[0,\infty)

be given. The
Hawkes process with
parameters
\mu
,
a
and
\pi
is defined by

(2.3)

\displaystyle H:=\bigcup_{T\in P}\left(T+\tilde{H}(T)\right),

where
P
is a Poisson process with intensity
\mu
and
\{\tilde{H}(j)\}_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}
is a
sequence of independent single-progenitor Hawkes processes with parameters

(a,\{{\pi}^{m}\}_{m\in{\mathbb{N}}})
,
independent of
P
. Thanks to the local finiteness of the Poisson process,
and the finiteness of single-progenitor Hawkes
processes (guaranteed by the assumption that
a<1
),
H
is also a locally
finite random measure.

2.2.
A convergence theorem

To describe the setting of our theorem, we consider a sequence
\{(a_{n},\{\pi^{m}_{n}\}_{m\in{\mathbb{N}}},\mu_{n})\}_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
of parameter triplets of nonhomogeneous Hawkes
processes. In particular, for each
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
,
a_{n}\in(0,1)
,
\mu_{n}
is a
locally bounded measure on
[0,\infty)
, and
\{\pi^{m}_{n}\}_{m\in{\mathbb{N}}}
is a sequence of
probability measures on
(0,\infty)
. For each
n
, we denote a Hawkes process
with parameters
(a_{n},\{\pi^{m}_{n}\}_{m\in{\mathbb{N}}},\mu_{n})
by
H_{n}
, and the associated
single-progenitor process by
\tilde{H}_{n}
. An important role will be played by the
sequence
\{\rho_{n}\}_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
of probability measures given by

(2.4)

\displaystyle\rho_{n}:=(1-a_{n})\Big(\delta_{0}+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}(a_{n})^{k}(\pi^{1}_{n}*\dots*\pi^{k}_{n})\Big)\in\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{p}}[0,\infty)\text{ for }n\in{\mathbb{N}}.

The measure

\rho_{n}
is the mean measure of
(1-a_{n})\tilde{H}_{n}
, i.e.,

\rho_{n}[B]/(1-a_{n})
is the
expected number of points of
\tilde{H}_{n}
in
B

for any
B\in\mathcal{B}[0,\infty)
.

For an infinitely divisible
\nu\in\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{p}}[0,\infty)

and
\tau>0
,
\nu^{\tau}
denotes the
\tau
-th convolutional
power of
\nu
, i.e.,
the probability measure on
[0,\infty)

characterized by
\widehat{\nu^{\tau}}=(\widehat{\nu})^{\tau}
.
We introduce the shorthand

(2.5)

\displaystyle m_{n}(\tau)=\lceil\tau(1-a_{n})^{-1}\rceil

and say that the array
\{\pi^{m}_{n}\}_{m,n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
is
locally infinitesimal
if

(2.6)

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\sup_{m\leq m_{n}(\tau)}\pi^{m}_{n}[\delta,\infty)=0\text{ for all }\tau,\delta>0.

Assumptions
A
and
A’
below, shown to be equivalent in Proposition

2.3
, provide sufficient structure on the array
\{\pi^{m}_{n}\}_{m,n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
for
our main theorem to hold.

Assumption A

(Aggregation at scale
(1-a_{n})^{-1}
)
.

There exists an infinitely divisible probability measure
\nu
on

[0,\infty)
with
\nu(\left\{0\right\})=0
such that

(2.7)

\displaystyle\pi^{1}_{n}*\dots*\pi^{m_{n}(\tau)}_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle w}}{{\longrightarrow}}\nu^{\tau}\text{ as $n\to\infty$ for all $\tau>0$.}

Remark 2.1
.

Note that Assumption
A
can be reformulated using a
row-wise independent array
\{X^{m}_{n}\}_{m,n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
of random variables
such that
X^{m}_{n}\sim\pi^{m}_{n}
as follows:

(2.8)

\displaystyle S_{n}(\tau):=\sum_{m=1}^{m_{n}(\tau)}X^{m}_{n}\Rightarrow L_{\tau}\text{ for all }\tau>0,

where

L
is a Lévy subordinator with
L_{1}\sim\nu
.

Assumption A’
.

There exists
a measure
\eta
on
(0,\infty)
with

\int_{(0,\infty)}\min(1,t)\,\eta(dt)<\infty
,
a constant
t_{0}>0
with

\eta(\left\{t_{0}\right\})=0
, and a constant
d\geq 0
such that

(1)

\{\pi^{m}_{n}\}_{m,n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
is locally infinitesimal,

(2)

\tfrac{1}{\tau}\sum_{m=1}^{m_{n}(\tau)}\int_{0}^{t_{0}}t\,\pi^{m}_{n}(dt)\to d+\int_{(0,t_{0}]}t\,\eta(dt)
for all
\tau>0
,

(3)

\tfrac{1}{\tau}\sum_{m=1}^{m_{n}(\tau)}\pi^{m}_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle v}}{{\longrightarrow}}\eta
on

(0,\infty)
for all

\tau>0
, and

(4)

either
d>0
or
\eta(0,\infty)=\infty
.

The infinitely divisible
distribution with the Laplace exponent

\lambda\mapsto\lambda d+\int_{0}^{\infty}(1-e^{-\lambda t})\,\eta(dt)

is then denoted by
\nu
.

Theorem 2.2
.

Let
\{H_{n}\}_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
be a sequence of Hawkes processes with parameter
triplets

\{(a_{n},\{\pi^{m}_{n}\}_{m\in{\mathbb{N}}},

\mu_{n})\}_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
with
a_{n}\nearrow 1
, such that
(equivalent) Assumptions
A
and
A’
hold and

(1-a_{n})\mu_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle v}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mu
for some
\mu\in\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{lf}}
.

Then there exists a locally finite random measure
\xi
on
[0,\infty)

such that

(2.9)

\displaystyle(1-a_{n})^{2}H_{n}\Rightarrow\xi.

Moreover,

\rho_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle w}}{{\longrightarrow}}\rho
, where
\hat{\rho}=(1-\log(\hat{\nu}))^{-1}
with
\nu

as in Assumption
A
, and
the law of
\xi
is characterized by

(2.10)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[e^{f*\xi}\big]=e^{h[f]*\mu}\text{ for all }f\in C[0,\infty)\text{ with }f(0)=0\text{ and }\left\lvert f\right\rvert\leq 1/2,

where
h[f]
denotes the unique locally bounded
solution of the convolutional Riccati equation

(2.11)

\displaystyle h=(f+\tfrac{1}{2}h^{2})*\rho.

While the proof of Theorem
2.2
is deferred to Appendix

A.4
, we comment on its assumptions, scope, and repercussions in
the next three subsections.

2.3.
Equivalence of and sufficient conditions for Assumptions
A
and
A’

The equivalence of Assumptions
A
and
A’
follows essentially
from the classical limit theorems for triangular arrays
going back to Kolmogorov and his co-authors. As with
all other proofs in this section, the
remaining details and references that complete the proof of Proposition
2.3
are deferred to
Appendix
A.1
.

Proposition 2.3
.

Assumptions
A
and
A’
are equivalent.

In view of this equivalence, we focus
solely on Assumption
A
in the sequel and
compare it with two natural weaker conditions, formulated as
Assumptions
B1
and
B2
, below. The first one
is stated in terms of the
mean measures
\{\rho_{n}\}_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
and not directly in terms of the excitation
profiles
\{\pi^{m}_{n}\}_{m,n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
. It is implied by Assumption
A
(via Theorem

2.2
) and is
clearly necessary for Theorem
2.2
to hold.

Assumption B1

(Convergence of mean measures)
.

There exists
\rho\in\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{p}}[0,\infty)
such that
\rho(\left\{0\right\})=0
and
\rho_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle w}}{{\longrightarrow}}\rho
.

The convergence of the sequence
\{\rho_{n}\}_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
is equivalent to the
convergence in distribution of the random sum

\sum_{m=1}^{G(1-a_{n})}X^{m}_{n}
, where
\{X^{m}_{n}\}_{m,n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
is as in Remark

2.1
and

G(p)
is an independent geometrically distributed random variable
with parameter
p
.

The second, weaker, condition is Assumption
A
specialized to
\tau=1
.

Assumption B2

(Central Limit Theorem)
.

There exists a probability measure
\nu
on
[0,\infty)
such that
\nu(\left\{0\right\})=0
and

\displaystyle\pi_{n}^{1}*\dots*\pi_{n}^{\lceil 1/(1-a_{n})\rceil}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle w}}{{\longrightarrow}}\nu.

It is clear that Assumption
B2
does not imply Assumption
A
.
As the following simple example demonstrates,
neither does Assumption
B1
, even under the additional assumption
of infinitesimality.

Example 2.4
.

Let
a_{n}=1-1/n
,
p_{n}=n^{-1/2}
,
q_{n}=1-p_{n}
,

m_{n}=\lceil\sqrt{n}\rceil
and define

\displaystyle\pi^{m}_{n}=\begin{cases}p_{n}\delta_{1}+q_{n}\delta_{0},&n-\sqrt{n}<m\leq n\text{
and $n$ is even,}\\
p_{n}\delta_{1}+q_{n}\delta_{0},&n<m\leq n+\sqrt{n}\text{ and $n$ is odd,}\\
\delta_{0},&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Since
p_{n}\to 0
,
(\pi^{n}_{m})
is an infinitesimal array.
The convolution
\pi^{1}_{n}*\dots*\pi_{n}^{n}
equals
\delta_{0}

when
n
is odd, and corresponds to the binomial
distribution with
1/p_{n}+o(1)
trials and success probability
p_{n}

when
n
is even.
Consequently, it does not converge
weakly as it approaches
a Poisson distribution with parameter
1

along even indices, and
\delta_{0}
along odd ones.

On the other hand, it is not difficult to see that
\rho_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle w}}{{\longrightarrow}}\rho
,
where
\rho
is a mixture of
\delta_{0}
and the Poisson distribution
with parameter
1
, with weights
1-e^{-1}
and
e^{-1}
.
The additional requirement
\rho(\left\{0\right\})=0
can be met by
redefining
\pi^{m}_{n}
from
\delta_{0}
to
\delta_{p_{n}}
for
m\leq\sqrt{n}
, which will simply shift all distributions above by
1
.

To bridge the gap between Assumptions
B1
and
B2
and Assumption

A
, we introduce another structural assumption requiring
approximate periodicity
with period
d_{n}=o((1-a_{n})^{-1})
, with an average error of size

o(1-a_{n})
. Before we state it,
we recall that the
Fortet-Mourier metric

d_{FM}
on
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{p}}[0,\infty)
is given by

(2.12)

\displaystyle d_{FM}(\nu_{1},\nu_{2})=\sup_{f}\left\lvert\int f\,d\nu_{1}-\int f\,d\nu_{2}\right\rvert

where the supremum is taken over all
[-1,1]
-valued

1
-Lipschitz
functions on
[0,\infty)
.

Assumption C

(Approximate periodicity)
.

The array
\{\pi^{m}_{n}\}_{m,n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
is locally infinitesimal and
there exists a sequence
\{d_{n}\}_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
in
{\mathbb{N}}
such that
(1-a_{n})d_{n}\to 0
and

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{m=1}^{m_{n}(\tau)}d_{FM}(\pi^{m}_{n},\pi_{n}^{m\,(\text{mod }d_{n})})=0\text{ for all }\tau>0,

where
m\,(\text{mod }d_{n})
denotes the remainder, taken in
\left\{1,\dots,d_{n}\right\}
,
after division by
d_{n}
.

We have the following implications between the assumptions above:

Proposition 2.5
.

Either Assumption
B1
or Assumption
B2
, together
with Assumption
C
, implies Assumption

A
.
Moreover, the probability measure
\rho
of Assumption
B1

and
\nu
of Assumption
A
are related by

\hat{\rho}=(1-\log(\hat{\nu}))^{-1}
.

3.
Properties of Feller random measures

Measures
\mu\in\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{lf}}[0,\infty)
and

\rho\in\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{p}}[0,\infty)
with
\rho(\left\{0\right\})=0

that appear in Theorem
2.2

uniquely define
a locally finite random measure
\xi
via
(
2.10
)
and (
2.11
).
We call such
\xi
the
Feller random measure
with
parameters
\mu
and
\rho
, and denote this by
\xi\sim F(\mu,\rho)
.
When it exists,
a nonnegative measurable process
\{Y_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
, such that

\xi[A]=\int_{A}Y_{t}\,dt\text{ for all }A\subseteq\mathcal{B}[0,\infty)
a.s.
is called the
density
of
\xi
.

Remark 3.1
.

It has been shown in

[
30
]
and
[
31
]
that, when
\rho
is the
Mittag-Leffler distribution with index
\alpha>1/2
and
\mu
is the
Lebesgue measure on
[0,\infty)
, the
Feller random measure
\xi

admits a density
Y

which has the same distribution as a solution to a
Volterra-type stochastic differential
equation of the form

(3.1)

\displaystyle Y_{t}=Y_{0}+c_{1}\int_{0}^{t}(t-s)^{\alpha-1}(\theta-Y_{s})\,ds+c_{2}\int_{0}^{t}(t-s)^{\alpha-1}\sqrt{Y_{s}}\,dB_{s},

where
c_{1},c_{2}
and
\theta
are constants and
B
is a Brownian motion.
The form of (
3.1
) explains why
Y
is called the
fractional (or rough) CIR (or Feller) process in the literature, and
why we adopt
the name Feller random measure for the general case. In addition to

[
30
,
31
]
, we refer the reader to
[
12
]

for further information on fractional CIR process and to

[
29
]
for a treatment of more general stochastic
differential equations of the Volterra type.

3.1.
Attainable limiting distributions
\rho

As we have seen above, a Feller random measure
F(\mu,\rho)
can be
constructed from measures

\mu\in\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{lf}}[0,\infty)
and
\rho\in\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{p}}[0,\infty)
, as long as they arise as limits in
Theorem
2.2
. While this clearly imposes no restriction on
\mu
, the
situation for
\rho\in\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{p}}[0,\infty)
is more subtle.

Definition 3.2
.

A random variable
X
is said to be

geometrically infinitely divisible (GID)
if
for each
p\in(0,1)
there exists an i.i.d. sequence
\{X_{m}(p)\}_{m\in{\mathbb{N}}}
of
random variables such that

X\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(d)}}{{=}}\sum_{m=1}^{G(p)}X_{m}(p),

where
G(p)
is
an
{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
-valued geometrically distributed random variable with
parameter (probability of success)
p
, independent of
the sequence
\{X_{m}(p)\}_{m\in{\mathbb{N}}}
.

This notion was introduced in
[
35
]
as part of the
answer to the following question posed by V. M. Zolotarev:
characterize the family
\mathcal{Y}
of distributions of random
variables
Y
such that,
for any
p\in(0,1)
, there exists a random variable
X(p)
such that

\displaystyle Y\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(d)}}{{=}}X(p)+B(p)Y

where
Y
,
X(p)
and
B(p)
are independent and

\mathbb{P}\left[B(p)=1\right]=1-\mathbb{P}\left[B(p)=0\right]=p
.
In the same paper, the authors show that

\mathcal{Y}
coincides with the set of all GID
distributions. Furthermore, they prove
that
a probability measure
\rho
on
[0,\infty)
is GID if and only if its
Laplace transform
\hat{\rho}
has the form

(3.2)

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}(\lambda)=\frac{1}{1-\log(\hat{\nu}(\lambda))},

where
\hat{\nu}
is the Laplace transform of some infinitely divisible
probability measure
\nu
on
[0,\infty)
.

By the Lévy-Khinchin representation, this is equivalent to

\hat{\rho}
admitting the following form

(3.3)

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}(\lambda)=\frac{1}{1+d\lambda+\int_{0}^{\infty}(1-e^{-\lambda t})\,\eta(dt)}\,,

for some
constant
d\geq 0
and some measure
\eta
on
(0,\infty)
with

\int_{0}^{\infty}\min(1,t)\,\eta(dt)<\infty
.

The very definition of infinite divisibility implies that for any sequence
\{a_{n}\}_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
with
a_{n}\nearrow 1
and
any infinitely divisible probability measure
\nu
on
[0,\infty)
there exists a
row-constant array, i.e., an array
of the form
\pi^{m}_{n}=\pi_{n}
, such that Assumption
B2
holds.
In this case, Assumption
C
holds trivially, and Theorem

2.2
yields the following result (for related work
in the context of the
theory of random summation
the reader is
referred to
[
36
]
):

Proposition 3.3
.

Any probability measure
\rho
that appears as the limit

\rho=\lim\rho_{n}

in Theorem
2.2
is GID. Conversely, given any sequence

\{a_{n}\}_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
with
a_{n}\nearrow 1
and any GID probability
\rho
, there exists
a locally infinitesimal, row-constant array

\{\pi^{m}_{n}\}_{m,n\in{\mathbb{N}}}=\{\pi_{n}\}_{m,n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
of excitation profiles so that

\rho=\lim\rho_{n}
.

Although general arrays
\{\pi^{m}_{n}\}_{m,n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
provide additional
modeling flexibility,
row-constant arrays, as stated in Proposition
3.3
,
already generate all possible
limiting probabilities
\rho
. However, when the dependence on
n

is further restricted, the class of attainable probability
measures
\rho
becomes more limited. In the case
when
\pi^{m}_{n}=\pi_{n}
is given by a rescaled version of the same probability
distribution
\pi
(as is the case, e.g., in
[
30
,
31
]
),
the limiting distribution, up to scaling, belongs to a specific
one-parameter family, which in turn essentially determines the sequence

\{a_{n}\}_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
.

Definition 3.4
.

A probability measure
\rho
on
[0,\infty)
is called the

Mittag-Leffler
distribution with parameter

\alpha\in(0,1]
if its Laplace
transform
\hat{\rho}
takes the form

(3.4)

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}(\lambda)=\frac{1}{1+\lambda^{\alpha}}.

The Mittag-Leffler distribution admits an explicit density

(3.5)

\displaystyle p^{\alpha}(t)=t^{\alpha-1}E_{\alpha,\alpha}(-t^{\alpha})\text{ for }t\geq 0,

where, for
\alpha,\beta>0
, the

Mittag-Leffler function

E_{\alpha,\beta}
is given by

\displaystyle E_{\alpha,\beta}(x)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{x^{n}}{\Gamma(\alpha n+\beta)}\text{ for }x\geq 0,

and
\Gamma
denotes the Gamma-function.
In the special case
\alpha=1
this distribution is exponential, with
rate
1
,
while for
\alpha=1/2
its density takes an especially simple form.
Indeed, a straightforward, albeit somewhat tedious, derivation directly from
the definition
yields

(3.6)

\displaystyle p^{1/2}(t)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\left(\Big.\sqrt{2t}-m\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2t}}\right)\right),

where
m(x)=\frac{1-\Phi(x)}{\varphi(x)}
is the ratio (known as the Mills
ratio) of the survival function
1-\Phi

and the density
\varphi
of the standard normal distribution.

For the convenience of the reader, we present here
a classical result (see
[
21
, Theorem 2.5.1, p. 35 and Theorem 2.5.2,
p. 37]
) reformulated to fit our setting:

Proposition 3.5
.

Let
\pi
be a probability measure on
[0,\infty)
with
\pi(\left\{0\right\})=0

and let the array
\{\pi^{m}_{n}\}_{m,n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
be given by

(3.7)

\displaystyle\pi^{m}_{n}(B)=\pi\left(nB\right)\text{ for all }B\in\mathcal{B}[0,\infty).

Then
\{\pi^{m}_{n}\}_{m,n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
satisfies Assumption
A

if and only if one of the following two conditions is satisfied:

(1)

\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{\pi[t,\infty)}{\frac{1}{t}\int_{0}^{t}\pi[s,\infty)\,ds}=0
, or

(2)

there exists
\alpha\in(0,1)
such that

\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{\pi[t,\infty)}{\pi[ct,\infty)}=c^{\alpha}
for all

c>0
.

In either case, if
\{a_{n}\}_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
is such that

a_{n}=1-\kappa_{n}n^{-\alpha}
for some
\{\kappa_{n}\}_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
with
\kappa_{n}\to\kappa\in(0,\infty)
, then
\{\rho_{n}\}_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
converges to
a (possibly scaled) Mittag-Leffler distribution with parameter
\alpha

(where
\alpha=1
in case
1
).

Remark 3.6
.

(1)

In the case
1
the limiting distribution

\rho
is exponential and the condition is satisfied,
in particular, if the probability
measure
\pi
admits a finite first moment
\int_{0}^{\infty}t\,\pi(dt)
as in
[
30
]
.

(2)

The case
2
covers all
\pi
such that

\pi[t,\infty)
is a regularly varying function with a nontrivial tail,
i.e.,

\displaystyle\pi[t,\infty)\sim l(t)t^{-\alpha}\text{ as }t\to\infty

for some
\alpha\in(0,1)
and some
slowly varying (e.g., constant) function
l
.

(3)

The choice of
n
as the scaling factor in (
3.7
) is
simply a convenient normalization and can be generalized easily.

3.2.
Distributional properties

Proposition
3.7
below provides a recursive scheme for
efficient computation of cumulants (and, therefore, moments) of
integral functionals of the Feller random measure
\xi
.
It is based on an the Adomian decomposition of the convolutional
Riccati equation (
2.11
) which
is described in Appendix
A.3
,
together with several other
properties of solutions of convolutional Riccati equations used in the proof
of the main theorem.

3.2.1.
Cumulants

We recall that a real sequence
\{\kappa_{n}[Y]\}_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
is called the

sequence of cumulants
of (the distribution of) the random variable
Y

if

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\varepsilon Y\right)\right]=\exp\left(\textstyle\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{\varepsilon^{n}}{n!}\kappa_{n}[Y]\right)

for
\varepsilon
in some neighborhood of
0
. For a pair
(X,Y)
, we also
define the
partial cumulants

\kappa_{n}[X,Y]
by

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(X+\varepsilon Y\right)\right]=\exp\left(\textstyle\sum_{n\geq 0}\frac{\varepsilon^{n}}{n!}\,\kappa_{n}[X,Y]\right),

provided the series converges for
\varepsilon
in some neighborhood of
0
.
As is well known, cumulants determine the moments of the
distribution.
Indeed, the two sequences
are related to one another via an explicit formula based on Faà di
Bruno’s formula and involving Bell polynomials (see
[
47
]
).

The next result shows that cumulants of random variables of the form

f*\xi
, where
\xi\sim F(\mu,\rho)
, satisfy a simple
recursion and admit explicit representation. Partial
cumulants can be expressed in terms of a solution of a system of
convolutional equations. The proof is provided in Appendix
A.2.2
.

Proposition 3.7
.

Let
\xi
be a Feller random measure with
parameters
\mu
and
\rho

and let
f,f_{0}:[0,\infty)\to{\mathbb{R}}
be continuous functions which vanish at
0
.

(1)

The cumulants
\kappa_{n}[f*\xi]
of
f*\xi
are given by

\kappa_{n}[f*\xi]=n!\,K_{n}*\mu\text{ for }n\geq 1,

where the functions
K_{n}\in\mathcal{S}^{\infty}_{\mathrm{loc}}[0,\infty)
are defined recursively by

(3.8)

\displaystyle K_{1}=f*\rho,\ K_{n}=\tfrac{1}{2}\left(\textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}K_{i}K_{n-i}\right)*\rho\text{ for }n\geq 2.

(2)

If
\sup f_{0}<1/2
, the partial cumulants
\kappa_{n}[f_{0}*\xi,f*\xi]
are given by

\kappa_{n}[f_{0}*\xi,f*\xi]=n!\,K^{\prime}_{n}*\mu\text{ for }n\geq 1,

where
\{K^{\prime}_{n}\}_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}}
is the unique solution in

(\mathcal{S}^{\infty}_{\mathrm{loc}}[0,\infty))^{{\mathbb{N}}_{0}}
of the system

(3.9)

\begin{split}K^{\prime}_{0}&=h[f_{0}],\quad\\
K^{\prime}_{1}&=f+\left(K^{\prime}_{0}K^{\prime}_{1}\right)*\rho,\text{ and }\\
K^{\prime}_{n}&=\tfrac{1}{2}\left(\textstyle\sum_{i=0}^{n}K^{\prime}_{i}K^{\prime}_{n-i}\right)*\rho,\text{ for }n\geq 2.\end{split}

Remark 3.8
.

The first three cumulants/moments are given below:

(3.10)

\begin{split}\kappa_{1}&=\mathbb{E}\left[f*\xi\right]=(f*\rho)*\mu\\
\kappa_{2}&=\mathrm{Var}\left[f*\xi\right]=\left(\Big.(f*\rho)^{2}*\rho\right)*\mu\\
\kappa_{3}&=\mathbb{E}\left[\left(f*\xi-\mathbb{E}\left[f*\xi\right]\right)^{3}\right]=3\left(\Big.\left(\Big.\left(\big.(f*\rho)((f*\rho)^{2}*\rho)\right)\right)*\rho\right)*\mu\end{split}

3.2.2.
Infinite divisibility

The following property of
F(\mu,\rho)
follows directly
from its characterization (
2.10
), (
2.11
).

Proposition 3.9
.

Suppose that
\xi_{1}\sim F(\mu_{1},\rho)
and
\xi_{2}\sim F(\mu_{2},\rho)
where

\mu_{1},\mu_{2}
are locally finite and
\rho
is a
probability measure with
\rho(\left\{0\right\})=0
. If
\xi_{1}
and
\xi_{2}

are independent, then

\xi_{1}+\xi_{2}\sim F(\mu_{1}+\mu_{2},\rho).

Corollary 3.10
.

Given
\xi\in F(\mu,\rho)
,
the random variable
(f*\xi)(t)
is infinitely divisible for all

t\geq 0
and
all
f\in C[0,\infty)
with
f(0)=0
.

A stochastic process
\left\{Y_{t}\right\}_{t\geq 0}
is said to be

infinitely divisible
if all of its finite-dimensional distributions
are infinitely divisible.

Corollary 3.11
.

Suppose that
F(\frac{1}{N}\mu,\rho)
admits a right-continuous density

Y^{(N)}
for each
N\in{\mathbb{N}}
. Then
Y^{(1)}
is infinitely divisible.

3.2.3.
The Covariance Structure

The polarization identity and
the expression for
\kappa_{2}
in (
3.10
) yield

(3.11)

\displaystyle\Cov[f*\xi,g*\xi]=\Big(\big((f*\rho)(g*\rho)\big)*\rho\Big)*\mu,

for all
f,g\in C[0,\infty)
with
f(0)=g(0)=0
.
Equation (
3.11
) can be rewritten as

(3.12)

\displaystyle\Cov[f*\xi,g*\xi](t)=\iint f(t-r)g(t-s)\gamma(dr,ds),

where

(3.13)

\displaystyle\gamma(dr,ds)=\int\rho(dr-u)\rho(ds-u)(\rho*\mu)(du)

i.e.,

\gamma(B)=\int\int\int 1_{{B+(u,u)}}(s,r)\rho(ds)\rho(dr)\,(\rho*\mu)(du)
for each Borel
B\subseteq[0,\infty)\times[0,\infty)
.
In the special case when
\rho
admits a density
p

with respect to Lebesgue measure, the measure
\gamma
is absolutely
continuous and

(3.14)

\displaystyle\gamma(dr,ds)=\Sigma(r,s)\,dr\,ds\text{ where }\Sigma(r,s)=\int p(r-u)p(s-u)(p*\mu)(u)\,du.

A further specialization yields tight asymptotics
around the diagonal
r=s
.
For functions
f:D_{f}\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}
and

g:D_{g}\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}
, we write
f\approx g
if for each bounded
B\in\mathcal{B}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})

there exists a strictly positive constant
C
such that

f\leq Cg
and
g\leq Cf
on
D_{f}\cap D_{g}\cap B
.

Proposition 3.12
.

Suppose that
\rho
is a Mittag-Leffler distribution with parameter

\alpha\in(0,1]
,
and that
\mu
is the Lebesgue measure on
[0,\infty)
.
Then

(3.15)

\displaystyle\Sigma(r,s)\approx B_{\alpha}(r,s)

where
B_{\alpha}:\left\{(s,r)\in(0,\infty)^{2}\,:\,s\neq r\right\}\to(0,\infty)
is
a symmetric function defined for
r<s
by

(3.16)

\displaystyle B_{\alpha}(r,s)=r^{2\alpha}s^{\alpha-1}\begin{cases}\left(1-\frac{r}{s}\right)^{2\alpha-1},&\alpha<\tfrac{1}{2},\\
1-\log(1-\frac{r}{s}),&\alpha=\tfrac{1}{2},\\
1,&\alpha>\tfrac{1}{2}.\end{cases}

Proof.

Being entire, the Mittag-Leffler function
E_{\alpha,\alpha}

satisfies

E_{\alpha,\alpha}\approx 1
, and so, thanks to (
3.5
),
we have
p(t)\approx t^{\alpha-1}
.
Moreover, since
\mu
is the Lebesgue measure, we have

(p*\mu)(t)\approx t^{\alpha}
so that

\displaystyle p(r-u)p(s-u)(p*\mu)(u)\approx(r-u)^{\alpha-1}(s-u)^{\alpha-1}u^{\alpha}.

Therefore, by (
3.14
),

(3.17)

\begin{split}\Sigma(r,s)&\approx\int_{0}^{r}(r-u)^{\alpha-1}(s-u)^{\alpha-1}u^{\alpha}\,du\\
&=r^{2\alpha}s^{\alpha-1}\int_{0}^{1}(1-w)^{\alpha-1}(1-\tfrac{r}{s}w)^{\alpha-1}w^{\alpha}dw.\end{split}

According to
[
42
, eq. (15.6.1)]
, we have

(3.18)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}(1-w)^{\alpha-1}(1-\tfrac{r}{s}w)^{\alpha-1}w^{\alpha}dw=\Gamma(\alpha+1)\Gamma(\alpha)\,{}_{2}F_{1}\left(1-\alpha,\alpha+1;2\alpha+1;\frac{r}{s}\right),

where
{}_{2}F_{1}
denotes the hypergeometric function.
We have the following three

3.2.4.
Absolute continuity and absence of atoms

It is a direct consequence of Proposition
3.12
, as formulated
in Corollary

3.13
below, that in the special case of the Mittag-Leffler
measure
\rho
, a density may or may not exist, depending
on the value of the parameter
\alpha
.

Corollary 3.13
.

Suppose that
\rho
is a Mittag-Leffler distribution with parameter

\alpha\in(0,1]
, and that
\mu
is the Lebesgue measure on
[0,\infty)
.
Then
\xi\sim F(\mu,\rho)
admits a square-integrable density
if and only if
\alpha>1/2
.

On the other hand, Feller measures are almost surely
non-atomic for all parameter pairs:

Proposition 3.14
.

For any GID
\rho
with
\rho(\left\{0\right\})=0
and any locally finite
\mu
, the
Feller random measure

\xi\sim F(\mu,\rho)
is almost surely atom-free, i.e.

\displaystyle\xi(\left\{t\right\})=0\text{ for all }t\geq 0\text{ a.s.}

Proof.

The relation (
3.12
) shows that
the measure
\gamma
given by (
3.13
) is the covariance measure of

\xi
, i.e., that

(3.19)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\iint 1_{{C}}(r,s)\,\xi(dr)\,\xi(ds)\right]=\gamma(C),\text{ for all Borel sets $C\subseteq[0,\infty)^{2}$. }

In particular, when
D=\left\{(t,t)\,:\,t\in[0,\infty)\right\}

is the diagonal
of
[0,\infty)^{2}
, equation (
3.19
) becomes

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{t\geq 0}\xi(\left\{t\right\})^{2}\Big]=\gamma(D),

where the sum is taken over the at most countable set of
t
with

\xi(\left\{t\right\})>0
.

It follows directly from (
3.13
) that

(3.20)

\displaystyle\gamma(D)=\int\sum_{t\geq u}\rho(\left\{t-u\right\})^{2}(\rho*\mu)(du)=\mu[0,\infty)\sum_{t}\rho(\left\{t\right\})^{2}.

Let
\nu
be the infinitely divisible probability measure on
[0,\infty)

related to
\rho
by (
3.2
), and let
d
and
\eta
be the
drift and the jump measure of
\nu
, respectively.
Since
\rho(\left\{0\right\})=0
, we have
\lim_{\lambda\to\infty}\hat{\rho}(\lambda)=0
,
which implies that either

or

\eta(0,\infty)=+\infty
. Either condition (see
[
46
, Theorem
27.4, p. 175]
) implies that
\nu^{\tau}
is atomless for each

\tau>0
. Being a mixture of the family

\left\{\nu^{\tau}\,:\,\tau>0\right\}

of atomless measures, the measure
\rho
is atomless as well, which implies that

\gamma(D)=0
via (

).
∎

used throughout the paper. We fix

and consider functions

A.2.1.
A stability estimate and a comparison principle

The central result of this subsection is a stability estimate
(
A.8
) in Proposition
A.1
below;
it will be used in the proof of Theorem
2.2
in
Section

.

Proposition A.1
.

Suppose that
M\geq 0
and
K_{i},F_{i}\in\mathcal{S}^{\infty}[0,T]
are such that

\left\lvert K_{i}\right\rvert_{\mathcal{S}^{\infty}[0,T]}\leq M
for
i=1,2
and

\displaystyle K_{i}=F_{i}+\tfrac{1}{2}K^{2}_{i}*\rho\text{ for }i=1,2,

then

(A.8)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\left\lvert K_{2}-K_{1}\right\rvert_{\mathcal{S}^{\infty}[0,t]}\,dt\leq C\int_{0}^{T}\left\lvert F_{1}-F_{2}\right\rvert_{\mathcal{S}^{\infty}[0,t]}\,dt.

where
C
depends only on
\rho
,
M
and
T
.

Proof.

For
t\in[0,T]
, we define

m(t)=\left\lvert K_{2}-K_{1}\right\rvert_{\mathcal{S}^{\infty}[0,t]}
and

m_{F}(t)=\left\lvert F_{2}-F_{1}\right\rvert_{\mathcal{S}^{\infty}[0,t]}
.
For
s\leq t\leq T
, we have

\displaystyle\left\lvert K_{2}(s)-K_{1}(s)\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\left\lvert F_{2}(s)-F_{1}(s)\right\rvert+\tfrac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{s}\left\lvert K_{2}^{2}(s-u)-K_{1}^{2}(s-u)\right\rvert\,\rho(du)

\displaystyle\leq m_{F}(s)+M\int_{0}^{s}m(s-u)\,\rho(du),

so that

\displaystyle m\leq m_{F}+M(m*\rho)\text{ on }[0,T].

We multiply both sides by
\exp\left(-\lambda\cdot\right)
and integrate over
[0,T]
to
obtain

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}e^{-\lambda t}m(t)\,dt

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{T}e^{-\lambda t}m_{F}(t)\,dt+M\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\lambda(t-u)}m(t-u)\,e^{-\lambda u}\rho(du)\,dt

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}e^{-\lambda t}m_{F}(t)\,dt+M\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{T-u}e^{-\lambda s}m(s)\,ds\,e^{-\lambda u}\rho(du)

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{T}e^{-\lambda t}m_{F}(t)\,dt+M\left(\int_{0}^{T}e^{-\lambda t}m(t)\,dt\right)\left(\int_{0}^{T}e^{-\lambda u}\rho(du)\right)

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{T}e^{-\lambda t}m_{F}(t)\,dt+\left(M\int_{0}^{T}e^{-\lambda u}\rho(du)\right)\int_{0}^{T}e^{-\lambda t}m(t)\,dt.

Since
\rho(\left\{0\right\})=0
, there exists

such that

\displaystyle M\int_{0}^{T}e^{-\lambda_{0}u}\rho(du)\leq 1/2.

With such

, we have

which implies (

.
∎

A.2.2.
The Adomian decomposition

This subsection implements the Adomian decomposition, introduced in

[
1
]
, in our particular setting.
No original content is claimed; the results here are
routine extensions of existing results (see
[

]

[

We start from an infinite triangular system of convolutional equations:

(A.9)

\begin{split}K_{0}&=B,\quad\\
K_{1}&=F+\left(K_{0}K_{1}\right)*\rho\\
K_{n}&=\left(\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=0}^{n}K_{i}K_{n-i}\right)*\rho\text{ for }n>2.\end{split}

in the unknown functions

, where

Lemma A.3
.

Suppose that
\left\lvert B\right\rvert_{\mathcal{S}^{\infty}[0,T]}<1
.
Then the system (
A.9

(\mathcal{S}^{\infty}[0,T])^{{\mathbb{N}}_{0}}

(A.10)

\displaystyle\left\lvert K_{n}[B,F]\right\rvert_{\mathcal{S}^{\infty}[0,T]}\leq Cn^{-3/2}\left(\frac{2\left\lvert F\right\rvert_{\mathcal{S}^{\infty}[0,T]}}{(1-\left\lvert B\right\rvert_{\mathcal{S}^{\infty}[0,T]})^{2}}\right)^{n}\text{ for all }n\in{\mathbb{N}}.

Proof.

We observe that for

n

A.9
) can be written in the form

(A.11)

\displaystyle K_{n}=F_{n}+\left(BK_{n}\right)*\rho,\text{ where }F_{n}=\begin{cases}F,&n=1,\\
\tfrac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}(K_{i}K_{n-i})*\rho,&n\geq 2.\end{cases}

We also observe that

F_{n}
does not involve
K_{n}
or any
K_{m}
with

.

Since
\left\lvert B\right\rvert_{\mathcal{S}^{\infty}[0,T]}<1
, it follows inductively
from a standard argument based

(
A.9
) has a unique solution
\{K_{n}[B,F]\}_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}}

in
(\mathcal{S}^{\infty}[0,T])^{{\mathbb{N}}_{0}}
and that its solution satisfies the following estimate

\displaystyle\left\lvert K_{n}[B,F]\right\rvert_{\mathcal{S}^{\infty}[0,T]}\leq M\left\lvert F_{n}\right\rvert_{\mathcal{S}^{\infty}[0,T]}\text{ where }M=(1-\left\lvert B\right\rvert_{\mathcal{S}^{\infty}[0,T]})^{-1}.

This further implies that

\left\lvert K_{1}[B,F]\right\rvert_{\mathcal{S}^{\infty}[0,T]}\leq M\left\lvert F\right\rvert_{\mathcal{S}^{\infty}[0,T]}
and that

\displaystyle\left\lvert K_{n}[B,F]\right\rvert_{\mathcal{S}^{\infty}[0,T]}\leq\tfrac{1}{2}M\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\left\lvert K_{i}[B,F]\right\rvert_{\mathcal{S}^{\infty}[0,T]}\left\lvert K_{n-i}[B,F]\right\rvert_{\mathcal{S}^{\infty}[0,T]}\text{ for }n\geq 2.

If

. Otherwise, we set

\displaystyle c_{n}=\frac{2^{n-1}\left\lvert K_{n}[B,F]\right\rvert_{\mathcal{S}^{\infty}[0,T]}}{M^{2n-1}\left\lvert F\right\rvert_{\mathcal{S}^{\infty}[0,T]}^{n}},\text{ for }n\geq 1,

so that, by (

[

\displaystyle C_{n}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}C_{k-1}C_{n-k},\ C_{0}=1.

Hence, by induction,

, for all

for Catalan numbers (see e.g.
[

for some universal constant

,

Lemma A.4
.

Let

be such that

\left\lvert B\right\rvert_{\mathcal{S}^{\infty}[0,T]}<1
and
\left\lvert F\right\rvert_{\mathcal{S}^{\infty}[0,T]}\leq\tfrac{1}{2}(1-\left\lvert B\right\rvert_{\mathcal{S}^{\infty}[0,T]})^{2}

be the

A.9
). Then
the series
\textstyle\sum_{n\geq 0}K_{n}[B,F]

converges absolutely in
\mathcal{S}^{\infty}[0,T]

\displaystyle K[B,F]:=\textstyle\sum_{n\geq 0}K_{n}[B,F]

satisfies the equation

(A.13)

\displaystyle K[B,F]=F+\tfrac{1}{2}(K[B,F])^{2}*\rho+B-\tfrac{1}{2}B^{2}*\rho

Proof.

Let
K=K[B,F]
and
K_{n}=K_{n}[B,F]
, for
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
.
Lemma
A.3
and
the assumption on the size of
F
imply that

\left\lvert K_{n}\right\rvert_{\mathcal{S}^{\infty}[0,T]}\leq Cn^{-3/2}\text{ for }n\in{\mathbb{N}}.

This, in turn, implies that
the series
\textstyle\sum_{n\geq 0}K_{n}

converges absolutely in
\mathcal{S}^{\infty}[0,T]
.
Moreover,

\displaystyle\tfrac{1}{2}K^{2}*\rho

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2}\Big(\textstyle\sum_{n\geq 0}K_{n}\Big)^{2}*\rho=\tfrac{1}{2}K_{0}^{2}*\rho+(K_{0}K_{1})*\rho+\textstyle\sum_{n\geq 2}\big(\tfrac{1}{2}\textstyle\sum_{i=0}^{n}K_{i}K_{n-i}\big)*\rho

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2}B^{2}*\rho+(K_{1}-F)+\textstyle\sum_{n\geq 2}K_{n}=\tfrac{1}{2}B^{2}*\rho-B+K-F.\qed

Lemma A.5
.

Suppose that
F\in\mathcal{S}^{\infty}[0,T]
satisfies
\left\lvert F\right\rvert\leq 1/2
and that

K
solves (
A.7
). Then

(A.14)

\displaystyle-\left\lvert F\right\rvert_{\mathcal{S}^{\infty}[0,t]}\leq K(t)\leq 1-\sqrt{1-2\left\lvert F\right\rvert_{\mathcal{S}^{\infty}[0,t]}}\text{ for all }t\in[0,T].

Proof.

To get the lower bound, we simply observe that
K\geq F
.
For the upper bound, we define

\displaystyle K_{2}(t)=1-\sqrt{1-2\left\lvert F\right\rvert_{\mathcal{S}^{\infty}[0,t]}}.

Since
K_{2}
is nonnegative and nondecreasing, we have
K_{2}^{2}*\rho\leq K_{2}^{2}
, and, so,

\displaystyle K_{2}(t)-\tfrac{1}{2}(K_{2}^{2}*\rho)(t)

\displaystyle\geq K_{2}(t)-\tfrac{1}{2}K^{2}_{2}(t)=\left\lvert F\right\rvert_{\mathcal{S}^{\infty}[0,t]}\geq F(t)

We have

\displaystyle(K+K_{2})(t)

\displaystyle\geq-\left\lvert F\right\rvert_{\mathcal{S}^{\infty}[0,t]}+1-\sqrt{1-2\left\lvert F\right\rvert_{\mathcal{S}^{\infty}[0,t]}}=\tfrac{1}{2}\left(1-\sqrt{1-2\left\lvert F\right\rvert_{\mathcal{S}^{\infty}[0,t]}}\right)^{2}\geq 0,

which allows us to use
Proposition
A.2
above with
K_{1}=K
and
F_{1}=F_{2}=F

to conclude that
K\leq K_{2}
.
∎

Proposition A.6
.

If
\left\lvert F\right\rvert_{\mathcal{S}^{\infty}[0,T]}\leq 1/2
the function

(A.15)

\displaystyle K[F]=\textstyle\sum_{n\geq 1}K_{n}[0,F]

defines the unique solution of (
A.7
) in
\mathcal{S}^{\infty}[0,T]
.
Moreover, if
G\in\mathcal{S}^{\infty}[0,T]
and
\varepsilon\in{\mathbb{R}}
are such that

\left\lvert F\right\rvert_{\mathcal{S}^{\infty}[0,T]}+\left\lvert\varepsilon\right\rvert\left\lvert G\right\rvert_{\mathcal{S}^{\infty}[0,T]}\leq 1/2
we have

\displaystyle K[F+\varepsilon G]=K[F]+\textstyle\sum_{n\geq 1}\varepsilon^{n}K_{n}[K[F],G].

with absolute convergence in
\mathcal{S}^{\infty}[0,T]
.

Proof.

In the special case
B=0
, the conditions of
Lemma
A.4
are satisfied as soon as

\left\lvert F\right\rvert_{\mathcal{S}^{\infty}[0,T]}\leq 1/2
. Therefore (
A.15
)
defines a solution to (
A.7
). Uniqueness is the
content of Proposition
A.1
above.

Let
F
,
G
and
\varepsilon
be as in the second part of the statement.
Thanks to
Lemma
A.5
above, we have

\displaystyle\left\lvert K[F]\right\rvert_{\mathcal{S}^{\infty}[0,T]}\leq\max\left(\left\lvert F\right\rvert_{\mathcal{S}^{\infty}[0,T]},1-\sqrt{1-2\left\lvert F\right\rvert_{\mathcal{S}^{\infty}[0,T]}}\right)=1-\sqrt{1-2\left\lvert F\right\rvert_{\mathcal{S}^{\infty}[0,T]}},

so that

\displaystyle\tfrac{1}{2}(1-\left\lvert K[F]\right\rvert_{\mathcal{S}^{\infty}[0,T]})^{2}\geq 1/2-\left\lvert F\right\rvert_{\mathcal{S}^{\infty}[0,T]}\geq\left\lvert\varepsilon G\right\rvert_{\mathcal{S}^{\infty}[0,T]},

which is exactly what is required for Lemma
A.4
to
apply. Therefore,

\displaystyle K[F+\varepsilon G]=K[F]+\textstyle\sum_{n\geq 1}K_{n}[K[F],\varepsilon G].

It remains to observe that functions
\varepsilon^{-n}K_{n}[K[F],\varepsilon G]
solve the system (
A.9
) for any

\varepsilon\neq 0
, so that, by uniqueness, we have

K_{n}[K[F],\varepsilon G]=\varepsilon^{n}K_{n}[K[F],G]\text{ for }\varepsilon\in{\mathbb{R}}.\qed

A.3.
Auxiliary facts about Hawkes processes

This section introduces partial Hawkes processes and
collects several facts
that will be used in the proof
of Theorem
2.2
in the Section
A.4
.
The notation is inherited from the
beginning of Section
2
.

A.3.1.
Partial Hawkes processes

The parameters
a
and
\{{\pi}^{m}\}_{m\in{\mathbb{N}}}
of a single-progenitor
Hawkes process can be used to construct
a double sequence of
partial single-progenitor Hawkes
processes

\tilde{H}^{[m,m+k)}
,
m\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
,
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
which will be needed in the sequel.
The process
\tilde{H}^{[m,m+k)}
starts with a single individual in
generation
m\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
, and accumulates individuals over the next
k-1
generations.
This construction is distributionally equivalent to collecting the first
k
generations
of a single-progenitor process with parameters
a
and

(\pi^{m+1},\pi^{m+2},\dots)
.
These individual generations are denoted by
\tilde{H}^{m,(m+j)}
,

j=0,\dots,k-1
so that

\tilde{H}^{[m,m+k)}=\bigcup_{j=0}^{k-1}\tilde{H}^{m,(m+j)}
.

For
k\geq 1
, conditioning on the first generation
\tilde{H}^{m,(m+1)}

of
\tilde{H}^{m}

gives the following fundamental recursive distributional equality:

(A.16)

\displaystyle\tilde{H}^{[m,m+k)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(d)}}{{=}}\left\{0\right\}\cup\bigcup_{T\in{\tilde{P}}^{m+1}}\left(T+\tilde{H}^{[m+1,m+k)}(T)\right)\text{ for }k\geq 1,

where
{\tilde{P}}^{m+1}
is a Poisson process with intensity

a{\pi}^{m+1}
, and
(\tilde{H}^{[m+1,m+k)}(T))_{T\in\tilde{P}^{m+1}}

are independent partial single-progenitor Hawkes processes.
We accumulate over all
k
in (
A.16
) to obtain

(A.17)

\displaystyle\tilde{H}^{m}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(d)}}{{=}}\left\{0\right\}\cup\bigcup_{T\in{\tilde{P}}^{m+1}}\left(T+\tilde{H}^{m+1}(T)\right).

The
convolutional moment-generating functional

M_{\xi}

is given by

(A.18)

\displaystyle M_{\xi}[f](t)=\mathbb{E}\left[e^{(f*\xi)(t)}\right]\in[0,\infty]\text{ for }f\in\mathcal{S}^{\infty}_{\mathrm{loc}}[0,\infty),\,t\in[0,\infty).

This version of the standard moment-generating
functional
proves to be easier to work with in the context of Hawkes processes
than its classical counterpart.
We note that, unlike in the standard case, the functional
M_{\xi}

depends on the parameter
t
. While this dependence does
not encode any additional information (it simply shifts the function

f
), it leads to significantly simpler notation in the sequel.

Lemma
A.7
below provides
the fundamental recursive relation
between convolutional moment-generating functionals of partial Hawkes processes.

Lemma A.7
.

Let
f\in\mathcal{S}^{\infty}_{\mathrm{loc}}[0,\infty)

and
m\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
be given. Then
M_{\tilde{H}^{[m,m)}}[f]=1
and

(A.19)

\displaystyle M_{\tilde{H}^{[m,m+k)}}[f]=\exp\left(f+a(M_{\tilde{H}^{[m+1,m+k)}}[f]-1)*{\pi}^{m+1}\right)\text{ for }k\geq 1.

If, additionally,
f\leq a-1-\log(a)

then

(A.20)

\displaystyle M_{\tilde{H}^{[m,m+k)}}[f]\leq\frac{1}{a}\text{ for all }k\in{\mathbb{N}}\quad\text{ and }\quad M_{\tilde{H}^{m}}[f]\leq\frac{1}{a}

as well as

(A.21)

\displaystyle M_{\tilde{H}^{m}}[f]=\exp\left(f+a\left(M_{\tilde{H}^{m+1}}[f]-1\right)*\pi^{m+1}\right).

Proof.

The equality (
A.19
) follows by conditioning on the
m
-th
generation and
using the well-known fact that for a Poisson process
P
with intensity
measure
\mu\in\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{lf}}[0,\infty)
and a positive
g\in\mathcal{S}^{\infty}_{\mathrm{loc}}[0,\infty)

bounded away from
0
, we have

(A.22)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\textstyle\prod_{T\in P,T\leq t}g(t-T)\right]=e^{((g-1)*\mu)(t)}\text{ for all }t\geq 0.

The inequalities in (
A.20
) follow inductively from (
A.19
) and
allow
us to pass to the limit as
k\to\infty
in
(
A.19
)
to obtain (
A.21
).
∎

The recursive structure of the Hawkes process can be used to derive an
expression for its moments. For
f\in\mathcal{S}^{\infty}_{\mathrm{loc}}[0,\infty)
, we let

\displaystyle\tilde{e}^{[m,m+k)}[f]=\mathbb{E}\left[f*\tilde{H}^{[m,m+k)}\right]\text{ and }\tilde{e}^{m}[f]=\mathbb{E}\left[f*\tilde{H}^{m}\right],\text{ for }m\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0},k\in{\mathbb{N}}.

If we set

\tilde{e}^{[m,m)}[f]:=f
for
m\in{\mathbb{N}}
,
the relation (
A.16
) implies that

(A.23)

\displaystyle\tilde{e}^{[m,m+k)}[f]=f+a\,\tilde{e}^{[m+1,m+k)}*\pi^{m+1}\text{ for }k\geq 1.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\tilde{e}^{[m,m+k)}[f]=f*\left(\textstyle\sum_{j=0}^{k}a^{j}\pi^{(m,m+j]}\right)

where

(A.24)

\displaystyle\pi^{(m,m+j]}:=\begin{cases}\delta_{0},&j=0,\\
\pi^{m+1}*\dots*\pi^{m+j},&j>0.\\
\end{cases}

Since
a<1
and
\lvert\pi^{(m,m+j]}\rvert=1
for all
m,j\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
,
we have convergence in
total variation in

(A.25)

\displaystyle\rho^{m}:=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(1-a)a^{k}\pi^{(m,m+k]}\in\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{p}}[0,\infty),

and the following identity holds

(A.26)

\displaystyle\tilde{e}^{m}[f]=\frac{1}{1-a}f*\rho^{m}\text{ for }f\in\mathcal{S}^{\infty}_{\mathrm{loc}}[0,\infty),m\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}.

In the case of a Hawkes process
H
with background intensity measure
\mu
,
we have the following immediate continuation of Lemma

A.7
. The proof uses (
A.22
) and follows the same pattern
as the proof of Lemma
A.7
so we omit it.

Lemma A.8
.

Given
f\in\mathcal{S}^{\infty}_{\mathrm{loc}}[0,\infty)
with
f\leq a-1-\log(a)
,
we have
M_{H}[f]\in\mathcal{S}^{\infty}
and

(A.27)

\displaystyle M_{H}[f]=\exp\left(\big.(M_{\tilde{H}}[f]-1)*\mu\right).

A well-known consequence of Lemma
A.8
above is the
following expression for the first moment

e[f]=\mathbb{E}[f*H]
of the Hawkes process which we record for later use:

(A.28)

\displaystyle e[f]=\tilde{e}[f]*\mu=\frac{1}{1-a}f*(\rho*\mu).

where
\rho=\rho^{0}
and
\rho^{0}
is given by (
A.25
).

A.3.2.
Asymptotics for the total number of points

Let
\tilde{H}
denote a single-progenitor Hawkes process. We are only
interested in the total number of points
\lvert\tilde{H}\rvert
of
\tilde{H}
here so
the excitation profile
\{\pi^{m}\}_{m\in{\mathbb{N}}}
plays no role. Indeed,
\lvert\tilde{H}\rvert
is the
same as the total number of points of a
Bienaymé-Galton-Watson process with the Poisson offspring
distribution with parameter
a
.
We start with a lemma where

W_{0}:[-e^{-1},\infty)\to{\mathbb{R}}
denotes
the principal branch of Lambert’s
W
function (see, e.g.

[
42
,
Section 4.13
]
).

Lemma A.9
.

For
\beta\in{\mathbb{R}}
, let
l(\beta):=\mathbb{E}[\exp(\beta\lvert\tilde{H}\rvert)]\in(0,\infty]
be the
moment-generating function of the total number
\lvert\tilde{H}\rvert
of points
in
\tilde{H}
. Then

(A.29)

\displaystyle l(\beta)=\begin{cases}+\infty,&\beta>a-1-\log(a),\\
-\frac{1}{a}W_{0}(-\exp\left(\beta-a+\log(a)\right)),&\beta\leq a-1-\log(a).\end{cases}

Proof.

Virtually the same conditioning argument leading to Lemma

A.7
above can be used to show that
the functions

l_{k}(\beta)=\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\beta\lvert\tilde{H}^{[0,k)}\rvert\right)\right]
,
k\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
satisfy

\displaystyle l_{k}(\beta)=\exp\left(\beta+a(l_{k-1}(\beta)-1)\right)\text{ for }k\in{\mathbb{N}}.

Assuming, first, that
\beta\geq 0
, the fact that

\lvert\tilde{H}\rvert
is the nondecreasing limit of
\lvert\tilde{H}^{[0,k)}\rvert
, as

k\to\infty
implies that
l_{k}(\beta)\nearrow l(\beta)
.
Since
l_{0}(\beta)=1
, it follows that
l(\beta)
is the smallest
fixed point above
1
, if one exists, of the function
F(x)=\exp\left(\beta+a(x-1)\right)
;
otherwise,
l(\beta)=+\infty
. The latter case occurs, in particular, when

\beta>a-1-\log(a)
, as can be easily seen by inspection.
For
\beta\leq a-1-\log(a)
,
the equation
F(x)=x
transforms into

(A.30)

\displaystyle(-ax)\exp\left(-ax\right)=-a\exp\left(\beta-a\right),

with solutions given by

\displaystyle x_{0}=-\frac{1}{a}W_{0}(-\exp\left(-a+\beta+\log(a)\right))\text{ and }x_{-1}=-\frac{1}{a}W_{-1}(-\exp\left(-a+\beta+\log(a)\right)),

where
W_{0}
and
W_{-1}
are the
two branches of the Lambert’s
W
function on
[-e^{-1},0)
.
The
principal solution
W_{0}
is increasing and
W_{-1}
is decreasing on

(-e^{-1},0)
, while

W_{0}(-e^{-1})=W_{-1}(-e^{-1})=-1
;
this is easily seen directly, but we also refer the
reader to
[
42
,
Section 4.13
]
for a more comprehensive treatment of the

W
function.
It follows that the smallest solution of
F(x)=x
above
x=1
is given by

x_{0}
defined in (
A.30
) above, which completes the proof of
(
A.29
).

The case
\beta<0
is almost identical, with the distinction that
the sequence
\{l_{k}(\beta)\}_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}
is
now nonincreasing and bounded from above by
1
, so we are looking
for the largest
fixed point of
F
below
1
. Since
F(1)<1
in this case,
l_{0}(\beta)=1
is
located between the two solutions of
F(x)=x
which leads us to choose the
principal branch
W_{0}
again.
∎

Proposition A.10
.

Let
\{\tilde{H}_{n}\}_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
be a sequence of single-progenitor Hawkes processes with
parameters
\{a_{n}\}_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
, and suppose that
\varepsilon_{n}\searrow 0
, where

\varepsilon_{n}=1-a_{n}
.
For
\delta\in[0,1)
we have

(A.31)

\displaystyle\lim_{n}\frac{1}{\varepsilon_{n}}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\varepsilon_{n}^{2}\tfrac{1-\delta}{2}\lvert\tilde{H}_{n}\rvert\right)\right]-1\right)=1-\sqrt{\delta},

and, for
\delta\in(0,1)
,

(A.32)

\displaystyle\lim_{n}\varepsilon_{n}^{2k-1}\mathbb{E}\left[\lvert\tilde{H}_{n}\rvert^{k}\exp\left(\varepsilon^{2}_{n}\tfrac{1-\delta}{2}\lvert\tilde{H}_{n}\rvert\right)\right]=\frac{(k-1)!}{2^{k-1}}\binom{2(k-1)}{k-1}\delta^{1/2-k}\text{ for }k\in{\mathbb{N}}.

Proof.

Let
w(x)=-W_{0}(-e^{-1-x^{2}})
for
x\geq 0
, where
W_{0}
is the principal
branch of Lambert
W
function.
Lemma
A.9
above states
that

(A.33)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\beta\lvert\tilde{H}_{n}\rvert\right)\right]=\frac{1}{1-\varepsilon_{n}}w(b_{n}(\beta))\text{ where }b_{n}(\beta)=(\beta^{max}_{n}-\beta)^{1/2},

for
\beta<\beta^{max}_{n}:=-\log(1-\varepsilon_{n})-\varepsilon_{n}
.
We have

b_{n}\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\varepsilon_{n}^{2}\right)=\Big(-\log(1-\varepsilon_{n})-\varepsilon_{n}-\tfrac{1}{2}\varepsilon_{n}^{2}\Big)^{1/2}=O(\varepsilon_{n}^{3/2}),

so the continuous differentiability of
w
on
[0,\infty)
and the fact that

w(0)=1
, imply that

\frac{1}{1-\varepsilon_{n}}w\left(b_{n}\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\varepsilon_{n}^{2}\right)\right)=1+\varepsilon_{n}+O(\varepsilon^{3/2}_{n}),

which, in turn, yields (
A.31
) for
\delta=0
.
Similarly, when
\delta\in(0,1)
we have

b_{n}\left(\tfrac{1}{2}(1-\delta)\varepsilon_{n}^{2}\right)=\sqrt{\tfrac{\delta}{2}}\varepsilon_{n}+O(\varepsilon^{2}_{n}),

and the limit in (
A.31
) follows from

\frac{1}{1-\varepsilon_{n}}w\left(b_{n}\left(\tfrac{1}{2}(1-\delta)\varepsilon_{n}^{2}\right)\right)=1+(1-\sqrt{\delta})\varepsilon_{n}+o(\varepsilon_{n}).

For the remainder of the proof, suppose that
\delta\in(0,1)
.
Standard
properties of moment-generating functions allow us to
differentiate
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
times inside the expectation sign at each

\beta<\beta^{max}_{n}
in (
A.33
) above to obtain

(A.34)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\lvert\tilde{H}_{n}\rvert^{k}\exp\left(\beta\lvert\tilde{H}_{n}\rvert\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1-\varepsilon_{n}}(w\circ b_{n})^{(k)}(\beta)\text{ for }\beta<\beta^{max}_{n},

where
(\cdot)^{(k)}
denotes the
k
-th derivative in
\beta
.
The formula of Faá di Bruno states that
(w\circ b_{n})^{(k)}(\beta)
admits
a representation of the form

(A.35)

\displaystyle\sum\frac{k!}{m_{1}!\dots m_{k}!}w^{(m_{1}+\dots+m_{k})}\Big(b_{n}(\beta)\Big)\prod_{j=1}^{k}\left(\frac{b_{n}^{(j)}(\beta)}{j!}\right)^{m_{j}},

where the sum is taken over all
m_{1},\dots,m_{k}\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
such that

m_{1}+2m_{2}+\dots+km_{k}=k
.

We have

b_{n}^{(j)}(\beta)=(-1)^{j}j!\binom{1/2}{j}(\beta^{max}_{n}-\beta)^{1/2-j}
so that

\displaystyle\prod_{j=1}^{k}\left(\frac{b_{n}^{(j)}(\beta)}{j!}\right)^{m_{j}}=K\,(\beta^{max}_{n}-\beta)^{\frac{m_{1}+\dots+m_{k}}{2}-k}\text{ where }K=\prod_{j=1}^{k}\left((-1)^{j}\binom{1/2}{j}\right)^{m_{j}}.

The lowest power of
(\beta^{max}_{n}-\beta)
appearing in
(
A.35
)
is
1/2-k
and is attained precisely at

m_{1}=\dots=m_{k-1}=0
,
m_{k}=1
.
Furthermore, all functions

w^{(m_{1}+\dots+m_{k})}(x)
converge to a finite limit as
x\searrow 0
which implies that

(w\circ b_{n})^{(k)}(\beta)=k!w^{\prime}(b_{n}(\beta))(-1)^{k}\binom{1/2}{k}(\beta^{max}_{n}-\beta)^{1/2-k}+o\left((\beta^{max}_{n}-\beta)^{1/2-k}\right).

Since

b_{n}\left(\tfrac{1}{2}(1-\delta)\varepsilon_{n}^{2}\right)=\sqrt{\delta/2}\,\varepsilon_{n}+o(\varepsilon_{n})
the
fact that
\lim_{x\searrow 0}w^{\prime}(x)=-\sqrt{2}
implies that

\lim_{n}\frac{(w\circ b_{n})^{(k)}(\beta_{n})}{\left(\delta\varepsilon_{n}^{2}/2\right)^{1/2-k}}=\sqrt{2}(-1)^{k-1}k!\binom{1/2}{k},

which, in turn, yields (
A.32
).
∎

A.4.
Proof of Theorem
2.2

The proof is divided into several lemmas. These lemmas depend on the results
and notation from Sections
A.2
and
A.3
above, as
well as on the notation and concepts introduced ahead of Theorem
2.2
in
Section
2
.

-

An arbitrary but fixed time horizon

T>0
is used throughout the proof, except in the very last paragraph
where the extension to
[0,\infty)
is discussed. All functions, measures, etc.
are implicitly restricted to
[0,T]

whenever necessary, without any change in notation.

-

We continue to use all
the notation from Subsection
1.5
, but we omit the
domain
I
because it will almost always be
[0,T]
. Any other domain
will be explicitly stated. We also let

\mathcal{L}^{p}
and
\mathcal{L}^{p}_{\mathrm{loc}}
,
p\in[0,\infty]

denote the standard Lebesgue spaces on
I
;
a.e.-equal functions are not identified.

C_{0}
denotes the family of
continuous functions
f
on
[0,T]
with
f(0)=0
.
The family of all signed measures on
[0,T]
is denoted by

\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{s}}
and we write
\left\lvert\mu\right\rvert
for the total variation of
\mu\in\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{s}}

on
[0,T]
.

-

A function
f\in\mathcal{S}^{\infty}
is said to be
of bounded
variation
if there exists a signed measure
Df\in\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{s}}
such that
f(t)=Df[0,t]
for all
t\in[0,T]
. The Hahn decomposition of
Df
is denoted by
Df=D^{+}f-D^{-}f
and the total variation measure
D^{+}f+D^{-}f
, associated to

Df
, by
\left\lvert D\right\rvert f
. The map
\left\lvert f\right\rvert_{\mathrm{BV}}:=\left\lvert\left\lvert D\right\rvert f\right\rvert_{\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{s}}}
is a
Banach norm on
\mathrm{BV}
.

-

Measures and functions are assumed to vanish outside
[0,T]
so that
limits of integration, as in
(f*\mu)(t)=\int f(t-\cdot)\,d\mu
,
do not have to be specified.

-

Young’s inequalities
\left\lvert h*\nu\right\rvert_{\mathcal{L}^{1}}\leq\left\lvert h\right\rvert_{\mathcal{L}^{1}}\left\lvert\nu\right\rvert
and
\left\lvert h*\nu\right\rvert_{\mathcal{S}^{\infty}}\leq\left\lvert h\right\rvert_{\mathcal{S}^{\infty}}\left\lvert\nu\right\rvert

are used throughout without explicit mention.

-

We use the notation of Subsection

A.3.1
with an
additional subscript
n
indicating
membership in a sequence. Furthermore, we adopt the
shorthand

\varepsilon_{n}=1-a_{n},

assume throughout that
\varepsilon_{n}\to 0
, and define random measures
\xi^{m}_{n}
and

Lemma A.11
.

If
\nu,\nu^{\prime}\in\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{f}}
and
h\in\mathrm{BV}
,
then
h*\nu\in\mathrm{BV}
with
D(h*\nu)=Dh*\nu
and

(A.39)

\displaystyle\left\lvert h*\nu\right\rvert_{\mathrm{BV}}

\displaystyle\leq\left\lvert h\right\rvert_{\mathrm{BV}}\lvert\nu\rvert

as well as

(A.40)

\displaystyle\left\lvert h*(\nu-\nu^{\prime})\right\rvert_{\mathcal{L}^{1}}

\displaystyle\leq\left\lvert h\right\rvert_{\mathrm{BV}}\,W^{1}_{[0,T]}(\nu,\nu^{\prime}).

Proof.

We have
(h*\nu)(t)=(Dh*\nu)[0,t]
, which implies that

h*\nu\in\mathrm{BV}
with
D(h*\nu)=Dh*\nu
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\left\lvert h*\nu\right\rvert_{\mathrm{BV}}\hskip-40.00006pt

\displaystyle\hskip 40.00006pt=\left\lvert D(h*\nu)\right\rvert=\left\lvert Dh*\nu\right\rvert

\displaystyle=\left\lvert Dh^{+}*\nu-Dh^{-}*\nu\right\rvert\leq(Dh^{+}*\nu)+(Dh^{-}*\nu)

\displaystyle\leq Dh^{+}\,\nu+Dh^{-}\,\nu=\left\lvert Dh\right\rvert\left\lvert\nu\right\rvert=\left\lvert h\right\rvert_{\mathrm{BV}}\left\lvert\nu\right\rvert.

To prove (
A.40
), we observe that

\displaystyle(Dh*\nu)[0,t]=(\nu*Dh)[0,t]=(F_{\nu}*Dh)(t)\text{ for }t\geq 0,

where
F_{\nu}(t)=\nu[0,t]
denotes the distribution function of
\nu
.
Hence,

\displaystyle\left\lvert h*\nu(t)-h*\nu^{\prime}(t)\right\rvert\leq\int\left\lvert F_{\nu}(t-u)-F_{\nu^{\prime}}(t-u)\right\rvert\,\left\lvert D\right\rvert h(du)\text{ for }t\geq 0,

and so,

\begin{split}\int_{0}^{T}\left\lvert h*\nu(t)-h*\nu^{\prime}(t)\right\rvert\,dt\hskip-40.00006pt&\hskip 40.00006pt\leq\int_{0}^{T}\int\left\lvert F_{\nu}(t-u)-F_{\nu^{\prime}}(t-u)\right\rvert\,\left\lvert D\right\rvert h(du)\,dt\\
&\leq\int\int_{0}^{T}\left\lvert F_{\nu}(t)-F_{\nu^{\prime}}(t)\right\rvert\,dt\,\left\lvert D\right\rvert h(du)=\left\lvert h\right\rvert_{\mathrm{BV}}W^{1}_{[0,T]}(\nu,\nu^{\prime}).\qed\end{split}

Lemma A.12
.

There exists a universal constant

such that
for

and

h^{m}_{n}\in\mathcal{S}^{\infty}

\displaystyle\inf_{u\in[0,T]}f(u)\leq h^{m}_{n}(t)\leq C\delta^{-1/2}\sup_{u\in[0,T]}f(u)\text{ for }t\in[0,T].

(2)

If, additionally,

, then
h^{m}_{n}\in\mathrm{BV}

and

(A.42)

\displaystyle\left\lvert h^{m}_{n}\right\rvert_{\mathrm{BV}}

\displaystyle\leq C\left\lvert f\right\rvert_{\mathrm{BV}}.

Proof.

We pick
\delta\in(0,1)
and
f\in\mathcal{S}^{\infty}
with
f\leq\tfrac{1}{2}(1-\delta)

as in the statement and set

(A.43)

\displaystyle Z^{m}_{n}=\exp\left(\tfrac{1-\delta}{2}\lvert\tilde{\xi}^{m}_{n}\rvert_{\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{lf}}[0,\infty)}\right)

A.36
).

(
A.32
) with
k=1

\begin{split}h^{m}_{n}(t)&\leq\varepsilon_{n}^{-1}\mathbb{E}\left[Z^{m}_{n}(f*\tilde{\xi}^{m}_{n})(t)\right]\leq\Big(\textstyle\sup_{u\in[0,T]}f(u)\Big)\varepsilon_{n}\mathbb{E}\left[Z^{m}_{n}\lvert\tilde{H}^{m}_{n}\rvert\right]\\
&\leq C\delta^{-1/2}\textstyle\sup_{u\in[0,T]}f(u)\text{ for }t\in[0,T].\end{split}

To obtain a lower bound, we use Jensen’s inequality and

A.26
):

\displaystyle h^{m}_{n}(t)

\displaystyle\geq\varepsilon_{n}^{-1}\left(\exp\left(\mathbb{E}\left[(f*\tilde{\xi}^{m}_{n})(t)\right]\right)-1\right)\geq\varepsilon_{n}^{-1}\mathbb{E}\left[(f*\tilde{\xi}^{m}_{n})(t)\right]

\displaystyle=(f*\rho^{m}_{n})(t)\geq\inf_{u\in[0,T]}f(u).

To establish (
A.42
), we pick

and observe that

(A.45)

\begin{split}\left\lvert h^{m}_{n}(s)-h^{m}_{n}(r)\right\rvert\hskip-50.00008pt&\hskip 50.00008pt\leq\varepsilon_{n}^{-1}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lvert\exp\left(f*{\tilde{\xi}^{m}_{n}}(s)\right)-\exp\left(f*{\tilde{\xi}^{m}_{n}}(r)\right)\right\rvert\right]\\
&\leq\varepsilon_{n}^{-1}\mathbb{E}\left[Z^{m}_{n}\left\lvert f*{\tilde{\xi}^{m}_{n}}(s)-f*{\tilde{\xi}^{m}_{n}}(r)\right\rvert\right]\\
&\leq\varepsilon_{n}^{-1}\left(F^{m}_{n}(r,s)+\hat{F}^{m}_{n}(r,s)\right),\end{split}

where

\displaystyle F^{m}_{n}(r,s)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[Z^{m}_{n}\int_{0}^{r}\left\lvert f(s-u)-f(r-u)\right\rvert\,\tilde{\xi}^{m}_{n}(du)\right],\text{ and }

\displaystyle\hat{F}^{m}_{n}(r,s)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[Z^{m}_{n}\int_{r}^{s}\left\lvert f(s-u)\right\rvert\,\tilde{\xi}^{m}_{n}(du)\right].

Since
\left\lvert f(b)-f(a)\right\rvert\leq\int 1_{{(a,b]}}\,d\left\lvert D\right\rvert f
,
for all
a<b
in
[0,T]
, we have

\displaystyle F^{m}_{n}(r,s)\leq\mathbb{E}\left[Z^{m}_{n}\iint 1_{\left\{v\in(r-u,s-u],u\leq r\right\}}\,\left\lvert D\right\rvert f(dv)\,\tilde{\xi}^{m}_{n}(du)\right].

Hence, for

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\kappa}\int_{0}^{T-\kappa}F^{m}_{n}(r,r+\kappa)\,dr\hskip-50.00008pt

\displaystyle\hskip 50.00008pt=

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[Z^{m}_{n}\iint\frac{1}{\kappa}\int 1_{\left\{r\in[v+u-\kappa,v+u)\cap[T-\kappa,u]\right\}}\,dr\,\left\lvert D\right\rvert f(dv)\tilde{\xi}^{m}_{n}(du)\right]

\displaystyle\hat{h}=\frac{(f+\tfrac{1}{2}h^{2})^{\widehat{\ }}}{1+\psi}=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\sigma(1+\psi)}(f+\tfrac{1}{2}h^{2})^{\widehat{\ }}\,d\sigma=\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\sigma(1+\psi)}(f+\tfrac{1}{2}h^{2})\,d\sigma\right)^{\widehat{\ }},

and it follows that the constant function
h^{\tau}=h
solves
(
A.54
).

Our next task is to show that
\tilde{h}^{\tau}=h
for all
\tau
.
To do so, we prove
that (
A.54
) admits a unique solution in the class of all measurable
functions
\tau\mapsto g^{\tau}
with
\sup_{\tau}\left\lvert g^{\tau}\right\rvert_{\mathcal{S}^{\infty}}<1
.
Thanks to the boundedness assumption we made
on the function
f
in the statement, both the constant function
h
and
the limit
\tilde{h}
have this property. Indeed, in the case of
h
it follows
from Lemma
A.5
, while in the case of
\tilde{h}
it is inherited
from the sequence
\{h_{n}^{\tau}\}_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
via the bound (
A.31
) of
Proposition
A.10
.

Suppose that
there exists two solutions,
g_{1}
and
g_{2}
, of (
A.54
) such
that

\lvert g^{\tau}_{1}\rvert_{\mathcal{S}^{\infty}},\lvert g^{\tau}_{2}\rvert_{\mathcal{S}^{\infty}}\leq 1-\kappa
for all
\tau>0
and some

\kappa>0
.
The function

M(\tau):=\left\lvert g^{\tau}_{1}-g^{\tau}_{2}\right\rvert_{\mathcal{S}^{\infty}}
satisfies the inequality

\displaystyle M(\tau)\leq\int_{\tau}^{\infty}\tfrac{1}{2}\Big(\lvert g^{\sigma}\rvert_{\mathcal{S}^{\infty}}+\lvert\tilde{g}^{\sigma}\rvert_{\mathcal{S}^{\infty}}\Big)M(\sigma)e^{-(\sigma-\tau)}\,d\sigma\leq(1-\kappa)\int_{\tau}^{\infty}M(\sigma)e^{-(\sigma-\tau)}\,d\sigma.

The last integral averages the function
M

over
[\tau,\infty)
so
for each
\tau
we can find
\tau^{\prime}\geq\tau
such that

M(\tau)\leq(1-\kappa/2)M(\tau^{\prime})
.
Iterating this procedure
and starting from any
\tau_{0}=\tau
,
we can find a nondecreasing sequence
\{\tau_{n}\}_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
such that

M(\tau)\leq(1-\kappa)^{n}M(\tau_{n})\leq 2(1-\kappa)^{n}
. This
implies that
M(\tau)=0
for each
\tau
, i.e.,
that
g_{1}=g_{2}
.
Hence, each convergent subsequence of
\{h_{n}\}_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
converges to the same
limit, so, by relative compactness, we may conclude that the entire sequence
converges to the constant function
\tau\mapsto h
.
∎

Conclusion of the proof of Theorem

.

Let

The expression (

\displaystyle\Big\{\exp\big((f*\xi_{n})(T)\big)\,:\,n\in{\mathbb{N}}\Big\}\text{ is a uniformly

In particular, the sequence

We pick an arbitrary convergent subsequence of

For

The weak convergence

for each

, and we

\displaystyle\left\lvert\varepsilon_{n}h_{n}*\mu_{n}-h*\mu\right\rvert_{\mathcal{L}^{1}}

\displaystyle\leq\left\lvert(h_{n}-h)*\varepsilon_{n}\mu_{n}\right\rvert_{\mathcal{L}^{1}}+\left\lvert h*(\varepsilon_{n}\mu_{n}-\mu)\right\rvert_{\mathcal{L}^{1}}

\displaystyle\leq C\left\lvert h_{n}-h\right\rvert_{\mathcal{L}^{1}}+\left\lvert h\right\rvert_{\mathrm{BV}}W^{1}_{[0,T]}(\varepsilon_{n}\mu_{n},\mu)\to 0.

Therefore, if, additionally,

,

\displaystyle M_{\xi}[f]=\lim_{n}M_{\xi_{n}}[f]=\lim_{n}e^{\varepsilon_{n}h_{n}*\mu_{n}}=e^{h*\mu}\text{ a.e.},

.
The estimates in (

) and

, and, consequently, that

The convolution

The random variable

.

\displaystyle M_{\xi}[f]=\exp\left(h[f]*\mu\right)\text{ for all }f\in C_{0}\cap\mathrm{BV}\text{ with }\sup\left\lvert f\right\rvert<1/2.
