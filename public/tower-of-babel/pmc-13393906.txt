Psychosocial and lifestyle factors associated with premenstrual syndrome symptoms among Chinese female college students: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Lixin Zeng, Xiuying Guo, Chai Nien Foo, Myo Oo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPremenstrual syndrome (PMS) refers to a range of physical and emotional symptoms occurring during the luteal phase of the menstrual cycle. It is closely linked to hormonal fluctuations, menstrual physiology, and lifestyle factors, and can significantly affect quality of life. This study aimed to investigate the prevalence of premenstrual syndrome symptoms and its associations with perceived stress, physical activity, and dietary quality among college students at a university in Hainan, China.MethodsA self-administered, structured questionnaire was used to collect data on socio-demographic and menstrual characteristics, perceived stress, physical activity and dietary quality. Self-reported PMS symptoms were assessed using the Chinese version of the Premenstrual Syndrome Scale (PMSS). Perceived stress was measured using the 10-item Perceived Stress Scale (PSS). Dietary quality was evaluated by calculating the Chinese Healthy Eating Index (CHEI) from a food frequency questionnaire. Physical activity levels were assessed with the short form of the International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ-SF). Data were analyzed using SPSS 26.0, with chi-square and Mann–Whitney U tests for group comparisons, and linear regression to assess factors associated with PMS symptoms score.ResultsThe prevalence of self-reported PMS symptoms among college students was 32.9%. In the multivariate linear regression model, perceived stress score was positively associated with PMS symptoms score (B = 0.378, p < 0.001). Moderate-intensity physical activity (MPA) was negatively associated with PMS symptoms score (B = −0.087, p = 0.037). Among the components of the CHEI, higher red meat score (indicating lower intake) was significantly associated with lower PMS symptoms score (B = −0.436, p = 0.007).ConclusionThe findings highlight psychosocial and lifestyle factors as important correlates of PMS symptoms and provide preliminary evidence to inform nursing screening and health promotion initiatives in campus health services. Longitudinal studies are needed to further explore these associations.

Introduction

Premenstrual syndrome (PMS) is a recurring condition affecting 30–40% of women of reproductive age. More than 200 symptoms have been attributed to PMS [1]. The most common symptoms include emotional instability, fatigue, appetite changes, and physical discomforts such as bloating, breast tenderness, and headache [2]. These symptoms typically commence shortly after ovulation, gradually intensify, reaching a peak approximately 5 days before the onset of menstruation, and gradually subside by the end of menstruation [3].

Due to limited understanding of the mechanisms underlying PMS, the precise etiology of these disorders remains unclear and effective treatments are limited. Leading hypotheses focus on hormonal fluctuations, neurotransmitter abnormalities involving gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA) and serotonin, and post-ovulation nutritional deficiencies such as vitamin B6, magnesium, and calcium [4], as well as the presumed role of circulating gonadal steroids in symptom development [5, 6]. Although widely studied, the exact cause of PMS is still unclear. Current drug treatments mainly manage symptoms rather than the root issue, with some women avoiding these options due to side effects or health concerns [7].

High-carbohydrate intake during the luteal phase may elevate serotonin levels, potentially intensifying mood-related symptoms and explaining premenstrual carbohydrate cravings [8]. Additionally, nutrient deficiencies (such as B-group vitamins, vitamin D, and magnesium) and glucose metabolism abnormalities, along with unhealthy dietary patterns (e.g., high intake of calorie-dense, high-fat, high-sugar, and high-salt foods) and lifestyle factors (e.g., smoking and lack of regular exercise), may significantly exacerbate PMS symptoms [3, 9–12]. Dózsa-Juhász et al. found that the severity of PMS symptoms is significantly influenced by regular physical activity, mental state, and perceived stress [13].

PMS can impact the academic performance and decrease quality of life of students [14]. Previous studies have shown that female students with PMS often exhibit poor health-seeking behaviors [15]. Furthermore, a review by Maity S et al. reported that only a minority of studies documented consultation with healthcare professionals, and none described structured campus-based wellness or screening programs for menstrual health [16]. However, comprehensive studies simultaneously examining psychosocial and lifestyle factors among young Chinese women remain limited. Emerging evidence indicates that untreated or severe premenstrual syndrome (PMS) will extend to premenstrual dysphoric disorder (PMDD). This may extend beyond adolescence into adulthood and contribute to a range of long-term adverse outcomes. These include an increased risk of mood and anxiety disorders, diminished occupational productivity, and difficulties in interpersonal functioning [17, 18]. From a nursing and public health perspective, this underscores the importance of early identification and timely management. Nursing professionals play a critical role in enhancing health literacy, encouraging appropriate health-seeking behaviors, and facilitating early screening. Nurses-led in delivering evidence-based interventions, including lifestyle modification, psychosocial support, and appropriate referral for medical evaluation is essential to mitigate symptom progression and improve long-term health outcomes among affected individuals.

Therefore, this cross-sectional study aimed to investigate the prevalence of premenstrual syndrome symptoms and its associations with perceived stress, physical activity, and dietary quality among college students at a university in Hainan, China. The findings of this study could provide preliminary evidence on PMS-related psychosocial and lifestyle factors to inform future nursing screening and health promotion initiatives in campus health services.

Material and methods

Study design and participants

The cross-sectional study was conducted between October 2024 and February 2025 among college students at the Yunlong Campus of Hainan Vocational University of Science and Technology. As of 28 September 2024, the total number of female students on campus was 6089. A stratified, proportional, and systematic random sampling method was used to recruit participants. The target population was first stratified by faculty based on enrollment data provided by the university administration. A complete list of enrolled female students was obtained and categorized into five faculties: Urban Construction, Nursing, Medicine, Accounting, and Education and Music. The total sample size was then allocated proportionally according to the size of each faculty to ensure representativeness. Within each stratum, participants were selected using systematic random sampling. This multistage sampling procedure, including stratification, proportional allocation, and systematic selection, ensured that the final sample accurately reflected the distribution of the target population across faculties.

Data were collected using self-administered, structured questionnaires distributed via the Questionnaire Star platform (Changsha Ranxing Information Technology Co., Ltd., Changsha, P.R.C.). Eligible female students completed the survey anonymously and without time constraints. Each account was limited to one submission, and the platform was designed to prevent duplicate entries. All participants provided informed consent prior to participation, and their personal information was kept strictly confidential. A total of 370 participants met the inclusion criteria and completed the study. Participants with implausible CHEI values (n = 5) were excluded from the analysis, resulting in a final analytical sample of 365 participants. No missing data were observed for the variables included in the analysis.

Sample size

The sample size calculation was performed based on a reported PMS prevalence of 22.2% in the study by Shi et al. [19]. In this study, the formula is n = (Z1-α)2(P (1-P)/D2),Z1-α = Z0.95 = 1.96 (value from normal distribution table, standard for CI of 95%), D = 5%=0.05, and the sample size was calculated as 266. Accounting for an estimated 20% incomplete or invalid responses and a 10% non‑response rate among eligible participants, the final sample size was estimated to be 370.

Measures

Socio-demographic characteristics

The socio-demographic questionnaire included general information (age, household type, height, weight and household income), lifestyle behavior (smoking, drinking, dietary habits, menstrual cycle status, etc.).

Premenstrual syndrome symptoms

PMS symptoms were assessed using the Chinese version of the 12-item Premenstrual Syndrome Scale (PMSS), which evaluates physical and psychological symptoms occurring during the 14 days before menstruation [20]. Participants rated each item on a 4-point scale (0 (no symptom) − 3 (severe symptoms). Total score was used to categorize PMS severity as mild (6–10), moderate (11–20), and severe (>20). Specifically, participants with a total PMS score ≥ 6 were classified as having PMS. The Cronbach’s α in Wu et al.’s study was reported as 0.8 [21], and the Cronbach’s α in the present study was 0.90, indicating acceptable reliability. The PMSS assesses PMS-related emotional and physical symptoms that overlap with criteria described in the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG) and the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM-5), such as depressed mood, anxiety, tension, and somatic symptoms (e.g., headache and abdominal distension). In addition, it includes items not explicitly included in these diagnostic frameworks, such as fidgeting and swelling of the hands and feet. Although the PMSS is not a diagnostic instrument, it is widely used to quantify premenstrual symptom severity and to distinguish between women with and without PMS [22].

Perceived stress

Perceived stress was assessed using the 10-item Perceived Stress Scale (PSS), a validated and reliable instrument [23]. Each item was rated on a 5-point Likert scale (0 = never to 4 = very often), indicating how frequently participants experienced stress-related feelings in the past month. Total score ranged from 0 to 40, with higher scores indicating greater perceived stress. Participants were categorized as having low (0–13), moderate (14–26), or high stress (27–40) [24]. The Cronbach’s α in the study by She et al. was 0.83 [25], and that in the present study was 0.86.

Physical activity

Participants reported their physical activity over the past 7 days using the Chinese version of the International Physical Activity Questionnaire-Short Form (IPAQ-SF), which has demonstrated good reliability in Chinese populations [26]. Total activity (MET-minutes/week) was calculated by summing walking, moderate-intensity, and vigorous-intensity activities. The sitting item was excluded from the total score and used solely to assess sedentary behavior [27].

Dietary quality

A Semi-Quantitative Food Frequency Questionnaire (SFFQ), a tool with established reliability and validity in large-scale epidemiological studies [28], was used to assess participants’ dietary intake. Dietary data were then used to calculate the Chinese Healthy Eating Index (CHEI), which was developed based on the Chinese Dietary Guidelines (CDG) 2016. The CHEI consists of 17 components: 12 adequacy components (total grains, whole grains and mixed beans, tubers, total vegetables, dark vegetables, fruits, dairy, soybeans, fish and seafood, poultry, eggs, seeds and nuts) and 5 limitation components (red meat, cooking oils, sodium, added sugars, and alcohol). Higher intakes of adequacy components increased scores, whereas higher intakes of limitation components decreased scores. The total score ranges from 0 to 100, with higher scores reflecting better adherence to dietary guidelines and overall diet quality [29].

Data analysis

Data were reviewed for completeness and accuracy before entry, and random questionnaires were cross-checked with entered values to ensure data quality. The dataset was cleaned, checked for missing data, outliers, and normality prior to analysis. SPSS 26.0 was used for statistical analysis. Continuous variables were expressed as medians with interquartile ranges (IQR), and categorical variables as frequencies and percentages. Group differences between the PMS and non-PMS groups were assessed using chi-square tests for categorical variables and Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables. Variables with a p < 0.25 in univariate linear regression analyses were considered potential predictors and were entered into the multivariate linear regression model. No additional variables were forced into the model based on conceptual considerations. A multiple linear regression model was constructed using the enter method. Prior to regression analyses, assumptions were assessed, including normality (Q-Q plot of standardized residuals), homoscedasticity (scatterplot of standardized residuals), multicollinearity (variance inflation factor, VIF < 5), and independence of residuals (Durbin–Watson statistic within 1.5–2.5). All regression coefficients (B) were unstandardized and represent the change in PMS symptoms score per one-unit increase in the predictor variable, adjusted for other variables in the model. All results were reported with 95% confidence intervals (CI) and p values. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

A total of 370 participants met the entry criteria and completed the study. After exclusion of five extreme CHEI values, 365 participants were included in the final analysis. Table 1 presents the demographic and menstrual characteristics of the participants. The median age was 20.0 (IQR 19.0–22.0) years, and the median BMI was 19.47 (IQR 18.03–21.63) kg/m2. Most participants were from rural areas (66.8%) and had a monthly household income below 5000 CNY (55.3%). Approximately two-thirds of participants reported irregular eating habits (65.8%). The prevalence of smoking and alcohol consumption was 5.8% and 13.4%, respectively. Menstrual characteristics fell within normal ranges: age at menarche was mainly between 13 and 16 years (59.5%), menstrual cycle length between 21 and 35 days (63.8%), duration of menstrual bleeding 4 to 7 days (85.2%), and menstrual flow volume 20 to 80 ml (87.9%). Dysmenorrhea was reported by 71.0% of participants, and 32.9% reported PMS symptoms. The overall level of perceived stress in the cohort was moderate, with a median PSS score of 18.0 (IQR 15.0–20.0). Physical activity levels were predominantly light and moderate (50.4% and 43.6%, respectively). Discussion

This study investigated the prevalence of PMS symptoms and its associations with perceived stress, physical activity, and dietary quality among college students at a university in Hainan, China. PMS prevalence among university students varies widely from 20.6% to 96.6%, with this study reporting a rate of 32.9%, consistent with findings in China and globally [19, 30–34].

The findings of this study indicate a significant positive association between PSS score and PMS symptoms score, suggesting that higher PSS score is associated with higher PMS symptoms score (B = 0.378). Previous studies suggest that stress may be associated with neuroendocrine responses, including activation of the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis and cortisol release, which may be related to PMS symptoms [35, 36]. Experimental studies have suggested that women with PMS may exhibit altered stress reactivity patterns and higher levels of negative affect under stress, which may help to explain the association between perceived stress and PMS symptoms observed in the present study [37]. Similarly, Nandakumar et al. [14] emphasized stress as a key risk factor for PMS with negative consequences on academic performance and quality of life. Reverse causation is plausible in this cross-sectional design, as individuals with more severe PMS may experience higher perceived stress and may also be less physically active due to symptom burden, such as irritability, anxiety, and insomnia.

Moderate physical activity was associated with lower PMS symptoms score, suggesting a potential inverse association, which is consistent with previous studies reported by Shi et al. [30]. Exercise has been recognized as a nonpharmacological approach for PMS management, and previous studies have reported a higher prevalence of PMS among sedentary women [38]. Regular exercise has been reported to be associated with lower PMS symptom severity and stress-related outcomes [13]. Physical activity may relieve PMS symptoms through multiple biological pathways, including the release of endorphins and improvements in both mental and physical health [39]. Although physical activity did not differ significantly between participants with and without reported PMS symptoms in the bivariate analysis, it was significantly associated with PMS symptoms score in the regression model. This discrepancy may be explained by methodological differences between the two approaches. The bivariate analysis relies on categorical comparisons, which may reduce statistical power and obscure subtle associations, whereas the regression model retains the full variability of physical activity as a continuous variable. These results suggest that the association between physical activity and PMS symptoms may be more nuanced than can be captured by simple group comparisons.

This study found moderate overall dietary quality among college students, as assessed by the Chinese Healthy Eating Index (CHEI). Higher CHEI component score for red meat (indicating lower red meat intake) was associated with lower PMS symptoms score. In addition, in the bivariate analysis, differences in CHEI component scores for fruit and alcohol intake were observed between participants with and without PMS. However, both associations were not statistically significant in the regression model. Therefore, dietary findings should be interpreted at the component level with caution.

Previous studies have reported mixed findings regarding the role of red meat and poultry in PMS. The study by Shi et al. found that the Traditional South China Diet (TSCD), characterized by high consumption of rice, red meat, and poultry, showed a significant inverse association with premenstrual disorders (PMDs), including PMS and PMDD [19]. Red meat is a good source of B vitamins, iron, and zinc [40], nutrients that have been associated with a reduced risk of PMS [9, 10]. In contrast, case–control studies have reported that Western dietary patterns are associated with an increased risk of PMS, particularly those characterized by higher consumption of red and processed meats, highlighting the need for further research to clarify their specific effects and underlying biological mechanisms [41]. In the present study, the red meat component of the CHEI is classified as a limitation component, with higher scores indicating lower intake. This finding is broadly consistent with previous evidence suggesting that poorer dietary quality, including higher consumption of ultra-processed foods, may be associated with greater PMS symptom severity [42].

Prior studies have shown a positive association between alcohol consumption and PMS symptoms, while reduced consumption of fruits was significantly related to PMS occurrence [43]. Besides animal-based foods, plant-derived components may also play an important role. Fruits are important sources of vitamin C, vitamin B6, vitamin E, dietary fiber, and various minerals such as potassium, iron, magnesium, and copper [44]. Evidence suggests that antioxidants and anti-inflammatory nutrients like vitamins D, C, and magnesium may help reduce PMS symptoms by targeting inflammation and oxidative stress. However, research findings are mixed, with some studies reporting a potential link between vitamin C intake and increased psychological symptoms, indicating the need for further investigation [45].

Overall, the findings suggest that lower perceived stress, moderate physical activity, and higher CHEI score for red meat, where higher scores indicate lower red meat intake, may be associated with lower PMS symptoms score among young women. Although the sample was drawn from multiple faculties within a single university and may not be fully representative of the wider student population in China, the findings may still be relevant to similar populations and may inform nursing practice in university settings, including routine screening of perceived stress, physical activity, and dietary habits, as well as providing lifestyle counseling and referral for students with higher PMS symptom severity. However, as this is a cross-sectional study, causal relationships cannot be established, and the findings should therefore be interpreted with caution. Future longitudinal or interventional studies are warranted to further investigate these associations.

Limitations

This study thoroughly explored the psychosocial and lifestyle factors influencing PMS, including stress, dietary quality, and physical activity. However, several limitations should be noted. First, the regression models primarily focused on the main variables of interest (i.e., perceived stress, physical activity, and CHEI) based on the study objectives. Although this approach allows for a more straightforward examination of the associations between these key predictors and PMS symptoms score, as demographic characteristics, BMI, and menstrual-related factors were not included in the regression model, and other health-related factors (e.g., hormonal contraceptive use and psychiatric disorders) were not measured in this study, which is likely to introduce residual confounding. Second, the cross-sectional design limits the ability to draw causal conclusions, and since the study was conducted exclusively with college students from a single university in Hainan, China, the results may limit its generalizability. Third, the relatively small sample size in the VPA group (n = 22) may have limited statistical power and reduced the ability to detect significant associations. In addition, temporal relationships between variables cannot be established, and the onset of PMS symptoms cannot be determined. The use of a single self-reported retrospective PMS scale, rather than prospective symptom charting across at least two menstrual cycles, may introduce recall bias due to memory inaccuracy and subjective interpretation, potentially affecting symptom classification accuracy. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted as reflecting PMS-related symptoms rather than a formal clinical diagnosis of PMS.

Conclusions

The study found that 32.9% of students reported PMS symptoms. Results indicated that higher perceived stress and lower levels of moderate-intensity physical activity (MPA) were associated with higher PMS symptoms score. Greater adherence to the CDG 2016, particularly lower red meat intake, may be associated with lower PMS symptoms score. These findings provide preliminary evidence on PMS-related psychosocial and lifestyle factors and may inform nursing screening and health promotion in campus health services. Longitudinal studies are needed to further explore these associations.
