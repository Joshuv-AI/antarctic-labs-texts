A semantic segmentation framework with UNet-pyramid for landslide prediction using remote sensing data
Authors: Arush Kaushal, Ashok Kumar Gupta, Vivek Kumar Sehgal
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Landslides are frequent all over the world, posing serious threats to human life, infrastructure, and economic operations, making them chronic disasters. This study proposes a novel landslide detection methodology that is automated and based on a hybrid deep learning approach. Currently, Deep Learning is constrained by the lack of applicability, lack of data, and low efficiency in landslide detection but with recent advancement in deep learning-based solutions for landslide detection has sparked considerable advantages over traditional techniques. In order to prevent and mitigate disaster, we introduced a hybrid model based on remote sensing technologies such as satellite images. Specifically, the proposed approach consists hybrid U-Net model integrated with a pyramid pooling layer for landslide detection, which uses high-resolution landslide images from the Landslide4Sense dataset. The UNet-Pyramid model has the following modifications: To improve feature acquisition and advancements to strengthen the model’s attention U-Net architecture is integrated with the pyramid pooling layers and OBIA technique. The UNet-Pyramid model was trained and validated using labeled images taken from the Landslide4Sense dataset and the validated set using OBIA to improve its efficacy. The overall Precision, Recall, and F1 Score of the UNet-pyramid model for landslide detection are 91%, 84%, and 87%, respectively.

Introduction

Landslides are a common natural phenomenon caused by various factors such as earthquakes, heavy rainfall, river erosion, artificial slope cutting, and unnatural or natural groundwater activity1,2. Currently, due to changes in climate, high urbanization, and seismic activities the number of landslide occurrences has gradually increased with long-term consequences, such as destruction on assets, structures, and human life3,4. Landslides are geological events where rocks, sediments, and soils move downslope under the influence of gravity. These movements can vary in speed and scale, often causing significant damage to infrastructure and posing risks to human lives. Historically, landslides were identified through manual exploration. Experts would physically survey areas susceptible to landslides, looking for visual indicators such as slope instability, cracks in the ground, or previous landslide scars5. While effective, manual methods were labor-intensive, time-consuming, and sometimes limited in scope, especially in rugged or remote terrain. The advent of geospatial technologies has revolutionized landslide detection. Aerial photogrammetry, for example, utilizes high-resolution aerial photographs to create precise 3D models of landscapes, enabling detailed analysis of terrain features and potential landslide risks. Satellite remote sensing has further enhanced this capability by providing continuous monitoring over large areas, allowing for the detection of subtle changes in landscape dynamics that may indicate impending landslides6. These technologies offer a diversified approach to identifying landslide-prone areas compared to traditional methods, significantly improving the efficiency and accuracy of detection.

To prevent potential risks and minimize the impacts on infrastructure, communities, and the environment, monitoring and detecting landslides are essential. Conversely, conventional approaches for the landslide prediction often relies on subjective visual interpretation of satellite or aerial imagery, which can be time consuming and human prone subjectivity.Machine Learning (ML), which has emerged as a game-changer in landslide detection. ML algorithms can be trained on large datasets of geospatial imagery to automatically detect patterns associated with landslides. These algorithms learn to recognize features such as changes in terrain elevation, vegetation cover, or surface morphology that indicate landslide susceptibility. By automating this process, ML not only enhances the speed and efficiency of landslide detection but also improves the accuracy and reliability of early warning systems. This technological advancement is crucial for mitigating the destructive impacts of landslides by enabling proactive measures such as evacuation planning, infrastructure reinforcement, and land-use management strategies in vulnerable areas. Therefore, there is a need for more objective and automated approaches to detect and map landslides. Most landslides have exposed soil or rock slopes with foliage, which imparts complicated inter and intra spectral features in optical imaging. Remote sensing technological advancements have increased the spatial and spectral resolutions of images obtained from satellites such as the Sentinel and Landsat series which provides significant aid in monitoring large landslide zones. Existing techniques for landslide prediction may be generally divided into different categories: local Field Exploration, ML approaches, and Deep Learning (DL) approaches as shown in Fig. 17,8. Although field exploration is acknowledged for its remarkable effectiveness in landslide prediction, it is hindered by fundamental challenges such as possible hazards to personnel, time constraints due to the vast geographic scope, substantial costs associated with deploying resources and carrying out on-site surveys, and inaccuracies in manually covering large terrains9. Manual exploration was formerly employed to locate landslides. However, the introduction of geospatial technology such as remote sensing (RS) images and aerial imaging, has resulted in different ways for detecting regions prone to landslides. The use of multiple image types, such as high-resolution satellite and aerial images has substantially boosted the accuracy of landslide prediction models10. Satellite imagery plays a crucial role in landslide prediction by providing comprehensive spatial data that can reveal important indicators of potential landslide activity. Despite significant advancements in landslide detection technologies, as evidenced by recent research, there remains a pressing need for the enhancement of hybrid ML and DL models. Models such as GMNet, FCADenseNet, BisDeNet, and GDSNet, along with traditional algorithms like KNN, SVM, RF, CNN, FCN, CTDNet, and HADeeNet have demonstrated varying degrees of success in detecting landslides using different methodologies. However, these models still exhibit limitations concerning scalability, generalizability, and accuracy across heterogeneous terrains and changing environmental conditions. For instance, while BisDeNet is recognized for its lightweight architecture and efficiency, it yields performance slightly below that of the DeiT model. Similarly, GDSNet, despite enhancing detection accuracy for small landslides post-earthquake, has opportunities for improvement in overall precision. Moreover, while traditional ML algorithms such as KNN, SVM, and RF offer robustness in certain contexts, they may not capture the spatial hierarchies and complex features present in high-resolution remote sensing data as effectively as DL models. Integrating the strengths of various approaches-such as employing the feature extraction capabilities of the Swin Transformer alongside the contextual understanding of OBIA, and the classification prowess of algorithms like U-Net yield more holistic detection framework. Such hybrid models leverage spectral and spatial information effectively to enhance the model’s ability to generalize across diverse conditions.

In this study OBIA is implemented that extracts and analyzes meaningful objects or regions in an image, rather than examining individual pixels in isolation. By focusing on the spatial relationships between objects, OBIA offers a comprehensive understanding of the scene, enhancing prediction accuracy in complex environments like landslide-prone areas. Traditional methods often struggle to accurately identify potential landslide events because they may not fully capture the intricate spatial relationships and fine details present in the data. The hybrid model proposed in this study addresses these challenges by integrating the U-Net architecture, which excels in semantic segmentation through its ability to capture fine details and spatial relationships, with a pyramid pooling layer that enhances feature extraction by aggregating information across multiple scales. Additionally, the incorporation of OBIA with Swin Transform techniques allows the model to analyze and understand contextual information within the images more effectively.

Unlike traditional segmentation frameworks, UNet-Pyramid with Swin Transform and OBIA introduces advanced features that enhance performance in various applications. There are three important contributions give by this research work. The rule-based OBIA technique enhances feature extraction by categorizing image objects through predefined rules, enabling a more structured and context-aware interpretation of the data. Coupled with the Swin Transformer, this approach focuses on hierarchical feature representation, further refining the identification of important features.

The Pyramid Pooling Layer improves segmentation by employing a multi-scale approach that effectively captures both local and global contextual information. This allows the model to identify fine-grained details while maintaining a broader understanding of the image structure. By integrating this layer into a hybrid U-Net-Pyramid model, the segmentation process becomes more robust, ensuring precise delineation of landslide boundaries in varying terrain conditions.

The hybrid U-Net-Pyramid model, with its combination of rule-based OBIA, Swin Transform, and Pyramid Pooling, delivers a powerful solution for landslide detection. Its ability to leverage multi-scale feature extraction, hierarchical representations, and advanced segmentation techniques leads to more accurate and reliable predictions. This makes it particularly well-suited for critical applications in natural disaster monitoring and management, providing timely and accurate insights for risk assessment and mitigation strategies.

Methodology

In this research, we focus on developing a hybrid DL methodology for improving landslide prediction accuracy. Landslides present significant risks to human lives and infrastructure, underscoring the need for precise predictive models. Traditional methods often struggle with extracting detailed spatial information from satellite imagery, motivating our approach to integrate advanced techniques. Our methodology centers around the U-Net model, a robust architecture known for its effectiveness in semantic segmentation tasks. By incorporating a pyramid pooling layer, we aim to aggregate multi-scale features, enhancing the model’s ability to capture relevant information across varying spatial resolutions. OBIA complements these efforts by analyzing coherent objects based on spectral, textural, and contextual attributes rather than individual pixels, thereby improving the model’s capability to discern complex patterns indicative of landslide susceptibility. The Landslide4Sense dataset, combining Sentinel-2 multispectral data with ALOS PALSAR data, serves as the foundation for training and evaluating our hybrid model. Data preprocessing steps, including normalization and noise reduction, ensure consistency and optimize model performance. During training, we employ data augmentation techniques to enhance dataset diversity and generalize the model’s predictions. This research aims to advance landslide prediction capabilities, providing valuable insights for proactive disaster management and mitigation strategies in landslide-prone regions.

Swin transform

The Swin Transformer (Shifted Window Transformer) is a type of vision transformer designed for efficient image processing and feature extraction in computer vision tasks. Unlike traditional transformers that operate on entire images as sequences of patches, the Swin Transformer introduces a hierarchical structure by dividing the image into non-overlapping windows (local regions), applying self-attention within each window. This window-based approach significantly reduces computational complexity compared to full-image self-attention. What sets Swin Transformer apart is its shifted window mechanism, where the windows are shifted between layers, allowing the model to capture both local and global interactions across the image. As the network deepens, the window size increases, enabling the model to handle progressively larger regions and capture increasingly abstract, high-level features. This hierarchical processing allows Swin to balance computational efficiency and the ability to learn rich, context-aware features.

Object-Based Image Analysis (OBIA)

It is a traditional image processing technique used primarily in remote sensing and geospatial image analysis, where the goal is to classify and analyze images based on objects rather than individual pixels. OBIA first segments an image into meaningful regions or objects using algorithms that group pixels with similar characteristics such as color, texture, and shape. These segments, which often correspond to real-world features like buildings, roads, or vegetation, are then analyzed individually using handcrafted features like shape, area, perimeter, and spatial relationships. This method is particularly useful for tasks where objects have distinct boundaries or are easily distinguishable from one another, such as land cover classification or object detection in satellite imagery. OBIA benefits from domain-specific knowledge, as experts can guide the segmentation and feature extraction processes. Unlike pixel-based classification, OBIA provides a more contextualized understanding of the image, making it suitable for analyzing high-resolution remote sensing images where objects are key to the analysis.

OBIA is highlighted as a sophisticated approach that goes beyond traditional pixel-based methods. Instead of analyzing individual pixels independently, OBIA treats groups of pixels as meaningful objects. This approach considers not only the spectral characteristics (such as color and intensity) of pixels but also their spatial context and geographical relationships within the image. By incorporating contextual information, OBIA can identify and analyze coherent objects within the imagery that are indicative of landslide-prone areas. For example, it can detect changes in land cover, vegetation density, terrain morphology, and other features that might precede or indicate landslide events. In contrast, pixel-based analysis focuses on examining each individual pixel based on its spectral properties alone. While straightforward, this method may struggle with images that exhibit complex textures, varied spectral signatures, or where there are subtle transitions between different land cover types or geological features. In such cases, OBIA’s ability to integrate spatial context and relationships between objects enhances its effectiveness in detecting and mapping landslides accurately. The advancement in OBIA and its application to landslide prediction is underscored by the availability of high-resolution satellite imagery and the development of advanced processing tools. These tools enable semi-automated or autonomous analysis, reducing the reliance on manual interpretation and significantly improving the efficiency and accuracy of landslide detection efforts. This technological evolution has led to a more refined approach in remote sensing-based landslide categorization and prediction, where OBIA and pixel-based analysis represent complementary methods suited for different types of satellite imagery and terrain conditions.

Comprehending the limits of pixelbased methods, especially for image classification, OBIA has grown in prominence. This is notably visible in overcoming the limitations of perpixel analysis, particularly in the framework of high-spatial resolution and Very High Resolution (VHR) remote-sensing imaging. OBIA offers unique image analysis approaches, such as satellite image analysis, in which feature organizations and occurrences are explored by evaluating image objects created from a segmentation method rather than relying merely on individual pixel values. Segmentation in OBIA is a critical process that transforms individual pixels into cohesive objects based on their spectral, textural, morphological, and topographical properties40. This transformation enhances the ability to detect landslides more accurately while minimizing false positives. In landslide detection using OBIA, the process typically involves two main steps: segmentation and classification. Segmentation is challenging because it requires defining parameters that determine the size and shape of objects within the image. This task often involves iterative approaches where different segmentation methods are tested and refined based on visual inspection to ensure the objects accurately represent meaningful features in the landscape40. Once objects are segmented, classification comes into play, where these objects are categorized based on predetermined criteria. For landslide detection, this classification step often involves setting thresholds manually based on specific diagnostic characteristics of landslides, such as changes in terrain elevation, vegetation cover, or geological features that indicate instability41. Despite its advantages over traditional per-pixel methods, OBIA faces obstacles when integrated with ML models for more complex applications. One significant challenge is determining the optimal scale parameter for analyzing objects related to geographic features like landslides. Landslide features can vary widely in size and spatial context within satellite imagery, making it difficult to consistently apply segmentation techniques across different scales42. This variability underscores the importance of refining OBIA methodologies and integrating them effectively with ML algorithms to improve accuracy in detecting and mapping landslides across diverse environmental conditions.

Fully convolutional network

Fully Convolutional Networks (FCN) have been developed as a deep learning architecture capable of efficiently dealing with the issue of training degradation, which can arise while adding additional convolutional layers to enhance the complexity of the model’s representation for handling features of varying shapes and sizes. FCN replace fully connected layers with convolutional and up sampling layers, making them appropriate for image-to-image mapping, which is very important in landslide prediction. FCN accept the input in the form of image patches which can be of any size and allow the analysis of images with all different resolutions. Further, this network collects the global and the local contextual features via a skip-connection between the down and up sampling layer to allow more precise segmentation. Among several FCN models U-Net is an effective model that can be train with a small number of patches as well. This is useful when data availability is very limited which is a usual case in landslide prediction30,43.

U-Net

U-Net was proposed by Ronneberger et al44. in 2015 for the purpose of biomedical image segmentation, since it has been widely used for a variety of image segmentation applications, including landslide detection. It is well-known for its U-shaped encoder-decoder architecture as shown in Fig. 9. These encoder-decoder allows U-Net for better image segmentation. They are the two fundamental components of the U-Net model which are linked by a skip connection that further connects the corresponding layers. The encoder part is useful in gathering contextual information from the input image, while the decoder part builds the segmentation maps in the segmentation process45. The encoder part consists convolutional layer which further consists max pooling layer. Convolutional layers are used to generate feature representations from the input images and max pooling layers downsample the spatial dimensions of the feature representations. Which further, enables the model to collect contextual information. The skip connections set up the direct connections between comparable levels in the decoder and encoder part.

These skip connections allow the data to be transmitted from the encoder to the decoder. Upsampling layers enhance the overall dimension of the feature maps and convolutional layers enrich the segmentation map by utilizing the features of the encoder. As the decoder advances, the spatial dimensions of the feature maps continuously rise as the number of channels decreases, finally producing a segmentation map containing pixel-level predictions. By upsampling and refining the features learnt during the encoding step, the U-Net model is able to provide precise and accurate segmentations. The U-Net model produces a segmentation map, which is a pixel-bypixel map in which each pixel is assigned a probability value indicating its chance to be assigned to certain class, such as landslide or non-landslide.

If the patch size is too small, it may fail to capture the entire extent of a landslide, resulting in incomplete segmentations. In remote sensing imaging, the negative-class (non-landslide) is frequently common than the positive-class (landslide), resulting in a class imbalance. Using larger patch sizes can worsen the class imbalance issue, as larger patches may contain more negative samples compared to positive samples, resulting in a biased model that may struggle to accurately identify landslides for a certain dataset and task, experimentation and evaluation are required.

The pyramid pooling layer in the UNet-Pyramid model plays a crucial role in enhancing the model’s ability to capture and integrate multi-scale features from input images. This layer operates by aggregating information from four different pyramid scales, each designed to capture features at varying levels of spatial granularity. At the coarsest level, indicated in red, global pooling is employed to produce a single summary output, effectively reducing the entire feature map to a single value. Subsequent pyramid layers further split the feature map into discrete sub-regions, each producing pooled representations for different spatial locations. Each pyramid level generates feature maps of different sizes, achieved through 1\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\times$$\end{document}×1 convolution layers that maintain the significance of global traits while reducing the dimensionality of the context image to 1/N of its original size, where N corresponds to the size of each pyramid level. Following this reduction, bilinear interpolation is used to upscale the low-dimensional features back to the original size of the feature map. The final pyramid is created by concatenating the features extracted from each pyramid level, thereby aggregating global features across multiple scales. The selection of pyramid levels and their respective sizes is critical, as it determines the overall effectiveness of the pyramid pooling layer. By employing pooling kernels of varying sizes at different stages, the layer ensures that it captures a suitable representation gap between different scales, abstracting meaningful details from discrete sub-regions of the input images. In the case of the UNet-Pyramid design described, the pyramid pooling module incorporates four levels, each with bin sizes of 1\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\times$$\end{document}×1, 2\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\times$$\end{document}×2, 3\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\times$$\end{document}×3, and 6\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\times$$\end{document}×6, illustrating its capability to handle features at different scales effectively. Overall, the pyramid pooling layer in the UNet-Pyramid model enhances feature extraction by integrating multi-scale contextual information, thereby improving the model’s ability to analyze complex spatial patterns and make accurate predictions, such as in the context of landslide detection or other image segmentation tasks. This design choice underscores the importance of adaptive feature pooling strategies in DL architectures for achieving robust performance across diverse datasets and applications. OBIA facilitates the incorporation and utilization of additional features, such as spectral information, geometric attributes, topological relationships, and textural details among generated objects, for image classification, contrasting with pixel-based techniques. In this study, we specifically leverage the geometrical properties and spectral information of objects using OBIA. Integrating such features through knowledge-based rulesets has the potential to enhance outcomes produced by ML or DL models, particularly in complex tasks like landslide prediction. Our objective is to utilize object features and hierarchical knowledge-based rulesets to improve the segmentation of landslide-prone areas generated by the U-Net-pyramid model, thereby reducing errors in detection algorithms. We have combined Swin Transformer and OBIA to create a hybrid approach for landslide prediction using remote sensing imagery, effectively leveraging the strengths of both methods to improve feature extraction and prediction accuracy. First, we applied OBIA to segment the remote sensing images into meaningful objects, based on properties like color, texture, and shape. This segmentation allows us to focus on critical regions such as steep slopes, vegetation, and water bodies-factors that play a key role in landslide susceptibility. OBIA extracts handcrafted features such as slope gradients, surface roughness, and land cover types, which provide valuable domain-specific insights and reduce the complexity of the image. Then, we passed these segmented objects into a Swin Transformer, which excels at learning hierarchical and global image features through self-attention mechanisms. The Swin Transformer captures complex, long-range relationships between different terrain features, such as the interaction between water drainage, vegetation cover, and topography, which are essential for accurate landslide prediction. By combining domain-driven, handcrafted features from OBIA with the context-aware, learned representations from the Swin Transformer, we can extract both local, detailed features and global, contextual information from the images. This hybrid approach not only improves prediction accuracy but also enhances the model’s ability to recognize complex patterns that contribute to landslides. Despite challenges like aligning OBIA segments with transformer patches and merging the two sets of features, our combined approach significantly enhances landslide susceptibility mapping, leading to more precise and reliable predictions of landslide-prone areas in remote sensing data. Within OBIA, a rule-based classification system is constructed to guide the categorization of objects into different types, with a specific focus on identifying potential landslide zones. The hierarchical rulesets consider diverse spectral signatures and object geometries, enhancing the sophistication of the categorization process. The integration of OBIA features into the UNet-Pyramid model seamlessly leverages the neural network’s capability to probabilistically estimate each pixel’s likelihood of being associated with landslides. To achieve optimal results, the parameters of this integrated model are fine-tuned post-training to enhance performance. Additionally, the hybrid architecture combines U-Net’s ability to generate landslide probability maps with OBIA’s enriched features. The Swin Transformer, renowned for capturing contextual information and long-term dependencies in images, complements this architecture, potentially enhancing landslide detection capabilities. Furthermore, employing a self-training technique enhances the model’s generalization ability in the test domain, enabling it to learn from new data and improve its performance on previously unseen datasets. This methodological approach aims to advance the accuracy and reliability of landslide prediction models, supporting proactive measures for disaster management and mitigation efforts.

This study presents a hybrid model for landslide detection that combines the UNet-Pyramid architecture, OBIA, and Swin Transformer. The UNet-Pyramid model utilizes a multi-scale feature extraction approach, integrating pyramid pooling layers with the U-Net encoder-decoder structure to effectively capture both local terrain variations and large-scale contextual patterns. This enables the model to preserve fine-grained details while also recognizing broader topographic features, essential for accurate landslide detection. Additionally, OBIA enhances the model’s efficiency by segmenting the remote sensing images into meaningful objects based on spectral and spatial features, such as slope and vegetation cover. These pre-processed objects help the model focus on relevant regions, reducing complexity and improving feature extraction. Handcrafted features like slope, aspect, and surface roughness, derived through OBIA, were also integrated into the model, providing domain-specific knowledge crucial for landslide susceptibility mapping. To further strengthen the model’s performance, the Swin Transformer was incorporated to capture long-range dependencies and global contextual information through self-attention mechanisms. This allowed the model to understand relationships between terrain features across various scales, making it particularly effective in complex landscapes. By combining multi-scale feature extraction, segmentation-based object grouping, and global contextual learning, the hybrid model demonstrated superior performance in landslide detection, showing significant improvements in both accuracy and robustness compared to traditional pixel-level DL models. The model’s ability to handle diverse geographic and environmental conditions makes it highly effective for real-world landslide prediction across varied terrains and regions.

To enhance the performance and computational efficiency of the UNet-Pyramid model for landslide detection, several optimization strategies were implemented, along with careful consideration of the architectural details. First, data visualization was employed to deeply understand the problem, by visualizing images and their corresponding masks to identify the critical features and patterns relevant to landslide detection. Data preprocessing followed, where the dataset was divided into 80% training, 10% validation, and 10% testing subsets to ensure robust model evaluation. Additionally, the input image size was standardized to 256\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\times$$\end{document}×256 pixels for all models to facilitate efficient processing. During the training process, the UNet-Pyramid model was trained using the prepared images and corresponding masks over 100 epochs to ensure convergence and optimal feature learning. The UNet-Pyramid architecture integrates the U-Net framework, known for its effective encoder-decoder structure, with pyramid pooling layers to capture multi-scale features. The U-Net’s encoder extracts hierarchical features from input images through successive convolutional layers and downsampling operations, while the decoder reconstructs the image with upsampling, allowing for accurate pixel-level segmentation. In the UNet-Pyramid model, the decoder includes skip connections that pass features directly from the encoder to the corresponding layers in the decoder, helping to retain fine-grained details during the reconstruction process. Pyramid pooling layers were introduced in the architecture to further improve feature extraction. These layers aggregate multi-scale contextual information by performing pooling at different scales, such as 1x1, 2x2, and 4x4 kernels, to capture both local and global patterns in the image. This helps the model better understand contextual relationships across different spatial levels of the image, which is particularly important for landslide detection, where both small-scale features (like individual landforms) and large-scale patterns (such as entire slope areas) contribute to landslide risk. After pooling, the features from different scales are concatenated, enhancing the model’s ability to recognize complex patterns that are not easily captured by standard convolutional layers alone.Fig. 14Visual representation of training dataset images.Fig. 15Visual representation of validation images.

The Adam optimization algorithm was used for model training, with the ReduceLROnPlateau function from Keras employed to dynamically adjust the learning rate during training. This function monitors the model’s performance and reduces the learning rate when progress stagnates, improving convergence and training efficiency. The initial learning rate was set to 0.01, with a minimum learning rate of 0.00001 to allow fine-tuning in the later stages of training. Finally, once the model was trained, it was used to make predictions on the test images, and the predicted masks were visualized to evaluate the accuracy and effectiveness of the model’s output. The integration of U-Net’s encoder-decoder structure with pyramid pooling layers allowed the model to efficiently handle both fine-grained details and global contextual patterns, making it highly effective for landslide detection. This combination of architectural strategies and computational techniques ensured that the UNet-Pyramid model was both efficient during training and effective in making accurate, real-time landslide predictions.

Conclusion

The primary goal of this research is to determine the possibility of integrating two domains: a Hybrid Deep Learning model and OBIA, with an emphasis on landslide prediction. By implementing OBIA as a modified procedure for the hybrid approach, we provided a feasible solution. In this context, we presented a novel landslide prediction integration approach, processing 3,799 patches that combined optical layers from the Sentinel-2 sensor with a digital elevation model and slope layer which are derived from the ALOS and PALSAR. A rule-based OBIA was developed specifically to improve the accuracy and a pyramid layer were added for multi-scale contextual information to recognize small landslides and obtain stronger features information in landslide prediction results derived from UNet-Pyramid model. On the Landslide4Sense dataset, UNet-Pyramid model surpassed other state-of-the-art model’s performance in landslide detection accuracy. The findings of this research work demonstrated the efficacy of the proposed model, with 87% F1-Score, 91% precision, and 84% recall rates.

While several DL models have shown amazing success in different object classification and segmentation tasks, there is much room for improvement, particularly by including object-based classification techniques rather than relying primarily on pixel-based ones. Approaches based on OBIA, which work on homogenous collections of image pixels, replicate how experts recognize landslide zones as unique entities. Given the difficulties in effectively detecting landslides using pixel based DL models such as the U-Net, combining the resulting observations from these models with a pyramid layer and OBIA presents a promising approach to incorporating prior knowledge into the landslide illustrating process at a regional level. Future research on landslide prediction using remote sensing data will likely focus on improving accuracy and application by including environmental factors such as rainfall and soil moisture. Understanding model integrations to solve DL issues such as fuzzy boundaries offers promises towards better results. The scope includes numerous applications such as building extraction, which provides vital insights into the efficacy of hybrid techniques in various settings.
