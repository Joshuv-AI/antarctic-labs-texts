Protocol for feeding strategy and proteomics analysis of zebrafish Danio rerio using S-trap and iTRAQ techniques
Authors: Kathiresan Purushothaman, Daanish Kaul, Saraphina Dianne Tneo Rwei Qing, Sérgio DC Rocha, Aleksandar B Göksu, Byron Morales Lange, Liv Torunn Mydland, Shubha Vij, Lin Qingsong, Margareth Øverland, Charles McL Press
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SummaryYeast serves as a functional alternative and sustainable protein source in aquaculture. This protocol outlines feeding strategy and intestinal proteome analysis of zebrafish (Danio rerio), using S-trap for digestion, iTRAQ, and mass spectrometry for protein quantification. Additionally, it details the analysis of chemical components in feed and functional assessments via Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG), Eukaryotic Orthologous Group (KOG), and Gene Ontology (GO).For complete details on the use and execution of this protocol, please refer to Purushothaman et al.1Subject areas: immunology, model organisms, proteomics

Before you begin

Yeast is a sustainable alternative protein source in aquaculture, rich in bioactive compounds that can act as immunostimulants to enhance fish growth and immunity.1,2,3,4 Notably, the yeast Cyberlindnera jadinii has been shown to modulate inflammatory responses induced by soybean meal in fish by enhancing pathways related to wound healing and adjusting innate immunity through taurine and hypotaurine pathways.5 Zebrafish, Danio rerio, is commonly used in laboratory research due to its well-annotated genome, transparent embryos, small size, and short generation time.6,7,8,9

To explore the impact of yeast inclusion in fish diets on immune response and health, a feeding trial was conducted using five different diets, each containing 10% of four distinct yeast strains, including autolyzed C. jadinii. This trial was performed with lab-bred zebrafish at the fish laboratory at NMBU, Norway. The fish were maintained in ZebTec Active Blue recirculating systems under controlled conditions: a temperature of 26°C–28°C, pH 7.0–7.5, and a 14/10-h light/dark cycle.

Here, we present a protocol for the feeding strategy and subsequent zebrafish proteomics analysis using S-trap, iTRAQ, and 2D LC-MS/MS to characterize specifically the effects of C. jadinii.10

Expected outcomes

The anticipated outcomes of the protocol encompass a comprehensive understanding of the effects of dietary interventions on various aspects of fish physiology and molecular pathways. Through meticulous execution of the outlined methodology, researchers can expect to produce valuable data elucidating critical insights into fish growth and survival dynamics, as well as protein expression patterns in response to different dietary regimes.

Specifically, the protocol aims to shed light on the impact of dietary composition on fish growth parameters and mortality rates. Researchers can anticipate generating data detailing trends in growth performance indicators, such as body weight gain, specific growth rate (SGR), and feed conversion ratio (FCR) over designated time intervals. These results will offer valuable insights into the efficacy of different dietary formulations in promoting optimal growth and health outcomes in fish populations.

In our publication, the fish in the pilot study group fed the high protein diet had a significantly higher body weight at the end of 28 days when compared to the low protein diet group, but not with the low protein diet group with C. jadinii yeast. This suggests that C. jadinii poses no adverse effects on growth. In the feeding trial, all five diets yielded similar results with no significant differences in body weight gain, SGR and feed conversion ratio (FCR).

In addition, the protocol characterizes the proteomic landscape of fish intestinal samples under various dietary conditions. Researchers can expect to quantify a substantial number of proteins and identify differentially abundant proteins (DAPs) in response to dietary interventions. By categorizing these proteins into higher abundance proteins (HAPs) and lower abundance proteins (LAPs), researchers can gain insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying dietary effects on protein expression.

Furthermore, the protocol aims to annotate and classify the identified proteins, providing functional annotations and insights into their roles in cellular processes. Through EuKaryotic Orthologous Groups (KOG) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis, researchers can anticipate elucidating the functional categories and pathways associated with DAPs. This comprehensive annotation will enable researchers to unravel the biological significance of differentially expressed proteins and their implications for fish health and metabolism.

In conclusion, the expected outcomes of the protocol encompass a multifaceted understanding of the interplay between dietary interventions and fish physiology at both the macroscopic and molecular levels. By generating comprehensive datasets and elucidating molecular mechanisms, the protocol aims to contribute significantly to the advancement of knowledge in aquaculture research and inform future dietary strategies for enhancing fish health and productivity.

Limitations

Regarding feed production

Cold pelleting is not representative of the extruded feed use in aquaculture. However, it is still a valid and accepted approach to test the effects of novel ingredients. This is especially the case if we are testing ingredients that are sensitive to high temperature during conventional extrusion in feed manufacturing. Pellets produced through cold pelleting in a pelleting machine can have similar nutritional values, when formulated accordingly, and they can have similar technical quality regarding hardness and sinking velocities.

Regarding proteomics

The iTRAQ protocol enables the simultaneous quantification of multiple peptides in a single experiment, but it may not provide comprehensive coverage of the proteome. Certain proteins or peptides may be missed due to limitations in detection sensitivity or the inability to identify and quantify peptides with certain physicochemical properties.

iTRAQ is more suitable for analyzing a limited number of samples (4 or 8) due to the availability of commercial multiplexing kits. For sample numbers greater than 8, they have to be divided into different groups and analyzed separately with different LC-MS runs. Only proteins commonly identified among different LC-MS runs can be compared, thus resulting in larger missing values.

iTRAQ quantification can be inaccurate due to precursor ion selection issues—where ions with similar m/z values may enter the collision cell and contribute to background signals of the reporter ions—and the impurity of isotopes, as the isotopes used to label each channel are not 100% pure, leading to signal contamination in adjacent reporter channels. Variability in labeling efficiency and fragmentation during mass spectrometry can also lead to quantification errors.

iTRAQ labeling requires a minimum amount of protein for successful labeling and detection. This requirement can be challenging when working with limited sample amount.

Troubleshooting

Problem 1

Assembly of pelleted feed

Inconsistent pellet length and/or formation of lumps with extruded pellets.

The diameter of pellets is regulated by the dye at the end of the barrel of the pelleting machine. However, working with small dimensions presents some challenges to obtain a consistent pellet length. The length of the pellets is regulated by the speed of the screw and the rotation speed of the knives. Since the dye is so small, the pressure, and consequently the temperature at the end of the barrel, will increase. Elevated pressure and temperature will affect the exit speed of the pellets, leading to an uneven length of the pellets. Furthermore, the amount of water has an important influence during cold pelleting. Water contributes to the fluidity of the dough in the barrel, but most importantly, it promotes the binding capacity of the ingredients. This might be the most challenging step, since the amount of water needed depends on the water absorption capacity of the ingredients used and other factors such as temperature, processing time and even air humidity. Low levels of water in the mixture will decrease fluidity and reduce the binding capacity of the ingredients. Too much water will lead to “sticky” pellets, creating doubled size pellets, which represents a critical problem when working with small fish.

Potential solution

An advantage of cold pelleting is that the pellets can be re-pelleted (before drying) if the technical quality is not optimal. However, depending on the 22°C and air humidity, the moisture of the mixture can vary from batch to batch which needs to be taken into consideration.

•Diameter and length of the pellet: The speed of the screw combined with the speed of the knives should be carefully tuned along the process. The temperature at the exit of the pelleting machine will increase gradually due to the pressure. Therefore, short breaks should be taken during pelleting to avoid overheating and the speed of the knife and/or the screw should be changed accordingly.

•Consistency of the pellet: Keep in mind that when starting the pelleting, the end of the barrel is still at 22°C and the quality of pellets will change once the pressure and temperature increase. If the strings are “too lumpy” or break too easily, it means more water should be added. If so, the strings should be air dried for some minutes before placing them back to the extruder. If in doubt, one should turn on the knife and perform a pre-test of the pellet size consistency. If most of the pellets are consistent in size, easy to separate and without dust (or have a flake-like appearance), one can start collecting the pellets. The pellets should be placed in a tray, forming a thin layer to avoid deformation of the pellets in the bottom. The pellets should be mixed a few times to facilitate the air drying and avoid the formation of clumps. Mixing is also recommended during the drying process.

Problem 2

Assembly of pelleted feed

There is too much dust/crumbles from the pellets.

Some dust/crumbles are expected during the process of feed handling. However, large amounts of dust lead to feed waste. This situation is especially a problem when the amount of feed/ingredients are limited. Moreover, too much dust could lead to loss of feed particles during feeding thus the feed intake will be overestimated.

Potential solution

The dust/crumbles from pellets can have two origins during the process.

•The pellets are overdried: The final water content should be between 8%–10% to reduce water activity and prevent microbial activity. However, when the pellets are too dry the chance of creating dust/crumbles increases and the quality of the pellets is reduced. The dry matter of the pellets should be measured several times during the drying, especially when the water content is close to 10%.

•The water content of the mixture could be too low, leading to poor binding of the ingredients after drying: A small amount of water should be added to the mixture, but not to the point when pellets become too sticky (check problem 1).
