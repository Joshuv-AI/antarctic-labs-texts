Open versus laparoscopic techniques for incisional hernia repair: A systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials
Authors: Thamer H Alghamdi, Yara A Alghamdi, Fadya Y Alghamdi, Lujain M Alzahrani, Joud S Alghamdi, Ghadi M Alghamdi, Naif A Alharthi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe choice between open and laparoscopic techniques for incisional hernia repair remains debated in surgical practice. This systematic review and meta-analysis was aimed at comparing the clinical outcomes of open versus laparoscopic incisional hernia repair in adults, with a focus on postoperative complications, recurrence, recovery, and patient satisfaction.MethodsA comprehensive literature search was performed according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines. Randomized controlled trials (RCTs) comparing open and laparoscopic repair of incisional hernias were included. The main outcomes were postoperative complications (wound infection, hematoma, seroma, ileus, and sepsis), recurrence rates, time to recovery, and quality of life. A total of 726 patients from studies conducted in Europe and the United States were included: 365 in the open surgery group and 361 in the laparoscopic group.ResultsThe meta-analysis revealed no statistically significant differences between techniques regarding overall postoperative complications, recurrence rates, or quality-of-life scores. Although the rate of wound infection and the incidence of ileus were slightly lower in the laparoscopic group than the open surgery group, the differences were not statistically significant. Additionally, the recurrence rates were ‏comparable between approaches (risk ratio = 1.00, 95 % CI: 0.64–1.57). However, seroma formation exhibited significant heterogeneity across studies. ‏Laparoscopic repair was associated with faster recovery and better long-term quality of life, particularly physical and emotional well-being.ConclusionThis systematic review and meta-analysis demonstrated no significant differences in primary clinical outcomes between open and laparoscopic incisional hernia repair techniques. Both approaches had comparable complication and recurrence rates. However, laparoscopic repair might provide advantages of faster recovery and improved postoperative quality of life, and therefore is a viable alternative to open repair for suitable candidates. Further studies with larger sample sizes and standardized protocols are necessary to confirm these findings.Keywords: Abdominal wall hernia, Incisional hernia, Laparoscopic hernia repair, Meta-analysis, Open hernia repair, Systematic review

Introduction

Incisional hernia remains a frequent and challenging postoperative complication after abdominal surgeries.1,2 The reported hernia incidence rates after abdominal surgery are ∼5 % and vary according to surgery type and patient risk factors.3 These hernias not only result in aesthetic and functional impairment but also are associated with substantial morbidity, including pain, bowel obstruction, and recurrence, which might necessitate reoperation.4 Surgical repair is the principal treatment. Over the past 20 years, two primary approaches have been used: open hernia repair and laparoscopic hernia repair.5, 6, 7 The open technique, long considered the traditional approach, provides direct access to the defect and enables reinforcement through mesh placement.8,9 However, this method is associated with substantial wound complications, including infection, seroma formation, and prolonged recovery time.9 In contrast, laparoscopic techniques, first introduced in the 1990s, have gained popularity over open repair because of their smaller incisions, less postoperative pain, shorter hospital stays, and faster return to normal activity.10,11 Despite these advantages, concerns persist regarding the technical difficulty of laparoscopic procedures, increased long-term recurrence rates, and intraoperative complications such as bowel injury.12

Many randomized controlled trials (RCTs) have directly compared laparoscopic and open repair outcomes for various types of hernias,13,14 although most have not focused exclusively on incisional hernias. However, the findings have been inconsistent across studies. Some trials have reported lower complication rates and faster recovery in the laparoscopic group, while others have highlighted higher recurrence risks or no significant differences in long-term outcomes.15 For instance, a previous systematic review by Chalabi et al., based on five randomized controlled trials, showed no significant difference between approaches in terms of recurrence rate, hospital length of stay, and operation time, whereas the incidence of wound infection was significantly greater in the open repair group.16 However, the review's inclusion of only a few trials with short follow-up limited its strength. In addition, in a systematic review conducted by Martins et al., based on nine studies, the laparoscopy group, compared with the open surgery group, was twice as likely to report hernia recurrence, and less likely to have complications, including local infection, wound dehiscence, and local seroma.17 However, because ventral and incisional hernias were analyzed in combination, the findings might not be specific to incisional hernia. Another systematic review based on six randomized controlled trials and five additional comparative studies has indicated a lower incidence of wound infections and shorter sick leave in patients who underwent laparoscopic procedures rather than open surgery; however, no significant differences in the rates of other complications or surgery duration were found between approaches.18 Because the review was limited to recurrent inguinal hernia, the findings cannot be directly applied to incisional hernia.

In contrast to the studies reporting lower hernia recurrence rates, one systematic review indicated no significant difference in hernia recurrence between approaches; however, the laparoscopic procedure was found to have lower operation times and lower costs.19 However, the review did not adequately assess patient-centered outcomes, such as postoperative pain, quality of life, or return to normal activity. Furthermore, the heterogeneity in mesh types, surgeons’ expertise, and follow-up periods across studies hinders definitive conclusions from being drawn.

Given the growing body of RCT evidence, a comprehensive synthesis of the available data is essential to inform surgical practice. Overall, the existing meta-analyses are old, limited in scope, or not specific to incisional hernia, thus leaving uncertainty regarding the comparative effectiveness of laparoscopic versus open repair. Therefore, this systematic review and meta-analysis was aimed at comparing the efficacy and safety of laparoscopic versus open techniques in the repair of incisional hernias, with a focus on outcomes such as postoperative complications, recurrence rates, operative times, hospital length of stay, and patient recovery. The study findings provide critical insights regarding the optimal surgical approach and might contribute to evidence-based decision-making in hernia surgery.

Methods

This systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted to compare and evaluate the outcomes of open versus laparoscopic techniques in the repair of incisional hernias among adult patients. The study adhered to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines.

This study was registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) under ID CRD42024598721.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The inclusion criteria included all RCTs published in English comparing open and laparoscopic surgical approaches in the treatment of incisional hernias in adults older than 18 years. In addition, the studies were required to report at least one primary outcome, including recurrence rate, postoperative pain, recovery time, operational duration, other complications, or quality of life among patients with a minimum follow-up of 1 year. Non-randomized studies, observational studies, or studies that lacked a comparator group; studies focusing on pediatric patients or non-incisional hernias; and studies without sufficient outcome data were excluded.

Search strategy

A systematic search of the literature was conducted through two electronic databases: PubMed and Google Scholar. To identify the full range of pertinent publications, the search strategy combined Medical Subject Headings and free-text keywords. The search terms included “incisional hernia,” “ventral hernia,” “abdominal wall hernia,” “laparoscopic hernia repair,” “laparoscopic incisional hernia repair,” “open hernia repair,” “open incisional hernia repair,” “recurrence,” “postoperative pain,” “complications,” “recovery time,” “return to work,” and “quality of life.” Boolean operators “AND” and “OR” were used to refine the results. Only studies written in English were considered in the search. Beyond the database search, the reference lists of the included studies were manually screened to identify any additional qualifying trials. The literature search included studies published until October 2024.

Study selection

Two authors independently performed the two-stage selection process. Titles and abstracts initially found to be relevant were selected. Studies that appeared to satisfy the inclusion criteria were then subjected to a full-text review. The reviewers’ disagreements regarding study eligibility were settled through debate or according to advice from a third reviewer.

Risk of bias assessment

Using the Cochrane Risk of Bias tool for randomized trials (RoB 2.0), we assessed the internal validity and quality of the included studies. This tool focuses on five areas of bias: the randomization method, deviations from planned interventions, missing outcome data, assessment of outcomes, and selective reporting of results. Each area was classified as having low, uncertain, or high risk of bias. Each reviewer independently evaluated two studies, and any disagreements were settled through discussion.

Data analysis

Two reviewers working independently used a standardized data extraction form to extract data including study design, sample size, patient demographics, intervention and comparator specifics, follow-up time, and all pertinent outcomes. For research in which combining numerical data was impossible, a narrative synthesis was performed. Sensitivity analyses were conducted, where feasible, with exclusion of studies evaluated to have high risk of bias, to assess the robustness of the combined estimates.

Statistical approaches

The Review Manager (RevMan) program was used for meta-analysis. For dichotomous results such as recurrence and complication rates, risk ratios (RRs) with 95 % confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated. For continuous results such as pain ratings and recovery time, mean differences or standardized mean differences were calculated according to the consistency of the outcome measurements across investigations. Heterogeneity in research was evaluated with the I2 index. We considered an I2 value of ≤25 % to reflect low heterogeneity; a value of 25–75 % to indicate moderate heterogeneity; and a value > 75 % to reflect high heterogeneity. A fixed-effects model was used in cases with low heterogeneity, and a random-effects model was applied to account for variability in the presence of considerable heterogeneity.

Results

The five RCTs14,20, 21, 22, 23 (Figure 1) conducted across Europe and the United States included a total of 726 patients, who were nearly equally distributed between the open (365 patients) and laparoscopic (361 patients) repair groups (Table 1). Overall, the patient populations had a comparable mean age of approximately 56–65 years (59.9 [3.1] in the open surgery group and 59.8 [1.3] in the laparoscopic group), and a mean body mass index in the overweight to obese category (28–31 kg/m2). Although some variation was found across studies, most participants in most groups were men. Recording of comorbidities including diabetes mellitus, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, smoking history, and anticoagulant use varied across studies, but the recorded values were generally balanced between groups. The American Society of Anesthesiologists classification score was also similarly distributed (Table 2).

The intervention techniques differed between studies but generally followed standardized open or laparoscopic approaches, often using polypropylene or composite meshes. Laparoscopic repairs generally required shorter operative times than open repairs in most studies (e.g., Olmi et al. reported a significant difference of 61 vs. 150.9 min22), although this finding was not consistent across all trials. Patients in the laparoscopic groups experienced shorter hospital stays: several studies reported postoperative stays approximately 1–2 days shorter than those in the open repair groups. Furthermore, the use of surgical drains was substantially lower in laparoscopic procedures than open repair (Supplementary Table 1).

Intraoperative complications were infrequently reported in detail. However, conversion from laparoscopy to open repair occurred sporadically, and blood loss was either not reported or minimal. Postoperative complications, including wound infection, seroma, hematoma, ileus, and urinary tract infection, varied but tended to be lower in the laparoscopic groups. For example, Olmi et al. documented fewer wound-related complications and a markedly lower incidence of postoperative ileus in the laparoscopic group than the open repair group.22 Additionally, mesh-related complications were more frequently observed in open repairs than laparoscopic repairs (e.g., 7.5 % vs. 0 % in Asencio et al.20).

Hernia recurrence rates in the laparoscopic repair groups were generally lower than, or comparable to, those in the open repair groups. However, some studies reported slightly higher recurrence in the open repair than laparoscopic repair groups (e.g., Asencio et al.: 23.59 vs. 18.94 recurrences per 1000 person-years20). Return to work, when measured, was also faster in the laparoscopic cohorts, and the quality-of-life scores (e.g., EuraHS-QoL and SF-36) favored laparoscopic repair, thereby indicating better postoperative recovery and satisfaction. Eker et al. (2013)21 evaluated SF-36 scores preoperatively, and at 3 months and 5 years postoperatively, for patients undergoing open incisional hernia repair (group 1) and laparoscopic incisional hernia repair (group 2). Physical functioning improved at 3 months in both groups but declined at 5 years (group 1: 65.9 to 70.7 to 61.1; group 2: 60.8 to 66.1 to 62.3). Role limitations due to physical health slightly fluctuated in group 1 and steadily improved in group 2 (group 1: 43.8 to 39.3 to 44.2; group 2: 45.8 to 47.2 to 65.8). Role limitations due to emotional problems increased in both groups over time (group 1: 62.5 to 57.1 to 65.4; group 2: 50.0 to 77.8 to 71.9). Energy/vitality showed mild changes (group 1: 65.6 to 67.1 to 55.5; group 2: 53.8 to 60.6 to 63.1). Emotional well-being improved overall (group 1: 68.2 to 80.0 to 77.5; group 2: 53.5 to 75.1 to 73.1). Social functioning was stable or improved slightly in both groups (group 1: 76.5 to 66.1 to 67.0; group 2: 69.2 to 72.2 to 73.0). Bodily pain decreased in group 1 but increased in group 2 (group 1: 75.3 to 67.1 to 64.4; group 2: 66.7 to 72.8 to 71.7). General health perception worsened in group 1 but improved in group 2 (group 1: 58.8 to 55.9 to 47.2; group 2: 33.3 to 58.3 to 55.6). Both groups showed a decline in health status at 5 years (group 1: 67.9 to 38.4; group 2: 66.7 to 46.1). In the open repair group vs. the laparoscopic group, Itani et al. (2010) reported 13 % vs. 9 % improvements, respectively, in physical functioning and 6 % vs. 4 % improvements, respectively, in vitality; however, other SF-36 domains were not reported23 (Supplementary Table 2).

A meta-analysis of postoperative complications comparing open versus laparoscopic repair showed no statistically significant differences in all outcomes. For wound infection (four studies, 325 open vs. 315 laparoscopic), the RR was 2.79 (95 % CI: 0.97–8.07, I2 = 0 %, p = 0.37, Figure 2A). Seroma (four studies) had an RR of 0.72 (95 % CI: 0.21–2.43, I2 = 72 %, p = 0.01), indicating substantial heterogeneity (Figure 2B). Hematoma (three studies, 240 open vs. 230 laparoscopic) showed an RR of 0.92 (95 % CI: 0.45–1.90, I2 = 0 %, p = 0.60, Figure 2C). Ileus (three studies) showed an RR of 0.61 (95 % CI: 0.18–2.12, I2 = 0 %, p = 0.65, Figure 2D), whereas sepsis (three studies) showed an RR of 0.36 (95 % CI: 0.07–1.92, I2 = 0 %, p = 0.52, Figure 2E). Bleeding (three studies, 274 open vs. 264 laparoscopic) had an RR of 0.93 (95 % CI: 0.19–4.45, I2 = 0 %, p = 0.68, Figure 2F). Finally, recurrence (five studies, 365 open vs. 361 laparoscopic) had an RR of 1.00 (95 % CI: 0.64–1.57, I2 = 0 %, p = 0.88, Figure 3). Overall, the findings indicate no significant difference between approaches, with the exception of seroma, which showed high heterogeneity.

Risk of bias assessment

Table 3 shows the risk of bias assessment for the included randomized controlled trials. ‏Most studies demonstrated a low overall risk of bias, particularly in terms of attrition and reporting biases, which were consistently rated as low across all studies. Selection bias was low in three studies,14,21,23 whereas two studies20,22 had unclear selection bias due to insufficient information regarding the randomization methods. Performance and detection biases were not applicable in the context of the available data. One study22 exhibited high attrition bias, which contributed to its classification as having a moderate overall risk of bias. No other significant sources of bias were found in any of the studies.

Discussion

Overview of the findings

This meta-analysis of data from 726 patients in RCTs performed across Europe and the United States thoroughly compared laparoscopic and open procedures for incisional hernia repair. The patient groups were well matched regarding comorbidities and demographics, thus ensuring a fair comparison of surgical results. Operative time differed across studies, some of which reported shorter times for laparoscopic than open repairs. For instance, in the study by Olmi et al., the laparoscopic group (61 min) showed shorter operative times than the open group (150.9 min).22 However, this result was not always reproduced in other studies, thereby suggesting variation in surgical skill and technique standardization across hospitals. Nonetheless, laparoscopic repairs, compared with open repairs, usually required fewer surgical drains and had lower postoperative recovery times 1–2 days shorter, reflecting their less invasive characteristics.24, 25, 26

Complications and adhesion formation

This primary focus of this study was on postoperative problems. The meta-analysis demonstrated no statistically significant differences in wound infection, hematoma, ileus, sepsis, and bleeding between the open and laparoscopic methods. However, seroma formation showed considerable variability suggesting variable study outcomes. These results are consistent with those from earlier publications indicating similar complication rates between methods, some of which have even reported a tendency toward fewer wound infections in laparoscopic than open repairs.27, 28, 29 For example, a previous systematic review by Zhang et al. has revealed no significant differences in the incidence of hernia recurrence, postoperative hematoma, seroma, bleeding, bowel obstruction, or reoperation between interventions. However, the laparoscopic intervention group showed a lower incidence of wound infection and drainage, along with a higher incidence of bowel injuries.30 In addition, in another meta-analysis, open surgery carried a higher incidence of complications and longer hospital stays, whereas no marked difference in operational duration was observed between techniques.31

The hernia recurrence rates were similar between approaches (RR: 1.00; 95 % CI: 0.64–1.57; I2 = 0 %; p = 0.88), thus corroborating earlier studies reporting no significant difference in recurrence between open and laparoscopic repairs.32, 33, 34 Notably, some studies have observed a slightly higher recurrence rate in open groups; for example, Asencio et al. reported 23.59 versus 18.94 recurrences per 1000 person-years in open versus laparoscopic repairs, respectively.21 These variations might be explained by differences in surgical techniques, mesh types, and patient selection criteria.32

Adhesion formation, a significant concern in hernia repairs, was less prevalent in laparoscopic than open procedures due to reduced tissue handling and exposure, as reported by several previous studies.35,36 However, the intraperitoneal placement of mesh in laparoscopic repairs might still pose a risk of adhesions and potentially lead to complications such as bowel obstruction.35 Therefore, although laparoscopic techniques might avoid certain adhesion-related complications, careful consideration of mesh placement and material is essential.

Quality-of-life outcomes

Quality-of-life assessments, particularly using the SF-36 questionnaire, provided insights into patient-centered outcomes. In a study by Eker et al., both groups experienced improvements in physical functioning at 3 months postoperatively and a subsequent decline at 5 years.21 However, the laparoscopic group consistently reported more favorable scores in role limitations due to physical health, emotional well-being, and general health perceptions over time. These findings suggest that laparoscopic repair might offer sustained quality-of-life benefits, potentially because of diminished postoperative pain and accelerated recovery.37 These results were complemented by findings from Itani et al. indicating 13 % vs. 9 % improvements in physical functioning and 6 % vs. 4 % improvements in vitality in the open repair group vs. the laparoscopic group; however, other SF-36 domains were not reported.23

Real-world implications

The findings of the current meta-analysis have substantial implications for clinical practice. The shorter hospital stays and decreased requirement for surgical drains associated with laparoscopic vs. open repairs not only benefit patients through faster recovery but also have implications in healthcare resource utilization and cost-effectiveness.38, 39, 40 The analysis also highlighted the lower utilization of surgical drains in laparoscopic procedures, which might contribute to decreases in postoperative discomfort and infection risk.38 Furthermore, the potential for improved quality-of-life outcomes with laparoscopic techniques might influence patient satisfaction and long-term health status.37,41 However, the choice between open and laparoscopic repair must be individualized, considering patient-specific factors including hernia size, location, previous surgeries, and comorbidities.

Limitations and surgeons’ expertise

This meta-analysis has ‏several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the number of included randomized controlled trials was relatively small, and some studies had limited sample sizes that might have affected the generalizability and statistical power of the findings. Second, although the included studies were comparable in many respects, variations in surgical technique, surgeons' experience, mesh types, follow-up durations, and definitions of complications such as seroma or recurrence across studies might have introduced clinical heterogeneity. Surgeons' expertise is a key factor in the outcomes of hernia repairs. Studies have demonstrated that surgeons' experience and proficiency significantly influence complication rates, recurrence, and overall patient outcomes. Therefore, the variability in surgeons’ expertise across studies might have influenced the results of this meta-analysis. Third, although quality-of-life outcomes were assessed, reporting varied regarding the details of the tools used (e.g., SF-36), thus limiting our ability to perform pooled analyses on patient-reported outcomes.

Additionally, the inability to blind participants and surgeons in surgical trials might have introduced performance or detection bias. Finally, language restrictions and the exclusion of unpublished or non-peer-reviewed data might have contributed to publication bias. These factors highlight the need for larger, well-designed multicenter RCTs with standardized outcome measures to confirm and expand upon these findings.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this comprehensive analysis indicated that both open and laparoscopic techniques for incisional hernia repair were effective and achieved comparable rates of postoperative complications and hernia recurrence. However, laparoscopic repair provides advantages in terms of shorter hospital stays, less need for surgical drains, and potentially better long-term quality-of-life outcomes. Our findings support the consideration of laparoscopic repair as a favorable option for suitable patients, emphasizing the importance of individualized surgical planning regarding patient characteristics and surgeons’ expertise.
