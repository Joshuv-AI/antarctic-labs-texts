Continuous versus interrupted epineural repair in forearm nerve injuries
Authors: Tarik Elma, Bulent Ozdemir
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPeripheral nerve repair traditionally employs interrupted suture techniques, but continuous suturing may offer advantages in operative efficiency without compromising outcomes. We compared functional recovery and surgical parameters between continuous and interrupted epineural repair techniques in forearm nerve injuries.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed 78 patients with acute forearm nerve injuries (median, radial, ulnar) treated between 2017 and 2020. Patients underwent primary epineural repair using either continuous (n = 40, 51.3%) or interrupted (n = 38, 48.7%) suture techniques. All procedures utilized 7–0 or 8–0 nylon sutures under microscopic visualization. Functional recovery was assessed using Highet–Zachary classification at minimum 12-month follow-up. Primary endpoints included motor/sensory recovery and operative duration. Secondary endpoints: operative times (isolated neurorrhaphy and total) and subgroup analyses by nerve type, injury level, and concomitant vascular repair.ResultsDemographics were comparable between groups (mean age 30.4 ± 11.1 years, 89.7% male). Distribution included ulnar (46.2%), median (30.4%), and radial (23.1%) nerve injuries. Mean follow-up was 37.8 ± 18.1 months. Continuous suture technique significantly reduced operative time compared to interrupted technique (59.3 ± 9.8 vs 96.1 ± 12.3 min, p < 0.001). Functional outcomes showed no significant differences between groups, with successful nerve recovery (≥ M3 motor, ≥ S3 sensory) achieved in 94.9% of continuous group and 94.7% of interrupted group patients. Subgroup analyses confirmed similar results across nerve types, injury levels, and in the presence/absence of vascular repair.ConclusionContinuous epineural suture technique achieves equivalent functional outcomes to conventional interrupted repair while significantly reducing operative time by approximately 38%. This approach may offer practical advantages in peripheral nerve reconstruction without compromising neurological recovery, potentially reducing anesthesia exposure and healthcare costs.

Introduction

The nerves in the forearm are vulnerable structures composed of many different cellular components and contain intraneural blood vessels. Nerve injuries can occur in a variety of circumstances, including nerve compression, such as carpal tunnel syndrome, or after severe crushes and cuts [1].

A digital nerve injury in only one finger may cause severe loss of function in the hand. In particular, injuries of major nerves in the forearm, such as the radial, median or ulnar nerves, may result in functional loss of the hand, adversely affecting the patient's professional and social life. The inability of patients to work after these injuries leads to substantial economic losses, as the majority of these patients are young, working-age people [1]. Proper and sufficient treatment of nerve injuries is crucial to prevent loss of function after nerve injuries and to achieve a satisfactory clinical outcome.

In acute nerve lacerations, primary nerve repair should be performed as soon as possible. With early surgery (within 2 to 3 days after the injury), repair and alignment of the nerve becomes easier [2]. The biological advantages of early repair also include increased neuronal survival [3] and reduced fibrosis in the distal stump [4]. After proper debridement of the nerve endings following injury, neurorrhaphy can be performed using sutures, fibrin glue or nerve tubes—given that it is possible to apply a tension-free end-to-end approach. If short gaps occur between the nerve endings after debridement, commercially available conduits may be used, but patients with greater gaps may require allografts or autografts. Regardless of the technique utilized, the main strategy for nerve repair should involve the proper alignment of nerve endings [5].

Suture nerve repair, which is the classic technique of nerve repair, remains the most commonly used technique to reattach disconnected nerve endings [6]. Minimizing the size, depth and number of sutures reduces iatrogenic nerve damage. The nerve endings should be properly aligned so that the fascicular groups "kiss" each other and do not protrude laterally. Placing these sutures in the external epineurium to indirectly align the fascicles is less traumatic [6]. The widely used interrupted suture technique can be applied to ensure proper alignment, but the continuous suture technique can also achieve alignment. Other techniques, such as interrupted fascicular repair and group fascicular repair have also been described; however, these have not been clearly shown to be superior [7]. Despite the widespread use of interrupted sutures in nerve repair, this technique can be time-consuming, particularly in complex cases with multiple nerve injuries or when concomitant vascular repair is required. The continuous suture technique, while theoretically offering advantages in terms of operative efficiency and potentially equivalent tensile strength, has received limited investigation in human nerve repair. Only one animal study by Saied et al. has compared these techniques, demonstrating equivalent regeneration outcomes with reduced operative time in sciatic nerve repair in dogs [8]. To date, no clinical study has comprehensively evaluated continuous versus interrupted epineural repair in major forearm nerves with detailed subgroup analyses, standardized functional outcome measures, and operative time stratification based on concomitant vascular procedures.

The aim of this study was to compare the continuous and interrupted suture techniques, the latter of which is the most commonly used technique in epineural nerve repairs. We hypothesized that continuous epineural suture technique would provide equivalent functional outcomes to interrupted repair while significantly reducing operative time, and that these effects would remain consistent across different nerve types, injury levels, and clinical scenarios.

Methods

Case selection

The study included patients who experienced forearm injuries in Medline Adana Hospital between 2017 and 2020. Patients who could not be followed up for 12 months, those injured more than 24 h prior to admission, had severe crush injury or tendon injury, subjects with concomitant hand or forearm fractures, and individuals who required nerve grafts after debridement were excluded. Fibrin glue or nerve tubes were not used in any patient who underwent nerve repair. After excluding patients who did not meet the inclusion criteria, the remaining 78 patients were included in the study. Ethics committee approval was obtained from the Private Medline Hospital Ethics Committee (Approval no: 07, date: December 7, 2020). As this was a retrospective analysis, the ethics committee approval focused on data collection and analysis of existing patient records rather than approval for technique implementation. The continuous suture technique had been selectively used in clinical practice based on surgical judgment, particularly in cases requiring rapid repair due to concomitant arterial injury. The ethics committee recognized that both continuous and interrupted suturing represent established variations of epineural repair, with technique selection based on clinical circumstances and surgeon preference.

Procedure

Regional anesthesia by axillary block or local intravenous anesthesia was applied to all patients who underwent nerve repair. The choice of suture technique was based on clinical factors present at the time of surgery. Continuous suture technique was employed in cases with concomitant arterial injury to minimize ischemia time and overall surgical duration. Interrupted suture technique was used in cases without arterial injury or when the surgeon deemed it most appropriate based on wound characteristics. The severed nerves were identified, appropriate debridement was performed, and epineural repair was carried out, with the interrupted suture technique under microscope in one group, while the continuous suture technique was employed in the other group. Depending on nerve diameters, 7–0 or 8–0 nylon sutures with circular needles were used (Figs. 1, 2, 3, 4). Splints were used for two weeks post-operatively. Long arm splints were used in patients with incisions in the proximal third of the forearm, and short arm splints were used in the others. Interrupted suture technique

In the interrupted epineural repair technique, 6–8 individual sutures were placed approximately 0.8–1.2 mm apart and 1–1.5 mm from the cut edge of the epineurium. Each suture was tied separately, and careful attention was paid to ensure correct fascicular alignment. Epineural blood vessels and fascicular orientation were used as landmarks when necessary. Each stitch was placed using microsurgical technique under 10–16 × magnification. The needle entered the epineurium at a 90-degree angle, and the bite depth was limited to the superficial epineurium to avoid fascicular injury. After all sutures were placed, they were tied sequentially using a microsurgical knot-tying technique with 3–4 throws per knot. Epineural blood vessel patterns and fascicular orientation landmarks were used to ensure proper rotational alignment.

Continuous suture technique

In the continuous technique, the nerve ends were first prepared by removing devitalized tissue, and two stay sutures were placed at 0° and 180° positions for orientation. The long end of one stay suture was then used to initiate a circumferential continuous epineural suture line. Each “bite” was placed 1–1.5 mm from the nerve edge, capturing only the superficial epineurium. Bite spacing was maintained at approximately 0.8–1.2 mm. For nerves with an average diameter of 3–4 mm, 8–12 bites were typically required to complete the circumference. The suture line was then tied to the opposite stay suture, and the knot was buried extraneurally. Fascicular alignment was constantly verified based on epineural vascular markings and fascicular orientation. If small residual gaps remained, 1–2 additional interrupted sutures were added to reinforce the repair. All injuries were caused by sharp trauma (glass punch injuries or knife/stabbing wounds). Injury classification followed established criteria for traumatic peripheral nerve injuries as described by Lavorato et al. [9]. Patients with crush injuries, significant comorbidities, or non-dominant hand preference were recorded. Type of injury, age, sex, affected nerve, and surgical duration were recorded for both groups.

All times were recorded by dedicated operating room nursing staff using standardized protocols. In cases with concomitant arterial repair, both isolated neurorrhaphy time and total operative time were documented separately to allow for stratified analysis.

Postoperative rehabilitation and follow-up

Postoperative rehabilitation followed a standardized protocol. Patients were immobilized for two weeks using long arm splints for proximal forearm injuries and short arm splints for distal injuries. Passive finger exercises were initiated on postoperative day 3, performed by the uninjured hand with the affected hand maintained in splint, consisting of gentle passive finger flexion 30 repetitions three times daily. At one-week, passive exercises were increased to 90 repetitions three times daily. After splint removal at two weeks, active finger flexion was initiated with the wrist in neutral position under physiotherapist supervision. Wrist extension exercises were delayed until three weeks postoperatively to protect the repair.

After surgery, patients were called for follow-up at 2 weeks, 1 month, 6 months and 12 months. Nerve recovery was evaluated using the Highet-Zachary classification, which is based on motor and sensory recovery. Success was defined as having a result of M3 or better for motor recovery (movement only against gravity) and a result of S3 or better for sensory recovery (pain and tactile perception with two-point discrimination greater than 15 mm).

All assessments were performed by the same experienced surgeon at each follow-up visit to ensure consistency and minimize inter-observer variability. In this scoring system, these limits are considered the minimal criteria for a significant recovery [10].

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to describe continuous variables (mean, standard deviation, median, minimum, maximum). The Shapiro–Wilk test was used to determine whether continuous variables were normally distributed. The Mann–Whitney U test was used to determine whether there was a difference between two independent groups that were non-normally distributed, while the Student's t-test was used to determine whether there was a difference between two independent groups that were normally distributed. The relationship between categorical variables was analyzed by Chi-Square test (or the Fisher's exact test where applicable). The significance level was set at 0.05. Analyses were performed using MedCalc® Statistical Software version 19.7.2 (MedCalc Software Ltd, Ostend, Belgium; https://www.medcalc.org; 2021). The primary endpoint was functional recovery defined as achieving ≥ M3 motor and ≥ S3 sensory recovery according to Highet-Zachary classification. Secondary endpoints included operative time and complication rates. Effect sizes are reported with 95% confidence intervals where appropriate.

Median, radial and ulnar nerve injuries were detected in 30.4%, 23.1%, and 46.2% of the cases, respectively. The continuous suture technique was used in 51.3% and the interrupted suture technique was used in 48.7% of the cases. Functional outcomes

No patients achieved only M3 recovery (movement against gravity). Two patients (2.6%) failed to reach M3 recovery, one in each group (continuous: 1/40, 2.5%; interrupted: 1/38, 2.6%). Functional outcomes by nerve and level

Sensory recovery demonstrated similar patterns between groups. S4 recovery (< 7 mm two-point discrimination) was achieved in 41 patients (52.6%), with nearly identical distribution between continuous (21/40, 52.5%) and interrupted (20/38, 52.6%) groups. S3 + recovery (7-15 mm) was observed in 25 patients (32.1%): 13/40 (32.5%) continuous and 12/38 (31.6%) interrupted. S3 recovery (> 15 mm) occurred in 10 patients (12.8%): 5/40 (12.5%) continuous and 5/38 (13.2%) interrupted. Two patients (2.6%) failed to achieve S3 recovery, one from each group (continuous: 1/40, 2.5%; interrupted: 1/38, 2.6%). The distribution of sensory recovery grades showed no significant difference between techniques (p = 0.998, Chi-square test).

Subgroup analysis by nerve type, injury level (proximal vs. distal), revealed comparable functional outcomes across all categories, with ≥ M3 motor recovery rates consistently approaching 100% and ≥ S3 sensory recovery exceeding 90%.

Complications

Four patients (5.1%) required secondary procedures. Two patients (2.6%) who failed to meet the ≥ M3/S3 recovery criteria underwent reoperation, where neuroma formation was identified and treated with nerve grafting, resulting in improved function. Six patients (7.7%) developed adhesions requiring tenolysis procedures. No patients experienced surgical site infections, wound healing complications, or other perioperative adverse events. The rate of complications was similar between continuous (n = 4, 10%) and interrupted (n = 4, 10.5%) groups (p = 1.000).

Discussion

Among the nerve repair methods, end-to-end nerve repair with sutures is still the most commonly used technique in patients who do not have a considerable gap after debridement [6]. In this study, we compared the interrupted suture technique, which is the most commonly used epineural nerve repair technique, with the continuous suture technique, and found that there was no difference between the two techniques in terms of functional outcome, but continuous suture significantly shortened the surgical duration. Our findings demonstrated that while there was no significant difference between the two approaches in terms of functional outcomes, the continuous technique significantly reduced surgical time.

There is no study in the literature that performed nerve repair with continuous suture technique. The allocation of patients to continuous versus interrupted repair was not randomized but was based on clinical factors, particularly the presence of concomitant arterial injury. This represents a limitation of our study design, as the decision was influenced by the surgeon's assessment of the need to minimize operative time in cases with vascular compromise.

Although previous studies compared the clinical outcomes of continuous or interrupted suture techniques in the repair of different tissues, as far as we have reviewed, the results of the use of these two suture techniques in nerve repair in humans have not been analyzed. Only one animal study has published relevant results on nerve repair. Sied et al. evaluated the results of continuous and interrupted suture techniques in sciatic nerve repair in dogs and found no significant differences between the groups in terms of light microscopy analysis and regeneration, whereas the surgery duration was significantly shorter in the continuous group [8]. Consistent with these studies, we found no significant differences between the two suture techniques in terms of clinical outcomes, and continuous suture was found to be associated with a significant decrease in surgical duration. The limited research on continuous nerve suturing highlights the need for further investigation to establish its broader clinical applicability and safety profile.

Various techniques have been introduced in nerve repair in recent years. One of these techniques is nerve repair with fibrin glue. The aim of this technique is to prevent needle-related trauma to the nerve endings and to prevent scar formation [11]. The disadvantage of this technique is the low holding strength against mechanical tension [12]. This may cause the nerves to detach again under tension.

Use of conduits is another technique for nerve repairs. In this technique, the nerve endings are placed in a tube in a way that they do not touch each other. The purpose of this technique is to protect the nerve endings from trauma and scar tissue [13]. Lundborg et al. have reported similar recovery with the use of conduits compared to primary repair, even in major nerves such as the median nerve [14]. Ease of use and effectiveness are the main clinical advantages of this technique. On the other hand, the most notable advantages of the continuous suture technique are its ease of application and shortened surgical time compared to the interrupted suture technique.

A key finding of our study is that the continuous technique shortened not only total operative time but also the isolated nerve repair time. This can be attributed to the inherent features of the method: time-consuming steps such as repeated needle changes and multiple knot-tying are eliminated, and the more homogeneous approximation of epineurial edges reduces the need for repositioning.

An interesting observation was that, in some cases, the total operative time was unexpectedly shorter in patients who underwent combined nerve and arterial repair compared with those receiving isolated nerve repair. This paradox can be explained by several technical factors. In isolated nerve repair, the operative duration was often prolonged due to challenges such as extensive proximal dissection, mobilization of retracted nerve ends, and meticulous fascicular alignment. Conversely, when arterial repair was required, surgeons were under greater time pressure due to ischemia risk and therefore aimed to complete neurorrhaphy more quickly and efficiently. The combined effect of these factors likely explains the paradoxical observation of shorter total operative times in some combined repair cases.

Especially in major nerve repairs, if the gap is too large, conduits fail and it may be necessary to use nerve grafts [15]. In this case, allografts or autografts can be used. In a study on major nerve repair, allografts were used in patients with nerve defects between 5 and 50 mm and a success rate of 86% was achieved [16]. Motor or sensory autografts in particular can be used in major nerve defects [17]. None of the patients in our study developed a gap. However, the continuous suturing technique can accelerate autograft application and shorten surgery in patients who have gaps and require grafts.

Our study has some limitations. First, the retrospective design of our study prevented the analysis of other parameters that may have an effect on the results. Only patients eligible for primary repair after debridement were included in the study. Repairs requiring grafts were not included in the comparison. The use of fibrin glue or conduits after nerve repairs were not evaluated. Moreover, the study focused exclusively on large-diameter forearm nerves; the applicability of continuous suturing in smaller digital nerves remains to be explored. Future research should address these limitations through prospective, randomized controlled trials and should include histological evaluation, long-term functional outcomes, and cost-effectiveness analyses.

Conclusion

This retrospective cohort study demonstrates that continuous epineural suturing provides clinical outcomes comparable to those of the traditional interrupted technique while significantly reducing operative time. However, due to the non-randomized allocation and potential confounders, causality cannot be established. Future randomized controlled trials are required to validate these findings.
