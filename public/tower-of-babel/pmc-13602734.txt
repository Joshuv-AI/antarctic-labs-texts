Balancing topographical guidance and spatial capacity: Optimized spatial density of micropatterned filaments for enhanced peripheral nerve regeneration
Authors: Xiushuai Shang, You Wu, Yifan Li, Jiafeng Li, Shuai Jiang, Miaoda Shen, Jingyao Chen, Deteng Zhang, Shude Yang, Sanzhong Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNerve guidance conduits (NGCs) require intraluminal fillers to bridge critical-sized peripheral nerve defects, yet balancing topographical guidance with adequate regenerative space remains a fundamental “spatial paradox”. Herein, a hierarchical composite nerve support conduit is engineered, comprising a micropatterned poly(L-lactide-co-caprolactone) (PLCL) outer membrane and double-sided micropatterned intraluminal filaments. To decode spatial design rules, the intraluminal volumetric filling ratio (0% to 50%) was systematically modulated in a 10-mm rat sciatic nerve transection model. Results demonstrate that an optimal 5%-10% filling ratio resolves the spatial paradox by maximizing anisotropic contact guidance while preserving crucial luminal capacity. This highly permissive 3D immunovascular microenvironment synergistically directed Schwann cell alignment, and facilitated robust endothelial vascularization, yielding functional recovery and remyelination comparable to autologous nerve grafts. Conversely, excessive dense fillings (>20%) physically obstructed tissue infiltration. Furthermore, comprehensive multi-level transcriptomic sequencing elucidated that these structural and spatial cues might be associated with focal adhesion and PI3K-Akt signaling pathways, highlighting Ccn1 as a key mechanosensitive gene driving extracellular matrix remodeling. This study defines the precise spatial thresholds in conduit design, establishing a paradigm for optimizing biomechanical microenvironments in neural tissue engineering.Keywords: Peripheral nerve regeneration, Nerve guidance conduit, Spatial paradox, Micropatterned topography, Transcriptomics, PLCL

Introduction

Peripheral nerve injury (PNI) represents a highly prevalent and debilitating clinical challenge, frequently leading to profound sensory and motor deficits, neuropathic pain, and severe deterioration of patients' quality of life [[1], [2], [3]]. For bridging critical-sized nerve defects, autologous nerve grafting remains the undeniable clinical “gold standard” [[4], [5], [6], [7]]. Consequently, tissue-engineered nerve guidance conduits (NGCs) have emerged as highly promising synthetic alternatives [1,8,9]. However, despite utilizing biocompatible polymers like poly(L-lactide-co-caprolactone) (PLCL), conventional hollow NGCs typically fail to bridge long-distance defects [10,11]. The absence of internal physical support within hollow conduits inevitably leads to the collapse of the initial fibrin cable [12,13], chaotic Schwann cell (SC) dispersion [4,14], and misdirected axonal outgrowth [15].

To overcome the inherent limitations of hollow conduits, recent engineering strategies have intensely focused on introducing intraluminal architectures such as aligned fibers, hydrogels, or micro-grooved substrates to mimic the anisotropic topography of the native nerve fascicle [[16], [17], [18], [19], [20], [21]]. However, the structural design of these intraluminal fillings is currently plagued by a fundamental yet largely overlooked “spatial paradox” in neural tissue engineering. On one hand, researchers strive to maximize the internal volumetric ratio of fillers to provide abundant topographical guidance signals for regenerating axons [22,23]. On the other hand, an over-densified intraluminal filling inevitably occupies the crucial regenerative lumen, thereby severely compromising the limited spatial capacity required for robust multicellular infiltration [24]. Specifically, overcrowded conduits restrict the essential mass transport of nutrients, impede the clearance of myelin debris by infiltrating macrophages, physically hinder the arborization of endothelial cells during angiogenesis, and ultimately form a mechanical barrier against axonal extension [3,25]. Conversely, under-filling fails to provide sufficient directional cues and mechanical support to bridge the gap [10,20]. Despite its critical importance to tissue repair, systematically defining this exact “Goldilocks zone”, which is the optimal spatial threshold between topographical guidance and regenerative lumen capacity remains an elusive challenge [26].

We propose that the ultimate success of artificial nerve repair fundamentally relies on constructing a meticulously balanced 3D regenerative microenvironment. This microenvironment must strike an optimal equilibrium among sufficient physical support, robust topographical signal guidance, and adequate spatial capacity. We hypothesize that defining this precise spatial threshold is an absolute prerequisite to orchestrating a highly permissive “immunovascular niche.” Within an optimally configured 3D space, sophisticated microscopic topographical cues can exert biophysical signals that modulate cellular mechanotransduction. Such precise physical stimulation can drive macrophages toward a pro-resolving and anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype, which is critical for remodeling the early immune microenvironment. Concurrently, these signals direct the robust longitudinal migration of SCs to form artificial “Bands of Büngner”. Crucially, the rationally preserved inter-filament luminal space must accommodate robust endothelial cell infiltration, permitting the rapid establishment of a vascular network that provides essential trophic support for the highly metabolically demanding process of remyelination. Orchestrating these interconnected multicellular behaviors requires a flawless spatial design within the conduit.

Guided by this biomechanical and spatial design philosophy, we engineered a hierarchical composite nerve guidance conduit comprising a structurally robust, micropatterned PLCL outer membrane internally filled with precisely designed, double-sided micropatterned PLCL filaments. To decode the spatial design rules and crack the “spatial paradox,” we systematically evaluated varying intraluminal filling ratios (ranging from 0% to 50% volumetric occupation) in a rigorous 10 mm rat sciatic nerve transection model. By comprehensively assessing gait functional recovery, electrophysiology, and histological myelination, we aimed to define the optimal spatial architecture that maximizes topographical guidance without impeding the regenerative niche.

Furthermore, to elucidate the “black box” of complex cell-material interactions at varying spatial densities, comprehensive transcriptomic sequencing was employed both in vitro (for SCs and macrophages) and in vivo (for regenerated nerves). Specifically, we sought to uncover the underlying molecular mechanisms, focusing on how defined biophysical topographies and spatial constraints might be associated with key pathways such as the PI3K-Akt signaling pathway and focal adhesion dynamics into cytoskeletal rearrangements. We further explored how these physical cues alter the expression profiles of key regenerative genes (e.g., Ccn1) to govern the overall repair process. By deciphering the molecular interplay between topological guidance and spatial capacity, this study not only presents a highly effective biophysical strategy for peripheral nerve repair but also establishes a crucial quantitative paradigm for the spatial optimization of complex tissue-engineered scaffolds.

Materials and methods

Materials

Poly(L-lactide-co-caprolactone) (PLCL), with formulations of LA:CL = 50:50 and LA:CL = 75:25, was obtained from Jinan Daigang Biotech Co., Ltd., China. 1,4-Dioxane, anhydrous ethanol, and tert-butanol were purchased from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., China. Polydimethylsiloxane was prepared using a Silicone Elastomer Kit, which was sourced from The Dow Chemical Company, USA. The CQAASIKVAV peptides were custom synthesized by Shanghai Top-Peptide Biotechnology Co., Ltd., China. Milli-Q water (Millipore, USA) was utilized for all experiments.

Manufacturing of composite nerve support conduits

The PDMS template was prepared according to the instructions provided with the kit. A mixture of silicone and a crosslinking agent was prepared in a mass ratio of 10:1 and maintained on ice under vacuum for 2 h to remove internal bubbles. The resulting mixture was then applied to the surface of a silicon chip that had been etched with microgrooves. The assembly was heated at 95 °C for 20 min to obtain a PDMS stamp with a micropattern surface structure. The preparation of PLCL was based on previous work. PLCL (50:50) and PLCL (75:25) were weighed at 0.6 g or 0.2 g in a 1:1 ratio and dissolved in 6 mL or 2 mL of 1,4-dioxane, respectively. The mixture was stirred at room temperature until completely dissolved and then poured into a rectangular polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) mold (2 cm × 5 cm). After solvent evaporation, the mold yielded a thicker, light-colored PLCL film and a thinner, translucent PLCL film. The 0.6 g PLCL film was then cut into pieces measuring 20 mm × 8 mm to serve as the supporting tubes for the nerve conduits. Using a “sandwich” method, the PLCL film was placed between two PDMS templates, heated at 180 °C for 5 min, and then removed after cooling, resulting in a micropattern imprinted on the surface of the PLCL film. After hot pressing with micro-patterning, all PLCL films were treated on both sides using a vacuum plasma system (Beijing OBR Scientific Instrument Co., Ltd.) to facilitate the adsorption of CQAASIKVAV peptides. Specifically, the oxygen plasma treatment was conducted at a power of 50 W for 5 min. The CQAASIKVAV peptide was prepared at a concentration of 1 mg/mL, and the membranes were incubated in this solution at 4 °C for 12 h. Additionally, 0.2 g of the PLCL film was cut into strands measuring 20 mm × 0.5 mm, which were then filled into the nerve conduits according to a specified volume ratio. Prior to in vitro cell experiments and in vivo implantation, both the PLCL films and the nerve conduits underwent sterilization using ultraviolet light and ozone treatment, followed by rinsing with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, Solarbio, China) or saline solution.

Characteristics of the nerve support conduits

To analyze the morphology of the conduits, the samples were fixed onto the sample stage using conductive adhesive tape and subsequently placed into the sample chamber. Real-time observations and recordings were conducted using a scanning electron microscope (Carl Zeiss, Sigma 500, Germany) under vacuum conditions.

The hydrophilicity of the PLCL films was characterized using an optical contact angle measurement system. 2 μL droplet of ultra-pure water was placed on the film surface and allowed to stand for 5 min, after which the contact angle was measured and calculated. Mechanical properties of the different groups of PLCL films were evaluated using a universal materials testing machine. The strain rate was set at 0.1 N/min, and the tensile modulus was calculated within the range of 5% to 15% strain. The tensile performance of the nerve conduits was assessed based on the stress-strain curves obtained during testing.

In vitro biocompatibility of composite nerve support conduits

To evaluate the hemolytic potential of the nerve conduits, fresh whole blood was obtained from C57 mice via cardiac puncture and centrifuged at 1500 rpm for 15 min to remove the supernatant. The blood cells were resuspended in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and centrifuged three additional times for washing. A 4% red blood cell suspension was prepared using PBS. The PLCL films were cut into 5 mm × 5 mm pieces and incubated with the red blood cell suspension at 37 °C for 12 h. The supernatant was then collected and transferred to a 96-well plate, where the absorbance at 545 nm was measured using a multifunctional microplate reader (Molecular Devices, USA). Triton X-100 and PBS were used as positive and negative controls, respectively.

SCs were obtained from the Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai). To assess the cytocompatibility of the PLCL films, SCs were seeded onto the sterile PLCL films fixed at the bottom of a well plate. After 48 h of incubation, a live/dead staining kit (Solarbio) was utilized according to the manufacturer's instructions. Fluorescent images were captured using an inverted microscope (Nikon, Japan).

Cell proliferation levels were quantitatively evaluated using a CCK-8 assay kit (TargetMol Biotechnology Co., Ltd., China). SCs were cultured on the surface of the PLCL films, and the absorbance at 450 nm was measured after a 1.5-h incubation with the CCK-8 working solution at one day and three days post-seeding.

Cell extraction

Bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs) were isolated from the femurs of 6- to 8-week-old C57BL/6 mice (Jinan Pengyue Experimental Animal Breeding Co., Ltd., China). After cervical dislocation, the mice were sterilized, and the femurs were dissected and stripped of surrounding soft tissue. Both ends of the femur were cut open, and a 1 mL syringe was used to flush the bone marrow cavity with complete culture medium. Following centrifugation, the cells were resuspended in high-glucose complete cell culture medium containing 10 ng/mL of M-CSF (Thermo Fisher, USA) and then cultured on the surface of the PLCL films.

Cell morphology on the surface of nerve support conduits

The PLCL films were fixed in a 24-well plate and subjected to standard sterilization procedures. SCs or BMDMs were then seeded onto the PLCL films, and after 3 or 7 days post-seeding, the cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and washed with PBS. The samples were subsequently immersed in a series of graded ethanol/t-butanol solutions at volumetric ratios of 5%, 10%, 15%, 30%, 50%, 75%, and 100% for 10 min each. After freeze-drying, the surfaces were coated with gold for 3 min, and cell morphology was analyzed using scanning electron microscopy (SEM).

Transcriptome sequencing and analysis

PLCL films were fixed in a 6-well cell culture plate for sterilization, after which SCs and BMDMs were seeded and cultured for 3 and 7 days. Cells were lysed using Trizol, and RNA was collected. The samples were sent to BGI Beijing Genomics Institute for RNA sequencing analysis. RNA was extracted, purified, and reverse transcribed into cDNA, and high-throughput sequencing was performed using the BGISEQ-500 platform.

Following the manufacturer's guidelines, a library was constructed using mRNA-seq with combinatorial probe-anchor synthesis (cPAS). Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were analyzed using DEGseq. Genes were considered differentially expressed with a q-value < 0.05; fold changes (FC) greater than 2 were classified as upregulated DEGs, while fold changes less than 0.5 were classified as downregulated DEGs, corresponding to |log2FC| > 1. Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis of DEGs was conducted using the DAVID GO database. Additionally, KEGG (Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes) enrichment analysis was performed on DEGs. Visualization of the DEGs enrichment analysis was conducted using the Microbial Bioinformatics website (https://www.bioinformatics.com.cn/) and the BGI Omics System (https://biosys.bgi.com).

Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats sciatic nerve repair

This study was conducted in accordance with the Animal Research Reporting In Vivo Experiments (ARRIVE) guidelines. All experimental procedures and animal care were approved by the Animal Protection and Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Zhejiang University School of Medicine (Approval No.2025-259). A total of 63 adult male SD rats (7-8 weeks old, weighing 220-250 g) were used, classified as SPF grade for experimental animals. The rats were randomly divided into seven groups and anesthetized with an intraperitoneal injection of sodium pentobarbital at a dosage of 40 mg/kg. Their dorsal fur was disinfected and removed, and the right sciatic nerve was isolated and exposed at the femoral location. A 5 mm segment of the sciatic nerve was excised, and in the autologous nerve graft group, the severed nerve segment was rotated 180° and sutured in place using 8-0 sutures. To ensure the geometric consistency essential for our spatial paradox hypothesis, we utilized a “flat-to-tubular” assembly technique. Specifically, the outer sheath of the nerve conduit was initially prepared as a flattened, single-layer PLCL rectangular film. The internal filling filaments were then evenly distributed and aligned parallel to the longitudinal axis on this planar film. Subsequently, the film was carefully wrapped around the filaments and sutured longitudinally to form the final tubular conduit. This assembly approach effectively locks the filaments in their parallel orientation and ensures a uniform spatial distribution within the lumen, successfully preventing them from aggregating on one side. During the nerve anastomosis, the proximal and distal nerve stumps were carefully inserted into the respective ends of the nerve conduit. Subsequently, epineurial suturing was performed to secure the conduit to the nerve stumps using 8-0 non-absorbable sutures (e.g., nylon) via an interrupted suturing technique under a surgical microscope. Postoperatively, the rats were housed in their original environment under a 12-h light-dark cycle with ad libitum access to food and water.

Gait analysis

Functional evaluations were conducted on the rats in each group at 12 weeks post-surgery. The right hind limb of each rat was stained with red ink, while the left hind limb was stained with black ink. Subsequently, the rats were allowed to freely navigate within an enclosure containing a piece of blank paper fixed to the bottom. A fresh sheet of white paper was used for each individual rat. At the conclusion of the experiment, the used white papers were collected for photographic documentation.

Electrophysiological analysis

After anesthetizing the rats, the sciatic nerve on the injured side was re-exposed. A monopolar recording electrode and a bipolar stimulation electrode system were utilized for the induction and collection of electrical signals. Short-duration electrical pulse parameters (amplitude: 20 mA, frequency: 1.0 Hz, pulse width: 0.2 ms) were applied to electrically stimulate the proximal and distal stumps of the regenerating nerve. The recording electrode was positioned on the belly of the gastrocnemius muscle, and a digital MYTO electromyograph was employed to measure the nerve conduction velocity (NCV).

Muscle wet weight ratio and histological assessment

At 12 weeks post-surgery, the gastrocnemius muscles from both sides of the rats were collected, immediately weighed, and recorded. The muscle tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for three days, followed by paraffin embedding and sectioning. Subsequently, the sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and Masson's trichrome stain for histological analysis.

Histological assessment of axonization and myelination

At the surgical site, a 2 cm segment of the regenerated nerve was dissected. Randomly selected samples from each group were immediately immersed in 4% paraformaldehyde, followed by paraffin embedding and sectioning for both transverse and longitudinal cuts. The sections were stained using hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining kits (Solarbio) and Nissl stain (Sevag). Additionally, immunohistochemical analysis was performed using GFAP (Abcam, ab7260) and CD31 (Abcam, ab281583). For immunofluorescence staining of tissue sections, primary antibodies included NF200 (Abcam, ab207176), S100β (Abcam, ab52642), and CD34 (Abcam, ab316277). Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (Abcam,1:400) for 1 h. The remaining samples were fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde (Sinopharm) and subsequently treated with 1% osmium tetroxide, followed by dehydration in ethanol and embedding in epoxy resin. Ultrathin sections were prepared using an ultramicrotome and observed using a transmission electron microscope (TEM, Hitachi, Japan) for imaging. Additionally, toluidine blue staining (Wuhan Google Biotechnology Co., Ltd.) was performed.

In vivo regenerated nerve sequencing and analysis

At 12 weeks post-surgery, regenerated nerve samples were collected and all conduit materials were removed before rapid freezing in liquid nitrogen. Following the same protocol, the samples were sent to BGI Beijing Genomics Institute for RNA sequencing. Transcriptomic analysis was conducted on the 0% filling group and the 5% filling group.

Statistical analysis

Each experiment was performed with three or more independent replicates, and each group consisted of at least four parallel samples to ensure the reliability of the findings. Animal experiments included a total of n = 15 rats per group, with three rats designated for RNA sequencing. All data were statistically analyzed using GraphPad PRISM 9.0 software (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA, USA), and results are presented as mean ± standard deviation (Mean ± SD). Comparisons among the three groups were conducted using One-Way ANOVA test followed by Turkey's post-hoc method. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant, with P < 0.05 denoted by *, P < 0.01 denoted by **, and P < 0.001 denoted by ***.

Results and discussion

Preparation of nerve support conduit and potential for biological application

Fig. 1 illustrates the exploration of the nerve support conduit concept with the overall line of inquiry. After implantation, the nerve guide catheter connects the proximal and distal ends of the defect area as a scaffold for the whole nerve. Its internal filled filaments provide support and simulate the nerve bundle before injury, which provides a strong guidance effect for regenerating axons. Previous studies have demonstrated that the 20/40 μm beam/groove microgroove structure can effectively promote the adhesion, proliferation and migration of SCs [27]. Various filling or channels in the nerve conduit can significantly promote the repair of peripheral nerve transection injury [[28], [29], [30]]. More attention should be paid to the proportion of the filling content inside the catheter, which will have the maximal guidance and supporting effect on the regenerated nerve after injury.

Schematic illustration of the nerve guidance conduits for peripheral nerve regeneration.

In this study, nerve conduits with anisotropic microgrooves on the surface and simultaneously filled with double-sided micropattern filaments internally were fabricated (Fig. 2A). In the key experiment, as in previous studies, the “sandwich” method was used, and the microgroove structure was copied from the chip onto the surface of biodegradable polyester PLCL membrane by PDMS template (Fig. S1) [31]. On the surface of PLCL film, the width of the raised column is 20 μm, and the width of the groove column is 40 μm. The height difference between the raised column and the groove column is 10 μm from the perspective of the section. There are microgroove structures on both sides, and the thickness of the filling guide filament is about 200 μm (Fig. 2B). SEM images also showed a significant correlation between the water contact Angle and the surface topology except for the smooth and flat surface of the outer membrane (Fig. 2C). To increase the cellular affinity of the hydrophobic PLCL membrane, plasma sputter was used to increase the surface activity and adsorb the derived peptide CQAASIKVAV from laminin, referring to a previous study [32,33]. In contrast, the contact angles of the planar PLCL membrane and the microgroove PLCL membrane in the parallel and perpendicular microgroove directions were measured, respectively. In the parallel direction, the droplets extended obviously, and the contact Angle decreased from 80.63 ± 3.27° to 44.62 ± 3.86°. Due to capillary action in the parallel anisotropic microgroove direction, hydrophilicity is preferentially increased. As a scaffold to support peripheral nerve regeneration, peripheral nerve should have appropriate mechanical properties, avoid entrapment at the place of connection with normal tissue, be suture-able during operation, and avoid collapse inside to withstand the characteristics of mobility during nerve regeneration [11,34]. The PLCL planar film had a similar stress-strain curve as the film with the surface microstructure (Fig. 2D). The Young's modulus of Flat surface film and Micropattern surface film were 27.38 KPa and 29.69 KPa, respectively. After internal filling with different volume ratios, the mechanical properties of the composite nerve support catheter are improved [35,36].

The Nerve Support conduit has the potential of application after its biocompatibility is first verified [37]. As shown in the figure, hemolysis experiments using the red blood cell suspension of SD rats showed no significant difference in the rate of membrane hemolysis among the PBS, flat, and micropattern groups, while almost all the red blood cells in the Triton X-100 group were hemolytic (Fig. 2E). The micropattern PLCL films had good blood compatibility. The cytocompatibility was further evaluated using SCs. After 3 days of seeding on the micropattern PLCL, the SCs still had good activity, uniform cell distribution, and normal intercellular contact, as shown by the live and dead staining images (Fig. 2F). The number of dead cells marked by red signal was low. Meanwhile, the distribution of SCs on the micropatterned PLCL films exhibited a characteristic parallel groove arrangement, with a notable increase in the distribution of green signals within the 40 μm grooves. Additionally, to investigate the potential distribution patterns of neurons on the PLCL film, SH-SY5Y cells were seeded at varying densities on the micropatterned PLCL film. The results indicated that the neurite extensions displayed a bidirectional trend along the grooves, all presenting a spindle-like morphology (Fig. S2). Furthermore, CCK-8 assays were conducted to assess the proliferation levels of cells cultured on the PLCL surface (Fig. 2G). The results demonstrated that cells maintained normal proliferation at both the first day and the third day, indicating that the PLCL membranes used in this study possess good cell compatibility and show promise for biological applications.

Micropattern regulation of cytoskeletal arrangement

Observations using scanning electron microscopy (SEM) revealed that SCs in the flat PLCL group exhibited a random distribution and orientation on the membrane surface [26,38]. In contrast, the SCs in the Micropattern PLCL group predominantly displayed a parallel arrangement corresponding to the micropatterns, with a tendency to extend within the grooves or along the ridge walls (Fig. 3A). We defined the ratio of the long axis to the short axis of the cells as the aspect ratio (the length/width ratio, y/x, Fig. 3D). Analysis indicated that the aspect ratio of SCs on the grooved micropatterned surface significantly increased, particularly along the direction of the parallel grooves (Fig. 3B). Additionally, we measured the angle between the cell long axis and the micropattern (defined as the orientation angle α, Fig. 3D) [39]. The orientation angles of the SCs on the flat membrane surface were distributed in various directions; however, due to the structure of the microgrooves, the orientation angles in the micropattern PLCL group were significantly reduced (Fig. 3C).

Further, SCs were extracted from flat films and micropattern films cultured for 3 days, and RNA sequencing analysis was performed to determine the underlying mechanism of cytoskeleton arrangement regulated by the 20/40 μm raised/groove micropattern. The results from the volcano plot indicated that the micropattern films significantly altered the molecular phenotype of SCs (Fig. 3E), revealing a total of 219 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) that were significantly upregulated and 152 DEGs that were significantly downregulated. Enrichment analysis of DEGs was performed by Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway annotation. The results showed that the differential gene pathways were mainly related to translation in Genetic Information Processing and Cell growth and death and Cell motility in Cellular Processing. Gene ontology (GO) enrichment analysis identified cytosolic ribosome and structural constituent of ribosome as key Cellular components [40].

Additionally, primary BMDMs were cultured on flat films and micropattern films for seven days. Notably, based on scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images, BMDMs predominantly exhibited two distinct morphologies: spherical (light yellow) and spindle or fusiform (pale orange) (Fig. 3H) [41]. Similarly, the longitudinal lengths and widths of 100 randomly selected BMDMs on the surface of the PLCL films were measured, and the aspect ratios were calculated (Fig. 3I). The results indicated a significant increase in the number of elongated cells. Macrophages are multifunctional immune cells that exist in various subtypes, including M0 (resting state), M1 (pro-inflammatory), and M2 (anti-inflammatory) [42]. Macrophages exhibit different geometric shapes when polarized in various directions; specifically, M2 macrophages tend to be elongated compared to M1 cells [43]. Consequently, the fusiform BMDMs and the total number of cells in nine SEM images were quantified, revealing a significant increase in the proportion of fusiform cells (Fig. 3K). Additionally, the orientation angles of 100 BMDMs were calculated, indicating that BMDMs on the surface of the micropattern films exhibited a similar orientation pattern to that of SCs (Fig. 3J). On the micropattern PLCL surface, the average angle of BMDMs relative to the grooves was found to be 24.18 ± 18.79° (mean ± SD).

Similarly, BMDMs cultured on PLCL films for 7 days were subjected to transcriptome sequencing analysis, resulting in the identification of 165 DEGs, of which 118 were significantly upregulated and 47 were significantly downregulated (Fig. 3L). Classification of the KEGG pathways revealed that the DEGs were primarily enriched in the PI3K-Akt signaling pathway and the MARK signaling pathway within the KEGG secondary category of “Environmental Information Processing,” as well as in “Cellular Processing,” including focal adhesion, signaling pathways regulating the pluripotency of stem cells, and adherens junctions. Additionally, significant enrichment was observed in axon Guidance and thermogenesis (Fig. 3M). Following the selection of upregulated DEGs, GO and KEGG enrichment analyses were performed, showing that the enriched pathways for upregulated genes were consistent with those identified for the aforementioned DEGs (Fig. 3N). Compared to Flat PLCL films, Micropattern PLCL films may positively regulate the cytoskeletal arrangement, cell adhesion, migration, and proliferation of BMDMs through the activation of signaling pathways such as focal adhesion, axon guidance, and the phosphoinositide 3-kinase/protein kinase B (PI3K/Akt) signaling pathway [[44], [45], [46]]. In particular, the PI3K-Akt signaling pathway is a crucial pathway associated with survival mechanisms, regulating signal transduction, differentiation, and axon growth [40]. However, the lack of inhibitor-based functional validation is a limitation.

Nerve support conduit facilitated functional recovery

The ability of Micropattern PLCL films to regulate the arrangement of various cell cytoskeletons demonstrates their potential as scaffolds for peripheral nerve tissue engineering [47]. To investigate this potential capability and determine the optimal filling ratio for the filling-type nerve conduits, a 10 mm complete transection model of the sciatic nerve was established using SD rats. In this model, the two-dimensional Micropattern PLCL films were rolled and filled internally with guiding fibers featuring dual-sided micropatterns, connecting the proximal and distal ends of the transected nerve. These constructs were then implanted into the rats for repair (Fig. S3A). Fig. 4A outlines the experimental design and timeline. Autologous nerve grafting was used as the control group, where the severed nerve was reversed and re-implanted into its original defect site, while the remaining experimental groups utilized nerve support conduits (Fig. S3B). To explore the optimal filling ratio, the total volume of the nerve conduit was calculated to be 0.05 cm3, with the volume of the internal filling fibers set at 0.001 cm3 (Fig. 4B). The filling ratios for the nerve support conduits were established at 0% (no filling), 5% (filling 3 fibers), 10% (filling 6 fibers), 20% (filling 12 fibers), 30% (filling 18 fibers), and 50% (filling 30 fibers).

During the 12 weeks post-surgery, no deaths were observed among the rats, and the surgical sites healed well without infection, with normal hair growth. Gait analysis was conducted on the experimental rats, where black paw prints represented the healthy hind limb (right) of one rat and red marks indicated the affected hind limb (left) (Fig. 4C). As shown in the figure, the healthy hind limbs were nearly identical across groups; however, when comparing the affected hind limbs, it was found that the Control group and the 5% group exhibited clear interdigital spaces and normal paw openings, while the other groups displayed signs of toe gnawing or weakness. To assess signal conduction, electrophysiological tests were performed, revealing that the regeneration nerve conduction velocities (NCV) in the 5% and 10% groups were significantly faster, exceeding those of the Control group by more than twice (Fig. 4G).

To assess the recovery status of the target muscles innervated by the sciatic nerve, bilateral gastrocnemius muscles were collected from the rats 12 weeks post-implantation. As illustrated, varying degrees of atrophy were observed in the affected side (right) compared to the healthy side (left) (Fig. 4D). Notably, the 0%, 30%, and 50% groups exhibited severe atrophy. The wet weight ratio of the gastrocnemius muscle (affected side muscle mass/healthy side muscle mass) confirmed that the Control group and those receiving nerve support conduits with filling ratios between 0% and 10% experienced the least muscle atrophy (Fig. 4H). Similar results were corroborated by H&E staining and Masson's trichrome staining. The Control, 5%, and 10% groups displayed tightly arranged muscle fibers, whereas the other groups exhibited more loosely organized fiber bundles (Fig. 4E). Furthermore, an analysis of the H&E staining images allowed for the quantification of muscle fiber density and diameter within the same field of view, revealing that the 5% and 10% groups had lower muscle fiber density and longer diameters, indicating thicker muscle fibers and reduced atrophy (Fig. 4I and J). Additionally, significant collagen deposition and muscle atrophy were observed in the 0%, 20%, 30%, and 50% groups (Fig. 4F). Overall, the results indicate that the implantation of nerve support conduits with filling ratios of 0%-10% led to the most significant recovery of function in the target muscle, the gastrocnemius, innervated by the sciatic nerve. The success of the conduit relies on an optimal therapeutic window for the intraluminal filling ratio. A filling ratio that is too low fails to provide sufficient topographical cues for axonal guidance. Conversely, ratios exceeding 20% likely induce severe mechanical impedance, creating physical barriers that hinder cellular infiltration while simultaneously restricting the diffusion of essential nutrients and oxygen, ultimately leading to regeneration failure.

Nerve support conduit promotes axonal growth, myelination, and vascularization in vivo

To evaluate the direct supportive role of implanted Nerve Support Conduits in the repair of injured sciatic nerves, as well as the regenerative status within the conduits at varying filling ratios, the study utilized an outer conduit made from Micropatterned PLCL films. After the removal of the outer conduit, the filled fibers and regenerated nerves were subjected to histological staining, with tissue sections analyzed using both cross-sectional and longitudinal approaches (see schematic diagram S5A). As shown in the figures, H&E staining of the major organs of the rats and the regenerated nerves did not reveal any evidence of inflammation or immune response, indicating good tissue biocompatibility (Fig. S4). The nerve tissue exhibited concurrent growth with the inserted support fibers (Fig. 5A and S5C). The Control and 5% groups demonstrated a dense and uniform structure of the regenerated nerves, with larger cross-sectional areas, whereas the other groups exhibited loose and disorganized fibers, suggesting a finer nerve structure (Fig. S5B and D). Furthermore, Nissl staining of the nerve tissue sections across the groups revealed basophilic Nissl bodies, which serve as indicators for assessing neuronal damage [48,49]. The Control and 5% groups exhibited the highest quantity of Nissl bodies, characterized by their deep blue granules and vivid coloration, with the cell nuclei appearing light blue, indicating a lesser degree of damage compared to the other groups (Fig. 5B, S6).

Axonal myelination regeneration plays a crucial role in the functional recovery following peripheral nerve injuries [44]. To evaluate the role of the nerve support conduit in remyelination, this study conducted sectioning of the mid-regions of regenerated nerves and employed toluidine blue (TB) staining (Fig. 5C). The results indicated that the Control group, 5% group, and 10% group exhibited higher densities of axonal myelination, while the no-fill group and groups with over 20% filling showed a significant reduction in regenerated myelin, with a more dispersed distribution. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images revealed the layered structure of the regenerated myelin. A random sampling of 100 regenerated myelin sheaths from each group demonstrated that the 5% group had the best performance in terms of myelin thickness and diameter, while the Control and 10% groups showed similar thicknesses (Fig. 5D, G, and H). Additionally, the myelin sheaths in the Control, 5%, and 10% groups were nearly circular in shape, whereas the other groups displayed a greater number of irregularly shaped myelin sheaths. NF200 serves as an important biomarker for axonal regeneration and its integrity, while S100 reflects the activity and supportive status of Schwann cells during the regeneration process [50,51]. Immunofluorescence staining for S100 and NF200 in the mid-regions of regenerated nerves revealed a significant presence of nerve fibers (NF200, red fluorescence) enveloped by myelin (S100, green fluorescence) [52]. In the 0%, 30%, and 50% groups, only a limited number of sparse NF200 signals were observed, whereas the other groups exhibited a greater intensity of fluorescence with a more uniform distribution. There were no significant differences in S100 fluorescence intensity among the groups, with circular fluorescence forming interconnected patterns in layered arrangements (Fig. 5E and I). Moreover, similar results were obtained through immunohistochemical staining of GFAP (Fig. S7).

During the process of nerve regeneration, angiogenesis plays a crucial role, as a rich vascular network can enhance nutrient transport and gas exchange within the local regenerative environment [32,53,54]. Immunofluorescence and immunohistochemical analyses were performed using vascular formation-related markers CD34 and CD31. The images of CD34 showed significant differences among the groups, with the 5% group exhibiting a markedly higher vascular density compared to the other groups. When the volume of the supporting strands exceeded 10%, it appeared to hinder vascular regeneration, resulting in only sparse signal distribution (Fig. 5F–J, and S8A). The immunohistochemical images of CD31 and their statistical analysis demonstrated similar results (Fig. S8B and C).

Transcriptomic analysis of regenerated nerves in nerve support conduit in vivo

12 weeks post-implantation of the nerve support conduit, regenerated nerves were collected, and the conduit material was removed for transcriptomic analysis of the axonal samples (Fig. 6A) [55]. The findings from this in vivo study in rats suggest that excessive filling within the nerve conduit has a retardative impact on various aspects of tissue repair, particularly when the filling ratio exceeds 20% [22,56]. In contrast, the hollow nerve conduit (0%) showed poor myelination, axon regeneration, and vascularization, indicating the necessity of internal filling guidance. When the filling ratio was within the range of 5% to 10%, the experimental results were nearly comparable to those of the autologous nerve graft (Control) group. Furthermore, based on a comprehensive analysis of various characterization results, we hypothesize that the optimal filling ratio may be around 5%, while remaining greater than 0% and less than 10%. Consequently, we conducted RNA sequencing in the animal model, focusing on a comparative transcriptomic analysis between the 0% and 5% groups.

First, the number of DEGs detected at the tissue level was significantly greater than that at the cellular level. A total of 13,541 DEGs were identified, with 612 genes upregulated and 641 genes downregulated (Fig. 6B). Through KEGG enrichment analysis, we found that among the top 10 highly enriched pathways, the first four included the cytoskeleton in muscle cells, focal adhesion, the PI3K-Akt signaling pathway, and ECM-receptor interaction, along with axon guidance (Fig. 6C). These pathways are closely related to cell cytoskeleton arrangement, cell adhesion, and cell contact [57,58]. In the GO analysis, the top four pathways related to Biological Process (BP) included extracellular matrix organization, extracellular structure organization, external encapsulating structure organization, and cell-substrate adhesion (Fig. 6D). The pathways most relevant to Cellular Component (CC) included extracellular matrix and external encapsulating structure, which corresponded directly to the BP findings. The DEGs were significantly enriched in Molecular Function (MF) categories, specifically in cell adhesion molecule binding and actin binding. Finally, we constructed a Venn diagram of gene sets based on three groups of gene clusters identified in the transcriptomic analysis of this study, which included the transcriptomic sequencing results of SCs and BMDMs from flat and micropattern surface, as well as the transcriptomic results of regenerated nerves with 0% and 5% filling in vivo (Fig. 6E). The results from the three group comparisons indicate that the incorporation of micropattern significantly influences cell morphology, adhesion, and migration behavior [59]. Additionally, we constructed a Venn diagram of DEGs based on the genes identified in each group (Fig. 6F). In the intersection of the three groups, three notable DEGs were identified: Ccn1, Inhba, and Ndufa4I2. Ccn1, also known as Cyr61, is a secreted protein belonging to the CCN family of signaling proteins and is closely associated with the ECM. It can modulate various cellular activities, including adhesion, migration, proliferation, differentiation, apoptosis, and senescence, through interactions with integrin receptors on the cell surface. Previous studies have confirmed that Cyr61 promotes the proliferation and migration of neural stem cells via the αvβ3 integrin and regulates the expression of c-Jun [60,61]. Furthermore, Cyr61 secreted by Schwann cells (SCs) can regulate the proliferation and migration of SCs, as well as promote the growth of motor neurons, thereby supporting axonal regeneration and facilitating the repair of peripheral nerves (Fig. 6G) [62].

Following peripheral nerve injury, SCs begin to proliferate and migrate to the site of injury, where they remove axonal and myelin debris and form the Büngner bands. During the process of peripheral nerve regeneration, the Schwann cells at the injured nerve stump exhibit a biphasic elongation and spindle-like morphology, which facilitates their parallel alignment along the basal lamina, thereby promoting the formation of the Büngner bands [34,63,64]. The shape of the nerve guidance conduit base provides structural guidance and molecular cues that direct regenerating axons and myelin toward the target tissue, thereby facilitating nerve repair [35,58]. Additionally, the interaction between the hierarchical topological structure and stem cells can enhance their differentiation into neurons [64]. These collaborative interactions synergistically regulate the process of nerve regeneration within the nerve guidance conduit, achieving repair outcomes that approach or even exceed those of autologous nerve grafts.

However, it should be noted that the 12-week observation period represents an early stage of PLCL degradation. Although aligned structural guidance was well-maintained and no obvious inflammatory responses were observed during this timeframe, the continuous breakdown of the polymer will inevitably release acidic byproducts locally over prolonged periods. It is plausible that the long-term accumulation of these acidic products could dynamically influence macrophage polarization potentially shifting it toward a pro-inflammatory phenotype and affect the remodeling and long-term stability of the newly formed extracellular matrix. Consequently, the absence of long-term evaluations regarding these degradation dynamics, along with their subsequent biological and mechanical impacts on nerve regeneration, remains a limitation of the current study and warrants further investigation.

Conclusion

In summary, this study successfully resolves the critical “spatial paradox” in peripheral nerve repair by engineering a hierarchical composite nerve guidance conduit with mathematically and biologically optimized intraluminal architectures. By systematically modulating the volumetric filling ratio of double-sided micropatterned PLCL filaments in a 10 mm rat sciatic nerve transection model, we conclusively established that a filling ratio between 5% and 10% represents the optimal structural threshold. Within this precise spatial equilibrium, the conduit exerts “Maximal Guidance”, providing robust anisotropic topographical cues to direct cellular alignment without compromising the essential luminal capacity required for tissue infiltration.

Consequently, the optimally filled conduits engineered a highly permissive 3D immunovascular microenvironment. This balanced spatial architecture profoundly facilitated Schwann cell alignment, orchestrated BMDM polarization toward a pro-regenerative elongated phenotype, and enabled robust endothelial vascularization. Ultimately, the 5%-10% filling ratios yielded exceptional electrophysiological recovery, remyelination, and target muscle preservation strictly comparable to the gold-standard autologous nerve grafts. In stark contrast, excessive fillings (>20%) inherently induced severe steric hindrance, physically obstructing axonal extension and angiogenesis. Furthermore, multi-level transcriptomic analyses elucidated the “black box” of these structural effects, revealing that topological and spatial cues might be associated with focal adhesion and PI3K-Akt signaling pathways. Notably, the mechanosensitive gene Ccn1 emerged as a critical molecular driver, synergistically modulating ECM remodeling and cellular adhesion in response to the engineered 3D microenvironment. Ultimately, this work goes beyond the fabrication of an advanced biomaterial; it delineates the fundamental spatial design rules governing multicellular dynamics in confined geometries, establishing a translatable paradigm for the biomechanical optimization of next-generation tissue-engineered scaffolds. Despite these promising results, this study has certain limitations. First, the long-term in vivo safety, degradation profile, and late-stage host responses of the implanted conduit require further investigation. Second, current efficacy was demonstrated only in rodent models; future validations in large animal models (e.g., canines or non-human primates) are crucial to bridge the gap toward clinical translation for human critical-sized nerve defects. Finally, incorporating dynamic molecular tracking will be necessary to fully map the early in vivo immune responses.
