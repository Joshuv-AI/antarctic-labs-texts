Independent origins of plant parasitism in Clavicipitaceae: phylogeny, divergence, and taxonomic revision
Authors: Eiji Tanaka
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe family Clavicipitaceae (Hypocreales, Ascomycota) includes a wide range of fungal lifestyles and ecological interactions, but the phylogenetic relationships and evolutionary origins of its plant-parasitic taxa have remained controversial. To establish a robust, phylogenetically consistent classification, molecular phylogenetic, fossil-calibrated divergence-time, and likelihood-based ancestral state reconstruction analyses were performed using a multi-locus nuclear dataset. These analyses identified two deeply divergent, monophyletic clades of plant parasites: designated as Clavicipitoideae, comprising Aciculosporium, Balansia, Claviceps, and Epichloë; and Commelinaceomycetoideae, comprising Ustilaginoidea and Commelinaceomyces. Ancestral state reconstruction suggests that plant parasitism evolved independently at least twice within the family. In Clavicipitoideae, plant parasitism most likely originated directly from an invertebrate-associated ancestor, whereas in Commelinaceomycetoideae, it appears to have evolved through a transitional state involving root endophytism coupled with invertebrate parasitism. Divergence-time estimates place the origins of both lineages in the mid-to-late Cretaceous, concurrent with the early diversification of their monocot hosts. On the basis of these evolutionary inferences, major taxonomic revisions are proposed, including the establishment of a new subfamily Commelinaceomycetoideae, a new tribe Commelinaceomyceteae, an emended circumscription of the subfamily Clavicipitoideae, and the validations of the tribes Balansieae, Clavicipiteae, and Ustilaginoideae.Keywords: Clavicipitoideae, Commelinaceomycetoideae, molecular dating, host jump, grass parasite

1. Introduction

1.1 Historical classification of Clavicipitaceae

The family Clavicipitaceae (Hypocreales, Sordariomycetes, Ascomycota) is morphologically characterized by cylindrical asci with thickened apices and filiform ascospores that often disarticulate into part-spores at maturity. The taxonomic foundation of the family was laid by Lindau (1897), who established the subfamily Clavicipiteae [sic] within Hypocreaceae (Table 1). Since the original subfamily designation lacked the conventional “-oideae” suffix used for subfamilies, it must be corrected to Clavicipitoideae (ICN Art. 19.7). Lindau defined the subfamily on the basis of the presence of filiform ascospores in perithecia not necessarily sharply delimited within a hypocreaceous stroma, with Claviceps as the type genus. The family name Clavicipitaceae was first introduced by Earle (1901), although this name was invalidly published (ICN Art. 38.1, 41.1) and remained nomenclaturally illegitimate until its formal validation by Rogerson (1970) (Table 1).

Molecular phylogenetic analyses since the 1990s have fundamentally challenged traditional morphological classifications. Morphological characters, particularly those associated with sexual reproduction, have often proven unreliable indicators of phylogenetic affinity. Multiple studies have demonstrated the polyphyletic nature of several key genera traditionally assigned to Clavicipitaceae. The genus Cordyceps, which historically encompassed all clavicipitaceous entomopathogenic fungi, has been shown to be polyphyletic, necessitating taxonomic reorganization and the establishment of numerous new genera (Stensrud et al., 2005). Additionally, molecular analyses indicated that plant-parasitic taxa within Clavicipitaceae do not form monophyletic groups (Bischoff et al., 2004; Tanaka & Tanaka, 2008). On the basis of these phylogenetic results, what was once regarded as a single, morphologically diverse family has been redefined as three distinct families: Clavicipitaceae sensu stricto, Cordycipitaceae, and Ophiocordycipitaceae (Sung et al., 2007; Table 1).

1.2. Plant parasitism in Clavicipitaceae

Among the three families segregated from the Clavicipitaceae sensu Earle, only Clavicipitaceae s.s. includes lineages that parasitize living plants. While Bischoff and White (2003) listed 22 plant-parasitic genera in Clavicipitaceae sensu Earle, subsequent studies have demonstrated that several of these taxa are actually parasites of scale insects, and several were originally described without molecular phylogenetic support. Since then, additional species have been described, and several genera have been unified nomenclaturally. Moreover, species of the genus Metarhizium in Clavicipitaceae s.s., long regarded as insect-pathogenic fungi, are now known to colonize plant roots as endophytes (Sasan & Bidochka, 2012) that form symbiotic associations, enabling the transfer of insect-derived nutrients to plants (Behie et al., 2012). However, Metarhizium has not been reported to infect aerial tissues such as leaves or flowers. Furthermore, two genera (Shimizuomyces and Morakotia) in Clavicipitaceae s.s. are known to parasitize dicotyledonous seeds and form fruiting bodies. The present study focuses on plant-parasitic taxa in Clavicipitaceae s.s. that are supported by molecular phylogenetic evidence and are known to infect the aerial tissues of growing plants, including Aciculosporium, Balansia, Claviceps, Commelinaceomyces, Corallocytostroma, Ephelis, Epichloë, Heteroepichloë, Myriogenospora, Nigrocornus, Periglandula, and Ustilaginoidea. These genera exhibit diverse life histories, ranging from strictly parasitic to mutualistic associations.

1.3. Classification of plant-parasitic taxa in Clavicipitaceae

The historical classification of plant-parasitic Clavicipitaceae has been shaped by Diehl (1950), who built upon the earlier framework of Gäumann (1926) (Table 1). Gäumann proposed three major groups within Clavicipitaceae sensu Earle: Oomyces-Ascopolyporus, Epichloë-Claviceps, and Cordyceps. Diehl (1950) informally divided Clavicipitaceae sensu Earle into three subfamilies―Oomycetoideae, Clavicipitoideae, and Cordycipitoideae―corresponding to Gäumann’s three groups. He further subdivided Clavicipitoideae into three tribes: Clavicipiteae, Balansiae [sic], and Ustilaginoideae. However, because these tribal names were introduced without Latin diagnoses or references to validly published descriptions, they were not validly published (Art. 39.1), as noted by Rykard et al. (1985). Despite these nomenclatural issues, Diehl’s classification system was pioneering in prioritizing conidial structures over ascostromatic features, which he considered insufficient for a natural classification. His taxonomic scheme can be summarized as follows:

Clavicipiteae is restricted to the genus Claviceps, characterized by the production of sclerotia confined to the ovary and distinctive sphacelial conidia―glutinous amerospores produced in a highly wrinkled, effuse palisade of clavate to linear phialides.

The tribe Balansiae [sic] included genera such as Atkinsonella, Balansia, and Epichloë, distinguished by systemic colonization of host tissues, the formation of a hypothallus, and the production of dry phialospores. Epichloë is specifically characterized by its typhodial conidial stage―a smooth, effuse layer of clavate-lageniform conidiophores (phialides) bearing dry conidia―whereas Balansiae is recognized by its distinctive ephelidial stage, consisting of scolecosporous conidia.

Ustilaginoideae was established for Munkia and Ustilaginoidea, distinguished by the production of superficial, smut-like conidia (radulaspores) borne pleurogenously on closely packed, parallel hyphae.

Although Diehl’s classification was not nomenclaturally valid, his framework correctly emphasized the importance of conidial structures for understanding phylogenetic relationships. His emphasis on life-history traits and conidial morphology anticipated insights later supported by molecular phylogenetic evidence. Nevertheless, some of his genus placements, such as the inclusion of Aciculosporium within Oomycetoideae, have since been rejected by molecular analyses showing a close affinity of Aciculosporium with Claviceps (Píchová et al., 2018; Tanaka et al., 2021).

1.4. Evolution of plant-parasitic taxa in Clavicipitaceae

Members of Clavicipitaceae s.s. exhibit a diverse range of host associations, including invertebrate pathogens, plant symbionts and parasites, and mycoparasites (Kepler et al., 2012). Their evolutionary history has been shaped by repeated inter-kingdom host jumps, resulting in multiple independent origins of plant parasitism from invertebrate-associated ancestral lineages (Kepler et al., 2012; Spatafora et al., 2007; Sung et al., 2008).

The diversification of grass-associated clavicipitaceous fungi has been examined in several molecular studies. For instance, Sung et al. (2008) estimated divergence times for major families of Hypocreales using the Cretaceous fossil Paleoophiocordyceps coccophagus to calibrate the Ophiocordyceps crown node, resulting in a proposed origin of Clavicipitaceae that coincides with the radiation of angiosperms and insects during the Cretaceous. Subsequently, Píchová et al. (2018), focusing on the genus Claviceps, used fossilized Palaeoclaviceps parasiticus from Cretaceous amber to date the emergence of grass-parasitic lineages. Their inferred time of emergence for the genus is at the Paleocene, subsisting on early BEP-PACMAD grasses. These studies have provided a foundational framework for understanding the diversification of Clavicipitaceae.

The present study focuses on taxa parasitizing the tissues of living growing plants (Fig. 1), corresponding to the Diehl's (1950) groups, namely Clavicipiteae, Balansiae, and Ustilaginoideae. While the Clavicipiteae and Balansiae group appear to form a monophyletic clade (Kepler et al., 2012), the phylogenetic placement of the Ustilaginoideae has long remained unresolved. Early molecular phylogenetic analyses based on single-locus sequences failed to resolve its relationship with other clavicipitaceous lineages (Bischoff et al., 2004; Tanaka & Tanaka, 2008). Subsequent multi-locus analyses also left its placement relative to the Clavicipiteae-Balansieae clade ambiguous (Kepler et al., 2012). The discovery of Commelinaceomyces, a lineage parasitizing plants in Commelinaceae, ultimately demonstrated that Ustilaginoideae represents a distinct monophyletic group separate from the Clavicipiteae-Balansieae lineage (Tanaka et al., 2020). This finding supports the hypothesis that host jumps leading to the parasitism of growing plants occurred independently at least twice within Clavicipitaceae, a hypothesis explicitly tested in this study using ancestral state reconstruction.

1.5. Taxonomy of plant-parasitic taxa in Clavicipitaceae: contributions from Japan

Studies conducted in Japan or on Japanese specimens have made substantial contributions to the taxonomy and understanding of plant-parasitic Clavicipitaceae. Notable contributions include:

Takahashi (1896): Examined the anamorphic stage of the rice false smut fungus and proposed a new combination, Ustilaginoidea virens (Fig. 1I), which remains the currently accepted name. The sexual stage (teleomorph) of this fungus was discovered in Japan, and Tanaka et al. (2008) later proposed the genus, Villosiclava, for it. While legitimate at the time, the “One fungus One Name” principle now supports the priority of the anamorphic name Ustilaginoidea virens for this taxon (Rossman et al., 2016).

Hennings (1901, 1906): Described one new species and one new variety of Ophiodothis based on specimens collected from Japan. These taxa are currently treated as Nigrocornus scleroticus (Ryley, 2006), although their taxonomic status requires further clarification.

Hennings (1904): Described Ephelis japonica (Fig. 1G) based on Japanese materials. The genus Ephelis represents the asexual morph of the genus Balansia. Subsequently, Tanaka et al. (2001) showed that E. japonica in East Asia and Japan comprises two distinct lineages closely related to Balansia, indicating the need for taxonomic refinement.

Miyake (1908): Established the genus Aciculosporium as the causal agent of witches’ broom disease of bamboo (A. take). Tanaka et al. (2021) provided a comprehensive treatment of this genus and its constituent species, and also reported the newly described species A. oplismeni (Fig. 1A).

Hara (1922): Described Epichloë sasae as a new species. Tanaka et al. (2002b) later established a new genus, Heteroepichloë, to accommodate this species (Fig. 1H).

Claviceps diversity: In Japan, remarkable species diversity has been reported, including several species and varieties (e. g. Liu et al., 2022a, b). The taxonomic study of Claviceps species in Japan began with the description of C. yanagawaensis by Togashi (1936). Tanaka et al. (2023) recently provided a comprehensive revision of Japanese Claviceps species and recognized 22 Claviceps species in Japan, 17 of which are typified by Japanese materials, including C. agropyri (Fig. 1B), C. humidiphila (Fig. 1C), and C. oplismeni (Fig. 1D).

Tanaka et al. (2020): Reclassified Ustilago aneilematis, previously regarded as a smut fungus, as a clavicipitaceous species closely related to Ustilaginoidea, and established the new genus Commelinaceomyces (Fig. 1E), including four new combinations.

Epichloë species: Epichloë endophytes, including E. typhina, have been reported from Japan, although no major taxonomic revisions have yet been proposed.

1.6. Aim of this study

The present study aims to establish a robust and phylogenetically consistent classification and to provide updated evolutionary timescales for the plant-parasitic Clavicipitaceae, by integrating dense taxon sampling with multi-locus phylogenetic data and fossil-calibrated Bayesian molecular dating. In addition, ancestral state reconstruction was performed to infer the evolutionary transitions among ecological strategies, with particular emphasis on the origin and establishment of plant parasitism within Clavicipitaceae. Further, this study also reviews previous research on Japanese plant-parasitic Clavicipitaceae. The resulting chronogram places the independent origins and subsequent rapid diversification of grass-associated Clavicipitaceae lineages in a temporal context. Based on the findings, this work proposes major taxonomic revisions within Clavicipitaceae. This revised classification provides a foundation for future research into inter-kingdom host jumps, the ecological processes that facilitated these evolutionary transitions, and the balance between phylogenetic constraints and adaptive innovation within this important fungal family.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Phylogenetic analysis within Clavicipitaceae

To determine the phylogenetic position of the plant-parasitic taxa within Clavicipitaceae s.s., a multi-locus dataset comprising five gene regions was assembled: nuclear rDNA segments encoding the 5’ region of 18S rRNA (18S; approximately 1025 bp) and the D1-D2 domains of 28S rRNA (28S; approximately 840 bp), translation elongation factor 1-alpha (TEF1α; approximately 940 bp), and the largest (RPB1; approximately 530 bp) and second largest (RPB2; approximately 940 bp) subunits of RNA polymerase II. The dataset was constructed to maximize generic coverage, and taxa represented by only a small number of loci were excluded. Sequence data were obtained from the DDBJ/EMBL/GenBank. Sequence data used in phylogenetic analyses are listed in Table 2, together with their source and known host associations (e.g. Castlebury et al., 2004; Chaverri et al., 2005a; Chen et al., 2024; Gao et al., 2021; Johnson et al., 2009; Kepler et al., 2014; Lin et al., 2025; Luangsa-ard et al., 2017; Nonaka et al., 2013; Shang et al., 2016; Tan & Shivas 2023; Wang et al. 2022, 2024; Wyka et al., 2021). Only ITS sequences were available for Ephelis japonica (two lineages), and Heteroepichloë bambusae, and H. sasae, (Tanaka et al., 2001, 2002b). Therefore, DNA preserved from the original extractions was used to generate additional loci, following the methods described by Tanaka et al. (2023). The newly generated sequences were deposited in the DDBJ (Table 2).

Ambiguously aligned regions were removed prior to concatenation, and alignments of the 18S, 28S, TEF1α, RPB1, and RPB2 were combined into a single matrix (the concatenated alignment is provided as Supplementary alignment S1). The final concatenated dataset comprised 4285 bp and included 67 taxa. Maximum-Likelihood (ML) analyses were performed using CLC Main Workbench. The analysis was conducted under the general time reversible (GTR) model with a gamma-distributed rate variation and invariant sites (GTR + Γ + I), selected as the best-fit model for each partition based on the built-in model test. Node support was assessed using 1,000 nonparametric bootstrap replicates. A heuristic search strategy was applied using nearest-neighbor interchange for tree rearrangements. All ML analyses were performed on the combined multi-locus dataset (nuclear rDNA including 18S and 28S, TEF1α, RPB1, and RPB2). Maximum parsimony analyses were conducted in MEGA 11.0.11 (Tamura et al., 2021) using heuristic searches with 1,000 replicates. Phylogenetic trees inferred from individual alignment (18S, 28S, TEF1α, RPB1, and RPB2), were generally poorly resolved (Supplementary Fig. S1). 2.2. Divergence time estimation

To estimate divergence times within Clavicipitaceae, a highly complete dataset comprising 18S, 28S, TEF1α, RPB1, and RPB2 was analyzed using BEAST v2.7.8 (Bouckaert et al., 2019). Taxa with missing data or ambiguous placement in the preliminary ML trees were excluded to improve the reliability of divergence-time estimates. Each locus was aligned in CLC Main Workbench and manually refined where necessary. An XML input file (Supplementary XML S1) was prepared in BEAUti v2.7.8 (Bouckaert et al., 2019), with separate partitions for each locus under the GTR + I + Γ model, as selected by ModelTest. An optimized, uncorrelated relaxed clock model was applied to account for rate variation among lineages, and a Yule process was used as the tree prior. Monophyly constraints were assigned to the Claviceps clade, the Claviceps + Aciculosporium clade, the Commelinaceomycetoideae clade, and the Clavicipitaceae clade, following the topology recovered in the ML analysis.

Fossil calibration was applied to the crown group of Clavicipitoideae using Palaeoclaviceps parasiticus (min. age = 97 Ma; Poinar et al., 2015). This fossil, preserved in mid-Cretaceous Burmese amber (~97 Ma), exhibits key Claviceps features such as infection of a single grass floret, sclerotium-like tissues, and sphacelia-like conidiogenesis. However, these characters are not unique to Claviceps, as single-floret parasitism occurring in some members of Aciculosporium and Balansia, and sphacelial-like conidiogenesis occurring in Atkinsonella and Epichloë. Accordingly, the fossil was conservatively treated as a minimum age constraint on the crown node of Clade Ⅰ (Clavicipitoideae) rather than definitive evidence for the Claviceps stem lineage. An exponential prior with an offset of 97.0 Ma and a mean of 4.0 (in log space) was used, allowing divergence estimates up to a soft maximum of ~101 Ma, corresponding to the estimated origin of Poaceae (Huang et al., 2022). This calibration strategy provides a soft temporal bound between the earliest known grass fossils and the fungal evidence from Palaeoclaviceps.

Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) analyses were run for 50,000,000 generations, sampling every 5,000 generations. Convergence and mixing were assessed using Tracer v1.7.2 (Rambaut et al., 2018), with all parameters showing Effective Sample Sizes (ESS) greater than 200. A prior-only analysis (with Sample From Prior set to true) was also conducted to confirm that the posterior estimates were informed by the sequence data rather than solely by the priors; Marginal prior-only and posterior distributions were compared for calibrated and non-calibrated nodes in Tracer and visualized in R (v4.5.1) using ggplot2 (Supplementary Fig. S3). The fossil-calibrated Clade Ⅰ crown node showed near identity between prior and posterior distributions, as expected for a strongly informative calibration (offset = 97 Ma, soft maximum = 101 Ma, mean = 4.0 in log space), whereas non-calibrated nodes exhibited narrower posterior distributions, indicating that sequence data contributed independent chronological information. After discarding the first 10% of samples as burn-in, the remaining trees were summarized using TreeAnnotator v2.7.8 (Bouckaert et al., 2019) to generate a Maximum Clade Credibility (MCC) tree that summarizes the mean node ages and 95% Highest Posterior Density (HPD) intervals. The resulting chronogram was visualized using FigTree v1.4.4 (Rambaut, 2018; Fig. 3).

2.3. Ancestral ecological state reconstruction

Ancestral ecological states were reconstructed on the MCC tree obtained from the BEAST divergence time analysis. Ecological strategies were coded as discrete characters based on information available in the published literature. Four ecological states were recognized: invertebrate parasitism, plant parasitism, mycoparasitism, and root endophytism with invertebrate parasitism. Ecological coding combined species-level ecological data with broader genus-level patterns. For species with well-documented ecological data, states were assigned based on published observations. For taxa lacking species-specific ecological information, states were inferred using conservative, literature based criteria. Specifically, species of Pochonia and Metarhizium were coded as root endophytism with invertebrate parasitism, reflecting evidence that members of these genera exhibit root endophytic colonization in addition to invertebrate parasitism (Macía-Vicente et al. 2009; Sasan & Bidochka, 2012). Keithomyces carneus and Marquandomyces marquandii were coded as invertebrate parasites based on their phylogenetical placement within predominantly invertebrate-associated lineages.

Ancestral state reconstruction was performed using a likelihood-based approach implemented in the R package ape ver. 5.8.1 (Paradis & Schliep, 2019) under R version 4.5.1 (R Core Team, 2025). Alternative models of character evolution, including equal-rated (ER), symmetric (SYM), and all-rates-different (ARD) models, were fitted using the function fitMK in phytools ver. 2.4.4 (Revell, 2024). Models were compared using Akaike’s Information Criterion (AIC), and Akaike weights were calculated to evaluate relative model support. The best-fitting model was used for subsequent ancestral state inference.

3. Results

3.1. Phylogenetic relationships of plant-parasitic taxa in Clavicipitaceae

Phylogenetic analyses based on the combined multi-locus dataset identified two major lineages comprising plant-parasitic taxa, forming two distinct clades (Clades Ⅰ and Ⅱ) that are clearly separated from the other invertebrate-pathogenic lineages (Fig. 2). Clade Ⅰ (Clavicipitoideae) includes genera such as Aciculosporium, Balansia, Claviceps, Epichloë, Heteroepichloë, and Periglandula, was strongly supported (posterior probability = 1.0, ML bootstrap = 90, MP bootstrap = 93). Within this monophyletic clade, Aciculosporium, Claviceps, Corallocytostroma, and Epichloë formed one well-supported monophyletic group (posterior probability = 1.0, ML bootstrap = 98, MP bootstrap = 99), and Balansia, Ephelis, Heteroepichloë, and Myriogenospora formed a well-supported subclade (posterior probability = 1.0, ML bootstrap = 100, MP bootstrap = 100). Although Periglandula, Tyrannicordyceps, and Verticillium epiphytum formed a monophyletic group, support for this grouping was relatively weak (posterior probability = 1.0, ML bootstrap = 89, MP bootstrap = 81), and their relationships within Clade Ⅰ remain uncertain. Clade Ⅱ (Commelinaceomycetoideae), comprising Commelinaceomyces and Ustilaginoidea, was also recovered with strong supported (posterior probability = 1.0, ML bootstrap = 98, MP bootstrap = 99), with Ustilaginoidea forming a strongly supported monophyletic lineage (posterior probability = 1.0, ML bootstrap = 100, MP bootstrap = 100).

Invertebrate-parasitic taxa, such as Chlorocillium, occupied basal positions in the Clavicipitaceae phylogeny (Fig. 2), with other large invertebrate-parasitic clades including the Metarhizium-Pochonia group, Conoideocrella group, and Moelleriella-Regiocrella group. Relationships among these lineages were not fully resolved. Shimizuomyces and Morakotia, both seed parasites, were placed within the Moelleriella-Regiocrella group and were clearly separated from both plant-parasitic clades.

Multi-locus analysis placed the newly sequenced H. sasae and two strains of E. japonica within the Balansieae clade. Among plant-parasitic taxa for which multi-locus data were not available, an additional analysis was conducted using the combined ITS and 28S rDNA datasets (Supplementary Table S2; Supplementary Figure S2). Although overall phylogenetic relationships among the groups remained unresolved in this analysis, Neomunkia was consistently placed within Clade Ⅱ. Ephelis japonica formed a clade with Balansia spp. (Balansieae), and Heteroepichloë spp. occupied a distinct position within Balansieae, separate from Balansia, Myriogenospora, and Nigrocornus.

3.2. Divergence time estimation of plant-parasitic taxa in Clavicipitaceae

The divergence time among plant-parasitic taxa in Clavicipitaceae was estimated using a fossil to calibrate the crown group of Clades Ⅰ (Clavicipitoideae) (Fig. 3). The calibration constrained the minimum age to 97 Ma, based on P. parasiticus, and applied a soft maximum bound of approximately 101 Ma, corresponding to the estimated origin of the Poaceae (Huang et al., 2022), the primary host lineage for many members of this group. Under this calibration, the divergence of Clades Ⅰ (Clavicipitoideae) was estimated to have occurred in the mid-Cretaceous at 99.2 Ma (95% HPD: 97.0-106 Ma), in agreement with the temporal placement suggested by the fossil record. The crown group of Clades Ⅱ (Commelinaceomycetoideae) was estimated to have diverged in the late Cretaceous at 79.0 Ma (95% HPD: 70.4-84.5 Ma).

These estimates suggest that diversification of the major plant-parasitic lineages within Clavicipitaceae began during the Cretaceous. Within Clade Ⅰ, the tribe Clavicipiteae diverged at 80.3 Ma (95% HPD: 74.2-87.5 Ma) in the late Cretaceous. Subsequent divergences of several lineages occurred near or after the Cretaceous-Paleogene boundary (ca. 66 Ma), the tribe Balansieae diverging at 63.7 Ma (95% HPD: 58.1-70.1 Ma), the Aciculosporium-Claviceps clade diverging at 64.7 Ma (95% HPD: 59.1-70.9 Ma), and the Claviceps clade diverging at 59.4 Ma (95% HPD: 54.2-65.5 Ma). Accordingly, most diversification within these lineages took place during the early Paleogene. These molecular dating results are consistent with independent origins of plant-parasitic lineages from invertebrate-parasitic ancestors, followed by diversification concurrent with the expansion of grasses and other monocot hosts.

3.3 Ancestral state reconstruction of ecological strategies

Model comparison based on AIC identified the ER model (AIC = 83.64, df = 1) as the best-fitting model for the evolution of ecological strategies in Clavicipitaceae, with lower AIC values than the SYM model (AIC = 88.74, ΔAIC = 5.10) and ARD model (AIC = 94.61, ΔAIC = 10.97) (Supplementary Table S3). Akaike weights indicated that the ER model had a high probability of support (96.8%) among the model compared, whereas the more complex models received little support (SYM: 3.0%; ARD: 0.2%).

Ancestral state reconstruction under the ER model inferred that the most recent common ancestor of Clavicipitoideae and Commelinaceomycetoideae was most likely invertebrate-associated (marginal ancestral state probability = 0.796). Plant parasitism, however, remained a plausible alternative state with moderate probability (marginal ancestral state probability = 0.203), indicating uncertainty at this deep ancestral node.

In contrast, ancestral state at their crown nodes of both plant-parasitic subfamilies were inferred with high confidence. The crown node of Clavicipitoideae was very inferred to be plant-parasitic (p = 0.975), and the crown node of Commelinaceomycetoideae was also inferred as plant-parasitic with high probability (p = 0.940). These reconstructions indicate that plant parasitism was established early within both subfamilies following divergence from an invertebrate-associated ancestor. Mycoparasitism was reconstructed as a derived and phylogenetically restricted ecological strategy, with high support limited to specific terminal lineages (Supplementary Fig. S4).

4. Taxonomy

The results of phylogenetic analyses demonstrate the necessity for a major taxonomic revision of Clavicipitaceae s.s. in order to reflect their evolutionary relationships more consistently. This study proposes a new subfamily, Commelinaceomycetoideae, and a new tribe Commelinaceomyceteae, together with the validation and emendation of several existing taxonomic names. Recognition of Commelinaceomycetoideae as a distinct subfamily establishes a more natural and evolutionarily coherent framework that recognizes the independent origin of this plant-parasitic lineage. This revised classification provides a stable taxonomic foundation for future studies on this ecologically important group of plant-parasitic fungi.

Note: The name Clavicipiteae was first used by Lindau (1897) as a subfamily name (corrected to Clavicipitoideae under Art. 19.7), but Diehl (1950) invalidly introduced Clavicipiteae at the tribal rank within his concept of Clavicipitoideae. However, Diehl’s tribal name was not validly published due to the lack of Latin diagnoses or references to validly published descriptions (Art. 39.1). Diehl (1950) characterized this tribe as monotypic, accommodating only Claviceps, and distinguished by the sphacelial conidial state. Subsequent molecular phylogenetic studies have demonstrated that several grass-associated genera (Aciculosporium, Corallocytostroma, and Epichloë) are closely allied with Claviceps. The generic concept of Aciculosporium was recently expanded by Píchová et al. (2018), who synonymized Neoclaviceps and Cepsiclava with Aciculosporium based on molecular phylogenetic analyses and the shared morphological characteristics, particularly the acicular (needle-shaped) conidia with dichotomously branched appendages, thereby consolidating these morphologically and phylogenetically related taxa. The present description validated the tribal name and expands the original circumscription to include these phylogenetically related genera.

Claviceps species are characterized by the sphacelial stage producing glutinous conidia and by the formation of hard sclerotia that replace the host ovary, from which stipitate ascostromata develop. The production of ergot alkaloids and other chemicals has been extensively studied in Claviceps species (e.g. Doi et al., 2023), and their biological functions are well elucidated (e.g. Doi et al., 2022a, b). In contrast, Aciculosporium species exhibit endophytic growth within in the meristematic tissues of vegetative organs of their host (Tanaka, 2009). The ecology and physiological characteristics of Aciculosporium have also been investigated (Tanaka et al., 2002a, 2003a, b; Tanaka, 2010). One representative, A. take, does not produce ergot alkaloids (Tanaka et al., 2021); rather, it synthesizes the plant hormone auxin (Tanaka et al., 2003a), which is presumed to be related to their endophytic lifestyle. Although some species of Aciculosporium resemble Claviceps by infecting individual floral organs and forming stipitate ascostromata, others produce sessile ascostromata on shoot apices. In culture, Aciculosporium species form filiform or acicular conidia with distinctive bifurcated appendages at the ends (Tsuda et al., 1997). Corallocytostroma species develop superficial conidiomata on inflorescences and stem nodes. Their holoblastic, globose conidia occur in slimy masses covering stromatic surfaces, and the presence of sclerotia bearing capitate-stipitate ascostroma suggests affinities to Claviceps. Epichloë species are systemic endophytes inhabiting the intercellular spaces of stems, leaves, inflorescences, and seeds, often exhibiting mutualistic or asymptomatic relationships with their hosts. Their conidial state produces dry phialidic (typhodial) conidia arranged in a palisade layer on the host surface. Epichloë species predominantly form ascostromata on reproductive culms, enveloping and permeating the immature inflorescences and surrounding leaf sheaths, although some species develop hypothalli on immature inflorescences (Fig. 1F; Yanagida et al., 2005).

Included genera: Commelinaceomyces E. Tanaka, Neomunkia Petr., Ustilaginoidea Bref. (syn. Villosciclava E. Tanaka)

Host families: Commelinaceae, Cyperaceae, Poaceae

Description: This subfamily is a member of Clavicipitaceae and comprises plant-parasitic fungi that predominantly form anamorphic reproductive structures on floral or culm tissues of monocotyledonous hosts. Conidiophores are produced from stromata or directly from host tissues, producing smut-like conidia. Sexual stages are rare or unknown in most species. This subfamily is distinguished from Clavicipitoideae by its smut-like conidia and distinct phylogenetic position.

Etymology: The name is derived from the type genus. The genus Ustilaginoidea was not selected as the type genus of the new subfamily because the resulting name would be structurally cumbersome and etymologically ambiguous due to the terminal -oidea in the generic name. To void this redundancy and ensure nomenclatural clarity and simplicity, the genus Commelinaceomyces was chosen as the type genus, resulting in the subfamily name Commelinaceomycetoideae, which provides a more straightforward and linguistically consistent designation.

Notes: This new subfamily accommodates plant-parasitic clavicipitaceous fungi that form a distinct monophyletic group. Commelinaceomyces and Ustilaginoidea are characterized by parasitism on plant flowers, while Munkia and Neomunkia produce stromata on bamboo branches. Anamorphic reproduction and the absence of ergot alkaloid production are predominant characteristics. Several genera (e.g., Munkia Speg., Dubiomyces Lloyd ) may belong here, but their positions require verification via molecular phylogenetic analyses. Sexual morphs in this subfamily are known only for U. virens and U. setariae. This subfamily represents an independent evolutionary origin of plant parasitism within Clavicipitaceae.

Tribe Commelinaceomyceteae E. Tanaka, trib. nov.,

MycoBank: MB 861276

Type genus: Commelinaceomyces E. Tanaka, designated here

Description: A member of Commelinaceomycetoideae (Clavicipitaceae), this tribe is characterized by the production of smut-like, thick-walled conidia that are formed acrogenously on conidiophores. Members of this tribe parasitize the floral organs of plants in the Commelinaceae.

Included genera: Commelinaceomyces

Host families: Commelinaceae

Etymology: The name is derived from the type genus.

Note: In Tanaka et al. (2020), Commelinaceomyces was assigned to the tribe Ustilaginoideae. However, the smut-like conidia in this group are not produced pleurogenously; rather, they are formed acrogenously at the apex of branched conidiophores. This monotypic tribe consists of Commelinaceomyces, and recognition of this tribe within the new subfamily is proposed.

Tribe Ustilaginoideae Diehl, ex E. Tanaka, trib. nov.

MycoBank: MB 861279

Basionym: Ustilaginoideae Diehl, Balansia and the Balansiae in America. Agriculture monograph 4: 12 (1950) (nom. invalid. Art. 39.1)

Type genus: Ustilaginoidea Bref.

Included genera: Munkia Speg. (syn. Shropshiria F. Stevens), Neomunkia Petr., Ustilaginoidea Bref. (syn. Villosciclava E. Tanaka)

Description: A member of Commelinaceomycetoideae (Clavicipitaceae), this tribe is characterized by the production of smut-like, dark, thick-walled conidia that are formed pleurogenously on compact, parallel hyphae. Members of this tribe are parasitic on the floral organs of grasses or sedges, or on branches of bamboos.

Host families: Cyperaceae, Poaceae

Etymology: The name is derived from the type genus.

Note: Diehl (1950) invalidly introduced the name Ustilaginoideae as one of three tribes within Clavicipitoideae based on conidial morphology. The present work validated the tribe name Ustilaginoideae. In the present circumscription, the tribe is restricted to genera that form pleurogenous conidia.

Among the species of Ustilaginoidea, Ustilaginoidea virens, the causal agent of rice false smut, has been the most extensively studied worldwide (e. g. Zhou et al., 2003; Tanaka et al., 2011, 2012). This fungus replaces the host rice ovary with its own conidiostromata that produce distinctive smut-like conidia. Ecologically, the smut-like spores persist in soil, and infect rice roots during the vegetative growth stage (Tanaka et al., 2017). Subsequently, the fungus grows epiphytically around the shoot apices of the above-ground tissues (Tanaka et al., 2017), extending its hyphae toward developing panicles, and penetrating immature spikelets via the slits at the tip before anthesis (Ashizawa et al., 2012). The sexual stage (teleomorph) occasionally develops when sclerotia form within the conidiostromata (Fig. 1I), from which capitate-stipitate ascostromata emerge (Tanaka et al., 2008).

5. Discussion

Phylogenetic analyses of a combined multi-locus dataset demonstrated that plant parasitism evolved independently at least twice within the family Clavicipitaceae, specifically within the two major lineages, Clavicipitoideae and Commelinaceomycetoideae. This finding provides an evolutionary framework that reconciles molecular phylogenetic results with classical morphology-based classifications, particularly those proposed by Diehl (1950). In this study, ecological strategies were classified into four discrete states (invertebrate parasitism, plant parasitism, mycoparasitism, and root endophytism with invertebrate parasitism), allowing likelihood-based inference of ancestral ecological states.

5.1. Independent origins of plant parasitism

The Clavicipitoideae clade, encompassing Claviceps, Balansia, Epichloë, and related genera, is inferred to have originated from invertebrate-associated ancestors in the mid-Cretaceous period at 99.2 Ma (95% HPD: 97.0-106 Ma; Fig. 3). This scenario is consistent with the “scale-insect bridge hypothesis” (e.g. Chaverri et al., 2005b; Korochl et al., 2004; White et al., 2002), which proposes that early clavicipitoid fungi originally parasitized scale insects inhabiting grass inflorescences and subsequently adapted to infect the host plant tissues themselves. The timing of this divergence coincides with the diversification of Poaceae, suggesting that ecological opportunities associated with the emergence of grasses facilitated a transition from invertebrate to plant parasitism.

In contrast, the Commelinaceomycetoideae lineage, represented by Commelinaceomyces, Neomunkia, and Ustilaginoidea, was estimated to have diverged at 79.0 Ma (95% HPD: 70.4-84.5 Ma), which is consistent with Jung et al.'s (2025) estimate of the origin of Commelinaceae at 81.4 Ma (95% HPD: 68.5-96.1 Ma). Each genus within this group exhibits distinct host specialization patterns, with Neomunkia parasitizing bamboo culms, Ustilaginoidea infecting grass and sedge florets, and Commelinaceomyces infecting florets of the family Commelinaceae. Unlike the invertebrate-associated origin inferred for Clavicipitoideae, ancestral state reconstruction suggests that Commelinaceomycetoideae evolved from an ancestor characterized by root endophytism with invertebrate parasitism, followed by a transitioning to plant parasitism at the crown node of the subfamily. Ecological evidence from Tanaka et al. (2017) demonstrated that U. virens can inhabit internal root tissues and the surface of apical meristems enclosed by juvenile leaves in the vegetative stage of rice plants. These findings support the hypothesis that the ancestors of this lineage may have originated as root-associated endophytes that subsequently adapted to colonize aerial reproductive structures. Such an evolutionary trajectory is consistent with derivation from soil-associated invertebrate pathogens or endophytes inhabiting rhizosphere environments, followed by host shifts to aerial plant tissues. This scenario may account for the distinct morphology of Commelinaceomycetoideae, host preference for monocots, and the absence of the alkaloid biosynthetic capacity characteristic of Clavicipitoideae.

5.2. Evolutionary and ecological implications

Although the two major lineages share broadly similar ecological strategies, including parasitism or endophytism on reproductive or vegetative organs of monocots, they differ markedly in morphology, host range, and biochemical capacity. Members of Clavicipitoideae typically form stromatic sexual morphs and frequently produce ergot alkaloids; in contrast, members of Commelinaceomycetoideae are characterized by predominantly anamorphic reproduction and an apparent lack of alkaloid biosynthesis. The extensive chemotypic diversity of alkaloids within clavicipitoid fungi Schardl et al. (2013) is closely linked to their varied host interactions, ranging from pathogenic to mutualistic. These contrasting characteristics between these two lineages are consistent with the hypothesis that plant parasitism in Clavicipitaceae evolved convergently via at least two independent evolutionary pathways.

The two major lineages described herein share the ability to colonize meristematic tissues, with a pronounced tendency to invade floral organs. This preference may be due to the ovary acting as a rich source of nutrients that facilitate fungal development and the formation of reproductive structures. Among these fungi, Claviceps appears to have evolved a high degree of specialization, restricting its parasitism solely to the floral organs. Conversely, Aciculosporium take, which infects infrequently flowering bamboos, has adapted by utilizing vegetative meristems as its primary reproductive site (Tanaka & Mochizuki, 2024), representing an evolutionary response to the unreliable flowering of its host. As a consequence of this shift to vegetative meristems, the fungus may have experienced relaxed selective pressure on ovary infection, potentially leading to the loss of this capability even when host flowers are produced. Notably, the timing of the transitions to plant parasitism in both clades coincides with major episodes of angiosperm diversification, suggesting that the emergence of novel ecological niches contributed to these evolutionary shifts.

5.3. Temporal patterns of diversification and evolutionary trajectories

Incorporating divergence-time estimates with the ecological and morphological contrasts outlined above, the temporal analyses indicate distinct evolutionary trajectories for the two major plant-parasitic lineages. The present analyses suggest that the diversification within Clavicipitoideae, which progressed throughout the Cretaceous to Paleogene, gave rise to the major lineages, such as Balansieae and Clavicipiteae. These lineages subsequently developed a wide range of host interaction strategies, ranging from pathogenesis to mutualism. In contrast, the Commelinaceomycetoideae appears to have largely retained pathogenic modes of interaction, reflecting a distinct evolutionary trajectory of host association. This temporal divergence suggests that the ancestral lineages already possessed molecular and physiological traits related to host interaction and nutrient acquisition, which were later recruited and modified during the independent evolution of plant parasitism and endophytism.

5.4. Evaluation of molecular clock analysis and fossil constraint

Comparison of the marginal prior-only and posterior distributions (Supplementary Fig. S3) showed that the calibrated node corresponding to the crown of Clavicipitoideae exhibited closely matching prior and posterior distributions. This outcome is consistent with the use of a strongly informative fossil prior, as the exponential calibration (offset = 97 Ma, soft maximum = 101 Ma) tightly constrained the node age and therefore had a strong influence on the posterior estimate. In contrast, the posterior distributions for non-calibrated nodes were substantially narrower than their corresponding prior distributions. This reduction in uncertainty indicates that the molecular data, together with the fossil constraint applied elsewhere in the tree, contributed additional temporal information that refined divergence-time estimates across the phylogeny. The narrowing of the posterior intervals for these uncalibrated nodes supports the adequacy of the calibration strategy and the molecular clock framework, resulting in more precise and phylogenetically meaningful temporal estimates.

5.5. Consistency with previous dating studies

The divergence time estimates obtained in this study are broadly consistent with previous molecular dating results, supporting the temporal framework presented here. Sung et al. (2008) estimated the crown age of the Clavicipitoideae clade at approximately 81 Ma (95% HPD: 60-105 Ma), while Píchová et al. (2018) inferred a slightly older age of about 100 Ma (95% HPD: 87-115 Ma). The present estimate for the crown age of Clavicipitoideae is at 99.2 Ma (95% HPD: 97.0-106 Ma), which closely agrees with these previous studies, supporting a mid-Cretaceous origin of the subfamily Clavicipitoideae. Similarly, the divergence time for the tribe Clavicipiteae estimated by Píchová et al. (2018) occurred at the Cretaceous, 73.4-101 Ma (95% HPD), which closely corresponds to the present estimate of 74.2-87.5 Ma (95% HPD). Taken together, this concordance indicates the temporal stability of node-age estimates across independent analyses, despite differences in taxon sampling and calibration strategy.

5.6. Justification of fossil calibration

The use of Palaeoclaviceps parasiticus as a fossil calibration point warrants careful evaluation. Although the fossil exhibits morphological characteristics found in extant Claviceps (such as single-floret infection, sclerotium-like tissue, and sphacelial-type conidia), these characteristics are not unique to Claviceps and are present in other clavicipitoid taxa. Accordingly, to avoid excessively constraining divergence-time estimates, the fossil was conservatively applied as a minimum bound for the crown node of Clavicipitoideae, rather than the Claviceps genus. A soft maximum bound of 101 Ma, corresponding to the estimated origin of Poaceae, was also applied to define an upper temporal limit consistent with host availability. This calibration strategy, together with comparative analysis of alternative fossil placements (stem vs. crown), yielded chronograms that are compatible with both fungal and plant evolutionary timelines and support a mid-to-late Cretaceous origin for grass-associated clavicipitaceous fungi.

5.7. Further insights obtained from phylogenetic analysis

These phylogenetic analyses provide further insight into the internal relationships among the plant-parasitic Clavicipitaceae. The genus Ephelis has long been recognized as the asexual state of Balansia; the analysis confirmed the close relationship between E. japonica and Balansia spp., indicating that a formal taxonomic revision is needed. Heteroepichloë was clearly resolved as phylogenetically distinct from the morphologically similar Myriogenospora, supporting their recognition as separate genera. Phylogenetic placement of the recently described Australian Ustilaginoidea cookiorum (Crous et al., 2024), Heteroepichloë sichuanensis (Yang et al., 2024), and two new Asian species of Aciculosporium (Mongkolsamrit et al., 2021; Yang et al., 2024) was consistent with their assignment to the respective genera. The analysis also showed that the fungicolous members of Clavicipitoideae, Tyrannicordyceps fratricida and V. epiphytum, form a clade, consistent with their shared verticillium-like conidiogenesis. These taxa formed a moderately supported cluster with the convolvulaceous epiphyte P. ipomoeae, suggesting that they represent a secondarily derived lineage allied to the tribe Balansieae. Overall, these findings underscore the important contribution of Japanese mycological research to the taxonomy of plant-parasitic Clavicipitaceae. To clarify remaining phylogenetic relationships and to further elucidate evolutionary diversity within this family, continued sampling of Japanese and other Asian isolates will be essential.

5.8 Independent origin and evolutionary stabilization of plant parasitism in Clavicipitaceae

The evolutionary origin of plant parasitism within Clavicipitaceae has long been debated. By integrating fossil-calibrated divergence-time estimation with likelihood-based ancestral state reconstruction, this study provides strong support for at least two independent origins of plant parasitism within the family.

Ancestral state reconstruction inferred the most recent common ancestor of Clavicipitoideae and Commelinaceomycetoideae as most likely invertebrate-associated. Based on these results, plant parasitism appears to have arisen independently in two deeply divergent lineages. In Clavicipitoideae, plant parasitism was reconstructed as originating directly from an invertebrate-associated ancestor, consistent with the predominance of invertebrate-associated fungi among early-diverging hypocrealean lineages. In contrast, in Commelinaceomycetoideae, plant parasitism appears to have evolved through a transitional ecological state involving root endophytism coupled with invertebrate parasitism, suggesting a different evolutionary history toward stable plant parasitism in this lineage.

Model comparison supported the ER model for transitions among ecological strategies, indicating that no particular transition direction was inherently more likely than others. This result suggests that the repeated emergence of plant parasitism within Clavicipitaceae cannot be explained by a strong directional bias in ecological transitions. Instead, the fixation of plant parasitism at the crown nodes of both plant-parasitic subfamilies likely reflects selective stabilization following host shifts rather than elevated transition rates toward plant parasitism.

Divergence-time estimates further place these ecological transitions in temporal context. The crown ages of both Clavicipitoideae and Commelinaceomycetoideae were estimated to fall in the mid-to-late Cretaceous, broadly coincident with the early diversification of monocot lineages, including Poaceae, Cyperaceae, and Commelinaceae. This temporal coincidence suggests that the emergence of plant parasitism in Clavicipitaceae may have been associated with the radiation of monocot hosts. Subsequent diversification during the Paleogene likely reflects lineage-specific specialization and host-associated radiation following the establishment of stable plant-parasitic lifestyles.

Together, these results support the interpretation that plant parasitism in Clavicipitaceae did not arise once and diversify secondarily, but instead evolved multiple times through distinct evolutionary pathways. The contrasting evolutionary histories inferred for Clavicipitoideae and Commelinaceomycetoideae indicate the evolutionary diversity of hypocrealean fungi in colonizing plant hosts and demonstrate the importance of integrating ancestral state reconstruction with time-calibrated phylogenies when inferring the origins of complex ecological strategies. This perspective provides a phylogenetically grounded basis for the revised classification proposed here and offers a foundation for future comparative studies on the genomic and ecological determinants of plant parasitism in Clavicipitaceae.

5.9. Taxonomic implications and future perspective

The recognition, supported by molecular phylogenetic evidence, of Commelinaceomycetoideae as a new subfamily and the emendation of Clavicipitoideae constitute major revisions to the current classification of Clavicipitaceae. This reorganization clarifies longstanding nomenclatural inconsistencies, including the improper use of Clavicipiteae by Lindau (1897), and provides a monophyletic basis for evolutionary and ecological studies of the family. The revised classification also retains Diehl's (1950) concepts of Balansiae, Clavicipiteae, and Ustilaginoideae, thus placing traditional taxonomy within a modern phylogenetic context. Together, these taxonomic revisions provide a clear basis for understanding the evolution and diversity of plant-parasitic Clavicipitaceae.

Future comparative genomics studies among Clavicipitoideae, Commelinaceomycetoideae, and related invertebrate-associated taxa will be important for testing the hypotheses concerning their independent origins, including derivation from scale-insect parasitism and root endophytism, respectively. The classification proposed here offers a phylogenetically based framework for identifying the genetic mechanisms underlying plant colonization and the diversification of secondary metabolites. Based on this refined taxonomy, research focusing on secreted effectors, cell wall-degrading enzymes, and secondary metabolite biosynthetic pathways may help clarify the molecular innovations that facilitated these evolutionary transitions. Such integrated approaches will advance understanding of host specialization, the evolution of virulence, and ecological diversification within plant-parasitic Clavicipitaceae.

6. Conclusions

This phylogenetic study demonstrates that plant parasitism evolved independently at least twice within the family Clavicipitaceae. This recognition of two distinct plant-parasitic lineages, here formalized as the subfamilies Clavicipitoideae and Commelinaceomycetoideae, clarifies the evolutionary history of ecological strategies within this fungal family. This revised classification provides a phylogenetically consistent basis for future studies on the molecular mechanisms of plant-fungal interactions, the evolution of virulence and mutualism, and the ecological factors underlying fungal diversification in Clavicipitaceae.
