Implementation of a nonprofit specialty clinic for vascular anomalies and birthmarks in Vietnam: A 12-year service evaluation
Authors: Mitchell Hanson, Allison Chiou, Minh Van Hoang, Vien The Tran, Tri Cao Le, Vy Truc Hoang Nguyen, Anh Quynh Ngoc Phan, Thuy Phung, Stuart Nelson, Martin Mihm, Richard Rox Anderson, Thanh Nga Tran
Published: 2026

To the Editor: Access to specialized care for vascular anomalies and other birthmarks remains limited in many low- and middle-income countries due to shortages of trained clinicians, equipment, and multidisciplinary expertise,1,2 and few reports describe the long-term sustainability of such programs.3 The Vietnam Vascular Anomalies Center (VVAC), a nonprofit multidisciplinary referral center established in 2009, expands access to care of vascular anomalies and other birthmarks in Ho Chi Minh City, Vietnam. We performed a retrospective service evaluation to characterize VVAC’s clinical activity and implementation experience over 12 years.

This retrospective review examined clinic records, procedural logs, and administrative databases (2012–2024) from VVAC. Data extracted included examinations, laser procedures, patient age group, historically documented diagnoses, and payment status. Descriptive statistics, χ2 tests, linear regression, and Pearson correlation were performed (P < .05).

VVAC conducted 63,393 encounters and 27,124 laser procedures (Fig 1), with annual encounters ranging from 1919 to 6389. Pediatric patients accounted for 59,162 (93.3%) examinations and 15,226 (56.1%) laser procedures, while adults accounted for 4231 (6.7%) examinations and 11,898 (43.9%) laser procedures (P < .001). While adult services required payment to support financial sustainability, all pediatric examinations and laser procedures were provided free of charge. The most common pediatric laser indications were infantile hemangiomas (37.1%), port-wine stains (18.8%), nevus of Ota (17.1%), and congenital melanocytic nevi (15.1%) (Supplementary Table I, available via Mendeley at https://data.mendeley.com/datasets/3c4ddxx773/5).4

Annual examinations and laser treatments at the Vietnam Vascular Anomalies Center (2012-2024), stratified by paid and free services. Patient volumes fluctuated with growth, pandemic disruption, and recovery.

Implementation approaches and experience-based considerations are summarized in Table I. VVAC developed a multidisciplinary model through local academic partnerships. Its operating model combined philanthropy and cross-subsidization, using adult cosmetic revenue to subsidize free pediatric care. Long-term partnerships with international collaborators and industry supported acquisition and maintenance of laser platforms that would otherwise be difficult to sustain (Supplementary Tables II-IV, available via Mendeley at https://data.mendeley.com/datasets/3c4ddxx773/5, Fig 1).4 However, reliance on donated or limited laser platforms, coupled with equipment maintenance requirements and occasional machine downtime, may have influenced treatment selection by necessitating use of available technologies rather than the ideal treatment modality.5 Treatment decisions therefore balanced device availability with patient goals, accessibility, and clinical considerations. VVAC supported regional expertise through monthly teleconsultations, annual conferences, hands-on laser training, a train-the-trainer model via in-person, and recorded clinical demonstrations. Limited pediatric propranolol availability, equipment maintenance, and high demand necessitated flexible treatment algorithms (Supplementary Fig 2, available via Mendeley at https://data.mendeley.com/datasets/3c4ddxx773/5), illustrated by cases (Supplementary Cases 1-4, available via Mendeley at https://data.mendeley.com/datasets/3c4ddxx773/5).4 Lastly, local stigma and misinformation concerning alternative therapies required targeted counseling and outreach.

Limitations include the retrospective, encounter-level data, which do not identify unique patients, retention, or longitudinal clinical outcomes and incompletely capture non-laser treatments. Cost, revenue, donor-contribution, and solvency data were unavailable; therefore, financial sustainability and donor dependence could not be evaluated. Diagnostic classification reflected documentation at evaluation, evolving imaging availability, and access to multidisciplinary consultation. These findings describe single-center implementation and sustained service delivery rather than treatment effectiveness or proof of a financially sustainable, generalizable model.

Our experience demonstrates sustained delivery of nonprofit specialty services and offers implementation considerations in multidisciplinary collaboration, clinician education, financing approaches, and adaptation to local resource constraints. Prospective outcome, retention, and financial data are needed before the model’s sustainability and transferability can be established.
