The central function and specific molecular mechanisms of the TLR4 pathway underlying impaired intestinal barrier in necrotizing enterocolitis
Authors: Xin Ren, Xiulan Su, Gaowa Arigong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNeonatal necrotizing enterocolitisis a fatal gastrointestinal emergency affecting preterm infants, which is linked to destruction of intestinal barrier. The structural overexpression and negative regulation of Toll like receptor 4, a key receptor of lipopolysaccharidefrom Gram-negative bacteria in preterm intestinal epithelial cells, constitute a core hub connecting flora dysbiosis and barrier breakdown. When TLR4 neutrally binds with LPS, it signals the activation of the NF-κB-mediated inflammatory cascade through the MyD88/TRIF pathway which in turn induces apoptosis, necroptosis, and tight junction protein degradation in intestinal epithelial cells. degradation of tight junction protein of intestinal epithelial cells. The body naturally possesses protective mechanisms such as competitive receptors, intracellular negative regulatory networks, and microbial metabolites, which can be the target of drugs. Methods for the protection of the intestinal barrier by fine-tuning TLR4 signaling activity include small-molecule drugs, nano-delivery systems and nutritional interventions. Nonetheless, clinical translation remains hindered by the issues of TLR4 gene polymorphism, gestational age-dependent regulation, and immune balance maintenance. In the future, efforts should concentrate on the regulatory mechanisms of small molecule drugs and naturally occurring foodborne peptides. The goal is to combine the precision offered by genetic screening with a specific delivery system. This will allow for the targeted prevention and control of TLR4 signal systems. Ultimately, this will lead to a breakthrough in tackling the challenges associated with NEC treatment.Keywords: Necrotizing enterocolitis, TLR4, Intestinal barrier, Molecular mechanisms

Introduction

Clinical dilemmas in the prevention and treatment of NEC and bottlenecks in mechanism research

NEC is a prevalent gastrointestinal emergency during the neonatal period and a significant cause of neonatal mortality. The incidence of NEC in the Neonatal Intensive Care Unit ranges from 2% to 5%, while the occurrence among very low birth weight infants is reported to be between 4.5% and 8.7%. Mortality rates associated with NEC are alarmingly high, with death occurring in 20% to 30% of cases, and among extremely low birth weight infants, mortality rates can reach as high as 30% to 50.9% [1]. The intestinal barrier comprises four major components: the physical barrier, chemical barrier, biological barrier, and immune barrier [2]. The physical barrier primarily involves tight junctions, including proteins such as Occludin, Claudins, and ZO-1, which are crucial for intestinal integrity. Damage to the gut leads to the release of pro-inflammatory factors, disruption of tight junctions, and exacerbation of inflammation [3]. Bacterial metabolites, particularly LPS in the intestines of preterm infants, further downregulate connexin expression and increase barrier permeability, significantly contributing to the progression of NEC.

As TLR4 is the principal pattern recognition receptor for LPS, it plays a critical role in the pathophysiology of NEC. TLR4 is highly expressed in the immature intestinal epithelial cells of preterm infants [4]. When Gram-negative bacteria proliferate excessively due to dysbiosis, a substantial amount of LPS is released. This LPS is recognized by the TLR4/MD-2 complex, facilitated by LPS binding protein and CD14 [5], leading to the excessive activation of the MyD88 and TRIF signaling pathways. Activation of these pathways initiates an NF-κB-mediated inflammatory cascade, resulting in the destruction of the intestinal mucosal barrier, death and necrosis of epithelial cells, as well as bacterial translocation and inflammatory injury (Fig. 1). However, current prevention and treatment strategies remain largely supportive rather than etiologically targeted, and a fundamental bottleneck lies in the lack of a systematic understanding of why TLR4 signaling becomes uncontrolled specifically in preterm intestine and how this dysregulation precipitates multi-dimensional barrier collapse.

Unique vulnerability of TLR4 signaling in preterm infants

Preterm infants, born before they can adapt to extra-uterine life, are at a significantly higher risk of various diseases. Their digestive tracts often do not mature normally, leading to challenges in microbial colonization that these infants struggle to cope with. This situation substantially increases the risk of developing NEC among preterm infants. According to experts, nearly 10% of preterm infants weighing less than 1500 g may develop this condition [6]. TLR4, present on intestinal epithelial cells, plays a crucial role in stimulating the proliferation of intestinal cells, modifying the cytoskeleton, and facilitating the development of the intestinal barrier in preterm infants [7]. Furthermore, Bifidobacterium infantis metabiotics have been shown to enhance the tight junctions of immature intestines, both in the presence and absence of TLR4, highlighting the importance of TLR4 signaling in preterm birth.

Review framework and core scientific questions

While previous reviews have extensively documented the pro-inflammatory role of TLR4 in NEC, they often treat TLR4 overexpression, barrier disruption, and therapeutic targeting as isolated themes. Consequently, the central question—how the developmental immaturity of negative regulatory networks in preterm infants renders TLR4 signaling uncontrolled and drives barrier breakdown—remains insufficiently addressed. This review distinguishes itself from existing literature by establishing an integrated framework centered on the “high expression–impaired negative regulation” axis of TLR4 in the preterm intestine. We posit that the loss of controlled TLR4 signaling, rather than TLR4 activation per se, constitutes the core driver of NEC pathogenesis.

Abnormal expression and regulation of TLR4 signaling in intestinal tract

Physiological control of TLR4 in the normal intestine and its disruption in preterm infants

In the healthy mature intestine, TLR4 is maintained in a quiescent state by multi-layered negative regulation: spatial restriction of TLR4/MD-2 to the basolateral membrane, intracellular checkpoints (SIGIRR interfering with MyD88 recruitment, A20 deubiquitinating TRAF6), post-transcriptional silencing by miR-146a/miR-155, and epigenetic repression via TLR4 promoter methylation. These mechanisms ensure that TLR4 acts as a transient sentinel with self-limiting activation. In stark contrast, the preterm intestine suffers a developmental collapse of this brake system. TLR4 hyperactivation arises not merely from receptor abundance, but from hierarchical defects in negative regulation: (i) transcriptional de-repression through DNMT1-mediated hypomethylation of the TLR4 promoter; (ii) post-transcriptional loss of miR-146a/miR-155 that normally suppresses adaptor protein expression; and (iii) protein-level failure of SIGIRR and A20 to terminate MyD88/TRAF6 signaling. This loss of controlled signaling—rather than TLR4 expression per se—constitutes the core driver of uncontrolled inflammation in NEC.

InWtestinal epithelial cells play a crucial role in defensin production, gut physiology, and microbial interactions. In preterm infants, TLR4 signaling is upregulated

The high expression of TLR4 on the basal side of IECs, mediated by MD2, recognizes LPS and activates the NF-kappa B pathway. To maintain homeostasis and tolerance to symbiotic bacteria, IECs typically restrict the expression of TLR4 and MD-2 [8]. A study has indicated that MD2 is highly expressed in inflammatory bowel disease, where Th1/Th17 cytokines induce upregulation of TLR4 [9]. MD2 enhances the presence of Th1 cytokines and the functional interaction of receptors with LPS, thereby accelerating the signaling process mediated by TLR4 and MD2. TLR4 exacerbates the inflammatory response through the dephosphorylation of the NF-kappa B pathway. Notably, TLR4 expression in the intestinal epithelium of preterm infants is higher than that in term infants, exhibiting significantly elevated levels and a low endocytosis rate. This leads to an excessive response to pathogen-associated molecular patterns [10]. High TLR4 expression in enterocytes activates autophagy, inhibiting RhoA-GTPase and enhancing cell-matrix adhesion [11]. The activation of GSK3β and the subsequent decrease in β-catenin activity [12] hinder epithelial cell growth, while the activation of PUMA through NF-κB signaling stimulates apoptosis in intestinal stem cells. Furthermore, TLR4 activation impairs goblet cell differentiation and mucus barrier function. Genetic loss of TLR4 increases the number of goblet cells, enhances the protection of antimicrobial peptides, and significantly reduces the severity of NEC [13]. LPS binds to TLR4 via the CD14/MD-2 complex, with CD14 triggering TLR4 endocytosis through the ITAM-mediated Syk/PLCγ2 pathway, subsequently transporting it to endosomes where a TRIF-dependent inflammatory cascade is activated [14].

In preterm infants, the elevated expression of TLR4 in the gut, combined with the hypoxia-ischemia resulting from LPS translocation, creates a synergistic effect. Among other consequences, the activation of TLR4 in endothelial cells leads to the production of MyD88 and the activation of eNOS, resulting in microvascular perfusion disorders. Additionally, the CD14-dependent hyperactivation of TLR4 in epithelial cells induces autophagy, inhibits RhoA GTPase activity, and impedes cell migration. It also reduces β-catenin activity through the activation of GSK3β, upregulates PUMA expression via NF-κB, and triggers the PERK-CHOP pathway. The cumulative effects of these processes contribute to reduced cell growth, increased apoptosis levels, and depletion of stem cells. Research indicates that LPS activates the TLR4-p38 signaling pathway, which, through a non-degradative mechanism, inhibits Sp1 binding to the SIGIRR promoter, leading to the downregulation of SIGIRR and the subsequent release of inhibition on the TLR4 signaling pathway [15]. Furthermore, hypoxia enhances the transcriptional expression of TLR4 and its associated proteins MyD88 and TRIF in intestinal stem cells via HIF-1α. The activation of TLR4 by MyD88 operates through a reverse mechanism that sustains HIF-1α expression, thereby forming a bidirectional positive feedback loop [16].

A provincialization mechanism operates within the embryonal brain data, suggesting the existence of embryonal biases. Sequencing of 18 preterm infants with NEC above stage II revealed that 10 cases carried loss-of-function variants in SIGIRR, significantly higher than that observed in typical neonates [17]. Functional validation demonstrated that these variants abolished SIGIRR's capacity to repress LPS-induced NF-κB activation and the production of IL-6, IL-8, and inducible nitric oxide synthase. The persistent signaling of TLR4 experienced by patients with TRAPS is attributed to abnormal post-transcriptional regulation mediated by microRNAs. Furthermore, a mutation in TNFRSF1A facilitates the activation of IRE1, leading to the accumulation of unfolded proteins. Consequently, these patients exhibit significant down-regulation of miR-146a and miR-155 [18]. The two miRNAs under investigation may negatively impact the TLR4 pathway by targeting the TRAF2, TAB2, and IKKε protein complexes; their deletion directly induces hyper-reactivity of macrophages to LPS. In LPS-induced MC3T3-E1 cells, the TLR4 promoter region is in a deinhibited state due to DNMT1-mediated reduction in DNA methylation [19], resulting in uncontrolled TLR4 transcription levels. The upregulation of TLR4 cooperatively facilitates NLRP3 inflammasome activation, promoting pyroptosis and the secretion of inflammatory factors through NF-κB pathway activation. The dysregulation of SIGIRR, down-regulation of miRNAs, and defects in DNA methylation collectively illustrate that the TLR4 signaling pathway is negatively regulated at the levels of protein, post-transcription, and transcription. This negative regulation constitutes a crucial mechanism underlying the loss of control over inflammation observed in infectious diseases (Fig. 2).

The cascade mechanism of TLR4 signaling activation destroying the intestinal barrier

Direct injury: TLR4 signaling induces apoptosis and necrosis of intestinal epithelial cells

The dysfunction of the TLR4 negative regulation pathway exacerbates intestinal septic injury via the MyD88-dependent pathway through the activation of apoptosis. Upon LPS stimulation, TLR4 binds to MyD88, initiating the activation of NF-κB, which promotes inflammation and recruits a storm of pro-inflammatory cytokines. This process particularly stimulates the production of IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α, leading to the upregulation of the pro-apoptotic protein Bax, while simultaneously activating caspases-3, -8, and -9, and downregulating the anti-apoptotic protein Bcl-2. Consequently, this cascade results in high levels of cell death among the intestinal epithelial cells, leading to the collapse of the intestinal barrier [20]. Additionally, pathways dependent on TRIF induce necroptosis due to defective RIPK1 death domain function. The introduction of the RIPK1 mutation R588E renders RIPK1 hypersensitive to ZBP1 and TRIF-mediated signals, resulting in perinatal lethal necrosis and adult inflammatory pathology. The activation of MLKL occurs through TRADD-dependent necroptosis signaling mediated by RIPK3, which operates via RHIM domain targeting [21]. The NF-κB-mediated pro-inflammatory response, triggered by the reactive oxygen speciesand reactive nitrogen speciesburst activated by TLR4 in macrophages, culminates in the simultaneous release of nitric oxide and superoxide anion, producing peroxynitrite that induces cell oxidation [22]. The Tau protein undergoes abnormal aggregation, leading to endoplasmic reticulum stress and subsequent activation of the PERK, IRE1, and ATF6-mediated unfolded protein response signaling pathways. Excessive UPR signaling further exacerbates endoplasmic reticulum dysfunction through increased JNK signaling. Ultimately, prolonged endoplasmic reticulum stress results in apoptosis of the affected cells [23].

Barrier structure disruption: degradation and redistribution of tight junction proteins

The activation of TLR4 signaling by LPS triggers an excessive pro-inflammatory response mediated by NF-κB, characterized by elevated levels of tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), interleukin-1 beta (IL-1β), and interleukin-6 (IL-6), alongside the down-regulation of tight junction proteins such as ZO-1, claudin-5, and occludin. This cascade ultimately impairs the integrity of the intestinal barrier during sepsis. The improper activation of the myosin light chain kinase/myosin light chain (MLCK/MLC) signaling pathway is attributed to TLR4 defects. Such defects can reverse alterations in tight junction protein levels and ameliorate dysbiosis of the intestinal flora [24]. In IBD, TLR4 activation stimulates MLCK through p38 mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK)-dependent phosphorylation, which promotes MLC phosphorylation and cytoskeletal rearrangement, leading to the disruption of TJs and facilitating the endocytic and lysosomal degradation of occludin and claudin-1, as well as the opening of cellular bypass channels. In vitro studies indicate that Modified Baitouweng decoction can inhibit the adhesion pathway in a dose-dependent manner and restore the expression of tight junction proteins [25]. LPS has the capacity to activate the TLR4-NADPH oxidase 1 (NOX1)/reactive oxygen species (ROS) signaling pathway. Following LPS treatment, the expression of occludin and ZO-1 is downregulated, and their polarized localization within the apical membrane is disrupted, resulting in the aberrant translocation of barrier proteins into the cytoplasm.

The reduction of TLR4 or NOX1 induced by shRNA may enhance the expression of ZO-1 and occludin, thereby confirming that this axis serves as the primary pathway regulating the endocytic redistribution of TJ proteins [26]. Bisphenol P establishes a positive feedback loop between TLR4 and the intestinal barrier through two mechanisms. One mechanism involves the disruption of intestinal flora, leading to an increased proportion of LPS-producing bacteria. Excessive LPS activates the TLR4/MyD88/NF-κB pathway, which downregulates tight junction proteins and increases barrier permeability, thus facilitating the translocation of additional LPS into the bloodstream to further activate TLR4 and perpetuate a pathological cycle [27]. Cytomegalovirus infection elevates TLR4 expression in the intestinal epithelium through TLR4 promoter hypomethylation, resulting in mitochondrial dysfunction and redox stress, thereby exacerbating NEC severity. Mice with TLR4 knockout specifically in the intestinal epithelium completely prevent the development of NEC, indicating that TLR4 is a crucial mediator in the CMV-NEC pathological axis [28]. The survival rate of NEC mice increased to 80%, and the RIPK1/RIPK3/MLKL programmed necrosis pathway was effectively inhibited following TLR4 gene knockout, resulting in decreased intestinal epithelial cell death and improved Th17/Treg balance, as well as tight junction protein expression. MyD88 activates the TNFR1-RIPK1 pathway [29] through TNF-α production via NF-κB, while TLR4 activates necrosis through TRIF activation. The expression of TLR4 in the lymphoid tissue of horse IBD rectal mucosa is significantly elevated, leading to excessive activation of Th17 cells. TLR4 activation induced by dendritic cells promotes the production of IL-23, which facilitates the differentiation of Th17 CD4+ T cells. Excessive IL-17a enhances the migration of neutrophils across the epithelium, resulting in the release of toxic molecules that directly kill cells and compromise the intestinal barrier [30] (Fig. 3).

Protective regulatory mechanism: the “natural brake system” of TLR4 signaling

The endogenous anti-inflammatory mechanism of the TLR4 signaling pathway involves the negative regulation of the TLR4 receptor through the competitive activation of receptors or molecular chaperones.

Competitive inhibitory receptors

TLR9 recognizes CpG-DNA and activates an anti-inflammatory signaling program via the MyD88-dependent pathway, utilizing shared downstream adaptor molecules of the TLR4-MyD88-NF-κB axis with opposing effects, thereby competitively inhibiting TLR4-driven pro-inflammatory signaling. NOD2 acts as a pattern recognition receptor, recognizing the bacterial molecule NOD2 to recruit downstream signaling protein RIPK2, which also inhibits TLR4 function, thus maintaining intestinal immunity and preventing TLR4 signaling initiation by commensal bacteria (LPS). Loss-of-function mutations in NOD2 hinder this negative regulation, rendering individuals susceptible to Crohn's disease. Hsp70, a stress-responsive protein, interacts with TLR4 to transmit signals. Under certain conditions, extracellular Hsp70, secreted via vesicular transport, can upregulate glucocorticoid receptor and DUSP1 in a TLR4-dependent manner [31]. The latter directly obstructs the phosphorylation of ERK1/2, JNK, and other MAPK, significantly decreasing the secretion of IL-6 and IL-8, thereby creating an anti-inflammatory regulatory circuit [32]. These competitive brakes are developmentally underdeveloped in preterm infants, limiting their efficacy against TLR4 hyperactivation.

Intracellular negative regulatory network

A20 inhibits the activation of NF-κB signaling. In preterm infants, the level of A20 in the intestines is approximately 50% lower than in term infants, leading to persistent ubiquitination of TRAF6 and subsequent initiation of the downstream TAK1-IKK-NF-κB cascade. This deficiency of A20 disrupts the negative feedback mechanism of TLR4 signaling. The intracellular TIR domain of SIGIRR competitively binds to MyD88, inhibiting its interaction with activated TLR4. Treatment with chitosan/pUNO-SIGIRR nanoparticles in a DSS colitis model [33] significantly upregulated SIGIRR expression in the colonic mucosa, reduced the levels of TLR4, MyD88, and NF-κB p65, and attenuated the inflammatory response. We will provide further insights into this topic in the future. The phosphorylation of GSK-3β through PI3K/Akt activation may inhibit this protein, thereby targeting the TLR4/MyD88/NF-κB signaling pathway. The compound PHI inhibits GSK-3β via activation of this pathway, exhibiting anti-inflammatory and anti-apoptotic effects in diabetic nephropathy models [34]. Following PHI therapy, Akt is activated, resulting in the phosphorylation and inactivation of GSK-3β, which directly reduces the expression of TLR4 and its downstream proteins MyD88 and NF-κB p65, thereby inhibiting the increase of inflammatory factors induced by high glucose. Collectively, these intracellular checkpoints are quantitatively or functionally deficient in the premature gut, where A20 levels are ∼50% lower than in term infants and SIGIRR loss-of-function variants are significantly enriched, leaving TLR4 signaling unchecked.

Regulation of microbial metabolites

TLR4 and gut flora engage in bidirectional crosstalk: dysbiosis increases LPS load to activate TLR4, while sustained TLR4 signaling alters the intestinal microenvironment and exacerbates microbial imbalance. Preterm infants are particularly vulnerable because delayed colonization by commensals reduces metabolite-mediated suppression. TLR4 expression is downregulated by butyrate through histone deacetylase inhibition. In a colorectal cancer model, sodium butyrate prevents M2 macrophage polarization by downregulating HDAC1, TLR4, and MyD88, demonstrating an anti-tumor effect comparable to that of aspirin [35]. To achieve a synergistic anti-tumor effect with PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors, NaB inhibits the expression of PD-L1 in tumor cells and the polarization of M2-like macrophages. Indole-3-propionic acidindirectly regulates TLR4-mediated inflammatory signaling by activating the aryl hydrocarbon receptor. IPA stably binds to AKT1 and cross-inhibits TLR4 downstream signaling pathways, including NF-κB1 and MAPK8, through the PI3K/AKT pathway [36]. The restoration of AKT1 levels can inhibit competition with LPS-producing bacteria and reduce the TLR4 ligand load from the source. Enhanced regulation of lipid metabolism occurs through PPARγ activation due to AhR activation. The intestinal microecological barrier can be established by human milk oligosaccharides, as they promote the colonization of beneficial bacteria, competitively inhibit the overgrowth of LPS-producing pathogens, and indirectly inhibit the over-activation of TLR4 [37]. HMOs directly inhibit the TLR4/NF-κB signaling axis in the prevention of NEC by downregulating membrane-bound TLR4, inhibiting the phosphorylation of NF-κB p65, activating the NLRP3 inflammasome, and reducing TLR4 ligand load through the remodeling of the flora structure. Collectively, they systematically reduce the levels of inflammatory factors and promote epithelial proliferation and repair (Fig. 4).

TLR4 signaling precision regulation strategies.

Novel therapeutic strategies based on TLR4 regulation

Drug interventions that accurately target TLR4 signaling

Two small molecule Flt 3 inhibitors, DCAM3 and DCAM4, serve as potent modulators of innate immune cell function and effectively attenuate intestinal inflammation in a DSS colitis model [38]. Both inhibitors suppress TLR4/TLR9-induced production of TNFα, IL-6, and IL-1β, while enhancing IL-10 expression. Specifically, DCAM3 promotes immune tolerance by increasing plasmacytoid dendritic cells (pDCs), whereas DCAM4 inhibits the infiltration of inflammatory macrophages. TAK-242, a selective TLR4 inhibitor, has been shown to reduce disease severity by up to 60% in the NEC model. Additionally, MDP regulates intestinal microbiota and the inflammatory response in an LPS model by activating the TLR4/NFκB pathway [39], which down-regulates the expression of pro-inflammatory mediators such as IL-1β, IL-6, TNFα, and iNOS. This modulation further contributes to the injury of multiple organs, enhances intestinal barrier function, reduces oxidative stress markers such as malondialdehyde (MDA), and increases antioxidant enzyme activities. Upon oral administration, gold nanoparticles (AuNPs) interact with macrophages, specifically targeting the TLR4 protein on the macrophage membrane through an endocytosis and co-internalization pathway. This interaction facilitates the decomposition of peroxy-nitrite and hydrogen peroxide, subsequently inhibiting LPS-induced NF-κB activation and the secretion [22] of IL-6 and TNF-α. Furthermore, hydroxyethyl starch-curcumin nanoparticles (HES-CUR NPs) activate the Nrf2/HO-1 antioxidant axis and synergistically inhibit the TLR4/MyD88/NF-κB pathway, thereby restoring the expression of tight junction proteins [40]. However, it is important to note that AuNPs can systematically diminish gut flora diversity and the abundance of beneficial bacteria, leading to intestinal flora dysbiosis. Locally administering TLR4 siRNA nanoparticles effectively silences TLR4 expression in the intestinal epithelium, presenting a novel approach for precise intervention.

Biological agents and cell therapy

FP7, a competitive TLR4 antagonist of MD-2, enhances antagonistic activity by approximately tenfold through alterations in the aggregation state of sugars and lipids when co-treated with cationic antimicrobial peptides, significantly inhibiting IL-1β production [41]. The acid-hydrolysis silk peptide regulates the TLR4-mediated innate immune response through multiple targets. In this model, SP significantly reduces the expression levels of TLR4, IRAK4, TRAF6, and TAK1 in the spleen and lymph nodes. Moreover, it blocks the phosphorylation of the NF-κB and MAPK pathways while concurrently decreasing serum pro-inflammatory factor levels [42]. SP exhibits a concentration-dependent inhibitory effect in vitro. Additionally, it demonstrates antioxidant properties by upregulating the expression of SOD1 and GPx 1 and reducing levels of ROS and NO. The blockade of TLR4 signaling by HMOs, such as 2′-fucosyllactose and 6′-sialyllactose, results in intestinal and brain protection against both enteral and parenteral LPS, thereby reducing the incidence of NEC and brain injury [43]. The metabolites of HMOs enhance blood-brain barrier integrity by upregulating TJPs at the blood-brain barrier. Clinical evidence indicates an inverse relationship between HMO concentrations in breast milk and the incidence of NEC in infants.

Nutritional intervention strategies

MAMP-1, a biologically active peptide isolated from the milk of premature infants, functions as an inhibitor of TLR4. Furthermore, it has the potential to repair the intestinal barrier by upregulating ZO-1 and inhibiting cell apoptosis through the regulation of the Bax/Bcl-2 ratio [44]. Additionally, MAMP-1 may enhance the structure of the gut microbiota, thereby reducing the abundance of pathogenic bacteria. The incorporation of n-3 polyunsaturated fatty acids, which include EPA and DHA, inhibits the nuclear translocation and extracellular secretion of HMGB1. It also obstructs the high-affinity binding between HMGB1 and RAGE as well as TLR4, ultimately diminishing the activation of the NF-κB pathway [45].

Clinical translational challenges and future directions

Therapeutic targeting of TLR4 faces the inherent challenges associated with precision medicine. This is exemplified by TLR4 gene polymorphisms that influence susceptibility to inflammatory diseases. Similar variability is observed in the white blood cell levels and gender distribution among patients with acute non-biliary pancreatitis [46]. In other words, polymorphisms may lead to distinct clinical responses among patients from different genetic backgrounds. The MyD88-independent pathway primarily contributes to term delivery, whereas preterm birth involves both MyD88-dependent and MyD88-independent pathways [47].

However, the therapeutic applicability of TLR4 antagonists is not uniform across preterm infants; it must account for the developmental stage of the intestine. In immature preterm gut, TLR4 not only mediates inflammatory injury but also physiologically regulates epithelial proliferation, cytoskeletal remodeling, and barrier maturation. Systemic TLR4 blockade during this critical developmental window risks disrupting these constructive roles. As gestational age advances and the intestinal barrier matures—accompanied by the progressive establishment of negative regulatory networks such as A20 and SIGIRR—the balance shifts from developmental dependence toward inflammatory vulnerability, permitting more selective TLR4 modulation rather than full antagonism. Therefore, rather than indiscriminate application across all preterm infants, TLR4-targeted strategies should be tailored to the developmental stage of the intestine, preserving the physiological functions of TLR4 in immature gut development while curbing its pathological hyperactivation.

Peptides and their derivatives, including antimicrobial peptides and silk peptides, represent a significant area of research for the treatment of NEC through the multi-target regulation of the TLR4 signaling pathway. For instance, AMPs enhance the antagonistic activity of TLR4, while acid-hydrolyzed silk peptides exhibit antioxidant activity by blocking the TLR4-NF-κB/MAPK signaling cascade, thereby inhibiting the production of inflammatory mediators. Glycopeptides present in human milk oligosaccharides have the potential to reduce the risk of NEC incidence and brain injury by acting on the TLR4 signaling pathway, which is associated with improved intestinal barrier functions and neuroprotective effects. Utilizing biological agents and peptides derived from natural food sources for the treatment of NEC adopts a multispecies approach. In the future, there is potential for greater clinical transformation through the use of these or combinations of new foodborne peptides.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that neonatal NEC can be effectively prevented by targeting TLR4 signaling pathways. The intestinal epithelium of preterm infants exhibits constitutively high expression of TLR4. The combination of LPS activates nuclear factor kappa-light-chain-enhancer of activated B cells (NF-κB) via the MyD88/TRIF dual pathway, leading to the degradation of tight junction proteins, apoptosis and necrosis of intestinal epithelial cells, and disorders in goblet cell differentiation. This cascade results in bacterial translocation, creating a self-amplifying cycle characterized by “TLR4-barrier damage-microbial translocation.” Consequently, the intestinal barrier collapses in multiple dimensions. It is important to exercise caution regarding the role of TLR4 in the physiological context of intestinal development and immune homeostasis, as systemic inhibition of TLR4 may compromise host defense mechanisms. Therefore, therapeutic strategies should shift from a paradigm of “full blockade” to one of “precise regulation,” targeting specific cell types, disease stages, and signal intensities to preserve beneficial physiological functions. Future efforts should focus on developing novel nano-delivery systems aimed at intestinal epithelial cells, exploring the potential of bioactive peptides, probiotic metabolites, and other regulatory molecules, utilizing genetic screening to identify high-risk individuals, and establishing a system for precise prevention and treatment based on TLR4 signal dynamics to overcome current challenges in NEC management.
