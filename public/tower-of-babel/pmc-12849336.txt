Listening to the voices of healthcare providers involved in the design of a new birthing room: an interpretative phenomenological analysis
Authors: Anwar Nader AlKhunaizi, Areej Ghalib Al-Otaibi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBirth is a process encompassing neurohormonal, physiological, and psychological elements, primarily controlled by oxytocinergic mechanisms. Neurohormonal processes can be negatively impacted by external environments, particularly unknown and stressful rooms that trigger the sympathetic nervous system, suppressing endogenous oxytocin release. This suppression can weaken or even halt labor contractions. Alternative birthing rooms can alleviate stress from traditional clinical settings, encourage natural childbirth, and allow healthcare providers to better observe and respond to the experiences and needs of mothers.MethodsAn interpretative phenomenological qualitative approach was conducted in a government hospital in the Eastern Province of Saudi Arabia. Data collection involved conducting semi-structured interviews with 15 participants. The sample consisted of physicians, and midwives, ensuring a diverse range of perspectives.ResultsAn interpretative phenomenological analysis was conducted for data analysis. One core category ‘Supportive Birth Environment for Natural Childbirth’ with four themes emerged from data analysis. The four themes are: living the contradiction: when physical space constrains professional values; advancing privacy via the new design; Impact of birthing environment design on the professional practices and responsibilities of midwives; and practical suggestions and strategic recommendations.ConclusionAll participants demonstrated awareness of the potential advantages of the new birthing rooms design as perceived by healthcare providers in supporting maternal well-being. They also noted observations of maternal responses, which can help contribute to a positive experience during their hospital stay. However, additional efforts are required to deliver messages to the decision-makers and responsible authorities at the Ministry of Health to foster their understanding of the importance of redesigning the birthing room at the national and international levels.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-025-13950-9.

Background

Birth is a process which encompasses neurohormonal, physiological and psychological elements, but is mainly controlled by oxytocinergic mechanisms. The latter are affected by sensory stimuli for example those coming from the uterus and the support which is offered by a birth companion. Neurohormonal processes can be impacted negatively by the external environment, since an unknown and stressful environment can trigger the sympathetic nervous system, and thus repress the amount of endogenous oxytocin being released. This, in turn, can weaken or even stop labor contractions. In contrast, women who give birth in a safe, peaceful and private room will experience lower levels of stress, which will improve the release of endogenous oxytocin and thereby increase the likelihood of a relaxed and efficient labor [1, 2]. Townsend et al. [3] state that there is a growing body of evidence which supports the view that the birth environment plays a major role in determining how women experience labor, birth and the practice of midwifery.

According to Nielsen and Overgaard [4], alternative birth environments can not only reduce the effects of negative healthcare, but can also result in women having a positive perception of patient-centered care and its capacity to meet the health and social care needs of future generations. Goldkuhl et al. [5] note that there is evidence which shows that hospital birthing room which have been designed to provide a comforting and familiar space, with supportive multisensory features such as calming lighting and sounds, and cozy furniture, make it less likely that women will request epidural analgesia in labor. Wronding et al. [6] add that this type of environment also lowers cesarean birth rates. Research into the design of birthing room is a relatively new and exciting development in the study of obstetric care, its risks and technological tools, in many countries. Andrén et al. [7] and Hodnett et al. [8] note that this fresh perspective is mirrored in how birthing room are designed and equipped. Bowden et al. [9] carried out a groundbreaking study which examined internet images of birthing room found in developed countries. Their research identified three different styles of room, and offered us a new understanding of these settings. The researchers found that approximately 50 per cent of the rooms prioritized technology, placing an electrically-operated birthing bed in the middle of the room, and a range of technical equipment- such as a cardiotocography machine (CTG), neonatal resuscitation unit, and surgical lamp -where the mother could see them. The second type of birthing room was less common and aimed to create a homely atmosphere, by using warm colors and lighting and comfortable furniture, and not placing the bed and the equipment in the middle of the room. Finally, a third type of birthing room used features of both forms, and although it usually situated the bed in the middle, the room also featured comfortable furniture and splashes of color [9]. These findings should act as a stimulus to undertaking further studies of birthing rooms and their functions from a new perspective. However, Lorentzen et al. [10] point out that many birthing rooms which feature in trials of the impact of birth environment on birth outcomes also reflect a number of other important features, such as continuity of care, in their organizational model, and this also has a positive impact on birth outcomes. Understanding the barriers faced by midwives within the birth environment that impede their ability to deliver best practices in intrapartum care is essential for ensuring women receive optimal support during childbirth. It is advisable to adopt a comprehensive strategy that addresses environmental factors within the birth unit [11]. For instance, integrating audiovisual enhancements such as natural images and sounds into the hospital setting may facilitate physiological labor in women who are at low risk of complications. These sensory improvements have been demonstrated to enhance women’s birth experiences and assist midwives in delivering woman-centered care [12]. At present it is clear that there is a knowledge gap and a need for further scientific evidence of how the birth environment affects labor, with particular emphasis on the role of the birthing room [13, 14]. Nilsson et al. [13] conducted a literature review into how birthing room design influences maternal and newborn outcomes, highlighting four themes: means of distraction, comfort and relaxation; raising the temperature in the birthing room; familiar features in the setting; and reducing the technocratic emphasis in the environment. The review concluded that healthcare professionals, architects and researchers all have a key role to play in influencing the design of birthing rooms, to support the health of birthing women and their babies, above all in hospital labor wards [13].

Nilsson et al. [13] underscored the importance of studying and determining the long-term effects of the design of the birthing rooms on how women experience childbirth. Given the lack of comprehensive information on this topic, it is vital to ensure that researchers carry out comprehensive research in this area. Since care providers can shape the healthcare environment through their presence, approach, support, and actions, they are influential in determining the quality of care delivered [15, 16]. Therefore, it is essential that the birth environment design supports maternity care professionals’ ability to provide personalized care and simultaneously addresses women’s emotional responses to the physiological process of birth. Healthcare providers’ perspectives on birthing rooms design are crucial because they directly influence how the environment supports natural labor and delivery processes. Their insights on how design elements can facilitate or hinder natural childbirth, enables them to recommend modifications that promote a supportive. These recommendations have practical implications, ensuring that the environment enhances the natural birth experience and aligns with evidence-based practices. This makes their input vital for developing birth environments that are both clinically effective and psychologically supportive, benefiting both mothers and healthcare professionals. Research on healthcare providers’ perspectives regarding the design of birthing room and its impact on mothers’ natural instinctive childbirth experiences is lacking. To address this gap, this study employed an interpretative phenomenological approach to explore how healthcare providers who play a crucial role in supporting and guiding mothers during childbirth view the design of birthing room and how these designs can influence mothers’ natural birth experiences throughout their childbirth journey. This study sets out to gain a detailed understanding of how healthcare providers in one setting viewed a newly renovated birthing rooms, and how healthcare providers believe it may influence their ability to support mothers during natural childbirth. The findings of this study support the redesign of birthing rooms in all hospitals to support low-risk mothers in having a natural, instinctive childbirth.

Method

Design

The present study utilized an interpretative phenomenological analysis (IPA) design to gain valuable insights into healthcare providers’ perceptions of the new birthing room and its influence on mothers’ natural instinctive childbirth. According to Smith and Nizza [17], interpretative phenomenological analysis focuses on uncovering personal lived experiences and engagement in specific events. In this study, the phenomenon is defined as healthcare providers’ lived experiences regarding how the newly renovated birthing room affects their practices and influences mothers’ instinctive experiences during childbirth. Heidegger emphasized that the key question to consider is “What is being?” He argued that interpretation and comprehension are more significant than merely describing human experiences. Heidegger posited that lived experiences are inherently interpretive. According to him, hermeneutics a fundamental aspect of human existence relates to the art and philosophy of interpreting the meaning of objects. The main objectives of conducting interpretative phenomenological studies are to immerse oneself in another person’s world and explore the practical knowledge and possibilities that emerge from it [18]. This approach was selected as it effectively describes, interprets, and understands participants lived experiences. IPA was selected because healthcare providers’ experiences working within different physical environments constitute embodied professional phenomena. Midwives and physicians don’t simply observe birthing rooms as neutral spaces they live within these environments for extended periods, and the physical space shapes their embodied practice, professional identity, and ability to enact their values of supporting natural birth. The phenomenon under investigation is: how healthcare providers experience the relationship between physical environment and their professional practice of supporting physiological birth. This is a legitimate phenomenological inquiry because it explores how professionals make sense of how space constrains or enables their practice, affects their professional satisfaction, and influences their ability to provide the care they value. The combination of physicians and midwives provides complementary perspectives on the same phenomenon working within birthing environments while maintaining sufficient homogeneity in their shared experience of providing intrapartum care.”

Setting

The participants were selected from a governmental hospital in Eastern Province, Saudi Arabia. The hospital, which has a 400-bed capacity and serves approximately one thousand mothers to give birth monthly, is considered the largest in the area. It is recognized as a mother baby family friendly hospital as it provides maternal care services with high standards and quality [19]. This hospital underwent full implementation of renovations in the birthing facilities, significantly enhancing maternal comfort and care. Before the renovation, the laboring rooms were designed to accommodate two mothers simultaneously. However, they lacked sufficient space for companions to sit comfortably or for mothers to move freely and perform exercises during labor. Additionally, after completion of the first stage of labor, mothers were transferred to a separate birthing room for the actual birth. The birthing room contained only a birth bed, with limited space for movement or supportive activities. Following the renovation, significant modifications were made to enhance the birthing room. The updated labor and birthing rooms accommodate a single laboring mother with her chosen companion. Labor and birth occur in the same room. This redesign provides ample space for movement, both on and off the bed, with room for floor mats or birth ball exercise, and space for relaxation during labor, promoting a more supportive and empowering environment for the mother. The renovated space is both about increasing comfort and also about improving maternal outcomes, through supporting natural labor processes, and facilitating better outcomes.

Participants and recruitment

The study population consisted of two groups: Seven physicians (including obstetricians and gynecologists), and eight midwives. The inclusion criteria included healthcare providers with English fluency, a minimum of five years of experience in maternity units, and those who worked in the birthing rooms. These criteria aimed to capture diverse and comprehensive lived experiences related to birthing room. Although Arabic is the official language in Saudi Arabia, the interviews were conducted in English, the official language used by physicians and midwives in hospitals. Before the interviews, participants were provided with a brief orientation about the research topic and the aim of clear communication and accurate data collection. This pre-interview briefing played a crucial role in ensuring all participants understood the purpose of the interview, the types of questions they’d be asked, and the overall process, thereby minimizing confusion. We adapted a purposeful sampling strategy to identify and select information-rich cases and individuals especially knowledgeable about the phenomenon of interest [20].

The data saturation approach was used to determine the required sample size; data collection was stopped when additional participants no longer provided further insight [21]. Smith and Nizza [17] point out that the ideal sample size is not a fixed number, but rather depends on the researcher’s commitment to conducting a thorough analysis and enhancing the richness of each transcript. In the context of IPA, a focus on a small number of cases is typically considered sufficient [17]. In this study, interviews were conducted until saturation was reached, meaning no new information could be gathered. The study consisted of two groups: seven physicians and eight midwives. Participants were recruited until no new ideas were evident in the interviews (n = 13). We assessed saturation by documenting when each theme first emerged across interviews conducted in chronological order [see Supplementary Table S1]. By interview 13, no new themes emerged, and interviews 14–15 reinforced existing themes without adding new dimensions. While IPA emphasizes idiographic depth over saturation per se, this stability across cases indicated we had captured the range of perspectives within this specific context. The participants were purposively selected based on their renovated working place in the same department and shared experience, which enabled them to answer the same research questions related to the phenomena under study. The use of Narratives (Filed Notes) provided detailed descriptions, and human judgment played a crucial role during the interpretation stage. To ensure the reliability of the results, a subset of interviews was re-coded by us and cross-checked for consistency. No substantial divergence was observed, confirming the stability of the themes generated during the robust Interpretative Phenomenological analysis (IPA). We ensured diversity among the healthcare providers in terms of job titles, including managers, staff, residents, specialists, and consultants. A mix of male and female physicians and midwives held different positions. The study excluded healthcare providers who do not speak English fluently, those with less than five years of experience in maternity units, and those who have not worked in the birthing rooms.

Data collection

After two years from the renovation of the birthing room, an announcement about the coming study was placed on the hospital bulletin boards, and details were sent by email to the target population. Participants were also recruited by word-of-mouth strategy. The interviews were conducted individually in May 2024 to protect privacy and confidentiality. The primary researcher, an assistant professor skilled in qualitative research methods, conducted the interviews. Data were collected by the primary researcher, an assistant professor with expertise in qualitative research methods. A pilot study helped refine the interview questions for clarity and validity especially question number one and four. The questions were semi-structured, and the interviewer also had a list of points to cover see (Table 1).

The interviews took between 40 and 60 min and were individually conducted in the hospital’s consultation room to protect privacy and confidentiality, which was also convenient for the participants. To minimize recall bias, we employed several strategies: (1) We showed participants standardized photographs of the previous birthing room layout at the beginning of each interview to activate specific memories rather than relying on general impressions; (2) We asked participants to describe specific incidents and mothers interactions from the pre-renovation period rather than general evaluations; (3) We triangulated retrospective accounts with any available contemporaneous documentation such as incident reports, mothers feedback from that period. However, we acknowledge that the two-year interval between renovation and data collection was not ideal. This timing was due to renovation completion delays. Participants’ answers were recorded and supplementary questions such as “Why?”, “How?” and “What exactly do you mean?” were asked, to clarify and expand on the interviewees’ answers. Study trustworthiness

Four methods, including credibility, dependability, confirmability, and transferability, were employed to determine the quality of the collected qualitative data [22]. Member checking or participant checking was done, we conferred with the interviewees and requested feedback from their perspectives on the transcripts to ensure credibility, as the participants’ perspectives are crucial in judging the credibility of the data. In the present work, we conferred with the interviewees and requested feedback on the transcripts to ensure credibility. All the participants were satisfied with the transcripts provided to them. Bracketing was done through our honest self-examination of the beliefs, values, interests and prior experiences. These were noted down and kept at the back of the mind right from proposal development through data collection, analysis and report writing. This thorough process ensured that the findings were the views of the participants and not our imaginations, instilling confidence in the research’s credibility. Dependability, and conformability were ensured through inquiry audit where by our reviewed and inspected the research process and the data analysis in order to ensure that the findings were consistent. To ensure confirmability, participants were asked to check and recheck the narrative data throughout the study, thereby ensuring the accuracy that reflected their true feelings and opinions. The research participants in this study were satisfied that the narratives reflected their true feelings and opinions. Finally, to assess transferability, we determined whether the results could be transferred to other contexts, such as different regions in Saudi Arabia. To assess the transferability in this study, the results can be applied in similar population characteristics such as other provinces in Saudi Arabia. By employing these methods, we were able to ensure the rigor of the findings.

Reflexivity

Reflexivity demonstrates high-quality, ethical inquiry and establishes the credibility and validity of the research process [23]. To maintain reflexivity, our research team has no connection to the selected hospital, and they consistently examined and discussed their viewpoints and opinions throughout the study, recognizing that beliefs, assumptions, and biases influence the thinking. We were acutely aware of our research roles, which guided our actions and decisions. We maintained rigor by staying grounded in the data, engaging in a reflexive approach, and conducting intersubjective checks to ensure that these influences minimized bias in the research outcomes [24]. To maintain the reliability of a study, Patton [25] suggested the use of reflexivity and bracketing, which to maintain the reliability of a study, Patton [25] recommended using reflexivity and bracketing to prevent personal biases from influencing the data. Additional techniques, such as triangulation incorporating multiple sources like audio recordings of interview responses, field notes, and participant observations further validated the study results [26]. We conducted in-depth, one-on-one interviews using semi-structured, open-ended questions.

Her deep understanding of maternal health, childbirth practices, and the challenges women face during and after delivery, gained from her extensive work in obstetrics and gynecology departments, is a testament to her credibility. This rich background, coupled with her teaching experience, has provided her with invaluable insights into the topic under study, thereby enhancing the interpretation of participants’ perspectives.

The second author (A.G.A.-O.) has academic experience in obstetrics and gynecology. Her expertise lies in teaching, research, and clinical training related to maternal and newborn well-being, equipping her with a strong foundation for engaging with the study’s subject matter. Her professional role allowed her to contribute an informed perspective to the analysis and interpretation of the findings, ensuring that the research was grounded in both clinical realities and academic rigor. Analysis is inherently subjective, as it is influenced by our own experiences, contexts, beliefs, and assumptions, which shape their interpretations of the data and contribute to the cocreation of knowledge [17].

The lead researchers play a pivotal role in this study, holding both “insider” and “outsider” positions. These roles likely influenced the interviews, data analysis, and overall findings, ensuring a comprehensive approach to the research. The healthcare providers lived experiences likely enriched the critical exploration of the topic, stemming from their awareness of and interest in the subject under investigation. Researchers accomplish this by applying their subjective judgment while openly demonstrating how their preconceptions influence the knowledge generated, using personal reflexivity as a process of self-analysis and self-evaluation throughout the study. We were diligent in addressing potential biases and examining how the lead researcher’s personal experiences and reactions to the data influenced interpretation. This was achieved through the use of a reflective diary and ongoing discussions between us. These measures were taken to ensure the integrity of the participants’ experiences was preserved throughout the analysis, instilling confidence in the research process.

Data analysis

Once the recorded interviews were completed, they were transcribed verbatim. The data gathered from each participant was analyzed using an iterative approach, so that revisions could be made on the basis of earlier interpretations. The transcripts were entered into the NVivo 12 software, which labels each paragraph with phrases representing specific ideas. The first author analyzed data using the interpretative phenomenological analysis framework [27]. When all the interviews were recorded and transcribed, an interpretive, phenomenological analysis was undertaken [28]. The authors discussed, revised, and reflected on the interpretations, incorporating concepts from the healthcare providers’ views and perceptions of the new birthing room, and agreed on the themes. To enhance research transparency and ensure the trustworthiness of the analysis, emergent themes were validated through a two-step procedure: First, thematic consistency was reviewed through iterative reading and comparison across cases. Second, selected themes and subthemes were double-checked with an external qualitative research expert who conducted an independent review of a subset of the coded data. This process, with the expert’s autonomy, supported the credibility and confirmability of the thematic structure.

This form of methodology aims to discover the participants’ views and attitudes, and to gain a detailed and thorough understanding of their experiences by using an inductive, idiographic approach. Results

Sample characteristics

In this study, we identified 23 healthcare providers (11 physicians, 12 midwives) who had worked in the department both before and after renovation. Of these, 3 had left the institution and were contacted via personal mobile phones; all 3 declined participation citing time constraints or loss of interest in the topic. Of the 20 current staff invited, 15 agreed to participate (7 physicians, 8 midwives) working in the obstetrics and gynecology departments opted to take part in the interviews, yielding a response rate of 75% overall (64% physicians, 67% midwives). Non-participants did not differ substantially from participants in terms of observable characteristics (age, experience level, or position).

Idiographic analysis

To illustrate the depth and complexity of individual experiences, we first present Participant 1, a 55-year-old consultant in the Department of Obstetrics and Gynecology with over 30 years of professional expertise, who provided valuable insights drawn from her long tenure in the birthing rooms. Having worked in both the old and newly designed birthing rooms, she offered a rich and detailed account of the evolution in childbirth settings and practices. Her extensive experience allowed her to vividly describe past incidents and compare how the redesigned rooms have transformed her professional practice. From her perspective, the new birthing room design not only enhances her ability to provide care but also creates a more supportive and calming atmosphere that facilitates natural, instinctive childbirth, empowering mothers and improving their overall birthing experience. She emphasized that this transformation enhances the psychological well-being of mothers by promoting relaxation, privacy, and a sense of control during labor. The layout allows mothers to move freely, use different birthing positions, and engage their companions for emotional and physical support, which together encourage instinctive birthing responses, also highlighted features such as soft lighting, access to birthing aids, and aesthetic elements that serve as positive distractions, helping to reduce anxiety and pain perception. She highlighted the importance of having windows that allow natural sunlight to enter during the day, as this significantly contributes to the mother’s psychological well-being and sense of comfort. The inclusion of birth balls, ropes for exercises and squatting, and even warm-water pools provides mothers with options to promote active, instinctive labor and reduce the need for medical interventions. From her perspective, these thoughtful design elements not only improve maternal experiences and outcomes but also support healthcare professionals in managing their responsibilities more effectively, reducing stress, and helping address challenges such as midwife shortages.

Participant 8 is a 43-year-old midwife manager with 19 years of midwifery experience, including over 15 years working as a staff midwife in birthing rooms before transitioning into a managerial role. Drawing upon her extensive clinical and leadership background, she provided an in-depth reflection on the birthing rooms environment before and after its renovation, emphasizing how the physical design directly affects maternal well-being and midwifery practice. She described that, before renovation, birthing rooms were highly medicalized and restrictive. Mothers were often confined to the bed, connected to cardiotocography machines, and limited in their ability to move or adopt different positions, which contributed to increased stress and discomfort. After the redesign, however, the inclusion of birth balls, bath pools, and ropes for squatting exercises allowed mothers greater freedom of movement and pain relief. She noted that the presence of midwives who actively support natural childbirth also helped transform mothers’ experiences into more positive and empowering ones. From a technical perspective, Participant 8 advocated for several functional improvements, including wireless fetal heart monitors to support maternal mobility, adjustable air-conditioning systems to maintain comfort during different labor stages, and birth beds with supportive handles to facilitate multiple birthing positions. She also elaborated on the midwife’s supportive role in fostering positive birth experiences through holistic care practices, such as back massage, verbal encouragement, and offering dates and water for energy. In addition, she proposed the development of unified educational sessions for couples preparing for natural childbirth. These sessions, she suggested, should include visits to the delivery rooms and practical training before labor, allowing mothers to become familiar with the environment and confident in applying learned techniques during the birth process. In summary, she emphasized that a well-designed birthing environment, one that ensures privacy, comfort, freedom of movement, and emotional support, enhances the promotion of natural physiological birth, strengthens midwives’ capacity to provide woman-centered care, and contributes to positive birth experiences for mothers in hospital settings.

Semi-structured interview findings

As all of the group experience themes are related to the significance of redesigning hospital birthing rooms and its impacts on supporting and promoting normal and natural childbirth in low-risk mothers, they can all be grouped under the core category of Supportive Birth Environment for Natural Childbirth. Moreover, it is important to note here that the design of the birthing room also impacts mothers’ experiences and the roles of the midwives. This core category emphasizes the importance of creating a space that supports the physiological, psychological, and emotional needs of low-risk mothers, facilitating natural birth without unnecessary medical interventions. The themes derived from healthcare provider opinions reflect this emphasis. After examining the findings, four relevant themes and thirteen subthemes associated with the birthing room were identified. The four themes are: living the contradiction: when physical space constrains professional values; advancing privacy via the new design; impact of birthing environment design on the professional practices and responsibilities of midwives; and practical suggestions and strategic recommendations as presented in Table 4.

The first theme, ' Living the Contradiction: When Physical Space Constrains Professional Values,’ includes sub-themes such as the Meaning of Natural Childbirth, Previous Birthing Rooms Design, and Mothers’ Psychological Well-being. These elements illustrate how the participants believed that understanding natural childbirth and improving the physical environment can promote maternal confidence and reduce anxiety, encouraging natural birth. The second theme, ' Advancing Privacy via the New Design,’ explores how modifications like natural light, calming colors, and personal belongings contribute to a homely atmosphere that supports women’s comfort and privacy during labor. Its sub-themes; Suggested New Birthing Rooms Design, Controlling Labor, Companion Involvement, and Positive Distraction detail specific strategies to foster a supportive and empowering space. The third theme, ' Impact of birthing environment design on the professional practices and responsibilities of midwives,’ emphasizes how environmental factors influence healthcare providers’ ability to perform their duties effectively, with sub-themes addressing midwife responsibilities and the impact of midwife shortages. Finally, the theme’ Practical Suggestions and Strategic Recommendations’ consolidates healthcare providers’ insights into practical measures like birthing room visits, community awareness, policy advocacy, and provider training to optimize the birth environment. These interconnected core categories, along with their themes and sub-themes, illustrate healthcare providers’ perspectives on how a thoughtfully designed environment directly supports natural childbirth, enhances maternal well-being, and empowers them to deliver more natural, gentle care while advocating for policy changes that support these goals.

Core category with themes and sub-themes

Theme 1: living the contradiction: when physical space constrains professional values

Healthcare providers experienced the previous birthing rooms as creating an ongoing tension between their knowledge of best practices and what the physical environment permitted. This wasn’t merely inconvenience it represented a fundamental challenge to professional identity. After exploring the opinions of healthcare providers, this emerging theme was created to help low-risk mothers give birth to children naturally without needless medical procedures or updated room layouts. Healthcare providers described a profound tension between their professional values and the limitations imposed by the physical design of the previous birthing rooms. This theme reflects how the built environment not only influences care delivery but also poses challenges in supporting natural childbirth. Participants expressed that the design of the previous birthing rooms often hindered their ability to uphold best practices, such as encouraging maternal mobility, facilitating natural birthing positions, or providing a calm and supportive atmosphere. However, the previous birthing rooms design, with its clinical appearance, limited space, and restrictive equipment placement, was perceived as undermining these ideals. Providers felt that such environments contributed to increased maternal stress and negatively affected the mother’s psychological well-being. Ultimately, this theme illustrates how healthcare professionals “lived the contradiction” between their knowledge of what constitutes optimal, woman-centered care and the spatial realities that constrained it. The experience underscored the importance of redesigning birthing spaces to align with both professional values and the emotional and psychological needs of mothers during natural childbirth. Three sub-themes were covered in this theme: Meaning of Natural Childbirth, Previous Birthing Rooms Design, and Psychological Wellbeing of the Mother.

Meaning of natural childbirth

A natural childbirth typically follows a healthy pregnancy in mothers who have not experienced health issues or chronic diseases that could prevent such a childbirth, which was noted by many of the participants. Participants emphasized that it occurs when a woman is in good physical and psychological health, free from chronic illnesses or complications, and when labor begins naturally through normal cervical dilatation and progresses without postpartum bleeding or adverse incidents. They viewed natural childbirth as a process that unfolds instinctively and without complications, highlighting the importance of allowing the body to function according to its natural rhythms. The presence of a companion in the birthing room was seen by healthcare providers as essential to provide comfort and emotional reassurance, helping to sustain the natural flow of labor. Furthermore, natural childbirth was portrayed as a continuation of traditional birthing practices, an instinctive process that existed long before the widespread use of medical technologies and interventions. Which was noted by many of the participants. For example:

“A mother can have a natural childbirth in the ninth month of pregnancy if they do not have health problems or chronic illnesses that impact childbirth and commences the childbirth journey with a natural cervical dilatation without incident or postpartum bleeding.” (P1, Physician), natural or instinctive childbirth was described as a spontaneous and uncomplicated process that reflects the mother’s innate ability to give birth without unnecessary medical interventions.

Previous birthing rooms design

Participants were of the view that natural, instinctive childbirth was not promoted by the design of the previous birthing rooms and, in many ways, constrained their ability to support mothers effectively. The earlier layout often required two mothers to share the same room, negatively affecting privacy, limiting the presence of a companion, and disrupting the mother’s comfort during labor. The shared spaces and lack of personal, calming environments were perceived as barriers to creating a supportive atmosphere conducive to natural birth. Participants described the previous rooms as small, clinical spaces dominated by a single bed that restricted the mother to the lithotomy position throughout labor and delivery, leaving little or no room for movement or freedom of choice in birthing positions. The environment lacked essential facilities that encourage natural childbirth, such as ropes, birthing balls, chairs, or bath pools, which are often used to facilitate relaxation, mobility, and pain relief. Instead, the design reflected a highly medicalized approach to childbirth, where unnecessary interventions were common, such as early rupture of membranes and stimulation of labor with medications, leading to stressful and negative birthing experiences for mothers. Overall, the healthcare providers experienced the previous birthing rooms as creating an ongoing tension between their knowledge of best practices and what the physical environment permitted. For example:

As one midwife explained “A number of practices can promote natural instinctive birth. However, the previous design does not. This is because the previous design was a small room with a bed that only enables the mother to sleep in the lithotomy position throughout the delivery process.” (P11, Midwife), revealing how architectural features became barriers to enacting core professional values.

“In the previous hospital rooms, multiple women were placed in the same room. In turn, this adversely impacts the presence of a companion in the room and disrupts the comfort of the mother.” (P15, Midwife)

Psychological wellbeing of the mother

They explained that the physical environment directly affects the mother’s thoughts, emotions, and ability to cope with labor pain. A calm, aesthetically pleasing room can reduce anxiety and promote relaxation, helping mothers to experience childbirth more positively. Soft colors and appropriate brightness were seen as essential in creating a sense of calm and control, enabling the mother to manage pain and stress more effectively. Exposure to sunlight was viewed as beneficial for enhancing mood and supporting mental health during labor. A well-designed space that integrates daylight, soothing colors, and gentle lighting is believed to help the mother feel safe and emotionally balanced. Beyond aesthetics, participants also stressed the importance of creating a psychologically supportive environment through thoughtful spatial arrangements. The opportunity for the mother to move freely, breathe comfortably, and engage in light exercises within the room was described by healthcare providers as essential to fostering a positive birthing experience. Overall, the participants viewed the birthing room as a space that should nurture both the body and mind, support emotional security, reduce fear, and enhance the mother’s psychological well-being during one of the most significant experiences of her life. Theme 2. Advancing privacy via the new design

The second theme was established after examining the opinions of participants, who felt that a birthing room should feel comfortable and safe for women. They were of the view that a hospital-style room is not homely at all but is rather created from a business and care perspective, taking into account the risks and dangers of childbirth. A hospital room can never be truly homelike, but there are some features that can help to make the space more comfortable, such as adding natural features, light, and personal belongings. Light plays a key role here in establishing a cozy atmosphere. Dimmable lights produce a comfortable, serene, and enveloping atmosphere. A homely feeling can also be facilitated through bright colors on the walls, nice posters, or even green plants. Three sub-themes were identified alongside this main theme, namely Suggested New Birthing Rooms Design, Controlling the Labor, Companion Involvement, and Positive Distraction.

Suggested new birthing rooms design

All of the healthcare providers believed that the cozy features of the newly designed space facilitated a homely room that would be advantageous for expectant mothers. In turn, this generated a feeling of solitude, and the transfer from home to the hospital went well. Moreover, the mothers would likely have more confidence in the professional conclusions if they were made to feel at home in a familiar room. Furthermore, sufficient space in the birthing room encourages the mother to move around and decide on the positions that she feels most comfortable in during childbirth. For example, the placement of yoga mats, rope [suspended from the ceiling to support the mother to lean on and move more freely], and a bathtub [for water immersion to aid pain relief] in the room can be particularly effective. As mentioned by most of the participants, placing distracting pictures and wall speakers in the room, ensuring that there is effective and dimmable lighting, and taking into consideration the presence of soothing green plants also play a critical role here.

“It is important that the space reminds her of her home environment, facilitating total privacy. “It is important that we promote the use of birthing balls and a rope that enable mothers to perform exercises and squats. Controlling the labor

It is important that mothers have the capacity to control their own labor through having choices such as the ability to select the position that they find the most comfortable as perceived by healthcare providers. Participants emphasized that allowing mothers to have control over their labor is fundamental to promoting natural childbirth and enhancing the overall birthing experience. They described how the opportunity for mothers to make choices, particularly regarding movement, positioning, and comfort, plays a vital role in supporting both physiological and psychological aspects of labor. Having the freedom to select the most comfortable birthing position enables mothers to feel empowered and actively engaged in the birth process rather than being passive recipients of care. The new rooms, designed to accommodate one mother throughout the entire process of labor and delivery, provided privacy and flexibility, allowing women to move freely, exercise, and give birth in the same room. This continuous and private space encouraged a smoother, more natural progression of labor and was associated with positive birth experiences. The ability to stay in one room throughout labor and birth reduced unnecessary stress and interruptions, helping mothers remain focused and confident. These tools not only facilitate physical comfort but also encourage mothers to listen to their bodies and respond naturally to the sensations of labor as described by healthcare providers. By enabling women to adopt upright or alternative positions, the design supports active birthing practices that can ease labor and reduce the need for medical interventions. Overall, participants viewed maternal control during labor as central to empowering women and promoting natural, instinctive childbirth. For example:

“Once the birthing rooms had been redesigned, mothers were given a separate room to use throughout labor and delivery. “A birthing chair should be provided in the new rooms to afford mothers the freedom to take control of the labor by selecting the most comfortable birthing positions.” (P4, Physician), participants also highlighted the importance of providing appropriate equipment, such as birthing chairs, to support mothers in choosing the most comfortable and instinctive positions for labor and delivery.

Theme 3: impact of birthing environment design on the professional practices and responsibilities of midwives

The third theme referred to the influence that the birthing room design has on the role of the midwives. The design of the birthing environment significantly influences midwives’ ability to perform their professional roles and fulfill their responsibilities while supporting natural childbirth. A well-designed room facilitates the use of non-pharmacological approaches, such as massage, warm baths, breathing exercises, and natural aromatic scents like lavender, to promote spontaneous labor. Midwives provide emotional support and encouragement, remind mothers to breathe, offer positive reinforcement, and attend to their physical needs by providing water, fruit, or other sources of energy. Features such as birth balls, mats for squatting, and spaces for movement enhance the midwife’s ability to help mothers reduce discomfort and pain, improving the overall childbirth experience. Participants also emphasized that these benefits are closely linked to adequate staffing levels. A shortage of midwives limits the capacity to provide one-to-one care, which is essential for supporting low-risk mothers during natural childbirth. Ensuring sufficient midwife availability allows for personalized support, promotes instinctive labor, and enables the effective use of birthing aids and exercises. Increasing the number of midwives is therefore critical to fully realize the positive impact of a supportive birthing environment on maternal care. Overall, the findings highlight that birthing room design not only enhances midwives’ professional practice and responsibilities but also underscores the need for sufficient midwifery staffing to optimize maternal outcomes and facilitate natural childbirth. Two sub-themes have been identified for this primary theme, namely the midwife’s role and responsibility, and the Midwife Shortage.

This last theme examines the recommendations made by healthcare providers by highlights the practical suggestions and strategic recommendations offered by healthcare providers to enhance maternal care and promote natural childbirth. One key recommendation was to include real-life visits to birthing rooms in childbirth education classes. Healthcare providers also emphasized the importance of raising community awareness about the significance of birthing room design. Discussion

This study used an interpretative phenomenology research design to understand of how healthcare providers in one setting viewed a newly renovated birthing room, and how healthcare providers believe it may influence their ability to support mothers during natural childbirth. The findings included a core category of supportive birth environment for natural childbirth with four themes: living the contradiction: when physical space constrains professional values, impact of birthing environment design on the professional practices and responsibilities of midwives, and practical suggestions and strategic recommendations. The analysis yielded themes that provide insight into the importance of listening to the voices of healthcare providers who participated in a new birthing room design in Saudi Arabia. The findings show that the participants discussed natural childbirth and its meaning, as well as how birthing rooms are designed and how healthcare providers perceived these factors as potentially influencing maternal psychological well-being. Other studies have highlighted similar findings. A study showed that a calm, private, and safe environment can reduce stress and enhance the release of endogenous oxytocin, potentially leading to more comfortable and efficient labor, which leads to natural childbirth [2]. Similarly, specially designed hospital birthing rooms, which offer sensory elements such as dimmable, soft, comfortable furniture, and calming sounds, can reduce women’s requirements for epidural analgesia during labor [5] and lower cesarean birth rates [6]. Furthermore, women’s experiences of birth environment influence their mental health and well-being [29]. Also, Townsend et al. [3] state that there is a growing body of evidence which supports the view that the birth environment plays a major role in determining how women experience labor, birth and the practice of midwifery.

In most countries, homelike environment/family-centered care units are designed for uncomplicated deliveries. The evidence shows that despite the global efforts toward the development of physiologic birth in the maternity unit, which refers to a birth process that is not interfered with unless necessary, the design of the delivery environment still needs reforms.

A study in Nigeria showed that the staff’s desire and efforts to control the birth situation transferred staff stress to the parturient women [30]. According to Nielsen and Overgaard [4], alternative birth environments can not only reduce the effects of negative healthcare, but can also result in women having a positive perception of patient-centered care and its capacity to meet the health and social care needs of future generations. Additionally, our findings indicated that the presence of a hospital-style room is not homely at all but is rather created from a business and care perspective, taking into account the risks and dangers of childbirth. A hospital room can never be truly homelike, but some features can help make the space more comfortable, such as adding natural features, light, and personal belongings. Light plays a key role here in establishing a cozy atmosphere. Dimmable lights produce a comfortable, serene, and enveloping atmosphere. Some participants commented that a homely feeling can also be facilitated through bright colors on the walls, nice posters, or even green plants. Similarly, a previous study opened up new avenues for understanding and designing birthing rooms and concluded that a homelike birthing room was characterized by warm colors, warm lighting, and comfortable furniture; the bed and medical equipment were not placed in the center of the room [9]. Goldkuhl et al., [5] also found that specially designed hospital birthing rooms with comfortable furniture can reduce women’s requirements for epidural analgesia during labor. Wronding et al. [6] add that this type of environment also lowers cesarean birth rates. Moreover, in the birthing room, the birth spaces’ psychological and sensory features can influence health outcomes and affect physiological birth by limiting interventions [31, 32].

As well, the authors demonstrated that some participants mentioned a recommended birthing room design, which included a coffee machine, soft bed, and chair to encourage mothers and their companions to participate in instinctual and natural childbirth. Similarly, a study conducted by AlKhunaizi et al., [33] showed that the active participation of birth attendants and providing adequate support and resources can help enhance mothers’ well-being and contribute to a positive experience during their hospital stay. The concept of the influence of birthing room design on midwives’ roles emerged as a key theme in our study. Midwives spend a large proportion of their working hours in the birthing room, which thus represents an important component of their physical working environment [34]. The participants referred to the influence that the birthing room design has on the role of the midwives such as the midwife’s role and responsibility, and the midwife shortage. According to a study conducted by Townsend and colleagues, the birth environment can influence women’s experiences of labor and birth and midwifery practice [3]. Moreover, a study showed that insufficient numbers of midwives in the workplace increased the effort necessary to complete their work, the general workload, and the time required to fulfill their responsibilities [16]. However, these findings contradict another study that showed that midwives were less likely to agree that they had everything they needed in case of an emergency and were also less likely to react appropriately in an emergency [21]. Goldkuhl et al. [15] also noted uncertainties in addition to the positive effects of redesigning birthing spaces. The uncertainties related to task performance may have a negative impact on midwives’ job satisfaction, while the positive effects could include improved patient outcomes and a more comfortable working environment.

Furthermore, our research identified several practical suggestions and strategic recommendations. These suggestions encompassed issues such as birthing room visits, community awareness, messages to decision makers and authority, and the training of healthcare providers. The participants stressed the importance of community awareness in promoting the benefits of the new birthing room design, making the audience feel responsible and engaged in this crucial aspect of maternal wellbeing. They recommended that childbirth education classes involve real-life visits to birthing rooms in order to reduce the anxiety and stress of expectant mothers. Similarly, a study conducted by Rashed et al., [35] recognized that the participation in childbirth classes has the potential to increase women’s knowledge about childbirth while also reducing anxiety and fear.

Similarly, a literature review on the influence of birthing room design on maternal and neonate outcomes revealed a crucial role of healthcare professionals, architects, and researchers in shaping birthing room design to promote health in birthing women and their babies, particularly in hospital-based labor wards. This research underscores the vital role of healthcare providers in this process, highlighting their value and importance in the design and implementation of birthing room changes [13]. Based on our research, hospital decision-makers must hear the opinions of healthcare providers regarding the birthing room design’s significance and its positive influence on birthing experiences. To ensure a comfortable and homelike environment for mothers, redesigning birthing rooms must be promoted and supported in the Ministry of Health maternity hospitals. This may involve the placement of equipment to support mobility for the laboring and birthing woman such as yoga mats on the floor, a suspended support rope in the ceiling, a bathtub in the room, pictures to provide distraction, and wall speakers for relaxing/distracting music in the room, ensuring adequate and dimmable lighting, and considering the presence of green plants, which may also play a critical role in creating a more homely environment. Ultimately, hospital policymakers must train healthcare providers to understand their roles, responsibilities, and duties in supporting natural birth; training sessions for healthcare providers and pregnant mothers with companions are required after the birthing rooms are redesigned.

Strength and limitations

This study has several strengths. It addresses the question of information needs regarding the perspective of healthcare providers on the new birthing room design and its influence on mothers’ natural instinctive childbirth. The participants had exclusive experience with the previous and the new birthing room designs. The qualitative approach, which allowed for a deep and thorough understanding of the perceptions of healthcare providers as they provided during the interviews, is a reassuring aspect of this study. Qualitative research relies on participants’ experiences and opinions and researchers’ interpretations and subjective judgments, so some subjectivity and interpretation challenges may be introduced as the limitations of this study. Moreover, this study is from the perspective of healthcare providers only and may not be applicable to women whose opinions should also be sought on the design of the birthing rooms this is still a gap to be addressed in future research. Additionally, the two-year gap between renovation and interviews introduces substantial recall bias that may affect the validity of comparisons. Research on memory shows that current satisfaction can distort recollections of previous conditions through ‘rosy retrospection’ or contrast effects. Healthcare providers may have unconsciously idealized or criticized the old environment based on their current experiences. This temporal distance means our findings about the ‘old’ birthing room should be interpreted cautiously they represent providers’ current memories and interpretations rather than contemporaneous accounts. Future research should collect baseline data before environmental changes to enable more valid comparisons. Despite this limitation, the consistency of themes across participants and the use of memory prompts provide some confidence in the findings. Finally, this study may be subject to selection bias, as staff who left the department after the renovation or those who declined to participate were not included, which may have limited the diversity of perspectives represented.

What this study adds

The findings contribute to the existing body of evidence on the positive impact of renovated birthing rooms design on healthcare providers’ perspectives. They emphasize the importance of exploring how healthcare providers in a specific setting perceived the newly renovated birthing room and how they believed it enhanced their capacity to support mothers during natural childbirth. These insights advance understanding of the significance of birthing room redesign and highlight its potential implications for maternity care practices at both national and international levels.

Implications for practice

Our findings support the redesign of birthing rooms in all hospitals to support low-risk mothers in having a natural, instinctive childbirth in Saudi Arabia and in Middle East Region. This redesign should ensure space for the mothers’ companion and provide facilities that enhance relaxation and physical comfort, such as home-like furniture, light, space, bathtub, yoga mat, rope, and green plants. However, amidst all these changes, one thing remains crucial-the one-to-one care of the midwives. It’s not just important, it’s a key factor in the mothers’ birth experience, and it’s something that should be ensured and maintained.

Conclusion

This qualitative study explored the perceptions of healthcare providers regarding the new birthing room design. All participants demonstrated awareness of potential advantages of the new birthing rooms design as perceived by healthcare providers in supporting maternal well-being. They also noted observations of maternal responses, which can help contribute to a positive experience during their hospital stay. However, additional efforts are required to deliver messages to the decision-makers and responsible authorities at the Ministry of Health to foster their understanding of the importance of redesigning the birthing rooms at the national and international levels. Future research should continue to explore and refine these environments to ensure optimal care for all mothers.
