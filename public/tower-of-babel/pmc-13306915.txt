Seeing Beyond GLP‐1: A Systems View of Sustainable Obesity Care
Authors: Yeeli Mui, Megan R. Winkler, Becky Ramsing, Anne Palmer, Daphene Altema‐Johnson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionGlucagon‐like peptide‐1 (GLP‐1) receptor agonists represent a major therapeutic advancement, producing substantial weight loss and rapidly reshaping clinical practice, commercial markets, and policy discussions. Their accelerating adoption, however, is unfolding alongside decades of public health progress to address the complex drivers of obesity. Without integration into a broader systems‐based approach, growing reliance on GLP‐1 therapies risks reinforcing a reactive, treatment‐driven model of obesity management that is costly, difficult to sustain, insufficient for addressing root causes, and likely perpetuating existing health inequities.MethodsDrawing on a synthesis of existing literature and emerging clinical evidence, we apply Habits of a Systems Thinker to examine: (1) the promises and pitfalls of growing dependence on GLP‐1 therapies; (2) how a systems thinking lens can illuminate unintended consequences and reveal overlooked leverage points; and (3) how dietitians and other healthcare providers can strengthen systems‐based dietetic practice to promote health equity, enhance obesity care delivery, and support lasting health outcomes.ResultsWe identified dominant mental models, gaps in system connections, and feedback loops that shape both obesity management and the integration of GLP‐1 therapies. We also highlight potential unintended consequences and outline opportunities to adapt and act strategically within the obesity care system.ConclusionDietitians hold a critical yet underutilized role, constrained by structural barriers. Fully leveraging their expertise will require system‐level changes, including integrating training on a systems approach to GLP‐1 therapies; strengthening connections to local and regional food resources to improve access to healthy foods; advocating for reimbursement reforms that support dietitians' contributions to comprehensive, systems‐oriented obesity care; and elevating dietitians' roles as citizens to advance equitable obesity care.

Introduction

Obesity has emerged as one of the most pressing global health challenges of the 21st century, affecting more than one billion people worldwide, including approximately 880 million adults and 159 million children and adolescents [1]. Recognised as a complex, chronic disease process shaped by long‐term interactions among biological, behavioural, environmental, and social systems, obesity requires continuous management and comprehensive strategies to prevent relapse [2]. Globally, obesity is associated with significant mortality and morbidity, including more than 50 related health conditions [3, 4]. Prevalence has risen sharply across nearly all regions of the world, including in the United States, where it increased from 31% to 42% between 2010 and 2020, further underscoring the need for more sustained treatment approaches [3, 4, 5, 6]. Because obesity is shaped by a complex web of factors, no single intervention is likely to fully address its long‐term drivers or consequences. This reality underscores the need to evaluate emerging therapies not only on clinical outcomes and individual wellbeing, but on their interaction with broader healthcare, food, policy, and social systems.

Within this context, pharmacological interventions have attracted growing attention as potential solutions. Frequently framed as breakthrough therapies, these medications produce clinically significant weight reductions of up to 21% [7], leading to their rapid integration into clinical practice, commercial markets, and policy discourse. The latest generation of agents, including glucagon‐like peptide‐1 (GLP‐1) receptor agonists, represents a substantive therapeutic advancement relative to earlier pharmacotherapies. Given the scale of adoption, expanding indications, intense media visibility, and rapidly increasing healthcare expenditures, GLP‐1 therapies warrant focused policy and systems‐level examination. Their diffusion is poised to reshape clinical decision‐making, payer priorities, pharmaceutical markets, and population expectations regarding obesity treatment. Concurrently, the clinical success of GLP‐1 therapies presents an opportunity to create new entry points for interdisciplinary treatment, expanded access to community‐based food and lifestyle programs, and stronger integration of pharmacotherapy within comprehensive obesity care models.

GLP‐1 therapies were first developed for the treatment of adult‐onset type 2 diabetes and obesity [8]. Clinical evidence shows that they not only improve glycemic control more effectively than other antihyperglycemic drugs, but also reduce weight, lower blood pressure, and confer cardioprotective benefits [9]. Unlike earlier obesity medications (e.g., Orlistat), which produced average weight losses of 5%–10% through blocking fat absorption in the gut, GLP‐1 reduces appetite and increases satiety, with demonstrated weight reductions of 15%–21% reported in trials [10]. The success of these medications in achieving significant weight loss has accelerated the rapid growth in use. Nearly 12% of Americans have used GLP‐1 therapy for weight loss, with the highest prevalence (~20%) among women aged 50–64 [11]. Utilisation rises to 22% among individuals who have been diagnosed with overweight or obesity [12].

The rapidly growing interest in GLP‐1 therapies is occurring against the backdrop of decades of public health progress. Since the 1990s, public health research has advanced multi‐level frameworks to address the many drivers of obesity [13]. Extensive evidence has demonstrated how urban design, broader food systems, socioeconomic inequities, marketing practices, and structural racism influence dietary behaviours and ultimately population‐level weight trends [14, 15, 16, 17, 18, 19, 20, 21]. As a result, obesity has come to reflect not only an individual health concern but also a multifaceted social, economic, and environmental issue with far‐reaching implications for population health and long‐term health system sustainability [22].

However, the renewed emphasis on pharmacological solutions risks obscuring these broader determinants [23]. An increased focus on individual‐level treatments could unintentionally undermine public health progress by reinforcing stigmatising narratives of personal responsibility, while diverting attention from the systemic and commercial determinants of obesity (i.e., the business, market, and political practices that promote unhealthy commodities) [24]. Moreover, GLP‐1 therapies remain costly and variably accessible due to insurance restrictions, raising significant concerns about exacerbating existing health disparities and long‐term fiscal sustainability [25, 26]. Most recently, the World Health Organisation released its first guideline, recommending GLP‐1 use for the treatment of obesity in adults, but only as part of a comprehensive care model. If not integrated with a broader, systems‐based approach, rising reliance on GLP‐1 therapies could accelerate a reactive, treatment‐driven model of obesity management that is financially burdensome, unsustainable over the long term, and worsens health inequities [9, 27].

A systems thinking lens offers a valuable framework to critically examine the expanding role of GLP‐1 therapies beyond the clinic. Scholars are increasingly calling for approaches that recognise and address the complexity of human health and behaviour, situating them within the broader socioeconomic, policy, and environmental systems that shape the obesity epidemic [28, 29, 30]. Central to this approach are key abilities of systems thinkers, who look beyond isolated events to understand complex problems—how relationships, underlying assumptions, and structures interact to generate observed outcomes over time. Viewing systems as dynamic rather than static allows systems thinkers to recognise the impact of time delays, feedback loops, and unintended consequences and to use this temporal awareness to anticipate and shape future outcomes. This holistic perspective also supports the identification of strategic leverage points, ranging from structural interventions to targeted programmatic actions that can produce ripple effects system‐wide. Importantly, systems thinkers maintain an adaptive learning mindset by continuously monitoring outcomes and adjusting strategies as the system evolves [31].

Thus, the increasing use of GLP‐1 therapies cannot be viewed in isolation. Building on a systems perspective, dietitians and other healthcare providers are essential to ensure that the use of GLP‐1 therapies strengthens rather than fragments obesity care. Dietitians are uniquely positioned to bridge this gap by integrating pharmacotherapy with comprehensive nutrition care, tailored meal planning, long‐term behaviour change strategies and environmental interventions. To support this effort, we applied key habits of systems thinking to analyse the rapidly expanding use of GLP‐1 therapies and the policy implications for obesity care. Specifically, we explored: i.The promises and pitfalls of increasing reliance on GLP‐1 therapies;ii.How a systems thinking lens can surface unintended consequences and leverage points that might otherwise remain less visible; andiii.How dietitians and other healthcare providers can advance systems‐based dietetic practice with GLP‐1 therapies to promote health equity, enhance obesity care delivery, and support lasting health outcomes.

Applying a Systems Lens

We applied a systems thinking lens to analyse the expanding use of GLP‐1 therapies. We drew on the Habits of Systems Thinker developed by the Waters Center for Systems Thinking [32], which outlines practices that help individuals understand how systems function, how actions influence outcomes over time, and foster problem‐solving through ongoing questioning and reflection. We selected the habits (Table 1) most germane to our analysis, organising them into three themes—recognising the whole system, understanding the system as dynamic, and acting strategically—to examine the complex interplay of factors influencing GLP‐1 access, adoption, impact, and sustainability.

Our analysis was informed by a synthesis of existing literature and emerging evidence from clinical trials, allowing us to integrate both established and evolving knowledge into a holistic understanding of GLP‐1 therapy. First, we identify the broader system in which GLP‐1 use occurs and consider how underlying beliefs, mental models, and relationships shape clinical decisions, environments, and patient experiences. Next, we assess how patterns of GLP‐1 use and outcomes evolve over time to identify feedback loops, time delays, and potential unintended consequences. Finally, building on these insights, we explore strategic points for action and close with recommendations for dietitians and other healthcare providers to advance systems‐based dietetic practice with GLP‐1 therapies.

Examining GLP‐1 Diffusion Using Habits of a Systems Thinker

How to See the Whole System

By striving to assess the whole system, we identified dominant mental models and connections (or lack thereof) between systems that influence obesity management and the integration of GLP‐1 therapies. Greater recognition that obesity is a chronic, multi‐factorial condition has helped reduce stigma and patient blame [33, 34]. However, prevailing beliefs and attitudes among healthcare providers continue to emphasise personal responsibility, despite strong evidence of social and environmental drivers [35]. Moreover, when these broader drivers are acknowledged, responses often remain narrowly focused on individual treatment rather than systemic solutions [23]. This disconnected framing limits progress by positioning obesity as either a medical or structural issue rather than integrating both perspectives that could link pharmacotherapy with nutrition, behavioural, and environmental support. Additionally, while interdisciplinary care models are beginning to emerge, they remain uncommon and lack the specificity or standardisation needed to ensure quality, consistent care [36]. Further, implementing and evaluating such holistic approaches has proven challenging within healthcare systems that remain highly siloed and disconnected [37]. These challenges are compounded further when looking beyond clinical settings to a broader public health landscape marked by unequal capacity across localities, inconsistent policy protections, and competing priorities that produce fragmented and often contradictory messaging about obesity care.

Applying a systems lens further revealed both the potential as well as tension across healthcare, food, and policy systems. For example, as GLP‐1 demand grows, the food industry has rapidly responded by reformulating its products and creating smaller‐portion meals to retain market share among consumers [38, 39, 40]. The disconnect between clinical care, public health nutrition, and broader food policy risks allowing market forces to shape responses to obesity, which prioritise profit over health [41]. Even more, this fragmentation obscures the food industry's longstanding and ongoing role in creating food systems and environments that promote unhealthy diets, limiting the long‐term effectiveness of interventions and leaving population health vulnerable to commercial interests [42].

How to See the System as Dynamic and Evolving Over Time

Viewed through a temporal lens, the relatively quick results of GLP‐1 therapies contrast sharply with the slow work of necessary lifestyle change [43, 44]. Clinical trials are beginning to show that while weight loss and appetite suppression occur quickly, maintaining these outcomes depends on continued access to nutrition counselling, behavioural support, and environments that make healthy choices feasible, such as affordable nutritious foods, safe spaces for physical activity, and supportive social norms [45, 46]. Yet these areas remain underfunded or poorly integrated in most policies and coverage frameworks [47, 48]—a vulnerability that is further exposed by federal funding reductions and stricter eligibility requirements for Supplemental Nutrition Assistant Program and Medicaid [49, 50, 51, 52]. In addition, the dramatic early improvements in weight and metabolic markers (e.g., high blood pressure) can inadvertently overshadow or delay the identification of other critical health concerns that led to the initial weight gain, including mental health, disordered eating patterns, and broader lifestyle behaviours. Without these resources and holistic attention, individuals are at risk for experiencing rebound weight gain, harmful weight cycling, and missed opportunities to address underlying or coexisting health issues [53, 54].

We also found circular relationships, or feedback loops, across healthcare and pharmaceutical systems, influencing the adoption, access, maintenance, and overall impact of GLP‐1 therapies. For example, clinical success and heightened public visibility have fuelled demand, prompting increased investment, marketing, and normalisation of pharmacotherapies [55, 56]. This reinforcing cycle expands the clinical and commercial footprint of GLP‐1 therapies while simultaneously diverting attention from prevention models of care [57, 58]. Moreover, cost and coverage disparities mean that the benefits of GLP‐1 therapies accrue disproportionately among privately insured and higher‐income groups, thereby widening health inequities [25]. Over time, these reinforcing dynamics may generate unintended consequences that extend beyond individual health outcomes. For instance, as reliance on pharmacotherapy deepens, resources and attention may continue shifting away from prevention‐oriented approaches, weakening the broader infrastructure needed for sustainable weight management. In addition, longer‐term risks include potential nutrient deficiencies because of appetite suppression, new forms of social stigma between GLP‐1 users and non‐users, and further undermining preventive health as pharmaceutical and food industries co‐adapt to shifting consumer behaviours—a pattern observed among other health‐harming industries [58, 59, 60, 61].

How to Adapt and Act Strategically Within the System

Applying a structural and adaptive perspective reveals opportunities for strategic intervention that can shift outcomes system‐wide. For example, current payment models prioritise short‐term weight loss outcomes, creating incentives for prescribers to focus on obesity management primarily through GLP‐1 therapy, with little to no support for complementary behavioural counselling and environmental interventions that are critical for long‐term success (i.e., maintained weight loss for at least 1 year) [62, 63, 64]. At the same time, the food industry is responding to shifting consumer demand by reformulating or rebranding ultra‐processed products as “GLP‐1 friendly” without meaningful improvements in nutritional quality [65]. The policy and regulatory environment also play a structural role. In particular, the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) shapes the pharmacotherapy landscape through its approval processes, which influence which drugs enter the market, how they are marketed, and the standards that define what counts as ‘effective’ treatment.

Drawing on these structural insights, one promising leverage action may involve incentivizing integrated care models that require nutrition and behavioural counselling for individuals prescribed GLP‐1 therapies, helping to sustain weight loss and reduce rebound effects. Bariatric surgery provides a useful precedent. Long‐standing clinical guidelines require patients to participate in multidisciplinary preparation and ongoing follow‐up, including nutritional counselling, psychological evaluation, behavioural therapy, and regular medical monitoring [66, 67, 68]. These requirements affirm that obesity is a chronic, multifactorial condition and that durable outcomes depend on long‐term coordinated care across multiple levels of the system. Prior work on weight regain after bariatric surgery also suggests that using the standard 1‐year time frame for ‘long‐term’ success limits understanding of what adaptations are necessary to achieve successful obesity management across a patient's lifetime [69].

Applying a similar multidimensional framework to GLP‐1 therapy would strengthen the behavioural, psychological, social, and environmental supports necessary for sustained weight management. In this model, dietitians provide individualised nutrition counselling, meal‐planning, and physical activity guidance [64, 70]; behavioural health specialists address coping strategies, stress management, and emotional eating; and physicians monitor clinical response and adjust dosing as needed. In addition, initiatives that expand access to whole, nutrient‐dense foods, such as through Food is Medicine programs, can complement and further amplify the effects of this multi‐disciplinary obesity care [58]. For example, clinics could partner with local farmers to provide patients with weekly produce boxes alongside nutrition counselling and cooking demonstrations or offer farmers' market vouchers that reinforce dietary guidance received in clinical visits. By linking clinical care to the local food system, such programs would make it easier for patients to practice healthy eating behaviours while simultaneously supporting local food economies [71, 72, 73, 74]. Embedding GLP‐1 therapy within this type of multidisciplinary ecosystem would help reduce rebound weight gain, strengthen self‐management skills, and ensure that pharmacotherapy acts as a catalyst rather than a standalone solution [57].

Regulatory structures also offer an avenue for systems‐level interventions. For example, reframing FDA evaluation criteria to include multidimensional indicators of behavioural, nutritional, and environmental contexts could help ensure obesity treatments are embedded within a broader ecosystem that supports lasting dietary and behavioural change [74]. The FDA's evaluation criteria for anti‐obesity medications have traditionally centred on weight loss and safety outcomes [75]. However, recent clinical trial designs indicate a shift toward a more holistic evaluation. In addition to requiring ≥ 5% placebo‐adjusted weight loss or a doubled responder rate, contemporary FDA reviews incorporate cardiometabolic improvements, glycemic control, body composition, functional capacity, quality of life, and psychiatric safety [76]. Long‐term concerns such as weight regain after discontinuation, sarcopenia risk, changes in eating behaviour, and kidney and cardiovascular outcomes have further expanded regulatory expectations [77]. These trends suggest a growing regulatory interest in outcomes that cannot be achieved through pharmacotherapy alone, reinforcing the potential value of integrated nutrition and behavioural counselling as part of GLP‐1 therapy.

These opportunities to adapt and intervene within the system, however, are not without challenges. Implementing multidisciplinary requirements would be a substantial undertaking, calling for reform and coordination across healthcare providers, insurers, and policy priorities that govern how obesity care is delivered and reimbursed. For example, proposed regulatory changes starting April 2026 would permit state Medicaid programs and Medicare Part D to cover GLP‐1 medications along with diet and exercise coaching. However, as written, state participation would be voluntary, and the mechanisms for ensuring consistent access to lifestyle support remain unclear [78].

Finally, as our understanding of GLP‐1 therapies continues to evolve, embedding feedback mechanisms will be critical to support iterative learning and allow healthcare providers and policymakers to adapt GLP‐1 guidance based on emerging evidence. So far, there is reason to believe that overreliance on short‐term outputs, such as prescription counts or healthcare cost savings, may obscure longer‐term outcomes like sustained weight loss [45]. Additionally, a narrow focus on these factors risks overlooking cultural, social, and equity factors essential for lasting impact. For example, patients may have access to GLP‐1 but still struggle to maintain healthy diets if guidance ignores cultural food practices or structural barriers to food access, especially within a commercial food system that continuously promotes low‐cost, unhealthy foods [42]. Further, inadequate data infrastructure or fragmented evaluation processes may further hinder system learning and adaptive decision‐making. Despite these challenges, explicitly targeting these structural leverage points while embedding feedback mechanisms offers a pathway to maximise the promise of GLP‐1 therapies.

Discussion

The Importance of Seeing the Whole System

Prior to GLP‐1 therapies, comprehensive obesity management combined lifestyle interventions, including nutrition, physical activity, and behaviour modification with medical management, ranging from first‐generation pharmacotherapy to bariatric surgery, as appropriate [7]. Bariatric surgery in particular has long recognised the necessity of multidisciplinary care, incorporating nutrition counselling, psychological assessment, behavioural therapy, and long‐term follow‐up to support sustained outcomes [67, 79]—reinforcing that effective obesity management requires coordinated, ongoing care rather than reliance on a single intervention. Multicomponent strategies (aside from bariatric surgery) have achieved 5% to 10% weight loss over 6 months, though weight regain is common when supportive programs end [7]. Yet these approaches share a fundamental limitation in that they remain largely biomedical in orientation, addressing individual‐level symptoms rather than the systemic and structural drivers of obesity—a gap that GLP‐1 therapies, despite their clinical promise, do not resolve.

GLP‐1 therapies represent a major advancement in obesity treatment, but they are insufficient on their own for achieving sustainable, long‐term obesity care. Obesity arises from dynamic interactions among behavioural, environmental, commercial, and policy forces, including the powerful influences of the food industry, pharmaceutical sector, and regulatory bodies like the FDA. These systems shape what foods are produced and marketed, how medications like GLP‐1 are developed and approved, and how obesity itself is framed within public and clinical discourse. Viewed within this broader system, a GLP‐1‐centred approach risks reinforcing a narrow biomedical model that overlooks the systemic conditions driving obesity inequities.

To maximise the impact of pharmacotherapies and build an equitable obesity care system that benefits both individuals and population health, GLP‐1 therapies must be embedded within a comprehensive, interconnected system of care [9, 36, 45]. Within this broader systems context, dietitians hold a critical yet consistently under‐utilised role. By activating targeted leverage points, dietitians can help reshape obesity care through practices, policies, and advocacy that influence long‐term outcomes and support sustained health beyond active GLP‐1 therapy.

Dietetic Practice Recommendations for an Interconnected System of Obesity Care

As experts in diet, nutrition science, and lifestyle counselling, dietitians are uniquely equipped to bridge individual dietary guidance with broader factors that influence food choices and health outcomes. However, current practice environments characterised by limited integration into healthcare teams, siloed food, clinical and public health systems, and insufficient reimbursement structures have limited dietitians' influence. Fully leveraging dietitians' expertise will require system‐level changes that expand their roles in chronic disease management and health promotion—moving beyond individual counselling to organisational, community, and policy engagement [80]. The following recommendations identify key leverage points for advancing systems‐based dietetic practice amid the rapid expansion of GLP‐1 therapies, which should not define the landscape of obesity care.

Integrate Training on a Systems Approach to GLP‐1 Therapies and Multi‐disciplinary Obesity Care

A systems approach to obesity care requires practitioners who can see the ‘big picture’, looking beyond individual behaviours and single‐intervention solutions. Integrating systems thinking training across provider roles, including dietitians, is therefore essential [81, 82]. Equipping practitioners with the skills to recognise the complex, interconnected factors that shape obesity risk and treatment trajectories would, in turn, foster a multi‐disciplinary, coordinated model of care from the outset that includes physicians, dietitians, behaviour health specialists, and others. In particular, pairing dietitians with behavioural health professionals to address the psychological stressors and trauma that often complicate weight management can yield more tailored and effective programs [83, 84]. Additionally, a systems approach creates an opportunity to reframe ‘mental models’ and messaging around obesity. By framing obesity as a multifactorial condition influenced by structural, social, and environmental determinants—not just individual choices—providers can more readily recognise how these forces influence health outcomes and tailor treatment accordingly. Such a perspective supports more compassionate, holistic care and opens opportunities to connect patients with services addressing underlying barriers such as under‐ or unemployment, limited healthy food access, and inadequate transportation. However, many practitioners are not equipped to identify or act on these broader determinants. Dietitians would benefit from comprehensive practice guidelines that articulate what systems‐oriented obesity care looks like in practice, including how GLP‐use affects nutrient needs and considerations of lifestyle factors. Clearer guidelines would support consistency, strengthen coordinated care, and ensure that obesity treatment extends beyond GLP‐1 therapies to encompass the broader services and supports needed for lasting behaviour change and health [36, 80].

Improve Patient Access to Nutritious, Healthy Foods by Leveraging Local and Regional Supply Chains

Building provider capacity through systems‐thinking training is only one component of advancing systems‐oriented obesity care. Because GLP‐1 therapies alone do not address patients' limited access to whole, nutrient‐dense foods, improving the food environments and food systems that shape daily dietary choices is equally important. Dietitians play a central role by connecting patients to nutritious foods, providing tailored nutrition guidance, and helping integrate these foods into sustainable eating patterns. Where feasible and affordable, healthcare systems can leverage existing supply chains and programs that source from local and regional food producers to increase access to healthy foods. For example, some communities have established Food is Medicine programs that use existing infrastructure to connect patients with nutrition‐related conditions to local and regional food sources [85], including medically tailored meals and produce prescriptions [58, 86]. Other areas may have a strong farmers' market network or subsidised produce boxes. By guiding patients using GLP‐1 therapies to engage with these resources, dietitians not only support individual adherence but also help generate demand for locally and regionally produced nutrient‐dense foods while attracting broader food system advocates to support these initiatives. At the same time, preserved foods, including frozen, canned, and dried options, can also support healthy dietary patterns by improving affordability, convenience, and year‐round access. Although food access initiatives may be temporary, dietitians can help patients build long‐term habits by connecting them to available resources and offering nutrition education and skill‐building in meal‐planning, food preparation (e.g., teaching kitchens), and shopping strategies.

Reform Reimbursement Models to Support Dietitians in Systems‐Oriented Obesity Care

Studies have shown that physicians recognise the importance of nutrition in patient care and the value of including dietitians on healthcare teams [87]. Yet, inadequate reimbursement remains one of the biggest barriers to fully integrating dietitians into obesity care [88]. Furthermore, while demand for dietetic services is projected to grow due to the rise of diet‐related chronic diseases, including obesity, current payment models rarely cover the full scope of dietitian‐led services. This underinvestment not only limits the integration of dietitians into obesity care but also hampers dietetic workforce growth and the training needed to meet future demand [89]. Therefore, to ensure sustainable patient outcomes and high‐quality care, it is critical to align reimbursement models with the realities of systems‐oriented obesity care. This includes adequately compensating dietitians for care coordination, partnership‐building with community food sources, and other activities that extend beyond traditional clinical encounters but are necessary for effective, long‐term obesity care. To this end, professional societies and dietitian organisations can help drive this shift, leveraging their collective influence to advocate for payment reforms that reflect the complexity and interdisciplinary nature of systems‐oriented obesity care [90].

Elevate Dietitians' Roles as Citizens to Advance Equitable Obesity Care

Beyond the confines of clinical practice, dietitians occupy a critical civic role that enables them to influence structural determinants of health. As engaged citizens, dietitians can leverage mechanisms such as voting, participation in advocacy coalitions, and collaboration with professional organisations to advance equity within obesity care. This advocacy may encompass promoting policy reforms and insurance payment models that facilitate equitable access to GLP‐1 therapies, Food Is Medicine initiatives, and other evidence‐based interventions. Furthermore, dietitians and other healthcare professionals (in their role as citizens) can support regulatory measures aimed at curbing the marketing of nutritionally detrimental food and beverage products, while urging for transparency in industry relationships with policymakers, insurers, and healthcare professionals [90]. By mobilising their professional expertise within civic spheres, dietitians can contribute to reshaping systemic conditions beyond the health care purview that perpetuate health disparities and foster a more equitable nutrition and healthcare landscape.

Conclusion

GLP‐1 therapies mark an important advancement in obesity treatment, offering meaningful improvements in weight and metabolic health for many individuals. However, their full potential will be realised when embedded within a broader, systems‐oriented approach that reflects the complex, interdependent forces shaping obesity risk and treatment outcomes. Systems thinking offers a critical lens for understanding these dynamic relationships, highlighting the upstream policy and environmental drivers that cannot be addressed through pharmacotherapy alone. Building a sustainable and equitable obesity care system will require reimagining the roles of dietitians and other healthcare providers, reshaping food environments and systems, and aligning payment models to support comprehensive care. The future of obesity care lies not in choosing medical versus structural approaches but integrating both within the broader food and policy systems to promote health equity, enhance obesity care delivery, and support lasting health outcomes. Looking ahead, there is an urgent need for research that monitors these systems interactions, incorporates feedback mechanisms, and applies iterative learning to adapt policies and practices as the obesity care landscape continues to evolve. By linking clinical, community, and policy‐level interventions with ongoing monitoring and evaluation, the obesity care system can move toward more sustainable, equitable, and prevention‐oriented approaches to weight management.
