Elevated Cathepsin B enzyme levels: a potential risk indicator for rheumatoid arthritis: Insights from a Mendelian randomization study
Authors: Ruolin Hou, Xin Liu, Changzhen Zhang, Shuiqi Cai, Chengyi Liu, Qun Huang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is a chronic autoimmune disease that significantly impacts the quality of life of affected individuals. Observational data have consistently pointed towards an associative relationship between cathepsins and the development of RA. Nonetheless, the establishment of a definitive causal nexus between members of the cathepsin family and the pathogenesis of RA remains elusive. In this study, we harnessed the principles of Mendelian randomization (MR) to interrogate the putative causal association between cathepsins and RA, and a series of sensitivity analyses were used to test the reliability of the MR results. Forward MR analyses substantiated a significant genetic correlation between the genetically predicted levels of Cathepsin B and the predisposition to RA, elevated levels of Cathepsin B exhibit a significant association with an increased risk of RA (OR = 1.0727, 95% CI: 1.0171–1.1314, P = .0098). In the reverse MR and multivariable MR analyses, no significant causal relationship was identified between cathepsins and RA. The findings suggest that Cathepsin B may serve as a biomarker for RA, thereby offering significant implications for the advancement of diagnostic and therapeutic strategies in the management of RA.

1. Introduction

Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is a chronic autoimmune disease. It is characterized by persistent and recurrent attacks of synovitis, which, if not properly managed, can eventually lead to irreversible joint damage. However, the underlying pathogenesis of RA is still not fully understood and requires further exploration. A study revealed that globally, RA prevalence could potentially reach up to 1%, inflicting considerable influence on patients’ overall quality of life.[1] The basic pathological manifestation of RA is synovitis, characterized by the influx of a large number of lymphocytes. The infiltration and migration of lymphocytes through the synovium require the degradation of local matrix. The main cell types involved in joint degradation are Synovial fibroblast-like cells, chondrocytes, and osteoclasts,[2] which destroy cartilage and subchondral bone by secreting proteinases and engulfing extracellular matrix components.[3]

Cathepsins play integral roles in various physiological functions including but not limited to protein breakdown, antigen presentation, and apoptosis. One of its unique features is the ability to be released into the extracellular matrix for the degradation of extracellular matrix components.[4,5] Many studies have elucidated the pivotal role of cathepsins as key proteases implicated in the degradation of crucial protein constituents within cartilaginous and osseous tissues. Atkinson et al found that the severity of arthritis in mice with low cathepsin levels is also low.[6] Jager et al found that Cathepsin S knockout mice have lower susceptibility to arthritis.[7] The Cathepsin S may have proteoglycan degradation activity, playing a powerful role in hydrolyzing cartilage aggrecan in neutral and acidic environments.[8] The observed augmentation in Cathepsin K expression has been associated with an expansion of the synovial fibroblast population within the synovial fluid and perivascular regions of RA specimens, thereby implicating a potential role for Cathepsin K in the degradation of the chondrocyte extracellular matrix, thus contributing to the occurrence of RA.[9]

While many research has been conducted on the role of cathepsins in the context of RA, it is noteworthy that the role of different types of cathepsin may vary greatly in RA, and the causality between different cathepsin isoforms and the onset or progression of RA remains inadequately explored.

Advancements in genomics have provided mounting evidence regarding the influence of heritability on the etiology of diseases.[10] As a complementary methodological approach for investigating causal relationships, Genome-wide association studies (GWAS)-based Mendelian randomization (MR) employs 1 or several genetic markers functioning as instrumental variables (IVs), which are strongly genetically correlated with the exposure under investigation and presumed to be insulated from the influence of potential confounders. Conducting MR research enables the estimation of the causal effect of an exposure on an outcome of interest.[11] Compared to randomized controlled trials, MR reduces the investment required in terms of time and financial resources, and it is not subject to the constraints imposed by inherent ethical considerations. Currently, MR is being utilized extensively as a substitute method to examine the causal connection among indicators.[12–14] In the present study setting, MR assessments were carried out to probe the directional influences of different types of cathepsins on the susceptibility to RA using both univariable and multivariable MR methodologies, aiming to provide new ideas for novel preventive and therapeutic strategies against RA.

2. Methods

2.1. Study design

As depicted in Figure 1, firstly, a univariate bidirectional MR analysis was implemented to probe the causal interplay between cathepsins and RA. In this analysis, cathepsins were designated as the exposure variable with RA as the outcome, followed by reversing the roles where RA served as the exposure and cathepsins as the outcome. In addition, a multivariate MR study was also conducted.

Workflow of this study design is shown in the sketch map.

Three key assumptions need to be satisfied in the study of MR: the selected single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) must exhibit a significant association with the exposure under investigation, in this case, cathepsins; SNPs must demonstrate no significant association with potential confounders that could exist between the exposure and the outcome variables; the SNPs exhibit no direct causal link with the outcome, RA, apart from the potential causal pathway mediated through cathepsins.[11,15]

2.2. Instrumental variables

Genetic tools to assess the concentrations of various subtypes cathepsin (µg/L) were procured from the INTERVAL study, including 3301 European individuals.[16] All participants were requested to furnish their informed consent for enrollment in the trial, and ethical clearance for the INTERVAL study was granted by The National Research Ethics Service (NRES reference: 11/EE/0538). The summary statistics dataset is publicly available for access through the web portal at https://gwas.mrcieu.ac.uk. In the MR analyses, cathepsin-related IVs were chosen according to certain standard: LD among instruments had to have an r2 measure of <0.001 within a 10,000 kb window; P-values were required to fall beneath the genome-wide significance threshold determined by the study (5 × 10−6; this value is determined based on the sample size limit). Moreover, the strength of each IV was ascertained using the equation: F = R2(N − 2)/1 − R2. Here, R2 signifies the fraction of cathepsin variability attributed to each IV, while N indicates the number of samples in the GWAS for the SNP-cathepsin association.[17] F-statistics exceeding 10 were typically interpreted as indicative of a robust relationship. For F-statistics below 10, the SNPs were deemed to be weak instrumental variables and were excluded from the analysis to minimize the bias associated with weak IVs.

The dataset for RA originates from the FinnGen database, which includes 12,555 cases and 240,862 controls (https://storage.googleapis.com/finngen-public-data-r9/summary_stats/finngen_R9_M13_RHEUMA.gz). All contributing studies were subject to rigorous institutional ethics review and received approval from the relevant committees at their respective institutions. To mitigate the risk of false-positive causal inferences due to anomalous SNPs, we subjected the SNPs to stringent screening procedures. We initially chose SNPs that met the threshold for genome-wide significance (P-value ≤ 1 × 10–5; this value is determined based on the sample size limit). Subsequently, Utilizing R software, we adjusted the r2 value to 0.001 and set the distance to kb = 10,000 in order to eliminate chain imbalance. Further, in order to guarantee that the allelic effects of SNPs on both the exposure and outcome were consistently aligned, we curated the data to exclude SNPs featuring palindromic structures and incompatible alleles.

2.3. Statistical analyses

The primary method used for the MR analysis in this study was the inverse-variance weighted (IVW) method, which aimed to assess the relationship between cathepsins and RA causality. In this approach, the impact of each genetic variant on the disease risk was quantified by considering its influence on the exposure using the Wald ratio method within the IVW framework. These individual MR estimates were then combined through a random-effects inverse-variance meta-analysis to generate an overall summary value.[18,19] Additionally, to further investigate causality, 2 supplementary MR analyses were incorporated: the weighted median (WM) method and MR-Egger analysis. The WM methodology retains its capability to compute causal effect estimates with precision, with more than 50% of the weight coming from valid IVs,[20] MR-Egger accounts for pleiotropy in cases where all IVs are deemed invalid.[21] All MR analyses, encompassing IVW, MR-Egger, and WM approaches, were conducted utilizing the R 2-Sample MR package.[22]

A series of analytical techniques, were employed to scrutinize the presence of pleiotropy in the study outcomes, including Cochran Q test, MR-Egger intercept assessments, leave-one-out (LOO) evaluations, and funnel plot analyses, were employed to scrutinize the presence of pleiotropy in the results. Notably, Cochran Q test served as a means to appraise heterogeneity, deeming its presence when the associated P-value was <.05. Upon encountering substantial heterogeneity among the SNPs, the random-effects model was invoked; conversely, in the absence of significant heterogeneity, the fixed effects model was utilized.[23] For the identification of outliers and horizontal pleiotropic influences, both the MR-PRESSO global test and the MR-Egger intercept analysis were conducted.[24] All the MR analyses mentioned were executed utilizing the 2-Sample MR package within the R programming environment.

All data analysis packages utilized were sourced from the open-access repository within the R software platform (version 4.1.1; R Development Core Team).

3. Results

3.1. Univariate MR study of cathepsins on RA

In order to investigate the impact of various cathepsins on the on the likelihood of developing RA, a 2-Sample MR analysis was initially conducted, focusing on 9 cathepsins along with their association with the overall risk of RA. Univariable MR analysis results revealed that high levels of Cathepsin B were significantly associated with an increased risk of overall RA (IVW: P = .0098, OR = 1.0727, 95% CI = 1.0171–1.1314) (Figs. 2, 3A and B). Moreover, a marginally positive relationship between Cathepsin Z levels and the risk of RA was noted, solely evidenced by the IVW method (P = .0762, OR = 1.0212, 95% CI: 0.9959–1.0865) (Table 1). No such effects were observed in the analysis of other tissue proteases.

3.2. Univariate MR result of RA on cathepsins

Univariate MR analysis was performed to investigate the association between RA and cathepsins. To examine the possibility for reverse causality, reverse MR analyses were conducted. The results presented in Table 1 did not reveal evidence of reverse causality between cathepsins and the incidence of RA. Nevertheless, the MR-PRESSO global test yielded a P-value suggestive of directional pleiotropy (.0310), heterogeneity was found in the effect of RA on Cathepsin E (MR-Egger method [P-value = .0324] and IVW method [P-value = .0352]), as detailed in Table 2. No compelling evidence was found to support a causal relationship between RA and various cathepsins.

3.3. Multivariable MR analysis between cathepsins and RA

In addition, a multivariable MR analysis was carried out to evaluate the genetic predisposition of various cathepsins in association with the risk of RA. No statistically significant causal association between cathepsins and RA was identified, as demonstrated in Table 3. Furthermore, the MR-Egger intercept assessment, as depicted in Table 3, did not reveal any horizontal pleiotropy.

4. Discussion

RA is a particularly severe chronic autoimmune disease in clinical practice, the exact etiology of which remains unclear, posing significant challenges for patient treatment. Current therapeutic options for RA include nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs (DMARDs), and steroids.[25] However, the substantial adverse effects associated with these treatments are a prominent concern that cannot be overlooked. Therefore, the exploration of novel therapeutic targets for the development of targeted drugs against RA holds crucial significance. Several studies have suggested a close association between cathepsins and RA. Nevertheless, the pathogenesis and causal relationship between RA and cathepsins remain obscure. In the present study, we conducted an investigation into the causal link between 9 different cathepsins and RA from a genetic perspective utilizing MR analysis for the first time. By amalgamating findings from forward analysis and reverse analysis, and multivariable analysis, we established that Cathepsin B represents a substantial risk factor for RA, no reverse causality was found for Cathepsin B.

Under normal physiological conditions, there exists a balance between bone formation and bone resorption. However, in patients with RA, this balance is disrupted in favor of bone resorption. Bone resorption relies on osteoclasts, which are multinucleated giant cells derived from the monocyte/macrophage lineage at the interface between synovial tissue and joint bone. Osteoclasts can be activated by cellular factors such as nuclear factor-kappa B (NF-κB), IL-1β, and TNF-α.[26,27] Cathepsins can hydrolyze the precursors of cytokines such as IL-1β, TNF-α, and NF-κB, which in turn activate these cytokines, promoting bone resorption and worsening joint damage. Additionally, pannus, an abnormal granulation tissue composed of immune cells, blood vessels, and fibroblasts, plays a crucial role in joint destruction in RA patients. Pannus originates in the synovium and invades the joints of RA patients, leading to irreversible bone and cartilage damage. Certain cathepsins are closely associated with vascular endothelial growth factor, promoting pannus formation and worsening bone destruction.[28]

Currently, observational studies have identified several cathepsins, including Cathepsin B, Cathepsin S, Cathepsin K, Cathepsin G, and Cathepsin L, that are associated with the pathogenesis of RA. These cathepsins play different roles in mediating synovial inflammation, promoting synovial pannus, and leading to bone destruction during the onset and progression of RA.[29] Specifically, research by Chen et al has shown that Cathepsin B is significantly upregulated in RA patients and positively correlates with macrophages M2, neutrophils, and T cell follicular helper cells. Additionally, the use of CA-074Me, a Cathepsin B inhibitor, has been observed to induce ferroptosis and reduce the migratory capacity of RA-FLS.[28] Further studies by Tong et al suggest that Cathepsin B modulates the activity and expression of MMP-2, enhancing the expression of migration-related enzymes FAK and cytoskeletal proteins, as well as the phosphorylation of the corresponding MAPK signaling pathways, and thus participates in the migration and invasion of synovial fibroblasts, promoting the occurrence and development of RA.[30] Hashimoto et al have shown that the activity of Cathepsin B is more than 19-fold higher in RA fluid compared to that of osteoarthritis (OA), and Cathepsin B in RA fluid has the ability to degrade collagen.[31] The findings from the univariate MR analysis suggested a positive association between heightened concentrations of Cathepsin B and an augmented risk of developing overall RA (IVW: P = .0098, OR = 1.0727, 95% CI = 1.0171–1.1314). These findings are consistent with previous research, further confirming the Cathepsin B may participate in joint destruction in RA and suggesting that its inhibitor may hold promise for RA management.

As health awareness grows, disease screening has garnered heightened attention. Detection of serum biomarkers exhibits considerable merits in the context of RA screening due to its convenience and rapid detection capabilities. The advantage of the present study lies in its employment of MR analysis and consideration of confounding variables, thereby facilitating the elucidation of bidirectional causal relationships between cathepsins and RA. This is the first investigation, to our knowledge, to explore their association utilizing genetic evidence.

However, our study is not without limitations. Previous observational research has suggested a significant association between Cathepsin S, Cathepsin K, Cathepsin G, and Cathepsin L with RA, the present MR analyses failed to replicate these associations within our dataset. This discrepancy may be attributed to the use of sample data. The GWAS data utilized in our research was obtained from the European population, while majority of prior observational research on the subject have been predominantly focused on Asian populations. The generalizability of our findings to non-European populations remains to be established through further inquiry. Additionally, although the sample size of the GWAS data in our study was large, it is important to recognize that more expansive and novel GWAS data may yield divergent conclusions.

In the future, it is imperative to validate the results through larger sample sizes of MR studies and randomized controlled trials. Furthermore, akin to all published MR studies, even with measures in place to identify and eliminate anomalous variations, the possibility of unobserved pleiotropy influencing results cannot be entirely excluded.[32] This study cannot infer the nonlinear correlation between cathepsins and RA.

5. Conclusion

In summary, the current genetic evidence from this investigation demonstrates a heightened risk of RA with elevated Cathepsin B levels. This finding holds promise for the identification of novel biochemical markers that could be utilized in the prediction, screening, early diagnosis, and prognosis of RA. Furthermore, therapeutic intervention through protease inhibitors aimed at selectively targeting the specific cathepsins implicated in RA pathogenesis could pave the way for efficacious treatments against the disease.
