Epigenetic reprogramming as the nexus of cancer stemness and therapy resistance: implications for biomarker discovery
Authors: Chu Xin Ng, Shin Yuh Lee, Xin Yi Yap, Yong Hui Wong, Jian Sheng Loh, Kuan Ping Ang, Wen Hwei Lee, Wei Xu, Phelim Voon Chen Yong, Sau Har Lee
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Therapeutic failure in cancer often arises from the ability of tumor cells to adapt to external stressors such as drug exposure, metabolic and oxidative stress, and immune surveillance within the tumor microenvironment (TME). This adaptive capacity is a significant factor contributing to poor clinical outcomes and the lethality associated with many aggressive cancer types. During tumor progression, a subpopulation of cells may acquire stem-like characteristics, leading to the development of cancer stem cells (CSCs), which play a pivotal role in resistance and disease recurrence. A key driver of this process is epigenetic reprogramming, which alters histone modifications and chromatin architecture in response to environmental stimuli. These modifications activate genes associated with stemness by creating transcriptionally permissive chromatin regions, while simultaneously silencing genes involved in differentiation, thereby maintaining the tumor cells’ undifferentiated state. This cellular plasticity enables cancer cells to undergo transdifferentiation or dedifferentiation, enhancing their capacity for survival and adaptation. Importantly, distinct transcriptional states are often regulated by specific epigenetic signatures, some of which are valuable for tumor subtype classification, prognosis assessment, and treatment response prediction. Furthermore, certain epigenetic biomarkers show considerable promise for detecting tumor recurrence and monitoring minimal residual disease with high sensitivity. In this review, we examine how epigenetic reprogramming influences chromatin plasticity to promote stemness and therapeutic resistance. Additionally, we discuss ongoing translational research aimed at leveraging these mechanisms to advance precision oncology, with the ultimate goal of improving diagnostic accuracy and therapeutic efficacy.

Introduction

Cancer stemness is the acquisition or retention of self-renewal capacity and multipotency, resembling cancer stem cells (CSCs) properties, which enable long-term tumor growth and survival, resistance to treatment, and regeneration after therapy. The cancer stem cell (CSC) model is well-established in malignancies such as glioblastoma, colorectal cancer, breast cancer, and leukemia, where CSCs contribute to tumor initiation and resistance [1]. However, in cancers like pancreatic, prostate, and lung, evidence for a hierarchical CSC structure is less definitive, with cellular plasticity playing a more dominant role. These differences highlight the importance of considering the context-specific nature of CSC biology and the need for precision oncology approaches that are tailored to the unique epigenetic and cellular dynamics of each tumor type [2]. Epigenetic reprogramming that promotes cancer stemness contributes to therapeutic resistance occurrences, hence posing a major challenge to achieving optimal and effective cancer treatment. By definition, epigenetic reprogramming involves dynamic and reversible modifications to DNA and histones, including DNA methylation, histone modification, non-coding RNA (ncRNA), and chromatin remodeling, whose predominant role is to preserve the originality of DNA sequences [3]. These events are crucial to establishing the epigenetic plasticity of cells and tissues during normal organogenesis and differentiation [4]. However, the aberration of epigenetic plasticity dynamics has been exploited by cancer cells to acquire primitive cellular states, thereby facilitating the acquisition of stemness, adaptability, therapeutic resistance, and metastasis through the establishment of the CSCs niche [5, 6].

Consequently, the maturity of tumor heterogeneity and plasticity is established through a series of evolutionary modulations, which give rise to phenotypic plasticity, where multiple phenotypes respond to the exposed environment, despite a single and non-varied genotype [7]. As mentioned, one of the critical phenotypes that ensures the survivability of cancer cells is the stemness behavior, since cancer stemness grants the self-renewal ability, along with further differentiation and proliferative capacity [8]. These allow cancer cells to initiate tumors, dominate the microenvironment, and develop therapeutic resistance.

In this context, cellular senescence has emerged as an important adaptive mechanism linked to cancer stemness and therapeutic stress response. Cellular senescence refers to a permanent arrest of cell division triggered by DNA damage, cellular stress, or oncogenic signaling [9]. Although senescent cells are irreversibly growth-arrested, they remain metabolically active and secrete a specific set of inflammatory agents, growth factors, and proteases, collectively referred to as the Senescence-Associated Secretory Phenotype (SASP), that further influence the tissue environment [10]. Initially, senescence functions as a tumor-suppressive barrier by limiting the proliferation of damaged or premalignant cells [11]. However, under chronic stress or within a therapy-altered TME, persistent SASP can paradoxically promote chronic inflammation, tissue dysfunction, and age-related diseases.

Importantly, recent studies suggest that CSCs can escape from senescence response. The SASP can promote reprogramming of surrounding non-tumorigenic cells into stem-like state, contributing to tumor relapse, and metastasis [12]. Moreover, CSCs can enter a quiescent or slow-cycling state in response to therapy-induced stress, enabling them to resist chemotherapeutic treatments through evading apoptosis and reinitiating tumor growth. This resilience associated with stemness enables cancer cells to repair therapy-induced DNA damage, evade apoptosis, and survive treatment. In contrast, CSCs exposed to conventional therapy often enter a quiescent state, which increases their invasiveness and contributes to the development of chemoresistance [13].

The ability of CSCs to endure hostile microenvironments is supported by a combination of mechanisms, including epigenetic regulation (such as DNA methylation, post-translational histone modifications (PTMs), chromatin remodeling, and non-coding RNA networks) alongside metabolic plasticity and adaptive signaling pathways [14, 15]. Focusing on epigenetic regulation, DNA methylation involves the addition of a methyl group to the carbon-5 of cytosine, predominantly occurring in CpG islands (high-density CpG regions) and CpG shores (low-density CpG regions). Cytosine methylation modifies the activity of cis-regulatory elements such as the promoter without altering the underlying DNA sequences [16–18]. Meanwhile, post-translational histone modifications (such as phosphorylation, acetylation, ubiquitination, and methylation) alter the amino acid (mostly lysine) composition of the histone, thereby changing the interaction between DNA and histone, loosening (transcriptionally active) or tightening (transcriptionally inactive) the chromatin structure, and directly influencing the transcription regulatory mechanisms [19]. Consequently, CSCs employ chromatin-modifying enzymes such as acetyltransferases, methyltransferases, serine/threonine kinases, ubiquitin ligases, and proline isomerases to restructure chromatin. This epigenetic reprogramming enhances transcriptional plasticity and facilitates transitions among proliferative, quiescent, and drug-resistant states, therefore fostering survival and adaptation in various environments [20]. Additionally, the histone methylation status is frequently regulated by the dynamics of histone methyltransferases and demethylases, which modulate the gene expression networks responsible for maintaining the CSC stemness phenotype [21].

A comprehensive understanding of how epigenetic reprogramming regulates cancer stemness and therapeutic resistance is crucial for the advancement of precision oncology. The frequency and function of CSCs differ significantly across tumor types. Well-defined CSC hierarchies have been identified in breast, colorectal, glioblastoma, and acute myeloid leukemia (AML), where specific stem-like cell populations drive tumor growth and recurrence [22]. In contrast, malignancies such as melanoma exhibit tumorigenic potential across diverse cell populations, suggesting that the CSC model does not universally explain all cancer types [23]. To address this, the study first explores the mechanisms via which epigenetic alterations govern stemness and resistance phenotypes (Sect. 2). Building on these mechanistic insights, it then evaluates epigenetic biomarkers associated with these adaptive states (Sect. 3). Subsequently, the discussion focuses on emerging technological tools that enable the discovery and characterization of these biomarkers (Sect. 4). Together, these findings extend from fundamental epigenetic biology to clinical applications, facilitating the advancement of more effective personalized treatment options for cancer.

Mechanisms of epigenetics-induced stemness and therapy resistance

While cancer has long been understood through the lens of permanent genetic alterations, emerging evidence suggests that epigenetic plasticity plays an equally critical role in therapeutic resistance and phenotypic transitions. Unlike irreversible mutations, epigenetic modifications offer dynamic, reversible, and context-sensitive responses to therapeutic stress and microenvironmental signals. This section outlines how therapy exposure, hypoxia, inflammation, stromal interactions, and immune pressures collectively drive stemness and resistance across various cancer types (Fig. 1).

Epigenetic plasticity and therapy-induced reprogramming

Pathological epigenetic plasticity is responsible for the acquisition of cancer cell stemness, as evidenced by the fact that cell identity is no longer a unidirectional developmental event, but can be interchanged and transdifferentiated or dedifferentiated between phenotypic states in response to intrinsic or extrinsic signals, such as therapy or microenvironmental factors [24–26]. Such acknowledgement raises awareness about the current approach to cancer therapeutics, where the existing treatments can lead to the secondary development of stemness through cancer cell dedifferentiation at the chromatin level.

For instance, melanoma cells exposed to anti-PD1 therapy or prolonged vemurafenib undergo a dedifferentiation mechanism upon epigenetic alterations and changes in chromatin accessibility, granting them stemness characteristics [24, 26]. Furthermore, various signaling pathways can also reinforce this process. For example, Notch activation in breast cancer cells following monocytes interaction enhances stemness markers expression, including aldehyde dehydrogenase (ALDH) and CD44+ [27]. Notably, Notch pathway activation can induce transcription factors such as HES1, which in turn recruit epigenetic co-regulators, thereby modulating chromatin accessibility and sustaining CSC phenotypes through histone modifications and enhancer remodeling [28]. Meanwhile, the adenosine A2B receptor (A2BR)-p38 MAPK signaling axis enhances the recruitment of chromatin modifiers, KDM6A and p300, to establish pluripotency-associated euchromatin landscape, thereby facilitating the dedifferentiation of breast cancer cells into breast cancer stem cells (BCSC) [29]. Valproic acid, as a histone deacetylase (HDAC) inhibitor, further amplifies breast cancer stemness by activating the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway [30].

While these processes are often cancer-cell intrinsic, therapy-induced epigenetic reprogramming can also affect non-cancerous cells in the TME. For example, taxane- and platinum-based chemotherapies induce dedifferentiation of Schwann cells into immature, repair-like states that promote demyelination and contribute to chemotherapy-induced peripheral neuropathy (CIPN) [31]. Although not malignant, this phenomenon demonstrates how therapeutic stress can drive epigenetic plasticity and dedifferentiation even in otherwise differentiated, post-mitotic cells, which provide a broader mechanistic parallel to CSC formation. Such microenvironmental changes may indirectly support cancer progression and resistance by remodeling local niches.

Besides, metabolic stress and senescence-driven stress also converge on epigenetic rewiring. For example, breast cancer therapy-induced serine starvation may utilize the Warburg effect to repress estrogen receptor alpha (ERα), reprogramming epigenetic states and glucose metabolism to desensitize the cancer cell from hormone therapeutic lethality [32]. Therapy-induced senescence (TIS) has been shown to promote breast cancer stemness and tumor aggressiveness via oncogene upregulation. As evidenced, Bhola et al. (2013) reported an enrichment of BCSC-associated TGF-β signaling transcripts in primary breast cancer biopsies following neoadjuvant chemotherapy [33].

In the context of pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC), prolonged treatment with gemcitabine has been shown to drive the clonal selection of TP53-mutated, treatment-resistant subpopulations. These resistant cells exhibited high expression of genes involved in glycolysis and oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS), reflecting a metabolic shift toward CSC-like behavior [34]. This metabolic reprogramming coincides with increased mesenchymal gene expression, as shown by Bulle et al. (2019), who observed increased mesenchymal markers expression in patient-derived PDAC models following gemcitabine exposure, showcasing gene expression shift to a more aggressive phenotype with the activation of epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EMT) [35].

Targeted therapies similarly provoke adaptive epigenetic response. Mitogen-activated extracellular signal-regulated kinase inhibitors (MEKi) treatment, used in PDAC, has been shown to promote adaptive resistance through histone modification, particularly through the activation of euchromatin histone lysine methyltransferase 2 (EHMT2) or histone deacetylase 3 (HDAC3) [36]. These enzymes reprogram chromatin landscape to support tumor cell survival despite pathway inhibition. A parallel phenomenon is observed in non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) treated with epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) inhibitors. While EGFR tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) initially induce tumor regression, adaptive resistance frequently emerges through epigenetic changes that enable bypass signaling or lineage plasticity [37]. Notably, transdifferentiation into a small-cell-like phenotype has been reported, mediated by the loss of histone acetylation and suppression of lineage-specific transcription factors, including TTF1 and FOXA2 [38]. These chromatin alterations promote a switch to a drug-tolerant state that is resistant to EGFR inhibition.

Collectively, these findings highlighted the effect of therapeutic stress in driving epigenetic reprogramming that enables dedifferentiation and plasticity, which ultimately fosters CSC-like states. However, therapy is not the sole driver of such transition. The surrounding TME also exerts significant influence through hypoxia, inflammation, and radiation stress, all of which synergize with epigenetic cues to shape cancer cell fate.

Hypoxia, inflammation, and microenvironmental factors

The TME is a key driver of cancer stemness, providing external stimuli such as hypoxia, radiation-induced stress, and inflammation that promote epigenetic reprogramming and intratumoral heterogeneity. The hypoxic TME is crucial for maintaining glioma stemness [39–41]. Epigenetic remodeling in glioma cells by inducing interleukin-8 (IL-8) with open chromatin marks (H3K27ac) at astrocyte enhancer regions stabilizes glioma stemness during anti-glioma chemotherapy, specifically temozolomide, altering the expression of H3K27me3, H3K9me3, and Enhancer of Zeste homolog 2 (EZH2) [42]. Similarly, temozolomide resistance in glioblastoma also arises from hypoxia-inducible factor (HIF)-signaling axis-mediated dedifferentiation. Consequently, the activation of HIF recruits pluripotency-related genes, enabling non-stem cells to acquire CSC properties, driving therapy resistance and tumor recurrence [43–45]. One remarkable example would be the HIF-1α and HIF-2α-mediated messenger RNA (mRNA) demethylation of NANOG, granting the development of BCSC phenotypes in MDA-MB-231 cells [46]. In addition, the co-activation of Notch signaling along with HIF-1α reveals a novel regulatory mechanism that promote stem-like cell properties in pancreatic adenocarcinoma, including self-renewal and multipotency [47].

Meanwhile, ionizing radiation has been shown to induce stemness-associated transcription factors such as Sox2, Nanog, and Olig2 through the activation of survivin and Tie2 pathways. Tie2 promotes downstream PI3K/AKT-mediated survival and pro-angiogenic activities, fostering a microenvironment that supports stem-like phenotypes and therapeutic resistance. In parallel, survivin, as a central node of several pro-survival pathways including PI3K/AKT, further underscores the cooperative role of radiation-induced Tie2 and survivin in maintaining the stem-like phenotype of glioblastoma cells [43, 44].

Beyond hypoxia, the inflamed microenvironment also amplifies stemness by upregulating chromatin modifiers and remodeling genes (such as SOX, OCT, and NANOG), allowing them to be transcriptionally active regions while repressing differentiation-associated loci [48, 49]. The inflammatory microenvironment also plays a critical role in promoting cancer stemness through secreting cytokines and chemokines which are critically involved in cellular plasticity and survival. Specifically, proinflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β, TNF-α, and CXC chemokines are crucially involved in EMT and enhance stem-like properties in cancer cells ([36]– [37]). Using the example of NSCLC, IL-1β upregulates SLUG gene, which facilitates the methylation of histone-3 lysine-27 and lysine-9 (H3K27 and H3K9), as well as DNA methylation that occurs around the nucleosomes of the CDH1 promoter. Such epigenetic modifications establish the EMT-associated phenotypes that persists even after termination of IL-1β exposure [50]. In addition, the presence of inflammation-related genes (including CXCL2, IL-1β, NOS2, and TNF-α) is associated with DNA methylation in Helicobacter pylori-infected gastric epithelial cells of a gerbil model, linking to tumorigenesis [51].

Inflammatory signaling such as IL-6/STAT3 signaling pathway likewise promotes immune-resistant states via epigenetic modifications in breast cancer cells [52]. Chemokines such as chemokine (C-C motif) ligand 2 (CCL2), CCL5, and CXCL12 play a role in maintaining stemness in cancers through modulating several pathways, including Notch, NF-κB, β-catenin, and HIF pathways, each tied to transcriptional accessibility of pluripotency genes. Specifically, CCL2 has been shown to recruit monocytes and promote tumor-associated macrophage polarization, which in turn secrete factors that sustain CSC phenotypes via Notch, β-catenin, and STAT3 signaling [53–55]. CCL5 promotes stemness in cancers by activating the β-catenin and NF-κB pathways, leading to the upregulation of stemness-associated markers, including ALDH1, CD44, and OCT4 [56, 57]. CXCL12, through its interaction with the receptor CXCR4, maintains CSC niches and supports stemness in cancers, by promoting PI3K/AKT and HIF-1α signaling, thereby enhancing survival, invasion, and resistance to therapy [58].

While hypoxia and inflammation exemplify the role of soluble factors within the TME in promoting stemness, cellular components such as cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) also involve in this epigenetic remodeling. In the following subsection, we explore how CAFs and extracellular vesicles (EVs)-mediated epigenetic remodeling, which further establishes non-cell-autonomous mechanisms of cancer plasticity and resistance.

Epigenetic roles of cancer-associated fibroblasts and extracellular vesicles

Fibroblasts, which normally function in tissue repair and scar formation, can also become key contributors within the TME by supporting the development and maintenance of cancer stem cells. Fibroblasts transformed into pro-tumorigenic CAFs, which play a pivotal role to support the evolution of cancer cells towards therapeutic resistance and stemness characteristics [59]. Epigenetic reprogramming plays a critical role in CAFs activation, involving both hypermethylation and hypomethylation events. For example, hypermethylation of multiple genes and microRNAs (miRNAs), including RUNX family, SOCS1, SMAD3, miR-145, and miR-200, and hypomethylation in EDARADD, HDAC4, altering their transcriptomic profiles to support CSC-like reprogramming in neighboring tumor cells [60–64]. While EDARADD and HDAC4 were particularly exhibiting consistent hypomethylation in CAFs in various cancers, the heterogeneity in CAFs in DNA methylation could arise from different cellular sources recruited into the CAF compartment [65]. Importantly, CAF-derived exosomes have emerged as key mediators in dedifferentiation and stem-like transitions in cancer cells through modulating core pluripotency markers such as CD133, ALDH, NANOG, and OCT4. Mechanistically, exosomal miRNAs from CAFs have been shown to regulate the Wnt/β-catenin signaling cascade, thereby inducing a CSC phenotype and promoting chemotherapy resistance [66]. Notably, this epigenetic alteration via EVs functions as a non-cell-autonomous mechanism of resistance, complementing intrinsic chromatin modifications within tumor cells. The microenvironment thereby gains an active role in shaping the epigenomic landscape of cancer through vesicle trafficking, creating a layered, cooperative resistance network [67]. Furthermore, the crosstalk between CAFs and hypoxia contributes to the epigenetic reprogramming of normal fibroblasts into pro-glycolytic CAFs, driving a metabolic shift toward lactate and pyruvate production, which supports breast tumor growth and development [68].

Beyond the stromal support provided by CAFs and EVs, the immune landscape of the TME also plays a central role in cancer persistence. Among immune cells, T cells undergo profound epigenetic remodeling under chronic stimulation. The subsdquent subsection will focus on T-cell exhaustion and its impact on immune escape via stable epigenetic modifications.

T-cell exhaustion and epigenetic remodeling in immune escape

T-cell exhaustion represents a strategically co-opted barrier to immune surveillance, driven not only by persistent antigen exposure but also by deep epigenetic reprogramming. This phenomenon is particularly prominent in the TME, where chronic antigen stimulation and inflammatory signals drive progressive loss of T cell effector functions. However, recent evidence suggests that exhaustion is not merely a functional or metabolic decline, but a stably imprinted epigenetic state that fundamentally alters the transcriptional landscape of T cells, thereby reinforcing immune suppression and resistance to immune checkpoint blockade [69, 70]. Exhausted T-cells are phenotypically characterized by the sustained upregulation of co-inhibitory receptors such as programmed cell death-1 (PD-1), T-cell immunoglobulin domain 3 (TIM-3), and Lymphocyte activation gene-3 (LAG-3), coupled with impaired cytokine production, cytotoxicity, and proliferative potential [71, 72]. Underlying this dysfunctional phenotype is a network of epigenetic remodeling events that reshape chromatin accessibility and histone modification patterns. Notably, exhaustion is marked by persistent chromatin accessibility at exhaustion-associated genes including inhibitory receptor loci, and repressive chromatin remodeling at effector-related regions. For example, silencing of key effector genes is mediated by the accumulation of repressive histone marks, such as H3K27me3, while exhaustion-related enhancers are enriched for H3K27ac, promoting their continued activity [73]. These changes result in the formation of an epigenetic “scar” that stabilizes the exhaust state, limiting the capacity of T cells to regain function, even when inhibitory signaling is pharmacologically blocked.

Importantly, T-cell exhaustion is a heterogeneous process, with distinct subsets exhibiting varying degrees of epigenetic fixation. Progenitor-exhausted T cells retain a partially accessible chromatin landscape at effector loci and show greater responsiveness to PD-1 blockade [74]. In contrast, terminally exhausted T cells acquire a deeply entrenched epigenetic program that restricts plasticity and impedes functional recovery [75, 76]. This hierarchical exhaustion model has significant clinical implications, as the therapeutic efficacy of immune checkpoint inhibitors may largely depend on the epigenetic plasticity of the T-cell pool present within the tumor.

To counteract these epigenetically stabilized exhaustion states, several epigenetic modulators have been investigated. Inhibitors targeting HDACs or EZH2, a methyltransferase responsible for H3K27me3 deposition, can partially reverse exhaustion by reopening silenced effector loci and attenuating expression of inhibitory receptors [77, 78]. However, these effects are often transient. The immunosuppressive microenvironment through persistent antigen exposure, inflammatory cytokines, and stromal-derived cues, can re-establish the exhausted phenotype even after pharmacologic intervention, highlighting the resilience of the exhaustion program [79].

Altogether, these insights underscore T-cell exhaustion as an epigenetically reinforced barrier to durable immune reactivation. Addressing this challenge will likely require multi-pronged approaches that integrate epigenetic reprogramming with therapies aimed at disrupting the extrinsic cues that sustain exhaustion. Such strategies hold promises for enhancing the efficacy of immunotherapy and achieving more sustained anti-tumor immune responses.

While classical models of therapy resistance emphasize irreversible genetic mutations in driver genes, these models fail to account for the rapid, reversible, and heterogeneous nature of resistance observed clinically. Contrarily, the epigenetic adaptability model suggests a more dynamic and reversible phenotypical state of cancer without altering DNA sequences. Granting tumor heterogeneity and phenotypical transition ability, this is indeed beneficial for cancer survival and resistance development to conventional chemotherapy.

Epigenetic biomarkers of stemness-persister-resistance continuum

Upon exposure to therapy, cancer cells without pre-existing resistance mutations undergo stepwise cell-state transitions that enable progressive increase in cell fitness to survive therapeutic challenges [80]. The stemness-persister-resistance continuum refers to the epigenetically reinforced, plasticity-enabled trajectories of adaptive cell-state transitions that increase cellular fitness along a resistance continuum. Epigenetic regulation in cancer is highly dynamic due to its reversibility and sensitivity to environmental stresses, including drug-induced stress [81, 82], inflammation [83], hypoxia [84], metabolic alterations [85], and oxidative stress [86].

The dynamic nature of epigenetic mechanism complicates treatment outcomes as effective interventions may miss the transient window of opportunity whereby cancer cells are temporarily targetable [80]. For instance, in TP53- and RB1-deficient prostate cancer, the reprogramming ability of transcription factor SOX2 drives lineage switching from androgen (AR)-dependent luminal cells to AR-independent basal-like cells, thereby conferring resistance to the anti-androgen drug, enzalutamide [87]. Nevertheless, the reversibility of epigenetic alterations also presents therapeutic opportunities to overcome acquired resistance by enabling therapeutic re-differentiation or conversion to clinically manageable, drug-sensitive lineages [80].

Epigenetically-induced stemness: mechanisms, impacted pathways, and epigenetic alterations

Stemness-associated epigenetic regulators

Stemness is a niche-dependent cell state that recapitulates tissue stem cell characteristics, including self-renewal capacity, multipotency, and context-dependent plasticity [126]. In tumors, cells transiently occupying this state are referred to as CSCs. Cancer stemness is a niche-conditioned, plastic cell state that confers tumor-initiating capacity, self-renewal, and multipotent lineage output [127]. Multiple lines of evidence demonstrate that cancer cells can transition from a differentiated-like state to an undifferentiated stem-like state in response to microenvironmental and therapeutic stresses [22]. The ability of cancer cells to assume both stem-like and differentiated-like states drives cellular plasticity and contributes to intratumoral heterogeneity [128]. The acquisition of stemness properties in tumors has been linked to lineage plasticity and switching [129], treatment resistance [130], hyperprogression [131], metastasis [132], and recurrence [22]. Clinically, high baseline tumor stemness at diagnosis is associated with an increased risk of induction failure and lineage plasticity [133, 134].

DNA methylation is a mitotically heritable DNA modification that involves the covalent addition of a methyl group to the 5-carbon of cytosine, predominantly at CpG dinucleotides in mammals [135, 136]. Cancer genomes are characterized by global DNA hypomethylation and site-specific hypermethylation [137]. For instance, isocitrate dehydrogenase (IDH)-mutant gliomas exhibit DNA hypermethylation that silences tumor-suppressor genes CDKN2A/B and disrupts the insulator function of CCCTC-binding factor (CTCF), leading to activation of proto-oncogenes PDGFRA [138, 139]. DNA hypermethylation is frequently present in tumors with isocitrate dehydrogenase (IDH) mutations, including low-grade gliomas. Mutant IDH converts α-ketoglutarate to the oncometabolite d-2-hydroxyglutarate (D-2-HG). D-2-HG competitively inhibits α-ketoglutarate-dependent dioxygenases, notably the TET1/2 DNA demethylases and JmjC domain-containing histone demethylases, leading to DNA and histone hypermethylation [140]. In IDH-mutant glioma, mutant IDH inhibitor and DNA methyltransferase 1 (DNMT1) inhibitor are associated with reduced DNA methylation, induction of interferon (IFN) signaling, and greater astrocytic-like differentiation, consistent with reduced stemness [141].

Preliminary clinical evidence in IDH-mutant gliomas indicates that mutant IDH inhibitors lower tumor D-2-HG concentrations and promote lineage differentiation from proliferative stem-like/neural progenitor cell (NPC)-like states toward astrocytic-like states with reduced proliferative capacity [142–144]. Additionally, mutant IDH inhibition is accompanied by chromatin remodeling, evidenced by decreased H3K9me1 and H3K27me1 with a concomitant increase in histone acetylation [144]. However, achieving durable clinical outcomes remains challenging. High baseline stemness, re-emergence of stem-like compartments, and co-occurring mutations contribute to diminished response and increase the risk of resistance and relapse [143, 145, 146].

The profiling of DNA methylation has established its clinical utility in the classification of brain tumors, with applications increasingly extending to other tumor types [147–149]. Notably, DNA methylation profiling is an emerging approach to predict drug response, tracks tumor evolution, and predict clinical outcomes [149, 150]. Metastatic breast cancers, particularly basal-like and triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) metastases, frequently exhibit HLA-A promoter CpG island hypermethylation, which is associated with reduced HLA-A protein expression, impaired adaptive immune signatures, and poorer survival [151]. However, epigenetic programs are dynamic and evolve with tumor stage and cell-state composition. In IDH-mutant gliomas, progression features a shift from slow-cycling, hypermethylated oligodendrocyte progenitor cell (OPC)-like cells to expansion of proliferative NPC-like cells. This shift is accompanied by a decline in global hypermethylation, transition from epigenetic to genetic oncogenic drivers, and stage-dependent IFN suppression [141]. Complementary data shows that recurrent IDH-mutant astrocytomas are associated with decreased genome-wide DNA methylation and increased cell cycling, with demethylation (reflected by a low Glioma Methylation Signature (GMS) score) correlating with higher tumor grade at recurrence [152]. Taken together, these findings underscore that epigenetic biomarkers are dynamic and cell-state–dependent, implying that a longitudinal, multi-modal panel will better capture risk and therapeutic opportunity.

Epigenetic regulators of drug-tolerant persisters

Drug-tolerant persister (DTP) cells are small, heterogeneous subpopulations of cancer cells that may pre-exist at low frequency or emerge in response to initial anticancer treatment. DTP cells enter a slow-cycling state via epigenetic, transcriptional, and metabolic reprogramming, allowing them to withstand lethal therapeutic pressures and avoid therapeutic elimination. Importantly, this tolerant phenotype is reversible upon drug withdrawal, enabling DTP cells to re-enter proliferative states. Ultimately, DTP cells serve as a reservoir from which cancer cells can evolve and develop acquired resistance, leading to tumor regeneration, cancer recurrence and distant metastases [153–155].

The upregulation and activity of histone lysine demethylases (KDMs), such as KDM5A/B and KDM6A/B, have been implicated as contributors of DTP state. Across multiple models, KDM5 demethylase activity is required for the survival of DTP cells by reducing H3K4me3, an active histone mark highly enriched at transcription start sites. The preservation of H3K4me3 via pre-treatment with a specific KDM5 inhibitor (CPI-455) reduced the number of DTP cells in NSCLC and melanoma cells treated with targeted agents [156]. Notably, higher KDM5B expression is associated with higher risk of treatment-resistant metastatic recurrence in estrogen receptor-positive (ER+) breast cancer patients treated with tamoxifen [157]. In TNBC, the emergence of DTP cells in response to chemotherapy exposure is dependent on the removal of H3K27me3, a repressive histone mark that otherwise prevents activation of the persister expression program. Co-administration of a KDM6A/B inhibitor with chemotherapy inhibits H3K27me3 demethylation, thereby preventing chemo tolerance and delaying recurrence [158].

Collectively, these data position DTP as an epigenetically plastic, reversible state that bridges initial response to acquired resistance. However, most evidence of DTP is derived from pre-clinical models. Prospective clinical studies using on-treatment patient samples and the identification of DTP-specific endpoints via novel assays will be required to capture its dynamic and heterogeneous nature and to pinpoint therapeutic targets.

Resistance-associated epigenetic regulators

Terminally resistant cancer clones represent a stage within the continuum of stemness, persistence, and resistance, characterized by stable epigenetic reprogramming. This often involves permanent epigenetic alterations in phenotypic plastic and cell fate determinants in slow-cycling persister subpopulation of cancer cells. These phenotypes commonly result from sustained enhancer activity, the integration of ncRNA regulatory networks, and the dysregulation of chromatin-modifying proteins, including bromodomain and extra-terminal (BET) family members and long non-coding (lncRNA)-mediated chromatin remodeling interactions. As a consequence, this grants cells in persister state to be either gaining survival ability after acute stress, or being able to develop a stable drug resistant phenotype [159].

Bromodomain and extra-terminal (BET) family protein

The bromodomain and extra-terminal (BET) family proteins, particularly bromodomain-containing protein (BRD4), function as chromatin readers that recognize acetylated lysines on histones, facilitating enhancer activation and transcription elongation. In the context of cancers, BRD4 directly interacts with super-enhancer clusters driving oncogenes such as C-MYC, BCL-2, and NF-κB, rendering it a pivotal driver of terminal resistance [160–162]. Persistent BRD4 occupancy stabilizes oncogenic transcriptional factors such as SNAI1 and SNAI2, particularly in castration-resistant prostate cancer (CRPC) [163]. BRD4 also interacts with SWI/SNF chromatin remodeling complexes subunits, including ARID1A and SMARCA4, forming resistance-specific protein complexes that reprogram chromatin architecture [164]. Loss of these remodelers sensitizes cells to BRD4 inhibition, revealing a synthetic vulnerability in resistant subclones. Conversely, BET inhibitors including JQ1 and OTX015, have shown efficacy in disrupting these enhancer circuits, particularly when combined with CDK9 inhibitors or HDAC inhibitors, enhancing transcriptional stress and promoting apoptosis in preclinical models [165, 166].

Non-coding RNAs (ncRNAs)

Non-coding RNAs play a major role in reinforcing resistant epigenomic states. This includes both miRNAs and lncRNAs, which modulate gene expression through chromatin remodeling, transcription factor recruitment, and post-transcriptional control [167].

HOXA transcript antisense RNA (HOTAIR) has emerged as a prototypical oncogenic lncRNA with multifaceted roles in drug resistance. Functionally, HOTAIR exhibits distinct transcriptional activation ability through scaffolding Polycomb Repressive Complex 2 (PRC2) for H3K27me3, and LSD1/CoREST/REST complexes for H3K4me2 and H3K9me3 [168]. This dual chromatin repression mechanism silences tumor suppressor genes such as cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitor 1 A (CDKN1A), DUSP5, and PTEN, enabling uncontrolled proliferation and survival under therapeutic pressure [169]. Importantly, HOTAIR expression is transcriptionally regulated by BRD4. In glioblastoma, BRD4 directly binds to the HOTAIR promoter and is essential for maintaining its expression, as shown by BRD4 ChIP-sequencing (ChIP-seq) and knockdown studies [170]. BET inhibition using JQ1 significantly reduces HOTAIR levels, impairing glioma stem-like cell viability and clonogenic potential. This axis links BRD4-driven enhancer activity to epigenetic silencing pathways downstream of HOTAIR, forming a feedforward circuit that sustains resistant phenotypes [170].

In hormone-dependent cancers, such as breast cancer, HOTAIR has been shown to promote resistance to treatments like tamoxifen and doxorubicin by epigenetically silencing estrogen-metabolizing enzymes and anti-apoptotic regulators [171, 172]. Similarly, HOTAIR enhances sorafenib, taxol, and temozolomide resistance via augmenting expression of miRNAs (e.g.: miR-217, miR-214 and miR-34a) and activation of the downstream signaling pathways, illustrating its crosstalk with miRNA regulatory network [173, 174]. In glioblastoma, HOTAIR serves as a scaffold enhancing the interaction between mesoderm-specific transcript (MEST) and super-enhancers, thereby activating the downstream STAT3, thus sustaining tumor progression [175]. Additionally, HOTAIR interacts with ataxia-telangiectasia mutated- and Rad3-Related (ATR) to facilitate DNA damage response (DDR) pathway through downregulating 53BP and γH2AX, which promotes radioresistance in colorectal cancer cells [176]. Notably, targeting HOTAIR re-sensitizes resistant cells in preclinical models, underscoring its potential as a therapeutic vulnerability in epigenetically driven resistance [176, 177].

Metastasis-associated lung adenocarcinoma transcript 1 (MALAT1) is highly expressed and conserved lncRNA implicated in cancer stemness and therapeutic resistance. Mechanistically, MALAT1 regulates gene expression through multiple modalities, including modulating alternative splicing via interaction with serine/arginine-rich (SR) splicing factors, regulating transcription elongation, and remodeling chromatin accessibility [178–180]. It localizes to nuclear speckles and dynamically associates with the transcriptional machinery [181].

In the settings of colorectal, lung, and gastric cancers, MALAT1 promotes drug resistance by enhancing Wnt/β-catenin and PI3K/AKT signaling pathways [182]. It stabilizes BRD4 occupancy on chromatin, thereby indirectly promoting oncogenic transcription programs [183]. Additionally, MALAT1 has also been shown to facilitate the recruitment of the Switch/Sucrose Non-Fermentable (SWI/SNF) complex chromatin remodeling complex to enhancer regions of resistance-associated genes [184]. In gastric cancer, MALAT1 promotes cisplatin resistance via autophagy induction and upregulation of ATG5, partly through miR-30b sequestration [185]. It also enhances radioresistance by regulating DNA repair genes and interacting with PARP1 to facilitate chromatin remodeling post-irradiation [186, 187].

Importantly, a crucial function of MALAT1 lies in its potential as a competitive endogenous RNA (ceRNA). By sponging miRNAs such as miR-145, miR-200c, and miR-30b, which are negative regulators of drug resistance and EMT [185, 187, 188], MALAT1 modulates post-transcriptional repression of stemness and anti-apoptotic genes, including SOX2, ZEB1, and BCL-2, contributing to chemoresistance in multiple malignancies [189]. Beyond its intracellular roles, MALAT1 can be packaged into EVs, particularly exosomes, allowing resistant tumor cells to horizontally transmit drug-tolerant phenotypes to naïve neighbors [190, 191]. These findings underscore the translational relevance of circulating MALAT1 as a non-invasive biomarker with prognostic significance across multiple solid tumors, including lung, gastric and colorectal cancers [192–194].

On the other hand, dysregulation of tumor-suppressive or oncogenic miRNAs has been associated with therapeutic resistance in various cancer types. While miRNAs have been known for their critical role in targeting key regulators involved in cancer survival, growth, and resistance, they serve as epigenetic regulators to actively target mRNAs encoding epigenetic modifiers, such as DNA methyltransferases (DNMTs), HDACs, and components of the PRC2, which help modulate the epigenetic landscape through feedback mechanisms. For instance, members of the miR-29 family negatively regulate DNMTs through degradation or translational repression of transcripts. In the context of cancer, increased expression of DNMTs promote hypermethylation and silencing of tumor suppressor genes, contributing to resistance to platinum-based chemotherapies in various cancers [195, 196]. Similarly, miR-101 exhibits negative correlation with EZH2 expression, whereby the depletion of miR-101 promotes repression of EZH2, contributing to the cisplatin resistance in epithelial ovarian cancer [197], and tamoxifen resistance in ER-positive breast cancer [198]. Notably, several miRNAs participate in reciprocal regulatory loops with epigenetic enzymes, such as miR-34a, which is epigenetically silenced by SIRT1, while concurrently repressing SIRT1 expression, thereby forming bistable regulatory networks that reinforce resistant phenotypes [199, 200]. These miRNA-driven circuits not only provide mechanistic insights but also represent potential therapeutic targets, such as miRNA mimics or inhibitors, to restore chemosensitivity in resistant cancer models. Overall, miRNAs function as both effectors and regulators of epigenetic reprogramming, and their dysregulation plays a key role in cancer cell plasticity and the development of therapeutic resistance.

Chromatin remodelers and nucleosome dynamics

Chromatin remodelers are essential for dynamically modifying the epigenetic landscape by regulating nucleosome positioning, histone-DNA interactions, and the accessibility of regulatory DNA elements. The four primary families of chromatin remodelers includes SWI/SNF (BAF), Imitation SWItch (ISWI), Chromodomain helicase DNA-binding (CHD), and INO80, which they mainly utilize ATP hydrolysis to reposition or remove nucleosomes, thereby influencing processes such as transcription, DNA replication, and repair [201]. Alterations in these complexes have increasingly been associated with the development of therapy resistance, particularly in tumors characterized by high cellular plasticity.

In oncogenesis, loss-of-function mutations, deletions, or epigenetic silencing of SWI/SNF complex components such as AT-rich interactive domain-containing protein 1 A (ARID1A), SMARCA4 (BRG1), ARID2, and PBRM1, are particularly abundant [202]. These genetic and epigenetic alterations contribute to widespread chromatin compaction, redistribution of enhancer regions, and suppression of lineage-specific gene expression, all of which can facilitate resistance to therapy.

The dysfunction of ARID1A is correlated to platinum-drug and estrogen-degraders resistance, suggested by its strong correlation with the activation of MRP2 in ovarian clear cell carcinoma (OCCC) and switching of breast cancer phenotypes, particularly from ER-dependent luminal cells to ER-independent basal-like cells [203, 204]. Additionally, the loss of ARID1A promotes a chromatin state conducive to EZH2-mediated H3K27me3 [205, 206], leading to the senescence state of malignant cells [207], as well as the repression of tumor suppressor and pro-apoptotic genes including TP53INP1, BIM, and CDKN1A [208, 209]. In addition, loss of ARID1A alters the chromatin acetylation landscape, which paradoxically upregulate HDACs like HDAC6 and HDAC7, which are actively involved in deacetylating histones of downstream target protein, thereby facilitating tumor survival [210, 211]. This synthetic lethality has been successfully targeted using EZH2 inhibitors such as tazemetostat, particularly in OCCC and endometrial cancer [212, 213], and has entered phase II clinical trials (NCT05023655). Interestingly, loss of ARID1A has been associated with adaptive immune resistance in breast cancer cells by increasing chromatin accessibility at the NPM1 locus, thereby enhancing NPM1 expression and promoting the transcriptional activation of the immune checkpoint molecule, programmed cell death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) [214].

SMARCA4, also known as BRG1, is a catalytic ATPase subunit of the SWI/SNF chromatin remodeling complex. It modulates chromatin structure through nucleosome eviction or sliding and plays a vital role in maintaining open chromatin configurations at enhancer regions of tumor suppressor genes [215, 216]. Importantly, loss or mutation of SMARCA4 is associated with significant epigenetic reprogramming in therapy-resistant cancers, particularly through impairing the innate immune response [217, 218]. In NSCLC, tumors lacking functional SMARCA4 exhibit reduced immune cell infiltration and diminished IFN signaling, which contribute to resistance to immune checkpoint blockade therapies [218]. Additionally, SMARCA4 deficiency results in the activation of Hippo-YAP/TAZ mediated EMT, resulting in transition of TNBC cells to mesenchymal-like, linking to cisplatin resistance [109].

In small cell carcinoma of the ovary, hypercalcemic type (SCCOHT), biallelic inactivation of SMARCA4 is a defining characteristic which contributes to resistance in epigenetic treatment, such as trichostatin A (TSA) 5’-AZAC [219–221]. These tumors demonstrate a high dependence on EZH2 and CDK4/6 signaling pathways, rendering them susceptible to combined EZH2 and CDK4/6 inhibition [222, 223]. Furthermore, SMARCA4 deficiency in SCCOHT leads to increased expression of PRC2 target genes and decreased expression of IFN-responsive genes, which may facilitate immune evasion and resistance to DNA-damaging treatments [224]. In parallel, prostate cancer with deficit of SMARCA4, exhibit altered AR signaling by reducing AR cistrome fidelity and activating bypass pathways through glucocorticoid receptor (GR) signaling [225, 226]. This reprogramming contributes to resistance against enzalutamide and suggests potential responsiveness to GR inhibitors or combination epigenetic therapies [226, 227].

Building on the role of SMARCA4, SMARCB1 (also known as BAF47) inactivation further exemplifies how disruption of SWI/SNF subunits reconfigures chromatin landscapes, enabling transcriptional reprogramming that supports drug tolerance and immune evasion [228]. Mechanistically, the absence of SMARCB1 unmasks PRC2 activity, resulting in increased H3K27me3 marks that silence tumor suppressor genes involved in cell cycle regulation (e.g., CDKN2A), apoptosis (e.g., TP53 pathway), and differentiation pathways (e.g., BMP and Notch signaling) [229–232]. The resulting repressive chromatin environment promotes cellular plasticity, stem-like properties, and a dedifferentiated state, which contribute to resistance against chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and emerging epigenetic therapies.

Recent research indicates that SMARCB1-deficient tumors depend heavily on EZH2, making them susceptible to EZH2 inhibitors such as tazemetostat [233]. However, resistance can develop through reactivation of alternative chromatin regulators, including BRD9, HDAC1/2, or residual BAF complexes lacking SMARCB1 [234]. Additionally, loss of SMARCB1 enhances the transcription of oncogenic drivers like MYC and SOX2, while downregulating immune-related gene signatures, including MHC class I molecules and IFN-stimulated genes (ISGs) [235, 236]. These changes can contribute to resistance to immune checkpoint inhibitors. Preclinical studies have shown that combining EZH2 inhibitors with CDK4/6 inhibitors or immune checkpoint blockade can partially overcome resistance mechanisms in SMARCB1-deficient models [113]. Overall, SMARCB1 functions as a key epigenetic regulator, and its deficiency creates a chromatin environment that fosters adaptive resistance to therapy, presenting opportunities for targeted, synthetic lethal treatment strategies.

Despite the significant role of SWI/SNF complexes in epigenetic reprogramming, tumor cells lacking key SWI/SNF components may compensate by upregulating alternative chromatin remodeling factors, such as CHD4 within the NuRD complex [237], SMARCA5 of the ISWI family [238], and INO80 [239], thereby preserving an undifferentiated and therapy-resistant phenotype. Overall, dysregulation of chromatin remodelers reshapes the tumor epigenome, supports survival pathways centered on DNA repair, and impairs apoptotic responses, thus, contributing to resistance across a broad spectrum of therapies.

Integrative model of epigenetic plasticity

Cancer resistance arises not from isolated molecular events, but through a dynamic and reversible reorganization of the chromatin landscape. This plasticity enables cancer cells to switch between stem-like, drug-tolerant, and fully resistant phenotypes in response to therapeutic interventions. These transitions are not driven solely by genetic mutations but by coordinated epigenetic reprogramming activity by a network of chromatin remodelers, histone modifiers, ncRNAs, and epigenetic readers. Recent studies highlight a “stemness–persister–resistance continuum,” wherein cells initially adopt a poised, quasi-stem-like state that supports survival under sublethal drug exposure [240]. From a therapeutic perspective, combinatorial strategies targeting epigenetic regulators, either within epigenetic-drugs or in synergy with chemotherapeutic and targeted agents, may offer promising approaches to disrupt epigenetic plasticity and transition to resistant states [241, 242]. Such interventions may not only halt resistance development but also re-sensitize tumors to previously ineffective treatments. Therefore, a deeper understanding of the temporal dynamics of epigenetic reprogramming will be critical for improving the cancer treatment efficacy.

Technological platforms for epigenetic biomarker discovery

As stated in the introduction, DNA-protein interactions and transcriptional regulatory mechanisms facilitate chromatin modifications and differential gene expression. Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assays have been applied to study histone modifications and transcription factor binding status [243]. Cleavage Under Targets and Release Using Nuclease (CUT & RUN) [244] or its variation, Cleavage Under Targets and Tagmentation (CUT&Tag) [245], meanwhile, is known for being an alternative to ChIP, offering an alternative method for mapping protein-DNA interactions. Both methods use antibody-guided cleavage to precisely map chromatin features, making it effective for analyzing small, limited clinical samples.

In order to distinguish the stem-like cells from more differentiated populations, chromatin accessibility profiling enables the identification of primitive, self-renewing cells within primary tumors [246]. Meanwhile, the single-cell Assay for Transposase-Accessible Chromatin using sequencing (scATAC-seq), which maps open chromatin regions and exposes epigenetic characteristics associated with resistance and stemness, is employed by researchers to address the heterogeneity at single-cell resolution [3]. Due to the various chromatin accessibility patterns, scATAC-seq is beneficial in identifying stem-like cancer cells from their more differentiated counterparts in multiple cancers, such as glioblastoma [246]. As such, scATAC-seq has provided its potential to identify rare, therapy-resistant clones in malignancies with distinct chromatin accessibility patterns, including breast cancer and glioblastoma. For instance, treatment resistance has been associated with subpopulations enriched for EMT-related transcription factors, including ZEB1 and AP1, in breast cancer and melanoma [8]. At the same time, mesenchymal-like and progenitor-like states have been found in glioblastoma [246]. By using these methods, accessibility-based biomarkers that predict treatment response or recurrence can be found. Although scATAC-seq provides unprecedented chromatin accessibility resolution in cancer stem-like cells, the current techniques are limited in their practical scalability by high computational demands, inconsistent coverage, and extreme data sparsity, which detects only ~ 1–10% of accessible peaks per cell [247].

On the other hand, the whole-genome bisulfite sequencing (WGBS), as well as DNA methylation arrays, have been applied for methylome analyses in a detailed and high-resolution manner [248, 249]. Still, the WGBS, with the support of advanced library preparation and data alignment, is the gold standard for single-base methylation analysis [250]. However, its high cost and analytical complexity limit its feasibility for large-scale or population-based studies [251]. Recently, the latest Infinium Human Methylation EPIC v2.0 (900 K EPIC v2) microarray demonstrated high reproducibility across diverse tissues and enabled comparisons between healthy and cancerous cells [252, 253]. This platform also supports large-scale DNA methylation profiling with lower cost and simpler bioinformatic tools, making it well-suited for large-scale population-based studies [253]. Together, these technologies provide powerful tools for epigenetic biomarker discovery, early cancer detection, and treatment monitoring, contributing to the advancement of precision medicine through accurate and scalable assessment of methylation dynamics in health and disease [249, 254].

Therapeutic implications: reversing resistance in cancer stem cell

As discussed, the significance of cancer stemness is closely linked to the phenotype of drug resistance. Targeting CSCs (either through oncogenic pathways or specific targets) has been proposed as a promising strategy for eradicating tumors [255]. This remains an active research area, particularly in efforts to reverse cancer stemness and overcome drug resistance, both of which are key to advancing this therapeutic direction. The EZH2, HDACs, and BET have emerged as prominent therapeutic strategies in recent research to tackle cancer stemness and drug resistance, as evidenced by multiple studies and ongoing clinical trials [256–258].

In the context of single epigenetic-targeting therapeutics, the EZH2 regulates the expression of stemness factors [258, 259], and it is particularly enriched in high-grade, complex karyotype sarcoma CSCs, conferring acquired resistance to anthracycline-based chemotherapies and providing a high-value therapeutic target for developing relevant treatments. Tazemetostat, an EZH2 inhibitor (EZH2i), has been shown to reverse resistance by resensitizing doxorubicin-resistant dedifferentiated liposarcoma cells to chemotherapy [260]. Additionally, through a study of hepatocellular carcinoma, EZH2 inhibition promotes the targeting of NOTCH1 by miRNAs, suppresses NOTCH1 signaling, and thereby reverses the stemness phenotype and resensitizes the cancer cells to sorafenib-induced elimination [261]. Additionally, EZH2 overexpression has been confirmed in prostate CSCs, and this contributes to the phenotype of docetaxel resistance. The upregulation of miR-101-3p and miR-138-5p potentially suppressed EZH2 levels and restored sensitivity to docetaxel [259]. Besides in vitro cell line studies, the in vivo nude mouse model shows that EZH2 downregulation in bladder cancer improves the radiotherapeutic outcome [262].

Regarding the role of EZH2i in immunotherapy, EZH2 can sensitize the cancer cells to T cell-mediated killing, promoting T cell proliferation, and IFN-γ production against head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC) [263]. In an in vitro melanoma study, resistance to anti-cytotoxic T–lymphocyte-associated antigen 4 (anti-CTLA-4) and interleukin-2 (IL-2) immunotherapies was associated with EZH2 enrichment. However, EZH2i inactivation was shown to reverse immune-evasion, and a synergistic tumoricidal effect was observed when combining an EZH2i with either IL-2cx or anti-CTLA-4 [264]. Whereas in the in vivo investigation in anti-PD-1-resistant mouse models, the combination of GSK126 (an EZH2i) with anti-PD-1 synergistically increased major histocompatibility complex (MHC) class I antigen expression, suggesting potential to enhance immunotherapy effectiveness [263].

The HDAC inhibitors are another promising target for therapeutic development. The novel HDAC inhibitor (HDACi), domatinostat, has been shown to induce the accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), specifically targeting the CSC subpopulation. In pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC), domatinostat sensitized tumors to gemcitabine (G)/taxol (T) doublet chemotherapy by promoting apoptosis through the downregulation of Forkhead Box M1 (FOXM1) mRNA and protein levels, which impaired the nuclear translocation and transcriptional activity, subsequently altering genes involved in redox homeostasis, DNA repair and stemness [265]. Through a study of NSCLC, the silencing of HDAC1 via an HDACi, together with tribbles pseudokinase 1 (TRIB1) knockdown, can reduce cisplatin-induced CSC enrichment and further enhance elimination via cisplatin re-sensitization in both in vitro and in vivo models [266].

Additionally, the BET proteins, bromodomain-containing protein 2 (BRD2), BRD3, and BRD4, which are associated with androgen receptor (AR) signaling, are overexpressed in castration-resistant prostate cancer [267, 268]. BET inhibitors (BETi) effectively downregulate these proteins, leading to reduced expression of androgen receptor splice variant 7 (AR-V7), which eventually disrupts persistent AR signaling and inhibits in vitro tumor growth, making BETis promising candidates for treating CRPC-resistant patients [269]. The BRD4 inhibitor JQ1 has also been shown to decrease MYC expression, thereby resensitizing estrogen receptor-positive (ER+) and/or long-term estrogen-deprived in vitro breast cancer cells to everolimus, a widely used mechanistic target of rapamycin (mTOR) inhibitor [270]. Notably, when JQ1 was combined with cisplatin, it improved survival in in vivo mice models with epithelial ovarian cancer by slowing tumor growth and downregulating stem-related gene expression [271].

Interestingly, the combinatorial epigenetic-targeting therapeutics target different CSC-associated biomarkers and yield superior therapeutic outcomes. This may be attributed to the complexity of solid tumors, where combination therapies can interact with multiple targets and pathways to enhance therapeutic outcomes [272]. The combinatorial therapy of EZH2 and BMI1 proto-oncogene (BMI1) inhibitors targets both proneural, perivascular and mesenchymal compartments of glioma TME effectively, bringing down the tumorsphere formation and eradicating the glioma stem cells in the in vitro studies [273]. At the same time, a similar observation was also consistent with the combinatorial treatment of EZH2i and BETi. Both provide a synergistic effect to suppress cell proliferation, reduce clonal formation, and induce a tumoricidal effect via cell cycle arrest, followed by apoptosis induction through the downregulation of c-MYC and NF-κB [274]. Moreover, the combinatorial treatment of BETi with HDACi has been applied against BRAF inhibitor-resistant melanoma via the disruption of AKT/Hippo signaling axis [275], as well as medulloblastoma via the repression of SYK and MSI1 activities, leading to the tumoricidal G2 cell cycle arrest and then apoptosis [276]. Additionally, a similar observation was also concluded in glioblastoma [277]. There are also in vivo studies supporting the combinatorial use of two inhibitors to effectively treat cancers. The combination of β-catenin and EZH2 inhibitors reduces CSC populations and tumor volume in HNSCC, both in vitro and in vivo, and also sensitizes cisplatin-resistant cells to chemotherapy [278]. Additionally, the combinatorial therapeutics of an EZH2 inhibitor and an HDAC inhibitor show curative potential against hormone-insensitive CRPC, establishing a tumoricidal effect in an in vivo mouse model. The mechanism behind this is the derepression of activating transcription factor 3 (ATF3), which overcomes the effect of EZH2-PRC2, enabling tumor cells to become sensitive to conventional chemotherapeutics [279].

The exploratory efforts of epigenetic-targeting therapeutics have never ended. For example, EZH2i like tazemetostat have completed phase I and II trials for relapsed or refractory B-cell non-Hodgkin lymphoma and advanced solid tumors, as well as in combination with prednisolone for diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL) [280]. Another EZH2i, CPI-1205, has completed a phase I study in B-cell lymphoma [281]. For BETi, compounds such as BMS-986,158 and BMS-986,378 (also known as CC-90010) have recently completed phase I clinical trials in pediatric cancer [282]. In contrast, CPI-0610 underwent a phase I trial in previously treated multiple myeloma [283]. In the case of HDACis, several inhibitors have advanced to clinical studies. Vorinostat completed a phase I trial in treating advanced, resistant BRAF V600-mutant melanoma [284]. Other trials include a combination study of belinostat with cisplatin and etoposide for small cell lung and other advanced malignancies [285], as well as the AR-42 combined with pomalidomide for relapsed multiple myeloma [286] and with decitabine for AML [287]. Preclinical studies in early phases demonstrated that the epigenetic drugs can be potentially applied in clinical settings, including EZH2, HDAC and BET inhibitors, as discussed earlier. Nevertheless, the translation into clinical success remains limited to only a few reaching phase III, such as the HDACi, Chidamide (NCT02482753) and BETi, Pelabresin (NCT04603495). This gap highlights the need for assessing technology readiness level and implementing robust cross-platform validation strategies. Addressing these traditional bottlenecks is critical to ensure that promising biomarkers can be effectively deployed in diverse clinical settings.

Despite promising preclinical and early clinical data, there are certain bottlenecks that hinder the clinical application of epigenetic-targeting therapeutics. Resistance to these epigenetic-targeting therapeutics is another hurdle to tackle for optimal treatment efficacy, as resistance evolution is a characteristic of tumors. In the context of solid tumors, resistance to EZH2i may be observed in DLBCL, which is associated with the activation of the IGF-1R-MEK-PI3K signaling axis [288]. The phosphorylation of EZH2, induced by ribosomal S6 kinase 4 (RSK4) overexpression in glioblastoma stem cells, as well as the subsequent signal cascade of STAT3 activation, also reduces the efficacy of EZH2i [289]. Furthermore, the leukemia-resistant phenotype, characterized by the acetylation of histone 3 lysine-27 residue (H3K27) upon modulation of the p300/CBP complex, has contributed to resistance against EZH inhibitors, as well as in mixed lineage leukemia 1 (MLL1) [290]. Besides this, resistance to BET inhibitors has been observed and reported in leukemia [291]. Several studies have previously aimed to solve this problem. For example, the combination of EZH2 and RSK4 inhibitors has been studied in vitro, to overcome the resistance in glioblastoma [289]. The inhibitory combination of EZH2 and DOT1L has been shown to have a synergistic tumoricidal effect in the in vitro study by targeting the histone methyltransferase in B-cell lymphoma [292]. Zhou and Yu raised concerns regarding the “off-target” effects of epigenetic modulators. In their review several clinical trial drugs such as DNA methylation inhibitors, HDACi or BETi, have been shown to induce hematologic toxicities, lymphatic and gastrointestinal systems disorder due to off-target activity [293]. Furthermore, the heterogeneity of CSCs suggests that the epigenetic landscape varies across different cell types. This variability can lead to genomic instability and incomplete reprogramming, which may contribute to aberrant outcomes when using epigenetic drugs in clinical settings [294]. Although epigenetic reprograming agents have shown potential to reverse resistance of cancer stem cells towards conventional therapies, their broader clinical application remains limited by several bottlenecks, including the development of resistance to epigenetic drugs, off-target activity that lead to adverse outcomes, and significant heterogeneity across cancer types. Therefore, future studies are encouraged to address these limitations, such as through the combination use of multiple inhibitors to enhance the therapeutic efficacy against drug resistant CSCs.

Conclusion and future perspectives

Epigenetic reprogramming plays a pivotal role in initiating and sustaining cancer stem-like phenotypes, which significantly contribute to therapeutic resistance across diverse tumor types [294, 295]. This process involves various epigenetic modifications, such as abnormal DNA methylation, altered histone modifications, dysregulation of ncRNAs, and chromatin remodeling [19], where they collectively regulate cellular plasticity with reversible transitions between differentiated and stem-like states. Consequently, the epigenetic reprogramming drives tumor heterogeneity, immune evasion, metastasis, treatment resistance, and disease recurrence in the context of oncogenesis, underscoring the importance of epigenetic biomarkers in cancer management [294, 295].

Dynamic and responsive epigenetic biomarkers offer a promising path toward personalized cancer management aligned with tumor evolution in real-time. Technological advancements have enabled high-resolution mapping of chromatin accessibility and methylation changes, enhancing the precision of biomarker discovery and therapeutic interventions [19, 296]. Additionally, liquid biopsy approaches utilizing EVs provide a minimally invasive means of detecting circulating epigenetic markers [297], while organoid models exposed to drug stimuli support translational biomarker discovery with clinical relevance [298, 299]. Again, epigenetic regulators therefore become targets and biomarkers for oncology clinical applications. The translational effort, meanwhile, is crucial for better treatment outcomes and robust diagnostics [300].

Future research in spatial epigenomics will be instrumental in advancing our understanding of tumor biology and therapeutic resistance [272]. The ability to simultaneously map epigenomic and transcriptomic features at near single-cell resolution opens new avenues to investigate how regulatory networks are spatially organized within the TME that leads to tumor heterogeneity [301]. Spatial epigenomics enables the identification of cell subtypes and regulatory elements, uncovering dynamic interactions that drive immune evasion, tumor progression, and therapy resistance [301–303]. A key priority will be to translate these insights into clinically actionable strategies that inform personalized treatment while reducing toxicity and cost. A major barrier, however, lies in the development of advanced computational tools capable of handling the high-dimensional complexity of spatial omics data. Equally important is the continued refinement of spatial transcriptomic technologies to achieve greater sensitivity, cost-effectiveness, broader tissue coverage, and higher spatial resolution [304]. Moving forward, the integration of proteomic and metabolomic layers with spatial epigenomic and transcriptomic data is expected to provide a more holistic view of tissue ecosystems, enabling the development of next-generation diagnostics and therapies tailored to the complexity of individual tumors [305].

Epigenetic–metabolic crosstalk is increasingly recognized as a promising avenue for targeting CSCs vulnerabilities, offering new opportunities to overcome therapy resistance and prevent relapse. Emerging evidence shows that CSCs depend on dynamic metabolic pathways that are tightly regulated by epigenetic mechanisms [306]. Key metabolites such as fumarate, α-ketoglutarate, and succinate not only sustain CSCs survival but also function as epigenetic modulators, reshaping gene expression programs and reinforcing stemness [307, 308]. Future therapeutic strategies are likely to focus on combining metabolic inhibitors with epigenetic agents, or by integrating these approaches with immunotherapies, to achieve synergistic efficacy and minimize drug resistance [309]. However, significant challenges remain, including the metabolic plasticity of CSCs and potential off-target effects of epigenetic drugs [308, 310]. Addressing these issues will be essential to translate epigenetic–metabolic interventions into effective and durable clinical therapies.

Furthermore, nanoparticle-mediated epigenetic reprogramming offers a powerful strategy to eliminate CSCs by reshaping their epigenetic architecture, diminishing stemness, and enhancing therapeutic response. Platforms such as gold nanoparticles (AuNPs) [311], epigenetic drug-loaded nanofibers [312], and silica nanoparticles [313] enable precise delivery, lower systemic toxicity, and the simultaneous modulation of multiple epigenetic marks, with tunable release profiles and preferential tumor accumulation. Yet, key challenges remain, including achieving strict specificity, avoiding unintended epigenomic alterations, and translating preclinical efficacy into clinical benefit. Addressing these hurdles is critical for advancing next-generation nanocarriers capable of delivering epigenetic therapeutics with heightened selectivity, not only in oncology but also in other diseases where epigenetic modulation holds significant promise [311, 314].

Meanwhile, real-time epigenetic monitoring technologies have emerged in translational oncology [315]. Along with the advancement of circulating cell-free DNA methylation profiling, DNAzyme-based sensors, and the Clustered Regularly Interspaced Short Palindromic Repeats (CRISPR)/dCas9 system, the dynamics of epigenetic alterations and tumor evolution can be closely traced and monitored [316]. For instance, demethylase-activated DNAzyme amplifiers enable precise detection of demethylase activity for gene regulation and live-cell imaging [317]. Epigenome-editing tools such as zinc finger proteins, transcription activator-like effector nucleases (TALENs), and CRISPR/dCas9 fusions are now employed to study and manipulate epigenetic marks with high specificity, offering a programmable platform for gene regulation and adaptive therapy [318].

Knowledge about chromatin 3D conformations, including chromatin topology and promoter-enhancer interaction (PEI), is suggested to be the next-generation epigenetic biomarkers [319]. The establishment of these conformations represents the portfolio of cellular state and function [320]. However, the technology limitation for scalable applications remains to be developed for such purposes. Furthermore, epigenetic clocks such as Horvath’s clock, Hannum’s clock, DNAm PhenoAge, and DNAm GrimAge offer insight into biological ageing, stem cell division history, and potentially cancer dormancy and its reactivation [321–323]. Mitotic-based clocks, such as epiTOC2, estimate cumulative stem cell divisions and are accelerated in both malignant and premalignant tissues [324]. More epigenetic markers will be revealed for their significance in oncogenesis. However, the association between epigenetic regulations and cancer dormancy remains to be elucidated with a better paradigm, to illustrate the mechanisms behind each epigenetic phenotypic marker [325].
