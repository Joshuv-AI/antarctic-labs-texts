Nighttime blood pressure levels and phenotypes: prognostic value and reproducibility
Authors: Francesca Coccina, Sante D Pierdomenico
Published: 2026

Several studies have shown that ambulatory blood pressure (BP) is superior to clinic BP in predicting cardiovascular outcomes in patients with hypertension.1 Moreover, ambulatory BP monitoring has given the opportunity to identify specific parameters, such as, daytime BP, nighttime BP and nighttime BP phenotypes, including dippers (nighttime BP fall ≥10% and <20%), nondippers (nighttime BP fall ≥0% and <10%), reverse dippers (nighttime BP fall <0%) and extreme dippers (nighttime BP fall ≥20%). Various studies, in different countries, have shown that nighttime BP levels and its phenotypes negatively affect prognosis.2–7 Globally, regarding phenotypes, it has been reported that nondippers, reverse dippers, and extreme dippers are at higher risk of cardiovascular events than dippers.2–5 These objective data have been obtained after several years of follow-up and taking into account other risk factors, suggesting that nighttime BP and its phenotypes matter and that nighttime BP pattern at baseline may reasonably represent the prevalent one of the studied subject in his/her days to come, although probably not always. The reproducibility of mean nighttime BP and its patterns has been questioned in some studies that reported poor/fair reproducibility according to various statistical metrics.8–10 It is well known that BP itself, regardless of the method of measurement and the parameter studied, varies over time due to several factors. The first consideration that arises is whether the reproducibility of mean nocturnal BP and its patterns is affected by the conditions under which it is assessed, which are themselves not standardized and not reproducible, rather than by the fact that nighttime BP is inherently unreliable. Notably, daytime BP may be affected by a number of factors, such as physical activity, emotional status, body posture, smoking habit, changes in lifestyle, and seasonal temperature.8–10 On the other hand, nighttime BP may be affected by sleep quality, number of night awakenings, sleep apnea, the valid number of BP readings, the use of fixed time intervals or diary to define nighttime period or an imperfect identification of the nighttime period despite the patient’s diary.8–10 In treated patients, therapeutic changes prescribed at different times of the day, at different dosages or with drugs having different pharmacokinetics, as well as a variable therapeutic adherence in the context of an unchanged therapy, may also be relevant confounding factors. Given the aforesaid considerations, it is not unexpected that in one single future reevaluation over a vast span of time the results may not be exactly the same. Thus, it is reasonable to assume that the reproducibility of mean nighttime BP and its patterns should be evaluated in the short term and under conditions that are as standardized as possible. For example, between the first and the second 24 h period of a 48-h ambulatory BP monitoring, it has been reported a good-to-excellent reproducibility of mean daytime systolic/diastolic and mean nighttime systolic/diastolic BP (intra-class correlation coefficients of 0.85/0.93 and 0.82/0.86, respectively) and a fair/moderate reproducibility of nondipping (kappa statistic 0.47).11 Another way to improve reproducibility is by using other methods less intrusive on nighttime rest. For example, it has been reported that mean nighttime systolic/diastolic BP measured by a less-disturbing wrist-type nocturnal home BP monitor has a good reproducibility (intra-class correlation coefficient 0.83/0.80),12 and is an independent risk factor for left ventricular hypertrophy and its potential sequelae.13 Moreover, the factors reported above, particularly those affecting daytime BP, and thus daytime to nighttime BP ratio, may be more frequently operating in the adult population whose daily life is quite heterogeneous. The vast majority of studies evaluating nighttime BP patterns reproducibility included adult patients.8–10 Only few studies with few subjects evaluated the reproducibility of mean nighttime BP and its patterns in older adults.14,15 These subjects show a more homogeneous daily life, suggesting a potential higher reproducibility of ambulatory BP parameters; in addition, these subjects have higher nighttime BP and higher prevalence of nondippers and reverse dippers, potentially suggesting greater attention to these parameters. Thus, it would be interesting to deepen our knowledge about mean nighttime BP levels and phenotypes reproducibility in older adults. Another aspect to consider is that overall, mean nighttime BP was found to be more reproducible than nighttime BP phenotypes.8–10 The lower reproducibility of nighttime BP phenotypes could be due to their definition with arbitrary cutoffs, whose values are more easily influenced by even minor BP variations. One last topic to discuss is that reproducibility of almost all ambulatory BP parameters is better than that of clinic BP.10,14,15 Even today, the decision to start or modify antihypertensive treatment is based on clinic BP which is less reproducible and has lower prognostic value than ambulatory BP parameters. Thus, considering the literature data on the prognostic value of ambulatory BP parameters, but also those on their reproducibility which however need to be explored in all age categories, perhaps the time has come to start taking morning BP/surge, daytime BP, nighttime BP, and nocturnal BP phenotypes—in short, the circadian BP profile—into consideration for therapeutic decisions, including both pharmacological intensification and timing of antihypertensive dosing.
