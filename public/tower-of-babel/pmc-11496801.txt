First Report on Salvia Sahendica Boiss & Buhs roots biocomponents characterization by GC-MS and HPLC and Antibacterial Potency
Authors: Elnaz Nourozi, Ahad Hedayati, Mohammad Hossein Mirjalili, Hadi Madani, Atousa Aliahmadi, Ali Akbar Zahedi, Zahra Aslani
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractTreatment of bacterial infections with antibiotic resistance is complicated. For this reason, new therapeutic methods are needed to control bacterial infections. Therefore, it is necessary to discover new antibiotics from medicinal plants that can destroy drug-resistant bacteria. Essential oils (EO) of some medicinal plants have antimicrobial effects and can be used as antimicrobial agents in the treatment of infections and food industries. The aim of this study was to determine the EO composition and antimicrobial effects of EO and polyphenols profile of Salvia Sahendica roots for the first time. Chemical compounds and antibacterial activity of EOs against Staphylococcus aureus and Bacillus cereus as gram-positive and Escherichia coli and Pseudomonas aeruginosa as gram- negative bacteria were evaluated using microtiter broth dilution method. Studied organs of S. sahendica showed significant diversity in terms of type and percentage of essential oil and polyphenol compounds. Overall, 44, 46, 42, and 45 compounds were identified in the essential oil of leaves, flowers, stems, and roots of this plant, which constituted 99.6, 99.3, 98.2, and 99.4% of total oil composition, respectively. The current study collectively demonstrated inhibitory effects ranged from strong (S. aureus) to low inhibition (P. aeruginosa). According to the HPLC result, the amounts of coumaric acid, chlorogenic acid, and gallic acid compounds in the root are higher than in other plant parts, which indicate that the root can be a suitable source for the production of these valuable polyphenol compounds. Due to the results, it is possible to hope for the application of EO from S. sahendica as natural antibacterial in the pharmaceutical and food industries.Keywords: Minimum growth inhibition, Essential oil, Chemical diversity, Antimicrobial activity, Gas chromatography, HPLCSubject terms: Biochemistry, Microbiology, Plant sciences

Introduction

The increase drug-resistance of microorganisms and inadequacy of common chemical drugs for antimicrobial use in infectious diseases as well as increasing consumers and health care providers perception on harmful effects of chemical and synthetic food preservatives, has been lead to discover extensive research for obtain natural compounds derived from plant, animal and microbial sources with a wide range of biological activities, in order to improve the shelf life of food against the harmful effects of chemical preservatives1. The antimicrobial effects of plant essential oils against both gram-positive and negative bacteria have been confirmed in many studies and in many cases, these properties have been related to the presence of active monoterpenes2,3. The study of medicinal plants in order to discover new medications with fewer side effects and higher economic value has extensively explored in worldwide (Anand et al., 2019). Plant essential oils and various bioactive metabolites are well-known as substances with antimicrobial activity and low or no toxic effects4. Therefore, plant-derived products can be used as a natural replacement for antibiotics with protective effect of medicinal and food materials against microbial spoilage. Medicinal plants play two major roles in food industry: they add flavors to the cuisine and preserving food from by their antimicrobial and anti-oxidation actions5. In this regard, due to varied climates and high plant diversity in Iran, it has been possible to identify secondary metabolites in different plants and extract them in order to produce these substances in large quantities and industrial scale.

Salvia is one of the most important genera of Lamiaceae, with about 1000 species distributed throughout most of the world. Recently, the medicinal and aromatic properties of different species of this genus have led to use as medicinal plants and food industry6. Salvia sahendica Boiss. & Buhse is an endemic species of Iran, which is used in folk medicine to treatment bacterial, fungal infections and indigestion7. Many biological activities (antibacterial, antioxidant, anti-HIV, anti-inflammatory, anti-Alzheimer, anti-ulcer and cholesterol reducer) of this plant have been reported8. The extracts of this plant have the highest total antibacterial activity compared to the antibiotics available in the market9. Essential oil is common bioactive compounds in this genus with more than 100 constituents, including monoterpene hydrocarbons, oxygenated monoterpenes, sesquiterpene hydrocarbons, oxygenated sesquiterpenes and diterpenes, which show many biological activities10. The quantity and quality of chemical compounds of essential oil isolated from several parts of plant are different and can be influenced by ecological and environmental factors, phenological growth stages and genetics11. The previous investigations were carried out on this plant have been found that essential oil content was 0.67% (w/w) and α-pinene (29.4%) and β-pinene (34.8%) were the main constituents. Other secondary metabolites found in salvia species include valuable polyphenolic compounds such as rosmarinic acid, gallic acid, chlorogenic acid, etc12.

Staphylococcus aureus, Bacillus cereus, Escherichia coli and Pseudomonas aeruginosa are most common bacteria that considered as the main cause of antibiotic resistance occurs in burns, surgical, etc. infections. Also, the reports indicate that these microorganisms are important pathogenic bacteria in relation to the milk, meat and many other foodstuffs products13. For this reason, finding a new antimicrobial substance, especially of plant origin, is an exclusively important objective. The antimicrobial and antifungal effects of plant essential oils on various microbes have been reported14. The essential oils functional mechanism is related to their chemical composition and antimicrobial activity, but do not have the same mechanism in all cases. Despite this, the effect of plant essential oils against the cell wall structure has been confirmed15. The hydrophobic property of essential oils, allows them to penetrate into the bacterial cell membrane and increase its permeability, which leads to disruption in all vital activities related to the cell membrane, release ions, vital compounds and eventually the bacterial cell death16. Toxic effects on membrane structure and act have been generally used to illustrate the antimicrobial potential of essential oils and their monoterpene compounds17. Most basic research on the antimicrobial effects of essential oils have been carried out in laboratory environment and then their functional properties have been evaluated in food models. In order to confirm the benefits of using natural preservatives and antimicrobial compounds, these compounds should be studied separate or in combination with another food safety factors to create synergistic effects18. According to the above, the combined preservatives system due to low economical cost of food production and guaranteed antibacterial effect is recommended.

The research on the antimicrobial effects of various essential oils, proves the ability of these compounds in inhibiting the growth of wide range of pathogenic and spoilage microorganisms in food. Therefore, according to these indications, we investigated the polyphenol compounds, chemical composition of S. sahendica roots essential oil, and its direct effects on some pathogenic bacteria, for the first time. The results of this research can be used for pharmaceutical and food industries as well as medicinal plant breeders in selecting each organ for consumption and breeding purposes.

Materials and methods

Preparing plant material

The four main parts (leaves, flowers, stems and roots) of S. sahendica plant were collected from Sahand’s mountain (altitude 1579 m, latitude: 37° 57’ N, longitude: 46° 05’ E) in east Azerbaijan province, northwestern Iran, at the flowering stage in July 2013 (Fig. 1A). In order to, extract and isolate essential oils, different organs were collected separately and dried in the room temperature at darkness condition and kept in closed containers until use. The voucher specimen number (1992) was deposited at the herbarium of research institute of medicinal plants and raw materials of Shahid Beheshti University-Tehran, Iran.

Geographical locations of S. sahendica plant collected from Sahand’s mountain (A) and Chemical structure of the main components of S. sahendica plant part essential oil (B).

Essential oil isolation

In order to extract and determine the percentage of essential oil by hydro distillation method, 30 gr dry material of each organ was extracted in a Clevenger apparatus and based on the British Pharmacopoeia19 for 3 h and with three repetitions. The resulting essential oil was dried with anhydrous sodium sulfate and kept in a sealed glass at 4 ◦C until analysis. Since essential oil has limited solubility in culture media, an emulsifier/stabilizer compound (E/S) without significant antimicrobial effects (dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO)) was used to dissolves essential oil (22 µl of essential oil and 282 µl of DMSO solvent was stirred with a shaker until solution went clear. This initial stock was used for subsequent experiments).

Essential oil analysis (GC-FID and GC-MS)

Finnigan-Thermoquest gas chromatograph (Trace GC 2000 Series) equipped with mass spectrometer (capillary column DB-5MS, Length 30 m x 0.25 mm inner diameter x 25/2 µm film thickness) was used. The injection port temperature was 250 °C and flame ionization detector (FID) temperature was300 ◦C. The oven temperature was held at 60 ◦C for 3 min, then the temperature was programmed from 60 °C to 250 °C at a rate of 5 °C/min and held for 10 min at 250 °C. The helium was used at a flow rate of 1/1 ml/ min, electron ionization (EI) of 70 electron volts (eV) and scan range of 43–456 m/z as a carrier gas. The sample injection into GC was manually without dilution. The quantitative determination of each constituent was used by area normalization method without consideration response factor (RF). The 6890 N Agilent gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC–MS) coupled with a DB-5 fused silica capillary column (0.25 μm film thickness 30 m x 0.25 mm); flame ionization detector (GC–FID) (Agilent); was used to analyze. The oven temperature program was as follows: injection chamber temperatures 250 ◦C; oven temperature 60–250 ◦C at a rate of 4 ◦C/min; detector temperature 300 ◦C; helium as carrier gas; linear speed of 31.5 cm/s; scan time 1 s; split ratio 1:60; EI 70 eV with 300 amu mass range. The oils diluted in dichloromethane (2 µL oil and 2 mL solvent) and 2 µL of each solution was injected.

Identification of essential oil components

The constituents of the essential oils were identified by retention indices, mass spectra and comparing with mass spectral library in the SATURN data system by Xcalibur software, version 1.4 (Thermo Finnigan, San Jose, CA)20.

Principal component analysis (PCA)

To determine the most important variables influencing the differentiation of various organs’ EO composition, principal component analysis (PCA) was performed using the SPSS ver. 19 software. In the analysis of the main components, the variables about > 0.5% of 42 essential oil components from four organs were selected.

Antibacterial activity bioassay

Bacterial strains preparation

The bacterial strains used in this research include: Staphylococcus aureus (PTCC1431) and Bacillus cereus (PTCC 1015) as gram-positive and Escherichia coli (PTCC1399) and Pseudomonas aeruginosa (PTCC1430) as gram- negative bacteria were purchased from Pasteur Institute of Iran (Tehran, Iran). All bacterial strains were grown at 37 °C on Mueller-Hinton agar medium.

Evaluation of minimum inhibitory concentrations (MIC)

The MIC was determined by using the CLSI (US Laboratory and Standard Institute) microtitre broth dilution method with minor modification21. Bacterial strains were inoculated from freshly cultured bacteria adjusted to the 0.5 McFarland turbidity standard. Serial dilutions of oil were prepared in concentrations ranging from 64 to 0.125 mg /ml in 96-well microdilution plastic trays containing Müller-Hinton broth with 0.5% Tween 80 as solvent. Cefixime was used as the standard antibiotic (positive control) under identical conditions and each experiment was performed in three replications. Pared Micro-dilution trays were incubated at 37 °C for 22 h.

Methanolic extraction for measuring polyphenolic compounds using high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC)

In order to extract polyphenolic compounds, 0.5 g of each of the leaf, flower, stem, and root were powdered with liquid nitrogen and 5 ml of 80% HPLC-grade methanol was added to it. then, the sample mixture was placed in an ultrasonic device at 30 ◦C for 15 min. Finally, the methanol extract was centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 10 min, and after filtration by a 0.22-µm filter, for analysis, it was injected into the HPLC device. For polyphenolic compounds separating, identifying, and determining the quantitative content, used the 1100 series HPLC device equipped with an injection loop with 20 µl volume, Four-Solvent Gradient Pump, and Diode-Array Detection (DAD) (Agilent Co., United States). The separation process was performed on the octadecylsilane column (in 25 cm length, the inner diameter of 4.6 mm, and particle size of 5 μm). The standard curves of polyphenolic compounds were drawn based on the standard area under the curve of all compounds (produced by Sigma Co.) in 5, 10, 25, 50, 100, 250, 500 mg mL− 1 concentrations. The solvent flow rate was 1 mL/min, and the mobile phase included Acetonitrile and Acetic Acid buffer in a 10:80 ratio. In this research, different wavelengths were used to analyze polyphenol compounds. (250 nm for Chlorogenic acid, Rosmarinic acid and Quercetin, 272 nm for Gallic acid and Cinnamic acid, 310 nm for Caffeic acid, Coumaric acid, Apigenin and Rutin)22.

Statistical analysis

The data analysis of essential oils was performed in three repetitions using MSTATC statistics software and cluster analysis was carried by Ward’s method based on squared Euclidean distance (SED) using the data matrix obtained from the components of the essential oil with SPSS version 19 software. The ANOVA analysis of antimicrobial activity was arranged in a completely randomized design using SPSS ver. 19 software and Duncan’s multiple range test (DMRT) at 0.01 probability.

Results

Essential oil yield and chemical composition

Variously colored essential oils i.e., light-green (leaves), yellow (flowers), dark-yellow (stems), and red (roots), were isolated by hydro distillation from the different parts of plant. The mean total-oil yield (w/w %, based on dry weight) of these four basic tissue types was varied significantly from 0.04% (roots) to 1.2% (leaves). The average yield of essential oil obtained from flowers and stems was 0.6 and 0.06%, respectively. The chemical composition of all the oil samples was mainly investigated using both GC/FID and GC/MS analyses. The contents and the retention indices (RIs) of the identified compounds of these essential oils are listed in Table 1; Fig. 1B, in the order of their elution on a DB-5 column. Studied organs of S. sahendica showed significant diversity in terms of type and percentage of essential oil compounds. Overall, 44, 46, 42, and 45 compounds were identified in the essential oil of leaves, flowers, stems, and roots of this plant, which constituted 99.6, 99.3, 98.2, and 99.4% of total oil composition, respectively. The main biogenic volatile compounds produced by different parts of S. sahendica include; trans-meta-Mentha-2,8-diene (11.1%), α-pinene (9.6%), bicyclogermacrene (9.3%), β-pinene (8.0%), germacrene D (5.7%), Bornyl acetate (5.4%), 1,8-Cineol (5.2%) and linalyl acetate (5.1%) from leaf; linalyl acetate (10.6%), trans-meta-Mentha-2,8-diene (10.6%), bornyl acetate (9.4%), α-pinene (7.2%), β-pinene (6.5%), linalool (6.1%) and 1,8-cineol (5.0%) from flower head-oil; β-pinene (16.0%), α-pinene (12.2%), bicyclogermacrene (8.8%), 1,8-cineol (8.5%), sabinene (7.0) and linalyl acetate (6.2%) from stem and manool (13.7%), abietatriene (12.7%), trans-meta-mentha-2,8-diene (8.9%), α-pinene (8.6%) and bicyclogermacrene (6.9%) from root, which made major constituents of essential oil. The chemical composition of the essential oils from different organs in terms of the chemical formula, indicates that the stem, flower, leaf, and root of S. sahendica are rich in monoterpene hydrocarbons (43%), oxygenated monoterpenes (41.4%), sesquiterpene hydrocarbons (23.2%) and oxygenated sesquiterpenes (11.8%), respectively (Fig. 2). These results also show that diterpene compounds such as abietatriene and manool, were made major parts of the root essential oil.

Clusters analysis of chemical compounds

The 28 common compounds of essential oil were similar between different organs, the most important of which are β-pinene (3.5–16.0%), manool (0.0-13.7%), abietatriene (0.0-11.2%), α-pinene (7.2–12.2%), trans-metamentha-2,8-diene (1.2–11.1%), linalyl acetate (3.2–10.7%), bornyl acetate (2.8–9.4%), bicyclogermacrene (3.6–9.3%) and 1,8-cineol (2.0-8.5%). The importance of diversity in different parts of the essential oil plant was determined by multivariate statistical analysis (cluster analysis (CA)). The dendrogram obtained as a result of CA is shown in Fig. 3A. Cluster analysis of the essential oil components divided studied plant organs into two main groups. The first class (Cluster I) was divided into two subgroups; first subgroup consisted of leaf and stem essential oils with the α-pinene, β-pinene, 1,8-cineol and bicyclogermacrene as major compounds, while in second subgroup include flower essential oil, trans-meta-Mentha-2,8-diene, linalool, linalyl acetate and bornyl acetate were the main constituents. Root essential oil with high have been grouped under class 2 (Cluster II) (Table 1; Fig. 3A).

Minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) assay

In order to correctly interpret the results of microbial tests, it is necessary to determine the minimum inhibitory concentration of the antimicrobial agents. The results showed that the MIC of essential oil from leaves and flowers of S. sahendica on Escherichia coli was 16 and 32 (mg/ml), respectively, while the stem and root essential oil had an inhibitory effect greater than 32 (mg/ml) against these bacteria. In regard to Bacillus cereus, the essential oil obtained from flowers and roots showed the highest inhibitory effect (16 mg/ml) and the high MIC value (32 mg/ml) was related to essential oil composition of leaves and stems. In general, the inhibitory effect of the extracted essential oils from different parts of S. sahendica against Pseudomonas aeruginosa was less compared to other bacterial species used in this experiment. On the other hand, the strongest antibacterial effects were apperceived using the flower, stem and root essential oils against Staphylococcus aureus with MIC values 16 mg/ml (Table 3).

Polyphenolic compounds content and clusters analysis

Based on HPLC results, nine polyphenolic compounds (including Coumaric acid, rosmarinic acid, gallic acid, caffeic acid, rutin, quercetin, chlorogenic acid, apigenin, and cinnamic acid) were detected in the extract of different plant parts of S. sahendica. The leaf and root organs showed high amounts of polyphenols, so that the highest amount of apigenin (0.35 mg g− 1 DW), cinnamic acid (0.21 mg g− 1 DW), quercetin (0.31 mg g− 1 DW), rosmarinic acid (0.87 mg g− 1 DW), rutin (0.19 mg g− 1 DW) and caffeic acid (0.27 mg g− 1 DW) compounds was found in the leaf organ and the highest amount of gallic acid (0.45 mg g− 1 DW), coumaric acid (0.62 mg g− 1 DW) and chlorogenic acid (0.65 mg g− 1 DW) compounds was found in the root organ. On the other hand, the lowest amount of polyphenolic compounds was observed in flower and stem organs. A cluster analysis (CA) diagram was drawn based on the amount of polyphenolic compounds in different plant parts of S. sahendica. Based on this, the studied organs were divided into two main groups. The first group (Cluster I) included leaf and root organs, which were placed together due to the high amount of coumaric acid, rosmarinic acid, gallic acid, caffeic acid, rutin, quercetin, chlorogenic acid, apigenin, and cinnamic acid. Due to the low amounts of polyphenolic compounds in flower and stem organs, these organs were placed together in the second group (Cluster II). (Fig. 4A)

The correlation analysis between the evaluated properties including polyphenolic compounds, essential oil yield, and key components of essential oil in various plant parts is shown in Fig. 4B. Discussion

One of the biggest concerns in biological and medical sciences is a rise in antibacterial and antifungal drug resistance. Unfortunately, 90% of known bacteria can become resistant to these drugs23. The application and importance of using medicinal plants in the treatment of diseases, preventing and hindering the growth of bacteria in the pharmaceutical industry, as well as controlling the growth of spoilage indicator microorganisms is clearly known14. On the other hand, the desire of consumers of food products for natural foods without chemical preservatives, due to the knowledge of the side effects of these compounds, has encouraged researchers in the field of food safety and health to find antimicrobial agents of plant origin24. The essential oils obtained from medicinal plants have active plant-derived compounds and can be used as a source of novel antimicrobial substances (alternative to common therapeutic antibiotics) against a wide range of pathogenic microorganisms14. S. sahendica is one of the medicinal plants that can be recommended for further use in the medical and food industries due to its availability. According to the results of evaluating the amount of essential oil in different organs, leaves had the highest essential oil yield (1.2%) and root had the lowest essential oil yield (0.4%). Various organs of plants have capacities to produce essential oil in varying amounts, and the chosen organ with high percentage of essential oil is the most important factor in order to achieve the maximum performance of essential oil25. The cases described can be of interest to medicinal plant breeders as a correction goal in the performance of dry matter as well as for use in the pharmaceutical, food, and health cosmetic industries. Considering to the accumulation of essential oils in the lamiaceae family is generally limited to the secretory hairs in the leaf surface of S. sahendica justifies the high percentage of leaf essential oil compared to other organs of this plant26. In previous research, the yield of essential oils (w/w %) obtained from aerial parts of S. sahendica in different phenological stages based on dry weight of plant were in the order full flowering (1.1%) > fruiting set (0.6%) > floral budding (0.5%) > and vegetative (0.3%) reported27. Considering that environmental factors influence on the growth and quality of the active substances in medicinal plants, harvesting a medicinal plant is cost-effective when its active substances have reached the desired level28. The identification of compounds in the essential oils of the studied organs also revealed that the percentage of monoterpenes was higher than sesquiterpenes hydrocarbons in all organs. Among the grouping of monoterpenes, in the essential oil of all organ’s monoterpene hydrocarbons were the major fraction except flowers essential oil that oxygenated monoterpenes were dominant. Clustering the essential oil of different organs allocated them in two main groups, this data shows the high variability in the type and amount of compounds, and according to the required composition, it is possible to extract the essential oil from the desired organ. α-pinene and β-pinene are among the dominant constituents in various organs, especially aerial organs of plant. The chemical diversity of plant essential compounds can be influenced by various factors such as ecological conditions, phenological stages, and also different types of organs28.

Antimicrobial compounds of medicinal plants (especially essential oils) are valuable and important sources in medical science and pharmaceutical and food industries. These compounds have different mechanisms to affect microorganisms than presently used antibiotics, which increases the scope of their biological activity29. Essential oils often affect the cell membrane structures of microorganisms, and due to their hydrophobic and oily nature can penetrate the bacterial cell membrane, this ultimately results in the release of ions, changes in the osmotic pressure of the cell and finally the death of the microorganism17. EL Euch et al.30 proved a direct relationship between the antimicrobial effect and the essential oil of S. sahendica. The results of recent work show that the essential oil obtained from different organs inhibited the growth of each four bacteria tested. As a whole, it seems that the essential oil obtained from the leaves had a significant inhibitory effect on Escherichia coli, the essential oils from flowers and roots have a significant inhibitory effect, against the gram-positive bacteria Bacillus cereus and Staphylococcus aureus, as well as, the essential oil from stem had the greatest effect on Staphylococcus aureus. generally, the results demonstrated the strong inhibitory action of S. sahendica essential oil against Staphylococcus aureus and the less effect on Pseudomonas aeruginosa. Similar to our study, other studies have indicated that gram-negative bacteria are more resistant to essential oils than gram-positive bacteria due to their cell membrane which consists of hydrophilic lipopolysaccharides and limits the penetration of the essential oil hydrophobic compounds into bacterial cells31. Nevertheless, the hydrophobic compounds of essential oil are able to pass through the periplasmic space of Gram-negative bacteria’s outer membrane via the porin proteins. Actually, the antibacterial property of essential oils is strongly related to their hydrophobicity as well as, and creating instability in the cell membrane and rupture in the bacterial cell32. Chiefly, terpenes are able to disruption of the cell membrane and penetrate into the lipid structure of the bacterial cell wall resulting in the denaturation of proteins, disintegration of the cell structure, leakage of the cytoplasm, and finally the death of the cell. According to the published reports, the inhibitory activity of essential oils containing terpenes on the bacterial cell may involve damage to the cell membranes and the leakage of potassium from the membrane of the treated bacteria and since that potassium ion is considered the most important intracellular cation of all living organisms and while having a major role in bacterial cells functions, thus, the loss of potassium ions across the cell membrane has been mentioned as the cause of further damage to the membrane and the destruction of the cell permeability barrier33. According to this, the inhibitory activity of S. sahendica is due to the presence of antibacterial composition, including α-pinene, β-pinene, 1,8-cineol, linalyl acetate and etc. in the essential oil. According to the reports, 1, 8-cineole is one of the most extensively antimicrobial compounds that directly target disrupting the outer membrane and reduces the concentration of the cytoplasm34. The antimicrobial effect of essential oils is not only due to their major constituents; bioactive compounds present in small quantities can also help the antimicrobial status of essential oil. In fact, the lower percentage compound might also have a synergistic effect with other main compounds35.

In similar studies, essential oils obtained from S. sahendica leaves and flowers had an inhibitory effect on Pseudomonas aeruginosa gram-negative bacteria, which was attributed to α-pinene and β-pinene monoterpenes36. Also in another study, the results of the study of antibacterial properties of S. sahendica essential oil showed the highest and lowest inhibitory effect on Staphylococcus aureus and Pseudomonas aeruginosa, respectively37. Yazgan et al.38 confirmed the antimicrobial properties of S. sahendica essential oil on Escherichia coli, Salmonella typhimurium, Listeria monocytogenes and Staphylococcus aureus, with greatest inhibitory effect on Staphylococcus aureus.

Conclusion

Medicinal plants have antimicrobial effects on different microorganisms, and even distinct from conventional antibiotics, they inhibit the growth of bacteria, and this necessitates more comprehensive research in the field of medicinal plants. This issue justifies the increasing research on the antimicrobial properties of medicinal plants. The chemical compositions of essential oils as antibacterial compounds are useful and valuable resources in the medicine, cosmetics, and food industry. Regarding polyphenolic compounds, the results showed that the amount of phenolic compounds in leaf and root organs was at a higher level than in flower and stem organs and these plant parts can be a suitable source for obtaining these compounds. Due to the adverse effects of chemical preservatives in processed foods, the use of safe antimicrobial compounds such as essential oils and plant extracts appears indispensable. Considering the prevalence and spread of infectious diseases and the resistance of pathogenic microorganisms to synthetic and chemical drugs, it is very important to precise identification of natural plants with therapeutic potential. The presence of various compounds with different properties makes medicinal plants a potential antibacterial resource, and because of this, drug resistance has not emerged with the irrational use of these plants. Our results shed light on the despite the variability in the essential oil content and composition, the oil obtained from different organs of S. sahendica has significant inhibitory effects on pathogenic bacteria, especially Staphylococcus aureus, which is an opportunistic pathogen in medicine that causes many poisonings, infections and autoimmune diseases. Therefore, it can be concluded that this plant can be the leading source of alternative antibacterial drugs with fewer side effects for the treat of infections.
