Posterior Cruciate Ligament Reconstruction in Elite Athletes: A Narrative Review
Authors: Darby Amelia Elliott, Chinmay Madhukar Gupte
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPosterior cruciate ligament (PCL) injuries are uncommon in sport but may be career-defining for elite athletes. Controversy remains regarding indications for reconstruction, optimal techniques and the role of braced non-operative care. This narrative review summarises evidence on operative and non-operative management of PCL injuries in elite athletes, focusing on return-to-play (RTP), graft selection, reconstruction strategy and elite-specific considerations.MethodsA narrative review was conducted in PubMed and Embase (inception-April 2025) with reference list screening. Clinical studies reporting return-to-play outcomes after operative or non-operative management of isolated PCL injuries and PCL-based MLKIs in athletic cohorts were prioritised; 51 studies were included, and heterogeneity precluded meta-analysis.ResultsIsolated PCL injuries in elite footballers are rare, whereas PCL-based multi-ligament knee injuries (MLKIs) are more common in collision sports. Early dynamic bracing with quadriceps-focused rehabilitation enables > 90% RTP in selected acute isolated grade II–III tears in high-level athletic cohorts (Level III evidence). For chronic instability or MLKIs, anatomic single-bundle reconstruction remains common, with double-bundle and tibial inlay approaches offering biomechanical but limited clinical advantages. Modern elite MLKI cohorts report 80–90% RTP, though full return-to-performance is less frequent.ConclusionsManagement of elite PCL injuries requires individualised, sport-specific decision-making that balances biological healing with performance demands. High-quality, sport-specific outcome data remain limited.Level of EvidenceLevel V, narrative review.Keywords: Posterior cruciate ligament, Elite athlete, Multi-ligament knee injury, Reconstruction, Return to play, Dynamic bracing

Introduction

Posterior cruciate ligament (PCL) injuries represent a minority of ligamentous knee trauma but may be disproportionately important in elite sport, where even small residual instability can impair performance and shorten careers [1]. In comparison with anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) rupture, PCL injuries in professional football and rugby are infrequent, but they are over-represented in multi-ligament knee injuries (MLKIs) and knee dislocations [2]. The PCL also differs biologically from the ACL in possessing greater intrinsic healing potential, which has fuelled longstanding controversy regarding the indications for reconstruction, optimal timing of surgery and the role of dynamic bracing [3]. Although ACL literature provides useful parallels for return-to-play (RTP) decision-making, ACL and PCL injury patterns and healing potential differ biomechanically (Fig. 1).

Elite athletes impose extreme and often repetitive loads on the knee joint, particularly at high flexion angles and during deceleration, cutting, scrummaging, and grappling. These sport- and position-specific demands, combined with commercial and psychological pressure for rapid RTP, mean that decision-making in this group cannot simply extrapolate from recreational populations [4]. For the purposes of this review, “elite athlete” refers to athletes competing at professional level, full-time semi-professional level, or national/international representative level (including top-tier collegiate/scholarship programmes where sport is the primary performance focus). The aim of this narrative review is therefore to synthesise the current evidence on PCL reconstruction (PCLR) and non-operative management in athletes, focusing on graft choice, reconstruction strategy, fixation techniques, outcomes, and elite sport-specific considerations. Methods

A narrative review of the literature was performed using PubMed and Embase from database inception to April 2025. The search combined terms including “posterior cruciate ligament,” “PCL reconstruction,” “elite athlete,” “multi-ligament knee injury,” and “return to play,” with Boolean operators and appropriate MeSH/Emtree headings. Reference lists of included articles and relevant systematic reviews were additionally screened to identify eligible studies. Titles and abstracts were screened, and full texts were reviewed against predefined eligibility criteria. Included studies were clinical papers reporting RTP outcomes following management of PCL injuries (operative reconstruction/repair or non-operative rehabilitation), including isolated PCL injuries and PCL-based MLKIs; where reported, cohorts comprising professional, semi-professional, collegiate scholarship, or national/international-level athletes were prioritised. Exclusion criteria were cadaveric/biomechanical-only studies, technique descriptions without clinical RTP outcomes, imaging-only studies, non-athletic populations without stratified athletic outcomes, and studies in which the PCL was not a primary injury or PCL-related outcomes were not reported. 51 studies were included in the narrative synthesis. Given heterogeneity in injury patterns, interventions, and outcome definitions, PRISMA-style reporting and meta-analytic pooling were not undertaken. As a narrative review, the synthesis is subject to potential publication bias, selective outcome reporting, and over-representation of higher-profile athletic cohorts; these limitations were considered when interpreting comparative claims and generalisability across professional, semi-professional, and mixed-level samples. Overall risk of bias was appraised qualitatively for the narrative synthesis: the evidence base was predominantly Level III–IV (retrospective cohorts/case series) with moderate-high risk of selection and performance bias, heterogeneous interventions/outcomes, and inconsistent RTP definitions/reporting, limiting causal inference and pooled estimation (Figs. 3, 4).

Epidemiology and Mechanism of Injury in Elite Sport

Epidemiological data from the UEFA Elite Club Injury Study suggest that PCL injuries in men’s professional football are rare, with an incidence of approximately 0.04 injuries per 1000 player-hours, much lower than for ACL or medial collateral ligament (MCL) injuries [5]. Most PCL injuries in this cohort result from contact mechanisms, including direct blows to the anterior tibia, collisions and tackles [3]. In contrast, non-contact pivoting mechanisms dominate for ACL injuries [6].

In rugby union, rugby league and American football, PCL injuries occur more frequently as part of MLKIs, especially in knee dislocations involving bi-cruciate rupture and disruption of one or both collateral ligaments and the posterolateral corner (PLC) [7]. However, collision-sport evidence is mixed and predominantly retrospective (Level III–IV evidence), so precise sport-specific incidence rates for PCL-based MLKIs remain poorly defined. These high-energy mechanisms reflect the collision nature of these sports and are associated with a higher risk of neurovascular compromise and long-term functional limitations, however, elite athlete–specific neurovascular incidence is rarely reported. Elite collision athletes with PCL-based MLKIs often face prolonged rehabilitation and uncertain return to pre-injury performance [8, 9].

Non-operative Versus Operative Management in Elite Athletes

The management of acute isolated high-grade PCL tears in elite athletes remains controversial. Historically, many such injuries were treated non-operatively, based on the PCL’s healing capacity and acceptable outcomes in the general population [8]. More recent data in high-level athletes support this approach when combined with early diagnosis, dynamic bracing and targeted rehabilitation [9].

In a prospective cohort of professional and semi-professional athletes with acute isolated Hughston grade II–III PCL tears (Level III evidence), a structured protocol using a dynamic PCL brace and quadriceps-focused rehabilitation achieved ≥ 90% RTP to the same or higher level at 2 years, with mean time to RTP around four months [10]. At 5 years, the majority remained in competitive sport [10]. When viewed as a “truly elite” (professional-only) sensitivity analysis, interpretation is constrained as most studies do not report elite purity and rarely provide outcomes stratified by professional status. Therefore, it is important to interpret the ≥ 90% RTP figure as reflecting mixed high-level cohorts (professional/semi-professional) rather than a professional-only population. These findings suggest that early braced non-operative management is a reasonable first-line strategy for acute isolated PCL injuries in many elite athletes, particularly in-season and when concomitant meniscal or chondral pathology does not require surgery [11–13], while highlighting the need for future athlete registries to report professional-only subgroup outcomes.

Reconstruction is generally indicated in the elite setting for chronic symptomatic posterior instability despite structured conservative management, high-grade posterior laxity (often ≥ 10–12 mm on stress radiographs), PCL involvement in MLKIs or knee dislocations, and bony avulsions not amenable to fixation [14]. The presence of high-grade posterior laxity should alert the clinician to the possibility of concomitant injury to other ligaments, especially the posterolateral and posteromedial corners. In these cases, operative stabilisation is undertaken with the dual aims of restoring functional stability for elite performance and potentially mitigating long-term joint degeneration [3, 14]. Graft Choice in Elite Athletes

Several systematic reviews and comparative studies have evaluated graft choice in PCLR, but most include heterogeneous patient groups rather than elite athletes. Overall, hamstring autograft, bone–patellar tendon–bone (BPTB) autograft and quadriceps tendon (QT) autograft all produce acceptable stability and functional outcomes [15, 16]. However, elite athletes raise additional considerations such as sprint performance, deep-flexion kneeling demands, previous graft harvests and long-term donor-site morbidity [14].

Hamstring autograft (semitendinosus with or without gracilis) is widely used, particularly for transtibial PCLR. It offers high tensile strength, sufficient graft length for double-bundle (DB) constructs and technical familiarity [17]. In elite sprint-dependent athletes and wide football players, however, potential reductions in eccentric hamstring strength and sprint performance are a concern [18]. BPTB grafts provide bone-to-bone healing and high initial fixation strength but are associated with anterior knee pain, kneeling discomfort and risk of patellar tendinopathy, which may be problematic for rugby forwards, goalkeepers and athletes in kneeling martial arts [15].

Quadriceps tendon autograft has gained popularity for PCLR because it offers a large graft diameter with minimal donor-site morbidity and preserves both hamstrings and patellar tendon [19]. This makes QT particularly attractive for most elite athletes, especially those relying on high-speed running, repeated accelerations and kicking [19]. Additional autograft options such as peroneus longus and combined grafts may be considered in revision settings or when traditional graft sites have been exhausted [20]. Allograft is often reserved for MLKIs, older patients and revisions [21], as concerns remain regarding slower graft incorporation and potentially higher failure rates in younger, high-demand athletes [4, 15, 22]; Key graft options and elite-sport considerations are summarised in Table 2.

Single- Versus Double-Bundle Reconstruction

The native PCL comprises an anterolateral (AL) and posteromedial (PM) bundle that function in a flexion-dependent and reciprocal manner [3]. Biomechanical studies indicate that DB reconstruction most closely reproduces native knee kinematics and restores posterior stability throughout the range of motion [23, 24]. Nevertheless, clinical series and meta-analyses comparing single-bundle (SB) and DB PCLR show only small differences in posterior laxity on stress radiographs and no consistent, clinically meaningful advantages in patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) such as International Knee Documentation Committee (IKDC) and Lysholm scores [25–27].

Several studies have reported that DB reconstruction produced a small improvement in stress radiograph side-to-side difference (pooled WMD with 95% CI) [28], but this incremental gain has not translated into demonstrably higher RTP rates or superior long-term function [24, 28]. In contrast, SB techniques are technically simpler, require less graft tissue and may be advantageous where multiple ligaments require reconstruction. There is currently no elite athlete-specific evidence to suggest that DB reconstruction leads to better performance or reduced re-injury risk compared with well-executed anatomic SB reconstruction.

In practice, surgeon familiarity with an anatomic SB technique, accurate tunnel placement on the native PCL footprint and robust fixation are likely to have a greater impact on outcomes than the number of bundles [3]. DB reconstruction may be considered in select cases such as large bony insertions, revision PCLR, or athletes with extreme deep-flexion demands, but its routine use in elite sport is not currently supported by clinical data [29]. Fixation Strategies and the Tibial Side Problem

The tibial side is widely regarded as the critical point in PCLR due to the so-called killer turn, where the graft bends sharply at the posterior tibial tunnel aperture and may be exposed to high local stresses and abrasion [15, 30]. The tibial inlay technique was developed to address this concern by fixing the graft directly to the posterior tibial cortex, thereby avoiding the killer turn [30]. Biomechanical studies demonstrate that inlay constructs reduce graft bending and may distribute load more evenly [30].

Despite these theoretical advantages, comparative series and reviews have not identified major differences in functional outcome scores, posterior laxity or osteoarthritis progression between tibial inlay and transtibial PCLR [10, 31]. Long-term follow-up studies of isolated PCLR show similar Lysholm, Tegner and IKDC scores for both techniques. As a result, many surgeons continue to favour transtibial approaches because they are less invasive, more familiar arthroscopically and compatible with remnant-preserving strategies [32].

Tibial inlay remains a valuable option in chronic PCL deficiency with significant attenuation, in revision PCLR where tunnel position or widening precludes a transtibial tunnel, and in cases where the posterior knee is already exposed for associated PLC reconstruction [33, 34]. On the femoral side, techniques that provide robust aperture or near-aperture fixation and minimise graft slippage during high-load rehabilitation are particularly important in elite athletes [15, 32].

Remnant-Preserving and Stump-Sparing Techniques

Remnant-preserving PCLR techniques aim to maintain viable portions of the native PCL tibial stump and meniscofemoral ligaments, most commonly by preserving the AL bundle remnant and integrating the graft alongside retained fibres [35]. Potential advantages include a biological “pulley” effect at the killer turn, enhanced graft revascularisation, preservation of proprioceptive mechanoreceptors has also been proposed, but any clinically meaningful proprioceptive benefit remains speculative and is supported primarily by low-level evidence (Level IV) [36]. Prospective series of remnant-preserving PCLR using trans-septal posterior portals have reported good posterior stability, largely normal graft appearance on magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and return to pre-injury sports levels in approximately 80–86% of patients [35].

Comparative studies suggest that remnant preservation may improve muscle torque and posterior stability relative to remnant-sacrificing techniques, although the evidence base remains limited and heterogeneous [35]. In the context of elite sport, the potential proprioceptive benefits and protective effect at the tibial bend are appealing, but no study has yet demonstrated superior RTP or return-to-performance (RTPe) outcomes specifically in elite athletes. Remnant-preserving PCLR is technically demanding and should be undertaken by surgeons experienced in advanced arthroscopic posterior knee surgery, particularly when deep flexion and rapid high-load rehabilitation are anticipated [37].

Rehabilitation and Return-to-Play Criteria

Rehabilitation after PCL injury or reconstruction must balance protection of the healing ligament or graft against the detrimental effects of prolonged immobilisation and deconditioning [38]. Contemporary PCLR protocols typically permit early but protected range of motion, often 0–90° during the first 4–6 weeks, with close control of posterior tibial translation [39]. Isolated hamstring loading is avoided in the early phases, while quadriceps activation is encouraged with the tibia held in an anteriorly translated position [8].

Dynamic PCL bracing is commonly used for 3–6 months after both non-operative and operative treatment to maintain the tibia in a relatively anterior position during functional activities [3]. Progressive closed-chain strengthening, neuromuscular training, plyometrics, and sport-specific drills are introduced in a phased manner according to healing milestones and objective testing [40].

Objective RTP criteria should include near-symmetrical strength, satisfactory performance on functional hop and cutting tests, absence of effusion or instability symptoms, and adequate psychological readiness [41, 42]. Commonly used functional batteries include the single-leg hop for distance, triple hop, crossover hop, and 6-m timed hop, with targets of ≥ 95% limb symmetry on at least 3/4 hop tests (and ≥ 90% symmetry for isokinetic strength), alongside acceptable movement quality during landing/cutting tasks [43]. Although the evidence base is smaller than for ACL, psychological readiness (confidence and fear of re-injury) is increasingly recognised as a relevant determinant of RTS following PCL injury and reconstruction, and should be screened using ACL readiness instruments (e.g., ACL-RSI) [44]. Accordingly, RTS decision-making after PCL injury should include assessment of psychological barriers, as these factors may delay RTS despite satisfactory physical recovery and are potentially modifiable with targeted training and graded exposure [44]. Full-contact RTP after isolated PCLR is commonly delayed until 9–12 months, while MLKIs may require 12–18 months [45]. In contrast, braced non-operative management of acute isolated PCL injuries has been reported to facilitate earlier RTP (approximately 4–5 months) in carefully selected elite athletes, although athlete level and sport demands should be considered when extrapolating timelines.

Objective match-based key performance indicators (KPIs) (minutes played, match exposure and performance indicators) should be reported alongside RTP, as RTP reflects participation, whereas RTPe reflects return of competitive output [46]. Reliance on RTP alone may overestimate successful recovery, as athletes may return to competition but with reduced game time or a decrease in performance [47].

However, in PCL-based injuries, the evidence base for RTPe is very limited: most published series report time-to-RTP and/or return to the same or higher level of sport (often using activity scales such as Tegner) [10], rather than objective match output; and often minutes played/match exposure are not reported [10]. This contrasts with the ACL literature, where multiple studies quantify post-injury game time and performance, often demonstrating reduced participation and/or performance after return [48]; comparable high-quality RTPe datasets for isolated PCL injury or PCL reconstruction remain largely absent, limiting firm conclusions about post-return performance. Outcomes and Return to Play After PCLR

Isolated PCLR in active patients typically yields substantial improvements in posterior stability and PROMs, with many series reporting 70–90% good to excellent outcomes [10–13]. In a cohort of patients treated with arthroscopic AL bundle QT PCLR, posterior laxity improved by approximately 7–8 mm and 81% of patients returned to their pre-injury activity level [19].

In MLKIs that include the PCL, long-term outcomes have historically been guarded. Older open reconstruction series in elite athletes reported high rates of return to sport, but only about one-third regaining their exact pre-injury level, and a decrease in Tegner activity scores over time [49]. Delays beyond 4–6 weeks before definitive reconstruction were associated with worse satisfaction and lower activity levels.

More recent elite MLKI cohorts treated with modern anatomic reconstruction have demonstrated encouraging results. A series of 136 elite athletes treated with single-stage anatomic cruciate reconstruction and hybrid collateral/PLC management reported RTP to elite sport in 88% at a mean of 12.8 months [50]. ACL-based and PCL-based MLKIs had similar RTP rates, but PCL-based injuries required longer rehabilitation, with mean time to RTP 15.2 months for PCL-based versus 11.9 months for ACL-based injuries (p < 0.01) [50]. Bi-cruciate injuries required the longest recovery, with mean RTP time ~ 16.0 months versus ~ 12.4 months for uni-cruciate patterns (p < 0.05) [50]. In the National Football League, multi-ligament patterns involving the PCL and lateral structures have been linked to lower RTP and longer time to RTP compared with ACL/MCL injuries, underlining the impact of PCL-based MLKIs in collision sports [45].

Knowledge Gaps and Future Directions

Significant knowledge gaps remain regarding PCL injury management in elite athletes. There is a paucity of prospective, sport-specific cohorts with clearly defined RTP and RTPe endpoints after PCLR or structured non-operative care. Comparisons of graft choice in elite populations—particularly QT versus hamstring versus BPTB versus allograft—are underpowered or absent. Going forward, there is the need for prospective elite registries both for graft choice and RTP outcomes.

Similarly, the relative merits of SB versus DB reconstruction and tibial inlay versus transtibial fixation have been explored biomechanically and in mixed clinical populations, but elite sport–specific data are lacking [51]. The long-term chondral and osteoarthritic sequelae of high-grade PCL laxity treated non-operatively in elite athletes remain unknown. Future research priorities include multi-centre registries of professional athletes with granular surgical, rehabilitation and performance data; prospective comparison of autograft and allograft in MLKIs involving the PCL; and development of validated PCL-specific RTP test batteries analogous to those now used after ACL reconstruction.

Long-term joint health after elite PCL injury is particularly under-reported. In a minimum 10-year follow-up of non-operatively treated acute isolated PCL injuries, Shelbourne et al. [13] reported moderate-to-severe radiographic osteoarthritis (OA) in 11% of patients, with generally preserved activity and subjective function; however, participants were not exclusively elite athletes, limiting direct extrapolation to professional sport [13]. Future prospective registries should pair imaging and symptoms with RTP KPIs to better capture the elite impact of PCL injury and its treatments.

Conclusion

Elite PCL injuries range from partial tears to PCL-based MLKIs and knee dislocations. In selected acute isolated grade II–III tears diagnosed early, dynamic bracing plus quadriceps-focused rehabilitation can restore stability and allow earlier RTP with encouraging medium-term results, though evidence is largely from mixed professional/semi-professional cohorts [9, 10]. When instability persists, laxity is high, or the PCL is part of an MLKI, surgical stabilisation is often required. Across graft types, single- versus double-bundle constructs, and fixation strategies, clinical differences are generally small [15], so technique should be tailored to sport demands, anatomy, associated injuries, and prior graft harvests rather than a single “best” method. Successful elite RTP depends on correct indication, near-anatomic reconstruction with robust fixation, phased sport-specific rehabilitation, and objective, criteria-based RTP decisions that respect graft biology and performance pressures. Evidence remains limited in truly elite-only cohorts, and better sport-specific outcome and performance reporting is needed to define expectations and optimise return-to-play decision-making.
