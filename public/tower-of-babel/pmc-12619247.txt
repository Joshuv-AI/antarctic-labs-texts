Multidisciplinary management of concurrent postpartum eclampsia, HELLP syndrome, ruptured hepatic subcapsular hematoma, and atypical hemolytic uremic syndrome: a case report
Authors: Yufan Liang, Sainan Wang, Haizhen Jiang, Qining Xing, Xue Yan, Chuanbing Hu, Yanan Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe concurrent presentation of postpartum eclampsia, HELLP syndrome (hemolysis, elevated liver enzymes, low platelets), ruptured hepatic subcapsular hematoma, and pregnancy-associated atypical hemolytic uremic syndrome (P-aHUS) represents a life-threatening obstetric emergency. To the best of our knowledge, this represents a novel presentation, as the concurrent manifestation of these complications has rarely, if ever, been documented. This case highlights the paramount challenge of differentiating thrombotic microangiopathies (TMAs) in the context of postpartum multi-organ failure.Case presentationA 39-year-old multiparous woman presented 1.5 h after an out-of-hospital vaginal delivery with postpartum eclampsia, HELLP syndrome, and hemorrhagic shock secondary to ruptured hepatic subcapsular hematoma (confirmed by 3,000 mL hemoperitoneum). Following emergency laparotomy and transfusion (52 units packed red blood cells, 8.25 L fresh frozen plasma), she developed P-aHUS (soluble C5b-9 1,079 ng/mL; C3 0.68 g/L) requiring 6 plasma exchanges and continuous renal replacement therapy. Platelet recovery began at postoperative day 12. The patient was discharged on postoperative day 40 with substantial clinical improvement.ConclusionThis case illustrates the importance of considering alternative etiologies, such as P-aHUS, in cases of persistent or new-onset thrombotic microangiopathy beyond 48–72 h postpartum. Early complement testing and multidisciplinary escalation (including plasma exchange) are vital for survival in such multi-organ failure situations. The clinical and laboratory features observed in this case underscore the diagnostic challenges and therapeutic strategies required for this rare constellation of postpartum complications.

Background

Thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) is a life-threatening disorder characterized by the diagnostic triad of microangiopathic hemolytic anemia (MAHA), thrombocytopenia, and end-organ damage [1]. Prompt recognition and the initiation of early, specific therapy are paramount, as they substantially reduce mortality [2]. In the peripartum and postpartum periods, the differential diagnosis of TMA is critically important and includes thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP), atypical hemolytic uremic syndrome (aHUS), and the syndrome of hemolysis, elevated liver enzymes, and low platelets (HELLP). Among these, HELLP syndrome is the most common obstetric TMA, often occurring in the context of pre-eclampsia and presenting in late pregnancy or immediately postpartum [3, 4]. While exceedingly rare, TTP represents a medical emergency due to severe ADAMTS13 deficiency and can occur at any point during pregnancy [5]. Pregnancy-associated atypical hemolytic uremic syndrome (P-aHUS) is also rare but carries a high risk of end-stage renal disease; it typically manifests in the postpartum period and is driven by complement dysregulation [6]. Distinguishing between these specific TMAs is diagnostically challenging due to their significant overlap in clinical features, yet it is essential as their management strategies diverge significantly [4].

The concurrent development of postpartum eclampsia, HELLP syndrome, spontaneous hepatic rupture, and P-aHUS represents an unprecedented clinical scenario. While postpartum HELLP syndrome and P-aHUS are individually well-documented [3], their co-occurrence with hepatic rupture remains, to the best of our knowledge, unreported in extant literature. This critical knowledge gap hinders the timely recognition of evolving multi-organ failure from overlapping TMAs.

We report the case of a 39-year-old woman who developed severe postpartum eclampsia complicated by HELLP syndrome and ruptured hepatic subcapsular hematoma requiring emergency laparotomy, whose postoperative course was further complicated by P-aHUS. This case underscores the critical importance of early recognition, meticulous differential diagnosis of postpartum TMAs, and aggressive, coordinated multidisciplinary management. A concise review of relevant literature is provided.

Case presentation

A 39-year-old gravida 5 para 3 (G5P3) woman presented to our hospital 90 min after an unassisted spontaneous vaginal delivery at 36⁺3 weeks gestation in a private vehicle. Her antenatal history was notable for borderline hypertension (blood pressure [BP] approximately 136/81 mmHg) and gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM), diagnosed by a 1-h 75 g oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) with a value of 12.0 mmol/L (reference: < 10.0 mmol/L). She had undergone no regular glucose monitoring.

On admission (December 27, 2024, at 7:13 PM), she was hypertensive (BP 165/99 mmHg), with minimal vaginal bleeding and a firm uterine tone. She was initially alert and oriented, exhibiting no symptoms suggestive of impending eclampsia such as severe headache or visual disturbances. Uterotonics (oxytocin, misoprostol, and carbetocin) were administered alongside 10 mg of oral nifedipine. By 8:30 PM, her BP had risen sharply to 185/108 mmHg. An additional 20 mg of nifedipine was administered orally, and a prophylactic magnesium sulfate infusion was initiated at 1 g/hour for eclampsia prophylaxis.

At 9:00 PM, the patient developed generalized seizures, consistent with postpartum eclampsia. Immediate resuscitative measures were implemented, including lateral positioning to protect the airway, supplemental oxygen, and intravenous pharmacotherapy. A loading dose of magnesium sulfate (5 g IV over 10 min) was given, and the infusion was continued at a maintenance rate of 1 g/hour for 24 h for seizure control and prophylaxis. Intravenous mannitol was administered for neuroprotection and intracranial pressure reduction. Seizure activity subsequently ceased.

At 9:08 PM, laboratory results revealed thrombocytopenia (platelets: 82 × 10⁹/L; reference: 100–450 × 10⁹/L), hemoglobin of 116 g/L (reference: 110–150 g/L), and hypofibrinogenemia (2.11 g/L; reference: 2–4 g/L). By 9:30 PM, her BP stabilized at 123/78 mmHg, and she was arousable without complaints. However, at 10:00 PM, she developed nausea and vomiting, initially attributed to magnesium sulfate toxicity, leading to a reduction in the infusion rate.

Thirty minutes later (10:30 PM), her hemodynamic status deteriorated abruptly, with BP dropping to 82/51 mmHg. The patient reported thirst, and her hypotension progressed rapidly to a nadir of 66/37 mmHg. Resuscitation for hemorrhagic shock was initiated immediately, involving rapid fluid infusion and high-dose vasopressor support (norepinephrine and epinephrine) to maintain perfusion. Urgent blood gas analysis revealed a hemoglobin level of 66 g/L. Bedside ultrasound identified approximately 3,000 mL of hemoperitoneum; paracentesis yielded non-coagulable blood. The leading differential diagnosis was spontaneous hepatic rupture complicating HELLP syndrome. Other considered causes included uterine rupture and splenic rupture. Given the life-threatening hemorrhagic shock, an emergency exploratory laparotomy was mandated. A multidisciplinary team (obstetrics, general surgery, and anesthesiology) was mobilized immediately. The massive transfusion protocol was activated. Preoperative preparation included securing large-bore intravenous access, initiating aggressive fluid resuscitation and vasopressor support, and making blood products (packed red blood cells, fresh frozen plasma, cryoprecipitate, and platelets) readily available in the operating room.

Emergency laparotomy was conducted from 11:40 PM on December 27, 2024, to 04:40 AM on December 28, 2024, confirming 3,000 mL of intraperitoneal blood and diffuse capsular oozing from a fatty right hepatic lobe without laceration. Hemostasis was achieved through right/middle hepatic artery ligation, cholecystectomy, fibrin sealant application, and perihepatic packing. Total estimated blood loss was 8,000 mL. Intraoperatively, she received 22 units of packed red blood cells, 2,200 mL of fresh frozen plasma, 10 units of cryoprecipitate, 2 doses of platelets, and 7,000 mL of crystalloid infusion, with a urine output of approximately 50 mL.

Postoperatively, the patient was transferred to the Intensive Care Unit (ICU) with a BP of 95/70 mmHg. She underwent comprehensive monitoring, including continuous invasive arterial pressure measurement, electrocardiography, pulse oximetry, and hourly urine output measurement. Throughout her resuscitation and postoperative course, she received a cumulative total of 52 units of packed red blood cells, 8,250 mL of fresh frozen plasma, 90 units of cryoprecipitate, and 11 doses of platelets.

Serial laboratory monitoring revealed progressive hypofibrinogenemia (fibrinogen nadir: 0.61 g/L), consistent with disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC). Complete blood counts showed profound thrombocytopenia (platelet nadir: 3–6 × 10⁹/L) and MAHA, evidenced by a peak lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) level of 3,993 U/L and schistocytes (1.82%) [reference: < 0.5%] on peripheral smear. Hepatocellular injury was significant, with aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) peaking at 3,629.3 U/L and 5,034.5 U/L, respectively. The patient developed anuria and acute kidney injury (AKI), requiring continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT).

Complement studies demonstrated dysregulation, with elevated sC5b-9 (1079 ng/mL; reference: < 300 ng/mL) and low C3 (0.68 g/L; reference: 0.9–1.8 g/L). P-aHUS was diagnosed based on AKI, TMA, complement abnormalities, and normal ADAMTS13 activity. Furthermore, immunological workup ruled out lupus (antinuclear antibody-negative) and antiphospholipid syndrome (anti-β2 glycoprotein/anticardiolipin antibodies negative) (Table 1). Discussion

Pregnancy is a well-established trigger for atypical hemolytic uremic syndrome (aHUS), resulting in its pregnancy-associated variant (P-aHUS) [7]. This rare but life-threatening TMA occurs in approximately 1 per 25,000 pregnancies. Notably, 79% of cases emerge in the postpartum period [8–10]. P-aHUS typically manifests as a severe hemolytic-renal syndrome. If left untreated, it progresses to end-stage renal disease (ESRD) in 60–70% of postpartum cases [8, 11–13]. The pathophysiology centers on complement dysregulation. Genetic mutations or acquired autoantibodies impair regulatory proteins [14], leading to uncontrolled alternative pathway activation. This cascade culminates in excessive production of C5a and the membrane attack complex (C5b-9), causing endothelial damage and systemic microthrombosis [15–17]. Obstetric TMAs including pre-eclampsia and HELLP syndrome, are well-recognized triggers for aHUS [18]. This pathogenic link was clearly demonstrated in our case. The patient's clinical course evolved sequentially from postpartum eclampsia with HELLP syndrome to P-aHUS. This progression highlights a potential continuum between these conditions. Postpartum eclampsia and HELLP syndrome represent severe hypertensive disorders of pregnancy. Although ruptured hepatic subcapsular hematoma complicates < 1% of HELLP cases, its maternal mortality exceeds 50% [19]. The concurrent presentation of eclampsia, HELLP syndrome, hepatic rupture, and P-aHUS in our patient underscores the critical need for timely multidisciplinary intervention to address these compounding, high-mortality conditions.

Distinguishing P-aHUS from other pregnancy-related thrombotic microangiopathies, such as pre-eclampsia and HELLP syndrome, is clinically challenging. This difficulty arises from the significant overlap in their presenting features with other obstetric complications [20–22]. HELLP syndrome, TTP, and P-aHUS all present with thrombocytopenia and MAHA [20]. As outlined in Table 4, three key features help differentiate thrombotic microangiopathies: timing of symptom onset, changes in lab values, and major organ involvement. In our case, the patient was initially diagnosed with HELLP syndrome, but she deteriorated postpartum despite successful laparotomy for hepatic rupture, developing progressive anemia, AKI, and persistent liver dysfunction—diverging from typical HELLP resolution [31]. Refractory anuria (> 13 days) despite aggressive therapy and worsening renal function prompted P-aHUS suspicion [32]. Diagnostic evaluation revealed: Persistent thrombocytopenia (nadir: 3–6 × 10⁹/L); MAHA (LDH > 3,000 U/L; reticulocytosis); AKI requiring CRRT; Normal ADAMTS13 (ANA); Elevated sC5b-9 (1,079 ng/mL; reference: < 300 ng/mL); with other secondary causes being excluded.

To date, the clinical presentation of P-aHUS remains highly heterogeneous, and its diagnosis continues to rely on exclusion criteria [33]. As demonstrated in our case, there is currently no definitive confirmatory test for P-aHUS. Diagnostic tests including peripheral blood smear analysis for schistocytes, complete blood count with differential, comprehensive immunologic profiling and complement system evaluation (demonstrating either decreased serum C3 levels or elevated sC5b-9 terminal complement complex) are shown in Table 1. To lessen the effects of P-aHUS, plasma exchange should be carried out as soon as feasible on a daily basis [7]. In our case, although P-aHUS was not laboratory-confirmed until POD 7, the medical team initiated emergent CRRT combined with plasma exchange as early as postpartum day 1, based on the patient's progressively worsening renal function and overt microangiopathic hemolytic manifestations. Six plasma exchange sessions addressed TMA by removing pathogenic complement factors [34–36]. It is important to note that the current standard of care for P-aHUS is complement blockade with eculizumab, a terminal complement inhibitor that blocks C5 cleavage. The use of eculizumab was clinically indicated based on the complement profile [15]. A remote consultation was obtained, and a plan was established to proceed with a renal biopsy and initiate eculizumab if anuria persisted for more than two weeks. However, the patient's urine output began to increase on POD12, and CRRT was discontinued on POD13. Consequently, in the context of this favorable response to initial therapy and the significant logistical and financial barriers to its procurement, eculizumab was not administered. Thus, while plasma exchange and immunosuppression (methylprednisolone and IVIG) served as a successful bridging therapy here, eculizumab remains the definitive treatment when required and available. Untreated P-aHUS carries high ESRD/mortality risk [37], but aggressive therapy (plasma exchange + complement blockade) induces remission in > 80% [36]. Recurrence risk in future pregnancies is significant [38].

It is important to acknowledge that several management decisions in this complex case deviated from standardized protocols, reflecting the need for individualized care in life-threatening, multi-system emergencies. First, the use of multiple uterotonics, including carbetocin, was aimed at securing uterine tone in a high-risk postpartum hemorrhage setting, despite the patient's hypertension. Second, the initial administration of magnesium sulfate as a maintenance infusion without a loading dose was a clinical oversight that was promptly corrected upon the onset of eclampsia. Third, the administration of intravenous mannitol was a neuroprotective measure in the acute setting of eclamptic seizures to mitigate cerebral edema, despite it not being a universal standard. Furthermore, the absence of initial liver and kidney function tests at the time of eclampsia presentation highlights the rapid pace of clinical deterioration, where immediate life-saving interventions took precedence over comprehensive laboratory workup. These variations underscore the dynamic and high-stakes nature of managing such critical cases.

In light of these experiences, our case reinforces that caution is warranted when administering medications that can elevate blood pressure, especially uterotonic agents; the concomitant use of multiple such agents should generally be avoided. Second, clinicians must recognize the paramount importance of the 72-h diagnostic window for differentiating TMAs. Additionally, vigilance is essential against unexpected hypotension signaling concealed hemorrhage, and the judicious use of platelet transfusions and pressors in this context. It is important to note that prophylactic platelet transfusion in patients with severe thrombocytopenia before a definitive diagnosis is established carries a theoretical risk of exacerbating microthrombosis. Transfusion should therefore be reserved primarily for active bleeding or prophylaxis before invasive procedures.

Massive transfusion itself may have significantly influenced the patient's postoperative course. Transfusion-related immunomodulation (TRIM) and systemic inflammatory response syndrome (SIRS) may have worsened endothelial injury and organ dysfunction [39, 40]. Additional complications such as transfusion-associated circulatory overload (TACO) or acute lung injury (TRALI) could also mimic or intensify systemic inflammation and respiratory failure [41]. Thus, while P-aHUS remained the primary diagnosis, massive transfusion likely compounded the injury, leading to more severe and prolonged multi-organ failure. This highlights the clinical challenge in which a vital intervention also introduces substantial iatrogenic risks that require careful management.

Conclusion

Although not confirmed by genetic testing, this case demonstrates the complex differential diagnosis of postpartum multi-organ failure. It offers a practical framework for distinguishing P-aHUS from other disorders using clinical and laboratory trends. In summary, this rare and severe case illustrates a life-threatening sequence of postpartum complications and provides key insights for clinicians. Specifically, it emphasizes the importance of early complement testing after delivery and the need for prompt multidisciplinary involvement. The combination of postpartum eclampsia, HELLP syndrome, ruptured hepatic subcapsular hematoma, and P-aHUS in one patient is exceptionally uncommon and dangerous. Recognizing and differentiating these conditions early is crucial, as their treatment strategies differ significantly. This report aims to increase awareness of this potentially fatal group of pregnancy-related complications and highlights how early recognition and team-based care can save lives.
