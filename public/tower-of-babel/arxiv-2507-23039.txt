Character theoretic techniques for nonabelian partial difference sets
Seth R. Nelson, Eric Swartz

Abstract
. A ( v , k , λ , μ ) (v,k,\lambda,\mu) -partial difference set (PDS) is a subset D D of size k k of a group G G of order v v such that every nonidentity element g g of G G can be expressed in either λ \lambda or μ \mu different ways as a product x ​ y − 1 xy^{-1} , x , y ∈ D x,y\in D , depending on whether or not g g is in D D . If D D is inverse closed and 1 ∉ D 1\notin D , then the Cayley graph Cay ⁡ ( G , D ) {\rm Cay}(G,D) is a ( v , k , λ , μ ) (v,k,\lambda,\mu) -strongly regular graph (SRG). PDSs have been studied extensively over the years, especially in abelian groups, where techniques from character theory have proven to be particularly effective. Recently, there has been considerable interest in studying PDSs in nonabelian groups, and the purpose of this paper is develop character theoretic techniques that apply in the nonabelian setting. We prove that analogues of character theoretic results of Ott [ 25 ] about generalized quadrangles of order s s also hold in the general PDS setting, and we are able to use these techniques to compute the intersection of a putative PDS with the conjugacy classes of the parent group in many instances. With these techniques, we are able to prove the nonexistence of PDSs in numerous instances and provide severe restrictions in cases when such PDSs may still exist. Furthermore, we are able to use these techniques constructively, computing several examples of PDSs in nonabelian groups not previously recognized in the literature, including an infinite family of genuinely nonabelian PDSs associated to the block-regular Steiner triple systems originally studied by Clapham [ 6 ] and related infinite families of genuinely nonabelian PDSs associated to the block-regular Steiner 2 2 -designs first studied by Wilson [ 33 ] .

PDSs have been the subject of great interest over the years (see, e.g.,

\chi(D)=\begin{cases}k,\text{ if $\chi$ is the trivial character,}\\
\frac{(\lambda-\mu)\pm\sqrt{\Delta}}{2},\text{ otherwise,}\end{cases}

There has been interest recently in PDSs in nonabelian groups

Up until now, the only restrictions that apply in the nonabelian setting are those of

]

Recently, Ott

The purpose of this paper is to extend Ott’s results to PDSs in nonabelian groups. (Indeed, just as techniques such as Equation (

These techniques have enabled us to rule out the existence of a PDS inside a range of groups and to provide severe restrictions on certain families of PDSs. We also develop new techniques for discovering and constructing new examples of PDSs inside of nonabelian groups. For a given group

We have used these methods to show that if a

2.1.
Partial difference sets

For our purposes,

Lemma 2.1

Let

2.2.
Strongly regular graphs

As we have mentioned, the notion of a strongly regular graph is closely related to that of a PDS, so we now take the opportunity to formally introduce SRGs. For further reference, see
[
16

\theta_{1}=\frac{1}{2}(\lambda-\mu+\sqrt{\Delta}),\;\;\;\theta_{2}=\frac{1}{2}(\lambda-\mu-\sqrt{\Delta})

, Chapter 10]

As we will see, the values

We set aside now two important relations for the eigenvalues, which we will use freely throughout the paper. Their proof follows immediately from the definition for

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
\Gamma

(v,k,\lambda,\mu)
-SRG with eigenvalues

(i)

\theta_{1}\theta_{2}=\mu-k
, and

.

Note that for our purposes

Furthermore, we say that the

Finally, we remark that the existence of a

2.3.
Some families of SRGs

We have already mentioned the particular class of finite geometries known as generalized quadrangles. We now provide some definitions and notation for generalized quadrangles, as we will need them in Proposition

(ii)

(iii)

We denote a

((s+1)(st+1),s(t+1),s-1,t+1)

[

One particularly important family of strongly regular graphs are those related to Steiner

-designs, which we use in Proposition

A Steiner

We will speak often of one particular class of Steiner

2.4.
Character theory and representations over local rings

Throughout this paper, we assume that the reader is familiar with fundamental results from representation theory and character theory. For reference, the reader may consult
[

For a specified prime

The local ring

Let

z^{2}-(\lambda-\mu)z+\mu-k=(z-\theta_{1})(z-\theta_{2})
by Lemma
2.2
. Moreover,
\theta_{1}\neq\theta_{2}
. For if
\theta_{1}=\theta_{2}
, then we must have
\mu\geqslant k
, but this is impossible, for
\mu\leqslant k
by definition, and if
\mu=k
, then
\lambda=\mu
cannot hold. So,
\theta_{1}\neq\theta_{2}
. Therefore, the minimal polynomial of
\mathfrak{F}(D)
is degree
1
or
2
with distinct roots, so
\mathfrak{F}(D)
is diagonalizable with
\theta_{1},\theta_{2}
on the diagonal. If
V_{\mathfrak{F}}(\theta_{i})
is the eigenspace corresponding to the eigenvectors of
\theta_{i}
, then
V_{\mathfrak{F}}=V_{\mathfrak{F}}(\theta_{1})\oplus V_{\mathfrak{F}}(\theta_{2})
and
\chi(D)=\dim V_{\mathfrak{F}}(\theta_{1})\theta_{1}+\dim V_{\mathfrak{F}}(\theta_{2})\theta_{2}
. Note that if
\chi
is a linear character, then it has one eigenspace of dimension
1
, in which case
\chi(D)=\theta_{1}
or
\chi(D)=\theta_{2}
.
∎

The following is a direct analogue of
[
25
, Lemma 2.3]
.

Lemma 3.3
.

Assume
{\rm Cay}(G,D)
is not a conference graph and
\mu>0
. Let
\xi\in\mathcal{L}
be a nonprincipal linear character of order
p
with kernel
N
. Then,
p|(k-\xi(D))
,
|N\cap D|=\frac{k-\xi(D)}{p}+\xi(D)
, and
|Na\cap D|=\frac{k-\xi(D)}{p}
for every
a\not\in N
.

Proof.

Now,
D\not\subseteq\ker\xi
, for
\xi
is a nonprincipal irreducible linear character. Therefore,
\xi(D)=\theta_{i}\neq k
by Lemma
3.2
, so for some
g\in D
we know that
\xi(g)=\omega
, for
\omega
a
p^{\text{th}}
root of unity. Recall from Lemma
3.1
that
\overline{\chi(D)}=\sum_{1=i}^{r}|h_{i}^{G}\cap D|\overline{\chi(h_{i})}
for conjugacy class representatives
h_{1},\dots,h_{r}
. Then,

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\xi(D)-\xi(D)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{r}|h_{i}^{G}\cap D|\overline{\xi(h_{i})}-\xi(D).

We re-express this as a sum over the cosets
Ng_{0},\dots,Ng_{p-1}
for coset representatives
g_{0},\dots,g_{p-1}\in G
. In addition, we may order the
g_{0},\dots,g_{p-1}
so that
\overline{\xi(Ng_{i})}=\omega^{i}
.

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{p-1}|Ng_{i}\cap D|\overline{\xi(Ng_{i})}-\xi(D)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{p-1}|Ng_{i}\cap D|\omega^{i}+(|N\cap D|-\xi(D))\omega^{0}

So,
\omega
is a root of the polynomial
p(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{p-1}|Ng_{i}\cap D|x^{i}+(|N\cap D|-\xi(D))
. Since
\xi(D)=\theta_{\alpha}\in\mathbb{Q}
by assumption, then the cyclotomic polynomial
x^{p-1}+\dots+x+1
divides
p(x)
, forcing

|N\cap D|-\xi(D)=|Ng_{1}\cap D|=\dots=|Ng_{p-1}\cap D|=c\in\mathbb{Z}.

Furthermore,

k=1_{G}(D)=\sum_{j=0}^{p-1}|Ng_{j}\cap D|=c+\xi(D)+(p-1)c=\xi(D)+pc

so
\frac{k-\xi(D)}{p}=c\in\mathbb{Z}
and
|N\cap D|=\frac{k-\xi(D)}{p}+\xi(D)
. In particular,
p
divides
k-\xi(D)
.
∎

We take this opportunity to prove some consequences of these results.

Lemma 3.4
.

Assume
G
contains a regular
(v,k,\lambda,\mu)
-PDS
D
.

(i)

If
\chi_{1}
and
\chi_{2}
are two irreducible characters of
G
,
\zeta
is a primitive
v^{\text{th}}
root of unity, and
\sigma
is an automorphism of
\mathbb{Q}(\zeta)
such that
\chi_{2}=\chi_{1}^{\sigma}
, then
\chi_{2}(D)=\chi_{1}(D)
. In particular, for all
\chi\in{\rm Irr}(G)
,
\chi(D)\in\mathbb{Z}
and hence
\overline{\chi}(D)=\chi(D)
.

(ii)

If
h_{1}^{G},\dots,h_{r}^{G}
denote the distinct conjugacy classes of
G
and
\chi\in{\rm Irr}(G)
, then

\sum_{i=1}^{r}|h_{i}^{G}\cap D|\chi(h_{i})=\chi(D).

(iii)

If
K
is a normal subgroup of

, then

\sum_{g\in K}\Phi(g)=|G|\cdot|K\cap D|.

(iv)

If
m_{i}
is the multiplicity of the eigenvalue

, then

m_{i}=\sum_{\mathfrak{F}\text{ irreducible}}(\dim V_{\mathfrak{F}})(\dim V_{\mathfrak{F}}(\theta_{i})).

Proof.

First, (i) follows directly from Lemma
3.2
(so
\chi_{1}(D)\in\mathbb{Z}
) and the fact that

is the identity:

\chi_{2}(D)=\chi_{1}^{\sigma}(D)=\chi_{1}(D)^{\sigma}=\chi_{1}(D).

Next, for (ii), Lemma

and its proof imply that

\sum_{i=1}^{r}|h_{i}^{G}\cap D|\chi(h_{i})=\frac{1}{|G|}\sum_{h\in G}\chi(h)\overline{|h^{G}\cap D|}=[\chi,\Phi]=\chi(D).

For (iii), if

are the distinct conjugacy classes of

, then

\displaystyle\sum_{g\in K}\Phi(g)

\displaystyle=\sum_{g\in G}|g^{G}\cap D||C_{G}(g)|

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{s}|g_{i}^{G}||g_{i}^{G}\cap D||C_{G}(g_{i})|

\displaystyle=|G|\cdot\sum_{i=1}^{s}|g_{i}^{G}\cap D|

\displaystyle=|G|\cdot|K\cap D|.

Finally, since the action of
G
on the associated SRG can be identified with the right regular representation
\rho
of
G
, and it is well known that its corresponding character
\chi_{\rho}
satisfies

\chi_{\rho}=\sum_{\chi\in{\rm Irr}(G)}\chi(1)\chi.

Lemma

implies that

m_{i}=\sum_{\mathfrak{F}\text{ irreducible}}(\dim V_{\mathfrak{F}})(\dim V_{\mathfrak{F}}(\theta_{i}))

for

, proving (iv).
∎

Remark 3.5
.

(1)

We again remark that the results in this subsection do not require that
G
be an abelian or nonabelian group or any conditions on the factors of
|G|
or
\sqrt{\Delta}
. Thus, these results may be applied even in cases when our techniques in the rest of the paper do not otherwise work; an example of such an application is given in Subsection
4.4

(2)

Unlike in the abelian case, while the conditions listed in Lemma
3.2
are necessary, they are not sufficient for the existence of a PDS in a nonabelian group; see Remark
4.8
.

(3)

The condition that
D=D^{(-1)}
is necessary in the proofs of
[
25
, Lemma 2.2]
and Lemma
3.2
. Unfortunately, most known difference sets are not reversible (and DSs are only conjectured to be reversible in very limited circumstances), so new ideas will be necessary to extend these results to general DSs.

(4)

The main technique in
[
25
]
and our own Theorem
3.8
is to extract information about
D
by analyzing
\mathfrak{F}(D)
over fields of characteristic
p
, when
p
does not divide
\sqrt{\Delta}=\theta_{1}-\theta_{2}
but does divide
|G|
. A critical element in this proof is that
\mathfrak{F}(D)
induces a representation on a certain field
L
of characteristic
p
, and that the induced representation
\overline{\mathfrak{F}(D)}
on
L
is diagonalizable, for its minimal polynomial is
(z-\theta_{1})(z-\theta_{2})\pmod{p}
, where
\theta_{1}\not\equiv\theta_{2}\pmod{p}
. As a result, while the results of this subsection still hold, the techniques in the following subsections largely fail if we ever have
|G|
sharing all of its prime divisors with
\theta_{1}-\theta_{2}
, for then the minimal polynomial of
\mathfrak{F}(D)
for any representation
\mathfrak{F}
is
(z-\theta_{1})(z-\theta_{2})\equiv(z-\theta_{1})^{2}\pmod{p}
, so
\mathfrak{F}(D)
is not diagonalizable. Unfortunately, this case happens reasonably often, so for such parameter sets techniques involving local rings are ineffective. In Subsection
4.4
, we provide an example computation for how character theory may still be applied to PDSs where
\sqrt{\Delta}
does divide
v
.

3.2.
Results that hold when a prime
p
divides
v
but not
\sqrt{\Delta}

We now prove an analogue of
[
25
, Corollary 2.1]
. Note again that the assumption that
\mu>0
, while necessary, amounts to assuming that the associated SRG is not the union of complete subgraphs of the same size. Going forward, we will tacitly assume
\mu>0
.

Lemma 3.6
.

Suppose
p
is a prime dividing
v
but not
\sqrt{\Delta}
. Assuming
\mu>0
, there exists a factorization
\mu=\mu_{1}\mu_{2}
such that
v_{1}=(k-\theta_{1})/\mu_{1}
,
v_{2}=(k-\theta_{2})/\mu_{2}
,

, and

divides only one of

,
v_{2}
.

Proof.

Recall for a primitive SRG that
v\mu=(k-\theta_{1})(k-\theta_{2})
(see
[
4
, Section 1.1.1]
). For some factorization of
\mu
into
\mu_{1}\mu_{2}
, we have
v=\frac{k-\theta_{1}}{\mu_{1}}\frac{k-\theta_{2}}{\mu_{2}}
where
v_{1}=\frac{k-\theta_{1}}{\mu_{1}}
and
v_{2}=\frac{k-\theta_{2}}{\mu_{2}}
are integers. If

divides

and
v_{2}
,

0\equiv 0-0\equiv(k-\theta_{2})-(k-\theta_{1})\equiv\theta_{1}-\theta_{2}\equiv\sqrt{\Delta}\pmod{p}

contradicting our assumption that

and

were relatively prime.
∎

Compare the following result to
[
25
, Lemma 2.4]
.

Lemma 3.7
.

Let
G
be a group with a regular PDS

and a nontrivial group of linear characters
\mathcal{L}
. Let

be a Sylow
p
-subgroup of
\mathcal{L}
, and suppose further that

and
\sqrt{\Delta}
are coprime. Then, for any
\xi^{\prime}\in P
,

\displaystyle\sum_{\xi\in P}\xi(D)=k+(|P|-1)\xi^{\prime}(D)
.

In particular, all nontrivial linear characters from
P
are equivalent over
D
.

Proof.

First,
|P|
divides
k-\xi(D)
by Lemmas
3.6
and
3.3
. Set
S\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\sum_{\xi\in P}\xi(D)
. Then,

\displaystyle S

\displaystyle=\sum_{\xi\in P}\xi(D)

\displaystyle=1_{G}(D)+\sum_{\xi\in P-\{1_{G}\}}\xi(D)

Fix
\theta_{\alpha}
to be an output of at least one
\xi\in P
on
D
, and let
r
of the remaining
\xi
evaluate to
\theta_{\beta}
on
D
. Then there are
|P|-r-1

in the sum. So,

\displaystyle S

\displaystyle=1_{G}(D)+r\theta_{\beta}+(|P|-r-1)\theta_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=1_{G}(D)-\theta_{\alpha}+r\theta_{\beta}+(|P|-r)\theta_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=k-\theta_{\alpha}+r\theta_{\beta}+(|P|-r)\theta_{\alpha}

On the other hand, if we define

to be the kernel of

,

\displaystyle S

\displaystyle=\sum_{g\in D}\sum_{\xi\in P}\xi(g)

\displaystyle=\sum_{g\in D\cap N_{p}}\sum_{\xi\in P}\xi(g)\ +\sum_{g\in D-N_{p}}\sum_{\xi\in P}\xi(g)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\xi\in P}\xi(D\cap N_{p})\ +\sum_{g\in D-N_{p}}0

\displaystyle=\sum_{\xi\in P}|D\cap N_{p}|+0

\displaystyle=|P||D\cap N_{p}|

Recall that

divides

. Thus, evaluating

. Further,

and

are coprime, so

or

. If

, then every
\xi

\theta_{\beta}
, which contradicts our selection of

to be the output of some

, so

. Therefore, all the linear characters of

are equivalent over

.
∎

For the next theorem (which is an analogue of
[
25
, Theorem 2.5]
), we once again set
\zeta
to be a
|G|^{\text{th}}
root of unity. Recall the definition of the local ring
\mathfrak{R}
given in Definition
2.3
. If
\mathfrak{R}
is the local ring for the prime ideal
\mathfrak{p}
, then we define the field

is a finite field of characteristic

.

Theorem 3.8
.

Let
\xi\in\mathcal{L}
be a linear character of order
p^{a}
with

coprime to
\sqrt{\Delta}
, and let
\chi\in{\rm Irr}(G)
. Then,
\xi\chi(D)=\chi(D)
.

The following proof is quite technical, so we provide a broad explanation of its various moving parts. We would like to show that
\chi(D)=\xi\chi(D)
. To do this, we use Theorem
2.5
to take a
\mathbb{Q}(\zeta)
-representation
\mathfrak{F}
affording the
\mathbb{C}
-character
\chi
, use Lemma
2.4
to assume that
\mathfrak{F}
is a
\mathfrak{R}
-representation, and construct a homomorphism, which we denote by
\overline{\phantom{x}}
, from the
\mathfrak{R}
-module underlying
\mathfrak{F}
to an
L
-module, which is also an
L
-vector space since
L
is a field. Under this homomorphism,
\overline{\xi(D)\mathfrak{F}(D)}=\overline{\mathfrak{F}(D)}
, since for all
x\in G
,
\overline{\xi(x)}
is a scalar whose order divides
p^{a}
in
L
, so
\overline{\xi(x)}
must evaluate to
1
. We then show that for any matrix
\mathfrak{F}(D)
with
\mathbb{Q}(\zeta)
-eigenspace

and
\mathfrak{R}
-eigenspace

, that

\dim_{\mathbb{Q}(\zeta)}V_{\mathfrak{F}}(\theta_{\alpha})={\rm rk}\;M_{\mathfrak{F}}(\theta_{\alpha})=\dim_{L}\overline{M_{\mathfrak{F}}(\theta_{\alpha})}=\dim_{L}\overline{M_{\overline{\mathfrak{F}}}}(\theta_{\alpha}),

where
\overline{M_{\overline{\mathfrak{F}}}}(\theta_{\alpha})
is the eigenspace of the representation
\overline{\mathfrak{F}}
induced by
\mathfrak{F}
on the
L
-vector space. This finally allows us to conclude

\dim_{\mathbb{Q}(\zeta)}V_{\mathfrak{F}}(\theta_{\alpha})=\dim_{L}\overline{M_{\overline{\mathfrak{F}}}}(\theta_{\alpha})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle*}}{{=}}\dim_{L}\overline{M_{\overline{\xi\mathfrak{F}}}}(\theta_{\alpha})=\dim_{\mathbb{Q}(\zeta)}V_{\xi\mathfrak{F}}(\theta_{\alpha}),

where we apply the equality
\overline{\xi\mathfrak{F}(D)}=\overline{\mathfrak{F}(D)}
at
*
. By Lemma
3.2
, equality of the dimensions of the eigenspaces is enough to establish

.

Proof of Theorem
3.8
.

Let
\chi
be a
\mathbb{C}
-character in
{\rm Irr}(G)
. By Theorem
2.5
, we may pick a
\mathbb{Q}(\zeta)
-representation
\mathfrak{F}
of
G
affording
\chi
. We denote the
\mathbb{Q}(\zeta)
-vector space underlying
\mathfrak{F}
as
V_{\mathfrak{F}}
, and we pick a basis
B
for this space. We define
\mathfrak{R}
as in Definition
2.3
, noting that it has a unique maximal ideal
\mathfrak{p}
containing
p
by Lemma
2.4
. Now,
V_{\mathfrak{F}}
contains a
\mathfrak{R}[G]
-module defined by taking all
\mathfrak{R}
-linear combinations of the vectors
\{\mathfrak{F}(g)v_{i}:v_{i}\in B,g\in G\}
. Call this
\mathfrak{R}
-module
M_{\mathfrak{F}}
. Since
\mathfrak{R}
is a PID by Lemma
2.4
, we may pick a basis
w_{1},\dots,w_{n}
of
M_{\mathfrak{F}}
. This is also a basis of
V_{\mathfrak{F}}
, for
B\subseteq M_{\mathfrak{F}}
. Therefore, we may coordinatize the space
V_{\mathfrak{F}}
by the basis vectors
w_{1},\dots,w_{n}
, so that all
\mathfrak{F}(g)
have their entries in
\mathfrak{R}
. Thus,
\mathfrak{F}(D)
is an
\mathfrak{R}
-matrix, proving part (i) of Lemma
2.4
. For further reference, see especially the exposition of Feit
[
12
, 4.1 in Chapter 4]
.

We define the submodule
\mathfrak{p}M_{\mathfrak{F}}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\{w\in M_{\mathfrak{F}}:w=qv,q\in\mathfrak{p},v\in M_{\mathfrak{F}}\}
. This is a closed subspace of
M_{\mathfrak{F}}
, so
M_{\mathfrak{F}}/\mathfrak{p}M_{\mathfrak{F}}
is well-defined. We therefore define the natural projection
\overline{\phantom{x}}:M_{\mathfrak{F}}\longrightarrow M_{\mathfrak{F}}/\mathfrak{p}M_{\mathfrak{F}}
. For an
\mathfrak{R}
-representation
\mathfrak{F}
, there is a corresponding representation
\overline{\mathfrak{F}}
on
M_{\mathfrak{F}}/\mathfrak{p}M_{\mathfrak{F}}
by
\overline{\mathfrak{F}(g)}\overline{w}=\overline{\mathfrak{F}(g)w}
. As before,
\overline{\xi(g)}\equiv 1\pmod{\mathfrak{p}}
, so for any vector
w\in M_{\mathfrak{F}}
, we have
\overline{\xi(g)}\overline{w}=\overline{w}
. Thus, for all
w\in V_{\mathfrak{F}}
,

\displaystyle\overline{\xi\mathfrak{F}(D)}\overline{w}

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in D}\overline{\xi(x)\mathfrak{F}(x)}\overline{w}

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in D}(1)\overline{\mathfrak{F}(x)}\overline{w}

\displaystyle=\overline{\mathfrak{F}(D)}\overline{w}

so
\overline{\xi\mathfrak{F}(D)}=\overline{\mathfrak{F}(D)}
as claimed.

Now, let
V_{\mathfrak{F}}(\theta_{\alpha})
and
M_{\mathfrak{F}}(\theta_{\alpha})
be the
\mathbb{Q}(\zeta)
-eigenspace and
\mathfrak{R}
-eigenspace, respectively, for the eigenvalue
\theta_{\alpha}
(
\alpha\in\{1,2\}
) of the matrix
\mathfrak{F}(D)
. We prove the sequence of equalities

\dim_{\mathbb{Q}(\zeta)}V_{\mathfrak{F}}(\theta_{\alpha})={\rm rk}\;M_{\mathfrak{F}}(\theta_{\alpha})=\dim_{L}\overline{M_{\mathfrak{F}}(\theta_{\alpha})}=\dim_{L}\overline{M_{\overline{\mathfrak{F}}}}(\theta_{\alpha}),

where
\overline{M_{\overline{\mathfrak{F}}}}
is the vector space
M_{\mathfrak{F}}/\mathfrak{p}M_{\mathfrak{F}}
acted on by
\overline{\mathfrak{F}}
. In particular,
\overline{M_{\overline{\mathfrak{F}}}}(\theta_{\alpha})
is the
\overline{\theta_{\alpha}}
eigenspace of
\overline{\mathfrak{F}(D)}
in
\overline{M_{\overline{\mathfrak{F}}}}
.

We first show that
\dim_{\mathbb{Q}(\zeta)}V_{\mathfrak{F}}(\theta_{\alpha})={\rm rk}\;M_{\mathfrak{F}}(\theta_{\alpha})
. We evidently have
{\rm rk}\;M_{\mathfrak{F}}(\theta_{\alpha})\leqslant\dim_{\mathbb{Q}(\zeta)}V_{\mathfrak{F}}(\theta_{\alpha})
, for we may pick a basis
v_{1},\dots,v_{k}
of
M_{\mathfrak{F}}(\theta_{\alpha})
by
[
22
, Theorem 7.1]
. These are linearly independent in
V_{\mathfrak{F}}(\theta_{\alpha})
, for if there exists some
\mathbb{Q}(\zeta)
-linear combination of the

such that
\frac{a_{1}}{b_{i}}v_{1}+\dots+\frac{a_{n}}{b_{n}}v_{n}=\frac{a_{j}}{b_{j}}v_{j}
, then setting

, we have
b(\frac{a_{1}}{b_{i}}v_{1}+\dots+\frac{a_{n}}{b_{n}}v_{n})=b\frac{a_{j}}{b_{j}}v_{j}
, a dependence relation in
M_{\mathfrak{F}}(\theta_{\alpha})
, forcing

for each

. On the other hand,
\dim_{\mathbb{Q}(\zeta)}V_{\mathfrak{F}}(\theta_{\alpha})\leqslant{\rm rk}\;M_{\mathfrak{F}}(\theta_{\alpha})
, for if we have a basis of
V_{\mathfrak{F}}(\theta_{\alpha})
, then the
\mathfrak{R}
-multiples of the basis vectors are in
M_{\mathfrak{F}}(\theta_{\alpha})
and thus linearly independent in
M_{\mathfrak{F}}(\theta_{\alpha})
.

We now prove
\dim_{L}\overline{M_{\mathfrak{F}}(\theta_{\alpha})}\leqslant{\rm rk}\;M_{\mathfrak{F}}(\theta_{\alpha})
. We must show first that there exists a set of basis vectors
v_{1},\dots,v_{k}
of
M_{\mathfrak{F}}(\theta_{\alpha})
which do not vanish in
\overline{M_{\mathfrak{F}}(\theta_{\alpha})}
. For this, we use the Elementary Divisor Theorem (see
[

, Chapter III, Theorem 7.8]
), which states that there exists set of vectors
v_{1},\dots,v_{n}
of

so that for some

, the vectors

form a basis for
M_{\mathfrak{F}}(\theta_{\alpha})
. Now, if
r_{i}v_{i}
is an eigenvector of
\mathfrak{F}(D)
, then
v_{i}
itself must be an eigenvector of
\mathfrak{F}(D)
, for
r_{i}
is a scalar. Therefore,
v_{i}
is in the span of the basis, so we may choose

to be

. Therefore,
v_{1},\dots,v_{k}
is a basis for
M_{\mathfrak{F}}(\theta_{\alpha})
. Since
\mathfrak{p}M_{\mathfrak{F}}
is defined as the set of
\mathfrak{p}
multiples of the basis vectors of
M_{\mathfrak{F}}
, then
\overline{v_{i}}\neq 0
for each
v_{i}
. Each
\overline{w}\in\overline{M_{\mathfrak{F}}(\theta_{\alpha})}
may be expressed as a sum of the
\overline{v_{i}}
for we may take some pre-image

of
\overline{w}
, and then

, so
\overline{a_{1}}\overline{v_{1}}+\dots+\overline{a_{k}}\overline{v_{k}}=\overline{w}
as needed.

The equality
\dim_{L}\overline{M_{\mathfrak{F}}(\theta_{\alpha})}={\rm rk}\;M_{\mathfrak{F}}(\theta_{\alpha})
is obtained by the fact that if for the basis vectors

and coefficients

, there is a dependence
\overline{a_{1}v_{1}+\dots+a_{n}v_{n}}=0
in

, then

where

. Therefore,
(a_{1}-b_{1})v_{1}+\dots+(a_{n}-b_{n})v_{n}=0
, so
a_{1}=b_{1},\dots,a_{n}=b_{n}
, and thus

. So, there exists only a trivial dependence relation among the
\overline{v_{i}}
in
\overline{M_{\mathfrak{F}}(\theta_{\alpha})}
, forcing
{\rm rk}\;M_{\mathfrak{F}}(\theta_{\alpha})\leqslant\dim_{L}\overline{M_{\mathfrak{F}}(\theta_{\alpha})}
, and
{\rm rk}\;M_{\mathfrak{F}}(\theta_{\alpha})=\dim_{L}\overline{M_{\mathfrak{F}}(\theta_{\alpha})}
by extension.

Finally, we show that
\dim_{L}\overline{M_{\mathfrak{F}}(\theta_{\alpha})}=\dim_{L}\overline{M_{\overline{\mathfrak{F}}}}(\theta_{\alpha})
, where
\overline{M_{\overline{\mathfrak{F}}}}
is the vector space
M_{\mathfrak{F}}/\mathfrak{p}M_{\mathfrak{F}}
acted on by
\overline{\mathfrak{F}}
. The inequality
\dim_{L}\overline{M_{\mathfrak{F}}(\theta_{\alpha})}\leqslant\dim_{L}\overline{M_{\overline{\mathfrak{F}}}}(\theta_{\alpha})
is straightforward, for
\overline{M_{\mathfrak{F}}(\theta_{\alpha})}
is a vector space contained in
\overline{M_{\overline{\mathfrak{F}}}}(\theta_{\alpha})
. Now,
{\rm rk}\;M_{\mathfrak{F}}=\dim V_{\mathfrak{F}}
, and
\dim_{L}\overline{M_{\overline{\mathfrak{F}}}}\leqslant{\rm rk}\;M_{\mathfrak{F}}
. Therefore,

\dim V_{\mathfrak{F}}=\dim V_{\mathfrak{F}}(\theta_{\alpha})+\dim V_{\mathfrak{F}}(\theta_{\beta})={\rm rk}\;M_{\mathfrak{F}}(\theta_{\alpha})+{\rm rk}\;M_{\mathfrak{F}}(\theta_{\beta})=\dim_{L}\overline{M_{\mathfrak{F}}(\theta_{\alpha})}+\dim_{L}\overline{M_{\mathfrak{F}}(\theta_{\beta})},

and

\dim_{L}\overline{M_{\mathfrak{F}}(\theta_{\alpha})}+\dim_{L}\overline{M_{\mathfrak{F}}(\theta_{\beta})}\leqslant\dim_{L}\overline{M_{\overline{\mathfrak{F}}}}(\theta_{\alpha})+\dim_{L}\overline{M_{\overline{\mathfrak{F}}}}(\theta_{\beta})\leqslant\dim_{L}\overline{M_{\overline{\mathfrak{F}}}}\leqslant\dim V_{\mathfrak{F}}.

Note, the inequality
\dim_{L}\overline{M_{\overline{\mathfrak{F}}}}(\theta_{\alpha})+\dim_{L}\overline{M_{\overline{\mathfrak{F}}}}(\theta_{\beta})\leqslant\dim_{L}\overline{M_{\overline{\mathfrak{F}}}}
holds because
\overline{\theta}_{\alpha}\neq\overline{\theta}_{\beta}
, for only in this case is
\overline{\mathfrak{F}}
diagonalizable. Otherwise, we have
\dim_{L}\overline{M_{\overline{\mathfrak{F}}}}(\theta_{\alpha})+\dim_{L}\overline{M_{\overline{\mathfrak{F}}}}(\theta_{\beta})>\dim_{L}\overline{M_{\overline{\mathfrak{F}}}}
.

Our sequence of inequalities gives

\dim V_{\mathfrak{F}}=\dim_{L}\overline{M_{\mathfrak{F}}(\theta_{\alpha})}+\dim_{L}\overline{M_{\mathfrak{F}}(\theta_{\beta})}\leqslant\dim_{L}\overline{M_{\overline{\mathfrak{F}}}}(\theta_{\alpha})+\dim_{L}\overline{M_{\overline{\mathfrak{F}}}}(\theta_{\beta})\leqslant\dim V_{\mathfrak{F}},

so the inequalities are saturated, forcing

\dim_{L}\overline{M_{\mathfrak{F}}(\theta_{\alpha})}+\dim_{L}\overline{M_{\mathfrak{F}}(\theta_{\beta})}=\dim_{L}\overline{M_{\overline{\mathfrak{F}}}}(\theta_{\alpha})+\dim_{L}\overline{M_{\overline{\mathfrak{F}}}}(\theta_{\beta}).

By the above equality,
\dim_{L}\overline{M_{\mathfrak{F}}(\theta_{\beta})}\leqslant\dim_{L}\overline{M_{\overline{\mathfrak{F}}}}(\theta_{\beta})
implies
\dim_{L}\overline{M_{\overline{\mathfrak{F}}}}(\theta_{\alpha})\leqslant\dim_{L}\overline{M_{\mathfrak{F}}(\theta_{\alpha})}
, so we achieve the equality
\dim_{L}\overline{M_{\overline{\mathfrak{F}}}}(\theta_{\alpha})=\dim_{L}\overline{M_{\mathfrak{F}}(\theta_{\alpha})}
.

Therefore, we have shown that
\dim_{\mathbb{Q}(\zeta)}V_{\mathfrak{F}}(\theta_{\alpha})={\rm rk}\;M_{\mathfrak{F}}(\theta_{\alpha})=\dim_{L}\overline{M_{\mathfrak{F}}(\theta_{\alpha})}=\dim_{L}\overline{M_{\overline{\mathfrak{F}}}}(\theta_{\alpha})
. By the equality
\overline{\mathfrak{F}(D)}=\overline{\xi\mathfrak{F}(D)}
we also have
\dim_{L}\overline{M_{\overline{\mathfrak{F}}}}(\theta_{\alpha})=\dim_{L}\overline{M_{\overline{\xi\mathfrak{F}}}}(\theta_{\alpha})
. Therefore,

\dim_{\mathbb{Q}(\zeta)}V_{\mathfrak{F}}(\theta_{\alpha})=\dim_{L}\overline{M_{\overline{\mathfrak{F}}}}(\theta_{\alpha})=\dim_{L}\overline{M_{\overline{\xi\mathfrak{F}}}}(\theta_{\alpha})=\dim_{\mathbb{Q}(\zeta)}V_{\xi\mathfrak{F}}(\theta_{\alpha}).

Combining this equality with Lemma
3.2
,

\displaystyle\xi\chi(D)

\displaystyle=(\dim_{\mathbb{Q}(\zeta)}V_{\xi\mathfrak{F}}(\theta_{1}))\theta_{1}+(\dim_{\mathbb{Q}(\zeta)}V_{\xi\mathfrak{F}}(\theta_{2}))\theta_{2}

\displaystyle=(\dim_{\mathbb{Q}(\zeta)}V_{\mathfrak{F}}(\theta_{1}))\theta_{1}+(\dim_{\mathbb{Q}(\zeta)}V_{\mathfrak{F}}(\theta_{2}))\theta_{2}

\displaystyle=\chi(D),

which proves the theorem.
∎

Corollary 3.9
.

Let
\xi,\chi
be linear characters of order
p^{a},q^{b}
respectively such that
p
and
q
are coprime to
\sqrt{\Delta}
. Then,
\xi(D)=\chi(D)
. In addition, all linear characters of order coprime to
\sqrt{\Delta}
are equivalent over
D
.

Proof.

By Theorem
3.8
,
\chi(D)=\xi\chi(D)=\chi\xi(D)=\xi(D)
. Now, suppose that
\xi
is a linear character of order coprime to
\sqrt{\Delta}
. By the fundamental theorem of abelian groups, there exist prime power order linear characters
\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n}
such that
\xi_{1}\dots\xi_{n}=\xi
. Again using Theorem
3.8
, we have by induction
\xi(D)=\xi_{1}\dots\xi_{n}(D)=\xi_{1}(D)
, for
\xi_{1}=\xi^{\prime}
of prime power order. For another linear character
\chi
of order coprime to
\sqrt{\Delta}
, we use the same technique to achieve
\chi(D)=\chi^{\prime}(D)
, for
\chi^{\prime}
of prime power order. Since
\chi^{\prime}(D)=\xi^{\prime}(D)
, we achieve
\xi(D)=\xi^{\prime}(D)=\chi^{\prime}(D)=\chi(D)
as claimed.
∎

In order to prove Corollary
3.10
, which is useful when ruling out the possibility of certain groups containing a PDS, we introduce the set of primes
\Pi_{\alpha}
and
\Pi_{\beta}
with products
\pi_{\alpha}
and
\pi_{\beta}
defined so that
\pi_{\alpha}
and
\pi_{\beta}
are the largest integers dividing
v_{\alpha}
and
v_{\beta}
, respectively, such that
\gcd(\sqrt{\Delta},\pi_{\alpha})=\gcd(\sqrt{\Delta},\pi_{\beta})=1
.

Corollary 3.10
.

Suppose that
\xi\in\mathcal{L}
has order dividing
\pi_{\alpha}
. Then, each
\xi\in\mathcal{L}
with order coprime to
\sqrt{\Delta}
has an order dividing
\pi_{\alpha}
.

Proof.

Let
\xi\in\mathcal{L}
of prime order
p
such that
p
divides
\pi_{\alpha}
. Let
\xi\in\mathcal{L}
have prime order
q
coprime to
\sqrt{\Delta}
. Then, by Corollary
3.9
,
\xi(D)=\chi(D)
. So,
k-\xi(D)=k-\theta_{\alpha}=k-\chi(D)
. Using Lemma
3.3
, both
p
and
q
divide
k-\theta_{\alpha}
. Since
p,q
divide
v
, and neither
p
nor
q
divide
k-\theta_{\beta}
by Lemma
3.6
, we conclude that
q
must divide
v_{\alpha}
. Since
q
is coprime to
\sqrt{\Delta}
, we have
q\in\Pi_{\alpha}
. It follows that all primes
q
dividing
|\mathcal{L}|
and coprime to
\sqrt{\Delta}
must divide
\pi_{\alpha}
. Therefore, all
q^{a}
dividing
|\mathcal{L}|
must also divide
\pi_{\alpha}
. In conclusion, for every linear character
\xi
of order
n
coprime to
\sqrt{\Delta}
, the order
n
is a product of prime powers
q_{1}^{a_{1}}\dots q_{m}^{a_{m}}
. Since each
q_{i}^{a_{i}}
is coprime to
\sqrt{\Delta}
, we know
q_{i}^{a_{i}}
divides
\pi_{\alpha}
, for each
i
, so
q_{1}^{a_{1}}\dots q_{m}^{a_{m}}=n
divides
\pi_{\alpha}
.
∎

For this next theorem, and for the remainder of the section, let
H
be a subgroup of linear characters with order coprime to
\sqrt{\Delta}
, and let
N
be the intersection of the kernels of the characters in
H
. The following theorem is a consequence of Corollary
3.9
. The reader may compare Theorem
3.11
to
[
25
, Theorem 2.7]
.

Theorem 3.11
.

If
a\not\in N
, then for
\xi\in H
,
|C_{G}(a)||a^{G}\cap D|=k-\xi(D)
.

Proof.

Let
\xi\in H
be a linear character of order coprime to
|G|
. Let
\chi
be an arbitrary irreducible character of
G
. We have

\displaystyle\Phi(a)

\displaystyle=|C_{G}(a)||a^{G}\cap D|

\displaystyle=\sum_{\xi\in H}\xi(D)\xi(a)+\sum_{\chi\not\in H}\chi(D)\chi(a).

By Corollary
3.9
, all the nonprincipal
\xi(D)
are equal. Therefore,

\displaystyle\Phi(a)

\displaystyle=k-\xi(D)+\xi(D)\sum_{\xi\in H}\xi(a)+\sum_{\chi\not\in H}\chi(D)\chi(a)

By orthogonality, the sum of characters in
H
is zero, leaving

\displaystyle\Phi(a)

\displaystyle=k-\xi(D)+\sum_{\chi\not\in H}\chi(D)\chi(a)

We now show that
\sum_{\chi\not\in H}\chi(D)\chi(a)
is zero. The abelian group
H
acts on the set of characters by left multiplication. Denote an orbit of
\chi
under
H
by
\mathcal{O}
. Then it suffices to show that

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{O}}\tau(a)

We let
H_{\chi}
be the stabilizer of
\chi
in
H
. Let
\xi_{1},...,\xi_{r}\in H
be coset representatives of
H_{\chi}
in
H
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{O}}\tau(a)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\xi_{i}\chi(a)

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{r}\xi_{i}(a)\right)\chi(a)

We multiply by the sum of elements in the stabilizer of
\chi
.

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{\eta\in H_{\chi}}\eta(a)\right)\left(\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{O}}\tau(a)\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{\eta\in H_{\chi}}\eta(a)\right)\left(\sum_{i=1}^{r}\xi_{i}(a)\right)\chi(a)

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{\eta\in H_{\chi}}\sum_{i=1}^{r}\eta\xi_{i}(a)\right)\chi(a)

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{\xi\in H}\xi(a)\right)\chi(a)

Again,
\left(\sum_{\xi\in H}\xi(a)\right)\chi(a)=0
by the orthogonality relations. If
\sum_{\eta\in H_{\chi}}\eta(a)\neq 0
, we are done. If it is
0
, then there must be some
\eta
such that
\eta(a)\neq 1
, since
a
is then not in the kernel of the stabilizer. In this case,
\eta(a)\chi(a)=\chi(a)
, so
\chi(a)=0
. Either way,
\sum_{\chi\not\in H}\chi(D)\chi(a)=0
, so
|C_{G}(a)||a^{G}\cap D|=k-\xi(D)
.
∎

Lemma 3.12
.

For
a\not\in N
, the greatest common divisor of
|C_{G}(a)|
and at least one of
\pi_{\alpha},\pi_{\beta}
is
1
.

Proof.

By Theorem
3.11
,
|C_{G}(a)|
divides
k-\xi(D)
. Without loss of generality, we may suppose
\xi(D)=\theta_{\alpha}
. Then, by Lemma
3.6
,
\gcd(|C_{G}(a)|,\pi_{\beta})=1
, for otherwise there exists some
p\in\Pi_{\beta}
dividing
|C_{G}(a)|
, and so by Theorem
3.11
,
p
divides
k-\theta_{\alpha}=|C_{G}(a)||a^{G}\cap D|
. Therefore,
p
divides both
k-\theta_{\alpha}
and
k-\theta_{\beta}
, a contradiction.
∎

Once again, we invite the reader to compare the following Theorem
3.13
to
[
25
, Theorem 2.8]
.

Theorem 3.13
.

Suppose that there exists a prime
p
dividing
|G|
so that
p\not|\;\sqrt{\Delta}
. Without loss of generality, let
p\in\Pi_{\alpha}
and suppose that
p
divides
|\mathcal{L}|
. Finally, let
P
be a Sylow
p
-subgroup of
G
. Then
\gcd(|N_{G}(P)|,\pi_{\beta})=1
. Moreover, if
G
is solvable, then

\pi_{\beta}\equiv 1\pmod{p}.

Proof.

Since
p
is coprime to
\sqrt{\Delta}
, and
p
divides
|\mathcal{L}|
, we have that
H
is nontrivial. Thus,
N\neq G
, so the hypotheses for Lemma
3.12
apply. Suppose there exists
q
dividing
|N_{G}(P)|
such that
q\in\Pi_{\beta}
. Then, there is an element
g\in N_{G}(P)
of order
q
. By
[
18
, Theorem 3.5]
,
P=C_{P}(g)[\langle g\rangle,P]
. Each element in
C_{P}(g)
centralizes
g
, so each element
a\in C_{P}(g)
contains
g
in its centralizer. Thus, for some
m
,
\gcd(|C_{G}(a)|,\pi_{\beta})=q\cdot m
, and since
a\in C_{P}(g)
and
a\in C_{G}(a)
, then the order of
a
divides both
|P|
and
|C_{G}(a)|
. Thus,
\gcd(|C_{G}(a)|,|P|)>1
, forcing
\gcd(|C_{G}(a)|,\pi_{\alpha})>1
. Therefore, by the contrapositive of Lemma
3.12
,
a
is in
N
. Thus,
P=C_{P}(g)[\langle g\rangle,P]\subseteq NG^{\prime}\subseteq N
(where
G^{\prime}=[G,G]
), given that
G^{\prime}\subseteq N
, since for each
\xi\in H
, we must have
G^{\prime}\subseteq\ker\xi
. So,
P\subseteq N
. But,
p
divides
|\mathcal{L}|
, a contradiction. Therefore,
\gcd(|N_{G}(P)|,\pi_{\beta})=1
.

Now, suppose in addition that
G
is a solvable group. By Hall’s Theorem, there is a subgroup
K
of
G
which is a
\pi_{\alpha}\pi_{\beta}
-group. Moreover, since
G
is solvable,
K
is solvable, so there exists a subgroup
U
of
K
which is a
\pi_{\alpha}
-group. Since
p\in\Pi_{\alpha}
, then
|P|
divides
\pi_{\alpha}
. Therefore,
P
is contained in some Hall subgroup,
L
. Any two Hall subgroups are conjugate, and thus there exists
g\in G
such that
g^{-1}Lg=K
. So,
K
contains a Sylow
p
-subgroup,
g^{-1}Pg
. Therefore, we are justified in assuming that
P\subseteq K
, forcing
P\subseteq U
. Thus by our preceding argument,
N_{K}(P)=N_{K}(U)
. Therefore, using Sylow III,

1\equiv\frac{|K|}{|N_{K}(P)|}\equiv\frac{\pi_{\alpha}\pi_{\beta}}{|N_{K}(P)|}\equiv\frac{\pi_{\alpha}}{|N_{K}(P)|}\pi_{\beta}\equiv\frac{|U|}{|N_{U}(P)|}\pi_{\beta}\equiv\pi_{\beta}\pmod{p}.

∎

3.3.
Intersections of
D
with certain normal subgroups

Let
G^{\prime}=[G,G]
be the derived subgroup of
G
, and let
\mathcal{L}
be the group of linear characters of
G
; note that
\mathcal{L}\cong G/G^{\prime}
. Recall that we defined
H
to be a subgroup of linear characters with order coprime to
\sqrt{\Delta}
and
N
to be the intersection of the kernels of the characters in
H
. In the case when
\gcd(v,\sqrt{\Delta})=1
, we see that
|\mathcal{L}|
is coprime to
\sqrt{\Delta}
, so we can choose
H
to be
\mathcal{L}
, in which case
N=G^{\prime}
. We note first the following result.

Proposition 3.14
.

If
|H|\neq\{1\}
and
\xi(D)=\theta_{\alpha}
for some nonprincipal linear character
\xi
in
H
, then
\xi(D)=\theta_{\alpha}
for all nonprincipal
\xi\in H
. Moreover, for all
a\in G\backslash N
,

\Phi(a)=|C_{G}(a)||a^{G}\cap D|=k-\theta_{\alpha}.

Proof.

This follows immediately from the above discussion, Corollary
3.9
, and Theorem
3.11
.
∎

Going forward, we will assume that
N
is a proper subgroup of
G
(so that
H
exists),
\xi(D)=\theta_{\alpha}
for all nonprincipal linear characters
\xi
of
H
, and we will denote the eigenvalue that is not
k
or
\theta_{\alpha}
by
\theta_{\beta}
. While Proposition
3.14
is quite strong, we will in fact be able to calculate
|N\cap D|
exactly.

Theorem 3.15
.

If
N<G
, then

|N\cap D|=\frac{k-\theta_{\alpha}}{|H|}+\theta_{\alpha}.

Proof.

First, we apply Lemma
3.4
(iii) to
N
to obtain

\sum_{x\in N}\Phi(x)=|G|\cdot|N\cap D|.

Next, using Proposition
3.14
and recalling that
|G|=|N|\cdot|H|
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{x\in N}\Phi(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{g\in G}\Phi(g)-\sum_{g\notin N}\Phi(g)

\displaystyle=|G|k-\left(|G|-|N|\right)(k-\theta_{\alpha})

\displaystyle=|N|(k-\theta_{\alpha})+|G|\theta_{\alpha}

Therefore,

\displaystyle|N\cap D|

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|G|}\left(|N|(k-\theta_{\alpha})+|G|\theta_{\alpha})\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{k-\theta_{\alpha}}{|H|}+\theta_{\alpha}.

∎

Next, we are able to put even more severe restrictions on the intersection of the other cosets of
N
with
D
.

Theorem 3.16
.

If
N<G
, and
a\in G\backslash N
, then

|Na\cap D|=\frac{k-\theta_{\alpha}}{|H|}.

Proof.

Note by assumption that
G^{\prime}\subseteq N
, since
G^{\prime}
is the intersection of the kernels of all linear characters. Moreover, if
b\in a^{G}
, then

b=a^{g}=g^{-1}ag=(g^{-1}aga^{-1})a=[g,a^{-1}]a\in G^{\prime}a,

and so
a^{G}\subseteq G^{\prime}a\subseteq Na
, and so
Na
will be a disjoint union of conjugacy classes. Let
a_{1}^{G},\dots,a_{s}^{G}
be the distinct conjugacy classes in
Na
. Then, using Proposition
3.14
, we have

\displaystyle|Na\cap D|

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{s}|a_{i}^{G}\cap D|

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{s}\frac{k-\theta_{\alpha}}{|C_{G}(a_{i})|}

\displaystyle=\frac{k-\theta_{\alpha}}{|G|}\sum_{i=1}^{s}|a_{i}^{G}|

\displaystyle=\frac{(k-\theta_{\alpha})|N|}{|G|}

\displaystyle=\frac{k-\theta_{\alpha}}{|H|}.

∎

When
\gcd(v,\sqrt{\Delta})=1
, it follows that
H=\mathcal{L}
and
N=G^{\prime}
, so we get the following corollary, which helps to explain why the sizes of conjugacy class intersections with a regular PDS take on very few values in practice in these situations (see Appendix
C
).

Corollary 3.17
.

If
G
contains a regular
(v,k,\lambda,\mu)
-PDS and
\gcd(v,\sqrt{\Delta})=1
, then

|G^{\prime}\cap D|=\frac{k-\theta_{\alpha}}{|\mathcal{L}|}+\theta_{\alpha}

and, for all
a\notin G^{\prime}
,

|G^{\prime}a\cap D|=\frac{k-\theta_{\alpha}}{|\mathcal{L}|}.

3.4.
Example applications

We now demonstrate how these generalizations of Ott’s work can be applied in other settings.

Proposition 3.18
.

There is no group acting regularly on the points of a GQ with order
(q,q^{2}-q)
when
q
is even.

Proof.

Let
q
be even, and assume that a group
G
acts regularly on the point set of a GQ with order
(q,q^{2}-q)
. This implies the existence of a
((q+1)(q^{2}-q+1),q(q^{2}-q+1),q-1,q^{2}-q+1)
-PDS; in this case, the eigenvalues
\theta_{1},\theta_{2}
are
q-1
,
-q^{2}+q-1
, and
\sqrt{\Delta}=q^{2}
. Note that if
p|\sqrt{\Delta}
, then
p\not|\;v=(q+1)(q^{2}-q+1)
, so we may apply Theorem
3.13
. Thus, we let
p
divide
v
. Now,
k-\theta_{1}=q^{3}-q^{2}+1
and
k-\theta_{2}=(q+1)(q^{2}-q+1)
. We show that
v_{1}=k-\theta_{1}
and
v_{2}=\frac{k-\theta_{2}}{\mu}
. So, suppose
p|\mu
and
p|k-\theta_{1}
. Then mod
p
,

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\equiv q^{3}-q^{2}+1

\displaystyle\equiv q(q^{2}-q+1)-q+1

\displaystyle\equiv 1-q

\displaystyle\equiv 1-q-(q^{2}-q+1)

\displaystyle\equiv q^{2}

So
p|q
and
p|1-q
, forcing
p=1
, so
\mu
cannot divide
k-\theta_{1}
. Therefore,
v_{1}=q^{3}-q^{2}+1
and
v_{2}=q+1
.

Note that
v
is odd, so if
G
acts regularly on the points of this GQ, then
G
is solvable by Feit-Thompson. Therefore, if there is some linear character of order
p
, then either
v_{1}\equiv 0
and
v_{2}\equiv 1\pmod{p}
or
v_{1}\equiv 1
and
v_{2}\equiv 0\pmod{p}
by Theorem
3.13
. If
v_{1}\equiv 0
and
v_{2}\equiv 1
, then
q\equiv 0
, forcing
v_{1}\equiv 1
, and thus
p=1
. If
v_{1}\equiv 1
and
v_{2}\equiv 0
, then
q\equiv-1
, so
v_{1}\equiv(-1)^{3}-(-1)^{2}+1\equiv-1
, forcing
-1\equiv 1
and thus
p=2
. Therefore,
2|q+1
, and so
q
is odd, a contradiction. Therefore, no such
G
exists.
∎

Proposition 3.19
.

If there exists a
(4w^{2}-1,2w^{2},w^{2},w^{2})
-PDS
D
in a group
G
, then
w\equiv 1\pmod{3}
, all linear characters of
G
evaluate to
-w
on
D
, every conjugacy class intersection with
D
is a multiple of
w
, and the order of the group of linear characters is a power of
3
.

Proof.

Note that
4w^{2}-1
is odd, so
G
is a solvable group. Moreover,
\sqrt{\Delta}=2w
,
\theta_{1}=w
, and
\theta_{2}=-w
, so
v_{1}=2w-1
and
v_{2}=2w+1
. Since
G
is solvable, it has a nontrivial group of linear characters, so let
p
be a prime dividing the order of this group. Then, by Theorem
3.13
, either
v_{1}\equiv 1\pmod{p}
and
v_{2}\equiv 0\pmod{p}
, or
v_{1}\equiv 0\pmod{p}
and
v_{2}\equiv 1\pmod{p}
. After some algebraic manipulation, it is clear that
v_{1}\equiv 0\pmod{p}
and
v_{2}\equiv 1\pmod{p}
implies
p=1
, and that
v_{1}\equiv 1\pmod{p}
and
v_{2}\equiv 0\pmod{p}
implies
p=3
. Therefore,
3
divides
2w+1
, implying
w\equiv 1\pmod{3}
. Additionally, by Theorem
3.13
, the linear group of characters must be a power of
3
.

Now, let
a\in G
be an element of order divisible by
3
such that
a\not\in[G,G]
. Such an element must exist, for
3
divides
|\mathcal{L}|
. In addition, let
\xi\in\mathcal{L}
. Then, by Theorem
3.11
,
|C_{G}(a)||a^{G}\cap D|=k-\xi(D)
. By Lemma
3.2
,
\xi(D)=\pm w
. Set
w=3m+1
for some
m\in\mathbb{Z}
. Therefore,

|C_{G}(a)|=\frac{k-(\pm w)}{|a^{G}\cap D|}=\frac{2w^{2}-(\pm w)}{|a^{G}\cap D|}=w\cdot\frac{2w-(\pm 1)}{|a^{G}\cap D|}=w\cdot\frac{6m+2-(\pm 1)}{|a^{G}\cap D|}.

Since
a
has order divisible by
3
, then
3
divides
|C_{G}(a)|
, so
6m+2-(\pm 1)\equiv 0\pmod{3}
. Since
w\neq 0\pmod{3}
, then
2-(\pm 1)\equiv 0\pmod{3}
. It follows that
\xi(D)=-w
, so that
2-(\pm 1)
equals
3
.
∎

4.
Computational methods

In the previous section we established certain theoretical results on the characters of groups containing a partial difference set. We now develop related techniques to construct partial difference sets inside of a group using the group characters. In particular, for a partial difference set
D
inside of a group
G
with
p^{\ell}
dividing
\sqrt{\Delta}
, we develop techniques to give the value of
|D\cap h^{G}|\pmod{p^{\ell}}
for particular primes
p
, and calculate
|D\cap h^{G}|
by extension.

These methods, combined with Theorem
3.11
, makes it rather quick to search for groups which could contain a PDS just by utilizing the irreducible characters of the group. In some cases, simply knowing the values
|D\cap h^{G}|\pmod{p^{\ell}}
can rule out the existence of a PDS for a certain parameter sets inside of a nonabelian group, such as in Example
4.2
. In other cases, targeted information about
|D\cap h^{G}|
can be used to quickly construct partial difference sets even inside of groups with up to
1000
elements, using the search method described in
[
2
]
.

4.1.
Modular computation of conjugacy class intersection sizes for PDSs

Now, as before, let
G
be a group containing a
(v,k,\lambda,\mu)
-PDS
D
with eigenvalues
\theta_{1}=\frac{1}{2}(\lambda-\mu+\sqrt{\Delta})
and
\theta_{2}=\frac{1}{2}(\lambda-\mu-\sqrt{\Delta})
. Let
\mathfrak{F}
be an irreducible
G
representation, and let
\chi\in{\rm Irr}(G)
be afforded by
\mathfrak{F}
. Denote the eigenspace of
\mathfrak{F}(D)
corresponding to
\theta_{i}
by
V_{\mathfrak{F}}(\theta_{i})
. For
p
dividing
\sqrt{\Delta}
, construct the corresponding ring
\mathfrak{R}
given by Definition
2.3
. Let
\ell
be maximal such that
p^{\ell}
divides
\sqrt{\Delta}
, and let
(\pi)=\mathfrak{p}
be the unique maximal ideal containing
p
. Then,
p^{\ell}\in(\pi^{\ell})
, so set
R=\mathfrak{R}/(\pi^{\ell})
. Then, we have the following. (Compare to
[
32
, Lemma 3.1]
.)

Lemma 4.1
.

For
h\in G
such that
h\neq 1
, suppose that
p^{\ell}
does not divide
|C_{G}(h)|
. Then, over the ring
R
,

|h^{G}\cap D||C_{G}(h)|\equiv k-\theta_{2}\pmod{(\pi^{\ell})},

and if
h=1
, then

|h^{G}\cap D||C_{G}(h)|\equiv k+\theta_{2}(|G|-1)\pmod{(\pi^{\ell})}.

In particular,

|h^{G}\cap D||C_{G}(h)|\equiv k-\theta_{2}\pmod{p^{\ell}},

and if
h=1
, then

|h^{G}\cap D||C_{G}(h)|\equiv k+\theta_{2}(|G|-1)\pmod{p^{\ell}}.

Proof.

First, for a character
\chi
afforded by a representation
\mathfrak{F}
,
\chi(1)=\dim V_{\mathfrak{F}}(\theta_{1})+\dim V_{\mathfrak{F}}(\theta_{2})
. Moreover, for
p^{\ell}
a prime power dividing
\sqrt{\Delta}
,
\theta_{1}\equiv\theta_{2}\pmod{p^{\ell}}
. Thus,
\overline{\chi(D)}\equiv\dim V_{\mathfrak{F}}(\theta_{1})\theta_{1}+\dim V_{\mathfrak{F}}(\theta_{2})\theta_{2}\equiv(\dim V_{\mathfrak{F}}(\theta_{1})+\dim V_{\mathfrak{F}}(\theta_{2}))\theta_{2}\equiv\chi(1)\theta_{2}\pmod{p^{\ell}}
. It follows that

\Phi\equiv k-\theta_{2}+\sum_{\chi\in{\rm Irr}(G)}(\chi(1)\theta_{2})\chi\pmod{(\pi^{\ell})}.

Then, when
h\neq 1
,

\Phi(h)\equiv k-\theta_{2}+\theta_{2}\sum_{\chi\in{\rm Irr}(G)}\chi(1)\chi(h)\equiv k-\theta_{2}\pmod{(\pi^{\ell})}

by column orthogonality of the characters, and when
h=1
, then

\Phi(1)\equiv k-\theta_{2}+\theta_{2}\sum_{\chi\in{\rm Irr}(G)}\chi(1)^{2}\equiv k-\theta_{2}+\theta_{2}|G|\equiv k+\theta_{2}(|G|-1)\pmod{(\pi^{\ell})}.

The full equality is given by the definition
\Phi(h)=|h^{G}\cap D||C_{G}(h)|
. Finally, the last equations follow since
|h^{G}\cap D||C_{G}(h)|
is an integer.
∎

Occasionally, Lemma
4.1
can be used to establish whether or not a PDS exists on a certain parameter set more or less by hand, occasionally aided by minor computer calculations.

Example 4.2

There does not exist an invertible

Let

.

Let

4.3.
PDSs constructed using Algorithm

For a number of groups, we used this method to calculate all possible sizes of intersections of the conjugacy classes of the group with a possible PDS inside this group. We then ran a restricted search for PDSs satisfying the class intersection restrictions using a modified Random Restart Hill Climb search described in
[
2
]
. Using the technique from
[
2
]
, we were able to construct nineteen distinct PDSs in nonabelian groups, many of which were already explicitly recognized, such as PDSs arising from triangular graphs. We found three parameter sets,
(111,44,19,16)
,
(305,76,27,16)
, and
(981,140,43,16)
, which correspond to an infinite family of groups acting regularly on Steiner systems
{\mathrm{S}}(2,4,p^{d})
for
p
prime. Five others that we were able to construct computationally – in SmallGroup(57, 1), SmallGroup(155, 1), SmallGroup(301, 1), SmallGroup(737, 1), and SmallGroup(1027, 1) – have parameters corresponding to an infinite family of PDSs corresponding to Steiner triple systems. The infinite families on
{\mathrm{S}}(2,4,p)
and
{\mathrm{S}}(2,3,p^{d})
correspond to block-regular Steiner
2
-design constructions given by Clapham in
[
6
]
and Wilson in
[

]
. Finally, we found a
(183,70,29,25)
-PDS in SmallGroup(183, 1) which is related to a
{\mathrm{S}}(2,5,61)
Steiner system and, as it turns out, is also part of an infinite family. To the authors knowledge, the PDSs arising from
[

have not previously been recognized in the literature, and we are only aware of one paper
[

recognizing the constructions given by Clapham

Proposition 4.9
.

Let

By the results of Clapham

∎

Remark 4.10
.

Clapham enumerated the number of isomorphism classes of such block-regular Steiner triple systems for each prime power

(see
[

). It is well known that two Steiner triple systems are isomorphic if and only if their block-intersection graphs are isomorphic (see, e.g.,

In fact, Wilson

is sufficiently large in relation to the size

Theorem 4.11

Let

4.4.
Non-modular techniques

The modular techniques are useful for a number of groups and parameter sets. Nevertheless, there exists a large class of parameters
(v,k,\lambda,\mu)
for which every prime dividing
\sqrt{\Delta}
divides
v
. (For example, every PDS in an abelian group has such parameters.) In these cases, we frequently cannot guarantee that there is a single prime dividing
\sqrt{\Delta}
which does not divide
|C_{G}(h)|
for most of the elements
h\in G
. As a result, we may only compute the modular values of some of the intersections
|h^{G}\cap D|
, and it can be quite computationally intensive to guess and check all possibilities for the remaining
|h^{G}\cap D|
.

Here we provide an example computation, which shows that even if
\sqrt{\Delta}
divides
v
, it is still possible to rule out the existence of a PDS for a particular group
G
just through analyses of the character table of
G
and the possible corresponding
\Phi
. Although the modular techniques of this paper do not apply in this setting, the results of Subsection
3.1
still do. Note that we are mandating that the difference sets in the following example are reversible; by doing so, we do not need to analyze concrete representations of our group
G
such as in, e.g.,
[
23
,
29
]
.

Example 4.14
.

We will prove that there is no reversible
(144,66,30)
-DS
D
in SmallGroup(144, 68). In this case,
\sqrt{\Delta}=12
, where
12^{2}=144
. Therefore, Theorem
3.8
, Theorem
3.11
, Theorem
3.13
, and Algorithm
CCI
are all inapplicable.

Examining the character table for
G
(which can be generated, for example, using GAP
[
15
]
), there are six conjugacy classes of noncentral elements of order
3
. Each of these conjugacy classes has size
16
, so each element in one of these classes has a centralizer of order
9
. Thus, for any noncentral element
x
of order
3
in
G
, we have

\Phi(x)=9|x^{G}\cap D|\equiv 0\pmod{9}.

Moreover, if
\mathcal{L}
denotes the set of linear characters for
G
, then
\chi\notin\mathcal{L}
implies
\chi(x)=0
. Note that
|\mathcal{L}|=9
, and each nonprincipal character has order
3
; other than the principal character, there are four conjugate pairs
\{\xi_{i},\overline{\xi_{i}}\},

1\leqslant i\leqslant 4
. Extracting the principal character from this sum and noting that
D
is reversible (so
\overline{\chi(D)}=\chi(D)
by Lemma
3.4
(i)), we have

\Phi(x)=66+\sum_{i=1}^{4}\xi_{i}(D)(\xi_{i}(x)+\overline{\xi_{i}}(x)).

Since each
\xi_{i}(D)
is
\pm 6
by Lemma
3.2
, there are only
2^{4}=16
possibilities in total. Checking which have
\Phi(x)
divisible by
9
, there are only
5
possibilities that work. (In each case,
\Phi(x)=36
or
\Phi(x)=72
.)

Of the
15
remaining nonlinear characters, there is one that equals its conjugate and seven conjugate pairs. Let
\chi
be a nonlinear irreducible character. Note that
\theta_{1}=6
and
\theta_{2}=-6
. By Lemma
3.2
, if
\mathfrak{F}
is the representation affording
\chi
, then
\chi(D)=6(\dim V_{\mathfrak{F}}(6)-\dim V_{\mathfrak{F}}(-6))
, where
-\chi(1)\leqslant(\dim V_{\mathfrak{F}}(6)-\dim V_{\mathfrak{F}}(-6))\leqslant\chi(1)
. So, we may conclude that
\chi(D)\in\{-18,-6,6,18\}
.

Thus, there are
4^{8}
possibilities for the coefficients
\chi(D)
of
\Phi
(
\chi
nonlinear), and, from what we have ascertained about the linear characters, there are
5\cdot 4^{8}
possibilities for
\Phi
.

Since either
1\in D
or
1\notin D
, we have
\Phi(1)\in\{0,144\}
. Of the
5\cdot 4^{8}
possibilities for
\Phi
, a GAP
[
15
]
calculation shows that only
15820
of these possibilites have
\Phi(1)\in\{0,144\}
.

Now,
Z(G)=\langle z\rangle
is cyclic of order
3
. Again, since
D
is reversible, either
\{z,z^{-1}\}\subseteq D
or
\{z,z^{-1}\}\cap D=\varnothing
. Thus, either

\Phi(z)=\Phi(z^{-1})=0

or

\Phi(z)=\Phi(z^{-1})=144.

None of the remaining possibilities for
\Phi
satisfy either of these constraints (in fact, all the values of these class functions are
6\pmod{12}
when evaluated at
z
), and so no such
\Phi
can exist. Therefore, there is no reversible
(144,66,30)
-DS (and hence no regular
(144,66,30,30)
-PDS) in
G
.
