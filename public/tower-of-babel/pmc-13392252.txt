Two-hourly resolved microbial and viral dynamics in the subtropical Daya Bay
Authors: Songze Chen, Shuaishuai Xu, Muhammad Zain UL Arifeen, Xiaomeng Wang, Kangli Guo, Jianchang Tao, Minxu Li, Hao Wang, Chuanlun Zhang, Shengwei Hou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Planktonic microbial and viral communities are fundamental drivers of biogeochemical cycling and energy flow in marine ecosystems. These communities display substantial variability in their composition at daily to sub-daily scales, which cannot be captured by conventional low-frequency monthly or weekly sampling. To reveal these high-resolution dynamics, we performed a time-series sampling of planktonic microbial and viral communities in the subtropical Daya Bay at 2-hour intervals over 3 days. Seawater samples were subjected to metagenomic and metatranscriptomic sequencing for the cellular size fraction (>0.2 μm) and metagenomic sequencing for the viral size fraction (0.02–0.2 μm). This approach enabled us to capture fine-scale temporal variations in the genomic composition and transcriptional activities of microbial and viral communities. The resulting comprehensive dataset, including 700 metagenome-assembled genomes (MAGs) and 118,242 viral operational taxonomic units (vOTUs), provides a valuable resource for investigating the metabolic potentials and dynamic interactions within natural planktonic microbial-viral assemblages in subtropical bay ecosystems, offering insights into their ecological roles that are inaccessible through low-temporal-resolution sampling.

Background & Summary

Daya Bay, located in the northeastern South China Sea, is a large, semi-enclosed bay of significant ecological and economic importance1,2. This bay connects to the deep seawater of the South China Sea while experiencing substantial anthropogenic disturbances from recreational activities and aquaculture3–5. It is also exposed to elevated nutrient loadings from the Pearl River estuary and underground water discharge, which exert selective pressure on the natural microbial and viral communities, favoring assemblages with locally adapted traits6,7. Although several ecological investigations on the microbiota of Daya Bay have been conducted8, a high-resolution time-series study that could capture the temporal dynamics of both microbial-viral composition and transcriptional activities at sub-daily scales has not been previously implemented. In this study, we collected surface seawater samples every two hours for three days (October 28–31, 2021) at three adjacent sites (A to C), each spaced 500 meters apart, in Daya Bay to investigate the temporal dynamics of marine planktonic microbial and viral communities (Fig. 1; Table S1). Here, we explicitly define the component retained by the 0.2 μm pore-sized polycarbonate membrane as the “cellular fraction”, encompassing cellular microorganisms, large cell debris, associated virions or intracellular viruses. And the “viral fraction” was operationally defined as the component passing through the 0.2 μm pore-sized membrane but retained by a 0.02 μm pore-sized anodisc aluminum oxide membrane, encompassing mainly viral particles, small cell debris, and macromolecules. Using metagenomic, metatranscriptomic and viromic sequencing (Fig. 2), we examined the microbial and viral genomic composition and activities along the time series. In total, 700 non-redundant prokaryotic metagenome-assembled genomes (MAGs) were reconstructed with the minimum quality criteria of ≥50% genome completeness and <10% contamination, comprising 661 bacterial and 39 archaeal MAGs (Fig. 3; Table S2). Among them, 151 MAGs exhibited >90% completeness, and 219 showed completeness in the range of 75% to 90%. Bacterial genomes were predominantly affiliated with the phyla Pseudomonadota, Bacteroidota, Planctomycetota, Verrucomicrobiota, and Myxococcota, while archaeal genomes were exclusively assigned to Thermoplasmatota. Metagenomic analysis of both cellular and viral size fractions identified 118,242 representative viral Operational Taxonomic Units (vOTUs) from predicted viral contigs (Fig. 4; Table S3). Taxonomic classification assigned only 11.2% (13,244/118,242) of these vOTUs to known viral families or genera, with the remaining 88.8% unclassified at the family level. Notably, 84.8% of all vOTUs (100,311 dominant members) belonged to tailed viruses (class Caudoviricetes), none of which received genus-level taxonomic annotations. Among taxonomically resolved families, Kyanoviridae dominated (32.7%, 4,335 vOTUs), followed by Phycodnaviridae (15.7%, 2,079 vOTUs) and Autographiviridae (13.4%, 1,779 vOTUs), whereas other families (e.g., Mimiviridae, Poxviridae) exhibited lower relative abundances (Fig. 4; Table S3). For computationally predicted host associations, 20.3% of vOTUs (24,068) were assigned to hosts at the phylum level, among which Pseudomonadota was the most prevalent host phylum (41.8%, 10,058 vOTUs), followed by Bacteroidota (30.7%, 7,389 vOTUs). This taxonomic pattern reveals the distinctive viral community structure and its strong linkage to Pseudomonadota and Bacteroidota hosts within the investigated ecosystem. Methods

Sample collection and physicochemical analyses

A time-series of 97 surface seawater (~1 m depth) samples was collected over 3 consecutive days (28 to 31 October 2021) from 3 spatially adjacent stations (A: 22.6604328 N 114.5221549E, B: 22.6634648 N 114.5257571E, and C: 22.6590591 N 114.5266852E, each 500 m apart) in the subtropical Daya Bay (Fig. 1). High-frequency sampling was implemented at 2-hour intervals using 3 autonomous unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs; Egretta QC EMC50 Pro, Customized, China) to pump surface seawater into sterile containers. An additional UAV equipped with a Multianna sensor (Shenzhen Lightsun Technology, China) was used for concurrent hydrographic profiling. After seawater collection, 1 liter (L) of sample from each station was vacuum-filtered onto 0.2 μm polycarbonate membranes (GTTP04700, Millipore) to enrich cellular microorganisms. The harvested membranes were immediately transferred into disposable sterile centrifuge tubes, preserved in liquid nitrogen, and then transported to the laboratory for long-term storage at −80 °C freezers. Subsequently, the seawater filtrate from Station A was further vacuum-filtered through 0.02 μm anodisc aluminum oxide membranes (Sterlitech 1360012) for selective viral particle enrichment. These viral fraction membranes were placed in disposable sterile Petri dishes, wrapped with parafilm, transported to the laboratory on dry ice, and then stored at −80 °C freezers. Viral analysis was restricted to Station A due to the extensive filtration time and high costs associated with sample processing. To mitigate potential cross-contamination, water sampling bottles and filtration modules were thoroughly rinsed three times with distilled water between consecutive sampling events. For quality control, field blanks were prepared by filtering distilled water, using the same sampling and storage protocols as for the experimental samples. No detectable DNA or RNA was recovered from these blank samples for sequencing, confirming the absence of cross-contamination during the sampling process. Filtrates passing through the 0.2 μm polycarbonate membranes were stored at −20 °C in the dark for subsequent nutrient analysis. Dissolved nutrients (NH₄+, NO₃−, NO₂−, PO₄³−, and SiO₃²−) were quantified using an automated discrete analyzer (CleverChem 380Plus, DeChem-Tech, Hamburg, Germany) following standard seawater colorimetric methods: NH₄+ by indophenol blue; NO₃− by cadmium reduction–diazotization; NO₂− by direct diazotization; PO₄³− by phosphomolybdenum blue; and SiO₃²− by silicomolybdenum blue as described by the WOCE protocols9. Detailed methodology and 16S rDNA amplicon sequencing data are available in Chen et al.8.

DNA and RNA extraction, and sequencing

The 97 filtered membranes derived from the cellular fractions were divided into aliquots for DNA and RNA co-extraction and purification, following validated protocols established by Tournier et al. (2015) and Chen et al.8,10. DNA extraction was performed using the FastDNA SPIN Kit for Soil (MP Biomedical, OH, USA). Subsequently, the filtrate retained in the collection tube was purified for RNA recovery. To eliminate residual DNA contamination, the crude RNA extract was treated with recombinant DNase I (RNase-free, TaKaRa 2270B) and further purified using the RNeasy MinElute Cleanup Kit (QIAGEN 74204). The efficacy of DNA removal was validated through qPCR analysis employing the bacterial 16S rRNA gene-specific primer pair: Bac331F (5′-TCCTACGGGAGGCAGCAGT-3′) and Bac797R (5′-GGACTACCAGGGTATCTAATCCTGTT-3′)11 on QuantStudioTM 5 Real-Time PCR Instrument (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc., Waltham, MA, United States). The thermal cycling protocol was as follows: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 5 s (denaturation), 55 °C for 30 s (annealing), and 72 °C for 60 s (extension). The rRNA depletion was performed only in silico using SortMeRNA with SILVA and RFAM databases; no experimental depletion was employed to avoid RNA loss from low-yield marine samples. Prior to complementary DNA (cDNA) synthesis, total RNA from each sample was quantified and quality-evaluated using the Qubit RNA High-Sensitivity (HS) Assay Kit on a Qubit 4 fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). To ensure consistency and comparability across all metatranscriptomic libraries, a fixed input of 500 ng of purified total RNA was used for cDNA synthesis per sample. This standardized RNA input guarantees that variations in downstream sequencing output and transcript abundance reflect genuine biological differences rather than technical discrepancies in starting material quantity. Then, cDNA was generated from total RNA using the PrimeScript Double Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit with random hexamer primers (TaKaRa 6111 A). This cDNA was used as the input for metatranscriptomic library preparation. Viral DNA was extracted from the 31 filtered membranes of the viral-size fraction using the FastDNA SPIN Kit for Soil (MP Biomedicals, Santa Ana, CA, USA). Prior to sequencing, metagenomic and metatranscriptomic libraries were subjected to quality control, including library concentration quantification (≥0.5 ng/μL dsDNA) with Qubit double stranded DNA high-sensitivity assay kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc., Waltham, MA, USA) using a Qubit 4 fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc., Waltham, MA, United States) and target fragment length verification via agarose gel electrophoresis (distinct ~1500/3000 bp bands for cDNA and ~20,000 bp bands for DNA). Only qualified libraries were sequenced. For metagenomic sequencing, both cellular and viral DNA libraries were prepared with the MGIEasy Fast DNA Fragmentation Library Prep Kit V2.0 following the standard protocol, and sequenced on the DNBSEQ-T5 platform (BGI-Shenzhen, BGI-Qingdao) to generate 2 × 150 bp paired-end reads. For metatranscriptomic analysis, cDNA libraries from the cellular fraction were constructed using the ALFA-SEQ DNA Library Prep Kit (FINDROP, Guangzhou, China) according to the standard workflow and sequenced on the Illumina HiSeq 2000 platform (Magigene Bioinformatics Technology Co., Ltd., Guangzhou, China) with the 2 × 150 bp paired-end configuration. In total, the cellular fraction generated 13.2 terabytes (TB) of raw metagenomic data (97 samples) and 4.7 TB of raw metatranscriptomic data (35 samples), while the viral fraction produced 4.0 TB of raw viral metagenomic data (31 samples).

Sequence quality control and assembly

Raw sequencing reads from metagenomic, metatranscriptomic, and viral libraries were quality-controlled by initial adapter trimming and low-quality filtering using fastp v0.19.5 with default parameters12. Human contaminant sequences were subsequently identified and removed using BBMap v38.92 (minid = 0.95, maxindel = 3, bwr = 0.16, bw = 12, quickmatch, fast) against the hg19_main_mask_ribo_animal_allplant_allfungus reference database (http://sourceforge.net/projects/bbmap). High-quality metagenomic reads were assembled using SPAdes v3.15.4 (--k-list 21,33,55,77,99,127)13,14. Additionally, co-assemblies were performed for each sampling station using MEGAHIT (--k-list 27,33,55,77,99,127)15,16. The contig continuity was subsequently evaluated using QUAST (v5.0.2)17, and the results are presented in Table S4.

For metatranscriptomic analysis, ribosomal RNA (rRNA) sequences were depleted using SortMeRNA v4.3.6 (--paired_in --fastx --out2) with the following databases: SILVA (silva-arc-16s-database-id95.fasta, silva-bac-16s-database-id85.fasta, silva-euk-18s-database-id95.fasta, silva-arc-23s-database-id98.fasta, silva-bac-23s-database-id98.fasta, silva-euk-28s-database-id98.fasta) and Rfam (rfam-5s-database-id98.fasta, rfam-5.8s-database-id98.fasta)18. The average number of clean reads after rRNA filtering was 11.8 M, accounting for ~3% of the raw reads.

MAGs generation, refinement, and analysis

Metagenomic contigs (≥1 kb) of the cellular fraction were binned using Metabat2 v2.12.1/MaxBin2 v2.2.4/Concoct v1.1.0 via BASALT (--more-sensitivity)19–22, Metabinner v1.4.4 (1000/2000 bp)23, Metacoag v1.124, SemiBin v1.5.1 (single_easy_bin, --self-supervised)25, and Vamb v4.1.0 (-m 2000; default, -l 24 -n 384 384, -l 40 -n 768 768) for individual samples26. Co-assemblies were binned using metaWRAP (Metabat2 v2.9.1/Concoct v0.4.0)27, Metacoag v1.124, SemiBin v1.5.125, and Vamb v4.1.026 with the same parameters used for individual samples. To obtain a comprehensive set of MAGs, we combined results from these different binning pipelines. MAGs were then purified using MDMcleaner v0.8.728, with genome quality validated via CheckM229, and subsequently dereplicated using dRep v3.4.030 with dual quality thresholds (-comp 50, -con 10) and 99% ANI clustering. Subsequently, their genomic characteristics and chimerism were further evaluated using prok-quality31, which integrates three software tools: GUNC (version 1.0.5; database version: v2.1)32, Barrnap (version 0.9; available at https://github.com/tseemann/barrnap), and tRNAscan-SE (version 2.0.6)33. The final MAG catalog strictly complies with the Minimum Information about a Metagenome-Assembled Genome (MIMAG) standards (The Genome Standards Consortium)34, retaining only 700 medium- to high-quality MAGs (completeness ≥50%, contamination <10%). Taxonomic profiling was performed using GTDB-Tk v2.4.0 (https://github.com/Ecogenomics/GtdbTk) with reference database release 22635. For abundance analysis, metagenomic and rRNA-depleted metatranscriptomic reads were mapped to quality-controlled MAGs using BWA v2.2.136, and RPKM (reads per kilobase contig per million mapped reads) values were calculated using CoverM v0.3.137 (--mapper bwa-mem -m RPKM --min-read-percent-identity 0.95 --min-read-aligned-percent 0.75). For phylogenomic analysis, a concatenated alignment of ribosomal marker genes was generated using markerfinder (-m ribo_rnap, -n, -c) from all MAGs38,39, followed by automated trimming with trimAl (-automated1) to remove less conserved residues40. Phylogenomic inference was performed using IQ-TREE v2.0.3 (-m LG + R10 -B 1000)41, and visualized using the Interactive Tree of Life (iTOL, https://itol.embl.de)42.

Viral metagenomic analysis

Viral contigs were assembled from both cellular and viral metagenomic libraries using MEGAHIT (k-list: 21,33,55,77,99,127)15,16. To refine the assembled viral sequences, we employed a multi-step process. First, short sequences (<5000 bp) were removed using SeqKit v2.11.0 to improve downstream analysis efficiency43, followed by initial deduplication via MMseqs2 (--min-seq-id 0.95 --cov-mode 1 -c 0.85) with 95% sequence identity over 85% coverage, to retain only cluster representative sequences44. Subsequently, viral sequences were predicted using two complementary tools: geNomad v1.11.1 (--min-score 0.8 --cleanup --splits 8) and VirSorter2 (--include-groups dsDNAphage,NCLDV,RNA,ssDNA,lavidaviridae --min-score 0.8 --provirus-off --max-orf-per-seq 20)45,46. For both tools, sequences were retained if they met either criterion: hallmarks >0 and viral_score ≥0.8, or hallmarks = 0 and viral_score ≥0.9. The predicted viral sequences from both tools were merged, duplicate sequences were removed using SeqKit v2.11.0 (seqkit rmdup --by-seq)43, and only sequences with ≥1 viral gene (validated via CheckV v1.0.1) were retained47. The remaining high-quality viral sequences were clustered into viral operational taxonomic units (vOTUs) using CD-HIT v4.8.1 (95% sequence identity, 85% coverage; -c 0.95 -aS 0.85 -T 0 -M 9000000 -d 0) to generate vOTU representative sequences48. Viral vOTUs were taxonomically annotated using geNomad v1.11.146, and their hosts were predicted using iPHoP v1.3.349. Finally, the abundance of each vOTU was estimated by mapping metagenomic and viral reads to the vOTU sequences using BWA v2.2.136, followed by RPKM calculation using CoverM v0.3.137 (--mapper bwa-mem -m RPKM --min-read-percent-identity 0.95 --min-read-aligned-percent 0.75). The overall data processing workflow is illustrated in Fig. 2.

Data Records

Raw sequencing reads generated in this study have been deposited in the NCBI Short Read Archive (SRA) under BioProject accession PRJNA94937750. This includes cellular (SRR23998739–SRR23998835) and viral (SRR32993520–SRR32993550) metagenomic libraries and metatranscriptomic libraries (SRR32119816–SRR32119850). Corresponding metadata and file names are available in Table S1. The assembled MAGs and vOTUs are publicly available on figshare51. The MAGs have also been deposited in the European Nucleotide Archive (ENA) under the BioProject accession number PRJEB10856452, with file names consistent with those listed in Table S2.

Technical Validation

To ensure the quality and integrity of both the MAG and viral datasets, rigorous validation pipelines integrating specialized tools and standardized criteria were implemented, as described in the Methods section. The integrated validation workflow for both datasets is illustrated in Fig. 2, prioritizing the removal of low-quality, redundant, or artifactual sequences and adherence to community-recognized standards to ensure both datasets meet general quality criteria. However, for specific research applications, we encourage users to adapt their validation approaches to further refine the MAGs or vOTUs.
