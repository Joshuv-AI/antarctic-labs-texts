Case Description

An 88-year-old man with prior bioprosthetic aortic valve replacement and chronic conduction disease (sinus bradycardia, right bundle branch block, and left anterior fascicular block) was referred for a persistent heart rate of 97 to 98 beats/min. Holter monitoring over several days showed an incessant rhythm with minimal beat-to-beat variability, absent discernible sinus P waves, and a wide QRS identical to baseline (Figure 1). Vitals: blood pressure 140/90 mm Hg, heart rate 98 beats/min, temperature 36.1 °C, respiratory rate 16/min, and oxygen saturation 99% on room air. Exam: scattered basilar crackles and 1+ lower-extremity edema with normal jugular venous pressure and a soft 1/6 systolic ejection murmur. Transthoracic echocardiography revealed new left ventricular systolic dysfunction (left ventricular ejection fraction 35%-40%; previously normal 6 months earlier).

Discussion

Rate invariance, rather than absolute rate, can signal incessant reentrant supraventricular tachycardia. A junctional-appearing rhythm without visible P waves and a wide QRS identical to baseline cannot reliably distinguish atrioventricular (AV) junctional automaticity from AV nodal–dependent reentry when retrograde atrial activation is buried within the QRS.1 This distinction matters when the rhythm is incessant and ventricular function is declining because arrhythmia-induced cardiomyopathy is reversible once the culprit rhythm is eliminated.2

Given new left ventricular dysfunction, observation alone risked progression. Beta-blocker escalation was constrained by baseline bradycardia and bifascicular block, and amiodarone is not first-line for stable supraventricular tachycardia of unclear mechanism. Adenosine was considered, but electrophysiology study offered definitive diagnosis and curative ablation in a single procedure.

During electrophysiology study, the clinical rhythm was induced by atrial premature beats and required critical atrial-His (AH) prolongation, arguing against a purely automatic junctional tachycardia. Short ventriculoatrial (VA) (<70 ms) with earliest retrograde atrial activation at the His region and a ventricle-atrium-His-ventricle (VAHV) response to ventricular entrainment supported typical (slow-fast) AV nodal re-entry tachycardia.1 His-refractory premature atrial complexes advanced the next atrial electrogram, confirming AV nodal–dependent reentry and excluding junctional automaticity.3 Slow-pathway ablation rendered the arrhythmia noninducible; ambulatory monitoring showed no recurrence, and left ventricular ejection fraction normalized at 3 months.
