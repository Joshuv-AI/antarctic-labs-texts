Perceived usefulness of new technologies in palliative care volunteering: mixed-methods study with stakeholders
Authors: Pilar Barnestein-Fonseca, Eva Víbora-Martín, Inmaculada Ruiz-Torreras, Rafael Gómez-García, María Luisa Martín-Roselló
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDuring the COVID-19 pandemic, face-to-face volunteer support for patients and families was not possible, making it necessary to explore alternative ways of reducing distress. New technologies emerged as a valuable resource, facilitating communication and information exchange, and supporting volunteer tasks.ObjectiveTo explore the perceived usefulness of new technologies in volunteering among different stakeholders (patients, relatives, professionals, and volunteers), and to examine how these perceptions relate to participants’ technological profiles.DesignA cross-sectional mixed-methods study was conducted to explore attitudes and preferences toward new technologies. Quantitative data were analyzed descriptively and through regression models, while qualitative data were examined using thematic analysis.MethodsParticipants were recruited through consecutive non-probabilistic sampling and included patients, relatives, healthcare professionals, and volunteers from various care settings. Quantitative measures assessed perceptions of usefulness, benefits, barriers, and satisfaction with volunteering, alongside the TechPH tool to profile technological attitudes. Qualitative data were collected through interviews and focus groups using open-ended questions to explore the perceived usefulness of new technologies in palliative care volunteering. Quantitative analysis involved descriptive statistics, Pearson correlations, ANOVA, and multiple linear regression. Qualitative data were analyzed using thematic analysis.ResultsA total of 402 individuals participated: 50 patients, 45 relatives, 136 professionals and 171 volunteers. Perceived usefulness of new technologies varied: 50% of patients, 63.6% of relatives, 77.8% of professionals, and 78.2% of volunteers found them beneficial. Three themes emerged from qualitative analysis: difficulties in new technologies use (mainly among patients), perceived benefits (e.g., enhanced communication), and the need for volunteer training in digital skills.ConclusionsAll stakeholder groups recognized new technologies as useful for volunteer support in palliative care, with the highest perceived usefulness among professionals and volunteers. However, professionals were the least involved in volunteer support. Patients reported the lowest acceptance, preferring a hybrid model in which technology complements, but does not replace, in-person support.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12904-025-01968-z.

Introduction

Palliative care (PC) is characterised by holistic care that addresses the physical, psychological, social, and spiritual dimensions of the person [1]. To achieve this, care from a multidisciplinary team—including professionals in medicine, nursing, psychology, social work, physiotherapy, occupational therapy, spiritual counselling, and volunteers—is essential. While specialist PC teams are central to the delivery of hospice and PC services, volunteers also make a substantial contribution to safe and high-quality care worldwide [2].

Volunteers support a wide range of activities in PC settings, including inpatient units, hospital-based teams, home care and community services [3, 4]. Trained volunteers accompany and support patients and families, often providing psychosocial support such as companionship, counselling, spiritual care, and referrals [5, 6]. Volunteers thus complement professional care, occupying a unique position between health care professionals, family and patients [3, 7, 8]. Evidence shows that volunteering improves quality of life and emotional well-being, reducing patients’ feelings of loneliness, isolation, and depression [4, 8–13]. Some studies even suggest that volunteer support and larger social networks may reduce mortality. For example, Herbest-Damm and Kulik [14] found that hospice patients visited by volunteers survived on average 80 days longer than those without volunteer visits, largely due to improved emotional well-being and reduced caregiver burden. Other studies show that volunteer support increases satisfaction with care and may reduce the risk of complicated grief among bereaved relatives. Furthermore, another study by Block et al. [15] also found that patients who benefit from the support of a volunteer have increased satisfaction with the service received and the organisation, which avoids complicated bereavements in the future for family members. Family members have to adapt their lives to new situation that occurs in the family nucleus, and which will alter it structurally and functionally, and may also cause certain difficulties in making professional and personal habits of different members caring for patient more flexible. Therefore, family also needs physical, emotional and instrumental care to offer them necessary support for these moments, and this also implies the support of volunteers. The role that volunteers play at the end of life also has benefits for caregivers, including respite care, emotional support, social support, practical assistance, and spiritual support if desired [10].

PC services are crucial for supporting patients and families during the profoundly challenging and emotionally distressing end-of-life period. It is therefore essential to ensure the availability of all necessary resources to facilitate this process. However, during the COVID-19 pandemic, in-person support for patients and families was often not feasible [16], As a result, alternative approaches were required to mitigate the distress caused by the pandemic, including fears of infection and the avoidance of professional visits. Tragically, many patients died in isolation due to hospital visitation restrictions, which exacerbated the grief experienced by bereaved relatives who were unable to be present or bid farewell to their loved ones [17].

In the specific case of this article, we will focus specifically on information and communication technologies, as they are the ones that can support us the most in the different tasks of a volunteer, as they facilitate communication and information exchange through tools such as the internet, mobile phones, social networks, etc [18]. The use of new technologies (NT), in recent years has been spreading to the entire population and in all spheres of daily life, representing an advance because it facilitates access to resources, tools and knowledge immediately and instantaneously, even facilitating the development of skills and being able to explore new realities [19]. In the area of health, all kinds of NT have been used, from the most commonly used in daily life (smartphones, tablets, computers) to the most advanced ones such as robots or genomic surgery devices to solve health problems and improve the quality of life of patients [20]. The outbreak of the COVID-19 pandemic forced many areas that had not yet considered incorporating NTs to do so in order to alleviate some of these dramatic situations. For example, some initiatives were carried out by medical teams on a voluntary basis with the aim of being able to inform relatives of their loved one’s condition and to be in contact with them virtually for a while [21] or some case studies evaluating the application of telehealth services in palliative home care during the pandemic [22–24]. In the area of volunteering, studies that relate accompaniment and NT mainly address the challenges of bringing this type of device to the elderly population and the digital gap but from the perspective of a volunteer who performs administrative tasks or to better organise with colleagues and the volunteer department [25, 26] or how volunteers perceive it as another resource to improve their relationship with family members, never perceiving it as useful for accompanying the patient [27], but still entities are far from including such resources in their practice. Although previous bibliography refers to digital volunteering, it tends to be of a more administrative nature [25, 26], so there are no studies that show digital accompaniment services.

Taking advantage of the fact that in recent years society can use NT as another tool that could allow volunteers to carry out new activities and communicate more quickly, comfortably and efficiently with patients and relatives, we wanted to evaluate, prior to the implementation of a technological serv for volunteers, what patients, relatives, volunteers and professionals think about its usefulness, positive and negative aspects. In our case, new technologies are only used as a communication channel to contact volunteers but not in accompaniment, and optional ways are always taken into account in case someone is not skilled enough to handle them. It is important that this study takes into account all the participants in the study, volunteers and patients/families, as they are going to establish communication between them but also the professional as the volunteer is part of the team and they have to be facilitators of the implementation of a new service. A collaborative approach to learning-by-doing and drawing on the skills and opinions of all stakeholders is fundamental to implementation research [28]. So the major aim of this study is to explore the perceived usefulness of NT for volunteering of all the stakeholders of this services. Also we analyse the relationship between the perceived usefulness and technological profile of the participants as NT are currently not used in volunteering.

Methodology

This study was nested within a cluster clinical trial (ITV-Pal study) previously described in detail by Barnestein-Fonseca et al. [29]. The ITV-PAL trial was approved by the Malaga Provincial Research Ethics Committee (25/02/2021). The overall aim of the project was to design, implement and evaluate a volunteer training programme in the use of NT (specifically smartphones and tablets) to support patients with life-limiting illness and their relatives.

The reporting of this study follows the ASSESS tool for mixed-methods implementation research [30].

Design

The best approach to evaluate the implementation process of complex intervention is the combination of quantitative and qualitative methods. Basic quantitative measures of implementation may be combined with semi-structured qualitative data to provide detailed understandings of intervention functioning on a small scale. Use quantitative methods to measure key process variables and allow testing of pre-hypothesised mechanisms of impact and contextual moderators. Use qualitative methods to capture emerging changes in implementation, experiences of the intervention and unanticipated or complex causal pathways, and to generate new theory.

Therefore, cross-sectional descriptive with mixed methods was used to analyze and identify the views of people involved in a volunteer service [31]. Cross-sectional descriptive design for quantitative variables and a thematic approach was adopted for qualitative data describing and explaining the perceived usefulness of NT for volunteering from the perspective of those who have experienced it (patients, relatives, HCP and volunteers).

Data collection

Data collection was conducted between July and December 2022, with both quantitative and qualitative data collected during this period.

Setting

Wards of palliative care in hospital, hospices and PC home teams.

The study was conducted in hospital palliative care units, a hospice, and home care teams. These settings represent the main models of PC delivery in Spain and ensured inclusion of all key stakeholders (patients, relatives, HCP, and volunteers). As all settings form part of the Spanish public health system, patients and families received free access to care. The diversity of settings also enhanced ecological validity by reflecting real-world PC contexts nationally.

Participants

Patients living with a life-limiting or terminal illness and their relatives. These patients could be included in any of the hospice services, either in home care or hospitalized in the hospice, HCP involved in PC and PC volunteers. All of them to be over 18 years of age. More than a year of experience in a specialized PC team in the case of HCP.

Recruitment

Patients and relatives were identified through the Cudeca Hospice Volunteer Department Database. Potential participants receiving volunteer support were contacted by telephone. If they agreed, an appointment was arranged to explain the study and obtain written informed consent, followed by interviews.

All Cudeca Hospice volunteers were invited via mailing lists by volunteer coordinators. Those who agreed received a detailed explanation of the study aims and provided informed consent prior to interviews.

HCPs were recruited through PC team across Spain (via symposia) and through Cudeca Hospice.

In all groups, non-probabilistic consecutive sampling was applied. Recruitment, data collection, and analysis were conducted in parallel. In the quantitative strand, mid-study analysis confirmed stability of results, allowing recruitment to be closed once sufficient data were obtained. In the qualitative strand, preliminary analyses were performed iteratively after each set of interviews or focus groups, and these analyses informed subsequent data collection until information saturation was reached [32]. The response rates were 83.3% for patients (50/60), 93.8% for relatives (45/48), 52.7% for healthcare professionals (136/258), and 54.5% for volunteers (171/314).

Variables

Sociodemographic variables were collected for all groups. These included age and gender; educational level and living situation (patients/relatives); specialty, years of professional experience, and volunteering background (HCPs); and years of collaboration with the organisation (volunteers).

Quantitative variables

For all groups, Likert-scale items (0–5) were used to assess the degree of agreement with the use of NT in volunteering, including its perceived usefulness, benefits, barriers, and the optimal frequency of use. The complete set of items administered to each group is presented in Annexes 1–4. In addition, Likert-scale items (0–5) were employed to evaluate satisfaction with the experience—specifically with the volunteers (patients, relatives, and healthcare professionals) or with volunteering itself (volunteers). A gradation has been made for the analysis of the answers in which answers 1–2 are disagree, 3 neither agree nor disagree and 4–5 agree.

Tecnological profile was measured by TechPH troughs two factor technology enthusiasm and technology anxiety [33] in all groups. The instrument consists of six items in two factors measuring techEnthusiasm and techAnxiety as factors of technophilia. It based on existing validated instruments and that it is short and simple to make it usable for older people. The scale comprises six items scored 1–5, where higher scores indicate greater technophilia.

Qualitative variables

We use the same questionnaire mentioned above with an open question format to further explore the perceived usefulness of the use of NT during PC volunteering. Also, structured interview schedule was generated based on previous literature and in accordance with the recommendations for descriptive thematic analysis. The questions focused on their experience as a care volunteer, how they think their volunteering would be most useful (fully face-to-face, blended or exclusively digital) and what circumstances, advantages and benefits of including NT in volunteering and difficulties. They were also asked about the training needed to carry out digital accompaniment and activities they feel able to carry out with the devices.

In addition, participants were invited to provide open-ended text answers about perceived benefits and barriers of using NT. Although these data were collected within the survey, their analysis followed a qualitative approach. A thematic categorisation was conducted to group responses into meaningful categories. This framework was developed inductively by two researchers through repeated reading of responses and iterative coding, and subsequently refined through consensus discussions within the research team. The final categories are presented in the Results section.

Quantitative data collection

For this study were used different ways for data collection depending on the group of participants and the propose of the data.

For all groups a survey with questions about the usefulness and acceptability of the use of NT for PC volunteering were used. This survey was carried out by a research team member for patients and relatives in person or by phone depending on the preferences of the participants. For volunteers and HCP, this survey was filled on-line.

Qualitative data collection

Participant of all of groups were invited to in face-to-face semi-structured interviews, in the case of patients and relatives, or focus groups with a semi-structured script for volunteers and HCP, to explore more in deep the perceived usefulness of NT for PC volunteering. The participant and researcher were present. The interviews were conducted by researcher EV (MSc/PhDc) who has previously been involved in other qualitative studies and was fully dedicated to this project. The interviews were audio-recorded and transcribed verbatim. Field notes were made during and after interviews by at least two members of research team.

Quantitative analysis

A descriptive statistical analysis was carried out for each of the study variables. The mean and standard deviations were calculated for the quantitative variables, while the absolute and relative frequencies were evaluated for the re-coded qualitative variables categorized. For open questions of the survey a categorization of the answer was carried out to facilitate the interpretation of the results. Correlation of Pearson for quantitative variables and ANOVA for qualitative variables were performed for bivariate analysis for perceived usefulness and the rest of variables. A multiple linear regression was performed with perceived usefulness as dependent variable and the statistically significant variables from bivariate analysis [34]. Saturation was reached in the quantitative results. A mid-study analysis was conducted to verify that the results did not change substantially (proportion and/or means) as the sample size increased; therefore, recruitment was considered complete.

Qualitative analysis

Thematic Analysis, with an iterative approach to coding and analysis. For example, what was said by one participant may inform or shed light on the words of another participant. The analysis focused on the “substance of the interview” to interpret the meanings and perceptions created and shared during the conversations. The data analysis explored the ideas, assumptions, or concepts underpinning participants’ accounts. Words or phrases were coded at both latent and semantic levels to label the meanings identified. Themes were then developed from these codes around a central observation or concept to reflect patterns of shared meaning. For example, this approach captured “something important” about how participants made sense of, and utilised, the NT within the context of end-of-life volunteering [35].

The analysis was conducted within a constructionist paradigm, which asserts that meaning and experience are socially produced. The stages of analysis were iterative and cyclical rather than linear, allowing movement back and forth between stages. The process followed these steps:

(A) Familiarisation and immersion with the data: repeated reading of the interviews;(B) Generation of initial codes: annotations of initial thoughts against items/categories of text;(C) Creating themes from codes: the minor items/categories initially identified from the text were interrogated to generate overarching themes. Engagement with relevant published literature (theoretical perspectives and related research) further enhanced the evolving interpretation;(D) Reviewing themes: ensuring that all collated extracts of text formed a coherent pattern, reorganising and refining themes as required;(E) Defining and naming themes: identifying the essence of each theme.

As themes were repeated, an iterative coding process was conducted to confirm saturation and through discussions among team members. Subsequent interviews confirmed that saturation had been reached by consistently reproducing the previously identified themes [36].

Mixed-methods analysis

Data integration was carried out during the interpretation phase, using a connection-based triangulation strategy, in which quantitative results guided the qualitative exploration and vice versa. Specifically, statistical findings were used to identify aspects that required deeper analysis, while qualitative insights helped contextualize and explain the differences observed between groups in the quantitative data.

This strategy not only allowed for cross-validation of findings from different sources but also revealed dimensions of the phenomenon that would not have been accessible through a single method. Thus, methodological triangulation added strength to the findings, promoting a more comprehensive and applied understanding of the perceived value of new technologies in the context of palliative care volunteering.

Perceived usefulness of NT for volunteering

The categorisation of open-ended survey responses was carried out inductively. Categories emerged from participants’ answers through iterative reading and coding by two researchers, and were refined through consensus discussions within the team. The final categories that resulted from this process are presented below.

In the case of the advantages of using NT in volunteering, participants’ responses could be categorised as follows: [1] no benefits: participants do not perceive any advantages of NT in volunteering [2], occasional use of NT: NT can be useful as an additional resource in exceptional situations where face-to-face volunteering is not possible, but it is not considered a regular or preferred mode of interaction [3], comfort: NT makes accompaniment easier and less demanding, as it avoids travel and saves time for both volunteers and families [4]., improved digital literacy after the pandemic: the COVID-19 pandemic increased familiarity with digital tools among the general population, making it easier to use NT in volunteering [5], less burden of face-to-face visits: for some patients and families, frequent in-person presence can be tiring or intrusive; NT provides a less disruptive alternative [6], geographical reach: NT enables volunteering to be offered in remote or rural areas where access to volunteers is otherwise limited [7], support for relatives: NT allows relatives to receive support without leaving the patient alone [8], another option: NT is perceived as a complementary resource that can be used alongside face-to-face volunteering, rather than as a substitute, to enrich and diversify the support provided [9], facilitating communication: NT improves interaction and understanding, especially for patients who experience communication difficulties due to illness or disability [10], coordination: NT enhances the organisation and communication of the volunteer department [11], avoiding language barriers: NT supports communication with patients or relatives who speak other languages (e.g., through translation tools) [12], training: NT facilitates easier and more flexible access to volunteer training [13], extended support: NT enables the volunteer service to last longer and provide more diverse resources to patients and [14] greater availability: NT makes it possible for volunteers to be available for accompaniment over longer periods of time.

In the case of the barriers to the use of NT in volunteering, participants’ responses could be categorised as follows: [1] none: participants do not identify barriers to the use of NT [2], lack of digital literacy: difficulties in understanding or managing NT [3], physical impairment: illness or age-related limitations (e.g., vision, hearing, motor difficulties) that prevent the use of technological devices [4], preference for face-to-face volunteering: some participants reject NT because they value in-person volunteering more highly [5], reluctance towards digital volunteering: resistance to the idea that volunteering could involve digital interaction [6], risk of overuse: the possibility that digital contact may lead to excessive demands or blurred boundaries in the relationship with volunteers [7], lack of human warmth: concern that NT may reduce emotional closeness and affective communication compared to face-to-face interactions.

Main results of perceived usefulness in the participants

HCP Health Care Professionals, CI95% Confidence Interval 95%

The patients surveyed were satisfied with the volunteer service with an average score of 8.70/10 (CI95% 8.42–8.98). 88% of patients (44/50) rating their volunteering experience as very good. Regarding NT and volunteering, 90% (45/50) preferred face to face volunteering (only in person) but also 54% (27/50) said that they could do some specific activity through NT but in a face to face accompaniment (in person + occasionally NT). More details are shown in the Fig. 1. About the usefulness, 50% considered NT useful for volunteering benefits and 46% (23/50) considered the NT as an advantage. The advantages they mostly noted were that a digital volunteer service could be more convenient (26%) and offer greater patient and family support (24%). Other benefits were: occasional use of NT as an additional resource (16%), face-to-face is very tiring so using NT is beneficial (4%) and this technological volunteering avoid the distance (2%).

62% of patients considered the use of NT during volunteering to be a barrier, mainly due to the lack of human warmth (40%). In addition, patients mentioned the lack of understanding of some digital resources (14%), physical impediments that made its use impossible (18%), and a direct preference for face-to-face volunteering (10%) as barriers to the use of NT.

Relatives surveyed were satisfied with the volunteer service with an average score of 7.60/10 (CI95% 7.18–8.02). 57.8% of relatives (26/45) reported their volunteering experience as very good. Regarding NT and volunteering, 75.5% (34/45) preferred face to face volunteering (only in person) and 60% (27/45) said that they could do some specific activity through NT but in a face to face accompaniment (in person + occasionally NT). More details are shown in the Fig. 1. About the usefulness, 62.2% (28/45) considered NT useful for volunteering benefits and the 62.2% (28/45) considered the NT as an advantage. The advantages most highlighted were the comfort provided by NT compared to face-to-face volunteering (20%) and the ability to provide more continuous support (37.8%). Other benefits were: occasional use of NT as an additional resource (6.7%) and this technological volunteering avoid the distance (6.7%).

60% (27/45) of relatives considered that the use of NT during volunteering would be a barrier mainly due to the lack of human warmth (33.3%) and the lack of comprehension of some digital resources (42.2%). In addition, the relatives mentioned physical impediments that make its use impossible (11.1%) as barrier to the use of NT.

HCP surveyed were satisfied with the volunteer service with an average score of 8.02/10 (CI95% 7.68–8.36). 61% of HCP (83/136) rating their experience with volunteers as part of the team and the support that volunteers offered to patients/relatives as very good. Regarding NT and volunteering, 52.2% (71/136) of HCP would alternate between digital and face-to-face volunteering (in person + NT (50/50)) and 30.9% (42/136) said that they could do some specific activity through NT but in a face to face accompaniment (in person + occasionally NT). The main reported benefits were the reduction of geographical dispersion (30.1%) and the ability to provide more continuous support (16.9%). Additional benefits included the occasional use of NT (4.4%) and its role as a complementary resource.

18.4% (25/136) of HCP considered that the use of NT during volunteering would be a barriers mainly due to the lack of human warmth (11.8%) and the lack of comprehension (49.3%) of some digital resources. In addition, preferred a face-to-face volunteer (6.6%), patients, and relatives could have physical impediments to using the NT.

Volunteers surveyed were satisfied with the volunteer service with an average score of 8.96/10 (CI95% 8.75–9.16). 86% of volunteers (147/171) considered their volunteering experience as very good. Regarding NT and volunteering, 83% (142/171) preferred face to face volunteering (only in person) and 70.8% (121/171) said that they could do some specific activity through NT but in a face to face accompaniment (in person + occasionally NT). Qualitative results

In order to obtain more detailed information about the perceived usefulness of NT in the different groups, semi-structured interviews were conducted for patients and relatives, and focus groups were conducted for HCP and volunteers. In depth interviews were carried out with 10 patients, 7 of whom were women with an age range of 50–82 years. Most of these patients were admitted to an inpatient unit of a PC centre. Therefore, the interviews with these patients were conducted face-to-face. Those who were interviewed at home were interviewed by telephone. In the group of relatives, ten family members were interviewed, eight of whom were women and two men. Six of them had a spousal relationship with the patient. The other four relatives were children of the patients. Seven of the interviewees were over 65 years of age. Nobody refused to participate or dropped out. The average duration of the interviews was 20–30 min.

Two focus groups of professionals were held in our hospice. In the first one, eight professionals from the following specialities took part: care coordination, supervision of the inpatient unit, psychology, physiotherapist, social work and nursing. Two months later and in order to reach saturation, a second focus group was held with the participation of five professionals from the specialities of: psychology, nursing, medicine and nursing assistant. The average duration of the focus group was one hour and 30 min.

Two focus groups were carried out with volunteers online. In both volunteer focus groups, 16 volunteers participated, 14 of whom were women and with an average time of collaboration of 5 years, were divided equally between two volunteer focus group. All the volunteers collaborate in the care field, most of them being volunteers in the Hospitalisation Unit and one of them in Home Care, 8 of them combine their activity at both sites. The average duration of the focus group was one hour and 30 min.

EV and IR were present in the focus groups that were conducted

Three themes about the perceived usefulness of NT for volunteering in PC were addressed form the qualitative analysis of the four groups of stakeholders: Difficulties in the use of NT, Interpersonal relationship, Benefits, Usefulness and Training for volunteers.

THEME 1: difficulties in the use of NT

The patients’ relatives point out was a difficulty the physical or cognitive limitations that the patients might have due to their illness or age, although they also state that they could help them in case they could not manage on their own.

“Perhaps my husband would have had difficulty holding the device because his hands hurt, but nowadays there are brackets” (Relative. Women, 57 years old).

“In my mother’s case, I would have handled them (refers NT), she was very limited, but the thing is to adapt to the user in question” (Relative. Women, 48 years old).

Volunteers mention that, in their experience, physical impairment can be an impediment in their virtual accompaniment. As well as loneliness, patients who find themselves without any social network will create a dependent relationship with the volunteer.

“A person with a recently cured trachea crying and choking is very difficult to listen to” (Volunteer 3, women, 8 years of experience).

“A relationship was created in which he called me outside the hours I called him, so it was very complicated to discern when it was a need and when it was dependence… It was very necessary for me to discern when to attend to him and when not to attend to him because it could create dependence” (Volunteer 3, women, 8 years of experience).

The volunteers also highlight as a difficulty other aspects such as the lack of closeness, affection, looks, silence and not having the support of non-verbal communication as when they accompanied in person. Likewise, the relationship with the family becomes more forced, it cannot be as natural, nor do they have the support of the environment to be able to bring up different topics of conversation with the patient.

“Keeping the conversation relaxed, respecting the silences when you enter a conversation and see the person sinking, never ending the conversation in a sad mood, always ending the conversation when the patient is in good state of mind, looking for the elements and tools so that the conversation does not end” (Volunteer 3, women, 8 years of experience).

“Something specific to communication through a screen, I can think now of light, backgrounds…” (Volunteer 2, women, 2 years of experience).

“How do you do when you talk to the patient’s wife: you are together in the same room, you ask her how she is, you talk about him in front of the patient…?” (Volunteer 3, women, 8 years of experience).

“Telephone accompaniment apart from the fact that you lack fundamental data such as non-verbal language, such as shared silences, touching, glances, that which is lacking… silences, you can interpret what they are crying, thinking, and these are elements that are complicated” (Volunteer 3, women, 8 years of experience).

Professionals and patients themselves highlight the digital divide as a difficulty, with professionals highlighting it especially in rural areas where they see patients, and although they believe that the pandemic has helped to narrow the gap, it is still a reality.

“The only requirement would be to level the population because not all the population does not have access to technology” (Social Worker, 1).

“Now I’ve done more video calls, Whatsapps with friends, family. Although every time I’ve had to do it, I’ve had to ask how to do it” (Patient. Women, 82 years old).

There are patients who say that they do not feel able to use NT.

“I don’t understand it very well. Just Whatsapp, call and take pictures, nothing else” (Patient. Women, 77 years old).

Patients emphasised in their interviews that they preferred the interpersonal relationship that is created between the volunteer and the patient by having a person they feel close to but also indicated that they understood that in certain circumstances the use of NT was necessary.

“It is always nice to have someone looking out for you”. (Patient. Women, 50 years old)

“I’m a face-to-face person, I’m not a video call person and so that has been a delay in the human relationship. But I understand the circumstances and if I had to do it that way, I would adapt” (Patient. Men, 66 years old).

THEME 2: benefits of NT

Professionals and volunteers highlight the benefits of NT as the savings in travel time for the volunteer. And that, in addition, NT and the use of video calls can be a middle ground for the barriers of a fully telephonic relationship.

“There are volunteers who can leave their home once a week to go to a patient’s home, but maybe if you give them the option that it can also be by video call, then maybe they get more involved because it allows them to organise themselves in a different way. I see it as positive” (Nurse, 2).

“I see two positive things with NT. One because before you could hear the voice and now you can see the face using NT with the pandemic. I’m in favour of that because right now you can’t give yourself a hug, you can’t give yourself a kiss. Now the other can see you without the mask” (Nurse coordinator).

“Physical contact through the screen is not going to be there, ever, but lips can be read that with masks cannot. Verbal language to a certain extent is going to be there. I think maybe it’s the middle ground.” (Volunteer 3, women, 8 years of experience).

“It is much easier to take an hour out of your daily chores at home, than to take half an hour to go to the gentleman’s house, be there for an hour and come back” (Volunteer 3, women, 8 years of experience).

Volunteers also noted as a benefit of accompaniment that they could have greater immediacy in attending to moments when the family member needed to talk to someone.

“This person, when they have a crisis, perhaps looking for knowledge, and even more so when there is already empathy, will look for you at any time. If they call you at 10 o’clock in the morning and you have not been able to attend to them or something happens, see how the patient accepts it. You have to be very clear that you have a certain number of hours and that is when you are available and they are fixed” (Volunteer 4, Men, 7 years of experience).

Patients also highlighted that for them, NT helped them to gain comfort when interacting with others.

“I think it’s more comfortable. It’s better for me that they call me and take care of me” (Patient. Women, 77 years old).

Relatives expressed that at least the first contacts should be face-to-face to build trust before moving on to digital accompaniment.

“I think that presence would help them to gain confidence. At the very least, the first one should have been face-to-face, knowing who to talk to and getting to know him” (Relative. Men, 46 years old).

The professionals pointed out that it was a good time for the incorporation of NT, especially when interpersonal relationships were not possible due to the pandemic.

“Right now I think the context is unbeatable. Right now, the lack of personal contact, the limitations of mobility and distance could make these tools make more sense and could be here to stay” (Nurse 1).

The relatives propose a variety of activities that can be carried out in digital volunteering. They included instrumental activities such as reading, sewing, games, social activities to laugh… Although they think of the role of the volunteer for the patient rather than for themselves. They do not see the usefulness of the service for them.

“My mother loved dancing, sewing, chatting. Any social activity where she could laugh, perfect” (Relative. Men, 46 years old).

“My mother really, I think she just needed to be able to talk to someone outside her family, even if she refused. Someone who would listen to her and she wouldn’t feel guilty about telling them something sad about her last days” (Relative. Men, 46 years old).

The volunteers did point out that they saw the use of NT as necessary and useful and were excited to start such a project and continue their collaboration in these circumstances.

“The technology is here and that is going to stay. Once people learn, I think it’s going to be a great invention” (Volunteer 4, Men, 7 years of experience).

“I think it is very necessary because society is asking for it because of the way things are going, because we can go much further virtually and we have to prepare for that” (Volunteer 4, Men, 7 years of experience).

EMERGING THEME 3: training of volunteers

The volunteers indicated that they were very excited to start this digital volunteering project but that they needed more training to be able to accompany as it was something new.

In fact, the volunteers asked for specific training on how to adapt their way of accompanying through NT. How to adapt communication when a person does not see you, if there was any kind of study that analyzed which light was better to have in a video call to transmit warmth or which background was the most appropriate….

“Something specific to communication through a screen, I can think now of light, backgrounds…” (Volunteer 2, women, 2 years of experience).

They also believed that this was something that they would inevitably have to adapt to, as society was using them more and more, but they needed training to get started.

“Because society is asking for it because of the way things are going, because we can go much further virtually and we have to prepare ourselves for it” (Volunteer 4, Men, 7 years of experience).

Discussion

This study explored the perceived usefulness of NT in PC volunteering from the perspectives of patients, relatives, HCP and volunteers. A total of 402 participants were included (50 patients, 45 relatives, 136 healthcare professionals, and 171 volunteers). Overall, most participants recognised the usefulness of NT, although perceptions varied across group. Volunteers (75.4%) and healthcare professionals (64.7%) reported the highest agreement followed by relatives (62.2%) and patients (50%). Satisfaction with volunteering was consistently high, regardless of groups, with scores ranging from 7.6/10 (relatives) to 8.96/10 (volunteers). Qualitative findings identified both benefits and barriers. Reported benefits included convenience, geographical reach, support for relatives, facilitation of communication, and improved coordination. Reported barriers included lack of human warmth, limited digital literacy, physical impairments, blurred boundaries of availability, and reluctance to adopt NT. The analysis also showed that perceived usefulness of NT was associated with younger age, higher educational level, and higher scores on the TechPH enthusiasm dimension.

All groups perceived the NT as a useful tool for volunteers’ accompaniment. This perceived usefulness is higher for HCP, however they are the group less involved in this accompaniment. The perceived usefulness is lower in the patient group. They prefer a mixed model using NT as a complement of the in person accompaniment. Maybe these finding may be due to the familiarity of these groups with the NT. While patients are older on average and less familiar with NT [37], professionals are younger and therefore tend to be more familiar with NT [38]. For relatives and volunteers, digital technologies were also perceived as helpful, but mainly for specific activities and always as a complement to face-to-face volunteering.

With regard to the mixed model of digital and face-to-face volunteering, similar results were reported by Kong and Soon during the pandemic [39]. The authors described a virtual volunteering with animals in a hospice in Singapore through two retrospective case studies. These case studies showed that a therapeutic alliance can be effectively built via virtual platforms. The benefits of virtual volunteering included continued service delivery and increased comfort for some patients. This is because virtual interactions can be less intimidating. They concluded that virtual volunteering may be considered to complement face-to-face volunteering in end-of-life care as part of normal practice [39]. Although this study focused on canine therapy, its findings may also be transferable to our context, since comfort was also a key outcome in our study.

There is limited literature linking volunteering and NTs. We can find studies that relate retired healthcare workers who volunteered to provide care services, especially during the pandemic in order to reduce the workload of active healthcare professionals, with positive outcomes for patients [40]. However, this is not directly comparable, since these volunteers were professionals substituting for other professionals, rather than lay volunteers complementing care. Furthermore, although many palliative care volunteer organisations implemented digital volunteering programmes during the pandemic, there is little evidence on the impact of these activities. More evidence may emerge in the near future as the results of these initiatives are published.

Studies in other areas of the health sector have highlighted that, during the pandemic, platforms such as Whatsapp, Zoom or Skype were used to organise online lunches for people with dementia [41], to implement digital volunteering programmes supporting older adults in the community [42], and to deliver music therapy sessions by telephone or even through newsletters [43]. These studies concluded that volunteer interventions were useful in these programmes.

The study on digital volunteers for older adults during the pandemic showed a significant decrease in participants’ anxiety levels after eight weeks of follow-up, with weekly phone calls for conversation and socialisation [44]. In our study, professionals and volunteers in the focus groups highlighted that a positive aspect of a digital volunteering during the pandemic was that patients could see volunteers’ faces without masks, which facilitated connection and communication. This approach may also help address loneliness or isolation caused by physical or architectural barriers [45]. Moreover, it could mitigate the lack of non-verbal communication inherent in digital accompaniment, serving as a hybrid between face-to-face and purely technological support [39].

Another similarity between the volunteers in that study and those in our study was that they felt highly motivated to adapt their previous roles and continue collaborating despite the challenging circumstances [39]. However, both groups of volunteers initially felt uncomfortable, as they compared their former face-to-face activities with the new digital format.

Studies have concluded that patients with limitations due to age or illness cannot fully benefit from these tools if support is restricted to exclusively digital accompaniment [39]. This is consistent with our findings. In our study, patients’ rejection of technology was mainly based on a perceived lack of usefulness in the volunteer’s role. In other aspects of life, they were not frequent technology users, but neither did they perceive technology as a threat. Only when physical problems prevented the use of digital tools did they identify a real barrier. This is understandable, as most patients face a prognosis in which their abilities will inevitably decline over time.

A finding shared by all groups in our study, and consistent with the literature, was the lack of human warmth in digital volunteering compared to face-to-face accompaniment; this was the main limitation highlighted, along with limited digital literacy among patients and, to some extent, their relatives [39]. However, participants also unanimously identified positive aspects of digital volunteering, such as reducing distances between patients and volunteers, particularly relevant in rural areas where it is more difficult to recruit volunteers, as well as greater convenience and increase opportunities for contact, which facilitated accompaniment. To our knowledge, no previous studies on volunteering have reported these findings, although in other areas such as telemedicine, the use of NTs has been shown to improve access in rural settings [46].

Another common theme is the need for training. Volunteers in PC require proper preparation [47] to fulfil their role in providing social support through leisure and recreational activities, as well as emotional and instrumental support for patients and their families during at the end of life. Through ongoing contact, a close relationship develops, fostering mutual comfort over time. Thus, volunteers serve as a vital link between healthcare professionals and patients and families, fostering communication and relational continuity. By offering their time and presence volunteers provide meaningful support, transforming their involvement into a significant and purposeful activity within the care process [3, 48].

We found that the training identified in our study was not directly related with the NT but rather to managing silences and non-verbal cues that are lost when accompaniment is not face to face. Hutchison et al. [49] reported similar findings regarding the use of technology in home based PC, not with volunteers but with professionals, yet the underlying experience was similar. Verbal communication often falls short as a key element of dialogue; tone of voice can convey empathy and support. The use of humour, silence, interpretation of body language, eye contact and touch remain challenges in digital communication. For example, silence might be misinterpreted by patients, relatives or volunteers as a technical problem.

The technological profile of the participants is a key factor in implementing digital volunteering programmes. This is particularly relevant for patients, volunteers, and in many cases relatives, as most are older adults with limited experience using technology. On the other hand, our study showed that the perceived usefulness of a digital programme was related to participants’ level of technophilia. Therefore, it would be good practice to assess the technological profile before implementing digital interventions, whether in volunteering or in care. The test applied in this study is simple and user-friendly, and could serve as a practical approach. However, more research is needed on this topic.

Although age and educational level play a role in the perceived usefulness of using NT, older adults quickly became familiar with these tools [24] when they had previous experience with computers or other devices [50, 51] received caregiver support [49] or were provided with explanations and training prior to use [50, 52, 53]. These studies did not report results on volunteer accompaniment, but they did report outcomes from PC care support delivered through technological solutions which raised concerns very similar to identified in our study. In these studies, negative emotions decreased while positive emotions increased after the consultations, as patients felt fully heard, understood and emotionally supported through the technological approach to their care. Despite the difficulties associated with the use of NT for PC volunteering, all groups in our study expressed similar feelings.

One of the strengths of this study is that it addresses a relatively new area of research, with limited prior literature. To our knowledge, this is the first study to evaluate the usefulness of these services by considering all stakeholders, as a first step toward implementing digital technologies into standard palliative care volunteer services. Many previous studies have not included all of these groups, although doing so is essential to design services that meet the needs of everyone involved.

In addition, the use of a mixed-methods approach to assess the usefulness of NT for PC volunteering allowed us to gain a more comprehensive understanding of the potential challenges for implementation.

This study also has some limitations. A large part of data collection took place during the COVID-19 pandemic, a period marked by a surge in such initiatives. At that time, the usefulness of the digital volunteering was evident, but once face-to-face volunteering resumed, this option was again preferred. However, we did not find differences between the groups before and after the pandemic. What changed was the perception of NT from being one of the only possible means of providing volunteer support, they came to be viewed as just another tool to facilitate accompaniment. We therefore believe that digital technologies should be used as a complementary tool, adapted to the needs of patients, families, and the entire care team, including volunteers.

Another limitation is that the interviews with patients were shorter than those with relatives, so we may have obtained less information from this group. This is understandable, as patients were in poorer health due to their illness, whereas some relatives were already in the grieving process, and the interview provided an opportunity to share their caregiving experiences. It is noteworthy that all patients completed the interviews, which, although briefer, still yielded highly relevant information despite the critical stage of their illness.

A further limitation is that the term “digital technologies” is broad. In the quantitative part of the study, this term could not always be specified, and participants may not have been referring to the same type of technology.

It is also necessary to assess stakeholders’ understanding of the volunteer role. Palliative care is provided by an interdisciplinary team in which volunteers also participate. The relationship between professionals and volunteers should be one of complementarity and teamwork. Volunteers do not perform a professional role but act as companions in leisure and recreational activities, fostering trust with patients and families and offering support as part of an organised programme within the institution. In this way, volunteers contribute psychosocial, spiritual, and practical support, and can provide information or supplement what healthcare professionals or family caregivers offer [10]. In some of the semi-structured interviews with patients and relatives, we occasionally found confusion between the roles of professionals and volunteers. Therefore, scores on perceived usefulness may be lower when participants are not fully aware of what volunteer services can offer and how they can help.

In summary, the findings of this study have significant practical implications for the development and implementation of volunteering programmes in palliative care supported by digital technologies. Firstly, the hybrid model of accompaniment—combining in-person and digital tools—appears to be the most widely accepted and effective option across all stakeholder groups. This approach could broaden access to volunteer services, particularly in rural areas or in contexts of physical limitations or health-related isolation, while preserving the irreplaceable value of face-to-face human contact. Volunteer organisations should also consider incorporating specific training in digital skills, as well as in communication strategies adapted to virtual settings (e.g., managing silence, interpreting non-verbal cues, modulating tone of voice), which participants identified as critical to ensuring the quality of volunteer–patient interactions.

From an institutional standpoint, the results suggest that assessing the technological profile of patients and volunteers could be systematically integrated into the initial phases of programme design, enabling interventions tailored to their capacities and preferences. Additionally, although healthcare professionals are the group that perceives the greatest usefulness of digital technologies, they are the least involved in coordinating or integrating volunteers—highlighting an opportunity for stronger interprofessional collaboration. Volunteer coordinators should consider strategies to raise awareness among care teams and foster greater involvement in monitoring and integrating volunteers.

In terms of research, this study opens avenues for exploring the long-term impact of digital volunteering on outcomes such as quality of life, bereavement support, and perceived loneliness, given that volunteering in general has also been relatively underexplored in the scientific literature. It is also necessary to strengthen the evidence base on the effectiveness, cost-effectiveness, and sustainability of hybrid models across diverse cultural and healthcare contexts. Finally, it will be essential to include the voices of end users, especially patients at the end of life, to ensure that future programmes are truly responsive to their needs and preferences.

Conclusion

Although the use of the implementation of digital volunteering is seen as useful, the results also show that they prefer it to be done as a mixed model or that NT is one more tool to be offered, as it cannot replace face-to-face volunteering. It is also important that volunteer services must prepare their services and train volunteers so that they can gradually be incorporated into the programmes and the new tools that they can use for their volunteering. In the future, exceptional situations may arise again, such as the COVID-19 pandemics, which do not allow for face-to-face, or there may be patient and family profiles that gradually demand it. Furthermore, we believe that in the future, as the new, more technophile generations become older, enter PC programmes and take on a more prominent role in volunteering, it will be more common for them to feel comfortable using technological devices.
