The impact of the pandemic on female physicians: “I just don’t think I will ever feel the same”
Authors: Huma Farid, Elizabeth Gagliardi, Kathryn M Atkins
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTheCOVID-19 pandemic profoundly affected female physicians, exacerbating existing gender disparities both at home and in the workplace. We aimed to understand how the effects of the pandemic continued to impact female physicians.MethodsWe conducted a qualitative study of 24 female physicians through focus groups consisting of 4 to 6 participants. Focus group transcripts were coded deductively.ResultsKey themes were identified, including lack of wellbeing, unsafe working conditions, increased work burden, lack of childcare, and the critical role of flexibility in scheduling. Lack of wellbeing, increased work burden, and the need for flexibility had persisted years after the pandemic.ConclusionsThe findings emphasize the need for institutional support to address these challenges and retain a robust, diverse workforce in medicine.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-024-11878-0.Keywords: Pandemic, Work-life balance, Physician wellbeing, Physician retention

Background

Women in medicine continue to encounter gendered societal expectations in their roles at home and experience gender discrimination at work. While at home, female physicians who have children spend an average of 100 min more per day on childcare and household tasks than their male partners [1]. This disparity persists even after adjusting for work hours [1]. At work, physician mothers report lack of pay parity and limited opportunities for personal and professional development [2]. Women are overrepresented at the rank of instructor and underrepresented in administrative leadership roles, with only 18% of department chairs identifying as women [3] nationally. Gender-based discrimination as well as discrimination during pregnancy, lactation, and childbearing [4–6] impact female physicians’ ability to progress in their careers.

The pandemic exacerbated gender-based differences for female physicians. Female physicians with young children experienced a decrease in income and work hours [7], likely due to inadequate childcare. School and daycare closures created challenges for physicians who were being asked to work longer hours during the pandemic [8]. Female physicians’ responsibilities at home clashed with their clinical responsibilities [9]. The impact of the pandemic became evident as female healthcare workers reported increased exhaustion at work and at home [10]. Studies noted an increased risk of depression for female physicians when compared to their male counterparts during this time [10].

In the context of broader burnout and attrition rates, these results are sobering. Rates of burnoutfor all genders inevitably spiked in 2021 to 63% [11], but only decreased slightly in 2022 to 53% [12], still representing a large increase compared to pre-pandemic rates of 38% [13]. While there have been limited data on attrition rates in medicine for female physicians, Chen et al. demonstrated that 40% of physicians left academic medicine over a 5-year period [14]. The pandemic exacerbated burnout and attrition, creating a crisis of physician staffing and wellbeing.

While the repeated waves of infection during the height of the pandemic have subsided, the residual stress of the pandemic has not disappeared. Analyzing the experiences of female physicians, the personal and professional challenges they faced, and the institutional support they received or lacked, is key to understanding how to better support female physicians and to maintain a robust workforce. The purpose of this study was to deepen our understanding of female physicians’ experiences during the pandemic and the stressors that may continue to burden them in order to guide institutions on how to best support and retain the workforce.

Methods

Two of the interviewers (HF and KMA) conducted four virtual focus groups between May to June 2023. We created a working focus group guide informed by data from our previously conducted survey analyzing the changes in work schedules for female physicians and rates of attrition during the pandemic [15] as well group discussions among the authors. The interviewers met with an expert in qualitative research at our institution to review the focus group guide. The authors finalized the focus group guide after achieving uniform consensus (see supplementary material). We opted to focus on four questions in order to ensure a breadth of discussion and to capture the full lived experiences of our participants. The questions asked participants to reflect upon: the biggest sources of stress during the pandemic; participant choices to change their jobs, and if so, their reasoning; participant-suggested institutional changes that might better support female physicians; and three years into the pandemic, the biggest changes in their professional lives due to the pandemic. The final question was open-ended, inviting participants to share any additional information. The focus group guide was not formally piloted.

The authors had previously administered a survey in 2021 that had asked survey respondents if they would like to participate in a focus group to discuss issues related to the pandemic in depth. Respondents included their email address if they were willing to participate in the focus group. In 2023, survey respondents who had included their email address and indicated willingness to participate in a focus group were then emailed by one author to inquire about participation in the focus group. Eligibility was limited to female physicians; physicians from all specialties were welcome to join. Recruitment continued until we had four focus groups with five to seven participants each; a total of 24 female physicians participated in the focus groups. An informed consent letter was sent to each physician who indicated interest in participating in the focus group; confirmation of participation indicated consent, although the participants were informed that they could leave the focus group at any time. Participants completed a REDCap survey to collect basic demographic information, such as specialty and whether they had children, prior to the start of the focus groups. During the focus group introduction, we asked participants to focus on their experiences during the initial waves of the pandemic from March 2020 through December 2021. Focus groups were recorded over zoom and then transcribed by a third party.

We implemented inductive methods to develop the coding scheme by reviewing the initial transcripts and identifying themes. After reviewing themes individually, the team of interviewers met to compare themes and create a codebook. Discrepancies in themes were resolved through discussion between the team, and the initial codebook was created through discussion and uniform agreement among all team members. Interviewers then independently coded the transcripts and extracted categories and subcategories of themes based on the initial transcripts. Interviewers then met to review the themes and subthemes and resolve any differences. Based on that joint discussion, we revised the codebook and then had another round of independent coding of the transcripts using the revised codebook with themes and subthemes. Finally, we met again to resolve any differences in coding and, through joint discussion, confirmed final coding for the transcripts. We used representative quotations to illustrate our findings. This study was approved by the institutional review board and informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to proceeding with focus groups.

The most common specialties represented in the focus groups were obstetrics and gynecology (16.7%), internal medicine (12.5%), emergency medicine, anesthesiology, neurology, and psychiatry (8.3% each). The most common practice type was an academic medical center (65.2%), followed by community medical center (26.1%). The majority of respondents’ region of practice was in the Northeast (58.3%). All but three of the participants had children, but those three physicians had other caretaking responsibilities. The majority of participants had partners (92%), most of whom (72.7%) were not in medicine.

Unsafe working conditions

The lack of appropriate personal protective equipment (PPE) persisted throughout the pandemic. One physician said, “they had to make certain policies on different mask requirements, not necessarily because that is what they thought we needed to do [but] because we literally did not have enough N95 at one point.” Physicians had to fight to wear N95s with COVID patients and reused their N95s for weeks at a time. Participants hoarded N95s because they didn’t know if they would have enough. Physicians described being on the front lines of the pandemic with minimal PPE to keep them safe.

Burden of work

The burden of work grew significant for physicians, some of whom felt that they were constantly working, particularly during the first phases of the pandemic. An emergency medicine physician commented on the increasing patient load seen in the emergency department (ED) because of outpatient clinic closures. Another emergency medicine physician emphasized, “we physically needed more help, more physicians and nurses and techs,” but help in the form of additional personnel never came, requiring the same number of physicians to care for an ever-increasing number of patients.

Even physicians with solely outpatient practices felt the increased burden of work. One dermatologist spoke about how when her practice resumed seeing patients in person, leadership “actually made it that we had to see essentially 50% more patients over a longer time, so everyone got extended work hours…so I ended up having to do more clinical work than I was getting paid for.” For another physician, the incorporation of telemedicine had a nefarious and negative aspect: “you are expected to do telemedicine when your kid is sick, when you are sick, etc.” This expectation continued beyond the initial waves of the pandemic.

While virtual meetings provided women physicians with more flexibility, they also blurred the boundaries between work and home life. An obstetrician gynecologist noted, “we lost a lot of boundaries with zoom.” A radiologist stated, “working online…it encroaches on your family time, your personal time.” Physicians struggled with declining virtual meetings “because the expectation is you can make that work, like whether you are in Timbuktu or you know, in Boston.” One neonatologist described feeling “always accessible” because of expectations to join meetings even when she was post-call. The beginning and end of the workday was often bookended by virtual meetings. A surgeon commented that virtual meetings have “extended the surgical work day, so the idea of a 6:30 or 7 AM meeting on Zoom is very common, or into the evening… whereas I think the in-person culture may have created a little bit better workday beginning and end times.”

Negative work environment

Female physicians described feeling unsupported by their institutional leadership. If they complained about unsafe working conditions or lack of PPE, they were told “you signed up for this.” As a result, one anesthesiologist said, “work went from being this thing that I really enjoyed to this thing that was making me absolutely miserable.” Another anesthesiologist commented about the lack of support: “really the most that I could count on was the required supplies in order to take care of patients.”

Another physician described how the hierarchical structure of her hospital’s leadership team meant that information was tightly controlled, even about resources such as the numbers of masks available. She said, “It was sort of baffling to me that they weren’t more transparent about the risk and the resource limitations of what masks we actually had.” In addition, lack of staffing resources created a significant source of stress for physicians.

Participants described feeling unacknowledged by leadership. One emergency medicine physician stated, “we never had any input…I would have loved some help and definitely some acknowledgement of how grueling it [the pandemic] was.”

One psychiatrist reflected on the current state of her job and the need to process the pandemic years: “Now, three years out from the pandemic, it would go a long way to have a little more…support or acknowledgment or space, time, a weekend getaway… everybody expects you to get back to your old self, and be happy and be like, wow look at all you’ve gone through and here you are, and…there’s still a lot of uncertainty about the future.”

Flexibility in schedule

A recurring theme of the pandemic was that it allowed for unprecedented avenues of flexibility within work schedules. In particular, the option of working from home and the mandate to conduct all meetings virtually allowed female physicians greater flexibility than medicine had previously afforded. A neurologist described one change that was implemented at the start of the pandemic was the ability to work from home for physicians within her group. As she reflected on the current environment, she noted “No one thinks you’re not working if you’re not in the office. It’s completely changed.” One surgeon reflected on the culture change within her department due to the pandemic: “I think in surgery, it’s a lot more acceptable now to work from home, post-pandemic…The faculty is much more willing to work from home if they have an academic day or take meetings from home …maybe pre-pandemic that would have been unusual.”

Flexibility in telehealth options and clinical scheduling were also crucial for women physicians as these changes enabled women physicians to continue to work during the pandemic. One participant noted, “The flexibility is so key. I wouldn’t have done half as much as I did the first six months if I couldn’t do some of it from home.”

During and post-pandemic, women physicians prioritized jobs that allowed them flexibility. One trainee who graduated at the beginning of the pandemic described, “I have a 2-hour commute with traffic each way into the city, and so I think from a work-life balance and getting kids on the bus and off to school, that was kind of… a deal-breaker for me, if I had to commute into the city five days a week. So the advent of having more work-from-home allowances definitely gave me more bargaining power.” Another participant thought about leaving her job at the end of 2021, but opted to remain because of options for flexibility. Conversely, jobs that had limited flexibility “had a huge mass exodus,” as noted by one participant.

Positive workplace culture

In settings where leadership was available and responsive to physicians, the physicians sensed the supportive environment and were grateful for it. One oncologist commented, “the president of the hospital was there every single day… our leadership was present.”

Increasing the frequency of faculty meetings helped people to feel connected with their colleagues and leadership and to feel valued. A neonatologist said, “the division chief increased the frequency of the faculty meetings from once a month to weekly and it was a really great community support on Fridays…there was transparency. She…acknowledged all the uncertainty, acknowledged all the heartache, and that was really supportive and reassuring.” An obstetrician gynecologist reinforced that her department had weekly meetings to communicate updates, which was “helpful in that time of uncertainty,” and an oncologist had a similar experience: “for better or worse we had a 7 AM phone call every single day talking about operations.”

Allowing physicians to express their humanity helped ease the stress of the pandemic. An adolescent medicine physician described that every Friday, her department “read poetry to us and musicians from the Symphony who were at home played for us…I felt just really, really celebrated and connected to my colleagues.”

A neonatologist said, “I never thought about leaving despite of it all because I felt really supported by the division, and I think that really helped in many ways…it wasn’t a monetary thing, it wasn’t a bonus, it was the support and autonomy and flexibility…that was really nice to acknowledge…all the other obligations that we had as well.”

Lack of wellbeing

The most commonly discussed theme among all four focus groups focused on the lack of wellbeing during the pandemic, both mental and physical. The uncertainty of the disease, especially during the initial waves of the pandemic, combined with the disruptions in day-to-day life, took a toll on participants. One obstetrician gynecologist commented, “trying to figure out what to do with all the moving parts was just really, really stressful.” One neonatologist described the stress of working in the intensive care unit, while managing home schooling for her four children, while also trying to launch her federally-funded research project, all during the first wave of the pandemic. Another internal medicine physician felt guilty because she was working “all the time,” leaving her husband, who was not in medicine, to manage everything else for their family during the pandemic.

Participants described the intense loneliness they felt during the pandemic due to isolation from their family and usual support networks. One urogynecologist said, “we don’t have family nearby and it just sort of felt like it was always sort of hanging by a thread.” One oncologist noted that she could not rely on carpooling anymore or her parents or in-laws for childcare because of the risks during the pandemic. She said, “I was like a black cloud.” In addition, participants recounted how isolated they felt from other families who grouped together during the pandemic, while their families were excluded because doctors were considered “high risk.”

Female physicians also felt isolated from their co-workers. People stopped eating lunch together. Because of staggered schedules to minimize COVID exposures, physicians often worked in clinical spaces alone. An adolescent medicine physician described feeling completely alone and wanting to be with her team, but not being able to safely work together in the same clinical spaces. When meetings and grand rounds became virtual, additional opportunities for in-person interaction were lost as well. A family medicine physician commented, “I was very alone … it has made me very sort of cynical and burned out and I just don’t think I will ever feel the same about medicine and my coworkers.”

Female physicians’ attitude towards medicine shifted. One physician noted that her institutional “leadership did not seem to understand…the impact [of the pandemic] on people in general, but especially people with small children,” and this made her feel unacknowledged at work for continuing to provide quality care while also managing young children at home with inconsistent childcare. One emergency medicine physician (who ultimately ended up leaving medicine) said, “I recognize that I developed a very bitter view of medicine and that was not why I went into medicine.” One urogynecologist described “feeling like as a family we were just like always drowning,” which then led to her not “finding as much joy in clinical medicine anymore.”

Female physicians’ mental health suffered. Multiple physicians noted the strain on mental health resources during the pandemic and that therapists were difficult to find. When an emergency physician asked for mental health resources at her institution, she was told, “we have Hershey kisses for the snack cabinet so we are meeting our wellness needs.” Another emergency medicine physician simply said, “I am drowning in cynicism.” A family medicine physician considered suicide at her lowest point; the only thing that stopped her was that she would leave her pre-teen daughter an orphan. Even when institutions provided mental health resources for their workers, physicians hesitated to use these resources for fear that it would impact their future medical license applications.

The return to “normalcy” in the post-pandemic world was jarring to some physicians. One dermatologist stated, “we basically took all the pandemic changes and threw them in the trash and went back to normal here… a lot of people thought once the pandemic ended and we went back to normal some things would change for the better, but that hasn’t really happened.” The pandemic forced one anesthesiologist to question her investment at work, and she reflected, “This job does not love you back. You get one life to live, and I want to be with my family.”

Childcare not available

Consistently, one of the biggest stressors for female physicians with children was the lack of stable childcare during the pandemic. Multiple participants commented on the challenges of ensuring that they had safe, adequate childcare and many could not rely on their parents to provide backup childcare as they did previously for fear of exposing elderly parents to COVID-19. Many participants also emphasized the loss of networks of family and friends who had previously helped with childcare and were unable to do so now because of fear of infection. One urogynecologist remarked, “there was no safety net of help like for child care or if someone were to get sick.”

With school becoming virtual, physician mothers struggled with how to balance working while their children remained at home. One neonatologist described how she was working full time with four school age children who were doing virtual school but were not “old enough to do a zoom by themselves at all and it was a disaster.” An emergency medicine physician stated, “I completely neglected my two children.”

When competing responsibilities between work and family arose, physician mothers felt torn between their varying responsibilities. This feeling especially intensified for women who had partners who worked, especially those with essential jobs. An oncologist said, “It really became like whose job is most important, who has to go into the office.”

Fear of self or family falling ill secondary to COVID infection

Being on the front lines of medicine could actually be physically harmful to physicians. One anesthesiologist described how intubating COVID positive patients could put her own health in jeopardy. The fear of falling ill due to infection with COVID, especially prior to vaccines, was a significant one for participants, especially those on the front lines, those with young children, or those who were pregnant. One physician noted, “My daughter was not yet one and my son was almost three. So I think my biggest fear was getting them sick.” Other physicians agreed with this sentiment, expressing concern about “killing your family” if they brought home COVID from the hospital. A neurologist said, “I had my first and only child in January 2020, so my first initial big stress was going back to work at the start of the pandemic and feeling like I had made a horrible decision about everything.” Physician mothers on the frontlines lived separately from their families in order to protect their partners and children from the risk of infection.

In addition, if they did become ill with COVID, different institutions had different policies around sick days. Some hospitals paid physicians who were infected; others did so only during the first wave of the pandemic, while others expected physicians to use vacation time or make up their clinical duties if they fell ill. The lack of sick leave policies during a pandemic was a source of frustration for many physicians.

Female physicians talked about facing the specter of death every day at the hospital and thinking about their own mortality, especially as stories about physicians becoming ill from COVID-19 and dying in other parts of the world became public. A physician shared, “I even still remember trying to make sure that we have all of our affairs in order.”

Discussion

Across specialties, geographic locations, and practice settings, the pandemic created similar challenges for female physicians, with a lack of wellbeing and unsafe working conditions impacting many women’s experiences. The stark reality of the pandemic, with limited access to PPE, an increasing burden of patient care, the inexorable demand on physicians to be constantly available, and the lack of acknowledgement, appreciation, and support from their institutions forced female physicians to questions the sacrifices they had made to pursue careers in medicine. Physicians described a sense of disillusionment and a feeling of cynicism with medicine. They set boundaries around their professional lives so that they could protect their time with their families. Two of the participants in our focus groups, both of whom were emergency medicine physicians, left medicine completely. A family medicine physician considered leaving but could not afford to do so financially as a single parent. The ramifications of the pandemic continue to shape female physicians’ perceptions of medicine, often highlighting the challenges and constraints of the modern healthcare system.

The pandemic magnified female physicians’ struggles within the profession. Inequities in pay, with female physicians earning between 17 and 28% less than men [16], gender-based discrimination as well as discrimination specifically around pregnancy and childbearing [5, 6], and stagnant career advancement, as evidenced by the dearth of female department chairs and medical school deans [17], compounded the challenges of the pandemic that specifically impacted female physicians. The lack of adequate childcare, sudden daycare or school closures due to COVID outbreaks, and the increasing responsibility that female physicians bore at home occurred while their institutions and society demanded increased attention from them. Many of our respondents described this interplay of feeling responsible for their family and their patients as one that ultimately negatively impacted their children, with one respondent sharing, “I lost years of my kids’ lives.” Female physicians had already been struggling pre-pandemic, and then after 2020, felt that the lack of work-life integration was becoming increasingly untenable, even as they shifted or decreased work hours in order to be more present at home [15].

Interestingly, a theme that arose in our focus group was that participants felt that there was more burnout now than in the acute phases of the pandemic. Physicians wanted time to decompress from the pandemic and additional support. For them, although the pandemic had ended, it wasn’t truly past them. An oncologist compared surviving the pandemic to surviving cancer: “once treatment is over, everybody expects you to get back to your old self and be happy…and that’s actually when I find the dust settles, and patients have to process what they’ve gone through, and there’s still a lot of uncertainty about the future. I think in some ways it’s a parallel process to us, like after the pandemic.” Another dermatologist described how after the pandemic ended, work schedules and expectations went back to normal, even though the concept of “normalcy” had shifted so much during the pandemic. It was business as usual, but for this group of women, it was deeply jarring to ignore how medicine had changed since 2020.

One of the key areas we examined through the focus groups was physician retention. This study demonstrated that the two most significant factors that led to retention and job satisfaction were a positive workplace culture and flexibility. When institutional and departmental leadership provided support to physicians and acknowledged the challenges that physicians experienced during the pandemic, it made them feel that their leadership teams understood the sacrifices they made in order to take care of patients. Those who felt heard and engaged in solution creation felt they were vital participants in the response to the pandemic, and this created a sense of camaraderie. The increased flexibility of working from home with telehealth and virtual meetings, as well as flexibility in scheduling clinical time, enabled physician mothers to have a sense of balance in their personal and professional lives. Physician mothers described actively choosing jobs where they would have flexibility post-pandemic and considered leaving positions that did not afford them flexibility. These changes led to greater physician wellbeing, which in our prior study was found to be crucial to institutional retention of female physicians [15].

The American Medical Association (AMA) found in a 2022 survey that those physicians who did not feel valued by their institutions were more likely to quit their jobs. Our findings similarly illustrated that lack of acknowledgement and support, as well as limited flexibility, prompted physicians to quit their jobs or leave medicine altogether. Physician attrition is costly to institutions and harmful to patients, and institutions should focus on tactics for physician retention, particularly of female physicians, since women now constitute the majority of medical students nationwide.

Based on the discussions generated in our focus groups, we identified three critical areas that institutions can focus on to increase retention among female physicians: physician wellbeing, flexibility, and clinical care (Fig. 1: Increasing Retention Among Female Physicians). Physicians should have access to free mental health services, opportunities for career advancement, adequate administrative support, and paid time off. Flexibility in schedules, incorporation of telehealth and virtual meetings, and backup childcare will allow physicians to thrive. Lastly, ensuring that physicians have support structures in place for their clinical work to allow them to focus on direct clinical care will create a sense of satisfaction in their daily work.

Increasing retention among female physicians

Strengths of this study include in-depth, robust responses to a breadth of questions. The focus groups enabled participants to reflect together and build upon each other’s ideas and comments, identifying common themes and shared experiences. Though there were only four questions for the participants, the vibrant discussion gifted us a significant amount of data to analyze. The qualitative design of the study enabled us to examine themes broadly and evaluate how they related to each focus group and the participants as a whole. The analysis of the data enabled us to understand a very crucial piece of information: factors that impact physician retention.

Limitations include that we relied on female physicians to volunteer their time, and therefore it is likely that only those who felt strongly about the pandemic agreed to participate. A certain amount of bias may be present, as the majority of our participants were mothers. We did not independently verify institutional policies that participants discussed. Lastly, there may be some recall bias as we conducted interviews in 2023, 3 years after the start of the pandemic.

Conclusion

Through these rich focus group discussions, we were able to better understand the impact of the pandemic on female physicians, particularly those with children or caretaking responsibilities. Women physicians emphasized the lack of wellbeing, both physical and mental, the increasing burden of work, negative work environments, and lack of reliable childcare as major sources of stress during the pandemic. Flexibility with the option of working from home and positive workplace cultures encouraged physician retention. Areas for future research should focus on how implementation of these recommendations impacts female physician retention.
