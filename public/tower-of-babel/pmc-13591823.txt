Multiple bee stings leading to a delayed fatal coronary event with probable Kounis syndrome: a case report
Authors: Mallapu Ajay Kumar, Poonam Arora, Bharatbhushan Bhardwaj, Parina Tejpal, Pavan Anjaiah, Jyoti Chauhan, Parampreet Singh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundKounis syndrome—an acute coronary syndrome occurring in the setting of allergic or hypersensitivity reactions—remains an under-recognised clinical entity. In many cases, the diagnosis is largely probabilistic, particularly in the absence of confirmatory evidence such as mast-cell activation markers or intracoronary imaging. In the present context, while the clinical history of bee sting strongly supports Kounis syndrome as the most likely differential diagnosis, definitive confirmation is limited by the lack of objective mast-cell–related investigations. We report a 72-year-old man with hypertension and diabetes mellitus, a reformed smoker, who presented following multiple (three to four) bee stings localised to the right shoulder. At presentation, he exhibited features limited to a local allergic reaction, with no evidence of systemic involvement or anaphylaxis. He was managed with sting removal, antihistamines, and analgesics, and remained asymptomatic and stable on continuous monitoring during a 12-hour observation period. He was subsequently discharged in stable condition. Approximately one hour post-discharge, he became unresponsive at home, and bystander cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) was initiated. On arrival at the emergency department, he was found to be in asystole. Advanced cardiac life support was continued, and return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC) was achieved. Post-resuscitation electrocardiography revealed inferior ST-elevation myocardial infarction with reciprocal changes. Bedside echocardiography demonstrated inferior wall hypokinesia with a left ventricular ejection fraction of 40%. Coronary angiography showed complete occlusion of the mid-right coronary artery with a non-obstructive left coronary system. A drug-eluting stent was successfully deployed, restoring TIMI 3 flow. However, approximately four to five hours after successful revascularisation, the patient developed refractory ventricular arrhythmias and succumbed despite ongoing resuscitative efforts.ConclusionThis case highlights a temporally plausible, though not definitively established, instance of delayed probable Type II Kounis syndrome following an apparently mild, local Hymenoptera sting reaction. It underscores the need for continued vigilance for delayed coronary events even after seemingly benign allergic presentations, and emphasises the inherent challenge of distinguishing Kounis syndrome from conventional atherosclerotic ST-elevation myocardial infarction in the absence of confirmatory allergic or mechanistic evidence.

Introduction

Kounis Syndrome (KS) refers to the concurrence of acute coronary syndromes with hypersensitivity or anaphylactic reactions mediated by mast cell degranulation [1]. The underlying pathophysiology involves activation of mast cells which releases inflammatory mediators such as histamine, tryptase, leukotrienes and platelet-activating factor, leading to coronary vasospasm, endothelial dysfunction, plaque erosion and thrombosis [2]. Three clinical variants are recognised: Type I (vasospasm in angiographically normal coronary arteries), Type II (plaque rupture or vasospasm in patients with pre-existing coronary artery disease) and Type III (stent thrombosis due to hypersensitivity reactions) [3, 4]. Type II is the most frequently encountered variant, while Types I and III are less common.

The syndrome is increasingly reported, with an incidence of approximately 7.9–9.6 per 100,000 annually. KS predominantly affects patients aged between 40 and 70 years, and occurs more frequently in males [5].

KS has been associated with prolonged hospitalisation and an in-hospital mortality of around 7% [6]. Although symptoms typically occur within minutes of allergen exposure, delayed presentations occurring several hours later have been reported.

We report this case to highlight the possibility of life-threatening coronary events following even mild antecedent allergic reactions to Hymenoptera stings. In such scenarios, while ST-elevation myocardial infarction remains a primary consideration, Kounis syndrome should be recognised as a strong differential diagnosis. The case also underscores the diagnostic uncertainty in definitively labelling such events as Kounis syndrome in the absence of confirmatory allergic or mechanistic evidence.

Case presentation

A 72-year-old man, a known hypertensive and diabetic on regular oral antihypertensive and oral hypoglycaemic therapy, and a reformed smoker (non-alcoholic), presented to the emergency department at 10:00 AM, one hour after sustaining multiple (three to four) bee stings over the right shoulder. He had no known history of drug allergy and no prior history of hypersensitivity to bee or other insect stings.

At presentation, he reported dragging pain and pruritus at the sting site. The airway was patent; respiratory rate was 20/min with oxygen saturation of 98% on room air; heart rate was 104/min and blood pressure 136/78 mmHg; capillary refill time was < 3 s; random blood glucose was 140 mg/dL. Systemic examination was otherwise unremarkable. Local examination revealed an erythematous, tender swelling measuring approximately 4 × 5 cm over the right shoulder, with three to four embedded stings. The clinical picture was consistent with a local allergic reaction only. There were no systemic allergic features — specifically no generalised urticaria, angioedema, flushing, wheeze, bronchospasm, gastrointestinal symptoms, hypotension, or syncope — at any point during the emergency department course.

The embedded stings were removed and the wound cleaned. He was treated with intravenous paracetamol 1 g, intravenous chlorpheniramine maleate 10 mg, oral levocetirizine 5 mg, and cold compresses. His symptoms resolved completely after treatment.

In view of his age and comorbidities, he was kept under continuous cardiac monitoring with serial vital-sign charting in the emergency department for 12 h (10:00 AM to 10:00 PM). As patient was asymptomatic, baseline ECG and troponin were not obtained during this observation period. He remained asymptomatic and haemodynamically stable throughout hence was discharged after observation of 12 h i.e. at 10:00 PM.

Approximately one hour after discharge, he developed sudden-onset dyspnoea at home followed by unresponsiveness; the event was witnessed, and bystander cardiopulmonary resuscitation was initiated immediately, with an estimated low-flow interval of approximately 3–4 min outside the hospital.

He was brought to the emergency department in cardiac arrest, with the initial documented rhythm being asystole. Advanced cardiac life support was continued in the emergency department, during which he received intravenous adrenaline 1 mg (1:1000) every 3–5 min followed by a 20 mL normal saline flush. After 10 min of advanced cardiopulmonary resuscitation (low flow interval), return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC) was achieved. A 12-lead electrocardiogram obtained approximately two minutes after ROSC revealed significant ST-segment elevation (> 2 mm) in the inferior leads (II, III, and aVF), with reciprocal ST-segment depression in the anterolateral leads (V2–V6, I, and aVL) (Fig. 1).

A loading dose of dual antiplatelet therapy with statin (tablet aspirin 325 mg, tablet clopidogrel 600 mg, and tablet atorvastatin 80 mg) was administered via Ryles tube, vasopressor support with intravenous norepinephrine, and an intravenous unfractionated heparin 3000 units followed by 600 units/hr intravenous infusion was started.

Bedside transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated hypokinesia of the inferior wall of the left ventricle, with an estimated left ventricular ejection fraction of 40% and no significant valvular abnormalities (Fig. 2).

Arterial blood gas analysis at this time showed severe metabolic acidosis, with a pH of 7.0, a lactate level of 15 mmol/L, and a base deficit of − 20, reflecting the preceding period of circulatory arrest. Intravenous sodium bicarbonate 100 meq was given followed by infusion.

Based on the clinical presentation and ECG findings, a primary diagnosis of acute inferior wall STEMI was made. The temporal association with the preceding bee stings raised a strong clinical suspicion for Kounis syndrome, though this diagnosis cannot be confirmed in the absence of documented systemic allergic features or mast-cell activation markers. After obtaining informed consent from the patient’s next of kin, he was transferred directly to the cardiac catheterisation laboratory for emergency coronary angiography.

Coronary angiography was performed via the right femoral artery. The left coronary system (left main, left anterior descending, and left circumflex arteries) was angiographically non-obstructive. The right coronary artery (RCA) showed a complete, abrupt occlusion in its mid-segment (Fig. 3), consistent with the inferior STEMI finding on ECG.

The lesion was crossed with a guidewire, and predilation was performed with a semicompliant balloon. A 3.5 × 28 mm drug-eluting stent was successfully deployed across the occluded mid-RCA segment, followed by postdilation with a noncompliant balloon to ensure optimal stent apposition. The final angiographic run demonstrated excellent stent placement with Thrombolysis in Myocardial Infarction (TIMI) grade 3 flow distally (Fig. 4). Intracoronary imaging (optical coherence tomography or intravascular ultrasound) was not performed.

Approximately four hours after percutaneous coronary intervention, the patient developed ventricular tachycardia progressing to ventricular fibrillation. Despite resuscitative efforts, he could not be revived and was declared dead.

The precise mechanism underlying the refractory ventricular arrhythmias could not be established with certainty. Possible contributing factors in this post-arrest and post coronary intervention setting included ongoing myocardial ischaemia, post-resuscitation myocardial dysfunction, electrolyte disturbance, the preceding severe metabolic acidosis, catecholamine effect, and reperfusion injury, with no single mechanism established as most likely.

Discussion

Bee stings can cause a range of reactions, starting with a local reaction characterized by immediate pain, swelling, pruritis, bleeding, erythema, and increased warmth. A large local reaction develops similarly but spreads more than 10 cm, lasting 1–2 days and appearing similar to bacterial cellulitis, though it is hypersensitivity-driven. Systemic reactions, resulting from multiple stings, can induce severe illness—including vomiting, diarrhea, generalized edema, and rhabdomyolysis—which may lead to fatal renal failure. Finally, an anaphylactic reaction is a serious, immediate, or delayed hypersensitivity reaction occurring 5–30 min or up to 8 h respectively after re-exposure, requiring immediate medical attention [7].

Kounis Syndrome depicts a complex interplay between hypersensitivity reactions and acute coronary events, mediated by mast cell activation and release of inflammatory mediators leading to coronary vasospasm, endothelial dysfunction and plaque destabilisation [8]. It has been described as the convergence of allergic inflammation with myocardial ischaemia, highlighting its relevance at the interface of emergency medicine, cardiology and immunology [9].

Although KS classically presents within minutes of allergen exposure, delayed presentations are increasingly recognised [10–13]. However, unlike stable in-hospital chest pain, 34 h after a mild local reaction managed with elective PCI for RCA occlusion in case reported by Lin et al. [11], our patient presented with cardiac arrest, required emergent PCI for RCA occlusion, and succumbed to refractory ventricular arrhythmias despite successful resuscitation and revascularisation. In this setting ongoing ischaemia, post-arrest metabolic derangement, and reperfusion injury were all plausible contributors rather than any single confirmed mechanism. In the present case, the development of acute coronary syndrome with the background of allergic insult, though only local allergic manifestations and no evidence of anaphylaxis, compels us to suspect KS as an important differential to consider in such setting.

Kounis syndrome versus conventional type 1 STEMI: a probabilistic diagnosis

Establishing Kounis syndrome with confidence requires integration of a plausible hypersensitivity trigger, allergic or anaphylactoid manifestations, objective evidence of myocardial ischaemia, coronary findings, and, where available, laboratory or histopathological evidence of mast-cell activation. A recent systematic review of 214 published cases of Kounis syndrome found hypertension, diabetes, and dyslipidaemia among the most common comorbidities in affected patients, illustrating that the comorbidity profile typical of atherosclerotic coronary disease is also common among reported Kounis syndrome cases, and therefore cannot by itself be used to favour one diagnosis over the other [14]. In our patient, the trigger (bee stings) and the ischaemic event (angiographically confirmed complete mid-RCA occlusion with inferior STEMI) are well documented, but the allergic component is comparatively weak: the initial presentation was a local reaction only, without urticaria, angioedema, bronchospasm, hypotension, or other systemic features, and no mast-cell activation markers were measured.

Consequently, a conventional type 1 STEMI due to atherosclerotic plaque rupture or erosion — a fully plausible mechanism in a 72-year-old man with hypertension and diabetes — cannot be excluded as an alternative explanation for the angiographic findings, and the temporal association with the bee stings, however suggestive, does not by itself establish causation. In the absence of intracoronary imaging, the angiographic finding of an occluded mid-RCA is mechanistically non-specific and is consistent with either process. In the absence of confirmatory tests and findings, KS can be only suspected as a probable differential.

A further differential consideration is the recently described ATAK complex (Adrenaline, Takotsubo cardiomyopathy, Anaphylaxis, and Kounis syndrome), in which exogenous or endogenous adrenaline administered during resuscitation or anaphylaxis management can itself precipitate stress (Takotsubo) cardiomyopathy that may mimic or coexist with Kounis syndrome [15]. Our patient did receive adrenaline during cardiopulmonary resuscitation, which raises this also as a differential; however, the echocardiographic finding of regional inferior wall hypokinesia corresponding to the right coronary artery territory, rather than the apical ballooning pattern characteristic of Takotsubo cardiomyopathy, together with the angiographically confirmed culprit RCA occlusion, favours a genuine ischaemic coronary event over a stress-cardiomyopathy in this case.

With respect to triggers, drugs—particularly antibiotics and non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs—are most implicated [16]. However, insect stings, foods and contrast agents are well-recognised precipitants [17, 18]. Hymenoptera stings, although relatively uncommon, are important triggers in emergency settings, especially in regions where exposure is frequent. Kounis syndrome has also been reported due to an idiopathic cause [19]. Recurrent episodes of KS have also been described, often in association with repeated allergen exposure or underlying allergic predisposition [20].

From an emergency care perspective, early recognition and prompt intervention are essential in hypersensitivity reactions. Current recommendations from the American Heart Association and Resuscitation Council UK stress immediate removal of the trigger, early intramuscular adrenaline in anaphylaxis, and appropriate airway and haemodynamic support [21, 22]. Observation is advised for at least 6 h in mild cases and up to 12–24 h in high-risk patients due to the risk of biphasic reactions. High risk patients include more severe form of anaphylaxis at initial presentation, initial reactions and symptoms requiring more than one dose of adrenaline and delay in adrenaline administration (> 30–60 min from symptom onset) [22–24]. Even though our patient did not have these high-risk features for biphasic reaction; he was observed for 12 h considering his comorbidities and age.

Risk factors of KS include history of previous allergy, hypertension, smoking, diabetes and hyperlipidemia. In a review of 131 KS cases, 9.2% of patients developed KS more than 6 h after the inciting trigger. However, we found no clear recommendations supporting extended observation in patients at high risk for KS [9].

Among the patients with a history of allergy, the presence of ischemic symptoms alongside allergic manifestations should raise suspicion for Kounis syndrome. Although intramuscular epinephrine is the first line recommended treatment in anaphylactic reactions, caution is warranted if patient of allergic history presents with features suggestive of Kounis syndrome as it may exacerbate myocardial ischemia and precipitate QT prolongation and arrhythmias [25]. Current ACLS guidelines do not recommend any modification in cardiac arrest management in suspected Kounis syndrome [26].

Serious complications are rare, cardiogenic shock occurs in 2.3%, cardiac arrest occurs in 6.3%, and the death rate is 2.9%, as observed in the present case [9]. While KS is often considered reversible with timely diagnosis and management, advanced age, pre-existing coronary artery disease and delayed recognition are associated with increased mortality [27]. The current guidelines of acute coronary syndrome lack specific recommendations on management of KS. Treatment includes shifting patient to cath lab immediately for coronary angiography to assess the type of Kounis syndrome, based on which further management is decided. The right coronary artery is the most involved coronary, although the exact cause remains unclear.

Despite an initially mild presentation and appropriate observation, our patient developed a delayed and fatal myocardial infarction. This case highlights the need for heightened vigilance and consideration of individualised, risk-based monitoring, laboratory tests to establish mast cell activation and baseline ECG in elderly patients or those with cardiovascular risk factors following hymenoptera stings, even in the absence of significant systemic allergic manifestations, while recognising that a conventional atherosclerotic event cannot be excluded as the underlying cause.

Cesarz et al. reported reversible Kounis-associated ST elevations that resolved with treatment of the anaphylactic reaction itself, without requiring emergent revascularization. In contrast, our patient developed fatal type II Kounis syndrome despite only a mild, non-anaphylactic local reaction to the initial bee stings, highlighting that clinical severity of the allergic component does not reliably predict the cardiac outcome [28].

Limitations

In our case, serum tryptase, histamine, eosinophil count, total IgE, or venom-specific IgE were not measured at arrival point, so objective evidence of mast-cell activation is lacking and the diagnosis of Kounis syndrome remains probable rather than definite. Second, no baseline ECG was obtained during the 12-hour emergency department observation period, so it cannot be determined whether evolving myocardial ischaemia was present but undetected prior to discharge. Third, no intracoronary imaging (optical coherence tomography or intravascular ultrasound) was performed during coronary angiography, so the underlying mechanism of the mid-RCA occlusion (plaque rupture, erosion, vasospasm, or embolism) could not be characterised, and the angiographic finding alone cannot reliably distinguish allergic plaque destabilisation from a conventional atherosclerotic type 1 STEMI. Finally, the precise mechanism of the terminal refractory ventricular arrhythmia could not be established with certainty given the multiple confounding factors present in the post-arrest period.

Conclusion

This case illustrates a temporally plausible, though not definitively proven, instance of delayed type II Kounis syndrome occurring after an apparently mild, local allergic reaction to multiple bee stings in an elderly man with cardiovascular comorbidities. In the absence of documented systemic allergic features, mast-cell activation markers, or intracoronary imaging, conventional type 1 ST-elevation myocardial infarction due to atherosclerotic plaque rupture remains an important alternative diagnosis that cannot be excluded.

Nonetheless, this case highlights the potential, even if unproven in this instance, for delayed coronary events after apparently benign hymenoptera-sting reactions, particularly in elderly or comorbid patients. We suggest that emergency physicians maintain a low threshold for evaluating mast cell activation markers along with ECG and troponin testing, and consider individualised, extended monitoring in patients at high risk of KS (e.g., the elderly or those with significant cardiovascular risk), in addition to those already identified as high risk for biphasic reaction.

Delayed presentations occurring several hours after an allergic insult may be misinterpreted as primary ST-elevation myocardial infarction, leading to potential underrecognition of Kounis Syndrome. This diagnostic challenge is particularly pronounced when the antecedent allergic event is mild, subtle, or not readily linked to the subsequent coronary manifestation. Early recognition and prompt, appropriate management remain essential to reducing associated morbidity and mortality.
