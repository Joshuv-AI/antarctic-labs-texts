Corrosion-resistant NiFe anode towards kilowatt-scale alkaline seawater electrolysis
Authors: Xiaogang Sun, Wei Shen, Hao Liu, Pinxian Xi, Mietek Jaroniec, Yao Zheng, Shi-Zhang Qiao
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractDevelopment of large-scale alkaline seawater electrolysis requires robust and corrosion-resistant anodes. Here we propose engineering NiFe layered double hydroxide (LDH)-based anodes by incorporating a series of anions into the LDH interlayers. The most optimal NiFe LDH anode with intercalated phosphates demonstrates stable operation at a high current density of 1.0 A cm−2 for over 1000 hours in a 2 W-scale alkaline seawater electrolyzer (ASWE). Fundamental studies indicate that the basicity, indicated by pKa values, of the intercalated anions in NiFe LDH governs its oxygen evolution reaction activity and corrosion resistance. Highly basic anions (i.e., phosphates) securely anchor Fe sites and facilitate proton transfer to boost both durability and activity. Notably, we demonstrate the proof-of-concept for the NiFe anode in an industrial 1 kW-scale ASWE stack (1,081.2 cm2 anode area in total). This unit achieves a stable operating current density of 0.5 A cm−2 at about 2.0 V, twice that of the commercial alkaline pure water electrolyzer, contributing to an economically competitive hydrogen production cost of US$ 1.96 kgH2−1.Subject terms: Electrocatalysis, Electrocatalysis, Electrocatalysis, Hydrogen energy

Introduction

Green hydrogen produced through water electrolysis is increasingly recognized as a key contributor for 2050 zero-carbon target1,2. The current industrial production cost of green hydrogen, primarily reliant on alkaline pure water electrolysis3, generally falls within the range of US$ 4–5.5 kgH2−1 (ref. 4). However, this cost is not yet competitive with that of grey hydrogen produced from fossil fuels (e.g., ~US$ 2.5 kgH2−1)5,6, limiting the widespread deployment of green hydrogen. Despite this, direct alkaline seawater electrolysis offers a practical route to producing economically competitive hydrogen. The economic feasibility of this approach is preliminarily validated through a techno-economic analysis (TEA) based on data collected from reports and the real market (Supplementary Note 1). Direct seawater electrolysis eliminates the demand for precious pure water input7, contributing to a significant reduction in annual operational expenditure (OPEX) (Fig. 1a). Although one may suggest integrating a desalination system with electrolysis to avoid direct usage of seawater with complex components8,9, the operation of such a desalination system would significantly increase the annual capital expenditure (CAPEX)10 (Fig. 1a). Collectively, direct alkaline seawater electrolysis demonstrates clear economic superiority compared to traditional pure water electrolysis and seawater desalination combined with electrolysis. More importantly, increasing the operating current from 0.2–0.3 A cm−2 (current commercial standard) to at least 0.5 A cm−2 in the high current zone can significantly lower hydrogen production cost (Fig. 1b). Therefore, developing large-current alkaline seawater electrolysis is economically crucial.

However, the current commercial alkaline pure water electrolyzer (AWE) electrodes (i.e., Raney Ni) are inadequate for seawater electrolysis due to severe anode corrosion and chlorine evolution reaction (ClER), even at a low current density of 0.2 A cm−2 (Figs. 1c–e and S3–S5). Therefore, achieving large-scale seawater electrolysis imposes higher demands on robust anodes. Currently, various anodes, such as nitrides11, phosphides12,13, and sulfides14,15, have been reported for seawater electrolysis, but they generally operate below 0.4 A cm−2 and/or 400 hours due to the corrosion concern. These anodes normally undergo surface reconstruction, which creates surface defects that provide opportunities for chloride (Cl−) to enter the electrode surface and trap active sites16,17 (Fig. 1c). This process leads to gradual electrode degradation in long-term operation. Meanwhile, the disordered anion distribution upon the electrode surface may affect the adsorption of OER intermediates, potentially reducing OER activity18. Therefore, it is challenging for these types of anodes to maintain stability under more rigorous large-current (e.g., ≥0.5 A cm−2) operating conditions.

Our previous work demonstrated the feasibility of NiFe layered double hydroxide (LDH)-based anode in alkaline seawater electrolyzer (ASWE) by introducing anomalous Cl− adsorption on Fe sites19 (Fig. 1c). This feature enabled stable operation of pristine NiFe LDH in a 100 W-scale ASWE at 0.2 A cm−2. Unfortunately, selective adsorption of Cl− on Fe sites fails to resist the more aggressive Cl− attack at a high current density, resulting in severe anode corrosion (Figs. 1c–f and S3–S5). Therefore, the pristine NiFe LDH is also inadequate for application in a large-current ASWE. However, importantly, this work shows that anions, which selectively occupy Fe sites can steer the overall structure stability of NiFe LDH while not impeding the OER at Ni sites. The layered structure and positive charge characteristics of NiFe LDH facilitate effective bonding and accommodation of intercalated anions20. It is expected that intercalated anions with a stronger bonding affinity to Fe sites will effectively block the bonding of Cl− to Fe. However, a comprehensive understanding of the influence of intercalated anions on LDH’s OER activity and corrosion resistance has not been achieved yet. Additionally, besides lab-scale two/three-electrode configurations, validating the feasibility of catalysts in large-scale ASWE emerges as a necessity to bridge the gaps between fundamental laboratory research and practical device demands.

In this context, we propose to boost the corrosion resistance of NiFe LDH via interlayer anion exchange strategy and ensure its stable operation in a kilowatt-scale ASWE. The effect of anions is clarified by investigating a series of NiFe LDH with different intercalated anions. We demonstrate that the basicity, characterized by pKa values (Table S1), of intercalated anions fundamentally determines the OER activity and corrosion resistance of NiFe LDH. The highly basic anion, e.g., phosphate, enables the resulting NiFe LDH-[PO43−] to outperform pristine NiFe LDH and several reported anodes. More importantly, we demonstrate the industrial feasibility of NiFe LDH-[PO43−]-based anode in a 1 kW-scale ASWE. The stable operating current density of this ASWE reaches up to 0.5 A cm−2, significantly exceeding that of the current commercial AWE (~0.2 A cm−2). This results in a 20% reduction in hydrogen levelized production cost.

Results

Engineering NiFe LDH with intercalated anions

The pristine hexagonal nanosheet-like NiFe LDH (noted as AP NiFe LDH) with a diameter less than 20 nm was synthesised by coprecipitation (Figs. 2a,b, and S6). Afterwards, seven types of engineered NiFe LDH (noted as NiFe LDH-[A]) were obtained by exchanging a series of anions, including chloride (Cl−), perchlorate (ClO4−), nitrate (NO3−), selenate (SeO42−), sulfate (SO42−), carbonate (CO32−), and phosphate (PO43−) (Figs. 2a,b, and S7). X-ray diffraction (XRD) spectra indicate that the alteration of anions does not affect the basic structure of NiFe LDH, while a slight increase in the degree of (003) peaks suggests a reduction in the interlayer distance induced by successful intercalating of anions and their electrostatic interactions with host layers21,22 (Fig. 2b). In addition, minimal changes in the coordination environments of Ni but significant alterations in those of Fe provide evidence of selective anions bonding with Fe sites rather than Ni sites (Fig. 2c).

The OER activity and corrosion resistance of NiFe LDH-[A] were initially assessed in alkaline saline. As shown in Fig. 2d, the onset potentials of NiFe LDH-[A] remained unaffected by intercalating various anions, but their electrochemical active surface area (ECSA)-normalized OER activities displayed noticeable differences with increasing potential. Similarly, Fig. 2e illustrates the variation in the corrosion resistance of NiFe LDH-[A] with different intercalated anions. Collectively, positive correlations were established between the OER activity and corrosion resistance of NiFe LDH-[A] and the basicity, described by pKa values23,24, of intercalated anions (Fig. 2f,g, Table S1). This indicates that the OER activity and corrosion resistance of NiFe LDH-[A] improve with the incorporation of more basic anions. Furthermore, varying degrees of current attenuation due to Ni/Fe leaching were observed at elevated potentials in cyclic voltammetry (CV) curves (Fig. S8), demonstrating a similar positive trend in the influence of anion basicity on the overall stability of NiFe LDH-[A]. In addition, the dependence of performance enhancement on structural parameters of anions, such as ionic radii and oxygen atom number, was excluded (Fig. S9). The crystallinity of NiFe LDH was also evidenced to have no impact on the feasibility of these positive correlations (Figs. S10, S11). The nature of pKa inspires us that these positive correlations are related to the distinct interactions of anions with metal cations/protons. For example, NiFe LDH-[PO43−] with the most basic anion intercalation was chosen to ensure stable operation of ASWE under large-current conditions.

Alkaline seawater electrolysis performance

The performance of AP NiFe LDH and NiFe LDH-[PO43−]-based anodes for alkaline seawater electrolysis were then evaluated using a two-electrode setup. As shown in Figs. 3a,b, S12, S13, NiFe LDH-[PO43−]-based anode displayed a significant improvement in both OER activity and corrosion resistance; it demonstrated more stable operation across various current densities ranging from 0.1 to 1.5 A cm−2. Though the AP NiFe LDH-based anode exhibited good resistance to Cl− and stability at current densities lower than 0.5 A cm−2, it suffered from severe electrode corrosion and ClER as the current density increased to higher levels (Figs. 3a and S12). This evolution indicates a more severe Cl− attack on the electrode at higher current density. In contrast, NiFe LDH-[PO43−]-based anode exhibited mild ClER and lower dissolved Ni/Fe concentrations at current densities from 0.1 to 1.5 A cm−2 (Figs. 3b and S12). These findings highlight the significant Cl− resistance and stability of NiFe LDH achieved by the incorporation of phosphates. Furthermore, the minimal phosphates and Ni/Fe concentrations observed throughout the CP tests (Fig. 3b), along with the consistent nanosheet-like morphology and components of NiFe LDH-[PO43−] after the CP tests (Fig. S14), suggest that the strong bonding of phosphates to Fe sites contributes to the overall structural stability25.

To verify its scale-up potential, the NiFe LDH-[PO43−]-based anode was assembled into a 2 W-scale alkaline electrolyzer with a commercial Raney Ni-based cathode and operated under industrial conditions. The target ASWE achieved a high current density of 1.0 A cm−2 at a low cell voltage of about 2.0 V, demonstrating a significant advantage over commercial Raney Ni- and AP NiFe LDH-based ASWEs (Fig. 3c). Notably, the target ASWE worked stably at 1.0 A cm−2 for over 1000 hours (Fig. 3d). This remarkable durability surpasses that of several reported anodes utilized in alkaline seawater electrolysis (Table S2). Those anodes were evaluated across various electrode configurations, including three-electrode, two-electrode, and electrolyzer systems, and typically operated below 0.4 A cm−2 and/or 400 hours (Table S2).

Correlation between NiFe LDH performance and anion basicity

The intrinsic effect of anion basicity on the OER activity and corrosion resistance performance of NiFe LDH were investigated to understand the enhanced performance of NiFe LDH-[A] in seawater electrolysis. Initially, the electron density was found to increase on Ni/O sites but no change on Fe sites with the incorporation of more basic anions (Figs. 4a,b, and S15–S17). This observation aligns with the inhibited Ni oxidation and delayed transition from α-Ni(OH)2 to γ-NiOOH in operando Raman tests (Figs. 4c, S18, S20). These results confirm the electron-donating nature of basic anions in NiFe LDH-[A]26. As a result, the electron-withdrawing effect of acidic Fe sites on the electrons of Ni/O sites was mitigated through the Ni−O−Fe←A bond21. Importantly, this heightened electron-donating effect leads to a stronger interaction between highly basic anions (e.g., phosphates) and Fe sites, which contributes to the enhanced stability of NiFe LDH-[PO43−].

In addition to anchoring metal cations, the electron-donating nature of basic anions enables their interaction with protons to influence the proton-coupled electron transfer (PCET) process during OER. As shown in the operando attenuated total reflectance-infrared absorption spectroscopy (ATR-IRAS) spectra, the absolute intensity of O–H stretching mode (νO–H) of water upon NiFe LDH-[PO43−] electrode decreased gradually with increasing potential (Fig. 4d). This suggests that the NiFe LDH-[PO43−] electrode strongly interacts with interfacial water as the polarized potential increases27. This enhanced interaction facilitates water supply and improves proton transfer efficiency28. Moreover, no detectable shift in the νO–H position and the configuration of interfacial water was observed upon the NiFe LDH-[PO43−] electrode (Figs. 4d and S21). This lack of shift indicates consistent components, such as OH− concentration, in the electrical double layer (EDL) upon the NiFe LDH-[PO43−] electrode27. In contrast, the νO–H position generally showed a red-shift with the increasing ratio of strongly bonded water upon the NiFe LDH-[Cl−] electrode (Figs. 4d and S21). This indicates a strengthened hydrogen-bond network with increasing potential29, which hinders water supply and efficient proton transfer in the EDL. The change in OH− concentration in the EDL were then quantitatively assessed using the IrOx-modified rotating ring-disk electrode (RRDE) technique30 (Figs. S22, S23). Notably, the pH in the EDL, referred to as pHdisk, upon the NiFe LDH-[PO43−] electrode was found to remain elevated as compared to that upon the NiFe LDH-[Cl−] electrode during OER (Fig. 4e). This result provides further evidence of the consistent components upon the NiFe LDH-[PO43−] electrode. The high concentration of OH− helps sustain stable OER activity and reduces Cl− influx into the EDL31,32, resulting in less ClER and better corrosion resistance (Fig. 4f). In contrast, without highly basic anions steering, slow proton transfer presents a challenge to achieving high OER activity and adequate OH− supplementation (Fig. S24). As a result, Cl− influxes with OH− into the EDL to balance the positive surface charge, leading to severe electrode corrosion and ClER (Fig. S24).

The dominant OER mechanism on NiFe LDH-[Cl−] and NiFe LDH-[PO43−] electrodes was determined as the adsorbate evolution mechanism (AEM) through differential electrochemical mass spectrometry (DEMS) analysis (Fig. S25). Based on our findings and a more recent mechanism study33, a dual-metal center synergistically involved AEM pathway was established (Fig. S26). Density functional theory (DFT) computations (Fig. S27, Supplementary Data 1) revealed that the incorporation of phosphates significantly lowers the energy barrier of the rate-determining step (RDS) from *O to *OOH. This accelerated OER kinetics can be attributed to the strong interaction between highly basic phosphates and H2O, *OH, and *OOH intermediates, aligning with the spectral and electrochemical findings.

Industrial potential and economic feasibility analysis

The scale-up performance of NiFe LDH-[PO43−]-based anode was assessed in an industrial hydrogen production system (John Cockerill (Suzhou) company), which includes a 1 kW-scale electrolyzer stack with 17 cells connected in series (Fig. 5a,b). The anode area for a single cell is about 63.6 cm2, while the total anode area for the entire stack is about 1,081.2 cm2. Each cell primarily consists of a commercial Raney Ni- or NiFe LDH-based anode, a commercial Raney Ni-based cathode, a commercial woven polymer diaphragm, and other components (e.g., seal rings and electrode plates, etc.) (Fig. 5b,c). The NiFe LDH-based catalysts have been preliminarily demonstrated to be suitable for large-scale production (Fig. S28).

The commercial Raney Ni-based AWE achieved a current density of 0.2 A cm−2 at about 2.0 V using pure 6 M KOH as electrolyte (Fig. 5d). In contrast, our NiFe LDH-based ASWE, with 6 M KOH + raw seawater as electrolyte, achieved about 0.33 and 0.5 A cm−2 on AP NiFe LDH- and NiFe LDH-[PO43−]-based anodes, respectively (Fig. 5d). Moreover, the NiFe LDH-[PO43−]-based ASWE demonstrated higher energy efficiency (EE), calculated based on the higher heating value (HHV) of hydrogen34 (Supplementary Note 2), across different current densities. It achieved 73.4% EE at 0.5 A cm−2 at about 2.0 V, which is nearly 20% better than that of commercial Raney Ni-based AWE (Fig. 5e). Notably, the target ASWE exhibited stable operation for 100 hours at a high current density of 0.5 A cm−2 (Fig. 5f). During the duration test, about 2.24 Nm3, equivalent to 0.2 kg, of pure hydrogen was produced every 10 hours; about 8.6 g of KOH was consumed per kilogram seawater for Ca2+/Mg2+ precipitation treatment; seawater after precipitation and filtration treatments was used as feedstock. This achievement represents the successful implementation of an industrial-scale seawater hydrogen production system under rigorous large-current conditions (Fig. S29).

TEA was then conducted to preliminary evaluate the economic feasibility of our ASWE (Supplementary Note 2). Considering a current electricity price of US$ 0.05 kWh−1 and a 10-year plant lifetime, the HLPC for an AWE plant was calculated at US$ 5.28 kgH2−1 under cell conditions of 0.2 A cm−2 and 2.0 V (Fig. 5g, Supplementary Note 1). Replacing pure water with seawater reduced the HLPC for an ASWE plant by US$ 0.49 kgH2−1 (Fig. 5g). Increasing the operating current density to 0.5 A cm−2 decreased the HLPC by an additional US$ 0.64 kgH2−1 (Fig. 5g). Utilizing offshore renewable electricity (i.e., US$ 0.02 kWh−1) and updating plant equipment (i.e., 30-year plant lifetime) could further decrease the HLPC to US$ 1.96 kgH2−1 (Fig. 5g). This cost of green hydrogen is competitive compared to that of grey hydrogen and meets the 2030 global targets (i.e., US$ 2.0–2.5 kgH2−1)35 (Fig. 5h). In contrast, the HLPC for an AWE plant would be challenged to meet these targets under the same conditions (Fig. S30).

In summary, this study focuses on the development of a durable and industrially feasible anode for alkaline seawater electrolysis. We successfully engineered NiFe LDH-based anodes with enhanced OER activity and corrosion resistance by intercalating a series of basic anions (e.g., phosphates). The optimal NiFe LDH-based anode exhibited stable operation in both 2W-scale and industrial 1 kW-scale ASWEs at high current densities, contributing to low-cost hydrogen production (i.e., US$ 1.96 kgH2−1). The findings provide an effective method and comprehensive insight into improving the feasibility of NiFe LDH-based anode through anion intercalation. Further exploration of efficient anions and alternative methods for enhancing NiFe LDH-based anodes is still encouraged for future applications. This work offers a valuable understanding of the mechanisms, technology, and economics for alkaline seawater electrolysis, helping bridge the gap between fundamental laboratory studies and practical device demands for large-scale green hydrogen production.

Methods

Synthesis of NiFe LDH-[A]

For the synthesis of initial AP NiFe LDH, a 10 mL of aqueous solution containing 0.075 M NiCl2·6H2O ( ≥ 98%, Sigma-Aldrich, Australia) and 0.025 M FeCl3·6H2O (97%, Sigma-Aldrich, Australia) was added dropwise to 20 mL of formamide (≥99%, Sigma-Aldrich, Australia) solution (23 vol.%) under magnetic stirring at 80 °C. The solution pH was simultaneously maintained at 9 − 10 with dropwise adding 0.25 M NaOH (≥98%, Sigma-Aldrich, Australia). The reaction was completed within 10 min. For the synthesis of NiFe LDH-[A], about 5 mg of AP NiFe LDH was added into a 5 mL of aqueous solution containing 1 M NaOH and 0.1 M sodium salt, including NaCl (≥99%, Sigma-Aldrich, Australia), NaClO4 (≥ 98.0%, Sigma-Aldrich, Australia), NaNO3 (≥ 99.0%, Sigma-Aldrich, Australia), Na2SeO4 (≥98.0%, Sigma-Aldrich, Australia), Na2SO4 (≥ 99.0%, Sigma-Aldrich, Australia), Na2CO3 (≥ 99.5%, Sigma-Aldrich, Australia), or Na3PO4 (≥99.9%, Sigma-Aldrich, Australia), respectively. The obtained slurries were firstly sonicated for 1 h and magnetically stirred for 12 h in sealed bottles at room temperature. Note that the 0.25 M NaOH and 1 M NaOH + 0.1 M sodium salt solutions were freshly prepared just before using to avoid the reaction between CO2 in air and the generation of CO32−.

Synthesis of E NiFe LDH-[A]

The electrodeposition was performed initially in a standard three-electrode setup with a Ti mesh (1 × 1 cm; Alibaba.com, China) working electrode, a graphite rod counter electrode, and an Ag/AgCl (saturated KCl) reference electrode. A 30 mL aqueous solution containing 0.06 M Ni(NO3)2·6H2O (≥97.0%, Sigma-Aldrich, Australia) and 0.048 M Fe(NO3)3·9H2O (≥98%, Sigma-Aldrich, Australia) was used as electrolyte. The electrodeposition potential was kept at −1.0 V vs. Ag/AgCl for 100 s. The electrolyte was refreshed for each electrodeposition process. The Ti meshes after electrodeposition were washed using deionized (DI) water (Milli-Q EQ 7000, 18.2 MΩ cm), and then immersed in 5 mL of aqueous solutions containing 1 M NaOH and 0.1 M sodium salt, including NaCl, NaClO4, NaNO3, Na2SeO4, Na2SO4, Na2CO3, or Na3PO4, respectively, in sealed bottles for 12 h at room temperature. Note that the NaOH + sodium salt solutions were freshly prepared just before using to avoid the reaction between CO2 in air and the generation of CO32−. The obtained electrodes were washed using DI water, and noted as E NiFe LDH-[A], respectively.

Electrochemical measurements

Electrochemical measurements shown in Fig. 2d were performed using a rotating disk electrode (RDE) in alkaline saline (1 M KOH + 0.5 M NaCl, pH = 13.63 ± 0.02; Electrochemical workstation: CHI 760E) (KOH: 90%, Sigma-Aldrich, Australia). NiFe LDH-[A] inks were prepared using 2 mg of catalyst, 20 μL of 10 wt.% Nafion perfluorinated resin solution (Fuelcell Store, USA), and 1980 μL of ethanol (100%, Chem-supply, Australia) mixed and sonicated for 90 min. 10 μL of ink was dropped onto RDE for each test. Catalysts were subjected to 20 cycles of CV at a scanning rate of 0.1 V s−1 and a RDE rotate speed of 1600 rpm. Electrochemical measurements shown in Fig. 2e were performed using a typical three-electrode setup with alkaline saline as electrolyte (Electrochemical workstation: CHI 760E). 50 μL of NiFe LDH-[A] ink was dropped onto a Ti foil (0.5 × 1.5 cm; Alibaba.com, China) as working electrode. The corrosion polarized curve was firstly obtained at a scanning rate of 5 mV s−1, and then transferred to Tafel plots by taking the logarithm of current density. Electrochemical measurements shown in Figs. 1d, 3a, b were performed in a two-electrode setup using alkaline seawater (1 M KOH + seawater) as electrolyte (Electrochemical workstation: Autolab PGSTAT302N). The raw seawater collected from Glenelg beach, Adelaide, Australia was filtered in advance to remove solid impurities and microorganisms. Commercial NiMo alloy foam (2 × 2 cm; Alibaba.com, China) was used as cathode. AP NiFe LDH and NiFe LDH-[PO43−]-based anodes were prepared by spraying the catalyst inks onto commercial Ni foams (1 × 1.5 cm; Alibaba.com, China). The loadings of catalysts were weighted as about 2.5 mg cm−2. The anolytes were collected after each CP test for produced hypochlorite and dissolved ions tests. Electrochemical measurements shown in Fig. S11 were performed using a typical three-electrode setup with alkaline saline (1 M KOH + 0.5 M NaCl, pH = 13.63 ± 0.02) as electrolyte (Electrochemical workstation: CHI 760E). The Ti meshes (1 × 1 cm; Alibaba.com, China) with E NiFe LDH-[A] were used as working electrode. The CV curve was collected as the 20th cycle of CV at a scanning rate of 0.1 V s−1. The corrosion polarized curve was obtained at a scanning rate of 5 mV s−1, and then transferred to Tafel plots by taking the logarithm of current density. Note that the alkaline saline electrolyte used for each measurement was freshly prepared and sealed in a bottle protected with argon (Ar) gas during the whole measurement process to avoid the reaction between CO2 in air and the generation of CO32−. Potentials for all three-electrode measurements were referenced to the reversible hydrogen electrode (RHE), calculated by adding 0.098 + 0.059 × pH V to the potential vs. Hg/HgO electrode.

Alkaline seawater electrolyzer (ASWE) measurements

The 2 W-scale ASWE was a single-cell configuration with a reactive area of 1 cm2. The industrial 1 kW-scale ASWE was a multi-cell configuration assembled with 17 stacked cells, and the total anode or cathode area was 1,081.2 cm2. Commercial woven polymer film (thickness 0.65 mm, John Cockerill, Suzhou, China) and Raney Ni (John Cockerill, Suzhou, China) were used as diaphragm and cathode, respectively. The performance of ASWE was determined using 6 M KOH + seawater as the initial electrolyte and precipitation-treated seawater as feedstock. The raw seawater collected from Bohai Sea, China was filtered in advance to remove solid impurities and microorganisms. Duration data for the 2 W- and 1 kW-scale ASWEs were collected using the DC power supply.

N, N’-diethyl-p-phenylenediamine (DPD) colorimetric method

After each CP test, 10 mL of electrolyte was collected and adjusted to pH ≈ 7 using H2SO4 (95–98%, Sigma-Aldrich, Australia). A DPD kit (HACH, Australia), including 0.05 g of DPD, 0.05 g of ethylene diamine tetraacetic acid (EDTA), and 0.4 g of Na2HPO4 (buffer), was then added into the anolyte. The solution was shaken fiercely to dissolve the additives and was tested using a portable ultraviolet-visible (UV-vis) spectrophotometer (HACH, DR900). The ClO− amount in electrolyte was calculated based on the calibration results shown in Fig. S4.

Operando Raman measurements

Raman spectra were determined using a confocal Raman microscope (Horiba LabRAM HR Evolution) with a 50X objective (Olympus) equipped with an excitation of 532 nm laser. Operando measurements were conducted in a homemade three-electrode cell (Fig. S19), assembled with a catalyst-loaded carbon paper working electrode, a graphite rod counter electrode, an Ag/AgCl (saturated KCl) reference electrode, and freshly prepared alkaline saline electrolyte.

Operando attenuated total reflectance-infrared absorption spectroscopy (ATR-IRAS) measurements

Operando ATR-IRAS measurements were determined on a Thermo-Fisher Nicolet iS50 equipped with a liquid nitrogen-cooled HgCdTe (MCT) detector and VeeMax III ATR accessory (Pike Technologies). A germanium (Ge) prism coated with catalyst as a working electrode was fixed in a commercial PIKE three-electrode cell with an Ag/AgCl (saturated KCl) reference electrode, a Pt-wire counter electrode, and freshly prepared alkaline saline electrolyte (Fig. S19). All IR spectra were obtained via averaging 32 scans at a spectral resolution of 4 cm−1.

IrOx-modified rotating ring-disk electrode (RRDE) technique

The pH evolution upon the surface of the disk electrode can be established by monitoring the open circuit potential (OCP) changes on IrOx-modified ring electrode30. IrOx was firstly electro-deposited onto Pt-ring electrode of RRDE by CV cycling (–0.45–0.75 V vs. Ag/AgCl, 1.0 V s–1, 300 cycles starting in the negative direction, Fig. S22). Electrolyte for the electrodeposition was prepared as reported30, and was saturated using Ar gas before and during electrodeposition. Then, the OCP (EOC) of the IrOx-modified ring electrode was recorded in 0.5 M NaCl electrolyte at different pH (Fig. S23), which was adjusted by gradually adding NaOH or H2SO4. The pH dependence relationship of the EOC was established by linear fitting (Fig. S23). The pHring values were obtained by converting EOC using the following equation (1): pHring = –(EOC – b)/a, where a and b are the slope and intercept values of the fitted line, respectively. The pH evolution upon the catalyst surface during OER was measured in Ar-saturated alkaline saline. 10 μL of the abovementioned NiFe LDH-[A] ink was dropped onto the disk electrode. LSV was performed on the disk electrode at a scanning rate of 1 mV s–1, and simultaneously EOC of the IrOx-modified ring electrode was recorded. The pHring of the IrOx-modified ring was calculated from the EOC using equation (1). Then the pHdisk of the catalyst-loaded disk electrode was calculated based on the pHring using the following equation (2): CH+,ring − COH−,ring = ND × (CH+,disk − COH−,disk) + (1 − ND) × (CH+,bulk − COH−,bulk), where Cm,n is the concentration of species m on the ring or disk electrode, or in the bulk electrolyte, respectively; ND is defined as the detection efficiency and was calculated as 0.37.

Operando differential electrochemical mass spectrometry (DEMS) measurement

Operando DEMS measurements were conducted on a HPR-40, HIDEN using a homemade three-electrode cell (Fig. S25). A catalyst-loaded Au-film-coated hydrophobic polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) membrane (pore size 0.02 μm, Hangzhou Cobetter Filtration Equipment Co.) was used as a working electrode, assembled with a Pt-wire counter electrode and an Ag/AgCl (saturated KCl) reference electrode. The pressure differential between the cell and the vacuum chamber drives the gaseous products through the PTFE membrane into the vacuum chamber for mass spectrometer analysis. Five CV cycles (1.01 to 1.70 V vs. RHE, 5 mV s−1) were initially performed to label the catalyst surface with 18O isotope. This was carried out in the electrolyte containing 1 M K16OH + 0.5 M NaCl with H218O (97%+, Novachem, Australia) as solvent. The catalysts and cell were then thoroughly washed with DI water (H216O) and dried in a vacuum-oven at 60 °C for 1 h to remove any residual H218O molecules. Finally, the catalysts with 18O isotope-labeled surfaces were subjected to CV cycles (1.01 to 1.70 V vs. RHE, 5 mV s−1) in the electrolyte containing 1 M K16OH + 0.5 M NaCl with H216O as solvent. A mass spectrometer was employed to monitor and record the gaseous products, including 32O2, 34O2, and 36O2. Prior to electrochemical measurement, the electrolytes were purged with high-purity Ar to remove dissolved 32O2.

NiFe LDH interlayer distance calculation

The interlayer distances (d) of AP NiFe LDH and NiFe LDH-[A] were calculated according to Bragg’s law (n ∙ λ = 2 d∙sinθ), where n is the diffraction order, n = 1; λ is the X-ray wavelength, λ = 1.5406 Å; θ is the glancing angle, obtained according to the XRD spectra of samples (Fig. 2b).

Electrochemical active surface area (ECSA) calculation

The area obtained by integration of the reduction peak on the stabilized CV curve of NiFe LDH-[A] (noted as P[A]) can be related to the amount of involved Ni sites in OER36,37. If the involved Ni sites in OER on a NiFe LDH-[Cl–] sample exactly cover a geometrical area of 1 cm2, the relative ECSA of the other NiFe LDH-[A] sample can be determined as P[A]/P[Cl–]. As reported, the ECSA normalized OER activities of the samples using both the reduction peak integration method and the double-layer capacitance (Cdl) method present consistent trends36,38.

Density functional theory (DFT) computations

Spin-polarized DFT calculations were performed using the Vienna Ab initio Simulation Package (VASP)39. The Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof generalized gradient approximation (PBE-GGA) functional was applied to model electron interactions40. An energy cutoff of 400 eV was applied, and a 1 × 2 × 1 Gamma-centered k-point grid was used for calculations. A vacuum region of 15 Å along the x and z axes was introduced to avoid interactions between adjacent surfaces. The electronic energy convergence criterion was set to 10−5 eV, and geometric optimization was considered converged when the forces on all atoms were less than 0.02 eV Å−1. The DFT-D3 method was applied to account for van der Waals (vdW) interactions41.

The reaction Gibbs free energy (ΔG) is defined as ΔG = ΔE + ΔEZPE − TΔS, where ΔE is the reaction energy, ΔEZPE is the zero-point energy, T is the temperature (298.15 K), and ΔS is the entropy difference derived from vibrational frequency calculations. The entropies for gas-phase H2 were obtained from the NIST database under standard conditions42.

Characterizations

X-ray diffraction (XRD) spectra were collected on a Rigaku MiniFlex 600 X-Ray diffractometer using Cu Kα X-ray source. High-angle annular dark-field scanning transmission electron microscopy (HAADF-STEM) images and corresponding energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) elemental maps were obtained via a FEI Titan Themis 80–200 operating at 200 kV. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images and corresponding EDS elemental maps were collected using a FEI QUANTA 450 FEG Environmental SEM OPERATING at 10 kV. Inductively coupled plasma-mass spectrometry (ICP-MS) detected by an Agilent 7500cx instrument was used to analyse the dissolved ion amount in electrolyte. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analysis was performed on a Kratos Axis Ultra with a Delay Line Detector photoelectron spectrometer using an Al monochromatic X-ray source. The C 1s peak at 284.8 eV was used as a reference to correct for charging effect. Synchrotron-based X-ray absorption spectroscopy (XAS) spectra were collected on the X-ray spectroscopy beamline at the Australian Synchrotron.
