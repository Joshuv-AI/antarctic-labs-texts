A radiomics model based on CTA subtraction imaging for differentiating symptomatic and asymptomatic carotid plaques: a retrospective study
Authors: Chaoren Deng, Xingke Wang, Rui Zhu, Shaoping Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThis study compared radiomics models constructed from carotid artery CTA subtraction images. Two ROI delineation strategies were employed: the global vascular wall and the semi-global vascular wall, which focuses on the plaque enrichment side at the vascular plaque site. The objective was to differentiate between symptomatic and asymptomatic internal carotid artery plaques. Additionally, the study aimed to investigate the differences between the two strategies to assess their potential clinical applications and pathways for transformation.MethodsThis retrospective study included 129 patients with carotid plaques, comprising 65 symptomatic and 64 asymptomatic cases. CTA and non-contrast images were coregistered, subtracted, and subsequently fused to generate the final CTA subtraction images. Using the carotid slice with the largest plaque as the reference, the full-circumference vascular wall, the half-circumference vascular wall (plaque-enriched side), and adjacent layers were delineated to generate two ROIs, which were then combined into a three-dimensional volume ROI.Radiomics features were extracted from each ROI, and feature selection was performed using LASSO regression. SVM models were constructed based on the selected features. Model performance in distinguishing symptomatic versus asymptomatic plaques was evaluated using AUC and accuracy.Result A total of 1,834 features were extracted from each ROI method. Eight features from the full-circumference ROI and 33 from the semi-circumference ROI were selected for modeling. The SVM models demonstrated strong discriminatory performance. In the training set, AUCs reached 0.871 (95% CI: 0.80–0.93) for the full-circumference ROI and 0.979 (95% CI: 0.95–0.99) for the semi-circumference ROI. Corresponding test-set AUCs were 0.893 (95% CI: 0.77–1.00) and 0.899 (95% CI: 0.78–1.00), respectively. Model accuracies were 0.806 and 0.913 in the training set and 0.846 for both ROI models in the test set.ConclusionRadiomics models utilizing half-week and full-week vascular wall ROIs demonstrate strong discriminatory performance in distinguishing between symptomatic and asymptomatic carotid plaques, with overall performance being comparable. The delineation of full-week vascular wall ROIs is relatively straightforward, suggesting greater potential for clinical application.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12872-026-06045-8.

Introduction

Carotid atherosclerosis is a significant contributor to ischemic stroke and transient ischemic attack (TIA), accounting for up to 20% of cases [1]. Distinguishing between symptomatic and asymptomatic carotid artery plaques is critical in the clinical management of patients. Symptomatic plaques are more prone to causing perfusion abnormalities or embolism-related changes compared to asymptomatic ones. Distinguishing between symptomatic and asymptomatic carotid artery plaques is crucial in clinical management. Symptomatic plaques are more likely to cause perfusion abnormalities or embolism-related changes than asymptomatic ones. This differentiation is essential for risk stratification, treatment planning, and intervention timing. Identifying imaging phenotypes and plaque characteristics associated with symptoms aids in comprehending clinical manifestations and guides clinical management decisions.

CTA produces high-resolution three-dimensional images of vascular reconstructions, effectively illustrating the degree and extent of vascular stenosis caused by plaques while maintaining the high sensitivity of computed tomography(CT) to calcification. However, these images are susceptible to the partial volume effect, which arises from the high concentration of contrast agent in the lumen and the plaque [2]. Consequently, it becomes challenging to accurately reflect the blood supply status of the vascular wall and plaques, limiting the analysis of their components. Radiomics—by transforming medical images into high-dimensional quantitative data [3]—has provided new opportunities for noninvasive characterization of atherosclerotic disease. Radiomics research utilizing magnetic resonance imaging ༈MRI༉ and CT has significantly advanced the detection and risk stratification of carotid atherosclerotic plaques. Specifically, multimodal radiomics models based on MRI, which include time-of-flight (TOF), T2-weighted imaging (T2WI), and enhanced sequences, have achieved an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.988 for identifying high-risk carotid plaques [4]. In contrast, the AUC for radiomics models based on CTA in differentiating symptomatic from asymptomatic plaques ranges from 0.73 to 0.883 [5–8]. These models primarily utilize single-phase CTA images, with the ROI typically delineated around the local area of the plaque.This approach may inadequately capture the heterogeneity of the vascular wall-plaque complex.

CT subtraction, using subtraction and precise registration algorithms, effectively suppresses intraluminal blood density and better delineates the relationship between the vessel wall and plaques [9, 10]. Building on this advantage, radiomic features—morphology, texture, and higher-order statistics—extracted from post-subtraction CT images may improve CTA’s ability to characterize carotid plaque and its association with symptoms. However, few studies have examined this approach.The difficulty in delineating the region of interest (ROI) of the vascular wall varies between the full-week and half-week (plaque enrichment side) methods. It is also important to investigate whether significant differences exist in the CTA subtraction image features extracted by these two methods and their respective abilities to identify symptomatic carotid plaques. To address these issues, this study focused on patients with symptomatic and asymptomatic plaques in the extracranial segment of the carotid artery. The aforementioned ROI strategies were employed to extract radiomic features and to construct discrimination models. We compared the efficacy of different ROI delineation methods in identifying symptomatic carotid artery plaques. This comparison aims to provide insights for optimizing ROI setting strategies in the design of AI software.

Materials and methods

Research Object

This retrospective study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Because of its retrospective design, the requirement for patient informed consent was waived by the institutional ethics committee.

We included 129 patients from our hospital, including 65 with symptomatic plaques (54 men, 11 women; age 69.42 ± 11.46 years) and 64 with asymptomatic plaques (40 men, 24 women; age 72.15 ± 8.23 years). Patients were classified as symptomatic or asymptomatic based on whether they had signs or symptoms of TIA or acute ischemic stroke in the anterior circulation supplied by the carotid artery within 2 weeks before CTA [4]. Symptoms included hemiparesis, unilateral sensory disturbance, language impairment, or monocular blindness [11]. TIA was defined as a focal neurological deficit lasting less than 24 h, whereas acute ischemic stroke was defined as a sudden focal neurological deficit due to inadequate cerebral arterial perfusion lasting more than 24 h [12].Notably, None of the asymptomatic patients included in this study exhibited any related symptoms in the past month. For each subject, only one carotid artery was selected for analysis. In the case of symptomatic patients, the carotid artery plaque corresponding to the side of the clinical symptoms was analyzed. If both carotid arteries of asymptomatic patients met the inclusion criteria, the artery with the more severe lumen stenosis was selected for study.

Inclusion criteria

Patients clinically diagnosed with carotid atherosclerosis between February 2021 and April 2025 who were subsequently confirmed by head and neck CTA to have extracranial carotid artery stenosis attributable to carotid plaques.

Exclusion criteria

(1) Incomplete clinical data; (2) Prior carotid endarterectomy or carotid artery stenting; (3) History of cardiogenic stroke; (4) Complete occlusion of the carotid artery; (5) Intracranial arteries with moderate-to-severe stenosis or primary intracranial arterial diseases, with the degree of intracranial arterial stenosis assessed according to the Warfarin–Aspirin Symptomatic Intracranial Disease(WASID )criteria [13]; moderate stenosis was defined as 50%–69%, and severe stenosis as ≥ 70% (6). Moderate-to-severe intracranial arterial stenosis or primary intracranial disease; (7) Image quality inadequate for diagnosis; (8) Positive symptoms in the posterior circulation supply area.

Scanning protocols for head and neck non-contrast scan and CTA

Non-contrast CT and CTA of the head and neck were acquired on United Imaging uCT 960+ (United Imaging Healthcare) and Siemens SOMATOM Definition AS (Siemens Healthineers). Patients were positioned supine with their arms at their sides. The scan range extended from the aortic arch to the cranial vertex. For CTA, Ultravist 370 (Bayer) was administered via a high-pressure injector into the cubital vein (50–60 ml at 4.0–5.0 ml/s). Bolus tracking was used with a monitoring threshold of 100 HU in the descending aorta; scanning commenced 2 s after threshold attainment. Detailed scanning parameters are listed in Table 1.

CT image subtraction processing

CTA and non-contrast head and neck scans (DICOM) were imported into 3D Slicer (version 5.6.2). Image registration was performed using the Elastix plugin, with the non-contrast scan as the fixed image and the CTA as the moving image. After registration, the registered CTA was subtracted from the non-contrast scan using the Subtract Scalar Volumes plugin. Finally, the resulting subtraction image was combined with the non-contrast scan via the Add Scalar Volumes plugin to produce CTA subtraction vascular wall images. Delineation of regions of interest

Two experienced radiologists with a minimum of five years of vascular diagnostic expertise individually chose transverse CTA images and subtraction images that matched the most prominent carotid artery plaque layer under double-blind circumstances for compliant images. Subtraction images allow for clear identification of vascular walls by naked-eye observation, unlike plain CT and CTA images, where over 80% of vascular walls are challenging to delineate due to lack of visual recognition (refer to Figs. 2 and 3).During the selection process, CTA images were used as a reference to ensure consistent and accurate definition of plaque boundaries. The circumferential vascular wall at the plaque location was delineated as ROI 1.Using the visually identified plaque-enriched area on that slice as the starting point, two intersecting orthogonal lines were drawn following a cross-sectional segmentation approach. The half-circumference of the vascular wall on the plaque-enriched side was defined as ROI 2, and the same angular segmentation was applied to the remaining slices. All ROIs were delineated on CTA subtraction images using 3D Slicer (version 5.6.2). Using the slice with the largest carotid plaque as the reference, three consecutive slices were manually contoured and subsequently merged to construct a three-dimensional volume ROI, while carefully avoiding adjacent anatomical structures. Any discrepancies were resolved through discussion to reach consensus. Schematic diagrams of ROI 1 and ROI 2 are shown in Figs. 2 and 3.

To mitigate the effects of varying scanning parameters in the inspection equipment, we employed B-spline interpolation for uniform image resampling of all CTA subtraction images, maintaining voxel dimensions of 1 mm x 1 mm x 1 mm. Additionally, we established a fixed window width of 650 HU and a window size of 30 HU to standardize contrast across the images.Reliability of ROI segmentation was assessed using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC). Two physicians independently re‑delineated the ROIs for a random subset of 20 patients after a 2‑week interval. An ICC > 0.75 was considered indicative of good agreement. Furthermore, we evaluated the stability of the extracted radiomics features across different scanners by calculating the Standardized Mean Difference (SMD).The radiomics workflows for ROI 1 and ROI 2 are presented in Figs. 4 and 5 ,respectively.

Feature extraction, screening and model establishment

A total of 129 patients were randomly assigned to a training set (n = 103) and a test set (n = 26) in an 8:2 ratio. During feature extraction, symptomatic patients were labeled as 1 and asymptomatic patients as 0. To prevent information leakage, all feature selection, normalization, and model parameter learning procedures were performed exclusively on the training set.We systematically extracted 1,834 radiomics features following the Imaging Biomarker Standardization Initiative (IBSI) guidelines [14]. These features fall into three principal categories: (I) geometric features, which describe the three-dimensional morphology of the vascular wall; (II) intensity features, which capture first-order statistical properties of voxel intensities within the vascular wall; and (III) texture features, which characterize higher-order spatial patterns and heterogeneity of voxel intensity. Texture extraction used established algorithms, including the gray-level co-occurrence matrix(GLCM), gray-level run-length matrix(GLRLM), gray-level size zone matrix(GLSZM), gray-level dependence matrix(GLDM) and neighborhood gray-level difference matrix(NGTDM).

In the training set, we performed Mann-Whitney U tests and conducted feature screening on all radiomic features, retaining only those with a p-value < 0.05. For features exhibiting high repeatability or redundancy, we calculated the Spearman rank correlation coefficient to assess the correlation between features. If the correlation coefficient between any two features exceeded 0.9, we retained only one of the features. To maximize the descriptive capability of the features while minimizing redundancy, we employed a greedy recursive deletion strategy for feature screening. In each iteration, we removed the feature with the highest redundancy from the current feature set.

Following the initial feature screening and standardization process, the radiomics features extracted from the two regions of interest (ROI 1 and ROI 2) were analyzed using the minimum absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) logistic regression model on the training set to establish the radiomics feature labels. LASSO facilitates feature selection by incorporating regularization weights (λ), which contract all regression coefficients toward zero. This method effectively compresses the regression coefficients of irrelevant features to zero, enabling adaptive feature selection. To identify the optimal regularization parameter (λ), a 10-fold cross-validation based on the minimum criterion was employed. The λ_min value that minimizes the cross-validation error was ultimately selected, and features with non-zero regression coefficients under this parameter were retained as the final selected features. The radiomics score for each patient was calculated based on the weighted linear combination of these features and their corresponding regression coefficients. The final feature set, along with the standardized parameters and model parameters determined from the training set, was subsequently applied to the independent test set for model performance evaluation. All analyses were conducted in the Python environment using the scikit-learn toolkit.

The selected features are input into various machine learning classifiers, including support vector machines, K-nearest neighbors, random forests, and LightGBM, to develop a risk prediction model for symptomatic plaques. To enhance model stability and mitigate overfitting, a five-fold cross-validation strategy is employed for training and validation. The model’s discriminative performance is assessed by plotting the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve, from which the area under the curve (AUC) is calculated.

Moreover, the Youden index is utilized to establish the optimal classification threshold [15]. Performance metrics such as accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value(PPV), negative predictive value(NPV), and precision are computed to facilitate a thorough comparison of classification performance among various models. Additionally, a calibration curve is employed to assess the alignment between the model’s predicted probabilities and the actual incidence rates. The model’s calibration ability is further evaluated using the Brier score. Based on the AUC results and model stability during cross-validation, the classifier demonstrating the best performance is selected as the final radiomics discriminant model. Its generalization ability is subsequently evaluated using the test set.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using the Onekey platform (version 5.6.15) and Python (v.3.7.12). Continuous variables are reported as mean ± standard deviation (X ± SD); categorical variables are reported as counts and percentages. For normally distributed variables, group comparisons used the t-test; when normality was not met, the Mann–Whitney U test was applied. Significance was defined as a two‑tailed P < 0.05 (95% confidence interval, CI).

Results

Feature construction

We used the Pyradiomics implementation (http://pyradiomics.readthedocs.io) to extract seven categories of radiomic features, yielding 1,834 features in total: 360 first-order features, 14 shape features, and the remainder as texture features. The feature distribution was consistent between ROI 1 and ROI 2. The relative proportions of feature classes were: Firstorder 24.0%, GLCM 19.6%, GLRLM 17.4%, GLSZM 17.4%, GLDM 15.3%, NGTDM 5.5%, and Shape 0.8% (Fig. 6-a). Detailed feature descriptions are provided in Supplementary Table 2 and Table 3 .

Comparison of feature P-value distributions between ROI 1 and ROI 2

For both ROIs, P-values for each feature family are concentrated near zero (median ≈ 0, IQR extremely narrow). However, ROI 1 exhibits a longer and thicker upper tail than ROI 2 for glrlm, glszm, shape (and some ngtdm) features, and shows greater density in the 0.3–0.9 range. In contrast, the ROI 2 distribution is more compact and convergent, with a lower proportion of non-significant features. Thus, while the central tendencies of the two ROI are similar, ROI 1 displays greater heterogeneity in feature significance, whereas ROI 2 presents a tighter, more compact significance distribution. The feature selection outcomes for ROI 1 (full-circumference vascular wall) and ROI 2 (half-circumference vascular wall) differ markedly. Under a stronger regularization parameter (λ = 0.1048), ROI 1 yielded 8 stable features that mainly reflect gray-level consistency and energy distribution—examples include ShortRunHighGraylevelEmphas and DifferenceEntropy, as well as GrayLevelNonUniformityNormalized—indicating stability in the overall homogeneity and signal distribution of the vessel wall. ROI 2 retained features shared with ROI 1, such as ClusterShade and InverseVariance, but also included numerous higher-order statistical descriptors of local texture complexity and gray-scale variation, for example Coarseness, Busyness, Contrast, and Kurtosis.

ROI 1 Multi-model performance comparison

In the task of delineating ROI 1, Fig. 9 illustrates the ROC curves for four machine learning models—SVM, KNN, Random Forest, and LightGBM—across both the training and test sets. In the training set, Random Forest emerged as the top performer, achieving an AUC of 0.909 (95% CI: 0.85–0.96), an accuracy of 0.825, a sensitivity of 0.692, a specificity of 0.961, a PPV of 0.947, and a NPV of 0.754. However, in the test set, SVM outperformed the others with an AUC of 0.893 (95% CI: 0.77-1.00), an accuracy of 0.846, a sensitivity of 0.769, a specificity of 0.923, a PPV of 0.909, and an NPV of 0.800 (see Table 2).In contrast, the KNN model exhibited significant performance degradation in the test set, indicating its limited generalization capability. Although Random Forest and LightGBM showed strong results in the training set, they both demonstrated tendencies toward overfitting. Given the importance of stability and generalization, the SVM model was ultimately chosen as the best classification model.

ROI 2 multi-model performance comparison

Fig. 10 shows the ROC curves for four machine-learning models (SVM, KNN, Random Forest, LightGBM) on the half-week vascular wall delineation task in the training and test sets. In the training cohort, SVM achieved the highest performance: AUC 0.979 (95% CI: 0.95–0.99), accuracy 0.913, sensitivity 0.827, specificity 1.000, PPV 1.000, and NPV 0.850. In the test set, SVM again performed best with AUC 0.899 (95% CI: 0.78–1.00), accuracy 0.846, sensitivity 1.000, specificity 0.692, PPV 0.765, and NPV 1.000 (see Table 2). The Random Forest AUC in the test set was 0.882 (95% CI: 0.73–1.00), slightly below SVM. In contrast, KNN and LightGBM showed marked declines in test-set performance, indicating poor generalization. Although SVM’s specificity in the test set is somewhat lower, its sensitivity and overall AUC are highest, yielding the best classification performance. For these reasons, SVM was selected as the final model to ensure stability and predictive accuracy.

The multi-model performance analysis for the two ROI delineation methods shows that ROI definition affects model classification performance. Overall, SVM consistently achieved the best balance of accuracy and generalizability across both ROI tasks. For ROI 1 (global vascular wall), Random Forest yielded excellent performance on the training set but overfit the test set. In contrast, SVM produced more stable results across training and test data, achieving an AUC of 0.893 and the best overall performance. For ROI 2 (half-cycle vascular wall), SVM again led the four algorithms, with AUCs of 0.979 on the training set and 0.899 on the test set, demonstrating strong discriminative power and robustness. In summary, SVM maintained high accuracy and stability across different ROI delineation strategies and outperformed the other models; therefore, it was selected as the optimal classifier for predicting symptomatic carotid plaques.

Model calibration performance evaluation

ROI 1 multi-model calibration curve and Brier score

The calibration performance of each model on the training set was relatively good, with most prediction points clustered near the diagonal. Among the models, Random Forest exhibited the best performance, achieving a Brier score of 0.130, while the other models displayed slight biases. On the test set, all models experienced a decline in calibration performance; however, Random Forest continued to demonstrate the best results, with a Brier score of 0.154. The performance of LightGBM was comparable to that of SVM. In contrast, KNN showed the most significant decline in calibration performance, with a Brier score of 0.248, indicating a substantial deviation from the ideal diagonal and reflecting its limited generalization ability. Consistency among observers

To assess the repeatability of radiomic features across different ROIs, we calculated the ICC for each feature in ROI 1 and ROI 2. The results indicate that the ICC for features in ROI 1 ranges from 0.7508 to 0.999, while the ICC for features in ROI 2 ranges from 0.7504 to 0.999. These findings suggest that the features extracted using both ROI delineation methods exhibit strong repeatability.

Discussion

Exploring the relationship between the characteristics of carotid artery plaques and clinical symptoms from an imaging perspective is of great significance for understanding the risks related to plaques.Multi-phase contrast-enhanced CT and high-resolution MRI (HR-MRI) better delineate intraplaque components [16, 17]—lipid core, fibrous cap, and hemorrhage—but these protocols are complex and time-consuming. As CT scanners have become faster and achieved higher spatial resolution, CTA has been increasingly adopted for routine physical exams and urgent vascular anatomical assessment. However, CTA has limited ability to depict vessel walls and plaque components, constraining its assessment of plaque composition and stability [2].Numerous studies have applied radiomics to carotid plaque analysis using CT and MRI, and overall MRI-based approaches generally outperform CT-based ones. To improve single-phase CTA radiomics, Chen et al. [6] developed separate radiomics models from the plaque body and from perivascular adipose tissue, obtaining AUCs of 0.740 and 0.618, respectively; combining both raised the AUC to 0.883. Wang et al. [8] applied a deep learning three-dimensional super-resolution reconstruction to CTA images, producing a radiomics model with an AUC of 0.820 .

This study utilized carotid artery CTA subtraction images for data collection, distinguishing it from prior research. This approach minimizes the impact of high-density contrast agents within the vascular lumen, facilitating clearer visualization of the vascular wall structure. Consequently, the delineation of the ROI for both the vascular wall and plaque area is achieved more efficiently and accurately. Furthermore, subtraction images, in contrast to single-phase CTA images, incorporate temporal and phase information from both plain scans and CTA images. This integration enhances the hemodynamic characteristics of plaques and vascular walls, thereby enriching the dataset for assessing plaque stability. Although a comparison of model evaluation efficacy values across various techniques reveals that the models in this study (with an AUC of 0.893 for ROI model 1 and 0.899 for ROI model 2) perform lower than multimodal models constructed from MRI’s TOF, T2WI, and enhanced sequences (which achieve an AUC of 0.988) [4], the combination of CTA plain scans with enhanced operations offers greater convenience and broader clinical applicability. Additionally, CTA subtraction modeling is easier to implement than MRI multimodal modeling, making it more conducive to the translation of AI applications in clinical settings.

A critical factor in radiomics research is ROI delineation, as it directly affects feature extraction quality and model performance [18]. We explored two ROI delineation methods for vascular wall subtraction images: half-week and full-week, to assess their effectiveness in distinguishing symptomatic from asymptomatic carotid plaques. AI-assisted diagnostic technology, which integrates conventional imaging signs and radiomics research, is increasingly used for plaque identification and analysis, including in carotid arteries [19, 20]. When AI automatically delineates the ROI, outlining the entire vascular wall is more convenient than focusing on half-cycle or local plaque delineation. However, the diagnostic efficiency should not be significantly lower than that of the half-cycle or local delineation methods. This aspect of the research, however, has yet to be reported.

This study utilized LASSO screening to identify differences in the composition of modeling texture features between full-cycle and half-cycle vascular wall ROIs. The ROI encompassing the entire circumference of the vascular wall (ROI 1) yielded only eight features. These features primarily reflected gray-level consistency and energy distribution, indicating that the overall texture was relatively uniform and the signal remained stable. In contrast, the semi-peripheral vascular wall ROI (ROI 2) identified 33 features, which exhibited greater diversity and included higher-order texture indicators such as Coarseness, Busyness, Contrast, and Cluster Shade. This suggests that the patchy enrichment side region displayed more pronounced texture heterogeneity and local signal fluctuations. The semi-peripheral vascular wall area is adjacent to the plaque-rich region, where variations in shear stress and disturbances in blood flow may contribute to increased texture heterogeneity.

Texture features such as coarseness, busyness, and cluster shade do not directly indicate the vulnerability or fibrous cap instability of a plaque. However, previous studies have demonstrated that increased texture heterogeneity in images is often linked to pathological processes. These processes include the formation of lipid necrotic cores within plaques, infiltration of inflammatory cells, hemorrhage, and the destruction of fibrous cap structures [21, 22]. Such pathological changes can complicate and create an uneven spatial distribution of local tissue signals, which is indirectly reflected in alterations of image texture features.

In theory, delineating the plaque itself most accurately captures plaque characteristics, followed by outlining the vessel wall around the plaque-enriched region and then the vessel wall of the entire segment. However, in both symptomatic and asymptomatic carotid disease, the adjacent vessel wall outside the plaque may be structurally or functionally abnormal and may correlate with clinical manifestations. This overlap could explain why both models in this study show relatively high diagnostic performance. Nevertheless, this hypothesis requires verification in further experiments.

In addition, The variability associated with scanning instruments indicates that radiomic characteristics may be influenced by differences in equipment and imaging parameters. This phenomenon has been documented in previous radiomics studies [23–25]. The SMD analysis in this study reveals that certain features exhibit distribution offsets across different devices, resulting in relatively poor stability. Although these features maintain discriminative capabilities and contribute to model construction, the model may partially depend on device-related information, which could compromise its cross-device generalization ability. While multi-device joint modeling more accurately reflects the conditions in most hospitals and can mitigate some biases, it is challenging to eliminate them entirely. Therefore, caution is warranted when interpreting results and applying them clinically. In future work, the model can be further refined using data coordination methods, such as ComBat, or by selecting features with low SMD values. Additionally, its robustness should be validated using multi-center data.

This study is a retrospective analysis with a limited sample size and lacks validation from independent external or multi-center cohorts. Additionally, it has several deficiencies. First, while consistent resampling processes were implemented, variations in scanning equipment, collection protocols, and population composition across different centers may still impact the model’s generalizability and clinical applicability. Second, the delineation of ROI relies on manual segmentation, which can introduce inter-observer variability and affect the stability of feature extraction. Third, the performance differences among various ROI models were described and compared, but significance analysis using the DeLong test or other statistical methods was not conducted on the test set. Consequently, the results should be interpreted with caution and considered exploratory findings. Fourth, due to the challenges in accurately identifying vascular wall structures on plain scans and conventional CTA images, this study did not perform a comparative analysis based on the modeling of plain scans, CTA, and CTA subtraction images. Furthermore, it did not incorporate additional clinical data into the model. Thus, the predictive performance and clinical interpretability of the model require further enhancement.

Conclusion

This study developed a radiomics model based on carotid CTA subtraction images, utilizing two strategies for delineating ROIs in the vascular walls: half-week and full-week. The findings indicate that both radiomics models effectively differentiate between symptomatic and asymptomatic carotid plaques, demonstrating comparable performance. Notably, the full-week vascular wall ROI delineation is simpler and may offer greater potential for clinical application.
