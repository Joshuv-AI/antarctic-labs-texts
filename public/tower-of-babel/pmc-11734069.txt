Right Atrial Cardiac Hemangioma
Authors: Jade Gourmelon, Valentin Loobuyck, Valentina Silvestri, Anne Chaput, Alexandre Altes
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe present a case of a 76-year-old man with de novo right heart failure. Echocardiography initially detected a mass near to the posterior area of the right atrium. Despite a comprehensive multimodality imaging assessment, the exact location of the mass remained unclear. Positron emission tomography scan revealed comparable hypermetabolic activity to that of the bloodstream, suggestive of a vascular tumor. Surgical resection via right anterior mini-thoracotomy confirmed an intracardiac mass that was ultimately diagnosed as a capillary hemangioma. This case underscores the challenges in the era of advanced cardiac imaging modalities in precisely localizing and characterizing masses, particularly rare entities such as cardiac hemangiomas.Key Words: cardiac hemangioma, cardiac masses, multimodality imaging

A 76-year-old man with hypertension, dyslipidemia, diabetes mellitus, past smoking, and previous stroke presented with worsening dyspnea and signs of right heart failure, which initially improved after fluid depletion.

Take-Home Message

•This case underscores the importance of multimodality imaging and a multidisciplinary team approach in patients presenting with cardiac tumors to ensure accurate diagnosis and optimize surgical planning.

This case underscores the importance of multimodality imaging and a multidisciplinary team approach in patients presenting with cardiac tumors to ensure accurate diagnosis and optimize surgical planning.

A transthoracic echocardiogram showed preserved biventricular systolic function, right heart chamber enlargement, and inferior vena cava dilatation. An isoechogenic mass was noted adjacent to the posterior right atrium (RA) (Video 1). Chest computed tomography identified an isodense mass measuring 47 × 37 × 37 mm, appearing to originate from the posterior and inferior pericardial layers (Figure 1A). Cardiac magnetic resonance showed the mass to be isointense on T1-weighted images, hyperintense on T2-weighted short inversion time inversion recovery images (Supplemental Figure 1), and isointense on steady-state free precession imaging (Videos 2 and 3). First-pass cardiac magnetic resonance perfusion did not demonstrate hypervascularity, whereas late gadolinium enhancement imaging revealed heterogeneous enhancement (Figure 1B). Transesophageal echocardiogram confirmed the mass’s location adjacent to the RA, in a posterior right position relative to the coronary sinus (Figure 1C, Videos 4 and 5). It was unclear whether the lesion involved or merely displaced the RA free wall. A positron emission tomography scan with fluorodeoxyglucose F 18 revealed mild uptake within the mass (maximum standard uptake value of 4.5), suggestive of a vascular tumor (Figure 1D).

(B) Cardiac magnetic resonance showing heterogeneous late gadolinium enhancement within the mass. (C) Transesophageal echocardiography showing the mass to localize adjacent to the posterior and inferior regions of the right atrium, in a posterior right position relative to the coronary sinus. (D) Fluorodeoxyglucose F 18–positron emission tomography (axial view) revealing mild uptake within the mass, with a maximum standard uptake value of 4.5 comparable to that of the bloodstream (maximum standard uptake value of 4.3). (E) Perioperative view of the mass. (F) Postoperative image showing complete resection of the mass. Solid arrow shows a solid nodular lesion.

The tumor was deemed unlikely to be malignant after comprehensive multimodality imaging. However, mild fluorodeoxyglucose F 18 uptake on positron emission tomography–computed tomography does not fully exclude malignancy.1 Due to uncertainty about the tumor’s nature and its proximity to the inferior vena cava orifice, the Heart Team recommended surgical resection. The mass was suspected to contribute to decreased venous return, causing peripheral edema, and increasing upstream pressure, reducing RA wall compliance. Surgical removal was deemed necessary to alleviate compression, prevent recurrence of heart failure, and provide a definitive histopathologic diagnosis.

The procedure was planned to be minimally invasive, because we initially assumed the mass to be extracardiac and intrapericardial, making it accessible through a right anterior mini-thoracotomy. Despite finding it intracardiac, we continued this approach, with bicaval cannulation, to avoid additional incisions. The RA was opened, revealing the mass embedded within the RA free wall (Figure 1E). A patent foramen ovale was closed. The mass was resected en bloc, and the RA wall was reconstructed using a bovine pericardial patch. The mass was far enough from the inferior vena cava and coronary sinus, so these structures did not require repair. A 4.4 × 3 × 2–cm fragment with a solid nodular lesion (2.3 × 1.5 × 1.4 cm) protruding into the endocardium was removed (Figure 1F). The definitive diagnosis after histopathologic examination was capillary hemangioma.

Postoperatively, the patient developed right heart failure that gradually resolved with diuretics. He was treated for paroxysmal atrial fibrillation and pneumonia. A postoperative transthoracic echocardiography showed no residual mass in the RA. The patient was discharged on postoperative day 19 without recurrent heart failure since. Follow-up clinical and echocardiographic evaluations are scheduled at 6 months and annually.

Cardiac hemangiomas are rare benign tumors, accounting for <5% of primary cardiac neoplasms.2,3 In this case, the exact nature and location of the mass were unclear after comprehensive multimodality imaging. This case highlights the diagnosis challenges posed by rare cardiac masses and the importance of a multidisciplinary team approach in management.
