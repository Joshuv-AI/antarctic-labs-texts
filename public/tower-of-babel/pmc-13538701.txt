Digitally enabled GP-specialist collaborative care helped improve hypertension outcomes and reduced hospital referrals in China
Authors: Xiaotong Li, Jie Zhao, Weiyan Jian, David Bishai, Gang Liu, Xinxin Han
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
As China deepens health system reform to strengthen primary care by integrating hospital-based specialist services into community settings, empirical evidence is still limited. This quasi-experimental difference-in-differences analysis examined the effect of the digitally enabled GP-specialist collaborative care model implemented in Shenzhen since January 2022, using 2021–2023 electronic health records of hypertensive adult patients receiving care at community health centers. The implementation of this collaborative care model was significantly associated with reduced quarterly mean diastolic and systolic blood pressure levels (difference, −0.84 mmHg and −0.73 mmHg), a 4.6-percentage-point increase in blood pressure control, and a 6.7-percentage-point decrease in hospital referral, with larger effects observed among women, the elderly, unemployed and those with diabetes. Digitally enabled GP-specialist collaborative care could improve care efficiency and hypertension outcomes in primary care settings, but more efforts, such as payment reform and incentive alignment, are still needed to sustain long-term implementation at scale.

Introduction

Strengthening primary healthcare has long been a global health priority1,2. In China’s three-tier healthcare system, primary care facilities serve as the backbone for delivering preventive and basic medical services to residents, including the National Basic Public Health Services (NBPHS)3, one of the five key priorities for action in the 2009 national health reform. A core service category under the NBPHS program is chronic disease management, with primary care providers tasked with screening, referring and following up on patients with hypertension, type 2 diabetes, severe mental disorders and tuberculosis at community level.

Nonetheless, China’s healthcare system faces long-lasting challenges in resource misallocation4. High-quality medical resources are concentrated in large tertiary hospitals, leaving primary care facilities with limited capacity, suboptimal care quality, and low public trust5. Over the past decade, China has invested considerable resources to improve primary care, including establishing the national general practitioner (GP) system in 2011, promoting the hierarchical medical system that aims at facilitating first-contact care at community level and two-way referrals since 2015, and expanding the primary care workforce6. Despite a 51% increase in primary care physician numbers during 2010–2019, patient flows remain skewed toward tertiary hospitals7. The persistent imbalance exacerbated care fragmentation, especially for chronic disease patients needing continuous care8.

In recent years, China has intensified structural reforms to further strengthen primary care, with one key initiative to integrate hospital-based specialist services into community and primary care settings9. Since then, a variety of delivery models have emerged nationwide in practice to facilitate care collaboration10. Digitally enabled collaborative care models across levels of health systems are increasingly being adopted, given their potential to improve care continuity and quality while enhancing resource use efficiency in primary care. By leveraging electronic health records, teleconsultation platforms, and shared care protocols, these models facilitate information exchange, specialist support, and coordinated management for patients with chronic conditions11.

In January 2022, Shenzhen, China’s tech hub with 20 million residents, implemented a district-wide service delivery innovation spanning multiple levels of the health system12. The policy initiated a collaborative care model that links GPs in community health centers and specialists in tertiary hospitals via a digital health platform, enabling real-time patient tracking, data sharing and care coordination across multi-level institutes and providers. This digitally enabled collaborative care model was piloted in Longhua District and implemented within two large medical consortia that cover all patients with hypertension managed within the jurisdiction. The implementation was facilitated with concurrent changes to public health funding and physician performance incentives by the local government.

The model was first adopted among patients with hypertension or diabetes who are managed by GPs in community health centers. Care delivery leveraged the existing NBPHS program, where GPs provide routine follow-up care to patients with chronic diseases in health centers, including office-based blood pressure (BP) measurement or blood glucose (BG) testing, counseling, health education and life style guidance. Patients’ BP or BG levels were continuously monitored and health records for those patients with abnormal BP or glucose readings in two consecutive visits would be automatically sent to specialists in tertiary hospitals via the digital platform. Specialists reviewed these cases and sent treatment or referral recommendations back to GPs via the platform to support patient treatment and subsequent follow-up management in health centers.

The digital health platform mainly comprises the following components (Fig. 1): (1) a centralized data repository that collects and stores patients’ health records with abnormal results, ensuring efficient patient tracking and management; (2) a specialist consultation platform where hospital-based specialists review cases and provide clinical recommendations; (3) a GP implementation platform through which GPs receive recommendations, act upon them, and provide feedback; and (4) a quality control module that monitors the quality and timeliness of care delivered. While prior studies in China have explored telemedicine or task-sharing for chronic disease management, most relied on randomized clinical trials in rural settings, focused on interventions led by village doctors or non-physician providers, and primarily reported clinical rather than system-level outcomes13–18. There is limited real-world evidence on large-scale service delivery models designed to enhance collaboration between primary and higher-level hospital specialty care, and none to our knowledge thus far has evaluated its system-wide impact using rigorous quasi-experimental design. Understanding the implementation effect is critical not only for China in pursuing a high-value, people-centered healthcare system, but also for informing global efforts to strengthen primary care.

This study examined the effects of the digitally enabled GP-specialist collaborative care model on hypertension outcomes and referral patterns among health center patients over a 24-month period following the implementation. We employed a quasi-experimental difference-in-differences (DID) design to analyze 1,725,318 electronic health records from 107,712 hypertensive patients. These records were collected between 2021 and 2023 from 277 community health centers across the district that adopted the digital care model and a non-adoption district in Shenzhen. We aimed to provide real-world evidence on adopting digital enabled care delivery innovations to improve resource allocation and inform policies for sustaining these efforts at scale.

Results

Study population

We identified 107,712 eligible adults with hypertension who were actively enrolled in the hypertension management program and received care at 277 community health centers across the treated district that adopted the digital collaborative care model (Longhua) and the control district that did not adopt the model (Longgang) between January 1, 2021, and December 31, 2023, yielding a total of 1,725,318 follow-up encounter records. We excluded encounter records if (1) the patient’s enrollment date preceded 2009, the year the NBPHS was launched; (2) the encounter occurred prior to the patient’s enrollment in the program; (3) the encounter occurred after the patient withdrew from the program during study period; or (4) multiple blood pressure readings with inconsistent values were recorded for the same visit day.

The analytical sample covered 106,162 adults with hypertension, comprising 51,678 in the treated district and 54,484 in the control district. We aggregated the dataset to the person-quarter level, yielding a total of 749,222 person-quarter observations from 2021 to 2023.

Among the 51,678 patients in the treatment group, the mean age was 54.18 ± 12.09 years and 18,449 (35.7%) were women. Among the 54,484 patients in the control group, the mean age was 54.31 ± 11.56 years and 18,796 (34.5%) were women. Covariate balance tests indicated that, at baseline, standardized mean differences exceeded 0.10 for only 2 variables (family history of hypertension and source of payment). After applying the inverse probability of treatment weights, all baseline characteristics were well balanced between the treatment and control groups. DID estimates on BP reduction and referral

Table 2 presents DID point estimates of the policy effects. Following the implementation of the cross-level digital collaborative care, on average, patients in the treatment group experienced a 0.837 mmHg (95% CI −0.933 to −0.742, P < 0.001) greater reduction in quarterly mean diastolic blood pressure (DBP) and a 0.731 mmHg (95% CI −0.859 to −0.603, P < 0.001) greater reduction in quarterly mean systolic blood pressure (SBP) compared with patients in the control group. Event study plots of policy dynamic effects

Figure 2 illustrates the dynamic policy effects on BP outcomes and referral patterns. Pre-implementation trends were largely flat across all outcomes, indicating that the parallel trends assumption required for the DID analysis was satisfied. Following implementation, both DBP and SBP declined in the treatment group. The reduction in DBP peaked in the fifth post-implementation quarter, whereas SBP reached its greatest decline in the third quarter and stabilized thereafter with a slight rebound. Consistent with these trends, the probability of patients achieving BP control increased, peaking in the fifth quarter. Heterogeneous analysis by patient characteristics

Larger effects on BP reduction and hospital referrals were observed among women, those aged ≥65 years, the unemployed, and patients comorbid with diabetes, compared with their respective counterparts. Similar heterogeneity patterns were observed for referral pattern. The reduction in referral rates was larger in women, with an absolute difference of 8.1 percentage points (95% CI −0.092 to −0.070, P < 0.001) between treatment vs. control groups, compared to a smaller difference of 6.0 percentage points (95% CI −0.069 to −0.052, P < 0.001) in men. The effect was most substantial in patients aged ≥65 years (difference 10.3 percentage points, 95% CI −0.116 to −0.090, P < 0.001), with an effect size nearly twice that of the other three age groups (18–44, 45–54, and 55–64). Additionally, patients comorbid with diabetes showed a greater reduction of 9.4 percentage points (95% CI −0.108 to −0.081, P < 0.001) in referrals relative to non-diabetic patients (difference 5.8 percentage points, 95% CI −0.065 to −0.050, P < 0.001), reinforcing the pattern of stronger policy effects in clinically vulnerable populations.

Discussion

In this quasi-experimental difference-in-differences study, the digitally enabled GP-specialist collaborative care model was significantly associated with improved BP levels and reduced upward referrals among hypertensive patients managed at community health centers in Shenzhen, China. Although the estimated reductions in BP levels were modest at the individual level, they may still be meaningful at the population level, as even small decreases in average BP are associated with substantial reductions in cardiovascular morbidity and mortality19. This highlights the importance of assessing effectiveness not only at the individual level but also in terms of broader population health impact.

These improvements are likely attributable to real-time data sharing, joint decision-making, and enhanced coordination across care levels, enabled by the adoption of digital health tools. In NBPHS protocol, GPs are required to refer patients with uncontrolled BP in two consecutive visits to higher-level hospitals, even though such fluctuations are often transient and clinically insignificant. Such referrals may increase unnecessary hospital visits and raise the risk of patient attrition along referral pathways20,21. By integrating specialist physicians at tertiary hospitals to remotely review cases and provide clinical recommendations that support continuity of care and enhance patient management capacity at the primary care level, the digital model improves care efficiency and prevents unnecessary referrals.

Our prior DID analysis using community health center-level data has shown increased hypertension standardized management rates and reduced referral rates at the center level following the implementation, along with a spillover effect of this model, beyond targeted clinical outcomes, on increased patient volumes in health centers22, suggesting an enhancement in primary care capacity for retaining and attracting patients. Together, our findings highlight the potential of the digital GP-specialist collaborative care to improve health outcomes and optimize resource allocation across health systems, particularly in low-resource settings where care fragmentation remains a persistent challenge.

It is worthwhile to note that, the digitally enabled collaborative care model was associated with larger improvements among disadvantaged populations, including women, the elderly, the unemployed and those comorbid with diabetes, suggesting its potential compensatory role in addressing the needs of those with higher ongoing care requirements. These results also suggest that by bringing specialist input closer to primary care management, the model may have helped narrow gaps in access to timely and coordinated care across patient groups. These findings are consistent with evidence from digital health interventions for hypertension management conducted in the United States23, suggesting that digital health tools may represent a promising strategy for advancing health equity across different health system contexts. Additionally, our analysis also found that the subgroup of patients with irregular follow-up also benefited more from this model, highlighting its capacity to sustain outcomes of usual care delivery under the NBPHS when patient adherence and engagement are suboptimal.

Dynamic analysis showed that DBP declined progressively, reaching its largest reduction in the fifth quarter of post-implementation, whereas SBP reduction peaked by the third quarter and then stabilized. These trajectories are consistent with evidence from randomized trials that clinically meaningful BP improvements typically emerge around one year24,25. The delayed yet sustained effects underscore the need for long-term monitoring and durable incentives to maintain provider engagement and system uptake. Although our analysis covered two years of post-implementation data, longer follow-up is needed to fully assess whether the observed improvements in blood pressure outcomes are sustained and whether the policy leads to broader benefits in disease progression, complications, and healthcare utilization, as integrated care models may generate effects that accumulate over time.

Our study results highlight several important policy implications that could affect the sustainability of this digital colloaborative care. First, the observed reduction in upward referrals raises important questions about its downstream impact for hospital service utilization and financing. While fewer referrals may allow hospitals to focus more on complex cases and increase clinical value per encounter26, it remains unclear whether these changes translate into reductions in outpatient volumes and revenue under current fee-for-service payment models. Future research should evaluate these downstream effects on hospital outpatient volums and patient case-mix in cardiovascular care. Without appropriate adaptations, hospitals may become less willing to facilitate downward referrals or seek to offset patient looses through introducing additional examinations or lab tests27. Complementary payment reforms, such as capitation or shared-saving models28–30, should be rolled out concurrently to better align hospital and physician incentives in long run.

Second, beyond payment policies, sustaining collaborative care also depends on continued workforce engagement. Although the initial implementation was supported by temporary financial incentives to ensure physician participation, long-term engagement requires addressing system-level issues such as workload redistribution and professional recognition. It is important for future research to optimize the digital collaborative workflows and pathways that support routine interprofessional collaboration31, and to evaluate implementation outcomes such as adoption, fidelity, timeliness, and sustainability. These inputs are particularly relevant for informing efforts to institutionalize digital collaborative care models at scale32.

Another important next step is to rigorously evaluate the economic benefit of digitally enabled collaborative care. Our preliminary back-of-the-envelope calculation suggested potential outpatient savings of approximately 1.53 million CNY per quarter, and if extrapolated citywide, the corresponding annual savings would be approximately 97 million CNY (eMethods 14). Although simplified, these calculations suggest that this digital care model could produce meaningful economic benefits alongside its clinical effects. These estimates should be interpreted cautiously as they were derived from retrospective averages rather than formal economic evaluation. Future studies should use comprehensive economic analyese to quantify impacts on healthcare expenditures, medical insurance spending, and long-term cost-effectiveness using real-world data and decision-analytic approaches such as Markov modeling.

In conclusion, using routinely collected electronic health record data and a quasi-experimental design, we found that digitally enabled GP-specialist collaborative care improved hypertension outcomes and reduced upward referrals in primary care settings. Our findings support digitally enabled GP-specialist collaborative care as a promising strategy to strengthen primary care and improve chronic disease management. Achieving sustainable scale, however, will still require complementary payment, implementation, and workforce policies that align incentives across the health system.

Methods

Study setting and design

Shenzhen is a major megacity in southeastern China with a population of 20 million and the third-largest GDP following Beijing and Shanghai. As China’s leading technology and innovation hub, Shenzhen has frequently served as a pilot city for national reforms and dgitial innovation. These characteristics make Shenzhen an ideal setting for evaluating innovative models of digitally enabled collaborative care and generating evidence for broader policy implementation.

The digitally enabled GP-specialist collaborative care model was piloted in Longhua District since January 2022. We adopted a quasi-experimental design with a difference-in-differences (DID) approach to examine the effects of this digital care model on BP outcomes and referral patterns among patients with hypertension managed in community health centers33. Given that randomized controlled trials are often infeasible for evaluating policy interventions, quasi-experimental methods including DID are widely used and have been shown to yield estimates comparable to randomized trials34.

The policy-exposed group, that is, the treatment group, included hypertensive patients managed in all 113 community health centers in Longhua District. We selected Longgang District, an adjacent district that had no similar policy being implemented, as the control district. The control group therefore included hypertensive patients managed in all 164 health centers in Longgang District. Longgang District was selected for the following reasons: (1) Longgang is geographically adjacent to Longhua, sharing similar resources and economic networks; (2) both districts were part of the former Bao’an County until 1993, with parallel urban development; (3) they rank second and third in population among Shenzhen’s ten districts, with comparable GDP level and medical resources; and (4) no similar digital integration policy was implemented in Longgang during the study period.

The pre-implementation period was defined as 2021 and the post-implementation period as 2022 and 2023. We compared changes in patient BP outcomes and referrals in treatment group before and after the implementation of the digital collaborative care model to the changes in control group over the same period. The DID approach allows us to disentangle the policy effects from the preexisting differences in outcomes between treatment and control groups before the policy was implemented and control for any group-specific, time-invariant unobserved factors that could have affected the outcomes, thereby enabling a more credible estimation of causal impact35. The DID apporach assumes parallel outcome trends between treatment and control groups, stable group composition in repeated cross-sectional design, and no spillover effects36.

A detailed description of study design and statistics comparison between Longhua and Longgang districts can be found in the Supplementary Information file (eIntroduction 1–2). The detailed analytical plan is also presented in the Supplementary Information file (eMethods 1–4).

Study population

Patients were included if they were aged ≥18 years, diagnosed with hypertension, enrolled in the hypertension management program, actively managed at community health centers, had at least one follow-up encounter record during the study period of 2021–2023, and residing in either Longhua or Longgang districts. A total of 107,712 eligible adult patients were identified, comprising 52,355 in the treatment group and 55,357 in the control group.

Data extraction

We extracted 2021–2023 electronic health records for eligible adults with hypertension at all 277 community health centers in Longhua and Longgang Districts from the Shenzhen Community Health Service Information System, administered by the Shenzhen Health Commission. The data capture detailed information of each follow-up encounter, including date of visit, blood pressure readings, and referral records. Patient characteristics were also extracted, including sociodemographic characteristics, family history of hypertension, date of enrolling in hypertension management program, and comorbid program enrollment status.

We constructed the data as a longitudinal person-quarter panel, with each patient observed for up to 12 quarters between 2021 and 2023. Data were extracted in September 2024 and analyzed from September 2024 to August 2025.

Outcome measures

The primary outcomes were (1) blood pressure levels, measured as mean systolic and diastolic blood pressure per patient per quarter; and (2) blood pressure control, measured as a binary variable equal to 1 if the patient’s quarterly mean systolic and diastolic blood pressure readings were both below the age-specific clinical target threshold: <140/90 mmHg for patients aged under 65 years and <150/90 mmHg for patients aged 65 years or older37, and 0 otherwise.

The secondary outcome was upward referral by GPs, measured as a binary indicator equal to 1 if the patient had at least one referral record to hospitals in a given quarter, and 0 otherwise. Under the NBPHS follow-up guidelines, a main indication for upward referral is poor blood pressure control, defined as failure to achieve target blood pressure after two consecutive follow-up visits.

Covariates

We controlled for a range of patient-level covariates. Sociodemographic characteristics included age, sex (male or female), educational level (primary school and below, middle school and equivalent, high school and equivalent, and college and above), employment status (employed or unemployed), marital status (married vs non-married/widowed/divorced), and source of payment (Urban Residence Basic Medical Insurance, Urban Employee Basic Medical Insurance, or self-pay). Family history of hypertension was defined as having at least one first-degree relative with a documented diagnosis of hypertension. Comorbid program enrollment status included enrollment in the diabetes management program and the older adult health management program under the National Public Health Services Program core services. The diabetes management indicator equaled 1 only for patients with a confirmed diagnosis of diabetes who were enrolled in the diabetes management program, and 0 otherwise. The older adult health management indicator equaled 1 for patients who were enrolled in the older adult health management program, and 0 otherwise.

Statistical analyses

To further account for the baseline between-group differences, we applied inverse probability of treatment weighting based on propensity scores to balance out observed baseline covariates between the treatment and control groups. Propensity scores were estimated using logistic regression models incorporating covariates as described earlier. Weights were calculated as the inverse of the probability of receiving the treatment that each patient actually received38,39. We then multiplied the weight by each patient’s duration of program enrollment to account for the cumulative effects of sustained hypertensive care. We stabilized these weights to a mean of 1 and truncated them at the 99th percentile to avoid the influence of extreme weights. Covariate balance before and after weighting was assessed using standardized mean differences (SMD), with a threshold of less than 0.1 considering adequate balance.

We fitted a multivariate linear regression model with district and year-quarter fixed effects, controlling for all observed patient characteristics and adjusting for inverse probability of treatment weights and duration of program enrollment, with robust standard errors applied. In the model, outcome variables were the quarterly mean diastolic and systolic blood pressure levels, whether patients achieving BP control and being referred to hospital within the quarter. For the two binary outcomes, we used linear probability models, so that the estimated coefficients can be directly interpreted as absolute changes in probability. The DID estimate represents an interaction term of a dummy variable indicating patients from Longhua District and a dummy variable indicating post-implementation period (2022–23). Additionally, we estimated event study regressions to evaluate the dynamic treatment effects and whether the parallel trends assumption required for DID models appeared reasonable.

We then estimated our DID model separately by subgroups as follows: age (18-44 years, 45–54 years, 55–64 years, versus ≥65 years), sex (male versus female), education level (primary school or below, middle school, high school, versus college or above), employment status (employed versus unemployed), family history of hypertension (yes or no), source of payment (Urban Residence Basic Medical Insurance, Urban Employee Basic Medical Insurance, versus self-pay or others), and comorbid program enrollment (diabetes or older adult health management).

Per the NBPHS care standards, regular follow-up was defined as a patient had at least one follow-up visit per quarter with a minimum of 4 visits within a year17. In practice, however, many patients do not fully adhere to this follow-up schedule and therefore receive less routine management services than those who meet the standard. Because follow-up adherence is closely related to the intensity of usual care and has been shown to be associated with clinical outcomes in chronic disease management40, it is important to also examine the degree to which the outcomes differed between groups by patients’ follow-up adherence. We further estimated a triple-difference (DDD) model by adding a three-way interaction of the policy, post-implementation period, and patient follow-up adherence indicator (where 0 = full follow-up adherence, i.e., patients who had at least one follow-up visit in each quarter and at least four visits within a year, and 1 = all other patients with suboptimal follow-up adherence). The triple interaction term captures whether the post-implementation change in patient outcomes in treated district relative to untreated district was different between patients who were fully compliant with NBPHS follow-up standards and those with suboptimal follow-up adherence. This could help clarify whether this digital collaborative care model reinforces NBPHS-based standard care or complements care for patients with suboptimal engagement in the NBPHS.

Two-tailed P-values were calculated using t tests, and significance was set at P < 0.05. All analyses were conducted using Stata, version 18.

The detailed analytical approach and all model specifications are presented in the Supplementary Information file (eMethods 1–4).

Sensitivity analyses

We conducted various sensitivity analyses to test the robustness of our findings, including (1) regressions without adjustment for control variables; (2) regressions without inverse probability of treatment weighting; (3) regressions without any analytical weights; (4) propensity score matching; (5) excluding patients observed only before or only after the policy implementation; (6) excluding patients with less than six months of enrollment or those who dropped out the program during the study period; (7) using the last recorded blood pressure value per quarter as the outcome; and (8) re-estimating the results using probit regression model for binary outcome variables. Results were consistent across all specifications and are presented in the Supplementary Information file (eMethods 5–12).

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths. First, although digital interventions and team-based care have been widely studied, evidence on large-scale, digitally enabled collaborative care models remains limited. Our study provides real-world evidence on a district-wide, digitally enabled GP-specialist collaborative care model implemented under routine primary care practice14. Second, we used a difference-in-differences approach combined with event study methodology to control for temporal and individual-level variations. We also applied inverse probability of treatment weighting to balance baseline characteristics between groups. These methods strengthen causal inference by ensuring that observed differences in patient outcomes can be more confidently attributed to the policy rather than to temporal trends or preexisting differences. Additional robustness checks further support the credibility of our estimated effects. Third, our analysis used electronic health record data with repeated blood pressure measurements collected over longitudinal follow-up, allowing assessment of sustained intervention effects over time. Finally, unlike previous studies focusing primarily on clinical outcomes, we evaluated the effects on both blood pressure outcomes and referral patterns, providing evidence on both patient- and system-level outcomes with broader implications for primary care policy and practice.

Several limitations should also be acknowledged. First, although we used a quasi-experimental design, the observational nature of the data warrants caution in making causal interpretations. Second, the lack of information on physician prescribing behavior, patient medication adherence, and health behaviors (e.g., smoking, physical activity) limited our ability to identify the mechanisms underlying the observed effects. Third, since we used data from only two districts in Shenzhen, caution should be taken when generalizing the findings to other regions or healthcare settings. Fourth, while our methodological approach partially accounted for unobserved time-invariant factors, residual confounding due to other policy changes or contextual time-varying factors may remain.
