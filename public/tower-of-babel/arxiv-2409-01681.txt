Static Nuel Games with Terminal Payoff
Symeon Mastrakoulis, Athanasios Kehagias

Abstract
In this paper we study a variant of the Nuel game (a generalization of the duel ) which is played in turns by N N players. In each turn a single player must fire at one of the other players and has a certain probability of hitting and killing his target. The players shoot in a fixed sequence and when a player is eliminated, the “move” passes to the next surviving player. The winner is the last surviving player. We prove that, for every N ≥ 2 N\geq 2 , the Nuel has a stationary Nash equilibrium and provide algorithms for its computation.

The

-uel Game

We will now define the
N
-uel game rigorously for every

2.1
States and Actions

Each

has the form
\mathbf{s}=s_{0}s_{1}...s_{N}
, where

and, for

,

(resp. 1) means that
P_{n}
is dead (resp.
alive). Now suppose that at time
t\in\left\{0,1,2,...\right\}
the game
state is
\mathbf{s}\left(t\right)=s_{0}\left(t\right)...s_{N}\left(t\right)
. We have the following possibilities.

1.

If
s_{0}\left(t\right)=n\in\left\{1,...,N\right\}
, then the
game is in progress and
P_{n}
“has the
move”, i.e.,
P_{n}
is the single player who will shoot in
the current turn. In this case we will also have
s_{1}\left(t\right)...s_{N}\left(t\right)\in\left\{0,1\right\}^{N}
;

s_{i}\left(t\right)=1
means that
P_{i}
is alive and
s_{i}\left(t\right)=0

means that he is dead.

2.

If
s_{0}\left(t\right)=0
, then the game has terminated (i.e.,
there is a single alive player) and we must actually have
s_{1}\left(t\right)...s_{N}\left(t\right)=0...010...0
; i.e., there exists a
single
n
such that
s_{n}\left(t\right)=1
(
P_{n}
is the single alive
player) and, for
m\neq n
,
s_{m}\left(t\right)=0
(
P_{m}
is dead).

We exclude from consideration

states, i.e.,
states which will never be visited during a play of the game

2

\displaystyle\forall k

\displaystyle\in\left\{2,...,N\right\}:S_{k}=\left\{\mathbf{s}=s_{0}s_{1}...s_{N}:s_{0}\neq 0\text{ and }s_{s_{0}}=1\text{ and }\sum_{n=1}^{N}s_{n}=k\right\},

\displaystyle\forall k

\displaystyle\in\left\{1,...,N\right\}:\widetilde{S}_{k}=\left\{\mathbf{s}=0s_{1}...s_{N}:\text{and }s_{k}=1\text{ and }s_{n}=0\text{ for

2.2
State Transitions

The game evolution is described in terms of state transitions.

1.

The game starts at some initial state
\mathbf{s}\left(0\right)=s_{0}\left(0\right)s_{1}\left(0\right)s_{2}\left(0\right)...s_{N}\left(0\right)
.

2.

Assume at

\mathbf{s}\left(t-1\right)=s_{0}\left(t-1\right)s_{1}\left(t-1\right)...,s_{N}\left(t-1\right)

(a)

For the

\displaystyle\Pr\left(s_{m}\left(t\right)=0\right)=p_{n}\text{ (i.e., }P_{n}\text{ hit and killed }P_{m}\text{),}

\displaystyle\forall i\in\left\{1,...,m-1,m+1,...,N\right\}:\Pr\left(s_{i}\left(t\right)=s_{i}\left(t-1\right)\right)=1,

The

3.

Finally, if
\mathbf{s}\left(t-1\right)=0...010...0

Using the above rules, for every

with state space

\displaystyle\forall\mathbf{s}^{\prime}

\displaystyle\in S\backslash S_{1}:\Pi_{\mathbf{s}^{\prime},\mathbf{s}^{\prime\prime}}\left(a\right)=\Pr\left(\mathbf{s}\left(t\right)=\mathbf{s}^{\prime\prime}|\mathbf{s}\left(t-1\right)=\mathbf{s}^{\prime},a\left(t\right)=a\right),

The state sequence obtained from the above controlled Markov chain corresponds

Because in each turn a player must shoot (unless he is the sole survivor) and

p_{n}>0

Proposition 2.1

For all
N
, the probability that the
N
-uel
terminates in finite time equals one.

The above complete the description of the

-uel, which we will also denote

We now proceed to define and discuss

In view of Proposition

\mathbf{h}\left(t\right)\mathbf{=}\mathbf{s}\left(0\right)a\left(1\right)\mathbf{s}\left(1\right)...\mathbf{s}\left(t\right)

For

\forall n\in\left\{1,...,N\right\},\forall\mathbf{h}\in H:\mathsf{Q}_{n}\left(\mathbf{h}\right)=\left\{\begin{array}[c]{ll}1&\text{iff }\mathbf{h=}\mathbf{s}\left(0\right)a\left(1\right)...\mathbf{s}\left(t\right)\in H_{0}\text{ and
}s_{n}\left(t\right)=1\text{,}\\

I.e., upon game termination, the single surviving player receives one payoff unit.

Let

denote the set of
N

\mathbf{x}=\left(x_{1},...,x_{N}\right)
. A

is a function

\sigma:H_{1}\rightarrow\Delta_{N}\cup\left\{\left(0,...,0\right)\right\}

\mathbf{h}=\mathbf{s}(0)a(1)...a(t-1)ns1...sN

\forall m\in\left\{1,...,N\right\}:x_{nm}=\Pr\left(\text{\textquotedblleft}P_{n}\text{ shoots }P_{m}\text{\textquotedblright}\right).

at the end of a terminal history no shooting takes place. We will only

, i.e., those which assign positive
probability only to admissible actions

3

\displaystyle=ms_{1}...s_{N}\in S_{K}:V_{i,\mathbf{s}}^{\left(t+1\right)}=\left(1-p_{m}\right)V_{i,\mathbf{N}(\mathbf{s})}^{(t)}+\sum_{n\in L_{m}(\mathbf{s})}x_{\mathbf{s},n}p_{m}V_{i,\mathbf{N}_{n}(\mathbf{s})}

\displaystyle\forall\mathbf{s}

\displaystyle=ms_{1}...s_{N}\in S_{K}:U_{i,\mathbf{s}}^{\left(t+1\right)}=\left(1-p_{m}\right)U_{i,\mathbf{N}(\mathbf{s})}^{(t)}+\sum_{n\in L_{m}(\mathbf{s})}x_{\mathbf{s},n}p_{m}V_{i,\mathbf{N}_{n}(\mathbf{s})}

We then have

\displaystyle\forall\mathbf{s}=ms_{1}...s_{N}\in S_{K}:\left|V_{i,\mathbf{s}}^{\left(t+1\right)}-U_{i,\mathbf{s}}^{\left(t+1\right)}\right|=\left(1-p_{m}\right)\left|V_{i,\mathbf{N}(\mathbf{s})}^{(t)}-U_{i,\mathbf{N}(\mathbf{s})}^{(t)}\right|\Rightarrow

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathbf{s}\in S_{K}}\left|V_{i,\mathbf{s}}^{\left(t+1\right)}-U_{i,\mathbf{s}}^{\left(t+1\right)}\right|\leq\sum_{\mathbf{s}\in S_{K}}\left(1-\min_{m}p_{m}\right)\left|V_{i,\mathbf{N}(\mathbf{s})}^{(t)}-U_{i,\mathbf{N}(\mathbf{s})}^{(t)}\right|\Rightarrow

\displaystyle\sum_{m\in L\left(\mathbf{s}\right)}\left|V_{i,\mathbf{s}}^{\left(t+1\right)}-U_{i,\mathbf{s}}^{\left(t+1\right)}\right|\leq\left(1-\min_{m}p_{m}\right)\sum_{\mathbf{s}\in S_{K}}\left|V_{i,\mathbf{N}(\mathbf{s})}^{(t)}-U_{i,\mathbf{N}(\mathbf{s})}^{(t)}\right|\Rightarrow

\displaystyle\sum_{m\in L\left(\mathbf{s}\right)}\left|V_{i,\mathbf{s}}^{\left(t+1\right)}-U_{i,\mathbf{s}}^{\left(t+1\right)}\right|\leq\left(1-\min_{m}p_{m}\right)^{t+1}\sum_{\mathbf{s}\in S_{K}}\left|V_{i,\mathbf{N}(\mathbf{s})}^{(0)}-U_{i,\mathbf{N}(\mathbf{s})}^{(0)}\right|.

Since
\left|1-\min_{m}p_{m}\right|\in\left(0,1\right)
, we
have

\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\sum_{m\in L\left(\mathbf{s}\right)}\left|V_{i,\mathbf{s}}^{\left(t+1\right)}-U_{i,\mathbf{s}}^{\left(t+1\right)}\right|=0

This means that, for every
i
and
\mathbf{s}
, the iteration tends to a
unique limit

\forall i,\mathbf{s}:\overline{V}_{i,\mathbf{s}}=\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\sum_{m\in L\left(\mathbf{s}\right)}V_{i,\mathbf{s}}^{\left(t\right)}

and we have

\forall i,\mathbf{s}:\overline{V}_{i,\mathbf{s}}=\left(1-p_{m}\right)\overline{V}_{i,\mathbf{N}(\mathbf{s})}+\sum_{n\in L_{m}(\mathbf{s})}x_{\mathbf{s},n}p_{m}V_{i,\mathbf{N}_{n}(\mathbf{s})}.

In other words
\overline{V}_{i,S_{K}}
satisfies the payoff equations, which
means that the iteration yields the unique solution of the payoff system.

It should be pointed out that Algorithms 1 and 2 are
guaranteed
to work
(i.e., solve the
N
-uel) when the
p_{n}
marksmanships belong to
\left(0,1\right)
; but the algorithms
may
also work even when some of the

p_{n}
’s are equal to zero or to one.

Proposition 4.1

,

,

Proof.
It suffices to consider
P_{1}

when the game is in state

x_{12}
and
x_{13}

V_{1,1111}=\frac{A_{1}+A_{2}+A_{3}-p_{1}A_{2}-p_{2}A_{3}-p_{1}A_{3}+p_{1}p_{2}A_{3}}{1-\left(1-p_{1}\right)\left(1-p_{2}\right)\left(1-p_{3}\right)}

\displaystyle F_{1}\left(x_{12},x_{13}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{A_{1}}{1-\left(1-p_{1}\right)\left(1-p_{2}\right)\left(1-p_{3}\right)}

\displaystyle=\frac{x_{12}p_{1}V_{1,3101}+x_{13}p_{1}V_{1,2110}}{1-\left(1-p_{1}\right)\left(1-p_{2}\right)\left(1-p_{3}\right)}

\displaystyle=\frac{x_{12}p_{1}\frac{p_{1}\left(1-p_{3}\right)}{p_{1}+p_{3}-p_{1}p_{3}}+x_{13}p_{1}\frac{p_{1}\left(1-p_{2}\right)}{p_{1}+p_{2}-p_{1}p_{2}}}{1-\left(1-p_{1}\right)\left(1-p_{2}\right)\left(1-p_{3}\right)}

. Since

\displaystyle p_{2}

\displaystyle>p_{3}\Leftrightarrow\left(1-p_{3}\right)\left(p_{1}+p_{2}-p_{1}p_{2}\right)>\left(1-p_{2}\right)\left(p_{1}+p_{3}-p_{1}p_{3}\right)

\displaystyle p_{2}

\displaystyle<p_{3}\Leftrightarrow\left(1-p_{3}\right)\left(p_{1}+p_{2}-p_{1}p_{2}\right)<\left(1-p_{2}\right)\left(p_{1}+p_{3}-p_{1}p_{3}\right)

\displaystyle\text{if }p_{2}

(17)

\displaystyle<p_{3}\text{ then }\widehat{x}_{12}=0\text{, }\widehat{x}_{13}=1

\displaystyle V_{1,2111}

\displaystyle=\frac{A_{2}+A_{3}+A_{1}-p_{2}A_{3}-p_{3}A_{1}-p_{2}A_{1}+p_{2}p_{3}A_{1}}{1-\left(1-p_{1}\right)\left(1-p_{2}\right)\left(1-p_{3}\right)}

Proposition 4.2

For every
N\in\left\{3,4,...,\right\}
, the
N
-uel has a
stationary deterministic Nash equilibrium.

Proof.
It suffices to consider
P_{1}
’s problem of determining his equilibrium
strategy
\widehat{\sigma}_{1}
. The proof will be inductive.

For all states
\mathbf{s}\in S_{3}
,
P_{1}
can determine the value of his
optimal strategy
\widehat{\sigma}_{1}\left(\mathbf{s}\right)
as follows.
For every such state in which he is not alive he has no strategy choice. For
every state in which he is alive, he must solve a
3
-uel against the other
two alive players; he can do this without considering the value of

\widehat{\sigma}_{1}\left(\mathbf{s}\right)
for states
\mathbf{s}\not\in S_{1}\cup S_{2}\cup S_{3}
.

Suppose that
P_{1}
has determined
\widehat{\sigma}_{1}\left(\mathbf{s}\right)
for all
\mathbf{s}\in\cup_{k=3}^{K-1}S_{k}
; now  he

\ \widehat{\sigma}_{1}\left(\mathbf{s}\right)
for all
states
\mathbf{s}\in S_{K}
. He has nothing to determine for states

\mathbf{s}\in S_{K}
in which he is not alive. There exist
\binom{N-1}{K-1}

sets of states with
P_{1}
and
K-1
other players are alive. Let
S^{\prime}
be any such set and let the alive players be
n_{1},...,n_{K}
; in

n_{1}=1
. With an appropriate state reordering, we can write

as

S^{\prime}=\left\{\mathbf{s}_{1},...,\mathbf{s}_{K}\right\}

where
P_{n_{k}}
is the player having the move in
\mathbf{s}_{k}
; in

\mathbf{s}_{1}
is the state in which
P_{1}
has the move. Now,
letting

\forall k\in\left\{1,...,K\right\}:\left\{\begin{array}[c]{l}Z_{k}=V_{1,\mathbf{s}_{k}}\\

the following payoff system must be satisfied

\left[\begin{array}[c]{cccc}1&-\left(1-p_{n_{1}}\right)&...&0\\

...&...&...&...\\
-\left(1-p_{n_{K}}\right)&0&...&1\end{array}\right]\left[\begin{array}[c]{c}Z_{1}\\
Z_{2}\\
...\\
Z_{K}\end{array}\right]=\left[\begin{array}[c]{c}A_{1}\\
A_{2}\\

A_{K}\end{array}\right].

(18)

The
Z_{k}
’s are the unknowns and, since each state
\mathbf{N}_{i}\left(\mathbf{s}_{k}\right)

belongs to
S_{K-1}
, the
A_{k}
’s are known (but depending on
the
x_{\mathbf{s}_{k},i}
’s).
P_{1}
wants to maximize

(for all

). We can

\left|\begin{array}[c]{cccc}1&1-p_{n_{1}}&...&0\\
0&1&...&0\\
...&...&...&...\\
1-p_{n_{K}}&0&...&1\end{array}\right|=1-\left(1-p_{n_{1}}\right)\left(1-p_{n_{2}}\right)...\left(1-p_{n_{K}}\right)>0,

and, expanding with respect to the first column, we have

A_{2}&1&1-p_{n_{2}}&...&0\\
A_{3}&0&1&...&0\\

A_{K}&0&0&...&1\end{array}\right|}{1-\left(1-p_{n_{1}}\right)\left(1-p_{n_{2}}\right)...\left(1-p_{n_{K}}\right)}

(19)

or

\displaystyle V_{1,\mathbf{s}_{1}}=Z_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{A_{1}\left|\begin{array}[c]{cccc}1&1-p_{n_{2}}&...&0\\
0&1&...&0\\

0&0&...&1\end{array}\right|-A_{2}D_{2}+A_{3}D_{3}-...}{1-\left(1-p_{n_{1}}\right)\left(1-p_{n_{2}}\right)...\left(1-p_{n_{K}}\right)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{i\in L_{1}\left(\mathbf{s}_{1}\right)}x_{\mathbf{s}_{1},i}p_{n_{1}}V_{1,\mathbf{N}_{i}\left(\mathbf{s}_{1}\right)}+B}{1-\left(1-p_{n_{1}}\right)\left(1-p_{n_{2}}\right)...\left(1-p_{n_{K}}\right)}.

k\in\left\{2,...,K\right\}

’s shooting probabilities

\displaystyle\widehat{i}

\displaystyle=\arg\max_{i\in L_{1}\left(\mathbf{s}_{1}\right)}V_{1,\mathbf{N}_{i}\left(\mathbf{s}_{1}\right)},

(20)

\displaystyle\forall i

\displaystyle\neq\widehat{i}:x_{\mathbf{s}_{1},i}=0\text{ and }x_{\mathbf{s}_{1},\widehat{i}}=1.

(21)

is understood as the smallest

k\in\left\{2,...,K\right\}

P_{1}
’s equilibrium strategy

\sigma_{1}
. The proof for all other players works the same way.

of rules
\mathbf{R}_{k}
(for

):

shoots at some

-uel with highest payoff to

What we have proved is that, for all

and all

-uel.

The above proof also furnishes an algorithm for computing

function

(

,

6
Conclusion

We have studied a
N
-uel game (a generalization of the
duel
) in which
finding a Nash equilibrium reduces to solving the system of nonlinear payoff
equations. We have proved that this system has a solution (hence the

N
-uel has a stationary Nash equilibrium) and we have provided
algorithms for its computational solution. In the future we want to study

1.

the existence and computation of
nonstationary
Nash equilibria.

2.

the properties of the
N
-uel variant in which each player can

abstain
, i.e., not shoot at any of his opponents.
