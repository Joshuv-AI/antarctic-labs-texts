Maternal Outcomes in Referred vs. Non-Referred Obstetric Cases: A Comparative Evaluation in a Tertiary Care Center
Authors: Priyanka Patel, Niharika Prasad, Seema Methre, Talha Saad, Sunil Kumar Saxena, Punya Pratap Singh, Raghav Gupta
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background:Referred obstetric cases often present with complications due to delayed intervention, posing increased risks to maternal health. This study aims to evaluate and compare maternal outcomes between referred and non-referred deliveries at a tertiary care center.Methods:A prospective observational study was conducted on 300 pregnant women admitted for delivery, equally divided into referred and non-referred groups. Data on delivery type, complications, and maternal morbidity were collected and analyzed using SPSS.Results:Cesarean delivery was significantly higher in referred cases (68%) than in non-referred cases (42%). Maternal complications, such as postpartum hemorrhage (10% vs. 3.3%), ICU admissions (8% vs. 1.3%), and mortality (1.3% vs. 0%), were more frequent in the referred group.Conclusion:Referred obstetric cases are at higher risk of complications. Strengthening peripheral health systems and timely referral are essential to improving maternal outcomes.

INTRODUCTION

Maternal health is a vital indicator of the overall functioning of the healthcare system, especially in developing nations like India. Effective antenatal care, skilled birth attendance, and timely obstetric intervention can significantly reduce maternal morbidity and mortality. However, due to disparities in healthcare access, many pregnant women from rural or peripheral areas are referred to tertiary care centers for the management of high-risk pregnancies or complications. These referrals are often delayed and poorly coordinated, resulting in adverse outcomes for both mother and fetus.[1]

Referred obstetric cases commonly present with conditions, such as hypertensive disorders, obstructed labor, sepsis, or antepartum hemorrhage, often in critical stages. Studies have shown that referred patients have higher rates of cesarean section, blood transfusions, ICU admissions, and even maternal mortality compared to non-referred counterparts who receive routine antenatal care and timely intervention at the same center.[2] The “three delays” model—delays in seeking care, reaching care, and receiving appropriate treatment—has been consistently identified as a significant factor contributing to maternal deaths in referral cases.[3]

This study aims to compare maternal outcomes in referred versus non-referred obstetric cases at a tertiary care center. The objective is to identify the impact of referral delays, evaluate the nature and severity of complications, and recommend evidence-based improvements to the referral and management protocols. Such comparative evaluation is essential to strengthen the referral system and reduce preventable maternal morbidity and mortality.

METHODOLOGY

Study design and setting

This was a hospital-based prospective observational study conducted in the Department of Obstetrics and Gynecology over a period of 12 months.

Study population

A total of 300 pregnant women admitted for delivery were included in the study. The participants were categorized into two groups:

Group A (Referred cases): Women referred from peripheral health centers, district hospitals, or private clinics due to obstetric complications or lack of facilities.

Group A (Referred cases): Women referred from peripheral health centers, district hospitals, or private clinics due to obstetric complications or lack of facilities.

Group B (Non-Referred cases): Women who received antenatal care and delivered directly at the tertiary center without referral.

Group B (Non-Referred cases): Women who received antenatal care and delivered directly at the tertiary center without referral.

Eligibility criteria

The inclusion criteria for this study comprised pregnant women with a gestational age of 28 weeks or more, carrying singleton pregnancies, and admitted to the hospital for delivery, whether by vaginal birth or cesarean section. Participation was voluntary, and only those women who provided informed written consent were enrolled in the study. Exclusion criteria included women with multiple gestations, known congenital fetal anomalies, and those referred solely for nonmedical reasons, such as personal preference, social circumstances, or logistical convenience, as these factors could introduce bias and were not indicative of medical risk necessitating referral.

Data collection

Detailed maternal demographic data, antenatal history, referral source, indication for referral, time delays, clinical condition on arrival, mode of delivery, maternal complications (e.g., PPH, sepsis, ICU admission), and outcomes were recorded using a pre-structured proforma.

RESULTS

A total of 300 pregnant women were included in the study, with 150 in the referred group (Group A) and 150 in the non-referred group (Group B). The mean maternal age was similar between both groups (24.6 ± 3.8 years in Group A vs. 25.1 ± 4.1 years in Group B).

Mode of delivery

Cesarean section was significantly higher in the referred group (68%) compared to the non-referred group (42%) (P < 0.05). Vaginal deliveries were more common in non-referred women (58%) than in referred women (32%) [Table 1].

Maternal complications

Postpartum hemorrhage (PPH) occurred in 15 cases (10%) in the referred group versus five cases (3.3%) in the non-referred group. The need for blood transfusion was also higher among referred cases (18% vs. 6%). Intensive care unit (ICU) admission was required in 12 referred women (8%) compared to only two women (1.3%) in the non-referred group. There were two maternal deaths in the referred group, while no deaths occurred in the non-referred group [Table 2].

DISCUSSION

This study highlights a significant disparity in maternal outcomes between referred and non-referred obstetric cases. Our findings reveal that referred women experienced higher rates of cesarean delivery (68%), postpartum hemorrhage (10%), ICU admission (8%), and maternal mortality (1.3%) compared to their non-referred counterparts. These results suggest that delayed referrals and poor pre-referral stabilization may contribute substantially to maternal morbidity.

Similar findings were reported by Konar and Chaudhuri, who observed that referral cases were more likely to have complications due to late-stage presentations and inadequate management at peripheral centers.[4] In our study, the higher rate of cesarean sections among referred patients was often due to fetal distress, obstructed labor, or hypertensive disorders, as also emphasized by Dwivedi et al.[5] in their comparative evaluation.

ICU admission and blood transfusion needs were significantly higher among the referred group, likely due to advanced-stage complications, such as eclampsia or severe anemia on admission. Jakhar et al. reported a similar trend, highlighting that preventable conditions led to intensive interventions in tertiary centers.[6] The absence of maternal deaths in the non-referred group further underscores the importance of timely antenatal care and well-coordinated obstetric management.

Moreover, Jha et al.[7] noted that strengthening peripheral healthcare services and improving transport logistics are essential to reducing the maternal burden in tertiary centers. There is a need for developing structured referral protocols and early risk stratification in community-level health programs.

The study emphasizes the critical importance of early referral, capacity building at peripheral institutions, and continued surveillance to ensure safer maternal outcomes across all levels of care.

Limitations and future recommendations

This study was limited to a single tertiary care center and may not represent the wider population. Referral delay intervals and reasons were not quantitatively assessed. Future multicentric studies with structured referral audits and time-delay analysis are recommended to develop targeted interventions for reducing maternal morbidity.

CONCLUSION

Referred obstetric cases experience significantly higher maternal morbidity and mortality compared to non-referred cases. Timely referral and adequate pre-referral management are key to improving outcomes. Strengthening the referral system and peripheral healthcare infrastructure is critical.
