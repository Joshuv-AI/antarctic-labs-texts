Identification of TSEN15 as a novel biomarker for diagnosis, prognosis, and immune infiltration in hepatocellular carcinoma
Authors: Li Yu, Dunzhen Chen, Jian Xu, Xichang Zhou, Ning Shi, Zhi Liu, Yu Liu, Yuechao Yu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractTRNA splicing endonuclease subunit 15 (TSEN15) is known for its role in cell cycle regulation, but its involvement in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) remains underexplored. TSEN15 influences mitosis and cancer cell proliferation. Its effects on immune infiltration and prognostic value in HCC demand further study. HCC patient data were analyzed using The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA). TSEN15 expression in HCC tissues was significantly higher than in normal liver tissues. High TSEN15 levels were associated with advanced HCC stages, such as T3, T4, and M1. Survival analyses showed TSEN15 as an independent negative prognostic marker for overall survival. Immune profiling revealed that elevated TSEN15 expression correlates with reduced infiltration of cytotoxic immune cells, including CD8 + T cells, NK cells, and dendritic cells. Functional enrichment analyses linked TSEN15 to pathways promoting tumor progression. Results were validated through Western blotting, RT-qPCR, and cellular assays. TSEN15 serves as a novel biomarker for HCC diagnosis and prognosis, indicating its potential as a target for therapeutic interventions. Introduction

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is the sixth most common type of primary cancer worldwide and remains a significant global health challenge, accounting for 80–90% of all liver cancers1. According to the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC), approximately 1.4 million new cases of liver cancer are projected to be diagnosed by 20402. Despite advancements in cancer treatments, the overall prognosis for patients remains poor, with a 5-year survival rate of only 12%3. Most patients are diagnosed at intermediate or advanced stages, and systemic therapies, such as tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs), show limited efficacy4. In recent years, immunotherapy, particularly immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) targeting PD-1/PD-L1, has rapidly advanced. These therapies work by stimulating the body’s immune system to enhance anti-tumor responses within the tumor microenvironment, slowing tumor progression. However, the benefits are still restricted to a select group of patients, highlighting the complexity of the liver cancer immune microenvironment5,6. This underscores the urgent need to identify new or complementary therapeutic targets for the effective treatment of liver cancer7,8.

All nuclear-encoded transfer RNAs (tRNAs) undergo processing and modification to form functional aminoacylated tRNAs9. In humans, among the 429 high-confidence predicted tRNA genes, 28 contain introns, which must be spliced out from precursor tRNAs (pre-tRNAs)10. The removal of introns and the ligation of exons are catalyzed by two multi-protein complexes: the tRNA splicing endonuclease (TSEN) and the tRNA ligase complex11,12. The human TSEN complex consists of two catalytic subunits, TSEN2 and TSEN34, as well as two structural subunits, TSEN15 and TSEN5413. tRNA splicing endonuclease subunit 15 (TSEN15) has been widely studied as a regulator of the cell division cycle14,15. Previous studies have reported that TSEN15 regulates mitosis and directly influences the proliferation of breast cancer and neuroblastoma cells16,17. However, its role in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) remains poorly understood and warrants further investigation.

Several lines of evidence suggest TSEN15 may play a crucial role in HCC pathogenesis. First, as a key regulator of cell cycle progression, TSEN15 may contribute to the uncontrolled proliferation that characterizes HCC development. Second, preliminary bioinformatics analyses of HCC transcriptomic data have revealed aberrant expression of TSEN15 in tumor tissues compared to normal liver tissues. Third, given the established link between tRNA processing defects and oncogenic transformation in various cancers, it is plausible that TSEN15-mediated tRNA splicing may represent a previously unexplored mechanism in hepatocarcinogenesis18. Furthermore, the potential involvement of TSEN15 in modulating the HCC immune microenvironment warrants particular attention, as dysregulated tRNA processing has been implicated in altering tumor immunogenicity and immune cell function in other cancer types19.

Recent advances in HCC research have highlighted the value of identifying novel molecular targets through integrated bioinformatics approaches. Comprehensive bioinformatics analyses have identified multiple hub genes critically involved in HCC pathogenesis, providing a framework for understanding disease progression and identifying potential therapeutic targets20. Additionally, studies investigating specific molecular markers, such as Vav1, have demonstrated their prognostic significance in HCC, with higher expression levels correlating with unfavorable clinicopathological features and poorer survival outcomes21. These findings underscore the importance of exploring novel molecular regulators in HCC to improve patient stratification and treatment strategies.

The aim of this study is to explore the transcriptional expression and prognostic significance of TSEN15 in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) using interactive tools. By employing bioinformatics technologies, we examine the relationship between TSEN15 expression and its clinical pathological characteristics, prognostic implications, and immune cell infiltration in HCC. Such research could help clinicians improve treatment strategies and overall prognosis for patients diagnosed with HCC.

Results

High expression of TSEN15 in hepatocellular carcinoma

Pan-cancer analysis revealed that the expression level of TSEN15 is significantly higher in most tumors compared to normal tissues, including hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), cholangiocarcinoma, breast cancer, and squamous cell carcinoma of the lung (Fig. 1A). In HCC cells, TSEN15 expression was notably higher than in normal tissues (Fig. 1B). Furthermore, the expression level of TSEN15 in HCC tissues was significantly higher than that in paired adjacent tissues (Fig. 1C). To assess the predictive value of TSEN15 for HCC, we performed Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve analysis, which demonstrated a high accuracy of TSEN15 expression in predicting the presence of HCC, with an Area Under the Curve (AUC) value of 0.961 (Fig. 1D). The ability of TSEN15 to distinguish HCC patients with high accuracy further supports its potential as a reliable biomarker for the early diagnosis of HCC.

Expression levels of TSEN15 in tumors. (A) TSEN15 is highly expressed in many solid tumors, including HCC (B, C). The ROC curve area is 0.961 (D), indicating that TSEN15 is a potential biomarker for HCC diagnosis.p-values were calculated using a two-tailed unpaired Student’s t-test, **p < 0.05, ***p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001.

TSEN15 promotes hepatocellular carcinoma progression in vitro

To investigate the presence and distribution of TSEN15 in HCC patients, we first validated TSEN protein expression in human liver cancer tissues. Hematoxylin and eosin (HE) staining was used to identify tumor and non-tumor tissues, and immunohistochemical results showed that TSEN15 expression was significantly higher in tumor tissues than in non-tumor tissues (Fig. 2A). Western blot analysis of protein expression in HCC and matched normal tissues confirmed that TSEN15 was notably elevated in tumor tissues (Fig. 2B). RT-qPCR analysis of TSEN15 mRNA levels yielded similar results (Fig. 2C).The reliability of the RT-qPCR quantification was supported by validation of the reference gene stability under our experimental conditions (Supplementary Figure S1A-B). Furthermore, RT-qPCR analysis of TSEN15 mRNA in HCC cell lines revealed the highest expression in the HEP G2 cell line, and confocal immunofluorescence showed that TSEN15 was predominantly localized in the cell nucleus (Fig. 2D-E). Consequently, we transfected HEP G2/3B cells with sh-TSEN15 and assessed transfection efficiency to evaluate its functional impact (Fig. 2F-G). All functional experiments were performed with three independent biological replicates to ensure reproducibility (Supplementary Figure S2A-E).The results showed that knocking down TSEN15 significantly reduced tumor cell proliferation (Fig. 2H-J) and migration (Fig. 2K-L). These findings suggest that TSEN15 plays a critical role in the proliferation and migration of liver cancer cells.

Correlation between TSEN15 expression and clinical variables

The study found that high expression of TSEN15 is significantly associated with pathological staging. Higher TSEN15 gene expression correlates with larger tumor size, greater extent of tumor spread, and an increased tendency for metastasis (Fig. 3A-C), although it shows a weaker correlation with lymph node metastasis (Fig. 3D). This suggests that TSEN15 expression is closely related to the proliferation and metastatic potential of tumor cells. Additionally, elevated TSEN15 expression is strongly associated with liver fibrosis, particularly with mild to moderate cirrhosis (Fig. 3E-F). Furthermore, high TSEN15 expression is also indicative of more severe inflammation surrounding the tumor (Fig. 3G).

Correlation between TSEN15 expression and clinical variables. (A) pathological staging, (B–D) T, M, N staging, (E) Child-Pugh grade, (F) Fibrosis Ishak score, and (G) inflammation staging. p-values were determined using two-tailed unpaired Student’s t-test, **p < 0.05, ***p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001.

Prognostic analysis of TSEN15 expression in clinical HCC patients

To better understand the relationship between TSEN15 gene expression and prognosis in HCC patients, we used the Kaplan-Meier (KM) method to assess the correlation. Patients were divided into two groups based on TSEN15 expression, using the minimum p-value of TSEN expression as the cutoff point: low-expression and high-expression groups. We found that patients in the low-expression TSEN15 group had significantly better overall survival (OS), longer progression-free interval (PFI), and lower disease-specific survival (DSS) rates compared to those in the high-expression group (Figs. 4A-C). Notably, the prognostic significance of TSEN15 expression remained consistent when assessed using alternative cutoff strategies, supporting the robustness of our findings (Supplementary Figure S3A-C).Additionally, in subgroups based on tumor size (T1/T2 vs. T3/T2), metastasis (M0 vs. M1), and tumor status, high TSEN15 expression was associated with worse prognosis (Fig. 5A-K). To further investigate the prognostic factors in HCC, univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses were conducted. The results identified several independent factors affecting overall survival (OS), including T3, T4 stages, M1 metastasis, and tumor status (Fig. 4D).

Pathway enrichment analysis of TSEN15-related differentially expressed genes (DEGs)

According to the database, a total of 942 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified between the high and low TSEN15 expression groups. Among these, 475 genes were upregulated, accounting for 50.4% of the total, while 467 genes were downregulated, making up 49.5% (|Log2FC|>1, P < 0.05) (Fig. 6A). We then selected the top 10 most significantly different genes from the DEGs to explore their relationship with TSEN15, including TIPRL, ACBD6, PIGC, SMG7, CACYBP, DUSP12, SF3B4, DEDD, STX6, POGK, SLC27A5, PCK1, MT-CO2, TTC36, GSTZ1, GPT, LDHD, ADH1B, HYAL1, CA5A, and AC138356.1. We further analyzed the correlation between these genes and TSEN15 (Fig. 6B). GO enrichment analysis of all DEGs revealed that biological processes (BP) were mainly involved in the mitotic cell cycle. In terms of cellular components (CC), enrichment was observed in the centromere region of chromosomes. For molecular functions (MF), the analysis highlighted carbohydrate binding, monosaccharide binding, and vitamin binding processes (Fig. 6C). Subsequently, GSEA confirmed significant enrichment of the mitotic cell cycle process in the high TSEN15 expression group (Fig. 6D).

The role of TSEN15 in immune infiltration

We explored the correlation between TSEN15 levels and immune cell infiltration, and the results showed a significant negative correlation between high TSEN15 levels and the number of infiltrating immune cells, particularly CD8 + T cells, NK cells, and DC cells (Fig. 7A-B). Additionally, the enrichment scores of CD8 + T cells, NK cells, and DC cells were significantly lower in the high TSEN15 expression group compared to the low TSEN15 expression group, whereas TH2 and T helper cells showed the opposite trend (Fig. 7C-N). Furthermore, using GEPIA, we found that TSEN15 expression was positively correlated with PD-L1 (CD274) expression in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) (P < 0.001, R = 0.16) (Fig. 7O).

To predict the prognosis of HCC patients, we conducted a detailed analysis using various independent factors to generate a comprehensive nomogram. We observed that as the total score on the nomogram increases, indicating the accumulation of adverse factors, the prognosis of HCC patients worsens. This is clearly illustrated in Fig. 8A, where a higher total score is associated with a poorer prognosis. Additionally, we used calibration curves to assess the accuracy and reliability of the nomogram’s predictive performance (Figs. 8B-D).

Discussion

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is the sixth most common primary cancer worldwide, with its incidence steadily increasing, particularly in Asia. Despite the growing array of treatment options for liver cancer, such as tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) and immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), the treatment outcomes remain limited due to the complexity of the liver cancer immune microenvironment and the fact that patients are often diagnosed at advanced stages. Especially for therapies targeting immune evasion mechanisms, although initial progress has been made, the benefiting population remains narrow. Therefore, identifying new therapeutic targets and exploring their relationship with the liver cancer immune microenvironment has become a critical area of research in liver cancer studies.

This study systematically investigated the expression characteristics, clinical pathological relevance, prognostic value, and relationship with immune cell infiltration of the TSEN15 gene in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) through bioinformatics analysis. The results suggest that high expression of TSEN15 in HCC is closely associated with multiple clinical pathological features, tumor progression, and changes in the immune microenvironment, further supporting its potential as a biomarker and therapeutic target. Firstly, pan-cancer analysis revealed that TSEN15 exhibited elevated expression in most cancer types, particularly in hepatocellular carcinoma (LIHC), cholangiocarcinoma, breast cancer, and lung squamous cell carcinoma, with expression levels significantly higher than those in normal tissues. Furthermore, TSEN15 expression was significantly upregulated in HCC tissues compared to normal liver tissues and paired adjacent tissues, indicating that TSEN15 is associated with the initiation and progression of liver cancer. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis demonstrated that TSEN15 expression in HCC patients had high accuracy in predicting the presence of liver cancer, with an area under the ROC curve (AUC) of 0.961, providing strong evidence for its potential as an early diagnostic biomarker. Further clinical pathological analysis revealed that high expression of TSEN15 was significantly associated with the pathological stage, tumor size, extent of spread, and metastatic potential of HCC. Notably, larger tumor size and greater extent of spread correlated with higher TSEN15 expression, suggesting that TSEN15 is linked to tumor aggressiveness potentially through its correlation with cell proliferation and migration. Additionally, we found that high expression of TSEN15 is closely associated with the occurrence of liver fibrosis, particularly in patients with mild and moderate cirrhosis. This suggests that TSEN15 may be involved in the long-term damage to the liver and the early development of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC). The study also indicated that TSEN15 is closely linked to the inflammatory response around the tumor, a phenomenon that may reflect its potential role in the immune microenvironment of HCC. High expression of TSEN15 is also strongly associated with the prognosis of HCC patients. Kaplan-Meier survival curve analysis showed that patients with high TSEN15 expression had significantly lower overall survival (OS) and progression-free survival (PFI) compared to the low expression group, and the disease-specific survival (DSS) rate was higher in the high expression group. This further supports the potential of TSEN15 as an independent risk factor for poor prognosis in HCC. Univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses revealed that high TSEN15 expression is significantly associated with clinical features such as T3, T4 stages, and M1 metastasis, and it is an independent factor influencing the prognosis of HCC patients. Our findings align with recent studies emphasizing the importance of developing robust prognostic models in HCC, such as those based on specific signaling pathways including the PPAR signaling pathway, which have demonstrated good predictive efficacy for patient outcomes22.

In conclusion, high TSEN15 expression is not only closely related to the progression of HCC but may also serve as a novel prognostic biomarker, providing important evidence for the clinical evaluation of HCC prognosis. In the pathway enrichment analysis of differentially expressed genes (DEGs), high expression of TSEN15 was found to be closely associated with processes such as the mitotic phase of the cell cycle, centromere regions of chromosomes, and functions related to carbohydrate binding. The enrichment of these pathways suggests that TSEN15 is correlated with tumor cell proliferation and is linked to cell cycle regulation, thereby being associated with the occurrence and progression of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC). Furthermore, Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) further confirmed that the high expression of TSEN15 significantly enriched the mitotic cell cycle process, providing additional evidence for the < u > association of TSEN15 in hepatocyte proliferation.

In our study on the role of TSEN15 in the immune microenvironment of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), we found that high expression of TSEN15 was significantly negatively correlated with the infiltration of immune cells, especially anti-tumor immune cells such as CD8 + T cells, natural killer (NK) cells, and dendritic cells (DCs). Further analysis revealed that the enrichment scores of immune cell infiltration in the high TSEN15 expression group were significantly lower than those in the low expression group, while TH2 cells and helper T cells (Th) exhibited the opposite trend. These results suggest that TSEN15 expression is associated with immune evasion in liver cancer and correlates with impaired immune cell infiltration, thereby being linked to tumor progression. Additionally, we observed a positive correlation between TSEN15 and the immune checkpoint molecule PD-L1 (CD274), which opens up new avenues for further research into TSEN15 as a potential target for immunotherapy. This finding is particularly relevant in the context of current immunotherapy approaches for HCC, where immune checkpoint inhibitors have revolutionized treatment for advanced disease but also raise important considerations regarding their application in specific clinical settings, including liver transplantation23. The association between TSEN15 and immune checkpoint molecules suggests its potential role in modulating the immune landscape of HCC, which could have implications for both immunotherapy efficacy and safety profiles.

In summary, the high expression of TSEN15 in hepatocellular carcinoma is closely associated with the clinical pathological features of the tumor, prognosis, and changes in the immune microenvironment. TSEN15 not only holds potential as a biomarker for liver cancer but also offers a new target for immunotherapy. Future research should further explore the molecular mechanisms of TSEN15 in hepatocellular carcinoma, particularly its role in cell cycle regulation and its association with immune evasion, and assess its clinical application prospects as a therapeutic target.

This study has certain limitations that should be considered when interpreting the results. First, the relatively small sample size (n = 50) may limit the statistical power of our analyses and potentially introduce selection bias. Second, the lack of an independent external validation cohort necessitates caution in generalizing our findings to broader HCC populations. Future studies with larger sample sizes and multi-center validation are warranted to confirm the clinical and biological significance of TSEN15 in HCC.

Patients and methods

Collection of samples

All experimental procedures involving human tissues were conducted in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations, including the Declaration of Helsinki. The study protocol and sample collection were approved by the Ethics Committee of the Second Affiliated Hospital of Xuzhou Medical University, Jiangsu Province (Ethics approval number: (2023)121501;Date of approval: December 15, 2023).

Written informed consent was obtained from all patients prior to tissue collection. From December 2023 to December 2024, a total of 50 liver cancer tissue samples were collected from patients undergoing hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) resection. Inclusion criteria required pathologically confirmed diagnoses of HCC.

In tissue collection, the definitions of “paired adjacent tissue,” “normal tissue,” and “non-tumor tissue” are as follows: Paired adjacent tissue refers to the area immediately surrounding the tumor (typically within 1–3 cm from the tumor margin), which may still be influenced by the tumor microenvironment;

Normal tissue is defined as histologically confirmed tissue without any pathological changes, including the absence of malignant or benign lesions. It may be obtained from healthy donors or from areas distant from the tumor (e.g., more than 3 cm away) in the same patient; Non-tumor tissue is a broad category encompassing all tissues without malignant tumor cells, including normal tissue, benign lesions, and paired adjacent tissue. During surgical resection, tissue samples are collected from the tumor core, areas located more than 1 cm from the tumor margin, and areas more than 3 cm away from the tumor for comparative analysis.

Patient inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) Pathologically confirmed diagnosis of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC); (2) Underwent curative or partial hepatic resection with sufficient surgical tissue available for analysis;3. Availability of complete clinical information (e.g., age, sex, HBV/HCV status, tumor stage, etc.); 4. Age ≥ 18 years; 5. Signed informed consent and approval by the institutional ethics committee.

Exclusion criteria included: (1) Non-HCC liver tumors (e.g., intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma, combined hepatocellular -cholangiocarcinoma); (2) History of other primary malignancies at different sites; (3) Receipt of preoperative anti-tumor therapies (e.g., TACE, radiotherapy, or targeted therapy), which may influence molecular expression profiles; (4) Presence of severe underlying conditions (e.g., advanced liver cirrhosis, severe infections) that could affect survival or analysis; (5) Poor-quality tissue samples unsuitable for downstream analyses (e.g., RNA degradation, extensive necrosis).

Hematoxylin and eosin (HE) staining

Liver cancer tissue samples were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 4 h, followed by dehydration, clearing, and paraffin embedding. The tissue was sectioned into 4–5 μm slices, stained with hematoxylin for nuclear staining and eosin for cytoplasmic staining. After staining, the slices were dehydrated, mounted with neutral resin, and observed under a microscope. Morphological changes, including tumor cell distribution, morphology, nuclear/cytoplasmic ratio, and tumor microenvironment features, were assessed.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

Liver cancer tissue samples were paraffin-embedded and sectioned into 4–5 μm thick slices. The sections were deparaffinized with xylene, dehydrated in a gradient alcohol series, and subjected to antigen retrieval. Endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked with 0.3% hydrogen peroxide. Non-specific binding was blocked at room temperature. The sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against TSEN15 (1:100-1:200 dilution). Afterward, HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies were applied and incubated for 1 h at room temperature. The expression of TSEN15 was visualized using DAB, counterstained with hematoxylin, and analyzed under a microscope.

Protein extraction and western blotting

Total protein was extracted using a protein extraction kit and quantified with a BCA assay. SDS-PAGE was performed to separate proteins, followed by transfer to PVDF or nitrocellulose membranes. Membranes were blocked with 5% milk or BSA, then incubated overnight with primary antibodies at 4 °C. After washing, HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies were applied, and chemiluminescent detection was used to analyze protein expression.

Real-time quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR)

TSEN15 RT-qPCR primer pair: Forward: GCGTTCTTGGTTTACCTGGACC, Reverse: TAGGCACCACAGTCTGTAACCC. Total RNA was extracted from cell or tissue samples using an RNA extraction kit. RNA quality and purity were confirmed by the A260/A280 ratio. cDNA was synthesized using a one-step RT-PCR kit. SYBR Green was used during RT-qPCR amplification to monitor gene expression in real time. Relative expression levels of TSEN15 were analyzed by the ΔΔCT method, comparing with GAPDH as an internal control.

Transwell assay

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) cells (e.g., HepG2) were transfected with TSEN15-specific shRNA or a negative control shRNA using Lipofectamine 3000 (Thermo Fisher) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. After 24–48 h of incubation to allow for gene knockdown, cells were trypsinized, resuspended in serum-free medium, and seeded into the upper chamber of Transwell inserts (Corning, 8-µm pore size) at a density of 2–5 × 10⁴ cells per well. For the invasion assay, Matrigel-coated Transwell inserts were used. The lower chamber was filled with medium containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) to serve as a chemoattractant. The cells were incubated at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO₂ for an additional 24–48 h. After incubation, non-migrated or non-invading cells on the upper surface of the membrane were gently removed with a cotton swab. The cells that had migrated or invaded through the membrane to the lower surface were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 20 min and stained with 0.1% crystal violet for 10–15 min. Stained cells were washed, air-dried, and counted in at least five randomly selected fields under a light microscope. The average number of migrated or invaded cells was calculated to evaluate the effect of TSEN15 knockdown on HCC cell motility.All Transwell migration and invasion assays were performed with three independent biological replicates, each containing three technical replicates. For each replicate, cells were counted in at least five randomly selected fields under a light microscope to ensure statistical reliability.

Scratch assay

To evaluate the impact of TSEN15 knockdown on the migratory capacity of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) cells, a wound healing assay was performed. HepG2 cells were seeded into six-well plates at an appropriate density and cultured until they reached approximately 80–90% confluence, forming a uniform monolayer. A straight and consistent scratch (wound) was made across the monolayer using a sterile 200 µL pipette tip. Detached cells were gently removed by washing with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), and the medium was replaced with fresh culture medium containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) to support cell viability during migration. Following this, TSEN15-specific shRNA or control shRNA was transfected into the cells using Lipofectamine 3000 (Thermo Fisher), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The cells were then incubated for an additional 24 to 48 h. Images of the wound area were taken at 0 h and after incubation using a phase-contrast microscope. The degree of wound closure was quantified by measuring the width of the scratch at different time points. The rate of cell migration was calculated and compared between the TSEN15 knockdown and control groups, providing insight into the role of TSEN15 in regulating HCC cell motility.The wound healing assay was conducted with three independent biological replicates, each including three technical replicates. Wound closure was quantified by measuring the scratch width at multiple positions in each replicate to minimize measurement bias.

CCK8 assay

To evaluate the effect of TSEN15 knockdown on hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) cell proliferation, a Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8, Dojindo) assay was performed. HepG2 cells were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of approximately 3000–5000 cells per well and cultured until they reached 60–70% confluence. The experiment was repeated three times independently (biological replicates) to ensure reproducibility. Cells were then transfected with TSEN15-specific shRNA or negative control shRNA using Lipofectamine 3000, according to the manufacturer’s protocol. After transfection, the cells were incubated for 12, 24, 48, 72, and 96 h. At each designated time point, 10 µL of CCK-8 reagent was added to each well, followed by incubation at 37 °C for an additional 2–4 h in the dark. The optical density (OD) at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader (e.g., BioTek or Thermo). Cell viability and proliferation were assessed based on the OD values at each time point. Growth curves were plotted to compare proliferation rates between the TSEN15 knockdown group and the control group, providing insights into the role of TSEN15 in HCC cell growth.

EdU assay

To assess the effect of TSEN15 knockdown on the proliferation of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) cells, an EdU (5-ethynyl-2’-deoxyuridine) incorporation assay was performed. HepG2 cells were seeded into 96-well plates with three technical replicates per group, and the experiment was performed in three independent biological replicates. Cells were cultured until reaching 60–70% confluence. Cells were then transfected with TSEN15-specific shRNA using Lipofectamine 3000, following the manufacturer’s protocol, and incubated for 24 h.

At the designated time point, 10 µM EdU solution was added to each well, and cells were incubated for an additional 4 h to allow incorporation of EdU into newly synthesized DNA during the S-phase of the cell cycle. After incubation, the culture medium was removed, and cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde. EdU detection was carried out using a commercially available EdU assay kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Subsequently, cells were counterstained with Hoechst 33,342 to visualize nuclei.

Fluorescent images were captured using a fluorescence microscope, and EdU-positive cells were quantified in multiple random fields per well from all replicates. The percentage of EdU-positive cells was calculated to evaluate DNA synthesis activity, reflecting cell proliferation rates. This method provides a sensitive and accurate measurement of proliferation, allowing detailed analysis of the inhibitory effect of TSEN15 shRNA on HCC cell growth.

Cell immunofluorescence assay

HepG2 cells were seeded onto confocal dishes and cultured to 60–70% confluence. TSEN15 shRNA was transfected into the cells and cultured for 24–48 h. The cells were then fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min at room temperature, followed by three washes with PBS. To permeabilize the cell membrane, 0.3% Triton X-100 was used for 10 min at room temperature. Cells were blocked with PBS containing 5% BSA for 1 h at room temperature to reduce non-specific binding. After blocking, primary antibodies targeting TSEN15 (TSEN15/C1orf19 Polyclonal Antibody, Rabbit, Bioss, Catalog No: bs-9783R) were added at appropriate dilutions, and the cells were incubated overnight at 4 °C. The next day, fluorescence-labeled secondary antibodies were incubated for 1 h. After washing three times with PBS, DAPI was added to stain the nuclei. Fluorescence signals were observed using a confocal microscope to analyze the effect of TSEN15 shRNA on the expression of the target protein inside the cells.

Colony formation assay

To investigate the effect of TSEN15 knockdown on the clonogenic potential of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) cells, HepG2 cells were transfected with TSEN15-specific shRNA. After 24–48 h post-transfection, cells were trypsinized, counted, and seeded into 6-well plates at a low density of 500–1000 cells per well to allow individual colony formation. The cells were cultured for 10 to 14 days under standard conditions (37 °C, 5% CO₂), with the culture medium replaced every 2 to 3 days to maintain optimal growth. At the end of the incubation period, the formed colonies were gently washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) to remove residual medium, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min at room temperature, and subsequently stained with 0.1% crystal violet solution for 20 min to visualize colonies. Excess stain was carefully washed off with distilled water, and plates were air-dried. The number of colonies, defined as clusters containing ≥ 50 cells, was photographed using a digital camera and manually counted under a light microscope. The results were used to assess the influence of TSEN15 knockdown on the colony-forming ability of HCC cells, providing insight into its role in cell proliferation and survival.

Cell culture

Human hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) cell lines (e.g., HepG2, Hep3B) were purchased from Servicebio (Wuhan Servicebio Technology Co., Ltd., China) and cultured in RPMI or DMEM medium purchased from Gibco (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). The culture medium was supplemented with penicillin-streptomycin solution and 10% fetal bovine serum. All cells were maintained in a controlled environment at 37 °C with 5% CO₂.

Data collection from TCGA database

All mRNA expression and clinical data for HCC patients included in this study were sourced from two primary databases: the TCGA (The Cancer Genome Atlas) and GTEx (Genotype-Tissue Expression) databases. The TCGA database provides comprehensive molecular data from tumor samples across multiple institutions, while the GTEx database offers genomic and expression data from normal tissues. By utilizing these databases, this study ensured a robust and representative dataset, comprising both tumor and normal tissue samples, enabling a thorough analysis of the mRNA expression profile of HCC patients and its correlation with clinical outcomes.

Differential expression analysis of HCC

Based on the minimum P-value of TSEN15 expression, patients from the TCGA cancer cohort were divided into two groups: the TSEN15 high-expression group and the TSEN15 low-expression group. To further investigate the gene expression differences between these two groups, the researchers used the DESeq2 R package developed by Love et al. in 2014. They set the thresholds for differential expression genes (DEGs) to an adjusted p-value < 0.05 and |log2-fold change (FC)| >1. The |log2FC| >1 threshold (equivalent to a two-fold change) was selected to focus on genes with substantial expression changes that are more likely to be biologically relevant. This threshold is widely accepted in transcriptomic studies and provides a balance between sensitivity and specificity for identifying functionally important genes. The adjusted p-value < 0.05 threshold was applied to ensure statistical significance. To further validate our findings, we also performed sensitivity analyses using more stringent criteria (|log2FC| >1.5 and FDR < 0.01) and found consistent results in the enriched pathways and functional categories.Additionally, the researchers aimed to evaluate the correlation between the expression of the top 10 DEGs and TSEN15. To do this, they employed Spearman’s correlation analysis, a statistical method used to measure the strength and direction of the monotonic relationship between two variables.

Enrichment analysis

Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) is a computational method used to standardize RNA-Seq data obtained from TCGA. This analytical tool, available on the MSigDB website, enables researchers to investigate the biological functions of TSEN15. To identify potential biological functions, we performed Gene Ontology (GO) terms and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analyse24. These analyses were conducted using the gene clustering analysis tool implemented in the R programming language. The GO terms are categorized into three aspects: Biological Process (BP), Molecular Function (MF), and Cellular Component (CC). By analyzing genes associated with TSEN15, researchers can gain insights into specific biological processes, molecular functions, and cellular components linked to this gene. Furthermore, KEGG pathway enrichment analysis allows researchers to explore potential pathways involving TSEN15. The KEGG pathway database catalogs various biological pathways and provides a comprehensive understanding of molecular interactions and signaling events related to specific genes or gene sets. To ensure the reliability of enrichment results, two criteria must be met: False Discovery Rate (FDR) < 0.05 and nominal p-value < 0.05. These thresholds help identify statistically significant enrichment results, suggesting that the observed biological functions and pathways associated with TSEN15 are unlikely to occur by chance.

Immune cell infiltration analysis

The immune infiltration levels of 24 immune cell types were analyzed in this study. Single Sample Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) was used to measure the relative enrichment scores of these immune cells in HCC, employing the R package GSVA [13]. Additionally, Spearman correlation analysis was performed to explore the relationship between TSEN15 expression levels and the 24 immune cell types. Furthermore, the Wilcoxon rank-sum test was used to assess differences in immune infiltration levels between the TSEN15 high and low expression groups.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.2.1). The normality of continuous data was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. For comparisons between two independent groups of non-normally distributed data, such as immune infiltration scores, the Wilcoxon rank-sum test was applied. Differential gene expression analysis was conducted using the DESeq2 package with thresholds of adjusted p-value < 0.05 and |log2FC| >1. Correlation analyses involving TSEN15 expression were performed using Spearman’s method. Survival differences were evaluated with Kaplan-Meier curves and log-rank test. Enrichment analyses (GSEA, GO, KEGG) used significance thresholds of nominal p-value < 0.05 and FDR < 0.05. For functional experiments with biological replicates, Student’s t-test or one-way ANOVA was used as appropriate. A two-tailed p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant throughout the study.
