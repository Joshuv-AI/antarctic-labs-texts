Unveiling Neuropsychiatric Lupus: A Case of SLE Presenting With Depressive Symptoms and Literature Review
Authors: Yuqiong He, Qiuxiang Ou, Li Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTA young woman presented with a rare depressive‐onset manifestation and showed a poor response to antidepressant therapy. Subsequently, with the involvement of multiple organ systems, positive autoantibodies, and multiple lesions on head MRI, she was diagnosed with systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE). Following the initiation of immunosuppressive treatment, the patient experienced rapid improvement in both the physical symptoms of SLE and depressive manifestations. The underlying pathophysiological mechanisms linking depression in SLE patients are thought to involve inflammatory processes and microcirculatory dysfunction.

Introduction

Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) is a common chronic, recurrent autoimmune disease that may involve all systems and organs of the body [1]; thus, a patient with SLE may present with a wide range of clinical manifestations, including systemic symptoms (e.g., fever and fatigue), cutaneous symptoms (e.g., malar rash and skin ulcers), hematological abnormalities (e.g., anemia, leukopenia and thrombocytopenia), gastrointestinal complaints (e.g., poor appetite and nausea), urinary system involvement (e.g., hematuria and proteinuria), as well as features affecting other organ systems. A patient with nervous system involvement can be diagnosed with neuropsychiatric SLE (NPSLE), which may manifest with neurological symptoms such as headache, seizures, limb paralysis, as well as psychiatric symptoms such as hallucinations, delusions, and depression [2]. Previous studies have reported that up to 37.2% of patients with SLE meet the diagnostic criteria for major depressive disorder (MDD) [3]. A meta‐analysis of 59 studies involving a total of 10,828 patients with SLE reported a pooled prevalence of MDD of 39% [4], underscoring the importance of paying greater attention to this vulnerable patient population.

Considering the high co‐incidence of depression and SLE, the relationship between the two conditions has attracted increasing research attention. Currently, it is widely accepted that people with SLE may experience symptoms of depression and anxiety due to chronic illness, repeated physical discomfort, prolonged medication use, and frequent hospital visits [5]. In addition, active disease status, fibromyalgia, older age, and active skin involvement are important risk factors for depression in patients with SLE [6]. In a minority of cases, depressive or anxiety symptoms may represent the initial presentation of SLE, rather than secondary symptom consequences. Such patients, especially those lacking typical clinical signs such as cutaneous involvement or hematuria, are at high risk of being misdiagnosed with depression or anxiety. Given the high burden of psychiatric comorbidities, especially depression and anxiety, early recognition of NPSLE is essential [7]. Timely identification and diagnosis can guide appropriate treatment and facilitate functional recovery, underscoring the importance of comprehensive clinical assessment and intervention.

In this report, we described a young female who initially presented with depressive symptoms for 6 months, and subsequently developed hematological, urinary, and neurological involvement. Following multidisciplinary evaluation, she was diagnosed with SLE. Standard immunotherapy resulted in significant improvement in both her physical and depressive symptoms. This case underscores the importance of considering SLE in patients with atypical psychiatric presentations at an early stage and provides a reference for timely diagnosis and management. Furthermore, we reviewed the existing literature on the pathophysiological mechanisms underlying depressive symptoms in SLE, aiming to enhance understanding of disease development and guide the establishment of more standardized treatment strategies.

Case Presentation

Case History and Examination

The patient was a 19‐year‐old female without any psychosocial or family history, who experienced depressed mood, unwillingness to communicate with others, crying for no reason, fatigue, poor appetite, and poor sleep in October 2022. She sought medical attention at a psychiatric clinic and was diagnosed with a “depressive state.” but she was not treated with any medication or psychotherapy. The patient developed recurrent fever with no obvious cause in February 2023, with the highest body temperature being above 39.0°C, which mostly occurred at night and could be resolved quickly after the use of medications such as ibuprofen. Her fever was accompanied by headache, occasional chest tightness, shortness of breath, and syncope, but no coughing or other flu‐like symptoms. Due to these abnormal symptoms, she was hospitalized several times for emergency treatment. After receiving symptomatic management (with specific details unavailable), her condition showed slight improvement and she was subsequently discharged. The patient was initially prescribed sertraline (dosage unknown) for 14 days. The treatment was then adjusted to sertraline 100 mg once daily and mirtazapine 15 mg. She developed mutism, immobility, refusal of food, and required assistance with daily activities. Subsequently, the patient developed pain and weakness in both shoulders and limbs, which led to dependence on others for basic self‐care, including eating and bathing. By May 2023, urinary retention appeared, and her overall condition continued to deteriorate. These symptoms progressed to profound mutism, immobility, anorexia, and complete loss of self‐care ability, prompting hospital admission.

During hospitalization, the patient had two episodes of seizure‐like activity, characterized by sudden loss of consciousness, trismus, and upward eye deviation triggered by changes in body position. Each episode lasted 5–10 min and resolved after intravenous administration of 10 mg diazepam.

Physical Examination

Upon admission, the patient was transported to the ward in a wheelchair. Vital signs were as follows: body temperature 36.5°C, pulse rate 101 beats/min, respiratory rate 20 breaths/min, and blood pressure 121/62 mmHg. The patient appeared pale and fatigued, consistent with anemia, and assumed a passive posture. She was uncooperative during the physical examination. No jaundice was observed. Multiple enlarged lymph nodes were palpable in the left cervical, bilateral axillary, and inguinal regions, with tenderness and well‐defined borders. Cardiovascular examination showed a heart rate of 101 beats/min with normal heart sounds, and no pathological murmurs were detected at any valve area.

Abdominal examination revealed distension, tenderness, and muscle guarding. Scattered petechiae were noted below both knees, particularly on the dorsum of the right foot, which were non‐blanching upon pressure. Neurological examination demonstrated involuntary twitching of the lips and upper limbs. The left lower limb was positioned in external rotation with mildly increased muscle tone. Muscle strength was Grade 4 in both upper limbs, Grade 4+ in the right lower limb, and Grade 3 in the left lower limb. The Babinski sign was positive on the left foot, and Kernig's sign was positive bilaterally.

Mental Status Examination

The patient's appearance was consistent with her stated age, and she was appropriately dressed. She was transported to the ward in a wheelchair by her family. She responded to questions slowly and with minimal speech, in a low voice, and exhibited a sad affect. She denied experiencing psychotic symptoms. She was unable to work or perform activities of daily living independently. She reported suicidal thoughts but without a specific plan. Her Hamilton Depression Scale (HAMD‐17) score was 28, while the Young Mania Scale score was 0.

Differential Diagnosis and Investigation

Complete color Doppler ultrasound of the neck and thyroid was performed, which demonstrated multiple hypoechoic nodules in the left cervical region (level V), suggestive of multiple enlarged lymph nodes.

A comprehensive CT scan of the abdomen revealed marked bladder distension, mild bilateral ureteral dilatation, and mild bilateral hydronephrosis of uncertain etiology, raising the possibility of a neurogenic (delayed) bladder. Multiple enlarged lymph nodes were observed in the retroperitoneum, bilateral axillary, and inguinal regions. Head CT revealed multiple low‐density lesions of indeterminate nature within the white matter of both cerebral hemispheres, as well as hypertrophy of the left inferior turbinate.

Laboratory testing revealed elevated D‐dimer and fibrinogen levels, indicating a hypercoagulable state. These findings prompted suspicion of a possible autoimmune disease. Further serological tests demonstrated multiple abnormal antibodies (Table 1), including anti‐dsDNA antibody and antinuclear antibody (ANA). Head MRI imaging revealed multiple brain lesions, with differential considerations, including hemorrhagic tumors, hemorrhagic infectious lesions, multiple cerebral infections with hemorrhage or other etiologies (Figure 1).

Treatment and Outcome

Based on the above findings, a multidisciplinary discussion was conducted; the patient was initially diagnosed with SLE. MRI findings suggested multiple vasculitis ‐like lesions, supporting a diagnosis of NPSLE. The patient was subsequently transferred to the rheumatology department for specialized management. Treatment included methylprednisolone at a dose of 80 mg/day for 1 week, followed by 20 mg/day for 10 consecutive days. Intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) pulse therapy was administered for 3 days. Additionally, mycophenolate mofetil and hydroxychloroquine were initiated for immunomodulatory therapy. Two weeks after treatment initiation, the patient showed significant clinical improvement: physical discomfort was reduced, social communication improved, and self‐care ability was enhanced. Upon discharge, the patient's Young Mania Scale score was 4 and the HAMD‐17 score was 17, indicating substantial improvement in mental status. Discussion

We present a case of NPSLE in which anxiety and depression were the presenting manifestations, which contrasts with the widely accepted view that depressive symptoms are usually considered common comorbidities in patients with SLE [5], rather than intrinsic manifestations of the disease itself [8]. The patient initially presented with depressive symptoms, including sadness, anxiety, and suicidal ideation, which were suggestive of depression. Such presentations underscore the diagnostic challenge posed by NPSLE, particularly when psychiatric manifestations dominate the clinical picture. Patients with atypical, treatment‐resistant psychiatric symptoms should raise suspicion of underlying organic disease. In line with our report, there were studies reporting cases that presented depression as the first clinical manifestation in SLE patients. Mervat et al. [9] suggested that depressive symptoms may represent the first clinical manifestation of SLE in adolescents, typically presenting as sadness, diminished interest in daily activities, poor communication, school absenteeism, fatigue, and irritability. Sethi et al. [8] reported a patient initially diagnosed with MDD beginning in childhood, who then, demonstrated resistance to antidepressant medications, and developed oral and facial movement disorders, oral ulcers, and joint pain even at low doses of quetiapine over a 12‐year disease course. Upon further evaluation, the final diagnosis was considered to be NPSLE. In the above cases, patients consistently presented with anxiety and depression as initial manifestations, with a definitive SLE diagnosis established only after prolonged and repeated clinical evaluations. Consequently, some studies hypothesize that depression may represent a prodromal feature of SLE, suggesting its potential utility as an early predictive sign in at‐risk individuals [9, 10]. The current case highlights that, in patients presenting with depressive symptoms accompanied by non‐specific physical complaints, laboratory and imaging investigations are essential for accurate diagnosis. Once SLE is confirmed, regular immunotherapy is essential. Consistent with this, the patient in our case experienced significant alleviation of both her physical and depressive symptoms after initiating immunotherapy, demonstrating that this treatment can effectively address the multifaceted manifestations of the disease. Elucidating the pathophysiological mechanisms underlying depression in SLE is critical for advancing our understanding of NPSLE. Among these mechanisms, inflammatory pathways and microcirculation disorders are currently a major focus of research [11].

Inflammation May Play a Key Role in the Pathogenesis of NPSLE

Previous studies have demonstrated that inflammatory factors such as tumor necrosis factor‐α (TNF‐α), interferon‐γ (IFN‐γ), interleukin‐6 (IL‐6), and interleukin‐8 (IL‐8) are closely related to the onset and progression of SLE [12, 13, 14]. For instance, Yoshio et al. [15] reported that the intrathecal concentrations of IL‐6 and IL‐8 in patients with NPSLE were independent of their serum levels, suggesting that these cytokines might be produced locally within the central nervous system and could contribute to the pathogenesis of central NPSLE. The role of inflammation has also been broadly implicated in psychiatric disorders [16, 17, 18, 19]. Large‐scale cohort analyses, including those from the UK Biobank and the Netherlands Study of Depression and Anxiety (NESDA), have further demonstrated that elevated CRP levels are strongly associated with low mood, poor appetite, sleep problems, fatigue, and other depressive symptoms [20]. Similarly, Mendelian randomization analyses have suggested a causal link between elevated IL‐6 and increased risk of fatigue and sleep problems [20].

Taken together, the above studies suggest that inflammatory factors play an important role in the onset and development of SLE and mental disorders. Notably, decreased complement C3 and C4 levels have been associated with depressive symptoms in patients with SLE [21], which is in line with the reduced C3 and C4 observed in the present case. Regrettably, no additional analyses of inflammatory markers were performed in this case, making it impossible to clarify potential alterations in other inflammatory indicators or their association with depressive symptoms. The present patient's depressive symptoms improved significantly following glucocorticoid therapy. Glucocorticoids exert anti‐inflammatory effects primarily by inhibiting the synthesis of inflammatory mediators such as IL‐6 and TNF‐α [22], while simultaneously enhancing the production of anti‐inflammatory cytokines, including interleukin‐10 and transforming growth factor‐β, to further suppress inflammation. Through these mechanisms, glucocorticoid treatment may reduce both systemic inflammatory activity and associated depressive symptoms. This highlights the clinical importance of considering immunotherapy in cases where depressive symptoms may be secondary to underlying autoimmune or inflammatory processes such as SLE.

Microcirculation Dysfunction May Represent an Important Factor in the Pathogenesis of NPSLE

Evidence suggests that microvascular dysfunction plays a critical role in SLE. In renal SLE, microvascular injury underlies proteinuria, the most common clinical manifestation [23]. Similarly, an autopsy study of patients with NPSLE suggested that the central nervous system is directly affected by microvascular injury [24]. Neurological symptoms have been closely related to cerebral microvascular damage, and psychiatric manifestations—including mania and depression—may also arise from microangiopathy, sometimes preceding overt vascular lesions [25]. The concept aligns with the “vascular depression” thought to result from disturbances in cerebral microcirculation, including increased blood–brain permeability and impaired perfusion regulation [26, 27, 28, 29]. These changes can injure specific neuronal populations and disrupt mood‐regulating circuits, thereby contributing to depressive symptoms and poor responsiveness to antidepressant response. Indeed, prior studies have demonstrated that patients with depression exhibit reduced cerebral blood flow velocity, increased vascular resistance, and impaired cerebrovascular reactivity [30]. One study found that elevated depressive symptoms were associated with hemodynamic abnormalities in the ventromedial prefrontal cortex and orbitofrontal regions [31]. A study using functional near‐infrared spectroscopy showed that SLE patients with depression exhibited significantly blunted increases in cerebral oxyhemoglobin (O2Hb) during exercise compared to those without depression [32].

Given the presence of multiple positive autoantibodies, the abnormal brain MRI findings are highly suggestive of a hemorrhagic infectious lesion. The autoantibody positivity indicates ongoing autoimmune activity, which can induce endothelial injury and complement activation, thereby predisposing the patient to microvascular damage. When accompanied by neuroimaging abnormalities, these findings strongly support the interpretation that cerebral microvascular pathology underlies the patient's neurological and psychiatric manifestations, consistent with the proposed pathophysiological mechanisms of NPSLE.

Diagnose and Treatment for SLE Patients Present With Depressive Symptoms

This case highlights the diagnostic and therapeutic challenges of NPSLE, particularly when depressive symptoms constitute the initial presentation. The diagnosis of NPSLE remains difficult owing to the absence of specific laboratory and neuroimaging biomarkers, especially in patients presenting with non‐specific symptoms such as depressive symptoms [33]. Individuals with non‐specific SLE symptoms are often first assessed in psychiatric settings, which increases the likelihood of misdiagnosis and delays timely recognition of the underlying autoimmune condition. In many instances, NPSLE is diagnosed only after neuropsychiatric manifestations have developed and SLE has been definitively established. Although brain MRI is regarded as the gold standard for evaluating NPSLE, more than 50% of affected patients show no significant abnormalities on imaging, further complicating diagnostic accuracy [34]. With respect to treatment, current evidence supports immunosuppressants remaining the cornerstone of therapy [35]. In the present case, high‐dose methylprednisolone during the acute phase, followed by immunomodulatory therapy with mycophenolate mofetil, hydroxychloroquine and intravenous immunoglobulin, led to a rapid clinical improvement. Nevertheless, the optimal duration of immunosuppressive therapy remains uncertain. Nevertheless, therapeutic strategies vary considerably depending on the type and severity of psychiatric symptoms [36].

Beyond immunotherapy, comprehensive management should also integrate psychiatric interventions. Psychotherapy may help alleviate depressive and anxiety symptoms, while pharmacological treatments—such as antidepressants, anxiolytics, or antipsychotics—can be tailored to specific psychiatric manifestations in NPSLE [36]. These findings underscore the importance of early recognition of NPSLE in patients with atypical psychiatric symptoms and emphasize the need for a multidisciplinary approach involving rheumatology, neurology, and psychiatry to optimize long‐term outcomes. Looking ahead, future research should prioritize the development of reliable biomarkers and precision treatment strategies to improve early diagnosis, guide individualized therapy, and ultimately enhance prognosis in patients with NPSLE.

Limitations

This case also has several limitations. First, there was a delay in establishing the diagnosis. Two significant factors contributing to the delay are the postponed examination of immune indicators and, furthermore, the failure to perform a cerebrospinal fluid analysis in a timely manner. Specifically, the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) puncture and antibody testing were not performed in a timely manner. CSF antibody testing serves as an important complement to the abnormal MRI findings and may further support the interpretation of vasculitic changes on neuroimaging. In addition, only complement C3 and C4 were measured as inflammatory markers, whereas other inflammatory and validation indicators were not assessed. A broader panel of inflammatory markers could have provided stronger evidence of immune dysregulation in SLE. Second, the patient received antidepressants for a relatively short duration—less than 6 weeks—at the early stage of treatment. Therefore, the assessment of antidepressant efficacy in this case was limited and insufficient to draw firm conclusions regarding their therapeutic effect. Third, although we reported the changes in the HAMD score of this subject before and after treatment, we acknowledge that detailed records regarding the subject's treatment tolerance, medication adherence, and possible side effects were not maintained. The aforementioned limitations underscore several critical lessons for clinical practice. In patients presenting with physical symptoms—particularly those involving multiple organ systems—a comprehensive physical examination should be performed early, accompanied by timely autoimmune serological testing. When neuropsychiatric manifestations are evident, early brain MRI and cerebrospinal fluid analysis are essential to promptly identify potential organic pathology.

Conclusions

For patients presenting with depression accompanied by non‐specific physical symptoms, comprehensive laboratory tests and imaging examinations should be completed to confirm or exclude underlying organic diseases such as SLE. The pathogenesis of depressive symptoms in SLE is thought to involve complex mechanisms, including inflammatory responses and disturbance of cerebral microcirculation. Systemic immunotherapy remains the mainstay of treatment, aiming to control inflammation and alleviate both the somatic and psychiatric manifestations of SLE.
