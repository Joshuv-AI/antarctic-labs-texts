Genital self-image as a correlate of sexual function in pregnancy: multivariable cross-sectional findings
Authors: Ezgi Şahi̇n, Turan Kaan Karakaya
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAlthough women’s genital self-image was thought to be related to sexual function, evidence for this association during pregnancy was limited. Therefore, this study examined the relationship between genital self-image and sexual function among pregnant women.MethodsThis study was conducted in northern Turkey between March and July 2025 at an Obstetrics and Pediatrics Training and Research Hospital with 1,000 pregnant women. The design was cross-sectional; participants were enrolled via consecutive sampling during routine antenatal visits. Sexual function was measured with the Female Sexual Function Index, 19-item version (FSFI-19), and genital self-image with the Female Genital Self-Image Scale (FGSIS). Statistical analyses reported Pearson correlations and multivariable linear regression models adjusted for age, parity, trimester, education, planned pregnancy, and years married, using HC3 robust standard errors.ResultsMost participants were aged 25–34 years (65.4%). Mean (SD) scores were FGSIS 17.42 (3.03) and FSFI-19 total 19.92 (4.77). Using the FSFI-19 cut-off of 26.55, 94.2% met screening criteria for likely sexual dysfunction (this threshold is not diagnostic in pregnancy). Genital self-image correlated positively with sexual function (FGSIS–FSFI: r = 0.489; domain r ≈ 0.32–0.47; all p < 0.001). In multivariable models, higher FGSIS remained associated with higher FSFI total (β = 0.408; p < 0.001; Adjusted R²=0.070; F(8, 991) = 9.98). Multiparity was also associated with higher FSFI (p < 0.001), whereas age, trimester, education, planned pregnancy, and years married showed no consistent adjusted associations.ConclusionsA more positive genital self-image is associated with better sexual function during pregnancy, independent of core demographic and obstetric factors. However, causality cannot be inferred from cross-sectional data. Although the effect size is modest, these findings highlight genital self-image as a potential target for psychosocial assessment and support in antenatal care. Longitudinal, multi-site studies using probabilistic/stratified sampling and testing mediating/moderating pathways are warranted to clarify temporality and inform interventions.Keywords: Pregnancy, Genital self-image, Female genital self-image scale, Sexual function, Female sexual function index, Body image, Psychosexual, Cross-sectional studies, Multivariable analysis

Introduction

Sexual function is a core component of women’s well-being and becomes particularly salient during pregnancy, a period of substantial hormonal, anatomical, and psychosocial change. These changes can alter desire, arousal, lubrication, orgasm, satisfaction and pain across gestation [1, 2]. Disruptions in sexual function may affect maternal quality of life, partner relationships, and postpartum sexual health. Among psychosocial factors relevant to sexual health in pregnancy, genital self-image has drawn increasing clinical interest. Genital self-image refers to a woman’s subjective perception, emotional evaluation, and cognitive appraisal of the appearance and structure of her genital area [3]. Pregnancy-related physiological changes—such as vulvar pigmentation, swelling, and weight gain—may shape genital self-perception and, in turn, relate to sexual self-esteem, motivation for intimacy, and partner communication [4].

Evidence from general and pregnant populations indicates that a more positive genital self-image is associated with greater sexual satisfaction and higher levels of arousal and orgasm, and with lower sexual avoidance [3, 5]. Conversely, women reporting a more negative genital self-image frequently describe reduced desire, dyspareunia and partner-related anxiety—particularly in late pregnancy, when bodily changes are most apparent [6].

Despite growing interest, the literature on genital self-image in pregnancy has several gaps. First, many studies use modest samples (approximately 200–400), limiting precision and generalisability [3, 7, 8]. Second, measurement approaches vary (FSFI-6 vs. FSFI-19), and not all reports employ validated local-language versions, complicating cross-study comparisons [9, 10]. Third, relatively few analyses adjust for key confounders (e.g. age, parity, trimester, education, planned pregnancy, years married), raising the likelihood of residual confounding. Fourth, data from more conservative sociocultural contexts remain limited, even though cultural norms may shape both genital self-image and sexual functioning [6, 7]. Together, these gaps constrain inferences about the strength and independence of the genital self-image–sexual function association during pregnancy.

This study aims to address these gaps by examining the association between genital self-image and sexual function in a large antenatal cohort (n = 1.000) recruited consecutively. We used the validated Turkish versions of the Female Genital Self-Image Scale (FGSIS) and the Female Sexual Function Index, 19-item version (FSFI-19) [9, 10]. We report effect sizes (correlations; multivariable β with 95% confidence intervals) and model fit, adjusting for age, parity, trimester, education, planned pregnancy, and years married, to provide confounder-adjusted, culturally grounded evidence on this association—without causal claims—and to inform patient-centred sexual health counselling within antenatal care.

Methods

Study design and participants

This cross-sectional study was conducted between March and July 2025, adhering to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines. The target population for this study comprised pregnant women who presented at the obstetrics clinics of an Obstetrics and Pediatrics Training and Research Hospital. For a known population of 2,254 pregnant women, the minimum required sample size was calculated as 329 using Cochran’s formula with finite population correction, assuming a 95% confidence level and a 5% margin of error. To increase statistical power and generalizability, a total of 1,000 pregnant women were ultimately included in the study.

Sampling and recruitment

Between March and July 2025, we consecutively recruited all eligible pregnant women presenting to the antenatal clinics of a single center. Women were approached at the point of care and enrolled after eligibility confirmation and informed consent. No stratification or quota sampling was used; trimester distribution reflected clinic attendance and was not set a priori.

Eligible participants were pregnant women ≥ 18 years, Turkish-speaking, literate, without a high-risk pregnancy diagnosis, who provided written informed consent.

Pregnant women with a high-risk pregnancy diagnosis (placenta previa, threatened preterm labor, preeclampsia, and other clinician-documented complications) and those prohibited from sexual intercourse were not included in the study. High-risk pregnancy” and “prohibited from sexual intercourse” were identified through obstetric chart review of clinician-documented diagnoses and/or physician recommendations, with confirmation by the attending obstetrician when necessary. Self-report alone was not used as the basis for exclusion.

Data collection tools

Data collection comprised two parts: (i) demographic and obstetric information (7 items) were obtained through face-to-face, interviewer-administered interviews; and (ii) the FGSIS (7 items) and FSFI (19 items) were self-administered in a private room, after which participants returned the completed scales in sealed envelopes. In total, participants responded to 33 items.

Demographic information form

This form was developed by researchers based on relevant literature review [1, 3, 5, 7, 8]. The form contains a total of 7 questions or items regarding socio-demographic and obstetric information.

Female Genital Self-Image Scale (FGSIS)

The scale used to assess women’s genital self-image was developed by Herbenick and Reece [7] and adapted into Turkish by Kaya et al. (2018) [9]. The scale consists of seven items with four-point Likert-type response options. The total score ranges from 7 to 28. A higher total score indicates a more positive self-perception regarding one’s genital area. The internal consistency of the Turkish version was found to be high, with a reported Cronbach’s alpha reliability coefficient of 0.81 [9]. In the current study, the Cronbach’s alpha value was calculated as 0.84.

Female Sexual Function Index (FSFI-19)

The 19-item Female Sexual Function Index (FSFI), developed by Rosen et al. [11], has reported Cronbach’s alpha reliability coefficients of ≥ 0.82 across its six subdomains. Its Turkish version, validated by Oksuz and Malhan [10], reports a total Cronbach’s α of 0.95. The total score ranges from 2 to 36, with scores below 26.55 indicating dysfunction. In the present study, Cronbach’s α was 0.93.

Data analysis

Descriptive statistics included means, standard deviations, and minimum–maximum values for numerical variables. Normality was assessed using skewness and kurtosis; values between − 2 and + 2 were considered acceptable [12]. Pearson correlations and multivariable linear regression models with HC3 robust standard errors, adjusted for age, parity, trimester, education, planned pregnancy, and years married. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Ethics approval

The study was conducted in accordance with institutional/national ethical standards and the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethical approval was obtained from the Giresun University Research Ethics Committee (Approval No: E-50288587-050.01.01.01.04–74414, Date: March 2025) and written institutional permission was obtained from the Obstetrics and Pediatrics Training and Research Hospital where the study was conducted (Approval No: E-87982892-514.01.01.01.02–270714626). All participants provided written informed consent before enrollment; participation was voluntary and confidentiality was maintained.

Results

Of the 1,000 pregnant women who participated in the study, the majority (65.4%) were aged 25–34 years. The trimester distribution was not set a priori; recruitment followed routine clinic attendance patterns, and the second trimester constituted the largest group (42.3%). A significant proportion (59.8%) had completed high school or held a university degree. Approximately two-thirds reported that the current pregnancy was planned. Regarding employment status, 69.5% were employed, and 47.4% had been married for four years or less. With respect to parity, 38.0% had not previously given birth.

The FGSIS and FSFI total scores were positively correlated (r = 0.489, p < 0.001). Weak-to-moderate positive correlations were also observed between FGSIS and each FSFI subdomain—desire, arousal, lubrication, orgasm, satisfaction, and pain (all p < 0.001) (Table 1).

Associations with age, years married, education, planned pregnancy, and trimester were not statistically significant. Model fit was modest (Adjusted R²=0.070; F (8.991) = 9.98; p < 0.001) (Table 2).

Discussion

Sexual function during pregnancy is shaped by an interplay of cognitive–behavioral, sociocultural, and biopsychosocial factors, with genital self-image emerging as a salient correlate. Women who internalize negative genital self-perceptions are more likely to report diminished desire, arousal, and orgasm, with plausible underlying mechanisms including heightened self-criticism and performance anxiety our comparatively larger coefficient [10, 11, 13]. Media-driven ideals surrounding vulvar aesthetics and the rising visibility of cosmetic genital procedures—combined with the physical changes of pregnancy—have been linked to greater dissatisfaction [2–4]. Hormonal fluctuations and pelvic floor adaptations may also be implicated. Longitudinal research suggests that positive body-image shifts and couple-based counseling are associated with reductions in psychosocial burden across trimesters [14–16].

We observed a moderate, positive association between genital self-image and sexual function (FGSIS–FSFI: r = 0.489, p < 0.001), consistent with prior evidence. A meta-analysis reported a mean r ≈ 0.375; the larger correlation coefficient observed in our study may be attributable to our use of the full FSFI-19 (as opposed to abbreviated versions) and the specific cultural context, as both factors are known moderators of this association [5]. Domain-level correlations—desire (r = 0.467), arousal (r = 0.459), lubrication (r = 0.455), orgasm (r = 0.320), satisfaction (r = 0.450) —indicate associations across the sexual response spectrum, aligning with meta-analytic patterns (strongest for desire/arousal) [5] and with Portuguese findings linking more negative genital self-image to lower desire, arousal, satisfaction and greater pain [17]. Prospective data from Iran showed r ≈ 0.29 in pregnancy, increasing to r ≈ 0.38 by 16 weeks postpartum, which the authors hypothesized may relate to the easing of childbirth-related restrictions and reduced concerns about fetal harm [3].

In this cohort, higher FGSIS scores (more positive genital self-image) were positively associated with FSFI total in multivariable linear regression adjusting for age, parity, trimester, education, planned pregnancy, and years married. The direction of effect is consistent with reports in pregnant and general samples linking body/genital image to sexual satisfaction, arousal, and reduced avoidance [3, 5–8]. Use of the validated Turkish versions of FSFI and FGSIS supports cultural appropriateness and measurement fidelity [9, 10]. This study contributes in two ways: (i) the large sample (n = 1000) enables more precise and potentially generalizable estimates than prior cohorts from Iran, Brazil, and Turkey [3, 7, 8]; (ii) the FGSIS–FSFI association remained independent after covariate adjustment, extending earlier findings with adjusted evidence [5, 6]. Analytically, HC3 robust standard errors and low collinearity (VIF values were low-to-moderate (all ≤ 2.40)) support coefficient stability. Multiparity was associated with higher FSFI scores compared to nulliparity. While plausible explanations include greater familiarity with bodily changes and reduced uncertainty, the cross-sectional design of this study precludes causal inference [7, 8]. The absence of consistent differences across trimesters suggests that, despite physiologic changes during gestation, individual adaptation and psychosocial context may be influential, consistent with prior observations [6, 7].

Descriptively, the mean FGSIS total (17.42 ± 3.03) indicates a moderate genital self-image and is close to values reported in an Iranian third-trimester cohort (14.5 ± 4.2) and in a Turkish sample (14.5 ± 4.1) [3, 7], suggesting regional consistency. The mean FSFI total (19.92 ± 4.77) was below the commonly used cut-off of 26.55 for likely dysfunction [10], indicating that a sizable subset may be at risk. However, because this cutoff is derived from non-pregnant samples, in pregnancy it should be treated as a screening threshold rather than diagnostic. In the meta-analysis of pregnant populations, subscale means were highest for satisfaction (~ 4.5) and desire (~ 4.3), intermediate for arousal and lubrication (~ 3.8–4.0), and lowest for orgasm (~ 3.3); this pattern is consistent with our study [5]. Consistent with Brazilian data (r = 0.377 between FSFI and genital self-image), pelvic floor dysfunction was associated with lower orgasm scores, which may partly explain weaker links with orgasm relative to desire/arousal [8].

Prior work shows that FSFI totals decline during gestation and may partially recover postpartum [3], and that both instrument choice (FSFI-19 vs. abbreviated forms) and cultural context shape score distributions [5]. In relatively conservative settings, sociocultural norms can further depress sexual function scores and heighten body/genital concerns [6–8]. Within this context, the high screening prevalence in our cohort is best viewed as contextual information rather than a diagnostic rate; nevertheless, it helps situate the modest but consistent association we observed between genital self-image and sexual function.

In sum, independent of core demographic and obstetric factors, a more positive genital self-image is associated with better sexual function during pregnancy. While effects are modest at the population level, they highlight a potentially actionable psychosocial dimension.

Clinical implications

A brief, privacy-assured FGSIS screen can be integrated into routine antenatal visits (booking and mid-pregnancy) as a self-completed tool. FGSIS total ranges from 7 to 28; higher scores indicate a more positive genital self-image. Because there is no universal clinical cut-off, services may flag women in the lowest quartile of local scores or those endorsing clinically concerning items. A stepped response can include (i) concise psychoeducation on pregnancy-related changes and body image, (ii) partner-communication guidance, (iii) comfort/pain strategies (e.g., positions, lubrication, pacing), and—when indicated—referral to pelvic-floor physiotherapy or mental-health support. Delivered with cultural sensitivity, this offers a practical pathway to address a modest but clinically meaningful psychosocial correlate of sexual function in pregnancy; its effectiveness should be evaluated in prospective studies [5, 6, 8, 10, 16, 17].

Conclusion

In this large antenatal cohort, a more positive genital self-image (FGSIS) was significantly associated with better sexual function (FSFI-19). This relationship remained robust after adjusting for key demographic and obstetric factors, although the overall model explained a modest proportion of the variance. Given the cross-sectional design, these findings reflect associations rather than causation.

Using the commonly applied FSFI-19 cut-off (26.55), a high screening prevalence of likely sexual dysfunction was observed. Because this threshold was derived from non-pregnant validations, such prevalence estimates in pregnancy should be interpreted cautiously, but they provide context for the observed FGSIS–FSFI relationship.

Clinically, a brief, privacy-assured FGSIS screen can be feasibly integrated into routine antenatal visits to help identify women who may benefit from stepped, culturally sensitive support—including concise psychoeducation on pregnancy-related bodily changes, partner-communication guidance, comfort/pain strategies, and referral to pelvic-floor physiotherapy or mental-health services when indicated.

Future work should prioritise longitudinal, multi-site designs (with probabilistic/stratified sampling), partner-focused/dyadic approaches, and qualitative or mixed-methods studies to clarify temporal dynamics and interpersonal mechanisms. Efforts to evaluate measurement invariance and to calibrate pregnancy-appropriate cut-points for FSFI-19 will further strengthen clinical interpretation. Together, these directions can inform scalable, culturally sensitive strategies to support psychosexual well-being in pregnancy.

Strengths and limitations of the study

Strengths

This study is among the few to comprehensively examine the association between genital self-image and sexual function during pregnancy using the validated Turkish versions of the FSFI-19 and FGSIS, with consecutive recruitment of 1,000 antenatal attendees providing adequate power and precise estimates. Data quality was supported by privacy-assured, self-completed questionnaires (private rooms, sealed-envelope return) and an explicit Turkish-literacy inclusion criterion. Analytically, we report effect sizes (correlations; adjusted β with 95% CI and standardised β), model fit (Adjusted R², F), HC3 robust standard errors, and low multicollinearity, enhancing the stability and interpretability of estimates. Reporting is transparent: FSFI domain-level results accompany totals, standard scoring is used, and both Observed (min–max) and Possible range (min–max) values are shown; a screening prevalence based on the widely used 26.55 cut-off is provided for context (acknowledging its non-pregnant origin and non-diagnostic role). Finally, the manuscript clearly separates results from interpretation and outlines clinically feasible, culturally sensitive implications—namely, brief FGSIS screening with stepped support (psychoeducation, partner-communication guidance, comfort/pain strategies, and referral when indicated)—yielding confounder-adjusted, culturally grounded evidence relevant to antenatal care.

Limitations

This cross-sectional design precludes causal inference. Self-reported measures may be susceptible to social desirability bias, particularly given the sensitivity of sexuality in conservative settings. Although the sample was large, recruitment occurred at a single Obstetrics, Gynecology, and Pediatrics Training and Research Hospital in northern Turkey; sociocultural attitudes, healthcare access, and partner dynamics may differ elsewhere, limiting generalizability. Consecutive, single-center sampling and a non-proportional trimester distribution (reflecting clinic flow) may further restrict external validity and introduce selection/nonresponse bias. The 26.55 cutoff for the FSFI has not been specifically validated in pregnancy, which may introduce misclassification and overestimation; accordingly, our primary inferences rely on continuous-score analyses. The FGSIS has no universal clinical cut-off; thus prevalence estimates are screening-based rather than diagnostic. Potential unmeasured confounding (e.g., relationship quality, mood symptoms, pelvic-floor disorders) and the absence of partner-reported data are additional constraints. Finally, the multivariable model explains a modest proportion of variance, so effect sizes should be interpreted cautiously.
