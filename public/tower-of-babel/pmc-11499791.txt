Fathers’ knowledge of neonatal danger signs and its associated factors in Northwest Ethiopia: a community-based cross-sectional study
Authors: Adugnaw Bantie Kebie, Tilksew Ayalew Abitie, Fikir Tadesse Mequanint, Ambaw Abebaw Emrie, Selamsew Kindie Nega, Werkneh Melkie Tilahun
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study aimed to assess fathers’ knowledge of neonatal danger signs and their associated factors in Northwest Ethiopia.DesignCommunity-based cross-sectional study.SettingEnarji and Enawga Woreda, Northwest Ethiopia.ParticipantsThe study was conducted on 613 participants in Enarji and Enawga Woreda from 13 May to 13 June 2022. Participants were selected using stratified simple random sampling. Participants with serious illnesses who were unable to respond to the questions were excluded from the study. A total of 602 participants completed the questionnaire. Data were collected through face-to-face interviews conducted by four well-trained data collectors. To assess participants’ knowledge, 10 structured and standardised questions were adopted from WHO and the Ethiopian Federal Ministry of Health. The data were analysed using SPSS V.26. A logistic regression model was fitted to identify factors associated with knowledge about neonatal danger signs. An adjusted OR (AOR) with a 95% CI and a p value <0.05 was used to declare factors as significantly associated.ResultLess than a quarter (23.26%) of fathers had good knowledge of neonatal danger signs (95% CI 19.9, 26.8). The study also found that several factors were significant predictors of good knowledge, including: secondary education (AOR 2.98, 95% CI 1.08, 8.24), college education or higher (AOR 3.45, 95% CI 1.09, 10.85), number of children (AOR 3.68, 95% CI 1.62, 8.34), history of sickness in the index baby (AOR 2.18, 95% CI 1.18, 4.01), fathers’ attendance on postnatal care visits (AOR 4.32, 95% CI 2.06, 9.08), history of neonatal death (AOR 3.94, 95% CI 1.80, 8.64), receiving information on neonatal danger signs (AOR 2.71, 95% CI 1.30, 5.64) and health professionals as a source of information (AOR 3.27, 95% CI 1.52, 7.04). These factors were all significantly associated with good knowledge of neonatal danger signs.ConclusionFathers’ knowledge of neonatal danger signs was found to be low. Therefore, substantial efforts are needed to enhance their knowledge. It is essential to develop strategies that actively involve fathers in the continuum of postnatal care for both mothers and newborns. Additionally, providing targeted information on neonatal danger signs through health professionals can significantly improve fathers’ knowledge.

Introduction

The neonatal period is the most critical time for child survival, as newborns are adapting to their new extrauterine environment.1 During this time, neonates are highly susceptible to illness, which can rapidly worsen. They often present with non-specific clinical signs that require careful assessment and timely intervention.2 3 These signs, known as neonatal danger signs (NDS), include fever, inability to breastfeed, persistent vomiting, convulsions, yellowish discolouration of the palms and soles, difficulty breathing, lethargy or unconsciousness, coldness or hypothermia, pus discharge from the umbilicus and redness or pus discharge from the eyes.36 Such signs can significantly increase the risk of mortality for newborns.5

Globally, 2.4 million newborns died in 2019, accounting for 46.2% of all under-five mortality and 64.9% of infant deaths reported in the same year.1 Although the neonatal mortality rate has decreased from 37 to 17 per 1000 live births between 1990 and 2019,7 its contribution to under-five mortality has increased, as it contributed to 41% of under-five deaths in 2000.8 Most of these deaths occur in low- and middle-income countries, including Ethiopia.9 In these nations, many of the deaths occur at home, where only a few family members can identify severe illness, and most neonates are not brought to health facilities when they are sick.9 10

Ethiopia is among the top five countries contributing to half of the neonatal deaths worldwide, with children in these areas having a ninefold greater risk of dying compared with those in high-income countries.11 The most recent Ethiopian Demographic and Health Survey reported a neonatal mortality rate of 33 per 1000 live births,12 increased from 29 per 1000 live births in the previous report.13

Most neonatal deaths are due to preventable and treatable causes.14 Approximately 26.2% of newborns are born with at least one danger sign at birth, and 17.7% will exhibit such signs within 6 weeks of birth.15 Delayed recognition and decision-making to seek appropriate medical care for these signs of serious illness by parents and caregivers remain significant contributors to neonatal deaths.16 17

The United Nations’ Sustainable Development Goal (SDG) 3.2, proposed in 2015, aimed to end preventable deaths of newborns and children under 5 years of age and to reduce neonatal mortality to at least 12 per 1000 live births by 2030.18 Many lives could be saved through early recognition and ensuring skilled care for newborns.1921 Fathers are crucial in improving home care practices and using skilled care for newborns.5 They are often the ultimate decision-makers and primary sources of funding for newborn care, particularly in patriarchal communities.22 This dominance can affect the mother’s access to healthcare when the newborn is ill.23 24

Since early recognition of NDS is critical for reducing neonatal mortality, it is important to improve parental knowledge of these signs and access to healthcare services on noticing them in their newborns.25 26 This is especially relevant in low- and middle-income countries like Ethiopia, where home deliveries and early discharges are common. Parental knowledge of NDS directly impacts their care-seeking behaviour.27 Fathers influence mothers’ healthcare-seeking behaviour and their knowledge of NDS.28 Therefore, enhancing fathers‘ knowledge of NDS is vital for reducing neonatal mortality by improving timely access to health services.29

Rationale of this study

Studies on fathers’ knowledge of NDS are still limited compared with the extensive research on mothers’ knowledge. This study aimed to assess fathers’ understanding of NDS and identify the factors associated with their knowledge. Despite various efforts and interventions, neonatal mortality rates continue to rise. This study, along with other related studies, will help policymakers develop strategies to meet SDG targets by addressing the high rates of neonatal mortality.

Materials and methods

Study design, setting and period

A community-based cross-sectional study was conducted in the Enarji and Enawga districts from 13 May to 13 June 2022. It is a district in the East Gojjam Zone of the Amhara regional state of Ethiopia. It is located 294 km northwest of Addis Ababa, the capital of Ethiopia, and 196 km southeast of Bahir Dar, the capital of the Amhara region. The district encompasses a total of 31 kebeles, including 4 towns and 27 rural areas, with an estimated population of 221 584: 107 385 males and 114 199 females. The under-five age group comprises 30 003 individuals, while those under 6 months of age were 2356. The district is served by 1 primary hospital, 7 health centres and 31 health posts.30 31

Study population

All fathers of children under 6 months of age residing in randomly selected kebeles of Enarji and Enawga Woreda.

Inclusion criteria

Fathers who had children under 6 months of age and had been residents of Enarji and Enawga Woreda for at least 6 months.

Exclusion criteria

Fathers who were seriously ill and unable to respond to questions during data collection were excluded from the study.

Sampling techniques and procedure

Stratified random sampling was used to select participants for the study. The Woreda comprises 31 kebeles, stratified into urban and rural areas (4 urban and 27 rural). Using a lottery method, 2 urban kebeles and 11 rural kebeles were randomly selected. Data on children born in the past 6 months within these kebeles were obtained from the district health information system. Health extension workers (HEWs) provided lists of households with fathers who had a child within this timeframe. The sample size was proportionally allocated across the 13 selected kebeles. Participants were then selected through computer-generated simple random sampling. HEWs and kebele administrators assisted in contacting the selected participants for data collection (figure 1).

Variables

Outcome variable

The dependent variable for this study was the fathers’ knowledge of NDS.

Independent variable

Sociodemographic factors considered in the study included the respondent’s age, the child’s age (in days), place of residence, marital status, the education level and occupation of both parents, the number of children in the household and the primary decision-maker in the family.

The study also examined several obstetric-related factors for the wife, including the place of birth for the index baby, the husband’s attendance during delivery, the mode of delivery, antenatal care (ANC) visits, postnatal care (PNC) visits and whether the husband accompanied the wife to these appointments. Additionally, it examined factors such as the health history of index babies, the type of health facility used, any history of neonatal death and information-related variables.

Operational definitions

NDS indicate abnormal health conditions that can occur during the first 28 days of life.32 In this context, ‘knowledge’ refers to awareness of these signs. To assess fathers’ knowledge, 10 NDS specified by WHO were used.36

Two major questions were employed to evaluate knowledge: the first was a yes/no question to determine whether the fathers were familiar with NDS. The second was an open-ended question that allowed fathers to spontaneously mention any NDS they knew.

Knowledge was assessed based on the fathers‘ ability to list these signs without prompts, using a scale from 0 to 10. According to classifications used by previous researchers,33 34 fathers‘ knowledge was categorised as either good or poor. Fathers were considered to have good knowledge if they could name at least the mean number of the 10 danger signs.33 Conversely, those who could not name at least the mean number were categorised as having poor knowledge.33

Data collection tools and procedures

Data were collected using a structured interviewer-administered questionnaire via the Epi-Collect 5 mobile application. The questionnaire was developed after reviewing relevant literature.3623 29 32 It comprised four sections: sociodemographic characteristics of the respondent and his wife, obstetric characteristics of the wife, information-related questions and knowledge-based questions. The questionnaire was initially prepared in English, translated into Amharic (the local language) and then retranslated into English to ensure consistency. Data were collected through face-to-face interviews conducted by four well-trained clinical nurses under the supervision of two BSc nurses familiar with the area.

Before data collection, the data collectors provided participants with detailed explanations about the study’s aims, potential risks and benefits and confidentiality issues. Participants who agreed to participate signed consent forms. During data collection, data collectors asked all predetermined structured questions in a calm manner.

To assess fathers’ knowledge, participants were first asked whether they were aware of NDS with a yes/no question. Those who answered ‘yes’ were then asked to name the signs they knew without any prompts. Supervisors visited the study sites once per day to address any unclear or ambiguous issues immediately. Completed questionnaires were reviewed daily for completeness and internal consistency by supervisors and the principal investigator.

Data quality control

Data quality was ensured by carefully designing the questionnaires. A pretest was conducted on 5% (31) of the sample size in Motta before the actual study, leading to some modifications. Both data collectors and supervisors received 1 day of training on procedures, techniques and data collection using the Epi-collect 5 mobile application. Supervisors reviewed the questionnaires daily for consistency and completeness. Additionally, the data were double-checked before analysis.

Sample size determination

A separate sample size was estimated for each specific objective. For the first objective, which aimed to determine the magnitude of fathers’ knowledge of NDS, the sample size was calculated using a formula for a single population proportion. The assumptions included a 95% CI level, a margin of error (d) of 0.05 and a proportion of fathers’ knowledge of 0.407, based on a previous study in Gurage Zone, SNNPR, Ethiopia.34

The formula used was:

ni=(zα2)2×p(1−p)d2=((1.96)2×0.407(1−0.407))÷(0.05)2=371

For the second objective, which involved estimating the sample size based on a double population proportion formula using Epi-Info V.7.2 and considering health professionals as a source of information,34 the calculated sample size was 328. Consequently, the sample size for the first objective was larger than that for the second objective. Finally, to account for a 10% non-response rate and a design effect of 1.5, due to the study being population-based with stratified random sampling, the final calculated sample size was adjusted to 613.

Data processing and analysis

The data were checked for completeness, cleaned and then exported to SPSS V.26 for analysis. Descriptive analyses were conducted and presented using text, tables and figures. A multicollinearity test was performed to assess the linear correlation between independent variables using the variance inflation factor (VIF) and tolerance. All values were deemed acceptable, with VIF values <10 and tolerance values >0.1.

Bivariable and multivariable binary logistic regression models were fit. The Hosmer-Lemeshow statistic was used to test the goodness of fit of the model, which was found to be well-fitted, as indicated by a p value of 0.78. Variables with a p value of 0.25 or less in the bivariable analyses were included in the multivariable logistic regression to control for potential confounders. In the multivariable logistic regression, a p value <0.05 with a 95% CI for the adjusted OR (AOR) was used to identify factors significantly associated with fathers’ knowledge of NDS.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and/or the public were not involved in the design, or conduct, or reporting, or dissemination plans of this research.

Results

Sociodemographic and obstetric-related characteristics of respondents and their wives

A study involving 602 fathers achieved a response rate of 98.2%. Participants had a median age of 36.5 years, with an IQR of 12.75 years. Most respondents were rural residents (84.5%) and married (92.4%). About 240 participants (39.9%) were illiterate, while 208 (34.6%) had completed only primary education. Approximately two-thirds of the participants (66.9%) worked as farmers. Regarding decision-making for family healthcare needs, 304 fathers (50.5%) reported making decisions jointly with their wives, while 279 fathers (46.3%) made decisions independently (table 1).

Regarding the obstetric-related characteristics of the respondents’ wives, only 23 (3.8%) were accompanied by their husbands during ANC visits. Most wives delivered their babies in health institutions (502, or 83.4%) and via spontaneous vaginal delivery (530, or 88.0%). A majority of the wives (490, or 81.4%) had PNC visits for their recent deliveries, but only 73 (12.1%) were accompanied by their husbands. Among the index babies, about 116 (19.3%) had experienced illness or exhibited danger signs. Of these, approximately 65 (56%) were not taken to health institutions. Additionally, 53 respondents (8.8%) reported a history of neonatal death (table 1).

Fathers’ knowledge of neonatal danger signs

Only 140 fathers (23.26%, 95% CI 19.9 to 26.8) demonstrated good knowledge of NDS. Knowledge scores ranged from a low of 0/10 (46.5%) to a high of 10/10 (0.33%). Among the 10 danger signs, fever was the most frequently recognised (261, or 81%), followed by difficulty breastfeeding or poor sucking (234, or 72.7%), and difficulty breathing (228, or 70.8%). In contrast, yellowish discolouration of the palms and soles was the least recognised danger sign (12, or 3.7%) (figure 3).

Factors associated with fathers’ knowledge of NDS

n the bivariable binary logistic regression analysis, factors with a p value of 0.25 or less were selected for multivariable analysis. These factors include the child’s age in days, residency, marital status, the education and occupation of both the respondent and the wife, the number of children, the family decision-maker, the father’s presence during ANC, the place and mode of delivery, the father’s attendance during delivery, the index baby’s history of illness, PNC visits, the father’s presence during PNC, history of neonatal death, receipt of information about NDS and whether health professionals were the source of this information.

In the multivariable regression analysis, the factors significantly associated with the fathers’ knowledge of NDS included the respondent’s level of education, having more than two children, a history of illness in the index baby, the fathers’ presence during PNC visits, a history of neonatal death, receiving information about NDS, and whether this information came from health professionals.

The odds of having good knowledge of NDS were 2.98 times higher among fathers with secondary education compared with those who were illiterate (AOR 2.98, 95% CI 1.08 to 8.24). Similarly, fathers with college education or higher had 3.45 times higher odds of possessing good knowledge of NDS compared with illiterate fathers (AOR 3.45, 95% CI 1.09 to 10.85). Fathers with three or more children had 3.68 times higher odds of having good knowledge of NDS than those with two or fewer children (AOR 3.68, 95% CI 1.62 to 8.34). Additionally, the odds of having good knowledge of NDS were 2.18 times higher among fathers whose index babies had a history of illness compared with those whose babies did not (AOR 2.18, 95% CI 1.18 to 4.01).

This study also found that the odds of having good knowledge of NDS were 4.32 times higher among fathers who accompanied their wives during PNC visits compared with those who did not (AOR 4.32, 95% CI 2.06 to 9.08). Similarly, fathers with a history of neonatal death had 3.94 times higher odds of possessing good knowledge of NDS compared with those without such a history (AOR 3.94, 95% CI 1.80 to 8.64). Fathers who had received information about NDS had 2.71 times higher odds of having good knowledge compared with those who had not (AOR 2.71, 95% CI 1.30 to 5.64). Furthermore, the odds of having good knowledge of NDS were 3.27 times higher among fathers who received information from health professionals compared with those who did not (AOR 3.27, 95% CI 1.52 to 7.04) (table 2).

Discussion

This study assessed fathers’ knowledge of NDS and the associated factors in Northwest Ethiopia. The results showed that less than one-fourth of the participants had good knowledge of NDS. Factors significantly associated with fathers’ knowledge of NDS included the respondent’s level of education, number of children, a history of illness in the index baby, the father’s presence during PNC visits, a history of neonatal death, receiving information about NDS and whether the information came from health professionals.

The findings of this study indicated that 23.26% of fathers had good knowledge of NDS (95% CI 19.9 to 26.8). This prevalence is lower than reported in Gurage Zone (40.7%)34 and Nepal (71.8%),29 but higher than in studies from Tanzania (8.8%),38 rural Bangladesh (7%)39 and southwestern India (0.83%).40 These differences may be due to variations in sociodemographic characteristics, sociocultural factors, sample sizes, time periods and accessibility of health services.

The present study found that the respondent’s level of education was significantly associated with fathers’ knowledge of NDS. Fathers with secondary and higher education levels were 2.98 and 3.45 times more likely to have good knowledge of NDS compared with those who were illiterate, respectively. This finding is consistent with previous studies conducted in the Gurage Zone, Ethiopia34 and India.40 41 One possible explanation is that educated individuals are more likely to have been exposed to health-related information through their academic experiences and are more adept at accessing and using information from various sources, including social media, which is often more information-oriented.42

Fathers with three or more children were 3.68 times more likely to have good knowledge of NDS compared with those with fewer children. This finding aligns with a study conducted in the Gurage Zone.34 This increased knowledge may be due to repeated exposure to healthcare information regarding various health issues experienced by previous children. Similarly, research among mothers indicated that attending clinic visits after childbirth and receiving advice on neonatal care significantly influenced their knowledge and decision to seek hospital care for danger signs.43 In this study, fathers whose index child had experienced illness were 2.18 times more likely to have good knowledge of NDS. This finding is consistent with previous research showing that exposure to information on newborn health from healthcare professionals enhances knowledge.44 45

Respondents who accompanied their wives during PNC visits were 4.32 times more likely to have good knowledge of NDS. This is likely because PNC visits often include counselling on when to seek medical attention for a newborn, which covers identifying danger signs, understanding both the mother’s and the newborn’s care needs and knowing where to access services if necessary.46 Fathers who attend these visits with their wives are thus more likely to receive and retain this crucial information.1 13 Similarly, respondents who had heard of NDS and received information from health professionals were 2.71 and 3.27 times more likely to have good knowledge of NDS, respectively. These findings are supported by studies conducted in Ethiopia34 and Tunisia.38 This association may be due to the thorough and credible information provided by health professionals, who are often highly respected and whose advice is given significant attention.46 47 Access to accurate information is essential for improving knowledge.47

Respondents with a history of neonatal death were 3.94 times more likely to have good knowledge of NDS. This finding is supported by previous reports, which indicate that mothers who experienced neonatal death tended to have better knowledge of NDS.48 A possible explanation for this is that fathers who have experienced neonatal death are likely to recognise the danger signs that contributed to the death of their previous newborn.

Our findings suggest that public health policy planners are better suited to consider increasing fathers’ involvement in PNC and ensure that sufficient information about NDS is provided during these appointments. Additionally, health professionals are better suited to encourage mothers to bring their husbands to PNC appointments.

Strength and limitation of the study

This community-based study aimed to explore factors that determine knowledge status, including fathers who have visited healthcare facilities and those who have not, for various reasons. However, due to the limitations of the cross-sectional study design, we were unable to establish causal relationships. Additionally, our study focused exclusively on fathers and quantitatively assessed factors associated with NDS.

Conclusion and recommendation

In this study, fathers’ knowledge of NDS was lower compared with a previous study conducted in Ethiopia. Factors significantly associated with better knowledge of NDS among fathers included having a secondary or higher level of education, the number of children, a history of illness in the current baby, attendance at PNC, a history of neonatal death, exposure to information on NDS and receiving this information from health professionals.

To improve fathers’ knowledge of NDS, strategies should be developed to enhance their participation in PNC and ensure they receive relevant information. Additionally, health professionals should encourage mothers to bring their husbands to subsequent postnatal care visits. Furthermore, future researchers should focus on investigating predictors of knowledge by including both mothers and fathers as participants and employing a qualitative research design.
