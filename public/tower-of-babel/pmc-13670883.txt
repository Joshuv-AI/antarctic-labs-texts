Reprogramming tumor cells with LCOR mRNA nanotherapy to enhance immunotherapy
Authors: Gabriel Serra‐Mir, Elena Haro‐Martínez, Sandra Blasco‐Benito, Chiara Cannatà, José Ángel Palomeque, Anna López‐Plana, Maria Sanz‐Flores, Marc Sabartés, Cristina Fornaguera, Toni Celià‐Terrassa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTranscription factors (TFs) are often considered undruggable due to their structural complexity. mRNA technologies can overcome this therapeutic limitation by enabling dynamic protein replacement therapies. Here, we explore the TF activity of LCOR (Ligand‐dependent corepressor), which suppresses tumor growth, at least in part by inducing the antigen presentation machinery (APM) in tumor cells and constraining cellular plasticity. These effects facilitate tumor immune recognition and immune‐mediated tumor eradication. We used poly β‐(amino esters) (pBAE) nanoparticles (NPs) for local delivery of Lcor mRNA in multiple breast cancer models. pBAE‐NPs efficiently encapsulate mRNA and facilitate cellular uptake, achieving optimal endosomal escape, resulting in high transfection efficiency in vitro and in vivo, restoring LCOR function in tumor cells and engaging their APM. In preclinical models, intratumoral delivery of Lcor mRNA reduced tumor growth. Importantly, the combination of Lcor mRNA‐loaded RH‐pBAE‐NPs with anti‐PDL1 or anti‐CTLA4 immunotherapies eradicated most of the tumors in our preclinical TNBC model. Overall, our nanotherapeutic strategy emerges as an innovative TF‐replacement therapy in a lock‐in/wipe‐out fashion, leveraging the immunogenic effects of LCOR to eradicate breast cancer tumors when combined with immunotherapy.Keywords: immunotherapy, mRNA therapy, phenotype constraint, transcription factor, triple‐negative breast cancer, tumor cell reprogramming

Introduction

Transcription factors (TFs) have traditionally been considered undruggable, mostly because of their unstructured three‐dimensional (3D) shape. This feature makes it almost impossible to target TFs with small molecules, making it challenging to modulate their activity for clinical applications [1]. In recent years, mRNA has emerged as a promising therapeutic biomolecule, bypassing the traditional drawbacks of small molecules and achieving unprecedented potential for a range of clinical needs, as evidenced by COVID‐19 vaccines. mRNA technology is not only valuable for prophylactic vaccines but also for transient protein replacement therapies, which aim to substitute or restore specific proteins in target cells, opening new avenues in modulating TFs' activity to treat different disorders [2, 3, 4, 5].

Despite significant progress in mRNA technologies and nucleic acid delivery systems, achieving direct in vivo effective expression in target cells remains a major challenge in protein replacement therapies. Limitations mainly arise from mRNA stability in physiological fluids and low cell membrane penetration, which restrict success to the carrier and delivery system. Cationic polymer‐based nanovectors are among the most promising candidates for mRNA delivery [6]. Polyplex formation relies on electrostatic interactions between the cationic amine groups of polymers and the polyanionic mRNAs. This interaction leads to polynucleotide condensation into nanosized particles with a positive surface charge, which protects the nucleic acid and enhances stability. Poly β‐(amino esters) (pBAEs) polymers are a class of biocompatible and biodegradable polymers composed of amines and cleavable ester bonds in their backbones. These structures facilitate plasmatic cell penetration thanks to their cationic charges and endosomal escape due to protonation of tertiary amines at lower pH [7]. The use of oligopeptide‐end‐modified variants enhances their transfection capacity while preserving their nontoxic character [8, 9].

mRNA protein replacement approaches have been used in tumor cells to correct genetic alterations in preclinical models [2, 3, 8]. However, these approaches have not been used to modulate master TFs reprogramming cell phenotypes, such as differentiation, stemness, or immune evasion. We have previously described Ligand‐dependent corepressor (LCOR) as a breast cancer (BC) tumor cell differentiating factor reducing stemness, as well as a master TF regulating tumor cell immunogenicity in triple‐negative breast cancer (TNBC) and hormone receptor‐positive (HR+) breast cancer [10, 11, 12]. LCOR increases the immune detection and immune‐mediated killing by orchestrating the transcriptional activation of the antigen processing and presentation machinery (APM) independently of interferon‐gamma (IFN‐γ) signaling [11]. This observation is relevant since the downregulation of the APM is a major cause of resistance to immune‐checkpoint blockade (ICB) therapy [13, 14, 15], which is related to the loss of components of the IFN‐γ signaling pathway [16, 17, 18].

In breast cancer, ICB is part of the standard‐of‐care (SOC) for stage II/III TNBC and PD‐L1+ advanced TNBC [19, 20]. Only anti‐PD‐1/PD‐L1 therapy has been approved for TNBC, although others are under intense research, such as anti‐CTLA4 therapy, which has shown moderately positive results in recent clinical trials in early and advanced TNBC [21, 22]. However, in these patients, ICB leads to only a modest increase in response rates compared to chemotherapy alone [20]. Understanding and overcoming these barriers is critical for fully harnessing the potential of ICB therapies and improving outcomes for patients with TNBC. Here we demonstrate proof‐of‐concept for the therapeutic modulation of breast cancer cell phenotypes using pBAE‐nanoparticles (NPs) loaded with Lcor mRNA in preclinical mouse models. This approach showed exceptional efficacy in intratumoral settings, leading to tumor eradication when combined with PD‐L1 or CTLA4 blockade therapies. These innovative therapeutic strategies in a lock‐in/wipe‐out or one‐two punch fashion, open up new avenues for expanding the use of LCOR and TFs replacement therapies in immunotherapy and oncology.

Material and methods

Reagents

The reagents used for pBAE synthesis were bought from Sigma Aldrich® (St. Louis, MO, USA) and the oligopeptides CH3 (NH2‐Cys‐His‐His‐His‐COOH) and CR3 (NH2‐Cys‐Arg‐Arg‐Arg‐COOH), with a purity higher than 95%, from Shanghai Yaxian Chemical Co. Ltd (Jiading District, Shanghai, China). All synthetic mRNAs were supplied by Cellerna Bioscience (Baesweiler, North Rhine‐Westphalia, Germany), while the Cyanine3 and Cyanine5 NHS ester dyes (Cy3 and Cy5) were purchased from Lumiprobe GmbH (Westminster, MD, USA).

Synthesis of end‐modified poly(β‐aminoester) polymers

The pBAE C6 polymer used was synthesized by a Michael addition reaction, as previously described [23]. Briefly, 5‐aminopentanol and 1‐hexylamine were added to 1,4‐butanediol diacrylate at the same ratio and using a slight excess of the latter (molar ratio of amine : diacrylate, 1 : 1.1). The reaction was carried out at 90 °C for 24 h. The polymer was further modified at its acrylate ends by the introduction of cationic oligopeptides, either CR3 or CH3 (molar ratio of polymer : peptide, 1 : 2.5). The mixture was stirred in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) overnight at room temperature to later be precipitated in a mixture of diethyl ether/acetone. 1H‐NMR spectral characterization was performed to verify each synthesis step and the formation of the desired end structure.

Nanoparticle formulation

The nanoparticles (NPs) were prepared using a mixture of polymer and nucleic acid, at a mass ratio of 25 : 1. Two starting solutions were prepared in sodium acetate buffer (12.5 mm, pH 5.2). One contained the mRNAs of choice at a final concentration of 0.5 mg·mL−1, while the other contained the polymer mixtures at a final concentration of 12.5 mg·mL−1. The polymer combinations used were C6‐CR3 and C6‐CH3 at a mass ratio of 60 : 40. The mRNA solution was pipetted into the polymer solution and thoroughly mixed for 15–20 s, followed by a 30‐min incubation at room temperature. Later, the solution was precipitated in the same volume of DEPC water as that of the NP formulation, and another equal volume of HEPES buffer (20 mm, pH 7.4) with 4% (w/v) of sucrose was added [24].

mRNA labeling

The pH of the commercially available mRNA was first tested to determine whether it needed to be basified to achieve a neutral pH. The mRNA was mixed with Cy3 at a ratio of 10 : 3 (Cy3 : mRNA) (V1) and stirred for 2 h at room temperature in the dark. Later, 0.1 times the V1 of a sodium chloride solution (5 m) was added to the previous mixture (V2). Twice the V2 of cold pure ethanol was then added to the mixture and frozen overnight. The mixture was then centrifuged in a precooled centrifuge at 14 000× g at 4 °C for 15 min. The supernatant was then removed, and the pellet was rinsed with 70% ethanol and centrifuged again several times until the supernatant was clear. The pellet was then left to dry and characterized using the nanodrop setting of an Infinite M Plex TECAN Plate reader, where the concentration and purity of the mRNA and the amount of Cy3 in the sample were calculated.

Polymer labeling

The C6‐CR3 at 100 mg·mL−1 was mixed with a solution of Cy5 at 10 mg·mL−1 in DMSO, taking 35 μL and 60 μL of each respectively. Triethylamine and DMSO were then added while protecting the reaction from light. The mixture was stirred for 20 h at room temperature. Next, it was precipitated in diethyl ether : acetone at a ratio of 7 : 3, vortexed, and centrifuged at 2254× g for 10 min. The supernatant was removed, and the pellet was rinsed twice with the same solution. The labeled polymer was left to dry for 24 h and then adjusted to 100 mg·mL−1 in DMSO. Later, the amount of Cy5 was tested through a calibration curve with several dilutions of Cy5 and read by fluorescence in an Infinite M Plex TECAN Plate reader.

C6‐CH3 labeling followed the same protocol but using 56 μL of C6‐CH3 and 100 μL of Cy5.

mRNA complexation capacity and stability

The complexation capacity of the mRNA in the NPs was tested using the Ribogreen kit. The NPs were formulated at a ratio of 60 : 40 (C6‐CR3 : C6‐CH3) of the oligopeptide‐modified polymers with e GFP mRNA as a reporter gene. For the test, fresh NPs (free mRNA) were prepared and diluted in buffer and also in buffer with heparin, causing NP disruption and thus total exposure of the encapsulated mRNA (total mRNA). The different NP conditions were then compared to three standard curves (mRNA range, heparin, and NPs with heparin). Following the equation below, the percentage of encapsulated mRNA or encapsulation efficiency was calculated.

Physicochemical characterization

NPs were characterized by Dynamic Light Scattering (DLS; ZetaSizer Nano ZS, Malvern). In this regard, their hydrodynamic diameter (nm) was measured, as well as their surface charge (mV) and poly‐dispersity index (PDI), by taking measures at room temperature with a 633 nm laser wavelength and a detector at an angle of 173°. The measurements were taken in triplicate, and the results are represented as mean ± standard deviation.

Stability by fluorescence resonance energy transfer

NP stability was tested by fluorescence resonance energy transfer (FRET). To this end, the genetic material was labeled with Cy3 and the polymer with Cy5, thereby allowing the study of the complexation and structural organization of the NPs. Higher energy transfer indicates closer proximity between the fluorophores, thereby signifying the formation of a complexed NP. Conversely, a decrease in energy suggests the separation of the polymer and genetic material, leading to NP disassembly. FRET measurements were carried out using a TECAN Infinite M Plex microplate reader and a flat black 96‐well plate. This test was performed in phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS) at 25 °C, simulating laboratory stability, and in a solution of 50 mg·mL−1 of Bovine Serum Albumin (BSA) at 37 °C to simulate physiological stability.

Cell culture

AT3 (RRID:CVCL_VN46), 4T07 (RRID:CVCL_B383), 66cl4 (RRID:CVCL_9721), 4T1 (RRID:CVCL_0125), and EpRAS cells were kindly provided by Dr. Yibin Kang (Princeton University). EO771 (RRID:CVCL_GR23), EMT6 (RRID:CVCL_1923), MDA‐MB‐231 (RRID:CVCL_0062), and MCF7 (RRID:CVCL_0031) were purchased from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC; Manassas, VA, USA). The identity of the cell lines used in this study was validated by short tandem repeat (STR) profiling and matched to reference databases to confirm authenticity. Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium (DMEM; ATCC) was used to culture cells. The cell culture medium was supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), Pen‐Strep (100 U·mL−1), and L‐Glutamine (100 μg·mL−1). Experiments were performed at 37 °C in 5% CO2 conditions and the normal level of O2 in a cell culture incubator. All cell lines were routinely tested for Mycoplasma contamination.

Cellular uptake of dye‐labeled mRNA‐loaded NPs

To monitor the cellular NP uptake, Cy3‐eGFP‐mRNA‐Cy5‐NPs and Cy3‐Δhth‐Lcor mutant‐mRNA‐Cy5‐NPs were prepared. AT3 or 4T07 cells were first seeded in 96‐well plates at a density of 1 × 104 cells per well and incubated at 37 °C in 5% CO2 for 24 h. They were then incubated with medium (DMEM) containing Cy3‐eGFP‐mRNA Cy5‐RH‐pBAE‐NPs or Cy3‐Δhth‐mRNA‐Cy5‐NPs for different times. The cells were then washed with PBS, resuspended using Trypsin‐0.25% EDTA, and processed for flow cytometry analysis or live cellular uptake monitoring. Differences in transfection efficacy are observed depending on the initial cell number and plate format used, underscoring the importance of maintaining a consistent μg mRNA‐NPs‐to‐cell number ratio across experiments.

RT‐qPCR analysis

Total RNA was purified using the RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen; Venlo, Limburg, Netherlands) and reverse transcribed into cDNA using the High‐Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Life Technologies; Carlsbad, CA, USA). RT‐qPCR was performed using the LightCycler 480 SYBR Green I Master Mix (Roche; Basel, Basel‐Stadt, Switzerland). Data were collected using quantstudio 12 k flex software. mRNA expression was normalized by the expression of Gapdh (RT‐qPCR primers are listed in Table S1).

Flow cytometry analysis

Flow cytometry data were collected on either a Fortessa Flow Cytometer (BD) using facsdiva v.9.0 software and analyzed by flowjo software v.10.8.1. Briefly, cells were trypsinized, washed twice with PBS, 5% FBS, and 0.5 mm EDTA, passed through a 70‐μm filter, and counted before proceeding with a general staining protocol using anti‐SIINFEKL (OVA) or anti‐H‐2Kd (Flow cytometry antibodies are listed in the Table S2).

Immunoblotting

Protein extracts from cultured cells were lysed using RIPA buffer. Equal amounts of protein were determined using a bicinchoninic acid protein assay kit (Pierce/Thermo Scientific), following the manufacturer's instructions. After gel electrophoresis and protein transfer, membranes were blocked with 5% BSA in TBST for 1 h at room temperature with gentle shaking. Membranes were rinsed and then incubated overnight at 4 °C with appropriate primary antibodies (listed in the Table S2). The immunoreactive bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) detection system (Merck; Rahway, NJ, USA). Data were collected using nine alliance q9 software in an UVITEC Cambridge system.

Animals

Approximately 8‐week‐old female BALB/c and C57BL/6J mice were purchased from Charles River Laboratories and housed in a pathogen‐free animal facility at the Barcelona Biomedical Research Park. All animal procedures presented in this study were approved by the Ethical Committee for Animal Research of the Barcelona Biomedical Research Park and the Departament de Medi Ambient i Habitatge de la Generalitat de Catalunya (Catalan Government) under authorization number 10578. For all experimental procedures, euthanasia was applied once tumors reached 1500 mm3 or when animal health was compromised. Five animals (with the same genotype and sex) were maintained in individually ventilated Makrolon cages at a temperature of 22 ± 2 °C, with a relative humidity of 55 ± 5%, 12 h light : dark cycle starting at 08 : 00 am, and food and water ad libitum.

Orthotopic tumor model preparation

For primary tumor models, 5 × 103 4T07 cells or 5 × 105 AT3 cells were orthotopically injected into the MFP using 1 : 1 PBS : Matrigel (Corning). Tumor volume was measured twice weekly with digital calipers, and calculations were applied (π × length × width2/6). In ICB assays, once tumors reached 30–50 mm3, they were treated with either pBAE‐RH mRNA at a dose of 250 μg·kg−1 by intratumoral (i.t.) injection twice a week, anti‐PDL1 at a dose of 5 mg·kg−1 by intraperitoneal (i.p.) injection once a week, or their combination. On the other hand, mice were treated with anti‐CTLA4 with an initial dose of 5 mg·kg−1, followed by five doses at 0.5 mg·kg−1, by i.p. injection every 7 days, or their combination with pBAE‐RH mRNA at a dose of 250 μg·kg−1 by i.t. injection twice a week (all ICB antibodies are listed in the Table S2). In the therapeutic experiments, the Δhth‐Lcor mutant mRNA, which lacks the DNA‐binding domain, was used as the control mRNA. Animals were classified as responders (R) when tumor regression was observed, nonresponders (NR) when tumor progressed, or treatment‐related deaths (TRD) when premature death associated with repeated anti‐PD‐L1 administration prevented final tumor‐response assessment. Treatment‐related deaths were censored at the time of death in the tumor‐related survival analysis.

Tissue immunofluorescence

Paraffin‐embedded tissue samples were cut into 3‐μm slices. After tissue dehydration and deparaffinization, antigens were retrieved with 0.5 m citrate, pH 6, in a pressure bath for 20 min. Tissue slices were incubated in blocking buffer (PBS, 2.5% Normal Goat Serum, 0.1% Triton X) and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibody (listed in the Table S2). Tissue was washed, and fluorescent conjugated secondary antibodies were incubated for 1 h at room temperature. It was then washed again, and samples were mounted in DAPI medium (0100‐20; SouthernBiotech; Birmingham, AL, USA).

Fluorescent in situ hybridization (FISH)

Paraffin‐embedded tissue samples were cut into 3‐μm slices. After tissue dehydration and deparaffinization, samples were treated for 10 min with hydrogen peroxide. Antigens were retrieved with 0.5 m citrate (pH 6) in a pressure bath for 20 min. Tissue slices were treated for 30 min with Protease solution at 40 °C. The RNAscope™ 2.5 HD detection kit was then used following the manufacturer's instructions (322360; ACD; Newark, CA, USA). Lcor mRNA was detected using mouse Lcor RNAscope™. Samples were washed and mounted in DAPI medium.

pBAE‐NP transfection efficacy in vivo

Once tumors reached the desired volume, mice were euthanized and tumors were extracted. Samples were mechanically digested followed by a 30‐min enzymatic digestion with 2 mg·mL−1 Collagenase A and 0.5 mg·mL−1 DNAse I. Cells were passed through a 100‐μm strainer and incubated for 5 min at 37 °C in ACK lysing buffer. They were then passed through a 40‐μm strainer, counted, and processed for flow cytometry analysis. Statistical analysis

The sample size of the animal studies was determined based on pilot experiments or previous studies. Experiments were performed with female mice of a similar age. Mice were randomized before cell injections and treatment allocation. Researchers were not blinded during experimentation and outcome since it was necessary to monitor each group. For all in vivo and in vitro experiments, independent biological replicates are indicated in the figure legends. Results are represented as mean ± S.E.M. Normality of data were checked using the Shapiro test and homoscedasticity using Bartlett's test. For comparisons between two groups, statistical significance was assessed using a t‐test or a Mann–Whitney U‐test. For multiple independent groups, one‐way ANOVA, the Kruskal–Wallis H‐test or multiple mixed‐effect analysis was used. For statistical significance. *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, ****P ≤ 0.0001. All experiments were reproduced in at least three independent biological replicates unless specified in the figure legend.

Results

mRNA delivery by pBAE‐NPs in vitro

pBAE‐NPs were formed by the electrostatic interaction of mRNA with the polymer, using a polymer combination of 60% arginine (R) and 40% histidine (H) oligopeptides as cationic end‐modified pBAEs (Fig. 1A), previously confirmed to be nontoxic [25]. NP synthesis yielded small nanometric and monodisperse particles (Fig. 1B and Fig. S1A) with high transfection efficiency in vitro and in vivo in several hosts [8, 26]. The efficiency of eGFP mRNA‐loaded RH‐pBAE‐NPs to transfect mRNA into different mouse breast cancer cells (AT3, 4T07, EO771, EMT6, 66cl4, EpRAS, and 4T1) was tested using NPs encapsulating eGFP mRNA, at a dose of 2.5 μg·mL−1. After 48‐h treatment, AT3 and 4T07 cells displayed the highest translated eGFP protein (Fig. S1B) after NPs uptake. In human models MDA‐MB‐231 and MCF7 cells, the NPs also showed high eGFP mRNA transfection efficiency (Fig. S1C). pBAE‐NPs efficiently encapsulated 60% of Lcor mRNA (Fig. S1D). To determine the endosomal escape of these NPs across the different cell lines, we fluorescently labeled the polymer and mRNA with Cyanine (Cy)5 and Cy3, respectively, allowing to monitor endosomal escape and mRNA translation kinetics by flow cytometry and fluorescence microscopy. Time course analysis by flow cytometry and fluorescence microscopy analysis of Cy5, Cy3, and eGFP fluorescence showed higher uptake and translation of eGFP mRNA in 4T07 cells (Fig. 1C,D), with the expected dose‐dependent effect (Fig. S1E), and flow cytometry of eGFP positivity percentage (Fig. 1E). Note that these percentages are influenced by differences in cell proliferation rates over the course of the culture period. These results demonstrate that tumor cells can uptake the mRNA‐loaded RH‐pBAE‐NPs and that these particles can escape the endosomal system and deliver the mRNA to the cytoplasm for proper translation of the protein.

Lcor mRNA delivery by pBAE‐NPs and TF functionality in tumor cells in vitro

To test the dynamics and ability of our system to deliver TFs to tumor cells, we treated AT3 and 4T07 cells in vitro with pBAE‐NPs loaded with Lcor mRNA. Synthetic Lcor mRNA contained a Cap1, 5′ and 3′ untranslated regions (UTR) and a standard polyA tail (Fig. S2A), and all uracils were replaced for 5‐methoxyuracil (5‐moU) to avoid immunogenic reactions [27, 28]. The Δhth‐Lcor mutant mRNA was used as negative control (Fig. S2A). This mutant lacks the HTH DNA‐binding domain (DBD), which is responsible for DNA binding to regulatory regions to induce gene expression [10, 11]. First, we measured and detected high levels of Lcor mRNA by qRT‐PCR (Fig. 2A), increasing in a dose‐dependent manner (Fig. S2B), as expected. RH‐pBAE NPs were stable at 25 °C for 24 h (Fig. S2C). In contrast, under conditions simulating the physiological environment (37 °C), a decrease in FRET signaling was detected as the incubation time increased, indicating disassembly of the NPs after 2 h (Fig. S2C). The rapid disassembly of NPs within 2 h at 37 °C is a crucial feature of our delivery system, as it facilitates timely release of the mRNA payload inside the target cells, which is essential for efficient translation and biological activity. After treatment of the cells with the NPs in vitro, we detected cellular uptake of mRNA, with a peak at 6 h, which was maintained up to 36 h in culture (Fig. 2A), thereby indicating a rapid uptake and release of mRNA and a transient cell expression. Importantly, to measure the translated product of the Lcor mRNA delivered, we performed western blot analysis. High levels of LCOR protein were observed in tumor cells after 12 h of Lcor mRNA‐loaded RH‐pBAE‐NPs treatment (Fig. 2B). These findings indicate that this technology can deliver synthetic Lcor mRNA into tumor cells, where it is efficiently translated into LCOR protein.

Next, to assess whether LCOR can restore its function, we challenged Lcor function as suppressor of stemness and as APM inducer [10, 11, 12]. The results showed a functional effect of the NP treatment mediated by LCOR‐inducing APM genes in AT3 and 4T07 cell lines after 36 h of treatment (Fig. 2C). In addition, we tested the previously described ability of Lcor to repress stemness by RT‐qPCR analysis. Remarkably, 24 h after a single dose of Lcor mRNA significantly reduced the expression of stem cell gene programs such as Sox2, Oct4, Nanog, and Zeb1 (Fig. 2D). In addition, we used a reporter with the Nanog promoter which has binding sites for Sox2, Oct4 and Nanog to measure their transcriptomic activity reported by Ds‐eGFP expression [29]. Treatment with Lcor mRNA reduced both the percentage of Ds‐eGFP+ cells and the mean fluorescence intensity in 4T07 cells (Fig. S2D), showcasing the ability of Lcor mRNA therapy (with a single dose) to suppress stemness‐associated cell states. Furthermore, Lcor mRNA showed a trend toward increasing other IFN targets such as, Pd‐l1 (Cd274) expression, while no changes were observed in Cd80 nor Cd86 (Fig. S2E). Together these results further support the reprogramming capacity of Lcor mRNA therapy, reconfiguring tumor cell states toward differentiation and high immunogenic cells.

Next, we used AT3 cells that constitutively overexpress ovalbumin (OVA). In these cells, OVA is cleaved, generating the SIINFEKL antigen peptide presented in the H‐2Kb context. This can be used to measure overall APM activity using the anti‐SIINFEKL antibody via flow cytometry [30]. The results showed an increase of OVA‐SIINFEKL presentation, indicating the enhanced activity of the APM induced by the Lcor mRNA‐loaded RH‐pBAE‐NPs (Fig. 2E). When comparing the effect of Lcor mRNA on 4T07 cell line, the results did not reach significance (Fig. 2F). Consistently, measuring Mhc‐I molecules on the cell surface of AT3 and 4T07 cells showed an increase of Mhc‐I levels. Overall, these results demonstrate the efficiency of our mRNA therapy nanotechnology to rescue the function of the LCOR TF in inducing tumor cell immunogenicity and thus modulating tumor phenotypes [11].

pBAE‐NPs efficiently deliver mRNA into breast cancer tumors in vivo

To test the capacity of pBAE‐NPs to deliver mRNA to tumor tissue from preclinical models, we encapsulated eGFP or Firefly Luciferase (FLuc) mRNA for fluorescence or bioluminescence (BLI) detection of recipient cells. We generated primary tumors by orthotopic mammary fat pad (MFP) injection of 4T07 cells into immunocompetent BALB/c mice. When tumors reached 40–50 mm3, we treated them intratumorally with pBAE‐NPs loaded with 5 μg of synthetic FLuc or eGFP mRNA. We detected BLI at 3 h, meaning that tumor cells had taken up the mRNA‐loaded NPs and translated a luciferase active protein within 3 h. Luciferase expression peaked 10 h after administration (Fig. S3A). In addition, we injected eGFP mRNA‐loaded RH‐pBAE‐NPs into mice bearing 4T07‐mCherry+ tumors. After 6 h, we harvested and digested the tumor tissues to evaluate, by eGFP fluorescence using flow cytometry, showing how tumor cells, and also stromal cells to a lesser extent, incorporated and translated the mRNA (Fig. 3A), thereby indicating substantial transfection capacity in vivo after one dose of 5 μg of mRNA‐loaded NPs.

Next, we tested the detection of Lcor mRNA delivery in vivo. To this end, we used specific Lcor probes for tissue in situ hybridization (ISH) for Lcor mRNA detection and immunofluorescence (IF) for LCOR protein detection. After local intratumoral administration of 5 μg of Lcor mRNA‐loaded RH‐pBAE‐NPs when tumors reached 40–50 mm3 (Fig. S3B), we observed a rapid increase in Lcor mRNA in the tumor tissue (Fig. 3B). Consistently, similar results were obtained by RT‐qPCR (Fig. 3C). To unravel the protein dynamics, we used synthetic Lcor mRNA including the HA tag sequence to generate LCOR‐HA protein and uniquely detect the ectopic protein using anti‐HA by IF (Fig. 3D). Importantly, we validated that Lcor mRNA therapy led to increased APM genes in 4T07 cells (Fig. 3E) and AT3 cells (Fig. S3C,D).

To further functionally validate the tumor reconfiguration and immune microenvironment impact induced by Lcor mRNA‐loaded RH‐pBAE‐NPs treatment in vivo, 4T07 tumors were collected at different time points after administration. After Lcor mRNA‐loaded RH‐pBAE‐NPs administration, increasing Lcor mRNA levels (Fig. S3E) and increasing T‐cell infiltration were detected up to 24‐h post‐treatment, while decreasing after 48 h after treatment (Fig. 3F). To further characterize the T‐cell activity induced by Lcor mRNA‐loaded RH‐pBAE‐NPs, we performed flow cytometry‐based immune profiling of 4T07 tumors after treatment. Although no significant differences were observed in the overall abundance of CD4+ or CD8+ T cells at 24 h (Fig. S3F), tumors treated with Lcor mRNA‐loaded RH‐pBAE‐NPs showed a significant increase in the proportion of activated CD8+ T cells expressing CD69, GZMB, and PD1 compared with control‐treated tumors (Fig. 3G). These findings suggest that, beyond transiently promoting lymphocyte infiltration, Lcor‐mRNA‐therapy enhances T‐cell activation and acquisition of an effector‐like phenotype within the tumor microenvironment.

Lcor mRNA nanotherapy synergizes with immune‐checkpoint inhibitors in preclinical models

To test the efficacy of the TF‐replacement therapy using Lcor mRNA‐loaded RH‐pBAE‐NPs, we first generated 4T07 breast primary tumors in immunocompetent BALB/c mice. Once tumors reached 40–50 mm3, Lcor mRNA‐loaded RH‐pBAE‐NPs were administered at a dose of 250 μg·kg−1 by intratumoral (i.t.) injection twice a week. In addition, we combined the Lcor mRNA therapy with the anti‐PDL1 immune‐checkpoint inhibitor (ICI), at a dose of 5 mg·kg−1, by intraperitoneal (i.p.) injection once a week (Fig. 4A). Results point toward Lcor mRNA‐loaded RH‐pBAE‐NPs monotherapy being sufficient to reduce 4T07 tumor growth (Fig. 4B, and Fig. S4B); however, the differences did not reach statistical significance. Importantly, the combination of Lcor mRNA‐loaded RH‐pBAE‐NPs with anti‐PDL1 therapy not only reduced tumor growth but also led to tumor eradication in 5 out of 7 mice (Fig. 4C). Regarding toxicity, 3 mice experienced premature death due to anti‐PDL1 toxicity, 2 mice in Δhth mRNA + anti‐PDL1 and one in Lcor mRNA + anti‐PDL1 (Fig. 4D), as previously reported in Balb/c mice bearing tumors from 4T1 series [31], however the combined Lcor mRNA‐loaded RH‐pBAE‐NPs plus anti‐PDL1 therapy did not have additional adverse immune effects, and mouse weights were similar across conditions during the treatment (Fig. S4A). The combination treatment also significantly increased tumor disease‐related survival, with treatment‐related deaths censored at the time of death (Fig. 4E). These results indicate that our Lcor mRNA nanotherapy synergized with anti‐PDL1 and overcame resistance in 4T07 tumors.

Overall, these results demonstrate the potential of our strategy in leveraging mRNA therapy to rescue the LCOR TF and reconfigure tumor phenotypic properties. The combination of Lcor mRNA‐loaded RH‐pBAE‐NPs with different ICIs showed high efficiency in preclinical models, thus supporting the potential of local delivery of Lcor mRNA‐loaded RH‐pBAE‐NPs in combination with ICIs as a promising immunotherapeutic strategy for potential future clinical applications.

Discussion

Here we provide evidence of the capacity of nanomedicine technologies to deliver TFs and reprogram cellular phenotypes into immunogenic tumor cell phenotypes. The identification of phenotype drivers or master TFs establishing core regulatory circuits and molecular memory presents potential opportunities for cell reconfiguration [32, 33]. However, TFs are usually nondruggable [1]. We overcame this therapeutic limitation by leveraging emergent RNA technologies and pBAE‐NPs, in combination with immunotherapy, to enhance LCOR expression in tumors and thus promote tumor eradication. These observations lead to a paradigm shift for driver‐cell state or driver‐phenotype therapies in cancer.

mRNA therapies emerge as versatile tools with the potential to transform medicine. Among the different types of mRNA therapy, which are focused mainly on personalized neoantigen vaccines, tumor suppressor replacement therapies of mutated p53 and PTEN have been used in preclinical studies [3, 34]. In our case, we modified LCOR expression, which is not mutated but downregulated in the most aggressive tumor cell populations, including immune‐evasive cancer stem cells [11]. In this regard, it represents an innovative strategy to fine‐tune tumor cell phenotypes based on a TF. This new approach of harnessing mRNA technologies for cancer therapy holds promise in oncology and immuno‐oncology. Moreover, a potential transversal impact on other disciplines of medicine, such as immune‐related disease and regenerative medicine, is also expected.

Polymeric NPs, specifically pBAE polyplexes, show a high transfection efficiency for the in vivo expression of diverse encapsulated nucleic acids with low toxicity [35, 36, 37, 38, 39]. In our preclinical models, these pBAE‐NPs effectively transfected a range of breast cancer cells both in vitro and in vivo, demonstrating their potential as versatile delivery vehicles. pBAE‐NPs offer advantages, including enhanced biocompatibility and biodegradability, chemical versatility, improved endosomal escape, reduced immunogenicity, effective targeting of extrahepatic tissues, suitability for local delivery and ease of manufacture with greater stability [6, 40]. We observed cell‐type dependence of NP uptake across the breast cancer lines tested, which could lead to differential efficiency in distinct types of tumors. Cell‐type‐dependent uptake was previously reported by our group and attributed to the differential endocytic pathways used by specific cell types [25, 26]. In addition, pBAE‐NPs are more prone to disassemble under physiological conditions than other NPs, that is, they efficiently release the mRNA in response to a decrease in pH within the endosomal pathway, thus facilitating endosomal escape. This avoids eventual toxicity caused by NP accumulation, a phenomenon observed in other types of nanomaterial. However, NP disassembly could also compromise the efficacy of systemic administration. Our study shows that local administration reduces systemic toxicity, and the effective dose needed, while also lowering manufacturing costs since specific targeting is not required. Thus, this delivery mode is suitable for local neoadjuvant intratumoral therapies, similar to the use of NPs in protein replacement therapies [41].

A limitation of this study is that the therapeutic strategy was evaluated using local intratumoral administration, which enabled efficient tumor transfection and proof‐of‐concept validation of LCOR transcription‐factor replacement, but does not yet address the challenges associated with systemic delivery or tumor‐specific targeting in disseminated disease. In addition, although our data demonstrate LCOR mRNA translation, APM induction, stemness suppression, immune remodeling, and therapeutic synergy with immune‐checkpoint blockade, the immune characterization was focused on selected pharmacodynamic readouts rather than an exhaustive dissection of all immune populations and mechanisms involved. Therefore, we cannot exclude additional tumor‐cell‐intrinsic and immune microenvironmental effects contributing to the observed therapeutic benefit.

Our results highlight not only the potential of LCOR nanotherapy as a standalone treatment but also when combined with different ICI therapies. Thus, our nanotherapy is designed to efficiently enhance immunogenicity as an optimal complementary therapy to ICI agents designed to restore T cells [11, 12]. In fact, in immuno‐oncology, various combination therapies, including chemotherapy, are used as induction treatments to increase immunogenicity, causing DNA damage and genomic instability [42, 43]. Here we propose Lcor mRNA therapy to specifically instigate immunogenic functions that could replace the need to use chemotherapeutic induction treatment strategies. Another breakthrough of our study is that we demonstrate, for the first time, the remarkable performance of Lcor mRNA therapy combined with anti‐CTLA4, as well as the preclinical benefit of using Lcor mRNA‐loaded RH‐pBAE‐NPs combined with anti‐PD‐L1 blockade. Of note, anti‐CTLA4‐based therapies have received less attention than anti‐PD‐L1 in breast cancer and have not been approved for clinical practice to date. New clinical trials have demonstrated the preliminary efficacy of anti‐CTLA4 blockade in early‐TNBC without the administration of chemotherapy [22]. Therefore, our results indicate the potential benefit of combining our nanotherapy with anti‐CTLA4 blockade, without the need for chemotherapy.

Conclusions

In conclusion, our study provides proof‐of‐concept that mRNA‐based replacement of the transcription factor LCOR can be used to reprogram tumor cell phenotypes therapeutically. Local delivery of RH‐pBAE‐NPs efficiently introduced Lcor mRNA and restored LCOR activity in breast cancer cells and tumors, promoting antigen presentation, suppressing stemness‐associated features, and modulating the tumor immune microenvironment. Importantly, this local Lcor mRNA nanotherapy enhanced the therapeutic response to PD‐L1 and CTLA4 immune‐checkpoint blockade in the 4T07 preclinical primary tumor model. Together, these findings support transcription factor replacement as a potential strategy to constrain aggressive tumor cell states and increase their susceptibility to immunotherapy, while further development will require evaluation of systemic or tumor‐targeted delivery approaches.
