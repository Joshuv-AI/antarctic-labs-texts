Simplified Construction of Integer Dimension Hausdorff Measures Preprint: AIP/123-QED
Luis A. Cedeño-Pérez

Abstract
A simplified construction of the integer dimension Hausdorff measures is given applying methods of geometry and generalized convergence. This construction prioritizes integration, yields the Area Formula as a byproduct of the construction and the Coarea Formula follows indirectly from the smooth case. Though this construction is not as general as the traditional one, it provides a much simpler introduction to the topic and is enough for certain applications to Geometric Analysis.



I
Introduction

I.1
A motivating problem

Let
f\colon\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
be a locally integrable function and consider a sequence of smooth curves
\{\gamma_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
such that
int\;\gamma_{n}
is precompact and
int\;\gamma_{n+1}\subset int\;\gamma_{n}
. A natural question that arises is if the limit

\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{\gamma_{n}}f

exists. Furthermore, does it follow that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{\gamma_{n}}f=\int_{\gamma}f

if
\gamma_{n}\to\gamma
in an adequate sense? Questions such as this arise frequently in Analysis and Geometry as well as many other applications. One may be tempted to employ the usual techniques of measure theory, but certain problems arise immediately. For example, it is well known that there exists measures
d\gamma_{n}
such that

\int f\;d\gamma_{n}=\int_{\gamma_{n}}f,

however, the measure is different for each curve
\gamma_{n}
. To solve this we may consider the sequence of measures
(d\gamma_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and try to establish some convergence in the sense of measures. Once again, this fails since the measures don’t even converge setwise.

We may instead regard each
\gamma_{n}
as a set and find a single measure
\mathscr{H}_{1\leq 2}
such that if
\gamma
is any smooth curve in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
then

\int_{\gamma}f\;d\mathscr{H}_{1\leq 2}=\int_{\gamma}f.

(1)

This would allow us to study the limit as the set changes in a way more familiar from measure theory.

The measure
\mathscr{H}_{1\leq 2}
does indeed exist and is known as a
Hausdorff measure
. Its construction, however, is not straight forward, since two things must be given:

1.

The definition must be given for any set in its domain.

2.

It must be shown that equation (
1
) is valid.

Neither of these is easy to do.

I.2
Hausdorff Measure

The Hausdorff measure
\mathscr{H}_{m\leq n}
, essentially, measures
m
-dimensional subsets of
n
-dimensional space. Grossly simplyfying, Hausdorff measures have 3 uses:

1.

Study irregular sets.

2.

Study non-integer dimension of sets.

3.

Study irregular functions in regular sets (manifolds or almost-manifolds).

The first two tasks can be done as soon as the Hausdorff measures are constructed, regardless of integration. The third one, however, can not be done until the strongest results of Hausdorff measures are established, namely, the Area and Coarea Formulas. This implies a long and often tortuous learning process that can be overwhelming for those who begin their study of Geometric Measure Theory and other similar subjects. Furthermore, the third task is the most related to classical problems of Geometry and Analysis, such as the motivating problem we just described.

In this paper we develop a simplified method for constructing integer dimension Hausdorff measures that, while insufficient for the first two tasks, is more than enough for the third one. The loss in generality is countered by a much simpler construction that is perfectly suited for introductions to the topic. The main ingredient is shifting our attention from the definition of the measure to the definition of the integral, thus the goal is to generate a measure that satisfies the Area and Coarea Formulas (generalizations of equation (
1
)).

To see how difficult the Hausdorff measures can be, let’s recall their construction, following
KP 08
. Let
m\in\mathbb{N}
and
\Omega_{m}
be the
m
-dimensional volume of the unit ball centered in zero, that is,

\displaystyle\Omega_{m}

\displaystyle=\lambda^{m}(B_{1}(0))

\displaystyle=\frac{2\pi^{\frac{m}{2}}}{m\Gamma(\frac{m}{2})}.

For
\delta>0
define

\mathcal{H}_{m,\delta}(A)=\inf\left\{\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\Omega_{m}2^{-m}(diam(B_{k}))^{n}\;\bigg|\;A\subset\bigcup_{k\in\mathbb{N}}B_{k}\textrm{ and }diam(B_{k})\leq\delta\right\}.

Due to Caratheodory’s construction it is easy to see that
\mathcal{H}_{m,\delta}
is an outer measure. Futhermore, one can verify that
\delta\longmapsto\mathcal{H}_{m,\delta}(A)
is decreasing, hence the limit

\mathcal{H}_{m}(A)=\lim_{\delta\to 0}\mathcal{H}_{m,\delta}(A)

exists, although it may be
\infty
. One then checks that
\mathcal{H}_{m}
is still an outer measure due to the monotonicity of
\delta\longmapsto\mathcal{H}_{m,\delta}(A)
. One can then apply the usual theory of Caratheodory to restrict
\mathcal{H}_{m}
to a measure on certain
\sigma
-algebra, although is not clear on whether the Borel
\sigma
-algebra
Bo(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is contained in it. To this end, one shows that
\mathcal{H}_{m}
is a metric outer measure, which means that if
A,B\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
are such that
d(A,B)>0
then

\mathcal{H}_{m}(A\cup B)=\mathcal{H}_{m}(A)+\mathcal{H}_{m}(B).

The next step is to show that for any metric outer measure the Borel measurable sets are Caratheodory measurable. This implies that
\mathcal{H}_{m}
is defined in a
\sigma
-algebra that contains the Borel
\sigma
-algebra of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

All the previous steps are rather technical and complicated, and all this effort is only to show that the Hausdorff measure
\mathcal{H}_{m}
is a properly defined measure in a
\sigma
-algebra that contains the Borel sets. It remains to show how the integral with respect to
\mathcal{H}_{m}
is computed, which is the content of the Area and Coarea Formulas, the proof of which is even more difficult.

The advantage of the previous construction is that it is not necessary for
m
to be a natural, but any positive real number
s
(the normalization constants
\Omega_{m}2^{-m}
are simply set to
1
). This allows one to define notions such as
Hausdorff dimension
. The relevant result is that for any measurable set
A
there exists exactly one real number
s\in[0,\infty]
such that

0<\mathcal{H}_{s}(A)<\infty,

in which case,
s
is the Hausdorff dimension of
A
. Another advantage is that the domain of
\mathcal{H}_{m}
is much larger than the Borel
\sigma
-algebra, hence, one may study sets that are not Borel measurable. Other references for the topic are
Fed 69
,
Fol 99
and
Eva 15
.

I.3
Summary of the Construction

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be an open set,
M\subset\Omega
an
m
-dimensional submanifold and
dV_{M}
its volume form. The volume form allows us to define the integral of continuous functions in
M
as the integral of the differential form
f\;dV_{M}
, that is

\int_{M}f=\int fdV_{M}.

It turns out that this defines an element of
C(M)^{\ast}
and the Riesz-Markov-Kakutani Theorem provides the existence of a Borel measure
\mu_{M}
such that integration with respect to this measure is the functional, that is,

\int f\;d\mu_{M}=\int_{M}f.

The measure
\mu_{M}
can be lifted to a measure on
\Omega
by the formula

\tilde{\mu}_{M}(A)=\mu_{M}(A\cap M),

however, it only actually measures subsets of
M
. One would be tempted to cover
\Omega
with a sequence of disjoint manifolds
(M_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
and consider something like

\mathscr{H}=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\mu_{M_{k}}.

Since the manifolds are disjoint the sequence of corresponding measures should have disjoint supports and convergence wouldn’t be a problem. This construction, however, fails for two very important reasons:

1.

\Omega
can’t, in general, be covered by a countable collection of lower dimension manifolds.

2.

The resulting measure depends on the selected cover and one would need to show independence on the cover.

Both problems arise from the countability of the cover, but the cover would need to be countable in order to make sense of the series. Thus, we need a more general notion of convergence for objects that are much larger than sequences. This is known as
generalized convergence
, which we will employ in the form of nets. Nets are a generalization of sequences that, essentially, are a sequence with a domain possibly larger than
\mathbb{N}
. We will require very basic notions of generalized convergence to make the previous idea work. This notion of convergence will allow us to study the process of pasting measures together in a consistent way, known as
inductive systems of measures
.

Once this is done, we will recall the ideas of Differential Geometry regarding volume measurement and combine them with the notions of generalized limits to construct the integer dimension Hausdorff measures. The Area Formula will follow immediately from definition. We will also prove the Coarea Formula, altough the proof won’t be as immediate. However, our proof of the Coarea Formula will still be shorter than the usual one.

II
Generalized Convergence

Recall that a sequence
(s_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
in
X
is actually a function
s\colon\mathbb{N}\to X
. A net consists on letting the domain be a more general set that has an order structure appropiate for taking limits. A preorder in a set
I
is a relation
\preceq
such that

1.

i\preceq i
for any
i\in I
.

2.

If
i\preceq j
and
j\preceq k
then
i\preceq k
.

It is not necessary that any two elements in
I
can be compared. For example, if
X
is any set and
I=\mathcal{P}(X)
then the relation
A\preceq B
if
A\subset B
is a preorder in
I
known as
direct inclusion
. Furthermore, given
A,B\in I
it may happen that neither
A\subset B
nor
B\subset A
. Another common preorder is given by
A\preceq B
if
A\supset B
, known as
inverse inclusion
.

The domain of a net needs a stronger order structure than a preorder.

Definition
.

A preordered set
(I,\preceq)
is a
directed set
if for any
i,j\in I
there exists
k\in I
such that
i\preceq k
and
j\preceq k
.

In words, given any two elements in
I
there exists a third element that can be compared to both of them and, furthermore, succeeds them. In a very intuitive way, a preorder guarantees that there is an ”infinity” while the directed set condition guarantees that there is exactly one ”infinity.” There are a couple of very familiar examples of directed sets.

1.

If
I=\mathcal{P}(X)
then
I
is a directed set with either direct or inverse inclusion.

2.

Both
\mathbb{N}
and
\mathbb{R}
are directed sets with their usual orders.

As an example of a preorder that is not a directed set consider
I=\{0,1\}\times\mathbb{R}
and declare that
(a,x)\preceq(b,y)
if
a=b
and
x\leq y
. It follows that
I
is a preordered set but not a directed set, since for any
x\in\mathbb{R}
the elements
(0,x)
and
(1,x)
can’t be dominated simultaneously. This is an example of a preordered set that fails to be a directed set since it has two ”infinities.” We can now define a net.

Definition
.

Given a set
X
, a net is a function
s\colon I\to X
such that
I
is a directed set. The net is denoted as
(s_{i})_{i\in I}
and each of its elements is denoted as
s_{i}
as a parallel to sequences.

Nets were designed to capture the properties of topological spaces in a similar way that sequences do in metric spaces. Most importantly, any topological space defines a criterion for convergence of nets. For simplicity, we start with the definition for nets of real numbers.

Definition
.

Let
(x_{i})_{i\in I}
be a net of real numbers. We say that
(x_{i})_{i\in I}
converges to
x\in\mathbb{R}
, denoted as
x_{i}\to x
, if for each
\epsilon>0
there exists
i_{\epsilon}\in I
such that if
i\succeq i_{\epsilon}
then
|x-x_{i}|<\epsilon
.

It is clear that every sequence is a net and that the convergence of sequences is a particular case of the convergence of nets. There is another example of convergence of nets that is familiar to every mathematician, although they may not know it. Let
[a,b]
be a bounded interval and
f\colon[a,b]\to\mathbb{R}
a bounded function. A tagged partition
(P,T)
of
[a,b]
is a partition
P=\{x_{i}\}_{i=0}^{n}
and a selection of points
T=\{t_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
such that
t_{i}\in[x_{i-1},x_{i}]
. Denote the set of tagged partitions of
[a,b]
by
I
and order it declaring
(P,T)\preceq(Q,S)
if
P\subset Q
, that is, if
Q
refines
P
. It is easy to verify that
I
is a directed set. Given any tagged partition
(P,T)
we can define the Riemann sum

S_{(P,T)}(f)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}f(t_{i})(x_{i}-x_{i-1}).

Hence, we have a net of Riemman sums
(S_{(P,T)})_{(P,T)\in I}
. It is easy to verify that
(S_{(P,T)})_{(P,T)\in I}
converges if and only if
f
is Riemann integrable and

S_{(P,T)}(f)\to\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\;dx.

This formalices the informal expression usually given to the Riemann integral as

\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\;dx=\lim_{|P|\to 0}\sum_{i=1}^{n}f(t_{i})(x_{i}-x_{i-1}).

As previously said, we won’t require much of the theory of Generalized Convergence. The first result we require is related to monotone nets, for which we say that a net of real numbers
(x_{i})_{i\in I}
is
increasing
if
x_{i}\leq x_{j}
whenever
i\preceq j
.

Lemma II.0.1
.

Let
(x_{i})_{i\in I}
be an increasing net of real numbers. If
(x_{i})_{i\in I}
is upper bounded then

x_{i}\xrightarrow{\mathbb{R}}\sup_{i\in I}x_{i}.

Proof.

Given
\epsilon>0
, by definition of supremum, we can find
i_{0}\in I
such that

\sup_{i\in I}x_{i}-\epsilon<x_{i_{0}}.

Since the net is increasing, if
i\succeq i_{0}
then

\sup_{i\in I}x_{i}-\epsilon<x_{i_{0}}<x_{i},

that is,

0<\sup_{i\in I}x_{i}-x_{i}<\epsilon.

It follows that
x_{i}\xrightarrow{\mathbb{R}}\sup\limits_{i\in I}x_{i}
.
∎

Since we will work with measures we must also study the case in which the net is not bounded, for which we will work in the extended real numbers, which we will denote as
[-\infty,\infty]
. Since this is a topological space, we need the general definition for convergence of nets.

Definition
.

Let
(X,\tau)
be a topological space. We say that a net
(x_{i})_{i\in I}
in
X
converges to
x\in X
if for every
U
neighbourhood of
x
there exists
i_{U}\in I
such that
x_{i}\in U
whenever
i\succeq i_{U}
.

We now study unbounded increasing nets.

Lemma II.0.2
.

Let
(x_{i})_{i\in I}
be an increasing net of real numbers. If
(x_{i})_{i\in I}
is unbounded then
x_{i}\to\infty
in
[-\infty,\infty]
.

Proof.

Since
(x_{i})_{i\in I}
is unbounded, for every
M\geq 0
there exists
i_{M}\in I
such that
x_{i_{M}}>M
. Since
(x_{i})_{i\in I}
is increasing we obtain that
x_{i}\geq x_{i_{M}}
whenever
i\succeq i_{M}
, hence
x_{i}>M
whenever
i\succeq i_{M}
. In other words,
x_{i}\in(M,\infty]
whenever
i\succeq i_{M}
. Since basic neighbourhoods of
\infty
in the topology of
[-\infty,\infty]
are of the form
(M,\infty]
we conclude that
x_{i}\to x
in the usual topology of
[-\infty,\infty]
.
∎

Combining the two previous results we obtain the following.

Proposition II.0.1
.

Every
(x_{i})_{i\in I}
increasing net in
[-\infty,\infty]
is convergent in the usual topology of
[-\infty,\infty]
.

The last generalized convergence result we require is a simple property of the continuity of addition. Note that none of the previous results actually need the property of a directed set, but only those of a preordered set. The next result shows that the index set must actually be directed in order for familiar properties of sequences to remain valid.

Proposition II.0.2
.

Let
(x_{i})_{i\in I}
and
(y_{i})_{i\in I}
be two nets of real numbers indexed by the same directed set
I
. If
x_{i}\to x
and
y_{i}\to y
the
x_{i}+y_{i}\to x+y
.

Proof.

Let
\epsilon>0
. By definition, there exist
i_{x},i_{y}\in I
such that
|x-x_{i}|<\frac{\epsilon}{2}
if
i\succeq i_{x}
and
|y-y_{i}|<\frac{\epsilon}{2}
if
i\succeq i_{y}
. Since
I
is directed there exists
i_{0}\in I
such that
i_{x}\preceq i_{0}
and
i_{y}\preceq i_{0}
. Hence, if
i\succeq i_{0}
both of the previous inequalities are valid, thus

\displaystyle|(x+y)-(x_{i}+y_{i})|

\displaystyle\leq|x-x_{i}|+|x-y_{i}|

\displaystyle<\frac{\epsilon}{2}+\frac{\epsilon}{2}

\displaystyle=\epsilon,

whenever
i\succeq i_{0}
.
∎

Note that two nets need not have the same domain, that is, they may be indexed by different sets. This is because we only require their domain to be a directed set, but not any specefic one. This is a difficulty not present in the case of sequences. Because of this, one in general can not sum two nets of real numbers, at least not in an obvious way.

Generalized Convergence is commonly employed in Topology and Functional Analysis. Further references for the topic are
Meg 98
and
Dug 66
.

III
Generalized Limits of Measures

We now apply the previous results of nets of real numbers to the context of measure theory. Specifically, we will study nets of measures and their setwise convergence. We will use the last two propositions of the previous section implicitly.

Theorem III.1
.

Let
(\Omega,\Sigma)
be a measurable space and
(\mu_{i})_{i\in I}
a net of measures such that for each
E\in\Sigma
the net
(\mu_{i}(E))_{i\in I}
is incresing. If
\nu\colon\Sigma\to[0,\infty]
is the function given by

\nu(E)=\lim_{i}\mu_{i}(E)

then
\nu
is a measure.

Proof.

Note that the hypothesis and lemma
II.0.1
imply

\displaystyle\nu(E)

\displaystyle=\lim_{i}\mu_{i}(E)

\displaystyle=\sup_{i\in I}\mu_{i}(E).

It is clear that
\nu
is non-negative and that
\nu(\emptyset)=0
. If
\{A_{n}\}_{n=1}^{m}
is a finite collection of disjoint sets in
\Sigma
then

\displaystyle\nu\left(\bigcup_{n=1}^{m}A_{n}\right)

\displaystyle=\lim_{i}\mu_{i}\left(\bigcup_{n=1}^{m}A_{n}\right)

\displaystyle=\lim_{i}\sum_{n=1}^{m}\mu_{i}(A_{n})

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{m}\lim_{i}\mu_{i}(A_{n})

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{m}\nu(A_{n}).

It follows that
\nu
is finitely additive, hence it suffices to show that it is lower continuous. Let
(A_{n})_{m\in\mathbb{N}}
be an increasing sequence in
\Sigma
. It follows that

\displaystyle\lim_{m\to\infty}\nu(A_{n})

\displaystyle=\lim_{m\to\infty}\lim_{i}\mu_{i}(A_{n})

\displaystyle=\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\sup_{i\in I}\mu_{i}(A_{n})

\displaystyle=\sup_{i\in I}\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\mu_{i}(A_{n})

\displaystyle=\lim_{i}\lim_{m\to\infty}\mu_{i}(A_{n})

\displaystyle=\lim_{i}\mu_{i}\left(\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}A_{n}\right)

\displaystyle=\nu\left(\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}A_{n}\right).

This shows that
\nu
is continuous and thus a measure.
∎

In the preceding situation we say that
(\mu_{i})_{i\in I}
is an increasing net of measures. The function
\nu
is known as the
generalized limit
of the net
(\mu_{i})_{i\in I}
and is denoted by

\nu=\lim_{i}\mu_{i}.

Theorem III.2
.

Let
(\Omega,\Sigma)
be a measurable space,
(\mu_{i})_{i\in I}
an increasing net of measures and
\nu=\lim_{i}\mu_{i}
. If
f\in M^{+}(\Omega,\Sigma)
then

\int f\;d\nu=\lim_{i}\int f\;d\mu_{i}.

Furthermore,
f\in M(\Omega,\Sigma)
is integrable if and only if
f^{+}
are
f^{-}
are integrable and the previous formula is valid in this case.

Proof.

If
A\in\Sigma
then

\displaystyle\int\chi_{A}\;d\nu

\displaystyle=\nu(A)

\displaystyle=\lim_{i}\mu_{i}(A)

\displaystyle=\lim_{i}\int\chi_{A}\;d\mu_{i}.

By linearity, this is also satisfied by simple functions. If
f\in M^{+}(\Omega,\Sigma)
we consider
(s_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
a sequence in
S^{+}(\Omega,\Sigma)
such that
s_{n}\nearrow f
, then

\displaystyle\int f\;d\nu

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\to\infty}\int s_{n}\;d\nu

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\to\infty}\lim_{i}\int s_{n}\;d\mu_{i}

\displaystyle=\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\sup_{i\in I}\int s_{n}\;d\mu_{i}

\displaystyle=\sup_{i\in I}\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\int s_{n}\;d\mu_{i}

\displaystyle=\lim_{i}\lim_{n\to\infty}\int s_{n}\;d\mu_{i}

\displaystyle=\lim_{i}\int f\;d\mu_{i}.

The rest of the result follows from linearity.
∎

Finally, we strengthen our previous results to a case where the net of measures is such that the measure
\mu_{i}
restricts to
\mu_{j}
whenever
j\preceq i
.

Definition
.

Let
(\Omega,\Sigma)
be a measurable space and
(\Omega_{i})_{i\in I}
an increasing net in
\Sigma
such that
\Omega_{i}\nearrow\Omega
. An
inductive system of measures
is an increasing net of measures in
(\mu_{i})_{i\in I}
in
\Sigma
such that if
i\succeq j
then for each
A\in\Sigma
the equation

\mu_{i}(A\cap\Omega_{j})=\mu_{j}(A\cap\Omega_{j})

is verified. The previous condition is known as the
compatibility condition
.

Intuitively, an inductive system of measures is such that as
i
increases the value of the measures is fixed in
\Omega_{i}
as
\mu_{i}
. Thus, the inductive system extends the previous measures as the index increases.

Define
\Sigma_{i}=\Sigma\restriction_{\Omega_{i}}
. Note that it suffices that each
\mu_{i}
is defined in
\Sigma_{i}
, since we can replace it by

\tilde{\mu}_{i}(A)=\mu_{i}(A\cap\Omega_{i}).

In this case the measures
\tilde{\mu_{i}}
are defined in
\Sigma
and as such can replace the original net.

Definition
.

If
(\mu_{i})_{i\in I}
is an inductive system of measures we define its
inductive limit

\mu
as the measure

\mu=\lim_{i}\mu_{i}.

It follows from the previous section that if
f\in M^{+}(\Omega,\Sigma)
then

\int f\;d\mu=\lim_{i}\int f\;d\mu_{i}.

The additional property obtained from the compatibility condition is the following.

Theorem III.3
.

Let
(\mu_{i})_{i\in I}
be an inductive system of measures and
\mu
its inductive limit. If
f\in M^{+}(\Omega,\Sigma)
vanishes outside of
\Omega_{i_{0}}
then

\int f\;d\mu=\int f\;d\mu_{i_{0}}.

Proof.

Since

\int f\;d\mu=\lim_{i}\int f\;d\mu_{i},

its is enough to show that for
j\succeq i_{0}
the equation

\int f\;d\mu_{j}=\int f\;d\mu_{i_{0}}

is valid. If
A\in\Sigma
satisfies
A\subset\Omega_{i_{0}}
then

\displaystyle\int\chi_{A}\;d\mu_{j}

\displaystyle=\int\chi_{A\cap\Omega_{i_{0}}}\;d\mu_{j}

\displaystyle=\mu_{j}(A\cap\Omega_{i_{0}})

\displaystyle=\mu_{i_{0}}(A\cap\Omega_{i_{0}})

\displaystyle=\int\chi_{A\cap\Omega_{i_{0}}}\;d\mu_{i_{0}}

\displaystyle=\int\chi_{A}\;d\mu_{i_{0}}.

By linearity the same is true for simple functions with sets contained in
\Omega_{i_{0}}
. If we define
\Sigma_{i_{0}}=\Sigma\restriction_{\Omega_{i_{0}}}
then
f\restriction_{\Omega_{i_{0}}}
is measurable in the space
(\Omega_{i_{0}},\Sigma_{i_{0}})
, hence the Approximation by Simple Functions Lemma implies that there exists a sequence
(s_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of simple functions measureble in this space such that
s_{n}\nearrow f\restriction_{\Omega_{i_{0}}}
. If we extend each
s_{n}
trivially to all of
\Omega
and do the same with
f\restriction_{\Omega_{i_{0}}}
then our hypothesis implies that this extension is actually
f
, while it is also true that
s_{n}\nearrow f
and these functions are measurable in
(\Omega,\Sigma)
. The Monotone Convergence Theorem then implies that

\displaystyle\int f\;d\mu_{j}

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\to\infty}\int s_{n}\;d\mu_{j}

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\to\infty}\int s_{n}\;d\mu_{i_{0}}

\displaystyle=\int f\;d\mu_{i_{0}}.

The result follows from this.
∎

More on inductive systems of measures can be found in
Mac 95
.

IV
Geometry of Volumes

The following can be found in standard Differential Topology and Geoemetry textbooks, such as
Lee 13
and
Tu 10
.

Before applying the theory of inductive systems of measures we just developed we need to recall the specifics of volume measurement developed in Differential Geometry. Let’s begin with the integration of differential forms. Given a continuous
n
-form on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
it must be of the form

\omega=f\;dx^{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge dx^{n},

hence we define

\int f\;dx^{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge dx^{n}=\int f\;dx^{1}\cdots dx^{n}.

If
M
is an
n
-dimensional manifold and
\omega
is a continuous
n
-form with support covered by a coordinate patch
\varphi\colon U\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}\to M
that preserves orientation then
\varphi^{\ast}\omega
is a continuous
n
-form in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, hence we define

\int_{M}\omega=\int_{U}\varphi^{\ast}\omega.

Finally, if
\omega
is a continuous
n
-form and
(U_{i})_{i\in I}
is a cover of its support by coordinate patches then consider a partition of unity
(\varphi_{i})_{i\in I}
subordinated to the cover, then
\varphi_{i}\omega
is continuous
n
-form with support in a coordinate patch, hence can be integrated as we previously discussed. We define

\int_{M}\omega=\sum_{i}\int_{M}\varphi_{i}\omega.

This does not depend on the cover or the partition of unity used. We now relate this to measure theory through the Riesz-Markov-Kakutani Theorem.

Theorem IV.1
.

Let
\omega
be an
n
-form in an orientable manifold
M
of dimension
n
. There is a unique measure
\mu_{\omega}
in
Bo(\tau_{M})
such that

\int f\;d\mu_{\omega}=\int_{M}f\omega

for any
f\in C_{c}(M)
.

Proof.

Integration of forms defines an element of
C_{c}(M)^{\ast}
, which means that if
(f_{m})_{m\in\mathbb{N}}
is a sequence of continuous functions with common compact support
K
such that
f_{m}\xrightarrow{unif}f
then

\int_{M}f_{m}\omega\to\int_{M}f\omega.

Thus the Riesz-Markov-Kakutani Theorem yields a Borel measure
\mu_{\omega}
such that

\int f\;d\mu_{\omega}=\int_{M}f\omega.

∎

The integral of an arbitrary differential form is not necessarily related to volumes, mainly because the integral may not be a positive functional, that is, it may not happen that

\int_{M}f\omega\geq 0

whenever
f\geq 0
. Hence, the measure
\mu_{M}
is in general a signed measure. When the manofld is riemannian, however, there exists a distinguished
n
-form such that integration is a positive functional.

Definition
.

Let
(M,g)
be an orientable riemmanian manifold. The unique
n
-form
V_{g}
such that for any orthonormal frame
\{E_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
(orthonormal with respect to
g_{p}
)

V_{g}(E_{1},\ldots,E_{n})=1

is known as the
volume form
of
(M,g)
.

The volume form exists and is unique whenever
(M,g)
is orientable. If coordinates are given and the metric tensor has coordinate components
g_{ij}
then

V_{g}=\sqrt{det\;g_{ij}}\;dx^{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge dx^{n}.

Since the volume form in a riemannian manifold is a distinguished form we can define the integral of continuous functions using this form. Precisely, if
f\in C_{c}(M)
we define

\int_{M}f=\int fV_{g}.

This functional turns out to be positive in the sense that if
f\geq 0
then

\int_{M}f\geq 0.

Thus, the corresponding measure is a positive measure by the positive version of the Riesz-Markov-Kakutani Theorem.

Corollary IV.1.1
.

Let
M
be an orientable riemannian manifold of dimension
n
. There is a unique positive measure
\mu_{M}
in
Bo(\tau_{M})
such that

\int f\;d\mu_{M}=\int_{M}f

for any
f\in C(M)
.

This measure is precisely what we will need for our purposes.

Definition
.

Let
M
be an orientable riemannian manifold of dimension
n
. The measure
\mu_{M}
is known as the
volume measure
of
M
.

Since we will work with submanifolds of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
we can always assume that the volume form exists and is the pullback of the volume form of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
under inclusion. Furthermore, assume that we have a disjoint collection of submanifolds
(M_{k})_{k=1}^{m}
. Their union may not be a submanifold, but it is a manifold regardless. Furthermore, each
M_{k}
has a volume measure
\mu_{k}
concentrated in
M_{k}
. Since the union is disjoint, we may define

\sum_{k=1}^{m}\mu_{M_{k}}.

This measure is properly defined and is positive. If we denote the disjoint union of
(M_{k})_{k=1}^{m}
as
M
, we will refer to the previous sum as the
volume measure of the disjoint union
, that is,

\mu_{M}=\sum_{k=1}^{m}\mu_{M_{k}}.

This also lets us deal with non-orientable submanifolds, since we can decompose the submanifold as a disjoint union of orientable ones and apply the previous construction. This shows that volume measures ignore orientations, unlike differential forms.

V
Integer Dimension Hausdorff Measures

Fix an open set
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
m\in\mathbb{N}
such that
m\leq n
. To each
m
-dimensional submanifold
M
of
\Omega
we can assign its volume measure
\mu_{M}
, which suggests how to define a net of measures. However, the collection of
m
-dimensional submanifolds does not form a directed set under inclusion, because the union of manifolds is not in general a manifold. To solve this problem consider two
m
-dimensional submanifolds
M
and
N
. We claim that while
M\cup N
is not necessarily a manifold, it is the disjoint union of manifolds. If
m=n
then both manifolds are open, hence its union is open and is again a submanifold of
\Omega
. If
m<n
then both
M
and
N
are closed in
\Omega
(it suffices to use slice charts to see that their complement is open) hence
M\cap N
is again closed. Note that

M\cup N=M\uplus(N\setminus(M\cap N)).

Since
M
is naturally a submanifold it suffices to show that so is
N\setminus(M\cap N)
. This is easy, since
M\cap N
is closed in
\Omega
and
N
is a submanifold then
M\cap N
is closed in
N
, hence
N\setminus(M\cap N)
is open in
N
and thus a submanifold of
N
. From this, we conclude that
N\setminus(M\cap N)
is actually a submanifold of
\Omega
. The previous argument can be extended to any finite number of submanifolds. Hence, the finite union of manifolds can be written as a finite disjoint union of manifolds and given the structure of a manifold, albeit, not a nice one. However, this is enough for our measure-theoretic purposes, since given a finite disjoint union of manifolds
M
we can associate to it its volume measure
\mu_{M}
, as in the previous section.

Define
M_{m\leq n}(\Omega)
as the collection of finite disjoint unions of
m
-dimensional submanifolds of
\Omega
. Our previous considerations imply that
M_{m\leq n}(\Omega)
ordered by direct inclusion is a directed set. Given
M\in M_{m\leq n}(\Omega)
consider its volume measure
\mu_{M}
. Through the formula

\tilde{\mu}_{M}(A)=\mu_{M}(A\cap M)

we may assume that each
\mu_{M}
is a measure on the Borel
\sigma
-algebra of
\Omega
. It follows that

(\mu_{M})_{M\in M_{m\leq n}(\Omega)}

is an incresing net of measures in
Bo(\Omega)
. This net is also an inductive system of measures since if
M\subset N
and
\iota_{M\subset N}
is the inclusion of
M
in
N
and
\omega_{M}
and
\omega_{N}
are their volume forms then

\omega_{M}=\iota_{M\subset N}^{\ast}\omega_{N},

which translates into the compatibility condition when passing to volume measures.

Definition
.

We define the
Hausdorff measure
of
local dimension

m
and
ambient dimension

n
as the measure
\mathcal{H}_{m\leq n}\colon Bo(\Omega)\to[0,\infty]
given by

\mathcal{H}_{m\leq n}=\lim_{M}\mu_{M}.

Note that our construction of the Hausdorff measure requires the local dimension to be an integer, as the tools of Differential Geometry wouldn’t be available otherwise. Applying the results of the inductive limit of measures we obtain the following.

Theorem V.1

(Area Formula. Particular Case.)
.

If
A\in Bo(\Omega)
is covered by a sequence
(M_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
of
m
-dimensional submanifolds then

\mathcal{H}_{m\leq n}(A)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\mu_{M_{k}}(A\cap M_{k}).

If
f
is an integrable function and
\{M_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is a disjoint sequence of submanifolds then

\int_{\bigcup\limits_{k\in\mathbb{N}}M_{k}}f\;d\mathcal{H}_{m\leq n}=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\int f\;d\mu_{M_{k}}.

Our definition of the Hausdorff measure is much more restricted than the usual one since we only defined it on
Borel subsets
, while the usual Hausdorff measure is defined on a much larger
\sigma
-algebra. This is the reason for our construction to be insufficient to study irregular sets. In
Fed 69
many other measures are constructed that satisfy the Area Formula, all of them defined in much larger
\sigma
-algebras than the Borel
\sigma
-algebra. Another disadvantage is that we lack an explicit formula for the Hasudorff measure as we constructed it, since we gave priority to knowing how to compute its integrals. We insist that the advantage of our method is that it is much simpler than the usual one and immediately yields the Area Formula.

The second fundamental result about the Hausdorff measure that we will prove is the Coarea Formula. Since our version of the Hausdorff measure comes from Differential Geometry, we first establish the smooth version of this result. We follow the proof of
Cha 84
.

Theorem V.2

(Smooth Coarea Formula)
.

Let
H\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
be a smooth function and for every regular value
t
let
dV_{t}
be the volume form of
H^{-1}(\{t\})
. If
dV
is the volume form of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
then

dV=\frac{1}{|\nabla H|}dt\wedge dV_{t}

for every regular value
t
. In consequence, for any continuous function
f
we have that

\int_{H^{-1}([a,b])}f\;dV=\int_{a}^{b}\int_{H^{-1}(\{t\})}\frac{f}{|\nabla H|}\;dV_{t}\;dt.

(2)

Proof.

Let
\Phi_{t}
be the flow of
\nabla H
in
H^{-1}(\{t\})
. For sufficiently small
t
and
x\in H^{-1}(\{t\})
the flow
\Phi_{t}(x)
defines smooth coordinates for
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. The one form
dt
is defined as the unique one form such that

dt(\nabla H)=|\nabla H|^{2}

and

dt(v)=0

if
v\in T_{x}H^{-1}(\{t\})
. If
\{v_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n-1}
is an orthonormal frame for
T_{x}H^{-1}(\{t\})
then
\left\{\frac{\nabla H}{|\nabla H|}\right\}\cup\{v_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n-1}
is an orthonormal frame for
T_{x}\mathbb{R}^{n}
. It follows that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|\nabla H|}dt\wedge dV_{t}\left(\frac{\nabla H}{|\nabla H|},v_{1},\ldots,v_{n-1}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|\nabla H|}dt\left(\frac{\nabla H}{|\nabla H|}\right)dV_{t}(v_{1},\ldots,v_{n-1})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|\nabla H|^{2}}dt(\nabla H)

\displaystyle=1,

hence

dV=\frac{1}{|\nabla H|}dt\wedge dV_{t},

(3)

as stated. For the equality of integrals, note that by Sard’s Theorem the set of critical values has measure zero, hence the function

t\longmapsto\int\frac{f}{|\nabla H|}\;dV_{t}

is defined except for a measure zero set, hence it can be integrated and equation (
3
) yields (
2
).
∎

By the Area Formula, the last conclusion of the theorem can be rewritten as

\int_{H^{-1}([a,b])}f\;d\lambda^{n}=\int_{a}^{b}\int_{H^{-1}(\{t\})}\frac{f}{|\nabla H|}\;d\mathscr{H}_{n-1\leq n}\;dt,

(4)

for any continuous function
f
. We wish to strengthen the result to non-continuous functions, which is what is known as the general Coarea Formula. The proof of this fact is not as clean as our proof of the Area Formula, but is still easier than the usual proof. We begin with a very dumb trick. If we choose
f=1
in (
4
) we find that

\lambda^{n}(H^{-1}([a,b]))=\int_{a}^{b}\int_{H^{-1}(\{t\})}\frac{1}{|\nabla H|}\;d\mathscr{H}_{n-1\leq n}\;dt.

Since
H
is any smooth function on a manifold we can consider an open set
A
and the restriction
H\restriction_{A}\colon A\to\mathbb{R}
and the previous equation should remain valid. Since
H\restriction_{A}^{-1}([a,b])=H^{-1}([a,b])\cap A
and
H\restriction_{A}^{-1}(\{t\})=H^{-1}(\{t\})\cap A
we find that

\lambda^{n}(H^{-1}([a,b])\cap A)=\int_{a}^{b}\int_{H^{-1}(\{t\})\cap A}\frac{1}{|\nabla H|}\;d\mathscr{H}_{n-1\leq n}\;dt

for any open set
A
. From now on we consider the total space to be
H^{-1}([a,b])
to clean notation a bit. From this very dumb trick we find the following

Lemma V.2.1
.

The equation

\lambda^{n}(A)=\int_{a}^{b}\int_{H^{-1}(\{t\})\cap A}\frac{1}{|\nabla H|}\;d\mathscr{H}_{n-1\leq n}\;dt

(5)

is valid for any open set
A
. In consequence, the function

t\longmapsto\int_{H^{-1}(\{t\})\cap A}\frac{1}{|\nabla H|}\;d\mathscr{H}_{n-1\leq n}

is measurable whenever
A
is open.

Using the usual approximation techniques by simple functions and the Monotone Convergence Theorem, the Coarea Formula will be proven if we can show that equation (
5
) is valid for all Borel sets. To this end, we will show that the right-hand side of (
5
) actually defines a Borel measure. That is, if we define

\mu(E)=\int_{a}^{b}\int_{H^{-1}(\{t\})\cap E}\frac{1}{|\nabla H|}\;d\mathscr{H}_{n-1\leq n}\;dt,

we will show that
\mu
is a Borel measure. The previous lemma then implies that the open sets are in the domain of
\mu
and that
\lambda^{n}(A)=\mu(A)
for open sets (note that both measures are
\sigma
-finite since they coincide in open sets). Since two Borel measures that coincide on open sets are actually the same measure, it will follow that
\lambda^{n}=\mu
and the Coarea Formula will follow.

The previous proofs will be carried out by the usual Dynkin System abstract nonsense, so we recall the pertinent concepts and results. We say that a family of sets
\Sigma
is a
Dynkin system
if
\Omega\in\Sigma
,
\Sigma
is closed under incresing countable unions and if
A,B\in\Sigma
and
A\subset B
then
B\setminus A\in\Sigma
. The intersection of any number of Dynkin systems is again a Dynkin system, hence given any family of subsets
\mathcal{A}
there exists a minimal Dynkin system containing
\mathcal{A}
denoted by
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A})
. The
Dynkin System Theorem
guarantees that if
\mathcal{A}
is closed under finite intersections then
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A})=\sigma(\mathcal{A})
. We now state the Coarea Formula and prove it.

Theorem V.3

(Coarea Formula. Particular Case.)
.

Let
H\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
be a smooth function. For any integrable function
f
the equation

\int_{H^{-1}([a,b])}f\;d\lambda^{n}=\int_{a}^{b}\int_{H^{-1}(\{t\})}\frac{f}{|\nabla H|}\;d\mathscr{H}_{n-1\leq n}\;dt

is valid.

Proof.

By the previous discussion, it is enough to show that
\mu
is a Borel measure. First, for any set
E
denote by
F_{E}(t)
the function

t\longmapsto\int_{H^{-1}(\{t\})\cap E}\frac{1}{|\nabla H|}\;d\mathscr{H}_{n-1\leq n}.

By definition of
\mu
, we have that
\mu(E)
is defined whenever
F_{E}
is measurable. We define

\Sigma=\{E\subset\Omega\;|\;F_{E}\text{ is measurable}\}.

By the previous lemma we have that
\tau\subset\Sigma
, where
\tau
is the topology of our space. We claim that
\Sigma
is a Dynkin system, which, if true, would imply that
\mathcal{D}(\tau)\subset\Sigma
. Since topologies are closed under finite intersections, we actually have that
Bo(\tau)=\mathcal{D}(\tau)
by the Dynkin System Theorem, hence
Bo(\tau)\subset\Sigma
and
\mu
would be defined on all Borel sets.

It is clear that
\Omega\in\Sigma
. Let
(A_{m})_{m\in\mathbb{N}}
be an incresing sequence in
\Sigma
, then
F_{A_{m}}
is measurable for any
m\in\mathbb{N}
. By the Monotone Convergence Theorem we have that

F_{\bigcup\limits_{m\in\mathbb{N}}A_{m}}=\lim_{m\to\infty}F_{A_{m}},

where the limit is pointwise. This implies that
F_{\bigcup\limits_{m\in\mathbb{N}}A_{m}}
is measurable and
\bigcup\limits_{m\in\mathbb{N}}A_{m}\in\Sigma
. Consider
A,B\in\Sigma
such that
A\subset B
. We have that

F_{B\setminus A}=F_{B}-F_{A}

and is thus measurable. This implies that
B\setminus A\in\Sigma
and shows that
\Sigma
is a Dynkin system. The previous considerations shows that
\mu
is defined on
Bo(\tau)
.

The proof that
\mu
is a measure is actually rather easy, as the only non-trivial part is showing
\sigma
-additivity. This is done by using the Monotone Convergence Theorem twice, once for each integral, the first time on increasing sets and the second one on a series of non-negative functions. It follows that
\mu
is Borel measure that coincides with
\lambda^{n}
in open sets and hence
\lambda^{n}=\mu
. Finally, one approximates any integrable function with an increasing sequence of simple functions and uses the Monotone Convergence Theorem to conclude.
∎

This last theorem can be strengthened even more using the Radamacher Theorem to replace smoothness on
H
by Lipschitz continuity, but we will not do so.

VI
Concluding Remarks

The construction of the Hausdorff measure presented in this paper is insufficient for studying irregular sets and Hausdorff dimension, but has many advantages other than its simplicity when compared to the usual one. Note that the results of the last section made no use of any special property of euclidean spaces. Thus, the open set
\Omega
can be replaced with a riemannianan manifold. This provides an alternative construction for Hausdorff measures in manifolds, which is much more difficult than that for euclidean spaces. Although we only proved the Area and Coarea Formulas, due to their complexity, many other results form Geometric Measure Theory can be proved much more easily, such as the Poincaré and Sobolev inequalities, the Gauss-Green Theorem, and Isoperimetric Inequalities. Other results related to Sobolev spaces such as the existence of trace and extension operators can be proved as well. In any case, the techniques of this paper provide a solid framework to develop further results related to geometric integration and serve as a solid introduction to these kinds of topics.
