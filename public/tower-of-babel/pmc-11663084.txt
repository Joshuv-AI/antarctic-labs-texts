Nurses' Attitudes Toward Family Importance in Nursing Care and Its Influencing Factors: A Cross‐Sectional Study
Authors: Cristina Alfaro‐Diaz, Ana Canga‐Armayor, Navidad Canga‐Armayor, María Pueyo‐Garrigues, Nuria Esandi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimTo know the nurses' attitudes toward family involvement in nursing care and factors that can influence it.DesignA cross‐sectional design study was carried out on 253 clinical nurses.MethodsData was collected from 253 clinical nurses using the Families' Importance in Nursing Care–Nurses' Attitudes Scale, the Demand‐Control‐Support Questionnaire (DCSQ) and the Iceland Health Care Practitioner Illness Beliefs Questionnaire. Data were analysed using descriptive statistics, t‐tests, analysis of variance, Pearson's and Spearman's correlations, and multiple linear regression.ResultsNurses generally had a positive attitude toward the importance of involving families in nursing care (M = 106.0; SD 11.9). Nurses who work in outpatient consultation, held a master's degree, and have a family‐oriented approach in their work unit showed more supportive attitudes. Multiple linear regression analyses showed that personal factors, including nurses' beliefs, and work‐related factors, including control over work, and job satisfaction, were the more influencing factors on nurses' attitudes toward family.ConclusionThis study offers valuable knowledge for understanding the attitudes of nurses toward family involvement in nursing care. Nurses have a positive attitude toward families. However, nurses' beliefs about family and illness, control over work, and perceived job satisfaction are key factors that influence these attitudes.Implications for the Patient CareUnderstanding the factors linked to nurses' favourable attitudes toward families can facilitate the adoption of a family‐centered approach in healthcare settings, ultimately enhancing the quality of care delivered to both the patient and their family.ImpactThe results may inform the development and implementation of strategies that facilitate working with a more family‐focused approach in daily nursing practice.Reporting MethodThis paper is reported according to the STROBE Statement.Patient or Public ContributionNo patient or public contribution.

Introduction

Starting from the moment a diagnosis is confirmed, family members actively endeavour to meet the challenges posed by the illness and take on new caregiving responsibilities. However, they usually navigate the journey of caring without the necessary knowledge and skills, and enough time to assume and adapt to the situation. As a consequence, they often report being unsure about the type of care they should provide or the time they should seek professional assistance. This, in turn, leads them to experience increased feelings of anxiety and insecurity (Rahmani et al. 2022). Moreover, the stress of feeling continuously “on duty” to provide physical, social, and emotional support to their loved one and to deal with their own fears may alter the dynamics of the family (Huang, Smith, and Wong 2019; Kehoe et al. 2019).

Within this context, evidence suggests that a family systems approach, which is based on collaborative, participatory, relational engagement, and support of families as both an interactive system and recipients of care, improves the health outcomes of patients and their family members (Bell 2013; Wright & Leahey, 2021). Family System Nursing adopts a systemic and holistic family‐oriented approach where the family is viewed as a unit of care. This perspective extends beyond studying family members individually and emphasises the interactions and reciprocity between individuals and the family unit (Bell 2013; Wright and Bell, 2021).

Nurses have a unique position to assess family needs and offer family interventions so that families may find a balance between the challenges posed by the condition and their family vital project (Årestedt et al. 2018; Whitehead et al. 2018). Recent research has indicated that maintaining a substantial and high‐quality relationship with healthcare professionals can potentially enhance the health status and self‐esteem of family members who are living with an illness (Alfaro‐Díaz et al. 2022; Hsiao et al. 2022). This favours the functioning and well‐being of the whole family and, therefore, the management and evolution of the illness situation (Svavarsdottir and Gisladottir 2019; Wright and Bell 2021).

Despite the well‐documented benefits of implementing family nursing interventions, these have not yet been implemented in practice in a systematic and professional way (Årestedt et al. 2018; Gilliss, Pan, and Davis 2019). In this sense, existing research indicates that nurses' attitudes toward families can either support or hinder the implementation of this practice (Landry, René, and Demontigny 2023; Trulsson et al. 2023). Fostering positive attitudes toward involving the family in nursing care is a fundamental requirement for establishing a therapeutic nurse‐family relationship, which can ultimately contribute to alleviating and easing the family's distress resulting from the experience of illness (Østergaard et al. 2020; Wright and Bell 2021). However, the evidence suggests that there are different aspects at the personal, educational, and work levels that influence nurses' attitudes toward including the family in care (Shamali et al. 2023).

Over the past few years, numerous studies have been undertaken to evaluate nurses' attitudes toward families, as well as influencing factors (Østergaard et al. 2020; Shamali et al. 2023). However, most of these studies have focused solely on sociodemographic factors such as gender, age, or working experience and have not included validated instruments that allow a reliable measurement of the constructs of interest. Thus, although there have been some initial observations regarding the factors that affect family engagement, additional research is imperative to acquire a more profound comprehension of how these factors impact the implementation of family engagement practices within healthcare environments.

Knowledge about factors that contribute to nurses' attitudes toward the importance of involving families in nursing care is essential to implement effective family nursing practices in healthcare contexts and improving the quality of care provided to the patient and their family. For that reason, the objective was to know nurses' attitudes regarding the significance of families in nursing care and to explore how personal, educational, and work‐related factors affect these attitudes using validated instruments. The present study can contribute new knowledge to improve family involvement in nursing care.

Methods

Study Design and Participants

A cross‐sectional descriptive study was carried out with a convenience sample comprising registered nurses (RNs) from different clinical divisions, such as general hospital wards, outpatient services, and other units (i.e., intensive care units and haemodialysis units) of a hospital located in Spain. The eligibility criteria for potential participants were: (a) actively employed at the commencement of the study, (b) proficient in the Spanish language, and (c) regularly interacting with families in their daily clinical duties.

The number of required samples was calculated using a 95% confidence level, and an assumed 50% distribution of results (Price et al. 2005). The STROBE Statement guidelines were followed (Appendix S1).

Measures

An ad‐hoc questionnaire including sociodemographic variables was used. Furthermore, participants were given the Spanish versions of the following self‐reported validated assessment tools.

The FINC‐NA

The 26‐item Families' Importance in Nursing Care–Nurses' Attitudes (FINC‐NA) was used to assess the attitudes of RNs concerning the importance of enabling family members to engage in the care of their ill family members (Alfaro‐Díaz et al. 2023). The scale consists of four subscales: (a) ‘Family as a resource in nursing care (Fam‐RNC)’; (b) ‘Family as a burden (Fam‐B)’; (c) ‘Family as a conversation partner (Fam‐CP)’, and (d) ‘Family as its own resource (Fam‐OR)’ utilising a 5‐point Likert scale (ranging from 1 = strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree). Higher scores represent more positive attitudes. Cronbach's alpha values for the Spanish version of the total scale is 0.90, and between 0.64 and 0.84 for the subscales.

The ICE‐HCP‐IBQ

The 7‐item Iceland Health Care Practitioner Illness Beliefs Questionnaire (ICE‐HCP‐IBQ) to evaluate a healthcare provider's beliefs regarding their comprehension of the significance of the illness experience for families. (Alfaro‐Díaz et al. 2020). This questionnaire is a unidimensional tool that employ a 5‐point Likert‐type scale (ranging from 1 = never to 5 = always). The possible score range spans from 7 to 35 points, where a higher score indicates a stronger level of confidence among healthcare professionals in illness beliefs. The Spanish version has demonstrated robust psychometric properties, reporting a Cronbach's alpha coefficient of 0.83 for the total questionnaire.

The DCSQ

The 17‐item Demand‐Control‐Support Questionnaire (DCSQ) includes four subscales for assessing (a) ‘Psychological demands,’ (b) ‘Control,’ (c) ‘Social support,’ and (d) ‘Job satisfaction’ (Alfaro‐Díaz et al. 2021). Respondents provide their answers using a 4‐point Likert‐type scale (ranging from 1 = strongly disagree to 4 = strongly agree). Higher scores within each subscale indicate increased levels of psychological demands (ranging from 5 to 20), greater decision‐making autonomy (ranging from 6 to 24), enhanced workplace social support (ranging from 6 to 24), and heightened job satisfaction (ranging from 5 to 20). The Spanish version has demonstrated satisfactory internal consistency on its distinct subscales: ‘Psychological demands’ (with a Cronbach's alpha of 0.76), ‘Control’ (with a Cronbach's alpha of 0.62), ‘Social support’ (with a Cronbach's alpha of 0.87) and ‘Job satisfaction’ (with a Cronbach's alpha of 0.76).

Data Collection

Data gathering occurred from March to May 2019. Initially, meetings were convened with the supervisors of each hospital unit, during which cooperation was sought in identifying potential participants. Subsequently, informational sessions were arranged across all hospital units, extending invitations to prospective participants. These sessions elucidated the study's objectives and design. Nurses who expressed willingness to partake in the research endorsed informed consent forms and individually completed hard‐copy questionnaires.

Ethical Approval

The study obtained ethical approval from the Ethical Committee of the University of Navarra (Reference identifier: 2018.086). The study participants were recruited on a voluntary basis. The data dissociation method was numerical coding, and no personal data were disclosed, except for demographic data. No ethical conflicts arose.

Statistical Analyses

A descriptive examination of all variables was conducted, involving the computation of frequency and percentage metrics for categorical variables, as well as measures of variability and central tendency for continuous variables. Given that the questionnaires' subscales comprise varying item counts, the subscale scores were initially converted to a 0–100 scale using the subsequent formula: [(Raw scale score − Minimum possible score)/Range of possible scores] × 100 (Andersson et al. 2022).

We utilised the independent two‐sample t‐test and either one‐way analysis of variance or the Mann–Whitney U test to examine variances in nurses' attitudes regarding the importance of families in nursing care, as assessed by the FINC‐NA, based on their individual, educational, and professional attributes. In this analysis, age was segmented into tertiles. Furthermore, we conducted a bivariate analysis, calculating Pearson and Spearman's correlation coefficients. Lastly, we conducted multiple linear regression analyses to explore how personal, educational, and work‐related characteristics functioned as predictors. In the personal variables category, we included age, gender, work experience, illness beliefs (measured via ICE‐HCP‐IBQ), and experience with a severe illness within one's own family. For the educational variables, we included the level of nursing education, job training, and training in family nursing. And for work‐related variables, we included primary working area, employment status, family‐centred approach at the workplace, as well as psychological demands, control, social support, and job satisfaction (measured by DCSQ).

In this research, participants with fewer than five unanswered items on any of the questionnaires (which amounted to 10% of the total items) were included in the analysis. Missing data were addressed through mean imputation. Significance was determined using a p‐value threshold of 0.05 or lower. All statistical analyses were performed using STATA Version 12.0, developed by StataCorp in College Station, TX, USA.

Results

Characteristics of Participants

Out of the 293 nurses who received invitations to participate in the study, a total of 253 were included. All of the participants were female, with an average age of 41.37 years (standard deviation = 10.8), and they had an average tenure of 12.93 years (standard deviation = 10.7) in their current department. Among the respondents, 94 (37.2%) were employed in hospital wards, and the majority of the participants (80.0%) held permanent employment contracts. Notably, 143 nurses (57.2%) reported that they incorporated a family‐oriented approach into their clinical practice, although only 44 of them (17.5%) mentioned receiving formal education in family nursing (see Table 1).

Scores of the Study Variables

The overall nurses' attitude concerning family involvement (FINC‐NA) mean score was 106.0 (SD 11.9; theoretical range [TR] 26–130). The highest mean score was found for the Fam‐RNC subscale (M = 42.0; SD 5.2; TR 10–50), and the lowest for Fam‐B subscale (M = 15.0; SD 2.9; TR 4–20).

The overall beliefs of health professionals about their understanding of the meaning of the illness to the family (ICE‐HCP‐IBQ) mean score was 24.7 (SD 3.6; TR 7–35). Moreover, based on the theoretical score range for the DCSQ subscales, the highest mean scores were found for the ‘Social Support at work’ subscale (M = 19.6; SD 2.8; TR 6–24), followed by the “Job Satisfaction” subscale (M = 16.3; SD 2.2; TR 5–20), ‘Control over work’ (M = 18.7; SD 2.1; TR 6–24) and finally, ‘Physiological demands’ (M = 13.8; SD 2.5; TR 5–20) (see Table 2).

Comparison of Personal, Educational, and Work‐Related Characteristics by Nurses' Attitudes

Regarding personal and educational variables, significant statistical differences were found for the level of nursing education and working experience. Specifically, nurses who held master's degrees and worked in a workplace with a family nursing approach, had more positive attitudes scores on the overall FINC‐NA scale, and most of the subscales. The results also showed that nurses with long‐term employment as nurses (> 2 years) scored significantly higher on the Fam‐B and Fam‐OR subscales than those with short‐term employment (< 2 years). Additionally, when comparing age terciles, the results revealed significant differences in the Fam‐OR subscale scores. Nurses aged 50 and older scored significantly higher on the Fam‐OR subscale compared to those aged 36–49 and those 35 and younger.

Regarding work‐related variables, significant statistical differences were found for the primary working area and family‐centred approach at the workplace. The results also showed that nurses who felt that their workplace had a general approach to the care of families scored significantly higher on the FINC‐NA scale and all subscales except Fam‐B than those without this perception. Furthermore, nurses who worked in outpatient consultation had higher scores on the FINC‐NA scale as well as on the Fam‐CP and Fam‐OR subscales than those who worked in inpatient services and other services (see Table 3).

Correlations Among the Study Variables

Regarding continuous variables, nurses' beliefs regarding their comprehension of the significance of illness for the family (evaluated with ICE‐HCP‐IBQ) were significantly positively correlated with the total FINC‐NA score (r = 0.426, p < 0.001), and all their subscales' scores (r = 0.288 ~ 0. 443, p < 0.001). ‘Control over work’, ‘Social support at work’ and ‘Job satisfaction’ were significantly positively correlated with the overall FINC‐NA score (r = 0.191, p < 0.05 ~ 0. 266, p < 0.001) and most of its subscales (r = 0.139, p < 0.05 ~ 0. 254, p < 0.001).

Among the categorical variables, the family approach at the workplace was positively correlated with the total score of the FINC‐NA scale (ρ = 0.213, p < 0.001), as well as with most of the subscales. The work department was significantly negatively correlated with the total FINC‐NA score (ρ = −0.150, p < 0.05). Furthermore, the education level was positively correlated with the Fam‐RNC subscale (ρ = 0.147, p < 0.05) (see Table 4).

The regression models for FINC‐NA subscales showed that R 2 ranged from 19.9% to 29.7%. For the Fam‐RNC subscale, nurses' beliefs (ICE‐HCP‐IBQ) (β = 1.222; p < 0.01), having a bachelor's degree (β = 1.577; p < 0.01) and ‘Control over work’ (β = 0.434; p < 0.01) showed a significant positive association while working in other department showed a significant negative association with the subscale (β = −1.577; p < 0.01). For the Fam‐B subscale, only two variables showed a significant association, nurses' beliefs (ICE‐HCP‐IBQ) (β = 1.222; p < 0.01) and having a postgraduate degree (β = 1.222; p < 0.01). For the Fam‐CP subscale, nurses' beliefs (ICE‐HCP‐IBQ) (β = 1.222; p < 0.01), ‘Control over work’ (β = 0.262; p < 0.01) and working in an outpatient consultation (β = 0.262; p < 0.05) presented a significant effect. Finally, for the Fam‐OR subscale, nurses' beliefs (ICE‐HCP‐IBQ) (β = 0.281; p < 0.001) and having a family‐oriented approach to care (β = 0.658; p < 0.05) showed a significant association. When the score for adopting a family‐centered approach to care increases by one point, this particular subscale demonstrates a corresponding increase of 0.658 points. This suggests that working in an environment with an overarching emphasis on family care is linked to a more receptive attitude toward utilising the family as a valuable resource in nursing care.

Discussion

The results of this study provide new knowledge about the personal, educational, and work‐related factors that influence nurses' attitudes toward the inclusion of the family in nursing care and therefore, facilitate the identification of areas for improvement to promote a family‐centred care in health care settings.

The research findings indicate that, on the whole, nurses possess a favourable attitude regarding the significance of including families in the care process. This suggests that these healthcare professionals recognise and value the role of families in their nursing care practices. This observation aligns with prior studies conducted in nursing care settings, as noted in the work of Østergaard et al. (2020) and Shamali et al. (2023). More specifically, in this study, nurses reported the highest scores for the Fam‐RNC subscale, indicating a positive stance toward families and an appreciation for their involvement in nursing care. This is a positive result since family members want to be a resource for the care of their relatives and actively participate in the care process (Andersson et al. 2022). However, it is essential that professionals are aware of the reasons why they involve the family. This means, including family in nursing care is important for the patient and family or this represents a resource for the professionals and alleviates their workload and care responsibilities. Since in this second case, the foundation of involving the family in the care process can be distorted and may even overwhelm family members with the caregiving responsibilities (Gilliss, Pan, and Davis 2019).

However, it is essential that professionals understand the reasons behind involving the family. Including the family in nursing care should primarily benefit the patient and the family rather than serving as a means to alleviate the professionals' workload and care responsibilities. The latter approach can undermine the foundational importance of family involvement in healthcare and may even overwhelm family members with the care situation (Gilliss, Pan, and Davis 2019).

Furthermore, the nurses involved in this study showed a less favourable disposition toward the Fam‐B and Fam‐OR subscales. This outcome suggests that nurses may be less inclined to assert that they have the time to provide care for the family, as well as to encourage the family to utilise their own resources and cultivate a trusting nurse‐family relationship. These findings are consistent with other research indicating that while nurses acknowledge the significance of family presence and its role in the patient's recovery, they may not consistently view the family as the central focus of care (Luttik et al. 2017). That is, the real needs, expectations, and wishes of the family are not considered, thus neglecting the support and care they deserve. In this sense, nursing educators and managers need to know the attitudes of nurses toward families and be aware of how personal, educational, and work‐related factors influence nurses' attitudes, to develop strategies that promote positive attitudes.

The study findings also indicated a distinct and positive association between nurses' beliefs regarding their comprehension of the significance of the illness situation for families and their attitudes toward recognising the importance of families. Furthermore, the multiple linear regression analysis confirmed the influence of nurses' beliefs on the total scale of FINC‐NA and on all subscales. This finding supports the results of a prior investigation conducted within an oncology unit (Alfaro‐Díaz et al. 2022 ). That study identified a connection between nurses' strong convictions about comprehending the significance of the illness situation for families and their more favourable inclinations toward including families in clinical practice. Consequently, there may be value in developing educational interventions in family nursing that concentrate on reinforcing facilitating beliefs and cultivating positive attitudes, aiming to establish a trusting rapport with the family.

In terms of educational factors linked to nurses' attitudes, those with a postgraduate degree exhibited more favourable attitudes toward the importance of family involvement in care. This observation aligns with prior research (Svavarsdottir et al. 2018), which demonstrated that nurses who have completed postgraduate education tend to acquire a skill set that enhances their sense of control and decision‐making capacity in clinical practice. Additionally, they possess a greater depth of knowledge in their field. Consequently, nurses with higher levels of education tend to have stronger competencies and are better equipped to work with families. This is consistent with our findings, where higher education was associated with a more positive view of families, specifically regarding them as less burdensome. These results underscore the importance of enhancing family nursing education in undergraduate curricula. Moreover, they highlight the necessity of ongoing, certified professional training throughout a nursing career to ensure nurses maintain proficiency in delivering family nursing practice.

This is consistent with our findings, where higher education was associated with a more positive view of families, specifically regarding them as less burdensome. These findings suggest that more focus should be given to family nursing in the undergraduate curriculum. Given these results, it is valuable to provide certified, ongoing professional training throughout a nursing career to ensure that nurses remain proficient in delivering family‐centered care.

Likewise, the evidence indicates that education in family nursing models is a key factor in promoting supportive nurses' attitudes toward the family. However, unlike other studies (Barreto et al. 2022), in this study, no significant associations were found between having received training in family nursing and having more positive attitudes toward the family. This may be explained by two factors: on the one hand, the small number of nurses who had received such training (n = 44, 17.5%), and on the other hand, the type of training received, without coaching or live/videotape supervision as teaching‐learning methods, since this type of methods had proven to be the most effective for acquiring the competency of family‐centred care (Gutiérrez‐Alemán et al. 2021).

The findings relating to work‐related variables provide novel information. Our findings have pinpointed the work unit as a potential determinant of nurses' positive attitudes. In particular, nurses assigned to consultation roles displayed more favourable attitudes toward the significance of involving families in care compared to their counterparts in hospitalisation wards or other departments. This difference may stem from the fact that nurses with allocated consultation time for patients have more conducive circumstances for implementing a family‐centered care approach, as has been suggested in previous research (Østergaard et al. 2020).

Additionally, this study highlights that nurses employed in units with a family‐oriented care approach expressed more favourable attitudes toward family involvement. This observation is consistent with earlier research (Årestedt et al. 2018; Alfaro‐Díaz et al. 2020), which established a connection between positive attitudes toward family engagement and the practical integration of knowledge and skills in family nursing. This association is attributed to the fact that nurses operating in environments that prioritise the family's role in nursing care are better positioned to implement a more supportive approach to family care, thereby fostering more positive attitudes (Voltelen, Konradsen, and Østergaard 2016). Furthermore, being part of a unit with a family‐oriented approach emerged as a significant predictor of the Fam‐OR subscale. Consequently, nurses and healthcare organisations should jointly take responsibility for creating work environments that promote active family involvement, facilitating the adoption of a more family nursing approach in healthcare, as emphasised by Barreto et al. (2022).

Finally, control over work and job satisfaction were identified as important influencing factors in nurses' attitudes. To the extent that a professional has decision‐making autonomy over his or her work and can decide what to do and how to do it, attitudes toward working with the family will be more positive. This is because, if the professional has sufficient control over his own work and considers family care as an intrinsic component of nursing care, the professional is more likely to initiate effective interactions with the family and involve the family in care (Hoplock et al. 2019). Likewise, as the satisfaction of professionals at work increases, attitudes will be more positive. This could be because healthy work environments where nurses feel fulfilled and supported, with meaningful recognition, true collaboration, and appropriate staffing are crucial to help retain nurses in the profession and produce quality outcomes for staff nurses and families (Trulsson et al. 2023). Therefore, due to the benefits, more interventions aimed at strengthening the control and satisfaction of professionals in their work are needed.

Limitations

The sampling method used in this study wasn't random, potentially introducing selection bias. Those who willingly participated in the survey might have had more experience in incorporating families into nursing care compared to non‐respondents. Nevertheless, the data collected closely resemble real‐world scenarios. It exhibits a well‐distributed range of responses, involves a substantial number of participants from various units, and produces results that align with previous studies. These factors enhance the credibility of the findings and lend greater stability to the results. It's important to note, however, that the sample exclusively comprised female nurses, which limits the ability to draw conclusions across gender‐diverse groups.

Conclusion

This study offers valuable perspectives on nurses' perspectives regarding the significance of family involvement in nursing care. The findings reveal that nurses generally hold a positive attitude toward the inclusion of families in patient care. Nevertheless, it's essential to recognise that several influential factors impact these attitudes, including nurses' personal beliefs, their level of control over their work, and their overall job satisfaction.

Due to the important role that the attitudes of nurses play in improving collaboration and relationships with patients' families, nurse managers should pay attention to the factors influencing these attitudes and, as a result, develop strategies that facilitate the implementation of a more family‐centred care approach in health care contexts.

Nurse managers should facilitate opportunities for nurses to participate in specialised educational programs aimed at enhancing their ability to involve families effectively in nursing care. These programs should also promote a more comprehensive understanding of the patient's and the family's health and illness experiences.

Conversely, it is imperative for administrators and healthcare policymakers to formulate strategies aimed at enhancing social support within the workplace and elevating job satisfaction. These strategies are essential for cultivating more favourable work environments that prioritise the well‐being of professionals while concurrently providing robust backing for the integration of family‐centered care.
