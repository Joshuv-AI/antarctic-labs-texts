Recovering Small Communities in the Planted Partition Model
Martijn Gösgens, Maximilien Dreveton

Abstract
We study community recovery in the planted partition model in regimes where the number and sizes of communities may vary arbitrarily with the number of vertices. In such highly unbalanced settings, standard accuracy or overlap-based metrics become inadequate for assessing recovery performance. Instead, we propose the correlation coefficient between partitions as a recovery metric, which remains meaningful even when the number or sizes of communities differ substantially. We then analyze a simple common-neighbor–based clustering rule which groups two adjacent vertices if they share more than one common neighbor. We establish explicit recovery conditions under sparse inter-community connectivity, without requiring prior knowledge of the model parameters. In particular, in graphs of size n n , this algorithm achieves exact recovery for communities with sizes Ω ⁡ ( log ⁡ n ) \Omega(\log n) , almost exact recovery for sizes ω ⁡ ( 1 ) \omega(1) and weak recovery for sizes Ω ⁡ ( 1 ) \Omega(1) . In contrast to most existing results, which assume (nearly) balanced communities, our method successfully recovers small and heterogeneously-sized communities, and improves existing guarantees even in some balanced settings. Finally, our results apply to community sizes that follow a power-law distribution, a characteristic frequently found in real-world networks.

Introduction

Community detection in random graphs is a central topic in random graph theory, with applications ranging from social and biological networks to information and communication systems. The planted partition model (PPM) provides a canonical framework for studying this problem. In its simplest form, the vertex set
[n]
is divided into latent communities, and edges between vertex pairs appear independently with probability
p_{n}
if both vertices belong to the same community and with probability
q_{n}
otherwise. The statistical and computational thresholds for recovering the planted partition are now well understood when the number of communities is finite or grows slowly, and when communities are approximately balanced in size (see
[
1
]
for a survey).

Despite the large literature on community recovery in the PPM, nearly all existing works make at least one of the following two assumptions: (i) the number of communities is finite or grows slowly with the number of vertices, and/or (ii) the community sizes are asymptotically of the same order. However, these assumptions are often unrealistic in practice and restrictive from a probabilistic viewpoint. For instance, if the partition of the vertex set is chosen uniformly at random from the set of all partitions of
[n]
, the number of communities grows as
n/\log n

[
25
,
29
]
. Moreover, empirical studies show that real-world networks contain many small groups and a few large ones, with community sizes following heavy-tailed, often power-law, distributions
[
18
,
32
]
. From a modeling perspective, it is therefore natural to consider the PPM in regimes where the number of communities can grow arbitrarily with
n
, and where community sizes may differ by orders of magnitude. Such regimes challenge both the usual probabilistic tools and the classical metrics for assessing recovery, since standard accuracy or overlap measures depend implicitly on the number and relative sizes of communities.

This work studies recovery in the planted partition model under minimal structural assumptions on the latent partition. We allow the number and sizes of communities to be arbitrary, including highly unbalanced cases where small and large groups coexist. To quantify recovery, we employ the correlation coefficient between partitions, a symmetric similarity index with a well-defined random baseline that is studied in
[
15
]
. In contrast to accuracy or overlap-based measures, the correlation remains interpretable even when the number of communities in the estimated partition differs from that in the ground truth, and it admits a clean probabilistic formulation amenable to theoretic analysis.

We then analyze a particularly simple community detection procedure. Given a graph
G
, the algorithm constructs a filtered graph
G^{*}
that retains only the edges that participate in at least two triangles, and outputs the connected components of
G^{*}
as the estimated communities. This rule can be viewed as a local common-neighbor thresholding scheme that does not require knowledge of the model parameters
p_{n}
or
q_{n}
nor the number of communities.

Our main results establish conditions under which the algorithm achieves exact, almost exact, and weak recovery in the PPM. Exact recovery means that the algorithm perfectly infers the planted partition, grouping all vertices correctly with high probability. Almost exact recovery allows for a vanishingly small error. Weak recovery ensures that the inferred partition is still meaningfully correlated with the true communities, performing strictly better than a random guess.

We focus primarily on establishing conditions under which Diamond Percolation succeeds at community recovery while imposing minimal structural assumptions on both the number and the sizes of communities. This objective contrasts with a complementary line of work that seeks to characterize information-theoretic recovery thresholds,
i.e.,
regimes in which no algorithm can succeed, but typically under stronger assumptions on the community structure.

In particular, while the simplicity of the algorithm allows us to obtain recovery guarantees with minimal assumptions on the partition, it nonetheless requires some conditions on the sparsity parameters. In particular, we cannot expect to recover the communities in standard regimes, where
p_{n}
and
q_{n}
scale as
\Theta(1/n)
or as
\Theta(\log n/n)
, but the number of communities is constant. Because these regimes have been extensively studied, they are not the focus on our work. Moreover, when there is an unbounded number of communities of same-size, exact recovery is impossible when
p_{n}\asymp q_{n}=\Theta(\log n/n)

[
8
, Corollary 3]
. Likewise, if the communities have constant sizes, scaling
p_{n}=o(1)
implies that almost all communities have zero inter-community edges. This motivates moving away from the standard setting by scaling
p_{n}
and
q_{n}
so that communities are sufficiently dense to allow recovery. Finally, we also note that for several inference problems where the number of communities is unbounded, triangle-counting algorithms have been shown to be near-optimal (see
[
8
,
28
]
and Section
7
for further discussion).

A key technical ingredient is a probabilistic refinement lemma showing that, under suitable sparsity conditions on
p_{n}
and
q_{n}
, the partition produced by the algorithm is with high probability a refinement of the true partition. We further prove that, whenever one partition refines another, the correlation coefficient between them concentrates around the square root of the ratio of their intra-community pair counts. This connection enables the correlation metric to be analyzed through simple moment conditions on community sizes and may be of independent interest for future research.

As a key application, we specialize our recovery results to power-law community size distributions, a setting that has not received any theoretical attention despite its empirical relevance. We prove that under mild growth conditions on the number of communities and appropriate scaling of
p_{n}
, the algorithm achieves exact, almost exact, or weak recovery depending on the sparsity regime. These are the first rigorous recovery guarantees for the PPM with power-law community sizes.

Problem Setting

In this section, we formalize the planted partition model and introduce the recovery criteria used throughout the paper. Our goal is to understand how accurately one can recover a latent community structure when the number and sizes of communities may vary arbitrarily with the total number of vertices.

Let
G_{n}=([n],E_{n})
be an undirected random graph generated according to a planted partition model (PPM). The vertex set
[n]
is partitioned into communities described by a random partition
T_{n}
. Conditioned on
T_{n}
, edges between vertex pairs
(i,j)
are drawn independently:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(i\stackrel{{\scriptstyle G_{n}}}{{\sim}}j\ \middle|\ T_{n}\right)\ =\ \begin{cases}p_{n}&\text{ if }i\stackrel{{\scriptstyle T_{n}}}{{\sim}}j,\\
q_{n}&\text{ otherwise,}\end{cases}

where
i\stackrel{{\scriptstyle G_{n}}}{{\sim}}j
indicate that an edge is present between
i
and
j
, and
i\stackrel{{\scriptstyle T_{n}}}{{\sim}}j
indicate that
i
and
j
belong to the same community. The goal is to infer the latent partition
T_{n}
from the observed graph.
In
Section

2.1
, we discuss the recovery criteria that we consider in this work. In
Section

2.2
, we motivate our choice for the correlation coefficient as performance measure. We discuss random partitions in
Section

2.3
. Finally, we discuss relevant PPM literature in
Section

2.4
.

2.1
Recovery Criteria

When the number or sizes of communities are heterogeneous, standard metrics such as agreement or normalized overlap become difficult to interpret, as they depend on the labeling and the number of communities. To overcome this limitation, we measure recovery quality using the correlation coefficient between partitions. This metric has been studied in
[
15
]
, where it was shown that it has several desirable properties that other metrics do not have. One of these desirable properties is that it has a
constant baseline
: if a partition
C_{n}
is drawn from a distribution that is symmetric w.r.t. vertex permutations, then the expected similarity between
C_{n}
and
T_{n}
is
0
.
Another reason for using this correlation coefficient is its analytical tractability under unbalanced partitions (see also
Section

2.2
).

Let
N={n\choose 2}
denote the number of vertex pairs. Given a partition
C
of
[n]
, we let

m_{C}\ =\ \#\left\{ij\ :\ i\stackrel{{\scriptstyle C}}{{\sim}}j\right\}

be the number of intra-community pairs of
C
. The quantity
m_{C}
is nonnegative and upper-bounded by
N
, where
m_{C}=0
corresponds to
n
communities of size
1
, and
m_{C}=N
corresponds to a single community of size
n
. Finally, for two partitions
C
and
T
, we define

m_{CT}\ =\ \#\left\{ij\ :\ i\stackrel{{\scriptstyle C}}{{\sim}}j\text{ and }i\stackrel{{\scriptstyle T}}{{\sim}}j\right\}.

Note that
m_{CT}
is upper-bounded by the minimum of
m_{C}
and
m_{T}
, where
m_{CT}=m_{C}=m_{T}
occurs if and only if
C=T
.

The
correlation

\rho(C,T)
between the two partitions
C
and
T
is defined as the Pearson correlation between the indicators
\mathbbm{1}\{i\stackrel{{\scriptstyle C}}{{\sim}}j\}
and
\mathbbm{1}\{i\stackrel{{\scriptstyle T}}{{\sim}}j\}
for a vertex-pair
ij
chosen uniformly at random
[
15
]
. It is given by

\rho(C,T)\ =\ \frac{m_{CT}N-m_{C}m_{T}}{\sqrt{m_{C}\cdot(N-m_{C})\cdot m_{T}\cdot(N-m_{T})}}.

(2.1)

This correlation lies in the interval
[-1,1]
. The case
\rho(C,T)=1
occurs iff
C=T
. Conversely,
\rho(C,T)=-1
implies that
C
and
T
are maximally dissimilar,
i.e.
,
i\stackrel{{\scriptstyle C}}{{\sim}}j\Leftrightarrow i\stackrel{{\scriptstyle T}}{{\not\sim}}j
for all
i,j\in[n]
. This can only occur when one of the two partitions corresponds to a single community of size
n
, while the other corresponds to
n
singleton communities. The correlation coefficient has the convenient property that if
C
is
uncorrelated
to
T
, then
\rho(C,T)\approx 0
. More precisely, if
T
is fixed with
0<m_{T}<N
and
C
is sampled from a distribution that is symmetric w.r.t. vertex permutations, then
\mathbb{E}[\rho(C,T)]=0
.
In addition, if
T_{n}
is a sequence of non-trivial partitions
1
1

1

By non-trivial partition, we exclude two border cases: the partition composed only of singletons and the partition with a single community. This ensures that
0<m_{T_{n}}<{n\choose 2}
.
and
C_{n}
is a sequence of random partitions, each sampled from a vertex-symmetric distribution, then
\rho(C_{n},T_{n})\ \xrightarrow{\mathbb{P}}\ 0
. This is known as the
constant baseline property

[
15
]
.

Denote the true partition by
T_{n}
, with which
G_{n}
is sampled, and denote the estimated partition by
C_{n}=\mathcal{D}(G_{n})
. We say that

achieves:

•

\mathbb{P}_{G_{n},T_{n}}(\rho(C_{n},T_{n})=1)\rightarrow 1
;

•

\rho(C_{n},T_{n})\ \xrightarrow{\mathbb{P}}\ 1
;

•

weak recovery if
\rho(C_{n},T_{n})\geq\rho_{0}+o{P}(1)
for some

. That is, for every
\varepsilon>0
,

\mathbb{P}(\rho(C_{n},T_{n})<\rho_{0}-\varepsilon)\to 0.

Our recovery criteria differ slightly from the definitions commonly used in the literature, as we use the correlation coefficient instead of the agreement (also known as accuracy). We discuss this choice in Section
2.2

2.2
Agreement versus Correlation

In this section, we motivate the recovery criteria based on the correlation coefficient and explain why they are more suitable in our setting than other criteria used in the literature. The recovery conditions are commonly defined using agreement rather than correlation (see
[
1
, Section 2.3]
for example).
Consider two partitions
T
and
C
, each with the
same
number
k
of communities. Two vectors
z,z^{\prime}\in[k]^{n}
can represent these partitions. We then define the agreement (also called accuracy) and the normalized agreement (also called overlap) between
C
and
T
as follows:

\displaystyle\mathrm{A}(C,T)

\displaystyle\ =\ \max_{\pi\in\operatorname{Sym}(k)}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{1}(z_{i}=\pi(z^{\prime}_{i})),

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathrm{A}}(C,T)

\displaystyle\ =\ \max_{\pi\in\operatorname{Sym}(k)}\frac{1}{k}\sum_{a=1}^{k}\frac{\sum_{i\in[n]}\mathbb{1}(z_{i}=a,\pi(z^{\prime}_{i})=a)}{\sum_{i\in[n]}\mathbb{1}(z_{i}=a)},

where
\operatorname{Sym}(k)
is the set of permutations of
[k]
.
A major disadvantage of (normalized) agreement is that it is only defined when
C
and
T
consist of the same number of communities. In practice, one typically does not know the exact number of communities, so that one cannot guarantee
C
to have the same number of communities as
T
.
The correlation coefficient does not suffer from this defect because it is based on the representation of
T
as a binary relation, instead of the labeling-based representation that agreement is based on.
This allows us to meaningfully measure the similarity between
C
and
T
even when their number of communities differ significantly.

Additionally, the correlation coefficient is one of the most effective metrics for comparing partitions. In particular, the correlation coefficient has the
constant baseline
property
[
15
]
, which ensures that
\mathbb{E}[\rho(C,T)]=0
whenever
C
is uncorrelated to
T
. In contrast, if
C
and
T
are uncorrelated and each have
k
communities, then
\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{A}(C,T)]\geq\tfrac{1}{k}
, with the exact value of the expectation depending on the sizes of the communities.
The definition of weak recovery is linked to the idea of outperforming random guessing. Therefore, to see if an agreement value is better than a random guess, we should compare it to this size-dependent expected value. In contrast, for the correlation measure, we only need to check if it is strictly positive.

Moreover, unlike agreement-based metrics, the correlation coefficient avoids the need to minimize over permutations of the community labels, which makes some arguments in the proofs tedious. Finally, the correlation coefficient is simple enough to facilitate rigorous theoretical analysis, making it well-suited for both practical and theoretical studies.

The Adjusted Mutual Information (AMI) is another widely used metric for comparing partitions that has many desirable properties
[
15
,
33
]
. However, theoretically analyzing the AMI of a partition produced by an algorithm relative to the true partition is highly challenging. Indeed, the AMI is an ‘adjusted-for-chance’ metric, and this adjustment introduces a term that complicates the theoretic analysis
[
33
]
. Additionally, computing the AMI has a time complexity of
\mathcal{O}(n\cdot k)
, where
k
is the number of communities
[
26
]
. In cases where
k=\mathcal{O}(n)
—such as those considered in
Theorem

20
—the time complexity becomes
\mathcal{O}(n^{2})
, which may even be higher than the complexity of Algorithm
1
. In contrast, the correlation coefficient has time complexity
\mathcal{O}(n)
.
For these reasons, we formulate our recovery criteria in terms of the correlation coefficient rather than AMI.

2.3
Random Partitions

The main contribution of this work is to establish exact, almost exact, and weak recovery conditions in the planted partition model where the latent partition has an arbitrary number of communities with arbitrary sizes. In this section, we highlight some examples of random partitions.

A
single community partition
consists of a single community of size
s_{n}
, formed by selecting
s_{n}
vertices uniformly at random, while each of the remaining
n-s_{n}
vertices form singleton communities.

In a
balanced partition
, the vertices are divided into
k
communities of equal size
s
, for
k\cdot s\leq n
.
We place the remaining
n-k\cdot s
vertices into singleton communities, so that the partition consists of
n-k(s-1)
communities.
These partitions are denoted by
T_{n}\sim\mathrm{Balanced}(n,k,s)
.
For
k=1
, this corresponds to a single community partition.

In the
uniform partition
,
T_{n}
is chosen uniformly from all partitions of
[n]
. This distribution has been extensively studied, and many of its asymptotic properties are known
[
17
,
25
]
. For example, it is known that the number of communities grows as
n/\log n
. We denote this distribution by
T_{n}\sim\text{Uniform}(n)
.

A
multinomial partition
is constructed by assigning each vertex
i\in[n]
independently to a community
a\in[k_{n}]
with probability
\pi_{a}
, where
(\pi_{a})_{a\in[k_{n}]}
is a given probability sequence.
By specifying different probability sequences, this allows one to construct a broad range of partition distributions.

As mentioned in the introduction, community sizes typically follow a power-law distribution. Such partitions can be sampled from a multinomial partition as follows. Let
\tau>2
and consider a sequence of i.i.d.
\text{Exp}(1)
random variables
(X_{a})_{a\in[k_{n}]}
and take the multinomial partition corresponding to the random probability sequence given by

\Pi_{a}\ =\ \frac{e^{X_{a}/\tau}}{\sum_{b\in[k_{n}]}e^{X_{b}/\tau}}.

We show in Theorem
23
that the sizes of the communities obtained from such random partition follow a power-law distribution.

2.4
Related Works and Special Cases of the PPM

The planted partition model encompasses several well-known special cases. When the partition
T_{n}
consists of a single community of size
s_{n}\geq 2
and all other communities are singletons (size 1), we recover the
planted dense subgraph
model. The
planted clustering model
arises when
T_{n}
contains
k
communities of equal size
s_{n}\geq 2
while the remaining
n-s_{n}\cdot k_{n}
vertices are singletons. When all community sizes are greater or equal to 2, we recover the
stochastic block model
(SBM) with homogeneous interactions.

When the vertex set is partitioned into
k=\Theta(1)
communities each of size
\Theta(n)
, tight conditions both for exact and weak recovery are available in the literature, and we refer to
[
1
]
for a review. In the following, we only highlight the results when
k
can grow with
n
.

Exact recovery.

[
8
]
establish several key results for the impossibility and possibility of exact recovery when the communities are of equal size
n/k
and
k
grows arbitrarily. Their paper highlights various phase transitions and–up to unspecified constants–precisely characterize those transitions. The problem progresses through four distinct stages: (1) being statistically unsolvable, (2) becoming statistically solvable but computationally expensive, (3) transitioning to being solvable in polynomial time, and finally (4) being solvable by a simple common-neighbor counting algorithm.
However, the equal-size community assumption is limiting, as we highlighted in the introduction: communities in real networks can have sizes with different orders of magnitude.
Moreover, the algorithms in
[
8
]
require knowledge of the number of communities and of
p_{n},q_{n}
. In contrast, we establish that Algorithm
1
, a simple common-neighbor counting algorithm, can achieve exact recovery even when the communities have arbitrary sizes and the number of communities is unknown.

Weak recovery.

Weak recovery for multinomial partitions with a uniform prior
\pi_{a}=1/k_{n}
has been investigated in recent preprints
[
6
,
9
,
19
]
. In particular, using non-backtracking walk statistics,
[
9
]
shows that weak recovery is achievable below the Kesten–Stigum threshold in the regime
\sqrt{n}\ll k_{n}\ll n
,
p_{n}=\Theta(k_{n}/n)
and
q_{n}=\Theta(1/n)
, and they propose a conjecture for the weak recovery threshold in this regime. The authors also propose a conjecture characterizing the precise weak recovery threshold in this setting. Supporting evidence for this conjecture is provided in
[
6
]
, which analyzes the problem within the low-degree polynomial framework and further extends the weak recovery region to denser regimes by incorporating clique-based statistics.

Moreover,
[
19
]
establishes low-degree hardness results for weak recovery in SBMs with multinomial partitions under a uniform prior
\pi_{a}=1/k
. Specifically,
[
19
, Theorem 5]
shows that when
k\leq\sqrt{n}
and
\frac{n(p_{n}-q_{n})^{2}}{k^{2}q_{n}(1-p_{n})}=o(1)
, no low-degree polynomial algorithm can achieve weak recovery. In the sparse regime
q_{n}=\Theta(n^{-1})
, this condition simplifies to
p_{n}\ll k/n
, in which case a typical vertex has no neighbors within its own community. When
k\geq\sqrt{n}
,
[
19
, Theorem 5]
instead yields the impossibility condition
p_{n}^{2}\ll q_{n}
, which further highlights that
p_{n}
and
q_{n}
cannot be asymptotically of the same order if weak recovery is to be achievable.

Planted dense subgraph

[
8
]
establishes several key results for the impossibility and
possibility of exact recovery when the partition comprises a single community of size
s_{n}\geq 2
and all other communities are singletons. However, they establish the possibility of exact recovery under the additional assumption
s\geq\log n
while
Theorem

7
can be applied for
s_{n}\gg 1
.

[
31
]
establishes criteria for the success and failure of low-degree polynomials in achieving weak recovery. In particular, polynomials of degree
n^{\Omega(1)}
fail at weak recovery if
\frac{p_{n}-q_{n}}{\sqrt{q_{n}(1-p_{n})}}\ll\min\{1,\frac{\sqrt{n}}{s_{n}}\}
. Conversely, polynomials of degree
\mathcal{O}(\log n)
succeed at weak recovery if
\frac{p_{n}-q_{n}}{\sqrt{q_{n}}}\gg\frac{\sqrt{n}}{s_{n}}
and
p_{n}s_{n}=\omega(1)
. In the regime
p_{n}=\Theta(n^{-a})
,
q_{n}=\Theta(n^{-a})
and
s_{n}=\Theta(n^{b})
for constants
a\in(0,2)
and
b\in(0,1)
, this implies low-degree hardness of recovery at degree
n^{\Omega(1)}
whenever
b<(1+a)/2
, while low-degree polynomials succeed whenever
b>(1+a)/2
. For related results on weak recovery in the planted dense subgraph model and its connection to the planted clique problem, we refer to
[
16
]
.

Estimation of the number of communities

Several methods have been proposed to address the model selection problem, specifically the estimation of the number of communities
k
. However, most theoretical works assume that the number of communities is either finite or, at best, grows slowly (typically,
k=o(\log n)
).
The only work that imposes no restrictions on the number of communities appears to be
[
7
]
. In this paper, the author proves that an information-theoretic criterion, based on the Minimum Description Length principle, is strongly consistent. However, computing this estimator is NP-hard. In contrast, in the regimes where Algorithm
1
achieves exact recovery, it provides an efficient method for recovering both the communities and their number, without prior knowledge of
k
. Moreover, when the assumptions of
Theorem

3
are satisfied, the partition inferred by Algorithm
1
is composed of more communities than the true partition, which provides an upper-bound on
k
.

3
Theoretical Framework and Algorithm

In this section, we present Diamond Percolation and discuss some of its properties. In addition, we formulate the assumptions that we make in order to prove the recovery criteria in Sections
4
and
5
.

3.1
Diamond Percolation

Consider an unweighted and undirected graph
G
with vertex set
[n]
, and let
W_{ij}
denote the number of common neighbors between
i
and
j
(i.e., the number of
wedges
from
i
to
j
). That is,

W_{ij}\ =\ \#\left\{u\in[n]\setminus\{i,j\}\ :\ u\stackrel{{\scriptstyle G}}{{\sim}}i\text{ and }u\stackrel{{\scriptstyle G}}{{\sim}}j\right\}.

We consider Algorithm
1
for detecting communities. This algorithm first constructs a graph
G^{*}
such that
i\stackrel{{\scriptstyle G^{*}}}{{\sim}}j
iff
i\stackrel{{\scriptstyle G}}{{\sim}}j
and
W_{ij}\geq 2
. In other words,
G^{*}
only keeps the edges of
G
that correspond to the middle edge of some
diamond
, i.e., if it is part of at least two triangles. We then consider the partition
C
formed by the connected components of
G^{*}
and return these as the detected communities. In the rest of the paper, we denote Algorithm
1
by
\mathcal{D}
and denote the resulting partition into communities by
C=\mathcal{D}(G)
. Note that the algorithm
\mathcal{D}(\cdot)
does not require knowledge of any model parameters. Algorithm
1
is illustrated in
Figure

1
.

Figure 1
:
Algorithm
1
is illustrated on a PPM consisting of two equally-sized communities of size
10
each, with
p=\tfrac{1}{2}
and
q=\tfrac{1}{20}
. The true communities correspond to the red circles and blue squares. The solid lines are the edges of
G^{*}
, while the dashed lines are the edges of
G
that are not retained in
G^{*}
. The orange shaded regions represent the detected communities. We see that the two communities are correctly separated, but that two vertices are incorrectly isolated.

The following lemma provides the space and time complexity of Algorithm
1
.

Lemma 1
.

Algorithm
1
has
\mathcal{O}(n+|E|)

\mathcal{O}(n+\sum_{i\in[n]}d_{i}^{2})

d_{i}
denotes the degree of vertex
i

.

Finally, we note that we use the name
Diamond Percolation
to refer to Algorithm
1
in analogy with local percolation-based clustering, though no percolation threshold is analyzed in this work.

3.2
Technical Tools for Studying Algorithm
1

In this section, we discuss the main tools used to prove the Theorem
7
,
13
,
17
and
20
. The full proofs are provided in Appendix
B
.

Recall that we write
i\stackrel{{\scriptstyle T}}{{\sim}}j
to indicate that two vertices
i
and
j
belong to the same community according to the partition
T
. Given two partitions
C
and
T
, we say that
C
is a
refinement
of
T
, denoted
C\preceq T
, if
i\stackrel{{\scriptstyle C}}{{\sim}}j
implies
i\stackrel{{\scriptstyle T}}{{\sim}}j
for all
i,j\in[n]
. This condition defines a partial order on the set of partitions.

To establish that Algorithm
1
recovers the true partition
T_{n}
, we first show that the partition
C_{n}
produced by Algorithm
1
is, with high probability, a refinement of
T_{n}
. Ensuring that
C_{n}
is a refinement of
T_{n}
requires the following assumption. We recall that the random variable
S_{n}
represents the size of the community to which a uniformly randomly chosen vertex belongs.

Assumption 2

(Size-sparsity assumption)
.

We assume
n^{2}\mathbb{E}[S_{n}^{2}]q_{n}^{3}p_{n}^{2}=o(1)
and
q_{n}=o(n^{-4/5})
.

Theorem 3
.

Let
G_{n}\sim\mathrm{PPM}(T_{n},p_{n},q_{n})
, such that Assumption
2
holds. Then, the partition
C_{n}
returned by Algorithm
1
satisfies
\mathbb{P}(C_{n}\preceq T_{n})\rightarrow 1
.

Assumption
2
ensures that, with probability tending to 1, any pair of vertices connected by an edge and belonging to different communities has at most one common neighbor. To see this, consider two vertices
i
and
j
belonging to different communities, say
a
and
b
, with respective sizes
s_{a}
and
s_{b}
. The number of common neighbors of
i
and
j
that belong to community
a
or
b
is distributed as
\operatorname{Bin}(s_{a}+s_{b}-2,p_{n}q_{n})
, and the number of common neighbors that belong to neither
a
nor
b
follows
\operatorname{Bin}(n-s_{a}-s_{b},q_{n}^{2})
. Consequently, the probability that
i
and
j
share more than two common neighbors is at most
\mathcal{O}((s_{a}+s_{b})^{2}p_{n}^{2}q_{n}^{2}+n^{2}q_{n}^{4})
. Because there are
\mathcal{O}(n^{2}q_{n})
pairs of vertices connected by an edge and belonging to different communities, the probability that at least one such pair has more than two common neighbors is vanishing if
n^{2}q_{n}\left(\mathbb{E}[S_{n}^{2}]p_{n}^{2}q_{n}^{2}+n^{2}q_{n}^{4}\right)=o(1)
. This condition is equivalent to Assumption
2
. A formal proof is provided in Appendix
A.2
.

Assumption

2
provides the main motivation for setting the threshold at
two overlapping triangles
. On the one hand, if we were to keep all edges that participate in triangles, then we would have a nonvanishing
2
2

2

Note that in an Erdős-Rényi graph with density
p_{n}=\lambda/n
, the number of triangles converges in distribution to
\text{Poi}(\lambda^{3}/6).
number of incorrect edges in
G^{*}
. On the other hand, if we only kept edges participating in three or more triangles, we would have less correct edges in
G^{*}
, leading to poorer recovery results.

As an example, consider the particular case where
p_{n}=\Theta(1)
and
q_{n}=\Theta(n^{-1})
. Under this setting, Assumption
2
simplifies to
\mathbb{E}[S_{n}^{2}]=o(n)
, a condition that holds for many types of partitions. For instance, in the case of balanced communities of size
s
,
i.e.,
when
T_{n}\sim\mathrm{Balanced}(n,\lfloor n/s\rfloor,s)
, this condition reduces to
s=o(\sqrt{n})
. Moreover, when
T_{n}\sim\text{Uniform}(n)
, we have
\mathbb{E}[S_{n}^{2}]=\mathcal{O}(\log^{2}(n))

[
14
]
and the condition is automatically satisfied.

More generally, whenever
S_{n}
is concentrated around its mean,
i.e.
, when
\mathbb{E}[S_{n}^{2}]\sim\mathbb{E}[S_{n}]^{2}
, then the expected internal degree of a random vertex is
d_{\text{in}}=p_{n}\mathbb{E}[S_{n}-1]
, while the expected external degree of a random vertex is
d_{\text{ex}}=q_{n}\cdot(n-\mathbb{E}[S_{n}])
, and
Assumption

2
can be rewritten to
d_{\text{in}}^{2}d_{\text{ex}}^{3}\ll n
and
d_{\text{ex}}\ll n^{1/5}.

The analysis of community detection algorithms typically requires some upper bound on the external degree and some lower bound on the internal degree. In contrast, we need upper bounds for both internal and external degrees to avoid diamonds between different communities.
Section

2.1
provides lower bounds on
d_{\text{in}}
that are needed to ensure sufficiently many diamonds inside the communities.

Obtaining a refinement of the true communities in itself is neither hard nor informative. For instance, the partition
\{\{1\},\{2\},\cdots,\{n\}\}
, which consists solely of singletons, is a refinement of
any
partition. Therefore,
C_{n}\preceq T_{n}
alone does not guarantee good performance in terms of the correlation coefficient
\rho(C_{n},T_{n})
as defined in (
2.1
). To ensure that
C_{n}\preceq T_{n}
translate into a result involving
\rho(C_{n},T_{n})
, we require the following assumption on the planted partition
T_{n}
.

Assumption 4

(Concentration of
m_{T_{n}}
)
.

For
m_{T_{n}}=\#\{ij\ :\ i\stackrel{{\scriptstyle T_{n}}}{{\sim}}j\}
, we assume that
1\ll\mathbb{E}[m_{T_{n}}]\ll n^{2}
and

\frac{m_{T_{n}}}{\mathbb{E}[m_{T_{n}}]}\ \xrightarrow{\mathbb{P}}\ 1.

The assumption
\frac{m_{T_{n}}}{\mathbb{E}[m_{T_{n}}]}\ \xrightarrow{\mathbb{P}}\ 1
holds for many classes of random partitions, including balanced partitions, uniform partitions
[
14
]
, and the power-law partitions studied in
Section

5
.

The following lemma simplifies the asymptotics of the correlation coefficient
\rho(C_{n},T_{n})
when
C_{n}\preceq T_{n}
.

Lemma 5
.

Let
C_{n},T_{n}
be sequences of partitions satisfying
\mathbb{P}(C_{n}\preceq T_{n})\to 1
and where
T_{n}
satisfies
Assumption

4
, then

\rho(C_{n},T_{n})-\sqrt{\frac{m_{C_{n}}}{\mathbb{E}[m_{T_{n}}]}}\ \stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathbb{P}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\ 0.

Lemma

5
tells us that when
C_{n}
refines
T_{n}
, the correlation essentially measures the proportion of correctly grouped vertex pairs.

Theorem

3
and
Lemma

5
are crucial for demonstrating both weak and almost exact recovery.

4
Recovery of Planted Partitions

In this section, we present the conditions for Algorithm
1
to recover a planted partition. Sections
4.1
,
4.2
, and
4.3
provide the results and several examples for exact, almost exact, and weak recovery, respectively. All proofs for this section can be found in Appendix
B
.

Throughout this section,
G_{n}\sim\mathrm{PPM}(T_{n},p_{n},q_{n})
is a PPM with vertex set
[n]
, planted partition
T_{n}
, internal connection probability
p_{n}
, and external connection probability
q_{n}
.

4.1
Exact Recovery

To derive a consistency result for exact recovery, we impose a lower bound on the size of the smallest non-singleton community.

Assumption 6

(Minimum community size)
.

There exists some sequence
s_{n}^{(\min)}\to\infty
so that

\mathbb{P}\left(\exists i\in[n]\colon\ 1\,<\,|T_{n}(i)|\,<\,s_{n}^{(\min)}\right)\rightarrow 0.

Assumption

6
ensures that no community becomes disconnected, which would otherwise make exact recovery impossible.
The following theorem states that if every community is sufficiently large and has enough internal edges, the algorithm reconstructs the true partition exactly.

Theorem 7
.

Consider a graph
G_{n}\sim\text{PPM}(T_{n},p_{n},q_{n})
, where the sequence of random partitions
T_{n}
satisfy Assumptions
2
and
6
, and

p_{n}=\sqrt{\frac{\log\mathbb{E}[m_{T}]+\log\log\mathbb{E}[m_{T}]+\omega(1)}{s_{n}^{(\min)}}}.

Then Algorithm
1
achieves exact recovery.

To compare
Theorem

7
with existing results in the literature, we present some examples of its application.

Example 8
.

Consider
G_{n}\sim\mathrm{PPM}(T_{n},p_{n},q_{n})
, where
T_{n}\sim\mathrm{Balanced}(n,k,s_{n})
, for
k
fixed,
1\ll s_{n}\ll n^{2/3}
,
q_{n}=\mathcal{O}(n^{-1})
and
p_{n}\geq\sqrt{3s_{n}^{-1}\log s_{n}}
. Then Algorithm
1
achieves exact recovery.

[
8
]
establishes exact recovery of a single community of size
s_{n}=\Omega(\log n)
(that is,
T_{n}\sim\mathrm{Balanced}(n,1,s_{n})
), while the previous example allows for one or more communities having a much smaller size (
1\ll s_{n}\ll\log n
).

Example 9
.

Consider
G_{n}\sim\mathrm{PPM}(T_{n},p_{n},q_{n})
, where
T_{n}\sim\mathrm{Balanced}(n,\lfloor n/s_{n}\rfloor,s_{n})
, where
\log n+3\log\log n\leq s_{n}\ll\sqrt{n}
,
q_{n}=\mathcal{O}(n^{-1})
and
p_{n}\geq\sqrt{s_{n}^{-1}(\log n+3\log s_{n})}
. Then Algorithm
1
achieves exact recovery. The condition
s_{n}\geq\log n+3\log\log n
is required to ensure
\sqrt{s_{n}^{-1}(\log n+3\log s_{n})}\leq 1
.

Let us compare Example
9
with results established in
[
8
]
. First, consider the case
s_{n}=\alpha\log n
. According to
[
8
, Theorem 10]
, a simple degree thresholding approach succeeds at exact recovery if
p\geq c\alpha^{-1/2}
for some unspecified constant
c>0
. However, this condition may never be satisfied if the unspecified
c
is too large. In fact, by scrutinizing the proof of
[
8
, Theorem 10]
, we observe that
c\geq 144
is needed. In contrast, our result provides an explicit lower-bound on
p_{n}
to guarantee the exact recovery by Algorithm
1
. More generally, for
\log n\ll s\ll n^{1/2}
,
[
8
, Theorem 10]
requires
p_{n}\geq c\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{s_{n}}}
(again with
c\geq 144
). Hence, the condition
p_{n}\geq\sqrt{\frac{\log n+3\log s_{n}}{s_{n}}}
in Example
9
is strictly less restrictive. Finally,
[
8
, Theorem 6]
shows that a convex relaxation of MLE achieves exact recovery if
s_{n}p_{n}\geq c\log n
. Again, this requires
s_{n}\geq c\log n
, which is more restrictive than our requirement.

Example 10
.

Consider a planted partition
T_{n}
consisting of an arbitrary number of communities whose sizes are in the range
[s_{n}^{(\min)},n^{\alpha}]
for
s_{n}^{(\min)}\gg\log n
and
\alpha<1/2
. We have
m_{T}\leq\tfrac{1}{2}n^{1+\alpha}
. Thus,
\mathcal{D}
achieves exact recovery for
q_{n}=\mathcal{O}(n^{-1})
and

p_{n}\ \geq\ \sqrt{\frac{(1+\alpha)\log n+o(\log n)}{s_{n}^{(\min)}}}.

This last example highlights that the large communities only increase the threshold by a constant factor. The condition
s_{n}^{(\min)}p_{n}^{2}\gtrsim\log n
is analogous to the condition
s_{n}p_{n}^{2}\gtrsim\log n
obtained in Example
9
. Notably, this result is new to the literature, as
[
8
]
focuses exclusively on communities of equal size. Finally, this result is consistent with the fact that the exact recovery threshold in the PPM (with a finite number of communities) is primarily determined by the difficulty of recovering the smallest community, as this is the most challenging community to identify.

Example 11
.

Consider an Erdős-Rényi random graph
G_{n}
with connection probability
q_{n}=o(n^{-4/5})
, or equivalently,
G_{n}\sim\mathrm{PPM}(T_{n},p_{n},q_{n})
, where
T_{n}
consists of
n
singleton communities and
p_{n}\in[0,1]
is arbitrary. Then Algorithm
1
achieves exact recovery. That is, Algorithm
1
correctly detects the absence of communities in
G_{n}
.

This last example highlights that our algorithm does not lead to false positives in Erdős-Rényi random graphs, as long as the graph is not too dense.

4.2
Almost Exact Recovery

While almost exact recovery has been studied in the case of
k=\Theta(1)
, it has (to the best of our knowledge) not been studied for
k
growing arbitrarily fast and in the presence of arbitrarily small communities. Therefore, the results in this section are the first results on almost exact recovery of small communities.
Similar to exact recovery, we impose a constraint on the number of small communities.

Assumption 12

(Soft minimum community size)
.

There exists some sequence
s_{n}^{(\min)}\to\infty
so that

\frac{\mathbb E[(S_{n}-1)\cdot\mathbb 1\{S_{n}<s_{n}^{(\min)}\}]}{\mathbb E[S_{n}-1]}\to 0.

Assumption

12
is less restrictive than
Assumption

6
. Specifically,
Assumption

12
ensures that communities of size smaller than
s_{n}^{(\min)}
have negligible contribution to the expectation of
\mathbb{E}[S_{n}-1]
. Small communities are still admissible and the algorithm may not correctly recover them, but it turns out that they do not hinder almost exact recovery in terms of the correlation coefficient
\rho
.
If we denote the size-biased version of
S_{n}-1
by
(S_{n}-1)^{*}
, then
Assumption

12
is equivalent to
(S_{n}-1)^{*}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathbb{P}}}{{\to}}\infty
. Since
(S_{n}-1)^{*}
stochastically dominates
S_{n}-1
, a sufficient condition for
Assumption

12
is
S_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathbb{P}}}{{\to}}\infty
.

Theorem 13
.

Consider a graph
G_{n}\sim\text{PPM}(T_{n},p_{n},q_{n})
, where the sequence of random partitions
T_{n}
satisfy Assumptions
2
and
4
, and Assumption
12
with
s_{n}^{(\min)}\to\infty
so that

p_{n}=\sqrt{\frac{2\log s_{n}^{(\min)}+\log\log s_{n}^{(\min)}+\omega(1)}{s_{n}^{(\min)}}}.

Then Algorithm
1
achieves almost exact recovery.

Recall that when Assumptions
2
and
4
hold,
Theorem

3
and
Lemma

5
ensure that
\rho(C_{n},T_{n})^{2}-\frac{m_{C_{n}}}{\mathbb{E}[m_{T_{n}}]}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathbb{P}}}{{\rightarrow}}0
. Thus,
\rho(C_{n},T_{n})\ \xrightarrow{\mathbb{P}}\ 1
holds whenever
\tfrac{\mathbb{E}[m_{C_{n}}]}{\mathbb{E}[m_{T_{n}}]}\to 1
. The proof of this result establishes
Theorem

13
.

Comparing
Theorem

13
to
Theorem

7
, we see that the
\mathbb{E}[m_{T}]
is replaced with
\left(s_{n}^{(\min)}\right)^{2}
. This reflects the fact that our proof is based on ensuring that for an arbitrary community of size at least
s_{n}^{(\min)}
, all vertex pairs inside that community have at least two common neighbors w.h.p., so that
\mathcal{D}
recovers it correctly.

The following
Example

14
shows that Algorithm
1
achieves almost exact recovery for balanced partitions, where each community has size
s_{n}\gg 1
, while
Example

9
requires
s_{n}=\Omega(\log n)
for exact recovery.

Example 14
.

Let
T_{n}\sim\mathrm{Balanced}(n,k_{n},s_{n})
for
1\ll s_{n}\ll\sqrt{n}
and
k_{n}s_{n}\leq n
, and suppose
p_{n}\geq\sqrt{\tfrac{3\log s_{n}}{s_{n}}}
and
q_{n}=o(n^{-4/5})
. Then Algorithm
1
achieves almost exact recovery.

Note that the
-1
in the denominator of
Assumption

12
is necessary to allow for cases where
\mathbb{E}[S_{n}]\to 1
, like
Example

14
with
k_{n}s_{n}^{2}\ll n
. For example,
k_{n}=1
and
s_{n}\sim\log\log n
.

Example 15
.

Suppose
T_{n}
is drawn uniformly from the set of all partitions of
[n]
. We recall that in that case, we have
S_{n}/\log n\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathbb{P}}}{{\rightarrow}}1
and
m_{T_{n}}/\mathbb{E}[m_{T_{n}}]\ \xrightarrow{\mathbb{P}}\ 1

[
14
]
. Hence, Algorithm
1
achieves almost exact recovery for
p_{n}=\omega\left(\sqrt{\tfrac{\log\log n}{\log n}}\right)
and
q_{n}=o(n^{-4/5})
.

4.3
Weak Recovery

In this section, we will present two different recovery results for weak recovery. One tailored to partitions where a relatively large fraction of vertices is part of large communities, while the other is tailored to the case where almost all vertices are part of communities of bounded size.
Note that weak recovery is trivially implied by almost exact recovery. Therefore, we will mostly focus on settings where
Assumption

12
does
not
hold, since otherwise we already achieve almost exact recovery. Whenever
Assumption

12
does not hold, then for every
s_{n}\to\infty
, we have

\varepsilon_{n}(s_{n})=\frac{\mathbb E[(S_{n}-1)\cdot\mathbb 1\{S_{n}<s_{n}\}]}{\mathbb E[S_{n}-1]}\not\to 0.

However,
\varepsilon_{n}(s_{n})\in[0,1]
, so that by the Bolzano-Weierstrass theorem, it must converge along some subsequence. That is, there exists some
\varepsilon\in[0,1]
and some subsequence
n_{\ell}
so that

\varepsilon_{n_{\ell}}(s_{n_{\ell}})\to\varepsilon
,
as
\ell\to\infty
.
It is easy to construct pathological partitions
T_{n}
and sequences
s_{n}\to\infty
for which
\varepsilon_{n}(s_{n})
oscillates, but these are not of theoretic interest.
Therefore, we will focus on cases where there exists some sequence
s_{n}\to\infty
for which
\varepsilon_{n}(s_{n})
converges, so that it converges along every subsequence.

Assumption 16

(Weak minimum community size)
.

There exists some sequence
s_{n}^{(\min)}\to\infty
and
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
so that

\frac{\mathbb E[(S_{n}-1)\cdot\mathbb 1\{S_{n}<s_{n}^{(\min)}\}]}{\mathbb E[S_{n}-1]}\to\varepsilon.

We present the following recovery result:

Theorem 17
.

Consider a graph
G_{n}\sim\text{PPM}(T_{n},p_{n},q_{n})
, where the sequence of random partitions
T_{n}
satisfy Assumptions
2
and
4
, and Assumption
16
with
s_{n}^{(\min)}\to\infty
and
\varepsilon>0
so that

p_{n}=\sqrt{\frac{2\log s_{n}^{(\min)}+\log\log s_{n}^{(\min)}+\omega(1)}{s_{n}^{(\min)}}}.

Then Algorithm
1
achieves weak recovery. More precisely,

\rho(\mathcal{D}(G_{n}),T_{n})\geq\sqrt{1-\varepsilon}+o{P}(1).

Proof.

We make use of
Lemma

5
and will prove that

\frac{m_{C_{n}}}{\mathbb{E}[m_{T_{n}}]}\geq 1-\varepsilon+o{P}(1).

We separate
m_{T_{n}}=m_{T_{n}}^{-}+m_{T_{n}}^{+}
into vertex pairs that are part of communities of size smaller than
s_{n}^{(\min)}
and communities of size at least
s_{n}^{(\min)}
. By
Assumption

16
, we have
\mathbb{E}[m_{T_{n}}^{-}]\sim\varepsilon\mathbb{E}[m_{T}]
and
\mathbb{E}[m_{T_{n}}^{+}]\sim(1-\varepsilon)\mathbb{E}[m_{T}]
. We similarly separate
m_{C_{n}}=m_{C_{n}}^{-}+m_{C_{n}}^{+}
. Following the same steps as in the proof of
Theorem

13
, we conclude that
m_{C_{n}}^{+}/\mathbb{E}[m_{T_{n}}^{+}]=1+o{P}(1)
.
We will bound
m_{C_{n}}^{-}\geq 0
and write

\displaystyle\frac{m_{C_{n}}^{-}+m_{C_{n}}^{+}}{\mathbb{E}[m_{T_{n}}]}\geq(1-\varepsilon)\frac{m_{C_{n}}^{+}}{(1-\varepsilon)\mathbb{E}[m_{T_{n}}]}=1-\varepsilon+o{P}(1),

which completes the proof.
∎

Comparing
Theorem

17
to
Theorem

13
, we see that we merely replace
Assumption

12
with
Assumption

16
. This allows us to ensure that sufficiently many communities are completely recovered, so that a nonvanishing fraction of the vertex pairs is correctly recovered.

Next, we develop a weak recovery result that allows for communities of bounded size.
If there is some
s_{n}\to\infty
for which
\varepsilon_{n}(s_{n})
converges, but
Assumption

12
and
Assumption

16
do not hold, then it must hold that for every
s_{n}\to\infty
, we have
\varepsilon_{n}(s_{n})\to 1
. The following lemma shows that this implies that
S_{n}
has a convergent subsequence:

Lemma 18
.

If for every
s_{n}\to\infty
, it holds that

\frac{\mathbb E[(S_{n}-1)\cdot\mathbb 1\{S_{n}<s_{n}\}]}{\mathbb E[S_{n}-1]}\to 1,

then the sequence of conditional distributions
(S_{n}\ |\ S_{n}>1)
is uniformly integrable and there exists some limiting random variable
S
and a subsequence
n_{\ell}
so that
(S_{n_{\ell}}\ |\ S_{n_{\ell}}>1)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\to}}S
as
\ell\to\infty
. Moreover,
\mathbb{E}[S_{n_{\ell}}\ |\ S_{n_{\ell}}>1]\to\mathbb{E}[S]
.

As we are mainly interested in the convergent subsequence, we will impose the following assumption.

Assumption 19

(Convergent size distribution)
.

There exists a distribution
S
with
\mathbb{E}[S]<\infty
and
\mathbb{P}(S\geq 4)>0
, and such that

(S_{n}\ |\ S_{n}>1)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\to}}S
and
\mathbb{E}\left[S_{n}\ \middle|\ S_{n}>1\right]\to\mathbb{E}[S].

Theorem 20
.

Consider a graph
G_{n}\sim\text{PPM}(T_{n},p,q_{n})
, where
p>0
,
T_{n}
satisfies Assumptions
2
,
4
and
19
. Then Algorithm
1
achieves weak recovery.

We first discuss the conditions of
Theorem

20
. Note that the condition
\mathbb{P}(S\geq 4)>0
is necessary because Algorithm
1
is based on finding subgraphs of size
4
.
Regarding
p_{n}=p>0
, note that for a partition with balanced communities of size
s<\infty
, weak recovery is not feasible if
p_{n}\cdot s\to 0

Recovery of Power-law Partitions

In this section, we focus on power-law partitions. We recall some results on power-law random variables in
Section

5.1
, show how to construct power-law partitions in
Section

5.2
, and state the results for recovering power-law partitions in

5.1
Power-law Random Variables

It has been observed in many real-world networks that the community sizes follow a power law
[
18
,
32
,
34
]
. Informally, this means that the probability of observing a community of size larger than
s
scales like
s^{1-\tau}
for some
\tau>1
. In our setting, we formalize this using the following definition:

Definition 22
.

A random partition
T_{n}
asymptotically follows a power law with exponent
\tau>1
if there is some scaling sequence
s_{n}
so that

\frac{S_{n}}{s_{n}}\ \stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\to}}\ S,

for some random variable
S
that satisfies

\mathbb{P}(S\geq x)\ =\ \Theta(x^{1-\tau}).

The random variable
S
in the above definition is said to follow a power-law distribution with exponent
\tau
. Note that other works, such as
[
34
]
, use a more general definition in which the
\Theta(x^{1-\tau})
is replaced by regularly-varying functions. For simplicity, we adopt the narrower definition given above.

If
S
follows a power-law distribution with exponent
\tau>2
, then
\mathbb{E}[S^{k}]<\infty
if
k<\tau-1
and
\mathbb{E}[S^{k}]=\infty
if
k>\tau-1
. The simplest example of a probability distribution that satisfies a power-law is the
Pareto distribution

Z\sim\text{Pareto}(c,\beta)
, given by

\mathbb{P}(Z>z)\ =\ (c/z)^{\beta},

where
c>0
is the scale parameter and
\beta>0
is the tail exponent. The Pareto distribution follows a power law with exponent
\tau=\beta+1
.

5.2
Construction of Power-law Partitions

Recall from
Section

2.3
that, if
T_{n}\sim\text{Powerlaw}(\tau,k,n)
for
\tau>2
and
k\in[n]
, then each vertex is assigned to the community
a\in[k]
with probability

\displaystyle\Pi_{a}\ =\ \frac{e^{X_{a}/\tau}}{\sum_{b\in[k]}e^{X_{b}/\tau}},

(5.1)

where
(X_{a})_{a\in[k]}
is a sequence of i.i.d. exponentially distributed random variables with parameter
1
.
We refer to
\Pi_{a}
as the
proportion
of community
a
and denote its distribution by
\Pi_{a}\sim\text{Prop}(\tau,k)
.
For
T_{n}\sim\text{Powerlaw}(\tau,k,n)
, let
\Pi^{*}_{n}
denote the proportion of the community of a vertex chosen uniformly at random. The distribution of
\Pi^{*}_{n}
corresponds to the
size-biased
distribution of
\Pi_{a}

[
3
]
. That is, given proportions
\Pi_{a}=\pi_{a}
for
a\in[k]
, we have

\mathbb{P}\left(\Pi^{*}_{n}=x\ \middle|\ \forall a\in[k]\colon\Pi_{a}=\pi_{a}\right)\ =\ x\cdot\left|\{a\in[k]\colon\pi_{a}=x\}\right|.

Because this distribution does not directly
3
3

3

It depends on
k
, which may depend on
n
.
depend on

The following theorem states that this construction of partitions leads to power-law distributed community sizes.

Theorem 23
.

Let
\tau>2
and
1\ll k_{n}\ll n
. If
T_{n}\sim\text{Powerlaw}(\tau,k_{n},n)
, then

\frac{S_{n}}{n/k_{n}}\ \stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\to}}\ \text{Pareto}\left(1-\tfrac{1}{\tau},\tau-1\right),

In particular,
S_{n}
asymptotically follows a power law with exponent
\tau
and scaling
s_{n}=n/k_{n}
.

When
k_{n}=\Theta(n)
, we can determine the limiting distribution of
S_{n}
exactly:

Lemma 24

Let
\tau>2
and
k_{n}\sim n/s
, for
s>1
. If
T_{n}\sim\text{Powerlaw}(\tau,k_{n},n)
, then

\mathbb{P}(S_{n}=r+1)\to\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{Z^{r}}{r!}e^{-Z}\right],

where
Z\sim\text{Pareto}(s\cdot(1-\tfrac{1}{\tau}),\tau-1)

with Pareto mixture, so that

\tau

Discussion and Future Work

In this work, we presented Diamond Percolation, a simple community detection method that runs in polynomial time and requires no parameter knowledge. We proved several conditions under which our algorithm achieves exact, almost exact, and weak recovery. In this section, we discuss ways in which the results could be extended and relate our methods to existing work.

Isolated vertices.
Because Diamond Percolation clusters vertices based on overlapping triangles, every vertex of degree less than three will be isolated in
G^{*}
. Therefore, such vertices will form singleton communities in
C_{n}
. Sometimes, as is the case in
Figure

1
, each of the edges of such a low-degree vertex connect to the same community. This suggests that we may be able to improve the performance by assigning such isolated low-degree vertices to a neighboring community. However, doing so may affect the validity of
Theorem

3
. Improving Algorithm
1
by relabeling these low-degree vertices is an interesting direction for future research.

Size-dependent community densities.
Our results allow for communities of heterogeneous sizes, where the largest communities may differ orders of magnitude from the smallest communities. For a vertex in a community of size
s_{n}\gg 1
, its degree is of the order
s_{n}p_{n}
. This leads to a linear dependence between the community size and the degree of a vertex. While it has been observed in practice that large-degree vertices tend to be part of large communities, this relation is typically sub-linear
[
32
]
. This suggests that large communities should be sparser than small communities, which would be achieved by allowing for a size-dependent density
p_{n}(s_{n})
that decreases in
s_{n}
for fixed
n
.
We believe that most of our results can be extended to allow for these size-dependent intra-community densities, at the expense of more complicated expressions.

Recovery thresholds.
We already discussed in Section
2.4
some recent works establishing information-theoretic thresholds for weak and exact recovery in the PPM. However, unlike Diamond Percolation, the algorithms studied in these works typically assume knowledge of the model parameters and focus on balanced or near-balanced community structures. As a result, the corresponding information-theoretic thresholds fall outside the scope of the present article, which prioritizes parameter-free recovery under highly flexible community configurations. A systematic investigation of these thresholds under similarly minimal structural assumptions is an important avenue of future research.

Recovering the largest communities.

The difficulty of community detection is typically driven by the presence of small communities. As a result, recent works study the recovering of the
largest
communities only (typically of size
\Omega(\sqrt{n})
) in the presence of an arbitrary number of smaller communities
[
2
,
21
]
. While in this paper, we focus on the recovery of all communities, we could adapt Example
8
in Section
4.1
to the recovery of communities of size at least
s_{n}
among smaller communities.

Single-linkage agglomerative clustering.

The Diamond Percolation algorithm shares a conceptual similarity with single-linkage hierarchical clustering, a widely used agglomerative clustering method. In single-linkage clustering, two clusters are merged if they contain at least one pair of points that are sufficiently close, gradually forming larger clusters. Similarly, Diamond Percolation constructs a refined version of the input graph by preserving only edges that participate in at least two triangles, effectively filtering out weak connections. The final communities are then identified as the connected components of this filtered graph. This approach can be seen as a form of hierarchical clustering where the linkage criterion is based on common neighbors rather than direct pairwise distances. By setting a threshold of two shared neighbors, Diamond Percolation implicitly prioritizes denser local structures.

Hence, one could study a version of Diamond Percolation with a threshold different than two. Varying the threshold would return a hierarchy of partitions, where larger thresholds give rise to finer partitions. Recent work studying hierarchical extension of the stochastic block model show that linkage algorithms recover the hierarchy of communities when the communities are of size
\Theta(n)

[
10
]
. Our analysis hints that one could recover the hierarchy using linkage algorithms even when the communities are of much smaller size.

Adjusting for degree heterogeneity.

Furthermore, while we chose the number of shared triangles as the similarity measure between two vertices
i
and
j
, this metric can be modified based on their degrees. For example,
[
20
]
re-weigh triangles based on the degrees of the participating vertices to account for degree heterogeneity. Additionally,
[
5
]
proposes a distance measure based on node pair sampling.

Geometric models.

Finally, beside the stochastic block model, triangle-counting algorithms recover the planted partition in models with geometry
[
13
]
. In such models, the typical number of triangles is much larger than in the PPM, and communities can be detected based on shared neighborhoods better than in the PPM. Establishing the recovery of planted partitions in geometric models with small-size communities is an interesting avenue for future research.

, then
W_{ij}^{+}\sim\mathrm{Bin}(s_{i}+s_{j}-2,p_{n}q_{n})
and
W_{ij}^{-}\sim\mathrm{Bin}(n-s_{i}-s_{j},q_{n}^{2})
. Note that
W_{ij}^{-}
is stochastically dominated by the random variable
X\sim\mathrm{Bin}(n,q_{n}^{2})
. We obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(W_{ij}\geq 2\ \middle|\ i\stackrel{{\scriptstyle T_{n}}}{{\not\sim}}j\right)\ \leq\

\displaystyle\ \mathbb{P}\left(W_{ij}^{+}\geq 2\ \middle|\ i\stackrel{{\scriptstyle T_{n}}}{{\not\sim}}j\right)

\displaystyle+\mathbb{P}\left(W_{ij}^{+}=1\ \middle|\ i\stackrel{{\scriptstyle T_{n}}}{{\not\sim}}j\right)\mathbb{P}(X\geq 1)

\displaystyle+\mathbb{P}\left(W_{ij}^{+}=0\ \middle|\ i\stackrel{{\scriptstyle T_{n}}}{{\not\sim}}j\right)\mathbb{P}(X\geq 2)

\displaystyle\ =\

\displaystyle\ \mathcal{O}\left((s_{i}+s_{j})^{2}p_{n}^{2}q_{n}^{2}\right)+\mathcal{O}\left(n(s_{i}+s_{j})p_{n}q_{n}^{3}\right)+\mathcal{O}(n^{2}q_{n}^{4}),

(A.1)

where in the last step, we used that for
Y\sim\mathrm{Bin}(k,p)
, it holds that
\mathbb{P}(Y=0)\leq 1
,
\mathbb{P}(Y=1)\leq kp
and
\mathbb{P}(Y\geq 2)\leq k^{2}p^{2}
.
We bound
(s_{i}+s_{j})^{2}\leq 2(s_{i}^{2}+s_{j}^{2})
.
This tells us that there is some
c>0
so that

\mathbb{P}\left(W_{ij}\geq 2\ \middle|\ i\stackrel{{\scriptstyle T_{n}}}{{\not\sim}}j\right)\ \leq\ c\cdot(p_{n}^{2}q_{n}^{2}(s_{i}^{2}+s_{j}^{2})+n(s_{i}+s_{j})p_{n}q_{n}^{3}+n^{2}q_{n}^{4}).

We rewrite this as

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{1}\{W_{ij}\geq 2\}\ \middle|\ i\stackrel{{\scriptstyle T_{n}}}{{\not\sim}}j,|T_{n}(i)|,|T_{n}(j)|\right]

\displaystyle\ \leq\

\displaystyle c\cdot\left(p_{n}^{2}q_{n}^{2}(|T_{n}(i)|^{2}+|T_{n}(j)|^{2})+n(|T_{n}(i)|+|T_{n}(j)|)p_{n}q_{n}^{3}+n^{2}q_{n}^{4}\right).

Next, to get rid of the conditioning on
i\stackrel{{\scriptstyle T_{n}}}{{\not\sim}}j
, we multiply with
\mathbb{1}\{i\stackrel{{\scriptstyle T_{n}}}{{\not\sim}}j\}
and write

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{1}\{i\stackrel{{\scriptstyle T_{n}}}{{\not\sim}}j\}\mathbb{1}\{W_{ij}\geq 2\}\ \middle|\ |T_{n}(i)|,|T_{n}(j)|\right]

\displaystyle\ \leq\

\displaystyle c\cdot\mathbb{1}\{i\stackrel{{\scriptstyle T_{n}}}{{\not\sim}}j\}\cdot\left(p_{n}^{2}q_{n}^{2}(|T_{n}(i)|^{2}+|T_{n}(j)|^{2})+n(|T_{n}(i)|+|T_{n}(j)|)p_{n}q_{n}^{3}+n^{2}q_{n}^{4}\right)

\displaystyle\ \leq\

\displaystyle c\cdot\left(p_{n}^{2}q_{n}^{2}(|T_{n}(i)|^{2}+|T_{n}(j)|^{2})+n(|T_{n}(i)|+|T_{n}(j)|)p_{n}q_{n}^{3}+n^{2}q_{n}^{4}\right).

(A.2)

To prove the theorem, we use
\mathbb{P}(C_{n}\preceq T_{n})=1-\mathbb{P}(\exists_{ij}:i\stackrel{{\scriptstyle G_{n}^{*}}}{{\sim}}j\wedge i\stackrel{{\scriptstyle T_{n}}}{{\not\sim}}j)
and bound, by Markov’s inequality,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\exists_{ij}:i\stackrel{{\scriptstyle G_{n}^{*}}}{{\sim}}j\wedge i\stackrel{{\scriptstyle T_{n}}}{{\not\sim}}j)

\displaystyle\ \leq\ \mathbb{E}\left[\#\{ij\ :\ i\stackrel{{\scriptstyle G_{n}^{*}}}{{\sim}}j\wedge i\stackrel{{\scriptstyle T_{n}}}{{\not\sim}}j\}\right]

\displaystyle\ =\ \sum_{ij}\mathbb{P}(i\stackrel{{\scriptstyle T_{n}}}{{\not\sim}}j)\mathbb{P}\left(i\stackrel{{\scriptstyle G_{n}^{*}}}{{\sim}}j\ \middle|\ i\stackrel{{\scriptstyle T_{n}}}{{\not\sim}}j\right)

\displaystyle\ \leq\ \sum_{i\in[n],j\in[n]}\mathbb{P}(i\stackrel{{\scriptstyle T_{n}}}{{\not\sim}}j)\mathbb{P}\left(i\stackrel{{\scriptstyle G_{n}^{*}}}{{\sim}}j\ \middle|\ i\stackrel{{\scriptstyle T_{n}}}{{\not\sim}}j\right).

(A.3)

We rewrite

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(i\stackrel{{\scriptstyle T_{n}}}{{\not\sim}}j)\mathbb{P}\left(i\stackrel{{\scriptstyle G_{n}^{*}}}{{\sim}}j\ \middle|\ i\stackrel{{\scriptstyle T_{n}}}{{\not\sim}}j\right)

\displaystyle\ =\ q_{n}\mathbb{P}(i\stackrel{{\scriptstyle T_{n}}}{{\not\sim}}j)\mathbb{P}\left(W_{ij}\geq 2\ \middle|\ i\stackrel{{\scriptstyle T_{n}}}{{\not\sim}}j\right)

(A.4)

\displaystyle\ =\ q_{n}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{1}\{i\stackrel{{\scriptstyle T_{n}}}{{\not\sim}}j\}\mathbb{1}\{W_{ij}\geq 2\}\ \middle|\ |T_{n}(i)|,|T_{n}(j)|\right]\right].

(A.5)

where we used (
A.2
) and the tower rule.
Substituting (
A.4
) into (
A.3
), and applying the bound from (
A.2
) leads to

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\exists_{ij}:i\stackrel{{\scriptstyle G_{n}^{*}}}{{\sim}}j\wedge i\stackrel{{\scriptstyle T_{n}}}{{\not\sim}}j)\leq c\cdot n^{2}\cdot q_{n}\left(p_{n}^{2}q_{n}^{2}\cdot 2\mathbb{E}[S_{n}^{2}]+n\cdot 2\mathbb{E}[S_{n}]p_{n}q_{n}^{3}+n^{2}q_{n}^{4}\right).

(A.6)

We need this quantity to vanish. For the last term to vanish, we require
n^{4}q_{n}^{5}\to 0
, i.e.,
q_{n}\ll n^{-4/5}
. For the first term to vanish, we need

n^{2}q_{n}^{3}p_{n}^{2}\mathbb{E}[S_{n}^{2}]\to 0.

If these two terms vanish, then the other term must also vanish. To see this, note that the geometric mean of
n^{4}q_{n}^{5}
and
n^{2}q_{n}^{3}p_{n}^{2}\mathbb{E}[S_{n}^{2}]
is upper-bounds the middle term by Jensen’s inequality:

\sqrt{n^{2}q_{n}^{3}p_{n}^{2}\mathbb{E}[S_{n}^{2}]\cdot n^{4}q_{n}^{5}}=n^{3}q_{n}^{4}p_{n}\sqrt{\mathbb{E}[S_{n}^{2}]}\geq n^{3}q_{n}^{4}p_{n}\mathbb{E}[S_{n}].

Hence, if the first and third term of (
A.6
) vanish, it implies that
\mathbb{P}(\exists_{ij}:i\stackrel{{\scriptstyle G_{n}^{*}}}{{\sim}}j\wedge i\stackrel{{\scriptstyle T_{n}}}{{\not\sim}}j)\to 0
, so that

\mathbb{P}(C_{n}\preceq T_{n})\to 1,

which completes the proof.
∎

A.3
Proof of Lemma
5

Proof of Lemma
5
.

Recall the definition of the correlation coefficient between two partitions given in (
2.1
).
Whenever
C\preceq T
, we have
m_{CT}=m_{C}\leq m_{T}
, so that (
2.1
) becomes

\displaystyle\rho(C,T)

\displaystyle=\frac{m_{C}\cdot(N-m_{T})}{\sqrt{m_{C}\cdot(N-m_{C})\cdot m_{T}\cdot(N-m_{T})}}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{m_{C}}{m_{T}}\cdot\frac{N-m_{T}}{N-m_{C}}}.

By
Assumption

4
,
\mathbb{E}[m_{T}]\ll N
. Markov’s inequality tells us that
\mathbb{P}(m_{T}>\sqrt{N\mathbb{E}[m_{T}]})\leq\sqrt{\mathbb{E}[m_{T}]/N}\to 0
.
Conditioned on the events
C\preceq T
and
m_{T}\leq\sqrt{N\mathbb{E}[m_{T}]}\ll N
and calculate

\displaystyle\rho(C,T)

\displaystyle=\frac{N\cdot m_{CT}-m_{C}m_{T}}{\sqrt{m_{C}\cdot(N-m_{C})\cdot m_{T}\cdot(N-m_{T})}}

\displaystyle=\frac{m_{C}\cdot(N-m_{C})}{\sqrt{m_{C}\cdot(N-m_{C})\cdot m_{T}\cdot(N-m_{T})}}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{m_{C}}{m_{T}}}\cdot\sqrt{\frac{N-m_{C}}{N-m_{T}}}\sim\sqrt{\frac{m_{C}}{m_{T}}},

where the last step follows from the fact that
m_{C}\leq m_{T}\ll N
. Therefore, for any
\varepsilon>0
, it holds that

\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\rho(C,T)-\sqrt{\frac{m_{C}}{m_{T}}}\right|>\varepsilon\ \middle|\ C\preceq T,m_{T}\leq\sqrt{N\mathbb{E}[m_{T}]}\right)=o(1).

Since the events that we condition on occur with high probability, we conclude

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\rho(C,T)-\sqrt{\frac{m_{C}}{m_{T}}}\right|>\varepsilon\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\rho(C,T)-\sqrt{\frac{m_{C}}{m_{T}}}\right|>\varepsilon\ \middle|\ C\preceq T,m_{T}\leq\sqrt{N\mathbb{E}[m_{T}]}\right)(1-o(1))+o(1)\rightarrow 0.

∎

Proof of Theorem

.

We prove that w.h.p., all intra-community vertex pairs have at least two common neighbors inside their community, so that
all edges in the planted community are contained in
G^{*}
. This additionally implies that the community is connected and has diameter at most
2
.
Theorem

3
additionally guarantees that none of the edges outside the planted community are contained in
G^{*}
. Together, this guarantees exact recovery.

We prove that w.h.p., there is no pair
ij
of vertices in the community with
W_{ij}^{+}<2
.
Conditioned on
T
, we use the Markov inequality to bound the probability that there exists such a pair by
\sum_{i\stackrel{{\scriptstyle T}}{{\sim}}j}\mathbb{P}(W_{ij}^{+}<2)
.
Suppose the community of
i
and
j
has size
s
. Then, the subgraph induced by their community corresponds to an ER graph with
s
vertices and connection probability
p_{n}
.
Since
W_{ij}^{+}\sim\text{Bin}(s-2,p_{n}^{2})
, we can write

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(W_{ij}^{+}<2)

\displaystyle=(1-p_{n}^{2})^{s-2}+(s-2)p_{n}^{2}(1-p_{n}^{2})^{s-3}

\displaystyle=(1-p_{n}^{2}+(s-2)p_{n}^{2})(1-p_{n}^{2})^{s-3}

\displaystyle=(1+(s-3)p_{n}^{2})(1-p_{n}^{2})^{s-3}

\displaystyle\leq(1+sp_{n}^{2})(1-p_{n}^{2})^{s-3}.

Taking the derivative of its log, we see that this quantity is decreasing for

\displaystyle\frac{p_{n}^{2}}{1+sp_{n}^{2}}+\log(1-p_{n}^{2})\leq 0

\displaystyle\Rightarrow

\displaystyle s\geq\frac{1}{-\log(1-p_{n}^{2})}-p_{n}^{-2}=\frac{p_{n}^{2}+\log(1-p_{n}^{2})}{-p_{n}^{2}\log(1-p_{n}^{2})}=\frac{1}{2}+\mathcal{O}(p_{n}^{2}).

This allows us to upper-bound this probability by substituting
s=s_{n}^{(\min)}
.
This yields

\mathbb{P}(\exists i,j:\ i\stackrel{{\scriptstyle T}}{{\sim}}j\wedge W_{ij}^{+}<2)\ \leq\ \mathbb{E}[m_{T}]\cdot(1+s_{n}^{(\min)}p_{n}^{2})(1-p_{n}^{2})^{s_{n}^{(\min)}-3}.

(B.1)

We show that the above requires
s_{n}^{(\min)}p_{n}^{2}\to\infty
.
Suppose
s_{n}^{(\min)}p_{n}^{2}=\mathcal{O}(1)
, then

(1-p_{n}^{2})^{s_{n}^{(\min)}}=e^{-p_{n}^{2}s_{n}^{(\min)}+\mathcal{O}(p_{n}^{4}s_{n}^{(\min)})}=\Omega(1),

since
p_{n}^{4}s_{n}^{(\min)}=\left(p_{n}^{2}s_{n}^{(\min)}\right)^{2}/s_{n}^{(\min)}\to 0
.
This implies that the right-hand-side of (
B.1
) is
\Omega(\mathbb{E}[m_{T}])\to\infty
, which indeed forms a contradiction.
Now, using
s_{n}^{(\min)}p_{n}^{2}\to\infty
, we rewrite the right-hand-side of (

) to

\mathbb{E}[m_{T}]\cdot(1+s_{n}^{(\min)}p_{n}^{2})(1-p_{n}^{2})^{s_{n}^{(\min)}-3}=\mathcal{O}\left(\mathbb{E}[m_{T}]\cdot s_{n}^{(\min)}p_{n}^{2}e^{-p_{n}^{2}s_{n}^{(\min)}}\right).

This tells us that we need

\mathbb{E}[m_{T}]\cdot p_{n}^{2}s_{n}^{(\min)}e^{-p_{n}^{2}s_{n}^{(\min)}}=o(1).

If we take
p_{n}
as given in the theorem, then

p_{n}^{2}s_{n}^{(\min)}=\log\mathbb{E}[m_{T}]+\log\log\mathbb{E}[m_{T}]+\omega,

for some sequence

. Substituting this yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[m_{T}]\cdot p_{n}^{2}s_{n}^{(\min)}e^{-p_{n}^{2}s_{n}^{(\min)}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbb{E}[m_{T}]\cdot\left(\log\mathbb{E}[m_{T}]+\log\log\mathbb{E}[m_{T}]+\omega\right)}{\mathbb{E}[m_{T}]\cdot\log\mathbb{E}[m_{T}]\cdot e^{\omega}}

Proof of Lemma

Proof of
Lemma

.

First, we prove that
\mathbb E[S_{n}]
is bounded. If not, then we can take
s_{n}=\frac{1}{2}\max_{n^{\prime}\leq n}\mathbb E[S_{n^{\prime}}]\to\infty
and take the subsequence
n_{k}
for which
\mathbb E[S_{n}]=\max_{n^{\prime}\leq n}\mathbb E[S_{n^{\prime}}]
, and write

\varepsilon_{n_{k}}(s_{n_{k}})=\frac{\mathbb E[(S_{n_{k}}-1)\mathbb 1\{S_{n_{k}}<s_{n_{k}}\}]}{\mathbb E[S_{n_{k}}-1]}<\frac{s_{n_{k}}-1}{\mathbb{E}[S_{n_{k}}-1]}\to\frac{1}{2},

which contradicts the claim that
\varepsilon_{n}(s_{n})\to 1
. We conclude that

is bounded.
The lemma’s assumption is equivalent to

1-\varepsilon_{n}(s_{n})=\frac{\mathbb E[(S_{n}-1)\mathbb 1\{S_{n}\geq s_{n}\}]}{\mathbb E[S_{n}-1]}\to 0.

A sequence of random variables is uniformly integrable if for every
\varepsilon>0
, there exists some
s<\infty
so that for all
n
,

\mathbb{E}\left[S_{n}\cdot\mathbb 1\{S_{n}\geq s\}\right]\leq\varepsilon.

Again, we prove this claim via contradiction. Suppose there is some
\varepsilon>0

m_{C^{\prime}}^{\geq}

m_{C^{\prime}}^{\geq}\leq m_{T}^{\geq}
and use (
B.3
) to write

\frac{m_{C^{\prime}}^{\geq}}{\mathbb{E}[m_{T}]}\ \xrightarrow{\mathbb{P}}\ 0.

Secondly, the random variables
(m_{C^{\prime}}^{(a)})_{a\in A_{<}}
are independent when conditioned on the true partition
T_{n}
, because
m_{C^{\prime}}^{(a)}
only depends on the edges inside
T_{n}^{(a)}
.
This allows us to apply Hoeffding’s inequality to the conditional probability

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(|m_{C^{\prime}}^{<}-\mathbb{E}[m_{C^{\prime}}^{<}]|>\varepsilon\mathbb{E}[m_{T}]\ \middle|\ T_{n}\right)

\displaystyle\ \leq\ 2\exp\left(-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}\mathbb{E}[m_{T}]^{2}}{\sum_{a\in A_{<}}(m_{T}^{(a)})^{2}}\right),

where we used
0\leq m_{C^{\prime}}^{(a)}\leq m_{T}^{(a)}
for each
a\in A_{<}
.
In this bound, the quantity
\sum_{a\in A_{<}}(m_{T}^{(a)})^{2}
is a function of the random variable
T_{n}
.
Note that the function
x\mapsto e^{-1/x}
is increasing for
x>0
. We can bound

\displaystyle\sum_{a\in A_{<}}(m_{T}^{(a)})^{2}=

\displaystyle\sum_{a\in A_{<}}{|T_{n}^{(a)}|\choose 2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq s_{n}^{2}\sum_{a\in A_{<}}{|T_{n}^{(a)}|\choose 2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle s_{n}^{2}m_{T}

\displaystyle=m_{T}\mathbb{E}[m_{T}]^{2/3}.

This allows us to bound

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(|m_{C^{\prime}}^{<}-\mathbb{E}[m_{C^{\prime}}^{<}]|>\varepsilon\mathbb{E}[m_{T}])

\displaystyle\ =\ \mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{P}\left(|m_{C^{\prime}}^{<}-\mathbb{E}[m_{C^{\prime}}^{<}]|>\varepsilon\mathbb{E}[m_{T}]\ \middle|\ T_{n}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\ \leq\ 2\,\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}\mathbb{E}[m_{T}]^{2}}{\sum_{a\in A_{<}}(m_{T}^{(a)})^{2}}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\ \leq\ 2\,\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(-\varepsilon^{2}\mathbb{E}[m_{T}]^{1/3}\frac{\mathbb{E}[m_{T}]}{m_{T}}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\to 0,

(B.4)

where we used
m_{T}/\mathbb{E}[m_{T}]\ \xrightarrow{\mathbb{P}}\ 1
and
\mathbb{E}[m_{T}]^{1/3}\to\infty
from
Assumption

4
. We have shown

\frac{m_{C^{\prime}}^{<}-\mathbb{E}[m_{C^{\prime}}^{<}]}{\mathbb{E}[m_{T}]}\ \xrightarrow{\mathbb{P}}\ 0.

Putting everything together, the conclude that

\displaystyle\frac{m_{C^{\prime}}}{\mathbb{E}[m_{T}]}-\frac{\mathbb{E}[m_{C^{\prime}}]}{\mathbb{E}[m_{T}]}

\displaystyle\ =\ \frac{m_{C^{\prime}}^{<}-\mathbb{E}[m_{C^{\prime}}^{<}]}{\mathbb{E}[m_{T}]}+\frac{m_{C^{\prime}}^{\geq}}{\mathbb{E}[m_{T}]}-\frac{\mathbb{E}[m_{C^{\prime}}^{\geq}]}{\mathbb{E}[m_{T}]}

\displaystyle\ =\ o{P}(1)+o{P}(1)-o(1).

∎

C.1
Size-bias Distribution

The following two lemmas establish the convergence in distribution of
k\Pi_{a}
and of
k\Pi^{*}
, respectively.

Lemma 28
.

For
\alpha\in[\tfrac{1}{k},1)
, the tail probability of
\Pi_{a}
defined in (
5.1
) is given by

\mathbb{P}(\Pi_{a}>\alpha)\ =\ \left(\frac{1-\alpha}{(k-1)\alpha}\right)^{\tau}\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\frac{1}{k-1}\sum_{b\neq a}e^{X_{b}/\tau}\right)^{-\tau}\right].

(C.1)

Moreover, when
k\gg 1
, we have

k\Pi_{a}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\ \to\ }}\text{Pareto}\left(1-\tfrac{1}{\tau},\tau\right).

Proof of Lemma
28
.

We rewrite

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\Pi_{a}>\alpha)

\displaystyle\ =\ \mathbb{P}\left(\frac{e^{X_{a}/\tau}}{e^{X_{a}/\tau}+\sum_{b\neq a}e^{X_{b}/\tau}}>\alpha\right)

\displaystyle\ =\ \mathbb{P}\left(e^{X_{a}/\tau}>\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}\sum_{b\neq a}e^{X_{b}/\tau}\right)

\displaystyle\ =\ \mathbb{P}\left(X_{a}>\tau\log\left(\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}\right)+\tau\log\left(\sum_{b\neq a}e^{X_{b}/\tau}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\ =\ \mathbb{P}\left(X_{a}>\tau\log\left(\frac{(k-1)\alpha}{1-\alpha}\right)+\tau\log\left(\frac{1}{k-1}\sum_{b\neq a}e^{X_{b}/\tau}\right)\right).

(C.2)

Note that
e^{X/\tau}\geq 1
, so that the second log term is nonnegative. However,
\log\left(\frac{(k-1)\alpha}{1-\alpha}\right)
is negative for
\alpha<\tfrac{1}{k}
. Hence, for
\alpha\geq\tfrac{1}{k}
, we have

\mathbb{P}(\Pi_{a}>\alpha)\ =\ \left(\frac{1-\alpha}{(k-1)\alpha}\right)^{\tau}\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\frac{1}{k-1}\sum_{b\neq a}e^{X_{b}/\tau}\right)^{-\tau}\right],

which proves (
C.1
). For
\alpha<\tfrac{1}{k}
, we condition on the value of
Y_{k}=\tfrac{1}{k-1}\sum_{b\neq a}e^{X_{b}/\tau}
and write

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\Pi_{a}>\alpha\ \middle|\ Y_{k}\right)

\displaystyle\ =\ \min\left\{1,\left(\frac{1-\alpha}{(k-1)\alpha}\right)^{\tau}\cdot Y_{k}^{-\tau}\right\}.

Taking the expectation with respect to
Y_{k}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\Pi_{a}>\alpha)

\displaystyle\ =\ \mathbb{P}\left(Y_{k}<\frac{1-\alpha}{(k-1)\alpha}\right)+\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\frac{1-\alpha}{Y_{k}(k-1)\alpha}\right)^{\tau}\cdot\mathbb{1}\left\{Y_{k}\geq\frac{1-\alpha}{(k-1)\alpha}\right\}\right]

\displaystyle\ =\ \left(\frac{1-\alpha}{(k-1)\alpha}\right)^{\tau}\cdot\mathbb{E}[Y_{k}^{-\tau}]+\mathbb{E}\left[\left(1-\left(\frac{1-\alpha}{Y_{k}(k-1)\alpha}\right)^{\tau}\right)\cdot\mathbb{1}\left\{Y_{k}<\frac{1-\alpha}{(k-1)\alpha}\right\}\right].

(C.3)

By the weak law of large numbers, we have

Y_{k}\ =\ \frac{1}{k-1}\sum_{b\neq a}e^{X_{b}/\tau}\ \xrightarrow{\mathbb{P}}\ \mathbb{E}\left[e^{X_{b}/\tau}\right]\ =\ \frac{1}{1-\frac{1}{\tau}}.

In addition,
f(x)=x^{-\tau}
is bounded for
x\geq 1
, so that the above convergence implies

\mathbb{E}\left[Y_{k}^{-\tau}\right]\ \to\ \left(1-\frac{1}{\tau}\right)^{\tau}.

After substituting
\alpha=z/k
for
z\geq 1,
this already tells us that

\left(\frac{1-z/k}{\frac{k-1}{k}z}\right)^{\tau}\cdot\mathbb{E}[Y_{k}^{-\tau}]\ \to\ \left(\frac{1-\tfrac{1}{\tau}}{z}\right)^{\tau}.

To complete the proof, we need to show that for
1-\tfrac{1}{\tau}<z\leq 1
, the second term in (
C.3
) vanishes. Note that
1-\tfrac{1}{\tau}<z\leq 1
implies

\frac{1-z/k}{(k-1)z/k}\ =\ z^{-1}+\frac{z^{-1}-1}{k-1}\ \to\ z^{-1}<(1-\tfrac{1}{\tau})^{-1},

so that

\mathbb{1}\left\{Y_{k}<\frac{1-z/k}{(k-1)z/k}\right\}\ \xrightarrow{\mathbb{P}}\ 0,

because
Y_{k}\ \xrightarrow{\mathbb{P}}\ (1-\tfrac{1}{\tau})^{-1}>z^{-1}
. This tells us that the second term in (
C.3
) vanishes for
\alpha=z/k
, which completes the proof.
∎

Lemma 29
.

Let
\Pi^{*}
be the proportion of the community of a random vertex
I_{n}
. Then

k\Pi^{*}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\to}}\text{Pareto}\left(1-\tfrac{1}{\tau},\tau-1\right).

Moreover, for
r>\tau-1
and
\beta\in[0,1]
, we have

\mathbb{E}[(\Pi^{*})^{r}\cdot\mathbb{1}\{\Pi^{*}<k^{\beta-1}\}]\ =\ \mathcal{O}(k^{(r+1-\tau)\beta-r}),

while for
r<\tau-1
, we have

\mathbb{E}[(\Pi^{*})^{r}]\ \sim\ k^{-r}\frac{(\tau-1)^{1+r}}{\tau^{r}(\tau-1-r)}.

Proof of Lemma
29
.

For
\alpha>1/k
, we rewrite (
C.1
) to

\mathbb{P}(\Pi_{a}>\alpha)\ =\ c_{k,\tau}\cdot(\tfrac{1}{\alpha}-1)^{\tau},

where

c_{k,\tau}\ =\ \mathbb{E}\left[\left(\sum_{b\neq a}e^{X_{b}/\tau}\right)^{-\tau}\right]\ \sim\ \left(\frac{1-\tfrac{1}{\tau}}{k}\right)^{\tau}.

Taking the derivative w.r.t.
\alpha
yields the density

f(\alpha)\ =\ -\frac{d}{d\alpha}\mathbb{P}(\Pi_{a}>\alpha)\ =\ \frac{c_{k,\tau}\tau}{\alpha^{2}}\left(\frac{1}{\alpha}-1\right)^{\tau-1}\ =\ \frac{c_{k,\tau}\tau(1-\alpha)^{\tau-1}}{\alpha^{\tau+1}}.

To derive the density of
\Pi_{a}
for
(1-\tfrac{1}{\tau})/k<\alpha\leq 1/k
, we need to take the second term in (
C.3
) into account. We rewrite it to

\mathbb{P}(\Pi_{a}>\alpha)=c_{k,\tau}\cdot(\tfrac{1}{\alpha}-1)^{\tau}+\int_{1}^{\frac{1-\alpha}{(k-1)\alpha}}f_{Y_{k}}(y)\cdot\left(1-\left(\frac{1-\alpha}{y(k-1)\alpha}\right)^{\tau}\right)dy,

where
f_{Y_{k}}(y)
is the density of

Y_{k}=\frac{1}{k-1}\sum_{b\neq a}e^{X_{b}/\tau}.

Taking the derivative of the second term w.r.t.
\alpha
, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{d}{d\alpha}\int_{1}^{\frac{1-\alpha}{(k-1)\alpha}}f_{Y_{k}}(y)\cdot\left(1-\left(\frac{1-\alpha}{y(k-1)\alpha}\right)^{\tau}\right)dy

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\frac{d}{d\alpha}\frac{1-\alpha}{(k-1)\alpha}\right)\cdot f_{Y_{k}}\left(\frac{1-\alpha}{(k-1)\alpha}\right)\cdot(1-1)+\int_{1}^{\frac{1-\alpha}{(k-1)\alpha}}f_{Y_{k}}(y)\tau\frac{\left(\tfrac{1}{\alpha}-1\right)^{\tau-1}\alpha^{-2}}{y^{\tau}(k-1)^{\tau}}dy

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\tau(\tfrac{1}{\alpha}-1)^{\tau-1}}{(k-1)^{\tau}\alpha^{2}}\mathbb{E}\left[Y_{k}^{-\tau}\cdot\mathbb{1}\left\{Y_{k}<\frac{1-\alpha}{(k-1)\alpha}\right\}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle o\left(k^{-\tau}\alpha^{-\tau-1}\right),

where the last step follows from

\frac{1-\alpha}{(k-1)\alpha}=\frac{1}{k\alpha}+\frac{\tfrac{1}{k\alpha}-1}{k-1}>\tfrac{1}{k\alpha},

and

\mathbb{E}\left[Y_{k}^{-\tau}\cdot\mathbb{1}\left\{Y_{k}<\frac{1-\alpha}{(k-1)\alpha}\right\}\right]\leq\mathbb{E}\left[Y_{k}^{-\tau}\cdot\mathbb{1}\left\{Y_{k}<\frac{1}{k\alpha}\right\}\right]\to 0,

by the weak law of large numbers.
This tells us that for
(1-\tfrac{1}{\tau})/k<\alpha\leq 1/k
, the density of
\Pi_{a}
is

f(\alpha)=\frac{c_{k,\tau}\tau(1-\alpha)^{\tau-1}}{\alpha^{\tau+1}}+o\left(k^{-\tau}\alpha^{-\tau-1}\right).

Since
\Pi^{*}
is the size-biased distribution of
\Pi_{a}
, its density is given by

\displaystyle f^{*}(\alpha)

\displaystyle\ =\ \frac{\alpha f(\alpha)}{\mathbb{E}[\Pi_{a}]}

\displaystyle\ =\ k\alpha f(\alpha)

\displaystyle\ =\ \frac{kc_{k,\tau}\tau(1-\alpha)^{\tau-1}}{\alpha^{\tau}}+o\left(k^{1-\tau}\alpha^{-\tau}\right).

The density of
k\Pi^{*}
is then

g_{k}(z)\ =\ k^{-1}f^{*}(z/k)\ =\ \frac{k^{\tau}c_{k,\tau}\tau(1-z/k)^{\tau-1}}{z^{\tau}}+o\left(z^{-\tau}\right),

for
z\geq 1-\tfrac{1}{\tau}
. This converges point-wise to

g(z)\ =\ \tau\left(1-\tfrac{1}{\tau}\right)^{\tau}z^{-\tau}.

Then, by Scheffé’s theorem
[
30
]
,
k\Pi^{*}
converges in distribution to a random variable with density
g(z)
. Integrating
g(z)
tells us that the corresponding tail function should be

\mathbb{P}(k\Pi^{*}>z)\to\int_{z}^{\infty}g(y)dy\ =\ \frac{\tau}{\tau-1}\left(1-\tfrac{1}{\tau}\right)^{\tau}z^{1-\tau}\ =\ \left(\frac{1-\tfrac{1}{\tau}}{z}\right)^{\tau-1},

which indeed corresponds to
\text{Pareto}\left(1-\tfrac{1}{\tau},\tau-1\right)
.

We now prove the asymptotics of the moments. For
r<\tau-1
, we use the dominated convergence theorem.
Let
c^{*}=\sup_{k}k^{\tau}c_{k,\tau}\tau
, which is finite since this sequence converges.
We bound

g_{k}(z)\ \leq\ \frac{k^{\tau}c_{k,\tau}\tau}{z^{\tau}}\ \leq\ c^{*}z^{-\tau},

(C.4)

so that

\mathbb{E}[(k\Pi^{*})^{r}]\ \leq\ c^{*}\int_{1-\tfrac{1}{\tau}}^{\infty}z^{r-\tau}dz,

since
r-\tau<-1
.
The dominated convergence theorem then allows us to interchange the limit and integration, so that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[(k\Pi^{*})^{r}]

\displaystyle\ \to\ \int_{1-1/\tau}^{\infty}z^{r}g(z)dz

\displaystyle\ =\ \tau(1-\tfrac{1}{\tau})^{\tau}\int_{1-1/\tau}^{\infty}z^{r-\tau}dz

\displaystyle\ =\ \frac{\tau}{\tau-1-r}(1-\tfrac{1}{\tau})^{\tau}\cdot(1-\tau^{-1})^{1+r-\tau}

\displaystyle\ =\ \frac{(\tau-1)^{1+r}}{\tau^{r}(\tau-1-r)}.

For
r>\tau-1
, we similarly write

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[(k\Pi^{*})^{r}\cdot\mathbb{1}\{k\Pi^{*}<k^{\beta}\}]\ \leq\ c^{*}\int_{1-\tfrac{1}{\tau}}^{k^{\beta}}z^{r-\tau}dz\ =\ \mathcal{O}\left(k^{\beta(r+1-\tau)}\right),

so that indeed
\mathbb{E}[(\Pi^{*})^{r}\cdot\mathbb{1}\{\Pi^{*}<k^{\beta-1}\}]\ =\ \mathcal{O}(k^{\beta(r+1-\tau)-r})
.

∎

C.3
Minimum Community Size in a Power-law Partition

Lemma 30
.

Let
\varepsilon>0
and suppose
T_{n}\sim\text{Powerlaw}(\tau,k_{n},n)
for
\tau>2
and
1\ll k_{n}\leq\tfrac{\varepsilon^{2}}{4}\tfrac{\tau-1}{\tau}\tfrac{n}{\log n}
. Then with high probability, all communities are larger than
(1-\varepsilon)\frac{\tau-1}{\tau}\frac{n}{k_{n}}
. That is,

\mathbb{P}\left(\exists i\in[n]:\ |T_{n}(i)|\leq(1-\varepsilon)\frac{\tau-1}{\tau}\frac{n}{k_{n}}\right)\to 0.

Proof of
Lemma

30
.

We first study the distribution of
\min_{a\in[k]}\{\Pi_{a}\}
.
Note that
X_{a}<X_{b}
implies
\Pi_{a}<\Pi_{b}
.
Hence, the
a
that minimizes
\Pi_{a}
is the one with the minimal
X_{a}
. The minimum among these
k_{n}
exponentially distributed random variables is exponentially distributed with rate
k_{n}
. Given that
a^{*}
is the minimizer, the distribution of
X_{b}-X_{a^{*}}
is exponential with rate
1
for
b\neq a^{*}
. This allows us to write

\displaystyle\Pi_{a^{*}}

\displaystyle\ =\ \frac{e^{X_{a^{*}}/\tau}}{\sum_{a\in[k]}e^{X_{a}/\tau}}\ =\ \left(1+\sum_{a\neq a^{*}}e^{(X_{a}-X_{a^{*}})/\tau}\right)^{-1}.

Then, by the weak law of large numbers,

k_{n}\Pi_{a^{*}}\ =\ \frac{k_{n}}{1+\sum_{a\neq a^{*}}e^{(X_{a}-X_{a^{*}})/\tau}}\ \xrightarrow{\mathbb{P}}\ 1-\frac{1}{\tau}.

Given
\Pi_{a}
, the distribution of the
a
-th community is binomially distributed with
n
trials and success probability
\Pi_{a}
.
The Markov inequality allows us to upper-bound the probability that there is a community smaller than
(1-\varepsilon)\frac{\tau-1}{\tau}\frac{n}{k_{n}}
by the expected number of such small communities.
This yields

\mathbb{P}\left(\exists i\in[n]:\ |T_{n}(i)|\leq(1-\varepsilon)\frac{\tau-1}{\tau}\frac{n}{k_{n}}\right)\ \leq\ \mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{a\in[k_{n}]}\mathbb{P}\left(\text{Bin}(n,\Pi_{a})\ \leq\ (1-\varepsilon)\frac{\tau-1}{\tau}\frac{n}{k_{n}}\right)\right].

Since
\text{Bin}(n,\Pi_{a})
stochastically dominates
\text{Bin}(n,\Pi_{a^{*}})
, the above is upper-bounded by

k_{n}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{P}\left(\text{Bin}(n,\Pi_{a^{*}})\ \leq\ (1-\varepsilon)\frac{\tau-1}{\tau}\frac{n}{k_{n}}\right)\right].

Since
x\mapsto\mathbb{P}\left(\text{Bin}(n,x)\leq(1-\varepsilon)\frac{\tau-1}{\tau}\frac{n}{k_{n}}\right)
is a bounded function, the weak law of large numbers tells us that

\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{P}\left(\text{Bin}(n,\Pi_{a^{*}})\leq(1-\varepsilon)\frac{\tau-1}{\tau}\frac{n}{k_{n}}\right)\right]\ \to\ \mathbb{P}\left(\text{Bin}\left(n,\frac{\tau-1}{k_{n}\tau}\right)\leq(1-\varepsilon)\frac{\tau-1}{\tau}\frac{n}{k_{n}}\right).

The Chernoff bound tells us that for
x,\varepsilon\in(0,1)
it holds that

\mathbb{P}(\text{Bin}(n,x)\leq n\cdot(1-\varepsilon)x)\ \leq\ e^{-n\cdot d_{KL}((1-\varepsilon)x\|x)},

where
d_{KL}(y\|x)
is the Kullback-Leibler divergence and can be lower-bounded by

d_{KL}((1-\varepsilon)x\|x)\ \geq\ \frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{4}x,

for
\varepsilon\in(0,\tfrac{1}{2})
.
Taking these together, we obtain

\mathbb{P}\left(\text{Bin}\left(n,\frac{\tau-1}{k_{n}\tau}\right)\leq(1-\varepsilon)\frac{\tau-1}{\tau}\frac{n}{k_{n}}\right)\ \leq\ \exp\left(-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{4}\frac{\tau-1}{\tau}\frac{n}{k_{n}}\right).

By our assumption on
k_{n}
,

\exp\left(-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{4}\frac{\tau-1}{\tau}\frac{n}{k_{n}}\right)\ \leq\ \frac{1}{n}.

We conclude that

\mathbb{P}\left(\exists i\in[n]:\ |T_{n}(i)|\leq(1-\varepsilon)\frac{\tau-1}{\tau}\frac{n}{k_{n}}\right)\ \leq\ \frac{k_{n}}{n}\ =\ o\left(\frac{1}{\log n}\right).

∎

C.4
Additional Lemmas

Lemma 31
.

Let
\tau>2
and
\max\{\sqrt{n},n^{\frac{1}{\tau-1}}\}\ll k_{n}\leq n
. If
T_{n}\sim\text{Powerlaw}(\tau,k_{n},n)
, then
\mathbb{E}[S_{n}^{2}]=o(n)
.

Proof.

Conditioned on the value of
\Pi^{*}
,
S_{n}-1\sim\text{Bin}(n-1,\Pi^{*})
. Hence,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[S_{n}^{2}\ \middle|\ \Pi^{*}\right]

\displaystyle\ =\ \mathbb{E}\left[1+2(S_{n}-1)+(S_{n}-1)^{2}\ \middle|\ \Pi^{*}\right]

\displaystyle\ =\ 1+2(n-1)\Pi^{*}+(n-1)\Pi^{*}(1-\Pi^{*})+(n-1)^{2}(\Pi^{*})^{2}

\displaystyle\ =\ 1+3(n-1)\Pi^{*}+(n-1)(n-2)(\Pi^{*})^{2}.

Taking the expectation w.r.t.
\Pi^{*}
, we obtain

\mathbb{E}\left[S_{n}^{2}\right]\ =\ 1+3(n-1)\mathbb{E}[\Pi^{*}]+(n-1)(n-2)\mathbb{E}[(\Pi^{*})^{2}].

Lemma

29
tells us that for
\tau>3
,
\mathbb{E}[\Pi^{*}]=\mathcal{O}(k_{n}^{-1})
and
\mathbb{E}[(\Pi^{*})^{2}]=\mathcal{O}(k_{n}^{-2})
. So that we need
k_{n}\gg\sqrt{n}
to ensure
n^{2}k_{n}^{-2}=o(n)
. For
\tau\in(2,3]
,
\mathbb{E}[(\Pi^{*})^{2}]=\mathcal{O}(k_{n}^{1-\tau})
, so that we require
k_{n}\gg n^{\tfrac{1}{\tau-1}}
to ensure
n^{2}k_{n}^{1-\tau}=o(n)
.
∎

Lemma 32
.

If
T_{n}\sim\text{Powerlaw}(\tau,k_{n},n)
for
\tau>2
and
k_{n}\to\infty
, then

\frac{m_{T_{n}}}{\mathbb{E}[m_{T_{n}}]}\ \xrightarrow{\mathbb{P}}\ 1,

and

\mathbb{E}[m_{T_{n}}]\ \sim\ \frac{n^{2}(\tau-1)^{2}}{2k_{n}\tau(\tau-2)}.

Proof.

To avoid overburdening the notations, we write
T
instead of
T_{n}
. First, we compute the expectation as

\mathbb{E}[m_{T}]\ =\ \sum_{i<j}\mathbb{P}(i\stackrel{{\scriptstyle T}}{{\sim}}j).

Now, we write

\mathbb{P}(i\stackrel{{\scriptstyle T}}{{\sim}}j)\ =\ \mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{a\in[k]}\Pi_{a}^{2}\right]\ =\ k_{n}\mathbb{E}[\Pi_{a}^{2}].

Because
\Pi^{*}
is the size-biased version of
\Pi_{a}
, their moments are related by

\mathbb{E}[(\Pi^{*})^{r}]\ =\ \frac{\mathbb{E}[\Pi_{a}^{1+r}]}{\mathbb{E}[\Pi_{a}]}\ =\ k_{n}\mathbb{E}[\Pi_{a}^{1+r}],

so that
k_{n}\mathbb{E}[\Pi_{a}^{2}]=\mathbb{E}[\Pi^{*}]
. Using
Lemma

29
, we obtain

\mathbb{E}[m_{T}]\ =\ {n\choose 2}\mathbb{E}[\Pi^{*}]\ \sim\ \frac{n^{2}}{2}\frac{(\tau-1)^{2}}{k_{n}\tau(\tau-2)}.

To show that
m_{T}/\mathbb{E}[m_{T}]\ \xrightarrow{\mathbb{P}}\ 1
, we distinguish two cases.

The case
\tau>3
.

We write

(m_{T})^{2}\ =\ \left(\sum_{i<j}\mathbb{1}(i\stackrel{{\scriptstyle T}}{{\sim}}j)\right)^{2}.

We distinguish the different products of indicators based on the number of distinct vertices that are involved. There are
{n\choose 2}
terms that involve two vertices (products of indicators with itself),
{n\choose 2}\cdot{n-2\choose 2}
terms with four distinct vertices, and
{n\choose 2}\cdot 2(n-2)
terms that involve three distinct vertices.
By symmetry, this allows us to write

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[m_{T}^{2}]\ =\

\displaystyle{n\choose 2}\mathbb{P}(1\stackrel{{\scriptstyle T}}{{\sim}}2)

\displaystyle+{n\choose 2}\cdot{n-2\choose 2}\mathbb{P}(1\stackrel{{\scriptstyle T}}{{\sim}}2\wedge 3\stackrel{{\scriptstyle T}}{{\sim}}4)

\displaystyle+{n\choose 2}2(n-2)\mathbb{P}(1\stackrel{{\scriptstyle T}}{{\sim}}2\stackrel{{\scriptstyle T}}{{\sim}}3).

(C.6)

To show convergence, we need to show
\mathbb{E}[m_{T}^{2}]\sim\mathbb{E}[m_{T}]^{2}
. The first term of (
C.6
) is
\mathbb{E}[m_{T}]=o(\mathbb{E}[m_{T}]^{2})
.
The third term of (
C.6
) can be computed using
Lemma

29
. We have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(1\stackrel{{\scriptstyle T}}{{\sim}}2\stackrel{{\scriptstyle T}}{{\sim}}3)\ =\ k_{n}\mathbb{E}\left[\Pi_{a}^{3}\right]\ =\ \mathbb{E}\left[(\Pi^{*})^{2}\right]\ =\ \mathcal{O}\left(k_{n}^{-2}\right),

so that this term is also negligible.

The term
\mathbb{P}(1\stackrel{{\scriptstyle T}}{{\sim}}2\wedge 3\stackrel{{\scriptstyle T}}{{\sim}}4)
requires some extra steps. We first write

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(1\stackrel{{\scriptstyle T}}{{\sim}}2\wedge 3\stackrel{{\scriptstyle T}}{{\sim}}4)\ =\ \mathbb{P}(1\stackrel{{\scriptstyle T}}{{\sim}}2\stackrel{{\scriptstyle T}}{{\sim}}3\stackrel{{\scriptstyle T}}{{\sim}}4)+\mathbb{P}(1\stackrel{{\scriptstyle T}}{{\sim}}2\stackrel{{\scriptstyle T}}{{\not\sim}}3\stackrel{{\scriptstyle T}}{{\sim}}4).

(C.7)

The first term of (
C.7
) is equal to
\mathbb{E}[(\Pi^{*})^{3}]=\mathcal{O}(k_{n}^{1-\tau})=o(k_{n}^{-2})
.
To handle the second term of (
C.7
), we sum over all possible labels for the community containing vertices
\{1,2\}
and vertices
\{3,4\}
. We write

\mathbb{P}(1\stackrel{{\scriptstyle T}}{{\sim}}2\stackrel{{\scriptstyle T}}{{\not\sim}}3\stackrel{{\scriptstyle T}}{{\sim}}4)\ =\ \sum_{a\neq b}\mathbb{E}\left[\Pi_{a}^{2}\Pi_{b}^{2}\right]\ =\ k_{n}(k_{n}-1)\mathbb{E}\left[\Pi_{1}^{2}\Pi_{2}^{2}\right].

Using the definitions of
\Pi_{1},\Pi_{2}
, this can be rewritten to

\mathbb{E}\left[\Pi_{1}^{2}\Pi_{2}^{2}\right]\ =\ \mathbb{E}\left[\frac{e^{2X_{1}/\tau}e^{2X_{2}/\tau}}{\left(\sum_{a\in[k_{n}]}e^{X_{a}/\tau}\right)^{4}}\right]\ \sim\ \frac{(\tau-1)^{4}}{k_{n}^{4}\tau^{2}(\tau-2)^{2}},

where we used the strong law of large numbers and
\mathbb{E}[e^{tX_{1}}]=(1-t)^{-1}
. It follows that

\mathbb{P}(1\stackrel{{\scriptstyle T}}{{\sim}}2\wedge 3\stackrel{{\scriptstyle T}}{{\sim}}4)\ \sim\ \frac{(\tau-1)^{4}}{k_{n}^{2}\tau^{2}(\tau-2)^{2}}.

Putting these together, we obtain that for
\tau>3
,

\mathbb{E}[m_{T}^{2}]\ \sim\ \frac{n^{4}}{4}\frac{(\tau-1)^{4}}{k_{n}^{2}\tau^{2}(\tau-2)^{2}}\ \sim\ \mathbb{E}[m_{T}]^{2}.

This implies that
\operatorname{Var}(m_{T}/\mathbb{E}[m_{T}])=o(1)
, so that
m_{T}/\mathbb{E}[m_{T}]\ \xrightarrow{\mathbb{P}}\ 1
for
\tau>3.

The case
2<\tau\leq 3.

We define

m_{T}^{L}\ =\ \sum_{a\in[k_{n}]}{|T_{a}|\choose 2}\cdot\mathbb{1}\left\{\Pi_{a}<k_{n}^{-\tfrac{1}{2}}\right\}.

We use the Markov inequality to show that
m_{T}=m_{T}^{L}
holds with high probability for
\tau\in(2,3]
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(m_{T}^{L}\neq m_{T})

\displaystyle\ =\ \mathbb{P}\left(\exists a\in[k]:\ \Pi_{a}\geq k_{n}^{-\tfrac{1}{2}}\right)

\displaystyle\ \leq\ k_{n}\mathbb{P}\left(\Pi_{a}\geq k_{n}^{-\tfrac{1}{2}}\right)

\displaystyle\ =\ k_{n}\cdot\mathcal{O}\left(\left(k_{n}\cdot k_{n}^{-\tfrac{1}{2}}\right)^{-\tau}\right)

\displaystyle\ =\ \mathcal{O}\left(k_{n}^{1-\tfrac{\tau}{2}}\right)\ \to\ 0,

where we used
Lemma

28
and
\tau>2
.
Additionally, we show that
\mathbb{E}[m_{T}^{L}]\sim\mathbb{E}[m_{T}]
, or equivalently, that
\mathbb{E}[m_{T}-m_{T}^{L}]=o(n^{2}/k_{n})
. We write

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[m_{T}-m_{T}^{L}\right]

\displaystyle\ =\ {n\choose 2}\mathbb{E}\left[\Pi^{*}\cdot\mathbb{1}\left\{\Pi^{*}>k_{n}^{-\tfrac{1}{2}}\right\}\right],

Using the upper bound on the density of
k_{n}\Pi^{*}
from (
C.4
), we obtain

\displaystyle k_{n}^{-1}\mathbb{E}[k_{n}\Pi^{*}\cdot\mathbb{1}\{k_{n}\Pi^{*}>\sqrt{k_{n}}\}]

\displaystyle\ \leq\ \frac{c^{*}}{k_{n}}\int_{\sqrt{k_{n}}}^{\infty}z^{1-\tau}dz\ =\ \mathcal{O}(k_{n}^{(2-\tau)/2-1})\ =\ o(k_{n}^{-1}),

so that indeed
\mathbb{E}[m_{T}^{L}]\sim\mathbb{E}[m_{T}]
.

In the remainder of the proof, we use Chebyshev’s inequality to prove that
m_{T}^{L}/\mathbb{E}[m_{T}^{L}]\ \xrightarrow{\mathbb{P}}\ 1
. Let
\Pi^{*}(i)
denote the
\Pi_{a}
that corresponds to the community that the vertex
i
is assigned to.
Similarly to (
C.6
), we write the second moment of
m_{T}^{L}
as a sum of indicators, and we distinguish the different products as follows

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[(m_{T}^{L})^{2}]\ =\

\displaystyle{n\choose 2}\mathbb{P}\left(1\stackrel{{\scriptstyle T}}{{\sim}}2,\Pi^{*}(1)<k_{n}^{-\tfrac{1}{2}}\right)

\displaystyle+{n\choose 2}\cdot{n-2\choose 2}\mathbb{P}\left(1\stackrel{{\scriptstyle T}}{{\sim}}2\wedge 3\stackrel{{\scriptstyle T}}{{\sim}}4,\Pi^{*}(1)<k_{n}^{-\tfrac{1}{2}},\Pi^{*}(3)<k_{n}^{-\tfrac{1}{2}}\right)

\displaystyle+{n\choose 2}2(n-2)\mathbb{P}\left(1\stackrel{{\scriptstyle T}}{{\sim}}2\stackrel{{\scriptstyle T}}{{\sim}}3,\Pi^{*}(1)<k_{n}^{-\tfrac{1}{2}}\right).

(C.8)

The first term of (
C.8
) is
\mathbb{E}[m_{T}^{L}]=o(\mathbb{E}[m_{T}^{L}]^{2})
. For the third term in (
C.8
), we use
Lemma

29
to compute

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(1\stackrel{{\scriptstyle T}}{{\sim}}2\stackrel{{\scriptstyle T}}{{\sim}}3,\Pi^{*}(1)<k_{n}^{-\tfrac{1}{2}}\right)

\displaystyle\ =\ k_{n}\mathbb{E}\left[\Pi_{a}^{3}\cdot\mathbb{1}\{\Pi_{a}<k_{n}^{-\tfrac{1}{2}}\}\right]

\displaystyle\ =\ \mathbb{E}\left[(\Pi^{*})^{2}\cdot\mathbb{1}\{\Pi^{*}<k_{n}^{-\tfrac{1}{2}}\}\right]

\displaystyle\ =\ \mathcal{O}\left(k_{n}^{(3-\tau)/2-2}\right),

so that the third term of (
C.8
) contributes as
\mathcal{O}(n^{3}k^{(3-\tau)/2-2})=o(n^{4}k^{-2})
.

The second term of (
C.8
) requires extra work. Firstly,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(1\stackrel{{\scriptstyle T}}{{\sim}}2\stackrel{{\scriptstyle T}}{{\sim}}3\stackrel{{\scriptstyle T}}{{\sim}}4,\Pi^{*}(1)<k^{-\tfrac{1}{2}})

\displaystyle\ =\ \mathbb{E}\left[(\Pi^{*})^{3}\cdot\mathbb{1}\{\Pi^{*}<k^{-\tfrac{1}{2}}\}\right]

\displaystyle\ =\ \mathcal{O}\left(k_{n}^{(3+1-\tau)\frac{1}{2}-3}\right)\ =\ \mathcal{O}(k_{n}^{-\frac{\tau}{2}-1})\ =\ o(k_{n}^{-2}),

where we used
Lemma

29
in the last step. Secondly,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(1\stackrel{{\scriptstyle T}}{{\sim}}2\stackrel{{\scriptstyle T}}{{\not\sim}}3\stackrel{{\scriptstyle T}}{{\sim}}4,\Pi^{*}(1)<k_{n}^{-\tfrac{1}{2}},\Pi^{*}(3)<k_{n}^{-\tfrac{1}{2}})

\displaystyle\ =\

\displaystyle k_{n}(k_{n}-1)\mathbb{E}[\Pi_{1}^{2}\Pi_{2}^{2}\cdot\mathbb{1}\{\Pi_{1}<k_{n}^{-\tfrac{1}{2}},\Pi_{2}<k_{n}^{-\tfrac{1}{2}}\}]

\displaystyle\ \leq\

\displaystyle k_{n}(k_{n}-1)\mathbb{E}[\Pi_{1}^{2}\Pi_{2}^{2}]

\displaystyle\ \sim\

\displaystyle\frac{(\tau-1)^{4}}{k_{n}^{2}\tau^{2}(\tau-2)^{2}}.

Putting everything together, we obtain the upper bound

\mathbb{E}[(m_{T}^{L})^{2}]\ \leq\ \mathbb{E}[m_{T}^{L}]^{2}+o(n^{4}k_{n}^{-2}).

Furthermore, Jensen’s inequality tells us that
\mathbb{E}[(m_{T}^{L})^{2}]\geq\mathbb{E}[m_{T}^{L}]^{2}
, which implies

\mathbb{E}[(m_{T}^{L})^{2}]\ \sim\ \mathbb{E}[m_{T}^{L}]^{2},

so that indeed
m_{T}^{L}/\mathbb{E}[m_{T}^{L}]\ \xrightarrow{\mathbb{P}}\ 1
.
In conclusion, for
2<\tau\leq 3
,

\frac{m_{T}}{\mathbb{E}[m_{T}]}\ \stackrel{{\scriptstyle w.h.p.}}{{=}}\ \frac{m_{T}^{L}}{\mathbb{E}[m_{T}]}\ \sim\ \frac{m_{T}^{L}}{\mathbb{E}[m_{T}^{L}]}\ \xrightarrow{\mathbb{P}}\ 1.

∎
