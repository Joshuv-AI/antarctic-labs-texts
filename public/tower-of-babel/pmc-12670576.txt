Oral delivery of chitosan/hyaluronic-acid-coated milk-derived exosomes for ulcerative-colitis amelioration
Authors: Chao Deng, Xinyi Cheng, Qingying Sun, Yeliaman Yeerbolati, Rui Zheng, Youyi Liu, Ying Yao, Yanbing Shen, Kewei Wang, Jianfeng Huang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractRestoring intestinal-barrier homeostasis is crucial in treating ulcerative colitis (UC). Milk-derived exosomes, known for their potent biological properties, hold promise in promoting intestinal-barrier repair. However, their stability and targeting ability during gastrointestinal transit remain challenging. To address this, we engineered exosomes via layer-by-layer (LBL) encapsulation, enhancing their stability, controlled release, and targeted delivery. In a C57BL/6 J mouse model of UC induced by dextran sulfate sodium, oral administration of LBL-encapsulated milk-derived exosomes (LBL-Exos) significantly improved the intestinal barrier, including the physical, mucus, and immune barriers, thereby effectively alleviating the symptoms of UC, even at half the exosome dosage. These effects were also associated with reduced apoptosis and inhibition of the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway. This study demonstrates the therapeutic potential of engineered milk-derived exosomes in UC treatment, offering a promising approach for treating colitis and paving the way for the broader use of natural exosomes in related diseases.Keywords: UC therapy, Exosomes, Layer-by-layer, Intestinal barrier, Anti-inflammatory activity

Introduction

Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) is a chronic, recurrent, gastrointestinal inflammatory disease, exhibiting in two primary forms: ulcerative colitis (UC) and Crohn's disease (CD) (Márquez et al., 2012). UC is characterized by a persistent, confluent inflammatory response, primarily affecting the colon, rectal mucosa, and submucosa. In severe cases, it may progress to total colonic lesions. In terms of the extent of the lesions, UC is typically limited to the mucosal layer of the colon (Maloy and Powrie, 2011). The prevalence of IBD is notably higher in developed countries, with approximately 1.6 million individuals diagnosed annually in the United States alone. The global incidence of IBD is rising at a concerning rate, exerting considerable social and economic pressure on public health systems worldwide (Ratajczak et al., 2023). Additionally, chronic colitis is considered a significant risk factor for colitis related cancers. The development and progression of colitis-associated cancer (CAC) are closely related to macrophage polarization (Chen et al., 2024a).

The precise pathogenic mechanisms of UC remain poorly understood, but it is widely accepted that its development is influenced by a combination of genetic factors, environmental triggers, immune dysregulation, and alterations in the gut microbiota (Segal et al., 2021). Colonic epithelial cells, mucosal barriers, and defects in the epithelial barrier are closely linked to the pathogenesis of UC. In pathological conditions, intestinal homeostasis is disrupted, leading to epithelial barrier damage, immune dysfunction, and microbial imbalances. This disruption creates a vicious cycle, exacerbating the inflammatory process. Key pathological features include thinning of the mucus layer, increase in the reactive‑oxygen species, and loss of tight junction proteins, all of which contribute to increased intestinal permeability. This facilitates the invasion of pathogens and antigens, thereby perpetuating immune-system activation (Jain et al., 2021). Current therapeutic options for UC, such as the administration of 5-aminosalicylic acid or glucocorticoids, the use of biological agents, surgical intervention, and traditional Chinese medicine. Among these, 5-ASA agents, such as Mesalamine and Sulfasalazine, are the most commonly prescribed. They alleviate intestinal inflammation by inhibiting the production of inflammatory mediators and cytokines. Corticosteroids, including Prednisone, Budesonide, and Hydrocortisone, exert potent anti-inflammatory and immunosuppressive effects, enabling rapid control of acute inflammation. For patient's refractory to 5-ASA and corticosteroid therapies, biologic agents, such as Infliximab, Adalimumab, and Ustekinumab, represent a viable alternative. These biologics target specific inflammatory pathways, offering an advanced approach to disease management in treatment-resistant cases. However, these medications primarily focus on symptom alleviation rather than cure and are often associated with potential adverse effects (Gearry and Barclay, 2005; Cheng et al., 2020). Therefore, there is an urgent need to identify safer, more effective, and long-term preventive or therapeutic strategies for UC.

Milk-derived exosomes (M-Exos) are naturally occurring vesicles that have shown the ability to modulate immune response, reduce inflammation, and influence various physiological processes. These exosomes can be absorbed by intestinal cells, where they exert their biological effects (Deatherage and Cookson, 2012; Zhong et al., 2023). Notably, M-Exos are capable of evading immune rejection in different species and are abundant, making them an ideal source of exosomes for therapeutic applications (Zempleni et al., 2019). Studies have demonstrated that M-Exos play a crucial role in promoting cell proliferation and repairing the intestinal barrier in the context of UC. M-Exos exhibit significant therapeutic benefits in UC animal models by alleviating oxidative stress and inflammatory responses, suggest that M-Exos could supply a novel pathway for UC treatment (Li et al., 2024). Specifically, they help reduce LPS (Lipopolysaccharide‌) induced cell apoptosis, stimulate intestinal epithelial-cell proliferation, increase the number of goblet cells, enhance mucin production, and ultimately restore the intestinal barrier and other related physiological functions (Li et al., 2019; Reif et al., 2020). Wolfgang et al. investigated the effect of M-Exos on intestinal inflammation and found that M-Exos exhibited cytoprotective and anti-inflammatory properties in a UC mice model. Their research underscored the potential of M-Exos in preventing the onset and progression of hereditary UC (Stremmel et al., 2020). However, M-Exos are susceptible to partial destruction or degradation by gastrointestinal fluids when administered orally (Gandham et al., 2020; Tong et al., 2023). Furthermore, inadequate targeting of the lesion sites leads to insufficient accumulation of exosomes at the inflammatory site, which can impair their therapeutic effectiveness (Gong et al., 2023).

Engineered exosomes are natural exosomes that have been modified through cell engineering or exosome-engineering techniques to enhance their drug-loading capacity, targeting efficiency, and therapeutic effects (Xi et al., 2010). The release and uptake process of exosomes is influenced by pH. Modified surface engineered exosomes provide higher specificity with low immunogenicity and toxicity for drug delivery to target cells (Mirgh et al., 2024). However, to fully realize the potential of exosome-based therapies, several challenges must be addressed. These challenges include the standardization of isolation techniques and the heterogeneity in source, size, and molecular diversity. Current research focused on refining isolation methodologies, improving scalability, establishing standardized protocols, and enhancing the engineering exosomes to optimize their therapeutic cargo and improve targeting specificity (Mirgh et al., 2024; Dhar et al., 2025). In recent years, numerous studies have explored the engineering M-Exos to enhance their targeting capacity and modulate therapeutic efficacy. This has been achieved using methods such as ligand modification and hydrophobic insertion of small lipid molecules (Wu et al., 2022). One commonly used approach is layer-by-layer (LBL) packaging, which is a versatile, adaptable, and cost-effective technique widely employed in the biomedical field. LBL technology is often used to construct multilayer nanoscale films, aiming to protect the coated substances and enhance their functionality and regulation (Decher, 1997).

To enhance the resilience of M-Exos against the gastrointestinal environment, direct their targeting to the inflammatory sites in the colon, and prolong their retention time within the intestine, we utilized LBL packaging to construct LBL-encapsulated M-Exos (LBL-Exos). These engineered exosomes offer several key advantages, including safety, high biocompatibility, robust biodegradability, and cost-effectiveness. Among the materials used, chitosan (CHS), the only naturally occurring polysaccharide with a positive charge, is commonly employed in oral administration. CHS exhibits unique mucoadhesive properties and excellent biocompatibility (George and Abraham, 2006), making it ideal for coating M-Exos via electrostatic interactions. Additionally, hyaluronic acid (HA), a glycosaminoglycan biopolymer, can specifically bind to the CD44 receptor (Jiang et al., 2008), which is highly expressed at inflammatory sites. This selective targeting allows the self-assembled polymer LBL-Exos to accumulate at the inflamed regions of the colon. In this study, we characterize the LBL-Exos polymer and assess its ability to target and accumulate in the inflammatory region of the colon. We also explore various aspects of intestinal barriers, including mechanical, mucosal, and immune barriers, which provides valuable insights and a theoretical foundation for using engineered milk exosomes in the prevention and treatment of UC.

Materials and methods

Materials

LPS (L6529) extracted from Escherichia coli O55:B5 was procured from Sigma Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). Fetal bovine serum (FBS), penicillin G, streptomycin, and Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium (DMEM) were sourced from Gibco (Carlsbad, CA, USA). Methylthiazolyldiphenyl-tetrazolium bromide (MTT) was obtained from Solarbio (China, ≥98 %). Dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) was acquired from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co. (China, ≥99 %). β-casein (CSN) rabbit monoclonal antibody and lactoferrin (LTF) mouse monoclonal antibody were purchased from Cloud clone (Wuhan, China). LY294002 (inhibitor of PI3K) was purchased from Glpbio (Montclair, USA). Rabbit antibodies anti-TLR4, anti-GAPDH, anti-iNOS, anti-COX2, anti-ZO1, anti-Occludin, and anti-Claudin1, and Cy3-labeled goat anti-rabbit IgG (H + L), were purchased from Beyotime (Shanghai, China). Rabbit anti-phospho-p65, anti-p65, anti-MPO antibodies and HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG, were obtained from Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China). Additionally, rabbit antibodies anti-phospho-IκBα and anti-IκBα were acquired from Abcam (Cambridge, UK), whereas rabbit antibodies anti-phospho-PI3K, anti-PI3K, anti-phospho-Akt, anti-Akt, anti-Bax, and anti-Bcl2 were sourced from Cell Signaling Technology (Massachusetts, USA). Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) stains were obtained from Solarbio (Beijing, China). Alcian Blue & Nuclear Fast Red Staining Kit and Colorimetric TUNEL Apoptosis Assay Kit were obtained from Beyotime (Shanghai, China).

Isolation of milk-derived exosomes

M-Exos were isolated from 720 mL of non-fat pasteurized bovine milk using a combination of centrifugation and isoelectric-point precipitation. The milk was first centrifuged at 13,000g for 30 min at 4 °C to remove fat globules and cellular debris. The pH of the supernatant was adjusted to 4.6 with 2 mol/L hydrochloric acid to precipitate casein. Following this, the supernatant was collected by centrifugation at 10,000g for 1 h at 4 °C, and the residual proteins were filtered out. The resulting supernatant was then ultracentrifuged at 135,000 g for 1 h at 4 °C. The precipitate was washed with sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and resuspended in PBS to obtain the purified M-Exos sample. The protein content was quantified using a bicinchoninic acid (BCA) protein assay kit (Beyotime, China).

Preparation and characterization of LBL-Exos

For LBL preparation, CHS was dissolved in 0.1 % acetic-acid solution to create a 2 mg/mL solution, whereas HA was dissolved in deionized water at the same concentration. Equal volumes of both solutions were combined to achieve final concentrations of 1 mg/mL for each and agitated for 15 min at 4 °C and 500 rpm. The LBL- Exos assembly process involved encapsulating negatively charged M-Exos with positively charged CHS and negatively charged HA. Initially, 1 mg/mL of M-Exos was mixed with an equal volume of cationic CHS (final concentrations of 1 mg/mL) solution and agitated for 15 min at 4 °C and 500 rpm. Excess unbound CHS was removed by centrifugation at 4 °C and 21,000 g for 20 min. The Exos–CHS polymer was then washed, resuspended, filtered, and mixed with an equal volume of HA solution (final concentrations of 1 mg/mL). The final product, Exos–(CHS/HA), was the self-assembled LBL-Exos polymer.

The size distribution of LBL-Exos was determined using a nanoparticle tracking analyzer (NS300, UK) and its morphology was observed via transmission electron microscopy (TEM, JEOL-2100F, Japan). The Fourier transform infrared spectrometer-attenuated total reflectance (FTIR) spectra of CHS, HA, CHS-HA, M-Exo, Exos–CHS and LbL-Exos were recorded via a Nicolet iS10 FT-IR spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) at wavelengths ranging from 1000 to 3500 cm−1. Protein expression profiles for TSG101, CD63, CD81, Calnexin, CSN, and LTF were analyzed by western blotting (WB) at a dilution of 1:1000. For absorbance measurements, CHS was labeled with Rhodamine B (Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., China) and the absorbance was quantified using a microplate reader (Multiskan, Thermo, USA).

Cell culture and treatment

RAW264.7 cells were obtained from the cell bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China) and cultured in DMEM with high glucose (4.5 g/L), 10 % FBS, 0.5 % penicillin G, and 0.5 % streptomycin. The cells were maintained at 37 °C in a humid atmosphere containing 5 % CO2 and subcultured every two days. When the cells reached 70–80 % confluence, they were detached with a scraper, and one-quarter of the suspension was replaced with fresh DMEM. The cells were then redistributed into a T25 flask (NEST, Wuxi, China) by gentle shaking and incubated under the same conditions for further culture. The supernatant of RAW 264.7 cells was collected for subsequent experiments.

Cell-viability evaluation using Methylthiazolyldiphenyl-tetrazolium bromide (MTT) assay

RAW264.7 cells were cultured in 96-well plates at a density of 1 × 104 cells per well, and divided into DMEM control group and LBL-Exos group. The control group was maintained in DMEM, while the LBL-Exos group was treated with varying concentrations of LBL-Exos (10, 20, 40, 50, 80, 100, and 120 μg/mL). After 24 h, 1 % MTT solution was added, and the cells were incubated in the dark for 4 h. The medium was then removed, and 100 μL of DMSO was added to dissolve the formazan crystals. Degradation of LBL-exos in different biological fluids

The LBL-Exos and 0.1 g/L hyaluronidase were incubated at 37 °C with shaking at 100 rpm to assess HA degradation, measured by the zeta potential. Both Exos and LBL-Exos were exposed to simulated gastric fluid (SGF) or simulated intestinal fluid (SIF) (according to Chinese Pharmacopoeia). After 4 h, LBL-Exos were centrifuged, washed with PBS, and the retention rate was determined using a Nanodrop One spectrophotometer (Thermo, USA).

Release experiments in different biological fluids

To evaluate in-vitro release, the LBL-Exos self-assembled polymer was resuspended in 1 mL of PBS or SIF and incubated at 4 or 37 °C, respectively, at a rotation speed of 100 rpm. At set intervals, 10 μL of the release solution was extracted and mixed with 10 μL of the corresponding PBS or SIF, and the concentrations of the released entities were quantified using a Nanodrop One spectrophotometer.

Cell uptake

RAW264.7 macrophages in the logarithmic growth phase were seeded in a laser confocal dish at a density of 2 × 104 cells per well and M-Exos labeled with DiR (Umibio, China) was added for co-culturing at intervals of 24 h, after which the medium was discarded. The cells were washed three times with PBS. Subsequently, 1 mL of 4 % paraformaldehyde solution was added to each dish and maintained at room temperature for 10 min. The cells were then washed three times with PBS, followed by staining with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) at room temperature in the dark for 15 min, and washed again three times with PBS. Finally, 200 μL of PBS were added to the dish, and the cell uptake was observed using a Nikon fluorescence microscope (Ti-U, Nikon Corporation, Japan).

Animal experiments

The experimental animals were all female C57BL/6 J mice (n = 98), aged 6–8 weeks, procured from Jiangsu Jicui Yaokang Biotechnology Co., Ltd. We selected a small sample size because the LBL-Exos was evaluated in vivo for the first time in the present study. The mice were housed in the Experimental Animal Center (specific-pathogen-free conditions, 20–26 °C, 45 ± 10 % humidity and 12 h/light–dark cycle) of Wuxi Medical College at Jiangnan University, which operates under the license number SYXK (SU) 2021–0056. Prior to the initiation of the animal experiments, the protocols were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Jiangnan University (JN. No20230830c1201005[355]). The guidelines for the care and use of laboratory animals in China and the animal management rules of the Ministry of Health of the People's Republic of China were adhered to in the maintenance and utilization of all animals. The individual mouse was considered the experimental unit within the study. No animals were excluded from the experiments.

Establishment of UC model for mice

To establish an acute UC model, mice were administered 3.0 % (w/v) dextran sodium sulfate (DSS) (36–50 kDa; Meilunbio, China) in their drinking water for 7 days, while the control group received normal water. Mice (n = 35) were randomly assigned identification numbers and divided into seven groups: 1) control group (Con), 2) DSS group (DSS), 3) 5-ASA-treated DSS group (5-ASA), 4) Exos-treated DSS group (Exos), 5) LBL-Exos-low-dose–treated DSS group (LBL-Exos L), 6) LBL-Exos-high-dose–treated DSS group (LBL-Exos H), 7) CHS/HA-layer–treated DSS group (LBL). Each group consisted of five mice. The Con and DSS groups received PBS daily, while the 5-ASA group was administered 150 mg/kg of 5-ASA orally, every day. The Exos group received 400 μg of Exos daily, the LBL-Exos (L) group received a daily dose of LBL-Exos containing 200 μg of Exos, and the LBL-Exos (H) group received a daily dose of LBL-Exos containing 400 μg of Exos, matching the Exos group dosage. The LBL group received a daily dose of CHS/HA, consistent with that received by the LBL-Exos (H) group. All groups were provided with standard maintenance feed and treatment at the same time every morning for 7 days, after which the mice were euthanized on the eighth day.

Changes in body weight of the mice were measured daily. The disease activity index (DAI) was scored in accordance with the standard scoring system [8]. Following the euthanasia of the mice, the colon and spleen tissues from each group were collected, measured, analyzed, and subsequently frozen and stored at −80 °C.

Biological compatibility test

Mice (n = 10) were numbered and divided into two groups, with five mice per group, and maintained on standard chow for 7 days. On day 8, the major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys) were harvested and stored at −80 °C for subsequent H&E staining and evaluation.

The groups were as follows: 1) CON group was provided with normal distilled water and gavaged with 200 μL of PBS per day. 2) LBL-Exos group was provided with normal distilled water and gavaged with 200 μL of LBL-Exos resuspended in PBS (containing 400 μg of M-Exos) per day.

Targeting experiments

In vitro cell experiment was used to investigate whether HA could specifically bind to CD44 receptors, which are highly expressed on the surface of inflammatory macrophages. RAW264.7 cells were seeded into a 6-well plate at a density of 3 × 105 cells per well. After LPS (1 μg/mL)-stimulated culturing for 24 h, the inflammatory cell model was established. The cells were collected by centrifugation at 300 g for 5 min, washed twice with PBS, and resuspended in flow cytometry staining buffer (MultiSciences Biotech Co., China). 5 μL of APC-CD44 (MultiSciences Biotech Co., China) were added and intervention to 100 μL cell suspension (with or without Exos, LBL-Exos, or LBL) and incubated for 2 h at room temperature in the dark. 1 mL of flow cytometry staining buffer was added to each tube and centrifuged at 300 g for 5 min. The supernatant was discarded and 500 μL of flow cytometry staining buffer were to resuspend cells. Fluorescent signal was then detected using BD Accuri C6 Plus (Becton, Dickinson and Company, USA).

To evaluate the targeting efficacy of Exos and LBL-Exos in a murine model of colitis, DIR-labeled Exos and LBL-Exos were administered to colitis mice via gavage. After UC-model establishment, the mice (n = 18) were fasted for 24 h, and then orally administered DiR (Umibio, China)-loaded Exos (n = 9) or LBL-Exos (n = 9). The mice were euthanized after 2, 4, and 6 h, and major organs (liver, heart, lungs, spleen, kidneys, and colon) were harvested. Exosome distribution was analyzed using the IVIS Spectrum imaging system (PerkinElmer, Waltham, USA).

Intestinal permeability assay

The grouping details of the mice (n = 35) were consistent with those in Section 2.10. After establishing the UC model, the mice were fasted for 12 h before receiving fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC)-dextran solution. After 4 h, blood samples were collected from the orbits, allowed to stand at room temperature for 1 h, and then centrifuged at 1500 g for 15 min to obtain the serum. The serum samples were added to 96-well plates at 100 μL per well, and the fluorescence intensity of each well was measured using a full-function microplate detector (Synergy H4, Bio Tek, USA) (excitation wavelength 480 nm, emission wavelength 520 nm). The concentration of FITC in the serum was calculated.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

The supernatant from RAW264.7 cells, serum, and colon tissue lysates of mice were analyzed using a commercial ELISA kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) to quantify the inflammatory cytokines IL-6 and TNF-α, following the manufacturer's instructions. Samples were collected by centrifugation at 12,000 g for 15 min at 4 °C (the supernatant of cell or tissue) or 1500 g for 15 min at 4 °C (serum).

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)

The qRT-PCR was used to measure the messenger ribonucleic acid (mRNA) expression levels of inflammatory cytokines (IL-6, TNF-α, iNOS, and COX-2). The total RNA was extracted from RAW264.7 cells or mouse colon tissue lysates using a commercial RNeasy™ Total RNA extraction kit (Beyotime, China) and reverse-transcribed with the Revert Aid First-Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Gene expression was quantified by qRT-PCR with an UltraSYBR mixture (CWBIO, China) in a Roche light cycler 96 real-time system. The expression levels of GAPDH served as a reference, and the data were analyzed by the 2−ΔΔCt method. The primer sequences for each target gene have been presented in Table S1.

Measurement of Nitric Oxide (NO), Malondialdehyde (MDA), and Glutathione (GSH)

Colon tissue was lysed using a tissue total protein lysis buffer, and the homogenates were centrifuged at 12,000 g for 10 min at 4 °C. The supernatants were collected and the protein concentrations were determined using the BCA protein assay kit. Serum was collected by centrifugation at 1500 g for 10 min at 4 °C. The supernatants of the colon tissue lysates and serum were collected for NO measurement using a commercial NO detection kit (Beyotime, China) following the manufacturer's instructions.

The precipitates of the lysed colon tissue were collected by centrifugation at 12,000 g for 10 min at 4 °C, and the protein removal reagent S solution (Beyotime, China) was added at three times the volume of the tissue precipitates. After vortexing thoroughly and incubating at 4 °C for 10 min, the homogenates were centrifuged at 10,000 g for 10 min. The total MDA or GSH in colon tissue were determined by using a commercial MDA assay kit (Beyotime, China) or a commercial total GSH assay kit (Beyotime, China), following the manufacturer's instructions.

Western blotting (WB)

Fresh colon tissue was lysed using radio-immunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) buffer (Beyotime, China) containing 1 mM PMSF (Sangon Biotech, China). After centrifugation at 12,000 g for 15 min, the supernatants were used in protein analysis. The protein concentrations were determined using the Pierce BCA protein assay kit. The proteins were separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS–PAGE) and transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes (Millipore, USA). After blocking the PVDF, the membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies before being incubated with secondary antibodies for 1 h. The protein bands were visualized using a chemiluminescence system (Tanon-5200, China). The antibodies used in the incubation included rabbit anti-ZO1, anti-Occludin, anti-Claudin1, anti-TLR4, anti-p65, anti-phospho-p65, anti-IκBα, anti-phospho-IκBα, anti-PI3K, anti-phospho-PI3K, anti-Akt, anti-phospho-Akt, anti-iNOS, anti-COX-2, anti-GAPDH, and HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG antibodies. Gray-scale analysis was performed using ImageJ software.

Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) staining

Mouse tissues were maintained in 4 % paraformaldehyde (Beyotime, China) for 24 h, followed by dehydration in ethanol, butanol, and xylene, and embedding in paraffin. After embedding, the tissue was placed in a refrigerator for 20 min. The embedded tissue was then sectioned into 4 μm slices, which were stored at 4 °C until further use. For staining, the tissue slices were dried at 60 °C for 1 h, followed by immersion in xylene for 10 min. The slices were then rehydrated using ethanol solutions of decreasing concentrations. After dewaxing, the tissue sections were stained with hematoxylin for 8 min, rinsed in acidic water for 5 s, and washed until a blue color developed. Eosin staining was then performed for 3 min. The slices were dehydrated in ethanol and xylene, and after xylene evaporation, the sections were photographed and processed using an automatic digital-scanning system (Pannoramic MIDI, 3DHISTECH Ltd., Hungary).

Immunofluorescence (IF) staining

Tissue slices were dried, dewaxed, and hydrated, followed by incubation in an antigen-repair solution (Sangon, China). They were then boiled for 10 min and cooled to room temperature. After washing, 100 μL of an endogenous peroxidase and biotin-blocking solution (Sangon, China) were added and incubated for 10 min. Rabbit anti-ZO-1, anti-Occludin, and anti-Claudin1 antibodies (1: 300) were applied, and the tissue sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C. The following day, the slides were warmed for 40 min, washed, and treated with Cy3-labeled goat anti-rabbit IgG antibody for 60 min in the dark. Finally, the slides were incubated with DAPI (Beyotime, China) for 15 min in the absence of light, followed by dehydration, sealing, and air drying, The stained sections were scanned and photographed using a fluorescence microscope (Zeiss Axio Imager2, Carl Zeiss AG, Germany).

Alixin Blue Staining

Tissue slices were dried, dewaxed, and hydrated, and then stained with Alxin blue. 50 μL Alxin Blue staining solution was dripped onto each section for 1 h, after which the staining solution was removed. 50 μL of Nucleus Fast Red staining solution was then applied for 10 min, and then removed. The sections were sequentially soaked in 70 %, 80 %, and 90 % ethanol for 10 s each, followed by soaking in absolute ethanol for 10 s. The slices were then sealed and scanned using the automatic digital-scanning system.,

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

Tissue slices were dried, dewaxed, and rehydrated, followed by antigen retrieval and sealing. 100 μL of rabbit anti-MPO antibody (1:500) was applied to each section, and the tissue was incubated overnight at 4 °C. The following day, the sections were incubated at room temperature for 40 min. After washing, 100 μL of HRP-labeled goat anti-rabbit antibody was applied and incubated in the dark for 1 h. 3,3′-diaminobenzidine (DAB) working solution (Sangon, China) was added, and the sections were developed in the dark for 5–20 min at room temperature (time was adjusted for optimal color development), followed by rinsing with distilled water. The nuclei were counterstained with hematoxylin for 8 min, washed, differentiated with 1 % hydrochloric acid alcohol for 5 s, and rinsed with running water until blue. The sections were dehydrated, sealed, and photographed using the automatic digital-scanning system.

TUNEL Assay

Tissue slices were dried, dewaxed, and rehydrated. 100 μL of protease K (without DNase) was added to each slice and incubated for 15 min. After washing, 100 μL of a potent endogenous peroxidase blocking solution was applied, and the tissue was incubated for 20 min. After washing, 50 μL of biotin solution were added, and the tissue were incubated in the dark at 37 °C for 1 h. The slices were washed again and incubated with 100 μL of a labeled reaction-termination solution for 10 min. After further washing, the sections were incubated with 100 μL of Streptavidin–horseradish peroxidase (HRP) working solution for 30 min. The tissue was then stained with DAB solution and counterstained through nuclear staining. Afterwards, the slices were dehydrated, sealed, and imaged using the automatic digital-scanning system.

Macrophage polarization experiment

RAW264.7 cells were seeded into a 6-well plate at a density of 3 × 105 cells per well. After 24 h of culture, the cells were collected by centrifugation at 300 g for 5 min, washed twice with PBS, and resuspended in a flow cytometry staining buffer (MultiSciences Biotech Co., China). The groups were as follows: 1) Con group, which consisted of cells cultured in DMEM alone for 24 h; 2) LPS group, which included cells cultured in DMEM supplemented with 1 μg/mL LPS for 24 h; 3) Exo group, which included cells cultured in DMEM supplemented with 1 μg/mL LPS and 100 μg/mL M-Exos for 24 h; 4) LBL-Exos group, which included cells cultured in DMEM supplemented with 1 μg/mL LPS and LBL-Exos (containing100 μg/mL M-Exos) for 24 h.

Macrophage M1 was labeled with Anti-Mouse CD86, PE (MultiSciences Biotech Co., China). 5 μL of PE-CD86 was added to 100 μL of the cell suspension and incubated for 15 min at room temperature in the dark. 1 mL flow cytometry staining buffer was added to each tube and centrifuged at 300 g for 5 min. The supernatant was then discarded, and 500 μL of flow cytometry staining buffer was added to resuspend cells. Fluorescent signal was then detected using BD Accuri C6 Plus (Becton, Dickinson and Company, USA).

Macrophage M2 was labeled with APC Anti-Mouse CD206 (MultiSciences Biotech Co., China). 100 μL of Fixation Buffer (Beyotime, China) were added to the cell precipitate, and incubated at room temperature in the dark for 15 min. 1 mL of flow cytometry staining buffer was added to each tube (containing cells) and centrifuged at 300 g for 5 min. The supernatant was then discarded, and 100 μL of cell permeate and 5 μL APC-CD206 were added, and the solution was incubated for 15 min at room temperature in the dark. 1 mL of flow cytometry staining buffer was again added to each tube and centrifuged at 300 g for 5 min. The supernatant was discarded, and 500 μL of flow cytometry staining buffer were added again to resuspend cells. Fluorescent signal was then detected using BD Accuri C6 Plus (Becton, Dickinson and Company, USA).

Statistical analysis

Data were presented as mean ± standard error of the mean. Statistical analysis was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 26.0. For data involving both two factors (group and time) and repeated measures, a Mixed Design Repeated Measures ANOVA was utilized. Multiple comparisons were performed using Tukey's post-hoc test. *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, ****p < 0.0001 were considered statistically significant. The results were visualized using GraphPad Prism version 8.

Results

Preparation and characterization of LBL-Exos

Self-assembled polymers were synthesized using the LBL method, and changes in the particle size during the preparation were monitored using dynamic light scattering (DLS) technology. The results demonstrated that, after LBL encapsulation, the particle-size distribution of the self-assembled polymer ranged from 150 to 190 nm, with a concentration peak at 172.25 nm (Fig. 1A). The particle-size distribution of the polysaccharide polymer (CHS/HA) was approximately 62.80 nm (Fig. S2). Rhodamine B was used to label CHS, and the absorbance of rhodamine B in the solution was detected at 554 nm (Fig. 1B). The absorbance of M-Exos after rhodamine-B–CHS encapsulation was significantly higher than that of untreated M-Exos. However, after the outer layer of Exos–CHS was sealed with HA, the absorbance decreased markedly, suggesting that the HA sealing layer blocked both the reception and emission of fluorescence signals, thus confirming the formation of LBL-Exos, a self-assembled polymer. The charge characteristics of the nanoparticles were measured by zeta potential analysis, which is commonly used to characterize the LBL self-assembly process. Initially, the potential shifted from negative to positive, and then reverted to negative (Fig. 1C). This sequence ultimately resulted in the formation of an LBL-Exos self-assembled polymer with a negative surface charge. Assessment of LBL-Exos properties

HA can be specifically degraded by hyaluronidase, and the degradation of HA in the outer layer of LBL-Exos self-assembled polymers was evaluated by detecting the change in the zeta potential. After the addition of hyaluronidase, a change in the zeta potential of the LBL-Exos self-assembled polymers was observed, shifting from negative to positive over time. After 90 min, the zeta potential reached a positive value, indicating that the negatively charged HA outer layer had degraded, exposing the positively charged CHS layer. In contrast, no change in the zeta potential was observed in the control group without hyaluronidase (Fig. 2A). The stability of LBL-Exos self-assembled polymers was further evaluated using Nanodrop One to measure the retention of M-Exos in SGF and SIF. M-Exos were placed in PBS, SGF, and SIF at 37 °C. After 4 h, over 50 % and approximately 40 % of the M-Exos were lost in SGF and SIF, respectively, with a partial loss also observed in PBS (Fig. 2B). In contrast, the M-Exos encapsulated by LBL demonstrated significantly enhanced protection, compared to the M-Exos alone. The LBL outer layer notably reduced the loss of M-Exos by 25 % to 30 % in SGF and SIF after 4 h and maintained the stability of M-Exos in PBS (Fig. 2C). In the in-vitro release experiments, LBL-Exos self-assembled polymers showed minimal release in PBS at 4 °C, indicating excellent stability (Fig. 2D). At 37 °C, M-Exos were slowly released from the self-assembled polymer, with the release rate in SIF being significantly higher than that in PBS. This suggests that SIF could accelerate the degradation of the LBL outer membrane, leading to the release of M-Exos. The maximum cumulative release reached approximately 75 % at 24 h, with approximately 68 % of the cumulative release retained in the solution at 72 h. In contrast, the release rate and cumulative release of M-Exos in SIF at 4 °C were similar to those observed in PBS at 37 °C. To assess whether M-Exos retained the anti-inflammatory capacity after encapsulation and the regulatory relationship between PI3K/AKT signaling pathway and the expression of downstream inflammatory factors, RAW264.7 macrophages were treated with LPS in the presence or absence of M-Exos released from self-assembled or LY294002 (inhibitors with ability to block PI3K, 50 μM) to evaluate the effect on pro-inflammatory cytokine secretion. The released M-Exos were effectively taken up by RAW264.7 cells (Fig. S5). The results (Fig. 2E–H) demonstrated that LY294002 significantly inhibited the secretion of inflammatory cytokine (IL-6 and TNF-α) and IL-6 and TNF-α transcript levels. The released M-Exos showed similar anti-inflammatory effects compared to that of LY294002, indicating that the encapsulated M-Exos retained their anti-inflammatory activity and PI3K might play a crucial role for the regulation of the expression of downstream inflammatory factors. Additionally, the results from scanning and photography following staining, presented in Fig. S6, showed that the LBL-Exos self-assembled polymers did not induce pathological damage to major organs in mice, indicating good biocompatibility. Additionally, we explored the effect of LBL layer on the polarization of macrophages in cells, and found that M-Exos intervention can effectively regulate the polarization of M1/M2 type of macrophages. LBL-Exos added with CHS and HA layers can further inhibit the polarization of macrophages to M1 type, and promote the transformation of macrophages to M2 type, which shows a stronger regulatory effect than m-Exos intervention alone (Fig. S7). This was consistent with the observation of inhibiting the expression of pro-inflammatory factors in colon tissue. Therefore, LBL-Exos could affect the inflammation by regulating the polarization of macrophages.

Effect of LBL-Exos on DSS-induced Colitis in mice

Compared to the control group, the colon length of mice treated with 3 % DSS was significantly shorter, and both the Exos group and the LBL-Exos groups with different concentrations could ameliorate the colon shortening induced by DSS. Particularly, the high-dose LBL-Exos group could recover to a colon length of 1.2 cm, on an average, which was approximately 20 % of that of the normal mice. However, there was no significant difference in the width of the colon. Notably, the high-dose LBL-Exos group exhibited significantly better results than the positive control 5-ASA group, whereas the LBL polysaccharide group showed no significant ameliorative effect (Fig. 3A–B). A reduction in the colon weight is an indicator of the severity of colon injury (Deng et al., 2017). The experimental data analysis revealed that the colon weight in the DSS group was significantly lower, while the treatment groups, excluding the LBL group, alleviated this reduction (Fig. 3C), showing a trend similar to that observed for the colon length in the different groups. The weight changes for the mice in each group (Fig. 3D) revealed that the mice in the control group exhibited a normal and gradual weight increase, whereas those receiving DSS started losing weight on day 5. On day 7, the average weight of mice in the DSS group had decreased by approximately 15 %, while the mice in the Exos and LBL-Exos groups demonstrated slower rates of weight loss. The average weight of the mice in the high-dose LBL-Exos group had decreased by only approximately 5 % on day 7, showing significantly better weight retention than that of the positive control group.

DAI assessment, based on weight loss, presence of sticky stool, and occult blood in the stool, revealed that DSS feeding significantly increased the DAI scores. In contrast, the treated groups exhibited reduced DAI scores, with the high-dose LBL-Exos group showing significantly better outcomes than the positive control 5-ASA group (Fig. 3E). Because inflammation in the colon increases the burden on the spleen (Zhang et al., 2022), we measured and analyzed the size of the spleen and spleen-to-body weight ratio in each group. The results (Fig. 3F–G) showed that, compared to the control group, the spleen in the DSS group was significantly enlarged. Oral administration of Exos and LBL-Exos effectively reversed this abnormal spleen enlargement, with a better effect than that observed in the positive control group, though no statistical difference was observed. Histological analysis of colon tissue was conducted after H&E staining (Fig. 3H–I). Pathological examination revealed that, compared to the control group, the DSS and LBL groups exhibited abnormal colon tissue structures, disorganized intestinal mucosa, loose submucosa with noticeable edema, damaged or absent glands and goblet cells, and a large infiltration of inflammatory cells, resulting in a high histological score. Destruction of glands and inflammatory-cell infiltration were also observed in the colon tissue of the Exos and low-dose LBL-Exos groups, though the mucosal structures remained relatively intact. However, the histological score was still higher than of the control group. In contrast, the colon structures of the 5-ASA and high-dose LBL-Exos groups were nearly restored to normal, with only slight gland damage; significant reduction in inflammatory cells and a markedly lower histological score were observed, suggesting that LBL-Exos has the potential to restore colon-tissue integrity and alleviate colitis. As shown in Fig. 3J, the concentration of FITC-dextran in the serum of mice in the DSS group was significantly higher than that in the control group. Changes in FITC-dextran concentration in the serum were used as an indicator of changes in paracellular permeability and tight junction function within the colon tissue, to assess the permeability of the intestinal epithelial barrier. Compared to the DSS group, no significant change in the serum FITC concentration was observed in the LBL group. However, compared to the DSS group, the serum FITC concentrations were reduced by approximately 55.9 %, 42.5 %, 55.8 %, and 76.5 % in the 5-ASA, Exos, LBL-Exos-L and LBL-Exos-H groups, respectively. Notably, no significant difference in the serum FITC concentration was observed between the LBL-Exos H group and the control group.

Targeting effect of LBL-Exos

The results showed that the fluorescence intensity of CD44 was increased in the inflammatory cell model and that the fluorescence intensity of CD44 in the cells could also be reduced in the Exos group (Fig. 4A). The fluorescence intensity of CD44 was the lowest in the LBL-Exos group, which indicated that the additive could compete with the antibody to bind CD44, and that the LBL(CHS/HA) coating greatly enhanced the binding ability of the assembly to the CD44 receptor.

LBL-Exos alleviation of DSS-induced disruption of intestinal barrier function

To investigate the effect of the LBL-Exos self-assembled polymer on the intestinal physical barrier in UC mice, the expression of tight junction protein (ZO-1, Occludin, and Claudin-1) in colon tissue was detected using WB. The results (Fig. 5A and Fig. S8) showed that, compared to the blank group, the expression levels of tight junction proteins ZO-1, Occludin, and Claudin-1 in the colon tissue of the DSS group were significantly lower. However, the expression levels of tight junction proteins in the 5-ASA, EXO, LBL-Exos-low-dose, and LBL-Exos-high-dose groups were significantly higher than that of the DSS group. Notably, the expression levels of ZO-1 in the LBL group were significantly higher than that of the DSS group, while no significant differences were observed for f Occludin and Claudin-1 (Fig. S9). These results indicated that high-dose LBL-Exos could improve the expression level of tight junction proteins in mouse colon tissue. Further confirmation of these findings was provided by the immunofluorescence staining of tight junctions (Fig. 5B and Fig. S6), which demonstrated that DSS treatment led to downregulation of ZO-1, Occludin, and Claudin-1 proteins in the colonic tissue, while high-dose LBL-Exos inhibited this DSS-induced decrease in tight junction protein expression. These data suggested that the self-assembled polymer LBL-Exos could enhance the intestinal physical barrier by increasing the levels of tight junction proteins in mouse colon tissue. Additionally, Alixin blue staining demonstrated a significant reduction in the mucin coverage of the colon surface in mice with DSS-induced colitis (Fig. 5C). In contrast, oral administration of 5-ASA, low-dose LBL-Exos, and high-dose LBL-Exos significantly restored the mucosal layer. Furthermore, Exo and LBL treatments also helped alleviate mucin loss to some degree. Collectively, these findings suggest that high-dose LBL-Exos can protect the intestinal mucus barrier in mice with UC.

LBL-Exos inhibition of DSS-induced impairment of immune function

To investigate the secretion of intestinal inflammatory factors, the levels of inflammatory factors in the serum and colon tissue of mice were quantified. As shown in Fig. 6A–I, the levels of IL-6, TNF-α, and NO were significantly elevated in the serum and colon tissue of DSS-treated mice. The EXO group also demonstrated a tendency to reduce the secretion of pro-inflammatory factors; however, this effect was significantly less pronounced compared to that in the LBL-Exos group. To further assess the inflammation, we evaluated the degree of inflammatory-cell infiltration in the colon using immunohistochemistry. The results revealed a marked elevation in the MPO (myeloperoxidase) activity in the DSS-treated mice, whereas the MPO activity in the colon of mice treated with 5-ASA, low-dose LBL-Exos, and high-dose LBL-Exos was significantly reduced. Notably, the reduction in MPO activity was more pronounced in the high-dose LBL-Exos group, compared to that in the EXO group (Fig. 6J). Additionally, to evaluate the impact of the LBL-Exos self-assembled polymer on intestinal oxidative stress, the levels of oxidative-stress–related biomarkers in the colon were analyzed. The high-dose LBL-Exos group exhibited the most significant improvement, although no significant differences were observed in the LBL-treated group. These findings indicated that the LBL-Exos self-assembled polymer protected the integrity of the intestinal barrier by reducing oxidative stress in the intestine.

Discussion

UC is an idiopathic IBD prone to recurrence (Seyed Tabib et al., 2020). Derived from natural foods, M-Exos exhibit potent anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties, presenting a promising avenue for nutritional prevention and treatment for UC. The remarkable structural stability of M-Exos enables them to deliver TNF-α siRNA without modifications. Orally administered milk exosomes loaded with TNF-α siRNA can effectively target the colonic tissue, potently inhibit the expression of TNF-α, and successfully ameliorate colitis symptoms in IBD mouse model (Han et al., 2024). However, HA modification was found to be able to direct drug-loaded milk exosomes to tumor cells via the specific ligand-receptor interaction between HA and CD44 expressed on tumor cell surface, therefore increasing the antitumor efficacy of chemotherapeutic drugs (Li et al., 2020). Our results also showed that the LBL-Exos self-assembled polymer significantly enhanced M-Exos stability in gastric and intestinal conditions. In-vivo imaging results confirmed that LBL-Exos improved the retention of M-Exos in the colon. This indicated that the LBL-Exos self-assembled polymer could diminish the consumption of M-Exos in the gastrointestinal environment with the assistance of CHS and HA, thereby facilitating the aggregation of M-Exos in the colon. In addition, Oral administration can diminish their efficacy because of the loss of targeting specificity. To address this, surface modification of M-Exos with targeting components, such as peptide ligands and endogenous proteins, is essential to enhance their delivery to the inflamed colon (Zhang et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2024b) Colon-targeted drug-delivery systems, especially those utilizing polysaccharide-based materials, have garnered significant interest owing to their superior drug-delivery capabilities (Kotla et al., 2022). Polysaccharide macromolecules can be degraded by enzymes from intestinal flora, possess specific charges, and offer antioxidant properties that regulate intestinal microbiota and repair mucosal damage (Pu et al., 2023). It has been demonstrated that the surface of colon mucosa undergoes a change from negative to positive when diseased, thereby facilitating its combination with negatively charged nanoparticles (Jubeh et al., 2004; Zhang et al., 2020). Therefore, we developed an LBL self-assembled polymer coating for M-Exos using CHS and HA. Characterizations via DLS, ELISA, and TEM confirmed successful polymer coating. WB analysis revealed that LBL-Exos retained the characteristic proteins of exosomes.

In-vitro MTT assays and in-vivo H&E staining demonstrated that LBL-Exos were biocompatible and nontoxic at certain concentrations. Zeta potential analysis showed that HA in the LBL-Exos structure could be degraded by hyaluronidase, ensuring gradual M-Exos release upon reaching the colon. In-vitro co-culture of released M-Exos with LPS-induced RAW264.7 cells demonstrated retained anti-inflammatory activity and were effectively taken up by RAW264.7 cells. In addition, in our previous research, we found that the mesenchymal stem cell-derived exosomes were internalized by both RAW264.7 cells and HT29 cells, transferred to the cytoplasm, and distributed around the nucleus, suggesting that exosomes might be absorbed by macrophages and intestinal epithelial cells at the site of intestinal inflammation (Deng et al., 2023). Upon oral administration, milk-derived exosomes reaching the colon may be cleared through multiple pathways, including uptake and degradation by intestinal epithelial cells, interactions with gut microbiota, clearance by immune cells, and direct excretion. The efficiency of this clearance process is influenced by the physicochemical properties of the exosomes, the colonic microenvironment, and interindividual variability (Yadav et al., 2025).

Our subsequent objective was to examine the therapeutic impact of the agent on mice with UC and its effect on the intestinal barrier. Our results showed that LBL-Exos significantly alleviated DSS-induced colitis in mice, as evidenced by the reduced weight loss, colon-length shortening, and DAI scores. Histopathological examinations revealed a marked improvement in colonic tissue, with reduced inflammation and crypt damage.

It is noteworthy that the M-Exos content in the low-dose LBL-Exos group was only half of that of the Exos group. This discrepancy could be attributed to the partial consumption of unpacked exosomes during their transit to the colon, which could have resulted in a suboptimal therapeutic outcome. Although low-dose LBL-Exos exhibited therapeutic benefits comparable to that of the Exos group, their therapeutic efficacy was enhanced at higher doses, likely due to more effective targeting and retention of exosomes in the colon.

Damage to the intestinal barrier is a critical factor in the pathogenesis of IBD, and its restoration is a key aspect of UC recovery (Di Tommaso et al., 2021; Chelakkot et al., 2018). The physical barrier of the intestinal barrier is primarily composed of tight junctions between intestinal epithelial cells and contain tight junction proteins such as ZO-1, Occludin, and Claudin-1 (Van Itallie et al., 2001). These proteins maintain the integrity of the intestinal barrier and effectively impede the penetration of pathogenic substances (Matar et al., 2024; Macura et al., 2024). Impairments in the functions of these proteins are commonly observed in IBD patients and are associated with disruptions in the intestinal barrier (Gou et al., 2022). In this study, we observed that the LBL-Exos self-assembled polymer effectively restored the severe deficiencies of ZO-1, Occludin, and Claudin-1 in the colon tissues of mice with DSS-induced UC, with superior results compared to both the Exos and 5-ASA groups. Intestinal permeability is another crucial indicator of the integrity of the intestinal barrier (Zhu et al., 2021; De Santis et al., 2015). Our results showed that the LBL-Exos self-assembled polymer significantly reduced the intestinal permeability in UC mice, thereby indicating its effectiveness in repairing the physical barrier. The intestinal mucosal barrier is also a critical component of the intestinal defense system (Segers and De Vos, 2023), and is primarily composed of mucus proteins such as MUC2 (Gustafsson and Johansson, 2022). Mucins, secreted by intestinal epithelial cells, form a protective mucus layer whose integrity reflects the overall health of the intestinal barrier (Yang et al., 2022). Damage to the mucus barrier is known to be a significant factor in the pathogenesis of IBD (Villablanca et al., 2022). In our study, we found a marked reduction in mucin coverage on the colonic surface of DSS-treated mice. However, treatment with 5-ASA, low-dose LBL-Exos, and high-dose LBL-Exos significantly enhanced mucus secretion. Oral administration of M-Exos and LBL also alleviated mucin loss to some extent. These findings suggested that LBL-Exos could protect the intestinal mucus barrier by promoting mucus production. Disruption of the mucus barrier leads to increased intestinal permeability, triggering inflammatory responses that further damage the immune barrier (Tong et al., 2023; La Fata et al., 2018).

To better understand the impact of LBL-Exos on the immune response, we quantified key inflammatory factors in the serum and colon tissues of DSS-treated mice. The results revealed elevated levels of IL-6, TNF-α, and NO in the serum and colon of mice with UC. In contrast, the low- and high-dose LBL-Exos groups exhibited substantial reductions in the secretion and transcription of these inflammatory markers. Disruption of the immune barrier facilitates neutrophil infiltration and triggers a cascade of inflammatory responses, exacerbating disease progression (Masoodi et al., 2012; Uhlig and Powrie, 2018). The colonic inflammatory microenvironment influences the immune environment, promotes tumor development, and contributes to the formation of colorectal cancer by inducing M2 polarization of macrophages (Chen et al., 2024a). LBL-Exos designed in this study could regulate the secretion of inflammatory factors by influencing cell polarization. This could offer a novel preventive approach to prevent colitis from progressing into intestinal cancer. Immunohistochemical analyses of inflammatory-cell infiltration revealed significant increases in the MPO activity in the colon of DSS-treated mice, whereas LBL-Exos–treated groups showed a marked decrease in the MPO activity, further confirming the anti-inflammatory effects of LBL-Exos.

Additionally, the LBL-Exos self-assembled polymer demonstrated a protective role against oxidative stress by reducing MDA levels and increasing GSH expression. These findings further highlighted the potential of LBL-Exos to protect the intestinal barrier from oxidative damage, thereby reducing the immune-barrier disruption induced by DSS. The PI3K/AKT signaling pathway, an important mediator of inflammation, plays a critical role in regulating downstream inflammatory responses. Activation of this pathway exacerbates barrier-function damage (Li et al., 2021). The PI3K/AKT pathway regulates cell apoptosis, proliferation, and survival, promoting anti-apoptotic and anti-inflammatory effects, as well as facilitating tissue repair and wound healing (Chen et al., 2022). The PI3K/AKT pathway can regulate inflammatory response by regulating the balance of pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory cytokines. Under certain conditions, activation of the PI3K pathway can inhibit the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines (such as TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6), and its inhibition mechanism may include inhibiting the activation of transcription factors, regulating the stability of mRNA, or affecting the translation process. Liu studied a DSS-induced colitis model and found that dietary dihydroquercetin inhibited PI3K phosphorylation through the SCFAs/miR-10a-5p/PI3K-Akt axis, significantly reduced the levels of TNF-α and IL-1β in colon tissue, and upregulated the expression of tight junction proteins (Liu et al., 2024). The PI3K/AKT signaling pathway, as a key cell-signaling pathway, can regulate the expression and function of tight junction proteins through various mechanisms, and affect the intestinal-barrier function. Studies have shown that the PI3K/Akt signaling pathway can regulate the integrity and function of tight junctions in various tissues and organs (Zhu et al., 2023). The upregulation of ZO-1, Occludin, and Claudin is related to the repair of intestinal barrier mediated by exosomes, which may be related to the anti-apoptosis effect of PI3K/AKT (Jing et al., 2024). The PI3K/AKT pathway is involved in regulating cell metabolism, proliferation, and secretion, which may indirectly affect mucus production. It can indirectly participate in the process of enhancing the mucus layer of MUC2 by regulating PI3K/AKT to promote cell proliferation (Yang et al., 2025). LBL-Exos modulated the phosphorylation of PI3K and AKT, reducing inflammation in colon tissues. The ratio of anti-apoptotic protein Bcl-2 to pro-apoptotic protein Bax represents a pivotal determinant of apoptotic regulation, with a reduction in the Bcl-2/Bax ratio indicative of apoptosis (Masoud et al., 2022). WB analysis revealed that both the low- and high-dose LBL-Exos groups exhibited an increase in the Bcl-2/Bax ratio, indicating reduced apoptosis and enhanced cell survival. These results suggested that the self-assembling polymer in LBL-Exos could protect the intestinal barrier by modulating the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway, thus mitigating DSS-induced colitis in mice.

In our preliminary investigation, we discovered that LBL-Exos self-assembled polymer could effectively mitigate DSS-induced colitis, highlighting the potential of engineered milk exosomes as a novel therapeutic strategy for UC. In this study, a natural M-Exos encapsulation system was developed using LBL self-assembly technology with CHS and HA. This innovative approach significantly enhanced the gastrointestinal stability and colon targeting of exosomes. It ensured absolute safety by avoiding traditional chemical crosslinking agents and relied on electrostatic interactions to mitigate toxicity risks. This system provided valuable technical support for the development of high-efficiency drug-delivery systems. The CHS/HA double-layer protection notably reduced the degradation of exosomes in gastrointestinal fluids, with a loss rate decrease of 25–30 %, and effectively targeted inflammatory sites through HA-CD44 interactions. This technology demonstrated multifunctional repair capabilities for the intestinal barrier, as LBL-Exos simultaneously enhanced the physical, mucosal, and immune barriers of the intestine. Furthermore, this method could be adapted for the oral delivery of other biodegradable macromolecules such as nucleic acids and protein drugs, making it suitable for the long-term management of gastrointestinal diseases.

Milk-derived exosomes play important roles in nutritional and immune regulation. They protect the intestine from harmful substances, contribute to intestinal mucosal homeostasis, improve the bioavailability of drugs, and have therapeutic potential in diseases such as malnutrition, inflammation, and stress damage. However, continuous intake of milk exosomes may increase the risk of chronic diseases and allergic diseases, including obesity, type 2 diabetes mellitus, osteoporosis, Parkinson's disease, common cancers, allergic rhinitis, allergic asthma, and allergic dermatitis (Yang et al., 2024). Thus, the long-term immune responses induced by continuous oral administration of milk-derived exosomes deserve to be investigated and prioritized. As a naturally derived biomaterial, M-Exos demonstrate high biocompatibility and safety, with no significant toxic side effects reported to date. Their multi-target mechanisms and potential efficacy in intestinal mucosal repair position them as a promising therapeutic strategy for UC. M-Exos can also serve as drug carriers and potentially enhance the oral bioavailability of insoluble drugs, thereby improving therapeutic efficacy and stability. 3-Hydroxylup-12-(13)-ene-17-carboxylic Acid, a Phytoisolates of Swertia alata, exhibits significant anti-inflammatory activity and gastric tolerance (Bajaj et al., 2021). Encapsulation of 3-Hydroxylup-12-(13)-ene-17-carboxylic Acid within M-Exos can enhance its stability and bioavailability, enable targeted delivery, reduce adverse effects, and potentially amplify synergistic interactions, thereby optimizing therapeutic outcomes. In contrast, while 5-ASA is widely used in clinical practice, its mechanism of action is relatively limited, and its efficacy may be suboptimal in certain patients. Additionally, 5-ASA is associated with side effects such as headache, nausea, and diarrhea, and long-term use may lead to renal or hepatic dysfunction. Consequently, exosome-based therapies may emerge as a significant complementary or alternative approach for UC treatment. However, our study has certain limitations. We have yet not conducted an in-depth analysis on the pharmacokinetics of LBL-Exos in-vivo and their impact on gut microbiota. Therefore, future research should investigate the metabolism of LBL-Exos in-vivo and their interactions with the gut microbiota to better understand the long-term effects of this therapeutic approach. Although our study demonstrates the potential of exosomes in inflammation therapy through in vitro experiments and animal models, the findings require further clinical validation. The application of exosomes as drug delivery vehicles and therapeutic targets remains in its early stages, necessitating large-scale prospective clinical trials to evaluate their safety and efficacy，and explore their personalized applications in diverse patient populations (Das et al., 2025). However, translational progress of exosomes-based therapeutics faces persistent hurdles, notably in achieving GMP-compliant large-scale manufacturing, where current isolation methods lack scalability and reproducibility. Emerging techniques such as microfluidics, tangential flow filtration, size-exclusion chromatography, and affinity-based isolation show potential for clinical-grade production, but challenges in batch-to-batch consistency and cost remain unresolved ((Das et al., 2025; Mirgh et al., 2024). At the same time, regulatory classification is still evolving, with exosomes variably considered biologics, advanced therapy medicinal products, or delivery systems, creating uncertainty in defining preclinical requirements and trial pathways (Lener et al., 2015). Immunogenicity also requires careful attention, as membrane proteins, cargo, or residual contaminants may trigger unintended responses (Palakurthi et al., 2024). Addressing these challenges is essential to align laboratory advances with clinical applicability.

Conclusion

In this study, CHS and HA were applied to the outer layer of M-Exos using LBL technology to create an engineered exosome formulation, LBL-Exos, specifically designed for targeted treatment of oral colitis. The LBL-Exos formulation enhanced the stability of M-Exos, facilitated a slow-release mechanism, and improved targeting capabilities, while demonstrating favorable biocompatibility. This approach effectively protected foreign substances from the natural food matrix as they traversed the digestive tract, allowing for targeted delivery to sites of colon inflammation and achieving a sustained-release effect that promoted anti-inflammatory activity. Compared to foreign substances administered without LBL, oral administration of LBL-Exos significantly reduced DSS-induced damage to the colonic barrier, lowered treatment costs, and improved safety profiles. In summary, LBL-Exos improved the high-efficiency tissue-repair capabilities of exosomes following oral administration, offering a novel therapeutic strategy for UC and broadening the potential applications of milk exosomes in related diseases. Future research should advance these preclinical findings through rigorous clinical trials to verify their safety and efficacy of LBL-Exos, thereby advancing their practical use in inflammation therapy.
