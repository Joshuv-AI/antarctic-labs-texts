How chiral vibrations drive molecular rotation
Ivan Pasqua, Gregorio Staffieri, Michele Fabrizio

Abstract
We analyze two simple model planar molecules: an ionic molecule with D 3 D_{3} symmetry and a covalent molecule with D 6 D_{6} symmetry. Both symmetries allow the existence of chiral molecular orbitals and normal modes that are coupled to each other in a Jahn-Teller manner, invariant under U ⁡ ( 1 ) U(1) symmetry with generator a pseudo angular momentum. In the ionic molecule, the chiral mode possesses an electric dipole but lacks physical angular momentum, whereas, in the covalent molecule, the situation is reversed. In spite of that, we show that in both cases the chiral modes can be excited by a circularly polarized light and are subsequently able to induce rotational motion of the entire molecule.

While the ultimate explanation of the EdH effect is the conservation of total angular momentum,
the precise mechanisms that enable the transfer of the angular momentum carried by microscopic internal degrees of freedom to the macroscopic rigid body rotation are considerably more challenging to elucidate. This stems primarily from the fact that commonly employed model Hamiltonians do not explicitly incorporate global degrees of freedom, such as the sample angle of rotation.
Furthermore, the EdH effect caused by the excitations of specific phonon modes encounters an additional challenge. In most cases, these phonons only carry a pseudo angular momentum
[
14
]
, and it remains unclear how the latter can be converted into a physical angular momentum.
It has also been noted
[
15
]
that a faithful description of experiments that detect evidence of phonon magnetic moments necessitates degenerate chiral phonons coupled to degenerate electronic orbitals, thereby realizing a form of Jahn-Teller effect that is in fact associated with the conservation of a pseudo angular momentum distinct from the physical one.

Based on these observations, in this work we aim to clarify these questions by introducing and analyzing two toy models that describe two distinct types of planar molecules: an ionic and a covalent one, exemplified by metal trifluoride MF
3
(M=Al, Sc, Y), and by benzene, C
6
H
6
, respectively. These models possess chiral degenerate normal modes and degenerate molecular orbitals, thereby accurately capturing the physical phenomena discussed above.
The choice of ionic and covalent molecules is motivated by their realization of two distinct types of Jahn-Teller coupling.

In the ionic molecule discussed in Sec.
I
,
the Jahn-Teller effect originates from the dependence on the ion displacement of the crystal field experienced by the electrons, and explicitly involves the global angle of rotation of the molecule, as shown in Sec.
I.2
.
Although this aspect is often overlooked, it is crucial for understanding how the pseudo-angular momentum associated with the Jahn-Teller effect interacts with the physical angular momentum. Through this interaction, as demonstrated in two hypothetical experiments, a circularly polarized light pulse induces the rotation of the entire molecule, as presented in Sec.
I.3
.

Conversely, in the covalent case discussed in Sec.
II
, the Jahn-Teller effect arises from the atomic displacements
affecting the electron covalent bonding, Sec.
II.2
,
and does not directly involve the molecule’s rotation angle, as shown in Sec.
II.3
. However, the chiral normal modes now possess a physical angular momentum, even though, lacking an electric dipole, they cannot directly couple to an electromagnetic field. Nonetheless, through the virtual photoexcitation of an optically active particle-hole pair, that subsequently de-excite via Jahn-Teller coupling, the electromagnetic field can couple to the chiral modes,
making them detectable in the infrared absorption spectrum, as discussed in
sections
II.4

II.5

I.2
Jahn-Teller coupling

The electron molecular orbitals (MOs) can also be classified based on the irreducible representations in Table

I.3
Hypothetical experiments

Here and in Section
II.5
, we explore how a light pulse circularly polarized in the plane of the molecule can induce molecular rotations. We will consider this possibility in a highly idealized setup: an isolated molecule struck by the laser pulse. A more realistic scenario could involve molecules in the gas phase within a cylindrical vessel that can rotate around its axis, and a light pulse with circular polarization perpendicular to that axis. In this case, we must also account for additional complications, such as intermolecular interactions, the interaction between the molecules and the vessel wall, and the thermally randomized orientation of the molecule planes relative to the light polarization.
Although such an experiment can be envisioned, we cannot predict whether the desired resulting rotation of the vessel would be measurable. Therefore, we restrict our analysis to the somewhat unrealistic setup of an isolated molecule.

Although our primary objective is to demonstrate how chiral normal modes can be excited by light and subsequently induce molecular rotation, we begin by presenting a different hypothetical experimental scenario. In this scenario, light is employed to generate an electronic excitation, and we show how, through the Jahn-Teller coupling, this excitation is transformed into a molecule’s rotation.

We assume that the above

\displaystyle i\,\frac{\displaystyle\;d\psi(\mathbf{r},t)\;}{\displaystyle\;dt\;}=H(t)\,\psi(\mathbf{r},t)\,.

We use the standard trick and make a change of variable using the reference frame in which the atoms are at rest, thus

\displaystyle\to\mathbf{r}(t)=\cos\omega t\;\mathbf{r}+\sin\omega t\;\mathbf{r}\times{\bm{z}}\,,

\displaystyle-\omega\,\mathbf{r}(t)\times{\bm{z}}\cdot\mathbf{p}\,\phi(\mathbf{r}(r),t)\,.

\displaystyle i\,\frac{\displaystyle\;\partial\phi(\mathbf{r},t)\;}{\displaystyle\;\partial t\;}

\displaystyle=\Big\{H+\omega\,\mathbf{r}\times{\bm{z}}\cdot\mathbf{p}\Big\}\,\phi(\mathbf{r},t)

\displaystyle=H(\omega)\,\phi(\mathbf{r},t)\,,

\displaystyle=-2\,t_{1}\,\cos\big(k_{\ell}+\Phi\big)-\frac{\displaystyle\;m\omega^{2}\;}{\displaystyle\;2\;}

\displaystyle={\epsilon}_{\ell}(\omega)-\frac{\displaystyle\;m\omega^{2}\;}{\displaystyle\;2\;}\;.

I note that, since
{\bm{A}}(\mathbf{r})\propto\omega
,

\displaystyle\frac{\displaystyle\;\partial H(\omega)\;}{\displaystyle\;\partial\omega\;}

\displaystyle=\frac{\displaystyle\;e\;}{\displaystyle\;\omega mc\;}\,{\bm{A}}(\mathbf{r})\cdot\mathbf{p}\,,

so that

\displaystyle\int d\mathbf{r}\,\psi_{\ell}(\mathbf{r})^{*}\,\bigg(\frac{\displaystyle\;e\;}{\displaystyle\;mc\;}\,{\bm{A}}(\mathbf{r})\cdot\mathbf{p}\bigg)\psi_{\ell}(\mathbf{r})

\displaystyle=\omega\,\int d\mathbf{r}\,\psi_{\ell}(\mathbf{r})^{*}\;\frac{\displaystyle\;\partial H(\omega)\;}{\displaystyle\;\partial\omega\;}\;\psi_{\ell}(\mathbf{r})

\displaystyle=\omega\,\frac{\displaystyle\;\partial\varepsilon_{\ell}(\omega)\;}{\displaystyle\;\partial\omega\;}=\Phi\;\frac{\displaystyle\;\partial{\epsilon}_{\ell}(\omega)\;}{\displaystyle\;\partial\Phi\;}-m\omega^{2}\,,

because also
\Phi\propto\omega
.
The actual value
E_{\ell}(\omega)
of the energy of the state
\psi_{\ell}(r,t)=\text{e}^{-i{\epsilon}_{\ell}(\omega)t}\;\phi_{\ell}(\mathbf{r}(t))
expanded at second order in
\omega

is

\displaystyle E_{\ell}(\omega)=i\,\int d\mathbf{r}\,\psi_{\ell}(r,t)^{*}\;\frac{\displaystyle\;d\psi_{\ell}(r,t)\;}{\displaystyle\;dt\;}

(36)

\displaystyle=\int d\mathbf{r}\,\psi_{\ell}(\mathbf{r})^{*}\,\bigg(\varepsilon_{\ell}(\omega)-\frac{\displaystyle\;e\;}{\displaystyle\;mc\;}\,{\bm{A}}(\mathbf{r})\cdot\mathbf{p}\bigg)\psi_{\ell}(\mathbf{r})

\displaystyle={\epsilon}_{\ell}(\omega)-\frac{\displaystyle\;m\omega^{2}\;}{\displaystyle\;2\;}-\Phi\;\frac{\displaystyle\;\partial{\epsilon}_{\ell}(\omega)\;}{\displaystyle\;\partial\Phi\;}+m\omega^{2}

\displaystyle\simeq{\epsilon}_{\ell}+\frac{\displaystyle\;m\omega^{2}\;}{\displaystyle\;2\;}+\frac{\displaystyle\;3\;}{\displaystyle\;8\;}\,\omega^{2}\,m^{2}\,{\epsilon}_{\ell}\,,

which implies that the moment of inertia of an electron in the wavefunction
\psi_{\ell}(\mathbf{r})
is

\displaystyle I_{\ell}

\displaystyle=m\,\left(1+\frac{\displaystyle\;3\;}{\displaystyle\;4\;}\,m\,{\epsilon}_{\ell}\right)\,.

We note that
\sum_{\sigma}\,\sum_{\ell}\,I_{\ell}=12m
, so that, if all levels are occupied, the electronic contribution to the moment of inertia is the expected one.
On the contrary, in the case with 6 electrons that we consider, the moment of inertia

\displaystyle I_{e}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\sigma}\,\sum_{\ell=-1}^{1}\,I_{\ell}=6m-3t_{1}\,m^{2}\,,

is reduced with respect to the bare value
6m
, a kind of diamagnetic response to
angular rotations.

We can repeat the calculations above assuming that the molecule is pierced by a real magnetic flux and readily find that each electron state carries a magnetic orbital moment
\text{L}_{\ell}
given by

\displaystyle\text{L}_{\ell}

\displaystyle=\mu_{B}\,\sqrt{3\,}\,m\,t_{1}\,\sin\frac{\displaystyle\;\pi\ell\;}{\displaystyle\;3\;}\;.

(37)

II.3
Coupling to the normal modes

We assume that the hopping between sites
n
and
n+m
is a decaying function of their distance

|\mathbf{r}_{n+m}-\mathbf{r}_{n}|
. It follows that, for small displacements
\mathbf{x}_{n}
and
\mathbf{x}_{n+m}
,
m=1,2,3
,

\displaystyle t_{m}\big(|\mathbf{r}_{n+m}-\mathbf{r}_{n}|\big)

\displaystyle\simeq t_{m}\Big\{1-g_{m}c_{m}\,\big(\alpha_{n+m}+\alpha_{n}\big)

(38)

\displaystyle-g_{m}s_{m}\,\big(\beta_{n+1}-\beta_{n}\big)\Big\}

\displaystyle\equiv t_{m}-\delta t_{m}\,,

where
g_{m}
is the coupling constant and

\displaystyle c_{m}

\displaystyle=\cos\frac{\displaystyle\;\pi(3-m)\;}{\displaystyle\;6\;}\;,

\displaystyle s_{m}

\displaystyle=\sin\frac{\displaystyle\;\pi(3-m)\;}{\displaystyle\;6\;}\;.

It follows that the coupling between the electrons and the normal modes is

\displaystyle V_{\text{el-vib}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=1}^{3}\,\delta t_{m}\,\sum_{n\sigma}\,\big(c^{\dagger}_{n+m\sigma}\,c^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{n\sigma}+c^{\dagger}_{n\sigma}\,c^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{n+m\sigma}\big)

(39)

\displaystyle=\frac{\displaystyle\;1\;}{\displaystyle\;\sqrt{6}\;}\sum_{m}\,g_{m}\,t_{m}\,\sum_{\sigma}\,\sum_{\ell_{1}\ell_{2}}\,c^{\dagger}_{\ell_{1}\sigma}\,c^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{\ell_{2}\sigma}

\displaystyle\Big(\text{e}^{-imk_{\ell_{1}}}+\text{e}^{imk_{\ell_{2}}}\Big)

\displaystyle\bigg\{c_{m}\,a_{\ell_{1}-\ell_{2}}\,\Big(\text{e}^{imk_{\ell_{1}-\ell_{2}}}+1\Big)

\displaystyle+s_{m}\,b_{\ell_{1}-\ell_{2}}\,\Big(\text{e}^{imk_{\ell_{1}-\ell_{2}}}-1\Big)\bigg\}\,,

where
\ell_{2}+\ell_{3}
or
\ell-\ell_{2}
are defined modulo 6 with integers between
-2 and 3. It is worth emphasizing that the momentum conservation in (
39
)
simply reflects the invariance of the Hamiltonian with respect to the
D_{3}
point group.

We note that the coupling to the normal modes vanishes identically whenever

\text{e}^{-imk_{\ell_{1}}}+\text{e}^{imk_{\ell_{2}}}=0
, namely, when

\displaystyle m\,\big(\ell_{1}+\ell_{2}\big)

\displaystyle=3(1+2n)\,,

\displaystyle m

\displaystyle=1,2,3\,.

In particular, the normal mode assisted tunneling between the electronic states with
\ell=\pm 1

and those with
\ell=\pm 2
only occurs via the next-nearest neighbor hopping
t_{2}
, an observation
that we use later.

II.4
Coupling to a circularly polarized electromagnetic field

We assume that a circularly polarized electromagnetic field (EMF) is applied to the molecule, with

\displaystyle{\bm{E}}(t)

\displaystyle=E\,\big(\cos\omega t,\sin\omega t\big)\,,

and thus vector potential

\displaystyle{\bm{A}}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{\displaystyle\;cE\;}{\displaystyle\;\omega\;}\,\big(-\sin\omega t,\cos\omega t\big)\,.

In the reference frame of the molecule, which is rotated by
\phi
,

\displaystyle{\bm{A}}(t,\phi)

\displaystyle=\frac{\displaystyle\;cE\;}{\displaystyle\;\omega\;}\,\Big(\sin(\phi-\omega t),\cos(\phi-\omega t)\Big)\,.

The bonds that connect atom
n
with
n+m
are the vectors
\mathbf{R}_{n,m}
,
m=1,2,3
, given by

\displaystyle{\displaystyle\bm{R}}_{n,m}=\mathbf{R}_{n+m}-\mathbf{R}_{n}

(40)

\displaystyle=\bigg(\cos\frac{\displaystyle\;(2n+3+m)\pi\;}{\displaystyle\;6\;}\,,\,\sin\frac{\displaystyle\;(2n+3+m)\pi\;}{\displaystyle\;6\;}\bigg)\,.

It follows that the Peierls phase acquired by the hopping term
c^{\dagger}_{n+m\sigma}\,c^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{n\sigma}
is

\displaystyle\phi_{n,m}

\displaystyle=\frac{\displaystyle\;e\;}{\displaystyle\;c\;}\,{\bm{A}}(t,\phi)\cdot{\bm{R}}_{n,m}

\displaystyle=\frac{\displaystyle\;eE\;}{\displaystyle\;\omega\;}\,\sin\Big((2n+3+m)\pi/6-\phi-\omega t\Big)

\displaystyle=-i\,\frac{\displaystyle\;eE\;}{\displaystyle\;2\omega\;}\,\Big(\text{e}^{i\frac{\pi}{6}(3+m)}\;\text{e}^{i(\phi-\omega t)}\;\text{e}^{in\pi/3}-c.c.\Big)

\displaystyle\equiv Q_{m}\,\text{e}^{i(\phi-\omega t)}\;\text{e}^{in\pi/3}+c.c.\,,

and behaves like the sum of a Bloch wave with momentum
k_{1}
and one with
k_{-1}
.
The first order correction in the EMF to the hopping Hamiltonian thus reads

\displaystyle\delta T

\displaystyle=-i\,\text{e}^{i(\phi-\omega t)}\,\sum_{m}\,Q_{m}\,t_{m}\,\sum_{\sigma}\,\sum_{\ell_{1}\ell_{2}\ell_{3}}\,c^{\dagger}_{\ell_{1}\sigma}\,c^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{\ell_{2}\sigma}

(41)

\displaystyle\bigg(\text{e}^{-ik_{\ell_{1}}m}-\text{e}^{ik_{\ell_{2}}m}\bigg)\Bigg\{\delta_{\ell_{1},\ell_{2}+1}

\displaystyle-\frac{\displaystyle\;g_{m}\;}{\displaystyle\;\sqrt{6}\;}\,c_{m}\,a_{\ell_{1}-\ell_{2}-1}\,\bigg(\text{e}^{ik_{\ell_{1}-\ell_{2}-1}m}+1\bigg)

\displaystyle-\frac{\displaystyle\;g_{m}\;}{\displaystyle\;\sqrt{6}\;}\,s_{m}\,b_{\ell_{1}-\ell_{2}-1}\,\bigg(\text{e}^{ik_{\ell_{1}-\ell_{2}-1}m}-1\bigg)\Bigg\}

\displaystyle+H.c.\,.

As expected, the circularly polarized light can induce on its own electronic excitations with
\Delta\ell=\pm 1
, or with different
\Delta\ell
in cooperation with the molecular vibrations.
We also note from (
41
) that, if the electronic configuration is prepared in a finite current state, i.e., with

\displaystyle J

\displaystyle=\sum_{m}\,t_{m}\,\sum_{\ell\sigma}\,\sin k_{\ell}m\;\langle\,c^{\dagger}_{\ell\sigma}\,c^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{\ell\sigma}\,\rangle\not=0\,,

then the light couples directly to the normal modes with
\ell=\pm 1
, despite the molecule is non-polar and thus the vibrations are not charged. This is not the sole such possibility, as we are going to show.

II.5
Hypothetical experiment

We assume that the molecule contains six electrons and therefore the electronic ground state has occupied

\ell=0,\pm 1
levels. If the EMF frequency
\omega
and the energy of the normal mode 1 with

\ell=\pm 1
are close to each other and much smaller than
{\epsilon}_{\ell=\pm 2}-{\epsilon}_{\ell=\pm 1}\equiv\Delta
, a coupling between the electromagnetic field and the normal mode is generated
by second order perturbation theory
[
16
]
. For simplicity, we only consider in (
41
) the field induced excitations
from the occupied
\ell=\pm 1
to the empty
\ell=\pm 2

without involvement of the molecular vibrations and limited to the dominant nearest neighbor hopping.
In this case,

\displaystyle\delta T

\displaystyle\simeq-\frac{\displaystyle\;eEt_{1}\;}{\displaystyle\;\omega\;}\sum_{\sigma}\Big\{\text{e}^{i(\phi-\omega t)}\;c^{\dagger}_{2\sigma}\,c^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{1\sigma}

(42)

\displaystyle+\text{e}^{-i(\phi-\omega t)}\;c^{\dagger}_{-2\sigma}\,c^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{-1\sigma}\Big\}+H.c.

\displaystyle\equiv\delta T^{+}+\delta T^{-}\,.

Concerning the coupling between electrons and normal modes, we just focus on the mode with
\ell=\pm 1

that is also able to transfer one electron from
\ell=\pm 1
to
\ell=\pm 2
.
As previously discussed, the coupling term (
39
) vanishes
for nearest neighbor hopping. We therefore consider the next-nearest neighbor one, in which case
(
39
) reads, through (
34
),

\displaystyle V_{\text{el-vib}}

\displaystyle\simeq-i\,g_{2}\,t_{2}\sum_{\sigma}\Big\{c^{\dagger}_{2\sigma}\,c^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{1\sigma}\;L^{+}_{1}+c^{\dagger}_{-2\sigma}\,c^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{-1\sigma}\;L^{-}_{1}\Big\}

(43)

\displaystyle+H.c.\equiv V_{\text{el-vib}}^{+}+V_{\text{el-vib}}^{-}\,.

It follows that, if
\ket{n}
are the electronic eigenstates with eigenvalues
E_{n}
, with
\ket{0}
the ground state, then, by second order perturbation theory,

\displaystyle V_{\text{EMF-vib}}=\sum_{n>0}\,\Bigg\{\frac{\displaystyle\;\bra{0}\delta T^{-}\ket{n}\bra{n}V_{\text{el-vib}}^{+}\ket{0}\;}{\displaystyle\;E_{n}-E_{0}\;}

(44)

\displaystyle+\frac{\displaystyle\;\bra{0}V_{\text{el-vib}}^{-}\ket{n}\bra{n}\delta T^{+}\ket{0}\;}{\displaystyle\;E_{n}-E_{0}\;}\Bigg\}

\displaystyle=2i\;\frac{\displaystyle\;eE\;}{\displaystyle\;\omega\;}\;\frac{\displaystyle\;g_{2}\,t_{1}\,t_{2}\;}{\displaystyle\;\Delta\;}\,\bigg\{\text{e}^{-i(\phi-\omega t)}\;L^{+}_{1}+H.c.\bigg\}\,.

In other words, the virtual excitation of a particle-hole pair with
\Delta\ell=\pm 1
mediates
a coupling between the EMF and the chargeless normal modes, rendering the latter observable in the infrared spectrum
[
16
]
.
We also note that (
44

\displaystyle\text{J}

\displaystyle=p_{\phi}+\text{L}_{\text{vib}}=\text{L}+\text{L}_{\text{vib}}\,.

(45)

Therefore, the EMF is able not only to excite the normal modes with
\ell=\pm 1
but also
to change the total angular momentum
L
of the molecule.

Since
\gamma\ll 1
, the angle
\theta_{1}\simeq\pi/4
, thus
L^{+}_{1}\simeq q_{1+1}
and

\displaystyle V_{\text{EMF-vib}}

\displaystyle\simeq 2i\;\frac{\displaystyle\;eE\;}{\displaystyle\;\omega\;}\;\frac{\displaystyle\;g_{2}\,t_{1}\,t_{2}\;}{\displaystyle\;\Delta\;}

(46)

\displaystyle\bigg\{\text{e}^{-i(\phi-\omega t)}\;q_{1+1}-\text{e}^{i(\phi-\omega t)}\;q_{1-1}\bigg\}\,.

We define dimensionless normal mode coordinates through

\displaystyle q_{1\pm 1}

\displaystyle\to\sqrt{K_{1}\;}\;q_{1\pm 1}\,,

\displaystyle p_{1\pm 1}

\displaystyle\to\frac{\displaystyle\;1\;}{\displaystyle\;\;\sqrt{K_{1}\,}\;\;}\;p_{1\pm 1}\,,

The

\displaystyle=i\,\Big(q_{1+1}\,p_{1-1}-q_{1-1}\,p_{1+1}\Big)\,.

We further define

\displaystyle q_{1\,+1}

\displaystyle=\frac{\displaystyle\;1\;}{\displaystyle\;\sqrt{2}\;}\big(a^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{-}+a^{\dagger}_{+}\big)\,,

\displaystyle p_{1\,-1}

\displaystyle\text{L}_{\text{vib}}

\displaystyle=a^{\dagger}_{+}\,a^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{+}-a^{\dagger}_{-}\,a^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{-}=n_{+}-n_{-}\,.

The simplified Hamiltonian

\displaystyle H(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{\displaystyle\;1\;}{\displaystyle\;2I\;}\,\Big(p_{\phi}-n_{+}+n_{-}\Big)^{2}

\displaystyle+\omega_{0}\,\Big(n_{+}+n_{-}+1\Big)

\displaystyle+i\,E(t)\,\gamma\,\Big\{\text{e}^{-i(\phi-\omega t)}\;\big(a^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{-}+a^{\dagger}_{+}\big)

III
Conclusions

We have shown how circularly polarized light can induce angular rotation of molecules that possess coupled doubly degenerate electronic orbitals and (chiral) normal modes that realize an
e\times E
Jahn-Teller effect. This coupling is invariant under
U(1)
rotations with generator a pseudo angular momentum distinct from the physical one. In particular, we have considered two exemplary cases: a
D_{3}
symmetric ionic molecule and a
D_{6}
symmetric covalent one, inspired, respectively, by metal trifluorides and by benzene. The Jahn-Teller effect originates from the dependence of the crystal field on the ion displacements within the ionic molecule, and, in the covalent case, of the electron covalent bonding on the atomic displacements, thereby providing a comprehensive representation of all possible physical realizations.

Although our results are specific to two molecular toy models, certain aspects might be relevant also to bulk systems. For instance, the interaction between circularly polarized light and the chiral normal modes of bulk crystalline samples can be direct in ionic materials or mediated by virtual particle-hole
excitations in covalent systems. In either case, this coupling
depends on the relative angle
\phi
between the sample reference frame and the laboratory frame. Consequently, the light can drive the sample into rotation, inducing a finite angular velocity

\dot{\phi}
.

Furthermore, if the chiral modes independently contribute to the physical angular momentum, as observed in the benzene molecule, their coherent excitation, regardless of its realization
[
10
,
11
,
12
,
13
]
, might yield comparable effects to circularly polarized light.

Appendix A
Derivation of the Hamiltonian

In this Appendix, we derive the expression for the atomic Hamiltonian of a planar molecule with a regular
N
-sided polygon shape and equivalent atoms of mass
M
at its vertices.
As in the main text, we disregard out-of-plane displacements. Consequently, the point group of the molecule is the two-dimensional
D_{N}
. We also neglect the motion of the center of mass, which coincides with the center of the polygon, but we account for the molecule’s rotations around this point. Our objective is to separate the global molecule rotation from all internal degrees of freedom, which is straightforward in this regular geometry.

The atoms at the vertices are at position
\mathbf{r}_{n}
,
n=0,\dots,N-1
, which we parametrize as

\displaystyle\mathbf{r}_{n}=\mathbf{R}_{n}+\mathbf{x}_{n}=\big(1+\alpha_{n}\big)\,\mathbf{R}_{n}+\beta_{n}\,{\bm{z}}\wedge\mathbf{R}_{n}\,,

(50)

where
\mathbf{R}_{n}
are the equilibrium positions,
{\bm{z}}
the unit vector perpendicular to the molecule plane, and
\mathbf{x}_{n}
the displacements. By symmetry,

\displaystyle\mathbf{R}_{n}

\displaystyle=C_{n2\pi/N}\big(\mathbf{R}_{0}\big)\,,

\displaystyle n

\displaystyle=0,\dots,N-1\,,

where
C_{\theta}
is a rotation by an angle
\theta
around the centre of the molecule.
Hereafter, we set
|\mathbf{R}_{0}|=1
our unit of length.

We denote by
\phi
the dynamical rotation angle of the molecule with respect to a fixed reference frame. Consistently, we must exclude the displacements that also
correspond to a global molecule rotation. With the parametrization (
50
), this
amounts to impose that

\sum_{n}\,\beta_{n}=\sum_{n}\,\mathbf{x}_{n}\cdot{\bm{z}}\wedge\mathbf{R}_{n}=\sum_{n}\,\mathbf{r}_{n}\cdot{\bm{z}}\wedge\mathbf{R}_{n}\equiv 0\,,

(51)

which implies that also the time derivatives vanish. To enforce (
51
), we
make the unitary transformation

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\alpha_{n}\\
\beta_{n}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\frac{\displaystyle\;1\;}{\displaystyle\;\sqrt{N}\;}\sum_{\ell}\,\text{e}^{ik_{\ell}n}\;\begin{pmatrix}a_{\ell}\\
b_{\ell}\end{pmatrix}

(52)

\displaystyle=\frac{\displaystyle\;1\;}{\displaystyle\;\sqrt{N}\;}\sum_{\ell}\,\text{e}^{ik_{\ell}n}\;\mathbf{q}_{\ell}\,,

where
k_{\ell}=2\pi\ell/N
,
\ell=-\lceil N/2\rceil+1,\dots,\lfloor N/2\rfloor

are the wave vectors corresponding to an
N
-site
chain with periodic boundary conditions, and
\mathbf{q}_{\ell}^{\phantom{\dagger}}=\mathbf{q}_{-\ell}^{*}
.
In this representation, the constraint (
51
) simply implies that

b_{0}\equiv 0
. Therefore,
\mathbf{q}_{0}
has only one component
a_{0}
. Another advantage of (
52
) is that
\mathbf{q}_{\ell}

transform automatically as the irreducible representations of the point group.
For instance,
a_{0}
represents the
A_{1}
breathing mode, and so we define
a_{0}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}\mathrel{\mkern-1.2mu}=q_{A_{1}}
.

With respect to the fixed reference frame, the atomic positions read

\displaystyle\mathbf{r}_{n}(\phi)

\displaystyle=C_{\phi}\big(\mathbf{r}_{n}\big)=\cos\phi\,\mathbf{r}_{n}+\sin\phi\,{\bm{z}}\wedge\mathbf{r}_{n}\,,

thus

\dot{\mathbf{r}}_{n}(\phi)=C_{\phi}\big(\dot{\mathbf{r}}_{n}+\dot{\phi}\,{\bm{z}}\wedge\mathbf{r}_{n}\big)=C_{\phi}\big(\dot{\mathbf{x}}_{n}+\dot{\phi}\,{\bm{z}}\wedge\mathbf{r}_{n}\big)\,.

(53)

Therefore, taking the constraint (
51
) into account and using (
52
), the angular momentum
L
has the expression

\displaystyle\text{L}

\displaystyle=M\,\sum_{n}\,\mathbf{r}_{n}(\phi)\wedge\dot{\mathbf{r}}_{n}(\phi)\cdot{\bm{z}}

(54)

\displaystyle=M\,\sum_{n}\,\mathbf{r}_{n}\wedge\big(\dot{\mathbf{r}}_{n}+\dot{\phi}\,{\bm{z}}\wedge\mathbf{r}_{n}\big)\cdot{\bm{z}}

\displaystyle=\dot{\phi}\,M\,\sum_{n}\,\text{r}_{n}^{2}+M\,\sum_{n}\,\mathbf{x}_{n}\wedge\dot{\mathbf{x}}_{n}\cdot{\bm{z}}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{:}}\mathrel{\mkern-1.2mu}=\dot{\phi}\,I_{0}+M\,\sum_{\ell\not=0}\,\mathbf{q}_{\ell}\wedge\dot{\mathbf{q}}_{-\ell}\cdot{\bm{z}}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}\mathrel{\mkern-1.2mu}=\dot{\phi}\,I_{0}+J\,,

where we remark that
\ell=N/2\equiv-N/2
for even
N
, and we can write

\displaystyle J

\displaystyle=M\,\sum_{\ell\not=0}\,\mathbf{q}_{\ell}\wedge\dot{\mathbf{q}}_{-\ell}\cdot{\bm{z}}=M\,\sum_{\ell\not=0}\,\dot{\mathbf{q}}_{\ell}\cdot{\bm{z}}\wedge\mathbf{q}_{-\ell}\,,

\displaystyle I_{0}

\displaystyle=M\,\big(\sqrt{N}+q_{A_{1}}\big)^{2}+M\,\,\sum_{\ell\not=0}\,\mathbf{q}_{\ell}\cdot\mathbf{q}_{-\ell}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{:}}\mathrel{\mkern-1.2mu}=I+M\,\,\sum_{\ell\not=0}\,\mathbf{q}_{\ell}\cdot\mathbf{q}_{-\ell}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}\mathrel{\mkern-1.2mu}=I+\delta I\,,

with
I
the total moment of inertia.
Similarly, the kinetic energy reads

\displaystyle T

\displaystyle=\frac{\displaystyle\;M\;}{\displaystyle\;2\;}\,\sum_{n}\,\dot{\mathbf{r}}_{n}(\phi)\cdot\dot{\mathbf{r}}_{n}(\phi)

(55)

\displaystyle=\frac{\displaystyle\;M\;}{\displaystyle\;2\;}\,\sum_{n}\,\big(\dot{\mathbf{x}}_{n}+\dot{\phi}\,{\bm{z}}\wedge\mathbf{r}_{n}\big)\cdot\big(\dot{\mathbf{x}}_{n}+\dot{\phi}\,{\bm{z}}\wedge\mathbf{r}_{n}\big)

\displaystyle=\frac{\displaystyle\;I_{0}\;}{\displaystyle\;2\;}\,\dot{\phi}^{2}+J\,\dot{\phi}+\frac{\displaystyle\;M\;}{\displaystyle\;2\;}\,\dot{q}_{A_{1}}^{2}+\frac{\displaystyle\;M\;}{\displaystyle\;2\;}\sum_{\ell\not=0}\,\dot{\mathbf{q}}_{\ell}\cdot\dot{\mathbf{q}}_{-\ell}\,.

By definition, the variables
p_{\phi}
,
p_{A_{1}}
and
\mathbf{p}_{-\ell}
conjugate to

\phi
,
q_{A_{1}}
and
\mathbf{q}_{\ell}
, respectively, are obtained through

\displaystyle p_{\phi}=\frac{\displaystyle\;\partial T\;}{\displaystyle\;\partial\dot{\phi}\;}=\dot{\phi}\,I_{0}+J\equiv\text{L}\,,

(56)

\displaystyle p_{A_{1}}=\frac{\displaystyle\;\partial T\;}{\displaystyle\;\partial\dot{q}_{A_{1}}\;}=M\,\dot{q}_{A_{1}}\,,

\displaystyle\mathbf{p}_{-\ell}=\frac{\displaystyle\;\partial T\;}{\displaystyle\;\partial\dot{\mathbf{q}}_{\ell}\;}=M\,\dot{\mathbf{q}}_{-\ell}+\dot{\phi}\;\frac{\displaystyle\;\partial J\;}{\displaystyle\;\partial\dot{\mathbf{q}}_{\ell}\;}\;.

Conversely,

\displaystyle\dot{\phi}=\frac{\displaystyle\;p_{\phi}-J\;}{\displaystyle\;I_{0}\;}\;,\qquad\dot{q}_{A_{1}}=\frac{\displaystyle\;p_{A_{1}}\;}{\displaystyle\;M\;}\;,

\displaystyle\dot{\mathbf{q}}_{\ell}=\frac{\displaystyle\;1\;}{\displaystyle\;M\;}\,\bigg(\mathbf{p}_{\ell}-\dot{\phi}\;\frac{\displaystyle\;\partial J\;}{\displaystyle\;\partial\dot{\mathbf{q}}_{-\ell}\;}\bigg)\,.

It follows that

\displaystyle J

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell\not=0}\,\dot{\mathbf{q}}_{\ell}\cdot\frac{\displaystyle\;\partial J\;}{\displaystyle\;\partial\dot{\mathbf{q}}_{\ell}\;}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell\not=0}\,\mathbf{q}_{\ell}\wedge\mathbf{p}_{-\ell}\cdot{\bm{z}}-\frac{\displaystyle\;\dot{\phi}\;}{\displaystyle\;M\;}\,\sum_{\ell\not=0}\,\frac{\displaystyle\;\partial J\;}{\displaystyle\;\partial\dot{\mathbf{q}}_{\ell}\;}\cdot\frac{\displaystyle\;\partial J\;}{\displaystyle\;\partial\dot{\mathbf{q}}_{-\ell}\;}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{:}}\mathrel{\mkern-1.2mu}=\text{L}_{\text{vib}}-\dot{\phi}\,M\,\sum_{\ell\not=0}\,\mathbf{q}_{\ell}\cdot\mathbf{q}_{-\ell}=\text{L}_{\text{vib}}-\dot{\phi}\,\delta I

\displaystyle=\text{L}_{\text{vib}}-\frac{\displaystyle\;\delta I\;}{\displaystyle\;I_{0}\;}\;\big(p_{\phi}-J\big)\,,

namely,

\displaystyle J

\displaystyle=\frac{\displaystyle\;\;I_{0}\,\text{L}_{\text{vib}}-\delta I\,p_{\phi}\;\;}{\displaystyle\;I\;}\;,

and thus

\displaystyle\dot{\phi}

\displaystyle=\frac{\displaystyle\;\;p_{\phi}-\text{L}_{\text{vib}}\;\;}{\displaystyle\;I\;}\;.

(57)

Therefore, if
V\big(\{\mathbf{r}_{n}\}\big)
is the inter atomic potential, minimum at

\mathbf{r}_{n}=\mathbf{R}_{n}
and independent of
\phi
