ERO1A promotes the proliferation, migration and invasion of bladder cancer through ALOX5 mediated activation of JAK-STAT signaling pathway
Authors: Jian Huang, Da Chen, Guanghai Ji, Jun Ma, Zhiwei Zhao, Hao Deng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEndoplasmic reticulum oxidoreductase 1 A (ERO1A) is a protein disulfide oxidoreductase. ERO1A is involved in the progression of various tumors, but the potential mechanism of its action in bladder cancer remains unclear.MethodsIn this study, we utilized data from public databases to investigate the expression pattern of ERO1A. Through CCK-8, EdU, wound healing, and Transwell invasion assays, we evaluated the effects of ERO1A on the proliferation, migration, and invasion abilities of bladder cancer cells. We further explored the specific molecular mechanism of ERO1A. Finally, a subcutaneous tumor formation model in nude mice was established to detect the function of ERO1A in vivo.ResultsSilencing the expression of ERO1A inhibits the proliferation, migration and invasion of bladder cancer cells, and also suppresses tumor growth in vivo. Mechanistically, ERO1A binds to and suppresses the phosphorylation of ALOX5, which in turn activates the JAK-STAT signaling pathway and subsequently suppresses autophagy.ConclusionIn conclusion, this study demonstrates that ERO1A could promote the proliferation, migration, and invasion of bladder cancer cells by regulating the ALOX5/JAK/STAT/autophagy signaling axis. ERO1A may serve as a novel molecular marker for the diagnosis and treatment of clinical bladder cancer patients.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-025-07613-w.

Introduction

Bladder cancer ranks as the ninth most common malignant tumors worldwide, with approximately 573,000 new cases and 200,000 deaths annually [1, 2]. Clinically, it is primarily classified into non-muscle-invasive bladder cancer (NMIBC) and muscle-invasive bladder cancer (MIBC). More than 90% of bladder cancer cases are histologically classified as urothelial carcinoma [3–6]. Transurethral resection of bladder tumor (TURBT) remains the primary surgical treatment for NMIBC [7, 8]. However, patients post-TURBT face a high recurrence risk, with > 50% relapsing within 1 year and 90% within 5 years [9]. Thus, investigating the pathogenesis of bladder cancer and identifying novel molecular targets is of great significance for the diagnosis and treatment of bladder cancer patients.

Endoplasmic reticulum oxidoreductase 1 (ERO1) is a flavin adenine dinucleotide (FAD)-containing enzyme located in the endoplasmic reticulum [10]. Its primary function is to maintain proper protein folding by catalyzing disulfide bond formation [11]. During its functional operation, ERO1A catalyzes the formation of disulfide bonds by oxidizing and reactivating protein disulfide isomerase (PDI), which produces reactive oxygen species (ROS) [10, 12]. Current research shows that ERO1A is upregulated in various cancer cell lines and cancer tissues (e.g., lung adenocarcinoma, pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma, and colorectal cancer) and is significantly associated with poor prognosis [13–18]. Additionally, ERO1A overexpression has been shown to markedly promote the malignant migration, invasion, and metastasis of tumor cells [19]. Depleting ERO1A from tumor cells can disrupt the endoplasmic reticulum stress response, leading to immunogenic cell death in cancer cells and amelioration of the tumor immune microenvironment, thereby synergistically enhancing the efficacy of immunotherapy [20]. In invasive triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC), inhibiting ERO1A expression can suppress tumor progression by downregulating VEGF and PD-L1 in the tumor microenvironment [16]. ERO1A is also an adverse prognostic indicator in epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR)-mutant non–small cell lung cancer (EGFRmut-NSCLC) [17]. Therefore, ERO1A is considered a novel diagnostic biomarker and therapeutic target for tumors. However, the role and mechanism of ERO1A in bladder cancer remain unclear, highlighting the need for more investigations.

In this study, we observed that ERO1A was upregulated in bladder cancer tissues and cells. Silencing ERO1A could inhibit the proliferation, migration, and invasion capabilities of bladder cancer cells; conversely, overexpressing ERO1A yielded the opposite effect. The findings demonstrated that ERO1A could regulate the JAK-STAT signaling pathway by suppressing ALOX5 phosphorylation, thereby inhibiting autophagy. In summary, we clarified the role of ERO1A in the progression of bladder cancer and its underlying mechanism, highlighting the key regulatory roles of ERO1A/ALOX5/JAK/STAT/autophagy in the progression and cellular signal transduction of bladder cancer.

Materials and methods

Data acquisition

The differential expression of the ERO1A gene in tumor tissues and normal tissues was investigated using bioinformatics tools available on the HelixLife platform (https://www.helixlife.cn/). RNA sequencing data and clinical information of bladder cancer patients were obtained from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/projects/TCGA-BLCA), and samples lacking follow-up data were excluded. Ultimately, 419 patients were included for subsequent analyses of gene expression.

Clinical samples

In this study, 12 pairs of human bladder cancer tissues and their corresponding adjacent normal tissues were collected from from the First Affiliated Hospital of Yangtze University. After the specimens were resected, they were immediately processed for sectioning, subsequently placed in labeled cryopreservation tubes, and stored in liquid nitrogen. Before sample collection, written informed consent was obtained from all patients, and the entire research plan was approved by the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Yangtze University, in strict accordance with the Helsinki Declaration and clinical research ethics guidelines.

Cell culture and transfection

The human bladder cancer cell lines BIU87, RT4, 5637, T24 and J82 were provided by Wuhan Sibo Biotechnology Co., Ltd. The human urothelial immortalized cell line SV-HUC-1 was provided by the National Infrastructure of Cell Line Resource (Shanghai, China). The cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium (Sibo Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Wuhan) containing 10% FBS (Gibco, USA) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin mixture, in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2 at 37 °C. To regulate gene expression, we generated ERO1A overexpression (LV-ERO1A) and knockdown (shERO1A) constructs using lentiviral particles from OBiO (Shanghai, China). shERO1A: 5’-TATTCTGAAGAAGCCAATAAT-3’. The overexpression construct was created by inserting the full-length human ERO1A coding sequence (NCBI reference sequence: NM_014350.4) with a Flag tag into the pCDH-CMV-MCS-EF1-Puro lentiviral vector. Additionally, the JAK-STAT signaling pathway agonist All-trans Retinoic Acid (ATRA), inhibitor AG490, chloroquine and colivelin were obtained from MedChemExpress (USA).

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was extracted from well-grown, stably transfected bladder cancer cell lines using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) following the manufacturer’s instructions. The extracted RNA was then reverse-transcribed into cDNA using the Promega M-MLV kit (Promega, Beijing, China). qRT-PCR was carried out with SYBR®Premix Ex Taq™ (TaKaRa, Tokyo, Japan).

Western blot

An optimal volume of RIPA lysis buffer (Servicebio, Wuhan, China) supplemented with protease inhibitors and phosphatase inhibitors was added to cell culture dishes containing confluent cells. Following 30 min of lysis on ice, the cells were harvested by repeated scraping, and protein concentration was subsequently measured using a BCA protein assay kit (Servicebio, Wuhan, China). Proteins were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes. The PVDF membranes were then incubated in TBST containing 5% non-fat dry milk at room temperature for 1 h, followed by three washes with TBST. Primary antibodies against the target proteins were diluted at the manufacturer-recommended dilutions and incubated at 4 °C overnight. After washing with TBST, the PVDF membranes were incubated with secondary antibodies diluted in advance in TBST. Finally, protein visualization was performed using an ultrasensitive ECL chemiluminescent reagent (Servicebio, Wuhan, China). The antibodies used in this study, along with their specific application details, are listed in Table 1.

CCK-8 and 5-ethynyl-2’-deoxyuridine (EdU) assays

Cells were trypsinized to prepare a cell suspension with a density of 4 × 10⁴ cells/mL. 100 µL of this suspension was added to each well of a 96-well cell culture plate, which was then incubated in a 37 °C humidified incubator with 5% CO₂. Cell viability was assessed at 24 h, 48 h, 72 h, 96 h, and 120 h post-seeding. At each time point, 10 µL of CCK-8 detection solution was added to each well, followed by incubation in the same incubator for 3 h. The absorbance of each well at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader.

Cells were co-incubated with 50 µM EdU at 37 °C for 4 h. Subsequently, the cells were fixed with 4% (w/v) formaldehyde at room temperature for 15 min. After permeabilization with 0.5% Triton X-100, 100 µL of 1× Apollo®567 reaction cocktail was added to the cells and incubated at room temperature for 30 min. Finally, the cells were stained with 100 µL of Hoechst 33,342 protected from light for 30 min and imaged using a fluorescence microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan).

Scratch healing assay

Following digestion and centrifugation, a specified number of cells were seeded into 6-well plates containing complete medium. Once the cells had reached confluence, three vertical parallel scratches were created in each well using a 1 mL pipette tip held perpendicular to the plate surface. After aspirating the medium, the cells were gently rinsed twice with PBS (phosphate-buffered saline), and 2 mL of serum-free medium was added to each well. The scratch positions were photographed and recorded as the 0-hour scratch morphology. After 36 h of continuous incubation, the same scratch locations were re-imaged and recorded; alterations in scratch width reflected the migratory potential of the cells.

Transwell invasion assay

First, 50 µL of Matrigel matrix (Corning, USA) was diluted with serum-free medium at a ratio of 1:20, and then coated onto the Transwell insert. 700 µL of complete medium containing 20% serum was added to the lower chamber. 3 × 10⁴ cells were resuspended in 150 µL of serum-free medium and seeded into the Transwell insert. The cells were cultured at 37 °C for 24 h. For fixation, 700 µL of 4% (w/v) paraformaldehyde was added to the lower chamber. For staining, cells were incubated with 0.1% crystal violet for 10 min. Finally, images of the invasive cells were acquired using an inverted phase contrast microscope.

RNA-seq analysis

The original sequencing data (in Fastq format) were filtered for low-quality reads using Trimmomatic (version 0.39). The filtered data were then aligned to the human reference genome (GRCh38.p13, Ensembl database, https://www.ensembl.org/Homo_sapiens/Info/Index) using HISAT2 (version 2.2.1). Transcript assembly and gene expression quantification (in FPKM units) were performed using StringTie (version 2.2.1) in combination with Ballgown. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were screened using DESeq2 (version 1.34.0), with the selection threshold set as: | log2 (fold change) | > 1 and adjusted P value (Padj) < 0.05. The functional annotation of differentially expressed genes was based on the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG, https://www.genome.jp/kegg/) database, and enrichment analysis was conducted using clusterProfiler (version 4.2.2) (with Padj < 0.05 indicating significant enrichment).

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

Paraffin-embedded sections were dewaxed by immersion in xylene. The tissue sections were then hydrated through immersion in absolute ethanol. Following antigen retrieval and endogenous peroxidase blocking, permeabilization was performed using 1×PBS solution containing 0.3% Triton X-100. Subsequently, primary and secondary antibodies were incubated sequentially, followed by DAB chromogenic reaction and hematoxylin staining. Finally, the tissue samples were imaged using an inverted microscope.

Animal models

Four-week-old male nude mice were purchased from Wuhan Shulaibao Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China). Six mice were inoculated with wild-type bladder cancer cells, and another six with shERO1A bladder cancer cells. The length and width of tumors were measured weekly, and tumor volume was calculated using the formula: V = L×W²/2 (where L represents tumor length and W represents tumor width). Mice were euthanized 5 weeks after injection for subsequent analyses. Each mouse was injected via the tail vein with 5 × 105 stable-transfected bladder cancer cells (T24 cells from the shNC group or the shERO1A group). The injection volume was kept consistent. After the cell injection, the general condition of the mice was continuously observed. The experiment was terminated 8 weeks after the injection (this time point was verified by pre-experiments: the mice in the shNC group had formed obvious metastatic foci in the lungs, and no mice died due to excessive tumor burden). Then, the mice were sacrificed and the lung tissues were isolated for HE staining and counting of the metastatic foci. All animal experiments were performed in accordance with the protocols approved by the First Affiliated Hospital of Yangtze University.

Statistical analysis

In this study, statistical analyses of all data were performed using Graphpad Prism 8.0 software and R (4.0.1) software. Quantitative data were presented as mean ± standard deviation. Differences between two groups were analyzed using Student’s t-test or Wilcoxon test, while differences among multiple groups were compared using one-way ANOVA. All experiments were repeated three times, and a P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

ERO1A expression is upregulated in bladder cancer and is associated with poor prognosis

To clarify the function of ERO1A in tumors, we analyzed the differential expression of ERO1A in different tumor types and normal tissues by bioinformatics methods. The analysis results showed that the expression level of ERO1A was higher in tumor tissues such as BLCA, BRCA, CESC, CHOL, COAD, ESCA, HNSC, KICH, KIRC, KIRP, LIHC, LUAD, LUSC, PCPG, READ, STAD, THCA and UCEC compared to adjacent normal tissues. Conversely, in PRAD tumor tissues, the expression of ERO1A was decreased (Fig. 1A). To investigate the expression pattern and clinical significance of ERO1A in bladder cancer patients, we conducted qRT-PCR and Western blot analysis on the collected bladder cancer patient samples (Fig. 1B-D). The results showed that the expression of ERO1A in bladder cancer tissues was higher than that in normal tissues. Similarly, we verified the expression of ERO1A in bladder cancer cell lines (BIU87, RT4, J82, 5637, T24) and normal cell lines, and the results showed that the mRNA and protein levels of ERO1A in bladder cancer cells were higher than those in normal cells (Fig. 1E-G). In the TCGA dataset, the expression of ERO1A in bladder cancer tissues was significantly higher than that in normal tissues, and its level was significantly higher in tumor tissues at different pathological stages than in normal tissues (Fig. 1H-I). Based on the TCGA data, a ROC curve was drawn, and the results showed that ERO1A is a meaningful prognostic indicator in bladder cancer (AUC = 0.849) (Fig. 1J). The above results indicate that ERO1A is upregulated in bladder cancer and is associated with poor prognosis of patients.

ERO1A knockdown suppresses bladder cancer cell proliferation, migration, and invasion

To investigate the functional role of ERO1A in the occurrence process of bladder cancer, we developed a recombinant lentivirus and established stable ERO1A knockdown models in the 5637 and T24 cell lines. The effectiveness of gene silencing was confirmed by qRT-PCR and western blot assays (Fig. 2A-C). ERO1A knockdown significantly attenuated the growth of bladder cancer cells, as evidenced by CCK-8 and EdU assays (Fig. 2D-G). Subsequently, the Transwell invasion assay further revealed that the inhibition of ERO1A expression weakened the invasion ability of bladder cancer cells (Fig. 2H-I). The wound healing assay was also conducted to evaluate the cell migration ability. The results showed that the migration of ERO1A knockdown cells was significantly inhibited (Fig. 2J-L). Finally, we evaluated the expression of EMT-related proteins in these two cell lines. Western blotting results showed that the protein level of E-cadherin increased in ERO1A knockdown cells, while the levels of N-cadherin and vimentin decreased (Fig. 2M-O). In summary, our research results indicate that inhibiting the expression of ERO1A can reduce the malignancy of bladder cancer cells.

ERO1A overexpression enhances bladder cancer cell proliferation, migration, and invasion

To elucidate the oncogenic role of ERO1A in bladder cancer, we constructed a stable ERO1A overexpression model using lentivirus. The overexpression of ERO1A at the transcriptional and translational levels was verified by qRT-PCR and western blot assays (Fig. 3A-C). CCK-8 and EdU assays indicated that ERO1A overexpression could promote the proliferation of bladder cancer cells (Fig. 3D-G). Subsequent Transwell invasion and wound healing assays revealed that bladder cancer cells in the ERO1A overexpression group exhibited significantly enhanced invasion and migration abilities compared to the control group (Fig. 3H-L). Finally, the protein level of E-cadherin was decreased, while the levels of N-cadherin and vimentin were increased in the LV-ERO1A group (Fig. 3M-O). These findings confirmed that upregulation of ERO1A expression could enhance the malignant phenotype of bladder cancer cells.

ERO1A promotes the malignant progression of bladder cancer by activating the JAK-STAT signaling pathway

In order to further explore the specific molecular mechanism of ERO1A on the malignancy of bladder cancer, we conducted RNA-seq analysis on the cells of the control group and the shERO1A group. The differential gene analysis revealed that 606 genes were downregulated and 983 genes were upregulated in the shERO1A group (Fig. 4A-B). Subsequently, we performed KEGG functional enrichment analysis on these differentially expressed genes (Fig. 4C). The research results indicated that ERO1A mainly acts on tumor-related pathways, including the JAK-STAT signaling pathway and autophagy signaling pathway. The JAK-STAT pathway is a well-established driver of malignancy in diverse tumors, including bladder cancer [21–23]. Therefore, we studied the effect of ERO1A protein expression on the expression of key molecules of JAK-STAT pathway in bladder cancer cells. Western blot results showed that silencing ERO1A led to a decrease in the expression of p-JAK2, p-STAT1 and p-STAT3 (Fig. 4D-F). Conversely, overexpression of ERO1A led to an increase in the expression of p-JAK2, p-STAT1 and p-STAT3 (Fig. 4G-I). Our subsequent rescue experiments demonstrated that the JAK-STAT signaling pathway inhibitor AG490 could reverse the promoting effect of ERO1A overexpression on the proliferation, migration and invasion abilities of bladder cancer cells (Supplementary Figure S1). In conclusion, ERO1A may accelerate the progression of bladder cancer by positively regulating the JAK-STAT pathway.

ERO1A regulates autophagy in bladder cancer

The above sequencing results indicate that ERO1A may regulate the progression of bladder cancer by influencing autophagy. To study the effect of ERO1A on autophagy in bladder cancer, we analyzed the expression changes of autophagy-related proteins between the control group and the shERO1A group. The results showed that silencing ERO1A could enhance the expression of Beclin1 and LC3B and inhibit the expression of P62 (Fig. 5A-E, Supplementary Figure S2A-B). Conversely, overexpression of ERO1A yielded opposite results, indicating that silencing ERO1A could promote autophagy (Fig. 5F-H, Supplementary Figure S2C-F). To determine whether ERO1A regulates autophagy by promoting initiation or inhibiting degradation, we blocked autophagolysosomal degradation by using the inhibitor chloroquine (CQ) and assessed autophagic flux via the dynamic changes in LC3B and P62 (Supplementary Figure S3). The results showed that after CQ treatment, LC3B further accumulated in the shERO1A group and P62 degradation was blocked, indicating that shERO1A indeed promotes autophagic flux (rather than inhibiting degradation). Similarly, after the addition of CQ, the accumulation of LC3B in the LV-ERO1A group was significantly lower than that in the LV-Control group, confirming that LV-ERO1A inhibits autophagy initiation (rather than accelerating degradation). Numerous recent studies have shown that the JAK-STAT signaling pathway is closely related to autophagy in tumors [24, 25]. Subsequently, to investigate whether ERO1A affects autophagy via this pathway, we treated two cell lines with the JAK-STAT pathway activator ATRA and the JAK-STAT pathway inhibitor AG490, respectively, and assessed autophagy-related protein expression. The Western blot results showed that ATRA could partially counteract the positive regulatory effect of silencing ERO1A expression on the autophagy signaling pathway (Fig. 5I-K). AG490 could also partially counteract the negative regulatory effect of overexpressing ERO1A on the autophagy signaling pathway (Fig. 5L-N). To investigate whether STAT3 is the key downstream mediator of ERO1A-induced autophagy, we transfected siSTAT3 and siNeg into T24 and 5637 cells respectively, and then detected the expression changes of autophagy-related proteins. The results showed that inhibiting STAT3 could suppress the expression of P62 and promote the expression of LC3B and Beclin1 (Supplementary Figure S4A-C). Inhibiting STAT3 can significantly reverse the reduction of LC3B and the increase of P62 induced by LV-ERO1A, confirming that STAT3 is a necessary downstream molecule for ERO1A to inhibit autophagy (Supplementary Figure S4D-F). To exclude the correlation between the changes in the JAK-STAT pathway and the autophagy changes as an accompanying phenomenon, we used the STAT3-specific activator Colivelin (which can directly promote STAT3 phosphorylation) for a reversal experiment in siERO1A cells. The results showed that Colivelin significantly restored the decreased level of p-STAT3 in shERO1A cells and partially reversed the autophagy activation induced by shERO1A (Supplementary Figure S4G-I). This result directly confirmed that ERO1A inhibits autophagy by activating the JAK-STAT pathway (especially STAT3).

ERO1A interacts with ALOX5 and regulates its phosphorylation level

To elucidate how ERO1A affects the malignant phenotype of bladder cancer cells by regulating the JAK-STAT pathway, we conducted mass spectrometry analysis on ERO1A and performed molecular docking simulations for potential interacting molecules. The results indicated that ERO1A might interact with ALOX5 (Fig. 6A). Subsequently, the immunoprecipitation (IP) experiment confirmed that in T24 and 5637 cells, ERO1A could form an immune complex with ALOX5 (Fig. 6B, Supplementary Figure S5A). We also verified the direct binding between Flag-ERO1A and HA-ALOX5 in 293T cells (Fig. 6C, Supplementary Figure S5B). By analyzing the structure of the ERO1A protein, we predicted that 154-ARG and 208-CYS play a crucial role in the interaction between ERO1A and ALOX5. Therefore, we constructed the corresponding mutants. According to the immunoprecipitation experiments, 154-ARG is essential for the interaction between ALOX5 and ERO1A (Fig. 6D). Additionally, immunofluorescence experiments confirmed the co-localization of these two proteins (Fig. 6E). To assess the regulatory effect of ERO1A on ALOX5, we detected the expression of ALOX5 in bladder cancer cells with shERO1A and LV-ERO1A groups. The results showed that silencing ERO1A could significantly increase the phosphorylation level of ALOX5 protein, without affecting mRNA level (Fig. 6F-H). Conversely, overexpressing ERO1A could significantly reduce the phosphorylation level of ALOX5 protein, without affecting mRNA level (Fig. 6I-K). In summary, ERO1A directly interacts with ALOX5 in bladder cancer cells and reduces its phosphorylation level.

ERO1A affects the autophagy process of bladder cancer cells through the JAK-STAT signaling pathway mediated by ALOX5

The above experiments indicate that ERO1A can regulate the expression of ALOX5. However, it is still unclear whether ALOX5 is also involved in the regulation of the JAK-STAT and the autophagy signaling pathway in bladder cancer. To explore this issue, we conducted knockdown and overexpression experiments of ALOX5 protein in bladder cancer cells, and evaluated the expression levels of proteins related to the JAK-STAT and the autophagy signaling pathway. Western blot analysis showed that overexpression of ALOX5 promoted the protein expression levels of Beclin1, and reduced the protein expression levels of p-JAK2, p-STAT1, p-STAT3 and P62 (Fig. 7A-C), while silencing ALOX5 resulted in the opposite outcome (Fig. 7D-F). These findings suggest that ALOX5 can negatively regulate the activation of the JAK-STAT signaling pathway and positively regulate the occurrence of the autophagy signaling pathway. Subsequent experiments demonstrated that silencing ALOX5 can partially reverse the inhibitory effect of ERO1A inhibition on the JAK-STAT signaling pathway and the promoting effect on the autophagy signaling pathway (Fig. 7G-I), while overexpression of ALOX5 can partially reverse the promoting effect of ERO1A overexpression on the JAK-STAT signaling pathway and the inhibitory effect on the autophagy signaling pathway (Fig. 7J-L). These results suggest that ERO1A may positively regulate the activation of the JAK-STAT signaling pathway through ALOX5, thereby inhibiting the autophagy signaling pathway. Next, we investigated the role of ALOX5 in the progression of bladder cancer. Consistent with the above conclusion, we observed that inhibiting ALOX5 in bladder cancer cells could partially counteract the inhibitory effect of ERO1A expression downregulation on the malignant phenotype of bladder cancer cells, while overexpression of ALOX5 could partially counteract the promoting effect of ERO1A expression upregulation on this phenotype (Supplementary Figure S6). In summary, our results indicate that ERO1A activates the JAK-STAT signaling pathway by modulating ALOX5 phosphorylation, consequently inhibiting autophagy signaling pathway, and enhancing bladder cancer cell proliferation, migration, and invasion. This highlights the crucial regulatory role of the ERO1A/ALOX5/JAK-STAT/autophagy in bladder cancer progression and cell signal transduction.

ERO1A affects the malignant development process of bladder cancer cells through the ALOX5/JAK/STAT/autophagy axis

Finally, we established a xenograft model to investigate the effect of ERO1A in vivo. Tumor size was measured weekly, and the mice were euthanized at 5 weeks. The subcutaneous tumors were then excised for photography and weighing. The results showed that silencing ERO1A inhibited the growth of subcutaneous tumors, and the tumor volume and weight in the shERO1A group were significantly smaller than those in the control group (Fig. 8A-C). To verify the regulation of JAK-STAT and autophagy signaling pathway by ERO1A in vivo, we evaluated the expression levels of proteins related to the JAK-STAT and autophagy signaling pathways in the two groups of tumors. Western blot analysis and IHC staining revealed that Beclin1 levels were significantly increased in the shERO1A group, while p-JAK2, p-STAT1, p-STAT3, and P62 levels were significantly decreased (Fig. 8D-F). Furthermore, in order to investigate the effect of silencing ERO1A expression on the distant metastasis of bladder cancer in vivo, we established a mouse lung metastasis model. Specifically, stable transduced bladder cancer cells were injected into mice via the tail vein. Through visual observation and hematoxylin-eosin (HE) staining, it was found that the number of lung metastatic lesions in the shERO1A group was significantly reduced. This indicates that ERO1A silencing significantly inhibits the lung metastasis potential of bladder cancer (Fig. 8G). In conclusion, ERO1A activates the JAK-STAT signaling pathway by regulating the phosphorylation process of ALOX5, thereby inhibiting autophagy and promoting the malignancy of bladder cancer both in vivo and in vitro.

Discussion

Bladder cancer is a prevalent malignancy of the urinary system [26–29]. Combination chemotherapy has long been the standard treatment.However, chemoresistance is a key factor contributing to poor prognosis in patients with bladder cancer [30]. Despite extensive research, its exact etiology remains elusive, underscoring the urgent need to identify additional molecular targets in patients with bladder cancer.

The endoplasmic reticulum is a cellular organelle with key functions, including calcium storage, protein synthesis, and lipid metabolism [31]. During protein synthesis, various posttranslational modifications occur, including protein folding, assembly, glycosylation, and the formation of disulfide bond. ERO1, first discovered in yeast, is a key enzyme involved in disulfide bond formation during protein posttranslational modification [32]. The human homologous gene encoding ERO1 is located on chromosome 14, and its genomic sequence comprises 16 exons and 15 introns. ERO1 has two homologs: ERO1A and ERO1B. ERO1A is ubiquitously expressed, while ERO1B is highly expressed only in secretory tissues. Studies have shown that ERO1A ablation disrupts the balance between the activity of IRE1α and PERK signaling pathways and induces a lethal unfolded protein response in tumor cells under endoplasmic reticulum stress, thereby enhancing antitumor immunity through immunogenic cell death [20]. ERO1A overexpression is associated with poor prognosis in patients with pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) [33]. Inhibiting ERO1A expression can modulate and ameliorate the tumor microenvironment associated with monocyte infiltration and differentiation into dendritic cells, which may affect the treatment response [15]. Studies have also shown that ERO1A mediates an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment that impairs the response to PD-1 blockade therapy [20]. Depleting ERO1A in tumor cells can significantly stimulate antitumor T-cell immunity and enhance the efficacy of anti-PD-1 therapy. These findings suggest that ERO1A plays a tumor-promoting role in various tumors. However, the role of ERO1A in bladder cancer remains elusive. Therefore, we conducted a study to investigate the role of ERO1A in bladder cancer development. Consistent with our bioinformatics analysis, we confirmed that ERO1A was upregulated in clinical bladder cancer specimens and cell lines. Subsequently, we assessed ERO1A expression in normal bladder and bladder cancer cell lines and found that ERO1A levels were higher in bladder cancer cell lines. Our preliminary analysis indicated that ERO1A may be an independent risk factor for bladder cancer. Furthermore, ERO1A overexpression significantly promoted the proliferation, migration, and invasion of bladder cancer cells. Conversely, silencing ERO1A expression inhibited malignancy and reduced tumor growth in vivo. In summary, our study indicates that ERO1A is an oncogene and a potential prognostic factor for bladder cancer, and holds promise as a therapeutic target.

To clarify the molecular mechanism by which ERO1A mediates bladder cancer progression, we conducted RNA sequencing analysis of cell lines with ERO1A knockdown. The results revealed that differentially expressed genes following ERO1A knockdown were significantly enriched in the JAK-STAT signaling pathway. Previous studies have shown that this pathway is a key regulator of cancer development and progression. In ovarian cancer, FCGBP enhances interleukin-6 expression by interacting with the NF-kappaB subunit p65, thereby activating the JAK-STAT signaling pathway and promoting ovarian cancer progression [34]. In gastric cancer, ADAMTS10 regulates TXNIP and ROS via the JAK/STAT/c-MYC pathway, potentially influencing both gastric cancer progression and macrophages polarization [35]. In bladder cancer, PLA2G7 modulates the JAK-STAT pathway by upregulating the STAT1 and STAT3 phosphorylation, promoting PD-L1 expression and inhibiting CD8 + T cells infiltration, thereby facilitating immune escape of bladder cancer [23]. This study confirmed that silencing ERO1A inhibits JAK2, STAT1, and STAT3 phosphorylation, ultimately impairing the proliferative capacity of bladder cancer cells. Conversely, ERO1A overexpression exerted the opposite effect. Additionally, JAK-STAT pathway reversal experiments further verified that ERO1A modulates bladder cancer cells proliferation by regulating the JAK-STAT pathway.

Autophagy is a catabolic process in which damaged organelles or cellular debris are enclosed in double-membrane vesicles for degradation [36]. Generally, autophagy is cytoprotective; however, dysregulation or excessive autophagic flux often results in cell death [37]. In this study, silencing ERO1A upregulated the autophagy-related proteins Beclin1 and LC3B expression and downregulated P62 expression, while ERO1A overexpression produced the opposite effects. These results suggest that ERO1A inhibits autophagy in bladder cancer cells. We then conducted autophagy flux experiments by using chloroquine to block autophagic degradation, and confirmed that LC3B accumulation in the shERO1A group was dependent on active autophagy (rather than blocked degradation). The reduction in LC3B in the LV-ERO1A group resulted from inhibition of autophagy initiation. Collectively, these results confirm that ERO1A regulates bladder cancer autophagy by inhibiting the autophagy initiation process (rather than interfering with degradation), which is consistent with the autophagy regulation mechanism mediated by the JAK-STAT pathway, thereby strengthening the conclusion of this study. Subsequently, through mass spectrometry and molecular docking, we found that ERO1A interacts with ALOX5. Functional experiments showed that ERO1A modulates the activation of the JAK-STAT signaling pathway and autophagy by regulating ALOX5 phosphorylation. Our results reveal a previously unknown function of ERO1A in bladder cancer: it establishes a critical link between the JAK-STAT and autophagy pathways by regulating ALOX5 as a pivotal node.This study proposes a new concept of an “ERO1A-ALOX5 signaling hub,” whose function is independent of its classical role in endoplasmic reticulum protein folding.

However, this study had some limitations. First, the clinical tissue sample size was relatively small, which may not have fully captured the clinical heterogeneity of bladder cancer, resulting in limited representativeness of the research conclusions in the clinical population. Moreover, the small sample size may have reduced statistical power, making it difficult to further analyze the correlations among ERO1A expression, patient recurrence time, and overall survival as prognostic indicators, thereby limiting the direct clinical relevance of the molecular mechanism identified in this study. Second, we confirmed the interaction between ERO1A and ALOX5 and its inhibition of ALOX5 phosphorylation. However, we have not yet conducted point-mutation experiments at the catalytic active site of ERO1A to determine whether its redox enzyme activity is necessary in this process or whether it functions in a nonenzymatic, “supporting protein” role. Finally, this study did not develop or test inhibitors targeting ERO1A, or evaluate potential synergistic effects between ERO1A inhibition and existing chemotherapeutic drugs.

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that ERO1A, as an oncogene, is upregulated in bladder cancer patients. ERO1A promotes the proliferation, migration, and invasion of bladder cancer cells by regulating the ALOX5/JAK/STAT/autophagy signaling axis. Our findings indicate that ERO1A directly interacts with ALOX5 and suppresses ALOX5 phosphorylation, ERO1A may serve as a novel molecular marker for bladder cancer patients.
