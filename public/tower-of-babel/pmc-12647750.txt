Multi-objective trajectory optimization method for industrial robots based on improved TD3 algorithm
Authors: Yuhang Xu, Shuhang Kong, Xiaowu Kong, Zixi Ying
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This paper addresses the challenge of simultaneously satisfying collision-free trajectory, path quality optimization, and execution time optimization in trajectory planning for industrial robots in confined spaces. A multi-objective integrated trajectory optimization algorithm is proposed based on the TD3 reinforcement learning framework. Firstly, the motion generation mechanism is improved using a Butterworth filter and a dynamic noise attenuation strategy to enhance trajectory smoothness. Secondly, a genetic algorithm is employed to automatically optimize the hyperparameters of the TD3 algorithm, and a prioritized experience replay mechanism is introduced to improve the utilization efficiency of critical experiences, thereby enhancing the algorithm’s convergence speed and stability. Finally, a composite reward function based on time-distance information is designed to effectively guide the industrial robot in optimizing trajectory execution time under collision-free conditions. This paper uses the Fairino Robot5 robotic arm model as the training object and conducts simulation experiments in a PyBullet environment, as well as real physical experiments. Compared to the RRT method, manual teaching method, traditional TD3 method, and SAC method, the actual trajectory execution time was reduced by 57.03% , 22.94% , 26.05%, and 20.5%, respectively.

Motivation and significance

With the continuous development of intelligent manufacturing, industrial robots have become indispensable assets in modern industrial production. A key trend in this evolution is the increasing deployment of robots in confined workspaces, such as in operations with extremely limited space like CNC lathe loading/unloading or precision assembly lines. Although most industrial robots can automatically perform specific trajectory planning tasks, manual programming or teaching is still required in advance1. Particularly in narrow spaces, challenges such as difficulty in point-to-point planning often arise. This limitation results in high dependence on manual expertise for trajectory planning, making it difficult to meet the high standards of teaching efficiency. Furthermore, during manual teaching, to avoid obstacles in narrow spaces and improve trajectory accuracy, robot motion commands are often linear. This limitation leads to slow execution speeds for planned trajectories, making it difficult to meet high standards of production efficiency. Therefore, it is essential to conduct research on industrial robot trajectory planning that balances collision-free motion, kinematic constraints, and time-optimized trajectory execution.

Related work

The core challenge of trajectory planning for industrial robots in confined spaces lies in simultaneously achieving three key objectives: collision-free movement, adherence to kinematic constraints, and execution time optimization. Classical industrial robot trajectory planning algorithms primarily include sampling-based algorithms, heuristic algorithms, and deep reinforcement learning-based algorithms.

Sampling-based algorithms primarily include the Probabilistic Road Map (PRM)2 and Rapidly-exploring Random Trees (RRT)3, along with their improved versions. Due to the advantages of RRT algorithm, such as probabilistic completeness, rapid exploration, and the ability to avoid the need for converting obstacles from the workspace to the joint space, it is widely applied in robotic arm trajectory planning. Qingyang Gao et al.4 proposed a robotic arm trajectory planning method that combines an improved RRT* algorithm with a BP neural network. This method optimizes the trajectory search process through neural networks, enhancing the algorithm’s intelligence and adaptability, and achieves the objectives of trajectory length optimization and obstacle avoidance. Haoning Zhao et al.5 proposed a robotic arm trajectory planning method combining an improved RRT algorithm with cubic spline interpolation. This method enhances the efficiency of planning a collision-free trajectory by introducing node control and bidirectional extension into the RRT algorithm. By incorporating cubic spline interpolation, the method ensures that the robotic arm’s movement satisfies kinematic constraints and is smoother. Subin Lim et al.6 proposed an improved RRT* algorithm that incorporates a cost function based on obstacle distance and path length, enabling a balance between path length and obstacle clearance, and achieving the dual objectives of obstacle avoidance and path length optimization. Qinglei Zhang et al.7 proposing an improved IPQ-RRT* algorithm, which builds upon the existing PQ-RRT* algorithm by incorporating a node greedy bidirectional expansion strategy, thereby enhancing planning efficiency. Building upon this, Bezier curve optimization is applied to the trajectory, and experimental results in a physical environment demonstrate that the algorithm can effectively generate collision-free and smooth trajectories.

Heuristic algorithms are strategies or methods that use experience-based rules, intuition, or heuristics to solve problems. They primarily include Genetic Algorithm (GA), Harris Hawk Optimizer (HHO), and Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO)8. Among them, Genetic Algorithm (GA) is widely used in robotic arm trajectory planning due to its ability to handle complex search spaces and excellent global search capabilities9. Masood Usama et al.10 proposed an efficient robotic arm obstacle avoidance trajectory planning method based on a genetic algorithm optimization approach, achieving an optimal motion smoothness objective based on obstacle avoidance. Wu Rong et al.11 proposed a trajectory optimization strategy based on hybrid particle swarm optimization (HPSO). This method addresses the issue of basic particle swarm algorithms easily getting trapped in local optima by combining chaotic mapping, simulated annealing, improved inertia weight, and learning factors to improve the particle swarm algorithm, optimizing trajectory planning for a 6-degree-of-freedom robot under kinematic constraints. Özge Ekrem et al.12 proposed an efficient robot trajectory planning strategy by combining the particle swarm optimization algorithm with fifth-order polynomial interpolation, achieving optimal path planning under constraints such as motion smoothness and collision avoidance for robotic arms.Yu Gu et al.13 proposed an improved Harris Hawk Optimizer (IHHO) for time-optimal trajectory planning. The method modifies the form of the prey escape energy factor in the HHO algorithm and introduces the Sine Cosine Algorithm (SCA) during the exploration phase to update population strategies, balancing global exploration and local exploitation capabilities. It achieves time-optimal trajectory execution under kinematic constraints.

However, due to the unique characteristics of industrial robots, such as nonlinearity and high-dimensional state spaces, the aforementioned algorithms often struggle with planning in confined spaces. They tend to get trapped in local optima, exhibit poor generalization ability, and fail to achieve effective trajectory planning. In contrast, deep reinforcement learning, as a learning-based algorithm, does not require a specific model of the environment. It can continuously learn from the environment to improve the performance of robotic trajectory planning and adaptively respond to environmental changes, thereby enhancing the efficiency and accuracy of trajectory planning. Due to these advantages, deep reinforcement learning has broad application prospects in the field of robotic arm trajectory research in narrow environments and has become one of the current research hotspots14–16.

In recent years, the continuous development of artificial intelligence has injected new vitality into intelligent manufacturing, leading to an increasing number of studies on the application of deep reinforcement learning in trajectory planning for industrial robots17. Compared with conventional trajectory planning methods, deep reinforcement learning has garnered significant attention from researchers due to its strong ability to address nonlinear planning problems and its high adaptability. For example, Ravi Raj et al.18 proposed a mobile robot navigation method based on Deep Q-Learning (DQN), using the Epsilon-Greedy algorithm for simulation to avoid obstacles and enhance navigation capabilities in unknown and complex environments. Shanbo Zhang et al.19 incorporated optimization metrics such as smoothness and energy efficiency into a multi-objective optimization function, embedding it into the reward function of the PPO algorithm. This achieved the energy-optimal objective under kinematic constraints and obstacle avoidance conditions. Osama Ahmad et al.20 installed a vision sensor on the robotic arm’s end effector, achieving the optimization objective of dynamic obstacle avoidance under kinematic constraints. Jiexin Xie et al.21 proposed a path planning algorithm based on the SAC algorithm for periodically moving obstacles. The neural network in the SAC algorithm learns the positional information of moving obstacles within a limited past time range, enabling superior performance in trajectory planning and overall strategy control for robotic arms. Although these studies have achieved promising results, most of the work focuses on task-space trajectory planning and is primarily validated in simulation environments rather than through physical experiments. Furthermore, existing trajectory planning algorithms based on deep reinforcement learning still have room for improvement in terms of convergence speed, algorithm stability, trajectory execution time, and path smoothness.

Problem description and system modelling

This paper is dedicated to addressing the trajectory planning problem of industrial robots in confined work environments, particularly focusing on how to optimize time, avoid collisions, and ensure path quality when planning in joint space. To address this issue, this paper proposes a decision model based on deep reinforcement learning (RL). The RL agent is trained through continuous interactions with the environment, enabling it to utilize environmental information to optimize the trajectory planning process of industrial robots, thereby achieving the following specific objectives: Time optimization: Complete tasks within a limited time, ensuring the efficiency of trajectory execution; Collision-free constraint: Avoid collisions with the surrounding environment during task execution, ensuring safety; Path quality optimization: Satisfy kinematic constraints, reduce path length, and improve trajectory smoothness.

In order to achieve these objectives,the simulation experiments and physical experiments in this paper are conducted in the robot’s joint space for trajectory planning. Compared to task space, trajectory planning in joint space offers the following advantages: First, compared to task space simulations, joint space simulations allow for direct setting and restriction of each joint’s motion range, which can preemptively avoid failures caused by joints reaching their motion limits, effectively protecting the hardware of the robotic arm and reducing wear and fault frequency. Second, joint space enables precise control over each joint angle compared to task space, thereby offering higher operational accuracy and reducing error accumulation. Finally, planning a trajectory in joint space within a simulation environment avoids the need for inverse kinematics solutions, thereby eliminating singularity issues23. When transferring simulation results to real-world robotic arm experiments, it reduces debugging time during the transition from simulation to physical implementation. Through deep reinforcement learning training, the RL agent will optimize the trajectory in joint space through continuous trial and error, ultimately enabling the robot to achieve precise and efficient trajectory planning in complex work environments, satisfying multiple optimization objectives of time, collision, and path quality.

RL agent system model

A Markov Decision Process (MDP) is a sequential decision-making mathematical model24 used to simulate an agent’s stochastic policy and rewards in a Markovian environment. As the fundamental mathematical framework of deep reinforcement learning, an MDP must be established when solving robotic arm trajectory planning problems using deep reinforcement learning methods. In the Markov Decision Process, the agent selects actions through interactions with the environment, with each decision influencing the system’s state.

The decision-making process for industrial robot trajectory planning can be abstracted as a Markov Decision Process, where continuous interactions occur between the industrial robot and its environment. By employing reinforcement learning algorithms to train industrial robots in decision-making related to joint angular velocities, the robot can autonomously select a trajectory with significant reward value, achieving a collision-free, highly smooth, and time-optimal trajectory for industrial robots.

To balance current and future rewards, a discount factor \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\gamma$$\end{document} is introduced in the trajectory planning decision-making process to prevent the algorithm from entering infinite loops and to ensure convergence. When \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\gamma$$\end{document} approaches 1, the importance of future rewards increases, whereas when \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\gamma$$\end{document} approaches 0, the importance of current rewards dominates. Rewards are typically used to measure the quality of a state-action pair.

In industrial robot trajectory planning within confined environments, the Markov property refers to the robot selecting the optimal action at each time step based on the current state, which includes the surrounding environmental state and its own position and posture information. After executing the action, the robot perceives changes in the environment and updates its own state to receive a feedback reward (return value). Based on the reward, the robot adjusts its next action and continues this process until an optimal policy is formed. The Markov property emphasizes that the choice of the current state depends solely on the current environmental state and not on past historical information.

Observation and action space setup

When training a DRL algorithm for an industrial robot, it is essential to define its state space and action space. Therefore, a reasonable state space must be designed for the robot and its environment to generate input data for the neural network. In the trajectory planning task of this paper, the state information mainly includes two aspects: environmental information and self-information. Environmental information includes collision detection between the robot and obstacles, the distance between the robot and the target point, and the deviation between the robot’s posture and the target posture. Self-information includes the angles of the six joints, whether the robot has reached the target position, and whether the robot has achieved the target posture. The posture of the robot’s end-effector is represented using quaternions, which effectively avoids the issue of gimbal lock25. The deviation between the posture of the robotic arm’s end-effector and the target posture can be quantitatively described using the Axis-Angle Representation. This method uses a rotation axis and rotation angle to represent the difference between the two, providing an accurate geometric representation that facilitates the adjustment and optimization of the end-effector’s posture. In this paper, the rotation angle is used to quantitatively analyze the deviation between the robot’s end-effector posture and the target posture. Reward function design

The essence of Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) lies in processing input state information through neural networks under the guidance of a reward function and establishing the relationship between state, value estimation, and decision-making. In DRL applications, the design of the reward function is crucial. By converting task objectives into mathematical forms, effective communication between the task and the algorithm is achieved, determining whether the agent can learn the required skills and directly influencing the algorithm’s convergence speed and robustness.

The composite reward function consists of six main components: \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R_a$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R_s$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R_t$$\end{document}, which guide the robotic arm to generate a collision-free, smooth, and time-optimal trajectory; \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R_l$$\end{document}, which controls the path length; \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R_o$$\end{document}, which penalizes proximity to obstacles; and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R_suc$$\end{document}, which rewards reaching the target object with the specified position and orientation. \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R_a$$\end{document} (Precision Reward):Dense shaping rewards are provided based on reducing position and orientation deviations, directly promoting execution time optimization. The dynamic adjustment factor \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha _{dis}$$\end{document} ensures a smooth transition from prioritizing position to prioritizing orientation as the end-effector approaches the target, thereby improving final precision. \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R_s$$\end{document} (Smoothness Reward) and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R_l$$\end{document} (Path Length Reward): These components are crucial for satisfying kinematic constraints. By penalizing large joint angles and high angular velocities (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R_s$$\end{document}) and encouraging shorter paths (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R_l$$\end{document}), the reward function inherently promotes smoother motion, which is less likely to violate the robot’s speed and acceleration limits. \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R_t$$\end{document} (Execution Time Optimization Reward):Since the simulation used in this paper is a fixed-step simulation, the reward guides the industrial robot to move from the starting pose to the target pose with the smallest time steps, thereby achieving execution time optimization. \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R_o$$\end{document} (Obstacle Penalty) and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R_{suc}$$\end{document} (Success Reward): These components are fundamental to ensuring collision-free motion. Severe penalties are imposed for behaviors that enter the danger zones around obstacles, strongly discouraging unsafe trajectories. A large positive reward provides a clear incentive for successfully reaching the target without collision.

Trajectory planning method

Applying the traditional TD3 algorithm to the multi-objective trajectory planning domain of the six-joint industrial robot proposed in this paper has the following drawbacks: In the standard TD3 algorithm, the inherent noise is Gaussian noise. During exploration, Gaussian noise is temporally uncorrelated, meaning the noise is independent for actions selected in consecutive steps. The independence of consecutive actions is maintained only through the state. In TD3 algorithm simulation tasks, when small-granularity time discretization issues arise, simply increasing the discretization granularity allows the independent Gaussian noise to remain effective. Thus, in simulation tasks, TD3 achieves satisfactory results using Gaussian noise without requiring temporally correlated noise like OU noise. However, in actual industrial robot trajectory planning systems, the control of joint angular velocities is an integration process, or an “inertial system”. The integration process acts as a typical low-pass filter, automatically suppressing high-frequency noise. This renders the independent Gaussian noise in the TD3 algorithm ineffective for exploration, and its influence on policy updates often causes the robot to fall into local optimal. Additionally, the TD3 algorithm lacks a built-in smoothing mechanism, making it prone to significant fluctuations during control, which prevents it from maintaining a stable motion trajectory.The experience replay buffer is designed to address the issue of high correlation between environmental samples, which makes convergence difficult. By mixing current feedback data samples with previously collected samples and randomly sampling from the mixed data during training, the network parameters are updated to accelerate algorithm convergence. However, the experience replay buffer contains a large number of failure experiences from collisions with obstacles during the robot’s early training. Adding these samples, which have very few successful experiences, into the buffer leads to a low success rate in subsequent training and significantly prolongs training time.In the traditional TD3 algorithm, the network structure is complex and involves numerous hyperparameters. The addition of OU noise and PER algorithms further increases the number of hyperparameters, making their selection crucial to algorithm performance. However, the traditional TD3 algorithm lacks an adaptive mechanism to automatically adjust these hyperparameters, relying instead on manual tuning based on experience. For complex tasks, especially in industrial robot trajectory planning, selecting appropriate hyperparameters is particularly critical. Poor hyperparameter selection may result in slow convergence, poor stability, or even an inability to effectively optimize the network during training. As a result, manual hyperparameter tuning has become a bottleneck in algorithm implementation. Moreover, different tasks and environments may require different hyperparameter combinations, increasing the complexity and cost of algorithm application and debugging.In this paper, a composite behavior strategy combining dynamically adjustable OU noise parameters and a sliding-window-based Butterworth filter is introduced, aiming to problems (1) above. The prioritized experience replay algorithm is introduced, aiming to problem (2). In the standard TD3 algorithm, the inherent noise is Gaussian noise. During exploration, Gaussian noise is temporally uncorrelated, meaning the noise is independent for actions selected in consecutive steps. The independence of consecutive actions is maintained only through the state. In TD3 algorithm simulation tasks, when small-granularity time discretization issues arise, simply increasing the discretization granularity allows the independent Gaussian noise to remain effective. Thus, in simulation tasks, TD3 achieves satisfactory results using Gaussian noise without requiring temporally correlated noise like OU noise. However, in actual industrial robot trajectory planning systems, the control of joint angular velocities is an integration process, or an “inertial system”. The integration process acts as a typical low-pass filter, automatically suppressing high-frequency noise. This renders the independent Gaussian noise in the TD3 algorithm ineffective for exploration, and its influence on policy updates often causes the robot to fall into local optimal. Additionally, the TD3 algorithm lacks a built-in smoothing mechanism, making it prone to significant fluctuations during control, which prevents it from maintaining a stable motion trajectory.

To evaluate the effectiveness of the trajectory planning algorithm, this study proposes the following evaluation metrics based on the key performance requirements of collision-free, high smoothness, and optimization of execution time in industrial robot applications. (1) Convergence speed of training: At the same level of accuracy, faster and more stable convergence of the reward curve and success rate indicates better algorithm performance. (2) Changes in joint angles and Jerk (rate of change of angular acceleration): In the successful test set, record the trends of joint angles, joint angular velocity, and Jerk. The smoothness of the path is evaluated by the continuity of joint angles and angular velocity changes, and the amplitude of Jerk oscillations. The smoother the changes in joint angles and velocities, and the smaller the Jerk fluctuation range, the smoother the trajectory. (3) Task success rate: In the successful test set, test the number of times the industrial robot successfully completes the task, with a total of 100 episodes tested. This metric provides a clear assessment of how effectively the robot avoids obstacles during its trajectory planning. (4) Actual runtime: In the successful test set, the time required to move from the initial pose to the target pose is recorded. The shorter the time, the better the time optimization performance.

To ensure a representative and reproducible comparison, we keep the conventional TD3 as a benchmark and introduce Soft Actor–Critic (SAC) as an additional DRL baseline. SAC is an off-policy, maximum-entropy actor–critic algorithm with high sample efficiency and stable training. These properties suit industrial-robot settings, where real-world interactions are costly and stringent requirements on safety and trajectory smoothness apply. Moreover, SAC’s mature open-source implementations and broad adoption enable fair, reproducible comparisons under identical network architectures, observation/action spaces, training budgets, and evaluation metrics. Accordingly, we compare our approach against these two baselines to verify its effectiveness and quantify its performance advantages.

Conclusion and future work

This study provides a comprehensive investigation into the trajectory planning problem for industrial robots and proposes a trajectory planning method based on the improved TD3 algorithm. The method integrates collision avoidance, optimal trajectory execution time, and kinematic constraints in narrow and complex working environments, thereby generating the desired trajectory for industrial robots. The input parameters are pose coordinates based on Cartesian space, while the output parameters are the changes in joint angles. Comparative analysis with the RRT algorithm, manual teaching method, and traditional TD3 algorithm, along with both simulation and physical experimental results, demonstrate the effectiveness and robustness of the proposed method.

Despite the promising experimental results in this study, there are still some limitations. For example, the current experiments only considered static work environments, and future research could further extend to dynamic environments to address trajectory planning issues in the presence of dynamic obstacles or changing environments. Furthermore, the algorithm in this paper has primarily been validated in a single environment scenario, and future work could consider testing it in different scenarios to assess its generalization ability.

These findings provide new insights for the future development of advanced robotic arm trajectory planning and open new prospects for the application of deep reinforcement learning in complex trajectory planning tasks.
