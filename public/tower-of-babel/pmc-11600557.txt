Drainage posterior to pancreaticojejunostomy reduces the severity of postoperative pancreatic fistula after pancreaticoduodenectomy
Authors: Yuan Zhou, Fengchun Lu, Xianchao Lin, Yuanyuan Yang, Congfei Wang, Haizong Fang, Ronggui Lin, Heguang Huang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundClinically relevant postoperative pancreatic fistula (CR-POPF) is a common postoperative complication after pancreaticoduodenectomy (PD) and is associated with severe complications. Drainage is an effective method to treat POPF and prevent POPF-related complications. However, controversy still exists about whether different drainage methods reduce the incidence or the severity of POPF after PD.MethodsA closed suction drainage was placed posterior to pancreaticojejunostomy in PD except for other routine drainage placements. A retrospective study was conducted to calculate the incidence and severity of CR-POPF and POPF-related complications and to evaluate the efficacy of this drainage method.Results295 patients who underwent PD were enrolled in this study, 130 patients in the trial group and 165 patients in the control group. The two groups were comparable in both preoperative and intraoperative characteristics. The overall incidence of CR-POPF was similar between the two groups. The trial group had a significantly decreased incidence of grade C POPF (0% vs. 3.6%, p < 0.05), post-pancreatectomy hemorrhage (PPH) (0% vs. 6.1%, p = 0.003), reoperation (0% vs. 3.6%, p = 0.036), intra-abdominal infection (13.1% vs. 25.5%, p = 0.008), and delayed gastric emptying (DGE) (2.3% vs. 8.5%, p = 0.024) than the control group. Subgroup analysis of patients with intermediate/high risk for CR-POPF mirrored these results. Logistic regression identified obstructive jaundice, biliary fistula, POPF, and DGE as independent risk factors for PPH and reoperation, though the results were not significant in multivariate analysis.ConclusionsThe drainage posterior to pancreaticojejunostomy reduces the severity of POPF and the incidence of POPF-related complications after PD.

Introduction

Pancreaticoduodenectomy (PD) is a classic procedure for most of the malignant and benign diseases located at the head of the pancreas and the peri-ampulla region [1]. PD has been considered one of the most complex surgical procedures due to multiple organ resection and technically challenging anastomosis [2]. With the rapid development of surgical techniques, minimally invasive PD, including laparoscopic pancreaticoduodenectomy (LPD) and robotic pancreaticoduodenectomy (RPD) has been routinely applied in some high-volume pancreatic centers [3, 4]. Encouragingly, the morbidity and mortality of PD has remarkably decreased in recent decades,

Clinically relevant postoperative pancreatic fistula (CR-POPF) is one of the most common complications after PD [5]. Postoperative pancreatic fistula (POPF), defined as an abnormal communication between the pancreatic ductal system and adjacent tissues or organs, occurs in approximately 5–25% of patients undergoing PD, leading to significant morbidity and mortality [6]. POPF is associated with many other postoperative complications, including intra-abdominal liquid collection, intra-abdominal infection (IAI), delayed gastric emptying (DGE), post-pancreatectomy hemorrhage (PPH), and reoperation. These complications may lead to prolonged hospital stays and ultimately affect the patient’s prognosis and quality of life [7]. Some of these severe complications may even be life-threatening [8].

Many studies have focused on mitigating the risk of POPF and POPF-related complications after PD [9], including modifications in surgical technique, application of somatostatin or octreotide [10], tissue sealants [11], and drainage management [12, 13]. Drainage is an essential tool and critical method for POPF. Drainage can divert potential pancreatic juice, alleviate caustic damage, and decrease the incidence of POPF-related complications. Many different drainage methods have been applied in PD [14], mainly including gravity drainage, closed suction drainage, and with the application of irrigation. However, only a few studies described the drainage placement and management details of PD.

In this study, we conducted a retrospective analysis of patients who underwent PD with and without a closed suction drainage posterior to pancreaticojejunostomy. Through a meticulous review of patient medical records, we aimed to evaluate the efficacy of this drainage method on the incidence and severity of CR-POPF and its related complications after PD.

Methods

Patients

The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Fujian Medical University Union Hospital. 295 patients who received PD between April 2020 and December 2023 at Fujian Medical University Union Hospital were enrolled in this retrospective study. The patients in the control group with routine drainage placement were included from April, 2020 to April, 2022, and the trial group with an additional closed suction drainage posterior to pancreaticojejunostomy in PD were included from May, 2022 to December 2023.

Drainage placement and management

Open pancreaticoduodenectomy (OPD), laparoscopic pancreaticoduodenectomy (LPD), and robotic pancreaticoduodenectomy (RPD) were included in this study. In the control group, a 10-mm multi-functional irrigation-suction tube (Sunlight Medical, Guangzhou, China) was routinely placed above the pancreaticojejunostomy (PJ), below the caudate lobe of the liver, defined as the PJ drainage [15]. A 28-Fr silicone tube was placed posterior to the biliojejunostomy (BJ), defined as the BJ drainage tube.

In the trial group, except for the routine placement of PJ and BJ drainage, a soft 12-Fr silicone tube was placed posterior to the pancreaticojejunostomy, in front of the portal vein (PV)/ superior mesenteric vein (SMV), defined as the PPJ drainage. The tube was drained with low closed negative pressure. There are many small holes along the tube, providing adequate drainage. The tube coursed inferior, posterior, and superior to the pancreaticojejunostomy. Hence, the PPJ tube can drain most of the peri-anastomotic liquid around pancreaticojejunostomy. The 10-mm multi-functional tube can also be used for irrigation and suction once the drainage amylase was at an extremely high level or with a sign of infection (Figs. 1 and 2).

The amylase levels of PJ, and PPJ drainage fluid were tested on postoperative day (POD) 3, and 7. The volume, characteristics, and amylase level of the drainage fluid and clinical symptoms were reference factors for drainage management [16]. The drainage tube was removed with no sign of leakage or infection or when the amylase level of the drainage fluid was ≤ 3 times the upper limit of the standard serum level [6]. On the POD 6–7, a routine abdominal computed tomography (CT) was performed to evaluate the potential postoperative complications, such as liquid collection, abscess.

Data collection

Clinical data, including patient demographics, preoperative comorbidities, operative details, and postoperative outcomes, were extracted from the hospital electronic records.

Study endpoint

The primary endpoints of the study were the incidence and severity of CR-POPF. The secondary endpoints were other postoperative complications, mainly including PPH, reoperation, IAI, DGE, bile leakage, and postoperative factors, including the restoration of a semi-liquid diet, duration of removal of PJ drainage, PPJ drainage, BJ drainage, and length of hospitalization. The International Study Group on Pancreatic Surgery (ISGPS) defines CR-POPF as an amylase level in the collected drainage fluid more significant than three times the serum amylase level with clinically relevant development/condition [6]. Intra-abdominal infection (IAI) diagnosis was based on a positive bacterial culture result of the surgical drainage fluid [17]. PPH was diagnosed according to the definition developed by the ISGPS [18].

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize patient demographics, perioperative characteristics, and postoperative outcomes. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages, while continuous variables were summarized as means with standard deviations or medians with interquartile ranges, as appropriate. Chi-square tests or Fisher’s exact tests were used to compare categorical variables between groups, while independent t-tests or Mann-Whitney U tests were employed for continuous variables, as appropriate. Logistic regression analysis was performed to assess the association between the drainage method and the incidence of severe complications, adjusting for potential confounders. A probability (p) value < 0.05 was considered as statistical significance. The statistical analysis was conducted with the SPSS 22.0 software (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA).

Comparison of postoperative complications and factors

The incidence of overall CR-POPF (grades B and C) in the control and the trial groups were 18.8% (31/165) and 16.9% (22/130), respectively (grade B 15.2% (25/165) vs. 16.9% (22/130), p ≥ 0.05), showing no significant difference (p ≥ 0.05) (Table 3). However, the trial group had a significantly lower incidence of grade C POPF than the control group (0% (0/130) vs. 3.6% (6/165), p < 0.05), indicating that the PPJ drainage significantly reduced the incidence of grade C POPF. Additionally, the trial group had remarkably decreased incidences of post-pancreatectomy hemorrhage (PPH) (0% vs. 6.1%, p = 0.003) and reoperation (0% vs. 3.6%, p = 0.036) than the control group. PPH and reoperation mainly occurred in the patients with severe POPF in the control group. Hence, PPJ drainage notably decreased the incidences of POPF-related severe complications, including PPH and reoperation. The incidence of intra-abdominal infection (IAI) (25.5% vs. 13.1%, p = 0.008) and delayed gastric emptying (DGE) (8.5% vs. 2.3%, p = 0.024), as well as the time to resume a liquid diet (4.74 ± 4.75 vs. 3.62 ± 2.69, p = 0.011), were significantly decreased in the trial group compared to the control group, indicating that PPJ drainage reduced the occurrence of postoperative intra-abdominal infections, and promoted early resumption of diet after surgery, facilitating patient’s recovery. The incidences of bile leakage (3% vs. 2.3%, p = 1), median duration of BJ drainage removal (12.99 days vs. 12.32 days, p = 0.316), median duration of PJ drainage removal (14.65 days vs. 14.7 days, p = 0.946), and median length of hospital stay (16.24 days vs. 15.82 days, p = 0.537) were comparable in both groups. The median duration of PPJ drainage removal was 14.36 ± 5.32 day.

Characteristics of intermediate/high patients in POPF and preoperative and intraoperative factors

A subgroup comparison of postoperative complications and influencing factors was shown in Table 5. The overall incidence of CR-POPF (grades B and C) in the control group and the trial group was 22.3% (27/121) and 18.1% (17/94). The incidence of grade B POPF was 18.2% (22/121) and 18.1% (17/94) in the control group and the trial group, respectively, showing no significant difference. The incidence of PPH was significantly higher in the control group than in the trial group (3.6% vs. 0%, p = 0.01), similar to that of the whole group comparison. The control group had increased incidences of grade C POPF (4.1% (5/121) vs. 0% (0/94)) and reoperation (6.6% (8/121) vs. 0% (0/94)) than the trial group. However, the results showed no significant difference, while we considered it was due to the insufficient sample size. Therefore, the PPJ drainage might decreased the severity of POPF and the incidences of PPH and reoperation in patients with intermediate/high risks for POPF. In addition, the incidence of IAI (30.6% vs. 12.8%, p = 0.002) was significantly decreased in the trial group compared with the control group. It also suggested that PPJ drainage reduced the incidence of intra-abdominal infections in the postoperative period. The incidence of biliary leakage (3.3% vs. 3.2%, p = 1), DGE (9.1% vs. 3.2%, p = 0.082), the median duration to restore a semi-liquid diet (4.84 ± 4.91 vs. 3.77 ± 3.15, p = 0.052), the median duration to BJ drainage removal (13.72 days vs. 12.48 days, p = 0.123), and PJ drainage removal (15.51 days vs. 15.06 days, p = 0.618), and the median length of hospitalization (17 days vs. 15.91 days, p = 0.161) were comparable in both groups. The median duration of PPJ drainage removal was 14.49 ± 4.56 day.

Discussion

Controversies exist about whether no-drainage or drainage strategy dramatically benefits the patients after pancreatectomy. A no-drainage strategy has attracted many surgeons’ attention recently. However, different from distal pancreatectomy, routine drain placement is still a common practice among most pancreatic surgeons for PD [20]. Drainage plays a vital role in preventing and treating POPF after PD [21]. A previous multi-center RCT showed that elimination of intraperitoneal drainage in PD increases the incidence and severity of complications [22]. The trial was stopped early because of the significantly higher mortality in the no-drain group. An Italian survey of pancreatic surgeons showed that intraperitoneal prophylactic drainage can not be omitted except for patients with low-risk of POPF after PD [23].

The types of drainage after pancreatectomy vary according to the surgeon’s preference. A retrospective study analyzed the efficacy of closed suction and gravity drainage after pancreatectomy. The results showed that suction drainage did not increase the incidence of POPF compared to gravity drainage [24]. Continuous drain irrigation is an effective risk mitigation strategy against POPF [25]. A recent study demonstrated that irrigation of the perianastomotic area significantly reduced the incidence of POPF after high-risk PD [26]. Our previous RCT study showed that irrigation did not reduce the incidence or severity of POPF after PD, but it significantly decreased the incidence of intra-abdominal infection [15].

Managing POPF presents a formidable challenge after PD. Peripancreatic fluid collections often requires invasive intervention, including percutaneous drainage [27], endoscopic ultrasound-guided drainage [28], open drainage [29], or even reoperation. Whereas, it is technical-challenging for intervention if the peripancreatic fluid collection occurs posterior to the pancreaticojejunostomy. The collected corrosive pancreatic juice might erode the adjacent PV/SMV system cause massive hemorrhage requiring emergency operation. Based on this, placing a drainage tube posterior to the pancreaticojejunostomy is reasonable. However, safety is essential for this drainage tube because of the posterior PV/SMV system. Therefore, a soft drainage tube with closed low suction pressure was placed posterior to the pancreaticojejunostomy for sufficient drainage for POPF in this study.

The efficacy of the drainage strategy can be attributed to several potential mechanisms in this study. There are numerous small holes along the PPJ tube, providing sufficient drainage pathways. The tube was connected with closed, low suction pressure, which was proved to be safe, though it was just placed in front of the PV/SMV without increasing the incidence of bleeding. The PPJ tube coursed inferior, posterior, and superior to the pancreaticojejunostomy, which means the tube can quickly and effectively drain the liquid in those directions and eliminate the potential risks of peripancreatic liquid collection. Meanwhile, the multi-functional tube was used for irrigation and suction once the drainage amylase was extraordinarily high or with the signs of infection. Additionally, the peri-anastomotic drainage system (PJ and PPJ tubes) can protect the pancreaticojejunostomy by adequate drainage and forms isolation from the BJ to prevent the potentially abnormally activated pancreatic digestion by bile juice/leakage. The PPD drainage may also act as a sentinel, providing early warning signs of anastomotic leakage through significantly elevated drain amylase levels or changes in drain output characteristics. It enables timely intervention and prevents the development of more severe complications, such as PPH. The value of drain amylase levels in predicting pancreatic fistula was highlighted by Teixeira et al. [30], further supporting the role of drains in the early detection and management of complications.

Our study showed that an additional PPJ tube remarkably decreased the severity of POPF and the incidence of POPF-related complications. The PPJ drainage significantly decreased the severity of POPF, and remarkably reduced the incidences of POPF-related complications, including PPH, reoperation, intrabdominal infection and DGE. These findings aligned with established knowledge, highlighted the crucial role of effective drainage management in optimizing postoperative outcomes after PD. The PPJ drainage did not increase other postoperative complications or prolong hospital stays, demonstrating its safety and feasibility in PD. Many studies have investigated the timing of drainage removal (early removal versus delayed removal) after pancreatectomy. Early drainage removal is associated with a reduction of surgical site infection after all types of pancreatectomy [31]. A recent multi-center RCT indicated that early drainage removal was safe for patients with low or intermediate risk of POPF after PD [32]. The median duration of removal of PPJ drainage was 14 days in the study. The volume of PPJ significantly decreased after POD 7 but usually with an elevated drain amylase level. Early removal of PPJ drainage would be adopted in the further study.

A routine postoperative computed tomography (CT) scan on POD6 or POD 7 was critical in detecting the signs of intra-abdominal liquid collection, abscess or POPF in this study. The position of drainage tubes was first adjusted regarding to the detection of the above signs. Therefore, the postoperative CT scan is to the benefit of reducing the severity of POPF and POPF-related complications. A recent study [33] demonstrated that postoperative computed tomography imaging played an essential role in avoiding failure-to-rescue after pancreatectomy. The early (postoperative day 3–7) CT group had shorter hospital stays and lower mortality than the delayed (postoperative day > 7) CT group. In line with the results, a routine postoperative CT scan was recommended.

Despite these encouraging results, our study has some limitations. Its retrospective design and single-center nature necessitate caution in generalizing the findings to a broader population. Future research should involve more considerable, prospective, multi-center studies to further validate this drainage system’s efficacy and safety across diverse patient populations and surgical settings. Comparative studies with other drainage techniques, including different drain types, placement locations, and management protocols, would provide valuable insights for refining drainage strategies and optimizing patient care. Furthermore, economic analyses are warranted to assess the system’s cost-effectiveness compared to traditional drainage methods.

Conclusion

The PPJ drainage posterior to pancreaticojejunostomy reduces the severity of POPF and the incidence of POPF-related complications after PD.
