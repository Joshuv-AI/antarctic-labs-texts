1.
Introduction

Denote by
\mathbb{N}
and
\mathbb{N}_{+}
the sets of all non-negative integers and all positive integers, respectively. For every
n\in\mathbb{N}
we denote by
\mathcal{S}_{n}
the set of all permutations of the set
\{1,\ldots,n\}
. For each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, let
\mathcal{D}(n)
denote the set of derangements of the set
\{1,\ldots,n\}
, that is, all the permutations in
\mathcal{S}_{n}
without fixed points:

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(n):=\big\{\ \sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{n}\ \big|\ \sigma(i)\neq i\textrm{ for all }i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}\ \big\}.

Let
d(n):=\#\mathcal{D}(n)
. The sequence
(d(n))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, called the sequence of derangements, appears in the context of various number sequences, for example in the paper of Sun and Zagier
[
8
]
, devoted to Bell numbers. Arithmetic properties of numbers of derangements were studied by Miska in
[
4
]
. In the same article arithmetic properties of numbers of even and odd derangements were investigated.

The numbers
d(n)
were generalized by Wang, Miska, and Mező in
[
9
]
. They introduced the notion of the number of
r
-derangements
d_{r}(n)
. The number
d_{r}(n)
is defined as the number of
r
-derangements of a set
\{1,\ldots,r+n\}
, i.e., derangements
\rho\in\mathcal{S}_{r+n}
such that any two numbers from the set
\{1,\ldots,r\}
lie in disjoint cycles of
\rho
written as a product of pairwise disjoint cycles. In particular,
d_{0}(n)=d(n)
and
d_{1}(n)=d(n+1)
. It is worth noting that not just the numbers of derangements were generalized in that manner. For other examples of
r
-generalizations of some combinatorial objects, see
[
2
,
6
,
7
]
.

Capparelli, Ferrari, Munarini, and Zagaglia Salvi in
[
3
]
studied the generalized derangement numbers
d_{n}^{(r)}
defined as numbers of fixed-point-free bijections between two sets with
n+r
elements that have exactly
n
elements in common. More generally, they were interested in the generalized recontres numbers
d_{n,m}^{(r)}
that count bijections between two
(n+r)
-element sets with
n
-element intersection which have exactly
m
fixed points. Their work was motivated by the article of Beggas, Ferrari, and Zagaglia Salvi
[
1
]
, who investigated the numbers of widened permutations, i.e., the numbers of bijections between two sets of cardinality
n+1
that have exactly
n
-elements in common.

Another generalization of derangements was proposed recently in a paper by Moshtagh and Fallah-Moghaddam
[
5
]
. They considered the numbers
d(r,n)
of so-called block derangements, that is, fixed-point-free permutations
\sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{r+n}
such that
\sigma(i)\not\in\{1,\ldots,r\}
for any
i\in\{1,\ldots,r\}
. Their main result is that
d(r,n)=r!d_{r}(n)
for every
r,n\in\mathbb{N}
.

Motivated by the aforementioned results, we introduce and study two new classes of sequences of derangements, where the first one generalizes block derangement numbers
d(r,n)
and the other one extends the notion of
r
-derangement numbers
d_{r}(n)
. Moreover, we shall see that generalized recontres numbers
d_{n,m}^{(r)}
can be represented as sums of certain numbers from the first class (see Proposition
2.1
). Our goal is to prove a strict connection between those two classes with the use of a combinatorial argument. We also study some further variations of these sequences by fixing the number of disjoint cycles in the counted permutations, their parity, or both.

We introduce the main definitions in the next section. In Section
3
we define the main tool used in our proofs: the class of cycle-splitting functions
{\bf T}_{\mathcal{A}}
. We also prove the crucial Lemma
3.1
. The main result, Theorem
4.1
, is presented in Section
4
. In this part, we rely on counting arguments. A different approach, based on (exponential) generating functions can be found in Section
5
. In Section
6
we find strict connections between our new sequences and the sequences of
r
-derangements
(d_{r}(n))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
. The last section is devoted to study of
r
-derangements with fixed parity. In particular, we present explicit formulae in Theorem
7.7
.

2.
Notation

For a permutation
\sigma
define its parity
\textrm{par}\hskip 1.42271pt\sigma
as follows:

\displaystyle\textrm{par}\hskip 1.42271pt\sigma:=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}0,&\hskip 5.69046pt\textrm{ if }\sigma\textrm{ is even},\\
1,&\hskip 5.69046pt\textrm{ if }\sigma\textrm{ is odd}.\end{array}\right.

Let
\mathcal{D}(r,u,m,n)
be the set of permutations
\sigma
of the set
\{1,\ldots,r+n\}
such that
\#(\sigma(\{1,\ldots,r\})\cap\{1,\ldots,r\})=u
and exactly
m
elements from the set
\{r+1,\ldots,r+n\}
are fixed points of
\sigma
. In other words,

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(r,u,m,n)

\displaystyle:=\big\{\ \sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{r+n}\ \big|\ \sigma(x)=y\textrm{ for exactly }u\textrm{ pairs }(x,y)\textrm{ such that }1\leq x,y\leq r\textrm{ and}

\displaystyle\hskip 75.39963pt\sigma(t)=t\textrm{ for exactly }m\textrm{ elements }r+1\leq t\leq r+n\ \big\}.

Put
d(r,u,m,n)=\#\mathcal{D}(r,u,m,n)
. Moreover, we define

•

\mathcal{D}_{k}(r,u,m,n)
as the subset of
\mathcal{D}(r,u,m,n)
containing precisely the permutations such that
1,\ldots,r
lie in exactly
k
disjoint cycles,

•

\mathcal{D}^{(i)}(r,u,m,n)
as the subset of
\mathcal{D}(r,u,m,n)
containing precisely the permutations of parity equal to
i
,

•

\mathcal{D}_{k}^{(i)}(r,u,m,n)
as the subset of
\mathcal{D}(r,u,m,n)
containing precisely the permutations of parity equal to
i
and such that
1,\ldots,r
lie in exactly
k
disjoint cycles.

Moreover, the numbers
d_{k}(r,u,m,n)
,
d^{(i)}(r,u,m,n)
,
d_{k}^{(i)}(r,u,m,n)
are the cardinalities of the sets
\mathcal{D}_{k}(r,u,m,n)
,
\mathcal{D}^{(i)}(r,u,m,n)
, and
\mathcal{D}_{k}^{(i)}(r,u,m,n)
, respectively.

It follows from the very definition that
d(r,0,0,n)=d(r,n)
is the number of block derangements. The following identity is a bit less obvious but still easy to prove.

Proposition 2.1
.

For each
n,m,r\in\mathbb{N}
we have

\displaystyle d_{n,m}^{(r)}=\sum_{u=0}^{r}d(r,u,m,n).

Proof.

Note that
\sum_{u=0}^{r}d(r,u,m,n)
is the number of all the permutations of a set with
n+r
elements that have exactly
m
fixed points among
r+1,\ldots r+n
. This is the same as the number of bijections between two
(n+r)
-element sets with
n
-element intersection that have exactly
m
fixed points. The latter number is
d_{n,m}^{(r)}
from the definition.
∎

The above proposition shows that the numbers
d(r,u,m,n)
carry more detailed information than
d_{n,m}^{(r)}
. Thus, they may be treated as a generalization or refinement of generalized recontres numbers.

Let
\mathcal{D}_{r,u,m}(n)
be the set of permutations
\sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{r+n}
such that every two distinct elements of the set
\{1,\ldots,r\}
are in two disjoint cycles of
\sigma
and exactly
u
elements from the set
\{1,\ldots,r\}
and
m
elements from the set
\{r+1,\ldots,r+n\}
are fixed points of
\sigma
. That is,

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{r,u,m}(n)

\displaystyle:=\big\{\ \sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{r+n}\ \big|\ \forall_{1\leq x<y\leq r}\ x\textrm{ and }y\textrm{ are in disjoint cycles of }\sigma,\textrm{ and}

\displaystyle\hskip 75.39963pt\sigma(z)=z\textrm{ for exactly }u\textrm{ elements }1\leq z\leq r,\textrm{ and}

\displaystyle\hskip 75.39963pt\sigma(t)=t\textrm{ for exactly }m\textrm{ elements }r+1\leq t\leq r+n\ \big\}.

By
\mathcal{D}_{r,u,m}^{(i)}(n)
we mean the subset of
\mathcal{D}_{r,u,m}(n)
containing permutations of parity equal to
i
. Put
d_{r,u,m}(n)=\#\mathcal{D}_{r,u,m}(n)
and
d_{r,u,m}^{(i)}(n)=\#\mathcal{D}_{r,u,m}^{(i)}(n)
. If
m=u=0
, then we recover
r
-derangements and thus write
\mathcal{D}_{r}(n)
,
d_{r}(n)
,
\mathcal{D}_{r}^{(i)}(n)
and
d_{r}^{(i)}(n)
instead of
\mathcal{D}_{r,0,0}(n)
,
d_{r,0,0}(n)
,
\mathcal{D}_{r,0,0}^{(i)}(n)
and
d_{r,0,0}^{(i)}(n)
, respectively. If additionally
r=0
, then we will write
\mathcal{D}(n)
,
d(n)
,
\mathcal{D}^{(i)}(n)
and
d^{(i)}(n)
, for short.

In the sequel, we will use unsigned Stirling numbers of the first kind, denoted by
\left[\begin{array}[]{c}r\\
k\end{array}\right]
for natural numbers
r
and
k
. They are defined as the coefficients of rising factorials:

\displaystyle(x)^{(r)}:=\prod_{j=0}^{r-1}(x+j)=\sum_{k=0}^{r}\left[\begin{array}[]{c}r\\
k\end{array}\right]x^{k}.

Equivalently,
\left[\begin{array}[]{c}r\\
k\end{array}\right]
is equal to the number of permutations of the set
\{1,\ldots,r\}
with exactly
k
disjoint cycles. In particular,

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{r}\left[\begin{array}[]{c}r\\
k\end{array}\right]=r!,

because this is the number of all permutations of
\{1,\ldots,r\}
.

For an illustration, let us list all the elements of the sets
\mathcal{D}(2,1,1,3)
and
\mathcal{D}_{2,1,1}(3)
, respectively. All the permutations below are written in the form of products of pairwise disjoint cycles.

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(2,1,1,3)

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cccccc}(234),&(235),&(243),&(245),&(253),&(254),\\
(134),&(135),&(143),&(145),&(153),&(154),\\
(1234),&(1235),&(1243),&(1245),&(1253),&(1254),\\
(2134),&(2135),&(2143),&(2145),&(2153),&(2154)\end{array}\right\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(2,1,1,3)

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cccccc}(234),&(235),&(243),&(245),&(253),&(254),\\
(134),&(135),&(143),&(145),&(153),&(154)\end{array}\right\}.

We can see above that the set
\mathcal{D}(2,1,1,3)
contains twice as many elements as
\mathcal{D}_{2,1,1}(3)
(that is,
24
elements vs
12
elements). As it turns out, in general the cardinality of
\mathcal{D}(r,u,m,n)
is
r!
times greater than the cardinality of
\mathcal{D}_{r,u,m}(n)
(see Theorem
4.1
(
5
)).

We have used PARI/GP code (written for us by Pavlo Yatsyna) to calculate the numbers
d(r,u,m,n)
and
d_{r,u,m}(n)
for small cases. Specifically, we chose
r\in\{2,3\}
,
u,m\in\{0,1,2\}
and
n\in\{0,1,2,3,4,5,6,7\}
. In fact, we managed to do that in two ways: first, by computing the sets
\mathcal{D}(r,u,m,n)
and
\mathcal{D}_{r,u,m}(n)
directly; next, by using the explicit formulae

\displaystyle d_{r,u,m}(n)=\binom{r}{u}\binom{n}{m}\sum_{j=r-u}^{n-m}\binom{j}{r-u}\frac{(n-m)!}{(n-m-j)!}(-1)^{n-m-j}

and
d(r,u,m,n)=r!d_{r,u,m}(n)
, which are shown in our Theorem
4.1
(
5
) and Corollary
6.2
. Therefore, we have verified the result numerically in the presented range.

3.
Main Lemma

Let us fix numbers
r,u,m,n\in\mathbb{N}
and a subset
\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathcal{D}(r,u,m,n)
. Let us consider a function
{\bf T}_{\mathcal{A}}:\mathcal{A}\to\mathcal{D}_{r,u,m}(n)
constructed in the following way. If
\sigma\in\mathcal{A}
, then we write
\sigma
as a product of pairwise disjoint cycles and begin each cycle from its least element. Next, every cycle in
\sigma
having more than one element from the set
\{1,\ldots,r\}
splits into several cycles as follows. Denote all the elements of the set
\{1,\ldots,r\}
contained in the cycle by
a_{1},\ldots,a_{s}
and denote the cycle in the form

(a_{1}b_{1,1}\ldots b_{1,t_{1}}a_{2}b_{2,1}\ldots b_{2,t_{2}}\ldots a_{s}b_{s,1}\ldots b_{s,t_{s}}),

where
s>1
and
t_{1},\ldots,t_{s}\in\mathbb{N}
. Next, we split the cycle:

(a_{1}b_{1,1}\ldots b_{1,t_{1}}a_{2}b_{2,1}\ldots b_{2,t_{2}}\ldots a_{s}b_{s,1}\ldots b_{s,t_{s}})\mapsto(a_{1}b_{1,1}\ldots b_{1,t_{1}})(a_{2}b_{2,1}\ldots b_{2,t_{2}})\cdots(a_{s}b_{s,1}\ldots b_{s,t_{s}}).

Finally, our permutation
{\bf T}_{\mathcal{A}}(\sigma)
is the permutation obtained from
\sigma
by splitting all its cycles containing more than one element from the set
\{1,\ldots,r\}
as above.

The following fact will be crucial for our argument.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
\rho\in\mathcal{D}_{r,u,m}(n)
. Let
\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathcal{D}(r,u,m,n)
be such that for every
\sigma\in\mathcal{A}
the number of disjoint cycles of
\sigma
containing numbers from
\{1,\ldots,r\}
satisfies a fixed condition
w
(i.e., the condition
w
may mean for example the conditions like ‘having some fixed value’ or ‘laying in some prescribed residue class modulo some positive integer’;
w
may depend on
\rho
). Then

\displaystyle\#{\bf T}_{\mathcal{A}}^{-1}(\rho)=r!\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j_{1},\ldots,j_{r}\in\mathbb{N}\\
j_{1}+2j_{2}+\cdots+rj_{r}=r\\
j_{1}+\cdots+j_{r}\ {\rm satisfies}\ w\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{\prod_{i=1}^{r}i^{j_{i}}j_{i}!}.

Proof.

We can create
\sigma\in\mathcal{D}(r,u,m,n)
such that
{\bf T}_{\mathcal{A}}(\sigma)=\rho
by gluing some cycles of
\rho
containing elements from the set
\{1,\ldots,r\}
according to the recipe:

(a_{1}b_{1,1}\ldots b_{1,t_{1}})(a_{2}b_{2,1}\ldots b_{2,t_{2}})\cdots(a_{s}b_{s,1}\ldots b_{s,t_{s}})\mapsto(a_{1}b_{1,1}\ldots b_{1,t_{1}}a_{2}b_{2,1}\ldots b_{2,t_{2}}\ldots a_{s}b_{s,1}\ldots b_{s,t_{s}}).

Let us compute in how many ways we can create
\sigma
from
\rho
as above.

We have
r
pairwise disjoint cycles in
\rho
containing elements of the set
\{1,\ldots,r\}
, as
\rho\in\mathcal{D}_{r,u,m}(n)
. Assume that we intend to partition these cycles into
j_{1}
subsets of cardinality
1
,
j_{2}
subsets of cardinality
2
,
j_{3}
subsets of cardinality
3,\ldots,j_{r}
subsets of cardinality
r
. We can do it in
\frac{r!}{(1!)^{j_{1}}j_{1}!\cdot(2!)^{j_{2}}j_{2}!\cdot(3!)^{j_{3}}j_{3}!\cdots(r!)^{j_{r}}j_{r}!}
ways. In each subset of
i
cycles we take as the first the cycle with the least element from the set
\{1,\ldots,r\}
and order the remaining cycles in
(i-1)!
ways. Next, we glue the cycles in this order. Let us notice that fixing the cycle with the least element as the first one in the ordering makes it so each cycle of
\sigma
can be attained by gluing from exactly one ordering. Notice that the assumption that
\sigma\in\mathcal{A}
implies that
j_{1}+\cdots+j_{r}
has to satisfy
w
. Summing up, the number of ways of creating
\sigma
from
\rho
is equal to

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j_{1},\ldots,j_{r}\in\mathbb{N}\\
j_{1}+2j_{2}+\cdots+rj_{r}=r\\
j_{1}+\cdots+j_{r}\textrm{ satisfies }w\end{subarray}}\frac{r!}{\prod_{i=1}^{r}(i!)^{j_{i}}j_{i}!}\cdot\prod_{i=1}^{r}((i-1)!)^{j_{i}}=r!\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j_{1},\ldots,j_{r}\in\mathbb{N}\\
j_{1}+2j_{2}+\cdots+rj_{r}=r\\
j_{1}+\cdots+j_{r}\textrm{ satisfies }w\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{\prod_{i=1}^{r}i^{j_{i}}j_{i}!}.

∎

4.
Main result

Our main result is the following.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let us fix
r,u,m,n,k\in\mathbb{N}
and
i\in\{0,1\}
. Then, we have

\displaystyle d_{k}(r,u,m,n)=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}r\\
k\end{array}\right]d_{r,u,m}(n).

In particular,

(5)

\displaystyle d(r,u,m,n)=r!d_{r,u,m}(n).

Moreover,

(6)

\displaystyle d^{(i)}(r,u,m,n)=\begin{cases}\frac{r!}{2}d_{r,u,m}(n),&\mbox{ if }r\geq 2,\\
d_{r,u,m}^{(i)}(n),&\mbox{ if }r\in\{0,1\},\end{cases}

and

\displaystyle d_{k}^{(i)}(r,u,m,n)=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}r\\
k\end{array}\right]d^{((r+k+i)\bmod{2})}_{r,u,m}(n).

Proof.

Lemma
3.1
applied to
\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{A}_{1}:=\mathcal{D}_{k}(r,u,m,n)
(that is, with the condition
w:=
‘be equal to
k
’) implies

\displaystyle\#{\bf T}_{\mathcal{A}_{1}}^{-1}(\rho)=r!\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j_{1},\ldots,j_{r}\in\mathbb{N}\\
j_{1}+2j_{2}+\cdots+rj_{r}=r\\
j_{1}+\cdots+j_{r}=k\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{\prod_{i=1}^{r}i^{j_{i}}j_{i}!}=:f(r,k)

for any
\rho\in\mathcal{D}_{r,u,m}(n)
. On the other hand,

d_{k}(r,u,m,n)=\sum_{\rho\in\mathcal{D}_{r,u,m}(n)}\#{\bf T}_{\mathcal{A}_{1}}^{-1}(\rho)=\sum_{\rho\in\mathcal{D}_{r,u,m}(n)}f(r,k)=f(r,k)d_{r,u,m}(n).

Observe that
f(r,k)
depends exclusively on
r
and
k
. Hence, substituting
u=r
and
m=n=0
we get
f(r,k)=f(r,k)d_{r,r,0}(0)=d_{k}(r,r,0,0)=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}r\\
k\end{array}\right]
. This concludes the proof of the equality
d_{k}(r,u,m,n)=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}r\\
k\end{array}\right]d_{r,u,m}(n)
for all
r,u,m,n,k\in\mathbb{N}
.

In order to prove (
5
) it is enough to sum both sides of (
4.1
) over
k\in\{0\ldots,r\}
. Indeed, we get

\displaystyle d(r,u,m,n)=\sum_{k=0}^{r}d_{k}(r,u,m,n)=\left(\sum_{k=0}^{r}\left[\begin{array}[]{c}r\\
k\end{array}\right]\right)d_{r,u,m}(n)=r!d_{r,u,m}(n).

The last equality follows from (
2
).

Let us move to the proof of (
6
). For any permutation
\sigma
of the set
\{1,\ldots,r+n\}
let
k_{\sigma}
denote the number of disjoint cycles in
\sigma
and
k^{\prime}_{\sigma}
denote the number of those cycles of
\sigma
which contain at least one element of
\{1,\ldots,r\}
. For any permutation
\rho\in\mathcal{D}_{r,u,m}(n)
and
i\in\{0,1\}
let
\mathcal{A}_{\rho,i}
be the set of all permutations
\sigma\in\mathcal{D}(r,u,m,n)
such that

(9)

\displaystyle k_{\sigma}^{\prime}\equiv r+i+\textrm{par}\hskip 1.42271pt\rho\pmod{2}.

Suppose that
\sigma\in{\bf T}_{\mathcal{D}(r,u,m,n)}^{-1}(\rho)
. Then, using the fact that
k_{\rho}^{\prime}=r
(because
\rho\in\mathcal{D}_{r,u,m}(n)
) and the well-known property

\displaystyle\textrm{par}\hskip 1.42271pt\sigma\equiv k_{\sigma}+r+n\pmod{2},

we obtain that

(10)

\displaystyle\textrm{par}\hskip 1.42271pt\rho\equiv k_{\rho}+r+n=k_{\rho}-k_{\sigma}+\big(k_{\sigma}+r+n\big)\equiv k_{\rho}-k_{\sigma}+\textrm{par}\hskip 1.42271pt\sigma=r-k^{\prime}_{\sigma}+\textrm{par}\hskip 1.42271pt\sigma\pmod{2},

where the last equality follows from the fact that
\sigma
and
\rho
have exactly the same cycles that do not contain any of the numbers
1,\ldots,r
. Therefore, the condition (
9
) is equivalent to
\textrm{par}\hskip 1.42271pt\sigma=i
. Indeed, it follows from (
10
) that

(11)

\displaystyle\textrm{par}\hskip 1.42271pt\sigma\equiv\textrm{par}\hskip 1.42271pt\rho+r+k^{\prime}_{\sigma}\pmod{2}.

So, if (
9
) holds, then inserting
r+i+\textrm{par}\hskip 1.42271pt\rho
in the place of
k^{\prime}_{\sigma}
in (
11
) gives us the equality
\textrm{par}\hskip 1.42271pt\sigma=i
. On the other hand, from (
10
) we conclude that

\displaystyle k^{\prime}_{\sigma}\equiv\textrm{par}\hskip 1.42271pt\rho+\textrm{par}\hskip 1.42271pt\sigma+r\pmod{2}.

Then, if
\textrm{par}\hskip 1.42271pt\sigma=i
, we get (
9
). Summing up, for every
\rho\in\mathcal{D}_{r,u,m}(n)
we have
\mathcal{A}_{\rho,i}=\mathcal{D}^{(i)}(r,u,m,n)
.

We now apply Lemma
3.1
to the subset
\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{A}_{\rho,i}
. Hence, the number of ways of creating a partition
\sigma
with
\textrm{par}\hskip 1.42271pt\sigma=i
from
\rho
is equal to

\displaystyle\#{\bf T}_{\mathcal{A}_{\rho,i}}^{-1}(\rho)

\displaystyle=r!\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j_{1},\ldots,j_{r}\in\mathbb{N}\\
j_{1}+2j_{2}+\ldots+rj_{r}=r\\
j_{1}+\cdots+j_{r}\equiv r+i+\textrm{par}\hskip 1.42271pt\rho\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 2)\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{\prod_{i=1}^{r}i^{j_{i}}j_{i}!}.

For
\varepsilon\in\{0,1\}
let

\displaystyle f_{\varepsilon}(r):=r!\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j_{1},\ldots,j_{r}\in\mathbb{N}\\
j_{1}+2j_{2}+\cdots+rj_{r}=r\\
j_{1}+\cdots+j_{r}\equiv\varepsilon\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 2)\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{\prod_{i=1}^{r}i^{j_{i}}j_{i}!}.

Then,

\displaystyle d^{(i)}(r,u,m,n)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\rho\in\mathcal{D}_{r,u,m}(n)}\#{\bf T}_{\mathcal{D}^{(i)}(r,u,m,n)}^{-1}(\rho)=\sum_{\rho\in\mathcal{D}_{r,u,m}(n)}\#{\bf T}_{\mathcal{A}_{\rho,i}}^{-1}(\rho)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\rho\in\mathcal{D}_{r,u,m}(n)}r!\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j_{1},\ldots,j_{r}\in\mathbb{N}\\
j_{1}+2j_{2}+\cdots+rj_{r}=r\\
j_{1}+\cdots+j_{r}\equiv r+i+\textrm{par}\hskip 1.42271pt\rho\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 2)\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{\prod_{i=1}^{r}i^{j_{i}}j_{i}!}

\displaystyle=f_{0}(r)\cdot\#\{\rho\in\mathcal{D}_{r,u,m}(n)\ |\ \textrm{par}\hskip 1.42271pt\rho\equiv r+i\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 2)\}

\displaystyle\hskip 28.45274pt+f_{1}(r)\cdot\#\{\rho\in\mathcal{D}_{r,u,m}(n)\ |\ \textrm{par}\hskip 1.42271pt\rho\equiv r+i+1\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 2)\}

\displaystyle=f_{0}(r)d_{r,u,m}^{((r+i)\bmod{2})}(n)+f_{1}(r)d_{r,u,m}^{((r+i+1)\bmod{2})}(n).

Let us consider two cases. First, suppose that
i\equiv r\pmod{2}
. Because both numbers
f_{0}(r)
and
f_{1}(r)
depend only on
r
, if we put
u=r
and
m=n=0
, we immediately get

\displaystyle f_{0}(r)=d^{(r\bmod 2)}(r,r,0,0)=\begin{cases}\frac{r!}{2},&\mbox{ if }r\geq 2,\\
1-r,&\mbox{ if }r\in\{0,1\}.\end{cases}

On the other hand, observe that by (
5
) we have
f_{0}(r)+f_{1}(r)=r!
. Hence, for
r\geq 2
we get
f_{0}(r)=f_{1}(r)=\frac{r!}{2}
, and finally

\displaystyle d^{(i)}(r,u,m,n)=

\displaystyle\ \frac{r!}{2}\big(d_{r,u,m}^{((r+i)\bmod{2})}(n)+d_{r,u,m}^{((r+i+1)\bmod{2})}(n)\big)=\frac{r!}{2}d_{r,u,m}(n).

If
r\in\{0,1\}
, then
f_{0}(r)=1-r
and
f_{1}(r)=r
. Thus,

\displaystyle d^{(i)}(r,u,m,n)=

\displaystyle\ (1-r)d_{r,u,m}^{((r+i)\bmod{2})}(n)+rd_{r,u,m}^{((r+i+1)\bmod{2})}(n)=d_{r,u,m}^{(i)}(n),

because we assume
i\in\{0,1\}
.

In the second case, that is
i\equiv r+1\pmod{2}
, we proceed analogously and obtain exactly the same formulae.

For the proof of (
4.1
) we apply Lemma
3.1
to the subset
\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{A}_{\rho,i,k}
of
\mathcal{D}_{k}^{(i)}(r,u,m,n)
containing all the permutations
\sigma
such that the number of the cycles of
\sigma
which contain at least one element of
\{1,\ldots,r\}
is equal to
k
and congruent to
r+i+\textrm{par}\hskip 1.42271pt\rho
modulo
2
(that is, with the condition
w:=
‘be equal to
k
and equivalent to
r+i+\textrm{par}\hskip 1.42271pt\rho
modulo
2
’). Hence, the number of ways of creating
\sigma\in\mathcal{D}_{k}^{(i)}(r,u,m,n)
from
\rho
is equal to

\displaystyle\#{\bf T}_{\mathcal{D}_{k}^{(i)}(r,u,m,n)}^{-1}(\rho)

\displaystyle=\#{\bf T}_{\mathcal{A}_{\rho,i,k}}^{-1}(\rho)=r!\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j_{1},\ldots,j_{r}\in\mathbb{N}\\
j_{1}+2j_{2}+\ldots+rj_{r}=r\\
j_{1}+\cdots+j_{r}=k\\
j_{1}+\cdots+j_{r}\equiv r+i+\textrm{par}\hskip 1.42271pt\rho\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 2)\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{\prod_{i=1}^{r}i^{j_{i}}j_{i}!}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}f(r,k),&\mbox{ if }\textrm{par}\hskip 1.42271pt\rho\equiv r+k+i\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 2)\\
0,&\mbox{ if }\textrm{par}\hskip 1.42271pt\rho\equiv r+k+i+1\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 2)\end{cases}.

where
f(r,k)=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}r\\
k\end{array}\right]
. Hence,

\displaystyle d_{k}^{(i)}(r,u,m,n)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\rho\in\mathcal{D}_{r,u,m}(n)}{\bf T}_{\mathcal{D}_{k}^{(i)}(r,u,m,n)}^{-1}(\rho)=\sum_{\rho\in\mathcal{D}_{r,u,m}(n)}{\bf T}_{\mathcal{A}_{\rho,i,k}}^{-1}(\rho)

\displaystyle=f(r,k)\cdot\#\{\rho\in\mathcal{D}_{r,u,m}(n)\ |\ \textrm{par}\hskip 1.42271pt\rho\equiv r+k+i\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 2)\}

\displaystyle=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}r\\
k\end{array}\right]d_{r,u,m}^{((r+k+i)\bmod{2})}(n).

The proof is complete.
∎

5.
Approach via Power Series

It is worth noting that the values
f(r,k)
from the proof of Theorem
4.1
can be computed with the use of power series. In fact, the following is true.

Proposition 5.1
.

For each
r\in\mathbb{N}
we have

(12)

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j_{1},\ldots,j_{r}\in\mathbb{N}\\
j_{1}+2j_{2}+\cdots+rj_{r}=r\end{subarray}}\left(\prod_{k=1}^{r}k^{j_{k}}j_{k}!\right)^{-1}=

\displaystyle\ 1,

(13)

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j_{1},\ldots,j_{r}\in\mathbb{N}\\
j_{1}+2j_{2}+\cdots+rj_{r}=r\\
2\mid j_{1}+\cdots+j_{r}\end{subarray}}\left(\prod_{k=1}^{r}k^{j_{k}}j_{k}!\right)^{-1}=

\displaystyle\ \begin{cases}1-r&\mbox{ for }r\in\{0,1\}\\
\frac{1}{2}&\mbox{ for }r\geq 2\end{cases},

(14)

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j_{1},\ldots,j_{r}\in\mathbb{N}\\
j_{1}+2j_{2}+\cdots+rj_{r}=r\\
2\nmid j_{1}+\cdots+j_{r}\end{subarray}}\left(\prod_{k=1}^{r}k^{j_{k}}j_{k}!\right)^{-1}=

\displaystyle\ \begin{cases}r&\mbox{ for }r\in\{0,1\}\\
\frac{1}{2}&\mbox{ for }r\geq 2\end{cases}.

In general,

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j_{1},\ldots,j_{r}\in\mathbb{N}\\
j_{1}+2j_{2}+\cdots+rj_{r}=r\\
j_{1}+\cdots+j_{r}=k\end{subarray}}\left(\prod_{k=1}^{r}k^{j_{k}}j_{k}!\right)^{-1}=\frac{\left[\begin{array}[]{c}r\\
k\end{array}\right]}{r!}

for each
r,k\in\mathbb{N}
.

In particular, if
d\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
and
c\in\{0,\ldots,d-1\}
, then

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j_{1},\ldots,j_{r}\in\mathbb{N}\\
j_{1}+2j_{2}+\cdots+rj_{r}=r\\
j_{1}+\cdots+j_{r}\equiv c\ (\mathrm{mod}\ d)\end{subarray}}\left(\prod_{k=1}^{r}k^{j_{k}}j_{k}!\right)^{-1}=\frac{1}{r!}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq k\leq r\\
k\equiv c\ (\mathrm{mod}\ d)\end{subarray}}\left[\begin{array}[]{c}r\\
k\end{array}\right].

Proof.

Let

\mathbb{N}^{(\mathbb{N}_{+})}=\{(j_{l})_{l\in\mathbb{N}_{+}}\ |\ \forall_{l\in\mathbb{N}_{+}}\ j_{l}\in\mathbb{N}\ \textrm{ and }\ j_{l}=0\ \textrm{ for all but finitely many $l$}\}.

Consider the power series

F(x,y)=\sum_{r=0}^{+\infty}\sum_{k=0}^{+\infty}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j_{1},\ldots,j_{r}\in\mathbb{N}\\
j_{1}+2j_{2}+\cdots+rj_{r}=r\\
j_{1}+\cdots+j_{r}=k\end{subarray}}\left(\prod_{l=1}^{r}l^{j_{l}}j_{l}!\right)^{-1}x^{r}y^{k}.

Compute:

\displaystyle F(x,y)=

\displaystyle\ \sum_{r=0}^{+\infty}\sum_{k=0}^{+\infty}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j_{1},\ldots,j_{r}\in\mathbb{N}\\
j_{1}+2j_{2}+\cdots+rj_{r}=r\\
j_{1}+\cdots+j_{r}=k\end{subarray}}\left(\prod_{l=1}^{r}l^{j_{l}}j_{l}!\right)^{-1}x^{r}y^{k}=\sum_{(j_{l})_{l\in\mathbb{N}_{+}}\in\mathbb{N}^{(\mathbb{N}_{+})}}\frac{x^{\sum_{l=1}^{+\infty}lj_{l}}y^{\sum_{l=1}^{+\infty}j_{l}}}{\prod_{l=1}^{+\infty}l^{j_{l}}j_{l}!}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{(j_{l})_{l\in\mathbb{N}_{+}}\in\mathbb{N}^{(\mathbb{N}_{+})}}\prod_{l=1}^{+\infty}\frac{x^{lj_{l}}y^{j_{l}}}{l^{j_{l}}j_{l}!}=\prod_{l=1}^{+\infty}\sum_{j_{l}=0}^{+\infty}\frac{1}{j_{l}!}\left(\frac{x^{l}y}{l}\right)^{j_{l}}=\prod_{l=1}^{+\infty}e^{\frac{x^{l}y}{l}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ e^{\sum_{l=1}^{+\infty}\frac{x^{l}y}{l}}=e^{y\log\frac{1}{1-x}}=\left(\frac{1}{1-x}\right)^{y}.

Note that

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j_{1},\ldots,j_{r}\in\mathbb{N}\\
j_{1}+2j_{2}+\cdots+rj_{r}=r\\
2\mid j_{1}+\cdots+j_{r}\end{subarray}}\left(\prod_{k=1}^{r}k^{j_{k}}j_{k}!\right)^{-1}x^{r}=\frac{1}{2}(F(x,1)+F(x,-1))=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{1}{1-x}+1-x\right)=1+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{r=2}^{+\infty}x^{r}.

Comparing the coefficients, we get the equality (
13
) for each
r\in\mathbb{N}
. Analogously,

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j_{1},\ldots,j_{r}\in\mathbb{N}\\
j_{1}+2j_{2}+\cdots+rj_{r}=r\\
2\nmid j_{1}+\cdots+j_{r}\end{subarray}}\left(\prod_{k=1}^{r}k^{j_{k}}j_{k}!\right)^{-1}x^{r}=\frac{1}{2}(F(x,1)-F(x,-1))=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{1}{1-x}-(1-x)\right)=x+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{r=2}^{+\infty}x^{r},

which proves (
14
). At last, (
12
) follows easily from summing (
13
) and (
14
) or from the consideration of the power series
F(x,1)
.

The identity (
5.1
) follows from the fact that

\displaystyle F(x,y)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1}{1-x}\right)^{y}=(1-x)^{-y}=\sum_{r=0}^{+\infty}\binom{-y}{r}(-x)^{r}

\displaystyle=\sum_{r=0}^{+\infty}\frac{(-y)_{(r)}}{r!}(-x)^{r}=\sum_{r=0}^{+\infty}\frac{(y)^{(r)}}{r!}x^{r}=\sum_{r=0}^{+\infty}\sum_{k=1}^{r}\frac{\left[\begin{array}[]{c}r\\
k\end{array}\right]}{r!}x^{r}y^{k},

where
(x)_{(r)}=\prod_{j=0}^{r-1}(x-j)
and
(x)^{(r)}=\prod_{j=0}^{r-1}(x+j)
are falling and rising factorials, respectively. Then, (
5.1
) is a direct consequence of (
5.1
).
∎

6.
Reduction to the numbers of
r
-derangements

The following result shows that studying arithmetic properties of the numbers
d_{r,u,m}(n)
and
d_{r,u,m}^{(i)}(n)
comes down to studying corresponding ones for the numbers
d_{r}(n)
and
d_{r}^{(i)}(n)
.

Proposition 6.1
.

For each
r,u,m,n\in\mathbb{N}
and
i\in\{0,1\}
we have

\displaystyle d_{r,u,m}(n)=\binom{r}{u}\binom{n}{m}d_{r-u}(n-m)

and

\displaystyle d_{r,u,m}^{(i)}(n)=\binom{r}{u}\binom{n}{m}d_{r-u}^{(i)}(n-m).

Proof.

Let
\sigma\in\mathcal{D}_{r,u,m}(n)
. After choosing
u
fixed points of
\sigma
from the set
\{1,\ldots,r\}
and
m
fixed points of
\sigma
from the set
\{r+1,\ldots,r+n\}
we can treat
\sigma
as a fixed-point-free permutation of a set with
(r-u)+(n-m)
elements. Note that omitting fixed points of
\sigma
does not affect its parity.
∎

With the use of the above proposition and an exact formula for the
r
-derangement numbers
[
9
, Theorem 4, equation (9)]
we immediately obtain an exact formula for the numbers
d_{r,u,m}(n)
.

Corollary 6.2
.

For each
r,u,m,n\in\mathbb{N}
and we have

\displaystyle d_{r,u,m}(n)=\binom{r}{u}\binom{n}{m}\sum_{j=r-u}^{n-m}\binom{j}{r-u}\frac{(n-m)!}{(n-m-j)!}(-1)^{n-m-j}.

At this moment our main result can be rewritten in the following way.

Corollary 6.3
.

Let
r,u,m,n,k\in\mathbb{N}
and
i\in\{0,1\}
. Then, we have

\displaystyle d_{k}(r,u,m,n)=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}r\\
k\end{array}\right]\binom{r}{u}\binom{n}{m}d_{r-u}(n-m).

In particular,

\displaystyle d(r,u,m,n)=r!\binom{r}{u}\binom{n}{m}d_{r-u}(n-m).

Moreover,

\displaystyle d^{(i)}(r,u,m,n)=\begin{cases}\frac{r!}{2}\binom{r}{u}\binom{n}{m}d_{r-u}(n-m),&\mbox{ if }r\geq 2,\\
\binom{r}{u}\binom{n}{m}d_{r-u}^{(i)}(n-m),&\mbox{ if }r\in\{0,1\},\end{cases}

and

\displaystyle d_{k}^{(i)}(r,u,m,n)=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}r\\
k\end{array}\right]\binom{r}{u}\binom{n}{m}d_{r-u}^{((r+k+i)\bmod{2})}(n-m).

7.
Formulae for numbers of even/odd
r
-derangements and their exponential generating functions

The properties of numbers of
r
-derangements are widely studied in
[
9
]
. However, the numbers of even and odd
r
-derangements
d_{r}^{(i)}(n)
,
i\in\{0,1\}
, have not been investigated up to now. This is why we state and prove some basic facts involving them. The first result is an analogue of
[
9
, Theorem 2]
.

Theorem 7.1
.

For each
r,n\in\mathbb{N}
and
i\in\{0,1\}
we have

\displaystyle d_{r}^{(i)}(n)=

\displaystyle rd_{r-1}^{(1-i)}(n-1)+(n-1)d_{r}^{(1-i)}(n-2)+(n+r-1)d_{r}^{(1-i)}(n-1),

where
d_{r}^{(i)}(n)=0
for
n<r
and
d_{r}^{(i)}(r)=\frac{r!}{2}(1+(-1)^{r+i})
.

Proof.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
\sigma\in\mathcal{D}_{r}^{(i)}(n)
. Consider the permutation
\rho=(r+n,\ \sigma(r+n))\circ\sigma
, where
\circ
denotes the usual product of permutations. We know that
\rho
fixes
r+n
and has parity
1-i
. We have three cases.

•

If
\sigma(\sigma(r+n))=r+n
and
\sigma(r+n)\in\{1\,\ldots r\}
, then
\rho
also fixes
\sigma(r+n)
and can be treated as an element of the set
\mathcal{D}_{r-1}(n-1)
.

•

If
\sigma(\sigma(r+n))=r+n
and
\sigma(r+n)\in\{r+1\,\ldots r+n-1\}
, then
\rho
also fixes
\sigma(r+n)
and can be treated as an element of the set
\mathcal{D}_{r}(n-2)
.

•

If
\sigma(\sigma(r+n))\neq r+n
, then
\rho
fixes only
r+n
and can be treated as an element of the set
\mathcal{D}_{r}(n-1)
.

We thus see that
\sigma\in\mathcal{D}_{r}^{(i)}(n)
can be constructed as
(r+n,\ \sigma(r+n))\circ\rho
, where exactly one of the possibilities holds:

•

\rho\in\mathcal{D}_{r-1}(n-1)
and
\sigma(r+n)
can be chosen in
r
ways;

•

\rho\in\mathcal{D}_{r}(n-2)
and
\sigma(r+n)
can be chosen in
n-1
ways;

•

\rho\in\mathcal{D}_{r}(n-1)
and
\sigma(r+n)
can be chosen in
r+n-1
ways.

Finally, we conclude that the recurrence from the statement of the theorem holds.

The equality
d_{r}^{(i)}(n)=0
for
n<r
is clear. For the proof of the equality
d_{r}^{(i)}(r)=\frac{r!}{2}(1+(-1)^{r+i})
it suffices to notice that the set
\mathcal{D}_{r}(r)
contains only products of
r
pairwise disjoint transpositions, which are permutations of parity
r\bmod{2}
.
∎

Our goal is to compute the exponential generating functions of the sequences
(d_{r}^{(i)}(n))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
,
r\in\mathbb{N}
,
i\in\{0,1\}
, and to derive exact formulae for the numbers
d_{r}^{(i)}(n)
. At first, we consider an auxiliary sequence
(c_{r}(n))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
given by the formula
c_{r}(n)=d_{r}^{(1)}(n)-d_{r}^{(0)}(n)
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Directly from Theorem
7.1
we get the following.

Corollary 7.2
.

For each
r,n\in\mathbb{N}
and
i\in\{0,1\}
we have

\displaystyle c_{r}(n)=

\displaystyle-rc_{r-1}(n-1)-(n-1)c_{r}(n-2)-(n+r-1)c_{r}(n-1),

where
c_{r}(n)=0
for
n<r
and
c_{r}(r)=(-1)^{r+1}r!
.

Now, we give a formula for the exponential generating function for the sequence
(c_{r}(n))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
.

Lemma 7.3
.

Let
r\in\mathbb{N}
. Then, the exponential generating function of the sequence
(c_{r}(n))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is

\displaystyle G_{r}(x)=\sum_{n=0}^{+\infty}\frac{c_{r}(n)}{n!}x^{n}=\frac{(-1)^{r-1}x^{r}e^{-x}}{(1+x)^{r-1}}.

Proof.

We proceed by induction on
r\in\mathbb{N}
.

For
r=0
we know that
d_{0}^{(i)}(n)=\frac{1}{2}[d_{0}(n)-(-1)^{n+i}(n-1)]
(see
[
4
, Corollary 1]
). Thus,
c_{0}(n)=(-1)^{n}(n-1)
, so
G_{0}(x)=-(1+x)e^{-x}
.

For
r\geq 1
use the recurrence from Corollary
7.2
to get the following:

\displaystyle G_{r}^{\prime}(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}\frac{c_{r}(n)}{(n-1)!}x^{n-1}=-\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}\frac{rc_{r-1}(n-1)+(n-1)c_{r}(n-2)+(n+r-1)c_{r}(n-1)}{(n-1)!}x^{n-1}

\displaystyle=-rG_{r-1}(x)-xG_{r}(x)-xG_{r}^{\prime}(x)-rG_{r}(x).

Hence, we obtain a differential equation

(1+x)G_{r}^{\prime}(x)=-rG_{r-1}(x)-(x+r)G_{r}(x).

With the use of the induction assumption

G_{r-1}(x)=\frac{(-1)^{r-2}x^{r-1}e^{-x}}{(1+x)^{r-2}}

we easily check that

G_{r}(x)=\frac{(-1)^{r-1}x^{r}e^{-x}}{(1+x)^{r-1}}

is the solution of the above equation satisfying the boundary condition
G_{r}(0)=0
.
∎

With the knowledge of the exponential generating function of the sequence
(c_{r}(n))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
we are able to derive an explicit formula for the number
c_{r}(n)
.

Corollary 7.4
.

For each
r\geq 2
and
n\geq r
we have

\displaystyle c_{r}(n)=(-1)^{n-1}\sum_{j=r}^{n}\frac{n!}{(n-j)!}\binom{j-2}{r-2}.

Proof.

We write the exponential generating function of the sequence
(c_{r}(n))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
as follows:

\displaystyle G_{r}(x)

\displaystyle=(-1)^{r-1}x^{r}e^{-x}\cdot\frac{1}{(1+x)^{r-1}}=\sum_{j=0}^{+\infty}\frac{(-1)^{j+r-1}}{j!}x^{j+r}\cdot\sum_{k=0}^{+\infty}\binom{k+r-2}{r-2}(-x)^{k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{+\infty}\frac{x^{n+r}}{n!}\cdot(-1)^{n+r-1}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}\frac{(k+r-2)!}{(r-2)!}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{+\infty}\frac{x^{n+r}}{(n+r)!}\cdot(-1)^{n+r-1}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{(n+r)!}{k!(n-k)!}\cdot\frac{(k+r-2)!}{(r-2)!}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=r}^{+\infty}\frac{x^{n}}{n!}\cdot(-1)^{n-1}\sum_{k=0}^{n-r}\frac{n!}{k!(n-r-k)!}\cdot\frac{(k+r-2)!}{(r-2)!}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=r}^{+\infty}\frac{x^{n}}{n!}\cdot(-1)^{n-1}\sum_{k=0}^{n-r}\frac{n!}{(n-r-k)!}\cdot\binom{k+r-2}{r-2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=r}^{+\infty}\frac{x^{n}}{n!}\cdot(-1)^{n-1}\sum_{j=r}^{n}\frac{n!}{(n-j)!}\cdot\binom{j-2}{r-2}.

Since
G_{r}(x)=\sum_{n=0}^{+\infty}\frac{c_{r}(n)}{n!}x^{n}
, by comparing the coefficients we get the statement.
∎

An immediate consequence of the above formula for
c_{r}(n)
is the following.

Corollary 7.5
.

For every
r\geq 2
and
n\geq r
we have

\displaystyle(-1)^{n}\left(d_{r}^{(0)}(n)-d_{r}^{(1)}(n)\right)>0.

Proof.

Corollary
7.4
gives

\displaystyle d_{r}^{(0)}(n)-d_{r}^{(1)}(n)=(-1)^{n}\sum_{j=r}^{n}\frac{n!}{(n-j)!}\binom{j-2}{r-2},

and the result follows.
∎

We are now ready to give exact formulae for exponential generating functions of the sequences
(d_{r}^{(i)}(n))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
,
r\in\mathbb{N}
,
i\in\{0,1\}
, and the numbers
d_{r}^{(i)}(n)
themselves.

Theorem 7.6
.

Let
r\in\mathbb{N}
and
i\in\{0,1\}
. Then, the exponential generating function of the sequence
(d_{r}^{(i)}(n))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is

\displaystyle F_{r}^{(i)}(x)=\sum_{n=0}^{+\infty}\frac{d_{r}^{(i)}(n)}{n!}x^{n}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{x^{r}e^{-x}}{(1-x)^{r+1}}+(-1)^{r+i}\frac{x^{r}e^{-x}}{(1+x)^{r-1}}\right).

Proof.

The statement follows easily from the fact that
d_{r}^{(i)}(n)=\frac{1}{2}[d_{r}(n)-(-1)^{i}c_{r}(n)]
and from the formula for the exponential generating function
F_{r}(x)=\frac{x^{r}e^{-x}}{(1-x)^{r+1}}
of the sequence
(d_{r}(n))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
(see
[
9
, Theorem 3]
).
∎

Theorem 7.7
.

Let
r,n\in\mathbb{N}
with
r\geq 2
and
i\in\{0,1\}
. Then,

\displaystyle d_{r}^{(i)}(n)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=r}^{n}\frac{n!}{(n-j)!}\left((-1)^{n-j}\binom{j}{r}+(-1)^{n+i}\binom{j-2}{r-2}\right).

Proof.

The equality follows from Corollary
7.4
and the explicit formula for
d_{r}(n)
in
[
9
, Theorem 4]
.
∎
