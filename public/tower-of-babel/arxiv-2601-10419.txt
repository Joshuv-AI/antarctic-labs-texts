On the Canonical Construction of Simple Lie Superalgebras
J. Dhamothiran and Saudamini Nayak

Abstract
. Axioms for the generalization of root systems were defined and classified (irreducible) by V. Serganova, which precisely correspond to the root systems of basic classical Lie Superalgebras. Here, we present a unified method for constructing simple Lie Superalgebras from the abstract root system, with the choice of base having the minimal number of isotropic roots.



2020 Mathematics Subject Classification
17B10, 17B22, 17B65.

1.
Introduction

Given a complex semisimple Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
, one can achieve a root space decomposition by fixing a Cartan subalgebra. In this process, to each
\mathfrak{g}
one associates an object called a root system, which is classified by the Cartan matrix and Dynkin diagram attached to it. Thus, complex semisimple Lie algebras are classified by their Dynkin diagrams; these results were achieved between 1890 and 1900 through the work of E. Cartan and W. Killing. Later, root systems are studied extensively and independently due to their rich algebraic and combinatorial structures.

Meanwhile, starting with a root system it is natural to ask how to retrieve the corresponding semisimple Lie algebra. In 1966, J. Tits achieved this by a subtle choice of signs in Chevalley’s integral basis, and J. P. Serre defined it by taking a suitable quotient of a free Lie algebra. In 1967, V. G. Kac and R. V. Moody introduced another method for obtaining a simple Lie algebra from a generalized Cartan matrix. The corresponding algebra is called a Kac-Moody algebra and it can be extended to any arbitrary matrix. Correspondingly, one can obtain a contragredient Lie algebra.

Following this, a contragredient Lie superalgebra was introduced as a generalization of a contragredient Lie algebra. In 1977, V. G. Kac classified finite dimensional simple contragredient Lie superalgebras
[
6
,
5
]
. In general, these algebras are close to being simple and not necessarily finite dimensional.

In this article, we present a unified approach to construct simple Lie superalgebras, motivated by the work of M. Geck
[
2
]
, who achieved a similar result in the context of Lie algebras. In particular, we construct a simple Lie superalgebra as a subalgebra of the general linear Lie superalgebra consisting of all linear maps on a complex vector superspace
M
having a homogeneous Chevalley-type basis induced by a root system. For this purpose, we begin with the notion of generalized root systems (in short, GRS), as introduced by V. Serganova
[
9
]
. In that article, the author classified irreducible generalized root systems with isotropic roots, which coincide with the root systems of the basic classical Lie superalgebras from the Kac’s list (with the exception of
A(1,1)
and
B(0,n)
), which are of contragredient type.

Following work of Serganova various root systems are introduced. For instance, M. Yousofzadeh introduced extended affine root supersystems,
[
10
]
, locally finite root supersystems,
[
11
]
. Another combinatorial object, generalized reflection root system is introduced by M. Gorelik
[
3
]
. These root systems are not finite (in general), and an irreducible finite one is exactly an irreducible GRS; this identification provides more flexibility in working with GRSs.

Section
2
contains some preliminary results about root strings in a GRS
R
. In section
3
we define specific homogeneous linear maps
e_{i},f_{i}\text{ and }h_{i}
on a finite-dimensional vector superspace. These maps satisfy relations that are expected of the Chevalley generators of a simple Lie superalgebra. In section
4
, it is shown that
e_{i},f_{i}
indeed generate a simple Lie superalgebra
\mathfrak{g}
with root system isomorphic to
R
. Finally, in
5
we sketch the quiver diagram of the positive system of all irreducible GRS, to emphasize some results.

2.
Preliminaries

We begin this section by recalling basic facts about generalized root systems;
[
9
]
. Let
V
be a finite dimensional complex vector space with a non-degenerate bilinear inner product
(\cdot,\cdot)
. A finite set
R\subset V\backslash\{0\}
is called a generalized root system if the following holds.

(1)

R
spans
V
and
R=-R
;

(2)

if
\alpha,\beta\in R
and
(\alpha,\alpha)\neq 0
then
(\beta,\alpha^{\vee}):=\dfrac{2(\beta,\alpha)}{(\alpha,\alpha)}\in\mbox{$\mathbb{Z}$}
and
r_{\alpha}(\beta)=\beta-(\beta,\alpha^{\vee})\alpha\in R
;

(3)

if
\alpha\in R
and
(\alpha,\alpha)=0
then there exists an invertible map
r_{\alpha}:R\rightarrow R
such that
r_{\alpha}(\beta)=\beta\text{ if }(\beta,\alpha)=0\text{ and }r_{\alpha}(\beta)\in\{\beta+\alpha,\beta-\alpha\}\text{ otherwise}
.

Let
R^{im}=\{\alpha\in R:(\alpha,\alpha)=0\}\text{ set of isotropic roots},R^{re}=\{\alpha\in R:(\alpha,\alpha)\neq 0\}
,
\alpha^{\vee}:=\alpha
for
\alpha\in R^{im}
and
\alpha^{\vee}:=\dfrac{2\alpha}{(\alpha,\alpha)}
for
\alpha\in R^{re}
. From this, if
\alpha\in R^{re}
then
(\alpha,\alpha^{\vee})=2
and if
\alpha\in R^{im}
then
(\alpha,\alpha^{\vee})=0
.

Lemma 2.1
.

If
\alpha\in R^{im}
, and
k\alpha\in R
, then
k=\pm 1
.

Proof.

For
\beta\in R^{re}
we have
(c\alpha,\beta^{\vee})\in\mbox{$\mathbb{Z}$}
implies that
c\in\mbox{$\mathbb{Z}$}
.
Suppose that
2\alpha\in R
and
2\alpha-\alpha\in R
, by (3) we have
(2\alpha,\alpha)\neq 0
. Which contradict
\alpha\in R^{im}
. By induction we can show no other integral multiples are not in
R
.
∎

Lemma 2.2
.

If
\alpha\in R^{re}
and
k\alpha\in R
, then
k\in\{\pm 1/2,\pm 1,\pm 2\}
.

Proof.

Since
k\alpha\in R^{re}
then
(k\alpha,\alpha^{\vee})=\dfrac{2(k\alpha,\alpha)}{(\alpha,\alpha)}=2k\in\mbox{$\mathbb{Z}$}
implies
k
is integral multiple of 1/2 and
(\alpha,(k\alpha)^{\vee})=\dfrac{2(\alpha,k\alpha)}{(k\alpha,k\alpha)}=2/k\in\mbox{$\mathbb{Z}$}.
All together this implies
k\in\{\pm 1/2,\pm 1,\pm 2\}
.
∎

Remark 2.3
.

Following the lemma, if
\alpha
is a root then
2\alpha
can be a root. Define
R_{\bar{1}}=R^{im}\cup\{\alpha\in R^{re}:2\alpha\in R\}
and
R_{\bar{0}}=R\backslash R_{\bar{1}}.
Clearly
\alpha\in R_{\bar{0}}
implies
\alpha\in R^{re}
having property that
2\alpha\notin R.
We have
R=R_{\bar{0}}\cup R_{\bar{1}}.
Whenever
\alpha\in R_{\bar{j}}
, we define degree
\overline{\alpha}=j
for
j\in\mbox{$\mathbb{Z}$}_{2}.
For
\alpha\in R_{\bar{0}}
(
\alpha\in R_{\bar{1}}
) we call them even (respectively odd) roots. Also we consider
\overline{\alpha\pm\beta}:=\overline{\alpha}+\overline{\beta}
whenever
\alpha,\beta,\alpha\pm\beta\in R
.

A base for a GRS is a subset
\Pi=\{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{n}\}
of

such that any
\alpha\in R
can be uniquely represented as a linear combination
\alpha=k_{1}\alpha_{1}+k_{2}\alpha_{2}+\cdots+k_{n}\alpha_{n}
such that either all
k_{i}\in\mbox{$\mathbb{Z}$}_{\geq 0}
or all
k_{i}\in\mbox{$\mathbb{Z}$}_{\leq 0}
; correspondingly, we have a partition
R=R^{+}\cup R^{-}
where
R^{+}=R\cap\mbox{$\mathbb{Z}$}_{\geq 0}-span~\Pi
is set of positive roots and
R^{-}=-R^{+}
is set of negative roots. Following this we have a partial order
\preceq
on
V
by setting
\lambda\preceq\mu
if
\mu-\lambda\in R^{+}
.

A generalized root system
R
is called reducible if it can be represented as a direct orthogonal sum of two nonempty generalized root systems
R_{1}
and
R_{2}
:
V=V_{1}\oplus V_{2}
,
R_{i}\subseteq V_{i}
and
R=R_{1}\cup R_{2}
. Otherwise the system is called irreducible. By the classification of irreducible GRS, we have a complete list of irreducible GRS (
[
9
]
, Theorem 5.10). Throughout this article, we let
R
be an irreducible GRS with isotropic roots, also we let a distinguished base
\Pi
for these root systems be given in
[
1
,
4
,
6
]
.

Let
I=\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
be the index set of
\Pi
, for
i\in I
, we denote
\bar{i}=\overline{\alpha_{i}}.
For
\alpha=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}k_{i}\alpha_{i}\in R
define the height
ht(\alpha)=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}k_{i}
. Also for
\alpha\in R
, we define
sgn(\alpha)=\begin{cases}1&\text{ if }\alpha\in R^{+}\\
-1&\text{ if }\alpha\in R^{-}\end{cases}
and suppose that
(\alpha,\alpha_{i})\neq 0
for
\alpha\in R
and
\alpha_{i}\in\Pi\cap R^{im}
, we let
(\alpha,\alpha_{i}):=sgn(\alpha)
.

2.1.
Root strings.

Let
\alpha,\beta\in R
, we can uniquely define two integers
r,q\geq 0
by the conditions that

\beta-r\alpha,\ldots,\beta-\alpha,\beta,\beta+\alpha,\ldots,\beta+q\alpha\in R

and
\beta-(r+1)\alpha\notin R
,
\beta+(q+1)\alpha\notin R
. The above sequence of roots is called the
\alpha
-string through
\beta
and we denote
m_{\alpha}^{+}(\beta)=q+1
and
m_{\alpha}^{-}(\beta)=r+1
. We also write
m_{i}^{\pm}(\beta):=m_{\alpha_{i}}^{\pm}(\beta)
with
i\in I
. If
\alpha\in R^{re}
and
\beta\in R
then the root string property
(\beta,\alpha^{\vee})=m_{\alpha}^{-}(\beta)-m_{\alpha}^{+}(\beta)
holds (
[
11
]
, Lemma 3.12).

We now collect some results which will be useful in the sequel.

Lemma 2.4

(
[
9
]
, Lemma 1.8)
.

Let
\alpha\in R^{im},\beta\in R^{re}
such that
(\beta,\alpha)\neq 0
.

(a)

if
r_{\alpha}(\beta)=\beta\pm\alpha\in R^{im}
then
(\alpha,\beta^{\vee})=\mp 1
,

(b)

if
r_{\alpha}(\beta)=\beta\pm\alpha\in R^{re}

(\alpha,\beta^{\vee})=\mp 2

Proof.

For (a) use the fact
(\beta\pm\alpha,\beta\pm\alpha)=0
which implies that
(\beta,\beta)\pm 2(\beta,\alpha)=0

(\alpha,\beta^{\vee})=\mp 1
.

For (b) we know
(\alpha,(\alpha+\beta)^{\vee})=\frac{2(\alpha,\beta)}{(\beta,\beta)+2(\alpha,\beta)}=\frac{(\alpha,\beta^{\vee})}{1+(\alpha,\beta^{\vee})}\in\mbox{$\mathbb{Z}$}
only if
(\alpha,\beta^{\vee})=-2
. Similarly if
\beta-\alpha\in R^{re}
implies that
(\alpha,\beta^{\vee})=2
.
∎

For

n
, then by induction
y\in\mathfrak{g}^{\mu}\text{ and }z\in\mathfrak{g}^{\nu}\text{ where }\mu,\nu\in Q^{+}
. By graded Jacobi identity,
[h_{i},[y,z]]=[[h_{i},y],z]+(-1)^{\overline{h_{i}}\overline{y}}[y,[h_{i},z]]=\mu(h_{i})[y,z]+\nu(h_{i})[y,z]
for all
i\in I
. Hence
x\in\mathfrak{g}^{\lambda}\text{ where }\lambda:=\mu+\nu\in Q^{+}
and argument for
\mathfrak{n}^{-}
is completely analogous.
∎

Remark 4.2
.

Since for any
g\in\mathfrak{g}
we have a linear map on
M
, then
M
has a
\mathfrak{g}
-module structure. If we define
(\alpha,\alpha_{i}^{\vee}):=\alpha(h_{i})
then we have
h_{i}(u_{j})=0\text{ and }h_{i}(v_{\alpha})=\alpha(h_{i})v_{\alpha}
, hence the homogeneous basis
\{u_{i}:i\in I\}\cup\{v_{\alpha}:\alpha\in R\}
is a set of
h_{i}~(i\in I)
weight vectors and we have a weight space decomposition
M=M^{0}\oplus\bigoplus\limits_{\alpha\in R}M^{\alpha}
where
M^{0}=\text{span}_{\mbox{$\mathbb{C}$}}\{u_{i}:i\in I\}\text{ and }M^{\alpha}=\text{span}_{\mbox{$\mathbb{C}$}}\{v_{\alpha}:\alpha\in R\}
. We can observe that the action of
e_{k},f_{k}~(k\in I)
on this homogeneous basis by, if
v\in M
is a weight vector with weight
\lambda\in R\cup\{0\}
, then either
e_{k}(v)=0~(\text{similarly }f_{k}(v)=0)
or
e_{k}(v)\text{ and }f_{k}(v)
are again wight vectors with weights
\lambda+\alpha_{k}\text{ and }\lambda-\alpha_{k}\text{ respectively}
.

Lemma 4.3
.

We have a direct sum decomposition
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{n}^{-}\oplus\mathfrak{h}\oplus\mathfrak{n}^{+}
.

Proof.

Following the arguments as in (
[
2
]
, Lemma 4.5) and relations from Lemma
3.1
, it is clear that
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{n}^{-}+\mathfrak{h}+\mathfrak{n}^{+}
. Suppose that
g=n^{-}+h+n^{+}=0\text{ where }n^{\pm}\in\mathfrak{n}^{\pm}\text{ and }h\in\mathfrak{h}
. Then
0=g(u_{i})=n^{-}(u_{i})+h(u_{i})+n^{+}(u_{i})=n^{-}(u_{i})+n^{+}(u_{i})
. Then
n^{-}(u_{i})\in M^{-\alpha}
and
n^{+}(u_{i})\in M^{\beta}
for some
\alpha,\beta\in R^{+}
. Hence by the decomposition of
M
, we have
n^{\pm}(u_{i})=0
for all
i\in I
and all
h\in\mathfrak{h}
. Now for
\alpha\in R^{+}
,
0=g(v_{\alpha})=n^{-}(v_{\alpha})+h(v_{\alpha})+n^{+}(v_{\alpha})\in M^{\alpha^{\prime}}\oplus M^{\alpha}\oplus M^{\alpha^{\prime\prime}}\text{ where }ht(\alpha^{\prime})\preceq ht(\alpha)\preceq ht(\alpha^{\prime\prime})
. Again by the decomposition
n^{\pm}(v_{\alpha})=0\text{ and }h(v_{\alpha})=0
, similarly we have this for
\alpha\in R^{-}
. Thus we have
n^{\pm}=0\text{ and }h=0
.
∎

Lemma 4.4
.

The abelian subalgebra
\mathfrak{h}
spanned by even homogeneous diagonalizable operators

is Cartan subalgebra of
\mathfrak{g}
, and we have decomposition

Since
\mathfrak{h}\subseteq\mathfrak{g}^{0}\text{ and }\mathfrak{n}^{\pm}\subseteq\bigoplus\limits_{\lambda\in Q^{\pm}}\mathfrak{g}^{\lambda}
. By above lemma we deduce that

is Cartan subalgebra of

be the root system of

with respect to
\mathfrak{h}
, then

. Since

which spans

and so we have the decomposition.
∎

Now we have a root space decomposition of
