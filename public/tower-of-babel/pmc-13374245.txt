Modelling the multidimensional structure of workplace violence against emergency medical technicians and mitigation strategies in Ghana: a confirmatory factor analysis
Authors: Yenube Clement Kunkuaboor, Azudaa R Atandigre, Jones Opoku-Ware, Godwin Armstrong Duku, Miilon Sommik Duut, Isaac Adjei Mensah, Adusei Bofa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEmergency Medical Technicians (EMTs) are routinely exposed to workplace violence, yet empirical evidence from low- and middle-income countries remains limited. This study examined the multidimensional structure of workplace violence against EMTs in Ghana, evaluated perceived mitigation strategies using a confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) approach and explored EMTs’ perceptions of workplace violence mitigation strategies.MethodsA cross-sectional survey was conducted among 336 EMTs across three regions of Ghana. Workplace violence was measured using 23 items, and mitigation strategies using 10 items. A two-step approach was employed: an exploratory factor analysis (EFA) to ascertain the underlying factor structure, followed by CFA to validate the identified structure from the EFA. We used a weighted least-squares mean-and-variance-adjusted estimator appropriate for ordinal data. Model reliability, convergent validity, and inter-construct relationships were assessed.ResultsThe CFA supported a three-factor structure comprising verbal/non-physical abuse, physical assault, and severe violent attacks, with strong internal consistency (α = 0.812–0.939) and high convergent validity (AVE > 0.75). The study revealed strong structural covariance between physical assault and severe violent attacks (r = 0.668), moderate covariance between physical assault and verbal abuse (r = 0.439), and weaker but significant covariance between severe attacks and verbal abuse (r = 0.347). Most respondents (84%) expressed high concern about workplace violence. Aggression management, defensive tactical training, and resilience training were perceived as the most effective mitigation strategies.ConclusionWorkplace violence against EMTs in Ghana is multidimensional and highly interrelated. Interventions should prioritize early de-escalation, organizational protection mechanisms, and national EMS violence-prevention policies. Addressing verbal and physical abuse concurrently may prevent escalation into severe violence.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-026-27661-1.Keywords: Workplace Violence, Emergency Medical Technicians (EMTs), Ghana, National Ambulance Service (NAS), Emergency Medical Services (EMS), Violent or Violence Attacks

Background

Workplace violence against healthcare workers has emerged as a major occupational health concern globally, with emergency medical personnel among the most exposed professional groups. Emergency Medical Technicians (EMTs) operate in unpredictable and often volatile environments, providing care under time pressure, emotional intensity, and limited security. Their work routinely places them in close contact with distressed patients, relatives, bystanders, and occasionally hostile crowds, increasing their vulnerability to aggression and assault [1–3]. International evidence indicates that between 8% and 38% of healthcare workers experience physical violence during their careers, with substantially higher rates of verbal abuse and threats, particularly among pre-hospital emergency responders [4, 5]. Compared with other health professionals, EMTs consistently report higher exposure to violence due to their frontline role, frequent public interactions, and limited control over their work environment [6, 7].

Workplace violence in emergency medical services encompasses a wide spectrum of behaviors, ranging from verbal abuse and intimidation to physical assault and severe violent attacks. Verbal and non-physical abuse, such as shouting, threats, and harassment, are the most reported forms and often precede physical aggression [8, 9]. Physical assaults, including punching, kicking, or being struck with objects, pose immediate risks of injury, while severe violent attacks such as stabbings, sexual assault, or attacks on ambulances represent extreme but increasingly reported events [10, 11]. Evidence suggests that these forms of violence are not isolated; rather, they frequently co-occur and escalate, with verbal hostility often serving as an early warning sign of more serious violence [12]. Repeated exposure to such incidents has been linked to psychological distress, burnout, post-traumatic stress disorder, reduced job satisfaction, and diminished quality of care delivery [13–15].

In Ghana, emergency medical services operate under the Anglo-American model, with EMTs providing pre-hospital emergency care and rapid transport to health facilities through the National Ambulance Service (NAS) [16–18]. Since its establishment, NAS has expanded nationwide and plays a critical role in emergency response. However, EMTs in Ghana continue to work under resource-constrained conditions characterized by high workload, delayed response times, high call turnaround time, limited security presence, and increasing public expectations. Although workplace violence against healthcare workers in Ghana has been documented, particularly among hospital-based clinical staff, empirical evidence focusing specifically on EMTs remains scarce [19–21]. Existing studies have largely relied on descriptive approaches and have not examined how different forms of violence interact or escalate within the pre-hospital environment.

Emerging literature increasingly recognises workplace violence as a multidimensional phenomenon rather than isolated events. Verbal abuse, physical assault, and severe attacks often form a continuum. Each type can reinforce the others, collectively shaping workers’ experiences and responses [7, 12, 14]. Workplace violence against EMTs has led to various safety interventions. Evidence suggests that aggression management training, verbal de-escalation, and situational awareness are effective [22, 23]. System-level measures such as dispatch risk assessment and police escort protocols are effective in high-income areas but may not translate to low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) [6, 22]. Budget limits, security gaps, sociocultural challenges, infrastructure deficiencies, and unique local dynamics constrain LMICs [3, 6, 22, 24]. Few LMIC studies use frameworks that model the inter-relationships of workplace violence. Most prior research treats violence as a single outcome or studies the parts separately, limiting insights into how aggression types interact or escalate in real emergencies [2, 3, 7, 25].

To fill this gap, the current study uses a multidimensional analytical method to investigate workplace violence among EMTs in Ghana. The EFA is used to conceptualize workplace violence using 23 observed indicators, allowing for the confirmation (CFA) of a theoretically grounded measuring structure. This technique makes it easier to examine construct validity and investigate correlations between different types of violence, resulting in a more nuanced knowledge of how violent behaviour emerges and can escalate in pre-hospital care settings. In addition, the study investigates EMTs’ perspectives of workplace violence reduction (prevention) options such as behavioral management approaches, defensive training, and organizational interventions. Examining these perspectives helps to discover practical and culturally relevant entry points for violence prevention, as well as supports the development of specific interventions to improve EMT safety and well-being.

Methods

Study design and sample size

A cross-sectional analytical study was conducted among EMTs employed by the Ghana National Ambulance Service (NAS). The NAS operates under the Anglo-American emergency medical service model and provides nationwide pre-hospital emergency care through a network of regional and district ambulance stations. As of 2020, the NAS had a total of 2,112 EMTs. We used the Yamane algorithm to compute the sample size [26]. The study employed a 95% confidence level and a p-value of 0.05 to select a sample of 336 EMTs. We chose151 from Ashanti, 137 EMTs from Greater Accra, and 48 from the Northern Region. These numbers were chosen proportionally based on the numerical strength of the EMTs in these Regions.

A significant proportion of the EMTs are males, 236 (70.2%), with women representing 100 (29.8%) of the sample (Fig. 1). The largest age group is 29–38 years old, numbering 199 (59.2%), followed by those aged 39–48 with 82 (24.4%), and 18–28 years old with 52 (15.5%). Most of the respondents are senior EMTs, totaling 124 (36.9%), followed by EMT basics at 102 (30.4%), and advanced EMTs (AEMTs) at 59 (17.6%). Majority of the EMTs have 6–10 years of experience, with 138 (41.1%), while the least common roles are EMT Basic, Advanced EMTs, and senior AEMTs. Nearly half of the respondents have secondary education, 166 (49.4%), 158 (47.3%) have higher education, and 11 (3.3%) have lower secondary education (e.g., drivers). Personnel of the service under operations make up most of the workforce, totaling 286 (85.1%), while officers in administrative roles, numbering 50 (14.9%), participated in the study. The NAS workforce primarily consists of young employees, most of whom have 1–10 years of experience, indicating a moderately experienced and stable workforce. This composition highlights strengths in capability and also reveals opportunities to improve gender balance, retention strategies, and career progression pathways.

Demographic characteristics of respondents

Sampling and data collection

We used a multistage sampling strategy to obtain a representative yet focused sample of respondents for the study. In the first stage, the three study regions, namely Greater Accra, Ashanti, and the Northern Region, were purposively selected based on their comparatively large number of ambulance stations and consistent record of emergency medical cases over time [27]. These locations were deemed most suitable for the research due to their operational relevance within the NAS and their ability to generate extensive data for analyzing violence against EMTs. This strategy ensured that the study encompassed a diverse range of emergency response environments, characterized by varying population densities and operational requirements. In the second step, we chose EMTs from diverse stations throughout the three regions using a simple random selection procedure. To do this, we collected a sample frame from the regional management that included a list of respondents/EMTs in each of the Ambulance Stations. Then, a balloting method was utilized to choose potential respondents from the sample frame across all the stations in each region. Three respondents were purposively selected from each station to ensure uniform station-level representation and comparability across stations, despite variations in staff strength. Each station has a staff strength of at least six EMTs, and we chose three from each station throughout the region. We wrote their staff IDs on a piece of paper, folded them, and randomly picked three to complete the questionnaire. If the respondent selected was not available to participate in the interview, another individual was drawn randomly from the box to replace. This sampling process continued until the required sample size was obtained.

Data collection

Data was collected using a structured, self-administered questionnaire developed from previously validated instruments used in studies of workplace violence among emergency and healthcare workers [10, 18, 19, 28]. The questionnaire was organized into three main sections:

Items measuring workplace violence were rated on a five-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (“not frequent”) to 5 (“very frequent”). Mitigation strategies were rated on a five-point scale ranging from 1 (“not effective”) to 5 (“very effective”). The questionnaire design and the data collected used English version. Prior to the main survey, the instrument was pre-tested among 20 EMTs in the Ahafo region, a non-study region to assess clarity, relevance, and internal consistency. Feedback from the pre-test informed minor refinements in wording and sequencing. The final questionnaire demonstrated satisfactory internal consistency and face validity. Data was collected electronically using Google Forms to enhance data completeness, minimize entry errors, and ensure respondent anonymity.

Data analysis approach

A cross-sectional study design was employed, using both descriptive and analytical statistical approaches. Descriptive statistics (Fig. 1) provided an overview of participants’ backgrounds and workplace violence mitigation strategies in (Figs. 6 and 7). The analysis was implemented in R software. A two-step approach was used: first, an exploratory factor analysis (EFA) using scree plot and eigenvalues (Fig. 2) to determine the underlying factors for 23 observed items, and subsequently, a confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) was conducted to validate the factor structure identified in the EFA. The CFA was used to test whether the observed data fit the theoretically hypothesized factor structure based on prior research [5, 29] and instrument design, thereby establishing construct validity (Table 3). Model fit was evaluated using widely accepted indices, including the chi-square statistic, Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA), Tucker-Lewis Index (TLI), Standardized Root Mean Square Residual (SRMR), and Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) measure of sampling adequacy [30].

Regression estimates

Given that the data were collected using Likert-type scales, the Weighted Least Squares Mean and Variance adjusted (WLSMV) estimator was employed. This approach is robust for categorical or ordinal indicators and provides more reliable estimates of model fit indices (CFI, TLI, RMSEA, SRMR) than maximum likelihood estimation in this context [31]. Structural Equation Modeling (SEM) extends CFA by incorporating structural (regression) paths between latent variables. However, in this study, the focus was on validating the measurement model rather than testing structural relationships.

To complement the CFA results and explore predictive relationships among items, a Random Forest Classification Approach was applied. This machine learning technique enables the detection of nonlinear interactions and the identification of the most relevant predictors, thereby enhancing both theoretical and predictive insights. Covariance-based analysis was also employed to assess correlations among latent constructs, thereby supporting a thorough and robust examination of the complex relationships within the model. Together, these methods provide a rigorous and reliable framework for evaluating workplace violence constructs and their interrelations.

Results

Model fitness

The results showed that the factor analysis model has great adequacy and fit (see Table 1). The Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) statistic is 0.93, which is significantly above the acceptable threshold of 0.60, indicating that the data is well-suited for factor analysis. The chi-square test returns a p-value of < 0.0001, suggesting that the entire model is statistically significant. Fit indicators corroborate the model’s robustness: the Comparative Fit Index (CFI) is 0.993, and the Tucker-Lewis Index (TLI) is 0.991, both beyond the frequently accepted threshold of 0.90, indicating high model fit [30]. The RMSEA of 0.081 is within the acceptable range (≤ 0.08), indicating a good fit between the model and the data. Additionally, the Standardized Root Mean Square Residual (SRMR) is 0.090, which is higher than the optimum cutoff of 0.08 but still within a reasonable range. Overall, these findings indicated that the factor analysis model is a credible and valid representation of the data’s underlying structure.

Factor loadings and reliability analysis

The component analysis in Table 2 and Appendix B classifies three common types of violence against EMTs, as shown in Figs. 2 and 3: verbal/non-physical abuse (V/NPA), severe violent attacks (SVA), and physical assault. This classification aligns with previous EMS and occupational violence studies, which indicate that workplace violence ranges from routine physical attacks to severe, weapon-related, life-threatening incidents [5, 29]. Attacks such as stabbing, shooting, and attempted rape are considered severe violent acts because of their life-threatening nature, higher injury burden, and occurrence in high-risk contexts [4, 29]. In the context of this current study, the first factor, V/NPA, explained the most variance (25.3%) and included verbal intimidation (0.769), yelling (0.773), threatened gestures (0.891), screaming with intent to offend (0.823), threats to harm (0.818), cursing (0.779), name-calling (0.764), and offensive sexual jokes (0.647). These high loadings and a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.939 indicate good internal consistency, implying that verbal abuse and intimidation were common types of non-physical violence. Tabachnick and Fidell’s [32] study in Multivariate Statistics, which gives a thorough review of factor analysis, mentioned that factor loadings greater than 0.6 are significant and show a strong link between the observed variable and the factor. The average variance extracted (AVE) for this factor is 0.755, further validating its measurement. These findings are consistent with previous research [5, 29] on workplace violence against EMTs, in which validated measurement methods have repeatedly demonstrated that verbal abuse is the most common type of violence encountered by pre-hospital emergency care workers. For example, global research conducted in Europe, Asia, and North America shows that verbal aggression—including insults, threats, and shouting—occurs significantly more frequently than physical attacks [11, 30].

The second category, SVA, accounted for 23.4% of the variation and included more serious types of violence such as being shot at (0.756), stabbed (0.618), spat on (0.737), beaten (0.780), attempted rape (0.821), and indecent assault (0.770). The Cronbach’s alpha for this factor was 0.834, indicating high reliability. The AVE for this construct is 0.779, suggesting effective variance capture. Similarly, Verbal/Non-Physical Abuse shows outstanding reliability. This means EMTs may face violent attacks at different risk levels. Life-threatening and serious attacks are less common than verbal abuse but pose higher physical and psychological risks. Extreme violent assaults involve intentional, high-risk, and traumatic violence and are more intense than regular physical assaults. Shootings, stabbings, and sexual assaults are examples of the greatest threats and psychological harm, usually happening in unstable settings. Workplace violence can therefore be grouped by risk: regular aggression versus severe incidents, based on danger and trauma potential [22, 29]. Studies in emergency medical services show these high-risk acts often accompany crime, hostility, or weapons, supporting their recognition as a separate, measurable construct [7, 10].

The third component contributed 13.4% of the variance and included acts of physical assault such as having hair pulled (0.695), being hit (0.628), being kicked (0.690), and hitting an ambulance (0.664). With a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.812, this factor was similarly quite reliable, indicating consistent measurement of physical assault events. The AVE of 0.777 demonstrates that the items explain a substantial portion of the variance in the construct.

In all, the three-factors explained 62.2% of the total variance, which is a substantial proportion. The high Cronbach’s alpha values across all three factors (0.812–0.939) and AVE values (0.755–0.779) indicate excellent internal consistency. High reliability coefficients across all three constructs support their use in research and practical applications aimed at understanding violence against EMTs. This is an indication that violence against EMTs is multidimensional, with verbal/non-physical abuse being the most dominant, followed by severe/violent attacks and physical assault. This highlights the urgent need for mitigation strategies in addressing not only physical protection but also the prevention of verbal harassment and severe forms of assault.

The weaker association between severe attacks and verbal abuse indicates partial independence, supporting the mediation model instead of a singular violent dimension.

Mitigation strategies

In Figs. 6 and 7, we analyzed the most perceived effective methods of controlling and preventing workplace violence against EMTs. 84% (84.0%) of EMTs are worried about workplace violence against them (Fig. 6). To address this, 58.0% perceived managing the aggressive behavior (MCV4) of the offender, 56.0% favored defensive tactical training (MCV3), and 46.0% supported training EMTs to be more resilient (MCV1) as the most effective methods (Fig. 6). Although weapon training was seen as moderately positive and effective (MCV2 = 41.0%) in controlling workplace violence, martial arts training (MCV5 = 41.0%) was not perceived as effective. Similar patterns appeared in perceived methods of preventing workplace violence. Management of bystander and family interference (MPV5 = 55.0%), gang awareness (MPV4 = 47.0%), and domestic violence management (MPV3 = 46.0%) were seen as effective in preventing violent attacks. By contrast, verbal de-escalation (MPV2 = 33.0%) received the lowest perceived effectiveness rating (Fig. 7). Overall, respondents’ perceptions indicate significant belief in most controlling and prevention measures, though certain items require closer attention for improvement.

Discussion

This study demonstrates that workplace violence against Emergency Medical Technicians (EMTs) in Ghana is best understood as a multidimensional and interrelated occupational hazard, rather than an isolated or episodic events. Using a confirmatory factors analysis through the structural equation-modelling framework appropriate for ordinal data, three correlated latent domains were identified: verbal or non-physical abuse, physical assault, and severe violent attacks. This structure aligns with established occupational violence frameworks in pre-hospital emergency service, which conceptualize violence as a continuum shaped by situational exposure, organizational protection, and responder vulnerability [7, 12, 14, 29]. The prominence of verbal and non-physical abuse within the model reinforces long-standing evidence that verbal aggression is the most common form of violence encountered by EMS personnel globally and is often embedded in routine emergency response work [8–10, 29].

A key contribution of this study lies in clarifying the relationships among different forms of violence. The strong association between physical assault and severe violent attacks indicates that EMTs who experience physical aggression face a markedly higher risk of exposure to extreme and potentially life-threatening violence. Similar clustering of physical and severe violence has been reported in EMS systems in high-income countries, where repeated physical assaults are associated with higher injury rates and fatal incidents [7, 10, 33]. In some contexts, such escalation has prompted structural responses such as armed escorts for ambulance crews, highlighting the extent to which severe violence can become normalized in the absence of effective system-level safeguards.

The moderate association between verbal or non-physical abuse and physical assault reflects a pattern widely documented in the pre-hospital emergency care settings, where verbal hostility frequently co-occurs with, or precedes, physical confrontation [9, 10, 25]. Verbal aggression often arises in response to perceived service delays, communication breakdowns, or dissatisfaction with care and may lower the threshold for physical violence during emotionally charged encounters. The weaker but statistically significant relationship between verbal abuse and severe violent attacks indicates that extreme violence does not always evolve gradually from verbal conflict. Severe incidents may instead emerge in distinct high-risk contexts, including criminal activity, domestic violence scenes, or situations involving intoxication, crowd dynamics, or weapons [29, 34]. This distinction is important for prevention, as it indicates that not all severe violence can be mitigated through communication-focused strategies alone.

The observed interdependence of violence domains affects EMT well-being and system performance. Exposure to workplace violence causes psychological harm for emergency responders, including post-traumatic stress disorder, anxiety, depression, and exhaustion [13–15, 35, 36]. Repeated exposure, high workload, short recovery periods, and little support worsen these effects [35–37]. Ghana’s EMS is growing and becoming more organized, but it still faces limited resources, high demand, and inefficient systems. Consequently, EMTs handle many emergencies and multiple calls per shift under these constraints. The ambulance-to-population ratio (1:84,000) falls below the WHO guideline of 1:50,000, further straining service delivery. Although the NAS has expanded, coverage remains uneven, and increasing demand continues to outpace available resources [3, 24]. As a result, EMTs experience higher workload intensity, scene stress, and burnout, as fewer crews must serve larger populations, especially when people perceive delays. Additional challenges such as traffic congestion, limited ambulances, and dispatch constraints further increase response times and expose EMTs to high-risk conditions, especially in peri-urban and rural areas [16–18, 24]. In urban areas Ghana, average response time is 16.9-minutes [38], while the national average is 20.35-minutes, well above the 8-minute international standard for high-priority emergencies. In remote regions in Ghana, responses take over 82-minutes because of travel time and resource shortages [38]. The high level of concern about workplace violence reported by EMTs in this study likely reflects sustained occupational strain rather than isolated fear. Studies across multiple settings have shown that repeated exposure to aggression, particularly when incidents are underreported or poorly addressed, erodes coping capacity and contributes to long-term mental health consequences [14, 15, 28–40].

Within the Ghanaian context, the findings are consistent with emerging evidence on workplace violence in healthcare. Although empirical research focusing specifically on EMTs has been limited, studies among Ghanaian nurses and emergency department staff in the Ghana Health Service (GHS) document high prevalence of verbal abuse and physical violence, along with normalization of aggressive behavior in emergency care environments [19–21]. This indicates that violence is not confined to pre-hospital care setting but reflects broader systemic and societal pressures affecting frontline health workers. Ghana’s EMS, while expanding and increasingly formalized, continues to operate under conditions of constrained resources, public misunderstanding of the EMT profession, delayed response times, and variable coordination with security services [16–18, 24]. These factors likely shape both the frequency and the form of violence experienced by EMTs and help explain the close linkage observed between physical assault and severe violent attacks.

The findings on mitigation strategies provide further guidance for policy and practice. EMTs identified aggression management training, defensive tactical skills, and resilience-building interventions as the most effective approaches for controlling violence. Preventive strategies related to managing bystander interference, improving situational awareness, and addressing domestic violence contexts, were prioritized. These preferences align with international evidence showing that structured aggression management training improves staff confidence and preparedness in high-risk care environments [23]. They also reflect EMS-specific prevention frameworks that emphasize early risk recognition, scene assessment, and coordinated response protocols rather than reactive or confrontational approaches [22].

Notably, weapon-based training and martial arts techniques were perceived as less effective. This is consistent with concerns raised in the literature that such approaches may escalate encounters or increase risk when applied in uncontrolled emergency settings [41]. The relatively low rating of verbal de-escalation training when used in isolation further underscores the limitations of communication-focused interventions in the absence of broader institutional support. Evidence from EMS and emergency department in hospital settings indicates that de-escalation is most effective when integrated with dispatch intelligence, clear withdrawal protocols, access to security support, and formal reporting mechanisms [22, 25].

From a policy perspective, the findings support a multi-level violence prevention framework for EMS systems in Ghana. First, the strong linkage between physical assault and severe violent attacks highlights the need for early containment and disengagement protocols once physical aggression occurs, including clear criteria for scene withdrawal and rapid coordination with law enforcement [22]. Second, the high prevalence of verbal abuse underscores the importance of treating routine hostility as a legitimate occupational hazard rather than an expected part of emergency work, with clear organizational policies for reporting and response [28, 42]. Third, institutional strengthening is critical. EMS organizations require formal incident reporting systems, post-incident review processes, and access to psychosocial support services to reduce underreporting and mitigate long-term harm [14, 23, 26]. At the national level, the results point to the need for EMS-specific violence prevention standards and stronger intersectoral collaboration, particularly in high-risk contexts identified by EMTs such as crowd control, domestic violence responses, and gang-related environments [22, 30, 34].

This study advances the literature by providing a validated latent structure of workplace violence among EMTs in a low-resource EMS context and by empirically demonstrating how different forms of violence co-occur. In contrast to prior studies that examine verbal or physical violence in isolation, the EMS approach highlights physical assault as a central domain linking routine verbal aggression with more extreme violence. Future research should extend this framework by incorporating organizational and contextual predictors, including station-level workload, dispatch practices, supervisory support, and scene characteristics, as well as longitudinal designs to examine escalation pathways and intervention effects over time [22, 29].

Emergency medical responders such as Emergency Medical Technicians (EMTs) play vital roles as frontline pre-hospital emergency care professionals, providing immediate stabilization, treatment, and transportation of patients under critical conditions to the nearest health facility [3]. Globally, health professionals continue to be vulnerable to workplace violence. The percentage of health personnel who experience physical and non-physical violence during their jobs ranges from 8% to 38% [4], and many more are subjected to verbal abuse or threats. The current study assessed the multidimensional nature of workplace violence against EMTs and mitigation strategies in the Ghana National Ambulance Service (NAS), which developed three constructs, including the types of physical assault, severe violent attacks, and verbal/non-physical abuse. The study found that over 80% of EMTs expressed their level of worry about violent attacks in their discharge of pre-hospital care. The issue of workplace violence is high; prior systematic review findings are in line with this current study’s results, which report between 53% and 93% of EMTs experiencing workplace violence attacks during emergencies [5]. Additionally, a qualitative study revealed that pre-hospital health professionals indicate persistent fear, stress, and perceived risk when delivering emergency care [43]. While the perceived mitigation strategies (controlling and preventing attacks) were identified as managing the aggressive behavior of offenders, defensive tactical training, and training EMTs to be more resilient, weapon training, and martial arts training were identified as methods of controlling workplace violence.

Limitations of study

Despite the study’s advantages, it has several shortcomings. The sample was purposely selected from three major cities, but it does not include all EMTs in Ghana. Therefore, the findings may not be entirely generalizable to the national workforce. Additionally, important contextual factors—such as organizational culture, supervisory procedures, and environmental circumstances—were not explicitly explored. This limits the analytical scope. Although advanced analytical approaches were used, latent-variable modelling is still vulnerable to underlying assumptions. These assumptions may not fully describe the multidimensional nature of workplace violence encountered by EMTs. The cross-sectional nature of this study also limits the ability to determine a causal relationship between different forms of workplace violence against EMTs. Therefore, the observed relationship between violence domains is viewed as correlational, not causal. Further studies can test structural relationships by assessing the direct and indirect effects of the latent variables. Again, to improve EMT performance and patient outcomes in the Ghana National Ambulance Service, longitudinal and multilevel research must focus on the impacts of workload, burnout, and workplace violence. Key factors include the effects of fatigue and psychological distress on response times and care quality. Immediate protective measures against physical aggression are essential due to rising threats, while proactive risk management is advocated.

Conclusion and policy implication

This study demonstrates that workplace violence against Emergency Medical Technicians in Ghana demands immediate, coordinated institutional and policy responses. The systemic nature of this occupational risk, including verbal abuse, physical assault, and severe violence, exposes the inadequacy of fragmented or reactive safety measures. Stakeholders must act decisively to embed comprehensive preventive efforts into formal EMS governance structures and move beyond individual coping strategies.

The findings support the urgent need for national EMS-specific violence prevention policies. Key recommendations are: (i) Clearly define thresholds for intervention, reporting, and escalation; (ii) Recognize physical assault as a trigger for measures like scene withdrawal and law enforcement involvement; (iii) Institutionalize documentation of verbal abuse to reduce normalization and improve monitoring; (iv) Prioritize regular training on situational awareness, risk assessment, and coordinated response—beyond de-escalation or self-defense. Integrate these steps into EMS policy and ensure enforcement through operational standards. This integrated approach is critical for staff safety and health system strengthening in Ghana.

At the system level, focus on strengthening reporting mechanisms, conducting thorough post-incident reviews, and ensuring access to psychosocial support for the EMS workforce. Integrate these actions into existing occupational health and safety frameworks and uphold clear administrative accountability. At the policy level, explicitly define workplace violence as a threat to service delivery, workforce retention, and patient safety, and require coordinated collaboration between health authorities, security agencies, and local government.

The study’s findings affect EMS research, practice, and policy. Serious violent assaults and physical aggression are closely linked. This means physical aggression should be treated as a warning sign for escalation. Immediate protective measures, such as dispatch escalation and scene retreat, are necessary. Proactive risk management is essential for EMS safety. The prevalence of verbal abuse in EMS underscores the need for institutional reform and proper documentation of occupational hazards to enhance worker well-being and inform policy development. This requires cultural reforms and better documentation to recognize it as an occupational hazard. Improved documentation helps policy and reduces underreporting. Exposure to low-level violence is linked to more stress and burnout for workers over time.
