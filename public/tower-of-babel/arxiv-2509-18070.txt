Long-wave instability of periodic shear flows for the 2D Navier-Stokes equations
Maria Colombo, Michele Dolce, Riccardo Montalto, Paolo Ventura

Abstract
. In 1959, Kolmogorov proposed to study the instability of the shear flow ( sin ⁡ ( y ) , 0 ) (\sin(y),0) in the vanishing viscosity regime in tori 𝕋 α × 𝕋 \mathbb{T}_{\alpha}\times\mathbb{T} . This question was later resolved by Meshalkin and Sinai. We extend the problem to general shear flows ( U ⁡ ( y ) , 0 ) (U(y),0) and show that every U ⁡ ( y ) U(y) exhibits long-wave instability whenever ‖ ∂ y − 1 U ‖ L 2 > ν \|\partial_{y}^{-1}U\|_{L^{2}}>\nu and α ≪ ν \alpha\ll\nu , with ν \nu being the kinematic viscosity. This instability mechanism confirms previous findings by Yudovich in 1966, supported also by several numerical results, and is established through two independent approaches: one via the construction of Kato’s isomorphism and one via normal-forms. Unlike in many other applications of the latter methods, both proofs deal with the presence of a delicate term in the linearized operator that becomes singular as α \alpha approaches 0 0 .



Key words:
Fluid Mechanics, Navier-Stokes equations, long wave perturbations, Kato spectral method, Normal Forms.

Instability of shear flows

1.1.2.
Linear to nonlinear instability

Once the linear stability properties of a given steady state are understood, it is natural to ask whether linear instability implies nonlinear instability (in an appropriate sense). For the Navier–Stokes equations, Yudovich
[
56

showed that linear and nonlinear stability/instability are indeed related, at least when the velocity is measured in the

norm, with
D

[

proved a fairly general result for the Euler equations, establishing that nonlinear instability in

holds whenever the linearized problem around a given steady state possesses a sufficiently large unstable eigenvalue. As a concrete example,
[

extended the methods of

to demonstrate the passage from linear to nonlinear instability in 2D Euler for shear profiles

on
\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{T}

Subsequently, Grenier
[
29
]
established another general criterion for the Euler equations, showing that sufficiently strong linear instability implies nonlinear instability of the velocity in
(L^{\infty}\cap L^{2})(D)
. Later, Z. Lin
[
42
]
extended and refined the results of
[
25
]
for the 2D Euler equations, while Friedlander, Pavlović, and Shvydkoy
[

generalized the results of

,

to demonstrate the passage from linear to nonlinear instability for the Navier–Stokes equations in

for any

.

Remark 1.2

(Nonlinear long-wave instability)
.

All the aforementioned results on linear-to-nonlinear instability rely on unstable eigenvalues whose size is at least comparable to that of the most unstable eigenvalue(s). For the Navier–Stokes equations, in view of these results and the standard properties of the Laplacian, the presence of an unstable component in the spectrum of
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{L}}_{\nu,\alpha}
suffices to guarantee a nonlinear instability statement. However, we do not characterize the full spectrum outside the long-wave regime (in particular, for
\delta_{0}\nu\leq\alpha|k|\leq 1
), and thus it remains unclear whether the eigenvalues identified in Theorem
1.1
are indeed the most unstable ones. Consequently, existing results cannot be directly applied to promote the linear instability established in Theorem
1.1
to a nonlinear instability statement. More precisely, one would like to track the expected exponential growth of the eigenfunction in
Eq.

1.8
, over a time scale on which the nonlinear correction remains negligible.

1.2.2.
Normal-Form reduction

We now describe the normal-form approach appearing in

3
for the analysis of the spectrum of the linear operator

given in

1.14
and
1.15
. Normal-form methods have been fruitfully used in Fluid Mechanics in order to construct periodic and quasi-periodic traveling-wave solutions of Euler and Navier-Stokes equations in several situations: see for instance
[
3
,
23
,
15
]
for the Euler equations on rectangular tori with quasi-periodic forcing terms,
[
22
]
for quasi-periodic traveling waves near Couette flows and
[
27
,
32
,
10
,
33
]
for quasi-periodic vortex patches. In the aforementioned papers, one deals with the so-called problem of small divisors, namely the fact that the linearized equation at the origin has spectrum accumulating to zero. The normal-form techniques are used essentially to integrate the linearized equations at any approximate solutions and for giving a sharp analysis of the spectrum of such operators. This methods allow to construct a bounded and invertible transformation (on Sobolev spaces) that molds the linearized operator into a much simpler one with a prescribed structure, i.e. in most cases a diagonal or block-diagonal operator. Consequently, the spectrum of the transformed operator is easily studied. In this case, we will consider
\mathcal{M}_{\nu,\varepsilon}
as the unperturbed operator whose spectrum is equal to the one of
\nu\mathcal{D}_{\varepsilon}
. Taking into account the “double resonances” of
\nu\mathcal{D}_{\varepsilon}
on
L^{2}(\mathbb{T})
(
-\nu\varepsilon^{2}
is a simple eigenvalue and
-\nu(j^{2}+\varepsilon^{2})
,
j\neq 0
, is double), we block diagonalize the operator
{\mathcal{L}}_{\nu,\varepsilon}
and we compute sharp asymptotic expansion of its eigenvalues. In particular, we obtain that the perturbed eigenvalue
\lambda^{(0)}_{\nu,\varepsilon}
, bifurcating from
j=0
, is simple and has positive real part, whereas for any
j\in\mathbb{N}
, there are two (or one double) perturbed eigenvalues
\lambda^{(\pm j)}_{\nu,\varepsilon}
which are
\varepsilon
-close to
-\nu j^{2}
. The major difficulty is again the presence of a non-perturbative term in
{\mathcal{L}}_{\nu,\varepsilon}
. In particular,
\frac{\mathrm{i}\,}{\varepsilon}U^{\prime\prime}(y)\Pi_{0}
in
{\mathcal{M}}_{\nu,\varepsilon}
(see
Eq.

1.15
) has size
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{-1})
. The key point to deal with this term is that this operator is rank-1 and nilpotent.
We describe more in detail our strategy, which is split in two parts:

(i)

First, in
Section

3.1
, we decouple the unstable direction of
\mathcal{L}_{\nu,\varepsilon}
from its stable invariant hyperplane. In this way, we isolate the unstable eigenvalue and obtain its asymptotic expansion.

(ii)

Then, in
Section

3.2
, we study the restriction of
\mathcal{L}_{\nu,\varepsilon}
to the stable hyperplane obtained in part
(\mathrm{i})
. This operator is a perturbation of size
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon)
of
\nu\partial_{yy}
–restricted to Fourier modes different from zero–
and it can be transformed into a new operator with only
2\times 2
diagonal blocks –related to the double eigenvalues of its leading part–. The block-diagonalization follows by a fixed-point argument, which we can implement because of the absence of small divisors.

(i)
Decoupling of the unstable direction.

To study
\mathcal{L}_{\nu,\varepsilon}
, we can use the natural direct sum decomposition of
H^{s}(\mathbb{T})=\mathbb{C}\oplus H^{s}_{0}(\mathbb{T})
, with
s\geq 2
and
H^{s}_{0}(\mathbb{T})
being zero-average functions, to represent the operator as the matrix

\mathtt{L}_{\nu,\varepsilon}\coloneqq\begin{bmatrix}\Pi_{0}{\mathcal{L}}_{\nu,\varepsilon}\Pi_{0}&\Pi_{0}{\mathcal{L}}_{\nu,\varepsilon}\Pi_{\neq}\\
\Pi_{\neq}{\mathcal{L}}_{\nu,\varepsilon}\Pi_{0}&\Pi_{\neq}{\mathcal{L}}_{\nu,\varepsilon}\Pi_{\neq}\end{bmatrix}.

By properties of the projection
\Pi_{0}
, one can think of
\Pi_{0}{\mathcal{L}}_{\nu,\varepsilon}\Pi_{\neq}
and
\Pi_{\neq}{\mathcal{L}}_{\nu,\varepsilon}\Pi_{0}
as an infinite dimensional row and column vector respectively. The part with
\Pi_{0}\mathcal{L}_{\nu,\varepsilon}\Pi_{0}
does instead encode the simple eigenvalue associated to
\mathcal{M}_{\nu,\varepsilon}
, which is the one being perturbed to generate the instability. To capture this, we aim at simplifying the structure of the matrix
{\mathtt{L}}_{\nu,\varepsilon}
by conjugating it with a matrix
{\mathtt{T}}
so that

(1.20)

{\mathtt{T}}\,{\mathtt{L}}_{\nu,\varepsilon}{\mathtt{T}}^{-1}=\begin{bmatrix}\lambda_{\nu,\varepsilon}^{(0)}&\boldsymbol{0}^{\top}\\
\boldsymbol{0}&{\mathcal{L}}_{\nu,\varepsilon}^{(1)}\end{bmatrix}.

Here
\lambda_{\nu,\varepsilon}^{(0)}
is exactly the eigenvalue in Theorem
1.1
and the unstable direction is indeed
(1,\boldsymbol{0})^{\top}
in the new basis, hence the desired decoupling would be achieved. Instead,
\mathcal{L}_{\nu,\varepsilon}^{(1)}
is a is a suitable modification of
\mathcal{L}_{\nu,\varepsilon}
acting on
H^{s}_{0}(\mathbb{T})
, accounting for the stable part of the operator discussed afterwards. To justify this decoupling of the unstable direction, it remains to find
{\mathtt{T}}
(and block-diagonalize
\mathcal{L}_{\nu,\varepsilon}^{(1)}
). Inspired by the finite dimensional case, we make an ansatz of the form

{\mathtt{T}}\coloneqq\begin{bmatrix}1&X^{\top}\\
Y&{\rm Id}\end{bmatrix}\,,

where
X,Y
are two
H^{s}_{0}(\mathbb{T})
functions and
{\mathtt{T}}
is invertible
if
\langle X,Y\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T})}\neq 1
(see Lemma
3.2
). To obtain the desired structure after the conjugation, one has to solve quadratic expressions for both
X
and
Y
, which is done by a fixed-point argument in Lemmas
3.6
and
3.7
. The outcome is that

X=\mathcal{O}\big(\frac{\varepsilon^{3}}{\nu}\big)\qquad\text{and}\qquad Y=\mathcal{O}\big(\frac{1}{\nu\varepsilon}\big)\,,

which imply that our transformation
{\mathtt{T}}={\rm Id}+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{-1})
is “far from the identity”, reflecting from the non-perturbative nature of the problem (compare with the need of applying the Shermann-Morrison formula in Kato’s approach).
To compute the desired expansion of
\lambda_{\nu,\varepsilon}^{(0)}
, we have to precisely expand the quadratic form involving
X,Y
that is defining the eigenvalue, which is done in Lemma
3.8
.

(ii).
Block-diagonalization of the stable part.
Having at hand the decomposition in
Eq.

1.20
, we need to block-diagonalize the operator
\mathcal{L}_{\nu,\varepsilon}^{(1)}
, where the notion of a block-diagonal operator is the natural one based on the standard Fourier basis, see
Eq.

1.26
. A first technical step is to introduce a suitable notion of block-diagonal decay norm
|\cdot|_{s}
, that provide us with a nice functional framework, see
Eq.

3.77
and the properties listed in the Lemmas below. For regular enough shear flow profiles, we are allowed to write

(1.21)

\mathcal{L}_{\nu,\varepsilon}^{(1)}=\nu{\partial}_{yy}+{\mathcal{Q}},\qquad|\mathcal{Q}|_{s}=\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon).

Here
\mathcal{Q}:H^{s}_{0}(\mathbb{T})\to H^{s}_{0}(\mathbb{T})
is a linear operator depending on
X,Y
defined before. In this formulation the problem becomes amenable to the application of the usual strategy in normal forms methods. Namely, whenever
\varepsilon\ll\nu
, we can construct by a fixed point argument
a map
\Psi:H^{s}_{0}(\mathbb{T})\to H^{s}_{0}(\mathbb{T})
with
|\Psi|_{s}=\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon\nu^{-1})
such that

({\rm Id}+\Psi)^{-1}{\mathcal{L}}_{\nu,\varepsilon}({\rm Id}+\Psi)=\nu\partial_{yy}+{\mathcal{Z}}

where
|{\mathcal{Z}}|_{s}=\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon)
and is
block-diagonal
with
2\times 2
blocks. This is proved in Proposition
3.13
. Thanks to this structure, we can easily conclude that the spectrum of
\nu\partial_{yy}+{\mathcal{Z}}
is the union of the spectra of each block-diagonal piece, which are given by
\{\lambda_{\nu,\varepsilon}^{(j)}\}_{j\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}}
with
\lambda_{\nu,\varepsilon}^{(j)}=-\nu j^{2}+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon)
. Hence all the eigenvalues of
{\mathcal{L}}_{\nu,\varepsilon}^{(1)}
have negative real parts and the stable part is also fully characterized.

1.3.
Notation

Given a function
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{T})
, its Fourier decomposition is given by

(1.22)

f(y)=\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}f_{j}e^{ijy},\qquad f_{j}\coloneqq\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{T}}e^{-\mathrm{i}\,jy}f(y){\rm d}y.

In particular we introduce the orthogonal projections

(1.23)

\Pi_{0}f\coloneqq f_{0}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{T}}f(y){\rm d}y,\qquad\Pi_{\neq}\coloneqq{\mathrm{Id}}-\Pi_{0}\,,

and, for every
j\in{\mathbb{N}}
,

(1.24)

\Pi_{j}f(y)\coloneqq f_{j}e^{\mathrm{i}\,jy}+f_{-j}e^{-\mathrm{i}\,jy}\,,

so that
f=\Pi_{0}f+\sum_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}\Pi_{j}f
.

Given a closed linear operator
{\mathcal{R}}:\mathrm{D}(\mathcal{R})\subseteq L^{2}(\mathbb{T})\to L^{2}(\mathbb{T})
, with
C^{\infty}(\mathbb{T})\subseteq\mathrm{D}(\mathcal{R})
, we can define its matrix elements as

{\mathcal{R}}_{j}^{j^{\prime}}\coloneqq\langle{\mathcal{R}}[e^{ij^{\prime}y}]\,,\,e^{ijy}\rangle_{L^{2}},\quad\forall j,j^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{Z}}\,.

Furthermore, one has

(1.25)

{\mathcal{R}}=\sum_{j,j^{\prime}\geq 0}\Pi_{j}{\mathcal{R}}\Pi_{j^{\prime}}

and for any
j,j^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{N}}
, we can identify the operator
\Pi_{j}{\mathcal{R}}\Pi_{j^{\prime}}
with the
2\times 2
matrix

\Pi_{j}{\mathcal{R}}\Pi_{j^{\prime}}\equiv\begin{pmatrix}{\mathcal{R}}_{j}^{j^{\prime}}&{\mathcal{R}}_{j}^{-j^{\prime}}\\
{\mathcal{R}}_{-j}^{j^{\prime}}&{\mathcal{R}}_{-j}^{-j^{\prime}}\end{pmatrix}\,.

In particular, the operator
{\mathcal{R}}
is said to be
block-diagonal
when

(1.26)

\Pi_{j}{\mathcal{R}}\Pi_{j^{\prime}}=0,\quad\forall j,j^{\prime}\geq 0,\quad j\neq j^{\prime}\,.

In the sequel, given a bounded linear operator
\mathcal{T}:X\to Y
, with
X,Y
being two normed spaces, we denote its standard operator norm as

(1.27)

\|\mathcal{T}\|_{\mathcal{B}(X,Y)}\coloneqq\sup_{\|x\|_{X}=1}\|\mathcal{T}x\|_{Y}.

We then apply Lemma

-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{\nu}\|{\partial}_{y}^{-1}U\|^{2}_{L^{2}}+\mathcal{O}_{\rm fun}(\nu^{-1}\varepsilon^{3}).

Being the unperturbed eigenvector
1
, this is the desired correction of the eigenvalue and is in agreement with the result announced in Remark
1.4
.

Let us mention that a case where the Taylor dispersion property of passive scalars can be exploited in fluid dynamics is for perturbations around the Couette flow
U(y)=y
in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Indeed, the linearized problem involves exactly an operator like
\mathcal{T}_{\nu,\varepsilon}
and Arbon and Bedrossian
[
1
]
were recently able to prove that Taylor dispersion holds also at the nonlinear level (using a hypocoercivity method).

Finally, in Kinetic Theory the diffusion is replaced by a more complicated collision operator with a larger kernel. In some cases, the Kato’s spectral approach used in
[
26
]
to deduce hydrodynamic limits should readily imply the Taylor dispersion mechanism in the Boltzmann equation, which was first proved in
[
5
]
with hypocoercivity methods (and for more general collision kernels).

3.
Normal-form approach

In this section we perform various conjugations which lead to a complete block-diagonalization of the operator
\mathcal{L}_{\nu,\varepsilon}
in
Eq.

1.13
. We denote by
H^{s}\equiv H^{s}(\mathbb{T})
,
s\geq 0
the standard
L^{2}
-based Sobolev space with norm
\|\cdot\|_{s}\equiv\|\cdot\|_{H^{s}}
. For simplicity, we require slightly more regularity on the background shear flow as we assume
U\in H^{s+2}(\mathbb{T})
for some
s>\tfrac{1}{2}
. In this way
the operator
\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon}
in
Eq.

1.15
is a bounded endomorphism of
H^{s}(\mathbb{T})
, the latter being an algebra. We denote by
\|\cdot\|_{2\times 2}
the standard Hilbert-Schmidt norm on
2\times 2
matrices.
The main result of this section is the following

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
s>\frac{1}{2}
and
U\in H^{s+2}(\mathbb{T})
be a shear flow profile with zero average. There exists constants
\delta_{0}\in(0,1)
and
C>1
depending only on
\|U\|_{s+2}
and
s
, such that, if
\varepsilon\nu^{-1}\leq\delta_{0}
then there exists an invertible map
\Phi_{\nu,\varepsilon}\in{\mathcal{B}}(H^{s},H^{s})
with inverse
\Phi_{\nu,\varepsilon}^{-1}\in{\mathcal{B}}(H^{s},H^{s})
such that

{\mathcal{N}}_{\nu,\varepsilon}\coloneqq\Phi_{\nu,\varepsilon}^{-1}{\mathcal{L}}_{\nu,\varepsilon}\Phi_{\nu,\varepsilon}

is block diagonal, i.e.

{\mathcal{N}}_{\nu,\varepsilon}[h]=\lambda^{(0)}_{\nu,\varepsilon}\Pi_{0}h+\sum_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}\Pi_{j}{\mathcal{N}}_{\nu,\varepsilon}\Pi_{j}[\Pi_{j}h]\,,

with the projections
\Pi_{j}
in
Eq.

1.24
.
The
2\times 2
matrices
\Pi_{j}\mathcal{N}_{\nu,\varepsilon}\Pi_{j}
and the maps
\Phi_{\nu,\varepsilon}^{\pm 1}
satisfy the estimates

(3.1)

\displaystyle\|\Pi_{j}{\mathcal{N}}_{\nu,\varepsilon}\Pi_{j}+\nu j^{2}{\mathrm{Id}}\|_{2\times 2}\leq C\varepsilon,\qquad\|\Phi_{\nu,\varepsilon}^{\pm 1}\|_{{\mathcal{B}}(H^{\sigma},H^{\sigma})}\leq C\varepsilon^{-1}\,,

for all
0\leq\sigma\leq s
and
j\in\mathbb{N}
.
Moreover, the eigenpair
(\lambda^{(0)}_{\nu,\varepsilon},V_{\nu,\varepsilon}^{(0)})

satisfies

(3.2)

\displaystyle\lambda^{(0)}_{\nu,\varepsilon}=\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{\nu}\Big(\|{\partial}_{y}^{-1}U\|^{2}_{L^{2}}-\nu^{2}+\mathcal{O}\big(\frac{\varepsilon}{\nu}+\varepsilon^{2}\big)\Big),\qquad\|V_{\nu,\varepsilon}^{(0)}-(U(y)-\mathrm{i}\,\nu\varepsilon)\|_{s+2}\leq C(\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{\nu^{2}}+\varepsilon^{2})\,.

In general, we have
\sigma_{L^{2}}(\mathcal{L}_{\nu,\varepsilon})=\{\lambda_{\nu,\varepsilon}^{(\pm j)}\}_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
with

|\lambda_{\nu,\varepsilon}^{(\pm j)}+\nu j^{2}|\leq C\varepsilon,\quad\forall j\in\mathbb{N}.

Note that if we choose
s=1
, i.e.
U\in H^{3}(\mathbb{T})
, the theorem above clearly implies Theorem
1.1
.

We split the proof of Theorem
3.1
into two subsections: we first identify the leading order transformations removing the parts of order
\varepsilon^{-1}
. Such transformation will also isolate the unstable part of the spectrum from its stable part. Then, we proceed with a more standard perturbative argument to block-diagonalize the full operator.

To ease the notation, in the proofs we will write
a\lesssim b
where
a\leq Cb
for a constant
C>0
depending only on
\|U\|_{s+2}
and
s
, but independent of
\nu,\varepsilon
. To invert some operators, we need

(3.3)

\,\varepsilon\leq\delta_{0}\nu\,,

with
\delta_{0}=\delta_{0}(s,\|U\|_{{s+2}})
sufficiently small. The precise smallness condition can be deduced from the rather explicit bounds given below. However, we do not keep track of this precise constant. We will abuse in notation and always denote with
\delta_{0}
the necessary smallness parameter needed in each different statement. The
\delta_{0}
in Theorem
3.1
is clearly the smallest of these ones.

3.1.
Decoupling of stable and unstable modes

We aim to separate the one-dimensional unstable eigenspace of the operator
\mathcal{L}_{\nu,\varepsilon}
in
Eq.

1.13
from its stable invariant space of codimension
1
.
We shall exploit the splitting, for every
s\geq 0
,

(3.4)

H^{s}(\mathbb{T})=\mathbb{C}\oplus H^{s}_{0}(\mathbb{T})\,,\quad\text{where}\quad H^{s}_{0}(\mathbb{T})\coloneqq\big\{f\in H^{s}(\mathbb{T})\;:\;\Pi_{0}f=0\big\}

is the closed subspace of
H^{s}(\mathbb{T})
formed by zero-average functions.
According to the splitting
Eq.

3.4
, we regard any bounded operator
\mathcal{A}:H^{s+t}(\mathbb{T})\to H^{s}(\mathbb{T})
of order

as a matrix
\mathtt{A}:\mathbb{C}\oplus H^{s}_{0}(\mathbb{T})\to\mathbb{C}\oplus H^{s}_{0}(\mathbb{T})
of the form

\displaystyle\mathtt{A}\coloneqq\begin{bmatrix}\Pi_{0}\mathcal{A}[1]&(\Pi_{\neq}\mathcal{A}^{*}[1])^{\top}\\
\Pi_{\neq}\mathcal{A}[1]&\Pi_{\neq}\mathcal{A}\Pi_{\neq}\end{bmatrix}\,,

where
\mathcal{A}^{*}
is the adjoint operator of
\mathcal{A}
with respect to the
L^{2}
-inner product and
f^{\top}
is the bounded linear functional that sends

, for every
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{T})
. We point out that the mapping
f\mapsto f^{\top}
is antilinear, namely
(\zeta f)^{\top}=\overline{\zeta}f^{\top}
for every
\zeta\in\mathbb{C}
.
The action of the matrix
\mathtt{A}
in
Eq.

3.5
is linked to its associated operator
\mathcal{A}
: more precisely, for every
\mathcal{A}\in\mathcal{B}\big(H^{s+t}(\mathbb{T});H^{s}(\mathbb{T})\big)
, the associated matrix
\mathtt{A}
in
Eq.

3.5
satisfies

(3.6)

\mathtt{A}\begin{bmatrix}\Pi_{0}h\\
\Pi_{\neq}h(y)\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\Pi_{0}\mathcal{A}[h]\\
\Pi_{\neq}\mathcal{A}[h](y)\end{bmatrix}\,\qquad\mbox{for every }h\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}).

Indeed,

(3.7)

\displaystyle\footnotesize\begin{bmatrix}\Pi_{0}\mathcal{A}[1]&(\Pi_{\neq}\mathcal{A}^{*}[1])^{\top}\\
\Pi_{\neq}\mathcal{A}[1]&\Pi_{\neq}\mathcal{A}\Pi_{\neq}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\Pi_{0}h\\
\Pi_{\neq}h\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}(\Pi_{0}\mathcal{A}[1])\Pi_{0}h+\langle\Pi_{\neq}h,\Pi_{\neq}\mathcal{A}^{*}[1]\rangle\\
(\Pi_{0}h)\Pi_{\neq}\mathcal{A}[1]+\Pi_{\neq}\mathcal{A}[\Pi_{\neq}h]\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\Pi_{0}\mathcal{A}[\Pi_{0}h]+\langle\mathcal{A}[\Pi_{\neq}h],1\rangle\\
\Pi_{\neq}\mathcal{A}[\Pi_{0}h]+\Pi_{\neq}\mathcal{A}[\Pi_{\neq}h]\end{bmatrix}

which gives the right-hand side of
Eq.

3.6
.

In our case, first observe that

(3.8)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\nu,\varepsilon}^{*}

\displaystyle=\nu\mathcal{D}_{\varepsilon}+\frac{\mathrm{i}\,}{\varepsilon}\Pi_{0}\circ(U^{\prime\prime}(y)\mathrm{Id})+\mathrm{i}\,\varepsilon\big(U(y)+\Pi_{\neq}\circ(-{\partial}_{yy}+\varepsilon^{2})^{-1}\circ(U^{\prime\prime}\mathrm{Id})\big),

(3.9)

\displaystyle\Pi_{\neq}(\mathcal{L}_{\nu,\varepsilon}^{*}[1])

\displaystyle=\mathrm{i}\,\varepsilon\big(U(y)-({\partial}_{yy}-\varepsilon^{2})^{-1}({\partial}_{yy}-\varepsilon^{2}+\varepsilon^{2})U(y)\big)=-\mathrm{i}\,\varepsilon^{3}({\partial}_{yy}-\varepsilon^{2})U(y).

Therefore, the matrix
\mathtt{L}_{\nu,\varepsilon}
associated as in
Eq.

3.5
with the operator
\mathcal{L}_{\nu,\varepsilon}
in
Eq.

1.13
is given by

(3.10)

\displaystyle\mathtt{L}_{\nu,\varepsilon}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\lx@cref{creftype\lx@tilde refnum}{matrixrepresentation}}}{{=}}

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\Pi_{0}\mathcal{L}_{\nu,\varepsilon}[1]&(\Pi_{\neq}\mathcal{L}_{\nu,\varepsilon}^{*}[1])^{\top}\\
\Pi_{\neq}\mathcal{L}_{\nu,\varepsilon}[1]&\Pi_{\neq}\mathcal{L}_{\nu,\varepsilon}\Pi_{\neq}\end{bmatrix}

(3.11)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\lx@cref{creftype\lx@tilde refnum}{cLnueps}}}{{=}}

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}-\nu\varepsilon^{2}&\big(-\mathrm{i}\,\varepsilon^{3}({\partial}_{yy}-\varepsilon^{2})^{-1}U(y)\big)^{\top}\\
-\frac{\mathrm{i}\,}{\varepsilon}U^{\prime\prime}(y)-\mathrm{i}\,\varepsilon U(y)&\Pi_{\neq}\mathcal{L}_{\nu,\varepsilon}\Pi_{\neq}\end{bmatrix}

In the following we will denote
\mathcal{L}_{\nu,\varepsilon}^{\sharp}\coloneqq\Pi_{\neq}\mathcal{L}_{\nu,\varepsilon}\Pi_{\neq}

We aim to conjugate the matrix

\mathtt{T}\coloneqq\begin{bmatrix}1&X(y)^{\top}\\

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
s\geq 0
,
X\in L^{2}_{0}(\mathbb{T})
and

. If

with inverse given by the matrix product

\mathtt{T}^{-1}=\begin{bmatrix}1&-X(y)^{\top}\\
-Y(y)&{\mathrm{Id}}\end{bmatrix}{\footnotesize\begin{bmatrix}\dfrac{1}{1-\langle Y,X\rangle}&0^{\top}\\

Proof.

Let us observe that

(3.14)

\displaystyle{\footnotesize\begin{bmatrix}1&X(y)^{\top}\\
Y(y)&{\mathrm{Id}}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}1&-X(y)^{\top}\\

As a consequence, if the “diagonal” matrix in the middle of

(3.15)

\mathtt{T}^{-1}=\begin{bmatrix}1&-X(y)^{\top}\\
-Y(y)&{\mathrm{Id}}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}1-\langle Y,X\rangle&0^{\top}\\

If

\begin{bmatrix}1-\langle Y,X\rangle&0^{\top}\\

∎

By applying first the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and then

For the function

\displaystyle\Psi\coloneqq{\bf T}({\mathcal{R}})\coloneqq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j,j^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{N}}\\

.
Thus, for every
j^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{N}}
,

\sum_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}\langle j-j^{\prime}\rangle^{s+1}\|\Pi_{j}{\bf T}({\mathcal{R}})\Pi_{j^{\prime}}\|_{2\times 2}^{2}\lesssim\nu^{-2}\sum_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}\langle j-j^{\prime}\rangle^{2s}\|\Pi_{j}{\mathcal{R}}\Pi_{j^{\prime}}\|_{2\times 2}^{2}\lesssim\nu^{-2}|{\mathcal{R}}|_{s}^{2}.

By taking the supremum with respect to
j^{\prime}\in\mathbb{N}
, we obtain

|{\bf T}({\mathcal{R}})|_{s+1}^{2}\lesssim\nu^{-2}|{\mathcal{R}}|_{s}^{2}
.
∎

The block diagonalization step

The main result of this section is the definition of an off-diagonal operator
\Psi
that block-diagonalize the operator
\mathcal{L}_{\nu,\varepsilon}^{(1)}
. More precisely, we have the following.

Proposition 3.13
.

Let
s>\frac{1}{2}
,
U\in H^{s+2}(\mathbb{T})
. Then there exists
\delta_{0}\in(0,1)
small enough, depending only on
s,\|U\|_{s+2}
, such that if
\varepsilon\nu^{-1}\leq\delta_{0}
then there exists unique
\Psi\in\mathcal{M}_{od}^{s}
,
\mathcal{Z}\in\mathcal{M}_{bd}
such that

(3.87)

\nu|\Psi|_{s}+|\mathcal{Z}|_{s}\lesssim\varepsilon

for which the following block-diagonalization holds true

(3.88)

({\mathrm{Id}}+\Psi)^{-1}\mathcal{L}_{\nu,\varepsilon}^{(1)}({\mathrm{Id}}+\Psi)=\nu{\partial}_{yy}+\mathcal{Z}\coloneqq{\mathcal{N}}_{\nu,\varepsilon}\,.

Proof.

Recall that
{\mathcal{L}}_{\nu,\varepsilon}^{(1)}=\nu\partial_{yy}+{\mathcal{Q}}
. We want to solve the equation

(\nu\partial_{yy}+{\mathcal{Q}})({\rm Id}+\Psi)=({\rm Id}+\Psi)(\nu\partial_{yy}+{\mathcal{Z}})

which is equivalent to the equation

[\nu\partial_{yy}\,,\,\Psi]={\mathcal{Z}}-{\mathcal{Q}}-{\mathcal{Q}}\Psi+\Psi{\mathcal{Z}}

We look for solutions
\Psi\in{\mathcal{M}}^{s}_{od}
and
{\mathcal{Z}}\in{\mathcal{M}}_{bd}
. If we apply the projection
\Pi_{bd},\Pi_{od}
to the latter equation, using
\Pi_{bd}\big([\nu\partial_{yy}\,,\,\Psi]\big)=0
and the fact that by Lemma
3.81
, one has
\Pi_{bd}\big(\Psi{\mathcal{Z}}\big)=0
,
\Pi_{od}(\Psi{\mathcal{Z}})=\Psi{\mathcal{Z}}
, we get

(3.89)

\begin{cases}[\nu\partial_{yy}\,,\,\Psi]=-\Pi_{od}({\mathcal{Q}})-\Pi_{od}({\mathcal{Q}}\Psi)+\Psi{\mathcal{Z}}\\
{\mathcal{Z}}-\Pi_{bd}({\mathcal{Q}})-\Pi_{bd}({\mathcal{Q}}\Psi)=0\,.\end{cases}

From the second equation we recover
{\mathcal{Z}}\equiv{\mathcal{Z}}(\Psi)
as a function of
\Psi
, namely

{\mathcal{Z}}(\Psi)\coloneqq\Pi_{bd}({\mathcal{Q}})+\Pi_{bd}({\mathcal{Q}}\Psi)

By applying Lemmas
3.12
and
3.9
, one easily shows that

(3.90)

\displaystyle|{\mathcal{Z}}(\Psi)|_{s}\lesssim\varepsilon(1+|\Psi|_{s})\,,

\displaystyle|{\mathcal{Z}}(\Psi_{1})-{\mathcal{Z}}(\Psi_{2})|_{s}\lesssim\varepsilon|\Psi_{1}-\Psi_{2}|_{s}\,.

Therefore, by substituting
{\mathcal{Z}}(\Psi)
in the first equation in
Eq.

3.89
, one obtains

(3.91)

[\nu\partial_{yy}\,,\,\Psi]={\mathcal{F}}(\Psi)\,,\quad{\mathcal{F}}(\Psi)\coloneqq-\Pi_{od}({\mathcal{Q}})-\Pi_{od}({\mathcal{Q}}\Psi)+(\Psi{\mathcal{Z}}(\Psi))\,.

By applying Lemmas
3.12
and
3.9
and using the estimates
Eq.

3.90
, one deduces that the map

{\mathcal{M}}^{s}_{od}\to{\mathcal{M}}^{s}_{od},\quad\Psi\mapsto{\mathcal{F}}(\Psi)

satisfies the estimates

(3.92)

\displaystyle|{\mathcal{F}}(\Psi)|_{s}\lesssim\varepsilon(1+|\Psi|_{s}+|\Psi|_{s}^{2})\,,\quad\forall\Psi\in{\mathcal{M}}^{s}_{od}

\displaystyle|{\mathcal{F}}(\Psi_{1})-{\mathcal{F}}(\Psi_{2})|_{s}\lesssim\varepsilon(1+|\Psi_{1}|_{s}+|\Psi_{2}|_{s})|\Psi_{1}-\Psi_{2}|_{s},\quad\forall\Psi_{1},\Psi_{2}\in{\mathcal{M}}^{s}_{od}\,.

By using Lemma
3.11
, where the operator
{\bf T}
is introduced, the equation
Eq.

3.91
is equivalent to the fixed-point equation

(3.93)

\Psi={\bf\Phi}(\Psi)\,,\quad{\bf\Phi}(\Psi)\coloneqq{\bf T}({\mathcal{F}}(\Psi))\,.

We define

{\mathcal{B}}_{s}(\rho)\coloneqq\Big\{\Psi\in{\mathcal{M}}^{s}_{od}:|\Psi|_{s}\leq\rho\Big\}

and we claim the following.

Claim.
There exist constants
C_{*}\equiv C_{*}(s)>0
,
\delta_{0}\equiv\delta_{0}(s)>0
such that if
\varepsilon\nu^{-1}\leq\delta_{0}
, then the map

{\bf\Phi}:{\mathcal{B}}_{s}(C_{*}\varepsilon\nu^{-1})\to{\mathcal{B}}_{s}(C_{*}\varepsilon\nu^{-1})

is a contraction.

To prove this claim, let
\Psi\in{\mathcal{B}}_{s}(C_{*}\varepsilon\nu^{-1})
. Then by Lemma
3.11
and estimate
Eq.

3.92
, one gets

|{\bf\Phi}(\Psi)|_{s}\leq C(s)\varepsilon\nu^{-1}(1+C_{*}\varepsilon\nu^{-1}+[C_{*}\varepsilon\nu^{-1}]^{2})\leq C_{*}\varepsilon\nu^{-1},

where we are taking
C_{*}\equiv C_{*}(s)\gg 1
large enough and
\varepsilon\nu^{-1}\ll 1
small enough.
Moreover by taking
\Psi_{1},\Psi_{2}\in{\mathcal{B}}_{s}(C_{*}\varepsilon\nu^{-1})
, one gets that

|{\bf\Phi}(\Psi_{1})-{\bf\Phi}(\Psi_{2})|_{s}\leq C(s)\varepsilon\nu^{-1}(1+2C_{*}\varepsilon\nu^{-1})|\Psi_{1}-\Psi_{2}|_{s}\leq\frac{1}{2}|\Psi_{1}-\Psi_{2}|_{s}

by taking
\varepsilon\nu^{-1}\ll 1
small enough. We have then proved the desired claim.

Thus, by the contraction mapping theorem we find a unique solution
\Psi\in{\mathcal{B}}_{s}(C_{*}\varepsilon\nu^{-1})
of the fixed point equation
\Psi={\bf\Phi}(\Psi)
. Note that by using again Lemma
3.11
(together with
Eq.

3.92
), we also have that
\Psi\in{\mathcal{M}}^{s+1}_{od}
and
|\Psi|_{s+1}\lesssim\varepsilon\nu^{-1}
. By recalling
Eq.

3.90
, we get
|{\mathcal{Z}}|_{s}\lesssim\varepsilon
. Finally, the invertibility of
{\rm Id}+\Psi
follows by the Neumann series argument in Lemma
3.9
-
Item

(iii)
(clearly by taking
\varepsilon\nu^{-1}\ll 1
small enough). The proof of the Proposition is then concluded.
∎

Appendix A
Rank-
1
update of a closed operator

In this section we prove Lemma
2.4
. We first observe that, if
\langle g,\mathcal{A}^{-1}f\rangle_{H}+1=0
, then the vector
\mathcal{A}^{-1}f\in H
is such that

(A.1)

(\mathcal{A}+f\langle g,\cdot\rangle_{H})\mathcal{A}^{-1}f=f+\langle g,\mathcal{A}^{-1}f\rangle_{H}f=0\,,

and the operator
\mathcal{A}+f\langle g,\cdot\rangle_{H}
is not invertible. On the other hand, if
\langle g,\mathcal{A}^{-1}f\rangle_{H}+1\neq 0
, then the operator

(A.2)

\mathcal{A}^{-1}-\frac{\mathcal{A}^{-1}(f\langle g,\cdot\rangle_{H})\mathcal{A}^{-1}}{1+\langle g,\mathcal{A}^{-1}f\rangle_{H}}:H\to H

is well-defined and bounded. We have

(A.3a)

\displaystyle\big(\mathcal{A}+f\langle g,\cdot\rangle_{H}\big)\Big(\mathcal{A}^{-1}-\frac{\mathcal{A}^{-1}(f\langle g,\cdot\rangle_{H})\mathcal{A}^{-1}}{1+\langle g,\mathcal{A}^{-1}f\rangle_{H}}\Big)

(A.3b)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\mathrm{Id}}-\frac{(f\langle g,\cdot\rangle_{H})\mathcal{A}^{-1}}{1+\langle g,\mathcal{A}^{-1}f\rangle_{H}}+(f\langle g,\cdot\rangle_{H})\mathcal{A}^{-1}-\frac{(f\langle g,\cdot\rangle_{H})\mathcal{A}^{-1}(f\langle g,\cdot\rangle_{H})\mathcal{A}^{-1}}{1+\langle g,\mathcal{A}^{-1}f\rangle_{H}}\,,

and

(A.3c)

\displaystyle\Big(\mathcal{A}^{-1}-\frac{\mathcal{A}^{-1}(f\langle g,\cdot\rangle_{H})\mathcal{A}^{-1}}{1+\langle g,\mathcal{A}^{-1}f\rangle_{H}}\Big)\big(\mathcal{A}+f\langle g,\cdot\rangle_{H}\big)

(A.3d)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\mathrm{Id}}+\mathcal{A}^{-1}(f\langle g,\cdot\rangle_{H})-\frac{\mathcal{A}^{-1}(f\langle g,\cdot\rangle_{H})}{1+\langle g,\mathcal{A}^{-1}f\rangle_{H}}-\frac{\mathcal{A}^{-1}(f\langle g,\cdot\rangle_{H})\mathcal{A}^{-1}(f\langle g,\cdot\rangle_{H})}{1+\langle g,\mathcal{A}^{-1}f\rangle_{H}}\,.

We observe that, by rearranging parentheses,

(A.4a)

(f\langle g,\cdot\rangle_{H})\mathcal{A}^{-1}(f\langle g,\cdot\rangle_{H})\mathcal{A}^{-1}=f(\langle g,\cdot\rangle_{H}\mathcal{A}^{-1}f)\langle g,\cdot\rangle_{H}\mathcal{A}^{-1}=(\langle g,\mathcal{A}^{-1}f\rangle_{H})f\langle g,\cdot\rangle_{H}\mathcal{A}^{-1}\,,

and

(A.4b)

\mathcal{A}^{-1}(f\langle g,\cdot\rangle_{H})\mathcal{A}^{-1}(f\langle g,\cdot\rangle_{H})=\mathcal{A}^{-1}f(\langle g,\cdot\rangle_{H}\mathcal{A}^{-1}f)\langle g,\cdot\rangle_{H}=(\langle g,\mathcal{A}^{-1}f\rangle_{H})\mathcal{A}^{-1}f\langle g,\cdot\rangle_{H}\,.

By applying
Eqs.

A.4a
and
A.4b
in
Eqs.

A.3a
and
A.3c
respectively, we obtain

(A.5a)

\displaystyle\big(\mathcal{A}+f\langle g,\cdot\rangle_{H}\big)\Big(\mathcal{A}^{-1}-\frac{\mathcal{A}^{-1}(f\langle g,\cdot\rangle_{H})\mathcal{A}^{-1}}{1+\langle g,\mathcal{A}^{-1}f\rangle_{H}}\Big)

(A.5b)

\displaystyle={\mathrm{Id}}-(1+\langle g,\mathcal{A}^{-1}f\rangle_{H})\frac{(f\langle g,\cdot\rangle_{H})\mathcal{A}^{-1}}{1+\langle g,\mathcal{A}^{-1}f\rangle_{H}}+(f\langle g,\cdot\rangle_{H})\mathcal{A}^{-1}={\mathrm{Id}}\,,

and

(A.5c)

\displaystyle\Big(\mathcal{A}^{-1}-\frac{\mathcal{A}^{-1}(f\langle g,\cdot\rangle_{H})\mathcal{A}^{-1}}{1+\langle g,\mathcal{A}^{-1}f\rangle_{H}}\Big)\big(\mathcal{A}+f\langle g,\cdot\rangle_{H}\big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\mathrm{Id}}+\mathcal{A}^{-1}(f\langle g,\cdot\rangle_{H})-(1+\langle g,\mathcal{A}^{-1}f\rangle_{H})\frac{\mathcal{A}^{-1}(f\langle g,\cdot\rangle_{H})}{1+\langle g,\mathcal{A}^{-1}f\rangle_{H}}={\mathrm{Id}}\,.
