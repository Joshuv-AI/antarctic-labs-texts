Molecular detection of Coxiella burnetii in the blood of febrile patients referred to laboratories and hospitals in Khorramabad, Iran from 2015 to 2016
Authors: Farnaz Ferdosi, Amin Jaydari, Nemat Shams, Peyman Khademi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundQ fever is a zoonotic infectious disease. Objectives: The objective of this study was to detect the presence of C. burnetii in blood samples of feverish individuals (Contact with livestock; sheep, goats, and cattle, Consumption of unpasteurized dairy products) collected from hospitals at Khorramabad, Iran.Material and methodsA total number of 199 blood samples were collected from patients with fever from August 2015 to November 2016. Nested-PCR, using specific primers targeting the Com1 and icd genes, was employed to diagnose C. burnetii.ResultsOut of 199 blood samples, 16 samples were positive with Com1 gene (8 %; 95 % CI: 5.5–17.4) primers and 13 samples were positive with icd gene (6.5 % 95 % CI: 3.9 %–10.9 %) primers (table 2 is necessary). No significant correlation was observed between the variables and the positive result of PCR (p < 0.05) according to the study conducted using SPSS software.ConclusionsQ fever is present in patients from Khorramabad according to this study. Therefore, it should be considered as one of the possible causes of fever and non-specific symptoms in patients. It should be taken into consideration by health policy makers.

Introduction

Q fever was initially identified by Drake at the Queensland Area Microbiology and Pathology Laboratory in Brisbane, Australia in 1935 as an unknown infectious disease presenting with symptoms similar to those of influenza [1]. The causative agent was initially named Rickettsia burnetii, however, was later renamed Coxiella [2]. Additionally, in 1940 a pseudo-flu infection called Balkan grippe was reported in German and American soldiers during the Second World War [3,4]. In the context of human infection the term ‘Q fever’ is used to describe both disease and infection, whereas, coxillosis is employed to refer to the same phenomenon in animals [5].

The relationship between Q fever and the consumption of milk and its unpasteurized derivatives remains unconfirmed (EFSA, 2010) and requires further investigation [6]. The most common route of transmission of this pathogen to humans is the inhalation of aerosols from the placenta, amniotic fluid, faeces and urine as well as milk that is contaminated with the bacterium C. burnetii. However, the consumption of unpasteurized milk and dairy products should also be considered as an alternative means of infection, given the lack of conclusive evidence on the effects of pasteurization on the pathogen [6].

While there have only been a few studies to date that have examined the profiles of consumers of milk and dairy products and the associated risks of infection, these studies have provided useful insights. In Brazil, studies have indicated that milk and unstandardized dairy product consumption is relatively high in certain states. For example, in the states of Minas Gerais and Alagoas, 18.49 % of the population consume milk and unstandardized dairy products [7,8] while in the state of Paraná, the figure is 17.6 % [9].

The primary route of human infection with the virus is through inhalation of minute droplets along with other potential pathways including the digestive tract, bite or accidental cases within the laboratory. However, the significance of milk contamination of dairy cows is a topic that is open to debate. It is considered to be one of the primary routes of disease transmission to humans via the digestive system [10]. Due to the biological hazards, isolation and propagation of C. burnetii requires biosafety level 3 [11]. Culturing and isolating the bacterium requires special technical expertise. Inoculation of embryonic eggs, cell culture, laboratory animals and the use of axenic culture environments are other methods used to isolate and propagate C. burnetii [12,13].

PCR is employed for the molecular identification of C. burnetii. PCR represents a rapid, specific and highly sensitive diagnostic method in comparison with other laboratory techniques [14,15]. The higher sensitivity of PCR for the purpose of detecting and quantifying low concentrations of bacterial DNA has significantly enhanced both diagnostic and research approaches [16]. MLVA and MST are the most common methods used to separate genotypes [17].

PCR can be employed for the diagnosis of C. burnetii in a variety of biological specimens including fetal membranes, fetal fluids, genital swabs or samples of aborted embryos (liver, lung, or milk content) [18]. C. burnetii can be detected via real-time PCR in a number of different biological samples including whole blood, serum, milk, urine, anus and throat swabs [19,20].

The PCR method targets IS1111 sequences, which are repetitive transposition-like elements in C. burnetii [21]. These sequences are highly sensitive and specific for genomic detection of this bacterium. However, it should be noted that the IS1111 gene cannot be used to determine the quantity of C. burnetii for genomic detection due to the misidentification of some pseudo-Coxiella species [22].

Additionally, the quantification of genomic DNA of C. burnetii can be facilitated through the utilization of other single-copy genes such as icd and com1. A pair of different primers can be used to target genes such as superoxide dismutase (SODB), isocitrate dehydrogenase (icd), com1, macrophage infection-enhancing protein (CBMIP), heat shock proteins including HtpA and HtpB and some plasmid-dependent genes such as QpH1, QpRS, QpDV, and QpDG. These primers can be used to detect C. burnetii [23].

In the study by Pustahija et al. (2024), data on confirmed human Q fever cases were obtained from Bosnia and Herzegovina, Croatia, Greece, Montenegro, North Macedonia and Serbia. Overall, 2714 Q fever cases were registered during the 20-year period [24]. Between 2007 and 2010, a major outbreak of Q fever occurred in the Netherlands. Serological screening studies estimate that over 40,000 humans were infected, with major impact on physical and psychological health [25].

Given that Q fever is considered an occupational disease and the significance of the disease in human transmission as well as the lack of information about Q fever in the human population of the province of Lorestan in the city of Khorramabad, this study aimed to examine the prevalence of the C. burnetii genomic based on specific initiators of the icd and Com1 genes with the Nested-PCR method in blood samples of feverish individuals (with a history of contact with livestock, dairy consumption) collected from hospitals in Khorramabad.

Material and Methods

Study area

The province of Lorestan exhibits a diverse climatic pattern. Climate studies indicated that Khorramabad experiences a mild winter and a warm summer. The city of Khorramabad is situated at the midpoint between the two climatic zones of cool and Tropical, which can be considered the tempering point for the air of the city of Khorramabad with tolerable levels of both cold and heat. The prevailing winds, the city's compactness and its location at the foot of mountains have collectively shaped the city's microclimate (Fig. 1).Fig. 1Sampling location map.Fig. 1

Sampling location map.

Samples tested

The sampling phase was conducted between the beginning of 2015 and the beginning of 2016. A total number of 199 complete blood samples were obtained from febrile or symptomatic patients with a history of contact with animals or consumption of dairy products. Visiting laboratories and hospitals in Khorramabad collected these samples. The blood samples were collected in tubes containing anticoagulants EDTA (tube CBC). The samples were transported to the Faculty Research Laboratory in a timely manner in robust containers and in the vicinity of ice and maintained at a temperature of minus 20C° until the DNA extraction test was conducted. The risk factors examined in this study included gender, season, consumption of unpasteurized milk, place of residence (urban or rural), and history of contact with livestock.

Collecting patient data

At the time of sampling of government centers in person and with the consent of patients, a history was taken according to the questionnaire prepared. Patient information was collected from private centers in collaboration with the laboratory.

DNA extraction

The extraction was conducted in accordance with the instructions provided by the manufacturer of the KIT (Gene All cell SV mini 250p, South Korea). Initially, 20 μl of the K-proteinase enzyme were transferred to the 1.5 ML microtube, followed by the addition of 200 μl of the sample. Subsequently, all subsequent steps were carried out in accordance with the protocol outlined in the manufacturer's instructions.

PCR and Nested-PCR tests

Nested-PCR was employed in order to ascertain the presence of the C. burnetii genome in the samples under investigation. The first-stage PCR employed a total volume of 25 μl, comprising 12.5 μl of master mix (Amplicon, Denmark), 1 μl of each primer (forward-reverse), 5 μl of suspected sample DNA, and 5.5 μl of distilled water. The second-stage primers (nested PCR) of the icd gene were designed with AmplifX (Version 2.1.1) software. Furthermore, it was employed in place of distilled DNA in the negative control sample. A positive control sample from (Nine Mile strain) was utilized. The polymerase chain reaction (PCR) products for each stage were subjected to electrophoresis on a 1.5 % agarose gel, which had been pre-stained with safe stain (Labnet, ENDURO™, USA). The resulting gels were then imaged using an intelligent imaging system (Syngene Bio-Imaging, UK).

Data analysis

The obtained data were statistically analyzed by Chi-square test using SPSS software Ver. 22 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL). The P value < 0.05 was considered significant.

Results by gender

Out of the 199 blood samples, 117 (58.8 %) were from females, 10 were positive (5 %), 107 were negative (53.8 %) and 82 (41.2 %) were from males, 6 were positive (3 %) and 76 were negative (38.2 %). Descriptively, the percentage of contamination in women was higher than that in men. According to statistical calculations, there was no significant correlation between the results obtained and gender (p = 0.753. (Table 2).

Results are based on patients place of residence

Out of 199 samples, 169 were from patients living in rural areas of which 14 (7 %) were positive and 155 (77/9 %) were negative. Out of the 30 samples from urban residents, 2 (1 %) were positive and 28 (14.1 %) were negative. Descriptively, the rate of contamination was higher among rural residents than among urban residents. However, according to statistical calculations, there was no significant relationship between the test results and the location of the people (p = 0.764) (Table 2).

Results based on contact with livestock or dairy consumption

Out of the 199 samples, 166 were from patients with a history of contact with livestock, of female 13 (6.5 %) were identified as positive and 153 (76.9 %) were identified as negative. Out of the 33 unprecedented patients who consumed unpasteurized dairy products, 3 were identified as positive (1.5 %) and 30 were identified as negative (16.6 %). Descriptively, the percentage of contaminated clients was higher among those with a history of having come into contact with livestock than among those who consumed unpasteurized milk. However, there was no statistically significant correlation between the test results and the number of clients with a history of contact with livestock (p = 0.808) (Table 2).

Results based on history of aborting livestock

Out of the 199 samples, 49 were from patients with a history of livestock abortion, of which 3 (1.5 %) were identified as positive and 46 (23.1 %) were negative. 150 were from patients without a history of livestock abortion, of which 13 (6.5 %) were positive and 137 (68.8 %) were negative. There was no statistically significant correlation between the results of the test and the patients with a history of miscarriage in livestock (p = 0.570). (Table 2).

Logistic regression findings

Season of sampling, sex, place of residence, history of contact with livestock and history of abortion in livestock were not significantly correlated with other variables.

Sanger sequencing

Four C. burnetii PCR products (two tick sample pools and two blood samples) that tested positive for the amplified Com1 and icd gene fragments (438 bp and 366 bp) were sent to SinaClon Company in Tehran, Iran for sequencing. The nucleotide sequences were compared against GenBank (National Center for Biotechnology Information, Rockville Pike, Bethesda, USA) using the advanced BLAST similarity search tool. Additionally, the sequences were compared to identical sequences of C. burnetii isolates from GenBank. Clustal W was used to align and compare the nucleotide sequences with other sequences from GenBank, and the phylogenetic tree was constructed using the Maximum Likelihood method in MEGA software version 11.

Discussion

Although the disease is primarily occupational, affecting individuals who handle animals or their products, its pathogen can persist in the environment and spread through the air, allowing infection of the broader population. It is now considered a significant emerging public health threat [30]. Identifying exposure and risk factors enables public health agencies to assess risks, allocate appropriate control resources, and define research priorities for effective prevention [31].

Because of the importance of the bacterium Coxiella burnetii, rapid and precise diagnosis is essential. Although several diagnostic techniques are available, they remain limited by cost, specialized laboratory requirements, and lengthy processing time. Molecular techniques offer greater accuracy and speed, making them highly reliable diagnostic tools. Therefore, national adoption of molecular methods is recommended for reliable Q-fever detection [32,33]. The study examined risk factors such as gender, season, unpasteurized milk consumption, residence (urban or rural), and livestock contact.

Limited data exist on acute Q-fever in Iran. Furthermore, Coxiella burnetii had not been molecularly confirmed in clinical samples from Iran. In the Khalili et al. study, which included 92 cases of suspected acute undifferentiated febrile illnesses (AUFI), seven were found to be infected with C. burnetii infection, indicating a prevalence of 7.36 %. This constitutes the first molecular evidence of C. burnetii in Iran [34].

A 2013 study conducted in Tabriz identified 116 livestock-exposed individuals showing fever symptoms. Phase II IgG antibodies to C. burnetii in these cases were detected by ELISA [35]. Positive sera occurred in 3.62 % of unpasteurized-milk consumers, 2.12 % of pregnant women (abortion-related), and 7.4 % of tick-bitten individuals. The study confirmed acute Q fever cases in northwestern Iran, consistent with reports from other Iranian regions [34].

The first chronic Q-fever case in Iran involved a 72-year-old woman diagnosed with serologically confirmed endocarditis. She responded to doxycycline and hydroxychloroquine therapy [35].

However, delayed diagnosis in Iran resulted from limited clinical recognition and inadequate laboratory infrastructure. In particular, endocarditis cases likely reflect low physician and institutional awareness [36,37]. For the detection of Coxiella burnetii DNA in clinical specimens, PCR should ideally be performed within the first 14 days of febrile illness before the onset of seroconversion. In this early phase, PCR serves as a highly valuable diagnostic tool, offering a significant advantage over immunofluorescence assay (IFA), which remains the gold standard for serological diagnosis of both acute and chronic Q fever. When only a single acute-phase sample is available, PCR-based detection of bacterial DNA in blood is more appropriate than IFA-based antibody detection, particularly if the febrile illness has lasted ≤14 days. Thus, PCR is the method of choice for diagnosing Q fever during the initial two weeks of symptom onset, whereas IFA is preferred for serological confirmation using paired serum samples collected at an interval of three to four weeks [38].

A 2005 study by Bashiri et al. in western Mazandaran province, analyzing 120 human blood samples via PCR, reported no positive detections [39]. The discrepancy from current findings may stem from variations in PCR target genes, DNA extraction procedures, or sampling methods. Among the 120 samples, none of the participants had a history of fever or clinical symptoms.

In 2016, Khalili et al. in Kerman detected the IS1111 gene of Coxiella burnetii in seven patients (36.7 %) of 93 febrile patients using nested PCR. All positive cases resided in rural areas, including a pregnant housewife, a young soldier, and five farmers. All patients had consumed unpasteurized dairy products [34].

Bae et al. (2019) in South Korea evaluated the diagnostic performance of PCR for detecting Q fever infections in humans. Among 35 suspected acute cases, 16 (46 %) were PCR-confirmed, while 19 (54 %) had other febrile illnesses. The PCR assay showed 81 % sensitivity for Q-fever diagnosis [32].

Moreover, Huang et al. (2021) in Lishu, China, reported a patient with tick exposure, recurrent fever, pneumonia, and liver injury initially diagnosed as Q-fever negative. Additionally, the early diagnosis relied on imaging and culture results showing no microbial growth. Nevertheless, subsequent Next Generation Sequencing identified C. burnetii in whole blood, confirming infection [40].

Furthermore, a case of endocarditis and osteomyelitis with sternal infection, confirmed by PCR, was reported by Moradi et al. (2023) in Iran [41]. Kidder et al. (2023) in the United States identified a C. burnetii-related hip infection in a livestock-exposed patient, confirmed by PCR [42].

Yang et al. (2023) later described a spinal infection caused by C. burnetii in China, diagnosed through PCR and metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS) [43]. Despite regional climatic variations and sample-size differences, similar PCR-based findings likely reflect comparable populations and airborne transmission of C. burnetii. The studied communities exhibited non-specific and acute symptoms, and in most positive cases reported livestock contact. These results highlight Q-fever prevalence among humans, particularly those with livestock exposure or unpasteurized dairy consumption.

Studies across the Middle East have reported high Q-fever prevalence in both human and animal populations. Similarly, Iranian investigations indicated elevated seroprevalence among humans and livestock [44,45]. Zheng et al. (1998) evaluated nested PCR for Q-fever diagnosis. The Com1 gene fragment was amplified in 135 of 155 IF-positive sera (87 %) and 11 of 100 IF-negative sera (11 %) [46]. In an earlier 1988 study, Zheng et al. used nested PCR to detect C. burnetii plasmids directly from human serum, confirming its diagnostic value [26].

Kato et al. (1998) in Japan applied the nested PCR method to 52 blood samples from untreated patients with chronic, non-specific symptoms, detecting C. burnetii in 33 % of cases. Among the positive patients, 94 % reported close contact with animals [47]. In 2005, Marmion et al. examined the persistence of C. burnetii after primary acute Q Fever by assessing phase I/II antibodies, PCR detection in bone marrow and peripheral blood mononuclear cells, and attempting culture isolation. Antibody and PCR positivity rates were 88 % and 65 %, respectively, but culture isolation was unsuccessful [48].

Turra et al. (2006) investigated acute Q fever diagnostics in southern Australia, testing serum samples by serology and PCR targeting Com1 and IS1111 genes. PCR detected infection within four days, compared with 17 days for serological tests, confirming PCR as the faster diagnostic approach [49]. Q fever has been recognized as an emerging zoonosis in countries including Spain, France, the United Kingdom, Ghana, and Mali. It has caused severe acute febrile illnesses and overt pneumonia or hepatitis cases [50,51]. Variations in climate, sampling period, methodology, and sample size likely account for differing prevalence rates between Iran and other regions.

Studies in the Iranian provinces confirmed the presence of C. burnetii in livestock [36,[52], [53], [54]]. Suggesting possible human infection due to close animal contact. On the other hand, limited awareness among livestock farmers about zoonoses, including Q fever, and insufficient preventive action by health authorities, increases the risk of chronic infection, which may lead to endocarditis, chronic fatigue syndrome, or recurrent miscarriages [55]. It is therefore necessary to diagnose and treat Q fever early. High-risk groups, including pregnant women, patients with cardiac valve defects, or immunocompromised individuals, require early diagnosis and monitoring. In response, Dutch authorities have implemented diagnostic and follow-up protocols for both acute and chronic Q fever, ensuring blood transfusion and transplant safety. Because C. burnetii may theoretically be transmitted via blood, the European Center for Disease Prevention and Control recommends active blood screening and tissue testing, although only a few transfusion-related cases have been reported [56].

This study identified Q fever among febrile patients in Khorramabad, with a 2 % prevalence in individuals with prior animal contact and 2 % among those consuming unpasteurized dairy but had no such exposure. Given the extensive industrial and traditional livestock farming in Loerestan province, Q fever should be prioritized by health authorities for its public health and economic implications.

Statistical analysis revealed no significant correlation between study variables and test outcomes. This may reflect a limited sample size, which could not be expanded due to time and testing costs, although it remained larger than in comparable national studies. Descriptively, the disease was more prevalent among rural residents, likely due to frequent livestock contact. Khorramabad, located in central Lorestan province, has a temperate climate within a mountainous region spanning the north to south part of the province. Rainfall is heaviest in winter and spring, while frost is rare. Seasonal shifts occur early, with spring starting in September and summer by mid-April [52,53]. The concentration of positive cases in late winter and spring likely reflects these climatic patterns.

Evidence suggests that Q fever is endemic in Iran, though its public and veterinary impact remains underrecognized due to subclinical infections and misdiagnosis as Maltese fever or influenza. Meanwhile, hard ticks serve as reservoirs of C. burnetii, maintaining the microorganism's long-term presence in developmental stages and transovarial transmission [57]. Ticks thrive in the humid and variable climate of Iran. Serological studies on Q fever have been conducted in small ruminants, large ruminants, rodents, and humans in Iran. The distribution of ticks, tick-borne diseases (TBD) and potential joint (human and livestock-related) risks to human and animal health are mainly influenced by environmental factors such as humidity and temperature. In addition, some studies have shown that ticks are more prevalent in the warm months of the year (June and August), as these months are more favorable for tick activity. Therefore, the possibility of Q fever outbreaks during warm seasons may be high in some areas [57].

This study confirmed the presence of Q fever among patients from Khorramabad. Accordingly, Q fever should be recognized as a potential cause of fever and other non-specific clinical symptoms. Health authorities should incorporate this consideration into diagnostic and surveillance policies. Livestock vaccination remains a key strategy for the prevention and control of Q fever. Furthermore, previous studies have shown that Q fever vaccination has reduced abortions and limited bacterial shedding in livestock populations [58].

Conclusion

The study demonstrated that C. burnetii contributes to febrile illness among residents of Khorramabad, but further research is needed due to the limited sample size and study duration. Future investigations should include larger sample sets collected year-round and across all regions of Lorestan province.
