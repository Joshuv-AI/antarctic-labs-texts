An omentum protrusion: a case of traumatic abdominal wall hernia in a child
Authors: Saida Hidouri, Sabrine Ben Ammar, Arije Zouaoui, Sinda Houidi, Mohamed Ali Chaouch, Riadh Jouini
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTraumatic abdominal wall hernia (TAWH) is a rare consequence of blunt abdominal trauma, especially in the pediatric population. It involves herniation through a disrupted abdominal wall without skin penetration. Its incidence is approximately 0.1%, and diagnosis may be delayed due to subtle clinical signs.Case presentationA 4-year-old boy presented with a minor superficial wound after falling onto a vertical iron bar. Initial physical and ultrasound evaluations were unremarkable. One week later, he developed a reducible right lower quadrant abdominal bulge, later confirmed via ultrasound as an omental herniation through a 20 × 10 mm fascial defect. Elective laparotomy revealed omentum prolapse with adhesions, which was repaired with primary suturing. Postoperative recovery was uneventful, and the patient experienced full resolution.DiscussionTAWHs are frequently caused by focused blunt trauma, such as bicycle handlebars, and often accompany intra-abdominal injuries. Diagnosis relies on clinical suspicion, imaging (ultrasound or CT), and exclusion of pre-existing hernias. Early surgical repair is recommended to prevent complications like strangulation or incarceration. The case highlights the importance of monitoring and re-evaluation even after initially normal assessments.ConclusionTAWH should be suspected in children presenting with abdominal pain after blunt trauma, even when initial findings are minimal. Timely diagnosis and appropriate surgical intervention can prevent morbidity. Due to its rarity, management should be individualized based on patient stability, injury severity, and associated intra-abdominal involvement.

Introduction

Trauma is one of the main causes of mortality in the pediatric population [1]. Blunt impact is responsible for 80% to 90% of abdominal injuries [2]. Traumatic abdominal wall hernia (TAWH) is defined as the herniation of peritoneal organs from a disrupted fascia and musculature, which did not exist before an abdominal trauma [3], [4]. Firstly described by Selby in 1906 [5], TAWH is a rare condition, mostly reported in individual case descriptions. Incidence of this unusual occurrence, as reported, is 0.1% [6]. Diagnosis is not always evident, but early recognition and treatment reduce the morbidity associated with the risk of strangulation. This condition is caused by a trauma that has the force to disrupt the underlying fascia and muscle without penetrating the skin [3]; it can result from either high-energy trauma, such as a motor vehicle accident, or low-energy trauma, such as a fall on a bicycle handlebar. Directly focused energy and the sudden increase of intra-abdominal pressure may lead to this interruption. The most common site is the lower abdomen, particularly the inguinal area [3]. Through our case, we describe an unusual site of traumatic abdominal wall hernia caused by a handlebar-like object in a child and discuss the presentation and the management.

Case presentation

A four-year-old previously healthy boy presented to our emergency department after falling onto an iron vertical bar while playing, causing a blunt abdominal trauma. At presentation, his vital signs were normal, and he was completely cognizant. He just reported having a dull pelvic pain. The only remarkable physical finding was a small open wound of 1 cm at the base of the penile body. There was no rebound or guarding. The bowel sounds were normal, and no bulging could be observed. There was no macroscopic or microscopic hematuria. The ultrasound imaging performed was normal, with no intra-abdominal post-traumatic injuries. The wound was sutured under general anesthesia. Peroperatively, the wound was superficial and did not communicate with the pelvic or the abdominal cavity. The patient was discharged after 24 h with careful observation at his home. One week later, the patient was presented in the emergency room for abdominal pain with a fluctuating bulge reported by his grandmother. On examination, there was a slightly tender hernia in the lower right quadrant with a positive cough impulse with no contusion of the overlying skin (Fig. 1). The hernia was reducible, and there were no clinical signs or features to suggest obstruction. The abdominal defect was palpable and measured approximately 30 mm. The patient was admitted for observation. One day after admission, the hernia was no longer reducible and was tender. Hematological and biochemical investigations were normal. He had an ultrasound examination showing herniation of the omentum in a defect of 20x10mm in the lower para-umbilical region in the middle of the rectus abdominal muscle. Elective laparotomy on the site of the hernia was performed. The incision was made with extreme care to avoid accidental enterotomy. At operation, a 20x10mm defect in the abdominal muscles and peritoneum was found immediately after dividing the skin (Fig. 2). The omentum prolapsed through this defect and had multiple adhesions, the aponeurosis, and the subcutaneous tissues (Fig. 3). No small bowel injury was noted. Next, the omentum was freed of the adhesions, and the traumatic hernia defect was closed using separate sutures of 2-0 Vicryl. Discussion

Blunt abdominal trauma is common and can result in abdominal wall or visceral injury. Both high and low energy trauma can give rise to a hernia by a discrete point of energy transfer such as a seatbelt, a handlebar or any other narrow unyielding object, blunt enough not to penetrate the skin, but small enough for the force to be localized and allow for transmission of a localized force to the underlying muscle–fascia layer [7].

According to Gauchi, the pathophysiology entails applying a blunt force to the abdomen over a surface area large enough to prevent the skin from penetration. The tangential forces causing the muscles and fascia of the abdominal wall to be disrupted by pressure, which permits the abdominal viscera to herniate subcutaneously through the defect [8].

Traumatic abdominal wall hernia was classified by Wood et al. into three types depending on the size of the defect and the cause of injury: Type I injuries involve a minor defect caused by blunt trauma such as from a handlebar, large defects that develop during high-energy events, such as motor vehicle crashes or falls from heights, are known as type II injuries. Type III defects are defined by the herniation of bowel through an abdominal wall defect, which is most often caused by injury during deceleration [4]. Type I injuries are most common in children and most often occur as a result of bicycle injuries, leading them to be commonly referred to as “handlebar hernias” [9], [10]. Traumatic abdominal wall hernias (TAWH) are often associated with severe intraabdominal injuries. Over 50% of patients present associated injuries, with some reports reaching 90% [11], [12]. Bowel injury is the most frequent (25%), while splenic and liver injuries are less common (3.8% and 1.9%). The incidence varies by TAWH mechanism and classification [7], [12].

Diagnosis of TAWH requires a high index of suspicion based on history and physical clues. The typical history is of a fall upon a bicycle handlebar with the immediate appearance of a tender bruised lump in the abdominal wall. For a physician to properly identify a TAWH, there must be no prior hernia in the same area, an abdominal wall injury upon presentation, as well as the appearance of a hernia either immediately or later [13].

Clinical features include an overlying skin with a tender subcutaneous swelling with or without bruising of the abdominal wall [3]. The swelling may increase or decrease in size depending on the position of the patient, Valsalva maneuver, or cough impulse [14]. The Cough impulse was noted in our case, but not constant. Additionally, in some cases, the “handlebar sign” may be present, which is represented by a ring-shaped ecchymotic area noted on the abdominal wall. Usually located at anatomically weak points in the lower abdomen, the defect's location is not always well associated with the site of impact. Most defects are found lateral to the rectus sheath, through the oblique and transverse abdominal muscles in the iliac fossa.

Only abdominal computed tomography (CT) or ultrasonography (US) can reveal occult hernias, and physical examinations might be misleadingly comforting [7]. The dynamic nature of the US makes it particularly helpful; it was enough in our case to give a preoperative description. Radiological investigations help describe the disrupted abdominal wall layers, the size, and the content of the hernia; they are also used to eliminate hematoma, which is a common differential diagnosis. In some patients, because of pain and muscle spasm, the abdominal wall defect may be masked on radiographic investigation. In such cases, delayed detection may result from muscle relaxation and increasing intra-abdominal pressure.

Once the diagnosis of TAWH is made, a therapeutic decision should be made. Management recommendation by most reports in the literature is early or delayed surgical repair to prevent complications such as incarceration or strangulation and subsequent morbidity and even mortality [[15], [16]]. In terms of surgical approach, both laparoscopic and open surgery have been reported to be appropriate. Laparoscopy can typically help in the diagnosis of TAWH. According to the literature, the decision to perform an exploratory laparotomy or laparoscopy should be made on an individual basis depending on the mechanism of injury, physical examination details, laboratory values, and radiologic data [15]. Naturally, urgent exploratory laparotomy is required for unstable patients. The treatment involves repairing the defect after surgical exploration. To adequately control the herniated contents and the defect, the incision should be made immediately over the traumatic swelling. In our case, repair was possible by elective laparotomy. Prosthetic materials are frequently utilized to repair major defects. Most reports describe a primary closure of the hernia, although repair using a prosthetic mesh has also been described in the pediatric age group [10], [13], [16]. Before primary tension-free closure, debridement of nonviable tissue and evacuation of subcutaneous hematoma should be performed. In the presence of clinical or radiological signs of intra-abdominal injury, a midline laparotomy is necessary to manage associated injuries.

Conclusion

The diagnosis of TAWH requires a high level of suspicion. Clinicians should approach pediatric patients with abdominal pain after blunting abdominal trauma with a high degree of suspicion for focal blunt impact injury. Early recognition and treatment of TAWH lead to a significant reduction of morbidity or even mortality associated with intraabdominal complications. At present, there is no clear consensus on the best way to manage this condition. The number of cases reported in the literature are still not sufficient to establish treatment guidelines therefore therapeutic decisions should be tailored on a single case and optimal management of TAWH should be individualized based on time of presentation, injury severity score, patient's condition and hemodynamic stability, associated abdominal visceral injuries, size of the abdominal wall defect and the risk of incarceration, and presence of abdominal contamination and concurrent hollow viscus injuries.
