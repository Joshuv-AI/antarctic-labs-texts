MISP promotes the progression of lung adenocarcinoma through Inhibition of the Hippo signaling pathway
Authors: Yuanjun Cheng, Yumei Shen, Wei Fang, Shanzhou Duan, Yifei Wang, Xiaoxiao Dai, Yongbing Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGlobally, lung cancer is a malignant tumor with the highest morbidity and mortality rate, of which lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) is the most common pathological type, accounting for about 40% of all cases. This study aims to investigate the impact and molecular mechanisms of mitotic spindle positioning (MISP) on the occurrence and progression of LUAD.MethodsThe expression level of MISP were evaluated by qPCR and Western blotting. Hippo signaling-related protein levels were measured by Western blotting. Co-immunoprecipitation assay was applied to assess the interaction between MISP and MST1. The roles of MISP in LUAD cells were determined through CCK-8 assay, transwell migration/invasion assay, and flow cytometric analysis. A xenograft tumor model (n = 6) was established to calculate the effect of MISP in vivo.ResultsMISP expression was significantly higher in LUAD tissues compared to normal tissues. MISP overexpression promoted LUAD cell proliferation and migration in in vitro models, as well as tumor growth and metastasis in LUAD animal models. Western blotting demonstrated that MISP overexpression increased the expression of YAP, TAZ and CYR61, and decreased the expression of MST1 and phosphorylated YAP, thereby blocking the Hippo signaling pathway. While MISP knockdown had the opposite effect. Mechanistic experiments revealed that MISP directly interacts with MST1, thereby inhibiting Hippo signaling.ConclusionsIn conclusion, our study reveals a novel discovery that MISP promotes the growth and invasion of LUAD cells by interacting with MST1 and suppressing the Hippo pathway.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s12672-025-03773-9.Keywords: Lung adenocarcinoma, MISP, Hippo pathway, MST1

Introduction

As a primary cause of global cancer-related death, lung cancer is classified into two subtypes, non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) and small cell lung cancer [1]. Lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) is the most prevalent type of NSCLC, and accounts for approximately 40% of NSCLC cases [2]. Patients with LUAD are often diagnosed at an advanced stage due to atypical early symptoms and inadequate screening methods [3]. Additionally, the propensity of LUAD to metastasize to the contralateral lung, lymph nodes, and distant organs further compounds the poor prognosis, even following treatment with current therapeutic protocols [4]. Thus, efforts to comprehensively elucidate the oncogenic molecular mechanisms underlying LUAD metastasis and identify potent therapeutic targets for metastatic LUAD are crucial and immediate priorities.

Mitotic Spindle Positioning (MISP), alternatively known as C19ORF21, is a crucial protein involved in regulating mitotic spindle positioning and centrosome clustering, as well as inducing stress fibers along with other thick actin filaments [5–7]. MISP is notably overexpressed in a range of cancers, such as pancreatic [8], colorectal [9], and stomach cancers [10]. More importantly, a recent study conducted by Zhang et al. reported that MSIP regulates ferroptosis in NSCLC through mediating YAP signaling [11]. However, the expression and function of MISP in human LUAD remains unclear.

The Hippo signaling pathway, an evolutionarily conserved cascade, plays a crucial role in preserving tissue homeostasis, controlling organ size, and inhibiting tumor growth [12]. The dysregulation of the Hippo pathway has been associated with multiple types of cancer, including lung cancer [13]. For example, in metastatic lung cancer, the suppression of cell migration and invasion was demonstrated by the knockdown of the mediators of the Hippo pathway (YAP1 and TAZ) [14]. The overexpression of YAP was shown to enhance the growth and metastasis of LUAD cells [15]. Furthermore, the dysregulation of the Hippo/YAP pathway has been linked to the emergence of drug resistance in lung cancer [16]. These studies offer compelling evidence for the importance of investigating the dysregulation of the Hippo signaling pathway in cancer biology. However, the relationship between MISP and the Hippo signaling pathway in LUAD is not clear.

Therefore, this study is to explore the role of MISP in LUAD progression and its association with the Hippo signaling pathway. Through in-depth research, our aim is to advance the comprehension of LUAD biology and identify potential treatment strategies to enhance patient outcomes.

Methods and materials

Patients and tissue specimens

Between June 2023 and January 2024, tumor samples of LUAD and the corresponding para-cancerous normal tissues were collected from People’s Hospital of Chizhou. The inclusion criteria of patients with LUAD were as follows: (1) The patients were diagnosed as LUAD by pathological examinations. (2) None of the patients had received treatments before the operation. (3) The clinical data of LUAD were complete. The samples were promptly frozen at − 80 °C for later utilization in experiments. The study received approval from the Research Ethics Committee at People’s Hospital of Chizhou (2022-SKY-01) and complied with the principles established in the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants involved in the study. Patients that cannot provide informed consent were excluded.

Cell lines

Bronchial cell line HBE and human LUAD cell lines A549, GLC-B2, H322 and PC9 (American Type Culture Collection, Manassas, VA, USA) were maintained in RPMI 1640 medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) containing 10% fetal bovine serum. They were cultured at 37 °C in a humidified 5% CO2 environment., and applied within forty passages. These cells tested negative for the presence of mycoplasma.

Cell transfection

A549 and PC9 cells at the logarithmic growth stage were harvested and diluted to a concentration of 2 × 106 cells/mL before being seeded in a 6-well plate. Transfection was carried out when cell confluency reached 50–60%. Small interfering RNA MISP (siRNA MISP) sequences (siRNA MISP-1, sense, 5’-GGCACGUGGUUCCCAUCAA-3’, antisense, 5’-UUGAUGGGAACCACGUGCC-3’; siRNA MISP-2, sense, 5’-ACAAGGAGAUGAAGACCUA-3’, antisense, 5’-UAGGUCUUCAUCUCCUUGU-3’; siRNA MISP-3, sense, 5’-GCAGAGAAAGAAGGAGCAA-3’, antisense, 5’-UUGCUCCUUCUUUCUCUGC-3’) and their respective negative controls (si NC, sense, 5’-UUCUCCGAACGUGUCACGUTT-3’; antisense, 5’-ACGUGACACGUUCGGAGAATT-3’) were designed, synthesized, and transfected into the cells using the Lipofectamine 2000 transfection kit from Invitrogen (Carlsbad, CA, USA). Following a 6-hour incubation, the original medium was replaced with fresh complete medium. Cells were then collected 48 h post-transfection.

For overexpression of MISP in vitro, lentivirus vectors harboring full-length MISP were designed, and an empty vector was employed as the negative control. The lentivirus vectors were obtained from GeneChem Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). The efficiency was confirmed by RT-qPCR and Western blots.

Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA extraction was conducted using Trizol Reagent (Yeasen Biotech Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China; Cat. 10606ES60). The concentration and quality of RNA samples were assessed using the Nanodrop™ 8000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Nanodrop™ 8000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was used to detect the concentration and quality of RNA samples. The quantitative PCR of cDNA was performed using Hieff® qPCR SYBR Green Master Mix (No Rox) (Yeasen Biotech Co., Ltd.; Cat. 11202ES08). Normalization to the internal control GAPDH was applied, and data analysis utilized the 2−ΔΔCt method. The primer sequences utilized are listed below:

MISP forward: CCCTGAGCACAAAGCAAGAG, MISP reverse: GCATCACCCGGAGGAGAAAT; GAPDH forward: GCCCATCGAGGAGTCAGAGT, GAPDH reverse: CATCTCTTCCGGGGTCTTGG.

Western blot analysis

Tissue and cellular protein samples were obtained using RIPA lysis buffer (Biosharp, Hefei, China; cat. BL504A) supplemented with PMSF (SEVEN Biotech, Beijing, China; cat. SW106-01). 30 µg protein sample separated by 10% sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis and transferred to polyvinylidene difluoride membranes for Western blotting. The membranes were then blocked with 5% skim milk and incubated with specific primary antibodies [anti-MISP (1:2000, 26338-1-AP; Proteintech, Manchester, UK), anti-MST1 (1:4000, 22245-1-AP; Proteintech), anti-YAP (1:5000, 13584-1-AP; Proteintech), anti-p-YAP (1:1000, 13008T; Cell Signaling Technology, Boston, MA, USA), anti-TAZ (1:5000, 23306-1-AP; Proteintech), anti-CYR61 (1:2000, 26689-1-AP; Proteintech), and anti-GAPDH polyclonal antibody (internal control, 1:50000, 60004-1-Ig, Proteintech)] at 4 °C overnight. Subsequently, membranes were incubated with secondary antibody (Abcam, Cambridge, UK) at 37 °C for 1 h, and visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) (BMU101-CN; Abbkine Scientific, Wuhan, China). The quantification of the visualized protein bands was performed utilizing ImageJ software.

CCK-8 assay

Transfected A549 cells (5 × 103 cells/well) and PC9 cells (5 × 103 cells/well) were seeded in 96-well plates. 100 ul 1640 containing 10 ul CCK-8 reagent (Abbkine Scientific, Wuhan, China) was added to each well at 4 different time points. After incubation at 37 °C for 1 h, absorption was detected at the 450 nm wavelength. Each group included three duplicate wells, and the experiments were repeated in triplicate.

Transwell migration and invasion assays

The migration and invasion assay were performed using chambers coated with BD matrigel matrix (BD Bioscience, Oxford, UK). Approximately 1 × 105-1 × 106 cells were seeded in the upper chambers with DMEM, and 600 ul DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS was added to the lower chambers. Plates were incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. We then used wet swabs to wipe the cells that did not invade or migrate through the pores. The remaining cells on the lower side of the filter were fixed using 4% paraformaldehyde, then stained with crystal violet, and finally counted to assess the invasive and migratory abilities. The experiments were repeated in triplicate.

Flow cytometric analysis

Cell cycle analysis was performed using Cell Cycle Staining Kit (Abbkine Scientific, cat. KTA2020). Briefly, 70% ice-cold ethanol was used to fix the cells. The ethanol was removed and the cells were suspended and washed using cold PBS. The cells were stained with 0.5 mL Staining Solution. A flow cytometry system (Beckman Coulter, Brea, CA, USA) was used to measure the cell-cycle distribution.

PC9 and A549 cell apoptosis rates were assessed using an apoptosis detection kit (KTA0002; Abbkine Scientific). After harvesting and washing with chilled PBS, the transfected cell suspension was incubated with 1×Annexin V, followed by staining with Annexin V and PI. Apoptosis was then analyzed within 1 h using a flow cytometry system (Beckman Coulter).

Co-immunoprecipitation assay

Cells were collected and prepared in Non-Denaturing Lysis Buffer containing protease inhibitor cocktail for 30 min at 4 °C. For co-immunoprecipitation, the cellular supernatants after centrifugation (12,000×g, 10 min, 4 °C) were collected. A small amount of protein lysate was treated with 2 µg anti-MISP (26338-1-AP, Proteintech) and anti-IgG (ab6715, Abcam) at room temperature, and then incubated overnight with Protein A/GAgarose in a shaker at 4 ◦C. The agarose beads containing immune complexes were rinsed thrice with lysis buffer for 3 times and the immunoprecipitated complexes were boiled with 1×SDS loading buffer at 100 °C for 5 min, subjecting to SDS-PAGE and immunoblotted with the specific antibodies.

Xenograft tumor model

A total of 24 female nude mice (4–6 weeks old; 22–24 g; EseBio, Shanghai, China) were assigned into 4 groups (n = 6) ad libitum. PC9 cells (5 × 106) transfected with sh-NC, sh-MISP, OE-NC or OE-MISP were subcutaneously injected into the right flank of mice. Tumor size was measured twice a week. Tumor volumes (V) were measured using a caliper every day based on the formula V = π/6×a2×b (b: length, a: width). After 8 weeks, the mice were sacrificed via an overdose of sodium pentobarbital (200 mg/kg), and tumors were imaged and weighed. Subsequently, one part of the tumors was fixed into 4% paraformaldehyde for HE staining, immunohistochemical staining, and TUNEL assay, while the remaining part was preserved at − 80 ℃ for molecular experiments. The experimental procedures were strictly in accordance with the Guidelines for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals Established by United States National Institutes of Health. All procedures in this study were approved by the ethical committee of People’s Hospital of Chizhou (2022-SKY-01). In animal experiments, the maximal tumor size permitted by the ethics committee is 1000 mm3. Throughout the entire research process, the tumor size of all mice did not exceed the maximum limits approved by the aforementioned ethics committee.

HE staining

The xenografts were fixed, deparaffinized and hydrated, followed by paraffin embedding. Afterwards, they were cut into 4-µm-thick. The sections were stained by HE for 3 min. After mounting with neutral gum, images were acquired under an optical microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan).

Immunohistochemical staining

The xenografts (4-µm-thick) were deparaffinized, hydrated, underwent antigen retrieval in 10 mM citrate buffer (pH 6.0) and then incubated with hydrogen peroxide (3%) for 10 min. After washing with PBS for three times, the samples were incubated with primary antibody MISP at 4 °C for overnight, followed by incubation with HRP-conjugated secondary antibody for 30 min at 37 °C. At last, it was reacted with DAB and then counterstained with hematoxylin for 1 min. Sample images were observed under an optical microscope (Olympus).

TUNEL assay

In accordance with the experimental operations in the manufacturer’s specifications of the Kit (Solarbio Science & Technology, Beijing, China), the apoptosis in xenografts was evaluated. The images were photographed using a fluorescence microscope (Olympus).

In vivo bioluminescence imaging

Tumor metastasis was monitored through in vivo bioluminescence imaging. Mice were treated with luciferin via intraperitoneal injection. Following a 15-minute incubation period, the animals were anesthetized with 5% isoflurane and transferred to the imaging chamber maintained at 2% isoflurane and 37 °C. Imaging commenced 15 min post-Luciferin injection, utilizing the XFOV-24 lens with an exposure time of 180 s, capturing images ventrally.

Statistical analysis

Three independent experiments were conducted for all data analyses, and the validity of the experimental data was confirmed using GraphPad Prism 9.0 software. Group comparisons were assessed using a T-test for two groups, and a one-way analysis of variance for multiple groups (followed by Tukey’s post hoc test). Results were reported as mean ± standard deviation, with statistical significance set at P < 0.05.

Results

The expression of MISP is increased in patients with LUAD

Firstly, we assessed MISP expression level in adjacent normal and cancerous tissues. The results of qRT-PCR and western blot demonstrated that the mRNA expression and protein level of MISP were upregulated in LUAD tissues compared with adjacent non-tumor tissues (Fig. 1A-C, P < 0.01).

The expression of MISP is increased in patients with LUAD. (A) The expression level of MISP was examined by qRT–PCR; (B-C) The expression level of MISP was examined by Western blot. **P < 0.01 and ***P < 0.001

MISP regulates the Hippo signaling pathway in LUAD cells

As illustrated in Fig. 2A, compared to HBE, relatively high levels of MISP were found in A549 and PC9 cells (P < 0.001). Therefore, these two cell lines were selected for subsequent experiments. Recent studies have demonstrated the crucial role of Hippo pathway in LUAD. To further explore the potential involvement of MISP in the Hippo pathway, MISP was silenced or overexpressed in LUAD cell lines (Fig. 2A, P < 0.001). Subsequently, we specifically investigated the expression levels of MST1, YAP, TAZ, and CYR61 (all the well-established Hippo target genes), under conditions involving MISP knockdown or overexpression. Our findings revealed that overexpression of MISP remarkably increased the expression of YAP, TAZ and CYR61, and decreased the expression of MST1 and p-YAP (Fig. 2B-H, P < 0.05). In contrast, knockdown of MISP significantly reversed the aforementioned changes (Fig. 2B-H, P < 0.001). Furthermore, we assessed the impact of MISP on MST1 expression in LUAD cells using co-immunoprecipitation experiment. The results showed that the anti-MISP antibody precipitated the MST1 protein, and conversely, the anti-MST1 antibody also precipitated the MISP protein, providing evidence of a direct interaction between MISP and MST1 proteins in LUAD cells (Fig. 2I). These findings indicate that MISP is a potentially positive regulator for Hippo signaling pathway in LUAD.

MISP regulates the Hippo signaling pathway in LUAD cells. (A) The expression level of MISP in LUAD cells was examined by qRT–PCR; (B) The transfection efficiency was confirmed by qRT-PCR; (C-I) The protein levels of MISP, MST1, YAP, p-YAP, TAZ and CYR61 in LUAD cells were measured by Western blot. (J) Co-immunoprecipitation shows the interaction between MISP and MST1. *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001

MISP promotes proliferation, migration and invasion ability, but inhibits apoptosis in LUAD cells

To study the role of MISP in LUAD cells, we firstly evaluated the effect of MISP on LUAD cell proliferation via CCK-8 assay. Our data revealed that overexpression of MISP promoted LUAD cell viability, when compared to the vector control (Fig. 3A-B). Flow cytometry analysis indicated that overexpression of MISP in LUAD cells contributed to increasing in G2 phase arrest and decreasing in G1 phase population (Fig. 3C-F). In contrast, transfection of siRNA MISP markedly inhibited LUAD cell proliferation compared to the siRNA NC (Fig. 3A-B). The downregulation of MISP increased G1 phase population and decreased the G2 phase population (Fig. 3C-F). We next evaluated the effect of MISP on the metastatic capacities of LUAD cells. We observed that the overexpression of MISP significantly promoted migration and invasion in PC9 and A549 (Fig. 4A-F, P < 0.001). On the contrary, MISP knockdown inhibited the migration and invasion of LUAD cells (Fig. 4A-F, P < 0.001). We further investigated the involvement of MISP in regulating apoptosis. As illustrated in Fig. 5A-C, overexpression of MISP significantly decreased the apoptosis rate of LUAD cells as compared with vector group (P < 0.001). While the downregulation of MISP obviously increased apoptosis rate of LUAD cells (P < 0.001). These data showed that MISP functioned as an oncogene by inducing cell proliferation, migration and invasion as well as inhibiting apoptosis in LUAD cells.

MISP promotes invasion and migration in LUAD cells. (A–C) Cell migration was evaluated by Transewell assay. (D–F) Cell invasion was evaluated by Transewell assay. Scale bar = 100 μm. ***P < 0.001

MISP inhibits apoptosis in LUAD cells. Cell apoptosis was evaluated by flow cytometry. ***P < 0.001

MISP regulates the Hippo signaling pathway in mice

To further assess the potential effects of MISP on the growth of LUAD, female node mice were subcutaneously injected with transfected cells on the right side to induce xenograft tumors. The transfection efficiency was demonstrated by immunohistochemical staining (Fig. 6A-B, P < 0.001). Western blot revealed that compared with the sh-NC group, the protein levels of MST1 and p-YAP were markedly increased in the sh-MISP group, while the levels of YAP, TAZ and CYR61 proteins were significantly decreased (Fig. 6C-I, P < 0.001). The opposite results were observed when OE-MISP was injected (Fig. 6C-I, P < 0.001).

MISP regulates the Hippo signaling pathway in mice. (A-B) The transfection efficiency of sh-MISP or OE-MISP in mice was confirmed by immunohistochemical staining; Scale bar = 100 μm; (C-I) The protein levels of MISP, MST1, YAP, p-YAP, TAZ and CYR61 in mice were measured by Western blot. *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001

MISP stimulates xenograft tumor growth and lung metastasis in mice

As illustrated in Fig. 7A-E, compared with the sh-NC group, MISP knockdown significantly inhibited the growth of tumors, while the upregulation of MISP promoted the growth of tumors. HE staining showed that compared to the sh-NC group, tumor cells in the sh-MISP group exhibited a remarkable reduction in size, displayed a patchy and loose arrangement, showed lighter staining, and demonstrated a significant amount of apoptosis (Fig. 8A). In contrast, when compared to the OE-NC group, tumor cells in the OE-MISP group were clustered together, arranged tightly, stained more intensely, and exhibited vigorous growth (Fig. 8A). Subsequently, TUNEL assays were performed to assess the effect of MISP on apoptosis in xenograft tumors. The results indicated that the percentage of TUNEL-positive cells were markedly increased following injection of sh-MISP, while were reduced when OE-MISP was injected (Fig. 8B-C, P < 0.001). Additionally, lung metastasis was examined by in vivo bioluminescence imaging within four weeks after injection. The results indicated that MISP knockdown significantly reduced both the number and size of lung metastatic lesions, while MISP overexpression markedly increased these parameters, as assessed by bioluminescence imaging (Fig. 9).

Discussion

Most patients with LUAD do not succumb to their primary cancer but rather from cancer metastasis [17]. However, the clinically relevant mechanisms remain inadequately elucidated. In this study, we demonstrated that MISP acts as an oncogene, capable of inducing cell proliferation, migration, and invasion while concurrently inhibiting apoptosis in LUAD. Moreover, our in vitro and in vivo results demonstrated a novel regulatory mechanism during LUAD progression. We found that MISP promotes the activation of Hippo signaling via targeting MST1. In detail, MISP can interact with MST1, regulating MST1 and other Hippo signaling proteins expression. Thus, MISP inhibits the Hippo pathway to enhance the progression and metastasis of LUAD.

MISP, a newly demonstrated protein, exhibits increased expression in various tumors and acts as an oncogene. In a bioinformatic literature, Huang et al. demonstrated that MISP expression is significantly upregulated in pancreatic cancer, which is associated with poor prognosis, immune infiltration, and response to immunotherapy [8]. During gastric cancer progression, MISP is found to be overexpressed. It is associated with an intestinal phenotype in gastric carcinogenesis and plays a role in cellular proliferation [18]. Similarly, MISP is upregulated in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) tissues and can influence immune escape in HCC cells by regulating the expression of PD-L1 [19]. These findings highlight the importance of MISP in tumor development. However, there is currently limited research on the expression and regulatory mechanisms of MISP in LUAD. In this study, a major finding was the increased expression of MISP in LUAD tissue when compared with normal tissues. We then explored its’ functions in LUAD progression via complementary overexpression and knockdown experiments. The results indicated that MISP plays crucial regulatory role in tumor growth, migration, invasion, apoptosis and metastasis in LUAD, which is probably associated with Hippo signaling.

The Hippo signaling pathway plays a critical role in various physiological and pathological processes, ranging from organ size regulation and tissue regeneration to tumorigenesis and stem cell self-renewal [20, 21]. Numerous studies have confirmed the role of the Hippo signaling pathway in LUAD. One specific mechanism may involve downregulated ACADL expression, which promotes the activation of YAP and then the Hippo pathway [22]. m6A-induced TRIB3 inhibits the Hippo signaling pathway by interacting with LATS1 to promote the progression of LUAD [23]. Moreover, circ_0067741 regulates LATS1 expression and then activates the Hippo/YAP pathway by sponging miR-183-5p, thus suppressing LUAD progression [24]. In the current study, the increased expression of YAP, TAZ and CYR61, and decreased expression of MST1 and p-YAP was observed in LUAD cells that overexpressing MISP. Similar results were observed in subcutaneous LUAD tumor tissues. Importantly, co‐immunoprecipitation experiments demonstrated the direct interaction between MISP and MST1. These findings indicated that MISP controls the Hippo signaling pathway by directly interacting with MST1, influencing its expression level and subsequently regulating downstream signaling processes.

Conclusions

There is a nonnegligible limitation in this study. Conducting the rescue experiments between MISP and Hippo signaling pathway may be more rigorous for the integrity of this study. Even so, our research offers preliminary insights into the role of MISP in LUAD. By exploring its interaction with the Hippo pathway, we highlight MISP’s ability to control the growth, invasion, migration, and metastasis of LUAD cells. These findings may have implications for exploring targeted treatments focused on the MISP-Hippo pathway in LUAD therapy.
