Optical Flares in the Luminous Fast Blue Optical Transient AT2022tsd (“Tasmanian Devil”)
Rachid Ouyed

Abstract
The delayed conversion of a neutron star (NS) into a highly magnetized hybrid star (HS) ejects ∼ 10 − 2 ​ M ⊙ \sim 10^{-2}M_{\odot} of outer NS layers with kinetic energy ∼ 10 50 \sim 10^{50} erg ( Keränen et al. 2005 ) ; the Quark-Nova (QN) ejecta. If this occurs in isolation (or after the SN ejecta had dissipated), the rotational energy of the HS powers a transient with rise and decay times of a few days, consistent with Luminous Fast Blue Optical Transients (LFBOTs) ( Ouyed 2025 ) . Breakup of the QN ejecta creates optically thin fragments that release trapped radiation over tens of minutes timescales, producing optical flares with luminosities comparable to the LFBOT peak value. X-ray emission arises from HS spin-down energy leaking through the gradually transparent ejecta, while radio emission is due to synchrotron radiation from the ejecta’s interaction with the ambient medium. Our model reproduces key features of AT2022tsd and other LFBOTs, including AT2020xnd, AT2020mrf, and AT2018cow. Flares are absent or dwarfed when fragmentation occurs before or during the LFBOT peak emission phase, as likely in AT2018cow. Our model provides a novel explanation for aged NSs with long spin periods that exhibit youthful magnetar-like activity, such as ASKAP J1832–0911 ( Wang et al. 2024 ) . It also carries implications for heavy-element enrichment in the universe, given the formation rate of LFBOTs and the neutron-rich composition of the QN ejecta ( Jaikumar et al. 2007 ; Ouyed et al. 2009 ) .

I
Introduction

LFBOTs are a class of optical transients characterized by rapid rise and decay times (on the order of days) and high peak luminosities (
M_{\rm peak}<-20
, or
L_{\rm peak}>10^{43}
erg s
-1
;
Drout et al. 2014
;
Pursiainen et al. 2018
;
Metzger 2022
). The prototype event, AT2018cow
(
Prentice et al. 2018
;
Rivera Sandoval et al. 2018
;
Margutti et al. 2019
;
Perley et al. 2019
)
, was followed by several analogs including AT2020xnd
(
Perley et al. 2021
)
, AT2020mrf
(
Yao et al. 2022
)
, AT2022tsd
(
Ho et al. 2023a
)
, and AT2023fhn
(
Chrimes et al. 2024a
)
.

Early-time LFBOT spectra show a hot, featureless blue continuum with blackbody (BB) temperatures of
\sim
15,000–30,000 K and a persistent lack of strong spectral lines, with H and He emerging only late (e.g.
Xiang et al. 2021
). The spectral energy distribution often deviates from a single BB, implying additional non-thermal contributions. Bright radio and X-ray emission suggest dense circumstellar interaction and pre-explosion mass loss
(
Ho et al. 2019
;
Huang et al. 2019
;
Coppejans et al. 2020
;
Bright et al. 2022
)
. LFBOTs typically occur in star-forming dwarf and spiral galaxies, though AT2023fhn resides in intergalactic environments (
Chrimes et al. 2024a
) but with a circumstellar environment similar to that of other LFBOTs (
Chrimes et al. 2024b
).

LFBOTs differ from core-collapse supernovae (CCSNe), which have slower evolution and are powered by radioactive decay. While LFBOTs can rival superluminous supernovae (SLSNe) in brightness, they lack hallmark SLSNe features such as broad O II absorption or nebular spectra
(
Gal-Yam 2019
)
, and they occur in different host environments.

Proposed models include low-ejecta-mass CCSNe with central engines powered by magnetar spin-down or black hole accretion (e.g.
Margutti et al. 2019
;
Chen et al. 2023
), shock interaction
(
Fox & Smith 2019
;
Pellegrino et al. 2022
)
, or embedded internal shocks
(
Pasham et al. 2021
)
. Others involve tidal disruption events (TDEs) by intermediate-mass black holes (
Kuin et al. 2019
;
Perley et al. 2019
) or stellar-mass black hole
(
Liu et al. 2018
;
Uno & Maeda 2020b
;
Kremer et al. 2021
)
. Alternative scenarios include jetted explosions via common envelope evolution (
Soker et al. 2019
; see also
Soker 2022
), and fallback or electron-capture SNe
(
Lyutikov & Toonen 2019
;
Quataert et al. 2019
;
Piro & Lu 2020
;
Uno & Maeda 2020a
;
Lyutikov 2022
)
.

Despite the variety of models, none fully explains the multi-wavelength behavior of LFBOTs. In
Ouyed 2025
, we propose that LFBOTs arise from a
non-explosive
phase transition of a NS into a HS, converting only the core to quark matter. The conversion deposits
\sim 10^{50}
erg into
\sim 0.01\penalty\ M_{\odot}
of the NS’s outer layers, producing the QN ejecta
(
Keränen et al. 2005
)
and forming a highly magnetized HS (
B_{\rm HS}\sim 10^{15}
G; see §4 of
Ouyed 2025
).

The QN ejecta is neutron-rich with conditions favorable for r-process nucleosynthesis to take place (
Jaikumar et al. 2007
).
It should in principle produce transients with kilonova-like characteristics
(
Paczyńsky & Wiita 1980
;
Metzger et al. 2010
)
but unlike
conventional kilonovae arising from compact object mergers, QNe are expected to occur in the wake of massive star explosions and/or in isolation, and in very different environments. The role of QNe in contributing to heavy-element enrichment, particularly in the early universe has been discussed
(
Ouyed et al. 2009
)
.

We show that fragmentation of the ejecta into optically thin clumps leads to rapid (
\sim
minutes) optical flares as trapped radiation escapes. These flares can match the peak luminosity of the LFBOT. X-ray emission arises from HS spin-down power escaping through the fragmented ejecta, while radio emission originates from interaction with the surrounding medium. Our model reproduces the emission properties of LFBOTs.

II
Our model

II.1
The NS-to-HS conversion

Hereafter, dimensionless quantities are defined as
f_{x}=f/10^{x}
with quantities in cgs units unless specified otherwise.
We work in the NS frame (i.e. the ambient medium). The speed of the QN ejecta is
v_{\rm QN}=\sqrt{2E_{\rm QN}/M_{\rm QN}}\sim 0.1c\penalty\ E_{\rm QN,50}^{1/2}M_{\rm QN,-2}^{-1/2}
where
c
is the speed of light. It propagates at this velocity until it reaches a radius
R_{\rm dec}=(3M_{\rm QN}/4\pi n_{\rm amb.}m_{\rm p})^{1/3}
, where it collects a comparable mass to its own and starts to decelerate. The ambient mass density is
\rho_{\rm amb}=n_{\rm amb}m_{\rm H}
with
m_{\rm H}
the hydrogen mass. From
R_{\rm dec}=v_{\rm QN}t_{\rm dec}
, we get

t_{\rm dec}\simeq 52.2\ {\rm days}\times\frac{M_{\rm QN,-2}^{5/6}}{E_{\rm QN,50}^{1/2}n_{\rm amb,6}^{1/3}}\ ,

(1)

where the ambient density is in units of
10^{6}
cm
-3
representative of the environment
around the converting NS (see §
IV
).

The ejecta’s density at the deceleration radius we can
derive from
\frac{4\pi}{3}R_{\rm dec}^{3}\rho_{\rm amb}=4\pi R_{\rm dec}^{2}\Delta R_{\rm dec}\rho_{\rm QN,dec}

so that
\rho_{\rm QN,dec}=(\rho_{\rm amb}/3)\times(R_{\rm dec}/\Delta R_{\rm dec})

where hereafter
\Delta R_{\rm dec}=10^{-2}R_{\rm dec}
is the ejecta’s thickness.

We consider a NS born with a mass
M_{\rm NS}=1.5M_{\odot}
, period
P_{\rm NS}\sim 10
ms and magnetic field
B_{\rm NS}=10^{12.5}
G and
a corresponding spin-down (SpD) timescale
t_{\rm NS,SpD}\sim 158\ {\rm yr}\penalty\ B_{\rm HS,12.5}^{-2}P_{\rm HS,-2}^{2}
.
It converts to a HS at time
t_{\rm HS}
after its birth. Its rotational energy is
E_{\rm rot}\sim 3\times 10^{50}\penalty\ P_{\rm NS,-2}^{-2}
erg for a moment of inertia
I_{\rm NS}\sim 2\times 10^{45}
g cm
2
. To ensure that this rotational energy is preserved and is used
up to power the LFBOT by the HS we impose
t_{\rm NS,SpD}>t_{\rm HS}
which translates to

t_{\rm HS}<158\ {\rm year}\penalty\ \frac{P_{\rm NS,-2}^{2}}{B_{\rm NS,12.5}^{2}}\ .

(2)

At
t_{\rm HS}
,
M_{\rm HS}\simeq M_{\rm NS}
,
P_{\rm HS}\simeq P_{\rm NS}
and
B_{\rm HS}\sim 10^{15}\ {\rm G}>>B_{\rm NS}
.

The HS initial rotational energy is that of the NS and is gradually
injected behind the QN ejecta at a rate, which for an
aligned force-free wind is given by
L_{\rm HS,SpD}\sim 6\times 10^{45}\ {\rm erg\ s}^{-1}\penalty\ B_{\rm HS,15}^{2}P_{\rm HS,-2}^{-4}(1+t/t_{\rm HS,SpD})^{-2}

with

t_{\rm HS,SpD}\sim 0.6\ {\rm days}\times B_{\rm HS,15}^{-2}P_{\rm HS,-2}^{2}\ ,

(3)

the HS characteristic SpD timescale.

III.1
Optical: the LFBOT

The QN ejecta’s diffusion timescale is
t_{\rm QN}=\sqrt{2\kappa_{\rm QN}M_{\rm QN}/\beta cv_{\rm QN}}

with
\beta=4\pi^{3}/9
a geometric correction factor (Arnett 1982). This gives

t_{\rm QN,d}\sim 6.3\ {\rm days}\penalty\ \kappa_{\rm QN,1}^{1/2}M_{\rm QN,-2}^{3/4}E_{\rm QN,50}^{-1/4}\ ,

(4)

where we made use of
v_{\rm QN}=\sqrt{2E_{\rm QN}/M_{\rm QN}}
. Because of the ejecta’s composition,
its opacity we take to be
\kappa_{\rm QN}=10
cm
2
gm
-1
(e.g.
Kasen et al. 2013
).

The total rotational energy deposited up to time
t
is
E_{\rm HS,SpD}\sim E_{\rm HS,SpD}(0)\times t/(t+t_{\rm QN,d})
and since

t_{\rm HS,SpD}<t_{\rm QN,d}
, the LFBOT luminosity peaks
at around
t_{\rm QN,d}
with
L_{\rm LFBOT,pk}\sim E_{\rm HS,SpD}/3t_{\rm QN,d}
(e.g.
Kasen & Bildsten 2010
and references therein).
Hereafter, the subscript “pk” stands for peak. We find

L_{\rm LFBOT,pk}\sim 7.3\times 10^{44}\ {\rm erg\ s}^{-1}\times\frac{E_{\rm QN,50}^{1/4}}{P_{\rm HS,-2}^{2}\kappa_{\rm QN,1}^{1/2}M_{\rm QN,-2}^{3/4}}\ .

(5)

For
L_{\rm LFBOT,pk}>10^{43.5}
erg s
-1
, we require
P_{\rm HS}<50\ {\rm ms}
for fiducial values.

The average time it takes the QN to become optically thin (i.e.
\tau_{\rm QN}\rho_{\rm QN}\Delta R_{\rm QN}\sim 1
) is

t_{\rm QN,tr}\sim 15\ {\rm days}\penalty\ \kappa_{\rm QN,1}^{1/2}M_{\rm QN,-2}E_{\rm QN,50}^{-1/2}\ ,

(6)

with
M_{\rm QN}=4\pi R_{\rm QN}^{2}\rho_{\rm QN}\Delta R_{\rm QN}
and
R_{\rm QN}=v_{\rm QN}t
.
Since
v_{\rm QN}<0.5c
, we have
t_{\rm QN,tr}>t_{\rm QN,d}
indicating that diffusion is nearly complete before the ejecta becomes transparent;
t_{\rm QN,tr}
is an average timescale, and we expect transparency to be reached in a spatially non-uniform manner across the ejecta.

We follow
Chatzopoulos et al. 2012
when computing the spin-down powered light-curves (LCs)
and include leakage of energy via hard emission with a leakage parameter
A_{\rm HS}=9\kappa_{\rm QN}M_{\rm QN}^{2}/40\pi E_{\rm QN}
(
Wang et al. 2015
).

III.2
Optical: the flares

The QN ejecta fragments as it expands and cools. We define
R_{\rm f}
and
t_{\rm f}
as the
fragments’ characteristic radius and formation time (since the NS conversion), respectively.

To model the fragments’ radius distribution, we employed a stochastic sampling approach based on the Weibull probability distribution (WPD;
Weibull 1951
).
Fragment radii were sampled iteratively from a WPD with the scale parameter
\lambda_{\rm radius}=R_{\rm f,pk}(\frac{k_{\rm radius}}{k_{\rm radius}-1})^{1/k_{\rm radius}}
defining the most probable fragment radius and
R_{\rm f,pk}
the peak fragment size;
k_{\rm radius}>1
is the shape parameter.
WPD is selected due to its flexibility in representing a range of fragment size distributions, particularly its ability to capture both broad (
k_{\rm radius}<2
) and sharply peaked (
k_{\rm radius}>2
) radius spectra.

The fragment formation time
t_{\rm f}
is sampled using a Weibull failure time model (WFTD) where fragments detach from the main QN ejecta at different times.
The ejecta is likely to start fragmenting following radiation pressure loss while it is being compressed/decelerated by ambient material.
I.e. we expect fragmentation to take place at
t_{\rm QN,d}<t<t_{\rm QN,tr}
. The scale parameter is set so that the characteristic fragment formation time is a fraction of the QN ejecta transparency timescale
t_{\rm f,pk}=\alpha_{\rm f}t_{\rm QN,tr}
with
\alpha_{\rm f}\leq 1
and
t_{\rm QN,tr}
given by Eq. (
6
); i.e.
\lambda_{\rm time}=t_{\rm f,pk}(\frac{k_{\rm time}}{k_{\rm time}-1})^{1/k_{\rm time}}
.
The distribution shape can vary from a spread in fragmentation time (
k_{\rm time}<2
)
to clustering of fragmentation events around
t_{\rm f,pk}
(
k_{\rm time}>2
).
The total number of fragments was not fixed in advance. Instead, fragment masses (
m_{\rm f}\propto\rho_{\rm f}R_{\rm f}^{3}
) were added sequentially and accumulated until their combined mass reached
M_{\rm QN}
.

At time
t_{\rm f}
, the fragment density (
\rho_{\rm f}
), opacity (
\kappa_{\rm f}
) and temperature (
T_{\rm f}
) are set to those of the QN ejecta. I.e.

\rho_{\rm f}=\rho_{\rm QN,f}=M_{\rm QN}/4\pi R_{\rm QN,f}^{2}\Delta R_{\rm QN,f}=(R_{\rm QN}/\Delta R_{\rm QN})M_{\rm QN}/4\pi R_{\rm QN,f}^{3}

with the size of the QN ejecta then being
R_{\rm QN,f}=v_{\rm QN}t_{\rm f}
so that
\rho_{\rm f}\propto t_{\rm f}^{-3}
.

The optical depth of a given fragment is
\tau_{\rm f}=\kappa_{\rm f}\rho_{\rm f}R_{\rm f}
.
If
\tau_{\rm f}<1
, a fragment is selected as a flare candidate otherwise it is ignored and it continues to contribute to the LFBOT LC.
Around the transparency time,
t_{\rm QN,tr}
, for example, when
\rho_{\rm QN,tr}=M_{\rm QN}/4\pi R_{\rm QN,tr}^{2}\Delta R_{\rm QN,tr}\sim 10^{-16}
g cm
-1
, we estimate
\tau_{\rm f,tr}\sim 0.1
for fiducial parameter values.

The duration of the flare corresponds to light-crossing time
R_{\rm f}/c
,

\Delta t_{\rm f,pk}\sim 55.6\ {\rm mn}\times R_{\rm f,pk,14}\ ,

(7)

which is in tens of minutes for a fiducial peak value of
R_{\rm f,pk}=10^{14}
cm.

The radiation energy stored in a fragment of volume
V_{\rm f}
is
E_{\rm f,rad.}\simeq aT_{\rm f}^{4}V_{\rm f}\sim E_{\rm HS,SpD}\times(V_{\rm f}/V_{\rm QN})

since
E_{\rm HS,SpD}=aT_{\rm QN,f}^{4}V_{\rm QN,f}
;
V_{\rm QN,f}
is the volume of the QN ejecta at
t_{\rm f}
and
a=7.566\times 10^{-15}\,\mathrm{erg\,cm^{-3}\,K^{-4}}
the radiation constant.

A detached fragment, if optically thin, suddenly releases the trapped radiation with a luminosity

\displaystyle L_{\rm f}

\displaystyle=\frac{E_{\rm f,rad}}{\Delta t_{\rm f}}\sim\frac{E_{\rm HS,SpD}(t_{\rm f})}{\Delta t_{\rm f}}\times\left(\frac{m_{\rm f}}{M_{\rm QN}}\right)

(8)

\displaystyle\sim L_{\rm LFBOT,pk}\times\left(\frac{t_{\rm QN,d}}{\Delta t_{\rm f}}\right)\left(\frac{m_{\rm f}}{M_{\rm QN}}\right)\ ,

where we made use of
\rho_{\rm f}\sim\rho_{\rm QN,f}
, and the fact that

E_{\rm SpD}\sim L_{\rm LFBOT,pk}t_{\rm QN,d}
. Thus, the sudden transparency of detached fragments results in stored thermal energy rapidly radiated away with
flare luminosities at the level of the LFBOT peak value;

t_{\rm QN,d}/\Delta t_{\rm f}\sim 10^{3}
and
m_{\rm f}/M_{\rm QN}\sim 10^{-3}
in our model (see below).
The computed flare luminosity is compared to those in AT2022tsd in
Figure
1
. Flares occurring during the LFBOT
peak phase will be hard to distinguish from the LFBOT (as in AT2018cow; see §
III.5
).

In general, a simultaneous fit to the LFBOT LC and to the optical flares can be obtained
by carefully choosing the shape and scale parameters used for the WPD and WFTD.
We find that a reasonable spread in radius (
k_{\rm radius}=1.5
) and formation time (
k_{\rm time}=1.5
) reproduces
AT2022tsd optical emission. The case shown in Figure
1
is for the parameters listed in Table

with
\alpha_{\rm f}=1.0
which is when the peak fragment formation time coincides with the QN ejecta optical transparency.

and

(from top to bottom) of the resulting, optically thin, fragments versus their mass
m_{\rm f}
(in units of

). The x-axis shows
m_{\rm f}

III.3
X-ray: the HS spin-down

The spin-down luminosity of the HS escapes through optically thin gaps in the fragmented ejecta, appearing intermittent and discontinuous. For times
t>t_{\rm HS,SpD}
, the spin-down luminosity scales as
L_{\rm HS,SpD}\propto B_{\rm HS,15}^{-2}t^{-2}
, becoming independent of the HS rotation period. Specifically,

L_{\rm x}\sim 2\times 10^{45}\ {\rm erg\ s}^{-1}\penalty\ B_{\rm HS,15}^{-2}\ t_{\rm day}^{-2}\ ,

(9)

where
B_{\rm HS,15}
is the surface magnetic field in units of
10^{15}
G, and
t_{\rm day}
is time in days. As shown in Figure
1
(the X-ray LC is shown starting at
t_{\rm QN,tr}
), this relation naturally reproduces the observed X-ray luminosity of AT2022tsd. It also implies that a significant fraction of the unprocessed spin-down energy is emitted directly as X-rays.

III.4
Radio: the QN shock

Radio emission arises from electrons accelerated at the shock front as the non-relativistic QN ejecta expands and interacts with the surrounding medium. These mildly relativistic electrons follow a power-law energy distribution with slope
2<p<3
. The spectral luminosity at an observed frequency
\nu_{\rm obs}
is given by (e.g.,
Nakar & Piran 2011
;
Granot, et al. 2006
;
Sironi & Giannios 2013
):

\displaystyle L_{\nu_{\rm obs}}(t)

\displaystyle=L_{\rm pk,dec}\left(\frac{\nu_{\rm pk,dec}}{\nu_{\rm obs}}\right)^{(p-1)/2}\times

(10)

\displaystyle\times\begin{cases}\left(\frac{t}{t_{\rm dec}}\right)^{\frac{3(7-5p)}{10}},&t\geq t_{\rm dec}\\
\left(\frac{t}{t_{\rm dec}}\right)^{3},&t<t_{\rm dec}\end{cases}

Here,
\nu_{\rm pk,dec}\sim 78.4\penalty\ {\rm GHz}\penalty\ g(p)^{2}\epsilon_{\rm e,-1}^{2}\epsilon_{\rm B,-2}^{1/2}E_{\rm QN,50}n_{\rm amb,6}^{1/2}t_{\rm dec,day}^{-3}
is the peak frequency at the deceleration time
t_{\rm dec}
(in days), and
L_{\rm pk,dec}\sim 1.7\times 10^{33}\penalty\ {\rm erg\penalty\ s^{-1}\penalty\ Hz^{-1}}\penalty\ \epsilon_{\rm B,-2}^{1/2}E_{\rm QN,50}^{4/5}n_{\rm amb,6}^{7/10}t_{\rm dec,day}^{3/5}
is the corresponding peak luminosity. The parameters
\epsilon_{\rm e}
and
\epsilon_{\rm B}
are the fractions of the internal energy in electrons and magnetic fields, respectively, and
g(p)=(p-2)/(p-1)
.

Since the peak synchrotron frequency
\nu_{\rm pk,dec}\sim\nu_{\rm m}
(corresponding to the minimal Lorentz factor
\gamma_{\rm m}
) and synchrotron self-absorption frequencies are below
\nu_{\rm obs}
, the deceleration time also marks the radio peak.

For a given ambient density
n_{\rm amb}
, which sets
t_{\rm dec}
, we fit the radio emission by adjusting
\epsilon_{\rm e}
,
\epsilon_{\rm B}
, and
p
, as listed in Table

. This model reproduces the observed radio light curve of AT2022tsd, as shown in Figure

.
Their luminosity is similar to

and would be hard to differentiate from the main LC; see Figure

n_{\rm amb}

It also required the highest value of
p

The observed X-ray emission can be fit with a decay profile steeper than

It is important to note that the X-ray emission expected from the same electron population responsible for the radio synchrotron emission is too weak to account for this excess. One possible contributor could be magnetar-like activity from the HS, though a detailed exploration of this scenario is beyond the scope of this paper (see bullet (iv) in §

IV
Discussion

There are unusual properties of LFBOTs worth discussing:

(i)
The Hot, Blue, Featureless Spectrum
: The combination of the QN ejecta’s low mass and high velocity is expected to result in strong Doppler broadening and line blending, effectively washing out any potential absorption features. By equating the BB luminosity to the SpD energy injection rate,

4\pi(v_{\rm QN}t)^{2}\sigma_{\rm SB}T_{\rm BB}^{4}\sim\frac{E_{\rm SpD}(0)}{t_{\rm QN,d}}\times\frac{t}{t+t_{\rm QN,d}},

we find that the effective BB temperature evolves as
T_{\rm BB}\propto t^{-1/4}
for
t<t_{\rm QN,d}
, and as
T_{\rm BB}\propto t^{-1/2}
for
t>t_{\rm QN,d}
, yielding characteristic temperatures of
T_{\rm BB}(t\sim t_{\rm QN,d})\sim 15{,}000
–
30{,}000\,\mathrm{K}
for fiducial parameters. Here,
\sigma_{\rm SB}
is the Stefan–Boltzmann constant.

The appearance of flares is expected to coincide with a spectral shift from a purely thermal BB to a composite spectrum that includes non-thermal components. Nevertheless, due to freely streaming photons, the underlying BB component should remain at least partially discernible in the flare spectra.

Hydrogen and helium lines have been observed at late times in the spectra of some LFBOTs (see §
I
). Given the expected heavy nuclear composition of the QN ejecta, these H and He signatures are likely not intrinsic to the ejecta. Instead, they are plausibly the result of mixing between the QN ejecta and the ambient medium. Consequently, these lighter elements are expected to appear only after sufficient mixing has occurred – typically near or after the onset of ejecta deceleration.

(ii)
Extremely delayed HS formation and off-galaxy LFBOTs
: In rare cases, the conversion of a NS into a HS could occur long after the NS is born – potentially millions of years later – if the conditions in the NS core make the quantum nucleation of quark matter extremely slow (see §4 of
Ouyed 2025
).

Using the condition for LFBOT peak luminosity,
L_{\rm LFBOT,pk}>10^{43.5}\ {\rm erg\ s^{-1}}
(Eq.
5
), and combining it with the constraint
P_{\rm HS}\sim P_{\rm NS}
from Eq. (
2
), we find that

t_{\rm HS}<4000\ {\rm yr}\times\frac{E_{\rm QN,50}^{1/4}}{\kappa_{\rm QN,1}^{1/2}M_{\rm QN,-2}^{3/4}B_{\rm NS,12.5}^{2}}.

(11)

This implies that an NS born with
P_{\rm NS}<50
ms,
B_{\rm NS}<10^{11}
G, and a typical kick velocity of
v_{\rm NS}\sim 300\ {\rm km\ s^{-1}}
, could travel several kiloparsecs before converting to an HS and producing an LFBOT. This might help explain the apparent host-less location of AT2023fhn
(
Chrimes et al. 2024a
)
.

Chrimes et al. 2024b
find that the local circumstellar environment of AT2023fhn is comparable to that of other LFBOTs, suggesting a common progenitor channel. One possibility is the progenitor of the NS, born in a stellar system, that receives a natal kick, propelling it far from its origin. The NS could then undergo a delayed conversion into a HS on fiducial timescales of tens of years. In this scenario, the circumstellar environment at the time of conversion would still reflect the original progenitor’s mass loss history; e.g., a dense wind environment shaped by a massive star and swept up by the earlier SN. For completeness, we note that NS formation through binary mergers is likely not viable in this context. Merger scenarios, including those involving white dwarfs or compact object pairs, appear disfavored as the origin of LFBOTs
(
Sarin et al. 2022
, e.g.,)
.

(iii)
LFBOT rate estimate
: In our model, the period of the converting neutron star (NS) is
P_{\rm NS}<50
ms. Additionally, to account for sources such as AT2023fhn if need to (see above), we impose a magnetic field threshold of
B_{\rm NS}>10^{10}
G.

We adopt a log-normal distribution for the NS birth magnetic field, characterized by a mean of
\mu_{\log B_{\rm NS}}=12.5
and a standard deviation of
\sigma_{\log B_{\rm NS}}=0.5
. For the birth spin periods, we use a normal distribution with a mean of
\mu_{P_{\rm NS}}=300
ms and a standard deviation of
\sigma_{P_{\rm NS}}=150
ms (e.g.,
Faucher-Giguère & Kaspi 2006
).

The maximum LFBOT rate is then estimated as:

\displaystyle r_{\rm LFBOT,\ max}

\displaystyle=\left[\frac{\int_{10}^{13}\exp\left(-\frac{(\log{B_{\rm NS}}-\mu_{\log{B_{\rm NS}}})^{2}}{2\sigma_{\log{B_{\rm NS}}}^{2}}\right)\,d\log B_{\rm NS}}{\int_{10}^{15}\exp\left(-\frac{(\log{B_{\rm NS}}-\mu_{\log{B_{\rm NS}}})^{2}}{2\sigma_{\log{B_{\rm NS}}}^{2}}\right)\,d\log B_{\rm NS}}\right]\times

\displaystyle\quad\left[\frac{\int_{4}^{50}\exp\left(-\frac{(P_{\rm NS}-\mu_{P_{\rm NS}})^{2}}{2\sigma_{P_{\rm NS}}^{2}}\right)\,dP_{\rm NS}}{\int_{1.5}^{\infty}\exp\left(-\frac{(P_{\rm NS}-\mu_{P_{\rm NS}})^{2}}{2\sigma_{P_{\rm NS}}^{2}}\right)\,dP_{\rm NS}}\right]\times

\displaystyle\quad r_{M_{\rm prog}}\times r_{\rm CCSN},

(12)

where
r_{M_{\rm prog}}
is the fraction of CCSNe that give birth to NSs, and
r_{\rm CCSN}
is the volumetric CCSN rate.
Naturally, only a subset of these NSs are expected to undergo conversion (
Ouyed 2025
).

Evaluating the above with representative values, we obtain

r_{\rm LFBOT,\ max}\sim 0.8\times 0.02\times 0.8\times r_{\rm CCSN}\sim 0.01\,r_{\rm CCSN},

which can be compared to the estimated LFBOT rate of
\lesssim 0.1\%
of the local CCSN rate (
Coppejans et al. 2020
;
Ho et al. 2023b
).

The lower bound of 4 ms in the period integral reflects the requirement that NS conversion occurs only when the SN ejecta are optically thin. Otherwise, the event would be reprocessed by the ejecta, producing a SLSN instead of a LFBOT (see
Ouyed 2025
). Accordingly, we expect the SLSN rate to be lower than
r_{\rm LFBOT,\ max}
.

(iv)
Aged magnetars with youthful activity
: In our model, magnetar-like behavior arises not at the time of NS birth, but during a much later transition – when the NS converts into a HS. This conversion leads to the emergence of strong surface magnetic fields (of magnetar strength), regardless of whether an LFBOT is produced (e.g., if
P_{\rm NS}>50
ms). The resulting object is still expected to exhibit magnetar-like activity.

Our model offers a plausible explanation for NSs with long spin periods exhibiting highly energetic emission that appears inconsistent with their inferred age.
ASKAP J1832
-
0911
(
Wang et al. 2024
)
may be one such candidate, where we propose that a delayed conversion to a HS triggers the magnetar-like emission.

(v)
Kilonova-like emission from QN ejecta:
The QN ejecta, being neutron-rich and a site of
r
-process nucleosynthesis
(
Jaikumar et al. 2007
)
, is expected to exhibit kilonova-like features (
Paczyńsky & Wiita 1980
;
Metzger et al. 2010
). However, unlike traditional kilonovae associated with compact object mergers, these events occur in isolation—far from typical binary merger environments—and may therefore evolve differently both observationally and dynamically. For a discussion of the implications of the QN channel for heavy-element enrichment in the early universe (see
Ouyed et al. 2009
).

(vi)
Flare Distributions
: Because the flare durations are expected to follow a Weibull distribution (with
\Delta t_{\rm f}\propto R_{\rm f}
), and the formation times (specifically the intervals between flare arrival times) are expected to follow a WFTD, we performed a quantile–quantile (Q–Q) test
(
Wilk & Gnanadesikan 1968
)
for the case of AT2022tsd to assess consistency between the observed and theoretical distributions.

In this analysis, the empirical quantiles correspond to the sorted observed values, while the theoretical quantiles were computed by applying the inverse cumulative distribution function (CDF) of the fitted model to uniformly spaced probabilities. Despite the limited sample size, Figure
5
shows that the theoretical quantiles broadly align with the 45
∘
reference line, indicating tentative agreement. However, a more robust statistical analysis is required to confirm this result.

(vii)
The ambient density:
Our fits to LFBOT observations suggest ambient densities of order
10^{6}\text{--}10^{7}\,\mathrm{cm^{-3}}
near the NS at the time of conversion. Whether such high densities persist 10-100 years after a supernova (SN) remains uncertain, but they are plausible under certain conditions. For high-mass SN ejecta, the slower-expanding inner layers can remain dense for extended periods, especially if they interact with a dense circumstellar medium shaped by pre-SN mass loss. This interaction can decelerate and compress the inner ejecta, potentially below the NS’s kick velocity, allowing the NS (moving at
\sim 300\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}
) to remain embedded in or near dense regions for decades. For example, with shock compression, an ejecta mass of
M_{\rm ej}=5\,M_{\odot}
, inner velocity
v_{\rm ej,in}=300\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}
, and time
t=100\,\mathrm{yr}
, a basic estimate of the number density is
n_{\rm amb}\sim 4\times 3M_{\rm ej}/4\pi(v_{\rm ej,in}t)^{3}m_{p}\sim 7\times 10^{6}\ \mathrm{cm^{-3}}
. A full parameter survey of LFBOT fits is pending and may further relax the constraints on the required ambient density.

V
Conclusion

The delayed conversion of a NS to a HS in isolation – or in the wake of a thin SN ejecta – ejects roughly
10^{-2}M_{\odot}
of the NS’s outer layers at velocities near
0.1c

(
Keränen et al. 2005
)
. The newly formed HS, endowed with a magnetar-strength magnetic field, powers the QN ejecta, successfully reproducing the LC of AT2022tsd.

We interpret the observed optical flares as radiation released from optically thin fragments formed by the breakup of the QN ejecta. These fragments emit stored spin-down energy from the HS on timescales of tens of minutes, comparable to their light-crossing times. The peak luminosities of these flares match that of the underlying LFBOT and may be obscured if fragments form during the LFBOT peak phase (as may be the case in AT2018cow).

X-ray emission originates from HS spin-down radiation escaping through gaps in the fragmented ejecta, coinciding with the ejecta becoming optically thin and beginning to transfer energy to the ambient medium. Radio emission arises from shock-accelerated electrons in the surrounding medium, energized by the interaction between the QN ejecta and the environment.

Our model successfully fits the optical, X-ray, and radio observations of AT2022tsd and other LFBOTs examined here. If this model accurately represents the LFBOT phenomenon, then indeed, the “Tasmanian devil” is in the details.
