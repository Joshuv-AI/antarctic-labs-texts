Sonographic identification of the tendinous septum of the semispinalis capitis muscle
Authors: Muhammed Oğuz, Burak Tayyip Dede, Bülent Alyanak, Fatih Bağcıer, Mustafa Turgut Yıldızgören
Published: 2026

The posterior cervical region is anatomically complex and includes multiple muscles, nerves, and fascial layers. Together with earlier cadaveric studies, recent sonographic studies have informed the development of several pain management techniques targeting this region [1]. However, this anatomical complexity can hinder the effective performance of therapeutic procedures and may lengthen the learning curve for clinicians in training.

The semispinalis capitis (SSCa) muscle is an important component of the posterior cervical region. It originates from the transverse processes of the C4–C7 and T1–T6 vertebrae and inserts into the superior nuchal line of the occipital bone. Bilateral contraction of the SSCa contributes to increased cervical lordosis, whereas unilateral contraction contributes to neck extension and ipsilateral lateral flexion. Its role in cervical rotation remains a topic of debate [2]. The SSCa is among the more superficial muscle groups in the posterior cervical region. It is a two-headed muscle: the medial head is termed the biventer cervicis, and the lateral head is termed the complexus [3]. At the upper cervical levels, the medial head of the SSCa is pierced by, and lies in close relation to, clinically important neural structures, including the greater occipital nerve and the third occipital nerve [4].

Ultrasound guidance is increasingly used for injections in the posterior cervical region. It can help clinicians plan the needle trajectory, reduce the risk of nerve and vascular injury, avoid intravascular injection, and observe the targeted intervention in real time [5]. In this context, sonographic characterization of the SSCa is clinically relevant because the muscle serves as an important reference point during greater occipital nerve blocks, third occipital nerve blocks, and facet joint injections [5]. The SSCa may also be targeted during botulinum toxin injections for cervical dystonia [6]. In addition, sonographic recognition of the SSCa may be useful in the treatment of myofascial pain syndrome. Across these applications, the tendinous septum of the SSCa serves as a useful sonographic landmark that distinguishes the SSCa from other posterior cervical muscular structures. Thus, the tendinous septum of the SSCa may be both a characteristic sonographic landmark and a clinically relevant anatomical structure.

Ohgoshi et al. [7] investigated why the dorsal rami of C2 and C3 were not affected by an inter-semispinal plane block performed between the SSCa and semispinalis cervicis muscles in cadavers. They reported that the tendinous septum, located between the lateral and medial heads of the SSCa and described as having a partial but strong connection with the dorsal edge of the semispinalis cervicis muscle, extends obliquely from superior to inferior and separates the dorsal rami of C3 and C4. The C2 and C3 dorsal rami emerge on the medial head, whereas the C4 dorsal ramus emerges on the lateral head. The study also indicated that injection into the medial head of the SSCa at the C1 level can block the greater occipital nerve and third occipital nerve but does not cross the tendinous septum. In this context, the tendinous septum should be carefully considered, particularly when each functional unit of the target muscle must be reached, such as during botulinum toxin injection. For these reasons, we propose a three-step approach for sonographic visualization of the tendinous septum of the SSCa, which we consider important from both sonoanatomical and clinical perspectives: (1) Palpate the C7 spinous process; (2) Place the probe transversely on the spinous process (Fig. 1A) and displace it laterally (Fig. 1B, C); (3) Identify the SSCa by detecting the tendinous septum within the muscle while moving the probe cranially (Fig. 2).

In conclusion, this letter describes the sonographic characterization of the tendinous septum of the SSCa and emphasizes the potential value of this structure for improving the effectiveness of clinical applications. Sonographic identification of the tendinous septum may also improve the understanding of posterior cervical region sonoanatomy.
