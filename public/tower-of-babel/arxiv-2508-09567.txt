Rotational Properties of Superradiant Condensates Journal: Physics Letters A
Yin-Da Guo, Kai-Dong Zhou, Shou-Shan Bao, Hong Zhang

Abstract
Superradiance enables scalar fields to extract energy and angular momentum from a rotating black hole (BH), leading to the formation of a BH-condensate system. The condensate appears to exhibit global rotation as a rigid body, with phase propagation occurring only in the azimuthal direction, such a picture leads to unphysical superluminal velocities at large distances. In this work, we demonstrate that the apparent rotation is a collective effect arising from local radial, polar, and azimuthal motions. In the region sufficiently far from the BH, the condensate exhibits a radial velocity magnitude that approaches ( r g ​ μ / n ¯ ) ​ sin ⁡ ( 2 ​ ω ​ t − 2 ​ φ ) (r_{g}\mu/\bar{n})\sin(2\omega t-2\varphi) , while the polar and azimuthal velocity magnitudes asymptotically decline as ∝ 1 / r \propto 1/r .

In this work, we focus on the simplest scalar scenario, clarifying a common misinterpretation regarding the rotational properties of superradiant condensates.
Intuitively, the condensate resembles a rigid body and appears to possess a global angular velocity equivalent to its frequency
\omega
. However, the spatial scale of the condensate is far larger than that of the BH and diffuses in the entire space. The scale increases further as the system approaches the non-relativistic limit. If the condensate indeed carries a global angular velocity, it has larger velocity as the distance increases. This indicates that regions of the condensate far from the BH would become relativistic, or even exceed the speed of light, which is clearly unphysical. Therefore, the condensate could not possess a global angular velocity. The precise manner in which the condensate rotates remains unclear in the literature, and will be clarified in Sec.
4
.

This paper is organized as follows. In Sec.
2
, we briefly review scalar superradiance and the BH-condensate system. Then the normalization of the scalar field is discussed in Sec.
3
. In Sec.
4
, we examine the rotational properties of the condensate. Finally, we present our summary and discussion in Sec.
5
.

\hbar=c=1

\displaystyle r_{\pm}=r_{g}\pm\sqrt{r_{g}^{2}-a^{2}}.

In this work, we investigate a real scalar field bound in the background of a Kerr BH. Given that the energy density of the condensate is much lower than that of the BH, its backreaction on the spacetime geometry can be safely neglected

\displaystyle\left(\nabla^{\nu}\nabla_{\nu}+\mu^{2}\right)\Phi=0,

\displaystyle\Phi(t,r,\theta,\varphi)=\sum_{l,m}\int d\omega\frac{f_{lm}(\omega)}{\sqrt{2\omega}}\left[\phi_{lm}(t,r,\theta,\varphi)+\phi^{*}_{lm}(t,r,\theta,\varphi)\right],

Generally, the eigenfrequencies are complex numbers and are characterized by three indices

\displaystyle\omega_{nlm}=\mu\left[1-\frac{\left(r_{g}\mu\right)^{2}}{2(n+l+1)^{2}}+\mathcal{O}\left(r_{g}^{4}\mu^{4}\right)\right].

(13)

Normalization

4
Rotation of condensate

It appears the condensate rotates with a global angular velocity as a rigid body. As discussed in the Introduction, If the statement is true, it would lead to an unphysical situation where the non-relativistic condensate becomes relativistic or even exceeds the speed of light far from the BH. Taking
r_{g}\mu=0.1
for instance, the characteristic radius of the condensate is approximately
r_{\mathrm{c}}\approx 200r_{g}
. At this radius, the velocity is
v\sim\mu r_{\mathrm{c}}=20\gg 1
, which is clearly unphysical.

4.2
Velocity

In fact, the apparent rotation of the condensate is a collective effect resulting from local motions with radial, polar, and azimuthal components. The local velocity is defined as

\displaystyle v^{i}=P^{i}/\rho,

(31)

where the momentum density
P^{i}
is given by

\displaystyle P^{i}\equiv T^{\nu}_{\ \sigma}\left(e_{t}\right)^{\sigma}\left(e^{i}\right)_{\nu}.

(32)

Figure
6
(a) presents the velocity distributions on the
z=0
plane at
t=0
. The color indicates the magnitude of the velocity, while arrows denote the velocity projections onto the
z=0
plane. For convenience, Fig.
6
(c) shows the distribution of
\delta\rho
, overlapped with the projected velocity direction. At
t=0
, the energy flux vanishes on the
y=0
plane, which separates the space into two regions. In the first (third) quadrant, some streamlines from the inner region extend to the outer region, while others flow into the second (fourth) quadrant. As a result, energy accumulates in the second and fourth quadrants, while dissipates from the first and third quadrants. Altogether, it produces the counter-clockwise apparent rotation of
\delta\rho
in the
x-y
plane. Accordingly, the zero-flux plane, which is
y=0
at
t=0
, rotates in the same pace as
\delta\rho
.

Besides the motion in the transverse directions, the energy flow also has non-zero
z
component. Figure
6
(b) shows the magnitude of the velocity on the
y=\tan(\pi/32)x
plane as well as its direction projection on this plane. Combining Fig.
6
(a,b), the three-dimensional distribution of streamlines with
y>0
are similar to the electric field lines of an electric dipole with positive and negative charges located at
(r,\theta,\varphi)\approx(200\,r_{g},\pi/2,0)
and
(200\,r_{g},\pi/2,\pi)
, respectively. On the other hand, the velocity steamlines in the
y<0
region are similar to the electric field lines of a reversed dipole. Then these two regions are glued at
y=0
plane, on which the energy flux vanishes. This picture is qualitatively corerect except in the region
r\gg r_{\mathrm{c}}
, which will be explained later.

From the symmetry argument, the energy flux is purely azimuthal on the
x=0
plane at
t=0
. To show there is no superluminal motion, the velocity magnitude
|v|\equiv\sqrt{v^{i}v_{i}}
along the positive
y
axis as a function of radial distance
r
is plotted with the solid red curve in Fig.
7
. As
r
increases,
|v|
first increases, then decreases to zero at infinity, peaking at
r=8.90\,r_{g}
where
|v|\sim 1
. The cruves along other directions exhibit a little more complex behavior. In Fig.
7
, the
|v|
in
\theta=\pi/2
and
\varphi=\pi/4
direction is plotted with the solid blue curve. The velocity magnitude initially exhibits a similar trend with a smaller peak of
0.747
at
r=8.63\,r_{g}
. It then reaches its minimum value of 0.0358 at
r=398.05\,r_{g}
, and subsequently increases, approaching a constant value of 0.0502 at infinity. Therefore, the condensate moves relativistically only in the region
r\lesssim 50r_{g}
. At the Bohr radius
r_{\text{b}}
, the motion can be safely taken as non-relativistic, which qualifies the perturbative treatment in Ref.
Baumann:2018vus
.

For deeper understanding of the rotational behavior, we analyze the system in the non-relativistic limit,
r_{g}\mu\ll 1
. Focusing on the region where
r\gg r_{g}
, the radial equation (
12a
) has an asymptotic solution
Detweiler:1980uk
,
Bao:2022hew
:

\displaystyle R_{nl}(r)\sim(-2qr)^{l}e^{qr}U(-n,2l+2,-2qr),

(33)

where
U
is the confluent hypergeometric function of the second kind. The spheroidal harmonic
S_{nlm}(\theta)
reduces to the associated Legendre function
\mathcal{P}_{lm}(\theta)
. In flat spacetime, the energy and momentum densities of the
\{0,1,1\}
mode are given by

\displaystyle\rho

\displaystyle\approx\omega_{011}R^{2}_{01}(r)\mathcal{P}^{2}_{11}(\theta),

(34a)

\displaystyle P^{r}

\displaystyle=R_{01}(r)R_{01}^{\prime}(r)\mathcal{P}^{2}_{11}(\theta)\sin\left(2\omega_{011}t-2\varphi\right),

(34b)

\displaystyle P^{\theta}

\displaystyle=\frac{R^{2}_{01}(r)\mathcal{P}_{11}(\theta)\mathcal{P}_{11}^{\prime}(\theta)\sin\left(2\omega_{011}t-2\varphi\right)}{r},

(34c)

\displaystyle P^{\varphi}

\displaystyle=\frac{2R^{2}_{01}(r)\mathcal{P}^{2}_{11}(\theta)\sin^{2}\left(\omega_{011}t-\varphi\right)}{r\sin\theta}.

(34d)

Substituting the asymptotic expressions of
R_{01}(r)\mathcal{P}_{11}(\theta)
, the velocity at large distances can be approximated as

\displaystyle v^{r}

\displaystyle\approx\frac{1-r/r_{\mathrm{c}}}{\mu r}\sin\left(2\omega_{011}t-2\varphi\right),

(35a)

\displaystyle v^{\theta}

\displaystyle\approx\frac{\cot\theta}{\mu r}\sin\left(2\omega_{011}t-2\varphi\right),

(35b)

\displaystyle v^{\varphi}

\displaystyle\approx\frac{2}{\mu r\sin\theta}\sin^{2}\left(\omega_{011}t-\varphi\right),

(35c)

where the approximations
\omega_{011}\approx\mu
and
r_{\mathrm{c}}\approx 2r_{\mathrm{b}}
are used. The asymptotic expression for
v^{r}
(
v^{\theta}
and
v^{\varphi}
) is valid when
\mu r\gg 1
(
\mu r\sin\theta\gg 1
). They are plotted as the dashed curves and compared with the full result at
t=0
in Fig.
7
. The agreement is excellent at
r\gtrsim r_{\text{b}}=100\,r_{g}
.

The asymptotic velocity components in Eqs. (
35
) share the same period as
\delta\rho
. The polar and azimuthal velocities decrease proportionally to
1/r
with increasing radial distance. The direction of
v^{\theta}
reverses across the equatorial plane, while the sign of
v^{\varphi}
is the same in the entire space, aligned with the BH spin. The radial velocity component
v^{r}
changes sign at
r=r_{\mathrm{c}}
. These observations are consistent with Fig.
6
.

We further focus on the region where
r\gg r_{\mathrm{c}}
. With
\omega_{011}t-\varphi=k\pi
and
k\in\mathbb{Z}
, all velocity components vanish, defining the zero-flux plane. With
\omega_{011}t-\varphi=(k+1/2)\pi
and
k\in\mathbb{Z}
, the flux is purely in the azimuthal direction. Otherwise, the flux is almost purely radial at
r\to\infty
, with a non-zero velocity approaching
(1/r_{\mathrm{c}}\mu)\sin(2\omega_{011}t-2\varphi)\approx(\mu r_{g}/2)\sin(2\omega_{011}t-2\varphi)\ll 1
. At
t=0
, using the same parameters of the blue curve in Fig.
7
, it gives
0.05
, only slightly smaller than the numerical result
0.0502
. More generally, this velocity can be approximated as
(\mu r_{g}/\bar{n})\sin(2\omega_{011}t-2\varphi)
, where
\bar{n}
is the principal number defined before Eq. (
13
).

With
r\gg r_{\mathrm{c}}
, the radial component of the velocity dominantes except close to the planes defined by
\omega_{011}t-\varphi=k\pi
or
(k+1/2)\pi
, where
k\in\mathbb{Z}
. This behavior is very difference from the dipole electric field lines, which always wind towards the negative charge no matter how far they are from the dipole. With the time
t
increases, these two planes rotate together with the field
\Phi
. The energy at
r\gg r_{\mathrm{c}}
flows inwards and outwards alternately along the radial direction with period
\pi/\omega_{011}
.

Finally, we discuss the velocity behavior as
z
varies. When
\mu z\gg 1
and
\mu\sqrt{x^{2}+y^{2}}\gg 1
, the velocity becomes

\displaystyle v^{x}

\displaystyle\approx\frac{\sin(2\omega_{011}t-\varphi)-\sin(\varphi)}{\mu\sqrt{x^{2}+y^{2}}},

(36a)

\displaystyle v^{y}

\displaystyle\approx-\frac{\cos(2\omega_{011}t-\varphi)-\cos(\varphi)}{\mu\sqrt{x^{2}+y^{2}}},

(36b)

\displaystyle v^{z}

\displaystyle\approx-\frac{\sin(2\omega_{011}t-2\varphi)}{\mu r_{\mathrm{c}}}.

(36c)

These asymptotic expressions indicate that the velocity is approximately a constant as
z
varies when
t
,
x
and
y
are fixed, which is consistent with Fig.
6

t=0
,
\sqrt{x^{2}+y^{2}}=200\,r_{g}
and
\varphi=\pi/32
in Fig.
6

|v|\approx 1.378\times 10^{-2}
while the asymptotic approximation gives
|v|\approx 1.383\times 10^{-2}
.

5
Summary and discussion

In this work, we performed a detailed investigation of the rotational properties of superradiant condensates around rotating BHs, focusing on real scalar fields without self-interactions or couplings to other fields. Although the phase propagation occurs only in the azimuthal direction, we demonstrated that the condensate does not possess a global rotation and the apparent rotation of the condensate is a collective effect arising from local motions in all three directions, by analyzing the spatial distribution and temporal evolution of the energy density and velocity.

We first briefly reviewed scalar superradiance and the BH-condensate system in Sec.
2
. Then, we discussed the normalization of the scalar field in Sec.
3
. We explicitly showed the squared distribution function
f_{lm}^{2}
in Eq. (
10
) is not equal to
M_{\mathrm{s}}/\omega_{nlm}
in curved spacetime when the scalar field satisfies the condition (
23
).

In Sec.
4
, we demonstrated that scalar field squared
\Phi^{2}
exhibits a dipole, while the energy density
\rho
of the condensate is approximately axisymmetric and peaks at the characteristic radius
r_{\mathrm{c}}\approx\bar{n}r_{\mathrm{b}}=\bar{n}/(r_{g}\mu^{2})
. To reveal the quadrupole structure of
\rho
, we defined the energy density difference
\delta\rho(t)\equiv\rho(t)-\rho_{\mathrm{ave}}
, where
\rho_{\mathrm{ave}}
denotes the time average of
\rho
over one period. The surfaces where
\delta\rho=0
divide the entire space into eight distinct inner and outer regions, as illustrated in Fig.
5
. Interestingly, the region where
\Phi^{2}
concentrates has negative values of
\delta\rho
.

Through detailed numerical and analytical studies, we found that the rotation of the condensate is actually a collective effect. In the region
r\lesssim r_{\mathrm{c}}
at
t=0
, the
velocity streamline distribution in the
y>0
region resembles the electric field lines of an electric dipole, with positive and negative charges located at
(r,\theta,\varphi)\approx(200\,r_{g},\pi/2,0)
and
(200\,r_{g},\pi/2,\pi)
, respectively. The velocity steamlines in the
y<0
region resemble the electric field lines of a reversed dipole. These two regions are glued at
y=0
plane, on which the energy flux vanishes. With the time
t
increases, the velocity streamlines and the hypothetical dipoles rotate with period
\pi/\omega_{011}
.

In the region
r\gg r_{\mathrm{c}}
, this analogy no longer holds. The radial velocity dominates throughout the region, except close to the planes defined by
\omega_{011}t-\varphi=k\pi
or
(k+1/2)\pi
, with
k\in\mathbb{Z}
. This behavior is markedly difference from the dipole electric field lines, which always wind towards the negative charge even at large distances. With the time
t
increases, these two planes rotate together with the filed
\Phi
. The energy at
r\gg r_{\mathrm{c}}
flows inwards and outwards alternately along the radial direction with period
\pi/\omega_{011}
.

These conclusions follow from the asymptotic forms of the velocity within the non-relativistic approximation. We showed that the magnitudes of the polar and azimuthal velocities decrease as
1/r
when
\mu r\sin\theta\gg 1
. The radial velocity exhibits a similar behavior when
1/\mu\ll r\ll r_{\mathrm{c}}
and approaches
(r_{g}\mu/\bar{n})\sin(2\omega t-2\varphi)
when
r\gg r_{\mathrm{c}}
. These asymptotic behaviors confirm that the motion remains non-relativistic at large distances and there is no superluminal motion. Additionally, the velocity as a function of
z
approaches a constant at large
z
with
t
,
x
, and
y
fixed.

Our findings offer a refined understanding of the rotation of superradiant condensates and clarify a common misconception regarding their global rotation. This work lays a foundation for further studies of more complex scenarios, such as condensates with couplings to the Standard Model particles.
