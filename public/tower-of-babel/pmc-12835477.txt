Evaluating the survival benefit of adjuvant chemotherapy based on tumor deposit status in stage II colon cancer patients from SEER database
Authors: Jiabin Ye, Zhifeng Lin, Rongrong Dai, Jianwen Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveColon cancer is a common malignancy with a high mortality rate. The staging of tumor deposits (TD) in colon cancer prognosis has been a subject of controversy. The aim of this study was to evaluate the impact of adjuvant chemotherapy (ACT) on overall survival (OS) and cancer-specific survival (CSS) in stage II colon cancer patients, with a specific focus on whether TD status modifies the treatment effect and to validate the AJCC 8th edition criteria for TD staging.MethodWe identified eligible patients with stage II colon cancer (N = 21,499) from the SEER database, covering the period from January 2000 to December 2018. We used subgroup analysis to explore the relationship between chemotherapy and tumor deposits, and a forest plot was drawn. A 1:1 Propensity Score Matching (PSM) was utilized to create a matched cohort with balanced baseline characteristics, reducing bias.ResultAfter PSM, ACT demonstrated significant OS benefits in both the overall population (HR, 0.87; P = 0.01) and the TD-free subgroup (HR, 0.88; P = 0.018), while the negative association with CSS was no longer significant (HR, 1.13; P = 0.079), indicating effective bias control. Interaction analyses confirmed statistically comparable treatment effects of ACT irrespective of TD status for both OS and CSS. Multivariate analysis identified ACT as an independent prognostic factor for improved OS, while tumor deposits were a significant independent adverse prognostic factor for both CSS and OS (all P < 0.001).ConclusionThis study confirms that tumor deposits is an independent and significant prognostic factor for colorectal cancer patients and provides some evidence for the accuracy of the AJCC 8th edition in classifying the presence of tumor deposits as N1c.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-025-04332-y.

Introduction

Colorectal cancer is a prevalent global malignancy with high mortality [1]. It ranks as the second leading cause of cancer-related deaths, accounting for approximately 881,000 deaths among 1.8 million new cases reported globally in 2018 [2–6]. In 2022, China recorded 408,000 new colorectal cancer cases, making it the second most commonly diagnosed cancer in the country. Despite advances in treatment, the 5-year survival rate of colorectal cancer remains below 60%, especially in patients with advanced disease [7, 8].

Standard therapeutic approaches for colorectal cancer include surgery, chemotherapy, and radiotherapy. For non-metastatic colorectal cancer, surgery remains the primary treatment; however, it often fails to achieve complete tumor resection, leading to recurrence risks that significantly impair patients’ quality of life [9]. Atul Batra et al. demonstrated that patients who received adjuvant chemotherapy (but did not meet trial eligibility criteria) had significantly better 5-year overall survival (OS) and cancer-specific survival (CSS) than those treated with surgery alone [10]. This finding supports the consideration of adjuvant chemotherapy in select patient populations. However, the decision to administer adjuvant chemotherapy requires comprehensive evaluation of factors such as age, general physical status, and comorbidities. Additionally, the full treatment course typically lasts 6 months. While adjuvant chemotherapy is widely accepted as the standard of care for stage III colorectal cancer, its efficacy in stage II disease remains controversial [11–13]. Current guidelines recommend adjuvant chemotherapy for stage II colorectal cancer patients with high-risk clinicopathological features [14]. Accurate determination of T and N stages is critical, as it guides the selection of appropriate chemotherapy regimens and ultimately influences patient outcomes [15].

Among key factors affecting TNM staging, tumor deposits (TD) in pericolonic and perirectal adipose tissue were first described in 1935 [16]. The definition of TD has evolved over time, and its prognostic significance remains debated. The 6th edition of the AJCC TNM staging system defines TD based on specific contouring criteria: TD with a firm, smooth contour are classified as positive lymph nodes (LN), while those with irregular contours are categorized under the primary tumor [17]. In the 7th edition, TD were designated as a distinct metastatic node subtype, termed pN1c [18]. The 8th edition retains TD as N1c but clarifies that TD do not affect the T stage of the primary tumor: if no regional lymph node metastasis is present, TD upgrades the N stage from N0 to N1c; if regional lymph node metastasis exists, TD do not count toward the total number of positive lymph nodes [19].

Cancer staging is critical for guiding accurate clinical decisions and providing prognostic information in cancer treatment. Staging of stage I–III colorectal cancer follows the TNM/AJCC system. Basnet S, Lou QF, and colleagues highlighted that TD status (presence vs. absence) is a key prognostic indicator for colorectal cancer [20]. Currently, radical surgery is the standard treatment for colorectal cancer, and adjuvant chemotherapy is known to reduce recurrence and mortality risks—particularly in high-risk stage II and all stage III patients [21]. However, the efficacy of adjuvant chemotherapy in high-risk stage II colorectal cancer remains controversial [22–24], and there is a paucity of research on the relationship between TD status and adjuvant chemotherapy response.

Consequently, this study aims to evaluate the survival benefit of ACT in stage II colorectal cancer, specifically to determine if the treatment effect is modified by TD status, by leveraging data from the large-scale Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) database. Our findings may provide insights into the utility of TD as predictive markers for chemotherapy and help validate the correctness of classifying TD as N1c in the AJCC 8th edition.

Materials and methods

Patient selection from SEER database

Data for this study were sourced from the National Cancer Institute’s SEER project, which functions as a robust repository of patient demographic details, cancer diagnoses, and outcomes from 18 cancer registries across the United States, encompassing approximately 28% of the U.S. population. The SEER database, devoid of any personal identifiers, is publicly accessible for epidemiology-based cancer studies.

In the present investigation, the SEER database was accessed using the National Cancer Institute’s SEER-stat software (version 8.3.5), through which eligible patients were meticulously chosen. We conducted a retrospective analysis of 21,499 patients diagnosed with stage II colon cancer between 2000 and 2018, using data obtained from the SEER database. Our primary objective was to assessed the effect of ACT on survival in stage II colon cancer and the modifying role of TD status. To achieve this, we retrospectively gathered variables readily available in the SEER database, including age, gender, primary tumor site, histological type, ethnicity, AJCC 6th edition TNM stage, T stage, N stage, M stage, tumor size, and the number of tumor deposits in colon cancer patients. Additionally, we collected data related to the treatment and survival of the included patients, such as primary site surgery, chemotherapy, OS and CSS.

We initially identified 516,760 patients diagnosed with colon cancer between 2000 and 2018 from the SEER database. To ensure a homogeneous study population and data quality, we applied sequential exclusion criteria: we first excluded patients with non-stage II colon cancer and non-adenocarcinoma histology (n = 328,080); then excluded those with missing data on key variables, including unknown race (n = 2,761), unknown surgery status (n = 471), or unknown tumor deposit (n = 146,985); we further excluded patients with unknown survival time or survival of less than one month (n = 15,308) to minimize immortal time bias; and finally excluded those with an unknown cause of death (n = 1,656). Following these exclusions, a total of 21,499 patients with stage II colon adenocarcinoma were included in the final analysis, as detailed in Fig. 1. In the SEER cohort, patients who received chemotherapy were categorized as the ACT group, while those for whom there was no evidence of chemotherapy in the medical records examined were classified as the non-ACT group.

Statistical analyses

We employed the Pearson chi-square test to compare various clinicopathological factors between the ACT and non-ACT groups. The primary endpoints of interest in our study were overall survival (OS) and cancer-specific survival (CSS). OS represents the duration from the randomization of patients in a clinical trial to death from any cause. CSS was calculated from the date of diagnosis to the date of death from colon cancer, with patients who succumbed to other causes being censored at their date of death.

The objective of this study was to examine whether a significant interaction exists between the presence or absence of tumor deposit and ACT. To achieve this, we conducted a subgroup analysis and plotted forest plots.

Since this study was retrospective, inherent disparities between patients based on the presence or absence of chemotherapy may introduce confounding biases. To mitigate the possibility of selection bias and adjust for significant clinicopathological covariates between the ACT and non-ACT groups, we implemented propensity score matching (PSM) using logistic regression modeling. We defined a logit that predicted the probability of receiving treatment as the propensity score, using variables such as sex, race, primary tumor location, tumor size, whether surgery was performed, age and T stage at diagnosis as matching criteria. Patients who received ACT were matched with patients who did not receive ACT within their respective risk groups, using nearest neighbor matching. The caliper for matching was set to 0.02. The matching process was conducted in a single, deterministic run and did not involve iterative optimization. The propensity score reflected the likelihood that a patient would or would not receive ACT based on their baseline characteristics.

Following PSM, we identified independent prognostic factors and hazard ratios (HR) for OS and CSS using univariate Cox proportional hazards models.

All statistical analyses were conducted using R (version 4.2.2). The R package MatchIt was employed for PSM. All statistical tests were two-sided, and a p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethics review

Data do not include use or personally identifiable information in human subjects, and informed consent is not required for this study.

Results

Baseline characteristics

Between 2000 and 2018, a total of 516,760 patients were diagnosed with colon cancer, out of which 21,499 had stage II colon cancer. The demographic and clinicopathological characteristics were analyzed, comprising 10,571 (49.2%) females and 10,928 (50.8%) males. Among these, 21,006 (97.7%) were in the group without tumor deposit, and 493 (2.30%) were in the group with tumor deposit. A total of 21,479 (99.9%) patients underwent surgery, while 20 (0.10%) did not. Additionally, 3,139 patients received adjuvant chemotherapy, whereas 18,360 patients did not. Table 1 presents the baseline demographic characteristics of the patients.

The results of Pearson’s chi-square test indicated that gender (P = 0.726) and whether surgery was performed (P = 0.06) were not associated with the receipt of chemotherapy and were not statistically significant. However, race, primary tumor site, tumor deposit level, tumor size, age, and T stage were significantly associated with the receipt of chemotherapy (P < 0.05, Table 1).

Subgroup survival analysis before and after matching

Before PSM, ACT did not significantly improve OS in the entire cohort (HR, 0.92; 95% CI, 0.85-1.00; P = 0.051); however, it demonstrated a significant survival benefit in the TD-free subgroup (HR, 0.91; 95% CI, 0.83–0.99; P = 0.03). In contrast, ACT was associated with worse CSS in the pre-PSM analysis (HR, 1.18; 95% CI, 1.07–1.32; P = 0.002), suggesting substantial confounding bias due to imbalanced baseline characteristics. After PSM was applied to balance these baseline features, ACT showed significant OS benefits both in the overall population (HR, 0.87; 95% CI, 0.79–0.97; P = 0.01) and in the TD-free subgroup (HR, 0.88; 95% CI, 0.79–0.98; P = 0.018). Meanwhile, the previously observed negative association between ACT and CSS disappeared, with the HR approaching 1 (HR, 1.13; 95% CI, 0.99–1.29; P = 0.079) and losing statistical significance, indicating that the bias was effectively controlled. Importantly, the interaction P-values were non-significant across all analyses, consistently demonstrating that the treatment effects of ACT, as quantified by the HRs, were statistically comparable regardless of TD status for both OS and CSS. Supplementary Figs. 1–4 present Kaplan-Meier curves demonstrating that patients with TD consistently had worse OS and CSS both before and after PSM (Fig. 3).

COX regression analysis after propensity score matching

In the univariate regression analysis, race, surgery status, age, T stage, tumor deposit, and receipt of ACT emerged as significant prognostic factors for CSS. Similarly, sex, race, tumour size, surgery status, age, T stage, tumor deposit, and receipt of ACT were found to be significant prognostic factors for OS (Table 3). Notably, in patients with stage II colorectal cancer, ACT was not significantly associated with CSS (HR = 1.130, 95% CI: 0.986–1.290, P = 0.079); however, it was associated with a significant improvement in OS (HR = 0.872, 95% CI: 0.786–0.968, P = 0.010). Furthermore, the presence of tumor deposits was a significant adverse prognostic factor, associated with worse CSS (HR = 2.200, 95% CI: 1.720–2.830, P < 0.001) and OS (HR = 1.880, 95% CI: 1.530–2.320, P < 0.001).

Discussion

Our findings confirms that tumor deposits is an independent and significant prognostic factor for colorectal cancer patients and provides some evidence for the accuracy of the AJCC 8th edition in classifying the presence of tumor deposits as N1c.

Main results

In this study, we primarily employed the staging system from the sixth edition of the AJCC, which still categorizes irregular TD under the T stage classification. Among our cohort of stage II colon cancer patients, we observed a 2.3% incidence of TD, with only 14.6% of patients opting for adjuvant chemotherapy. These statistics suggest that the occurrence of irregular TD and the adoption of adjuvant chemotherapy are relatively low among stage II colon cancer patients. This phenomenon may be attributed to the fact that the primary treatment approach for stage II colon cancer is complete surgical resection, which generally offers a favorable prognosis. However, recent studies have reported recurrence rates as high as 29% in some stage II patients [25–27]. Consequently, the identification of risk factors for stage II colon cancer and the provision of adjuvant therapy have become high-priority concerns. Some of these risk factors encompass perforation or obstruction, histopathological T4 tumors, inadequate lymph node sampling, vascular or perineural invasion, and poorly differentiated histology [28, 29].

Our study delves into the impact of tumor deposit status on the efficacy of adjuvant chemotherapy through detailed subgroup analyses and interaction tests. The results from the forest plots and interaction P-values constitute the core finding: the survival benefit (or risk) associated with adjuvant chemotherapy in stage II colon cancer patients is statistically similar, regardless of TD status. This indicates that TD primarily functions as a strong prognostic factor, identifying a patient subgroup with a poorer prognosis, but it does not serve as a predictive biomarker for determining which patients are more or less likely to benefit from ACT.

Specifically, in the matched cohort, adjuvant chemotherapy provided a significant OS benefit in both the overall population and the TD-free subgroup, but no significant CSS benefit was observed in either subgroup. Most crucially, the interaction between TD status and ACT was non-significant for all endpoints (both OS and CSS). This provides a more nuanced perspective compared to studies that focus solely on the prognostic value of TD or generally classify TD as a high-risk feature warranting chemotherapy. Our data suggest that while TD-positive patients have a higher absolute risk of death, the relative treatment effect of ACT in them is comparable to that in TD-negative patients. This implies that clinical decision-making should not presume that the presence of TD necessarily confers an extra relative benefit from ACT beyond that seen in other patients; the decision must still involve comprehensive consideration.

This finding has important implications for understanding the AJCC 8th edition staging update. By classifying TD in the absence of lymph node metastasis as N1c, this edition effectively “upstages” these patients based on the strong prognostic effect of TD. Our subgroup analysis results indirectly support the rationale for this classification, as they confirm that TD indeed identifies a unique high-risk subgroup within stage II. However, our data do not indicate that this subgroup responds fundamentally differently to ACT compared to other stage II patients. Future research could explore whether the optimal adjuvant therapy for this subgroup, which is now classified as stage III (N1c) according to AJCC 8th, should be identical to that for traditional stage III patients with lymph node metastases.

In our study, we employed PSM to ensure the consistency of background data between the populations of stage II colon cancer patients with and without chemotherapy. Prior research has indicated that age can impact the likelihood of patients receiving adjuvant chemotherapy [30]. Generally, younger patients tend to have a higher tolerance for adjuvant chemotherapy, while older patients may be less inclined to tolerate the treatment due to the presence of multiple chronic diseases, reduced physiological function, or diminished drug tolerance. Additionally, studies have suggested that patients of different racial backgrounds may make distinct treatment choices for colon cancer. Potosky AL’s study revealed that individuals of African descent diagnosed with colon cancer were generally less likely than their Caucasian counterparts to receive adjuvant chemotherapy [31]. This discrepancy may be attributed to genetic, environmental, and lifestyle factors. Furthermore, Price TJ et al.‘s research in colon cancer indicated that patients with right-sided primary tumors exhibited a greater number of adverse prognostic factors and a poorer prognosis when compared to those with left-sided primary tumors [32]. Therefore, factors such as age, race, and tumor location can significantly influence the assessment of the effectiveness of adjuvant chemotherapy. PSM allows us to mitigate the influence of these potential confounding factors.

In our study, univariate Cox regression analyses within the matched cohort confirmed the prognostic significance of several established factors for survival outcomes. Advancing age was consistently associated with worse OS and CSS, aligning with the understanding that increasing age often brings a higher burden of comorbidities and may influence treatment tolerance and outcomes. While some evidence suggests comparable efficacy and toxicity of certain adjuvant regimens (e.g., 5-FU/LV or capecitabine) between older and younger populations [33], the decision for ACT in elderly patients must remain highly individualized.

Crucially, our subgroup and interaction analyses revealed that the association between ACT and survival was not significantly modified by TD status for either OS or CSS. This key finding indicates that while TD is a powerful independent adverse prognostic factor, it does not appear to predict a differential magnitude of benefit from ACT in stage II colon cancer. Specifically, regarding CSS, ACT did not demonstrate a significant survival benefit in the overall matched cohort. This aligns with the mixed evidence in the literature, where some large observational studies [34, 35] failed to show a significant CSS benefit for ACT in stage II disease, including high-risk groups, while others, like the study by Casadaban et al. [36], reported improved survival. The nuanced interpretation of our data, in the context of existing evidence, underscores the complexity of ACT decision-making for stage II colon cancer, which must weigh absolute risk, potential benefit, and patient preferences, rather than relying on a single high-risk feature.

The strong independent prognostic value of TD reinforced by our analysis provides empirical support for its significant role in cancer staging. The evolution of its definition in the AJCC staging system, culminating in its classification as N1c (stage III disease) in the absence of lymph node metastasis in the 8th edition, is a critical development. This reclassification fundamentally alters the treatment paradigm for these patients, as adjuvant chemotherapy is a well-established standard for stage III colon cancer. Studies like those from the IDEA collaboration highlight the importance of precise sub-staging for optimizing adjuvant therapy. Therefore, the accurate identification and reporting of TD are paramount, as they directly impact staging, which in turn guides treatment selection and influences patient outcomes [15]. Our findings, which robustly confirm the adverse prognosis associated with TD, thereby lend indirect support to the clinical rationale behind the AJCC 8th edition’s reclassification, as it effectively upstages a group of patients with a demonstrably higher risk of mortality.

In the TD-positive subgroup of our matched cohort, no statistically significant difference in CSS was observed between patients who received ACT and those who did not. This finding indicate that the effect of ACT on CSS was not significantly modified by TD status. Therefore, while TD is confirmed as a strong adverse prognostic factor, our study does not provide evidence that it predicts a specific CSS benefit from ACT in stage II colon cancer. This distinction between prognostic and predictive effects is critical for clinical interpretation. Prior studies have consistently reported an association between TD and unfavorable prognosis in colorectal cancer [37–40], which our results strongly support. However, translating this prognostic risk into a demonstrated survival benefit from standard ACT in stage II disease requires further prospective validation.

Advantages

To the best of our knowledge, there have been limited studies assessing the prognostic and predictive significance of TD compared to adjuvant chemotherapy in colon cancer. In our investigation, we leveraged the SEER database to explore the impact of TD in the context of adjuvant chemotherapy for individuals with Stage II colon cancer. The extensive dataset and its robust representation of the U.S. patient population enhanced the statistical credibility and applicability of our findings. Furthermore, we employed PSM to mitigate selection bias, thereby strengthening the validity of our conclusions, which in turn provide insights into the application of ACT in Stage II colon cancer.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. Firstly, it is a retrospective study, which inherently lacks the prospective nature.Secondly, to ensure a cohort with definitive TD status for our analysis, we excluded cases with missing TD data. While this was necessary to address our primary research question, it may have introduced selection bias, as the population with complete data might not be fully representative of the broader stage II colon cancer population. The impact of this potential bias on our results cannot be ruled out. Thirdly, as a common limitation of studies utilizing the SEER database, crucial clinical information such as patients’ comorbidities is not available. The SEER database does not provide the means to distinguish between different types of chemotherapy, including preoperative and postoperative CT. However, considering that preoperative CT is not a standard treatment for stage IIA disease, it would be appropriate to label patients who received chemotherapy simply as having an ‘ACT’ receipt in the ‘chemotherapy recode’ variable. Furthermore, the SEER database did not provide detailed information on specific chemotherapy regimens (e.g., FOLFOX, CapeOX), drug types, or number of treatment cycles, which made it impossible for us to assess the impact of treatment regimen heterogeneity during the study period on CSS and OS. Lastly, it is important to note that the SEER database offers information solely on OS and CSS, without encompassing data on disease progression or tumor-free survival. This limitation could potentially introduce bias in the analysis of oncology patients. While PSM was implemented in this study to mitigate discrepancies in patients’ baseline characteristics, it is worth acknowledging that certain parameters remained unbalanced due to the complex interplay of various factors. Hence, our conclusions still need to be validated in other cohorts, especially in large RCTs. It is crucial to reiterate that while our results provide robust empirical support for the AJCC 8th edition’s classification of TD as N1c, this observational evidence cannot directly establish its validity due to the constraints outlined above.

Conclusion

This large-scale, propensity score-matched analysis demonstrates that tumor deposits are a significant adverse prognostic factor in stage II colon cancer, associated with worse CSS and OS. Importantly, the presence of tumor deposits was associated with a markedly poorer prognosis, providing some evidence for the AJCC 8th edition’s classification of TD as N1c. Though further prospective studies are warranted to confirm these observational results.
