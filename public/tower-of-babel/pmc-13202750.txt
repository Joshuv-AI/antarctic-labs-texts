Improving the transparency of animal experiments: valuable lessons learned from a systematic analysis of registration protocols
Authors: Shaowei Yi, Jianhua Ruan, Zhe Wang, Fengxing Ding, Yanli Liu, Qi Ao, Ziyu Ren, Caizhi Wu, Yating Luo, Muting Niu, Yue Zhao, Youhu Wang, Bin Ma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo comprehensively analyze the epidemiological characteristics of research protocols on international animal experiment registration platforms, explore potential factors influencing protocol publication, and offer recommendations to improve animal experiment registration systems.MethodsResearch protocols registered on the Netherlands’ PCT(Preclinical Trials) and Germany’s ASR(Animal Study Registry) animal experiment registration platforms up to December 30, 2024, were included. Relevant data were extracted, and frequency distributions and percentages were calculated to statistically describe the findings. Factors influencing publication rates were identified using chi-square tests and multivariate binary logistic regression analysis.ResultsA total of 272 registered protocols were analyzed, including 124 from the PCT platform and 148 from the ASR platform, originating from 31 countries, spanning 47 research areas, and involving 11 different animal species. Among the studies, 10.0% (27/272) did not report randomization, and none of the randomized studies (132/272) addressed allocation concealment. Additionally, 24.6% (67/272) and 35.3% (96/272) failed to report blinding during intervention implementation and outcome measurement, respectively. Only 43.3% (97/224) of the experiments were completed within the specified timeframe, and among these, merely 23.7% (23/97) achieved full publication. Animal species were identified as potential influencing factors. Studies using pigs as animal models were more frequently published.ConclusionAs a vital measure to enhance the quality of animal experiments, the registration of animal protocols requires joint emphasis and coordinated promotion by multiple stakeholders, including researchers, research institutions, ethics committees, journal editors, funding agencies, and regulatory authorities. Strengthening protocol registration will not only improve transparency, reproducibility, and ethical oversight, but also build a more trustworthy foundation for high-quality evidence and accelerate the clinical translation of animal research.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12917-026-05468-x.

Introduction

Animal experimentation is a cornerstone of biomedical research, yet its clinical translation rate remains low due to ongoing concerns regarding validity and reproducibility [1]. The limited clinical translation of animal experiment results may stem from various factors, including suboptimal animal model selection, flawed experimental design, improper statistical analysis, and selective data publication [2]. One critical factor is publication bias, in which positive or favorable results are more likely to be published, while negative or null findings are often disregarded [3]. This bias undermines the credibility and validity of animal experiments, leading to unnecessary use of animals and heightened ethical risks [4]. The publication bias not only leads to a decrease in the authenticity and credibility of animal experiments, but also causes waste of experimental animals and increases ethical risks.

In 2004, the World Health Organization (WHO) mandated the registration of all clinical trials to mitigate the impact of publication bias [5]. Since then, 39 clinical trial registration platforms, including 17 primary-level platforms, have been established worldwide to support trial registration [6]. Over the past 20 years, researchers in many countries have monitored and analyzed registration practices across various clinical specialties, contributing to continuous improvements in both the quantity and quality of registered trials. Compared with clinical trials, publication bias exerts a more pronounced and severe impact on animal experiments [1]. However, it was not until 2014 that some scholars began to advocate the establishment of animal experiment pre-registration (AEP) and related platforms to cope with the harm of publication bias on the results of animal experiments [7]. The PCT in the Netherlands and the ASR in Germany are the main global platforms for animal experiment registration, aiming to enhance transparency and traceability, improve quality and efficiency, reduce unnecessary repetition, uphold ethical standards, and promote social responsibility in animal research [7]. Examining the registration characteristics of these platforms helps identify existing issues, facilitating the continuous improvement of the registration mechanism. However, despite detailed descriptions of these platforms and their registration processes [8], no systematic analysis of registered animal experiment protocols has been conducted, leaving the limitations of current international registration platforms unclear.

Pre-registration systems for protocols, whether for clinical trials or animal experiments, aim to reduce selective reporting and promote full transparency. “As previously noted, despite mandatory clinical trial registration, the rate of full-text publication of protocols remains unsatisfactory. For example, data from 2009 showed that only 46% (311/677) of registered trials on the U.S. platform were eventually published in full-text journals, while the proportion in China was even lower, at 35% (156/443) [9, 10]. There is currently no global mandatory policy for animal experiment protocol registration. Nevertheless, the two main pre-registration platforms, PCT and ASR, have received applications from scholars in over 40 countries (PCT: 23; ASR: 17), and the number of registrations continues to grow [8]. Despite this growth, no study has systematically examined how potential influencing factors in registered animal experiment protocols affect publication outcomes.

Therefore, this study aims to analyze in detail the characteristics of research protocols registered on the International Platform for Registration of Experiments on Animals, and to provide animal researchers with comprehensive reference materials to improve protocol registration. The study will also offer recommendations for the development and enhancement of animal experiment registration platforms. In addition, it examines potential factors affecting protocol publication, offering guidance for researchers and contributing to greater transparency in reporting the full process of animal experiments.

Methodology

Data extraction

Animal experiment protocols from the Dutch PCT (Preclinical Trials, https://preclinicaltrials.eu/database) and German ASR (Animal Study Registry, https://www.animalstudyregistry.org/) platforms were included, irrespective of study type, covering the period from each platform’s establishment to December 30, 2024. Two trained researchers (YSW, RJH) independently extracted and recorded relevant information from the protocols, with any discrepancies resolved by a third expert (MB). Data were then compiled for analysis. The PCT platform used the web printing function of EDGE browser to export the registration protocols as PDF, and the ASR platform downloaded it directly from the platform.

Full-text data were extracted using Microsoft Excel 2019 and included the following categories: (1) Basic protocol information: registration platform, study start date, expected completion date, embargo status and lifting date, study title, authors, affiliations, countries, and funding types; (2) Experimental basis: type of intervention, research field, animal species, strain, sex and sample size calculation method; (3) Experimental methods: randomization and blinding procedures (for intervention and assessment); (4) Protocol completion: stage of experimental conduct and publication status. All extracted data were cross-checked across different fields within the same record to ensure internal consistency and completeness. After verification, unavailable information was classified into two categories:

“NR” (not reported): used for items where information was not provided in the registration record or could not be determined after cross-checking. “Embargo”: used for items explicitly labeled as embargoed by the registration platform, indicating that information was temporarily withheld under the platform’s confidentiality protection mechanism.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using Microsoft Excel 2019 and SPSS version 27.0 (IBM Corp, Armonk, NY, USA). Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the characteristics of registered animal study protocols. All variables were categorical and were presented as frequencies and percentages. Due to the platform’s embargo mechanism, the final tally reports separately the portions of each component affected by the embargo [8].

Associations between protocol publication status and potential influencing factors—including registration platform, funding type, intervention type, animal species, randomization, and blinding—were examined using univariable analysis. Both Pearson’s chi-square test and Fisher’s exact test were used, depending on whether the expected cell-count assumptions were met [11, 12]. Not reported and embargoed entries were treated as missing and excluded from hypothesis testing. Therefore, the valid sample size varied across factors. Variables of theoretical or observed relevance were subsequently included in a multivariate binary logistic regression model to identify independent predictors of protocol publication. Multivariable logistic regression was planned only if the number of publication events was adequate for reliable estimation [13, 14]. If this criterion was not met, we conducted univariable analysis to further examine associations among candidate factors. The goodness-of-fit of the logistic regression model was evaluated using the Hosmer–Lemeshow test. A non-significant result (P > 0.05) indicates an adequate model fit between predicted and observed outcomes. Both univariable analysis and logistic regression analysis used a significance threshold of P < 0.05. As a sensitivity analysis, we repeated the primary analysis after excluding embargoed records to assess the robustness of the results to restricted data access. The 272 registered protocols covered 47 research areas. The top three areas were pediatrics (7.4%, 20/272), neuroscience (6.6%, 18/272), and cardiology (5.1%, 14/272). Characteristics related to the implementation of animal experimentation programs

Randomization was not specified in 9.9% (27/272) of studies, which reported group allocation without indicating whether it was randomized. Among the studies that did report randomization, none described concealed allocation, except those in the “Embargo” category. Blinding was not reported in 24.6% (67/272) of trials at the intervention stage and in 35.3% (96/272) at the outcome assessment stage. Sample size determination was not described in 13.2% (36/272) of studies. In terms of funding, 11.4% (31/272) of studies were supported by non-public sources. Details are shown in Fig. 4.

Registered plan completion and outcome indicator reporting characteristics

Of the 272 included protocols, 48 correspond to experiments that are still within the expected completion time (cutoff January 2024). Of the remaining 224 protocols that exceeded the expected completion time, only 43.3% (97/224) were completed on schedule.

Among these, only 23.7% (23/97) were published in full text. Notably, of the 23 published studies, 56.5% (13/23) reported inconsistencies between the number of outcome indicators in the full text and those pre-specified in the plan. None of these studies provided explanations for these discrepancies.

Discussion

The promotion and adoption of animal experiment registration platforms require significant enhancement

A total of 272 animal experiment protocols were included in this study. This represents a significantly smaller number compared to protocols registered on clinical trial platforms [8]. The distribution of registrations by country shows that the number of registrations in the countries where the platform was established (the Netherlands and Germany) is higher than in other countries. Several factors may explain these discrepancies.

First of all, the establishment of AEP platforms is relatively recent. Currently, only two specialized platforms exist, both established after 2014 [7, 17]. In contrast, clinical trial pre-registration platforms have been in operation longer, and there are currently several international platforms, such as the World Health Organization’s ICTRP and the U.S. ClinicalTrials.gov platforms, and all of these platforms have been in operation since before 2000 [18]. Consequently, the visibility and accessibility of these platforms remain limited, contributing to the low registration numbers.

Secondly, the purpose and importance of AEP platforms may not yet be widely recognized or supported, particularly in some developing countries and regions. The main purposes of AEP platforms are to improve the credibility and replicability of animal studies, and to reduce the risk of selective reporting and data manipulation. In addition, pre-registration helps to avoid unnecessary animal use, enhance animal welfare and ethical standards, and promote knowledge sharing and innovation [7]. However, some researchers may believe that AEP will increase their workload, restrict their research freedom, prematurely disclose their research concepts and methodologies, or affect the publication and application of their research results [19]. Therefore, promoting AEP platforms requires stronger advocacy and education efforts, as well as clear communication of embargo policies to address researchers’ concerns and enhance their awareness and participation.

Finally, countries that have established AEP platforms may have stronger motivation and better conditions to promote and support their further implementation and development. The Netherlands and Germany are both developed countries in Europe with better research systems and infrastructures, stricter regulations and standards for animal experiments [20, 21]. Researchers in these countries may be more receptive to the requirements and processes of AEP, while benefiting from stronger technical and resource support.

Policies, journals, funding agencies, academic organizations, and other parties in these countries may also be more inclined to encourage and reward pre-registration initiatives for animal experiments. This tendency may be further reinforced by supportive national policies or mandates, funding schemes that incentivize pre-registration, and a culture that emphasizes transparency and reproducibility. Adequate research funding and flexible use of resources not only ensure the availability of essential experimental equipment and materials but also support the development and training of research teams, thereby facilitating the uptake and sustained implementation of pre-registration. In addition, growing public concern for animal welfare and ethical accountability has also played an important role in promoting openness and encouraging policymakers and institutions to strengthen registration systems. Active promotion and training efforts, together with the concentration of well-established research centres, may collectively contribute to higher rates of pre-registration and more effective implementation. Establishing additional pre-registration platforms across more countries and regions is essential to improve the accessibility and usability of such systems. In parallel, greater efforts should be made to strengthen publicity and training, thereby enhancing researchers’ awareness and participation in pre-registration activities.

Standardizing methodological design and ensuring the transparency of platform information are essential for improving registration quality

This study reveals that the transparency of methodological details in animal experimentation protocols registered on the International Platform for Animal Experimentation remains inadequate, particularly concerning randomization and blinding.

The science of methodological design is critical to ensure the internal validity of preclinical animal models and experiments, and internal validity is critical for translation [22, 23]. A growing number of studies have shown that random assignment, concealed grouping, and blinding are equally important measures to reduce the risk of bias inherent in an interventional animal experiment [24]. However, Bello et al. [20] showed that in animal experiments with subjective endpoints (e.g., scales or histologically assessed outcomes), the lack of blinding of outcome assessors led to substantial bias. Specifically, intervention effects were, on average, overstated by 59%. Tight control of the various types of risk of bias that may arise during the design and implementation phases of animal experiments will help to reduce the risk of translating the results of animal experimental studies to clinical applications [25]. Therefore, it is recommended that researchers focus on strengthening the design and reporting of randomization and blinding, which are core research animal characteristics elements. Emphasizing these aspects can improve the accuracy and transparency of animal experiment protocol registration.

It is recommended that the registration platform strengthen the supervision and audit of AEP to improve the scientific and standardized registration protocol. At the same time, it is recommended that more uniform and standardized standards and guidelines for the AEP be developed to enhance the quality and consistency of pre-registration. Given that the auditing process may involve additional resource requirements, multiple avenues of funding support can be explored in order to balance the resource requirements with the effectiveness of auditing. For example, apart from recruiting public funding, consideration can also be given to collaborating with relevant industry organizations or applying for special research funding. In addition, the establishment of a diversified funding support mechanism can also help enhance the sustainability and flexibility of the program to ensure the smooth running and continuous improvement of the process. It should also formulate more uniform and standardized standards and guidelines for the AEP, so as to improve the quality and consistency of the AEP.

Embargo policies should balance the need for information transparency and knowledge protection trade-offs

The embargo policy of the AEP Platform aims to secure the integrity of registration information, prevent misuse or leakage, and safeguard researchers’ intellectual property and competitive advantages. Typically, embargo rules impose a restricted period following submission, during which key registration details—such as objectives, hypotheses, designs, methods, and analysis plans—remain inaccessible to the public [7, 8, 17, 18]. Although these rules are reasonable, they may also affect the transparency of animal experiments. This is particularly true for studies in which experimental protocols are modified during implementation or lack rigorous design, making it difficult for the process to be accurately and fully reported. The results of the present study confirm that some information in animal experiment protocols is subject to embargo restrictions. Except for protocols from the author’s country, part of the registered information is not publicly accessible, and the extent of embargoed content varies across countries. Transparency in animal experimentation entails timely and comprehensive access to relevant information and its evaluation by stakeholders—including researchers, reviewers, and policymakers. Such transparency helps identify and address deficiencies, enhance research quality, minimize unnecessary animal use, and promote ethical standards and knowledge sharing [26]. Transparency in animal experimentation is closely related to the credibility and replicability of research and represents one of the main purpose of pre-registration platforms. Overly stringent or prolonged embargo policies may compromise the transparency of animal experiments, diminishing their value and significance.

Therefore, it is suggested that registration platforms, when formulating embargo rules, balance the need for transparency of registration information with the protection of researchers’ interests. In addition, appropriate flexibility and convenience should be provided—for example, allowing partial access to certain research information upon justified request, while tracking access to the viewed content. The benefits include: (1) improving traceability by enabling the tracking and recording of processes and outcomes, thereby enhancing credibility and replicability; (2) fostering comparability with similar experiments, allowing researchers to identify and adopt best practices; and (3) increasing utility by providing valuable data and evidence for related studies, thereby advancing knowledge sharing and innovation.

The embargo rules of AEP platforms should balance the need for information transparency with the protection of intellectual content. These rules should be formulated according to the specific characteristics of animal studies and the reasonable demands of stakeholders, in order to maximize their scientific validity.

Applying the ARRIVE guidelines to minimize publication bias

Pre-registration is widely acknowledged as an effective strategy to mitigate publication bias [27], as it helps reduce selective reporting by prepublishing expected trial outcomes. However, discrepancies between published studies and their registration protocols are common and often lack adequate explanation. Our findings revealed that only a small fraction of studies registered were fully completed and published. Furthermore, the endpoints reported in published articles often deviated from those predefined in the protocols.

Kaiyan Hu et al. [28]’s study showed that non-Cochrane systematic reviews (SRs) and their protocols registered on the largest registry platform, PROSPERO, often differed from the outcomes of subsequent studies. However, these discrepancies were rarely explained, which is consistent with our findings.

In response to this discrepancy, the PRISMA 2020 Guidelines added a note about inconsistencies between registered protocols and published studies. However, simple requirements have not significantly ameliorated this problem, despite the requirements of PCT platforms. The ARRIVE guidelines (Animal Research: Reporting of In Vivo Experiments) are internationally recognized standards for reporting animal research and have been included in the EQUATOR Network. An updated version, ARRIVE 2019, was subsequently released, comprising 10 core items (the ARRIVE Essential 10) and 11 additional recommended items (the Recommended Set) [29, 30]. The ARRIVE Essential 10 comprise the items that are mandatory for reporting in animal studies, whereas the Recommended Set includes items that are encouraged to be reported. The aim is to ensure complete reporting of the Essential 10 and to progressively integrate the Recommended Set until comprehensive and routine reporting is achieved.

Although the revised version includes inconsistencies between registered protocols and published studies as a recommended item, evidence shows that reporting in this area remains inadequate [16]. Whether a single study adequately reports this entry may have little impact on its quality, but when multiple studies are combined to form a body of evidence, it can create a large bias in the translation of evidence. For example, a study by Quentin Beytout et al. [31] showed that published clinical randomized controlled trials (RCTs) in psoriasis reported significantly fewer adverse events and deaths than those listed on the U.S. Clinical Trials Registry platform. Such discrepancies may introduce considerable bias when translating research evidence into clinical practice.

In addition, despite the wide recognition and endorsement of the ARRIVE guidelines by major journals, their actual implementation in animal research remains suboptimal. Recent evaluations—for instance, a study analyzing animal studies on peritoneal dialysis conducted between 2021 and 2023 after the introduction of the ARRIVE updated guidelines—revealed that adherence to ARRIVE items was still inconsistent and frequently incomplete [32].

To minimize translational bias, we propose elevating this entry from the recommended to the core category in the ARRIVE guidelines. It is also recommended that journals prioritize articles that provide information about the protocol when reviewing manuscripts. A concerted effort between journals and researchers is needed to minimize publication bias.

Potential impact factors (Animal Species)

To validate our hypotheses regarding influencing factors, we employed statistical methods to investigate potential determinants of protocol publication. The results suggest that the animal species used in the study may be potential factors influencing the publication of the protocol.

The choice of experimental animal model also affects the publication of research results. While mice are widely used as experimental models, pigs are preferred in certain fields due to their physiological and anatomical similarities to humans, offering more clinically relevant insights [33]. Utilizing large animal models like pigs can potentially reduce the overall number of animals required while increasing the likelihood of meeting publication standards [34]. At the same time, in recent years, as concerns about animal ethics and welfare have increased, researchers must adhere to strict ethical and animal welfare standards when designing and performing experiments [35]. Good animal welfare practices are not only an ethical responsibility, but also improve the quality and reliability of experimental data [36].

In conclusion, animal welfare and research funding are critical considerations in the scientific research process. Good animal welfare improves the ethics of research and the quality of data, while industry funding presents unique advantages and challenges in advancing applied research. Researchers need to maintain a balance between these aspects to ensure the smooth conduct of research and the successful publication of results.

This study has certain limitations. The limited data available on the AEP platform may not accurately represent global practices. Additionally, embargo settings on both platforms restrict data access, potentially leading to discrepancies between statistical findings and actual conditions. Moreover, the small sample size, the low number of publication events, and the limited amount of missing data precluded reliable multivariable regression modelling; therefore, our analysis were restricted to univariable comparisons. This also underscores the need for broader uptake of pre-registration and more complete reporting to enable more robust, adjusted analysis in future research. Finally, for “published,” our study focused on peer-reviewed journal articles and did not capture grey literature (e.g., preprints, institutional or funder reports, dissertations, and other non-journal outputs), which may lead to an underestimation of dissemination beyond traditional journals.

Conclusion

In summary, registration of animal experiments should be a necessary step for all studies, rather than an optional or arbitrary process. Achieving this goal requires the collaboration of multiple stakeholders: government funding agencies to provide flexible financial support, journal editors to enforce stringent registration requirements, registration platforms to enhance their mechanisms, and researchers to increase awareness and improve protocol quality.

This study hopes to offer valuable recommendations and insights on animal experiment registration, fostering effective collaboration between registration platforms and researchers to enhance transparency and experimental quality. This effort will lay a more robust and practical foundation for the clinical translation of animal experiments.
