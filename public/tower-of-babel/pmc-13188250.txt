Endometrial cancer mortality and its trends in China from 2004-2021
Authors: Yajuan Zhong, Haoxin Ouyang, Qi Rao, Yilin Wang, Qixiang Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEndometrial carcinoma (EC) is a significant cause of mortality among women globally. The mortality patterns and temporal trends of EC in China warrant close attention and intensive study.MethodsMortality data of EC from 2004 to 2021 were obtained from the Chinese Death Cause Monitoring Results Datasets, which provide systematically stratified mortality information for the Chinese population, including death counts, rates, and cause-specific compositions by region, urban-rural classification, age, and sex. Cases were stratified into urban and rural categories based on standardized classification criteria, and temporal trends in EC mortality were analyzed using Joinpoint regression.ResultsFrom 2004 to 2021, a mortality of 2.63/105 population from EC were recorded in China. Both the crude mortality rate (CMR) and the age-standardized mortality rate (ASMR) exhibited an initial decline followed by a slight increase. The overall average annual percentage change (AAPC) in ASMR was − 5.44%. Specifically, the ASMR declined significantly before 2015, with the maximum annual percent changes (APCs) of -20.59% occurring from 2012 to 2015, and then it increased slightly. However, differences were observed between the trends of urban and rural ASMR. The urban ASMR declined significantly at a sustained rate (AAPC/APC = -5.47%), whereas the rural ASMR exhibited three distinct stages: APCs of -3.37% from 2004 to 2012, -24.07% from 2012 to 2015, and 1.32% after 2015. Overall, the rural ASMR showed a greater decline (AAPC = -5.83%) compared to urban areas.ConclusionsFrom 2004 to 2021, the mortality of EC in China initially declined and then increased. Despite the rural ASMR declined more pronounced than urban ASMR, its mortality remained consistently higher. These findings provide evidence to support health resource prioritization in EC prevention and clinical management.Keywords: Endometrial carcinoma, Global burden of disease, Mortality rate, Joinpoint regression model

Introduction

Endometrial carcinoma (EC) is a malignant epithelial tumor of the endometrium, with postmenopausal bleeding as its hallmark presentation [1, 2]. Advanced disease frequently proves fatal due to local invasion, distant metastasis, and severe complications [3]. Tumor progression may involve adjacent structures and distant organs [4], accompanied by refractory hemorrhage, severe infection and cachexia [5, 6], ultimately culminating in multiple organ failure. Additionally, cardiovascular disease represents a significant competing cause of death [7].

EC is the second most prevalent gynecological cancer in China [8] and the most common in the US [9]. Previous global burden of disease studies have demonstrated that the disease burden of EC remains high in China. In 2020, the age-standardized incidence rate of EC in China reached 5.3/105, while the ASMR was approximately 1.4 per 100,000 [10]. In 2021, uterine cancer accounted for 4.055 × 10⁵ disability-adjusted life years (DALYs), with an age-standardized DALY rate of 37.86 per 100,000 [11]. Notably, “uterine cancer” in the Global Burden of Disease Study predominantly denotes EC (> 95% of uterine malignancies) [12]. In recent years, EC incidence has risen [13, 14], driven largely by increasing obesity and unhealthy lifestyle habits [15–18]. However, EC mortality trends in China remain debated. Although declining mortality has been reported [19, 20], attributed to advances in molecular research [21], oral contraceptive use [22], and improved treatment modalities [23, 24], conflicting findings exist: stable trends attributed to enhanced detection and management [25], and increasing trends due to population aging [26, 27]. Factors such as progesterone therapy may contribute to these divergent results [28]. These discrepancies likely reflect heterogeneity in study periods, data sources, methods, and population stratification. Given these inconsistencies and the paucity of recent national analyses, this study examined EC mortality trends in China from 2004 to 2021 to inform targeted prevention strategies.

Materials and methods

Data sources

Mortality data on patients with endometrial carcinoma (ICD-10: C54) from 2004 to 2021 was collected based on the “Chinese Death Cause Monitoring Results Datasets” published by the National Center for Chronic and Noncommunicable Disease Control and Prevention (NCNCD) of the Chinese Center for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) and the National Health Commission Statistical Information Center [29, 30].

The National Death Surveillance System employs a multi-stage stratified cluster random sampling method to select representative surveillance points across 31 provinces, autonomous regions, and municipalities [31]. The monitored population expanded from approximately 6% of the national population in 2003 to 24% in 2013, achieving robust provincial-level representativeness and effectively reflecting the mortality patterns of the national population.

EC deaths were identified using ICD-10 codes (C54). Exclusion criteria comprised: (1) other malignant neoplasms; (2) cervical cancer (C53) and cancer of unspecified uterine sites (C55); and (3) cases with missing or unclassifiable codes. Standardized ICD-10 coding and surveillance protocols were uniformly applied throughout 2004–2021 to ensure data comparability.

All deaths within surveillance areas—whether in medical institutions, at home, other settings, or involving judicial intervention—were certified by attending physicians, community doctors, or township clinicians using the standard Resident Death Medical Certificate (Inference), and reported via the China CDC Cause-of-Death Reporting System. To maximize data integrity and accuracy, the surveillance system employed standardized reporting procedures, online verification mechanisms, and continuous monitoring of quality indicators, including the highest diagnostic level and diagnostic basis. Nevertheless, underreporting and misclassification of causes of death remain inherent limitations.

To examine urban-rural disparities in endometrial carcinoma mortality, urban and rural areas were defined according to the National Bureau of Statistics classification criteria, which remained consistent throughout the study period. All districts were classified as urban, while all counties, inclusive of county-level cities, were classified as rural. Structural disparities in healthcare systems between urban and rural settings may contribute to differential mortality patterns [32].

Research methods

Age-specific mortality rates were calculated using the following age groups: 0–, 1–, 5–, 10–, 15–, 20–, 25–, 30–, 35–, 40–, 45–, 50–, 55–, 60–, 65–, 70–, 75–, 80–, and 85– years. The CMR and ASMR for EC were calculated using the direct standardization method, with the 2020 Seventh National Population Census data released by the National Bureau of Statistics serving as the standard population to eliminate the confounding effect of age structure variations across different periods or regions.

Data analysis was performed using Excel 2017 software, and regression analysis was conducted using the Joinpoint regression program, version 5.0.0.0, developed by the National Cancer Institute [33, 34]. The maximum number of joinpoints was set to three, and a log-linear model was fitted. The optimal model was selected based on the weighted Bayesian information criterion, with statistical significance evaluated through 4,999 permutation tests.

Statistics analysis

The ASMR described annual EC characteristics. CMR and ASMR trends were compared across age groups and residence (urban vs. rural) to understand EC mortality epidemiology.

To investigate the temporal trends in reported mortality rate, we utilized the Joinpoint regression model, which incorporated natural logarithmic transformation. This approach allowed us to identify segmented trends and discern whether these trends were increasing or decreasing. The selection of the optimal model was based on the mean squared error, where a lower mean squared error indicated a superior model fit.

The trends were further evaluated by calculating the annual percent change (APC), the average annual percent change (AAPC), and the corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Rates increased if APC/AAPC > 0 (p < 0.05), decreased if < 0 (p < 0.05). The absolute value of the APC or AAPC reflected the extent of change in the trend, and trends with non-significant indicators were deemed stable.

All statistical analyses were conducted at a significance level of 0.05, with two-sided tests applied throughout.

Results

This study analyzed data from 2004 to 2021 to assess the temporal trends in EC mortality in China, with a focus on both crude and age-standardized mortality rates, stratified by urban and rural areas.

Crude mortality of EC in China from 2004 to 2021

From 2004 to 2021, 39,157 deaths from EC were recorded in China, with an average mortality rate of 2.63/105. The CMR initially declined from 2004 to 2013, then experienced an accelerated decrease between 2013 and 2016, followed by an increase from 2016 to 2021; however, the final mortality rate still showed a significant reduction (Fig. 1). The highest mortality rate was 4.34/105 in 2004, while the lowest mortality rate was 1.83/105 in 2016. In 2021, the mortality was 2.35/105 (Table 1). Comparing the data from 2004 to 2021, a decline of nearly 45.83% was observed during the study period.

The Joinpoint Regression Model analysis (Fig. 1) revealed that the overall trend changes could be divided into three distinct stages. From 2004 to 2013, the APC was − 2.13% (95% CI: -3.87% to -0.80%, p = 0.127), which was not statistically significant. From 2013 to 2016, the APC was − 19.33% (95% CI: -23.64% to -10.36%, p < 0.001), indicating a highly significant decline. From 2016 to 2021, the APC increased to 7.75% (95% CI: 2.87% to 17.60%, p < 0.001), which was also statistically significant.

Crude mortality changes of EC stratified

Over the past 18 years, the cumulative death toll of EC in cities and villages were 11,408 and 27,749. The number of deaths in villages was more than twice that in cities. The average CMRs were approximately 2.25 per 100,000 individuals in cities and 2.83 per 100,000 individuals in villages.

Similar to the trend observed in total CMR, urban CMR also exhibited a pattern of initial decline followed by an increase. The urban CMR declined from 2004 to 2013, declined more rapidly between 2013 and 2016, and subsequently increased from 2016 to 2021. The highest CMR was 3.52/105 in 2004, while the lowest rate was 1.61/105 in 2016. By 2021, the urban CMR had risen to 2.15/105 (Table 1), representing a 38.82% decrease compared with the rate in 2004.

The results of Joinpoint Regression Model (Fig. 2) revealed that APCs in urban CMR could be segmented into three distinct periods: a decreasing trend from 2004 to 2013, a significantly accelerated decline from 2013 to 2016, and a significant increase trend from 2016 to 2021. The corresponding APCs were − 2.71% (95% CI: -4.34% to 5.75%, p = 0.174), -14.16% (95% CI: -19.19% to -5.71%, p = 0.006), and 7.24% (95% CI: 1.89% to 22.43%, p = 0.012).

In contrast, the rural CMR exhibited a more complex pattern characterized by multiple fluctuations: it initially decreased from 2004 to 2006, subsequently increased between 2006 and 2012, declined again from 2012 to 2015, and finally rose from 2015 to 2021 (Fig. 3). The highest mortality was 4.77/105 in 2004 and 2005, while the lowest mortality was 1.94/105 in 2017 (Table 1). By 2021, the rural CMR had decreased to 2.46/105, representing a 48.41% decline compared with 2004. The APCs in rural CMR were complex but not statistically significant. Specifically, the APCs for rural mortality were − 12.60% (95% CI: -21.70% to 2.12%, p = 0.131) from 2004 to 2006, 2.82% (95% CI: -23.72% to 14.29%, p = 0.217) from 2006 to 2012, -24.03% (95% CI: -28.90% to 10.06%, p = 0.118) from 2012 to 2015, and 4.43% (95% CI: -6.63% to 17.04%, p = 0.112) from 2015 to 2021.

Age-standardized mortality changes of EC from 2004 to 2021

Between 2004 and 2021, the ASMR demonstrated a trend of initial decline followed by a subsequent increase. Specifically, the ASMR declined from 2004 to 2012, at an accelerated pace between 2012 and 2015, and subsequently increased from 2015 to 2021. In 2004, the mortality rate was 6.48/105 population. This rate decreased progressively, reaching its nadir of 2.06/105 population in 2016. After 2016, a modest upward trend was observed, with the rate increasing to 2.21/105 population by 2021 (Table 1). Despite the increase in the later years, a significant overall reduction of approximately 65.89% in mortality rate was observed when comparing the data from 2004 to 2021.

To further investigate the observed trends, we analyzed the APC across different segments of the study period (Fig. 4). Specifically, from 2004 to 2012, the ASMR exhibited a stable trend (APC = -3.80%, 95% CI: -5.96% to -0.96%, p = 0.088). In contrast, from 2012 to 2015, the most pronounced decrease was observed, with an APC of -20.59% (95% CI: -25.29% to -10.77%, p = 0.001), indicating a highly significant reduction. However, this downward trend was reversed after 2015. From 2015 to 2021, the ASMR of EC showed a slight increase, with an APC of 0.83% (95% CI: -3.15% to 14.61%, p = 0.556), which was not statistically significant.

Age-standardized mortality changes of EC stratified by residence

From 2004 to 2015, both urban and rural areas exhibited a downward trend in ASMRs. The urban ASMR continued to decline steadily from 2004 to 2021. In contrast, the rural ASMR decreased from 2004 to 2012, with a more rapid decline observed between 2012 and 2015. However, from 2015 to 2021, the rural ASMR showed a slight increase, whereas the urban ASMR continued its downward trajectory. In 2004, the ASMRs in urban and rural areas were 4.31/105 and 7.96/105, respectively. By 2021, these rates had decreased to approximately 1.75/105 in urban areas and 2.53/105 in rural areas. Notably, the lowest mortality rates were recorded in 2016 and 2017, at 1.60/105 in urban areas and 2.34/105 in rural areas, respectively (Table 1). Over the 18-year study period, rural residents consistently had higher ASMRs than their urban counterparts.

Urban areas experienced a sustained and significant decline in ASMR from 2004 to 2021 (Fig. 5), with an APC of -5.47% (95% CI: -6.55% to -4.34%, p < 0.001). This persistent reduction was a defining characteristic throughout the study period. In contrast, the trend in rural areas was more complex (Fig. 6). From 2004 to 2012, the APC in rural areas was − 3.37% (95% CI: -5.74% to 1.28%, p = 0.114), indicating a non-significant decline that was less pronounced than that observed in urban areas. Notably, from 2012 to 2015, rural areas experienced a statistically significant and marked decline in APC, with a rate of -24.07% (95% CI: -29.00% to -13.30%, p < 0.001), which outpaced the reduction observed in urban areas. However, from 2015 to 2021, in contrast to the continued decline in urban mortality, the APC in rural areas showed a non-significant positive trend of 1.32% (95% CI: -3.13% to 15.76%, p = 0.463). In summary, the APC values underscored the differing trends in ASMR between urban and rural areas over the study period, with particular emphasis on the statistically significant and pronounced decline in rural APC from 2012 to 2015.

AAPC of mortalities of EC

The Joinpoint Regression Model analysis (Table 2) revealed significant trends in both CMR and ASMR for the period 2004–2021. For CMR, notable decreases were observed, with AAPCs of -2.70% (95% CI: -3.66% to -1.58%, p < 0.001) for the overall population, -2.07% (95% CI: -3.10% to -0.68%, p = 0.04) for urban areas, and − 3.85% (95% CI: -5.01% to -2.07%, p < 0.001) for rural areas. These statistically significant declines indicate a general reduction in CMR across different regions.

When transitioning from crude to the ASMR, the Joinpoint regression model further demonstrated a significant decline in EC mortality after adjusting for age. The average annual decrease in ASMR over this period was 5.44% (95% CI: -6.48% to -4.10%, p < 0.001).

Consistent with the overall trend, urban areas exhibited a persistent reduction in ASMR. The AAPC for urban areas was − 5.47% (95% CI: -6.55% to -4.34%, p < 0.001), reflecting a steady decline in mortality rates. An even more pronounced decline in ASMR was found in rural regions, with an AAPC of -5.83% (95% CI: -6.97% to -4.40%, p < 0.001) from 2004 to 2021.

Discussion

This study statistically analyzed multiple indicators of endometrial cancer mortality in China from 2004 to 2021. Over the 18-year period, 39,157 deaths from EC were recorded in China, with an average mortality rate of 2.63/105 population. In China, ASMR peaked at 6.48/105 (2004) [5] and reached its lowest at 2.06/105 (2016) [5]. However, the cumulative number of deaths in rural areas was more than twice that in urban areas, at 27,749 and 11,408 respectively. Moreover, the rural ASMR was consistently higher than that in urban areas over the 18-year study period, but the AAPC of the ASMR in rural areas showed a more significant downward trend than in urban areas, at -5.83% and − 5.47%, respectively.

The ASMR in China of EC have shown consistent declines among Chinese women overall, as well as in both urban and rural populations. Specifically, a statistically significant decrease in the overall ASMRC of EC was observed during 2012–2015 with an APC of -20.59% (95%CI: -25.29% to -10.77%, p = 0.001). The trend in rural areas was consistent with the overall trend, with an APC of -24.07% (95% CI: -29.00% to -13.30%, p < 0.001). Meanwhile, the urban ASMR of EC demonstrated a continuous downward trend from 2004 to 2021 (AAPC = -5.47%, p < 0.05). This is consistent with the global trend of a decrease in age-adjusted mortality rates of EC from 1990 to 2017 (AAPC = -1.19%, 95% CI: -1.24% to -1.14%) [35], and aligns with the mortality reduction observed in China from 1990 to 2019 [36]. We speculate that the significant decline in the ASMR from 2012 to 2015 may be attributed to the following factors: First, during this period, Chinese vigorous implementation of cancer prevention initiatives, expansion of health insurance coverage and health education policies, facilitated early detection and treatment of EC [36, 37]. Second, advancements in treatment modalities including minimally invasive surgery [38], precision radiotherapy [39], chemotherapy regimens [40], targeted therapies [41, 42], and immunotherapy [43], along with the widespread adoption of the multi-disciplinary team model [44], collectively improved the treatment effect and the survival rates. Additionally, promoting public health education and management has also contributed to the decrease in the mortality rate of EC. However, Tan W et al.‘s New York State study (1979–2001) indicated cervical cancer mortality improvement primarily resulted from progress in preventive measures, while EC reduction stemmed from earlier diagnosis and treatment. This highlights the differences in EC mortality decline mechanisms in China and the United States under distinct healthcare systems that the rational allocation of gynecological medical resources under the hierarchical medical system in the United States have enhanced treatment accessibility and the strengthening of specialized education and training in gynecological oncology have promoted the popularization of early diagnosis and intervention, which may be an important reason for the decrease in the mortality rate of EC in the United States [45, 46]. Notably, EC, unlike cervical and breast cancers, is not included in China’s national free screening program, resulting in low public awareness and screening rates.

However, no significant overall mortality changes of EC were observed in Chinese women from 2004 to 2012 and from 2015 to 2021. We speculate that although the diagnosis and treatment of EC in China had achieved breakthrough progress in the early stage during 2015–2021, the combined effects of counteracting factors including the intensification of population aging, environmental impacts (e.g., rising prevalence of obesity, hypertension and other metabolic disorders), and plateauing technological advancements in later-stage treatments may have offset these effects, resulting in no further significant mortality change during this period. First, the acceleration of population aging may have expanded the size of the high-risk population. According to China’s Seventh National Population Census in 2020, the proportion of the population over 60 years old increased rapidly from 16.1% in 2015 to 18.7% in 2020, while the proportion of those over 65 years old rose from 10.5% to 14.2% during the same period, indicating a marked acceleration of population aging in China after 2015. The risk of endometrial cancer incidence and mortality increases significantly with age, particularly among the elderly population. Even with improvements in diagnosis and treatment, the mortality risk may remain elevated. Second, obesity and diabetes are well-established risk factors for endometrial cancer and are associated with poor prognosis. This suggests that the increasing prevalence of metabolic risk factors may have contributed to both the rising incidence and worsened prognosis of endometrial cancer. Third, the incidence of endometrial cancer in China has shown a sustained upward trend, implying that the number of cases requiring treatments has been continuously increasing. Consequently, even with improvements in management strategies, the number of deaths may not decline. Finally, potential changes in diagnostic coding or mortality reporting practices may have influenced the observed mortality trends during 2015–2021. The coverage and reporting quality of medical certification of cause of death in China improved substantially during this period. Given the weaker baseline of death registration in rural areas, the magnitude of quality improvement was likely greater there, potentially leading to the gradual inclusion of previously unreported deaths and thus affecting the continuity of mortality trend estimates. Concurrently, efforts to reduce garbage codes—non-specific cause-of-death codes such as “senility” or “heart failure”—may have altered the attribution of deaths to specific diseases. For instance, in Shanghai, the proportion of garbage codes decreased from 31% to 2.3% after routine data quality assurance procedures. In summary, the deceleration and even reversal of the declining trend in endometrial cancer mortality in China during 2015–2021 cannot be attributed to a single factor but rather resulted from the interplay of multiple factors, including demographic structure, disease spectrum, healthcare resources, and data systems.

Stratified by urban-rural categories, the ASMR of rural residents remained consistently higher than that of urban residents during the 18-year study period. This aligns with Whitney E. Zahnd et al.‘s 2004–2013 U.S. study [47] showing rural endometrial-cancer patients had lower surgery, nodal evaluation, and lymphadenectomy rates and higher mortality. Our study further found that while urban ASMR of EC showed a continuous decline from 2004 to 2021, rural areas exhibited a significant reduction only during 2012–2015. The disparity may stem from the long-standing structural disadvantages in rural healthcare infrastructure in China. Firstly, rural areas in China face relative shortages of gynecologic oncology specialists and advanced medical equipment, leading to inadequate implementation of the “early detection, early diagnosis, and early treatment” strategy for EC, which may result in delayed diagnosis and suboptimal treatment. Additionally, the existing “two-cancer screening” programs only cover cervical cancer and breast cancer, lacking specific screening policies for EC. Secondly, limited health insurance coverage imposes a heavier financial burden on rural patients, potentially reducing treatment adherence. Moreover, rural women generally have weak health awareness and tend to seek medical care only after symptom onset, thereby leading to diagnosis at advanced stages, which complicates treatment and increases mortality risk [8]. Economic growth, resource reallocation, technology advances, and pro-rural policies modestly improved rural care in 2012–2015. However, the progress was not sustained, suggesting that future efforts should focus on strengthening rural healthcare resource allocation, expanding health education, and refining prevention policies to further reduce EC mortality and narrow the urban-rural gap.

Notably, between 2004 and 2021, although healthcare environments in both urban and rural areas improved, the AAPC in the ASMR of EC in rural areas (AAPC = -5.83%) declined significantly faster than that in urban areas (AAPC = -5.47%, p < 0.05). This reversed disparity may reflect the cumulative effects of the following policy interventions: First, the New Rural Construction Strategy in 2005 significantly increased medical and health investment in rural areas, systematically improving the infrastructure and service capabilities of primary medical care. Second, expanded New Rural Cooperative Medical Scheme plus equipment upgrades raised early diagnosis. Additionally, telemedicine and rural medical assistance narrowed urban–rural quality gap. This “policy-resource-capacity” linkage mechanism may represent a critical pathway for reducing health inequalities. Interestingly, in Jianyang Feng et al.‘s study, which divided countries and regions into five sub-regions (low, lower-middle, middle, high-middle, and high) based on the Socio-Demographic Index (SDI), a comprehensive indicator of development status, the most pronounced decline in the age-standardized mortality rate of EC from 1990 to 2019 was observed in the high-middle SDI region (AAPC = -1.1%, 95% CI: -1.3% to -0.9%, p < 0.001), while the low SDI region exhibited a non-significant increase (p = 0.307) [14]. This suggests that at the national and regional levels, the long-term cumulative effects of “economy-education-population” may have shaped healthcare systems in ways that contributed to the reduction in disease burden in the high-middle SDI region. However, Chinese proactive policy interventions in rural areas have demonstrated the breakthrough potential of “precise supplementation of shortfalls” in reducing health inequalities and improving health outcomes, offering significant insights for global health governance that in the future countries should attach importance to the initiative of policy intervention on the basis of respecting the objective developmental patterns reflected by SDI, be vigilant against the “urban medical suction effect” and the “rural lag effect” and promote the realization of health equity.

This study has certain limitations. First, complex endometrial-cancer factors lacked attribution and etiological analyses. Second, the analysis was performed only at the national level, and the disease data were not stratified by province or city, limiting its guidance for tailored prevention and treatment strategies in different regions. Future studies should collect more granular data to enhance the research.

Conclusion

From 2004 to 2021, the mortality rate of EC in China experienced an initial decline followed by a subsequent increase, with an overall decline of 45.83%. While the rural AAPC showed a significantly steeper downward trend than that in urban areas, the rural mortality rate has been persistently higher than that in urban populations and the number of EC-related deaths in rural areas consistently exceeded twice that in urban areas throughout the study period. This epidemiological pattern characterized by “a steep decline but high baseline” reflects that there are still significant deficiencies in the current EC prevention and treatment system in rural areas. Based on these findings, we suggest continuously optimizing the spatial allocation of medical resources in rural areas, constantly expanding the screening coverage of high-risk groups, and intensifying the programs of standardized treatment plans, with the ultimate goal of achieving an equitable decline in the disease burden of EC in both urban and rural areas.
