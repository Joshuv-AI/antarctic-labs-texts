Evaluation of Beverage Can-opening Status as a Screening Indicator for Sarcopenia: A Cross-sectional Study
Authors: Shuntaro Tsuji, Yohei Sawaya, Tamaki Hirose, Takahiro Shiba, Ryo Sato, Lu Yin, Masafumi Itokazu, Tomohiko Urano
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectives:This study aimed to examine whether the ability to open a canned beverage can be associated with sarcopenia and to determine whether beverage can-opening status could serve as a simple screening indicator for sarcopenia.Methods:A cross-sectional study was conducted among 109 older adults requiring long-term care/support attending daycare rehabilitation services (81.1 ± 6.9 years; 55 men, 54 women). Participants were instructed to open a stay-on-tab beverage can while sitting and were classified into Success or Failure groups according to single-attempt performance. Handgrip strength and skeletal muscle mass index (SMI) were measured to diagnose sarcopenia according to the Asian Working Group for Sarcopenia 2025 criteria. Logistic regression analyses were performed to examine the associations between sarcopenia, handgrip strength, SMI, and beverage can-opening status after adjusting for age, sex, body mass index, Barthel Index, and Charlson Comorbidity Index. Screening performance for the inability to open a can for sarcopenia was evaluated.Results:The Success and Failure groups comprised 78 and 31 participants, respectively. Can-opening failure was significantly associated with sarcopenia (odds ratio, 4.5; 95% confidence interval, 1.4–14.1; P = 0.009), and similar associations were observed for low handgrip strength and low SMI. The inability to open a canned beverage demonstrated a moderate discriminatory ability for sarcopenia (area under the curve, 0.62; sensitivity, 42.2%; specificity, 81.3%; P = 0.038).Conclusions:Beverage can-opening status may reflect sarcopenia status. Difficulty in opening cans, an everyday sign readily noticed by individuals and family members, may facilitate early detection of sarcopenia without specialized measurements.

INTRODUCTION

Sarcopenia is a progressive, age-associated reduction in skeletal muscle mass and muscle strength, with its prevalence rising as individuals grow older.1) It has been linked to adverse outcomes, including higher mortality, an elevated risk of falls and fractures, and a decline in quality of life.2) In Asia, the diagnostic criteria for sarcopenia were revised from the Asian Working Group for Sarcopenia (AWGS) 2019 to AWGS 2025, shifting toward a concept that emphasizes muscle health across the lifespan.1) AWGS 2025 also introduced new criteria for middle-aged adults (50–64 years), highlighting the importance of early detection and intervention. However, diagnosing sarcopenia requires the use of medical devices, such as those used for bioelectrical impedance analysis (BIA) and muscle strength tests (e.g., handgrip strength). These methods require some level of training and expertise, which can make routine assessment challenging.

Several non-instrumental sarcopenia screening methods, such as calf circumference, SARC-F (five-item sarcopenia questionnaire), and SARC-CalF (SARC-F + calf circumference), have been proposed. However, these assessments also require some level of professional knowledge, making the methods less accessible in some settings. In contrast, the Yubi–Wakka test is a simple, self-administered screening tool. This test evaluates whether the maximal calf circumference of the nondominant leg is smaller than the ring formed by the participant’s thumbs and index fingers, with sarcopenia suspected when a gap is present between the fingers and the calf.3) Recently, ability to open a polyethylene terephthalate (PET) bottle cap has been proposed as a sarcopenia screening action based on daily-life movements. This method determines sarcopenia according to whether the bottle cap can be opened, offering greater everyday relevance and enabling easy implementation regardless of location or environment.4,5) Screening approaches that capture the early signs of sarcopenia through familiar activities of daily living (ADL) allow older adults, as well as their caregivers and family members, to notice a functional decline at an early stage. Furthermore, expanding such screening to include a broader range of routine daily activities beyond bottle cap opening may further enhance opportunities for the early detection of sarcopenia.

Decline in handgrip strength, a core component of sarcopenia, has been reported to be significantly associated with reduced ADL performance.6) Such a functional decline may reduce the ability of older adults to open beverage packaging, thereby interfering with daily activities.7) Martin et al.8) examined the relationship between handgrip strength and manual dexterity and reported that age-related decreases in handgrip strength were closely associated with reduced hand and finger dexterity. Among daily life tasks, opening a beverage container is a representative packaging-related action. Canned beverages, including vegetable juice, coffee, and beer, are widely consumed daily. For these beverage cans, the use of stay-on-tab designs, in which the tab remains attached to the can body after opening, has been widely adopted to address environmental concerns and prevent accidental ingestion or aspiration.9,10) For stay-on-tab beverage cans, engineering issues related to finger accessibility have been reported, including the narrow gap under the tab, which makes inserting the fingertip difficult, discomfort or pain in the fingertip when lifting the tab, and increased localized fingertip load depending on the tab shape.11) Therefore, opening a stay-on-tab can requires fingertip insertion and lifting movements and involves fine manual dexterity. In older adults, such opening tasks may become a barrier in daily life.12) From a biomechanical perspective, opening a stay-on-tab beverage can requires an upward pulling force applied to the tab, which has been evaluated in Newtons (N) in previous ergonomic studies.11) By contrast, opening a PET bottle cap primarily requires rotational torque (N·m) generated through a twisting motion.13) Therefore, beverage can opening and PET bottle opening are considered different tasks. In addition, ring-pull cans, which have a structure similar to stay-on-tab cans, require greater opening force than PET bottles, metallized pouches, and thermoformed trays.7) Furthermore, in subjective assessments, both older adults with frailty and active older adults perceived ring-pull cans as more difficult to open than PET bottles.7)

We hypothesized that opening a canned beverage represents a distinct functional task that may serve as a simple and accessible method for screening sarcopenia or muscle weakness in daily life. This study aimed to clarify whether beverage can-opening status is associated with sarcopenia, handgrip strength, and skeletal muscle mass index (SMI), and whether the ability to open a canned beverage has potential for use as a screening tool for sarcopenia.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study Design

This cross-sectional study was conducted from September 2024 to November 2025. All participants received a detailed explanation of the study objectives and procedures, provided both orally and in written form, and written informed consent was obtained prior to participation. The research protocol was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board of the International University of Health and Welfare (approval no. 21-Io-22–3), and the study was conducted in compliance with the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki.

Study Setting and Participants

The participants were older adults attending daycare rehabilitation services and were certified as requiring support or care under the Japanese long-term care insurance (LTCI) system. This single-facility census study included all 173 participants for whom body composition measurements could be obtained.

Assessment of Sarcopenia

Sarcopenia was assessed according to the AWGS 2025 criteria.1) Handgrip strength was measured in the seated position using a Smedley-type dynamometer (Takei GRIP-D T.K.K5401; SANKA, Sanjo, Japan), with two trials performed on each side; the highest value was used for analysis. The SMI was determined with a body composition analyzer employing BIA technology (InBody S10, InBody, Seoul, South Korea) and calculated by dividing the appendicular skeletal muscle mass by height squared. Sarcopenia was diagnosed based on the AWGS 2025 criteria as the coexistence of low handgrip strength and SMI. Low handgrip strength was defined as less than 28 kg in men and less than 18 kg in women. Low SMI was defined as less than 7.0 kg/m2 in men and less than 5.7 kg/m2 in women.1) Moreover, commonly used sarcopenia screening tools, including calf circumference, SARC-F, and SARC-CalF, were used.

Assessment of Beverage Can-opening Status

The beverage can-opening task used an unopened 190-g can of tomato juice (Kagome, Nagoya, Japan) equipped with a stay-on tab. Stay-on tabs, which remain attached after opening, are widely used in commercially available beverage cans in Japan (see https://www.toyo-seikan.co.jp/e/product/beverage/can/cap/). Therefore, in this study, opening a stay-on-tab beverage can, which older adults in Japan may encounter in daily life, was used as the assessment task. This task does not require specialized equipment and reflects a common activity in daily life. The measurement was carried out while the participant was in a seated position. Participants held the can in front of their bodies with one hand and opened the can independently by lifting the pull tab with the fingers of the other hand. They were allowed to use their usual, natural method of opening. However, fixing the can against the thigh was prohibited, and the participants were instructed to hold the can in a manner that prevented spilling. The task was performed only once, and participants were allowed approximately 30 s. Before the task, all participants were provided the following standardized instructions: “Please open the can in the way that feels most natural and easiest for you; Please hold the beverage can with one hand and open the tab with the other hand; Please do not support the beverage can with your thigh or body; Please open the can without spilling the beverage.” Success or failure was determined based on whether the can was completely opened and the beverage was ready to drink. A physical therapist evaluated the performance and classified the participants into the Success or Failure group based on whether they were able to open the can. Using this standardized procedure, can-opening ability was assessed under consistent conditions. Additionally, to assess whether participants usually experienced difficulty opening beverage cans in daily life, self-reported beverage can-opening ability was evaluated based on a previous study.14) The questionnaire assessed their condition during the past month and was administered prior to the actual can-opening task.

Other Assessments

From the medical records of the daycare rehabilitation facility, we collected data on age, sex, height, weight, body mass index (BMI), care-need level, medical conditions, and Barthel Index (BI). Disease information was collected by referring to referral letters and medical records based on physician diagnoses, and the Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) was also calculated.15) Gait speed was assessed on a 5-m walkway with acceleration and deceleration zones, and usual walking speed was measured once.16)

Statistical Analysis

The Success and Failure groups were compared using independent t-tests, Mann–Whitney U tests, chi-square tests, and Fisher’s exact tests. Independent t-tests were performed after confirming normality. Binary logistic regression analyses were then performed, with sarcopenia, low handgrip strength, or low skeletal muscle mass as dependent variables and can-opening status (success/failure) as independent variables. Models were examined without adjustment and in adjusted Model I after adjustment for age, sex, BMI, and BI (≥85 or <85),17) which were included as covariates because sarcopenia may be influenced by age, sex, BMI, and ADL.18,19,20) In adjusted Model II, CCI (<3 or ≥3) was included as a covariate to account for the influence of comorbidities, in addition to the covariates included in adjusted Model I.15) Odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were determined. To evaluate the discriminatory ability of can-opening status for sarcopenia, we calculated the sensitivity, specificity, area under the curve (AUC), positive predictive value, and negative predictive value. We also assessed the screening performances of the calf circumference, SARC-F, and SARC-CalF. Finally, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was conducted to determine the handgrip strength cutoff value that best distinguished between the Success and Failure groups in the overall sample, as well as separately for men and women. All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS Statistics version 26 (IBM Japan, Tokyo, Japan). A significance threshold of 5% was applied.

RESULTS

Of the 173 individuals initially enrolled, 64 were excluded for the following reasons (Fig. 1): dementia (n = 19), aphasia (n = 13), hemiplegia (n = 12), paraplegia (n = 1), rheumatoid arthritis (n = 6), ataxia (n = 4), difficulty walking independently (n = 4), and missing data because of absence or discontinuation during the assessment period (n = 5). Ultimately, 109 participants (55 men and 54 women; mean age 81.1 ± 6.9 years) were included in the analysis, with 78 (71.6%) in the Success group and 31 (28.4%) in the Failure group.

Figure 2 shows the ROC analysis used to determine the handgrip strength cutoff, distinguishing between the Success and Failure groups. In the overall sample, the optimal cutoff value was 17.9 kg (AUC = 0.74; sensitivity, 64.5%; specificity, 80.8%; P <0.001). In men, the handgrip strength cutoff value was 20.1 kg (AUC = 0.71, 95% CI = 0.52–0.89; sensitivity, 41.7%; specificity, 95.3%; P = 0.031), whereas in women, the cutoff value was 16.1 kg (AUC = 0.73, 95% CI = 0.60–0.87; sensitivity, 73.7%; specificity, 74.3%; P = 0.005).

DISCUSSION

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to investigate whether the ability to open a beverage can—a representative ADL—is associated with sarcopenia. We observed that failure to open a beverage can demonstrated some level of screening ability for sarcopenia. Thus, everyday observations, such as “having more difficulty opening containers,” may potentially indicate sarcopenia, even without specialized equipment or formal screening tools, and may contribute to its early detection. The novelty of this study is that the naturally occurring daily life cue of difficulty in opening a canned beverage may be an early indicator of functional decline, which would distinguish it from conventional screening methods that require professional assessment.

The beverage can-opening task is a complex movement requiring fine pinch precision and wrist stabilization.21) Common grasp types in daily activities include the pinch, lateral pinch, and cylindrical grasp,22) with multiple grasp patterns depending on the task.23) Opening a canned beverage relies on these grasp types. The wrist primarily moves along the “dart-thrower’s motion,” a natural oblique plane from radial extension to ulnar flexion, which is used in many ADL tasks such as tying shoelaces and opening cans.24) Recent reports indicate that pinch strength predicts adverse outcomes similar to grip strength25) and that aging is associated with prolonged opening time, increased muscle activity, and greater wrist motion during pop-top opening, although a different type of can was used in that study.26) Together, these findings support the interpretation that can-opening performance reflects age-related functional changes and daily motor function.

In this study, participants who were unable to open a beverage can showed a higher prevalence of sarcopenia, along with a significantly lower grip strength and SMI. Thus, the seemingly simple daily life task of opening a can is closely related to key components of sarcopenia, including muscle strength and skeletal muscle mass. The handgrip strength cutoff that best discriminated can-opening performance was 17.9 kg, which is typically indicative of muscle weakness and showed good accuracy (AUC = 0.74). The Failure group also had lower BI scores and higher care-need levels than the Success group, suggesting that can-opening ability reflects real-world functional performance. Overall, difficulty in opening a can serves as an early sign of sarcopenia and helps detect declines in ADL.

The can-opening task requires no specialized equipment, can be readily observed by individuals or family members in daily life, and can be performed easily and quickly by anyone in most settings. Opening a can lid is an action that can be repeated and checked by oneself. Such self-assessment and self-monitoring may promote behavioral change and help individuals understand the link between lifestyle modifications and functional outcomes, thereby complementing existing screening approaches.27) Previous studies have reported the diagnostic performance of sarcopenia screening tools, including calf circumference (sensitivity, 70.0%; specificity, 57.5%; AUC = 0.647), SARC-F (sensitivity, 10.4%; specificity, 92.8%; AUC = 0.516), and SARC-CalF (sensitivity, 48.4%; specificity, 76.7%; AUC = 0.625),28) and meta-analyses have also reported the diagnostic performance of SARC-F29) (sensitivity, 27%; specificity, 91%; AUC = 0.73) and SARC-CalF30) (sensitivity, 53.3%; specificity, 87.3%; AUC = 0.78). Another meta-analysis evaluated the three screening tools of calf circumference (sensitivity, 81%; specificity, 73%; AUC = 0.88), SARC-F (sensitivity, 34%; specificity, 90%; AUC = 0.78), and SARC-CalF (sensitivity, 59%; specificity, 85%; AUC = 0.78).31) In comparison, we consider that can-opening performance demonstrated moderate discriminatory ability. Despite being a simple daily activity, these findings support the potential practicality and generalizability of this method as a sarcopenia screening tool.

This study has some limitations. First, because this was a cross-sectional study conducted at a single facility, causal relationships could not be determined. Therefore, potential selection bias may have occurred, and the findings should be generalized with caution. Second, only one type of beverage container was used, and differences in tab shape or opening resistance across products may limit generalizability. In addition, although some participants may have performed the task in a manner different from their habitual method in daily life, the present findings should be interpreted as can-opening performance under standardized conditions. Furthermore, because pinch movement and manual dexterity were not assessed in this study, whether can-opening performance reflects pure muscle strength or overall upper limb function remains unclear. Third, because the sample size was limited, the confidence intervals in the logistic regression analyses were wide, and some statistical instability remained. Fourth, dementia was determined based on physician diagnoses, and cutoff values based on assessment tools such as the Mini-Mental State Examination could not be provided. Finally, the influence of disease status was adjusted for only using the CCI, and detailed assessments of mild cognitive impairment and pain could not be performed. Furthermore, the influence of unadjusted confounding factors may have remained. Future studies should include multicenter designs and multiple types of beverage containers to improve generalizability and further clarify the relationship between sarcopenia and can-opening performance.

CONCLUSION

To the best of our knowledge, no previous studies have examined the relationship between beverage can-opening ability and sarcopenia. The inability to open a beverage can may have a modest screening capability for sarcopenia. Difficulty in opening beverage cans in daily life may serve as a simple and accessible indicator of early sarcopenia without the need for specialized measurements.
