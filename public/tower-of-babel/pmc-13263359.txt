Perceived service quality scale for community pharmacies: tool translation of the short form and service quality exploration
Authors: Norah O. Abanmy, Carl R. Schneider, Stephen R. Carter
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background and AimIn the community pharmacy sector the concept of service quality is important. Pharmacists working in these settings provide useful services that go beyond merely dispensing medications. This study aimed to translate the short Perceived Service Quality Scale (PSQS−SF) to Arabic and to use this scale to assess the determinants of service quality of community pharmacies in relation to competitively priced high quality service and loyalty intention.MethodsA pre−validated short Perceived Service Quality Scale (pSQS−A−SF) was translated from English to Arabic, validated and approved by the original authors. The scale consisted of six questions assessing pharmacy service quality, two questions evaluating the perception of price competitiveness, and three questions measuring loyalty intentions. All of the items were rated on a 5−point Likert scale. Community pharmacy users have been approached to answer the survey and descriptive statistics were reported.ResultsThe pSQS−SF was successfully translated and culturally adapted to Arabic context (pSQS−A−SF) with minor modifications. A total of 207 respondents completed the survey. Perceived service quality was rated highly across most items, whereas perceptions of price competitiveness were lower. In confirmatory factor analysis, the measurement model showed improved fit after minor modification (deletion of one service−quality item), with acceptable factor loadings and composite reliability, although average variance extracted was marginal. Service quality was strongly correlated with loyalty intentions. In the structural model, higher perceived service quality predicted greater patronage loyalty (β=0.26, p=0.023) and prescription loyalty (β=0.41, p=0.002), while price competitiveness did not significantly predict loyalty outcomes; private health insurance showed a small positive association with patronage loyalty (β=0.156, p=0.022).ConclusionThe Arabic version of the perceived service quality short-form demonstrates preliminary evidence of construct validity and reliability and can support evaluation of patient experience in Saudi community pharmacies. Perceived service quality—rather than price competitiveness—was linked with self-reported patronage loyalty in this sample. Further research should strengthen validation (including discriminant validity and broader sampling) and assess performance across settings and subgroups.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s44446-026-00095-x.

Introduction

Background

In the community pharmacy sector service quality from the patient’s perspective is important as pharmacists working in community pharmacies are capable of providing useful services beyond just dispensing medication (Tsuyuki et al. 2018). Pharmacists working in community pharmacies can provide patients with detailed instruction on how to use the medication properly such as medication review assessment, patient education and counselling especially for asthmatic and diabetic patients. This can signify a strong relationship between patients and their chosen pharmacy. The environment of increased competition between community pharmacy and discount stores (specialty retailers that primarily focus on the sale of cosmetics and skincare/haircare items) requires loyal patients to community pharmacy (Alfageh et al. 2024).

In Saudi Arabia a new electronic prescription system has been initiated by the Ministry of Health (MOH) and the National Unified Procurement company (NUPCO) that allows patients visiting a government health provider to obtain their medication from participating Community pharmacies for free called “Wasfaty” (Alshammari et al. 2023; Wasfaty 2026). Wasfaty is an Arabic term meaning “my prescription”, through Wasfaty a substantial number of patients are expected to visit community pharmacy and benefit from their health services. For this reason building a strong relationship between community pharmacy and patients by increasing loyalty has the potential to create a positive impact upon patients’ health (Rasheed et al. 2024; Alzahrani et al. 2024).

Previous studies have examined patient satisfaction within Saudi community pharmacies. These studies developed satisfaction metrics based on both personal insights and practical research experience. For example, Alzahrani et al. (2024) surveyed 66,541 patients who had used the Wasfaty system (Alzahrani et al. 2024). More than 70% of participants reported being satisfied with the service. Notably, satisfaction levels were higher among older and female patients, particularly regarding the five key aspects of Wasfaty services that were evaluated. However, the measures were not theoretically grounded in the broader satisfaction or service quality literature. Since the measures were not used to predict the outcomes of patient experience, for example patronage behaviour or loyalty intentions, it is not clear that the measures of patient experience have predictive validity. Recently in the setting of community pharmacies, Carter et al. developed and tested a theoretical model of how consumers’ perceptions of service quality are traded-off with their perceptions of price competitiveness to influence patronage behaviour (Carter et al. 2023; Zeithaml 1988). It was demonstrated that consumers’ positive evaluations of service quality and their perceptions that the pharmacy has competitive prices will lead them to more frequently visiting the pharmacy (patronage loyalty) and decrease the number of other pharmacies that they visit (patronage disloyalty). In Carter et al.’s model, service quality perceptions arise primarily through interactions with staff around obtaining prescription and purchasing non-prescription medicines in the pharmacy setting and the opportunity for the development of relationship quality arising from repeated positive experiences. In the present study therefore, the act of obtaining prescriptions medicines from a community pharmacy was identified as a patronage behaviour. According to Saudi General Authority for statistics, there were 22104 community pharmacies in Saudi Arabia in 2017, corresponding to 1 pharmacy per 1,401 people (General authority of statistics 2024). By comparison, The Organization for Economic co-operation and Development (OECD) data indicate an average of around 28 community pharmacies per 100,000 population (approximately 1 per 3570 people) across European Union countries (OECD 2023). This high prevalence of community pharmacies has the potential to create a positive impact on patients’ health (Gavilan et al. 2014).

In this work the pSQS-A-SF will be translated and culturally adapted to address the quality of pharmacy services. The quality of pharmacy services will be reflected by positive experience with staff as well as the services that are provided by them to patients and the competitive pricing and its impact on patient’s loyalty to the rated community pharmacy.

The Arabic translation of the pSQS-A-SF is essential for assessing service quality in Saudi Arabia and for facilitating continuous improvement, which is a priority at this stage of the country’s health transformation. This is particularly important within the context of the Wasfaty system and the expansion of pharmacy services.

Aim

The main aim of this work is to translate from English to Arabic and validate a theoretically grounded patient-reported experience measure (PREM) and a measure of patients’ perceptions of price competitiveness. In this work, the descriptive statistics of patient experience, perceptions of price competitiveness and behavioural loyalty within Saudi community pharmacies will be reported. In addition, this work will investigate whether positive patient experience and positive perceptions of price competitiveness will predict behavioural loyalty to community pharmacies”.

Methods

The Arabic translation

The pSQS-A-SF survey was originally developed in English, by Carter, Mudarris and Schneider (Mirzaei et al. 2019). Permission to use and translate the survey items into Arabic was obtained from the original authors (CRS and SRC). Translation into Arabic was performed by a bilingual expert translator from King Saud University Translation Center. The Arabic version (pSQS-A-SF) was then back translated into English by another bilingual pharmacy expert who was not involved in the forward translation. The original authors (SRC and CRS) reviewed the back translated version for correspondence with the original. Minor modifications and clarifications were discussed and corrected iteratively between the translator (NG) and the original authors. Two native Arabic speakers (one of them is a pharmacy expert) who are a frequent community pharmacy users were recruited as pilot participants to answer the survey and to provide feedback. They completed the survey and provided feedback on the clarity, cultural relevance, and comprehensibility of the items. Based on their feedback, minor revisions were made by the researcher (NA). The revised sections were then back-translated and reviewed by the original authors (SRC and CRS).

Ethics approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Board at King Saud University (No. KSU-HE-24–861). A survey was distributed online to assess participants’ perceptions of service quality, price competitiveness, attitudinal and behavioural loyalty and demographic questions. The survey was constructed using Google Forms (Google LLC, Mountain view, CA,USA) and distributed through WhatsApp and email between October 2024 to December 2024. The targeted population was adults above 18 years old using at least one prescription-only- medicine (POM) regularly, obtained from community pharmacies. The survey consisted of an overview of the aim of the survey and thereafter participants were asked to consent to participate. A screening question was used after consent to participate was obtained, asking the participant whether they had obtained a POM from a community pharmacy. If the participant answered “yes”, the participants were asked to complete the survey. If they answered “no” they were thanked and were not offered the opportunity to continue answering the survey questions.

Survey questions

In the current study, The Arabic translated short-form was used to measure three constructs of the perceptions of service quality scale in community pharmacy (pSQS-A-SF). The three constructs were, perceived service quality (pSQ), perceived price competitiveness (pPC), and perceived loyalty (PL).

There were two questions addressing perceptions of price competitiveness (pPC)and three questions addressing loyalty intentions (Mirzaei et al. 2019; Grew et al. 2019; Carter et al. 2022, 2021). All of these items were rated using a 5-point Likert scale (strongly disagree to strongly agree) with one extra option for “I don’t know”.

Following this there were three behavioural items which became the dependent variables in this analysis. There were two questions to determine aspects of behavioural loyalty. Patronage loyalty was defined as the frequency of visits to the rated pharmacy ranging from a low of “at least annually” to a high of “at least daily” (Carter et al. 2023). There was a yes/no question for another aspect of behavioural loyalty – Prescription loyalty, which was defined as whether the rated pharmacy was the source of prescription medications (yes/no). There was one question for patronage disloyalty, which was defined as the number of different pharmacies visited in the previous month, ranging from a low of “one” (the rated pharmacy only)” to a high of “more than 3 pharmacies”).

Additional questions gathered demographic information (age, education, employment status, monthly salary, number of medications used daily and presence of medical insurance) as well as whether the rated community pharmacy was a pharmacy chain or independent.

Data analysis

The data were analysed using IBM SPSS Statistics 25, and MPLUS Version 8.5.

Confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) and structural equation modelling (SEM)

To validate the translated versions of the scales, (pSQS-A-SF), along with pPC and loyalty intentions, CFA was performed to allow for construct validity testing. It was expected that there would be a high correlation of pSQS-A-SF with loyalty intentions and a much lower between pPC and loyalty intentions (Carter et al. 2023). CFA and SEM was conducted using a weighted least squares means and variance adjusted (WLSMV) estimator to manage ordinal data arising from the 5-point response categories with missingness (Schlomer et al. 2010). Model fit was reported using the fit indices recommended by Schreiber (Schreiber 2017). Absolute fit was reported with the c, Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA) and Standardized Root Mean Squared Residual (SRMR) (Zeithaml 1988; Hooper and Coughlan 2008). Relative fit indices, including the comparative fit index (CFI), and Akaike’s information criteria (AIC) were also reported (Hooper and Coughlan 2008). There are important limitations to the interpretability of these fit criteria, arising from variations in study population size, number of parameters, factor loadings, correlations between factors, data distribution and estimators (Goretzko et al. 2024; Groskurth et al. 2024). For the context of this study, the targets were RMSEA < 0.1, SRMR < 0.8, CFI > 0.95. Convergent validity was confirmed if all the indicators had standardized factor loadings > 0.5, the composite reliability (CR) value was ≥ 0.7 and the average variance extracted (AVE) of each construct achieved a value ≥ 0.5 (Hair et al. 2017). Discriminant validity was assessed according to the recommendations of Carter (Carter 2016). Discriminant validity concerns generally arise if the correlation coefficients between factors exceeds 0.85. If this threshold was exceeded, the nested modelling test was used, where a difference in the Chi square (p < 0.05) provides some evidence for discriminant validity (Fornell and Larcker 1981). Finally, if that threshold is met, a high level of confidence that the constructs are unique is provided by the average variance extracted test (Fornell and Larcker 1981).

A path model was specified with patronage loyalty, prescription loyalty and patronage disloyalty as dependent variables. Predictor variables were pSQS-A-SF and pPC, along with any of the participants’ demographic characteristics and/or pharmacy characteristics that showed univariate correlation with any of the dependent variables with p < 0.05.

Univariate associations

In order to conduct multivariate analyses, unweighted summative scores were created for pSQS-A-SF and pPC intentions. For patronage loyalty, prescription loyalty and patronage disloyalty, raw data was used and raw data was used for demographic and pharmacy characteristics. Univariate associations between variables were tested using point-biserial or Spearman’s correlation coefficients.

Sample size requirements

The sample size calculation was based on the sample size required to conduct confirmatory factor analyses (CFA) using the method of Kelly and Lai for the purposes of validation of translated survey (Kelley and Lai 2011). In the previously published validation study where pSQS, the Root mean Square of Approximation (RMSEA) was 0.085 (Grew et al. 2019). Therefore, it was estimated that for the measurement model including pSQS-A-SF, pPC and loyalty intentions to obtain a population root RMSEA of 0.08 [with 95% confidence interval of 0.04], with 42 degrees of freedom an adequate sample size would be approximately 210 participants.

Results

Translation

The English version was successfully translated and culturally adapted to Arabic language (supplement 1). The back translated version was reviewed by an expert in pharmacy whom suggested to reduce the response options from a 7-point to a 5-point Likert scale which was approved by the original authors. The expert also suggested adding a screening question in the beginning of the survey to include only participants using at least one POM.

Descriptive statistics

In this survey 260 individuals have been approached, three of them did not agree to participate and only 207 of participants obtained their medicine from a community pharmacy, thus completing the survey. The majority of respondents visited two community pharmacies to obtain their medications (38%), 53% of participants reported that they frequently visited the pharmacy they were rating (at least once a month) and 62% of participants obtained their medicine from the visited pharmacy. Chain pharmacies were reported by 90% of participants as the most common rated and visited type of pharmacy (Table 1). In addition, the majority of participants had no medical insurance (63.8%) and 95% of them used between one and five medications daily. Responses of “I do not Know” were considered missing data. Perceived service loyalty

Confirmatory factor analysis of perceived service quality and price competitiveness with loyalty intentions

The goodness of fit indices shows that the CFA measurement model for pSQS-A-SF, pPC and loyalty intentions showed evidence of model misspecification χ2 = 198, df = 41, p < 0.001, CFI = 0.933, SRMR = 0.063 and RMSEA = 0.137 (90% confidence interval = 0.118–0.156). A range of modifications to the model were attempted based on the Lagrange Multiplier Test for adding parameters. There were several cross loadings with HMA (health & medicines advice) to other items within the PSQ scale, in particular RQ (relationship quality) and HO (health outcomes). A decision was made to delete this item for three reasons: 1) The PSQ scale is derived from a latent construct, and so the CFA is specified as a reflective model, accordingly. The implications of this are that indicator items are interchangeable and can be added or deleted without affecting the underlying meaning of the scale; 2) the originators of the pSQS (CS & SC) were consulted and agreed that the underlying meaning of the 5-item revised pSQS-A-SF scale was not unduly compromised in the absence of HMA; and 3) Alternatively, while specifying covariation between error terms was possible, no single specification substantively improved model fit and multiple error covariances are not recommend (El-Den et al. 2020). Following deletion of HMA, fit improved, χ2 = 120, df = 32, p < 0.001, CFI = 0.961, SRMR = 0.046 and RMSEA = 0.116 (90% confidence interval = 0.095—0.139). While the RMSEA was not at target, no further modifications could be made that did not compromise meaning or statistical validity. A major reason for misspecification was covariation between individual items on the pSQS-A-SF scale and loyalty intentions.

CFA statistics are presented in Table 5 and the diagram is presented in Fig. 2. Overall, there was some evidence for convergent validity in that each of the indicators had standardised factor loadings > 0.5, the composite reliability was greater than the target 0.7, however the AVE was marginally below the target 50%. Confirmatory factor analysis

The correlation between pPC and pSQS-A-SF was 0.667, p < 0.001 and between pPC and loyalty intentions was 0.665, p < 0.001. There was an extremely high correlation between pSQS-A-SF and Loyalty Intentions of 0.987, p < 0.001 which is suggestive of a lack of discriminant validity between factors20. Both the AVE method and the nested modelling test failed to demonstrate discriminant validity.

Given the lack of discriminant validity between pSQS-A-SF and Loyalty Intentions, and the focus of investigating the interplay between pPC and pSQS-A-SF with behavioral loyalty in the SEM, a repeat CFA was performed including only pPC and pSQS-A-SF. The resulting fit was reasonable, χ2 = 34, df = 13, p < 0.001, CFI = 0.976, SRMR = 0.037 and RMSEA = 0.088 (90% confidence interval = 0.052—0.125). It is noted that the RMSEA is often high with small degrees of freedom (Shi et al. 2022).

Path model

A summary of the path model is that the path from pSQS-A-SF to patronage loyalty was weakly positive (β = 0.26, p = 0.023); the path from pSQS-A-SF to prescription loyalty was moderately positive (β = 0.41, p = 0.002); and the path from pSQS-A-SF to patronage disloyalty was negligible (β = 0.123, p = 0.349). The paths from pPC to patronage loyalty was negligible (β = −0.069, p = 0.537), the path from pPC to prescription loyalty was negligible (β = −0.137, p = 0.340) and the path from pPC to patronage disloyalty was negligible (β = −0.22, p = 0.101). There was just one path from the demographic/pharmacy variables for which the p < 0.05, which was the path from Private Insurance to patronage loyalty which was weakly positive (β = 0.156 p = 0.022). The model predicted 8.2% of the variation in patronage loyalty, 13.1% of the variation in prescription loyalty and 2.9% of the variation in patronage disloyalty.

Discussion

The perceived service quality (pSQ) in community pharmacy short form scale and other scales measuring related constructs and behaviours were translated to Arabic in collaboration with the original authors. The relationship between pSQ, perceptions of price competitiveness and loyalty to rated pharmacies were also explored. The majority of approached participants rated the service quality of community pharmacy as high, have a low level of agreement with perception of price competitiveness, while reporting a high level of loyalty intentions. This work indicates also that pSQ plays a meaningful role in shaping pharmacy loyalty. Indeed, pSQ overlaps so closely with loyalty intentions that measurement of associations between the two constructs using these scales in this setting is not recommended due to a lack of discriminant validity. In relation to the predictive potential of the pSQ scale in driving patronage loyalty, high scores of pSQS-A-SF scale were associated with participants reporting a small but significantly higher visit frequency to the rated pharmacy and higher likelihood of getting their prescriptions dispensed at that pharmacy. Alternatively, perceptions of price competitiveness has no meaningful association with patronage loyalty.

The presence of medical insurance was associated with patronage loyalty, this suggests that individuals with medical insurance may be more inclined to remain loyal to pharmacies that offer high-quality services and competitive pricing, as these factors align with their healthcare needs and financial considerations. These findings align with a recent study by Carter et al,. (2023) which explored the relative effects of consumers' perceptions of service quality and price competitiveness on patronage loyalty in community pharmacies (Carter et al. 2023). While the study did not directly examine the presence of medical insurance, it highlighted that factors such as service quality and price competitiveness significantly influence patronage loyalty (Carter et al. 2023). The observed negative association between chain pharmacies and pPC highlights a potentially important finding; however, the factors underlying this relationship were not explored and should be examined in future research. Moreover, prescription loyalty was positively associated with chain pharmacy, this may be because the consumer trusts this chain pharmacy since they have strong presence in the market. In a recent study investigated factors affecting consumer loyalty to pharmacy chains in Vietnam, it was found that pharmacy chain brands, price, facilities, convenience, and employee knowledge positively influenced loyalty (Huong et al. 2025). However, it is important to note that while price was identified as a factor, the study did not explicitly state whether chain pharmacies were perceived as more expensive. Nevertheless, the association between price and loyalty suggests that consumers may associate higher prices with better service or quality in chain pharmacies. High service quality of community pharmacy has a positive impact on loyalty intentions and medication adherence as reported previously by (Carter et al. 2021).

In Italy, a study to identify determinants of community pharmacy loyalty found that both trust in community pharmacists and customer satisfaction affected loyalty positively (Castaldo et al. 2016).

Overall, the model explains a small portion of the variation in the loyalty and disloyalty outcomes, with prescription loyalty showing the strongest explained variance.

In Saudi Arabia, citizens receive medications free of charge at government hospitals. In case the medication is not available or in case of refill, the citizen forwarded to any community pharmacy to get the medicine freely. The community pharmacies collaborate with ministry of health through a program called “Wasfaty”, in which patients’ information can be retrieved automatically and medication dispensed. The Wasfaty program offers a good opportunity to help community pharmacies to review their services and improve their quality to attract such patients and so improve loyalty. Community pharmacies need loyal patient so will not lose their custom, in addition community pharmacies need to meet the medical and non-medical needs of their customers.

The translation process in this paper adhered to the International Society for Pharmacoeconomics and Outcomes Research (ISPOR) principles of Good Practice for the translation and Cultural Adaptation of patient-reported outcomes (PRO) measures (Wild et al. 2005). The English to Arabic translation was found to be easy to interpret by the participants involved in the validation process and was culturally adapted, with adequate assessment of its Psychometric properties. However certain limitation of this work still exists due to the small number of participants in the pilot testing. This limitation will be addressed in future translation of the comprehensive scale (pSQS-A). In addition, as a convenience sampling method was used, potential selection bias should be considered, as it may affect the external validity and generalizability of this study.

In this study, the measurement of pSQS-A-SF scale required one modification from the English version. One of the six items (HMA), which measures the extent to which patients believe that they are supported with prescriptions, was deleted due to measurement issues. As explained in the Results section, deletion of this item was supported because perceived service quality is a latent construct and therefore to some extent items can be added or deleted without changing the meaning of the scale (El-Den et al. 2020). It is recommended that future research should aim to translate the comprehensive 19-item, multidimensional scale (pSQS) to Arabic. This could allow for the inclusion of one of the other two HMA sub-scale items to replace the one used in this study. It is also recommended to conduct more comprehensive investigation of the drivers of consumer loyalty within the Saudi community pharmacy setting. While initial findings suggest that factors such as service quality and price competitiveness play a role, a broader exploration is needed to fully understand the range and impact of these drivers. Potential areas of focus include service-related factors (e.g., prompt and professional service), economic considerations (e.g., competitive pricing for prescription and over-the-counter products), and convenience aspects such as location accessibility and opening hours. Additionally, store environment, consumer privacy, and confidentiality practices may significantly influence loyalty but remain underexplored. By examining these dimensions in greater depth, future studies can provide clearer insights into how community pharmacies can effectively foster long-term patient loyalty.

Conclusion

This study translated the pSQS-SF into Arabic (pSQS-A-SF) and assessed the relationship between perceived service quality, price competitiveness, loyalty intentions and patronage behaviours. The pSQS-A-SF has good internal consistency but explains less than the desired 50% average variance extracted. Future work is required to improve the % variance extracted, potentially through the inclusion of more items with the translation and psychometric evaluation of the comprehensive pSQS-A scale. Further research should strengthen validation and assess performance across settings and subgroups.
