Machine learning-based prediction of global solid waste generation and composition
Authors: Ajaya Subedi, Sahil Shrestha, Santosh Giri, Shukra Raj Paudel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe rapid growth of municipal solid waste (MSW) driven by urbanization, population expansion, and economic development has emerged as a critical global environmental challenge, thereby requiring effective planning and resource management. However, accurate prediction of MSW generation and composition—which is essential for effective governance—remains hindered by inconsistent and highly heterogeneous global data. To address this gap, this study proposes a data-driven framework using multi-linear regression (MLR) and artificial neural networks (ANN) to forecast MSW across 217 countries. These models incorporate socioeconomic and demographic parameters, including GDP, population, literacy rate, urbanization, and household size. The results indicated that ANN outperformed MLR in terms of predictive accuracy, achieving R² of 0.94 for total MSW generation, compared to ~ 0.57 for MLR and ~ 0.68 reported in the existing global model. While prediction of waste generation exhibited strong accuracy, composition prediction remained challenging (R² up to 0.15), indicating the influence of unaccounted behavioral and regional factors. Despite limitations in accuracy due to data heterogeneity and compositional complexity, the findings of this study can support policymakers and planners in enhancing waste management strategies, optimizing resource recovery, and enabling data-driven decision-making toward sustainable and circular waste management systems aligned with the Sustainable Development Goals.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-56324-9.Keywords: Municipal solid waste, Machine learning, Waste prediction modeling, Artificial neural network (ANN), Multi-linear regression (MLR)Subject terms: Environmental sciences, Environmental social sciences, Environmental studies, Geography, Geography

Introduction

Municipal solid waste (MSW) management has become an increasingly pressing global concern, closely associated with urbanization, economic growth, and environmental sustainability1,2. Driven by population growth, a rising proportion of elderly population3, and economic expansion, MSW generation is expected to increase substantially4,5, thereby imposing considerable environmental and financial pressure for municipalities worldwide6. In 2018, the global MSW production was estimated to be around 2 billion tons per year and is projected to reach 3.4 billion tons by 20506. To address such growing waste challenge, a range of advanced and traditional treatment methods—including mechanical-biological treatment (MBT), thermochemical (e.g., incineration, pyrolysis, gasification), physicochemical (e.g., hydrothermal treatment), and biological processes (e.g., composting and anaerobic digestion), often collectively referred to as ‘waste-to-X’ technologies, which enable recovery of energy, fuels, chemicals, and materials—have emerged, promoting resource recovery and minimizing environmental impacts. Traditionally, landfilling and open dumping remain the dominant disposal methods often prioritized due to their lower operational costs however, their limited emphasis on resource recovery and waste valorization6, coupled with the fact that MSW landfills constitute the third-largest anthropogenic source of methane (CH₄) emissions7, make them incompatible with long-term sustainability goals.

Furthermore, these waste treatment techniques pose considerable environmental risks. For instance, variations in the composition of organic waste during incineration may lead to uneven combustion and inconsistent thermal behavior, ultimately reducing process efficiency8. As a result, harmful emissions such as sulfur oxides (SOₓ), nitrogen oxides (NOₓ), and dioxins are released, posing serious risks to human health and environmental quality9,10, across both developed and developing regions. Similarly, uncontrolled dumping near water bodies and landfill sites could further result in extensive groundwater and surface water contamination worldwide, introducing a variety of dissolved organic and inorganic pollutants, and degrading aquatic ecosystems11. Therefore, managing the growing MSW stream presents significant environmental, economic, and public health challenges. In parallel with the global shift toward circular economy principles, solid waste management systems (SWMS) must adapt to handle increasing waste volumes more efficiently12 while maximizing resource recovery and reducing both environmental impacts and economic expenses13,14. In this regard, among the several advanced solutions, Waste-to-Energy (WtE) technologies have emerged as a promising alternative energy pathway15, with the estimated 2.0 billion tons of MSW having an energy potential of approximately 2.247 quadrillion kilojoules (kJ)16. This approach not only utilizes the latent energy potential of waste streams but also enhances resource efficiency and reduces environmental burdens while generating economic value17,18.

Despite such conceptual advancements, the waste sector continues to face emerging challenges, including declining international demand for recyclables and the limited availability of environmentally sustainable treatment technologies, which necessitates strengthening sustainable local waste valorization capacities19,20. Given these challenges, global sustainability frameworks emphasize the need for strategic policy interventions and technological innovations in waste management aligning with global sustainability initiatives, including the United Nations Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs), particularly SDG 11 (Sustainable Cities and Communities), SDG 12 (Responsible Consumption and Production), and SDG 3 (Good Health and Well-being), all of which are closely linked to effective waste management systems21.

However, the successful implementation of these strategies depends on a comprehensive understanding of MSW generation, composition, and management practices22, which requires reliable and detailed data on waste generation rates, material composition, seasonal variations, collection efficiency, and disposal methods. Nevertheless, many administrative areas, particularly in developing nations, lack consistent and high-quality datasets on waste generation and composition due to irregular data collection practices, the presence of informal waste sectors, and limited technological capacity. These deficiencies restrict the development of evidence-based policies and impede accurate forecasting of waste trends23,24. To address these challenges, numerous studies have explored the socioeconomic and environmental factors affecting MSW generation, identifying key variables such as GDP, population size, urbanization rates, household size, literacy levels, and economic activity25,26. Higher GDP levels, for instance, correlate with increased waste generation, particularly of non-biodegradable materials such as plastics and e-waste6,27. Despite these insights, effective policy formulation still depends on robust predictive models, as MSW generation is governed by complex and nonlinear interactions among multiple variables28. Traditional statistical models, including regression analysis and time-series forecasting, often struggle to capture those non-linear and multi-dimensional interactions29,30. These models typically assume fixed relationships, leading to inaccuracies when applied across diverse socioeconomic and environmental contexts. Empirical studies highlight these challenges, as demonstrated in a study conducted in a Mexican city26, where a regression analysis model was employed to examine the relationship between socioeconomic factors (education and income) and per capita waste generation. Despite a localized survey, the model exhibited low predictive accuracy (R² ~ 0.51), likely due to insufficient predictor variables and potential model misspecification. Another study employing fixed-effect and Feasible Generalized Least Squares (FGLS) models in OECD countries assessed the impact of household (HH) size and urban population on MSW generation31. Yet again, the study reported relatively low predictive capabilities (R² as low as 0.337), further highlighting the limitations of conventional econometric methods in capturing region-specific and nonlinear waste generation dynamics.

In response, Machine Learning (ML) techniques have gained increasing attention as effective alternatives, leveraging complex datasets and advanced computational techniques to enhance predictive accuracy32. Moreover, the computational cost of ML model training is very low compared to the potential benefits they provide33. Unlike traditional statistical models, ML techniques such as artificial neural networks (ANN), Support Vector Machine (SVM), and Gaussian Process Regression (GPR) can capture complex nonlinear relationships and interactions within high-dimensional datasets. Among these, ANNs are particularly notable due to their self-learning capability and strong performance in modeling nonlinear systems34. For example, a study conducted in Mashhad, Iran35, utilized weekly MSW data collected from 2004 to 2007 to develop a Feedforward ANN model. While the model performed well during training (R² = 0.94), its lower testing accuracy (R² = 0.72) indicates potential overfitting or limited generalization capacity, possibly due to an imbalanced dataset or suboptimal hyper-parameter tuning. In contrast, studies leveraging extensive, long-term datasets and advanced ML methodologies have demonstrated significantly higher predictive accuracy. A study in Turkey, which applied GPR with Bayesian Optimization to MSW data spanning from 1994 to 2017, successfully integrated multiple socioeconomic indicators, resulting in exceptionally high R² values (> 0.98 in both training and testing36. Furthermore, an investigation into MSW generation trends in Logan City, Australia37, demonstrated that hybrid approaches, such as Adaptive Neuro-Fuzzy Inference Systems (ANFIS) have demonstrated superior predictive accuracy (R² = 0.98) in certain contexts, outperforming standalone ANN (R² = 0.46) and SVM (R² = 0.71) models, emphasizing the importance of adaptive and hybrid modeling frameworks. Despite growing interest in ML-based waste prediction models, existing research remains fragmented. The challenges persist in model generalization as most studies have been conducted at localized scales33,38, while existing broader models often rely on a narrow set of predictor variables, resulting in suboptimal performance39.

Furthermore, while previous research has largely focused on total MSW generation, relatively few studies have attempted to predict waste composition, and which is critical for optimizing recycling and waste diversion strategies39. Among them, a study in 2025 from Ghana40 applied Linear Regression (LR), Random Forest (RF), and Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) models to predict solid waste generation and composition. Although the models examined the relationships and importance of input variables with respect to waste composition, it did not explicitly develop a dedicated predictive model for composition estimation. Another study from city of Johannesburg41 used meteorological variables such as temperature, humidity, and wind speed to predict MSW composition and seasonal variation using ANN models. The results showed that a single hidden-layer ANN can satisfactorily estimate organic, paper, plastic, and textile waste fractions based on these inputs. However, existing research on composition prediction remains largely limited to city-level case studies, and robust generalized model with broad applicability and validation are still lacking. This clearly highlights a significant research gap in the field.

Despite considerable progress in MSW prediction modeling, notable limitations remain. Most research is geographically constrained, limiting applicability across diverse contexts. Additionally, earlier global-scale models primarily rely on traditional statistical approaches or limited ML frameworks with a narrow set of predictor variables, often resulting in moderate predictive performance. Moreover, the majority of studies focus exclusively on total waste generation (Supplementary Table S1), with limited attention given to waste composition, which is essential for designing effective recycling and resource recovery strategies. In contrast, this study integrates a broader set of socioeconomic and demographic indicators within both linear and nonlinear modeling frameworks and evaluates their applicability at a global scale. Importantly, this work attempts to extend prediction beyond total waste generation to include compositional fractions, thereby addressing a critical gap in literature, although with acknowledged limitations in predictive performance.

This study offers several advantages, including the use of a globally representative dataset, the integration of multiple socioeconomic predictors, and the application of interpretable modeling approaches, typically Multi-Linear Regression (MLR) and ANN. The selection of both models, MLR and ANN was driven by extensive literature review along with their beneficial characteristics in prediction modeling (Supplementary Table S1). Amid several challenges, this study aims to develop a global ML-based framework for predicting MSW generation and composition. First, a comprehensive global dataset comprising 217 countries was compiled and preprocessed to ensure consistency and reliability. Next, relevant socioeconomic and demographic variables were selected based on literature and correlation analysis. Subsequently, MLR and ANN models were developed and optimized through appropriate training and validation techniques. The models were then evaluated using standard performance metrics, and a comparative analysis was conducted to assess their predictive capabilities. Finally, the results were interpreted to provide insights for evidence-based policy formulation and sustainable waste management planning.

Data and methodology

This study follows a systematic and well-defined framework comprising data collection, preprocessing, model development, validation, and evaluation to develop a robust ML-based prediction model for global solid waste generation and composition. The methodology, depicted in Fig. 1, ensures the integration of geographically diverse datasets from 217 countries, the application of suitable ML algorithms, and a comprehensive assessment of model performance using standard evaluation metrics.

Data collection and preprocessing

Data sources and variables

The dataset utilized in this study consists of country-level information for the year 2018 on MSW generation and composition6 from 217 countries, along with key socioeconomic and demographic indicators (Supplementary Table S2). Data were compiled from multiple reliable sources, including the World Bank data42 and World Population Review43. Based on extensive literature review and data availability, the selected input variables include GDP, population, GDP per capita, literacy rate, urban population percentage, and HH size44–46. While GDP and GDP per capita are highly correlated, only one of them was used in the MLR model to avoid multicollinearity. However, in the ANN model, both variables were retained since ANN architectures are generally more resilient to multicollinearity and can still effectively learn nonlinear relationships without significant degradation in the performance. The output variables comprise Total MSW generation (tons/year) and the percentage composition of key waste fractions, including organic waste, plastic, paper, metal, glass, and electronic waste.

Data preprocessing and feature selection

Data preprocessing represents a critical step in the ML workflow; wherein raw datasets are systematically transformed into structured formats suitable for analysis. This process is essential, as unprocessed data often contains irrelevant, redundant, or noisy information that can compromise model performance and accuracy, particularly when dealing with diverse datasets47–49. Given the diversity in data sources and the wide range of parameter values, comprehensive preprocessing procedures were implemented to ensure data quality and consistency. As detailed in Section S4 of Supplementary Information, missing values across input parameters were primarily addressed using mean imputation. However, for HH size—which had a relatively high proportion of missing data—a regression-based estimation approach was employed. In contrast, rows with missing output values were excluded from the analysis to maintain the integrity of supervised learning, as these outputs represent the ground truth. To address scale disparities among variables, Z-score normalization was applied across all input features, ensuring uniformity and reducing bias caused by large numerical differences. Furthermore, correlation analysis (Fig. 4) was performed to evaluate the relationships between predictors and MSW generation, enabling the selection of only statistically significant features for model training50.

Correlation matrix outlining the interdependencies among various input and output variables, highlighting the significance of feature selection.

Model development and evaluation

Model selection

Model selection was guided by literature review, theoretical considerations (Supplementary Table S1), and dataset characteristics, particularly the high variability and heterogeneity observed in the global dataset (Supplementary Table S2), which suggests the presence of both linear and nonlinear relationships. MLR was selected as a baseline model due to its high interpretability39 and ability to explicitly quantify linear relationships between socioeconomic predictors and MSW generation. This facilitates clear understanding of the relative importance of key socioeconomic variables, which is valuable for policy-related interpretations. ANN, on the other hand, were adopted to capture the complex, nonlinear interactions inherent in global waste systems as validated in several previous studies51–53. Given the multivariate and non-uniform nature of the dataset, ANN provides a flexible framework capable of learning higher-order relationships that cannot be represented by linear models. As illustrated from Supplementary Table S1, together, MLR and ANN form a complementary modeling framework, balancing interpretability and predictive capability for the available global dataset, and are therefore well-suited for global-scale MSW estimation.

Model training and optimization

The Google Colab environment was used for model development in the Python programming language, incorporating various libraries for specific functions (Supplementary Table S4). For MLR model development, the dataset was randomly divided into training and testing subsets, with an 80/20 split adopted due to its favorable performance in preliminary assessments. However, recent studies indicate that k-fold validation is effective in reducing data uncertainties and mitigating overfitting concerns54. Hence, together with the train/test split, a 5-fold cross-validation approach was also implemented for the ANN model, ensuring robust evaluation across multiple iterations. Following the initial model training, optimization techniques were applied to enhance model performances. For MLR, different combinations of predictors were evaluated to identify the most significant variables for each output. For ANN, hyperparameter tuning was performed using GridSearchCV to optimize the ANN model by adjusting parameters such as the number of hidden layers, neurons per layer, activation functions, learning rates, and dropout regularization techniques (Fig. 2 and Supplementary Table S5 and S6). Separate ANN architectures were developed for generation and composition prediction, as joint optimization of all outputs resulted in reduced predictive performance, indicating increased model complexity.

Results and discussion

Overview of waste generation, composition and influencing factors

At the outset, a comprehensive assessment of global waste composition and the interrelationships among key influencing variables— including GDP, population, literacy rate, urban population percentage, and HH size, —was conducted across 217 countries. The findings revealed considerable variability in waste generation patterns as a function of these determinants. As illustrated in Fig. 3, organic waste emerges as the dominant fraction worldwide. Developing nations such as Bangladesh (80.58%) and Ethiopia (87.6%) exhibit a strong predominance of organic waste, likely influenced by dietary patterns and prevailing waste handling practices. Although the current data indicates that the paper and plastic fraction is relatively lower compared to organic waste, their volumes are expected to increase markedly in the near future driven by the expanding global consumption of plastic-based materials56. Moreover, hold particular importance due to their high recyclability and potential for paving the circular economy pathway57. The growing prevalence of e-waste in technologically advanced nations like Singapore (5.35%) and Australia (4.3%) further highlights a growing global trend in electronic waste generation58.

Average global MSW composition, demonstrating organic waste as the dominant fraction followed by papers, and plastics6,42,43.

Furthermore, the relationships between input and output variables were examined using correlation analysis (Fig. 4), which identified strong positive associations between GDP and population with the generation of total MSW tons/year, aligning with earlier research30,59,60 and reinforcing the link between economic growth and waste generation61,62. Conversely, variables such as GDP per capita and literacy rate demonstrated comparatively weaker correlations with total waste quantities, but exerted a notable influence on waste composition. Organic waste proportion exhibited a negative correlation with economic indicators, suggesting a transition toward inorganic waste streams in higher-income regions due to industrialization and urbanization.

The data preprocessing phase played a crucial role in enhancing dataset reliability and interpretability. Missing value imputation ensured dataset completeness, thereby reducing potential bias and instability. Additionally, correlation-based feature evaluation (Fig. 4) facilitated the identification of key predictors and their relative influence on target variables, improving both model efficiency and interpretability by focusing on the most relevant socioeconomic drivers.

Model development and evaluation

As illustrated in Fig. 1, the preprocessed data — following imputation, standardization, and feature selection— was fed into the MLR and ANN models for analysis. These steps significantly contributed to model stability and performance. In particular, feature scaling ensured numerical consistency across variables, which is essential for ANN convergence, while the removal of redundant or weakly correlated predictors reduced noise in the input space. Model performance was evaluated using metrics such as training versus validation loss, MSE and R². Subsequently, the MLR model was refined through optimal predictor selection, whereas the ANN model was enhanced via hyperparameter optimization techniques. After the optimization, the ANN model demonstrated substantially superior predictive performance compared to MLR.

MLR model

The MLR model was initially developed as a baseline approach to benchmark more advanced models for predicting MSW generation and composition. The coefficients for regression equations interrelating different variables as obtained from the model have been presented in Supplementary Table S8. Initially, a single MLR model was developed, yielding moderate predictive accuracy for total MSW tons/year (R² = 0.57) and MSW tons/capita/year (R² = 0.62). However, its performance deteriorated significantly for compositional predictions, with Organic% (R² = 0.18), and Paper% (R² = 0.39), while Plastic%, Metal%, Glass%, and E-waste% exhibited very low predictive capability (R² < 0.1). To improve accuracy, an optimized model was developed using targeted predictor selection (Supplementary Table S9). For instance, total MSW generation (tons/year) is found to be mostly influenced by population, GDP per capita, urban population %, and HH size. This aligns with the expectation that larger populations and highly urbanized regions generate more waste due to intensified consumption and economic activity63. The inclusion of HH size further suggests that waste production patterns vary from one household to another, potentially due to differences in consumption behaviors in smaller versus larger households. Interestingly, MSW tons/capita/year is driven primarily by GDP per capita and urban population %, rather than total population, indicating that economic affluence drives higher individual consumption and waste production, particularly in developing economies transitioning toward consumer-based systems64. Meanwhile, urban areas with higher incomes and more disposable goods tend to generate more waste per capita due to lifestyle differences and increased packaging waste from commercial products.

While the predictor selection improved R² values for some variables—with an increase in Organic% to 0.37 and MSW tons/capita/year to 0.64— overall predictive capability for composition remained limited. Regarding waste composition, the relationship between E-waste % and GDP per capita is particularly noteworthy, reinforcing concerns that economic growth accelerates electronic consumption and disposal rates, leading to increasing challenges in e-waste management65. Additionally, literacy rate showed unexpected correlations with metal and glass waste fractions, possibly reflecting higher consumption patterns or improved reporting accuracy in more educated populations66,67.

The improvement observed after predictor selection further highlights the importance of feature engineering. By eliminating less relevant variables and retaining only statistically significant predictors for each output, the optimized MLR model achieved better performance and interpretability. Despite the valuable insights about affecting parameters, the results also highlight the limitations of MLR in modeling non-linear relationships. The findings advocate that incorporating additional explanatory variables or switching to more advanced ML models, such as ANNs, is necessary for enhanced predictive performance.

ANN model

Although the optimized predictor selection highlighted a strong relationship between socioeconomic factors and both the quantity and composition of MSW, the poor performance of MLR models necessitates the development of the ANN model. The results from various ANN models for predicting both the generation and composition of MSW demonstrate the complexity and challenges inherent in modeling waste-related variables. The two deep neural network models—M1 and M2 (Table 1)—for total MSW prediction were constructed to evaluate the effect of validation strategy on model generalization and robustness. Both of them exhibited exceptional performance, with R² values reaching as high as 0.95 during training and 0.90 to 0.94 during testing for the M1 model and 0.94 for the M2 model with k-fold validation which is significantly higher than the only previous global study (R2 = 0.61–0.68) that employed MLR and Additive Model (AM)39.

A further comprehensive evaluation of the M2 model using multiple statistical indicators—including MSE, RMSE, MAE, MAPE, and R²—is presented in Supplementary Table S10. The results demonstrate consistently high predictive accuracy across all folds, with R² values ranging from 0.91 to 0.99, confirming the robustness and generalization capability of the model. The relatively low error magnitudes across folds further indicate stable model performance despite the heterogeneity of the dataset. The training vs. validation loss plot (Fig. 5) demonstrates good model convergence without significant overfitting, suggesting that the model effectively captures the underlying patterns in total MSW generation. The minimal gap between training and testing R² values of the M1 model (Table 1 and Supplementary Table S7) plus the training vs. validation loss plot of the M2 model (Fig. 5) indicates that no overfitting was observed, and the selected hyperparameters—were well-suited to the dataset68. These highlight the power of deep learning in managing complex, multivariate datasets, making it highly effective for predicting total MSW generation.

The Generation Prediction Model (M3), designed to predict both MSW tons per year per capita and total MSW tons per year, yielded somewhat lower performance with R² values up to 0.85 for the training phase and ranging from 0.73 to 0.84 for the testing phase. The discrepancy between these values points to mild overfitting, indicating that the model performed better on the training set than on unseen data68. This indicates that the model might have captured data-specific patterns that don’t generalize well, likely due to the heterogeneity in waste generation patterns across different demographic and economic groups69. The simultaneous prediction of multiple output variables added to the complexity of the model, making optimization and hyperparameter tuning challenging70. The difficulty in achieving optimal predictive accuracy for both total and per capita waste generation simultaneously highlights the need for tailored models for different types of waste prediction. Overall, these findings highlight the effectiveness of ANN—particularly the M2 model—in modeling complex, multivariate relationships in global MSW generation, demonstrating its superiority over traditional linear approaches for this application.

In contrast, the Composition Prediction Model (M4) demonstrated considerably weaker performance, with R² values only up to 0.15. This low explanatory power indicates that the model struggles to account for the variance in MSW composition, underscoring the challenges of predicting the proportions of waste types such as organic, paper, plastic, metal, glass, and e-waste using general socioeconomic and demographic predictors solely. The complexity of MSW composition, influenced by factors such as cultural behavior, seasonal variations, and recycling infrastructure, is not easily captured by broad, aggregated predictors71,72. Furthermore, the attempt to optimize a single model for multiple waste components may have led to poor predictive accuracy due to the unique relationships each waste fraction has with the input variables70. These results indicate that the M4 model, in its current form, is not suitable for practical or field-level applications and its contribution is future research directions rather than a validated predictive model. These findings also highlight an important limitation of current global datasets and modeling approaches: accurate prediction of MSW composition requires more granular, high-resolution, and domain-specific input variables. Rather than representing a model deficiency alone, the results underscore a broader data gap in global waste characterization. Future research should therefore focus on incorporating additional predictors and advanced techniques such as ensemble learning architectures to better capture the complexity of waste composition69 and improve model performance.

Comparison between MLR and ANN

A comparison analysis (Table 2) clearly demonstrates the superiority of ANN models over MLR, aligning with the results of previous studies73. The Total MSW prediction (tons/year) shows a substantial difference in predictive performance. The ANN model achieved an R² value between 0.90 and 0.94 (for M1) and 0.94 (for M2), indicating that the model explains over 90% of the variance in total MSW generation. In contrast, the MLR model only explains about 57% of the variance, which significantly limits its ability to predict the actual waste generation values. This stark contrast highlights the deep learning model’s capacity to capture the complex, non-linear relationships among predictors, which MLR, with its linear assumptions, fails to account for effectively55.

Real world implications of model’s result

The optimized model provides valuable insights into the real-world behavior of MSW generation drivers. To illustrate these effects, the response curves in Fig. 6 depict the intricate nonlinear relationships between the factors and total MSW generation, as predicted by the M2 ANN model. Each curve isolates the effect of a single predictor while holding other variables at their mean values, enabling a focused analysis of their influence.

Predicted total MSW generation as a function of (a) GDP, (b) population, (c) literacy rate, (d) household size, and (e) urban population percentage.

GDP and population exhibit strong positive linear relationships, reinforcing their role as primary drivers of waste generation. Higher GDP correlates with increased economic activity, consumption, and material use, while population growth inherently drives more waste production. These findings align with existing waste management literature, where economic and demographic expansion are primary waste generation drivers63. Conversely, urban population percentage follows a nonlinear pattern, suggesting a compounding effect. At lower urbanization levels, waste generation increases gradually, likely due to residual rural consumption behaviors. However, as urbanization intensifies, waste production accelerates, reflecting higher economic activity and consumer-oriented lifestyles.

The literacy rate demonstrates a non-monotonic trend: moderate to high literacy levels (about 30 to 85%) correspond to reduced waste generation, potentially due to greater environmental awareness and sustainable consumption67. However, at higher literacy levels, rising economic prosperity and purchasing power may offset these effects, leading to increased consumption and waste66. Furthermore, household size exhibits a U-shaped relationship with MSW generation. Initially, larger households benefit from economies of scale, reducing per capita waste. Beyond a threshold (4–6 individuals), total waste generation rises, possibly due to increased consumption diversity and absolute waste volume39. These response curves underscore the intricate interplay of socioeconomic and demographic factors in waste generation. Understanding these interactions is critical for designing effective waste management strategies that address both linear growth drivers and nonlinear mitigating factors.

Furthermore, the developed ANN framework—particularly the M2 model—has strong potential for real-world deployment in MSW management systems. In practical applications, municipalities can utilize readily available socioeconomic and demographic data (e.g., population, GDP per capita, urbanization rate, and household size) as model inputs to estimate total waste generation. These predictions can be integrated into decision-support systems or urban management platforms (e.g., GIS-based tools or smart city dashboards) to support infrastructure planning, including landfill capacity estimation, transfer station design, and optimization of waste collection and transportation systems.

For forward-looking applications, the model can be extended to forecast future MSW generation, provided that key input variables are projected using appropriate economic and demographic models. This enables scenario-based planning, allowing policymakers to evaluate the impacts of urban growth, economic development, and policy interventions on future waste generation. Such integration supports data-driven decision-making for long-term sustainability planning and resource allocation. However, this study highlights that constructing a universal global model for MSW composition remains challenging due to regional heterogeneity, suggesting that future research should focus on region-specific or hybrid modeling approaches for improved prediction accuracy.

Limitations and future research needs

Despite the strong performance of the ANN models in predicting total MSW generation, several limitations associated with the model should be acknowledged. The study relies on a single-year cross-sectional dataset, limiting its ability to capture temporal variations in waste generation patterns. Moreover, the use of aggregated national-level data may mask regional and local variability in waste generation and composition—shaped by behavioral, cultural, seasonal, and policy-related factors. Although multiple evaluation metrics were employed, explicit confidence intervals and advanced uncertainty quantification methods were not incorporated. In addition, the composition prediction model (M4) demonstrated weak predictive performance, indicating that generalized socioeconomic and demographic variables alone are insufficient to accurately predict complex waste fractions at the global scale. The local sensitivity analysis conducted in this study also does not fully capture synergistic interactions among multiple predictors.

These limitations thus highlight several priorities for future research. First of all, proper data collection on waste generation and composition should be prioritized globally. That could enable future studies to incorporate longitudinal and region-specific datasets, together with more granular predictors, such as consumption behavior, recycling practices, policy interventions, and waste management infrastructure. Advanced uncertainty quantification, interaction analysis, and hybrid or ensemble ML approaches should also be explored to improve model robustness and predictive capability. Overall, the findings suggest that accurate MSW composition prediction may require region-specific or hierarchical modeling frameworks rather than a single universal global model.

Summary and conclusions

The sharp increase in MSW has become a key global environmental concern in recent years. In this regard, accurately predicting both the generation and composition of MSW critical for implementing effective and sustainable waste management strategies. This study developed a global predictive model capable of estimating solid waste generation and evaluating its performance against existing modeling approaches. To achieve this, an ANN model was developed and compared with the existing MLR and AM global models using a diverse set of socioeconomic and demographic input variables. The integration of these variables highlighted the multifaceted nature of MSW generation and composition, reinforcing the need for comprehensive data collection and analysis frameworks. The findings demonstrated that the ANN model outperformed conventional approaches, achieving a higher level of accuracy (R2 = 0.94) and effectively capturing nonlinear relationships among key predictors. The study also highlighted the comparative study between MLR and ANN, underscoring the significance of ANN-based predictive modeling in waste management planning and offering a data-driven approach that enhances prediction accuracy compared to conventional regression techniques. However, due to the lack of sufficient and high-quality global datasets, accurate prediction of waste composition still remains a significant challenge—despite the model’s promising performance—underscoring the dependence of ML models on historical data. Future studies, thus, should prioritize establishing standardized data collection frameworks; while incorporating additional influencing factors, such as seasonal variations, collection efficiency, behavioral factors, policy interventions, regional variations, economic shocks, and technological advancements. Additionally, the potential of hybrid modeling approaches that combine ANN with optimization algorithms could further refine predictive accuracy and interpretability. Strengthening global collaboration and leveraging emerging technologies, such as AI-driven automation, cloud computing, and decentralized learning, will be crucial in developing a strong foundation for more generalized and scalable future waste prediction models, ultimately supporting sustainable waste management strategies worldwide.
