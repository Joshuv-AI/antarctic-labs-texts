Giant galactocele in an adult female: a rare presentation and surgical management

Introduction

Galactocele, occasionally termed lactocele, is a benign milk containing cyst that primarily arises due to a persistent obstruction of the lactiferous duct [1–4]. They are mostly detected during lactation or in the late third trimester of pregnancy. However, in rare cases, the condition may occur after breastfeeding has stopped, as milk is retained and becomes stagnant within the lactiferous ducts [5–7]. Although rare, it may also occur in infants and male patients [8–12]. Clinically, the mass is usually firm, fairly non-tender and discrete, and often gives the impression of a solid tumor [13]. The first-line imaging technique is ultrasound (US), which must be complemented by mammography and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) if there are any clinical or US suspicious signs like indistinct margin, posterior acoustic shadowing, internal vascularity and solid components [14]. A Galactocele can have a wide range of sonographic appearances and can mimic other lesions of the breast, both benign and malignant. The clues to the diagnosis are recent childbirth and lactation and, on sonography, the presence of a well-defined cystic lesion with posterior acoustic enhancement [15]. Here, we present a giant galactocele masquerading as a solid mass, similar to a likely phyllodes tumor on physical examination, and mimicking a complex breast cyst on ultrasound examination. The diagnosis of a massive galactocele in this case was confirmed after fine needle aspiration, which demonstrated milky fluid.

Case presentation

A 34-year-old Para 3 woman who stopped breastfeeding 5 years ago was presented with swelling in her right breast that has been slowly growing for over 1year. The mass was initially small and painless but has been progressively enlarging throughout this period, attaining its current massive size and causing discomfort for the patient. She has been on combined oral contraceptives for the past 2 years, but there is no history of trauma to the area or prior surgery. No signs and symptoms of hyperprolactinemia were identified. On physical examination, it has a boss elated surface and was moderately firm to touch but does not have any associated signs such as redness, warmth, or change in overlying skin and ulcer. It measures approximately 12 × 15 cm in size (Fig. 1). No tenderness elicited during palpation. It is freely mobile and does not appear fixed to underlying structures. No clinically significant lymphadenopathy palpated in the axillary region. Vital signs are within normal limits and other systems examinations are unremarkable. Complete blood count and prolactin level are within normal range.

Shows a firm mass in the right breast at upper outer quadrant extending to the retro-areolar region. It appears mobile and non-tender. The overlying skin was intact with no evidence of attachment to adjacent structures

Ultrasound examination using sonoscape P20 machine initially, high frequency linear probe but later shifted to low frequency curvilinear probe due to the size of the mass, reveals a massive, well-defined cystic mass measuring 15 × 12 × 10 cm in size with homogenous low-level echodebris within it with thin septa in the center (Fig. 2). No, other solid components or irregularities at the margins were identified. No significant vascularity was noted on color Doppler imaging and no suspicious features like mural nodule and solid component with vascularity were observed. Posterior acoustic enhancement is noted, indicating a fluid-filled structure. No evidence of intramammary and axillary lymphadenopathy. The differential diagnosis based on sonographic findings are, giant galactocele, breast abscess and cystic lymphangioma. Additional diagnostic imaging like mammography and breast MRI was not indicated in this case, because no vascularized solid components, mural nodules, or irregular margins were observed on ultrasound. The patient was directly advised on fine needle aspiration for possible diagnosis and to guide further management.

A large, well-defined, cystic mass was identified within the right breast demonstrating a predominantly homogeneous low-level internal echogenicity with hyperechoic debris. A solitary incomplete septum is visualized. It demonstrates posterior acoustic enhancement with no internal vascularity on color doppler imaging. No other solid components or irregularities at the margin were identified. The mass measures approximately 15 × 12 × 10 cm. The mass was located approximately 1 cm away from the nipple-areolar complex.

Ultrasound-guided aspiration using a 5 ml syringe was performed, and it demonstrated milk solutions in the aspirates (Fig. 3). Surgical excision of the mass under general anesthesia was done, and the mass was cystic with milky fluid as its contents (Fig. 4). The patient had no complications on the immediate post-operative period and was discharged after 2 days of surgery with normal vital signs. Gross examination of the mass reveals a multiloculated cystic mass filled with cheese-like friable material with a maximum thickness of 0.4 cm (Fig. 5A and B). Finally, histopathological analysis was performed, and galactoceles were confirmed (Fig. 6A and B). The patient was followed every 3 months clinically and by ultrasound and no recurrence was identified after 1 year of follow-up.

Discussion

Galactocele are the most common benign breast lesion typically occurring in young lactating women and in the third trimester of pregnancy. Sometimes, the condition can occur after cessation of breast feeding [1–6]. Although the exact mechanism is still unknown, galactoceles in infants and young boy are also rarely reported in the literature [7–10].

The majority of galactoceles are small although large ones greater than 10 cm are rarely reported and diagnostically challenging, especially in non-lactating patients like in this case. Ultrasound is the primary imaging modality for evaluating breast masses during lactation, while mammography and MRI may be added when sonographic or clinical features raise concern. Galactocele has various sonographic findings, many of which are similar to those of suspicious solid breast masses. However, a giant galactocele may mimic abscess and complex cysts like cystic lymphangioma, which may pose diagnostic challenges using ultrasound like in our case [3–5].

Differentiating galactocele from other breast pathologies, such as cysts, fibroadenomas, phyllodes tumors, abscesses, or malignancies, is essential. Ultrasonography of a galactocele typically reveals a cystic fluid collection, and in symptomatic cases, fine-needle aspiration (FNA) may be performed for both diagnostic and therapeutic purposes [1].

In general, an ultrasound-guided fine needle aspiration (FNA) and/or core needle biopsy should be performed if a lesion is small and has an atypical imaging appearance of galactocele or a benign breast lesion in a lactating woman. However, if the lesion is large enough and the composition is cystic fine needle aspiration alone can be sufficient to diagnose galactocele [5].

Ultrasound-guided aspiration is generally both diagnostic and therapeutic for most galactoceles, and conservative observation after aspiration is widely recommended. However, surgical excision becomes appropriate when the cyst remains extremely large after aspiration, refills rapidly, or causes significant discomfort or cosmetic deformity [5, 16].

In our case, although aspiration yielded typical milky fluid, the lesion showed minimal reduction in size and continued to produce marked breast deformity. Therefore, surgical excision was selected as a reasonable management option.

Conclusion

Giant galactoceles are rare. Ultrasound allows accurate diagnosis and differentiation from malignancy in most of the patients. Aspiration is usually sufficient. However, exceptionally large lesions that fail to regress or cause significant cosmetic deformity may appropriately managed, with surgical excision.
