Respiratory Support and Mortality Risk Across the Spectrum of Cardiogenic Shock Severity
Authors: Talal El Zarif, Cesar Caraballo, Angela M. Victoria-Castro, Israel Safiriyu, Maria Gabriela Gastanadui, David M. Dudzinski, Balimkiz Senman, Carlos Alviar, Guido Tavazzi, Andrea Elliott, Aniket S. Rali, Mark Jacobs, Jason N. Katz, Ann Gage, P. Elliott Miller
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe Society for Cardiovascular Angiography & Intervention (SCAI) SHOCK stages classification schema risk-stratifies patients with cardiogenic shock (CS). The updated 2022 SCAI SHOCK stages removed the use of respiratory support, either noninvasive (NIV) or invasive mechanical ventilation (IMV), as a criterion. We sought to investigate the impact of receiving respiratory support on in-hospital mortality for patients with CS stratified by SCAI SHOCK stages.MethodsUtilizing a nationally representative database, adults aged ≥18 years admitted from 2015 to 2023 with a diagnosis of CS were used to assess for the association between respiratory support, either NIV or IMV, on the first day of admission, with in-hospital mortality stratified by SCAI SHOCK stages B through E. We utilized inverse probability treatment weighting, adjusting for demographic characteristics, comorbidities, hospital characteristics, and vasoactive/mechanical circulatory support.ResultsWe identified 317,325 patients with CS, including 2.4%, 39.0%, 34.2%, and 24.5% with SCAI stages B through E, respectively. Respiratory support was utilized in 38.0% (n = 120,594) of patients, with 5.4% receiving NIV, 33.8% receiving IMV, and 1.1% receiving both on the first day of admission. After inverse probability treatment weighting, respiratory support use remained associated with an increased mortality overall (weighted mean mortality increase of 18.3%; 95% CI, 17.9%-18.7%), when stratified by each SCAI SHOCK stage and in several key sensitivity analyses.ConclusionsCompared with patients not receiving respiratory support, the use of respiratory support was associated with an increased mortality for each SCAI stage of CS and could be a simple, easily identifiable CS risk modifier.

Introduction

Respiratory failure is now one of the most common etiologies for admission to the contemporary cardiac intensive care unit.1,2 More importantly, there are numerous reports detailing the importance of respiratory failure and its association with increased mortality in various cardiovascular patient populations.3, 4, 5, 6 In particular, the use of respiratory support, both noninvasive (NIV) and invasive mechanical ventilation (IMV), has been associated with an increased mortality in patients with cardiogenic shock (CS).7 Although frequently necessary to rescue or support patients with respiratory failure, prolonged respiratory support may lead to interruptions in critical therapies and early interventions.8 However, it is unclear whether respiratory failure is an indication of overall critical illness or whether the need for respiratory support is independently associated with worse outcomes in patients with CS, especially given the multitude of potential etiologies.

The initial Society for Cardiovascular Angiography & Interventions (SCAI) SHOCK staging schema was developed to create a simple, easily identifiable, and reproducible staging system for both clinical communication and research.9 In 2022, the SCAI SHOCK stages were updated, removing respiratory support from their CS staging criteria.10 Other easily identifiable risk factors, such as cardiac arrest, have been designated as shock modifiers.11 Given the previous reports detailing worse outcomes in patients requiring respiratory support and changes to the SCAI staging criteria, we assessed for the impact of respiratory support use stratified by SCAI SHOCK stage.

Materials and methods

Data source and study population

We utilized the Vizient Clinical Data Base, which includes over 1350 hospitals from 49 US states and over 97% of academic medical centers and their affiliated hospitals. It includes administrative, financial, laboratory, and pharmacy-related inpatient data time-stamped to the day after admission. Our data query included adults aged ≥18 years admitted between October 1, 2015, and June 30, 2023 with a diagnosis of CS. Respiratory support was defined as receiving either NIV or IMV on the day of admission. The International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision, Clinical Modification (ICD-10-CM) code for CS (R57.0) has a high specificity (>95%) and positive predictive value (>90%).12 Patients without CS coded as present on admission, without medication data, and unknown discharge mortality were excluded (Figure 1). All data obtained from Vizient were deidentified and exempt from the Yale University Institutional Review Board review. Data was used with permission from Vizient, Inc. Variables of interest

Demographic characteristic variables included age, sex, race and ethnicity, primary payer (commercial, Medicaid, Medicare, or other), and smoking history. Comorbidities included having a history of dyslipidemia, acute myocardial infarction, previous percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI), previous coronary artery bypass grafting, congestive heart failure, peripheral vascular disease, cerebrovascular disease, chronic obstructive lung disease, chronic liver disease, chronic renal disease, end-stage renal disease, history of left ventricular assist device (LVAD), heart transplantation, diabetes, cancer, acquire immunodeficiency syndrome, and dementia. Hospital characteristics included region (Midwest, Northeast, South, and West), bed size (<125, 125-300, 301-500, and >500), and Association of American Medical Colleges teaching status.

Identified procedures performed during the admission included left and right heart catheterization, PCI, intra-aortic balloon pump, Impella, extracorporeal membrane oxygenation, heart transplantation, durable LVAD, NIV, IMV, renal replacement therapy, and tracheostomy. ICD-10-CM codes used to identify procedures are listed in Supplemental Table S1.

Admission laboratory values included lactate, pH, creatinine, blood urea nitrogen, sodium, glucose, bicarbonate, aspartate aminotransferase, alanine aminotransferase, total bilirubin, albumin, white blood cell count, hemoglobin, and platelets.

SCAI SHOCK stage was calculated utilizing the Critical Care Cardiology Trials Network approach and the updated 2022 SCAI SHOCK stages (Supplemental Table S2).10,13

Outcomes

The primary outcome was the association between respiratory support on the first day of admission and in-hospital mortality. Secondary outcomes included total hospital cost, as well as hospital and ICU length of stay (LOS).

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics were compared between patients who received respiratory support on the first day of admission and those who did not. Continuous variables were described as means and SDs or medians and interquartile range as appropriate. Categorical variables were described as frequencies and percentages. The t test was used to compare continuous variables, and the χ2 test for categorical variables. We used inverse probability treatment weighting (IPTW), adjusting for demographic characteristics (age, sex, race and ethnicity, and primary payer), comorbidities (smoking, coronary artery disease, dyslipidemia, history of acute myocardial infarction, prior PCI, prior coronary artery bypass grafting, diabetes, history of congestive heart failure, peripheral vascular disease, cerebrovascular disease, prior heart transplant, prior LVAD implantation, chronic kidney disease, end-stage renal disease, chronic obstructive lung disease, chronic liver disease, cancer, dementia, rheumatic disease, and acquire immunodeficiency syndrome), Charlson comorbidity index,14 critical admission diagnoses (out-of-hospital cardiac arrest and acute renal failure), any mechanical circulatory support (MCS) day 1, any vasoactive medication on day 1, and hospital/admission characteristics (bed size, region, Association of American Medical Colleges-teaching status, and admission timing). Weight stabilization and truncation at the first and 99th percentiles were utilized to minimize extreme weights.15 We targeted a weighted standardized difference <0.10, suggesting good balance between covariates. To evaluate hospital and ICU LOS, we used Poisson regression expressed as an incidence rate ratio (IRR). A gamma regression model was used to evaluate adjusted total hospital costs.

To address potential respiratory support use for surgical cases, we performed a sensitivity analysis excluding patients with postcardiotomy shock (ICD-10 code T81.11XA). We also performed a sensitivity analysis defining respiratory failure using ICD-10-CM diagnosis and procedure codes (IMV and NIV codes on day 1), consistent with prior work.16 In addition, we performed several subgroup and sensitivity analyses stratified by SCAI SHOCK stages. First, we assessed the association between patients who received NIV only or IMV only compared with patients without any respiratory support. Second, given potential treatment limitations related to patient goals of care, we repeated analyses excluding patients with a do-not-resuscitate (DNR) ICD-10 code present on admission. Third, we repeated our analysis in only patients who received MCS on the first day of admission. Fourth, given the high mortality and common need for respiratory support, we repeated our analysis excluding patients presenting with out-of-hospital cardiac arrest. Fifth, we repeated analyses stratified by a primary admission diagnosis of acute myocardial infarction or heart failure. Finally, to assess practice variation across centers, we fit a mixed-effects logistic regression model with a random intercept for hospital to account for clustering by site. Each model included the same set of fixed-effect covariates used in the primary multivariable analysis and was stratified by SCAI SHOCK stage. We repeated this analysis separately for IMV and NIV on the first day of admission. Using hospital-level variance, we calculated median odds ratios to quantify between-hospital variation in respiratory support use. All analyses were performed on STATA 16.0 (Stata Corp) with statistical significance considered at a 2-tailed P < .05.

Results

Baseline characteristics

Among 317,325 patients with CS, 120,594 (38.0%) received respiratory support on the first day of admission (Central Illustration). Among patients receiving respiratory support, 107,209 (33.8%) received IMV, 17,030 (5.4%) received NIV, and 3645 (1.1%) received both (Figure 1). Compared with those not receiving respiratory support, those receiving respiratory support were slightly younger (64.2 vs 65.2 years) and less likely to be male (62.3% vs 64.2%) (both P < .001) (Table 1). Mortality

The unadjusted in-hospital mortality was 52.0% in patients requiring respiratory support compared with 22.9% in those without respiratory support (P < .001). Predicted in-hospital mortality was higher with advancing SCAI SHOCK stage and consistently higher among patients requiring respiratory support (Figure 4). After IPTW, all covariates were well balanced (Supplemental Table S3), and early respiratory support was associated with an increased weighted mean mortality of 18.3% (95% CI, 17.9%-18.7%, P < .001) and remained associated with a higher mortality with each SCAI SHOCK stage (Figure 5). Secondary outcomes

Patients requiring respiratory support had shorter median total hospital LOS (7 vs 8 days) but longer ICU LOS (4 vs 3 days) (both P < .001). After multivariable adjustment, respiratory support utilization remained associated with a shorter total hospital LOS (IRR, 0.941; 95% CI, 0.939-0.943; P < .001) but longer ICU LOS (IRR, 1.069; 95% CI, 1.065-1.072; P < .001). Median unadjusted total hospital cost was higher in the respiratory failure group ($34,469 vs $30,781; P < .001), but lower after multivariable adjustment (−$1431; 95% CI, −$2151 to −$712; P < .001). Given the substantially higher mortality in the respiratory failure group, we repeated analyses in-hospital survivors. Among hospital survivors, adjusted total hospital LOS (IRR, 1.16; 95% CI, 1.15-1.16), ICU LOS (IRR, 1.26; 95% CI, 1.26-1.27), and total hospital costs ($9549; 95% CI, $8540-$10,557) were all higher in patients receiving respiratory support (all P < .001).

Sensitivity analyses

Using ICD-10-CM codes to define respiratory failure in the original cohort, 61.4% (n = 381,732) of patients met the criteria, and respiratory failure was associated with a 23.4% (95% CI, 23.2-23.6%, P < .001) increase in in-hospital mortality. When NIV and IMV on the first day of admission were separated and compared against patients not requiring respiratory support, IMV remained associated with a higher adjusted mortality for each SCAI, but only SCAI stages C and D remained significantly associated with a higher mortality for patients receiving only NIV, although to a lesser extent than IMV (Table 5). Excluding those with DNR present on admission, respiratory support on admission was associated with higher in-hospital mortality (weighted mean mortality 17.9%; 95% CI, 17.5%-18.3%, P < .001) and remained significant when stratified by each SCAI SHOCK stage (Table 6). Similarly, the use of respiratory support on the first day of admission remained associated with a higher in-hospital mortality in analyses only including patients receiving MCS on admission or excluding patients presenting with out-of-hospital cardiac arrest (Supplemental Table S4). Finally, we found significant variation across hospital centers for respiratory support utilization stratified by SCAI SHOCK stage, including when analyzed separately for IMV and NIV on the first day of admission (Supplemental Table S6). After multivariable adjustment, the highest variation was seen at lower SCAI SHOCK stages for all 3 groups (all, IMV, and NIV), and among the NIV only group. For example, the median odds ratio for NIV only patients and SCAI stage B was 2.37 (P < .001), suggesting that 2 statistically similar patients with SCAI SHOCK B had a 2.37-fold differing likelihood of receiving NIV at 2 randomly selected hospitals.

Discussion

In this nationwide, multicenter study, we examined the impact of respiratory support utilization on in-hospital mortality across SCAI SHOCK stages in more than 300,000 patients with CS. We found that the need for respiratory support, including either NIV or IMV, was associated with an over 50% unadjusted mortality compared with 23.0% in patients not requiring respiratory support. In-hospital mortality remained higher across all SCAI stages and persisted in several sensitivity analyses. When NIV and IMV were analyzed separately, IMV remained associated with higher mortality for all SCAI stages, but NIV was no longer significant for SCAI SHOCK B and E. Patients requiring respiratory support were more critically ill and associated with higher SCAI SHOCK stages, admission lactate levels, and required more cardiac and noncardiac procedures and therapies. Our findings highlight the impact of respiratory support as a readily identifiable marker of illness severity and worse outcomes, regardless of SCAI SHOCK stage.

The initial SCAI SHOCK schema was a crucial step in both clinical and research standardization of CS staging. The subsequent validation and rapid uptake led to numerous studies, including analyses to identify stage modification. In addition, the 2022 update fairly requested “ongoing validation, refinement, knowledge translation, and implementation.”10 In this effort, especially given the removal of respiratory support from the SCAI SHOCK stages, we attempted to re-evaluate the impact of respiratory support among patients with CS. In light of our findings and the fundamental goal of the SCAI SHOCK classification to provide easily identifiable indicators of severity, we believe that future iterations should consider the inclusion of respiratory support.

Our study is not the first to identify respiratory support utilization as a predictor of worse outcomes. In a multicenter registry of cardiac intensive care units, both patients requiring NIV or IMV therapy had a higher mortality compared with those who did not require respiratory support.1 In comparison, a study from the FRENSHOCK registry, including over 700 patients with CS, IMV but not NIV was associated with a higher mortality at 30 days compared with patients requiring either NIV or no ventilation.17 Neither study investigated clinical outcomes across the SCAI stages. In the AltShock-2 registry (n = 657), 60-day mortality was significantly higher in patients treated with IMV compared with those treated with NIV or oxygen therapy (40.2% vs 26% and 29.3%, respectively). This association remained significant after excluding patients with cardiac arrest and adjusting for age, lactate, and MCS use (IMV vs NIV: HR, 2.24; 95% CI, 1.36-3.68).18 Our study is additive to theirs, including a much larger sample size, which allowed for the evaluation of SCAI stage E as well as a more robust multivariable analysis. In addition to identifying patients likely to have more advanced shock, the association of respiratory support, especially IMV, with increased mortality may be attributed to several factors. Although potentially lifesaving and often necessary, IMV may expose patients to several complications, such as ventilator-associated pneumonia, acute lung injury, and potential interruptions in critical therapies and procedures.8 The impact of prolonged sedatives and immobilization can predispose to worse outcomes, which can persist after hospital discharge.19 NIV is a reasonable treatment option in the properly selected patient,17,18 but delays in IMV may also predispose to worse outcomes.20 Notably, we found substantial variation in respiratory support use, especially for NIV use, across centers, highlighting the need for dedicated research on patient selection for different respiratory support strategies. Furthermore, patients with cardiovascular disease are frequently excluded or poorly represented in intensive care clinical trials assessing respiratory therapies.21 The limited available data have led to wide variability in practice patterns and sparse recommendations from societal guidelines and are therefore a key research priority in the field of critical care cardiology.22

There are several limitations that need to be considered when evaluating our findings. First, our retrospective study utilizing an administrative database undoubtedly includes unmeasured confounders. Although we attempted to mitigate confounding and imbalance with IPTW and several sensitivity analyses, confounding by indication remains an important limitation of our analyses. Patients requiring respiratory support were overall more critically ill and may have had clinical features not readily captured in administrative datasets, and therefore, residual bias likely remains despite multiple sensitivity analyses and careful adjustment. For instance, although our database has many strengths, including time-stamped procedures, labs, and medications, we lack data on vital signs, invasive hemodynamics measures, and medication doses. We are also not able to identify the use of high-flow nasal cannula, an important respiratory support modality. Second, we are unable to identify the etiology of respiratory failure or the reason why respiratory support was deployed. Although our cohort presented with CS and many likely had cardiogenic pulmonary edema, it is likely that the study population includes patients with a heterogeneous group of respiratory failure etiologies. Our goal was to simply highlight the impact of respiratory support regardless of etiology. Third, although we performed a sensitivity analysis excluding patients with a DNR status to assess patient goals around treatment intensity, there is no do-not-intubate ICD-10 code, which would have certainly influenced respiratory support decisions.

In conclusion, respiratory support on the first day of admission was associated with an increase in in-hospital mortality in patients with CS. Given that other easily identifiable clinical variables, such as cardiac arrest, have been identified as modifiers, our study highlights the potential utility of respiratory support as an additional risk factor, regardless of cardiac arrest status. Reintegrating respiratory support as a risk modifier in future SCAI classification iterations could improve our ability to identify high-risk patients with CS and inform treatment strategies for this unique patient population.
