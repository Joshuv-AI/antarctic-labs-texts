Blink of an eye: a simple theory for feature localization in generative models
Marvin Li, Aayush Karan, Sitan Chen

Abstract
Large language models can exhibit unexpected behavior in the blink of an eye. In a recent computer use demo, a language model switched from coding to Googling pictures of Yellowstone, and these sudden shifts in behavior have also been observed in reasoning patterns and jailbreaks. This phenomenon is not unique to autoregressive models: in diffusion models, key features of the final output are decided in narrow “critical windows” of the generation process. In this work we develop a simple, unifying theory to explain this phenomenon using the formalism of stochastic localization samplers. We show that it emerges generically as the generation process localizes to a sub-population of the distribution it models. While critical windows have been studied at length in diffusion models, existing theory heavily relies on strong distributional assumptions and the particulars of Gaussian diffusion. In contrast to existing work our theory (1) applies to autoregressive and diffusion models; (2) makes no distributional assumptions; (3) quantitatively improves previous bounds even when specialized to diffusions; and (4) requires basic tools and no stochastic calculus or statistical-physics-based machinery. We also identify an intriguing connection to the all-or-nothing phenomenon from statistical inference. Finally, we validate our predictions empirically for LLMs and find that critical windows often coincide with failures in problem solving for various math and reasoning benchmarks.

1.1
Our contributions

In this work, we develop a simple theoretical framework that characterizes critical windows in both diffusion models and autoregressive models. Our theory is fully rigorous and show that such windows arise generically when the model localizes from a larger sub-population to a smaller one, distilling the phenomenon of critical windows to very general facts about the data distribution. In particular, we show that the critical window is parameterized by the last step of the generative model at which the larger and smaller sub-populations are indistinguishable and the
first step of the generative model at which that smaller and larger sub-populations have negligible overlap between each other. We will formalize these notions in Section
3
.

The key insight of our theory is to apply to the formalism of
stochastic localization samplers
(see Section
2.1
for a formal description) to understand critical windows
Mon23a ()
;
CE (22)

Roughly speaking, a stochastic localization scheme is any generative model given by a time-reversal of a Markovian degradation process which takes a sample from the target distribution and generates progressively less informative “observations” of it. In diffusion models, the degradation is a convolution of the original sample with larger and larger amounts of Gaussian noise. In autoregressive models, the degradation is the masking of entries from right to left. Importantly, our theory does not use anything about the specific structure of the sampler beyond the Markovianity of the observation process. Below we highlight our main contributions:

1.

Generality
: In comparison to existing work, our theory (Theorem
2

)

2.

To illustrate the flexibility of our bounds, we explicitly compute the locations and widths of these windows for different generative models and data modalities (Section

We also use our theory to argue that autoregressive models can support deeper hierarchies than diffusion models.

Technical preliminaries

Probability notation.

Given distributions
P,Q
defined on
(\Omega,\mathcal{F})
with a base measure

is defined as
\mathrm{TV}(P,Q)\triangleq\frac{1}{2}\int|dP-dQ|d\mu

as shorthand to denote the

of the measures of

\mathrm{supp}(P)=\{x\in\Omega|dP(x)>0\}
denote the support. We will also use the following well-known relationship.

Lemma 1
.

For probability measures

\mdmathbb{E}_{x\sim P}\left[\frac{\mathrm{d}Q}{\mathrm{d}P+\mathrm{d}Q}\right]\leq\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{1-\mathrm{TV}^{2}(P,Q)}.

To study feature localization in diffusion and autoregressive models, we consider a
forward-reverse
experiment. A forward-reverse experiment considers the amount of “noise” one would need to add to a generation so that running the generative model starting from the noised generation would still yield a sample with the same feature. For a diffusion model, this could mean taking an image of a cat, adding Gaussian noise, and resampling to see if the result is still a cat. For a language model, it could mean truncating a story about a cat and resampling to check if the story remains about a cat. Now, we will use the language of stochastic localization to place these analogous experiments for diffusion and language models within the same framework.

\mathrm{TV}(p^{S_{\mathrm{init}},\widehat{T}},p^{S_{\mathrm{target}}})
is small:

Theorem 2
.

Let
S_{\mathrm{init}}\subset S_{\mathrm{target}}\subset\Theta
and
W=\frac{\sum_{\theta\in\Theta-S_{\mathrm{target}}}w_{\theta}}{\sum_{\theta\in S_{\mathrm{target}}}w_{\theta}}
. For
\epsilon>0
, if
\widehat{T}\in\mathbf{I}\cap[T_{\mathrm{end}}^{S},T_{\mathrm{start}}^{S}]
, then

\mathrm{TV}(p^{S_{\mathrm{init}},\widehat{T}},p^{S_{\mathrm{target}}})\leq\epsilon\cdot\bigl(1+\max\left(1,W\right)/\sqrt{2}\bigr).

Intuitively,
T_{\mathrm{start}}^{S}
represents the largest
t
for which there is still separation between
S_{\mathrm{target}}
and
\Theta-S_{\mathrm{target}}
, and
T_{\mathrm{end}}^{S}
represents the smallest
t
for which samples from
S_{\mathrm{init}},S_{\mathrm{target}}
are indistinguishable. Thus, running it for
\widehat{T}\in\mathbf{I}\cap[T_{\mathrm{end}}^{S},T_{\mathrm{start}}^{S}]
erases the differences between samples from
S_{\mathrm{init}}
and
S_{\mathrm{target}}
but preserves the difference between
S_{\mathrm{target}}
and
\Theta-S_{\mathrm{target}}
, yielding samples looking like
p^{S_{\mathrm{target}}}
.

Figure 3
:
Intuition with the forward-reverse experiment with
S_{\mathrm{init}}=\{\texttt{orange cat}\}
and
S_{\mathrm{target}}=\{\texttt{orange cat},\texttt{brown cat}\}
. In the low-noise regime, the forward-reverse experiment does not modify the image non-trivially, meaning we still sample from
S_{\mathrm{init}}
; in the high-noise regime, the forward-reverse experiment destroys all information in the image and thus could yield a dog, lying outside of
S_{\mathrm{target}}
. Thus there is a "sweet spot" where the differences between orange and brown cats collapse but those between cats and dogs persist.

A priori
it is not clear why
T_{\mathrm{end}}^{S}
should be smaller than
T_{\mathrm{start}}^{S}
, and indeed in general it need not be and our bound would be vacuous. In Section
4
however, we show that in many natural settings for diffusion models and autoregressive models, the relation does hold.

Remark 3
.

Note a similar bound was shown in the context of diffusions by
LC (24)
(see Theorem 7 therein). Our result is a strict improvement of that bound along several important axes. First, our results apply to all stochastic localization samplers, not just diffusions. Secondly,
LC (24)
needed to assume that the components of
p
were strongly log-concave and that the score, i.e. gradient of the log-density, of
p_{t}
was Lipschitz and moment-bounded for all
t
. Thirdly, their final bound includes a polynomial dependence on the moments of the score, which scale with the dimension
d
; in contrast, our final bound is independent of
d
.

With Theorem
2
in place, we are ready to formally define
critical windows
. These capture the moments where we transition from sampling from a sub-mixture to a subset of that sub-mixture.

Definition 4
.

Define
S_{\mathrm{after}}\subset S_{\mathrm{before}}\subset\Theta
. For
S_{\mathrm{before}}
, we define
T_{\mathrm{before}}^{C}=\inf\{t\in\mathbf{I}:\mathrm{TV}(p_{t}^{S_{\mathrm{after}}},p_{t}^{S_{\mathrm{before}}})\leq\epsilon\text{ and }\mathrm{TV}(p_{t}^{S_{\mathrm{before}}},p_{t}^{\Theta-S_{\mathrm{before}}})\geq 1-\epsilon^{2}\}
(
S_{\mathrm{init}}\triangleq S_{\mathrm{after}};S_{\mathrm{target}}\triangleq S_{\mathrm{before}})
. For
S_{\mathrm{after}}
, consider
T_{\mathrm{after}}^{C}=\sup\{t\in\mathbf{I}:\mathrm{TV}(p_{t}^{S_{\mathrm{after}}},p_{t}^{\Theta-S_{\mathrm{after}}})\geq 1-\epsilon^{2}\}
(
S_{\mathrm{init}},S_{\mathrm{target}}\triangleq S_{\mathrm{after}}
). A
critical window
is the interval
[T_{\mathrm{after}}^{C},T_{\mathrm{before}}^{C}]
, where there is a transition from sampling from
S_{\mathrm{before}}
to the smaller subset
S_{\mathrm{after}}
.

Note that this definition does not directly explain why or when a critical window is sharp, which depends on the properties of the model of the data and forward process. In Section
4
, we analytically compute critical windows in different toy settings and demonstrate that they are sharp in a natural sense. These examples also provide general intuition where critical windows are sharp, i.e. for autoregressive LLMs, critical windows may occur when a few tokens are very unlikely under one mode than other in Example
4
. In general, we intuit that sharp critical windows occur when it only takes a few steps from the forward process to erase the differences between
S_{\mathrm{before}}
and
S_{\mathrm{after}}
. This could happen if the data has a multi-scale hierarchical structure, where a feature is decided in a narrow intermediate band of the tree (Section
5
).

3.2
Proof of Theorem
2

Crucially, our proof relies in several places on the Markov property of stochastic localization samplers, together with the data processing inequality. We also define some helpful notation for our proof. For nonempty
S\subset\Theta
and
t\in\mathbf{I}
, we define
P^{\scriptscriptstyle\leftarrow}(\cdot|Y_{t},S)
to be the posterior of
X
with the prior
X\sim p^{S}
. We similarly define
P^{\scriptscriptstyle\leftarrow}_{t\to\Theta}(\cdot|Y_{t})
and
P^{\scriptscriptstyle\leftarrow}_{t\to\Theta}(\cdot|Y_{t},S)
to be the posterior of
\theta
conditioning on
Y_{t}
with
X\sim p
or
X\sim p^{S}
, respectively. When
S=\{i\}
, we exclude the braces.

Proof of Theorem
2
.

By the triangle inequality, we can write

\displaystyle\mathrm{TV}(p^{S_{\mathrm{init}},\widehat{T}},p^{S_{\mathrm{target}}})\leq\underbrace{\mathrm{TV}(p^{S_{\mathrm{init}},\widehat{T}},p^{S_{\mathrm{target}},\widehat{T}})}_{\text{(I)}}+\underbrace{\mathrm{TV}(p^{S_{\mathrm{target}},\widehat{T}},p^{S_{\mathrm{target}}})}_{\text{(II)}}.

p^{S_{\mathrm{init}},\widehat{T}}
and
p^{S_{\mathrm{target}},\widehat{T}}
are the laws of the posterior
P^{\scriptscriptstyle\leftarrow}(\cdot|\cdot)
but applied to
Y_{\widehat{T}}
with distributions
p^{S_{\mathrm{init}}}_{\widehat{T}}
and
p^{S_{\mathrm{target}}}_{\widehat{T}}
. Using the Markov property of localization-based samplers (Definition
1
), we apply the data processing inequality twice and the definition of
T_{\mathrm{end}}^{S}
to bound (I) via

\mathrm{TV}(p^{S_{\mathrm{init}},\widehat{T}},p^{S_{\mathrm{target}},\widehat{T}})\leq\mathrm{TV}(p^{S_{\mathrm{init}}}_{\widehat{T}},p^{S_{\mathrm{target}}}_{\widehat{T}})\leq\mathrm{TV}(p^{S_{\mathrm{init}}}_{T_{\mathrm{end}}^{S}},p^{S_{\mathrm{target}}}_{T_{\mathrm{end}}^{S}})\leq\epsilon.

To bound (II), we use the definition of
\mathrm{TV}
and a coupling argument. Observe that the observation processes associated with
p^{S_{\mathrm{target}},\widehat{T}}
and
p^{S_{\mathrm{target}}}
have the same distribution at index
\widehat{T}
. Thus, taking
Y_{\widehat{T}}\sim p^{S_{\mathrm{target}}}_{\widehat{T}}
, we can express by the law of total probability,

\displaystyle p^{S_{\mathrm{target}},\widehat{T}}(x)

\displaystyle=\mdmathbb{E}[P^{\scriptscriptstyle\leftarrow}(x|Y_{\widehat{T}})]

\displaystyle p^{S_{\mathrm{target}}}(x)

\displaystyle=\mdmathbb{E}[P^{\scriptscriptstyle\leftarrow}(x|Y_{\widehat{T}},S_{\mathrm{target}})].

as these observation processes have the same distribution at index
\widehat{T}
. Thus,

\displaystyle\mathrm{TV}(p^{S_{\mathrm{target}},\widehat{T}},p^{S_{\mathrm{target}}})=\frac{1}{2}\int\left|p^{S_{\mathrm{target}},\widehat{T}}(x)-p^{S_{\mathrm{target}}}(x)\right|dx=\frac{1}{2}\int\left|\mdmathbb{E}[P^{\scriptscriptstyle\leftarrow}(x|Y_{\widehat{T}})]-\mdmathbb{E}[P^{\scriptscriptstyle\leftarrow}(x|Y_{\widehat{T}},S_{\mathrm{target}})].\right|dx.

By Jensen’s inequality and Fubini’s theorem, we bring the expectation outside the integral,

\displaystyle\mathrm{TV}(p^{S_{\mathrm{target}},\widehat{T}},p^{S_{\mathrm{target}}})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\int\mdmathbb{E}\left[\left|P^{\scriptscriptstyle\leftarrow}(x|Y_{\widehat{T}})-P^{\scriptscriptstyle\leftarrow}(x|Y_{\widehat{T}},S_{\mathrm{target}})\right|\right]dx=\frac{1}{2}\mdmathbb{E}\left[\int\left|P^{\scriptscriptstyle\leftarrow}(x|Y_{\widehat{T}})-P^{\scriptscriptstyle\leftarrow}(x|Y_{\widehat{T}},S_{\mathrm{target}})\right|dx\right].

To simplify the above expression, we use the following two lemmas, proved in App.
C.1
.

Lemma 3
.

By applying the law of total probability and Bayes’ rule, we can show for
Y_{\widehat{T}}\in\mathrm{supp}(p^{S_{\mathrm{target}}}_{\widehat{T}})
,

\displaystyle\int\left|P^{\scriptscriptstyle\leftarrow}(x|Y_{\widehat{T}})-P^{\scriptscriptstyle\leftarrow}(x|Y_{\widehat{T}},S_{\mathrm{target}})\right|dx\leq 2\sum_{\theta\in\Theta-S_{\mathrm{target}}}P^{\scriptscriptstyle\leftarrow}_{t\to\Theta}(\theta|Y_{\widehat{T}}).

Lemma 4
.

By Bayes’s rule, we can derive for
Y_{\widehat{T}}\in\mathrm{supp}(p_{\widehat{T}})
,

\displaystyle\sum_{\theta\in\Theta-S_{\mathrm{target}}}P^{\scriptscriptstyle\leftarrow}_{t\to\Theta}(\theta|Y_{\widehat{T}})\leq\max\left(1,W\right)\frac{p^{\Theta-S_{\mathrm{target}}}_{\widehat{T}}(Y_{\widehat{T}})}{p^{\Theta-S_{\mathrm{target}}}_{\widehat{T}}(Y_{\widehat{T}})+p^{S_{\mathrm{target}}}_{\widehat{T}}(Y_{\widehat{T}})}

Combining Lemmas
3
and
4
, we find

\displaystyle\mathrm{TV}(p^{S_{\mathrm{target}},\widehat{T}},p^{S_{\mathrm{target}}})\leq\max\left(1,W\right)\mdmathbb{E}\left[\frac{p^{\Theta-S_{\mathrm{target}}}_{\widehat{T}}(Y_{\widehat{T}})}{p^{\Theta-S_{\mathrm{target}}}_{\widehat{T}}(Y_{\widehat{T}})+p^{S_{\mathrm{target}}}_{\widehat{T}}(Y_{\widehat{T}})}\right].

Then, finally applying Lemma
1
, we are able to bound the total variation in terms of
\epsilon
,

\displaystyle\mathrm{TV}(p^{S_{\mathrm{target}},\widehat{T}},p^{S_{\mathrm{target}}})\leq\frac{1}{2}\max\left(1,W\right)\sqrt{1-\mathrm{TV}^{2}(p^{\Theta-S_{\mathrm{target}}}_{\widehat{T}},p^{S_{\mathrm{target}}}_{\widehat{T}})}\leq\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\max\left(1,W\right)\epsilon.

Combining our bounds on (I) and (II) achieves the desired result.
∎

Examples of critical windows

In this section, we analytically compute

for diverse stochastic localization samplers and models of data, including diffusion and autoregression processes. In these natural settings, the critical window is small in the sense of having a size which shrinks or does not depend on the dimension or context length. We shall also connect our framework to in-context learning and the all-or-nothing phenomenon.

4.1
Diffusion

We first consider two examples of Gaussian Mixture Models and a diffusion model. We show that with two isotropic Gaussians, the critical window appears around a single point,
\ln\|\mu\|
, with width independent of the dimension.

Example 1

(Two Isotropic Gaussians)
.

Let
\Theta=\{\pm 1\}
,
p^{+1}=\mathcal{N}(\mu,\mathrm{Id})
,
p^{-1}=\mathcal{N}(-\mu,\mathrm{Id})
. Then, we have a critical window transitioning from sampling from both components to the component
+1
between
T_{\mathrm{before}}^{C}=\ln\|\mu\|+\ln 2+\ln 1/\epsilon
and
T_{\mathrm{after}}^{C}=\ln\|\mu\|-\ln\ln\frac{1}{2\epsilon^{2}}
. When
\widehat{T}\leq T_{\mathrm{after}}^{C}
, then
\mathrm{TV}(p^{+1,\widehat{T}},p^{+1})\lesssim\epsilon
. When
\widehat{T}\geq T_{\mathrm{before}}^{C}
,
\mathrm{TV}(p^{+1,\widehat{T}},p)\lesssim\epsilon
.

For an isotropic Gaussian mixture model with randomly selected means, the critical window between sampling from one component to the entire mixture is also narrow. Note that we derive dimension-free widths in Example
2
, an improvement over
(
LC, 24
)
who had a
\ln\ln d
dependence on dimension for isotropic Gaussians.

Example 2

(Random mean spherical Gaussians)
.

We first sample
\mu_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\mathrm{Id})
for
i\in[K]
i.i.d. and let
\Theta=\{\mathcal{N}(\mu_{i},\mathrm{Id})\}_{i\in[K]}
. We let
S_{\mathrm{before}}=\Theta
and
S_{\mathrm{after}}=\{\mu_{i}\}
. Then, we can compute
T_{\mathrm{before}}^{C}=\max_{j\in[K]}\ln\|\mu_{i}-\mu_{j}\|+\ln(1/\epsilon)
and
T_{\mathrm{after}}^{C}=\min_{j\in[K],i\neq j}\ln\|\mu_{i}-\mu_{j}\|-\frac{1}{2}\ln 8\ln\frac{K}{\epsilon}.
Furthermore, with high probability over the selection of the means,
T_{\mathrm{before}}^{C}-T_{\mathrm{after}}^{C}=O_{K,\epsilon}(1)
as
d\to\infty
.

We also compute the critical windows of a discrete diffusion model. As the context length
T
goes infinity, we show that the length of the critical window goes to
0
.

Example 3

(Two Dirac delta functions with a random masking procedure)
.

Let
p\in\{\pm 1\}^{T}
, and consider a forward process with index set
\mathbf{I}=[0,1]
,
Y_{0}=X
, and
Y_{t}\in\{\pm 1,\mathrm{[MASKED]}\}^{T}
. For
t\in\mathbf{I}
, we let all the value at index
i\in[T]
be set to
\mathrm{[MASKED]}
with probability
t
independently. For a mixture of two Dirac delta functions, we can express the critical window in terms of the
Hamming distance
between the corresponding strings. Let
\Theta=\{\theta_{\pm 1}\},\ell_{\pm 1}\in\{\pm 1\}^{T},p^{\theta_{\pm 1}}\sim\delta_{\ell_{\pm 1}},w_{\pm 1}=\frac{1}{2}
. Then, on component
1
we have the critical window

\displaystyle T_{\mathrm{before}}^{C}=\exp\left[\frac{\ln(1-\epsilon)}{d_{H}(\delta_{\ell_{1}},\delta_{\ell_{-1}})}\right],T_{\mathrm{after}}^{C}=\exp\left[\frac{\ln\epsilon^{2}}{d_{H}(\delta_{\ell_{1}},\delta_{\ell_{-1}})}\right]

(2)

For sufficiently large
d_{H}(\delta_{\ell_{1}},\delta_{\ell_{-1}})
, the window size
T_{\mathrm{before}}^{C}-T_{\mathrm{after}}^{C}=O\left(\frac{1}{d_{H}(\delta_{\ell_{1}},\delta_{\ell_{-1}})}\right)
. If
d_{H}(\delta_{\ell_{1}},\delta_{\ell_{-1}})
increases with
T
, then the width of the critical window negligible.

Autoregression

Example 4

(“Critical Tokens” for Jailbreaks and Yellowstone
(
QPL+, 24
;
Ant, 24
)
)
.

Again consider an autoregressive language model, with
\mathcal{A}
denoting the vocabulary,
p\in\mathcal{A}^{T}
, a forward process indexed by
\mathbf{I}=\{0,1,2,\dots,T\}
, and
Y_{t}
to be the first
T-t
tokens of
X
. Let
\Theta=\{\theta_{\textbf{harmful}},\theta_{\textbf{safe}}\}
(or
\{\theta_{\textbf{Googling Yellowstone}},\theta_{\textbf{coding}}\}

T-T^{\prime}\in\mathbf{I}
. Between
T-T^{\prime}
and
T-T^{\prime}-k
, the distributions become nearly disjoint,
P_{x\sim p_{T-T^{\prime}-k}^{\theta_{\textbf{harmful}}}}\left(x\in\mathrm{supp}(p_{T-T^{\prime}-k}^{\theta_{\textbf{safe}}})\right)\leq\epsilon.
In the jailbreaking example,

and they are disjoint because the first tokens generated in the safe mode is always some form of refusal. In the Yellowstone example, they are disjoint the first time the agent decides to Google Yellowstone pictures. Then, on component

Notice that we can actually mitigate the effect of these critical windows by finetuning on examples of corrections to increase

. This explains the effectiveness of finetuning on corrections in
(

)
. Furthermore, the quantity that measures probability of mode-switching,
p^{\theta_{\textbf{harmful}}}/p
, suggests using a likelihood ratio to distinguish between harmful and benign prompts. In App.
D.1.2

\times
the false positive rate for
5
different types of jailbreaks (Table
2
). We can also identify a critical window for a stylized model of solving a math problem as a random walk.

Example 5

(Math problem-solving as a random walk)
.

We model solving a math problem as taking a random walk on
\mdmathbb{Z}
with stepsize
1

T
. If the random walk hits
+A
, then it has ‘solved‘ the problem; if the random walk hits
-A
, then it has obtained an incorrect solution. Assume that we have two modes: a strong problem solving mode (denoted
+1
), which takes a
+1
step with probability
0.5+\Delta
, and a weak problem solving mode (denoted
-1
), which takes a
+1

0.5-\Delta
. Assuming that
\frac{\ln(2/\epsilon^{2})}{2\Delta^{2}}<A
and
\epsilon^{2}<10^{-3}(0.5-\Delta)(0.5+\Delta)

T_{\mathrm{before}}^{C}=T-\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{\Delta^{2}}+2

Note the critical window has width

4.2.1
In-context learning

Autoregressive critical windows can also be applied to describe in-context learning. In particular, we can capture the idea that with sufficiently many in-context examples, we learn the
\theta^{*}\in\Theta
that generated the transitions for in-context examples, with a sample complexity in terms of
T_{\mathrm{after}}^{C}
.

Example 6

(Informal, see Example
11
)
.

Consider an in-context learning setup, where the context

[x_{1},y_{1},o,\dots,x_{T+1},y_{T+1},o]

consists of question-answer pairs
(x_{i},y_{i})
, delimiters
o
, and
x_{i}\to y_{i}
sampled from
p^{\theta^{*}}
for some
\theta^{*}\in\Theta
. In the forward-reverse experiment, we truncate it to
[x_{1},y_{1},o,\dots,x_{T+1}]
, and then resample with
p
to produce
[x_{1},\dots,x_{T+1},\tilde{y}_{T+1},o]
. The total variation between the sequences
[x_{1},y_{1},o,\dots,x_{T+1}]
and
[x_{1},\dots,x_{T+1},\tilde{y}_{T+1},o]
can be viewed as the average-case error of the in-context learner and can be bounded within our critical windows framework. We have
T_{\mathrm{after}}^{C}=3T+3-O_{\epsilon}(1)
, with
O_{\epsilon}(1)
independent of
T
(
S_{\mathrm{after}}\triangleq\{\theta^{*}\}
). Note that
T_{\mathrm{after}}^{C}
is the order of how many samples that can be erased so that we still are able to learn
\theta^{*}\in\Theta
.

One might ask if there is a
T_{\mathrm{before}}^{C}
for in-context learning, a threshold such that it is impossible to distinguish between
S_{\mathrm{after}},S_{\mathrm{before}}
with that many samples. In the next section, we will provide an example of a
T_{\mathrm{before}}^{C}
for in-context learning with the all-or-nothing phase transition.

4.2.2
All-or-nothing phenomenon

Here we elucidate a formal connection between the critical windows phenomenon in in-context learning and the
all-or-nothing
phenomenon. To begin, we first define the notions of strong and weak detection:

Definition 5
.

Let
(N_{s})
be an increasing sequence of integers. Given sequences of distributions
(p_{s}),(q_{s})
over
z\in\mdmathbb{R}^{N_{s}}
, a sequence of test statistics
(\mathcal{A}_{s}:\mdmathbb{R}^{N_{s}}\to\mdmathbb{R})
with threshold
(\tau_{s})
achieves:

•

strong detection
if
\limsup_{s\to\infty}\{\Pr_{z\sim p_{s}}[\mathcal{A}_{s}(z)<\tau_{s}]+\Pr_{z\sim q_{s}}[\mathcal{A}_{s}(z)\geq\tau_{s}]\}=0
.

•

weak detection
if
\limsup_{s\to\infty}\{\Pr_{z\sim p_{s}}[\mathcal{A}_{s}(z)<\tau_{s}]+\Pr_{z\sim q_{s}}[\mathcal{A}_{s}(z)\geq\tau_{s}]\}<1
.

By the operational characterization of TV distance, strong detection is (information-theoretically) possible if and only if
\liminf_{s\to\infty}\mathrm{TV}(p_{s},q_{s})=1
, and weak detection is (information-theoretically) possible if and only if
\liminf_{s\to\infty}\mathrm{TV}(p_{s},q_{s})>0
.

Now we consider the following Bayesian inference problem, given by a joint distribution
\pi
over
(\theta,z)\in\mdmathbb{R}^{n}\times\mdmathbb{R}^{m}
. Nature samples unknown signal
\theta\in\mdmathbb{R}^{n}
from
\pi_{\theta}
. Given sample size
N
, we receive observations
\{z_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
drawn i.i.d. from
\pi_{z\mid\theta}
; the goal is to infer
\theta
from these observations. Let
\pi^{(N)}
denote the distribution over
\{z_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
, the mixture of product measures parametrized by
\theta
.

Definition 6
.

Let
(\pi_{s})
be a sequence of inference tasks over
\mdmathbb{R}^{n_{s}}\times\mdmathbb{R}^{m_{s}}
and
(\pi^{\mathsf{null}_{s}})
be a sequence of distributions over
\mdmathbb{R}^{m_{s}}
.
(\pi_{s})
exhibits an
all-or-nothing phase transition at threshold
(N_{s})
with respect to null models
(\pi^{\mathsf{null}_{s}})
if:

•

For any
\beta<1
: weak detection between
(\pi^{(\beta N_{s})})
and
((\pi^{\mathsf{null}_{s}})^{\otimes\beta N_{s}})
is information-theoretically impossible

•

For any
\beta>1
: strong detection between the planted model
(\pi^{(\beta N_{s})})
and the null model
((\pi^{\mathsf{null}_{s}})^{\otimes\beta N_{s}})
is information-theoretically possible

All-or-nothing phase transitions have been established for a number of natural inference tasks like sparse linear regression
(
RXZ, 19
;
GZ, 19
)
, sparse PCA
(
NWZ, 20
)
, generalized linear models
(
BMR, 20
)
, group testing
(
TAS, 21
;
COGHK+, 22
)
, linear and phase retrieval models
(
SC, 16
;
TS, 20
)
, planted subgraphs
(
MNWS+, 23
)
, and planted Gaussian perceptron
(
NWZ, 23
)
. Here is an example for sparse linear regression:

Theorem 5

(
RXZ (19)
)
.

Let
\pi_{s}
be the distribution over
\mdmathbb{R}^{n_{s}}\times\mdmathbb{R}^{m_{s}}
for
n_{s}=s
and
m_{s}=s+1
where the marginal over
\theta
is given by the uniform distribution over
k_{s}
-sparse vectors in
\{0,1\}^{s}
, and the conditional distribution
\pi_{z\mid\theta}
is given by sampling
x\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\mathrm{Id}_{s})
, taking
y=\langle\theta,x\rangle+\xi
for
\xi\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}_{s})
, and outputting observation

\pi^{\sf null}_{s}
is given by sampling

If
\sigma^{2}_{s}\ll k_{s}\leq s^{0.499}

Having defined the all-or-nothing phenomenon, we rigorously instantiate it as a critical window for in-context learning. We first define a mixture model
p^{\Theta}_{(N)}
for sequence lengths
N
onto which we will identify a critical window.

Definition 7
.

To any inference task
\pi
, null model
\pi^{\sf null}
, and sequence length
N
, we can associate the following in-context learning task. Let
\Theta=\Theta_{\sf signal}\sqcup\{\mathsf{NULL}\}

\Theta_{\sf signal}\triangleq\mathrm{supp}(\pi_{\theta})

\theta\in\mathrm{\mathrm{supp}}(\pi_{\theta})

p_{(N)}^{\theta}

(z_{1},\ldots,z_{N},\mathrm{?},\theta)

p_{(N)}^{\mathsf{null}}
denote the distribution over observations
(z_{1},\ldots,z_{N},\mathrm{?},\mathsf{NULL})
where
z_{1},\ldots,z_{N}
are i.i.d. samples from
\pi^{\sf null}

Under this model of data, we have the following theorem expressing the all-or-nothing phase transition in terms of
T_{\mathrm{before}}^{C},T_{\mathrm{after}}^{C}

Theorem 6
.

Suppose
(\pi_{s})
is a sequence of inference tasks that exhibits an all-or-nothing phase transition at threshold
(N^{*}_{s})
with respect to null models
(\pi_{s})
. Given
N_{s}\geq N^{*}_{s}
, let
(p_{(N_{s})}^{s;\theta})_{\theta\in\Theta_{s}}
denote the sequence of in-context learning tasks. For any constant
0<\epsilon<1
, there exist constants
\delta,\underline{s}
such that for all
s\geq\underline{s}
, next-token prediction for
(p_{(N_{s})}^{s;\theta})_{\theta\in\Theta_{s}}
exhibits a critical window over
[N_{s}+2-(1+\delta)N^{*}_{s},N_{s}+2--(1-\delta)N^{*}_{s}]
in which we transition from sampling a distribution
O(\epsilon)
-close in TV to
S_{\rm before}=\Theta_{s;\mathsf{signal}}
, to sampling from a distribution
O(\epsilon)
-close in TV to
S_{\rm after}=\Theta_{s}
.

In other words, we have
T_{\mathrm{before}}^{C}\triangleq N+2-(1-\delta)N^{*}_{s}
and
T_{\mathrm{after}}^{C}\triangleq N+2-(1+\delta)N^{*}_{s}
.

is essentially immediate from Theorem
2
and the definition of the all-or-nothing phase transition:

5
Hierarchies in stochastic localization samplers

Herein we propose a theory of hierarchical sampling within our critical windows framework.
It is motivated by the observation that a single trajectory can contain multiple critical windows (Figure
4
), each splitting a sub-population into smaller sub-populations. This hierarchy is naturally represented as a tree: the root signifies that all sub-populations are indistinguishable under enough noise, while the leafs represent distinct modes in
p
. A path from the root to a leaf captures the progressive refinement of the original distribution
p
into increasingly specific components. To formalize this, we introduce the concept of an
\epsilon
-mixture tree, which decomposes
p
into a hierarchical structure.

Definition 8
.

For an error term
\epsilon>0
and mixture model
p
, an
\epsilon
-mixture tree
is a tuple

(T,\{P^{\scriptscriptstyle\rightarrow}(\cdot|\cdot)\},\mathbf{I},\Theta,\{p^{\theta}\}_{\theta\in\Theta},\text{{Subset}},\text{{NoiseAmount}}).

The tree
T=(V,E)
is associated with a function Subset
:V\to 2^{\Theta}\backslash\{\emptyset\}
, which maps vertices to sub-mixtures. We require
Subset
satisfies the following two properties: (1) Subset
(\textrm{root})=\Theta
; (2) If
u
is a parent of
v
,
\textrm{Subset}(v)\subset\textrm{Subset}(u)
. We consider a NoiseAmount
:V\to\mdmathbb{R}^{\geq 0}
, which characterizes the noise levels that result in the aggregations of mixture components described by vertices in the mixture tree.
\text{{NoiseAmount}}(u)
is defined such that all
p^{\theta}_{\text{{NoiseAmount}}(u)}
for
\theta\in\text{{Subset}}(u)
overlap greatly and for
p^{\text{{Subset}}(u)}_{\text{{NoiseAmount}}(u)},p^{\Theta-\text{{Subset}}(u)}_{\text{{NoiseAmount}}(u)}
have negligible overlap. Thus we require that
NoiseAmount
satisfy three properties: (1) For distinct
\theta_{i},\theta_{j}\in\Theta
with leaf nodes
w,v
such that
\theta_{i}\in\textrm{Subset}(w),\theta_{j}\in\textrm{Subset}(v)
, if
u
is the lowest common ancestor of
w,v

\mathrm{TV}(p^{\theta_{i}}_{\textrm{NoiseAmount}(u)},p^{\theta_{j}}_{\textrm{NoiseAmount}(u)})\leq\epsilon

u\in V

\textrm{Subset}(u)
and
\Theta-\textrm{Subset}(u)
in terms of
\mathrm{TV}

\mathrm{TV}(p^{\textrm{Subset}(u)}_{\textrm{NoiseAmount}(u)},p^{\Theta-\textrm{Subset}(u)}_{\textrm{NoiseAmount}(u)})\geq 1-\epsilon^{2};
and (3) If

is a parent of

\textrm{NoiseAmount}(u)<\textrm{NoiseAmount}(v)

establishes bounds on

T_{\mathrm{start}}^{S}

Discussion

In this work, we developed a simple yet general theory for critical windows for stochastic localization samplers like diffusion and autoregressive models. Already, practitioners have applied critical windows to make LLMs safer
(
QPL+, 24
)
and reason better
(
AAB+, 24
;
LLX+, 24
)
. Our theory significantly streamlines our understanding of critical windows and provides concrete insights for practitioners. We pair our theory with extensive experiments, and demonstrate its usefulness in monitoring for jailbreaks and understanding reasoning failures.

Limitations.

The theory applies to the most prominent and empirically successful generative models (autoregressive language models, continuous diffusion and flow matching models). However, some less widely-used generative models do not belong to the family of localization-based samplers. Generative adversarial networks
(
GPAM+, 14
)
and consistency models
(
SDCS, 23
)
both use a singular evaluation of a neural network to map noise into an image. However, we argue the restriction to the localization-based samplers is extremely minor because these models are either not widely used in practice or based on localization-based samplers.

Future Work.

While we instantiate our theory in many settings (Section
4
), an important future direction is compute the location of critical windows in other settings and connect it to statistical physics predictions for other models of data, e.g., the random hierarchy model
(
SFLW, 24
)
.

Impact statement.

Though this paper is largely theoretical in nature, it does describe a theory for jailbreaks which could impact model safety in the future. We hope the insights in this manuscript about jailbreaks lead to better alignment strategies and training methods.

Chain of thought.

[
42
,
1
]
also observed that the presence of critical windows in the chain of thought of math and reasoning tasks and their significance in leading the model to incorrect outputs, concurrent with our results in Figure

Existing work on jailbreaks has studied the appearance of critical windows in the first few generated tokens
[
57
,
74
,
35
,

]

[
57
]

4

) similar to the adversarial contrast decoding method proposed by
[

]

Example 8

[

For continuous diffusion models, the forward process progressively degrades samples
X\sim p
into pure Gaussian noise through scaling and convolution with Gaussian noise. It is the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process, a stochastic process
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
given by the following stochastic differential equation (SDE),

\mathrm{d}X_{t}=-X_{t}\,\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{2}\mathrm{d}B_{t}\,,\qquad X_{0}\sim p\,,

(4)

where
(B_{t})_{t\geq 0}

q_{t}\triangleq\mathrm{law}(X_{t})
for

, and observe that as
t\to\infty

converges exponentially quickly to the standard Gaussian distribution
\mathcal{N}(0,\mathrm{Id})
. Assume we end the forward process at time

For the reverse process
(X^{\scriptscriptstyle\leftarrow}_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
, we employ the

\mathrm{d}X^{\scriptscriptstyle\leftarrow}_{t}=\{X^{\scriptscriptstyle\leftarrow}_{t}+2\nabla\ln q_{T-t}(X^{\scriptscriptstyle\leftarrow}_{t})\}\,\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{2}\,\mathrm{d}B_{t}\,,\qquad X^{\scriptscriptstyle\leftarrow}_{T}\sim q_{T}\,

(5)

where here
(B_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is also a Brownian motion. Defining
(Y_{t})_{t\in\mathbf{I}}=(X_{t})_{t\in\mathbf{I}}
, we see that the forward process satisfies the Markov property in Definition
1
, and the information from the original sample
X_{0}
is degraded by more steps in the SDE. Furthermore, the reverse SDE with parameterized by the score function
\nabla\ln q_{T-t}(X^{\scriptscriptstyle\leftarrow}_{t})

\displaystyle\mathrm{TV}((C^{u}_{t})_{t\in[\widehat{T},T]},(C^{v}_{t})_{t\in[\widehat{T},T]})\leq\underbrace{\mathrm{TV}((C^{u}_{t})_{t\in[\widehat{T},T]},(C^{u\to v}_{t})_{t\in[\widehat{T},T]})}_{\text{(I)}}+\underbrace{\mathrm{TV}((C^{u\to v}_{t})_{t\in[\widehat{T},T]},(C^{v}_{t})_{t\in[\widehat{T},T]})}_{\text{(II)}}.

To bound (I), observe that the SDEs have different scores but the same initializations. We apply Theorem
10
to
\mathrm{TV}((C^{u}_{t})_{t\in[\widehat{T},T]},(C^{u\to v}_{t})_{t\in[\widehat{T},T]})
and obtain

\displaystyle\mathrm{TV}((C^{u}_{t})_{t\in[\widehat{T},T]},(C^{u\to v}_{t})_{t\in[\widehat{T},T]})

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\mathrm{KL}((C^{u}_{t})_{t\in[\widehat{T},T]}||(C^{u\to v}_{t})_{t\in[\widehat{T},T]})}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\int_{\widehat{T}}^{T}\mdmathbb{E}_{X\sim C^{u}_{t}}\|\nabla\ln p^{u}_{t}(X)-\nabla\ln p^{v}_{t}(X)\|^{2}dt}

We simplify the inner expectation by using the

\displaystyle\|\nabla\ln p^{u}_{t}(X)-\nabla\ln p^{v}_{t}(X)\|

\displaystyle=\left\|\left[(\mathrm{Id}+\alpha e^{-2t}uu^{\top})^{-1}-(\mathrm{Id}+\alpha e^{-2t}vv^{\top})^{-1}\right]x\right\|

\displaystyle=\left\|\left[\left(\mathrm{Id}-\frac{\alpha e^{-2t}}{1+\alpha e^{-2t}}uu^{\top}\right)-\left(\mathrm{Id}-\frac{\alpha e^{-2t}}{1+\alpha e^{-2t}}vv^{\top}\right)\right]x\right\|

\displaystyle\lesssim\alpha e^{-2t}\|uu^{\top}-vv^{\top}\|_{\rm op}\left\|\prod_{\textrm{span}(u,v)}x\right\|,

\displaystyle=\alpha e^{-2t}\sqrt{1-\langle u,v\rangle^{2}}\left\|\prod_{\textrm{span}(u,v)}x\right\|.\quad(\text{Lemma}~\ref{lem:opnormtwovec})

We can upper bound
\mdmathbb{E}_{X\sim C^{u}_{t}}\|\prod_{\textrm{span}(u,v)}X\|^{2}
by considering right-triangular
L
such that
L^{\top}L=\mathrm{Id}+\alpha uu^{\top}
. and
\prod_{\textrm{span}(u,v)}X=\prod_{\textrm{span}(u,v)}LY
, where
Y\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\mathrm{Id})
. The operator norm of
\prod_{\textrm{span}(u,v)}L
is

\displaystyle\left\|\prod_{\textrm{span}(u,v)}\circ L\right\|_{\rm op}\leq\left\|L\right\|_{\rm op}\leq\sqrt{1+\alpha^{2}e^{-2t}}\leq\sqrt{1+\alpha^{2}}.

\prod_{\textrm{span}(u,v)}\circ L
is also rank
2
and
\left(\prod_{\textrm{span}(u,v)}\circ L\right)Y=\left(\prod_{\textrm{span}(u,v)}\circ L\right)\circ\prod_{L^{-1}\textrm{span}(u,v)}Y
, where
\prod_{L^{-1}\textrm{span}(u,v)}Y\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\mathrm{Id}_{2})
. Thus we have

\displaystyle\mdmathbb{E}_{Y\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\mathrm{Id})}\left[\left\|\left(\prod_{\textrm{span}(u,v)}\circ L\right)Y\right\|^{2}\right]=\mdmathbb{E}_{Y^{\prime}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\mathrm{Id}_{2})}\left[\left\|\left(\prod_{\textrm{span}(u,v)}\circ L\right)Y^{\prime}\right\|^{2}\right]\lesssim 1+\alpha^{2}

Combining this information together, we are able to compute,

\displaystyle\mathrm{TV}((C^{u}_{t})_{t\in[\widehat{T},T]},(C^{u\to v}_{t})_{t\in[\widehat{T},T]})

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\int_{\widehat{T}}^{T}\mdmathbb{E}_{X\sim C^{u}_{t}}\|\nabla\ln p^{u}_{t}(X)-\nabla\ln p^{v}_{t}(X)\|^{2}dt}

\displaystyle\lesssim\alpha\sqrt{1+\alpha^{2}}\sqrt{1-\langle u,v\rangle^{2}}e^{-2\widehat{T}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\epsilon.

To bound (II), we observe that both are run with the same score so we need only bound the difference at initialization. By the data processing inequality, we again have
\mathrm{TV}((C^{u\to v}_{t})_{t\in[\widehat{T},T]},(C^{v}_{t})_{t\in[\widehat{T},T]})\leq\mathrm{TV}(p^{u}_{T},p^{v}_{T})
. We can again apply the triangle inequality to get
\mathrm{TV}(p^{u}_{T},p^{v}_{T})\leq\mathrm{TV}(p^{u}_{T},{}^{d})+\mathrm{TV}({}^{d},p^{v}_{T})\leq
. For any
\theta\in\Theta
, we have by the forward convergence of the OU process
\mathrm{TV}(p^{u}_{T},{}^{d})\leq\exp(-T)\sqrt{\mathrm{KL}(p_{u}||{}^{d})}
. We can explicitly compute
\mathrm{KL}(p_{u}||{}^{d})
as

\displaystyle\mathrm{KL}(p_{u}||{}^{d})=\frac{1}{2}\left[d+\alpha-d-\log(\mathrm{Id}+\alpha uu^{\top})\right]=\frac{1}{2}\left[\alpha-\log(1+\alpha)\right].

Thus, we obtain the following bound on

\mathrm{TV}((C^{u\to v}_{t})_{t\in[\widehat{T},T]},(C^{v}_{t})_{t\in[\widehat{T},T]})
of

\displaystyle\mathrm{TV}((C^{u\to v}_{t})_{t\in[\widehat{T},T]},(C^{v}_{t})_{t\in[\widehat{T},T]})

\displaystyle\lesssim\exp(-T)\sqrt{\alpha-\log(1+\alpha)}\lesssim\epsilon.\qed

C.2.4
Interweaving transitions from other distributions

In this section, we extend our critical windows framework to the setting where at certain steps of sampling procedure, instead of using the reverse Markov transition kernel from the original stochastic localization sampler, we use an alternative distribution which is not necessarily related to the original sampler. This includes many important applications of generative models, in which one seeks to combine the priors learned from data with some other algorithm. For example, one may want to combine the language model with a problem generation oracle in in-context learning
[
19
]
.

As
[
72
]
points out, the transition from the answer to one problem to the problem statement of another example in-context learning is determined by an alternative transition kernel (which they call
p_{\textrm{prompt}}
). Although the probability of transition from one answer to the problem statement of another example is extremely low under the natural data distribution, one still hopes that with sufficiently many samples, the model selects the correct
\theta^{*}\in\Theta
if these lower probability transitions are overcome by the distributional difference for
\theta\in\Theta
with
\theta\neq\theta^{*}
. Similarly, under our critical windows framework, we can hope to capture the idea that we specialize to a particular
\theta^{*}
given a sufficiently long context. In Section
C.2.5
, we first present a general framework for characterizing critical windows in this setting. Then, in section
C.2.6
, we consider the case of in-context learning by autoregressive language models and prove convergence.

C.2.5
General interweaving framework

We present this framework for the case where the index set
\mathbf{I}=\{0,1,\dots,m\}
is discrete. Like before, assume we have a series of reverse Markov transition kernels
P^{\scriptscriptstyle\leftarrow,p}_{k\to k-1}(\cdot|\cdot)
, for
k\in\mathbf{I}
, but we also assume we have an alternative distribution
P^{\scriptscriptstyle\leftarrow,\mathrm{alt}}_{k\to k-1}(\cdot|\cdot)
that we use to sample for transitions
k\in A\subsetneq\mathbf{I}
. For our sampling procedure, we sample
Y_{m}
, and for
k=m-1,m-2,\dots,0
, we take
Y_{k}\sim P^{\scriptscriptstyle\leftarrow,p}_{k+1\to k}(\cdot|Y_{k+1})
for
k\in\mathbf{I}-A
and
Y_{k}\sim P^{\scriptscriptstyle\leftarrow,\mathrm{alt}}_{k+1\to k}(\cdot|Y_{k+1})
for
k\in A
. We denote the final distribution
p^{\mathrm{alt}}
.

Now, we also need to adjust our definitions of
p^{S}
to this particular sampling procedure. We define
p^{S,\mathrm{alt}}
for
S\subset\Theta
to the distribution over outputs when we instead use the kernels
P^{\scriptscriptstyle\leftarrow}_{k+1\to k}(\cdot|Y_{k+1},S)
instead of
P^{\scriptscriptstyle\leftarrow}_{k+1\to k}(\cdot|Y_{k+1})
. To relate
p^{\theta,\mathrm{alt}}
to
p^{\theta,\mathrm{alt}}
for
\theta\in\Theta
, we need to assume transitions from
\mathrm{alt}
do not affect the posterior distribution over
p^{\Theta}(\theta|Y_{t})
.

Assumption 1
.

For all
y\in\mdmathbb{R}
and
x\in\mathrm{supp}(P^{\scriptscriptstyle\leftarrow,\mathrm{alt}}_{k+1\to k}(\cdot|y))
, we have for all
\theta,\theta^{\prime}\in\Theta
, the equality
P^{\scriptscriptstyle\leftarrow}_{k+1\to k}(x|y,\theta)=P^{\scriptscriptstyle\leftarrow}_{k+1\to k}(x|y,\theta^{\prime})
.

Adopting our definitions from Section
3
, we let

\displaystyle T_{\mathrm{lower},\mathrm{alt}}(\epsilon)\in\{

\displaystyle t\in\mathbf{I}:\mathrm{TV}(p_{t}^{S_{\mathrm{init}},\mathrm{alt}},p_{t}^{S_{\mathrm{target}},\mathrm{alt}})\leq\epsilon\}

(9)

\displaystyle T_{\mathrm{upper},\mathrm{alt}}(\epsilon)\in\{

\displaystyle t\in\mathbf{I}:\mathrm{TV}(p_{t}^{S_{\mathrm{target}},\mathrm{alt}},p_{t}^{\Theta-S_{\mathrm{target}},\mathrm{alt}})\geq 1-\epsilon^{2}\,\}.

(10)

The main challenge of the below corollary is simply show that the final distribution
p^{\Theta,\mathrm{alt}}
can be written as a mixture of
p^{\theta,\mathrm{alt}}
with the same mixing weights as before.

Corollary 3
.

Under Assumption
1
, for
\epsilon>0
, if
\widehat{T}\geq T_{\mathrm{lower},\mathrm{alt}}(\epsilon)
and
\widehat{T}\leq T_{\mathrm{upper},\mathrm{alt}}(\epsilon)
, then

\mathrm{TV}(p^{S_{\mathrm{init}},\widehat{T}},p^{S_{\mathrm{target}},\mathrm{alt}})\leq\left(1+\sqrt{2}\max\left(1,\frac{\sum_{\theta\in\Theta-S_{\mathrm{target}}}w_{\theta}}{\sum_{\theta\in S_{\mathrm{target}}}w_{\theta}}\right)/2\right)\epsilon.

(11)

Proof.

We need only show that
p^{\Theta,\mathrm{alt}}\triangleq\sum_{\theta\in\Theta}w_{\theta}p^{\theta,\mathrm{alt}}
. It suffices to shows that the probability of generating a path
Y_{m},Y_{m-1},\dots,Y_{0}
are the same under both density functions. We need only consider transitions for
k\in\mathbf{I}-A
, because for
k\in A
, the transitions are both given by the alternative distribution. For the transitions not given by
\mathrm{alt}
, note that we are using the original model, so

\displaystyle p^{\Theta,\mathrm{alt}}(Y_{k-1}|Y_{k})=\frac{\sum_{\theta\in\Theta}w_{\theta}p^{\theta}(Y_{k})P^{\scriptscriptstyle\leftarrow}_{k\to k-1}(Y_{k-1}|Y_{k},\theta)}{\sum_{\theta\in\Theta}w_{\theta}p^{\theta}(Y_{k})}.

(12)

Furthermore, for the mixture model, this probability is

\displaystyle p^{\mathrm{mix}}=\frac{\sum_{\theta\in\Theta}w_{\theta}p^{\theta,\mathrm{alt}}(Y_{m},Y_{m-1},\dots,Y_{k+1},Y_{k})P^{\scriptscriptstyle\leftarrow}_{k\to k-1}(Y_{k-1}|Y_{k},\theta)}{\sum_{\theta\in\Theta}w_{\theta}p^{\theta,\mathrm{alt}}(Y_{m},Y_{m-1},\dots,Y_{k+1},Y_{k})}.

(13)

The distinction between Equation
12
and Equation
13
is that in the former we are using the likelihood of
p^{\theta}
instead of
p^{\theta,\mathrm{alt}}
. Thus it suffices to show that
p^{\theta}\propto p^{\theta,\mathrm{alt}}
. We explicitly write out the probability,

\displaystyle p^{\theta}(Y_{m},Y_{m-1},\dots,Y_{k+1},Y_{k})=\prod_{i=k+1}^{m}p^{\theta}(Y_{i-1}|Y_{i})\propto\prod_{i=k+1,i\notin A}^{m}p^{\theta}(Y_{i-1}|Y_{i}),

where the proportionality follows from the fact that we can ignore the probability of the transitions produced by
\mathrm{alt}
under Assumption
1
. By definition, this is proportional to
p^{\theta,\mathrm{alt}}(Y_{m},Y_{m-1},\dots,Y_{k+1},Y_{k})
up to a normalization constant independent of
\theta
.
∎

C.2.6
In-context learning

Now, we will specialize our framework to the case of in-context learning. As in
[
72
]
, we assume that the language model is given inputs of the form
[x_{1},y_{1},o,x_{2},y_{2},o,\dots,x_{t},y_{t},o,x_{t+1}]
, where
x_{1}
is the input,
y_{1}
is the output, and
o
is a delimiter token that separate different in-context samples from each other. We assume that the transitions
y_{i}\to o\to x_{i+1}
are sampled by some alternative probability distribution
P_{\textrm{other}}(\cdot|\cdot)
. We require that
P_{\textrm{other}}
selects the
x_{i}
i.i.d.

Assumption 2
.

The distribution of
P_{\textrm{other}}(x_{t+1}|x_{1},y_{1},o,x_{2},y_{2},o,\dots,x_{t},y_{t},o)=P_{\textrm{other}}(x_{1})
.

Then we assume that the transitions
x_{i}\to y_{i}
are generated by some
\theta^{*}\in\Theta
, which does not depend on any of the previous tokens before the delimiter.

Assumption 3
.

(Well-specification)
There exists some
\theta^{*}\in\Theta
such that
y_{i}
is generated from
y_{i}\sim P^{\leftarrow}(\cdot|[x_{1},y_{1},o_{1},\dots,o_{i-1},x_{i}],\theta^{*})
.

Assumption 4
.

For all
\theta\in\Theta
, we have
P^{\leftarrow}(\cdot|[x_{1},y_{1},o_{1},\dots,o_{i-1},x_{i}],\theta)=P^{\leftarrow}(\cdot|x_{i},\theta)
.

We also assume statistical separation of
\theta^{*}
from
\Theta-\{\theta^{*}\}
in terms of Hellinger distance.

Assumption 5
.

Let
p_{(x,y)}^{S}
for
S\subset\Theta
be the distribution of
(x_{1},y_{1},o)
where
x_{1}\sim P_{\textrm{other}}(\cdot|o)
and
y_{1}\sim p^{S}(\cdot|x_{1})
. There exists
\delta>0
such that
H^{2}(p_{(x,y)}^{\Theta-\{\theta^{*}\}},p_{(x,y)}^{\theta^{*}})\geq\delta.

Example 11
.

Let
T\geq\ln\left(\frac{1-\delta/2}{\epsilon}\right)
. Under Assumptions
1
,
2
,
3
,
4
, and
5
, we have

\mathrm{TV}([x_{1},\dots,x_{T+1},y_{T+1}],[x_{1},\dots,x_{T+1},\tilde{y}_{T+1}])\lesssim\epsilon/w_{\theta^{*}}.

Proof.

It suffices to upper bound
1-\frac{1}{2}H^{2}(P^{\theta,\mathrm{alt}}_{3T},P^{\Theta-\{\theta\},\mathrm{alt}}_{3T})
by
O(\epsilon)
. First observe that the distribution
p^{S,\mathrm{alt}}
for
S\subset\Theta
factors along the delimiters by a factor independent of
S
using Assumptions
2
and
4
, so we have

P^{S,\mathrm{alt}}_{3t}(x_{1},y_{1},o_{1},x_{2},y_{2},o_{2},\dots,x_{t},y_{t},o_{t})\propto\prod_{i=1}^{t}P^{S,\mathrm{alt}}_{3}(x_{i},y_{i},o_{i}).

Using the tensorization property of Hellinger distance and our definition of
T,\delta
, we have

\displaystyle 1-\frac{1}{2}H^{2}(P^{\theta,\mathrm{alt}}_{3T},P^{\Theta-\{\theta\},\mathrm{alt}}_{3T})

\displaystyle\lesssim\left[1-\frac{1}{2}H^{2}(P^{\theta,\mathrm{alt}}_{3},P^{\Theta-\{\theta\},\mathrm{alt}}_{3})\right]^{T}\leq\epsilon.\qed
