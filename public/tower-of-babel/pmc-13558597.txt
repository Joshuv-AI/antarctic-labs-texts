Batteryless wireless tilt sensor with self-calibration capability for structural health monitoring
Authors: Ali-Reza Moznebi, Hamidreza Laribi, Kambiz Moez, Rashid Mirzavand
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAging infrastructures require constant monitoring to prevent major disasters. Accurate tilt detection is vital in determining the instability of bridges and foundations; however, existing systems depend on batteries, cables, and periodic calibration. We present a battery-free wireless tilt sensor based on a dual-band microwave structure that allows self-calibration against varying environmental effects and distance ambiguity. The sensor uses harvested energy from signals transmitted by a reader and integrates sensing, communication, and antenna functionalities into one device. The measurement results show that tracking the phase difference between two bands mitigates environmental factors. A 36° phase shift over a tilt range of -10° to +10° is observed, corresponding to a sensitivity of 1.8° of measured phase per 1° of actual tilt. The system is fully integrated, IoT-ready, and facilitates easy integration into a sensor network. It also creates a reliable platform for monitoring critical infrastructures in real-time to ensure rapid measurement of tilts from multiple sensors.Subject terms: Mechanical engineering, Electrical and electronic engineering, Civil engineering

Introduction

The collapse of the Morandi Bridge in Genoa, Italy1, and the tragedy of the Champlain Towers South condominium in Surfside, Florida2, tragically reveal the catastrophic consequences of neglected structural health monitoring (SHM)3. However, even beyond these high-profile failures, today’s transportation infrastructure remains a cornerstone of economic growth and public safety, now threatened by rapid deterioration. According to recent evaluations by the American Society of Civil Engineers (ASCE)4 and the Federal Highway Administration (FHWA)5, more than 43,000 bridges in the United States are classified as poor and more than 305,000 are considered fair, triple the figure from three decades ago. This has been driven by severe weather patterns, high-traffic movements, and the cost of maintenance activities. Higher vulnerability to breakdowns has been witnessed due to the aforementioned factors.

Although national regulations require routine bridge inspections6, the available techniques involve visual inspections or the use of basic instruments7. Consequently, the collected data tend to be inconsistent and rife with human errors8. In addition to traditional techniques, SHM has proven effective as a proactive approach. By installing sensors capable of tracking damage-sensitive features, such as the strain, inclination of the bridge foundations, and vibration signals, SHM provides real-time information on the condition of a structure9–13. Through this data-driven framework, potential failures can be detected and addressed long before they escalate, ultimately reducing repair costs, improving safety, and extending the service life of critical infrastructure.

Numerous sensor technologies have been explored in academic and industrial research for measuring tilt (or inclination) in bridge foundations and other critical structures. Traditional solutions, including mechanical and servo inclinometers, are highly reliable but often more complex and expensive to implement, requiring frequent calibration14, electrolytic tilt sensors, which provide precise data through fluid-level changes but can be vulnerable to temperature fluctuations15, and MEMS (Micro-Electro-Mechanical Systems)-based sensors, stand out for their small size and low power consumption but are sometimes limited in measuring small angular displacements16. Other advanced methods, such as fiber optic inclinometers, known for their high accuracy and immunity to electromagnetic interference, and laser-based systems, which offer noncontact monitoring, tend to be costly or sensitive to environmental conditions, restricting their use in large-scale, distributed deployments17–21.

Although each of the sensors has its advantages, the common limitations among the many sensors currently available are as follows. The main issues are the strict power requirements of the sensors. In most cases, sensor power consumption requires battery replacement or wires, making the whole process of installing sensors more complicated and the maintenance process more laborious22,23. In some sensors that can produce accurate results, regular calibration or the use of components vulnerable to external factors such as temperature, humidity, and the magnetic field can pose a serious limitation. On the other hand, cost factors might hinder the use of advanced sensor systems. Hence, there is a clear need for next-generation tilt monitoring devices that can deliver precise, continuous measurements within a more sustainable, low-maintenance framework.

In response, emerging low-profile, IoT-compatible sensors are gaining traction within the SHM community. These designs aim to reduce both the cost and the physical footprint of tilt sensors while harnessing wireless connectivity for near-real-time data acquisition24,25. One such promising innovation is the microwave sensor26–28. Characterized by its minimal height and compact form factor, the microwave sensor is batteryless with regard to power harvesting through radio frequency identification (RFID)-style coupling and is low cost, utilizing readily available components and standardized production methods29–32. Specifically, its native IoT connectivity enables seamless integration with existing networks, making it possible to deploy a large number of sensors at multiple foundation points33. By addressing common issues such as power supply, cost, and scalability, this technology demonstrates how tilt monitoring solutions can evolve to meet the increasingly demanding requirements of modern SHM.

However, many current sensors still face two major issues. Most of them have a single resonance (without a reference point) and cannot be used in practical applications because they are prone to environmental cross-sensitivity, which leads to measurement errors and calibration issues34,35. In addition, the lack of a communication link and the need for a bulky vector network analyzer (VNA) complicate system installation and data access in harsh environments36. To eliminate the first shortcoming, one feasible approach is to use a dual-band sensor (two sensing and reference frequencies in a single structure)37–43. This type of sensor is more resistant to environmental factors. In addition, to address the VNA issue, a wireless sensor network with real-time monitoring capabilities, easy deployment, and low installation costs would be suitable44–46. Such a system can detect relevant changes in a specific environment and transmit data to central hubs or other devices, providing real-time updates on physical or chemical parameters.

Addressing these challenges, we introduce a wireless tilt detection sensor based on a dual-band substrate integrated waveguide (SIW) configuration in the ultra-high frequency (UHF) RFID band. This tilt monitoring device was designed to overcome the limitations of current technologies by harnessing two distinct bands. The sensor effectively compensates for environmental fluctuations and mitigates distance ambiguity. The proposed SIW sensor combines a low-profile, battery-free design with IoT-enabled data acquisition, integrating the sensing element, antenna, and RFID chip into a single compact device. This streamlined design simultaneously tackles cross-sensitivity, distance ambiguity, noise interference, and the reliance on a VNA, ultimately offering a scalable and cost-effective solution for large-scale infrastructure monitoring. This robust solution has great potential for numerous other RFID applications, especially in the area of bridge foundation tilt tracking. Methods

Dual-band SIW configuration

In North America, various frequency bands are assigned for industrial, scientific, and medical (ISM) applications. Among these, the UHF range of 902-928 MHz is particularly prominent due to its ability to achieve extended reading distances and elevated data rates47. The proposed sensor leverages this UHF band. To fulfill sensing requirements while mitigating the effects of environmental fluctuations, a dual-band response is proposed. In this configuration, one band is designated for sensing, whereas the second band remains stable and serves to compensate for environmental variations.

The SIW structure inherently confines the electromagnetic fields, as it is an intrinsically shielded configuration compared to open planar structures. As a result, the fields are largely isolated from external electromagnetic interference, and the sensors response is less susceptible to nearby objects than that of open planar structures. Several approaches exist to realize a dual-band SIW circuit. Common methods include coupling SIW cavities or incorporating metamaterial unit cells on the surface of the SIW cavity48–54. Although the use of metamaterial loading can reduce the overall size of the design, this method introduces radiation losses that result in a lower quality factor compared to SIW cavity resonators, making it unsuitable for achieving a dual-band response within the UHF ISM band with only 26 MHz bandwidth53,55. Moreover, the use of SIW cavities with etched metamaterial unit cells has some limitations when trying to detect high-permittivity materials such as water. In such cases, the fluid should pass through a specific point in the unit cell via a tube to avoid disturbing the electromagnetic field, and the entire area of the unit cell cannot be utilized54. As a result, the approach used in this case consists of the connection of two SIW cavities.

In structure, an SIW resonator consists of a dielectric substrate containing metal layers at the top and bottom of the substrate surrounded by four parallel rows of metalized via holes. This configuration performs the function of a rectangular waveguide resonator in a planar form. In particular, in the present design, copper tape is employed to connect the upper and lower metallic surfaces, negating the need for conductive vias and the associated calculations of effective width and length typically required in conventional SIW structures.

The configuration of the proposed dual-band circuit is displayed in Fig. 2a. In this design, two nearly square SIW cavities of R1 and R2 are connected according to the concept of the iris window. When the structure is excited in the first band, each square cavity (w & l1 or w & l2) supports a fundamental TE101 mode, and the coupling coefficient between them depends on the width of the iris window (wc). By tuning wc, the degree of coupling between the two cavities can be adjusted, altering the lower resonant frequency. In contrast, in the second band, the two cavities together function as a single rectangular cavity (w & l1 + d + l2) supporting the TE102 mode, where the aperture region coincides with a null field and thus has negligible impact on the higher-frequency resonance. This design is simulated on a Rogers RO4003C substrate (εr = 3.55, tanδ = 0.0027) with a thickness of 1.524 mm. Copper layers with a thickness of 0.018 mm form the top and bottom surfaces, while copper tape substitutes for metal vias to provide the sidewall enclosure. As shown in Fig. 2a, the 50Ω microstrip lines are transitioned to a grounded coplanar waveguide (GCPW) feed to excite the structure. The width of the gap between the center conductor and the grounded plane of the GCPW (wt1 and wt2), along with the length of the GCPW (lt1 and lt2), are used for matching purposes. The proposed structure is optimized in Ansys HFSS to achieve desirable performance, with the final dimensions of: w = 112.9, wm = 3.435, wt1 = 13, wt2 = 8, wc = 24, l1 = 115, l2 = 132, lt1 = 21, lt2 = 21, and d = 1.1 (all in mm).

The field distributions shown in Fig. 2b–c clearly reveal that at the lower frequency (TE101), the iris window affects the coupling between the two square sub-cavities rather significantly. On the other hand, at the higher frequency (TE102), the iris window aligns with a null field. This reduces the effect due to the iris window to a great extent. Thus, changing the width of the window aperture (wc) affects only the first resonant frequency band, while the second band remains constant. The simulation reflection coefficients in Fig. 2d demonstrate this effect, while wc is increased from 24 to 30 mm, the resonant frequency of the first band gradually reduces from 915.9 to 908.8 MHz, while the second band is always constant at 925.7 MHz. In this design, wc = 24 mm is selected because the sensor is intended to detect high-permittivity materials, which cause a decrease in the first resonance frequency. Choosing this value ensures that the shifted resonance remains within the UHF ISM band (902-928 MHz). The magnitude and phase of S21 in Fig. 2e and f exhibit similar trends, with the magnitude and phase changes occurring only in the first band. As shown, a 23.5° phase shift in the first band at 911.8 MHz can especially be used as a sensing parameter. A low insertion loss of 1.1 dB is obtained in both bands, indicating efficient transmission of the signal. This enclosed structure does not present open spaces for sensing and wireless communication. Thus, the structure must be modified to function as an effective wireless sensor.

SIW wireless sensor

The previously discussed dual-band two-port configuration requires an in-built antenna for wireless data transmission and an open sensing region for sensing purposes. As explained in the previous section, the resonance frequency of the first operational band is controlled by adjusting the width of the coupling window (wc), while the second band remains stable. Now, this mechanism must be performed by the open sensing region without changing wc. As shown in Fig. 3a–c, several structural modifications are introduced to implement the wireless sensor, including: (i) Port 1 is replaced with a slot antenna integrated into the bottom metal layer; (ii) two sensing holes (width ws, length ls) are embedded within the coupling window; and (iii) to achieve a compact design, the SIW cavity of R2 is converted into a half-mode SIW (HMSIW) structure by introducing a central gap.

Proposed wireless sensor.

a Schematic in 3D view, b Schematic in top view, c Schematic in bottom view, d Simulated electric field distribution of the first band, e Simulated electric field distribution of the second band, f Sensitivity adjustment based on geometric parameters and the number of sensing holes, and g Simulated S11 for unloaded (blue solid line) and loaded (red dashed line) conditions.

Electric field simulations (Fig. 3d–e) illustrate the working mechanism. In the first band (TE101 mode), variations in permittivity within the sensing holes influence the coupling coefficient of two cavities, leading to a shift in the resonant frequency. In contrast, the second band (TE102 mode) is formed by merging the two cavities into a single larger resonant structure. Since the sensing holes are positioned in the fictitious electric wall of this mode, permittivity variations do not affect the response. This dual-band operation allows for simultaneous sensing and calibration within distinct frequency bands, forming the basis for effective permittivity measurement.

To physically realize this concept, a single 50-Ohm microstrip feedline, connected to the resonator R1 through a GCPW transition, acts as the excitation port. Key parameters, such as the width (wt) and length (lt) of the GCPW gap, are optimized to enhance impedance matching. The structural symmetry of the HMSIW gap, slot antenna, and feedline allows fabrication on either metal layer without affecting performance.

The sensor sensitivity is influenced by the geometric parameters (ws, ls) and the number of sensing holes (Fig. 3f). Increasing the size or density of the hole increases sensitivity; however, these modifications must be carefully controlled to ensure that the resonant modes remain within the operating frequency range. For applications requiring the detection of a broad permittivity range (e.g., εr = 1 to 80), lower sensitivity settings are preferred, while higher sensitivity configurations are ideal for the detection of narrower permittivity ranges (εr = 1 to 20). In this implementation, the sensor is specifically designed for inclination measurements in outdoor areas using a 40% ethanol-water mixture, chosen for its environmental stability. Complex permittivity characterization (εr = 56.83 and tanδ = 0.137 at 915 MHz) was performed using an open-ended coaxial probe in the range of 10 MHz to 1 GHz at 23 °C. The simulated reflection coefficients (Fig. 3g) reveal a 4 MHz drop in the first resonant frequency (from 911.9 to 907.9 MHz) when exposed to the ethanol-water mixture, while the second resonance (926.8 MHz) remains unchanged. This confirms that the first band functions as the permittivity-sensitive sensing channel, while the second serves as a stable calibration reference. The final sensor dimensions (in mm) are as follows: w = 114, wm = 3.435, wt = 7, wc = 19.4, ws = 3, wh = 0.5, wa1 = 12.2, wa2 = 30, l1 = 108, l2 = 75, lt = 23.9, ls = 7.6, lh = 100, la1 = 100.5, la2 = 6, d = 1.1, dh = 7.5, da = 39.8, and p = 6.

In the implementation of the present design, the sensor relies on the VNA for measurement. In order to overcome the above limitation, the sensor is modified by directly matching the feed to an RFID chip. As a result, wireless communication can be achieved as a function of the RFID chip and the sensor would no longer rely on the VNA.

RFID-based wireless sensor

The top and bottom views of the RFID-based wireless tilt sensor are shown in Fig. 4a–b, respectively. The device consists of a small container with sensing apertures, partially (about 50%) filled with a 40% ethanol-water mixture. The sensor is precisely matched to an Alien Higgs-4 RFID chip by designing the dimensions of the feed port (wt = 11.5 mm and lt = 16.2 mm). By utilizing this commercial integrated circuit (IC), the sensor maintains full compliance with the EPC Gen2 standard (ISO/IEC 18000-6C), ensuring compatibility with standard RFID readers capable of extracting backscatter phase metadata58. Furthermore, the system supports multi-node operation by leveraging the protocol’s native anti-collision mechanisms and unique EPC identification to synchronize and address multiple sensors within a distributed network. The RFID chip has an equivalent input parallel resistance of 1800 Ω and a capacitance of 0.95 pF at an input power of −18.5 dBm. Near 915 MHz-at the center of the North American RFID band (902–928 MHz)-this corresponds to an input impedance of roughly Zin = 18 − j181Ω. Although the sensor designed in the previous section had to be tested using a VNA, this version includes an RFID chip with an embedded antenna, allowing it to communicate directly with an RFID reader over a practical distance.

Proposed RFID-based wireless sensor.

a Schematic in top view, b Schematic in bottom view, and c Tilt range as a function of db and lb.

Adjusting the liquid volume inside the container can adjust the sensor’s sensitivity to inclination. Figure 4c illustrates how the maximum detectable tilt range of the liquid-based inclination sensor varies according to two key geometric parameters: (i) the horizontal offset (db) of the rectangular liquid container relative to the two sensing holes of the sensor and (ii) the length of the container (lb) along the tilt axis. Each curve in the figure represents a fixed length of the container, while the other axis represents the chosen offset db. On the vertical axis, the maximum angle of tilt is marked as the angle beyond which the holes in the sensor become fully dry or wet. In the plot, increasing db moves the reservoir farther from the holes and increases the usable tilt range. A larger db causes the liquid level in the holes to change more slowly with tilt, so the onset of wetting and drying occurs at more widely separated angles. Furthermore, the length of the container (lb) influences how quickly the meniscus crosses both holes as the tilt changes. A longer container can, in some configurations, promote steeper fluid transitions, causing the holes to wet or dry over smaller increments in angle. A shorter container, in contrast, confines the liquid more tightly near the holes, generally resulting in a broader tilt range before both holes become simultaneously wet or dry. Figure 4c serves as a practical design chart, since the resolution and the sensitivity of the liquid tilt sensor depend upon the speed at which the meniscus traverses the holes. After the tilt angle has been fixed (which determines the level of sensitivity required), the required dimensions of the container (lb or db) can be derived based on Equation (2), given that ws, ls, and p are fixed. In other words, the desired maximum tilt angle (and resolution) can be determined first, and then the container geometry can be tailored to fulfill those specifications. It should be noted that this prototype is designed for monitoring along a single axis, that is, the longitudinal axis of the reservoir. Monitoring along the orthogonal axes will require reconfiguration of the liquid container and sensing apertures. In the fabricated prototype, the dimensions of the container are lb = 60 mm, wb = 40 mm, db = 1 mm, and the height h = 6 mm. Each container was filled to about 50% of its internal volume with a 40% ethanol-water solution, corresponding to about 7.2 mL per container, for an overall total of 14.4 mL, since there are containers on both sides of the circuit. All these settings were made to achieve a controlled wetting/drying transition in the range of ± 10 °.

Fabrication

The proposed wireless sensor was fabricated on a Rogers RO4003C substrate (1.524 mm thickness, εr = 3.55, and tanδ = 0.0027). Copper was used for all conductive elements, with an electrical conductivity of 5.8 × 107 S/m and a thickness of t = 0.018 mm. The copper layers were patterned and through holes were drilled using an LPKF Protolaser U3 and LPKF ProtoMat S62, respectively. To establish robust interlayer connections and form vertical metallic walls, copper tape was applied around the sensor periphery and soldered to the top and bottom copper layers.

Results and discussion

SIW wireless sensor

To test the SIW wireless sensor, a coaxial connector was attached to the feeding microstrip line and subsequently interfaced with the R&S® ZVA67 VNA for reflection coefficient measurements. Two measurement conditions were investigated: (i) an unloaded state in which the sensing holes were filled with air and (ii) a loaded state in which the holes contained a 40% ethanol-water mixture. Figure 5a–d present photographs of both conditions, as well as top and bottom views of the fabricated sensor. Figure 5e shows the measured reflection coefficients. The first resonance occurs at approximately 910.3 MHz for air-filled conditions and shifts slightly to 907.4 MHz when loaded with ethanol-water mixture, while the second resonance remains at approximately 925 MHz with both states. The magnitude of S11 for the first resonance is approximately −24.5 dB in the unloaded state and −38.5 dB under the loaded condition; for the second resonance, it remains close to −16 dB for both states. These measurements agree well with the simulated data (Fig. 3g), and the deviations can be attributed to the differences in the substrate permittivity and fabrication tolerances. In general, the above result indicates the ability of the sensor to detect variation in the dielectric properties of the sample. This further validates the sensor’s potential for wireless communication.

RFID-based wireless sensor

Distance and tilt influence

To validate the circuit under practical conditions, the VNA was replaced with interrogation by a commercial UHF RFID reader (Fig. 6a). A ThingMagic M6e RFID reader drove a circularly polarized panel antenna (MTI MT-242025/TRH/A, 7.5 dBi) to illuminate the proposed RFID-based wireless tilt sensor. The sensor was positioned on a motorized pan-tilt head, allowing controlled adjustment of the angle of tilt while maintaining a fixed sensor location. The antenna boresight orientation was aligned with the sensor, and the orientation allowed a clear line of sight between the reader and the sensor. The reader-sensor distance was fixed at 1.3 m to ensure that the measurement is conducted in the radiative far-field. This enables the variation in the returned signal to depend predominantly on the tilt angle rather than on proximity effects. This configuration forms the basis for the following studies.

To assess whether range variations bias the readout, we monitored the reader-reported backscattered phase at the two resonance frequencies of the sensor, 915 and 927 MHz, while holding the sensor angle fixed and varying only the reader-to-sensor separation. As shown in Fig. 6b, the reader antenna was displaced along the line of sight from position 1 to position 2 with the sensor stationary. This reader-sensor separation procedure is shown in Supplementary Movie 1. The time traces in Fig. 6c show that during the motion interval the phases at 915 and 927 MHz are temporarily disturbed, which produces transient excursions in both traces. Once the reader arrives and stops at position 2, the differential metric Δϕ = ϕ915 − ϕ927 returns to and remains at the level observed at position 1 within measurement repeatability. This behavior is consistent with the intended sensor design. Distance mainly adds a common-mode propagation term that affects the two closely spaced interrogation frequencies in a similar way, and taking their difference cancels this term.

In another experiment, the effect of the sensor tilt with the empty container was investigated. The sensor was tilted to − 10° and + 10°, as shown in the bottom views in Fig. 6d–f with the complementary top view in Fig. 6g. This empty-container tilt test is shown in Supplementary Movie 2. The backscattered phases at 915 and 927 MHz in Fig. 6h closely track each other throughout the tilt, so the differential phase Δϕ remains unchanged within the measurement noise. Thus, tilting the empty container does not alter the phase difference.

Together, these two experiments show that the proposed differential-phase readout suppresses measurement errors as a result of both the reader-to-sensor distance and tilt of an empty container. With the tilt held constant, moving the reader does not change the steady-state value of Δϕ, and tilting an empty container leaves Δϕ flat within the noise. This property underpins the robustness of the subsequent tilt measurements. It should be noted that, in addition to the algorithmic measures introduced, practical RFID readers further mitigate environmental interference through built-in filtering stages, coherent detection schemes, and phase-locked local oscillators implemented in standard commercial interrogators, which collectively improve phase stability and measurement accuracy in real deployment scenarios.

Liquid test, adding liquid, and temperature influence

To quantify the influence of temperature on the liquid used with the proposed sensor, we assembled the testbed shown in Fig. 7a. The setup comprises a ThermalAir TA-5000B temperature-forcing system with a temperature chamber, a VNA (R&S® ZVA67), an open-ended coaxial probe connected to the VNA, a thermocouple probe from the ThermalAir system, and a glass beaker filled with a 40% ethanol–water mixture. Both probes, the open-ended coaxial probe and the thermocouple, were immersed in liquid inside the beaker, and the beaker was placed in the chamber to ensure uniform, controlled heating. The chamber temperature was swept from 0 °C to 40 °C in controlled increments, and at each set point measurements were taken after the thermal equilibrium was reached on the basis of the thermocouple reading. The VNA recorded the reflection coefficient S11 of the open probe in the frequency band of interest. Using the probe standard inversion algorithm, the complex permittivity and loss tangent of the liquid were extracted from the VNA data.

Figure 7f plots the measured phase versus time at two resonance frequencies, 915 and 927 MHz, while the container transitions from empty to filled. As expected based on the measurements of the empty device, tilting without liquid changes both resonances similarly, so the phase difference is nearly constant. The amount of liquid that crosses the sensing area thus defines the effective tilt threshold and sets the usable range for a given application.

Figure 7h–i presents the evaluation of the wireless readout under controlled temperature conditions. In Fig. 7h, the chamber-based experimental setup is shown, where the sensor with the container filled was placed inside the temperature chamber and interrogated by the reader through a fixed antenna. The chamber was set sequentially at 0 °C, 20 °C, and 40 °C, with each temperature maintained long enough to ensure thermal equilibrium. During this period, the reader continuously recorded the backscattered phase at 915 and 927MHz.

The time-series measurements in Fig. 7i show that the phases at both frequencies remain essentially constant across all three temperature stages. The differential phase between the two tones also shows no measurable drift within the repeatability of the measurements. This stability confirms that, when the container is filled, the phase-difference readout is insensitive to temperature variations within the 0–40 °C range. This observation aligns with Fig. 7b–c, which show that temperature variations alter both εr and tanδ. During 0–40 °C, the liquid’s εr and tanδ decrease, producing opposing effects on the sensing resonance frequency of the sensor (915MHz): the drop in εr shifts the resonance and reduces Q, while the drop in tanδ reduces the loss and increases Q, causing a phase rotation in the opposite direction. Therefore, owing to the stability of the 927 MHz reference resonance, both resonances exhibit no frequency shift, and the differential phase remains unchanged, confirming that the sensor response is inherently immune to temperature variations.

Finally, Fig. 7j provides a comprehensive summary of the sensor’s performance across the three primary experimental conditions, position change (distance and tilt), temperature change, and adding liquid. This matrix clearly highlights the effectiveness of the differential sensing approach. Only the condition of adding liquid induces a significant change (a decrease of ~ 36°) in the differential phase (difference column), as a result of the change in the resonance 915 MHz, while the reference 927 MHz remains stable. Conversely, both the position change (distance and tilt) and the temperature change are rejected by the differential readout, as these perturbations affect both the 915 MHz and 927 MHz phases in a similar manner, resulting in a near-zero phase difference change. This visualization reinforces that the differential phase readout successfully isolates the target metric (liquid presence) from common-mode interference.

The small difference likely arises from the slightly higher loss in the fabricated sensor than in simulation (beyond the nominal 1.1 dB) and from geometric factors such as the relative placement and orientation of the sensor and reader antennas.

Final system measurement

Figure 8a–b illustrates the two extreme operating states of the device. At a tilt angle of −10° (Fig. 8a), the sensing holes remain dry, whereas at +10° (Fig. 8b) both holes are fully wetted. Figure 8c plots the measured phase as a function of time at 915 and 927 MHz as the sensor is cycled between −10° and +10°. The final system operation during repeated dry–wet cycling is shown in Supplementary Movie 4. Both resonances are affected by the mechanical tilt. The 927 MHz resonance is used as the internal reference and is unaffected by wetting. As a result, the phase variation of the 927 MHz resonance depends only on tilt. The 915 MHz resonance depends both on the tilt and the filling and/or emptying of the sensing holes. Taking the differential phase, Δϕ ≡ ϕ915 − ϕ927, cancels the tilt-induced component and isolates the wetting effect, producing a ~ 36° step between the dry and wet states, which implies a transfer sensitivity of ~ 1. 8° of the phase per degree of tilt.

The 927 MHz resonance acts as an internal reference that follows tilt only, whereas the 915 MHz resonance additionally reports filling or emptying of the holes. A simple model is as follows: ϕ915(t) = ϕrot(t) + ϕfill(t), ϕ927(t) = ϕrot(t), so that Δϕ(t) = ϕ915(t) − ϕ927(t) = ϕfill(t) ≈ 36° between the dry and wet states.

Long-term performance

To assess the long-term stability of the device, it was subjected to repeated thermal sweeps over a range of 0–40 °C and multi-day measurements. Throughout these repeated tests, the responses remained stable and visual inspection indicated that no leakage occurred. This confirms the durability of the device under standard operating conditions. It should be noted that the 40% ethanol–water mixture ensures a freezing point below −24 °C, preventing solidification under typical outdoor deployment conditions.

In addition, as specified in the Alien Higgs-4 datasheet, the integrated memory supports data retention for up to 50 years61. A remaining consideration is that environmental exposure may cause slight changes in substrate dielectric properties over time. However, our proposed differential sensing inherently mitigates these effects by canceling out common-mode variations and maintaining consistent sensing performance throughout the device’s intended life.

Conclusions

We propose a compact, batteryless dual-band SIW tilt sensor for real-time monitoring of infrastructure such as bridge foundations. This self-referencing sensor relies on the phase comparison between the two nearby resonances at 915 and 927 MHz. As a result, the effects of the distances between the reader and the sensor, as well as the influence of the environment, are self-compensated. Experiment results reveal that the phase difference is 36° at a mechanical tilt angle of −10° to 10°, translating to a resolution of 1.8° phase per 1° of physical tilt. The entire tilt sensor consisting of the sensor part, the antenna, as well as the RFID reader interface module, has been integrated into a low-cost and compact solution. This solution relies solely on the RF energy provided by the reader and communicates in the ISM band. This solution has shown the feasibility of scalable real-time tilt detection of structures and can demonstrate the versatility of the aforementioned self-referencing dual-band approach for the development of other passive microwave sensors. As a next step, future research will focus on practical field implementation, including deployment at industrial construction sites and testing under sub-zero outdoor conditions. Given that the SIW structure is inherently self-shielded, these prototypes will utilize sealed liquid reservoirs and radomes to ensure protection against mechanical impact, contamination, and harsh weather.
