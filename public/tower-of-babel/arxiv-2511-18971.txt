Self-Similar Radially Symmetric Solutions of the Relativistic Euler Equations with Synge Energy
Tommaso Ruggeri, Ferdinand Thein, and Qinghua Xiao

Abstract
We consider self-similar, radially symmetric solutions of the relativistic Euler equations with constitutive relations from relativistic kinetic theory, based on Synge energies for monatomic and its extension to diatomic gases. For the corresponding initial–boundary value problem, including the spherical piston problem, we prove existence and uniqueness of solutions valid for all values of the relativistic parameter γ = m ​ c 2 / ( k B ​ T ) \gamma=mc^{2}/(k_{B}T) , thus covering both the classical limit ( γ → ∞ \gamma\to\infty ) and the ultra-relativistic regime ( γ → 0 \gamma\to 0 ). We further establish key structural properties of Synge energies, showing the strict negativity of the second derivative with respect to pressure at constant entropy and the monotone dependence of the characteristic velocity on γ \gamma . These results extend the classical theory of self-similar flows to the relativistic framework with kinetic-theory-based constitutive equations.

1
Introduction

The relativistic Euler system governs the motion of a non-dissipative fluid and arises from the conservation of particle number and energy–momentum in a relativistic framework.
It plays a central role in physics: in astrophysics and cosmology it models relativistic jets, supernova explosions, neutron star mergers, and the early universe; in high-energy physics it provides the basic continuum description of the quark–gluon plasma in relativistic heavy-ion collisions.
From the mathematical viewpoint, it represents a fundamental example of nonlinear hyperbolic PDEs, yet the theory is still far less developed than for the classical Euler equations.

Since the pioneering work of Taub
Taub
, a substantial literature has developed on the qualitative theory and numerical analysis of relativistic Euler equations, including shock waves, self-similar solutions, and the Riemann problem. In the general-relativistic setting, the coupling with Einstein’s field equations introduces additional challenges that, at present, can only be fully addressed numerically (see, e.g.,
Dumbser
). Numerical studies in the ultra-relativistic regime have recently been presented in
KLW
;
Kunik2024
;
RT2025
.

A crucial aspect of relativistic fluid dynamics is the closure relation, namely the constitutive equation linking pressure, energy density, and particle number density.
In contrast to classical fluid mechanics—where the ideal gas law provides a well-established model—formulating an accurate and general equation of state in the relativistic context is a highly nontrivial task. Most commonly used relations are valid only in asymptotic regimes: either the classical (non-relativistic) limit or the ultra-relativistic regime, where the rest mass of the particles becomes negligible or the temperature becomes extremely high.
As highlighted by Ruggeri, Xiao, and Zhao
Ruggeri-Xiao-Zhao-ARMA-2021
, this limitation constitutes one of the weakest points of the current theory of relativistic fluids.
To overcome it, insights from the mesoscopic scale—particularly relativistic kinetic theory—can be employed.

For rarefied gases, Synge
Synge
derived a constitutive relation based on the equilibrium Jüttner distribution, leading to a physically consistent expression involving ratios of modified Bessel functions of the second kind.
The Synge equation has the advantage of reducing to the classical ideal gas law in the low-temperature limit while approaching simplified ultra-relativistic laws as the temperature grows.
Pennisi and Ruggeri
Pennisi_Ruggeri
later extended this framework to polyatomic gases, producing Synge-type energies with explicit formulas in the diatomic case. Although physically consistent, these models are mathematically intricate, and only recently has the Riemann problem with Synge’s law been rigorously solved
Ruggeri-Xiao-Zhao-ARMA-2021
.

There is also a long tradition of studying self-similar solutions of the radial Euler equations, including the important
spherical piston problem
, which describes the motion of a spherical boundary
r=R(t)
driving a compressible fluid through a prescribed radial velocity condition.
Classical contributions are due to Guderley
Guderley-1942
, Taylor
Taylor-PRSA-1950
, and Neumann
Neumann-Collected works-1963
; see also Courant–Friedrichs
Courant-Friderichs
and the survey
Jessen-2011
.
The relativistic extension has been extensively investigated in connection with astrophysical phenomena such as supernova explosions and gamma-ray bursts.
Taub
Taub
derived the relativistic Rankine–Hugoniot conditions, while the Blandford–McKee solution
Blandford_McKee
describes ultra-relativistic spherical blast waves.
General treatments of relativistic piston-driven flows are found in Anile
anile
and in subsequent work on imploding relativistic shocks.
More recently, Lai
Lai2019
investigated self-similar solutions of the radial relativistic Euler equations with classical constitutive laws, while Peng–Lien
Peng-Lien-2012
, Ding–Li
Ding-Li-ZAMP-2013
, and others
Ha-QAM-2014
;
Lai2023
;
Lu-QAM-2024
studied the piston problem in various regimes.

Regarding the classical regimes, several further remarks are in order. In
Zhang-ARMA-1998
, self-similar solutions to the isentropic Euler system with axisymmetry were analyzed.
Thanks to the particular structure of the Euler equations, the self-similar variables
(u,v)
satisfy an autonomous system after a suitable transformation, enabling a precise structural analysis.
The resulting self-similar solutions are continuous for general initial data with
u_{0}\geq 0
, and of class
C^{1}
in the special case
v_{0}=0
.
The method of
Zhang-ARMA-1998
was extended in
HL-BLMSS-2014
to the full Euler system with radial symmetry.
For the corresponding initial–boundary value problem, the authors constructed a global bounded continuous solution for
u_{0}\geq 0
and a discontinuous weak solution with a shock wave for
u_{0}<0
, providing a detailed analysis of their structure.
Further contributions on solutions with symmetry or similarity can be found in
Jenssen2018
;
Jenssen2023
.
Smooth self-similar solutions to the Euler equations have also been investigated in the preprint
Merle2019
.

The purpose of this work is to extend these studies to the relativistic Euler equations with Synge energies for monoatomic and diatomic gases under radial symmetry.
We establish existence and uniqueness theorems for self-similar solutions and prove structural properties of Synge’s energy—most notably, the strict negativity of the second derivative with respect to pressure at constant entropy, and the monotone dependence of the characteristic velocity on the relativistic temperature parameter.

Governing Equations

Relativistic flows are governed by the conservation of particle number and energy–momentum

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\partial_{\alpha}V^{\alpha}=0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\frac{1}{c}\partial_{t}V^{0}+\partial_{i}V^{i}=0,\\
&\partial_{\alpha}T^{\alpha\beta}=0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\begin{cases}\dfrac{1}{c}\partial_{t}T^{0j}+\partial_{i}T^{ij}=0,\\[4.30554pt]
\dfrac{1}{c}\partial_{t}T^{00}+\partial_{i}T^{0i}=0.\end{cases}\end{split}

is the space dimension. We write

denotes the light velocity while

are time and space coordinates.
The particle flux and the energy–momentum tensor are

\displaystyle V^{\alpha}=\rho U^{\alpha},\qquad T^{\alpha\beta}=\frac{e+p}{c^{2}}U^{\alpha}U^{\beta}-pg^{\alpha\beta},

(2.2)

where

is the density,

the particle number,

the four-velocity with Lorentz factor

is the pressure,
g^{\alpha\beta}
the Minkowski metric, i. e diagonal with

and the other terms in the diagonal equal to
-1
. The energy reads

where

is the specific internal energy. In space–time form, (

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\partial_{t}\!\left(\frac{c\rho}{\sqrt{c^{2}-v^{2}}}\right)+\partial_{i}\left(\frac{c\rho{v^{i}}}{\sqrt{c^{2}-v^{2}}}\right)=0,\\
&\partial_{t}\!\left(\frac{e+p}{c^{2}-v^{2}}v^{j}\right)+\partial_{i}\!\left(\frac{e+p}{c^{2}-v^{2}}v^{i}v^{j}+p\delta^{ij}\right)=0,\\
&\partial_{t}\!\left(e+\frac{v^{2}}{c^{2}-v^{2}}(e+p)\right)+\partial_{i}\left(\frac{c^{2}(e+p)}{c^{2}-v^{2}}{v^{i}}\right)=0.\end{split}

(2.3)

As a consequence of (

\displaystyle\partial_{\alpha}(\rho\eta U^{\alpha})=0,\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad\partial_{t}\!\left(\frac{c\rho\eta}{\sqrt{c^{2}-v^{2}}}\right)+\partial_{i}\left(\frac{c\rho\eta{v^{i}}}{\sqrt{c^{2}-v^{2}}}\right)=0,

\displaystyle T\,d\eta=d\varepsilon-\frac{p}{\rho^{2}}\,d\rho

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\eta+v_{j}\partial^{j}\eta=0.

3
Constitutive equations and Synge Energy for monatomic and diatomic gas

The system (
2.3
) consists of
d+2
scalar differential equations for
d+3
fields
(\rho,v^{j},p,e)
.
Therefore, a constitutive equation is required in order to have as many equations as unknowns.
For example, one may prescribe
e\equiv e(\rho,p)
.
However, it is usually more convenient to introduce the temperature
T
as a parameter and to specify the so-called thermal and caloric equations of state:

e\equiv e(\rho,T),\qquad p\equiv p(\rho,T).

Starting with Taub in 1949
Taub
, many authors have associated the relativistic Euler equations with classical thermal and caloric equations of state, or with simplified constitutive relations in the ultra-relativistic regime.
As observed in
Ruggeri-Xiao-Zhao-ARMA-2021
, to obtain more realistic equations in the relativistic regime, particularly for rarefied gases, it is necessary to justify this association at the mesoscopic scale using kinetic theory.

Within the relativistic kinetic framework, we have the Boltzmann–Chernikov equation
Cercignani-Kremer
:

p^{\alpha}\partial_{\alpha}f=Q,

(3.6)

where
f\equiv f(x^{\alpha},p^{\beta})
is the distribution function,
p^{\alpha}
is the four-momentum with the property
p^{\alpha}p_{\alpha}=m^{2}c^{2}
, and
Q
is the collision term.
In this case,
V^{\alpha}
and
T^{\alpha\beta}
are given as moments of the distribution function:

\displaystyle\begin{split}V^{\alpha}&=mc\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}f\,p^{\alpha}\,d\vec{P},\qquad T^{\alpha\beta}=c\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}f\,p^{\alpha}p^{\beta}\,d\vec{P},\end{split}

(3.7)

where

d\vec{P}=\frac{dp^{1}\,dp^{2}\,dp^{3}}{p^{0}}.

It is well known that the equilibrium distribution is the so-called Jüttner distribution:

f_{J}=\frac{1}{4\pi m^{3}c^{3}}\,\frac{n\gamma}{K_{2}(\gamma)}\,\exp\!\left(-\frac{\gamma}{mc^{2}}U_{\beta}p^{\beta}\right),

(3.8)

where
\gamma
is the dimensionless parameter, some time called
relativistic temperature parameter
or
coldness parameter
(see, e.g.
Cercignani-Kremer
)

\gamma=\frac{mc^{2}}{k_{B}T},

(3.9)

with
k_{B}
being the Boltzmann constant.
This parameter measures the relativistic character of the fluid: the fluid is relativistic if
\gamma\ll 1
; the classical limit corresponds to
\gamma\to\infty
; and the ultra-relativistic limit corresponds to
\gamma\to 0
.

Substituting (
3.8
) into (
3.7
) and considering (
2.2
), we obtain the following expressions for pressure and energy:

\displaystyle\begin{split}p&=\frac{\rho c^{2}}{\gamma}=\frac{k_{B}}{m}\rho T,\quad e=\frac{\rho c^{2}}{K_{2}(\gamma)}\left(K_{3}(\gamma)-\frac{1}{\gamma}K_{2}(\gamma)\right),\end{split}

(3.10)

where
K_{i}(\gamma)
with
i\geq 0
denotes the modified Bessel function of the second kind.
The expression for the energy in (
3.10
) is known as the Synge energy
Synge
;
anile
;
Rezzolla

Pennisi and Ruggeri

, proposed a generalized Boltzmann–Chernikov equation. This retains the form of (

) but extends the distribution function

, which accounts for the energy due to a molecule’s internal degrees of freedom. Instead of (

), they considered the following moments:

\displaystyle\begin{split}V^{\alpha}&=mc\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\int_{0}^{+\infty}f\,p^{\alpha}\,\phi(\mathcal{I})\,d\vec{P}\,d\mathcal{I},\\
T^{\alpha\beta}&=\frac{1}{mc}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\int_{0}^{+\infty}f\,(mc^{2}+\mathcal{I})\,p^{\alpha}p^{\beta}\,\phi(\mathcal{I})\,d\vec{P}\,d\mathcal{I},\end{split}

where the energy–momentum tensor is influenced by the internal modes.
Here,
\phi(\mathcal{I})
is the state density of the internal modes, meaning that
\phi(\mathcal{I})\,d\mathcal{I}
represents the number of internal states of a molecule with microscopic internal energy between
\mathcal{I}
and

Using the

\displaystyle\begin{split}\partial_{t}\!\left(\frac{c\rho}{\sqrt{c^{2}-u^{2}}}\right)+\partial_{r}\!\left(\frac{c\rho u}{\sqrt{c^{2}-u^{2}}}\right)&=-\frac{d-1}{r}\frac{c\rho u}{\sqrt{c^{2}-u^{2}}},\\
\partial_{t}\!\left(\frac{e+p}{c^{2}-u^{2}}u\right)+\partial_{r}\!\left(\frac{e+p}{c^{2}-u^{2}}u^{2}+p\right)&=-\frac{d-1}{r}\frac{e+p}{c^{2}-u^{2}}u^{2},\\
\partial_{t}\!\left(e+\frac{u^{2}}{c^{2}-u^{2}}(e+p)\right)+\partial_{r}\!\left(\frac{c^{2}(e+p)u}{c^{2}-u^{2}}\right)&=-\frac{d-1}{r}\frac{c^{2}(e+p)u}{c^{2}-u^{2}}.\end{split}

(4.32)

The corresponding entropy law (
2.4
) becomes:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\!\left(\frac{c\rho\eta}{\sqrt{c^{2}-u^{2}}}\right)+\partial_{r}\!\left(\frac{c\rho\eta u}{\sqrt{c^{2}-u^{2}}}\right)=-\frac{d-1}{r}\frac{c\rho\eta u}{\sqrt{c^{2}-u^{2}}},

and hence

\displaystyle 0=\partial_{t}\eta+u\partial_{r}\eta.

If in addition the flow is self-similar, i.e. variables depend on
(t,r)
only through
s=t/r
, then (
4.32
) reduces to the ODE system

\displaystyle\begin{split}\frac{du}{ds}&=\frac{(d-1)u(c^{2}-u^{2})(u-sc^{2})}{g},\\
\frac{dp}{ds}&=\frac{(d-1)uc^{2}(us-1)(e+p)}{g},\\
-s(us-1)\frac{d\eta}{ds}&=0,\\
g&=c^{2}e_{p}(us-1)^{2}-(u-sc^{2})^{2}.\end{split}

(4.33)

Note that for relativistic flows
0\leq\xi\leq c
, where
\xi=1/s
. The derivation parallels
Lai2019
, with adapted notation.

5
Main result and proof for the general radially symmetric case

Theorem 5.1

Let
(u_{0}(r),p_{0}(r),\eta_{0}(r))\in(-c,c)\times\mathbb{R}_{>0}\times\mathbb{R}_{>0}
be an initial datum such that

(u,p,\eta)(0,r)=(u_{0},p_{0},\eta_{0}),

and considering the following boundary condition:

(e(p,\eta)\,u)(t,0)=0.

Then the system (
4.32
) admits a unique self-similar entropy solution.

•

If
u_{0}\in[0,c)
, the solution is continuous and free of shocks.

•

If
u_{0}\in(-c,0)
, the solution contains a single shock followed by a constant state.

The proof of Theorem
5.1
is presented in the sequel through the proofs of several lemmas.
Subsection
5.1
begins with an analysis of the denominator of (
4.33
).
The argument is then divided into two cases:
s\in(0,1/c]
, treated in Subsection
5.2
, and
s>1/c
.
For
s>1/c
, the structure of the solution depends crucially on the initial velocity
u_{0}
, and three possible cases for
u_{0}\in(0,c)
are distinguished in Subsection
5.4
.
Finally, for
u_{0}\in(-c,0)
shocks appear, and the proof is completed in Subsection
5.5
.

5.1
Investigation of the denominator
g

For the proof of Thm.
5.1
, it is crucial to discuss the denominator
g
given by
\eqref{systemODE}_{4}
due to its occurrence in the ODEs of velocity and pressure in system (
4.33
).
We first study the denominator
\eqref{systemODE}_{4}
for
|u|<c
and
s\in(0,1/c]
. We obtain

\displaystyle g

\displaystyle=c^{2}e_{p}(us-1)^{2}-(u-sc^{2})^{2}=\frac{2}{3}c^{2}e_{p}(us-1)^{2}+\frac{1}{3}c^{2}e_{p}(us-1)^{2}-(u-sc^{2})^{2}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{ep2}}}{{>}}\frac{2}{3}c^{2}e_{p}(us-1)^{2}+c^{2}(us-1)^{2}-(u-sc^{2})^{2}=\frac{2}{3}c^{2}e_{p}(us-1)^{2}+\underbrace{(u^{2}-c^{2})}_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle|u|<c}}{{<}}0}\underbrace{(c^{2}s^{2}-1)}_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle cs\leq 1}}{{\leq}}0}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{2}{3}c^{2}e_{p}(us-1)^{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{ep2}}}{{>}}2c^{2}(us-1)^{2}>0.

(5.34)

For the case
s>1/c
, we rewrite
g
as follows:

\displaystyle\begin{split}g&=c^{2}e_{p}(us-1)^{2}-(u-sc^{2})^{2}=A\cdot B,\\
A&:=c\sqrt{e_{p}}(us-1)-(u-sc^{2})=(c\sqrt{e_{p}}s-1)u-(c\sqrt{e_{p}}-sc^{2}),\\
B&:=c\sqrt{e_{p}}(us-1)+(u-sc^{2})=(c\sqrt{e_{p}}s+1)u-(c\sqrt{e_{p}}+sc^{2}).\end{split}

(5.35)

Further we have the relations

\displaystyle\begin{split}&A\leq 0\quad\Rightarrow\quad B<0\quad\Rightarrow\quad g\geq 0,\\
&B\geq 0\quad\Rightarrow\quad A>0\quad\Rightarrow\quad g\geq 0.\end{split}

(5.36)

Thus, we can conclude that

\displaystyle g<0\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad A>0\;\wedge\;B<0.

Additionally, we define functions

\displaystyle\varphi_{A}(s)=\frac{c\sqrt{e_{p}}-sc^{2}}{c\sqrt{e_{p}}s-1},\qquad\varphi_{B}(s)=\frac{c\sqrt{e_{p}}+sc^{2}}{c\sqrt{e_{p}}s+1}.

(5.37)

For
s>1/c
, we have for both denominators

\displaystyle c\sqrt{e_{p}}s+1>c\sqrt{e_{p}}s-1>c\sqrt{3}\frac{1}{c}-1>0,

and therefore we can directly conclude

\displaystyle A\begin{cases}&<0,\\
&=0,\\
&>0\end{cases}\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad u\begin{cases}&<\varphi_{A},\\
&=\varphi_{A},\\
&>\varphi_{A}\end{cases}\quad\text{and}\quad B\begin{cases}&<0,\\
&=0,\\
&>0\end{cases}\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad u\begin{cases}&<\varphi_{B},\\
&=\varphi_{B},\\
&>\varphi_{B}\end{cases}.

(5.38)

A straightforward calculation exploits the relation between
\varphi_{A}
,
\varphi_{B}
, and
1/s
, that is,

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\varphi_{A}-\dfrac{1}{s}&=\dfrac{1-s^{2}c^{2}}{s(c\sqrt{e_{p}}s-1)},\\[10.00002pt]
\varphi_{B}-\dfrac{1}{s}&=\dfrac{s^{2}c^{2}-1}{s(c\sqrt{e_{p}}s+1)},\end{cases}\Rightarrow\quad\begin{cases}&\varphi_{A}(s)=\varphi_{B}(s)=c,\;s=1/c,\\[5.0pt]
&\varphi_{A}(s)<\dfrac{1}{s}<\varphi_{B}(s),\;s\in(1/c,\infty)\end{cases}.

(5.39)

We hence can conclude that
g>0
for
s\in(0,1/c]
and
g\leq 0
may only occur for
s\in(1/c,\infty)
.
The discussion of
g
guides the way for the proof of the main theorem and we will split the proof into the cases for
s\in(0,1/c]
and
s\in(1/c,\infty)
due to (
5.39
).

5.2
The case
s\in(0,1/c]

Lemma 1

Let
(u_{0},p_{0},\eta_{0})
be an initial datum with
p_{0}>0
and
0<|u_{0}|<c
.
Then (
4.33
) admits a unique solution for
s\in(0,1/c]
, which furthermore satisfies

p(s)>0,\qquad|u(s)|<c\quad\text{for all }s\in(0,1/c].

Proof

Due to the differentiability of the RHS and because of (
5.34
), for the initial datum satisfying
0<|u_{0}|<c
and
s<1/c
, we have a unique local solution of the ODE system (
4.33
).
We further want to show that the solution satisfies
p>0
and
0<|u|<c
for
s\in(0,1/c]
.
This will be done using a contradiction argument.

Assume that there exists a point
s^{\ast}\in(0,1/c]
such that
|u(s)|<c
for all
s\in(0,s^{\ast})
and
|u(s^{\ast})|=c
.
Then, from
\eqref{systemODE}_{1}
, we integrate on
(s^{\ast}-\varepsilon,s^{\ast})
for
\varepsilon>0
to obtain

\displaystyle\frac{\textup{d}u}{\textup{d}s}

\displaystyle=\frac{(d-1)u(c^{2}-u^{2})(u-sc^{2})}{g}

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\quad\frac{1}{c^{2}-u^{2}}\frac{\textup{d}u}{\textup{d}s}

\displaystyle=\frac{(d-1)u(u-sc^{2})}{g}

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\quad\int_{u(s^{\ast}-\varepsilon)}^{u(s^{\ast})}\frac{\textup{d}u}{c^{2}-u^{2}}

\displaystyle=\int_{s^{\ast}-\varepsilon}^{s^{\ast}}\frac{(d-1)u(u-sc^{2})}{g}\,\textup{d}s.

The LHS becomes infinite, whereas the RHS stays bounded and thus we have a contradiction.

In an analogous way, we assume that there exists a
s^{\ast}\in(0,1/c]
such that the local solution satisfies
|u(s)|>0
for all
s\in(0,s^{\ast})
and
u(s^{\ast})=0
.
Then, by integration on
(s^{\ast}-\varepsilon,s^{\ast})
for
\varepsilon>0
, we obtain from
\eqref{systemODE}_{1}
that

\displaystyle\frac{\textup{d}u}{\textup{d}s}

\displaystyle=\frac{(d-1)u(c^{2}-u^{2})(u-sc^{2})}{g}

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\quad\frac{1}{u}\frac{\textup{d}u}{\textup{d}s}

\displaystyle=\frac{(d-1)(c^{2}-u^{2})(u-sc^{2})}{g}

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\quad\int_{u(s^{\ast}-\varepsilon)}^{u(s^{\ast})}\frac{\textup{d}u}{u}

\displaystyle=\int_{s^{\ast}-\varepsilon}^{s^{\ast}}\frac{(d-1)(c^{2}-u^{2})(u-sc^{2})}{g}\,\textup{d}s.

Again, the LHS becomes infinite, whereas the RHS stays bounded and thus we have a contradiction.

With similar arguments, we show the positivity of the pressure. Let us assume that we have a local solution on
(0,1/c]
, and assume there exists a
s^{\ast}\in(0,1/c]
such that
p(s^{\ast})=0
.
Then we obtain by integration on
(s^{\ast}-\varepsilon,s^{\ast})
for
\varepsilon>0
from
\eqref{systemODE}_{2}

\displaystyle\frac{\textup{d}p}{\textup{d}s}

\displaystyle=\frac{(d-1)uc^{2}(us-1)(e+p)}{g}

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\quad\frac{1}{e+p}\frac{\textup{d}p}{\textup{d}s}

\displaystyle=\frac{(d-1)uc^{2}(us-1)}{g}

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\quad\int_{p(s^{\ast}-\varepsilon)}^{p(s^{\ast})}\frac{g\textup{d}p}{e+p}

\displaystyle=\int_{s^{\ast}-\varepsilon}^{s^{\ast}}(d-1)uc^{2}(us-1)\,\textup{d}s.

Obviously, the integration on the RHS is finite. Now we estimate the integration on the LHS.
Note that for
|u|<c
and
s\in(0,s^{\ast}]
,

(cs^{\ast}-1)^{2}<(us-1)^{2}<(cs^{\ast}+1)^{2},

which together with (
5.34
) implies that

\displaystyle\frac{2}{3}c^{2}(cs^{\ast}-1)^{2}e_{p}<\frac{2}{3}c^{2}e_{p}(us-1)^{2}<g<c^{2}e_{p}(us-1)^{2}<c^{2}(cs^{\ast}+1)^{2}e_{p}

Thus
g
is bounded from above and below by
e_{p}
with appropriate constants and we hence study the integrand on the LHS with
e_{p}
replacing
g
.
According to (
3.15
), (
3.19
), (
3.20
), and (
3.26
), one has
\textup{d}p=p_{\gamma}\textup{d}\gamma
and

\frac{e_{p}}{e+p}p_{\gamma}=\frac{\gamma^{2}\Phi^{\prime}_{i}(1+\gamma\Phi_{i})}{\gamma^{2}\Phi^{\prime}_{i}-1}\frac{\gamma^{2}\Phi^{\prime}_{i}-1}{\gamma(1+\gamma\Phi_{i})}=\gamma\Phi^{\prime}_{i}=\gamma\left(h^{2}_{i}-1\right)+(2i+1)h_{i}-\frac{3}{\gamma}.

(5.40)

Since
\gamma\to\infty
as
p\to 0
the integrand on the left basically behaves as
-1/\gamma
and we refer to (
A.84
) for a detailed asymptotic expansion.
This together with (
5.40
) implies that the integration on the LHS becomes minus infinity and thus leads to contradiction since the RHS stays bounded. This conclude the proof.

Note that for
u_{0}=0
, the solution is constant in time and given by
(0,p_{0},\eta_{0})
.

5.3
The case
s\in(1/c,\infty)

The main outcome of the previous discussion for the case
s\in(0,1/c]
is that
0<|u|<c
for
0<|u_{0}|<c
.
Thus we have

u_{0}\in\begin{cases}(0,c),\\
(-c,0)\end{cases}\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad u\left(\frac{1}{c}\right)\in\begin{cases}(0,c),\\
(-c,0)\end{cases}.

The following lemma is important as it suggests to distinguish the cases of positive and negative initial velocity for the remainder of the main proof.

Lemma 2

Assume
u>0
and
p>0
for
s\in(1/c,\infty)
.
Then
u<\varphi_{A}
, and hence
g>0
on
(1/c,\infty)
.

For the sake of brevity, we provide the proof of Lem.
2
in the appendix.
Considering the previous result, we split the following part of the proof into two cases
u_{0}\in(0,c)
and
u_{0}\in(-c,0)
.

5.4
The case
u_{0}\in(0,c)

(a)

Case I: Solution including vacuum for large
u_{0}
, i.e., close to
c
.

(b)

Case II: Solution becoming stationary for small
u_{0}
, i.e., close to zero.

(c)

Case III: Solution for intermediate region.

This particular classification is due to the observation that for
u_{0}=0
, the solution is stationary. Moreover, it is shown in
Ruggeri-Xiao-Zhao-ARMA-2021
that expansion into vacuum by rarefaction waves is possible if the difference of the initial velocities exceeds a certain threshold.

Case I:
Solution including vacuum for large
u_{0}
.

Lemma 3

Let
u_{0}>\overline{u}>0
, where
\overline{u}
is given by (
5.44
).
Then there exists
s^{\ast}>1/c
such that

0<u(s)<\varphi_{A}(s)\quad\text{for }s\in(1/c,s^{\ast}),

and moreover

u(s^{\ast})=\varphi_{A}(s^{\ast})=\frac{1}{s^{\ast}},\qquad p(s^{\ast})=0.

Proof

From the proof of Lemma
2
, we have
0<g
and
0<u(s)<\varphi_{A}(s)
for
s\in(1/c,s^{\ast})
. Due to (
5.39
), we can conclude that
us-1<0
for
s\in(1/c,s^{\ast})
. On the other hand, for
s>1/c
, we have

u-sc^{2}<u-c<0.

Thus we can directly verify that
u(s)
and
p(s)
are monotonically decreasing for
{s\in(1/c,s^{\ast})}
.
Moreover, we can conclude from
u<\varphi_{A}
that
A<0
and hence we yield

\displaystyle 0>A=c\sqrt{e_{p}}(us-1)-(u-sc^{2})\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad c\sqrt{e_{p}}>\frac{u-sc^{2}}{us-1}.

(5.41)

From
\eqref{systemODE}_{1}
and
\eqref{systemODE}_{2}
, we obtain

\frac{1}{c^{2}(e+p)}p^{\prime}(s)=\frac{us-1}{(c^{2}-u^{2})(u-sc^{2})}u^{\prime}(s)

and thus together with (
5.41
) we yield

\displaystyle\frac{\sqrt{e_{p}}}{c(e+p)}p^{\prime}(s)=\frac{c\sqrt{e_{p}}(us-1)}{(c^{2}-u^{2})(u-sc^{2})}u^{\prime}(s)<\frac{1}{c^{2}-u^{2}}u^{\prime}(s)

(5.42)

due to
u^{\prime}(s)<0
. Let
\overline{s}\in(0,1/c)
be defined by Lemma
8
and hence

u(s)-sc^{2}\begin{cases}&\geq 0,\quad s\in(0,\overline{s}],\\
&<0,\quad s\in(\overline{s},\infty)\end{cases}.

Further let
\sigma\in(\overline{s},s^{\ast})
and therefore, due to the monotonicity of the functions,
p^{\prime}(s)<0
on
(0,s^{\ast})
,
u^{\prime}(s)>0
on
(0,\overline{s})
and
u^{\prime}(s)<0
on
(\overline{s},s^{\ast})
we get

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{s^{\ast}}\frac{\sqrt{e_{p}}}{c(e+p)}p^{\prime}(s)\textup{d}s<\int_{0}^{\sigma}\frac{\sqrt{e_{p}}}{c(e+p)}p^{\prime}(s)\,\textup{d}s<\int^{\sigma}_{\overline{s}}\frac{\sqrt{e_{p}}}{c(e+p)}p^{\prime}(s)\,\textup{d}s

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{ineq:dp_du_vac}}}{{<}}

\displaystyle\int^{\sigma}_{\overline{s}}\frac{u^{\prime}(s)}{c^{2}-u^{2}}\,\textup{d}s<\int^{\sigma}_{\overline{s}}\frac{u^{\prime}(s)}{c^{2}-u^{2}}\,\textup{d}s+\underbrace{\int_{0}^{\overline{s}}\frac{u^{\prime}(s)}{c^{2}-u^{2}}\,\textup{d}s}_{>0}=\int^{\sigma}_{0}\frac{u^{\prime}(s)}{c^{2}-u^{2}}\,\textup{d}s.

In particular we have

\displaystyle\int_{p_{0}}^{p(\sigma)}\frac{\sqrt{e_{p}}}{c(e+p)}\,\textup{d}p<\int^{u(\sigma)}_{u_{0}}\frac{1}{c^{2}-u^{2}}\,\textup{d}u.

(5.43)

We want to comment on the integral

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{p}:=\int_{0}^{p_{0}}\frac{\sqrt{e_{p}}}{c(e+p)}\textup{d}p

and will show that
\mathcal{I}_{p}
is also bounded from above.
Firstly, by the aid of (
3.15
), (
3.19
), and (
3.20
) we get

\displaystyle\frac{\sqrt{e_{p}}}{e+p}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{p(\gamma\Phi_{i}(\gamma)+1)}\left[\frac{\gamma^{2}\Phi^{\prime}_{i}(\gamma)(\gamma\Phi_{i}(\gamma)+1)}{\gamma^{2}\Phi^{\prime}_{i}(\gamma)-1}\right]^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\gamma_{p}(\gamma^{2}\Phi^{\prime}_{i}(\gamma)-1)}{\gamma\sqrt{\gamma\Phi_{i}(\gamma)+1}}\left[\frac{\gamma^{2}\Phi^{\prime}_{i}(\gamma)}{\gamma^{2}\Phi^{\prime}_{i}(\gamma)-1}\right]^{\frac{1}{2}}=-\gamma_{p}\left[\frac{(\gamma^{2}\Phi^{\prime}_{i}(\gamma)-1)\Phi_{i}^{\prime}(\gamma)}{\gamma\Phi_{i}(\gamma)+1}\right]^{\frac{1}{2}}

since
\gamma^{2}\Phi^{\prime}_{i}(\gamma)-1<0
by (
B.87
) and (
B.90
).
Thus we obtain with
\gamma_{p}<0
and (
A.86
) that

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle<\mathcal{I}_{p}=\int_{0}^{p_{0}}\frac{\sqrt{e_{p}}}{c(e+p)}\textup{d}p=-\frac{1}{c}\int_{0}^{p_{0}}\left[\frac{(\gamma^{2}\Phi^{\prime}_{i}(\gamma)-1)\Phi_{i}^{\prime}(\gamma)}{\gamma\Phi_{i}(\gamma)+1}\right]^{\frac{1}{2}}\gamma_{p}\textup{d}p

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{c}\int_{\gamma_{0}}^{\infty}\left[\frac{(\gamma^{2}\Phi^{\prime}_{i}(\gamma)-1)\Phi_{i}^{\prime}(\gamma)}{\gamma\Phi_{i}(\gamma)+1}\right]^{\frac{1}{2}}\textup{d}\gamma<+\infty.

Obviously, the bounded integral
\mathcal{I}_{p}
increases w.r.t.
p_{0}
. Let us define the value
\bar{u}
as follows

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\bar{u}}\frac{1}{c^{2}-u^{2}}\textup{d}u=\mathcal{I}_{p}\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad\bar{u}=c\frac{\exp(2\mathcal{I}_{p})-1}{\exp(2\mathcal{I}_{p})+1}\in(0,c).

(5.44)

Since
u_{0}>\overline{u}
, we have

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{u_{0}}\frac{1}{c^{2}-u^{2}}\textup{d}u>\int_{0}^{\overline{u}}\frac{1}{c^{2}-u^{2}}\textup{d}u\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{def_velo_bound}}}{{=}}\mathcal{I}_{p}\geq\int_{p(\sigma)}^{p_{0}}\frac{\sqrt{e_{p}}}{c(e+p)}\textup{d}p\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{ineq:p_vac_int}}}{{>}}\int_{u(\sigma)}^{u_{0}}\frac{1}{c^{2}-u^{2}}\textup{d}u.

Clearly, the pressure must be zero prior to the velocity (both are decreasing functions), since otherwise the inequality would be violated and thus have
p(s^{\ast})=0
and
u(s^{\ast})>0
. From
\eqref{systemODE}_{2}
, we conclude that
p^{\prime}(s)=0
for
s\geq s^{\ast}
, i.e., the pressure remains zero.
Due to the fact
e_{p}\to\infty
as
p\to 0
, we further obtain

\lim_{s\to s^{\ast}}\varphi_{A}(s)=\lim_{s\to s^{\ast}}\frac{c\sqrt{e_{p}}-sc^{2}}{c\sqrt{e_{p}}s-1}=\lim_{s\to s^{\ast}}\dfrac{1-\dfrac{sc}{\sqrt{e_{p}}}}{s-\dfrac{1}{c\sqrt{e_{p}}}}=\frac{1}{s^{\ast}}.

Therefore, it remains to show that
u(s^{\ast})=1/s^{\ast}
. Since
e_{p}\to\infty
as
p\to 0
for
s\to s^{\ast}
, there exists an
\varepsilon>0
such that for all
s\in(s^{\ast}-\varepsilon,s^{\ast})
, we have

\frac{g}{c^{2}e_{p}}=(us-1)^{2}-\frac{(u-sc^{2})^{2}}{c^{2}e_{p}}>\frac{1}{2}(us-1)^{2}.

We integrate
\eqref{systemODE}_{2}
for
s\in(s^{\ast}-\varepsilon,s^{\ast})
along the solution, assuming that
u(s^{\ast})<1/s^{\ast}
, and thus yield

\displaystyle\int_{p(s^{\ast}-\varepsilon)}^{p(s^{\ast})}\frac{c^{2}e_{p}}{e+p}\textup{d}p=\int_{s^{\ast}-\varepsilon}^{s^{\ast}}\dfrac{c^{2}(d-1)u(us-1)}{(us-1)^{2}-\dfrac{(u-sc^{2})^{2}}{c^{2}e_{p}}}\textup{d}s>\int_{s^{\ast}-\varepsilon}^{s^{\ast}}\frac{2c^{2}(d-1)u}{us-1}\textup{d}s.

(5.45)

For the left integral, we have with
p(s^{\ast})=0

\displaystyle\int_{p(s^{\ast}-\varepsilon)}^{p(s^{\ast})}\frac{c^{2}e_{p}}{e+p}\textup{d}p=c^{2}\int_{\gamma(s^{\ast}-\varepsilon)}^{\infty}\gamma\Phi_{i}^{\prime}(\gamma)\textup{d}\gamma=-\infty.

However this contradicts with the lower bound in (
5.45
) which has a finite value for
u(s^{\ast})<1/s^{\ast}
. Thus we have
u(s^{\ast})=1/s^{\ast}
.

Case II:
Solution becoming stationary for small
u_{0}
.

Lemma 4

For
u_{0}>0
sufficiently small, there exists an
s^{\ast}
such that

0<u(s)<\varphi_{A}(s)<\frac{1}{s},\;\,s\in(1/c,s^{\ast}),\quad u(s^{\ast})=\varphi_{A}(s^{\ast})=0.

Proof

-
Since the right-hand side of the ODE system (
4.33
) is smooth, the solution depends continuously on the initial datum for
s\in(0,1/c]
.
We compare the two solutions at
s=1/c
corresponding to the initial data
(u_{0},p_{0},\eta_{0})
and
(0,p_{0},\eta_{0})
, respectively.
Note that the latter yields a stationary solution, and that the entropy remains constant for smooth solutions.
For this purpose, the velocity is scaled by
1/c
, while the remaining quantities are scaled by their corresponding initial values.
We thus obtain for the scaled quantities

\displaystyle\left\|\begin{pmatrix}u(1/c)/c\\
p(1/c)/p_{0}\\
\eta(1/c)/\eta_{0}\end{pmatrix}-\begin{pmatrix}0\\
p_{0}/p_{0}\\
\eta_{0}/\eta_{0}\end{pmatrix}\right\|\leq\tilde{C}\left\|\begin{pmatrix}u_{0}/c\\
1\\
1\end{pmatrix}-\begin{pmatrix}0\\
1\\
1\end{pmatrix}\right\|=\tilde{C}\frac{u_{0}}{c}.

Here
\tilde{C}
is a constant depending on the Lipschitz constant of the RHS of the ODE system (
4.33
).
Hence, in order for the solutions to be closer than any given
\varepsilon_{c}>0
, we choose
u_{0}<c\varepsilon_{c}/\tilde{C}=:\varepsilon_{0}
. Thus we further have
0<u(1/c)<c\varepsilon_{c}
and
0<p_{0}-p(1/c)<p_{0}\varepsilon_{c}
.

For
s>1/c
, we get

\frac{us-1}{u-c^{2}s}=\frac{1-us}{c^{2}s-u}<\frac{1}{c}.

Combining this with
\eqref{systemODE}_{1}
and
\eqref{systemODE}_{2}
, we obtain that for
s>1/c

\displaystyle\frac{\textup{d}p}{\textup{d}u}=\frac{c^{2}(e+p)(us-1)}{(c^{2}-u^{2})(u-sc^{2})}<c\frac{e+p}{c^{2}-u^{2}}.

6
The spherical piston problem

In this section we consider the classical
spherical piston problem
for the
relativistic Euler system (
4.32
).
We prescribe a uniform rest state

(u,p,\eta)(0,r)=(0,p_{0},\eta_{0}),\qquad p_{0}>0,\;\eta_{0}>0,

and impose a boundary condition at a moving spherical surface of radius

r=\alpha t
, whose prescribed radial velocity is

u(t,\alpha t)=\alpha,\qquad\alpha\in(0,c).

Physically, this setup describes a rigid spherical piston expanding outward with constant speed
\alpha
, driving a compression wave into the surrounding relativistic fluid.
The induced flow is radially symmetric and may develop shocks due to wave steepening, a phenomenon of particular relevance in high-energy astrophysical events, such as supernova explosions or relativistic jets.
Relativistic effects, including Lorentz contraction and the coupling of energy and momentum, play a crucial role in shaping the dynamics, leading to non-linear behavior absent in classical fluids.

Because both the initial data and the piston trajectory are scale-invariant, the induced motion cannot select any characteristic spatial or temporal scale.
Consequently, the flow organizes itself into a self-similar pattern depending only on the similarity variable

\xi={r}/{t}.

The main goal of this section is to establish existence and uniqueness of such a self-similar solution, which captures the entire piston-driven compression dynamics in a single reduced system of ordinary differential equations.
Self-similar solutions also reveal universal features of relativistic flows, independent of specific initial conditions, making this problem a useful testbed for both theoretical analysis and numerical simulations.

Theorem 6.1
(Spherical Piston)

For any given initial datum
(u_{0},p_{0},\eta_{0})
satisfying
p_{0}>0,\eta_{0}>0
, and

(u,p,\eta)(0,r)=(0,p_{0},\eta_{0}),\;u(t,\alpha t)=\alpha,\quad\alpha\in(0,c),

the spherical piston problem for system (
4.32
) has a unique self-similar solution.

The proof of Theorem
6.1
is presented subsequently. Note that we have a slightly different situation here. Due to the initial datum
u_{0}=0
, the solution up to the shock is constant. Then the state on the other side of the shock is governed by the jump conditions and from there the solution evolves according to (
4.33
), with the perturbed state being the initial datum. Thus we have an unperturbed state ahead of the shock given by
(0,p_{0},\eta_{0})
. Since a singularity occurs in (
4.33
) when
\varphi_{A}(\overline{s})=0
for

\overline{s}=\frac{1}{c}\sqrt{e_{p}(p_{0},\eta_{0})},

a shock wave will appear for some
s_{P}\in(1/c,\overline{s})
with the speed
\sigma_{P}=1/s_{P}
.
Therefore,
we need to look for a shock wave solution of the ODE system (
4.33
), which satisfies

(u,p,\eta)(s_{P})=(u_{\delta},p_{\delta},\eta_{\delta})(s_{P}),\quad u(1/\alpha)=\alpha

for
\alpha\in(0,c)
.

Since we are considering a forward Lax shock, we have

\sigma_{P}<\lambda_{\delta}\quad\text{and}\quad 0=u_{0}<u(s_{P})=u_{\delta}<\sigma_{P}.

A direct calculation shows (omitting the arguments)

\displaystyle 0<\sigma_{P}<\lambda_{\delta}=\frac{c\sqrt{e_{p}(\gamma_{\delta})}u_{\delta}+c^{2}}{c\sqrt{e_{p}(\gamma_{\delta})}+u_{\delta}}\quad\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sigma_{P}=1/s_{P}}}{{\Leftrightarrow}}\quad\frac{c\sqrt{e_{p}(\gamma_{\delta})}-s_{P}c^{2}}{cs_{P}\sqrt{e_{p}(\gamma_{\delta})}-1}<u_{\delta}\quad\Rightarrow\quad A>0,

\displaystyle B=\left(c\sqrt{e_{p}(\gamma_{\delta})}s_{P}+1\right)u_{\delta}-\left(c\sqrt{e_{p}(\gamma_{\delta})}+s_{P}c^{2}\right)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle u_{\delta}<\sigma_{P}}}{{<}}\frac{1}{s_{P}}(1-s_{P}^{2}c^{2})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 1/c<s}}{{<}}0

and thus we have

g=A\cdot B=c^{2}e_{p}(\gamma_{\delta}(s_{P}))\left(u_{\delta}(s_{P})s_{P}-1\right)^{2}-\left(u_{\delta}(s_{P})-s_{P}c^{2}\right)^{2}<0.

Therefore the solution of (
4.33
) for the considered datum
(u,p,\eta)(s_{P})=(u_{\delta},p_{\delta},\eta_{\delta})(s_{P})
is locally well-defined.

We can directly verify for the solution of the (
4.33
) behind the shock that

u^{\prime}(s)>0\quad\text{and}\quad p^{\prime}(s)\,\begin{cases}&>0,\;us<1,\\
&<0,\;us>1.\end{cases}

Collecting the previous results we have
\varphi_{B}(s)>1/s
for
s>1/c
and
\varphi_{A}(s)<u(s)
as well as
\varphi_{A}^{\prime}(s)<0
and
u^{\prime}(s)>0
for
s\geq s_{P}>1/c
. Thus we find a
\tilde{s}(s_{P})>s_{P}
such that

u(\tilde{s}(s_{P}))=\frac{1}{\tilde{s}(s_{P})}.

Note that
\tilde{s}(s_{P})
will play the role of
1/\alpha
.
Since the solution of (
4.33
) is continuous for
s>s_{P}
and the given datum,
\tilde{s}
is a continuous function of
s_{P}
.
Due to
u^{\prime}(s)>0
in the interval
(s_{P},\tilde{s}(s_{P}))
, we have

\frac{1}{\tilde{s}(s_{P})}=u(\tilde{s}(s_{P}))>u(s_{P})=u_{\delta}(s_{P}).

Now, due to the Lax condition, we have

u(s_{P})=u_{\delta}>\frac{\sigma_{P}c\sqrt{e_{p}(\gamma_{\delta})}-c^{2}}{c\sqrt{e_{p}(\gamma_{\delta})}-\sigma_{P}}.

Together with
e_{p}>3
, we hence conclude that for
s_{P}>1/c
,

\displaystyle\lim_{s_{P}\to 1/c}u(s_{P})=c,\quad\lim_{s_{P}\to 1/c}\tilde{s}(s_{P})=\frac{1}{c}.

(6.66)

Now we claim that

\displaystyle\lim_{s_{P}\to\overline{s}}u(s_{P})=0,\quad\sup_{s_{P}\in(1/c,\overline{s})}\tilde{s}(s_{P})=\infty.

(6.67)

Suppose the claim is false, then there exists a constant
R_{0}>1/c
such that

\displaystyle\sup_{s_{P}\in(1/c,\overline{s})}\tilde{s}(s_{P})=R_{0}

(6.68)

Then, for
\overline{R}=R_{0}+\overline{s}
, we consider the following equation

\displaystyle\frac{dv}{ds}=\frac{(d-1)v(c^{2}-v^{2})(v-sc^{2})}{c^{4}\overline{s}^{2}(vs-1)^{2}-\left(v-sc^{2}\right)^{2}},\quad\frac{1}{c}<s<\overline{R}

(6.69)

with
v(\overline{R})=1/\overline{R}
. By similar proof as in
(
Lai2019
, Lemma 2.6)
, we can obtain that the solution to (
6.69
) satisfies
v(s)>0
for
s\in(1/c,\overline{R})
. This implies the solution
u=v(s)
of (
6.69
) should intersect the curve

u=\frac{c^{2}(s-\overline{s})}{1-c^{2}s\overline{s}},\quad s>\frac{1}{c}

at some point
(\hat{s},\hat{u})
with
\hat{s}<\overline{s}
and
\hat{u}>0
.

Due to
\lim\limits_{s_{P}\to\overline{s}}u_{\delta}(s_{P})=0
for the solution of (
4.33
), there exists some
\hat{s}_{P}
, which is sufficiently close to
\overline{s}
, such that

u_{\delta}(\hat{s}_{P})<\hat{u},\quad\hat{s}<\hat{s}_{P}<\overline{s}.

Let
\left(\widetilde{u},\widetilde{p},\widetilde{\eta}\right)(s)
be the solution of (
4.33
) with the initial condition

(u,p,\eta)(\hat{s}_{P})=(u_{\delta},p_{\delta},\eta_{\delta})(\hat{s}_{P}).

Due to the assumption (
6.68
), there exists some
\check{s}\in(\hat{s}_{P},\overline{R})
such that

v(\check{s})=\widetilde{u}(\check{s}),\qquad v(s)>\widetilde{u}(s),\,\,s\in\left(\hat{s}_{P},\check{s}\right).

Then we have

\displaystyle v^{\prime}(\check{s})-\widetilde{u}^{\prime}(\check{s})\leq 0.

(6.70)

Since
e_{pp}<0
and
e_{p\eta}<0
from (
3.25
), we can obtain

\overline{s}_{0}^{2}\equiv e_{p}\left(\widetilde{p}(\check{s}),\eta_{\delta}(\hat{s}_{P})\right)<e_{p}\left(p_{0},\eta_{\delta}(\hat{s}_{P})\right)<e_{p}\left(p_{0},\eta_{0}\right)=\overline{s}_{1}^{2}

since
\widetilde{p}(\check{s})>p_{\delta}(\hat{s}_{P})>p_{0}
and
\eta_{\delta}(\hat{s}_{P})>\eta_{0}
. Therefore, we further get

v^{\prime}(\check{s})=\frac{(d-1)v(c^{2}-v^{2})(v-\check{s}c^{2})}{c^{4}\overline{s}_{1}^{2}(v\check{s}-1)^{2}-\left(v-\check{s}c^{2}\right)^{2}}>\frac{(d-1)\widetilde{u}(c^{2}-\widetilde{u}^{2})(\widetilde{u}-\check{s}c^{2})}{c^{4}\overline{s}^{2}_{0}(\widetilde{u}\check{s}-1)^{2}-\left(\widetilde{u}-\check{s}c^{2}\right)^{2}}=\widetilde{u}^{\prime}(\check{s}).

This contradicts with (
6.70
) and thus the claim (
6.67
) is valid. Hence Thm.
6.1
follows from (
6.66
) and (
6.67
) and thus we completed the proof.

7
Conclusion

In this work we have studied the relativistic Euler equations in two and three dimensions with the physically realistic Synge equation of state for monoatomic gases, as well as its generalized version for diatomic gases.
We have established the existence and uniqueness of radially symmetric self-similar solutions to these systems, including the spherical piston problem.
A remarkable aspect of our analysis is that, thanks to the structure of the Synge energy, our results remain valid for the entire range of the relativistic temperature parameter
\gamma
(
3.9
): from the ultra-relativistic regime where
\gamma\to 0
, through the relativistic regime where
\gamma\ll 1
, up to the classical limit corresponding to polytropic gases when
\gamma\to\infty
.
This stands in clear contrast with previous studies in the literature, which typically require restrictive assumptions on the constitutive equations.
In addition, we have rigorously proved the inequality
e_{pp}<0
