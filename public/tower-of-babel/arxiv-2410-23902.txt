Responsible Retrieval Augmented Generation for Climate Decision Making from Documents
Matyas Juhasz, Kalyan Dutia, Henry Franks, Conor Delahunty, Patrick Fawbert Mills, Harrison Pim

Abstract
Climate decision making is constrained by the complexity and inaccessibility of key information within lengthy, technical, and multi-lingual documents. Generative AI technologies offer a promising route for improving the accessibility of information contained within these documents, but suffer from limitations. These include (1) a tendency to hallucinate or mis-represent information, (2) difficulty in steering or guaranteeing properties of generated output, and (3) reduced performance in specific technical domains. To address these challenges, we introduce a novel evaluation framework with domain-specific dimensions tailored for climate-related documents. We then apply this framework to evaluate Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) approaches and assess retrieval- and generation-quality within a prototype tool that answers questions about individual climate law and policy documents. In addition, we publish a human-annotated dataset and scalable automated evaluation tools, with the aim of facilitating broader adoption and robust assessment of these systems in the climate domain. Our findings highlight the key components of responsible deployment of RAG to enhance decision-making, while also providing insights into user experience (UX) considerations for safely deploying such systems to build trust with users in high-risk domains.

Introduction

Large Language Models (LLMs) such as Llama 3

Despite the now widespread use of LLMs, hallucinations (fabrication of false information), the ability to safeguard behaviour (especially when presented with malformed or malicious user input), and the ability to deterministically design properties of generated output text remain open research problems. Best practices for deploying LLM-supported applications safely and responsibly are still being developed
[
4
]
. Recent results have shown that these constraints are further exacerbated when LLMs are used in niche or highly specific domains, such as the legal domain
[

Alongside techniques such as supervised fine-tuning
[
6

or RLHF
[
7
]
, Retrieval Augmented Generation (RAG)
[
8
]
is emerging as an effective approach for improving the quality of domain-specific applications of LLMs. RAG uses retrieval to ground the model responses using trusted external information sources, reducing the extent of hallucinations significantly
[
9
]
. However, it does not eliminate them completely. In addition to these techniques that aim to reduce hallucinations (prevention), there are many others which aim at identifying hallucination in generated text (detection)
[
4
]
. Since no single technique is 100% effective, we advocate for a defense-in-depth approach when deploying LLM-supported applications.

We focus on the domain of climate law and policy, in which access to information contained within relevant documents is key for supporting humanity’s response to the climate crisis. Policy makers, analysts, academics, and other actors who work on interpreting climate laws and policies are all constrained by limited time and resources. Key information is buried within lengthy, difficult-to-parse documents, and written in many different languages and with heterogeneous terminology. These constraints are particularly acute for organisations and actors in low and middle income countries, where resources are especially limited, but who are also typically most impacted by the consequences of climate change. These considerations further highlight RAG as a good candidate for helping decision-makers access data buried within these documents.

Helping those looking to make (or challenge) climate-aligned decisions represents a high stakes application of AI, since such decisions can have a significant impact on human well-being and safety. Climate change in particular is an issue that disproportionately affects vulnerable groups, and irresponsible deployment of AI systems in this domain can significantly exacerbate these impacts through reduced decision making quality and the opportunity costs entailed by mis-allocation of resources. Undervaluing data (both inputs and outputs of models) can cause cascading downstream negative effects in AI systems
[
10
]
. The likelihood of such effects can be lowered by measuring appropriate metrics for data quality and model performance within LLM pipelines, designing for full transparency and observability during the AI life cycle, and involving domain experts throughout the development process
[
11
]
. We apply these principles by (1) involving domain experts throughout the development process and also as users, (2) defining and assessing key relevant quality and performance metrics throughout our pipeline, and (3) designing explicitly for transparency and user-empowerment in the end-user experience (UX).

A key principle in our responsible AI approach is that of user empowerment: accepting that mitigations of limitations in LLM capabilities are less than 100% effective, it is incumbent on system designers to ensure users have the tooling, user experience design, and support to easily, intuitively validate the information generated by the system and identify when failure modes might be impacting output quality. Responsible AI systems must build and honour trust, effectively inform users of capabilities and limitations, transparently communicate provenance chains, and support users regardless of technical expertise. We apply these principles in multiple ways in our deployed prototype, as described in Section
4
.

The rest of this paper details our approach to responsibly deploying a RAG system that can assist decision-makers in accessing valuable and reliable information faster. In particular we provide our evaluation approach to assess retrieval- and generation-quality of RAG systems in the climate domain. We publish (1) a live demo

Finally we discuss our approach to UX, and its role in the responsible deployment of Generative AI applications. We intend for this paper to act as an initial playbook and a conversation starter to guide the climate and policy communities to build robust and responsible generative AI solutions.

1.2
A systems approach

Our prototype RAG system consists of a sequence of components operating in a linear pipeline. As per typical RAG architectures, this involves (1) user input collection and guardrails for checking for malicious or problematic intent, (2) an information retrieval component to identify relevant source passages from the external information store, (3) an answer synthesis component to generate the final answer to the user’s initial query based on the retrieved information, and (4) a final set of guardrails to identify any problematic outputs from the LLM. We further incorporate online instantiations of our evaluation components, exposing the results of these evaluations to the user to empower them to assess their usage of the information provided appropriately.

Figure 1:
Components in our RAG pipeline

The robustness of our pipeline is derived from the aggregate behaviour of these components: we are able to evaluate and optimise the performance of each component individually, but do not expose the individual components to the user. Instead, we provide interfaces to the inputs and outputs of the entire pipeline only. As such the user’s interaction with the LLM itself is significantly more constrained than in general purpose LLM-supported applications like ChatGPT or Gemini. Underpinning this approach is a recognition that (1) user input must be treated as untrusted by system designers aiming to minimise systemic risks entailed by LLM-supported applications, and (2) users should not be required to, or assumed to, have the technical expertise to mitigate the risks of LLM-supported application usage alone. On the former, there is a growing body of literature around adversarial prompting of LLMs to get them to output unsafe or toxic responses, termed "jailbreaking"
[
24
]
. With safety experts employing ever more sophisticated techniques such as red-teaming using LLMs
[
25
]
and RLHF
[
7
]
, jailbreaking has become an adversarial arms race. Most recent developments show that an LLM with its logits exposed can be jailbroken with certainty
[
26
]
. This means that exposing "naked" LLMs in user-facing applications will by definition be breakable no matter how sophisticated the techniques deployed to align them. This does not mean that alignment techniques should be neglected; instead this argument motivates two foundational design principles: (1) defense-in-depth, in which we employ multiple strategies with complementary trade-offs, and (2) assuming that our approach will not be 100% effective, and therefore empowering the user via our UX and surrounding documentation and communications. The defense-in-depth approach mirrors those in other fields characterised by incomplete information on failure modes and vulnerabilities, such as cyber-security (see, for example,
[
27
]
).

We have used
[
28
]
as input guardrails for pre-filtering adversarial queries. We have also run extensive red-teaming, and prompt-iteration for intent detection for further checks. Finally our auto-evaluators run online for every generation, enabling UX approaches which can handle varying qualities of responses along different dimensions – while we employ best efforts to mitigate before something undesirable reaches the user, we accept that that will happen and therefore aim to empower the user to identify those situations.

Evaluator Design

We separately evaluate the two key pipeline components determining system performance:

•

retrieval: evaluating the relevance of retrieved passages, discussed in
2.2

•

generation: evaluating the quality of the generations taking retrieval as fixed, discussed in
2.3

In our evaluations we combine a number of approaches: using LLMs as automatic judges
[
29
]
, wherein larger, more powerful models act as evaluators of generations / retrieved passages; grounding the auto-evaluators against human judgements; and deploying LLM-as-judge evaluators in conjunction with other types of evaluators such as fine-tuned models. LLMs-as-judges are widely used, with a widespread implementation being G-Eval
[
30
]
which we used as base prompts in our approach. Where possible we also grounded the auto-evaluators with human judgements or deployed them in conjunction with other types of evaluators.

2.1
Datasets

Benchmarks and human annotated ground truth data are crucial to assess the evaluators that are then relied upon to make further decisions. This is so that errors do not propagate in complex pipelines (especially those that stack multiple LLMs as generators, drafters, evaluators, and so on), introducing invisible and unpredictable shifts in quality.

The data used for the different datasets generated throughout our work was sourced from the Climate Policy Radar (CPR) database
[
31
]
of national laws and policies and UNFCCC submissions containing over 6,000 documents published by every national government world-wide. For the purpose of this work we sampled 550 documents equally across World Bank Regions to ensure we could measure performance of models and prompts with distributional equity. The mean length of documents in this dataset is 80 pages with several documents over 1,000 pages long, and documents frequently contain layouts, tables and figures that are vital to sense-making. The complexity of retrieval at even a single document level motivates our single document scope as input to the system described in this paper. We plan to incrementally scale to successively larger groups of documents once our assessments show sufficient quality at each stage.

For the RAG preferences dataset
[
32
]
discussed in
2.3.1
, this was combined with a sample of documents related to energy systems published by the IEA, IAEA, OSCE and WMO.

2.2
Retrieval Evaluation

The objective of evaluating retrieval is to identify the method which retrieves the most relevant text passages given a query, measured against a set of judgements from human experts. We focus on open-source methods since this supports transparency, reproducibility, and accessibility of our contributions in the wider ecosystem. We designed an LLM judge to make ’pointwise’ judgements of whether a query is relevant to an individual text passage, and then tuned its prompt (via edits and few-shot examples) to align its pointwise judgements to those of human experts. For retrieval evaluation we used an LLM-as-a-judge with GPT-4o and a prompt based on G-Eval
[
30
]
.

We compared 4 open-source dense retrieval models against a BM25
[
33
]
baseline. Each dense retrieval model was also set up in a hybrid setting with BM25, with the query-passage relevance score
\alpha\cdot\text{BM25}(q,p)+\mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{p}
(with
\alpha=0.2
,
q
as query text,
p
as passage text, and their bolded counterparts as their dense vector representations). See the results table (
2
) for a list of all of the retrieval methods evaluated.

The annotation dataset was created from 194 synthetic questions generated using corresponding documents (see Section
B
) sampled from the CPR dataset. Our sampling ensured equal representation of author’s region and whether the document had been machine-translated. Passages to label were chosen by randomly selecting a retrieval method for each query and then sampling randomly from the top 30 search results, to ensure a balance of relevant and less relevant examples.

Following this, two domain expert annotators rated the relevance of each passage considering the query on a scale of 0 (not relevant at all) to 2 (highly relevant) – see Appendix
C
for full details. A round of discussion and corrective annotation took place after the initial annotation, which maintained some disagreements in relevance judgment. This was expected due to the subjectivity of search relevance. Final results used to compare against the LLM judge were the
maximum
of either human judgement, chosen to aim to ensure relevance for both expert perspectives in the design of the LLM judge. Judgement results were also collapsed to a binary setting, where the positive class ’relevant’ maps only to a label of 2 (highly relevant).

The final LLM judge achieved an
F1 score of 75.3%
, with a precision of 82.3% and recall of 69.0%. A confusion matrix for the final LLM judge is shown in Figure

The process of asking domain expert annotators to annotate a pairwise relevance dataset revealed that the framing of this text retrieval task could be improved upon, both for a human reader of search results and an LLM in a RAG system. Issues found are shown below; we highlight these to encourage on further work to RAG systems that not only focuses on better retrieval, but also different framings of the passage retrieval problem itself. Promising approaches to solve some of these issues include hierarchical and multi-hop retrieval
[
34
]
, contextual chunk representations
[
35
]
, multi-modal methods
[
36
]
and better representation of documents as semi-structured data
[
37
]
.

Human annotators found the following issues with judging relevance in the passage ranking framing:

1.

Useful information nearby
: human experts mark passages as relevant to queries if they contain any information that could indicate that a useful passage is nearby. This occurs especially when annotating section headings, or table and figure captions.

2.

Unspecific language
: sometimes the language in a passage is not specific enough to be able to tell whether a passage is relevant. E.g. for a query "what are the responsibilities of the Minister for Climate", and the passage is "the minister is responsible for…". This is likely to be common in expert documents as they are carefully edited to respect character limits.

3.

Document metadata required
: to respond to some queries (specifically questions), information about a document such as its title, year published or author is needed. E.g. the query "what are Tajikistan’s approaches to adaptation" requires knowledge of whether the document was published by Tajikistan to assess relevance of text within the document to the query.

2.3
Generation Evaluation

For our generation evaluation, we identified a set of dimensions that are relevant and important for our use-case: the alignment of the generated text with an acceptable content policy congruent with our organisational values; the faithfulness of the generated text against the retrieved sources; the extent to which the output is well-formatted; and whether the system answered (and should or should not have done, given the information available in the sources). Thus by “dimension” we mean an aspect of the generation that tells us about its quality as appropriate for the high-stakes social impact domain in which we operate. 2.3.2
Faithfulness

Hallucinations are segmented into factuality (divergence from real-world facts) and faithfulness (divergence from provided context) issues
[
4
]
, where the latter is more relevant in our case: the system should strictly restrict its responses to content provided in the context, and our dataset of climate law and policy documents can be treated as a high quality information store to ground against. Various methods have been explored for hallucination detection in language models. Two broad categories of approaches include:

•

LLM-based methods: these use a powerful model to critique the generation taking into account the query and any context

•

NLI methods: these employ smaller, fine-tuned models that are meant for detecting hallucinations given a query, context and response

Traditional, n-gram based methods have been shown to correlate poorly with human judgement. Even though large-scale diverse benchmarks exist for evaluating hallucination-detection methods, such as
[
44
]
, the specificity and nature of our set of documents renders these unusable for our use-case.

2.3.3
Formatting & Did System Respond

Formatting
encodes the rules set out in the draft generation guidelines
A.1
into regex- and text-checks, also giving a reason for failure. Our experiments showed that formatting violations (especially missing or fictitious citations) often coincide with cases that involve hallucinations: this is unsurprising, yet provides a simple tool to quickly filter and flag potential issues of a response to users using only rule-based checks. Strict constraints on LLM output formatting, such as by constraining to pre-defined JSON schema, have been shown to reduce LLM performance
[
49
]
. As such, our formatting guidelines are lean, requiring an output of markdown-formatted bullet point lists only. This is sufficient for parsing into more structured representations in our UI without overly limiting LLM output flexibility.

Since we have defaulted to preset messages for the no-response case (cases where the model should decline to respond), a simple text-search suffices for detection. This highlighted a significant category, mostly observed in smaller models, whereby the response starts with the preset message of "I cannot respond…" yet subsequently continues providing a response to a query it shouldn’t have answered in the first place – often with high co-occurrences of hallucination. Using the system-response evaluator is hence valuable for filtering these low-quality responses and provides a different path for the system to handle them. We include values for these no response checks in our published
rag-climate-expert-eval
dataset
[
32
]
.

Experiments

As with the evaluator designs, we run two main component-experiments: retrieval and generation, relying on the auto-evaluators designed and developed in

After consolidating a best retrieval strategy (Section

For query generation we used a number of approaches: seeding with queries collected during user interviews, generating different types of questions (including a typo, or those that yield numerical answers – see Table

Retrieval

After obtaining an auto-evaluator that correlates with human judgement sufficiently and is deployable at scale, we benchmarked the retrieval methods in Table (

For the single-document question answering task we consider recall the highest priority metric, as this corresponds to the proportion of the relevant information accessible to the document that is fed to the LLM. As well as scaling the number of documents we can annotate, the use of an LLM relevance judge here gives us the ability to estimate recall – given the lengths of the documents in the CPR dataset this would require an unattainable number of human hours of annotation otherwise. Secondary to recall we are interested in both precision and nDCG, as these both assess the average relevance of a passage in the top K results in different ways.

At both k=10 and k=20, precision and recall performance is poor. The reasons for this are twofold: 1) our dataset is harder than the task itself, containing questions rather than a mix of questions and keyword queries, and 2) based on initial user testing this seems to be because simply chunking and then retrieving relevant chunks is not suitable for the specifics of this retrieval task, which often requires attention to document structure and wider document context. 4
UX for responsible Generative AI applications

While conversational interfaces have been studied in depth, the advent of generative AI technologies has resulted in a Cambrian explosion of new UI/UX approaches with best practices still evolving. Our deployed prototype is not strictly a conversational interface, since we do not provide opportunity for multi-turn interaction within a question/answer thread and are not attempting to model a human conversational pattern. Instead, we use Reeves’ articulation of "conversation-sensitive design"
[
54
]
.

Given that (1) issues of hallucination and performance (particularly in technical domains) remain open research problems and (2) the mitigations we discuss in this paper are imperfect, we adopt an assumption that we cannot guarantee optimal (i.e. faithful, content-policy aligned) responses are presented to users. A responsible approach to AI-supported systems therefore entails a principle of empowering the user to easily validate the provenance and accuracy of information generated by the AI. Integral to HCI is a pragmatic approach to behavioural psychology, such as notions of cognitive budget/overhead
[
55
]
. A system that provides links to cited materials but that introduces friction in the process of comparing them to generated assertions is not empowering, since the cognitive overhead of the approach entails that many will not make use of it.

Similarly, AI entails violations of HCI-norms around the provenance of content – users can no longer assume that text on software has been written or reviewed by humans. We adopt the "dual-use technology" approach pioneered in nuclear and biotechnology domains, which characterises such technologies as being usable for good and bad purposes, especially when those bad purposes can have potentially disastrous consequences. Per
[
56
]
: "dual-use dilemmas arise when it is unclear how to prevent the harms associated with misuse without also forgoing the benefits of the technology." The authoritative tone, opaque and jagged frontier of model capability, and black-box nature of generative AI systems exacerbate these concerns, as does the vulnerability of such systems to jailbreaking, prompt injection, and other techniques for over-riding the guardrails and safeguards of system designers. We use
[
57
]
’s recommendations – in the context of the HCI design in particular, by making it clear to the user what text has been generated by AI and what is either UI copy or source material.

Our deployment of our RAG system
Queried
follows
[
58
]
’s taxonomy of research chatbot goals: showcasing its capabilities, acquiring conversational and user feedback data to improve the system, finding points of safety failure and discovering future use cases and inappropriate or favourable usages. It is therefore key to (1) build a system users find valuable and want to use (to generate sufficiently sized datasets for analysis), and (2) effectively communicate the limitations and capabilities of the system to users (to promote responsible usage and mitigate potential harms).

Fortunately, responsible approaches to generative AI system deployment align well with the factors that impact user perception of usability and user satisfaction with generative AI tools. Notably, trust is consistently the strongest predictor of perceived value or intent to use
[
59
]
. In-system components of trust (i.e. those within our power to affect via system design) include perceived ease-of-use, quality of interpretation of requests and advice, presentation, perceived security and privacy, the responsiveness of the chatbot, the quality control of the answers, and the ability to ask "stupid questions"
[
60
]
. User frustration spikes significantly in situations where users do not understand the system or its behaviour.
[
58
]
notes "the burden of anchoring the user’s inevitable perception of the chatbot is at least partly on the interface, interaction, and experience design". When AI systems do not reliably communicate their capabilities, users have to both converse and build a mental model of how they can converse at the same time, which is a negatively impacting increase in cognitive load.
[
61
]
found that models that are less willing to generate unsafe language show – up to a point – a significant increase in 30 day user retention. As such even-handed and lightweight but effective moderation, with input sanitisation and user feedback, engenders user trust in the system.

Task-completion and tone are two other important identified factors
[
62
,
58
]
. Task-completion - the rate or probability of completing a given task using the system - can be complex in generative AI systems. In particular, we aim to strictly only provide answers when the sources contain relevant information and doing so would align with our content generation policy. This means that a high proportion of questions will entail a refusal to answer, reducing task completion rates. We mitigate this by providing multiple further routes to task completion (such as other queries and searches on our law and policy search engine) as well as a neutral summary of retrieved sources in case the user assesses that information as relevant. Adopting an "exadiagetic" tone, a meta- or non-content text and UI around the answers that (1) explains limitations and capabilities, and (2) educates, explains, and contextualises behaviour, is shown to improve user trust and perceptions of usability. This aligns well with the transparency requirements of responsible dual-use AI deployment.

Given the above context, we adopted the following principles in our design of the deployed prototype system. These principles have been synthesised from relevant literature as referenced.

•

Cited sources are displayed next to AI-generated assertions, with highlighting emphasising which citations are relevant to which assertion

•

Likely-relevant sub-sentences within cited passages are further emphasised visually to draw the eye and reduce the cognitive overhead of parsing a cited passage to identify the relevant information

•

Online evaluation results below the answer both help inform users of limitations and suggest, when appropriate, additional prompts to validate or inspect carefully the answer given

•

Exadiagetic tone surrounds the AI-generated copy, such as within our online evaluation results below the answer

•

AI-generated text is flagged with a label denoting it as AI-generated

•

We treat user input as untrusted and do not incorporate it into any prompts except the answer synthesis prompt itself. If the LLM flags that it does not feel able to answer given our instructions, we provide a neutral summary of retrieved sources without reference to user input.

Considering our system’s approach for the RAG application we have recognised the UI as a key component for building trust with the users and providing them with the tools that allow them to understand the fidelity they should place in the answers (hence being explicit about the tool’s capabilities) and actively prompt them to cross-reference with source documents.

Figure 9:
User interface of the CPR Queried tool showcasing live evaluation scoring and source cross-referencing

The evaluations scores (Section
2.3
) are presented live to users after each response (Figure
9
) to inform them about potential issues with its quality across the 4 dimensions. This transparency allows us to handle the unavoidable cases of low-quality responses to damage trust as little as possible.

A key component of our UI is the unique side-by-side view of the model response and the source document citations. This reduces the mental overhead required to cross-reference information, and functionality such as citation-highlighting, and the inter-citation highlighting further improve the experience associated with fact-checking the model response. Relying on our strict generation policy and evaluation, this makes each piece of information easy to cross-reference.

5
Conclusion

In creating a RAG pipeline for climate decision making from policy- and law documents we have aimed at responsible deployment of generative AI. Our main contributions to the field consist of providing a playbook for the safe deployment of similar applications made up of an evaluation framework; multiple unique, open sourced datasets providing human feedback on generations in the climate domain (both for retrieval and generations); and the use of an experimental selection of parameters using the auto-evaluators. Our recommendations include:

•

using an ensemble of evaluators for important dimensions, and grounding LLM evaluators with human judgement where possible

•

using domain-specific benchmarks

•

providing tools to users for provenance and communicating certainty of answers to them

By open-sourcing the above assets we aim to bridge the gap created by the resource intensive nature of creating human judgement datasets and experiments at this scale.

6
Future Work

From the limitations and opportunities of this work we have identified beneficial future work in this domain in:

•

query understanding and brittleness
: in a technical domain, how do we ensure that queries get consistently good results when changed slightly, or written with more or less expert language?

•

domain-specific hallucination benchmarks
: as this is a setting in which hallucination carries a large potential risk, an expert-annotated hallucination benchmark and associated LLM judge for this task would be of great value

•

supporting translation in the RAG workflow
: can LLMs reliably be used as query and passage translators, in a way that aligns to the ethical considerations required for this domain?

•

enhanced retrieval
: through our user-interviews it has become clear that document structure carries a lot of value both for a) improving retrieval quality, and b) building trust and informing users.

•

multi-turn conversation
: in its current form our system can only facilitate single-turn question-answering. An obvious path for improvement is to allow for multi-turn conversations. Open questions remain about whether evaluators still correlate with human judgement on multi-turn datasets, and how the shift from single- to multi-turn impacts the other components of the system (e.g. advanced multi-turn jailbreaking against the guardrails)

•

evaluation on real-world user queries
: our experiments were run on synthetic queries. Repeating these using user queries would increase the robustness of the evaluations and their alignment to real-world use

•

measuring carbon emissions and exposing them to users
: measuring the carbon emissions of each query and informing users would support users’ decisions on whether to use a powerful system for their query

•

open source LLM judges
: these judges would enable further scaling at a lower cost than commercial APIs

A.1
Draft guidelines

General

The system should:

•

respond fairly, impartially and objectively to queries

•

restrict its responses to queries that can be answered based on the document

•

identify and respond to risks relating to human well-being

•

only respond as CPR RAG bot (naming TODO) and never assume any other persona

•

provide concise and helpful answers except for queries not to be answered based on any of the above

Formatting

•

all responses should be in English

•

all facts should be followed by 1 or more citations

•

longer responses should be structured in a bullet-point format with citations at the end of each line

•

quotes that are verbatim from the source document should be enclosed in quotation marks

No response cases

There are a number of cases where no response can be provided. In each of these the system should respectfully and helpfully respond, and provide options (e.g. suggested searches, alternative query structures) to the user.

1.

Prompt violating CPR guideline: the system should provide a reason why it cannot fulfil the request (i.e. the guideline violation, e.g. “the request is illegal” or “the request requires subjective judgement”) and offer an alternative safe query

2.

Insufficient information retrieved: the system should clearly signal uncertainty and the fact that information couldn’t be retrieved relating to the query (which does not necessarily mean it’s not present in the document), while also presenting the information that was retrieved to assist the user

3.

Unclear query: the system should be able to make reasonable assumptions (e.g. “climate” → “What does this document say about climate?”) but in all cases:

(a)

communicate that such assumptions were made

(b)

ask for clarification / follow-up in the end to make sure the user has the opportunity to steer the conversation

A.4
CPR Generation Policy Evaluator - labelling conclusions

Four internal expert labelers decided whether the response is indeed a violation of our policy guidelines in light of the query and the retrieved passages, leading to iterating on the policy guidelines, after realising various points:

•

formatting rules are better handled separately from the policy violations

•

user intent handling is a guardrail that falls outside of the realms of policy alignment: our systems approach allows for pre-filtering queries using an intent-detection layer, thus significantly reducing the work that needs to be done under this umbrella

•

"keyword search" which is a category of unclear intent needs to be handled specifically: from our user interviews we understand this as a major category, that needs to be facilitated

•

it is best to return a preset answer in the "no response case"
