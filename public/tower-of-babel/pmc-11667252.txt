Prioritising and including children in intersectoral policymaking: uncovering Immunity to Change in Ghana
Authors: Leonie Akofio-Sowah, Maria Lisa Soraghan, Srivatsan Rajagopalan, Kwame S Sakyi, Aku Kwamie, Bethany Jennings, Florence Ayisi Quartey, Isabella Sagoe-Moses, Prince Owusu, Sarah L Dalglish
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTDespite strong commitments to improving children’s well-being, nearly a third of Ghanaian children aged 36–59 months are not developmentally on track, with additional challenges due to the COVID-19 pandemic. Improvements in children’s health and well-being rely on effective intersectoral policies, however, not enough is known about how to achieve this in practice, particularly in low- and middle-income countries. We report on a case study of participatory intersectoral policymaking for child health in Ghana in 2021, feeding into the national Early Childhood Care and Development Policy. We used systematic methods to analyse policy documents from 22 Ministries, Departments and Agencies; procedural outputs from national policy convenings; and children’s inputs via a national competition, worksheets at a policy convening and video interviews. Data sources were analysed separately using content analysis for real-time application of findings into the policy process. Subsequently, data were re-analysed together, using an ‘insider-outsider’ approach, to provide a holistic view of the policy process. Beyond traditional child-centred policy areas (ie, health, education, youth and sports, social protection), most ministries lacked budgeted child-specific policies, partly because policymakers felt this was outside their mandate. Analysing children’s inputs to the policy process showed they had substantive policy ideas on their health and well-being that were intersectoral in nature. While underlying social and cultural assumptions about children’s place in society impeded their participation in policymaking, stakeholders nonetheless expressed their commitment to amplifying and institutionalising children’s contributions. Our study adds evidence on how to operationalise intersectoral partnerships, including by mapping mandates, budgets and roles across different functions and levels of government, with the mobilising participation of civil society. We also report on new mechanisms for including children’s perspectives in policymaking. Our findings have implications for child health policy and for other health initiatives that require effective intersectoral, participatory approaches.Keywords: Child health, Health policy

Background

Despite a nearly 50% reduction in child mortality since 2000, 6.5 million children aged 0–18 years still die each year, many because their basic needs are not fulfilled.1 Millions more children suffer from preventable ill health that can cause lasting harms across the life course. Many of the most effective interventions to reduce child mortality and improve child health and well-being lay outside the health sector.2 A series of case studies conducted on countries that made the most progress towards the United Nations (UN) Millennium Development Goal of reducing child mortality found that approximately half of these reductions in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) between 1990 and 2012 were attributable to investments made outside the health sector.3 Synthesising these trends, the 2020 WHO-UNICEF-Lancet Commission2 called for integrated national policies for children, defined as people aged 0–18 years, with augmented governmental capacity to carry them out and deliberate design choices to ensure sectors act jointly for children.

There is ample evidence that meaningful action for children’s health and well-being relies on activity in multiple sectors.4,8 The current global development agenda, outlined in the UN’s Sustainable Development Goals, requires intersectoral action and collaborative governance,9 as do broader efforts by governments to improve integration and internal efficiency.10 Despite widespread recognition of the importance of intersectoral approaches to development,8 11 challenges remain in achieving this in practice, particularly regarding integration across sectors, levels and domains of policymaking.

Drawing on existing explorations of collaborative governance, this paper understands ‘integrative’ governance as the process of public policy decision-making that engages with multiple organs of government, across Ministries, Departments and Agencies (MDAs), as well as with non-governmental actors to carry out political, economic or administrative actions.7 12 Intersectoral collaboration, on the other hand, is the overarching process by which diverse stakeholders interact to yield action.7 Integrative governance is the tool by which intersectoral collaboration can be accomplished effectively.7

Existing frameworks on how best to design and implement intersectoral health policies have infrequently been developed from or applied to LMICs.7 13 Much remains to be learnt about the mechanisms underlying successful integrative governance and intersectoral collaboration beyond high-income countries. The few studies exploring intersectoral collaboration in LMICs have frequently employed political economy analyses7 8; these and others outside LMIC contexts highlight the necessity for effective policymaking of issue saliency, incentives for action, shared information and resources, ownership of decision-making, and identification of problems and common goals.7,912

A crucial element of intersectoral policymaking for health is in the inclusion of all stakeholders, requiring participation to be at the forefront of agenda-setting and policy action. In the literature on intersectoral health policy, there are calls for greater participation in decision-making across government, as well as from non-governmental actors such as civil society organisations.10 11 Participation can also be extended to the public at large with increasing recognition that such processes improve understanding of the policy problem; create greater accountability, legitimacy and trust; and lead to more effective implementation.14,16

Children’s right to participate in decision-making in areas of life that affect them is codified in the UN Convention on the Rights of the Child, ratified by all countries except the USA.17 Country experiences have shown that children, in practice frequently meaning older children and adolescents, can meaningfully participate in analysing policy problems and designing solutions.18 In Barra Mansa, Brazil, municipal laws and a dedicated budget for children’s councils have ensured participation of children aged 9–15 years in budgeting processes and policy prioritisations since 1998.19 Similarly, in Nicaragua, child and youth participation in local governance has been facilitated through strong partnerships with local authorities and civil society, allowing for effective dialogue between children and politicians.20 Yet despite children’s right and ability to participate in policymaking processes, their contributions are rarely included in practice.21 The Lundy model22 identifies space, voice, audience and influence as the four essential components of child participation. Children should be provided with the appropriate space, through meaningful opportunities and a safe environment, to voice their views to an identified audience that has the power to respond. Influence is evidenced by these views being taken seriously and acted on.

In this paper, we provide a case study of intersectoral collaboration for children’s health in Ghana, with the active participation of multiple stakeholders within and outside government, including children. We draw lessons for intersectoral collaboration applicable to LMICs and any country seeking to enhance collaboration across sectors and improve policy processes for and with children.

Methods

Study design

This implementation research study uses mixed methods to improve knowledge and adoption of child-centric policies in Ghana by analysing a participatory, intersectoral policy process and feeding into that process in real time.23 Retrospectively, we used case study methodology to combine these strands and understand the structures and mechanisms underlying the integrative governance (or lack thereof) for children in the policy process.24 25

Description of the policy process for children in Ghana

Children under 18 make up 41.8% of the Ghanaian population.26 Strong political commitments have been made in recent years to improve children’s development, including the adoption of an intersectoral Early Childhood Care and Development (ECCD) policy in 2004; Child and Family Welfare Policy in 2015; Justice for Children Policy in 2016, National Gender Policy in 2016; National Domestic Violence Policy in 2009 and National School Feeding Policy in 2016. Recent policies on children have also included their participation, for instance in the drafting of the Child and Family Welfare, Justice for Children and the Voluntary National Reporting Mechanism.

Nonetheless, over 30% of Ghanaian children aged 36–59 months are not developmentally on track.27 The COVID-19 pandemic worsened children’s educational and developmental outcomes, with school closures affecting over 9 million primary and secondary school children from March 2020 to January 2021,28 contributing to learning lags and mental health issues.29 To address these issues, the Center for Learning and Childhood Development-Ghana (CLCD), a civil society organisation; the Ghanaian Ministry of Gender, Children and Social Protection (MoGCSP); and Children in All Policies 2030, a global initiative created to implement the recommendations of the 2020 WHO-UNICEF-Lancet Commission2 organised a multi-step policy process to bring together stakeholders across sectors and develop a coordinated policy agenda for children (figure 1). The process, initiated in June 2021, was overseen by a steering committee of child-focused stakeholder organisations from government and civil society organisations across the country. Activities were led and implemented by CLCD, beginning with a situational analysis that reviewed 22 government strategic plans submitted to Ghana’s National Development Planning Committee (NDPC) to determine which sectors had formalised plans for children.

Overview of the intersectoral policy process to put ‘Children in All Policies’ in Ghana. CLCD, Center for Learning and Childhood Development.

A national conference was organised in September 2021 to introduce and develop the intersectoral approach and the inclusion of children’s voices in policymaking. All ministries were invited to participate. On day 1, 150 participants from government, civil society, academia and the private sector met to discuss how to incorporate children’s needs, views and perspectives in all policies. Across stakeholder groups, registration data show that 17% were from the health sector (from 22 organisations), 33% were from education (from 45 organisations) and 10% were from justice (14 organisations), with remaining participants hailing from other sectors. Day 2 consisted of a focused sector-level meeting, bringing together representatives from the 22 MDAs whose strategic plans had been analysed, representing outreach to most, if not all, major ministries and sectors of government, with a few notable omissions such as the Ministry of Works, Housing and Transport. At this meeting, participants (including children) completed worksheets to identify barriers to change, following an approach from organisational psychology called ‘Immunity to Change’.30 To amplify children’s voices, CLCD produced a video featuring children, which was presented to policymakers on day 1, alongside themes from a competition called ‘Build Me a Future’, in which children used art and storytelling to articulate their envisioned future, presented on day 2.

Outputs from these activities were analysed to produce a roadmap for integrating children into all national and sectoral-level policies. The roadmap was formally launched by the MoGCSP and is being implemented at the time of writing. A full description of the national conference and related activities and outputs is available in online supplemental materials.

Data collection

Data collection took place over the course of the policy process in Accra, Ghana from June to October 2021 and included three types of data. First, as part of the situational analysis, strategic plans for the period 2013–2021 were collected from 22 MDAs, including those that typically are concerned with children’s affairs (Ministry of Health; Ministry of Education; Ministry of Youth and Sports; MoGCSP), those that may deal with children indirectly (Ministry of Special Development Initiatives, Ministry of Employment and Labour Relations) and others which have few dealings with children under 18 years (Ministry of Aviation, Ministry of Energy, Electoral Commission, Ministry of Chieftaincy and Religious Affairs).

Second, work products from the national conference and focused sector-level meeting were collected, including notes and minutes from breakout sessions (n=9), worksheets from the ‘Immunity to Change’ exercise (n=2), and the roadmap, the main interim product of the policy process.

Third, children’s inputs into the policy process were collected via artwork entries to the ‘Build Me a Future’ competition (physical objects, images and children’s descriptions), adapted from Lego Foundation’s ‘Build me a City’ competition, as well as a video in which children were invited to expand on their ideas and talk about ‘what they would do if they were president’. For the competition, children were recruited through fliers distributed to approximately 100 schools in Greater Accra, Ashanti, and Bono regions via the National Day Care Association, National Council for Private Early Childhood Growth and Development and selected government schools (see online supplemental materials for additional details). The competition was open from late July to late August 2021, and 30 submissions were received and analysed (20 from Greater Accra region, 5 from the Northern region, 3 from Bono region and 2 from Ashanti region). Participants ranged in age from 12 to 15 years and were evenly split between boys and girls; most hailed from the greater Accra region. For the video, children were approached in a secondary school and in three community settings (rural, poor and middle-income) in and around Takoradi and asked to participate in the video. Written consent was provided by both children and parents for all participating in the video and competition.

Data analysis

As this study fed directly into an ongoing policy process, data analysis was necessarily iterative. Data analysis procedures were similar for the three different data sources, which were first analysed separately to feed back to policymakers in real time (led by LA-S and KS, with FAQ, ISM and PO contributing). For the situational analysis of strategic plans, an inductive approach was used to develop a codebook from a sample of seven documents, which were read in their entirety with relevant text coded line by line. A codebook was developed and applied to all documents to evaluate the extent to which different ministries had policies, plans or activities for children, using text searches for terminology identified as relevant, including the words ‘child’, ‘children’, ‘infant’, ‘youth’ and ‘adolescent’. Dedoose V.9.0.17, a data management software, was used to facilitate data coding and identification of themes. To categorise and present data thematically, we then deductively applied UNICEF’s framework for five child-focused SDG themes.31

A similar process was used to analyse the two other types of data. For example, work products from policy conferences were analysed to feed into the ongoing policy process by re-reading and/or using discussion and consensus to identify key themes inductively. Specifically, the organising team of six staff members first reviewed notes, worksheets and other outputs to decide on an initial analysis frame (usually the same questions used in the policy meetings) and conduct a first round of line-by-line coding to identify emerging themes. Codes were then refined with outside team members and the steering committee.

For the third type of data, children’s inputs into the policy process, children’s statements in the video and their descriptions of their submissions to the ‘Build Me a Future’ were transcribed verbatim. Text was first coded line-by-line to generate an initial list of codes, which was refined via discussions between four members of the organising committee. Contest submissions were coded twice according to which government sectors they represented, with disagreements resolved by consensus. Further details on the analysis methods are available in online supplemental materials.

After the completion of the policy events discussed in this paper, we re-analysed data holistically by reviewing all data sources with ‘fresh eyes’ (notably MLS, SR and SLD) and using discussion between team members to establish key themes and findings. We used an ‘insider-outsider’ approach, by which variations in researchers’ personal connection to the topic broaden available analysis perspectives and aid in honing interpretations.32 We used several strategies to enhance the quality of our analysis.33 We triangulated between data sources and types of informants and used member checking by regularly consulting with participants in the policy process throughout preliminary data analysis and while preparing this article.

Patient and public involvement

No patients were involved in this study. The study engages with policymakers, government officials, civil society organisations and children from the public who gave consent to participate, along with their parents. The policy process and research study were conducted sequentially, meaning results fed back into the process and informed next steps. Therefore, all participants were informed of the results in real time and had influence in how those results were used and shared. The paper will be widely disseminated by CLCD-Ghana who are in communication with all participants.

Results

The ‘Children in All Policies’ process brought together national policymakers from across sectors from June to October 2021 to discuss children’s needs in Ghana, provide a comprehensive mapping of existing policies to address those needs, and decide what is required moving forward.

Beyond traditional child-centred areas, most ministries lack budgeted child-specific policies

Outside of the sectors traditionally concerned with children’s health and well-being, such as health, education, and child protection, few sectors incorporate children into their agendas. While some MDAs’ strategic plans mentioned children in their objectives, most did not articulate operational plans for them. Of the strategic plans reviewed in the situational analysis, 41% (9/22) had no specific policies or activities for children—even among ministries that cover areas integral to children’s health and well-being, including the Ministry of Food and Agriculture; Ministry of Environment, Science, Technology and Innovation; and the Public Service Commission. Only 32% (7/22) of MDAs had specific child-related policies. These departments were typical to the children’s sector such as the MoGCSP; Ministry of Youth and Sports; and Ministry of Health. Additionally, there were six ministries that mentioned children in their plans but did not have any specific policies aimed at or involving children.

The strategic plans that included specific policies targeting children’s needs and interests tended to focus on education and training, with the most common policies covering high school programmes (n=3 policies), employment (n=2) or training activities (n=4) (figure 2). One example is the Ministry of Youth and Sports’ launching the Steps Towards Employment and Productivity programme, which equipped 5000 young adults ages 15–35 with artisanal skills. The Ministry of Employment’s plan included work to increase awareness about child labour laws and protection from violence and exploitation. Other policies and activities focused more on engagement, such as youth empowerment (n=4 policies), the Children’s Parliament (n=1) and family involvement (n=2), such as the Ministry of Chieftaincy and Religious Affairs’ programme to identify traditional cultural beliefs that are harmful to women and children.

Ministries, Departments and Agencies (MDAs) with existing policies and activities for children. MDAs in grey indicate no policies or activities, n=9.

As part of the situational analysis of strategic plans, the UNICEF framework of five child-focused SDG themes was used to analyse policies for coverage across key needs.31 Of the identified policies, the largest proportion (39%) engaged with the theme of giving children ‘a fair chance at life’, followed by 26% supporting ‘learning’ and 22% supporting ‘survive and thrive’. Only 9% of policies addressed children’s need to ‘live in a safe and clean environment’ and 4% covered ‘protection from violence and exploitation’. Further, the situational analysis found that policy and advocacy activities were uncoordinated, even in traditional children’s sectors, potentially limiting impact and deepening child inequality. The analysis also revealed duplications in policies, another result of insufficient coordination.

The limited extent of policies and activities across sectors, and particularly those not traditionally involved with children’s health and well-being, was reflected in a lack of allocated funds. Only four ministries, even among those that included children in their policies, had set aside funds for child-related activities in their strategic plans—all of which explicitly mentioned funding shortfalls. These ministries were the Ministry of Health, Ministry of Youth and Sports, Ministry of Business Development and the MoGCSP. For example, the Medium-Term Development Plan (2018–2021) of the Ministry of Youth and Sports stated:

Many youth-related activities were not implemented during the period because of inadequate budgetary allocation and non-release of funds. Most of the releases that came to the sector were used to support the activities of the National Teams leaving very little for implementation of youth programmes.

These budgetary limitations were found across government: a UNICEF budget analysis examined separately from the situational analysis, covering 2016–2020, found that the national budget allocated for child-related activities increased from 0.10% to 0.26%, resulting in public disappointment.34 Taken together, these findings suggest that children’s issues do not receive adequate funding.

In discussions at the national conference, policymakers were not in agreement about the appropriate role of sectors across government in addressing children’s concerns. For example, during breakout room discussions, some stakeholders said that not every MDA had a mandate to focus on children, and thus the lack of specific policies and activities in some sectors was understandable. Other stakeholders said all sectors had a responsibility to include and prioritise children in their policies. There was a suggestion that whether a sector directly relates to children’s health and well-being or not, children should be included as stakeholders in policymaking processes (Memo from Breakout Group 9). These concerns were addressed directly in the ‘Roadmap’ document that was produced as an output of the policy process and adopted by the MoGCSP (see online supplemental materials). The roadmap called for a review of the constitutional mandates of each Ministry as a preliminary step towards advocating for the inclusion of children in strategic plans, as well as for conducting internal child-focused reviews in each MDA.

Children have substantive policy ideas on their health and well-being that are intersectoral in nature

Children’s inputs into policy discussions via the ‘Build Me a Future’ competition and their comments in the video reflected the wide array of activities that they considered to be integral to their good health and well-being. The 30 contest submissions were cross-cutting in nature, with the most common themes being education, health, child protection and welfare, sanitation, economic well-being, transportation, housing and recreation (figure 3). Most children’s submissions were categorised under multiple themes (categorisations are not mutually exclusive): 10 submissions (33%) were categorised under one theme, 6 submissions (20%) under two themes, 7 submissions (23%) under three themes, 3 submissions (10%) under four themes and 4 submissions (13%) under five themes. For example, a child’s description of a ‘simple toilet facility’ was categorised only under sanitation because it focused strictly on WASH facilities. On the other hand, a description of a government project building ‘good roads’ that ‘can create jobs for people’ was categorised under transportation and economic well-being.

Analysis of themes in children’s entries in ‘Build Me a Future’ competition (n=30 submissions). Each submission could be categorised under multiple themes.

Across submissions, education was the most common theme illustrated by children. Over 60% of submissions depicted improved education in the form of building new schools, libraries, computer and science labs, or providing school materials such as chairs, desks and laptops. New school buildings was the most mentioned element in the children’s explanations of their submissions (figure 4a). For example:

Sample of children’s submissions to the ‘Build Me a Future’ competition. Clockwise from top left: (a) a model of a school with adequate facilities, such as bathrooms; (b) a drawing by a child showing illegal mining; (c) a house with traffic lights built using mixed materials; (d) a community vision highlighting the integration of safe play areas.

We want the government to build enough schools so that children sitting [attending school] under trees can learn. (Young girl)

Similarly, in the video in which children were asked what they would do ‘if they were president’, one adolescent girl said:

Some schools don’t have classrooms; they study under trees. I would provide classes for them and … laboratories for them.

Another important theme mentioned by children was health, with 50% of submissions in the competition depicting building hospitals and health facilities. Health was commonly mentioned in conjunction with other themes like transportation and housing. Participants mentioned problems for pregnant women and sick children in receiving care because there are no hospitals close to where they live. Children also spoke of diseases from being homeless or having no toilet facilities. For example:

In some communities, the hospitals are far, so when a child gets sick, they have to send the child to the far hospital … So I am appealing to the government to build a health facility. (13-year-old boy)

I would like the government to build this affordable housing … It will help children not to contract diseases like malaria, fever and cholera. (15-year-old girl)

Children mentioned child protection and welfare in 40% of submissions. Many participants depicted and discussed the needs of orphans and children in street situations across the themes of education, housing and health. Five submissions to the ‘Build Me a Future’ Competition mentioned building orphanages specifically, and others talked about the need to prevent child homelessness and protect children living on the street:

More orphanages should be built so that the children on the streets will have access to quality education and a brighter future for tomorrow. (12-year-old girl)

His Excellency the president, this is my passionate appeal to you. I will be most grateful if you can use your high office through your ministers to relocate these children from the streets and to get more formal and informal education for a better Ghana tomorrow. (14-year-old girl)

I’d like the government to build this affordable housing, which our parents can afford. … That will reduce streetism, prostitution and child abuse. (15-year-old girl)

Sanitation was heavily represented in children’s submission. At least six children specifically requested adequate toilet facilities, ‘so we can stop defecating around the sea’. This intersected with the need for better housing and schools, which the children noted lacked toilet facilities. The children also recognised the relationship between sanitation and health:

I am urging the government to give small loans to community members so they can construct toilet facilities in their areas. That will help reduce open defecation that can lead to several diseases. (14-year-old boy)

Others mentioning improving waste management and cleaning the environment:

I built the dustbin so that children can put waste inside it to prevent littering of the environment. (14-year-old boy)

Economic well-being was a cross-cutting theme, mentioned in conjunction with other themes. Economic well-being and sanitation were depicted together by one submission, showing the harms of illegal mining and the dumping of wastewater on the environment (figure 4b). Children mentioned the need for jobs, affordable housing, and improved traffic lights and roads (figure 4c):

The government can create jobs for people who don’t have jobs and would like to work. (Young girl)

I want the government to build houses, hospitals, and good roads … This will promote clean roads and also reduce accidents. (Young girl)

To a lesser extent, children represented recreation, with three submissions depicting communities with safe play areas and parks, mentioning football fields and sports stadiums (figure 4d). Recreational improvements were always mentioned alongside other sectors.

Underlying assumptions about children’s place in society impede participatory policymaking

First, adult policymakers expressed a collective commitment to including children’s voices in policymaking, but several assumptions and hidden commitments suggested that achieving this goal would be challenging. Many assumptions had to do with the idea of children’s proper place in society (table 1). The most entrenched barrier identified by both children and adults was the existing culture of hierarchy, where adults know best and children are supposed to be meek. Adult policymakers expressed a fear that including children in decision-making would encourage them to be disrespectful or disagreeable towards adults. They described the Ghanaian cultural belief system as emphasising age hierarchy where children rank below other members of society. There was a fear that children may feel equal to adults if they are allowed to make decisions; and if given too much space in society, the voices of adults will be less valued. Children could become too active, independent and confident, changing the cultural status quo:

Giving children more room to make decisions will be disrespectful [to elders]. (Adult Sector leader)

The fear is that children will be infiltrated with other cultural values when given enough space or place to make decisions. (Adult Sector leader)

Children were aware of the cultural hierarchy around age and expectations in terms of respect and ‘not talking back’. However, children identified this assumption as not always true; talking back to adults does not always have the intent to disrespect.

Policymakers also identified the underlying assumption that adults should provide for the needs of children, rather than involving them in decision-making (table 1). They feared that children were not knowledgeable enough to make decisions and did not know what they wanted. In general, there was an overwhelming belief that children lacked the competencies, maturity, and rationality required for decision-making:

Children cannot prioritise, and they may advocate for policies at the detriment of the environment and climate change. (Adult sector leader)

Involving children in policymaking would therefore be detrimental to the political process and society’s well-being. As such,

Providing children’s needs is more important than their engagement. (Adult sector leader)

Children were well aware of the low regard adults held about their views and opinions:

They think whatever we say does not make sense and children are straight-up stupid or dumb. (Child participant in Immunity to Change exercise)

The child participants said these attitudes created a negative feedback loop, where adults’ perceptions of children discouraged them from speaking up and sharing their views, further excluding them from participating in policy discussions.

Alongside these barriers, leaders with political considerations expressed fear of losing public support and not being re-elected or re-appointed if they were to consult with children. The concern was that society is not used to including children’s voices, so policymaking that consults with children may not be taken seriously. Children interpreted adults’ political considerations in a more cynical light. They were concerned with the quality and priorities of leaders, fearing they lacked connections with children and ignored the importance of children’s health and well-being:

Politicians are misled by money and their focus is only on money. (Child participant in Immunity to Change exercise)

Politicians only focus on projects that will win them votes come elections. (Child participant in Immunity to Change exercise)

Despite the significant scepticism about whether it was appropriate for children to participate in policymaking processes, there was sustained discussion at the national conference about ways to amplify children’s voices and capture their feedback. Across breakout rooms, there was resounding agreement that a child parliament should be organised by the government, funded properly and sustained through local government and the educational system. Speakers also consistently said the child parliament must be representative and diverse.

We need to give them [children] freedom to express themselves to facilitate transparency of thought. Child Parliament is a good thing. But it must be decentralised to include children in diverse areas, including deprived areas. (Memo from Breakout Group 5)

Social media platforms could allow for children and young people to comment on policy issues too. (Memo from Breakout Group 6)

These discussions were reflected in the roadmap document, which called for institutionalising and bolstering a child’s parliament, as well as documenting and demonstrating its impact. The roadmap also called for investments in shifting socio-political and cultural views about children’s roles in policymaking, for example, via a media campaign using traditional and social media.

Discussion

This implementation research study found that while Ghanaian policymakers were attuned to the need for intersectoral action for national child health objectives, coordinated policy formulation and implementation has proved difficult. Outside of the traditional domains of health, education and social protection, most government institutions lack developed agendas for children. There was a significant gap between the ambition of children’s own policy ideas and existing activities, which tended to centre around educational or training programmes, many of which had insufficient or no budgetary support. Children’s messages to policymakers showed that they conceived of their needs holistically, in sector-spanning ways—for example, suggesting improved housing or road safety interventions to protect health. Despite these valuable contributions, cultural and political assumptions, such as children’s perceived need to be ‘seen and not heard’, their supposed inability to provide substantive input and politicians’ fear of losing public support, impeded children’s participation in policymaking processes.

A major outcome of the policy process described in this paper is the update to Ghana’s ECCD policy, covering children aged 0–8 years, still in draft form at the time of writing.35 The draft policy rationale includes ‘rejuvenating intersectoral collaboration’ and its text provides detailed provisions for this joint work. First, the new policy makes explicit its alignment with the mandates of each involved MDA, building on the situational analysis to map existing initiatives that already contribute to achieving the ECCD policy’s objectives (and their allocated budgets), then detailing the additional activities required (and their needed budget). Next, a ‘Framework for Coordinated Implementation’ lays out specific roles, including technical coordination (at national level by the MoGCSP); operational coordination and management (by various bodies at national and local levels, and at service delivery points); mechanisms for systems-wide integration (via the NDPC and the Inter-Ministerial Coordinating Committee on Decentralization); and various provisions for accountability mechanisms. Sector responsibilities for policy interpretation, adoption and integration are outlined for at least nine ministries, with implementation roles and responsibilities for non-state actors. In an annex, specific guidance is presented on developing a costed implementation plan for intersectoral work, which proved ‘the most challenging’ of the policy development activities.

Intersectoral action is critical for achieving children’s health goals.2 3 36 Our study provides additional evidence that problems around commitments and coordination within and across sectors need to be addressed first to have fruitful collaboration.11 37 In Pakistan, intersectoral action around nutrition was better organised compared with early childhood development, in part because of the former’s clear and inclusive menu of actions for stakeholders and the existence of structural platforms for catalysing coordination.8 Ghana’s new draft ECCD policy, while yet to be implemented, is a further suggestion that specificity and alignment with existing mandates is key—as is the consensual, deliberative nature in which collaborative frameworks are built. Chile’s successful intersectoral programme for early childhood development, Chile Crece Contigo, which has been associated with measurable decreases in the proportion of children with developmental delays, also has a sophisticated design for intersectoral collaboration constructed via deliberate consensus-building by technical and political stakeholders.38

Our study demonstrates the key role of civil society organisations in mobilising intersectoral action. Given the demands on government institutions, much of the process in Ghana was driven by a well-connected NGO, CLCD-Ghana, with other studies confirming that civil society plays an essential role in advocating for policies, linking to communities, providing political support and increasing agency.11 37 In India civil society and international agencies provided a key convening forum to permit intersectoral action around One Health.11 However, the involvement of civil society must also be managed effectively. In the Philippines, civil society organisations helped drive adoption of an important Reproductive Health Law, however they tended to look at the impact on individuals and neglected groups rather than larger outcomes, requiring work to balance against other priorities.37

One strong finding of our study was the cultural, social and institutional resistance to including children’s voices in policymaking discussions about children’s issues (box 1). In the new draft ECCD policy, several of the children’s ideas from the competition and video have been incorporated, including expanded provision of play, recreational and cultural amenities, such as community centres and libraries, and guidelines for the establishment of safe water and sanitation in schools, kindergartens, ECD facilities and informal care venues. Yet while ‘child participation’ is mentioned as a guiding principle of the draft policy, no specific provisions are mentioned in the policy that would enable their participation, at least in the version we reviewed. Our findings showed that adults had strongly held notions of age hierarchy, well understood by children, which discouraged them from speaking up and demanding inclusion. Children nonetheless had thoughtful goals for Ghana’s development, depicting cross-cutting policy ideas and showing an awareness of crucial problems, belying adults’ scepticism about their abilities. For example, an adult stakeholder in the ‘Immunity to Change’ exercise was quoted questioning the knowledge of children around climate change, and yet their artwork spoke to issues of land degradation, clean water, and proper waste disposal.

Children’s ability to express themselves is influenced by their social and cultural settings and the extent to which there is space and support for their participation.39 Policymakers must create the necessary platforms that give children the chance to influence policies that affect their future. While policymakers understood children’s right to space and voice as described in Lundy’s model, there was not an adequate appreciation for the right to influence.22 Positive and lasting relationships need to be established and fostered between policymakers and children’s rights organisations to achieve greater impact through participation. A study of child participation initiatives in South Africa, Swaziland and Zambia found key components to overcoming barriers were partnerships, communication and identifying cultural values and practices that encourage child participation.18 Children could achieve more substantive influence via the Children’s Parliament, which has been called on an ad hoc basis in Ghana but never institutionalised. This platform has the potential to ensure that all policies reflect children’s interests by combining children’s voices with policymakers’ initiatives to develop long-term national interventions and regulations.

This research study was nested within a broader—and ongoing—policy process. Intermediate findings, for example from the situational analysis and children’s policy priorities, were fed back to policymakers and other stakeholders and then re-analysed here, allowing for great depth and a more holistic view. There are growing calls to use implementation research to bridge research and practice in global health, as implementation is likely to be successful and sustained when there are effective partnerships co-producing knowledge and sharing lessons learnt.23 Existing scholarship points to potential trade-offs between the rigour of design and data collection versus usefulness of research; generalisable knowledge versus context-specific problem-solving; and incentives or disincentives for researchers versus implementing agencies.23 Our research also confronted these trade-offs: the study’s proven usefulness to the Ghanaian policy process (demonstrated in the draft ECCD policy) suggest the value to be found in the research’s process, as much as the content. Arguably, our partnership could have been made more effective by working with policymakers to clearly define the research cycle and plan the research inputs into policy processes.40

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study examining a combined intersectoral and participatory approach to policymaking on issues of children’s health and well-being. The embedded nature of the research provided benefits in terms of immediate feedback into the policy process and contextual usefulness. The ‘Immunity to Change’ exercise with children and policymakers is potentially a novel way for participants to identify and debate important issues, presumptions and behaviours that prevent the development and application of child-specific strategies. Some participants had trouble finishing the worksheets in the allotted time, however this was not a major detriment to the overall activity since answers were synthesised across participants. This study has some limitations. We were only able to obtain 22 out of 30 medium-term sector plans from government institutions. The Ministry of Health’s previous medium-term sector plan (2013–2017) was examined because the most recent version (2018–2020) was not readily available, and the Ministry of Education’s medium-term sector plans were not available. The focus on these plans, to the exclusion of other types of strategies, may have led to the exclusion of some relevant child-centred activities. Furthermore, interactions with policymakers and children were generally not transcribed verbatim, and the ages of children who participated in the videos were not recorded. Although detailed notes were taken, it is possible that some accuracy was lost. Finally, the ‘Build Me a Future’ competition has some limitations, notably the small number of submissions, the large proportion of submissions from the Greater Accra Region, and the possibility that submissions were influenced by adults or older siblings. Future studies should look to collect submissions from all regions of Ghana, in part by expanding the duration of the competition and using in-person visits to schools.

Conclusion

Protecting and improving children’s health and well-being requires institutions across government and civil society to work together collaboratively in an integrated manner, yet much remains to be learnt about how to do so effectively. Even less is understood about how to integrate the views of children into decision-making processes that affect them. This implementation research study adds to what is known about the existing landscape such policy initiatives may face, the need for detailed operational frameworks and importance of addressing cultural barriers to children’s participation. Further research on this topic would be a timely and necessary contribution to bridge research, policy and practice to catalyse improvements to children’s health and well-being.
