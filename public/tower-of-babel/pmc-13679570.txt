Latent Profiles of Fear of Complications and Associated Factors Among Older Inpatients with Type 2 Diabetes: A Cross-Sectional Study
Authors: Jinxian Xu, Juan Gao, Qin Wu, Yuhan Duan, Shuqin Zeng, Jiaqi Liao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AimIn the context of diabetes, fear of complications (FoC) refers to a psychological response to the anticipated onset or progression of diabetes-related chronic complications. However, analyses based solely on total scores may conceal the heterogeneity among patients. This study aimed to identify latent FoC profiles among older inpatients with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) and examine the demographic and clinical characteristics, hope, and self-management factors associated with these profile.MethodsIn this cross-sectional study, 310 older inpatients with T2DM were recruited from the endocrinology department of a tertiary hospital between January and October 2024, and 299 valid questionnaires were analyzed. Data were collected through face-to-face interviews using a general information questionnaire, the Fear of Complications Questionnaire (FCQ), the Adult Dispositional Hope Scale (ADHS), and the Summary of Diabetes Self-Care Activities (SDSCA). Latent profile analysis was conducted in Mplus 8.3, group comparisons and hierarchical multinomial logistic regression were performed using SPSS 26.0.ResultsThree profiles were identified: low FoC (36.8%), moderate FoC (43.8%), and high FoC (19.4%). Compared with the low FoC profile, higher hope scores, higher self-management scores, and absence of complications, were associated with lower odds of membership in both moderate FoC profile (ORs = 0.867, 0.955, and 0.390 respectively; all P < 0.01) and high FoC profile (ORs = 0.694, 0.811, and 0.194 respectively; all P < 0.05).ConclusionA person-centered analysis identified three profiles characterized by increasing levels of FoC among older inpatients with T2DM. Higher-FoC profiles were associated with greater complication burden, lower hope, and lower self-management capacity, suggesting that FoC assessment may benefit from integrating clinical characteristics with psychological and behavioral factors. External replication and prospective validation are needed before these profiles can be applied to bedside classification or profile-matched interventions.

Introduction

Population aging and the increasing burden of diabetes in China have increased the need for sustained care among older adults.1–3 T2DM accounts for more than 95% of diabetes cases4 and requires long-term management. Persistent hyperglycemia can affect the cardiovascular system, kidneys, eyes, peripheral nerves, and lower limbs, contributing to disability, recurrent hospitalization, financial burden, and premature mortality.2,3 Hospitalization concentrates symptom assessment, treatment adjustment, complication evaluation, and risk communication; concerns about future complications may therefore become particularly salient in this setting.

FoC is a diabetes-specific emotional response to the anticipated onset or consequences of chronic complications.5–7 It overlaps with, but is narrower than, fear of disease progression,8,9 because it centers on outcomes such as nephropathy, retinopathy, cardiovascular disease, diabetic foot disease, blindness, organ failure, and amputation.7,10,11 Some concerns may encourage preventive behavior, whereas more intense fear may coexist with anxiety, avoidance, diabetes distress, uncertainty, reduced treatment confidence, and poorer self-management.6,7,9–13 Accordingly, Its clinical relevance therefore depends on both severity and context.

Direct evidence of FoC in T2DM remains limited. Existing studies suggest that a substantial minority of patients experience clinically important fear of complications.14,15 Evidence on the broader construct of fear of disease progression has linked greater fear with complication burden,10,11,15 glycemic control,10,11,15 previous hypoglycemia,16 illness perceptions,16,17 psychological resources,15 family support,15 and self-management capacity.12,13 Qualitative work also identifies uncertainty, support, and coping as components of patients’ FoC experiences.7 These findings support examining FoC alongside clinical, psychological, and behavioral factors while maintaining the conceptual distinction between the two fear constructs.

Most studies of FoC have relied on total scores and variable-centered analyses. Although the total score provides valuable information regarding overall FoC severity, it may not fully capture potential heterogeneity in item-level responses to specific complication-related concerns. Latent profile analysis (LPA) is a person-centered approach that can identify such subgroups and examine the factors associated with profile membership.18,19 The number of profiles should be selected using information criteria, likelihood-ratio tests, classification quality, profile size, parsimony, and interpretability rather than a single index.20,21 Because profiles are sample-dependent statistical groupings, they should not be interpreted as clinical cutoffs without external validation.

This study had two objectives: to identify latent FoC profiles among older inpatients with T2DM and to examine the demographic, disease-related, psychological, and behavioral factors associated with profile membership. Hope and diabetes self-management were included because they are clinically relevant psychological and behavioral factors associated with fear-related outcomes and diabetes management.12,13,17 Hierarchical multinomial logistic regression was used to examine associations after demographic characteristics, complication status, hope, and self-management were entered into the successive blocks. The study was intended to inform profile-based assessment and future intervention research, not to validate a bedside classification rule or to establish the effects of profile-matched care.

Materials and Methods

Study Design, Setting, and Participants

This cross-sectional study used convenience sampling to recruit older inpatients with T2DM from the endocrinology department of a tertiary Grade A hospital between January and October 2024. Eligible patients were (1) aged ≥60 years; (2) diagnosed with T2DM according to the Chinese Guidelines for the Diagnosis and Treatment of Diabetes in the Elderly (2024 Edition);3 and (3) conscious, able to understand the questionnaire, and willing to participate. Patients were excluded if they had a diagnosed psychiatric disorder, cognitive impairment, or another severe condition that prevented communication or questionnaire completion. Assuming at least 50 participants per latent profile18 and three to five potential profiles, the minimum sample size was 250. The final analytic sample comprised 299 participants (Figure 1). The hospital ethics committee approved the study (LLSC-2024 No. 095), and all participants provided written informed consent. Measures

General Information Questionnaire

A self-designed questionnaire based on a literature review, was used to collect demographic and clinical characteristics, including gender, age, religious affiliation, educational attainment, marital status, place of residence, living arrangement, monthly personal income, disease duration, HbA1c level, presence of complications, and comorbid chronic diseases.

Fear of Complications Questionnaire

The Fear of Complications Questionnaire (FCQ) was developed by Taylor et al22 in 2005 and translated into Chinese version by Dai Yu.23 The 15-item questionnaire assesses four domains. Five items measure general fear, four assess fear of cardiovascular complications, three assess fear of renal complications, and three assess fear of ocular and foot complications. Each item is scored from 0 (“never”) to 3 (“always” or “very much”), yielding a total score of 0–45; higher scores indicate more severe FoC, and a score ≥30 suggests clinically significant FoC. In the current study, Cronbach’s alpha for the FCQ was 0.929.

Adult Dispositional Hope Scale

The Adult Dispositional Hope Scale (ADHS), developed by Snyder et al24 and translated into Chinese version by Ren Jun,25 was used to assess hope level. It consists of 12 items, including four agency items, four pathways items, and four distractor items that are not included in scoring. Participants rate each item on a 4-point Likert scale ranging from 1 to 4. The total score spans from a minimum of 8 to a maximum of 32, with higher scores indicating greater levels of dispositional hope. In the present study, Cronbach’s alpha for the scale was 0.934.

Summary of Diabetes Self-Care Activities

The Summary of Diabetes Self-Care Activities (SDSCA), originally developed by Toobert et al,26 adapted for use in China by Wan Qiaoqin et al.27 This scale was used to assess diabetes self-care behaviors. The 11-item self-report questionnaire measures the frequency of diabetes self-management activities performed during the previous 7 days, including diet, exercise, blood glucose monitoring, foot care, medication adherence, and smoking. Responses are rated on an 8-point Likert scale from 0 to 7, producing a total score between 0 and 77. Higher scores indicate better self-management behavior. Cronbach’s alpha for the SDSCA was 0.848 in this study.

Data Collection

Trained researchers administered the questionnaires through face-to-face interviews using standardized instructions. When a participant could not complete the questionnaire independently, a researcher read each item aloud, provided neutral clarification when needed, and recorded the responses without prompting a specific answer.

The questionnaires were checked immediately after completion. A questionnaire was excluded if more than 10% of the responses were missing, all responses on a page were identical, or an obvious response pattern was present. Two researchers independently entered the data and reconciled discrepancies through logical checks.

Statistical Analysis

LPA was conducted in Mplus 8.3 with the 15 FCQ item scores as the manifest indicators. Models with one to five profiles were estimated. The fit was evaluated using the Akaike information criterion (AIC), Bayesian information criterion (BIC), adjusted BIC (aBIC), entropy, Lo–Mendell–Rubin likelihood-ratio test (LMR-LRT), and bootstrap likelihood-ratio test (BLRT). Lower AIC, BIC, and aBIC values indicate a better relative fit. Entropy values closer to 1 indicate a clearer classification, and values >0.80 are commonly considered acceptable. A significant LMR-LRT or BLRT (P < 0.05) favored the k-profile model over the k−1 model. The final solution was selected by considering fit indices, classification quality, profile size, parsimony, and interpretability. Participants were assigned to the profile with the highest posterior probability. Because modal assignment does not fully account for classification uncertainty, this limitation was considered when interpreting subsequent analyses.

Other analyses were performed using SPSS 26.0. Categorical variables are reported as frequencies and percentages and were compared using the χ2 test. Continuous variables are reported as mean ± standard deviation or median (P25, P75), according to their distribution, and were compared using analysis of variance, the independent-samples t-test, the Mann–Whitney U-test, or the Kruskal–Wallis H-test, as appropriate. Hierarchical multinomial logistic regression was used to examine the factors associated with profile membership, with the low-FoC profile as the reference. Variables were selected from the univariate analyses together with prior evidence and clinical relevance. All tests were two-sided, and statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Latent Profile Analysis

Models with one to five profiles were fitted to the 15 FCQ items. The AIC, BIC, and aBIC decreased as profiles were added. The three-profile model showed high classification accuracy (entropy = 0.933), and both the LMR-LRT and BLRT were significant. Although the four- and five-profile solutions showed lower information criteria, the additional profiles mainly reflected further subdivisions of the existing severity gradient rather than qualitatively distinct item-response patterns. Specifically, the four-profile solution primarily separated individuals with high FoC scores into smaller subgroups without demonstrating a clinically meaningful difference in fear configuration (Supplementary Figure S1). Considering statistical fit, classification accuracy, profile size, parsimony, and interpretability, the three-profile solution was retained. The profiles were labeled according to their observed FCQ patterns. C1 was the low-FoC profile (n = 110, 36.8%), C2 was the moderate-FoC profile (n = 131, 43.8%), and C3 was the high-FoC profile (n = 58, 19.4%). Univariate Comparisons Across Profiles

Age, educational attainment, marital status, place of residence, monthly personal income, and complication status differed across the three profiles in univariate analyses (all P < 0.05; Table 1).

Hierarchical Multinomial Logistic Regression

Hierarchical multinomial logistic regression was conducted with FoC profile membership as the dependent variable and the low FoC profile as the reference category. Variables were entered sequentially: demographic variables significant in the univariate analyses in block 1, complication status in block 2, and total hope and self-management scores in block 3. Hope and self-management scores were entered as continuous variables using their original values. Categorical variables were coded as follows: age, 60–79 years = 1 and ≥80 years = 2; educational attainment, elementary school or below = 1, junior high school = 2, and high school or vocational school or above = 3; place of residence, urban = 1 and rural = 2; marital status, married = 1 and unmarried = 2; monthly personal income, ≤RMB 1000 = 1, RMB 1001–2999 = 2, RMB 3000–5000 = 3, and ≥RMB 5001 = 4; and complication status, no complications = 1 and presence of complications = 2.

The pseudo-R2 indices demonstrated progressive improvement across the three models. The Cox–Snell R2 increased from 0.152 in the demographic model to 0.186 after complications were added and further to 0.560 in the full model. Similarly, the Nagelkerke R2 increased from 0.174 to 0.214 and ultimately to 0.644 after incorporating hope and self-management.

After adjustment for all variables included in the model, urban residence was associated with lower odds of membership in the moderate FoC profile compared with the low FoC profile (OR = 0.442, 95% CI: 0.218–0.897, P = 0.024). Compared with patients with diabetes-related complications, those without complications had lower odds of moderate FoC profile membership (OR = 0.390, 95% CI: 0.203–0.749, P = 0.005). Higher self-management scores (OR = 0.955, 95% CI: 0.931–0.980, P < 0.001) and hope scores (OR = 0.867, 95% CI: 0.806–0.933, P < 0.001) were associated with reduced odds of moderate FoC profile membership. Discussion

Graded Heterogeneity of FoC Identified by Item-Level LPA

This study identified three graded profiles of fear of complications (FoC) among older inpatients with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) using item-level latent profile analysis (LPA). The identified profiles were characterized by progressively increasing levels of FoC rather than qualitatively distinct patterns of concern. Factors associated with profile membership involved clinical experience of diabetes complications, psychological resources, behavioral capacity, and contextual factors, suggesting that FoC should be understood within the broader context of disease burden, available resources, and everyday diabetes management demands. The proportion of participants with clinically significant FoC was higher than that reported in previous studies involving patients with T2DM. Differences in age distribution, clinical setting, complication burden, measurement approaches, and sampling strategies may contribute to variations across studies. Therefore, these findings should be interpreted within the context of older hospitalized adults with established diabetes-related care needs rather than generalized to all individuals with T2DM. Validation in community-based and multicenter samples is needed to determine whether similar FoC profiles exist across different healthcare settings.

FoC represents a complex response to perceived risks associated with future complications. FoC severity alone does not indicate psychological dysfunction or adaptive risk awareness. Some concerns may coexist with preventive behaviors, whereas more intense fear may be accompanied by avoidance, sleep disturbance, reduced treatment confidence, and poorer adherence.6,11–13 Assessment should therefore consider whether fear promotes risk awareness, interferes with self-care, contributes to emotional distress, or reflects inaccurate risk understanding. Previous FoC profile research has commonly relied on aggregated dimension scores. By applying item-level LPA, the present study retained information on specific complication-related concerns. Nevertheless, the resulting profiles were primarily differentiated by overall fear severity rather than qualitatively distinct patterns, suggesting that heterogeneity in this sample was largely graded. Item-level analysis nonetheless adds detail about the specific concerns underlying these differences that may be obscured by total-score approaches.

The item-level analysis showed that general fear, ocular-related concerns received relatively higher ratings. The present findings further indicate that FoC among older adults with T2DM encompasses both general emotional concerns and specific complication-related fears. Dimension-level analysis showed that general fear and ocular/foot-related fear were relatively prominent components of FoC, suggesting that patients’ concerns extended beyond a single complication category and reflected broader worries about future health consequences. In addition, examination of individual FCQ items provided further insight into the specific concerns contributing to FoC, revealing that certain anticipated complications were more prominent among participants. Together, these findings demonstrate that FoC encompasses both overall fear intensity and specific complication-related concerns. Although the present study cannot determine why these complications generated greater concern, blindness, diabetic foot problems, and related functional consequences may be particularly salient among older adults because they may affect mobility, independence, and daily functioning.7 These findings support the value of complication-specific communication during diabetes care.

The three-profile solution was supported by model fit indices, entropy, posterior probabilities, profile size, parsimony, and clinical interpretability, but should be interpreted as a statistical representation of graded FoC heterogeneity rather than as evidence of discrete psychological subtypes.

The application of item-level LPA extended previous profile research that commonly relied on aggregated dimension scores.10,19 Using individual FCQ items retained information regarding specific fear experiences and avoided potential information loss caused by averaging responses across predefined domains. This distinction is important because profile analyses in chronic disease research may identify statistical groupings without necessarily indicating different underlying psychological processes.

In the present study, the value of item-level analysis lies in providing a more detailed view of how specific complication-related concerns are structured across patients.

Nevertheless, greater measurement granularity did not produce qualitatively distinct subgroups in the present sample. These findings emphasize that latent profiles should be interpreted as sample-dependent statistical patterns rather than fixed clinical types.20,21 Low FoC should not automatically be interpreted as successful adaptation, and high FoC should not itself be regarded as evidence of psychological vulnerability.

Hope and Self-Management as Psychological and Behavioral Resources

Higher hope scores were associated with lower odds of belonging to both moderate- and high-FoC profiles. This finding provides a positive psychological perspective to previous FoC research, which has predominantly examined psychological distress, illness perceptions, and quality of life.15,17 Rather than representing the absence of fear, hope may reflect patients’ perceived ability to maintain goal-directed actions while managing chronic illness.

According to Snyder’s theory, hope consists of agency thinking, referring to motivation and confidence in pursuing goals, and pathways thinking, referring to the perceived ability to identify approaches for achieving those goals.24,25 Among older adults with T2DM, these components may be relevant because complication prevention requires sustained engagement in medication use, glucose monitoring, dietary adjustment, physical activity, foot care, and follow-up attendance.

Patients with greater hope may perceive diabetes-related challenges as more manageable and may feel more confident in carrying out recommended self-care activities. However, the direction of this association remains uncertain. Higher hope may contribute to greater perceived control over complication risks, whereas persistent FoC may influence confidence and goal pursuit. Given the cross-sectional design, hope should therefore be interpreted as a clinically relevant correlate of FoC profile membership rather than as an established protective factor against FoC. In the context of aging with chronic illness, hope may be particularly relevant because older adults often need to balance concerns about future complications with the need to maintain meaningful daily activities and self-care routines. Thus, examining hope may help clarify why individuals with similar clinical risks experience different levels of FoC.

In addition to psychological resources, self-management capacity represents an important behavioral dimension underlying differences in FoC experiences, reflecting patients’ ability to translate intentions and treatment recommendations into daily diabetes care. Higher self-management scores were related to lower likelihood of membership in higher-FoC profiles. Self-management represents more than adherence to prescribed treatments; it reflects patients’ ability to integrate medication management, glucose monitoring, lifestyle adjustment, symptom recognition, and follow-up behaviors into everyday life.26,27 Better self-management may be linked to greater predictability and perceived control because patients become more familiar with how to prevent, detect, and respond to potential complications.

Conversely, elevated FoC may interfere with confidence, decision-making, and engagement in self-care behaviors. A reciprocal relationship between self-management and FoC is theoretically plausible; however, the current cross-sectional findings cannot determine whether reduced self-management contributes to greater fear, whether fear interferes with self-management, or whether both processes are influenced by other underlying factors. Importantly, lower self-management scores should not be interpreted simply as poor motivation or inadequate adherence. Among older adults, self-management capacity is shaped not only by individual motivation but also by functional ability, cognitive resources, treatment complexity, and the availability of supportive environments. Therefore, differences in self-management may reflect interactions between patients and their care contexts rather than isolated individual limitations. Difficulties may arise from limited health literacy, functional impairment, emotional distress, treatment complexity, financial burden, or restricted access to continuing care.

Clinical Context: Complications and Perceived Threat

The presence of diabetes-related complications was associated with a greater likelihood of belonging to higher-FoC profiles, consistent with previous studies examining FoC among patients with T2DM.15,19 Patients who have experienced complications may perceive future deterioration as more personally relevant because feared outcomes are no longer abstract possibilities but are connected to previous symptoms, functional limitations, treatment burden, and uncertainty regarding disease progression.

However, complication status alone does not fully capture the complexity of FoC. Patients with similar clinical conditions may differ in their interpretation of risks, coping responses, confidence in disease management, and available support resources. The current study assessed complication presence but did not measure complication type, severity, symptom burden, functional consequences, or perceived controllability. Therefore, the observed association should be interpreted as reflecting the relationship between clinical experience and FoC profile membership rather than a direct causal effect of complications on fear.

Social Context and Access-Related Factors

Urban residence was associated with lower odds of moderate FoC compared with low FoC membership; however, the corresponding association between urban residence and high FoC vs low FoC membership was not statistically significant. Therefore, this finding should be interpreted cautiously and does not indicate a consistent protective effect of urban residence across all higher-FoC profiles.

Residential location may represent broader contextual factors related to healthcare accessibility, health literacy, transportation availability, family support, and continuity of chronic disease management.7,15 These potential pathways were not directly examined in the current study. Future research incorporating healthcare access, social support, and family involvement may provide a more comprehensive understanding of contextual influences on FoC.

Clinical Implications and Future Directions

The findings suggest that FoC assessment among older adults with T2DM should move beyond identifying whether fear is present and consider the broader pattern of psychological and behavioral characteristics associated with different levels of fear. Importantly, the clinical goal is not necessarily to eliminate fear, but to help patients develop accurate risk understanding, confidence in self-care, and practical strategies for managing complication-related concerns. Although the LPA-derived profiles were primarily distinguished by FoC intensity, differences in hope, self-management capacity, and clinical experiences provide useful information for patient-centered assessment.

Profile-informed care should focus on understanding patients’ specific concerns rather than assigning individuals to fixed categories. For patients with moderate FoC, clinical conversations may explore which complications are most concerning and whether these concerns support or interfere with daily management. For patients with high FoC, assessment of emotional distress, avoidance behaviors, sleep disturbance, and interference with self-care may be appropriate when clinically indicated.

The inclusion of psychological resources and behavioral capacity suggests that future supportive strategies may consider both emotional and practical dimensions of diabetes management.

Future longitudinal and intervention studies are needed to determine whether strategies targeting hope, self-management capacity, risk communication, and supportive care improve psychological and diabetes-management outcomes across FoC profiles.

Limitations

The cross-sectional design limits conclusions regarding temporal relationships among FoC, hope, self-management, and clinical characteristics. Longitudinal studies are needed to examine the stability of these profiles and the directionality of these associations.

Participants were older inpatients with T2DM recruited from a single tertiary hospital using convenience sampling, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other healthcare settings, community populations, or younger individuals with T2DM. Therefore, the identified profiles should be considered preliminary and require validation in multicenter, longitudinal, and diverse samples before potential clinical translation.

Most variables were self-reported, which may introduce recall or social desirability bias. Future research combining patient-reported measures with clinical records and objective indicators of self-care may provide additional insights.

Finally, LPA-derived profiles represent sample-dependent statistical patterns rather than fixed clinical categories. Replication in larger samples is required before these profiles can be considered for clinical classification, particularly given the relatively small size of the high FoC profile.

Conclusion

Among older inpatients with T2DM, item-level, person-centered analysis identified three profiles characterized by increasing levels of FoC. Higher hope and self-management scores, together with the absence of complications, were associated with lower odds of membership in both moderate and high FoC profiles. These findings extend previous FoC profile research by demonstrating that FoC heterogeneity involves both differences in overall fear severity and variation in specific complication-related concerns.

By highlighting the relevance of psychological and behavioral factors alongside clinical characteristics in understanding FoC heterogeneity and may provide preliminary evidence for individualized psychological assessment and supportive care planning. However, the identified profiles are descriptive and sample-dependent and should not be interpreted as clinical cutoffs or validated risk categories. Given the single-center convenience sample of older hospitalized patients, multicenter replication and longitudinal validation are required before these profiles can be considered for broader clinical application or profile-guided interventions.
