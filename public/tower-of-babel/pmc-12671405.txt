Diagnosis and Management of Visceral Leishmaniasis in Children: A French Retrospective Study
Authors: Justine de Larminat, Naïma Dahane, Nicolas Argy, Mahmoud Rifai, Olivier Haas Ferrua, Christelle Pomares, Nicolas Rasandisona Ravonjena, Damien Dupont, Jean-Philippe Lemoine, Sarah Dutron, Olivia Pineau, Philippe Picherit Steinbrucker, Thibault César, Séhomi Azohana, Sophie Guilmin-Crepon, Philippe Minodier, Irina Allouche, Mathie Lorrot, Christophe Ravel, Albert Faye
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFew studies have investigated the diagnosis and management of pediatric visceral leishmaniasis (VL) in high-income countries.MethodsAll children hospitalized for VL in university hospitals across mainland France between January 2012 and August 2022 were included in this retrospective study.ResultsSixty-three patients were included with an M/F sex ratio of 1.25 and a median age of 2 years (IQR: 1; 5.4). At least 36 patients had autochthonous VL. The median delay between symptom onset and diagnosis was 20 days (IQR: 11.7; 35.2) [1–193]. The most common symptoms were fever (92%) and splenomegaly (78%). Biological findings included inflammatory syndrome (90%), pancytopenia (79%), and hepatic cytolysis (52%). Twenty-four children (44%) presented hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (HLH). Bone marrow smears, bone marrow real-time PCR (qPCR), and blood qPCR were positive for VL in 64%, 90%, and 96% of the tested patients, respectively. Positivity reached 100% in patients who underwent combined blood and bone marrow PCR. All the patients were treated with liposomal amphotericin B (chemical name, (1R,3S,5R,6R,9R,11R,15S,16R,17R,18S,19E,21E,23E,25E,27E,29E,31E,33R,35S,36R,37S)-33-[(2R,3S,4S,5S,6R)-4-amino-3,5-dihydroxy-6-methyloxan-2-yl]oxy-1,3,5,6,9,11,17,37-octahydroxy-15,16,18-trimethyl-13-oxo-14,39-dioxabicyclo[33.3.1]nonatriaconta-19,21,23,25,27,29,31-heptaene-36-carboxylic acid;2-aminoguanidine). Ten different regimens were used, ranging from 18 to 30 mg/kg cumulative doses given from 2 to 8 days. The median follow-up duration was 2.8 months. Four patients (6.3%) relapsed, one of whom was not retreated. All the children recovered.ConclusionsPediatric VL remains rare in France, with long diagnostic delays and a high frequency of HLH. The combination of blood and bone marrow PCR optimizes the diagnosis. Standardization of therapeutic regimens and follow-up is necessary.Keywords: children, hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis, leishmaniasis PCR, liposomal amphotericin B, visceral leishmaniasis

Leishmaniasis is considered one of the most neglected tropical diseases, despite an incidence of 50 000 new cases per year of visceral leismaniasis (VL), distributed across 4 main global areas: Brazil, East Africa, the Mediterranean region, and the Indian subcontinent [1]. This anthropozoonosis is caused by Leishmania sp., a protozoan parasite transmitted by the bites of female sandflies.

Although the Mediterranean area of mainland France is considered an endemic area for Leishmania infantum, VL remains rare, with 574 cases recorded between 1998 and 2020, affecting mainly immunocompromised individuals. Among the immunocompetent patients, 40% are children under 6 years of age [2].

Recent data on the clinical presentation, diagnosis, treatment and follow-up of pediatric visceral leishmaniasis (VL) are limited in high-income countries. Furthermore, atypical forms can complicate the diagnosis, leading to delays in treatment and unnecessary investigations [3–5].

In this retrospective study, we report the data of pediatric VL patients diagnosed at French university hospitals from 2012 to 2022 to update the data concerning clinical presentation, parasitic diagnosis and therapeutic options.

METHODS

Study Population

In France, reporting of VL is not mandatory, but university parasitology laboratories, where a diagnosis is often made, voluntarily reported the cases to the National Reference Center for Leishmaniasis (NRCL). To retrospectively identify cases during this period (from January 1, 2012 to August 31, 2022), we used the NRCL database as well as the databases of each parasitological laboratory of 22 French university hospitals. Exclusion criteria were complete unavailability of medical records, complete absence of treatment information, coding errors, or misdiagnosis.

Children under 18 years of age with a confirmed diagnosis of VL during this period were included in this retrospective study. The diagnosis was determined if direct microscopic examination of the bone marrow smear or Leishmania sp. real-time PCR (qPCR) in blood or bone marrow was positive. The qPCR used across France was previously described by Mary et al [6]. We also recorded serology results, performed by ELISA and confirmed by Western Blot. Species identification, if available, was performed using culture-based methods, as previously described [7], or qPCR, with DNA sequencing provided in the NCRL.

The data collected included demographic characteristics such as age and sex, medical history, previous stays in endemic areas, residence area in France, clinical symptoms, delay between symptom onset, hospitalization and diagnosis, biological parameters, parasitological tests used for diagnosis, treatments with doses and durations, follow-up modalities and relapse characteristics. Prolonged fever was defined as any fever lasting 15 days or more [8]. Hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (HLH) was defined according to the HLH 2024 criteria [9].

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive results are expressed as percentages for qualitative variables and were compared using the χ2 test and Fisher's test. Quantitative variables are described as medians and were compared using the Wilcoxon test. A P value < .05 was considered to indicate statistical significance. Analysis was performed using R software version 4.3.1 and R Studio Desktop version 202.09.1+494.

Ethic

As recommended in the ethical standards of the French Ethics Committee on Human Experimentation and in accordance with the Helsinki Declaration of 1975, as revised in 2008, in agreement with French Health Public Law (CSP Art L1121-1.1 and Jardé law, n°2012-300 du 5 mars 2012, category 3) for noninterventional retrospective study, no written or verbal informed consent to participate in the study from the patient was necessary. A declaration was made and approved by the Commission Informatique et Libertés (declaration number 2228569).

RESULTS

Study Population

During this period, 73 children younger than 18 years old were diagnosed at one of the 22 French university hospitals. In total, 63 children (86.3%) were included (Figure 1).

Study's flowchart.

The median number of cases per year was 5.5 (IQR: 4.5; 6.5, [2–7]).

The male/female ratio was 1.3 (35 boys and 28 girls). The median age was 2 years (IQR: 1; 5.4; [0–17.5]. Boys were significantly older than girls, with median ages of 4 years (IQR: 1.3; 9.7); [0.4–17.6], and 2.8 (IQR: 1.1; 6.5); [0–6.2], respectively (P < .0001).

Geographic distribution of patients, according to their city of management.

All resided in or had traveled to endemic areas. Thirty-six patients (57.1%) were diagnosed with autochthonous VL, 29 lived in southern France, and the others had traveled to this zone. Twenty-one patients (33.3%) had imported VL as they had traveled abroad, in Spain (n = 9), Morocco (n = 7), Senegal or Cameroon (n = 2 each), Portugal, Greece, Turkey, Albania, Niger, or Nigeria (n = 1 each). The remaining 6 patients (9.5%) lived or had traveled either in southern France or in other outside endemic areas (Spain, Ivory Coast, Italy, or Greece). When available the median time during travel was 60 days (IQR: 22.5 days; 62.3 days) [14 days; 390 days].

Clinical Presentation

The most common symptom was fever (92.1%), which lasted more than 15 days (prolonged fever) in 34.5% of the patients. Thirty-nine patients (61.9%) had deterioration of general condition (DGC). Forty-seven patients (74.6%) had splenomegaly, and 29 patients (46.0%) had hepatomegaly, which was always associated with splenomegaly. No skin darkening was observed. The other symptoms varied and are shown in Figure 3.

Proportion and distribution of patient symptoms.

No patient was considered immunosuppressed. However, 1 patient was a newborn whose VL was acquired from his mother with AIDS and chronic L. infantum VL with multiple relapses [10]. The neonate was HIV-negative. Two patients were also diagnosed with concomitant autoimmune diseases: autoimmune hepatitis and juvenile idiopathic arthritis. Two other children, both migrants, were coinfected, 1 with hepatitis B virus and 1 with Mycobacterium tuberculosis (osteoarticular form) and malaria.

The median delays between symptom onset, hospitalization, and diagnosis were heterogeneous (Figure 4). The median delay between the first day of hospitalization and diagnosis was 3.5 days (IQR: 1 day; 7 days) but it could be as long as 162 days. Among the 13 patients without splenomegaly, 10 had a diagnosis delay since symptom onset, ranging from 7 to 188 days, with a median of 29 days.

Distribution of various delays between symptom onset, the first day of hospitalization, and diagnosis according to the number of patients.

Baseline Biological Data

Sixty patients had anemia (95.2%), 41 (68.3%) of whom were nonregenerative (reticulocytes < 150 G/L). Fifty-seven patients had thrombocytopenia (90.5%), 49 had leukopenia (77.8%), and 46 had neutropenia (73.0%). Twenty-one patients had lymphopenia (33.3%). A total of 50 patients (79.4%) had pancytopenia and 6 patients (9.5%) had bicytopenia. Fifty-seven patients (90.5%) had a biological inflammatory syndrome (C-reactive protein concentration > 5 mg/L). Thirty-two patients (49.2%) had hepatic cytolysis, and 7 (21.9%) had associated cholestasis (Table 1).

Parasitologic Diagnosis

Bone marrow smears were positive in only 37/58 children (64%). Four children whose bone marrow smears were positive did not have both blood and bone marrow qPCR performed. Bone marrow qPCR was positive in 38/42 of the children (90%). Sixteen patients with negative bone marrow smears had positive bone marrow qPCR results (42%). Fifteen patients with negative bone marrow smears had positive blood qPCR results (33%). Four patients with positive blood qPCR results had negative bone marrow qPCR results. Overall, bone marrow smears, bone marrow qPCR, and blood qPCR were positive in 64%, 90%, and 96% of the patients tested, respectively. Thirty patients were tested by both bone marrow and blood qPCR; all of them (100%) were diagnosed by one of the tests. Serology (IgM) results were positive in 42 out of 44 patients (95.5%). Blood parasite load was assessed in 19 patients. The median parasite count was 150/mL, with a wide range (IQR: 30; 1350) [3; 3.650.000]. Species identification was performed in 19 patients (30.2%), and L. infantum was identified in all the patients (Table 3).

Therapeutic Management

All patients were treated with liposomal amphotericin B (chemical name, (1R,3S,5R,6R,9R,11R,15S,16R,17R,18S,19E,21E,23E,25E,27E,29E,31E,33R,35S,36R,37S)-33-[(2R,3S,4S,5S,6R)-4-amino-3,5-dihydroxy-6-methyloxan-2-yl]oxy-1,3,5,6,9,11,17,37-octahydroxy-15,16,18-trimethyl-13-oxo-14,39-dioxabicyclo[33.3.1]nonatriaconta-19,21,23,25,27,29,31-heptaene-36-carboxylic acid;2-aminoguanidine). However, 10 different regimens of liposomal amphotericin B were used, with cumulative total doses ranging from 18 to 30 mg/kg and treatment durations ranging from 2 to 8 days (Table 4 and Figure 5).

No adverse effects of amphotericin B treatment were reported.

Ten patients (15.9%), of whom 8 (80.0%) had HLH, also received corticosteroids, with doses ranging from 1 to 2 mg/kg/day. Corticosteroid therapy was sometimes prolonged (>1 month) and/or combined with another immunosuppressive treatment in 7 children because of a long delay in diagnosis, severe and/or chronic HLH, or an associated autoimmune disease.

Patient Follow-up and Relapses

The median follow-up duration was 2.8 months with a wide range (IQR: 1.8 months; 7.1 months) [0.3; 24.7]. The majority of patients underwent clinical and biological follow-up and experienced rapid clinical improvement within days of treatment with liposomal amphotericin B, except for the 4 children with other comorbidities. There were no deaths. Overall, 33/54 patients underwent follow-up with specific blood Leishmania qPCR to determine the clearance of the infection, which was performed at least 1 month after the end of the treatment, with a median of 1 qPCR per patient.

Four patients (6.3%) relapsed. Three patients were symptomatic and one only has biological abnormalities (hepatocellular cytolysis). None of these 4 patients had any other associated disease. The median time after treatment before relapse was 5 months (IQR: 2.5–6) [2–6]. The median age of patients who relapsed was 3 years (IQR: 1.3–6.6) [0.8 years–9 years], which was older than the median age of the entire cohort (2 years). One patient received the same cumulative dose of liposomal amphotericin B as in the first treatment (20 mg/kg), while the other 2 received a higher cumulative total dose (28 and 40 mg/kg, respectively). The fourth patient, a 4-year-old girl, experienced 2 asymptomatic relapses at 2 months and 6 months post-treatment, with low-positive blood Leishmania detection by qPCR and mild hepatocellular cytolysis. She recovered spontaneously without any new treatment.

DISCUSSION

Despite the potential severity of VL, only a few recent European pediatric studies exist [11–13]. Compared with most other studies, our cohort was nationwide, allowing us to provide an overview of the diagnosis and treatment of VL in children at the national level. Moreover, a diagnostic approach involving blood and/or bone marrow Leishmania qPCR was widely performed in our study, with very good sensitivity. Finally, we described the largest cohort of children treated with a 2-day regimen of liposomal amphotericin B.

We observed a long median delay in diagnosis (20 days), which was related to both the rarity of cases leading to diagnostic delay and misdiagnosis among doctors as well as the scarcity of first signs in the context of fever without a source [14].

More than half of the patients had autochthonous VL, and one-third had imported VL. This finding is in agreement with previous European and French epidemiologic studies in which the proportion of autochthonous VL is increasing and represents 70% of cases [2, 15].

Interestingly, more than half of the patients were younger than 2 years. This finding is consistent with studies suggesting that immunological immaturity enhances parasite dissemination [3]. However, despite a balanced sex distribution, we cannot explain why boys were significantly older than girls were at diagnosis.

Atypical clinical presentations were observed. One in five patients had no splenomegaly, indicating that VL should be considered even in its absence. The short duration of symptoms prior to diagnosis may partly account for the absence of splenomegaly in some patients. However, the median delay in diagnosis since symptom onset was similar to the overall population delay in diagnosis. Two patients had associated autoimmune disease. Symptoms of VL may mimic autoimmune manifestations. Arthralgia and vascular skin lesions have been described previously [5]. Biological abnormalities such as decreased complement levels, increased rheumatoid factor levels, and the presence of ANCAs and cryoglobulins have been reported [16–18]. These abnormalities may be due to polyclonal B lymphocyte activation with IgG secretion of autoantibodies [3].

We observed a special case of congenital VL in a neonate whose mother had AIDS. This vertical transmission is very rare [10, 19]. Maternal persistence of infection appears to be a risk factor, with chronic bone marrow involvement and the development of CD4 T-cell lymphopenia, even in the absence of immunosuppression [20].

The majority of patients had pancytopenia. The occurrence of inflammatory syndrome was almost constant. In the pathophysiology of the disease, the parasite may migrate to the liver, which may explain these findings.

Interestingly, 44% of the patients had HLH and those aged 2 years or younger had a higher risk. These finding may explain the risk of diagnosis confusion between genetic lymphohistiocytosis, which also occurs in this age group, and VL. HLH may be related to immunological immaturity. In its promastigote form, the parasite binds to the complement receptor CR3 and is phagocytized by macrophages. After macrophage lysis, the parasite might not be destroyed and may again be phagocytosed. This results in chronic macrophage activation with continuous secretion of proinflammatory cytokines [3]. Therefore, screening for leishmaniasis infection in patients who have traveled to endemic areas is critical to avoid initiating treatment with corticosteroids and/or immunosuppressants for genetic HLH, as we have observed in some patients and in the literature [21]. This could exacerbate VL in the absence of specific antiparasitic treatment. It is also important to screen for HLH in patients with VL because of its potential severity.

There are no official guidelines for diagnostic testing in France. Bone marrow smears have a low sensitivity despite their high specificity because identification is laborious and requires a high level of expertise [14, 22]. Our study confirms that Leishmania qPCR in bone marrow is a more sensitive test [22], with a positivity rate of 90%. Almost half of the patients with a positive bone marrow qPCR result had a negative smear. Similarly, Leishmania blood qPCR seems to be a very sensitive test for diagnosis, with a positivity rate of 96%. Four patients had a positive blood qPCR result but a negative bone marrow qPCR result. Thus, the combination of blood and bone marrow Leishmania qPCR results seems to be particularly accurate for diagnosis. A sequential approach, first via blood qPCR and, if negative, a bone marrow smear for direct diagnosis and qPCR would be easier to perform. However, bone marrow smears are essential for the differential diagnosis and culture of Leishmania.

An indirect parasitological diagnosis can also be made by specific Leishmania serology (IgM and IgG). This test has excellent sensitivity but variable specificity [22]. It may be useful when access to Leishmania qPCR is limited.

Since qPCR is currently widespread across mainland France, as evidenced by some patients in this study and as described in previous studies [6, 23–25], it could be beneficial to perform systematically qPCR on pediatric patients during follow-up to early detect VL relapses in the future.

All patients received liposomal amphotericin B treatment, which is the first-line therapy in high-income countries [26, 27]. We observed a wide variety of therapeutic regimens with good tolerance. The WHO and the Infectious Diseases Society of America (IDSA) recommended a regimen (3 mg on Days 1 to 5 and on Days 14 and 21, with a cumulative dose of 21 mg/kg) which is prescribed to more than half of the patients. However, a 2-day course at a dosage of 10 mg/kg/day was also prescribed, as its efficacy rate was 97% at 6 months, and the tolerance was as good as that of the other regimens [22, 27]. A study even tested the efficacy of a regimen of 7.5 mg/kg/day for 2 days, achieving 100% efficacy without relapse at 6 months [28]. These 2-day regimens were reported to result in faster resolution of symptoms and improvement in biological parameters [29]. Finally, we confirmed that the 2 days of liposomal amphotericin B treatment has a good tolerability, and is associated with a rapid recovery and a shorter hospitalization. However, this finding, these findings should be confirmed in larger studies.

Few patients received corticosteroids and/or immunosuppressants prior to, or in conjunction, with VL treatment. The primary objective was to control HLH. The sample size of our study did not allow us to draw conclusions about the effect of adding these therapies. In the context of severe HLH, the addition of corticosteroids did not seem to worsen the infection, but it should not be systematically prescribed, especially when HLH is moderate.

Only half of the patients underwent long-term follow-up. In addition, the median time post-treatment to relapse was 5 months, almost double the overall median follow-up time of the patients observed in our study. Accordingly, since relapses may occur 6 to 12 months after treatment [30], it seems to be appropriate to emphasize the need for clinical but also parasitological follow-up after treatment as suggested by some studies assessing the value of qPCR [23–25].

Overall, 6.3% of the patients relapsed. These findings are in line with those of previous studies, with values ranging from 4.5% to 6.8% [31, 32]. However, subclinical relapses that healed spontaneously cannot be excluded. Owing to the small sample size, we could not statistically investigate risk factors for relapse, but the cumulative doses were similar in cured and relapsing patients. We cannot rule out amphotericin B resistance or poor sensitivity, which has been reported in India in patients with Leishmania donovani VL. Among the 4 relapses, 1 patient with a 2-day treatment regimen was from West Africa. It cannot be ruled out that this failure was related to the Leishmania species acquired in Africa, which might necessitate higher doses of liposomal amphotericin B, as previously described [27, 33, 34]. In people living in highly endemic areas, mainly adults [31, 32], the risk factors included HIV coinfection, the presence of lower limb edema, the secondary onset of pneumonia, worsening thrombocytopenia, and an age of <12 years. The IDSA recommends that after initial treatment with liposomal amphotericin B, relapses should again be treated with the same drug but with an increased cumulative total dose [27]. Among the 4 patients who relapsed, only one did not receive retreatment because the biological and parasitological abnormalities resolved spontaneously. These findings are particularly intriguing and suggest that host immunity plays an important role in controlling disease, at least in the short and medium term.

Our study has several limitations. First, it is a retrospective study, with some missing data. Second, the low number of cases with different types of management limits the evaluation of factors associated with HLH and relapses. Finally, differences in parasitological diagnosis and management preclude a detailed analysis of the infecting Leishmania species and the evolution of parasite load, especially since they are not systematically performed in France. In conclusion, pediatric VL remains rare in France. Clinical and biological features are similar to those observed in previous studies. The combination of blood and bone marrow qPCR seems to be particularly sensitive for diagnosis. The modalities of liposomal amphotericin B treatment should be standardized, considering that a 2-day schedule could be more practical and cost-saving, except for children who acquired LV in Africa, for whom a higher cumulative dose of liposomal amphotericin B might be needed. VL relapses are rare. Nevertheless, clinical and parasitological follow-up should be optimized. Finally, large-scale pediatric studies investigating the role of immunity in parasite control would be particularly valuable for understanding the pathophysiology of relapses and optimizing their management.
