Artificial intelligence for white matter hyperintensity segmentation: performance, determinants, and clinical implications
Authors: Jinhua Hu, Zhihao Zhang, Peng Lei, Jupeng Zhang, Xiqi Zhu, Baosheng Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveWhite matter hyperintensities (WMH) are key neuroimaging markers of cerebral small vessel disease, associated with cognitive decline and stroke risk. Accurate quantification is essential yet manual segmentation is time-consuming and variable. We systematically evaluated AI performance for automated WMH segmentation across disease contexts and identified factors influencing model performance.MethodsFollowing PRISMA guidelines, we searched PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, Scopus, and IEEE Xplore from database inception to February 3, 2026 (PROSPERO: CRD420261355769). Studies employing AI-based WMH segmentation and reporting Dice similarity coefficient (DSC) were included. A random-effects meta-analysis was conducted, along with subgroup analyses, meta-regression, sensitivity analyses, and publication bias assessment.Results26 studies comprising 4,288 participants contributed to quantitative syntheses: 13 evaluated WMH segmentation in general cohorts, and 13 evaluated AD or CSVD-specific cohorts. In the general cohort meta-analysis, the pooled DSC was 0.78 (95% CI, 0.74–0.82). Disease-specific pooled DSC estimates were 0.75 for AD and 0.78 for CSVD. Exploratory meta-regression suggested an association between scanning parameters and DSC (p = 0.042), whereas magnetic field strength showed a borderline association (p = 0.052).ConclusionAI demonstrates promising performance for automated WMH segmentation, with U-Net–based architectures showing superior accuracy. However, substantial heterogeneity highlights the need for standardized imaging protocols and robust external validation. Future multicenter studies are essential to improve model generalizability and facilitate clinical translation.

Introduction

White matter hyperintensities (WMH) represent the most common neuroimaging manifestation of cerebral small vessel disease (CSVD) and are highly prevalent in the aging population as well as in patients with neurodegenerative disorders (Cheng et al., 2025; Qiu et al., 2025). On T2-weighted and T2-FLAIR magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), WMHs typically appear as hyperintense lesions in the periventricular and deep white matter (Duering et al., 2023; Wardlaw et al., 2013). Accurate quantification of WMH burden is essential for understanding their clinical significance and monitoring disease progression. Previous studies have demonstrated that WMHs are associated with cognitive decline, gait disturbances, dementia, and an increased risk of recurrent stroke (Jansma et al., 2024; Iveson et al., 2026; Johnsen et al., 2025; Whitman et al., 2001). As global population aging accelerates, precise and automated WMH quantification has become increasingly important for both neuroimaging research and clinical practice.

Manual delineation performed by expert raters remains the reference standard for WMH quantification. However, this approach is time-consuming, labor-intensive, and subject to substantial intra- and inter-observer variability, limiting its applicability in large-scale studies. Early semi-automated techniques, such as threshold-based or region-growing methods, have improved efficiency to some extent (Ramirez et al., 2011). Nevertheless, their robustness and cross-scanner generalizability remain limited due to the indistinct boundaries and heterogeneous characteristics of WMHs.

In recent years, artificial intelligence (AI), particularly deep learning approaches based on convolutional neural networks (CNNs), has demonstrated considerable potential in medical image segmentation (Ilesanmi et al., 2024). Among these, U-Net and its variants have become the dominant architectures owing to their encoder–decoder design and skip connections, which facilitate multi-scale feature fusion and improve segmentation accuracy (Zhang et al., 2020). More recently, advanced frameworks such as nnU-Net and Transformer-based models have further improved segmentation performance (Qin et al., 2026). However, their generalizability and clinical applicability remain to be fully validated.

Despite the rapid growth of AI-based WMH segmentation studies, several important issues remain unresolved. First, systematic comparisons across different algorithms are limited, as most studies focus on optimizing individual models. Second, WMH characteristics vary across disease contexts, such as Alzheimer’s disease (AD) and CSVD, and the stability of AI models under different pathological conditions remains unclear. Third, reported Dice similarity coefficient (DSC) values vary widely (0.52–0.92), suggesting that segmentation performance may be influenced by multiple factors, including dataset heterogeneity, imaging parameters, and algorithm architecture (Kervadec et al., 2021). Finally, a comprehensive meta-analytic evaluation of key moderating variables—such as field strength, external validation, and multicenter data—remains lacking.

Therefore, this systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to address these gaps through four objectives: (1) to compare the performance of different AI algorithms for WMH segmentation; (2) to evaluate the influence of disease context on segmentation accuracy; (3) to estimate the pooled performance of AI-based WMH segmentation using DSC; and (4) to explore potential moderators, including imaging parameters and validation strategies, using meta-regression analysis. Ultimately, this study aims to provide evidence-based guidance for optimizing automated WMH segmentation models and facilitating their clinical translation.

Materials and methods

Search strategy and registration

This systematic review and meta-analysis was designed and reported in strict accordance with the PRISMA (the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses) guidelines (McInnes et al., 2018). The study protocol was registered with the International Prospective Registry of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) prior to data extraction (registration number CRD420261355769).

A systematic search was conducted across five electronic databases: PubMed, IEEE Xplore, Scopus, Web of Science, and Embase. The search covered the period from database inception to February 3, 2026. The search strategy was built around three core concepts—namely, “white matter hyperintensity,” “artificial intelligence,” and “segmentation”—and employed a combination of Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms and free-text keywords. The detailed search strategies adopted for each database are provided in the Supplementary materials. Two researchers (J.H. and Z.Z.) independently reviewed the search results and cross-checked their findings to ensure that retrieval was complete.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

This study established predefined inclusion criteria based on the PICOS principle: study participants were adult subjects undergoing brain MRI examinations, including healthy controls and individuals with AD, or CSVD; the intervention or testing method involved the use of AI technology (including but not limited to deep learning and machine learning) for the segmentation of white matter hyperintensities; The reference standard included manual segmentation by experts or validated semi-automated methods; the outcome measure is the DSC; and the study type is original research, including prospective, retrospective, and cross-sectional studies.

Exclusion criteria include: non-original studies (e.g., reviews, conference abstracts, commentaries, editorials, case reports, and guidelines); non-human studies; studies involving participants under 18 years of age; studies not using AI-based segmentation methods; studies that did not report or could not calculate the DSC; non-English language literature; and duplicate publications (only the study with the most complete data or the most recent publication date will be included).

Study selection and data extraction

After importing retrieved records into EndNote and removing duplicates, two researchers (J.H. and Z.Z.) independently performed a two-stage screening process: an initial assessment based on titles and abstracts, followed by a full-text review of potentially eligible articles. Disagreements regarding eligibility were resolved by consensus or, when necessary, by consultation with a third researcher (X.Z.). The screening process is summarized in the PRISMA flowchart.

The same two researchers independently extracted data using a standardized form that captured: study characteristics (first author, publication year, country/region, study design, sample size, participant age, and sex); MRI parameters (scanner manufacturer, magnetic field strength, and sequence type); AI model characteristics (algorithm type, use of multicenter data, use of external validation, and development environment); and performance metrics (i.e., mean and standard deviation of the DSC). Extracted data were subsequently cross-checked for accuracy, and corresponding authors were contacted to request additional information where necessary. In addition to study and model characteristics, we extracted information on reference-label construction, including the annotation method, number and expertise of raters where reported, use of consensus or adjudication procedures, semi-automated processing followed by manual editing, and the availability of inter-rater reliability data. Reference-label construction was classified as manual annotation (single-rater or multi-rater consensus/adjudication), semi-automated segmentation followed by manual editing, or insufficiently reported. No included study was identified as using an automated algorithm alone as the reference standard. Detailed study-level information is presented in Supplementary Table S2.

Quality assessment

Two researchers (J.H. and Z.Z.) independently assessed study quality using the QUADAS-2 tool for diagnostic accuracy studies. The QUADAS-2 framework comprises—patient selection, index test, reference standard, and flow and timing—each of which is evaluated for risk of bias as well as concerns regarding applicability. Each study received a classification of low, high, or unclear risk; a summary of the assessment is provided in Supplementary Table S1. Any disagreements were resolved by consensus or by consultation with a third researcher (X.Z.).

Statistical analysis

This study used the DSC as the primary outcome measure and employed a random-effects model using the DerSimonian–Laird method for meta-analysis to account for the expected heterogeneity among studies. For each study, the 95% confidence interval (CI) of the pooled DSC was calculated from the reported mean and standard deviation, while heterogeneity across studies was assessed with the I2 statistic (where I2 > 50% suggests substantial heterogeneity and I2 > 75% indicates considerable heterogeneity).

To explore the sources of heterogeneity, a subgroup analysis was conducted. Studies were grouped by algorithm type into three categories: those employing U-Net architectures and their variants, those utilizing other deep learning approaches, and those relying on traditional convolutional neural networks. Pooled DSC estimates were then calculated for each subgroup. In parallel, we conducted univariate meta-regression to examine the association between potential covariates and reported DSC values. Covariates considered included algorithm type, scanning parameters, use of external validation, field strength, availability of multicenter data, and country. Because reference-label categories were sparse and reporting was incomplete, a reliable confirmatory subgroup analysis or meta-regression by reference-standard type was not feasible. We therefore conducted only an exploratory univariable analysis of reference-standard type, summarized reference-label construction descriptively, and interpreted any association cautiously.

A leave-one-out sensitivity analysis was undertaken to evaluate the stability of the pooled estimates. Publication bias was assessed using the Begg rank correlation test, the Egger linear regression test, and visual inspection of funnel plots. A p-value < 0.10 on Egger’s test or <0.05 on Begg’ test was taken to suggest potential publication bias. All statistical analyses were conducted using Stata (version 17.0 or later), and a two-tailed p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Study selection

The database searches identified 2,594 records. After the removal of 647 duplicates, 1,947 records underwent title and abstract screening, of which 1,778 were excluded. The full texts of 169 reports were sought for retrieval, and 11 reports could not be obtained. Consequently, 158 full-text reports were assessed for eligibility. Of these, 132 were excluded, including 17 animal studies, 73 reviews, letters, guidelines, editorials, or conference abstracts, 14 studies not specifically addressing WMH segmentation, and 28 studies involving infants or children. Ultimately, 26 studies were included in the systematic review and meta-analysis (Figure 1; Zhao et al., 2021, 2025; Zhang et al., 2018, 2021; Wu et al., 2019, 2026; Viteri et al., 2022; Tsuchida et al., 2024; Sundaresan et al., 2022; Rieu et al., 2021; Rachmadi et al., 2018; Mojiri Forooshani et al., 2022; Lo et al., 2024; Hotz et al., 2022; Shan et al., 2021; Li et al., 2022; Jeong et al., 2025; Huang et al., 2023; He et al., 2025; Fiford et al., 2020; Eldianto et al., 2023; Vanderbecq et al., 2020; Røvang et al., 2023; Pham et al., 2021; Park et al., 2023; Liang et al., 2021).

Basic characteristics of included studies

The 26 included studies were published between 2017 and 2026 and originated from several countries, predominantly China (n = 10), followed by the United States, South Korea, France, and Canada. These studies enrolled a total of 4,288 participants, including healthy controls, individuals with WMH, and patients diagnosed with AD or CSVD. The MRI protocols primarily included T1 and T2-FLAIR sequences, and the field strengths were predominantly 1.5 T or 3.0 T. The U-Net architecture and its derivatives were the most commonly employed AI segmentation frameworks, while a subset of studies reported the use of multicenter data and/or performance of external validation. Quality assessment results

Quality of the included studies was assessed with the QUADAS-2 tool; a summary of the findings is presented in Supplementary Table S1. The assessment encompassed the four QUADAS-2 domains: patient selection, index test, reference standard, and flow and timing. For the majority of studies, risk of bias was deemed low across all domains, and concerns regarding applicability were generally minimal. These findings suggest that the overall methodological quality of the evidence base was high.

Meta-analysis results

Overall segmentation performance

A total of 13 studies contributed data on the DSC for WMH segmentation. The random-effects meta-analysis yielded a pooled DSC estimate of 0.78 (95% confidence interval [CI], 0.74–0.82), accompanied by substantial heterogeneity across studies (I2 = 98.7%, p < 0.001; Figure 2). Individual study DSC estimates ranged from 0.52 to 0.92. Among the studies that included AD patients, eight contributed data, producing a pooled DSC of 0.75 (95% CI, 0.68–0.83) and exhibiting an I2 of 99.4%. Similarly, five studies provided data on CSVD patients, for whom the pooled DSC was 0.78 (95% CI, 0.72–0.85) with an I2 value of 99.1% (Figure 3).

Algorithm subgroup analysis

A subgroup analysis was performed according to algorithm type (Figure 4), which classified studies into three groups: those employing U-Net architectures and their variants, those using other deep learning approaches, and those relying on traditional CNNs. The pooled DSC estimate for studies employing U-Net architectures and their variants was 0.80 (95% CI, 0.76–0.84), compared with 0.76 (95% CI, 0.70–0.82) for those utilizing other deep learning methods and 0.74 (95% CI, 0.67–0.81) for those applying traditional CNNs. Substantial heterogeneity was evident within each subgroup.

Meta-regression analysis

Reference-label construction varied across the included studies. Thirteen studies used manual annotation, including single-rater annotation or multi-rater consensus/adjudication; four used semi-automated segmentation followed by manual editing; and nine did not provide sufficient information to determine the construction procedure. No study was identified as using an automated algorithm alone as the reference standard. These data are summarized in Supplementary Table S2.

The findings from the univariate meta-regression analysis are presented in Table 3. In exploratory univariable meta-regression, scanning parameters were associated with reported DSC values (p = 0.042), whereas magnetic field strength showed a borderline association (p = 0.052). Given the limited number of studies and incomplete reporting of some covariates, these findings should be interpreted cautiously and considered hypothesis-generating rather than definitive. In contrast, no significant associations were detected for algorithm type, external validation, multicenter data, or country (all p > 0.05). In an exploratory univariable analysis, reference-standard type classified as manual annotation (single-rater or multi-rater consensus/adjudication), semi-automated segmentation followed by manual editing, or insufficiently reported was not significantly associated with reported DSC values (p = 0.245). Given the sparse categories and incomplete reporting, this result should not be interpreted as evidence that reference-standard variability does not contribute to DSC variation or heterogeneity.

Results of the leave-one-out sensitivity analysis are depicted in Supplementary Figure S1. Exclusion of any single study did not materially alter the pooled DSC, indicating that the findings were robust.

Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis provide a comprehensive evaluation of AI algorithms for WMH segmentation across different disease contexts. The results indicate that AI achieves moderate-to-high segmentation performance, with a pooled DSC of 0.78. However, substantial heterogeneity was observed across studies, suggesting considerable variability in model performance. Exploratory univariable meta-regression suggested an association between scanning parameters and reported DSC values, while the association with magnetic field strength was borderline. These findings are hypothesis-generating and do not establish independent or causal effects. These exploratory findings suggest that differences in imaging protocols may contribute to variability in reported DSC values, but their independent effects remain uncertain.

The associations observed between DSC and scanning parameters, as well as the borderline association with magnetic field strength, should be interpreted as exploratory. The number of studies available for meta-regression was limited, and covariate information was incompletely reported for some studies. In addition, imaging characteristics, participant populations, lesion burden, reference-standard procedures, and model-development practices are correlated across studies. Therefore, the present analyses cannot establish an independent or causal effect of scanning parameters or field strength on segmentation performance.

To provide context rather than a benchmark of equivalence, Heinen et al. (2019) reported an inter-rater DSC of 0.80 ± 0.09 for a subset of reference segmentations generated using a semi-automated iso-contouring procedure. Although this value is numerically close to the pooled AI DSC (0.78), the different populations and annotation workflows preclude a claim of human-equivalent performance. Future work should therefore focus on generalisability, robustness, and clinical utility.

U-Net architectures and their variants had the highest descriptive pooled DSC. Because the studies used different datasets, reference standards, and validation designs, this pattern should not be interpreted as evidence of algorithmic superiority (Kuijf et al., 2019). Emerging approaches, including transformer-based models, foundation models, domain adaptation, self-supervised learning, and federated learning, require standardized multicentre external validation for WMH segmentation.

Disease-specific analyses yielded a numerically higher pooled DSC in CSVD-specific studies (0.78) than in AD-specific studies (0.75). This difference should not be interpreted as evidence of superior algorithmic performance in CSVD. DSC is sensitive to lesion burden and morphology: when WMH lesions are smaller, sparser, or more diffuse, a similar absolute number of discordant voxels can result in a larger proportional reduction in DSC. Differences in lesion burden, lesion distribution, MRI protocols, reference-label construction, dataset composition, and validation strategies may therefore have contributed to the observed variation between AD and CSVD subgroups. Because lesion-volume and lesion-morphology data were incompletely reported, these factors could not be assessed quantitatively. Accordingly, the disease-specific pooled estimates should be interpreted as descriptive summaries rather than evidence of disease-specific differences in algorithmic accuracy.

Interpretation of DSC requires consideration of the study-specific reference standards. Across the included studies, reference masks were based on manual delineation (single-rater or consensus), semi-automated segmentation with manual editing, or incompletely reported procedures. We retained DSC because it was the most consistently reported overlap metric; however, the pooled value reflects agreement with heterogeneous study-specific reference masks rather than objective biological ground truth. More broadly, the choice and interpretation of segmentation metrics should be aligned with the scientific or clinical task, because overlap-based metrics alone do not capture all clinically relevant error modes (Reinke et al., 2024; Maier-Hein et al., 2024).

The exploratory reference-standard analysis was limited by sparse categories and incomplete reporting; a non-significant association cannot exclude an effect on DSC or heterogeneity. Future studies should report the number and expertise of annotators, consensus procedures, manual editing, and inter-rater agreement.

AI-based WMH segmentation may support large-scale imaging research and longitudinal quantification, but the current evidence does not establish improved risk stratification or clinical decision-making. Prospective multicentre validation, standardized protocols, and evaluation of clinical impact are needed before routine implementation.

This review used a systematic search and a unified meta-analytic framework, but the exploratory meta-regression and publication-bias assessment should be interpreted cautiously given the small number of studies and substantial heterogeneity.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. The extreme heterogeneity, small subgroup sizes, and incomplete reporting of MRI protocols, reference standards, lesion burden, and external validation limit the interpretation of the pooled DSC. DSC captures overlap with a study-specific reference mask but not boundary error, lesion-wise detection, or volumetric agreement; complementary metrics were too inconsistently reported for quantitative synthesis. In addition, QUADAS-2 does not fully address several AI-specific risks, including data leakage, model-selection bias, limited reproducibility, and dataset shift. Accordingly, the findings should be interpreted cautiously. Future studies should follow AI-specific reporting guidance, such as CLAIM (Mongan et al., 2020), and use validation designs that minimize optimism from model selection and quantify performance in independent external datasets (Bradshaw et al., 2023).

Conclusion

In conclusion, AI-based WMH segmentation shows promise for research applications and longitudinal quantification, with U-Net–based approaches yielding the highest descriptive pooled DSC. However, the substantial between-study heterogeneity, heterogeneous reference standards, and limited multicentre external validation restrict the clinical interpretation and generalisability of the findings. Prospective multicentre studies using standardized imaging and annotation protocols, rigorous external validation, and direct evaluation of clinical impact are required before routine clinical implementation can be recommended.
