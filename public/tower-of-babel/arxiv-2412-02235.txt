Testing vs Estimation for Index-Invariant Properties in the Huge Object Model
Sourav Chakraborty, Eldar Fischer, Arijit Ghosh, Amit Levi, Gopinath Mishra, Sayantan Sen

Abstract
The Huge Object model of property testing [Goldreich and Ron, TheoretiCS 23] concerns properties of distributions supported on { 0 , 1 } n \{0,1\}^{n} , where n n is so large that even reading a single sampled string is unrealistic. Instead, query access is provided to the samples, and the efficiency of the algorithm is measured by the total number of queries that were made to them. Index-invariant properties under this model were defined in [Chakraborty et al., COLT 23], as a compromise between enduring the full intricacies of string testing when considering unconstrained properties, and giving up completely on the string structure when considering label-invariant properties. Index-invariant properties are those that are invariant through a consistent reordering of the bits of the involved strings. Here we provide an adaptation of Szemerédi’s regularity method for this setting, and in particular show that if an index-invariant property admits an ϵ \epsilon -test with a number of queries depending only on the proximity parameter ϵ \epsilon , then it also admits a distance estimation algorithm whose number of queries depends only on the approximation parameter.

1
Introduction

Distribution testing is a sub-field of property testing
[
RS96
,
GGR98
]
which has received a significant amount of attention in the past few decades (See e.g.,
[
Fis04
,
Ron08
,
Ron10
,
CS10
,
RS11
,
Gol17
,
Can20
,
Can22
,
BY22
]
). In the classical setting of distribution testing, an algorithm can draw independent samples from an unknown input distribution
\mu
, and needs to either accept or reject it based on the drawn samples. An
\epsilon
-testing algorithm for some distribution property
\mathcal{P}
is required to accept every distribution satisfying
\mathcal{P}
with high probability, and reject every distribution that has distance of at least
\epsilon
from any distribution satisfying
\mathcal{P}
with high probability, while drawing as few samples as possible.

One particular setting of interest, mainly due to its resemblance to real-world data, is the setting where the distribution is supported on a high dimensional product space. In the classical setting of distribution testing, it is implicitly assumed that the algorithm has complete access to the sampled elements. However, for product spaces with extremely large dimension, such access is impractical: Even obtaining the entire label of a single drawn element might be too expensive.

To handle this setting, Goldreich and Ron
[
GR23
]
defined and initiated the
Huge Object model
. In this model the algorithm can draw samples from the distribution, but since the samples are not fully accessible, the algorithm is provided with query access to the sampled strings. The distance notion is changed accordingly from the total variation distance to the
Earth Mover’s distance
. Various properties and algorithmic behaviors have been studied under the huge object model (See
[
GR23
,
CFG+23
,
AF24
,
AFL24
,
CSY25
]
).

When considering properties of distributions, it is commonly the case that the property at hand has some “symmetry” in its structure. One such symmetry is
label-invariance
. A property is label-invariant if applying any permutation to the labels does not effect membership in the property. Label-invariant properties have been extensively studied in the literature
[
BDKR05
,
Pan08
,
GR11
,
Val11
,
DKN14
,
CDVV14
,
ADK15
,
VV17
,
BC17
,
DKS18
,
CFG+22
]
.

Recently, Chakraborty et al.
[
CFG+23
]
considered the general task of testing
index-invariant
properties under the huge object model. A property of distributions supported on
\{0,1\}^{n}
is called index-invariant if any permutation
\pi:[n]\to[n]
of the indices of the strings does not effect membership in the property. Note that the class of index-invariant properties extends the class of label-invariant properties, since the invariance condition is weaker compared to label-invariance: It only considers relabeling strings by rearranging their symbols through a common permutation of the indices. In particular, index-invariance still allows for properties to have some string-related structure. For example, monotonicity of the probability mass function with respect to the natural partial order on
\{0,1\}^{n}
is an index-invariant property but not a label-invariant one.

The main result of
[
CFG+23
]
showed that the number of queries is controlled through upper and lower bounds depending on the VC-dimension of the support of the distributions in the property (along with the proximity parameter).
In addition, they showed that for properties that are not index-invariant such bounds are not guaranteed, and that there exists an almost tight quadratic gap between adaptive testers (i.e., the setting where queries might depend on answers to previous queries) and non-adaptive testers (i.e., the setting where queries are determined in advanced) of index-invariant properties. Note that such a distinction is non-existent in the classical model of distribution testing, as there is no notion of adaptivity when the algorithm is only allowed to draw independent samples from the distribution.

In this work, we complement the result of
[
CFG+23
]
and show that for any index-invariant property that is
\epsilon
-testable using a constant (depending only on
\epsilon
) number of queries, there exists an estimation algorithm that approximates the distance of the input distribution from the property, by performing only a constant (depending only on
\epsilon
) number of queries.

Theorem 1

(Informal statement of Theorem
2
)
.

If
\mathcal{P}
is an index-invariant property of distributions over
\{0,1\}^{n}
which is
\epsilon^{\prime}
-testable with a constant number of queries for any fixed
\epsilon^{\prime}>0
, then there is an estimation algorithm with a constant number of queries for any fixed
\epsilon>0
, that for a distribution
\mu
of distance
d
from
\mathcal{P}
, outputs a distance approximation
\boldsymbol{\eta}
such that with high probability
|\boldsymbol{\eta}-d|\leq\epsilon
.

This brings index-invariant properties under the umbrella of properties for which constant-query testability implies constant-query estimability, similar to dense graph properties, and in contrast to general string properties and also general Huge Object model properties. Along the way, we provide a “regularity-like” framework to index-invariant properties, to treat them using a “meta-testing” scheme. More on this will be discussed in Section
1.1
. It is important to note that the dependencies here are not a tower function of
1/\epsilon
, a distinction from most regularity-like frameworks in the literature, which usually provide a dependency which is a tower in the other parameters, or even worse. In particular, the query complexity in our result has double-exponential dependency on
1/\epsilon
.
Section
1.2
contains more information on this and relations to prior works.

1.1
Technical overview

The proof of our main result is motivated by Szemerédi’s regularity lemma for graphs
[
Sze75
]
. Intuitively, Szemerédi’s regularity lemma states that a graph can be partitioned into a bounded number of parts such that the subgraphs between the parts are “random-like”. In addition, we take inspiration from
[
FN07b
]
that proves an analogous estimation result for the dense graph testing model, and in particular we define and use a notion inspired by the notion of robust partitions from
[
FN07b
]
.

1.1.1
Detailings

Our starting point is to define a notion that resembles that of a graph partition for the setting of distributions. For technical reasons, we define a notion that is slightly more generalized. A
detailing

\xi
of a distribution
\mu
over
\{0,1\}^{n}
with respect to some set
A
is a distribution on
\{0,1\}^{n}\times A
such that the distribution on
\{0,1\}^{n}
that is projected from
\xi
is equal to
\mu
. Loosely speaking, a detailing of
\mu
with respect to
A
is in particular a representation of
\mu
as a weighted sum of distributions, where the weights are given by the distribution over
A
projected from
\xi
, and for every
a\in A
the corresponding “component distribution”
\mu_{a}
results from conditioning
\xi
to the event
\{0,1\}^{n}\times\{a\}
.

One particular type of a detailing which we consider extensively in this work is a
detailing by a set of variables
. For a set
U\subseteq\{1,\ldots,n\}
the detailing of
\mu
with respect to
U
is a distribution
\mu^{U}
supported over
\{0,1\}\times A
for
A=\{0,1\}^{U}
, where for
a\in A
the component
\mu_{a}
is exactly the conditioning of
\mu
to the event
\{x\in\{0,1\}^{n}:x_{U}=a\}
. Note that this detailing is actually a partition of the probability space to weighted subcubes indexed by assignments to variables in
U
. A detailing by variables can be better handled by a testing algorithm as compared to a general detailing, because a draw
(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{a})\sim\mu^{U}
can be easily obtained by taking a sample
\boldsymbol{x}\in\{0,1\}^{n}
from
\mu
and augmenting it with
\boldsymbol{a}=\boldsymbol{x}_{U}
. In comparison, a draw from a general detailing
\xi
is not always easily obtainable given only samples from the input distribution
\mu
.

The notion of a detailing allows us to extract some useful
statistics
of the underlying distribution. The statistic of a detailing
\xi
with respect to
A
is essentially the distribution
\eta
over
A
that is the projection of
\xi
to the second argument, along with a multiset of
type
vectors
\{t_{1},\ldots,t_{n}\}
over
[0,1]^{A}
where
t_{i}(a)=\mathop{{\bf Pr}\/}_{\boldsymbol{x}\sim\mu_{a}}[\boldsymbol{x}_{i}=1]
. Namely,
t_{i}(a)
is the probability that the variable
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}
is set to one conditioned on the event that a draw from
\xi
lies in
\{0,1\}^{n}\times\{a\}
. Equivalently, the multiset of type vectors can be identified with a distribution
\Lambda
supported on vectors in
[0,1]^{A}
. We refer to
\eta
as the
weight distribution
, and to
\Lambda
as the
type distribution
of the detailing
\xi
. Intuitively, one can consider the statistic as a sort of a “lossy compression scheme” for the underlying distribution.

1.1.2
Goodness and predictability

One important property that a detailing can have is being
(\epsilon,q)
-good
. For
\epsilon\in(0,1)
and
q\in\mathbbm{N}
, we say that a single distribution
\nu
is
(\epsilon,q)
-good if at least a
1-\epsilon
fraction of the
q
-tuples of variables under this distribution are
\epsilon
-close to being independent (this is a weak form of being “somewhat
q
-wise independent”). We say that a detailing
\xi
of
\mu
is
(\epsilon,q)
-good if at least a
1-\epsilon
fraction of the component distributions
\mu_{a}
(counted by their weights in the detailing) are
(\epsilon,q)
-good.

This property is extremely beneficial for finding a distribution that approximates the distribution of samples and queries made by a
canonical algorithm
using the statistic of
\xi
. A canonical algorithm for a distribution property
\mathcal{P}
is a randomized procedure that picks a set of
s
independent samples drawn from the distribution
\mu
, and a set of
q
variables (that is “coordinates” from the space
\{0,1\}^{n}
, identified by their indices from
\{1,\ldots,n\}
), chosen uniformly at random, and makes its decision (either accept or reject) based on the samples restricted to the chosen variables (and possibly on its internal random coins). In particular, the acceptance probability of the tester is determined by a function
\alpha:\{0,1\}^{s\times q}\to[0,1]
. Considering canonical testers suffices for our needs, since for any index-invariant property having a testing algorithm making
s
samples and
q
queries, there exists a corresponding canonical tester which makes at most
sq
queries, by
[
CFG+23
]
.

The goodness property allows us to simulate the behaviour of a canonical algorithm by using only the detailing statistic (this is essentially the “meta-testing” concept that was featured in
[
FN07b
,
AFNS06
]
). In particular, to simulate the behaviour of a canonical algorithm with
s
samples and
q
queries we first draw
\boldsymbol{a}_{1}\ldots,\boldsymbol{a}_{s}
independently from the weight distribution
\eta
of the detailing, and then draw
q
vectors
\boldsymbol{t}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{t}_{q}
from
\Lambda
independently. Then, we draw a random matrix
\mathbf{M}\in\{0,1\}^{s\times q}
such that for every
(i,j)\in\{1,\ldots,s\}\times\{1,\ldots,q\}
, the value
\mathbf{M}_{i,j}
is drawn independently according to the Bernoulli distribution with parameter
\boldsymbol{t}_{j}(\boldsymbol{a}_{i})
.

Essentially, for every
i\in\{1,\ldots,s\}
the draw
\boldsymbol{a}_{i}
simulates the choice of the respective component distribution
\mu_{\boldsymbol{a}_{i}}
of
\xi
, and the values
\boldsymbol{t}_{1}(\boldsymbol{a}_{i}),\ldots,\boldsymbol{t}_{q}(\boldsymbol{a}_{i})
simulate the queries to the sample
i
. Note that since
\boldsymbol{t}_{j}(\boldsymbol{a}_{i})
corresponds to the
j
-th marginal of a draw from the component distribution
\mu_{\boldsymbol{a}_{i}}
, and the drawn component distribution with high probability satisfies the goodness condition, the distribution of the string
\mathbf{M}_{i,1},\ldots,\mathbf{M}_{i,q}
is close to the distribution of a sample drawn from
\mu
and restricted to the set of
q
uniformly random indices. The matrix
\mathbf{M}
is then used along with
\alpha
to determine the acceptance probability of the algorithm.
This implies a
predictability
property of the detailing: One can approximate the acceptance probability of a canonical algorithm by using only its statistic and without actually performing the queries to the input.

1.1.3
Robustness and inheritance

The
predictability
notion is tied to a more general notion of
robustness
. Roughly speaking, a detailing is robust if whenever it is extended to a more refined detailing (but still with a limited degree of refinement), the perceived probabilities of the outcomes in individual indices will not change by much. In other words, for the most parts, the values appearing in the type distribution of the extended detailing will be
inherited
from their counterparts in the type distribution of the original detailing. Such an inheritance feature will be crucial for designing the distance estimation algorithm.
To facilitate such a feature, instead of tracking all the individual probabilities we go the route of Szemerédi’s regularity lemma, and define a single “detailing-index” measure for a detailing, with respect to which robustness is defined. The above robustness notion is then proved to follow from satisfying the detailing-index-related measure.

However, due to the access we have to the input we are unable to find such a robust detailing. We can only consider extensions which are obtained by fixing a subset of the bits in the string. As a result, we define a weaker notion of robustness. A detailing
\xi
is said to be
weakly robust
if extending it to a more refined detailing obtained by fixing a randomly chosen and not too large subset of the bits (as opposed to arbitrary refinements in the general definition of robustness) will not change the perceived probabilities of the outcomes in individual indices by much. We later prove that being a weakly robust detailing implies the general notion of robustness, and implies the above mentioned goodness notion as well.

1.1.4
Distance estimation algorithm

A main engine of our work is an efficient query algorithm that provides an approximation of the statistic of a robust detailing. We set the robustness requirement to go beyond what is needed just for predictability, as the above-mentioned inheritance feature will be required for distance estimation later on. Given the detailing statistic, we then consider hypothetical distributions with hypothetical detailings featuring predictability.

For such a hypothetical detailing we first require that its statistic must predict acceptance (or at least predict a high enough probability of not rejecting) by a testing algorithm. For a statistic satisfying this requirement, we check its hypothetical distance from the distribution (which we actually measure using a distance measure between the corresponding type statistics). The smallest such hypothetical distance gives us our estimate.

On the one hand, if there exists a relatively close distribution that satisfies the property, it would imply a corresponding hypothetical detailing statistic: We consider a predictive detailing of this target distribution, and show how to “combine” it with the detailing we obtained from the input to demonstrate a distance close to the hypothetical one. Here the inheritance property is crucial, since the combined detailing is related to a refinement of the detailing of the input, and we need it to maintain its type statistic.

On the other hand, if there exists a good hypothetical detailing statistic with a small hypothetical distance, it would imply the existence of a “fake” distribution that has a similar distance bound from the input distribution, and additionally is not rejected by the test (and hence there must be a “real” distribution satisfying the property that is not much further away). Building such a distribution is mostly a matter of considering a draw from a detailing, and then altering each coordinate independently with some probability that is tailored to eventually match the hypothetical acceptable statistic.

1.2
Relation to prior works

The study of tolerant property testing and distance approximation (also known as estimation) was initiated by Parnas, Ron and Rubinfeld
[
PRR06
]
. In the setting of tolerant testing, the algorithm is required to accept with high probability if the input is close to the property, and reject with high
probability if the input is far from any other input satisfying the property. The relation between “non-tolerant” property testing and distance approximation depends much on whether invariance restrictions are imposed on a property. While testable graph properties (which must be invariant with respect to graph isomorphism) were proved to admit full distance estimation in
[
FN07b
]
, in the general string testing model (where the properties are not required to satisfy any invariance condition) the existence of non-tolerantly testable properties which are not estimable, and in fact not even tolerantly testable, was proved in
[
FF06
]
. Later, it was proved in
[
BFLR20
]
that there are testable properties that require a near-linear number of queries for a tolerant test.

The results here exhibit the role that invariance requirements can play in the setting of the Huge Object Model. In the most general Huge Object Model setting, features of traditional string testing factor in, and in particular one can use the reductions from the original
[
GR23
]
to convert the properties from
[
FF06
]
or
[
BFLR20
]
to create non-tolerantly testable properties which are not tolerantly testable in the Huge Object model (as mandated by Theorem
1
, these properties are indeed not index-invariant).

In
[
GR23
]
two invariance notions were considered. These are label-invariance which discards most of the “string-ness” of this model and focuses on distribution properties (such as uniformity and bounded support size) that just happen to be represented by distributions on strings, and the even more restrictive notion of mapping-invariance. In
[
CFG+23
]
a study of a milder notion of invariance was initiated, namely the notion of
index-invariance
. This work proves that index-invariant properties already belong to the domain where testability implies estimability, as is the case with the dense graph model.

The influence of Szemerédi’s regularity-like constructions in property testing also has a long history, starting with
[
AFKS00
]
(in fact a mathematical result in this direction has already been presented in
[
RD85
]
). Such constructions were usually required to go beyond the original regularity lemma, at times in terms of strength (as in
[
AFKS00
]
) and at other times in terms of the objects involved outside standard graphs, such as product posets
[
FN07a
]
, graphs in the sparse model
[
BS11
,
BCCZ19
,
BCCZ18
,
CFSZ21
]
or vertex-ordered graphs
[
ABEF17
]
.

To avoid ad-hoc constructions, there are several mathematical approaches to systematically strengthening the notion of regularity and defining it for other objects, and two of them gained particular prominence. The most influential one is the analytic approach, developed in
[
LS06
,
BCL+06a
,
BCL+06b
,
LS07
,
BCL+08
,
LS10
,
BCL+12
]
, which has many applications to other research domains (see e.g.,
[
HKM+13
,
CR20
,
GHHS20
,
Sim23
]
). For an extensive introduction to the analytic approach see
[
Lov12
]
.
This approach works particularly well in settings where the exact dependency functions do not matter. The other approach is that of robust partitions (or objects) which was first used in
[
FN07b
]
. Many times both approaches work, compare e.g. the robustness approach of
[
ABEF17
]
with its analytic counterpart
[
BEFLY21
,
GHHS20
]
. It is the robustness approach that we use here.

A disconcerting feature of regularity-related approaches is that they almost always provide parameter dependencies that are a tower function or even worse (e.g. tower of towers). The analytic approach tends to do away with explicit dependencies altogether. However, there are exceptions, and much research went into reducing the dependencies in cases that allow it, starting with
[
FK99
]
. Of particular interest is the work of
[
GKS23
]
, which provided a version of the estimability result from
[
FN07b
]
whose parameter dependency is not a tower. Also in this work the resulting dependency is not a tower, although its (constant) number of exponentiations leaves something to be desired. Considering the lower bounds in
[
CFG+23
]
related to the VC dimension, it is unlikely that this dependency can be reduced.

Another interesting insight from the comparison with the work of
[
GKS23
]
is gained by noting that in our work the regularity framework in itself has a non-tower dependency, while for the dense graph setting this is not possible by the lower bound of
[
Gow97
]
(see also
[
MS16
]
). In
[
GKS23
]
they manage to move the specific estimability proof to use the weak regularity framework of
[
FK99
]
instead.

1.3
Organization of the paper

We start with somewhat longer than usual preliminaries, Section
2
, that provide the basic groundwork for our handling of distributions. Then, in Section
3
we develop our definitions of a detailing of a distribution, and the definition for its goodness and robustness. In Section
4
we show how from only knowing the statistic of a good detailing we can predict the behavior of a canonical test over a given input distribution, while in Section

2
Preliminaries

2.1
Basic handling and manipulation of distributions

For an integer
n
, we will denote the set
\{1,\ldots,n\}
as
[n]
. We use boldface letters (such as
\boldsymbol{x}
) to denote random variables.
Given two vectors
{x}
and
{y}
in
\{0,1\}^{n}
, we denote by
d_{H}({x},{y})
the normalized Hamming distance between
{x}
and
{y}
, that is,
d_{H}(x,y)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rm def}}{{=}}\frac{1}{n}\cdot\left|\{i\in[n]\,:\,x_{i}\neq{y}_{i}\}\right|.

Given a discrete distribution
\mu
over
\Omega
, for
x\in\Omega
we use
\mathop{{\bf Pr}\/}_{\mu}[x]
and
\mu(x)
interchangeably (while for an event
S\subseteq\Omega
we only use
\operatorname{{\bf Pr}}_{\mu}[S]=\sum_{x\in S}\mu(x)
for its probability). For
p\in[0,1]
, we let
\operatorname{Ber}(p)
denote the Bernoulli distribution with parameter
p
.

To streamline the arguments and analysis in the following, we will define and use a somewhat uncommon notation for some very common probabilistic notions.

Definition 2.1

(Restriction of a distribution)
.

Let
\mu
be a distribution over
\Omega
and
S\subseteq\Omega
be an event such that
\operatorname{{\bf Pr}}_{\mu}[S]\neq 0
. Then
\mu|^{S}
is the distribution
\mu
restricted to
S
, which is defined over
S
as follows: For every
x\in S
,
\operatorname{{\bf Pr}}_{\mu|^{S}}[x]=\frac{\operatorname{{\bf Pr}}_{\mu}[x]}{\operatorname{{\bf Pr}}_{\mu}[S]}
. We also sometimes pad the conditional distribution to
\Omega
by defining
\mu|^{S}(x)=0
for
x\in\Omega\setminus S
.

Definition 2.2

(Projection of a distribution)
.

Let
\mu
be a distribution over
\Omega=\prod_{\ell=1}^{n}A_{i_{\ell}}
and
i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}
be a set of integers from
[n]
such that
i_{1}<\cdots<i_{k}
. The
projection

\mu|_{\{i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}\}}
of
\mu
to a set of coordinates
\{i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}\}
is the distribution supported on
\prod_{\ell\in[k]}A_{i_{\ell}}
obtained by first drawing
\boldsymbol{x}\sim\mu
and returning
\boldsymbol{x}|_{\{i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}\}}
. Moreover, for an event
S\subseteq\Omega
, we denote by
\mu|^{S}_{\{{{i_{1}}},\ldots,{{i_{k}}}\}}

the conditioning of
\mu
over
S
followed by the projection on
\{i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}\}
.

Definition 2.3

(Conditioning shorthand)
.

When we have a distribution
\mu
on a set
\Omega
which is a product, we use a shorthand notation when we condition it by the coordinate values. For example, for
\Omega=A\times B
and
a\in A
, we use
\mu|^{1:a}
to denote the conditioning
\mu|^{\{a\}\times B}
, and similarly for
A^{\prime}\subseteq A
use
\mu|^{1:A^{\prime}}
to denote
\mu|^{A^{\prime}\times B}
. We extend this notation to multiple coordinates as well. For example, for
\Omega=A\times B\times C
and
a\in A
and
c\in C
, we use
\mu|^{1,3:(a,c)}
or
\mu|^{1:a,3:c}
to denote
\mu|^{\{a\}\times B\times\{c\}}
.

For two distributions that “agree on a common variable” we define a way to unify them into a single distribution, named after the database operation resembling it.

Definition 2.4

(Join of distributions)
.

Given two distributions
\mu
over
A\times B
and
\nu
over
B\times C
that satisfy
\mu|_{2}=\nu|_{1}
, we define their
join

\mu\bowtie\nu
, as the following distribution over
A\times B\times C
:

(\mu\bowtie\nu)(a,b,c)=\mu(a,b)\cdot\nu|_{2}^{1:b}(c)=\mu|_{1}^{2:b}(a)\cdot\nu(b,c).

Whenever
\mu|_{2}(b)=\nu|_{1}(b)=0
we (as expected) define
(\mu\bowtie\nu)(a,b,c)=0
. In cases where it is not clear which coordinate we unify (for example
when
A=B
) we state it explicitly.

We also define a way to “adjust” a distribution over a product set to have a desired projections to one of its coordinates.

Definition 2.5

(Adjustment)
.

Given a distribution
\eta
over
A\times B
and a distribution
\nu
over
A
, such that
\nu(a)=0
whenever
\eta|_{1}(a)=0
, the
adjustment of
\eta
to
\nu
, denoted
\nu\rhd\eta
, is defined as the following distribution over
A\times B
:

(\nu\rhd\eta)(a,b)=\nu(a)\cdot\eta|^{1:a}_{2}(b).

Whenever
\eta|_{1}(a)=0
(and then also
\nu(a)=0
) we as expected define
(\nu\rhd\eta)(a,b)=0
.

2.2
Distribution distances

The following is the most basic distance notion between distributions.

Definition 2.6

(Variation distance)
.

Let
\mu
and
\tau
be two probability distributions over a discrete set
\Omega
.
The
variation distance
between
\mu
and
\tau
is defined as:

d_{\mathrm{TV}}(\mu,\tau)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rm def}}{{=}}\frac{1}{2}\sum_{w\in\Omega}|\mu(w)-\tau(w)|.

Central to our work will be the notion of Earth Mover distance, with some variants. Before defining it, let us consider the notion of transfer distribution.

Definition 2.7

(Transfer distribution)
.

Let
\mu
and
\tau
be two distributions defined over two sets
A
and
B
, respectively. A distribution
T
over
A\times B
is said to be a
transfer distribution
between
\mu
and
\tau
if for every
a\in A
,
\mathop{{\bf Pr}\/}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})\sim T}[\boldsymbol{x}=a]=\mu(a)
, and for every
b\in B
,
\operatorname{{\bf Pr}}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})\sim T}[\boldsymbol{y}=b]=\tau(b)
(in other words,
T|_{1}=\mu
and
T|_{2}=\tau
). The set of all transfer distributions between
\mu
and
\tau
is denoted by
\mathcal{T}(\mu,\tau)
.

Definition 2.8

(EMD with respect to a distance function)
.

Let
\mu
and
\tau
be two distributions defined over a set
\Omega
and
d_{\Omega}
be a distance function defined over
\Omega
. Then the
Earth Mover distance
(EMD) between
\mu
and
\tau
with respect to
d_{\Omega}
is defined as follows:

d_{\mathrm{EM}}(\mu,\tau)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rm def}}{{=}}\inf_{T\in\mathcal{T}(\mu,\tau)}\mathop{{\bf E}\/}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})\sim T}\left[d_{\Omega}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})\right]

where
\mathcal{T}(\mu,\tau)
denotes the set of all possible transfer distributions between
\mu
and
\tau
.

Unless stated otherwise, whenever
\Omega=\{0,1\}^{n}
, we use
d_{\mathrm{EM}}
to refer to the EMD over the normalized Hamming distance.

We will not really have to take care of limits in our arguments due to the following observation.

Observation 2.9
.

If
\mu
and
\tau
are distributions over a finite set, then
\mathcal{T}
is compact and in particular there exists a transfer distribution
T\in\mathcal{T}
achieving the respective EMD distance.

The following observation, that variation distance is essentially also a special case of the Earth Mover distance, is well-known.

Observation 2.10
.

The Earth Mover distance over the Kronecker distance function is identical to the total variation distance. That is,

d_{\mathrm{TV}}(\mu,\tau)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rm def}}{{=}}\inf_{T\in\mathcal{T}(\mu,\tau)}\mathop{{\bf E}\/}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})\sim T}\left[\boldsymbol{1}_{\{\boldsymbol{x}\neq\boldsymbol{y}\}}\right],

where
\boldsymbol{1}_{\{\boldsymbol{x}\neq\boldsymbol{y}\}}
is the indicator function for the event that
\boldsymbol{x}\neq\boldsymbol{y}
.

Keeping with tradition, in the variation distance setting we will also refer to a transfer distribution as a
coupling
. The following lemma (which will be useful to us in the sequel) is a good example of an application of the above observation.

Lemma 2.11
.

If
\nu
is a distribution over
A
and
\eta
is a distribution over
A\times B
, then the adjustment of
\eta
to
\nu
(see Definition
2.5
) satisfies
d_{\mathrm{TV}}(\eta,\nu\rhd\eta)\leq d_{\mathrm{TV}}(\eta|_{1},\nu)
.

Proof:

Assume that
T
is an optimal coupling between
\eta|_{1}
and
\nu
. That is,
\mathop{{\bf E}\/}_{(\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{a}^{\prime})\sim T}\left[\boldsymbol{1}_{\{\boldsymbol{a}\neq\boldsymbol{a}^{\prime}\}}\right]=d_{\mathrm{TV}}(\eta,\nu)
. We use it to define a (not necessarily optimal) coupling
T^{\prime}
between
\eta
and
\nu\rhd\eta
for which
d_{\mathrm{TV}}(\eta,\nu\rhd\eta)\leq\mathop{{\bf E}\/}_{((\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b}),(\boldsymbol{a}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{b}^{\prime}))\sim T^{\prime}}\left[\boldsymbol{1}_{\{(\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b})\neq(\boldsymbol{a}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{b}^{\prime})\}}\right]\leq\mathop{{\bf E}\/}_{(\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{a}^{\prime})\sim T}\left[\boldsymbol{1}_{\{\boldsymbol{a}\neq\boldsymbol{a}^{\prime}\}}\right]
, which completes the proof.

A draw
((\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b}),(\boldsymbol{a}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{b}^{\prime}))\sim T^{\prime}
is taken as follows: We first draw
(\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{a}^{\prime})\sim T
. If
\boldsymbol{a}=\boldsymbol{a}^{\prime}
, then we let
\boldsymbol{b}=\boldsymbol{b}^{\prime}
be the result of a single draw from
\eta|^{1:\boldsymbol{a}}_{2}
. If
\boldsymbol{a}\neq\boldsymbol{a}^{\prime}
, then we let
\boldsymbol{b}
be the result of a draw from
\eta|^{1:\boldsymbol{a}}_{2}
, and let
\boldsymbol{b}^{\prime}
be the result of an independent draw from
\eta|^{1:\boldsymbol{a}^{\prime}}_{2}
.

It is not hard to see that
T^{\prime}
is indeed a transfer distribution between between
\eta
and
\nu\rhd\eta
. For example,
T^{\prime}|_{1}(a,b)=T|_{1}(a)\cdot\eta|^{1:a}_{2}(b)=\eta|_{1}(a)\cdot\eta|^{1:a}_{2}(b)=\eta(a,b)
, and the proof for
T^{\prime}|_{2}=\nu\rhd\eta
is similar. It remains to bound the probability for the event
(\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b})\neq(\boldsymbol{a}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{b}^{\prime})
, and this follows from noting that the definition of
T^{\prime}
explicitly states that
\boldsymbol{a}=\boldsymbol{a}^{\prime}
implies
\boldsymbol{b}=\boldsymbol{b}^{\prime}
.

In the sequel, we will also use the Earth Mover distance over the weighted
\ell_{1}
norm.

Definition 2.12

(EMD with respect to weighted
\ell_{1}
-distance)
.

Let
\eta
be a distribution over
A
. Also, let
x
and
y
be two vectors in
[0,1]^{A}
. The
\eta
-weighted
\ell_{1}
-distance between
x
and
y
is defined as

d_{\ell_{1}}^{\eta}(x,y)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rm def}}{{=}}\mathop{{\bf E}\/}_{\boldsymbol{a}\sim\eta}[|{x_{\boldsymbol{a}}-y_{\boldsymbol{a}}}|].

Considering two distributions
\Lambda
and
\Upsilon
defined over
[0,1]^{A}
, the EMD between them with respect to the
\eta
-weighted
\ell_{1}
-distance is denoted by
d_{\mathrm{EM}}^{\eta}(\Lambda,\Upsilon)
.

In our setting, the distributions
\Lambda
and
\Upsilon
will always be finitely-supported (even that
\Omega
itself is infinite), and in particular, Observation
2.9
will still apply.

2.3
The testing model

The Huge Object model uses the following oracle access to the unknown input distribution.

Definition 2.13

(Huge Object oracle)
.

In the Huge Object model, the algorithm can access the input distribution
\mu
in the following manner: At every stage, the algorithm may ask for a new sample
\boldsymbol{x}\sim\mu
, independently of all previous samples, or it may ask to query a coordinate
j\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
of a previously obtained sample
\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}
. When such a query is made, the output of the oracle is
\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}_{j}\in\{0,1\}
.

Definition 2.14

(Distribution Property)
.

A
distribution property

\mathcal{P}
is a sequence
\mathcal{P}_{1},\mathcal{P}_{2},\ldots
such that for every
n\geq 1
,
\mathcal{P}_{n}
is a compact subset of the set of all distributions over
\{0,1\}^{n}
.

Definition 2.15

(Distance of a distribution from a property)
.

Let
\mathcal{P}=(\mathcal{P}_{1},\mathcal{P}_{2},\ldots)
be a property and
\mu
be a distribution over
\{0,1\}^{n}
for some
n
. The
distance of
\mu
from
\mathcal{P}
is defined as
d_{\mathrm{EM}}(\mu,\mathcal{P})=\min_{\tau\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}\{d_{\mathrm{EM}}(\mu,\tau)\}
.

Testing and tolerant testing are defined as follows.

Definition 2.16

(
\epsilon
-tester)
.

Fix
0<\epsilon\leq 1
and let
\mathcal{P}
be a property of distributions supported on
\{0,1\}^{n}
. An
\epsilon
-tester
for
\mathcal{P}
is a randomized procedure that has Huge Object oracle access to an input distribution
\mu
and satisfies the following with probability at least
2/3
:

1.

(Completeness) If
\mu\in\mathcal{P}
, then the algorithm outputs
Accept
.

2.

(Soundness) If
d_{\mathrm{EM}}(\mu,\mathcal{P})>\epsilon
, then the algorithm outputs
Reject
.

Definition 2.17

(
(\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2})
-tolerant tester)
.

Fix
0\leq\epsilon_{1}<\epsilon_{2}\leq 1
and let
\mathcal{P}
be a property of distributions supported on
\{0,1\}^{n}
. An
(\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2})
-tolerant tester
for
\mathcal{P}
is a randomized procedure that has Huge Object oracle access to an input distribution
\mu
and satisfies the following with probability at least
2/3
:

1.

(Completeness) If
d_{\mathrm{EM}}(\mu,\mathcal{P})\leq\epsilon_{1}
, then the algorithm outputs
Accept
.

2.

(Soundness) If
d_{\mathrm{EM}}(\mu,\mathcal{P})>\epsilon_{2}
, then the algorithm outputs
Reject
.

Note that an (non-tolerant)
\epsilon
-tester for a property
\mathcal{P}
is the same as a “
(0,\epsilon)
-tolerant” tester for
\mathcal{P}
.

In this work we will be interested in the following class of properties.

Definition 2.18

(Index-invariant property)
.

Let
\mu
be a distribution over
\{0,1\}^{n}
. For any permutation
\pi:[n]\to[n]
, let
\mu_{\pi}
be the distribution such that
\mu(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})=\mu_{\pi}(x_{\pi(1)},\ldots,x_{\pi(n)})
for every
x\in\{0,1\}^{n}
. A distribution property
\mathcal{P}
is called
index-invariant
if
\{\mu_{\pi}:\mu\in\mathcal{\mathcal{P}}\}=\mathcal{P}
for every permutation
\pi
.

2.4
Canonical testers

The following is the definition of the information obtained by a specific sampling and querying pattern from an input distribution
\mu
.

Definition 2.19

(Canonical distribution)
.

Fix
s,q\in\mathbbm{N}
and let
\mu
be a distribution over
\{0,1\}^{n}
. The
(s,q)
-canonical distribution
for
\mu
is a distribution
\mathcal{D}^{s,q}_{\mathsf{test}}
over
\{0,1\}^{s\times q}
obtained by the following process. Draw
s
independent samples
(\boldsymbol{x}^{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{x}^{s})
from
\mu
, then pick a uniformly random
q
-tuple
(\boldsymbol{j}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{j}_{q})\in[n]^{q}
, and finally return the following matrix:

\mathbf{M}=\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{x}^{1}_{\boldsymbol{j}_{1}}&\cdots&\boldsymbol{x}^{1}_{\boldsymbol{j}_{q}}\\
\vdots&&\vdots\\
\boldsymbol{x}^{s}_{\boldsymbol{j}_{1}}&\cdots&\boldsymbol{x}^{s}_{\boldsymbol{j}_{q}}\end{bmatrix}.

When
s
and
q
are clear from the context, we omit the superscript and use
\mathcal{D}_{\mathsf{test}}
to denote the above.

Canonical testers are defined as those testers that sample and query according to the above pattern.

Definition 2.20

(
(s,q)
-canonical tester)
.

Fix
\epsilon\in(0,1)
. An
(s,q)
-canonical tester with proximity parameter
\epsilon
for some distribution property
\mathcal{P}
, is a randomized procedure that acts by obtaining a matrix
\mathbf{M}
from the
(s,q)
-canonical distribution
\mathcal{D}_{\mathsf{test}}
and then accepting or rejecting based on
\mathbf{M}
and possibly some internal coin tosses. We let
\alpha_{\epsilon}:\{0,1\}^{s\times q}\to[0,1]
so that
\alpha_{\epsilon}(\mathbf{M})
denotes the probability that the
(s,q)
-canonical tester (with proximity parameter
\epsilon
) accepts
\mathbf{M}
. The acceptance probability of the tester is denoted by
\textsf{acc}_{\epsilon}(\mu)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rm def}}{{=}}\mathop{{\bf E}\/}_{\mathbf{M}\sim\mathcal{D}_{\mathsf{test}}}[\alpha_{\epsilon}(\mathbf{M})]
.

In particular, an
(s,q)
-canonical
\epsilon
-test must satisfy the following. If
\mu\in\mathcal{P}
then the tester accepts with probability at least
2/3
(i.e.,
\textsf{acc}_{\epsilon}(\mu)\geq 2/3
) and if
d(\mu,\mathcal{P})>\epsilon
, then the tester rejects with probability at least
2/3
(i.e.,
\textsf{acc}_{\epsilon}(\mu)<1/3
). The total number of queries of such a test is
sq
.

The following lemma states that for index-invariant properties, general (even adaptive) testers can be converted to canonical testers at a cost that is at most quadratic in their number of queries.

Lemma 2.21

(Theorem 1.7 in
[
CFG+23
]
)
.

Fix
\epsilon\in(0,1)
. Let
\mathcal{P}
be an index-invariant property such that there exists a (possibly adaptive)
\epsilon
-test for
\mathcal{P}
with
s
samples and
q
queries. Then there exists an
(s,q)
-canonical
\epsilon
-test for
\mathcal{P}
performing at most
sq\leq q^{2}
queries.

2.5
Quantized distributions and distance bounds

We now define the notion of quantized distributions, which will feature heavily in our estimation algorithm. Quantizing will allow us to apply “finite” algorithms over objects which are defined over infinite sets.

Definition 2.22

(
\rho
-quantized distribution)
.

For
r\in\mathbbm{N}
and
\rho=1/r\in(0,1)
, we say that a distribution
\mu
over
\Omega
is
\rho
-quantized
if for any
w\in\Omega
it holds that
\mu(w)
is an integer multiple of
\rho
. That is, the measure takes the form
\mu:\Omega\to\{0,\rho,2\rho,\ldots,1\}
.

The following lemma is folklore. For completeness we provide it with a proof sketch.

Lemma 2.23
.

Any distribution
\mu
over a finite set
\Omega
can be transformed to a
\rho
-quantized distribution
\mu^{\prime}
so that
\mu^{\prime}(x)\in\{\rho\lceil\mu(x)/\rho\rceil,\rho\lfloor\mu(x)/\rho\rfloor\}
for every
x\in\Omega
. In particular
\mu^{\prime}(x)=\mu(x)
whenever
\mu(x)\in\{0,1\}
,
|\mu(x)-\mu^{\prime}(x)|<\rho
for all
x\in\Omega
, and
d_{\mathrm{TV}}(\mu,\mu^{\prime})<\frac{\rho}{2}\cdot|\Omega|
.

Proof:

We construct
\mu^{\prime}
by finding for every
x\in\Omega
a choice of
\mu^{\prime}(x)\in\{\rho\lceil\mu(x)/\rho\rceil,\rho\lfloor\mu(x)/\rho\rfloor\}
so that
\mu^{\prime}
will be a distribution, i.e.
\sum_{x\in\Omega}\mu^{\prime}(x)=1
. To produce this choice, we set an order
\Omega=\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{r}\}
where
r=|\Omega|
, and then inductively choose
\mu^{\prime}(x_{i})=\rho\lceil\mu(x_{i})/\rho\rceil
if
\sum_{j\in[i-1]}\mu^{\prime}(x_{j})\leq\sum_{j\in[i-1]}\mu(x_{j})
and
\mu^{\prime}(x_{i})=\rho\lfloor\mu(x_{i})/\rho\rfloor
otherwise. It is not hard to see that this way we maintain the invariant
\sum_{j\in[i]}\mu(x_{j})-\rho<\sum_{j\in[i]}\mu^{\prime}(x_{j})<\sum_{j\in[i]}\mu(x_{j})+\rho
, and in particular
\sum_{j\in[r]}\mu^{\prime}(x)=1
since it is an integer multiple of
\rho
that lies strictly between
1-\rho
and
1+\rho
.

We continue with more definitions and results regarding approximate distributions, mainly centered around the quantization notion.

Definition 2.24

(
\rho
-legitimate approximation)
.

Fix
t\in[0,1]^{A}
and
\rho\in(0,1)
. A vector
\tilde{t}\in[0,1]^{A}
is a
\rho
-legitimate approximation
of
t
if
\|\tilde{t}-t\|_{\infty}\leq\rho
.

Observation 2.25
.

Let
\eta
be a distribution over a set
A
. For any vector
t\in[0,1]^{A}
we have a
\rho
-legitimate approximation
\tilde{t}
satisfies
d^{\eta}_{\ell_{1}}(\tilde{t},t)\leq\rho
.

Proof:
By definition of the
\eta
-weighted
\ell_{1}
distance

d^{\eta}_{\ell_{1}}(\tilde{t},t)=\sum_{a\in A}\eta(a)\cdot|\tilde{t}(a)-t(a)|\leq\rho
.

We will need to quantize not only the probabilities, but also the probability space itself (if it were not finite to begin with).

Definition 2.26
.

For
\rho\in(0,1)
and a distribution
\Lambda
over
[0,1]^{A}
, we define
\Lambda_{\rho}
to be a distribution over
\{0,\rho,2\rho,\ldots,1\}^{A}
obtained in the following way. Sample
t
according to
\Lambda
and round each entry in
t
to the nearest multiple of
\rho
to obtain a
\rho
-legitimate approximation
\tilde{t}
of
t
.

Now we have the following lemma which connects these two definitions.

Lemma 2.27
.

Let
\rho\in(0,1)
and fix a distribution
\Lambda
over
[0,1]^{A}
. Then for any distribution
\eta
over
A
,
d_{\mathrm{EM}}^{\eta}(\Lambda,\Lambda_{\rho})\leq\rho/2
.

Proof:
We consider the following transfer function
T:[0,1]^{A}\to[0,1]^{A}
from
\Lambda
to
\Lambda_{\rho}
:

T(x,y)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rm def}}{{=}}\begin{cases}\Lambda(x)&\text{if }y=\tilde{x}\\
0&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

where
\tilde{x}
denotes the vector obtained from
x
by rounding the entries to the nearest multiple of
\rho
, which is at most
\rho/2
away from the unrounded value. From Observation
2.25
we have that whenever
T(x,y)\neq 0
we have
d^{\eta}_{\ell_{1}}(x,y)\leq\rho/2
. Therefore,

\displaystyle d_{\mathrm{EM}}^{\eta}(\Lambda,\Lambda_{\rho})\leq\sum_{x,y\in[0,1]^{A}}T(x,y)\cdot d^{\eta}_{\ell_{1}}(x,y)\leq\frac{\rho}{2}\sum_{x,y\in[0,1]^{A}}T(x,y)\leq\rho/2.

As a sort of a summary of the above, we now formulate a “quantize everything” lemma.

Lemma 2.28
.

Fix
\rho=1/r
for some
r\in\mathbb{N}
, and let
\Lambda
be a distribution over
[0,1]^{A}
for some finite set
A
of size
\ell
. There exists a
\rho/(r+1)^{\ell}
-quantized distribution
\Lambda^{\prime}
over
\{0,\rho,2\rho,\ldots,1\}^{A}
such that
d_{\mathrm{EM}}^{\eta}(\Lambda^{\prime},\Lambda)<\rho
.

Proof:

We use Lemma
2.27
to obtain a distribution
\Lambda_{\rho}
over
\Omega=\{0,\rho,2\rho,\ldots,1\}^{A}
such that
d_{\mathrm{EM}}^{\eta}(\Lambda,\Lambda_{\rho})\leq\rho/2
, and apply Lemma
2.23
using
\rho^{\prime}=\rho/(r+1)^{\ell}=\rho/|\Omega|
to obtain a
\rho^{\prime}
-quantized distribution
\Lambda^{\prime}
over
\{0,\rho,2\rho,\ldots,1\}^{A}
, such that
d_{\mathrm{TV}}(\Lambda_{\rho},\Lambda^{\prime})\leq\rho/2
. Overall, using the triangle inequality, we have:

d_{\mathrm{EM}}^{\eta}(\Lambda^{\prime},\Lambda)\leq d_{\mathrm{EM}}^{\eta}(\Lambda^{\prime},\Lambda_{\rho})+d_{\mathrm{EM}}^{\eta}(\Lambda_{\rho},\Lambda)\leq\rho.

We conclude with a lemma that allows us to move to guarantee a small change in the weighted Earth Mover distance when we need to use an
approximation
of the weight distribution
\eta
.

Lemma 2.29
.

Let
\eta
and
\eta^{\prime}
be two distributions over
A
satisfying
d_{\mathrm{TV}}(\eta,\eta^{\prime})\leq\rho
, and let
\Lambda
and
\Upsilon
be two discrete distributions over
[0,1]^{A}
. Then,
d_{\mathrm{EM}}^{\eta^{\prime}}(\Lambda,\Upsilon)\leq d_{\mathrm{EM}}^{\eta}(\Lambda,\Upsilon)+2\rho
.

Proof:
Let
T
denote an optimal transfer function between
\Lambda
and
\Upsilon
with respect to
d_{\mathrm{EM}}^{\eta}(\Lambda,\Upsilon)
(which might be sub-optimal with respect for
d_{\mathrm{EM}}^{\eta^{\prime}}(\Lambda,\Upsilon)
). Using also the fact that
|t(a)-t^{\prime}(a)|\leq 1
for any
t,t^{\prime}\in[0,1]^{A}
and
a\in A
we obtain:

\displaystyle d_{\mathrm{EM}}^{\eta^{\prime}}(\Lambda,\Upsilon)-d_{\mathrm{EM}}^{\eta}(\Lambda,\Upsilon)

\displaystyle\leq\mathop{{\bf E}\/}_{(\boldsymbol{t},\boldsymbol{t}^{\prime})\sim T}\left[\mathop{{\bf E}\/}_{\boldsymbol{a}\sim\eta^{\prime}}|\boldsymbol{t}(\boldsymbol{a})-\boldsymbol{t}^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{a})|\right]-\mathop{{\bf E}\/}_{(\boldsymbol{t},\boldsymbol{t}^{\prime})\sim T}\left[\mathop{{\bf E}\/}_{\boldsymbol{a}\sim\eta}|\boldsymbol{t}(\boldsymbol{a})-\boldsymbol{t}^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{a})|\right]

\displaystyle=\mathop{{\bf E}\/}_{(\boldsymbol{t},\boldsymbol{t}^{\prime})\sim T}\left[\sum_{a\in A}(\eta^{\prime}(a)-\eta(a))\cdot|\boldsymbol{t}(a)-\boldsymbol{t}^{\prime}(a)|\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathop{{\bf E}\/}_{(\boldsymbol{t},\boldsymbol{t}^{\prime})\sim T}\left[\sum_{a\in A}|\eta^{\prime}(a)-\eta(a)|\cdot|\boldsymbol{t}(a)-\boldsymbol{t}^{\prime}(a)|\right]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{a\in A}|\eta^{\prime}(a)-\eta(a)|\leq 2d_{\mathrm{TV}}(\eta,\eta^{\prime})\leq 2\rho.

2.6
Some useful probabilistic inequalities

We will use some well-known large deviation inequalities.

Lemma 2.30

(Multiplicative Chernoff bound, see
[
DP09
]
)
.

Let
\mathbf{X}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{X}_{n}
be independent random variables such that
\mathbf{X}_{i}\in[0,1]
for every
i\in[n]
. For
\mathbf{X}=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{X}_{i}
, the following holds for any
0\leq\delta\leq 1
.

\mathop{{\bf Pr}\/}[\left|\mathbf{X}-\mathop{{\bf E}\/}[\mathbf{X}]\right|\geq\delta\mathop{{\bf E}\/}[\mathbf{X}]]\leq 2\exp{\left(-{\mathop{{\bf E}\/}[\mathbf{X}]\delta^{2}}/{3}\right)}.

Lemma 2.31

(Additive Chernoff bound, see
[
DP09
]
)
.

Let
\mathbf{X}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{X}_{n}
be independent random variables such that
\mathbf{X}_{i}\in[0,1]
for every
i\in[n]
. For
\mathbf{X}=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{X}_{i}
and
a\leq\mathop{{\bf E}\/}[\mathbf{X}]\leq b
, the following hold for any
\delta>0
.

(i)

\mathop{{\bf Pr}\/}\left[\mathbf{X}\geq b+\delta\right]\leq\exp{\left(-{2\delta^{2}}/{n}\right)}
.

(ii)

\mathop{{\bf Pr}\/}\left[\mathbf{X}\leq a-\delta\right]\leq\exp{\left(-{2\delta^{2}}/{n}\right)}
.

Lemma 2.32

(Hoeffding’s Inequality, see
[
DP09
]
)
.

Let
\mathbf{X}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{X}_{n}
be independent random variables such that
a_{i}\leq\mathbf{X}_{i}\leq b_{i}
and
\mathbf{X}=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{X}_{i}
. Then, for all
\delta>0
,

\mathop{{\bf Pr}\/}\left[\left|\mathbf{X}-\mathop{{\bf E}\/}[\mathbf{X}]\right|\geq\delta\right]\leq 2\exp\left({-2\delta^{2}}/{\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}(b_{i}-a_{i})^{2}}\right).

Lemma 2.33

(Hoeffding’s Inequality for sampling without replacement
[
Hoe94
]
)
.

Let
n
and
m
be two integers such that
1\leq n\leq m
, and
x_{1},\ldots,x_{m}
be real numbers, with
a\leq x_{i}\leq b
for every
i\in[m]
. Suppose that
\mathbf{I}
is a set that is drawn uniformly from all subsets of
[m]
of size
n
, and let
\mathbf{X}=\sum\limits_{i\in\mathbf{I}}x_{i}
.
Then, for all
\delta>0
,

\mathop{{\bf Pr}\/}\left[\left|\mathbf{X}-\mathop{{\bf E}\/}[\mathbf{X}]\right|\geq\delta\right]\leq 2\exp\left({-2\delta^{2}}/{n\cdot(b-a)^{2}}\right).

We will also use some second moment lower bounds.

Lemma 2.34

(Cauchy-Schwartz inequality)
.

Given any distribution with two random variables
\mathbf{X}
and
\mathbf{Z}
,

\mathop{{\bf E}\/}_{\mathbf{Z}}\left[\mathop{{\bf E}\/}[\mathbf{X}^{2}\mid\mathbf{Z}]\right]\geq\mathop{{\bf E}\/}_{\mathbf{Z}}\left[\mathop{{\bf E}\/}[\mathbf{X}\mid\mathbf{Z}]^{2}\right].

This is a direct implication of
\mathop{{\bf E}\/}[\mathbf{X}^{2}]-(\mathop{{\bf E}\/}[\mathbf{X}])^{2}=\mathop{{\bf E}\/}[(\mathbf{X}-\mathop{{\bf E}\/}[\mathbf{X}])^{2}]\geq 0
when considered over any possible conditioning of the probability space to a value of
\mathbf{Z}
.

Observation 2.35

(Defect form Cauchy-Schwartz inequality)
.

If
\mathbf{X}
is a random variable satisfying
\mathop{{\bf Pr}\/}[|\mathbf{X}-\mathop{{\bf E}\/}[\mathbf{X}]|\geq\alpha]\geq\beta
, then

\mathop{{\bf E}\/}[\mathbf{X}^{2}]-(\mathop{{\bf E}\/}[\mathbf{X}])^{2}=\mathop{{\bf E}\/}[(\mathbf{X}-\mathop{{\bf E}\/}[\mathbf{X}])^{2}]\geq\alpha^{2}\beta.

In addition, we will use the following well-known bound.

Lemma 2.36

(Reverse Markov’s inequality)
.

For any
\gamma\in(0,1)
, let
\mathbf{X}
be a random variable in
[-1,1]
with
\mathop{{\bf E}\/}[\mathbf{X}]\geq\gamma
. Then
\mathop{{\bf Pr}\/}[\mathbf{X}\geq\gamma/2]\geq\gamma/2
.

3
Detailing, variable types and robustness

In this section we define the main objects that we use throughout this work.

3.1
Detailing and refinements

Our most basic definition is that of a detailing of a distribution
\mu
. Essentially it is a possible “deconstruction” providing additional information to aid with the analysis of
\mu
, in analogy to the notion of a vertex partition of a graph.

Definition 3.1

(Detailing)
.

Let
\mu
be a distribution over
\Omega
and
A
be a set. A
detailing

\xi
of
\mu
with respect to
A
is a distribution over
\Omega\times A
that satisfies
\xi|_{1}=\mu
.

|A|
is referred to as the
length
of the detailing. When
|A|=1
, we call
\xi
the
trivial detailing
and identify it with
\mu
itself.

The following immediate observation is in fact the motivation for Definition
2.5
.

Observation 3.2
.

If
\nu
is a distribution over
A
and
\eta
is a distribution over
A\times B
, then the adjustment
\nu\rhd\eta
of
\eta
to
\nu
is in particular a detailing of
\nu
with respect to
B
.

We will also define
\rho
-quantized detailings, even when the original distribution is not quantized. Such detailings are not quantized by themselves, only their “detailing portion” is quantized.

Definition 3.3

(Quantized detailing)
.

Let
\mu
be a distribution over
\Omega
,
A
be a set and
\xi
be a detailing of
\mu
with respect to
A
. For
\rho\in(0,1)
for which
1/\rho\in\mathbb{N}
, we say that
\xi
is
\rho
-quantized
if for every
x\in\Omega
for which
\xi|_{1}(x)>0
, the conditional distribution
\xi|^{1:x}
is
\rho
-quantized.

The following is an immediate consequence of Lemma
2.23
.

Observation 3.4
.

For a distribution
\mu
on a finite set
\Omega
, any detailing
\xi
of
\mu
with respect to a finite
A
can be transformed to a
\rho
-quantized detailing
\xi^{\prime}
, so that
\xi^{\prime}|^{1:x}(a)\in\{\rho\lceil\xi|^{1:x}(a)/\rho\rceil,\rho\lfloor\xi|^{1:x}(a)/\rho\rfloor\}
for every
x\in\Omega
and
a\in A
. In particular
\xi^{\prime}(x,a)=\xi(x,a)
whenever
\xi^{\prime}|^{1:x}(a)\in\{0,1\}
and
d_{\mathrm{TV}}(\xi,\xi^{\prime})<\frac{\rho}{2}\cdot|A|
.

Proof:

We will use Lemma
2.23
over
\xi|^{1:x}
for every
x\in\Omega
with
\xi|_{1}(x)>0
.

Any given detailing of a distribution can be “detailed further”, resulting in a refinement.

Definition 3.5

(Refinement of a detailing)
.

Let
\mu
be a distribution defined over
\Omega
and
\xi
be a detailing of
\mu
with respect to
A
. A
refinement
of
\xi
by a set
B
is a detailing
\xi^{\prime}
of
\xi
with respect to
B
. As there is a natural bijection between
\Omega\times(A\times B)
and
(\Omega\times A)\times B
,
\xi^{\prime}
can and will be considered also as a detailing of
\mu
with respect to
A\times B
.

Specific to our investigation, we also need a definition for a “do-nothing” detailing (or refinement), to which we refer as flat.

Definition 3.6

(Flat detailing)
.

Let
\mu
be a distribution over
\Omega
and
A
be a set. A detailing
\xi
of
\mu
with respect to
A
is said to be a
flat detailing
if it is a product distribution over
\Omega\times A
, i.e.
\xi(x,a)=\mu(x)\cdot\xi|_{2}(a)
for every
x\in\Omega,a\in A
.

Definition 3.7

(Flat refinement of a detailing)
.

Let
\mu
be a distribution over
\Omega=\{0,1\}^{n}
and
\xi
be a detailing of
\mu
with respect to
A
. Consider a refinement
\xi^{\prime}
of
\xi
with respect to
B
, i.e., a detailing of
\mu
with respect to
A\times B
for which
\xi^{\prime}|_{1,2}=\xi
. Such a

\xi^{\prime}
is said to be a
flat refinement
if it is a flat detailing of
\xi
.

A detailing will usually have a continuum of possible refinements, even with respect to a set of size
2
. Moreover, there is no good way to sample from a detailing or a refinement in its general form when we only have access to samples from
\mu
, and even then we only have query access to the samples from
\mu
. A specific way to define a detailing which is tangible for a testing algorithm is to “partition”
\mu
by the restriction of the samples to a specific set of variables.

Definition 3.8

(A refinement by a set of variables
U
)
.

Let
\mu
be a distribution over
\Omega=\{0,1\}^{n}
and
\xi
be a detailing of
\mu
with respect to a set
A
. For a subset
U\subseteq[n]
, the
refinement
\xi^{U}
of
\xi
by the set
U
is the distribution over
\Omega\times(A\times\{0,1\}^{|U|})
that satisfies the following:

1.

\xi^{U}(x,a,v)=0
if
x_{U}\neq v
, and

2.

\xi^{U}(x,a,v)=\xi(x,a)
if
x_{U}=v
.

Note that the length of the detailing
\xi^{U}
is
|A|\cdot 2^{|U|}
. When
\xi
is the trivial detailing (i.e.
\xi=\mu
) we denote the refinement by
U
as
\mu^{U}
, and also call it the
detailing of
\mu
defined by the variable set
U
.

3.2
Type distributions

When trying to analyze a distribution by its detailing with respect to a set
A
, a major role is given to its “weights” as given by its projection over
A
.

Definition 3.9

(Weight distribution of a detailing)
.

Let
\mu
be a distribution defined over
\Omega
, and let
\xi
be a detailing of
\mu
with respect to a set
A
. The
weight distribution of
\xi
is defined as
\xi|_{2}
.

We sometimes go the other way and define a flat refinement of a detailing
\xi
by its weight distribution (which should “conform” to the weight distribution of
\xi
).

Definition 3.10

(Flat refinement by weights)
.

Given a detailing
\xi
of
\mu
over
A
, and a detailing
\eta
of
\xi|_{2}
over
B
(that is, a distribution over
A\times B
for which
\eta|_{1}=\xi|_{2}
, thus “extending” the weight distribution of
\xi
), we denote by
\xi_{\langle\eta\rangle}
the specific flat refinement of
\xi
defined by
\xi_{\langle\eta\rangle}(x,a,b)=\xi(x,a)\cdot\eta|^{1:a}_{2}(b)=\xi|^{2:a}_{1}(x)\cdot\eta(a,b)
. In other words,
\xi_{\langle\eta\rangle}=\xi\bowtie\eta
.

We now define the other important aspect of a detailing of a distribution over
\{0,1\}^{n}
, the “type” of a variable
i\in[n]
as defined by its distribution in the component
\xi|^{2:a}_{1}
for every
a\in A
.

Definition 3.11

(Variable types of a detailing)
.

Let
\mu
be a distribution of
\{0,1\}^{n}
. Let us consider a detailing
\xi
of
\mu
with respect to
A
. The
type

t_{i}
of a variable
i\in[n]
with respect to
\xi
, is a vector in
[0,1]^{A}
defined by

t_{i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rm def}}{{=}}\left\langle\mathop{{\bf Pr}\/}_{(\boldsymbol{x},a)\sim\xi|^{\mu\times\{a\}}}[\boldsymbol{x}_{i}=1]\right\rangle_{a\in A}.

For any
a\in A
for which
\xi|_{2}(a)=0
, the value
t_{i}(a)
is set arbitrarily (and will not affect any computation in the following).

Definition 3.12

(Type distribution)
.

Let
\xi
be a detailing of
\mu
with respect to
A
.
The
type distribution
of
\xi
is denoted by
\Lambda
and defined over
[0,1]^{A}
, where a sample from
\Lambda
is obtained as follows: Choose
i\in[n]
uniformly at random and report its type
t_{i}
.

We also look at the types that we would expect from a flat refinement of a detailing.

Definition 3.13

(Flat extension)
.

Given a vector
t\in[0,1]^{A}
and a set
B
, its
flat extension
with respect to
B
is the vector
t^{\prime}\in[0,1]^{A\times B}
defined by
t^{\prime}(a,b)=t(a)
for all
a\in A
and
b\in B
. Given a finitely supported distribution
\Lambda
over
[0,1]^{A}
, its
flat extension
with respect to
B
, denoted by
\Lambda_{\langle B\rangle}
, is correspondingly defined as the distribution for which
\Lambda_{\langle B\rangle}(t^{\prime})=\Lambda(t)
for any
t\in[0,1]^{A}
and
t^{\prime}\in[0,1]^{A\times B}
which is the flat extension of
t
.

Observation 3.14
.

If
\xi
is a detailing of
A
, and
\xi^{\prime}
is any flat refinement of
\xi
with respect to
B
, then for any
i
, the type
t^{\prime}_{i}
of any variable
i
with respect to
\xi^{\prime}
is exactly the flat extension of the type
t_{i}
of
i
by
\xi
with respect to
B
(not counting pairs
(a,b)
for which
\xi^{\prime}|_{2,3}(a,b)=0
, for which
t^{\prime}_{i}(a,b)
is anyway arbitrarily defined).

Respectively, the type distribution of such an
\xi^{\prime}
is the flat extension with respect to
B
of the type distribution of
\xi
.

While usually converting the multi-set of variables’ types to a distribution as per Definition
3.12

\Omega

for some

(Implementation)

Note that an implementation of

The proof of the following lemma is obtained using linear programming.

3.3
Notions of robustness

Definition 3.20

(Index of a detailing)
.

Let
\xi
be a detailing of
\mu
with respect to
A
.
The
index
of
\xi
is defined as

\mathrm{Ind}(\xi)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rm def}}{{=}}\mathop{{\bf E}\/}_{\boldsymbol{i}\sim[n]}\left[\mathop{{\bf E}\/}_{\boldsymbol{a}\sim\xi|_{2}}\left[\operatorname{{\bf Pr}}_{\boldsymbol{x}\sim\xi|_{1}^{2:\boldsymbol{a}}}[\boldsymbol{x}_{i}=1]^{2}\right]\right]

Note that for any detailing
\xi
,
0\leq\mathrm{Ind}(\xi)\leq 1
.

We first define the robustness of a detailing in its general form.

Definition 3.21

(Robust detailing)
.

Let
\delta\in(0,1)
and
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
be constants. A detailing
\xi
of
\mu
with respect to
A
is called
(\delta,\ell)
-robust
if there exists no refinement
\xi^{\prime}
of
\xi
with respect to a set
B
such that
|B|\leq\ell
and
\mathrm{Ind}(\xi^{\prime})\geq\mathrm{Ind}(\xi)+\delta
.

The second type of robustness concerns only refinements obtained by a set of variables, and even then puts its requirement over most (rather than all) candidate variable sets.

Definition 3.22

(Weakly robust detailing)
.

Let
\delta\in(0,1)
and
k\in\mathbb{N}
be constants. A detailing
\xi
is said to be
(\delta,k)
-weakly robust
if for at least a
1-\delta
fraction of the sets
U\subseteq[n]
of size at most
k
, it holds that
\mathrm{Ind}(\xi^{U})<\mathrm{Ind}(\xi)+\delta
.

A useful feature of the way robustness is defined, is that the existence of a robust refinement of a given detailing follows almost immediately from the definition. We state here the variant of such a lemma that will be useful to us later on.

Lemma 3.23
.

For any
k
and
\delta
, if
\xi
is any detailing of
\mu
with respect to a set
A
, then there exists a set
U
of size at most
k/\delta
such that
\xi^{U}
is
(\delta,k)
-robust.

Proof:

We consider the following sequence of sets
U_{0},U_{1},\ldots,U_{r}
defined by the following inductive process: We start with the basis
U_{0}=\emptyset
, and given
U_{i}
we check whether there exists a set
V_{i}\subset[n]
of size at most
k
so that
\mathrm{Ind}(\xi^{U_{i}\cup V_{i}})\geq\mathrm{Ind}(\xi^{U_{i}})+\delta
. If there exists such a set, we set
U_{i+1}=U_{i}\cup V_{i}
and continue this process for
U_{i+1}
. Otherwise, we set
r=i
,
U=U_{i}
and terminate the process.

By definition, once this process is terminated, we obtain a
(\delta,k)
-weakly robust detailing
\xi^{U}
(in fact the termination condition is slightly stronger than the condition of Definition
3.22
). Additionally, noting that for every
i\leq r
we have
\mathrm{Ind}(\xi^{U_{i}})\geq\mathrm{Ind}(\xi)+i\cdot\delta
and
|U_{i}|\leq ik
by induction, and that
\mathrm{Ind}(\xi^{W})\in[0,1]
by definition, for any set
W\subseteq[n]
, we conclude that
r\leq 1/\delta
and so
|U|\leq rk\leq k/\delta
, as required.

We now define what it means for a detailing to be
(\epsilon,k)
-good, a notion that will provide us a degree of predictability with respect to testing algorithms.

Definition 3.24

(
\epsilon
-independent tuple)
.

Fix
\epsilon\in(0,1)
and let
\mu
be a distribution over
\{0,1\}^{n}
, and
i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}\in[n]
be a set of integers. We say that the
k
-tuple
(i_{1},\ldots,i_{k})
an
\epsilon
-independent tuple
with respect to
\mu
if
d_{\mathrm{TV}}\left(\mu|_{\{{{i_{1}},\ldots,{i_{k}}}\}},\prod_{\ell=1}^{k}\mu|_{{i_{\ell}}}\right)\leq\epsilon
.

Definition 3.25

(
(\epsilon,k)
-good detailing)
.

Let
\mu
be a distribution supported on
\{0,1\}^{n}
and let
\xi
be a detailing of
\mu
over some
A
. Let
J\subseteq A
be the set of elements where
a\in J
if at least
(1-\epsilon)n^{k}
of the
k
-tuples in
[n]
are
\epsilon
-independent with respect to
\xi|_{1}^{2:a}
. We call the detailing
(\epsilon,k)
-good
if
\mathop{{\bf Pr}\/}_{\boldsymbol{a}\sim\xi|_{2}}[\boldsymbol{a}\in J]\geq 1-\epsilon
, and refer to
J
as the
good set
.

3.4
From variable weak robustness to goodness

We prove here that variable weak robustness implies goodness, as stated in the following lemma.

Lemma 3.26
.

If a detailing
\xi
is
(\frac{\epsilon^{6}}{64k^{3}},k-1)
-weakly robust, then it is also
(\epsilon,k)
-good.

Before we prove the lemma, we set the stage with some technical tools and definitions. In the following, for a distribution
\mu
supported on
\{0,1\}^{n}
, given a tuple
\alpha=(i_{1},\ldots,i_{k})\in[n]^{k}
and an assignment
v=(v_{i_{1}},\ldots,v_{i_{k}})\in\{0,1\}^{k}
we let
\mu|^{i_{1}:v_{i_{1}},\ldots,i_{k}:v_{i_{k}}}
denote the conditioned distribution
\mu|^{\boldsymbol{x}_{\alpha}=v}
. Note that this is not a new definition but just a new use for Definition
2.3
.

Lemma 3.27
.

If the tuple
\{i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}\}
is not
\epsilon
-independent,
then there exists an index
1\leq\ell\leq k
and a set

V\subseteq\{0,1\}^{k-1}
, such that
\mathop{{\bf Pr}\/}_{\mu|_{\{{i_{1}},\ldots,{i_{\ell-1}},{i_{\ell+1}},\ldots,{i_{k}}\}}}[V]\geq\epsilon/2k
, and for every member
v=(v_{i_{1}},\ldots,v_{i_{\ell-1}},v_{i_{\ell+1}},\ldots,v_{i_{k}})\in V
we have

\left|\mu|_{{i_{\ell}}}(1)-\mu|^{i_{1}:v_{1},\ldots,i_{\ell-1}:v_{\ell-1},i_{\ell+1}:v_{\ell+1},\ldots,i_{k}:v_{k}}_{{i_{\ell}}}(1)\right|\geq\epsilon/2k
.

The proof of Lemma
3.27
follows from the following one.

Lemma 3.28
.

Let
\mu
be a distribution over
\{0,1\}^{n}
. Then

d_{\mathrm{TV}}\left(\mu|_{\{i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}\}},\prod_{j=1}^{k}\mu|_{i_{j}}\right)\leq\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}\mathop{{\bf E}\/}_{\boldsymbol{x}\sim\mu}\left[\left|\mu|^{i_{1}:\boldsymbol{x}_{i_{1}},\ldots,i_{\ell-1}:\boldsymbol{x}_{i_{\ell-1}}}_{i_{\ell}}(1)-\mu|_{i_{\ell}}(1)\right|\right]

Proof:

We use Observation
2.10
, and prove the statement by constructing an appropriate coupling (transfer distribution)
T
which demonstrates this distance bound (with respect to the Kronecker norm) between
\nu\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rm def}}{{=}}\mu|_{\{i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}\}}
and
\tau\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rm def}}{{=}}\prod_{j=1}^{k}\mu|_{i_{j}}
. To draw
(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})\sim T
(where
(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})\in(\{0,1\}^{k})^{2}
), for
\ell\in[k]
we draw
\boldsymbol{x}_{\ell}
and
\boldsymbol{y}_{\ell}
inductively considering the values of
\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{x}_{\ell-1}
and
\boldsymbol{y}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{y}_{\ell-1}
. The base case (before we draw any bits) is trivial.

To draw
\boldsymbol{x}_{\ell}
and
\boldsymbol{y}_{\ell}
we first calculate

\alpha_{\ell}=\mathop{{\bf Pr}\/}_{\boldsymbol{z}\sim\mu|^{i_{1}:\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\ldots,i_{\ell-1}:\boldsymbol{x}_{{\ell-1}}}}[\boldsymbol{z}_{j_{\ell}}=1]=\mu|^{i_{1}:\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\ldots,i_{\ell-1}:\boldsymbol{x}_{{\ell-1}}}_{i_{\ell}}(1)

and
\beta_{\ell}=\mu|_{i_{\ell}}(1)
. Then if
\alpha_{\ell}\geq\beta_{\ell}
we set
(\boldsymbol{x}_{\ell},\boldsymbol{y}_{\ell})=(1,1)
with probability
\beta_{\ell}
,
(\boldsymbol{x}_{\ell},\boldsymbol{y}_{\ell})=(1,0)
with probability
\alpha_{\ell}-\beta_{\ell}
, and
(\boldsymbol{x}_{\ell},\boldsymbol{y}_{\ell})=(0,0)
with probability
1-\alpha_{\ell}
. If
\alpha_{\ell}\leq\beta_{\ell}
then we analogously set
(\boldsymbol{x}_{\ell},\boldsymbol{y}_{\ell})=(1,1)
with probability
\alpha_{\ell}
,
(\boldsymbol{x}_{\ell},\boldsymbol{y}_{\ell})=(0,1)
with probability
\beta_{\ell}-\alpha_{\ell}
, and
(\boldsymbol{x}_{\ell},\boldsymbol{y}_{\ell})=(0,0)
with probability
1-\beta_{\ell}
.

It is not hard to see that this is indeed a coupling between
\nu
and
\tau
. Also, for every
\ell

\mathop{{\bf Pr}\/}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})\sim T}[\boldsymbol{x}_{\ell}\neq\boldsymbol{y}_{\ell}]=\mathop{{\bf E}\/}_{\boldsymbol{x}\sim\mu}\left[|\alpha_{\ell}-\beta_{\ell}|\right]=\mathop{{\bf E}\/}_{\boldsymbol{x}\sim\mu}\left[\left|\mu|^{i_{1}:\boldsymbol{x}_{i_{1}},\ldots,i_{\ell-1}:\boldsymbol{x}_{j_{\ell-1}}}_{i_{\ell}}(1)-\mu|_{i_{\ell}}(1)\right|\right]

and hence by a union bound

\mathop{{\bf Pr}\/}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})\sim T}[\boldsymbol{x}\neq\boldsymbol{y}]\leq\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}\mathop{{\bf E}\/}_{\boldsymbol{x}\sim\mu}\left[\left|\mu|^{i_{1}:\boldsymbol{x}_{i_{1}},\ldots,i_{\ell-1}:\boldsymbol{x}_{j_{\ell-1}}}_{i_{\ell}}(1)-\mu|_{i_{\ell}}(1)\right|\right]

completing the proof.

Proof of Lemma
3.27
:

Assume that the conclusion of the lemma does not hold. We define for all
\ell\in[k]
the set
V_{\ell}\subseteq\{0,1\}^{k-1}
of all
v
for which
\left|\mu|_{{i_{\ell}}}(1)-\mu|^{i_{1}:v_{1},\ldots,i_{\ell-1}:v_{\ell-1},i_{\ell+1}:v_{\ell+1},\ldots,i_{k}:v_{k}}_{{i_{\ell}}}(1)\right|\geq\epsilon/2k
. We then note that
\delta_{\ell}=\mathop{{\bf E}\/}_{\boldsymbol{x}\sim\mu}\left[\left|\mu|^{i_{1}:\boldsymbol{x}_{i_{1}},\ldots,i_{\ell-1}:\boldsymbol{x}_{j_{\ell-1}}}_{i_{\ell}}(1)-\mu|_{i_{\ell}}(1)\right|\right]
satisfies

\displaystyle\delta_{\ell}

\displaystyle=\mathop{{\bf E}\/}_{\boldsymbol{x}\sim\mu}\left[\left|\mathop{{\bf E}\/}_{\boldsymbol{z}\sim\mu}\left[\mu|^{i_{1}:\boldsymbol{x}_{i_{1}},\ldots,i_{\ell-1}:\boldsymbol{x}_{j_{\ell-1}},i_{\ell+1}:\boldsymbol{z}_{i_{\ell+1}},\ldots,i_{k}:\boldsymbol{z}_{k}}_{i_{\ell}}(1)-\mu|_{i_{\ell}}(1)\right]\right|\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathop{{\bf E}\/}_{\boldsymbol{x}\sim\mu}\left[\left|\mu|^{i_{1}:\boldsymbol{x}_{i_{1}},\ldots,i_{\ell-1}:\boldsymbol{x}_{j_{\ell-1}},i_{\ell+1}:\boldsymbol{x}_{i_{\ell+1}},\ldots,i_{k}:\boldsymbol{x}_{k}}_{i_{\ell}}(1)-\mu|_{i_{\ell}}(1)\right|\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathop{{\bf Pr}\/}_{\mu|_{\{{i_{1}},\ldots,{i_{\ell-1}},{i_{\ell+1}},\ldots,{i_{k}}\}}}[V_{\ell}]\cdot 1+\mathop{{\bf Pr}\/}_{\mu|_{\{{i_{1}},\ldots,{i_{\ell-1}},{i_{\ell+1}},\ldots,{i_{k}}\}}}[\{0,1\}^{k-1}\setminus V_{\ell}]\cdot\frac{\epsilon}{2k}=\frac{\epsilon}{k}

Then Lemma

would have implied that

, in contradiction to our premise.

Definition 3.29

(Pivot tuple)
.

Fix
k\in[n]
,
let
\mu
be a distribution over
\{0,1\}^{n}
and
\epsilon\in(0,1)
. We say that a
(k-1)
-tuple
({i_{1}},\ldots,{i_{\ell-1}},{i_{\ell+1}},\ldots,{i_{k}})\in[n]^{k-1}
is a
pivot tuple for
{i_{\ell}}
with respect to
\mu
if the following holds. There exists a set
V\subseteq\{0,1\}^{k-1}
such that
\mathop{{\bf Pr}\/}_{\mu|_{\{{i_{1}},\ldots,{i_{\ell-1}},{i_{\ell+1}},\ldots,{i_{k}}\}}}[V]\geq\epsilon/2k
, and for every
v\in V
we have

\left|\mu|_{{i_{\ell}}}(1)-\mu|^{i_{1}:v_{1},\ldots,i_{\ell-1}:v_{\ell-1},i_{\ell+1}:v_{\ell+1},\ldots,i_{k}:v_{k}}_{{i_{\ell}}}(1)\right|\geq\epsilon/2k
.

We now prove, in two stages, that if a detailing is not
(\epsilon,k)
-good, then there exist many tuples that are pivot over much of the weight for many variables.

Observation 3.30
.

Fix
k\in[n]
, let
\mu
be a distribution over
\{0,1\}^{n}
and
\epsilon\in(0,1)
. If there exists a set
\mathcal{S}\in[n]^{k}
of at least
\epsilon n^{k}
tuples that are not
\epsilon
-independent, then there is a set
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}\subseteq[n]^{k-1}
of at least
\frac{\epsilon}{2k}\cdot n^{k-1}
tuples such that each of them is a pivot tuple for at least
\frac{\epsilon}{2k}\cdot n
variables outside of it.

Proof:

Note that Lemma
3.27
immediately implies that for every
U\in\mathcal{S}
there exists
i_{U}\in U
so that
U\setminus\{i_{U}\}
is a pivot tuple with respect to
i_{U}
. By the premise of the observation, if we uniformly pick a tuple
U^{\prime}\in[n]^{k-1}
and
i\in[n]
, then with probability at least
\epsilon
we will obtain that
U^{\prime}\cup\{i\}\in\mathcal{S}
(and also in particular
|U^{\prime}\cup\{i\}|=k
), and so with probability at least
\epsilon/k
we obtain that
U^{\prime}
is a pivot tuple for
i
(since the probability for
i=i_{U^{\prime}\cup\{i\}}
conditioned on
U^{\prime}\cup\{i\}=U\in\mathcal{S}
is
1/k
).

A reverse Markov inequality then implies that there exists a set
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
of at least
\frac{\epsilon}{2k}\cdot n^{k-1}
tuples so that for every
U^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
there exist at least
\frac{\epsilon}{2k}\cdot n
many variables for which it is a pivot tuple.

3.5
From variable weak robustness to general robustness

In this section we prove that weak variable robustness implies even the seemingly much stronger notion of general robustness, as stated in the following lemma.

Lemma 3.32
.

Fix
\gamma\in(0,1)
,
\ell\in\mathbbm{N}
and let
\xi
be a detailing of
\mu
with respect to
A
. If
\xi
is
(\gamma/2,C\cdot\frac{\ell^{8}}{\gamma^{38}})
-weakly robust for some absolute constant
C>1
, then it is
(\gamma,\ell)
-robust.

The above lemma is a direct consequence of the following lemma (which will be proved here), which is a slightly strengthened restatement of Lemma
3.32
in a “contrary form”.

Lemma 3.33
.

Fix
\gamma\in(0,1)
, let
\xi
be a detailing of
\mu
with respect to
A
, and let
\zeta
be a refinement of
\xi
with respect to
A\times B
such that
\mathrm{Ind}(\zeta)-\mathrm{Ind}(\xi)\geq\gamma
. Then, there exists
k=O\left(\frac{|B|^{8}}{\gamma^{38}}\right)
such that the following holds. A uniformly random
k
-tuple
\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in[n]^{k}
satisfies
\mathrm{Ind}(\zeta)-\mathrm{Ind}(\xi^{\boldsymbol{\alpha}})<\gamma/2
, with probability at least
1/2
.

To prove Lemma
3.33
, we need the following
progress lemma
to be proved in Section
3.5.1
.

Lemma 3.34
.

If
\xi
is a detailing of
\mu
with respect to
A
, and
\zeta
is

\xi
with respect to

\Omega\left(\frac{\gamma^{19}n}{|B|^{4}}\right)

\mathrm{Ind}(\xi^{\{i\}})-\mathrm{Ind}(\xi)\geq\Omega\left(\frac{\gamma^{19}}{|B|^{4}}\right)

We now define some notations. Let
\boldsymbol{\alpha}=(\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{x}_{k})\in[n]^{k}

\mathbf{X}_{t}=\begin{cases}0,&\text{if }\;\mathrm{Ind}(\xi^{\{\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{x}_{t}\}})-\mathrm{Ind}(\xi^{\{\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{x}_{t-1}\}})<\rho\\

\mathbf{R}_{t}=\begin{cases}\boldsymbol{x}_{t},&\text{if }\;\mathbf{X}_{t}=1\\

Now we have the following lemma which lower bounds the probability of the event
\mathbf{R}_{t}\neq 0
conditioned on the values of
\mathbf{R}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{R}_{t-1}
for every

Lemma 3.35
.

Let
t\in[k]
and

\mathrm{Ind}\left(\zeta\right)-\mathrm{Ind}(\xi^{\{r_{1},\ldots,r_{t-1}\}\setminus\{0\}})\geq\gamma/2

\mathop{{\bf Pr}\/}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}\sim[n]^{k}}[\mathbf{R}_{t}\neq 0\mid\mathbf{R}_{1}=r_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{R}_{t-1}=r_{t-1}]\geq\rho,

where
\rho=\Omega\left(\frac{\gamma^{19}}{|B|^{4}}\right)
.

Proof:
We apply Lemma
3.34
with the detailing
\xi^{\{r_{1},\ldots,r_{t-1}\}\setminus\{0\}}
, and
\gamma/2
instead of
\gamma
. By our assumptions we have
\mathrm{Ind}\left(\zeta\right)-\mathrm{Ind}(\xi^{\{r_{1},\ldots,r_{t-1}\}\setminus\{0\}})\geq\gamma/2
, so there exists a set
S\subseteq[n]
of size at least
\Omega\left(\frac{\gamma^{19}n}{|B|^{4}}\right)
, such that for any
x_{t}\in S
, we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{Ind}(\xi^{(\{r_{1},\ldots,r_{t-1}\}\setminus\{0\})\cup\{x_{t}\}})-\mathrm{Ind}(\xi^{\{r_{1},\ldots,r_{t-1}\}\setminus\{0\}})\geq\Omega\left(\frac{\gamma^{19}}{|B|^{4}}\right).

(1)

Note that if
\boldsymbol{x}_{t}\in S
, then since Equation (
1
) holds, we must have that
\mathbf{X}_{t}=1
and
\mathbf{R}_{t}=\boldsymbol{x}_{t}\neq 0
. As the probability that
\boldsymbol{x}_{t}\in S
is at least
\Omega\left(\frac{\gamma^{19}}{|B|^{4}}\right)
the lemma follows.

Now we will use the following lemma from
[
AFL24
]
.

Lemma 3.36

(
[
AFL24
]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{G}\subset\mathbb{R}^{*}
be a set of goal sequences, satisfying that if
u
is a prefix of

and

then
v\in\mathcal{G}
. Additionally, let
\mathbf{R}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{R}_{M}
be a set of random variables and

be values in

, such that for every

\mathbf{X}_{t}\in\{0,1\}

\mathbf{X}=\sum_{t=1}^{M}\mathbf{X}_{t}

0<\delta<1

\mathop{{\bf Pr}\/}\left[((\mathbf{R}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{R}_{M})\notin\mathcal{G})\wedge\left(\mathbf{X}<(1-\delta)\sum_{t=1}^{M}p_{t}\right)\right]<\left(\frac{e^{-\delta}}{(1-\delta)^{1-\delta}}\right)^{\sum_{t=1}^{M}p_{t}}

3.33
:
We define the set of goals as follows

\mathcal{G}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rm def}}{{=}}\left\{(r_{1},\ldots,r_{k})\in([n]\cup\{0\})^{k}:\mathrm{Ind}(\zeta)-\mathrm{Ind}(\xi^{\{r_{1},\ldots,r_{k}\}\setminus\{0\}})<\gamma/2\right\}

(3)

Finally, by an averaging argument (noting that
|B_{a}|\leq|B|
), for every

which satisfies Equation (
3
), completing the proof of the first item.

For the second item, note that from Equation (
2
) for any fixed
a\in A^{\prime}
we have that

\displaystyle\gamma/2\leq\mathop{{\bf E}\/}_{i\sim[n]}\left[\mathop{{\bf E}\/}_{\boldsymbol{b}\sim\zeta|_{3}^{2:a}}\left[\mathop{{\bf Pr}\/}_{\boldsymbol{x}\sim\zeta|_{1}^{2,3:(a,\boldsymbol{b})}}\left[\boldsymbol{x}_{i}=1\right]^{2}\right]-\mathop{{\bf Pr}\/}_{\boldsymbol{x}\sim\xi|_{1}^{2:a}}\left[\boldsymbol{x}_{i}=1\right]^{2}\right]=\mathrm{Ind}(\zeta|^{2:a})-\mathrm{Ind}(\xi|^{2:a}).

(4)

Suppose that there is
b^{*}\in B
such that
\zeta|_{3}^{2:a}(b^{*})>1-\gamma/8
. Then,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Ind}(\zeta|^{2:a})

\displaystyle=\mathop{{\bf E}\/}_{i\sim[n]}\left[\mathop{{\bf E}\/}_{\boldsymbol{b}\sim\zeta|_{3}^{2:a}}\left[\mathop{{\bf Pr}\/}_{\boldsymbol{x}\sim\zeta|_{1}^{2,3:(a,\boldsymbol{b})}}[\boldsymbol{x}_{i}=1]^{2}\right]\right]=\mathop{{\bf E}\/}_{\boldsymbol{b}\sim\zeta|_{3}^{2:a}}\left[\mathrm{Ind}(\zeta|^{2,3:(a,\boldsymbol{b})})\right]

\displaystyle=\zeta|_{3}^{2:a}(b^{*})\cdot\mathrm{Ind}(\zeta|^{2,3:(a,b^{*})})+\sum_{b\neq b^{*}\in B}\zeta|_{3}^{2:a}(b)\cdot\mathrm{Ind}(\zeta|^{2,3:(a,b)})

\displaystyle\leq\mathrm{Ind}(\zeta|^{2,3:(a,b^{*})})+\frac{\gamma}{8}.

(5)

On the other hand,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Ind}(\xi|^{2:a})

\displaystyle=\mathop{{\bf E}\/}_{i\sim[n]}\left[\mathop{{\bf E}\/}_{\boldsymbol{b}\sim\zeta|_{3}^{2:a}}\left[\mathop{{\bf Pr}\/}_{\boldsymbol{x}\sim\zeta|_{1}^{2,3:(a,\boldsymbol{b})}}[\boldsymbol{x}_{i}=1]\right]^{2}\right]\geq\mathop{{\bf E}\/}_{i\sim[n]}\left[\left(\zeta|_{3}^{2:a}(b^{*})\cdot\mathop{{\bf Pr}\/}_{\boldsymbol{x}\sim\zeta|_{1}^{2,3:(a,b^{*})}}[\boldsymbol{x}_{i}=1]\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\geq(1-\gamma/8)\mathrm{Ind}(\zeta|^{2,3:(a,b^{*})}).

Plugging in Equation (
5
) we get that

\mathrm{Ind}(\zeta|^{2:a})\leq\frac{\mathrm{Ind}(\xi|^{2:a})}{(1-\gamma/8)}+\gamma/8\leq\mathrm{Ind}(\xi|^{2:a})(1+\gamma/8)+\gamma/8\leq\mathrm{Ind}(\xi|^{2:a})+\frac{\gamma}{4},

which is a contradiction to Equation (
4
).

Definition 3.38

Lemma 3.39
.

Fix

By the reverse Markov inequality there exists a set

\displaystyle\mathop{{\bf Pr}\/}_{\boldsymbol{x}\sim\zeta|_{1}^{2,3:(a,b)}}[\boldsymbol{x}_{i}=1]-\mathop{{\bf Pr}\/}_{\boldsymbol{x}\sim\xi|_{1}^{2:a}}[\boldsymbol{x}_{i}=1]\geq\gamma/4.

(6)

Lemma 3.40
.

For any

Proof:
Let
\mathbf{X}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rm def}}{{=}}\sum_{i\in S_{a}}\boldsymbol{x}_{i}

Lemma 3.41

4
Predicting the acceptance probability of a canonical tester

In this section we show that knowing the statistics of a good detailing is sufficient to approximate the acceptance probability of a property testing algorithm without actually running it.

4.1
Simulation of a canonical tester

We describe here a randomized process,
Simulate
(see Figure
1
), that given the statistic of a detailing
\xi
of a distribution
\mu
provides a simulated result of a canonical query pattern, without making any further queries to
\mu
(eventually we will only use queries to find
\xi
and its statistic).

Procedure
\texttt{Simulate}(s,q,\eta,\Lambda)

Input:

s,q\in\mathbbm{N}
corresponding to samples and queries, weight distribution
\eta
and type distribution
\Lambda
of a detailing
\xi
.

Output:
A matrix
\mathbf{M}\in\{0,1\}^{s\times q}
.

1.

For each

, draw
\boldsymbol{t}_{j}\sim\Lambda
independently.

2.

For each
i\in[s]
, draw
\boldsymbol{a}_{i}\sim\eta
independently.

3.

For every
(i,j)\in[s]\times[q]
, draw
\mathbf{M}_{i,j}\sim\operatorname{Ber}(\boldsymbol{t}_{j}(\boldsymbol{a}_{i}))
independently.

4.

Return

\mathbf{M}

Figure 1:
Description of the
Simulate
procedure.

From now on we denote by
\mathcal{D}_{\mathsf{sim}}(\eta,\Lambda)
the distribution over the output of
\texttt{Simulate}(s,q,\eta,\Lambda)
(note that this output is a randomized

matrix). We now show that given a robust enough detailing of
\mu
, this simulated distribution is indeed close to the distribution
\mathcal{D}_{\mathsf{test}}
generated by an actual canonical

query pattern.

Lemma 4.1
.

Fix

and

Proof:

(see Observation
2.10

between
\mathcal{D}_{\mathsf{sim}}(\eta,\Lambda)

{\mathcal{D}}_{\mathsf{test}}

[n]
(with repetitions), and let

be their respective types in
\xi

\xi|_{2}

(independently of

For every

denote an optimal transfer distribution between

and
\xi|^{2:\boldsymbol{a}_{i}}_{\{\boldsymbol{j}^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{j}^{\prime}_{q}\}}
. That is,
\operatorname{{\bf Pr}}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}})\sim\mathbf{T}_{i}}[\boldsymbol{x}\neq\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}]=d_{\mathrm{TV}}(\prod_{j\in[q]}\operatorname{Ber}(\boldsymbol{t}_{j}(\boldsymbol{a}_{i})),\xi|^{2:\boldsymbol{a}_{i}}_{\{\boldsymbol{j}^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{j}^{\prime}_{q}\}})
. To obtain
\mathbf{M}
and
\widetilde{\mathbf{M}}
, for every
i\in[s]
independently, we draw
((\mathbf{M}_{i,1},\ldots,\mathbf{M}_{i,q}),(\widetilde{\mathbf{M}}_{i,1},\ldots,\widetilde{\mathbf{M}}_{i,q}))
from
\mathbf{T}_{i}
.

In addition, we define the following random events:

•

\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}_{0}
is the event that (
\boldsymbol{j}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{j}_{q})=(\boldsymbol{j}^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{j}^{\prime}_{q})
.

•

\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}_{1}
is the event that
\boldsymbol{a}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{a}_{s}\in J
where
J
is the good set as per Definition
3.25
.

•

\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}_{2}
is the event that the
q
-tuple
(\boldsymbol{j}^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{j}^{\prime}_{q})
is
\epsilon^{\prime}
-independent with respect to all
\xi_{\boldsymbol{a}_{1}},\ldots,\xi_{\boldsymbol{a}_{s}}
.

•

\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}_{3}
is the event that
(\mathbf{M}_{i,1},\ldots,\mathbf{M}_{i,q})=(\widetilde{\mathbf{M}}_{i,1},\ldots,\widetilde{\mathbf{M}}_{i,q})
for all
i\in[s]
.

We now note that

\displaystyle d_{\mathrm{TV}}(\mathcal{D}_{\mathsf{sim}},\mathcal{D}_{\mathsf{test}})

\displaystyle\leq\operatorname{{\bf Pr}}_{\mathbf{T}^{*}}[\mathbf{M}\neq\widetilde{\mathbf{M}}]=\operatorname{{\bf Pr}}[\neg\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}_{3}]

\displaystyle\leq\operatorname{{\bf Pr}}[\neg\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}_{0}]+\operatorname{{\bf Pr}}[\neg\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}_{1}]+\operatorname{{\bf Pr}}[\neg\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}_{2}\wedge\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}_{1}]+\operatorname{{\bf Pr}}[\neg\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}_{3}\wedge\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}_{0}\wedge\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}_{1}\wedge\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}_{2}]

\displaystyle\leq\operatorname{{\bf Pr}}[\neg\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}_{0}]+\operatorname{{\bf Pr}}[\neg\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}_{1}]+\operatorname{{\bf Pr}}[\neg\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}_{2}\mid\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}_{1}]+\operatorname{{\bf Pr}}[\neg\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}_{3}\mid\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}_{0}\wedge\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}_{1}\wedge\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}_{2}]

By the fact that the detailing is
(\epsilon^{\prime},q)
-good, using a union bound we get
\mathop{{\bf Pr}\/}[\neg\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}_{1}]\leq s\cdot\epsilon^{\prime}
. Similarly, the probability of a
q
-tuple
(\boldsymbol{j}^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{j}^{\prime}_{q})
to be
\epsilon^{\prime}
-independent with respect to
\xi|^{2:\boldsymbol{a}_{i}}
is at least
1-\epsilon^{\prime}
, and thus the probability that the
q
-tuple is
\epsilon^{\prime}
-independent with respect to all
\xi|^{2:\boldsymbol{a}_{1}},\ldots,\xi|^{2:\boldsymbol{a}_{s}}
is greater than
1-s\cdot\epsilon^{\prime}
(equivalently
\mathop{{\bf Pr}\/}[\neg\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}_{2}|\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}_{1}]\leq s\cdot\epsilon^{\prime}
). To bound the probability of
\neg\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}_{0}
, note that if
n\geq 2q^{2}/\epsilon^{\prime}
, then by the birthday paradox
\mathop{{\bf Pr}\/}[\neg\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}_{0}]\leq\frac{q(q-1)}{2n}\leq\epsilon^{\prime}
. It is left to bound
\operatorname{{\bf Pr}}[\neg\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}_{3}|\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}_{0}\wedge\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}_{1}\wedge\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}_{2}]
.

\displaystyle\operatorname{{\bf Pr}}[\neg\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}_{3}|\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}_{0}\wedge\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}_{1}\wedge\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}_{2}]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i\in[s]}\mathop{{\bf Pr}\/}_{(\mathbf{M}_{i,*},\widetilde{\mathbf{M}}_{i,*})\sim\mathbf{T}_{i}}\left[\mathbf{M}_{i,*}\neq\widetilde{\mathbf{M}}_{i,*}\mid\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}_{0}\wedge\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}_{1}\wedge\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}_{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i\in[s]}d_{\mathrm{TV}}\left(\prod_{j\in[q]}\operatorname{Ber}(\boldsymbol{t}_{j}(\boldsymbol{a}_{i})),\xi|^{2:\boldsymbol{a}_{i}}_{\{\boldsymbol{j}^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{j}^{\prime}_{q}\}}\right)\leq s\cdot\epsilon^{\prime},

where the last inequality follows from the
q
-tuple being
\epsilon^{\prime}
-independent with respect to all
\xi_{\boldsymbol{a}_{i}}
with
i\in[s]
.
Overall, we get

d_{\mathrm{TV}}(\mathcal{D}_{\mathsf{sim}}(\eta,\Lambda),\mathcal{D}_{\mathsf{test}})\leq s\cdot\epsilon^{\prime}+2s\cdot\epsilon^{\prime}+\epsilon^{\prime}=(3s+1)\epsilon^{\prime}\leq\epsilon.

However, our eventual estimation algorithm will not be able to accurately find
\eta
and
\Lambda
, but only approximations thereof. For this reason we will need “continuity lemmas” for these quantities, showing that the output distribution
\mathcal{D}_{\mathsf{sim}}
of
Simulate
will not degrade by much.

The next lemma handles the setting where we only have an approximation
\tilde{\Lambda}
of the type distribution
\Lambda
, that is close enough to it in the
\eta
-weighted
\ell_{1}
Earth Mover distance.

Lemma 4.2
.

For
q,s\in\mathbbm{N}
and
\epsilon\in(0,1)
, if
d_{\mathrm{EM}}^{\eta}(\Lambda,\tilde{\Lambda})\leq\frac{\epsilon}{s\cdot q}
then
d_{\mathrm{TV}}(\mathcal{D}_{\mathsf{sim}}(\eta,\Lambda),{\mathcal{D}}_{\mathsf{sim}}(\eta,\tilde{\Lambda}))\leq\epsilon
.

Proof:
By the premise of the lemma we have that
d_{\mathrm{EM}}^{\eta}(\Lambda,\tilde{\Lambda})\leq\frac{\epsilon}{s\cdot q}
, so let
\mathbf{T}
be a the transfer distribution over
([0,1]^{A})^{2}
exhibiting the distance. We consider the following coupling
\mathbf{T}^{*}
between
\mathcal{D}_{\mathsf{sim}}(\eta,\Lambda)
and
{\mathcal{D}}_{\mathsf{sim}}(\eta,\tilde{\Lambda})
. Sample
(\boldsymbol{t}_{1},\tilde{\boldsymbol{t}}_{1}),\ldots,(\boldsymbol{t}_{q},\tilde{\boldsymbol{t}}_{q})\sim\mathbf{T}
and
\boldsymbol{a}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{a}_{s}\sim\eta
, all independently.
To set
\mathbf{M}
and
\widetilde{\mathbf{M}}
, perform the following for every
i\in[s]
and
j\in[q]
independently:

(i)

If
\boldsymbol{t}_{j}(\boldsymbol{a}_{i})\leq\widetilde{\boldsymbol{t}}_{j}(\boldsymbol{a}_{i})
, then we do the following:

(a)

with probability
\boldsymbol{t}_{j}(\boldsymbol{a}_{i})
set
(\mathbf{M}_{i,j},\widetilde{\mathbf{M}}_{i,j})=(1,1)
,

(b)

with probability
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{t}}_{j}(\boldsymbol{a}_{i})-\boldsymbol{t}_{j}(\boldsymbol{a}_{i})
set
(\mathbf{M}_{i,j},\widetilde{\mathbf{M}}_{i,j})=(0,1)
,

(c)

with probability
1-\widetilde{\boldsymbol{t}}_{j}(\boldsymbol{a}_{i})
set
(\mathbf{M}_{i,j},\widetilde{\mathbf{M}}_{i,j})=(0,0)
.

(ii)

If
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{t}}_{j}(\boldsymbol{a}_{i})\leq\boldsymbol{t}_{j}(\boldsymbol{a}_{i})
, then we do the following:

(a)

with probability
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{t}}_{j}(\boldsymbol{a}_{i})
set
(\mathbf{M}_{i,j},\widetilde{\mathbf{M}}_{i,j})=(1,1)
,

(b)

with probability
\boldsymbol{t}_{j}(\boldsymbol{a}_{i})-\widetilde{\boldsymbol{t}}_{j}(\boldsymbol{a}_{i})
set
(\mathbf{M}_{i,j},\widetilde{\mathbf{M}}_{i,j})=(1,0)
,

(c)

with probability
1-\boldsymbol{t}_{j}(\boldsymbol{a}_{i})
set
(\mathbf{M}_{i,j},\widetilde{\mathbf{M}}_{i,j})=(0,0)
.

Using the above coupling, we have

\displaystyle d_{\mathrm{TV}}(\mathcal{D}_{\mathsf{sim}}(\eta,\Lambda),{\mathcal{D}}_{\mathsf{sim}}(\eta,\tilde{\Lambda}))

\displaystyle\leq\mathop{{\bf Pr}\/}_{(\mathbf{M},\widetilde{\mathbf{M}})\sim\mathbf{T}^{*}}[\mathbf{M}\neq\widetilde{\mathbf{M}}]\leq\sum_{(i,j)\in[s]\times[q]}\mathop{{\bf Pr}\/}_{(\mathbf{M},\widetilde{\mathbf{M}})\sim\mathbf{T}^{*}}[\mathbf{M}_{i,j}\neq\widetilde{\mathbf{M}}_{i,j}]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{(i,j)\in[s]\times[q]}\mathop{{\bf E}\/}_{(\boldsymbol{t}_{j},\tilde{\boldsymbol{t}}_{j})\sim\mathbf{T}}\mathop{{\bf E}\/}_{\boldsymbol{a}_{i}\sim\eta}[|\boldsymbol{t}_{j}(\boldsymbol{a}_{i})-\tilde{\boldsymbol{t}}_{j}(\boldsymbol{a}_{i})|]

\displaystyle=\sum_{(i,j)\in[s]\times[q]}d_{\mathrm{EM}}^{\eta}(\Lambda,\tilde{\Lambda})\leq\epsilon.

Next, we handle the setting where we only have a distribution
\tilde{\eta}
that is close to
\eta
in the variation distance.

Lemma 4.3
.

For any
q,s\in\mathbbm{N}
,
\epsilon\in(0,1)
,
if
d_{\mathrm{TV}}(\eta,\tilde{\eta})\leq\frac{\epsilon}{s}
, then
d_{\mathrm{TV}}(\mathcal{D}_{\mathsf{sim}}(\eta,\Lambda),\mathcal{D}_{\mathsf{sim}}(\tilde{\eta},\Lambda))\leq\epsilon
.

Proof:
Let
\mathbf{T}
be an optimal coupling (transfer distribution) between
\eta
and
\tilde{\eta}
. Namely,
d_{\mathrm{TV}}(\eta,\tilde{\eta})=\operatorname{{\bf Pr}}_{(\boldsymbol{a},\tilde{\boldsymbol{a}})\sim\mathbf{T}}[\boldsymbol{a}\neq\tilde{\boldsymbol{a}}]
. We construct a coupling
\mathbf{T}^{*}
between
\mathcal{D}_{\mathsf{sim}}(\eta,\Lambda)
and
\mathcal{D}_{\mathsf{sim}}(\tilde{\eta},\Lambda)
. First, draw
(\boldsymbol{a}_{1},\tilde{\boldsymbol{a}}_{1}),\ldots,(\boldsymbol{a}_{s},\tilde{\boldsymbol{a}}_{s})\sim\mathbf{T}
and
\boldsymbol{t}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{t}_{q}\sim\Lambda
, all independently. We define
\mathbf{M}
and
\widetilde{\mathbf{M}}
as follows: For every
(i,j)\in[s]\times[q]
, if
\boldsymbol{a}_{i}=\tilde{\boldsymbol{a}}_{i}
, then draw a bit
\boldsymbol{b}\sim\operatorname{Ber}\left(\boldsymbol{t}_{j}(\boldsymbol{a}_{i})\right)
and set
\mathbf{M}_{i,j}=\widetilde{\mathbf{M}}_{i,j}=\boldsymbol{b}
. If
\boldsymbol{a}_{i}\neq\tilde{\boldsymbol{a}}_{i}
, then independently set
\mathbf{M}_{i,j}\sim\operatorname{Ber}(\boldsymbol{t}_{j}(\boldsymbol{a}_{i}))
and
\widetilde{\mathbf{M}}_{i,j}\sim\operatorname{Ber}(\boldsymbol{t}_{j}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{a}}_{i}))
.
Then,

\displaystyle d_{\mathrm{TV}}(\mathcal{D}_{\mathsf{sim}}(\eta,\Lambda),\mathcal{D}_{\mathsf{sim}}(\tilde{\eta},\Lambda))\leq\mathop{{\bf Pr}\/}_{(\mathbf{M},\widetilde{\mathbf{M}})\sim\mathbf{T}^{*}}[\mathbf{M}\neq\widetilde{\mathbf{M}}]\leq\sum_{i\in[s]}\operatorname{{\bf Pr}}_{(\boldsymbol{a}_{i},\tilde{\boldsymbol{a}}_{i})\sim\mathbf{T}}[\boldsymbol{a}_{i}\neq\tilde{\boldsymbol{a}}_{i}]\leq\epsilon.

The following lemma bundles together the approximation results in this section.

Lemma 4.4
.

Fix
s,q\in\mathbbm{N}
and
\epsilon\in(0,1)
and suppose that
d_{\mathrm{EM}}^{\eta}(\Lambda,\tilde{\Lambda})\leq\epsilon/3sq
and
d_{\mathrm{TV}}(\eta,\tilde{\eta})\leq\epsilon/3s
. If
n\geq 18q^{2}(s+1)/\epsilon
and
\xi
is
\left(\frac{\epsilon}{9(s+1)},q\right)
-good then
d_{\mathrm{TV}}(\mathcal{D}_{\mathsf{sim}}(\tilde{\eta},\tilde{\Lambda}),\mathcal{D}_{\mathsf{test}})\leq\epsilon
.

Proof:
First note that by the triangle inequality

\displaystyle d_{\mathrm{TV}}\left(\mathcal{D}_{\mathsf{sim}}(\tilde{\eta},\tilde{\Lambda}),\mathcal{D}_{\mathsf{test}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq d_{\mathrm{TV}}\left(\mathcal{D}_{\mathsf{sim}}(\tilde{\eta},\tilde{\Lambda}),\mathcal{D}_{\mathsf{sim}}(\eta,\tilde{\Lambda})\right)+d_{\mathrm{TV}}\left(\mathcal{D}_{\mathsf{sim}}({\eta},\tilde{\Lambda}),\mathcal{D}_{\mathsf{sim}}(\eta,\Lambda)\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+d_{\mathrm{TV}}\left(\mathcal{D}_{\mathsf{sim}}(\eta,\Lambda),\mathcal{D}_{\mathsf{test}}\right).

Applying Lemma
4.3
, Lemma
4.2
and Lemma
4.1
, all with
\epsilon/3
instead of
\epsilon
, gives that every term in the above sum is bounded by
\epsilon/3
.

\textsf{Accept-Probability}(s,q,\eta,\Lambda,{\epsilon^{\prime}})

\eta

\Lambda

\xi

Lemma 4.5
.

Fix
\epsilon^{\prime}\in(0,1)
and let
\mathcal{P}
be an index-invariant property that admits a canonical tester with proximity parameter
\epsilon^{\prime}
using sample complexity
s=s(\epsilon^{\prime})

. Fix
\epsilon\in(0,1)
, and let

(\eta,\Lambda)
be the parameters of an
\left(\frac{\epsilon}{9(s+1)},q\right)

\mu

, we have
d_{\mathrm{TV}}(\mathcal{D}_{\mathsf{sim}}(\tilde{\eta},\tilde{\Lambda}),\mathcal{D}_{\mathsf{test}})\leq\epsilon
. We next analyze the calculated quantity
\widetilde{\textsf{acc}}_{\epsilon^{\prime}}(\eta,\Lambda)

\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}

5
Finding a weakly robust detailing and estimating its parameters

This section is devoted to finding a variable set defining a (weakly) robust partition, and then to estimating its weight and type distributions. The final estimation algorithm will make all its queries deploying the algorithms developed here.

5.1
Estimating the index of a detailing

In this section, we describe and analyze an algorithm for estimating the index of a detailing (Figure
3
).

Procedure
\textsf{Estimate-Index}(U,\kappa,\gamma)

Input:
Sample and query access to a distribution
\mu
over
\{0,1\}^{n}
, a set of indices
U\subseteq[n]
, and parameters
\kappa,\gamma\in(0,1)
.

Output:
An estimate
\widetilde{\mathrm{Ind}}(\mu^{U})
for
\mathrm{Ind}(\mu^{U})
such that
\left|{\widetilde{\mathrm{Ind}}(\mu^{U})-\mathrm{Ind}(\mu^{U})}\right|\leq\kappa

1-\gamma
.

1.

Take a multi-set of samples
\mathbf{S}
from
\mu
with
|{\mathbf{S}}|=\left\lceil 1000\cdot\frac{2^{2|{U}|}}{\kappa^{3}}\log\left(\frac{2^{|U|}}{\kappa\gamma}\right)\right\rceil
.

2.

Construct the multi-set of vectors
\mathbf{S}|_{U}
by restricting the samples over the indices
U
.

3.

For every

\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\eta}}(v)=\frac{|\left\{{\boldsymbol{s}}\in\mathbf{S}:{\boldsymbol{s}}_{{U}}=v\right\}|}{|\mathbf{S}|}.

Sample a set of indices uniformly at random without replacement
\mathbf{I}\subseteq[n]
where

For every

For every
v\in\{0,1\}^{|U|}

\widetilde{\boldsymbol{t}}_{v}=\frac{1}{|{\mathbf{I}}|}\cdot\sum\limits_{i\in\mathbf{I}}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{t}}_{i,v}^{2}
.

.

Lemma 5.1

.

.

.

Lemma 5.4

(Approximating

’s)

Lemma 5.6

\displaystyle\mathop{{\bf Pr}\/}\left[|\widetilde{\boldsymbol{t}}_{v}-\boldsymbol{t}_{v}^{\prime}|>\frac{\kappa}{10}\right]

(7)

5.2
Verifying and searching for a weakly robust detailing

In this section we will show how to obtain a weakly robust detailing with respect to a set of variables. We first describe a procedure that will be used as a single step towards this goal.

Procedure
\textsf{Test-Weakly-Robust-Detailing}(\delta,k,\gamma,U)

Input:
Sample and query access to a distribution
\mu
over
\{0,1\}^{n}
,
U\subseteq[n]
, parameters
k\in[n]
and
\gamma\in(0,1)
.

Output:
Either
Accept
or a set
U^{\prime}\subseteq[n]\setminus U
with
|{U^{\prime}}|\leq k
.

1.

Set
\widetilde{\mathrm{Ind}}(\mu^{U})=\textsf{Estimate-Index}(U,\delta/10,\gamma/3)
.

2.

Let
\mathcal{L}=\{U^{\prime}\subseteq[n]\setminus U:\ |U^{\prime}|\leq k\}
.

3.

Sample
r=\left\lceil\frac{\log(3/\gamma)}{\delta}\right\rceil
sets
\mathbf{U}^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{U}^{\prime}_{r}\in\mathcal{L}
uniformly at random.

4.

For
i=1
to
r
do:

(a)

Set
\widetilde{\mathrm{Ind}}(\mu^{U\cup\mathbf{U}^{\prime}_{i}})=\textsf{Estimate-Index}(U\cup\mathbf{U}^{\prime}_{i},\delta/10,\gamma/3r)
.

(b)

If
\widetilde{\mathrm{Ind}}(\mu^{U\cup\mathbf{U}^{\prime}_{i}})-\widetilde{\mathrm{Ind}}(\mu^{U})>7\delta/10,

Return
\mathbf{U}^{\prime}_{i}
.

5.

Return Accept
.

Test-Weakly-Robust-Detailing

Lemma 5.10
.

Fix
\gamma,\delta\in(0,1)
,
U\subseteq[n]
,
k\in[n]
.
Consider
Test-Weakly-Robust-Detailing
(\delta,k,\gamma,U)

1-\gamma

U
or reports some
U^{\prime}\subseteq[n]\setminus U
such that
|{U^{\prime}}|\leq k
, satisfying the following:

1.

If
\mu^{U}
is not
(\delta,k)
-weakly robust, then
Test-Weakly-Robust-Detailing
does not accept and outputs some
U^{\prime}
;

2.

If
Test-Weakly-Robust-Detailing
outputs some
U^{\prime}
, then
\mathrm{Ind}(\mu^{U\cup U^{\prime}})>\mathrm{Ind}(\mu^{U})+4\delta/10
.

.

Proof:

We start by proving (
1

5.1
, we have that with probability at least
1-\gamma/3
,
|\widetilde{\mathrm{Ind}}(\mu^{U})-\mathrm{Ind}(\mu^{U})|\leq\delta/10
. In addition, since

is not
(\delta,k)
-weakly robust, we have that for at least
\delta|\mathcal{L}|
of the sets

. Therefore, with probability at least

is sampled in Step

5.1
and a union bound, we have that with probability at least
1-\gamma/3

it holds that
|\widetilde{\mathrm{Ind}}(\mu^{U\cup\mathbf{U}^{\prime}_{i}})-\mathrm{Ind}(\mu^{U\cup\mathbf{U}^{\prime}_{i}})|\leq\delta/10
. Therefore, in particular,

\widetilde{\mathrm{Ind}}(\mu^{U\cup\mathbf{U}^{*}})-\widetilde{\mathrm{Ind}}(\mu^{U})\geq{\mathrm{Ind}}(\mu^{U\cup\mathbf{U}^{*}})-\mathrm{Ind}(\mu^{U})-\frac{2\delta}{10}\geq\frac{8\delta}{10},

and
\mathbf{U}^{*}
(or some other set) will be returned by the algorithm. A union bound over the above three events completes the proof of this item.

To prove (
2
), note that when some
\mathbf{U}^{\prime}
is returned, we have that
\widetilde{\mathrm{Ind}}(\mu^{U\cup\mathbf{U}^{\prime}})-\widetilde{\mathrm{Ind}}(\mu^{U})>7\delta/10
. By Lemma
5.1
, similarly to item (

), with probability at least

Conditioned on this, if

, then we have

which is a contradiction to
\mathbf{U}^{\prime}
being reported by the algorithm.

To finish the proof we bound the total number of queries that the algorithm makes. Note that the algorithm uses one call to
Estimate-Index
with
U
and the rest of the

calls to
Estimate-Index
with a set

which can be of size at most

. Therefore, the total number of queries the algorithm makes is at most

.

\mu
over
\{0,1\}^{n}
,

.

For every

\displaystyle=\mathop{{\bf E}\/}_{i\sim[n]}\left[\mathop{{\bf E}\/}_{(\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b})\sim\eta}\left[\left(\mathop{{\bf Pr}\/}_{\boldsymbol{x}\sim\xi^{\prime}|^{2,3:(\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b})}_{1}}[\boldsymbol{x}_{i}=1]-\mathop{{\bf Pr}\/}_{\boldsymbol{x}\sim\xi|^{2:\boldsymbol{a}}_{1}}[\boldsymbol{x}_{i}=1]\right)^{2}\right]\right].

\displaystyle d_{\mathrm{EM}}(\Xi|_{1},\Xi|_{2})

\displaystyle\leq\mathop{{\bf E}\/}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})\sim\Xi|_{1,2}}[d_{H}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})]=\mathop{{\bf E}\/}_{(\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b})\sim\eta}\left[\mathop{{\bf E}\/}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})\sim\Xi|^{3:(\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b})}}\left[d_{H}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})\right]\right]

\displaystyle=\mathop{{\bf E}\/}_{i\sim[n]}\left[\mathop{{\bf E}\/}_{(\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b})\sim\eta}\left[\mathop{{\bf Pr}\/}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})\sim\Xi|^{3:(\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b})}}[\boldsymbol{x}_{i}=0\wedge\boldsymbol{y}_{i}=1]+\mathop{{\bf Pr}\/}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})\sim\Xi|^{3:(\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b})}}[\boldsymbol{x}_{i}=1\wedge\boldsymbol{y}_{i}=0]\right]\right].

(12)

For a fixed
i\in[n]
and
(a,b)\in A\times B
we have that

\displaystyle\mathop{{\bf Pr}\/}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})\sim\Xi|^{3:(a,b)}}[\boldsymbol{x}_{i}=0\wedge\boldsymbol{y}_{i}=1]

\displaystyle=\mathop{{\bf Pr}\/}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})\sim\Xi|^{3:(a,b)}}[\boldsymbol{y}_{i}=1\mid\boldsymbol{x}_{i}=0]\cdot\mathop{{\bf Pr}\/}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})\sim\Xi|^{3:(a,b)}}[\boldsymbol{x}_{i}=0]

\displaystyle=\left(1-(H(i))_{1}(a,b)\right)\cdot\max\left\{0,\frac{(H(i))_{2}(a,b)-(H(i))_{1}(a,b)}{1-(H(i))_{1}(a,b)}\right\}

\displaystyle=\max\left\{0,{(H(i))_{2}(a,b)-(H(i))_{1}(a,b)}\right\},

and similarly,

\mathop{{\bf Pr}\/}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})\sim\Xi|^{3:(a,b)}}[\boldsymbol{x}_{i}=1\wedge\boldsymbol{y}_{i}=0]=\max\left\{0,(H(i))_{1}(a,b)-(H(i))_{2}(a,b)\right\}.

Therefore,

\mathop{{\bf Pr}\/}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})\sim\Xi|^{3:(a,b)}}[\boldsymbol{x}_{i}=0\wedge\boldsymbol{y}_{i}=1]+\mathop{{\bf Pr}\/}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})\sim\Xi|^{3:(a,b)}}[\boldsymbol{x}_{i}=1\wedge\boldsymbol{y}_{i}=0]=\Big|(H(i))_{2}(a,b)-(H(i))_{1}(a,b)\Big|.

Plugging into Inequality (
12
), we have the final result:

\displaystyle d_{\mathrm{EM}}(\Xi|_{1},\Xi|_{2})

\displaystyle\leq\mathop{{\bf E}\/}_{i\sim[n]}\left[\mathop{{\bf E}\/}_{(\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b})\sim\eta}\left[\Big|(H(i))_{2}(\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b})-(H(i))_{1}(\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b})\Big|\right]\right].

The final and rather important property of the output distribution of
Change-types
is the preservation of goodness. Namely, that if
\xi
was an
(\epsilon,q)
-good detailing of the unknown input distribution
\mu
, then
\Xi|_{2,3}
will be an
(\epsilon,q)
-good detailing of the “new”
\tau
.

Lemma 6.9
.

Fix
\epsilon\in(0,1)
and
q\in\mathbbm{N}
. Let
\xi
be a detailing of
\mu
with respect to
A
, and let
\eta
be a distribution over
A\times B
which is a detailing of
\xi|_{2}
with respect to
B
. If
\xi
is
(\epsilon,q)
-good, then the detailing
\Xi|_{2,3}
of
\tau=\Xi|_{2}
, as obtained by the output distribution of
Change-types
, is also
(\epsilon,q)
-good.

Proof:

Since
\xi
is
(\epsilon,q)
-good, there exists
J\subseteq A
with
\mathop{{\bf Pr}\/}_{\xi|_{2}}[J]>1-\epsilon
such that for any
a\in J
, at least
(1-\epsilon)n^{q}
of the
q
-tuples in
[n]^{q}
are
\epsilon
-independent. Consider the flat extension
\xi_{\langle\eta\rangle}
with respect to
\eta
. Since
\xi_{\langle\eta\rangle}
is a flat extension, for any
a\in J
and
\alpha\in[n]^{q}
which is
\epsilon
-independent with respect to
\xi|_{1}^{2:a}
, the tuple
\alpha
is also
\epsilon
-independent with respect to
\xi_{\langle\eta\rangle}|_{1}^{2:(a,b)}=\xi|^{2:a}
for all
b\in B
.

Next we claim that if a tuple
\alpha\in[n]^{q}
is
\epsilon
-independent with respect to
\xi_{\langle\eta\rangle}|_{1}^{2:(a,b)}
, then it is also
\epsilon
-independent with respect to
\nu\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rm def}}{{=}}\Xi|^{3:(a,b)}_{2}
. Let
(j_{1},\ldots,j_{q})\in[n]^{q}
be an
\epsilon
-independent tuple with respect to
\xi_{\langle\eta\rangle}|_{1}^{2:(a,b)}
. Then, we have that

d_{\mathrm{TV}}\left(\left(\xi_{\langle\eta\rangle}|_{1}^{2:(a,b)}\right)|_{\{j_{1},\ldots,j_{q}\}},\prod_{\ell\in[q]}\left(\xi_{\langle\eta\rangle}|_{1}^{2:(a,b)}\right)|_{j_{\ell}}\right)\leq\epsilon.

Let
\sigma
denote an optimal coupling from the distribution
\left(\xi_{\langle\eta\rangle}|_{1}^{2:(a,b)}\right)|_{\{j_{1},\ldots,j_{q}\}}
to the corresponding product distribution
\prod_{\ell\in[q]}\left(\xi_{\langle\eta\rangle}|_{1}^{2:(a,b)}\right)|_{j_{\ell}}
, in the sense that

d_{\mathrm{TV}}\left(\left(\xi_{\langle\eta\rangle}|_{1}^{2:(a,b)}\right)|_{\{j_{1},\ldots,j_{q}\}},\prod_{\ell\in[q]}\left(\xi_{\langle\eta\rangle}|_{1}^{2:(a,b)}\right)|_{j_{\ell}}\right)=\mathop{{\bf E}\/}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})\sim\sigma}\left[\boldsymbol{1}_{\{\boldsymbol{x}\neq\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}\}}\right]

We construct a coupling
\sigma^{\prime}
from the distribution
\nu|_{\{j_{1},\ldots,j_{q}\}}
to the corresponding product distribution
\prod_{\ell\in[q]}\nu|_{j_{\ell}}
and and prove that

\mathop{{\bf E}\/}_{(\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime})\sim\sigma^{\prime}}\left[\boldsymbol{1}_{\{\boldsymbol{y}\neq\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}\}}\right]\leq\mathop{{\bf E}\/}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})\sim\sigma}\left[\boldsymbol{1}_{\{\boldsymbol{x}\neq\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}\}}\right].

We define the coupling
\sigma^{\prime}
as follows. We first sample
(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})\sim\sigma
. Then for every
\ell\in[q]
, if
\boldsymbol{x}_{j_{\ell}}=\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}_{j_{\ell}}
then we apply Step (
2
) of
Change-types
for
\boldsymbol{x}_{j_{\ell}}
to obtain
\boldsymbol{y}_{j_{\ell}}
and set
\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}_{j_{\ell}}=\boldsymbol{y}_{j_{\ell}}
(this means that we “use the same random coins” for handling
\boldsymbol{x}_{j_{\ell}}
and
\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}_{j_{\ell}}
). If
\boldsymbol{x}_{j_{\ell}}\neq\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}_{j_{\ell}}
then we just apply Step (
2
) of
Change-types
separately for
\boldsymbol{x}_{j_{\ell}}
to obtain
\boldsymbol{y}_{j_{\ell}}
and for
\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}_{j_{\ell}}
to obtain
\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}_{j_{\ell}}
(note that in this case only one of the two applications uses randomness). The last two things to note are that
\boldsymbol{y}
has the same distribution as the output of
Change-types
over
\boldsymbol{x}
restricted to
\{j_{1},\ldots,j_{q}\}
, while
\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}
distributes as the corresponding product, and that under this process
\boldsymbol{x}=\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}
implies
\boldsymbol{y}=\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}
. Hence
\mathop{{\bf E}\/}_{(\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime})\sim\sigma^{\prime}}[\boldsymbol{1}_{\{\boldsymbol{y}\neq\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}\}}]\leq\mathop{{\bf E}\/}_{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})\sim\sigma}[\boldsymbol{1}_{\{\boldsymbol{x}\neq\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}\}}]
, as required.

Since the above argument holds for any
\epsilon
-independent tuple with respect to
\xi_{\langle\eta\rangle}|_{1}^{2:(a,b)}
for any
(a,b)\in A\times B
(showing the tuple to be
\epsilon
-independent also for
\Xi|_{2}^{3:(a,b)}
), it implies that
(\epsilon,q)
-goodness is indeed transferred from
\xi_{\langle\eta\rangle}
to
\Xi|_{2,3}
.

Our application to this lemma would be to show that predictability still holds for the resulting detailing of
\tau
(with respect to the weight distribution
\eta
and the type distribution
\Lambda_{t}
), just as it held for a good enough detailing
\xi
of
\mu
.
