Comparative evaluation of disease activity measures for adult-onset Still’s disease: a multicentre, retrospective, cohort study
Authors: Stefan Vordenbäumen, Tim Filla, Rhea Friedrich, Norbert Blank, Diana Ernst, Jörg Henes, Gernot Keyßer, Philipp Klemm, Martin Krusche, Anna Meinecke, Jürgen Rech, Nils Schulz, Simon Michael Petzinna, Anne Pankow, Valentin S Schäfer, Alexander Pfeil, Sebastian Klapa, Georg Pongratz, Eugen Feist, Anna Kernder
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo comparatively evaluate recent adult-onset Still’s disease (AOSD) activity measures—Development and Validation of Disease Activity in AOSD (DAVID), Still’s Disease Activity Score (SDAS), Still Activity Score (SAS) with Pouchot’s Systemic Score (PSS) in an independent multicentre cohort.MethodsDAVID and SDAS were modified (m) by omitting global assessments due to availability and to circumvent incorporation bias. Criterion validity was assessed by receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis against the treating physician’s determination of disease activity (active vs inactive). Responsiveness was evaluated by comparing mean change in scores between remission and active disease, and Cohen’s d. Longitudinal sensitivity to change was assessed using linear mixed-effects models with z-standardised scores and marginal R². Primary analysis uses imputed data, with complete-case sensitivity analysis.ResultsThe cohort comprised 86 patients with initially active AOSD (64% female; mean age 39.4±15.0 years). In ROC analyses, mSDAS, mDAVID and SAS showed comparable area under the curves (0.69, 0.68 and 0.68, respectively), whereas PSS had a lower AUC (0.60). Responsiveness was greatest for mDAVID (Cohen’s d 0.76 (95% CI 0.30 to 1.20)), followed by mSDAS (0.61 (0.13 to 1.1)), SAS (0.46 (−0.01 to 0.90)) and PSS (0.22 (−0.30 to 0.68)). Longitudinal sensitivity to change, quantified by marginal R², was highest for mDAVID (0.65 (95% CI 0.59 to 0.70)), followed by mSDAS (0.61 (0.55 to 0.67)), PSS (0.61 (0.54 to 0.67)) and SAS (0.55 (0.45 to 0.64)). Sensitivity analyses using complete cases yielded similar results.ConclusionmDAVID and mSDAS demonstrated the most favourable and largely comparable performance profiles across the evaluated metrics, with mDAVID showing greater responsiveness and mSDAS demonstrating a stronger correlation with the articular disease domain.Trial registration numberISRCTN86135778.Keywords: Still's Disease, Adult-Onset; Inflammation; Biomarkers

Introduction

Adult-onset Still’s disease (AOSD) is a potentially life-threatening autoinflammatory disease typically characterised by intermittent fever, evanescent rash and arthritis, with possible complications including serositis, myocarditis, interstitial lung disease and macrophage activation syndrome.1 2 The corresponding illness with childhood onset has historically been referred to as Still’s disease or systemic juvenile idiopathic arthritis (sJIA). Recent European Alliance of Associations for Rheumatology (EULAR) recommendations for AOSD propose unifying both entities under the umbrella term ‘Still’s disease’ because of largely overlapping genetic predisposition, clinical features and therapeutic responses.2 3

Even before this conceptual unification, marketing authorisation of effective medications for AOSD in some countries was granted based on extrapolation from sJIA trials, as randomised controlled trials in adults frequently failed to meet their primary endpoints,4–6 despite showing clear clinical efficacy in subsequent analyses.7–10 The absence of uniform, representative and consensus-based disease activity endpoints has been identified as a key obstacle to clinical research and drug development in AOSD.2 11 12

Traditionally, Pouchot’s Systemic Score (PSS)—originally developed as a severity score13 and predictive of a complicated disease course14—has been used as a surrogate activity measure in AOSD, often alongside composite indices designed for rheumatoid arthritis.4 5 However, these instruments do not adequately capture the systemic nature of AOSD, have not performed well in clinical trials and lack validation for activity assessment. This has recently prompted the development of designated AOSD activity measures: a joint group of specialists from Türkiye proposed the Still Activity Score (SAS),14–16 a group from Algeria proposed the Still’s Disease Activity Score (SDAS),17 while an international European expert group developed a measure during the initiative ‘Development and Validation of Disease Activity in AOSD’ (DAVID).18

Nevertheless, these scores were derived in different settings, have varying content and weighting of clinical and laboratory domains and have not been directly compared in an independent cohort. It is therefore unclear which instrument provides the most valid and responsive quantification of AOSD disease activity and which is best suited as a standardised endpoint for clinical trials and drug development. To address this gap, we aimed to comparatively evaluate SAS, SDAS and DAVID against PSS in an external, well-characterised multicentre cohort of patients with AOSD.7 Specifically, we assessed their criterion validity against physicians’ global assessments of disease activity (PhGA), responsiveness to clinical change and longitudinal sensitivity to change, with the goal of supporting the implementation of a uniform and robust activity instrument in clinical research and regulatory trials.

Methods

Study design and participants

This multicentre, retrospective cohort study included patients with AOSD from 16 German secondary and tertiary rheumatology centres. Eligible patients had active AOSD and were not receiving any maintenance treatment, including glucocorticoids, between 1 January 2007 and 30 September 2022. The cohort has been described in detail previously.7 Briefly, demographic, clinical, laboratory, treatment and outcome data were collected at onset (baseline), at week 12 (acceptable range 6–24 weeks) and at week 72 (acceptable range 60–100 weeks).

The study was registered in the ISRCTN registry prior to data collection (ISRCTN86135778, https://www.isrctn.com/ISRCTN86135778) for an analysis of treatment strategies.

Activity scores and outcomes

The primary objective of this study was to comparatively evaluate four AOSD activity scores. Importantly, DAVID and SDAS were modified (m) due to data availability and to circumvent incorporation bias. The scores were defined as follows:

PSS13: PSS is a sum score based on the presence of fever, rash, pleuritis, pneumonitis, pericarditis, hepatopathy, hepatosplenomegaly, lymphadenopathy, leucocytosis, pharyngodynia, myalgia and abdominal pain (range 0–12).

PSS13: PSS is a sum score based on the presence of fever, rash, pleuritis, pneumonitis, pericarditis, hepatopathy, hepatosplenomegaly, lymphadenopathy, leucocytosis, pharyngodynia, myalgia and abdominal pain (range 0–12).

SAS15: SAS is a sum score assigning points for the presence of fever (2 points), arthralgia (2 points), arthritis (one additional point if arthralgia is present), neutrophilia >65% (1 point) and ferritin >350 ng/mL (2 points) (range 0–7).

SAS15: SAS is a sum score assigning points for the presence of fever (2 points), arthralgia (2 points), arthritis (one additional point if arthralgia is present), neutrophilia >65% (1 point) and ferritin >350 ng/mL (2 points) (range 0–7).

Modified version of the SDAS17: SDAS is a sum score with fever ≥38.5°C (1 point), rash (1 point), arthralgia (1 point), one to three swollen joints (two points), ≥4 swollen joints SJC (three points), PhGA VAS ≥5/10 or a raise in PhGA VAS ≥2/10 (3 points), patient global assessment (PtGA) VAS ≥5 or a raise in PtGA VAS ≥2/10 (1 point) and C reactive protein (CRP) >10 mg/L (1 point) or CRP >100 mg/L (2 points). The original score may be interpreted as inactive disease (0–1 point), low disease activity (2–3 points), moderate disease activity (4–7 points), severe disease activity (≥8 points). Since the PhGA of active versus inactive disease was used as gold standard, we omitted physician and patient global to derive the mSDAS and render the scores comparable.

Modified version of the SDAS17: SDAS is a sum score with fever ≥38.5°C (1 point), rash (1 point), arthralgia (1 point), one to three swollen joints (two points), ≥4 swollen joints SJC (three points), PhGA VAS ≥5/10 or a raise in PhGA VAS ≥2/10 (3 points), patient global assessment (PtGA) VAS ≥5 or a raise in PtGA VAS ≥2/10 (1 point) and C reactive protein (CRP) >10 mg/L (1 point) or CRP >100 mg/L (2 points). The original score may be interpreted as inactive disease (0–1 point), low disease activity (2–3 points), moderate disease activity (4–7 points), severe disease activity (≥8 points). Since the PhGA of active versus inactive disease was used as gold standard, we omitted physician and patient global to derive the mSDAS and render the scores comparable.

Modified version of DAVID18: in the original definition, DAVID is a categorical measure. ‘Active disease’ is defined by the presence of fever and at least one of the following: rash, arthritis, CRP >10 mg/L or PtGA ≥2/10; alternatively, by the absence of fever and at least three of these findings. Low disease activity is defined as one finding without fever; remission corresponds to the absence of all findings. For this study, DAVID was adapted for several reasons. First, PtGA was not available in our cohort and was therefore omitted. Furthermore, PhGA and PtGA are closely correlated in AOSD,16 thereby potentially introducing an incorporation bias due to physician determined remission status being used as the gold standard outcome criterion. The original publication reported a slightly reduced but still acceptable performance without PtGA in sensitivity analyses.18 Second, to obtain a numerical score, we ordered and categorised DAVID and assigned points as follows: ‘active with fever’ (5), ‘active without fever’ (4), ‘moderate disease activity’ (3), ‘low disease activity’ (2) and ‘inactive’ (1). The category ‘moderate disease activity’ is not part of the original definition but was introduced in this study to capture patients without fever but with more than one finding. As a sensitivity analysis, longitudinal analyses were additionally performed using a binary mDAVID variable, contrasting ‘active’ (with or without fever) versus ‘remission/low disease activity’.

The criterion (‘gold standard’) for disease activity was the treating physician’s determination of disease status (active vs inactive) at onset and week 72.

Statistical analysis

To assess criterion validity, we compared each score with the physician’s determination of disease status (active vs inactive disease) at week 72, which was available within the cohort at this time point, and was therefore used as gold standard. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis was used to calculate the area under the curve (AUC) with 95% CIs. ROC curves for the scores were displayed in a single plot. AUCs were compared between scores using the method described by Venkatraman.19

To investigate whether the four composite scores reflect distinct underlying dimensions of disease activity and to identify groups of items capturing similar latent components, we conducted an exploratory factor analysis (EFA) on the binary item-level data using maximum likelihood extraction and oblique rotation (oblimin), with tetrachoric correlations computed internally (R package psych, function fa, cor = “tet”), assuming that each binary item reflects an underlying continuous latent variable. All items contributing to at least one of the three scores, and which were present at least once in the cohort, were included. The number of components was determined using both statistical and clinical criteria. Statistically, we considered eigenvalues >1.0 and inspected the scree plot based on the tetrachoric correlation matrix. Clinically, we evaluated the interpretability and plausibility of different factor solutions. Based on these criteria, a two-component solution was selected as optimal. For the final two-component model, factor loadings were examined to characterise the latent dimensions and to identify clusters of items representing similar clinical or laboratory domains. Individual factor scores for each patient were then estimated using regression-based scoring. To quantify how strongly each composite score reflects the identified components, we calculated Spearman’s rank correlation coefficients between the factor scores and each of the sum scores. Two-sided t-tests were used to assess the statistical significance.

To evaluate responsiveness, we z-standardised all three scores (mean 0, SD 1) and calculated the mean change from baseline to week 72 separately for patients in remission and those with active disease at week 72 according to the treating physician. Standardised mean differences (Cohen’s d) with 95% CIs were computed to quantify the magnitude of change; larger absolute values were interpreted as indicating greater responsiveness.

To assess longitudinal sensitivity to change, we fitted linear mixed-effects models for each score (PSS, SAS, mSDAS and numerical mDAVID) with random intercepts for patients. The general model specification was: ‘activity_score~time×therapy+time×physician_activity+(1 | ID)’, where ‘activity_score’ denotes the z-standardised PSS, SAS, mSDAS or mDAVID score; ‘therapy’ the treatment category (methotrexate vs biologic therapy); ‘physician_activity’ (active vs inactive) and ‘time’ the measurement occasion (baseline, week 12, week 72). From these models, we obtained marginal R² according to Nakagawa and Schielzeth,20 with higher marginal R² indicating a greater proportion of variance explained by the fixed effects (time, therapy and physician-assessed activity). Ninety-five per cent CIs for marginal R² were derived via parametric bootstrap with 1000 simulations.

As a sensitivity analysis, we additionally fitted a generalised linear mixed-effects model with a binomial link using the binarised mDAVID outcome as originally intended (active vs remission/low disease activity) and the same fixed and random-effects structure.

Missing data on CRP, leucocyte count, neutrophil count and ferritin at baseline, week 12 and week 72 were imputed prior to score calculation using predictive mean matching. The number of imputed values was as follows: CRP (n=1, 10, 12 at baseline, week 12 and week 72, respectively), leucocyte count (n=13, 17, 19), neutrophil count (n=25, 32, 33) and ferritin (n=4, 18, 31). As a sensitivity analysis, all primary analyses were repeated in the complete-case dataset.

All analyses were performed using R (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria; V.4.1.2).

Results

Patient characteristics and distribution of activity scores

The cohort consisted of 86 patients with initially active AOSD (64% female; mean age 39.4±15.0 years) fulfilling modified Yamaguchi criteria, with follow-up assessments at weeks 12 and 72. The cohort has been described in detail previously.7 Baseline characteristics are summarised in table 1. At week 72, the scores were positively skewed as expected, with the majority of patients being in lower disease activity or remission state (online supplemental figure 1). The AOSD scores correlated strongly (mSDAS vs SAS Spearman’s correlation coefficient (r)=0.81; mDAVID vs mSDAS r=0.80; mDAVID vs PSS: r=0.75; mDAVID vs SAS r=0.69) to moderately (mSDAS vs PSS r=0.58; SAS vs PSS r=0.49, all p<0.0001) among each other.

Criterion validity

Criterion validity of the activity scores was assessed against the treating physician’s determination of disease status (‘active’ vs ‘inactive’). In ROC analyses, mSDAS, mDAVID and SAS all showed a similar AUC of 0.69, 0.68 and 0.68, respectively, whereas PSS had a lower AUC of 0.60 (figure 1, results and cut-off values detailed in table 2). Similar results were observed in the sensitivity analysis restricted to complete cases (online supplemental table 1).

To explore potential reasons for the observed moderate discrimination compared with the physician’s determination of disease status, additional exploratory analyses were conducted: patients who were categorised as active based on the scores numerically showed higher scores when considered to be active by the physicians compared with those considered to be inactive (PSS 1.7±1.1 vs 1.3±0.8, p (t-test)=0.3; SAS 3.6±1.4 vs 3.1±1.1, p=0.3; mSDAS 2.4±1.2 vs 1.6±0.9, p=0.04; mDAVID 3.0±1.4 vs 2.4±0.8, p=0.1, respectively). Furthermore, patients who were categorised as inactive based on the scores tended to be more often off any immunosuppressive medication when considered to be inactive by the physicians as opposed to those considered to be active (PSS 80% vs 20%, p (Fisher’s exact test)=1.0; SAS 75% vs 25%, p=0.5; mSDAS 75% vs 25%, p=1.0; DAVID 75% vs 25%, p=1.0).

To investigate whether the AOSD activity scores reflect distinct dimensions of disease activity, thereby accounting for differences in the observed criterion validity, a latent component analysis of disease activity was performed using all single items contained within the three scores. The two components thus identified clinically represented an arthritic/leucocytosis phenotype and a systemic inflammation/lymphoproliferative phenotype (online supplemental table 2). Among the AOSD activity scores, mDAVID and mSDAS showed moderate to strong associations with both components. Of note, mDAVID was rather equally associated with both components, whereas mSDAS was considerably more strongly associated with the arthritis/leucocytosis component. Furthermore, SAS showed weak to moderate associations, whereas PSS showed weak associations (detailed in table 3).

Responsiveness

Responsiveness of the scores was evaluated by z-standardising all three scores and calculating the mean change from baseline to week 72 separately for patients classified as active or inactive at week 72 according to the gold standard. In addition, Cohen’s d was calculated using the original (non-scaled) scores. As shown in table 4, there was a substantial mean reduction in all three scores over time in both patients with residual active disease and those in remission at week 72. The difference in score reduction between active and inactive patients was statistically significant for mDAVID and mSDAS and borderline for SAS. Considering standardised mean differences, the largest effect size was observed for mDAVID (Cohen’s d (95% CI) 0.76 (0.30 to 1.20)), followed by mSDAS (0.61 (0.13 to 1.08)), SAS (0.46 (−0.01 to 0.9)) and PSS (0.22 (−0.30 to 0.68)). Sensitivity analyses using complete cases yielded comparable results, with the exception that borderline statistical significance for SAS was no longer noted (online supplemental table 3).

Longitudinal sensitivity to change

Longitudinal sensitivity to change was examined using linear mixed-effects models with z-standardised scores. As illustrated in figure 3, the steepest slopes (indicating the greatest change over time) were observed for mDAVID, particularly among patients in remission at week 72, and mDAVID also showed the largest difference in slopes between active and inactive patients (detailed estimates in table 5).

Marginal R² values, reflecting the proportion of variance explained by the fixed effects (time, physician-assessed activity status and treatment strategy (biologic vs conventional therapy)), were similar, and numerically highest for mDAVID (0.65, 95% CI 0.59 to 0.70), followed by mSDAS (0.61, 95% CI 0.55 to 0.67), PSS (0.61, 95% CI 0.54 to 0.67) and SAS (0.55, 95% CI 0.45 to 0.64). Sensitivity analyses in the complete-case dataset and in a mixed-effects logistic regression analysis with a binarised mDAVID outcome (active vs remission/low disease activity) produced similar results (online supplemental tables 4-6).

Discussion

The lack of widely accepted and representative disease activity measures for AOSD has long been recognised as an obstacle to clinical research and drug development.11 12 15 18 In their absence, clinicians and researchers have relied on instruments that were not originally designed as disease activity measures, such as PSS, which primarily reflects disease severity,13 or they have adopted composite indices developed for rheumatoid arthritis, which do not adequately capture the systemic features of AOSD.4 5 These strategies have not been particularly successful in AOSD clinical trials.4 5 Emerging evidence suggests that composite measures incorporating both articular and systemic inflammatory manifestations better reflect overall disease activity and may more readily discriminate effective treatment options.7

Recently, several groups have proposed AOSD-specific activity measures and provided initial validation.15 16 18 In the present study, we comparatively evaluate these newer instruments in a distinct, multicentre cohort. Overall, mDAVID, mSDAS and SAS showed numerically slightly better performance than PSS across all dimensions assessed, including responsiveness and longitudinal sensitivity to change. Based on the current analysis, mDAVID consistently showed numerically superior responsiveness and longitudinal sensitivity compared with mSDAS and SAS.

Differences observed in the performance of the scores are likely a result of their specific composition and construction: SAS was developed through a logistic regression model to determine independent predictors of physician’s global assessments of disease activity. Clinically and statistically significant variables were weighted according to regression coefficients. SDAS was developed through a systematic literature review, expert adjunction and a Delphi consensus process. DAVID was developed by an international expert panel using a standardised, consensus-based process after systematic literature review and component analysis, which in turn might be regarded as a conceptual advantage. The scores demonstrated distinct representation of the disease components ‘systemic inflammation/lymphoproliferation’ and ‘arthritis/leucocytosis’ identified in our analysis. While mDAVID and mSDAS generally capture the same key domains of AOSD activity (fever, rash, articular involvement and CRP), mDAVID was more balanced between the two components, whereas mSDAS demonstrated the strongest correlation with the articular/leucocytosis component and was therefore skewed towards it. This might theoretically imply advantages of mDAVID in capturing short-term changes in AOSD activity, which indeed is in line with the observed superior sensitivity to change. On the other hand, mSDAS might better capture long-term morbidity, which is associated with a chronic articular phenotype.21 This hypothesis would have to be addressed in future research before firm conclusions can be drawn.

Notably, in our cohort, we used an adapted version of DAVID (mDAVID) and SDAS (mSDAS) that excluded the PtGA, and the closely correlated PhGA,16 respectively, out of concerns for incorporation bias/circularity and due to limited availability in our cohort. The original publication of DAVID reported even better performance for the full version including PtGA,18 suggesting that the performance of mDAVID in our study may actually represent a conservative estimate of its potential, and this is likely also true for mSDAS. In addition, DAVID was originally conceived as a categorical measure,18 whereas we transformed it into a numerical score to allow direct comparison with PSS and SAS. However, sensitivity analyses using a binary version of mDAVID as an outcome in mixed-effects models supported the robustness of our findings.

Interestingly, in a previous analysis using the same cohort, we compared conventional and biological treatment strategies in AOSD applying propensity score weighting.7 For that study, remission was defined based on expert consensus as CRP <10 mg/L, absence of arthritis, rash and fever—a definition closely aligned with the DAVID remission criteria apart from PtGA18 and also in line with the clinical treatment target of ‘clinically inactive disease’ as suggested by EULAR.2 Using this definition, we observed a marked difference in remission rates between biologic and conventional therapies (eg, 68% vs 49% at week 12),7 which further supports the clinical validity and discriminative capacity of the DAVID criteria.

This study has several limitations, some of which relate to the underlying cohort and have been discussed previously.7 When comparing the diverse activity measures, the physician-determined remission status of the patient was used as a gold standard. This strategy was chosen due to a lack of uniformly consented instruments, even though physician determination of disease status or PhGA suffers from well-known challenges such as subjectivity, inter-rater variability and a lack of standardisation.22 Indeed, we analysed factors that contributed to differences between PhGA and the AOSD activity measures. Since the treating physicians were not blinded to any clinical information and a lack of standardisation, a clear tendency not to assign patients to the remission category in case they were still on immunosuppressive medication was observed. Of note, a EULAR expert committee recently recommended to distinguish ‘clinically inactive disease’ as a short-term target and ‘drug-free remission’ as an ultimate target in Still’s disease, thus stressing the clinically relevant distinction between those states. According to our observations, this approach will likely improve standardisation of physician-determined remission status when implemented in Still’s disease cohorts. Additionally, the relatively modest absolute AUC values of the ROC analyses should be interpreted as a consequence of these limitations rather than a lack of precision of the respective scores. In order to overcome these challenges, prospective studies in AOSD could aim to formally validate activity measures such as DAVID and SDAS in their original form (including physician and patient global assessments) by employing longitudinal outcomes, such as time to flare defined as intensification of immunosuppressive medication, as a gold standard in the future.

Another limitation is that all patients in our cohort presented with active, newly onset or relapsing AOSD at onset and received prompt treatment. This may limit generalisability to chronic articular disease and less severe phenotypes. Furthermore, a substantial improvement in disease activity over time was noted even among those patients classified as having residual activity at week 72. Despite this overall improvement, mDAVID, mSDAS and to a lesser extent SAS clearly differentiated patients who were still considered active from those in remission according to the treating physicians, as reflected by distinct slopes and effect sizes. This supports the ability of these scores to discriminate between active and inactive disease states in a real-world, treated cohort.

Finally, the move towards a unified ‘Still’s disease’ concept encompassing sJIA and AOSD2 raises the question of whether a single disease activity measure should be developed or adapted for use across age groups. Such an approach could facilitate combined clinical studies and harmonise outcome assessment, but might also compromise sensitivity or validity if age-specific disease manifestations or feasibility constraints (eg, the use of PtGA in very young children) are not adequately addressed.23 In particular, patient-reported components may need adaptation or substitution in paediatric populations.

In conclusion, mDAVID and mSDAS demonstrated the most favourable and comparable performance profiles across the evaluated metrics, with mDAVID showing greater responsiveness and mSDAS a stronger correlation with the articular disease domain. Our data support further prospective validation of the use of DAVID, SDAS or, alternatively, SAS as preferred disease activity measures in future clinical trials.
