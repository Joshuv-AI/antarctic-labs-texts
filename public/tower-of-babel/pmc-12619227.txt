Evaluating the effects of physical activity and antidepressive agents on depressive symptoms in older adults: a meta-analysis of existing evidence
Authors: Shuai Zhang, Chenglong Miao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAlthough a wide range of treatments for depression are currently available, the condition continues to pose a substantial threat to the physical health of older adults. In recent years, physical activity has attracted increasing attention for its beneficial effects in alleviating depressive symptoms, compared to antidepressant medication.ObjectiveThis meta-analysis aimed to estimate and compare the standardized effects of physical activity and antidepressive agents in alleviating depressive symptoms among older adults aged 65 and above. By quantitatively synthesizing results from multiple independent studies, the goal is to derive a more precise and generalizable estimate of intervention efficacy, thereby informing evidence-based clinical practice in geriatric mental health.MethodsSearched the PubMed, Web of Science, and Cochrane Library databases for published English literature and screened 22 target articles with depression symptoms as outcome indicators. Meta-analysis statistics were conducted using Revman 5.3 to calculate effect sizes and standard mean differences reflecting the magnitude of the effect indicators.ResultsThe meta-analysis, conducted using a random-effects model, demonstrated that both physical activity (PA-SD) and antidepressive agents (AA-SD) significantly reduced depressive symptoms (DS) among older adults. The effect size for PA-SD reached a statistically significant moderate level (SMD = − 0.60, 95% CI [–0.77, − 0.43], Z = 7.05, p < 0.00001), while that for AA-SD reached a statistically significant small level (SMD = − 0.30, 95% CI [–0.44, − 0.16], Z = 4.15, p < 0.0001). The overall pooled effect size across all included studies was SMD = − 0.46 (95% CI [–0.59, − 0.33], Z = 6.87, p < 0.00001). A statistically significant difference in effect sizes was observed between the two intervention types (Chi² = 7.20, df = 1, p = 0.007).ConclusionsPhysical activity and antidepressive agents can both alleviate depression symptoms in older adults, but there are significant differences in impact methods or mechanisms between the two.Trial registrationThis study has been registered in PROSPERO (ID: CRD420251008013).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12877-025-06626-w.Keywords: Physical activity, Antidepressive agent, Older adults, Depressive effects, Depression symptoms

Introduction

Depression is a common mental health issue that significantly impacts the quality of life, physical health, and social functioning of older adults. In the older population, the onset of depressive mood is often caused by factors such as life changes, health problems, and social isolation [1]. Additionally, older adults may face issues of social isolation, especially when children leave home or when living in nursing homes, which can exacerbate their depressive mood [2, 3]. Dealing with depressive mood among older adults requires attention to social support, encouragement of participation in activities, and provision of mental health support, as even subclinical levels of depression may negatively impact the quality of life and health of older adults [4–6].

In this review, we use the people-first term “older adults” to denote individuals aged 65 years and older. This definition aligns with the criteria established by the World Health Organization [7], which designates age 65 as the onset of older adulthood for health research and policy purposes. This age threshold is widely accepted in geriatric clinical studies and population-based research [8, 9], and is commonly used to define this population as a distinct demographic category in population-level studies.

The influence of physical activity on depressive effects in older adults

Physical activity refers to any activity that increases energy expenditure, encompassing various forms of exercise, workouts, and physical activities in daily life. It includes various forms of bodily movements, ranging from simple walking to high-intensity aerobic exercises, as well as strength training, stretching exercises, etc [10, 11].

Physical activity has a significant positive impact on alleviating depressive effects in older adults [12]. Moderate physical activity is believed to alleviate depressive symptoms, and regular participation in physical activity can reduce the severity of depression in older patients, improving their mental health status. Physical activity can help older patients with depression enhance psychological resilience and improve their ability to cope with stress and negative emotions [13]. Through exercise, older adults may become more confident, optimistic, and proactive in facing life’s challenges. Participating in physical activity is often a social opportunity, which can help older patients with depression build social connections, reduce feelings of loneliness and social isolation, and improve their level of mental health [14–18]. Physical activity helps regulate the biological clock and improve sleep quality, which is crucial for restoring mental health in older adults and can alleviate depressive symptoms [19].

Overall, the effects of physical activity on depressive effects in older adults are multifaceted. Through improving physiological health, regulating the nervous system, promoting social interaction, and other pathways, physical activity helps alleviate depressive symptoms and enhance resilience against depression. Therefore, physical activity is considered as one of the important natural therapeutic approaches and can be integrated as part of depression treatment, especially when used in conjunction with other therapeutic methods for better efficacy.

The effects of antidepressive agents on depressive effects in older adults

Antidepressive agents are a class of medications used to treat depression and related mental health issues. They have diverse mechanisms of action, primarily working by regulating neurotransmitter levels in the brain to alleviate depressive symptoms [20]. Common antidepressive agents include Selective Serotonin Reuptake Inhibitors (SSRI), Selective Norepinephrine Reuptake Inhibitor (SNRI), Tricyclic Antidepressants (TCA), Monoamine Oxidase Inhibitors (MAOI), and others [21]. Antidepressive agents usually take some time to achieve optimal effects [22], so patients need to continue using them as directed by their doctors while closely monitoring for any adverse reactions.

Antidepressive agents also have a significant positive impact on alleviating depressive symptoms in older adults, even when certain symptoms do not meet the clinical diagnostic criteria for major depression. These medications can help older adults alleviate depressive mood, including feelings of low mood, helplessness, sadness, and anxiety, by regulating neurotransmitter levels in the brain, thus improving mood and fostering a more positive and optimistic outlook in older patients [23]. A growing body of recent empirical evidence further reinforces these clinical observations, highlighting the therapeutic efficacy of antidepressant medication specifically in older adults. For example, a systematic review and meta-analysis conducted by Tham et al. (2016) demonstrated that selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs), particularly escitalopram and sertraline, exhibited significant improvements in depressive symptoms and overall quality of life compared to placebo among older adults [24]. Notably, this improvement encompassed not only mood regulation but also critical aspects of daily functional autonomy, thereby substantially enhancing the clinical relevance of pharmacological treatments. Similarly, Hsu et al. (2021) performed a network meta-analysis comparing the efficacy of SSRIs and SNRIs in older patients with major depressive disorder [25]. The results showed that duloxetine (RR = 1.62), paroxetine (RR = 1.48), and sertraline (RR = 1.28) were significantly more effective than placebo in achieving treatment response, with duloxetine ranking highest in both efficacy and acceptability. These findings further support the clinical use of antidepressants in older patients and provide evidence-based guidance for medication selection based on comparative effectiveness.

A common symptom of depression in many older adults is sleep disturbances, including insomnia, shallow sleep, and frequent awakenings [26]. Most antidepressive agents can help improve sleep quality, making it easier for patients to fall asleep, stay asleep, and obtain sufficient rest [27]. Recent clinical research further substantiates these benefits. For instance, a randomized controlled trial conducted by Gebara et al. (2018) found that older adults with major depressive disorder treated with SSRIs, such as escitalopram and sertraline, showed significant improvement in both subjective and objective sleep parameters, including reduced sleep latency, decreased nocturnal awakenings, and increased total sleep duration, compared with placebo groups [28]. Similarly, a meta-analysis by Kok et al. (2017) highlighted that antidepressant treatments among older patients with depression commonly resulted in improved sleep architecture, notably increased rapid-eye-movement (REM) sleep and reduced sleep fragmentation, both critical for emotional and cognitive recovery [29]. These findings highlight the meaningful role of antidepressants in addressing sleep-related issues among older patients with depression, further supporting their therapeutic use in clinical practice.

Depression often leads to a loss of interest and motivation in daily activities among older adults [30, 31]. Antidepressive agents can help restore these interests and vitality, enabling patients to reintegrate into social activities, hobbies, and daily tasks. For example, Kok and Reynolds (2017) conducted a narrative review synthesizing findings from clinical trials and cohort studies on pharmacological treatment for late-life depression, and reported that patients receiving antidepressants frequently exhibited improvements in autonomy, social functioning, and overall quality of life [32]. In addition, Taylor (2014) provided a clinical practice-focused review that summarized evidence from randomized controlled trials and longitudinal studies, showing that sustained antidepressant therapy in older patients was associated with enhanced daily functioning and reintegration into social and personal activities [33]. These synthesis-based findings suggest that antidepressant medications not only reduce depressive symptoms but also play a crucial role in revitalizing psychological vitality and restoring functional independence in older adults.

However, the physical condition and depressive symptoms of each older patient vary significantly, making personalized treatment essential. For instance, Mallery et al. (2019) conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis of second-generation antidepressants in adults aged 65 and older [34]. They reported that frail individuals or those with multiple comorbidities did not experience significant improvements from antidepressant treatment compared to placebo, and were more prone to adverse effects. In contrast, healthier individuals within the same age group showed relatively better treatment responses, suggesting that physical health status plays a critical role in modulating both the efficacy and safety of pharmacological interventions. These findings highlight the importance of tailoring antidepressant strategies to the individual needs of older patients, based on comprehensive assessments of physical health, comorbidity profiles, and symptom severity.

In summary, antidepressive agents can help alleviate depressive symptoms in older adults, but close collaboration with a doctor is necessary to ensure the safety and effectiveness of the treatment plan. Medication therapy is typically combined with other treatment methods such as psychotherapy and lifestyle changes to achieve the best treatment outcomes. Although depression has not yet been fully treated, the use of exercise to treat depression has received increasing attention recently, and exercise has been shown to yield beneficial effects in alleviating depressive symptoms, potentially comparable or superior to antidepressive agents in some contexts [35].

Although significant progress has been made in the research on depression, there are still many mysteries surrounding its etiology and intervention methods. Some studies suggest that engaging in physical activity can reduce the risk of depression and improve mental health [36], while others have not found a clear correlation. This divergence indicates that there is still controversy surrounding the understanding of the relationship between depression and physical activity, necessitating further exploration and clarification. In this context, it becomes increasingly important to consider whether such inconsistencies may stem from differences in individual characteristics, particularly age. Compared to younger adults, older adults exhibit distinct physiological, psychological, and social profiles that may influence how they respond to various interventions. As such, understanding age-related variation in treatment effects is critical to interpreting heterogeneous findings in the literature and to refining intervention strategies for older adults. Moreover, tailoring interventions to the specific needs and characteristics of older adults may enhance both their efficacy and long-term adherence.

Age-related variation in response to physical activity

The psychological and physiological mechanisms through which physical activity alleviates depressive symptoms may differ across age groups. In younger adults, exercise is commonly associated with increases in self-efficacy, goal-directed behavior, and cognitive reframing of negative thoughts [37]. In contrast, older adults may derive greater benefits from improvements in functional mobility, stabilization of circadian rhythms, pain reduction, and enhanced opportunities for social interaction, all of which are frequently compromised in late-life depression [38, 39]. Moreover, the neurobiological response to exercise, including the promotion of brain-derived neurotrophic factor expression and the reduction of systemic inflammation, may be especially relevant for older adults due to their increased vulnerability to neurodegeneration and age-related inflammatory processes [40, 41]. These observations suggest that physical activity may operate through distinct mechanisms depending on age, which reinforces the necessity of considering age-specific factors when evaluating the antidepressant efficacy of exercise interventions.

Age-related variation in response to antidepressive agents

The clinical effectiveness of antidepressive agents may vary significantly across age groups due to physiological and pharmacological changes associated with aging. While younger adults generally metabolize antidepressants with greater efficiency and show more consistent symptom improvement under standardized dosages, older adults often exhibit age-related alterations in drug absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion. These include reduced hepatic and renal clearance, increased blood–brain barrier permeability, and heightened central nervous system sensitivity to psychoactive medications [42, 43]. Such changes may result in diminished therapeutic benefit, heightened vulnerability to side effects, and reduced adherence in older patients. Moreover, polypharmacy—prevalent among older adults—amplifies the risk of drug–drug interactions, further complicating pharmacological treatment strategies [44]. Clinical research has also suggested that the presence of multimorbidity and frailty, more common in late life, may attenuate the antidepressant response, especially among those with subthreshold depression or overlapping cognitive impairments [45]. Overall, these findings underscore that the effects of antidepressive agents cannot be assumed to be uniform across age groups, and that age-specific pharmacological considerations are essential when evaluating treatment outcomes.

Depression symptoms are widely used indicators in clinical practice [46, 47], considered essential measures for assessing the effectiveness of depression treatment and can be quantified and evaluated through standardized assessment tools [48, 49]. By measuring depression symptoms, it is possible to determine the effectiveness of depression treatment and consequently understand whether the mental health status of older adults has improved. Given the fragmented nature of existing studies, which differ in sample characteristics, intervention types, and outcome measures, a meta-analytic approach is particularly suited to synthesize heterogeneous evidence and evaluate the comparative efficacy of these two intervention strategies in late-life populations. Therefore, this study adopts depressive symptoms (DS) as the primary outcome indicator and aims to systematically review and analyze existing empirical research through meta-analysis to examine the effects of physical activity and antidepressive agents on alleviating depressive symptoms in older adults.

To enhance conceptual clarity, this study was designed in accordance with the PICO framework. The Population (P) consisted of older adults aged 65 years and older who exhibited depressive symptoms or had been clinically diagnosed with depression. The Intervention (I) included physical activity programs such as aerobic exercise, resistance training, Tai Chi, and yoga, as well as other socially engaging, community-based exercise activities. The Comparison (C) comprised standard pharmacological treatments using commonly prescribed antidepressive agents, including selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors, serotonin–norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors, and tricyclic antidepressants. The Outcome (O) was the reduction in depressive symptoms, assessed using validated depression rating scales employed across the included studies.

Specifically, this study evaluates the overall intervention effects of both strategies by extracting and comparing the standardized mean differences (SMDs) reported across studies. This approach aims to identify the magnitude and generalizability of treatment effects for each intervention, thereby providing an evidence-based foundation to guide decision-making in late-life depression management across both theoretical and clinical domains. By synthesizing heterogeneous findings, this meta-analysis offers a more precise and statistically grounded estimate of the comparative effectiveness of physical activity and antidepressive agents in alleviating depressive symptoms among older adults.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria: (1) Participants included in the study are older adults with depressive symptoms or diagnosed with depression; (2) Study type is experimental research; (3) Demographic characteristics such as age and sample size are clearly stated in the literature; (4) Interventions involving physical activity or antidepressive agents for depression in participants; (5) Accurate experimental data and conclusions are presented in the literature; (6) Results presented in the discussion or conclusion of the literature directly reflect depressive symptoms. Exclusion criteria: (1) Literature that cannot be accessed in full text; (2) Systematic reviews, book review chapters, conference abstracts, etc.; (3) In principle, An article not indexed in JCR (Journal Citation Reports) or Chinese Academy of Sciences classification; (4) Belongs to journal alert literature; (5) Not included in all keywords or not consistent with the main topic. Literature retrieval, screening, and inclusion were independently conducted by one researcher. In case of disagreement, consultation with a second researcher was used to reach consensus.

Information extraction and encoding

This study included a total of 22 articles, among which 14 articles reported the changes in DS induced by PA-SD, while 8 articles reported the changes in DS induced by AA-SD.

The coding content for PA-SD in this study includes:1) Study characteristics: Authors, publication year. 2) Sample information: Participant type, average age, sample size. 3) Physical activity intervention features: Type of Physical Activity. 4) Outcome measures: Depression Symptoms. Data analysis

Using Review Manager 5.3 software for statistical analysis. The outcome measures of the included studies in this research are continuous variables, with statistical significance set at P ≤ 0.05. Due to variations in testing methods and units among the indicators, the effect size is represented by the standardized mean difference along with a 95% confidence interval. Where M1-M2 represents the difference in mean outcomes between groups, and SD pooled is the pooled standard deviation of the outcomes among subjects, calculated using the following formula:

In that case, SD21 represents the square of the standard deviation of the first group, and SD22 represents the square of the standard deviation of the second group.

According to Cohen’s research [72], standardized mean differences are classified as follows: < 0.2 (trivial), 0.2–0.49 (small), 0.5–0.79 (moderate), and >0.8 (large)。.

Heterogeneity among the included studies was assessed using both the Chi-square test for heterogeneity (with a significance level of p = 0.1) and the I² statistic, which quantifies the percentage of variation across studies that is due to true heterogeneity rather than chance. The I² value ranges from 0% to 100%, with higher values indicating greater heterogeneity. According to the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions (Version 6.3, 2022), an I² value ≤ 50% typically indicates low to moderate heterogeneity, warranting the use of a fixed-effects model, while an I² >50% suggests substantial heterogeneity, for which a random-effects model is more appropriate. In this meta-analysis, model selection was based on the actual I² values obtained, in accordance with these Cochrane guidelines.

Research results

Quality assessment of included studies

The quality of the literature was assessed using the Cochrane risk of bias assessment tool [73]. Review Manager 5.3 software was used to evaluate the risk of bias in seven aspects: Random sequence generation, Allocation concealment, Blinding of participants and personnel, Blinding of outcome assessment, Incomplete outcome data, Selective reporting, and Other bias. The risk of bias assessment criteria includes: Low risk of bias, Unclear risk of bias, and High risk of bias. A score of 1 point was assigned for low risk of bias, while unclear and high risk of bias did not receive any points. When the score exceeded 5 points, the quality of the literature was considered high; when the score ranged from 2 to 5, the quality was considered moderate; when the score was < 2, the quality was considered low. Literature quality assessment was conducted independently by two researchers, followed by cross-checking. In case of discrepancies, consensus was reached through discussion and negotiation.

All literature included in the meta-analysis scored between 2 and 5 points, indicating the absence of low-quality literature. Although informed consent was obtained from all participants in accordance with ethical standards, this does not inherently preclude the implementation of blinding procedures. In exercise intervention trials, participant blinding is often infeasible due to the visible nature of physical activity. Nevertheless, detection bias can still be mitigated through assessor blinding, such as the use of independent raters or standardized self-report measures. Accordingly, the overall risk of bias related to blinding was considered moderate and context-specific, rather than uniformly high. Overall, the quality of the included studies is considered moderate, and no literature was excluded due to quality assessment (Fig. 2).

Publication bias test

In meta-analysis, bias refers to systematic errors in the study, which may lead to deviations of the results from the true situation [74]. Bias can originate from various aspects such as study design, data collection, analysis, and reporting, impacting the accuracy and credibility of the results. Studies without publication bias or with minimal publication bias are more statistically significant.

Funnel plot is a graphical tool used to visualize the degree of bias in research results, typically employed to detect or identify potential publication bias or other types of bias. Normally, when the bias of study results is small and the precision is high, the funnel plot presents a symmetric funnel-shaped pattern, with a wider base and a narrower top. However, if publication bias or other biases exist, the funnel plot may exhibit an asymmetric or non-uniform shape. For instance, publication bias often manifests as small studies tending to cluster at the top, while larger studies are distributed at the bottom, resulting in a skewed or asymmetrical appearance of the funnel plot.

As shown in Fig. 3, most effect sizes of DS are evenly and symmetrically distributed around the center line, with fewer effect sizes distributed on the lower right side, suggesting a minimal likelihood of publication bias visually.

Comparison of the effects of physical activity and antidepressive agents on depressive symptoms

According to the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions (Version 6.4), the choice between a fixed-effects model and a random-effects model should be guided by both statistical and clinical heterogeneity. As shown in Fig. 4, the heterogeneity test in the present meta-analysis yielded an overall I² value of 50% (Chi² = 41.93, df = 21, p = 0.004; Tau² = 0.04), indicating a moderate level of heterogeneity among the included studies. Although the I² value sits at the conventional threshold between low and high heterogeneity, the statistically significant Chi² test result (p < 0.1) further supports the presence of non-negligible heterogeneity. Therefore, a random-effects model was employed to better account for between-study variability and enhance the generalizability of the findings.

Forest Plot of Comparative Effects of Physical Activity and Antidepressive Agents on Depressive Symptoms in Older Adults

Under the random-effects model, the meta-analysis revealed that both physical activity (PA-SD) and antidepressive agents (AA-SD) had significant effects in reducing depressive symptoms (DS) among older adults. Specifically, the effect size for PA-SD was statistically significant and reached a moderate level (SMD = − 0.60, 95% CI [–0.77, − 0.43], Z = 7.05, p < 0.00001), while the effect size for AA-SD was also significant but smaller in magnitude (SMD = − 0.30, 95% CI [–0.44, − 0.16], Z = 4.15, p < 0.0001).

The overall pooled analysis across all included studies showed a combined effect size of SMD = − 0.46 (95% CI [–0.59, − 0.33], Z = 6.87, p < 0.00001), suggesting that both interventions are effective, but physical activity yielded a greater reduction in depressive symptoms. In addition, a statistically significant difference was found between the two intervention types (Chi² = 7.20, df = 1, p = 0.007), suggesting superior efficacy of physical activity.

Discussion and conclusion

This meta-analysis indicates that both physical activity and antidepressant pharmacotherapy significantly alleviate depressive symptoms in older adults. In the random effects model, the standardized mean difference for physical activity was numerically larger than that for antidepressant agents; however, because the two evidence bases differ systematically in inclusion criteria and baseline symptom severity, with most antidepressant trials enrolling clinically depressed samples while a substantial proportion of physical activity trials included mixed samples, this numerical difference represents an indirect comparison and should not be interpreted as evidence of superior comparative efficacy. Accordingly, the current evidence supports the clinical significance of both interventions within their respective target populations. Physical activity may additionally yield benefits by concurrently enhancing physical fitness, psychological functioning, and social participation, thereby supporting overall well-being in later life.

Although meta-analysis has become a widely adopted approach in psychological and clinical research, its application remains methodologically justified when synthesizing fragmented evidence and evaluating the relative efficacy of distinct intervention modalities. In the context of late-life depression, where considerable variability exists in intervention design, outcome measurement, and sample characteristics, meta-analytic techniques offer a statistically coherent framework for comparison.

The alleviation of depression symptoms in older adults presents an important challenge in both medical and psychological fields, with physical activity and antidepressive agents playing crucial roles as two main intervention strategies. Physical activity, as a non-pharmacological intervention for alleviating depression symptoms in older adults, has unique advantages. Firstly, it is a natural, economical, and easily implementable method, suitable for almost all age groups [75]. Older adults can choose various forms of exercise, such as walking, dancing, swimming, or simple gymnastics, to meet their personal preferences and physical conditions. Secondly, compared to pharmacological interventions, physical activity has almost no side effects and helps improve overall physical health [76–79]. By enhancing muscles and bones [80–82], improving cardiovascular function, controlling weight and blood pressure [83], physical activity has a positive impact on the health status of older adults, further promoting the improvement of mental health.

In older adults, Firstly, pharmacological treatment can significantly improve depressive symptoms in a short period [84]. For older adults with severe conditions or in urgent need of rapid relief from emotional distress, pharmacological intervention may be more attractive. Secondly, antidepressive agents have been clinically validated to have robust efficacy in treating depression, allowing physicians to choose the most suitable type and dosage of medication based on individual patient circumstances to achieve the optimal therapeutic outcome.

Overall, physical activity and antidepressive agents both play crucial roles in alleviating depression symptoms in older adults. When selecting treatment modalities, physicians should consider the individual’s health status, medical history, preferences, and treatment goals to develop the most appropriate personalized treatment plan. Future research should further explore the long-term effects, safety, and applicability of these two interventions in different populations to provide more comprehensive and effective guidance for the treatment of depression in older adults.

When discussing the treatment options for depression symptoms in older adults, it is important to consider individual differences and the importance of comprehensive therapy. While physical activity and antidepressive agents are two main intervention methods, they are not mutually exclusive choices. In fact, many studies have shown that combining physical activity with medication therapy may lead to better treatment outcomes. Some older adults may be more sensitive to medication therapy, while others may prefer non-pharmacological interventions such as physical activity or psychotherapy. Therefore, when formulating treatment plans, factors such as the older adult’s physical health status, cognitive level, social support network, as well as personal preferences and interests should be fully considered. Additionally, for those older adults who are unable or unwilling to engage in physical activity, exploring other forms of non-pharmacological interventions, such as cognitive-behavioral therapy, social support, and psychological education, can also help alleviate depression to some extent, thereby improving their quality of life and psychological well-being.

To more comprehensively explore the implications of the present findings and to investigate potential age-related differences in the effects of physical activity and antidepressive agents, it is necessary to contextualize the current results within existing evidence from studies involving younger populations. Previous meta-analyses involving younger adults have demonstrated the effectiveness of both interventions. For instance, Cooney et al. (2013) conducted a Cochrane review of 39 randomized controlled trials and reported that physical activity yielded a moderate antidepressant effect (SMD ≈ −0.62) in adults aged 18–65, with supervised aerobic and group-based exercises showing the most pronounced benefits [85]. Similarly, Kvam et al. (2016) found that physical exercise yielded a large effect size when compared with no-treatment control groups (Hedges’ g ≈ −0.68) and demonstrated a moderate effect size when directly compared with antidepressant medication [86]. These findings from younger adult populations support the broader applicability of both interventions, while also highlighting the potential of exercise as a viable alternative or complement to pharmacological treatment.

Building upon these findings from younger adult populations, the present meta-analysis further demonstrates that both physical activity and antidepressive agents significantly alleviate depressive symptoms among older adults. Notably, the standardized mean difference for physical activity in this study, estimated under a random-effects model to account for moderate heterogeneity, was comparatively greater than that for antidepressive agents, suggesting that exercise may yield enhanced therapeutic benefits in older populations. This age-related difference may be attributed to several factors. Compared to younger adults, older individuals often face increased sensitivity to medication side effects, reduced metabolic capacity, and higher rates of polypharmacy, all of which can limit the practicality and effectiveness of pharmacological interventions. In contrast, physical activity offers not only psychological benefits but also substantial improvements in physical health and functional independence, which are particularly relevant for aging populations. Therefore, the present findings support a growing body of evidence that underscores the unique advantages of exercise-based interventions for late-life depression and advocate for its broader integration into geriatric mental health strategies.

Limitations

Despite the strengths of this meta-analysis, several methodological limitations may influence the interpretation and generalizability of the findings. First, some degree of clinical heterogeneity was observed across the included studies, particularly in participant selection criteria. While all medication-based studies explicitly enrolled older adults diagnosed with clinical depression, some physical activity studies included broader samples with subclinical or self-reported depressive symptoms. These differences may affect the comparability of intervention effects.

Second, variability in intervention protocols—such as exercise type, frequency, intensity, and duration, or dosage and treatment length of antidepressants—may further influence the comparability of treatment effects. This methodological diversity contributes to heterogeneity and may weaken the conclusiveness of the findings.

Lastly, the analysis was limited to English-language publications retrieved from selected databases, potentially introducing language and publication bias and excluding relevant studies published in other languages. Future reviews should consider including non-English databases and applying broader search strategies to reduce potential bias and ensure inclusivity.
