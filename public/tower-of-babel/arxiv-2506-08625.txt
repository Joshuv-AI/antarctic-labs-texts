RAISE: Enhancing Scientific Reasoning in LLMs via Step-by-Step Retrieval
Minhae Oh, Jeonghye Kim, Nakyung Lee, Donggeon Seo, Taeuk Kim, Jungwoo Lee

Abstract
Scientific reasoning requires not only long-chain reasoning processes, but also knowledge of domain-specific terminologies and adaptation to updated findings. To deal with these challenges for scientific reasoning, we introduce RAISE , a step-by-step retrieval-augmented framework which retrieves logically relevant documents from in-the-wild corpus. RAISE is divided into three steps: problem decomposition, logical query generation, and logical retrieval. We observe that RAISE consistently outperforms other baselines on scientific reasoning benchmarks. We analyze that unlike other baselines, RAISE retrieves documents that are not only similar in terms of the domain knowledge, but also documents logically more relevant.

Introduction

Recently, large language models (LLMs) have demonstrated remarkable capabilities for diverse scientific domains that require intricate reasoning and the integration of diverse knowledge sources
(
Zhang et al. 2024c
;
Zhang et al. 2024a
;
Prabhakar et al. 2025
;
Rueda et al. 2025
)
. In contrast to many other domains, scientific reasoning is especially demanding due to the need for advanced reasoning skills, knowledge of specialized terminology, and awareness of continually evolving knowledge. To enhance the capability of LLMs to solve scientific reasoning problems, two approaches are widely used: (1) step-wise reasoning, and (2) retrieval-augmented generation. For step-wise reasoning, it has been shown that solving reasoning problems through step-wise reasoning process is more effective than attempting to solve them in a single step, similar to how humans approach challenging problems
Wei et al. 2022
;
Zhao et al. 2024a
;
Jin et al. 2024
;
Lai et al. 2024
;
Yao et al. 2023
. For retrieval-augmented generation (RAG), it is known that retrieving relevant external knowledge can mitigate hallucination and deal with timely knowledge, which is especially important for science domain
Lewis et al. 2020b
;
Bang et al. 2025
;
Zhong et al. 2025
;
Xiong et al. 2024
.

To utilize the benefits of both worlds, recent works have adopted RAG for step-wise reasoning on various domains
(
Jeong et al. 2024
;
Zhang et al. 2025a
)
. However, most of the works focus on relatively simple multi-hop question answering tasks or non-scientific domains
(
Guan et al. 2025
;
Jin et al. 2025
;
Wang et al. 2025
)
or assume task-related retrieval corpus which is hard to obtain compared to in-the-wild retrieval source such as Wikipedia
(
Zhong et al. 2025
;
Qian et al. 2023
;
Anand et al. 2023
)
. Solving challenging scientific reasoning tasks such as graduate-level biology, physics, etc, using an in-the-wild corpus is very challenging since merely retrieving superficial knowledge is insufficient for these tasks. Instead, the retrieved information should contain relevant logics needed to solve the problem
(
Rueda et al. 2025
)
. Moreover, the knowledge required for each intermediate step can vary significantly even within the same problem. Without considering the evolving information needed for each reasoning process, RAG might even deteriorate the downstream task performance. The question of
what to search for
and
how to retrieve
the appropriate external knowledge for each step when solving scientific reasoning tasks is underexplored.

Preliminary

Step-by-Step Reasoning in LLMs.
LLMs are capable of performing multi-step reasoning over complex input queries by internally chaining intermediate inferences. This step-by-step reasoning process involves decomposing a question into sub-problems, maintaining coherence across steps, and generating a final answer. Formally, given a query
x
, the model implicitly constructs a latent reasoning trajectory
\{r_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T}
, and generates the answer
y
conditioned on this chain:

p(y\mid x)=\sum_{r_{1},\dots,r_{T}}p(y\mid r_{1:T},x)\cdot\prod_{t=1}^{T}p(r_{t}\mid r_{<t},x).

However, standard LLMs rely solely on their parametric knowledge, which limits performance in scenarios requiring up-to-date or external information.

RAG for Single-Step Reasoning.
We address the task of generating a response
y
given an input
x
, enhanced by retrieval from an external corpus
\mathcal{D}
. RAG combines a retriever and a generator to condition the output on both the input and relevant documents.

A standard language model defines:

p(y\mid x)=\prod_{t=1}^{T}p(y_{t}\mid y_{<t},x).

In RAG, generation is conditioned on retrieved documents
\{d_{j}\}_{j=1}^{k}
, typically approximated as:

p(y\mid x)\approx\sum_{j=1}^{k}p(y\mid x,d_{j})\cdot p(d_{j}\mid x).

The retriever encodes queries and documents via
f_{q}(x)
and
f_{d}(d)
, scoring relevance by:

\text{sim}(x,d)=f_{q}(x)^{\top}f_{d}(d).

Top-
k
documents are retrieved, and a generator (e.g., BART
Lewis et al. 2020a
, T5
Raffel et al. 2020
) produces
y
based on both
x
and
d_{i}
.

RAISE

We propose
RAISE
(step-by-step
R
etrieval-
A
ugmented
I
nference for
S
cientific r
E
asoning), a retrieval-augmented generation framework for scientific reasoning designed to support multi-step reasoning through fine-grained, step-aware retrieval. Rather than retrieving documents based on the entire problem as a single query, RAISE decomposes the problem into intermediate reasoning steps and performs targeted retrieval for each. The overview of RAISE is provided in Figure
1
and Algorithm
1
. The method consists of three main stages:

(1)

Problem Decomposition
, where the original question
x
is broken down into subquestions
r_{1},\dots,r_{n}
along with corresponding search queries
q_{1},\dots,q_{n}
that are intended to retrieve relevant knowledge for each step;

(2)

Logical Query Generation
, where each search query
q_{i}
is further enriched into a logic aware explanation
q_{i}^{*}
, which serves as the final retrieval query; and

(3)

Logical Retrieval
, where external knowledge
D_{i}
is retrieved for each subquestion
r_{i}
and used to generate the subanswer
a_{i}
.

Problem Decomposition.
RAISE begins with the decomposition of a scientific reasoning problem
x
into a sequence of intermediate subquestions
\{r_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
, each corresponding to a distinct reasoning step. This planning process is performed by prompting a language model
P_{\theta}
with
x
and prompt
p_{1}
to identify the logical structure required to solve the problem. Formally, we generate:

\{(r_{i},q_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}\sim P_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x,p_{1})

(1)

While recent work has increasingly explored problem decomposition, many retrieval-based approaches still operate at the problem level, retrieving information using a single query without explicitly modeling intermediate reasoning steps. In contrast, this stage produces a structured sequence of subquestions that reflects the multi-step nature of scientific reasoning.

Along with each subquestion
r_{i}
, a corresponding
search query

q_{i}
is generated to guide retrieval. These queries are not used directly for retrieval but rather serve as an initial query for the next stage. As a result, this stage outputs subquestion-query pairs
\{(r_{i},q_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
, forming the basis for step-wise retrieval and generation.

Logical Query Generation.
In the second stage, each initial search query
q_{i}
and its corresponding subquestion
r_{i}
are jointly used to generate a logically enriched
logical query

q_{i}^{*}
. Search queries
q_{i}
are short key phrases, where the logical process is absent. Therefore, using
q_{i}
for retrieval will focus on domain similarity instead of logical similarity. Instead, this step constructs a logically coherent query that aligns with the reasoning intent of the subquestion. The model is prompted with both
q_{i}
and
r_{i}
, along with a reformulation prompt
p_{2}
(e.g.
add essential scientific or mathematical explanation the search result would contain
):

q_{i}^{*}\sim P_{\theta}(\cdot\mid q_{i},r_{i},p_{2})

(2)

Using only the subquestion
r_{i}
to generate a query often results in noisy or overly specific formulations that include problem-specific variables or conditions irrelevant to general knowledge retrieval. On the other hand, using only the initial query
q_{i}
lacks sufficient context to capture the intended reasoning path. By combining both, we enable the model to synthesize a logical query that is purpose-driven and aligned with the knowledge required to answer
r_{i}
.
Even if the reformulated query
q_{i}^{*}
contains facutal inaccuracies, it tends to retrieve passages from corpus
\mathcal{C}
that are logically relevant and supportive of the reasoning required for solving the original problem. Figure
2
presents example queries generated by RAISE ,Step-back and HyDE, illustrating RAISE’s ability to generate logical queries that are well-aglined with the reasoning intent.

Logical Retrieval.
The reformulated query
q_{i}^{*}
is used to retrieve external knowledge:

D_{i}=\mathcal{R}(q_{i}^{*},\mathcal{C},k)

(3)

Here,
\mathcal{R}
is a top-
k
retrieval function over in-the-wild corpus
\mathcal{C}
(e.g., Wikipedia), and
D_{i}=\{d_{i1},\dots,d_{ik}\}
denotes the retrieved documents. The goal is not to retrieve direct answers but to obtain passages that contain relevant background knowledge or reasoning procedures necessary to solve
r_{i}
. Also, since irrelevant documents might be also retrieved, we set a retrieval threshold
T
, where we retrieve documents that have higher retrieval similarity than
T
.

After retrieval, for each subquestion
r_{i}
, the model predicts its solution
a_{i}
. Importantly, the prediction is conditioned not only on the retrieved documents
D_{i}
, but also on the original question
x
and the previously answered subquestions
\{(r_{j},a_{j})\}_{j=1}^{i-1}
. We write:

a_{i}\sim P_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x,\{(r_{j},a_{j})\}_{j=1}^{i-1},r_{i},D_{i},p_{3})

(4)

Finally, all subanswers are aggregated to predict the final answer
y
:

y\sim P_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x,\{(r_{i},a_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n},p_{4})

(5)

This step-wise approach ensures that the model constructs its final answer based on grounded and explicitly structured intermediate reasoning.

4.1
Experimental Setup

Datasets.
To evaluate our method in settings that require multi-step scientific reasoning, we select three datasets focused on high-level STEM and professional reasoning. We provide more details on the evaluation dataset in Appendix
A
.

•

GPQA (Graduate-level Physics Question Answering)
Rein et al. 2024
: A collection of graduate-level physics, biology, and chemistry questions requiring multi-step reasoning and access to external scientific fact. We use this dataset as our primary benchmark.

•

SuperGPQA
(
Team et al. 2025
)
: This dataset evaluates the knowledge and reasoning abilities of Large Language Models across 285 graduate-level disciplines. We focus on three subsets that are challenging enough and require step-by-step reasoning in the science domain: science-hard, science-middle, and engineering-hard.

•

MMLU
(
Hendrycks et al. 2021
;
Wang et al. 2024b
)
: Among various domains and difficulty levels of MMLU
(
Hendrycks et al. 2021
)
and MMLU-Pro
(
Wang et al. 2024b
)
, we focus on chemistry domain since it requires step-by-step reasoning with knowledge of specialized terminology.

Retriever Configuration.
We adopt Dense Passage Retrieval (DPR)
Karpukhin et al. 2020
as our retriever, using the publicly available model trained on the Natural Questions (NQ) dataset
Kwiatkowski et al. 2019
.

Language Model.
For GPQA, which is the primary focus of our study, we use Mistral Small 3.1-Instruct-2503
Mistral AI 2025
, a 24B state-of-the-art small-scale open-sourced LLM that achieves strong performance on scientific reasoning tasks. For the remaining tasks, we use the smaller LLaMA 3.1-8B model
Grattafiori et al. 2024
due to computational constraints, applying it to SuperGPQA, and MMLU. We provide detailed hyperparameters and experiment setups in Appendix
B

4.2
Baselines

To assess the importance of multi-step reasoning and step-aware retrieval, we conduct experiments with two groups of baselines:
Direct Reasoning
and
Decomposed Reasoning
.
Direct Reasoning methods solve the problem in a single inference step without decomposing it into subquestions. This group includes three prompt-based approaches and one retrieval-augmented method. The three prompting methods-
Chain-of-Thought (CoT)

Wei et al. 2022
,
Plan-and-Solve (PS)

Wang et al. 2023
,
Step-back

Zheng et al. 2024
-differ in how the prompt guides the language models’ reasoning. Additionally, we include
CoT+RAG

Lewis et al. 2020b
, a conventional retrieval-augmented method that retrieves documents using the original question as query.

Decomposed Reasoning methods first break down the original problem into subquestions and solve them sequentially. This group includes
Least-to-Most

Zhou et al. 2023
, which solves each subquestion directly without retreival,
Step-back
, which applies principle extraction at the subquestion level, and
RAG
, which retrieves and uses evidence for each subquestion individually. More detailed descriptions of each baseline can be found in Appendix
B.1

4.3
Main Results

As shown in Table
1
, our proposed method, RAISE, consistently outperforms all baseline reasoning strategies across three benchmark datasets: GPQA, SuperGPQA, and MMLU.
On GPQA, RAISE achieves 51.01%, surpassing the next-best method (CoT+RAG) by over 5 percentage points. On SuperGPQA, which consists of especially challenging science and engineering questions, RAISE achieves the highest performance across all subdomains, with gains especially notable in the science-middle subset (19.60%, +2 pts over the best baseline). On MMLU, RAISE also shows significant improvement, particularly in (Pro) Chemistry (28.36%) and STEM College Chemistry (51.00%), indicating its robustness across both professional and academic scientific reasoning tasks. This result shows that RAISE effectively decomposes scientific reasoning questions and retrieves documents that help the model to solve the questions.

Unlike RAISE, decomposed reasoning methods do not always lead to better performance. This is often due to a lack of logically relevant knowledge especially for smaller LLMs, which limits their ability to reason effectively across multiple steps
(
Hosseini et al. 2024
;
Xu et al. 2025
)
. In fact, the original papers of our baseline models that use decomposed reasoning mostly focused on proprietary large LLMs while we focus on open-sourced smaller LLMs. Despite well-structured decomposition into subquestions, a lack of logically relevant knowledge may cause the model to produce hallucinations or unsupported reasoning steps. Especially for smaller models that lack sufficient prior knowledge, problem decomposition can become counterproductive unless logically relevant documents are retrieved to support the reasoning process.

Also, we observe that in some cases, models without retrieval outperform their retrieval-augmented counterparts. While retrieval is generally expected to enhance performance by providing additional information, these results reveal that naively applying retrieval can be ineffective or even harmful. This issue becomes particularly pronounced in settings involving open-domain or in-the-wild corpora, where the risk of retrieving noisy or irrelevant content is high. Conventional RAG methods often retrieve documents that are only superficially related or worse, focus on irrelevant variables or distractors rather than the core principles required for reasoning. These findings highlight the importance of retrieving logically relevant knowledge, and further underscore the effectiveness of our method.

Impact of Logical Query Generation.
To evaluate the impact of logical query generation in retrieval-augmented reasoning, we conduct an experiment comparing our method, RAISE, with three RAG-based decomposed reasoning baselines: RAG, step-back+RAG
(
Zheng et al. 2024
)
, and HyDE
(
Gao et al. 2023a
)
. While all methods incorporate problem decomposition and retrieval, they differ in how the retrieval query is constructed.

RAISE begins with a short key phrase known as the initial query, and expands it into a logical query that captures not only domain similarity but also the underlying reasoning intent of the subquestion. In contrast, the standard RAG baseline directly uses the subquestion itself as the retrieval query. The step-back+RAG approach attempts to abstract a general principle from the subquestion and uses that principle as the query. HyDE generates a synthetic passage in response to the subquestion and uses this generated text as the retrieval query, aiming to enrich the query context.

As shown in Table

These results confirm that RAISE’s logical query generation goes beyond retrieving documents that are merely domain-relevant or superficially similar, enabling the retrieval of knowledge that is logically aligned with the reasoning required to solve the problem. 4.5
Analysis of RAISE

To further investigate our hypothesis that RAISE retrieves documents that are logically more relevant compared to other baselines, we use LLM-as-a-judge (GPT-4o-mini) to evaluate the logical relevancy of the retrieved documents. Conditioned on the question, subquestion for a specific step, and the retrieved documents, the evaluator model evaluates the logical relevancy among 4 levels of logical relevancy: (1)
Not relevant at all
, (2)
Superficially relevant
(topically related but logically unhelpful), (3)
Partially logically relevant
(some useful reasoning content), and (4)
Fully logically relevant
(logically sufficient to solve the subquestion).

The results are illustrated in Figure
4
. Compared to other baselines that also applies RAG, RAISE has the lowest ratio of documents that are irrelevant at all or only superficially relevant (relevant in terms of domain knowledge, but not relevant logically) and highest ratio of documents that are at least partially logically relevant. This indicates that RAISE avoids retrieving documents that may interrupt the reasoning process for scientific reasoning through logical query generation.

We also qualitatively demonstrate the examples when RAISE retrieves logically relevant documents compared to convential RAG in Table
4
. While RAG often retrieves documents that are topically related yet fail to address the reasoning needs of the subquestion, RAISE consistently identifies documents that include essential scientific principles, mechanisms, or equations. For instance, in questions involving chemical reactions or physical formulas, RAISE surfaces materials that explain the specific reactivity or the retarded time calculation, whereas RAG may return vague definitions or unrelated economic concepts. These cases illustrate how RAISE’s retrieval is not only domain-aware but also aligned with the logical demands of solving complex scientific problems.

Related Works

LLMs for Scientific Reasoning.
Recent works have shown that LLMs can be applied for challenging scientific reasoning tasks. Unlike other domains, scientific reasoning requires not only step-by-step thinking, but also knowledge of specialized terminology and adaptation to continually evolving knowledge. Due to this challenging nature, many benchmarks have been proposed recently to tackle scientific reasoning with LLMs
(
Rein et al. 2024
;
Zhong et al. 2025
;
Zhang et al. 2025b
;
Wang et al. 2024a
;
Jiang et al. 2024
)
. Many works enhance scientific reasoning capabilities of LLMs through domain-specific training
(
Taylor et al. 2022
;
Prabhakar et al. 2025
;
Zhang et al. 2024b
)
, step-by-step reasoning
(
Rueda et al. 2025
;
Rein et al. 2024
)
, or retrieval of external knowledge or tools
(
Ma et al. 2024
;
Zhong et al. 2025
;
Wellawatte et al. 2025
;
Li et al. 2025
)
. Unlike previous works, we focus on applying step-by-step document retrieval from in-the-wild corpus without assuming access to well-curated and domain-specific corpus.

Step-wise Reasoning.
A growing body of research has shown that decomposing complex problems into structured intermediate steps can enhance the reasoning abilities of LLMs. An influential early approach, Chain-of-Thought prompting
Wei et al. 2022
, introduced explicit, sequential reasoning steps, making the model’s thought process more transparent and coherent. This inspired methods such as Plan-and-Solve
Wang et al. 2023
, which emphasizes high-level planning before answering, and Step-Back Prompting
Zheng et al. 2024
, which encourages abstraction by prompting the model to reflect before solving. Least-to-Most prompting
Zhou et al. 2023
extends this by breaking down tasks into simpler subproblems, solved in increasing order of difficulty.

While prior work has focused on prompting strategies that help LLMs better use their internal reasoning capabilities, our work addresses a complementary challenge: enabling LLMs to retrieve and apply information from in-the-wild sources like Wikipedia, particularly during step-wise problem solving. We investigate how external evidence can be integrated at each step to improve reasoning beyond what internal knowledge alone can achieve.

Retrieval-Augmented Generation.
Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG)
Lewis et al. 2020b
was initially proposed to improve LLMs’ factual accuracy and knowledge by retrieving relevant external documents during generation
Lewis et al. 2020b
;
Karpukhin et al. 2020
;
Izacard and Grave 2021
;
Khattab and Zaharia 2020
.

Recently, RAG has been extended for multi-hop reasoning, performing retrieval iteratively at multiple reasoning steps
Liu et al. 2025
;
Zhao et al. 2024b
;
Zheng et al. 2024
. In parallel, query reformulation and expansion techniques have been developed to enhance retrieval. Instead of using the original question, models generate enriched queries through prompting, such as intermediate answers or summaries. For example, HyDE
Gao et al. 2023a
and CSQE
Lei et al. 2024
demonstrate that carefully crafted queries greatly improve retrieval in complex, multi-step tasks.

Building on this line of work, we redesign query expansion techniques with the specific goal of retrieving documents that contain the key logic or underlying principles required at each step of a step-wise reasoning process. This enables the model to supplement its limited internal knowledge with external sources, leading to more complete problem solving, especially in complex, multi-step tasks.

6
Conclusion

We introduce RAISE, a step-by-step retrieval framework for scientific reasoning. We first decompose the problem into multiple subquestions and search queries, and then generate logical queries and retrieve logically relevant documents from in-the-wild corpus. We demonstrate the effectiveness of RAISE on three scientific reasoning benchmarks by comparing with various baselines. Our analysis shows that RAISE retrieves documents that are not only relevant in terms of the domain (e.g. definition of specificalized terminology) but also logically relevant documents for each subquestion, assisting the step-by-step reasoning process required for scientific reasoning.

Limitations

In our study, we primarily conducted experiments using relatively smaller language models, mainly due to computational resource constraints. As a result, the performance observed may not fully reflect the potential of larger-scale models. Future work could explore scaling up the model size to assess its impact on reasoning capabilities and overall performance.

For the retrieval process, we used only DPR as the retriever. While DPR has been widely recognized for its effectiveness, we believe that more recent retrieval models
Gao et al. 2023b
;
Basu et al. 2024
;
Shi et al. 2023
, could lead to a substantial improvement in performance. Incorporating these advanced retrievers remains a promising direction for future research.

Furthermore, our work is particularly focused on scientific reasoning tasks. While this domain presents unique challenges and complexities, extending our approach to other domains such as legal, commonsense, or medical reasoning could be an interesting and valuable avenue for further investigation. Such cross-domain studies may help validate the generalizability and robustness of RAISE.

Additionally, RAISE relies on multiple calls to large language models during the logical query generation phase. Although this multi-step querying is essential for generating effective logical queries that maximize retrieval efficiency, exploring strategies to reduce the number of LLM invocations, such as more efficient prompting, intermediate caching, or lightweight planning modules, could enhance the overall efficiency and practicality of our method in real-world applications.

Dataset Details

GPQA

Rein et al. 2024
This dataset consists of physics, biology, and chemistry questions written by domain experts. We use GPQA diamond subset, which consist of 198 high-quality questions selected based on human performance. Specifically, this subset includes questions that both experts answer correctly while the majority of non-experts fail to solve. Each question typically demands multi-step reasoning, precise formula manipulation, and access to external scientific facts (e.g., physical constants, definitions). Due to its alignment with our target setting, GPQA serves as the primary evaluation benchmark throughout our experiments.

For GPQA, the original dataset does not include standardized multiple-choice labeled as (A), (B), (C) and (D). To ensure consistency during evaluation, we proprocessed each question by randomly shuffling the correct answer along with the three distractors, and assigning them uniformly to choice labels (A) through (D).

SuperGPQA

(

SuperGPQA is a large-scale benchmark designed to evaluate graduate-level reasoning across 13 disciplines, 72 fields, and 285 graduate-level disciplines. In alignment with the scientific reasoning focus of our work, we select science and engineering domains for evaluation. Each domain is further divided by three difficulty levels(easy, medium, and hard). To reduce computational overhead while maintaining consistency, we randomly sample 199 questions per subset using a fixed seed (42). Specifically, our experiments include 199 examples each from science-hard, science-middle, and engineering-hard subsets.

MMLU

(
Hendrycks et al. 2021
;

The MMLU benchmark covers a wide range of subjects across multiple domains. For out experiments, we focus on

These subsets are chosen to evaluate our method’s ability to perform scientific reasoning in both academic and professional contexts involving complex domain knowledge.

Appendix B
Experiment Details

B.1
Baseline Details

CoT

Wei et al. 2022
;
Kojima et al. 2023
We apply Chain-of-Thought prompting for direct reasoning, where the model is encouraged to explicitly generate intermediate reasoning steps through prompting (Think step by step).

PS

Wang et al. 2023
Plan-and-Solve prompting is a direct reasoning strategy where the model first outlines a plan and then executes it step by step. We use the same prompt as the original paper.

Step-back
(Direct reasoning)
Zheng et al. 2024
Step-back prompting guides reasoning using abstracted principles derived from the original question. In our implementation, we follow the original Step-back paper’s prompting strategy to extract high-level principles necessary for solving the question. These extracted principles are then provided together with the original question as input to guide the model’s reasoning process.

CoT+RAG

Lewis et al. 2020b
We implement CoT+RAG by combining Chain-of-Thought prompting with retrieval, where the model is prompted to solve the problem step-by-step while also leveraging external knowledge. Specifically, we provide the model with a CoT-style prompt encouraging step-by-step reasoning, alongside the original question and documents retrieved using the original question as the search query.

Least-to-Most

Zhou et al. 2023
Least-to-Most is a decomposed reasoning strategy that breaks down a complex problem into a sequence of simpler subquestions, which are then solved sequentially without retrieval augmentation. This subquestion decomposition pipeline serves as the foundational structure for other decomposed reasoning methods as well.

Step-back
(Decomposed reasoning)
Zheng et al. 2024
We implement Step-back for decomposed reasoning by applying the Step-back prompting method to each subquestion in a decomposed reasoning framework. While the original Step-back paper does not cover the application of this method to decomposed subquestions, we extend it for a fair comparison with our approach. Specifically, after decomposing the original question into subquestions, we use the Step-back prompting strategy to extract a high-level principle for each subquestion, and then provide the subquestion along with its corresponding principle to guide the model’s reasoning.

RAG
(Decomposed reasoning)
Liu et al. 2025
We implement RAG by first decomposing the original problem into subquestions and then retrieving documents using each subquestion as a query. The retrieved documents are provided to the model along with the corresponding subquestion to support its reasoning.

B.2
Retriever Configuration

We use the pre-trained DPR encoder from the ’facebook/dpr-question_encoder-single-nq-base’ model
Karpukhin et al. 2020
, which is a BERT-based encoder trained for open-domain question answering. This encoder is trained on the Natural Question (NQ) dataset
Kwiatkowski et al. 2019
and is designed to map questions into 768-dimensional dense vector representations for retrieval.

For the retrieval corpus, we use the preprocessed Wikipedia passages provided by ’facebook/wiki_dpr’
Karpukhin et al. 2020
, a corpus widely used to evaluate DPR-based retrieval models. This corpus is constructed from the December 20, 2018 Wikipedia dump, where each article is split into multiple, disjoint text blocks of 100 words, resulting in approximately 21 million passages. Each passage is accompanied by the title of the wikipedia page it comes from along with DPR embedding.

To enable efficient retrieval over the passage embeddings, we use an exact FAISS index. FAISS (Facebook AI Similarity Search)
Johnson et al. 2019
;
Douze et al. 2024
is widely used library for fast similarity search over dense vectors.

Throughout all experiment, we retrieve top-10 documents per query.

B.3
RAISE

To reduce the impact of potentially irrelevant documents by DPR, we apply a similarity threshold
T
in RAISE.Specifically, we discard any retrieved passage whose DPR similarity score falls below
T
. DPR similarity is computed as the inner product between L2-normalized query and passage embeddings. Higher scores indicate greater semantic similarity, with values closer to 1 representing stronger alignment between the query and passage.

We set
T=0.84
for GPQA, SuperGPQA, and MMLU-Pro, which are composed of more challenging reasoning problems. For MMLU-STEM (college chemistry), we use a slightly lower threshold of
T=0.80
, considering that the questions are generally simpler than those in other datasets.
