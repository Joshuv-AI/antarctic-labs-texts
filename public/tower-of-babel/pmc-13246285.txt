Notch signaling is an evolutionarily conserved pathway that plays an essential role in embryonic development, including arterial–venous specification, vascular morphogenesis, and hematopoietic differentiation 1 , 2) . Although initially described as a developmental signaling pathway, accumulating evidence indicates that Notch signaling is reactivated in adult vascular disease 3) . In this regard, the cellular plasticity and chronic inflammation inherent to atherosclerosis may facilitate the re-engagement of embryonic signaling programs in the adult vessel wall ( Table 1 ) .

Notch Ligands and Atherosclerosis: Selected Evidence

Among the Notch ligands, Delta-like ligand 4 (Dll4) has been extensively investigated in cardiometabolic diseases. In hyperlipidemic Ldlr−/− mice, the antibody-mediated blockade of Dll4 attenuated atherosclerotic lesion formation and metabolic abnormalities 4) . Similarly, in an Ldlr−/− mouse vein graft model, macrophage-derived Dll4 promoted lesion development and vascular remodeling 5) . These studies established Dll4 as a pro-atherogenic Notch ligand that enhances macrophage inflammatory activation.

In this issue, Orkhonselenge et al. demonstrated that the antibody-mediated blockade of Delta-like ligand 1 (Dll1) attenuates atherosclerotic lesion development and modulates the features associated with plaque vulnerability in ApoE−/− mice 6) . Dll1 inhibition reduced lesion formation at the aortic root during early disease and, in advanced plaques, decreased lipid accumulation, suppressed Mmp9 expression, increased collagen content, and enhanced fibrous cap smooth muscle thickness 6) . Although direct mechanical ruptures were not assessed, these coordinated changes suggest that Dll1 signaling may influence plaque destabilization rather than simply the lesion size.

Mechanistically, stimulation with Dll1 enhanced the expression of inflammatory cytokines and the activation of NF-κB in macrophages, whereas the inhibition of the canonical Notch transcriptional complex did not suppress TNF induction 6 - 8) . These findings suggest that Dll1 engages non-canonical signaling pathways involving NF-κB and may therefore regulate the ligand-specific inflammatory programs in atherosclerosis.

Single-cell RNA-sequencing analyses revealed suggestive, but not definitive, expression patterns of DLL1 and DLL4 across the macrophage subsets within human plaques 6 , 9) . These observations argue against a uniform model of Notch activation and support the concept of the ligand-specific regulation of immune responses. While Dll4 has been linked to sustained inflammatory activation in hyperlipidemic models 4 , 5) , Dll1 may preferentially influence extracellular matrix remodeling and plaque vulnerability 6) .

Other Notch ligands, including Jagged1 and Jagged2, are expressed in vascular and immune cells. Jagged1 has been implicated in endothelial identity and vascular remodeling during development 2) and it has also been shown to exert context-dependent effects in angiogenesis, sometimes opposing Dll4-mediated signaling 10) . Although the direct evidence linking Jagged ligands to atherosclerosis remains limited, the diversity of ligand expression suggests that Notch signaling in vascular disease represents a complex ligand-specific network rather than a single linear pathway.

In a wider context, plaque progression may involve the partial reactivation of developmental signaling programs in the adult vascular wall. The phenotypic modulation of smooth muscle cells, endothelial activation, macrophage plasticity, and extracellular matrix remodeling are processes reminiscent of developmental biology 1 - 3) . Dll1-mediated signaling may represent one node within this re-engaged developmental framework that contributes to plaque instability.

Several important questions remain. Dll1 is expressed by multiple vascular cell types, and systemic antibody blockade does not distinguish macrophage-autonomous effects from non-autonomous effects. Given the fundamental roles of Notch signaling in tissue homeostasis, therapeutic targeting will require careful evaluation of both safety and specificity. Nonetheless, this study expands the understanding of Notch signaling in atherosclerosis beyond a Dll4-centric model and suggests that the ligand-specific modulation of developmental and inflammatory pathways may contribute to plaque destabilization.
