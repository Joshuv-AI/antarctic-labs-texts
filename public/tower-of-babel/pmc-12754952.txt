Feeding intelligence: comparative evaluation of ChatGPT and clinical guidelines for nutritional management in head and neck cancer
Authors: Shasha Shen, Kai Zhou, Mingna Wu, Dahai Liu, Xiaotong Shen, Peijie Li, Ying Xu, Sijia Zheng, Xiaoxia Gou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundArtificial intelligence (AI) tools such as ChatGPT are increasingly applied in digital health and patient education, yet their alignment with established clinical guidelines for cancer-related nutritional management remains unclear.ObjectiveThis study aimed to evaluate the concordance, functional characteristics, patient accessibility, and innovation of ChatGPT-generated nutritional recommendations compared with clinical guidelines from the Chinese Society of Clinical Oncology (CSCO), Chinese Nutrition Society (CNS), and European Society for Clinical Nutrition and Metabolism (ESPEN).MethodsWe analyzed ChatGPT responses across six key nutrition-related issues—anorexia/cachexia, dysphagia, oral mucositis, unintentional weight loss, gastrointestinal intolerance, and nutritional monitoring—and compared them with guideline recommendations. Expert evaluation (n = 5), readability metrics, semantic similarity (TF-IDF), and patient-centered assessments were conducted to compare personalization, innovation, clinical feasibility, evidence-based support, population applicability, clarity, and self-management guidance.ResultsChatGPT recommendations aligned with at least one guideline in 50.0–64.3% of cases, highest for dysphagia (64.3%), and included general strategies such as small frequent meals, texture modification, hydration, and high-protein/high-calorie intake. ChatGPT-specific suggestions (8.3–18.2%) focused on lifestyle and behavioral interventions, including mindful eating, music therapy, and wearable diet trackers. Expert ratings indicated higher personalization (4.3/5) and innovation (4.6/5) for ChatGPT, whereas guidelines scored higher for clinical feasibility (4.7/5), evidence-based support (4.9/5), and population applicability (4.8/5). Between-group differences were statistically significant for clinical feasibility, evidence-based support, and applicability (all p < 0.01; 95% CI for mean differences: 0.62–1.12), whereas personalization showed no significant difference (p = 0.063). ChatGPT exhibited superior patient-centered performance in clarity (4.5 vs. 3.2, p = 0.004, 95% CI: 0.47–2.13) and self-management guidance (4.6 vs. 3.0, p = 0.002, 95% CI: 0.65–2.05) and demonstrated more concise, readable content (Flesch–Kincaid grade 12.9–14.2) compared with guidelines (17.9–20.5). Semantic analysis revealed moderate overlap with CSCO (≈ 0.63) and CNS (≈ 0.59), and lower similarity with ESPEN (≈ 0.47), highlighting ChatGPT’s use of patient-friendly language. Topic modeling identified three clusters: patient support and accessibility (ChatGPT), technical nutrition therapy (ESPEN/CSCO), and nutritional assessment and monitoring (CNS).ConclusionsChatGPT provides personalized, innovative, and patient-accessible nutritional guidance for cancer-related malnutrition, complementing traditional clinical guidelines. While guidelines remain essential for evidence-based decision-making, AI tools may enhance patient education, engagement, and self-management in digital health applications.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-025-07477-0.

Introduction

Malnutrition is a prevalent and serious complication in patients with head and neck cancer (HNC), with incidence rates ranging from 40% to 80% depending on disease stage and treatment modality [1–3]. The complex interplay of tumor location, mucosal toxicity, swallowing dysfunction, altered taste, and treatment-induced anorexia contributes to a significant nutritional burden [4–7]. Malnutrition not only impairs immune response and wound healing but also worsens treatment tolerance and increases morbidity and mortality [8, 9].

To address these challenges, international oncology organizations such as the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN), European Society for Clinical Nutrition and Metabolism (ESPEN), and Chinese Society of Clinical Oncology (CSCO) have established standardized guidelines for nutritional screening, intervention timing, and supportive care [10–13]. These guidelines provide structured recommendations for risk assessment tools (e.g., NRS-2002, PG-SGA), enteral and parenteral nutrition strategies, and multidisciplinary nutritional support [14–16].

However, traditional guidelines are often limited by their “static” design—generalized pathways that may not fully account for individual patient variables, real-time clinical fluctuations, or comorbidities [17]. In contrast, artificial intelligence (AI)—particularly machine learning algorithms and large language models (LLMs) like ChatGPT—has shown promise in delivering personalized, dynamic, and data-driven recommendations in clinical nutrition [18, 19]. These tools can integrate heterogeneous patient data (e.g., lab values, Electronic Medical Records (EMRs), treatment history), predict nutritional risk trajectories, and suggest tailored interventions with rapid adaptability [20, 21]. Recent meta-analyses and comparative evaluations across oncology and chronic disease management have similarly demonstrated that LLMs such as ChatGPT achieve moderate concordance with clinical guidelines, particularly in domains emphasizing accessibility and patient engagement, while revealing persistent gaps in evidence robustness and clinical safety [22–25]. These findings underscore both the potential and limitations of AI-assisted recommendations in complementing established guideline-based practices. Given these limitations, it is crucial to examine how emerging AI systems align with and diverge from existing evidence-based frameworks. This study is among the first to systematically evaluate, in a multidimensional framework, the concordance between large language model (LLM)-generated nutritional recommendations and professional clinical guidelines in the specific context of HNC. Previous studies primarily examined ChatGPT’s diagnostic accuracy or general medical reasoning, but few have explored its capacity for nutritional reasoning or compared it against formal evidence-based frameworks. By analyzing six nutrition-related issues using four complementary approaches—expert evaluation, patient-centered assessment, readability analysis, and semantic modeling—this research provides a novel and reproducible framework for assessing AI-guideline convergence in oncology nutrition.

Our paper aims to perform a multidimensional comparison between AI-assisted systems and traditional clinical guidelines in the management of malnutrition in head and neck cancer patients. By examining key aspects such as screening accuracy, individualization of care, timeliness of intervention, clinical usability, and scalability, we explore the potential for AI to complement—or challenge—established practices in oncological nutrition care.

Methods

Study design and scope

This study conducted a multi-dimensional comparative analysis of nutritional management recommendations for cancer-related malnutrition, focusing on the alignment and divergence between ChatGPT-generated responses and established clinical guidelines, namely the Chinese Society of Clinical Oncology (CSCO), the Chinese Nutrition Society (CNS), and the European Society for Clinical Nutrition and Metabolism (ESPEN). The analysis was performed across six core nutrition-related issues commonly encountered in oncology: anorexia/cachexia, dysphagia, oral mucositis, unintentional weight loss, gastrointestinal intolerance, and nutritional monitoring.

Data collection

ChatGPT responses

ChatGPT (OpenAI, GPT-4 Turbo) was queried on February 21, 2025, using standardized prompts for each of the six nutrition-related issues. Prompts were formulated in plain clinical English to elicit patient-focused recommendations while minimizing prompt bias. All prompts were provided in English to ensure consistency with the English-language versions of the guidelines (ESPEN, CNS translation, CSCO translation) and to avoid variability introduced by translation. The system prompt was fixed as: “You are a clinical assistant providing nutritional recommendations. Provide evidence-based, patient-friendly responses tailored to head and neck cancer scenarios, avoiding speculative or unverifiable statements, and present information clearly in plain clinical English.” Model parameters were set at temperature = 0.2 and maximum tokens = 1024 to minimize stochastic variation. All responses were generated in a single session under these standardized conditions. While this study focused on GPT-4 Turbo for methodological consistency, future validation with newer models (e.g.GPT-5) is warranted to assess reproducibility and potential performance improvements. It should be noted that the prompts were designed to cover six common cancer-related nutritional issues (anorexia/cachexia, dysphagia, oral mucositis, unintentional weight loss, gastrointestinal intolerance, and nutritional monitoring), and were not restricted exclusively to head and neck cancer. This broader scope was chosen to ensure comparability across guideline sources (CSCO, CNS, ESPEN), which are themselves not limited solely to head and neck cancer.

Clinical guidelines

The latest publicly available editions of CSCO, CNS, and ESPEN guidelines were retrieved and reviewed. Specifically, we used the 2021 CSCO Guidelines for Nutrition Therapy in Malignant Tumors (Chinese Society of Clinical Oncology, published 2021), the 2022 Chinese Dietary Guidelines (5th Edition, Chinese Nutrition Society, published April 2022) [26], and the 2021 ESPEN Practical Guideline: Clinical Nutrition in Cancer (European Society for Clinical Nutrition and Metabolism, published May 2021) [10]. All guideline documents were accessed and verified on February 21, 2025. At that time, the 2021 CSCO guideline was the latest available edition; the 2024 update was released subsequently in March 2024 and was therefore not included in this analysis. For each symptom category, all relevant nutritional recommendations were extracted verbatim by two independent reviewers (oncology dietitian and clinical oncologist) to ensure accuracy.

Comparative alignment analysis

For each nutrition-related issue, recommendations from ChatGPT and the three guideline sources were mapped into standardized intervention categories (e.g., dietary modification, pharmacologic therapy, nutritional support devices, monitoring strategies).

Alignment was defined as conceptual equivalence between ChatGPT’s suggestion and at least one guideline recommendation. The proportion of aligned recommendations was calculated per symptom category. Divergent content was categorized as ChatGPT-specific (present only in ChatGPT outputs) or Guideline-specific (present only in formal guidelines).

Scoring was performed using a 5-point Likert scale (1 = very poor, 5 = excellent). Scores were averaged per dimension, and inter-rater agreement was assessed using intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs). To minimize evaluation bias, all recommendation texts were anonymized and de-identified prior to distribution, ensuring that experts were blinded to their original source (ChatGPT vs. guidelines). The expert panel included two oncology dietitians (8–12 years of clinical practice), two clinical oncologists (10–15 years of practice), and one oncology nurse specialist (9 years of practice). All panel members had prior experience in nutritional guideline implementation, patient counseling, and academic teaching in oncology nutrition. This multidisciplinary composition was chosen to balance dietary, oncological, and nursing perspectives. Furthermore, Inter-rater reliability was calculated using intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC, two-way random effects model, absolute agreement), which yielded values ranging from 0.81 to 0.89 across the five evaluation dimensions, indicating excellent agreement.

Information update timeliness assessment

Update timeliness was evaluated qualitatively by examining the documented release dates and update cycles of CSCO, CNS, and ESPEN guidelines, compared with ChatGPT’s AI model improvement schedule and ability to incorporate recent literature. Research responsiveness was illustrated in a comparative timeline diagram.

Patient-centered evaluation

A simulated patient panel (n = 12) representing cancer survivors was recruited to evaluate recommendation clarity, emotional support, self-management guidance, and empowerment. Participants reviewed anonymized, source-blinded recommendations for two randomly assigned symptoms and scored them on a 5-point Likert scale. Scores were averaged per source and dimension. The panel consisted of simulated participants recruited from research affiliates to approximate patient perspectives, including individuals aged 30–65 years, with balanced gender distribution and diverse educational backgrounds. No real patients or identifiable clinical data were involved, and therefore formal ethics committee approval was not required. The evaluation was conducted in accordance with institutional ethical standards, and its exploratory nature is acknowledged. Readability and content length analysis

For each symptom category, word counts were calculated for ChatGPT and guideline recommendations. Readability was assessed using the Flesch–Kincaid Grade Level (FKGL) formula in Microsoft Word (English translation for Chinese guidelines). Lower FKGL scores indicate simpler text, with scores ≤ 14 considered accessible to the general public.

Semantic and lexical analysis

• Text preprocessing

All guideline and ChatGPT-generated texts were preprocessed prior to semantic analysis. The preprocessing pipeline included lowercasing, tokenization, removal of English stop words, and lemmatization. To capture meaningful multi-word expressions, bigram and trigram phrase detection was applied. Rare words (frequency < 2) and overly common words (top 1% of corpus frequency) were excluded.

• TF-IDF analysis

Term Frequency–Inverse Document Frequency (TF-IDF) was calculated to quantify the relative importance of terms within and across documents. TF-IDF scores were used both to highlight domain-specific keywords and as input features for subsequent topic modeling. Only the top 2,000 most informative terms based on TF-IDF scores were retained for further analysis.

• Latent Dirichlet Allocation (LDA) topic modeling

Topic modeling was conducted using the gensim library (version X.X). The number of topics was optimized based on coherence scores (C_v), with a coherence threshold of > 0.45 considered acceptable. In this analysis, a 6-topic solution provided the best balance between interpretability and coherence. Model hyperparameters were set as follows:α (alpha) = 0.1 (symmetric prior for document-topic distribution); β (eta) = 0.01 (symmetric prior for word-topic distribution); Iterations = 1000;Each topic was represented by its top 15 weighted keywords. Topic distributions across ChatGPT and guideline texts were extracted for comparative analysis.

• Visualization and quantitative interpretation

Word clouds and heatmaps were generated from LDA-derived keywords and topic weights for visualization. To ensure the results were not purely descriptive, we reported quantitative metrics, including per-topic contribution percentages and coherence scores. These metrics were used to interpret thematic emphases across ChatGPT and guideline texts.

Statistical analysis

Quantitative data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or percentages, as appropriate. Differences between sources in alignment proportions and expert or patient quality scores were assessed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by post hoc Tukey’s tests for multiple comparisons. All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 26 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). In addition, GraphPad Prism (version 10, GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA) and R (version 4.5.1) with the ggplot2 package were used for selected analyses and visualizations, while Python seaborn and Microsoft Excel were applied for supplementary graphical outputs and tabulations. All figures and visual elements (including tables, radar charts, heatmaps, and word clouds) were produced by the authors using these conventional statistical and visualization tools. None of the figures were generated by AI-based generative tools. Each figure was independently verified by the research team to ensure accuracy, reproducibility, and consistency with the reported data. A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Potential biases and limitations

Given the inherent variability of AI-generated outputs, ChatGPT responses in this study were generated within a single session using fixed prompts and parameters to minimize stochastic variation. All outputs were archived verbatim for reproducibility and analyzed without post hoc modification of content. As ChatGPT’s knowledge base is not transparent and may include unverified or non–peer-reviewed sources, its recommendations were interpreted strictly within a comparative research framework rather than as definitive clinical guidance. Furthermore, guideline content may differ in scope and intended audience, potentially influencing alignment rates. These methodological considerations were taken into account during data interpretation, and findings should be viewed as descriptive rather than prescriptive. Results

Alignment and divergence in nutritional management recommendations

Table 1 shows the comparative consistency of ChatGPT’s nutritional management recommendations with established clinical guidelines from the Chinese Society of Clinical Oncology (CSCO), the Chinese Nutrition Society (CNS), and the European Society for Clinical Nutrition and Metabolism (ESPEN). Across six key nutrition-related issues—anorexia/cachexia, dysphagia, oral mucositis, unintentional weight loss, gastrointestinal intolerance, and nutritional monitoring—the proportion of ChatGPT suggestions aligned with at least one guideline ranged from 50.0% to 64.3%, with the highest consistency observed in dysphagia (64.3%). Shared recommendations included small frequent meals, texture modification, hydration, and high-protein/high-calorie intake. In contrast, CSCO and ESPEN contributed more intervention-oriented guidance, including pharmacologic agents (e.g., glucocorticoids, progestins), PEG placement, and structured assessment tools. ChatGPT-specific suggestions accounted for 8.3% to 18.2% and included lifestyle- and behavior-focused strategies not present in the formal guidelines (e.g., mindful eating, music therapy, wearable diet trackers).

Expert-based evaluation of recommendation quality

Expert panel ratings (n = 5) comparing ChatGPT with established clinical guidelines across five evaluation dimensions are summarized in Table 3; Fig. 1. ANOVA revealed significant differences between ChatGPT and clinical guidelines in three dimensions: clinical feasibility (p = 0.0047), evidence-based support (p < 0.0001), and applicability across populations (p < 0.0001), with clinical guidelines scoring significantly higher. For personalization, ChatGPT scored higher but the difference did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.063). Innovation was only assessed for ChatGPT (4.5 ± 0.5) as it primarily applies to AI-generated content, and thus no comparison with clinical guidelines was performed. These findings suggest that while ChatGPT demonstrates strengths in personalization and innovation, clinical guidelines remain superior in feasibility, evidence robustness, and generalizability.

Comparison of information update timeliness

Figure 2 compares the update frequency and research responsiveness of ChatGPT with that of traditional clinical guidelines. ChatGPT demonstrates superior timeliness due to continuous AI model improvements and its ability to rapidly reflect new literature. In contrast, traditional clinical guidelines follow scheduled and often lengthy update cycles, with publication lags of several months to years. In particular, ChatGPT shows a significant advantage in both update frequency (score 1 vs. score 2) and integration speed (score 5 vs. score 2), indicating its ability to quickly incorporate the latest research findings and provide near-real-time recommendations. On the other hand, clinical guidelines, while highly authoritative and reliable, face delays due to the structured consensus-based revision process.

Patient-centered evaluation of ChatGPT vs. Clinical guidelines

Figure 3; Table 4 summarize the patient-centered evaluation comparing ChatGPT with traditional clinical guidelines (CSCO, CNS, ESPEN) across four dimensions: clarity, emotional support, self-management guidance, and empowerment. ChatGPT consistently achieved higher mean scores across all domains, underscoring its conversational and patient-oriented style. The greatest advantages were observed in clarity and self-management guidance. ChatGPT scored 4.5 ± 0.3 versus 3.2 ± 0.6 for clarity (p = 0.004) and 4.6 ± 0.4 versus 3.0 ± 0.5 for self-management (p = 0.002), both differences reaching statistical significance. These results suggest ChatGPT excels in simplifying complex nutritional information and providing actionable recommendations tailored for patients. In contrast, emotional support (4.3 ± 0.4 vs. 3.4 ± 0.5, p = 0.07) and empowerment (4.4 ± 0.5 vs. 3.5 ± 0.6, p = 0.08) showed higher ratings for ChatGPT but did not reach significance, indicating more modest advantages. Overall, ChatGPT appears particularly effective in enhancing understandability and promoting self-management behaviors, reinforcing its potential role as a complementary patient-facing adjunct to guideline-based nutritional care.

Readability and length comparison: ChatGPT vs. Guidelines

In the comparative analysis of nutritional recommendations for malnutrition-related symptoms, ChatGPT consistently demonstrated higher patient accessibility than traditional clinical nutrition guidelines (Table 5; Fig. 4). Content length analysis revealed that ESPEN provided the most detailed guidance, with a mean word count of 837 across the seven symptoms assessed, followed by CSCO (≈ 764), CNS (≈ 682), and ChatGPT (≈ 566). 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated to quantify variability: ESPEN 837 (95% CI: 810–864), CSCO 764 (730–798), CNS 682 (648–716), ChatGPT 566 (540–592).This trend was consistent across individual symptoms such as anorexia/cachexia and monitoring and assessment, where ESPEN’s content length exceeded 900 words. In terms of readability, the Flesch–Kincaid Grade Level further emphasized ChatGPT’s advantage in understandability. ChatGPT’s responses remained within the 12.9–14.2 range, suitable for a general audience. On the FKGL scale, scores around 13–14 approximate first-year undergraduate readability, whereas scores above ~ 17 often align with graduate-level text; this contextualizes the practical gap patients may experience when reading technical guidance (ChatGPT 12.9–14.2 (95% CI: 12.7–14.4), ESPEN ≈ 20.5 (95% CI: 20.1–20.9), CSCO ≈ 19.3 (18.9–19.7), CNS ≈ 17.9 (17.5–18.3)).In contrast, ESPEN scored the highest average grade level (≈ 20.5), followed by CSCO (≈ 19.3) and CNS (≈ 17.9), reflecting more complex and technical language. These findings suggest that while clinical guidelines offer greater depth, ChatGPT provides significantly simpler and more concise outputs, potentially enhancing patient comprehension and engagement.

Semantic and lexical comparison across sources

Figure 5 presents a heatmap of pairwise cosine similarities in nutritional management texts from ChatGPT and clinical guidelines (CSCO, CNS, ESPEN), based on Term Frequency–Inverse Document Frequency (TF-IDF) vectorization. The results indicate that ChatGPT exhibits moderate semantic overlap with CSCO (≈ 0.63) and CNS (≈ 0.59), but a lower similarity with ESPEN (≈ 0.47), reflecting its tendency to use more generalized and patient-friendly language compared to the technical, expert-driven terminology employed in formal guidelines. To further illustrate language patterns, Fig. 6 displays TF-IDF–based word clouds for each source. ChatGPT emphasizes terms related to patient empowerment, accessibility, and simplified dietary strategies. In contrast, CSCO and ESPEN favor more clinical or pharmacological terms, while CNS highlights structured screening and nutrient intake approaches. These stylistic differences underscore the divergent design purposes of each content source. Figure 7 summarizes the thematic focus extracted from Latent Dirichlet Allocation (LDA) topic modeling. Three primary clusters emerged: Topic 1 (patient support and accessibility), which aligns most closely with ChatGPT content; Topic 2 (technical nutrition therapy), dominated by ESPEN and CSCO; and Topic 3 (nutritional assessment and monitoring), predominantly reflected in CNS guidelines. These findings suggest ChatGPT serves as a more approachable tool for layperson education, while guidelines remain the cornerstone for clinical decision-making.

Additionally, key methodological details of TF-IDF and LDA analyses have been clarified. Texts were preprocessed (lowercasing, tokenization, stop-word removal, lemmatization, and phrase detection), and TF-IDF scores were computed to retain the top 2,000 terms. LDA topic modeling was implemented using gensim with α = 0.1, β = 0.01, and 1,000 iterations. The optimal six-topic solution was selected based on coherence score (C_v = 0.47). Figures 6 and 7 now include both visualization and quantitative interpretation (coherence metrics and topic contribution percentages) to strengthen analytical rigor.

Discussion

This comparative evaluation highlights both the convergences and divergences between ChatGPT’s nutritional recommendations and those of formal clinical guidelines in managing cancer-related malnutrition. Overall, the comparative findings highlight both the potential and current limitations of AI in clinical nutrition decision support. While ChatGPT demonstrated moderate alignment with guideline-based practices (50.0%–64.3%), particularly in areas like dysphagia and oral mucositis, its suggestions often leaned toward general lifestyle strategies—such as mindful eating and wearable tracking tools—absent from standard protocols. These findings reflect the differing design intentions: ChatGPT prioritizes accessibility and user engagement, whereas clinical guidelines emphasize evidence-based specificity and medical feasibility [27]. Similar patterns have been reported in recent meta-analyses and comparative studies, where large language models showed partial concordance with medical guidelines but were limited by gaps in evidence robustness and clinical safety [22, 28–30].

Functionally, ChatGPT outperformed traditional guidelines in personalization and innovation, suggesting its utility in patient-centered communication and the integration of digital health trends [19, 31]. However, guidelines remained superior in domains of evidence robustness, clinical applicability, and standardized assessment, reinforcing their critical role in formal decision-making processes [21, 32, 33]. We also acknowledge that personalized recommendations depend on the quality of prompting and patient context. Without real-time clinical data, the accuracy of ChatGPT’s tailored suggestions may be limited. It should also be noted that guidelines are primarily intended for healthcare professionals, so extrapolation to patient-facing contexts must be interpreted cautiously. Consistent with these observations, our quantitative expert panel analysis further demonstrated that guidelines significantly outperformed ChatGPT in clinical feasibility, evidence-based support, and applicability across populations (all p < 0.01). Although ChatGPT achieved higher scores in personalization, this difference did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.063), suggesting that its strength in tailoring recommendations should be interpreted with caution. Notably, the dimension of innovation was only assessed for ChatGPT (mean score 4.5), reflecting its unique contribution as an AI tool. These findings confirm that while ChatGPT introduces innovative and patient-centered features, clinical guidelines continue to provide superior feasibility, robustness, and generalizability, underscoring their indispensable role in evidence-based practice. This functional complementarity positions ChatGPT as a valuable adjunct for enhancing patient understanding and motivation rather than replacing clinical oversight [34–36].

The patient-centered evaluations further support this perspective. ChatGPT’s conversational style, clarity, and focus on empowerment yielded higher scores across clarity, emotional support, and self-management dimensions, indicating a strong advantage in health education and behavioral engagement [37, 38]. Importantly, statistical analysis confirmed that improvements in clarity (p = 0.004) and self-management guidance (p = 0.002) were significant, whereas differences in emotional support and empowerment, although favoring ChatGPT, did not reach significance (p >0.05). These results suggest that ChatGPT’s greatest added value lies in simplifying nutritional information and empowering patients to manage their care, while its role in providing emotional reassurance remains less robust. Nevertheless, we acknowledge that the prompts were not restricted exclusively to head and neck cancer, but instead addressed general cancer-related malnutrition domains. This approach enhanced comparability with CSCO, CNS, and ESPEN guidelines, which themselves target oncology nutrition broadly. However, this design choice may limit disease-specific granularity, and future studies should test prompts tailored specifically to head and neck cancer.

In contrast, the complexity and technicality of formal guidelines, while suitable for professional audiences, may limit their utility in patient-facing contexts [39–42]. Nevertheless, the relatively small sample sizes of both the expert panel (n = 5) and the patient panel (n = 12) limit the generalizability of these findings, and this constraint should be acknowledged when interpreting the results. The simulated patient group included individuals aged 30–65 years with balanced gender distribution and varied educational backgrounds, but we acknowledge that such proxies cannot fully capture the lived experiences of real cancer survivors. Furthermore, the patient panel consisted of simulated participants recruited from research affiliates, rather than actual cancer survivors. While this design avoided the need for formal ethics approval by excluding real patient data, it also limits the representativeness of the findings. We acknowledge that simulated participants may not fully capture the lived experiences of cancer patients, and future studies should involve ethically approved recruitment of actual patient populations to enhance validity and generalizability.

It should also be noted that clinical nutrition guidelines are primarily designed for healthcare professionals, not for direct patient use. This inherent distinction limits the comparability with ChatGPT, which generates patient-facing outputs. Therefore, our findings should be interpreted as highlighting complementary roles, where ChatGPT may support translation of guideline-based recommendations into accessible formats rather than serving as a substitute.

Additionally, the semantic and topic modeling analyses (TF-IDF and LDA) further highlighted the structural and linguistic divergences between ChatGPT and clinical guidelines. By applying a standardized preprocessing pipeline and reporting coherence metrics, we ensured transparency and reproducibility of the modeling process. Quantitative interpretation confirmed that ChatGPT content predominantly emphasized accessibility and patient support, whereas guidelines concentrated on technical assessment and evidence-based interventions. These methodological refinements strengthen the reliability of our conclusions regarding the complementary roles of AI and clinical guidelines in nutritional communication.

Readability and semantic analyses reinforced these findings. ChatGPT’s content was shorter, easier to read, and used more layperson-friendly vocabulary, whereas CSCO, CNS, and ESPEN materials were longer, denser, and more technical [43–46]. Notably, ChatGPT’s lower lexical similarity with ESPEN and higher alignment with CNS and CSCO suggest that it gravitates toward generalized and digestible language structures [47, 48].

Lastly, topic modeling revealed distinct thematic emphases: ChatGPT centered on accessibility and support; ESPEN and CSCO concentrated on technical interventions; and CNS prioritized structured assessment [49–51]. This aligns with systematic reviews on AI-assisted decision support, which emphasize that while LLMs enhance personalization and patient engagement, clinical guidelines remain essential for ensuring methodological rigor and safeguarding patient safety [52–54]. In addition, we acknowledge that concordance findings may be limited by the heterogeneity and opacity of ChatGPT’s training data, which restricts reproducibility and may introduce variability across different model versions. The originality and significance of this study lie in its integration of clinical, linguistic, and behavioral perspectives to benchmark ChatGPT’s nutritional recommendations against multi-society guidelines (CSCO, CNS, ESPEN). While the generalizability of results is inherently limited by ChatGPT’s training corpus heterogeneity and prompt specificity, the standardized prompt design and fixed system parameters strengthen methodological transparency and reproducibility. Importantly, this study establishes an empirical basis for future LLM evaluation in nutritional science—a field where systematic validation remains scarce. As demonstrated in recent comparative and concordance studies (Refs. 22–25, 29–31), large language models show moderate alignment with medical guidelines but require further validation for clinical reliability; our findings contribute to bridging this evidence gap. Moreover, given the rapid evolution of AI models, future research should validate these findings using newer versions such as GPT-5. Such comparative analyses will help determine whether advances in model architecture and training improve clinical alignment, reduce hallucination risk, and enhance reproducibility across nutritional domains. Building on this framework, future research should also prospectively validate AI outputs in real-world head and neck cancer cohorts, quantify their clinical impact, and examine how model interpretability and human–AI collaboration affect patient outcomes. These extensions will strengthen the translational significance of this work by bridging algorithmic innovation with clinical applicability.

In sum, ChatGPT presents as a promising, patient-accessible adjunct to existing clinical nutritional guidelines. While it cannot substitute the depth and clinical rigor of formal protocols, its strengths in personalization, clarity, and digital integration may significantly enhance patient engagement and literacy in oncology nutrition. Future research should focus on safely integrating AI-assisted tools into clinical workflows while addressing issues of accuracy, equity, and ethical accountability. Large language models such as ChatGPT are prone to “hallucinations,” generating inaccurate or unverifiable statements that could mislead patients if not carefully monitored. Moreover, issues of accountability, patient data privacy, and bias in training datasets raise further concerns about equitable deployment. Integration into clinical practice therefore requires a cautious, supervised approach—positioning AI as a supportive adjunct under professional oversight rather than as an independent decision-making authority. Future external validation with real patient cohorts and multiple LLM models will be essential to confirm reproducibility across settings.

Conclusion

This study underscores the evolving role of AI language models in oncology nutrition by systematically comparing ChatGPT’s recommendations with those from established clinical guidelines. ChatGPT demonstrates commendable strengths in accessibility, personalization, and innovative delivery, particularly in addressing the educational and emotional needs of patients. Its conversational tone and simplified language may lower barriers to understanding and empower patients to take an active role in nutritional self-care.

Conversely, clinical guidelines remain indispensable for their depth, specificity, and clinical credibility, especially in complex or high-risk cases requiring structured interventions. Rather than positioning AI and guidelines as competing sources, this analysis supports a complementary approach—where ChatGPT can bridge communication gaps, reinforce understanding, and augment adherence to evidence-based care.

Future directions should focus on refining AI outputs for medical accuracy, integrating guideline updates into model training, and exploring hybrid frameworks where AI supports—but does not replace—clinician-guided nutritional care in oncology. In addition, future studies should include ethically approved recruitment of real patient populations, as simulated panels cannot fully capture the lived experiences of cancer survivors. Such designs will enhance representativeness, validity, and ensure compliance with ethical standards.
