Serum Anti-Saccharomyces cerevisiae Antibody Is Associated with Disease Severity in Patients with Primary Sclerosing Cholangitis
Authors: Niv Zmora, Lian Bannon, Roie Tzadok, Noy Nachmias, Haim Leibovitzh, Ehud Zigmond
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionPrimary sclerosing cholangitis (PSC) is a chronic liver disease accompanying inflammatory bowel disease (IBD). Anti-Saccharomyces cerevisiae antibody (ASCA) is a serological marker associated with aggressive IBD phenotype. Data on the association between ASCA and liver outcomes remain elusive. We aimed to explore whether ASCA seropositivity is associated with liver disease severity in people living with PSC-IBD.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective study on adult patients with PSC-IBD at a tertiary center, between 2010 and 2023. Medical records were reviewed for demographics, clinical, and laboratory data. Severity of liver disease was assessed using the Amsterdam-Oxford prognostic model (AOM) and liver stiffness measurement (LSM), assessed by vibration-controlled transient elastography (FibroScan®).ResultsThe cohort comprised of 58 subjects with PSC-IBD and an available ASCA result. Thirty patients were diagnosed with ulcerative colitis (UC) and 28 with Crohn’s disease (CD). Six of 30 (20%) patients with UC and 14 of 28 patients (50%) with CD had positive ASCA. In a univariate analysis, AOM (p = 0.021) and LSM (p = 0.018) were significantly higher in those positive for ASCA. In a multivariable model, ASCA positivity remained significantly associated with higher AOM score (p = 0.026) and a higher LSM (p = 0.036), even after adjustment for IBD subtype and disease duration.ConclusionIn subjects with IBD with concomitant PSC, the presence of serum ASCA is associated with increased liver stiffness and AOM score, well-known markers for disease severity and reduced transplant-free survival. Further studies are warranted to validate these findings and to explore the biological mechanism underlying this association.

Introduction

Primary sclerosing cholangitis (PSC) is a cryptic chronic cholestatic liver disease, which involves genetic, immunologic, and environmental components [1]. The disease is characterized by inflammation, fibrosis, and strictures of the intra- and extra-hepatic bile ducts, leading to biliary cirrhosis and predisposes to hepatobiliary carcinogenesis [2]. The clinical course of PSC varies significantly, with some cases exhibiting a protracted and benign disease, while others rapidly progress to develop clinically relevant bile ducts strictures with recurrent events of acute cholangitis as well as liver cirrhosis. In the latter case, treatment options are limited and often resort to liver transplantation [3]. Hence, previous studies strived to identify clinical and laboratory features associated with disease course and proposed several serum biomarkers to predict disease progression and complications. Serum anti-proteinase 3 antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibody (PR3-ANCA) was found to be associated with more severe liver biochemistry in PSC [4], and recently has been shown to be correlated with model for end-stage liver disease score and associated with cholangiocarcinoma development and shorter transplant free survival [5]. Interestingly, increased levels of IgA antibodies against glycoprotein 2 (anti-GP2) as well as against F-actin were shown to be of prognostic significance in PSC, as they identify patients at risk of severe disease, poor survival and biliary cancer [5, 6]. Other autoantibodies commonly found in patients with PSC, such as anti-biliary epithelial cell, peri-nuclear antineutrophil cytoplasmic (pANCA), antinuclear, anti-cardiolipin, and anti-smooth muscle antibodies showed low specificity and their role in PSC is unclear [7].

PSC is strongly associated with inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), reaching up to 80% in prevalence [8]. The most investigated antibodies in IBD are ANCA and anti-Saccharomyces cerevisiae antibodies (ASCAs) [9, 10]. ANCA seropositivity is found in 50–70% of patients with ulcerative colitis (UC), and in only about 10% of patients with Crohn’s disease (CD). These antibodies, particularly the pANCA subtype, are a sensitive marker for PSC regardless of concomitant UC [11, 12]. Data regarding the association of ANCA to PSC severity and progression is conflicting. While some studies showed that ANCA status was related to a more extensive biliary disease and disease complications, such as biliary calculi or cholangiocarcinoma [13, 14], others found no correlation with disease activity or stage [12, 15].

ASCA, directed against baker’s yeast, were first described in patients with CD in the 1980s. Their prevalence in CD is 60–70%, while in UC they are less prevalent reaching 10–15% [16]. The specificity of ASCA for CD is 87%, although they are relatively not sensitive (57%) and found more commonly in patients with a proximal disease. In patients with CD, ASCA seropositivity is associated with an aggressive phenotype and a higher chance to require surgery, and is strongly associated with familial CD [17, 18]. Previous studies have shown dysbiosis of the fungal gut community in subjects with PSC [19]; however, data on the abundance of Saccharomyces and presence of ASCA in PSC-IBD is conflicting with limited information on its potential clinical relevance in this population [19–21]. Therefore, we aimed at portraying the association between ASCA seropositivity and PSC severity in PSC-IBD patients.

Methods

Medical records of 182 patients diagnosed with PSC and IBD between 2010 and 2023 at the hepatology clinic in the Tel Aviv Sourasky Medical Center, were anonymously and retrospectively reviewed using the MDClone platform. Of these records, 77 were excluded: 45 were not confirmed with diagnosis of IBD, 8 had no confirmed diagnoses of PSC, 5 had secondary sclerosing cholangitis, 17 did not attend a frontal doctor visit, 1 was poorly documented, and 1 did not return for follow-up after disease onset (shown in Fig. 1). The final cohort included 58 patients. Data regarding patients’ demographics, medical history, comorbidities, treatment, laboratory results, and imaging tests were collected from hospital records.

Flowchart of patients’ selection.

Eligible patients were above 18 years of age. PSC diagnosis was made by lab tests demonstrating cholestatic liver enzymes elevation and suggestive findings on magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography and/or liver biopsy. IBD diagnosis was determined by endoscopy and imaging studies and confirmed by histology. Patients who were followed-up for a period shorter than 1 year or missing ASCA serology were excluded from the study. If several follow-up visits were recorded, the latest one was used for analysis, so that the most advanced disease course would be assessed. Laboratory tests results and the degree of liver fibrosis evaluated by liver stiffness measurement (LSM) assessed by vibration-controlled transient elastography (VCTE, FibroScan®), were retrieved from the medical records. The Amsterdam-Oxford prognostic model (AOM) for PSC (composed of PSC subtype, age at PSC diagnosis, albumin, platelets, aspartate aminotransferase, alkaline phosphatase, and bilirubin) was used to predict transplant-free survival probabilities [22]. Data are available in online supplementary material (for all online suppl. material, see https://doi.org/10.1159/000552341).

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed and data plotted using GraphPad Prism version 9.5.1. Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation, while categorical variables are presented as percentage. Student’s t test for independent samples was performed to compare continuous variables between two groups, and Pearson’s chi-square test or Fischer’s exact test was performed to compare categorical variables. The threshold for significance was determined as two-sided p value of <0.05 for all analyses. Multivariable linear regression models adjusted for gender, IBD subtype, IBD duration, and PSC duration were used to assess associations between ASCA and the outcomes of AOM and LSM values.

Results

Patient Characteristics

A total of 58 patients with PSC-IBD and an available ASCA result were included in the present study, of whom 20 (34.5%) were seropositive. Fifty (86.2%) patients had sufficient demographic and laboratory parameters for calculation of the AOM score, and 36 (62.1%) patients underwent VCTE. Subjects in the ASCA-positive and ASCA-negative groups did not differ significantly in terms of age, gender, smoking status, IBD and PSC duration at evaluation, IBD phenotype, frequency of extra-intestinal manifestations, large duct and small duct PSC phenotype, ANCA seropositivity, or history of hepatic decompensation. As expected, a greater proportion of ASCA-positive patients had CD and a greater proportion of ASCA-negative patients had UC (p = 0.016) (Table 1). Similarly, the proportions of ASCA positivity were 14/28 (50%) for CD and 6/30 (20%) for UC (p = 0.016), consistent with previous local [23, 24] and international data [24–26]. Other than ASCA serostatus, patients with CD and UC were comparable in all the variables collected (online suppl. Table 1).

ASCA Positivity Is Associated with Increased AOM Score and Liver Stiffness

We next evaluated PSC severity based on ASCA serostatus. ASCA-positive patients exhibited significantly higher AOM scores than ASCA-negative patients (mean 2.204 vs. 1.730; p = 0.021; shown in Fig. 2a). These scores correspond to a 15-year transplant-free survival probability of 45% and 60%, respectively. Notably, AOM scores did not differ significantly between patients with CD and UC (p = 0.589). In a multivariable analysis, adjusting for potential confounders including demographics, IBD or PSC duration and IBD subtype, we found that ASCA seropositivity remained an independent determinant of AOM score (p = 0.026, Table 2).

Additionally, ASCA-positive patients showed increased liver stiffness on VCTE compared to ASCA-negative patients at the time of evaluation (10.18 kPa and 7.00 kPa, respectively, p = 0.018, shown in Fig. 2b). Thus, 50% of ASCA-positive patients as compared with 11.54% of ASCA-negative patients were diagnosed with advanced liver fibrosis (F3-4) (shown in Fig. 2c). Liver stiffness also did not differ significantly between patients with CD and UC (p = 0.945), suggesting that the observed differences in PSC severity are associated with ASCA status rather than the underlying IBD subtype. Discussion

In this study, we proposed utilizing ASCA, a commonly used serological test for IBD, to stratify patients with PSC-IBD by the risk for PSC-related outcomes of hepatic fibrosis and advanced disease. We showed that the presence of ASCA seropositivity in patients with PSC and IBD, was associated with increased AOM score and liver stiffness, irrespective of IBD subtype, IBD duration or PSC duration. To our knowledge, this is the first study that evaluated this association.

The natural history of PSC is highly variable and difficult to predict, ranging between an asymptomatic disease to an advanced and complicated disease culminating in end-stage liver disease. Therefore, noninvasive prognostic biomarkers are useful tools in the clinical armamentarium, as they may affect the management by influencing the intensity of surveillance, the decision to perform additional work-up or invasive investigations, and consideration of experimental therapeutic options.

Indeed, previous studies have identified serum biomarkers, which can serve as laboratory-based prognostic factors, and proposed composite scores to predict the risk for complications and liver transplantation for patients with PSC, such as the AOM [22], Mayo risk score [27], UK-PSC score [28], and the PREsTo model [29]. ASCA has been investigated previously in a small cohort of patients with autoimmune liver diseases [21]. In that study, ASCA prevalence in PSC patients was found to be 44% (11/25) and did not correlate with biochemical parameter examined [21].

Special consideration should be given to ASCA-positive UC patients, a phenotype rarely observed in clinical practice (0–16%) [25]. We found no significant differences in intestinal disease characteristics, such as age at onset, duration, or extent between ASCA-positive and ASCA-negative UC patients although our study was not specifically powered to detect differences within this small subgroup. Regardless of its association with bowel disease activity, ASCA status appears to be a valuable prognostic marker for the severity of the underlying biliary disease in patients with comorbid UC and PSC.

The fecal microbiota composition of PSC patients is unique, with dysbiosis present in patients with PSC with and without concomitant IBD and distinct from that of IBD alone [30]. Moreover, the intestinal mycobial consortium of PSC is also distinct from that of patients with IBD, with a decrease in the Saccharomycetales order, specifically in the S. cerevisiae species, as well as an increase in the abundance of Trichocladium griseum and Candida species [19, 30]. These species have shown to have an immunologic potential to induce a Th17 response, which is involved in PSC pathogenesis [31]. In IBD, the mycobiota was shown to be associated with gut barrier dysfunction, potentially via a regulatory effect on immune cells and epithelial membranes and by hyphal and biofilm formation [32]. Gut barrier dysfunction has also been suggested to play a role in PSC pathogenesis via microbial translocation to the bile, subsequently activating cholangiocytes and inducing inflammation and fibrosis within the liver [33]. We may speculate that the presence of ASCA in PSC may also reflect mycobial alterations associated with impaired intestinal permeability, leading to a more advanced liver disease.

Additionally, we found that male patients develop more advanced liver fibrosis as compared to female patients, consistent with a previous large multi-center retrospective trial [34]. However, in contrast to their observation that patients with PSC-UC exhibit worse prognosis compared to PSC-CD, we did not find differences between IBD subtype.

Our study has several limitations. First, this is a retrospective observational study and therefore may not capture potential confounders that may have influenced liver-related outcomes. Second, although we found a significant association with liver stiffness, part of the patients lacked VCTE measurements. Further studies are needed to evaluate the association of ASCA and liver fibrosis, including other measures of fibrosis, such as histology. Lastly, we did not include PSC patient without a diagnosis of IBD. However, ASCA is usually taken as part of IBD workup and therefore the probability of measuring ASCA in PSC without IBD is low and needed to be evaluated prospectively.

In summary, we showed that ASCA may serve as a biomarker for more advanced liver disease in subjects with concomitant IBD-PSC. This observation merits validation by prospective trials with larger sample size, to corroborate our findings, and raises the hypothesis that a member of the microbiota, in particular the mycobiota (S. cerevisiae), may play a role in PSC pathogenesis. Furthermore, our results suggest that ASCA should be incorporated into future prognostic tools to improve risk stratification at disease onset and affect the management of people who are living with PSC.
