Burden of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease attributable to non-optimal temperature, 1990–2044: six countries on the same isotherm
Authors: Zhengyang Shi, Jianjiang Shao, Chenxian Dong, Guanling Song, Yunhua Hu, Qiang Niu, Yizhong Yan
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundWith the frequent occurrence of extreme weather worldwide, non-optimal temperature increased the risk of death from respiratory diseases. The burden of non-optimal temperature on chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) was quantitatively assessed, and its influencing factors were discussed to provide a basis for the prevention and treatment of COPD.MethodsBased on GBD 2019, we characterized the age-standardized mortality rate (ASMR) and years of life lost rate (ASYR) of COPD attributable to non-optimal temperature in three groups of countries at different isotherms (China and USA, South Africa and Australia, Iraq and Portugal) between 1990 and 2019. We constructed the age-period-cohort model to analyze age, period and cohort effects on mortality and the Bayesian age-period-cohort model to predict ASMR in six countries. We analyzed the relationship of socio-demographic index (SDI) with ASMR and ASYR by restricted cubic spline and quantile regression using data from 21 GBD regions.ResultsASMR of COPD attributable to non-optimal temperature in 2019 was 11.03/100,000 (China), 5.62/100,000 (USA), 2/100,000 (Australia), 0.93/100,000 (Iraq), 3.74/100,000 (Portugal), 4.13/100,000 (South Africa). Low temperature had a greater impact on COPD. The mortality showed an increasing trend with age, the period effect only showed a decreasing trend in China, and cohort effect showed a decreasing trend. The higher COPD burden was concentrated in areas with SDI values of 0.39–0.78. Implied quantile regression of mortality to SDI fit was meaningful at P5 and P75, and ASYR was at P5, P25, P75, and P95. We predicted an upward trend in COPD ASMR over the next 25 years only in the USA.ConclusionsIn COPD burden caused by non-optimal temperature, low temperature played a more important role, and it was affected by sex, age, period, cohort and SDI. Over the next 25 years, ASMR in COPD was predicted to decline in all countries except the USA.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-024-20622-6.

Introduction

Globally, extreme weather events had become more frequent, intense, and prolonged due to climate change [1]. Non-optimal temperature caused an estimated 169 million deaths worldwide in 2019 [2]. Exposure to either high or low temperature led to increased risks of morbidity and mortality for a range of adverse health outcomes [2–4]. A study analyzing the effect of ambient temperatures on outpatient visits found that high and low temperatures also increased outpatient visits [5]. In recent years, non-optimal temperature has been a health risk factor worthy of attention.

Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) was recognized for its heterogeneity [6]. This condition was identified by ongoing respiratory symptoms and airflow obstruction that was both progressive and irreversible, primarily resulting from abnormalities in the airways or alveoli [7]. COPD caused a severe disease burden globally, with 212.3 million cases and 3.3 million deaths globally in 2019, in addition to 74.4 million disability-adjusted life years (DALYs), one of the most important causes of DALYs among non-communicable diseases around the world [8]. The most significant increase in COPD prevalence was in North Africa and the Middle East, with a 30.6% increase between 1990 and 2019 [9]. COPD was often linked to other conditions such as heart disease, depression, and others that may lead to physical inactivity and reduced patient survival and quality of life [10]. As a chronic respiratory disease, COPD had a significant disease burden in the aging population. It not only affected the respiratory function of the elderly, but also increased the financial pressure on the family due to the high cost of treatment as the disease gradually worsened [11]. In addition, COPD patients often suffered from psychological problems, and this psychological burden further exacerbated the overall burden of disease in older adults, which made it even more difficult for them to deal with their chronic illnesses [12]. COPD had become a major public healthcare challenge in the 21st century owing to its high prevalence and associated morbidity and mortality [13].

The impact of smoking and particulate matter pollution on COPD had been widely investigated. Moreover, the increasing occurrence of extreme temperature events had attracted attention to the potential effects of non-optimal temperature in this condition [14]. COPD patients showed a high sensitivity to temperature variability, with higher temperature worsening the respiratory system and leading to dyspnea, lower temperature may aggravate bronchitis and increase the burden of COPD [15]. Regions such as sub-Saharan Africa, North Africa and the Middle East, and Southern Europe were significantly affected by the heat effect, and regions such as North America, Eastern and Central Europe and East Asia were significantly affected by the cold effect [2]. High or low temperatures may also exacerbate COPD patients in these regions. In a study of 125 million older adults in 213 U.S. cities, the risk of hospitalization for COPD increased by 4.3% for every 5.3℃ increase in daily mean temperature [16]. A study from another country based on health insurance registry data found that for every 1℃ decrease in average temperature, the acute exacerbation rate of COPD increased by 0.8% [17]. Thus, non-optimal temperature can influence the progression and outcomes of COPD. Additionally, the level of development in various countries or regions may also play a role in this relationship.

Studying the regional variations in COPD burden related to non-optimal temperature can enhance our understanding of the underlying factors contributing to these differences. This analysis may also inform the development of targeted interventions at national or regional levels. In the first section of this study, three groups of countries were selected based on different isotherms, which aimed to assess the burden of COPD related to non-optimal temperature and to investigate the effects of age, period, and cohort on mortality rate. The second section analyzed the correlation between the socio-demographic index (SDI) and both the age-standardized mortality rate (ASMR) and the age-standardized years of life lost rate (ASYR) in 21 regions. Lastly, the third section predicted the ASMR due to COPD caused by non-optimal temperature in six countries over the next 25 years after 2019. This study’s all-around analysis of the relationship between non-optimal temperature and COPD may provide scientific and accurate data to support the reduction of the burden of COPD.

Methods

Data on the burden of COPD caused by non-optimal temperature came from the 2019 Global Burden of Disease (GBD) study. The database provided a thorough estimation of 369 diseases and injuries, along with 87 risk factors, across 204 countries or territories. The data was systematically organized by age, sex, country or territory, and year of occurrence [18, 19].

Case definition

According to GBD, the definition of COPD followed the J41-J44.9 codes in ICD-10 [19]. Based on standardized methods and definitions, GBD generated mortality estimates according to sex, age, year, and region. The years of life lost (YLLs) referred to life lost resulting from premature death, calculated as a combination of mortality and life expectancy [19]. The GBD defined non-optimal temperature as being either above or below the Theoretical Minimum Risk Exposure Level (TMREL) for a given day [18]. Exposures that exceeded the TMREL were designated as high temperature, while those that fell below the TMREL were classified as low temperature [18].

Data sources

COPD mortality estimates were obtained from the GBD 2019 cause of death database, which covered death certificate records, cause-of-death inferences, epidemiologic surveys, medical records, and statistical information from official and regulatory agencies [19]. In order to quantify mortality rates for specific causes of death in different geographic areas, age and sex groups, the disease model-Bayesian meta-regression (DisMod-MR) 2.1 model was used for data fitting and analysis [19]. The calculation of YLLs was based on globally standardized life expectancy and the use of modeling life Table [19].

Data on non-optimal temperature were primarily obtained from the ERA5 reanalysis data [2]. The meta-regression—Bayesian, regularised, trimmed (MR-BRT) model was used to integrate COPD mortality and temperature exposure data, construct relative risk-response relationship curves for temperature exposure, and calculate population-attributable fractions (PAFs), which reflected the relative contribution of non-optimal temperature to the COPD burden [18]. To measure the non-optimal temperature related disease burden, the PAFs were multiplied by the total COPD burden [18].

SDI

The SDI was a composite measure that included the average educational achievement of individuals, the total fertility rate of women under 25 years, and the per capita income of a country, adjusted for one prior period [20]. It reflects society’s development and the health outcomes of the population. Theoretically, the larger the SDI value, the higher the development level associated with the social development and health outcomes of the population in the area.

Statistical analysis

Age-period-cohort model and bayesian age-period-cohort model

In recent years, the age-period-cohort (APC) model has been widely used in epidemiology, sociology and other areas [21, 22]. We used the model to analyze trends in mortality with age, period and cohort effects. However, the classic APC model cannot provide valid and unique results due to the covariance of independent variables. The intrinsic estimator can solve this problem and provide a relatively valid estimation result [22]. In this study, the data was classified into 13 age groups (25–29, 30–34, 35–39, 40–44, 45–49, 50–54, 55–59, 60–64, 65–69, 70–74, 75–79, 80–84, and 85–89 years), five-period groups (1994, 1999, 2004, 2009, 2014, 2019) and 18 cohort groups (1905–1909, 1910–1914, 1915–1919, 1920–1924, 1925–1929, 1930–1934, 1935–1939, 1940–1944, 1945–1949, 1950–1954, 1955–1959, 1960–1964, 1965–1969, 1970–1974, 1975–1979, 1980–1984, 1985–1989, 1990–1994).

Bayesian age-period-cohort (BAPC) model was used to predict the ASMR for six countries in 2020–2044. In the APC model, Bayesian inference employs a second-order random walk to smooth the prior distributions for age, period, and cohort effects, as well as to estimate posterior mortality rates. It uses the Integrated Nested Laplace Approximation (INLA) to estimate the marginal posterior distribution, avoiding some of the problems caused by traditional Markov chain Monte Carlo sampling techniques using Bayesian methods [23].

Quantile regression

This study used quantile regression to explore the relationship between ASMR and ASYR and SDI at different percentiles. Quantile regression allows for a more comprehensive description of the trend of the response variable with the explanatory variables at different quartiles rather than just analyzing the response variable’s conditional expectation (mean) [24]. It is rooted in the most minor absolute deviation estimation and can be used to explain the relationship between a set of explanatory variables and an outcome. The minimum absolute deviation of this regression is estimated as follows:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:L\left(\theta\:\right)=\underset{\xi\:\subset\:R}{\text{min}}\left\{\sum\:_{i:{Y}_{i}\ge\:\xi\:}\tau\:|{Y}_{i}-\xi\:|+\sum\:_{i:{Y}_{i}\le\:\xi\:}(1-\tau\:)|{Y}_{i}-\xi\:|\right\}$$\end{document}

Compared with ordinary linear regression, quantile regression doesn’t require the data to follow a normal distribution, and it is not sensitive to outliers in the data. Therefore, it has better robustness than ordinary linear regression [25].

Restricted cubic splines

A restricted cubic spline (RCS) with three knots was employed to assess the relationship between SDI and both ASMR and ASYR. The spline function is used when the independent variable does not satisfy a linear relationship with the dependent variable. The spline function is a segmented function that is continuous and derivable in each interval. RCS is essentially a spline function with a restriction added, which requires the function to be linear in the two intervals at either end of the independent variable’s range and continuously derivable over the intervals [26].

All statistical analyses were performed using R software 4.3.1 and Stata 17.0. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Burden of COPD attributable to non-optimal temperature from 1990 to 2019

In six countries, the number of COPD deaths and years of life lost (YLLs) due to non-optimal temperature was higher in 2019 than in 1990 except China, which had a higher burden than the other countries.

The ASMR due to non-optimal temperature for COPD in 1990 were 37.84/100,000 (China), 4.66/100,000 (USA), 2.99/100,000 (Australia), 0.96/100,000 (Iraq), 5.18/100,000 (Portugal) and 5.56/100,000 (South Africa), and that in 2019 were 11.03/100,000 (China), 5.62/100,000 (USA), 2.00/100,000 (Australia), 0.93/100,000 (Iraq), 3.74/100,000 (Portugal), 4.13/100,000 (South Africa). The ASYR in 1990 were: 574.4/100,000 (China), 84.95/100,000 (USA), 49.62/100,000 (Australia), 17.19/100,000 (Iraq), 80.67/100,000 (Portugal) and 97.76/ 100,000 (South Africa), and that in 2019 were 148.2/100,000 (China), 93.56/100,000 (USA), 29.92/100,000 (Australia), 15.7/100,000 (Iraq), 49.1/100,000 (Portugal) and 69.64/100,000 (South Africa). From 1990 to 2019, ASMR and ASYR increased in the USA but decreased in other countries. (Table 1)

YLLs, years of life lost

Deaths attributable to non-optimal temperature in COPD were mainly from low temperature (Fig. 1). Over the two decades, China had the highest ASMR. China and South Africa had declined over time, while the United States had risen slowly; Iraq and Australia had changed slightly (Fig. 1). Changes in ASYR were similar to ASMR. (Fig. 2). In an exploration of the impact of ASMR and ASYR due to non-optimal temperature on COPD in different gender, males had higher ASMR and ASYR than females in all six countries (Fig. 3).

Age-period-cohort effects on COPD mortality attributable to non-optimal temperature

There was an increasing trend in the age effect in six countries, with the lowest risk of death in the 25–29 age group: -3.54 (Australia), -3.00 (China), -2.25 (Iraq), -2.26 (Portugal), -2.39 (South Africa), and − 3.89 (USA). The highest risk of death at ages 85–89 were 2.85 (Australia), 3.32 (China), 2.50 (Iraq), 3.36 (Portugal), 2.57 (South Africa), and 2.75 (USA).

China’s period effect showed a decreasing trend from 0.28 to -0.28 from 1994 to 2019; the rest of the countries showed an increasing trend, -0.25-0.47 (Australia), -0.22-0.18 (Iraq), -0.01-0.04 (Portugal), -0.37-0.03 (South Africa), and − 0.48 − 0.45 (USA).

The cohort effect was generally decreasing, with the highest risk of death in 1905–1909: 1.60 (Australia), 1.27 (China), 1.09 (Iraq), 0.96 (Portugal), 1.30 (South Africa), 1.50 (USA); and the lowest in 1990–1994: -1.82 (Australia), -1.91 (China), -1.49 (Iraq), -1.80 (Portugal), -1.76 (South Africa), -1.68 (USA) (Fig. 4).

Prediction of ASMR of COPD attributable to non-optimal temperature

Australia’s ASMR declined slowly, from 3.022 (95% UI: 3.016–3.027) in 1990 to 2.400 (95% UI: 2.291–2.509) in 2044 (Fig. 5A). China had the most significant decline in ASMR and will remain at a low level after 2040, with ASMR of 37.909 (95% UI: 37.903–37.916) and 7.812 (95% UI: 6.856–8.769) in 1990 and 2044, respectively (Fig. 5B). The results for Iraq decreased from 0.896 (95% UI: 0.892-0.900) in 1990 to 0.703 (95% UI: 0.671–0.734) in 2044 (Fig. 5C). The ASMR for Portugal was 5.260 (95% UI: 5.250–5.270) in 1990, 3.170 (95% UI: 2.984–3.357) in 2044, respectively, (Fig. 5D). The ASMR in South Africa first increased from 5.512 (95% UI: 5.502–5.522) in 1990 to 7.780 (95% UI: 7.771–7.789) in 2000, and then declined annually to 0.498 (95% UI: 0.382–0.613) in 2044 (Fig. 5E). The ASMR in the USA was rising every year and the ASMR will increase from 5.620 (95% UI: 5.618–5.622) in 2019 to 10.562 (95% UI: 7.885–13.238) in 2044 (Fig. 5F).

Relationship between ASMR and ASYR attributable to non-optimal temperature in COPD and SDI

We described the relationship between ASMR and ASYR and SDI in COPD patients in 21 GBD regions worldwide from 1990 to 2019. The restricted cubic spline analysis results indicated that ASMR and ASYR were higher in regions with low-middle (0.39–0.47), middle (0.47–0.58), and high-middle (0.58–0.78) SDI levels. The quantile regression results ranged from P95 to P5, including P75, P50, and P25, with significant findings noted for P5 and P75, which showed an increase in ASMR with increasing SDI (Fig. 6A). The significance in the quantile regression of ASYR fitted to SDI were P5, P25, P75, and P95. The P5 and P25 curves indicated that the ASYR increased with increasing SDI, and the opposite was observed for P75 and P95. The remaining quantile regression results were not statistically significant (Fig. 6B).

Discussion

A higher effect of low temperature was associated with COPD burden, and the burden was also influenced by sex, age, period, cohort and SDI. The higher ASMR and ASYR for COPD were due to non-optimal temperature in China. Low temperature was more harmful than high temperature. Males had a higher burden than females. Mortality from COPD attributable to non-optimal temperature increased with age. The period effect showed a decreasing trend in China, while other countries showed minimal change or a slight increase. Meanwhile, the cohort effect showed an overall decreasing trend. A region or country with an SDI of 0.39–0.78 had higher ASMR and ASYR. The USA showed an upward trend in COPD ASMR attributable to non-optimal temperature over the next 25 years, and the rest of the countries showed a downward trend.

The burden of COPD due to non-optimal temperature in China was higher than in other countries. This was consistent with a previous study [27]. This may be related to China’s large population base, the serious aging of the population, and uneven levels of medical resources due to urban-rural differences [28]. In addition, the growing urbanization may increase the burden of COPD [29]. For example, the urban heat island effect caused by urbanization would increase the burden of COPD through increased exposure to extreme temperatures [30]. Our study found a higher mortality burden from COPD due to low temperature. Cold may induce a constriction of bronchial airways, inhibit mucociliary defenses and other immune responses, with the result in localized inflammation and an increased risk of respiratory tract infections [31]. A study on global mortality noted that more excess deaths were associated with low temperature (8.52%) and fewer with high temperature (0.91%) [1]. Low temperature caused more lasting pathological changes in the organization than high temperature did [32]. Therefore, we should establish health-related temperature detection and early warning systems to alleviate the health impacts of extreme cold events.

Males had a higher burden of COPD due to non-optimal temperature than females. An Indian study found that the mortality burden of COPD was greater in males than in females, regardless of high or low temperatures. Physiological and metabolic factors may account for this difference [33]. On the COPD phenotype, males were more likely to have emphysema, and females were more likely to have chronic bronchitis, with the former showing a faster rate of lung function decline and higher mortality [34]. The APC results indicated that increasing age was a risk factor for non-optimal temperature leading to COPD. Regardless of temperature, a Chinese national study found that elderly people had a greater risk of COPD than young people [35]. The elderly were more sensitive to changes in temperature, and the prevalence of lung disease in the elderly would continue to increase as the temperature variation continued to increase [36]. The period effect of China showed a downward trend, while other countries showed little change or a slightly upward trend, which may be due to the limitation of data and the short period of selection [37]. The lower risk of death in patients with a later birth cohort may be related to improved accuracy of COPD detection, which had changed from confirming the diagnosis of COPD with a forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV1)/forced vital capacity (FVC) ratio of less than 0.7 after bronchodilator was used to further confirm the diagnosis of COPD by repeating measurements of patients with FEV1/FVC ratios in the range of 0.6–0.8; It was also related to the rational use of medications (bronchodilators, antibiotics, glucocorticoids, etc.) and rational preventive management, such as regular vaccination, smoking cessation, and pulmonary rehabilitation [7, 38].

Due to non-optimal temperature, regions with low-middle, middle, and high-middle SDI levels exhibited higher ASMR and ASYR for COPD. The SDI level was directly associated with a country’s overall population health, which can vary across nations at different stages of development due to differences in the quality of healthcare and educational attainment [39]. A study of life loss per death in 364 regions of China found that the YLLs rates were higher in undeveloped regions than in developed regions due to non-optimal temperature [40]. In addition, educational level affected the mortality burden associated with temperature, while having low years of education exacerbated the burden of high and low temperatures on cardiopulmonary disease [41]. Resident’s education level influenced their cognition, prevention and treatment of the disease, and COPD patients could prevent exacerbation of the disease by improving their quality of life and health behaviors through chronic disease management [42]. Therefore, countries with low SDI should promote economic and social development, strengthen medical facilities, improve medical levels, and raise the education level of the population to improve the health condition of the people and thus reduce the mortality rate [20].

The number of COPD deaths and YLLs due to non-optimal temperature showed an increasing trend in all countries except China from 1990 to 2019. Predictions showed a decline in ASMR due to non-optimal temperature for COPD in all countries except the USA in the next 25 years. Nonetheless, the number of incidences and deaths of COPD will continue to increase in the future with global climate change, changes in high-risk factors (smoking, ambient particulate pollution, ozone pollution), and an aging population in the coming years [43]. Therefore, COPD should be given more attention in all countries. Measures should be taken to reduce the impact of risk factors such as non-optimal temperatures on COPD. Furthermore, enhancing diagnostic and treatment capabilities for COPD was essential to reduce the associated disease burden.

There were some limitations in this study. First, the quantity, quality and method of correcting data for GBD modelling impacted the original COPD disease data. Secondly, the study didn’t include people under the age of 25 or those over the age of 95 due to the limited number of COPD-related deaths in the younger cohort. Moreover, people over the age of 95 didn’t qualify for use in APC modelling. Finally, non-optimal temperature was susceptible to both temporal and spatial influences, and our analytical method wasn’t considered. The effects of non-optimal temperature should be explored in the future by considering both temporal and spatial factors.

Conclusion

The burden of COPD attributable to non-optimal temperature was associated with a higher impact of low temperature and was influenced by sex, age, period, cohort and SDI. The ASMR associated with non-optimal temperature causing COPD over the next 25 years was on the upward trend in the USA and a downward trend in other countries. Targeted policies should be developed in each country to strengthen the control and management of risk factors attributed to COPD.
