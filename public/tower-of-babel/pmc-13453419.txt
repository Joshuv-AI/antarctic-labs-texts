Association of Serum Chitinase-3-Like Protein 1 with Liver Fibrosis Severity in Autoimmune Liver Diseases
Authors: Yazhen Zhang, Jianfeng Bao, Wenyan Yu, Zhongbao Zuo, Kenv Pan, Aifang Xu, Miaochan Wang, Lei Xia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAutoimmune liver diseases (AILDs) require precise fibrosis staging, but current non-invasive tools are limited. This study aimed to evaluate the diagnostic performance of serum chitinase-3-like protein 1 (CHI3L1) for liver fibrosis in AILDs.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study consecutively enrolled 226 AILDs patients (including those with autoimmune hepatitis, primary biliary cholangitis, and overlap syndrome) and 40 healthy controls. Cohort 1 (n=112) underwent liver biopsy (Scheuer staging). Cohort 2 (n=114) underwent liver stiffness measurement (LSM) by transient elastography, with no overlap between cohorts. Diagnostic performance was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis and compared with aspartate aminotransferase-to-platelet ratio index (APRI) and the fibrosis-4 index (FIB-4). Multivariable binary logistic regression adjusted for histological inflammation identified independent predictors of advanced fibrosis.ResultsSerum CHI3L1 levels increased significantly with advancing histological (S0-1: 50.6 ng/mL; S2: 80.8 ng/mL; S3-4: 156.3 ng/mL; P < 0.001) and LSM-based fibrosis stages (F0-1: 57.8 ng/mL; F2: 70.9 ng/mL; F3-4: 164.5 ng/mL; P < 0.001). CHI3L1 was significantly correlated with APRI (r = 0.451), FIB-4 (r = 0.511), and LSM (r = 0.671). For diagnosing significant fibrosis (S≥2) and advanced fibrosis (S≥3), CHI3L1 showed areas under the ROC curve (AUC) of 0.833 and 0.851, respectively, performing comparably to FIB-4 and better than APRI. In the LSM cohort, CHI3L1 maintained high diagnostic accuracy (AUC: 0.828 for F≥2, 0.835 for F≥3). Multivariate analysis confirmed serum CHI3L1 is independently associated with advanced liver fibrosis.ConclusionCHI3L1 level is independently associated with advanced liver fibrosis and shows good diagnostic performance for both significant and advanced liver fibrosis. It is a valuable non-invasive biomarker to supplement existing fibrosis assessment strategies, pending validation across individual AILDs subtypes.Keywords: chitinase-3-like protein 1, autoimmune liver diseases, liver fibrosis, biomarker

Introduction

Autoimmune liver diseases (AILDs) are a group of chronic inflammatory liver disorders mediated by immune dysregulation.1 This spectrum classically includes autoimmune hepatitis (AIH), primary biliary cholangitis (PBC), primary sclerosing cholangitis (PSC) and their pairwise overlap syndromes,1 with AIH-PBC overlap being the most frequently observed.2 Notably, these subtypes differ substantially in inflammatory activity, cholestatic burden, and fibrosis progression rates.1,3 This inherent heterogeneity complicates disease management and contributes to a growing clinical burden. Although AILDs are considered rare diseases, their population-based incidence and prevalence are rising.4 AILDs are significant causes of liver fibrosis, cirrhosis, and ultimately hepatic failure.5 Accurate assessment of liver fibrosis forms the basis for managing AILDs as it fulfills both diagnostic and prognostic objectives.6,7 Therefore, accurate assessment of liver fibrosis stage is of paramount clinical importance for disease management, therapeutic decision-making, and prognosis evaluation.

Currently, the histopathological evaluation following percutaneous liver biopsy remains the gold standard for staging liver fibrosis. According to the 2024 British Society of Gastroenterology guideline for AIH, liver biopsy confirms diagnosis, excludes mimics, and assesses necro-inflammatory activity alongside fibrosis stage.8 In suspected AIH-PBC overlap, moderate-to-severe interface hepatitis on biopsy remains a diagnostic prerequisite.9 For PBC, the European Association for the Study of the Liver (EASL) and the American Association for the Study of Liver Diseases (AASLD) Delphi consensus notes that while biopsy provides essential population-level data, its individual use is limited by sampling variability and inter-observer heterogeneity.10 These limitations are particularly pertinent in the context of AILDs heterogeneity, underscoring the pressing need for reliable non-invasive biomarkers capable of accurately staging fibrosis across this diverse spectrum. However, beyond its invasive nature and limitations such as sampling variability and inter-observer heterogeneity,11,12 liver biopsy is not suitable for dynamic monitoring. These drawbacks have prompted extensive exploration of noninvasive diagnostic approaches. Existing noninvasive assessment tools primarily include imaging-based transient elastography (TE) and serum-based models. Liver stiffness measurement (LSM) by TE demonstrates high diagnostic accuracy for staging liver fibrosis in patients with AILDs.13 However, its accuracy is frequently confounded by factors such as obesity, active inflammation, ascites, advanced age, and operator inexperience,14–16 which increase the risk of technical failure or unreliable readings. These limitations reduce its standalone utility for fibrosis staging in clinical practice. Among serum-based models, the aspartate aminotransferase-to-platelet ratio index (APRI) and the fibrosis-4 index (FIB-4) are widely used in clinical practice due to their simplicity and low cost.17,18 However, these indices were primarily developed for viral hepatitis and generally exhibit only modest diagnostic accuracy in AILDs.19–21 Consequently, there is a pressing clinical need to identify and validate superior and more specific noninvasive biomarkers for accurately assessing the degree of liver fibrosis in patients with AILDs.

In the search for novel biomarkers to address this clinical need, chitinase-3-like protein 1 (CHI3L1, also known as YKL-40) has garnered increasing attention. CHI3L1 is a glycoprotein secreted by various cell types, including activated macrophages, hepatic stellate cells, and cholangiocytes.22 It plays a pivotal role in inflammatory responses, tissue repair, and extracellular matrix remodeling.22,23 Substantial evidence has confirmed that in various chronic liver diseases, such as viral hepatitis and metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), serum CHI3L1 levels correlate closely with the severity of liver fibrosis and demonstrate promise as a non-invasive fibrotic biomarker.24–27 However, most existing AILDs-focused studies lacked histologically confirmed fibrosis staging as the reference standard.28

To address these gaps, this retrospective cohort study evaluates the association between serum CHI3L1 and liver fibrosis severity in AILDs, using both a biopsy-confirmed cohort and an independent LSM cohort. Our findings aim to provide histologically validated, incremental evidence to support the non-invasive assessment of liver fibrosis in AILDs.

Material and Methods

Study Population

This retrospective cohort study was conducted at Hangzhou Xixi Hospital. Serum CHI3L1 testing was performed as part of the routine clinical evaluation for fibrosis assessment in hospitalized AILDs patients. To minimize selection bias, we consecutively enrolled all eligible patients who underwent this testing. Two independent groups of patients diagnosed with AILDs who underwent serum CHI3L1 testing were consecutively enrolled between October 2018 and November 2025. Cohort 1 (the histological cohort) comprised AILDs patients who underwent liver biopsy for clinical evaluation. Cohort 2 (the LSM cohort) consisted of an independent set of AILDs patients who underwent TE for LSM assessment; none of the patients in Cohort 2 underwent liver biopsy, ensuring no overlap with Cohort 1. All patients met the corresponding international diagnostic criteria (eg, for AIH, PBC, or AIH-PBC overlap syndrome).8,9,29 Exclusion criteria for all patients were: (1) age < 18 years or pregnancy/lactation; (2) coexistence of other confirmed etiologies of liver disease (eg, viral, alcoholic, or drug-induced liver injury, etc); (3) concomitant malignancies or decompensated cardiac, cerebral, or renal diseases; and (4) incomplete clinical or laboratory data. Additionally, a control group of healthy individuals was recruited from the hospital’s health examination center during the same period. All healthy controls underwent serum CHI3L1 testing and had no abnormalities on routine physical examination, laboratory tests (including liver function), or imaging studies, with no history of liver or other systemic diseases.

Data Collection

Clinical data and laboratory test results for all enrolled participants were systematically extracted from the hospital’s electronic medical record system. Laboratory parameters, including complete blood count, liver function tests, coagulation profile, and immunoglobulin levels, were measured using fully automated analyzers following standardized operating procedures. For patients in the histological cohort (Cohort 1), fasting peripheral venous blood samples were collected in the morning within one week prior to the liver biopsy procedure, during a period when they were not receiving any specific treatment for liver fibrosis or its complications. These therapies encompassed both Western agents with anti-fibrotic activity (eg, glycyrrhizin preparations, silibinin) and designated Chinese patent medicines for liver fibrosis (eg, Fuzheng Huayucapsule, Compound Biejia Ruangantablet). For patients in the LSM cohort (Cohort 2), fasting blood samples were collected in the morning prior to the LSM examination.

Serum CHI3L1 Level Determination

Serum CHI3L1 levels were quantified using a commercially available enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kit (Hangzhou Proprium Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Hangzhou, China). All CHI3L1 testing was performed as part of routine clinical care for AILDs patients at our hospital between October 2018 and November 2025. Certified technicians in the Department of Clinical Laboratory conducted all assays. Peripheral venous blood samples were collected during routine clinical visits after an overnight fast of at least 8 hours. For samples that could not be analyzed on the day of collection, serum was immediately separated and aliquoted into single-use volumes. These aliquots were stored at −80°C until analysis to preserve stability. All samples were tested within 7 days of collection, and repeated freeze-thaw cycles were strictly avoided. Prior to analysis, samples were diluted 500-fold with the specific diluent provided in the kit, strictly adhering to the manufacturer’s protocol. Routine clinical quality control was implemented throughout the study period, with an inter-assay coefficient of variation <8%. This study retrospectively extracted all CHI3L1 results from the hospital’s electronic medical record system. Consecutive patients who underwent routine CHI3L1 testing during the enrollment period were included to avoid selection bias.

Liver Biopsy

Percutaneous liver biopsy was performed under bedside ultrasound guidance. The obtained liver tissue specimens were fixed in formalin, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned. All sections were stained with hematoxylin-eosin and Masson’s trichrome stain. Specimens containing at least six complete portal tracts were considered adequate for evaluation. Liver fibrosis and histological inflammation grade were staged independently by two senior pathologists, who were blinded to all clinical data, using the Scheuer scoring system: S0 (no fibrosis), S1 (portal fibrosis without septa), S2 (portal fibrosis with few septa), S3 (numerous septa without cirrhosis), and S4 (cirrhosis). For histological inflammation grading, the system defined: G0 (no inflammatory activity), G1 (portal inflammation), G2 (periportal inflammation/interface hepatitis), G3 (bridging necrosis), and G4 (multilobular necrosis). For subsequent analysis, significant fibrosis and advanced fibrosis were defined as a fibrosis stage of S ≥ 2 and S ≥ 3, respectively.

Transient Elastography Measurement

LSM was performed using Liver transient elasticity scanner (Fibrotouch, Wuxi Heiskell Co., Ltd., Wuxi). With the patient lying in the supine position and the right arm fully abducted, an experienced operator placed the probe over the right lobe of the liver through an intercostal space at the level of the right mid‑axillary line (between the 7th and 9th intercostal spaces). At least ten valid measurements were successfully obtained for each patient. The median value was automatically calculated by the device and recorded as the final LSM result for analysis, provided that the interquartile range to median ratio (IQR/M) was less than 30%, a criterion used to ensure measurement reliability. For the LSM cohort, significant fibrosis (≥F2) and advanced fibrosis (≥F3) were defined by LSM cut‑off values of 7.3 kPa and 9.7 kPa, respectively.30,31

Formulas

Two widely used noninvasive serum indices for liver fibrosis, the APRI and the FIB‑4, were calculated based on routine laboratory test results. The formulas were as follows: APRI = (aspartate aminotransferase (AST) (U/L)/upper limit of normal (ULN) of AST)/platelet (PLT) (109/L) × 100;32 FIB-4 = (age (years) × AST (U/L))/(PLT (109/L) × (alanine aminotransferase (ALT) (U/L))1/2).33

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS (version 26.0) and GraphPad Prism (version 10.0) software. Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation for normally distributed data or as median (interquartile range) for non-normally distributed data. Categorical variables were presented as numbers (percentages). Group comparisons for continuous variables were conducted using one-way ANOVA or the Kruskal–Wallis H-test, followed by appropriate post-hoc tests, whereas the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test was applied for categorical variables. The associations between serum CHI3L1 levels and other continuous clinical parameters (eg, liver biochemical markers and non-invasive fibrosis scores) were evaluated using Spearman’s rank correlation analysis. The diagnostic performance of the biomarkers for liver fibrosis was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis, and the optimal cutoff value was determined by maximizing the Youden index. The independent association between serum CHI3L1 and advanced fibrosis was evaluated using a multivariable binary logistic regression model adjusted for potential confounders. Variables with a univariable P value <0.10 were selected and entered into the final model. Multicollinearity was assessed using the variance inflation factor (VIF), with a VIF > 5 indicating significant collinearity. All tests were two-tailed, and a P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline Characteristics of the Study Population

A total of 226 patients with AILDs and 40 healthy controls were finally enrolled in this study. The patients were categorized into two independent cohorts based on the assessment method.

Histological Cohort (Cohort 1): This cohort consisted of 112 AILDs patients, comprising 58 cases of AIH, 29 cases of PBC, and 25 cases of AIH-PBC overlap syndrome. Their baseline characteristics are presented in Table 1. The median age of the patients was 57.0 years, with a female predominance (102/112, 91.1%). No significant sex difference in CHI3L1 levels was observed within this cohort (96.5 ng/mL in males vs 98.5 ng/mL in females, P = 0.582). As the histological fibrosis stage progressed from S0-1 to S3-4, patient age, prothrombin time (PT), AST, total bilirubin (TBIL), direct bilirubin (DBIL), immunoglobulin G (IgG), and immunoglobulin A (IgA) levels increased significantly (all P < 0.05). In contrast, PLT and ALB levels decreased significantly (all P < 0.001). Serum CHI3L1 levels were significantly higher in patients with more advanced fibrosis (S0-1: 50.6 ng/mL; S2: 80.8 ng/mL; S3-4: 156.3 ng/mL; P < 0.001). The distribution of histological inflammation grades differed significantly across fibrosis stages (P < 0.001). G3-4 inflammation was present in 78.3% of patients with advanced fibrosis but absent in those with S0-1 fibrosis. The non-invasive scores APRI and FIB-4 also showed an increasing trend with worsening fibrosis (both P < 0.001). No significant differences were observed among the groups regarding sex ratio, hemoglobin (HGB), red cell distribution width (RDW), ALT, alkaline phosphatase (ALP), gamma-glutamyl transferase (GGT), total protein (TP), or immunoglobulin M (IgM).

LSM Cohort (Cohort 2): This cohort consisted of 114 AILDs patients, comprising 62 cases of AIH, 42 cases of PBC, and 10 cases of AIH-PBC overlap syndrome. Their baseline characteristics are shown in Table S1. Female patients in this cohort exhibited significantly higher CHI3L1 levels than males (110.7 ng/mL vs 38.5 ng/mL, P = 0.033). The mean age was 60.0 years, with a similarly high proportion of females (106/114, 93.0%). When stratified by LSM-defined fibrosis stages (F0-1, F2, F3-4), CHI3L1 levels also increased significantly with advancing fibrosis severity (F0-1: 57.8 ng/mL; F3-4: 164.5 ng/mL; P < 0.001). Trends for age, PLT, and ALB were consistent with those observed in the histological cohort.

Although the LSM cohort was significantly older than the histological cohort (P = 0.004), the distributions of sex ratios were comparable between the two groups (P = 0.596). Importantly, CHI3L1 levels did not differ significantly between the histological and LSM cohorts (P = 0.752). Among the entire AILDs cohort, serum CHI3L1 levels were 106.6 (65.4–194.5) ng/mL in 120 patients with AIH, 83.4 (50.7–149.8) ng/mL in 71 patients with PBC, and 113.5 (61.7–204.2) ng/mL in 35 patients with AIH-PBC overlap syndromes. No significant difference was observed across these subtypes (P = 0.126).

The healthy control group comprised 40 individuals (22 males and 18 females) with a mean age of 44.1 years. In this cohort, serum CHI3L1 levels were similar between males and females (39.4 ng/mL vs 30.9 ng/mL, P = 0.201).

Differences of Serum CHI3L1 Levels in AILDs Patients with Different Stages of Liver Fibrosis

As shown in Figure 1, serum CHI3L1 levels were lowest in healthy controls and were significantly elevated in patients with AILDs. In the histological cohort (Figure 1A), CHI3L1 levels increased in a stepwise manner with the severity of fibrosis stage. Specifically, the median CHI3L1 level in patients with no or mild fibrosis (stage S0-1) was 50.6 ng/mL, which was higher than that in healthy controls (34.5 ng/mL), but the difference was not statistically significant. Patients with advanced fibrosis (stage S3-4) had the highest median CHI3L1 level, which was significantly greater than that in patients with stage S2 fibrosis (P < 0.01), stage S0-1 fibrosis (P < 0.0001), and healthy controls (P < 0.0001). The median CHI3L1 level in patients with stage S2 fibrosis was also significantly higher than that in healthy controls (P < 0.0001). Although CHI3L1 levels were increased in stage S2 patients compared to stage S0-1 patients, this difference did not reach statistical significance.

A consistent trend was observed in the independent LSM cohort (Figure 1B). AILDs patients defined as having advanced fibrosis (stage F3-4) based on LSM values exhibited a median CHI3L1 level that was significantly higher than that in patients with stage F2 fibrosis (P < 0.05), stage F0-1 fibrosis (P < 0.0001), and healthy controls (P < 0.0001). The median CHI3L1 level in patients with stage F2 fibrosis was also significantly higher than that in healthy controls (P < 0.01).

Correlation Between Serum CHI3L1 Levels and Liver Fibrosis

Spearman correlation analysis revealed a weak but significant positive correlation between serum CHI3L1 levels and age in patients with AILDs (r = 0.328, P < 0.001). Consistent with this, CHI3L1 concentrations showed significant positive correlations with established fibrosis assessment tools. As shown in Figure 2, Spearman correlation analysis revealed significant positive correlations between serum CHI3L1 and both the APRI (r = 0.451, P < 0.001) (Figure 2A) and the FIB-4 (r = 0.511, P < 0.001) (Figure 2B) in the histological cohort. A positive correlation was also observed between serum CHI3L1 and LSM values in the LSM cohort (r = 0.671, P < 0.001) (Figure 2C).

Correlation of CHI3L1 with Liver Biochemical and Inflammatory Indices

To explore the relationship between serum CHI3L1 levels and hepatic injury as well as inflammatory activity, we further analyzed its correlation with conventional liver biochemical indices (Figure 3). Spearman correlation analysis in the histological cohort showed that serum CHI3L1 levels did not demonstrate a statistically significant correlation with ALT (r = 0.118, P = 0.216), GGT (r = 0.004, P = 0.969), or ALP (r = –0.034, P = 0.724) (Figure 3A, C and D, respectively). However, serum CHI3L1 levels showed weak but statistically significant correlations with AST (r = 0.333, P < 0.001), TBIL (r = 0.257, P = 0.006), and DBIL (r = 0.367, P < 0.001) (Figure 3B, E and F, respectively).

Diagnostic Performance of CHI3L1 for Liver Fibrosis

To evaluate the diagnostic performance of serum CHI3L1 for liver fibrosis in patients with AILDs, we conducted ROC curve analysis and compared its value with that of two widely used non-invasive serum markers, APRI and FIB-4 (Figure 4 and Table 2). For the diagnosis of significant fibrosis (histological stage S ≥ 2), the area under the curve (AUC) for CHI3L1 was 0.833, which was slightly higher than that for APRI (AUC=0.797) and comparable to that for FIB-4 (AUC=0.801) (Table 2 and Figure 4A). At its optimal cutoff value of 89.00 ng/mL, CHI3L1 demonstrated a sensitivity of 67.0%, a specificity of 87.5%, and an accuracy of 71.4% for identifying significant fibrosis. For the diagnosis of advanced fibrosis (histological stage S ≥ 3), CHI3L1 showed good diagnostic performance with an AUC of 0.851. This value was higher than that of APRI (AUC=0.784) and was comparable to that of FIB-4 (AUC=0.840) (Table 2 and Figure 4B). At the optimal cutoff value of 106.63 ng/mL, CHI3L1 achieved a sensitivity of 80.4%, a specificity of 78.8%, and an accuracy of 81.2% for diagnosing advanced fibrosis.

These results from an independent, non-invasively assessed cohort further validated the reliable diagnostic value of CHI3L1 for liver fibrosis.

Univariate and Multivariate Analysis for Factors Associated with Advanced Liver Fibrosis

Univariable analysis identified older age, elevated CHI3L1, prolonged PT, higher bilirubin, and reduced ALB as significant correlates of advanced fibrosis, with G3-4 inflammation conferring a 27-fold elevated risk relative to G0-1. Discussion

Early identification of liver fibrosis is of paramount importance for the clinical management of AILDs, as fibrosis progression represents a critical and modifiable stage preceding the development of cirrhosis and its associated complications.34–36 Otherwise, progression to cirrhosis becomes the strongest factor contributing to the increased mortality risk in AILDs.5 However, due to the limited accuracy of existing non-invasive diagnostic tools in AILDs patients and the inherent clinical limitations of invasive liver biopsy, an ideal non-invasive assessment method is still lacking in clinical practice. While CHI3L1 has been established as a fibrosis biomarker across various liver diseases, our findings provide histologically validated, incremental evidence supporting its utility in assessing liver fibrosis within a well-characterized AILDs cohort. The core finding of this study is that serum CHI3L1 levels increase progressively with fibrosis severity in AILDs patients. While levels in patients with no or mild fibrosis (S0-1) were comparable to those in healthy controls, a significant elevation was observed in patients with significant fibrosis. More importantly, compared with other serum diagnostic tools, CHI3L1 demonstrated superior diagnostic performance in identifying both significant and advanced fibrosis. This performance was further validated in an independent LSM cohort. Multivariate logistic regression analysis further confirmed that serum CHI3L1 level is an independent factor associated with advanced liver fibrosis.

CHI3L1, secreted by various cell types, is predominantly enriched in the liver,37 making it an ideal biomarker for liver diseases. The present study found that CHI3L1 demonstrated good diagnostic value for liver fibrosis in AILDs, which is consistent with the findings of studies on this biomarker in other types of chronic liver diseases.38 In the field of viral hepatitis, CHI3L1 has been confirmed to be significantly correlated with histological fibrosis stages, and its diagnostic performance, especially for advanced fibrosis, is often superior to that of traditional serological models.37,39,40 In MASLD, although the diagnostic efficacy of CHI3L1 alone is limited, its incorporation as a core component into composite diagnostic models can significantly enhance the diagnostic accuracy for both significant and advanced fibrosis.26,27 Similarly, CHI3L1 also demonstrates good performance in the diagnosis of fibrosis associated with alcohol-related liver disease.41 Collectively, this evidence establishes the broad applicability of CHI3L1 as a serum biomarker for liver fibrosis across diverse etiologies; however, research specifically focused on AILDs remains limited. A current study on AILDs assessed the value of non-invasive tools, including CHI3L1, but the included studies predominantly relied on LSM rather than liver biopsy for fibrosis staging.28 While LSM is an important non-invasive assessment tool, its results can be confounded by factors such as hepatic inflammation and obesity, potentially leading to inaccuracies in LSM-based fibrosis staging, thereby affecting the accurate evaluation of CHI3L1’s diagnostic performance. Therefore, based on a prospectively collected cohort of AILDs patients with histologically confirmed fibrosis stages, this study provides more direct and reliable evidence for the diagnostic value of CHI3L1 in AILDs, addressing a gap in the existing literature.

This study confirmed that serum CHI3L1 level is an independent factor associated with advanced liver fibrosis and serves as an effective diagnostic biomarker in AILDs. The mechanistic foundation lies in the fact that CHI3L1 is not only a biomarker of fibrosis but also a key effector molecule driving the fibrogenic process. Activated hepatic stellate cells (HSCs) and macrophages are major cellular sources of CHI3L1, and its expression increases in parallel with fibrogenic activity.22 CHI3L1, acting in an autocrine/paracrine manner, binds to the IL-13Rα2 receptor on the surface of HSCs, activating downstream signaling pathways such as p38 MAPK/ATF3.42 This directly upregulates the expression of pro-fibrogenic mediators, thereby promoting the deposition of the extracellular matrix. This core mechanism reasonably explains the strong correlation between CHI3L1 and LSM observed in this study, as well as its good diagnostic performance for fibrosis staging. Furthermore, CHI3L1 levels showed no significant association with ALT, GGT, or ALP, but were specifically correlated with AST and bilirubin. This selective correlation profile is consistent with a recent AILDs study that reported the same pattern.28 Thus, this dissociation does not appear to be a cohort-specific finding, but rather a reproducible feature in AILDs patients. The reasons behind this observation remain unknown, and further studies are needed to elucidate the underlying mechanisms of this specific association.

The results of this study provide strong evidence supporting CHI3L1 as a biomarker for liver fibrosis in AILDs; however, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this single-center retrospective study included two internally validated cohorts (histological and LSM-based) but had a modest sample size due to AILDs rarity, and lacked an external validation cohort, limiting generalizability. Second, although no significant difference in CHI3L1 levels was observed between the S0-1 group and healthy controls, the latter were younger and had a higher proportion of males. These demographic discrepancies may introduce bias; therefore, future studies with age- and sex-matched healthy controls are warranted to validate these findings. Third, longitudinal monitoring of CHI3L1 levels was not performed; therefore, the predictive value of serial CHI3L1 measurements for disease progression or treatment response could not be assessed. Fourth, although serum CHI3L1 levels did not differ significantly across AILDs subtypes, the current cohort sizes particularly within the overlap syndrome group were insufficient to conduct robust subgroup analyses of diagnostic performance. Pooling these subtypes may obscure potential disease-specific variations in biomarker accuracy. Therefore, the findings reflect the overall performance of CHI3L1 across the AILDs spectrum. Future large-scale, multi-center studies incorporating independent histological validation are warranted to confirm the diagnostic utility of CHI3L1 across broader populations.

In summary, serum CHI3L1 is independently associated with advanced liver fibrosis and shows good diagnostic performance for fibrosis staging in a mixed cohort of AILDs. Given the distinct pathophysiological features across AILDs subtypes, further investigations in larger, subtype-specific cohorts are warranted to fully define the biomarker’s performance characteristics. Notably, the absence of an independent external cohort with histological confirmation limits the generalizability of our findings, underscoring the need for multi-center validation prior to clinical translation. CHI3L1 holds promise as a complementary biomarker to optimize current non-invasive fibrosis assessment approaches in AILDs.
