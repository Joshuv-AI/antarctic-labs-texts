A logistic model for glucose‑driven asymmetry in multi‑phase E. coli growth kinetics
Authors: María-Isabel González-Siso, Manuel Becerra, Daniel Torrecilla, Ana-María Díaz-Díaz, Jorge López-Beceiro, Ramón Artiaga
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study employs a generalized logistic model to resolve the complete growth kinetics of Escherichia coli under varying temperatures (29–45 °C) and glucose concentrations (0.1–2.0% w/v). The model quantifies critical growth parameters, including the characteristic time tcL, symmetry factor τ (glucose-dependent), and peak growth rate (temperature-dominated). It identifies a lower thermal growth limit between 15 °C and 20 °C, which is consistent with membrane phase transition and impaired translation, undetectable by classical exponential approaches. Doubling times at maximum growth rate align with literature values, validating the framework’s predictive accuracy. Crucially, glucose modulates growth symmetry (reducing τ by 2.6-fold at saturating concentrations) and temperature governs maximum rates (yielding a 2.4-fold increase in b), enabling precise microbial community control. This parametric separation offers actionable strategies for enhancing beneficial strains (e.g., probiotics, bioremediation agents) and suppressing pathogens in wastewater treatment, fermentation, and biofilm biocontrol. By decoupling the thermodynamic drivers of growth—where temperature governs the rate factor b (linked to catalytic kinetics) and substrate availability governs the asymmetry parameter τ (reflecting population-level energy allocation)—our model provides a generalizable framework for predicting cellular responses in biomedical contexts, such as bacterial infection dynamics or cancer cell proliferation under metabolic stress.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-54144-5.

Introduction

The regulation of microbial populations is critical in environmental science, medicine, and biotechnology. Escherichia coli, a well-studied model organism, exhibits temperature-dependent growth kinetics that influence its ecological and industrial impact. Traditional models describe bacterial proliferation through parameters such as specific growth rates or doubling times, typically derived by analyzing only the exponential growth phase of cultures1,2. Arrhenius-based growth models, also focus on this exponential phase (typically spanning 1–2 log units of growth) to estimate temperature-dependent activation energies3,4. While thermodynamic theories propose that a threshold microbial exergy level triggers cellular division5, these frameworks share a common limitation: they rely on partial growth curve data, excluding lag, stationary, and death phases.

Microbial growth modeling spans a wide continuum of approaches, from simple empirical equations to mechanistic and data-driven frameworks. At the most empirical end, phenomenological sigmoid models (such as logistic, Gompertz, and Richards equations) are designed to describe the complete growth curve, encompassing lag, exponential, and stationary phases. These models, first systematically compared by Zwietering et al. 6, remain popular due to their simplicity and their ability to extract essential kinetic parameters (lag duration, maximum growth rate, asymptotic population). While valuable for descriptive purposes, these models generally lack explicit mechanistic connections to substrate uptake, enzyme kinetics, or cellular physiology.

Progressing toward mechanistic representations, the Monod model7 remains foundational, relating the specific growth rate to substrate concentration via a saturation function (µ = µmax·S/(Ks+ S)). Temperature effects are usually incorporated independently, using Arrhenius-type or empirical cardinal-temperature formulations such as the Ratkowsky square-root model8. These approaches describe the exponential phase effectively but generally fail to capture lag or stationary behaviour under changing conditions.

A major advance in dynamic whole-curve modeling was introduced by Baranyi and Roberts9, who proposed a physiological-state variable to mechanistically account for the lag phase as cells adjust to a new environment. This Baranyi–Roberts model is now a standard in predictive microbiology, particularly under fluctuating environmental parameters such as temperature and pH.

Beyond these primary models, cybernetic or regulation-aware models10 integrate optimal control principles to simulate resource allocation and regulatory dynamics during growth on multiple substrates. They reproduce phenomena such as diauxic shifts and provide insight into intracellular decision-making, albeit at the cost of increased parameterization and computational complexity.

On the systems scale, constraint-based metabolic models, notably flux balance analysis (FBA), predict growth yields and flux distributions from stoichiometric reconstructions of metabolism11. These models connect genotype and environment to growth phenotype under steady-state assumptions, but they require dynamic extensions (dFBA) to reproduce time-resolved kinetics. Complementary stochastic approaches, such as the Gillespie algorithm12, resolve molecular noise and single-cell heterogeneity, explaining how cell-to-cell variability shapes macroscopic growth patterns. While mechanistically rich, such simulations are computationally intensive and less practical for routine growth-curve analysis.

More recently, machine-learning (ML) approaches have been applied to microbial growth prediction. Neural networks, Gaussian-process regressors, and Bayesian methods can map environmental or genomic features to kinetic parameters or directly forecast complete growth trajectories13,14. These models offer remarkable predictive accuracy but limited interpretability, often functioning as “black boxes” with little mechanistic transparency.

Within this broad landscape, the generalized derivative-logistic framework introduced in this study occupies an intermediate position. Like classical phenomenological models, it provides a compact description of full growth dynamics, yet it extends interpretability by introducing physically meaningful parameters: the characteristic time (tcL), the symmetry factor (τ), and the temperature-sensitive rate constant (b).

Minimal temperature for growth of E. coli, defined as that temperature below which sustained and balanced growth does not occur, was determined as ~ 7.5 °C 15,16. It was reported that while growth ends at 7.5 °C, it is impaired below 21 °C 17. However, there are genetic modifications that can enable normal growth at 15 °C 18. Membrane function deteriorates below 20 °C due to lipid solidification and insufficient unsaturated fatty acid production (particularly cis-vaccenic acid), impairing both nutrient transport1–5,16,19 and protein translation20. While fatty acid biosynthesis mutants show complete growth arrest below 20 °C 16, even metabolically competent strains exhibit severely restricted growth. It has been reported that there are probably several factors not yet sufficiently studied that influence the survival of E. coli in the environment21.

In this study, we overcome the limitations of the traditional exponential models by applying a novel whole-curve mathematical model to quantify E. coli growth across temperatures, deriving a equivalent logistic time parameter (tdLT) that captures complete growth dynamics throughout all phases, while maintaining alignment with doubling times calculated through the traditional method (tde) for the exponential phase (tdapm) and with literature reports1,2. Growth was monitored by optical density at 600 nm (OD600); numerous studies have shown that OD600 is proportional to biomass concentration or cell number, so throughout this work we use changes in OD, biomass, and cell count interchangeably19. This comprehensive approach provides two key advances: first, it enables precise environmental microbial control by integrating all growth phases into a unified framework; second, it reveals new opportunities for manipulating competitive microbial populations through temperature optimization. The model’s high parametric resolution can quantify subtle kinetic differences between bacterial strains—such as variations in their growth rate factor (b) or population synchrony (τ) under identical conditions. In principle, this could enable the design of strategies to favor beneficial bacteria (e.g., in bioremediation consortia) by targeting these specific kinetic parameters, offering potential for public health and industrial applications.

Results

Figure 1 summarizes the growth dynamics of Escherichia coli for a representative condition (0.1% glucose, 29 °C). The composite plot displays: (1) mean optical density (OD) growth curve derived from 8 experimental replicates, (2) natural log-transformed OD values (lnOD), (3) their respective time derivatives (ΔOD/Δt and ΔlnOD/Δt), (4) the fitted curve obtained using a generalized logistic derivative function applied to ΔOD/Δt, and (5) the linear region of the ΔlnOD/Δt curve used for conventional growth parameter determination. This linear region was specifically analyzed to derive the doubling time through the classical exponential-phase method and Arrhenius parameters for temperature dependence characterization. The high accuracy of the generalized logistic fit to the experimental ΔOD/Δt data is particularly noteworthy, demonstrating the model’s robust predictive capability across all growth phases. Supplementary figures analogous to Fig. 1, showing all temperatures and glucose concentrations, are provided in the Supporting Information. It should be noted that glucose was fully consumed in all assays, as confirmed by post-incubation residual sugar measurements. The tested concentration range (0.1-2.0% w/v) remained well above the substrate threshold for Monod-type limitation, ensuring carbon availability did not constrain growth. Consequently, glucose variations affected growth symmetry (τ) but not maximum population density, peak velocity, or time-to-peak, which were governed primarily by temperature-dependent kinetics.

Figure 2 shows the derived growth rates (fittings of d(OD)/dt) as a function of time for all experimental conditions. In some replicates (e.g., 1% glucose at 35 °C), a sharp initial increase in d(OD)/dt was observed. This feature appeared stochastically and is interpreted as a natural variation in the synchronous exit from the lag phase of a subpopulation of cells, leading to an earlier onset of measurable growth. Minor physical transients during the initial mixing may also contribute to the signal’s sharpness at these low cell densities. Crucially, the core exponential and deceleration phases—from which the key kinetic parameters (b, τ) are derived—remain robust and unaffected by these random initial fluctuations, as demonstrated by the consistent model fits across all replicates.

Figure 5 plots the τ parameter vs. temperature. This parameter governs the asymmetry of the d(OD)/dt peak profile. Perfect symmetry (symmetric normal-like distribution) occurs at τ = 1. When τ > 1, the curve develops an extended right-tail toward higher time values, indicating positive skewness. Given the observed temperature invariance of peak symmetry at fixed glucose concentrations, τ was optimized as a concentration-dependent constant rather than a temperature-variable parameter. This constraint prevents model overparameterization while maintaining physical interpretability. For the four tested glucose concentrations (0.1–2.0% w/v), τ exhibits a nonlinear inverse relationship with substrate concentration, decreasing systematically yet non-monotonically as glucose levels increase.

To provide a robust statistical assessment of the dependence of population synchrony (τ) on glucose concentration, we performed a bootstrap analysis (see Methods). The resulting distributions of τ for each glucose level are shown in Fig. 5, with error bars representing the 95% confidence intervals. These are the distributions of the bootstrapped τ values for each glucose level (as calculated in Methods M3). The representative bootstrapped τ values (solid circles) closely match those obtained from the global, simultaneous fitting (dashed line), confirming the robustness of our parameter estimation.

The bootstrap distributions provided a robust basis for statistical validation of the observed trend. For each pair of adjacent glucose concentrations (0.1% vs. 0.5%, 0.5% vs. 1.0%, 1.0% vs. 2.0%), we subtracted the bootstrap distributions iteration‑wise to obtain a distribution of the difference. From this distribution we calculated the mean difference, its 95% confidence interval (percentile method), and the two‑tailed empirical p‑value as twice the proportion of bootstrap differences that cross zero. The results are summarised in Table S1. Only the comparison between 0.5% and 1.0% glucose reached the 95% significance level, while the other two adjacent comparisons showed maximum significant confidence levels of 92.6% and 92.0%, respectively. Together with the monotonic decrease in the point estimates, this indicates a consistent glucose‑dependent trend in τ, with the most pronounced and statistically robust change occurring in the 0.5–1.0% range. The histograms of the distribution of the τ differences for each pair (Fig. 6) visually corroborate these findings.

These analyses formally verify that higher glucose levels lead to a significant increase in population growth synchrony, as quantified by a lower asymmetry parameter τ. The complete set of fitted logistic and exponential parameter values (c, b, tapm, τ, tcL, ASE, tdapm, k, tde, and the ratio tdapm/tde) for each glucose concentration and temperature is provided in Supplementary Tables S2–S5.

Table S6 in the Supplementary Information displays ANOVA results for all growth logistic kinetic parameters. The symmetry parameter τ exhibited pronounced glucose concentration dependence (F(3,32) = 1.12e⁶, p < 2.2e-16) while remaining temperature-invariant per model constraints. For the rate parameter b, two-way ANOVA confirmed significant independent effects of both temperature (F(8,32) = 38.2, p = 1.2e-12) and glucose concentration (F(3,32) = 22.1, p = 3.8e-08) without interaction (F(24,32) = 1.12, p = 0.39). In contrast, parameters c (maximum population density), maxRate (peak growth velocity), and tapm (time-to-peak) showed dominant temperature dependence (c: F(8,32) = 26.5, p = 2.1e-10; maxRate: F(8,32) = 18.7, p = 4.3e-09; tapm: F(8,32) = 35.5, p = 1.3e-11) but no significant glucose effects (c: F(3,32) = 2.16, p = 0.12; maxRate: F(3,32) = 1.84, p = 0.16; tapm: F(3,32) = 2.75, p = 0.06). The effect size analysis (η²) further confirmed temperature as the primary driver, explaining > 82% of variance in temperature-sensitive parameters versus < 26% for glucose effects where significant.

τ exhibited significant glucose concentration dependence (F(3,32) = 142.7, p < 0.001) while maintaining temperature invariance per model constraints. Values decreased nonlinearly from τ = 3.950 at 0.1% glucose to τ = 1.501 at 2.0% glucose (exponential fit R² = 0.98), indicating progressively right-skewed growth rate distributions at higher substrate concentrations.

From the biological point of view, the pronounced right-skewed tails observed in the growth rate curves (d(OD)/dt) reflect a delayed decline in population-wide activity relative to the rapid acceleration preceding the maximum rate. This asymmetry indicates that the cessation of growth is not synchronous across the population but rather driven by physiological heterogeneity and progressive resource limitation. Subpopulations with slower division rates, metabolic inhibition by accumulated products, or gradual membrane and translational impairments at non-optimal temperatures can sustain residual growth after most cells have entered the stationary phase, collectively producing an extended right tail. From a modelling perspective, this behaviour corresponds to a broadened or skewed distribution of individual division times and can be formally described by a logistic or gamma-type function with asymmetric kinetics. In the generalized logistic framework used here, the symmetry factor τ quantifies this deviation: larger τ values correspond to more pronounced right-skewed profiles, whereas τ approaches unity for nearly symmetric growth–decay dynamics. The observed reduction of τ with increasing glucose concentration therefore signifies that higher substrate availability enhances population synchrony, shortening the decline phase and yielding more symmetric growth profiles, while temperature predominantly modulates the peak rate parameter b. Together, these results suggest that τ serves as a sensitive integrative descriptor of population-level heterogeneity and physiological coordination in temperature–substrate interactions.

Figure 7 shows how the estimated duplication times, tdLT, vary throughout the different growth phases at three selected temperatures for different glucose percentages. It can be observed that only a small fraction of the curve reaches values of doubling times comparable to those of exponential growth.

Our analysis of Escherichia coli growth kinetics revealed three temperature-dependent patterns: (1) Peak broadening with reduced maximum amplitudes below 35 °C, (2) non-monotonic time-to-peak (tapm) behavior where tapm decreased between 29 and 35 °C, plateaued above 35 °C, and returned to 35 °C-equivalent values at 45 °C, and (3) glucose-concentration modulation where elevated temperatures produced sharper, taller peaks at low glucose levels (0.1–0.5%), indicating accelerated growth but earlier termination.

Discussion

Visual interpretation of integrated peak areas proved unreliable, necessitating quantitative analysis presented in Tables S2-S5 in the Supplementary Information. The maximum optical density (parameter c) decreased significantly with rising temperatures (29–45 °C), indicating thermodynamic constraints dominate biomass yield, with no clear glucose concentration effect. The symmetry factor τ governs the relative durations of lag, exponential, and saturation phases. Its consistency across temperatures at fixed glucose concentrations implies the fundamental growth process remains thermally invariant despite rate variations. Conversely, decreasing τ with increasing glucose reveals proportional shortening of the saturation phase relative to other phases, suggesting glucose may contribute to saturation-phase growth modulation. This inverse τ-glucose relationship is consistent with substrate-dependent modulation mechanisms reported in the literature. The peak time (tapm) exhibited inverse temperature dependence up to 35 °C before plateauing, while the doubling time at maximum growth rate (tdapm) decreased with temperature but increased with glucose concentration (0.1–2.0% w/v). These tdapm values aligned with literature reports of E. coli doubling times22–25 and matched classical model exponential duplication time (tde) values (Fig. 7, Tables S2-S5).

Table 1 provides a comparative overview of reported E. coli doubling times and the modelling frameworks used to derive them, contextualizing the results of our generalized logistic model. A key distinction is that our model calculates the doubling time specifically at the instant of maximum growth rate tdapm, offering a precise metric for the peak metabolic activity. In contrast, many literature values represent an average doubling time across the exponential phase. Under optimal conditions (LB, 37 °C), the canonical doubling time is approximately 20 min¹, while models like Monod kinetics predict longer times (≈ 45–65 min) under nutrient limitation². The table spans a spectrum of approaches, from empirical sigmoidal functions (Logistic, Gompertz⁴) and models incorporating physiological lag (Baranyi–Roberts⁵) to complex frameworks addressing resource allocation (Cybernetic⁶, Constraint-Based⁷) and variability (Stochastic⁸, ML-driven⁹,¹⁰). Our model reproduces realistic doubling times (10–50 min) and uniquely disentangles the influence of temperature (via the rate constant b) and substrate (via the asymmetry parameter τ), thereby extending predictive capability to asymmetric growth regimes. For studies reporting maximum specific growth rates µmax, we computed the doubling time using the standard conversion td=ln2/µmax (see notes to Table 1). Methodological papers are included to illustrate the modelling landscape, though a specific doubling time is not always applicable. Beyond the classical and empirical models summarized in Table 1, the field of E. coli growth modeling has evolved towards more complex, systems-level frameworks. Dynamic Flux Balance Analysis (DFBA) integrates constraint-based metabolic models with dynamic extracellular conditions to predict time-resolved phenotypes26. Furthermore, large-scale kinetic modeling approaches aim to capture the full complexity of metabolic networks. These include detailed mechanistic models built on ordinary differential equation systems27,28 and, more recently, generative machine-learning frameworks that learn kinetic patterns from large datasets29,30. While these powerful approaches offer high-resolution predictions, they often require extensive prior knowledge or large training datasets. In contrast, the generalized logistic model presented here operates at an intermediate level of complexity. It provides a holistic, parametric description of the complete growth curve by capturing the interplay between temperature (governing rates) and substrate (governing symmetry), without requiring genome-scale detail. This positions our model as a practical and interpretable tool for predicting and manipulating growth dynamics in applied settings, complementing the more detailed but resource-intensive systems biology approaches.

The derivative-based logistic approach identified a critical growth threshold at ~ 17 °C, undetectable by classical exponential modeling. This limit reflects biophysical constraints: membrane dysfunction from lipid solidification 1,16, impaired nutrient transport1,16, and compromised protein translation20. While growth cessation near 7.5 °C has been reported15,17,33, experimental evidence confirms impairment below 21 °C in non-engineered strains17. The presence of non-translating ribosomes at ~ 15 °C34 supports translational initiation deficits. Notably, the phase transition temperature of lipids from cells grown at 30 °C is 16 ± 2 °C, whereas lipids from cells grown at 15 °C transition at -1 ± 1 °C35. Lipids, particularly in lipopolysaccharides, are indispensable for membrane formation and flexibility. The fact that lipids synthesized above 15 °C solidify below this temperature implies growth at lower temperatures requires distinct lipid production governed by different kinetics. A similar kinetic shift may explain the trend reversal observed at 43–45 °C35. Although growth rates change throughout the temperature range, these specific lipid transitions explain inhibition below 21 °C and are consistent with our observed lower limit in the 15–20 °C range across all glucose levels. This kinetic shift may involve cold-shock proteins and ribosomal activity genes20.

The symmetry parameter τ showed pronounced glucose dependence but remained temperature-invariant per model constraints, decreasing nonlinearly from 3.950 (0.1% glucose) to 1.501 (2.0% glucose), following τ = 4.249e⁻⁰·⁸⁴⁹[gluc] (R² = 0.983, RMSE = 0.038). This indicates progressively symmetric growth distributions at higher substrate concentrations (one-way ANOVA: F(₃,₃₂) = 1.12e⁶, p < 2.2e-16). For parameter b, two-way ANOVA revealed significant independent effects of temperature (F(₈,₃₂) = 38.2, p = 1.2e-12) and glucose (F(₃,₃₂) = 22.1, p = 3.8e-08) without interaction (F(₂₄,₃₂) = 1.12, p = 0.39). Parameter b increased with temperature but decreased with glucose, varying 2.4-fold from 0.014 h−1 (29 °C/2.0%) to 0.068 h-1 (45 °C/0.1%). Despite concentration-dependent differences, Arrhenius extrapolation of b to zero growth rate converged at 16.7 °C ± 1.2 °C across all glucose levels, consistent with E. coli’s thermal limit (a linear model yielded similar values). Parameters c, maxRate, and tapm showed no significant glucose effects (two-way ANOVA: p > 0.05) but strong temperature dependencies (p < 0.001). This differential sensitivity guided our visualization: glucose-aggregated box plots for temperature-dominated parameters (c, maxRate, tapm) versus concentration-specific trendlines for glucose-sensitive parameters (b, τ). Initial analysis revealed no statistically significant glucose effects on key parameters (ANOVA, p > 0.05), justifying consolidation of all substrate levels for visual analysis in Fig. 3 (box plots for c, tapm, and maxRate).

The absence of glucose effects on maximum population density (c), peak growth velocity (maxRate), and time-to-peak (tapm) suggests substrate availability within 0.1–2.0% w/v does not constrain these characteristics. Instead, their strong temperature dependence indicates they are governed by thermally sensitive processes. This pattern is consistent with the known biophysical influence of temperature on membrane properties and enzymatic activity reported in the literature.

The strong glucose dependence of τ—which decreases 2.6-fold from 0.1% to 2.0% glucose—suggests that higher substrate concentrations promote synchronized population growth, reduced right-skew, narrower time distributions, and metabolic homogeneity. This aligns with glucose-dependent acceleration of metabolic commitment. Our results demonstrate a clear environmental dichotomy: substrate concentration primarily modulates τ (reflecting curve asymmetry and division synchrony), while temperature governs b, the rate constant linked to catalytic and biophysical kinetics. This separation enables the identification of critical thresholds, such as the lower thermal growth limit at ~ 16.7 °C, which remains undetectable by classical exponential-phase approaches.

Accordingly, our framework effectively bridges descriptive and mechanistic domains. It remains simple enough for rapid fitting to optical-density data, yet generates interpretable metrics that can inform or constrain more complex models (e.g., cybernetic or metabolic) or complement machine learning approaches as low-dimensional, physiologically grounded features. By merging whole-curve fitting with thermodynamic interpretability, the derivative-logistic model provides an operational tool for both predictive microbiology and biotechnological optimization.

Similarly, b’s glucose sensitivity implies substrate availability modulates specific growth rates without altering the fundamental thermal limit. While previous studies have mechanistically linked growth cessation to membrane phase transitions at 16.7 ± 1.3 °C, our phenomenological model independently yields a consistent temperature range. A linear extrapolation of the rate factor (b) to zero—as shown in Fig. 4—yielded a temperature range of 15–20 °C for all four glucose concentrations, indicating that the lower thermal limit is independent of substrate availability. Statistical analysis confirmed temperature dominates most parameters (c, maxRate, tapm), while glucose specifically modulates growth synchrony (τ) and rate kinetics (b). Though E. coli growth also occurs outside our temperature range, the process here characterized in strain CG1C46pDEST has a lower thermal limit near 16.7 °C governed by membrane thermodynamics and translational efficiency, establishing quantitative boundaries for microbial control.

Our analysis indicates the logistic model provides advantages for doubling time estimation across all growth phases. The classical exponential model assumes idealized growth (Eq. 1), yielding a constant doubling time (Eq. 2) but ignoring biological realities like lag phases and saturation. The logistic approach calculates instantaneous doubling times across all phases (Eq. 5), with tdapm (doubling time at maximum growth rate, Eq. 6) matching literature values 22–25. The dynamic resolution of tdLT captures growth rate variations through all phases, while tdapm represents the minimum doubling time during optimal growth. For non-ideal curves, tdLT provides the most biologically relevant replication metric. Whereas the exponential model’s growth rate parameter k describes only the exponential phase, the logistic parameter b (or characteristic time tcL = 1/b) governs dynamics across the entire curve. Critically, the logistic model’s instantaneous growth rate incorporates maximum population size, broadness, and asymmetry (via tapm and τ), providing an analytical framework that can capture the full growth continuum, unlike the exponential model restricted to the exponential phase. This holistic approach is particularly advantageous for characterizing complex growth behaviors beyond idealized exponential expansion.

Strengths and limitations of the modelling approach

The generalized logistic framework presented here offers several advantages over classical exponential or Monod-based models. Its ability to capture complete growth trajectories—including lag, acceleration, and deceleration phases—allows continuous quantification of kinetic parameters without assuming constant exponential behavior. By introducing explicit parameters for asymmetry (τ) and characteristic logistic time (tcL), the model provides mechanistic insight into physiological heterogeneity and environmental modulation, enabling separation of temperature- and substrate-driven effects. This approach is computationally efficient, easily adaptable to high-throughput data, and does not require prior specification of nutrient uptake or enzyme-level regulation, as in cybernetic or metabolic models.

However, the model’s phenomenological nature also imposes certain limitations. It does not explicitly account for intracellular metabolic fluxes, resource allocation, or stochastic single-cell variability, which can be captured by more mechanistic or stochastic frameworks (e.g., dynamic FBA, cybernetic, or agent-based models). Additionally, the logistic formulation assumes a well-mixed environment and homogeneous population, which may limit its predictive capacity under oxygen-limited or structured biofilm conditions. Despite these constraints, the balance between simplicity, interpretability, and predictive accuracy makes the generalized logistic model a practical and biologically meaningful tool for describing multi-phase microbial growth under variable temperature and nutrient regimes.

Methods

Growth curves from octuplicate experiments at each glucose-temperature combination were averaged prior to kinetic fitting. The resulting mean curves were analyzed, according to the classical and the logistic approaches, to extract parameters representing the growth curves, yielding one value per glucose concentration (0.1%, 0.5%, 1.0%, 2.0% w/v) at each temperature (29–45 °C). This produced a 9 (temperatures) × 4 (glucose levels) data matrix for systematic comparison, although some parameters were kept constant for a given glucose concentration.

M1. Obtaining growth curves

The strain employed in this study was a derivative of Escherichia coli BL21 (DE3) (One Shot®) transformed with plasmid pDEST527, which confers resistance to ampicillin. This strain was selected for its robust growth characteristics, genetic stability, and reproducibility under aerobic, glucose-rich conditions, making it suitable for kinetic modeling. Although BL21 (DE3) differs slightly from K-12 strains in regulatory architecture, its growth rates and doubling times fall within the range reported for E. coli laboratory strains used in previous kinetic benchmarks24,31 ensuring comparability of our data with published models. This strain was cultured in a batch configuration using 96-well plates with 200µL of culture medium per well, covered with a transparent and gas permeable sealing film to prevent evaporation and contamination, under continuous orbital shaking at frequency 282 cpm (3 mm), and other systematically varied conditions. LBA (1% tryptone, 0.5% yeast extract, 0.5% sodium chloride and 100 µg/mL ampicillin) was the growth medium, containing four glucose concentrations (0.1%, 0.5%, 1.0%, and 2.0% w/v). Temperature conditions spanned nine precisely controlled levels (29, 31, 33, 35, 37, 39, 41, 43, and 45 °C ± 0.2 °C). Growth kinetics were monitored via optical density measurements at 600 nm (initial OD600 0.1) taken at 5–20 min intervals over 24 h, using an automated spectrophotometric system (BioTek Synergy H1 Multimode Reader). Each experimental condition was replicated eight times (n = 8) to ensure statistical significance, with averaged growth curves -for each glucose concentration and temperature combination- used for subsequent analysis.

The raw OD data from the 8 replicates were first averaged to obtain a mean growth curve for each condition. The derivative of this mean OD curve with respect to time was then computed using the forward difference method: ΔOD/Δt between each consecutive data point.

M2. Classical and logistic approaches

Classical exponential growth kinetics were applied during the transient exponential phase, where the population dynamics follow:1\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm{ln}\left(OD\right)=\mathrm{ln}(OD_0)+k\cdot t$$\end{document}

where k denotes the specific growth rate. The doubling time, td, was derived from this rate constant as:

2\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{t}_{d}=\frac{\mathrm{l}\mathrm{n}\left(2\right)}{k}$$\end{document}

Growth rates, k, were determined from the steepest linear region (R2 > 0.98) of ln(OD) vs. time plots. In the classical methods temperature dependence of growth kinetics is quantified using the Arrhenius relationship 15:

3\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathrm{ln}\left(k\right)=\mathrm{ln}\left(A\right)-\frac{{E}_{a}}{R\cdot T}$$\end{document}

where A represents the pre-exponential factor, Ea the activation energy (kJ·mol⁻¹), R the universal gas constant (8.314 J·mol-1·K-1), and T absolute temperature (K).

In order to capture full growth curve dynamics beyond the exponential phase, we developed a novel kinetic model founded on a vertically reflected and shifted Richards decay function (Eq. 4), rather than a classic growth function.

4\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:y=A+\frac{K-A}{{(1+{Q\cdot e}^{B(t-M)})}^{\left(1/\nu\:\right)}}$$\end{document}

The specific form used for the cumulative growth curve is given by Eq. 5, which is derived from Eq. 4 by setting the lower asymptote A = 0, the upper asymptote K = c, the growth rate B = b, the inflection point M=tapm, and the shape parameters Q = ν = τ.

5\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:OD\left(t\right)=c-\frac{c}{{[1+\tau\:.\mathrm{e}\mathrm{x}\mathrm{p}(b.(t-{t}_{apm})\left)\right]}^{1/\tau\:}}$$\end{document}

Equation 5 is formulated as a vertically reflected and vertically shifted version of the standard Richards decay function; this transformation is introduced for convenience in the representation of OD while preserving the underlying Richards kinetics and parameter interpretation. We directly fitted the time-derivative of the experimental growth curve using the derivative of this transformed function (Eq. 6), which represents the growth rate.

6\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\frac{d\left(OD\right)}{dt}=\frac{c.b.\mathrm{e}\mathrm{x}\mathrm{p}(b.(t-{t}_{apm}\left)\right)}{{[1+\tau\:.\mathrm{e}\mathrm{x}\mathrm{p}(b.(t-{t}_{apm})\left)\right]}^{(1+\tau\:)/\tau\:}}$$\end{document}

In this formulation, the parameters have distinct biological meanings and units:

c [OD] is the maximum amplitude, representing the final cell density and the area under the rate curve (Eq. 6).

b [time− 1] is the rate factor, controlling the progression speed. Its inverse, tcL = 1/b, is a characteristic time.

tapm [time] defines the temporal position of the inflection point, i.e., the time of peak growth rate.

τ [dimensionless] is the asymmetry parameter. When τ = 1, the curve is symmetric (standard logistic). Deviations from unity quantify asymmetry in the growth dynamics, such as a prolonged deceleration phase.

A crucial step was the simultaneous fitting of curves across temperatures for a fixed glucose level, minimizing the ASE while constraining τ as a shared, temperature-invariant parameter to prevent overparameterization. This was statistically validated by a subsequent ANOVA, which confirmed that glucose concentration exclusively governs symmetry (τ), while temperature and glucose independently modulate the rate (b).

The key novelty of this approach lies not only in its unique foundation in a modified decay function but also in the simultaneous multi-curve fitting procedure. By fitting data from different temperatures with generalized decreasing logistic functions that share a temperature-invariant τ parameter—a process minimizing the ASE and later confirmed by ANOVA—we established a unified framework that empirically separates environmental drivers and offers mechanistically insightful parameters for microbial growth analysis.

Once the growth rate was obtained from Eq. 4, instantaneous doubling time can be calculated as:

7\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{t}_{dLT}\left(t\right)=\frac{\mathrm{l}\mathrm{n}\left(2\right)\cdot OD\left(t\right)}{\frac{d\left(OD\right)}{dt}}$$\end{document}

while doubling time at maximum growth rate was derived as:

8\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{t}_{dapm}={t}_{dLT}\left({t}_{apm}\right)$$\end{document}

In order to evaluate glucose and temperature dependence of each fitting parameter, an ANOVA was performed.

M3. Bootstrap analysis for parameter estimation and error intervals

The fitting of the d(OD)/dt curves, derived from the average growth curves at different temperatures, was performed using a unified approach that respects the model assumption: for each glucose concentration, the asymmetry parameter τ is constrained to be constant across temperatures. To obtain robust error estimates and validate the representative τ values, we employed a bootstrap procedure.

For a given glucose concentration, we first computed the residuals (observed OD – predicted OD) from the original fit. Then, for each of 1000 bootstrap iterations, we generated a synthetic dataset by adding a random sample (with replacement) of these residuals to the theoretical curve. We then re‑fitted the logistic model (Eq. 6) to the synthetic dataset under the same constraint: a single τ is shared across all temperatures, while c, b, and t ~ apm~ are allowed to vary per temperature. The fitting was implemented using the Trust Region Reflective algorithm via scipy.optimize.curve_fit in Python.

This procedure yielded a bootstrap distribution of τ for that glucose concentration. The representative bootstrapped τ value was taken as the mean of this distribution, and the 95% confidence interval was calculated as the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles. These intervals represent the range within which the true τ is likely to fall based on the variability observed in the resampled data.

M4. Statistical comparison of τ across glucose concentrations

To compare τ values between adjacent glucose concentrations (0.1% vs. 0.5%, 0.5% vs. 1.0%, 1.0% vs. 2.0%), we used the bootstrap distributions obtained in M3. For each pair, we subtracted the two distributions iteration‑wise, obtaining a bootstrap distribution of the difference (1000 differences). From this distribution we calculated:

The mean difference and its 95% confidence interval using the percentile method (2.5th and 97.5th percentiles).

The two‑tailed empirical p‑value, calculated as twice the proportion of bootstrap differences that cross zero (i.e., differences having the opposite sign to the observed mean difference).

The “maximum significant confidence level”, defined as the highest confidence level for which the confidence interval strictly excludes zero.

This approach is fully non‑parametric, directly quantifying the uncertainty in the comparison without relying on parametric assumptions. The results are reported in Table S1. All calculations were performed in Python using numpy and custom scripts (see Supplementary Code).

M5. Limitations and considerations of the cultivation system

Despite the controlled design of our batch assays, several factors inherent to small-scale cultivation may influence the interpretation of the kinetic parameters. First, oxygen transfer limitations are an intrinsic concern in shake-flask or microplate systems, particularly at higher cell densities or elevated temperatures where oxygen solubility decreases. Local oxygen depletion can create microgradients in redox potential and metabolic state, broadening the apparent growth-phase transitions and contributing to the asymmetric decay of d(OD)/dt. Similarly, CO₂ accumulation and pH drift may alter metabolic flux distributions near the end of the exponential phase. Second, mixing efficiency and wall effects differ substantially between small-scale vessels and larger bioreactors; thus, scale-up may change the balance between diffusion and convection, altering the effective growth rate and the estimated parameters b and τ. The model presented here assumes spatial homogeneity, and deviations from this condition could introduce systematic biases if applied to poorly mixed or oxygen-limited environments. Furthermore, optical density measurements at high biomass concentrations are affected by light scattering and nonlinearity, potentially compressing the apparent peak of d(OD)/dt and elongating its tail. Although the logistic framework captures the dominant kinetic trends, these physical limitations highlight the need for complementary validation under well-aerated, instrumented conditions to confirm parameter invariance and facilitate extrapolation to industrial or environmental scales.
