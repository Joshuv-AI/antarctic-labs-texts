Quantum theory does not need complex numbers
Timothée Hoffreumon, Mischa P. Woods

Abstract
Quantum theory was radically different from the theories of nature which came before it. One key difference was its use of complex numbers. This opened a longstanding debate over whether quantum theory fundamentally requires complex numbers—or if their use is merely a convenient choice. Until recently, this question was considered open. However, in a 2021 Nature article, a decisive argument was presented asserting that quantum theory needs complex numbers since real-number quantum theory is inconsistent with the postulates of quantum theory. In this work, we show that this conclusion was premature, and in actual fact, a real-number quantum theory is consistent with the postulates of quantum theory. Our theory retains key features such as representation locality (i.e. local physical operations are represented by local changes to the states). A direct consequence of our results is that quantum theory based on real or complex numbers are experimentally indistinguishable.



I
Introduction

I.1
Early days

Quantum theory (QT) required a huge conceptual departure from the classical theories which came before it. Its Hilbert space representation also manifestly uses complex numbers. Many have questioned whether these complex numbers are necessary and intrinsically linked to the weird new concepts—such as superposition or entanglement—which the new theory presented. Indeed, the history of whether QT requires complex numbers, or if these are a mere mathematical convenience, has a long and rich history.

Schrödinger’s conceptual struggle with the appearance of complex numbers in his fundamental equation is well documented
[
1
,
2
,
3
,
4
]
. In 1926 he sought to find a physical interpretation of the
real part
of his wavefunction—in analogy to how one can conveniently encode classical wave theory into the real part of complex numbers. While he ultimately succeeded in generating a real representation, he reluctantly ended up abandoning it in favor of the complex formulation we know today
[
1
,
5
]
.

In 1934, the possibility of representing QT using only real numbers was theorized within the algebraic approach of Jordan, Wigner, and von Neumann
[
6
]
. This was subsequently studied in the 60s mainly by Stückleberg
[
7
,
8
,
9
]
who swapped the usual complex representation in
d
dimensions for a real representation in
2d
dimensions. His theory could reproduce the same measurement statistics as QT, but he noticed discrepancies when trying to phrase the real version of the uncertainty principle.

It is worth remarking that this issue—and thus the present article—exclusively concerns the
mathematical representation
of QT. The necessity of an intrinsic ‘complex structure’ in its abstract formulation is a different issue altogether, and has been treated elsewhere in the 60s (see e.g., Ref.
[
10
]
and follow-up works).

I.2
Insufficiency of the early-day approaches and recent innovations

The real representation of QT studied in the literature is not ‘representation-local’, meaning that a local change in one of the components of a system at the physical level is not reflected by a local change at the level of its mathematical representation. The conventional complex-number representation of QT, in contrast,
is
representation-local; see
Figure

1
. The absence of this feature in the above-mentioned real representations of QT is the usual objection against them. This argument was first put forth in Refs.
[
11
,
12
]
, on top of the failure of local tomography argument popularized by Wooters
[
13
]
.
A different attempt at finding a formulation of quantum theory which only uses real numbers is to use the same construction considered in these works, but now restrict to a subset of states which are representation local and try to embed these in a larger, real space. This is the approach considered in
[
14
]
. They proved that this approach fails.

Nevertheless, the above arguments cannot rule out the existence of a representation-local real formulation of QT—they merely highlight its absence from the real formulation put forth thus far.

Here we prove the existence of a real formulation of quantum theory which is consistent with the postulates. The core conceptual step is to recognize that the Kronecker product is only
one
possible matrix representation of the tensor product, but not the only one. The attempts at a real formulation in
[
11
,
12
,
13
,
14
]
use Kronecker-product by definition, but this is not mandated by the postulates.

Figure 1:
Representation locality.
In a characterization of QT which is representation local, the following holds: a) Two non-interacting atoms which are independently prepared, have a mathematical description of the form
\rho\otimes\sigma
. b) Suppose a laser pulse is subsequently applied exclusively to the first atom (prompting it to change state), while the second atom is not affected by the laser pulse (such that it remains in the identical state). The mathematical description is updated to

\rho^{\prime}=h(\rho)

Postulates and structure

Postulates

II.2
Towards a postulate-consistent embedding of QT into a real-number theory

To set the stage, we consider the scenario in which the total system is composite with

Since the aim is to form a real representation of QT which is consistent with the postulates and reproduces the same statistics on a subset of states and measurements, we consider the case in which there is a one-to-one correspondence between every state in QT and every state in our real representation, and likewise for the measurements (POVMs). Moreover, we consider the case in which there is a linear map between these objects. Since in QT states and POVM elements live in a vector space, namely the tensor-product space of complex Hermitian matrices,

\displaystyle M(\cdot)

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{2}\Gamma_{d_{1}}(\cdot)\otimes\Gamma_{d_{2}}(\cdot)\otimes\ldots\otimes\Gamma_{d_{n}}(\cdot),

(2)

\displaystyle E(\cdot)

\displaystyle:=2M(\cdot).

II.3
Consistency with the statistics of quantum theory (QT)

It is not sufficient to have a real theory which is merely consistent with the postulates (i)-(iv)

We say a particular real theory gives rise to a

\displaystyle\Tr[\mathtt{e}_{r}\>\rho]=\Tr[\widetilde{\mathtt{e}}_{r}\>\widetilde{\rho}]

The importance of tensor-product representation

In the special case of only one subsystem, namely

Real-number quantum theory (RNQT) exists

III.1
Additional properties and the real-number quantum theory (RNQT)

The state space in QT can be partitioned into two subsets—separable states (statistical mixtures over product states) and entangled states. Due to the linearity of the local map
M
, our results preserve this bi-partition. Pure states—those which satisfy
\Tr[\rho^{2}]=1
—are also preserved. Finally, an intriguing and useful property of QT is tomographic locality
[
13
]
. This is the property that, by performing local measurements on many copies of an unknown quantum state, the state can be fully determined. The surprising part of this result is that one does not need non-local measurements to determine the state. A direct consequence of our results is that our RNQT is also tomographically local for the states reproducing QT: the set
\mathsf{S}_{\mathbbm{R}}
. This follows from the fact that our state-space and local measurements are in one-to-one correspondence with those of QT.

We have focused the discussion on the subspace of special symmetric matrices since it is the one whose states—the set of special states
\mathsf{S}_{\mathbbm{R}}
—give the same statistics as QT. However, states which live outside of this subspace, namely live in the larger symmetric space
\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{H}_{n,d})_{\mathbbm{R}}\backslash\mathsf{S}_{\mathbbm{R}}
, are consistent with quantum physics: On the one hand, states with support on this extended space do not ‘spill’ over into the special symmetric subspace under the combination of distinct systems via
\otimes_{\textup{r}}
. This is important, since had this not been the case, states from the extended space could have changed the statistics on the special symmetric subspace, which in turn would have led to measurement statistics for RNQT that are incompatible with QT. On the other hand, another important property is the conservation of non-negativity under system combination via
\otimes_{\textup{r}}
. This implies that if two local-system states are combined to form a state on the combined system, said combined state is still a valid state. These properties are proven in
Supplementary

E
. Of course, these ‘additional states’ in the representation can simply not be used. In which case the set of states and measurements reduces to that of the special states and special measurements.

III.2
Dynamics: an additional postulate

The postulates presented (i)-(iv) do not concern dynamics. Dynamics requires a fifth postulate, which for QT is often formulated as follows.
(v)
C
The evolution of an initial state
\rho

\displaystyle\hbar\frac{d\rho}{dt}=-i[H,\rho].

(9)

Here

is a Hermitian operator acting on the Hilbert space,
\mathcal{H}_{\mathsf{S}}
, called the Hamiltonian, and
\hbar\in{\mathbbm{R}}
is Planck’s constant.
As is well known, this gives rise to unitary dynamics, i.e.,
\rho(t)=U(t)\rho U^{\dagger}(t)
. We discuss in
Proposition

B.12

\Gamma_{d}(\rho(t))=\Gamma_{d}(U(t))\Gamma_{d}(\rho)\Gamma_{d}(U(t))^{T}

\Gamma_{d}(U(t))
is orthogonal, i.e.,
\Gamma_{d}(U(t))\Gamma_{d}(U(t))^{T}=\Gamma_{d}(U(t))^{T}\Gamma_{d}(U(t))=I

Observe that in finite

, where

is an anti-Hermitian matrix. With this observation, the RNQT counterpart of (v)

\rho
and

\displaystyle\hbar\frac{d\Gamma_{d}(\rho)}{dt}=-(J\otimes_{\textup{K}}{\mathbbm{1}}_{\mathsf{S}})\big[\Gamma_{d}(H),\Gamma_{d}(\rho)\big].

(10)

One can readily verify that
(J\otimes_{\textup{K}}{\mathbbm{1}}_{\mathsf{S}})\big[\Gamma_{d}(H),\Gamma_{d}(\rho)\big]=\big[(J\otimes_{\textup{K}}{\mathbbm{1}}_{\mathsf{S}})\Gamma_{d}(H),\Gamma_{d}(\rho)\big]
where
(J\otimes_{\textup{K}}{\mathbbm{1}}_{\mathsf{S}})\Gamma_{d}(H)
is anti-symmetric as required.

IV
Discussion and outlook

Our RNQT addresses an elementary question which puzzled Schrödinger
[
1
,
2
,
3
,
4
,
5
]
and our contemporaries alike
[
11
,
12
,
14
]
:
can we formulate QT without complex numbers in a satisfactory manner
? Our real-number formulation is consistent with the postulates of QT and has the same statistics. This guarantees representation locality is maintained, among other things. Core to this discovery is that the Kronecker-product representation of the tensor product is only implied by the postulates when they are stated over a complex Hilbert space, but this is not so over the the reals.

On a more philosophical note, some may argue that our real representation of QT merely simulates standard QT, since one can identify exactly where the complex numbers are represented within our real-number quantum theory (RNQT). There are two important counterpoints to this view:

1. Analogy with classical wave theory

While this observation is true, an analogous situation arises in classical wave theory. The original formulation employed cosine and sine functions, using real trigonometric identities to describe wave propagation. It was later discovered that complex numbers could also be used to represent classical waves, simplifying the mathematics by replacing trigonometric identities with Euler’s relations. One could equally well claim that this “complex wave theory” simulates “real wave theory”, yet such a viewpoint is rarely, if ever, adopted.

2. The notion of simulation and reality

The idea that one system simulates another implicitly suggests that the latter is somehow more real. For example, we might say that a computer algorithm simulates the turbulence experienced by an airplane wing: the turbulence is a physical phenomenon, whereas the algorithm provides only an abstract representation of it. In contrast, both the conventional complex formulation of quantum theory and the real formulation introduced here are abstract mathematical depictions of physical reality. In this sense, neither can be said to simulate the other, nor is one more real than the other—they are isomorphic to one another.

We provide a way to do so in which the dimension of the real matrix representations of states and POVM elements in the subspace that embeds QT is double that of their QT counterparts. This is what one would naively expect, since a complex number requires 2 real degrees of freedom. However, beyond this, using our RNQT presents only minor inconveniences compared to the standard complex theory. Our results demonstrate that the utility of complex numbers in the representation of QT is on par with their role in many classical theories such as wave mechanics: a mere mathematical convenience.

Our results allow for an important conceptual unification: classical theories, such as classical and statistical mechanics, and General Relativity, only require real numbers and are representation-local. It is an ongoing challenge to fully appreciate the non-classical aspects of quantum mechanics (e.g.
[
17
,
18
,
19
]
), and to unify it with gravity (e.g.
[
20
,
21
,
22
,
23
]
). The up-until-now apparent need for complex numbers for the representation of QT might have been an obscurifying factor in this endeavor.

{\mathbbm{1}}_{n}=(\delta_{i,j})^{n,n}_{i=1,j=1}

I=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\end{smallmatrix}\right)
and (
I={\mathbbm{1}}_{2}
) and
X,Y,Z
be the Pauli matrices such that
Y=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&-i\\
i&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)
and
J:=XZ=-iY=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&-1\\
1&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)
. When clear from the context, we will also put a subscript referring to some dimension to mean “extend this matrix by taking its Kronecker product with the unit matrix of the corresponding dimension”. That is, the following kind of shorthand notation will be used in this appendix and the next one:
I_{n}:=I\otimes_{\textup{K}}{\mathbbm{1}}_{n}
,
J_{n}:=J\otimes_{\textup{K}}{\mathbbm{1}}_{n}
,
X_{n}:=X\otimes_{\textup{K}}{\mathbbm{1}}_{n}
, etc.

Let
\Gamma
be the mapping defined as in
eq.

4
with the subscript referring to the dimension omitted for the whole section as we focus on a single system of dimension
d=n
. In this appendix, we extend by linearity the domain and codomain of
\Gamma
from

\mathsf{Herm}_{n}\left({\mathbbm{C}}\right)\rightarrow\mathsf{SY}_{n}({\mathbbm{R}}),

to

{\mathbbm{C}}^{n\times n}\rightarrow{\mathbbm{R}}^{2n\times 2n}
.
That is, for any matrix
M\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
which decomposes as

M=\text{Re}(M)+i\cdot\text{Im}(M)=(A-D)+i\cdot(B+C)\>,

(S.B.31)

the map
\Gamma:{\mathbbm{C}}^{n\times n}\rightarrow{\mathbbm{R}}^{2n\times 2n}
reads

M\mapsto\Gamma\left\{M\right\}=I\otimes_{\textup{K}}\text{Re}(M)+J\otimes_{\textup{K}}\text{Im}(M)=\begin{pmatrix}A-D&-(B+C)\\
B+C&A-D\end{pmatrix}

(S.B.32)

Proposition B.4
.

The map
\Gamma
is a unital
*
-ring homomorphism
(\mathbb{C}^{n\times n},+,\cdot,{~}^{\dagger})\rightarrow(\mathbb{R}^{2n\times 2n},+,\cdot,{~}^{T})
. That is, for any two matrices
M,N\in{\mathbbm{C}}^{n\times n},

\begin{gathered}\Gamma\left\{M+N\right\}=\Gamma\left\{M\right\}+\Gamma\left\{N\right\}\>;\quad\Gamma\left\{MN\right\}=\Gamma\left\{M\right\}\Gamma\left\{N\right\}\>;\\
\Gamma\left\{{\mathbbm{1}}_{n}\right\}={\mathbbm{1}}_{2n}=I_{n}\>;\quad\Gamma\left\{M^{\dagger}\right\}=\Gamma\left\{M\right\}^{T}\>.\end{gathered}

(S.B.33)

Proof.

\displaystyle\Gamma\left\{M\right\}^{T}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}(A-D)^{T}&(B+C)^{T}\\
-(B+C)^{T}&(A-D)^{T}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}A+D&-(B-C)\\
B-C&A+D\end{pmatrix}

(S.B.34)

\displaystyle=I\otimes_{\textup{K}}(A+D)+J\otimes_{\textup{K}}(B-C)=\Gamma\left\{M^{\dagger}\right\}\>.

This is because
A
and
C
are symmetric while
B
and
D
are antisymmetric.
∎

So this mapping sends the ring structure of complex matrices to the one of real matrices, and even preserves the adjoints. We can see by construction that it is injective but not surjective and that
\Gamma
will preserve any real linear combination. However, this mapping is not an algebra
*
-homomorphism. Indeed, it cannot properly map the scalars in
{\mathbbm{C}}^{n\times n}
to those in
{\mathbbm{R}}^{n\times n}
since it does not handle multiplication by a complex number as expected:
\Gamma\left\{(i\cdot M)\right\}\neq i\cdot\Gamma\left\{M\right\}
as
i
is not an element of
{\mathbbm{R}}
. Rather, multiplying any matrix
M
by a complex
c\in{\mathbbm{C}}
gives

\Gamma\left\{(cM)\right\}=r[(\cos\theta I+\sin\theta J)\otimes_{\textup{K}}\mathrm{Re}\left(M\right)+(\cos(\theta+\frac{\pi}{2})I+\sin(\theta+\frac{\pi}{2})J)\otimes_{\textup{K}}\mathrm{Im}\left(M\right))\>,

(S.B.35)

for
c=re^{i\theta}
with
r\in{\mathbbm{R}}
and
\theta\in[0,2\pi[
.

Similarly, despite the preservation of adjoints, it will not be able to be an isometry with respect to the inner-product space structure induced by the Frobenius inner product (
\text{tr}\left[\cdot^{\dagger}\>\cdot\right]
in
{\mathbbm{C}}^{n\times n}
and
\text{tr}\left[\cdot^{T}\>\cdot\right]
in
{\mathbbm{R}}^{2n\times 2n}
) because the base fields are different. Any complex-valued inner product will be mapped to 0 through the mapped inner product
\text{tr}\left[\Gamma\left\{\cdot\right\}^{T}\>\Gamma\left\{\cdot\right\}\right]
since,
\forall M,N\in{\mathbbm{C}}^{n\times n}
,

\text{tr}\left[\Gamma\left\{N\right\}^{T}\>\Gamma\left\{M\right\}\right]=2\>\mathrm{Re}\left(\text{tr}\left[N^{\dagger}\cdot M\right]\right)\>,

(S.B.36)

as directly follows from
J
having trace 0 and
J^{2}=-I
.

Of course, requiring
\Gamma\left\{(i\cdot M)\right\}=i\cdot\Gamma\left\{M\right\}
and
\text{tr}\left[N^{\dagger}\cdot M\right]=\text{tr}\left[\Gamma\left\{N\right\}^{T}\cdot\Gamma\left\{M\right\}\right]
is naive. The
\Gamma
mapping can nevertheless be both a
*
-algebra homomorphism as well as an inner product space isomorphism by seeing the ‘base field’ of
\mathbb{R}^{2m\times 2m}
as the linear span of
\{I,J\}
in
\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}
instead of
{\mathbbm{R}}
. In this case, scalar multiplication would correspond to
Eq.

S.B.35
and the corresponding inner product would be such that
\langle{\Gamma\left\{N\right\}}|{\Gamma\left\{M\right\}}\rangle=\text{tr}\left[N^{\dagger}\cdot M\right]
(up to a normalisation constant). But this exactly amounts to identifying
{\mathbbm{C}}
as a
*
-algebra over
{\mathbbm{R}}
with its representation in
{\mathbbm{R}}^{2\times 2}
given by
(1,i)\mapsto(I,J)
such that (the notation at the space level is not rigorous but is there to help understanding the chain of equivalences involved):

\begin{gathered}{\mathbbm{C}}^{n\times n}\cong{\mathbbm{C}}\otimes{\mathbbm{R}}^{n\times n}\cong({\mathbbm{R}}\otimes{\mathbbm{R}}^{n\times n})\oplus({\mathbbm{R}}\otimes{\mathbbm{R}}^{n\times n})\rightarrow{\mathbbm{R}}^{2\times 2}\otimes{\mathbbm{R}}^{n\times n}\cong{\mathbbm{R}}^{2n\times 2n}\>:\\
M=1\otimes_{\textup{K}}\mathrm{Re}\left(M\right)+1i\otimes_{\textup{K}}\mathrm{Im}\left(M\right)\mapsto I\otimes_{\textup{K}}\mathrm{Re}\left(M\right)+J\otimes_{\textup{K}}\mathrm{Im}\left(M\right)\end{gathered}\>.

(S.B.37)

In that regard, one can summarize the objections with the Renou et al. approach presented in this paper as a review of the issues faced when considering
{\mathbbm{R}}
to be the scalars of the real theory instead of the representation of
{\mathbbm{C}}
in
{\mathbbm{R}}^{2\times 2}
.
In the postulates, requiring every mention of
{\mathbbm{C}}
to be replaced by
{\mathbbm{R}}
instead of the real span of
\{I,J\}
causes them to lose structure when passing from complex quantum theory to their real theory, which explains their findings: if parts of the mathematical structure is lost when mapping from QTto a real theory, it is expected that not all predictions of the former can be recovered in the latter. Using the particular mapping
eq.

4
is just a way to pinpoint what has been lost under the change of representation, and how to fix it.

To start, it is necessary to first understand how the complex numbers are mapped under
\Gamma
.
As
I_{n}=\Gamma\left\{{\mathbbm{1}}_{n}\right\}
and
J_{n}=\Gamma\left\{i\>{\mathbbm{1}}_{n}\right\}
, the matrices
I
and
J
represent the real and imaginary unit scalars.
Using the decomposition (
S.B.20
), we have
iM=-(C+iD)+i(A+iB)=-(B+C)+i(A-D)
and
\Gamma\left\{iM\right\}=\begin{pmatrix}-(B+C)&-(A-D)\\
A-D&-(B+C)\end{pmatrix}=J\otimes_{\textup{K}}(A-D)-I\otimes_{\textup{K}}(B+C)=J_{n}\Gamma\left\{M\right\}
.
This shows that

\forall a,b\in{\mathbbm{R}},\quad\Gamma\left\{(a+bi)M\right\}=a\Gamma\left\{M\right\}+bJ_{n}\Gamma\left\{M\right\}\>,

(S.B.38)

meaning that the
\Gamma
map preserves linear sums when the suitable
i\mapsto\Gamma\left\{i\>{\mathbbm{1}}_{n}\right\}=J_{n}
has been applied.
In particular, the
b=0
case shows that
\Gamma
is real-linear.
Going back to polar coordinates
(a,b)\mapsto(r\cos\theta,r\sin\theta)
, this equation reads
\Gamma\left\{(re^{i\theta})M\right\}=r\cos\theta\Gamma\left\{M\right\}+r\sin\theta J_{n}\Gamma\left\{M\right\}
. Assuming
r=1
in the case where the imaginary part of the complex matrix
M
is zero (i.e.,
B=C=0
), that is, when it is real (i.e.,
M=\mathrm{Re}\left(M\right))
, the multiplication by a complex phase amounts to doing an
SO(2)
rotation of the matrix
M
:

\forall\theta\in[0,2\pi[\,,\>\forall M=\mathrm{Re}\left(M\right),\quad\Gamma\left\{e^{i\theta}M\right\}=(\cos\theta I+\sin\theta J)\otimes_{\textup{K}}M=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta&-\sin\theta\\
\sin\theta&\cos\theta\end{pmatrix}\otimes_{\textup{K}}M\>.

(S.B.39)

Hence, the discrepancy (
S.B.35
) sort of disappears in this case; the unitary phase freedom has become a planar rotation. The other discrepancy,
Eq.

S.B.36
also disappears (up to a factor of 2) as
\mathrm{Re}\left(\text{tr}\left[N^{\dagger}\cdot M\right]\right)=\text{tr}\left[N^{\dagger}\cdot M\right]
for real matrices.

However, the postulates (i)
C
to (iii)
C
tell us that the predictions of quantum theory are inner products of positive, thus Hermitian, matrices, rather than of real matrices. When required to work with Hermitian matrices only, the phase discrepancy (
S.B.35
) remains, whereas the inner product discrepancy (
S.B.36
) also disappears (as will be proven below).
Thence, it appears plausible that the mathematical structure lost when passing from (i)-(iii)
C
to (i)-(iii)
R
has no observable consequences. Indeed, it will not change the inner products between positive operators, so none of the probabilities predicted through the Born rule. We now prove this claim.

In the main text, we name the image of Hermitian matrices through
\Gamma
the special symmetric matrices.

Definition B.5
.

The Special Symmetric Matrices are the matrices
M\in\mathbb{R}^{2n\times 2n}
which have the form

M=\begin{pmatrix}A&-B\\
B&A\end{pmatrix},

for
A\in\mathsf{Sym}_{n}\left({\mathbbm{R}}\right)
and
B\in\mathsf{ASym}_{n}\left({\mathbbm{R}}\right)
. The space of all such matrices is denoted
\mathsf{SY}_{n}({\mathbbm{R}})
.

An interesting observation of Stükleberg was that the special symmetric matrices are precisely the symmetric matrices that behave correctly with the representation of the imaginary unit, meaning that they commute with it
[
7
]
.

Lemma B.6
.

Special symmetric matrices are the real matrices that are both symmetric and tetradionic (see
Definition

E.1
). In symbols,

\Gamma\left\{M\right\}\in\mathsf{SY}_{n}({\mathbbm{R}})\iff\Gamma\left\{M\right\}\in\mathsf{Sym}_{2n}\left({\mathbbm{R}}\right)\>\&\>J_{n}\Gamma\left\{M\right\}=\Gamma\left\{M\right\}J_{n}\>.

(S.B.40)

From its characterization, it is obvious that
\mathsf{SY}_{n}({\mathbbm{R}})
is a subspace of
{\mathbbm{R}}^{2n\times 2n}
of dimension
n^{2}
. It is also direct to see that the image of
\mathsf{Herm}_{n}\left({\mathbbm{C}}\right)
through the
\Gamma
map is contained in
\mathsf{SY}_{n}({\mathbbm{R}})
. From there, one can infer the
\Gamma
map to be an isomorphism between
*
-algebras over
{\mathbbm{R}}
.

Proposition B.7
.

The map
\Gamma
is an isomorphism of
*
-algebras over
{\mathbbm{R}}
between
\mathsf{Herm}_{n}\left({\mathbbm{C}}\right)
and
\mathsf{SY}_{n}({\mathbbm{R}})
. That is, in addition to the ring structure obtained from
Proposition

B.4
, the following holds for any two Hermitian matrices
H,L
and reals
a,b
:

\Gamma\left\{aH+bL\right\}=a\Gamma\left\{H\right\}+b\Gamma\left\{L\right\}\>.

(S.B.41)

Proof.

The only missing part was the linearity w.r.t. real coefficient of
\Gamma
when applied to Hermitian matrices. It thus follows from taking
Eq.

S.B.38
in the case where
b=0
.
∎

Focusing on Hermitian matrices is relevant not only because they form a real algebra. Indeed, what is important in a theory is its predictive power. Yet, the main quantities that are lost when considering the representation of QTover the reals are the complex-valued inner products. However, they should have little impact on the predictive power of the theory, as all quantum states and effects are positive operators, leading to positive-valued inner products. While it is true, as we will now show, the loss of the complex-algebraic structure will still have an influence, but a more subtle one.

Proposition B.8
.

The map
\Gamma
when applied from
\mathsf{Herm}_{n}\left({\mathbbm{C}}\right)
to
\mathsf{SY}_{n}({\mathbbm{R}})
is, up to renormalization of the trace by a factor of 1/2, an isometry of inner product spaces.

Proof.

It follows from
Eq.

S.B.36
, but we will prove this formula using the decomposition
Eq.

S.B.20
for completeness.

For two such decomposed matrices
M=(A+iB)+i(C+iD)
and
N=(A^{\prime}+iB^{\prime})+i(C^{\prime}+iD^{\prime})
, it can be computed that

\text{tr}\left[M^{\dagger}N\right]=(A-D)(A^{\prime}-D^{\prime})-(B-C)(B^{\prime}+C^{\prime})+i\left((B-C)(A^{\prime}-D^{\prime})+(A-D)(B^{\prime}+C^{\prime})\right)

and that

\text{tr}\left[\Gamma\left\{M\right\}^{T}\Gamma\left\{N\right\}\right]=2\left((A-D)(A^{\prime}-D^{\prime})+(B+C)(B^{\prime}+C^{\prime})\right)\>.

M
and
N
are made Hermitian by setting
C,D,C^{\prime}
and
D^{\prime}
to 0. In such a case, the mapping is one-to-one,
\Gamma\left\{\mathsf{Herm}_{n}\left({\mathbbm{C}}\right)\right\}=\mathsf{SY}_{n}({\mathbbm{R}})
, and the above computation leads to

\forall H,L\in\mathsf{Herm}_{n}\left({\mathbbm{C}}\right),\quad\text{tr}\left[\Gamma\left\{H\right\}\Gamma\left\{L\right\}\right]=2\text{tr}\left[HL\right]\>,

(S.B.42)

showing that
\Gamma
is an isometry (up to a factor of 2) when applied to Hermitian matrices.
∎

Hence, passing from
\mathsf{Herm}_{n}\left({\mathbbm{C}}\right)
to
\mathsf{SY}_{n}({\mathbbm{R}})
under
\Gamma
is more sensible than from
{\mathbbm{C}}^{n\times n}
to
{\mathbbm{R}}^{2n\times 2n}
as less structure is lost in the process. We can now focus on the set of states, and for that we need to track what happens to the trace as well as the positivity.

For the positivity condition, we look at the image through
\Gamma
of the set
\mathsf{Herm}^{+}_{n}\left({\mathbbm{C}}\right)
of the
n\times n
positive semi-definite matrices over
{\mathbbm{C}}
.

Theorem B.1
.

Under the mapping
\Gamma
, the positive semi-definite matrices in
\mathsf{Herm}_{d}\left({\mathbbm{C}}\right)
are mapped to positive semi-definite matrices in
\mathsf{SY}_{n}({\mathbbm{R}})
. Moreover,
\Gamma(H)=\textup{Spec}(H)\cup\textup{Spec}(H)
,
\forall H\in\mathsf{Herm}_{n}\left({\mathbbm{C}}\right)
where
\textup{Spec}(\cdot)
denotes the spectrum.

Proof.

Let
M\in{\mathbbm{C}}^{n\times n}:\>M\geq 0
meaning that
z^{\dagger}Mz\geq 0

\forall z\in{\mathbbm{C}}^{n}
. Then

\displaystyle\forall z:

\displaystyle z^{\dagger}Mz\geq 0\iff

(S.B.43)

\displaystyle\forall x,y\in{\mathbbm{R}}^{n},\forall A\in\mathsf{Sym}_{n}\left({\mathbbm{R}}\right),\forall B\in\mathsf{ASym}_{n}\left({\mathbbm{R}}\right):

\displaystyle(x-iy)^{T}(A+iB)(x+iy)\geq 0\iff

\displaystyle x^{T}Ax+y^{T}Ay+2y^{T}Bx\geq 0\>.

The last line has been reached by distributing the parentheses, then using
x^{T}My=y^{T}M^{T}x
, which implies that
x^{T}Bx=0
for
B
antisymmetric and
x^{T}Ay-y^{T}Ax=0
for
A
symmetric.

Now let
\Gamma\left\{M\right\}\in{\mathbbm{R}}^{2n\times 2n}:\>\Gamma\left\{M\right\}\geq 0
meaning that
\Gamma\left\{z\right\}^{T}M\Gamma\left\{z\right\}\geq 0

\forall\Gamma\left\{z\right\}\in{\mathbbm{R}}^{2n}
. Then,

\displaystyle\forall\Gamma\left\{z\right\}:

\displaystyle\Gamma\left\{z\right\}^{T}M\Gamma\left\{z\right\}\geq 0\iff

(S.B.44)

\displaystyle\forall x,y\in{\mathbbm{R}}^{n},\forall A\in\mathsf{Sym}_{n}\left({\mathbbm{R}}\right),\forall B\in\mathsf{ASym}_{n}\left({\mathbbm{R}}\right):

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}x\\
y\end{pmatrix}^{T}\begin{pmatrix}A&-B\\
B&A\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}x\\
y\end{pmatrix}\geq 0\iff

\displaystyle x^{T}Ax+y^{T}Ay+2y^{T}Bx\geq 0\>,

so we have reached the same condition as the above one. This should be no surprise from
\Gamma
being an algebra
*
-homomorphism and the variables have been chosen to make it clear that the second condition is but the image of the first under
\Gamma
.

To prove
\Gamma(H)=\textup{Spec}(H)\cup\textup{Spec}(H)
,
\forall H\in\mathsf{Herm}_{n}\left({\mathbbm{C}}\right)
, we note that the unitary

U=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix}{\mathbbm{1}}_{n}&{\mathbbm{1}}_{n}\\[4.0pt]
i\,{\mathbbm{1}}_{n}&-\,i\,{\mathbbm{1}}_{n}\end{pmatrix},

block-diagonalises
\Gamma(H)
:

U\Gamma(H)U^{\dagger}=H\oplus H^{\dagger}=H\oplus H,

where
\oplus
is the direct sum. Since the spectrum is invariant under a unitarily transformation, it follows
\Gamma(H)=\textup{Spec}(H)\cup\textup{Spec}(H)
,
\forall H\in\mathsf{Herm}_{n}\left({\mathbbm{C}}\right)
follows.
∎

For the trace, we have just shown that
\text{Tr}[\Gamma\left\{H\right\}\Gamma\left\{L\right\}]=2\text{Tr}[HL]
for
H
and
L
Hermitian. Taking
L={\mathbbm{1}}_{n}
, we recover the definition of the
M
map in the main text,
eq.

2
, for a single subsystem.

Therefore, the states in
\text{Herm}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
are in one-to-one correspondence with states in
\mathsf{SY}_{n}({\mathbbm{R}})
under
M
. As for the effects, since the
\Gamma
map preserves inner products and positivity, it preserves duals as well so they also correspond. This also hold if the effects are restricted to projectors, whose image through
\Gamma
are also proportional to projectors by consequence of
Eq.

S.B.33
.

We now make a short detour to investigate what are the matrices outside of the image of Hermitian matrices.

Lemma B.9
.

A\in\mathsf{Sym}_{2n}\left({\mathbbm{R}}\right)\setminus\mathsf{SY}_{n}({\mathbbm{R}})\iff A\in\mathsf{Sym}_{2n}\left({\mathbbm{R}}\right)\>\&\>J_{n}A=-AJ_{n}\>.

(S.B.45)

Proof.

Starting from a symmetric matrix
S\in\mathsf{Sym}_{2n}\left({\mathbbm{R}}\right)
, one defines
\mathrm{Tetr}(S):=\frac{S-JSJ}{2}
and
\mathrm{ATetr}(S):=\frac{S+JSJ}{2}
. (See
Eq.

S.E.106
below for more details on these maps. Note that
(JSJ)^{T}=JSJ
so both matrices are symmetric.) Next, remark that
J_{n}\mathrm{Tetr}(S)=J_{n}S+SJ_{n}=\mathrm{Tetr}(S)J_{n}
so
\mathrm{Tetr}(S)
commutes with
J_{n}
and so
\mathrm{ATetr}(S)
anticommutes with
J_{n}
. Using
Lemma

B.6
, it must be that
\mathrm{Tetr}(S)\in\mathsf{Sym}_{n}\left({\mathbbm{R}}\right)
which implies
\mathrm{ATetr}(S)\in\mathsf{Sym}_{2n}\left({\mathbbm{R}}\right)\setminus\mathsf{SY}_{n}({\mathbbm{R}})
. Therefore, we have proven the right implication in the above formula. Finally, the converse also holds because
Lemma

B.6
is a necessary and sufficient condition.
∎

Corollary B.10
.

The set of symmetric matrices that are not special symmetric is orthogonal to the set of special symmetric matrices:

\forall M\in\mathsf{SY}_{n}({\mathbbm{R}}),\>\forall A\in\mathsf{Sym}_{2n}\left({\mathbbm{R}}\right)\setminus\mathsf{SY}_{n}({\mathbbm{R}}),\quad\text{tr}\left[MA\right]=0\>.

(S.B.46)

As a consequence, these matrices are traceless.

Remark B.11
.

Notice that the state space of the real quantum theory considered in
[
14
]
is larger than a trace-normalized subset of
\mathsf{SY}^{+}_{n}({\mathbbm{R}})
, as it is taken to be one of
\mathsf{Sym}^{+}_{2n}\left({\mathbbm{R}}\right)
and
\mathsf{SY}^{+}_{n}({\mathbbm{R}})\subset\mathsf{Sym}^{+}_{2n}\left({\mathbbm{R}}\right)
.
In the case of the map
\Gamma
, this is not a problem to restrict the state space to

, as the “states” in
\mathsf{Sym}^{+}_{2n}\left({\mathbbm{R}}\right)\setminus\mathsf{SY}^{+}_{n}({\mathbbm{R}})
are orthogonal to those in
\mathsf{SY}^{+}_{n}({\mathbbm{R}})
.

Provided that the dimension is at least
2n
, the corresponding QTcan be represented in
\mathsf{SY}^{+}_{n}({\mathbbm{R}})
, and the presence of extra states, i.e. those in

.

Going back to the action of
\Gamma
on complex quantum theory, the last aspect of the theory that has not been addressed is its dynamics. We start by considering the evolution of a state
\rho
under unitary dynamics
U
,
\rho\mapsto U\rho U^{\dagger}
. Because
\Gamma
is an algebra homomorphism, the action of a unitary transform decomposes into
\Gamma\left\{U\rho U^{\dagger}\right\}=\Gamma\left\{U\right\}\Gamma\left\{\rho\right\}\Gamma\left\{U\right\}^{T}
. We already know what the image of the set of valid states is, so what remains is to characterize the image of unitary matrices through
\Gamma
.

Proposition B.12
.

If
U\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
is unitary then
\Gamma\left\{U\right\}\in\mathbb{R}^{2n\times 2n}
is orthogonal & symplectic. This means the following:

U^{\dagger}={U}^{-1}\quad\iff\Gamma\left\{U\right\}^{T}=\Gamma\left\{U\right\}^{-1}\quad\&\quad\Gamma\left\{U\right\}J_{n}=J_{n}\Gamma\left\{U\right\}\>.

(S.B.47)

Such
\Gamma\left\{U\right\}
matrix will be referred to as orthosymplectic.

If
V\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
is antiunitary, meaning that
V=UK
with
K
the complex conjugation map
K\ket{\psi}=\ket{\overline{\psi}}

\forall\psi
then
\Gamma\left\{V\right\}\in\mathbb{R}^{2n\times 2n}
is orthogonal & antisymplectic. This means the following:

V=UK:\quad U^{\dagger}=U^{-1}\quad\&\quad\forall c\in{\mathbbm{C}}:\>Kc=\overline{c}\quad\iff\quad\Gamma\left\{V\right\}^{T}=\Gamma\left\{V\right\}^{-1}\;\&\;\Gamma\left\{V\right\}J_{n}=-J_{n}\Gamma\left\{V\right\}\>.

(S.B.48)

See Ref.
[
27
]
for a proof. (If needed, the definitions of orthogonal and symplectic matrices are collected in
Definition

E.1
.)

Remark B.13
.

As a side note, and building upon an observation of Myrheim
[
27
]
, it would be interesting to use RNQT as a means to have a linear representation of time-reversal in quantum theory. Remember that due to Wigner’s theorem: pure dynamics in quantum theory can only be unitary or antiunitary, with the antiunitary transformations corresponding to unitary transformations followed by a time-reversal. Collecting some results, we see the potential of the real quantum theory: antiunitary transformations have a real-linear representation in RNQT as antiorthosymplectic matrices (characterized by
Eq.

S.B.48
) and the (unormalised) time-reversed states are exactly those in
\mathsf{Sym}^{+}_{n}\left({\mathbbm{R}}\right)\setminus\mathsf{SY}_{n}({\mathbbm{R}})
(characterized by
Lemma

B.9
), since they are the image of quantum states that have undergone an antiunitary transform.

As an example of an antiunitary transformation representable in RNQT, take
\overline{M}=A-iB-iC-D
, one computes that this antiunitary is now but a
\frac{\pi}{2}
rotation of the
{\mathbbm{R}}^{2\times 2}
matrices used to represent the complex scalars,
\Gamma\left\{\overline{M}\right\}=Z_{n}\Gamma\left\{M\right\}Z_{n}
. Here
Z_{n}=Z\otimes{\mathbbm{1}}_{n}
where
Z
is the Pauli
Z
matrix.

With the discussion so far, one may wonder when the predictions of real quantum theory may diverge from its complex counterpart.
No big discrepancy has been found so far, except that the non-physical antiunitary transformation admits a linear representation. Still, this is also feasible in a mixed state representation of QTvia the transposition. So where do the theories diverge? The answer depends on how the combination rule is defined. Many authors in the 2000s started considering the Kronecker product
\otimes_{\textup{K}}
for combination in the real theory by formal analogy with QT. However, a mapping from QTto its real representation that preserves the Kronecker product will face an issue: this product is defined with respect to the base field. While both Kronecker products are formally similar, passing from the definition over the complex
\otimes_{\textup{K}}:{\mathbbm{C}}^{m\times n}\times{\mathbbm{C}}^{q\times p}\rightarrow{\mathbbm{C}}^{mp\times nq}
to the one over the reals
\otimes_{\textup{K}}:{\mathbbm{R}}^{m^{\prime}\times n^{\prime}}\times{\mathbbm{R}}^{q^{\prime}\times p^{\prime}}\rightarrow{\mathbbm{R}}^{m^{\prime}p^{\prime}\times n^{\prime}q^{\prime}}
is a non-trivial assumption, and even when enlarging the dimensions
m^{\prime},p^{\prime},n^{\prime},q^{\prime}
of the real counterpart, these two products are as dissimilar as a Laurent series is to a Taylor series.

We have seen already that trying to map the scalars in
{\mathbbm{C}}
to those in
{\mathbbm{R}}
result in loosing structure; one should instead map the base field
{\mathbbm{C}}
to its representation in
{\mathbbm{R}}^{2\times 2}
, which is the real span of
\{{\mathbbm{1}},J\}
and look for a combination rule that is bilinear with respect to this representation.
Yet, we have also seen above that ignoring this mathematical subtlety leads to no issue when restricting oneself to Hermitian operators: the inner products are preserved under
\Gamma
and thus there can be no discrepancies between the probability distributions obtained from single-partite quantum systems described by QTand by RNQT. In the next section, we will investigate whether this is still the case for multipartite systems.

B.3
Positivity-preserving mapping on two subsystems

So far, the theory relied on representing
{\mathbbm{C}}
as matrices over the reals, with the map
\Gamma_{n}
applying the representation on the complex matrices in
{\mathbbm{C}}^{n\times n}
to obtain real matrices in
{\mathbbm{R}}^{2n\times 2n}
. Defining the action of the mapping over the combination of multiple subsystems then amounts to taking the tensor product of representations.
But it is well-known that the tensor product of two representations is rarely an irreducible representation. The approach taken in this section can then be seen as a Clebsch-Gordon procedure: we will try to build an irreducible representation out of the one provided by applying the Kronecker product as a naive representation of the tensor product.

We start with the bipartite case. In terms of isomorphisms of spaces, the issue can be phrased in symbols as

\begin{array}[]{lclcllrclcl}{\mathbbm{C}}^{n\times n}&\otimes&{\mathbbm{C}}^{m\times m}&\cong&({\mathbbm{C}}&\otimes&{\mathbbm{R}}^{n\times n})&\otimes&({\mathbbm{C}}&\otimes&{\mathbbm{R}}^{m\times m})\\
\downarrow\Gamma_{n}&&\downarrow\Gamma_{m}&&\hphantom{(}\downarrow\Gamma_{1}&&&&\hphantom{(}\downarrow\Gamma_{1}&&\\
{\mathbbm{R}}^{2n\times 2n}&\otimes&{\mathbbm{R}}^{2m\times 2m}&\cong&({\mathbbm{R}}^{2\times 2}&\otimes&{\mathbbm{R}}^{n\times n})&\otimes&({\mathbbm{R}}^{2\times 2}&\otimes&{\mathbbm{R}}^{m\times m})\\
&&&\cong&{\mathbbm{C}}&\otimes&{\mathbbm{R}}^{mn\times mn}&&&&\\
&&&&\downarrow\Gamma_{1\times 1}&&&&&&\\
&&&\cong&{\mathbbm{R}}^{4\times 4}&\otimes&{\mathbbm{R}}^{mn\times mn}&&&&\\
&&&\cong&{\mathbbm{C}}^{mn\times mn}&&&&&&\\
&&&&\downarrow\Gamma_{n\times m}&&&&&&\\
&&&\cong&{\mathbbm{R}}^{4mn\times 4mn}&&&&&&\end{array}\quad.

(S.B.49)

And so we are trying to find a way to compose
\Gamma_{n}
with
\Gamma_{m}
such that it returns some map
\Gamma_{n\times m}
derived from
\Gamma_{n}\otimes\Gamma_{m}
and which provides an irreducible representation of the underlying complex quantum theory.

There is a way to directly see that the Kronecker product of two representations will not be irreducible: since the Kronecker product
\otimes_{\textup{K}}
is a bilinear map over different fields (
{\mathbbm{C}}
and
{\mathbbm{R}}
), there is a phase invariance that will be problematic to pass from one representation to the other. That is, the map
\Gamma_{n}\otimes_{\textup{K}}\Gamma_{m}
will have trouble preserving the equality
M_{A}\otimes_{\textup{K}}M_{B}=(e^{i\theta}M_{A})\otimes(e^{-i\theta}M_{B})
for two arbitrary matrices
M_{A}\in{\mathbbm{C}}^{n\times n}
and
M_{B}\in{\mathbbm{C}}^{m\times m}
since it is not complex-bilinear.
Compare
\Gamma_{n}\left\{M_{A}\right\}\otimes_{\textup{K}}\Gamma_{m}\left\{M_{B}\right\}
to
\Gamma_{n}\left\{e^{i\theta}M_{A}\right\}\otimes_{\textup{K}}\Gamma_{m}\left\{e^{-i\theta}M_{B}\right\}
; from the algebra, these two expressions are equivalent when
\Gamma_{n}\left\{e^{i\theta}Id_{n}\right\}\otimes_{\textup{K}}\Gamma_{m}\left\{e^{-i\theta}Id_{m}\right\}=I_{n}\otimes_{\textup{K}}I_{m}
, i.e., when
\theta
is a multiple of
\pi
as
\Gamma_{n}\left\{e^{i\theta}\>Id_{n}\right\}\otimes_{\textup{K}}\Gamma_{m}\left\{e^{-i\theta}\>Id_{m}\right\}=\Gamma_{n}\left\{{\mathbbm{1}}_{n}\right\}\otimes_{\textup{K}}\Gamma_{m}\left\{{\mathbbm{1}}_{m}\right\}
in that case.
However we see that the real-linearity of
\Gamma
induces an issue:
\Gamma_{n}\otimes_{\textup{K}}\Gamma_{m}
cannot be an isomorphism as, for example, when
\theta=\pi/2
one has
Id_{n}\otimes_{\textup{K}}Id_{m}=-(i\>Id_{n})\otimes_{\textup{K}}(i\>Id_{m})
but
\Gamma_{n}\left\{Id_{n}\right\}\otimes_{\textup{K}}\Gamma_{m}\left\{Id_{m}\right\}\neq-\Gamma_{n}\left\{(i\>Id_{n})\right\}\otimes_{\textup{K}}\Gamma_{m}\left\{(i\>Id_{m})\right\}
since
I_{n}\otimes_{\textup{K}}I_{m}\neq-J_{n}\otimes_{\textup{K}}J_{m}
.
Even worse, it cannot be an isometry, as it fails to preserve traces; in
I_{n}\otimes_{\textup{K}}I_{m}\neq-J_{n}\otimes_{\textup{K}}J_{m}
, the left-hand side is traceless while the right-hand side has a trace of
4nm
, for instance.
Actually, since
\text{tr}\left[J_{n}\Gamma_{n}\left\{M_{A}\right\}\right]=0
for any Hermitian matrix, the equality
\text{tr}\left[M_{A}\otimes_{\textup{K}}M_{B}\right]=-\text{tr}\left[iM_{A}\otimes_{\textup{K}}iM_{B}\right]
fails to be reproduced through
\Gamma_{n}\otimes_{\textup{K}}\Gamma_{m}
:
\text{tr}\left[\Gamma_{n}\left\{M_{A}\right\}\otimes_{\textup{K}}\Gamma_{m}\left\{M_{B}\right\}\right]=\text{tr}\left[\Gamma_{n}\left\{iM_{A}\right\}\otimes_{\textup{K}}\Gamma_{m}\left\{iM_{B}\right\}\right]
would imply
\text{tr}\left[M_{A}\right]\text{tr}\left[M_{B}\right]=0
for any pair of Hermitian matrices, which cannot be true.

Generalizing for any matrices
M_{A},N_{A}\in{\mathbbm{C}}^{n\times n}
and
M_{B},N_{B}\in{\mathbbm{C}}^{m\times m}
, one has

0=\text{tr}\left[(\Gamma\left\{M_{A}\right\}\otimes_{\textup{K}}\Gamma\left\{M_{B}\right\})^{\dagger}(\Gamma\left\{iN_{1}\right\}\otimes_{\textup{K}}\Gamma\left\{iN_{2}\right\})\right]\neq-\text{tr}\left[(M_{A}\otimes_{\textup{K}}M_{B})^{\dagger}(N_{1}\otimes_{\textup{K}}N_{2})\right]\>.

(S.B.50)

The underlying reason behind this issue is the following: trying to define
\Gamma_{n\times m}
using the Kronecker product makes it so that the Kronecker product between the two copies of the representation of
{\mathbbm{C}}
in
{\mathbbm{R}}^{2\times 2}
yields a representation of
{\mathbbm{C}}
in
{\mathbbm{R}}^{4\times 4}
that is reducible; it has induced a two-fold degeneracy
1\rightarrow\{I_{n}\otimes_{\textup{K}}I_{m},-J_{n}\otimes_{\textup{K}}J_{m}\}
and
i\rightarrow\{J_{n}\otimes_{\textup{K}}I_{m},I_{n}\otimes_{\textup{K}}J_{m}\}
under which
\Gamma\left\{\cdot\right\}\otimes_{\textup{K}}\Gamma\left\{\cdot\right\}
and
-\Gamma\left\{i\cdot\right\}\otimes_{\textup{K}}\Gamma\left\{i\cdot\right\}
provide the two orthogonal representations.

Rather, we would prefer a map
\Gamma_{n\times m}
which directly sends the two spaces to be composed into the irreducible
4nm
-dimensional representation
1\rightarrow I_{n\times m}
and
i\rightarrow J_{n\times m}
. To do so, the standard procedure is to mix over the irreps:

\Gamma\left\{M_{A}\right\}\otimes_{\textup{R}}\Gamma\left\{M_{B}\right\}:=\frac{1}{2}\left[\Gamma\left\{M_{1}\right\}\otimes_{\textup{K}}\Gamma\left\{M_{2}\right\}-\Gamma\left\{iM_{1}\right\}\otimes_{\textup{K}}\Gamma\left\{iM_{2}\right\}\right]\>.

(S.B.51)

As we will see, this procedure solves every representation-induced problem. But to grasp its content more easily, one needs to do some reshuffling as well as take a detour into stabilizer state formalism.

The first thing we will do is to reshuffle the tensor factors ordering for easier-to-read equations. In
Eq.

S.B.51
above, it is assumed that the underlying space of matrices factors as
{\mathbbm{R}}^{4n\times 4n}={\mathbbm{R}}^{2\times 2}_{A}\otimes{\mathbbm{R}}^{n\times n}_{A}\otimes{\mathbbm{R}}^{2\times 2}_{B}\otimes{\mathbbm{R}}^{m\times m}_{B}
, where we use subscripts
A
and
B
to label the relevant spaces. Until the end of this appendix, we use the convention of putting all the ‘scalar’ spaces on the left while retaining the parties’ ordering. For the above example this means putting the two
{\mathbbm{R}}^{2\times 2}
spaces where the complex numbers are represented on the left of the factorization,
{\mathbbm{R}}^{4n\times 4n}={\mathbbm{R}}^{2\times 2}_{A}\otimes{\mathbbm{R}}^{2\times 2}_{B}\otimes{\mathbbm{R}}^{n\times n}_{A}\otimes{\mathbbm{R}}^{m\times m}_{B}
, so swapping the second and third factor.

We will need the following
4
-dimensional representation of
\{1,i\}
in
{\mathbbm{R}}^{4\times 4}
:

\displaystyle\overline{I}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}}:=\frac{1}{2}\left({\mathbbm{1}}\otimes_{\textup{K}}{\mathbbm{1}}-J\otimes_{\textup{K}}J\right)\>;

(S.B.52a)

\displaystyle\overline{J}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}}:=\frac{1}{2}\left({\mathbbm{1}}\otimes_{\textup{K}}J+J\otimes_{\textup{K}}{\mathbbm{1}}\right)\>.

(S.B.52b)

As well as the expression of the real and imaginary parts of a Kronecker product, recall that

\displaystyle\mathrm{Re}\left(M_{A}\otimes_{\textup{K}}M_{B}\right)=\mathrm{Re}\left(M_{A}\right)\otimes_{\textup{K}}\mathrm{Re}\left(M_{B}\right)-\mathrm{Im}\left(M_{A}\right)\otimes_{\textup{K}}\mathrm{Im}\left(M_{B}\right)\>;

(S.B.53a)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Im}\left(M_{A}\otimes_{\textup{K}}M_{B}\right)=\mathrm{Re}\left(M_{A}\right)\otimes_{\textup{K}}\mathrm{Im}\left(M_{B}\right)+\mathrm{Im}\left(M_{A}\right)\otimes_{\textup{K}}\mathrm{Re}\left(M_{B}\right)\>.

(S.B.53b)

Using these four substitutions, a long but straightforward computation yields

\Gamma\left\{M_{A}\right\}\otimes_{\textup{R}}\Gamma\left\{M_{B}\right\}=\overline{I}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}}\otimes_{\textup{K}}\mathrm{Re}\left(M_{A}\otimes_{\textup{K}}M_{B}\right)+\overline{J}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}}\otimes_{\textup{K}}\mathrm{Im}\left(M_{A}\otimes_{\textup{K}}M_{B}\right)\>.

(S.B.54)

One formally recovers the formula
Eq.

S.B.32
in the above! There is a reason for that. Of course, the pair
\{\overline{I}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}},\overline{J}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}}\}
is indeed a representation of
\{1,i\}
in
{\mathbbm{R}}^{4\times 4}
as it can be checked that they obey the proper multiplication rules,
\overline{I}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}}=\big(\overline{I}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}}\big)^{2}=-\big(\overline{J}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}}\big)^{2}
and
\overline{J}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}}=\overline{I}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}}\overline{J}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}}=\overline{J}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}}\overline{I}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}}
, while the transpose plays the role of conjugation:
\big(\overline{I}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}}\big)^{T}=\overline{I}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}}
and
\big(\overline{J}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}}\big)^{T}=-\overline{J}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}}
, and that they are orthogonal to each other,
\text{tr}\left[\big(\overline{I}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}}\big)^{T}\cdot\overline{J}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}}\right]=0
.
But what is useful with this representation is that it handles the local application of smaller-dimensional representations properly:

(I\otimes_{\textup{K}}J)\overline{I}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}}=\overline{I}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}}(I\otimes_{\textup{K}}J)=(J\otimes_{\textup{K}}I)\overline{I}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}}=\overline{I}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}}(J\otimes_{\textup{K}}I)\ =\overline{J}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}}\overline{I}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}}=\overline{J}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}}\>.

(S.B.55)

This property is what makes these representations useful in stabilizer state formalism. For our purpose, it makes the
\otimes_{\textup{R}}
behave properly with respect to the phase freedom of the underlying complex quantum theory.
To prove it, one should first show that the
\otimes_{\textup{R}}
product is
absorbing
the Kronecker product in the following sense:

Lemma B.14
.

Let
M_{A}\in{\mathbbm{C}}^{n\times n}
and
M_{B}\in{\mathbbm{C}}^{m\times m}
be matrices, let
I_{d}=I\otimes_{\textup{K}}Id_{d}
where
I
is the 2-by-2 identity matrix and
Id_{d}
its
d
-by-
d
counterpart, and let
\Gamma_{n}
and
\Gamma_{m}
as defined above. Then, the following relations hold

\displaystyle\Gamma_{n}\left\{M_{A}\right\}\otimes_{\textup{R}}\Gamma_{m}\left\{M_{B}\right\}=\left(I_{n}\otimes_{\textup{R}}I_{m}\right)\left(\Gamma_{n}\left\{M_{A}\right\}\otimes_{\textup{K}}\Gamma_{m}\left\{M_{B}\right\}\right)

(S.B.56a)

\displaystyle\Gamma_{n}\left\{M_{A}\right\}\otimes_{\textup{R}}\Gamma_{m}\left\{M_{B}\right\}=\left(\Gamma_{n}\left\{M_{A}\right\}\otimes_{\textup{K}}\Gamma_{m}\left\{M_{B}\right\}\right)\left(I_{n}\otimes_{\textup{R}}I_{m}\right)

(S.B.56b)

Proof.

Starting from the definition of the left-hand side,

\displaystyle\Gamma_{n}\left\{M_{A}\right\}\otimes_{\textup{R}}\Gamma_{m}\left\{M_{B}\right\}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left(\Gamma_{n}\left\{M_{A}\right\}\otimes_{\textup{K}}\Gamma_{m}\left\{M_{B}\right\}-\Gamma_{n}\left\{iM_{A}\right\}\otimes_{\textup{K}}\Gamma_{m}\left\{iM_{B}\right\}\right)

(S.B.57)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left(\Gamma_{n}\left\{M_{A}\right\}\otimes_{\textup{K}}\Gamma_{m}\left\{M_{B}\right\}-(J_{n}\Gamma_{n}\left\{M_{A}\right\})\otimes_{\textup{K}}(J_{m}\Gamma_{m}\left\{M_{B}\right\})\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left(I_{n}\otimes_{\textup{K}}I_{m}-J_{n}\otimes_{\textup{K}}J_{m}\right)(\Gamma_{n}\left\{M_{A}\right\}\otimes_{\textup{K}}\Gamma_{m}\left\{M_{B}\right\})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left(({\mathbbm{1}}\otimes_{\textup{K}}{\mathbbm{1}}-J\otimes_{\textup{K}}J)\otimes_{\textup{K}}({\mathbbm{1}}_{n}\otimes_{\textup{K}}{\mathbbm{1}}_{m})\right)(\Gamma_{n}\left\{M_{A}\right\}\otimes_{\textup{K}}\Gamma_{m}\left\{M_{B}\right\})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\overline{I}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}}\otimes_{\textup{K}}({\mathbbm{1}}_{n}\otimes_{\textup{K}}{\mathbbm{1}}_{m})\right)(\Gamma_{n}\left\{M_{A}\right\}\otimes_{\textup{K}}\Gamma_{m}\left\{M_{B}\right\})

\displaystyle\overset{\eqref{eq:2mix_Re_Im}}{=}\left(I_{n}\otimes_{\textup{R}}I_{m}\right)(\Gamma_{n}\left\{M_{A}\right\}\otimes_{\textup{K}}\Gamma_{m}\left\{M_{B}\right\})\>.

In the above, the refactorisation announced earlier in the appendix has been performed when going to the third line, and so equalities should be understood up to the insertion of an ad hoc swap operator. The second statement follows directly by the same reasoning, and using
\Gamma_{n}\left\{iM_{A}\right\}=\Gamma_{n}\left\{M_{A}i\right\}=\Gamma_{n}\left\{M_{A}\right\}J_{n}
.
∎

Notice that the previous proof was made without needing the underlying complex matrix, only that the matrices were commuting with
J_{n}\otimes_{\textup{K}}J_{m}
. Hence, this result holds for any pair of matrices in
{\mathbbm{R}}^{2n\times 2n}\times{\mathbbm{R}}^{2m\times 2m}
whose Kronecker product commutes with
J_{n}\otimes_{\textup{K}}J_{m}
. We will get back to this in the next section and use it to extend the definition of the
\otimes_{\textup{R}}
combination rule to all real matrices. More generally, any result proven for the image of some complex matrix through
\Gamma
will also hold for an arbitrary real matrix as long as it commutes with
J
.

Meanwhile,
Lemma

B.14
implies that real-bilinearity and the mixed product property are inherited from the Kronecker product.

Corollary B.15
.

The combination rule
\otimes_{\textup{R}}
as defined by
Eq.

S.B.51
is a real bilinear map obeying the mixed product property. That is,

\forall\widetilde{H},\widetilde{K}\in\mathsf{SY}_{n}({\mathbbm{R}}),\forall\widetilde{L},\widetilde{M}\in\mathsf{SY}_{m}({\mathbbm{R}})
and
\forall a,b\in{\mathbbm{R}}
, the following holds:

\displaystyle(a\widetilde{H}+b\widetilde{K})\otimes_{\textup{R}}\widetilde{L}

\displaystyle=a(\widetilde{H}\otimes_{\textup{R}}\widetilde{L})+b(\widetilde{K}\otimes_{\textup{R}}\widetilde{L})\>;

(S.B.58a)

\displaystyle\widetilde{H}\otimes_{\textup{R}}(a\widetilde{L}+b\widetilde{M})

\displaystyle=a(\widetilde{H}\otimes_{\textup{R}}\widetilde{L})+b(\widetilde{H}\otimes_{\textup{R}}\widetilde{M})\>;

(S.B.58b)

\displaystyle(\widetilde{H}\widetilde{K})\otimes_{\textup{R}}(\widetilde{L}\widetilde{M})

\displaystyle=(\widetilde{H}\otimes_{\textup{R}}\widetilde{L})(\widetilde{K}\otimes_{\textup{R}}\widetilde{M})\>;

(S.B.58c)

Proof.

As mentionned, one simply uses
Lemma

B.14
to factor out the
I_{n}\otimes_{\textup{K}}I_{m}
factor, uses the properties of the Kronecker product
\otimes_{\textup{K}}
, and then reapplies the factor. For instance, the mixed-produc rules is infered by

\displaystyle(\widetilde{H}\widetilde{K})\otimes_{\textup{R}}(\widetilde{L}\widetilde{M})

\displaystyle=(I_{n}\otimes_{\textup{R}}I_{m})[(\widetilde{H}\widetilde{K})\otimes_{\textup{K}}(\widetilde{L}\widetilde{M})]

(S.B.59)

\displaystyle=(I_{n}\otimes_{\textup{R}}I_{m})[(\widetilde{H}\otimes_{\textup{K}}\widetilde{L})(\widetilde{K}\otimes_{\textup{K}}\widetilde{M})]

\displaystyle=(I_{n}\otimes_{\textup{R}}I_{m})(I_{n}\otimes_{\textup{R}}I_{m})[(\widetilde{H}\otimes_{\textup{K}}\widetilde{L})(\widetilde{K}\otimes_{\textup{K}}\widetilde{M})]

\displaystyle=(I_{n}\otimes_{\textup{R}}I_{m})(\widetilde{H}\otimes_{\textup{K}}\widetilde{L})(I_{n}\otimes_{\textup{R}}I_{m})(\widetilde{K}\otimes_{\textup{K}}\widetilde{M})

\displaystyle=(\widetilde{H}\otimes_{\textup{R}}\widetilde{L})(\widetilde{K}\otimes_{\textup{R}}\widetilde{M})\>,

where, we use the mixed product property for
\otimes_{\textup{K}}
at the second line, then the idempotency of
(I_{n}\otimes_{\textup{R}}I_{m})
at the third, followed by applications of
Lemma

B.14
until we reached the final line.
∎

We are now in a position to prove that the new bilinear combination rule properly accounts for the phase freedom.

Proposition B.16
.

The real bilinearity of
\otimes_{\textup{R}}
properly renders the complex bilinearity of the complex Kronecker product
\otimes_{\textup{K}}
through the mapping
\Gamma
in the following sense:
\forall\theta\in{\mathbbm{R}},M\in{\mathbbm{C}}^{n\times n},N\in{\mathbbm{C}}^{m\times m}
,

\begin{gathered}\Gamma_{n}\left\{e^{i\theta}M\right\}\otimes_{\textup{R}}\Gamma_{m}\left\{N\right\}=\Gamma_{n}\left\{M\right\}\otimes_{\textup{R}}\Gamma_{m}\left\{e^{i\theta}N\right\}\\
=\cos{\theta}(\Gamma_{n}\left\{M\right\}\otimes_{\textup{R}}\Gamma_{m}\left\{N\right\})+\sin{\theta}(\Gamma_{n}\left\{iM\right\}\otimes_{\textup{R}}\Gamma_{m}\left\{N\right\})\>.\end{gathered}

(S.B.60)

In particular, the complex phase freedom of
\otimes_{\textup{K}}
is preserved through
\Gamma
:

\Gamma_{n}\left\{M\right\}\otimes_{\textup{R}}\Gamma_{m}\left\{N\right\}=\Gamma_{n}\left\{e^{i\theta}M\right\}\otimes_{\textup{R}}\Gamma_{m}\left\{e^{-i\theta}N\right\}

(S.B.61)

Proof.

The equivalence between the first and last formulae,
\Gamma_{n}\left\{e^{i\theta}M\right\}\otimes_{\textup{R}}\Gamma_{m}\left\{N\right\}=\cos{\theta}(\Gamma_{n}\left\{M\right\}\otimes_{\textup{R}}\Gamma_{m}\left\{N\right\})+\sin{\theta}(\Gamma_{n}\left\{iM\right\}\otimes_{\textup{R}}\Gamma_{m}\left\{N\right\})
is direct by bilinearity. To show equivalence with the second formula, we use
Lemma

B.14
as well as
\Gamma_{n}
being an algebra homomorphism, this gives
\Gamma_{n}\left\{e^{i\theta}M\right\}\otimes_{\textup{R}}\Gamma_{m}\left\{N\right\}=(\Gamma_{n}\left\{e^{i\theta}\>{\mathbbm{1}}_{n}\right\}\otimes_{\textup{R}}\Gamma_{m}\left\{{\mathbbm{1}}_{m}\right\})(\Gamma_{n}\left\{M\right\}\otimes_{\textup{K}}\Gamma_{m}\left\{N\right\})
. It remains to show that the phase can pass on the right-hand side of the
\otimes_{\textup{R}}
in
(\Gamma_{n}\left\{e^{i\theta}\>{\mathbbm{1}}_{n}\right\}\otimes_{\textup{R}}\Gamma_{m}\left\{{\mathbbm{1}}_{m}\right\})
. By successively using
Eq.

S.B.54
and
Eq.

S.B.55
we have

\begin{aligned} \Gamma_{n}\left\{e^{i\theta}\>{\mathbbm{1}}_{n}\right\}&\otimes_{\textup{R}}\Gamma_{m}\left\{\>{\mathbbm{1}}_{m}\right\}\\
=&\cos{\theta}(\Gamma_{n}\left\{{\mathbbm{1}}_{n}\right\}\otimes_{\textup{R}}\Gamma_{m}\left\{{\mathbbm{1}}_{m}\right\})+\sin{\theta}({J_{n}}\Gamma_{n}\left\{{\mathbbm{1}}_{n}\right\}\otimes_{\textup{R}}\Gamma_{m}\left\{{\mathbbm{1}}_{m}\right\})\\
=&\cos{\theta}(\overline{I}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}}\otimes_{\textup{K}}({\mathbbm{1}}_{n}\otimes_{\textup{K}}{\mathbbm{1}}_{m}))+\sin{\theta}({J_{n}}\otimes_{\textup{K}}I_{m})(\overline{I}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}}\otimes_{\textup{K}}({\mathbbm{1}}_{n}\otimes_{\textup{K}}{\mathbbm{1}}_{m}))\\
=&\cos{\theta}(\overline{I}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}}\otimes_{\textup{K}}({\mathbbm{1}}_{n}\otimes_{\textup{K}}{\mathbbm{1}}_{m}))+\sin{\theta}(I_{n}\otimes_{\textup{K}}J_{m})(\overline{I}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}}\otimes_{\textup{K}}({\mathbbm{1}}_{n}\otimes_{\textup{K}}{\mathbbm{1}}_{m}))\\
=&\cos{\theta}(\Gamma_{n}\left\{{\mathbbm{1}}_{n}\right\}\otimes_{\textup{R}}\Gamma_{m}\left\{{\mathbbm{1}}_{m}\right\})+\sin{\theta}(\Gamma_{n}\left\{{\mathbbm{1}}_{n}\right\}\otimes_{\textup{R}}J_{m}\Gamma_{m}\left\{{\mathbbm{1}}_{m}\right\})\\
&=\Gamma_{n}\left\{{\mathbbm{1}}_{n}\right\}\otimes_{\textup{R}}\Gamma_{m}\left\{e^{i\theta}{\mathbbm{1}}_{m}\right\}\end{aligned}\quad.

(S.B.62)

The rest follows.
∎

So the
\otimes_{\textup{R}}
is free of the phase issue faced by the
\otimes_{\textup{K}}
. Combining the mixed product property with
Lemma

B.14
shows that the
\otimes_{\textup{R}}
product will ‘absorb’ any Kronecker product it encounters, so it is replacing it in every formula once it is introduced.
Nonetheless, it is doing it in a fashion that does not change the traces of Hermitian matrices,

\text{tr}\left[\Gamma_{n}\left\{M_{A}\right\}\otimes_{\textup{R}}\Gamma_{m}\left\{M_{B}\right\}\right]=\text{tr}\left[\Gamma_{n}\left\{M_{A}\right\}\otimes_{\textup{K}}\Gamma_{m}\left\{M_{B}\right\}\right]=2^{2}\text{tr}\left[M_{A}\right]\text{tr}\left[M_{B}\right]\>,

(S.B.63)

as can be computed using the definition (
S.B.51
).

But its robustness and fixing the phase issue are only qualitative reasons why
\otimes_{\textup{R}}
is a better combination rule than
\otimes_{\textup{K}}
. These are model-dependent arguments and do not tell us much about the achievable distributions. There is nonetheless a ‘better’ choice of combination rule.

As we mentioned in the previous section, on single-partite systems, the
\Gamma
map is a sensible choice of mapping between complex and real theories as it injectively maps the complex states and effects spaces into their real counterparts.
In the following, we show that it is still reasonable in light of postulate (iv). That is, we show that the composite state spaces of complex quantum theory is embedded in those of the real theory through
local application
of the map
\Gamma
.

The first step to show this lies in the property
(
S.B.29
)
: the real span of a composite complex state space can be spanned by its tensor products. It will not be the case for its image as
\Gamma_{nm}\left\{\mathsf{Herm}_{nm}\left({\mathbbm{C}}\right)\right\}\subset{\mathbbm{R}}^{2nm\times 2nm}
whereas
\Gamma_{n}\left\{\mathsf{Herm}_{n}\left({\mathbbm{C}}\right)\right\}\otimes_{\textup{K}}\Gamma_{m}\left\{\mathsf{Herm}_{m}\left({\mathbbm{C}}\right)\right\}\subseteq{\mathbbm{R}}^{4nm\times 4nm}
for
\otimes=\{\otimes_{\textup{K}},\otimes_{\textup{R}}\}

Supplementary E
Extending the alternative representations of the tensor product to the full space

An objection that may be raised against what is done in the previous appendix as well as in the main text is that the image through the map
\Gamma_{n}
of the Hermitian matrices
\mathsf{Herm}_{n}\left({\mathbbm{C}}\right)
is only a proper subspace of the real symmetric matrices, namely
\mathsf{SY}_{n}({\mathbbm{R}})
. While we showed it to be orthogonal to the image of the Hermitian matrices, one is still legitimate in wondering
what is this other part?

Another objection is that the ‘real quantum theory’ we present in this paper is but a representation of the complex quantum theory. One is also legitimate in requiring that we extend the theory to be consistent with the axioms (i)-(iv)
R
of the real theory, rather than those of complex quantum theory. That is, that the sets of (unnormalized) states and effects under consideration are the full space of real positive semi-definite matrices, instead of the image of the complex positive semi-definite matrices within it.

In this appendix, we address both these lines of concern by showing how to extend the definition of the
\otimes_{\textup{R}}
and
\otimes_{\textup{r}}
combination rules so that our real quantum theory can be taken as a stand-alone theory defined over real Hilbert spaces.
More precisely, we will introduce a generalization of
Definition

2
so as to extend it from a combination rule restricted on spaces of special symmetric matrices (
\otimes_{\textup{r}}:\mathsf{SY}_{d_{1}}({\mathbbm{R}})\times\mathsf{SY}_{d_{2}}({\mathbbm{R}})\rightarrow\mathsf{SY}_{d_{1}d_{2}}({\mathbbm{R}})
) to one between any pair of even-dimensional real matrices (
\otimes_{\textup{r}}:{\mathbbm{R}}^{2d_{1}\times 2d_{1}}\times{\mathbbm{R}}^{2d_{2}\times 2d_{2}}\rightarrow{\mathbbm{R}}^{2d_{1}d_{2}\times 2d_{1}d_{2}}
).

E.1
Restatement of the problem, preliminary remarks on dimensionality, and the notion of tensor product of sets

In the previous appendix, we presented the map
\Gamma
as acting on the ‘complex scalar’ multiplying a real matrix to turn it into a complex matrix, i.e. as if
\Gamma
primarily acts on the
{\mathbbm{C}}
part of
{\mathbbm{C}}^{n\times n}\cong{\mathbbm{C}}\otimes{\mathbbm{R}}^{n\times n}
. This mapping then consists in turning the complex unit into a matrix through the well-known isomorphism
\{1,i\}\mapsto\{{\mathbbm{1}}{},J\}
in the decomposition
{\mathbbm{C}}^{n\times n}\ni M=1\times\mathrm{Re}\left(M\right)+i\times\mathrm{Im}\left(M\right)\mapsto{\mathbbm{1}}\otimes_{\textup{K}}\mathrm{Re}\left(M\right)+J\otimes_{\textup{K}}\mathrm{Im}\left(M\right)=\Gamma(M)\in{\mathbbm{R}}^{2n\times 2n}
. This yielded a representation of complex matrices in which the image of the Hermitian matrices forms the real subspace of symmetric matrices
\mathsf{SY}_{n}({\mathbbm{R}})
characterized by
\mathsf{SY}_{n}({\mathbbm{R}})=\{\widetilde{H}\in{\mathbbm{R}}^{2n\times 2n}|\widetilde{H}={\mathbbm{1}}\otimes_{\textup{K}}A+J\otimes_{\textup{K}}B,A\in\mathsf{Sym}_{n}\left({\mathbbm{R}}\right),B\in\mathsf{ASym}_{n}\left({\mathbbm{R}}\right)\}
. In this space, the set of (complex) quantum states was proven to correspond to a trace-normalized slice of its positive cone
\mathsf{SY}_{n}({\mathbbm{R}})^{+}
.

The main question driving the narrative was then how to combine two local states into a global bipartite one?
As the Kronecker product
\otimes_{\textup{K}}
led to phase issues, we instead derived a first candidate to the combination rule,
\otimes_{\textup{R}}
, which is a real-bilinear map compatible with the inner product and that obeys the mixed product property. It was however not totally satisfactory as it was not a combination rule for the state space in this subspace, meaning that the elements obtained after using
\otimes_{\textup{R}}
are not contained in the corresponding state space, i.e.
\widetilde{H}\otimes_{\textup{R}}\widetilde{L}\notin\mathsf{SY}_{nm}({\mathbbm{R}})^{+}
,
\forall\widetilde{H}\in\mathsf{SY}_{n}({\mathbbm{R}})^{+},\forall\widetilde{L}\in\mathsf{SY}_{m}({\mathbbm{R}})^{+}
since
\widetilde{H}\otimes_{\textup{R}}\widetilde{L}\in{\mathbbm{R}}^{4nm\times 4nm}
whereas
\mathsf{SY}_{nm}({\mathbbm{R}})^{+}\subset{\mathbbm{R}}^{2nm\times 2nm}
.
Nonetheless, real-linear combinations of the matrices
\widetilde{H}\otimes_{\textup{R}}\widetilde{L}
still span a vector space isomorphic to
\mathsf{SY}_{nm}({\mathbbm{R}})
within
\mathsf{SY}_{2nm}({\mathbbm{R}})
. This is one of the reasons why the second combination rule,
\otimes_{\textup{r}}
, has been developed: it has the property
\mathsf{SY}_{n}({\mathbbm{R}})\otimes_{\textup{r}}\mathsf{SY}_{m}({\mathbbm{R}})=\mathsf{SY}_{nm}({\mathbbm{R}})
.

The question, then, is what happens when we look at the full state space of the real theory in which we embedded this representation of the complex quantum theory –
\mathsf{Sym}_{2nm}\left({\mathbbm{R}}\right)
, namely?

For a quantum theory defined over a complex Hilbert space of dimension
n
, we are dealing with not one but three relevant spaces to be considered for its real Hilbert space counterpart: 1) the space of special symmetric matrices
\mathsf{SY}_{n}({\mathbbm{R}})
; 2) the space of symmeric matrices
\mathsf{Sym}_{2n}\left({\mathbbm{R}}\right)
; and 3) the space of real matrices
{\mathbbm{R}}^{2n\times 2n}
. (It should be clear that these three spaces are real vector spaces contained within one another,
\mathsf{SY}_{n}({\mathbbm{R}})\subset\mathsf{Sym}_{2n}\left({\mathbbm{R}}\right)\subset{\mathbbm{R}}^{2n\times 2n}
.)
In terms of interpretation, the first is the vector space containing the representation of the state space of the complex quantum theory, the second is the vector space containing the cone of positive matrices therefore the full state and effect spaces of the real theory, and the third is simply the matrix space over which the representation of both theories are defined.

Now, given two copies of any of these spaces, one can wonder how to compose them into a global bipartite space.
If one required the tensor product to be represented by the Kronecker product regardless of the base field, there is a direct answer, as this bilinear mapping is defined for all real matrices. But while the Kronecker product
\otimes_{\textup{K}}
has a broad enough definition for it to be meaningfully defined on each of the three spaces, it is not the case for the combination rules
\otimes_{\textup{R}}
and
\otimes_{\textup{r}}
; one needs to extend their definitions to real matrix spaces.

A priori, an immediate problem lies in the special symmetric matrices being defined only over spaces of even-dimensional matrices. While it would certainly be interesting to discuss odd-dimensional matrix representations of
\{1,i\}
such that the representatives are orthogonal (w.r.t. the Frobenius inner product) to one another and that the representative of
i
is antisymmetric, one can instead ignore this issue altogether. Indeed, following the arguments put forward in Ref.
[
14
]
, as there is no way to witness the dimension of the underlying spaces, this should not matter. It suffices to prove that the theory is consistent in space of even-dimensional real matrices and, for our purposes, to further show that the correlations obtained by complex quantum theory are achievable in these spaces to “unfalsify quantum theory based on real numbers”; we nevertheless leave the liberty to the reader to conclude whether ‘Nature needs even dimensions’.

Another a priori issue can be quickly identified by looking at the dimension of these spaces. Take
\otimes_{\textup{r}}
since it is the ‘nicely’ behaved combination rule for the special symmetric matrices. One can first wonder if there is a way to extend the domain and codomain of
\otimes_{\textup{r}}
from
\mathsf{SY}_{n}({\mathbbm{R}})\times\mathsf{SY}_{m}({\mathbbm{R}})\rightarrow\mathsf{SY}_{nm}({\mathbbm{R}})
to
\mathsf{Sym}_{2n}\left({\mathbbm{R}}\right)\times\mathsf{Sym}_{2m}\left({\mathbbm{R}}\right)\rightarrow\mathsf{Sym}_{2mn}\left({\mathbbm{R}}\right)
.
We showed that for special symmetric matrices,
\mathrm{dim(}\mathsf{SY}_{n}({\mathbbm{R}})\otimes_{\textup{r}}\mathsf{SY}_{m}({\mathbbm{R}}))
=
\mathrm{dim}(\mathsf{SY}_{n}({\mathbbm{R}}))\mathrm{dim}(\mathsf{SY}_{m}({\mathbbm{R}}))=\mathrm{dim}(\mathsf{SY}_{nm}({\mathbbm{R}}))
. But if this property were to carry to the full space, any extension would obviously be a surjection as follows from a dimensionality argument: the codomain is a real vector space of dimension
nm(2nm+1)
while the domain is one of dimension
n(2n+1)\times m(2m+1)=nm(2nm+1)+nm(2nm+2n+2m)
.
Similarly, the extension to real matrices
{\mathbbm{R}}^{2n\times 2n}\times{\mathbbm{R}}^{2m\times 2m}\rightarrow{\mathbbm{R}}^{2mn\times 2mn}
would also be a surjection as the codomain is of dimension
4n^{2}m^{2}
while the domain is of dimension
16n^{2}m^{2}
.

Fortunately, we know that this property of the Kronecker product does not hold in general for the new products. For instance, we already showed that
\mathsf{SY}_{n}({\mathbbm{R}})\otimes_{\textup{R}}\mathsf{SY}_{m}({\mathbbm{R}})\simeq\mathsf{SY}_{nm}({\mathbbm{R}})
while being a subspace of
\mathsf{SY}_{2nm}({\mathbbm{R}})
. Hence, that
\mathrm{dim(}\mathsf{SY}_{n}({\mathbbm{R}})\otimes_{\textup{R}}\mathsf{SY}_{m}({\mathbbm{R}}))
=
\mathrm{dim}(\mathsf{SY}_{n}({\mathbbm{R}}))\mathrm{dim}(\mathsf{SY}_{m}({\mathbbm{R}}))\neq\mathrm{dim}(\mathsf{SY}_{2nm}({\mathbbm{R}}))
.
This is a symptom of the difference there exists between the 3 kinds of spaces listed above: they agree for complex quantum theory represented as positive semi-definite matrices combined via the Kronecker product. This is very convenient, but it fools us into forgetting to specify which spaces we are taking the tensor products of. In the real theory, this agreement is lifted, and the specification of exactly which spaces, algebras, cones, etc. are being joined through the tensor product becomes important. Concretely, with respect to the rules
\otimes_{\textup{K}},\otimes_{\textup{R}};\otimes_{\textup{r}}
, it will become important to identify for which space they connect together they happen to be a representation of the tensor product (if they happen to be one).

\displaystyle\mathrm{ASym}\left(\mathrm{Tetr}\left(M\right)\right)=I\otimes_{\textup{K}}D+J\otimes_{\textup{K}}C

\displaystyle\in\mathsf{ASY}_{n}({\mathbbm{R}})\>;

(S.E.112c)

\displaystyle\mathrm{ASym}\left(\mathrm{ATetr}\left(M\right)\right)=X\otimes_{\textup{K}}H+Z\otimes_{\textup{K}}G

\displaystyle\in\overline{\mathsf{ASY}}_{n}({\mathbbm{R}})\>.

(S.E.112d)

Proof.

The latter part (
S.E.112
) is direct to prove using the relations
\mathcal{T}(I)=\mathcal{J}(I)=I
,
\mathcal{T}(J)=\mathcal{J}(J)=-J
,
\mathcal{T}(X)=\mathcal{J}(X)=X
, and
\mathcal{T}(Z)=\mathcal{J}(Z)=-Z
.

The complete decomposition is obtained by realizing that the four projectors are pairwise orthogonal (using
Lemma

E.2
) and sum up to the identity projection:
\mathcal{I}=\mathrm{Sym}\circ\mathrm{Tetr}+\mathrm{Sym}\circ\mathrm{ATetr}+\mathrm{ASym}\circ\mathrm{Tetr}+\mathrm{ASym}\circ\mathrm{ATetr}
.
∎

E.3
Towards defining the extended combination rule

The particular form of the decomposition in
Proposition

E.4
was chosen because the Pauli matrices appear in it, the algebra of which is well-known and has a discrete group structure that we will leverage to build our tensor product. This connection with the representation of an implicit discrete group will be discussed in the subsection. For the time being, we now have enough background to justify the extended form of the
\otimes_{\textup{R}}
-combination rule and to add some more motivation why one should use it instead of the Kronecker product.

With it, we can make it clearer why the Kronecker product is not the appropriate tensor product when representing the complex matrices within the reals. There are two relevant decompositions of a real matrix: into its symmetric and antisymmetric parts, as the former will contain the positive cone, and into its tetradionic and antitetradionic parts, as the former will contain the image of the complex matrices since it is the part that commutes with the representation of
i
.

Focusing on the symmetric and the tetradionic parts, it directly follows from
M^{T}\otimes_{\textup{K}}N^{T}=(M\otimes_{\textup{K}}N)^{T}
that the Kronecker product of two symmetric matrices remains symmetric, i.e.,
\mathsf{Sym}_{2n}\left({\mathbbm{R}}\right)\otimes_{\textup{K}}\mathsf{Sym}_{2m}\left({\mathbbm{R}}\right)\subset\mathsf{Sym}_{4nm}\left({\mathbbm{R}}\right)
. Under the same logic, the Kronecker product of two tetradionic matrices is tetradionic as well, i.e.,
\mathsf{Tetr}_{n}\left({\mathbbm{R}}\right)\otimes_{\textup{K}}\mathsf{Tetr}_{m}\left({\mathbbm{R}}\right)\subset\mathsf{Tetr}_{2nm}\left({\mathbbm{R}}\right)
. However, the special symmetric matrices, i.e., those which are both tetradionic and symmetric, cannot keep both properties when taken in Kronecker products, i.e.,
\mathsf{SY}_{n}({\mathbbm{R}})\otimes_{\textup{K}}\mathsf{SY}_{m}({\mathbbm{R}})\subset\mathsf{SY}_{2nm}({\mathbbm{R}})
.

As we have proven in another section, going to the ad hoc representation of
\{1,i\}
in the tensor product space
{\mathbbm{R}}^{2n\times 2n}\otimes{\mathbbm{R}}^{2m\times 2m}
amounts to replacing the unit with the irreducible representation of the group identity
\overline{I}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}}\in{\mathbbm{R}}^{4\times 4}
extended by the Kronecker product with the units, i.e. when requiring the substitution
I_{n}\otimes_{\textup{K}}I_{m}={\mathbbm{1}}_{2n}\otimes_{\textup{K}}{\mathbbm{1}}_{2m}\rightarrow\overline{I}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}}_{nm}=\overline{I}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}}\otimes_{\textup{K}}{\mathbbm{1}}_{n}\otimes_{\textup{K}}{\mathbbm{1}}_{m}
. By
Lemma

B.14
, this procedure has the nice property that it replaces any Kronecker product with the other tensor product
\otimes_{\textup{R}}
. Indeed,
A\otimes_{\textup{K}}B=(I_{n}\otimes_{\textup{K}}I_{m})(A\otimes_{\textup{K}}B)\mapsto(I_{n}\otimes_{\textup{R}}I_{m})(A\otimes_{\textup{K}}B)\overset{\eqref{eq:2mix_absorbant}}{=}A\otimes_{\textup{R}}B
.

We now turn this property into a definition to extend the domain and codomain of
\otimes_{\textup{R}}
from
\mathsf{SY}_{n}({\mathbbm{R}})\times\mathsf{SY}_{m}({\mathbbm{R}})\rightarrow\mathsf{SY}_{nm}({\mathbbm{R}})
to
{\mathbbm{R}}^{2n\times 2n}\times{\mathbbm{R}}^{2m\times 2m}\rightarrow{\mathbbm{R}}^{4nm\times 4nm}
.
However, and in comparison to the
\mathsf{SY}_{n}({\mathbbm{R}})\times\mathsf{SY}_{m}({\mathbbm{R}})
case, the obtained elements are no longer automatically tetradionic, therefore they might not commute with
\overline{I}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}}_{nm}
. Hence, in the extended definition, the Kronecker products have to be multiplied by the representation of the identity element from each side. We arrive to the matrix
A\otimes_{\textup{R}}B\in{\mathbbm{R}}^{4nm\times 4nm}
defined as

A\otimes_{\textup{R}}B:=\overline{I}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}}_{nm}(A\otimes_{\textup{K}}B)\overline{I}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}}_{nm}\>,

(S.E.113)

where
A\in{\mathbbm{R}}^{2n\times 2n}
and
B\in{\mathbbm{R}}^{2m\times 2m}
be two matrices.
This definition amounts to projecting the Kronecker product into the
(2nm)^{2}
-dimensional subspace defined by the rank-
2nm
projector
\overline{I}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}}_{nm}
.

We posit this simple definition because it is the most economical way to extend our tensor product representation: we already showed that it will contain the irreducible
4
-dimensional representation of
\{1,i\}
and that it is the adequate tensor product for special symmetric matrices. In the extended space, we only need to show that it is a reasonable notion of a combination rule.
But to show this, it remains to generalize the definition for multiple parties and then to study its properties.
In order to do this, we need to make another short group-theoretic-detour to look at the group properties of the matrices appearing in the decomposition (
S.E.111
).

E.4
Irreducible representation of the dihedral group of order 8 on even-dimensional real matrices

To keep this appendix streamlined, the technical presentation of the relevant group theory and all the proofs for this section have been regrouped to a different appendix,
Supplementary

F
.

The group generated by
\{1,i\}
under multiplication and represented by the matrices
\{I,J\}
is
{\mathbbm{Z}}_{4}
, the cyclic group of order four. In the decomposition (
S.E.111
), these two matrices appear alongside two other matrices
X,Z
, so we take the set
\{I,J,X,Z\}
as the representation of a bigger group that contains
{\mathbbm{Z}}_{4}
as a subgroup.

Since these four matrices are none other than the ‘real version’ of the Pauli matrices obtained after the substitution
Y\mapsto J=-iY
, the bigger group is the finite group generated by these matrices under multiplication. This group is
D_{8}={\mathbbm{Z}}_{2}\rtimes{\mathbbm{Z}}_{4}
, the diehedral group of order 8, and
\{I,J,X,Z\}
provides an irreducible representation of it (see
proposition

F.2
).

Going into the tensor product space,
{\mathbbm{R}}^{2\times 2}\otimes{\mathbbm{R}}^{2\times 2}\cong{\mathbbm{R}}^{4\times 4}
, taking the Kronecker products of two representations of
D_{8}
in
{\mathbbm{R}}^{2\times 2}
,
\{\pm I,\pm J,\pm X,\pm Z\}\otimes_{\textup{K}}\{\pm I,\pm J,\pm X,\pm Z\}
yields a set of 32 elements which certainly contain more than one representation of
D_{8}
in
{\mathbbm{R}}^{4\times 4}
. In contrast, the
\otimes_{\textup{R}}
-composition of the two representations combines the 32 elements into (after some computations) the following five distinct elements (the
\pm
signs have been removed for conciseness).

\displaystyle\overline{I}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}}:=

\displaystyle I\otimes_{\textup{R}}I=-J\otimes_{\textup{R}}J

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(I\otimes_{\textup{K}}I-J\otimes_{\textup{K}}J)\>;

(S.E.114a)

\displaystyle\overline{J}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}}:=

\displaystyle J\otimes_{\textup{R}}I=I\otimes_{\textup{R}}J

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(I\otimes_{\textup{K}}J+J\otimes_{\textup{K}}I)\>;

(S.E.114b)

\displaystyle\overline{X}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}}:=

\displaystyle X\otimes_{\textup{R}}X=-Z\otimes_{\textup{R}}Z

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(X\otimes_{\textup{K}}X-Z\otimes_{\textup{K}}Z)\>;

(S.E.114c)

\displaystyle\overline{Z}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}}:=

\displaystyle X\otimes_{\textup{R}}Z=Z\otimes_{\textup{R}}X

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(X\otimes_{\textup{K}}Z+Z\otimes_{\textup{K}}X)\>;

(S.E.114d)

\displaystyle I\otimes_{\textup{R}}X=X\otimes_{\textup{R}}I=X\otimes_{\textup{R}}J=J\otimes_{\textup{R}}X

\displaystyle=I\otimes_{\textup{R}}Z=Z\otimes_{\textup{R}}I=Z\otimes_{\textup{R}}J=J\otimes_{\textup{R}}Z

\displaystyle=0\>.

(S.E.114e)

Hence, it automatically provides four non-zero elements that do indeed form an irreducible four-dimensional representation (see
proposition

F.4
).

The interesting bit is that the
\otimes_{\textup{R}}
automatically forbids cross terms, meaning that every term in equation (
S.E.114e
) is equal to 0. At the level of the matrices this representation accompanies, it means that in a matrix
M\otimes_{\textup{R}}N
, the tetradionic parts of
M
and
N
will never interact with the corresponding antitetradionic parts. In particular, that each special and non-special symmetric parts do not get mixed; i.e.,
\mathsf{SY}_{n}({\mathbbm{R}})\otimes_{\textup{R}}\overline{\mathsf{SY}}_{m}({\mathbbm{R}})=\overline{\mathsf{SY}}_{n}({\mathbbm{R}})\otimes_{\textup{R}}\mathsf{SY}_{m}({\mathbbm{R}})=\emptyset
.

Remark that the extended representation loses its locality property for the
\overline{X}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}}
and
\overline{Z}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}}
parts. By this, we mean that local application of one of the matrices on
\overline{X}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}}
does not act like a global application. For example,
(I\otimes_{\textup{K}}Z)\overline{X}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}}\neq\overline{Z}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}}\overline{X}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}}
or
(X\otimes_{\textup{K}}X)\overline{Z}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}}\neq\overline{I}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}}\overline{Z}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}}
. This is in contrast to Equations (
S.B.55
), but since the
\{\overline{X}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}},\overline{Z}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}}\}
part of the space does not have an interpretation as an embedding of complex quantum theory into the reals, there is no reason to require this property to hold in the first place.
A longer discussion on the locality issue and the related reason why the
\otimes_{\textup{R}}
automatically provides an irreducible representation of
D_{8}
is provided in
section

F.3
.

Finally, we extend the representation to an arbitrary number of parties as the
n
-partite generalization of (
S.E.113
) would require it. To do so, we can infer a recursive rule from the above bipartite representation to define the
n
-partite representation of
D_{8}
. On
\bigotimes_{n}{\mathbbm{R}}^{2\times 2}\cong{\mathbbm{R}}^{2^{n}\times 2^{n}}
, we define the matrices used in the representation via the following recursive rules:

\displaystyle\overline{I}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(n)}}:=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}(\overline{I}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(n-1)}}\otimes_{\textup{K}}I-\overline{J}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(n-1)}}\otimes_{\textup{K}}J)\>;

(S.E.115a)

\displaystyle\overline{J}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(n)}}:=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}(\overline{I}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(n-1)}}\otimes_{\textup{K}}J+\overline{J}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(n-1)}}\otimes_{\textup{K}}I)\>;

(S.E.115b)

\displaystyle\overline{X}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(n)}}:=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}(\overline{X}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(n-1)}}\otimes_{\textup{K}}X-\overline{Z}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(n-1)}}\otimes_{\textup{K}}Z)\>;

(S.E.115c)

\displaystyle\overline{Z}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(n)}}:=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}(\overline{X}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(n-1)}}\otimes_{\textup{K}}Z+\overline{Z}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(n-1)}}\otimes_{\textup{K}}X)\>.

(S.E.115d)

These matrices generate a
2^{n}
-dimensional irreducible representation of
D_{8}
.

Remark that the recursive relations do not depend on the ordering of the factors. E.g.,

\displaystyle\overline{I}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(n)}}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}(\overline{I}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(n-1)}}\otimes_{\textup{K}}I-\overline{J}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(n-1)}}\otimes_{\textup{K}}J)=\frac{1}{2}(I\otimes_{\textup{K}}\overline{I}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(n-1)}}-J\otimes_{\textup{K}}\overline{J}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(n-1)}})\>;

(S.E.116a)

\displaystyle\overline{J}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(n)}}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}(\overline{J}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(n-1)}}\otimes_{\textup{K}}I+\overline{I}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(n-1)}}\otimes_{\textup{K}}J)=\frac{1}{2}(J\otimes_{\textup{K}}\overline{I}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(n-1)}}+I\otimes_{\textup{K}}\overline{J}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(n-1)}})\>;

(S.E.116b)

\displaystyle\overline{X}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(n)}}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}(\overline{X}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(n-1)}}\otimes_{\textup{K}}X-\overline{Z}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(n-1)}}\otimes_{\textup{K}}Z)=\frac{1}{2}(X\otimes_{\textup{K}}\overline{X}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(n-1)}}-Z\otimes_{\textup{K}}\overline{Z}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(n-1)}})\>;

(S.E.116c)

\displaystyle\overline{Z}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(n)}}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}(\overline{X}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(n-1)}}\otimes_{\textup{K}}Z+\overline{Z}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(n-1)}}\otimes_{\textup{K}}X)=\frac{1}{2}(X\otimes_{\textup{K}}\overline{Z}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(n-1)}}+Z\otimes_{\textup{K}}\overline{X}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(n-1)}})\>.

(S.E.116d)

Also, remark that the local properties of
I
and
J
carry through the
n
-partite representation. One can indeed show equalities like

\displaystyle(J\otimes_{\textup{K}}I\otimes_{\textup{K}}I\otimes_{\textup{K}}\ldots\otimes_{\textup{K}}I\otimes_{\textup{K}}I)\overline{I}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(n)}}=(\overline{J}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(n-1)}}\otimes_{\textup{K}}I)\overline{I}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(n)}}=(\overline{I}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(n-1)}}\otimes_{\textup{K}}J)\overline{I}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(n)}}=\overline{J}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(n)}}\>;

(S.E.117a)

\displaystyle(J\otimes_{\textup{K}}J\otimes_{\textup{K}}I\otimes_{\textup{K}}\ldots\otimes_{\textup{K}}I\otimes_{\textup{K}}I)\overline{J}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(n)}}=(\overline{J}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(n-1)}}\otimes_{\textup{K}}J)\overline{J}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(n)}}=(J\otimes_{\textup{K}}\overline{J}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(n-1)}})\overline{I}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(n)}}=-\overline{J}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(n)}}\>;

(S.E.117b)

etc. (The proofs are but recursions of the bipartite proof, see
Eq.

S.B.55
.)

With this, we have enough background to define the
\otimes_{\textup{R}}
combination rule for the general case and to subsequently derive the
\otimes_{\textup{r}}
rule from it.

E.5
Definition and properties of the extended combination rule

We begin with the
\otimes_{\textup{R}}
combination. Its definition is obtained by generalizing to
n
parties the initial proposition made in
Eq.

S.E.113
.

Definition E.5
.

Let
M_{1}\in{\mathbbm{R}}^{2d_{1}\times 2d_{1}}
,
M_{2}\in{\mathbbm{R}}^{2d_{2}\times 2d_{2}}
, …, and
M_{n}\in{\mathbbm{R}}^{2d_{n}\times 2d_{n}}
be
n
matrices. Their
\otimes_{\textup{R}}
-combination is given by the matrix
M_{1}\otimes_{\textup{R}}M_{2}\otimes_{\textup{R}}...\otimes_{\textup{R}}M_{n}\in{\mathbbm{R}}^{2^{n}d_{1}...d_{n}\times 2^{n}d_{1}...d_{n}}
defined as

M_{1}\otimes_{\textup{R}}M_{2}\otimes_{\textup{R}}...\otimes_{\textup{R}}M_{n}:=\overline{I}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(n)}}_{d_{1}d_{2}...d_{n}}(M_{1}\otimes_{\textup{K}}M_{2}\otimes_{\textup{K}}...\otimes_{\textup{K}}M_{n})\overline{I}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(n)}}_{d_{1}d_{2}...d_{n}}\>,

(S.E.118)

where
\overline{I}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(n)}}_{d_{1}d_{2}...d_{n}}
is equivalent, up to rearrangements of tensor factors, to
\overline{I}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(n)}}\otimes_{\textup{K}}{\mathbbm{1}}_{d_{1}}\otimes_{\textup{K}}{\mathbbm{1}}_{d_{2}}\otimes_{\textup{K}}\ldots\otimes_{\textup{K}}{\mathbbm{1}}_{d_{n}}
and with
\overline{I}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(n)}}
given by
Eq.

S.E.116a
.

Firstly, it is direct to show that it is associative.

Proposition E.6
.

The
\otimes_{\textup{R}}
-combination is an associative and real-bilinear map.

Proof.

Bilinearity comes directly from the Kronecker product. As for associativity, let
M_{1}\in{\mathbbm{R}}^{2d_{1}\times 2d_{1}}
,
M_{2}\in{\mathbbm{R}}^{2d_{2}\times 2d_{2}}
, and
M_{3}\in{\mathbbm{R}}^{2d_{3}\times 2d_{3}}
be arbitrary matrices, then

\displaystyle(M_{1}\otimes_{\textup{R}}M_{2})\otimes_{\textup{R}}M_{3}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\big(\overline{I}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}}_{d_{1}d_{2}}\otimes_{\textup{K}}I_{d_{3}}-\overline{J}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}}_{d_{1}d_{2}}\otimes_{\textup{K}}J_{d_{3}}\big)

(S.E.119)

\displaystyle\big[(\overline{I}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}}_{d_{1}d_{2}}(M_{1}\otimes_{\textup{K}}M_{2})\overline{I}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}}_{d_{1}d_{2}})\otimes_{\textup{K}}M_{3}\big]\frac{1}{2}\big(\overline{I}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}}_{d_{1}d_{2}}\otimes_{\textup{K}}I_{{d_{3}}}-\overline{J}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}}_{d_{1}d_{2}}\otimes_{\textup{K}}J_{d_{3}}\big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\overline{I}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(3)}}_{d_{1}d_{2}{d_{3}}}\big[(\overline{I}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}}_{d_{1}d_{2}}(M_{1}\otimes_{\textup{K}}M_{2})\overline{I}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}}_{d_{1}d_{2}})\otimes_{\textup{K}}M_{3}\big]\overline{I}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(3)}}_{d_{1}d_{2}{d_{3}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\overline{I}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(3)}}_{d_{1}d_{2}{d_{3}}}\big[(\overline{I}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}}_{d_{1}d_{2}}\otimes_{\textup{K}}I_{d_{3}})(M_{1}\otimes_{\textup{K}}M_{2}\otimes_{\textup{K}}M_{3})(\overline{I}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}}_{d_{1}d_{2}}\otimes_{\textup{K}}I_{d_{3}})\big]\overline{I}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(3)}}_{d_{1}d_{2}{d_{3}}}\>

as follow from the definitions and factorizing the
\overline{I}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}}_{d_{1}d_{2}}
terms in the last equality. One then uses the following locality property of
\overline{I}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(n)}}
:

\overline{I}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(3)}}=\overline{I}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(3)}}(\overline{I}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}}\otimes_{\textup{K}}I)=\overline{I}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(3)}}(I\otimes_{\textup{K}}\overline{I}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}})=(I\otimes_{\textup{K}}\overline{I}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}})\overline{I}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(3)}}=(\overline{I}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}}\otimes_{\textup{K}}I)\overline{I}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(3)}}

(S.E.120)

in order to ‘shift’ the
n=2
representation onto
M_{2}
and
M_{3}
such that:

\displaystyle(M_{1}\otimes_{\textup{R}}M_{2})\otimes_{\textup{R}}M_{3}=

\displaystyle\overline{I}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(3)}}_{d_{1}d_{2}{d_{3}}}\big[(\overline{I}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}}_{d_{1}d_{2}}\otimes_{\textup{K}}I_{d_{3}})(M_{1}\otimes_{\textup{K}}M_{2}\otimes_{\textup{K}}M_{3})(\overline{I}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}}_{d_{1}d_{2}}\otimes_{\textup{K}}I_{d_{3}})\big]\overline{I}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(3)}}_{d_{1}d_{2}{d_{3}}}

(S.E.121)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\overline{I}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(3)}}_{d_{1}d_{2}{d_{3}}}\big[(I_{n}\otimes_{\textup{K}}\overline{I}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}}_{d_{2}d_{3}})(M_{1}\otimes_{\textup{K}}M_{2}\otimes_{\textup{K}}M_{3})(I_{d_{1}}\otimes_{\textup{K}}\overline{I}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}}_{d_{2}d_{3}})\big]\overline{I}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(3)}}_{d_{1}d_{2}d_{3}}

\displaystyle...

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\big(I_{n}\otimes_{\textup{K}}\overline{I}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}}_{d_{2}d_{3}}-J_{n}\otimes_{\textup{K}}\overline{J}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}}_{d_{1}d_{2}}\big)

\displaystyle\big[M_{1}\otimes_{\textup{K}}(\overline{I}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}}_{d_{2}d_{3}}(M_{2}\otimes_{\textup{K}}M_{3})\overline{I}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}}_{d_{2}d_{3}})\big]\frac{1}{2}\big(I_{d_{1}}\otimes_{\textup{K}}\overline{I}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}}_{d_{2}d_{3}}-J_{d_{1}}\otimes_{\textup{K}}\overline{J}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}}_{d_{1}d_{2}}\big)

\displaystyle=M_{1}\otimes_{\textup{R}}(M_{2}\otimes_{\textup{R}}M_{3})\>.

Thus,
\otimes_{\textup{R}}
is associative.
∎

The bilinearity guarantees that convex sums are preserved. The associativity allows us to focus solely on the properties of bipartite systems, as the
n
-partite generalization will then follow. We begin by noticing that the mixed product property does not hold in general, as for example:

\overline{X}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}}=X\otimes_{\textup{R}}X\neq(I\otimes_{\textup{R}}X)(X\otimes_{\textup{R}}I)=\overline{I}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}}(I\otimes_{\textup{K}}X)\overline{I}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}}(X\otimes_{\textup{K}}I)\overline{I}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}}=0\>.

(S.E.122)

Nonetheless, the combination rule retains all local properties globally.

Proposition E.7
.

The
\otimes_{\textup{R}}
-combination of two matrices
\widetilde{H}\in{\mathbbm{R}}^{2n\times 2n}
and
\widetilde{L}\in{\mathbbm{R}}^{2m\times 2m}
preserves symmetric, tetradionic, and positive matrices in the following sense:

\displaystyle\widetilde{H}^{T}=\widetilde{H}\>,\>\widetilde{L}^{T}=\widetilde{L}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\quad

\displaystyle(\widetilde{H}\otimes_{\textup{R}}\widetilde{L})^{T}=\widetilde{H}\otimes_{\textup{R}}\widetilde{L}\>;

(S.E.123a)

\displaystyle J_{n}\widetilde{H}=\widetilde{H}J_{n}\>,\>J_{m}\widetilde{L}=\widetilde{L}J_{m}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\quad

\displaystyle\overline{J}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}}_{mn}(\widetilde{H}\otimes_{\textup{R}}\widetilde{L})=(\widetilde{H}\otimes_{\textup{R}}\widetilde{L})\overline{J}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}}_{mn}\>;

(S.E.123b)

\displaystyle\widetilde{H}\geq 0\>,\>\widetilde{L}\geq 0

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\quad

\displaystyle(\widetilde{H}\otimes_{\textup{R}}\widetilde{L})\geq 0\>.

(S.E.123c)

Proof.

The symmetry preservation comes from the
\otimes_{\textup{R}}
commuting with the transpose,
(\widetilde{H}\otimes_{\textup{R}}\widetilde{L})^{T}=\widetilde{H}^{T}\otimes_{\textup{R}}\widetilde{L}^{T}
(this is direct to prove using
Eq.

S.E.125
).
The tetradionicity preservation comes from
(J_{n}\otimes_{\textup{K}}J_{m})(\widetilde{H}\otimes_{\textup{K}}\widetilde{L})=(\widetilde{H}\otimes_{\textup{K}}\widetilde{L})(J_{n}\otimes_{\textup{K}}J_{m})
implying
\overline{I}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}}_{mn}(\widetilde{H}\otimes_{\textup{K}}\widetilde{L})=(\widetilde{H}\otimes_{\textup{K}}\widetilde{L})\overline{I}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}}_{mn}
which can in turn be used in
\overline{J}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}}_{nm}(\widetilde{H}\otimes_{\textup{R}}\widetilde{L})=\overline{J}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}}_{nm}\overline{I}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}}_{mn}(\widetilde{H}\otimes_{\textup{K}}\widetilde{L})=\overline{I}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}}_{mn}\overline{J}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}}_{nm}(\widetilde{H}\otimes_{\textup{K}}\widetilde{L})=\ldots=(\widetilde{H}\otimes_{\textup{R}}\widetilde{L})\overline{J}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}}_{nm}
.
Finally, the positivity preservation follows from the definition together with
\overline{I}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}}_{mn}
being an orthogonal projector (i.e.,
(\overline{I}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}}_{mn})^{2}=(\overline{I}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}}_{mn})^{T}=\overline{I}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}}_{mn}
, and the Kronecker product preserving positivity. Since the
\otimes_{\textup{R}}
is a projection of the
\otimes_{\textup{K}}
, one has, for all
\ket{\psi}
,
\widetilde{H}\otimes_{\textup{K}}\widetilde{L}\geq 0\iff\bra{\psi}\widetilde{H}\otimes_{\textup{K}}\widetilde{L}\ket{\psi}\geq 0
. Specilalizing it to the vectors of the form
\overline{I}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}}_{nm}\ket{\psi}
, this inequality implies
\bra{\psi}\overline{I}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}}_{nm}(\widetilde{H}\otimes_{\textup{K}}\widetilde{L})\overline{I}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}}_{nm}\ket{\psi}\geq 0
and therefore
\bra{\psi}\widetilde{H}\otimes_{\textup{R}}\widetilde{L}\ket{\psi}\geq 0
for all
\ket{\psi}
, which is the positivity condition for the matrix
\widetilde{H}\otimes_{\textup{R}}\widetilde{L}
.
∎

This implies that the combination of any two valid states will yield a valid state, provided the trace normalisation follows. Take two matrices
\widetilde{H}\in{\mathbbm{R}}^{2n\times 2n}
and
\widetilde{L}\in{\mathbbm{R}}^{2m\times 2m}
, using that
\{I,J,X,Z\}
is a basis for
{\mathbbm{R}}^{2\times 2}
, let them decompose like

\displaystyle\widetilde{H}=I\otimes_{\textup{K}}H_{I}+J\otimes_{\textup{K}}H_{J}+X\otimes_{\textup{K}}H_{X}+Z\otimes_{\textup{K}}H_{Z}\>,

(S.E.124a)

\displaystyle\widetilde{L}=I\otimes_{\textup{K}}L_{I}+J\otimes_{\textup{K}}L_{J}+X\otimes_{\textup{K}}L_{X}+Z\otimes_{\textup{K}}L_{Z}\>,

(S.E.124b)

for
H_{I},H_{J},H_{X},H_{Z}\in{\mathbbm{R}}^{n\times n}
and
L_{I},L_{J},L_{X},L_{Z}\in{\mathbbm{R}}^{m\times m}
. Then, using
Eq.

S.E.114a
we obtain a characterization of their
\otimes_{\textup{R}}
combination:

\displaystyle\widetilde{H}\otimes_{\textup{R}}\widetilde{L}=

\displaystyle\overline{I}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}}\otimes_{\textup{K}}(H_{I}\otimes_{\textup{K}}L_{I}-H_{J}\otimes_{\textup{K}}L_{J})+\overline{J}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}}\otimes_{\textup{K}}(H_{I}\otimes_{\textup{K}}L_{J}+H_{J}\otimes_{\textup{K}}L_{I})

(S.E.125)

\displaystyle+\overline{X}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}}\otimes_{\textup{K}}(H_{X}\otimes_{\textup{K}}L_{X}-H_{Z}\otimes_{\textup{K}}L_{Z})+\overline{Z}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}}\otimes_{\textup{K}}(H_{X}\otimes_{\textup{K}}L_{Z}+H_{Z}\otimes_{\textup{K}}L_{X})\>.

This can be used to compute the trace of a
\otimes_{\textup{R}}
combination,

\text{tr}\left[\widetilde{H}\otimes_{\textup{R}}\widetilde{L}\right]=2(\text{tr}\left[H_{I}\right]\text{tr}\left[L_{I}\right]-\text{tr}\left[H_{J}\right]\text{tr}\left[L_{J}\right])\>,

(S.E.126)

and so we see that the usual distributivity of the trace holds (up to the factor of 2) whenever either of
\widetilde{H}
or
\widetilde{L}
is symmetric:

\widetilde{H}^{T}=\widetilde{H}\text{ or }\widetilde{L}^{T}=\widetilde{L}\quad\Rightarrow\quad\text{tr}\left[\widetilde{H}\otimes_{\textup{R}}\widetilde{L}\right]=\frac{1}{2}\text{tr}\left[\widetilde{H}\right]\text{tr}\left[\widetilde{L}\right]\>.

(S.E.127)

Proposition E.8
.

For any four symmetric matrices
\widetilde{H},\widetilde{L}\in{\mathbbm{R}}^{2n\times 2n}
and
\widetilde{Q},\widetilde{R}\in{\mathbbm{R}}^{2m\times 2m}
,

\text{tr}\left[(\widetilde{H}\widetilde{L})\otimes_{\textup{R}}(\widetilde{Q}\widetilde{R})\right]=2\text{tr}\left[(\widetilde{H}\widetilde{L})\otimes_{\textup{K}}(\widetilde{Q}\widetilde{R})\right]\>,

(S.E.128)

holds if and only if at least one of the four matrices is special symmetric. Similarly,

\text{tr}\left[(\widetilde{H}\widetilde{L})\otimes_{\textup{R}}(\widetilde{Q}\widetilde{R})\right]=\text{tr}\left[(\widetilde{H}\otimes_{\textup{R}}\widetilde{Q})(\widetilde{L}\otimes_{\textup{R}}\widetilde{R})\right]\>,

(S.E.129)

holds if and only if each side of the product feature at least one special symmetric matrix.

Proof.

A straightforward computation gives the following

\displaystyle\text{tr}\left[(\widetilde{H}\widetilde{L})\otimes_{\textup{K}}(\widetilde{Q}\widetilde{R})\right]=\text{tr}\left[\widetilde{H}\widetilde{L}\right]\text{tr}\left[\widetilde{Q}\widetilde{R}\right]\>;

(S.E.130a)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \text{tr}\left[\overline{\sigma}_{0,mn}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}((\widetilde{H}\widetilde{L})\otimes_{\textup{K}}(\widetilde{Q}\widetilde{R})){\overline{\sigma}_{0,mn}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}}\right]&\\
=\frac{1}{2}\Big(\text{tr}\left[\widetilde{H}\widetilde{L}\right]\text{tr}\left[\widetilde{Q}\widetilde{R}\right]-&\text{tr}\left[J_{n}\widetilde{H}\widetilde{L}\right]\text{tr}\left[J_{m}\widetilde{Q}\widetilde{R}\right]\Big)\>;\end{aligned}

(S.E.130b)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \text{tr}\left[\overline{\sigma}_{0,mn}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}(\widetilde{H}\otimes_{\textup{K}}\widetilde{Q})\overline{\sigma}_{0,mn}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}(\widetilde{L}\otimes_{\textup{K}}\widetilde{R})\overline{\sigma}_{0,mn}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}\right]&\\
=\frac{1}{4}\Big(\text{tr}\left[\widetilde{H}\widetilde{L}\right]\text{tr}\left[\widetilde{Q}\widetilde{R}\right]-\text{tr}\left[J_{n}\widetilde{H}\widetilde{L}\right]&\text{tr}\left[J_{m}\widetilde{Q}\widetilde{R}\right]\\
-\text{tr}\left[\widetilde{H}J_{n}\widetilde{L}\right]\text{tr}\left[\widetilde{Q}J_{m}\widetilde{R}\right]&+\text{tr}\left[J_{n}\widetilde{H}J_{n}\widetilde{L}\right]\text{tr}\left[J_{m}\widetilde{Q}J_{m}\widetilde{R}\right]\Big)\>.\end{aligned}

(S.E.130c)

Discarding the trivial cases of when the matrices commute or are traceless, we see that, for symmetric matrices, whenever either of
\widetilde{H}
or
\widetilde{L}
commutes with
J_{n}
,
\text{tr}\left[J_{n}\widetilde{H}\widetilde{L}\right]=0
and similarly, whenever either of
\widetilde{Q}
or
\widetilde{R}
commute with
J_{m}
,
\text{tr}\left[J_{n}\widetilde{Q}\widetilde{R}\right]=0
. Thus, as soon as one of the four matrices is special symmetric,
Eqs.

S.E.130a
and
S.E.130b
are equivalent up to a factor of 2.
Additionally, when both sides of the composition feature at least one special symmetric matrix,
\text{tr}\left[\widetilde{H}\widetilde{L}\right]\text{tr}\left[\widetilde{Q}\widetilde{R}\right]=\text{tr}\left[J_{n}\widetilde{H}J_{n}\widetilde{L}\right]\text{tr}\left[J_{m}\widetilde{Q}J_{m}\widetilde{R}\right]
and
Eqs.

S.E.130b
and
S.E.130c
are equivalent.

To show these to be necessary conditions whenever the problem is not trivial (i.e., no matrix were assumed traceless, to commute with the others, or to be a function of the others), we inject the decomposition of the matrices in terms of
\{I,J,X,Z\}
, e.g.,
\widetilde{H}=I\otimes_{\textup{K}}H_{I}+J\otimes_{\textup{K}}H_{J}+X\otimes_{\textup{K}}H_{X}+Z\otimes_{\textup{K}}H_{Z}
, into the above. Some more computations lead to

\displaystyle\text{tr}\left[\widetilde{H}\widetilde{L}\right]=\text{tr}\left[H_{I}L_{I}-H_{J}L_{J}+H_{X}L_{X}+H_{Z}L_{Z}\right]\>;

(S.E.131a)

\displaystyle\text{tr}\left[J_{n}\widetilde{H}\widetilde{L}\right]=\text{tr}\left[-H_{I}L_{J}-H_{J}L_{I}-H_{X}L_{Z}+H_{Z}L_{X}\right]\>;

(S.E.131b)

\displaystyle\text{tr}\left[\widetilde{H}J_{n}\widetilde{L}\right]=\text{tr}\left[-H_{I}L_{J}-H_{J}L_{I}+H_{X}L_{Z}-H_{Z}L_{X}\right]\>;

(S.E.131c)

\displaystyle\text{tr}\left[J_{n}\widetilde{H}J_{n}\widetilde{L}\right]=\text{tr}\left[-H_{I}L_{I}+H_{J}L_{J}+H_{X}L_{X}+H_{Z}L_{Z}\right]\>.

(S.E.131d)

When the matrices are symmetric, their
J

\widetilde{H}^{T}=\widetilde{H}
and
\widetilde{L}^{T}=\widetilde{L}
implies
\text{tr}\left[H_{J}\right]=\text{tr}\left[L_{J}\right]=0
and by extension
\text{tr}\left[H_{I}L_{J}\right]=\text{tr}\left[H_{J}L_{I}\right]=0
since the
I

\widetilde{H}^{T}=\widetilde{H},\>\widetilde{L}^{T}=\widetilde{L}\quad\Rightarrow\quad\text{tr}\left[J_{n}\widetilde{H}\widetilde{L}\right]=-\text{tr}\left[\widetilde{H}J_{n}\widetilde{L}\right]\>.

(S.E.132)

Therefore, when the matrices are all symmetric,

\text{tr}\left[\overline{\sigma}_{0,mn}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}((\widetilde{H}\widetilde{L})\otimes_{\textup{K}}(\widetilde{Q}\widetilde{R})){\overline{\sigma}_{0,mn}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}}\right]=\text{tr}\left[\overline{\sigma}_{0,mn}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}(\widetilde{H}\otimes_{\textup{K}}\widetilde{Q})\overline{\sigma}_{0,mn}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}(\widetilde{L}\otimes_{\textup{K}}\widetilde{R})\overline{\sigma}_{0,mn}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}\right]\>,

(S.E.133)

provided

\text{tr}\left[\widetilde{H}\widetilde{L}\right]\text{tr}\left[\widetilde{Q}\widetilde{R}\right]=\text{tr}\left[J_{n}\widetilde{H}J_{n}\widetilde{L}\right]\text{tr}\left[J_{m}\widetilde{Q}J_{m}\widetilde{R}\right]\>.

(S.E.134)

Since the choices of
\widetilde{H},\widetilde{Q},\widetilde{L}
, and
\widetilde{R}
are independent, the non-trivial case for which this equality is true is when
\text{tr}\left[\widetilde{H}\widetilde{L}\right]=\pm\text{tr}\left[J_{n}\widetilde{H}J_{n}\widetilde{L}\right]
and the same holds for the other trace with the same sign in front of the right-hand side.
But
\widetilde{H}
and
\widetilde{L}
are not in general traceless, thus
\text{tr}\left[H_{I}L_{I}\right]\neq 0
and we cannot have
\text{tr}\left[\widetilde{H}\widetilde{L}\right]=\text{tr}\left[J_{n}\widetilde{H}J_{n}\widetilde{L}\right]
in the general case.
The only possibility left is
\text{tr}\left[\widetilde{H}\widetilde{L}\right]=-\text{tr}\left[J_{n}\widetilde{H}J_{n}\widetilde{L}\right]
and
\text{tr}\left[\widetilde{Q}\widetilde{R}\right]=-\text{tr}\left[J_{m}\widetilde{Q}J_{m}\widetilde{R}\right]
. In the case of
\text{tr}\left[\widetilde{H}\widetilde{L}\right]=-\text{tr}\left[J_{n}\widetilde{H}J_{n}\widetilde{L}\right]
, this holds when
\text{tr}\left[H_{X}L_{X}+H_{Z}L_{Z}\right]=0
. However,
H_{X},L_{X},H_{Z},L_{Z}
could be any
n\times n
symmetric matrix in principle, so the only way to ensure that
\text{tr}\left[H_{X}L_{X}+H_{Z}L_{Z}\right]=0
is to require

or

, in other words, to require either
\widetilde{H}
or
\widetilde{L}
to be special symmetric. The same reasoning applies to
\widetilde{Q}
and
\widetilde{R}
.

By a similar rationale, from
Eq.

S.E.132
, it can deduced that the only way for
Eqs.

S.E.130a
and
S.E.130b
to be equivalent is that either of the four matrices were special symmetric.
∎

What this proposition shows is that the trace is the proper inner product on the extended space whenever it is restricted to special symmetric matrices. That is, the extension coincides with the usual idea of an RQT when discussing only the part containing the image of CQT.
In general, however, the bilinear form induced by the representation
\otimes_{\textup{R}}
of the tensor product and representing the inner product cannot be the trace because of the above. Instead, the inner product on the space of symmetric matrices will be some other function
<\cdot\>,\>\cdot>
obeying
Eq.

S.A.12
, and that happens to reduce into a trace when applied to special symmetric matrices.

Finally, we study the image of this combination rule. We proved in the previous section that if both
\widetilde{H}
and
\widetilde{L}
are special symmetric, then so will be
\widetilde{H}\otimes_{\textup{R}}\widetilde{L}
. We also show that the linear combinations of such terms will span a space isomorphic to the special symmetric matrices of dimension
n\times m
.
If now
\widetilde{H}
and
\widetilde{L}
are taken to be any two matrices, we can see in
Eq.

S.E.125
that it will yield four orthogonal matrices with
(nm)^{2}
real parameters (i.e., each of the terms in the sum). Thus, elements of the form
\widetilde{H}\otimes_{\textup{R}}\widetilde{L}
span a real subspace of
{\mathbbm{R}}^{4nm\times 4nm}
of dimension
4(nm)^{2}
. By its dimension, we see that this subspace must be isomorphic to
{\mathbbm{R}}^{2nm\times 2nm}
.
In the case where
\widetilde{H}
and
\widetilde{L}
are symmetric, elements of the form

span a real subspace of at least dimension
\mathrm{dim}(\mathsf{Sym}_{nm}\left({\mathbbm{R}}\right))+\mathrm{dim}(\mathsf{ASym}_{nm}\left({\mathbbm{R}}\right))+2\mathrm{dim}(\mathsf{Sym}_{n}\left({\mathbbm{R}}\right))\mathrm{dim}(\mathsf{Sym}_{m}\left({\mathbbm{R}}\right))
which is close to the dimension of the corresponding space of symmetric matrices since

. Can it be that the

combination of symmetric matrices yields a space isomorphic to the full space of symmetric matrices? We leave this question open for future work.

Summarizing the above discussion: the
\otimes_{\textup{R}}
product preserves special symmetric matrices and it is, up to an isomorphism, a tensor product for the spaces of special symmetric matrices. It also preserves symmetric matrices, but it is unclear whether it is a tensor product for their associated spaces. In symbols:

\begin{array}[]{cccccr}\vphantom{\overset{?}{\simeq}}\mathsf{SY}_{nm}({\mathbbm{R}})&\simeq&\mathsf{SY}_{n}({\mathbbm{R}})\otimes_{\textup{R}}\mathsf{SY}_{m}({\mathbbm{R}})&\subset&\mathsf{SY}_{2nm}({\mathbbm{R}})&;\\
\mathsf{Sym}_{2nm}\left({\mathbbm{R}}\right)&\overset{?}{\simeq}&\mathsf{Sym}_{2n}\left({\mathbbm{R}}\right)\otimes_{\textup{R}}\mathsf{Sym}_{2m}\left({\mathbbm{R}}\right)&\subset&\mathsf{Sym}_{4nm}\left({\mathbbm{R}}\right)&;\\
\vphantom{\overset{?}{\simeq}}{\mathbbm{R}}^{2nm\times 2nm}&\simeq&{\mathbbm{R}}^{2n\times 2n}\otimes_{\textup{R}}{\mathbbm{R}}^{2m\times 2m}&\subset&{\mathbbm{R}}^{4nm\times 4nm}&.\end{array}

(S.E.135)

These relations are to be compared with those induced by the Kronecker product:

\begin{array}[]{cccr}\vphantom{\overset{?}{\cong}_{m}}\mathsf{SY}_{n}({\mathbbm{R}})\otimes_{\textup{K}}\mathsf{SY}_{m}({\mathbbm{R}})&\not\subset&\mathsf{SY}_{2nm}({\mathbbm{R}})&;\\
\mathsf{Sym}_{2n}\left({\mathbbm{R}}\right)\otimes_{\textup{K}}\mathsf{Sym}_{2m}\left({\mathbbm{R}}\right)&\subset&\mathsf{Sym}_{4nm}\left({\mathbbm{R}}\right)&;\\
\vphantom{\overset{?}{\cong}}{\mathbbm{R}}^{2n\times 2n}\otimes_{\textup{K}}{\mathbbm{R}}^{2m\times 2m}&\cong&{\mathbbm{R}}^{4nm\times 4nm}&.\end{array}

(S.E.136)

In a similar fashion to what we did in the previous appendix, we can define a combination rule derived from
\otimes_{\textup{R}}
and that is effectively the tensor product for special symmetric matrices. Let
\tau_{\otimes_{\textup{R}}}:{\mathbbm{R}}^{2^{n}d_{1}\ldots d_{n}\times 2^{n}d_{1}\ldots d_{n}}\rightarrow{\mathbbm{R}}^{2d_{1}\ldots d_{n}\times 2d_{1}\ldots d_{n}}
be the following map

\tau_{\otimes_{\textup{R}}}(\cdot):=I\otimes_{\textup{K}}\frac{\text{tr}_{D_{8}}\left[I^{n}\>\cdot\right]}{2}-J\otimes_{\textup{K}}\frac{\text{tr}_{D_{8}}\left[J^{n}\>\cdot\right]}{2}+X\otimes_{\textup{K}}\frac{\text{tr}_{D_{8}}\left[X^{n}\>\cdot\right]}{2}+Z\otimes_{\textup{K}}\frac{\text{tr}_{D_{8}}\left[Z^{n}\>\cdot\right]}{2}\>,

(S.E.137)

where “
\mathrm{Tr}_{D_{8}}
” indicates a partial trace over only the
n

{\mathbbm{R}}^{2\times 2}
tensor factors that carry the matrices
\{\overline{I}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(n)}},\overline{J}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(n)}},\overline{X}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(n)}},\overline{Z}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(n)}}\}
representing
D_{8}
.

Definition E.9
.

Let

,
M_{2}\in{\mathbbm{R}}^{2d_{2}\times 2d_{2}}
, …, and
M_{n}\in{\mathbbm{R}}^{2d_{n}\times 2d_{n}}

Supplementary F
Extended discussion on the real-matrices representations of the cyclic and dihedral groups

We begin by recalling the definitions of the groups involved in this paper.

Definition F.1

(Cyclic and Dihedral groups)
.

The cyclic group of order
n
, noted
{\mathbbm{Z}}_{n}
, is the finite Abelian group whose presentation is the following:

\left\langle a|a^{n}=e,bab^{-1}=a^{-1}\right\rangle\>.

(S.F.140)

Where
e
is the identity element in the above.

The dihedral group of order
2n
, noted
D_{2n}
, is obtained as the semi-direct product of the cyclic group of order 2 with the one of order
n
,
D_{8}={\mathbbm{Z}}_{2}\rtimes{\mathbbm{Z}}_{n}
. It is the finite non-Abelian group whose presentation is the following:

\left\langle a,b|a^{n}=b^{2}=e,bab^{-1}=a^{-1}\right\rangle\>.

(S.F.141)

Its maximal cyclic subgroup is
Z_{n}
presented as

\left\langle a|a^{n}=e\right\rangle\>.

(S.F.142)

Its center is
Z_{2}
presented as

\left\langle a^{2}|(a^{2})^{2}=e\right\rangle\>.

(S.F.143)

F.1
Single-partite representation of
D_{8}

As a convenience for the proofs throughout this appendix, the matrices
\{I,J,X,Z\}
will be relabelled via
\{\sigma_{\mu}\}_{\mu=0}^{3}
using the convention:

\displaystyle\sigma_{0}:=I=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}\>;

(S.F.144a)

\displaystyle\sigma_{1}:=X=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}\>;

(S.F.144b)

\displaystyle\sigma_{2}:=J=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}\>;

(S.F.144c)

\displaystyle\sigma_{3}:=Z=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&-1\end{pmatrix}\>.

(S.F.144d)

We will also use the physicists’ convention that Greek indices run from
0
to
3
whereas the Latin ones run from
1
to
3
, their algebraic relations are concisely given by

\displaystyle\sigma_{\mu}\sigma_{0}=\sigma_{0}\sigma_{\mu}=\sigma_{\mu}\>;

(S.F.145a)

\displaystyle\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}=\epsilon_{ijk}(-1)^{\delta_{k2}}\sigma_{k}+\delta_{ij}(-1)^{\delta_{i2}}\sigma_{0}\>;

(S.F.145b)

where
\delta_{\mu\nu}

These relations can be shown to be equivalent to the presentation of the dihedral group of order

\displaystyle\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}\sigma_{1}^{-1}=\sigma_{2}^{3}\>.

Proposition F.2
.

The set of matrices
\left\{\sigma_{\mu}\right\}\subset{\mathbbm{R}}^{2\times 2}
generates (through matrix multiplication), a real, irreducible representation of the dihedral group
D_{8}
under the identification
e\mapsto\sigma_{0}
,
a\mapsto\sigma_{2}
, and
b\mapsto\sigma_{1}
.

Explicitly, the eight elements of the group are given by
\left\{\pm\sigma_{0},\pm\sigma_{1},\pm\sigma_{2},\pm\sigma_{3}\right\}
; the four elements of its maximal cyclic subgroup are given by
\left\{\pm\sigma_{0},\pm\sigma_{2}\right\}
; and the two elements of its centre by
\{\pm\sigma_{0}\}
.

Proof.

As mentioned above, from direct computation, one can check that
\sigma_{2}^{4}=(-I)^{2}=I
which implies that the inverse of
\sigma_{2}
is
\sigma_{2}^{-1}=\sigma_{2}^{3}=-\sigma_{2}
, that
\sigma_{1}^{2}=I
which implies that the inverse of
\sigma_{1}
is
\sigma_{1}
, and that
\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}\sigma_{1}^{-1}=\sigma_{2}^{-1}\iff\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}\sigma_{1}=-\sigma_{2}
. It then suffices to compute the words of the form
a^{n}b^{m}\mapsto\sigma_{2}^{n}\sigma_{1}^{m}
to obtain all eight elements.

To show irreducibility, one shows orthogonality of the characters i.e. that
\frac{1}{8}(\text{tr}\left[\pm I\right]^{2}+\text{tr}\left[\pm\sigma_{2}\right]^{2}+\text{tr}\left[\pm\sigma_{1}\right]^{2}+\text{tr}\left[\pm\sigma_{3}\right]^{2})=1
. Since every matrix has zero trace except
{\mathbbm{1}}
which has trace 2, one is left with
\frac{1}{8}(\text{tr}\left[I\right]^{2}+\text{tr}\left[-I\right]^{2})=1
.
∎

Remark F.3
.

An interesting but tangent thing to notice is that the ‘real’ Pauli matrices under consideration,
\{\sigma_{\mu}\}_{\mu}=\{I,X,J,Z\}\subset{\mathbbm{R}}^{2\times 2}
correspond to the dihedral group. This is contrasting with the usual Pauli matrices
\{I,X,Y,Z\}\subset{\mathbbm{C}}^{2\times 2}
, which correspond to the quaternion group

\Gamma
to some map
\Gamma^{\prime}
which image contains every

{\mathbbm{H}}^{n\times n}\overset{\Gamma^{\prime}}{\nrightarrow}{\mathbbm{R}}^{2n\times 2n}
), contrary to what one might have expected.

(S.F.148a)

\displaystyle a

\displaystyle\mapsto\overline{\sigma}_{2}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}\sim\sigma_{0}\otimes_{\textup{K}}\sigma_{2}\sim\sigma_{2}\otimes_{\textup{K}}\sigma_{0}\>;

(S.F.148b)

\displaystyle b

\displaystyle\mapsto\overline{\sigma}_{1}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}\sim\sigma_{1}\otimes_{\textup{K}}\sigma_{1}\sim\sigma_{3}\otimes_{\textup{K}}\sigma_{3}\>;

(S.F.148c)

where the similarity relation
\sim
in the above means

M\sim\overline{\sigma}_{\mu}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}\iff\forall\nu:\quad M\overline{\sigma}_{\nu}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}=\overline{\sigma}_{\mu}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}\overline{\sigma}_{\nu}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}\quad\&\quad\overline{\sigma}_{\nu}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}M=\overline{\sigma}_{\nu}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}\overline{\sigma}_{\mu}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}\>.

(S.F.149)

This allows to obtain the
\{\overline{\sigma}_{\mu}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}\}
matrices from the group structure: since every element of
D_{8}
has the form
a^{j+k}b^{i}e
for some
i,j,k\in\{0,1\}
, one obtains

\pm\overline{\sigma}_{\mu}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}=((\sigma_{1}^{i}\sigma_{2}^{j})\otimes_{\textup{K}}(\sigma_{1}^{i}\sigma_{2}^{k}))\overline{\sigma}_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}\>,

(S.F.150)

from the similarity relations.

In addition, these relations show that the subgroup generated by
\{\overline{\sigma}_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)},\overline{\sigma}_{2}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}\}
is indeed a local representation of
{\mathbbm{Z}}_{4}
. As explained in
section

B.3
,
Eq.

S.F.148b
implies that applying
\sigma_{2}
on each side of the Kronecker product has the same effect as applying
\overline{\sigma}_{2}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}
.

F.3
Why the extended composition is defined as such

Nonetheless, the other bipartite generator does not coincide with a local representation, in the sense that
(\sigma_{1}\otimes_{\textup{K}}\sigma_{0})\nsim\overline{\sigma}_{1}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}
, for example, and, more generally, that

\overline{\sigma}_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}(\sigma_{j}\otimes_{\textup{K}}\sigma_{0})\neq\epsilon_{ijk}(-1)^{\delta_{k2}}\overline{\sigma}_{k}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}+\delta_{ij}(-1)^{\delta_{i2}}\overline{\sigma}_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}\>.

(S.F.151)

This is due to the fact that the extended group
D_{8}
is no longer Abelian, which leads to difficulties with the Kroncker product. Indeed, if
\overline{\sigma}_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}(\sigma_{j}\otimes\sigma_{0})
were equal to
\overline{\sigma}_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}\overline{\sigma}_{j}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}
then it would result to an ambiguity when applying two local operations since
\overline{\sigma}_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}(\sigma_{j}\otimes_{\textup{K}}\sigma_{k})=\overline{\sigma}_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}(\sigma_{j}\otimes_{\textup{K}}\sigma_{0})(\sigma_{0}\otimes_{\textup{K}}\sigma_{k})=\overline{\sigma}_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}\overline{\sigma}_{j}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}\overline{\sigma}_{k}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}
and
\overline{\sigma}_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}(\sigma_{j}\otimes_{\textup{K}}\sigma_{k})=\overline{\sigma}_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}(\sigma_{0}\otimes_{\textup{K}}\sigma_{k})(\sigma_{j}\otimes_{\textup{K}}\sigma_{0})=\overline{\sigma}_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}\overline{\sigma}_{k}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}\overline{\sigma}_{j}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}
, making it impossible for the
\{\overline{\sigma}_{\mu}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}\}
to generate a non-Abelian group.

Remark F.5
.

The reader may have guessed that with
\overline{\sigma}_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}
as our choice of identity element and a nonlocal bipartite representation of the
b
generator, some algebraic difficulties have been induced. Using this representation of
D_{8}
as a way to define a tensor product space will ultimately lead to a tensor product that does not obey the mixed product formula, as will be shown in the following.

The reader may think there could still be a possibility to find a representation
\overline{\tau}_{\mu}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}
of
D_{8}
that is local, for example by requiring the left-hand side of the Kronecker product to act from the left like

\overline{\tau}_{\mu}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}(\sigma_{\nu}\otimes_{\textup{K}}\sigma_{\kappa})=\overline{\tau}_{\nu}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}\overline{\tau}_{\mu}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}\overline{\tau}_{\kappa}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}\>,

(S.F.152)

for all values of indices. Such a representation would in principle make more sense, as its generators are the weighted mixture of all the local operations whose action are equivalent globally, i.e.,

\overline{\tau}_{\mu}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}:=\sum_{\nu,\kappa:\sigma_{\nu}\otimes_{\textup{K}}\sigma_{\kappa}\sim\overline{\tau}_{\mu}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}}f(\nu,\kappa)\>(\sigma_{\nu}\otimes_{\textup{K}}\sigma_{\kappa})\>,

(S.F.153)

for some weights
f(\nu,\kappa)
. This property would guarantee the representation to be local by construction.
For instance, the bipartite identity element would be

\overline{\tau}_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}:=\frac{1}{4}\left(\sigma_{0}\otimes_{\textup{K}}\sigma_{0}+\sigma_{1}\otimes_{\textup{K}}\sigma_{1}-\sigma_{2}\otimes_{\textup{K}}\sigma_{2}+\sigma_{3}\otimes_{\textup{K}}\sigma_{3}\right)

(S.F.154)

instead of
\overline{\sigma}_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}
.

At first glance, such a local representation has another consequent advantage over the one we chose: since the equivalence classes under
\sim
mimic the multiplicative relations (i.e., the
\overline{\tau}_{\mu}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}
’s are obtained by setting
\sigma_{i}\otimes_{\textup{K}}\sigma_{j}\sim\epsilon_{ijk}(-1)^{\delta_{k2}}\overline{\tau}_{k}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}+\delta_{ij}(-1)^{\delta_{i2}}\overline{\tau}_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}
), the mixed product formula will hold.

After closer inspection, however, using such representation would dramatically complexify our methods (figuratively and literally). As a matter of fact, finding the scalar weights
f(\nu,\kappa)
in
\overline{\tau}_{2}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}=\sum_{\nu,\kappa}f(\nu,\kappa)(\sigma_{\nu}\otimes_{\textup{K}}\sigma_{\kappa})
such that
(\overline{\tau}_{2}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)})^{2}=-\overline{\tau}_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}
is impossible in
{\mathbbm{R}}
… but also in
{\mathbbm{C}}
! The solution requires non-commuting scalars (i.e., Grassman numbers).

Because of that, a representation of
D_{8}
in
{\mathbbm{R}}^{4\times 4}
which is local w.r.t. the relation
\sim
seems unlikely to exist. Yet, finding such a representation would induce a greatly simplified composition rule for the alternative real quantum theory presented in this article. Therefore, we leave this question open for further research.

Still, while the
\{\overline{\sigma}_{\mu}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}\}
matrices are not a local representation of
D_{8}
, they enjoy some remarkable properties.

Lemma F.6
.

For any
\mu,\nu\in\{0,1,2,3\}
, the following conditions are equivalent

1.

\mu+\nu
is even.

2.

\exists a,a^{\prime}\in\{0,1,2,3\},\>\exists b\in\{0,1\}:\quad(\sigma_{\mu}\otimes_{\textup{K}}\sigma_{\nu})=(\sigma_{1}^{b}\sigma_{2}^{a})\otimes_{\textup{K}}(\sigma_{1}^{b}\sigma_{2}^{a^{\prime}})\>.

(S.F.155)

3.

\forall\kappa,\>\exists\lambda:\quad(\sigma_{\mu}\otimes_{\textup{K}}\sigma_{\nu})\overline{\sigma}_{\kappa}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}=\pm\overline{\sigma}_{\lambda}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}\>;

(S.F.156)

4.

\overline{\sigma}_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}(\sigma_{\mu}\otimes_{\textup{K}}\sigma_{\nu})=(\sigma_{\mu}\otimes_{\textup{K}}\sigma_{\nu})\overline{\sigma}_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}\>;

(S.F.157)

5.

\overline{\sigma}_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}(\sigma_{\mu}\otimes_{\textup{K}}\sigma_{\nu})\overline{\sigma}_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}\neq 0\>;

(S.F.158)

Proof.

The proof will consist of showing a looping chain of implications:
1.\Rightarrow 2.\Rightarrow 3.\Rightarrow 4.\Rightarrow 5.\Rightarrow 1.

First,
1.\Rightarrow 2.
: any
\sigma_{\mu}
has the form
\sigma_{1}^{b}\sigma_{2}^{a}
. Consequently, the Kronecker product of two such matrices has the form
\sigma_{\mu}\otimes_{\textup{K}}\sigma_{\nu}=(\sigma_{1}^{a}\sigma_{2}^{b})\otimes_{\textup{K}}(\sigma_{1}^{a^{\prime}}\sigma_{2}^{b^{\prime}})
.
Since
\sigma_{1}^{2}=\sigma_{0}
, the index of
\sigma_{\mu}
(respectively,
\sigma_{\nu}
) can be odd only when
b
(resp.
b^{\prime}
) is odd. Therefore, if
\mu+\nu
is even, either both indexes are even or they are odd, which is ensured by imposing
b^{\prime}=b
, thus condition 2.

Then, to show
2.\Rightarrow 3.
, we use that every
\overline{\sigma}_{\mu}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}
has the form
Eq.

S.F.150
.
We inject this as well as condition
2.
into the left-hand side of
3.
to obtain
(\sigma_{\mu}\otimes_{\textup{K}}\sigma_{\nu})\overline{\sigma}_{\kappa}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}=(\sigma_{1}^{b}\sigma_{2}^{a})\otimes_{\textup{K}}(\sigma_{1}^{b}\sigma_{2}^{a^{\prime}})((\sigma_{1}^{i}\sigma_{2}^{j})\otimes_{\textup{K}}(\sigma_{1}^{i}\sigma_{2}^{k}))\overline{\sigma}_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}
which, using
Eq.

S.F.145
, can be reduced to
(\sigma_{\mu}\otimes_{\textup{K}}\sigma_{\nu})\overline{\sigma}_{\kappa}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}=(-1)^{(a+a^{\prime})i}((\sigma_{1}^{b+i}\sigma_{2}^{a+j})\otimes_{\textup{K}}(\sigma_{1}^{b+i}\sigma_{2}^{a^{\prime}+k}))\overline{\sigma}_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}
and this is equivalent to
Eq.

S.F.150
up to a minus sign, proving the implication.

The implication
3.\Rightarrow 4.
is proven by first observing that
(\sigma_{\mu}\otimes_{\textup{K}}\sigma_{\nu})\overline{\sigma}_{\kappa}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}=\pm\overline{\sigma}_{\kappa}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}(\sigma_{\mu}\otimes_{\textup{K}}\sigma_{\nu})
as the bipartite matrices are sums of Kronecker product of single parties ones (up to a minus sign), and that every pair of single-partite matrices either commute or anticommute. Knowing that, we set
\kappa=0
in condition 3. and square both sides, leading to
(\sigma_{\mu}\otimes_{\textup{K}}\sigma_{\nu})\overline{\sigma}_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}(\sigma_{\mu}\otimes_{\textup{K}}\sigma_{\nu})\overline{\sigma}_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}=(-1)^{\delta_{2\lambda}}\overline{\sigma}_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}
. Using the previous observation, we inject
(\sigma_{\mu}\otimes_{\textup{K}}\sigma_{\nu})\overline{\sigma}_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}=\pm\overline{\sigma}_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}(\sigma_{\mu}\otimes_{\textup{K}}\sigma_{\nu})
into the equality, giving
\pm(\sigma_{\mu}^{2}\otimes_{\textup{K}}\sigma_{\nu}^{2})\overline{\sigma}_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}=(-1)^{\delta_{2\lambda}}\overline{\sigma}_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}
. Now remark that
(\sigma_{\mu}^{2}\otimes_{\textup{K}}\sigma_{\nu}^{2})
gives the identity matrix
\sigma_{0}\otimes_{\textup{K}}\sigma_{0}
except in the case where
\mu=2
and
\nu=0
and vice-versa, in which case an overall minus sign is added. As these two cases precisely correspond to
\lambda=2
, we can write
(\sigma_{\mu}^{2}\otimes_{\textup{K}}\sigma_{\nu}^{2})\overline{\sigma}_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}=(-1)^{\delta_{2\lambda}}\overline{\sigma}_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}
. Simplifying, this then gives
\pm(\sigma_{0}\otimes\sigma_{0})\overline{\sigma}_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}=\overline{\sigma}_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}
, where the
\pm
depends on whether
\overline{\sigma}_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}
was commuting or anticommuting with
\sigma_{\mu}\otimes_{\textup{K}}\sigma_{\nu}
. In order for the equality to hold, therefore, we must conclude that condition 4. holds when
3.
does.

The next implication,
4.\Rightarrow 5.
is again proven by squaring
(\sigma_{\mu}^{2}\otimes_{\textup{K}}\sigma_{\nu}^{2})\overline{\sigma}_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}
. We get
(\sigma_{\mu}\otimes_{\textup{K}}\sigma_{\nu})\overline{\sigma}_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}(\sigma_{\mu}\otimes_{\textup{K}}\sigma_{\nu})\overline{\sigma}_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}=\overline{\sigma}_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}
, and so using the commutation this is equivalent to
\overline{\sigma}_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}(\sigma_{\mu}\otimes_{\textup{K}}\sigma_{\nu})\overline{\sigma}_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}\overline{\sigma}_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}(\sigma_{\mu}\otimes_{\textup{K}}\sigma_{\nu})\overline{\sigma}_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}=\overline{\sigma}_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}
. Thus, the square of
\overline{\sigma}_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}(\sigma_{\mu}\otimes_{\textup{K}}\sigma_{\nu})\overline{\sigma}_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}
is a non-zero matrix, leading to the conclusion that it is one itself.

Finally,
5.\Rightarrow 1.
follows by direct computation: expand
\overline{\sigma}_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}(\sigma_{\mu}\otimes_{\textup{K}}\sigma_{\nu})\overline{\sigma}_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}=\frac{1}{4}(\sigma_{\mu}\otimes_{\textup{K}}\sigma_{\nu}-(\sigma_{2}\sigma_{\mu})\otimes_{\textup{K}}(\sigma_{2}\sigma_{\nu})-(\sigma_{\mu}\sigma_{2})\otimes_{\textup{K}}(\sigma_{\nu}\sigma_{2})+(\sigma_{2}\sigma_{\mu}\sigma_{2})\otimes_{\textup{K}}(\sigma_{2}\sigma_{\nu}\sigma_{2}))
, and observe that
\sigma_{2}\sigma_{\mu}=\sigma_{\mu}\sigma_{2}
if
\mu
is even and else
\sigma_{2}\sigma_{\mu}=-\sigma_{\mu}\sigma_{2}
. Thus, when both indices are even or odd, the negative and positive terms can be regrouped,
\overline{\sigma}_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}(\sigma_{\mu}\otimes_{\textup{K}}\sigma_{\nu})\overline{\sigma}_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}=\frac{1}{2}(\sigma_{\mu}\otimes_{\textup{K}}\sigma_{\nu}-(\sigma_{2}\sigma_{\mu})\otimes_{\textup{K}}(\sigma_{2}\sigma_{\nu}))
, whereas when the parity of the indices is different it becomes
\overline{\sigma}_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}(\sigma_{\mu}\otimes_{\textup{K}}\sigma_{\nu})\overline{\sigma}_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}=\frac{1}{4}(\sigma_{\mu}\otimes_{\textup{K}}\sigma_{\nu}-(\sigma_{2}\sigma_{\mu})\otimes_{\textup{K}}(\sigma_{2}\sigma_{\nu})+(\sigma_{2}\sigma_{\mu})\otimes_{\textup{K}}(\sigma_{2}\sigma_{\nu})-(\sigma_{2}^{2}\sigma_{\mu})\otimes_{\textup{K}}(\sigma_{2}^{2}\sigma_{\nu}))=0
. Hence, if
\overline{\sigma}_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}(\sigma_{\mu}\otimes_{\textup{K}}\sigma_{\nu})\overline{\sigma}_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}\neq 0
then
\mu+\nu
is even, showing that
5.\Rightarrow 1.
which closes the proof.
∎

These algebraic properties are quite interesting, as they show that the
\{\sigma_{\mu}\}
matrices send any product of the form
\sigma_{\mu}\otimes_{\textup{K}}\sigma_{\nu}
to a stable subspace.
Indeed, as the
\{\sigma_{\mu}\}
matrices is an orthogonal set (w.r.t. the Frobenius inner product) of
{\mathbbm{R}}^{2\times 2}
which actually spans it, the
\{\overline{\sigma}_{\mu}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}\}
form an orthogonal set in
{\mathbbm{R}}^{4\times 4}
, which spans a subspace isomorphic to
{\mathbbm{R}}^{2\times 2}
. One can form the projector onto this subspace by forming the following sum of dyads,

\mathcal{P}(\cdot):=\sum_{\kappa}\frac{(-1)^{\delta_{2\kappa}}}{2}\>\overline{\sigma}_{\kappa}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}\>\text{tr}\left[(\overline{\sigma}_{\kappa}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)})^{T}\>\cdot\right]\>,

(S.F.159)

where the
\frac{(-1)^{\delta_{2\kappa}}}{2}
coefficients are used for normalisation purposes. This is an orthogonal projection of
{\mathbbm{R}}^{4\times 4}
into itself, as it can be swiftly checked. This projection will be the map useful to define the extended composition rule, as we shall see in the following.

In relation to that, the second item of
lemma

F.6
is interesting. It shows that applying the representation of the identity element on both sides of a matrix projects onto a subspace, which turns out to be exactly the subspace spanned by the
\{\sigma_{\mu}\}
matrices.

Proposition F.7
.

\sum_{\kappa}\frac{(-1)^{\delta_{2\kappa}}}{2}\>\overline{\sigma}_{\kappa}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}\>\text{tr}\left[(\overline{\sigma}_{\kappa}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)})^{T}\>M\right]=\>\overline{\sigma}_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}\>M\>\overline{\sigma}_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}\>.

(S.F.160)

Proof.

Because
\{\sigma_{\mu}\otimes_{\textup{K}}\sigma_{\nu}\}
is a basis of
{\mathbbm{R}}^{4\times 4}
, every
M
can be decomposed as
M=\sum_{\mu,\nu}m_{\mu\nu}(\sigma_{\mu}\otimes_{\textup{K}}\sigma_{\nu})
. Injecting into the right-hand side and using
lemma

F.6
, we see that it is non-zero only when
(\sigma_{\mu}\otimes_{\textup{K}}\sigma_{\nu})
has the form
(\sigma_{1}^{b}\sigma_{2}^{a})\otimes_{\textup{K}}(\sigma_{1}^{b}\sigma_{2}^{a^{\prime}})
for some naturals
a,a^{\prime}
and
b
. Subtituting these non-zero elements like
m_{\mu\nu}(\sigma_{\mu}\otimes_{\textup{K}}\sigma_{\nu})=m^{\prime}(a,a^{\prime},b)((\sigma_{1}^{b}\sigma_{2}^{a})\otimes_{\textup{K}}(\sigma_{1}^{b}\sigma_{2}^{a^{\prime}}))
where
m(a,a^{\prime},b)
is a real-valued function of
a,a^{\prime}
and
b
, the right-hand side then takes the form
\overline{\sigma}_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}\>M\>\overline{\sigma}_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}=\overline{\sigma}_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}(\sum_{a,a^{\prime},b}m^{\prime}(a,a^{\prime},b)((\sigma_{1}^{b}\sigma_{2}^{a})\otimes_{\textup{K}}(\sigma_{1}^{b}\sigma_{2}^{a^{\prime}}))\overline{\sigma}_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}
.
Finally, we use the third item of
lemma

F.6
to further rewrite it into

\overline{\sigma}_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}\>M\>\overline{\sigma}_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}=\pm\sum_{a,a^{\prime},b}m^{\prime}(a,a^{\prime},b)\overline{\sigma}_{\lambda(a,a^{\prime},b)}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}\>,

(S.F.161)

where
\lambda(a,a^{\prime},b)
is a function of
a,a^{\prime}
and
b
taking values in
\{0,1,2,3\}
and the
\pm
sign also depends on the values of
a,a^{\prime}
and
b
.

We now do the same rewriting for the left-hand side. We first remove the transpose within the trace since every
\overline{\sigma}_{\kappa}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}
is symmetric. Then we decompose
M
in the same basis as before and conclude that the trace is only non-zero when the
(\sigma_{\mu}\otimes_{\textup{K}}\sigma_{\nu})
’s have the form
(\sigma_{1}^{b}\sigma_{2}^{a})\otimes_{\textup{K}}(\sigma_{1}^{b}\sigma_{2}^{a^{\prime}})
because
\text{tr}\left[\overline{\sigma}_{\kappa}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}(\sigma_{\mu}\otimes_{\textup{K}}\sigma_{\nu})\right]=\text{tr}\left[\overline{\sigma}_{\kappa}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}\overline{\sigma}_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}(\sigma_{\mu}\otimes_{\textup{K}}\sigma_{\nu})\overline{\sigma}_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}\right]
.

Now, using
\text{tr}\left[\overline{\sigma}_{\kappa}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}(\sigma_{\mu}\otimes_{\textup{K}}\sigma_{\nu})\right]=\text{tr}\left[\overline{\sigma}_{\kappa}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}(\sigma_{\mu}\otimes_{\textup{K}}\sigma_{\nu})\overline{\sigma}_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}\right]
and the third item of
lemma

F.6
, the left-hand side of
Eq.

S.F.160
is equivalent to

\sum_{\kappa}\frac{(-1)^{\delta_{2\kappa}}}{2}\>\overline{\sigma}_{\kappa}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}\>\text{tr}\left[(\overline{\sigma}_{\kappa}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)})^{T}\>M\right]=\sum_{\mu}\frac{(-1)^{\delta_{2\mu}}}{2}\>\overline{\sigma}_{\mu}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}\>\text{tr}\left[\overline{\sigma}_{\mu}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}\>\left(\pm\sum_{a,a^{\prime},b}m^{\prime}(a,a^{\prime},b)\overline{\sigma}_{\lambda(a,a^{\prime},b)}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}\right)\right]\>,

(S.F.162)

where
\lambda(a,a^{\prime},b)
is the same four-valued function as above. Finally, we can permute the two summations to obtain a sum over terms that all contain some trace of the form
\text{tr}\left[\overline{\sigma}_{\mu}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}\>\overline{\sigma}_{\lambda(a,a^{\prime},b)}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}\right]
. Using the algebra of the
\overline{\sigma}_{\mu}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}
’s matrices to compute its value of
\text{tr}\left[\overline{\sigma}_{\mu}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}\>\overline{\sigma}_{\lambda(a,a^{\prime},b)}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}\right]=2(-1)^{\delta_{2\mu}}\delta_{\mu\lambda(a,a^{\prime},b)}

\sum_{\kappa}\frac{(-1)^{\delta_{2\kappa}}}{2}\>\overline{\sigma}_{\kappa}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}\>\text{tr}\left[(\overline{\sigma}_{\kappa}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)})^{T}\>M\right]=\pm\sum_{a,a^{\prime},b}m^{\prime}(a,a^{\prime},b)\overline{\sigma}_{\lambda(a,a^{\prime},b)}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}\>.

(S.F.163)

We therefore conclude that both sides of

S.F.160
are equivalent.

F.4
Multipartite representation of

Proposition F.8
.

The set of matrices
\left\{\overline{\sigma}_{\mu}^{\scriptscriptstyle(n)}\right\}\subset{\mathbbm{R}}^{2n\times 2n}
defined by
Eq.

S.E.115a
generates (through matrix multiplication) a real, irreducible representation of the dihedral group

,

Proof.

First, we show the group relations. For

n-1

\overline{\sigma}_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle(n)}\overline{\sigma}_{1}^{\scriptscriptstyle(n)}=\frac{1}{4}\left(\overline{\sigma}_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle(n-1)}\otimes_{\textup{K}}\sigma_{1}+\overline{\sigma}_{1}^{\scriptscriptstyle(n-1)}\otimes_{\textup{K}}\sigma_{0}-\overline{\sigma}_{1}^{\scriptscriptstyle(n-1)}\otimes_{\textup{K}}\sigma_{1}^{2}-(\overline{\sigma}_{1}^{\scriptscriptstyle(n-1)})^{2}\otimes_{\textup{K}}\sigma_{1}\right)

\begin{gathered}\frac{1}{2}(\overline{\sigma}^{(n-1)}_{i}\otimes_{\textup{K}}\sigma_{j}+(-1)^{i+j}\>\overline{\sigma}^{(n-1)}_{j}\otimes_{\textup{K}}\sigma_{i})\frac{1}{2}(\overline{\sigma}^{(n-1)}_{k}\otimes_{\textup{K}}\sigma_{l}+(-1)^{k+l}\>\overline{\sigma}^{(n-1)}_{l}\otimes_{\textup{K}}\sigma_{k})=\\
\frac{1}{4}(\overline{\sigma}^{(n-1)}_{i}\overline{\sigma}^{(n-1)}_{k}\otimes_{\textup{K}}\sigma_{j}\sigma_{l}+(-1)^{i+j}\>\overline{\sigma}^{(n-1)}_{j}\overline{\sigma}^{(n-1)}_{k}\otimes_{\textup{K}}\sigma_{i}\sigma_{l}+(-1)^{k+l}\>\overline{\sigma}^{(n-1)}_{i}\overline{\sigma}^{(n-1)}_{l}\otimes_{\textup{K}}\sigma_{j}\sigma_{k}\\
+(-1)^{i+j+k+l}\>\overline{\sigma}^{(n-1)}_{j}\overline{\sigma}^{(n-1)}_{l}\otimes_{\textup{K}}\sigma_{i}\sigma_{k})=\\
\frac{1}{2}\big(\frac{1}{2}\big[\overline{\sigma}^{(n-1)}_{i}\overline{\sigma}^{(n-1)}_{k}\otimes_{\textup{K}}\sigma_{j}\sigma_{l}+(-1)^{i+j+k+l}\>\overline{\sigma}^{(n-1)}_{j}\overline{\sigma}^{(n-1)}_{l}\otimes_{\textup{K}}\sigma_{i}\sigma_{k}\big]+\\

’s and

’s do, which is true by hypothesis.
For the irreducibility, the proof using characters for the

arbitrary case.
