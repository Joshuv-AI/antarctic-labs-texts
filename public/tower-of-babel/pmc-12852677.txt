Comparative efficacy of pulmonary rehabilitation delivery models on dyspnoea, exercise capacity and health-related quality of life in chronic respiratory disease: a systematic review and network meta-analysis
Authors: Jia Song, Qin Wang, Chongyang Zhao, Lei Wang, Li Li, Deying Kang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis systematic review aimed to systematise the different models used to deliver pulmonary rehabilitation (PR) during chronic respiratory diseases (CRDs) and explore which ones are the most effective in terms of dyspnea, exercise capacity, and health-related quality of life (HRQoL). The literature search strategy involved structured searches of PubMed, Web of Science, and Cochrane Library for relevant articles published from January 2013 to March 2025. The risk of bias was assessed using ROB 2.0. Descriptive analysis and meta-analysis were performed. Forest plots and the node-splitting model presented results. Network meta-analysis was conducted in R 4.3.2. 33 studies(n = 2538) were included in this review and of those, 27 studies(n = 2106) were used for meta-analysis. 22 (66.7%) studies were at high risk of bias and the certainty of evidence for all outcomes (6MWT, dyspnea, HRQoL) was rated as low due to study limitations and imprecision. Compared with usual care, PR patients have significant improvement on 6MWT as well as mMRC (both P < 0.01). The cumulative ranking probability curves and forest plot analyses revealed a hierarchical efficacy profile among rehabilitation modalities for CRDs, with outpatient presented the larger effects on mMRC (mean difference (MD)= -0.82, 95%Cl [-1.45, -0.19]), 6MWT(MD = 65.45, 95%Cl [45.06, 85.84]). Our findings suggest that PR probably improves exercise capacity and reduces dyspnea in patients with CRDs, with outpatient-based programmes generally showing the largest effects, while the high risk of bias limits interpretation of this finding.PROSPERO ID: CRD420251013615.Subject terms: Diseases, Health care, Medical research

Introduction

Chronic respiratory diseases (CRDs) affect over half a billion people globally with 81.7 million people in Europe, which reduce health-related quality of life (HRQoL) and functional status, contribute significantly to disability, premature death and economic loss by 20251. Pulmonary rehabilitation(PR) has been shown to be highly effective in improving exercise capacity, function and quality of life for people with CRDs2,3. It is a comprehensive intervention, based in thorough patient assessment and tailored therapies, such as exercise training, education, and behavioural modifications, to improve physical and psychological well-being in individuals with CRDs and promote sustained health-enhancing behaviours4. Most importantly, PR has been shown to reduce mortality risk and should be implemented as routine practice for people with CRDs5,6.

The models of PR are very diverse, including inpatient rehabilitation6, outpatient rehabilitation7, community/home-based rehabilitation8, telerehabilitation9. Previous meta-analyses or randomized controlled trials (RCTs) have compared some of these modalities in pairs, with mixed findings. For example, a meta-analysis comparing inpatient and outpatient PR reported conflicting results for exercise tolerance, dyspnea, and healthcare costs, while most studies suggested no significant differences or a slight advantage of inpatient rehabilitation10. Bondarenko et al. found that there were no differences between centre-based and home-based PR in the proportion of participants achieving minimal clinically important difference (MCID)11. Similarly, Cox et al. suggested that telerehabilitation delivered for people with CRDs achieved outcomes comparable to centre-based PR without safety concerns.12. However, these pairwise comparisons do not provide a comprehensive picture, and there remains unclear which model of PR is most effective across key outcomes.

A network meta-analysis (NMA) is a statistical technique to determine the effectiveness of a range of interventions. This method compares multiple treatments by incorporating both direct and indirect evidence, allowing for a more precise estimate compared to estimates from direct studies alone13. Although several NMAs have examined PR interventions14,15, there is still a lack of evidence directly comparing the clinical effectiveness of four distinct PR delivery models, namely inpatient, outpatient, community/home-based, and telerehabilitation. To date, no study has comprehensively evaluated their relative benefits in improving dyspnea, exercise capacity, and HRQoL among patients with CRDs.

Therefore, we conducted a NMA to compare the clinical outcomes of four PR delivery models, including inpatient, outpatient, community/home-based, and telerehabilitation, and to determine the optimal modality and duration for improving dyspnea, exercise capacity, and HRQoL in patients with CRDs.

Methods

The study protocol was registered with the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO), registration number CRD420251013615.

Search strategy and study selection

The literature search strategy involved structured searches of PubMed, Web of Science, and Cochrane Library for relevant articles published from January 2013 to March 2025. Studies that met the criteria shown in Table 1 were eligible. The complete search strategy and can be found in Supplementary material A.

Only RCTs involving adult patients with a confirmed diagnosis of CRDs were considered. Eligible studies were required to explicitly report the implementation of PR interventions and to compare PR against usual care, or to perform head-to-head comparisons between distinct rehabilitation modalities. Facility-based rehabilitation includes outpatient rehabilitation and inpatient rehabilitation, non facility-based rehabilitation includes community/home-based rehabilitation and telerehabilitation.

Two researchers (Jia Song and Qin Wang) conducted the study selection independently, and all disagreements were resolved by a third investigator (Deying Kang). Duplicated and irrelevant studies were rejected first by examining titles and abstracts. After that, full texts of potentially eligible studies were obtained and reviewed according to inclusion and exclusion criteria. When the full text was not available, we contacted the authors to obtain raw data, and if the authors did not answer in two weeks, the study was excluded from the selection.

Data extraction

Two independent researchers (Jia Song and Qin Wang) independently extracted and reviewed information from eligible articles. Any disagreements were resolved through discussion, with interpretation by a third author (Deying Kang). Two investigators used predesigned forms for data extraction and recording. The characteristics of the included patients, including study, country, experimental group, control group, duration and outcomes, were recorded in detail. For healthcare use and adverse event, the related tables and results were recorded.

Risk of bias assessment

The Cochrane Risk of Bias 2.0 (ROB2.0) tool for randomised trials16 was used to assess the risk of bias within the included studies. Two independent reviewers (Jia Song and Qin Wang) independently assessed each included study for each primary outcome using this tool and classified studies as having low, high or some concerns across all domains. An overall summary risk-of-bias judgement was derived for each outcome following published guidelines17, with the overall risk-of-bias judgement for each study determined by the highest risk-of-bias level across the domains. Additionally, the certainty of evidence was evaluated using the Confidence in Network Meta-Analysis framework (CINeMA), while Egger’s test and funnel plots were utilized to investigate potential small-study effects or publication bias17. CINeMA assessments were guided by Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development, and Evaluations (GRADE) principles and based on the full body of evidence contributing to each comparison.

Quality-of-evidence assessment

The quality of evidence pertaining to each outcome assessed within the NMA was individually rated by two authors (Jia Song and Qin Wang) according to the GRADE considerations18. Footnotes were provided to explain any decisions to downgrade the quality of evidence.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis conducted using R 4.3.2 and RevMan 5.3. A random-effects pairwise meta-analysis compared PR with usual care. Where applicable, subgroup analyses were performed by intervention duration, classified as short-term (<8 weeks) or long-term (≥8 weeks). Heterogeneity was quantified using the I² statistic, with thresholds categorized as: might not be important (0% to 40%), may represent moderate heterogeneity (30% to 60%), may represent substantial heterogeneity (50% to 90%), and considerable heterogeneity (75% to 100%)19. The NMA was performed using a random-effects model in a frequentist framework with the netmeta package in R20. Ranking plots illustrated the probability of each treatment being most effective. Node-splitting was used to assess inconsistency in closed loops, with P < 0.05 indicating significance. Transitivity was evaluated based on demographic similarity (e.g., age, sex), consistent CRDs diagnosis criteria, and study design (RCTs only). Sensitivity analyses were conducted using pairwise comparisons to confirm robustness. Each outcome will be analyzed separately by questionnaire type. In addition, we conducted a subgroup analysis restricted to the COPD population and comparative analyses across PR settings, given that COPD accounts for most of the evidence on PR and many guidelines are based on this population.

Results

Characteristics of eligible studies

Study selection is summarized in the PRISMA flow diagram. After deduplication, 33 studies (n = 2538) met the inclusion criteria and 27 (n = 2 106) contributed data to the meta-analysis. The complete PRISMA flow diagram illustrating the process of study identification and selection through database searching is presented in Fig. 1.

PRISMA Flowchart of the included studies.

A total of 5097 records were identified through the database search. After removing duplicates, 3293 titles and abstracts were screened, and 2309 records were excluded. The full text of 984 potentially relevant articles was reviewed, of which 950 were excluded. Ultimately, 33 studies were included in this review, and 27 studies were used in the meta-analysis.

Characteristics of interventions

Further details were included in Supplementary material B for a comprehensive overview of the 33 RCTs and their characteristics6–9,12,21–48. Exercise capacity was assessed in 24 studies6–9,12,23,24,28–33,35–39,41–43,46–48, with 23 employing the 6-minute walk test (6MWT)6,10–12,15,26,27,31,32,34–36,38–42,44–46 and one utilising the endurance shuttle walk test (ESWT). Dyspnea severity was evaluated in nine studies6–8,22,29,32,36,40,42,46–48: eight administered the modified Medical Research Council (mMRC) questionnaire6–8,22,29,32,40,42 and one applied the London Chest Activity of Daily Living (LCADL) scale. HRQoL outcomes were reported across all 24 studies6,7,9,21,23–27,29,30,32,33,35,36,38–41,43–46,48, measured through heterogeneous instruments such as Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease Assessment Test (CAT)6,7,24,27,29,39,44 and St George’s Respiratory Questionnaire (SGRQ)23,29,33,36,44,45.

Presentation of overall analysis results

A summary of the analysis results was presented in Figs. 2 and 3, taken usual care as the common control. No significant inconsistency was found in the NMA. Visual inspection of the funnel plot revealed symmetrical distribution of effect estimates, indicating a low risk of publication bias (Supplementary material H). Egger’s regression test demonstrated no statistically significant evidence of small-study effects (P > 0.05).

Exercise capacity

Data from 23 studies6–9,12,23,24,28,29,31–33,35–39,41–43,46–48, comprising 1517 subjects, were analyzed for 6MWT effects. 18 studies comparing PR with usual care, we observed that PR demonstrated a statistically significant improvement in 6MWT compared with usual care (P < 0.01; Fig. 2a). No significant differences were detected between long-term interventions and short-term interventions (P = 0.46; Supplementary material I). We further evaluated diverse rehabilitation modalities through a network meta-analysis incorporating 23 RCTs. Four comparisons against control were performed (Supplementary material C). Consistency tests for this closed-loop comparison suggested that P were all >0.1(Supplementary material G). Facility-based rehabilitation programmes (MD = 54.31, 95% confidence interval (95%Cl) [32.48, 76.13]) demonstrated a marginally greater mean difference (MD) compared with non facility-based (MD = 42.35, 95%Cl [17.90, 66.81]) (Fig. 3a). Based on forest plots and cumulative ranking probability analyses, the treatment modalities for CRDs patients were ranked in descending order of efficacy as follows: outpatient (MD = 65.45, 95%Cl [45.06, 85.84]), community/home-based (MD = 61.92. 95%Cl [31.58, 92.26]), telerehabilitation (MD = 36.07, 95%Cl [8.80,63.34]), inpatient (MD = 8.67, 95%Cl [-30.54, 47.89]) (Fig. 3c, Supplementary material D).

Dyspnea

Dyspnea severity was assessed using mMRC. Overall, 8 studies6–8,22,29,32,40,42, comprising 609 subjects, were included in this analysis on dyspnea. A meta-analysis of 7 eligible RCTs revealed that PR significantly reduced dyspnea severity in patients with CRDs compared with usual care (MD = -0.62, 95%Cl [-0.97, -0.27]) (Fig. 2b). The NMA suggests that facility-based rehabilitation (MD = -0.68, 95%Cl [-1.07, 0.29]) was associated with a greater reduction in dyspnea severity than usual care (MD = -0.45, 95%Cl [-1.02, 0.11]) (Fig. 3b). Four comparisons against control were performed (Supplementary material C). Consistency tests for this closed-loop comparison suggested that P were all >0.1(Supplementary material G). Based on the cumulative ranking probability curves and the forest plots, a hierarchical efficacy pattern was observed across rehabilitation modalities for CRDs. Outpatient programmes appeared to perform best for reducing dyspnea (MD = −0.82, 95% CI − 1.45 to −0.19), followed by community/home-based (MD = −0.56, 95% CI [ − 1.48, 0.35]), inpatient (MD = −0.50, 95% CI [ − 1.28, 0.28]), telerehabilitation (MD = −0.39, 95% CI [ − 1.51,0.73]), and usual care (Fig. 3d; Supplementary Material D).

Health-related quality of life (exploratory network meta-analysis)

HRQoL was measured by the validated SGRQ (n = 6) and CAT (n = 6). Overall, the studies showed that PR significantly improved CAT (MD = -2.71, 95%Cl [−4.46, −0.95]), whereas no statistically significant change was observed on SGRQ (MD = -4.26, 95%Cl [-14.09, 5.58]). The corresponding network meta-analyses are reported as exploratory meta-analysis, the results can be found in the Supplementary material J.

Subgroup analysis of COPD

PR was superior to usual care for both 6MWT (MD = 43.86, 95%Cl [16.29, 71.43]) and dyspnea measured by mMRC (MD = -0.52, 95%Cl [-1.03, -0.01]). Because of an insufficient number of studies, subgroup analyses were not performed for SGRQ or CAT. In the 6MWT network, community/home-based PR ranked highest (MD = 77.44, 95% CI [14.47, 140.42]), followed by outpatient PR (MD = 67.13, 95% CI [34.17–100.09]). Telerehabilitation and inpatient PR showed no clear advantage over usual care (Supplementary material L).

Healthcare use

Among the included studies, only one7 provided detailed healthcare use outcomes7. This trial showed that PR resulted in annual per-patient savings of RMB 3,655.94 over two years, with a cost-benefit ratio of 1:3.29.

Adverse event

Five studies reported adverse events during the intervention6,26,29,34,47, with two providing detailed summaries. Although Chul Kim et al. found no significant intergroup differences in adverse event incidence and most serious adverse events (SAEs) were unrelated to the intervention29, Sally Majd et al. reported two exercise-induced bronchospasm (EIB) cases in their cohort26.

Risk-of-bias assessment

In accordance with the RoB 2.0 tool, for the 33 studies, four (12.1%) study were recognized to be low risk of bias, and 22 (66.7%) studies were at high risk of bias (Supplementary material E). The most common domain resulting in studies being classified as at high risk of bias was domain 2 (“Deviations from the intended interventions”).

Quality-of-evidence assessment

The evidence from all thee outcomes of interest within this review (6MWT, dyspnea, HRQoL) were deemed to be of “low certainty” (Supplementary material F). Downgrading was due to high overall risk of bias, as well as imprecision due to most articles including these outcomes as secondary outcomes.

Discussion

In summary, our systematic review identified and selected 33 studies from 14 different countries with a total of 2538 patients. Overall, PR was reported as being effective in terms of enhancing exercise capacity, reducing dyspnea severity, consistent with prior evidence2,14. Among the modalities, outpatient rehabilitation generally performed best, improving exercise capacity and reducing dyspnea.

There was considerable variability in the disease spectrum of participants, programme content and intensity, which reflects clinical heterogeneity. Our research covers COPD, asthma, ILD and other conditions whose distinct pathophysiology, symptom burden and responsiveness to exercise may lead to different therapeutic gains and distinct likelihoods of achieving the MCID49. It should be expected that not all models of PR will be equally suitable for all patients with CRDs. And researchers adopt personalized training intensities based on the different severity levels of patients3. For example, Guo et al. demonstrated that moderate COPD patients need to receive intensive PR training; the improvement degrees from PR intervention were higher than those of the severe COPD patients50. Besides, the formats and content of programmes reported in the studies vary markedly such as resistance training, education, breathing exercises, resulting in heterogeneous effects35. British Thoracic Society (BTS) Clinical Statement on PR emphasized that endurance and resistance training in PR require individualized prescriptions51.

It should be noted that while PR benefits multiple CRDs49, most evidence remains derived from studies on COPD52. Grade 1 evidence supports the benefits of PR for COPD, including improved exercise capacity, reduced dyspnea, enhanced HRQoL, and decreased hospitalization rates. Our COPD subgroup analysis results are consistent with previous research evidence. Growing evidence also suggests its efficacy in improving similar outcomes for other conditions, such as ILD, bronchiectasis, and pulmonary hypertension49. This represents an important research direction for advancing PR in future studies.

For the overall CRDs analysis and in the COPD subgroup, facility-based rehabilitation may produce greater improvements than non facility-based. The superiority of facility-based over non facility-based may be related not only to supervision of health professionals but also to equipment and ability to regulate training intensity in hospital settings53. The effectiveness of inpatient rehabilitation appears to be lower than that of outpatient rehabilitation, potentially attributable to differences in baseline patient severity45. Inpatients generally exhibiting more severe clinical conditions or in the acute phase or greater comorbidities, while most of the outpatient rehabilitation patients may be in the stable phase53.

Although community/home-based rehabilitation and telerehabilitation have not yet demonstrated superior outcomes compared to usual care, there remains a lack of long-term cohort studies to evaluate their effects over extended periods52. Outpatient and inpatient rehabilitation have achieved good results, but there are still some barriers in the real world. Health-system barriers include commissioning gaps, referral inertia and inefficient service pathways. Patient-level barriers include travel and transport burdens, comorbidities, inconvenient scheduling and disruption of established routines53. Therefore, more attention can be paid to community/home-based rehabilitation, telerehabilitation and other alternative models to improve the accessibility of PR in future.

Furthermore, it needs to acknowledge methodological heterogeneity. Several trials show elevated risk of bias39,44, for example due to deviations from intended interventions and unclear allocation concealment. There was also considerable between-study inconsistency, with I² values frequently exceeding 70% and 95% CIs remaining wide19. For example, the findings for SGRQ and CAT should be interpreted as exploratory which may be partly attributable to the small sample size and wide confidence intervals.

Looking to the future, researchers might provide a “suite” of evidence-based PR models, which all offer the essential components while differing in complexity and mode of delivery. It is recommended that researchers follow the PR framework provided in the ATS/ERS guidelines to design intervention content3,4, and the selection of PR intervention content should be combined with an increasingly mature evidence base and local context3.

Certainly, this article also exhibits significant strengths. A comprehensive systematic search and screening process was conducted in accordance with the PRISMA guidelines, which are widely recognized as the gold standard for conducting systematic reviews. Furthermore, the incorporation of a network meta-analysis enabled both direct and indirect comparisons across multiple design aspects.

Conclusion

Our findings suggest that PR probably improves exercise capacity and reduces dyspnea in patients with CRDs, with outpatient-based programmes generally showing the largest effects, while the high risk of bias limits interpretation of this finding.
