Mental health literacy profiles and associated factors among young Chinese male soldiers: a latent profile analysis
Authors: Mei Wang, Hong Xin Ni, Xiao Zhang, Jie Gao, Jun Li Zhou, Lan Yun Qu, Chun Ping Ni, Song Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMental health literacy (MHL), defined as an individual’s knowledge, beliefs, and resources related to mental health disorders, is crucial for mental health. However, there is a lack of research evidence on young soldiers’ levels of MHL. To address this gap, this study aimed to explore MHL profiles and associated factors among young Chinese male soldiers.MethodsA cross-sectional study with 1,561 young Chinese male soldiers was conducted from April to December 2024. The participants were surveyed using the Multicomponent Mental Health Literacy Measure, Perceived Stress Scale, Self-Rating Anxiety Scale, and Self-Rating Depression Scale. Latent profile analysis, chi-square tests, multiple logistic regression, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), and the Student-Newman-Keuls (SNK) test were conducted.ResultsLatent profile analysis revealed three profiles characterized by high (n = 870, 55.73%), medium (n = 405, 25.95%), and low (n = 286, 18.32%) levels of MHL. The factors found to influence all HML profiles were age 18–23 years, less than 2 years of military service, and engagement in active psychological learning (P < 0.05). Recent exposure to stressful life events influenced the high and medium MHL profiles (P < 0.05). The low MHL profile had the highest risk of mental health problems, whereas the high MHL profile had the lowest risk (P < 0.001).ConclusionsThree latent MHL profiles were identified among young Chinese male soldiers. To promote soldiers’ mental health, specific interventions should be developed to target the specific characteristics of each profile.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12888-026-08002-3.

Introduction

Young adulthood is a critical transitional phase characterized by significant physical, emotional, and social changes [1]. During this period, individuals are particularly vulnerable to psychological challenges due to increased stress from academic demands, career expectations, and interpersonal relationships [2]. These stressors significantly increase the risk of mental health issues such as stress, anxiety, and depression [3]. Empirical research indicates that young people have limited interaction with the healthcare system, and suicide resulting from untreated mental health issues has become one of the most common causes of premature death in this population [4, 5]. Studies on young adults’ mental health indicate that mental health literacy (MHL) is an accessible, shareable, and intervenable resource for mental health promotion, attracting the attention of global healthcare providers, researchers, and policymakers [6–8].

The concept of MHL was first introduced in 1997 and defined as “knowledge and beliefs about mental disorders which aid their recognition, management and prevention” [9]. In 2012, Jorm expanded this definition to encompass five components: knowledge of prevention, recognition, help-seeking, effective treatment strategies, self-help techniques, and psychological first-aid skills [10]. As research has progressed, the concept has continued to evolve and has yet to achieve a fully unified definition, now being broadly understood as the knowledge, beliefs, and resources that enable individuals to promote mental health and address mental health problems [11–13]. Specifically, the knowledge dimension refers to understanding mental disorders and coping strategies; the beliefs dimension involves attitudes toward mental health issues, stigma, and self-efficacy; and the resources dimension includes the awareness of and ability to utilize help-seeking channels, support systems, and related services [2, 10, 14]. Individuals with higher MHL are significantly better at recognizing psychological issues and are more likely to adopt effective coping strategies [12]. Improving MHL can significantly reduce mental health issues, including stress, anxiety, depression, and suicidal ideation [15–18]. Therefore, enhancing MHL is crucial for promoting mental well-being. Given the dynamic changes in young adults’ mental health and the difficulty in providing timely interventions, it is imperative to enhance their MHL [5, 19–21].

Young military personnel are exposed to more intense stressors than the general population, including restricted social interactions, arduous training regimens, prolonged family separations, and unpredictable work environments [2]. Consequently, they are at a heightened risk of experiencing mental health issues and warrant greater attention from researchers. The prevalence of anxiety symptoms among Chinese military personnel varies widely, from 2.86% to 81.61% [22–26], while that of depressive symptoms ranges from 3.26% to 23.10% [23–27]. A 2020 survey of 334 young Chinese male soldiers found that 29.64% experienced health-threatening stress [28]. Nevertheless, few studies have explored the relationships between MHL and symptoms of anxiety, depression, and stress in this population. Previous studies have tended to aggregate scores to evaluate MHL and its associated factors [2–4], treating it as a unidimensional rather than a multidimensional construct and ignoring its inherent heterogeneity.

To address this gap, our study used latent profile analysis (LPA), a person-centered approach, to identify distinct MHL subgroups within the population [29–32], thus enabling a more comprehensive examination of its associated factors. We specifically focused on anxiety, depression, and stress symptoms, as they represent the most prevalent mental health challenges among military personnel [22–28]. According to the diathesis-stress model [33], psychological disorders result from interactions between vulnerability factors and environmental stressors. MHL, as a positive cognitive resource and a component of coping ability, may buffer against stress and mitigate symptom development. Thus, we postulated that different MHL profiles would be associated with varying levels of anxiety, depression, and stress symptoms in this group. Given the scarcity of data on MHL among young male soldiers, our analyses were exploratory.

Methods

Study design

A cross-sectional descriptive design was adopted, and the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology guidelines [34] were followed.

Sample size estimation

According to Kendall’s sample size estimation method, a sample size should be 5–10 times the number of independent variables [35]. This study included 19 independent variables: 12 general information questionnaire indicators, 3 national MHL questionnaire dimensions, 2 perceived stress dimensions, 1 anxiety dimension, and 1 depression dimension. Therefore, calculating 10 times the number of variables and assuming 20% invalid questionnaires, the required sample size was determined to be at least 228 participants. Considering that LPA requires at least 50 samples for each category [36], we estimated that there were two to four potential categories, and thus 100–200 participants were needed. Accordingly, the sample size was determined to meet the requirements of the statistical approaches used.

Participants

This cross-sectional study was conducted between April and December 2024. A total of 1,561 young Chinese male soldiers were recruited using a convenience sampling method, which was necessitated by the operational constraints of military settings. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) active-duty military personnel with official unit permission and voluntary informed consent; (2) aged 18–35 years; and (3) intact cognitive and communicative abilities. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) inconsistent responses (identified through specific validation items in the survey) and (2) completion of the questionnaire in < 5 min or > 30 min (indicating inattentiveness, based on a pilot study).

Procedure

The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the No. 991 Hospital (Approval Number: 991YJ-202206). Prior to data collection, all investigators underwent a 30-minute standardized training to ensure consistent implementation of the study protocol and communication with the participants. All participants were informed of the study’s purpose, including the non-anonymous nature of their participation, and provided informed consent voluntarily. The WeChat questionnaire (with mandatory response settings to prevent missing data) was distributed via QR code to all participants, who then independently completed it online in a centralized, supervised group setting to ensure data quality and independence of responses. Following submission, the research team conducted quality control checks (including roster verification for name, age and sex) to verify adherence to the inclusion and exclusion criteria. The flowchart of participant screening see in additional file 1.

Measures

General information questionnaire

The researcher-designed general information questionnaire collected the participants’ demographic and psychosocial variables, which consisted of 12 items including: age, military service duration, educational attainment, only child family background, marital status, family structure, number of close friends, availability of someone to confide in during times of distress, engagement in active psychological learning, sleep quality over the past week, chronic disease status, and recent exposure to stressful life events. The inclusion of these variables is justified by the existing literature [2, 4, 10, 15], which identifies them as key factors influencing mental health literacy and outcomes.

Mental health literacy (MHL)

MHL was assessed using the validated Chinese version of the Multicomponent Mental Health Literacy (MMHL) scale [2]. This 22-item instrument measures three dimensions: mental health knowledge (10 items), beliefs (8 items), and resources (4 items). Responses are dichotomously scored (correct = 1, incorrect = 0), yielding a total score range of 0–22, with higher scores indicating greater literacy. The scale demonstrates satisfactory psychometric properties in the Chinese military population [2, 11], with a Cronbach’s α of 0.868 in the present study. The Cronbach’s α coefficients of the three dimensions were 0.857 (knowledge), 0.822 (beliefs), and 0.851 (resources).

Perceived stress

The Chinese version of the Perceived Stress Scale-14 [37, 38] was used to evaluate the stress levels experienced by the participants during the preceding month. This 14-item instrument has been validated among Chinese military personnel [28]. Total scores range from 0 to 56, with scores > 25 indicating health-threatening stress levels in Chinese military personnel [28, 39]. The Cronbach’s α coefficient for the total scale was 0.862 in this study.

Anxiety

Anxiety symptoms were measured using the Self-Rating Anxiety Scale [40], which assesses the frequency of symptoms over the past week. The 20 items are rated on a 4-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (“very seldom”) to 4 (“most of the time”). Raw scores are summed and multiplied by 1.25 to derive standardized scores (range: 25–100). Higher scores indicate more severe anxiety levels. Scores of 0–50 indicate no clinical anxiety, 50–59 mild anxiety, 60–69 moderate anxiety, and ≥ 70 severe anxiety [22–26]. This scale has been widely used in studies involving the Chinese military, and the validated Chinese version has been widely used in China [22–26]. In the present investigation, the total scale’s Cronbach’s α was 0.725.

Depression

Depressive symptoms were assessed using the Self-Rating Depression Scale [41], a 20-item instrument that measures symptom frequency over the preceding week. Items are measured on a 4-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (very seldom) to 4 (most of the time). The raw summed score is multiplied by 1.25 to yield a standardized score (range: 25–100). Higher scores indicate more severe depression symptoms. Scores of 0–53 indicate no clinical depression, 53–62 mild depression, 63–72 moderate depression, and ≥ 73 severe depression [23–27]. The Chinese version demonstrates adequate reliability and validity in military populations [23–27], and the internal consistency coefficient (Cronbach’s α) was 0.878.

Data analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to present the data. Means and standard deviations were calculated for normally distributed continuous variables, and frequencies and percentages for categorical variables.

Following the descriptive analysis, we estimated latent profile models, evaluating solutions from one to five profiles using Mplus version 9.0. The distribution of the profile sizes was considered in the model evaluation to address concerns related to the generalizability and stability of solutions with small profiles. Preference was given to solutions in which each latent profile represented at least 15% of the total sample. Model fit was assessed using the following criteria: the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), the Bayes Information Criterion (BIC), the adjusted Bayesian Information Criterion (aBIC), and entropy. Lower AIC, BIC, and aBIC values indicate a better model fit. Entropy, which ranges from 0 to 1, reflects the accuracy of the profile classification, with values closer to 1 indicating higher precision. Entropy values exceeding 0.8 suggested that over 90% of the participants were correctly categorized. The Lo-Mendell-Rubin Test (LMRT) and bootstrap likelihood ratio test (BLRT) were used to assess the P values in the comparisons of models with different numbers of classes. A low P value indicated that the k-class model fitted better than the k-1 class model. After the optimal model was selected, each profile was named to best describe its features and distinguish it from the other profiles.

Subsequently, a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was conducted to evaluate the differences in perceived stress, anxiety, and depression scores among the identified MHL profiles, followed by the Student-Newman-Keuls (SNK) test for post-hoc comparisons. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05 (two-tailed).

Results

Sample characteristics and MHL scores

A total of 1,730 participants were eligible for the study. Of these, 169 were excluded according to the exclusion criteria: 103 individuals had a response time of less than 5 min, 11 individuals had a response time exceeding 30 min, and 55 individuals gave inconsistent responses. Consequently, 1,561 valid questionnaires were retained, yielding an effective response rate of 90.23%.

The mean MHL score was 0.51 ± 0.230, and the mean dimension scores were 0.53 ± 0.301 for knowledge, 0.37 ± 0.299 for beliefs, and 0.75 ± 0.355 for resources. The detailed general information are presented in Table 1.

Latent profile analysis

Using the mean scores of the MHL dimensions as the observed indicators, latent profile models with one to five profiles were estimated (Table 2). The three-profile solution emerged as the most interpretable, with each profile reflecting distinct patterns consistent with previous research [30–32] and representing at least 15% of the total sample. Although the five-profile solution had the lowest BIC value (-353.55), the Lo-Mendell Rubin ratio test was not statistically significant (P = 0.34), indicating no substantial improvement over the four-profile model. The four-profile solution was considered suboptimal due to significant overlap between two profiles and less than 15% representation for three of the profiles, raising concerns about their generalizability. Considering the statistical fit indices, model parsimony, theoretical interpretability, and practical relevance of the adequately sized profiles, the three-profile solution was chosen as the optimal model.

Multiple logistic regression analysis across the identified latent profiles

The results of the univariate analyses, presented in Table 3, revealed that the three MHL profiles had statistically significant differences in age, military service time, marital status, engagement in active psychological learning, sleep quality over the past week, and recent exposure to stressful life events (P < 0.05). Using the high MHL profile as the benchmark reference, a multiple logistic regression analysis was conducted with the profiles as the dependent variable and the statistically significant factors from the univariate analyses as the independent variables. The coding (assignment) of the independent variables was as follows: Age (years) 18–23 = 0, 24–34 = 1; Military service time (years) < 2 = 0, ≥ 2 = 1; Engagement in active psychological learning No = 0, Yes = 1; Marital status No = 0, Yes = 1; Sleep quality over the past week Poor = 0, Good = 1; Recent exposure to stressful life events No = 0, Yes = 1. The results of the multiple logistic regression analysis of these factors, presented in Table 4, identified various patterns of MHL. Compared with the high MHL profile, the three MHL profile scores were significantly influenced by age 18–23 years, less than 2 years of military service, and engagement in active psychological learning (P < 0.05). Recent exposure to stressful life events influenced the high and medium MHL profiles (P < 0.05).

Differences in perceived stress, anxiety, and depression among the profiles

As shown in Table 5, perceived stress, anxiety, and depression scores differed significantly across the three MHL profiles (all P < 0.001). One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) revealed that the higher MHL profile had lower perceived stress, anxiety, and depression scores. Specifically, perceived stress scores were 11.46 ± 8.505 for high MHL profile, 14.45 ± 8.439 for moderate MHL profile, and 15.95 ± 9.121 for low MHL profile. Corresponding anxiety scores were 33.76 ± 6.834, 35.26 ± 6.507, and 36.88 ± 7.037, while depression scores were 34.88 ± 9.759, 37.13 ± 10.213, and 39.93 ± 11.785. SNK post-hoc tests confirmed the significant ordinal relationship high MHL profile < moderate MHL profile < low MHL profile across all three measures. Comparisons between the high and low MHL profiles yielded medium effect sizes for anxiety (Cohen’s d = 0.453) and depression (Cohen’s d = 0.490), but a small effect for perceived stress (Cohen’s d = 0.172). In contrast, differences between the high and moderate profiles were uniformly small, with Cohen’s d values ranging from 0.223 to 0.352.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first study to use LPA to identify distinct MHL profiles among young Chinese male soldiers. The results demonstrated that these profiles varied significantly in terms of demographic and psychosocial characteristics, perceived stress, anxiety, and depression. The findings provide new insights for mental health intervention strategies aimed at addressing mental health challenges in young soldiers.

This study identified three distinct MHL profiles in young soldiers: high (55.73%), moderate (25.95%), and low (18.32%). Further analysis revealed that the young male soldiers with high MHL profiles exhibited relatively high levels of knowledge (0.60 ± 0.287), beliefs (0.42 ± 0.296), and resources (1.00 ± 0.000). These outstanding scores across all three dimensions are founded upon the Chinese military’s robust mental health support system, which provides structured education, psychological services, and professional teams. This institutional framework is further strengthened by the considerable attention and resources that military units allocate to soldiers’ psychological well-being [42–44].

Those with a moderate MHL profile had moderate levels of knowledge (0.47 ± 0.282) and resources (0.67 ± 0.116), but the lowest level of beliefs (0.35 ± 0.293). This pattern reveals a distinct bottleneck, where belief scores lag significantly behind knowledge and resource. The discrepancy may be related to a blockage in the translation from knowledge to belief, according to the Knowledge-Attitude-Practice model, which posits that deficits in mental health knowledge may create a bottleneck in the translation to positive beliefs, resulting in inadequate utilization of mental health resources among this group [2, 45]. This finding suggests that interventions aimed at improving MHL among young soldiers should emphasize the importance and practical value of mental health knowledge, as well as fostering positive beliefs to preserve psychological well-being.

In contrast, individuals with a low MHL profile exhibited the lowest scores for knowledge (0.39 ± 0.298), beliefs (0.24 ± 0.278), and particularly resources (0.08 ± 0.115). These consistently low scores likely reflect the prevalent stigma surrounding mental health help-seeking and negative attitudes toward psychological services within the Chinese military population [46]. The literature suggests that military culture, which emphasizes traits such as resilience and self-control, may inadvertently lead some personnel to internalize the perception of psychological distress as a personal weakness [47–49]. This perception can inhibit their willingness and behavior to actively seek professional help, a mechanism that aligns well with the “low beliefs and low resources” pattern in this profile. Future research should longitudinally examine the dynamics of this profile to inform the development of tailored mental health support interventions.

Three factors were significantly associated with MHL scores across the three profiles (P < 0.001): individuals aged 18–23 years, those with less than 2 years of military service, and engagement in active psychological learning. Recent exposure to stressful life events significantly influenced the high and medium MHL profiles (P < 0.05).

The findings revealed that individuals aged 18–23 years were significantly more likely to fall into the moderate or low rather than the high MHL profile. As young adults entering a demanding military environment, they often possess limited life experience and poorer mental health knowledge, which can impede their effective adaptation and accurate appraisal of psychological challenges. Furthermore, younger male soldiers are generally in better physical health than older soldiers and undergo rigorous psychological screening during the recruitment process [44]. These circumstances may contribute to a lower perceived need for MHL among this demographic. Therefore, MHL promotion efforts should prioritize this group by providing foundational and developmentally appropriate education.

Soldiers with less than two years of military service had significantly lower MHL scores than those with longer service. This may be attributable to their lack of experience, insufficient cognitive preparation, and the stigma surrounding help-seeking [47–49]. Together, these factors help explain why younger junior soldiers tend to exhibit reduced MHL. A tiered strategy, ranging from universal mental health promotion to targeted interventions, should be implemented based on the psychological risk profiles of new recruits. Simultaneously, prevention efforts could employ AI technology to facilitate scalable and efficient improvements in MHL [50].

Furthermore, young soldiers who actively pursued mental health knowledge were more likely to be categorized into the high MHL profile. Individuals who proactively seek health information tend to exhibit more positive attitudes and a stronger awareness of the importance of mental health promotion [10]. The incremental acquisition of mental health knowledge may enhance their ability to accurately recognize mental health issues and effectively address psychological challenges through appropriate channels. Consequently, military healthcare providers should intensify mental health education, elevate MHL awareness, and cultivate environments that systematically encourage knowledge acquisition among young military personnel.

Notably, stressful life events significantly differentiated between the high and moderate MHL profiles. One possible explanation is that the moderate MHL profile may possess fewer stress coping strategies, relying on a combination of positive and avoidance strategies. Alternatively, the positive strategies they use may be less effective, resulting in greater exposure to stressful events compared to the high MHL profile. Consequently, the moderate MHL profile likely exhibits greater sensitivity to such events. This distinctive pattern sheds light on how stress uniquely affects individuals within the moderate MHL profile.

As hypothesized, the distinct MHL profiles differed significantly in levels of anxiety, depression, and stress symptoms among young Chinese male service members. As shown in Table 5, the high MHL profile exhibited the lowest levels of perceived stress, anxiety, and depression. This may be explained by a combination of comprehensive knowledge, positive beliefs, and accessible resources that together are associated with more adaptive coping and help-seeking. In contrast, individuals with a low MHL profile exhibited the highest levels of perceived stress, anxiety, and depression. Their deficits across these dimensions may compound their risk of experiencing mental health symptoms, as limited knowledge impairs problem recognition, negative beliefs foster self-stigma, and poor access to resources impedes help-seeking. Together, these factors can create a vicious cycle of avoidance [45–47]. Notably, the moderate MHL profile presented an intermediate but elevated level of perceived stress, anxiety, and depression, characterized by a “high-resource but low-belief” discrepancy. Despite having relatively high resource awareness, their skepticism or negative beliefs about mental health services may create a “belief bottleneck” that impedes the translation of knowledge and available resources into effective action [51]. Consequently, they rely on conflicted or avoidant coping strategies and experience heightened sensitivity to stressful life events, as reflected in their significantly higher stress exposure compared with the high MHL group (P < 0.05). Collectively, these findings show that distinct MHL profiles closely correlate with various mental health symptoms, highlighting the imperative for comprehensive and specific MHL interventions to address these interconnected issues.

The distinct MHL profiles identified in our study differed significantly in their characteristics, predictive factors, and psychological outcomes. This necessitates a shift in future mental health promotion efforts from a “one-size-fits-all” approach to precise and stratified interventions. First, interventions for those with a high MHL profile, who form the majority, should capitalize on their strengths by providing practical mental health training and enabling them to perform vital peer-support roles. For those with a moderate MHL profile, characterized by high resources but low beliefs, interventions should bridge the gap between the availability of support resources and subjective cognitive recognition. Military mental health workers can design targeted cognitive intervention modules integrated with specific scenarios, such as combat readiness drills and mission deployment stressors, to correct misconceptions about mental health problems, enhance the perceived necessity and effectiveness of seeking professional help, and guide soldiers to actively utilize internal military support systems (e.g., psychological counseling stations and Internet service platforms for military mental health) [52–54]. For individuals with a low MHL profile, characterized by comprehensive deficits, interventions must address the vicious cycle of stigma and resource inaccessibility inherent to military culture. Evidence suggests that education, peer support, and leadership engagement can effectively mitigate help-seeking barriers in military populations [53, 55]. Frontline squad leaders can serve as first-line mental health identifiers, equipped with standardized knowledge and skills. Pairing them with peers who have high mental health literacy would allow for the delivery of tailored, scenario-based education. This education can be seamlessly integrated with military readiness scenarios to address the generally low level of MHL within this group [52, 56].

In addition, the identified associated factors in this study, including age 18–23 years and less than 2 years of military service, clarify potential targets for intervention. The initial period of military service represents a critical window for MHL initiatives [44]. The stratified strategies outlined above should be implemented systematically during this period to prevent the consolidation of a low MHL profile and the accumulation of psychological risks. Currently, there is a gap in MHL interventions for young soldiers in China. It is imperative to develop and implement targeted, culturally adaptable intervention strategies in accordance with the distinct characteristics of this group to enhance the mental health of young soldiers more effectively.

Limitations

This study bridges the gap in the literature by using LPA to identify distinct patterns of MHL and associated factors among young Chinese male soldiers. However, it has several limitations that should be addressed in future studies. First, the participants were recruited through convenience sampling in a single geographic area and exhibited an uneven sex distribution, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to the broader military populations. Second, self-reported measures may introduce recall and social desirability biases. Future research should incorporate population-based designs, structured clinical interviews, and objective assessments to enhance validity. Third, while significant differences in perceived stress, anxiety, and depression were identified across MHL profiles, the cross-sectional design precluded causal inferences regarding the underlying mechanisms. Therefore, the mechanisms underlying the relationships between mental health symptoms and the identified MHL profiles may vary, necessitating exploration in future studies. Fourth, although this study examined mental health literacy profiles and associated factors among young male soldiers, it did not include variables such as resilience, social support, and help-seeking [53–56]. Future research could employ structural equation modeling to construct a more comprehensive framework for these latent variables.

Conclusion

This study is the first to delineate three distinctive MHL profiles among young Chinese male soldiers: high, moderate, and low. The key factors associated with each profile were age 18–23 years, less than 2 years of military service, engagement in active psychological learning, and levels of perceived stress, anxiety, and depression. Additionally, recent exposure to stressful life events was found to significantly influence the high and moderate MHL profiles. To promote soldiers’ mental health, intervention strategies concentrating on MHL should be tailored according to the specific characteristics of each profile.
