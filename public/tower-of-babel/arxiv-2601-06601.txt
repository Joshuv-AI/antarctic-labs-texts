Abstract
. Consider an ( n + 1 ) (n+1) -dimensional circular cone. Using a calibration argument, we prove that if n ≥ 4 n\geq 4 and the aperture of the cone is sufficiently large, the intersection of the cone with an axial hyperplane is area-minimizing with respect to free-boundary variations inside the cone.

49Q05, 49Q10, 35R35

1.
Introduction

Consider a volume
v>0
of liquid inside a container, here identified with a Lipschitz open set
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
. According to Gauss’ formulation, in the absence of gravity, the configuration
E
assumed by the liquid minimizes the free-energy functional

(1.1)

P(F;\Omega)+\gamma\,P(F;\partial\Omega)

among all sets
F\subset\Omega
such that
P(F;\Omega)<+\infty
and satisfying the volume constraint
|F|=v
.
Here,
\gamma\in[-1,1]
is the so-called relative adhesion coefficient between the liquid and
\partial\Omega
, and
P(F;U)
stands for the relative perimeter of
F
in a Borel set
U

(see
[
26
,
11
]
for more details).

From a mathematical point of view, given an open set
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
, a well-established internal regularity theory for minimizers of (
1.1
) is available in the literature. In fact, let
E
be a minimizer of (
1.1
) and indicate by
\partial^{\ast}E
its reduced boundary.
Letting
M:=\overline{\partial^{\ast}E\cap\Omega}
, one can show that
M\cap\Omega
, that is, the internal portion of
M
, is formed by the union of a smooth hypersurface and a singular set
\Sigma
whose Hausdorff dimension
\mathop{\rm dim}_{{\mathcal{H}}}\Sigma
does not exceed the critical value
n-7
, being in particular nonempty only when
n\geq 7
; see
[
6
]
and the references therein.

Much less is known regarding the boundary regularity for minimizers of (
1.1
), that is, the regularity of the free-boundary interface
M\cap\partial\Omega
. One of the first general regularity results, which holds for smooth containers
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
, is due to Taylor
[
35
]
, who proved that
M\cap\partial\Omega
does not exhibit singularities. This result has been generalized much later by De Philippis and Maggi in
[
6
]
for minimizers of anisotropic capillarity functionals. In the isotropic case, which includes the energy functional (
1.1
), their result ensures that, if
\partial\Omega
is
C^{1,1}
, then
M
is the union of a
C^{1,1/2}
hypersurface with boundary and a singular set
\Sigma
with the property that
\mathop{\rm dim}_{{\mathcal{H}}}(\Sigma\cap\partial\Omega)\leq n-3
. Further improvements on the Hausdorff dimension of
\Sigma\cap\partial\Omega
have been provided in
[
4
]
when
\gamma
is close to either
0
,
1
or
-1
. See also
[
32
]
for stability and rigidity properties of singular capillary cones arising in the capillarity setting.

Focusing now on the special case
\gamma=0
, corresponding to the relative perimeter in
\Omega
, something stronger regarding the regularity of
M\cap\partial\Omega
was proved by Grüter-Jost
[
15
]
and Grüter
[
14
,
13
]
in the setting of varifolds, under the assumption that
\partial\Omega
is
C^{2}
.
In particular, in
[
15
]
, the authors demonstrate that if a varifold
M
is close to a free-boundary hyperplane in
\Omega
, then
M
is
C^{1,\beta}
-graphical with respect to this hyperplane. Subsequently, in
[
13
]
, the author exploits the latter result to show that, as in the interior case, the Hausdorff dimension of the singular set of the free-boundary satisfies

\mathop{\rm dim}_{{\mathcal{H}}}(\Sigma\cap\partial\Omega)\leq n-7
.

The common denominator of the boundary regularity results mentioned above, both for
\gamma\neq 0
and
\gamma=0
, is that the container
\Omega
must be smooth. The literature concerning boundary regularity becomes sparser when one looks for results involving nonsmooth containers
\Omega
. In this situation, the main available studies typically consider specific nonsmooth domains or classes of domains. This is the case, for example, in the series of works
[
16
,
17
,
18
,
19
]
by Hildebrandt and Sauvigny, where the authors perform a thorough study of minimal surfaces in wedges (i.e., three-dimensional domains obtained as the intersection of two half-spaces); in particular, in
[
19
, p. 73]
, they prove a regularity result for free-boundary minimal graphs on a plane orthogonal to the wedge.

The latter result has been recently generalized by Edelen and Li in
[
8
]
.
In that work, they prove a boundary regularity theorem in the same spirit as Grüter-Jost
[
15
]
, holding for a varifold
M
under the assumption that
\Omega
is locally polyhedral and that
M
is close to a horizontal plane (for instance, if
\Omega=W^{2}\times\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
is a wedge, horizontal planes are those containing
W^{2}\times\{0\}
).
In the same work, Edelen and Li also prove that
\mathop{\rm dim}_{{\mathcal{H}}}(\Sigma\cap\partial\Omega)\leq n-2
, with a sharper estimate holding when the dihedral angles of their cone models are nonobtuse; see
[
8
, Theorem 1.2]
.
In the setting of their polyhedral domains, the singular set
\Sigma
is defined as the complement of the points around which the interface
M
is the graph of a
C^{1,\beta}
function over a horizontal plane.

In this paper, we prove that in dimensions
n\geq 4
and for circular cones with sufficiently large aperture, axial hyperplanes are area-minimizing with respect to free-boundary variations.
This result fits naturally into some very recent contributions by the author in collaboration with Leonardi
[
25
,
23
]
. In the latter, we establish a boundary monotonicity formula that holds for almost minimizers of the relative perimeter under minimal regularity assumptions on
\partial\Omega
, and we then employ this formula to prove a minimizing-cone property for a blow-up sequence at a boundary point of
\Omega
.
In
[
25
]
, the authors prove a Vertex-skipping theorem, which states that the boundary of any almost minimizer of the relative perimeter
E
in a convex domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
cannot contain isolated singularities (say, vertices) of
\partial\Omega
.
We highlight that, after a dimension-reduction argument, this result combined with
[
8
, Theorem 1.1]
implies the bound
\mathop{\rm dim}_{{\mathcal{H}}}(\Sigma\cap\partial\Omega)\leq n-3
, improving the estimate given in
[
8
, Theorem 1.2 (1)]
.
The study of the behavior of
E
near a singularity of the container is also relevant from the perspective of free-boundary minimal surfaces, since the interface

M=\overline{\partial^{\ast}E\cap\Omega}

is a free-boundary minimal hypersurface in
\Omega
. We refer to
[
3
]
and the references therein for background on free-boundary minimal surfaces.

In this framework, the main result proved here provides a counterexample to the validity of Vertex-skipping results in higher dimensions.
The question of the validity of the Vertex-skipping for
n\geq 3
was already tackled in
[
24
]
. In that work, using a suitable Lipschitz flow of deformations for a free-boundary surface in a Lipschitz container
\Omega
, which in particular allows
\partial\Omega
(including its singular set) to move, we establish a stability result for axial hyperplanes in circular cones, stated as follows.
Given an integer
n\geq 2
, and for
\lambda>0
, consider the circular cone

\Omega_{\lambda}:=\left\{(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\,:\,t>\lambda\sqrt{x_{1}^{2}+\dots+x_{n}^{2}}\,\right\}.

Note that the opening angle
\alpha_{\lambda}\in(0,\pi)
of
\Omega_{\lambda}
is related with
\lambda
by
\lambda=\cot(\alpha_{\lambda}/2)
. Then, if
n=2
, a hyperplane of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
containing the axis of the cone (up to rotations,
x_{1}=0
) is unstable in
\Omega_{\lambda}
for every
\lambda>0
. This is in accordance with the aforementioned Vertex-skipping theorem.
In partial contrast, when
n\geq 3
, we show that there exists a threshold
\lambda^{\ast}(n)>0
such that if
0<\lambda\leq\lambda^{\ast}(n)
, i.e., the circular cone has a sufficiently large opening angle
\alpha\geq\alpha_{\lambda^{\star}(n)}
, the axial hyperplane is stable in
\Omega_{\lambda}
, whereas for
\lambda>\lambda^{\ast}(n)
it is unstable.
In this paper, we intend to analyze whether, for
n\geq 3
and
\lambda\leq\lambda^{\ast}(n)
, an axial hyperplane is merely stable or even area-minimizing in
\Omega_{\lambda}
. We will provide a basically positive answer for
n\geq 4
.

We say that a measurable set
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
has locally finite perimeter provided
P(E;U)<+\infty
for every
U\subset\!\subset\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
, see
[
12
,
26
]
. For
(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
and
r>0
, we denote by
B_{r}(x,t)
the ball of radius
r
centered at
(x,t)
(with
B_{r}:=B_{r}(0,0)
).
Our main result is the following:

Theorem 1.1
.

For
n\geq 4
, consider the threshold parameter

(1.2)

\bar{\lambda}(n):=\dfrac{1}{2}\dfrac{n-3}{\sqrt{n-2}},

and assume that
0<\lambda\leq\bar{\lambda}(n)
. Let also

E:=\{(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\,:\,x_{1}>0\}.

Then
E
is a minimizer of the relative perimeter in
\Omega_{\lambda}
, that is, given a locally-finite perimeter set
F\subset\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
satisfying
E\Delta F\subset\!\subset B_{R}
, for some
R>0
, one has

P(E;\Omega_{\lambda}\cap B_{R})\leq P(F;\Omega_{\lambda}\cap B_{R}).

We emphasize that the above theorem provides a definitive counterexample to the validity of a Vertex-skipping theorem such as
[
25
, Theorem 1.1]
in higher dimensions, but only when
n\geq 4
. The case
n=3
is a limiting situation where, in view of
[
24
]
, the portion of hyperplane
H_{\lambda}:=\partial E\cap\Omega_{\lambda}
is stable with respect to free-boundary variations in
\Omega_{\lambda}
,
\lambda\geq\lambda^{\ast}
, but our minimality result, Theorem
1.1
, does not apply.
We also remark that, despite
\Omega_{\lambda}
not being a polyhedral cone, Theorem
1.1
appears to suggest that the dimensional bound for the singular set of the free-boundary proved in
[
8
, Theorem 1.2 (1)]
cannot be improved beyond the threshold
n-4
. In fact, when
n=4
and
\lambda\leq 1/2\sqrt{2}
, the result above implies that
\partial E\cap\partial\Omega_{\lambda}=\{0\}
, and so
0\in\Sigma\cap\partial\Omega_{\lambda}
.

The proof of Theorem
1.1
is based on a calibration argument.
The calibration method was originally introduced by Bombieri, De Giorgi, and Giusti in their celebrated work
[
2
]
to prove the minimality of Simon’s cone in
\mathbb{R}^{8}
. Since then, several authors have contributed by proposing new versions of the original method
[
21
,
10
,
29
,
5
,
7
]
or by developing various generalizations to different contexts, such as the Steiner tree problem
[
27
,
28
,
33
]
and the Mumford-Shah functional
[
1
,
30
]
.

Here, the calibration method is adapted to the free-boundary setting in the sense that we require the calibrating vector field
Z
to be tangent to
\partial\Omega_{\lambda}
. This turns out to be crucial for carrying out the calibration argument, as it allows us to exclude the contribution coming from the area of
(E\Delta F)\cap\partial\Omega_{\lambda}
.
We point out that the vector field
Z
will not be defined in the entire closed cone
\overline{\Omega}_{\lambda}
, but only outside a
2
-dimensional subset of
\overline{\Omega}_{\lambda}
. This will force us to perform an approximation argument to apply the Divergence Theorem on
(E\Delta F)\cap\Omega_{\lambda}
, see Section
5
.

The existence of the vector field
Z
will be shown in Section
4
, and its construction will be carried out in two steps. Let
S_{\lambda}:=\partial\Omega_{\lambda}\setminus\{0\}
, and denote by
e_{1},\dots,e_{n},e_{t}
the canonical basis of
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
.
We begin by calibrating the
(n-1)
-dimensional surface
S^{0}_{\lambda}:=H_{\lambda}\cap S_{\lambda}
in
S_{\lambda}
; that is, we build a divergence-free vector field
Y
, defined on a suitable subset
S_{\lambda}^{\prime}
of
S_{\lambda}
, such that
Y|_{S^{0}_{\lambda}}=e_{1}
and
|Y|\leq 1
.
In a slightly different formulation via differential forms, this was essentially done by Morgan in
[
31
]
, using ideas from Lawlor’s PhD thesis
[
20
]
.
Second, we extend the calibration obtained in the previous step to (a suitable portion of)
\Omega_{\lambda}
by vertical projection onto
S_{\lambda}
: we will show that, due to the structure of the metric on
S_{\lambda}
, the vector field
Z
obtained in this way is, in particular, divergence-free in its domain.

1.1.
Organization of the paper.

In Section
2
we provide some preliminary notation and facts concerning differentiable manifolds (especially Riemannian ones) and sets of finite perimeter. In Section
3
we introduce further notation and perform some basic computations needed in the subsequent parts of the paper. Then, Section
4
is devoted to the construction of the calibration
Z
, whose main properties are collected in Theorem
4.1
. Finally, in Section
5
we present the calibration argument; namely, we show how the existence of the vector field
Z
from Theorem
4.1
can be used to demonstrate Theorem
1.1
.

2.
Preliminary notions and basic notation

2.1.
Manifolds and
k
-forms.

Given a
n
-dimensional manifold
M
, we denote by
TM
,
T^{\ast}M
the tangent and cotangent bundles of
M
respectively. For any
k\geq 1
, we define the bundle
\bigwedge^{k}T^{\ast}M
as the disjoint union of the vector spaces
\bigwedge^{k}(T_{p}M)^{\ast}
(and we observe that
\bigwedge^{1}T^{\ast}M=T^{\ast}M
). When
k=0
, we identify
\bigwedge^{0}T^{\ast}M
with
C^{\infty}(M)
. A function
\omega:M\to\bigwedge^{k}T^{\ast}M
with the property that
\omega(p)\in\bigwedge^{k}(T_{p}M)^{\ast}
, for all
p\in M
, is called
k
-form. Given a local chart
\varphi=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
for
M
, we denote by
\partial_{i}
and
dx_{i}
, for
1\leq i\leq n
, the local frames induced by
\varphi
on
TM
and
T^{\ast}M
respectively.
We introduce the following multilinear algebra notation: given a basis
u_{1},\dots,u_{n}
of a
n
-dimensional vector space
V
and a multi-index
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{k})\in\mathbb{N}^{k}
with
1\leq\alpha_{1}<\dots<\alpha_{k}\leq n
, we set

u_{\alpha}=u_{\alpha_{1}}\wedge\dots\wedge u_{\alpha_{k}}.

Moreover, fixing
1\leq i<j\leq k
, we define

(2.1)

\begin{split}&\,\,\,u^{i}_{\alpha}=u_{\alpha_{1}}\wedge\dots\wedge u_{\alpha_{i-1}}\wedge u_{\alpha_{i+1}}\wedge\dots\wedge u_{\alpha_{k}}\\
u^{i,j}_{\alpha}=u_{\alpha_{1}}&\wedge\dots\wedge u_{\alpha_{i-1}}\wedge u_{\alpha_{i+1}}\wedge\dots\wedge u_{\alpha_{j-1}}\wedge u_{\alpha_{j+1}}\wedge\dots\wedge u_{\alpha_{k}}.\end{split}

Any
k
-form can be then represented by

\omega=\sum_{\underset{1\leq\alpha_{1}<\dots<\alpha_{k}\leq n}{\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{k})\in\mathbb{N}^{k}}}\omega_{\alpha}\,dx_{\alpha},

for suitable scalar functions
\omega_{\alpha}:M\to\mathbb{R}
.
We indicate by
\mathfrak{X}(M)
,
\mathcal{A}^{k}(M)
the space of the vector fields on
M
and the space of the
k
-forms on
M
, respectively.

2.2.
Riemannian manifolds.

Let
(M,g)
be a Riemannian manifold. Given two vectors
v,w\in T_{p}M
, we set
\langle v,w\rangle_{g}:=g_{p}(v,w)
,
|v|_{g}:=\sqrt{g_{p}(v,v)}
, with the convention that, if the subscript
g
is omitted, we are referring to the Euclidean scalar product.
The metric
g
can be expressed in terms of the local frame for
TM
, by introducing the
\mathbb{R}
-valued functions

g_{i,j}(p):=\langle\partial_{i}(p),\partial_{j}(p)\rangle_{g}\,,\qquad\text{for $1\leq i,j\leq n$.}

With a little abuse, we denote by
g
the symmetric, positive-definite matrix
g=(g_{i,j})_{1\leq i,j\leq n}
, while we indicate by
\tilde{g}=(\tilde{g}_{i,j})_{1\leq i,j\leq n}
the inverse of
g
.
The metric
g
on
M
can be extended to
\bigwedge^{k}T^{\ast}M
, for all
k\geq 1
. When
k=1
, we set

\langle dx_{i},dx_{j}\rangle_{g}:=\tilde{g}_{i,j}\,,\qquad\text{for $1\leq i,j\leq n$,}

and, when
k\geq 2
, given two multi-indices
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{k}),\beta=(\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{k})\in\mathbb{N}^{k}
, we define

(2.2)

\begin{split}\langle dx_{\alpha},dx_{\beta}\rangle_{g}&:=\det(\langle dx_{\alpha_{i}},dx_{\beta_{j}}\rangle_{g})_{1\leq i,j\leq k}\\
&=\det(\tilde{g}_{\alpha_{i},\beta_{j}})_{1\leq i,j\leq k}.\end{split}

These definitions can be then extended to
\bigwedge^{k}T^{\ast}M
by multi-linearity.
By employing the metric structure of a Riemannian manifold, one can establish an identification between the tangent and the cotangent bundles.
In local coordinates, this identification acts as follows: given a vector field
X=\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}\partial_{i}\in TM
one can consider the
1
-form
X^{\flat}
defined by

X^{\flat}=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}g_{i,j}X_{i}\,dx_{j}\,;

conversely, given a
1
-form
\omega=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\omega_{i}\,dx_{i}\in T^{\ast}M
, one defines the vector field

\omega^{\#}=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\tilde{g}_{i,j}\,\omega_{i}\,\partial_{j}\,.

Note that
|X^{\flat}|_{g}=|X|_{g}
,
|\omega^{\#}|_{g}=|\omega|_{g}
, and also
X^{\flat\#}=X
,
\omega^{\#\flat}=\omega
.

Let now
M
and
N
be Riemannian manifolds and consider a diffeomorphism
F:M\to N
. Given a vector field
X\in\mathfrak{X}(M)
, the push-forward of
X
with respect to
F
is a vector field on
N
defined by

F_{\ast}X(q)=dF_{F^{-1}(q)}(X(F^{-1}(q)))\,,\qquad\text{for all $q\in N$.}

We denote by
\div^{M}X
the divergence of a vector field
X
on a Riemannian manifold
M
,
and we omit the superscript
M
when referring to the Euclidean divergence. One can easily verify that, if
F
is an isometry,

(2.3)

\div^{N}F_{\ast}X\,(q)=\div^{M}X\,(F^{-1}(q)),\qquad\text{for every $q\in N$.}

We conclude by stating the following formula for the divergence on
M
: if
X
is expressed in local coordinates by
X=\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}\partial_{i}
, we have

(2.4)

\div^{M}X=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{\det g}}\,\sum_{i=1}^{n}\,\partial_{i}\left(\sqrt{\det g}\,X_{i}\right),

where
\det g
is the determinant of the matrix
g=(g_{i,j})_{1\leq i,j\leq n}
introduced above.

2.3.
The Hodge-
\star
operator.

Consider an oriented Riemannian manifold
(M,g)
and denote by
\nu
its volume form (in local coordinates,
\nu=\sqrt{\det g}\,dx_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge dx_{n}
). For any
k\in\mathbb{N}
, the Hodge-
\star
operator is a linear isometry
\star:\bigwedge^{k}T^{\ast}M\to\bigwedge^{n-k}T^{\ast}M
which is uniquely defined by the condition

(2.5)

\omega\wedge(\star\psi)=\langle\omega,\psi\rangle_{g}\,\nu,\qquad\text{for all $k$-forms $\omega$ and $\psi$.}

It can be shown that
\star\circ\star=\star^{2}
coincides with the multiplication by
(-1)^{k(n-k)}
, and that
\star\nu=1
.
In particular, when
k=n-1
, the operator
\star
allows to associate to every
(n-1)
-form
\omega\in\mathcal{A}^{n-1}(M)
a
1
-form
\star\omega\in\mathcal{A}^{1}(M)
.
Given a vector field
X\in\mathfrak{X}(M)
, denote by
i_{X}\nu\in\mathcal{A}^{n-1}(M)
the contraction between
X
and the volume form
\nu
. We have the following technical Lemma (see
[
22
, p. 52]
).

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
X\in\mathfrak{X}(M)
. The following identities hold:

(i)

\star X^{\flat}=i_{X}\nu
;

(ii)

\star d(i_{X}\nu)=\div^{M}X
.

2.4.
Locally finite perimeter sets under set operations

Given a measurable set
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
and
s\in[0,1]
, we define

E^{(s)}:=\left\{(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\,:\,\lim_{\rho\to 0^{+}}\dfrac{|E\cap B_{\rho}(x,t)|}{|B_{\rho}(x,t)|}=s\right\}.

If
E
is a locally finite perimeter set, we indicate by
\partial^{\ast}E
its reduced boundary and by
\nu_{E}
the inner unit normal to its reduced boundary
[
26
]
.
Assume now that
E
and
F
have locally finite perimeter. We denote by
Q(E,F)^{\pm}
the set

Q(E,F)^{\pm}:=\{(x,t)\in\partial^{\ast}E\cap\partial^{\ast}F\,:\,\nu_{E}(x,t)=\pm\nu_{F}(x,t)\}.

It is well known that also
E\cap F
and
E\setminus F
have locally finite perimeter and, up to
{\mathcal{H}}^{n}
-negligible sets, one has (see
[
26
, Theorem 16.3]
)

(2.6)

\displaystyle\partial^{\ast}(E\cap F)=\left(F^{(1)}\cap\partial^{\ast}E\right)\cup\left(E^{(1)}\cap\partial^{\ast}F\right)\cup Q(E,F)^{+}

(2.7)

\displaystyle\partial^{\ast}(E\setminus F)=\left(F^{(0)}\cap\partial^{\ast}E\right)\cup\left(E^{(1)}\cap\partial^{\ast}F\right)\cup Q(E,F)^{-},

and

(2.8)

\displaystyle\nu_{E\cap F}=\nu_{E}\mathbin{\vrule height=6.02773pt,depth=0.0pt,width=0.55974pt\vrule height=0.55974pt,depth=0.0pt,width=5.59721pt}\,F^{(1)}+\nu_{F}\mathbin{\vrule height=6.02773pt,depth=0.0pt,width=0.55974pt\vrule height=0.55974pt,depth=0.0pt,width=5.59721pt}\,E^{(1)}+\nu_{E}\mathbin{\vrule height=6.02773pt,depth=0.0pt,width=0.55974pt\vrule height=0.55974pt,depth=0.0pt,width=5.59721pt}\,Q(E,F)^{+}

(2.9)

\displaystyle\nu_{E\setminus F}=\nu_{E}\mathbin{\vrule height=6.02773pt,depth=0.0pt,width=0.55974pt\vrule height=0.55974pt,depth=0.0pt,width=5.59721pt}\,F^{(0)}-\nu_{F}\mathbin{\vrule height=6.02773pt,depth=0.0pt,width=0.55974pt\vrule height=0.55974pt,depth=0.0pt,width=5.59721pt}\,E^{(1)}+\nu_{E}\mathbin{\vrule height=6.02773pt,depth=0.0pt,width=0.55974pt\vrule height=0.55974pt,depth=0.0pt,width=5.59721pt}\,Q(E,F)^{-}.

3.
Further notation and preliminary computations

Let
n\geq 2
be an integer number. For
x^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
, we set
x=(x_{1},x^{\prime})
, and

r:=|x^{\prime}|=\sqrt{x_{2}^{2}+\dots+x_{n}^{2}}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\rho:=|x|=\sqrt{x_{1}^{2}+r^{2}}\,.

With this notation, the definition of
\Omega_{\lambda}
given in the Introduction becomes

\Omega_{\lambda}=\{(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\,:\,t>\lambda\,\rho\}.

We recall that
S_{\lambda}=\partial\Omega_{\lambda}\setminus\{0\}
and we consider the following subsets of
\Omega_{\lambda}
and
S_{\lambda}
, respectively:

\Omega_{\lambda}^{\prime}:=\{(x,t)\in\Omega_{\lambda}\,:\,x^{\prime}\neq 0\}\qquad\text{and}\qquad S_{\lambda}^{\prime}:=\{(x,t)\in S_{\lambda}\,:\,x^{\prime}\neq 0\}.

In other words,
\Omega_{\lambda}^{\prime}
(resp.
S_{\lambda}^{\prime}
) coincides with
\Omega_{\lambda}
(resp.
S_{\lambda}
) minus the
2
-plane
x^{\prime}=0
.
We observe that the hypersurface
S_{\lambda}
endowed with the Euclidean metric is a smooth Riemannian manifold. We consider the Riemannian manifold
M:=\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
equipped with the metric

(3.1)

g_{i,j}(x):=\delta_{i,j}+\lambda^{2}\,\dfrac{x_{i}\,x_{j}}{\rho^{2}},\qquad\text{for $x\in M$ and $1\leq i,j\leq n$.}

A direct computation yields the expression of the metric on the cotangent bundle

\tilde{g}_{i,j}(x)=\delta_{i,j}-\dfrac{\lambda^{2}}{1+\lambda^{2}}\,\dfrac{x_{i}\,x_{j}}{\rho^{2}},\qquad\text{for $x\in M$ and $1\leq i,j\leq n$.}

One can immediately check that
\mathcal{I}:M\to S_{\lambda}
,
\mathcal{I}(x):=(x,\lambda|x|)
, establishes an isometry between
S_{\lambda}
and
M
. Moreover,
\mathcal{I}
restricts to an isometry between
M^{0}:=\{x\in M\,:\,x_{1}=0\}
and
S^{0}_{\lambda}:=S_{\lambda}\cap H_{\lambda}
.
Consider the multi-index
\alpha=(2,\dots,n)
. Recalling notation (
2.1
), we introduce the
(n-2)
-form
\psi_{0}\in\mathcal{A}^{n-2}(M)
defined by

(3.2)

\psi_{0}(x):=\dfrac{\sqrt{1+\lambda^{2}}}{n-1}\,\sum_{i=2}^{n}\,(-1)^{i}\,x_{i}\,dx_{\alpha}^{i},\qquad\text{for $x\in M$.}

Additionally, denoting by
M^{\prime}:=\{x\in M\,:\,x^{\prime}\neq 0\}
,
we let

(3.3)

\theta:=\arctan(x_{1}/r)\in(-\pi/2,\pi/2),\qquad\text{for $x\in M^{\prime}$.}

In the following technical result we gather some useful identities.

Lemma 3.1
.

The following identities hold:

(3.4)

\displaystyle\hskip 73.97733pt\det g=1+\lambda^{2}

(3.5)

\displaystyle|d\rho|_{g}=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{1+\lambda^{2}}}\qquad|d\theta|_{g}=\dfrac{1}{\rho}\qquad\langle d\rho,d\theta\rangle_{g}=0.

(3.6)

\displaystyle\hskip 56.9055pt|\psi_{0}|_{g}=\dfrac{r}{n-1}\sqrt{1+\lambda^{2}}.

Proof.

Fix
x\in M
, and, for
1\leq i\leq n
, let

\partial_{i}=e_{i}+A_{i}\,e_{t}\qquad\text{where}\qquad A_{i}:=\lambda\,\dfrac{x_{i}}{|x|}\,.

Standard properties of the determinant guarantee that
\det g=|\partial_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge\partial_{n}|^{2}
. Define the multi-index
\beta=(1,\dots,n-1)
. We have

(3.7)

\displaystyle\partial_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge\partial_{n}

\displaystyle=e_{\beta}\wedge e_{n}+A_{n}\,e_{\beta}\wedge e_{t}+\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}(-1)^{n-1-i}A_{i}\,e^{i}_{\beta}\wedge e_{t}\wedge e_{n}.

Now the wedge products appearing in (
3.7
) are orthonormal each other, hence

|\partial_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge\partial_{n}|^{2}=1+\sum_{i=1}^{n}A_{i}^{2}=1+\lambda^{2},

and this proves (
3.4
).

Let us now show the validity of (
3.5
). We start observing that, by the definitions of
\rho
and
\theta
,

(3.8)

d\rho=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\dfrac{x_{i}}{\rho}\,dx_{i}\qquad\text{and}\qquad d\theta=\dfrac{1}{\rho^{2}}\left(r\,dx_{1}-\dfrac{x_{1}}{r}\sum_{i=2}^{n}x_{i}\,dx_{i}\right).

Hence we have

\begin{split}|d\rho|_{g}^{2}&=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\dfrac{x_{i}x_{j}}{\rho^{2}}\,\langle dx_{i}\,,\,dx_{j}\rangle_{g}\\
&=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\dfrac{x_{i}x_{j}}{\rho^{2}}\left(\delta_{i,j}-\dfrac{\lambda^{2}}{1+\lambda^{2}}\dfrac{x_{i}x_{j}}{\rho^{2}}\right)\\
&=\dfrac{1}{\rho^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{2}-\dfrac{\lambda^{2}}{\rho^{4}(1+\lambda^{2})}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{2}\right)\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}x_{j}^{2}\right)\\
&=\dfrac{1}{1+\lambda^{2}};\end{split}

\begin{split}|d\theta|_{g}^{2}&=\dfrac{1}{\rho^{4}}\left(r^{2}|dx_{1}|_{g}^{2}-2x_{1}\sum_{i=2}^{n}x_{i}\,\langle dx_{i}\,,\,dx_{1}\rangle_{g}+\dfrac{x_{1}^{2}}{r^{2}}\sum_{i,j=2}^{n}x_{i}x_{j}\,\langle dx_{i}\,,\,dx_{j}\rangle_{g}\right)\\
&=\dfrac{1}{\rho^{4}}\left(r^{2}\left(1-\dfrac{\lambda^{2}}{1+\lambda^{2}}\dfrac{x_{1}^{2}}{\rho^{2}}\right)+2x_{1}\sum_{i=2}^{n}\dfrac{\lambda^{2}}{1+\lambda^{2}}\dfrac{x_{1}x_{i}^{2}}{\rho^{2}}+x_{1}^{2}\left(1-\dfrac{\lambda^{2}}{1+\lambda^{2}}\dfrac{r^{2}}{\rho^{2}}\right)\right)\\
&=\dfrac{1}{\rho^{4}}\left(r^{2}+x_{1}^{2}\right)\\
&=\dfrac{1}{\rho^{2}};\end{split}

\begin{split}\langle d\rho\,,\,d\theta\rangle_{g}&=\dfrac{1}{\rho^{2}}\left\langle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\dfrac{x_{i}}{\rho}\,dx_{i}\,,\,r\,dx_{1}-\dfrac{x_{1}}{r}\sum_{i=2}^{n}x_{i}\,dx_{i}\right\rangle_{g}\\
&=\dfrac{1}{\rho^{2}}\left(\dfrac{r}{\rho}\,x_{1}\,|dx_{1}|_{g}^{2}+\left(\dfrac{r}{\rho}-\dfrac{x_{1}^{2}}{r\rho}\right)\sum_{i=2}^{n}x_{i}\,\langle dx_{1}\,,\,dx_{i}\rangle_{g}-\dfrac{x_{1}}{r\rho}\sum_{i,j=2}^{n}x_{i}x_{j}\,\langle dx_{i}\,,\,dx_{j}\rangle_{g}\right)\\
&=\dfrac{1}{\rho^{2}}\left(\dfrac{r}{\rho}\,x_{1}\left(1-\dfrac{\lambda^{2}}{1+\lambda^{2}}\dfrac{x_{1}^{2}}{\rho^{2}}\right)-\dfrac{\lambda^{2}}{1+\lambda^{2}}\left(\dfrac{r}{\rho}-\dfrac{x_{1}^{2}}{r\rho}\right)\sum_{i=2}^{n}\dfrac{x_{1}x_{i}}{\rho^{2}}-\dfrac{x_{1}}{\rho}\,r\left(1-\dfrac{\lambda^{2}}{1+\lambda^{2}}\dfrac{r^{2}}{\rho^{2}}\right)\right)\\
&=\dfrac{1}{\rho^{2}}\left(\dfrac{r}{\rho}x_{1}\left(1-\dfrac{\lambda^{2}}{1+\lambda^{2}}\dfrac{x_{1}^{2}}{\rho^{2}}-\right)-\dfrac{\lambda^{2}}{1+\lambda^{2}}\left(\dfrac{r}{\rho}-\dfrac{x_{1}^{2}}{r\rho}\right)x_{1}\,\dfrac{r^{2}}{\rho^{2}}-\dfrac{x_{1}}{\rho}\,r\left(1-\dfrac{\lambda^{2}}{1+\lambda^{2}}\dfrac{r^{2}}{\rho^{2}}\right)\right)\\[5.69046pt]
&=0.\end{split}

Ultimately, we prove (
3.6
).
We have

(3.9)

\begin{split}|\psi_{0}|_{g}^{2}&=\langle\psi_{0},\psi_{0}\rangle_{g}\\
&=\dfrac{1+\lambda^{2}}{(n-1)^{2}}\left\langle\sum_{i=2}^{n}(-1)^{i}\,x_{i}\,dx_{\alpha}^{i}\,,\sum_{j=2}^{n}(-1)^{j}\,x_{j}\,dx_{\alpha}^{j}\right\rangle_{g}\\
&=\dfrac{1+\lambda^{2}}{(n-1)^{2}}\sum_{i,j=2}^{n}(-1)^{i+j}\,x_{i}\,x_{j}\,\langle dx_{\alpha}^{i}\,,dx_{\alpha}^{j}\rangle_{g}.\end{split}

Denote by
\tilde{g}_{1}
the
(n-1)\times(n-1)
submatrix of
\tilde{g}
obtained by cancelling the first row and the first column of
\tilde{g}
.
By the definition of the metric on
\bigwedge^{k}T^{\ast}M
given in (
2.2
) (in this case
k=n-2
) and the properties of the adjugate matrix (see
[
34
, p. 73]
), we have

(3.10)

\begin{split}\langle dx_{\alpha}^{i}\,,dx_{\alpha}^{j}\rangle_{g}=(-1)^{i+j}\,(\tilde{g}_{1}^{-1})_{i,j}\,\det(\tilde{g}_{1}).\end{split}

A mere computation yields

(3.11)

(\tilde{g}_{1}^{-1})_{i,j}=\delta_{i,j}+\dfrac{\lambda^{2}}{\rho^{2}+\lambda^{2}\,x_{1}^{2}}\,x_{i}\,x_{j},

The same calculation performed above to compute
\det g
guarantees also that

(3.12)

\det(\tilde{g}_{1})^{-1}=\det(\tilde{g}_{1}^{-1})=1+\dfrac{\lambda^{2}\,r^{2}}{\rho^{2}+\lambda^{2}\,x_{1}^{2}}.

Consequently, inserting (
3.12
) and (
3.11
) inside (
3.10
), by (
3.9
), we infer that

\begin{split}|\psi_{0}|_{g}^{2}&=\dfrac{1+\lambda^{2}}{n-1}\sum_{i,j=2}^{n}\,x_{i}\,x_{j}\,\left(\delta_{i,j}+\dfrac{\lambda^{2}}{\rho^{2}+\lambda^{2}\,x_{1}^{2}}\,x_{i}\,x_{j}\right)\left(1+\dfrac{\lambda^{2}\,r^{2}}{\rho^{2}+\lambda^{2}\,x_{1}^{2}}\right)^{-1}\\
&=\dfrac{1+\lambda^{2}}{n-1}\,r^{2},\end{split}

that is precisely (
3.6
).
∎

Construction of the calibration

The main result of this section is the following Theorem.

Theorem 4.1
.

For
n\geq 4
, consider the threshold
\bar{\lambda}(n)
defined in (
1.2
).
Then, if

, for all

;

(v)

in

.

A key passage for the proof of Theorem

-dimensional surface

inside

. This is the subject of the next result.

Theorem 4.2

For

, let

. Then there exists a vector field

satisfying the following conditions:

(i)

|Y(p)|\leq 1
, for all

;

(ii)

Y(p)=e_{1}
, for all

;

(iii)

on

The proof of Theorem
4.2
can be in part found in
[

.
For the sake of clarity, we provide a complete proof here.

Proof.

Let
\alpha
be the multi-index
(2,\dots,n)
, and consider the
(n-1)
-form
\omega_{0}=\sqrt{1+\lambda^{2}}\,dx_{\alpha}
. A direct computation shows that, in
M^{\prime}
,

(4.1)

\omega_{0}=d\psi_{0}=\dfrac{n-1}{r}\,dr\wedge\psi_{0},

where
\psi_{0}
is as in (
3.2
). Additionally, set
u:=x_{1}/r
. Given a differentiable function
h
, we define

\psi_{h}(x):=h(u)\,\psi_{0}(x)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\omega_{h}(x):=d\psi_{h}(x),\qquad\text{for $x\in M^{\prime}$.}

By the properties of the exterior derivative, we have

(4.2)

\omega_{h}=dh\wedge\psi_{0}+h\,\omega_{0}\,,

where

(4.3)

dh=\dfrac{h^{\prime}(u)}{r}\,dx_{1}-\dfrac{x_{1}}{r^{2}}\,h^{\prime}(u)\,dr\,.

Putting together (
4.2
), (
4.3
) and using (
4.1
), we infer that

\displaystyle\omega_{h}(x)

\displaystyle=\left(\dfrac{h^{\prime}(u)}{r}\,dx_{1}-\dfrac{x_{1}}{r^{2}}\,h^{\prime}(u)\,dr\right)\wedge\psi_{0}(x)+h(u)\,\omega_{0}(x)

(4.4)

\displaystyle=\left[\dfrac{r}{n-1}\left(\dfrac{h^{\prime}(u)}{r}\,dx_{1}-\dfrac{x_{1}}{r^{2}}\,h^{\prime}(u)\,dr\right)+h(u)\,dr\right]\wedge\left(\dfrac{n-1}{r}\,\psi_{0}(x)\right)

\displaystyle=\left[\left(h(u)-\dfrac{x_{1}}{(n-1)\,r}h^{\prime}(u)\right)dr+\dfrac{h^{\prime}(u)}{n-1}\,dx_{1}\right]\wedge\left(\dfrac{n-1}{r}\,\psi_{0}(x)\right)\,.

Step 1.

We look for a continuously differentiable function
h:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
such that:

(4.5)

h(0)=1,\qquad h^{\prime}(0)=0\qquad\text{and}\qquad|\omega_{h}(x)|_{g}\leq 1\quad\text{for all $x\in M^{\prime}$.}

Applying the Cauchy-Schwartz inequality for wedge products (see
[
9
, pag. 32]
, and note that
1
-forms are always simple), by (
4
), we obtain

|\omega_{h}(x)|_{g}\leq\left|\left(h(u)-\dfrac{x_{1}}{(n-1)r}h^{\prime}(u)\right)dr+\dfrac{h^{\prime}(u)}{n-1}dx_{1}\right|_{g}\cdot\,\,\left|\dfrac{n-1}{r}\psi_{0}(x)\right|_{g}\,.

Thanks to (
3.6
), to have
|\omega_{h}|_{g}\leq 1
, it suffices to impose

(4.6)

\left|\left(h(u)-\dfrac{x_{1}}{(n-1)\,r}h^{\prime}(u)\right)dr+\dfrac{h^{\prime}(u)}{n-1}\,dx_{1}\right|_{g}^{2}\leq\dfrac{1}{1+\lambda^{2}}\,.

We now observe that, taking
\theta\in(-\pi/2,\pi/2)
as in (
3.3
), one has

u=\tan\theta,\qquad x_{1}=\rho\sin\theta\qquad\text{and}\qquad r=\rho\cos\theta.

The identities (
3.5
) permit us to rewrite (
4.6
) in the following way:

(4.7)

h(u)^{2}+(1+\lambda^{2})\left[\dfrac{(1+u^{2})\,h^{\prime}(u)}{n-1}-u\,h(u)\right]^{2}\leq 1+u^{2}\,.

Thus we are reduced to find a solution
h
to the Cauchy problem for the differential inequality (
4.7
) with initial conditions
h(0)=1
,
h^{\prime}(0)=0
.
In order to solve (
4.7
), we apply the ansatz

h(u)=\dfrac{\cos(\beta(\arctan u))}{\cos(\arctan u)}=\dfrac{\cos(\beta(\theta))}{\cos\theta}\,,

where
\beta:(-\pi/2,\pi/2)\to(-\pi/2,\pi/2)
is a continuously differentiable function with
\beta(0)=0
. By this approach,
h(0)=1
. Moreover, we have

(4.8)

h^{\prime}(u)=\dfrac{-\sin(\beta(\theta))\beta^{\prime}(\theta)\cos\theta+\cos(\beta(\theta))\sin\theta}{(1+u^{2})\cos^{2}(\theta)}\,,

and then, in particular,
h^{\prime}(0)=0
. We can now rewrite the differential inequality (
4.7
) in terms of
\beta
and
\theta
. Thanks to (
4.8
), we have

\displaystyle\dfrac{(1+u^{2})\,h^{\prime}(u)}{n-1}-u\,h(u)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{-\sin(\beta(\theta))\beta^{\prime}(\theta)\cos\theta+\cos(\beta(\theta))\sin\theta}{(n-1)\cos^{2}(\theta)}-\tan(\theta)\dfrac{\cos(\beta(\theta))}{\cos\theta}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{n-1}\left(-\beta^{\prime}(\theta)\dfrac{\sin(\beta(\theta))}{\cos\theta}+\dfrac{\tan\theta}{\tan(\beta(\theta))}\dfrac{\sin(\beta(\theta))}{\cos\theta}\right)-\dfrac{\tan\theta}{\tan(\beta(\theta))}\dfrac{\sin(\beta(\theta))}{\cos\theta}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{\sin(\beta(\theta))}{\cos\theta}\left(-\dfrac{\beta^{\prime}(\theta)}{n-1}+\dfrac{2-n}{n-1}\dfrac{\tan\theta}{\tan(\beta(\theta))}\right)\,.

Thus, by the definition of
h
and the identity
\cos^{-2}(\theta)=1+u^{2}
, (
4.7
) becomes

(4.9)

\left|\beta^{\prime}(\theta)+(n-2)\dfrac{\tan\theta}{\tan(\beta(\theta))}\right|\leq\dfrac{n-1}{\sqrt{1+\lambda^{2}}},\qquad\text{for $\theta\in(-\pi/2,\pi/2)$.}

We want to find a solution
\beta
to the above differential inequality with initial condition
\beta(0)=0
. We claim
that such a solution can be found among the functions
\beta_{\gamma}:(-\pi/2,\pi/2)\to(-\pi/2,\pi/2)
defined by

\beta_{\gamma}(\theta):=\mathop{\mathrm{sgn}}(\theta)\arccos\left(\cos^{1+\gamma}(\theta)\right)\qquad\text{for $\gamma>0$,}

i.e., that there exists
\gamma>0
such that
\beta_{\gamma}
satisfies (
4.9
) and
\beta_{\gamma}(0)=0
.
Note that, for any
\gamma>0
,
\beta_{\gamma}(0)=0
. Let us rewrite (
4.9
) for
\beta_{\gamma}
.
We observe that
\beta_{\gamma}
is differentiable, and

\beta_{\gamma}^{\prime}(\theta)=\mathop{\mathrm{sgn}}(\theta)\dfrac{(1+\gamma)\cos^{\gamma}\theta\,\sin\theta}{\sqrt{1-\cos^{2(1+\gamma)}(\theta)}}\,.

Moreover, we have

\dfrac{1}{\tan(\beta_{\gamma}(\theta))}=\dfrac{\cos(\beta_{\gamma}(\theta))}{\mathop{\mathrm{sgn}}(\beta_{\gamma}(\theta))\sqrt{1-\cos^{2}(\beta_{\gamma}(\theta))}}=\dfrac{\mathop{\mathrm{sgn}}(\theta)\cos^{1+\gamma}(\theta)}{\sqrt{1-\cos^{2(1+\gamma)}(\theta)}},

and consequently,

\left|\beta^{\prime}(\theta)+(n-2)\dfrac{\tan\theta}{\tan(\beta(\theta))}\right|=(n-1+\gamma)\left|\dfrac{\tan\theta}{\tan(\beta_{\gamma}(\theta))}\right|.

Hence, for this family of functions
\beta_{\gamma}(\theta)
, (
4.9
) transforms into

(4.10)

\left(1+\dfrac{\gamma}{n-1}\right)^{2}\tan^{2}\theta\leq\dfrac{\tan^{2}(\beta_{\gamma}(\theta))}{1+\lambda^{2}}\,,\qquad\text{for $\theta\in(-\pi/2,\pi/2)$.}

Let us set
z:=\cos^{-2}(\theta)
. In this way,
\cos^{-2}(\beta_{\gamma}(\theta))=z^{1+\gamma}
, and condition (
4.10
) becomes

(4.11)

\left(1+\dfrac{\gamma}{n-1}\right)^{2}\leq\dfrac{1}{1+\lambda^{2}}\,\dfrac{z^{1+\gamma}-1}{z-1}\,,\qquad\text{for $z\geq 1$.}

Let
w(z):=(z^{1+\gamma}-1)/(z-1)
. For
z\geq 1
,
w
is monotonically increasing, hence it suffices to test condition (
4.11
) only as
z\to 1^{+}
. Since
\lim_{z\to 1^{+}}w(z)=1+\gamma
, we get the condition

(4.12)

\left(1+\dfrac{\gamma}{n-1}\right)^{2}-\dfrac{1+\gamma}{1+\lambda^{2}}\,\leq\,0\,.

In particular, (
4.12
) implies (
4.11
). We now observe that the function on the LHS of (
4.12
) has a global minimizer in

\bar{\gamma}:=(n-1)\left(\dfrac{n-1}{2(1+\lambda^{2})}-1\right).

Let
\gamma=\bar{\gamma}
. If (
4.12
) is satisfied, by construction, the function
\beta=\beta_{\gamma}
solves (
4.9
) and
\beta(0)=0
.
A mere computation gives that (
4.12
) holds for
\gamma=\bar{\gamma}
if and only if

(4.13)

n\geq 4\qquad\text{and}\qquad 0<\lambda\leq\bar{\lambda}(n):=\dfrac{1}{2}\dfrac{n-3}{\sqrt{n-2}}\,,

and this concludes the proof of Step 1.
In particular, when (
4.13
) is fulfilled, the function
h
for which (
4.5
) holds true is given by

\displaystyle h(u)

\displaystyle=\cos^{\gamma}(\arctan u),\qquad\text{where}\qquad\gamma=(n-1)\left(\dfrac{n-1}{2(1+\lambda^{2})}-1\right).

Notice that
h
is smooth, and so
\omega_{h}\in\mathcal{A}^{n-1}(M)
is smooth.

Step 2.
For
x\in M^{\prime}
, define

X=(-1)^{n-1}(\star\,\omega_{h})^{\#}\in\mathfrak{X}(M^{\prime})\qquad\text{and}\qquad Y=\mathcal{I}_{\ast}X\in\mathfrak{X}(S_{\lambda}^{\prime}).

We now show that
Y
satisfies (i), (ii) and (iii).
By construction,
|\omega_{h}|_{g}\leq 1
, and since
\star
and
\mathcal{I}
are isometries, we trivially infer that
|Y(p)|\leq 1
, for all
p\in S_{\lambda}^{\prime}
, that is (i).
Moreover, by (
4.5
) and (
4
), since
u=0
as
x_{1}=0
, we have
\omega_{h}=\omega_{0}=\sqrt{1+\lambda^{2}}\,dx_{2}\wedge\dots\wedge dx_{n}
on
M^{0}
. It is easy to check that, by (
2.5
) (note that
\nu=\sqrt{\det g}\,dx_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge dx_{n}=\sqrt{1+\lambda^{2}}\,dx_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge dx_{n}
), the following equality holds

\star\left(\sqrt{1+\lambda^{2}}\,dx_{2}\wedge\dots\wedge dx_{n}\right)=(-1)^{n-1}e_{1}^{\flat},

hence
X(x)=e_{1}
, for every
x\in M^{0}
. By the definition of
\mathcal{I}
, we have
Y(p)=e_{1}
, for all
p\in S^{0}_{\lambda}
, that is precisely (ii).
Finally, let us prove (iii). The smoothness of
Y
immediately follows from that of
\omega_{h}
. Now, by (i) of Lemma
2.1
,
\star X^{\flat}=i_{X}\nu
,
hence we have

i_{X}\nu=(-1)^{n-1}\star^{2}\omega_{h}=\omega_{h}.

By (ii) of Lemma
2.1
and the definition of
\omega_{h}
, we deduce that

\div^{M}X=\star d\omega_{h}=\star dd\psi_{h}=0,

and then (iii) immediately follows from (
2.3
).
∎

Let us now proceed with the proof of Theorem
4.1
. The idea is to extend the vector field
Y
of Theorem
4.2
to
\Omega_{\lambda}^{\prime}
by vertically projecting onto
S_{\lambda}^{\prime}
. This operation will produce a divergence-free vector field in
\Omega_{\lambda}^{\prime}
.

Proof (of Theorem
4.1
).

Consider the vector field

Z(x,t):=Y(x,\lambda|x|)\,.

Since
Y
is defined on
S_{\lambda}^{\prime}
, it is clear that
Z
is defined in
\Omega_{\lambda}^{\prime}
. By construction, (i) of Theorem
4.1
holds, while the statements (ii), (iii), (iv) immediately follow from the properties of
Y
. It remains to demonstrate (v). For sure,
Z
is smooth because
Y
is. We need to show that
\div Z=0
in
\Omega_{\lambda}^{\prime}
. Consider the (non-orthonormal) frame
\partial_{1},\dots,\partial_{n},\partial_{t}
for
T\Omega_{\lambda}^{\prime}
defined by

\partial_{i}(x,t)=\partial_{i}(x)=e_{i}+\lambda\,\dfrac{x_{i}}{|x|}\,e_{t}\qquad\text{for $1\leq i\leq n$}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\partial_{t}=e_{t}.

In addition, let
\hat{g}
the matrix whose entries are the scalar products
\langle\partial_{i},\partial_{j}\rangle
,
\langle\partial_{i},\partial_{t}\rangle
, for
1\leq i,j\leq n
. By (
3.7
), the determinant of
\hat{g}
satisfies

\displaystyle\det\hat{g}

\displaystyle=|\partial_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge\partial_{n}\wedge e_{t}|^{2}

\displaystyle=|e_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge e_{n}\wedge e_{t}|^{2}

\displaystyle=1,

and, in particular, is constant. Represent now
Y
and
Z
in coordinates with respect to
\partial_{1},\dots,\partial_{n}
and
\partial_{1},\dots,\partial_{n},\partial_{t}
respectively. Then, for suitable
Y_{i}\in C^{\infty}(S_{\lambda}^{\prime})
,
Z_{i},Z_{t}\in C^{\infty}(\Omega_{\lambda}^{\prime})
,

Y(x,\lambda|x|)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}Y_{i}(x,\lambda|x|)\partial_{i}(x)\qquad\text{and}\qquad Z(x,t)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}Z_{i}(x,t)\partial_{i}(x)+Z_{t}(x,t)\partial_{t},

for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
t\geq\lambda|x|
. By construction, we have

Z_{i}(x,t)=Y_{i}(x,\lambda|x|)\quad\text{for any $1\leq i\leq n$}\qquad\text{and}\qquad Z_{t}\equiv 0.

Consequently, since by (
3.4
) also
\det g=1+\lambda^{2}
is constant, (
2.4
) ensures that

\div Z(x,t)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\partial_{i}Z_{i}(x,t)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\partial_{i}Y_{i}(x,\lambda|x|)=\div^{S_{\lambda}^{\prime}}Y(x,\lambda|x|)=0.

This closes the proof.
∎

5.
Minimality of
E

In this section, we demonstrate Theorem
1.1
. The idea is to apply the Divergence Theorem to the vector field
Z
in the intersection between
E\Delta F
and a sequence of sets invading
\Omega_{\lambda}^{\prime}\subset\Omega_{\lambda}
. Subsequently, since
Z
is tangent to
S_{\lambda}^{\prime}
, by an approximation argument, we will be able to infer the minimality condition of Theorem
1.1
.

Proof (of Theorem
1.1
).

Up to rescaling, we can assume
R=1
. For
\varepsilon>0
, let

A^{\varepsilon}:=\{(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\,:\,|x^{\prime}|\leq\varepsilon\}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\Omega_{\lambda}^{\varepsilon}:=\Omega_{\lambda}\setminus A^{\varepsilon}.

We observe that, for every
\varepsilon>0
,
\Omega_{\lambda}^{\varepsilon}\subset\Omega_{\lambda}^{\prime}
. When
0<\lambda\leq\bar{\lambda}(n)
, we can consider the vector field
Z:\Omega_{\lambda}^{\prime}\to\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
of Theorem
4.1
. By the Divergence Theorem (see
[
26
]
) and since
Z
is divergence-free, we have

(5.1)

0=\int_{(F\setminus E)\cap\Omega_{\lambda}^{\varepsilon}}\div Z=\int_{\partial^{\ast}((F\setminus E)\cap\Omega_{\lambda}^{\varepsilon})}\left\langle Z\,,\,\nu_{(F\setminus E)\cap\Omega_{\lambda}^{\varepsilon}}\right\rangle\,d{\mathcal{H}}^{n-1}.

Owing to (
2.6
), there holds

\partial^{\ast}((F\setminus E)\cap\Omega_{\lambda}^{\varepsilon})=\left(\Omega_{\lambda}^{\varepsilon}\cap\partial^{\ast}(F\setminus E)\right)\cup\left((F\setminus E)^{(1)}\cap\partial\Omega_{\lambda}^{\varepsilon}\right)\cup Q\left(F\setminus E,\Omega_{\lambda}^{\varepsilon}\right)^{+},

and this in combination with (
5.1
) and (
2.8
) ensures that

(5.2)

\begin{split}0=\int_{(F\setminus E)\cap\Omega_{\lambda}^{\varepsilon}}\div Z&=\int_{\Omega_{\lambda}^{\varepsilon}\cap\partial^{\ast}(F\setminus E)}\left\langle Z\,,\,\nu_{F\setminus E}\right\rangle\,d{\mathcal{H}}^{n-1}+\\
&\qquad\qquad+\int_{\left((F\setminus E)^{(1)}\cap\partial\Omega_{\lambda}^{\varepsilon}\right)\,\cup\,Q\left(F\setminus E,\Omega_{\lambda}^{\varepsilon}\right)^{+}}\left\langle Z\,,\,\nu_{\Omega_{\lambda}^{\varepsilon}}\right\rangle\,d{\mathcal{H}}^{n-1}\\
&=(I)+(II).\end{split}

By (
2.7
), because
Z=e_{1}=\nu_{E}
on
\partial^{\ast}E\cap\Omega_{\lambda}=H_{\lambda}
, one has

(5.3)

\begin{split}(I)&=\int_{\Omega_{\lambda}^{\varepsilon}\cap E^{(0)}\cap\partial^{\ast}F}\left\langle Z\,,\,\nu_{F}\right\rangle\,d{\mathcal{H}}^{n-1}-\int_{\Omega_{\lambda}^{\varepsilon}\cap F^{(1)}\cap\partial^{\ast}E}\left\langle Z\,,\,\nu_{E}\right\rangle\,d{\mathcal{H}}^{n-1}-\int_{\Omega_{\lambda}^{\varepsilon}\,\cap\,Q(E,F)^{-}}\left\langle Z\,,\,\nu_{E}\right\rangle\,d{\mathcal{H}}^{n-1}\\[5.69046pt]
&\leq P(F;E^{(0)}\cap\Omega_{\lambda}^{\varepsilon})-P(E;F^{(1)}\cap\Omega_{\lambda}^{\varepsilon})\\
&=P(F;\bar{E}^{c}\cap\Omega_{\lambda}^{\varepsilon})-P(E;F^{(1)}\cap\Omega_{\lambda}^{\varepsilon}).\end{split}

Let us now estimate
(II)
. Because
E\Delta F\subset\!\subset B_{1}
, clearly
\partial^{\ast}(F\setminus E)\subset B_{1}
and
(F\setminus E)^{(1)}\subset B_{1}
. Thus, by applying (
2.7
) to
\Omega_{\lambda}^{\varepsilon}=\Omega_{\lambda}\setminus A^{\varepsilon}
and (
2.9
) to its normal, we have

(5.4)

\begin{split}|(II)|&\leq\int_{B_{1}\cap\partial\Omega_{\lambda}^{\varepsilon}}\left|\left\langle Z\,,\,\nu_{\Omega_{\lambda}^{\varepsilon}}\right\rangle\right|\,d{\mathcal{H}}^{n-1}\\
&\leq\int_{B_{1}\cap A^{\varepsilon}\cap\partial\Omega_{\lambda}}\left|\left\langle Z\,,\,\nu_{\Omega_{\lambda}}\right\rangle\right|\,d{\mathcal{H}}^{n-1}+\int_{B_{1}\cap\Omega_{\lambda}\cap\partial A^{\varepsilon}}\left|\left\langle Z\,,\,\nu_{A^{\varepsilon}}\right\rangle\right|\,d{\mathcal{H}}^{n-1}.\end{split}

Now, since
Z
is tangent to
S_{\lambda}^{\prime}
, the first integral on the right hand side of (
5.4
) vanishes. This implies that

|(II)|\leq{\mathcal{H}}^{n-1}(B_{1}\cap\partial A^{\varepsilon})=C(n)\,\varepsilon^{n-2}.

This in combination with (
5.2
) and (
5.3
) yields

(5.5)

P(E;F^{(1)}\cap\Omega_{\lambda}^{\varepsilon})\leq P(F;\bar{E}^{c}\cap\Omega_{\lambda}^{\varepsilon})+C(n)\,\varepsilon^{n-2}.

By applying the Divergence Theorem in
(E\setminus F)\cap\Omega_{\lambda}^{\varepsilon}
and arguing very similarly as above, we also obtain

(5.6)

\begin{split}P(E;F^{(0)}\cap\Omega_{\lambda}^{\varepsilon})&\leq P(F;E^{(1)}\cap\Omega_{\lambda}^{\varepsilon})+C(n)\,\varepsilon^{n-2}\\
&=P(F;E\cap\Omega_{\lambda}^{\varepsilon})+C(n)\,\varepsilon^{n-2}\end{split}

Summing up (
5.5
) and (
5.6
), noticing that

P(E;F^{(1)}\setminus B_{1})=P(E;F^{(0)}\setminus B_{1})=0\qquad\text{and}\qquad P(F;E\setminus B_{1})=P(F;\bar{E}^{c}\setminus B_{1})=0,

we deduce that

P(E;(F^{(0)}\cup F^{(1)})\cap\Omega_{\lambda}^{\varepsilon}\cap B_{1})\leq P(F;(\bar{E}^{c}\cup E)\cap\Omega_{\lambda}^{\varepsilon}\cap B_{1})+C(n)\,\varepsilon^{n-2}.

By applying Federer’s Theorem
[
26
, Theorem 16.2]
, we then get

P(E;\Omega_{\lambda}^{\varepsilon}\cap B_{1})\leq P(F;\Omega_{\lambda}^{\varepsilon}\cap B_{1})+C(n)\,\varepsilon^{n-2}.

Finally, passing to the limit as
\varepsilon\to 0^{+}
in the relation above, we obtain that

P(E;\Omega_{\lambda}\cap B_{1})\leq P(F;\Omega_{\lambda}\cap B_{1}),

and we conclude.
∎
