SIRT3-dependent mitochondrial redox homeostasis protects against age-related tendon degeneration
Authors: Yiqiong Nie, Jiefu Zhou, Xueying Zhang, Yang Chen, Tao Zhang, Wei Peng, Linfeng Wang, Shouan Zhu, Hongbin Lu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AimsMitochondrial redox homeostasis is closely linked to the ageing processes of tendons, whether in normal or pathological conditions. This study employed an innovative approach to examine the role of sirtuin 3 (SIRT3) in mitochondrial dysfunction in the aged murine supraspinatus tendon (ST).MethodsPathological changes and collagen organization in tendons, along with variations in SIRT3 levels and oxidative stress, were initially assessed in mice of different ages. In vitro experiments were then performed to evaluate the effects of oxidative senescence on tendon cells, including cellular activity, phenotype, and collagen secretion. Finally, we explored the underlying mechanisms via which SIRT3 regulates mitochondrial redox homeostasis.ResultsHistological and immunofluorescence analyses revealed a decline in SIRT3 levels within tenocytes as ageing progressed, accompanied by mitochondrial dysfunction and ageing-related phenotypes. The reduction in SIRT3 with age led to an increase in reactive oxygen species levels in tendon cells. However, enhancing SIRT3 expression in cells under oxidative stress was found to activate the SOD2 and Keap1/Nrf2/HO-1 signalling pathways, effectively reversing cellular senescence.ConclusionSIRT3 plays a pivotal role in maintaining mitochondrial redox homeostasis in ST by regulating the mitochondrial antioxidant network to mitigate ageing and oxidative stress in tendon cells. Targeting SIRT3 in ST degeneration may offer a promising therapeutic approach.Cite this article: Bone Joint Res 2026;15(9):1123–1135.

Introduction

Ageing is a multifaceted physiological process that accompanies the progression of chronological age and the associated decline in bodily functions. Rotator cuff tear (RCT) is a prevalent musculoskeletal disorder that causes shoulder pain and limits upper limb mobility. A 2020 cross-sectional study indicated that the incidence of full-thickness RCT increases with age, rising from 22% in individuals aged 65 years to over 62% in those aged over 80 years.1 The supraspinatus tendon (ST) is one of the most commonly affected tendons. The aetiology and pathological mechanisms of RCT are complex, involving both intrinsic and extrinsic factors, with ageing serving as a key independent risk factor.2 Even in physiological conditions, subtle structural and functional changes in the ST are observed as part of the ageing process.

A previous study has linked mitochondrial dysfunction and structural changes to ageing and age-related disorders.3 As cellular ageing progresses, mitochondrial function typically deteriorates, leading to increased reactive oxygen species (ROS) production and a corresponding reduction in adenosine triphosphate (ATP) synthesis.4 ROS dysregulation occurs when ROS production surpasses the capacity of the cellular antioxidant defense systems, potentially causing oxidative damage to cellular components. Mitochondria are central to intracellular ROS metabolism. Tendon injuries often occur in the hypoxic critical zone, where mechanical overload and fluoroquinolone stimulate ROS production in tenocytes, contributing to tendinopathy and RCT.5-7 Recently, antioxidants targeting tendons, mitochondrial transplantation, and the mitochondrial transfer of mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) have emerged as potential therapeutic strategies to enhance mitochondrial function and mitigate tendon degeneration.8-10 However, the precise molecular mechanisms driving mitochondrial dysfunction in tendons, particularly in the context of ageing, remain unclear.

Sirtuin 3 (SIRT3) is a mitochondrial deacetylase enzyme that plays a key role in maintaining mitochondrial homeostasis, regulating metabolism, influencing mitochondrial dynamics, balancing redox reactions, and ensuring genomic stability through the regulation of protein acetylation.11 Over 60% of mitochondrial proteins are acetylated, impacting mitochondrial metabolism, fatty acid oxidation, the tricarboxylic acid cycle (TCA), the electron transport chain (ETC), and oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS).12 SIRT3 expression in humans decreases by approximately 40% by the age of 65 years.13 Dysfunction of SIRT3 is associated with a range of age-related degenerative conditions, including cancer, insulin resistance, osteoporosis, osteoarthritis, hypertension, cardiovascular diseases, metabolic syndrome, fibrosis, and neurodegenerative diseases.14-16 Increased SIRT3 expression in mice undergoing caloric restriction is linked to reduced oxidative damage and protection against age-related disorders.17-19 SIRT3 is essential for regulating cellular redox homeostasis.20 Therefore, a potential relationship between SIRT3, ROS, and age-related degenerative changes in the rotator cuff may exist. This study investigated the changes in SIRT3 during tendon ageing and its role in tendon degeneration, aiming to further elucidate the underlying mechanisms involved in this complex process.

Methods

Study design and ethics statement

Animal studies were conducted in accordance with the ethical standards set forth in the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki and the principles outlined by the Animal Ethics Committee of Central South University.21 Adherence to the ARRIVE guidelines was maintained, and the ARRIVE checklist is included in the Supplementary Material. Male C57/BL6 mice were purchased from the Department of Central South University Laboratory Animals (Changsha, China). Eight mice from each age group (8, 24, 52, and 78 weeks) were euthanized, and the STs were collected for biomechanical testing, histological analysis, immunofluorescence (IF) staining, and gene expression analysis by quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR).

Mice aged 78 weeks were randomly assigned to two groups: the natural ageing control group and the HKL treatment group. Both groups were fed a standard diet and allowed free activity. The HKL treated group received daily intra-glenohumeral joint injections of HKL (0.2 mg/kg), whereas the control group was administered peanut oil for four weeks.

Biomechanical testing

Biomechanical testing followed an established protocol.22 Tendons were flattened and secured using sandpaper and cyanoacrylate glue (Krazy Glue; Elmer, USA). The specimens were then subjected to uniaxial tensile testing using a materials testing system (MTS) to measure tendon failure force, with loading applied at a rate of 1 mm/min without preload. Data on load-to-failure and the site of graft failure were recorded, and stiffness was calculated from the load-deformation curves (slope of the linear portion).

Histological analysis

All shoulder specimens were obtained and stained for histological analysis. Haematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining was used for cellular components and overall structure, Alcian blue for cell ground substance, and picrosirius red for collagen arrangement. Imaging was performed using bright-field microscopy (CX31; Olympus Optical Co. Ltd., Japan) for H&E and Alcian blue staining, and polarized-light microscopy (Nikon, USA) for picrosirius red staining. Bright-field and detection parameters were kept consistent across samples for comparison.

Semi-quantitative analysis of the sections was performed by three blinded observers (XZ, YC, TZ) using a pathological scoring system. Regions of interest were graded (0 to 3) across five categories: fibre structure, fibre arrangement, nuclear rounding, regional variations in cellularity, and reduced collagen stainability.

Immunofluorescence

For IF, sections were incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies against Sirt3 (1:200, Abcam, UK) and scleraxis (Scx) (1:80, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, USA). After incubation with corresponding secondary antibodies for one hour, the slides were stained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI). Images were captured using a Zeiss LSM 710 confocal microscope (Carl Zeiss, Germany) and analyzed with Carl Zeiss ZEN 2010 software.

qRT-PCR

Following experimental interventions, total RNA was extracted from tenocytes using TRIzol (Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Messenger RNA (mRNA) was reverse-transcribed into complementary DNA (cDNA) using the GoScript Reverse Transcription System (Promega, USA). Gene expression was quantified by qRT-PCR using GoScript qPCR Master Mix (Promega) on a real-time PCR system (FTC-3000, Funglyn Biotech, Canada). Relative expression levels of mRNA were normalized to the housekeeping gene glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) and calculated using the 2ΔΔCt or 2ΔCt method. Mouse-specific primers for genes related to Sirt3, Col1, Col3, Tnmd, Scx, SOD2, P53, P16, and GAPDH were designed by Sangon Biotech (China), and the primer sets are listed in Supplementary Table S1.

Western blot

Equal amounts of protein extracted from murine STs or cells were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred onto a polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membrane via electroblotting. The membrane was blocked for one hour at room temperature using 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) in TBST (50 mM Tris–HCl, 150 mM NaCl, and 0.02% Tween-20, pH 7.5), and then incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies. The primary antibodies used were as follows: SOD2 (1:5000), NADPH oxidase 2 (NOX2) (1:1,000), Nrf2 (1:1,000), Keap1 (1:2,000), HO-1 (1:1,000), and GAPDH (1:10,000). After washing with TBST, the membranes were incubated with secondary antibodies for one hour at room temperature. Following additional washes with TBST, bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescent detection kit (Millipore). Optical bands were captured using a FujiLas-3000 dark box (Fujifilm, Japan) and Kodak X-Omat BT Films (Carestream Health, USA), and the intensity was quantified using ImageJ software 2.1 (National Institutes of Health, USA).

Isolation and culture of mouse tenocytes

Tendons from C57BL6 mice (8 weeks) were harvested, and the skin and epitendineum were carefully removed. Tendon samples were cut into 1 × 1 × 1 mm3 pieces and digested with Collagenase II (3 mg/mL, Gibco, USA) for two hours at 37°C. The cells were then seeded in culture medium (Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM), 10% fetal calf serum (FCS), and 1% Penicillin/Streptomycin (P/S); Biochrom, Germany) in T25 flasks at 37°C with 5% CO2. After two weeks of culture, the cells reached 80% confluence.

Oxidation and deoxidation induced in vitro

To induce oxidative stress, tenocytes were treated with 0.5 mM H2O2 for eight hours or 1.0 mM H2O2 for one hour, followed by medium replacement for subsequent experiments. For antioxidation induction, cells were treated with 5.0 mM N-Acetyl-L-cysteine (NAC, Aladdin, China) for one hour. In vitro, tenocytes were also treated with 10 μM honokiol (Chengdu Must Bio-Technology, China) as a SIRT3 agonist.

β-gal staining

Senescence-associated beta-galactosidase (SA-β-Gal) staining was performed using the Beta Galactosidase Staining Kit (Solarbio, China) to assess cellular senescence.

Antivirus and plasmid transfection

Lentivirus-based SIRT3-RNAi particles were designed and chemically synthesized by Shanghai Genechem (China). After 48 hours of cultivation, the tenocyte medium was replaced with DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS.

The pEXP-RB-Mam-sirt3/pEXP-RB-Mam-H248Y plasmids (functional site mutation of SIRT3) were chemically synthesized by RiboBio (China). After 48 hours of transfection, the culture medium was refreshed.

Measurement of ROS level and mitochondrial membrane potential

Intracellular ROS levels were measured using the peroxide-sensitive dye H2DCF-DA (1:1,000, Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, China). Mitochondrial ROS generation was assessed by colocalizing MitoSOX with Mitotracker (Yeasen Biotech, China). Mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP) was evaluated using the JC-1 assay kit (Yeasen Biotech), with nuclei counterstained by DAPI. Fluorescence was captured using a Zeiss LSM 710 confocal microscope (Carl Zeiss) and analyzed using Carl Zeiss ZEN 2010 software.

For murine tendons, samples were digested to isolate suspension cells, which were then stained with H2DCF-DA.

SOD activity

Superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity was determined using a SOD assay kit (Solarbio) according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Supplementary Figure S1 and Table S2). SOD activity was calculated using the following equation: LR = ((Ablank1 - Ablank2) - (Asample - Ablank3)) / (Ablank1 - Ablank2) × 100%.

CCK-8 test

Cell viability was assessed using Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8; Dojindo, Japan), according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Absorbance was measured using a microplate reader (Multiskan SkyHigh; Thermo Fisher Scientific) at 450 nm.

Statistical analysis

Sample size was calculated using Gpower (v. 3.1, Heinrich-Heine-Universität Düsseldorf, Germany). Data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 9 (GraphPad Software, USA). Results are presented as mean (SD) from at least three independent experiments. Statistical comparisons between experimental groups were made using independent-samples t-test or one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by the Bonferroni post hoc test. Differences were considered statistically significant at p < 0.05.

Results

Pathological changes of the supraspinatus tendon occurred in mice with ageing

Cellular morphology, collagen structure, and composition of the degenerated ST exhibit significant changes with ageing. H&E staining (Figure 1a) revealed an increase in cellular variation, with nuclei becoming rounder and larger, particularly in the 78 week (78 w) group. Additionally, collagen stainability was diminished in the 78 w group. Alcian blue staining demonstrated a marked accumulation of mucin in the aged groups. Picrosirius red staining images (Figure 1a) showed that tendon fibre bundles in the ageing groups were separated, with a loss of bundle demarcation. Pathological scores were highest in the 78 w group, followed by the 52 w group, which scored higher than the 8 w and 24 w groups, indicating greater degeneration with age (Figure 1b).

The status of tenocytes was assessed through the expression of tendon-related genes. Gene expression was evaluated by qRT-PCR (Figure 1d). In the 52 w and 78 w groups, the expression of collagen I (Col I), collagen III (Col III), tenomodulin (Tnmd), and Scx was reduced. Notably, Col I expression decreased more significantly than Col III in the 78 w group, indicating a decline in collagen production, with Col I synthesis lower than Col III in aged cells. Additionally, Tnmd and Scx expression were also reduced in the 52 w and 78 w groups, suggesting diminished tendon formation and repair capacity.

The microstructure and composition of tendons impact their load-transmitting function. To evaluate the function of the ST, biomechanical testing was conducted. The mean failure force in the 78 w group (3.64 N (SD 0.49)) decreased by 32% compared to the 24 w group (5.39 N (SD 0.55)), with no significant differences between other age groups. This indicates that the ST in the aged mice is more prone to tearing. The mean stiffness in the 78 w group (5.04 N/mm (SD 0.46)) did not significantly differ from that in the other groups (Figure 1c, Table I). One-way ANOVA was used to compare data across age groups. The failure site of the ST in the 78 w group was equally split between the mid-substance tendon and the insertion site (50% each), whereas the other groups exhibited 77% failure in the mid-substance (Table I). These results suggest that force transmission from insertion to mid-substance becomes more challenging with age, likely due to the decreased alignment of the fibres.

SIRT3 and the level of oxidative stress in the murine tendon changed with ageing

ST samples were collected, and the antioxidant enzyme SOD2 levels were quantitatively analyzed by western blotting across different age groups (Figures 2d and 2e). SOD2 expression was notably reduced in the 52 w and 78 w groups. Additionally, ROS levels were significantly elevated in the 52 w and 78 w groups (Figure 2c), demonstrating an increase in oxidative stress within the tendons as ROS production exceeded the body’s antioxidant capacity, accumulating as part of the ageing process.

The effect of oxidative stress in mouse tenocytes in vitro

To investigate the effect of oxidative stress on tenocytes in vitro, ROS injury was induced using varying concentrations of H2O2.23,24 Cell viability was assessed using the cell counting kit-8 (CCK-8) assay (Figure 3a), which revealed a significant reduction in tenocyte viability in a time- and dose-dependent manner following H2O2 treatment. To counteract the effects of ROS, NAC, a ROS scavenger, was applied. The expression of senescence-related genes, P16 and P53, increased in response to oxidative stress induced by H2O2, but this effect was mitigated by NAC (Figures 3b). Additionally, SA-β-gal activity was evaluated (Figures 3c and 3d), further indicating that tenocytes undergo the senescence process as oxidative stress intensifies.

Next, intercellular ROS levels, mitochondrial ROS levels, and MMP were measured to assess oxidative stress in tenocytes (Figures 3e and 3f ). The results showed a significant increase in both intercellular and mitochondrial ROS levels following H2O2 treatment, accompanied by a marked loss of MMP. These results suggest that mitochondrial function and cell viability are significantly impaired under oxidative stress, but the condition can be alleviated by NAC, suggesting the dysregulation of antioxidant networks and excessive ROS production in ageing cells.

SIRT3 reduced oxidative stress by regulating the production of ROS in tenocytes

To investigate the role of SIRT3 in oxidative stress, a lentivirus (LV-Sirt3-) was used to target the inactivation of the Sirt3 gene in tenocytes. Additionally, SIRT3 expression was enhanced through transfection with pEXP-RB-Mam-sirt3/pEXP-RB-Mam-H248Y (a functional site mutation of Sirt3). The transfection efficiency was assessed by qRT-PCR (Supplementary Figure S2A and B).

SOD2 activity was measured using a SOD assay kit (Figure 4a). In the H2O2 treatment group, SOD2 activity was decreased. The inhibition of SOD2 activity was further aggravated in the H2O2 + LV-Sirt3- group, while it was partially reversed in the H2O2 + pRB-Sirt3+ group. Similarly, SOD2 activity was restored in the H2O2 + Honokiol group. These results suggest that Sirt3 knockdown exacerbates the reduction in SOD2 activity caused by oxidative stress, while elevating SIRT3 expression can relieve this inhibition. Oxidative stress was further evaluated by measuring the levels of NOX2 and NOX4 proteins (Figure 4c). The oxidative stress in the H2O2 + LV-Sirt3- group was higher compared to both the PBS (no H2O2) and H2O2 + pRB-Sirt3+ groups. This suggests that decreased SIRT3 expression exacerbates oxidative stress and cellular injury in tenocytes.

Additionally, intercellular and mitochondrial ROS levels were assessed to evaluate the cells’ oxidative status. Oxidative stress was alleviated in the H2O2 + pRB-Sirt3+ group, while the opposite effect was observed in the H2O2 + LV-Sirt3- group. MMP was also measured to assess mitochondrial function (Figure 4e). Mitochondrial activity and function in tenocytes were significantly impaired when SIRT3 levels decreased under oxidative stress.

Finally, the condition of tenocytes was assessed by evaluating the expression of tendon-related genes (Figure 4b). The expression of Col I, Tnmd, and Scx was more significantly reduced in the H2O2 treatment and H2O2+ LV-Sirt3- groups compared to the H2O2 + pRB-SIRT3 group. However, there was no significant difference in Col III expression between the H2O2 + pRB-Sirt3+ and H2O2 + LV-Sirt3- groups. This suggests that while oxidative stress reduces Col III expression in tenocytes, overexpression of SIRT3 does not restore Col III levels, indicating a more complex relationship between Col III, ROS, and SIRT3.

Keap1 protein levels are reduced under oxidative stress conditions. To investigate whether the Keap1/Nrf2 signalling pathway contributes to the protective effects of SIRT3 against oxidative stress, the expression of Keap1, Nrf2, and HO-1 proteins was examined. Under oxidative stress, the levels of these proteins decreased; however, overexpression of SIRT3 restored the levels of Keap1, Nrf2, and HO-1 (Figure 4d). These results suggest that SIRT3 mitigates intracellular oxidative stress by activating the Keap1/Nrf2/HO-1 signalling pathway.

To further confirm the effect of SIRT3 enhancement on senescent mice tenocytes in vivo, Honokiol was injected into the glenohumeral joint. This treatment increased the expression of SOD2, NOX4, Keap1, Nrf2, and HO-1 (Figure 5e), indicating activation of both intracellular and mitochondrial antioxidant defense systems.

Histological analysis showed that SIRT3 alleviated tenocyte, extracellular matrix, and collagen ageing (Figure 5c). Biomechanical testing further revealed that the treatment group exhibited improved mechanical conduction and greater resistance to damage, as evidenced by higher failure force and stiffness values (Figure 5b).

Discussion

Age-related RCT is a common clinical condition that impairs motor function in the elderly. Previous studies have suggested that RCT is influenced by both intrinsic and extrinsic factors. Extrinsic factors include anatomical changes and friction resulting from these alterations,25,26 while intrinsic factors primarily involve ageing, microvascular supply, and tensile overload of the tendon.27,28 Various strategies targeting the pathogenesis of RCT have been explored, some of which have shown promise in alleviating age-related RCT.29-31 Recent studies have also linked age-related tendon degeneration to oxidative metabolic disturbances in tenocytes.5,32 This study examines the histopathological and biomechanical changes in the murine ST from maturation to senescence, exploring the relationship between intracellular oxidation and ageing-related pathology.

Pathological changes in tendons often precede the onset of clinical symptoms. To assess the pathological status of the tendon, this study reviewed several established methods for tendon pathology scoring, including the Bonar and Movin scores.33,34 Age-related RCT is frequently characterized by pronounced collagen degeneration, disorganized collagen fibres, and an increase in tenocytes with rounded nuclei.35 In the present study, significant pathological changes were observed in the aged group (78 weeks). Compared to younger groups, the aged tendons exhibited disordered fibre composition, irregular fibre structure, increased tenocytes with rounded nuclei, and reduced collagen stainability.

Previous studies have shown that age-related tendon degeneration is marked by structural and functional changes, including an increase in collagen fibre diameter and a decrease in total collagen content, particularly collagen type I, as well as impaired collagen turnover. These changes make tendons more susceptible to rupture under mechanical stress.36,37 In line with these findings, our study revealed a significant reduction in staining intensity and collagen arrangement in the 78 week group, as observed in H&E and Alcian blue staining. Additionally, the neogenesis of collagen types I and III, as well as the ratio of collagen type I to type III, decreased in the 52-week and 78-week groups, as confirmed by picrosirius red staining. These findings were consistent with the reduced expression of Col I and Col III in tenocytes, as measured by qRT-PCR.

Scx and Tnmd play key roles in tenocyte proliferation, maturation, and collagen fibre regeneration.8 In our qRT-PCR results, the decreased expression of Scx and Tnmd likely contributed to the disorganization of collagen fibre orientation and a reduction in tensile strength. Biomechanical testing of tendons from the aged group revealed a lower failure force at the tendon insertion site, although stiffness remained unchanged. This suggests that structural changes in the tendon affect its mechanical transmission capabilities. The observed reduction in tendon failure force is likely related to the altered orientation of the collagen fibres.

Mitochondria play a central role in intracellular active oxygen metabolism, supported by complex ROS regulatory networks that include antioxidant enzymes such as SOD, NOX, and catalase (CAT), as well as enzymatic defense systems regulated by stress-response transcription factors NRF2 and nuclear factor-κB (NF-κB).38-40 As ageing progresses, mitochondrial function declines, leading to reduced expression of key antioxidant enzymes, resulting in ROS accumulation and increased oxidative stress.23,41,42 Oxidative stress has been shown to induce senescence and functional disorders in auditory cells, disc cells, endothelial cells, and MSCs.43-46 The acetylation modification function of SIRT3 is critical for maintaining mitochondrial redox homeostasis, and its deficiency is observed in various oxidative degenerative diseases.24,47-50 SIRT3 regulates cellular energy metabolism and redox homeostasis through deacetylation.51 In our study, a decline in SIRT3 levels was observed in the tendons of aged mice. Specifically, SIRT3 expression in tendon tissues was significantly reduced in mice older than 52 weeks, consistent with clinical studies indicating a higher incidence of RC injuries in humans after the age of 50 years.52 SIRT3 activity decreases in the elderly compared to younger individuals.53 Some studies suggest that SIRT3 is regulated by the deacetylation of SIRT1/PGC1-α, while others indicate that its reduction may be related to a decrease in NAD+ levels.54,55 Further investigation is required to elucidate the exact mechanisms.

This study also investigated the ROS levels in tendons from mice of different ages and explored their relationship with tendon degeneration. As ageing progressed, ROS levels in cells increased, while SOD2 content decreased. It is hypothesized that age-related degeneration is linked to changes in SIRT3. To explore this hypothesis further, additional experiments were conducted to examine the relationship between oxidative senescence in tendon cells, ROS, and SIRT3.

Elevated ROS levels can activate oxidative stress pathways in cells, inducing apoptosis via mitochondrial pathways, including the release of apoptosis-related molecules such as Cyt-c, Diablo, and HtrA2, along with the disruption of the ETC and loss of MMP.56 Bisaccia et al5 reported that hydrogen peroxide-induced oxidative stress could cause tendinopathic changes in rat patellar tendons. Our study demonstrates that SIRT3 regulates antioxidant stress network enzymes, maintaining mitochondrial and intracellular redox homeostasis, by inducing oxidative stress in tendon cells with hydrogen peroxide.

Nrf2 plays a critical role in the antioxidant response within the cytoplasm. Upon detection of ROS, Nrf2 dissociates from Keap1 and translocates to the nucleus, where it binds to the antioxidant response element. In the nucleus, Nrf2 activates the expression of downstream antioxidative genes, such as heme oxygenase-1 (HO-1), NADPH quinone oxidoreductase 1 (NQo1), glutamate-cysteine ligase catalytic subunit (Gclc), catalase, and GS homeobox1 (Gsx1), which collectively function to reduce ROS levels.57-59 SOD2, a key component of the mitochondrial antioxidant network, works alongside CAT, TRX2, and TRXR2 to regulate ROS levels by enhancing the function of the ETC. SOD2 can be activated by SIRT3, thereby reducing mitochondrial ROS.48 To investigate the molecular mechanism linking ROS and SIRT3, this study first manipulated mitochondrial SIRT3 to clarify its role in oxidative stress in tenocytes. Knockdown of SIRT3 using SIRT3-specific small interfering RNA (siRNA) exacerbated oxidative stress, inhibited the Keap1/Nrf2/HO-1 signalling pathway, and reduced SOD2 activity. Conversely, overexpression of SIRT3 using pRB-Sirt3+ alleviated mitochondrial and intracellular ROS levels, activated the Keap1/Nrf2/HO-1 pathway, and restored SOD2 activity under oxidative stress conditions. These findings suggest that modulating SIRT3 function could offer a therapeutic approach for age-related RCT. In vivo, honokiol, a SIRT3 agonist, was injected into the shoulder joints of aged mice. SIRT3 expression activated the Keap1/Nrf2/HO-1 signalling pathway, alleviating intracellular oxidative stress, as evidenced by changes in signalling proteins and oxidative stress markers such as NOX2 and NOX4.

This research provides detailed insights into redox homeostasis and the pathology of tendons across four distinct age groups. The study demonstrates that SIRT3, which is associated with ageing, plays a pivotal role in maintaining redox homeostasis in tenocytes. Moreover, it investigates the therapeutic potential of honokiol in treating age-related tendon injuries through modulation of the SIRT3 antioxidant network. However, there are limitations to this study. First, no age-related RCT animal models have been established in previous research. Thus, this study used widely accepted tendinopathy models and selected different age groups of mice to evaluate pathological scoring and mechanical properties. Second, while the protective function of SIRT3 in redox homeostasis was revealed through the Keap1/Nrf2/HO-1 pathway in age-related RCT, this study did not use SIRT3 knockout mice to intervene in aged mice to further assess ROS levels and Keap1/Nrf2/HO-1 activity in vivo. Third, future studies should therefore incorporate additional experiments, such as investigating the upstream influencing factors of SIRT3 changes in mice of different ages. Despite these limitations, our findings provide valuable insights and suggest a potential therapeutic target for age-related tendon degeneration.

In conclusion, this study represents the first demonstration of SIRT3’s protective role in age-related RCT. Our findings indicate that the reduction of SIRT3 in tenocytes contributes to ROS accumulation in tendons, leading to oxidative stress. This may be an intrinsic factor contributing to the susceptibility of tendons in the elderly to RCT degeneration. This discovery opens a new avenue for preventing age-related RCT by increasing SIRT3 expression in tenocytes.
