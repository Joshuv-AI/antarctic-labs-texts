Enhancing bovicin HC5 production in Streptococcus equinus HC5 through adaptive laboratory evolution under thermal stress
Authors: Rodrigo G. Dias, Yasmin N. V. Sabino, Katialaine C. A. Domingues, Juliana S. Silva, Nivea M. Vieira, Wendel B. Silveira, Hilario C. Mantovani
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBovicin HC5, a bacteriocin produced by Streptococcus equinus HC5, demonstrates inhibitory activity against pathogenic and spoilage microorganisms. However, low production yields hinder its widespread application. This study investigated the impact of temperature on S. equinus HC5 growth and employed adaptive laboratory evolution (ALE) under heat stress to obtain variants with improved bovicin HC5 production. The optimal growth temperature for the wild-type strain was determined to be 42 °C, with growth ceasing above 49 °C. Following 400 generations of ALE at 47 °C and 48 °C, eight variants were selected. Two of these variants exhibited significantly enhanced bovicin HC5 production, reaching up to a 140% increase (P < 0.05). The variant with the highest bacteriocin yield showed increased expression of bvcA, the gene encoding the bovicin HC5 precursor peptide. This high-producing variant also displayed enhanced thermal resistance, a higher growth rate (μ = 1.33 ± 0.02 h−1), and increased biomass accumulation (OD600nm = 4.03 ± 0.06) at 48 °C compared to the wild-type strain (μ = 0.98 ± 0.04 h−1; OD600nm = 1.96 ± 0.12) (P < 0.05). Furthermore, the selected variants exhibited alterations in membrane composition, characterized by an increased concentration of saturated fatty acids and a reduced Zeta potential (P < 0.05). Genomic analysis of these variants identified mutations in genes involved in protein modification, transcriptional regulation, and cellular transport, including a lantibiotic permease. These results demonstrate the effectiveness of ALE for generating S. equinus HC5 variants with improved bovicin HC5 production and provide valuable insights for optimizing bacteriocin biosynthesis strategies. Key points• The optimal growth temperature for the Streptococcus equinus HC5 strain was determined to be 42 °C, with growth ceasing above 49 °C• The variant Streptococcus equinus HC5 40048 with the highest bacteriocin yield showed increased expression of bvcA, the gene encoding the bovicin HC5 precursor peptide• ALE is an efficient metabolic engineering strategy to increase bacteriocin production in Streptococcus equinus HC5Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00253-025-13642-8.

Introduction

The emergence of multidrug-resistant bacteria and the slow pace of new novel antibiotic discovery present a critical challenge to global public health (Murray et al. 2022; Tängdén et al. 2024). Consequently, research efforts have increasingly explored alternative therapeutic strategies and the repurposing of existing antimicrobials for applications across food, biomedical, and agricultural sectors (MacNair et al. 2024); Soltani et al. 2021). Among potential antimicrobial agents, antimicrobial peptides (AMPs) stand out as promising alternatives to conventional antibiotics due to their inherent advantages, including high chemical stability, a broad or narrow spectrum of activity, low cytotoxicity, and a reduced propensity to induce bacterial resistance (Bucataru and Ciobanasu 2024; Saubenova et al. 2025).

Bacteriocins are ribosomally synthesized AMPs produced by bacteria and exhibit significant diversity in their chemical, structural, and functional properties (Cotter et al. 2005; Saubenova et al. 2025). Among the well-studied bacteriocins, bovicin HC5, a class I lantibiotic with 2449 Da synthesized by Streptococcus equinus HC5, has several potential applications (Mantovani et al. 2002; Lee et al. 2002; De Carvalho et al. 2007, 2008; Paiva et al. 2012; Pimentel-Filho et al. 2021; Ribeiro et al. 2022). Notably, bovicin HC5 is the most extensively characterized bacteriocin isolated from the rumen ecosystem. The biosynthetic gene cluster comprises nine genes (bvcATCBFEGRK), with an estimated size of 10.1 kb. The bvcA gene encodes the bovicin HC5 precursor; bvcB and bvcC are enzymes responsible for post-translational modification; bvcT shows homology to a lantibiotic secretion transporter; bvcF, bvcE, and bvcG encode immunity proteins; and bvcR and bvcK are associated with the regulation of peptide biosynthesis (Azevedo et al. 2015; Sousa et al. 2021). The highest specific activity of bovicin HC5 is observed when S. equinus HC5 reaches the stationary phase. Interestingly, the cationic nature of bovicin HC5 leads to its significant binding to the producer cell envelope (Mantovani et al. 2002; Moreira et al. 2021).

The high thermal and acidic stability, low in vivo toxicity, and broad spectrum of activity of bovicin HC5 make it particularly attractive for industrial applications (Mantovani et al. 2002; Flythe and Russell 2004; Houlihan et al. 2004). Several studies have highlighted its potential in the food industry (Mantovani and Russell 2003; De Carvalho et al. 2007, 2008; Prudêncio et al. 2015; Ribeiro et al. 2022) and agriculture (Lee et al. 2002; Flythe and Russell 2004; Lima et al. 2009; Godoy-Santos et al. 2019). However, scaling up the production of bacteriocins, including bovicin HC5, faces technological challenges, particularly in increasing batch yields.

Previous efforts to enhance bovicin HC5 production have focused on optimizing abiotic factors and growth conditions to improve cell biomass or bacteriocin yield (De Carvalho et al. 2009; Barbosa et al. 2015). Our group previously demonstrated the efficacy of adaptive laboratory evolution (ALE) in selecting S. equinus HC5 strains with enhanced bovicin HC5 production under acidic stress conditions (Moreira et al. 2021). ALE is a valuable tool in industrial microbiology, involving the prolonged culture of microbial populations under defined conditions to select strains with desired phenotypes (Mavrommati et al. 2022; G. Wang et al. 2023a, b). In this study, we hypothesized that subjecting S. equinus HC5 to ALE under thermal stress would result in the selection of strains with cell envelope modifications leading to increased bovicin HC5 production. To test this hypothesis, we aimed to select S. equinus HC5 variants with enhanced bovicin HC5 production using thermal stress-induced ALE and subsequently characterize these variants through whole-genome sequencing. This novel strategy to overcome current limitations in bovicin HC5 production could potentially facilitate its wider application as a natural antimicrobial in various sectors.

Materials and methods

Microorganisms and culture conditions

S. equinus HC5 was cultivated at 42 °C in a basal (PC) semi-synthetic medium containing (per liter): 292 mg of KH₂PO₄, 480 mg of (NH₄) SO₄, 480 mg of NaCl, 100 mg of MgSO₄·7H₂O, 64 mg of CaCl₂·2H₂O, 500 mg of hydrochloric acid-cysteine, 1000 mg of Trypticase® (BBL Microbiology Systems, Cockeysville, MD, USA), 500 mg of yeast extract, 4000 mg of Na2CO3, and 8000 mg of glucose. The culture medium was prepared anaerobically under a flow of O2-free CO2, and the final pH was adjusted to 6.5 with NaOH (3 mol L−1). Bacterial growth was monitored by measuring optical density at 600 nm (OD600nm) with a Spectronic 200E spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Madison, WI, USA). Lactococcus lactis ATCC 19345, a bacterium susceptible to bovicin HC5, was used as the indicator microorganism for quantification of antimicrobial activity. The indicator strain was cultured aerobically in Brain Heart Infusion (BHI) broth (Sigma-Aldrich®, São Paulo, Brazil), at 37 °C, for 24 h prior to use.

Adaptive laboratory evolution

Effect of temperature on the growth of S. equinus HC5

To determine the temperature range for cultivating S. equinus HC5 during ALE and to identify the optimal and maximum growth temperatures, S. equinus HC5 was cultured for 24 h at various temperatures, ranging from 33 to 51 °C. The specific growth rate (µ) and optical density (OD600nm) were measured. The specific growth rate (µ) was calculated using the linear regression of the natural logarithm (ln) of OD600nm values over time (h). These results were used to construct an Arrhenius Plot [ln (µ, h−1) × 1/Temperature (K−1)]. The highest temperature at which the culture sustained growth through successive inoculum transfers was selected as the initial temperature for the batch ALE experiment.

Selection of S. equinus HC5 variants using adaptive laboratory evolution under thermal stress

Before initiating the adaptive evolution process under thermal stress, S. equinus HC5 was grown in anaerobic PC medium at the previously established optimal growth temperature (42 °C). The culture was then incubated for 48 h on solid anaerobic PC medium in an anaerobic chamber, and a single colony was selected and inoculated again into anaerobic PC medium. To start the batch culture, S. equinus HC5 was inoculated into a Hungate tube (16 × 125 mm) containing 5 ml of anaerobic PC medium (8 g l−1 glucose). The initial OD600nm was adjusted to 0.05, and the incubation was performed at 47 °C in a water bath (Digital Thermostatized Bath, model B105-DE, OVAN, Barcelona, Spain). Serial transfers of the inoculum (3% v/v) into a new tube containing fresh medium were performed when the culture reached the stationary growth phase. Every 50 generations, the OD600nm, specific growth rate (µ, h−1), and generation time (h) were determined. An increase in µ and OD600nm was used as a criterion for adaptation to the temperature. After meeting the adaptation criteria, the incubation temperature was increased to 48 °C. To preserve the adapted variants of S. equinus HC5, samples were collected every 50 generations were stored in glycerol (25% v/v) at – 20 °C and – 80 °C for subsequent phenotypic characterization. Phenotypic characterization of S. equinus HC5 strains obtained by ALE

The S. equinus HC5 variants obtained through ALE were characterized by comparing their growth kinetics (OD600nm, dry cell weight, and specific growth rate) and bovicin HC5 production against the wild-type strain. Select adapted variants exhibiting significantly altered bovicin HC5 recovery compared to the wild type were further analyzed for membrane lipid composition. Additionally, the expression levels of genes involved in bovicin HC5 biosynthesis, immunity, and regulation were quantified using real-time quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR). The final S. equinus HC5 variant obtained at the culmination of the ALE experiment had its surface charge determined by Zeta potential measurement. All these analyses were conducted in batch cultures maintained at the optimal growth temperature for S. equinus HC5 (42 °C).

Kinetic growth parameters

To determine the OD600nm and specific growth rate (µ, h−1) of the wild-type strain and the variants obtained through ALE, the cultures were anaerobically grown in PC medium at 42 °C for 24 h. The OD600nm was monitored using a Spectronic 200E spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Madison, WI, USA). The specific growth rate (µ) was calculated from the linear regression of the natural logarithm (ln) of OD600nm values over time (h).

Bovicin HC5 extraction

To evaluate the efficiency of bovicin HC5 extraction from the wild-type S. equinus HC5 and the variants obtained in the ALE process, the protocol described by Mantovani et al. (2002) was used under different extraction temperatures, as described below. After 24 h of fermentation, the pH of the cultures was adjusted to 7.0 with NaOH (2 M). Subsequently, the cultures were incubated at 75 °C for 30 min to inactivate peptidases. The cells were then centrifuged at 10,000 g for 20 min at 4 °C (Sigma 4K15 centrifuge–Sigma Laborzentrifugen GmbH, Osterode, Germany), and the supernatant was discarded. The cells were resuspended in 10 ml of sodium chloride solution (100 mM), and the pH was adjusted to 2.0 using HCl solution (1 M). The cell suspensions were then incubated under two different conditions: 5 ml of each suspension was incubated at 4 °C for 16 h with agitation, and 5 ml was heat-treated at 100 °C for 10 min. After incubation, the cell suspensions were centrifuged at 10,000 g for 20 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was then collected to evaluate bovicin HC5 activity. The antimicrobial activity of the extract was analyzed using the agar-well diffusion method according to Tagg et al. (1976). Lactococcus lactis ATCC 19345 grown in BHI (optical density adjusted to McFarland 0.5 scale) was used as the indicator microorganism. The plates were incubated at 4 °C for 16 h and then at 37 °C for 24 h.

Dry mass and bovicin HC5 activity recovery

The recovery of bovicin HC5 activity from wild-type S. equinus HC5 and its variants obtained in the ALE process was carried out according to Mantovani et al. (2002), using an extraction temperature of 100 °C for 10 min. The recovered extract was used to assess bovicin HC5 activity, and the cell biomass was used for dry mass quantification. Dry mass was determined after drying the microbial biomass in an oven at 105 °C until constant weight (FANEM 315 SE, São Paulo, Brazil) (De Carvalho et al. 2009).

The antimicrobial activity of the bovicin HC5 extract was analyzed using the agar well assay (Hoover and Harlander 1993). Serial dilutions of the extract were made in ultrapure water, and aliquots of 25 μl from each dilution were placed in wells on BHI agar plates, containing L. lactis ATCC 19345 as the indicator microorganism. The plates were incubated at 4 °C for 16 h and then at 37 °C for 24 h. The titer of bovicin HC5, expressed in activity units (AU), was calculated as follows: AU/ml = (1000/25)*(FD), where FD is the highest dilution where the extract produced an inhibition zone of at least 5 mm in diameter from the edge of the well. The specific activity of bovicin HC5 was calculated by dividing the bacteriocin titer by the biomass (AU/ml per mg of dry cell mass).

Membrane fatty acid extraction and analysis of methyl esters

Wild-type S. equinus HC5 and the adapted variants were cultivated in PC medium in anaerobic serum bottles (50 ml) at 42 °C for 24 h. Subsequently, the cultures were centrifuged at 10,000 g for 10 min (Eppendorf 5417 C Centrifuge, Hamburg, Germany), and the cell biomass was washed with 0.85% NaCl solution. Membrane fatty acids were saponified, methylated, and extracted following the protocol recommended by Rozès et al. (1993).

Saponification was performed by adding 1 ml of Reagent 1 (3.75 mol L−1 NaOH:CH3OH, 1:3 v/v), followed by brief agitation and heating the tubes at 95 °C for 25 min. Methylation was carried out by incubating the cooled tubes with 2 ml of Reagent 2 (CH3OH:6N HCl, 85:1) at 80 °C for 10 min. The extraction reactions were then performed by adding 1.25 ml of Reagent 3 (C6H14:C5H12O, 1:1 v/v) and gently shaking the tubes for 10 min. The lower aqueous phase was removed, and 3 ml of Reagent 4 (0.3 mol L−1 NaOH) was added to the organic phase. After shaking for 5 min, the upper organic layer was transferred to a vial for gas chromatography (GC) analysis.

The analysis of fatty acid methyl esters (FAMEs) was performed on an Agilent Technologies 7890 A GC system (Wilmington, DE, USA) equipped with a methyl-silicone capillary column (HP–ULTRA 2–25 m × 0.2 mm × 0.33 μm). The initial oven temperature was held constant at 310 °C, and hydrogen (H2) was used as the carrier gas. The FAMEs were identified using Sherlock software (RTSBA6 Library, Version 3.9, Microbial ID, Newark, DE, USA), based on retention times of the external calibration standard prior to sample analysis. The calibration standard was composed of a mixture of linear chain saturated fatty acids ranging from 9 to 20 carbon atoms in length (9:0 to 20:0) and five hydroxy acids.

Zeta potential estimation

Zeta potential is influenced by the composition of the cell surface, as well as factors in the surrounding environment, such as salt concentration and pH of the environment, as they influence the adsorption of ions in bacterial cells and the degree of ionization of charged moieties on the cell surface (Soon et al. 2011). To estimate the Zeta potential, S. equinus HC5 cells were cultured at 42 °C for 12 h. Subsequently, the cells were washed twice in saline solution (0.85%) and resuspended in 0.5 mM potassium phosphate buffer solution (pH 7.4) to minimize any influence of pH. The Zeta potential was measured using the ZetaSizer NanoSeries device (Malvern, UK), equipped with a Helium–Neon laser (633 nm) as a light source, with detection at a scattering angle of 90° at room temperature (22 °C). The experiments were performed using 3 biological and 3 technical replicates (Halder et al. 2015).

Analysis of bovicin HC5 biosynthetic gene cluster expression by real-time quantitative PCR

Real-time quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR) was performed to quantify the relative expression of the bovicin HC5 gene cluster in S. equinus HC5 strains. Primer pairs were designed to flank the biosynthetic genes (bvcATCBFEGRK) involved in the bovicin HC5 biosynthesis using the GenScript Real-time PCR (TaqMan) Primer Design Tool (https://www.genscript.com/tools/real-time-pcr-tagman-primer-design-tool) (Moreira et al. 2021). The specific primer sequences for the bovicin HC5 gene cluster are detailed in Supplementary File Table S1.

For the extraction of total RNA from wild-type S. equinus HC5 and adapted variants, anaerobic cultures were grown in PC medium at 42 °C for 12 h, when they reached the stationary phase. The cultures were centrifuged (10,000 g/10 min), 1 ml of liquid nitrogen was added to the cell pellet, and the cells were macerated using a pestle. Subsequently, 1 ml of TRI Reagent® (Sigma-Aldrich Corp, WI, USA) was added for RNA extraction, following the manufacturer’s instructions. The total concentration and purity of nucleic acids were determined by absorbance readings (260 nm and 280 nm) using a Nanodrop 2000c spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Massachusetts, USA). To verify the RNA integrity, the samples were subjected to agarose gel electrophoresis (1%) at 80 V for 30 min.

The extracted RNA was treated with DNAse (Promega, Madison, WI, USA) and then converted into its complementary DNA (cDNA) using the High-Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Madison, WI, USA). Twenty nanograms of cDNA was used to perform RT-qPCR using the CFX96 Real-Time PCR Detection System (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA). The reactions were carried out in 96-well plates, in volumes of 10 μl, containing 5 μl Maxima SYBR Green qPCR Master Mix (2 ×) (Thermo Fisher Scientific), 0.8 μl of each primer (10 μmol L−1), 2 μl of diluted cDNA, and 1.4 μl of RNase-free water. The amplification reaction consisted of an initial denaturation at 95 °C (10 min), followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C (30 s), annealing, and extension at 60 °C (1 min), following the manufacturer’s instructions. The melting curve analysis was performed immediately after the last cycle of RT-qPCR with a preliminary denaturation step at 95 °C for 1 min, initial maintenance at 60 °C/1 min, followed by a temperature increase to 95 °C with a ramp rate of 1 °C/min and continuous fluorescence acquisition. The melting curves were generated by plotting fluorescence values against temperature.

The Bio-Rad CFX Manager 2.0 version 2.0.885.0923 software (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA) was used to calculate cycle threshold (Ct) values and collect melting point data. For each primer set, R2 was determined, and the amplification efficiency was calculated using the following equation: E = (10(−1/slope))−1 from the control cDNA concentration (Souza et al. 2019). The 16S ribosomal RNA (16S rRNA) and DNA gyrase A (gyrA) genes of S. equinus HC5 were used as endogenous reference genes to calculate the relative expression levels. The results were expressed as the mean relative expression obtained from each endogenous gene.

Whole genome sequencing of S. equinus HC5 wild-type and variants

To investigate the genetic basis underlying the changes in bovicin HC5 production, whole-genome sequencing was performed for S. equinus HC5 wild-type as well as the variants obtained in the adaptive evolutionary analysis, including S. equinus HC5 10047, S. equinus HC5 20048, S. equinus HC5 30048, and S. equinus HC5 40048. The genomic DNA was extracted using Wizard® Genomic DNA Purification Kit (Promega, Madison, USA). The quality of the DNA was verified using gel electrophoresis and NanoDrop. The genomes were sequenced in an Illumina NovaSeq (Macrogen, South Korea). The sequence quality was assessed using FastQC (Andrews 2010). Trimmomatric was used to trim the sequences (Bolger et al. 2014), using the following criteria: trailing: 10, leading: 10, sliding window: 4:20, minlen: 50.

Genome assembly, annotation, and variant calling

De-novo assembly of short reads into contigs was performed by SPAdes 3.15.4, using the coverage parameter as “auto” and kmers 21,33,55,77,99,127 (Bankevich et al. 2012). Progressive mauve alignment was used to visualize the synteny between the genomes (Darling et al. 2010). The assembled genome sequences were annotated using Prokka 1.14.6. Snippy was used to detect variants based on the assembled genome of S. equinus wild-type strain (Seemann 2014). Lastly, SnpEff 4.3t software was used to annotate the variants using the annotated genome of the wild-type strain as the reference (Cingolani et al. 2012). When a mutation was detected in a hypothetical protein, Uniprot blast was used to evaluate the protein function, using a cut-off of 50% aa identity. Venn Diagram was used for visualization of the variant annotations (https://bioinformatics.psb.ugent.be/webtools/Venn/).

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed with three biological and a minimum of two technical replicates. The results were subjected to one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA one-way), followed by the Tukey and Bonferroni post hoc tests at a significance level of 95%. The results were presented as mean ± standard deviation.

Results

Effect of temperature on the growth of S. equinus HC5

S. equinus HC5 was cultivated across a temperature range of 33 °C to 51 °C. The highest specific growth rate (µ = 2.16 ± 0.03 h−1) was observed at 42 °C (Fig. 2A). Temperatures above 42 °C led to a reduction in specific growth rates, with temperatures higher than 49 °C completely inhibiting bacterial growth (P < 0.05). The optimal optical density at 600 nm (OD600nm) was recorded between 40 °C and 42 °C, with a value of 5.24 ± 0.41, showing no significant difference within this range. At temperatures higher than 42 °C, OD600nm values decreased, reaching approximately 1.91 at 48 °C. Adaptive laboratory evolution

The adaptive laboratory evolution (ALE) was initiated and maintained at 47 °C, as this was the highest temperature at which the sustained biomass production necessary for sequential transfers could be achieved. Under these conditions, S. equinus HC5 exhibited a specific growth rate of 1.12 ± 0.02 h−1, and an OD600nm of 3.17 ± 0.08 (Fig. 3A and B). During the first 150 generations, the specific growth rate increased by approximately 0.26 h−1, and OD600nm rose by approximately 1.31 compared to the wild-type strain (P < 0.05). After 150 generations, thermal stress was intensified by elevating the incubation temperature to 48 °C. This resulted in a significant decrease in the specific growth rate from 1.37 ± 0.02 to 0.89 ± 0.02 h−1 (P < 0.05). There was no significant difference in the diameter of the inhibition zones for extracts from variants HC5 15047, HC5 20048, HC5 25048, HC5 30048, and HC5 35048. Specific activity of bovicin HC5

The specific activity of bovicin HC5 was compared between the wild-type S. equinus HC5 strain and the variants selected through ALE. The variant HC5 40048 exhibited the highest specific activity of bovicin HC5 among the evaluated variants, with a value of 1022.22 ± 38.49 AU ml−1 mg dry mass−1 (P < 0.05). This represents a 140% increase in activity compared to the wild-type strain, which had a specific activity of 425.10 ± 14.12 AU ml⁻1 mg dry mass⁻1. Additionally, the variant HC5 20048 showed a 26% increase in specific activity of bovicin HC5 (539.36 ± 5.22 AU ml−1 mg dry mass−1) when contrasted with the wild-type strain (P < 0.05). The wild-type S. equinus HC5 strain and four variants obtained through the ALE process (HC5 10047, HC5 20048, HC5 30048, and HC5 40048) were evaluated for membrane fatty acid composition. Comparative genomics

The genome sizes of the wild-type S. equinus HC5 and its evolved variants were approximately 1.85 Mb, with a consistent GC content of 37.08%. Assembly statistics for each genome are detailed in Supplementary File Table S2. Progressive Mauve alignment revealed overall synteny among the genomes, alongside evidence of genomic region rearrangements and sequence polymorphisms between the wild-type strain and the variants (Supplementary File Fig. S1). Prokka annotation identified a similar number of genes across the genomes, ranging from 1825 to 1828 (Supplementary File Table S3). A core set of 1101 unique genes was shared between the wild-type strain and all the analyzed variants. Notably, the blgA gene, encoding a phospho-beta-glucosidase, was exclusively detected in the genomes of S. equinus HC5 WT and S. equinus HC5 10047. Conversely, the wfgD gene, encoding a glucosyltransferase, was uniquely identified in the genomes of S. equinus HC5 30048 and S. equinus HC5 40048.

Variant calling analysis identified a total of 90 variants in the genomes of the adapted cultures compared to the wild-type strain. The distribution of these variants varied, with three in HC5 10047, 28 in HC5 20048, 30 in HC5 30048, and 29 in HC5 40048 (Supplementary File Table S3). The majority of these variants were single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) resulting in missense mutations. Insertions and deletions, leading to frameshift variants, were also observed. Focusing on high and moderate impact mutations (excluding upstream and synonymous mutations), a total of 28 proteins were found to be mutated across all analyzed genomes (Table 2). The highest number of these mutations was shared between the HC5 30048 and HC5 40048 strains (n = 22), while 11 were common among HC5 20028, HC5 30048, and HC5 40048. Single unique mutations were observed in HC5 10047 and HC5 30048, and four unique mutations were identified in HC5 20048, with no unique high or moderate impact mutations found in HC5 40048 (Supplementary File Fig. S2). Interestingly, a significant proportion of the moderate and high-impact mutations occurred in genes encoding proteins involved in protein modification, such as glycosyltransferases, transcriptional regulators (including members of the MutR and MerR families), and cellular transport proteins, including a lantibiotic transport permease. Discussion

The rumen bacterium S. equinus HC5 is a producer of the antimicrobial peptide bovicin HC5 (Mantovani et al. 2002). A notable characteristic of bovicin HC5 is its cationic nature, which leads to its strong association with the bacterial cell envelope during growth (Mantovani et al. 2002; Moreira et al. 2021). Strategies that alter the cell wall can be employed to develop adapted variants with improved bacteriocin production and recovery. To enhance yield and extraction efficiency, we applied adaptive laboratory evolution (ALE) under thermal stress to select S. equinus HC5 variants. ALE mimics natural selection under controlled conditions and is increasingly utilized in metabolic engineering and industrial microbiology to optimize desired microbial traits (Wang et al. 2023a, b).

Our initial characterization of the wild-type strain revealed that S. equinus HC5 exhibits optimal growth at 42 °C, achieving the highest specific growth rates and biomass accumulation at this temperature. Conversely, its maximum tolerated temperature was determined to be 49 °C, beyond which significant growth impairment occurs. These fundamental growth characteristics provide a baseline for understanding the strain’s response to thermal stress.

The natural habitat of S. equinus HC5, the rumen, typically maintains a stable temperature range of 38–42 °C, with most resident microorganisms exhibiting optimal growth around 39 °C (Lees et al. 2018; Kim et al. 2022). Interestingly, studies have reported an increased prevalence of Streptococcus spp., particularly the Streptococcus bovis/Streptococcus equinus complex (SBSEC), in animals experiencing heat stress (Zhao et al. 2019). The observed high growth rate of S. equinus HC5 at elevated temperatures in our study could potentially explain this increased abundance under such conditions. Given that S. equinus HC5 is primarily a lactate producer, its proliferation in the rumen during heat stress might contribute to elevated lactate levels, consequently lowering the pH and potentially disrupting the delicate microbial balance, potentially leading to ruminal acidosis (Yadav et al. 2013; Zhao et al. 2019). Such a pH reduction could further inhibit cellulolytic bacteria, thereby compromising the overall metabolic capacity of the rumen (Asanuma and Hino 2002; Zhao et al. 2019).

In this study, subjecting S. equinus HC5 to thermal stress at 47 °C and 48 °C for 400 generations resulted in the isolation of eight phenotypically distinct variants. Notably, two of these variants, HC5 20048 and HC5 40048, demonstrated enhanced bacteriocin production, with HC5 40048 exhibiting a substantial 140% increase compared to the wild-type strain. This outcome aligns with previous research demonstrating the efficacy of ALE in improving desired traits under stress conditions. For instance, Papiran and Hamedi (2021) successfully employed ALE under thermal and oxidative stress to obtain Lactococcus lactis variants with improved physiological characteristics, including enhanced biomass production (1.87-fold) and increased nisin synthesis (1.48-fold). Similarly, an L. lactis strain adapted to thermal stress alone showed a 1.20-fold increase in cell biomass and a 1.32-fold increase in nisin production compared to its wild-type counterpart. The authors attributed these phenotypic changes to specific genomic mutations that conferred stress tolerance and improved metabolic performance. Consistent with these findings, our thermally adapted S. equinus HC5 variants, while exhibiting improved thermal resistance at 47 °C and 48 °C, displayed reduced growth performance at the optimal growth temperature of 42 °C, as evidenced by lower specific growth rates and OD600nmcompared to the wild-type strain. This trade-off could be attributed to physiological and structural modifications within the cell, potentially including alterations in membrane composition, such as increased fatty acid diversity and a higher proportion of saturated fatty acids. While these modifications might enhance membrane stability at elevated temperatures, they could concurrently impair substrate and nutrient transport at lower temperatures, thereby affecting metabolic flux. Further investigations are warranted to test this hypothesis. Additionally, changes in the optimal temperature of cellular enzymes or persistent alterations in gene expression regulation following heat stress adaptation could also contribute to the observed growth patterns (Russell 2002; Pandey et al. 2022).

The enhanced production of bovicin HC5 in variant HC5 40048 is likely due to differential expression of genes within the bacteriocin biosynthetic cluster. Our RT-qPCR analysis revealed a significant upregulation of bvcA, the gene encoding the bovicin HC5 precursor peptide, in this variant. Conversely, genes associated with transport (bvcT), immunity (bvcF, bvcE, and bvcG), and regulation (bvcK and bvcR) were downregulated relative to the wild-type strain. Interestingly, the expression levels of the post-translational modification genes (bvcB and bvcC) remained relatively unchanged. These findings echo our previous observations in an acid stress-adapted S. equinus HC5 variant, which also exhibited increased bovicin HC5 production coupled with a significant upregulation of all genes in the biosynthetic cluster, particularly bvcA (Moreira et al. 2021). Taken together, these results strongly suggest that enhancing bvcA expression represents a key strategy for boosting bacteriocin production in S. equinus HC5. Therefore, we hypothesize that transcription of the bovicin HC5 structural gene (bvcA) is a key rate-limiting factor for overall bacteriocin yield. Upregulating bvcA, as observed in the HC5 40048 lineage, probably increases the pool of precursors available for processing by enzymes of the biosynthetic machinery (which could function non stoichiometrically at baseline levels to maintain functionality), directly contributing to higher bacteriocin output. Further experiments using proteomics will be needed, however, to confirm protein levels despite transcript downregulation.

Furthermore, the recovery of bovicin HC5 from the ALE-derived variants appeared to be significantly influenced by cytoplasmic membrane composition. We observed poor extraction efficiency at low temperatures, which improved at higher temperatures. This trend correlated with an increased ratio of saturated to unsaturated fatty acids in the adapted variants. A higher proportion of saturated fatty acids is known to enhance membrane rigidity, reducing fluidity and improving thermal resistance. Conversely, at high temperatures, increased membrane fluidity can compromise selective permeability and potentially lead to cell lysis due to osmotic stress (Shin et al. 2019). Cells with a lower level of unsaturated fatty acids have been shown to exhibit greater resistance to thermal stress, which could, in turn, impact bacteriocin release (Chen et al. 2015; Haddaji et al. 2015; Fonseca et al. 2019; Shin et al. 2019). Moreover, the ALE variants displayed increased diversity in their membrane fatty acid composition, which can affect cell envelope hydrophobicity and charge balance. Our Zeta potential analysis revealed a significant 22% reduction in the residual negative charge on the surface of the HC5 40048 variant compared to the wild-type strain, further supporting alterations in membrane properties that are likely to influence bacteriocin recovery.

The reduced Zeta potential in HC5 40048 suggests a greater retention of bovicin HC5 within the cell envelope, consistent with our observation of no detectable bacteriocin activity in the cell-free supernatant of this variant. The bovicin HC5 biosynthetic gene cluster includes the bvcFEG genes, which encode an ATP-binding cassette (ABC) transporter system responsible for both bacteriocin export and self-immunity (Stein et al. 2003; AlKhatib et al. 2012; Azevedo et al. 2015). The absence of bovicin HC5 activity in the supernatant of HC5 40048 strongly implies a loss of function in its immunity proteins, preventing efficient export. Supporting this, our comparative genomic analysis identified a SNP in the lantibiotic transporter permease gene (bvcE) in the HC5 20047, HC5 30048, and HC5 40048 variants. BvcE is an integral transmembrane protein that, together with BvcF and BvcG, forms a functional ABC transporter complex crucial for immunity. A mutation-induced structural alteration in any of these proteins can lead to dysfunction of the entire transporter complex (AlKhatib et al. 2012). This is consistent with findings in nisin-producing L. lactis, where nisin is only found in the supernatant when all proteins of the nisFEG genes are expressed and form a functional complex (AlKhatib et al. 2014).

Interestingly, the HC5 40048 variant exhibited greater thermal robustness at high temperatures, displaying a higher specific growth rate, increased biomass accumulation, and a shorter generation time compared to the wild-type strain under heat stress. These findings are consistent with previous studies by Chen et al. (2015) and Papiran and Hamedi (2021), who reported the selection of L. lactis strains with enhanced thermal adaptability and robustness following ALE under heat stress. Our comparative genomic analyses revealed insertion mutations in the wfgD gene, encoding a glycosyltransferase, in S. equinus HC5 20048, HC5 30048, and HC5 40048—the very variants that displayed greater tolerance and robustness to heat stress. Glycosyltransferase enzymes play a critical role in cell membrane composition and function by facilitating the glycosylation of lipids and proteins in the cell envelope. Protein glycosylation is known to enhance thermal stress resistance by stabilizing proteins and preventing denaturation, thus contributing to cellular adaptation (Feng et al. 2019). The upregulation of glycosyltransferase expression in response to thermal stress has been previously documented in other bacteria, such as Alicyclobacillus acidoterrestris (Feng et al. 2019) and Streptococcus mutans (Cheng et al. 2016), further supporting a role for these enzymes in heat adaptation.

In conclusion, this study provides the first detailed investigation into the effects of temperature on S. equinus HC5 growth, establishing its optimal growth conditions. Through ALE under thermal stress, we successfully selected eight S. equinus HC5 variants with distinct phenotypes, two of which demonstrated increased production of the antimicrobial peptide bovicin HC5. The HC5 40048 variant, which exhibited the highest bacteriocin production, showed increased expression of bvcA, the gene encoding the precursor peptide, suggesting that upregulation of this single gene can significantly enhance bovicin HC5 biosynthesis. Furthermore, HC5 40048 displayed enhanced thermal stress resistance, characterized by higher biomass accumulation at elevated temperatures, modifications in the cell envelope composition (including an increased concentration of saturated fatty acids), and a reduced negative residual surface charge, as indicated by Zeta potential measurements. Genomic analyses of this variant identified mutations in genes associated with crucial cellular processes, including protein modification (e.g., glycosyltransferases), transcriptional regulation (e.g., MutR and MerR family proteins), and cellular transport (e.g., the lantibiotic transporter permease BvcE). The mutation in bvcE likely contributed to the absence of bovicin HC5 activity in the cell-free supernatant of the final ALE-derived variants (Fig. 8), suggesting an impairment in bacteriocin export.
