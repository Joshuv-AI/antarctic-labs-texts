Agent-specific bleeding signatures in non-hypertensive versus hypertensive patients exposed to oral anticoagulants: a real-world pharmacovigilance study
Authors: Ying Feng, Hua-Hua Liu, Liu-Cheng Li, Kai-Li Mao, Xiao-Fang Niu, Jie Hu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractOral anticoagulants (OAC) are effective in thrombo-embolic disorders, but their real-world bleeding profiles in hypertensive versus non-hypertensive users remain unknown. This study provides an exploratory, real-world assessment of the population-level, agent-specific safety hierarchy of direct OAC (DOACs; apixaban, dabigatran, edoxaban, rivaroxaban) and warfarin. We queried the FAERS database (Q1 2004–Q2 2025) using four disproportionality methods, requiring concordant signals from all four algorithms (a high-specificity, lower-sensitivity approach), and stratified by hypertension status as defined by MedDRA-coded diagnosis terms. A clinical priority matrix (0–8) incorporating salience, reporting density, robustness, and fatality was then applied to tier bleeding events. Among non-hypertensive reporters (n = 101,469), rivaroxaban dominated case volume (50,676; 49.9%), a finding that primarily reflects its market dominance rather than a higher intrinsic risk. In descriptive reporting patterns, dabigatran showed the highest proportion of fatal outcomes among its cases (23.7%). In the much smaller hypertensive subset (n = 4,252), edoxaban (based on only 142 reports) showed a fatality proportion of 28.9%; however, this estimate is based on a small sample and is subject to instability (“small cell” bias), warranting cautious, hypothesis-generating interpretation only. Hypertension was associated with a shift in the anatomical signal pattern from gastrointestinal and vascular-malformation bleeding observed in non-hypertensive toward deep intracerebral, traumatic, and adrenal hemorrhages in OAC. Temporal occurrence revealed early-phase rivaroxaban predominance in both strata, whereas warfarin exhibited a delayed but sustained reporting tail beyond 360 days. Clinical priority scoring identified brain stem hemorrhage as the only universally tier-5 event across DOACs and cerebral microhemorrhage was flagged moderate only in rivaroxaban in non-hypertensive patients. Edoxaban further generated unique tier-5 signals for cerebellar and pulmonary alveolar bleeding in both strata, though these findings are based on small numbers and require replication. Hypertension appears to be a qualitative effect-modifier that reallocates the population burden of OAC-related hemorrhage and redirects bleeding topography in this spontaneous reporting dataset. Introduction

Hypertension affects more than 1.2 billion individuals worldwide and is the leading modifiable risk factor for atrial fibrillation, ischemic stroke, and systemic thrombo-embolism (Alomar et al. 2020; Apostolakis et al. 2012). Oral anticoagulation (OAC) is therefore recommended for most hypertensive patients with concomitant cardio-embolic indications, yet the choice between warfarin and the four direct OACs (DOACs; apixaban, dabigatran, edoxaban, and rivaroxaban) remains contentious (Bruins Slot and Berge 2018). Randomized controlled trials have provided conflicting head-to-head comparisons, and meta-analyses have relied on composite bleeding end-points that mask agent-specific, anatomically resolved hazards (Campbell et al. 2023; Dawwas et al. 2021). It is unknown whether hypertensive microangiopathy predisposes more to anticoagulant-associated intracerebral hemorrhage (di Biase et al. 2025). It has been shown that effective control of blood pressure may alter the risk and prognosis of cerebral hemorrhage with DOACs (Dimitriadis et al. 2023). Consequently, clinicians lack granular, real-world evidence to align the benefit–harm balance of individual anticoagulants with the vascular phenotype of chronically hypertensive patients (Elsebaie et al. 2019).

Spontaneous-report pharmacovigilance has emerged as a critical complementary data stream (Gorst-Rasmussen et al. 2016). The FDA Adverse Event (AE) Reporting System (FAERS) captures > 20 million post-marketing safety narratives (Hellenbart et al. 2017; January et al. 2019)and has successfully detected bleeding signals for single DOACs (Jin et al. 2025; Li et al. 2024, 2026). However, previous analyses were limited by mono-algorithm metrics, short observation windows, or a focus on aggregate rather than site-specific end-points. Moreover, no study has simultaneously benchmarked all five OACs across latency, demographic, and reporter strata in a purely hypertensive cohort while enforcing primary-suspect exclusivity and multi-algorithm consensus. Harnessing the full temporal breadth of FAERS may help generate hypotheses to distinguish drug-specific from class-specific bleeding liabilities and to inform hypertension-tailored monitoring strategies, pending confirmatory studies.

Accordingly, we interrogated the entire FAERS repository to perform the first pharmacovigilance comparison of warfarin and four DOACs in hypertensive and non-hypertensive users. This exploratory study aimed to delineate agent-specific bleeding signals, quantify early versus late temporal clustering, and identify demographic and reporter determinants of signal magnitude using FAERS data.

Materials and methods

Study design and data source

We interrogated the FAERS for the entire repository of AE signals associated with OAC use in hypertensive and non-hypertensive patients, spanning Q1 2004 through Q2 2025. FAERS captures spontaneous reports submitted by healthcare professionals, pharmaceutical manufacturers, and consumers worldwide. Each event is coded with the Medical Dictionary for Regulatory Activities (MedDRA), which provides a medically validated, five-level hierarchy that structures the complete pharmacovigilance life-cycle. First released in 2004, the dictionary delivers highly granular, consistently organized data with extensive informational depth. To counteract lexical redundancy and enhance query reproducibility, the MedDRA Maintenance and Support Services Organization introduced Standardized MedDRA Queries (SMQ); these queries are synchronously updated with each MedDRA release to ensure semantic continuity.

Data processing

After excluding non-drug and duplicate reports, we retained only those in which the OAC was recorded as the primary suspect (PS) drug. The PS drug names accepted were: apixaban (Eliquis), warfarin (Coumadin), dabigatran etexilate (Pradaxa), rivaroxaban (Xarelto), edoxaban (Savaysa), and their entry terms in https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/mesh/. The bleeding reports associated with these agents were included for analysis. All AEs were coded with Medical Dictionary for Regulatory Activities (MedDRA, version 25.1) and mapped to preferred terms (PTs) within the system organ class (SOC) hierarchy. PTs signifying any form of hemorrhagic toxicity were retrieved across all SOCs. To capture the most contemporary safety signals, the entire FAERS repository spanning Q1 2004 through Q2 2025 was interrogated.

Because FAERS does not contain a dedicated field for comorbidity diagnosis, hypertension status was ascertained exclusively from the narrative text coded using MedDRA PTs and SMQs. The specific MedDRA PTs used to identify hypertension included (but were not limited to): “Hypertension,” “Essential hypertension,” “Secondary hypertension,” “Hypertensive crisis,” “Hypertensive urgency,” “Malignant hypertension,” and “Labile hypertension.” SMQ broad searches for “Hypertension” were also employed. A patient report was classified as hypertensive if any PT or SMQ term denoting essential hypertension, secondary hypertension, or hypertensive crisis (e.g. “hypertension,” “essential hypertension,” “hypertensive urgency”) was present anywhere in the report, regardless of whether it was listed as an adverse event, medical history, or concomitant condition. All remaining reports, those containing no such hypertension-related term, were classified as non-hypertensive. Reports with ambiguous or possible hypertension-related terms without explicit diagnosis (e.g. “blood pressure increased” without a hypertension code) were assigned to the non-hypertensive group to maintain specificity. We explicitly acknowledge that this approach introduces non-differential misclassification bias: true hypertensive patients who lacked an explicit MedDRA hypertension code (e.g. because the reporter did not list it, because it was not captured in the narrative, or because it was considered unrelated to the adverse event) would be incorrectly assigned to the non-hypertensive group. This misclassification would bias the observed differences between strata toward the null hypothesis, meaning that our reported “hypertensive shift,” including higher fatality signals and redistribution of bleeding topography, likely represents a conservative, lower-bound estimate of the true effect. Conversely, differential misclassification (e.g. more complete recording of hypertension in patients who experienced severe bleeding) is possible but less likely to systematically bias results in a predictable direction. This approach follows established pharmacovigilance practices for comorbidity stratification using FAERS. No external validation against electronic health records or claims data was performed, and the sensitivity and specificity of this coding-based classification method are unknown.

Data mining and statistical analysis

Disproportionality was interrogated through 2 × 2 contingency tables (Supplementary Table 1). To detect AE signals associated with OAC in hypertensive and non-hypertensive patients, we deployed four complementary data-mining algorithms: reporting odds ratio (ROR), proportional reporting ratio (PRR), Bayesian confidence propagation neural network (BCPNN), and multi-item gamma Poisson shrinker (MGPS). ROR minimizes bias when event counts are low; PRR trades sensitivity for heightened specificity; BCPNN excels at integrating and cross-validating heterogeneous data sources; MGPS is particularly powerful for capturing rare-event signals. This conservative four-algorithm concordance rule was deliberately chosen to maximize specificity and minimize false-positive signals, a critical consideration when analyzing five drugs across hundreds of PTs. This high-specificity approach reduces alert fatigue and prevents spurious signals from entering the clinical prioritization matrix. However, we acknowledge that this strict rule reduces sensitivity and may miss signals for rarely reported events or those where only one or two algorithms meet the threshold due to low statistical power. Importantly, disproportionality analyses measure reporting associations, not causal relationships. Positive signals may be influenced by prescribing volume, channeling bias, stimulated reporting, and other unmeasured confounders inherent to FAERS. Thus, all signals should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating, requiring confirmation in independent data sources before causal inference or practice change. For instance, a rare but catastrophic bleeding event with a high ROR but non-significant BCPNN would be excluded. Accordingly, this study represents a high-confidence subset of potential signals, not an exhaustive list of all possible OAC-bleeding associations. Detailed equations and decision thresholds are summarized in the following section (Supplementary Table 2).

Clinical prioritization evaluation

Disproportionality signals that achieved simultaneous positivity across ROR, PRR, BCPNN and MGPS were further stratified through a semi-quantitative priority matrix (Supplementary Table 3). The composite score (0–8) partitioned events into low (0–2), moderate (3–5), or high (6–8) tiers, reflecting both public-health impact and evidence strength. Four orthogonal dimensions were appraised: (1) Clinical salience: Events were cross-walked against the European Medicines Agency catalogs of Important Medical Events (IME) and Designated Medical Events (DME), awarding higher weight to overlapping terms. This weighting is justified by the European Medicines Agency’s regulatory consensus that such events warrant expedited reporting and signal investigation, reflecting established pharmacovigilance practice rather than novel subjectivity. (2) Reporting density: The AE share relative to the entire anticoagulant safety profile was expressed using conventional trial language: very common (≥ 10%), common (1–10%) or uncommon (≤ 1%), with descending scores. The 10% and 1% thresholds align with standard CIOMS working group definitions for adverse event frequency bands, providing an objective anchor. (3) Signal robustness: Consistency of the four-algorithm flag was interrogated across three complementary comparator sets (full database, drug-class restricted, and indication-restricted). Full concordance across all three comparator analyses yielded the maximum rating. This approach minimizes false-positive inflation from comparator selection bias. (4) Fatal outcome ratio: The proportion of fatal reports among all cases of a given AE was computed. Recognizing that underlying oncological morbidity inflates background mortality, we applied a conservative cut-off: only AEs with a case fatality rate > 50% received the top score. This threshold was chosen because an event more likely than not to result in death warrants the highest alert level, a principle consistent with FDA’s “serious and unexpected” adverse event criteria. The composite score (0–8) was then calculated as the sum of scores from each dimension (detailed scoring criteria in Supplementary Table 3). We acknowledge that the weighting scheme, while anchored in regulatory definitions and pharmacovigilance conventions, retains elements of expert judgment and has not been externally validated against independent databases (e.g. EudraVigilance, VigiBase, or electronic health records). The priority hierarchy should be regarded as a way of ordering hypotheses rather than a clear classification of risk categories with clinical significance. Future studies should formally evaluate the matrix’s performance using sensitivity analyses (e.g. varying the fatality rate threshold from 25 to 75%) and external replication in independent spontaneous reporting systems.

Based on the composite score (0–8), bleeding events were classified into three tiers to guide clinical and regulatory attention: High-priority (scores 6–8), events that are almost uniformly fatal, severely disabling, or require immediate life-saving intervention. These signals warrant consideration for label updates, regulatory communication, or changes to clinical monitoring protocols. Moderate-priority (scores 3–5), events that are serious, often require hospitalization, but are not uniformly fatal. These should be incorporated into patient-informed consent documents, shared decision-making tools, and hypertension-specific risk communication. Low-priority (scores 0–2), events that are typically self-limited, require no or minimal intervention, or are procedural in nature (e.g. conjunctival hemorrhage, gingival bleeding, injection site hematoma). These do not alter the benefit-risk balance but may affect patient adherence and quality of life. This tiered framework is intended for hypothesis generation and signal prioritization; external validation in independent real-world cohorts is required before clinical implementation.

Temporal occurrence analysis

For each AE report with a documented drug start date and event onset date, the time interval (in days) between treatment initiation and bleeding event was calculated. Reports with missing, incomplete, or clearly erroneous dates (e.g. event onset before drug start) were excluded from temporal analyses. Intervals were categorized into seven clinically relevant windows: 0–30 days (acute initiation phase, capturing early adverse reactions), 31–60 days, 61–90 days, 91–120 days, 121–180 days (subacute phase), 181–360 days (chronic phase), and > 360 days (very late phase, capturing cumulative or delayed effects). These cutoffs were chosen based on standard pharmacovigilance practice for latency analysis and to align with typical clinical follow-up schedules for anticoagulation monitoring. The absolute number of bleeding AE reports within each interval was then tabulated separately for each OAC and each hypertension stratum.

Statistical analysis

For every AE report, we extracted demographic variables, administrative descriptors, patient outcome, and the interval (in days) between treatment start and event onset. The analyses were performed with R v4.2.3. (Vienna, Austria).

Results

Clinical characteristics of the reports

Clinical characteristics of the reports in non-hypertensive patients

Among 101,469 bleeding-related AE reports linked to OAC in non-hypertensive patients extracted from the FAERS, marked heterogeneity was observed across the five agents under study (Table 1). Rivaroxaban contributed the largest share of cases (N = 50,676; 49.9%), followed by dabigatran etexilate (19,451; 19.2%), apixaban (18,546; 18.3%), warfarin (11,381; 11.2%) and edoxaban (1,415; 1.4%). However, for rivaroxaban, the raw count is influenced by its wider prescribing volume and longer market presence, and should not be misinterpreted as evidence of higher bleeding risk, a concept known as “denominator neglect.” These absolute case counts reflect a combination of true bleeding signals, prescribing volume (market share), and reporting biases; they should not be misinterpreted as directly measuring comparative bleeding risk (a concept known as “denominator neglect”). Disproportionality analyses (ROR, PRR, BCPNN, MGPS) described below partially adjust for background reporting rates to identify signals, but cannot fully substitute for incidence data. We must not regard the original case numbers, mortality rates or imbalances as direct indicators for assessing the relative safety of the drug. For example, a higher ROR for one drug versus another does not necessarily imply a higher true bleeding risk, as differences in prescribing patterns, patient comorbidities, reporting thresholds, and time on market can all affect these metrics. The primary value of this study lies in signal detection (identifying PTs that meet four-algorithm concordance) and clinical prioritization of those signals, endpoints that are less sensitive to denominator effects, not in head-to-head safety ranking. In terms of observed fatality proportions among reported cases, dabigatran showed 23.7%, compared with 21.7% for rivaroxaban and 18.0% for edoxaban. These are descriptive proportions only; they are not adjusted for baseline risk, age, comorbidity, or indication, and therefore should not be used to compare drug-specific fatality risk.

Hospitalization rates followed a similar gradient: rivaroxaban 51.4%, dabigatran 46.1%, edoxaban 48.8%, warfarin 33.7%, and apixaban 31.3%. Notably, 92.4% of edoxaban-associated events were classified as serious, second only to rivaroxaban (97.5%), whereas warfarin exhibited the lowest proportion of serious cases (67.6%). Female predominance was evident for rivaroxaban (46.0%) and dabigatran (44.4%), whereas male cases prevailed with warfarin (54.3%). Age stratification revealed that 65–85 years constituted the most frequently affected cohort across all drugs (31.2%–53.2%), yet rivaroxaban and dabigatran bore a disproportionate burden in patients < 65 years (22.1% and 8.9%, respectively). Under-reporting of body-weight data was common (60.7%–80.3%), but excess weight (> 100 kg) was most prevalent with dabigatran (8.7%) and warfarin (9.1%).

Geographic clustering indicated the United States as the primary reporting source (> 70% for all agents except edoxaban), with Japan contributing a notable minority for apixaban, dabigatran and rivaroxaban. These real-world data suggest a differential safety signal among DOACs in the absence of hypertensive comorbidity, with rivaroxaban and dabigatran suggesting elevated bleeding morbidity, whereas edoxaban, despite limited exposure, carries a concerning fatality signal. Such insights mandate continuous pharmacovigilance and may inform precision medicine algorithms that integrate agent-specific bleeding risk beyond conventional hypertension-adjusted scores.

Clinical characteristics of the reports in hypertensive patients

Among 4,252 bleeding-related AE reports in hypertensive patients, rivaroxaban (N = 1,726; 40.6%) predominated, followed by dabigatran etexilate (N = 1,479; 34.8%), apixaban (N = 661; 15.5%), warfarin (N = 244; 5.7%) and edoxaban (N = 142; 3.3%) (Table 2). As in the non-hypertensive cohort, these raw counts are influenced by market share and reporting patterns and do not directly equate to bleeding risk. Edoxaban had the lowest case count (N = 142). Among these, the observed fatality proportion was 28.9% (41/142), significantly exceeding the proportion of rivaroxaban (21.0%), apixaban (22.5%), dabigatran (17.3%) and warfarin (11.9%; P < 0.001). We emphasize that this estimate is derived from a very small sample size (“small cell” size), which makes it vulnerable to random variation, reporting bias (e.g. a few outlier reports disproportionately influencing the rate), and should be interpreted with extreme caution. Besides, the cross-drug comparisons of these unadjusted proportions are strongly discouraged. This finding should be considered hypothesis-generating only, not as evidence of excess lethality, and requires independent confirmation in larger datasets. Correspondingly, 99.3% of edoxaban reports were coded as “serious,” rivalling rivaroxaban (97.2%) and apixaban (98.0%), but markedly surpassing warfarin (74.6%). Hospitalization occurred in > 50% of cases for rivaroxaban (53.9%) and edoxaban (51.4%), compared with 38–43% for the other agents. Life-threatening events were most frequent with apixaban (17.7%) and dabigatran (9.7%). Age distribution was skewed toward the 65–85 years stratum (64–66% across DOACs), yet rivaroxaban carried the heaviest burden in patients < 65 years (17.4%). Female representation was the highest with dabigatran (51.9%) and lowest with edoxaban (25.4%). Weight data were missing in 47–61% of reports; when available, obesity (> 100 kg) was over-represented with dabigatran (8.0%) and rivaroxaban (6.4%).

Comparison of clinical characteristics of the reports in non-hypertensive and hypertensive patients

Comparative pharmacovigilance of bleeding-related FAERS reports revealed that hypertension is a qualitative effect-modifier that reshapes the safety hierarchy of OACs (Tables 1 and 2). In non-hypertensive patients (N = 101,469), the absolute event load was driven by rivaroxaban (49.9%) and dabigatran (19.2%), with dabigatran displaying the highest case-fatality rate (23.7%). Once hypertension was present (N = 4,252; 4.0% of total), the relative contribution of dabigatran fell to 34.8% while rivaroxaban remained dominant (40.6%); most importantly, edoxaban, already carrying an 18.0% fatality signal in non-hypertensive subjects, saw its case-lethality escalate to 28.9%, translating into a 1.61-fold hypertensive versus non-hypertensive excess. Although statistically significant, the wide confidence interval (reflecting the small sample size) and the potential for “small cell” instability warrant cautious interpretation; this finding should be considered hypothesis-generating rather than definitive. Conversely, warfarin’s death rate was attenuated by hypertension (11.9% vs 8.7%), and dabigatran showed only a modest rise (17.3%). The hypertensive cohort was older (≈65% aged 65–85 years vs 43–53% in non-hypertensives) and more obese (> 100 kg: 6–8% vs 4–9%), yet the fraction of “serious” reports reached 99.3% for edoxaban and 97–98% for the direct factor Xa inhibitors regardless of blood-pressure stratum, while warfarin seriousness remained lower (67.6% non-hypertensive, 74.6% hypertensive). Geographic signal origin also diverged: U.S. reports dominated both strata, but Japan contributed disproportionately to dabigatran events among hypertensives, arguing against regional reporting bias as the sole explanation. Collectively, these real-world data suggest that hypertension may amplify agent-specific hemorrhagic fatality, with a signal shift in the risk apex from dabigatran to edoxaban.

Number of reported bleeding AEs in different years for five OACs

Among 101,469 spontaneous reports of non-hypertensive users of OAC uploaded to FAERS between 2004 and 2025, the annual case-count trajectories differed markedly across the five agents (Fig. 1). Warfarin dominated early reporting (292 cases in 2004, rising to a peak of 1288 in 2016). Thereafter its yearly totals declined steadily, reaching 56 in 2025; the cumulative count was 11,381. Rivaroxaban entered the database in 2009 (1 case) and escalated rapidly after market launch, surpassing warfarin in 2012 (722 vs 423 cases) and peaking at 10,181 reports in 2020. With 50,676 cases it contributed exactly half of the entire study cohort, though this reflects its widespread use and longer market presence relative to some DOACs, not necessarily a higher intrinsic bleeding risk. Apixaban reporting began in 2007 (2 cases), accelerated after 2013, and reached its maximum in 2017 (2,407 cases); the 20-year sum was 18,546, making it the third most commonly reported agent. Dabigatran etexilate appeared in 2010 (28 cases), peaked earlier than the other DOACs (3,816 cases in 2014), then contracted sharply to 78 cases in 2025; the overall tally was 19,451. Edoxaban, the latest entrant (87 cases in 2015), never exceeded 179 reports in any single year and totaled 1,415 cases, the smallest contribution among the five drugs.

Temporal occurrence of bleeding AEs

Among non-hypertensive FAERS reporters, the temporal distribution of AE reports differed markedly across the five OAC (Fig. 3A). It is critical to emphasize that these temporal patterns reflect a combination of true bleeding events, drug utilization patterns (e.g. market share, time on market, prescribing trends), and reporting biases (e.g. stimulated reporting after launch, the Weber effect). Consequently, the absolute number of reports in each interval, and comparisons between drugs or intervals, should not be interpreted as directly reflecting the time-varying bleeding risk of any drug. For example, the higher early counts for rivaroxaban and dabigatran may partly reflect stimulated reporting after their market entry, while warfarin’s delayed tail reflects its long-standing, stable reporting pattern with less stimulated reporting. No denominator data (e.g. total patient-years of exposure per drug) are available in FAERS. Therefore, cross-drug comparisons or cross-interval comparisons of raw report counts are scientifically invalid for estimating comparative safety or temporal hazard patterns. This figure serves only to illustrate the raw data structure and should be disregarded for any risk inference. Readers are strongly cautioned against citing or interpreting this figure in any comparative manner. During the first 30 days after initiation, rivaroxaban generated the largest raw count (n = 5677), followed by dabigatran etexilate (n = 1806) and apixaban (n = 1,053); warfarin and edoxaban yielded 701 and 149 reports, respectively. From 61–90 days onward, absolute counts declined for all agents; nevertheless, the relative gap between rivaroxaban and its comparators continued to widen incrementally. By 91–120 days, the rivaroxaban:apixaban ratio reached 6.0 (983 vs 165), whereas the rivaroxaban:warfarin ratio peaked at 8.6. After 180 days, the temporal gradient reversed: warfarin accumulated 1,715 reports beyond 360 days, surpassing both apixaban and edoxaban. Edoxaban exhibited the smallest absolute accrual in every interval, producing only 73 reports in 181–360 days and 89 thereafter. Thus, as visually confirmed in Fig. 3A, early-phase signal (0–30 days) was the highest for rivaroxaban, apixaban, and edoxaban, whereas warfarin and dabigatran etexilate displayed a delayed yet sustained reporting pattern beyond one year (particularly warfarin in the > 360 days interval).

In hypertensive FAERS reporters, early-phase event accrual was dominated by dabigatran etexilate and rivaroxaban, with 246 and 226 reports in the first 30 days, respectively (Fig. 3B). From 61–90 days onward, absolute counts fell, yet the relative hierarchy persisted. Rivaroxaban consistently generated nearly twofold reports of apixaban and tenfold of warfarin. Warfarin accumulated 36 reports beyond 360 days, quadrupling its 0–30-day tally and compressing the rivaroxaban:warfarin ratio to nearly 4. Edoxaban never exceeded 21 reports in any single period. Thus, as shown in Fig. 3B, among hypertensive patients dabigatran and rivaroxaban produced the highest early reporting burden (0–30 days), warfarin exhibited a delayed but perceptible late rise (> 360 days), and edoxaban and warfarin consistently generated the small absolute event counts across the entire observation window.

Comparing the two strata, the non-hypertensive cohort yielded 25-fold more 0–30-day reports for Rivaroxaban than the hypertensive cohort (5677 vs 226), whereas the corresponding ratio for Warfarin was 78-fold (701 vs 9), indicating a disproportionately lower capture of early Warfarin events in hypertension. However, these ratios are influenced not only by true differences in bleeding incidence but also by differences in prescribing volume, reporting practices, and hypertension documentation rates between the two strata. Therefore, cross-stratum comparisons of absolute report counts should be interpreted with particular caution. Conversely, the early dabigatran excess in hypertension (246 vs 1806) translated into the smallest non-hypertensive/hypertensive ratio (7.3) among the five drugs. These divergent trajectories remained consistent across all follow-up intervals without rank reversal within either stratum. Warfarin accounted for 50% of the occurrence of AE in different patients over 360 days.

Signal detection at the PT level

Signal detection at the PT level in non-hypertensive patients

Because a signal was affirmed only when all four algorithms (ROR, PRR, BCPNN, MGPS) simultaneously met their predefined thresholds, the following PT-level lists represent a high-confidence subset of potential associations; rare or borderline signals detected by fewer than four algorithms were excluded by design. The number of positive signal of bleeding AEs at the PT level for apixaban, dabigatran etexilate, warfarin, rivaroxaban, and edoxaban were 116, 110, 117, 159, and 66 in non-hypertensive patients, respectively. Table 3 summarizes the 10 most strongly associated bleeding PTs ranked by ROR for each OAC in the non-hypertensive stratum. For apixaban, the top bleeding signal was hemorrhagic cholecystitis (ROR 41.71; 95%CI 24.71–70.4), followed by hemorrhagic hepatic cyst (ROR 41.01; 95%CI 23.06–72.96). All ten PTs showed ROR > 20, with pericardial hemorrhage contributing the largest absolute count (n = 185). Bayesian metrics (MGPS EBGM05) confirmed robustness. For dabigatran etexilate, lower gastrointestinal hemorrhage generated the highest ROR (72.21; 95%CI 67.71–77.01) and the greatest case burden (n = 1,118). For warfarin, arteriovenous graft-site hemorrhage produced an extreme ROR of 174.5 (95%CI 63.4–480), although only five cases were recorded. More clinically relevant signals included thyroid hemorrhage (ROR 130.9; n = 12) and central-nervous-system hemorrhage (ROR 78..07; n = 55). Gastric hemorrhage remained the commonest event (n = 882; ROR 52.69).

Comparison of signal detection at the PT level in non-hypertensive and hypertensive patients

Using the FAERS repository, we compared bleeding-related PTs reported with apixaban, dabigatran etexilate, warfarin, rivaroxaban, and edoxaban in non-hypertensive (Table 3) versus hypertensive patients (Table 4). Across all OAC, the PT-level counts of positive bleeding-safety signals were consistently higher in patients without hypertension than in those with hypertension. In the absence of hypertension, the strongest pharmacovigilance signals were concentrated in the gastrointestinal and vascular malformation spectra. Rivaroxaban showed the highest ROR for any single PT: hemorrhagic arteriovenous malformation, while dabigatran generated the largest case series and a robust signal for lower-gastrointestinal hemorrhage. Warfarin was prominently linked to thyroid hemorrhage and adrenal hemorrhage. Apixaban produced its largest ROR in rare sites hemorrhagic cholecystitis, whereas edoxaban’s highest signal was putamen hemorrhage. When hypertension was present, the overall signal pattern shifted toward deep intracerebral and traumatic bleeding. Apixaban now displayed its greatest ROR for basal-ganglia hemorrhage, followed by traumatic intracranial hemorrhage. Dabigatran’s leading signal remained lower-gastrointestinal bleeding, but the ROR fell to 19.79. Warfarin was most strongly associated with adrenal hemorrhage. Rivaroxaban again generated the single highest ROR in the data set—hemorrhagic arteriovenous malformation, while edoxaban’s dominant cerebral signal persisted with putamen hemorrhage.

Thus, hypertension not only increases bleeding report frequency but also modifies the anatomical distribution of high-signal events. Non-hypertensive cases are driven by gastrointestinal and structural malformation bleeding, whereas hypertensive patients exhibit a conspicuous excess of deep, traumatic and adrenal hemorrhagic complications across all five OACs.

Discussion

With the continuous increase in the global burden of hypertension (Li et al. 2025), the fine-tuning of anticoagulant safety has become an urgent public-health priority (Liu et al. 2023; Martinez et al. 2018). Randomized trials that underlie current guideline recommendations were powered for thrombo-embolic end-points and typically reported composite bleeding outcomes that mask site-specific, latency-dependent, or demographic heterogeneity (Mills et al. 2016; Morris et al. 2024). Our interrogation of the entire FAERS provided the first head-to-head, multi-algorithm consensus pharmacovigilance map of five OAC restricted to hypertensive and non-hypertensive users, thereby generating hypotheses that address a recognized evidence gap. By enforcing primary-suspect exclusivity and requiring simultaneous positivity across ROR, PRR, BCPNN and MGPS (Osman et al. 2025; Parry-Jones et al. 2025), we reduced false-discovery rates inherent to single-metric screens and improved upon some methodological aspects of earlier FAERS-based studies, while retaining the inherent limitations of spontaneous reporting (Pisters et al. 2010).

Before interpreting the specific findings, an important methodological caveat warrants emphasis. Disproportionality analyses measure reporting associations, not causal relationships or comparative incidence. Raw case counts cannot be interpreted as measures of drug risk because they are heavily influenced by prescribing volume (market share), time on market, stimulated reporting, and channeling bias. Therefore, all findings reported here are hypothesis-generating and must be confirmed in independent data sources (e.g. electronic health record cohorts, prospective registries) before any causal inference or clinical practice change is warranted.

Among 105,721 spontaneously reported hemorrhagic events spanning 2004Q1–2025Q2, the presence of hypertension was associated with a shift in the observed case-fatality signal of warfarin (11.9%), apixaban (22.5%), and edoxaban (28.9%), which were higher than those for non-hypertensive patients despite the significant differences in quantity. For edoxaban, however, because this estimate is based on a small number of reports (n= 142), it is unstable and susceptible to “small cell” bias. We therefore caution against over-interpretation of this specific signal. At present, this finding should be regarded as generating the hypothesis that edoxaban may warrant closer safety monitoring in hypertensive patients, rather than as definitive evidence of excess lethality. Independent replication in larger, controlled cohorts and pharmacovigilance databases (e.g. EudraVigilance, VigiBase, JADER) is essential before any regulatory or clinical conclusion can be drawn. Until such validation is available, this signal should be demoted from a conclusive finding to a preliminary observation that can inform future hypothesis testing but not clinical practice or regulatory action. Rivaroxaban accounted for the largest absolute number of bleeding reports in both strata. However, as discussed in the limitations, these raw counts primarily reflect rivaroxaban’s high prescribing volume and should not be misinterpreted as evidence of excess bleeding risk without denominator adjustment. However, there is no evidence that warfarin modifies mortality in patients with elevated blood pressure, and the benefits and harms of non-vitamin K antagonist OAC in this population have not been studied in clinical trials (Ruff et al. 2014). This underscores the value of real-world pharmacovigilance for generating rich signal that requires confirmatory studies. Our finding that hypertension shifted the anatomical topography from gastrointestinal and vascular-malformation bleeding toward deep cerebral, cerebellar, and adrenal territories is biologically plausible. The mechanism may be due to that lipohyalinosis caused by chronic hypertension mainly affects the basilar-thalamic penetrating arteries, increasing the fragility of their vessel walls, and making them more prone to rupture and bleeding under anticoagulant or antithrombotic conditions (Sakaeda et al. 2013). Importantly, this shift from gastrointestinal to deep intracerebral bleeding in hypertensive patients is not specific to OACs; it represents a general feature of hypertensive vasculopathy that would be expected regardless of the antithrombotic agent used. However, our data raise the hypothesis that different OAC classes may differentially exploit this underlying vascular fragility. Specifically, factor Xa inhibitors (rivaroxaban, apixaban, edoxaban) generated stronger disproportionate signals for deep cerebral hemorrhage (basal ganglia, thalamus, putamen, cerebellum) in hypertensive patients compared with dabigatran (a direct thrombin inhibitor) or warfarin (a multi-factor inhibitor), although these observations are hypothesis-generating and require confirmation in studies with exposure denominators and detailed blood pressure control data.

Temporal occurrence patterns in this spontaneous reporting dataset showed early-phase rivaroxaban predominance in both hypertensive and non-hypertensive users, whereas warfarin exhibited a delayed but sustained reporting tail beyond 360 days. However, as discussed below, these patterns are confounded by drug utilization trends, market share differences, and reporting biases, and should not be interpreted as direct evidence of differential temporal safety profiles. The strongest singular signal was hemorrhagic arteriovenous malformation of rivaroxaban in hypertensive (ROR 501.54) and non-hypertensive users (ROR 392.45), a finding that aligns with Danish registry observations of excess bleeding of rivaroxaban therapy (Sakaeda et al. 2014). Conversely, apixaban clustered around rare but catastrophic cavity bleeds such as hemorrhagic cholecystitis in non-hypertensive patients and hemorrhage in the brain in hypertensive patients, a pattern not previously emphasized in RCTs or claims-based analyses (Sarker et al. 2015; Shantsila et al. 2022). Dabigatran displayed a cohesive lower-gastrointestinal profile in both groups. Warfarin signals were dominated by diffuse, procedure-associated sites (thyroid, arteriovenous graft), and a prolonged late tail beyond one year, reflecting the complex pharmacogenomics and narrow therapeutic index that mandate life-long intensity monitoring (Shimada et al. 2019). Edoxaban constrained by smaller numbers (only 142 reports in hypertensives), centered on deep cerebral structures (thalamus, putamen); however, these anatomical signals are more vulnerable to random variation and require confirmation in larger datasets. Collectively, these agent-specific topographies suggest potential differences that may challenge a simple “class-effect” paradigm and support further investigation into hypertension-tailored drug selection. We emphasize that the temporal patterns shown in Fig.3 are descriptive and hypothesis-generating; they do not account for changes in prescribing volume over time or for potential reporting biases such as the Weber effect (peak reporting in the second year after drug launch). An important caveat regarding the temporal occurrence analysis (Fig. 3) warrants explicit discussion. The observation that rivaroxaban and dabigatran generated more early-phase reports (0–30 days) while warfarin exhibited a delayed tail beyond 360 days is descriptively accurate for this spontaneous reporting dataset. However, these patterns are confounded by drug utilization trends: rivaroxaban and dabigatran entered the market later (2009–2010) and were adopted rapidly for stroke prevention in atrial fibrillation, coinciding with increased pharmacovigilance awareness and stimulated reporting. Warfarin, by contrast, has been available for decades; its reporting patterns reflect a mature, stable safety profile with less stimulated reporting. The Weber effect, a peak in adverse event reporting during the second year after drug launch followed by a decline, may also contribute to the observed patterns for the DOACs. Consequently, the temporal distributions shown in Fig. 3 should not be interpreted as evidence that rivaroxaban or dabigatran carry higher early bleeding risk than warfarin, nor that warfarin’s risk is uniquely delayed. Definitive conclusions about the timing of bleeding risk require incidence data from large electronic health record cohorts or randomized controlled trials with standardized follow-up and adjustment for prescribing volumes.

Our findings on DOACs-associated bleeding in hypertensive and non-hypertensive patients should be interpreted alongside evidence from other high-risk cardiovascular populations. A recent network meta-analysis specifically examined antithrombotic strategies in patients with atrial fibrillation undergoing percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) (Southworth et al. 2013). Among 11,542 patients pooled from randomized controlled trials, dual antithrombotic therapy (DAT) based on a DOAC significantly reduced major bleeding compared with triple antithrombotic therapy (TAT) based on a vitamin K antagonist (RR 0.57, 95%CI 0.40–0.82), with DAT-based DOACs ranking as the safest strategy (P-score = 0.91). Although our study did not specifically isolate the atrial fibrillation + PCI subgroup, several points of convergence and complementarity are noteworthy. First, both analyses consistently point to a differential bleeding profile among DOACs versus VKAs, supporting the hypothesis that drug selection matters for bleeding outcomes. Second, the meta-analysis focused on a homogeneous, high-ischemic-risk population (atrial fibrillation + PCI) with standardized antithrombotic regimens, whereas our study reflects real-world, heterogeneous OAC users with and without hypertension, providing complementary generalizability. Third, neither study can establish causality: the meta-analysis is subject to trial selection bias and short follow-up, while our FAERS analysis is limited by spontaneous reporting biases. Importantly, neither the meta-analysis nor our study specifically examined whether hypertension modifies the bleeding risk of DAT versus TAT in the post-PCI setting. Future prospective studies or individual-patient-data meta-analyses should address whether hypertensive patients with atrial fibrillation + PCI derive differential benefit from DOACs-based DAT compared with VKA-based regimens, and whether the agent-specific bleeding signals we observed (e.g. edoxaban’s higher fatality signal in hypertensives) are replicated in the PCI population.

The other striking innovation lies in the anatomically resolved priority matrix that couples disproportionality strength with clinical salience, reporting density, and fatality rate. Clinical priority scoring identified brain-stem hemorrhage as the only universally tier-5 event across all DOACs, while cerebral microhemorrhage was flagged moderate only in rivaroxaban in non-hypertensive patients. Edoxaban further generated unique tier-5 signals for cerebellar and pulmonary alveolar bleeding in both strata.

From a clinical management perspective, our latency analysis offers hypothesis-generating observations that, if confirmed, could provide actionable guidance. The front-loaded vigilance during the first 30–60 days was most critical for rivaroxaban and dabigatran, whereas warfarin required sustained surveillance extending well beyond one year, particularly in patients with labile international normalised ratio control. The absence of high-priority (score ≥ 6) events for any agent in our matrix provided reassuring evidence that fatal on-label bleeding remained rare once channeling and reporting biases were accounted for. Nevertheless, the moderate-priority intracranial and visceral events that we identified should be incorporated into hypertension-specific risk-communication tools, informed consent documents, and point-of-care clinical decision support systems. Whether the observed agent-specific differences in deep cerebral hemorrhage signals reflect true pharmacodynamic distinctions or are artifacts of channeling bias (e.g. preferential prescribing of certain DOACs to patients with poorer blood pressure control) cannot be determined from FAERS data. Nevertheless, the hypothesis that factor Xa inhibitors may be more prone than dabigatran to exploit hypertension-induced microvascular fragility is biologically plausible, given that thrombin generation has local protective effects on vessel integrity, and direct thrombin inhibition might theoretically preserve some perivascular homeostasis that factor Xa inhibition does not. This speculative mechanism warrants investigation in preclinical models and prospective clinical cohorts.

The temporal patterns shown in Fig. 3 are based on absolute case counts and are therefore uninterpretable for estimating time-varying bleeding risk or for comparing drugs. As noted in the results, raw FAERS counts are influenced by market share, time on market, the Weber effect, and stimulated reporting, none of which are adjusted for. Consequently, the observation that rivaroxaban generated more early-phase raw reports than warfarin does not imply higher early bleeding risk; it likely reflects rivaroxaban’s larger prescribing volume and more intensive post-marketing surveillance after its launch. Conversely, warfarin’s relatively higher raw count after 360 days does not indicate a uniquely delayed risk, but rather its decades-long availability and stable reporting pattern. We explicitly advise not to use Fig. 3 for any comparative or temporal risk inference. This section was retained solely for data transparency and to illustrate the profound impact of denominator neglect in spontaneous reporting analyses. For any valid assessment of temporal bleeding risk, future studies must use electronic health record cohorts or registry data with exposure denominators and time-to-event analyses.

Several important limitations inherent to spontaneous reporting systems must be critically acknowledged. FAERS is subject to underreporting (the majority of adverse events are never reported), stimulated reporting around regulatory milestones or media attention (e.g. Weber effect), and reporting biases (e.g. disproportionate reporting of severe or novel events). Furthermore, the absence of denominator data (i.e. total number of patients exposed to each drug) precludes calculation of incidence rates or relative risks, meaning that our disproportionality analyses can only generate signals of association, not causal estimates of bleeding probability. Despite our efforts to deduplicate exact reports and restrict analyses to primary-suspect drugs, residual duplicate entries and channeling bias (e.g. preferential prescribing of certain OACs to lower-risk patients) cannot be fully excluded. Missing data on weight, renal function, concurrent antiplatelet therapy, and blood pressure values (hypertension status was derived from MedDRA coding rather than measured values) further limited adjustment for validated bleeding-risk scores (e.g. HAS-BLED, ATRIA) (Sridharan and Sivaramakrishnan 2025; Sun et al. 2022). Consequently, our findings should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating and are best used to guide future prospective studies rather than to establish causal relationships. Throughout the manuscript, we have therefore replaced strong causal language with terminology reflecting associational signal detection (e.g. “signal,” “association,” “reporting pattern” instead of “cause,” “effect,” or “risk”).

Our requirement for simultaneous positivity across all four disproportionality algorithms (ROR, PRR, BCPNN, MGPS) was intentionally conservative to prioritize specificity and minimize false-positive discoveries, especially given the large number of comparisons (five drugs × hundreds of PTs). However, this approach inevitably reduces sensitivity and may overlook clinically relevant signals, particularly for rare adverse events or those where reporting patterns are captured by only one or two algorithms due to low case counts, variance instability, or algorithm-specific mathematical properties (e.g. BCPNN’s shrinkage factor penalizing very small numerators). For example, a bleeding event with a strong ROR but an EBGM05 below the MGPS threshold due to a case count of 3 would be excluded from our analysis. Consequently, our signal set is best interpreted as a high-confidence subset of potential OAC-bleeding associations rather than a comprehensive census. Future pharmacovigilance studies could adopt a more sensitive screening strategy (e.g. positivity in any two algorithms) followed by independent replication in a separate database (e.g. EudraVigilance, JADER) to capture additional signals while controlling false discovery.

Hypertension status was derived indirectly from MedDRA-coded terms in FAERS narrative texts, a methodology that carries inherent and substantial misclassification risk. We initially posited non-differential misclassification (i.e. true hypertensive patients lacking an explicit code being assigned to the non-hypertensive group), which would bias effect estimates toward the null, rendering our observed “hypertensive shift” a conservative estimate. However, a reporter is decidedly more inclined to document a patient’s history of hypertension when the AE is a catastrophic hypertensive bleed (e.g. basal ganglia hemorrhage, intracerebral hemorrhage) compared to a non-hypertensive event (e.g. mild gastrointestinal bleeding). Such differential recording would systematically concentrate true hypertensive patients in the “hypertensive” stratum for severe, hypertension-congruent bleeding events, thereby artificially inflating the strength of the observed associations, including the higher fatality signals and the shift toward deep intracerebral bleeding topography. Consequently, while our data cannot disprove the existence of a genuine hypertensive effect-modification, the magnitude of the reported “hypertensive shift,” particularly the increased proportionate reporting of deep cerebral hemorrhages and the elevated fatality signal for edoxaban, may be substantially overestimated due to this differential misclassification bias. We have therefore reframed our conclusions accordingly, emphasizing that these findings represent hypothesis-generating signals requiring cautious interpretation and independent confirmation in studies with prospectively, objectively ascertained blood pressure measurements, rather than quantifiable effect estimates for clinical decision-making. This limitation fundamentally precludes any causal inference or direct comparison of relative safety between strata based on the present data. Among hypertensive patients, edoxaban showed the highest observed case-fatality rate, which was significantly higher than for other oral anticoagulants and represented a 1.61-fold excess relative to non-hypertensive edoxaban users. However, this finding is based on a relatively small number of reports, leading to greater uncertainty. Therefore, while the edoxaban signal warrants further investigation, it should not be overinterpreted as definitive evidence of excess lethality. Our study can only generate signals for further investigation; it cannot provide stable risk estimates for uncommon drug-event pairs in small subgroups. Readers and policymakers should therefore place little weight on the precise fatality percentage for edoxaban in hypertensives; instead, the appropriate interpretation is that this signal, albeit weak and unstable, merits hypothesis-driven evaluation in larger, independent datasets with proper denominator data.

The clinical prioritization matrix, while structured, incorporates subjective elements. As correctly noted by the reviewer, the thresholds for fatality rate (> 50%) and reporting density (10%, 1%) are based on regulatory pharmacovigilance guidelines (CIOMS, EMA) but lack empirical validation. We fully acknowledge this limitation: the cutoffs were chosen as pragmatic, rule-based anchors to operationalize the matrix transparently and reproducibly, not because they have been proven optimal or externally calibrated. The scoring system also assumes equal weighting across four dimensions (salience, density, robustness, fatality), a pragmatic simplification that may undervalue rare but uniformly fatal events. Several biases may affect score assignment: reporting bias (severe outcomes preferentially reported), channeling bias (sicker patients receiving certain drugs), and detection bias (easily diagnosed events overrepresented). Consequently, priority tiers should be interpreted as signal prioritization for further investigation, not as definitive clinical risk rankings. Future studies should empirically validate or recalibrate these thresholds by testing alternative cutoffs (e.g. fatality rate thresholds of 25% or 75%) in sensitivity analyses, and by correlating the matrix’s priority tiers with known bleeding risk scores (e.g. HAS-BLED) or with incidence rates from independent cohorts. Cross-database replication in EudraVigilance or VigiBase is also needed to assess the robustness of the priority ranking across different pharmacovigilance systems. All findings reported here are exploratory and hypothesis-generating, not suitable for causal inference or immediate practice change without independent confirmation. Raw case counts of bleeding events do not represent bleeding risk or comparative safety. FAERS lacks denominator data, and reporting rates are influenced by prescribing volume, market share, time on market, and reporting biases. Disproportionality analyses partially address these limitations by comparing observed versus expected proportions but cannot fully correct for market share differences or channeling bias. Therefore, readers should not interpret raw case counts or relative proportions as evidence of comparative bleeding risk. The value of this study lies in signal detection (positive PTs meeting four-algorithm concordance) and clinical prioritization of those signals, endpoints less sensitive to denominator effects. Definitive conclusions about comparative drug safety require incidence data from large electronic health record cohorts or randomized trials.

Future research should integrate FAERS signals with large-scale electronic health records to validate incidence rates and to adjust for confounders such as age, renal function, and concomitant medications (Wang et al. 2023). Machine-learning approaches that incorporate natural-language processing of free-text narratives could refine anatomical localization and uncover novel bleeding phenotypes masked by PT aggregation. Prospectively, hypertension-enriched cohorts comparing site-specific bleeding rates across the five agents will be essential to translate our disproportionality findings into quantitative risk estimates that can directly inform guideline updates. Clinical pharmacists can actively minimize drug-related problems by monitoring laboratory and clinical response, identifying interacting co-medications, and educating patients on adherence (January et al. 2019; Wilson et al. 2024; Zhang et al. 2019; Zhou et al. 2021). Future studies should be formally evaluated in prospective pharmacist-driven anticoagulation stewardship trials. Finally, examination of genetic polymorphisms affecting warfarin metabolism or DOACs transport proteins may elucidate the mechanistic underpinnings of the late-tail warfarin signal and of the cerebral predilection observed with edoxaban, paving the way for precision anticoagulation in the non-hypertensive and hypertensive population.

Conclusion

Within the limitations of spontaneous reporting, hypertension emerged as a qualitative effect-modifier for OAC-related bleeding, with edoxaban showing a numerically higher observed fatality proportion in the hypertensive subgroup (based on only 142 reports, a small sample that is prone to instability). This finding is hypothesis-generating only, not confirmatory. Due to potential misclassification of hypertension status, the true association may be stronger than observed. Temporal patterns reflect a complex interplay of events, utilization trends, and reporting biases, not direct evidence of peak bleeding risk. Disproportionality signals are hypothesis-generating, not causal; our prioritization matrix requires external validation. These findings may inform future hypotheses and guideline updates but not direct clinical practice, pending randomized evidence.
