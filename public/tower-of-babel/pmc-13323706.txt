Learning fuzzy representations for hypergraph node classification
Authors: Zhishu Sun, Ruijing Geng, Ge Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAs an extension of standard graphs, hypergraphs have demonstrated significant advantages in modeling high-order complex relationships compared with standard graphs. Existing literature has witnessed the great success of hypergraph representation learning methods in classifying nodes. However, most of them seek to obtain low-dimensional crisp representations, overlooking the fuzzy and uncertain nature of node attributes. In fact, node attributes such as paper keywords may contain noise or be incomplete, which leads to uncertain semantics. To address this issue, in this paper, we propose learning fuzzy representations for hypergraph node classification. Specifically, we develop a novel method called Hypergraph Collaborative Fuzzy Network (HyperCFN), which studies hypergraph representations with fuzzy logic. Firstly, HyperCFN augments the original hypergraph into two hypergraphs, which are then put into the proposed fuzzy hypergraph encoders. The fuzzy hypergraph encoders consist of hypergraph collaborative networks and fuzzy logic to learn fuzzy representations for every node and hyperedge. Subsequently, the learned representations are enforced node-, hyperedge-, and membership-level contrast. Lastly, to further preserve the hypergraph structure, we develop decoders to reconstruct the augmented hypergraphs. We perform extensive experiments on several datasets, and the promising results demonstrate that the effectiveness of the proposed model and learning fuzzy representations for hypergraphs is valid.Keywords: Hypergraphs, Fuzzy logic, Fuzzy representation, Node classification, Contrastive learningSubject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

Graph representation learning1–5 has been a hot topic in the field of artificial intelligence in recent years. Traditional graphs, a.k.a., standard graphs, can effectively model pair-wise relationships between nodes but struggle to study high-order complex relationships in many real-world scenarios. Hypergraphs6–11, as an extension of standard graphs, have emerged as a powerful tool for modeling complex high-order relationships in diverse domains, ranging from social networks12 to multimedia analysis13. Unlike standard graphs limiting to pair-wise relations, an edge (a hyperedge) in hypergraphs can connect more than three nodes, enabling richer representations of real-world systems such as academic networks6,14 and traffic forecasting15. For example, in academic networks, hypergraphs can represent collaborations among multiple authors on a single paper.

Recent advances have witnessed the remarkable success of hypergraph representation learning models in node classification. For instance, Feng et al.16 proposed HyperGraph Neural Networks (HGNN) to extend convolutional operations to hypergraphs and obtain promising results. Moreover, Dong et al.17 developed Hypergraph Networks with Hyperedge Neurons (HNHN) to introduce the hyperedge information into representation learning. Specifically, HNHN learns representations of nodes and hyperedges alternatively. Furthermore, Wu et al.6 proposed Hypergraph Collaborative Network (HCoN) to learn features of nodes and hyperedges by aggregating both node and hyperedge information layer-by-layer, such that the information loss is reduced. It turns out that the hyperedges contain valuable information for learning effective node features. More recently, contrastive learning18 has been introduced to hypergraph learning due to its good capacity in learning effective features. For example, Wu et al.19 proposed Collaborative Contrastive Learning (CCL), which first generates a standard graph and then performs contrast between representations learned by convolutional operations on the generated standard graph and original hypergraph. In20, Lee et al. developed Tri-directional Contrastive Learning (TriCL), including node-, hyperedge-, membership-level contrast, and obtained good performance.

Although the above-mentioned approaches have significantly improved the node classification performance, they predominantly focus on learning low-dimensional crisp representations, often overlooking the inherent uncertainty and fuzziness in node attributes. For instance, in academic networks, the keywords of a paper can usually be incomplete, show ambiguous semantics, or contain noise. This uncertainty can negatively impact the accuracy and reliability of node classification results, which motivates the learning of fuzzy representations for hypergraphs. Fortunately, fuzzy logic provides an effective strategy for handling uncertainty and fuzziness. It can model and reason about uncertain information, offering a potential solution to the fuzzy and uncertain characteristics of node attributes in hypergraphs. In21, Zhang et al. proposed Fuzzy Graph Convolution Neural Network (FGCNN) to incorporate fuzzy logic into node representation learning. Nevertheless, this model is proposed for standard graphs and cannot be directly used in hypergraphs.

Motivated by the above discussions, in this paper, we propose studying fuzzy representation for hypergraphs. The reasons are three-fold. Firstly, crisp representation constitutes a specific case of fuzzy representation22, demonstrating that the fuzzy representation inherently provides enhanced representational capacity. Secondly, different from crisp representations, fuzzy representations introduce a critical theoretical extension through the concept of graded membership degrees23, a property absent in classical crisp systems. Lastly, real-world data usually exhibit inherent ambiguity and imprecise information, necessitating fuzzy analytical approaches21. Quantitatively, fuzzy representations increase the information entropy of node features by up to , where F is the number of Gaussian membership functions per feature dimension, bits per dimension compared with crisp vectors24, thereby offering strictly greater representational capacity for modeling ambiguous semantics such as noisy paper keywords. Specifically, we develop a method named Hypergraph Collaborative Fuzzy Network (HyperCFN), which takes advantage of both node and hyperedge information, contrastive learning, and fuzzy logic. Firstly, considering the power of contrastive learning, we generate two hypergraphs based on the original hypergraph. Then, the two generated hypergraphs are put into fuzzy hypergraph encoders to obtain different representations for contrast. In particular, in view of the effectiveness of involving hyperedge information in representation learning, we adopt HCoN6 as the basis of the proposed fuzzy hypergraph encoder and further improve HCoN with fuzzy logic. Specifically, we attach the fuzzy logic at the end of each layer in HCoN, and this forms the proposed fuzzy hypergraph encoder. As a result, we obtain fuzzy representations for the nodes and hyperedges in each generated hypergraph. Subsequently, according to TriCL20, the tri-directional contrastive learning is effective, and thus we apply node-, hyperedge-, and membership-level contrast to the learned fuzzy representations. Lastly, we develop decoders to reconstruct the augmented hypergraphs to further preserve the hypergraph structure.

Related work

Hypergraph learning

Hypergraphs are treated as an extended form of graphs, and are particularly well-suited to tasks involving complex and multi-faceted data relationships, due to their capacity to naturally represent high-order relationships. Hypergraph learning, a machine learning method based on hypergraphs, was introduced by Zhou et al.25 who also provided a unified framework for hypergraph learning and validation of its superior performance in modeling complex data. Recent advancements in deep learning have prompted a growing number of academics to incorporate hypergraphs within their research, with a view to achieving enhanced outcomes in the processing of complex data. The technology has been applied to a variety of contexts, including computer vision26,27, social networks28, and recommendation systems29–31, with promising success in enhancing performance.

HGNN16 can be regarded as the first work that extends the convolution operation to hypergraphs. In32, Gao et al. proposed HGNN+ to further improve HGNN from the hypergraph modeling and convolution aspects, which is implemented by linking multi-type data with hyperedge and hyperedge groups. HyperGCN33 proposes to train graph convolutional networks (GCN)1 on hypergraphs using spectral theory. Specifically, HyperGCN first converts the hypergraph into a weighted graph and applies standard GCN to learn representations. In17, Dong et al. proposed that the hyperedges contain important information and learning representations for nodes and hyperedges alternatively. Wu et al.6 further developed HCoN to jointly learn the node and hyperedge representations layer-by-layer by incorporating both node and hyperedge information from previous layers.

Recently, contrastive learning18 has demonstrated its effectiveness in learning discriminative feature representations, and researchers have introduced it into hypergraph learning. Zhang et al.34 proposed jointly adopting hypergraph augmentation scheme and contrastive learning to handle the data sparsity issue in group recommendation. Lee et al.20 proposed first generating two augmented views from the original hypergraph and then applying node-, hyperedge-, and membership-level contrast to obtain informative representations.

The above-mentioned methods have achieved promising results in hypergraph representation learning. Nevertheless, they are proposed to learn low-dimensional crisp feature representations. In fact, node attributes usually contain inherent uncertainty and fuzziness. There are very few studies learning fuzzy representations for graph structural data. Zhang et al.21 proposed learning fuzzy node representations by introducing fuzzy logic into graph learning. However,21 is only suitable for standard graphs and cannot be directly applied to hypergraphs. How to learn fuzzy representations for hypergraphs remains an open problem.

Fuzzy graph neural networks

With the rapid advancement of fuzzy machine learning, numerous studies21,35–37 combine graph learning with fuzzy logic to process graph-structured data, thereby addressing key problems such as node classification and link prediction. Zhang et al. proposed the Deep Fuzzy Graph Convolution Neural Network (DFGCNN)21, which deeply incorporates fuzzy logic into graph convolution process. By fully exploiting feature-level uncertainties through mechanisms such as Gaussian membership functions, DFGCNN converts traditional crisp representations into fuzzy representations with membership information, ultimately significantly enhancing the representational expressiveness of graph and network data. In35, to address the limitation of traditional Euclidean distance in handling the uncertainty in graph data, Fuzzy Graph-based Label Propagation (FGLP) uses a novel Fuzzy Distance Matrix (FDM) based on fuzzy logic, and thus structures the undirected fuzzy-weighted graph, which enhances the model’s interpretability. Fuzzy Dynamic Graph representation Learning (FuzzyDGL)36 proposes to apply fuzzy logic to dynamic graph representation learning, aiming to alleviate the impact of intrinsic uncertainties in dynamic graphs and modeling feature fuzziness. Moreover, Lin et al. introduce FuzzyGCN37, a fuzzy neural network model dedicated to representation learning on uncertain graphs. It aims to address the issues that traditional crisp graph neural networks tend to generate unstable representations when processing graph data with inherent uncertainties.

In real-world scenarios, massive data naturally exhibit hypergraph structures, which have motivated a few works38,39 to combine hypergraph learning with fuzzy logic. For instance, Fuzzy Attention Hypergraph Neural Network (HFATN)38 is a representation learning model for hypergraph node classification that introduces fuzzy attention vertex convolution and fuzzy attention hyperedge convolution. It resolves the node and hyperedge uncertainty in existing hypergraph attention networks and improves model classification performance. Fuzzy Hypergraph Contrastive Representation Learning (FHCRL)39 leverages the fuzzy logic from input signals to message passing and inference with this design effectively boosting the model’s interpretability. Furthermore, the model combines contrastive learning to overcome the sparsity and uncertainty of labels.

More recently, several studies have further explored the integration of fuzzy logic with hypergraph learning to better handle uncertainty in node representations. Zhang et al.40 proposed the Fuzzy Attention Hypergraph Neural Network (HFATN), which introduces fuzzy attention mechanisms on both vertices and hyperedges to quantify uncertain contributions and improve node classification under noisy features. Similarly, Xu et al.41 presented the Hypergraph-based Assembly Fuzzy Representation (HARF) framework at NeurIPS 2024, which combines hypergraph modeling with fuzzy representation to address uncertainty and distribution skew in open-set 3D object retrieval through structure fuzzy reconstruction. These works demonstrate the growing interest in fuzzy hypergraph representation learning. However, most existing methods primarily focus on attention-based fuzzification or task-specific uncertainty modeling, while our HyperCFN emphasizes collaborative fuzzy encoding between nodes and hyperedges, combined with multi-level contrastive learning and structure-preserving reconstruction for general hypergraph node classification.

Fuzzy Logic Encoder. We aim to obtain expressive and powerful representations for nodes and hyperedges by learning their fuzzy representations. Nevertheless, fuzzy features significantly increase the number of parameters associated with the feature dimension. Therefore, if initial attributes are obscured at the beginning, it will greatly increase the burden on computational efficiency and make the model susceptible to overfitting. In consequence, we develop the fuzzy logic encoder to introduce fuzziness into node and hyperedge representations learned by the above node and hyperedge encoders, thus learning fuzzy representations for nodes and hyperedges. Theoretically, attaching the fuzzy logic module layer-by-layer enables hierarchical refinement of uncertainty: low-level layers capture local feature ambiguity, while high-level layers model global semantic fuzziness in the hypergraph structure21. Specifically, we attach a fuzzy logic encoder in each layer of node and hyperedge encoders, i.e., Eqs. (2) and (3). The fuzzy logic module is attached after each convolutional layer for two key reasons. First, every layer aggregates information from multi-hop neighbors and hyperedges, which inevitably introduces additional uncertainty. Modeling this uncertainty in the fuzzy domain right away ensures it is properly propagated to later layers. Second, applying fuzzification progressively allows the network to capture uncertainty at multiple scales: early layers focus on local fuzzy patterns, while deeper layers combine these into high-order fuzzy relationships across the entire hypergraph. This approach preserves richer uncertainty information throughout the whole process, rather than adding it only at the final output. Note that we do not use nonlinear activation functions in Eqs. (2) and (3) because the fuzzification process already provides nonlinear properties.

Datasets

We adopt the ACMv947, Citationv147, and DBLPv747 datasets to build semi-supervised node classification tasks. Specifically, we follow48 and preprocess them as co-citation data. We have three classes, i.e., “Artificial Intelligent”, “Computer Vision”, and “Information Security”, in the ACMv9 and Citationv1 datasets and five classes, i.e., “Database”, “Artificial Intelligent”, “Computer Vision”, “Information Security”, and “Networking”, in the DBLPv7 dataset. Each paper is represented by the sparse bag-of-words features extracted from the paper title. As presented in48, here, we also construct two settings on the three datasets and obtain the ACM co-I, Citation co-I, DBLP co-I, ACM co-II, Citation co-II, and DBLP co-II datasets. In each constructed dataset, 20 nodes per class are randomly sampled as training data, and the rest are used as test data. To enhance scalability for larger hypergraphs, future work may incorporate sparse tensor operations or mini-batch sampling within the encoders.

Conclusion

In this paper, we propose a novel hypergraph representation learning method called Hypergraph Collaborative Fuzzy Network (HyperCFN). Specifically, HyperCFN introduces fuzzy logic into hypergraph learning to address the fuzzy and uncertain nature of node attributes in hypergraphs. HyperCFN first generates two different hypergraphs through stochastic hypergraph augmentation functions. Then, it employs fuzzy hypergraph encoders, which are based on HCoN and enhanced with fuzzy logic, to learn fuzzy representations for nodes and hyperedges. To further enhance the discrimination of these representations, we apply multi-level contrastive learning, including node-, hyperedge-, and membership-level contrast. Lastly, we develop decoders to reconstruct the augmented hypergraphs, thus preserving their hypergraph structure. The extensive experiments on several real-world datasets demonstrate that HyperCFN outperforms state-of-the-art approaches in hypergraph node classification tasks. Furthermore, the flexible design of HyperCFN can be readily migrated to other hypergraph tasks. For example, while link prediction may adapt the decoder reconstruction loss as the primary objective, retaining contrastive learning for representation quality. One limitation of the current method is that it works in a transductive setting, which means it cannot make predictions for sequential nodes. Moreover, in the proposed method, the hypergraph convolution is based on the hypergraph Laplacian matrix, which has limited capability when dealing with ultra-large-scale datasets due to the high computational and memory costs.
