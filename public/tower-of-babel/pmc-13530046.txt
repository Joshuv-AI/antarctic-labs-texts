A Dual Regulatory Model Linking Alexithymia to Internet Addiction: Affective and Cognitive Pathways
Authors: Gauri Nagyal, Smita Pandey
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundInternet addiction is increasingly conceptualised as a disturbance in affective and cognitive self-regulation rather than excessive use. Alexithymia, characterised by impaired emotional awareness, may heighten vulnerability by reducing the ability to identify and regulate emotions, thereby increasing the chances of indulging in maladaptive coping behaviours such as internet addiction.PurposeThe study aimed to examine the mechanism underlying alexithymia and internet addiction by investigating the mediating roles of psychological distress (affective dysregulation) and mindfulness (cognitive-regulatory capacity) in college students.MethodsA cross-sectional, quantitative research study was carried out among college students (n = 316) recruited from universities in the Gandhinagar district, Gujarat. Ethical clearance was obtained from the Institutional Ethics Committee for the study. Convenience sampling was employed to select the participants. College students between 18 and 25 years of age were included in the study. Data were collected using the Internet Addiction Test (IAT), Toronto Alexithymia Scale (TAS-20), General Health Questionnaire (GHQ-12) and Mindfulness Attention Awareness Scale (MAAS). SPSS, Version 22, and Hayes’ PROCESS macro (Model 4) were used to carry out descriptive statistics, correlation and mediation analyses.ResultsAlexithymia was positively associated with internet addiction (r = 0.35, p < .05), and negatively associated with mindfulness (r = −0.63, p < .01). Psychological distress was positively associated with internet addiction (r = 0.64, p < .05). The indirect effects of alexithymia on internet addiction were significant via psychological distress (B = 0.130, 95% CI [0.059, 0.204]) and mindfulness (B = 0.108, 95% CI [0.027, 0.186]). The mediation model accounted for 50% of the variance in internet addiction. The direct effect remained significant, indicating partial mediation.ConclusionThe findings supported parallel dual-regulatory pathways in which alexithymia was linked to internet addiction, with psychological distress and mindfulness serving as significant statistical pathways. These results indicated the relevance of integrated self-regulation frameworks in understanding behavioural addictions.

Introduction

Background

In the past few years, a steady increase has been observed in cases of internet addiction (IA) among college students, with approximately 60% of college students reporting some level of IA.1, 2 While IA is not recognised as an independent diagnosis in the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM-5), Internet Gaming Disorder has been included as a condition for further study. IA, also known as ‘problematic internet use’ and ‘generalised pathological internet use’, 3 involves certain components, such as excessive use leading to a loss of sense of time, persistent preoccupation with internet use, experiencing withdrawal symptoms if unable to access the internet and facing dysfunction in daily activities, as well as other negative consequences.4, 5 IA has been linked to several psychological issues, such as depression, anxiety, loneliness, low self-esteem, sleep issues and substance use disorder.6, 7

Contemporary models conceptualise IA as emerging from the interaction between person-related vulnerabilities and deficits in emotion regulation and self-control. 8 The Interaction of Person–Affect–Cognition–Execution (I-PACE) model 9 proposes that individuals with certain predispositions (e.g., trait vulnerabilities or psychopathological features) respond to internal or external triggers with specific affective and cognitive reactions. Over time, these reactions (craving, negative affect or maladaptive coping thoughts) interact with weakened inhibitory control and executive functioning, increasing the likelihood of developing and maintaining addictive behaviours.

Out of these, maladaptive emotion-related processes, such as difficulty in understanding or processing emotions, have received increasing attention. Research has shown that maladaptive use of the internet is driven by unmet emotional needs, not exposure to negative states alone. Deficits in emotion awareness or regulation may also lead to individuals relying on maladaptive or avoidant behaviours, such as IA, to manage their negative internal states. 8

Studies have reported that alexithymia (AX) has been proposed as a significant risk factor for IA and other compulsive behaviours10, 11 as a maladaptive strategy to cope with emotions. 12 AX corresponds to deficits in emotional processing and is defined as the inability to identify and describe emotions, difficulty in differentiating between emotions from their bodily sensations, an externally oriented cognitive style and limited fantasising and daydreaming. 13

Multiple studies have concluded that AX is a positive predictor of IA across various groups.14–16 AX leads to poor emotional insight, as it affects how emotions are noticed, interpreted and regulated. This, in turn, leads to increased reliance on maladaptive coping mechanisms and addictive behaviours, including IA, to cope with emotional discomfort and distress.17, 18 However, AX alone may not be directly associated with IA. It may act through intermediary processes by disrupting the integration of affective signals with regulatory control processes. 19 Accordingly, examining the underlying emotional, cognitive and regulatory mediating mechanisms is important to explain its association with IA.20, 21

Psychological distress (PD) is one of the major consequences of AX and may function as an intermediary mechanism linking AX with IA. When emotional signals remain poorly identified and unresolved, they may increase susceptibility to PD, which can manifest in the form of stress, anxiety, depression and sleep problems.22, 23 Moreover, according to the Compensatory Internet Usage Theory, 8 individuals use the internet as a means of escape from negative emotions or PD, including anxiety, depression or loneliness. The temporary relief that is felt after using the internet to regulate mood reinforces usage, which, in turn, creates a vicious cycle of internet overuse. Taken together, PD may function as a bottom-up affective pathway, whereby emotional distress arising from impaired emotional awareness influences self-regulation and increases vulnerability to maladaptive internet use.

While PD reflects emotional discomfort that arises from difficulties in understanding and managing emotions, an individual’s capacity to be aware of and regulate their internal experiences may independently influence vulnerability to IA. In other words, the extent to which a person can notice and recognise their emotions and thoughts in an adaptive manner may reduce the likelihood of IA. One such cognitive-regulatory pathway, which reflects this type of non-judgemental awareness and self-regulation, may be through mindfulness (M).

M is defined as the ability to maintain purposeful, present-moment attention with an attitude of openness and non-judgement towards one’s ongoing experience. 24 It involves consciously observing an individual’s thoughts, emotions and physiological sensations as they occur, thus fostering greater self-awareness and understanding. 25 Increased awareness enables individuals to recognise their emotional experiences more clearly, which, in turn, allows them to respond adaptively in a more deliberate and regulated way rather than reacting automatically.

Previous empirical studies have reported that practising M can help individuals with AX enhance their emotional awareness and regulation.26, 27 M strengthens attentional control and promotes non-judgemental acceptance of internal experiences, thereby improving emotion regulation. 28 M also strengthens self-control capacities and metacognitive awareness by improving attentional control, inhibitory control and self-monitoring, thereby reducing addictive urges. 29 Reduced M may therefore serve as a cognitive-regulatory pathway through which AX increases vulnerability to maladaptive internet use. 30

Due to the transitional nature of college life, academic pressure and social and emotional changes, college students are particularly vulnerable to affective dysregulation, maladaptive coping strategies and maladaptive behaviours. 31 Recent findings indicate that a substantial number of Indian college students experience problematic internet use, with 39.5% reporting some level of IA. 32 Furthermore, AX has also been observed among college populations, with at least 23.6% of students exhibiting elevated levels. 33 However, limited studies have been carried out to examine emotional vulnerabilities and maladaptive coping mechanisms within this population in India.

Objectives of the Current Research Study

After integrating the theoretical account mentioned above and the limited prior findings, the present cross-sectional study proposed a dual-path regulatory system referring to two complementary regulatory mechanisms consisting of an affective pathway via PD and a cognitive-regulatory pathway via M, which act as mediators between AX and IA. Age and gender were included as covariates because previous studies have reported variations in IA across age groups and gender, indicating that these demographic factors may influence internet usage patterns.34, 35 The following hypotheses were tested: H1: AX will be positively associated with IA.H2: AX will be positively associated with PD and negatively related to M.H3: PD will be positively associated with IA, while M will be negatively associated with IA.H4: PD and M will each independently mediate the relationship between AX and IA, representing affective and cognitive-regulatory pathways, respectively.

Methods

Study Design and Setting

The present study adopted a cross-sectional, quantitative research design to examine whether affective dysregulation (PD) and cognitive-regulatory capacity (M) account for the association between AX and IA. The data were analysed using correlation and mediation analyses. Ethical clearance was obtained from the Institutional Ethics Committee prior to conducting the research. Participants were recruited from universities in the Gandhinagar district between September 2025 and December 2025. Data were collected in classroom settings within the university campuses.

Participants

The final sample included N = 316 college students (Figure 2), of whom 49.68% were women (n = 157) and 51.31% were men (n = 159). Convenience sampling was used to select the participants for the research.

Flowchart of Participant Recruitment, Exclusion, and Data Analysis.

Inclusion criteria required participants to be college students aged between 18 and 25 years who were currently pursuing undergraduate or postgraduate programmes at the selected universities. Participants who self-reported that they had been diagnosed with any significant psychiatric illness or had a history of substance use were excluded from the study (n = 10). Participants engaged in paid online work (n = 18) while pursuing their degree were also excluded from the study, as such usage may not objectively reflect problematic internet usage.

Data were collected from the participants using paper-based questionnaires in classroom settings to minimise environmental distractions. Participation in the research was voluntary and anonymous. The participants were briefed about the research study in detail before providing written informed consent. The participants took around 20–25 min to complete the form.

Variables

AX was treated as the primary independent variable and measured using the Toronto Alexithymia Scale (TAS-20). IA was the outcome variable and was assessed using the Internet Addiction Test (IAT). PD and M were examined as parallel mediators and measured using the General Health Questionnaire (GHQ-12) and the Mindfulness Attention Awareness Scale (MAAS), respectively. Age and gender were covariates, as they have been reported to have an association with IA.

Measures

Internet Addiction Test

Created by Young, 4 the IAT is a self-report measure designed to measure the presence and severity of IA. With a total of 20 items, the rating ranges from 1 for ‘Rarely’ to 5 for ‘Always’ on a five-point Likert scale. Both total scores (ranging from 20 to 100) and severity categories were calculated. Higher scores indicated greater levels of addiction. Intensity scores were classified as follows: mild (31–49), moderate (50–79) and severe (80–100) levels of IA. The scale has shown excellent psychometric properties in the Indian context, including high internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.905), 36 good construct validity and convergent validity. 37

Toronto Alexithymia Scale

The TAS-20, created by Bagby et al., 38 is a 20-item questionnaire used to measure deficits in emotional awareness and cognitive representation of affect. Scoring is done on a five-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 for ‘Strongly disagree’ to 5 for ‘Strongly agree’. The scores range from 20 to 100, with higher scores indicating greater impairment due to AX. The scale comprises three subscales, which measure difficulty in identifying feelings, difficulty in describing feelings and externally oriented thinking. The scale demonstrates good psychometric properties, with good convergent and divergent validity. 39

General Health Questionnaire

The GHQ-12 40 is used to measure PD levels, reflecting increased negative affect and emotional dysregulation. It focuses on symptoms of anxiety, depression and social dysfunction. The GHQ-12 is a four-point Likert scale with 12 items. The GHQ-12 can be scored using either the bimodal method (0-0-1-1) or the Likert method (0-1-2-3). For the current research, scoring was carried out using the Likert method, as it yields greater variability. The score range is 12–48, with higher scores indicating greater PD. The scale demonstrates high internal consistency, with values of Cronbach’s α = 0.78–0.9341, 42 and has strong convergent validity. 42

Mindfulness Attention Awareness Scale

The MAAS, created by Brown and Ryan, 43 measures dispositional M, which includes present moment awareness and attentional monitoring functions reflecting executive control and self-regulation. It includes 15 items rated on a six-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (almost always) to 6 (almost never). Scoring includes calculating the average of all the items and ranges from 1 to 6, with higher scores suggesting higher levels of M with attention to present experiences. The MAAS demonstrates good internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.82), as well as satisfactory convergent and discriminant validity. 44

Bias

Possible self-report bias was minimised by ensuring the anonymity and confidentiality of the data being collected from the participants. Data collection was conducted in a supervised environment to maintain standardisation conditions of the setting.

Study Size

Sample size was calculated using G*Power software. 45 With an assumed effect size of f2 = 0.05 (a small effect size according to Cohen), α = 0.05, power = 0.80 and 5 predictors, the required sample size was N = 316. To account for errors in filling out the questionnaire, data were collected from 350 students. Of these, n = 6 did not complete the questionnaire and n = 28 did not meet the inclusion criteria and were excluded from the study.

Quantitative Variables

All variables, including AX, IA, PD and M, were scored and analysed in a continuous manner for correlation and mediation analyses. IAT scores (mild, moderate, severe) were used only for descriptive reporting. Age and gender were included as covariates.

Statistical Methods

To carry out the statistical analysis, SPSS, Version 22, software was used. After preliminary descriptive analyses, Pearson correlation was employed to examine the associations among IA, AX, PD and M. Finally, mediation analysis was carried out using Hayes’ PROCESS macro software, 46 employing 5,000 bootstrapped resamples with 95% confidence intervals to test parallel mediation effects while controlling for age and gender. Given the cross-sectional design, mediation reflects statistical indirect effects rather than causation. Statistical significance was set at p < .05. However, results significant at p < .01 and p < .001 were also reported. Incomplete questionnaires (n = 6) were excluded prior to analysis. All remaining cases had complete data. No cluster adjustments were applied because convenience sampling was used to collect the data.

Similarly, the mediation analysis (Table 3) reported that AX had a significant direct effect on IA (B = 0.141, p = .016, 95% CI [0.027, 0.256]), even after controlling for PD, M, age and gender. The total indirect effect of AX on IA through PD and M was significant (B = 0.238, 95% CI [0.126, 0.350]). Specifically, the indirect effect of AX on IA via PD was statistically significant (B = 0.130, 95% CI [0.059, 0.204]). M also emerged as a significant mediator (B = 0.108, 95% CI [0.027, 0.186]). Moreover, the indirect effect through PD was modestly larger than that through M, suggesting that affective dysregulation may represent a comparatively stronger statistical pathway. The results (as depicted in Figure 3) supported a partial mediation model. This suggests that, although both mediators significantly explained part of the association between AX and IA, a significant direct association remained after accounting for these indirect effects.

Discussion

Key Findings

The present study examined the associations among AX, IA, PD and M in college students and further tested whether PD and M mediated the relationship between AX and IA. Correlation analysis supported H1, H2 and H3, as all hypothesised direct associations were significant among the variables. Mediation analysis further supported the proposed parallel dual-regulatory system in which AX was associated with IA through both increased affective load (PD) and reduced cognitive-regulatory capacity (M). However, because the direct effect of AX on IA remained significant after accounting for both mediators, the findings supported a partial mediation model.

Consistent with H1, AX demonstrated a moderate positive association with IA. This finding aligns with previous research suggesting that elevated levels of AX are associated with internet overuse.16, 47 Individuals with higher levels of AX often face difficulty in identifying and expressing their emotional experiences. Consequently, the online world may serve as a means of escape or avoidance. 48 Excessive internet use may also act as an alternative for compensatory social interaction, temporarily reducing discomfort. 49 Over time, this reliance may reinforce repeated use, contributing to IA.

In support of H2, AX was positively associated with PD, while a significant negative association was observed between AX and M. Persistent difficulty in identifying and regulating emotions may lead to confusion, alienation and loneliness, eventually contributing to PD. 50 In fact, Preece et al. 51 reported that emotional deficits are associated with affect-related disorders such as depression and anxiety, both manifestations of PD.

The strong negative association between AX and M suggests that deficits in emotional awareness may occur with reduced attentional monitoring capacity, pointing towards broader self-regulatory impairment rather than affective deficits only. M involves present-moment awareness, emotional clarity and non-judgemental acceptance. These abilities are fundamentally impaired in individuals with high AX. 27 Individuals with higher levels of M are better at identifying, understanding and expressing emotional experiences with openness and compassion, suggesting that M may act as a protective factor against the emotional deficits associated with AX. 52

The findings also supported H3, indicating that PD and IA were positively associated, whereas M was negatively associated with IA. This finding also aligns with the Compensatory Internet Use Theory, which states that individuals use the internet as a means of escape from negative emotions or PD, such as anxiety, depression or loneliness. The temporary relief that is felt after using the internet to regulate mood reinforces usage, which, in turn, creates a vicious cycle of internet overuse. 8 Therefore, PD may act as an emotion amplifier, leading to greater internet use to gain immediate relief.

In contrast, M and IA demonstrated a moderate negative association. M has been associated with lower engagement in addictive behaviours, including mobile phone addiction and IA.53, 54 By improving behavioural regulation and self-regulation capacity, M may interrupt automatic or impulsive online behaviours, thus acting as a protective factor against IA. 55

The mediation analysis provided support for H4 by demonstrating that PD and M each independently mediated the association between AX and IA. Specifically, AX was associated with higher levels of PD, which, in turn, predicted greater IA. Similarly, AX was associated with lower levels of M, which was associated with greater IA. These findings suggest that PD and M help explain the association between AX and IA by representing mechanisms through which AX is associated with IA. Similarly, Mei et al. 20 reported that PD partially mediated the association between AX and mobile phone addiction, such that AX exerted both direct and indirect effects through elevated PD. AX demonstrated both direct and indirect associations with phone addiction, as well as an indirect impact via PD. Similarly, Hao and Jin 21 found that higher AX predicted lower ‘acting with awareness’ facet of M, which, in turn, predicted greater mobile phone addiction. Overall, the results suggest that increased distress and reduced M are important statistical pathways linking AX to behavioural addictions.

Both PD and M significantly mediated the association, with the indirect effect through PD being numerically larger. Lastly, the direct effect of AX on IA remained significant after including both mediators. This suggests that, while PD and M explain part of the relationship, other factors may also play a role. Age and gender showed significant associations in the mediation model, suggesting that demographic factors may also contribute to variability in IA. Future research may further examine the role of demographic factors in these relationships.

Limitations and Future Directions

However, certain limitations related to sampling technique and research design must be acknowledged. First, the generalisability of the results may be limited due to the sampling technique. Moreover, the cross-sectional design limits causal interpretations. Longitudinal or intervention-based designs incorporating behavioural or neurocognitive measures of executive function would allow more direct testing of regulatory pathways. Exploring specific subcomponents of AX may provide a better understanding of vulnerability to IA. Therefore, future studies using larger, more representative samples and longitudinal designs are recommended to enhance the robustness of the results.

The present findings support the emotion-regulation model of IA by demonstrating that AX contributes to IA through parallel affective and cognitive-regulatory pathways. Specifically, the results suggest that both heightened PD and reduced M influence the association between AX and IA. By integrating both pathways within a single mediation model, the study moves beyond simple associations and provides deeper insight by clarifying the mechanisms underlying the association between AX and IA.

From a clinical perspective, screening for AX traits among college students may help identify individuals at increased risk of IA. Moreover, individuals with higher levels of AX may benefit from therapies that enhance emotion identification and regulation skills. Additionally, M-based interventions may strengthen self-regulatory capacity and reduce compulsive online engagement.

Conclusion

Despite the limitations, this study provides evidence that AX influences IA through distinct affective and cognitive-regulatory mechanisms. These findings also highlight the significance of integrating emotional awareness and self-regulation in understanding IA and other behavioural addictions. Interventions aimed at enhancing emotional insight and strengthening self-regulation may help reduce the risk of IA among individuals with high AX.
