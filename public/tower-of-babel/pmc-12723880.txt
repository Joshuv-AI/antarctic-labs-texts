Impact of digital fabrication techniques on the color stability of complete dentures base and artificial teeth
Authors: Ali Sameer Alsharif, Ramtin Sadid-Zadeh, Rui Li, Wendy Auclair Clark, Ahmed Yaseen Alqutaibi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study evaluated the color stability of digitally fabricated complete dentures compared to conventional ones.Materials and methodsForty-five specimens were fabricated using 3 different manufacturing methods (injection mold, one-piece milling, and two-piece 3D printing and milling techniques), with 15 specimens per group. Background

Over the past decade, the prevalence of edentulism has decreased; however, the total number of edentulous patients has increased, primarily due to longer life expectancy facilitated by advancements in healthcare [1, 2]. Although implant-supported fixed prostheses present a viable treatment option, numerous patients continue to rely on traditional complete dentures. This reliance is frequently attributed to financial limitations, anatomical challenges such as bone deficiencies, and medical contraindications for surgical interventions [3]. Therefore, optimizing complete dentures is essential for restoring oral function and enhancing the quality of life, particularly concerning aesthetics, phonetics, and functionality [3].

The fabrication method for complete dentures significantly impacts their mechanical and physical properties. For instance, conventional polymerization techniques employed in producing polymethyl methacrylate (PMMA) dentures yield varying performance characteristics. Research indicates that Computer-Aided Design and Computer-Aided Manufacturing (CAD-CAM) PMMA materials outperform traditional heat-polymerized PMMA regarding flexural strength and surface quality [4]. Conversely, heat-polymerized PMMA exhibits lower flexural strength, higher surface roughness, and diminished hardness [5].

The emergence of digital technology, particularly CAD-CAM, has transformed the fabrication of dentures by addressing the shortcomings of traditional methods. CAD-CAM processes alleviate issues such as polymerization shrinkage and material fractures through the utilization of advanced urethane dimethacrylate (UDMA)-based materials, which offer enhanced durability [6]. Techniques such as milling and 3D printing have further enabled the production of dentures with improved mechanical properties and precision [7, 8].

Among the innovations in CAD-CAM technology, computer numerical control (CNC) milling has proven particularly impactful, providing high-quality denture bases with reduced porosity and shrinkage. Nevertheless, CNC milling presents certain disadvantages, including substantial material waste and challenges in complex undercuts [9, 10]. To mitigate these limitations, 3D printing has gained traction as an alternative fabrication method. Techniques such as stereolithography (SLA) and digital light processing (DLP) offer high precision, scalability, and reduced material waste, rendering them well-suited for dental applications [8, 11].

The longevity of complete dentures, both aesthetically and functionally, is influenced by factors such as color stability. Material porosity, exposure to acidic beverages, food pigments, and inadequate maintenance contribute to discoloration and microbial accumulation Although CAD-CAM fabrication methods for complete removable dentures were introduced in the 1990 s, their application remains relatively novel in clinical practice. Importantly, there is still a lack of comprehensive evidence addressing their color stability [9, 12], especially when compared systematically across different fabrication techniques under simulated clinical conditions. This gap in the literature formed the basis for the present study, which was specifically designed to evaluate and compare the discoloration behavior of complete dentures fabricated using conventional injection molding, one-piece milling, and two-piece digital printing and milling.

Given the significance of accurately mimicking the appearance of oral mucosa, color stability is crucial for patient acceptance of dentures. Over time, heat-polymerized PMMA is susceptible to discoloration due to exposure to cleansers, beverages, foods, and plaque accumulation [13–16]. This study aimed to evaluate the color stability of 3D-printed and milled complete dentures compared to traditionally fabricated dentures following artificial aging. The null hypothesis posits no significant difference in color stability among the three fabrication methods.

Materials and methods

Sample size determination was conducted utilizing a power analysis for a two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) within a factorial design comprising 3 (manufacturing methods) × 3 (staining solutions), with color change serving as the primary outcome variable. Drawing on pilot data from a comparable study assessing the stainability of denture base resins (observed interaction effect: F = 6.469, df = 4,36; partial η²≈0.418; MS_error = 0.091), an alpha level of 0.05 and a desired power of 0.80 were established. Calculations employing the noncentral F-distribution indicated a minimum requirement of 3 specimens per subgroup to adequately detect the interaction effect. To enhance statistical robustness, accommodate potential variability , and ensure alignment with the design of the referenced study, a total of 5 specimens per subgroup were selected, resulting in 15 specimens per manufacturing method and an overall sample size of 45.

Sample preparation

A typodont model was used to design a denture base with two central incisors. The model was scanned using a desktop scanner after removing the incisors and sealing their sockets with silicone. The design software was then used to create a denture base with central incisors. An M5 file was generated for the one-piece denture, and STL files were created for the two-piece version (Fig. 1).

Designed denture base and two central incisors on the typodont (A); On-piece designed denture (B); Two-piece designed denture (C)

Injection mold technique

For the conventional group, the STL file of the denture base (Fig. 1C) was used to mill a wax base from billets. The inner surface of the wax base was modified to fit two central incisors, and alignment marks were added using wax. The modified base was then rescanned, and the alignment marks were refined. Based on this file, 15 wax denture bases were milled (Wax Press, Harvest) using a 5-axis milling machine. Artificial teeth were passively fitted onto each wax base, and a silicone jig was used to secure them with additional wax. Festooning wax was then applied at the tooth-base junctions (Fig. 2).Denture patterns were flasked, dewaxed, and processed with PMMA (Ivocap High Impact, Ivoclar) using the Ivobase Injector. Samples were then separated and stored for finishing and polishing.

Injection mold technique (A) Milled denture base wax pattern. B Denture base wax pattern. C Silicon base. D Wax elimination. E Festooned wax denture.F Teeth positioning jig. G Processed acrylic denture

Milling technique

For the subtractive (milled) group, the one-piece M5 file (Fig. 1B) was utilized to fabricate samples through a subtractive manufacturing technique on a 5-axis milling machine. A total of fifteen PMMA billets were milled to produce fifteen uniform specimens. Each denture was positioned within individual discs, ensuring standardized milling axes across all specimens (Fig.3). Subsequently, the dentures were extracted from the disc using an E-Cutter, and the supports were removed before finishing and polishing.

Milling technique (A) Ivotion A1/Dark US. B milling machine. C Milled Monolithic denture and acrylic

3D printing-milling technique

For the printing-milling group, using two-piece STL files, 15 specimens were fabricated using additive and subtractive manufacturing techniques. The denture base was 3D-printed with a polymer material using an M2 Carbon printer (Dentsply Sirona). Meanwhile, artificial teeth were milled from a multilayered PMMA billet using a 5-axis milling machine (Fig 4). The Lucitone Digital Fuse protocol was implemented to attach the artificial teeth to the denture base securely.

Finishing and polishing procedures

Samples were polished using a modified conventional denture polishing protocol [15, 17] performed by a single laboratory technician. The procedure commenced with smoothing the denture base and junctions utilizing two cutters at a rotational speed of 12,000 RPM with an electric handpiece. Subsequently, polishing was performed using medium coarse pumice applied to a rag wheel for 30 s, followed by fine polishing with flour pumice for 20 s, employing a polishing unit at 3,000 RPM. Buffing was finalized with a buffing agent on a rag wheel at 1,500 RPM for 10 s. Ultimately, the samples were steam cleaned, labeled, and coded according to group and specimen number, designating M for subtractive, P for additive-subtractive, and C for the injection mold.

Staining and cleaning protocol

Staining protocol

Samples from each group were immersed in three different media: distilled water, red wine (Vina Zancara), and coffee (Tim Hortons Original Blend) (Fig. 5 A &B), for seven days at 37 °C in an incubator. The media were refreshed daily to simulate clinical exposure equivalent to 34–67 months [18]. Following the staining procedure, the specimens were rinsed and dried.

Staining and Cleaning media: A Coffee. B Red wine. C Denture cleaner

Cleaning protocol

Post-staining samples were subjected to a cleaning process utilizing denture cleaner (Polident) for a duration of 21 min, which is equivalent to one week of standard cleaning practices (Fig. 5 C). Following this, subsequent tests were conducted to evaluate whether the original appearance of the samples was restored. If successful, the staining and shade testing processes were repeated as a second procedure.

Shade assessment

Shade was assessed using a photograph, a spectrophotometer, and visual inspection. Before baseline color measurements, all specimens were stored in a humid incubator at 37 °C for 24 h.

Shade assessment using photography

Photographs were captured under daylight within a viewing booth utilizing a digital camera, Canon EOS 6D Mark II, set to ISO 200, aperture F2.8, and a shutter speed of 1/100 seconds. Consistent positioning of the subjects facilitated reliable Lab* measurements, which were analyzed using ImageJ software. Color differences (ΔE00) were computed employing the CIEDE2000 formula [19–21].

Shade assessment using a spectrophotometer

A VITA Easyshade V spectrophotometer was employed to assess shade consistency, utilizing a clear 3D-printed jig to standardize the positioning of the instrument. A putty jig facilitated the placement of the probe at the middle third interproximal area of each denture tooth (Fig. 6).

Tooth Shade assessment using a spectrophotometer (A) 3D printed resin jig. B Putty Positioning Jig with resin jig

Visual assessment

A visual inspection was performed at 3X magnification to identify any discoloration at the interface between the artificial teeth and the denture base. Samples that appeared questionable were subsequently examined at 20X magnification to ensure accuracy in the assessment.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using Prism Version 10 (GraphPad Software). Normality was assessed with the Anderson-Darling test, and color differences were analyzed with a two-way ANOVA, followed by Tukey multiple comparison post-hoc tests, with significance set at 0.05.

Results

The color difference values (ΔE) for denture teeth, interproximal areas, and denture base resins were evaluated after staining, cleaning, and subsequent staining. Figure 7 illustrates these ΔE values, and detailed results of the two-way ANOVA are provided in supplementary Tables 2–10. Shade match rates are outlined in supplementary Tables 11–19. All samples passed normalization via the D’Agostino & Pearson test (P > 0.05).

After the first staining, coffee immersion caused the most significant discoloration, with ΔE values higher than all other groups (P < 0.05) and a 0% Vita shade match rate (Supplementary Tables 3, 12). Milled specimens showed less color change compared to conventional groups, but no significant difference was observed between milled and mill-print groups (P = 0.038 and 0.272). Red wine caused less discoloration than coffee, particularly affecting the mill-print group, with mean ΔE values of 0.0040 and 0.0123. The Vita shade matching system showed comparable results across all groups stained with red wine. In interproximal areas, coffee-stained conventional and mill-print groups had a 0% shade match rate, while the milled group achieved 40%. The milled group attained a 100% match rate with red wine, compared to 40% for the mill-print group (Supplementary Table 12). The milled group exhibited significantly lower ΔE values with coffee than the conventional group (P < 0.05). At the denture teeth-base junction, junctional discoloration occurred in 60% of the conventional group exposed to red wine, while other groups showed no such changes (Supplementary Table 13, Fig. 7). Both coffee and red wine caused increased ΔE values in denture bases, with the conventional group showing the highest discoloration under coffee exposure (P < 0.05).

After cleaning, denture teeth and interproximal areas were restored to their original shade (A1) with ΔE values below 1, indicating minimal residual discoloration (Supplementary Tables 5, 15). No discoloration was observed at the denture teeth-base junction post-cleaning, confirming the effectiveness of the cleaning agent in eliminating junctional staining (Supplementary Table 16). Denture bases had ΔE values below 0.5 for all groups, indicating complete removal of stains (Supplementary Tables 5–7).

After the second staining, coffee resulted in high ΔE values and a 0% shade match rate, consistent with the first staining (Supplementary Table 17). Milled specimens showed significantly less discoloration than the mill-print group when exposed to red wine (P < 0.0455). In interproximal areas, coffee led to a 0% match rate across all groups. With red wine, only the milled group achieved a 100% match rate; the mill-print group had 0%, and the conventional group reached 60% (Supplementary Table 18). At the tooth-base junction, discoloration occurred in 60% of conventional and mill-print specimens with red wine, and 20% of conventional with coffee, while the milled group remained unaffected (Supplementary Table 19). Both coffee and red wine increased ΔE values for all denture bases. The mill-print group showed the highest ΔE with red wine (P < 0.05), and the conventional group had the greatest ΔE rise with coffee. Only the milled group maintained ΔE values below the acceptability threshold (ΔE = 3.7) (Supplementary Table 10).

Discussion

The objective of this study was to evaluate and compare the color stability of complete dentures fabricated using conventional injection molding, one-piece CAD-CAM milling, and two-piece 3D printing–milling techniques under simulated clinical conditions. The results demonstrated that coffee caused the greatest discoloration across all groups, while red wine produced less pronounced changes. Milled dentures consistently showed superior color stability compared to conventional and mill-print groups, with fewer interfacial discolorations and ΔE values remaining below clinical thresholds in several conditions. Cleaning with denture cleanser effectively restored the original shade in all groups, confirming the reversibility of most staining effects. The originality of this study lies in being the first to comprehensively assess and compare these three fabrication methods under standardized staining and cleaning protocols, thereby addressing a significant gap in the literature. By demonstrating distinct patterns of discoloration and recovery, our findings contribute valuable insights to the evidence base, supporting clinicians in selecting fabrication techniques that optimize the esthetic durability and patient acceptance of complete dentures.

Instrumental methods, such as spectrophotometers, provide a more objective and precise assessment of color changes compared to visual evaluation, reducing bias by approximately 33% [22–24]. While traditional instruments like spectrophotometers and chromameters are reliable, they are limited in handling uneven or small samples and lack image documentation capabilities [25]. In this study, ImageJ software was employed for its efficiency and reproducibility, quantifying color changes within the CIELab color space, which facilitates the detection of clinically perceptible variations [26, 27].

The findings align with Al-Qarni et al. (2020), who reported color changes in materials immersed in staining solutions influenced by the materials’ water absorption properties. Lighter-colored materials (e.g., shade A1) were chosen for their higher susceptibility to noticeable discoloration. Consistent with prior research, coffee caused more intense staining than red wine, with a complete loss of Vita shade matching. Discrepancies in red wine’s impact may stem from variations in preparation and components of coffee solutions across studies. For instance, 3D printings like sugar or creamer can modulate coffee staining by altering surface adsorption mechanisms.

Our findings align with the research conducted by Al-Qarni et al. (2020), which demonstrated that the degree of color change in denture base materials immersed in staining solutions is closely associated with their water absorption characteristics [28]. Consistent with prior studies, lighter shades such as A1 were intentionally selected due to their increased susceptibility to perceptible discoloration, thus facilitating a sensitive evaluation of color stability [14, 29, 30]. In agreement with earlier investigations, coffee exhibited the most significant staining, resulting in a complete loss of Vita shade matching, whereas red wine induced comparatively less discoloration [18, 28, 31]. The variations noted in the effects of red wine across different studies may be attributable to differences in beverage composition and preparation, as well as the presence of additives such as sugar or creamer in coffee, which can affect surface adsorption and staining potential [23, 29]. By systematically comparing these effects across three fabrication methods under standardized conditions, the present study contributes to the existing body of knowledge and offers clinically relevant evidence concerning the aesthetic durability of CAD-CAM and conventional denture bases.

The study highlights the optical stability of CAD/CAM PMMA materials. Milled specimens exhibited reduced discoloration compared to conventional dentures, corroborating findings by Alp et al. (2019), who attributed this to CAD/CAM PMMA’s low water absorption and hydrophobicity [32]. Red wine immersion revealed a 100% interproximal Vita shade matching rate for milled dentures, contrasting with mill-printed specimens, which achieved only 40% due to surface irregularities from the bonding process. Improved polishing protocols in this study likely mitigated staining effects, aligning with Al-Qarni et al. (2020), who associated discoloration with voids at the interface [28].

Coffee emerged as the most potent staining agent, producing the highest ΔE00 values, particularly in conventional dentures, due to the penetration of hydrophobic colorants into PMMA porosities [12]. Red wine significantly affected 3D-printed bases, likely due to water sorption and the reduced filler content of these materials [33–35]. While anthocyanidins in red wine contribute to staining, its lower alcohol concentration (12%) had minimal impact, keeping color changes below clinically significant thresholds (ΔE >2.8) [14, 36–38].

Denture hygiene proved effective in restoring samples to their original shade, echoing findings by Hollis et al. (2015) that denture cleansers enhance color stability through oxidizing agents [14]. After two staining cycles, milled Ivoclar teeth showed superior resistance to coffee stains, maintaining clinically acceptable color stability. All denture base samples remained within acceptable discoloration limits.

The limitations of this study include several important considerations. First, while the use of a typodont model and controlled laboratory conditions allowed for consistency and reproducibility, they do not fully replicate the complex intraoral environment, where factors such as saliva composition, dietary habits, and mechanical wear may differentially influence staining and cleaning outcomes. Therefore, clinical trials are necessary to validate these findings under real-life usage conditions. Second, the modest sample size per subgroup (n = 5), although consistent with prior in vitro investigations, may restrict the generalizability of the statistical findings, and larger sample sizes would provide greater robustness. Third, the relatively brief durations of staining and cleaning, despite being calibrated to approximate clinical exposure, may not capture the long-term or cumulative effects associated with daily use. Additionally, although finishing and polishing procedures were standardized and performed by a single technician, minor variations may still have occurred. Finally, the use of only one brand of cleaning agent and staining media constrains the scope of the study, as other commercially available products may yield different outcomes. Despite these limitations, the study provides valuable insights into material selection and maintenance protocols, offering clinicians guidance in optimizing the aesthetics and durability of denture materials.

Conclusions

Within the limitations of this study, it can be concluded that all denture teeth exhibited elevated ΔE₀₀ values after exposure to staining agents, with coffee resulting in more pronounced discoloration in comparison to red wine. Milled specimens consistently demonstrated smaller color changes than their conventionally fabricated counterparts, particularly upon exposure to red wine, indicating a higher overall resistance to staining. However, the mill-print group was found to be more susceptible to staining from red wine compared to fully milled specimens. Interproximal Vita shade matching revealed significant differences, with milled groups again exhibiting superior resistance to stains relative to conventional counterparts. The implemented cleaning protocol effectively restored all denture teeth to their original shades, with no discoloration observed at the junction between the denture teeth and base. Upon re-staining, similar discoloration patterns emerged, reinforcing the greater staining potential of coffee and the enhanced stain resistance of milled specimens. Visual examinations further confirmed more pronounced discoloration at the denture tooth-base interface in the conventional group. Finally, denture bases also displayed increased ΔE₀₀ values following staining, with milled bases demonstrating superior resistance to staining overall. These findings highlight the importance of material selection and cleaning protocols for maintaining denture aesthetics and longevity.
