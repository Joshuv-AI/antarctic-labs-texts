Pediatric laparoscopic splenectomy using a repurposed sterile diathermy pouch as a retrieval bag: preliminary experience

Introduction

Laparoscopic splenectomy (LS) is widely accepted as the preferred surgical approach for pediatric patients requiring elective splenectomy for benign hematological disorders [1, 2]. Nevertheless, specimen retrieval remains a significant technical challenge in children, particularly in cases with massive splenomegaly [3]. Although commercially available retrieval bags facilitate controlled in-bag fragmentation and extraction through a small incision, their cost and limited availability in resource-constrained settings restrict their routine use [4]. While numerous homemade retrieval systems have been described, most have been designed for small specimens such as in appendectomy or cholecystectomy, and primarily in adult populations, thereby limiting their applicability to pediatric splenic surgery [5].

In this study, we report our preliminary experience using a repurposed sterile diathermy pouch as a retrieval bag during pediatric LS.

Methods and patients

This cohort study included pediatric patients (aged ≤ 18 years) who underwent elective LS for benign hematologic indications at Alexandria University Children’s Hospital between November 2023 and January 2025. Patients undergoing emergency surgery, those with traumatic splenic injury, or cases associated with significant portal hypertension (hepatic venous pressure gradient > 10 mmHg) were excluded. Patients with suspected or confirmed malignant splenic disease were also excluded as piecemeal fragmentation during specimen retrieval carries a potential risk of intraperitoneal tumor dissemination.

Preoperative preparation included complete laboratory evaluation, vaccination against encapsulated organisms administered at least two weeks before the procedure, and ultrasound (US) assessment to determine spleen size and identify any accessory splenic tissue. The study protocol was approved by the ethical committee of Alexandria Faculty of Medicine under number 0307403. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants’ legal guardians. All procedures were performed by the same surgeon.

Preparation of the retrieval pouch

The specimen retrieval pouch was fashioned from a sterile, double‑chambered diathermy pouch (40 × 35 cm), supplied as part of the disposable sterile towel set. A 15 × 30 cm segment was obtained from the larger compartment, as illustrated in Fig. 1. This configuration provided sufficient capacity for tension‑free enclosure of spleens of varying sizes, without the need for precise volumetric matching. The prepared pouch was then tightly rolled into a rod‑like configuration to facilitate insertion through the umbilical port. Prior to use, the pouch was carefully inspected and manually tested to confirm integrity and absence of defects, particularly along the factory‑sealed edges.

Operative technique

After induction of general anesthesia, patients were positioned in a modified right lateral decubitus position at approximately 45 degrees with proper padding of all pressure points and secure strapping to stabilize the patient during the procedure.

The port placement configuration included a 10 mm umbilical port using an open Hasson technique for the 30-degree laparoscope. Gradual insufflation with pressures were set at 10–12 mmHg for younger children and up to 15 mmHg for adolescents. Under direct laparoscopic guidance, two 5 mm working ports were inserted: one in the midline approximately halfway between the xiphoid process and umbilicus, and another in the left midclavicular line about 2 cm below the umbilical level. A fourth port for retraction may be added in the epigastric region or right upper quadrant to assist with exposure, depending on operative needs.

The procedure began with a laparoscopic survey to delineate the splenic anatomy and detect any accessory splenic tissue. Dissection commenced with division of the splenocolic ligament, performed in a limited manner, allowing the colon to fall away to improve exposure of the lower pole and splenic hilum while preserving residual splenic attachments to maintain splenic stability during hilar dissection. This was followed by division of the gastrosplenic ligament with transection of the short gastric vessels. Hilar dissection was then undertaken, with careful skeletonization of the splenic artery and vein, which were individually secured using Hem‑o‑lok clips. Complete splenic mobilization was achieved by dividing the splenorenal and splenophrenic ligaments, freeing both superior and inferior pole attachments.

Following complete splenic mobilization, the retrieval pouch was introduced through the 10‑mm umbilical port, unrolled within the abdominal cavity, and positioned adjacent to the spleen. Using atraumatic graspers, the spleen was gently guided into the pouch with careful preservation of capsular integrity. Adequate tension‑free enclosure of the spleen was confirmed prior to pouch closure.

A 0‑Prolene suture mounted on a needle was introduced percutaneously approximately 5 cm to the left of the umbilicus, leaving the free end of the suture extracorporeal. The needle was then used to close the pouch opening in a continuous running fashion from left to right. Upon completion of pouch closure, the laparoscopic camera was transferred to the lower working port, and the needle was removed together with the umbilical trocar. The umbilical incision was then slightly extended, allowing digital retrieval of the free end of the suture. Subsequently, both suture ends were brought out through the umbilical incision, enabling secure control and exteriorization of the pouch (Fig. 2).

A After sealing the open side of the bag with a running Prolene suture, both suture ends were brought out through the umbilical incision after removal of the umbilical port. B The mouth of the retrieval pouch is delivered externally through a slight extension of the umbilical incision. C Complete retrieval of the fragmented spleen

The mouth of the retrieval pouch was then exteriorized through the slightly enlarged umbilical incision. The spleen was fragmented within the pouch using blunt ring forceps or digital manipulation, avoiding the use of sharp instruments within the pouch. Continuous control of the pouch neck was maintained throughout the fragmentation process to avoid any spillage (Fig. 2).

Following specimen retrieval, the umbilical port was reinserted, and the abdominal cavity was thoroughly irrigated and inspected for residual splenic tissue and hemostasis. All trocars were removed under direct vision. The umbilical fascial defect was closed, and skin closure was completed using absorbable sutures or skin adhesive (Fig. 3).

Postoperative care

Postoperatively, all patients were managed in the pediatric surgical ward with continuous monitoring of vital signs, urine output, and signs of bleeding. Oral intake was typically resumed within 4–8 h after surgery, once the patient was fully awake and nausea-free. Hemoglobin levels and platelet counts were monitored within the first 24 h to assess for anemia or post-splenectomy thrombocytosis. Education regarding signs of overwhelming post-splenectomy infection (OPSI) was reinforced with caregivers prior to discharge. Where indicated, long-term prophylactic penicillin was prescribed based on hematologic diagnosis and immune status.

Postoperative follow-up

Patients were followed in the outpatient clinic at one week, one month, and three months postoperatively. Hematology consultations were arranged in parallel to assess disease status and determine the need for ongoing medical surveillance.

Three months after surgery, patients were asked to rate their satisfaction with the cosmetic outcome in comparison to photos of scars from open surgery and laparoscopic surgery with a Pfannenstiel incision using a standardized Likert scale, where 1 indicated complete dissatisfaction and 5 indicated complete satisfaction.

Data collection and statistical analysis

Data collected included patient demographics (age, sex, weight), operative parameters (spleen size, dissection time, retrieval time), and postoperative outcomes (hospital stay, complications, satisfaction scores). Dissection time was defined as the interval from initiation of the procedure to complete mobilization of the spleen. Retrieval time was defined as the interval from introduction of the retrieval pouch through the umbilical port till complete exteriorization of all splenic fragments.

Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median with interquartile range (IQR), as appropriate, while categorical variables are expressed as frequencies and percentages. Data normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Comparisons between groups were performed using independent-samples t-tests or Mann–Whitney U tests, depending on data distribution. Pearson’s correlation coefficient was used to evaluate associations between continuous variables and to assess learning-curve trends. Statistical significance was defined as a two-tailed p value < 0.05. All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS software, version 28 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Results

This study evaluated 25 pediatric patients who underwent laparoscopic splenectomy using a handcrafted retrieval pouch, comprising 14 female (56%) and 11 male (44%). The mean age was 9.2 ± 2.8 years (range: 3–15 years; median: 9 years). The mean body weight was 25.2 ± 9.1 kg (range: 13–42 kg; median: 24 kg). According to standardized pediatric growth charts, all participants were classified as slightly underweight.

Preoperative US revealed a mean splenic length of 15.8 ± 2.8 cm (range: 12–22 cm), with most measurements falling within the IQR of 14–18 cm (Table 1). A statistically significant positive correlation was observed between spleen size and body weight (Pearson’s r = 0.62, p = 0.001).

The underlying hematological diagnoses included hereditary spherocytosis in 10 patients (40%; 7 females, 3 males), thalassemia in 11 patients (44%; 5 females, 6 males), sickle thalassemia in 2 patients (8%; 1 female, 1 male), sickle cell disease in 1 patient (4%; 1 female), and immune thrombocytopenic purpura (ITP) in 1 patient (4%; 1 male). The index case was a 9-year-old female with hereditary spherocytosis and a spleen measuring 14 cm in length.

The mean splenic dissection time was 53.8 ± 8.4 min (range: 40–80 min; Table 1). The mean retrieval time was 36.4 ± 14.9 min (range: 20–120 min), with the maximum duration (120 min) noted in the first case. Analysis demonstrated a statistically significant inverse correlation between retrieval time and case sequence (Pearson’s r = -0.72, p < 0.001), indicating a learning curve. Exclusion of the index case reduced mean retrieval time to 33.1 ± 9.8 min and the mean total operative time to 90.2 ± 21.0 min (range: 60–135 min) (Fig. 4).

Scatter plot showing the learning curve in retrieval time across the 25 consecutive cases with a marked drop after the first case and fluctuations stabilizing over time

Stratification by underlying diagnosis revealed notable differences in surgical parameters (Table 2). Patients with hereditary spherocytosis (n = 10) demonstrated smaller spleens (14.2 ± 1.5 cm), shorter retrieval times (25.5 ± 3.2 min), and reduced total operative duration (75.5 ± 8.3 min). In contrast, thalassemia patients (n = 11) had significantly larger spleens (17.8 ± 2.1 cm; p < 0.001), required nearly double the retrieval time (43.3 ± 10.4 min; p = 0.002), and showed prolonged total operative duration (102.5 ± 18.3 min; p = 0.003). The learning curve effect was particularly notable in thalassemia cases, where later operations showed 35% faster retrieval times than initial cases (r=-0.72, p < 0.001). The single ITP case represented the smallest spleen (12 cm) and shortest operative time (60 min). These differences appear to be related to variations in splenic size; however, due to the limited sample sizes, they should be regarded as exploratory without a definitive conclusion (Fig. 5).

Group A demonstrated shorter mean retrieval times (25.0 ± 2.7 min vs. 47.0 ± 10.6 min, p < 0.001) and reduced total operative duration (80.2 ± 9.1 min vs. 110.8 ± 16.3 min, p < 0.001). A fourth laparoscopic port was required only in four cases: the youngest patient (3 years old with massive hepatomegaly); another patient with a 22 cm spleen; and two additional patients with spleens measuring 19 cm. All other procedures were completed using a standard three-port technique.

Accessory splenic tissue was identified in 8 patients (32%), with a median of one splenule per patient (range: 1–3). All splenules were located adjacent to the splenic hilum and were excised either separately or en bloc with the spleen.

No intraoperative complications occurred, including bag failure, intraperitoneal spillage of splenic tissue, or iatrogenic injury to adjacent structures.

Postoperative recovery was uneventful in all cases. Surgical drains were selectively placed in five patients (20%) and were removed within 48 h in all cases.

The mean hospital stay was 1.7 ± 0.8 days. Of the 25 pediatric patients, 11 (44%) were discharged on postoperative day one, 10 (40%) on day two, and only 4 patients (16%) required a three-day inpatient stay. Prolonged hospitalization was predominantly associated with increased splenic size, particularly among patients diagnosed with thalassemia.

All patients attended scheduled follow-up visits at 1 week, 1 month, and 3 months postoperatively. No wound-related complications were observed. At the 3-month follow-up, no late complications were detected. Twenty-three patients (92%) assigned a satisfaction score of 5/5 for scar appearance, while two adolescent females assigned a score of 4/5 due to minor concerns about umbilical scar visibility despite normal healing.

Discussion

Splenectomy is a relatively common procedure in pediatric patients performed for a variety of conditions, with benign hematological disorders representing the most frequent indication across different geographic regions [6, 7]. Since the first LS reported by Delaitre and Maignien in 1991 [8], and the subsequent pediatric application described by Tulman and Holcomb in 1993 [9], LS has become widely adopted in pediatric surgical centers as the preferred approach for elective splenectomy, owing to its well-established advantages over open surgery, including reduced intraoperative blood loss, faster postoperative recovery, shorter hospital stay, fewer wound-related complications, and improved cosmetic outcomes [10–12].

Despite these benefits, the procedure remains technically demanding, particularly in cases of massive splenomegaly with limited working space and difficult hilar dissection [13]. Also, the associated learning curve and dependence on specialized instruments may hinder its broader implementation, especially in resource-limited settings [14, 15]. Fortunately, along with the surgical innovation and accumulating experiences, those obstacles seem to be successfully resolved at present [12, 16].

Furthermore, retrieval of the spleen remains a critical technical challenge following laparoscopic dissection. It is typically accomplished either by in-bag fragmentation with piecemeal extraction through an enlarged trocar site or by intact removal via an auxiliary incision, most commonly a Pfannenstiel incision for large spleens, or less frequently an extended periumbilical incision for smaller specimens [16, 17]. The latter approach, while technically straightforward, requires a larger incision, increasing the procedural invasiveness and it may be associated with greater postoperative pain, longer hospital stay, and less favorable cosmetic outcomes, potentially offsetting some of the benefits of a minimally invasive procedure [2, 15, 18].

In contrast, the use of laparoscopic retrieval bags enables in-bag fragmentation (to prevent spillage) and piecemeal specimen extraction without the need for additional incisions preserving the minimally invasive nature of the procedure [19]. However, the fixed capacity of commercially available retrieval bags may limit their effectiveness in cases of markedly enlarged spleens [17, 20]. In addition, limited availability and high cost can restrict their use, particularly in resource-constrained healthcare settings, where the expense of disposable laparoscopic devices may represent a significant burden [21, 22].

Consequently, surgeons have explored low-cost and readily available alternatives, and numerous homemade specimen retrieval bags have been described in the laparoscopic literature, including condoms [21], zipper-type plastic bags [22, 23], the Nadiad bag [24], plastic bags obtained from surgical drain packages [25], and surgical gloves [4, 26, 27]. These techniques were primarily introduced to reduce cost and improve availability and were almost exclusively applied for retrieval of small or moderately sized specimens such as gallbladders, appendices, gynecologic, and urologic specimens, rather than large solid organs. In contrast, splenic specimen retrieval represents a substantially greater challenge due to the large organ size, difficult intra-abdominal manipulation, and the requirement for in-bag fragmentation using a retrieval system that can withstand mechanical stress without tearing. To the best of our knowledge, only four publications have specifically evaluated the use of handmade retrieval bags during LS [28–31] (Table 3).

The earliest experiences reported by Su et al. [28] involved a purely adult population (20–73 years) with predominantly portal hypertension–related splenomegaly (mean 729 ± 74 g), a clinical setting characterized by very large spleens and demanding operative conditions. In that series, retrieval was performed using a homemade bag fashioned from an intravenous infusion container. Operative times were correspondingly prolonged (100–252 min; mean 149 ± 31 min). Similarly, Velidedeoglu et al. [29] used a one-liter sterile saline bag for specimen retrieval in adult patients (18–72 years) with ITP, where splenic enlargement was generally mild to moderate (median length ≈ 13 cm), resulting in shorter operative times (45–85 min; mean 61.7 ± 12.9 min). They reported one complication of iatrogenic bowel injury during morcellation, highlighting the importance of careful intra-bag handling.

In contrast, more recent reports by Mostafa et al. [30] and Arafa et al. [31] focused on pediatric and adolescent patients (4–20 years), in whom splenectomy was performed mainly for benign hemolytic disorders. In both studies, specimen retrieval relied on homemade bags constructed from laparoscopic camera covers, requiring multilayer folding and sutured closure. These pediatric series reported acceptable operative durations (means of approximately 73 and 67 min) and short hospital stays (1–4 days).

The present study further extends the pediatric experience by including younger children (3–15 years) and encompassing a wider splenic size range up to 22 cm. The retrieval technique using a sterile diathermy pouch differed from previously reported methods by eliminating the need for bag tailoring or multilayer construction. Operative and retrieval times were comparable to those reported in prior studies with larger splenic size being associated with longer retrieval times. Despite the inclusion of a wide range of splenic sizes, laparoscopic completion was achieved in all cases without conversion or retrieval-related complications. These findings are consistent with published pediatric series demonstrating that LS remains safe and feasible even in the setting of massive splenomegaly, where splenic size represents a technical challenge rather than an absolute contraindication [12, 15, 32].

Compared with published LS series using commercial retrieval bags, operative outcomes achieved with handmade retrieval systems appear broadly comparable. Recent pediatric series employing commercial bags have reported operative times ranging from 65 to 155 min and postoperative hospital stays of 1.5–4 days [33–37], while adult series, which may be affected by larger splenic size and underlying disease complexity, demonstrated generally longer operative durations (120–180 min) and hospital stays (3–5 days) [38–40].

From an economic perspective, the advantages of handmade retrieval bags can be considered in three domains. First, they incur negligible or no additional cost, in contrast to commercial bags, which range from 50 to 300 USD per unit, depending on size and manufacturer. Second, their use does not appear to prolong operative time, which is a critical determinant in procedural cost calculations, with reported durations comparable to those achieved using commercial retrieval bags. Third, the avoidance of a large auxiliary extraction incision may provide indirect cost benefits through faster postoperative recovery and shorter hospital stay.

Another practical advantage of handmade retrieval pouches is their inherent size flexibility, allowing accommodation of specimens with considerable variability in size compared with fixed-capacity commercial bags. In our series, a single pouch configuration was successfully used for spleens ranging from 12 to 22 cm. In contrast, previously published reports have noted limitations of commercial retrieval bags in cases where specimen size approaches or exceeds device capacity, sometimes necessitating auxiliary incisions [28, 39].

Nevertheless, handmade alternatives such as the present technique may be technically demanding due to their pliable structure and lack of dedicated deployment mechanisms, requiring greater surgical precision and potentially prolonging retrieval time during the initial learning phase [19, 21]. In our series, the first case served as a developmental procedure in which two key technical challenges were identified. First, although the operating surgeon had prior experience using the same pouch material in its smaller compartment for smaller specimens (e.g., appendectomy), using the larger compartment for splenic retrieval posed a novel challenge related to control of the pouch opening during exteriorization. This was resolved by securing the pouch mouth with a running Prolene suture. Second, the first case represented the initial experience with controlled in-bag splenic fragmentation. Once these steps were standardized, the retrieval time was stabilized, consistent with learning curve patterns reported in previous series [41].

In this study, Hem-o-lok® polymer clips were used exclusively for splenic hilar control, with arterial ligation performed before venous division to limit venous congestion and reduce intraoperative blood loss through controlled splenic decompression [41, 42]. Alternative hilar control techniques such as endoscopic staplers, LigaSure®, and Harmonic scalpel® have been reported [43]; however, their use was avoided in our practice due to limited working space for staplers in children, higher disposable costs, and concerns regarding reliable sealing of large-caliber splenic vessels in the setting of splenomegaly. Notably, our series reported no intraoperative complications and no conversions, which compares favorably with published literature that reports conversion rates ranging from 3% to 20% due to hemorrhagic complications [18, 44, 45].

Selective drain placement was employed in five patients in this series, and all drains were removed within 48 h. This conservative approach aligns with current practice, which discourages routine drainage in uncomplicated procedures [2, 46]. No postoperative collections or abscesses were noted, further supporting the safety of limited drain use.

Cosmetic outcomes and patient satisfaction were particularly encouraging as the patients and families appreciated the small, discreet scars compared to traditional open splenectomy or Pfannenstiel incision approaches. Twenty-three patients (92%) reported the highest satisfaction rating, with only minor cosmetic concerns expressed by two adolescent females regarding the umbilical scar. These results align with previous studies that highlighted the importance of cosmetic outcomes in minimally invasive surgery [6, 47, 48].

Taken together, this study and previous reports support the feasibility of low-cost handmade specimen retrieval strategies in LS without compromising procedural safety, provided that appropriate materials and retrieval techniques are employed with due caution and tailored to splenic size and the clinical context.

Finally, this study has some limitations, including a small sample size, single-surgeon experience, and a non-comparative design, which reflect its early, hypothesis‑generating purpose. Additionally, the risk of pouch perforation cannot be completely excluded, although no pouch-related complications were observed in this series. Finally, cost analysis was not formally quantified, although clear qualitative economic advantages were observed.

Conclusion

In this preliminary series, the use of a repurposed sterile diathermy pouch for specimen retrieval during pediatric LS was feasible and safe across a broad range of splenic sizes. The technique enabled controlled in-bag fragmentation and extraction without the need for additional incisions or retrieval-related complications. It is based on readily available, low-cost materials, and does not require specialized instruments. These findings warrant further evaluation through larger, multicenter prospective studies with comparative or randomized designs.
