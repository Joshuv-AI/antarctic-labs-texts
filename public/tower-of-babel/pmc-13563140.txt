Non-antibiotic treatments for the management of recurrent urinary tract infection in the multidrug resistance era: A narrative review
Authors: Dong Soo Kim, Chang Il Choi, Jin Bong Choi, Taesoo Choi, Jeong Woo Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Recurrent urinary tract infections (UTIs) are a significant global health burden, increasingly complicated by antibiotic resistance. Traditional approaches with antibiotics increase the risk of multidrug-resistant (MDR) strains, emphasizing the need for new non-antibiotic treatments. Several promising approaches have emerged during the last decade. Microbiome-based therapies, including probiotics, asymptomatic bacteriuria strains, and fecal microbiota transplantation, aim to restore microbial balance. Immunomodulation, through cytokine targeting and bacterial vaccines, shows potential for boosting host defenses. Bacteriophage therapy offers precision targeting of MDR pathogens and biofilms. Nanoparticles enable targeted delivery and biofilm disruption through both organic and inorganic carriers. Additionally, agents like methenamine hippurate, D-mannose, estrogen, and cranberry extracts have shown varying degrees of efficacy and safety. These strategies represent essential steps toward sustainable UTI management, but most will require further clinical validation before their use in the general population.

INTRODUCTION

Urinary tract infection (UTI) is one of the most common bacterial infections worldwide and is constantly a major public health issue [1]. In the United States alone, 60% of adult women experience at least one episode in their lifetime and the social cost from medical fees and absence from work can add up to 3.5 billion dollars a year [12]. Unfortunately, UTIs are commonly known to recur, especially in sexually active young women [3]. A nationwide surveillance of antimicrobial resistance for uncomplicated cystitis in Korea reported recurrent infection in 11.5% of the patients [4]. Current guidelines define recurrent UTI (rUTI) as at least three UTIs per year or two UTIs in the last six months [5]. Repeated infections require thorough evaluation, and treatment. In 2019, the costs for evaluation of rUTIs added up to around $390 to $730 per workup, $10 to $3,970 in antibacterial treatment, and $50 to $1,290 in conservative management [6]. The risk of a simple cystitis progressing to pyelonephritis is known to be low [7], but recurrent attacks can increase risk of aggravation by four-fold [8] and will ultimately result in kidney function loss if not managed properly [9]. UTIs are also one of the most over-diagnosed infections, especially in senior hospitalized patients with indwelling catheters [10]. Despite many physicians adhering to stewardship, excessive and misguided antibiotics use will inevitably bring about the emergence of multidrug-resistant (MDR) bacterial strains [11]. The increase in studies on the choice and regimen of prophylactic modalities in invasive urologic procedures reflects the gravity of decision making between stewardship and UTI prevention [1213]. Recurrent infection can also have a major impact on a patient’s quality of life, reducing intimacy, social relationships, self-esteem and the capacity to work [14].

In order to turn the tide, research has focused on discovering new methods to treat, manage and prevent rUTI without the help of antibiotics. These include microbiome-based treatment, immunomodulation and bacterial vaccines, bacteriophage therapy, and nanoparticles (Table 1). In the current manuscript, we present a brief narrative review of the current non-antibiotic strategies for rUTI.

METHODS

We performed a nonsystematic narrative review of published articles in English from 2000 to December 2024. The basic search terms were as follows: (“urinary tract infection” OR “recurrent urinary tract infection”) AND (“treatment” OR “prevention” OR “strategies”). We also searched related articles on PubMed and manually searched the reference list of identified articles to identify additional relevant articles.

NON-ANTIBIOTIC TREATMENTS FOR rUTI

1. Microbiome-based treatments

The discovery of the urinary microbiome radically shifted our understanding of urinary health and disease. For many years, the urinary tract was considered sterile, and this assumption had been implemented in traditional UTI management strategies. However, advanced culturing techniques such as enhanced quantitative urine culture and sequencing technologies have demonstrated that diverse microbial communities inhabit the urinary tract, even in healthy individuals. Species such as Firmicutes, Actinobacteria, Proteobacteria, and Bacteroidetes have been identified in both normal male and female urinary tracts. The presence of a core microbiome, though not yet clearly defined, has been hypothesized, and its disruption is increasingly thought to be related to the development of rUTIs [1516].

Antibiotic treatment can induce long-term changes in microbial communities, leading to dysbiosis and promoting the emergence of MDR pathogens. This is particularly concerning in the context of rUTIs, where up to 60% of recurrences are caused by the same strain that initiated the primary infection. Considering the socio-economical burden of rUTIs, there is a growing interest in microbiome-based therapies, aimed at restoring a healthy microbial balance [1718].

Several microbiome-based strategies are under investigation. Probiotic therapy has shown some promise, using the Lactobacillus species. One phase 2 randomized trial by Stapleton et al. [19] investigated the effect of vaginally administered Lactobacillus crispatus (Lactin-V) in women with a history of rUTIs. In this study, 50 women received Lactin-V, and the recurrence rate was 15% compared to 27% in the placebo group. The strain was chosen for its known role in maintaining a low vaginal pH and production of lactic acid and hydrogen peroxide, both known to inhibit urinary pathogens [19]. More recent studies have revisited Lactobacillus rhamnosus GR-1 and related strains as potential treatment candidates. A large randomized, double-blind trial by Beerepoot et al. [20] evaluated the efficacy of oral L. rhamnosus GR-1 and Lactobacillus reuteri RC-14 in preventing UTIs in 252 postmenopausal women over 12 months. The mean number of UTIs dropped from 6.8 to 3.3 in the probiotic group, although the trial did not meet non-inferiority criteria compared to antibiotic prophylaxis. Notably, antibiotic resistance increased in the antibiotic treated group but not in the probiotic group [20]. Mishra et al. [21] conducted a prospective, double-blind, randomized placebo-controlled trial on adult female patient with rUTI history. The intervention group received multi-strain probiotics containing Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium species along with standard care. Their results showed a striking 75% recurrence-free rate in the probiotic group compared to the 33% for the placebo with relative risk for absence of recurrence was 2.67. Mean number of UTI episodes decreased in the probiotic group, but it was not statistically significant. The small sample limits confidence but this study highlights the role of probiotics in rUTI [21].

Another approach involves deliberate colonization of the bladder with asymptomatic bacteriuria (ABU) strains. Escherichia coli 83972, originally isolated from a patient who had asymptomatic colonization for over three years, is the most well-studied example [2223]. Clinical trials, such as that by Sundén et al. [24], showed that inoculation with E. coli 83972 significantly reduced symptomatic episodes in patients with incomplete bladder emptying due to neurogenic bladders. The strain lacks functional fimbriae and other adherence factors, reducing its pathogenicity, but appears to competitively inhibit uropathogenic E. coli (UPEC) strains through nutrient competition and possibly bacteriocin production.

Microbiota transplantation strategies are also being explored. Fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT), well established for treating recurrent Clostridioides difficile infection, has shown benefits in reducing rUTI. Tariq et al. [25] described a patient who experienced a significant reduction in rUTI episodes following FMT for recurrent C. difficile infection. Further support comes from a clinical study by Jeong et al. [26], which investigated 11 patients undergoing FMT for recurrent C. difficile infection. All patients experienced a complete resolution of recurrent UTIs during a two-year follow-up period, with a statistically significant drop in infection frequency [26]. Larger controlled trials are needed to confirm efficacy, optimal donor selection, and long-term safety.

The growing understanding of the urinary microbiome’s role in rUTI opens new possibilities for sustainable, microbiome-preserving therapies. While further mechanistic studies and large-scale trials are needed, early evidence suggests that probiotics, ABU strains, and microbiota transplants may help restore urinary health and reduce recurrence without contributing to antibiotic resistance.

2. Immunomodulation and bacterial vaccines

Immunomodulation refers to manipulating the human immune response to achieve a certain goal. In this case, enhance protective responses and reduce harmful inflammation to prevent or manage urinary tract infection. Both the innate and adaptive immune systems are implicated and includes treatment with bacterial vaccines or other molecules of interest [27].

Innate immunomodulation therapy seeks to restore balance by targeting specific regulators of the immune response. Transcription factors IRF3 and IRF7 are central to this process. IRF3 promotes protective immunity, while IRF7 drives hyperinflammation. In animal models, silencing IRF7 using siRNA significantly reduced kidney pathology and improved bacterial clearance, with effects comparable to antibiotics [27]. In acute cystitis, overexpression of IL-1β and MMP7 increased inflammation and pain. Interventions using IL-1 receptor antagonists (such as anakinra) and MMP7 inhibitors have been shown to regulate these effects and enhance pathogen clearance [2829].

Vaccination is a well-known form of immunomodulation that targets adaptive immunity. A recent systemic review and meta-analysis evaluated five bacterial vaccines for rUTI [30]. Uro-Vaxom (OM-89), an oral vaccine made from E. coli lysates, demonstrated a pooled risk ratio of 1.94 for achieving UTI-free status versus placebo, though this result was not statistically significant [30]. Uromune (MV140), a sublingual vaccine containing inactivated E. coli, Klebsiella pneumoniae, Enterococcus faecalis, and Proteus vulgaris, showed more promising results, with one study reporting a risk ratio of 2.23, the highest among all reviewed vaccines [31]. Solco-Urovac, administered vaginally, yielded limited benefit under standard regimens, but booster protocols significantly improved efficacy [32]. ExPEC4V, an injectable vaccine targeting E. coli O-antigens, was safe and immunogenic in early trials, though its clinical efficacy remains uncertain [33]. These results suggest that vaccines with broader pathogen coverage may offer superior protection against rUTIs. The number needed to treat ranged widely, with values as low as 2.8, indicating potential clinical utility. Adverse events were generally mild, with no vaccine-related mortality reported [30].

Immunomodulation therapy for rUTI—whether via innate immune targeting or adaptive vaccination strategies—holds considerable promise. However, further research is needed to validate these approaches in larger, clinical trials.

3. Bacteriophage therapy

Bacteriophages (phages) are viruses that selectively infect and destroy bacteria. After attaching to bacterial cells, phages inject their genetic material, hijacking the bacterium’s machinery to produce new phage components. This ultimately leads to the lysis of the bacterial cell, allowing the phage life cycle to begin anew [34]. Discovered by Frederick Twort in 1915 and Félix d’Hérelle in 1917 [3536], phages were studied for therapeutic potential before antibiotics were widely used. While most modern medicine societies largely abandoned them following the success of antibiotics, phage therapy persisted in Eastern Europe, where research on personalized treatments for infections, including UTI, were continued [3738]. This laid the groundwork for renewed interest in phages as an alternative against MDR infections.

Phage therapy shows several advantages over traditional antibiotic treatment. It offers high host specificity, minimizing disruption of commensal flora, and demonstrates efficacy against bacterial biofilms. Phages also self-replicate at the site of infection, potentially increasing their effectiveness without requiring high systemic doses [34]. Recent interest in phage research has led to a rapid increase in publications, with over 700 articles on phage therapy published in 2022 alone, more than threefold increase since the last decade [39]. However, clinical evidence for phage use remains limited. Al-Anany et al. [40] looked into 55 studies on phage therapy for UTIs, encompassing more than 2,000 participants. Clinical improvement was seen in approximately 73% of cases, with microbiological eradication in 78%, most commonly targeting E. coli, but also covering pathogens like K. pneumoniae, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Enterococcus spp., and Acinetobacter baumannii [40]. Modes of administration included intravesical instillation, oral or rectal delivery, and sometimes even intravenous routes. The most frequently used regimen lasted 7 to 14 days, although extended durations were reported in severe or recurrent cases.

Among the different routes of administration intravesical instillation appears to be particularly promising. A pilot randomized controlled trial involving kidney transplant recipients with rUTI showed that phage therapy could reduce the mean annual infection rate from seven to two, with complete suppression of recurrence when combined with a probiotic [41]. However, another trial using intravesical Pyo phage cocktail showed no superiority over placebo or antibiotics. Factors such as phage titers (typically 104–106 PFU/mL), strain matching, and treatment duration could have been the reasons for the differences [42].

Phages have also been seen as very strong against biofilm-forming pathogens, one of the primary drivers of treatment failure in rUTI. Engineered phages that express biofilm-degrading enzymes (e.g., depolymerases) have demonstrated superior biofilm eradication and synergism with antibiotics. This combined approach has shown enhanced bacterial suppression, deeper biofilm penetration, and reduced development of resistance [4344].

Resistance to phages remains a concern, but its clinical significance is debatable. Personalized phage therapy, where phages are selected or engineered based on patient-specific bacterial isolates, is being investigated as a mean to overcome resistance and expand therapeutic scope. Case reports and early-phase trials suggest that this approach may be particularly suited to rUTI, which is often caused by diverse and evolving strains [4546]. Recently, a Phase 2 trial (ELIMINATE) demonstrated the safety and pharmacodynamics of LBP-EC01, a CRISPR-Cas3-enhanced bacteriophage cocktail, which showed rapid reduction of E. coli in urine when combined with antibiotics in women with a history of drug-resistant UTIs [47]. Notably, the safety profile of phage therapy appears reassuring. Across multiple studies, adverse events were mild and infrequent. Only 5.1% of patients experienced mild-to-moderate side effects, mostly limited to local irritation or transient fever. No serious adverse events were attributed directly to phages [39].

Despite its potential, phage therapy has yet to achieve mainstream clinical adoption, due to regulatory and logistical barriers, limiting its routine clinical use to only experimental settings. The personalized nature of phage production complicates large-scale commercialization and issues such as intellectual property protection, stability of phage preparations, and variability in protocols have hindered broader applications.

4. Nanoparticles

Nanoparticles (NPs) are submicron-sized particles (typically ranging from 1 to 1,000 nanometers) that can be engineered from a wide array of materials, including polymers, metals, lipids, and ceramics [48]. Their small size and large surface area allow for unique interactions with biological systems, including enhanced tissue penetration, prolonged circulation times, and targeted drug delivery [49]. These features have made them attractive platforms to use in complex infectious diseases [50]. Recurrent infections often involve intracellular bacterial reservoirs, persistent biofilms, and rising antibiotic resistance, which can hinder the effectiveness of standard antibiotics. NPs offer a versatile solution by enhancing antimicrobial delivery, disrupting biofilms, and enabling localized or mucosal administration [4850].

Among the diverse NPs under research, organic nanoparticles such as chitosan- or PLGA-based nanocarriers have demonstrated biofilm inhibition and sustained antimicrobial release. Chitosan-coated PLGA nanoparticles loaded with amphotericin B exhibited potent antifungal activity against Candida species with minimal cytotoxicity, addressing the increasingly recognized role of fungal co-pathogens in UTIs [51]. Glycerol monolaurate-loaded nanocapsules also showed enhanced antibiofilm effects against P. aeruginosa compared to free drug [52].

Inorganic NPs, such as silver, zinc, copper, and gold, have shown broad-spectrum antibacterial activity. Silver nanoparticles (AgNPs) remain the most extensively studied, with some formulations already used in catheter coatings. AgNPs was seen to have increased binding to bacterial lectins and significantly enhanced bactericidal activity against P. aeruginosa [53]. Green-synthesized AgNPs, produced using plant extracts, have further improved biocompatibility and ecological safety [54]. Other inorganic systems, such as zinc oxide or copper oxide nanoparticles, have demonstrated antibiofilm activity and are being explored as catheter coatings or drug carriers. For example, Zn-doped CuO coatings significantly delayed the onset of catheter associated urinary tract infections in vivo [55].

Despite these advances, most studies remain preclinical. Very few have progressed to human trials, and pharmacokinetic and toxicological profiles remain insufficiently characterized. Still, nanoparticle systems represent one of the most promising innovations in the prevention and treatment of recurrent UTIs, especially when designed for local delivery and biofilm disruption.

5. Other notable treatment to consider

As research continues to broaden our understanding of recurrent UTIs, the scope of potential treatment options is also expanding. Several alternatives, including methenamine hippurate, D-mannose, vaginal estrogen therapy, and cranberry extracts have demonstrated efficacy.

Methenamine hippurate is a urinary antiseptic that exerts bactericidal activity by decomposing into formaldehyde in acidic urine. A recent systematic review in 2023 concluded that methenamine hippurate is not inferior to daily antibiotic prophylaxis in preventing rUTIs in women and may be associated with fewer side effects and reduced antimicrobial resistance development [56]. Especially, the ALTAR trial demonstrated comparable effectiveness to low-dose antibiotics, with a slightly higher incidence of UTI episodes but improved antibiotic resistance profiles in the methenamine group. They also reported high patient adherence and tolerability, indicating real-world feasibility for long-term prophylactic use [57].

D-mannose, a sugar that inhibits adhesion of UPEC to urothelial cells, has also shown promising results in several trials. Systematic review shows that D-mannose significantly reduced recurrence rates, prolonged UTI-free intervals, and improved quality of life in both catheter and non-catheter users [58]. A randomized controlled trial by Kranjčec et al. [59] involving over 300 women found that D-mannose was as effective as nitrofurantoin in preventing recurrent infections, with fewer side effects and higher patient satisfaction. While dosage and duration protocols vary, D-mannose is well tolerated and may serve as an effective prophylactic option.

Vaginal estrogen therapy for rUTI is recommended for postmenopausal women by multiple guidelines [60]. A Cochrane review found that topical vaginal estrogen significantly reduced UTI recurrence compared to placebo [61]. In addition, the 2022 European Association of Urology guidelines cite randomized controlled trials where estrogen therapy decreased UTI incidence by restoring the protective vaginal microbiota and improving urogenital tissue integrity [62].

Cranberry extracts, rich in proanthocyanidins, are believed to inhibit bacterial adhesion to the uroepithelium and are easily accessible compared to other medical treatments [60]. Some studies, such as those included in a Cochrane review, have shown modest benefit in reducing rUTI recurrence in women. However, the heterogeneity in formulations and dosing regimens has led to inconsistent findings across clinical trials, contributing to conflicting guideline recommendations [63].

PHENOTYPICAL CLASSIFICATION AND PRECISION PROPHYLAXIS

The different strategies mentioned above have their own mechanisms for inhibiting rUTI (Fig. 1). However, for each method to achieve its maximal potential, clinical stratification is needed to figure out which choice is correct for each patient. Phenotypic classification of rUTI patients enables the optimization of treatment regimens by directly addressing the specific underlying pathophysiology of their recurrence. For example, postmenopausal women would benefit from vaginal estrogen therapy to restore normal flora with D-mannose supplementation to inhibit UPEC adhesion. Patients with neurogenic bladder and incomplete emptying would be prone to MDR colonization and therefore benefit more from ABU strain colonization. Many rUTI patients often use some method of catheterization, these patients would benefit from methenamine hippurate by maintaining a constant antiseptic environment and nanoparticle-coated catheter could help prevent biofilm formation. Finally for patients with refractory MDR rUTI where standard prophylaxis fails, bacteriophage therapy or FMT (if concurrent gastrointestinal dysbiosis exists) could offer a chance of remission from infection. Phenotypical classification of rUTI also signifies that they shouldn’t be grouped collectively into one vague category of difficult conditions to treat. They should be correctly divided according to etiology and prophylaxis used differently for each category, ultimately leading to “precision prophylaxis”.

CONCLUSIONS

The management of recurrent UTIs has reached a critical crossroads where the necessity for antibiotic-sparing strategies is no longer optional but imperative. Methenamine hippurate and D-mannose currently offer the most robust evidence for immediate clinical integration and newer oral vaccines show higher efficacy. However, several barriers remain. The transition of nanotechnology from preclinical models to human trials is hindered by a lack of long-term toxicological data, and the variability in phage therapy protocols prevents large-scale commercialization.

Future efforts must prioritize the identification of a ‘core’ urinary microbiome and the development of ‘precision prophylaxis’—a phenotypic approach that stratifies patients by their specific risk factors to offer tailored, non-antibiotic interventions. By shifting from reactive treatment to etiology-specific, ecological management, we can preserve antibiotic efficacy while significantly improving the quality of life for patients burdened by recurrent infections.
