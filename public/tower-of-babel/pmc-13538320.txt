NIR-II conjugated polymers for tumor sonodynamic therapy
Authors: Weiying Qin, Sufang Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Sonodynamic therapy (SDT) utilizes ultrasound to activate sonosensitive agents, generating reactive oxygen species (ROS) within tumor cells to induce oxidative damage. The advantages of ultrasound, such as deep tissue penetration, non-invasiveness, and spatial controllability, make it a promising candidate for cancer treatment. However, its practical application is still limited by factors such as low in vivo delivery efficiency, tumor hypoxia and antioxidant defense mechanisms, insufficient conversion of immunogenic cell death into durable anti-tumor immunity, and the lack of standardized ultrasound parameters, ROS quantification, and material quality control. Near-infrared II (NIR-II) conjugated polymers offer a promising platform because their tunable π-conjugated backbone, stable nano-assembly structure, organic composition, and modular design allow for the integration of imaging, ultrasound-triggered ROS generation, drug delivery, and immune microenvironment modulation into a single system. This article reviews recent research progress in NIR-II conjugated polymer SDT for cancer treatment. First, the physicochemical mechanisms, ROS generation pathways, and validation methods of sonodynamic therapy (SDT) are discussed, and conjugated polymers are compared with traditional small molecule and inorganic/semiconductor sonosensitive agents. This paper analyzes the significance of near-infrared II (NIR-II) imaging for surgical drug delivery (SDT) in terms of drug tracing, tumor localization, and therapeutic window. It further discusses the molecular design, aggregation state regulation, degradability, nanoassembly, delivery, and translational applications of organic conjugated polymer materials. Tumor-related barriers, including hypoxia, delivery obstacles, immunosuppressive microenvironment, and metabolic disorders, are then analyzed, and representative combined therapy strategies involving oxygen supply, chemotherapy, immunotherapy, myeloid cell remodeling, and matrix modulation are summarized. Finally, the challenges faced by materials in NIR-II signal quantification, ROS validation, long-term biosafety, ultrasound dosimetry, and clinical translation are discussed.

Introduction

Cancer remains one of the major diseases threatening human health worldwide. Modern medicine has developed several established therapeutic modalities, including surgery, chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and immunotherapy. Surgical resection often fails to completely remove highly heterogeneous tumor tissues, and residual tumor cells can lead to recurrence or distant metastasis [1]. The efficacy of chemotherapy is frequently limited by drug resistance and systemic toxicity. In advanced disease, tumor heterogeneity is often accompanied by antigen loss or overexpression of immunosuppressive molecules such as programmed death ligand 1 (PD-L1). With increasing understanding of tumor biology, the accumulation of regulatory T cells (Tregs) and myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), together with insufficient infiltration of cytotoxic immune cells in the tumor microenvironment, has been widely recognized as an important cause of treatment failure. Clinical studies indicate that only approximately 20–30% of patients eventually obtain durable benefit from immune checkpoint inhibitors [2]. Therefore, developing more precise, efficient, and low-toxicity anticancer strategies remains an important goal in current cancer therapy research.

Among emerging therapeutic approaches, photodynamic therapy (PDT) has attracted considerable attention because photosensitizers can generate reactive oxygen species (ROS) under irradiation with specific wavelengths of light, thereby inducing tumor cell death. After absorbing light, PDT materials are excited from the ground state to the excited state and subsequently undergo intersystem crossing to form an excited triplet state. They then generate singlet oxygen through Type II energy transfer or produce superoxide anions and hydroxyl radicals through Type I electron/hydrogen transfer, leading to direct tumor cell killing. Tumor cell damage can also induce the release of damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), which may activate antitumor immune responses to some extent [3]. However, PDT still has obvious limitations in practical applications. The penetration depth of excitation light in biological tissues is usually only several millimeters. Because long-term therapeutic benefit against deep tumors requires effective intervention within deeper lesions, PDT is generally more suitable for superficial tumors [3, 4].

Sonodynamic therapy, an emerging non-invasive treatment modality, provides a new strategy to overcome the limitations of PDT. When drugs are designed to target tumor cells, low-intensity ultrasound can activate sonosensitizing materials and generate high levels of intracellular ROS, thereby killing tumor cells [5]. Compared with PDT, ultrasound can penetrate biological tissues to a depth of approximately 10 cm and can achieve precise energy deposition through focusing. Owing to its minimal invasiveness and low cost, SDT shows more obvious advantages for the treatment of deep solid tumors [6].

Currently, commonly used sonosensitizers for SDT include porphyrin-based molecules and certain nanomaterials, which often suffer from poor water solubility, insufficient chemical stability, and limited ROS generation efficiency [7]. Traditional organic small-molecule sonosensitizers and nanosonosensitizers have provided an important basis for mechanistic validation and early applications of SDT, but both still face distinct limitations in precise deep-tumor therapy. Traditional small-molecule sonosensitizers, such as porphyrins, phthalocyanines, Ce6, rose bengal, and BODIPY, usually possess clear chemical structures and relatively well-defined excited-state pathways; however, their water solubility, tumor accumulation, aggregation stability, and multifunctional integration capacity remain limited [8–14]. Inorganic/semiconductor nanomaterials, such as TiO2, ZnO, black phosphorus, and metal oxides, can enhance ROS generation through bandgap regulation, surface defects, and catalytic reactions, but long-term in vivo retention, degradation and clearance, metal-related safety, and batch-to-batch consistency remain important concerns for clinical translation [15–22].

NIR-II imaging has attracted broad attention in biomedical research because of its unique capacity for visual localization. Compared with the traditional first near-infrared window (NIR-I, 700–900 nm), the NIR-II window exhibits markedly reduced tissue scattering and autofluorescence, enabling a higher signal-to-background ratio and deeper tissue penetration [23, 24]. Unlike MRI, CT, PET/SPECT, ultrasound imaging, or photoacoustic imaging, which mainly provide anatomical, functional, or complementary diagnostic information, NIR-II fluorescence is particularly suitable for real-time and dynamic tracing of nanomedicine distribution, tumor localization, and treatment-window selection during image-guided therapy. Several NIR-II material platforms, including rare-earth-doped nanoparticles, quantum dots, inorganic nanomaterials, and small-molecule organic dyes, have been explored for deep-tissue tumor imaging. For example, NaYF4-based lanthanide nanoparticles and NIR-II quantum dots can provide stable optical signals, whereas small-molecule NIR-II dyes possess clearer chemical structures and flexible modification potential. However, these systems may still face limitations related to long-term retention, element-associated safety, degradability, tumor retention, or multifunctional loading capacity. In this context, conjugated polymers show distinct advantages owing to their tunable π-conjugated electronic structures, programmable nanoassembly behavior, and broad functional integration capacity. Through rational molecular design, such as constructing donor–acceptor architectures or regulating side-chain groups, their absorption and emission wavelengths can be precisely tuned to the NIR-II region [25, 26]. These materials can assemble into conjugated polymer nanoparticles (CPNs), which typically exhibit high absorption cross sections, excellent photostability, and favorable photothermal or photoacoustic conversion capability [27]. More importantly, they can integrate NIR-II fluorescence imaging, photoacoustic imaging, drug delivery, ultrasound-triggered ROS generation, and immune/TME modulation within one platform, making them particularly suitable for SDT systems that require in vivo tracing, lesion localization, treatment-window selection, and multifunctional therapeutic activation [27, 28].This Review systematically summarizes recent progress in NIR-II conjugated polymers for tumor SDT. First, the mechanisms of SDT are introduced, and the structural characteristics and application limitations of different sonosensitizer systems are compared. Subsequently, the role of NIR-II imaging in drug tracing, tumor localization, and treatment-window selection is discussed, together with molecular design, nanoassembly, and translational standardization issues related to NIR-II conjugated polymers. Furthermore, this Review analyzes non-material factors that limit the practical application of SDT from the perspectives of tumor hypoxia, delivery barriers, immunosuppressive microenvironments, and metabolic abnormalities, and summarizes representative advances in NIR-II conjugated polymer platforms combined with oxygen supply, chemotherapy, immunotherapy, and TME modulation. Finally, future directions in imaging-signal quantification, ROS mechanism validation, long-term biosafety, ultrasound dosimetry standardization, and clinical translation are discussed.

We conducted a focused search on English-language literature published between January 2010 and May 2026 in the Web of Science Core Collection, PubMed, Scopus, and Google Scholar databases. Search terms included combinations of keywords such as “sonic dynamics therapy,” “sound sensitizers,” “reactive oxygen species,” “near-infrared II,” “conjugated polymers,” “semiconductor polymer nanoparticles,” “tumor microenvironment,” “hypoxia,” “immunotherapy,” and “cancer nanomedicine.” We also included early, landmark studies explaining the development and mechanisms of sonic dynamics therapy.

Mechanisms of SDT

Physicochemical basis and energy deposition process of SDT

The therapeutic efficacy of SDT mainly originates from ultrasound-induced activation of sonosensitizers and subsequent ROS generation. In current preclinical and translational studies, SDT is often performed using low-intensity pulsed ultrasound (LIPU), with working frequencies typically in the MHz range and spatial-average intensities commonly between 0.5 and 5 W cm−2 [8, 29]. SDT is a continuous process involving coupled physical and chemical effects. Effective energy deposition mainly includes cavitation, mechanical effects, thermal effects, and, under specific conditions, sonoluminescence and sonochemical effects [9, 29]. Among these processes, cavitation is a core step in SDT. It is a dynamic process initiated by pre-existing gas nuclei and accompanied by bubble oscillation, collapse, and secondary mechanical and chemical effects [29–32]. Nano- to microscale gas nuclei may already exist in liquids or tissue interstitial spaces. When an ultrasound field alternately imposes compression and rarefaction phases, these nuclei expand during the rarefaction phase and contract during the compression phase. At relatively low acoustic pressure, bubbles mainly undergo stable cavitation, which refers to continuous oscillation around the equilibrium radius with gradual growth through rectified diffusion; microstreaming, local shear stress, and membrane permeability changes are more common under these conditions. When peak negative pressure further increases, or when frequency, gas-nucleus size, medium viscoelasticity, and dissolved gas conditions favor nonlinear growth, bubbles enter inertial cavitation, characterized by rapid expansion during the rarefaction phase and violent collapse during the compression phase [29–32]. Extreme compression inside collapsing bubbles can induce sonochemistry and sonoluminescence. The interior of collapsing bubbles can form a “hot spot in a cold liquid”, with local temperatures as high as approximately 20,000 K, pressures of several thousand bar, and heating/cooling rates exceeding 1012 K s−1 [31]. Under these conditions, water and dissolved gases can undergo pyrolysis to generate reactive intermediates such as OH radicals and H radicals. Meanwhile, nanosecond-scale short-wavelength light emission may indirectly excite sonosensitizers with matching absorption bands [29, 31]. Thus, ultrasound first establishes a locally controllable cavitation microenvironment and then realizes energy deposition and biological damage through mechanical, chemical, and light-emission pathways. Under inertial cavitation, violent microbubble collapse produces localized high temperature, high pressure, and strong microjet conditions, thereby creating favorable conditions for subsequent radical generation and sonosensitizer activation [29]. Ultrasound-induced mechanical perturbation can increase membrane permeability and promote nanomedicine diffusion within the tissue interstitium, whereas local thermal effects may further sensitize cells to oxidative stress [8, 29]. However, current evidence suggests that the relative contributions of sonoluminescence, cavitation-induced radical generation, and purely mechanical disruption are not constant across different systems. Instead, they are jointly influenced by ultrasound frequency, duty cycle, acoustic pressure, dissolved gas content, tissue interfaces, and the energy-level structure and aggregation state of the sonosensitizer [9, 29]. Taking the 5-ALA-derived PpIX system as an example, PpIX is a classic photo/sono-dual sensitizing molecule, and PpIX-conjugated nanoparticles can also act as cavitation nuclei. Researchers used Au-PpIX as cavitation nucleation sites and recorded integrated sonoluminescence signals in the 220–700 nm range under 1 MHz ultrasound. The results showed that the Au-PpIX-containing gel model displayed significantly stronger sonoluminescence signals than the control group, consistent with chemiluminescent dosimetry results [32]. In cellular experiments, H22 hepatoma cells treated with PpIX exhibited an 82.6% decrease in viability after SDT, accompanied by increased mitochondrial ROS, mitochondrial swelling, and cytochrome c release (19.16%), suggesting that PpIX sonoactivation is associated not only with cavitation chemistry but also with mitochondrial apoptosis pathways [32–34]. In animal tumor models, 5-ALA/PpIX systems also provide more convincing evidence than spectroscopic inference alone. In a rat intracranial C6 glioma model, 5-ALA was administered at 100 mg kg−1, followed by focused ultrasound irradiation at 1.04 MHz and 10 W cm−2 for 5 min. The maximal coronal tumor area in the SDT group was 18.32 ± 5.69 mm2, significantly lower than that in the sham group (29.94 ± 10.39 mm2) and the ultrasound-alone group (30.81 ± 9.65 mm2) [35]. Jeong et al. further applied 5-ALA at 60 mg kg−1 and fractionated ultrasound irradiation (1 MHz, 2.65 W cm−2; 16 min irradiation + 3 min recess + 4 min irradiation) in an orthotopic C6 glioma model. Two weeks after treatment, tumor volume in the SDT group was only 10.50 ± 8.20 mm3, compared with 122.48 ± 39.64 mm3, 87.42 ± 21.40 mm3, and 56.42 ± 12.48 mm3 in the sham, ultrasound-alone, and Radachlorin + ultrasound groups, respectively [36]. These results indicate that the 5-ALA/PpIX system can achieve significant selective tumor inhibition in deep glioma models without relying on craniotomy-based light irradiation. Overall, ultrasound intervention and its biological outcomes are not governed by a single process or mechanism but involve multiple contributing factors, whose systematic evaluation is essential for further mechanistic investigation of SDT.

ROS generation pathways and quantification

From the therapeutic perspective, the direct cytotoxic effect of SDT is highly dependent on the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS). Current studies generally suggest that SDT can generate singlet oxygen (1O2; a Type II-like pathway) through energy transfer and can also produce reactive species such as superoxide anions (O2·-) and hydroxyl radicals (·OH) through electron/hydrogen transfer (Type I-like pathways) (Fig. 1) [10, 29]. At the experimental level, 1O2 is commonly verified using Singlet Oxygen Sensor Green (SOSG), electron spin resonance (ESR) spin trapping, or radical scavenging assays, whereas ·OH and O2·- are more often assessed using DMPO-based spin-trapping systems combined with multiple fluorescent or colorimetric probes for cross-validation [10]. It should be noted that different probe systems are susceptible to interference from pH, metal ions, photobleaching, and ultrasound-induced thermal effects. Using DCF as a ROS probe, de Haan et al. systematically compared pH 6.0, 7.4, and 9.0 in cell-free experiments and found that DCF formation was significantly suppressed under acidic conditions, whereas DCFH-DA deacetylation was accelerated when the system pH increased to 9.0, leading to elevated DCF signals. FCS, heat-inactivated FCS, and BSA all increased background fluorescence; therefore, ROS levels may be overestimated if cell-free controls are not included [37]. Ohashi et al. demonstrated that DCFH can be directly oxidized by heme, hemoglobin, myoglobin, and cytochrome c. In K562 cells, DCFH oxidation increased with intracellular heme content after delta-ALA pretreatment, indicating that iron-containing porphyrin or metal-catalytic environments can themselves generate false-positive signals [38]. For 1O2 detection, Ragas et al. reported that SOSG can photosensitize singlet oxygen under ultraviolet or visible light irradiation, whereas Prasad et al. further showed that white light can cause obvious accumulation of SOSG-EP fluorescence and that red light markedly reduces this artifact. Thus, enhanced fluorescence does not necessarily indicate a true increase in sample-derived 1O2 [39, 40]. In addition, Surzhikova et al. showed that the ratiometric signal I488/I435 of fluorescein systems increases linearly with temperature from 10 to 80 °C at pH 6.5, suggesting that local temperature elevation caused by ultrasound may introduce thermal artifacts for probes based on fluorescein/xanthene readout scaffolds [41]. These studies indicate that, for systems designed for deep-tumor therapy, ESR, spin trapping, specific scavengers, cell-free/cellular controls, and repeated validation under tissue-covering conditions should be combined to improve the reliability of conclusions [37–41]. Therefore, many studies emphasize multi-method cross-validation, especially for deep-tumor systems where ROS signals must remain effectively inducible and detectable under tissue-covered conditions. This issue has been well illustrated in organic conjugated polymer-based sonosensitizing systems. A deep-tissue-activatable polymeric sono-immunotherapy platform maintained approximately 65% of its characteristic ESR signal when the tissue-covering thickness increased from 0 to 10 cm. Under 1.0 MHz ultrasound at 1.2 W cm−2 with a 50% duty cycle, the system still generated 1O2 effectively and released a high proportion of the immunomodulatory module [42]. Sonosensitizer materials

Traditional small-molecule sonosensitizers

Sonosensitizing materials are central determinants of SDT efficacy. According to material origin and structural features, commonly used sonosensitizers can be broadly divided into three categories: traditional organic small-molecule sonosensitizers, inorganic/semiconductor nanomaterials, and recently developed organic conjugated polymer nanomaterials (Fig. 2). The earliest SDT materials were organic small-molecule sonosensitizers. Existing studies generally classify them into porphyrins/porphyrin derivatives, chlorins, phthalocyanines, xanthene dyes, cyanine dyes, phenothiazine dyes, and BODIPY molecules [9, 10]. Their common structural features include tetrapyrrole macrocycles and extended pi-conjugated frameworks, which provide strong absorption and relatively stable excited states, making them classic sensitizers for PDT and SDT [11, 25]. Chlorins such as Ce6 represent important structural extensions of porphyrin systems, and their red-region absorption offers additional advantages in certain tumor treatment settings [35, 36]. Phthalocyanines consist of highly conjugated phthalocyanine macrocycles and central metal ions, showing high photostability and a large space for peripheral substituent modification [13]. In addition, xanthene dyes such as rose bengal and BODIPY derivatives can enhance intersystem crossing and ROS generation potential through halogen substitution or donor–acceptor regulation [8, 14]. Under ultrasound irradiation, cavitation and associated local energy release can induce excited-state formation, enabling small molecules to transition from the ground state to excited states and then undergo intersystem crossing (ISC) to the triplet state. Subsequently, triplet-state molecules interact with surrounding oxygen or substrates through electron transfer to generate 1O2, O2·-, and ·OH [9]. Hematoporphyrin (Hp) is a classic porphyrin-based photo/sonosensitizing molecule. Yumita et al. used Hp as a sonosensitizer in tumor-bearing mice and applied ultrasound to the tumor region after drug injection; the tumor inhibition rate reached approximately 74% relative to the control group [11], whereas Hp alone showed no obvious antitumor effect. This indicates that Hp itself does not exert marked cytotoxicity but enhances the sensitivity of tumor cells to acoustic energy after ultrasound activation. Studies using 5-ALA in animal models reached similar conclusions [35, 36]. This characteristic was a key finding in the early development of SDT, as external ultrasound activation can largely reduce adverse effects caused during in vivo drug delivery and provides greater control over the site and timing of drug action [10].

Traditional small-molecule sonosensitizers have clear material structures, high pi-conjugation, and relatively well-defined excited-state pathways. However, they usually lack sufficient hydrophilic substituents or ionizable groups and readily undergo hydrophobic aggregation and pi-pi stacking in aqueous and physiological environments. Therefore, when used alone, small-molecule sonosensitizers often suffer from poor water solubility, insufficient aggregation stability, and aggregation-caused quenching. These issues ultimately result in poor targeting, low lesion retention, insufficient ROS production, and limited tumor suppression. Thus, at the preclinical experimental level, these molecules are suitable for SDT mechanism validation, but the accumulation of multiple limitations makes it difficult for them to demonstrate unique advantages in real clinical settings or in more complex deep-tumor environments. Their in vivo accumulation, tumor delivery, and deep-lesion treatment capacity still rely on subsequent structural modification, amphiphilic carrier encapsulation, or nanodelivery optimization.

Inorganic/semiconductor nanomaterials

Inorganic/semiconductor nanomaterials represent attractive alternative strategies for improving ROS efficiency in SDT and overcoming the poor efficacy caused by the intrinsic limitations of small-molecule sonosensitizers. Representative materials include TiO2, ZnO, MnO2, MnWOx, black phosphorus nanosheets, noble-metal composites, and various heterojunction or piezoelectric semiconductor nanostructures [15]. Unlike small-molecule sonosensitizers, the acoustic responsiveness of inorganic/semiconductor materials is jointly determined by inorganic lattices, semiconductor band structures, surface defects, metal doping, and nanoscale morphology. Typical metal oxides, such as TiO2 and ZnO, usually possess stable crystalline structures, defined conduction/valence band positions, and abundant surface hydroxyl groups and oxygen vacancies. These structural characteristics facilitate electron–hole separation and surface redox reactions under ultrasound-induced cavitation and local sonochemical environments [15]. Carboxymethyl dextran-modified TiO2 nanoparticles (HTiO2 NPs) have been constructed, in which polymer coating improved the dispersion stability of TiO2 in PBS and serum. After ultrasound treatment in tumor tissues, this system markedly enhanced 1O2 signals and achieved significant tumor inhibition in deep liver tumor models [16]. Another important advantage of inorganic semiconductor sonosensitizers is that doping, defect engineering, heterostructure construction, and surface modification can be used to regulate band structures and charge-separation efficiency. For example, Fe/Mn co-doped ZnO nanosonosensitizers used Fe and Mn to improve electron–hole separation in ZnO and further combined glutathione depletion and ferroptosis to enhance SDT efficacy [17]. Black phosphorus nanosheets are two-dimensional piezoelectric/semiconductor sonosensitizing materials. Their layered wrinkled structures and anisotropic lattices can generate piezoelectric polarization under ultrasound-induced mechanical strain, thereby promoting charge separation and ROS generation [18].

Inorganic/semiconductor materials have stable inorganic lattices and tunable band structures, allowing conduction/valence band regulation to modulate ROS generation pathways and increase ROS-mediated oxidative tumor damage. Surface defects, oxygen vacancies, and metal doping can enhance charge separation and surface catalytic reactions, while nanoscale morphology provides a larger specific surface area and more reaction sites [19]. To some extent, these materials overcome the limited functionality, poor delivery efficiency, and weak tumor-killing effects of small-molecule sonosensitizers. Nevertheless, their limitations are also prominent. In one study, labeled TiO2 nanoparticles were intravenously administered to rats, and tissue distribution and excretion were analyzed using ex vivo fluorescence imaging, gamma counting, and TEM. The results showed that TiO2 nanoparticles mainly accumulated in the liver and spleen and remained in these tissues for more than 30 days [20]. At higher exposure doses, TiO2 nanoparticles induced acute toxicity in the brain, lung, spleen, liver, and kidney of mice [21]. The aqueous dispersibility and colloidal stability of these materials depend on surface coatings. Although surface modification can prolong circulation, some materials still face long-term retention, metal-ion release, unclear degradation/clearance pathways, and potential chronic toxicity [15, 22]. Therefore, the clinical translation of inorganic/semiconductor nanomaterials is mainly constrained by long-term biosafety, metabolic clearance, and batch controllability. Potential metal residue remains a core translational concern [10, 29], and degradable, clearable, and long-term applicable systems remain challenging.

Organic conjugated polymer materials

Based on previous exploration of small-molecule sonosensitizers and inorganic/semiconductor materials, organic conjugated polymers have recently emerged as a more promising class of sonosensitizing platforms for SDT [43–45]. Compared with traditional small-molecule sonosensitizers and inorganic/semiconductor materials, the most notable advantage of organic conjugated polymers lies in their stronger functional extensibility. These materials are not simply functionalized derivatives of individual dye molecules but polymeric or polymer-like semiconducting platforms composed of repeating donor/acceptor units, pi-conjugated main chains, side-chain substituents, and nanoassembly layers. They usually contain three structural components. First, the main chain is commonly built from electron donor or acceptor units such as thiophene, benzothiadiazole, diketopyrrolopyrrole (DPP), benzobisthiadiazole (BBTD), isoindigo, and cyclopentadithiophene. These units regulate bandgaps, absorption/emission wavelengths, and charge-transfer capability through D-A or A-D-A structures. Spatial separation of the highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) and lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) favors red-shifted absorption, promotes efficient separation of photoinduced electron–hole pairs, and ultimately improves ROS generation [43, 45]. Second, alkyl chains, PEG, targeting ligands, or responsive linkers can be introduced into side chains or terminal groups to confer solubility, nanoassembly stability, and favorable protein-interaction properties [46]. Third, hydrophobic pi-conjugated backbones generally require nanoencapsulation, amphiphilic polymer coating, or copolymer modification to form stable colloidal particles [47]. Jiang et al. designed an amphiphilic semiconducting polymer, PEG-PCB, consisting of a hydrophobic semiconducting backbone and hydrophilic PEG side chains. It could self-assemble into uniform nanoparticles in water. The pi-conjugated backbone served as a NIR fluorescence/photoacoustic imaging component and loaded DOX through hydrophobic and pi-pi interactions, thereby integrating imaging, drug delivery, and therapeutic functions [48]. Similar SPNs benefit from long pi-electron-delocalized backbones, which provide high absorption coefficients and photostability. Thus, the advantage of conjugated polymer sonosensitizers is not only their ability to improve therapeutic efficacy but also their capacity to integrate optical signals, acoustic responsiveness, drug delivery, and multiple functional modules into a single nanostructure, thereby addressing multiple practical barriers in a stepwise manner. Through main-chain design, surface modification, and responsive linkages, these materials can be developed into multilevel nanoplatforms for delivery, imaging, SDT, and immune modulation [43–47]. Polymer nanoparticles combined with catalase have been used to improve penetration and SDT efficacy in large tumors through size optimization and oxygen regulation [47]. Polymers can also serve as activatable organic sonosensitizing cores that synchronously release immunotherapeutic agents, such as anti-CTLA-4 antibodies, after ultrasound activation, enabling temporally controlled immunotherapy [49]. Strategies integrating STING activation, MDSC alleviation, IDO inhibition, and deep orthotopic tumor immunotherapy have also been reported [50]. However, many conjugated polymers still possess hydrophobic rigid backbones. Similar to inorganic/semiconductor materials, their aqueous dispersibility often depends on PEGylation or nanoencapsulation. Because these platforms have a large design space, incorporation of different functional modules may lead to markedly different therapeutic outcomes. Consequently, unified efficacy evaluation standards are still lacking, particularly regarding molecular weight, chain-length distribution, batch consistency, degradation pathways, and in vivo clearance. Moreover, substantial clinical data are still unavailable for assessing their therapeutic value and defining suitable application scenarios. Therefore, the advantage of these materials is not that they completely eliminate the problems of small molecules and inorganic materials, but that they combine the strengths of different material classes and provide an SDT platform that can more readily integrate imaging, delivery, acoustic responsiveness, immune modulation, and other functions according to clinical needs (Fig. 2).Fig. 2Representative sonosensitizer materials for SDT. Functional module design of conjugated polymers

Roles of NIR-II conjugated polymers in SDT

Imaging and tracing play important roles in drug delivery and nanomedicine research because they reveal the complete pharmacological process from delivery to therapeutic action. For nanomedicines, in vivo efficacy is usually affected by multiple sequential steps, including blood circulation, tissue distribution, tumor accumulation, interstitial penetration, cellular uptake, and intracellular release. If drug behavior can only be indirectly inferred from endpoint tumor volume, histological sections, or ex vivo organ distribution without real-time visualization of dynamic processes, it is difficult to identify the true source of delivery failure [53, 54]. Clinical imaging studies of nanomedicines have shown that imaging can reveal pronounced differences in nanoparticle uptake among different patients and lesions, thereby helping to explain heterogeneous therapeutic responses and provide more accurate guidance for individualized dosing and treatment strategies [53–55].

Current imaging modalities for drug distribution and lesion localization mainly include MRI, CT, PET/SPECT, photoacoustic imaging, and fluorescence imaging. After long-term development, MRI provides excellent soft-tissue resolution and clinical applicability, but its relatively limited sensitivity usually requires the introduction of contrast agents such as Gd, Mn, or Fe3O4. CT offers high spatial resolution and broad clinical availability but has limited soft-tissue contrast and involves ionizing radiation; nanoparticle platforms commonly require iodine, gold, bismuth, or other elements for contrast enhancement and are often used for initial lesion mapping. PET/SPECT provides high sensitivity and whole-body quantitative capability, making it suitable for evaluating nanomedicine pharmacokinetics and biodistribution, but radiolabeling, higher cost, and stricter operational and safety requirements limit its accessibility [53, 54, 56]. Ultrasound imaging is inexpensive and widely used clinically, but conventional ultrasound lacks molecular specificity for nanomedicines unless microbubbles, phase-transition materials, or acoustic-responsive contrast modules are further introduced. Photoacoustic imaging combines optical absorption contrast with ultrasound detection depth and is suitable for monitoring blood oxygenation, vasculature, and absorptive nanoprobes; however, its signals are still influenced by tissue light fluence, acoustic attenuation, and endogenous absorption backgrounds, and its ability to interrogate deep visceral tumors remains limited in a manner partly analogous to PDT [57, 58]. NIR-II fluorescence imaging is more suitable for low-background, real-time, and dynamic observation of drug distribution. Compared with visible and NIR-I imaging, the NIR-II region generally exhibits lower tissue scattering, weaker autofluorescence, and a higher signal-to-background ratio, allowing clearer fluorescence images in deeper tissues [59, 60]. Within this imaging window, several material classes have been developed, including rare-earth-doped nanoparticles, quantum dots, inorganic nanoplatforms, small-molecule organic dyes, and conjugated polymers [23, 24, 51]. For example, NaYF₄-based lanthanide nanoparticles show narrow emission bands, long luminescence lifetimes, and favorable photostability [52], whereas NIR-II quantum dots, such as Ag2S-, PbS-, and InAs-based systems, usually provide high brightness and stable emission for vascular and tumor imaging [61, 62]. Small-molecule NIR-II dyes have clearer chemical structures and stronger molecular modifiability, making them attractive for tumor-targeted imaging and surgical guidance [24]. However, inorganic or heavy-element-containing probes may still raise concerns related to long-term retention, element-associated safety, degradation, and clearance, while small-molecule dyes may suffer from limited photostability, tumor retention, or multifunctional loading capacity [24, 63]. In comparison, NIR-II conjugated polymers occupy a distinct position because their tunable π-conjugated backbones, programmable nanoassembly, organic composition, and broad module-integration capacity allow imaging, ultrasound-responsive ROS generation, drug loading, and TME modulation to be coordinated within one platform [25, 27, 42]. For example, the NIR-II conjugated polymer pDA-PEG has been used for high-speed in vivo imaging of mouse blood flow; fluorinated polymer dots m-PBTQ4F have been applied to quantitative brain tumor imaging; and planar-twisted pNIR-4 nanoparticles have enabled NIR-IIa fluorescence imaging-guided precise tumor resection. Thus, NIR-II conjugated polymer platforms are particularly valuable for SDT systems that require drug tracing, lesion localization, treatment-window selection, and multifunctional therapeutic integration.The efficacy of SDT is strongly related to the time and location of ultrasound activation. As discussed above, ultrasound activation provides greater control over the time and site of drug action. To obtain optimal therapeutic benefit, it is necessary to observe in vivo drug distribution and determine the best ultrasound intervention window and precise irradiation site. If ultrasound irradiation is applied before sufficient accumulation of sonosensitizers at the tumor site, ROS production may be insufficient and treatment may fail. Conversely, if high material retention persists in the liver, spleen, or other normal tissues, non-target tissue exposure may increase. Therefore, determining an accurate ultrasound time window is also a key requirement for clinical translation of SDT. Several studies have already achieved favorable therapeutic outcomes by integrating NIR-II imaging. For instance, the NPRBOXA platform first used NIR-II fluorescence to assess in vivo distribution and tumor accumulation, and then selected the ultrasound-triggering window to generate ROS and promote conversion of OXA-IV into active OXA-II, integrating imaging localization, SDT, chemotherapy, and ICD induction [64]. DPP5-CBT NPs established a therapeutic self-evaluation process in which caspase-3-triggered nanoparticle disassembly restored NIR-II fluorescence after treatment, linking SDT-induced apoptosis and caspase-3 activation to NIR-II signal feedback [65]. The absence of accurate and reliable in vivo localization information can directly impair the effectiveness of externally activated therapy. For example, Huang et al. compared two small-molecule sonosensitizers, 5-ALA and sodium fluorescein, in subcutaneous and orthotopic brain C6 glioma models. Both 5-ALA and fluorescein suppressed tumor growth through SDT in subcutaneous C6 tumors. However, in orthotopic brain tumors, 5-ALA-SDT significantly inhibited tumor progression and improved survival, whereas fluorescein-SDT failed to show obvious efficacy because of insufficient early accumulation in the brain tumor region [64]. Thus, treatment failure may not necessarily result from the intrinsic inactivity of the sonosensitizer but may arise from insufficient lesion accumulation, tissue-barrier restriction, or mismatched activation timing. In this context, drug distribution and tracing information become particularly important. As an integrated framework connecting conjugated polymers with SDT, NIR-II imaging can provide higher signal-to-background imaging at greater tissue depths [60, 66]. Before ultrasound irradiation, it can clarify the location of sonosensitizer accumulation and help determine treatment timing and the optimal activation window, thereby improving spatial precision and reducing off-target exposure [66]. Molecular design can be used to achieve absorption/emission red shifts, bandgap regulation, and aggregation-state optimization, thereby enhancing NIR-II signal output and improving ultrasound-triggered excited-state formation and ROS generation efficiency [10, 66]. NIR-II conjugated polymers are readily constructed into nanoplatforms and can further integrate oxygen supply, immune modulation, drug release, photoacoustic imaging, photothermal therapy, and other functional modules [64]. A switchable-excitation NIR-II nanotheranostic system has been reported, in which NIR-II signals were used for deep-tumor localization before ultrasound-triggered ROS generation and ICD induction, achieving a sequence of lesion localization, controlled drug activation, and immune activation. Cui, Tang, and co-workers constructed the cRGD-modified NPRBOXA nanoplatform to target tumor-cell surface receptors [64]. This system realized functional division through sequential switching of excitation modes: first, under 808 nm excitation, NPRBOXA emitted NIR-II fluorescence to determine nanoparticle distribution and fluorescence intensity in vivo, thereby selecting the optimal therapeutic window; subsequently, ultrasound irradiation triggered ROS production and promoted reduction of the OXA-IV prodrug to active OXA-II, integrating SDT, chemotherapy, and immune activation into one platform. Li et al. screened DPP-based sonosensitizing molecules and obtained DPP5, a julolidine-regulated molecule with an extended conjugated structure that possessed strong NIR-II emission and ultrasound-activated ROS generation. By introducing a caspase-3-cleavable peptide sequence, Cys-Asp-Glu-Val-Asp-Lys-CBT, DPP5 self-assembled into fluorescence-quenched DPP5-CBT NPs under physiological conditions [65]. After ultrasound irradiation, DPP5-CBT NPs generated ROS and induced tumor cell apoptosis, while activated caspase-3 in turn promoted nanoparticle disassembly and restored bright NIR-II fluorescence [65]. This system used caspase-3 as an endogenous post-therapeutic molecular readout and established a closed feedback loop from ROS therapy to apoptosis initiation, caspase-3 activation, nanoparticle disassembly, and NIR-II fluorescence recovery. This work is notable because it shifts SDT efficacy evaluation from conventional endpoint tumor-volume assessment to real-time feedback at the molecular-event level, allowing NIR-II signals to be used not only for pretreatment localization but also for post-treatment self-evaluation.

For NIR-II conjugated material-mediated SDT, methodological standardization should first establish ROS quantification based on ESR/SOSG cross-validation and tissue-covering models to improve comparability among systems [10]. Second, NIR-II signals should be linked to specific therapeutic biomarkers to improve dose control and treatment scheduling. MRI, CT, and PET/SPECT are more suitable for clinical anatomical localization or whole-body quantitative pharmacokinetic studies; photoacoustic and ultrasound imaging are more appropriate for monitoring structure, blood flow, or absorption contrast; and NIR-II fluorescence is more suitable for real-time, low-background, and repeatable tracing of the drug itself in organic conjugated polymer platforms. Particularly for ultrasound-triggered therapy, NIR-II signals can answer two key questions before treatment: whether the drug has reached the lesion and when irradiation should be performed [51, 65–67]. Nevertheless, NIR-II signals should not be simply equated with true drug concentration or therapeutic efficacy. Future studies still need to combine ex vivo tissue quantification, pharmacokinetic analysis, ESR/SOSG-based ROS validation, and immunological marker detection. Reliable methodology and reproducible quantitative evaluation standards are prerequisites for clinical translation of this imaging strategy [55, 65, 67]. At present, NIR-II imaging is undoubtedly an important entry point for exploring SDT nanotheranostic platforms and is one of the key validation tools for advancing SDT toward practical application. Earlier conjugated polymer systems have already demonstrated that π-conjugated backbones can serve as ultrasound-responsive ROS-generating cores and can further integrate drug delivery, oxygen regulation, immune activation, or feedback modules [49–52]. However, when the imaging readout mainly remains in the visible/NIR-I region or relies on indirect ex vivo validation, real-time tracking in deep tumors, accurate lesion localization, and treatment-window determination are still restricted by tissue scattering, autofluorescence, and limited penetration depth [59, 60]. Therefore, the key distinction of NIR-II conjugated polymer-mediated SDT is not simply that it produces ROS more efficiently, but that it preserves the structural tunability and modular integration ability of conjugated polymers while extending the imaging readout into a deeper and lower-background optical window. Molecular and nanoengineering design

In accordance with clinical needs and the current background of nanoparticle-based cancer therapy, SDT material design for tumor treatment should focus on three major aspects. First, in vivo drug tracing and localization features should be incorporated, mainly through the construction of NIR-II optical backbones, to determine the optimal ultrasound intervention window. Second, ultrasound-triggered excited-state regulation and controllable reactive-site design should be used to enhance tumor killing. Third, material degradability and long-term biosafety should be considered. Backbone construction determines the functions of the material and whether stable and clear distribution monitoring can be achieved in deep-tissue environments. SDT-related design determines whether the system can efficiently convert ultrasound energy into biological effects [10, 29], while material degradation is directly related to in vivo safety.

From the perspective of molecular backbone design, donor–acceptor (D-A) and acceptor–donor-acceptor (A-D-A) structures remain among the most representative construction strategies. By enhancing intramolecular charge transfer, narrowing the bandgap, and promoting red-shifted absorption, these backbones help extend the absorption/emission window to the NIR-II region and provide more favorable electronic structures for excited-state formation, energy transfer, and ROS generation [43, 45]. Existing studies have shown that quinoidal backbones, strong acceptor units, and pi-bridge regulation can improve charge-separation efficiency and energy-level matching to varying degrees, thereby enhancing NIR-II absorption, photothermal/photoacoustic conversion, and ROS output. When coupled with immunomodulatory or cleavable modules, these backbones not only enhance local energy conversion but also connect with immune activation processes, ultimately improving the overall therapeutic performance of the system [68]. On the other hand, aggregation-induced absorption/emission attenuation and nonradiative dissipation repeatedly occur when conjugated materials move from the molecular state to nanoparticle platforms. To address this issue, the introduction of aggregation-induced emission (AIE) characteristics, chain-packing regulation, and aggregation-state engineering has increasingly been regarded as being as important as backbone design. By introducing bulky and planar acceptor dimers into the polymer backbone, researchers increased steric hindrance and local dihedral angles to weaken excessive intermolecular pi-pi stacking, thereby alleviating fluorescence quenching in the aggregated state. Regulation of donor–acceptor interaction strength allowed the system to maintain strong intramolecular charge transfer while achieving NIR-II absorption and aggregation-induced NIR-II emission. The optimized SP3 also showed favorable ROS generation and photothermal conversion, allowing the polymer to maintain strong NIR-II fluorescence output in the aggregated state. Organic semiconducting molecules with both NIR-II responsiveness and AIE behavior can maintain strong signal output in the aggregated state and provide a more stable signal for integrated imaging and therapy [69]. More specifically, different NIR-II conjugated backbones may contribute to SDT through different mechanism-related pathways. DPP-, BBTD-, and isoindigo-containing backbones can extend absorption into the red/NIR region and improve overlap with sonoluminescence-related emission, while their donor–acceptor electronic structures facilitate intramolecular charge transfer, excited-state separation, and subsequent ROS generation. Halogenated or heavy-atom-containing chromophores, such as BODIPY derivatives, may further promote ISC and triplet-state formation, thereby enhancing Type II 1O2 generation or Type I radical pathways. Therefore, backbone design should be evaluated together with ROS validation methods, such as ESR, SOSG, DCFH-DA, and radical scavenging assays, rather than being judged only by NIR-II fluorescence intensity or optical absorption.

In addition, degradability and clearance pathways must be considered when designing in vivo ultrasound-responsive material systems. Studies on clinical translation of nanomedicines indicate that conjugated polymers still face systematic challenges in GMP scale-up, batch-to-batch consistency, and predictability of in vivo behavior. Minor differences in particle size, surface potential, hydrophilic/hydrophobic exposure, and protein corona formation may alter liver and spleen uptake, blood circulation time, tumor penetration depth, and clearance routes, ultimately leading to marked differences in pharmacokinetics, biodistribution, and toxicological outcomes. Therefore, the relationship between critical quality attributes (CQAs) and pharmacokinetic/toxicological behavior requires continuous attention [70]. Consequently, backbone design should simultaneously consider degradable fragment introduction, control of hydrophobic exposure, and reduction of liver/spleen retention, rather than relying on later pharmacokinetic and toxicological compensation.

In view of these SDT material design requirements, pseudo-conjugated or pseudo-semiconducting polymers provide a new solution for balancing high optical performance and in vivo degradability in NIR-II conjugated polymers. Cleavable or self-immolative backbones can reduce the long-term retention risk of traditional semiconducting polymers, while incorporation of acceptor/chromophore units such as BODIPY enables coordinated regulation of ISC, ROS generation, and multimodal imaging [71–73]. Different from conventional fully pi-conjugated main chains, these systems usually introduce responsive cleavage units, such as thioketal motifs, or sacrificial bridging structures into the main chain. This design maintains NIR-II absorption/emission and energy-conversion capability while providing clearer degradation and clearance potential and promoting drug release from nanoparticles [71–75]. Although this strategy was initially developed mainly for photothermal/photodynamic therapy, its logic for optimizing backbone stability, excited-state utilization, and in vivo safety is highly relevant to SDT [73, 75]. Related studies first demonstrated that constructing cleavable pseudo-conjugated connections in polymer main chains can maintain effective NIR-II absorption without completely relying on conventional fully conjugated backbones and can significantly improve degradability and in vivo safety [74]. Subsequent work designed self-immolative degradable pseudo-semiconducting polymer nanoparticles by integrating BODIPY and other photosensitive units with ROS-responsive bonds into polymer backbones or assembly systems, achieving NIR-II fluorescence imaging, photodynamic immunotherapy, and photoactivated chemotherapy in one platform [71]. Degradable NIR-II pseudo-conjugated polymers have also been used to co-deliver TME-modulating modules, amplifying immunotherapy by alleviating hypoxia and reprogramming tumor-associated macrophages [74]. These studies provide important design concepts for subsequent SDT materials.

Nanoassembly, delivery, and translational considerations

Tumor delivery of drugs mainly depends on the EPR effect; however, the delivery process is also influenced by tumor vascular heterogeneity, interstitial barriers, immune clearance, and individual differences [76, 77]. Therefore, delivery systems must be systematically optimized in terms of size, morphology, surface chemistry, and interfacial stability to balance circulation, extravasation, penetration, and cellular uptake [76, 78]. Current preparation strategies for conjugated polymer nanoparticles have been extended to self-assembly routes [79], indicating that the nanoassembly method of NIR-II conjugated polymer systems for clinical application is a key determinant of quality control, batch consistency, and predictability of in vivo behavior.

Protein corona formation and PEGylation strategies also need to be reconsidered. Increasing evidence suggests that the protein corona is deeply involved in nanoparticle transport between blood, tissues, and cells [80, 81]. Meanwhile, although PEGylation is widely used to prolong circulation and reduce nonspecific clearance, its effects vary among tumor types. In KRAS-mutant tumor models, non-PEGylated and PEGylated lipid nanoparticles showed different in vivo behaviors; PEGylation could reduce tumor accumulation and cytotoxicity, whereas specific protein corona compositions could instead facilitate cellular uptake in certain models [80]. Therefore, for NIR-II conjugated polymer-based SDT platforms, a more rational strategy is to optimize the entire system in advance according to tumor type, administration route, and target readout mode [78, 80]. Targeting ligands should improve tumor uptake while ensuring that the drug can exert its therapeutic effect. Surface modification should therefore be considered together with the platform readout mode, such as NIR-II imaging or photoacoustic imaging, and the activation mode, such as ultrasound-window parameters [69]. This represents an important difference between NIR-II conjugated polymer-based SDT platforms and conventional nanomedicine delivery systems.

However, the key limitation to clinical advancement of nanoplatforms is whether a stable and verifiable relationship can be established among the mechanism of action, manufacturing process, CQAs, and in vivo behavior [82, 83]. FDA guidance for drug products containing nanomaterials emphasizes that such formulations are highly sensitive to processing conditions and scale-up processes; CQAs related to clinical performance should be identified as early as possible, and corresponding process-control and stability-evaluation systems should be established [83]. FDA documents on nanomedicine analysis also state that mean particle size and size distribution are among the most central regulatory variables. In existing products, approximately 80% have mean diameters below 300 nm, indicating that the particle-size window and consistency have become fundamental constraints in nanomedicine development [82]. For NIR-II conjugated polymer-based SDT platforms, quality control should include platform-specific CQAs, such as conjugated backbone composition, aggregation-state stability, NIR-II absorption/emission characteristics, ultrasound-triggered ROS efficiency, and the coupling relationship between post-assembly acoustic responsiveness and colloidal stability [83, 84]. High-frequency deficiencies observed in clinical trial materials for nanomedicines more commonly involve insufficient characterization, incomplete analytical method validation, inadequate justification of specifications, and weak evidence chains for stability and comparability [85]. For these platforms, a more rational development route is to adopt a Quality by Design (QbD) strategy at an early stage, linking particle parameters, CQAs, and in vivo validation to reduce uncertainty during later scale-up and regulatory submission [84]. In addition, SDT platforms are directly constrained by ultrasound equipment and dosimetry standards, which fundamentally distinguishes them from ordinary nanomedicines. Recent standardization reports have emphasized that if ultrasound studies lack systematic reporting of transducers, driving settings, free-field acoustic parameters, pulse sequences, and in vivo exposure estimation, experimental comparability is weakened and reproducibility for subsequent clinical translation is markedly compromised [86]. FDA guidance for ultrasound devices also emphasizes that device-performance evaluation must cover key aspects such as acoustic power predictability, beam profile, and risk assessment of accurate energy delivery to the target region [87]. For NIR-II conjugated polymer-based SDT platforms, this means that material development cannot be discussed independently from acoustic boundary conditions. The same nanoplatform may exhibit completely different ROS output, tissue exposure, and safety windows under different frequencies, duty cycles, peak negative pressures, or focal-zone volumes. Therefore, truly translatable systems should be co-designed from the outset by integrating materials, assembly methods, ultrasound parameters, and quality standards as a single system.

In summary, clinical advancement of NIR-II conjugated polymer-based SDT platforms should not focus solely on higher ROS yield, stronger NIR-II signals, or more complex functional integration. Practical application should be considered from the initial stage of material-property design, improving the in vivo adaptability of the material and then establishing an integrated evaluation system that combines imaging readouts with ultrasound activation and therapeutic efficiency. For such platforms, material selection and design, particle size, PDI, surface chemistry, polymer backbone composition, and loading strategy determine circulation stability, safety, in vivo delivery efficiency, tumor penetration, and organ distribution. NIR-II absorption/emission characteristics, fluorescence brightness, and optical/acoustic stability determine whether the platform can truly reflect in vivo drug accumulation and the therapeutic time window. Ultrasound frequency, power density, duty cycle, irradiation duration, and focal-zone distribution directly affect ROS output, tissue exposure, and safety boundaries. Future translatable NIR-II conjugated polymer-based SDT platforms should consider nanoassembly processes, CQAs, QbD strategies, in vivo pharmacokinetic/toxicological evaluation, and ultrasound dosimetry standards early in the design stage, rather than supplementing these validations only after preliminary efficacy has been obtained. Challenges facing NIR-II conjugated polymer-based SDT strategies in solid tumor therapy

Current status of NIR-II imaging-guided SDT

NIR-II conjugated polymers currently applied in SDT are improved materials developed through the evolution from traditional small-molecule sonosensitizers to inorganic semiconductor materials, and they are more compatible with in vivo therapeutic scenarios than earlier systems. NIR-II conjugated polymers provide clear imaging-guidance capability for SDT. After synthesized NIR-II polymer nanoparticles are injected into small animals, NIR-II fluorescence imaging can precisely localize tumor tissues [86]. Once drug accumulation in the tumor is confirmed, ultrasound irradiation can be applied to achieve effective killing of deep tumors. Compared with strategies relying only on an ultrasound window, NIR-II imaging can provide a visible therapeutic window in vivo and significantly improve the selectivity and operational safety of the treatment area [27, 88]. Some studies use NIR-II fluorescence to trace in vivo distribution and determine treatment timing, followed by ultrasound-triggered ROS generation and prodrug activation, ultimately inducing ICD and enhancing antitumor immunity [64]. These platforms emphasize the temporal coupling between imaging localization and sonodynamic activation, representing an important direction for precision SDT using NIR-II conjugated polymers. Multiple experimental results suggest that such platforms can achieve localization-guided SDT in deep tumors, leading to significant tumor-volume reduction and relatively low damage to normal tissues. It should be noted that the key to realizing this strategy is multifunctional module integration. In the same material system, NIR-II fluorescence signals and ultrasound responsiveness are combined. Through aggregation-state regulation, imaging and energy conversion are coupled so that nanoparticles show stable NIR-II signals in vivo and efficiently generate ROS under ultrasound irradiation. In deep-tumor settings such as orthotopic pancreatic cancer, this design has preliminarily achieved a continuous therapeutic workflow from localization to precise intervention [64].

In vivo drug tracing, tumor-cell killing, and safety are practical requirements for cancer therapy. NIR-II conjugated polymer-based SDT strategies address several long-standing problems in SDT, including unclear lesion localization, difficulty in determining the therapeutic window, dependence of ROS generation on material accumulation, and insufficient therapeutic feedback. Various animal-model studies have preliminarily demonstrated that NIR-II conjugated polymer platforms can realize imaging-guided SDT in deep, orthotopic, or solid tumor models while enhancing tumor killing and reducing normal tissue damage [42, 64, 65, 86]. The integration of NIR-II signals, conjugated polymer sonosensitizing backbones, and ultrasound-triggered therapy represents an important transition of SDT from simple local ROS-mediated killing to a locatable, activatable, and monitorable theranostic modality.

Limitation of SDT by tumor hypoxia

With increasing understanding of tumor metabolism and immune-system-related mechanisms, researchers have realized that the final efficacy of many therapeutic strategies is not determined only by the functions and design of materials or drugs. Intrinsic tumor factors, including vascular architecture, interstitial barriers, hypoxia, antioxidant systems, immunosuppressive cells, cytokine networks, and metabolic reprogramming, are common obstacles faced by many drugs. This indicates that the design of NIR-II conjugated polymer-based SDT platforms must move beyond simply improving material performance and further focus on adapting to and remodeling the tumor microenvironment.

Both traditional small-molecule sonosensitizers and organic semiconducting/conjugated polymers rely heavily on oxygen-involved ROS generation in SDT, particularly pathways associated with 1O2. This indicates that tumor killing by SDT is relatively oxygen-dependent, which creates a major hidden limitation for practical application. Solid tumors are frequently hypoxic to different degrees because of abnormal vasculature, rapid proliferation, and increased oxygen consumption. Hypoxia severely restricts ROS generation and is one of the major reasons why traditional small-molecule sonosensitizers and inorganic semiconductor materials are difficult to apply effectively in many tumor environments. It directly weakens the in vivo ROS output of traditional small molecules, inorganic semiconductor materials, and organic semiconducting/conjugated polymer platforms [42]. Organic semiconducting/conjugated polymers can improve ROS generation efficiency through their strong capacity for functional module integration; however, the tumor environment contains multiple overlapping factors, including HIF-1alpha, VEGF, EMT-related signaling, metabolic disorders, and complex vascular structures that continuously supply nutrients to tumor cells, promote oxygen uptake and consumption, and maintain closed hypoxic regions. At the same time, irregular and complex vascular environments may prevent drugs from efficiently reaching lesions through EPR-mediated processes. Cytokine accumulation and matrix infiltration also make it difficult for improved organic conjugated polymer platforms to continuously and effectively correct this condition, eventually contributing to tumor recurrence and drug resistance [4, 89]. Consequently, many improved drugs show favorable antitumor effects at the cellular level but fail to reproduce comparable efficacy in real tumor models or exhibit marked resistance and non-responsiveness during application. Huang et al. constructed an oxygen-sufficient core–shell nanoplatform, PPID-NPs, in which a perfluorocarbon core carried oxygen and the outer polymeric structure co-loaded a sonosensitizer and chemotherapeutic drug. This study showed that oxygen supplementation not only promoted sonosensitizer-mediated generation of more 1O2 under ultrasound irradiation and induced tumor cell apoptosis but also weakened hypoxia-associated resistance and enhanced chemo-SDT efficacy [66]. Hypoxia is therefore a key tumor-associated factor limiting in vivo SDT efficacy rather than a purely material-performance issue. For NIR-II conjugated polymer-based SDT platforms targeting deep solid tumors, future designs should not only pursue higher in vitro ROS yields but also incorporate oxygen-supply modules, oxygen-consumption inhibition, Type I ROS generation pathways, or hypoxia-responsive drug-release mechanisms according to the hypoxic characteristics of tumors. Overall, material design should shift from simply enhancing the sonosensitizer itself toward adapting to and remodeling the hypoxic tumor microenvironment, so that ultrasound-triggered ROS generation can be truly translated into in vivo antitumor efficacy.

Challenges imposed by the tumor microenvironment on SDT

Similar to many clinically used therapeutic drugs and modalities, SDT also faces challenges imposed by the complex TME. ROS-induced oxidative damage generated by ultrasound-activated SDT usually targets local tumors and often lacks durable tumor-control capacity. Solid tumors are not composed of a single homogeneous tumor-cell population but are dynamic ecosystems containing heterogeneous tumor cells, cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs), abnormal blood vessels, dense extracellular matrix, tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), MDSCs, Tregs, and diverse cytokines and metabolites [2, 89, 90]. These components jointly affect tumor-cell survival, local invasion, distant metastasis, and therapeutic resistance. Although many SDT platforms show good tumor-killing effects in cellular experiments or early subcutaneous tumor models, they may still be limited by TME barriers in more complex orthotopic, metastatic, or recurrent tumor models. The tumor-killing mode of SDT is relatively localized. Stromal barriers and tumor heterogeneity in the TME can markedly weaken the uniform therapeutic effect of SDT, meaning that different subpopulations within the same tumor may respond differently to SDT. Abnormal vasculature and dense ECM also limit the homogeneous intratumoral distribution of sonosensitizing nanoparticles, leaving more material around blood vessels or tumor margins. Tumor cells in different regions differ in proliferation rate, redox state, anti-apoptotic capacity, and immunogenicity [2, 89]. Therefore, even if some tumor cells are efficiently eliminated by SDT, residual tolerant cell populations may continue to survive with TME support and drive recurrence. Tumor heterogeneity has been identified as an important cause of drug resistance and treatment failure, and TME heterogeneity also influences responses among different patients, lesions, and even different regions within the same tumor [32]. Thus, SDT based solely on ROS-mediated tumor killing should also consider intratumoral delivery uniformity, the status of residual tumor cells, and recurrence risk.

SDT-induced tumor cell damage has been shown in multiple studies to trigger ICD. However, immunosuppressive cell populations within the TME can greatly restrict the immune amplification effect after SDT-induced ICD. In theory, SDT-induced ICD releases CRT, HMGB1, ATP, and tumor-associated antigens, promoting DC maturation and T cell activation. In a real immunosuppressive TME, however, this process is often counteracted by M2-like TAMs, MDSCs, and Tregs [2, 89–91]. M2-like TAMs can be recruited to tumor sites by tumor-cell-derived inflammatory factors and further secrete VEGF to promote disordered peritumoral angiogenesis, tumor invasion, and immunosuppression. They also secrete IL-10, TGF-beta, CCL2, and other factors to weaken effector T cell antitumor activity. Recruited MDSCs further suppress antitumor immunity through arginine depletion, ROS/RNS generation, PD-L1 expression, and Treg induction [90]. Tumor metabolic disorders also weaken post-SDT immune clearance. Lactate accumulation, acidic pH, adenosine enrichment, activation of the tryptophan-kynurenine pathway, and glucose competition commonly found in solid tumors collectively inhibit DC maturation, CD8+ T cell proliferation, and effector function, while promoting immunosuppressive phenotypes in TAMs and Tregs [2, 89–91]. Therefore, local tumor antigen release induced by SDT may fail to convert into systemic antitumor immunity. Clearly, practical drug application faces far more challenges than in vitro validation. At the current stage, further optimization of the material structure alone may be insufficient to overcome the challenges presented by most solid tumors. Expanding drug functions by combining the advantages of different agents to address extreme tumor environments may be a more promising direction.

Limitations associated with drug delivery and targeting

Drug delivery and targeting in solid tumors represent another key obstacle limiting in vivo SDT efficacy. After entering the body from the bloodstream, a drug must sequentially undergo blood circulation, vascular-wall crossing, tumor interstitial diffusion, cell-surface recognition, cellular uptake, and intracellular release before fully exerting its therapeutic effect. Obstruction at any step may lead to insufficient local drug concentration, deviation of ROS generation from the target region, or inaccurate determination of the therapeutic window [92]. For a long time, tumor accumulation of nanomedicines has mainly been attributed to the enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effect. However, the EPR effect is highly heterogeneous in real tumors and is influenced by tumor perfusion, vascular permeability, lymphatic drainage, tumor type, tumor size, and individual differences. Wilhelm et al. analyzed nanoparticle tumor delivery studies over the preceding decade and found that the median dose of nanoparticles delivered into solid tumors was only approximately 0.7% of the injected dose [93]. This indicates a considerable gap between the injected drug amount and the therapeutically available dose. This problem is particularly important for SDT because, even if ultrasound penetrates deep tissues, ROS cannot be generated uniformly within the tumor parenchyma if the sonosensitizer accumulates only sparsely around tumor margins or perivascular areas. Nanoparticle entry into tumors is not limited to passive extravasation through endothelial gaps. Sindhwani et al. showed that most nanoparticle entry into solid tumors may depend on active transcytosis by endothelial cells rather than simple passive leakage through vascular endothelial gaps [94]. This finding suggests that the traditional EPR-based passive targeting model cannot fully explain the actual entry of nanoparticles into tumors. Material design focused only on whether particle size is suitable for the EPR effect may therefore limit delivery performance. More complex delivery processes, including endothelial crossing, perivascular retention, tumor-cell uptake, and intracellular localization, should be considered, and biological components may need to be modified when necessary.

After entering the extravascular tumor space, drugs encounter physical barriers such as dense stroma, high interstitial fluid pressure, and solid stress. In pancreatic cancer, glioma, and highly fibrotic breast cancer, increased ECM density and tissue stiffness can restrict drug diffusion within the tumor interstitium [76, 92]. Active targeting is a potentially powerful strategy that uses antibodies, peptides, folate, hyaluronic acid, RGD, ApoE, or cell-membrane coatings to improve recognition of tumor cells or tumor vascular endothelium [93, 95]. Although these approaches can markedly enhance sensitivity, complex tumor environments are accompanied by tumor heterogeneity, and higher sensitivity may be associated with lower specificity. Therefore, balancing sensitivity and specificity is critical. Targeting strategies should first be designed according to tumor type and subtype, which also makes strong-targeting strategies more costly and time-consuming. It should also be noted that active targeting often improves cell binding and cellular uptake but does not necessarily increase the initial bulk accumulation of nanoparticles in tumor tissues. In other words, drugs must first reach the tumor region through circulation and vascular barriers before surface ligands can exert receptor-recognition effects [95]. In addition, after entering the bloodstream, drug surfaces rapidly adsorb plasma proteins to form a protein corona. The corona may alter the in vivo “biological identity” of materials, mask targeting ligands, and change cellular uptake pathways [96]. Salvati et al. demonstrated, using transferrin-functionalized nanoparticles as an example, that biomolecular corona formation can shield transferrin-receptor binding and abolish the original targeting-recognition capability [97].

In summary, drug delivery, lesion accumulation, and targeting remain long-standing and unavoidable issues for improved material delivery. Effective delivery and accumulation can reduce adverse effects, and only on this basis can imaging techniques such as NIR-II imaging be used to determine the drug activation window and maximize therapeutic efficiency. Therefore, NIR-II imaging-guided SDT should not only highlight imaging and ROS generation but also emphasize whether the material can overcome circulation, vascular, stromal, and cellular barriers to reach the effective therapeutic region. This is a key precondition for subsequent combination strategies aimed at improving delivery and targeting.

NIR-II conjugated polymer-mediated combination therapy applications

Relieving tumor hypoxia to restore oxygen-dependent ROS generation in SDT

To address restricted ROS generation caused by hypoxia in the tumor environment, recent combination strategies mainly follow three directions: exogenous oxygen delivery, in situ oxygen generation, and reduction of tumor-cell oxygen consumption. The common goal of these strategies is not simply to increase platform complexity but to improve local tumor oxygen supply so that ultrasound-triggered ROS generation can occur effectively in real tumor tissues [98, 99]. Reversing or alleviating tumor hypoxia has become one of the core directions for enhancing SDT, and oxygen supplementation, inhibition of oxygen consumption, and hypoxia-responsive mechanisms are among the most commonly used design strategies.

Introducing oxygen-carrying components can directly increase local oxygen content in tumors. Perfluorocarbon materials, which have high gas solubility, are often used to construct oxygen-storage or oxygen-transport nanoplatforms. For example, an oxygen-sufficient PPID nanoplatform was constructed, in which a perfluorocarbon core carried oxygen and was combined with a sonosensitizer and chemotherapeutic drug for chemo-SDT against hypoxic tumors. This strategy first relieved local tumor hypoxia through the oxygen-carrying component and then enhanced 1O2 generation under ultrasound irradiation, thereby increasing tumor cell apoptosis and improving the combined therapeutic effect [99]. Zhang et al. developed a CD44 receptor-targeted, oxygen-carrying, redox/ultrasound-responsive CS-Rh-PFC nanoplatform composed of chondroitin sulfate, the sonosensitizer Rhein, and perfluorocarbon. The results showed that PFC introduction significantly increased intracellular oxygen levels in B16F10 melanoma cells and improved SDT efficiency. Control nanoparticles lacking PFC produced lower ROS under ultrasound and showed weaker tumor inhibition in vitro and in vivo. After further loading docetaxel, the platform enhanced secretion of cytokines such as IFN-gamma, TNF-alpha, IL-2, and IL-6 and increased the proportions of tumor-infiltrating CD4+ and CD8+ T cells, indicating that improved tumor killing after hypoxia relief can extend to subsequent immune activation and further increase drug utilization efficiency [98].

In addition, strategies using endogenous H2O2 in tumors for in situ oxygen generation have considerable application value. Tumor tissues often contain elevated H2O2 levels, and oxygen-generating modules such as catalase, MnO2, and Pt nanozymes can convert H2O2 into O2, thereby continuously relieving hypoxia within tumors. For organic semiconducting polymer-based SDT platforms, these designs transform intrinsic tumor characteristics into therapeutic advantages and provide stronger adaptability to the TME. They may represent key solutions for overcoming poor tumor-killing effects or strong resistance under extreme tumor conditions. Wang et al. reported catalase-modified semiconducting polymer nanoparticles (SPNCs) that enhanced SDT by simultaneously improving tissue penetration and hypoxia in large tumors. The key feature of this study was the integration of size regulation, tumor penetration, and catalase-mediated oxygen production into one platform, allowing nanoparticles to distribute more deeply in large solid tumors and generate sufficient 1O2 under ultrasound irradiation, thereby improving therapeutic efficacy [47]. Such studies indicate that optimization of SDT should consider not only whether the drug can enter the tumor core but also whether sufficient oxygen is available after entry to support ROS generation.

Reducing tumor-cell oxygen consumption or converting hypoxia itself into a therapeutic trigger can also significantly improve drug adaptability within tumor environments. These methods reduce competition for oxygen by inhibiting mitochondrial respiration, regulating metabolism, or introducing hypoxia-responsive drugs. Mitochondrial respiration inhibitors can be combined with sonosensitizing nanoplatforms to reduce oxygen consumption and indirectly improve oxygen availability during SDT [100]. In other studies, hypoxia-activated prodrugs such as tirapazamine have been combined with SDT, using hypoxia as the activation condition to improve targeting and compensate for the cytotoxicity loss caused by insufficient ROS generation [100].

Overall, these strategies suggest that, once NIR-II signals are used to determine tumor accumulation and the ultrasound irradiation window, conjugated polymer backbones can provide the sonosensitizing and drug-loading basis, while oxygen supplementation, oxygen generation, or oxygen-consumption reduction modules can improve the in vivo effectiveness of ultrasound-triggered ROS generation in hypoxic TME. Such integrated therapeutic approaches may be better suited to real tumor environments. In future studies, hypoxia relief will be a key step for translating NIR-II conjugated polymer-based SDT from in vitro efficacy to reliable in vivo application.

Combining immunotherapy to improve the prognosis of SDT

With increasing understanding of cancer therapy and the continuous elucidation of tumor mechanisms, cancer treatment is no longer limited to killing tumor cells. To obtain more stable long-term therapeutic benefits, many treatment regimens now combine multiple pathways or modalities to block key processes that support tumor proliferation and immune escape (Fig. 4). Accordingly, the combination of NIR-II conjugated polymer-based sonodynamic intervention with immunotherapy has gradually emerged as one of the most representative directions in current SDT research. For example, a deep-tissue-activatable semiconducting polymer sono-immunotherapy platform, SPIND, was reported. In a Panc02 pancreatic cancer model bearing primary and distant tumors, this platform combined the IDO inhibitor NLG919 with an anti-PD-L1 antibody. Compared with SDT alone, the strategy combining an NIR-II sonosensitizing polymer platform with PD-1/PD-L1 blockade more effectively prolonged animal survival and produced stronger systemic antitumor effects in distant tumors or rechallenge models. Under combination therapy, Treg proportions in both primary and distant tumors were significantly reduced, and the proportion of splenic effector memory T cells was approximately 1.9-fold higher than that in the control group, indicating favorable systemic antitumor immunity and long-term immune memory [42]. The core mechanism underlying this effect is that SDT induces ICD and promotes antigen release, whereas immune checkpoint inhibition further alleviates immunosuppression within the TME, producing a combined effect superior to either monotherapy [42].

In addition, the synergy between NIR-II photothermal therapy and SDT has attracted considerable attention. Because some NIR-II conjugated polymers inherently possess high photothermal conversion efficiency, researchers have used this feature to construct dual photothermal-sonodynamic platforms. One reported hybrid nanoparticle, SPNH, consisted of two semiconducting polymers: PCPDTBT was responsible for photothermal/photodynamic effects, whereas PFODBT mediated sonodynamic effects. In a subcutaneous 4T1 breast tumor model, this system achieved a tumor-inhibition rate significantly higher than those of the SDT-alone and laser-alone groups and almost completely inhibited lung metastasis formation. These results suggest that this synergistic strategy facilitates tumor-tissue destruction and enhances deep-tumor therapy [101]. Other studies have used NIR-II conjugated polymers to integrate both photosensitizing and sonosensitizing functions, constructing photo-sono dual-modal therapeutic systems for subcutaneous and orthotopic tumor models. Initial PDT killing was induced by light irradiation, followed by ultrasound-triggered amplification of ROS output, which significantly improved overall antitumor efficacy [68]. Thus, NIR-II conjugated polymers may be particularly suitable as backbone materials for integrating multiple energy-responsive and therapeutic modules.

Chemotherapy combined with SDT is another important direction. Platinum-based chemotherapeutic agents can be loaded into NIR-II conjugated polymer nanostructures, followed by ultrasound-triggered ROS generation and conversion of the OXA-IV prodrug into active OXA-II, thereby integrating SDT, chemotherapy, and ICD activation in one system [64]. Shen et al. reported a THPP-Oxa(IV)-PEG covalent organic polymer that integrated a sonosensitizing porphyrin, a GSH-responsive oxaliplatin prodrug, and PEG into the same backbone. Under ultrasound irradiation, this platform generated effective ROS and released active oxaliplatin in the reductive tumor environment, further inducing ICD; its therapeutic efficacy was validated in CT26 tumor-bearing mice [102]. Additional studies have shown that when chemotherapeutic drugs themselves have ICD-inducing potential, chemo-SDT contributes more prominently to immune amplification. Zheng et al. co-encapsulated oxygen, ICG, and oxaliplatin in phase-transition core–shell nanoparticles for combined photo-sonodynamic therapy and chemotherapy. This strategy promoted calreticulin exposure and HMGB1 release and simultaneously suppressed primary and distant tumors in a bilateral syngeneic mouse ovarian cancer model, suggesting a clear ICD synergy between oxaliplatin-based drugs and ROS/sonodynamic-triggered cell death [99]. When chemotherapeutics are converted into prodrugs, locally activated, and combined with ROS-generating processes, such integrated strategies generally show higher tumor-inhibition rates and lower systemic toxicity in in vivo tumor models than single-drug groups, highlighting the advantages of local triggering and local release [64].

Immunoregulatory cells, especially MDSCs and M2-like TAMs, can also be targeted. MDSCs suppress T cell function through Arg1, ROS/RNS, PD-L1, and Treg induction, whereas M2-like TAMs commonly secrete IL-10, TGF-beta, VEGF, and CCL2, promoting immunosuppression, angiogenesis, invasion, and metastasis [90, 91]. Ding et al. constructed the SPNTi platform, which integrates a semiconducting polymer sonosensitizer, the hypoxia-activated drug TPZ, and the MDSC-targeting drug ibrutinib. After ultrasound irradiation, SPNTi produced 1O2 and triggered drug release. Because SDT can further consume oxygen and aggravate local hypoxia, TPZ was activated under hypoxic conditions to enhance ICD, while ibrutinib alleviated MDSC-mediated immunosuppression. This platform suppressed the growth of both primary and distant tumors and reduced metastasis in a bilateral tumor model [100]. This example illustrates that combination strategies targeting immunoregulatory myeloid cells can compensate for insufficient immune effects after SDT-induced ICD alone.

Overall, NIR-II conjugated polymer-mediated combination therapy platforms are evolving from simple SDT-enhancing systems into SDT-centered multimodal therapeutic systems, with the application focus shifting from local tumor killing to immune activation, microenvironmental intervention, and systemic therapy.

Improving sonosensitizer delivery and tumor targeting through combination strategies

Abnormal vasculature, heterogeneous EPR effects, dense ECM, elevated interstitial pressure, and protein corona formation in solid tumors can all restrict nanoparticle extravasation, deep tumor penetration, and target-cell uptake.

Combining matrix-remodeling drugs can reduce the obstruction of dense ECM to nanomedicines in solid tumors. Losartan, a clinically used angiotensin II receptor blocker, has been reported to inhibit collagen I production by cancer-associated fibroblasts, thereby improving the distribution and efficacy of nanotherapeutics within the tumor interstitium [103]. PEGPH20 can degrade highly accumulated hyaluronic acid in tumor tissues, reduce interstitial pressure, and reopen compressed blood vessels. In a pancreatic cancer model, Jacobetz et al. found that PEGPH20 treatment depleted hyaluronic acid and increased intratumoral delivery of doxorubicin and gemcitabine. PEGPH20 combined with gemcitabine suppressed tumor growth and prolonged survival more effectively than gemcitabine alone [104]. Preconditioning tumors by lowering ECM barriers before SDT or before administration of sonosensitizing nanomedicines may therefore achieve more pronounced efficacy than relying solely on the EPR effect. Penetration-promoting agents can also be combined. iRGD first binds to alpha-v integrins on tumor blood vessels or tumor cells and then promotes tissue penetration through the CendR/neuropilin-1 pathway. Sugahara et al. reported that coadministration of iRGD with anticancer drugs enhanced the penetration of multiple drugs and nanoparticles in tumor tissues and improved antitumor efficacy [105]. Tumor blood vessels often show disordered structure, uneven perfusion, and high permeability with low functional efficiency, leading to highly unstable drug entry into tumors. Appropriate anti-VEGF/anti-angiogenic therapy within specific dose and time windows can temporarily improve vascular structure and perfusion, thereby enhancing oxygen and drug delivery [106]. These strategies are particularly instructive for SDT. If NIR-II conjugated polymer sonosensitizers are combined with iRGD-like penetration enhancers or anti-angiogenic agents, the spatial distribution of sonosensitizers within tumor parenchyma may be effectively improved during the retention period, thereby expanding the effective ROS-killing range. When material-structure design alone cannot overcome delivery barriers imposed by tumor tissues and environments, combination strategies can be used to specifically address delivery obstacles and pave the way for improved therapeutic efficacy.

Validation in deep orthotopic and refractory tumor models

One of the greatest advantages of combining NIR-II imaging with SDT is visualized therapy for deep tumors. Therefore, increasing studies have begun to investigate clinically more relevant orthotopic and refractory tumor models, with pancreatic cancer and brain tumors serving as representative deep solid tumors. In pancreatic cancer models, NIR-II semiconducting polymer platforms with sonodynamic functions have achieved deep imaging localization and therapy in orthotopic lesions, significantly prolonging animal survival and inhibiting distant metastasis [86]. In brain tumor studies, the blood–brain barrier and brain-tissue safety remain important thresholds for evaluating nanoplatforms. Some studies have used small-sized NIR-II conjugated polymer nanosystems that enter brain tumor lesions through increased blood-tumor barrier permeability after glioma formation, receptor-mediated transcytosis, or cell-mediated delivery. These systems achieved favorable lesion localization and ultrasound therapeutic effects in glioma or related brain tumor models and inhibited tumor growth without causing obvious neurotoxicity, supporting their further development for tumors of the central nervous system [107]. Although current work remains largely at the small-animal stage, these results indicate that after transition from subcutaneous tumors to orthotopic deep tumors, NIR-II conjugated polymers can still maintain effective imaging and therapeutic capacity.

In addition to these models, many studies have focused on tumor recurrence models, distant metastasis models, and immune-cold tumor models [42, 86]. Summary and outlook

Early SDT studies mainly focused on whether sonosensitizers could efficiently generate ROS under ultrasound irradiation. With the development of organic semiconducting/conjugated polymer systems, the research focus has shifted toward practical application issues, including stable in vivo distribution, real-time tracing, precise activation within the therapeutic time window, and conversion into effective therapeutic benefit in complex tumor microenvironments. Owing to their tunable pi-conjugated backbones, stable nanoassembly, organic biocompatibility, and multifunctional integration capacity, NIR-II conjugated polymers can integrate lesion localization, drug delivery, ultrasound activation, ROS generation, drug release, immune modulation, and therapeutic feedback within a single platform. Nanoassembly, PEGylation, protein-corona regulation, and quality-by-design concepts can further improve in vivo delivery, batch consistency, and translational potential. The problems caused by hypoxia, immunosuppression, and delivery barriers in solid tumors suggest that combination strategies may help SDT overcome low therapeutic efficiency or drug resistance. However, the overall quality of evidence remains insufficient. Most current studies are still concentrated on mouse subcutaneous tumor models or short-term treatment models, with inadequate validation in orthotopic tumors, metastasis models, recurrence models, large tumors, large-animal models, and long-term survival endpoints. Ultrasound frequency, power density, duty cycle, irradiation time, dose, sonosensitizer concentration, ROS probes, and evaluation endpoints vary markedly among studies, making cross-platform comparison difficult. Although NIR-II fluorescence signals can be used for in vivo distribution tracing and treatment-window determination, fluorescence intensity does not directly represent true lesion drug concentration, accumulation capacity, ROS output, or final therapeutic efficacy. Therefore, complete evidence chains should be established by combining ex vivo tissue quantification, pharmacokinetic analysis, ESR/SOSG cross-validation, ICD markers, and immunological indices. From the materials perspective, polymer molecular weight, chain-length distribution, batch consistency of nanoassembly, degradability, liver/spleen accumulation, long-term toxicity, and ultrasound equipment/dosimetry standards remain key issues that must be addressed before clinical translation.

In practical SDT applications, several material-specific limitations of NIR-II conjugated polymers need to be clearly addressed. While NIR-II fluorescence can improve the accuracy of tumor localization and determine effective drug activation windows, tumor delivery remains limited by tumor vascular heterogeneity, dense extracellular matrix, protein crown formation, and reticuloendothelial system (RES) uptake, requiring improvements through rational nanoassembly, surface engineering, and permeability enhancement strategies. Oxygen-dependent ROS generation is inhibited by hypoxia and antioxidant defense mechanisms in the tumor environment, necessitating oxygen supply, oxygen generation, or hypoxia-responsive modules. Even if tumor cells are successfully killed, they can still thrive in complex and variable environments due to their heterogeneity, metastasis, and immunosuppressive conditions; simple killing alone cannot achieve long-term therapeutic benefits. Combining effective drugs based on tumor characteristics can overcome key obstacles in SDT. Finally, long-term biosafety requires a degradable polymer backbone, reproducible formulations, and standardized quality control.

An ideal translatable platform should not only possess stable NIR-II signals and efficient ultrasound responsiveness but also consider material composition, nanoassembly processes, critical quality attributes, in vivo pharmacokinetics/toxicology, ultrasound dosimetry, and compatibility with the TME at the early design stage. Future studies should design structures whose material properties are consistent with in vivo distribution patterns and provide reliable NIR-II imaging, while also standardizing ultrasound activation parameters and predicting or evaluating therapeutic responses related to ROS, ICD, and TME remodeling. Only when stable, reproducible, and quantifiable integrated materials are obtained can SDT move from animal-model validation toward practical clinical application. In this way, NIR-II conjugated polymer-mediated SDT may become a truly valuable theranostic strategy for precision treatment of deep solid tumors.
