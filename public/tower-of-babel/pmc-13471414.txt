The Role of F-18 FDG PET/CT in the Evaluation of Solitary Pulmonary Nodules
Authors: Erdal Cetinkaya, Fatih Guzel, Sadiye Altun Tuzcu, Ilbey Erkin Cetin, Ibrahim Ibiloglu, Esref Argin, Feray Altun Cetin, Bekir Tasdemir
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryObjective:Fluorine-18 fluorodeoxyglucose positronemissiontomography/computedtomography (F-18 FDG PET/CT) is commonly used in the evaluation of solitary pulmonary nodules (SPNs). This study aimed to investigate the predictive value of maximum standardized uptake value (SUVmax) and its ratios to liver and mediastinal blood pool SUVmax values for malignancy in SPNs, using histopathological findings as the reference standard.Methods:The study included 130 patients who underwent F-18 FDG PET/CT and had either histopathological confirmation or at least two years of radiological follow-up. Demographic data (age and sex), nodule size, location, contour characteristics, nodule SUVmax, nodule-to-liver SUVmax ratio (NLR), and nodule-to-mediastinal blood pool SUVmax ratio (NBR) were recorded. Nodules were classified as benign or malignant based on histopathological results.Results:Among the 83 patients with histopathological diagnosis, 54% (n = 44) had malignant lesions and 46% (n = 39) had benign lesions. The mean size of malignant nodules was approximately 20 mm. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis demonstrated that an SUVmax cutoff value of 4.02 provided the highest diagnostic accuracy for predicting malignancy. Both NLR and NBR showed significant diagnostic value in malignancy prediction. These parameters demonstrated high sensitivity (84–91%) and negative predictive value (76–84%), indicating their usefulness in excluding benign nodules.Conclusion:The F-18 FDG PET/CT is a valuable imaging modality in the evaluation of solitary pulmonary nodules. In addition to SUVmax, the use of NLR and NBR may improve diagnostic accuracy and contribute to clinical decision-making.

Introduction

Lung cancer remains the leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide [1]. Whereas its initial presentation as a SPN occurs in a limited number of cases, the clinical significance of these nodules is substantial. First, these are typically associated with smaller tumor sizes and the early stages of the disease. Consequently, surgical resection can yield 5-year survival rates approaching 70%. The detection rate of SPNs has significantly increased, correlating with the heightened utilization of chest CT. Furthermore, the extensive adoption of lung cancer screening initiatives globally means that healthcare professionals need to have comprehensive knowledge regarding the suitable assessment and management of these nodules [2]. An SPN is defined as a single, isolated opacity measuring less than 3 cm in diameter that is typically round. The lesion must be entirely encased in healthy lung parenchyma and should not exhibit any accompanying pathological conditions, including atelectasis, hilar lymphadenopathy, or pleural effusion [3]. The assessment of SPNs using noninvasive methods is frequently conducted utilizing imaging techniques, including CT and PET/CT [4]. Based on contemporary studies, PET/CT has been shown to provide higher specificity than CT in the differentiation of benign and malignant solitary pulmonary nodules [5].

The F-18 FDG PET/CT is widely used to assess tumor glucose metabolism. Malignant lesions generally show higher FDG uptake than benign lesions and normal tissues due to increased metabolic activity. Meta-analyses have shown that PET/CT provides high diagnostic accuracy in detecting malignant pulmonary nodules. The study revealed sensitivity and specificity values of 89% and 70%, respectively, for PET/CT [6]. Contemporary studies report that PET/CT is a reliable noninvasive imaging modality for the evaluation of pulmonary nodules [5]. Nonetheless, the SUVmax value assessed via PET/CT can be affected by many factors. Variability in FDG uptake can be influenced by factors such as the scanner hardware, patient-related factors, imaging protocols, and reconstruction algorithms [7]. Consequently, exclusive dependence on tumor SUVmax values is associated with intrinsic limitations. To mitigate these uncertainties, the normalization of tumor SUVmax values to those of surrounding tissues, including the mediastinal blood pool or the liver, has been suggested [8].

This study aimed to evaluate the diagnostic performance of F-18 FDG PET/CT in patients with SPNs, using histopathological findings as the reference standard. The evaluation focused on the correlation between quantified SUVmax values and the derived NBR and NLR values, in relation to the malignancy potential of the nodules. In addition, the effect of other parameters such as the nodule size, location and morphology on the diagnostic accuracy was assessed.

Methods

Patients with SPNs measuring 8–30 mm who underwent whole-body F-18 FDG PET/CT between June 2018 and April 2025 at Dicle University were retrospectively evaluated. Ethical approval was obtained from the Dicle University Clinical Research Ethics Committee (IRB No: 43; January 15, 2025). Patients who had either histopathological confirmation or available radiological follow-up data of at least 2 years after PET/CT imaging were included in the study. Patients without histopathological verification and those lacking sufficient follow-up data were excluded. Additionally, patients with a previously diagnosed primary malignancy were excluded to avoid potential bias. Demographic characteristics (age and sex) and nodule-related features including size, anatomical location, contour, and morphological characteristics were recorded. Nodules with histopathological examination were classified as benign or malignant.

Imaging protocol and image analysis

All patients had been initially diagnosed with SPNs based on previously acquired chest CT images. Subsequently, F-18 FDG PET/CT imaging was performed at our nuclear medicine center. Imaging was conducted using a 2016 Siemen+s Horizon PET/CT scanner (Siemens Healthcare GmbH, Erlangen, Germany) equipped with three-dimensional time-of-flight (3D-TOF) technology. The PET images were acquired with a slice thickness of 3 mm and reconstructed using iterative reconstruction algorithms (block-sequential regularized expectation maximization, bp-LOR). For attenuation correction and anatomical localization, a low-dose CT protocol (80 mA, 120 kV) was applied. Patients fasted for at least 6 hours prior to imaging, and blood glucose levels were confirmed to be below 200 mg/dL at the time of FDG injection. Image interpretation was independently performed by two experienced nuclear medicine physicians. Regions of interest (ROIs) were placed over the areas of highest metabolic activity within each nodule to obtain SUVmax values. Additionally, reference ROIs were drawn over the mediastinal blood pool and normal liver parenchyma to measure SUVmax values for normalization. Using these measurements NBR and NLR were calculated.

Pathological and follow-up assessment

Following PET/CT imaging, biopsy was primarily recommended for nodules with SUVmax values greater than 2.5 or imaging features suspicious for malignancy. These lesions were classified based on histopathological examination and considered the reference standard. For nodules with low metabolic activity on PET/CT and deemed clinically low risk, histopathological confirmation was not routinely performed. Instead, these patients were evaluated using thoracic CT follow-up. Nodules that remained stable in size or showed regression over a minimum follow-up period of two years were classified as benign. Thus, the final diagnosis was established based on either histopathological findings or long-term radiological stability/regression.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for macOS, version 30.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies (n) and percentages (%), whereas continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation and median (minimum–maximum). Due to non-normal data distribution, comparisons between groups were performed using the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables were compared using the Chi-square test or Fisher's exact test when appropriate. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was conducted to determine the diagnostic performance of parameters predictive of malignancy, and optimal cutoff values were calculated. A p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 83 patients who underwent biopsy for pulmonary nodules were analyzed. Among them, 54 (65.1%) were male and 29 (34.9%) were female. The mean age was 60 years (range: 27–89). Histopathological evaluation revealed benign lesions in 47% (n = 39) of the patients and malignant lesions in 53% (n = 44). Evaluation of nodule contour characteristics showed that approximately 55% of the nodules (n = 46) had irregular margins (Table 1).

The ROC curve analyses were performed to evaluate the discriminative ability of NBR, NLR, and nodule SUVmax for predicting malignancy. The quantitative results are presented in Table 3, and the corresponding ROC curves are shown in Fig. 1. The area under the curve (AUC) for NBR was calculated as 0.709, and the optimal cut off value was 1.50. For the other parameters, the AUC and corresponding cut-off values were 0.698 and 0.94 for NLR, and 0.714 and 4.02 for nodule SUVmax, respectively. The AUC reflects the overall discriminative performance of a diagnostic test. In the present study, NBR and nodule SUVmax demonstrated moderate discriminatory ability (AUC 0.70–0.80), whereas NLR showed a low to fair discriminatory performance (AUC 0.60–0.70) for predicting malignancy.

The demographic and clinical characteristics of these patients are summarized in Table 4. Among them, 26 (55.3%) were male and 21 (44.7%) were female. Among the 2 patients initially evaluated as benign who showed nodule growth, one patient demonstrated an increase in lesion size during subsequent follow-up; however, detailed follow-up data were unavailable. In the other patient, repeat F-18 FDG PET/CT imaging revealed an increase in SUVmax from 1.5 to 1.6, accompanied by an approximately 15% increase in lesion size.

The mean age of patients with stable nodule size was higher than that of patients with decreased nodule size.

Discussion

Assessing the risk of malignancy associated with SPNs is crucial for early diagnosis and appropriate treatment planning. The conclusive identification of suspicious nodules is typically accomplished through biopsy; however, biopsy procedures are invasive and associated with potential complications, including pneumothorax and hemorrhage. Therefore, there is a clear need for reliable, noninvasive imaging methods capable of predicting the malignant potential of SPNs. In this context, F-18 FDG PET/CT has garnered significant interest because it can be used to assess metabolic parameters, such as the SUVmax, alongside morphological features, including the shape, location, and structural characteristics. Multiple studies have established the improved diagnostic accuracy of F-18 FDG PET/CT for predicting malignancy with SPNs [9,10,11]. Accordingly, the aim of the current study was to evaluate the diagnostic performance of F-18 FDG PET/CT in differentiating benign and malignant solitary pulmonary nodules (SPNs) using semi-quantitative parameters, including SUVmax, NBR and NLR.

In the SPN assessment, an SUVmax> 2.5 based on F-18 FDG PET/CT is commonly accepted as an indicator of malignancy [12,13]. In an 88-patient series conducted by Hou et al., ROC analysis identified an optimal cutoff value of an SUVmax > 3.635 for distinguishing malignant nodules, yielding a sensitivity of 83.3% and a specificity of 62.5% [14]. Similarly, Corica et al [5] reported an optimal SUVmax cutoff value of 3.625. In the multicenter SPUTNIK study that included 355 patients, Weir-McCall et al [15] suggested that SUVmax cutoffs should vary according to the nodule size, proposing cutoff values of 1.75 for nodules < 12 mm, 2.55 for nodules measuring 12–16 mm, and 3.6 for nodules > 16 mm. In our study, the ROC analysis demonstrated that a nodule SUVmax value > 4.02 had significant discriminatory power for predicting malignancy. In the examined patient cohort, characterized by a mean nodule diameter of approximately 20 mm, this cutoff value had high diagnostic efficacy, achieving a sensitivity of 90.9% and a negative predictive value of 84.0%. The cutoff value of an SUVmax > 4.02, identified in our study, while slightly elevated, demonstrates a significant degree of alignment with those of prior studies (Table 3). The observed difference can be attributed to the relatively larger mean nodule diameter of approximately 20 mm in our cohort, variations in imaging devices and protocols, and the relatively limited sample size of our study. However, these cutoff values should be interpreted cautiously and require validation in larger, multicenter cohorts.

In patients with SPNs, increased FDG uptake in infectious and granulomatous lesions can lead to false-positive results. Moreover, SUVmax measurements can be influenced by a range of technical and biological factors, such as the specific PET/CT device used, the time elapsed after injection, variations in the imaging protocol, and the patient's glycemic condition. Therefore, whereas SUVmax is a key parameter for malignancy predictions, it may not be sufficient when considered alone. Many studies have explored the potential enhancement of malignancy prediction through assessments of the SUVmax in relation to the nodule/mediastinal blood pool or nodule/liver background. In this context a multicenter study by Evangelista et al [16] reported that the nodule SUVmax/mediastinal blood pool ratio significantly increased the specificity of F-18 FDG PET/CT. In the SPUTNIK study, Weir-McCall et al. emphasized mediastinal FDG uptake as a reliable reference, showing that mediastinal-equivalent uptake or SUVmax ≥ 1.75 in nodules < 12 mm and uptake higher than that in the mediastinum or SUVmax ≥ 3.6 in nodules > 16 mm yielded the most accurate results [15]. Our study also demonstrated that both the NLR and NBR SUVmax ratios have significant diagnostic value in predicting malignancy. The ROC analysis showed that cutoff values of NLR > 0.94 and NBR > 1.50 provided high sensitivity (84.1–90.9%), although specificity was relatively limited (53.8-59%) (Table 3). These findings suggest that these parameters are particularly useful for excluding benign nodules and may play a supportive role in clinical decision-making owing to their high negative predictive values (84% for NLR and 76.7% for NBR). However, the diagnostic performance of these parameters, as reflected by the AUC values, indicates moderate accuracy rather than high accuracy. Therefore, while PET/CT provides valuable functional information, its findings should be interpreted in conjunction with clinical and radiological data.

Age is another important factor affecting the probability of malignancy in SPN evaluations. Several studies have demonstrated that age is a significant and independent predictor of malignancy risk [12,17]. In our study, the median age of patients diagnosed with malignant SPNs, as determined based on biopsy results, was 64 years. In contrast, the median age for those with benign nodules was 58 years (Table 2). The observed difference, while statistically limited, indicates a potentially significant trend toward an elevated risk of malignancy associated with an advancing age.

The location of an SPN within the lung is a critical determinant affecting the probability of malignancy during the SPN evaluation. Several studies have suggested that upper-lobe nodules carry a higher risk of malignancy [18,19]. In our study, an analysis of the distribution of the localization between benign and malignant cases revealed that 56.8% of malignant nodules were situated in the upper lobe, in contrast to 43.6% of benign nodules found in the same region. However, this difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.323). These results indicate that whereas an upper-lobe location tends to have increased malignancy, it is insufficient as a standalone discriminatory parameter.

Among 47 patients who were radiologically followed up for 2 years without biopsy, most nodules (95.7%) remained stable or regressed. This finding supports the notion that SPNs with low SUVmax values are benign and may have long-term stability. The low mean SUVmax and low NLR and NBR values observed in this group further indicate low metabolic activity. Similarly, in a study conducted by Al-Ameri et al [20], an assessment of the malignancy risk of pulmonary nodules revealed that F-18 FDG PET/CT provides significant diagnostic value in distinguishing benign from malignant nodules. These results indicate that an assessment of F-18 FDG PET/CT findings, when integrated with clinical and radiological follow-up, may play a significant role in mitigating the need for unnecessary biopsies.

Strengths and limitations

The main strength of this study is the combined evaluation of SUVmax with NLR and NBR, which may provide additional diagnostic value beyond conventional measurements. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. The relatively small sample size and single-center design limit the generalizability of the findings, particularly regarding the proposed cut off values. In addition, the use of both histopathological results and radiological follow-up as reference standards may introduce heterogeneity in outcome assessment. Furthermore, the potential presence of verification bias should be considered when interpreting the results.

In conclusion, F-18 FDG PET/CT showed moderate diagnostic performance in the evaluation of SPNs, and the inclusion of NBR and NLR alongside SUVmax may improve the differentiation between benign and malignant lesions. Given this moderate performance, PET/CT findings should be interpreted with caution and in conjunction with clinical and radiological data. Further large-scale, multicenter studies are required to validate these results and establish standardized cut off values.
