Integrated van der Waals waveguides for all-optical nonlinear photonic circuits
Authors: Xiaowen Zhang, Xiangpeng Ou, Zhican Zhou, Han Xiao, Yuxuan Huang, Chen Liu, Yujia Zhai, Xiaofeng Liu, Xixiang Zhang, Yating Wan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAll-optical deep neural networks offer significant improvements in processing speed and energy efficiency compared with traditional electronic systems. However, achieving broadband all-optical signal transmission with ultrafast processing speed in conventional photonic waveguides remains challenging. In this work, we present an integrated photonic circuit incorporating vertically grown molybdenum disulfide (MoS2), which is also compatible with wafer-scale manufacturing. By leveraging the saturable absorption properties of MoS2, this platform enables all-optical nonlinear activation over both the telecommunication O-band and C-band, with response speeds as fast as ~ 10 ps. This broadband operability enables flexible deployment as an optical activation function for neural network inference. Using a standardized evaluation framework, we benchmarked task-level performance against mainstream digital activation functions across diverse network architectures and quantization settings. These findings highlight the potential of van der Waals materials like MoS₂ for enabling in-situ nonlinearity in all-optical neural network hardware, advancing the development of scalable photonic computing systems.Subject terms: Nonlinear optics, Integrated optics

Introduction

All-optical nonlinear processing is a promising route to scale deep neural networks beyond the bandwidth, latency, and energy constraints inherent in traditional electronic and electro-optic systems1–5. By using light as both the information carrier and the computational medium, optical neural networks (ONNs) can execute inference at photonic speeds while avoiding repeated optical-electrical conversions that increasingly dominate end-to-end system latency. However, a key challenge in implementing fully ONNs is the development of effective activation functions that operate entirely in the optical domain6–8. At present, most optical systems still rely on electronic components to achieve the necessary nonlinearity for activation functions, which introduces latency and undermines the benefits of all-optical approaches9,10.

Although various solutions, such as externally controlled or quasi-static tuning mechanisms (including thermo-optic heaters and phase-change materials) or engineered resonances in ring resonators and photonic crystal cavities, have been explored for nonlinear activation functions in free-space and on-chip configurations11–13, these methods often trade speed for tunability. Thermal mechanisms are intrinsically slow compared with the ultrafast dynamics achievable by intrinsic optical nonlinearity, while resonant structures are typically wavelength-selective and sensitive to detuning. As ONN hardware moves toward broadband operation and large-scale integration, this wavelength dependence becomes a practical bottleneck, motivating resonance-free activation functions that are robust to wavelength variations and can be implemented directly in waveguides.

Van der Waals (vdW) materials, particularly transition metal dichalcogenides (TMDs), offer an alternative pathway by providing strong intrinsic optical nonlinearities in an ultrathin, integration-friendly form14–17. Their high refractive indices and pronounced optical anisotropy can enhance light-matter interaction under tight electromagnetic confinement in integrated photonics18–22. In addition, many TMDs generally exhibit a broad transparency window (approximately 700 to 3000 nm) together with sizable nonlinear optical responses23,24. In particular, their third-order nonlinearities (χ⁽³⁾) enable ultrafast nonlinear optical operation through rapid changes in refractive index (Δn) or absorption (Δα), supporting optical switching and pulse shaping on picosecond to femtosecond timescales25,26, These attributes make TMDs conceptually well suited to serve as ultrafast, resonance-free nonlinear units. Despite these advantages, important obstacles remain before TMD nonlinearities can be translated into practical activation functions for telecom-band integrated ONNs. First, fabricating photonic circuits solely from TMDs is challenging from the perspectives of compatibility and scalability. Second, although low propagation loss supports signal fidelity, the associated weak nonlinear interaction in the telecommunication band often compromises the efficiency of optical activation. Bridging this gap requires a device architecture compatible with on-chip photonics that enhances nonlinear absorption in the O- to C-band. In this context, saturable absorption in vdW materials has been engineered through electrical gating and heterostructure design27,28, while defect-state engineering29 is particularly attractive for telecom-band photonics because it can enhance sub-bandgap absorption and remain compatible with monolithic integration.

Here, we propose and experimentally demonstrate monolithic, transfer-free MoS2 waveguides that leverage enhanced saturable absorption as a wavelength-robust all-optical activation mechanism across the telecommunication O- to C-band, with response speeds approaching ~ 10 ps. We further establish a structure–property link between edge-exposed surface defect states and the strengthened telecom-band nonlinear absorption, supported by theoretical modeling and experimental validation. As a proof-of-concept, we evaluate optical nonlinear activation capability based on MoS2 waveguides within representative neural network architectures for handwritten digit and natural image classification. Within a standardized framework, the custom MoS2-based optical activation achieves performance comparable to mainstream digital activation functions across diverse tasks, architectures, and low-bit quantization settings. These results highlight the promise of broadband, resonance-free vdW nonlinear waveguides as practical building blocks for scalable and energy-efficient photonic neural networks.

Results

Design concept of the MoS2 nonlinear activation waveguide

We utilize the intrinsic optical nonlinearity of vdW materials to construct a multifunctional photonic computing platform, employing MoS2-based waveguides that support both linear data transmission and simultaneous processing of sensed and computational data (Fig. 1a). Each layer of the network leverages MoS2’s dual functionality to enable simultaneous signal transmission and nonlinear processing directly within the waveguide. This combination streamlines data transmission and computation within a single platform, reducing the need for external electronic components.

We initially examine the light confinement and propagation within a fundamental waveguide (Fig. 1b and Supplementary Fig. 1). Photonic integrated circuits based on MoS2 waveguides require sufficient material thickness to preserve their intrinsic optoelectronic properties. Calculated electromagnetic field profiles confirm that the optical modes are tightly confined at a wavelength of 1550 nm. We further analyze the mode profiles and effective refractive indices for MoS2 with varying widths and thicknesses (Supplementary Fig. 2), observing robust optical waveguide modes even at a minimal thickness of approximately 40 nm. MoS2 exhibits a high refractive index (n > 3) in the NIR wavelength range (Fig. 1c). The high refractive index of MoS2 results in an increased propagation constant β within its waveguides, reducing the effective guided wavelength λeff = 2π/β below the free-space wavelength λ0. Consequently, enhanced optical confinement in MoS2 leads to tighter mode profiles and smaller effective mode areas compared to conventional dielectric waveguides.

Optical attenuation is another key factor affecting the performance. While extensive research has focused on light-exciton polaritons22,30,31, the sub-bandgap optical losses in TMDs have been less explored. Our study shows that MoS2 exhibits a relatively low baseline extinction coefficient within the NIR range (1000–1600 nm, Fig. 1c), indicating that the intrinsic band-to-band absorption is weak in the telecom window. While this allows for signal transmission, it limits the efficacy of nonlinear optical response in the NIR region. Although pristine MoS2 exhibits prominent saturable absorption in the visible spectrum, its large bandgap ( ~ 1.8 eV for monolayer and 1.2 eV for bulk) limits intrinsic optical absorption at telecom wavelengths, thereby reducing both the modulation depth and nonlinear efficiency.

Promising strategies to address these issues include increasing sulfur vacancies and enhancing edge states, both of which effectively narrow the bandgap and improve nonlinear optical susceptibility29,32,33. In nanostructured MoS2, defects and edge states significantly alter the absorption spectrum. For example, liquid-phase exfoliated MoS2 nanosheets exhibit increased sub-bandgap absorption compared to pristine MoS2, primarily due to defect energy levels introduced by exposed edges34. We investigated the impact of S vacancies and edge states on the band structure of MoS2 through density functional theory (DFT) calculations. Our findings indicate that sulfur vacancies form more readily at edge states, with a formation energy ΔEf of ∼4.72 eV, whereas the ΔEf in the interlayer of bulk MoS2 is ∼7.44 eV (Supplementary Fig. 3). The lower formation energy at the edges leads to a higher concentration of defects, introducing localized states within the bandgap and facilitating sub-bandgap optical transitions (Fig. 1d). This is consistent with our experimental observations, where few sulfur vacancies are noted in the central regions of MoS2 (Supplementary Fig. 4).

Moreover, previous studies have shown that the significant optical absorption differences between interior and edge sites of MoS2 are primarily attributed to exposed edges35. Our DFT calculations of the electronic band structure and total density of states (DOS) for edge-exposed MoS2 reveal that edge states intersect the Fermi level, exhibiting semi-metallic characteristics (Fig. 1e and Supplementary Fig. 5). The total DOS for the edge shows a significant sub-bandgap distribution compared to the interior DOS, resulting in free-carrier-induced saturation absorption behavior like that observed in plasmonic nanoparticles36,37.

Experimental characterizations of the MoS2 waveguide

The edge states of MoS2 significantly enhance its absorption characteristics due to increased exposure of unsaturated atoms. This alignment facilitates stronger light-matter interactions, which are particularly promising to enhance the nonlinear optical responses of MoS2, making it beneficial for photonic waveguides operating in the IR telecommunication bands. To maximize the exposure of these edge states, we carefully designed the sulfurization process, optimizing the growth rate and thickness to promote vertical orientation and thereby increase the number of exposed edge states. Further control of the edge state densities and crystallinity of MoS2 was realized with post-thermal annealing treatment (Fig. 2a).

Before probing the optical properties of the MoS2 waveguides, we characterized the structural integrity and quality of our MoS2 sample after sulfidation. Scanning transmission electron microscopy (STEM) and energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) techniques confirmed the complete conversion of Mo structures into MoS2, with uniform distributions of Mo and S elements throughout the waveguide (Fig. 2b–d and Supplementary Fig. 6). High-resolution TEM (HRTEM) images exhibit the polycrystalline nature and vertical growth orientation of our MoS2 samples, with gaps of approximately 0.62 nm between neighboring layers, which is consistent with MoS2 interlayer distances. Further morphological and structural analyses were conducted using atomic force microscopy (AFM) combined with Raman spectroscopy to evaluate the surface topology and uniformity of the as-grown MoS2 waveguide. Raman mapping and line-scan measurements performed on the MoS2 waveguide (Fig. 2e, f and Supplementary Fig. 7) consistently exhibit the characteristic E2g1 and A1g vibrational modes of 2H-MoS238. Minimal variation across the measured locations supports consistent crystal quality. Additional multi-location Raman mapping further supports spatial uniformity (Supplementary Fig. 8). Height profiles showed an increase from ~ 20 nm for original Mo films to ~ 45 nm with an average roughness of 2.6 nm after sulfidation, reflecting successful conversion to smooth MoS₂ (Fig. 2g, h). After SiO2 overcladding, the outer surface roughness decreased to ~ 1.1 nm, and the guided mode is weakly influenced by the top interface (Supplementary Figs. 9, 10). The fundamental linear propagation losses of the fabricated MoS2 waveguides were measured using the standard cut-back technique39. For the straight waveguides, the propagation losses (α) at 1550 nm and 1310 nm were determined to be 18.6 ± 0.6 and 19.9 ± 0.5 dB mm–1, respectively (see Supplementary Fig. 11 and Supplementary Table S1 for details). Based on α, we estimate a propagation-related insertion loss of ~ 1.9–9.5 dB over a 100–500 μm guided interaction length. Benchmarking against other compact functional photonic platforms is summarized in Supplementary Table S2.

We further investigated the sulfurization process and its effect on growth dynamics to understand the mechanism governing the orientation of MoS2 films. Unlike typical CVD processes that form MoS2 by gas-phase reactions, our approach involves the direct sulfurization of dense Mo films (Supplementary Fig. 12). This conversion-based growth is significantly influenced by the initial Mo film density, thickness and the heating rate during sulfurization. During sulfurization, the Mo seed layer undergoes substantial volume expansion due to the formation of Mo–S bonds as sulfur atoms incorporate into the lattice. This transformation from the body-centered cubic (bcc) structure of elemental Mo to the hexagonal structure of MoS2 involves substantial changes in bonding and atomic arrangement. Both structures exhibit a similar lattice constant and comparable bond lengths between Mo−Mo and Mo−S bonds (Supplementary Fig. 13). In contrast, the unit cell of MoS2 contains one Mo atom, whereas the bcc unit cell of Mo contains two Mo atoms, indicating an effective increase in total volume upon sulfurization. This volumetric expansion creates strain that influences growth orientation. In discontinuous seed layers, strain relaxes through lateral expansion, making horizontal growth energetically favorable. This preference arises because horizontally grown films expose low-energy basal planes, minimizing surface energy and strain. Conversely, continuous and thicker seed layers are constrained by the substrate, inhibiting lateral expansion and leading to the accumulation of in-plane strain. To alleviate this strain, the growth mechanism shifts to favor vertical alignment, allowing the film to expand perpendicularly to the substrate. The increased surface energy will be compensated by the release of strain energy and thus stabilize more exposed high-energy edge sites in vertical growth. As a result, the balance between managing strain energy and surface energy dictates the orientation of MoS2 layers in thicker films. Moreover, the diffusion process controlled by temperature and sulfur supply also influences growth behavior, with vertical alignment favored when diffusion along the vdW gaps is faster than across layers. A slower ramping rate allows for more uniform sulfur diffusion and reduces the formation of a transient horizontal MoS2 capping layer38. This facilitates the preferential migration of sulfur along vdW gaps and promotes immediate edge nucleation (Supplementary Fig. 14). Optimizing the Mo seed thickness and sulfurization conditions favors vertical alignment of MoS2 layers. The enhanced exposure of edge sites is crucial for maximizing the nonlinear optical properties of MoS2 waveguides. This edge exposure can be quantitatively tracked by the Raman A1g/E2g1 ratio and shows a consistent correlation with the extracted nonlinear metrics across representative samples (Supplementary Fig. 23 and Supplementary Table S3).

Optical nonlinearity of the MoS2 waveguide

Building on this optimized vertical morphology, which maximizes edge-state density, we characterized the nonlinear optical response of the fabricated waveguide. Transmission measurements were performed in the key telecom bands (1310 nm, O-band, and 1550 nm, C-band) using an edge-coupled on-chip setup (Fig. 3a). These specific bands were selected due to their widespread use in integrated photonic circuits and data-center interconnections40. For these measurements, the device comprises a 2.3 mm-long MoS2 waveguide with a cross-section of approximately 45 nm in thickness and 4 µm in width (Supplementary Fig. 15). The geometry was chosen to balance propagation loss and optical confinement factor (Γ rises from 0.22 to 0.48 between 45 nm and 90 nm; Supplementary Figs. 16–19). For instance, the rougher edges in thicker waveguides exhibit higher scattering loss. We recorded the transmitted spectrum at varying input powers (Fig. 3b), then plotted the normalized transmittance as a function of input power (Fig. 3c). A standard nonlinear fit to this curve clearly revealed the saturation absorption characteristics of the MoS2 waveguides. Repeated measurements over multiple cycles confirm stability of the nonlinear transfer characteristics (Supplementary Fig. 20).

To characterize the intrinsic nonlinear optical response, we fabricated uniform film samples on glass substrates and evaluated their absorption properties under free-space illumination. The incident laser beam was focused onto the sample, and the transmitted signal was collected using open-aperture Z-scan measurement (see Supplementary Fig. 21). We evaluated the optical performance of the as-grown MoS2 sample across multiple wavelength bands from O-band to C-band (Fig. 3d, e and Supplementary Fig. 22). The nonlinear transmittance responses and corresponding fitting curves obtained for these wavelengths confirm the broadband saturable absorption capabilities of our samples. In comparison, the waveguide configuration is far more sensitive and efficient at driving nonlinear processes, and enables substantial nonlinear absorption at input powers lower than those needed in free space due to the combination of tight optical confinement and longer light-matter interaction length (Supplementary Fig. 21).

Time-resolved pump-probe spectroscopy (515 nm pump, 1100–1600 nm probe) was employed to reveal ultrafast carrier dynamics (Supplementary Fig. 24). We observed initial bleaching (Pauli blocking) within a few picoseconds, followed by recovery into enhanced absorption as carriers become trapped at edge-related defect states42. Comparisons of edge-exposed MoS2 (e-MoS2) and pristine MoS2 (p-MoS2) in both linear absorption spectra and transient dynamics confirm the presence of these sub-bandgap states. Samples with higher densities of exposed edges exhibit increased NIR absorption and extended carrier lifetimes (Fig. 3f, g and Supplementary Fig. 25). Notably, e-MoS2 displays a slower absorption decay, attributed to defect-assisted carrier recombination43,44. This extended relaxation persists across the telecom window, indicating a broad defect-level distribution (Fig. 3h). Furthermore, we measured a fast carrier relaxation component of ~10.34 ps (Supplementary Fig. 26), which is a primary factor determining the operation speed of the device45. This demonstrates the potential of our proposed MoS2 configuration to be operated as an ultrafast all-optical nonlinear activation unit.

Fundamentally, the observed nonlinear response at these sub-bandgap photon energies ( ~ 0.8–0.95 eV) is primarily enabled by the edge sites of the MoS2. While the basal planes of MoS2 are transparent due to robust in-plane bonding, the edges introduce a high density of exposed atoms that create an electronically active environment46. These edges provide free carriers and generate localized energy levels within or near the bandgap region, serving as sub-bandgap absorption channels and carrier relaxation sites. The combination of free carriers from edge states and continuous sub-bandgap channels amplifies the nonlinear optical susceptibility of MoS2 by modifying its electronic structure (Fig. 3i). By judiciously controlling defect densities and edge conditions, it is possible to tailor the spectral and temporal characteristics of MoS2 for specific photonic applications. This approach enables flexible nonlinear functionality tailored to the operating wavelength of the device without modifying its physical structure, making it highly adaptable for a variety of optical applications. Such control over defect and edge-state density also suggests a possible monolithic hybrid architecture, in which lower-defect guiding sections are interfaced with edge-rich nonlinear sections. Simulations of the p-MoS2/e-MoS2 junction suggest low additional interfacial loss and similar mode profiles across the interface (see Supplementary Fig. 27 for details).

Evaluating optical activation from MoS2 waveguide

To minimize latency in broadband ONNs, activation functions must be implemented in-situ. The inherent saturable absorption effect of MoS2 naturally enables such nonlinear operations directly in the optical domain, thereby reducing latency and power consumption while making it well-suited for integrated photonic neural networks (see Supplementary Table S2). To validate the effectiveness of our MoS2 waveguide-based activation function, we employed a standardized evaluation framework in which candidate nonlinearities are directly integrated into established neural network models and benchmarked through end-to-end training and inference (Fig. 4a). In this framework, the power-dependent transmission (T-P) curve of the fabricated waveguide was experimentally measured, fitted to obtain an analytical activation function fMoS₂(x), and implemented as a custom nonlinear layer. This layer was incorporated into representative convolutional architectures covering different depths and connectivity patterns (e.g., VGG-type models with uniform depth and ResNet-type models with residual connections) and evaluated on widely used image classification datasets (e.g., MNIST for handwritten digits, CIFAR-10 for natural images), enabling a realistic assessment of accuracy, convergence, and quantization robustness (Supplementary Fig. 28). Measurements of saturable absorption behaviors in the MoS2 waveguides, as shown in Fig. 4b, are fitted using a standard Michaelis-Menten-type saturating function. The resulting activation curve was then integrated into the network evaluation, where we conducted a comparative analysis with mainstream nonlinear functions, including ReLU, Tanh, and ELU, covering commonly used activation types, such as piecewise-linear, bounded-saturating, and unbounded-smooth, and found that the MoS2-based activation achieved comparable convergence speeds and classification accuracy on the MNIST dataset (Fig. 4c). Figure 4d further presents the confusion matrix from the generalization test on MNIST, summarizing correct and incorrect predictions for each class, and shows that the hardware activation incurs less than ~0.5% accuracy degradation compared to the software implementation on unseen data. The extended comparison is summarized in Fig. 4e, using the natural-image CIFAR-10 dataset to assess performance across different network architectures and quantization bit-widths under realistic deployment settings. Across all settings, our MoS2-based activation incurs less than 1% accuracy loss across different network architectures, demonstrating robustness to model choice and quantization (see Supplementary Figs. 29 and 30). In addition, consistent classification performance is achieved when activation functions extracted from the same device at O-band and C-band are applied under identical network configurations, indicating broadband operability of the proposed activation unit (Supplementary Fig. 31). Details of model construction and training are provided in the Supplementary Note 2. Hardware-relevant metrics derived from the measured device (footprint, required optical power, energy per activation, thermal budget, and activation SNR) are summarized in Supplementary Note 3.

Discussion

In conclusion, we introduce ultra-compact vdW nonlinear activation waveguides that capitalize on the distinct properties of vertically grown MoS2 for all-optical deep neural networks. This integration effectively addresses the challenges inherent in achieving simultaneous multiband all-optical signal transmission and processing at ultrafast speeds. By leveraging the saturable absorption characteristics and exposed edges of MoS2, we not only enhance the nonlinear optical performance but also expand the operational wavelength range, enabling highly efficient nonlinear activation capability. Our findings demonstrate that the customized activation function applied within these MoS2-based waveguides yields less than 1% performance loss across diverse network structures and under conditions of low-bit precision. The demonstrated capabilities of these vdW nonlinear waveguides pave the way for future exploration into scalable photonic computing technologies, which show potential for enhanced energy efficiency and faster processing capabilities for complex computational tasks.

Methods

Growth and fabrication of MoS2 waveguide

Silicon wafers featuring a 2 µm-thick SiO2 layer grown by thermal oxidation served as the substrates for MoS2 device fabrication. This SiO2 layer is sufficiently thick to prevent substrate optical leakage. After spin-coating AZ 1505 photoresist, photonic structures were patterned using a direct-write laser lithography system (DWL66, Heidelberg). Subsequently, Mo films of specific thickness were deposited onto the Si/SiO2 substrates at room temperature using a Singulus ROTARIS magnetron sputtering system. After removing the residual photoresist, MoS2 films were synthesized by sulfurizing the Mo film at low pressure using a tube furnace equipped with mass flow controllers and a vacuum pump. The substrates were placed on an alumina boat containing S powder, placed at different heating zones. After evacuating the quartz tube to <10 Pa, Ar gas was introduced at 100 sccm. The furnace temperature was ramped at 5 °C/min to 750 °C and maintained for an additional 30 min. The devices are electrode-free; the patterned Mo precursor is directly converted into MoS2 during sulfurization. Following the reaction, the furnace was cooled down to room temperature, and a 220 nm SiO2 cladding layer was subsequently deposited onto the surface by plasma-enhanced chemical vapor deposition (PECVD).

Characterization

(i) Microscopy measurements. The topography was acquired by the AFM (Asylum Research MFP-3D) with the AC in Air mode at room temperature. AFM measurements were performed by tapping mode with the Si tips (spring constant, 3 N m-1; resonance frequency, 75 kHz) and driven with 0.8 V AC voltage. High-resolution STEM images and EDS mapping were obtained using FEI Titan Themis G2 at an acceleration voltage of 300 kV.

(ii) Optical measurements. Optical images were acquired with an Mshot MSX10 microscope. Raman spectra were obtained on a WITec-Alpha300R Raman system using a 532 nm laser.

(iii) Nonlinear optical measurements. Open-aperture Z-scan measurement was performed by using a Spectra-Physics Spirit laser operating from 1030 to 1600 nm, with a pulse duration of 230 fs and a pulse repetition rate of 10 kHz (Light Conversion, UAB). For the measurement of the MoS2 waveguide, a tunable laser was edge-coupled into the sample with a single-mode fiber. The incident power was monitored simultaneously on a multi-channel optical power meter (N7742C, Keysight) using the 10% port of a 9:1 beam splitter as a reference.

(iv) Carrier dynamics measurements. Transient absorption (TA) spectra were recorded using an fs pump-probe system (Time-Tech Spectra, Dalian, China). A white continuum produced by irradiating a sapphire crystal with the fs laser was used as probe light, and pump light was obtained from a light parametric amplifier. All measurements were performed at room temperature.

Details for algorithm verification

All experiments were implemented and executed on the supercomputing cluster utilizing NVIDIA Tesla V100 GPUs (16 GB memory per GPU). The code was written in Python 3.8 using the PyTorch 2.0.0 deep learning framework with CUDA 11.8. The initial learning rate was set to 0.01 and gradually decreased as training progressed, while the weight decay was set to 10-5. The SGD optimizer with a momentum of 0.9 was used. For the MNIST and CIFAR-10 datasets, the number of training epochs was set to 15 and 250, respectively, with a batch size of 128. For activation functions like ReLU and ELU, Kaiming initialization was employed, while Xavier initialization was used for Tanh and custom activation functions. The low-bit hardware robustness testing was conducted using static 8-bit quantization.
