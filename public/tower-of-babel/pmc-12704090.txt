Acute effects of a single session of multitarget continuous Theta-Burst stimulation for essential Tremor: A Randomized, Sham-Controlled trial
Authors: Anling Luo, Hongyan Huang, Jiaming Feng, Muke Zhou, Ling Liu, Yanming Xu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Multitarget cTBS over bilateral M1 and cerebellum was safe and well-tolerated.•A single session of multitarget cTBS yielded significant acute anti-tremor effects.•Reduced left-hemisphere intracortical facilitation correlated with tremor relief.Keywords: Essential tremor, RCT, Transcranial magnetic stimulation, Continuous theta-burst stimulation, cTBS

Introduction

Essential tremor (ET) is one of the most common movement disorders among adults, with a prevalence of 0.9 % in the general population. It is primarily characterized by kinetic and postural tremors affecting the limbs, voice, chin, or neck (Louis and Ferreira 2010). Tremor severity usually increases with age, resulting in social embarrassment, functional disability, and reduced quality of life (Zesiewicz et al., 2011). Although pharmacotherapy is the mainstream recommendation for tremor improvement, approximately 25 % to 55 % of ET patients respond poorly to treatment, either due to intolerable side effects or incomplete response to anti-tremor therapy (Zesiewicz et al., 2013, Louis, 2016). While deep brain stimulation (DBS) and other conventional surgical treatments, such as thalamotomy, have proven effective, many patients are hesitant to undergo what they perceive as major surgery (Hedera 2017). Consequently, there is an urgent need for new therapies.

The pathophysiology of ET may involve abnormalities at both cerebral and cerebellar levels. Cortical alterations are supported by MRI evidence of cerebral gray matter volume losses (Cameron et al., 2018), while cerebellar involvement includes functional overactivation (Hanajima et al., 2016) and structural pathology like Purkinje cell loss and axonal swellings (“torpedoes”) (Axelrad et al., 2008, Louis et al., 2011b). Clinically, cerebellar dysfunction manifests as limb kinetic tremor, gait impairment, and oculomotor abnormalities (Deuschl et al., 2000, Rao et al., 2011, Wojcik-Pedziwiatr et al., 2016). Notably, tremor improvement or even resolution has been observed following strokes affecting cerebral or cerebellar regions (Dupuis et al., 1989, Kim et al., 2006). Pathological oscillations within the cerebello-thalamo-cortical (CTC) network have been implicated in ET (Helmich et al., 2013, Steina et al., 2025), identifying both cerebral cortex and cerebellum as plausible therapeutic targets.

Continuous theta-burst stimulation (cTBS), a variant of transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS), induces lasting after-effects, with effects such as cerebellocortical inhibition (CBI) persisting for tens of minutes (Huang et al., 2005, Popa et al., 2010, Hu et al., 2017). We chose cTBS over an excitatory protocol such as intermittent theta-burst stimulation (iTBS) based on the current understanding of ET pathophysiology, which suggests the cerebellar overactivation (Hanajima et al., 2016) and pathological oscillations in the CTC network (Helmich et al., 2013, Steina et al., 2025). Functional imaging studies have shown cerebellar overactivity and abnormal activation patterns in brain regions including the primary motor cortex (M1), premotor cortex, thalamus, and cerebellum in ET patients (Schnitzler et al., 2009). Given this evidence of pathological hyperactivation, we hypothesized that suppressing this activity would yield therapeutic benefits. As a non-invasive modulator of cortical excitability and synaptic plasticity, cTBS may represent a promising approach for targeting the hyperactive oscillatory network in ET, offering a potential low-cost therapeutic strategy. Previous studies applying cTBS to the primary motor cortex (M1) or premotor cortex (PM) reported subclinical reductions in tremor amplitude (Hellriegel et al., 2012, Chuang et al., 2014). In an open label trial, cerebellar repetitive TMS (rTMS) demonstrated prolonged clinical benefits in tremor improvement and re-establishment of the CTC network (Popa et al., 2013). However, these studies were limited to single-target stimulation, lacking a multi-target approach capable of modulating the distributed neural circuitry of ET.

Therefore, this study was designed to evaluate the safety and acute effects of a single session of multitarget cTBS applied to bilateral M1 and cerebellum in ET patients. We hypothesized that this intervention would produce short-term tremor reduction. In addition, hemodynamic and neurophysiological changes were assessed using functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS) and TMS, respectively, to explore potential treatment mechanisms.

Methods

Participants

This randomized double-blind, sham-controlled trial was conducted at West China Hospital, Sichuan University, from September 1, 2022 to September 31, 2024. The diagnosis of ET was established based on the Movement Disorders Consensus Criteria (Bhatia et al., 2018). Late-onset tremor was defined as tremor onset occurring after the age of 45 years (Hopfner et al., 2016). Individuals were eligible if they were at least 18 years old, right-handed, and willing to participate in the trial. Exclusion criteria included: (1) trauma within 3 months prior to tremor onset that clearly led to neurological dysfunction, such as limb tremors; (2) neurological disorders other than ET; (3) tremor induced by drug or alcohol withdrawal; (4) psychogenic, physiological, orthostatic, or task-specific tremor; (5) contraindications to magnetic stimulation, such as a history of seizures, intracranial metal implants, or pacemakers; (6) skin lesions at stimulation sites; and (7) pregnancy or breastfeeding. Participants continued their anti-tremor medication if it had been started more than one month prior to randomization. Additionally, participants were instructed to avoid beverages (such as coffee and alcohol) that might affect tremor assessment for at least 24 h prior to intervention.

All subjects provided written informed consent prior to the intervention. The study was approved by the local ethics committee of West China Hospital of Sichuan University and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. This randomized controlled trial adhered to the CONSORT guidelines for behavioral RCTs (Schulz et al., 2010) and was registered with the Chinese Clinical Trials Registry (24/09/2022, No. ChiCTR2200064037).

Randomization and blinding

A randomization sequence corresponding to treatment allocation was centrally computer-generated and administered to participants, who were randomly assigned in a 1:1 ratio to cTBS or control arms. Treatment allocation was concealed from participants and outcome assessors. To achieve this, personnel involved in machine operation and treatment allocation did not participate in data analysis or patient contact.

Procedures

Before randomization, all participants were evaluated using a specially designed case report form in a controlled, quiet environment. Two pre-specified neurologists conducted face-to-face interviews to collect demographic information, baseline characteristics, and tremor assessments. Following enrollment, participants received a single session of either active cTBS or sham stimulation.

The primary outcome was the tremor assessment using the Fahn-Tolosa-Marin Tremor Rating Scale (FTM-TRS), including parts A, B, C, and total scores. To capture the short-term effects, these assessments were conducted at baseline, and at 0, 10, and 45 min post-intervention (Stacy et al., 2007). Secondary outcomes included detailed FTM-TRS subscores, the Clinical Global Impression-Improvement (CGI-I) scores post-intervention (Busner and Targum 2007), hemodynamic changes measured by fNIRS before and immediately after the intervention, and neurophysiological changes measured by TMS before and after the intervention.

The cTBS and sham treatment

The single treatment session of cTBS was performed with a CCY-I Magnetic Stimulator (YIRUIDE Medical Co., Wuhan, China) using an air-cooled, figure-of-eight 70 mm coil. The stimulation sites during TMS sessions were the bilateral M1 and bilateral cerebellum. Real cerebellar cTBS was delivered to lobule VIII of the cerebellar hemispheres, with the coil placed 3 cm lateral and 1 cm inferior to the inion (Koch et al., 2008). We targeted the hemispheres based on the established somatotopic organization of the cerebellum, which locates limb function to the hemispheres rather than the vermis (Louis et al., 2003). This rationale is further supported by functional imaging evidence showing cerebellar hemispheric overactivity in ET patients (Hanajima et al., 2016), as well as clinical observations of tremor improvement following hemispheric lesions (Dupuis et al., 2010). Additionally, other cerebellar symptoms in ET, such as gait abnormalities and oculomotor deficits, also suggest involvement of the cerebellar hemispheres (Rao et al., 2011, Wojcik-Pedziwiatr et al., 2016). The cTBS protocol consisted of three-pulse bursts at 50 Hz repeated every 200 ms. A total of 600 pulses, lasting about 40 s, were applied, resulting in a sustained reduction in corticospinal excitability that persisted for 45 min post-stimulation (Huang et al., 2005). It was delivered at an intensity corresponding to 80 % of the active motor threshold (AMT), the minimum stimulation intensity that evokes motor-evoked potentials (MEPs) of approximately 200 μV in amplitude in tonically contracted muscles (Popa et al., 2010, Hoffland et al., 2012).

The control group received sham stimulation to mimic the sensation induced by real cTBS. Sham stimulation was performed using an inactive coil without actual brain stimulation, thereby preventing neurophysiological changes in CTC connections. A small device is affixed to the coil, generating 300-millisecond low-intensity electrical stimulation (up to 2 mA). It can stimulate the sound of cTBS, and deliver the stimulation via 10-millimeter gold disc electrodes (one cathode and one anode) to induce an electrical sensation on the scalp.

In each treatment session, cTBS or sham stimulation was first delivered to the bilateral M1 (right side first, then left side, with an interval of less than 5 min), followed by stimulation of the bilateral cerebellum (right side first, then left side) after a 5-minute interval (Olfati et al., 2020).

Adverse events were recorded before, during, and after the stimulation session (reported by patients, relatives, nursing workers, or medical staff).

Behavioral assessment

The primary outcome was tremor assessment, measured using the FTM-TRS (Stacy et al., 2007). It consists of three parts to evaluate tremor severity: Part A assesses tremor severity, Part B focuses on specific writing and drawing tasks, and Part C evaluates functional disability. Each item is scored on a 5-point scale (0 to 4), representing different degrees of tremor in the patient. The total score of FTM-TRS ranges from 0 to 160, with higher scores indicating more severe tremor.

The secondary outcome measure were detailed subscores of FTM-TRS (including midline tremor (MT) and limbs kinetic tremor (LKT)) and the CGI-I rating after stimulation (Busner and Targum 2007). From the FTM-TRS, we can derive the MT scores (the sum of scores involving the neck, voice, face, and/or trunk in part A), the upper limbs kinetic tremor (ULKT) scores (calculated by adding the kinetic and intention tremor scores from questions 5 and 6 of part A to the total score in part B), the lower limbs kinetic tremor (LLKT) scores (calculated by adding the kinetic and intention tremor scores from questions 8 and 9 of part A), and the all limbs kinetic tremor (ALKT) scores (the sum of kinetic tremor scores for both upper and lower limbs). The CGI-I rating scale comprises three parts: severity of illness (SI), global improvement (GI), and efficacy index (EI), which are used to evaluate the clinical efficacy of treatment. The SI is compared with other patients in the same study to assess the current condition of the subjects. The GI assesses whether there is any change in the current condition of the evaluated patients relative to their baseline state prior to the study. Both SI and GI are scored on an 8-point scale from 0 to 7. The efficacy index evaluates both the efficacy and side effects of the treatment, ranging from 0 to 4.

Tremor taskas and fNIRS measurement

The fNIRS data were acquired during tremor tasks, measured both at baseline and again immediately after the intervention. Tremor-task fNIRS measurements were collected for 6 min, with participants asked to perform tremor tasks. The task procedure was designed to induce upper limb tremors to facilitate the detection of changes in the concentration of oxy-hemoglobin (oxy-Hb). Each trial included a 30-second pre-task rest period, a 270-second task period (each task lasting 30 s with 30-second intervals, for a total of five tasks), and a 60-second rest period following the last task (Fig. 1a). Throughout the process, participants were instructed to keep their heads as still as possible and avoid torso movements. During the rest period, participants sat in a chair, keeping their body relaxed without moving their heads. During the task period, individuals were instructed to complete the following five tasks: straighten both upper limbs forward (task 1); bend the elbow joint, lift both upper limbs horizontally, and maintain both palms in front of the chest (task 2); perform the finger-to-nose test with the right hand (task 3); draw the Archimedean spiral on paper with the left hand (task 4); draw the Archimedean spiral on paper with the right hand (task 5). All participants were trained before formal testing, followed by a practice session to ensure that they fully understood the procedure. A dedicated investigator supervised the entire process to ensure that all participants were fully involved.

Tremor tasks procedure during functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS) measures (a). Location of probes and channel settings in 37-channel near-infrared spectroscopy (b).

Dynamic fluctuations in oxy-Hb concentrations in the pre-motor and supplementary motor cortices were recorded using a 37-channel fNIRS system (BS-3000, Wuhan Union Medical Technology Co., Wuhan, China) equipped with 12 emitters and 12 detectors. The distance between emitter and detector was 30 mm for contiguous optodes with two wavelengths (695 and 830 nm) of infrared light. A channel refers to the measurement area between a detector and source probe pair. The sampling rate of the device was set to 20 Hz. Channel 18 was placed at Cz according to the International 10–20 System of electroencephalogram electrode placement. Channels 1 to 37 were symmetrically distributed in the left and right hemispheres of the brain (Okamoto et al., 2004). The arrangement of channels, near-infrared emitters, and detectors, as well as the channel groupings, is illustrated in Fig. 1b.

TMS and Electromyographic measurement

Electromyography (EMG) of the first dorsal interosseous (FDI) muscle was recorded using a belly-tendon montage. Signals were amplified, filtered, and digitized (bandpass set at 20 ∼ 1000 Hz, scanning time of 30 ms/div) by the NS-5000 paired pulse transcranial magnetic stimulator from Wuhan Yiruide Medical Co., Ltd. in China. Electrophysiological measures included resting motor threshold (RMT), short-interval intracortical inhibition (SICI) with a stimulation interval of 2 ms (ms), intracortical facilitation (ICF) with a stimulation interval of 12 ms, and cortical silent period (CSP).

RMT was defined as the minimum stimulation intensity over the motor hot-spot (the site on the scalp where TMS produced the largest MEP from the FDI) that could elicit an MEP of no less than 50 μV in five of 10 trials during voluntary muscle contraction of the FDI (Rossini et al., 2015). Single pulse TMS was used to measure the RMT for the left and right hemispheres. A lower RMT measurement value indicates higher cortical excitability. CSP is the interruption of EMG activity in the contractile muscle after TMS triggering. The duration of each CSP is measured and the average value of 10 CSPs is calculated for further analysis. The longer the duration of CSP indicates greater cortical inhibition (Maeda et al., 2000, Hupfeld et al., 2020). Paired pulse TMS was used to measure SICI-2 ms and ICF-12 ms over bilateral hemispheres with a total of 12 trials per condition. Two consecutive TMS stimuli (one subthreshold at 80 % RMT and the other suprathreshold at 130 % RMT) were applied to the M1 of the contralateral side of the target muscle (Maeda et al., 2000). The stimulation interval of the two TMS stimuli during the SICI measurement was set to 2 ms, whereas for ICF measurement, it was set to 12 ms. SICI and ICF values are defined as a percentage of the MEP amplitude induced by the suppressed or facilitated suprathreshold test stimulus relative to the baseline MEP amplitude, respectively. A decrease in the SICI percentage represents enhanced intracortical inhibition, while an increase in the ICF indicates greater intracortical facilitation (Kobayashi and Pascual-Leone 2003).

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using R software (version 3.6.0) and Matlab software (version 2014b). The participants’ baseline clinical and demographic characteristics, including age, sex, age at onset, tremor duration, educational years, comorbidities, scores and subscores of FTM-TRS were compared between the cTBS and control groups using independent sample t-tests or Mann-Whitney U tests for continuous data, and Chi-square tests (χ2) or Fisher's exact chi-square tests for categorical data. Clinical tremor outcomes were analyzed using linear mixed-effects models with fixed effects for group, time, and their interaction, along with a random intercept for subjects. This approach offers greater flexibility and robustness than repeated-measures analysis of variance (RM-ANOVA), particularly in handling missing data and violations of sphericity. The false discovery rate (FDR) method was applied to correct for multiple comparisons. All tests were two-sided with significance set at α = 0.05.

NIRS data were analyzed using MATLAB software (version 2014b). Mann-Whitney U tests were used to compare oxy-Hb values during the tremor tasks for each channel between the cTBS and control groups. The Wilcoxon test was performed to analyze the differences in oxy-Hb values during the tremor tasks for each channel before and after the intervention. The FDR was used for multiple comparisons for neural activation in the probes of 37 channels.

Results

Participant characteristics and the quality of blinding

A total of eighty-two individuals volunteered for the study and provided written consent, of which twenty-two were excluded. Nine did not meet the inclusion criteria after detailed consultation, eight withdrew their informed consent (six due to scheduling conflicts with work or family commitments, and two because of alcohol consumption within 24 h prior to intervention), and five were excluded due to transportation problems. Sixty individuals completed the baseline evaluation and randomization, with participants assigned 1:1 to the cTBS group (n = 30) and the control group (n = 30) (Fig. 2). All participants completed the clinical protocol and were included in the analysis.

Flow diagram of patients through the study.

The average age of the sixty ET patients was 53.65 ± 15.32 (57.50) years old, ranging from 21 to 82 years old, with 43.3 % (26/60) being males. The baseline and demographic data for the cTBS and control groups are shown in Table 1 and are similar in terms of age, sex distribution, age of onset, tremor duration, body mass index (BMI), education, anti-tremor medication, comorbidities, scores and subscores of FTM-TRS. In addition, the blinding measures in our study were considered effective, as the number of participants who could correctly identify treatment allocation between the cTBS group and the control group were not significantly different (χ2(1) = 0.873, p = 0.350).

Primary outcome

The FTM-TRS total and subscale scores for the cTBS and control groups at baseline and all post-intervention time points were summarized in Table 2. Linear mixed-effects models demonstrated significant treatment effects on FTM-TRS domains. For the total FTM-TRS score, significant group x time interactions were observed at all post-intervention assessments (post0min: β = -4.63; post10min: β = -5.90; post45min: β = -3.17; all p < 0.001, FDR-corrected, Table 3), indicating treatment benefits in the cTBS group. Significant interactions were also found for FTM-TRS Part A and Part B across all time points (all p < 0.05). Specifically, FTM-TRS Part A decreased markedly in the cTBS group by 4.17 points at post0min, 5.27 points at post10min, and 3.10 points at post45min, relative to minor reductions in the control group. As shown in Fig. 3, the patient’s Archimedes spirals showed reduced upper limb tremor following cTBS intervention. For FTM-TRS Part C, significant group x time interactions were observed at post0min and post10min (both p < 0.01), with no interaction at post45min and no significant main effects of time.

Secondary outcomes

Detailed FTM-TRS subscores

Linear mixed-effects models demonstrated distinct treatment responses across tremor subtypes. Neurophysiological measures revealed significant between-group differences. In the cTBS group, the ICF at 12 ms in the left hemisphere showed a significant decrease after intervention (p = 0.047), with no significant change in the control group (p = 0.120). Adverse events

Treatment tolerability was generally good, with no significant adverse events or unanticipated adverse device effects reported. A total of nine adverse events occurred in seven patients during the study (see Supplementary Table S3). The occurrence of adverse events was similar across the two groups, including mild headache, scalp numbness, dizziness, limbs numbness, and limbs pain. Mild headache was the most common adverse event, reported in two patients in the cTBS group and one in the control group. The proportion of mild headache was 6.67 % (2/30) in the cTBS group and 3.33 % (1/30) in the control group (p = 1.000).

Discussion

This randomized clinical trial demonstrated that a single session of multitarget cTBS treatment over bilateral M1 and the cerebellum yields immediate therapeutic effects in ameliorating tremor in ET patients, while maintaining a favorable safety profile with no serious adverse events observed. The therapeutic response was linked to a decrease in left-hemisphere ICF, suggesting that reduced intracortical facilitation is a potential mechanism for tremor improvement.

TMS therapy has demonstrated both short-term and long-term effectiveness in improving tremor. Single-pulse TMS targeting the motor cortex can temporarily alleviate tremor symptoms in ET patients (Britton et al., 1993, Gironell et al., 2002, Avanzino et al., 2009). A five-day uncontrolled trial with bilateral cerebellar 1 Hz rTMS demonstrated significant and enduring tremor improvement, which was attributed to the restoration of defective information processing in the CTC network (Popa et al., 2013). Our findings align with previous studies that have shown tremor improvement following cTBS (Batra et al., 2022). By employing a blinded and sham-controlled design, our study minimized the placebo effects and bias to further investigate the safety and immediate effects of multitarget cTBS treatment in ET patients. Tremor amplitude or power assessed with accelerometry was significantly reduced after applying cTBS (Hellriegel et al., 2012, Chuang et al., 2014), and our study also highlighted behavioral improvements in tremor attributed to multitarget cTBS intervention and provided evidence supporting multitarget cTBS treatment in ET patients. The inconsistent results reported in the existing literature regarding the efficacy of cTBS or rTMS for tremor can be attributed to several factors (Bologna et al., 2015, Shin et al., 2019). First, there is a lack of standardized and effective cTBS treatment parameters (including stimulation site, intensity, pulse number, treatment duration, etc.) for ET patients. Second, tremor symptoms are influenced by various factors, such as emotion (Milanov 2007), fatigue (Lippold 1981), and social environment, complicating tremor assessment. Additionally, slight differences in tremor are often challenging to observe, contributing to the heterogeneity of results.

Our study demonstrated the immediate and short-term effects of a single session of multitarget cTBS treatment in improving tremor. The effects of cTBS on brain and behavior may be associated with long-term depression (LTD) plasticity changes in synaptic connections within the cortical networks (Popa et al., 2010, Obeso et al., 2015). Previous studies have suggested abnormal changes in the cerebellum and cerebral cortex in ET patients (Hellwig et al., 2001, Pinto et al., 2003, Raethjen et al., 2007). Irregular oscillatory activity might originate from regions such as the inferior olive (Sharifi et al., 2022). In ET patients, cerebellar Purkinje cells, which release GABA and inhibit the thalamic cortical projection, decrease by more than 25 % (Jones, 1993, Louis et al., 2011a). The cTBS treatment could enhance GABA release and inhibit synaptic transmission, causing LTD-like plasticity changes in Purkinje cells and improving tremor symptoms (Stagg et al., 2009). Additionally, TMS might indirectly affect subcortical areas, benefiting tremor regulation in ET patients (Strafella et al., 2003). This might be associated with changes in cortical excitability and inhibition before and after cTBS intervention. The cTBS treatment targeting the motor cortex or premotor cortex could inhibit the excitability of the motor cortex in ET patients, but the duration of this effect is significantly shorter than that of healthy individuals (Chuang et al., 2014, Birreci et al., 2025). The decrease in cortical excitability was evidenced by an increase in CSP (Batra et al., 2022). Our findings highlight different excitatory changes between the left and right hemispheres. The observed reduction in ICF in the left hemisphere (indicating decreased facilitation) and the increased SICI in the right hemisphere (reflecting enhanced inhibition) may be associated with the LTD-like plasticity mechanisms induced by cTBS. However, these findings should be interpreted with caution. The hemispheric specificity of the effects suggests that the changes are not generalized LTD across the entire brain, which is likely related to the sequential stimulation protocol in our study and potential interregional network interactions. In each treatment session, cTBS or sham stimulation was applied in the following order: right M1, left M1, right cerebellum, and left cerebellum. It is plausible that cTBS applied to one region after inhibiting a connected brain area may be less effective, which could potentially explain the asymmetry observed in the neurophysiological outcomes. Furthermore, these observed changes in these indirect neurophysiological measures could also be attributed to other mechanisms, such as network-level modulation (Strafella et al., 2003).

Preclinical and clinical studies suggest that a defective GABAergic system may be a potential pathophysiological mechanism underlying ET (Kralic et al., 2005, Boecker et al., 2010, Boecker, 2013). These microscopic electrophysiological changes could be detected by TMS measurements. Multi-site cTBS over bilateral M1 and the cerebellum was effective in reducing tremor in the acute post-stimulation period. This improvement may be due to remodeling the defective GABAergic system in the CTC network, altering synaptic transmission, regulating the inhibitory loop in the brain of ET patients, and in some ways, reducing the excitability of the cortex and enhancing cortical inhibition (Steina et al., 2025). However, we did not observe synchronous hemodynamic changes in fNIRS results after FDR correction. This may be attributed to several methodological and physiological factors. The fNIRS is limited to cortical measurements and did not cover key stimulated regions like the cerebellum. Additionally, its spatial resolution prevents detection of subcortical activity (e.g. thalamus) (Rahman et al., 2020). The acute nature of single-session cTBS may not produce detectable cortical hemodynamic changes over short intervals or within limited cortical regions. Finally, the specific motor tasks performed during fNIRS, though novel, may require further validation to establish its sensitivity in capturing tremor-related cortical activity.

The limited improvement in midline tremor observed in our study can be attributed to several factors. According to cerebellar somatotopy, axial functions such as head and neck control are primarily represented in the midline vermis, whereas limb functions localize to the hemispheres (Louis et al., 2003, Louis et al., 2011b). Our cTBS protocol targeted the bilateral cerebellar hemispheres rather than midline structures like the vermis, which may explain the insufficient modulation of vermal regions involved in midline tremor. Furthermore, this site-specific treatment response appears to be linked to tremor topography, with limb tremor generally showing better responsiveness than head tremor. Previous studies have reported that head tremor shows less improvement than limb tremor with various treatments, including medications such as propranolol (Paparella et al., 2018), arotinolol (Morita et al., 2005), primidone (Findley et al., 1985), and barbiturates (Sasso et al., 1991), as well as with cerebellar rTMS (Olfati et al., 2020). Despite participants’ reports of overall improvement (CGI-I), their performance on specific Part C tasks (e.g., dressing, writing, or using utensils) did not significantly differ from the sham group at 45 min post-cTBS. This discrepancy likely arises because the CGI-I captures global subjective change (Busner and Targum 2007), whereas Part C assesses performance of well-practiced tasks. Thus, the transient effects of a single cTBS session were felt subjectively but were insufficient to improve specific task performance. Future studies should determine if repeated sessions can translate these perceived benefits into functional gains.

To the best of our knowledge, this double-blind, randomized controlled trial has demonstrated the safety and effectiveness of a single session of cTBS treatment across multiple sites in ET patients, while also providing insight into its potential neurophysiological mechanisms through objective TMS measures. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the study was limited by a small sample size and single-center design, which calls for caution in interpreting and generalizing the results. Second, although efforts were made to minimize bias through joint assessments and in-person interviews, tremor evaluation based on FTM-TRS scores, tremor assessment based on FTM-TRS scores is subjective, and the influence of a placebo effect cannot be fully excluded despite blinding assessment. Third, the absence of cerebellar-brain inhibition (CBI) measurements constrains the interpretation of cerebellar-cortical connectivity mechanisms (Popa et al., 2010, Fernandez et al., 2018). Fourth, the use of a fixed stimulation order across the four targets, without randomization, may have introduced cumulative effects between regions, complicating the interpretation of outcomes. Fifth, the lack of an active comparator (e.g., single-site cTBS) prevents definitive conclusions regarding the superiority of multi-target stimulation. Sixth, the precision of stimulation targeting was limited by the absence of neuronavigation and individualized dosing; future studies should consider MRI-guided navigation and electric-field modeling to improve accuracy (Stokes et al., 2007). Finally, this study focused only on the acute effects of a single session, with follow-up limited to 45 min. The short-term improvements in FTM-TRS scores observed here may not reflect actual functional gains or long-term clinical benefits and should be interpreted cautiously. Repeated cTBS sessions with prolonged follow-ups are needed to evaluate long-term efficacy. However, these findings provided preliminary data for the acute anti-tremor effects of multitarget cTBS and paved the way for further larger studies to evaluate whether repeated sessions of multitarget cTBS can produce sustained clinical improvements, including direct comparisons wth single-target protocols.

Conclusions

Our data suggest that multitarget cTBS over M1 and the cerebellum produces significant acute anti-tremor effects in ET patients, offering a promising noninvasive intervention. Further studies are warranted to determine whether multitarget cTBS offers superior benefits compared to single-site stimulation, as well as to evaluate its long-term therapeutic efficacy in ET.
