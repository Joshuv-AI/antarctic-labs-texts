Prevotella intermedia culture supernatant impairs alveolar macrophage function and promotes Mycobacterium avium persistence
Authors: Koki Fukushima, Naoki Iwanaga, Kazuaki Takeda, Tatsuro Hirayama, Masataka Yoshida, Shotaro Ide, Masato Tashiro, Takahiro Takazono, Kosuke Kosai, Noriho Sakamoto, Koichi Izumikawa, Mariko Naito, Katsunori Yanagihara, Hiroshi Mukae
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTMicrobiome analyses using 16S ribosomal RNA gene sequencing have revealed a higher prevalence of Prevotella species in patients with nontuberculous mycobacterial pulmonary disease (NTM-PD) than in those with non-NTM-PD bronchiectasis. Prevotella intermedia culture supernatant (P. int. sup.) has been reported to exacerbate pneumonia, but its effect on Mycobacterium avium infection remains unclear. Thus, we manipulated bone marrow cells to differentiate into alveolar macrophage-like cells (AMLCs), infected them with M. avium in the presence of P. int. sup. or control medium, and analyzed their phagocytic and bactericidal activities. Intracellular bacteria were quantified, and antimicrobial gene expression was assessed using real-time reverse transcription PCR and bulk RNA sequencing. In vivo, female C57BL/6J mice (7–8 weeks old) were oropharyngeally inoculated with M. avium with or without P. int. sup. P. int. sup. impaired intracellular killing of M. avium in AMLCs. RNA sequencing revealed downregulation of autophagy, type I interferon, and anti-mycobacterial genes in the P. int. sup.-treated group. Furthermore, flow cytometry analysis revealed that P. int. sup. increases levels of microtubule-associated protein 1 light chain 3 and may inhibit lysosome maturation. In mice, P. int. sup. increased the pulmonary M. avium load and suppressed type I and II interferon expression in the lungs. In this experimental mouse model of NTM-PD, P. int. sup. increased pulmonary M. avium burden, while in mouse derived AMLCs P. int sup. was associated with impaired autophagy‑related and interferon responses and reduced antimicrobial gene expression, supporting a potential role for P. intermedia in NTM-PD progression.KEYWORDS: Prevotella intermedia, alveolar macrophage, nontuberculous mycobacterial pulmonary disease, bactericidal activity, interferon responses

Introduction

Nontuberculous mycobacterial pulmonary disease (NTM-PD), with an increasing incidence in Japan [1] and the United States [2], shows a high mortality rate, especially the fibrocavitary type, despite the availability of effective therapies [3]. Furthermore, recurrence rates remain high even after the American Thoracic Society (ATS)/Infectious Diseases Society of America (IDSA) guideline-based treatment for Mycobacterium avium complex (MAC)-lung disease [4]. Overall, factors contributing to NTM-PD progression and recurrence require further study.

Microbiome analyses using 16S ribosomal RNA gene sequencing indicate a higher prevalence of Prevotella species in patients with NTM-PD than in those with non-NTM-PD bronchiectasis [5]. Furthermore, anaerobes and other oral bacteria are more frequently detected in patients with community-acquired pneumonia [6]. Among these, Prevotella intermedia, an obligate anaerobe residing in periodontal pockets, causes periodontal disease [7,8]. Concurrently, poor oral hygiene can induce respiratory infections, including NTM-PD [9,10]. Moreover, co-infection with P. intermedia culture supernatant (P. int. sup.) is known to exacerbate Streptococcus pneumoniae- and oral Streptococcus species-induced pneumonia in mouse models [11,12]. In contrast, the culture supernatants of Fusobacterium nucleatum (F. nuc. sup.) and Porphyromonas gingivalis showed no such effects S. pneumoniae-induced pneumonia [11,12]. Overall, P. int. sup. shows unique pathogenic potential in the respiratory tract.

MAC infection itself can impair macrophage function by promoting differentiation toward non-polarized phenotypes, thereby reducing phagocytosis [13]. We hypothesized that evaluating the effects of MAC infection on alveolar macrophages could reveal the mechanisms underlying the early infection phase, exacerbation, and recurrence in NTM-PD. However, whether P. int. sup. affects the intracellular survival of M. avium in alveolar macrophages remains unknown.

In this study, we investigated the effects of P. int. sup. on NTM infection, particularly on alveolar macrophage functions [14], using primary alveolar macrophage-like cells (AMLCs) induced from undifferentiated bone marrow (BM) cells in vitro [15] instead of cancer cell-derived lines to better approximate physiological conditions. These findings may help elucidate the contribution of oral bacteria to NTM-PD exacerbation and recurrence, potentially informing new preventive or therapeutic strategies.

Methods

Mice

Female C57BL/6J (wild-type, WT) mice (7–8-week-old), purchased from the Jackson Laboratory (Kanagawa, Japan), were maintained under specific pathogen-free conditions at the Research Center for Biomedical Models and Animal Welfare, Nagasaki University Graduate School of Biomedical Sciences. All experimental protocols were approved by Nagasaki University (2109091745) and performed in accordance with the guidelines of the Research Center for Biomedical Models and Animal Welfare at Nagasaki University. No unexpected adverse events occurred during the study. No humane endpoints utilizing monitoring weight loss, randomization, measures to minimize confounders, and inclusion criteria were applied to the animals or data points. The study has adhered to the ARRIVE guidelines.

Bacteria

Mycobacterium avium ATCC 700737 was cultured on Middlebrook 7H10 agar with 10% oleic acid–albumin–dextrose–catalase enrichment (Becton Dickinson and Company, Fukushima, Japan) for 7 days at 37°C in 5% CO2, then resuspended in Middlebrook 7H9 broth (Becton Dickinson and Company) containing 0.5% Tween 80 (Tokyo Chemical Industry Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan), and incubated at 37°C with shaking for 3–5 days (log growth phase). Aliquots (300 μL) were stored at −80°C for at least 1 day before use. The P. intermedia strain OMA14 isolated from the periodontal pocket of a Japanese patient with periodontitis [16], and a F. nucleatum strain ATCC 10953 were anaerobically cultured on PV Brucella HK agar (Kyokuto Pharmaceutical Industrial Co., Tokyo, Japan) for 48 h. Bacterial colonies were scraped and suspended in a modified Gifu anaerobic medium (GAM) broth (Shimadzu Diagnostics Co., Tokyo, Japan). To prepare P. int. sup., bacterial suspensions (5.0 × 107-1.0 × 108 CFU/mL) were anaerobically incubated in 30 mL GAM broth for 36–48 h. Bacterial suspensions were then centrifuged (1690 × g for 10 min), the supernatants were collected, filtered through a 0.22-μm pore-size membrane filter (Sartorius Japan K.K., Tokyo, Japan), and confirmed as sterile by cultivation. For comparison with the similar anaerobic bacterial species, F. nuc sup. (F. nucleatum strain ATCC10953) was collected using the same method.

Alveolar macrophage-like cells

Alveolar macrophage-like cell (AMLC) generation from undifferentiated BM cells in vitro was described previously [15]. Briefly, BM was harvested from 8–10-week-old female C57BL/6J (WT) mice and cultured in 12-well plates with Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Tokyo, Japan) supplemented with 10% deactivated fetal bovine serum (FBS; Sigma-Aldrich, Tokyo, Japan), penicillin (Sigma-Aldrich), and streptomycin (Sigma-Aldrich). The cultures were maintained in the presence of 20 ng/mL granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor (GM-CSF; Funakoshi Company, Tokyo, Japan), 2 ng/mL transforming growth factor beta (TGF-β; Funakoshi Company), and 0.1 mM peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor γ (PPAR-γ; Sigma-Aldrich) agonist rosiglitazone for 9 days. Differentiated AMLCs were identified using flow cytometry based on high surface expression of sialic acid-binding Ig-like lectin-F (Siglec-F), anti-cluster of differentiation 11c (CD11c), and anti-F4/80, and low surface expression of anti-CD11b (Supplementary Figure S1A). CD80 and CD163 expression was evaluated using flow cytometry to assess AMLC polarization status, with low expression of both markers indicating non-polarized macrophages (Supplementary Figure S1B). AMLCs were stored as aliquots of 1.0 × 106 cells/mL in Cell Banker (Zenogen Pharma, Fukushima, Japan) at −80°C or in liquid nitrogen. The antibodies for flow cytometry are shown in Supplementary Table S1.

Infection

M. avium stocks stored at −80°C were thawed, resuspended in Middlebrook 7H9 broth, and incubated with shaking for 3–5 days. The suspension was centrifuged at 1690 × g for 10 min, then vortexed with glass beads to break up clumps. For in vitro infection or non-infection, AMLCs (1.0 × 105/well) were seeded in 24-well plates and infected with or without M. avium (1.0 × 106 colony-forming units (CFUs)/well). AMLCs incubated with M. avium at 37°C, 5% CO2 were defined as”M. avium-infected AMLCs.” Further, the treatment with P. int. sup (or GAM broth) was defined as “M. avium-infected AMLCs treated with P. int. sup. (or control medium).” For in vivo experiments, 50 μL P. int. sup. mixed with 50 μL of M. avium suspension (2.0 × 106 CFU/mL) was oropharyngeally administered to the mice under isoflurane inhalation anesthesia. For equal exposure across experimental groups, the protein concentrations of P. int. sup., F. nuc. sup., and GAM broth were measured using the BCA protein assay kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer’s instructions and then adjusted to 800 μg/mL. In the therapeutic assay, IFNγ (PeproTech) was added to the infected AMLCs treated with P. int. sup or control medium at a final concentration of 25 ng/mL.

Bacteriological examinations in vivo

Two days after infection, mice from each group were euthanized by exposure to CO2, followed by exsanguination. The left lung was excised, homogenized in 1 mL PBS, and serially diluted. Aliquots of each dilution were plated onto Middlebrook 7H10 agar supplemented with PANTA (polymyxin B, amphotericin B, nalidixic acid, trimethoprim, azlocillin) antibiotics (Becton Dickinson and Company) and incubated at 37°C, 5% CO2 for 7 days.

Phagocytosis index and intracellular survival index

Mycobacterium avium-infected AMLCs treated with P. int. sup. (or control medium) for 4 h. Cells were then washed thrice with PBS. AMLCs were subsequently harvested using Accutase (Sigma-Aldrich), treated with 0.1% Triton X in PBS (Sigma-Aldrich), and plated on Middlebrook 7H10 agar. Subsequently, the plates were incubated at 37°C with 5% CO2 for 7 days, and CFUs were counted (each procedure was defined as “cell washing, cell collection, and CFU counting). To assess intracellular survival, AMLCs were washed and incubated for an additional 44 h. The CFU count at 4 h post-infection was defined as the phagocytic ability index, and the change in CFU counts between 4 and 48 h was defined as the intracellular survival index, as described previously [17].

Bactericidal ability assay of AMLCs

Mycobacterium avium-infected AMLCs for 4 h. Following infection, the medium was removed, and the cells were washed. Subsequently, P. int. sup. (or GAM broth) was added, and the cells were further incubated at 37°C with 5% CO2 for 44 h to assess the CFU.

Real-time RT-PCR

Total RNA was extracted from lung tissue using the RNeasy Mini kit (QIAGEN). cDNA was synthesized using the iScript Reverse Transcription Supermix for RT-qPCR (Bio-Rad, Tokyo, Japan). Quantitative PCR (qPCR) was performed on an Applied Biosystems QuantStudio 12 K Flex system (Thermo Fisher Scientific) using TaqMan PCR Master Mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and premixed primer-probe sets (Supplementary Table 2).

Bulk RNA-seq

RNA-seq library preparation, sequencing, read mapping, and gene expression analysis were performed using DNAFORM software (Yokohama, Kanagawa, Japan). Double-stranded cDNA libraries (RNA-seq libraries) were prepared using the SMART-Seq Stranded Kit (Clontech, 634,442) and DNBSEQ MGIEasy Universal Library Conversion Kit (MGI Technology, Tokyo, Japan; catalog#1000004155) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The RNA-seq libraries were sequenced using paired-end reads (150 nt of reads 1 and 2) on a DNBSEQ-G400RS platform (MGI Technology).

Mouse secretory leukocyte protease inhibitor (SLPI) enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay

SLPI protein antibacterial effect assay

M. avium-infected AMLCs for 4 h were treated with mouse SLPI protein (Cosmo Bio Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) for 1 h. On the other hand, to evaluate the direct bactericidal activity of SLPI protein, M. avium was mono-incubated with mouse SLPI protein for 48 h.

Flow cytometry

Samples were kept on ice until processing, then centrifuged at 400 × g for 10 minutes at 4°C. Cell pellets were resuspended in fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) buffer (PBS containing 0.1% bovine serum albumin). For assessment of microtubule-associated protein 1 light chain 3 (LC3) flux, bafilomycin A1 (Bio-Techne; final concentration: 200 nM) was added 4 h before harvesting. To remove cytosolic LC3-I, cells were permeabilized with 0.1% saponin (FUJIFILM Toyama Chemical Co., Ltd.). After permeabilization, samples were washed and resuspended in FACS buffer and then incubated with Anti-LC3 antibody (Cosmobio). Samples were washed and resuspended in FACS buffer, then stained with appropriate antibodies (Supplementary Table 1). Cells were washed again in FACS buffer and analyzed using an Attune NxT Flow Cytometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Flow cytometry data were analyzed using FlowJo Mac, version 10.10.0 (Tree Star Inc.). Compensation was performed using single-stained beads (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Events were gated sequentially as follows. (1) FSC-A vs SSC-A to exclude debris; (2) to include singlets (FSC-A vs FSC-W); (3) final populations with marker definitions. Gates were set using FMO controls. Frequencies are reported as a percentage of total cell counts.

Statistical analysis

GraphPad Prism version 10 was used for generating graphs and statistical analyses. For comparisons between two groups, the non-parametric Mann–Whitney U-test was used. For comparisons among more than two groups, either one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s multiple comparison test (for parametric data) or the Kruskal–Wallis test followed by Dunn’s multiple comparison test (for non-parametric data) was used. Survival differences were analyzed using the log-rank test and Kaplan–Meier curves. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Prevotella intermedia supernatant augmented M. avium intracellular survival index in AMLCs

AMLCs generated from mouse BM were identified as non-polarized macrophages (Supplementary Figure 1). The results of the experiment outlined in (Figure 1(A)) showed a significantly higher number of intracellular bacteria (colony-forming units; CFUs) in M. avium-infected AMLCs treated with P. int. sup. for 4 h (phagocytosis index) than in the control group (Figure 1(B); p < 0.001). Similarly, after 4 h of incubation with M. avium and 44 h of post-infection incubation with P. int. sup. (or control medium), the number of intracellular bacteria remained significantly higher in the P. int. sup. group than in the control group (Figure 1(C); p < 0.001). The intracellular survival index was significantly higher in the P. int. sup. group than in the control group (Figure 1(D); p < 0.05). However, the proteinase K-treated P. int. sup. or F. nuc. sup. groups did not show the efficacy (Supplementary Figure S2). P int. sup. significantly increase the intracellular burden of M. avium in AMLCs 48 h post-infection, whereas no significant change was observed at 72 h or 96 h post-infection (Supplementary Figure S3). Furthermore, the effect of P. int. sup. for macrophage bactericidal function was dose-dependent (no dilution, 1: 10 dilutions, 1: 100 dilutions control medium) (Supplementary Figure S4). The number of live or dead cells did not change significantly among the groups, suggesting that P. int. sup. did not induce cytotoxicity in AMLCs (Supplementary Figure S5). Growth curves of M. avium incubated with P. int. sup. (both undiluted and diluted) analyzed daily from day 0 to 6 showed no significant difference between dilution groups (Supplementary Figure S6).

Bulk RNA-seq revealed broad downregulation of numerous immune-related genes in P. intermedia supernatant-treated M. avium-infected AMLCs

Bulk RNA-seq of M. avium-infected AMLCs for 4 h treated with P. int. sup. for an additional 4 h showed significant downregulation of Ccl3, Ccl4, Ccl5, Tnfa, Isg15, Rsad2, Ccrl2, Slpi, Il6, Hspa1a, Ifit1, Sp140, Cxcl10, Oasl1, and H2-Ab1 genes compared with those in the control group. In contrast, the Clec7a and Cd36 genes were significantly upregulated (Figure 3(A)). Pathway analysis showed significant downregulation of several key pathways in the P. int. sup. group, including lipid and atherosclerosis, IL-17 signaling, viral protein interaction with cytokines and cytokine receptors, TNF signaling, rheumatoid arthritis, legionellosis, cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction, influenza A, toll-like receptor signaling, and NOD-like receptor signaling (Figure 3(B)).

mRNA expression analysis based on bulk RNA-seq findings revealed suppression related to intracellular bactericidal functions

M. avium-infected AMLCs treated with P. int. sup. for 4 h also revealed significant downregulation of Isg15, Ifit1, Slpi, and Ifnb1 than in the control group using real-time RT-PCR (Figure 4(A–D); p < 0.05). In contrast, Il6 and H2-Ab1 showed significantly higher expression in the P. int. sup. group than in the control group (Figure 4(E,F); p < 0.01). Furthermore, the expression levels of Ifna, Ifnar1, Ifnar2, Rsad2, Oasl1, and Sp140 tended to be lower (Supplementary Figure S9A–E), and those of Tnfa, Ifngr1, and Ifngr2 tended to be higher (Supplementary Figure S9F–H) in the P. int. sup. group. SLPI protein level tended to be lower in the P. int. sup. group than in the control group, but this difference was not significant (Supplementary Figure S10). P. int. sup. in the absence of M. avium did not alter mRNA expression including Irgm1 in AMLCs (Supplementary Figure S11). M. avium-infected AMLCs treated with Fuso. nuc. sup. for 4 hours showed no significant difference in intracellular M. avium burden (Supplementary Figure S12A). Furthermore, there was no significant difference in intracellular M. avium burden or mRNA expression, except for Isg15, between the two groups (Supplementary Figure S12B).

P. int. sup. increases M. avium CFUs in mouse lungs

M. avium and P. int. sup. were co-administered to mice via oropharyngeal inoculation (Figure 6(A)). At 4 h post-challenge, the number of bacteria in the lungs did not differ significantly between the groups (Figure 6(B(a))). In contrast, at 48 h post-infection, the lung bacterial burden was significantly higher in the P. int. sup. group than in the control group (Figure 6(B(b)); p < 0.05). Real-time RT-PCR using the lungs of mice infected with M. avium and treated with P. int. sup. at 4 h post-infection showed significant downregulation of Irgm1 and Isg15 compared with that in the control group (Figure 6(C(a, b)); p < 0.05). Ifng mRNA expression showed a decreasing trend in the P. int. sup. group than in the control group, but the difference was not significant (Figure 6(C(c))). Tnfa expression was significantly higher in the P. int. sup. group than in the control group (Figure 6(C(d)); P < 0.05), whereas the expression levels of Slpi, Ifnb1, Il17a, and Marco tended to be reduced in the P. int. sup. group (Supplementary Figure S15A–D). In contrast, at 48 h post-infection, the lung mRNA expression was not significantly different between the two groups (Figure 6(D)).

Discussion

Our findings show that P. int. sup. aggravates M. avium infection by weakening antimicrobial responses in alveolar macrophage–like cells. In vitro, P. int. sup. increased intracellular CFU at 4 h and after 44 h, elevating both the phagocytosis and intracellular survival indices without directly accelerating M. avium growth, consistent with impaired macrophage killing rather than enhanced microbial proliferation. Furthermore, the duration of this effect seemed to be less than 48 hours, and it was diminished without M. avium infection. Mechanistically, P. int. sup. reduced the expression of Irgm1, a key regulator of autophagy, in M. avium-infected AMLCs, suggesting that autophagy is central to the control of intracellular pathogens [18]. Its suppression by P. int. sup. suggests an early immune evasion strategy for M. avium persistence within macrophages. Notably, the most significant decrease in Irgm1 expression occurred 4 h after P. int. sup. exposure, indicating a rapid inhibition of autophagy-related defenses. Lack of significant phagocytosis-associated gene expression changes in M. avium-infected AMLCs treated with P. int. sup. for 2 h [19], suggested that the increased bacterial load was not because of enhanced phagocytic uptake but rather due to impaired macrophage killing. Additionally, P. int. sup. itself did not directly promote M. avium growth but instead modulated AMLC function to favor the pathogen’s efficient intracellular survival.

Bulk RNA‑seq revealed broad suppression of immune programs, including Toll‑like and NOD‑like receptor signaling, IL‑17 and TNF signaling, RIG‑I–like receptor and cytosolic DNA‑sensing pathways, and other modules associated with intracellular infection. As M. avium is an intracellular pathogen and alveolar macrophages are major target cells in NTM infections [19], suppression of these pathways may contribute to facilitating infection. Furthermore, Isg15, Slpi, Ifnb, and Ifit1 were significantly downregulated in the P. int. sup. group, whereas Il6 and H2-Ab1 were significantly upregulated. ISG15, a 15 kDa ubiquitin-like protein induced by type 1 IFN signaling [20], facilitates ISGylation in cells, exerts anti-mycobacterial efficacy, and downregulates exosome secretion, thereby preventing bacterial escape to the extracellular space [21]. Moreover, secreted ISG15 promotes extracellular IFNγ production [22]. Overall, P. int. sup. suppresses ISG15 expression in macrophages, thereby impairing its anti-mycobacterial effects and facilitating the intracellular survival of M. avium. Overall, P. int. sup. can inhibit IFNβ1 signaling, leading to the downstream suppression of ISG15 and IFNγ, which may collectively enhance M. avium growth during the early phase of infection. SLPI protein, secreted by epithelial cells and macrophages, inhibits serine proteases such as elastase and cathepsin G to prevent tissue damage [23,24]. Additionally, SLPI protein demonstrates bactericidal activity by increasing the permeability of M. tuberculosis cell membranes and inhibiting its growth [25]. We proved that direct incubation of SLPI protein with M. avium significantly reduced the bacterial counts, indicating the direct bactericidal effect of SLPI protein on M. avium. Collectively, our findings suggest that P. int. sup. inhibits SLPI protein, potentially reducing the direct damage to M. avium membrane. IFIT1, highly expressed during the interferon response and produced by macrophages, directly inhibits viral protein synthesis and upregulates innate immune signaling [26]. The bulk RNA-seq findings suggest that P. int. sup. inhibits Ifit1 and down-regulates innate immune signaling pathways such as IFN1 signaling, and contributes toward increased M. avium protein synthesis. Conversely, H2-Ab1, encoding the mouse major histocompatibility complex (MHC) class II β chain, was significantly upregulated. H2-Ab1 controls M. tuberculosis infection by binding MHC class II to M. tuberculosis antigens and regulating antigen presentation to CD4+ T cells [27]. Thus, increased H2-Ab1 expression may reflect a compensatory host response to enhance adaptive immunity. Moreover, we also observed increased Clec7a (dectin‑1), which supports non‑opsonic, β‑glucan‑dependent phagocytosis [28]. This phagocytic pathway operates independently of Fc receptor-mediated mechanisms [29]. Coupled with the concurrent downregulation of Irgm1, a key regulator of autophagy and intracellular pathogen clearance, the results imply augmented uptake but impaired autophagic killing, together promoting intracellular persistence. Furthermore, upregulation of Cd36, a scavenger receptor mediating the uptake of oxidized low-density lipoprotein [30], promotes foam cell formation, thereby contributing to the intracellular survival of M. avium within foam cell-like macrophages [31].

Flow cytometry analysis showed that P. int. sup. increases LC3 levels, whereas this increase was not further enhanced by a lysosomal maturation inhibitor. LC3 is commonly used as a marker for autophagosomes [32]. Bafilomycin A1, a V-type ATPase inhibitor, is commonly used to inhibit lysosomal acidification and assess autophagic flux, including in flow cytometry analysis [33–35]. These findings suggested that P. int. sup. induces LC3 accumulation and may impair lysosomal maturation.

These in vitro findings were largely replicated in vivo, with a focus on the early phase of M. avium infection. In mice, oropharyngeal co-administration of M. avium and P. int. sup. did not alter lung CFU at 4 h but significantly increased CFU and mRNA expression at 48 h, mirroring early in-vitro dynamics, suggesting that P. int. sup. promotes M. avium proliferation during the early phase of infection in the experimental mouse model of NTM-PD. Lungs sampled at 4 h from P. int. sup.–treated mice showed decreased tendency of Irgm1 and Isg15 and increased Tnfa, indicating suppression of autophagy especially lysosome maturation and type I interferon signaling with heightened inflammatory activation [36]. The pattern suggests relative TNFα upregulation with IFNγ inhibition, potentially disrupting their synergy against mycobacteria. Furthermore, IFNγ treatment tended to mitigate the effect of P. int. sup. in M. avium-infected macrophages, suggesting that IFNγ signaling may be involved in the P. int. sup. mediated phenotype. In addition, IFNγ treatment did not reduce the intracellular burden in the control group, suggesting that the effect of IFNγ may be specific to P. int. sup.–treated M. avium–infected macrophages. This is consistent with P. int. sup.–mediated upregulation of genes associated with IFN-γ receptor including Ifngr1 and Ifngr2, and downregulation of IFN-γ–responsive genes including Isg15 and Irgm1. The effect disappeared after heat or proteinase K treatment and was not reproduced by F. nucleatum supernatant, although it did upregulate Isg15 mRNA expression. This suggests the involvement of heat-sensitive protein components unique to P. intermedia. Therefore, P. int. sup. exerts a distinct effect on macrophages, and its protein components may influence macrophage behavior. Its virulence activity is likely mediated by heat-labile protein components rather than endotoxins, which are primarily lipid-based and generally resistant to heat and protease treatment. Identification of these specific active components in greater detail is currently underway [37].

This study has several limitations. First, it focused primarily on the early stages of infection, both in vitro and in vivo, without evaluating the long-term effects of P. intermedia on M. avium infection. To better reflect the pathophysiology of human NTM-PD, further studies employing chronic infection models are necessary to determine the sustained effect of P. intermedia on disease progression and host immune responses.

Finally, our findings suggest a potential association between P. intermedia and the experimental model of NTM-PD progression. However, further research is needed to investigate whether P. intermedia influences clinical aspects of NTM-PD, including disease severity, prevalence, and prognosis.
