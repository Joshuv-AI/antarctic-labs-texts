Risk factors of peri-intubation cardiac arrest in critically ill pediatric patients presenting to the emergency department of a low-middle-income country: a case-control study
Authors: Noman Ali, Erum Shakeel, Ahmed Raheem, Hania Ather, Surraiya Bano
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPeri-intubation cardiac arrest is defined as cardiac arrest that occurs within 20 min of airway management. Incidence in the pediatric population ranges from 0.7% to 1.7%. Identifying pre-intubation risk factors may improve preparedness and outcomes during emergency intubations. This study aims to identify clinical and physiological predictors associated with peri-intubation cardiac arrest in pediatric patients undergoing emergency airway management in a tertiary care emergency department.MethodsA retrospective case-control study was conducted at the emergency department of a tertiary care hospital in Karachi, Pakistan, from January 2019 to June 2023. Pediatric patients (< 18 years) who experienced cardiac arrest within 20 min of intubation were included as cases. For each case, four controls were randomly selected from the same cohort who were intubated without arrest. Pre-intubation clinical data, laboratory parameters, and vital signs were analyzed. Multivariable logistic regression was used to identify independent predictors of peri-intubation cardiac arrest.ResultsA total of 1031 pediatric patients underwent intubation in the ED, with 25 cases (2.4%) of peri-intubation cardiac arrest identified. For comparison, 100 controls without cardiac arrest were randomly selected, resulting in a total study cohort of 125 patients. Multivariate analysis identified several independent predictors of peri-intubation cardiac arrest. Age less than one year (aOR: 4.37; p = 0.042), hypoxemia (SpO₂ < 92%) (aOR: 19.81; p = 0.003), elevated heart rate (aOR: 1.42; p = 0.045), low systolic blood pressure (aOR: 6.56; p = 0.043), elevated shock index (≥ 1.2) (aOR: 5.52; p = 0.046), modified shock index ≥ 1.3 (aOR: 6.27; p = 0.012), lactate ≥ 2 mmol/L (aOR: 10.09; p = 0.047) and capillary refill time > 3 s (aOR: 5.78; p = 0.038), were found to be independent predictors of peri-intubation cardiac arrest.ConclusionPre-intubation physiological instability is strongly associated with peri-intubation cardiac arrest in pediatric patients. Early recognition of high-risk features such as hypoxemia, tachycardia, hypotension, elevated lactate levels, delayed capillary refill time, and elevated shock indices may allow for better preparation and resuscitation planning. These findings support the need for structured pre-intubation assessment protocols in emergency settings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12245-025-01099-5.

Background

Airway management is a critical component of resuscitation in critically ill pediatric patients presenting for emergency care. Although relatively infrequent, endotracheal intubation is often necessary for conditions such as respiratory failure, altered mental status, shock, and cardiac arrest, with an estimated incidence of 2 to 33 per 10,000 pediatric emergency department (ED) visits [1, 2]. Pediatric airway management usually requires unique challenges due to anatomical and physiological variations across various age groups and the limited physiological reserves in young children, which increase their susceptibility to rapid decompensation [3].

Rapid sequence intubation (RSI) has become the standard technique to achieve definitive airway management in emergency settings, offering the benefits of rapid onset and improved intubation conditions [4, 5]. The safety and efficacy of RSI are well established, but on the other hand, it is associated with various complications like post-intubation hypoxemia, hypotension, bradycardia, and even cardiac arrest [6]. Increasing evidence supports the use of modified RSI techniques, including agent selection, dose adjustment, and alternative approaches like delayed sequence intubation, in pediatric patients who are hypoxic, hypotensive, or agitated. These modifications reduce the risk of hypoxemia and hemodynamic complications without increasing aspiration risk and are considered safe and feasible in emergency and critical care settings [7, 8]. Peri-intubation cardiac arrest (CA) is typically defined as cardiac arrest occurring within 20 min of intubation, marked by chest compressions. This timeframe is consistent with definitions in the literature, which vary from 5 to 20 min [9–12]. The reported incidence rate of peri-intubation CA in the pediatric population ranges from 1.7% to 7% [13–15].

Several studies have evaluated the risk factors of peri-intubation (CA) in the pediatric population, including younger age, pre-intubation hypoxemia, hypotension, elevated shock index, and a history of previous pulmonary disease [12, 14, 15]. However, most of the available data are derived from studies conducted in intensive care units or high-resource settings. There remains a significant gap in the literature related to ED-based cohorts, particularly from low- and middle-income countries, where differences in infrastructure, staffing, and pre-intubation optimization strategies may influence outcomes. Therefore, in this study, we aim to identify the clinical and laboratory parameters associated with peri-intubation CA in pediatric patients undergoing emergency intubation in the ED of a tertiary care hospital in a lower-middle-income country.

Methods

Study design and setting

This retrospective case-control study was conducted at the ED of the Aga Khan University Hospital (AKUH), a tertiary care teaching hospital located in Karachi, Pakistan. The study period spanned four and a half years, from January 1, 2019, to June 30, 2023. AKUH serves a large urban population and is equipped to manage a broad spectrum of pediatric emergencies.

Study population

The study included pediatric patients (< 18 years of age) who underwent tracheal intubation in the ED during the defined study period. Patients transferred after intubation from other facilities were excluded from the study.

Case and control selection

Cases were defined as pediatric patients who experienced cardiac arrest within 20 min following administration of sedative or paralytic agents for intubation. For each case, four controls were randomly selected from the same cohort of intubated patients who did not experience cardiac arrest. We intentionally did not match cases and controls on age, gender, presenting complaints, or comorbid conditions, as these variables were included in the analysis to evaluate their role as potential independent predictors of peri-intubation cardiac arrest. No trauma patients or children with a prior pre-hospital cardiac arrest were identified among the cases or controls; therefore, these presentations were not part of the study cohort.

Data collection

Patient data were extracted using a standardized data collection form through manual chart review and electronic medical records (supplementary file 1). Data collectors were trained before data collection to ensure uniformity, and periodic oversight was provided by the principal investigator. Variables collected included demographic details, comorbidities, presenting complaints, vital signs (just before intubation), and pre-intubation laboratory parameters (lactate).

Given that normal vital signs vary by age in pediatric patients, we applied age-specific thresholds for all hemodynamic parameters based on the Pediatric Advanced Life Support (PALS) guidelines. These guidelines are widely utilized in clinical practice to support pediatric resuscitation efforts. Hemodynamic variables such as heart rate and blood pressure were subsequently categorized as normal, elevated, or low according to these age-adjusted reference ranges. In addition to standard vital signs, we calculated the Shock Index, Pediatric Age-Adjusted (SIPA), to assess hemodynamic instability across different age groups. SIPA was derived by dividing the patient’s heart rate by the systolic blood pressure and evaluated against established age-specific thresholds [16]. Abnormal SIPA values were defined as ≥ 1.2 for children aged 1 month to 6 years, ≥ 1.0 for those aged 7 to 12 years, and ≥ 0.9 for patients older than 12 years. Furthermore, we calculated the modified shock index (MSI) by dividing the heart rate by mean arterial pressure. An MSI value of ≥ 1.3 was considered elevated. Information on the use of sedatives and paralytics, level of the intubating provider (resident, fellow, or attending), number of intubation attempts, and reasons for intubation (e.g., shock, low GCS, respiratory failure) was also recorded. To handle missing values (< 5% for any variable), we used multiple imputation via predictive mean matching.

In our ED, RSI is a standard approach for pediatric intubations and is usually performed by emergency medicine residents or fellows supervised by attending physicians. All residents and fellows undergo structured training in airway management, including simulation and supervised clinical intubations. Commonly used induction agents include ketamine and benzodiazepines, while atracurium and succinylcholine are the most frequently employed paralytics. There is no rigid departmental protocol mandating a specific drug regimen, and final medication choice is determined by the attending physician based on the clinical status of the patient.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were assessed for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test and summarized as mean with standard deviations (SD) or medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs). The Wilcoxon rank-sum test was used for group comparisons of skewed continuous data. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages and analyzed using Chi-square, Fisher’s exact, or Mann-Whitney U test, as appropriate.

Univariate and multivariate logistic regression were performed to estimate both unadjusted and adjusted odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Variables with p ≤ 0.25 in univariate analysis were considered for inclusion in the multivariable model, with a significant threshold set at p ≤ 0.05. All statistical analyses were conducted using R (version 4.4.2).

Results

A total number of 1031 pediatric patients underwent intubation in the ED between January 01, 2019, to June 30, 2023. Among these patients, 25 patients (2.4%) met the inclusion criteria. To establish a comprehensive analysis, 100 patients were randomly selected from the same cohort who did not experience peri-intubation CA, creating a ratio of 1 case to 4 controls. This resulted in a total study population of 125 patients.

Infants represent the highest proportion of the patients who underwent peri-intubation CA (13, 52%), with predominantly male gender (16, 64%). No significant difference was observed between the age group (p = 0.40) and gender distribution (p = 0.10). Patients who had a fever and vomiting with diarrhea were significantly higher in number in the peri-intubation CA group (p < 0.05) (Table 1). The return of spontaneous circulation was achieved in 10 cases (40%), with survival to discharge in 4 (16%) cases.

Patients in the cardiac arrest group demonstrated higher physiologic instability markers. A greater percentage exhibited elevated heart rate (80% vs. 52%, p = 0.011), low systolic blood pressure (68% vs. 45%, p = 0.04), elevated shock index (SI) ≥ 1.2 (72% vs. 44%, p = 0.012), elevated modified shock index (MSI ≥ 1.3: 92% vs. 65%, p = 0.008), and lactate ≥ 2 mmol/L (80% vs. 53%, p = 0.014).

Furthermore, oxygen saturation levels were significantly lower in patients who experienced peri-intubation CA compared to those who did not (73.4% ± 18 vs. 86.5% ± 17.9, p = 0.001). All patients in the cardiac arrest group had a capillary refill time exceeding three seconds, in contrast to 75% in the non-arrest cohort (p = 0.005).

For pharmacological agents used during intubation, there was no significant difference between the groups related to any sedative or paralytic agent used for intubation. Similarly, the provider’s level of training in performing intubation, the underlying reason for intubation, and the number of intubation attempts didn’t show any significant variation between the two cohorts (Table 2).

We selected 28 variables for univariate analysis, and those with a p-value of ≤ 0.25 for subsequent analysis. (Supplementary Table 1). Multivariate logistic regression analysis identified several independent predictors associated with peri-intubation CA. Infants aged less than one year demonstrated significantly higher odds of experiencing cardiac arrest during intubation (aOR: 4.37, p = 0.042). Similarly, hypotension before intubation, indicated by a low systolic blood pressure, was a strong predictor (aOR: 6.56, p = 0.043). Elevated heart rate was also associated with increased odds of arrest (aOR: 1.42, p = 0.045), as was hypoxemia, defined by oxygen saturation below 92% (aOR: 19.81, p = 0.003).

Furthermore, both traditional and modified shock indices were significant indicators of risk. An elevated shock index was linked to a fivefold increase in the likelihood of cardiac arrest (aOR: 5.52, p = 0.046), while an elevated modified shock index was associated with even higher odds (aOR: 6.27, p = 0.012 ). Elevated lactate levels (≥ 2 mmol/L) also emerged as a strong predictor (aOR: 10.09, p = 0.047), reflecting significant underlying metabolic stress before intubation. Additionally, a capillary refill time greater than three seconds was significantly associated with cardiac arrest (aOR: 5.78, p = 0.038), further reinforcing the importance of bedside indicators of perfusion in assessing risk before intubation (Table 3).

Discussion

The study identified several risk factors associated with peri-intubation CA in pediatric patients presented to the ED with particular emphasis on pre-intubation hemodynamic parameters. These findings highlight the critical importance of optimizing the physiological status of patients undergoing endotracheal intubation and align with the existing literature on pediatric airway management.

The incidence of peri-intubation cardiac arrest in our cohort (2.4%) was on the lower end of the range reported in prior pediatric studies (1.7–7%) [13–15]. This relatively lower rate may be attributed to consistent clinical practices within our emergency department that prioritize early recognition of hemodynamic instability and timely pre-intubation resuscitative measures. Although no formal airway management protocol is rigidly enforced, the presence of experienced providers and routine implementation of stabilization strategies may have contributed to improved physiological optimization before intubation.

In contrast, the survival outcomes were lower than many reported IHCA cohorts (32% to 43%) [17, 18] This likely reflects that the arrests that do occur in the ED represent a highly selected, severely ill subgroup (profound pre-intubation hypoxia or shock) with limited physiologic reserve, and not failures of procedural technique. In addition, differences in case mix, the acute nature of ED presentations, limited immediate access to advanced post-resuscitation supports, and the small number of events in our sample likely contributed to the lower survival observed.

Infants represented a disproportionately high number of patients who experienced peri-intubation CA in our study. This finding is consistent with previous literature suggesting that younger age, particularly less than one year, is associated with increased risk due to limited physiological reserves, increased metabolic demands, and challenging airway anatomy​ [19, 20]. A study done by Pokrajac et al. showed that age below one year was an independent predictor of peri-intubation CA in a pediatric ED setting, a finding consistent with our multivariable model [13].

Hemodynamic instability, manifested by hypotension, tachycardia, and elevated shock indices, was strongly associated with peri-intubation CA in our study. This observation is consistent with multiple studies conducted in both adult and pediatric populations, which have consistently shown the link between pre-intubation circulatory compromise and adverse peri-intubation outcomes [3, 14, 21, 22]. While the traditional shock index has been validated as a predictor of peri-intubation CA in both age groups [13, 23], , the role of the MSI has been primarily explored in adult studies, where it has shown a strong correlation with intubation-related cardiac events [24, 25]. However, there remains a paucity of literature regarding the predictive value of MSI in pediatric patients. Our findings suggest that MSI could serve as a rapid bedside screening tool to identify children at risk of hemodynamic collapse during airway management. Incorporating MSI into pre-intubation safety checklists may help clinicians recognize unstable patients earlier and implement targeted resuscitative interventions before intubation.

Pre-intubation hypoxemia has been consistently identified as a significant risk factor for peri-intubation cardiac arrest across several pediatric studies [13–15]. Hypoxemia before airway intervention compromises cardiovascular stability and reduces the margin for physiologic tolerance during laryngoscopy and induction. Our findings are consistent with these observations, reinforcing the strong association between pre-intubation oxygen desaturation and cardiac arrest risk. In particular, Pokrajac et al. 15 highlighted that pre-intubation hypoxemia or an unobtainable pulse oximetry reading was the most powerful predictor of peri-intubation CA in a pediatric emergency department cohort. These results emphasize the critical importance of maximizing oxygenation before intubation, including the use of strategies such as preoxygenation and apneic oxygenation, especially in high-risk pediatric patients [26].

Elevated lactate levels and prolonged CRT are both markers of impaired perfusion, were significantly associated with peri-intubation cardiac arrest in our study. While the association between hyperlactatemia and peri-intubation adverse events, [25, 27, 28] particularly hypotension and cardiac arrest, has been well-documented in adult populations, the literature is scarce in pediatric emergency settings. Our findings suggest that lactate, as a marker of systemic hypoperfusion, may hold similar predictive value in children and could be incorporated into risk stratification before intubation.

Prolonged CRT has been traditionally considered a quick and non-invasive indicator of circulatory status, particularly in resource-limited or time-sensitive settings. However, its role in predicting peri-intubation deterioration has not been extensively studied in pediatric emergency care. To date, we have identified only one study conducted in a pediatric ED context that demonstrated a significant association between delayed CRT and increased risk of peri-intubation CA​.15 Although CRT is inherently subjective, its ease of use and potential predictive value warrant further prospective evaluation in larger pediatric cohorts.

Strengths and limitations

One of the major strengths of our study is its contribution to the limited body of literature exploring the risk factors of peri-intubation CA in critically ill pediatric patients. The study offers important insights that can inform airway management practices in resource-limited settings by focusing on pre-intubation physiological and clinical variables. Recognizing these markers allows clinicians to optimize oxygenation and perfusion and ensure that the most experienced provider manages high-risk airways. These measures are especially relevant in low- and middle-income settings, where limited resources and variable expertise heighten the risk of peri-intubation complications.

This study has several limitations. It was conducted at a single tertiary care center, which may limit its generalizability to other settings, particularly those with different patient populations or resource availability. As it is a retrospective study, the reliance on medical records introduces potential issues related to documentation quality and data completeness. In particular, there was a lack of information regarding pre-intubation interventions such as fluid resuscitation, vasopressor use, and difficult airway assessments, all of which have been known to be associated with adverse peri-intubation events, including bradycardia, hypoxemia, and hypotension. Furthermore, differences in age, gender, comorbidities, and presenting complaints between cases and controls were not adjusted for, potentially introducing residual confounding. Additionally, the choice of a case–control design, while methodologically appropriate for the low event rate, may have introduced selection bias in the process of control selection. Post-intubation variables, such as hypotension, hypoxia, or aspiration events, were also not evaluated, which limits the ability to assess contributors to cardiac arrest. Lastly, the relatively small sample size may have restricted the statistical power to detect associations for less frequent exposures, as reflected by the wide confidence intervals in some of the adjusted analyses.

Despite these limitations, the study provides important hypothesis-generating data. The identified risk factors warrant validation in larger, multicenter, prospective cohorts to confirm their predictive value and inform future risk stratification tools in pediatric airway management.

Conclusion

In this study, we identified potential risk factors of peri-intubation CA among critically ill pediatric patients presenting to the ED of our hospital. We found infantile age, hypoxemia, elevated lactate levels, and hemodynamic instability as significant predictors of peri-intubation cardiac arrest. These findings highlight the importance of early recognition and correction of hemodynamic and oxygenation abnormalities before intubation. Future large-scale, prospective studies are needed to validate these predictors and guide preventive strategies in pediatric emergency airway management.
