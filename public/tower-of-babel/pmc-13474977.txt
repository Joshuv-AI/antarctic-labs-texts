Temporal epidemiological characteristics of acute aortic dissection incidence patterns in east China: a retrospective analysis of 1,038 patients
Authors: Fei Sun, Haiyan Wang, Teng Cai, Lijun Gan, Yuntao Cheng, Fanhua Meng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study aims to investigate the temporal biological distribution characteristics of acute aortic dissection (AAD) onset in East China, analyze differences in onset patterns between patients with different types (Stanford Type A and Type B) and genders, and provide regional epidemiological evidence to inform our understanding of the temporal distribution of AAD in East China.MethodClinical data were retrospectively collected from 1,038 patients with confirmed AAD diagnosed between January 2009 and September 2025. SPSS 27.0 software was employed to analyze temporal distribution patterns of onset, including seasonal, monthly, weekly, and daily rhythmic characteristics, whilst investigating the influence of gender and disease subtype on temporal biological features.ResultA total of 1,038 patients with AAD were included, comprising 571 cases (55.0%) of Stanford Type A AAD and 467 cases (45.0%) of Stanford Type B AAD; 767 were male (73.9%), with a mean age of 55.7 ± 13.5 years. Circadian patterns showed that 17:00–20:00 was the peak period for onset (21.4%), whilst 01:00–04:00 was the trough (12.5%); females exhibited more pronounced fluctuations across certain time scales. Regarding weekly distribution, Stanford Type B cases had the lowest proportion on Saturdays (11.8%); the weekly distribution of female patients showed a certain degree of variation. At the monthly and seasonal levels, cases were concentrated in January, February, March, November and December; the incidence was highest in winter (32.6%), followed by autumn (25.4%), spring (24.6%) and summer (17.4%). Seasonal differences were statistically significant (P < 0.0001), and the seasonal fluctuations in Stanford Type A AAD were greater than those in Stanford Type B AAD. Furthermore, significant differences were observed between patients of different subtypes in terms of gender distribution, history of smoking and alcohol consumption, and the prevalence of comorbidities (P < 0.05).ConclusionThe incidence of AAD in East China exhibits distinct circadian patterns, with potential variations across different subtypes and gender groups at certain temporal scales. These findings provide an epidemiological basis for understanding the temporal distribution of AAD incidence in East China and for the preparation of clinical resources.

Introduction

Acute aortic dissection (AAD) is a cardiovascular condition characterized by sudden onset and extremely high mortality. It is classified into Stanford type A and Stanford type B AAD based on whether the ascending aorta is involved [1–3]. Globally, the incidence of AAD ranges from 3 to 5 cases per 100,000 people annually [4, 5]. In recent years, circadian rhythms across multiple timescales—daily, weekly, monthly, and seasonal—have been demonstrated to be significantly associated with the risk of developing AAD [1, 6, 7]. The underlying mechanisms involve circadian misalignment, abrupt shifts in behavioral patterns, and fluctuations in external factors such as ambient temperature and atmospheric pressure [2]. However, existing research predominantly focuses on Western populations, with few Chinese cases included in samples. Furthermore, there is a lack of detailed temporal epidemiological data on Stanford A and B subtypes of AAD stratified by gender. This limits the generalizability of current conclusions and hinders the provision of region-specific epidemiological evidence for China’s temperate monsoon climate zones. Therefore, systematically analyzing the diurnal, weekly, monthly, and seasonal distribution characteristics of AAD in the Chinese population, and further elucidating temporal differences between subtypes and genders, has become crucial for filling evidence gaps and refining region-specific understanding of temporal patterns. This study aims to systematically analyse the temporal patterns of AAD incidence in East China and other regions characterised by a temperate monsoon climate at hourly, daily, monthly and seasonal scales and to further investigate the heterogeneity of incidence across different anatomical subtypes and gender groups. The objective is to refine the epidemiological data on the temporal distribution of AAD incidence in East China, thereby providing a reference for subsequent multicentre studies in this field.

Study population and methods

Study population

This retrospective study, utilizing the hospital’s electronic medical records system, included 1,038 patients diagnosed with AAD between January 2009 and September 2025. Inclusion criteria: (1) Age ≥ 18 years; (2) Diagnosis of aortic dissection according to the 2022 ESC guidelines; (3) Precise traceability of symptom onset time (to the hour); (4) Time from symptom onset to presentation ≤ 14 days. Exclusion criteria: (1) Uncertain onset time of symptoms; (2) Concurrent connective tissue disorders such as Marfan syndrome or Ehlers-Danlos syndrome, or iatrogenic/traumatic dissection; (3) Conditions with similar imaging features such as isolated intramural hematoma or penetrating aortic ulcer.

Data collection

This study systematically characterizes the temporal distribution rhythms of AAD symptom onset across four temporal scales—seasonal, monthly, weekly, and diurnal—using the onset time point as the entry point. Seasonal divisions are based on the temperate monsoon climate characteristics of the study region: spring (March–May), summer (June–August), autumn (September–November), and winter (December–February of the following year). The intraday rhythm was segmented into 6 × 4-hour equidistant intervals: 01:00–04:00, 05:00–08:00, 09:00–12:00, 13:00–16:00, 17:00–20:00, and 21:00–24:00 (Beijing Time, UTC + 8). This study utilises monthly distribution analysis to provide a detailed picture of the annual trends in AAD incidence, employs seasonal distribution to summarise the overall patterns of clustering, and uses weekly distribution to further examine potential fluctuations in incidence across different days of the week. Concurrently, stratified analyses were conducted according to the Stanford A/B classification and gender to assess the influence of anatomical subtypes and gender on the chronobiological characteristics of AAD. To enhance the generalisability of the findings and minimise redundancy between analyses at different temporal scales, this study further employs a classification based on working days/non-working days and daytime/night-time to provide supplementary insights into the distribution of cases within the week and throughout the day.

The following variables were extracted via standardized electronic case report forms: (1) Demographic data: age, gender; (2) Temporal information: date and time of symptom onset (24-hour format), location of onset, time of admission; (3) Risk factors: history of hypertension, coronary heart disease, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, and lifestyle behaviors such as smoking and alcohol consumption. All time data were recorded according to Beijing Time (UTC + 8) and independently verified by two researchers. Discrepancies were resolved by consulting original imaging and nursing records.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed using SPSS 27.0 statistical software. Descriptive statistics for categorical variables were presented as frequencies (n) and proportions, whilst continuous variables were presented as means ± standard deviations (x ± s). For comparisons of categorical variables between groups, the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test was selected according to the data type. Furthermore, to address the multiple comparisons across the various subgroups (overall, male, female, Stanford types A/B) and time scales (intraday, weekly, monthly, and seasonal) involved in this study, the Benjamini–Hochberg method was used for false discovery rate (FDR) correction in subgroup and supplementary analyses; a p-value of q < 0.05 was considered statistically significant following correction. To further assess the independent associations between gender, Stanford classification and major clinical characteristics with the temporal distribution of AAD onset, this study employed multivariate logistic regression analysis. The dependent variables were whether the event occurred during the evening peak (17:00–20:00) and whether the event occurred in winter, with age, sex, Stanford classification, and clinical factors such as smoking, alcohol consumption, and hypertension included as covariates. Odds ratios (OR) and 95% confidence intervals (95% CI) were reported. Regarding the handling of missing values, cases with missing data for the key variable (time of onset) were excluded. For the remaining variables, where there were only a small number of missing values, a complete-cases analysis was performed without imputing missing values. All statistical tests were two-sided, and a P-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Basic characteristics of the study population

In this study, 2,089 cases of aortic dissection (AD) were initially screened using the hospital’s electronic medical records system. Following a two-stage selection process, 1,038 patients with typical symptoms and radiologically confirmed acute aortic dissection (AAD) were ultimately included (Fig. 1). In the first stage, 561 cases were excluded (178 due to missing onset time and 383 due to lack of a clear diagnosis), leaving 1,528 cases with a clear diagnosis and complete imaging data; In the second stage, a further 490 cases were excluded (119 cases without acute symptoms, 14 cases of traumatic dissection, 29 cases of congenital anomalies, 5 cases of iatrogenic dissection, and 323 cases of isolated intramural haematoma or penetrating atherosclerotic ulceration). Among the included patients, 571 (55.0%) were Stanford type A and 467 (45.0%) were type B; 767 (73.9%) were male, with a mean age of 55.7 ± 13.5 years, and 623 (60.0%) were aged less than 60 years. Regarding behavioural factors, the prevalence of smoking and alcohol consumption was 53.8% and 49.2%, respectively; the prevalence of concomitant hypertension, coronary heart disease and diabetes was 81.9% (95% CI 79.4%–84.1%), 15.0% and 5.4%, respectively. There were statistically significant differences between patients of different subtypes in terms of gender distribution, smoking, alcohol consumption, hypertension and coronary heart disease (Table 1).

Daily and weekly distribution of AAD onset times

The weekday distribution of AAD is illustrated in (Fig. 2). Overall, no significant weekday–weekend difference was observed (χ²=10.95, df = 6, p = 0.09), nor was there heterogeneity in Stanford Type A AAD (χ²=6.67, df = 6, p = 0.324). The distribution of onset days for Stanford Type B AAD showed significant fluctuations (χ² = 14.77, df = 6, P = 0.022), with only 55 cases occurring on Saturdays, accounting for 11.8% (95% CI 9.2%–15.0%), which was significantly lower than on other days; this difference remained statistically significant after FDR correction. Analysis by gender revealed that female patients may exhibit a distribution trend of ‘relatively higher incidence at the beginning of the week and relatively lower at the weekend’; however, after FDR correction, this trend did not reach statistical significance. Therefore, this study provisionally regards it as an observed distribution trend rather than a stable statistical association. Further supplementary analyses were conducted using the classification of working days/non-working days and daytime/night-time. The results of the overall and subgroup analyses were generally consistent with the trends observed in the original weekly and daily distribution analyses.

Dissection and seasonal distribution

Monthly and seasonal distribution of AAD incidence

The incidence of AAD exhibited significant monthly variation (χ²=60.76, df = 11, p < 0.001, Fig. 4). The annual trough occurred in June, with fewer than half the cases seen at the December peak. This trend held after stratification by subtype and gender: persistently low incidence from May to August, with relatively higher rates from January to March and November to December. Females exhibited a slight anomaly, showing a single peak in March (38 cases, accounting for 14% of all female cases). Seasonal analysis further validated this distribution: winter (338 cases, 32.6%) > autumn (264 cases) > spring (255 cases) > summer (181 cases, 17.4%), with highly significant differences (χ²=47.73, df = 3, P < 0.0001, Table 3). Stanford B-type AAD exhibited smaller seasonal fluctuations than Stanford A-type AAD. Regarding gender, winter incidence was significantly higher among males. Female Stanford A-type AAD showed extreme seasonal variation, whereas seasonal composition of Stanford B-type AAD was not statistically significant (χ²=1.22, df = 3, P = 0.748, Fig. 5). Additional analyses based on working-day/non-working-day and daytime/night-time classifications are presented in Figs. 6 and 7, respectively.

Multivariate regression analysis of the time of onset of aortic dissection and clinical variables

To further assess the independent association between the primary temporal distribution characteristics of AAD and clinical variables, this study constructed multivariable logistic regression models, using whether AAD occurred during the evening rush hour (17:00–20:00) and whether it occurred in winter as dependent variables. The results showed that, after adjusting for confounding factors such as age, Stanford classification, smoking, alcohol consumption, hypertension, coronary heart disease, diabetes and pulmonary disease, there was no significant independent association between gender and whether AAD occurred during the evening peak period (OR = 0.81, 95% CI: 0.53–1.25, P = 0.350); similarly, the Stanford classification showed no significant independent association with occurrence during this period (OR = 0.85, 95% CI: 0.62–1.15, P = 0.293). In models where the occurrence of AAD during winter was the dependent variable, neither gender (OR = 0.99, 95% CI: 0.68–1.45, P = 0.978) nor the Stanford classification (OR = 0.91, 95% CI: 0.70–1.19, P = 0.500) showed a significant independent association, whereas diabetes was significantly and independently negatively associated with winter onset of AAD (OR = 0.43, 95% CI: 0.21–0.88, P = 0.020) (Table 4).

Discussion

In this single-center retrospective study of 1,038 patients with AAD in East China, we found that 81.9% (95%CI 79.4%–84.1%) of cases had concomitant hypertension, whereas only 5.4% had concomitant diabetes. These findings reaffirm the epidemiological status of hypertension as an independent risk factor for AAD [8, 9] and further support the hypothesis that diabetes may exert a protective effect against AAD [10, 11]. Moreover, regardless of whether it is Stanford Type A or B aortic dissection, males constitute the overwhelming majority. The underlying mechanisms may be associated with men’s higher exposure to risk factors such as smoking and alcohol consumption, as well as the adverse biological effects of androgens on the aortic wall. Basic research indicates that androgens may promote vascular smooth muscle cell proliferation and increase vascular tension, thereby reducing aortic wall compliance and rendering it more susceptible to tearing under hemodynamic fluctuations [12].

This study confirms that the onset of AAD exhibits a markedly non-random distribution across multiple temporal scales. While partially consistent with the ‘morning peak’ and ‘winter peak’ patterns reported in European and American cohorts [13–15], it also demonstrates pronounced regional variations. Previous multicenter studies have consistently indicated that events such as ischaemic stroke, acute myocardial infarction, and sudden cardiac death exhibit a morning peak occurring between 06:00 and 12:00 [16]; However, this study indicates that the primary diurnal peak occurs between 17:00 and 20:00. This rhythm may be associated with the following factors: ① During the evening period, daytime activities have not yet fully subsided, sympathetic output remains elevated, both heart rate and blood pressure increase, [17] and aortic wall shear stress rises abruptly; ② Dinner, social engagements, and light physical activity are concentrated during this period. 65% of AAD episodes are triggered by exertion or exercise, exhibiting significant overlap with the peak window [18, 19]; ③ Behavioral factors such as alcohol consumption and high-fat diets compound the effects, further exacerbating vascular endothelial injury and amplifying blood pressure fluctuations, thereby synergistically inducing intimal tears [20]. With regard to the Stanford classification, the proportion of Stanford type B AAD cases occurring during the 01:00–04:00 and 21:00–24:00 time periods was slightly higher than that for type A, suggesting that there may be some differences in the distribution of nocturnal onset times across different Stanford subtypes. Multivariate logistic regression analysis also showed that, after adjusting for relevant clinical confounding factors, neither gender nor the evening peak nor winter onset showed an independent association. Therefore, the partial gender differences observed in the stratified analysis should be regarded as the result of the combined effects of multiple factors, rather than being independently determined by gender itself.

AAD exhibits a pronounced seasonal pattern with higher incidence in winter and lower in summer: winter cases account for 32.6% of annual occurrences, whereas summer accounts for only 17.4%. This correlates with cold stimulation activating the sympathetic nervous system-renin-angiotensin axis, peripheral vasoconstriction, and elevated blood pressure peaks [21]. Reduced synthesis of 25-hydroxyvitamin D due to insufficient winter ultraviolet radiation may further contribute to elevated blood pressure [22]. Large-scale meteorological-epidemiological studies have confirmed that both low ambient temperatures and abrupt temperature drops over several days independently increase the risk of AAD [23, 24]. Notably, female Stanford Class A AAD patients exhibit an isolated peak incidence in March (accounting for 14% of annual cases among women), a phenomenon not observed in Caucasian populations. This suggests East Asian women may possess unique seasonal susceptibility factors, potentially linked to estrogen-vasotonic interactions and differences in hypertension management. Multicenter prospective studies are urgently required to validate these findings. Overall, the weekly distribution of AAD showed no significant variation; however, contrary to the findings reported by Liang et al. [25], only 11.8% of Stanford Type B cases in this study presented on Saturdays, significantly below the daily average. Further stratified analysis revealed that the incidence among female patients tended to be higher at the beginning of the week and lower at the weekend; however, after FDR correction, this difference was not statistically significant. These findings are exploratory in nature and require validation through studies with larger sample sizes. This phenomenon may relate to altered behavioral patterns over weekends: work-related stress peaks from Monday to Wednesday, activating the sympathetic-adrenal axis and exacerbating blood pressure fluctuations, thereby increasing the risk of dissection. Conversely, weekend relaxation and reduced psychological pressure stabilize blood pressure, lowering the incidence risk [26, 27]. From a multi-timescale perspective, the onset patterns of AAD exhibit hierarchical characteristics ranging from the macro to the micro level: seasonal distribution reflects the overall clustering trend in incidence, weekly distribution reveals short-term fluctuations, whilst monthly distribution highlights local variations within the annual pattern. The overall trends observed in the monthly and seasonal analyses are largely consistent, but the former suggests more nuanced temporal differences within certain subgroups; for instance, female patients exhibit a peak in incidence in March, and their temporal distribution is not entirely uniform. Following validation stratified by working days/non-working days and daytime/night-time, the primary temporal distribution characteristics of AAD did not undergo any substantial changes, suggesting that the core findings of this study regarding the temporal rhythm of onset demonstrate good stability.

This study has several limitations. Firstly, as this is a single-centre retrospective study, the data were sourced from the hospital’s electronic medical record system; some clinical information relies on medical records and patient self-reports, which may introduce a degree of information bias. Although cases with missing onset dates were excluded and a small number of missing data points were analysed using the complete cases method, the records for some clinical variables remain incomplete. Secondly, the study included only cases where the onset date could be clearly traced and excluded traumatic, iatrogenic and certain special types of cases. Whilst this enhanced the consistency of the temporal distribution analysis, it reduced the representativeness of the sample to some extent. Thirdly, the sample size of this study represents the actual number of eligible cases included during the study period; no prospective sample size estimation was conducted. Consequently, the sample size for some subgroups is small, limiting statistical power. Fourthly, existing databases lack information on blood pressure control, medication adherence, occupational exposure and pharmacological interventions. Adjustment for potential confounding factors remains inadequate, and the relevant conclusions require further validation in future multicentre, large-scale studies.

Conclusion

The incidence of AAD in East China exhibits distinct temporal patterns, with potential differences across subtypes and gender groups at certain time scales. These findings provide region-specific epidemiological evidence for understanding the temporal distribution of AAD in East China and may help inform clinical resource allocation and future preventive strategies. Further multicentre studies are needed to confirm these observations.
