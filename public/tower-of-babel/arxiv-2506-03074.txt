GL-LowPopArt : A Nearly Instance-Wise Minimax-Optimal Estimator for Generalized Low-Rank Trace Regression
Junghyun Lee, Kyoungseok Jang, Kwang-Sung Jun, Milan Vojnović, Se-Young Yun

Abstract
We present GL-LowPopArt , a novel Catoni-style estimator for generalized low-rank trace regression. Building on LowPopArt ( Jang et al. 2024 ) , it employs a two-stage approach: nuclear norm regularization followed by matrix Catoni estimation. We establish state-of-the-art estimation error bounds, surpassing existing guarantees ( Fan et al. 2019 ; Kang et al. 2022 ) , and reveal a novel experimental design objective, GL ⁡ ( π ) \mathrm{GL}(\pi) . The key technical challenge is controlling bias from the nonlinear inverse link function, which we address by our two-stage approach. We prove a local minimax lower bound, showing that our GL-LowPopArt enjoys instance-wise optimality up to the condition number of the ground-truth Hessian. Applications include generalized linear matrix completion, where GL-LowPopArt achieves a state-of-the-art Frobenius error guarantee, and bilinear dueling bandits , a novel setting inspired by general preference learning ( Zhang et al. 2024b ) . Our analysis of a GL-LowPopArt -based explore-then-commit algorithm reveals a new, potentially interesting problem-dependent quantity, along with improved Borda regret bound than vectorization ( Wu et al. 2024 ) .

Main Contributions.

While prior work has made significant progress, a crucial aspect has been overlooked: the instance-specific nature of curvature.
To our knowledge, all the existing analyses rely on worst-case bounds for curvature, neglecting its variation and obscuring the problem’s true difficulty.
For example, known performance guarantees for generalized linear matrix completion depend inversely w.r.t.
\min_{|z|\leq\gamma}\dot{\mu}(z)
, where
\gamma>0
is such that
\max_{i,j}\left|(\bm{\Theta}_{\star})_{ij}\right|\leq\gamma
and
\dot{\mu}
is the derivative of the inverse link function.
For instance, when
\mu(z)=(1+e^{-z})^{-1}

e^{\gamma}

(
Faury et al. 2020
)

This dependency is instance-
independent
, in the sense that it arises from the worst-case
\dot{\mu}
over the entry-wise domain
[-\gamma,\gamma]

\Theta_{\star}
.

Overview of

We present

It takes two designs

When the learner is in the adaptive learning scenario, she can (and will) choose

3.3
Knowledge of the GLM and Model Misspecification

Our algorithm design and analysis assume a well-specified GLM, a common assumption in the statistical and bandit literature.
Addressing model misspecification typically requires fundamentally different techniques
(
Lattimore & Szepesvári 2020
, Chapter 24.4)
, as it can introduce challenges such as biased estimates and reduced efficiency; see
Fortunati et al. 2017
for a survey.
In particular, under misspecification, the Stage I MLE is known to converge not to the true
\bm{\Theta}_{\star}
, but to the KL projection of the assumed model class onto the true data-generating distribution
(
White 1982
)
.
As a result, the Stage I initialization may be significantly biased, and this bias may not vanish even as
N_{1}
increases.
Consequently, the refined estimator from Stage II can suffer a persistent error due to this bias.

That said, our method may still tolerate mild forms of misspecification.
For example, in the Gaussian case, an overestimation of the noise variance
\sigma^{2}
leads to a larger choice of the regularization parameter
\lambda_{N_{1}}
in Stage I, which results in a conservative but still statistically consistent estimate.
4
4

4

For certain applications, such as noisy matrix completion, one could utilize an alternate adaptive estimator, such as the square root LASSO-type estimator proposed in
Klopp 2014
.

In such cases, the Stage I output may remain sufficiently close to
\bm{\Theta}_{\star}
for Stage II to provide effective refinement.

We leave to future work exploring robustness to more general model misspecifications, or designing variants of
GL-LowPopArt
that explicitly account for GLM uncertainty – such as through Bayesian methods
(
Walker 2013
)
or misspecification-robust estimators
(
Robins et al. 1994
)
.

Our lower bound explicitly depends on the “optimistic” instance-specific curvature,

Comparing our lower bound with the performance guarantee of

This stands in contrast to the nuclear norm-regularized estimator, which achieves at best a rate of
\widetilde{{\mathcal{O}}}\left(\frac{(d_{1}\vee d_{2})d_{1}d_{2}r}{\kappa^{2}\lambda_{\min}({\bm{V}}(\pi_{2}))N}\right)

\pi_{2}
(see
Theorem

3.4
and
Appendix

); note the additional factor of

, which corresponds to the worst-case curvature.
As a result, although the nuclear norm-regularized estimator is nearly instance-wise optimal in the linear setting
(
Rohde & Tsybakov 2011
;
Koltchinskii et al. 2011
)
, it fails to achieve such optimality in the nonlinear GLM case.
This underscores the strength of our method,
GL-LowPopArt
, which is nearly instance-wise optimal across all GLMs satisfying Assumption
3
.

Requirement on
N
.

A keen reader may observe that our local minimax lower bound holds under the condition
N\gtrsim\frac{R_{s}^{2}r(d_{1}\vee d_{2})}{\lambda_{\max}({\bm{H}}(\pi;\bm{\Theta}_{\star}))}
.
We emphasize that this requirement is not restrictive and actually provides an intuitive justification for Stage I as a warm-up phase; in fact, we believe that some condition of this form on
N
is necessary—although we do not currently have a formal proof.
The requirement on
N
arises when bounding the KL divergence between the true model
\bm{\Theta}_{\star}
and an alternative model from the constructed local packing.
Intuitively, this stems from the necessity for the two models to be sufficiently close for self-concordance properties to take effect; this was also the case for prior local minimax lower bounds
(
Abeille et al. 2021
, Theorem 2)

(
Simchowitz & Foster 2020
, Theorem 1)
, where the requirement on horizon length
T
arises in a similar fashion.
Finally, we point out that in the linear setting (i.e.,
\mu(z)=z\Rightarrow R_{s}=0
), our requirement on
N
vanishes.

5.1

In

{\mathcal{A}}={\mathcal{X}}=\{{\bm{e}}_{i}({\bm{e}}_{j}^{\prime})^{\top}:(i,j)\in[d_{1}]\times[d_{2}]\}

Here, we focus on the

(

Let us denote

We first compare the error bound of

6
Conclusion and Future Work

This work addresses the critical gap in prior work by explicitly considering instance-specific curvature in generalized low-rank trace regression.
We introduce
GL-LowPopArt
, a novel estimator that achieves state-of-the-art performance, adapting to both the nonlinearity of the model and the underlying arm-set geometry.
We establish the first instance-wise minimax lower bound, demonstrating the near-optimality of
GL-LowPopArt
.
We showcase its benefits through applications to generalized linear matrix completion and bilinear dueling bandits, a novel setting of independent interest for general preference learning
(
Zhang et al. 2024b
)
.

Other than the future directions mentioned in the main text, another is deriving an instance-wise improved estimator for other structures, such as row (column)-wise sparsity
(
Zhao & Leng 2014
)
or even their superposition
(
Yang & Ravikumar 2013
;
Oymak et al. 2015
;
Richard et al. 2012
;
Zhao et al. 2017
)
.
A promising starting point for this is to extend
PopArt

(
Jang et al. 2022
)
to the sparse trace regression.

)
, and even with the only assumption of bounded variance
(
Klopp 2014
)
.
We refer interested readers to
Davenport & Romberg 2016
for an overview of works on matrix completion.
Note that our model implicitly implies that for each
(i,j)\in[d_{1}]\times[d_{2}]
may be observed multiple times, which is often the case in recommender systems and bandits where the same item can be recommended multiple times for exploration, or it may be that “users are more active than others
and popular items are rated more frequently.”
(
Klopp et al. 2015
)
.
On a slightly different note, many works have explored the same setting under the assumption that each entry of

can be sampled at most once

Lu & Negahban 2015

Rajkumar & Agarwal 2016
;

;
Zhang et al. 2024b
)
.

Burer-Monteiro Factorization

The Burer–Monteiro factorization (BMF,
Burer & Monteiro 2003
;
Burer & Monteiro 2005
) approach has been extensively studied for noiseless low-rank matrix recovery from deterministic linear measurements
(
Candès & Recht 2009
;
Candès & Plan 2011
)
, primarily from an optimization perspective
(
Bi et al. 2022
;
Ge et al. 2017
;
Park et al. 2017
;
Zhang et al. 2024a
;
Boumal et al. 2016
;
Yalçın et al. 2022
;
Bhojanapalli et al. 2016
;
Stöger & Soltanolkotabi 2021
;
Kim & Chung 2023
)
.
In contrast, our work focuses on noisy matrix completion under a generalized linear model (GLM) framework, aiming to achieve accurate estimation with high probability as the sample size increases.
This fundamental difference in problem settings implies that the optimization complexity measures used to analyze BMF methods, such as the optimization complexity metric (OCM) introduced by
Yalçın et al. 2022
and
Zhang et al. 2024a
, are not directly comparable to our statistical analysis.
Specifically, their OCM quantifies the non-convexity of the BMF landscape, which is related to the success of local search methods (e.g., gradient descent), while our “statistical complexity metric”, arguably
\lambda_{\max}({\bm{H}}(\pi;\bm{\Theta}_{\star}))
that pops up in our lower bound (
Theorem

4.1
), is information-theoretic and dictates the minimum sample size required for any estimator to obtain a desired accuracy with high probability.

While BMF methods offer computational efficiency and have been shown to perform well empirically, especially in large-scale problems, they all rely on some non-convex optimization, whose landscape is not always guaranteed to be benign, especially in the presence of noise
(
Ma & Fattahi 2023
)
.
Our
GL-LowPopArt
only involves convex optimization subroutines and thus is computationally tractable, but inefficient: for instance,
GL-LowPopArt
requires computing the SVD and inverting
d^{2}\times d^{2}
matrices.
Therefore, while BMF and our work both address low-rank matrix recovery, their respective advantages depend on the specific problem context.

Low-Rank Matrix Bandits.

Researchers in low-rank bandits have long focused on fundamental and specific models. For example,

Kotłowski & Neu 2019
;

;
Huang et al. 2021
)
.
For bilinear bandits,
Kotłowski & Neu 2019
assumed that

The first study on low-rank bandits with general arm sets is

However, none of these studies explicitly addressed experimental design; rather, they handled the issue of experimental designs by assuming that their arm sets are sufficiently well-distributed in all directions. Recent work by

They then analyzed the experimental design assumptions underlying previous studies and successfully proved that their

Our paper further extends the

In this Appendix, let us denote
N=N_{1}

\displaystyle{\mathcal{L}}_{N}(\bm{\Theta})

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{t=1}^{N}\frac{m(\langle{\bm{X}}_{t},\bm{\Theta}\rangle)-y_{t}\langle{\bm{X}}_{t},\bm{\Theta}\rangle}{g(\tau)}

(15)

\displaystyle\bm{\Theta}_{0}

\displaystyle:=\argmin_{\bm{\Theta}\in\Omega}\left\{{\mathcal{L}}_{N}(\bm{\Theta})+\lambda_{N}\left\lVert\bm{\Theta}\right\rVert_{*}\right\}

(16)

C.1
Definition of RSC and Constraint Cone
{\mathcal{C}}

We first recall the definition of local restricted strong convexity (LRSC)
(
Negahban & Wainwright 2011
;
Negahban et al. 2012
;
Fan et al. 2018
;
Fan et al. 2019
)
:

Definition C.1
.

Let
\bm{\Theta}_{\star}\in\Omega\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{1}\times d_{2}}
be the ground-truth parameter of rank
r\leq d_{1}\wedge d_{2}
, and let us denote
{\mathcal{B}}^{d_{1}\times d_{2}}_{F}(W):=\{\bm{\Theta}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{1}\times d_{2}}:\left\lVert\bm{\Theta}\right\rVert_{F}\leq W\}
.
Let
{\mathcal{C}}\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{1}\times d_{2}}
be a constraint cone,
W,\xi>0
and
\tau\geq 0
.
A loss function
{\mathcal{L}}(\cdot)
satisfies
\mathrm{LRSC}({\mathcal{C}},W,\xi,\tau)
at
\bm{\Theta}_{\star}
if the following holds:

B^{s}_{\mathcal{L}}(\bm{\Theta}_{\star}+\Delta,\bm{\Theta}_{\star})\triangleq\frac{1}{2}\langle\nabla{\mathcal{L}}(\bm{\Theta}_{\star}+\Delta)-\nabla{\mathcal{L}}(\bm{\Theta}_{\star}),\Delta\rangle\geq\xi\left\lVert\Delta\right\rVert_{F}^{2}-\tau,\quad\forall\Delta\in{\mathcal{C}}\cap{\mathcal{B}}^{d_{1}\times d_{2}}_{F}(W),

(18)

where
B^{s}_{\mathcal{L}}(\cdot,\cdot)
is the symmetric Bregman divergence induced by
{\mathcal{L}}
.

Remark 7
.

The “original” definition of LRSC is in terms of the unsymmetric Bregman divergence and must hold for all points near
\bm{\Theta}_{\star}
, namely, for some neighborhood
{\mathcal{N}}
of
\bm{\Theta}_{\star}
,

B_{\mathcal{L}}(\bm{\Theta}+\Delta,\bm{\Theta})\triangleq{\mathcal{L}}(\bm{\Theta}+\Delta)-{\mathcal{L}}(\bm{\Theta})-\langle\nabla{\mathcal{L}}(\bm{\Theta}),\Delta\rangle\geq\xi\left\lVert\Delta\right\rVert_{F}^{2}-\tau,\quad\forall\Delta\in{\mathcal{C}},\ \forall\bm{\Theta}\in{\mathcal{N}}.

(19)

As one can see later, we only require the symmetric version for the final proof, and we only need the above to hold for
\bm{\Theta}=\bm{\Theta}_{\star}
.
Indeed, this is also the case in the proof of Theorem 1 of
Fan et al. 2019
.

We follow the proof strategy for Lemma 1 of
Negahban & Wainwright 2011
, part of which dates back to
Recht et al. 2010
.
Let
\bm{\Theta}_{\star}={\bm{U}}{\bm{D}}{\bm{V}}^{\top}
be its SVD,
{\bm{U}}_{r}
be the first
r
columns of
{\bm{U}}
, and
{\bm{U}}_{r}^{\perp}
be the remaining columns.
We define
{\bm{V}}_{r}
and
{\bm{V}}_{r}^{\perp}
analogously.
Note that as
\mathrm{rank}(\bm{\Theta}_{\star})=r
, the singular values corresponding to
{\bm{U}}_{r}^{\perp}
and
{\bm{V}}_{r}^{\perp}
are zero.
Define the two subspaces

\displaystyle{\mathcal{M}}

\displaystyle:=\left\{\bm{\Theta}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{1}\times d_{2}}:\mathrm{row}(\bm{\Theta})\subseteq\mathrm{row}({\bm{V}}_{r}),\mathrm{col}(\bm{\Theta})\subseteq\mathrm{col}({\bm{U}}_{r})\right\},

(20)

\displaystyle\overline{{\mathcal{M}}}^{\perp}

\displaystyle:=\left\{\bm{\Theta}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{1}\times d_{2}}:\mathrm{row}(\bm{\Theta})\perp\mathrm{row}({\bm{V}}_{r}),\mathrm{col}(\bm{\Theta})\perp\mathrm{col}({\bm{U}}_{r})\right\},

(21)

where
\mathrm{row}(\cdot)
and
\mathrm{col}(\cdot)
denote row and column spaces, respectively.

For any
\Delta\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{1}\times d_{2}}
, let
{\bm{U}}^{\top}\Delta{\bm{V}}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{\Gamma}_{11}(\Delta)&\bm{\Gamma}_{12}(\Delta)\\
\bm{\Gamma}_{21}(\Delta)&\bm{\Gamma}_{22}(\Delta)\end{bmatrix}
, where
\bm{\Gamma}_{11}(\Delta)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{r\times r}
,
\bm{\Gamma}_{22}(\Delta)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{(d-r)\times(d-r)}
,
\bm{\Gamma}_{12}(\Delta)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{r\times(d-r)}
, and
\bm{\Gamma}_{21}(\Delta)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{(d-r)\times r}
.
Then, one could consider the following decomposition:

\Delta=\underbrace{{\bm{U}}\begin{bmatrix}\bm{\Gamma}_{11}(\Delta)&\bm{\Gamma}_{12}(\Delta)\\
\bm{\Gamma}_{21}(\Delta)&{\bm{0}}\end{bmatrix}{\bm{V}}^{\top}}_{\triangleq\Delta_{\overline{{\mathcal{M}}}}}+{\bm{U}}\begin{bmatrix}{\bm{0}}&{\bm{0}}\\
{\bm{0}}&\bm{\Gamma}_{22}(\Delta)\end{bmatrix}{\bm{V}}^{\top}=\Delta_{\overline{{\mathcal{M}}}}+\begin{bmatrix}{\bm{0}}&{\bm{0}}\\
{\bm{0}}&\Delta_{\overline{{\mathcal{M}}}^{\perp}}\triangleq{\bm{Q}}_{d-2r}\bm{\Gamma}_{22}(\Delta){\bm{Q}}_{d-2r}^{\top}\end{bmatrix}.

(22)

Note that
\mathrm{rank}(\Delta_{\overline{{\mathcal{M}}}})\leq 2r
.

We then consider the following constraint cone:

{\mathcal{C}}(\bm{\Theta}_{\star}):=\left\{\Delta\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{1}\times d_{2}}:\left\lVert\Delta_{\overline{{\mathcal{M}}}^{\perp}}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{nuc}}\leq 3\left\lVert\Delta_{\overline{{\mathcal{M}}}}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{nuc}}\right\}.

(23)

C.2
{\mathcal{L}}_{N}
Satisfies LRSC With High Probability

We will now show that
{\mathcal{L}}_{N}
satisfies LRSC with high probability:

Lemma C.2
.

Let
W>0
be fixed, and suppose that
|\mathrm{supp}(\pi)|<\infty
.
Then, with probability at least
1-\frac{\delta}{2}
,
{\mathcal{L}}_{N}(\cdot)
satisfies
\mathrm{LRSC}({\mathcal{C}},W,\lambda_{\min}({\bm{H}}_{A}(\pi;\bm{\Theta}_{\star})),\tau(W))
with
\tau(W):=16rW^{2}R_{\max}\left(\sqrt{\frac{|\mathrm{supp}(\pi)|\log 2+\log\frac{2}{\delta}}{N}}+4\sqrt{2r}WR_{s}\right)
.

Proof.

Let
\Delta\in{\mathcal{C}}(\bm{\Theta}_{\star})\cap{\mathcal{B}}_{F}^{\mathrm{Skew}(d)}(W)
be arbitrary, and denote
\bm{\Theta}=\bm{\Theta}_{\star}+\Delta
.

Note that

\displaystyle\langle\nabla{\mathcal{L}}_{N}(\bm{\Theta})-\nabla{\mathcal{L}}_{N}(\bm{\Theta}_{\star}),\Delta\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\langle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{t=1}^{N}(\mu(\langle{\bm{X}}_{t},\bm{\Theta}\rangle)-\mu_{t}(\langle{\bm{X}}_{t},\bm{\Theta}_{\star}\rangle)){\bm{X}}_{t},\Delta\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\langle\sum_{{\bm{X}}\in\mathrm{supp}(\pi)}\frac{N({\bm{X}})}{N}(\mu(\langle{\bm{X}},\bm{\Theta}\rangle)-\mu(\langle{\bm{X}},\bm{\Theta}_{\star}\rangle))\mathrm{vec}({\bm{X}}),\mathrm{vec}(\Delta)\right\rangle

(
N({\bm{X}}):=\sum_{t=1}^{N}\mathds{1}[{\bm{X}}_{t}={\bm{X}}]
)

\displaystyle=\sum_{{\bm{X}}\in\mathrm{supp}(\pi)}\frac{N({\bm{X}})}{N}(\dot{\mu}(\langle{\bm{X}},\bm{\Theta}_{\star}\rangle)+G(\bm{\Theta}_{\star},\bm{\Theta};{\bm{X}})\left\langle\mathrm{vec}({\bm{X}}),\mathrm{vec}(\Delta)\right\rangle)\left\langle\mathrm{vec}({\bm{X}}),\mathrm{vec}(\Delta)\right\rangle^{2},

(first-order Taylor expansion,
\mathrm{vec}(\Delta)=\mathrm{vec}(\bm{\Theta}_{\star}-\bm{\Theta})
)

where we define

G(\bm{\Theta}_{\star},\bm{\Theta};{\bm{X}}):=\int_{0}^{1}(1-z)\ddot{\mu}(\langle{\bm{X}},z\bm{\Theta}+(1-z)\bm{\Theta}_{\star}\rangle)dz.

(24)

Note that

\displaystyle|G(\bm{\Theta}_{\star},\bm{\Theta};{\bm{X}})|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{1}(1-z)\left|\ddot{\mu}(\langle{\bm{X}},z\bm{\Theta}+(1-z)\bm{\Theta}_{\star}\rangle)\right|dz

\displaystyle\leq R_{s}\int_{0}^{1}(1-z)\dot{\mu}(\langle{\bm{X}},z\bm{\Theta}+(1-z)\bm{\Theta}_{\star}\rangle)dz

(self-concordance)

\displaystyle\leq R_{s}R_{\max}\int_{0}^{1}(1-z)dz

(
\dot{\mu}\leq R_{\max}
)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}R_{s}R_{\max}.

Let us also define the empirical Hessian:

\widehat{{\bm{H}}}(\pi;\bm{\Theta}_{\star}):=\sum_{{\bm{X}}\in\mathrm{supp}(\pi)}\frac{N({\bm{X}})}{N}\dot{\mu}(\langle{\bm{X}},\bm{\Theta}_{\star}\rangle)\mathrm{vec}({\bm{X}})\mathrm{vec}({\bm{X}})^{\top}.

(25)

Then, we can bound as

\displaystyle\langle\nabla{\mathcal{L}}_{N}(\bm{\Theta})-\nabla{\mathcal{L}}_{N}(\bm{\Theta}_{\star}),\Delta\rangle

\displaystyle=\mathrm{vec}(\Delta)^{\top}\widehat{{\bm{H}}}(\pi;\bm{\Theta}_{\star})\mathrm{vec}(\Delta)+\sum_{{\bm{X}}\in\mathrm{supp}(\pi)}\frac{N({\bm{X}})}{N}G(\bm{\Theta}_{\star},\bm{\Theta}_{0};{\bm{X}})\left\langle\mathrm{vec}({\bm{X}}),\mathrm{vec}(\Delta)\right\rangle^{3}

\displaystyle\geq\mathrm{vec}(\Delta)^{\top}\widehat{{\bm{H}}}(\pi;\bm{\Theta}_{\star})\mathrm{vec}(\Delta)-\frac{1}{2}R_{s}R_{\max}\sum_{{\bm{X}}\in\mathrm{supp}(\pi)}\frac{N({\bm{X}})}{N}|\left\langle{\bm{X}},\Delta\right\rangle|^{3}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{vec}(\Delta)^{\top}\widehat{{\bm{H}}}(\pi;\bm{\Theta}_{\star})\mathrm{vec}(\Delta)-\frac{1}{2}R_{s}R_{\max}\left\lVert\Delta\right\rVert_{\mathrm{nuc}}^{3}.

(matrix Hölder’s inequality,
\left\lVert{\bm{X}}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}\leq 1
by Assumption
2
)

The first term is bounded as

\displaystyle\mathrm{vec}(\Delta)^{\top}\widehat{{\bm{H}}}(\pi;\bm{\Theta}_{\star})\mathrm{vec}(\Delta)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{vec}(\Delta)^{\top}{\bm{H}}(\pi;\bm{\Theta}_{\star})\mathrm{vec}(\Delta)+\mathrm{vec}(\Delta)^{\top}(\widehat{{\bm{H}}}(\pi;\bm{\Theta}_{\star})-{\bm{H}}_{A}(\pi;\bm{\Theta}_{\star}))\mathrm{vec}(\Delta_{0})

\displaystyle\geq\left\lVert\Delta\right\rVert_{F}^{2}\lambda_{\min}({\bm{H}}(\pi;\bm{\Theta}_{\star}))+\underbrace{\mathrm{vec}(\Delta)^{\top}\left(\sum_{{\bm{X}}\in\mathrm{supp}(\pi)}\left(\frac{N({\bm{X}})}{N}-\pi({\bm{X}})\right)\dot{\mu}(\langle{\bm{X}},\bm{\Theta}_{\star}\rangle)\mathrm{vec}({\bm{X}})\mathrm{vec}({\bm{X}})^{\top}\right)\mathrm{vec}(\Delta)}_{\triangleq E}.

Let us now lower bound
E
:

\displaystyle E

\displaystyle=\sum_{{\bm{X}}\in\mathrm{supp}(\pi)}\left(\frac{N({\bm{X}})}{N}-\pi({\bm{X}})\right)\dot{\mu}(\langle{\bm{X}},\bm{\Theta}_{\star}\rangle)\langle{\bm{X}},\Delta\rangle^{2}

\displaystyle\geq-\left\lVert\Delta\right\rVert_{\mathrm{nuc}}^{2}\sum_{{\bm{X}}\in\mathrm{supp}(\pi)}\left|\frac{N({\bm{X}})}{N}-\pi({\bm{X}})\right|\dot{\mu}(\langle{\bm{X}},\bm{\Theta}_{\star}\rangle)

(matrix Hölder’s inequality,
\left\lVert{\bm{X}}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}\leq 1
)

\displaystyle\geq-\frac{R_{\max}}{4}\left\lVert\Delta\right\rVert_{\mathrm{nuc}}^{2}\sum_{{\bm{X}}\in\mathrm{supp}(\pi)}\left|\frac{N({\bm{X}})}{N}-\pi({\bm{X}})\right|.

(
\dot{\mu}\leq R_{\max}
)

For the last term, we utilize the following concentration for learning discrete distributions (of finite support) in
\ell_{1}
-distance:

Lemma C.3

(Theorem 1 of
Canonne 2020
)
.

Let
{\mathcal{X}}
be a finite space,
\pi\in{\mathcal{P}}({\mathcal{X}})
, and
\delta\in(0,1)
.
We are given
\{X_{i}\}_{i\in[N]}
with
X_{i}\overset{i.i.d.}{\sim}\pi
.
Let
\hat{\pi}_{N}\in{\mathcal{P}}({\mathcal{X}})
be defined as
\hat{\pi}_{N}(X):=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i\in[N]}\mathds{1}[X_{i}=X]
.
Then, we have the following:

{\mathbb{P}}\left(\left\lVert\pi-\hat{\pi}_{N}\right\rVert_{1}:=\sum_{X\in{\mathcal{X}}}|\pi(X)-\hat{\pi}_{N}(X)|\geq 2\sqrt{\frac{|\mathrm{supp}(\pi)|\log 2+\log\frac{2}{\delta}}{N}}\right)\leq\frac{\delta}{2}.

(26)

Combining everything, we have that with probability at least
1-\frac{\delta}{2}
,

\displaystyle\langle\nabla{\mathcal{L}}_{N}(\bm{\Theta})-\nabla{\mathcal{L}}_{N}(\bm{\Theta}_{\star}),\Delta\rangle

\displaystyle\geq\lambda_{\min}({\bm{H}}(\pi;\bm{\Theta}_{\star}))\left\lVert\Delta\right\rVert_{F}^{2}-\frac{R_{\max}}{2}\left(\sqrt{\frac{|\mathrm{supp}(\pi)|\log 2+\log\frac{2}{\delta}}{N}}+R_{s}\left\lVert\Delta\right\rVert_{\mathrm{nuc}}\right)\left\lVert\Delta\right\rVert_{\mathrm{nuc}}^{2}.

As
\Delta\in{\mathcal{C}}(\bm{\Theta}_{\star})\cap{\mathcal{B}}_{F}^{\mathrm{Skew}(d)}(W)
, recalling the orthogonal subspace decompositions,
\overline{{\mathcal{M}}}
and
\overline{{\mathcal{M}}}^{\perp}
:

\displaystyle\left\lVert\Delta\right\rVert_{\mathrm{nuc}}

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert\Delta_{\overline{{\mathcal{M}}}}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{nuc}}+\left\lVert\Delta_{\overline{{\mathcal{M}}}^{\perp}}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{nuc}}

(triangle inequality)

\displaystyle\leq 4\left\lVert\Delta_{\overline{{\mathcal{M}}}}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{nuc}}

(
\Delta\in{\mathcal{C}}(\bm{\Theta}_{\star})
)

\displaystyle\leq 4\sqrt{2r}\left\lVert\Delta_{\overline{{\mathcal{M}}}}\right\rVert_{F}

(
\mathrm{rank}(\Delta_{\overline{{\mathcal{M}}}})\leq 2r
, Cauchy-Schwartz inequality on the singular values)

\displaystyle\leq 4\sqrt{2r}\left\lVert\Delta\right\rVert_{F}

\displaystyle\leq 4\sqrt{2r}W.

(
\Delta\in{\mathcal{B}}_{F}^{\mathrm{Skew}(d)}(W)
)

Plugging it in, we have that

\displaystyle\langle\nabla{\mathcal{L}}_{N}(\bm{\Theta})-\nabla{\mathcal{L}}_{N}(\bm{\Theta}_{\star}),\Delta\rangle

\displaystyle\geq\lambda_{\min}({\bm{H}}(\pi;\bm{\Theta}_{\star}))\left\lVert\mathrm{vec}(\Delta)\right\rVert_{F}^{2}-16rW^{2}R_{\max}\left(\sqrt{\frac{|\mathrm{supp}(\pi)|\log 2+\log\frac{2}{\delta}}{N}}+4\sqrt{2r}WR_{s}\right).

∎

Remark 8

(Importance of
|\mathrm{supp}(\pi)|<\infty
)
.

If
\pi
is absolutely continuous w.r.t. the Lebesgue measure, than the usual empirical distribution
\widehat{\pi}_{N}:=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{t=1}^{N}\delta_{{\bm{X}}_{t}}
does not converge to
\pi
in the total variational (TV) distance
(
Barron et al. 1992
)
.
Indeed, a stronger statement is possible: for any
\delta\in(0,1/2)
and for any sequence of distribution estimators
\{\pi_{N}\}
on
{\mathbb{R}}
(with Borel
\sigma
-algebra), there exists a probability measure
\pi
such that
\inf_{N\geq 1}\left\lVert\pi_{N}-\pi\right\rVert_{1}>\frac{1}{2}-\delta,\ a.s.

(
Devroye & Györfi 1990
)
.
Thus, to deal with
\pi
’s with continuous densities, one must consider an alternate form of empirical Hessian
\widehat{{\bm{H}}}
via histogram or kernel density estimator
(
Tsybakov 2009
)
. We leave this to future work.

C.3
Choosing
\lambda_{N}
such that
\left\lVert\nabla{\mathcal{L}}_{N}(\bm{\Theta}_{\star})\right\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}
is Well-Controlled

The following lemma explicitly characterizes (up to absolute constants!) the “correct” choice of
\lambda_{N_{1}}
:

Lemma C.4

(Setting
\lambda_{N_{1}}
)
.

Let
\delta\in(0,1)
and define
v(\delta,d_{1},d_{2}):=\log(2\max(d_{1},d_{2}))+\min(d_{1},d_{2})\log\frac{5}{\delta}
.
By setting
\lambda_{N_{1}}=f(\delta,d_{1},d_{2})\sqrt{\frac{1}{N}}
with
f(\delta,d_{1},d_{2})
as described below, we have
{\mathbb{P}}(\left\lVert\nabla{\mathcal{L}}_{N}(\bm{\Theta}_{\star})\right\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}\leq\frac{\lambda_{N}}{2})\geq 1-\delta
:

(i)

When
|y-\mu(\langle{\bm{X}},\bm{\Theta}_{\star})|\leq M
a.s.:
f(\delta,d_{1},d_{2})=\sqrt{\frac{8R_{\max}}{g(\tau)}\log\frac{d_{1}+d_{2}}{\delta}},
given that
N\geq\frac{2M^{2}}{9R_{\max}g(\tau)}\log\frac{d_{1}+d_{2}}{\delta}
,

(ii)

When GLM is
\sigma
-subGaussian:
f(\delta,d_{1},d_{2})=\frac{16\pi\sigma}{g(\tau)}\sqrt{v(\delta)},

(iii)

When Poisson: if
R_{\max}>e
,
f(\delta,d_{1},d_{2})=g_{1}(R_{\max})+\frac{4}{1-2R_{\max}^{-1}}v(\delta,d_{1},d_{2})
with
g_{1}(R_{\max}):=\frac{1}{2}(1-2R_{\max}^{-1})(R_{\max}+2\log R_{\max}+2\log\frac{2(1-2R_{\max}^{-1})}{e})+4R_{\max}\log R_{\max}
;
otherwise,
f(\delta,d_{1},d_{2})=g_{2}(R_{\max})+8v(\delta,d_{1},d_{2})
with
g_{2}(R_{\max}):=\frac{1}{8}(R_{\max}+4\log R_{\max}+4\log(8+2R_{\max}))+4R_{\max}\log R_{\max}
.

Proof.

The proof is heavily inspired by Appendix C of
Lee et al. 2024a
, where the authors compute a high-probability bound for the global Lipschitz constant of
{\mathcal{L}}_{N}
.
Here, we only need to bound it at
\bm{\Theta}_{\star}
, making our guarantee a bit tighter.
During the proof, we also identify and improve suboptimal dependencies in
Lee et al. 2024a
, correctly leading to
\lambda_{N}
scaling as
\sqrt{1/N}
for all considered GLMs.

Let us prove each part separately:

C.3.1
Proof of (i) – GLM bounded by
M

Here, “bounded by
M
” means
|y-\langle{\bm{X}},\bm{\Theta}_{\star}\rangle|\leq M\ a.s.

The original proof of
Lee et al. 2024a
is too loose, and thus we instead utilize the matrix Bernstein inequality
(
Tropp 2015
, Theorem 6.6.1)
, which we recall here:

Theorem C.5

(Restatement of Theorem 6.1.1 of
Tropp 2015
)
.

Let
\{{\bm{A}}_{t}\}_{t=1}^{N}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{1}\times d_{2}}
be independent with
\left\lVert{\bm{A}}_{t}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}\leq L
and
\mathbb{E}[{\bm{A}}_{t}]={\bm{A}}
, and define their matrix variance statistics as

\sigma_{N}^{2}:=\max\left\{\left\lVert\sum_{t=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}[{\bm{A}}_{t}{\bm{A}}_{t}^{\top}]\right\rVert_{\mathrm{op}},\left\lVert\sum_{t=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}[{\bm{A}}_{t}^{\top}{\bm{A}}_{t}]\right\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}\right\}.

Then we have that for any
\delta\in(0,1)
, as long as
b(N)^{2}\geq\sigma_{N}^{2}\geq\frac{2L^{2}}{9}\log\frac{d_{1}+d_{2}}{\delta}
for a
b:{\mathbb{N}}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}_{>0}
,

{\mathbb{P}}\left(\left\lVert\frac{1}{N}\sum_{t=1}^{N}{\bm{A}}_{t}-{\bm{A}}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}\leq\frac{2b(N)}{N}\sqrt{2\log\frac{d_{1}+d_{2}}{\delta}}\right)\geq 1-\delta.

(27)

As
\left\lVert\nabla{\mathcal{L}}_{N}(\bm{\Theta}_{\star})\right\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}=\left\lVert\frac{1}{N}\sum_{t=1}^{N}\frac{\mu_{t}(\bm{\Theta}_{\star})-y_{t}}{g(\tau)}{\bm{X}}_{t}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}
, we set
{\bm{A}}_{t}=\frac{\mu_{t}(\bm{\Theta}_{\star})-y_{t}}{g(\tau)}{\bm{X}}_{t}
, which satisfies
{\bm{A}}=\mathbb{E}[{\bm{A}}_{t}]={\bm{0}}
.
Its maximum deviation is bounded as

\left\lVert\frac{\mu_{t}(\bm{\Theta}_{\star})-y_{t}}{g(\tau)}{\bm{X}}_{t}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}\leq\frac{M}{g(\tau)}.

Its matrix variance statistics is bounded as

\displaystyle\sigma_{N}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{g(\tau)^{2}}\max\left\{\left\lVert\sum_{t=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{X}}\sim\pi}[{\bm{X}}{\bm{X}}^{\top}\mathbb{E}[(\mu(\langle{\bm{X}},\bm{\Theta}_{\star}\rangle)-y)^{2}]]\right\rVert_{\mathrm{op}},\left\lVert\sum_{t=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{X}}\sim\pi}[{\bm{X}}^{\top}{\bm{X}}\mathbb{E}[(\mu(\langle{\bm{X}},\bm{\Theta}_{\star}\rangle)-y)^{2}]]\right\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}\right\}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{g(\tau)}\sum_{t=1}^{N}\dot{\mu}(\langle{\bm{X}},\bm{\Theta}_{\star}\rangle)

(
\mathbb{E}[(\mu(\langle{\bm{X}},\bm{\Theta}_{\star}\rangle)-y)^{2}]=\mathrm{Var}[y|{\bm{X}}]=g(\tau)\dot{\mu}(\langle{\bm{X}},\bm{\Theta}_{\star}\rangle)
,
\left\lVert{\bm{X}}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}\leq 1
)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{NR_{\max}}{g(\tau)}.

We then conclude by applying the matrix Bernstein inequality.

C.3.2
Proof of (ii) –
\sigma
-subGaussian GLM

Here, we first utilize a covering argument to reduce the problem to
\sigma
-norm-subGaussian vector concentration, where we utilize the results of
Jin et al. 2019
, refined in Appendix C.2 of
Lee et al. 2024a
.

Let
\widehat{{\mathcal{B}}}^{d_{2}}(1)
be a
\frac{1}{2}
-cover of
{\mathcal{B}}^{d_{2}}(1):=\{\bm{\theta}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{2}}:\left\lVert\bm{\theta}\right\rVert_{2}\leq 1\}
.
By Corollary 4.2.13 of
Vershynin 2018
, we can find a cover with
|\widehat{{\mathcal{B}}}^{d_{2}}(1)|\leq 5^{d_{2}}
.
For each
{\bm{u}}\in{\mathcal{B}}^{d_{2}}(1)
, let
\hat{{\bm{u}}}\in\widehat{{\mathcal{B}}}^{d_{2}}(1)
be such that
\left\lVert{\bm{u}}-\hat{{\bm{u}}}\right\rVert_{2}\leq\varepsilon_{N}
.
Then, we have that

\displaystyle\left\lVert\nabla{\mathcal{L}}_{N}(\bm{\Theta}_{\star})\right\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}

\displaystyle=\sup_{\left\lVert{\bm{u}}\right\rVert\leq 1}\left\lVert\frac{1}{N}\sum_{t=1}^{N}\frac{\mu_{t}(\bm{\Theta}_{\star})-y_{t}}{g(\tau)}{\bm{X}}_{t}{\bm{u}}\right\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{\left\lVert{\bm{u}}\right\rVert\leq 1}\left\{\left\lVert\frac{1}{N}\sum_{t=1}^{N}\frac{\mu_{t}(\bm{\Theta}_{\star})-y_{t}}{g(\tau)}{\bm{X}}_{t}({\bm{u}}-\hat{{\bm{u}}})\right\rVert_{2}+\left\lVert\frac{1}{N}\sum_{t=1}^{N}\frac{\mu_{t}(\bm{\Theta}_{\star})-y_{t}}{g(\tau)}{\bm{X}}_{t}\hat{{\bm{u}}}\right\rVert_{2}\right\}

(triangle inequality)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert\nabla{\mathcal{L}}_{N}(\bm{\Theta}_{\star})\right\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}+\sup_{\hat{{\bm{u}}}\in\widehat{{\mathcal{B}}}^{d_{2}}(1)}\left\lVert\frac{1}{N}\sum_{t=1}^{N}\frac{\mu_{t}(\bm{\Theta}_{\star})-y_{t}}{g(\tau)}{\bm{X}}_{t}\hat{{\bm{u}}}\right\rVert_{2},

and thus,

\left\lVert\nabla{\mathcal{L}}_{N}(\bm{\Theta}_{\star})\right\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}\leq 2\sup_{\hat{{\bm{u}}}\in\widehat{{\mathcal{B}}}^{d_{2}}(1)}\left\lVert\frac{1}{N}\sum_{t=1}^{N}\frac{\mu_{t}(\bm{\Theta}_{\star})-y_{t}}{g(\tau)}{\bm{X}}_{t}\hat{{\bm{u}}}\right\rVert_{2}.

For each fixed
\hat{{\bm{u}}}
and
\delta^{\prime}\in(0,1)
, applying Corollary 7 of
Jin et al. 2019
8
8

8

see Lemma C.1 of
Lee et al. 2024a
for the version with explicit constants.
gives

{\mathbb{P}}\left(\left\lVert\frac{1}{N}\sum_{t=1}^{N}\frac{\mu_{t}(\bm{\Theta}_{\star})-y_{t}}{g(\tau)}{\bm{X}}_{t}\hat{{\bm{u}}}\right\rVert_{2}\leq\frac{4\pi\sigma}{g(\tau)}\sqrt{\frac{1}{N}\log\frac{2d_{1}}{\delta^{\prime}}}\right)\geq 1-\delta^{\prime}.

By the union bound, we finally have that

Note that

First, via the same covering argument, it suffices to bound (with high probability)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}R_{s}R_{\max}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}E}^{2}\sqrt{\frac{1+2R_{s}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}E}}{\kappa_{\star}}\max\left\{\mathrm{tr}\left(\sum_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}m}=1}^{d}{\bm{D}}_{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}m}^{(\mathrm{row})}\right),\mathrm{tr}\left(\sum_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}m}=1}^{d}{\bm{D}}_{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}m}^{(\mathrm{col})}\right)\right\}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}R_{s}R_{\max}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}E}^{2}\sqrt{\frac{(d_{1}\vee d_{2})(1+2R_{s}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}E})}{\kappa_{\star}}\max\left\{\lambda_{\max}\left(\sum_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}m}=1}^{d}{\bm{D}}_{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}m}^{(\mathrm{row})}\right),\lambda_{\max}\left(\sum_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}m}=1}^{d}{\bm{D}}_{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}m}^{(\mathrm{col})}\right)\right\}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}R_{s}R_{\max}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}E}^{2}\sqrt{\frac{(d_{1}\vee d_{2})(1+2R_{s}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}E})}{\kappa_{\star}}\mathrm{GL}(\pi)}.

(45)

Combining everything we have that:

\displaystyle\left\lVert\bm{\Theta}_{1}-\bm{\Theta}_{\star}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert(\bm{\Theta}_{1}-\bm{\Theta}_{0})-\mathrm{Proj}_{\Omega}(\mathbb{E}[\widetilde{\bm{\Theta}}_{t}])\right\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}+\left\lVert\mathrm{Proj}_{\Omega}(\mathbb{E}[\widetilde{\bm{\Theta}}_{t}])-(\bm{\Theta}_{\star}-\bm{\Theta}_{0})\right\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{(1+2R_{s}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}E})\mathrm{GL}(\pi)}\left(\sqrt{\frac{1}{N_{2}}\left(g(\tau)+\frac{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}E}^{2}R_{\max}^{2}}{\kappa_{\star}}\right)\log\frac{4(d_{1}+d_{2})}{\delta}}+\frac{1}{2}R_{s}R_{\max}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}E}^{2}\sqrt{\frac{d_{1}\vee d_{2}}{\kappa_{\star}}}\right).

(46)

Combining above with
Theorem

3.4
(Guarantee for Stage I), it can be deduced that with

N_{1}\gtrsim\max\left\{\widetilde{N}_{1},\frac{R_{s}R_{\max}f(\delta,d_{1},d_{2})^{2}r^{2}}{C_{H}(\pi_{1})^{2}}\sqrt{\frac{(d_{1}\vee d_{2})N_{2}}{g(\tau)\kappa_{\star}^{5}\log\frac{d_{1}\vee d_{2}}{\delta}}}\right\},

(47)

the following holds with probability at least
1-\delta

\left\lVert\bm{\Theta}_{1}-\bm{\Theta}_{\star}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}\leq\sigma_{\mathrm{thres}}\triangleq 2\sqrt{\frac{2(1+R_{s})g(\tau)\mathrm{GL}(\pi)}{N_{2}}\log\frac{4(d_{1}+d_{2})}{\delta}}.

As the last step of the proof, we recall the Weyl’s inequality for singular values:

Lemma D.4

(Problem 7.3.P16 of

.

For any

Proof of Lemma

We will bound
\left\lVert\mathbb{E}[\widetilde{\bm{\Theta}}_{t}\widetilde{\bm{\Theta}}_{t}^{\top}]\right\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}

We first establish the following: by the fundamental theorem of calculus,

\left|\mu(\langle{\bm{X}},\bm{\Theta}_{\star}\rangle)-\mu(\langle{\bm{X}},\bm{\Theta}_{0}\rangle)\right|=\left|\langle{\bm{X}},\bm{\Theta}_{\star}-\bm{\Theta}_{0}\rangle\right|\int_{0}^{1}\dot{\mu}(\langle{\bm{X}},(1-z)\bm{\Theta}_{\star}+z\bm{\Theta}_{0}\rangle)dz\leq{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}E}R_{\max},

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[(y-\mu(\langle{\bm{X}},\bm{\Theta}))^{2}]

\displaystyle\leq 2\mathbb{E}[(y-\mu(\langle{\bm{X}},\bm{\Theta}_{\star}))^{2}]+2\mathbb{E}[(\mu(\langle{\bm{X}},\bm{\Theta}_{\star})-\mu(\langle{\bm{X}},\bm{\Theta}))^{2}]

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\widetilde{\bm{\Theta}}_{t}\widetilde{\bm{\Theta}}_{t}^{\top}]

\displaystyle\left\lVert\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{X}}\sim\pi}\left[\dot{\mu}(\langle{\bm{X}},\bm{\Theta}_{\star}\rangle){\bm{A}}_{\bm{X}}{\bm{A}}_{\bm{X}}^{\top}\right]\right\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}

E.1
Upper Bound of
\mathrm{GL}(\pi)

We have that

\displaystyle H^{(\mathrm{col})}(\pi)

\displaystyle=\lambda_{\max}\left(\sum_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}m}=1}^{d_{2}}{\bm{D}}_{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}m}^{(\mathrm{col})}(\pi)\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}m}=1}^{d_{2}}\lambda_{\max}\left({\bm{D}}_{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}m}^{(col)}(\pi)\right)

(
\lambda_{\max}
is convex and 1-homogenous)

\displaystyle=\sum_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}m}=1}^{d_{2}}\max_{{\bm{u}}\in{\mathcal{S}}^{d_{1}-1}}{\bm{u}}^{\top}{\bm{D}}_{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}m}^{(\mathrm{col})}(\pi){\bm{u}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}m}=1}^{d_{2}}\max_{{\bm{u}}\in{\mathcal{S}}^{d_{1}-1}}({\bm{e}}_{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}m}\otimes{\bm{u}})\top{\bm{H}}(\pi;\bm{\Theta}_{0})^{-1}({\bm{e}}_{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}m}\otimes{\bm{u}})

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}m}=1}^{d_{2}}\max_{{\bm{u}}\in{\mathcal{S}}^{d_{1}d_{2}-1}}{\bm{u}}^{\top}{\bm{H}}(\pi;\bm{\Theta}_{0})^{-1}{\bm{u}}

\displaystyle=d_{2}\lambda_{\max}({\bm{H}}(\pi;\bm{\Theta}_{0})^{-1})

\displaystyle=\frac{d_{2}}{\lambda_{\min}({\bm{H}}(\pi;\bm{\Theta}_{0}))}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{d_{2}(1+R_{s})}{\lambda_{\min}({\bm{H}}(\pi;\bm{\Theta}_{\star}))}.

One can similarly prove that
H^{(\mathrm{row})}(\pi)\leq\frac{d_{1}(1+R_{s})}{\lambda_{\min}({\bm{H}}(\pi;\bm{\Theta}_{\star}))}
, and the desired conclusion follows.

E.2
Lower Bound of
\mathrm{GL}(\pi)

We first consider the case of
{\bm{X}}\in{\mathcal{B}}^{d_{1}\times d_{2}}_{\mathrm{op}}(1)
.

Again, by definition,

\displaystyle\mathrm{GL}(\pi)

\displaystyle\geq\lambda_{\max}\left(\sum_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}m}=1}^{d_{2}}[({\bm{H}}(\pi;\bm{\Theta}_{0})^{-1})_{jk}]_{j,k\in\{\ell+d_{1}({\color[rgb]{0,0,1}m}-1):\ell\in[d_{1}]\}}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{d_{1}}\mathrm{tr}\left(\sum_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}m}=1}^{d_{2}}[({\bm{H}}(\pi;\bm{\Theta}_{0})^{-1})_{jk}]_{j,k\in\{\ell+d_{1}({\color[rgb]{0,0,1}m}-1):\ell\in[d_{1}]\}}\right)

(
\lambda_{\max}({\bm{A}})\geq\frac{1}{d}\mathrm{tr}({\bm{A}})
for any symmetric
{\bm{A}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d\times d}
)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d_{1}}\mathrm{tr}\left({\bm{H}}(\pi;\bm{\Theta}_{0})^{-1}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{d_{1}}\frac{(d_{1}d_{2})^{2}}{\mathrm{tr}\left({\bm{H}}(\pi;\bm{\Theta}_{0})\right)},

(AM-HM inequality on the eigenvalues of
{\bm{H}}(\pi;\bm{\Theta}_{0})
)

and similarly,

\mathrm{GL}(\pi)\geq\frac{1}{d_{2}}\frac{(d_{1}d_{2})^{2}}{\mathrm{tr}\left({\bm{H}}(\pi;\bm{\Theta}_{0})\right)},

i.e.,
\mathrm{GL}(\pi)\geq\frac{(d_{1}d_{2})^{2}}{(d_{1}\wedge d_{2})\mathrm{tr}({\bm{H}}(\pi;\bm{\Theta}_{0}))}
.

Now note that

\displaystyle\mathrm{tr}\left({\bm{H}}(\pi;\bm{\Theta}_{0})\right)

\displaystyle\leq(1+R_{s})\mathrm{tr}\left({\bm{H}}(\pi;\bm{\Theta}_{\star})\right)

(Lemma
D.3
)

\displaystyle=(1+R_{s})\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{X}}\sim\pi}\left[\dot{\mu}(\langle{\bm{X}},\bm{\Theta}_{\star}\rangle)\mathrm{tr}(\mathrm{vec}({\bm{X}})\mathrm{vec}({\bm{X}})^{\top})\right]

\displaystyle=(1+R_{s})\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{X}}\sim\pi}\left[\dot{\mu}(\langle{\bm{X}},\bm{\Theta}_{\star}\rangle)\left\lVert{\bm{X}}\right\rVert_{F}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq(1+R_{s})(d_{1}\wedge d_{2})\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{X}}\sim\pi}\left[\dot{\mu}(\langle{\bm{X}},\bm{\Theta}_{\star}\rangle)\right]

(
{\bm{X}}\in{\mathcal{B}}^{d_{1}\times d_{2}}_{\mathrm{op}}(1)\Rightarrow{\bm{X}}\in{\mathcal{B}}^{d_{1}\times d_{2}}_{F}(\sqrt{d_{1}\wedge d_{2}})
)

\displaystyle=(1+R_{s})(d_{1}\wedge d_{2})\overline{\kappa}(\pi).

Chaining the above two inequalities gives
\mathrm{GL}(\pi)\geq\frac{(d_{1}d_{2})^{2}}{(1+R_{s})(d_{1}\wedge d_{2})^{2}\overline{\kappa}(\pi)}=\frac{(d_{1}\vee d_{2})^{2}}{(1+R_{s})\overline{\kappa}(\pi)}
.

From the above proof, it is clear that when
{\bm{X}}\in{\mathcal{B}}^{d_{1}\times d_{2}}_{F}(1)
, we can shave off an extra
d_{1}\wedge d_{2}
from the denominator, leading to the desired conclusion.

5.2
– Bilinear Dueling Bandits Part I (Setting)

H.1
Motivation

Transitivity — the property that if
i\succ j
and
j\succ k
, then
i\succ k
— is one of the key assumptions that distinguish the dueling bandit setting
(
Yue & Joachims 2009
;
Yue et al. 2012
;
Sui et al. 2018
;
Bengs et al. 2021
)
.
Within this stochastic transitivity framework, the most commonly considered model is the Bradley-Terry-Luce (BTL) model
(
Bradley & Terry 1952
)
: each arm
k
has an unknown utility(reward)
r_{k}\in{\mathbb{R}}
such that for each
(i,j)\in[K]\times[K]
,
p_{i,j}:={\mathbb{P}}(i\succ j)=\mu(r_{i}-r_{j})
with
\mu(z):=(1+e^{-z})^{-1}.

When
K
is large, without any additional structural assumption, the statistical guarantees (e.g., regret in dueling bandits) often increase polynomially in
K
.
One very natural way of bypassing this issue is to impose a linear structure on the utility, resulting in the so-called linear BTL model: each arm
k
is endowed with a known feature vector
\bm{\phi}_{k}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and
r_{k}=\langle\bm{\phi}_{k},\bm{\theta}_{\star}\rangle
for some unknown
\bm{\theta}_{\star}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
.
This model has been successfully applied in various domains, with reinforcement learning with human feedback
(
Rafailov et al. 2023
)
being one of the most prominent applications.
Coming back to dueling bandits, with such linear structure, the regret of dueling bandits has been improved from
\mathrm{poly}(K)
to
d
or
\sqrt{d\log K}
by exploiting the linear BTL model
(
Saha 2021
;
Bengs et al. 2022
)
.

However, the literature has two main gaps, both of which we intend to fill with our newly proposed setting and new analyses.

Linear-like Structure in Dueling Bandits with General Preferences.

The (linear) BTL model cannot model nontransitive preferences, which hinders its applicability in various scenarios, from simple nontransitive games such as rock-paper-scissors, Blotto-style games
(
Balduzzi et al. 2018
;
Balduzzi et al. 2019
;
Bertrand et al. 2023
)
, and even human preferences
(
May 1954
;
Tversky 1969
;
Munos et al. 2024
;
Azar et al. 2024
;
Swamy et al. 2024
;
Zhang et al. 2024b
)
.

In most of the prior literature on dueling bandits and general preference learning (i.e., not assuming linear BTL model), the learner must either learn or adapt to the entire unstructured preference matrix
{\bm{P}}\in[0,1]^{K\times K}
.
This means that, again, the statistical guarantees are expected to depend polynomially in
K
.
Given that the linear structure has enabled the development of efficient algorithms for linear and dueling bandits with large action spaces and contextual information, the question of how to impose linear-like structure to arbitrary preference matrix
{\bm{P}}
has been a significant and longstanding open question.

There have been two notable advancements in this direction, one theoretical and one practical.
The first advancement is by
Wu et al. 2024
, whose setting we briefly describe here.
The learner has access to a feature map
(i,j)\in[K]\times[K]\mapsto\bm{\phi}_{i,j}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
satisfying
\bm{\phi}_{i,j}=-\bm{\phi}_{j,i}
.
The preference probability is defined as
p_{i,j}=\mu(\langle\bm{\phi}_{i,j},\bm{\theta}_{\star}\rangle),
where
\bm{\theta}_{\star}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
is unknown.
With this model, the authors have improved the Borda regret’s dependency on
K
from polynomial to logarithmic.
However, it is unrealistic to know all item
pair-wise
features that linearly encode the underlying preferences.
Arguably, a more realistic scenario is knowing only item-wise features, namely,
\bm{\phi}_{k}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
for
k\in[K]
.

One may wonder if there is a contextual preference model that incorporates
item-wise
features while being potentially nontransitive.
The second advancement, due to
Zhang et al. 2024b
, tackles this by proposing the contextual bilinear preference model: for each item pair
(i,j)\in[K]\times[K]
, the preference model is defined as

p_{i,j}=\mu\left(\bm{\phi}_{i}^{\top}\bm{\Theta}_{\star}\bm{\phi}_{j}\right),

(62)

where
\bm{\Theta}_{\star}
is a
d\times d
skew-symmetric matrix of low rank.
However, their paper does not provide any statistical guarantees when this is used in dueling bandits, or even regarding the estimation error of the preference model; rather, their main focus is experimentally validating this model in modeling human preferences and its implications for the downstream RLHF task.
Note that we adopt the same preference model, exept we allow for the underlying arm-set
{\mathcal{A}}
to be continuous.

Although not discussed further in
Zhang et al. 2024b
, we believe this is a very natural way of incorporating some sort of linearity into general preferences, and that it deserves more attention from the dueling bandits community as well.
Indeed, such bilinear model has been used in modeling interaction of two items, with applications to drug discovery
(
Luo et al. 2017
)
, server scheduling
(
Kim & Vojnović 2021
)
, personalized recommendation
(
Chu & Park 2009
)
, link prediction
(
Menon & Elkan 2011
)
, relational learning
(
Nickel et al. 2011
)
, and more.
The bandit community was introduced to this model by bilinear bandits
(
Jang et al. 2021
;
Jun et al. 2019
)
, later extended to low-rank matrix-armed bandits
(
Lu et al. 2021
;
Kang et al. 2022
;
Jang et al. 2024
)
; refer to Appendix
A
for further related works on low-rank bandits.
Roughly speaking, the learner now only needs to learn
\Theta(d^{2})
parameters of
\bm{\Theta}_{\star}
instead of
\Theta(K^{2})
parameters of
{\bm{P}}
.
Furthermore, using the low-rank structure of
\bm{\Theta}_{\star}
, the learner can further improve the regret’s dependency in
d
.
Although not discussed in
Zhang et al. 2024b

Rajkumar & Agarwal 2016

\mu^{-1}({\bm{P}})=\bm{\Phi}^{\top}\bm{\Theta}_{\star}\bm{\Phi}

\bm{\Phi}=[\bm{\phi}_{1}\cdots\bm{\phi}_{K}]\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d\times K}

\mu^{-1}

Variance-Aware Borda Regret Bound.

The Borda regret resembles the strong regret
(
Yue et al. 2012
)
, and it “respects” the inherent problem of the difficulty of dueling bandits where two arms are chosen rather than a single arm
(
Saha et al. 2021
;
Wu et al. 2024
)
.
Its original motivation is from search engine, in which the regret corresponds to “the fraction of users who would prefer the best retrieval function over the selected ones.”
(
Yue & Joachims 2009
)
.

All the existing guarantees for the Borda regret either assume a fixed gap
(
Saha et al. 2021
)
or incur a
1/c_{\mu}
dependency
(
Wu et al. 2024
)
, where
c_{\mu}
can be thought of as the worst-case badness of linear approximation of the true preference signal.
In other words, the current Borda regret bound seems to suggest that the lower the variance (which roughly corresponds to the derivative of the inverse link function in the context of GLMs), the higher the regret.
However, the vast literature on logistic and generalized linear bandits
(
Abeille et al. 2021
;
Lee et al. 2024a
;
Lee et al. 2024b
)
suggest otherwise.

Abeille et al. 2021
first proved a
\widetilde{{\mathcal{O}}}(d\sqrt{T\kappa_{\star}})
regret bound for logistic bandits as well as a matching (local minimax) lower bound, the correct dependency on the variance-dependent quantity.
Thus, it should be expected that a similar variance-dependent quantity should pop up in the optimal Borda regret bounds.

H.2
A Sufficient Condition for the Bilinear Preference to be Stochastic Transitive

A preference model is
stochastic transitive w.r.t.
\mu

(
Bengs et al. 2022
)
if there exists a
f:[K]\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
such that
({\bm{P}})_{ij}=\mu(f(i)-f(j))
.
Here, we prove that certain collinearity between the features
\bm{\phi}_{i}
’s in the bilinear preference model (Eqn. (
62
)) implies stochastic transitivity:

Theorem H.1
.

If there exists an orthonormal
{\bm{Q}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d\times d}
such that
\{(({\bm{Q}}^{\top}\bm{\phi}_{k})_{2m-1},({\bm{Q}}^{\top}\bm{\phi}_{k})_{2m})\}_{k\in[K]}
is collinear in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
for each
m\in[r]
, then the bilinear preference model is stochastic transitive w.r.t.
\mu
.
When
r=1
(i.e.,
\mathrm{rank}(\bm{\Theta}_{\star})=2
), this is also a necessary condition.

Proof.

The proof is heavily inspired by
Jiang et al. 2011
, where the authors provide a decomposition of the space of preferences via combinatorial Hodge theory; this has been also utilized in later machine learning literature on ranking with potentially nontransitive components
(
Bertrand et al. 2023
;
Balduzzi et al. 2018
;
Balduzzi et al. 2019
)
.

From the combinatorial Hodge decomposition
(
Jiang et al. 2011
, Theorem 2)
, a
f
that satisfies the stochastic transitivity exists if and only if for any
(i,j,k)\in[K]^{3}
,

\bm{\phi}_{i}^{\top}\bm{\Theta}_{\star}\bm{\phi}_{j}+\bm{\phi}_{j}^{\top}\bm{\Theta}_{\star}\bm{\phi}_{k}+\bm{\phi}_{k}^{\top}\bm{\Theta}_{\star}\bm{\phi}_{i}=0.

The quantity on the LHS is known as the
combinatorial curl

(
Jiang et al. 2011
)
.

Let
\bm{\Theta}_{\star}={\bm{Q}}\bm{\Lambda}{\bm{Q}}^{\top}
be its canonical form (Lemma
H.2
), and let
\bm{\varphi}_{i}:={\bm{Q}}^{\top}\bm{\phi}_{i}
.
Let
\{\lambda_{m}\}_{m\in[r]}\subset{\mathbb{R}}_{>0}
be the nonzero components of
\bm{\Lambda}
.
Then, the above curl-free requirement boils down to

\sum_{m=1}^{r}\lambda_{m}\underbrace{\begin{vmatrix}1&1&1\\
(\bm{\varphi}_{i})_{2m-1}&(\bm{\varphi}_{j})_{2m-1}&(\bm{\varphi}_{k})_{2m-1}\\
(\bm{\varphi}_{i})_{2m}&(\bm{\varphi}_{j})_{2m}&(\bm{\varphi}_{k})_{2m}\end{vmatrix}}_{\triangleq V_{m}}=0.

(63)

One sufficient condition for above to hold (necessary as well if
r=1
) is if
V_{m}=0
for all
m\in[r]
.
Geometrically,
V_{m}
is the signed volume of the parallelopipe in
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
, spanned by the three column vectors.
For the volume to be zero, it must be that
\{((\bm{\varphi}_{i})_{2m-1},(\bm{\varphi}_{i})_{2m}),((\bm{\varphi}_{j})_{2m-1},(\bm{\varphi}_{j})_{2m}),((\bm{\varphi}_{k})_{2m-1},(\bm{\varphi}_{k})_{2m})\}
is collinear in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
.
As this must hold for any
i,j,k\in[K]^{3}
, it must be that
\{((\bm{\varphi}_{k})_{2m-1},(\bm{\varphi}_{k})_{2m})\}_{k\in[K]}
is collinear as well, for each
m\in[r]
.
∎

Remark 12
.

We believe that the above result is extendable to the general case via decomposing the general preference into its transitive and cyclic components
(
Jiang et al. 2011
)
.
But then, geometrically, it is unclear how to choose the right features such that the non-transitive and transitive components are compatible with each other, which corresponds to the “harmonic” component from the combinatorial Hodge decomposition
(
Jiang et al. 2011
)
.

H.3
Miscellaneous Mathematical Preliminaries

Here, for completeness and to foster future directions, we provide a bit orthogonal, yet interesting (and hopefully useful) mathematical preliminaries regarding skew-symmetric matrices and anti-symmetric tensor product space.

H.3.1
Skew-Symmetric Matrix

A matrix
{\bm{A}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d\times d}
is
skew-symmetric
(or anti-symmetric) if
{\bm{A}}^{\top}=-{\bm{A}}
.
It is known that the rank of a skew-symmetric matrix must be even
(
Hoffman & Kunze 1971
, Section 10.3)
, and it admits the following decomposition, which is its canonical form:

Lemma H.2

(Corollary 2.5.11 of
Horn & Johnson 2012
12
12

12

A fun(?) historical note: this decomposition has been repeatedly rediscovered and renamed: Murnaghan-Wintner decomposition
(
Murnaghan & Wintner 1931
)
, Youla decomposition
(
Youla 1961
)

(

, although the latter is a bit inaccurate as Schur decomposition should result in an upper triangular matrix in the middle.

.

{\bm{A}}
is a skew-symmetric of rank

(i.e.,
{\bm{Q}}^{\top}{\bm{Q}}={\bm{Q}}{\bm{Q}}^{\top}={\bm{I}}_{d}

\bm{\Lambda}=\left(\bigoplus_{\ell\in[r]}\lambda_{\ell}{\bm{S}}\right)\oplus{\bm{0}}_{d-2r},

(64)

where

{\bm{S}}:=\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\
-1&0\end{bmatrix}

We also remark that the above form can be quite efficiently computed

;
Penke et al. 2020
)

Let
\mathrm{Skew}(d):=\{\bm{\Theta}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d\times d}:\bm{\Theta}^{\top}=-\bm{\Theta}\}
.
It is a well-known that
\mathrm{Skew}(d)
is a linear subspace of
{\mathbb{R}}^{d\times d}
, and that the mapping
{\bm{A}}\mapsto\frac{1}{2}({\bm{A}}-{\bm{A}}^{\top})
is an orthogonal projection onto
\mathrm{Skew}(d)

(
Hoffman & Kunze 1971
, Chapter 6.6)
.
We will also consider rank-constrained
\mathrm{Skew}(d)
, defined as
\mathrm{Skew}(d;2r):=\{\bm{\Theta}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d\times d}:\bm{\Theta}^{\top}=-\bm{\Theta},\ \mathrm{rank}(\bm{\Theta})=2r\}
.
This is a matrix manifold whose dimension is given as follows (see Appendix
H.4
for the proof):

Proposition H.3
.

\dim(\mathrm{Skew}(d;2r))=2dr-(2r^{2}+r).

H.3.2
2nd-Order Tensor Product Space

Here, we largely follow the exposition of Section 2 of
Garcia et al. 2023
and Section I.5 of
Bhatia 1997

We define the

of

as
({\mathbb{R}}^{d})^{\otimes 2}:=\{{\bm{x}}\otimes{\bm{y}}:{\bm{x}},{\bm{y}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\}

13
13

13

Such inner product is unique
(
Bhatia 1997
, Proposition 3.8.2)
.
is such that
\langle{\bm{x}}_{1}\otimes{\bm{x}}_{2},{\bm{y}}_{1}\otimes{\bm{y}}_{2}\rangle=\langle{\bm{x}}_{1},{\bm{y}}_{1}\rangle\langle{\bm{x}}_{2},{\bm{y}}_{2}\rangle

Then, its orthonormal basis is given as
\{{\bm{e}}_{i}\otimes{\bm{e}}_{j}\}_{(i,j)\in[d]^{2}}

Consider the symmetrization and antisymmetrization operators, defined as
{\mathcal{P}}_{S}({\bm{x}}\otimes{\bm{y}}):={\bm{x}}\odot{\bm{y}}:=\frac{1}{2}({\bm{x}}\otimes{\bm{y}}+{\bm{y}}\otimes{\bm{x}})

{\mathcal{P}}_{A}({\bm{x}}\otimes{\bm{y}}):={\bm{x}}\wedge{\bm{y}}:=\frac{1}{2}({\bm{x}}\otimes{\bm{y}}-{\bm{y}}\otimes{\bm{x}}).

Then, one can orthogonally decompose
({\mathbb{R}}^{d})^{\otimes 2}=({\mathbb{R}}^{d})^{\odot 2}\oplus({\mathbb{R}}^{d})^{\wedge 2}
, where the two spaces are spanned by their respective
orthonormal
basis:
({\mathbb{R}}^{d})^{\odot 2}=\mathrm{span}\left(\left\{{\bm{e}}_{i}\odot{\bm{e}}_{i}\right\}_{i\in[d]}\cup\left\{\sqrt{2}({\bm{e}}_{i}\odot{\bm{e}}_{j})\right\}_{1\leq i<j\leq d}\right),
and
({\mathbb{R}}^{d})^{\wedge 2}=\mathrm{span}\left(\left\{\sqrt{2}({\bm{e}}_{i}\wedge{\bm{e}}_{j})\right\}_{1\leq i<j\leq d}\right).

Let us focus on the antisymmetric part.
It is known that
{\mathcal{P}}_{A}
is an orthogonal projection onto
{\mathbb{R}}^{\wedge 2}
with the following idempotent, full row-rank matrix representation of
{\mathcal{P}}_{A}
:

{\bm{P}}_{A}:=\sqrt{2}\begin{bmatrix}{\bm{e}}_{1}\wedge{\bm{e}}_{2}&{\bm{e}}_{1}\wedge{\bm{e}}_{3}&\cdots&{\bm{e}}_{d-1}\wedge{\bm{e}}_{d}\end{bmatrix}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d^{2}\times\binom{d}{2}}.

(65)

It satisfies
{\bm{P}}_{A}^{\top}{\bm{P}}_{A}={\bm{I}}_{\binom{d}{2}}
and
{\bm{P}}_{A}{\bm{P}}_{A}^{\top}({\bm{x}}\otimes{\bm{y}})={\bm{x}}\wedge{\bm{y}}
.

H.4
Proof of Proposition
H.3

The proof utilizes some tools from topology, Lie group theory and matrix theory.
Our main references are
Munkres 2018
, Chapter 21 of
Lee 2012
and
Horn & Johnson 2012
.

Consider the generalized linear group
\mathrm{GL}_{d}({\mathbb{R}}):=\{{\bm{X}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d\times d}:\det({\bm{X}})\neq 0\}
, which is a Lie group of dimension

.
We then define the group action of
\mathrm{GL}_{d}({\mathbb{R}})
on
\mathrm{Skew}(d;2r)
as the following:

({\bm{X}},{\bm{A}})\mapsto{\bm{X}}{\bm{A}}{\bm{X}}^{\top},\quad{\bm{X}}\in\mathrm{GL}_{d}({\mathbb{R}}),{\bm{A}}\in\mathrm{Skew}(d;2r).

(66)

We now utilize the following lemma:

Lemma H.4

(Theorem 21.20 of
Lee 2012
)
.

Let
X
be a set and
G
be a Lie group that acts on
X
transitively, i.e., for any
x,y\in X
there exists a
g\in G
such that
(g,x)=y
.
Suppose that there exists a point
p\in X
such that the stabilizer group
G_{p}
is closed in
G
.
Then,
X
has a unique smooth manifold structure w.r.t. which the given action is smooth.
With this structure,
\dim X=\dim G-\dim G_{p}
.

We first show that our group action indeed satisfies the assumptions of the above lemma.
For simplicity, let us denote

{\bm{S}}_{d,2r}:=\underbrace{\bigoplus_{\ell\in[r]}\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\
-1&0\end{bmatrix}}_{=:{\bm{S}}_{2r}}\oplus{\bm{0}}_{d-2r}.

(67)

Claim H.1
.

The action as defined in Eqn. (
66
) is transitive.

Proof.

To see this, consider two
{\bm{A}},{\bm{B}}\in\mathrm{Skew}(d;2r)
.
Then by Lemma

, there exists
{\bm{U}}_{\bm{A}},{\bm{U}}_{\bm{B}}\in O(d)
and
\{\lambda_{\ell,{\bm{A}}}^{2},\lambda_{\ell,{\bm{B}}}^{2}\}_{\ell\in[r]}
such that
{\bm{A}}={\bm{U}}_{\bm{A}}\bm{\Lambda}_{\bm{A}}{\bm{S}}_{d,2r}\bm{\Lambda}_{\bm{A}}^{\top}{\bm{U}}_{\bm{A}}^{\top}
and
{\bm{B}}={\bm{U}}_{\bm{B}}\bm{\Lambda}_{\bm{B}}{\bm{S}}_{d,2r}\bm{\Lambda}_{\bm{B}}^{\top}{\bm{U}}_{\bm{B}}^{\top}
, where

\bm{\Lambda}_{\bm{A}}=\operatorname{diag}(\underbrace{\lambda_{1,{\bm{A}}},\lambda_{1,{\bm{A}}}}_{\text{twice}},\cdots,\underbrace{\lambda_{r,{\bm{A}}},\lambda_{r,{\bm{A}}}}_{\text{twice}},\underbrace{0,0,\dots,0}_{\text{remaining entries}})

and similarly for
\bm{\Lambda}_{\bm{B}}
.
Then, defining
{\bm{X}}=({\bm{U}}_{B}\bm{\Lambda}_{B})({\bm{U}}_{A}\bm{\Lambda}_{\bm{A}})^{-1}\in\mathrm{GL}_{d}({\mathbb{R}})

({\bm{X}},{\bm{A}})={\bm{B}}
.
∎

For the point
p
in the above lemma, we choose
{\bm{S}}_{d,2r}\in\mathrm{Skew}(d;2r)
.
Let us denote its stabilizer group as
S_{d,2r}:=\{{\bm{X}}\in\mathrm{GL}_{d-2r}({\mathbb{R}}):{\bm{X}}{\bm{S}}_{d,2r}{\bm{X}}^{\top}={\bm{S}}_{d,2r}\}
.

Claim H.2
.

S_{d,2r}
is closed in
\mathrm{GL}_{d}({\mathbb{R}})
.

Proof.

Consider a mapping
\rho:{\bm{X}}\mapsto{\bm{X}}{\bm{S}}_{d,2r}{\bm{X}}^{\top}
, which is continuous.
Noting that
S_{d,2r}=\rho^{-1}(\{{\bm{S}}_{d,2r}\})
and that
\{{\bm{S}}_{d,2r}\}
is closed (in Hausdorff space, which
\mathrm{GL}_{d}({\mathbb{R}})
is),
S_{d,2r}
is also closed by continuity.
∎

We now characterize
S_{d,2r}
.

Using block matrix notation, we need to characterize
{\bm{X}}=\begin{bmatrix}{\bm{X}}_{11}&{\bm{X}}_{12}\\
{\bm{X}}_{21}&{\bm{X}}_{22}\end{bmatrix}
such that
{\bm{X}}
is invertible and
{\bm{X}}{\bm{S}}_{2r}{\bm{X}}^{\top}={\bm{S}}_{2r}
.
After some tedious computations, we have that

\begin{bmatrix}{\bm{X}}_{11}{\bm{S}}_{2r}{\bm{X}}_{11}^{\top}&{\bm{X}}_{11}{\bm{S}}_{2r}{\bm{X}}_{21}^{\top}\\
{\bm{X}}_{21}{\bm{S}}_{2r}{\bm{X}}_{11}^{\top}&{\bm{X}}_{21}{\bm{S}}_{2r}{\bm{X}}_{21}^{\top}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}{\bm{S}}_{2r}&{\bm{0}}_{2r\times(d-2r)}\\
{\bm{0}}_{(d-2r)\times 2r}&{\bm{0}}_{2r\times 2r}\end{bmatrix}.

Consider the first block.
Taking the determinant, we can deduce that
\det({\bm{X}}_{11})^{2}=1\neq 0
, i.e.,
{\bm{X}}_{11}
should be invertible.
As
{\bm{S}}_{2r}
is also invertible, the antidiagonal blocks implies that
{\bm{X}}_{21}={\bm{0}}_{(d-2r)\times 2r}
.

So far, we have that
{\bm{X}}
should be of the form

{\bm{X}}=\begin{bmatrix}{\bm{X}}_{11}&{\bm{X}}_{12}\\
{\bm{0}}_{(d-2r)\times 2r}&{\bm{X}}_{22},\end{bmatrix}

where
{\bm{X}}_{11}\in\mathrm{Sym}(2p):=\{{\bm{X}}\in\mathrm{GL}_{n}({\mathbb{R}}):{\bm{X}}{\bm{S}}_{2r}{\bm{X}}^{\top}={\bm{X}}\}
.
By Schur’s determinant formula, as
{\bm{X}}
must be invertible, we must have that

\det({\bm{X}})=\det({\bm{X}}_{11})\det({\bm{X}}_{22})\neq 0,

i.e.,
{\bm{X}}_{22}
should also be invertible.

We now derive the dimension of
\mathrm{GL}_{d-2r}({\mathbb{R}})

\mathrm{Sym}(2r)
.

Claim H.3
.

\dim(\mathrm{GL}_{d-2r}({\mathbb{R}}))=(d-2r)^{2}
.

Proof.

Let
n=d-2r
.
Then, note that
\mathrm{GL}_{n}({\mathbb{R}})=\det^{-1}({\mathbb{R}}\setminus\{0\})
.
As
\det
is continuous and
{\mathbb{R}}\setminus\{0\}
is open,
\mathrm{GL}_{n}({\mathbb{R}})\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times n}
is open, and we are done.
∎

Claim H.4
.

\dim(\mathrm{Sym}(2r))=2r^{2}+r
.

Proof.

We do this by counting the number of independent constraints, then subtracting it from
\dim(\mathrm{GL}_{2r}({\mathbb{R}}))=4r^{2}
.
Let us denote
{\bm{S}}:=\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\
-1&0\end{bmatrix}
for simplicity.
First, for a
{\bm{A}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2\times 2}
, note that

{\bm{A}}{\bm{S}}{\bm{A}}^{\top}=\det({\bm{A}}){\bm{S}}.

Now consider a
{\bm{X}}\in\mathrm{GL}_{2r}({\mathbb{R}})
, consisting of
r
number of
2\times 2
blocks:

{\bm{X}}=\begin{bmatrix}{\bm{X}}_{11}&{\bm{X}}_{12}&\cdots&{\bm{X}}_{1r}\\
{\bm{X}}_{21}&{\bm{X}}_{22}&\cdots&{\bm{X}}_{2r}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
{\bm{X}}_{r1}&{\bm{X}}_{r2}&\cdots&{\bm{X}}_{rr}\end{bmatrix}.

Then, by the block matrix multiplication and the above result, we have that

\displaystyle\left({\bm{X}}{\bm{S}}_{2r}{\bm{X}}^{\top}\right)_{i,j}=\begin{cases}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{r}\det({\bm{X}}_{ik})\right){\bm{S}},\quad&i=j,\\
\sum_{k=1}^{r}{\bm{X}}_{ik}{\bm{J}}{\bm{X}}_{kj}^{\top},\quad&i\neq j\end{cases}=\begin{cases}{\bm{S}},\quad&i=j,\\
{\bm{0}}_{2\times 2},\quad&i\neq j\end{cases}.

where here,
(\cdot)_{i,j}
refers to the
2\times 2
block at the
(i,j)
location.

There are
r
constraints for
i=j
and
4\binom{r}{2}=2r(r-1)
constraints for
i\neq j
, which amounts to
2r^{2}-r
constraints in total.
Thus, the dimension of
\mathrm{Sym}(2r)
becomes
4r^{2}-(2r^{2}-r)=2r^{2}+r
.
∎

All in all, we have that

\displaystyle\dim(S_{d,2r})

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\dim(\mathrm{Sym}(2r))}_{\text{degrees of freedom for }{\bm{X}}_{11}}+\underbrace{\dim({\mathbb{R}}^{2r\times(d-2r)})}_{\text{degrees of freedom for }{\bm{X}}_{12}}+\underbrace{\dim(\mathrm{GL}_{d-2r}({\mathbb{R}}))}_{\text{degrees of freedom for }{\bm{X}}_{22}}

\displaystyle=(2r^{2}+r)+2r(d-2r)+(d-2r)^{2}

\displaystyle=d^{2}+2r^{2}+r-2dr.

Applying Lemma
H.4
, we have that

\dim(\mathrm{Skew}(d;2r))=\dim(\mathrm{GL}_{d}({\mathbb{R}}))-\dim(S_{d,2r})=2dr-(2r^{2}+r).

∎

Reduction to

To our knowledge,
Wu et al. 2024
is the only comparable competitor in our setting of Borda regret minimization.
To do that, we first describe how to reduce our bilinear dueling bandits to their setting.
Recall that
Wu et al. 2024
require vector-valued features for each pair of items,
\bm{\phi}_{i,j}=-\bm{\phi}_{j,i}
.
As
\bm{\Theta}_{\star}=\widetilde{\bm{\Theta}}_{\star}-\widetilde{\bm{\Theta}}_{\star}^{\top}
for some
\widetilde{\bm{\Theta}}_{\star}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d\times d}
, one can rewrite the bilinear preference as

\mu\left(\bm{\phi}_{i}^{\top}(\widetilde{\bm{\Theta}}_{\star}-\widetilde{\bm{\Theta}}_{\star}^{\top})\bm{\phi}_{j}\right)=\mu\left(\left\langle\widetilde{\bm{\Theta}}_{\star},\bm{\phi}_{i}\bm{\phi}_{j}-\bm{\phi}_{j}\bm{\phi}_{i}^{\top}\right\rangle\right).

One may be tempted to set
\bm{\phi}_{i,j}=\mathrm{vec}(\bm{\phi}_{i}\bm{\phi}_{j}^{\top}-\bm{\phi}_{j}\bm{\phi}_{i}^{\top})
.
However, recalling the discussions from
Section

H.3.2
, one must set
\bm{\phi}_{i,j}={\bm{P}}_{A}^{\top}\mathrm{vec}(\bm{\phi}_{i}\bm{\phi}_{j}^{\top}-\bm{\phi}_{j}\bm{\phi}_{i}^{\top})
for
\bm{\phi}_{i,j}
’s to be able to fully span
{\mathbb{R}}^{\wedge 2}
.
Setting
\bm{\theta}_{\star}={\bm{P}}_{A}^{\top}\mathrm{vec}(\widetilde{\bm{\Theta}}_{\star})
and the reduction is complete.

Regret Upper Bound.

A naïve application of the algorithm of
Wu et al. 2024
using the above reduction attains a Borda regret bound of
\widetilde{{\mathcal{O}}}(c_{\mu}^{-1}d^{4/3}T^{2/3})
up to some epsilon-net error (see their Remark 5.3), where

c_{\mu}:=\min_{\left\lVert{\bm{x}}\right\rVert_{2}\leq 1,\left\lVert\bm{\theta}-\bm{\theta}_{\star}\right\rVert\leq 1}\dot{\mu}(\langle{\bm{x}},\bm{\theta}\rangle)>0.

(73)

They have also assumed that
\lambda_{\min}({\bm{V}}(\pi^{U}))\geq\lambda_{0}
for some constant
\lambda_{0}>0
, where
\pi^{U}\sim\mathrm{Unif}({\mathcal{A}}\times{\mathcal{A}})

(
Wu et al. 2024
, Assumption 3.1)
.
We remark that in many cases,
\lambda_{0}
is
not
constant and can be arbitrarily small dimension-wise. In particular, both
Wu et al. 2024
and our work assumes
\|\phi_{i,j}\|_{2}\leq 1
, one can prove that
\lambda_{0}\leq\frac{1}{d^{2}}
for any
{\mathcal{A}}
under this assumption and it is
impossible
to make
\lambda_{0}
as a constant, since

\displaystyle\mathrm{tr}\left({\bm{V}}(\pi)\right)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{tr}\left(\sum_{i,j}\pi(\phi_{i,j})\phi_{i,j}\phi_{i,j}^{\top}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j}\pi(\phi_{i,j})\mathrm{tr}\left(\phi_{i,j}\phi_{i,j}^{\top}\right)

(Linearity of
\mathrm{tr}
)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i,j}\left(\pi(\phi_{i,j})\right)=1

(For a vector
v

\mathrm{tr}(vv^{\top})=\|v\|_{2}^{2}
and
\phi_{i,j}\leq 1
)

and
\mathrm{tr}({\bm{V}}(\pi))=\sum_{i=1}^{d^{2}}\lambda_{i}({\bm{V}}(\pi))

Still, for a fair comparison, let us first compare with our bound under the same assumption.
By
Theorem

5.1
and

, our

\widetilde{{\mathcal{O}}}\left(\left(\frac{\left(\kappa_{\star}^{B}\right)^{2}}{\lambda_{0}\kappa_{\star}}\right)^{1/3}d^{1/3}T^{2/3}\right)
. While the regret depends on the geometry of
{\mathcal{A}}

{\mathcal{A}}
is ill-distributed, our algorithm demonstrates a superior regret bound in terms of

in many practical scenarios. Notably, when

, which holds in a wide range of common settings, our method outperforms

-dependency of
Wu et al. 2024

Curvature-wise, it is easy to see that
c_{\mu}\leq\kappa_{\star}^{B}
, and the gap may be large
(
Faury et al. 2020
, Section 2)
.
Indeed, our Borda regret bound analysis provides an interesting quantity that determines the problem difficulty,
\frac{(\kappa_{\star}^{B})^{2}}{\kappa_{\star}}
, which has not been reported before.
Let us first recall their definitions:

\kappa_{\star}:=\min_{\bm{\phi},\bm{\phi}^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{A}}}\dot{\mu}\left(\bm{\phi}^{\top}\bm{\Theta}_{\star}\bm{\phi}^{\prime}\right),\quad\kappa_{\star}^{B}:=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\phi}^{\prime}\sim\mathrm{Unif}({\mathcal{A}})}[\dot{\mu}(\bm{\phi}^{\top}\bm{\Theta}\bm{\phi}^{\prime})].

(74)

\kappa_{\star}
is the worst-case flatness across all pairs of arms while
\kappa_{\star}^{B}
is the worst-case flatness for the
Borda winner
vs. other arms.
This then gives the interpretation that if the hardness of identifying the true winner for all possible pairs is of same order (i.e.,
\kappa_{\star}^{B}\asymp\kappa_{\star}
), then our regret bound scales as
\widetilde{{\mathcal{O}}}(\kappa_{\star}^{1/3}(dT)^{2/3})
, i.e., flatter problem indicates lower permanent regret.
Here, permanent means the regime after identifying
\bm{\Theta}_{\star}

(
Abeille et al. 2021
)
.
On the other hand, if there exists an item pair such that identifying the true winner is much harder than that when one of the items is the Borda winner (e.g.,
(\kappa_{\star}^{B})^{2}\asymp\kappa_{\star}
), then our permanent regret does not benefit from the flatness.
This is because our
GL-LowPopArt
exploits the low-rankness of
{\mathcal{A}}
(which is of rank
1
) and the parameter space
\mathrm{Skew}(d;2r)
, analogous to bilinear bandits
(
Jun et al. 2019
;
Jang et al. 2021
)
and low-rank bandits
(
Jang et al. 2024
;
Lu et al. 2021
;
Kang et al. 2022
)
.

Remark 13
.

Surprisingly, our regret bound is independent of the rank
r
of the matrix
\bm{\Theta}_{\star}
, which is also the case for bilinear bandits
(
Jang et al. 2021
, Theorem 4.6)
albeit for a different reason.
We believe that this showcases how
GL-LowPopArt
is adaptive to the arm-set geometry of
{\mathcal{A}}\subseteq{\mathcal{B}}^{d\times d}_{\mathrm{op}}(1)
, quantified by
\mathrm{GL}_{\min}({\mathcal{A}})\leq\frac{d}{\kappa_{\star}\lambda_{0}}
.

Regret Lower Bound.

Wu et al. 2024
obtain a regret lower bound of
\Omega(d^{2/3}T^{2/3})
for
\bm{\phi}_{i,j},\bm{\theta}_{\star}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, and a similar lower bound for unstructured dueling bandits has been obtained by
Saha et al. 2021
;
T^{2/3}
stems from the fact that the exploration and exploitation cannot be mixed.
This suggests that at least in terms of

is also optimal.

However, their lower bound cannot be directly applied to our setting, as our bilinear dueling bandits, in essence, constrain the matrix arm to be of rank-1.
It is clear that their hard instance, based on the lower bound for stochastic linear bandits
(
Dani et al. 2008

We leave obtaining a tight lower bound to future work, considering how even in stochastic bilinear bandits (non-dueling), the lower bound remains open
(
Kotłowski & Neu 2019

Jang et al. 2021
;
Jun et al. 2019

A potential starting point may be from the regret lower bound of

J.3
Ablation: Necessity of Stage I

A natural question is whether Stage I is truly necessary in practice. Theoretically, Stage I provides an asymptotically consistent initial estimator that linearizes the problem, which is essential for the self-concordance analysis underlying the Stage II Catoni estimator.

We empirically investigate this by comparing Stage II performance under four different initializations: U+GL, E+GL, 0-GL (a zero initialization:
\bm{\Theta}_{0}={\bm{0}}
), and Rand-GL (a random Gaussian initialization:
\bm{\Theta}_{0}\sim{\mathcal{N}}(0,1)^{d_{1}\times d_{2}}
).
For the latter two initializations (which we refer to as “naïve”, we allocate the entire sample budget
N
to Stage II. For (i) and (ii), we follow the same protocol as done previously, splitting the budget into
N_{1}=\lfloor N/2\rfloor
for Stage I and
N_{2}=N-N_{1}
for Stage II.

As illustrated in
Figure

2
, the MLE-based initializations from Stage I (both with and without the E-optimal design) significantly outperform the naïve alternatives; notably, those alternatives’ errors do not decay with the number of samples.
This highlights the practical importance of Stage I in reducing bias and enabling effective downstream estimation in Stage II.
