The hidden consequences of marine heatwaves: rising Vibrio risk in European coastal waters
Authors: Jan C Semenza, Joaquin A Trinanes, Jaime Martinez-Urtaza
Published: 2026

It is becoming alarmingly easy to grow accustomed to an unrelenting succession of broken climate records. June 2026 was the warmest June ever recorded in Western Europe, with mean temperatures reaching 3.06 °C above the 1991–2020 average and surpassing the previous record set only a year earlier.1 While prolonged terrestrial heatwaves dominate public attention, because of their immediate impacts on human health, they often overshadow equally profound changes occurring in the oceans. During the same month, the western Mediterranean Sea and adjacent Atlantic coastal waters experienced a record-breaking marine heatwave.1 These events illustrate a defining feature of the climate system: the world’s oceans have absorbed approximately 90% of the excess heat generated by greenhouse gas emissions.2

Unlike elevated air temperatures, whose health consequences — including excess mortality during heatwaves — are well recognized, rising sea surface temperatures have received comparatively little attention from the public health community. Yet warming coastal waters are reshaping marine ecosystems in ways that increasingly threaten human health.3 Among the most concerning consequences is the expanding distribution of pathogenic Vibrio species, including non-O1/non-O139 Vibrio cholerae, Vibrio vulnificus, Vibrio parahaemolyticus, and Vibrio alginolyticus, bacteria capable of causing severe gastroenteritis, wound infections, necrotizing fasciitis, septicemia, limb amputation, and death.2, 3, 4 These naturally occurring microorganisms flourish in warm, brackish environments such as estuaries and low-salinity coastal waters, particularly the Baltic Sea.

In Europe, Vibrio infections were once associated predominantly with the consumption of contaminated shellfish.4 Since 2010, however, the epidemiology has shifted towards waterborne transmission. Marine heatwaves have emerged as the principal driver of these infections, particularly ear, wound, and bloodstream infections, notably the Baltic Sea heatwave of 2018, that tripled infections over expected.5 Human exposure is amplified by the convergence of terrestrial and marine heatwaves: as air temperatures rise, more people seek relief in coastal waters precisely when elevated sea surface temperatures promote rapid Vibrio proliferation. Climate change is projected to expand and propagate the ensuing Vibrio infections to Northern latitudes.6 However, Vibrio risk associated with recreational water exposure has become evident in southern European countries, including France, Spain, Italy, and Bulgaria.7, 8, 9, 10 Many reported cases have involved necrotizing fasciitis, progressing to fatal sepsis. Although the prominence of these severe cases partly reflects detection bias, it also mirrors the demographic profile of many southern European coastal areas where older populations are disproportionately vulnerable. Men over 80 years of age, often with underlying comorbidities and diminished immune function, are especially susceptible to severe and fatal outcomes.3

Recent media reports warning of “flesh-eating bacteria” at European beaches have heightened public concern. These reports refer primarily to increased risk of necrotizing fasciitis resulting from severe Vibrio wound infections, in warming Mediterranean waters. Yet, the true disease burden remains difficult to quantify. In the absence of a coordinated European surveillance system, available evidence indicates that overall infection risk in the Mediterranean remains very low. High salinity across most of the basin, limits bacterial proliferation with elevated risk confined to a small number of localized, moderately saline coastal hotspots.

This evolving landscape brings European vibrio epidemiology closer to that long observed in the United States, demanding adequate surveillance. Because Vibrio infections are not yet routinely notifiable across Europe, we have developed a novel, near-real-time, high-resolution environmental suitability platform to monitor coastal conditions favorable for Vibrio growth (https://probono.usc.es/VIBRIO_EUROPE/). The platform identifies areas—including those in southern Europe—where environmental conditions are conducive to infection risk and provides five-day forecasts that can serve as an early warning resource for public health authorities, beach managers, aquaculture operators, fishers, sailors, and the public (Fig. 1). These forecasts are designed to enhance situational awareness rather than predict individual infections, as they estimate environmental suitability rather than bacterial abundance or direct human exposure, and should therefore be interpreted alongside local environmental monitoring and epidemiological information.

It integrates hydrodynamic analyses and forecasts from the Copernicus Marine Service with NOAA observations to estimate sea surface temperature and salinity.6 The underlying model combines sea surface temperature and salinity thresholds (temperature ≥18 °C and salinity < 26 PSU) to identify coastal environments suitable for pathogenic Vibrio species.6 The high-resolution approach, at a 1-km grid, enables detailed assessment of estuaries, river mouths, bays, deltas, and other partially enclosed coastal waters where freshwater mixes with seawater and conditions favor Vibrio proliferation (see insert). The platform overlays information on beaches, aquaculture sites, and nearby populations, within a 50-km radius, allowing for the identification of coastal areas where environmental suitability may coincide with increased opportunities for human exposure. Dots denote aquaculture with color denoting suitability. Yellow color along coastal areas indicates the population at risk living in those areas with suitable conditions for Vibrio spp. bacteria.

The recent introduction by the European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control of a voluntary reporting system for Vibrio infections marks an important step towards coordinated surveillance. With high-resolution environmental forecasting, the Vibrio suitability tool offers a powerful opportunity to identify emerging hotspots, strengthen early detection, and guide targeted public health interventions. As marine heatwaves become an increasingly persistent consequence of climate change, Europe must recognize that warming seas are not only an environmental concern but an emerging threat to human health. Environmental forecasting tools can help manage this growing risk, by alerting the public and health care providers and enacting beach closures, but lasting protection ultimately depends upon addressing its root cause through a rapid transition away from fossil fuel emissions.
