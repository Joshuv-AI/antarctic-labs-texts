Re: Grzegorz L. Fojecki, Elena Atrakhimovich, Martin Bruhn, et al. Reduced Rate of Complications with Transperineal Prostate Biopsy: Real-world Data from a National Cohort Derived from the Danish Prostate Cancer Registry. Eur Urol Open Sci 2026;87:1–7
Authors: Xiaowei Zhang, Zheng Wang
Published: 2026

We read with great interest the nationwide cohort study by Fojecki et al [1] evaluating the Danish transition from transrectal (TR) to transperineal (TP) prostate biopsy. The scale of the study and magnitude of the observed association are impressive: as TP biopsy uptake rose rapidly, the estimated 7-d hospitalization risk remained at around 0.8% for TP versus at approximately 4.8% for TR, even after adjustment. This is an important real-world contribution to the current discussion on biopsy safety.

However, what makes this paper especially thought-provoking is that its central message may be broader than “TP is safer than TR.” In this cohort, TR biopsy was performed under region-specific antibiotic protocols, while rectal swab screening and povidone-iodine rectal preparation were not introduced, despite evidence supporting these measures [2]. By contrast, prophylactic antibiotics were generally omitted in the TP pathway [1]. In other words, the study compares not only two needle routes, but also two distinct procedural ecosystems. That distinction matters. The Danish experience is highly persuasive, yet its most transferable lesson may be that a safer biopsy program depends on how the entire pathway is organized, rather than on the access route alone. Framing the result this way would make the study even more useful for centers where a fully optimized TR strategy is still the practical comparator rather than a historical one.

A second point concerns how clinicians communicate these findings to patients. The major strength of this study is its use of a hard, clinically meaningful endpoint—hospital admission within 7 d. Still, once infectious complications are substantially reduced, counseling conversation does not end with sepsis avoidance. Patients often care just as much about immediate non-infectious burdens, including urinary retention, pain, and chance of prolonged observation after biopsy. Notably, the authors cite prior population-level data showing more overnight stays and slightly higher rates of urinary retention-related admissions after TP biopsy, even when infection outcomes favor TP [3]. This does not weaken the argument for TP biopsy; rather, it sharpens it. The most patient-relevant comparison is not “infection versus no infection,” but whether the overall trade-off is acceptable, predictable, and well explained before the biopsy [4].

For that reason, we believe the paper’s clinical value would be further strengthened by explicitly presenting the Danish transition as a success of pathway redesign and by emphasizing that adoption of TP biopsy at bedside should be accompanied by equally careful counseling about the non-infectious aspects of the patient experience.
