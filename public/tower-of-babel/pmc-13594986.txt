Restoration Plantings Promote Functional Recovery of Soil Fungi
Authors: Carl D Watson, Alfie J Lem, Andrew Bissett, Christian Cando‐Dumancela, Michael G Gardner, Riley J Hodgson, Craig Liddicoat, Shawn D Peddle, Martin F Breed
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe global scale of biodiversity loss and land degradation has prompted the establishment of ambitious international restoration agreements, with the goal of returning biodiverse and functional ecosystems. Restoration interventions are often vegetation focused, and while they yield positive, measurable outcomes, they do not always lead to whole ecosystem recovery. Although soil microbiota such as fungi are integral ecosystem components (e.g., as symbionts, decomposers and drivers of nutrient cycling), they are largely absent from restoration planning and monitoring. Therefore, how fungi respond to ecosystem restoration, especially over longer time scales, is largely unknown. Here, we show trends of reduced dominance of soil fungal pathotrophs across a restoration chronosequence 6 years after initial sampling. This decline in soil fungal pathotrophs is consistent with continued functional recovery, despite little evidence of additional whole‐community fungal recovery. Fungal community composition was also strongly associated with soil properties, particularly phosphorus, consistent with persistent land‐use legacies constraining further recovery. The observed change in fungal functional composition is consistent with previous studies reporting higher relative abundance of pathotrophic fungi in ecologically degraded sites. Our study provides valuable insights into how soil fungal communities respond to restoration plantings and highlights the importance of repeated sampling. Our findings also demonstrate the value of considering both taxonomic and functional indicators when assessing ecosystem recovery.Keywords: chronosequence, ecosystem degradation, ecosystem restoration, eDNA, longitudinal sampling design, microbiome, restoration planting, soil fungi, soil microbiota

Introduction

Ambitious ecosystem restoration targets have been established globally (IUCN 2011; Verdone and Seidl 2017) in response to the alarming scale of land degradation and biodiversity loss (Gibbs and Salmon 2015; Ceballos et al. 2020). However, effective restoration (i.e., the recovery of resilient and biodiverse ecosystems) faces a conundrum: despite the tremendous demand, restoration ecology is deficient in long‐term research to guide restoration practices (Perring et al. 2015; Suding et al. 2015). Many restoration studies, particularly the few with a soil microbiota focus (recently reviewed by Watson et al. 2022), have adopted a chronosequence study design that use geographical space as a proxy for time since restoration interventions. These cross‐sectional studies involve collecting data at a single timepoint across a cluster of localised sites, typically including restoration planting sites of differing ages, local reference sites and un‐planted sites (Peddle et al. 2025). While chronosequence‐based studies allow for timely inferences of long‐term ecosystem dynamics, they cannot be used directly to determine causation (Damgaard 2019; Kreyling 2025; Peddle et al. 2025). However, combining chronosequence studies with a longitudinal component can bolster evidence of ecological change as the same restoration patches can be sampled through time (Lem et al. 2022).

The lack of long‐term research is reflected in the fact that restoration actions are often implemented without robust scientific evidence (Perring et al. 2015). The prevailing theory is that by creating suitable habitats (e.g., by establishing native vegetation communities), the return of associated ecosystem components will proceed, ultimately leading to complete restoration of an ecosystem (Hobbs et al. 2011; Guiden et al. 2021). Restoration efforts have primarily concentrated on the return of vegetation (Hilderbrand et al. 2005; Cross et al. 2020; but see also Chazdon et al. 2024 for discussion of the restoration intervention continuum), often yielding positive, measurable ecosystem outcomes, including increasing carbon sequestration and habitat provision (Crouzeilles et al. 2016; Prober et al. 2025). However, relying heavily on restoration plantings is a problem as ecosystems are highly complex and dynamic. In many cases, restoration plantings do not lead to whole ecosystem recovery (Hillebrand et al. 2018; Brudvig et al. 2021; Watson et al. 2022). As a result, there are knowledge gaps on whether restoration plantings achieve their desired ecosystem outcomes (e.g., return of reference‐ecosystem‐like biological communities) including for some of the most important ecosystem components such as microbiota.

Soil microbial communities (e.g., fungi, bacteria, archaea) are often overlooked in restoration practice (Breed et al. 2019; Markovchick et al. 2023; Wood et al. 2026). Assessing their recovery is challenging because these communities are highly complex and variable, making it hard to distinguish responses to restoration from background ecological fluctuations (Wood et al. 2026). Nevertheless, soil microbial communities perform many key ecological roles, including nutrient cycling, organic matter decomposition, contributing to soil‐structure formation, and influencing plant growth and health (Bakker et al. 2014; Trivedi et al. 2019; Jansson and Hofmockel 2020). Understanding how these communities respond to ecosystem restoration is therefore essential for evaluating restoration outcomes, setting realistic restoration goals and ultimately achieving effective restoration outcomes (Harris 2009; Peddle et al. 2025; Wood et al. 2026).

Soil fungi are particularly relevant to restoration plantings because they interact closely with vegetation through processes including mycorrhizal symbioses, decomposition of plant detrital matter and plant disease (van der Heijden et al. 2015; Niskanen et al. 2023; Lackmann et al. 2025). Changes in soil fungal community composition may therefore both influence and reflect the development of restoration plant communities. Despite their ecological importance, long‐term responses of soil fungal communities to restoration remain less well characterised than those of many aboveground ecosystem components (Watson et al. 2022; Markovchick et al. 2023).

Here, we employed high‐throughput amplicon sequencing of the fungal ribosomal internal transcribed spacer region (ITS) to examine soil fungal communities at two time points (December 2014 and April 2020) across a well‐studied restoration planting chronosequence at Mt Bold, South Australia. Our first hypothesis was that fungal communities would differ between cleared (degraded), restoration planting and ecological reference sites. Our second hypothesis was that, after an additional 6 years, the soil fungal communities in restoration planting sites would show further evidence of recovery, becoming more similar to reference sites, and that fungal communities in cleared and reference sites would remain distinct. Finally, we hypothesised that with further time since restoration, the functional profile of restoration planting sites would become more similar to reference sites.

Methods

Study Design

We sampled soil at a large‐scale restoration project at Mt Bold, South Australia (35.07° S, 138.42° E) on lands managed by South Australia's water utility (SA Water; Figure 1). Mt Bold is a water catchment reserve spanning 5500 ha in the Mt Lofty Ranges, a region recognised as a national biodiversity hotspot (Guerin et al. 2014). Large parts of Mt Bold have experienced severe ecological degradation, with clearing and low‐density grazing occurring for over a century until 2003 (Armstrong et al. 2003). As part of restoration efforts between 2005 and 2009, extensive restoration plantings using consistent practices occurred with the objective of restoring the open Eucalyptus leucoxylon grassy woodland that originally dominated the catchment area prior to clearing. This included shallow surface ripping and planting of local native species (including: overstory Eucalyptus leucoxylon , Eucalyptus viminalis ; shrub layer Acacia pycnantha , Bursaria spinosa ssp. spinosa, Dodonaea viscosa ), which took place in late winter across all sites. Following planting, fencing was installed to exclude livestock, and annual manual slashing was conducted to suppress weed growth. Consequently, a restoration chronosequence was established, and soil samples were collected from sites that underwent restoration plantings in 2005, 2007, 2008 and 2009 (Figure 1, Figure S1). Each planting year was represented by a different restoration site, meaning that planting age was partly confounded with site identity, and differences among planting years cannot be attributed unequivocally to time since restoration.

Map of the sampling sites within the Mt Bold water catchment reserve in the Mt Lofty Ranges, South Australia. For quadrat latitudes and longitudes, see Table S1. For photographs of the sampling sites, see Figure S1.

We selected three remnant grassy woodland sites adjacent to the restoration project as ecological reference sites (hereafter referred to as reference). Each of the three reference sites consisted of open grassy woodland dominated by Eucalyptus leucoxylon , which is a characteristic vegetation type in the area and was the desired ecosystem type outcome of the restoration efforts. Reference Site A had undergone some clearing within the past century and had a history of low‐density grazing, while Reference Sites B and C remained undisturbed without any historical clearing or grazing activities. All reference sites experienced the same management, primarily focusing on minor weed control measures to support conservation objectives. We also selected a cleared site that represented the degraded conditions of the planting sites prior to restoration intervention. The cleared site was located near (< 0.8 km) both a restoration planting site (2009 Planting) and a reference site (Reference Site C), and was managed with yearly mowing to function as a fire break (Figure 1, Figure S1).

Soil Sampling

Soil sampling was carried out in December 2014 and April 2020 following the Australian Microbiome protocols (Bissett et al. 2016; Australian Microbiome 2018). Three 25 m × 25 m quadrats at least 25 m apart were sampled at each of the sites, except Reference Site C which had four 25 m × 25 m quadrats, giving a total of 25 quadrats and a minimum of three replicates per treatment (for quadrat latitudes and longitudes, see Table S1). Quadrats were randomly selected for the 2014 sampling event (Yan et al. 2018), and the identical locations were used in the 2020 sampling event.

In each quadrat, we selected nine subsample points to capture the heterogeneity within the quadrat. At each subsample point, we collected soil from two different depths, specifically 0–10 and 20–30 cm. This resulted in a total of nine subsamples from each soil depth. To prevent cross‐contamination, we sterilised the sampling implements using Decon 90 and 5% bleach between quadrats and between soil depths, following the approach described by Cando‐Dumancela et al. (2021, 2023). The soil collected from each quadrat was combined and homogenised in a sterile plastic bag in the field. This process yielded a representative 500 g sample from each soil depth within each quadrat. From each homogenised sample, approx. 50 g subsamples were taken and stored in sterile 50 mL tubes, which were then frozen (−20°C) prior to DNA extraction the following week. For chemical analysis, we sent 300 g of each sample to CSBP Laboratories in Perth, Western Australia. The chemical analysis included quantification of soil moisture, nitrate, ammonium, phosphorus, organic carbon and pH. For detailed information on the methods used for quantifying soil physical and chemical properties, see Bissett et al. (2016).

DNA Extraction, Sequencing and Sequencing Analysis

Frozen subsamples were sent to the Australian Genome Research Facility (AGRF) in Urrbrae, South Australia where DNA was extracted using MoBio PowerLyzer PowerSoil DNA Isolation Kit and pooled in triplicate from each technical replicate. The fungal ITS region was PCR amplified using the ITS1F (CTTGGTCATTTAGAGGAAGTAA) and ITS4 (TCCTCCGCTTATTGATATGC) primers (Innis et al. 1990; White et al. 1990; Gardes and Bruns 1993). Amplicons were sequenced using paired‐end sequencing on the Illumina MiSeq platform.

Sequence data were generated by Australian Microbiome (AM) using their amplicon analysis workflow (Bissett et al. 2016; Australian Microbiome 2022b) and were downloaded as zero‐radius OTU (zOTU) abundance tables from the AM data portal (sample numbers 19281–19330, 140218–140267; Australian Microbiome 2022a; downloaded 18 November 2022). Both the 2014 and 2020 samples were processed with the same AM analysis workflow (v2.2.0), and from the same database version (v3.2_202203021355). A brief summary of the AM workflow is as follows. Fungal ITS1 and ITS2 regions were identified and isolated from neighbouring ribosomal genes with ITSx (Bengtsson‐Palme et al. 2013). Mothur's screen.seqs command (Schloss et al. 2009) was used to screen and remove reads that were too short/long, had ambiguities, or long homopolymer runs. Reads passing these filtering steps were dereplicated and de‐noised to zOTUs using the UNOISE3 algorithm in USEARCH (Edgar 2010). All reads were then mapped to zOTUs to construct a zOTU by read count table. zOTUs were assigned taxonomy with the RDP Bayesian classifier (Wang et al. 2007) with the UNITEv8 ITS database (Abarenkov et al. 2024). zOTUs not classified as Fungi at the kingdom level were excluded from the data before downloading.

Statistical Analysis

All subsequent statistical analyses were performed using R v4.2.2 (R Core Team 2022). The sequence data were filtered to avoid unrepresentative taxa by removing zOTUs that did not occur in at least two samples and those that were unclassified at the phylum level. One sample (ID 140232, cleared site, 0–10 cm, 2020 sampling event) was re‐sequenced due to an initial low read count (1010 total reads). However, as the re‐sequenced sample was compositionally inconsistent with all other samples, it was removed from further richness and compositional analysis. Due to the coarser resolution of trophic modes, all 100 samples were retained in the trophic mode analysis. Samples were then rarefied without replacement to 20,630 reads, the lowest sequencing depth of the remaining samples. Our final dataset consisted of 2,042,370 sequences and 8623 unique zOTUs across our 99 samples.

Richness

Fungal richness was assessed by calculating observed richness (zOTU counts), and estimating Chao 1 richness, Gini‐Simpson (Simpson) and Shannon–Weiner (Shannon) diversity indices with the estimate_richness function in the phyloseq v1.38.0 package (McMurdie and Holmes 2013). To examine the effect of both restoration treatment and soil depth on these diversity metrics, a permutational ANOVA was used with 5000 permutations using the aovp function in the lmperm v.2.1.0 package (Wheeler and Torchiano 2016).

Composition

Bray–Curtis dissimilarity was used to quantify compositional differences. Fungal community composition variation (beta diversity) was visualised with non‐metric multidimensional scaling (NMDS) of the Bray–Curtis dissimilarities of rarefied zOTU abundances across restoration treatments using ordinate in the phyloseq v1.38.0 package. To assess fungal community composition variation across restoration treatments, permuted multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA) was applied via adonis2 in the vegan v2.6.4 package (Oksanen et al. 2019). Homogeneity of group dispersions was tested with the Betadisper function in vegan. The trajectory of soil microbiota composition differences across the restoration chronosequence was visualised using boxplots of pairwise Bray–Curtis dissimilarity to reference values for each sample site, faceted by sampling event and soil depth (Liddicoat et al. 2022). To determine differences between the similarity to reference values across restoration treatments, we used a Kruskal–Wallis test and a post hoc Dunn test using the FSA v0.9.1 package (Ogle 2018).

Soil Physicochemical Properties and Composition

To adjust for different units, the soil physicochemical properties were standardised with the scale function in R. To examine and select the variables that best predicted the differences in fungal community composition, stepwise model selection was run using the ordistep function in the vegan v2.6.4 package (Oksanen et al. 2019). To test whether there was an overall effect, a multivariable PERMANOVA was applied with the adonis2 function in vegan with 999 permutations. A distance‐based redundancy analysis (dbRDA) was used to visualise and explore which variables were important and how they influenced fungal community composition along the chronosequence using the dbrda function in vegan. The dbRDA analysis constrains an ordination of community composition by the combined effects of soil physicochemical properties. An ANOVA was run on the dbRDA to examine the contributions of variables to community composition patterns using the anova.cca function in vegan (Oksanen et al. 2019).

Functional Composition

The FungalTraits database (Põlme et al. 2020) was used to examine changes in the functional composition of fungal communities. The database was cross‐referenced at the genus level to assign a primary fungal lifestyle (e.g., soil saprotroph, ectomycorrhizal, plant pathogen). A corresponding broad trophic mode was then assigned, consistent with the definitions used commonly in fungal community ecology (saprotroph = receiving nutrients by breaking down dead host cells; symbiotroph = receiving nutrients by exchanging resources with host cells; pathotroph = receiving nutrients by harming host cells; Nguyen et al. 2016). For our analysis, we focused only on the primary lifestyle and corresponding primary trophic mode, consistent with the methods of Qu et al. (2024). We generated stack plots to visualise the mean relative abundance of primary lifestyle and trophic modes across the chronosequence. To account for the inherent compositionality of the data, we applied a transformation to the trophic mode relative abundances. Log‐ratio transformations are a standard approach in compositional data analysis and provide a compositionally‐adjusted measure of relative abundance, allowing univariate inference (Aitchison 1982; Greenacre et al. 2023). To compare overall differences in trophic mode ALR log‐ratio values between sampling events (i.e., 2014 vs. 2020), we used a Wilcoxon signed‐rank test with the wilcox.test function in R. To control the family‐wise error rate due to multiple comparisons, we applied the Holm correction using the p.adjust function in R.

Similar to our whole fungal community composition analysis, a return of functional composition to a reference‐like state can imply a degree of recovery. We expected that with further time since restoration, the functional profile of restoration planting sites would become more similar to the reference sites. To quantify differences over time, the relative abundance of each of the primary trophic modes was compared to the reference sites, stratifying by sampling event (i.e., treatment‐reference contrasts were calculated separately for each sampling event). For this part of the analysis, the three reference sites (n = 3, 3, 4) were grouped. A ratio was calculated, comparing the relative abundance for each treatment sample (n = 3 per treatment), to each grouped reference sample (n = 10), for a total of 30 comparisons per treatment. A log transformed ratio was also calculated to examine the variation within the grouped reference sites. As the assignment of numerator and denominator determines the sign of a log transformed ratio, each of the reference to reference sample comparisons was calculated in each direction (i.e., Sample 1/Sample 2, and Sample 2/Sample 1). This resulted in a total of 90 pairwise comparisons, P(10,2), and the mean ratio of reference compared to reference being 0.

Due to heterogeneous distributions, permutation tests were used to quantify the mean differences in trophic mode similarity to reference values between the two sampling events. For each of the trophic modes and treatment levels, the log transformed ratio of similarity to reference values for 2014 and 2020 was pooled. The pooled values were then randomly subsampled and compared to the observed mean difference between the two sampling events. This random resampling was repeated 9999 times and a p‐value was calculated. This was repeated for each of the trophic modes (n = 3), and treatments compared to reference (n = 5). The p.adjust function and Holm method in R were used to control the family‐wise error rate. To account for the compositionality of the data, analogous permutation tests were also conducted on the mean difference in ALR log‐ratio values between treatment and reference sites.

Spatial Autocorrelation

Spatial autocorrelation analysis was carried out to examine how much sampling location impacted fungal community composition. A geographic haversine distance matrix was calculated using the distm function in the geosphere package v1.5.18 (Hijmans 2024), and community composition was expressed as a Bray–Curtis dissimilarity matrix with the vegdist function in the vegan v2.6.4 package (Oksanen et al. 2019). The mantel function with the spearman method and 9999 permutations in the vegan v2.6.4 package was used to run a Mantel test to find correlations between the two matrices (Oksanen et al. 2019).

Effect of Season of Sampling on Composition

As our 2014 samples were collected in December and 2020 samples in April, there is potential that differences observed are explained in part by the time of the year the sampling occurred. For example, fungal growth rates can be impacted by soil moisture levels, and therefore it follows that community composition may differ with seasonal climatic variation (Bahram et al. 2018; Xie and Yin 2022). To assess if season impacted fungal composition, we analysed fungal amplicons of comparable soil samples. We did this by searching the Australian Microbiome database (Australian Microbiome 2022a; accessed 18 November 2022) for datasets with comparable land use (e.g., conservation or natural environments), biome type (e.g., temperate/Mediterranean) and vegetation type (e.g., woodland; see Table S2 for full filtering step details). A total of 344 AM samples were identified for analysis of seasonal effects on fungal composition. We retained only sequences classified as Fungi to the phylum level, rarefying these to the lowest sequencing depth (n = 19,228).

Seasonal fungal community composition variation (beta diversity) was visualised with non‐metric multidimensional scaling (NMDS) of the Bray–Curtis dissimilarity from rarefied zOTU abundances, using ordinate in the phyloseq v1.38.0 package (McMurdie and Holmes 2013). To assess fungal community composition variation across sampling seasons, permuted multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA) was applied via adonis2 in the vegan v2.6.4 package (Oksanen et al. 2019). Homogeneity of group dispersions was tested with betadisper in vegan.

Results

Sequences were downloaded as de‐noised zOTU abundance tables from the Australian Microbiome portal on 18 November 2022. After filtering to include only sequences classified as fungi to the Phylum level, sequences occurring in at least two samples, and after rarefaction (to 20,630 reads), 2,042,370 sequences of 8623 unique zOTUs remained. Fungal Community Composition

Both the 2014 and 2020 datasets individually showed a clear trend of fungal community composition becoming progressively more similar to reference sites with time since restoration (Figures 2 and 3; Figure S5). Fungal community composition varied by restoration treatment and soil depth for both the 2014 and 2020 data (2014 sampling PERMANOVA: restoration treatment R 2 = 0.381, F = 3.7326, p = 0.001; soil depth R 2 = 0.020, F = 1.3828, p = 0.047; 2020 sampling PERMANOVA: restoration treatment R 2 = 0.428, F = 4.5357, p = 0.001; soil depth R 2 = 0.034, F = 2.5068, p = 0.001; Figure 2). Heterogeneity of community composition was not different within treatment groups (2014 sampling multivariate homogeneity of group dispersions: p = 0.128; 2020 sampling: p = 0.106). For both 2014 and 2020, samples clustered by restoration treatment and were distributed across NMDS axis 1, indicating that the community composition of planting sites became increasingly similar to reference sites with time since restoration plantings (Figure 2).

With both sampling events combined, there was an effect of the interaction between sampling event and restoration treatment (PERMANOVA with quadrat as strata term R 2 = 0.109, F = 2.2155, p = 0.001; see Table S3 for results of all terms and interactions), indicating that treatment‐related variation differed between sampling events. However, these results may partly reflect differences in group dispersions (permuted homogeneity of group dispersions: p < 0.05 for all terms and interactions). When the two sampling events were combined into a single ordination, the chronosequence clustering remained; however, there was separation by sampling event across NMDS axis 2 (Figure S5).

We visualised compositional changes across the chronosequence with trajectory plots—similarity to reference values calculated from Bray–Curtis dissimilarities (Figure 3). The similarity to reference values differed across the restoration treatments at each sampling event and both soil depths (Kruskal–Wallis: p < 0.001 for each subset of sampling event and soil depth). Individually, each subset shows a trend of planting sites becoming more similar to reference with planting age. In the 2014 data, the reference sites were distinct from all sites except the 2005 and 2007 plantings at both soil depths (Figure 3). In the samples collected in 2020, the reference sites were distinct from all sites except the 2005 planting at 20–30 cm, and the 2007 planting at both soil depths (Figure 3). For both of the sampling events, the highest median similarity to reference occurred in the 2007 planting site (8.8%, 0–10 cm, 2014 sampling; 16.2% 0–10 cm, 2020 sampling). The lowest median similarity to reference for 2014 occurred in the 2009 planting site (2.3%, 20–30 cm); however, the lowest median similarity in 2020 was in the cleared site (4.4%, 20–30 cm). There was also noticeable inter‐reference site variation in each dataset, with an inter quartile range in the 2014 reference site samples of 9.6 and 7.9 at 0–10 and 20–30 cm depths, and 12.3 and 7.1 at 0–10 and 20–30 cm depths in the 2020 reference site samples.

Soil Physicochemical Properties and Composition

The multivariable PERMANOVA identified strong associations between fungal community composition and soil physicochemical properties. Soil moisture (R 2 = 0.037, F = 4.169, p = 0.001), phosphorus (R 2 = 0.033, F = 3.734, p = 0.001), pH (R 2 = 0.032, F = 3.636, p = 0.001), organic carbon (R 2 = 0.023, F = 2.564, p = 0.001), nitrogen (R 2 = 0.022, F = 2.518, p = 0.001), sulphur (R 2 = 0.013, F = 1.491, p = 0.023) and clay content (R 2 = 0.014, F = 1.589, p = 0.009) each strongly associated with community composition. Soil depth (R 2 = 0.024, F = 2.725, p = 0.001) and sampling event (R 2 = 0.017, F = 1.907, p = 0.003) also showed strong effects. Combined, these factors explained 21.7% of the variation in community composition.

When constraining the ordination via dbRDA, the constraining variables collectively explained 17.54% of the total variance in soil fungal community composition. The plotted axes dbRDA1 and dbRDA2 accounted for 5.05% and 3.90% of the total variance respectively (Figure 4). Phosphorus exhibited strong loading scores on dbRDA1, indicating its strong influence on sample separation along this primary axis. Soil moisture showed a negative loading on dbRDA2 (see also Figure S6 for version of dbRDA controlling for sampling event). Functional Composition

Across all treatments (for both soil depths and sampling events), 50%–80% of sequences were assigned a primary lifestyle and corresponding trophic mode from the FungalTraits database (Figure 5, Figure S7). The mean relative abundance of pathotrophs across all treatments and soil depths increased from 7% in the 2014 sampling event to 15% in 2020 (Wilcoxon signed‐rank test on log‐ratio abundance (ALR transformed; see Section 2): V = 47, p adj < 0.001). For symbiotrophs, the mean relative abundance across all treatments and soil depths decreased from 37% in the 2014 sampling event to 27% in 2020 (Wilcoxon signed‐rank test on log‐ratio abundance: V = 1062, p adj < 0.001). For saprotrophs, the mean relative abundance across all treatments and soil depths did not differ (28% in the 2014 sampling event, 30% in 2020; Wilcoxon signed‐rank test on log‐ratio abundance: p adj = 1). Similarly, the mean relative abundance of sequences unable to be assigned a trophic mode did not differ between the two sampling events (27% and 29%, respectively; Wilcoxon signed‐rank test on log‐ratio abundance: p adj = 1).

To assess changes between the two sampling events, we compared the relative abundance of a trophic mode for each treatment to the reference sites, stratified by sampling event (Figure 6, Figure S8). Permutation tests (n = 9999) showed a difference between sampling events in the log‐ratio abundance of pathotrophs compared to reference sites for the 2009 and 2007 planting sites (p adj < 0.001 for both; Figure 6C). For symbiotrophs, there was a difference between sampling events for the cleared sites, and for the 2009, 2007 and 2005 planting sites (p adj = 0.035, p adj < 0.001, p adj = 0.002, p adj < 0.001; Figure 6B). For saprotrophs, there was a difference between sampling events for the cleared sites, and for the 2009 and 2005 planting sites (p adj < 0.001 for all; Figure 6A). Analogous permutation tests on the mean difference in ALR log‐ratio values between treatment and reference sites produced largely equivalent results (Table S4), indicating that our findings were not strongly influenced by compositionality of the data.

Spatial Autocorrelation

The Bray–Curtis distances of soil fungal communities showed weak spatial autocorrelation between samples in both 2014 and 2020, across both soil depths (Spearman Mantel test: 2014 0–10 cm r = 0.247, p < 0.001; 2020 0–10 cm r = 0.260, p = 0.002; 2014 20–30 cm r = 0.280, p < 0.001; 2020 20–30 cm r = 0.249, p = 0.002; Figure S9), indicating that fungal community composition was more similar among geographically closer sites.

Effect of Season of Sampling on Composition

As our 2014 sampling was done at a different time of year to our 2020 sampling (December 2014, April 2020), we explored the role of season on soil fungal communities across comparable datasets in the Australian Microbiome database (see Table S2 for inclusion criteria and filtering steps). After rarefying to 19,228 reads, our final dataset consisted of 6,614,432 sequences and 46,248 unique zOTUs across 344 samples. Season of sampling had an effect on fungal community composition (PERMANOVA: R 2 = 0.033, F = 3.838, p = 0.001); however, seasons only differed in dispersion in one pairwise comparison (Tukey HSD pairwise differences in dispersion: Winter‐Autumn p adj = 0.027, all other pairwise comparisons p adj > 0.05; Figure 7).

Fungal community composition of comparable Australian Microbiome samples (n = 344), grouped by season. Permutational analysis of variance (PERMANOVA) showed an effect of season on composition (R 2 = 0.033, F = 3.838, p = 0.001), with pairwise comparisons showing only a difference in group dispersions between winter and autumn (p adj = 0.027) and no difference for all other pairwise comparisons (p adj > 0.05).

Discussion

The extent to which restoration plantings result in the recovery of biodiversity more broadly has fundamental consequences for restoration ecology. Despite their functional importance to terrestrial ecosystems, the responses of soil microbiota to restoration plantings remain poorly understood (Harris 2009; Peddle et al. 2025; Wood et al. 2026). Much of the research to date has relied on observational chronosequence designs that use space as a proxy for time (Watson et al. 2022), highlighting the need to revisit these systems through repeated sampling to determine whether apparent recovery patterns are borne out through time. Here, we examined longitudinal soil fungal community responses to restoration plantings by resampling a well‐studied chronosequence 6 years apart, in 2014 and 2020. While we found little evidence of additional whole fungal community recovery over this time period, we detected continued functional change through reduced relative dominance of pathotrophic fungi. Together, the lack of further whole‐community recovery and the association between fungal composition and soil properties, particularly soil phosphorus, are consistent with persistent land‐use legacies constraining recovery. More broadly, our results highlight the value of longitudinal sampling for revealing changes that may not be apparent from single‐time‐point comparisons, and of considering both taxonomic and functional components of microbial recovery.

Functional Recovery

While the whole soil fungal community did not show additional signs of recovery between our two sampling periods, the reduction in the relative dominance of pathotrophs is consistent with continued functional recovery. One possible explanation is that fungal community development is closely linked to the vegetation transition in restoration sites from degraded, grass‐dominated habitat towards increasingly mature woody native vegetation. Ecologically degraded sites may favour disturbance‐associated taxa, and their fungal communities are often characterised by fewer symbiotrophs (Tedersoo et al. 2014) and a greater dominance of saprotrophs and pathotrophs (Yan et al. 2018; Abrahão et al. 2022; Qu et al. 2024). As woody plants establish and become dominant, changes in host availability, root inputs, the composition and timing of organic matter inputs and soil microclimate may favour different fungal taxa and trophic strategies. The decline in pathotroph dominance is consistent with continued movement away from a disturbance‐associated fungal community towards a functional profile more characteristic of the reference ecosystems.

This interpretation is consistent with Yang et al. (2022), who examined ecosystem development on abandoned agricultural land across a successional transition from farmland to mature forest. They found fungal communities shifting from early dominance by saprotrophs and pathotrophs towards communities dominated by symbiotrophs. We also observed shifts in the relative abundances of saprotrophs and symbiotrophs in our study. However, the substantial variation in our control sites between the two sampling events is indicative of broadscale seasonal fluctuation, potentially obscuring recovery trends present in these two trophic groups. Together, these results suggest that taxonomic and functional components of fungal recovery may not proceed in parallel. Functional composition may continue to change even where whole‐community taxonomic convergence has slowed. Assessments based solely on taxonomic composition may therefore overlook ongoing functional recovery, highlighting the value of incorporating both taxonomic and functional indicators when evaluating ecosystem recovery.

Seasonal Variation vs. Reference Site Degradation

Although we identified a decrease in the dominance of pathotrophs in restoration sites relative to reference sites, the overall relative abundance of pathotrophs in fact increased across all treatments, including in reference sites. While this increase could reflect broadscale changes across the study site (e.g., seasonal variation, climate change impacts), we found little evidence that sampling in different seasons strongly influenced the observed recovery patterns. Our findings are also consistent with studies on seasonal variation in soil fungal communities, which show that larger community changes occur over years than across seasons (Sun et al. 2017; Fierer et al. 2021). This suggests that while sampling within a season remains preferable for restoration monitoring, seasonal differences may not necessarily obscure longer‐term recovery patterns where appropriate reference or control sites are available to contextualise temporal variation.

As our recovery trajectories were calculated relative to reference sites, our results could be partially explained by temporal change in the reference sites. However, the inter‐reference site variability was greater than the changes in reference sites between sampling events (i.e., reference sites retained their compositional identity relative to other treatments), which suggests no substantial compositional change to these reference sites through time (see Figures S10–S13, Tables S5 and S6 for additional analyses on potential reference site degradation). This provides evidence that the observed recovery patterns were not primarily driven by temporal shifts in the reference sites. Nonetheless, with only two timepoints and only a single cleared site, we are limited in what we can infer about the observed changes. Repeated sampling of both reference and cleared sites would provide stronger context for interpreting restoration trajectories by capturing background temporal change at both ends of the restoration gradient.

Community Recovery Plateau Indicates Barriers to Recovery

With 6 years between our two sampling events, we expected the fungal taxonomic composition of the restoration planting sites to become increasingly similar to that of reference sites. However, the lack of additional progress we observed suggests that fungal community recovery may have plateaued, potentially because persistent land‐use legacy effects are limiting further recovery, including soil nutrient legacies from past fertiliser addition.

Our analysis of soil physicochemical properties showed strong associations between soil phosphorus levels and fungal community composition. Notably, the distribution of restoration treatments across the primary ordination axes (from cleared to reference sites) corresponded with decreasing phosphorus levels and, to a lesser extent, increasing pH, organic carbon and clay levels. These patterns are consistent with persistent phosphorus differences acting as a barrier to further fungal community recovery. Potential phosphorus legacies may alter the costs and benefits of plant‐fungal nutrient exchange (Johnson et al. 2015), thereby filtering fungal mutualists and influencing which plant species are favoured. These altered plant‐fungal associations may then generate feedbacks that help maintain non‐reference plant and fungal communities, impeding recovery (Bever et al. 2010). This interpretation is also consistent with previous studies that have identified elevated soil phosphorus levels as a potential barrier to soil microbial community recovery (Peddle et al. 2024; Davies et al. 2026).

Persistent abiotic differences may also interact with biotic barriers, including dispersal limitation, priority effects and stabilising feedbacks within non‐reference fungal communities (Pausas and Bond 2020; Albornoz et al. 2023; Wood et al. 2026). Experimental studies that manipulate soil conditions and/or microbial inoculum are needed to distinguish abiotic filtering from dispersal limitation or self‐reinforcing biotic feedbacks. Where biotic limitation is demonstrated, locally appropriate soil inocula or whole‐soil translocations may help reintroduce missing fungal taxa. However, such interventions are unlikely to succeed if underlying soil conditions remain unsuitable for their establishment (Peddle et al. 2025; Wood et al. 2026).

The compositional patterns we observed in 2014 were consistent with those reported from the same sampling event by Yan et al. (2018), as well as with other restoration chronosequence studies in which microbial communities in restoration plantings generally become more similar to those in reference sites (Watson et al. 2022). However, because each planting year was represented by a different site, planting age was partly confounded with site identity. The apparent chronosequence pattern therefore cannot be attributed unequivocally to time since restoration and may partly reflect unmeasured differences among sites. Nevertheless, resampling the same sites in 2020 allowed us to assess within‐site change through time and showed that the apparent cross‐sectional recovery pattern did not correspond to further convergence between 2014 and 2020. The lack of additional convergence in fungal community composition is also consistent with a detailed analysis of soil bacterial communities from the same chronosequence (Lem et al. 2022), suggesting that bacterial and fungal communities may be constrained by shared barriers to recovery.

Conclusion

Our longitudinal resampling study identified changes consistent with continued functional recovery over 6 years, despite little additional progress in whole fungal community recovery. These contrasting responses suggest that our study site likely has abiotic soil legacies (e.g., elevated soil nutrient levels in the post‐agricultural sites, including the sites that received restoration plantings) acting as potential barriers to fungal community recovery. Future studies should prioritise identifying these barriers to recovery, and solutions to overcome them. We support previous studies that have urged caution when drawing cause‐effect and future recovery conclusions from cross‐sectional chronosequence studies (Christie et al. 2019; Peddle et al. 2025). Our study was limited to a single restoration system, and the barriers to microbial recovery are likely to differ among land‐use histories, restoration approaches and ecosystems. Further longitudinal studies across restoration systems are therefore needed to determine when microbial recovery becomes constrained, identify the mechanisms responsible and test interventions that may overcome these barriers. Incorporating both taxonomic and functional indicators will also provide a more complete assessment of ecosystem recovery.
