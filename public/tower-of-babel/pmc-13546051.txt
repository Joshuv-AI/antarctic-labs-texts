From Symbiont to Potential Carcinogenic Contributor: Examining the Pathogenic Evolution of Morganella morganii in Colorectal Cancer
Authors: Kosar Soleymanpour, Behrooz Shokouhi, Hossein Samadi Kafil
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMorganella morganii (M. morganii), a member of the Enterobacteriaceae family, and a constituent of the human gut microbiota, has recently emerged as a candidate microbial contributor to the development of adenomatous polyps (AP) and promoting colorectal carcinogenesis. The present study is a narrative review compiled to evaluate the role of M. morganii in the development and progression of AP and colorectal cancer (CRC). A comprehensive literature search was conducted using Google Scholar and PubMed/Medline (2020–2026) using keywords related to the gut microbiota, M. morganii, and its impact on CRC and intestinal polyps. The ability of M. morganii to produce a specific type of genotoxin, indolimine, is a distinguishing feature of this bacterium compared to other intestinal bacteria. Because its production is independent of the pks gene cluster and the clbI and clbP genes, it exhibits a different damage pattern, namely, DNA smearing. According to reports from previous studies, strains lacking the aat gene exhibited no detectable genotoxic activity, supporting the role of aat in genotoxicity. Available data from in vitro experiments, animal models, a limited number of human observational studies, and findings from research groups suggest that M. morganii may, through the production of a highly potent genotoxin, be associated with distinct genetic damage, metabolic changes, and facilitation of polyp growth, and contribute to the development and acceleration of CRC. These findings establish a new theoretical framework in which CRC is not simply caused by gut microbiota dysbiosis, but rather reflects the activation of genotoxic bacteria and their functional interaction within the intestinal epithelial microenvironment. Nevertheless, owing to the scarcity of longitudinal human studies, it cannot yet be conclusively established whether M. morganii plays a predisposing, an initiating, or merely a consequential role in the development of CRC. Larger human studies investigating the association between M. morganii colonization and CRC development are essential for understanding the causal relationship.

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is one of the most prevalent cancers worldwide, ranking third in terms of incidence and second in terms of mortality. On the other hand, among all gastrointestinal cancers, the risk of developing CRC is reported to be the highest, at approximately 38.5%, with a mortality rate of about 28.2%.1,2 One of the notable points is the close connection and interaction between CRC and the gut microbiota, which leads to the formation of a tumor microenvironment. Gut dysbiosis and disruption in the number, composition, and diversity of the gut microbiota may not only be a side effect but also the primary cause of cancer.3 Metabolites produced by the gut microbiome can lead to multiple damages or mutations in the DNA of intestinal epithelial cells by attacking them. Failure to repair this damage in the long term can lead to CRC. Genotoxins are metabolites that ultimately cause mutations by damaging DNA.4 Among the beneficial bacteria present in the gut, Morganella morganii (M. morganii), a member of the gut microbiome and an opportunistic organism, can play a role in the development and progression of CRC by producing certain metabolites.5 The present study aimed to provide an integrated review of the emerging role of M. morganii in precancerous stages and CRC. Examining the evidence related to genotoxicity, alterations in the gut microbiome, and the genomic characteristics of newly discovered strains provides new insights into the shift in the position of M. morganii from a common and unimportant member of the gut microbiota to a significant element in colorectal carcinogenesis pathways. According to evidence, the fundamental distinction between M. morganii and other classic bacteria associated with CRC is that, independent of the pks-colibactin pathway, it forms a new framework for microbial carcinogenesis through the production of indolimines with dual genotoxic and inflammatory effects and the induction of a unique DNA smearing damage pattern. In this framework, the bacterium is not merely a promoter and collaborator but can be the primary trigger of the cancerous pathway. This sets the stage for future research to identify functional mechanisms, discover other strains involved in carcinogenesis, and develop microbial-related therapeutic targets for cancer.

Search Strategy and Study Selection

For the writing of this article, a comprehensive search was conducted using the scientific databases Google Scholar and PubMed/Medline, focusing on English articles published between 2020 and 2026. A search of scientific texts was conducted using the keywords (“Morganella morganii” [All Fields]) AND (“DNA damage” [All Fields] OR genotoxic* [All Fields]) AND (indolimine* [All Fields] OR genotoxin* [All Fields]) AND (carcinogenesis [All Fields] OR tumorigenesis [All Fields] OR “colorectal cancer” [All Fields] OR “adenomatous polyp*” [All Fields]). Non-English articles, studies lacking valid data, duplicate sources, or studies lacking methodological transparency were excluded from the analysis. Various types of studies, including reviews, laboratory, genomic and sequencing, clinical and epidemiological, animal models, and case reports, were included in the study for a comprehensive and accurate examination of different aspects. A total of 521 records were initially identified. A total of 348 articles remained after duplicate articles were eliminated and 259 articles remained after the publication date filter between 2020 and 2026. After screening titles and abstracts based on the inclusion and exclusion criteria, 30 relevant studies were considered and included in the final review.

Morganella morganii: A Review of Classification, Genomic Features, and Distinct Metabolic Characteristics

One of the bacteria commonly found in the environment and natural flora of the human gut, mammals, and reptiles is M. morganii, which was first isolated and identified in 1906 by Morgan et al from stool samples of children with diarrhea. From a biological classification perspective, this organism is considered a member of the Enterobacteriaceae family, and in terms of metabolic characteristics, Morganella, along with Proteus and Providencia, are placed in the Proteeae tribe. The genus Morganella includes only one main species, M. morganii, which is divided into two valid subspecies, M. morganii subsp. morganii and M. morganii subsp. sibonii, based on its ability to ferment trehalose, which depends on the presence of the trehalose operon consisting of treR, treB, and treP genes.6–8 This organism is a motile, gram-negative bacillus with peritrichous flagella and is a facultative anaerobe that does not form spores. It appears as flat, colorless colonies on MacConkey agar and blood agar.9,10 Among the biochemical and metabolic characteristics that distinguish this bacterium from other members of the Enterobacteriaceae family are lactose negativity, citrate negativity, indole positivity, the absence of swarming motility on the medium, and, one of the most prominent, urease positivity.11 This bacterium has a genome approximately 4 megabases long and contains 4000 protein-coding sequences (CDSs).12 Among the genetic evidence that distinguishes M. morganii from other members of the Proteeae family is the C+G content in the genome, which is approximately 51% in M. morganii and less than this amount, around 39 to 43%, in other members. Although M. morganii was initially known as Proteus morganii, genetic studies using DNA-DNA hybridization have revealed significant differences, leading to its classification in the separate genus Morganella.7

A New Perspective on Bacterial Genotoxicity and Its Potential Role in Colorectal Carcinogenesis

The natural habitat of M. morganii is the digestive systems of humans, mammals, and reptiles. However, this organism is considered an opportunistic pathogen that can cause various hospital and non-hospital infections under specific conditions such as a weakened immune system or hospitalization. Clinical observations have demonstrated its prominent role in tract infections and wound healing. Studies have shown that bloodstream infections (BSIs) caused by M. morganii primarily affect older individuals with a history of at least one underlying disease. They also reported a mortality risk of approximately 21.2% within a 30-day period.8,13 In some cases, the gut microbiota, through the production of unknown genotoxins, plays a role in the development and progression of CRC. One of the most well-known of these toxins is colibactin, which is produced by certain strains of the bacterium that produces it, Escherichia coli (E. coli), and leads to double-strand DNA breaks in intestinal cells by alkylating DNA. Researchers, hypothesizing the potential presence of other bacteria producing different genotoxins, began examining the gut bacteria of patients with inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) for potential DNA damage. The test results showed that the production of a type of genotoxin with a different damage pattern than previous genotoxins, which unlike colibactin, did not require the pks gene cluster for their synthesis.2 The discovered genotoxin was indolimine produced by M. morganii, and the aat gene was identified as essential for the production of this type of toxin. By producing mutant strains of this bacterium lacking the aat gene, researchers found that indolimines are directly linked to carcinogenicity.2,14 The distinct and independent pathogenic pattern of M. morganii, characterized by the production of a novel family of metabolites, indicates the unique and distinctive mechanisms of this bacterium. Owing to the genetic instability it induces, it may contribute to the development or progression of colorectal carcinogenesis. However, given limited evidence, a causal relationship has not yet been fully established.

Microbial Risk Factors in Colorectal Cancer: A Look at Known Bacterial Models

pks+ Escherichia coli

One of the most well-known bacterial genotoxicity models linked to CRC is the pks+ E. coli strains, which, by producing colibactin, can create specific genetic mutation signatures single base substitution (SBS)88 and insertion/deletion (ID)18 in the DNA of colon epithelial cells. These signatures can be identified not only in tumor tissues but also in normal colonic tissues. Studies have shown that their accumulation in individuals under 50 is much higher than in those over 50. Also, these signatures appear during the early stages of tumor formation, and their distribution patterns vary across different geographic regions.15 Whole genome sequencing (WGS) analysis of a human study limited to Japanese patients, using a combination of WGS, transcriptomic, and fecal metagenomic analyses, showed that ID18 and SBS88 are specific mutation patterns and genetic markers unique to pks⁺ E. coli, which were identified as primary clonal genetic lesions in 44.8% of non-hypermutated patients. Interestingly, the presence of these mutation patterns was significantly higher in younger generations, in other words, people born from 1960 onwards.16 In a study based on targeted sequencing, researchers analyzed 5292 CRC tumors and found that about 7.5% of the tumors were SBS88 positive, which is linked to colibactin. Of these, around 98.7% were microsatellite stable (MSS)/microsatellite instability low (MSI-L) type tumors, meaning they had mostly healthy or low-error DNA repair systems. The study also showed that SBS88 positive tumors were more often found in the distal colon and rectum.17

Fusobacterium nucleatum

Fusobacterium nucleatum (F. nucleatum) is a part of the commensal oral bacterium, but under certain conditions, it can disseminate to other parts of the body, including the gut, and cause disease. According to a 2012 study, researchers first found that the abundance of this bacterium is higher in CRC tumor tissues compared to the surrounding healthy tissues, and this abundance increases as CRC progresses.18 F. nucleatum in colorectal tumors, by secreting formic acid, can lead to increased glucose transporter 1 (GLUT1) expression in cancer cells, which results in more glucose uptake and higher lactate production. The extra lactate then causes histone lactylation and ultimately boosts the stemness characteristics of tumor cells.19 A recent study reached new findings. The results show that F. nucleatum by entering CRC cells, leads to the activation of the transcription factor ETS1, and following this activity, the production of a circular RNA called circPTBP3 increases. miR-760, as the target RNA of circPTBP3, is sequestered and inactivated by it. The inactivity of miR-760 leads to the release of the PUM1 gene and an increase in its expression, which in turn results in more cancer cell proliferation and tumor growth. In this study, circPTBP3 was introduced as a biomarker in CRC development related to F. nucleatum.20

Bacteroides fragilis

Bacteroides fragilis (B. fragilis) is a part of the human gut microbiota, and the Enterotoxigenic B. fragilis (ETBF) strain produces B. fragilis toxin (BFT), a metalloprotease, and carries the bft gene, which has been linked to digestive disorders, especially CRC and IBD. When this bacterium colonizes the colon, BFT is produced, causing damage to the intestinal mucosa and activating the transcription factor signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (STAT3). This leads to increased expression of zinc finger e-box binding homeobox 2 (ZEB2) and induction of epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT). Long-term STAT3 activation creates and maintains a pro-inflammatory, pre-cancerous environment in colonic tissue, increasing invasion and metastasis. Additionally, ETBF cleaves E-cadherin, activating β-catenin signaling and triggering proliferative genes. Another mechanism by which ETBF may cause cancer is through activating the inflammatory response via IL-17.18,21

Levels of Evidence Supporting the Role of M. morganii in Colorectal Carcinogenesis

In vitro Evidence

Unlike E. coli, which possesses the clbI and clbP genes for the complete production and activation of colibactin, M. morganii lacks these genes. Consequently, it produces a genotoxin that is different from colibactin, with different types of damage. The genotoxic damage pattern produced by M. morganii involves DNA smearing, which leads to multiple breaks and DNA damage. Tests showed that even placing cells in a Morganella culture supernatant led to a significant increase in γ-H2AX (the most sensitive marker for detecting DNA damage in cells). This indicates M. morganii produces a soluble, stable, and diffusible genotoxin. These characteristics are completely opposite to those of known genotoxins22 (Figure 1). Animal Models

In gnotobiotic mouse models, exposure to indolimine-producing M. morganii has been associated with DNA damage, increased intestinal wall permeability, upregulation of cell division-related genes, and subsequently promotes colon tumor growth.23

Human-Associated Evidence

Recent cross-sectional analyses have reported that the number of existing species and their distribution in adenomatous polyps (AP) are significantly lower than normal, and the observed pattern indicates a paradox between microbial and metabolic composition. In these types of tissues, simultaneous with a decrease in beneficial gut bacteria, including members of the Lachnospiraceae family, some serious metabolic changes occur, such as an increase in acetate and β-hydroxybutyrate (BHB) in the polyp microenvironment. This makes the environment conducive to the growth of opportunistic bacteria, including M. morganii. Recent studies have shown that M. morganii is more abundant in AP tissues than in healthy tissues. These findings are consistent with a possible role for M. morganii in the initiation of tumorigenesis.24 Nevertheless, these results were obtained from a cross-sectional study with a small sample size and single-center design (limited to a specific region), which was not methodologically capable of distinguishing strains carrying the aat gene or of directly measuring indolimines. In this study, the role of other factors affecting gut bacteria was not considered. Additionally, there was no validation cohort to confirm the results. Therefore, it remains unclear whether M. morganii colonization precedes polyp formation, expands in response to altered tissue metabolism, or is influenced by other microbial and host factors. Considering the aforementioned points, no direct causal relationship has been established between M. morganii and CRC.

Complementary Molecular Pathways in Carcinogenesis Induced by Morganella morganii

Indolimines are genotoxic metabolites derived from indole. The biosynthetic pathway for indolimines begins with tryptophan. This is achieved by M. morganii metabolizing tryptophan, leading to the production of specific metabolites, including indole-3-aldehyde (I3Ald). The produced metabolite must bind to a primary amine (R–NH2), such as phenethylamine, and this process is carried out by the activity of amino acid decarboxylase (AADC). Finally, the condensation reaction between I3Ald and the primary amine produces the final product, indolimines25 (Figure 2). Examination of three types of indoles, namely 200, 214, and 248, showed that all three types have the ability to activate the aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AHR) as ligands, which subsequently leads to increased expression levels of Cyp1a1 and increased CYP1A1 enzyme activity. This enzyme can metabolize the carcinogen benzo(a)pyrene in microsomal assays. It has also been found that indoles, along with cytokines, can have a synergistic effect and intensify the inflammatory effect of IL-6, an inflammatory cytokine associated with cancer progression. On the other hand, this study showed that indolimine-248 at a specific concentration does not necessarily lead to DNA damage and does not have genotoxic properties, but is only capable of being effective in inflammation and carcinogenesis through AHR activation.25 These observations suggest that these two effects are independent and dependent on different concentrations. As previously mentioned, indolimines can lead to an increase in IL-6 levels. On the other hand, nuclear factor kappa B (NF-Κb) is one of the most important activators and regulators of IL-6, and IL-6 is also capable of activating the STAT3 pathway, which results in inflammation. This inflammation can lead to activation of NF-κB in the long term. In fact, it can be said that a cyclical and reinforcing process takes place, in which IL-6 indirectly has the ability to induce chronic inflammation and maintain NF-κB. This stability leads to increased expression of cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2) and inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS). This, in turn, promotes chronic inflammation and DNA damage. In parallel, upregulation of cytochrome c oxidase via NF-κB contributes to increased proliferation and survival of cancer cells.5,26 Based on the current experimental evidence, it may be inferred that M. morganii, by releasing a metabolite through two independent functions and mechanisms, may initiate mutations, perpetuate inflammation, and promote tumor growth. In other words, indolimines are not just an immediate stimulus, but can also be effective in causing persistent inflammation and ultimately tumorigenesis by creating a self-reinforcing cycle in cells and tissues. Nevertheless, validation of this requires prospective longitudinal human cohorts. Considering that carcinogenesis is typically a multifactorial and complex process, it is possible to propose other complementary or independent mechanisms and pathways for colorectal carcinogenesis. Genomic Insights into New Strains of Morganella morganii

Strain CTX51T was the first strain of M. morganii to be isolated and identified from human cecal colorectal adenocarcinoma tumor tissue. Using advanced genome sequencing techniques (single-molecule real-time sequencing, SMRT-Seq) revealed that the bacterium’s genome is a circular chromosome 4.19 million base pairs long, with a cytosine and guanine ratio of approximately 50.4%. Additionally, it possesses a plasmid measuring 8.48 thousand base pairs, virulence factors, and genes involved in drug resistance. Prior to that, the complete genomes of 34 strains of M. morganii had only been isolated and sequenced from environmental sources and common infections.27 IPS008, IPS012, IPS030, and IPS040 are four well-known strains of M. morganii that were isolated from stool samples of patients with CRC, and their genomes have been fully sequenced. The genome size of these strains was approximately 3.8–3.9 megabases with a GC content of approximately 51%. Each strain contained approximately 3700 protein-coding genes and 80 RNA-coding genes.28 This similarity in the size and structure of the genomes indicates genetic stability among the reported isolates. However, minor differences could potentially reflect diverse functional and metabolic pathways related to virulence or synthesis of genotoxic compounds. Since only a limited number of tumor-associated strains have been identified to date, it cannot be said with certainty that this stability applies to the entire species and that there is a uniform and reproducible genomic pattern associated with CRC. Another important point is that the mere presence of M. morganii in cancer tissues does not prove a causal role in tumorigenesis. Despite these limitations, the research conducted and the findings obtained can serve as a guide for scientists to identify other bacterial toxins that lead to various intestinal-related diseases. This research also allows the design of new therapeutic goals, from new therapeutic drugs to methods for neutralizing toxins. Ultimately, M. morganii, which was previously considered a common intestinal bacterium, has now become a key candidate for microbiome-related carcinogenesis.

Beyond Cross-Sectional Evidence: A Novel Approach to Proving Morganella morganii’s Role in Colorectal Cancer

Considering that the studies conducted so far are mostly cross-sectional and tumors require years or decades to develop, to prove the causality and the precise role of M. morganii in CRC, we need prospective cohort studies. The Prospective Cohort Incident-Tumor Biobank Method (PCIBM) is a novel approach that has been recently introduced, which can give us the opportunity to investigate the relationship between long-term exposure to risk factors (bacteria/gut microbiota, genetic factors, environmental factors, lifestyle, etc.) and the molecular characteristics of cancerous and precancerous tumors. For example, applying PCIBM in large cohorts like the Nurses’ Health Study (NHS) and UK Biobank showed that long-term risk factors, including smoking and diet (yogurt consumption), do not have the same effect on all colorectal tumors. Rather, it depends on tumor tissue microbiome characteristics.29,30 Using this method may in the future determine whether prolonged presence of M. morganii in the gut increases the risk of CRC or not. It can also clarify whether M. morganii is a contributor of CRC, an initiator, or a consequence. Such studies are a crucial step to confirm the causal role of this bacterium in cancer, which can ultimately lead to the design of personalized therapeutic strategies.

Conclusion

Current evidence identifies M. morganii as an emerging microbial candidate in CRC. The transition of this bacterium from a symbiotic member of the gut microbiota to a potential carcinogen represents a paradigm shift in our understanding of microbial carcinogenesis. Experimental evidence has shown that under certain conditions, this organism, by taking a path independent of other bacteria, produces a specific and potent genotoxin, indolimine, and induces a DNA smearing pattern of damage, leading to enhanced intestinal permeability, cell proliferation, and adenomatous polyp growth in animal models and human cross-sectional studies. Previous studies have shown that M. morganii may not only cause mutations, but may also act as a promoter of tumor survival, meaning that in addition to genotoxic properties, indolimines can be effective in creating a tumorigenic environment by activating inflammatory and signaling pathways. Therefore, in recent years, M. morganii has paradoxically been elevated from a common and insignificant member of the microbiota to a prominent candidate in causing genomic instability and tumor microenvironment formation in the intestine. Given that current evidence is mainly obtained from experimental systems and cross-sectional human observations, a complete understanding of the exact role of this bacterium in CRC, as an “initiating, promoting or exacerbating factor”, remains unclear and the current evidence is insufficient to prove a causal relationship. Therefore, conducting prospective longitudinal studies based on examining the colonization rate of this bacterium in high-risk populations, understanding the causality and interaction between the increase or decrease of M. morganii and its metabolites with other metabolic and microbial changes in the intestine, as well as thorough investigation of the synthesis and functional mechanism of indolimine or investigating the presence of other involved genes that make this bacterium an effective factor in the pathogenesis of CRC, represents an urgent and essential need for the future.

Potential Future Research and Clinical Directions

If the association between M. morganii, indolimine production, and colorectal neoplasia is confirmed in prospective studies, bacterium- or metabolite-based assays could be used as candidate tools for risk assessment or early diagnosis. In other words, the development of diagnostic kits using specific metabolites of this bacterium (indolimines) as biomarkers can significantly aid in the prognosis or early detection of CRC. However, the discovery or development of genotoxic inhibitors or the aat gene could lead to the development of therapeutic and preventive strategies. In the future, probiotics can be designed to not only increase beneficial bacteria, but are also capable of metabolizing the produced indolimine or disrupting its production pathway by adopting anti-genotoxic activity. On the other hand, by designing and constructing engineered probiotics capable of consuming indolimines produced by M. morganii or other genotoxins, or probiotics with the ability to compete for the raw material needed to produce indolimines (tryptophan), probiotics with dual functionality and anti-tumor activity can be provided without eliminating the bacteria. This strategy also applies to other antibiotic alternatives with the aim of inhibiting and eliminating the aat gene or eliminating indolimines. Upon obtaining strong causal evidence and definitive proof in the field of microbiota-associated carcinogenesis, microbiota transplantation can be considered more than a simple replacement intervention. Targeted microbiota links or defined microbial consortia lacking the genotoxic indolimines metabolomic signature or containing indole-consuming bacteria can be proposed as complementary strategies to reduce the genotoxic activity of M. morganii. Such approaches, especially in high-risk AP patients, have the potential to be used in personalized medicine and preventive interventions. Considering all these factors, slowing the progression of colorectal carcinogenesis could become a significant advancement in the medical world. Ultimately, given the importance and high prevalence of CRC, the findings can open up new perspectives in cancer research and clinical studies, paving the way for advancements in prediction, prevention, diagnosis, and treatment. These aspects are not currently feasible to implement, but they can be explored in future studies. The current literature supports a biologically plausible and testable hypothesis, but not yet a causal role for M. morganii in human CRC.
