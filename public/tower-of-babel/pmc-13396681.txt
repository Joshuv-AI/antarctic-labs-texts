Associations of the uric acid–to–high-density lipoprotein cholesterol ratio with hypertension: a cross-sectional study from the China health and retirement longitudinal study
Authors: Chaoyang Liang, Lan Gao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHypertension is tightly linked to metabolic dysregulation, oxidative stress, and vascular dysfunction. The uric acid–to–high-density lipoprotein cholesterol ratio (UHR) has emerged as an integrated marker reflecting metabolic burden and oxidative stress. However, evidence regarding its association with hypertension remains limited in large, population-based samples. This cross-sectional study included 9,255 adults aged ≥45 years from the 2011 baseline survey of the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS). UHR was calculated as serum uric acid divided by HDL-C. Hypertension was defined as systolic/diastolic blood pressure ≥140/90 mmHg on examination, self-reported physician diagnosis, or current use of antihypertensive medication. Multivariable logistic regression was used to estimate odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for hypertension per 1-standard deviation (SD) increment and across categories of UHR. Nonlinearity and potential inflection points were explored using restricted cubic splines and segmented regression. The mean age was 59.6 years, and 53.3% of participants were women. Overall, 3,844 individuals (41.5%) had hypertension. Each 1-SD higher UHR was associated with greater odds of hypertension (OR 1.21, 95% CI 1.15–1.28). Compared with participants in the low-UHR group, those in the high-UHR group had substantially higher odds of hypertension (OR 1.46, 95% CI 1.33–1.61). The association followed a nonlinear pattern; segmented regression suggested an inflection point at UHR = 9.09, with a steeper positive association at lower UHR values and attenuation at higher values. Higher UHR was independently associated with increased odds of hypertension, exhibiting a nonlinear exposure–response relationship. Introduction

Hypertension is among the most prevalent cardiovascular conditions worldwide and is tightly intertwined with metabolic dysregulation, chronic low-grade inflammation, and progressive vascular injury1. In the context of rapid population ageing and an escalating burden of cardiometabolic disease, there is a growing need for accessible biomarkers that reflect early pathophysiological perturbations relevant to hypertension and may help characterize individuals with an unfavorable metabolic profile.

The uric acid–to–high-density lipoprotein cholesterol ratio (UHR) has recently been proposed as a composite indicator capturing metabolic and oxidative stress status2. First introduced by Koçak et al., UHR appears to outperform uric acid or HDL-C alone in discriminating metabolic syndrome and related cardiometabolic abnormalities. This advantage likely stems from its ability to integrate a pro-oxidative, pro-inflammatory milieu with diminished antioxidative and anti-atherogenic capacity. Mechanistically, elevated uric acid can promote oxidative stress and inflammation via activation of ERK/p38 signaling, inhibition of the Nrf2–ARE and PI3K/Akt pathways, and activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome. In parallel, reduced or dysfunctional HDL-C compromises cholesterol efflux and attenuates endothelial protection and antioxidative activity3,4. Collectively, these disturbances contribute to endothelial dysfunction, increased vascular stiffness, and ultimately elevated blood pressure.

Accumulating experimental and epidemiological evidence supports a central role for oxidative stress and metabolic imbalance in vascular remodeling and the development of hypertension. Redox disequilibrium, low-grade inflammation, and lipid dysfunction act in concert to accelerate arterial stiffening, reduce nitric oxide bioavailability, and promote microvascular injury—processes that are fundamental to the onset and progression of hypertension5–7. Accordingly, composite metabolic markers that reflect both heightened oxidative burden and impaired lipid-related protection may offer additional value for characterizing individuals with an unfavorable hypertension-related metabolic profile.

Nevertheless, population-based evidence linking UHR to hypertension remains limited, and the possibility of a nonlinear association has not been adequately evaluated in large, nationally representative datasets. Therefore, using data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS), we aimed to examine the association between UHR and hypertension and to further characterize potential dose–response patterns in a nationally representative sample of middle-aged and older Chinese adults.

Methods

Study population and study design

This cross-sectional analysis was based on data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS), a nationally representative cohort of community-dwelling adults aged ≥45 years in China. CHARLS employed a multistage, stratified, probability-proportional-to-size sampling design spanning 150 counties and 450 villages across 28 provinces. The baseline survey was administered in 2011. All participants provided written informed consent, and the study protocol was approved by the Biomedical Ethics Review Committee of Peking University (IRB00001052–11015). Detailed descriptions of the sampling framework, field procedures, and quality control have been published previously8,9.

A total of 17,708 participants were enrolled at baseline. We excluded individuals with missing measurements of serum uric acid (UA) or high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) (n = 8,106), those reporting a history of cancer (n = 121), and participants aged <45 years (n = 226). The final analytical sample comprised 9,255 participants.

This study was reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) statement for cross-sectional studies.

Serum uric acid and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol

Fasting venous blood samples (8 mL) were obtained by trained staff from the Chinese Center for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) using standardized protocols. Specimens were maintained at 4 °C, processed within 2 hours of collection, and transported to local laboratories for initial processing. Plasma and buffy coat fractions were subsequently separated, aliquoted, and stored at −20 °C before being shipped to the CDC central laboratory in Beijing within 2 weeks. All samples were then stored at −80 °C until biochemical measurements were performed at the central laboratory of Capital Medical University under strict quality-control procedures. Detailed methods for biospecimen collection, handling, transport, and laboratory testing have been described previously10.

Serum UA was quantified using the UA Plus enzymatic uricase–peroxidase method, and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) was measured by an enzymatic colorimetric assay. UHR was calculated as: UHR (%) = [UA (mg/dL)/HDL-C (mg/dL)] × 100.

Assessment of hypertension

Blood pressure was measured by trained nurses using a validated automated sphygmomanometer (Omron HEM-7200) under standardized conditions. Participants were seated and rested for at least 5 minutes before assessment. Three systolic and diastolic blood pressure readings were obtained at 45-second intervals, and the mean of the three measurements was used in the analysis.

Hypertension was defined by any of the following criteria:

(1) systolic blood pressure ≥140 mmHg and/or diastolic blood pressure ≥90 mmHg; or

(2) self-reported physician diagnosis of hypertension; or

(3) current use of antihypertensive medication.

This definition aligns with contemporary Chinese and international hypertension guidelines11,12.

Assessment of covariates

Baseline data were collected using standardized questionnaires, physical examinations, and laboratory assessments. Prespecified covariates included age, sex, educational attainment, smoking status, alcohol consumption, physical activity, and body mass index (BMI). Smoking and drinking were categorized as never vs ever. Physical activity was classified using metabolic equivalent task (MET) minutes per week (≥600 vs <600 MET-min/week), with a separate category for missing physical activity data.

Clinical covariates included total cholesterol, triglycerides, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), and medication use for hypertension, diabetes, and dyslipidemia. Prevalent diabetes was defined as fasting plasma glucose ≥7.0 mmol/L, HbA1c ≥6.5%, or current use of glucose-lowering medication. Cardiovascular disease (CVD), chronic kidney disease (CKD), and hyperlipidemia were defined based on self-reported physician diagnosis or verified medical history. To retain the full analytical sample, missing continuous variables were imputed using the mean, whereas missing categorical variables were handled by creating an additional “missing” category.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and categorical variables as counts (percentages). UHR was standardized using Z-score transformation. In addition, UHR was dichotomized into low and high groups using an optimal cutoff value of 8.65 derived from receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis for discrimination of prevalent hypertension (Figure S1). Because the present analyses were based on a restricted analytic sample with complete biomarker data and included several modeling procedures that were not implemented in a fully unified survey-weighted framework, sampling weights were not applied in the primary analyses; accordingly, the findings should be interpreted as associations within the analytic sample rather than weighted national estimates.

Potential confounders were prespecified based on prior epidemiological evidence and clinical relevance13,14. We used a hierarchical adjustment strategy to assess the robustness of associations with progressively greater covariate control. Three models were constructed: Model 1 adjusted for age and sex; Model 2 further adjusted for smoking status, drinking status, and physical activity; and Model 3 additionally adjusted for BMI, CVD, CKD and diabetes.

Associations between UHR and hypertension were examined using multivariable logistic regression, with results reported as odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). UHR was analyzed both as a continuous exposure (per 1-SD increment) and as a categorical variable (low vs high). Potential nonlinear exposure–response relationships were evaluated using restricted cubic spline models with four knots at the 5th, 35th, 65th, and 95th percentiles; nonlinearity was assessed using likelihood ratio tests comparing models with and without nonlinear spline terms. To explore potential changes in slope, segmented logistic regression was used to identify data-driven inflection points and to estimate associations below and above the detected point.

Prespecified subgroup analyses were conducted by age, sex, C-reactive protein (CRP) level, diabetes status, cardiovascular disease, hyperlipidemia, chronic kidney disease, and smoking status. Effect modification was evaluated by including multiplicative interaction terms and comparing nested models using likelihood ratio tests.

Sensitivity analyses included multiple imputation using chained equations; additional adjustment for CRP, medication use, and hyperlipidemia; and alternative hypertension definitions based solely on self-reported physician diagnosis or current antihypertensive treatment. We also conducted an additional sensitivity analysis further adjusting for systolic blood pressure alone. E-values were calculated to evaluate the minimum strength of association that an unmeasured confounder would need to explain away the observed associations.

To assess the cross-sectional discriminative performance and potential clinical usefulness of adding UHR to traditional factors, we evaluated model performance. Discrimination was quantified using ROC curves and the area under the curve (AUC), with 95% CIs estimated by DeLong’s method and AUCs compared using the DeLong test. Incremental discriminative ability beyond the base model (Model 3) was further assessed using category-free net reclassification improvement (NRI) and integrated discrimination improvement (IDI), with 95% CIs derived via bootstrap resampling (1,000 iterations). Calibration was examined using calibration plots by deciles of predicted probability, comparing mean predicted probabilities with observed event rates. Clinical usefulness was evaluated using decision curve analysis (DCA), which estimates net benefit across a range of threshold probabilities by weighing true-positive against false-positive classifications; net benefit curves were generated for the base model, the UHR-extended model, and reference strategies of treating all or treating none. Given the cross-sectional design, these analyses were intended to assess discriminative ability rather than prospective prediction.

All analyses were conducted in R (version 4.2.3). A two-sided P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Baseline characteristics of participants according to hypertension status in the CHARLS Cohort.

Among the 9,255 participants, 3,844 (41.5%) had hypertension at baseline. Compared with normotensive individuals, participants with hypertension were older (61.98 ± 9.56 vs 57.88 ± 8.96 years; P < 0.001) and had higher UHR levels (10.36 ± 5.01 vs 9.12 ± 4.60; P < 0.001). Overall, hypertensive participants exhibited a less favorable cardiometabolic profile, including higher BMI (25.32 ± 4.84 vs 23.03 ± 4.06 kg/m2; P < 0.001), triglycerides (148.10 ± 122.90 vs 122.48 ± 90.21 mg/dL; P < 0.001), LDL-C (118.92 ± 36.53 vs 114.80 ± 33.85 mg/dL; P < 0.001), and total cholesterol (197.74 ± 39.58 vs 190.67 ± 37.74 mg/dL; P < 0.001). Comorbidities were more common in the hypertensive group, with higher prevalences of diabetes (19.8% vs 11.7%), cardiovascular disease (19.8% vs 8.7%), and hyperlipidemia (15.2% vs 5.4%) (all P < 0.001). Correspondingly, use of antihypertensive (46.4% vs 1.2%), glucose-lowering (5.4% vs 2.4%), and lipid-lowering medications (9.0% vs 2.8%) was substantially more frequent (all P < 0.001). Women comprised a slightly larger proportion of participants with hypertension (55.1% vs 52.1%; P = 0.005). No significant between-group differences were observed for educational attainment, smoking or drinking status, or chronic kidney disease prevalence.

Associations of UHR With hypertension

As shown in Table 2, higher UHR was consistently associated with greater odds of hypertension across all models. In the fully adjusted model, each 1-SD increment in UHR was associated with a 21% higher odds of hypertension (OR 1.21, 95% CI 1.15–1.28; P < 0.001).

Dose–response relationships between UHR and hypertension

As shown in Figure 1, restricted cubic spline models suggested a nonlinear association between UHR and the odds of hypertension (P for nonlinearity = 0.065). The odds increased more steeply at lower UHR values and then tended to plateau at higher levels.

A model-derived inflection point was identified at UHR = 9.09. The association appeared stronger at lower UHR levels and weaker at higher levels. Odds ratios were adjusted for age, sex, BMI, smoking status, drinking status, physical activity, and cardiovascular disease.

In segmented logistic regression, a model-derived inflection point was identified at UHR = 9.09. Below this observed inflection point, each 1-SD increase in UHR was associated with markedly higher odds of hypertension (OR 1.73, 95% CI 1.42–2.12; P < 0.001). Above this point, the association was attenuated but remained statistically significant (OR 1.08, 95% CI 1.01–1.15; P = 0.032).

Subgroup analyses

Subgroup analyses are presented in Figure 2. The association between UHR and hypertension was broadly consistent across most predefined strata, with no evidence of effect modification by sex, CRP level, chronic kidney disease, diabetes, hyperlipidemia, or smoking status (all P for interaction >0.05). In contrast, a significant interaction was observed with age (P for interaction <0.001), with a modestly stronger association among participants aged <65 years than among those aged ≥65 years.

Sensitivity analyses

As shown in Table S2, the association between UHR and the odds of hypertension was highly consistent across all five imputed datasets and in the pooled estimates derived using Rubin’s rules, and was fully concordant with the findings of the primary analyses. Additional adjustment for CRP, antihypertensive medication use, and hyperlipidemia did not materially change the association between UHR and hypertension (Table S3), and similar results were observed in a separate sensitivity analysis further adjusting for systolic blood pressure alone (Table S4). To assess the potential impact of unmeasured confounding, E-values were calculated for the main associations (Table S5). The E-values for UHR were 1.56 per 1-SD increase and 1.99 for high versus low UHR, suggesting that relatively modest unmeasured confounding could attenuate these associations. Furthermore, results were materially unchanged in sensitivity analyses using a blood pressure-independent definition of hypertension (Table S6).

Discriminative performance and clinical utility of UHR

Addition of UHR to the base model significantly improved discrimination for prevalent hypertension, with the AUC increasing from 0.696 to 0.712 (P < 0.001). Reclassification metrics showed meaningful improvement, with a category-free NRI of 0.251 and an IDI of 0.024. Calibration analyses indicated good agreement between predicted and observed classification. DCA demonstrated higher net benefit for the UHR-extended model across clinically relevant threshold probabilities, suggesting potential utility for cross-sectional classification (Figure 3).

Discussion

In this large, nationally representative sample of middle-aged and older Chinese adults, elevated UHR was consistently associated with higher odds of hypertension. Participants with higher UHR levels carried a substantially greater burden of hypertension. Furthermore, we found that adding UHR to a conventional base model improved discrimination, reclassification, calibration, and clinical net benefit. These findings support the potential utility of UHR as a supplementary marker associated with hypertension.

Experimental and clinical evidence suggests that hyperuricemia contributes to endothelial dysfunction and arterial stiffening through pathways involving oxidative stress, reduced nitric oxide bioavailability, and activation of the renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system15,16. UA can promote vascular injury through redox-sensitive signaling and inflammatory activation, thereby facilitating vascular remodeling and blood pressure elevation. In contrast, HDL particles exert antioxidative and anti-inflammatory effects beyond their role in cholesterol transport, although HDL-C concentration is an imperfect surrogate for HDL function. At the population level, lower HDL-C levels are generally associated with reduced antioxidative capacity and weaker vascular protection17. By integrating these two biologically opposing components, UHR may reflect the balance between pro-oxidative burden and antioxidative defense. Consistent with prior studies linking higher UHR to adverse cardiometabolic profiles18,19, our results extend the evidence by demonstrating an independent association between UHR and hypertension in a nationally representative Chinese population.

Beyond the individual effects of uric acid and HDL-C, UHR may have particular biological relevance because it jointly reflects two opposing processes involved in blood pressure regulation, namely increased oxidative and inflammatory burden on the one hand and reduced endothelial and antioxidative protection on the other20–23. From this perspective, a higher UHR may indicate a state in which uric acid-related endothelial injury, reduced nitric oxide bioavailability, and vascular stress are insufficiently counterbalanced by HDL-mediated vasoprotective effects, including antioxidative and anti-inflammatory activity. Such an imbalance may contribute to impaired vascular homeostasis, increased arterial stiffness, and elevated blood pressure. Therefore, compared with either uric acid or HDL-C alone, UHR may provide a more integrated reflection of the metabolic–vascular milieu associated with hypertension.

We also observed suggestive evidence of a nonlinear association between UHR and hypertension. Restricted cubic spline analysis indicated that the odds of hypertension increased more steeply at lower-to-moderate UHR levels and appeared to attenuate at higher values. Segmented regression identified a potential inflection point; however, this finding should be interpreted cautiously given the cross-sectional design, the data-driven nature of the estimation, and the absence of external validation. Although the underlying explanation remains uncertain, it is possible that modest deterioration in metabolic and oxidative balance may already be sufficient to contribute to vascular dysfunction, whereas at higher UHR levels the association may become less pronounced because of biological saturation, coexisting comorbidities, treatment effects, or other sources of heterogeneity. Similar nonlinear or plateau-like associations have been reported for other composite metabolic and inflammatory biomarkers in relation to cardiovascular outcomes, including the triglyceride-glucose index and C-reactive protein-based indices24–26. Nevertheless, the observed pattern requires confirmation in prospective studies with repeated measurements and in independent populations.

We also observed stronger associations between UHR and hypertension among participants aged <65 years. One possible explanation is that, in relatively younger individuals, metabolic and oxidative disturbances may exert a more detectable influence on vascular function and blood pressure regulation before advanced vascular stiffening and multimorbidity become dominant. In contrast, among older adults, the association may be attenuated by age-related arterial remodeling, a higher burden of comorbidities, medication use, and greater heterogeneity in the determinants of blood pressure27,28. These possibilities require confirmation in future studies.

In addition to the primary association analyses, we assessed the incremental discriminative ability of UHR in a clinical modeling framework. These results should be interpreted as evidence of discriminative ability rather than prospective predictive performance. The improvement in AUC after adding UHR was modest, which is not unexpected when a baseline model already includes strong correlates such as age and adiposity. Moreover, AUC may be relatively insensitive to clinically relevant gains. Importantly, complementary measures supported added value, with improvements in NRI and IDI that indicate better separation of participants with and without prevalent hypertension. Calibration remained acceptable, and decision curve analysis suggested higher net benefit across clinically relevant thresholds. Taken together, these results suggest that UHR provides incremental discriminative information for hypertension beyond traditional risk factors.

Clinical implications

In our analyses, UHR showed a nonlinear association with hypertension. The odds of hypertension appeared to increase more steeply at lower-to-moderate UHR levels and then attenuated beyond the data-driven inflection point, although this pattern requires prospective confirmation. In cross-sectional discrimination analyses, adding UHR to conventional factors yielded modest but statistically significant improvements in discrimination, together with consistent gains in classification metrics and net benefit. Given its low cost and wide availability, incorporating UHR into standard assessment may help refine the identification of individuals with higher odds of hypertension.

Limitations

Several limitations warrant consideration. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference and does not allow determination of temporal directionality. Therefore, we cannot establish whether elevated UHR contributes to the development of hypertension or instead reflects metabolic and vascular alterations associated with existing disease. Second, although CHARLS uses a complex, nationally representative sampling design, sampling weights were not applied in the present analyses. Because the analytic sample was restricted to participants with available laboratory measurements and complete data for key variables, the findings may not fully represent the target national population. Accordingly, both the estimated prevalence of hypertension and the observed associations should be interpreted with caution with respect to population-level generalizability. Third, residual confounding from unmeasured dietary and lifestyle factors remains possible. Because sufficiently detailed information on dietary intake and lifestyle interventions was not available in the present analytic dataset, these factors could not be incorporated into further adjustment or sensitivity analyses. Fourth, UHR was measured at a single time point, and we were unable to account for within-person variability over time. Finally, because the study included Chinese adults aged 45 years and older, the findings may not be directly generalizable to younger individuals or to other ethnic groups, and external validation in diverse populations is needed. Moreover, the identified inflection point was derived from a data-driven modeling approach and should therefore be interpreted cautiously rather than as evidence of a definitive biological threshold.

Conclusions

In summary, higher UHR was independently associated with greater odds of hypertension in a nationally representative sample of middle-aged and older adults. UHR exhibited a nonlinear dose–response relationship with hypertension and it provided incremental discriminative value beyond conventional risk factors. Together, these findings support UHR as a practical marker of metabolic and oxidative stress that may help refine the clinical characterization of hypertension-related risk profiles.
