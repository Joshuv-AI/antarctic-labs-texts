Isolation and characterization of a bacteriophage targeting Proteus mirabilis for use in culture media
Authors: Songül Ötkün, Müzeyyen Demir, Erdal Polat, Sevil Erdenliğ Gürbilek
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Proteus mirabilis is an important opportunistic bacterium that affects both animal and human health. One of the key characteristics of this bacterium is its distinctive zonal spreading on solid media. This movement, known as swarming, complicates the isolation and identification of pathogenic agents present alongside P. mirabilis in clinical samples. The aim of this study was to isolate a lytic bacteriophage targeting P. mirabilis to prevent its growth and evaluate the use of these phages in cultures of clinical samples contaminated with Proteus. A phage designated as P5663 was isolated using general phage isolation and purification methods. Genomic and electron microscopic analyses suggested that the phage belongs to the class Caudoviricetes, and genome-based comparisons supported its close relatedness to bacteriophages within the family Casjensviridae. The isolated phage was analyzed for its lytic activity against 16 P. mirabilis, one P. vulgaris, and 19 different non-Proteus bacterial species. The growth of P. mirabilis on Tryptic Soy Agar (TSA) medium prepared with the isolated phage was evaluated. All Proteus isolates analyzed in the study were lysed by P5663 Φ, while the other bacterial species were not affected. It was observed that while P. mirabilis suspensions of varying concentrations exhibited growth in the inoculation area on TSA containing P5663 Φ, the zonal spreading (swarming) was inhibited. In conclusion, this study presents a novel perspective on the use of bacteriophages, which have a wide range of applications. It was concluded that phages could be used to inhibit the swarming behavior of P. mirabilis in culture media without affecting the growth of non-target bacteria.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12866-026-05141-1.

Introduction

Proteus species, part of the Enterobacteriaceae family, are widely found in nature. Within this genus, six species have been identified: P. mirabilis, P. vulgaris, P. penneri, P. hauseri, P. terrae, and P. cibarius [12]. P. mirabilis is the primary species associated with Proteus infections and is commonly found in the gastrointestinal tract of animals and humans as well as in the environment [4, 8]. These bacteria play a significant role in urinary tract, wound, and nosocomial infections [4, 5]. P. mirabilis is a Gram-negative, motile, facultative anaerobic microorganism with high urease activity. A key feature of these bacteria is their characteristic zonal spreading (swarming) on solid media [5, 15]. Due to their relatively faster division rate compared to many other bacterial agents and their swarming movement, isolating pathogens that co-exist with P. mirabilis in clinical samples becomes highly challenging [15, 17]. P. mirabilis is naturally resistant to many antimicrobial agents and can grow as a contaminant even on selective media [28]. Various compounds with anti-swarming properties, such as urea, activated charcoal, and ethanol, have been reported to be effective in preventing swarming behavior in cultures [15, 17]. However, these conventional agents may exert non-specific effects on bacterial physiology and can interfere with the growth of non-target microorganisms, particularly in mixed cultures. In addition, their use may introduce variability and affect the reliability of microbiological analyses [15, 17]. Therefore, alternative approaches such as bacteriophage-based strategies may provide a more targeted and biologically relevant solution.

Bacteriophages are procaryotic viruses and they were scientifically recognized in the early 1900s. With advances in electron microscopy, their viral nature was revealed in the 1940 s [1, 2]. It is reported that phages are ubiquitous in the biosphere wherever bacteria exist, with an estimated abundance of approximately 1031 [7]. The host range of phages varies; some phages exhibit narrow bacterial specificity, while others can infect multiple bacterial species. Understanding the effects of phages on bacteria has paved the way for their use in various applications [16, 26].

The aim of this study is to isolate a lytic bacteriophage targeting Proteus species, specifically to prevent the growth of P. mirabilis, which can complicate the isolation of target pathogens in clinical samples. Due to the characteristic "bull’s eye" spreading of the host bacterium from a central point, the goal was to achieve growth only in the inoculation area. Therefore, additionally, this study seeks to evaluate the use of these phages in cultures of clinical samples contaminated with Proteus. To the best of our knowledge, no previous study has specifically investigated the use of Proteus-specific bacteriophages incorporated into culture media to control swarming behavior.

Materials and methods

Bacterial strains

Proteus mirabilis isolates (n = 17) used in this study were obtained from clinical samples previously submitted to the Veterinary Microbiology Laboratory for routine diagnostic purposes and were part of the existing laboratory culture collection. No samples were collected specifically for this study. The isolates were identified by MALDI-TOF MS as previously described [23].

To determine the host specificity of the isolated phage, strains from the collection of the Microbiology Laboratory at Harran University’s Faculty of Veterinary Medicine were used. These strains included P. vulgaris, Brucella abortus, Brucella melitensis, Enterococcus faecalis, Enterococcus faecium, Escherichia coli (n = 3), Escherichia fergusonii, Klebsiella oxytoca, Kluyvera intermedia, Lactococcus lactis, Ochrobactrum intermedium, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Pseudomonas alcaligenes, Salmonella Infantis, Salmonella Kentucky, Serratia marcescens, Staphylococcus aureus, Staphylococcus epidermidis, Staphylococcus pseudintermedius, and Streptococcus uberis.

All the P. mirabilis isolates used in this study were previously characterized as multidrug resistant.

Bacteriophage isolation

Sewage water samples were collected from a municipal wastewater treatment plant located in Şanlıurfa Province, Türkiye, for phage isolation. The method employed was a modification of classical phage isolation techniques [3], adapted in our laboratory. The collected sewage water was centrifuged at 6000 rpm for 20 min. The resulting supernatant was filtered through 0.45 μm (Sartorius, Germany) and subsequently 0.2 μm filters, and the obtained filtrate was used as the phage source.

CITA medium, a selective medium for Brucella, contains five different antimicrobials: amphotericin B, colistin, nystatin, nitrofurantoin, and vancomycin (Merck, Germany). It is used for the isolation of Brucella from samples with a high likelihood of contamination [6]. For this study, a P. mirabilis strain (P-4) from the laboratory's strain collection, capable of growing on CITA medium, was used as the host. A suspension of the host strain was prepared at a concentration of 1 × 10⁹ CFU/mL. For the double-layer agar plaque assay, the required number of tryptic soy agar plates containing 2 mM Ca2⁺ were prepared and left to stand at room temperature. Meanwhile, the phage solutions to be counted were serially ten-fold diluted in SGM buffer (50 mM Tris–HCl, 100 mM NaCl, 8.1 mM MgSO₄, and 0.01% (w/v) gelatin, pH 7.5), starting from the undiluted solution to a 10⁻⁹ dilution. Then, 100 µL of each phage dilution and 100 µL of an overnight broth culture of the host bacterium were quickly added to tryptic soy soft (molten) agar with a 0.4% agar concentration, and the mixture was poured onto the top layer of a tryptic soy agar petri dish (approximately 3 mL)and spread evenly. After allowing the top agar to solidify for approximately 30 min, the plates were incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. At the end of the incubation period, lytic plaques were observed. Since each plaque originates from a single phage particle, phages were collected from individual plaques into SGM buffer using a micropipette, carefully avoiding contact with surrounding bacterial growth.

Phage purification

To obtain phages specific to P. mirabilis, TSA medium was prepared with a 4% agar concentration as described by Little et al. [21]. P. mirabilis P4 suspension prepared at McFarland 0.5 density was spread onto the agar surface using a swab and allowed to dry. Meanwhile, collected phage suspension in SGM was used as phage source in double-layer agar plaque assay. The areas where individual phage plaques had formed were collected using a sterile pipette tip and transferred into 1 mL of SGM. The resulting suspension was diluted tenfold, and the process was repeated three times in order to ensure phage purification.

Phage enrichment

The purified phage suspension (100 µL) was then mixed with 5 mL of LB broth and 150 µL of P. mirabilis P4 host culture (0.5 McFarland density) and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h in a shaking incubator at 200 rpm to increase the phage concentration. After incubation, the culture was centrifuged at 5000 × g for 10 min, and the supernatant was sterilized by filtration through a 0.22 µm membrane filter [10], and it was plated on Tryptic Soy Agar (TSA, BD, USA). After the agar surface had dried, 25 µL of the sterile phage filtrate was pipetted onto the agar surface and then evenly spread using a sterile inoculating loop. The plates were incubated at 37 °C for one day, allowing the agar surface to dry beforehand. Following incubation, the agar surface was gently washed with the remaining sterile phage filtrate to recover potential phage particles. The collected suspension was centrifuged at 6000 rpm for 20 min, and the supernatant was sequentially filtered through 0.45 μm and 0.2 μm membrane filters. The obtained filtrate was then subjected again to the same inoculation procedure described above. This enrichment cycle was repeated until distinct lysis zones were observed on the plates. The phage titer of the resulting enrichment phage suspension was determined according to the method described by Kropinski et al. [18] by performing a double-layer agar plaque assay. The enriched and counted phage filtrate was stored at −20 °C for future use.

Transmission electron microscopy

Twenty microliters of the phage suspension, diluted at a ratio of 1:5 with deionized water, was placed on sterile parafilm. A 200-mesh copper grid coated with formvar was placed on top of the sample, with the formvar-coated side facing the sample. After waiting for 5 min, the grid was removed from the sample and left to dry at room temperature for 5 min. Once the sample was dry, it was stained at room temperature for 45 s with 20 µL of 2% uranyl acetate. After the grid dried, it was examined using a JEOL 1010 model Transmission Electron Microscope (TEM) at the Dicle University Science and Technology Research and Application Center (DÜBTAM). Images were captured using a GATAN/782 ES500W Erlangshen CCD camera attached to the electron microscope.

Whole-genome sequencing and bioinformatic analysis

Genomic DNA of the isolated bacteriophage was extracted from a purified high-titer phage lysate using a commercial DNA extraction kit (GeneMATRIX Viral RNA/DNA Purification Kit, Poland) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Whole-genome sequencing was performed using a next-generation sequencing platform (Illumina NovaSeq). Raw paired-end sequencing reads were subjected to quality control and preprocessing using fastp (1.0.1), including adapter trimming, quality filtering, and removal of low-quality reads. The quality of raw and filtered reads was assessed using FastQC (0.12.1), and comprehensive quality reports were generated with MultiQC (1.33). Genome assembly was performed using Shovill (1.1.0), which integrates SPAdes for de novo assembly of short-read sequencing data. Assembly quality and contiguity were evaluated using QUAST, including assessment of assembly length, number of contigs, N50 value, and largest contig size. Based on these analyses, the largest contig (~ 59.7 kb), representing the bacteriophage genome, was used for all downstream analyses. Short contigs (< 500 bp) were excluded to eliminate potential assembly artifacts, and subsequent analyses were conducted using the finalized single-contig genome. Genome annotation was performed using Prokka (1.14.6) for automated prediction of coding sequences (CDSs). Assembly and annotation metrics, as well as sequencing quality statistics, are provided in supplementary Table S1.

Whole-proteome-based phylogenetic analysis was performed using ViPTree, which constructs a viral proteomic tree based on genome-wide similarity of predicted protein sequences. Reference bacteriophage genomes were automatically retrieved from the ViPTree database, and detailed information on the reference viruses used for the phyloproteomic analysis is provided in supplementary Table S2.

The complete genome sequence of Proteus phage P5663 has been deposited in the GenBank database under the accession number PX739550.

Incorporation of bacteriophage into the medium

Three hundred milliliters of prepared TSA medium was cooled to 45–50 °C after sterilization and distributed into conical centrifuge tubes, (Corning®, Merck) in 25 mL portions. Sterile phage suspension (stock: 1X107 Plaque Forming Unit, PFU/mL) was added to the medium at 5% (v/v) (1.25 mL stock) and 10% (v/v) (2.5 mL stock) concentrations, mixed, and then poured into 90 mm diameter Petri dishes. Control plates without phage supplementation were prepared in parallel under identical conditions. After the medium solidified at room temperature, P. mirabilis (P-4) bacterial suspensions prepared at 1, 0.5, and 0.1 McFarland densities were spotted (2 µL) in the center of the medium using the spot technique. All experimental conditions were performed in triplicate. Plates were allowed to dry at room temperature and then incubated at 37 °C for 18 h. Results were evaluated after incubation.

To assess the performance of the phage-containing medium under mixed-culture conditions, P. mirabilis and S. epidermidis were co-inoculated onto both phage-supplemented and control TSA plates. Equal volumes of bacterial suspensions adjusted to 0.5 McFarland were mixed and 5 µL of the mixture was spotted onto the agar surface. Following incubation at 37 °C for 18 h, bacterial growth patterns and swarming behavior were visually evaluated.

Discussion

P. mirabilis is a significant opportunistic pathogen affecting both animal and human health. Due to its ability to spread rapidly across surfaces, combating this pathogen becomes challenging [4, 5, 8, 11, 12, 25]. The high resistance of Proteus species to antibiotics and harsh environmental conditions (including survival on selective media) presents a significant obstacle in isolating pathogens from contaminated materials [28]. Various microorganisms have been studied to inhibit the swarming movement of Proteus species. [22] reported that hemolytic E. coli inhibited P. mirabilis swarming in their study of anti-swarming properties of A. baumannii, E. coli, K. pneumoniae, and P. aeruginosa. In the present study, a lytic Proteus phage (P5663 Φ) was isolated and evaluated for its potential use in clinical samples. The results showed that, although bacterial growth was observed on solid media at different inoculum densities (1, 0.5, and 0.1 McFarland), swarming motility was inhibited. These findings indicate that the effect of the phage on P. mirabilis is dependent on phage–bacterium interaction conditions. Under high local phage concentrations, as observed in spot assays, rapid infection leads to complete lysis. In contrast, when phages are distributed within the agar matrix, bacterial growth may initially occur, while swarming motility is inhibited. Additionally, the latent period of the phage may allow initial bacterial replication, enabling the formation of localized colonies before effective phage-mediated lysis occurs. Little et al. [21] investigated the swarming behavior of P. mirabilis by altering agar concentration in their media, reporting no swarming beyond the inoculation area in media with 4% agar concentration. In contrast to these approaches, swarming inhibition in the present study was achieved using a lytic bacteriophage without altering the composition of the growth medium, highlighting its potential as a targeted and adaptable strategy.

The bacteriophage (P5663) isolated in the present study demonstrated a high degree of host specificity, exhibiting lytic activity against Proteus strains while having no observable effect on bacteria belonging to other genera and species. This selective activity represents a significant advantage, particularly in facilitating the isolation of non-target bacteria in mixed cultures containing Proteus. In addition, genomic and electron microscopic analyses indicated that the phage belongs to the class Caudoviricetes and is closely related to bacteriophages within the family Casjensviridae. These findings are consistent with previously reported Proteus bacteriophages in terms of host specificity and taxonomic characteristics, and are in agreement with similar studies in the literature [14, 20, 27].

Hernandez et al. [15] reported the effectiveness of a method involving the addition of 2 drops of 90% ethanol to the culture medium to inhibit P. mirabilis swarming, noting that this technique did not affect the growth of other bacteria such as Staphylococcus aureus, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Enterobacteriaceae, and Streptococcus. There are many culture media reported to have inhibitory effects on the swarming of Proteus (such as Cystine-Lactose-Electrolyte-Deficient agar, and Phenylethyl Alcohol agar); however, these media can also suppress the growth of certain other microorganisms [19, 24]. Therefore, while preventing the swarming of Proteus, their inhibitory effects on the growth of other species constitute a disadvantage in diagnostic processes and especially in contamination analyses. The present study aimed to develop a technique that does not require changes in the medium composition and does not affect the growth of non-target bacteria. It was found that the isolated phage did not lyse non-Proteus bacteria commonly encountered in clinical samples.

Bacteria develop resistance to antibiotics for various reasons. The lytic effect of bacteriophages on antimicrobial-resistant bacteria provides a significant alternative for combating these pathogens [13]. In addition to these limitations, the increasing problem of antimicrobial resistance further highlights the importance of alternative approaches such as bacteriophage-based strategies. Although CITA medium contains antimicrobials that inhibit Proteus contaminants [6], in the present study, the CITA medium also played a crucial role as a host for isolating P. mirabilis phage. There is a need for comprehensive studies covering various Proteus species and strains together with WGS analysis.

One of the major limitations of phage-based approaches is the potential for bacteria to develop resistance to phages [9]. To minimize the likelihood of prolonged exposure to phages in culture or the presence of phage-resistant Proteus strains in the medium, the use of different Proteus phage cocktails can be recommended.

Conclusion

Bacteriophages are a group of microorganisms with a wide range of applications and potential. Due to their various advantages, the use of phages for different purposes is increasingly being explored. This study demonstrated that bacteriophages might be effectively used to prevent the growth and swarming of P. mirabilis, a contaminant or secondary pathogen, in contaminated materials. However, this prevention effect should be tested in mixed culture conditions in order to evaluate prevention of contamination more accurately. In conclusion, phages have the potential to be utilized in contamination control.
