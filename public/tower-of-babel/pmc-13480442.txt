Ciprofol versus Propofol for Emergence Agitation and Recovery Quality in Pediatric Dental Anesthesia: A Randomized Controlled Trial
Authors: Yaqiu Zhang, Long Wu, Zhenzhen Gao, Minjuan Zhang, Caihua Feng, Hui Zhang, Haopeng Zhang, Bing Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeThis prospective randomized controlled trial compared the effects of ciprofol versus propofol on emergence agitation severity, as measured by Pediatric Anesthesia Emergence Delirium (PAED) scores, and on postoperative recovery quality in children undergoing dental procedures under general anesthesia.Patients and MethodsThis single-center, prospective, double-blind trial enrolled 160 children aged 3–7 years (ASA I–II) scheduled for dental treatment under general anesthesia. Participants were randomized 1:1 to receive ciprofol (n=80) or propofol (n=80). The primary outcome was EA incidence, defined as a Pediatric Anesthesia Emergence Delirium (PAED) score >10 at 5 minutes after awakening. Secondary outcomes included PAED score, extubation and emergence times, postoperative pain (FLACC), respiratory complications, quality of recovery (QoR-15), and parental satisfaction.ResultsThe incidence of EA was 3.8% (3/80) with ciprofol vs 7.5% (6/80) with propofol, with no statistically significant difference between groups (absolute difference: −3.75%; 95% CI: −12.22% to 4.00%; P=0.305). However, ciprofol significantly reduced total PAED scores [median 2.00 (IQR 2.00, 4.00) vs 5.00 (3.00, 7.00); P<0.001], with better scores across all five subscales (all P<0.01). Extubation time (3.83±0.96 vs 5.79±1.42 min; P<0.001) and emergence time (20.90±1.57 vs 22.77±3.28 min; P<0.001) were shorter with ciprofol. QoR-15 scores were higher (129.94±14.95 vs 121.97±16.22; P=0.002), and more parents reported being “very satisfied” (95.0% vs 85.0%; P=0.033) in the ciprofol group. Coughing incidence (5.0% vs 15.0%; P=0.035) and rescue sedation requirements (0.25±1.76 vs 2.33±8.38 mg; P=0.033) were also reduced. No significant difference was observed in FLACC pain scores (P=0.085).ConclusionWhile the incidence of emergence agitation did not differ significantly between groups, ciprofol was associated with significantly lower PAED scores, indicating reduced EA severity, along with faster emergence, improved recovery quality and higher parental satisfaction compared with propofol. These findings suggest that ciprofol may be an effective alternative to propofol in pediatric outpatient dental anesthesia, though the lack of a statistically significant difference in the primary outcome warrants cautious interpretation.

Introduction

Dental diseases in children, particularly early childhood caries, are highly prevalent and often require comprehensive multi-tooth treatment under general anesthesia to achieve efficient and comfortable care.1,2 Although dental treatment under general anesthesia can resolve multiple carious lesions in a single session, the narrow surgical field, variable stimulation intensity, and shared airway for both oral procedures and emergence significantly increase the risk of emergence agitation (EA), which directly compromises the quality of postoperative recovery.3,4 EA is particularly common in preschool-aged children5,6 and is characterized by disorientation, inconsolable crying, and agitation.7 This condition is associated with interactions among residual anesthetic effects, oropharyngeal blood and secretions, and pain or anxiety, and can lead to self-extubation, surgical site bleeding, reduced parental satisfaction, and prolonged post-anesthesia care unit (PACU) stay.8,9 Therefore, ensuring a rapid yet smooth emergence, reducing the incidence of EA, and enhancing the quality of postoperative recovery have become central concerns in pediatric outpatient dental anesthesia practice and research.

Due to its rapid onset of action and rapid clearance, propofol continues to be the agent of choice for achieving expedited emergence in pediatric anesthesia, notably in the context of outpatient dental procedures.10–15 Recent evidence further indicates that intravenous propofol maintenance is associated with significantly lower emergence delirium rates compared with sevoflurane inhalational maintenance in children.6 However, its dose-dependent cardiorespiratory depression and injection pain often force anesthesiologists to balance between “deepening anesthesia to prevent EA” and “reducing the dose to avoid respiratory depression”.16–18 The rationale for deepening anesthesia lies in its ability to suppress central nervous system excitability during emergence by enhancing GABA-A receptor-mediated inhibitory neurotransmission, thereby reducing the hyperarousal response to nociceptive stimuli and airway irritation.4,10,15 Conversely, shallower anesthesia may permit breakthrough arousal and exaggerated sympathetic responses during the transition from unconsciousness to wakefulness, predisposing to agitation.5,16 This clinical dilemma is particularly pronounced during brief and variably stimulating pediatric dental procedures, where it may lead to fluctuations in anesthetic depth upon emergence and consequently elevate the risk of EA.9,10,19 Hence, there is an urgent clinical need for an alternative agent that retains the benefits of rapid onset and recovery while offering more stable hemodynamics and a lower risk of adverse events.

In recent years, a novel short-acting intravenous anesthetic, ciprofol (chemical name: 2,6-diisopropylphenol-3-hydroxy-4-methyl-cyclopropanecarboxylate), a derivative of propofol with higher selectivity for the β2/β3 subunits of the GABA-A receptor, has been developed. Adult studies suggest that its sedative potency is 1.5–2.0 times that of propofol, while reducing the decrease in mean arterial pressure by 30%–40% and significantly lowering the incidence of apnea and injection pain. Patients can meet Steward≥4 discharge criteria within 5–8 minutes after discontinuation.20–22 However, evidence regarding the use of ciprofol in the pediatric population remains extremely limited. To date, only a few studies have preliminarily explored its safety and dosing in children,23,24 and no direct high-quality evidence exists specifically in the context of outpatient dental anesthesia—a clinical setting characterized by high-intensity stimulation, shared airway management, and rapid patient turnover. Theoretically, the more stable plasma concentration decay profile and reduced residual sedative effects of ciprofol may mitigate emotional rebound and pain sensitization following dental procedures in children, thereby potentially reducing the incidence of emergence agitation and improving postoperative recovery quality. However, robust evidence supporting its use in pediatric populations, particularly in the high-stimulation, fast-turnover setting of outpatient dental care, remains lacking.

Based on this rationale, we formulated the following hypothesis: compared with propofol, ciprofol may reduce the severity of emergence agitation (as reflected by lower PAED scores) and improve postoperative recovery quality in children undergoing dental procedures under general anesthesia. To test this hypothesis, we designed and conducted this prospective, randomized, double-blind, controlled trial to compare the incidence of emergence agitation between ciprofol and propofol in children undergoing dental treatment under general anesthesia. This study aimed to generate high-quality evidence to optimize anesthesia management for pediatric day-case dental procedures and to provide a safer, more comfortable intravenous anesthetic alternative for clinical practice.

Materials and Methods

Trial Design

This study was a single-center, prospective, randomized, double-blind, parallel-controlled clinical trial. The protocol was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of the Fourth Military Medical University Stomatological Hospital (Approval No.: KQ-YJ-2025-258; Date: October 21, 2025) and was prospectively registered in the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (Registration No.: ChiCTR2500111981; Date: November 7, 2025). The design and conduct of the study strictly adhered to the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from the legal guardians of all participating children before enrollment.

Study Population

The study aimed to enroll children scheduled for elective outpatient dental caries treatment under general anesthesia in our hospital between November 2025 and April 2026. The inclusion criteria were: ① age 3–7 years; ② American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status classification I or II; ③planned comprehensive dental treatment under general anesthesia for extensive caries (≥10 teeth); ④ written informed consent obtained from a legal guardian. This threshold was selected because patients with ≥10 carious teeth typically represent the majority of those requiring dental rehabilitation under general anesthesia, and this degree of extensive treatment serves as a substantial surgical stressor associated with prolonged operative time and increased risk of emergence agitation.25–27 Selecting this higher-risk subgroup increases the likelihood of detecting meaningful differences between the two anesthetic regimens. The exclusion criteria were: ① concurrent upper respiratory infection; ② cognitive impairment or neurodevelopmental disorders; ③ congenital systemic diseases; ④ known allergy or contraindication to study-related anesthetics (eg, propofol, ciprofol); ⑤ severe hepatic or renal dysfunction; ⑥ Exclusion of patients with craniofacial congenital anomalies or an anticipated difficult airway, as indicated by a modified Mallampati score of III–IV, mouth opening < 3 finger breadths, thyromental distance < 3 finger breadths, restricted neck extension (< 35°), or inability to protrude the lower incisors beyond the upper incisors.

Randomization and Blinding

Eligible participants were randomly assigned in a 1:1 ratio to either the ciprofol group (Group C) or the propofol group (Group P) using a computer-generated random number sequence. The allocation scheme was concealed in sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes, which were managed by an independent researcher not involved in clinical assessment or data collection. The study employed a double-blind design, ensuring that the children and their guardians, anesthetists, postoperative assessors, and data recorders remained blinded to group assignment. Study medications (ciprofol or propofol) were prepared and labeled by the central pharmacy in syringes identical in appearance and labeling according to the random sequence, thereby maintaining blinding throughout the trial. To maintain blinding despite the volume difference between ciprofol (0.2 mg/kg) and propofol (1 mg/kg), the following measures were implemented. First, all study drugs were prepared by an independent research nurse who was not involved in clinical care or outcome assessment. The drugs were drawn up in identical syringes, and the syringe barrels were fully covered with opaque labels to prevent visualization of the actual volume by the administering anesthesiologist. The syringes were labeled only with a random study code and the patient’s weight, with no indication of the drug identity or group assignment. Second, the attending anesthesiologist who administered the study drugs was responsible solely for drug delivery and intraoperative anesthesia management, and was not involved in any postoperative outcome evaluations. Third, all PACU nurses performing PAED assessments, data collectors, and statistical analysts remained blinded to group allocation throughout the entire study period. Fourth, to minimize the risk of unblinding due to hemodynamic differences, all anesthetic management decisions during the procedure (including fluid administration and vasopressor use) were guided by a standardized hemodynamic protocol based on predefined thresholds, rather than left to clinical judgment. These measures ensured that the drug administrator remained effectively blinded throughout the procedure, and that the potential for performance or detection bias was minimized.

Study Procedures and Interventions

All children were required to fast preoperatively according to established guidelines. Anesthesia induction was initiated in the presence of a parent, using a facemask to deliver nitrous oxide/oxygen mixed gas with low-concentration incremental sevoflurane inhalation. After the child lost consciousness via this inhalational induction, intravenous access was established. Subsequently, both groups received intravenous atropine (0.01 mg/kg), cisatracurium (0.15 mg/kg), fentanyl (2 μg/kg), and dexamethasone (0.2 mg/kg) based on body weight. Subsequently, Group C received ciprofol (20 mL: 50 mg; Shenyang Haisco Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd.; National Drug Approval No.: H20200013) at 0.5 mg/kg intravenously, while Group P received propofol (20 mL: 0.2 g; Beijing Fresenius Kabi Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd.; National Drug Approval No.: HJ20150655) at 2 mg/kg intravenously to complete anesthesia induction. After nasotracheal intubation, pressure-controlled ventilation was maintained intraoperatively, with end-tidal carbon dioxide tension kept between 35 and 45 mmHg, and anesthesia was maintained by continuous inhalation of 2%–3% sevoflurane. Thirty minutes prior to the completion of the procedure, all children received a prophylactic intravenous dose of ondansetron (2 mg) for antiemesis.

Upon completion of the procedure, the administration of sevoflurane was discontinued. Subsequently, the inspired oxygen concentration was adjusted to 100% with an oxygen flow rate set at 6 L/min. Group C received an additional intravenous dose of ciprofol 0.2 mg/kg,22,28 and Group P received an additional intravenous dose of propofol 1 mg/kg.29,30 Extubation was performed in the operating room after meeting predetermined deep anesthesia extubation criteria: end-tidal sevoflurane concentration<1.0%, patient in the lateral position with no significant movement/coughing, adequate spontaneous respiration (respiratory rate 14–18 breaths/min, tidal volume 6–8 mL/kg, end-tidal carbon dioxide tension 45–55 mmHg), suppressed airway reflexes, and confirmed physiological stability. If movement or coughing occurred during extubation, additional sedative medication corresponding to the assigned group was administered.29,31

Following extubation, the patient’s head was extended, oxygen was delivered via facemask, and the patient was transferred in the lateral position to the post-anesthesia care unit (PACU), where oxygen saturation (SpO2) was continuously monitored until full recovery of consciousness.

Outcome Measure

The primary outcome of this study was the incidence of emergence agitation (EA), defined as a Pediatric Anesthesia Emergence Delirium (PAED) score >10.32,33 The PAED scale consists of five items: eye contact with the caregiver, purposeful actions, awareness of surroundings, restlessness, and inconsolability. Each item is scored from 0 to 4, yielding a total score ranging from 0 to 20. The PAED scale used in this study was applied with written permission from the original authors. In this study, a PAED score >10 at 5 minutes after awakening was defined as emergence agitation, consistent with previous validation studies. The PAED score was assessed at 5 minutes after awakening in the post-anesthesia care unit (PACU) by a trained research nurse who was blinded to the group assignment and had undergone standardized training in the use of the scale to ensure consistent and reliable assessment.33

Secondary outcomes included: ①Perioperative hemodynamic parameters, including heart rate and blood pressure, were recorded at the following time points: baseline (before anesthesia), after induction of anesthesia, at the commencement of the procedure, immediately before extubation, and after extubation;②Time-related indicators, including extubation time (time from discontinuation of maintenance anesthesia to tracheal tube removal) and emergence time (time from discontinuation of anesthesia to the child being able to correctly answer simple questions); ③ Postoperative pain assessed using the Face, Legs, Activity, Cry, Consolability (FLACC) scale at PACU discharge;34–36 ④ Incidence of airway-related adverse events associated with deep extubation, including hypoxia, coughing, and laryngospasm; ⑤ Proportion and dose of postoperative rescue sedation medication; ⑥ Postoperative quality of recovery evaluated using the 15-item Quality of Recovery (QoR-15) scale,37–39 completed by guardians prior to discharge; ⑦ Guardian satisfaction with the child’s overall perioperative recovery and medical services was evaluated using a 5‑point Likert scale (1 = very dissatisfied to 5 = very satisfied),40 which is a commonly used tool for assessing patient and family satisfaction in perioperative settings. The scale ranged from 1 (very dissatisfied) to 5 (very satisfied), and guardians were asked to rate their level of agreement with statements regarding the child’s comfort during the recovery period, the quality of communication with the medical staff, and their overall experience with the perioperative care provided.

Rescue Sedation Protocol

Rescue sedation was administered when the child exhibited persistent agitation (PAED score >10 for ≥3 consecutive minutes) or severe movement that posed a risk of self-injury or surgical site bleeding, despite the absence of hypoxemia or inadequate analgesia (FLACC score ≤4). The sedation regimen consisted of propofol 0.5 mg/kg for children in the propofol group or ciprofol 0.1 mg/kg for children in the ciprofol group, administered intravenously. The dose could be repeated once after 3 minutes if agitation persisted, up to a maximum of two doses. All rescue sedation administrations were recorded, including the total dose per patient.

Statistical Analysis

This study is a randomized, double-blind, parallel-controlled trial. The primary endpoint was the incidence of emergence agitation (EA). The sample size was calculated based on preliminary single-center pilot data from our team. The pilot study included 15 patients per group (30 patients in total). In the propofol group, the incidence of EA was 33.3% (5/15), and in the ciprofol group, the incidence was 13.3% (2/15). With 85% statistical power and a two-sided significance level of 0.05, using PASS 15.0.5 software (NCSS, Kaysville, UT, USA), it was calculated that at least 76 analyzable cases were required per group (actual power: 85.1%). Considering an approximate 6% rate of loss to follow-up or protocol deviation, 81 cases were ultimately determined per group, resulting in a total sample size of 162. We acknowledge that the observed EA incidence in the formal study (<8%) was substantially lower than the 30% rate used for sample size calculation, a discrepancy we attribute primarily to the rigorous exclusion criteria and optimized perioperative management implemented in the formal trial. Consequently, the study is underpowered to detect a statistically significant difference in the binary primary outcome of EA incidence. However, the sample size provides adequate power for the continuous secondary outcomes, particularly the PAED total score and QoR‑15 score, which demonstrated statistically significant between‑group differences. Nevertheless, given that the sample size was calculated based on the primary outcome, findings from secondary analyses should be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis‑generating. The pilot data were entirely independent from the formal study population, with no patient overlap between the pilot and formal studies, and were used solely for sample size estimation.

Statistical analyses followed the intention-to-treat principle. For the primary outcome, the difference in EA incidence between the two groups was calculated with a 95% confidence interval. Secondary outcomes were analyzed as follows: continuous variables with normal distribution were expressed as mean ± standard deviation and compared using the independent samples t-test; continuous variables with non-normal distribution were expressed as median (interquartile range) and compared using the Mann–Whitney U-test. Categorical variables were presented as frequency (percentage) and compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. Repeated measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to analyze hemodynamic parameters over time. For the age-subgroup analysis, children were stratified into two groups: <6 years and ≥6 years. This cutoff was selected for an exploratory post hoc analysis based on clinical experience in pediatric anesthesia, as children aged 6 years and above generally demonstrate better cooperation, more stable hemodynamic responses, and more reliable communication during emergence, whereas younger children are at higher risk for EA and exhibit distinct physiological responses to anesthetics. Given that the study was not powered for subgroup comparisons and no adjustment was made for multiple testing, these findings should be interpreted cautiously as exploratory and hypothesis-generating, rather than confirmatory. All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.5.2; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). A two-sided P-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

A total of 160 patients underwent randomization and were included in the final analysis. Security Indicators

The dose of postoperative rescue sedation administered was also significantly lower in the ciprofol group compared to the propofol group (0.25±1.76 mg vs 2.33±8.38 mg; P=0.033). Discussion

This study provides the first direct comparison of the incidence of emergence agitation between ciprofol and propofol in children receiving general anesthesia for outpatient dental procedures. Our findings demonstrated that the incidence of emergence agitation was 3.8% in the ciprofol group and 7.5% in the propofol group, with no statistically significant difference between the two groups (P=0.305). Given that the primary outcome did not reach statistical significance, this study should be understood as a comparative assessment of the safety and recovery profiles between ciprofol and propofol, rather than a demonstration of superiority of ciprofol in preventing EA. Accordingly, all interpretations of secondary outcomes should be regarded as exploratory and hypothesis‑generating.

Our findings demonstrate that ciprofol was associated with a lower incidence of emergence agitation compared with propofol (3.8% vs 7.5%), along with significantly better emergence quality as reflected by lower total PAED scores and improved performance across all five behavioral dimensions. This finding aligns with the pharmacological profile of ciprofol. As a novel intravenous anesthetic with higher selectivity for β2/β3 subunits of the GABA-A receptor and a potentially more stable pharmacokinetic profile,41 ciprofol may mitigate early postoperative emotional fluctuation and cognitive disturbance, thereby contributing to a smoother emergence process as observed in behavioral assessments. However, it should be noted that no objective assessments such as electroencephalographic monitoring, bispectral index (BIS) measurements, pharmacokinetic analyses, or biomarker evaluations were performed in this study. Consequently, statements linking the observed clinical findings to specific receptor selectivity, pharmacokinetic properties, or neurocognitive mechanisms should be presented as speculative hypotheses rather than established conclusions, and interpreted with appropriate caution.

However, we acknowledge that several confounding factors beyond the pre-administered ciprofol or propofol may have contributed to the incidence and severity of emergence agitation in this study. Sevoflurane, used for maintenance of anesthesia, is well-documented to increase the risk of EA in children,4 an effect attributed to its relatively low blood-gas partition coefficient, rapid emergence, and potential central nervous system excitatory effects.4 Fentanyl (2 microg/kg), while providing intraoperative analgesia, has a context-sensitive half-time of approximately 20–30 minutes in children, and by the time of extubation (approximately 90 minutes after administration in this study), its central effect has substantially declined.42 The residual effects of cisatracurium (0.15 mg/kg) are unlikely to be a major contributor to EA given its short elimination half-life (approximately 22–29 minutes in children).43 Pain (as reflected by FLACC scores, which showed no significant between-group difference, P=0.085) and airway irritation from the nasotracheal tube are more relevant confounders.3,8 Notably, the incidence of coughing was significantly lower in the ciprofol group (5.0% vs 15.0%; P=0.035), suggesting that ciprofol may offer better suppression of airway reflexes during emergence, which could indirectly reduce EA by attenuating noxious stimuli.

More importantly, the additional pre-extubation doses of ciprofol (0.2 mg/kg) and propofol (1 mg/kg) were administered immediately upon completion of the procedure, approximately 3–5 minutes before extubation. Given that the elimination half-life of propofol in children is approximately 60–80 minutes,10 and that of ciprofol is approximately 30–45 minutes,20,44 these doses would not be fully cleared by the time of extubation. However, their concentration-effect relationship suggests that by 5 minutes after emergence, the peak effect has passed, and the residual effect is primarily related to redistribution rather than sustained receptor occupancy.10,24 Nevertheless, these pre-extubation agents may still influence emergence quality through indirect mechanisms, including: more stable pharmacokinetic profiles that reduce inter-individual variability in emergence;20 differential effects on airway reflex sensitivity upon extubation; and potential modulation of the balance between GABAergic inhibition and glutamatergic excitation during the recovery phase.41 The observed differences between the two groups may thus reflect differential receptor binding affinity—ciprofol has higher selectivity for beta2/beta3 subunits of the GABA-A receptor41—and distinct redistribution kinetics,22,27 which could translate into more stable emergence behavior.

Notably, the incidence of emergence agitation in our study (3.8% with ciprofol and 7.5% with propofol) was substantially lower than the 10%–80% rates typically reported in the literature.3,5–7 This discrepancy likely reflects our stringent selection criteria—we excluded children <3 years (a demographic with EA rates often exceeding 50%), patients with cognitive or neurodevelopmental disorders, and those with craniofacial anomalies, all established risk factors for EA3,5,7—as well as our standardized multimodal anesthetic protocol incorporating fentanyl, dexamethasone, ondansetron, and deep extubation under controlled conditions, which minimized noxious stimuli such as airway irritation, pain, and nausea.8,9,29,31 Additionally, parental presence during inhalational induction may have attenuated preoperative anxiety, a recognized predictor of postoperative behavioral disturbances,2,5 while the single 5-minute PAED assessment with a strict >10 cutoff and blinded, trained evaluators likely improved specificity compared with less standardized approaches.5,7 The nature of dental surgery itself—limited tissue trauma, minimal blood loss, and brief operative duration—further contributed to lower surgical stress.

We acknowledge that the findings of this study should be interpreted as evidence of an association between ciprofol use and improved emergence outcomes in this specific clinical context, rather than as definitive proof of a direct causal relationship. The observed benefits are likely multifactorial, reflecting the net effect of the entire anesthetic protocol—including induction, maintenance with sevoflurane, analgesic and antiemetic adjuncts, and the deep extubation strategy—rather than an isolated drug effect. Causal attribution to ciprofol versus propofol alone should be made with caution. Future studies employing pharmacokinetic‑pharmacodynamic modeling, along with continuous monitoring of anesthetic depth (eg, bispectral index), are warranted to further disentangle the relative contributions of these factors.

These considerations underscore that our findings should be interpreted within the context of a highly controlled, single-center trial with optimized perioperative care. While the absolute EA incidence was lower than literature benchmarks, the relative difference between ciprofol and propofol remains clinically meaningful and consistent with ciprofol’s pharmacological advantages. The 3.75% absolute risk reduction, if confirmed in broader populations including higher-risk subgroups (younger children, developmental disorders, longer or more invasive procedures), could translate to substantial clinical benefit given the global volume of pediatric dental procedures under general anesthesia.1,2 Future studies should evaluate ciprofol in higher-risk subgroups (including younger children, those with developmental disorders, and longer or more invasive surgical procedures) to determine the generalizability of our findings.

A key methodological consideration in this trial was the inclusion criterion of requiring ≥10 carious teeth. This threshold was established based on two primary rationales. First, clinical practice and retrospective analyses indicate that the majority of pediatric patients undergoing comprehensive dental treatment under general anesthesia present with dental caries involving 10 or more teeth.45,46 Therefore, this criterion enhances the clinical representativeness of our study cohort, ensuring its relevance to real-world patient populations. Second, although the relationship between the extent of dental disease and postoperative outcomes remains incompletely characterized, extensive multi-quadrant rehabilitation represents a substantial surgical stressor associated with prolonged operative time and increased risk of emergence agitation.47,48 By selecting this higher-risk subgroup with substantial treatment needs, the study was designed to evaluate the differential effects of the anesthetic regimens within a more clinically challenging context. This approach increases the likelihood of detecting meaningful clinical differences and strengthens the practical applicability and reference value of the findings for managing pediatric dental patients undergoing extensive rehabilitation under general anesthesia.

The study also observed that ciprofol significantly shortened both extubation time and emergence time. This is likely related to its distinctive pharmacokinetic properties. As a new short‑acting intravenous anesthetic, ciprofol may exhibit faster redistribution and clearance, enabling children to regain consciousness and orientation more rapidly after surgical stimulation ends.49,50 This not only enhances the operational efficiency of outpatient surgical units but may also reduce anxiety by minimizing the duration of emergence‑related discomfort. From a clinical perspective, this provides an objective basis for improving the peri‑emergence experience of children and increasing parental satisfaction.

In terms of safety, ciprofol demonstrated, with a significantly lower incidence of coughing compared to propofol.51,52 This may be due to a more moderate suppression of airway reflexes or reduced irritant effects on laryngeal and tracheal mucosa. Additionally, the lower need for postoperative rescue sedation in the ciprofol group suggests that its anesthetic profile may be more stable or that residual sedative effects are milder, thereby reducing the requirement for supplemental medication due to inadequate or excessive sedation/analgesia during emergence.

Postoperative recovery quality represents a pivotal outcome measure for assessing the success of outpatient procedures.50 This study found that children in the ciprofol group achieved significantly higher QoR‑15 scores, and parental satisfaction was correspondingly higher. This comprehensive advantage likely stems from the combined improvements across multiple dimensions: a smoother emergence, fewer airway‑related adverse events, and faster recovery, together contributing to an enhanced overall postoperative experience. These findings constitute robust supporting evidence for the application of ciprofol in pediatric day‑case procedures within an enhanced recovery framework.53

Hemodynamic stability is a core safety indicator for anesthetic agents. Although the overall group‑by‑time interaction did not reach statistical significance, children in the ciprofol group exhibited more moderate trends in heart rate and blood pressure changes throughout the perioperative period compared to the propofol group. However, given that neither the group effect nor the interaction effect reached statistical significance, the available data do not provide robust evidence to support a definitive claim that ciprofol offers superior hemodynamic stability compared with propofol. Age‑stratified subgroup analysis revealed age‑dependent differences in the hemodynamic effects of ciprofol; these subgroup findings should be regarded as exploratory. In children aged ≥6 years, the ciprofol group demonstrated more stable hemodynamic parameters at multiple time points (eg, baseline, post‑induction, and post‑intubation), consistent with previous reports in adults indicating less circulatory depression with ciprofol [20‑22]. The underlying mechanism may involve a more moderate modulation of sympathetic tone and vascular smooth muscle function by ciprofol, leading to more effective buffering of stress‑induced cardiovascular responses during high‑stimulus events such as induction and intubation.54 Conversely, among children <6 years of age, although overall hemodynamic trends were stable, ciprofol was associated with significantly lower heart rate only immediately after tracheal intubation. This specific observation should be interpreted with caution, given the small sample size and the absence of adjustment for multiple comparisons.

In contrast, among children <6 years of age, although overall hemodynamic trends were stable, ciprofol was associated with significantly lower heart rate only immediately after tracheal intubation. This specific observation may reflect unique pharmacological characteristics of ciprofol in the developing cardiovascular system. Younger children typically exhibit higher sympathetic tone and may be more sensitive to anesthetic agents.54 The higher receptor subtype selectivity of ciprofol might lead to differential modulation of the sympatho‑adrenal axis or baroreflex during intense stimulation such as intubation, resulting in more pronounced heart rate suppression compared with propofol.24 Moreover, distinct pharmacokinetic parameters in children—especially infants and toddlers—such as volume of distribution and clearance, could influence peak plasma concentration or effect‑site concentration at the time of stimulation, thereby affecting hemodynamic responses at specific time points.55,56 Thus, this study suggests that ciprofol offers favorable hemodynamic stability in children, with age‑related characteristics: in older children, stability is observed across multiple time points, while in younger children it may manifest as differential regulation of sympathetic responses to specific strong stimuli (eg, intubation). These findings provide important physiological and pharmacological rationale for the age‑tailored selection of anesthetic agents in clinical practice. Future studies integrating pharmacokinetic‑pharmacodynamic modeling and more detailed autonomic monitoring are needed to further elucidate the mechanisms underlying age‑dependent hemodynamic effects of ciprofol in children.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, as a single‑center trial, caution is warranted when generalizing the findings, and future multi‑center clinical trials are needed for validation. Second, the study focused primarily on early postoperative recovery and did not systematically evaluate longer‑term outcomes such as behavioral changes or sleep quality after discharge, thus leaving the long‑term impact of the two agents on recovery incompletely characterized. Furthermore, the deep extubation strategy employed in this study may itself influence emergence agitation, representing a potential confounding factor. The lower-than-expected EA incidence, while reflecting our optimized perioperative care, also limits the statistical power to detect smaller effect sizes and may reduce the external validity of our findings to less controlled clinical environments. Future research could explore the performance of both agents under different extubation timing strategies, in broader pediatric populations including higher-risk subgroups, or employ pharmacokinetic‑pharmacodynamic modeling to more precisely elucidate their mechanisms of action, thereby providing a basis for individualized clinical use.

Conclusions

In this pediatric outpatient dental anesthesia cohort, the incidence of emergence agitation did not differ significantly between ciprofol and propofol. However, ciprofol was associated with lower PAED scores (reduced EA severity), faster emergence, improved recovery quality and higher parental satisfaction. Definitive claims of superior hemodynamic stability are not supported; age-subgroup findings are exploratory and hypothesis-generating. These findings suggest ciprofol may be an effective alternative to propofol, though the negative primary outcome warrants cautious interpretation and secondary benefits should be considered exploratory.
