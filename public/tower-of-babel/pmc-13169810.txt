Psychometric properties of the Spanish structured interview of personality organization revised (STIPO-R) in a clinical and community sample
Authors: Maria Jesus Rufat, Josep Lluís Matalí, Fernando Gutiérrez, Marc Ferrer, Elisabet Sánchez, Claudia Zumel, Eulàlia Ripoll, Joan Vegué, Natàlia Calvo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe diagnosis of personality disorders (PD) remains challenging in clinical practice. The Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders reviewed (DSM-5-TR) proposes an alternative diagnostic hybrid model (AMPD), that emphasizes dimensional assessment of personality pathology, particularly severity of impairment in personality functioning (Criterion A), a construct conceptually derived from Kernberg’s model of personality organization (PO) but operationalized in a highly simplified form. However, few structured diagnostic interviews aligned with this framework are available in Spanish. The Structured Interview of Personality Organization–Revised (STIPO-R) provides a dimensional assessment of the original PO construct and may offer clinically valuable information beyond current Criterion A measures. The aim of the present study was to validate the Spanish version of the STIPO-R and to examine its reliability (internal consistency) and construct validity.MethodA cross-sectional study was carried out with a sample of 206 subjects distributed into two groups: a clinical group comprised 134 outpatient psychiatric patients diagnosed with PD who were administered both the SCID-II and the Spanish version of the STIPO-R. The control group comprised 72 subjects without a PD diagnosis in the preceding ten years to whom the Spanish STIPO-R was administered. Reliability (internal consistency) and four types of validity evidence were examined: criterion, content, structural and concurrent with the SCID-II.ResultsThe results confirmed that the Spanish STIPO-R has adequate reliability (internal consistency), and content, structural, criterion and concurrent validity. The CFA confirmed a six-factor solution consistent with the domain structure proposed in the original English version: (1) Identity, (2) Object relations, (3) Primitive defenses, (4) Advanced defenses, (5) Aggression and (6) Moral values.ConclusionsThe Spanish STIPO-R is empirically confirmed to be valid and reliable for assessing normal and pathological personality organization according to Kernberg’s full theoretical model; including Defense Mechanisms, absent from the AMPD, and Aggression and Moral values, only partially represented within its trait domains - thereby offering clinicians a more comprehensive assessment of personality pathology.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40479-026-00342-6.

Background

Personality Disorders (PD) are associated with wide-ranging psychological and social impairment. Prevalence rates in community populations across the Western world range from 4.4 to 21.5% [1]. Traditionally considered chronic and intractable [2], pose severe limitations on personal functioning [3, 4] and are associated with lower quality of life and premature mortality [5, 6]. Accurate and timely diagnosis is therefore essential to guide effective treatment [7].

The assessment of PD poses significant challenges for clinicians owing to their clinical heterogeneity and high comorbidity [2, 7–13]. These challenges have driven a progressive shift in the field from categorical toward dimensional models that distinguish personality functioning from personality traits. Central to this shift is Kernberg’s hybrid structural theory of personality pathology [14], which emphasizes motivational components in self- and interpersonal functioning as the organizing core of personality, rather than signs and symptoms alone, anticipating the foundational features of current nosological systems by more than five decades. Early empirical support for this separation came from Parker et al. [15, 16], who provided evidence that disordered functioning and personality style are independent dimensions, with disordered functioning operating as a transdiagnostic marker common to all PD types. Kernberg’s concept of personality organization (PO) subsequently provided the theoretical foundation for the Level of Personality Functioning Scale (LPFS), the general severity criterion of the Alternative Diagnostic Model for PD (AMPD) Criterion A, which was formalized through a systematic review of clinician-rated dimensional measures identifying identity, self-direction, empathy, and intimacy as its core components [17]. The AMPD organizes PD assessment around two criteria: (1) Assessing disability in personality functioning in all its sub criteria: self (identity and self-direction) and interpersonal (self in relation to others: empathy and intimacy) - Criterion A-; (2) Assessing pathological personality traits - Criterion B- [18]. Some instruments have been developed to operationalize the AMPD. For categorical diagnosis, in Sect. 2, the Structured Clinical Interview for Personality Disorders − 5th edition [19] is recommended although a validated Spanish version is not yet available. It employs diagnostic criteria equivalent to those of the SCID-II [20]. SCID-II supports both categorical and dimensional assessment, given the number of criteria met provides dimensional information of symptom severity and continues to be widely used in research. For dimensional diagnosis of Criterion A (severity), in Sect. 3, the Level of Personality Functioning Scale (LPFS) [21]. However, according to some authors it requires the use of other complementary measures to assess severity [22, 23]. For Criterion B, the Personality Inventory for the DSM-5 (PID-5) is recommended [24].

The distinction between a general severity dimension rooted in self- and interpersonal dysfunction and more specific features of personality pathology maps directly onto Kernberg’s formulation of PO levels and their intrapsychic determinants [17, 25]. Indeed, identity psychopathology and its impact on self and interpersonal functioning were already considered core domains in the diagnosis of personality pathology by Otto Kernberg [14] within his structural personality theory [26]. For Kernberg, personality is organized on a continuum ranging from normal to pathological PO. Within the pathological range ‘Borderline Personality Organization’ (BPO) occupies a central position. Three key domains in the differential diagnosis between normal and pathological personality are: Identity diffusion syndrome, with predominance of Primitive defenses for coping with anxiety and impaired Reality testing under states of high emotionality. Other key domains, such as Object relationships, Aggression and Moral values, have been established by the author and his group [26–28]. This multidimensional framework has received broad theoretical and empirical support, addressing Identity [29–33]; Defense mechanisms [34–38]; and Object relations, understood, in part, as the interpersonal relations between oneself and others [30, 39–42]. As well as Aggression [43, 44] and Moral values [45, 46]. However, the LPFS captures only a subset of the structural determinants originally proposed by Kernberg, which additionally includes the defensive operations, the quality and management of aggression, and moral functioning and internalized values as core indicators of PO severity [17, 47]. Specifically, Defense Mechanisms are absent from the AMPD, whereas Aggression and Moral values are only partially represented within its trait domains [17]. Despite their well-documented clinical relevance to the diagnosis, severity assessment, prognosis, and treatment planning of PD, these three domains are thus not fully operationalized in current classification systems.

To date, two semi-structured interviews grounded in Kernberg’s model have been developed for the assessment of PO: the Structured Interview of Personality Organization (STIPO) [34] and the Structured Interview of Personality Organization - Revised (STIPO-R) [48]. However, they differ in some points. STIPO [34] comprises 87 items distributed across six clinical domains: (1) Identity, (2) Primitive defenses, (3) Advanced defenses, (4) Object relationships, (5) Aggression, and (6) Moral values. The first three domains are considered central to the diagnosis of PO; the remaining three characterize the severity of pathological organization [49]. Although some validation studies included a seventh domain (Reality testing), it was not retained in the final version. The STIPO-R [48, 50] was reorganized following accumulated clinical experience with the STIPO. The manual describes five domains and eleven subdomains across 55 items, with the Advanced defenses domain removed. The interview itself retains six domains and eleven subdomains: (1) Identity; (2) Object Relations; (3) Primitive defenses; (4) Advanced defenses; (5) Aggression and (6) Moral values. Therefore, assesses six clinical domains [48]: (1) Identity refers to an integrated and stable sense of self and others, including investment capacity and the ability to infer others’ mental states accurately. Impairment is reflected in identity diffusion, characterized by fragmented, unstable, and polarized self- and other representations. (2) Object relations refers to the quality of interpersonal relationships and internal self- and other representations, evaluated in terms of stability, depth, empathic capacity, and relational commitment. (3) Primitive defenses are anxiety-regulating mechanisms - including splitting, idealization/devaluation, denial, omnipotent control and projective identification - characteristic of severe personality pathology. (4) Advanced defenses are assessed along a continuum from adaptability to rigidity in response to stress. (5) Aggression refers to the extent to which aggressivity dominates the individual’s inner life and behavior, including self and other-directed destructive behavior. (6) Moral values refers to the internalization of stable ethical standards, assessed through moral decision-making and the capacity for guilt. In addition to these six domains, the STIPO-R incorporates a Narcissism scale. The scale comprises 11 items drawn from existing domains, assessing: satisfaction with work or studies, consistency of self-concept, and self-esteem (Identity); egocentrism, lack of interest in others, transactional view of relationships, and empathy (Object relations); idealization-devaluation and admiration-seeking (Primitive defenses); envy (Aggression); and exploitation of others (Moral values) [48, 50].

The validity and reliability of the clinical dimensions underlying Kernberg’s model have been assessed via the following instruments: the Inventory of Personality Organization (IPO) [51], used in clinical and nonclinical samples [46, 49, 52–55]. The STIPO [34], used in clinical samples [56–64]. And the STIPO-R [48] used in clinical and nonclinical samples [65].

IPO psychometric properties have been confirmed according to several studies: structural validity [46, 54, 55]; concurrent validity [46, 49, 52–54]; criterion validity [54, 55]; reliability (internal consistency using Cronbach’s alpha, tested by all validity studies); and test-retest [49, 53].

STIPO psychometric properties have been tested according to studies. Structural validity: confirmatory factor analysis supported a seven-factor structure comprising the three core domains of PO (Identity, Primitive defenses, and Reality testing) and four additional domains (Object relations, Defense mechanisms, Aggression, and Moral values) with acceptable fit indices [58, 59]. Convergent validity: STIPO demonstrated significant associations with established measures of PO (IPO; [56, 58, 59]); SCID II PD [20] and personality functioning (LPFS 12 items version [66]). Primitive defenses showed stronger associations with DSM Cluster B traits, whereas Reality testing was more strongly associated with Clusters A and C [56]. Other studies identified additional domain-cluster associations: Primitive defenses and Reality testing with Cluster A; Moral values with Cluster B; Identity and Advanced defenses with Cluster C [64]. Further associations were found with dissociative experiences, defensive styles, stress coping, state-trait aggression, attachment in intimate relationships, and dimensional PD indices [58, 59, 62]. Associations were particularly strong with Cluster B pathology - especially antisocial PD [62, 63] - and related constructs such as negative and positive affect, aggression, and interpersonal adjustment [56]. Criterion validity: STIPO discriminated between patients with and without personality pathology [57–59, 62].

Identity and Primitive defenses were the most discriminating factors; Reality testing did not reach significance in some studies [58, 59]. Adequate internal consistency and test-retest reliability were demonstrated in all mentioned studies for all domains except Reality testing [56, 58, 59, 62], below the threshold established by Nunnally and Bernstein [67]. One study provided evidence of criteria and convergent validity of the original English STIPO-R, showing its ability to differentiate levels of personality pathology severity, and established empirically derived cutoff-points for each domain consistent with Kernberg’s theoretically described levels of personality organization [65].

Several semi-structured interviews validated in Spanish support the categorical diagnosis of PD, such as the Structured Clinical Interview for DSM-5 Personality Disorders [19] or the International Personality Disorders Examination for ICD-10 [68]. Also, for assessing borderline personality disorder, such as the Revised Diagnostic Interview for Borderlines, adapted in Spanish [69]. For dimensional assessment, several self-report instruments are available in Spanish, as well as the Personality Disorders Inventory (PID-5) [24]. However, no Spanish-language interview currently allows the simultaneous categorical and dimensional assessment of personality structure, in an agile manner, with evidence of validity and reliability, in line with and beyond the AMPD [18], comprehensively assessing Kernberg’s original construct of PO. The German validation study of the STIPO highlighted the need for a validated shorter version to enhance its clinical applicability [62].

The present study aimed to validate the Spanish version of the STIPO-R [48] by examining its reliability (internal consistency) and construct validity. It provides novel empirical contributions on structural diagnosis, by evaluating the six domains of Kernberg’s full theoretical personality model, including Defense mechanisms, absent from the AMPD, and Aggression and Moral values, only partially represented within it [17]. Although assessing DSM categorical PD is not a primary aim of the STIPO-R, prior STIPO and STIPO-R validation studies have consistently examined its relationship to the categorical diagnostic system using the DSM cluster framework [56, 58, 59, 62–64]. While the DSM-5 abandoned this system due to insufficient validity - a decision supported by bifactor studies demonstrating that PD variance is better accounted for by a general psychopathology factor and specific residual dimensions than by discrete categorical groupings [70] – empirical research indicates that DSM clusters retain systematic, though overlapping, associations with broad psychopathology spectra and PID-5 domains: Cluster B disorders align primarily with externalizing traits (disinhibition and antagonism), Cluster C disorders with internalizing traits and negative affectivity, and Cluster A disorders with detachment and psychoticism-related dimensions [71, 72]. The cluster framework was therefore retained in the present study to ensure continuity with prior research, to support construct validation within its nomological network and to facilitate direct comparison with the existing evidence base, despite the well-known limitations of the categorical model [73, 74]. Such analyses help delineate the scope of the PO construct and ensure comparability with prior STIPO and STIPO-R validation studies. We hypothesized that the Spanish STIPO-R has adequate psychometric properties, including: content; structural (confirming six clinical domains, as proposed in the original English version [48]: (1) Identity; (2) Object relationships; (3) Primitive defenses; (4) Advanced defenses; (5) Aggression and (6) Moral values); concurrent and criterion validity; and reliability (adequate internal consistency).

Method

Participants

A total of 206 participants were recruited between September 2018 and December 2023 from the outpatient treatment program of a public health care community unit in Barcelona (Spain). All participants were over 18 years old and provided written consent prior to inclusion. The sample was distributed into two groups. The clinical group (n = 134) comprised patients with PD diagnosis, who were administered both the SCID-II and the Spanish STIPO-R. The control group (n = 72) comprised students from two different universities in Barcelona without PD diagnosis in the preceding ten years, to whom the Spanish STIPO-R was administered. All tests were administered by the main author of the present study.

Instruments

The STIPO-R is a 55-item semi-structured interview developed to measure six clinical domains: (1) Identity; (2) Object relations; (3) Primitive defenses; (4) Advanced defenses; (5) Aggression and (6) Moral values. It includes a Narcissism scale comprising eleven items drawn from the domains described above [48]. Three domains (Identity, Primitive defenses and Advanced defenses) are the core for the diagnosis of pathological PO. The remaining domains (Aggression, Object relations and Moral values) characterize the severity of pathological PO according to their degree of dysfunction [49]. Items are scored on a three-point scale ranging from 0 to 2. A score of 0 indicates the absence of the assessed characteristic or no impact on the respondent’s personal functioning. A score of 1 indicates that the characteristic is present but has low impact on functioning. A score of 2 indicates the presence of severe pathology and a high impact on functioning. Two scores are obtained [50]: (1) The arithmetic mean score of the domains and subdomains (categorical diagnosis of BPO). (2) And the five-point scale score based on clinical judgement to evaluate severity, that is, the impact of the assessed quality on personal functioning (dimensional diagnosis).

The Spanish SCID-II [20] was used as the gold standard for PD diagnosis [18].

Procedure

Permission for the present study was obtained from the authors of the original English STIPO-R [48]. Next, translation and back-translation from the English STIPO-R into Spanish were carried out to rigorously ensure the equivalence of the original English version with the adapted Spanish version. Two independent native Spanish speakers familiar with the underlying constructs and target population (SM and MB) produced an initial Spanish translation. A consensus version was then blindly back-translated into English by a native English speaker and compared with the original. Discrepancies were resolved through meetings between the translators and two external experts, and reviewed by the authors of the instrument, John Clarkin and Barry Stern. Prior to final approval, the Spanish STIPO-R was piloted with 20 subjects from the target population to ensure comprehensibility and feasibility, semantic and conceptual equivalence. The above procedures ensured rigorous linguistic and conceptual equivalence between the original and adapted version throughout the adaptation process, following the standard recommendations for questionnaire adaptation [75].

The assessment protocol was designed, including the selection of data collection procedures and instruments. Permission for the study was obtained from the Ethics Committee of Research of the Vall d’Hebron University Hospital (Barcelona, Spain), and the database was subsequently created. Sample size was then estimated. Clinical group recruitment was conducted across three successive visits. During the first visit, a clinical screening interview was conducted individually to assess the presence of PD criteria according to the DSM Manual, and to confirm the absence of diagnosis of schizophrenia, bipolar disorder type I, active substance use disorder and any organic condition that could better explain the symptoms. Patients who met the criteria for inclusion in the study were invited to participate voluntarily, and those who agreed to participate were asked to sign a written informed consent form. During the second visit, the SCID-II was administered individually, confirming the PD diagnosis and relevant sociodemographic variables were collected. During the third visit, the Spanish STIPO-R was administered individually to the clinical group. A total of 134 subjects completed the full assessment protocol. 10 did not complete it and were excluded. The control group was recruited concurrently over two successive visits. At the initial assessment, control group participants underwent a clinical screening interview to confirm the absence of any PD diagnosis within the preceding ten years, as well as the absence of the other diagnoses considered for the clinical group. SCID-II was not administered to the control group as this instrument is specifically designed to evaluate pathological personality in clinical populations [20]. People who met the criteria for inclusion in the study were informed about the study, invited to participate voluntarily, and those who agreed to participate were asked to sign a written informed consent form. At the second visit, the Spanish STIPO-R was administered individually.

Content validity, defined as the degree to which instrument content represents the intended construct, constitutes the foundational step for evaluating other sources of validity [76] and was therefore prioritized in the study design. Its conceptual framework comprises four components: domain definition, domain representativeness, content relevance, and instrument construction quality [77].

Content validity of the STIPO was established through expert clinical judgment: its domains, subdomains, and items were drafted, defined, and evaluated as representative and content-relevant by John Clarkin, Eve Caligor, Barry Stern, and Otto Kernberg, internationally recognized experts in PD and object relations theory at the Personality Disorders Institute, New York. With item placement additionally reviewed by Otto Kernberg [50]. The STIPO-R retains the same domain and subdomain structure but includes fewer items, omits the ‘Reality testing’ domain, and adds a Narcissism scale absent from the STIPO. The same authors determined which items were retained within each domain and subdomain, their relative importance, and their inclusion in the Narcissism scale. The present study sought empirical evidence of the content validity of the Spanish STIPO-R by examining whether an independent expert panel classified its items consistently with the original authors’ theoretical assignments, combining expert judgment with quantitative statistical analyses. The panel evaluated concordance in item classification both across Judges and relative to the predefined assignments in the Spanish STIPO-R, encompassing domains, subdomains, and the Narcissism scale.

To support this evaluation, items were renumbered from 55 to 58 to account for three sub-items within the first interview question. Two databases recorded each Judge’s domain, subdomain, and Narcissism scale assignments relative to the predefined Spanish STIPO-R classifications. Four independent item randomizations yielded a uniquely ordered interview version for each Judge, accompanied by a reference list of all domains, subdomains, and the Narcissism scale.

Expert selection criteria were established based on a literature review. Panel size depends on the accessibility and availability of qualified individuals [78]; although some authors recommend between two and ten experts [79], no consensus exists, and size should reflect the expertise and domain knowledge of its members [80]. Judges were selected on the basis of demonstrated expertise, peer recognition, and capacity to provide well-grounded evaluations.

The final panel comprised four expert Judges (all women; VL, IDA, ES, JS), recruited by convenience sampling, with a mean age of 47.2 years. All held MSc degrees in clinical psychology, had 8–17 years of experience diagnosing and treating PD in the Spanish publicly funded health system, and were familiar with the theoretical model underlying the STIPO-R but had no prior knowledge of the interview itself. Each judge received an individually randomized version of the Spanish STIPO-R along with a reference list of all domains and subdomains and was informed that the Narcissism scale comprises items drawn from across the instrument rather than constituting a dedicated set. Working independently, each judge assigned every item to a domain and subdomain and indicated whether it belonged to the Narcissism scale.

Data analysis

A descriptive analysis of the sociodemographic and clinical variables was carried out according to the condition of the sample (clinical group - control group). Next, descriptive statistics were obtained from the SCID-II [20] for the clinical group. In addition, calculations of the values of the domains, subdomains and the Spanish STIPO-R Narcissism scale have been carried out [50]. Decisions regarding data analysis to assess validity were made following recommendations for each type of validity [77, 81].

To assess concurrent and criterion validity, measurement invariance across groups was examined prior to conducting between-group comparisons [82]. However, this analysis could not be performed given the lack of variability of the control group in the clinical domain Aggression.

Content validity was assessed using the expert judgement method [76] combined with statistical procedures [83, 84]. Four expert Judges [80, 79] unfamiliar with the interview but knowledgeable in the underlying theoretical model, blindly and independently classified the items of the Spanish STIPO-R into domains, subdomains and the Narcissism scale according to their own criteria. Inter-rater agreement between each judge’s item assignments and the predefined structure of the Spanish STIPO-R was quantified using Cohen’s Kappa index [85] interpreted according to McHugh [86]. Overall concordance across all the Judges and the Spanish STIPO-R was estimated using the global Kappa coefficient for all the Judges jointly [87]. This was measured at three levels: the STIPO-R domain vs. the Judges’ overall domain assignments; the STIPO-R Narcissism scale vs. Judges’ overall Narcissism scale assignments; and the STIPO-R subdomain vs. Judges’ overall subdomain assignments. Pearson’s chi-square test [88] was subsequently used to evaluate the concordance in Kappa values across the three considered levels.

Structural validity was examined using Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA). The sample size adequacy was calculated on two grounds: first, with six factors and a sample size of 100 or more participants, medium-to-large effect sizes can be detected [89]; second, a minimum sample size of 200 participants yields stable parameter estimates [90]. Statistical power of the CFA [91] was determined by comparing models [92] considering the RMSEA value as a reference [93]. This approach accounted for 55 variables (Spanish STIPO-R items), 171 parameters, and 1.424 degrees of freedom, with the significance level set at p = .05.

The CFA was carried out following established procedures for Structural Equation Modelling [94–96]: model specification and estimation of model parameters via the Diagonally Weighted Least Square method (DWLS) with Lavaan R 6.1 [97]. Factor loadings were freely estimated for each theoretically specified factor, and the rest were constrained to zero. Errors were assumed to be independent. Model fit was evaluated using the following indices: the Likelihood ratio test (p > .05; [98, 99]); the Comparative Fit index (CFI > 0.90; [100]); the Tucker Lewis index (TLI > 0.90; [101]); the Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA; [93]); and the Standardized Root Mean Square Residual (SRMR < 0.08; [102]). The hypothesized six-factor model demonstrated adequate fit from the outset and no model re-specification was required.

Thereafter, reliability (internal consistency) was assessed using McDonald’s Omega index (ω; [103]), with commonly considered acceptable values from 0.7 to 1.00 [104].

Concurrent validity was subsequently tested. For categorical variables, frequencies and rates were calculated. For quantitative variables, basic descriptive statistics by condition were obtained, and appropriate contrasts were calculated.

Finally, criterion validity was assessed using complementary approaches: First, group differences between the clinical and control groups were tested using the Mann-Whitney-Wilcoxon test [105] to evaluate discriminative capacity. Second, Receiver Operating Characteristic Analysis (ROC) [106–108] was conducted to provide an index of known-groups discrimination between patients with PD and healthy controls. Given the non-representative nature of the sample, these analyses were not intended to establish clinically applicable cutoffs or diagnostic efficiency, but to additionally support criterion validity.

Eight logistic regression models were fitted of the subject condition (clinical - control) from the arithmetic global mean of the Spanish STIPO-R and each of the six clinical domains of the STIPO-R, as well as for the Narcissism scale. ROC curves were plotted for each model considered to obtain the cut-off for discriminating clinical and healthy control groups. The cut-off was calculated using the Area Under the Curve (AUC) [109]; the Accuracy Ratio [110] and the Youden Index [111].

All statistical analyses were carried out using Statistical Analysis Software (SAS) v9.4, SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA, and R version 4.1.2 [112]. Structural validity analyses were performed using the statistical package Subscale Explorer: Psychometric scale explorer and management, R package version 0.1.1 [113].

Results

Descriptives and distributions

Excluding age, significant differences were found between the outpatient clinical group (n = 134; M = 35.54; SD = 10.74) and the control group (n = 72; M = 32.53; SD = 8.82) across all the variables studied. Sociodemographic factors, such as a greater proportion of women in the clinical group (91%) and single or divorced individuals (84.1%), were present in most of the sample, with an inferior educational level (primary or secondary studies) (64.9%) and a precarious labor situation (75.4%). Compared to the control group, clinical participants had received prior treatment and were currently engaged in outpatient treatment (86.6%), had suicidal behavior (59.7%), and required psychiatric inpatient attention (53.7%).

According to the SCID-II, distribution of the clinical group by PD clusters was as follows: 33.6% (n = 40) met criteria for Cluster A, 86.51% (n = 109) for Cluster B and 63.71% (n = 79) for Cluster C. Clusters B and C were thus most prevalent. Borderline personality disorder was the most common diagnosis, followed by avoidant, obsessive-compulsive, paranoid, dependent, antisocial, narcissistic, histrionic, schizoid and schizotypal PD.

Validity and reliability (internal consistency)

Content validity

Overall interrater agreement (Kappa overall) and concordance between Judges’ classifications and the Spanish STIPO-R predefined assignments were strong–very strong [86]: domain (Kappa=0.85), subdomain (Kappa=0.94) and Narcissism scale (Kappa=0.86). Kappa equality tests indicated no significant differences between Judges and the Spanish STIPO-R for subdomain classifications (χ²(1) = 3.10; p=.07) and the Narcissism Scale (χ²(3, n = 4) = 99; p=.80). Kappa equality could not be tested for domain classifications as only one judge showed variability.

Structural validity

Concerning the estimation of the power of the CFA, the present study provided 82% power to detect an effect size in terms of RMSEA [93] variance of +/-0.006.

Regarding structural validity, most dimensions of the Spanish STIPO-R and the Narcissism scale yielded association coefficients between 0.6 and 0.8. Dimension 6 (Moral values) obtained Spearman correlation coefficients ranging from 0.42 with dimension 4 (Advanced defenses), indicating a weak positive monotonic relationship, to 0.7 with the total interview score, indicating a strong positive monotonic relationship (Table 1).

Internal consistency

The Spanish STIPO-R showed adequate internal consistency across all domains, with Omega coefficients at or above 0.7 [104]: Identity (ω = 1); Object relationships (ω = 0.93); Primitive defenses (ω = 0.89); Advanced defenses (ω = 0.77); Aggression (ω = 0.91); and Moral values (ω = 0.79).

Concurrent validity

Criterion validity

Mean comparisons

A comparison of Spanish STIPO-R mean level scores between the clinical and control groups yielded χ² (5, n = 206) = 189.81; p < .01), indicating statistically significant differences in its distribution levels across conditions. The proportions of Neurotic 1 and Neurotic 2 were significantly higher in the clinical group, while Borderline categories were exclusively present in the clinical group. The clinical group scored significantly higher (p < .01) than the control group across all domains, subdomains, the Narcissism scale, and the overall STIPO-R mean. The overall STIPO-R mean score differed significantly between the clinical and control groups (S = 2664; p < .01), with the clinical group scoring significantly higher (Tables 5 and 6. Supplementary material).

ROC analysis

Eight logistic regression models were fitted to predict group membership (clinical vs. control) based on mean scores for each of the six clinical domains, the Narcissism scale and the overall STIPO-R score. Figure 2 presents the ROC curve for the total mean score derived from the fitted model. The ROC analysis revealed the following results: the AUC of the group according to the overall mean of the Spanish STIPO-R was 0.99 (SE = 3.81; Wald test (W (1, n = 206) = 20.86; p<.01) (Fig. 2). This was followed by the means of Identity (0.99; SE = 1.89; W (1, n = 206) = 31.84; p<.01); Aggression (0.98; SE = 2.58; W (1, n = 206) = 30.67; p<.01); Object relationships (0.96; SE = 1.39; W (1, n = 206) = 43.69; p<.01); Narcissism scale (0.96; SE=0.97; W (1, n = 206) = 33.75; p<.01); Primitive defenses (0.95; SE=0.96; W (1, n = 206) = 43.51; p<.01); Advanced defenses (0.94; SE=0.63; W (1, n = 206) = 53.54; p<.01); and finally, the mean in the Moral values domain with 0.81; SE=0.97; W (1, n = 206) = 33.75; p<.01. The confidence interval did not include 0.5 points in any case.

Using the AUC [109] and Youden Index criterion [111] the cut-off point was 0.52, yielding a sensitivity of 0.96, specificity of 0.99, and an overall correct classification rate of 97%, with the smallest distance to the point (0.1). That is, values higher than 0.52 were predicted as belonging to the clinical group, and those lower than 0.52 to the control group, where the proportion of correctly predicted cases (clinical vs. control) was 97.09%. Using the Accuracy ratio criterion [110] the cut-off point was 0.4, yielding a correct classification rate of 97.57%, marginally higher than the two previous criteria. Across all three criteria, cut-off values ranged between 0.4 and 0.52, i.e., scores above these thresholds on the overall Spanish STIPO-R mean were predicted to belong to the clinical group, and those below to the control group.

Discussion

This study describes the adaptation and validation of the Spanish STIPO-R, which assesses normal and pathological PO and its severity based on Kernberg’s model [14, 47]. The Spanish STIPO-R proved its adequate psychometric properties: content; structural (with empirical confirmation of six clinical domains as proposed by the original English STIPO-R [48]: (1) Identity; (2) Object relationships; (3) Primitive defenses; (4) Advanced defenses; (5) Aggression and (6) Moral values); concurrent (with associations with the categorical measure of PD (SCID-II) [20]); and criterion validity (with ability to differentiate normal from pathological PO). And reliability (internal consistency). The overall conclusions show that the Spanish STIPO-R reliably differentiates normal from pathological personality and enables the diagnosis of personality structure and its psychopathological alterations according to Kernberg’s model. Its use enables clinicians to assess Kernberg’s original construct of PO [14], which forms the theoretical foundation of the AMPD’s diagnostic approach [17]. Unlike the AMPD, the Spanish STIPO-R retains domains that are highly relevant to the diagnosis of PO but were completely (Defense Mechanisms) or partially (Aggression and Moral values) excluded from the AMPD [17]. This enriches and improves the information available to clinicians in the diagnostic process and clinical practice with PD. Some points warrant further comment.

Concerning content validity, the results are consistent with the definition and consideration of its items as representative and relevant across domains, subdomains and Narcissism scale by the original authors of the English STIPO and STIPO-R [34, 48]. This was further confirmed by statistical procedures [83, 84]: global Kappa coefficients for its six psychopathological domains exceeded 0.80 (strong agreement); global Kappa coefficients for its eleven subdomains exceeded 0.90 (very strong to near-perfect agreement); global Kappa coefficients for the Narcissism scale exceeded 0.80 (strong agreement) [86]. These findings provide empirical evidence for the domains and subdomains of the Spanish STIPO-R as clearly defined and theoretically relevant constructs. Notably, although the Narcissism scale draws items from across the instrument’s domains and subdomains, expert Judges consistently identified them as relevant to evaluate the narcissism construct. These results extend the evidence provided by previous studies and are consistent with Kernberg’s conceptualization of narcissistic pathology as spanning from the highest (neurotic) to the lowest (borderline) levels of PO [114]. Narcissistic pathology spans a spectrum of severity whose core components — grandiosity and vulnerability — are well-supported in the literature [33, 47, 117, 118, 120–123]; though the AMPD incorporates only grandiosity within the Antagonism domain [18]. The Spanish STIPO-R Narcissism scale does not differentiate between these two components, which may partly account for the non-significant discrepancies observed in expert Judges’ item classifications. Clinically, it enables the diagnosis of narcissistic personality structure within a single instrument, without additional measures.

Regarding structural validity, the CFA empirically confirmed six latent factors corresponding to the six psychopathological domains of the original English version [48, 50]: (1) Identity (items 1 to 15); (2) Object relationships (items 16 to 30); (3) Primitive defenses (items 31 to 36); (4) Advanced defenses (items 37 to 40); (5) Aggression (items 41 to 49) and (6) Moral values (items 50 to 55). All items and domains showed significant variability except for Primitive defenses, which yielded low response variability — suggesting that the items of this domain may warrant revision [91]. The results are consistent with Kernberg’s theory of PO [47, 118] and with previous studies using the IPO [46, 51, 54, 55] and the STIPO [58, 59], which confirmed the internal structure of the instrument and obtained adequate model fit indices. The six psychopathological domains confirmed by the CFA were originally conceived to measure distinct aspects of PO and are well-supported in the literature; notably, the present study is the first to provide empirical CFA-based evidence for Spanish STIPO-R domains, extending prior findings in the same direction. Clinically, these findings provide evidence of the strong ability of the Spanish STIPO-R for assessing all clinically relevant domains included in pathological PO, according to Kernberg’s model [116].

Regarding reliability, the results confirmed that the six psychopathological domains of the Spanish STIPO-R have adequate internal consistency as measured through McDonald’s Omega coefficient [103]. Its 55 items show a high degree of correlation and coherence, are homogeneous, and evaluate a common underlying theoretical construct [124], namely, Personality Organization [47, 116] and its psychopathological domains [50]. These results are in line with previous validation studies of the IPO [49, 51, 53–55] and the STIPO [56, 58, 59, 62], which used Cronbach’s alpha [125] and obtained similar results according to the criteria established by Nunnally and Bernstein [67]. The high omega coefficients for the six domains (ω = 0.83–1.00; [103]) indicate that each subscale reliably measures a coherent construct. The hierarchical CFA further suggests that the moderate to high (r = .42–0.89) covariation among these domains is largely explained by a single, higher-order factor of general severity, consistent with the theoretical severity continuum from neurotic to borderline/psychotic organization. However, this does not imply that the Spanish STIPO-R is strictly unidimensional, as the subscales retain meaningful domain-specific variance, particularly for Moral values, consistent with Kernberg’s view that domains are distinct but interdependent, capturing meaningful aspects of personality functioning [116]. Taken together, these results support interpreting the Spanish STIPO-R both in terms of a strong general severity dimension of personality pathology and in terms of domain-specific profiles that capture clinically relevant nuances of PO [66].

Concerning concurrent validity, the results confirmed the hypothesized association between the categorical measure of pathological personality dysfunction (SCID-II) [20], in line with previous studies measuring the same construct [46, 56, 62–64]. The associations between the Aggression domain and Cluster B are also consistent with previous findings. The STIPO Aggression domain has been associated with Cluster B [56, 64]. Similarly, Döering et al. [62] found the strongest associations of STIPO with antisocial PD as measured by the Assessment of DSM-IV Personality Disorders (ADP-IV) [125]. Esguevillas et al. [63] found significant associations between the STIPO and Cluster B, especially antisocial PD. As well as positive associations between the IPO Aggression factor [46] and the SCID-II antisocial PD and between the STIPO Aggression and the SCID-II antisocial PD [46]. Across all cited studies, associations were most consistently found with antisocial PD [62] or with Cluster B [63]. This result may reflect the high diagnostic specificity of the SCID-II for antisocial PD and its comparatively lower specificity for borderline and narcissistic PD [124].

The non-association between the Aggression domain and Cluster A is discrepant with Hörz-Sagstetter et al. [46] conducted with STIPO [34]. They found a positive association with paranoid PD only, attributing this to the low prevalence of the rest of Cluster A participants [46], which also occurred in the present study. It may further reflect the SCID-II differential specificity across Cluster A [124]. The structural difference is that the STIPO [34], unlike the Spanish STIPO-R, includes a Reality testing domain assessing psychotic symptoms that may be present in all PDs and are more characteristic of Cluster A [126, 127].

The non-association with Cluster C is consistent with previous studies. Hörz-Sagstetter et al. [46] reported a negative association between the IPO Aggression factor and Cluster C.

The Identity domain was significantly associated with Clusters A and B, consistently with previous STIPO studies [46], but not with Cluster C. This may reflect the lower overall severity of the outpatient sample and the fact that, according to Kernberg’s structural personality model, subjects with Cluster C correspond to less severe levels of personality pathology [47] — a distinction that the categorical SCID-II does not capture.

The Primitive defenses domain was associated with Clusters A and B, consistently with previous studies [46, 56]. Although characteristic of the severest BPO, primitive defenses such as idealization or splitting are typical BPO defense mechanisms [128]. Such defense mechanisms may also be part of other pathological PO according to Kernberg’s theory [47, 56, 116], which may account for the weaker association between Primitive defenses and Cluster A.

The Advanced defenses domain was associated with both Clusters B and C. The Cluster B association is consistent with previous studies [56] and with Kernberg and Caligor’s theoretical conceptualization that BPO patients may present a predominance (though not exclusive use) of Primitive defenses [47]. The association with Cluster C, alongside the absence of a Primitive defenses association with this Cluster, may reflect the lower severity of the outpatient sample, where defense mechanisms may range from predominantly Primitive to mixed or predominantly Advanced, in line with previous studies [56, 58, 59, 62].

The Object relationships domain was not associated with any SCID-II PD cluster, a finding with no precedent in previous studies. This may reflect the domain’s closer alignment with the dimensional AMPD approach [18]. Specifically, Criterion A [21], which assesses personal and interpersonal functional impairments on a severity continuum, including in part the object relations model [31, 129, 130]. SCID-II initially assesses maladaptive features of Criterion B [131–134] and does not explore the impact of relationships on self and interpersonal functioning. Therefore, SCID-II does not capture the Object relationships domain, which seems to be more related to Criterion A, in line with Di Pierro et al. [66] who found that STIPO Object relationships showed closer alignment with LPFS Criterion A.

The Moral values domain was associated with Clusters A and C but not with Cluster B, contrasting with Döering et al. [62]. This discrepancy may reflect the low prevalence of antisocial PD in the present sample. According to Kernberg, the moral values system is a complex structure and one of the components of PO. Its degree of pathology indicates severity and prognosis across structural levels of PO [27]. Moral values dimension is present in all structural organizations of personality and affects pathological personality to varying degrees. Dysfunction in the Moral values system is usually associated with traits present in antisocial PD according to the DSM Manual. For Kernberg, under circumstances of trauma, deprivation, neglect, abuse, and severe aggressive impulses, identity diffusion occurs [117]. This implies that there may be failures in the integration and internalization of the Moral values system, influencing the development of different levels of psychopathology and predisposing the individual to antisocial behavior [27]. Moreover, SCID-II antisocial PD items assess behavioral expressions, whereas the STIPO-R Moral values dimension assesses moral functioning, emotional and cognitive aspects of the Moral value system in addition to antisocial behavior, examining the degree and individual capacity for guilt and adherence to common values and social norms in interpersonal behavior [27, 50].

Unlike its predecessor instruments, the Spanish STIPO-R includes a Narcissism scale, which showed associations with all three SCID-II clusters. Narcissistic PD and its relationship with the IPO is mentioned in a previous study [51] in which the authors concluded that the IPO did not capture any of the narcissistic PD characteristics of the SCID-II. These results are consistent with Kernberg’s theory of pathological personality, where narcissistic pathology spans from the highest (neurotic) to the lowest (borderline) levels of organization [114] and comprises various personality structures, such that narcissistic traits can be found across all three clusters of the SCID-II [121]. Narcissistic pathology spans a spectrum of severity whose core components — grandiosity and vulnerability [33, 47, 115, 116, 118–121] — though the AMPD incorporates only grandiosity within the Antagonism domain [18]. The Spanish STIPO-R Narcissism scale does not differentiate between these two components, which may partly account for the non-significant discrepancies observed in expert Judges’ item classifications. In summary, the present study confirms the hypothesized association between the Spanish STIPO-R and the SCID-II, particularly for Cluster B. Clinically, it enables the diagnosis of narcissistic personality structure within a single instrument, without additional measures.

Concerning criterion validity, the results confirmed the ability of the Spanish STIPO-R to successfully differentiate the clinical group (outpatients with PD) from the healthy control group, as confirmed by mean score comparisons and the complementary ROC analysis. These results are consistent with previous STIPO studies [34] with mean comparisons, which provided evidence that the instrument discriminates patients with BPO from those without [57, 58] and its different degrees of severity [62] according to Criterion A of the AMPD [18]. Further empirical support was provided by Biberdzic et al. [65], who identified five levels of personality functioning — from healthy to severe — consistent with Kernberg’s theoretically described levels of PO, and established empirically derived cutoff-points for each domain.

Regarding key clinical advantages, the Spanish STIPO-R generates a comprehensive profile of patients’ personality functioning across assessed domains: those enabling a categorical diagnosis of PO (Identity and Defense Mechanisms), and those informing the clinician about the severity of personality functioning impairment (Aggression, Object relationships, and Moral values), ranging from adequate to severely impaired functioning. This approach is grounded in Kernberg’s structural theory, which constitutes the conceptual foundation of the AMPD’s LPFS [17, 18], a convergence acknowledged by the DSM-5-TR Sect. 3, which reincorporates dimensional assessment of personality functioning, including impairment in personal (identity and self-direction) and interpersonal (empathy and intimacy) domains.

Building on these strengths, the Spanish STIPO-R offers clinicians a validated, psychometrically sound semi-structured interview that reliably discriminates between normal and pathological PO within Kernberg’s structural framework and enables the diagnosis of narcissistic personality structure through its Narcissism scale, without requiring additional instruments. The instrument simultaneously assesses severity, yielding a clinical profile of personality functioning from adequate (healthy - low severity) to impaired (pathological, with progressively increasing severity). These levels of PO support clinical decision-making across three key areas. First, treatment eligibility: severity profiles help identify patients — such as those with malignant narcissistic or antisocial PO — for whom standard treatments may not be indicated. Second, treatment planning: domain-specific severity ratings allow clinicians to establish prioritized, hierarchically organized therapeutic goals targeting the most dysfunctional areas. Third, level-of-care allocation: the instrument supports matching patients to appropriate care settings, including outpatient, day hospital, subacute, or rehabilitation services.

There are some limitations that deserve comment.

Both the STIPO-R and the SCID-II were administered by the same rater, which may have inflated associations between instruments due to shared method variance and confirmation bias. Future studies should involve independent raters to allow estimation of interrater reliability and to ensure generalizability to other evaluators.

The invariance analysis could not be conducted due to limited variability among healthy controls of the sample in the domain Aggression. Although some authors consider strict invariance unnecessary for group comparisons, this limitation should be taken into account when interpreting the results.

This study employed a case-control design, comparing patients with confirmed PD diagnoses to healthy controls. This extreme-groups approach facilitates initial validation and clear differentiation but has limitations: diagnostic thresholds are somewhat arbitrary, the sample does not capture the full continuum of personality pathology and observed group differences may be inflated. Future studies should test the STIPO-R in more representative clinical samples, including subthreshold or mixed-diagnosis cases.

Conclusions

The present study empirically confirms for the first time the adequate psychometric properties of the Spanish STIPO-R: reliability (internal consistency), content, structural (obtained evidence of six clinical domains — Identity, Object relationships, Primitive defenses, Advanced defenses, Aggression and Moral values). And concurrent and criterion validity. Results are consistent with prior studies. The AMPD’s LPFS is itself conceptually rooted in Kernberg’s original PO construct. Grounded in Kernberg’s full theoretical model, the Spanish STIPO-R captures structural dimensions that are theoretically central to PO but absent from the AMPD (Defense mechanisms) or only partially operationalized within it (Aggression and Moral values), thereby complementing and extending its diagnostic approach and enriching the information available to clinicians for the assessment, diagnosis, severity determination, and treatment planning of PD. These results support the clinical use of the Spanish STIPO-R as a comprehensive, psychometrically sound tool for personality pathology assessment in both public and private mental health settings, in line with - and extending beyond - the DSM-5-TR AMPD framework.
