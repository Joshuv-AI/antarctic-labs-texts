Minigene-based splicing analysis uncovers pathogenic splice-altering effects of PAX2
Authors: Dongxuan Chi, Ke Xu, Yali Ren, Jie Ding, Fang Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMissense variants in disease-associated genes cause aberrant pre-mRNA splicing, carrying profound implications for molecular diagnostics, pathogenic mechanism elucidation, and personalized therapy development. The distinct genotype-phenotype correlation observed in PAX2-related disorders underscores the need to evaluate how identified PAX2 gene variants impact pre-mRNA splicing. This study aimed to investigate the effects of PAX2 missense variants localized to the first or last nucleotides of an exon on pre-mRNA splicing.MethodsFour PAX2 variants located at the last nucleotide of exons [NM_000278.5:c.212G > A (p.Arg71Lys), c.212G > T (p.Arg71Met), c.212G > C (p.Arg71Thr), and c.919G > A (p.Gly307Ser)] obtained from the Human Gene Mutation Database, along with one variant identified in our cohort [c.427G > C (p.Val143Leu)], were analyzed. A minigene splicing assay was performed to evaluate their functional effects on pre-mRNA splicing. Comprehensive clinical data were collected from the patient harboring the c.427G > C variant.ResultsThree bioinformatic prediction tools indicated that aforementioned five candidate variants were likely to affect RNA splicing. The in vitro minigene assay demonstrated that all these variants consistently induced the skipping of target exons. For the patient with the PAX2 c.427G > C variant, renal dysfunction was observed and postulated to be linked to renal hypoplasia; concomitantly, the patient presented with proteinuria, a complication primarily ascribed to obesity-related focal segmental glomerulosclerosis. Following three years of clinical management, the patient achieved weight normalization, accompanied by stable renal function and proteinuria.ConclusionsTranscript-level RNA analysis reclassified PAX2 missense variants located at the last nucleotide of exons as splice-altering variants, which highlights the indispensable role of transcript-level assessment in characterizing exonic point variants of disease-causing genes to advance pathogenic mechanism research and personalized therapy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12887-026-06843-1.

Background

Heterozygous pathogenic variants in the paired box gene 2 (PAX2), which is located on chromosome 10q24.31 and consists of 11 coding exons (reference sequence: NM_000278.5), are associated with PAX2-related disorder—a clinically heterogeneous disease primarily involving the kidneys. The classic phenotypes of this condition include papillorenal syndrome (or renal coloboma syndrome, OMIM 120330) and focal segmental glomerulosclerosis type 7 (OMIM 616002). The former is characterized by renal hypodysplasia and ocular abnormalities, while clinically presents with proteinuria or nephrotic syndrome. Beyond the classic phenotypes, a broad spectrum of renal, ocular, and non-renal and non-ophthalmologic abnormalities has been documented. For example, congenital anomalies of the kidney and urinary tract including bilateral kidney hypoplasia, multicystic dysplastic kidney, oligomeganephronia, vesicoureteral reflux, and ureteropelvic junction obstruction have been reported [1]. Notably, kidney abnormalities may ultimately progress to end-stage kidney disease (ESKD) [2–4]. Clinical manifestations alone are insufficient for establishing the diagnosis of PAX2-related disorder, and molecular genetic testing is therefore essential. Genotype-phenotype correlations have been well documented in PAX2-related disorder: nonsense, frameshift, splicing variants, and large deletions are associated with papillorenal syndrome, as well as poorer renal and ocular outcomes [5].

Disrupted pre-mRNA splicing is an important deleterious mechanism in hereditary diseases [6]. Previous studies, including our own and those from other research groups, have demonstrated that missense variants in disease-associated genes can induce aberrant mRNA splicing [7–11]. These findings underscore the importance of routinely integrating RNA-based splicing analysis into genetic diagnostics, the understanding of hereditary disease, as well as the potential utility of oligonucleotide-based therapies targeting splicing defects.

According to disease databases such as ClinVar (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/clinvar/) and the Human Gene Mutation Database (HGMD. http://www.hgmd.cf.ac.uk/), missense variants of the PAX2 gene detected at the genomic DNA level represent the most common type of variation. However, the impact of PAX2 missense variants located at ± 1 bp exonic positions—regions adjacent to critical splice sites—on mRNA splicing had not been studied. Therefore, this study aimed to investigate whether PAX2 missense variants occurring in the first or last nucleotides of an exon exert effects on splicing and to elucidate whether such a mechanism underlies variant-induced exon skipping in the PAX2 gene.

To address this knowledge gap, the present study systematically evaluated the splicing effects of five PAX2 missense variants located at the last nucleotide of exons. We first performed in silico splicing predictions to assess their potential impact, followed by functional validation using a minigene splicing assay. In addition, clinical data from a patient harboring one of these variants were analyzed to explore the genotype–phenotype correlation. By integrating computational prediction, experimental validation, and clinical observation, this study aimed to clarify whether such exonic substitutions should be reclassified as splice-altering variants and to further refine variant interpretation in PAX2-related disorders.

Methods

Variant nomenclature

Variant nomenclature was described according to the MANE Select transcript NM_000278.5 (ENST00000355243.8) based on the GRCh38 reference genome, following the recommendations of the Human Genome Variation Society (HGVS). Protein variants were annotated using NP_000269.3.

Candidate variant selection

The study protocol was approved by the Ethical Committee of Peking University First Hospital (approval number: 2020[72] and 2016[1179]), and informed consent was obtained from the participants or their legal guardians. Among 163 reported PAX2 missense variants documented in the HGMD (last accessed August 13, 2024), four variants located at the last nucleotide position of exons, namely c.212G > A (p.Arg71Lys; not currently registered in ClinVar), c.212G > T (p.Arg71Met; ClinVar Variation ID: 2683957), c.212G > C (p.Arg71Thr; ClinVar Variation ID: 13805) and c.919G > A (p.Gly307Ser; not currently registered in ClinVar), were included in this study, and no variants located at the first nucleotide position of exons. Additionally, a similar PAX2 missense variant c.427G > C (p.Val143Leu; novel variant identified in the present study) identified in one of patients admitted at our hospital between August 2012 and August 2024 was also included. Notably, the PAX2 missense variants c.212G > A (p.Arg71Lys), c.212G > T (p.Arg71Met) and c.212G > C (p.Arg71Thr) have been shown to be associate with Papillorenal syndrome [12]. In contrast, the PAX2 missense variant c.919G > A (p.Gly307Ser) is specifically associated with congenital optic nerve abnormality without accompany renal involvement [13].

In order to assess the splicing effects of the identified variants, the available in silico splice tools including NetGene2 (https://services.healthtech.dtu.dk/services/NetGene2-2.42/), dbNSFP Data (http://sites.google.com/site/jpopgen/dbNSFP) and SpliceAI (https://spliceailookup.broadinstitute.org/) were employed. Predicted sites with scores exceeding the respective cutoffs—0.5 for NetGene2, 0.6 for dbNSFP, and 0.2 for SpliceAI—are designated as potential splice sites, with higher scores indicating a greater likelihood of these sites being authentic splice sites.

Generation of minigene constructs

To verify the probable splicing effects caused by PAX2 five candidate variants mentioned above, a minigene splicing assay was performed in vitro, and the pMini-CopGEP vector (Beijing Hitrobio Biotechnology Co., Beijing, China) was used. Supplementary Table S1 shows five pairs of primers that were designed to amplify the targeted fragments. The wild-type and mutant minigene constructs were prepared and built.

Cell culture and transfection

For minigene assays, human embryonic kidney 293T (HEK293T) cells were purchased from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, USA) and cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and incubated at 37 ℃ and 5% CO2. One day before transient transfection, the cells were seeded in 6-well cell culture plates, reached 70–80% confluency and transfected 4 µg plasmids per well using Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Total RNA was extracted from cells cultured for 48 h with TRIzol Reagent (Invitrogen) and cDNA synthesis was performed using the FastKing gDNA dispelling RT SuperMix (TIANGEN).

Polymerase chain reaction amplification of cDNA

Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) was conducted with the primers F-GGCTAACTAGAGAACCCACTGCTTA and R-CTGTGCTTCTACAGTTGCTAAGC. The PCR parameters were optimized as follows: 94 ℃ for 90 s, 30 cycles of denaturation (94 ℃ for 20 s), annealing (55 ℃ for 20 s), and elongation (72 ℃ for 45 s), and a final elongation at 72 ℃ for 5 min. The amplification products were analyzed by 1.3% agarose gel electrophoresis and determined by Sanger sequencing.

Characterization of the phenotype of the patient who had the PAX2 Variant c.427G > C

The detailed clinical data of the patient who had the PAX2 variant c.427G > C was retrospectively retrieved from the online registry of pediatric inherited kidney diseases in China (http://pikic.bjmu.edu.cn/).

Renal histology

Renal tissue sections were fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin for 24 h, embedded in paraffin, and cut into 4 μm sections. These sections were then stained with periodic acid–Schiff (PAS) and periodic acid–silver methenamine (PASM). Representative images were acquired using a light microscope (Leica DM2500, Germany).

Clinical evaluation

Clinical data, including gender, age, renal and extrarenal manifestations, renal histopathology findings, and medications, were obtained from electronic medical records. The patient was followed up clinically approximately every 3 months.

Five PAX2 missense variants affect pre-mRNA splicing

Agarose gel electrophoresis demonstrated that the wild-type (WT) construct containing NM_000278.5:c.212 produced three distinct bands of comparable intensity (Fig. 1A). Sanger sequencing revealed that the smallest band corresponded to complete skipping of the exon harboring c.212 (Fig. 1A, band 1), whereas the largest band (approximately 500 bp) represented the full-length transcript containing exons 1–3 (Fig. 1A, band 2). The intermediate band could not be reliably characterized by sequencing.

In contrast, all three variants at position c.212 (c.212G > C, c.212G > A, and c.212G > T) produced a single predominant transcript corresponding to exon skipping (Fig. 1A and B). This aberrant splicing event introduced a frameshift and a premature termination codon (p.Pro15Serfs*11), indicating a loss-of-function effect.

Similarly, aberrant splicing was observed for the other two variants, c.427G > C and c.919G > A (Figs. 1C-F). The c.427G > C variant induced skipping of the exon harboring c.427, resulting in a frameshift (p.Arg137Serfs14). Likewise, c.919G > A caused out-of-frame exon skipping (p.Gly288Valfs62). These findings demonstrate that all five variants primarily exert their pathogenic effects through disruption of normal splicing rather than by simple amino acid substitution.

Clinical characteristics of the patient with aberrant splicing caused by a putative PAX2 missense variant

In our hospital, a total of 8 pediatric patients were identified as carrying PAX2 missense variants. Notably, a de novo missense variant c.427G > C (p.Val143Leu) affecting the final nucleotide of exon harboring NM_000278.5:c.427 was detected in only one 16-year-old female child among these cases. This female patient was admitted to our hospital complaining of renal dysfunction and proteinuria. She is the only child of non-consanguineous, healthy Chinese parents. She has little inclination for physical activity in her daily life. Since the age of 14, she has gained considerable weight. Three months before admission, she visited the local hospital for obesity. Laboratory results showed serum creatinine of 304~334 µmol/L, serum uric acid of 590 ~ 596 µmol/L, serum urea of 11.35 ~ 12.5 mmol/L, hyperlipidemia (cholesterol 6.04 mmol/L, triglycerides 2.07 mmol/L), proteinuria (1.24 g/day), urine specific gravity of 1.009, hemoglobin of 108 g/L, serum parathyroid hormone of 93.8pg/mL. Serum tests for multiple autoantibodies and markers of hepatitis B virus were negative. Serum complement C3 and C4 and immunoglobulin IgA, IgM and IgG levels were normal. Renal ultrasonography showed the left kidney to be 9.0 × 4.7 cm and the right kidney to be 9.0 × 4.3 cm, with bilateral poor corticomedullary differentiation. The kidney biopsy (Fig. 2) showed 12 glomeruli by light microscopy: one glomerulus presented global sclerosis, one exhibited ischemic sclerosis, and the remaining 10 glomeruli—relatively sparse in distribution—demonstrated hypertrophic glomerular capillary loops, along with 3 segmental glomerulosclerotic lesions located at the vascular poles. Some renal tubules were hypertrophic with dilated lumens, and a small amount of protein casts were seen in the lumens. Focal infiltration of lymphocytes, monocytes and a small number of foam cells accompanied by fibrosis were observed in the renal interstitium, and the small arteries were thickened. The final pathological diagnosis was glomerular hypertrophy with focal segmental glomerulosclerosis (FSGS)-like lesions, with clinical consideration of obesity-related FSGS. Routine ophthalmological examination revealed binocular large optic cups, refractive errors and unilateral amblyopia. She was treated with losartan potassium 50 mg/day and febuxostat 20 mg/day. On admission, her height and weight were 162 cm and 76 kg, respectively. Her body mass index (BMI) was 29 kg/m² (> 97th centile), and her blood pressure was 123/81mmHg. Laboratory test results were as follows: hemoglobin, 106 g/L; serum creatinine, 300.3 µmol/L; uric acid, 601 µmol/L; urea, 13.74 mmol/L; hyperlipidemia (low-density lipoprotein 3.79 mmol/L, triglycerides 2 mmol/L); proteinuria, 0.87 g/day; urinary protein electrophoresis showed low-molecular-weight proteins 4.5%, albumin 83.1%, and macromolecular proteins 12.4%. Whole-exome sequencing of parent-child trios followed by Sanger sequencing revealed PAX2 exon harboring NM_000278.5:c.427 de novo heterozygous variant c.427G > C (p.Val143Leu) in the patient. Neither the literature nor public databases, including HGMD and ClinVar, document this variant. The minigene- based approach showed c.427G > C abolishes exon harboring NM_000278.5:c.427 (Fig. 1D). According to the guideline of the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics [12], this variant was classified as a pathogenic variant (PVS1 + PS2+PM2), confirming the diagnosis of PAX2 related disorders and explaining the cause of renal hypoplasia.

Discussion

This study emphasizes the significance of transcript analysis in the functional evaluation of exonic sequence variants in the causative gene. Specifically, our minigene results revealed that 5 single-base substitutions at the last nucleotide position on the exons of PAX2 caused aberrant splicing, which reclassifies these substitutions as splicing variants and provides further validation of the hypothesis that some missense variants can influence splicing [8, 14]. In addition, mRNA-level analysis should be incorporated as a routine analytical step for the detection of splicing defects, thus facilitating the investigation of exon skipping therapy using antisense oligonucleotides.

In this study, we demonstrated that the variants c.212G > C, c.212G > A, and c.212G > T induced the same alternative splicing event. A similar phenomenon has been previously reported for the c.913G > A and c.913G > T variants of the TSC1 gene [15]. Collectively, these observations highlight the fact that the precision of pre-mRNA splicing regulation is highly dependent on the intrinsic sequence features of the target gene. Given that our functional conclusions rely on a minigene-based assay, several methodological considerations should be discussed.

An additional point worth noting is the detection of an out-of-frame transcript resulting from skipping of the exon harboring NM_000278.5:c.212 from the wild-type construct. Although this finding may appear unexpected, it is biologically plausible and can be explained by the intrinsic dynamics of splice-site recognition. Exon definition is governed by a delicate balance between splice-site strength and auxiliary cis-regulatory elements. When only limited intronic regions are included in a minigene construct, distal enhancers or silencers that stabilize exon inclusion in vivo may be absent, thereby increasing susceptibility to low-frequency exon skipping. Furthermore, heterologous cell systems may differ in their repertoire or abundance of splicing factors compared with the native tissue, potentially unmasking minor splice isoforms that would otherwise be tightly regulated or rapidly degraded in vivo.

Notably, skipping of the exon harboring NM_000278.5:c.212 introduces a frameshift and is predicted to generate a premature termination codon, which would likely trigger nonsense-mediated mRNA decay (NMD) under physiological conditions. Therefore, the presence of this aberrant product in the minigene assay may reflect the lack of endogenous RNA surveillance mechanisms acting on the reporter transcript. Importantly, the pathogenic relevance of the variant is supported not by the mere existence of exon skipping in the wild-type background, but by the substantial shift toward the aberrant isoform observed in the mutant construct. Such disruption of the normal splicing equilibrium is a recognized mechanism underlying splice-altering pathogenic variants.

Taken together, while the minigene assay does not fully recapitulate the endogenous genomic and cellular context, it provides a controlled system to directly compare wild-type and mutant alleles. The consistent and marked alteration in splicing pattern observed here strongly supports a functional impact of the variant.

Results of the recent study by Kim et al. indicate that the genotype-phenotype correlations in PAX2-related disorder can help to improve management of the kidney and ocular involvements [5]. Our patient advanced the understanding of clinical phenotypes. The severity of renal dysfunction at disease onset was not explicable by the degree of proteinuria, furthermore, renal ultrasound demonstrated that kidney length was below both the 2.5th percentile for age and gender (93.6 cm) and the 2.5th percentile for body length/height (89.5 cm) [16], while kidney biopsy showed obesity-related FSGS. Consequently, renal dysfunction is postulated to be associated with renal hypoplasia-a phenotype of PAX2-related disease, and proteinuria is primarily attributed to obesity-related FSGS rather than PAX2-related FSGS. Following 3 years of clinical management, the patient achieved weight normalization, with stable renal function and proteinuria to date. These results underscore the importance of weight reduction and proteinuria control in delaying disease progression.

This study has several limitations. First, the number of PAX2 variants analyzed was relatively small. Second, clinical data were limited, precluding a robust genotype–phenotype correlation analysis.

Our results also highlight the practical relevance of in silico splicing prediction tools. Variants located at the last nucleotide of an exon are frequently annotated as missense changes; however, our data demonstrate that such substitutions may primarily exert their pathogenic effect through splicing disruption. While prediction algorithms provide valuable guidance and showed concordance with our experimental findings, functional assays remain crucial to confirm their biological impact. Therefore, splicing predictions should be considered a reliable screening tool, but not a substitute for transcript-level validation.

Conclusions

We have performed a comprehensive RNA analysis of PAX2 variants located at the exonic last nucleotide position using the minigene assay. For the first time, we demonstrated that the presumed missense variants c.212G > C, c.212G > A, c.212G > T, c.427G > C and c.919G > A affected pre-mRNA splicing, which extends the mutational spectrum of PAX2 gene and highlights the importance of transcript analysis of the exonic point variants of the causative gene to understand the disease-causing nature and to develop genotype-specific therapeutic strategies.
