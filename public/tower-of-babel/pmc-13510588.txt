A pro-natural product design strategy affords ester-based green fungicide candidates
Authors: Peng Lei, Hanxiao Yuan, Xingxing Lu, Tiantian Sun, Yanyan Qu, Le Wang, Tan Dai, Jianqiang Miao, Yanqing Gao, Xili Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Natural products (NPs) serve as both inspirations and resources for the discovery of bioactive compounds. However, their application in green agrochemical discovery faces limitations such as low activity, short duration of efficacy, potential toxicity and residue issues from structural modifications, and constraints in exploring chemical and biological space. Here, we propose the concept and design principles of pro-natural products (PrNPs), which effectively address these limitations and open a broad avenue for bioactive compound discovery. PrNPs are functional compounds designed to degrade into NPs under application conditions. Using this strategy, we designed and synthesized a structurally diverse ester-based PrNP collection with 170 members by combining natural acids and alcohols and found a promising green fungicide candidate, PrNP 6E, with excellent control efficacy against major plant diseases such as rapeseed Sclerotinia stem rot, rice sheath blight, and apple Valsa canker. Cheminformatic analysis, antifungal activity, degradation behavior, and safety assessment reveal that 6E combines the advantageous properties of both NPs and agrochemicals and exhibits suitable duration of efficacy, controllable degradation into NPs after fulfilling its function, broad safety, and environmental compatibility. The PrNP strategy shows great potential in enhancing the efficiency of finding green agrochemicals with novel chemotypes and mechanisms of action and in promoting sustainable development of agriculture.

INTRODUCTION

Food security, as the core of the global strategy, directly sustains the foundation of human survival and the quality of development (1, 2). Agrochemicals, the iconic invention of the agricultural revolution in the 20th century, are indispensable in agricultural production. By managing harmful biotic stresses from pests, pathogens, and weeds, they underpin stable and high crop yields (3–5). The global pesticide market approaches an annual value of $80 billion, with usage exceeding 3.5 million tons (6). Developed countries have a high demand for pesticides. For example, the United States consumes more than 400,000 tons of pesticides annually (7). However, traditional pesticides have issues such as target resistance, ecological risks, and residues, which have become the primary bottlenecks restricting the sustainable development of agriculture (Fig. 1A) (8–10).

Natural products (NPs) play a dual role as inspiration and resources in agrochemical discovery, representing a key area for sustainable agricultural development, with substantial market demand (11, 12). NPs are the outcome of millions of years of evolution and natural selection in nature. They have characteristics such as novel structures, diverse bioactivities, and unique action mechanisms (13, 14). Moreover, NPs are typically biodegradable, offering high safety and favorable environmental compatibility (15, 16). These attributes position NPs as a central resource for developing green agrochemicals that address the main challenges of modern agrochemicals and align with the needs of sustainable agriculture (11, 12). There are generally two development models for NPs in the field of agrochemicals: (i) direct utilization of active substances extracted from plants or microbial fermentation, which often suffers from high cost, low activity, and short duration of efficacy, thus limiting widespread use (17, 18); and (ii) structural modification of NPs via chemical synthesis, which, while simplifying structures and enhancing activity to increase practical value, focuses on characteristic scaffolds and thereby limits the exploration of chemical and biological space (Fig. 1B) (19–21). Furthermore, with a vast number of NPs remaining undiscovered and known NPs representing only a fraction of nature’s total chemical space, along with numerous unelucidated biosynthetic pathways, the current strategies have far from fully exploited the potential of NPs in inspiring chemical frameworks (19, 22). Waldmann et al. innovatively proposed the pseudo-NP strategy through the recombination of NP fragments, effectively addressing the limitations of natural-source active compound discovery in exploring chemical and biological spaces and leading the fields of medicine and agrochemical into an era of transformation (19, 23, 24). However, for agrochemicals, changes in the structure of NPs may lead to toxicity to nontarget organisms (e.g., bees) and residues in food or the humans, which is not conducive to the concept of green and sustainable development in agriculture (25).

Here, we innovatively propose the concept of pro-natural products (PrNPs). PrNPs are functional compounds designed to decompose into NPs under their application conditions. This strategy can break through the abovementioned bottlenecks, further broaden the practical utility of NPs in the discovery of bioactive compounds, and open up a broad avenue for green plant protection and agrochemical discovery (Fig. 1C). We verified the feasibility of the PrNP strategy in creating new green agrochemicals (Fig. 2). Nature abounds with natural alcohols and acids, yet most of the ester compounds formed by their combination are not found in NPs, indicating that they have not been biologically explored. On the basis of this, we found ester-based PrNP fungicide candidates with good antifungal activity. Cheminformatic analysis and safety evaluation indicate that the potential PrNP fungicides combine the advantageous characteristics of both NPs and agrochemicals and are low-toxic to nontarget organisms. In addition, PrNPs degrade into the original NPs after exerting their pesticidal effects. The PrNP strategy preserves the green attributes of NP-based agrochemicals while overcoming the inherent defects of traditional development models, opening up a direction for the discovery of green agrochemical molecules, providing a technical pathway for sustainable agriculture, and having broad application prospects and high market demand.

RESULTS

Design and synthesis of the ester-based PrNP collection

PrNPs are the recombination of NPs, broadening the chemical space derived from NPs. Therefore, the selection of NPs for combination is crucial. Here, we propose the design principles of PrNPs as follows: select naturally abundant or chemically synthesizable, readily available NPs; preferentially combine NPs from different organisms or biosynthetic pathways; NPs should be easily combined through chemical synthesis and can decompose into the original NPs under application conditions. PrNP agrochemical molecules should conform to the pesticide-likeness rules. Referring to the empirical “rule of three” core indicators with appropriate modifications (26, 27), the selected NPs should have the following physicochemical properties: molecular weight (MW) ≤300, CLogP ≤4.0, number of hydrogen-bond acceptors (HBA) ≤4, and number of hydrogen-bond donors (HBD) ≤2.

The selected NPs are without any structural modification, and PrNPs should be able to degrade into the original NPs after exerting their effects. This concept can be further extended to that PrNPs are composed of combined NPs, but the degraded compounds are required to be naturally occurring NPs, not necessarily the original NPs; or they can be not composed of NPs, as long as the lastly degraded compounds are naturally occurring NPs. Therefore, the core strategy of PrNP design is to be able to degrade into naturally occurring NPs. PrNPs not only have the high efficiency of synthesized active compounds but also degrade into NPs after exerting their effects, enabling better utilization of nature’s capacity to handle naturally occurring substances. This is particularly important in agricultural production and is more in line with the requirements of sustainable development.

Natural alcohols and acids widely exist in nature, and they combine with each other to form ester-based PrNPs (28, 29). Ester compounds can be hydrolyzed into alcohols and acids (30). This property enables ester-based PrNPs to degrade back into their original NPs in the application environment after controlling the damage of pathogens to crops. Notably, the ester structure is prevalent among existing agrochemicals (31). For instance, azoxystrobin, deltamethrin, and pyriftalid all have ester moieties. The carbonyl group within the ester structure has the potential to form hydrogen bonds with the target protein, thereby enhancing the interaction with the target. This is likely conducive to improving the pesticidal activity (32, 33). Consequently, in this study, we constructed an ester-based PrNP library by combining natural acids and alcohols. According to the design principles of PrNP agrochemicals, to further enrich the structure diversity, we selected natural alcohol compounds such as guaiacol, methyl salicylate, eugenol, vanillylacetone, hinokitiol, geraniol, linalool, sesamol, umbelliferone, pterostilbene, 2-hydroxypyridine, phenethyl alcohol, menthol, perillyl alcohol, carvacrol, and benzyl salicylate; and natural acid compounds such as cinnamic acid, isovaleric acid, veratric acid, nicotinic acid, cuminic acid, sorbic acid, piperonylic acid, coumaric acid, lauric acid, 3-pyridylacetic acid, pyroglutamic acid, and auxin. The mutual combination and synthesis of these natural acids and alcohols yielded ester-based PrNP collection. Given the specific requirements of agrochemicals, which necessitate easy large-scale preparation (23), we excluded a small number of compounds that were either difficult to synthesize or had low yields. Ultimately, 170 target compounds were synthesized (figs. S1 to S343). Without any structural modification, it ensures that the final degradation products are the original NPs (Fig. 3).

Cheminformatic analysis

PrNPs have the inherent advantages of NPs, can broaden the chemical space of natural-source compounds, and have biological relevance, potentially generating new bioactivities. Accordingly, we compared the structures, NP-likeness, and pesticide-likeness of the designed PrNPs. The diversity of the structural shapes of PrNPs was assessed using principal moments of inertia (PMI) (34). As shown in Fig. 4A, the ester-based PrNPs were not crowded on the rod-disk axis distribution and were somewhat dispersed toward the disk-like and sphere-like shapes, broadening the chemical space of the original NPs. The structural characteristics of NPs were compared using the NP-likeness score (35). The value of the NP-likeness score ranges from −5.0 to +5.0, and a larger value indicates that the compound is more in line with the characteristics of NPs. The NP-likeness scores of the designed ester-based PrNPs ranged from −0.9 to +2.2, distributed in a relatively narrow region of the original NPs’ range, having the basic characteristics of NPs (Fig. 4B). Pesticide-likeness describes the physicochemical properties that a pesticide should have, such as MW, CLogP, HBA, HBD, and rotatable bonds (ROB) (36). Different from pharmaceuticals, pesticides need to be used outdoors, so photostability is one of the important factors to be considered. Therefore, there is a requirement for the number of aromatic bonds (ARB) in the pesticide-likeness rules (37). Most of the ester-based PrNPs have MW ≤435, CLogP ≤6, HBA ≤6, HBD ≤2, ROB ≤9, and ARB ≤17 (Fig. 4, C and D, and table S1), conforming to the pesticide-likeness rules and indicating their potential as agrochemical candidates (37). The above results indicated that PrNPs combined the advantageous properties of NPs and agrochemicals.

Biological activity

The PrNP strategy is applicable to the discovery of all active compounds and may generate new bioactivities. Ideally, bioactivity screening should be as comprehensive as possible; for example, unbiased cell-based assays are used in drug discovery (38). To our knowledge, no such unbiased method exists for agrochemical activity screening. To find new agricultural fungicides, we selected 10 representative plant pathogens for biological activity tests according to the characteristics of agricultural diseases—major cereal pathogens: Rhizoctonia solani, Fusarium graminearum, Gaeumannomyces graminis, and Curvularia lunata; major oilseed pathogen: Sclerotinia sclerotiorum; major fruit and vegetable pathogens: Valsa mali, Botryosphaeria dothidea, Alternaria solani, Fusarium oxysporum, and Colletotrichum orbiculare.

The bioactivity results of ester-based PrNPs and their corresponding original NPs were shown in Fig. 5 (A and B) and tables S2 and S3, respectively, indicating distinct changes in activity upon NP combination. While most original NPs showed some antifungal activity, the obtained ester-based PrNPs generally exhibited notable improved activity, showing different antifungal activities and spectra. NPs E, J, and O had good antifungal activity. Except that 2E had similar activity to its original NP E at the primary screening concentration, the other PrNPs combined with E, J, or O all exhibited different types of antifungal activities. These results indicated that PrNPs can yield bioactivities distinct from the original NPs, which may be due to changes in chemical structures leading to different action mechanisms. Therefore, the PrNP strategy can find active compounds with novel structures and potentially novel mechanisms.

For the PrNPs and the corresponding original NPs with an inhibition rate of ≥90% at a concentration of 50 μg/ml, their median effective concentration (EC50) values were tested (tables S4 and S5). The EC50 values of the vast majority of compounds were in the range of 1 to 10 μg/ml, showing good potency. Notably, some PrNPs with similar activities to the original NPs at 50 μg/ml showed a slower decline in activity at lower concentrations, outperforming the corresponding original NPs. For example, NP E had an inhibition rate of >90% against S. sclerotiorum at 50 μg/ml with an EC50 of 7.22 μg/ml. In contrast, four E-derived PrNPs (1E, 2E, 5E, and 6E) with >90% inhibition rates had EC50 values of 0.54, 3.80, 0.63, and 1.07 μg/ml, respectively, which were lower than E’s, indicating substantial activity enhancement. A similar pattern was observed for V. mali.

On the basis of the in vitro antifungal activity, we selected the representative important pathogens S. sclerotiorum, Rhophitulus solani, and V. mali for in vivo activity tests and chose carbendazim, a widely used commercial fungicide registered for the control of related plant diseases, as the positive control. Rapeseed is a globally important oil crop, and Sclerotinia stem rot is its most devastating disease, severely threatening the yield and quality of rapeseed worldwide (39). Similar to the in vitro results, on rapeseed plants, the PrNPs 1E, 5E, and 6E all showed excellent control efficacy. The control effects at 200, 100, and 50 μg/ml were greater than 92, 85, and 72%, respectively, outperforming original NP E. At low concentrations, they performed comparably or slightly better than carbendazim, and at high concentrations, they had significantly better effects than carbendazim (Fig. 5C and table S6). Rice is one of the three major food crops in the world. Sheath blight is a global fungal disease that causes rice yield reduction and seriously threatens food production and global food security (40). Although NP E showed excellent in vitro activity against R. solani with an EC50 value of 1.54 μg/ml, the designed PrNPs 5E and 6E showed better control effects on rice leaves. In particular, 6E had a protective and curative effect of >90% at 200 μg/ml and, at 50 μg/ml, matched the efficacy of E at 200 μg/ml. In addition, 6E also had a slightly better effect than carbendazim (Fig. 5D and table S7). Apple is an economically important fruit widely cultivated globally, and Valsa canker is the major disease restricting global apple production, causing huge economic losses to fruit farmers in various countries (41). Similar to rapeseed Sclerotinia stem rot, 1E, 2E, and 6E had better activities against V. mali both in vitro and in vivo than NP E, and they showed either better or similar effects when compared with carbendazim (Fig. 5E and table S8).

Compound 6E demonstrated the best overall performance, with around 90% protective and curative efficacy against the three diseases at 200 μg/ml and >70% even at a lower concentration of 50 μg/ml, outperforming both original NPs and the control agent carbendazim. Moreover, 6E has the characteristics of previously unidentified structure, easy synthesis, and low cost, with great development potential and application prospects.

Degradation behavior

PrNP active compounds are required to have a suitable duration of efficacy and be able to degrade into naturally occurring NPs. The potential agricultural fungicide PrNP 6E was selected to study its degradation in water and soil.

Hydrolysis was studied in three pH buffers and Wei River water at 25° and 50°C. In the pH 4, pH 7 buffer solutions and river water, the residue amount of 6E over time followed the first-order kinetic equation (Fig. 6, A and B, and tables S9 and S10). At 25°C, the half-lives of 6E in pH 4, pH 7 buffer solutions, and river water were 44.72, 433.22, and 247.55 hours, respectively (Fig. 6A). However, when 6E was incubated in the pH 9 buffer solution for 4 hours, 98% of it had been hydrolyzed, indicating that 6E was more readily degraded under alkaline conditions. Under the same pH value, an increase in temperature accelerated the degradation of 6E. When the pH was 7 or 4, the hydrolysis rate constant at 50°C was 10.25 and 3.45 times greater than at 25°C, respectively (Fig. 6B). The Wei River is the largest tributary of the Yellow River in China. Compared with the pH 7 buffer solution, the half-life of 6E in Wei River water was substantially shortened, suggesting that microbial degradation played an important role in the hydrolysis process of 6E in the actual use environment. In summary, the hydrolysis rate of 6E in different pH solutions and natural Wei River water followed the order of alkaline > acidic > river water > neutral. Under neutral conditions (25°C), the half-life of 6E was 18 days, while under all other conditions, it was less than 11 days, which was shorter than the half-life requirement for easily degradable pesticides (<30 days) (42).

The degradation characteristics of pesticides in soil are the crucial indicators for evaluating their environmental fate and ecological risks, directly determining the level of pesticide residues, soil environmental carrying capacity, and the quality and safety of agricultural products (43). Three typical representative soils (black soil, fluvo-aquic soil, and red soil) were selected to study the degradation of PrNP 6E. Degradation in all soils followed first-order kinetic model, with half-lives of 20.39 to 30.81 hours (Fig. 6C and tables S11 and S12). The results indicated that 6E was easily degradable in soils from different regions. Among them, the degradation rate in red soils with low organic matter content was faster than that in the other two soils, which might be due to the strong adsorption of pesticides by high-clay or high–organic-matter soils, and the soil adsorption might affect the biodegradation of pesticides (44).

To verify that PrNP 6E would degrade into its original NPs after exerting its pesticidal effect in the application environment, we analyzed the hydrolysis samples of Wei River water using a triple-quadrupole liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS). The results were shown in Fig. 6D, and the chromatographic peaks of the PrNP 6E and the original NPs 6 and E were detected in the hydrolysis samples. This proved that the PrNP could decompose into the original NPs after use. Overall, the potential agricultural fungicide PrNP 6E exhibits a suitable duration of efficacy and could degrade into the original NPs after exerting its effect, achieving a closed-loop cycle from nature back to nature.

Safety assessment

Mammals, soil organisms, and pollinating insects are key indicator organisms for the ecological risk assessment of pesticides, and their toxicity data serve as important bases for the safe application of pesticides (43, 45, 46). The toxicity of PrNP 6E to mice, earthworms, and bees was determined to clarify its environmental safety, providing essential support for the subsequent environmental risk management and scientific application of this active molecule.

As a model organism for mammals, the toxicity test on mice can reflect the potential harm of pesticides to higher animals and provide an essential reference for human health risk early warning (45). In the 14-day acute oral toxicity study, the administration of 6E at a dose of 1000 mg/kg did not cause any notable toxic signs or mortality, indicating that 6E is of low toxicity to mammals (table S14) (47).

The toxic response of earthworms can indicate the degree of pesticide interference in the soil environment, which is related to soil fertility and ecological sustainability (43). The acute toxicity of 6E to earthworms was determined using the artificial soil method. The limit test results showed that when the concentration of 6E was 100 mg/kg, no earthworm mortality was observed at 7 or 14 days. According to the toxicity classification standard of pesticides to earthworms, 6E can be judged to be of low toxicity to earthworms (table S15) (48).

Honeybees are key pollinators in the agricultural ecosystem. Their survival status is closely related to crop yields and biodiversity, and the toxicity of pesticides to honeybees directly affects ecological balance (46). The acute toxicity test on Apis mellifera ligustica workers revealed that the median lethal dose (LD50) of 6E over 48 hours exceeded 200 μg of active ingredient (a.i.) per bee, which was significantly higher than the threshold for low-toxic pesticides (LD50 > 11 μg of a.i. per bee) (table S16) (49). When newly emerged honeybee adults were exposed to 6E at concentrations of 50 and 200 mg/liter, this did not reduce their life span (χ2 = 4.267, P = 0.118; Fig. 6E and table S17). In addition, it had no significant effect on the homing rate within 2 hours of honeybee foragers (F = 0.129, P = 0.882; Fig. 6F and table S18). These findings suggested that 6E displayed acute low toxicity to honeybees, and no detectable chronic effects were observed. Therefore, it is improbable that 6E would substantially disrupt the key neurophysiological or developmental processes essential for honeybee orientation, flight, and colony maintenance (50).

DISCUSSION

Agrochemical use is an inevitable choice for the development of modern agriculture and is crucial for ensuring food production and food security. However, current issues such as environmental pollution, ecological damage, and severe resistance, as well as the risks to human and animal health from agrochemical residues, have become the primary bottlenecks restricting the sustainable development of agriculture. There is an urgent need to develop novel and safe green agrochemical molecules to promote the sustainable development of agriculture. This can not only ensure contemporary food production but also not affect the well-being of future generations.

Natural active substances are usually easily degradable and have high safety and good environmental compatibility, meeting the requirements of green agrochemicals. However, in the discovery of new natural-source agrochemicals, their inherent limitations have also emerged. For example, the direct use of NPs has disadvantages such as low activity and short duration of efficacy, and structural modification may bring toxicity to nontarget organisms and residue issues. Both approaches focus on the characteristic scaffolds of NPs, which also limits the exploration of chemical and biological spaces. How can we fully explore the inspiration and resources provided by NPs? It is both necessary and urgent to innovate new strategies for the discovery of active compounds based on NPs.

We innovatively proposed the concept of PrNPs and their design principles. PrNPs are functional compounds that can degrade into naturally occurring NPs under application conditions. The unique features of this strategy compared with previous methods lies in that the resulting degradation products must be naturally occurring NPs without any chemical modification, thereby retaining the advantages of the original NPs while avoiding toxicity and residue risks associated with structural modification. Because PrNPs come from the recombination of NPs with different structures, they can break through the structural limitations of single NPs and effectively broaden the chemical space of NPs. Furthermore, due to inheriting the characteristic elements of the structures and properties of NPs, they have biological relevance but may bring unexpected new bioactivities. In general, PrNPs have the advantages of high activity and suitable duration of efficacy, resulting from NP structure modification, and also expand the chemical space of NPs. Because of their controllable degradation characteristics in the use environment, they can achieve a closed-loop cycle from nature back to nature and also have the advantage of good environmental compatibility of direct use of NPs. The proposal of this strategy is not aimed at replacing traditional design methods of NP-based agrochemicals. It serves as an effective complement to the existing system. In the actual R&D process, appropriate strategies should be flexibly selected, or multiple methods should be jointly applied according to specific application scenarios and target requirements, so as to maximize the innovative potential and application value of NPs in the development of green agrochemicals.

We systematically applied the PrNP strategy to find novel green agricultural fungicide molecules. By combining different natural acids and alcohols, we designed and synthesized an ester-based PrNP collection with 170 members and analyzed their cheminformatics, antifungal activity, degradation behavior, and safety. The ester-based PrNP collection combines the favorable properties of NPs and agrochemicals and can exhibit different chemical types and biological activities from the original NPs. Among them, the PrNP 6E showed better control effects against rapeseed Sclerotinia stem rot, rice sheath blight, and apple Valsa canker than the widely used fungicide carbendazim. After exerting its pesticidal effect, 6E can be degraded controllably in the use environment and only degrade into naturally occurring NPs, forming a closed and waste-free cycle, reducing the risks of persistent pollution and bioaccumulation. In addition, 6E itself is of low toxicity to mammals, soil organisms, and pollinating insects, demonstrating its high safety and low ecological and environmental risks in practical applications.

The PrNP 6E has the characteristics of a novel structure, readily available raw materials, simple preparation procedures, and low costs, making it suitable for large-scale production. It combines the favorable properties of NPs and agrochemicals, having high activity against multiple major diseases and a suitable duration of efficacy while also being able to degrade into naturally occurring NPs, demonstrating excellent safety and environmental compatibility. These characteristics endow 6E with broad application prospects, significant development value, and strong market demand.

In summary, the PrNP strategy fully leverages the inspirational advantage of NPs, providing an idea for bioactive compound discovery. The successful application of PrNPs in the discovery of green agrochemical proves that it is a general and efficient tool for the discovery of active compounds, opening up a broad avenue for the R&D of new natural-source green agrochemicals. The characteristic that PrNP fungicides degrade into NPs after exerting their agrochemical effects endows them with high safety and good environmental compatibility, promoting the development of sustainable agriculture and ensuring the well-being of both current and future generations.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Synthetic methods

The ester-based PrNPs are generated by combination of the corresponding natural acids and alcohols. The specific synthesis methods of target compounds are provided in the Supplementary Materials.

Cheminformatic analysis

The cheminformatic analysis was performed using Python and specific toolkits for computation and data processing. Comprehensive details pertaining to cheminformatic analysis are provided in the Supplementary Materials.

Bioactivity assays

In vitro antifungal activity

The in vitro antifungal activities of all designed PrNPs against 10 pathogenic fungi, including R. solani, F. graminearum, G. graminis, C. lunata, S. sclerotiorum, V. mali, B. dothidea, A. solani, F. oxysporum, and C. orbiculare, were evaluated by the mycelial growth rate method. Dimethyl sulfoxide was used as the solvent control. Compounds with an inhibition rate of ≥90% at 50 μg/ml were selected to test and calculate their EC50 values (14).

In vivo antifungal activity

The protective and curative effects of PrNPs 1E, 5E, and 6E against rapeseed Sclerotinia stem rot, 1E, 2E, and 6E against apple Valsa canker and 5E and 6E against rice sheath blight were evaluated using the reported methods (14).

Degradation experiments

Hydrolysis

Hydrolysis was conducted in sterile buffers (pH 4, 7, and 9) and Wei River water (Yangling, Shaanxi). The containers used were sterilized. An aqueous solution with a concentration of 30 mg/liter was prepared, and three replicates were set for each group. The samples were wrapped with tin foil and incubated in the dark at 25° or 50°C. Samples were taken regularly, and after extraction and purification, 6E was quantitatively analyzed using high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) (51).

Soil degradation

Aqueous 6E was added to black soils, fluvo-aquic soils, and red soils and vortex mixed to an initial concentration of 30 mg/kg. Ultrapure water was added to achieve saturated conditions (∼30% moisture), and the mixtures were incubated in the dark. Each treatment was replicated three times. Water loss was periodically replenished. Samples were taken regularly. After extraction and purification, the content of 6E was determined using HPLC (51).

Toxicity evaluations

The acute oral toxicity in mice (45), acute toxicity of earthworms (48), acute and chronic toxicity of honeybees in laboratory, and the assessment of their homing ability in the field were tested following publicly available or reported methods (52, 53). The specific methods for toxicity evaluations are provided in the Supplementary Materials.
