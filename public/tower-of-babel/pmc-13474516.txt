Climate change and health: gender and social inequalities in contexts of urban vulnerability among women
Authors: Maria Fernanda Terra, Sheila Aparecida Ferreira Lachtim, Fernanda Tavares de Mello Abdalla, Luciana Rodrigues Fagnoni Costa Travassos, Débora Camara Ribeiro, Larissa Oliveira Moraes Fernandes, Nathalia Caroline Hortolan Pereira, Lucas Santos Silva, Heitor Marinho da Silva Araújo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundClimate change represents a global challenge with unequal impacts on vulnerable populations, particularly women living in vulnerable urban contexts. This study examines, from the perspective of women residing in an urban occupation within the metropolitan region of São Paulo, how climate change exacerbates social and gender inequalities, affecting living conditions and health.MethodsThis qualitative study was guided by Emancipatory Action Research and conducted in the Lélia Gonzalez occupation, linked to the Homeless Workers’ Movement (MTST), in Santo André (São Paulo, Brazil), between March and August 2025. Thirteen women participated, including both cisgender and transgender individuals. Data were generated through six emancipatory workshops and a complementary questionnaire, supported by audio recordings, full transcription, and field notes. Data were analyzed using open coding and the development of emergent categories, interpreted through the Social Determination of Health framework and the 4 S principles: sustainability, security, solidarity, and sovereignty.ResultsParticipants associated climate change with everyday experiences such as extreme heat, flooding, respiratory diseases, food insecurity, and precarious housing conditions. These phenomena were perceived as intensifying gender inequalities, increasing work and care burdens, exacerbating situations of violence, and reinforcing processes of social exclusion.ConclusionsClimate change deepens social and gender inequalities, directly affecting the health and daily lives of women in vulnerable urban settings. Emancipatory Action Research was a useful approach for articulating experiences, fostering critical reflection, and supporting collective action. Introduction

The climate crisis and extreme events represent one of the greatest global challenges of the present time, with increasingly evident impacts on health, as well as on social, environmental, and economic systems. Environmental degradation, driven by processes such as deforestation, loss of agricultural land, and scarcity of natural resources, disproportionately affects low- and middle-income countries [1]. This situation restricts access to water, food, and essential resources for livelihoods and economic activities, in addition to limiting women’s political participation. According to UN Women [2], a significant proportion of the global female population lives in extreme poverty, which intensifies the effects of climate shocks [3]. The most socioeconomically vulnerable populations have fewer resources to cope with these adversities and, consequently, tend to lose a greater share of their assets during such events.

Structural vulnerabilities inherent to the Global South, compounded by historical inequalities, create significant challenges for adaptation to climate change. Regions such as Latin America, Africa, and South Asia are characterized by higher levels of poverty, dependence on primary economic activities (such as agriculture and extractivism), high levels of informality, and fragile social protection systems. These determinants compromise the resilience of populations, both in rural and urban areas, in the face of environmental collapse [4]. Therefore, climate impacts should not be viewed in isolation, but rather as part of a historical process of exploitation that has resulted in fragile and dependent economies. This colonial legacy not only perpetuates existing inequalities but also limits the response capacity of countries in the Global South [5].

In the Latin American context, these dynamics take on specific characteristics, marked by historical processes of unequal urbanization and social exclusion. Climate change produces particularly severe effects in large cities such as São Paulo, due to the ways in which natural resources are appropriated, environmental degradation associated with urban expansion, and delays in implementing infrastructure compatible with the pace of growth of urban areas not designed to face such transformations [6]. In Brazil, 85% of the population lives in urban areas, with only 5% of cities concentrating 50% of the population [7]. Between 2017 and 2021, 2,916 municipalities experienced droughts, 1,956 suffered flooding, 1,972 were impacted by flash floods, and 935 experienced landslides [7]. These events impact living conditions, health, and well-being, but their effects are not distributed homogeneously across the population.

The magnitude of these inequalities is substantial: Brazil ranks as the 14th most unequal country in the world, where the richest 1% earns 30.8 times more than the poorest 50% [8]. This scenario compromises the capacity to respond to health and climate impacts among significant portions of the population living without adequate access to housing and sanitation [9].

Despite its relevance, the topic remains underexplored in the health field and, consequently, in nursing, although there is increasing demand for health services related to the effects of climate change. Understanding this problem requires a critical perspective on modes of production and consumption. Climate change is closely related to capitalist dynamics, whose logic of accumulation intensifies inequalities and reshapes social life [10]. Within this framework, Breilh proposes addressing these challenges through the “4S” principles: sustainability, sovereignty, solidarity, and security, guiding the construction of a sustainable economy and a socially oriented approach to life, with comprehensive protection and autonomy of subjects. Thus, climate change should not be treated merely as a factor affecting care strategies, but as a constitutive dimension of the social determination of health [10, 11].

It is important to recognize that climate change is not gender-neutral [12]. Projections from the United Nations (UN Women) indicate that, by 2050, 158 million women and girls will be pushed into poverty, with worsening food insecurity affecting 236 million people. The climate crisis intensifies conflicts, forced migration, and discourses opposing human rights, disproportionately affecting women, refugees, and other vulnerable groups [13, 14]. These scenarios have immediate and long-term impacts on mental health, while also aggravating pre-existing gender inequalities and affecting livelihoods, health, and safety [12].

To better understand these inequalities, this study adopts a perspective of gender as a relational and historical category. Scott [15] defines gender as a constitutive element of social relations based on perceived differences between sexes, and as a primary way of signifying power relations. This theoretical perspective highlights how the identities of women and men are constructed, regulated, and, at the same time, open to contradictions and redefinitions, as norms of femininity and masculinity are never fully internalized by individuals in practice.

In this sense, violence against women and girls represents a barrier to poverty reduction and, therefore, to mitigating the impacts of climate change [16]. Embedded within a structural and dynamic system that permeates different social segments, this violence is coextensive with the capitalist mode of production: the social disadvantages imposed on women maximize the extraction of absolute surplus value through the intensification and extension of labor and the maintenance of lower wages [17]. In this context, the climate crisis reinforces the urgency of recognizing human rights, adapting health systems, and promoting social justice with explicit attention to gender issues [12].

Given this context, this study aims to examine, from the perspective of women living in an urban occupation in the metropolitan region of São Paulo, how climate change exacerbates social and gender inequalities, affecting living conditions and health.

Methods

Study design

This is a qualitative study grounded in Historical-Dialectical Materialism and developed according to the principles of Emancipatory Action Research (EAR). Within this perspective, external researchers and individuals who experience the studied reality actively participate in the research process, problematizing the object in its immediate form as it appears in everyday experience and advancing toward an understanding of its deeper determinations. This process seeks to reveal the essence of the phenomenon within the totality of social relations, that is, through its connections with economic, political, cultural, and environmental dimensions of social life.

EAR guides investigative processes aimed at transforming social reality through the active participation of those involved. In this context, workshops function as pedagogical and political spaces in which everyday experiences are critically examined, enabling the identification of social contradictions and the development of collective strategies for action [18, 19]. In the present study, these principles guided the collective construction of workshop themes, the dialogical facilitation of meetings, and the shared validation of interpretations produced throughout the research process. The reporting of this study followed the recommendations of the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ), which establishes standards for transparency in qualitative research reporting. This framework ensured the explicit description of methodological procedures, data generation and analysis processes, as well as aspects of rigor and reflexivity adopted in the study [20].

Study setting

The study was conducted in the Lélia Gonzalez occupation, part of the Homeless Workers’ Movement (MTST), located in the city of Santo André, São Paulo, Brazil. The occupation is situated on land that had been abandoned for more than 15 years and was considered irregular due to unpaid municipal taxes. It was established in 2022 with approximately 300 residents and currently houses more than 2,500 people engaged in the struggle for adequate housing. According to the local government, the land is classified as a Social Interest Zone.

The selection of this territory as the study setting was based on an existing partnership with the School of Nursing of the University of São Paulo, within the scope of public health education activities, as well as collaboration with the Federal University of ABC, which develops extension projects in the community.

Ethical procedures

The study was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of the School of Nursing, University of São Paulo (CAAE: 84975924.0.0000.5392), in accordance with Brazilian National Health Council Resolution No. 466/2012 [21] and the General Data Protection Law (Law No. 13.709/2018) [22], which incorporate the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants agreed to take part in the workshops and to complete the complementary questionnaire by signing a written informed consent form.

This study was supported by the Fundação de Apoio à Universidade de São Paulo (FUSP).

Participants

The research team consisted of faculty members in the field of Public Health with experience in conducting EAR, as well as a faculty member in Territorial Planning specializing in the relationship between territory and climate change. Some researchers had prior engagement with the territory through teaching and extension activities, which facilitated the establishment of initial relationships with community leaders and participants.

Participants were women engaged in the MTST, involved in political education activities and in the struggle for the constitutional right to housing. Recruitment took place through invitations made during movement meetings, including women who expressed interest in participating in the workshops and agreed to join the study. This inclusion criterion resulted in the participation of women already engaged in collective and political processes within the movement, a factor later considered in the analysis and limitations of the study.

Participant inclusion continued until the workshop group was formed, taking into account participants’ interest and the collective dynamics inherent to EAR. A purposive sampling strategy was adopted, guided by EAR principles, prioritizing the inclusion of women with direct experience of the study context and availability to participate in the workshops. This configuration enabled the incorporation of elements of participatory research, involving both external and internal actors in the collective production of knowledge.

Among the 13 women who completed the questionnaire, ages ranged from 21 to 70 years. Eleven identified as cisgender women and two as transgender women. Regarding race/ethnicity, three identified as White, six as mixed-race, three as Black, and one as Indigenous. In terms of education, eight had completed secondary education, three of whom were enrolled in technical training; three had completed primary education; two had incomplete primary education and were enrolled in Youth and Adult Education (EJA); and one was enrolled in higher education. Regarding occupation, five were unemployed, three were retired, and five worked as a receptionist, domestic worker, cleaning assistant, logistics clerk, and cook.

Data production and organization

Data were generated through six emancipatory workshops and a complementary questionnaire administered at the end of the workshop cycle. The questionnaire was completed individually by participants, with researcher support when necessary. Its design was informed by themes that emerged during the workshops, aiming to deepen aspects related to perceptions of climate change, health, and gender inequalities.

The workshops were facilitated by the research team, with support from a local community leader, in order to encourage active and critical participation. These spaces were designed to stimulate the expression of opinions, experiences, beliefs, and collective reflections in pedagogically structured environments [18].

Meetings were held between March and August 2025, on weekends, in the afternoon, with an average duration of 90 min. The themes addressed and pedagogical strategies used in each meeting are presented in Table 1. Participation varied across sessions due to work demands, caregiving responsibilities, and the dynamics of life in the occupation.

Collective meaning-making on climate change

The first workshop aimed to collectively construct an understanding of climate change. In subsequent meetings, discussions were organized based on issues that emerged from the group itself, reflecting how the topic intersected with participants’ daily lives.

All workshops were audio-recorded with participants’ consent and subsequently transcribed in full. In addition, researchers produced field notes after each meeting, documenting group interactions, contextual aspects, and preliminary analytical reflections. These records contributed to a deeper understanding of the collective process and supported the organization of the empirical material. The use of multiple recording strategies aimed to ensure greater depth and reliability of the data generated.

Data analysis

Data analysis was guided by the dialectical method, which understands social reality as a dynamic totality marked by contradictions and historical determinations [23]. In this sense, participants’ accounts were interpreted considering the relationships between individual experiences, social conditions, and structural processes that produce inequalities.

The analytical process was conducted in successive stages. First, workshop transcripts, field notes, and questionnaire responses were organized into a single analytical corpus. Second, two researchers independently conducted repeated readings of the material, identifying units of meaning related to perceptions of climate change, health, gender, housing, work, care, and violence. Third, these units were coded openly and subsequently grouped by thematic similarity. Fourth, initial codes were discussed in team meetings, where interpretations were compared, divergences examined, and analytical groupings refined.

This process resulted in the construction of the study’s analytical categories: collective meaning-making about climate change; everyday experiences of climate change; climate justice; gender-based violence in women’s daily lives; gender roles in climate change; and the letter to COP30.

Critical Discourse Analysis was used as a complementary approach to examine not only the content of participants’ statements but also the meanings they constructed regarding the relationship between climate, health, and gender. This involved analyzing recurring discursive patterns, contradictions, silences, naming practices, and the ways participants connected individual experiences with broader social processes. This step enabled the identification of how participants articulated climate events with structural inequalities, gender relations, housing conditions, and forms of violence.

Interpretive validation occurred at two levels: among researchers, through collective discussion of codes and categories, and with participants, through the return of interpretations at the end of the workshop cycle. This process allowed for the assessment of the relevance of the categories and the adjustment of interpretations in light of participants’ collective experience.

The complementary questionnaire, applied at the end of the workshops, enabled the systematization of individual perceptions regarding how climate events directly affected women and their families. The integration of these different data sources, including workshops, the questionnaire, and field notes, contributed to a broader understanding of participants’ experiences. In dialogue with Critical Discourse Analysis, this material made it possible to understand how women attribute meaning to the relationship between climate, health, and gender, revealing social vulnerabilities and potentials for community strengthening.

Reflexivity, rigor, and limitations

Researchers acknowledged that their academic trajectories and institutional affiliations influenced both the facilitation of workshops and the interpretation of data. Efforts were made to explicitly address the relationship between researchers and participants, considering that affiliation with public higher education institutions may have influenced power dynamics in the field.

The presence of a local community leader played a mediating role, helping to reduce asymmetries and foster trust and dialogue, although it may also have influenced interaction dynamics and the expression of certain viewpoints.

Additionally, interpretations produced throughout the analytical process were systematically returned to participants at the end of the workshop cycle, allowing for validation, critique, and potential reformulation based on participants’ contributions. This process was understood as a constitutive element of EAR and contributed to strengthening reflexivity.

The credibility of the study was enhanced through triangulation of data sources, including workshops, the questionnaire, and field notes, as well as participant validation of findings. Limitations include the small number of workshops and variation in participation across sessions, as not all participants attended every meeting.

Furthermore, the specificity of the study context, namely a particular urban occupation linked to the MTST, means that findings should be interpreted in light of these socioterritorial conditions. Variations in participation may have influenced the continuity and depth of certain discussions. The presence of community leadership, while essential for mediation and trust-building, may also have shaped interactions. Finally, as in all self-reported data, participants’ accounts are subject to individual interpretations, memory, and situational contexts.

Results

The analysis is presented following the sequence in which the workshops were conducted, highlighting the main axes of debate collectively constructed. This analytical choice is grounded in the perspective of Emancipatory Action Research (EAR), in which the collective process of knowledge production is central. The results are presented through analytical categories that emerged from the empirical material, which cut across the different workshops and are made explicit throughout the findings, articulated with the experiences reported by the participants.

Workshop 1: collective meaning-making about climate change

The initial discussion was prompted by the collective reading of a newspaper article, used as a trigger for constructing meanings about climate change. Participants’ accounts highlighted concrete and articulated concerns, including aspects such as heat, flooding, pollution, illness, unemployment, housing, and insecurity, which were quickly associated with their concrete experiences in the territory.

Heat was recalled as a cause of sleepless nights, a feeling of suffocation in small houses, and illness among children and older adults. Flooding evoked material losses, fear of contamination from dirty water, and the need to leave one’s home to seek shelter.

Insecurity was understood both as urban violence and as the constant fear of environmental disasters. Unemployment appeared in relation to environmental crises and income instability, especially among women in informal work. Pollution was associated with increased respiratory diseases, while traffic emerged as a barrier to daily mobility, further worsened by flooding. Illness was understood as a direct consequence of climate change, including dengue, viral infections, and respiratory problems. Housing emerged as a central theme in the debate, particularly regarding the fragility of homes in the face of intense rainfall and high temperatures.

These elements show that climate change is perceived by participants through its concrete effects on everyday life, articulating environmental, social, and health dimensions, and being embedded in the material conditions of life in the territory.

Workshop 2: experiences with climate change

In Workshop 2, the central theme was women’s everyday life in the face of climate change. The discussion was stimulated by statements on the topic, which prompted positioning and debate among participants. Among the points raised were statements such as “climate change is an invention,” “it affects the rich more,” and “it has no relationship with health”, which generated disagreement and the exchange of arguments.

Many women reported direct experiences that contradicted these ideas, such as floods that affected their homes, respiratory diseases intensified by pollution, and food insecurity aggravated by rising prices.

The accounts show that the recognition of climate change as a concrete phenomenon is constructed through lived experience, in tension with social discourses that deny, minimize, or displace its effects. In this sense, participants articulate extreme events, health impacts, and instability in living conditions as interdependent dimensions of the problem.

Workshop 3: climate justice

In Workshop 3, the focus was on politics, representation, and social justice, with the proposal to construct a collective letter addressed to Minister Marina Silva, fostering debate on the relationship between local demands and institutional responses.

Participants’ accounts highlighted concrete and articulated concerns, including climate security, associated with the protection of families in the face of flooding and heat waves; the defense of accessible and healthy food, understood as a basic condition for health and dignity; the encouragement of conscious consumption, in contrast to waste and the exploitation of natural resources; the taxation of large fortunes, as a form of economic justice in the face of growing inequality; the expansion of women’s political representation, especially Black and peripheral women, in decision-making spaces; and the defense of Indigenous peoples, recognized as guardians of the land and biodiversity.

The accounts show that participants understand climate change as a political problem, articulated with structural inequalities and the unequal distribution of resources, risks, and power. In this sense, the formulation of collective demands expresses not only the identification of vulnerabilities but also the development of critical positions regarding the role of the State and forms of social organization.

Workshop 4: gender-based violence in women’s daily lives

In Workshop 4, the theme was violence and gender roles. The discussion was stimulated by an audiovisual resource, the short film Vida Maria, which prompted reflections on the reproduction of gender roles, the naturalization of inequality, and its intergenerational transmission. During the debate, participants related the film to their own experiences, bringing accounts of violence suffered, both physical and symbolic, as well as the burden of domestic work, and the difficulties imposed by gender inequality.

The accounts indicate the presence of different forms of violence associated with living conditions and gender inequalities in the territory. They also show that these forms of violence are articulated with gender inequalities and material conditions of existence, and are intensified in contexts of social vulnerability. In this sense, participants’ accounts indicate that the climate crisis worsens pre-existing situations, increasing women’s exposure to different forms of violence and deepening structural inequalities.

Workshop 5: gender roles in climate change

In Workshop 5, the central theme was gender roles. The discussion was stimulated by references that prompted reflections on the social construction of femininity, articulated with the experiences shared by participants. Among the expressions highlighted were: “being a woman means struggling every day,” “violence is not normal,” and “the right to housing is the right to life.”

Participants’ accounts revealed the burden of responsibilities assigned to women, as well as the presence of multiple forms of violence in everyday life. Participants related these experiences to living conditions in the territory and to gender inequalities.

The accounts indicate that the social organization of care falls disproportionately on women and is intensified in contexts of housing precarity and socioeconomic instability. In this sense, climate change increases the burden of care by requiring women to manage the everyday impacts on family and community life.

Workshop 6: letter to COP30

In Workshop 6, the group prepared a letter addressed to Ministers Marina Silva and Sônia Guajajara, as a synthesis of the reflections accumulated in the previous workshops. The process was participatory, with women suggesting and negotiating the themes to be included.

The letter brought together demands such as climate justice, conscious consumption, taxation of large fortunes, defense of Indigenous peoples, the guarantee of decent housing, confrontation of gender-based violence, and expansion of political representation. During the debate, the demand for quality public transportation was also added, based on the difficulties experienced in everyday life.

The preparation of the letter shows the articulation between everyday experiences, structural inequalities, and demands for recognition and rights, indicating the construction of a broader understanding of climate change as a social and political problem. In this process, participants not only reported impacts but also produced critical interpretations and collective proposals for action, grounded in their concrete living conditions.

The meeting closed the cycle of workshops, synthesizing the main demands and perceptions of the participants. This synthesis expresses the collective construction of responses to climate change, articulating social, environmental, and gender dimensions.

Discussion

Participants had different lengths of engagement with the MTST. While some were more closely involved in the movement’s activities, others had more recent participation and were still in the process of developing political awareness. Nevertheless, with regard to gender and violence, it was possible to identify structural inequalities that permeate women’s daily lives, regardless of their level of engagement in the movement. This finding indicates that the experience of gender inequality is not restricted to the level of political participation, but is embedded in broader structural conditions that shape women’s everyday lives.

The presence of transgender women in the study broadens the understanding of these inequalities, as their experiences are shaped by multiple, intersecting forms of vulnerability. Although they were not analyzed separately, their inclusion in the group indicates that the intersections between gender identity, socioeconomic conditions, and climate change constitute an important area for future research. This aspect reinforces that gender inequality is not limited to levels of political participation but is rooted in broader social conditions that are consistently reflected in participants’ accounts. The workshops highlighted how climate change contributes to reinforcing social and gender inequalities, as well as affecting health, based on participants’ reports of how they experience these issues in their everyday lives, reaffirming the framework of the social determination of health [23]. Participants’ accounts demonstrate that extreme environmental phenomena are not perceived as isolated events, but rather as expressions of broader economic processes that prioritize consumption over social reproduction, systematically producing inequalities. They also emphasized direct impacts, such as heat, flooding, disease, and insecurity, which act as multipliers of health risks, through both direct and indirect mechanisms, amplifying pre-existing inequalities [24].

Drawing on the framework proposed by Parsons et al. [25], which conceptualizes how populations are affected by climate change across three levels, these effects can be understood as primary (directly related to ecosystems), secondary (resulting from environmental changes), and tertiary (long-term social transformations). All three levels were evident in participants’ experiences, as demonstrated by reports of flooding, illness, food insecurity, and income instability, highlighting the overlapping vulnerabilities present in contexts of social inequality.

Marginalized populations, in particular, contribute little to greenhouse gas emissions, yet they are the most affected by their consequences, both through direct health impacts and through intensified challenges in sustaining everyday life. This condition was reflected in participants’ accounts, which linked climate change to housing precarity, unemployment, and insecurity. Furthermore, although these populations are the most exposed and affected, they remain systematically excluded from decision-making processes [26]. In this sense, it is essential to promote spaces for dialogue and collective reflection on climate change and mitigation strategies, particularly among populations most affected by its impacts.

The findings also align with Scott [15], as participants’ accounts reveal how power relations are materially embedded in their lives, through work overload, limited access to adequate housing, and multiple forms of violence exacerbated by climate crises. In this context, prioritizing women’s rights to a dignified life and promoting gender equality in mitigation strategies does not mean framing women as victims, but rather recognizing how macrostructures reproduce inequalities, including gender-based inequalities [24, 27].

In this direction, Breilh’s 4 S framework sustainability, sovereignty, solidarity, and security provides a useful lens for critically organizing women’s perceptions of climate change as an expression of the social determination of health [10, 11]. Sustainability emerged in critiques of predatory consumption, as evidenced in participants’ accounts of pollution, flooding, and extreme heat, associated with a capitalist model of production oriented toward accumulation and the exploitation of natural resources [28]. Although the debate on ethical consumption remains incipient, it involves adopting positions that oppose individualistic interests in favor of the common good, understanding consumption as a means of promoting social well-being [28].

Sovereignty was expressed through demands for access to healthy and affordable food, decent housing, and the taxation of large fortunes, as observed in participants’ contributions to the collective letter, reinforcing the need for community autonomy over living conditions. Solidarity was evident in the collective strengthening fostered through the workshops and in the collaborative development of the letter addressed to national authorities, demonstrating the power of collective action in confronting structural inequalities. Security was expressed in demands for protection against climate and housing risks, as well as in addressing gender-based violence, highlighting the centrality of public policies that integrate social, environmental, and human rights protections. At this point, it is important to emphasize that participants recognized that climate change cannot be understood in isolation, but its embedding within an unequal and unjust world, a dimension that has not yet been fully assimilated by society and remains, to some extent, neglected even within scientific structures [26].

Women’s constructions of climate change extend beyond an individual perspective, as they were collectively developed through shared knowledge, mutual support, and a sense of collective strength oriented toward the common good, as evidenced in the participatory process of the workshops and the co-production of the letter [26]. Their perceptions go beyond the immediate environmental dimension, recognizing that political, social, and economic hierarchies are at the root of a problem that, for them, is urgent, concrete, and not distant.

This understanding contrasts with part of the literature, which suggests that most people perceive climate change as an individual, uncertain, and distant phenomenon [29, 30]. This dialogue highlights that the voices of women from the occupation not only confirm the relevance of the critical 4 S framework, but also extend it by incorporating gendered perspectives and urban struggles for climate justice.

Conclusion

This study highlights the inseparable relationship between climate change, social inequalities, health, and gender. Within the scope of this exploratory study, situated in a specific context of urban vulnerability, Emancipatory Action Research was a relevant approach to support the collective construction of reflections on addressing the climate emergencies experienced by this social group. The experiences of the women, as evidenced throughout the workshops, revealed how extreme environmental phenomena can become more threatening to life and health in urban contexts marked by precarious housing and labor conditions.

The study enabled participants to articulate the burden of work, housing precarity, and different forms of violence, demonstrating that the effects of the climate crisis are not neutral, but rather are shaped by and reproduce hierarchies and power relations. These findings contribute to a deeper understanding of these dynamics in similar contexts, without implying generalization. Thus, addressing the climate crisis from a gender perspective goes beyond recognizing women’s vulnerability; it also involves understanding their political agency and their central role in processes of resistance and struggles for social justice, as expressed in the experiences and collective constructions developed throughout the workshops.

In light of the 4 S theoretical framework, it was possible to articulate critical perceptions and collective demands. These included critiques of excessive and predatory consumption of natural resources, reflected in participants’ accounts of pollution, heat, and flooding; the defense of access to affordable food and adequate housing as expressions of sovereignty; the strengthening of social participation in public policies, as expressed in the collectively developed letter; and the need for security in the face of climate events and gender-based violence, as reflected in reports of insecurity and vulnerability. These elements suggest potential pathways for reflection and policy formulation, to be further explored in studies with greater diversity of contexts and participants.

Finally, the process developed through workshops, grounded in public health and Emancipatory Action Research, contributed to strengthening spaces for dialogue and collective construction, transforming reflections into instruments of political action, as evidenced in the production of the letter and the systematization of participants’ demands. In this sense, the study contributes to understanding elements that may support the development of strategies to address social inequalities intensified by climate emergencies.

Thus, climate change moves beyond being an abstract phenomenon and becomes recognized as an urgent issue based on the concrete experiences reported by participants, pointing to the need for coordinated responses among science, public policies, and social movements, guided by climate justice and gender equity. For nursing, this implies incorporating socio-environmental and gender dimensions into education, research, and practice, and strengthening the role of the profession in contributing to collective and equitable responses to climate emergencies.
