Environmental and epidemiological drivers of dengue forecasting across heterogeneous transmission settings in Brazil and Panama
Authors: Lauren E Charles, Trevor Edwards, Conner Phillips
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT

Introduction

Dengue fever has emerged as the fastest-growing mosquito-borne viral disease globally, with transmission intensifying across tropical and subtropical regions over recent decades. The Americas have experienced particularly substantial increases in burden, culminating in unprecedented epidemics in 2024, during which Brazil and Panama reported historically high case counts that placed considerable strain on public health systems [1], [2], [3]. These events highlight persistent limitations in anticipating where and when transmission will intensify, despite ongoing surveillance and vector control efforts, and underscore the need for forecasting approaches that can account for environmental variability and changing transmission conditions [4].

Dengue transmission is shaped by interacting climatic, ecological, demographic, and land-use processes that influence vector abundance, viral replication, and opportunities for human–vector contact. Temperature and precipitation affect mosquito development, survival, biting activity, and extrinsic incubation of dengue virus, while urbanization, land-cover change, environmental fragmentation, and human settlement patterns alter habitat suitability and exposure risk [5], [6], [7]. These drivers operate across multiple spatial and temporal scales, producing heterogeneous transmission environments that complicate both mechanistic understanding and outbreak prediction [8]. As dengue expands under changing environmental and demographic conditions, understanding how these drivers interact across ecological settings is increasingly important for climate-informed disease surveillance. These interacting environmental, ecological, and human drivers reflect the interconnected systems emphasized by the One Health approach to infectious disease prevention and preparedness.

Previous studies have identified important environmental determinants of dengue transmission and developed forecasting approaches to predict dengue incidence and spatial risk. However, many forecasting systems remain limited by narrow geographic scope, reliance on single modelling approaches, or substantial manual data integration. Differences in surveillance systems, environmental data sources, and implementation contexts further limit model transferability across regions [9], [10]. Consequently, whether environmental drivers contribute similarly across contrasting ecological settings remains insufficiently understood.

Brazil and Panama provide useful contrasting settings for evaluating this question. Brazil spans a large and environmentally heterogeneous landscape with state-level surveillance data, whereas Panama represents a smaller national-scale setting with different spatial resolution and transmission structure. Comparing these countries allows assessment of whether dengue forecasting performance is shaped by geographic scale, environmental heterogeneity, and the relative importance of environmental versus epidemiological predictors.

To address these gaps, we adopted a One Health approach by integrating epidemiological surveillance with climatic, environmental, ecological, and demographic datasets to predict dengue incidence and spatial risk across Brazil and Panama. Building on our previously developed infectious disease forecasting framework [11], we extended the approach to evaluate how environmental heterogeneity influences dengue forecasting across contrasting ecological settings through integration of diverse environmental and epidemiological data. We aimed to determine whether integrating heterogeneous environmental datasets could improve dengue forecasting and whether environmental heterogeneity alters the relative importance of environmental and epidemiological predictors across transmission settings.

Methods

Study design and setting

We conducted a retrospective spatiotemporal environmental epidemiology study to evaluate how climatic, environmental, ecological, and demographic factors influence dengue forecasting across Brazil and Panama. These countries were selected to represent contrasting transmission settings characterized by substantial differences in geographic scale, climate, land-cover, population density, and surveillance resolution. Epidemiological and environmental datasets spanning 01 January 2015 to 31 December 2024 were integrated within a reproducible modelling framework to evaluate environmental drivers of dengue transmission and prediction accuracy.

Epidemiological data

Monthly dengue case counts were obtained from national surveillance systems for Brazil and Panama (Appendix Table A.1). For Brazil, data were aggregated at the state level to capture spatial heterogeneity in transmission dynamics. For Panama, data were available at the national level and analyzed accordingly.

All case data were standardized to monthly time steps and aligned temporally across datasets. Where necessary, administrative boundaries and naming conventions were harmonized to ensure consistency across sources. For Espírito Santo, a change in the regional dengue reporting system required supplementation of the national surveillance series with data from a separate state-level source for 2022–2025 to maintain continuity of the state-level time series. These records were harmonized to the monthly resolution used in the Brazil forecasting dataset.

Environmental and epidemiological predictor variables

A One Health predictor framework was developed to characterize the environmental, ecological, demographic, and epidemiological conditions associated with dengue transmission (Appendix Table A.1). Environmental variables included temperature, precipitation, derived climate indicators, land-use and land-cover classifications, mosquito habitat suitability, livestock density, and human population estimates.

Variables were selected based on biological plausibility and published evidence linking environmental conditions with vector ecology and dengue transmission. Relative humidity was considered as a candidate climatic predictor in both countries. In Brazil, relative humidity was excluded from the final models because 75·7% of observations were missing in the available dataset; given the extent of missingness, imputation was considered likely to introduce substantial uncertainty. Relative humidity was retained as a climatic predictor in Panama, where sufficient data were available for model development. Temperature- and precipitation-based climatic indicators with greater temporal and spatial coverage were retained in both settings. Collectively, these datasets represent complementary One Health domains by integrating environmental conditions (climate and land-cover), ecological indicators (mosquito habitat suitability and livestock density), and human health information (population characteristics and dengue surveillance). Livestock density variables were included as proxies for broader agricultural land-use and landscape composition rather than as direct biological determinants of dengue transmission.

Data harmonization and processing framework

We developed a reproducible environmental data harmonization workflow to integrate heterogeneous climatic, ecological, demographic, and epidemiological datasets (Fig. 1). This framework enables reproducible integration of epidemiological and environmental data streams while minimizing manual preprocessing.

One Health framework for environmental and epidemiological data integration for dengue forecasting. Schematic overview of the automated framework integrating epidemiological, climatic, environmental, and demographic data to generate dengue forecasts. Heterogeneous data sources are harmonized through a standardized processing pipeline, followed by model training using multiple statistical, machine learning, and neural-network approaches. Missing data were addressed using predictor-specific temporal and spatial imputation approaches. Missingness was quantified for candidate predictors before model development. For climate indicators lacking 2024 observations, missing values were imputed using the median observed for the corresponding calendar month during 2020–2023. Imputation was performed within each state for Brazil and at the national level for Panama, thereby preserving country-specific seasonal patterns. Missing values in slowly varying and static predictors, including population, livestock density, land-cover, and mosquito suitability, were addressed using forward and backward filling within geographic units. Relative humidity was excluded from the Brazil models because of substantially greater missingness (75·71%). Predictor-specific missingness before imputation is reported in Appendix Table A.2. All preprocessing steps were applied consistently across datasets to ensure comparability.

Modelling framework

Forecasting models

We evaluated multiple statistical and machine learning approaches to quantify relationships between integrated environmental predictors and dengue incidence, consistent with previous work demonstrating variability in forecasting performance across diseases and contexts [12], [13]. Models included autoregressive time-series methods (AutoRegressive Integrated Moving Average, ARIMA), tree-based ensemble models (Random Forest; eXtreme Gradient Boost, XGBoost), and the Time-series Dense Encoder (TiDE) neural network architecture.

Model selection was based on mean absolute error (MAE) calculated on pre-2024 development validation data. For each model class, the candidate configuration with the lowest development-validation MAE was retained. The final model comparison was then performed across the retained ARIMA, Random Forest, XGBoost, and TiDE models using the same validation criterion. For Brazil, model classes were compared using quarterly rolling forecasts, and TiDE was also evaluated using a six-month forecasting configuration. The model with the lowest development-validation MAE was selected separately for each state to form the state-specific ensemble. For Panama, ARIMA, Random Forest, XGBoost, and TiDE were first compared using quarterly rolling forecasts, and TiDE was additionally evaluated using a seasonal six-month forecasting configuration. Because Panama was modeled as a single national series, the selected final model constituted the Panama ensemble.

To improve predictive robustness and reduce model-specific bias, ensemble predictions for Brazil were generated by selecting the best-performing forecasting model for each state based on validation MAE and combining these state-specific predictions into the national forecast. Because Panama was modeled at the national level, the single best-performing model constituted the ensemble forecast. Multi-model ensemble approaches have increasingly been adopted in infectious disease forecasting because they can leverage complementary strengths across modelling frameworks while reducing sensitivity to the assumptions of any single model [14]. The MAE was selected as the primary criterion for ensemble model selection because it provides an interpretable measure of forecast error in the original units of the data, facilitates direct comparison across modelling approaches, and is less sensitive than root mean squared error (RMSE) to extreme errors associated with rare epidemic events while remaining comparable across heterogeneous transmission settings. Model training incorporated both historical dengue case counts and monthly environmental predictors, enabling capture of temporal dependence and nonlinear interactions in transmission dynamics. Historical dengue incidence was represented using monthly lagged case counts from the preceding 12 months (t − 1 through t − 12), allowing the models to capture both short-term persistence and annual seasonal patterns in transmission. Climatic variables were incorporated exclusively as past covariates, ensuring that only environmental information available prior to the forecast period contributed to model development and avoiding the use of contemporaneous climate observations.

A rolling-origin forecasting strategy was implemented to evaluate multiple forecast horizons. Quarterly rolling forecasts were generated using three-month prediction windows, with model inputs updated at each forecast origin using the most recently available observations. TiDE was also evaluated using a six-month forecast horizon to assess performance over longer seasonal prediction windows.

Model optimization and implementation

Model parameterization and optimization were conducted separately for each modelling approach using training and development-validation data preceding the independent 2024 evaluation period. All models were implemented within the Darts time-series forecasting framework. Model-selection procedures and all implementation settings recoverable from the retained analysis artifacts are summarized in Appendix C.

TiDE was implemented using Darts version 0.35.0 and PyTorch version 2.7.1. The model used a 12-month input window, a learning rate of 1 × 10−4, and static covariates over both a rolling three-month and six-month forecast horizon. Training used fixed random seeds and deterministic computation where supported. Probabilistic forecasts were generated for Brazil using a quantile-regression likelihood and for Panama using a Gaussian likelihood. Both settings generated 100 samples, with the median (50th percentile) used as the point prediction and the 5th and 95th percentiles defining a central 90% predictive interval.

Candidate parameter configurations within each model class were compared using development-validation MAE, and the configuration producing the lowest MAE was retained for each geographic unit and model class. All model configuration and selection steps were completed before evaluation against the independent 2024 data.

Land-cover weighting and risk mapping

Predicted dengue case counts were used to generate spatial risk estimates across study regions. We increased the spatial granularity of predicted case counts by distributing cases within states (Brazil) or country (Panama) based on land-cover weighting. Predicted incidence values were subsequently classified into predefined risk categories based on cases per 100,000 population.

To translate environmental heterogeneity into spatially explicit dengue risk estimates, predicted case counts were distributed according to land-cover characteristics. Land-cover weights were assigned using a heuristic framework to preserve the relative ranking of landscape classes expected to differ in human-vector contact based on published evidence relating urbanization, population density, land-use, and landscape composition to dengue transmission [15], [16], [17]. The weights were not empirically estimated but were selected to preserve the relative ranking of land-cover classes based on expected human–mosquito contact while maintaining the total allocation of predicted cases within each administrative unit. This heuristic approach was intended to evaluate the feasibility of incorporating landscape information into spatial forecasting rather than to estimate the true contribution of individual land-cover classes. Urban areas received the highest weights, cropland and mosaic vegetation received intermediate weights, forested landscapes received lower weights, and water bodies, bare land, and sparse vegetation received the lowest weights (Table 1).

A one-at-a-time sensitivity analysis was conducted to assess the robustness of the heuristic land-cover weighting scheme. Individual land-cover weights were perturbed by up to ±0·10 while all other weights and settings were held constant. Weights were bounded at zero where subtraction would otherwise produce negative values. Thus, land-cover classes with original weights below 0·10 were assigned a weight of zero in the downward perturbation. For originally unweighted classes, the upward perturbation assigned a weight of 0·10. Resulting risk maps were compared with the original-weight baseline, and the proportion of raster pixels changing risk category was quantified. Land-cover classes absent from a country's raster were recorded as not observed.

Risk levels were classified using the following standardized thresholds established by the Brazilian Ministry of Health: Low risk (fewer than 100 cases per 100,000 individuals), Medium risk (between 100 and 300 cases per 100,000 individuals), and High risk (more than 300 cases per 100,000 individuals) [18].

Model validation and performance assessment

Predictive performance was evaluated using a temporally independent validation framework in which only observations available before each forecast origin were used for model training. The primary evaluation period was Jan 1, 2015, to Dec 31, 2023 except for the Panama seasonal 6 X 6 TiDE configuration which followed its predefined December 2023-November 2024 forecast cycle, as described below. These were evaluated against observed dengue transmission patterns during the 2024 epidemic period, providing out-of-sample assessment under real-world epidemic conditions.

Performance was assessed using MAE (average of the absolute differences between predicted and actual values), RMSE (square root of the average squared differences), mean absolute percentage error (MAPE, errors are a percentage of the actual values), and mean bias error (MBE, average of the raw (non-absolute) differences). Each metric provides a different view into performance: MAE treats all errors equal and is robust to outliers; RMSE more strongly penalizes large errors and outliers; MAPE allows comparison across datasets with different scales but fails if actual values are zero; MBE preserves the sign of the error to identify if the model consistently over or under predicts. Ensemble model MAE performance was compared with individual models to evaluate improvements in predictive accuracy and stability.

To provide a simple forecasting benchmark, model performance was additionally compared with a seasonal-naïve baseline in which the predicted dengue case count for each month was defined as the observed case count for the corresponding month of the preceding year (t − 12). The primary seasonal-naïve baseline was evaluated over the same January–December 2024 validation period using MAE, RMSE, MAPE, and MBE. For direct comparison with the Panama seasonal 6 × 6 TiDE forecast, the seasonal-naïve benchmark was additionally evaluated over the matched December 2023–November 2024 forecast period. All other models used the January 2024–December 2024 evaluation period.

Predictive uncertainty was quantified for probabilistic TiDE forecasts using central 90% predictive intervals defined by the 5th and 95th percentiles around the median forecast. Because comparable probabilistic intervals could not be established for ARIMA, Random Forest, and XGBoost from the available model implementations, predictive intervals were not combined across model classes to estimate uncertainty for the Brazil ensemble.

To assess agreement between predicted and observed risk classifications, predicted and observed monthly dengue incidence rates were assigned to the predefined risk categories described above. Agreement between observed and predicted risk categories was evaluated using Cohen's quadratic-weighted kappa (κ) and Gwet's AC2 statistics. Both metrics quantify agreement beyond chance for ordinal risk categories and were interpreted using the Landis and Koch classification scheme, where values between 0·61 and 0·80 indicate substantial agreement. Gwet's AC2 was additionally calculated because it remains stable in situations with highly imbalanced category distributions or limited variation in observed risk levels.

Feature importance and interpretability

To identify the environmental and epidemiological factors contributing most strongly to dengue prediction across contrasting ecological settings, feature importance analyses were conducted. Feature importance was summarized across models to identify environmental and epidemiological variables that consistently contributed to accurate dengue prediction across contrasting ecological settings. To evaluate feature importance across heterogeneous model classes, we used model-agnostic permutation importance. For each fitted model, baseline MAE was first calculated using the unmodified evaluation data. Each predictor was then randomly permuted while all other predictors were held unchanged, predictions were regenerated, and the resulting change in MAE (ΔMAE) relative to baseline performance was calculated. This procedure was repeated 20 times for each predictor, and the mean ΔMAE across permutations was used as the measure of predictor importance. Larger positive ΔMAE values indicate greater reliance of the fitted model on the information contained in that predictor. The average ΔMAE across all models was calculated for each feature to determine their impact in predicting dengue for both Brazil and Panama. Permutation importance was interpreted as predictive rather than causal importance, because correlated predictors and temporal structure may influence the magnitude of ΔMAE.

Reproducibility

All analyses were conducted using a standardized computational pipeline designed for reproducibility and scalability across diseases and geographic regions. Data preprocessing, modelling, and evaluation steps were implemented consistently across both countries. The standardized workflow was designed to facilitate adaptation to additional diseases and geographic regions through integration of publicly available environmental and epidemiological datasets.

Analyses were implemented in Python 3.12. The neural network forecasting workflow used Darts 0.35.0 and PyTorch 2.7.1, with pandas 2.3.0 and NumPy 2.2.6 used for data processing. Random seeds were fixed for Python, NumPy, and PyTorch, and deterministic GPU computation was enabled where supported to improve reproducibility. Additional details on model implementation, selection, and recoverable parameter settings are provided in Appendix C.

Results

Spatial and temporal patterns of dengue transmission

Dengue transmission exhibited pronounced spatial and temporal heterogeneity across Brazil and Panama over the study period, reflecting differences in climate, land-cover, population distribution, and transmission intensity (Fig. 2). In Brazil, case counts increased sharply in 2024 relative to preceding years, consistent with a large-scale epidemic and representing a substantial deviation from historical patterns. Transmission intensity was highest in densely populated southeastern and northeastern regions, while persistently lower incidence was observed across parts of the northern region.

Temporal trends in reported dengue cases in Brazil and Panama, 2015–2024. Monthly reported dengue case counts in Brazil and Panama from Jan 2015 to Dec 2024. A marked increase in transmission is observed in 2024, consistent with a large-scale epidemic across the Americas. Seasonal variation is evident, with higher case counts during rainy periods and lower but persistent transmission during dry seasons.

Brazil reported 14·8 million dengue cases during the study period (2015–2024), with marked temporal variability reflecting recurrent epidemic cycles. Monthly case counts averaged 137,110 ± 276,981 cases (median 39,906 cases), ranging from 6864 to 1,720,401 cases. Transmission reached an unprecedented peak during the 2024 epidemic, when monthly case counts exceeded 1·7 million cases, more than four times higher than previous epidemic peaks recorded in 2016 and 2019. Median monthly state-level case counts ranged from 12 cases in Roraima to 5851 cases in Goiás, illustrating the considerable environmental and epidemiological heterogeneity represented across Brazilian states.

Panama reported 111,917 dengue cases during the study period (2015–2024), with a median monthly case count of 502·5 cases and a maximum monthly peak of 8428 cases during the 2024 epidemic. Monthly case counts exhibited substantial variability (mean ± SD: 932·6 ± 1211·0 cases), reflecting the episodic nature of dengue transmission and the presence of multiple epidemic waves.

Despite substantial differences in geographic scale and disease burden, both countries exhibited pronounced seasonal transmission patterns, with case counts increasing during rainy periods and declining during dry seasons. These spatial and seasonal patterns corresponded with climatic variability, land-cover, and population distribution, highlighting the environmental heterogeneity underlying dengue transmission across the two study settings.

Environmental and epidemiological drivers of transmission

Environmental and epidemiological predictors contributed differently to dengue forecasting across Brazil and Panama, demonstrating substantial variation in the environmental drivers associated with transmission (Fig. 3).

Relative importance of environmental and epidemiological drivers of dengue forecasting. Feature importance rankings for the top predictors contributing to dengue forecasting in Brazil (a) and Panama (b). Feature importance was quantified using permutation importance, expressed as the mean increase in MAE (ΔMAE) following random permutation of each predictor. Larger positive values indicate greater loss of predictive accuracy when the information contained in that predictor was disrupted. Environmental predictors, including climatic and land-cover variables, were prominent contributors to predictive accuracy in Brazil, whereas recent dengue transmission history contributed more strongly in Panama. Climatic variables, including precipitation- and temperature-related indicators, also contributed substantially in Panama.

In Brazil, prediction performance was driven primarily by environmental variables, with land-cover characteristics, livestock density, mosquito habitat suitability, and climatic indicators ranking among the strongest contributors. The diversity of influential predictors likely reflects Brazil's considerable environmental heterogeneity and suggests that multiple environmental processes jointly influence dengue transmission across regions.

In contrast, recent transmission history was the strongest predictor in Panama, while climate variables, including precipitation and temperature-related indicators, also contributed substantially. This pattern suggests that temporal persistence played a greater role in forecasting than environmental heterogeneity at the national scale.

The one-at-a-time land-cover sensitivity analysis showed that mapped risk classifications were generally stable to ±0·10 perturbations in individual weights, although sensitivity varied by country and land-cover class. In Brazil, most perturbations produced no change in categorical risk assignments. The largest changes occurred when the weight for mosaic natural vegetation was increased from 0·12 to 0·22, shifting 21·35% of pixels from low to medium risk, and when cropland was increased from 0·45 to 0·55, shifting 8·02% of pixels from medium to high risk. In Panama, sensitivity was concentrated among several low-weight land-cover classes. Closed evergreen broadleaf forest showed the largest change: reducing its weight from 0.08 to 0.00 resulted in 55.28% of pixels becoming unallocated, whereas increasing the weight to 0.18 shifted 55.28% of pixels from low to medium risk. Smaller changes were observed for open evergreen broadleaf forest, mosaic natural vegetation, herbaceous wetland, permanent water bodies, and shrublands. Cropland and urban-area perturbations produced no changes in categorical risk classifications. Increasing the ocean weight from 0.00 to 0.10 produced no change in terrestrial risk classifications. Full results are provided in Appendix Tables E.1–E.2.

Collectively, these findings demonstrate that the relative contribution of environmental and epidemiological predictors varies across ecological settings, highlighting the importance of adapting forecasting approaches to local environmental conditions rather than assuming a common set of predictors across regions. These differences in predictor importance were consistent with the contrasting ecological and surveillance characteristics of the two study settings.

Model performance and comparative evaluation

Forecast performance differed according to the environmental and spatial complexity of the study setting. Ensemble forecasts achieved the lowest MAE and RMSE in both countries, although TiDE, XGBoost, and the seasonal-naïve baseline achieved lower MAPE values in Brazil (Table 2). Although the seasonal-naïve baseline achieved a slightly lower MAPE than the ensemble in Brazil (57·6% vs 61·3%), the ensemble achieved lower MAE and RMSE. In Brazil, the ensemble reduced MAE by approximately 7·7% relative to the next best-performing candidate model (TiDE) and by 24·4% relative to the seasonal-naïve baseline. TiDE was evaluated using both quarterly rolling and six-month forecast configurations during model development. During the independent 2024 evaluation, the quarterly rolling TiDE configuration achieved lower national error than the six-month configuration (MAE = 348,994 vs 442,009; RMSE = 567,327 vs 688,625; MAPE = 57·7% vs 65·4%). Errors from the six-month configuration were concentrated during the January–June 2024 epidemic surge, whereas performance improved substantially during the second six-month forecast period. In Panama, TiDE had the lowest development-validation MAE among the four model classes under the quarterly rolling framework and was, therefore, evaluated further using the six-month seasonal configuration. During independent evaluation, the seasonal TiDE configuration achieved lower error than quarterly rolling TiDE across their respective evaluation periods (seasonal TiDE: MAE = 1199.63, RMSE = 1585.38, MAPE = 37.01%; quarterly rolling TiDE: MAE = 1655.45, RMSE = 2223.16, MAPE = 49.55%). Using the matched December 2023–November 2024 evaluation period, the seasonal TiDE configuration reduced MAE by 29.0% relative to the seasonal-naïve benchmark. The seasonal TiDE configuration constituted the final Panama forecast and, because Panama was modeled as a single national series, the Panama ensemble. These findings indicate that the ensemble improved absolute forecast accuracy relative to a simple seasonal benchmark in both settings, although performance gains varied according to the evaluation metric. Compared with ARIMA, the Panama ensemble reduced MAE by 62·5%.

Model selection patterns differed substantially between countries and formed the bases of the Brazil ensemble forecast. In Brazil, no single modelling approach consistently outperformed alternatives, with ARIMA and quarterly rolling TiDE each selected in ten of 27 states, XGBoost in six states, and Random Forest in one state. Although a six-month TiDE configuration was also evaluated during model development, it was not retained for the final state-specific ensemble (Appendix Table B.1). In contrast, TiDE was consistently selected in Panama, suggesting that a single modelling framework adequately captured the dominant transmission patterns at the national scale. These findings indicate that prediction accuracy is influenced by the environmental heterogeneity and transmission complexity of the study setting.

Spatial prediction of dengue risk

Spatial forecasts revealed distinct and heterogeneous patterns of dengue risk across both countries (Fig. 4). In Brazil, predicted high-risk regions included southeastern states such as São Paulo, Rio de Janeiro, and Minas Gerais, as well as northeastern coastal areas, while low-risk regions were consistently identified in northern states including Amazonas and Roraima. In Panama, elevated risk was concentrated in urban areas, particularly Panama City, consistent with observed transmission patterns.

Observed and predicted spatial distribution of dengue risk in Brazil and Panama, 2024. Spatial distribution of dengue risk based on observed case data (left panels; a and c) and model predictions (right panels; b and d) for 2024. Risk levels are classified using standardized thresholds based on cases per 100,000 population. Predicted maps show qualitative visual agreement with observed patterns, accurately identifying high-risk regions in southeastern and northeastern Brazil (a and b) and urban areas in Panama (c and d), including Panama City. Minor discrepancies are observed in regions affected by rapid changes in transmission dynamics.

Agreement between predicted and observed risk classifications was substantial in both countries. Across Brazil, pooled analysis of 324 state-month observations yielded substantial agreement between observed and predicted risk categories (Cohen's κ = 0·617; Gwet's AC2 = 0·616), with exact agreement achieved for 78% of state-month classifications. In Panama, predicted and observed monthly risk categories matched in 10 of 12 months (83·3% concordance), corresponding to substantial agreement (Cohen's κ = 0·621; Gwet's AC2 = 0·607). Discrepancies were limited and primarily reflected overprediction of risk during October and November 2024, when observed low-risk periods were classified as medium risk.

Seasonal forecasts successfully captured shifts in transmission intensity between wet and dry periods. In Panama, forecast accuracy remained stable across seasons (peak-season MAPE = 36·0% vs non-peak MAPE = 38·8%), demonstrating the model's ability to represent temporally dynamic transmission patterns. Similar trends were observed in Brazil, where prediction error remained consistent between wet/peak and dry/non-peak periods (peak-season MAPE = 62·2% vs non-peak MAPE = 60·4%), despite substantial geographic heterogeneity across states.

Forecast validation during the 2024 epidemic

Forecast outputs successfully reproduced spatial and seasonal patterns of dengue transmission during the 2024 epidemic. Predicted spatial risk patterns were consistent with observed case distributions, capturing both persistent endemic hotspots and regions undergoing rapid increases in transmission.

Seasonal forecasts further demonstrated the ability to anticipate periods of heightened risk associated with environmental conditions, particularly during rainy seasons (Fig. 5). Although some deviations were observed in areas affected by rapid and unexpected shifts in case counts, overall agreement between predicted and observed patterns indicates that the framework captured broad large-scale transmission patterns during the 2024 epidemic despite substantial variation in state-level forecasting performance.

Rainy seasons (Brazil: December–May; Panama: June–November) correspond to elevated precipitation, whereas dry seasons reflect lower precipitation. Predicted maps capture seasonal increases during rainy periods and reduced but persistent risk during dry seasons, demonstrating the model's ability to reproduce temporally dynamic transmission patterns and identify seasonally shifting high-risk regions.

Among the 10 Brazilian states for which TiDE was selected, 103 of 120 monthly observations (85·8%) fell within the central 90% predictive interval, with state-level empirical coverage ranging from 66·7% to 100% (Appendix Table D.1). All observations outside the interval exceeded the upper predictive bound. In Panama, 8 of 11 monthly forecasts from the final seasonal 6 × 6 TiDE configuration (72·7%) contained the observed case count within the central 90% predictive interval. The observed count exceeded the upper predictive bound in February, whereas October and November fell below the lower predictive bound (Appendix Table D.2).

Discussion

This study demonstrates that the environmental and epidemiological factors governing dengue forecasting differ across contrasting ecological settings. By integrating climatic, land-cover, ecological, demographic, and epidemiological data, we found that environmental predictors dominated forecast performance in Brazil, whereas recent transmission history contributed more strongly in Panama. These findings indicate that forecasting systems should be adapted to local environmental conditions rather than assuming that a common set of predictors applies across regions.

Although quantitative case-count errors were substantial during the unprecedented 2024 epidemic, spatial risk classifications remained relatively robust under independent temporal validation, suggesting that the framework may retain utility for identifying areas of elevated transmission risk even when precise case-count prediction is difficult. Substantial agreement between predicted and observed risk classifications in Brazil (κ = 0·617) and Panama (κ = 0·621) indicates that the approach captured operationally relevant risk categories rather than simply reproducing broad temporal trends. Ensemble forecasts improved predictive performance in Brazil, consistent with evidence that combining multiple modelling approaches can improve accuracy and reduce sensitivity to model-specific assumptions [19], [20]. Importantly, the ensemble also reduced MAE relative to a seasonal-naïve benchmark in both Brazil (24·4%) and Panama (29·0%), indicating that the forecasting framework provided improved absolute forecast accuracy beyond simple annual persistence, although performance gains varied by evaluation metric. Although prediction errors increased during the unprecedented 2024 epidemic, the framework continued to identify broad spatial transmission patterns and operationally relevant risk categories.

The 2024 epidemic represented an out-of-distribution forecasting scenario, in which transmission intensity substantially exceeded the range of observations available during model training. Such extreme epidemic conditions are inherently difficult for data-driven forecasting models because they require extrapolation beyond previously observed epidemiological patterns. Consequently, elevated prediction errors observed during 2024 likely reflect the exceptional magnitude of transmission rather than limitations unique to any individual modelling approach.

Predictive-interval performance further illustrated the challenges of forecasting under an unusually severe epidemic. TiDE central 90% predictive intervals achieved 85.8% empirical coverage across the Brazilian states for which TiDE was selected. For Panama, the final seasonal 6 × 6 TiDE configuration achieved 72.7% empirical coverage (8 of 11 months) during January–November 2024. Although both estimates were below the nominal 90% coverage level, most observed case counts remained within the predictive intervals during the independent evaluation period. The remaining interval misses occurred during periods of abrupt changes in transmission, indicating that forecast uncertainty may still be underestimated when epidemic dynamics shift rapidly beyond patterns represented during model development.

The forecasting performance observed in this study is broadly consistent with previous systematic reviews and dengue forecasting studies demonstrating that predictive accuracy varies considerably across geographic settings, modelling approaches, and epidemic conditions [9], [10], [12]. Similar to these studies, we found that no single modelling framework consistently outperformed alternatives and that integrating multiple environmental and epidemiological data streams improved forecasting performance. The elevated prediction errors observed during the unprecedented 2024 epidemic further highlight the challenges of forecasting outbreak conditions that extend beyond the range of historical observations used for model development.

The contrasting results between Brazil and Panama highlight the importance of geographic scale, surveillance resolution, and environmental complexity. Brazil spans 27 states and approximately 8·5 million km2, with median monthly state-level dengue counts ranging from 12 cases in Roraima to 5851 cases in Goiás. This variation suggests that dengue transmission in Brazil reflects multiple regional transmission systems influenced by distinct climatic, ecological, demographic, and land-use conditions. In contrast, Panama was analyzed as a single national unit, where temporal persistence in case counts appeared to capture much of the dominant transmission signal.

Forecast-horizon performance also differed between settings. In Brazil, the 6 × 6 seasonal TiDE configuration produced greater 2024 forecast error than the quarterly rolling configuration, with the largest errors occurring during the rapid January–June epidemic surge. In Panama, by contrast, the seasonal six-month TiDE configuration improved performance relative to quarterly rolling TiDE. These contrasting results suggest that forecast horizon, as well as model class, may need to be adapted to local transmission dynamics. More frequent updating may be advantageous in heterogeneous settings characterized by rapidly changing regional epidemics, whereas longer seasonal forecast windows may perform better where transmission exhibits a stronger coherent seasonal signal.

The importance of climatic variables identified in this study is consistent with established ecological mechanisms linking temperature, precipitation, urbanization, and land-cover characteristics to mosquito development, survival, viral replication, and human–vector contact [5], [6], [7], [8]. The contribution of land-cover characteristics, livestock density, and other land-use variables should be interpreted as indicators of broader ecological and human land-use patterns rather than direct causal drivers of dengue transmission. Livestock density likely reflects agricultural landscapes, peri-urban environments, and associated patterns of human activity that influence vector habitat suitability and opportunities for human–vector contact, consistent with previous landscape-based assessments of dengue risk [16]. Similarly, the importance of land-cover characteristics and mosquito habitat suitability reflects the influence of landscape composition on vector habitat availability and human exposure. Together, these findings suggest that environmental predictors can provide important information for dengue forecasting, particularly in settings with substantial spatial heterogeneity. This finding reinforces that environmental predictors contribute substantially to forecasting performance in environmentally heterogeneous regions.

Sensitivity testing showed that the heuristic land-cover weighting scheme was broadly stable for many classes, particularly in Brazil, but also identified classes for which categorical risk assignments were sensitive to plausible weight perturbations. Mosaic natural vegetation and cropland produced the largest substantive category changes in Brazil, whereas Panama showed greater sensitivity to low-weight forest and related land-cover classes, particularly when weights were reduced to zero and pixels consequently became unallocated. These findings indicate that the weighting scheme captures useful relative spatial structure but should not be interpreted as a uniquely identified set of ecological coefficients.

These results also indicate that there is unlikely to be a universally optimal dengue forecasting model. Instead, forecasting systems should be designed around the environmental, epidemiological, and surveillance characteristics of the region in which they are deployed. Ensemble approaches may provide the greatest benefit in environmentally heterogeneous settings such as Brazil, whereas a single high-performing model may be sufficient in settings where transmission dynamics are more temporally coherent at the scale of analysis.

Although overall agreement across Brazil was substantial, forecasting performance varied considerably among individual states. States exhibiting lower agreement or larger prediction errors generally experienced low incidence, limited variation in observed risk categories, or abrupt epidemic fluctuations during the 2024 outbreak, all of which reduce the stability of agreement statistics and increase forecasting difficulty. Conversely, states with more stable transmission patterns or consistently elevated risk demonstrated stronger agreement between observed and predicted classifications. These findings reinforce that forecasting performance depends not only on model selection but also on the epidemiological and environmental characteristics of individual regions, supporting the need for locally adapted forecasting approaches.

By relying only on historical epidemiological observations and previously available environmental covariates, the framework more closely reflects an operational forecasting scenario in which future climate observations are not yet available. Consequently, by identifying regions at elevated risk before peak transmission periods, integrated forecasting systems can support more efficient allocation of vector control resources, healthcare capacity planning, and public health communication efforts [21]. As dengue burden continues to expand across the Americas [1], forecasting approaches that integrate environmental, ecological, and epidemiological information may become increasingly important components of climate-informed surveillance and are consistent with international One Health strategies promoting integrated surveillance across human, animal, and environmental systems. Collectively, these findings support the application of a One Health approach to dengue surveillance and forecasting, recognizing that interactions among environmental conditions, vector ecology, animal populations, and human health influence transmission dynamics and should be considered within climate-informed surveillance systems [22], [23]. Our findings further demonstrate that integrating environmental, ecological, and epidemiological information provides a more comprehensive understanding of dengue transmission than considering any single domain in isolation, reinforcing the value of One Health approaches for climate-sensitive vector-borne diseases.

Several limitations should be considered. Dengue surveillance data are subject to underreporting and variability in diagnostic capacity, which may influence observed transmission patterns. Differences in surveillance resolution between Brazil and Panama limit direct comparison of model performance and may partially explain differences in model selection and predictor importance. State-level agreement statistics were calculated from only 12 monthly observations during the independent validation period; therefore, estimates of Cohen's κ and Gwet's AC2 for individual states should be interpreted cautiously, particularly where observed risk classifications showed limited temporal variation. Accordingly, individual state-level agreement estimates should be regarded as descriptive, whereas the pooled Brazil estimates provide a more stable summary of overall classification performance. Environmental predictors represent proxies for vector suitability and human–vector contact rather than direct measures of mosquito abundance or infection. Relative humidity, an established environmental determinant of Aedes ecology and dengue transmission, was excluded from the Brazil models because of substantial missingness in the available dataset but was retained in the Panama model. Future applications of the framework in settings with incomplete humidity observations could incorporate more complete estimates from alternative reanalysis products. Although missing values in retained predictors were addressed using standardized imputation procedures, the effect of imputation on forecasting performance was not independently evaluated and may have introduced additional uncertainty. The 2019 land-cover survey and 2020 mosquito-suitability and livestock-density layers were treated as static ecological covariates and therefore may not capture changes in these characteristics between their reference year and the 2024 evaluation period. Unmeasured factors, including human mobility, socioeconomic conditions, local vector control, and health system capacity, may also influence transmission. Because the 2024 epidemic substantially exceeded the range of observations available during model development, model performance may be reduced during similarly unprecedented outbreaks that require extrapolation beyond historical transmission patterns. Finally, although overall performance and risk classification showed substantial agreement at the national level, forecasting accuracy varied among individual Brazilian states, reflecting the differences in local transmission dynamics and environmental conditions. Although the standardized workflow was designed to facilitate adaptation through integration of publicly available environmental and epidemiological datasets, the framework's transferability to regions with substantially different ecological, climatic, or surveillance conditions requires further evaluation using independent validation datasets from additional dengue-endemic regions.

Building on our previously developed infectious disease forecasting framework [11], this study extends its application by demonstrating that integrating heterogeneous environmental datasets with epidemiological surveillance improves understanding and prediction of dengue transmission across contrasting ecological settings. The differing importance of environmental and epidemiological predictors between Brazil and Panama suggests that forecasting systems developed in one ecological setting may not generalize directly to another. Adapting forecasting approaches to local environmental conditions may provide a more effective pathway toward scalable early warning systems for dengue and other climate-sensitive infectious diseases.

Model implementation and selection details

All four forecasting approaches were implemented within the Darts time-series forecasting framework. ARIMA used the statsmodels backend, Random Forest used the scikit-learn backend, XGBoost used the XGBoost backend, and TiDE used PyTorch. Lag-based model inputs used the preceding 12 months of observations. Candidate configurations within each model class were compared using development-validation mean absolute error (MAE), and the configuration with the lowest MAE was retained before independent evaluation on the 2024 data.

Complete final hyperparameter values for ARIMA, Random Forest, and XGBoost were not retained in the archived analysis artifacts and therefore are not reported speculatively. For TiDE, the retained workflow documented Darts 0.35.0 and PyTorch 2.7.1, a learning rate of 1 × 10−4, and probabilistic forecasting. Brazil used quantile regression, whereas Panama used a Gaussian likelihood. Both TiDE implementations generated 100 probabilistic samples, with the 50th percentile used as the point forecast and the 5th and 95th percentiles defining a central 90% predictive interval.

Percentages represent the proportion of mapped pixels whose categorical risk classification differed from the original-weight baseline. “Unallocated” indicates pixels whose tested land-cover weight became zero. “Not observed” indicates that the land-cover class was absent from the country-specific raster.
