Probability laws associated to the independence preserving quadrirational Yang-Baxter maps - the ultimate case
Bartosz Kołodziejek, Gérard Letac, Mauro Piccioni, Jacek Wesołowski

Abstract
. A map F : 𝒳 × 𝒴 → 𝒰 × 𝒱 F\colon\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}\to\mathcal{U}\times\mathcal{V} is said to be independence preserving (IP) if there exists a pair of independent random variables ( X , Y ) (X,Y) valued in 𝒳 × 𝒴 \mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y} such that the two coordinates of ( U , V ) = F ⁡ ( X , Y ) ∈ 𝒰 × 𝒱 (U,V)=F(X,Y)\in\mathcal{U}\times\mathcal{V} are also independent. Recently, Sasada and Uozumi (2024) observed that a hierarchy of quadrirational Yang-Baxter maps gives rise to independence preserving transformations, and identified corresponding families of probability distributions. In view of the limiting properties of these IP maps, the newly defined generalized second kind beta ( GB I ​ I \mathrm{GB}_{II} ) model stands at the top of the hierarchy: for independent random variables X X and Y Y following a GB I ​ I \mathrm{GB}_{II} distribution, Sasada and Uozumi (2024) showed that when a special quadrirational Yang-Baxter map F ( α , β ) F^{(\alpha,\beta)} , parameterized by ( α , β ) ∈ ( 0 , ∞ ) 2 (\alpha,\beta)\in(0,\infty)^{2} , is applied to the pair ( X , Y ) (X,Y) , it produces another pair ( U , V ) (U,V) of independent GB I ​ I \mathrm{GB}_{II} -distributed random variables. Interestingly, the boundary cases of α ∈ { 0 , ∞ } \alpha\in\{0,\infty\} (or β ∈ { 0 , ∞ } \beta\in\{0,\infty\} ) are related to one of the Matsumoto-Yor IP maps identified in Koudou and Vallois (2012). The aim of this paper is to show that the IP property of F ( α , β ) F^{(\alpha,\beta)} uniquely identifies distributions of X , Y , U X,Y,U and V V as belonging to the GB I ​ I \mathrm{GB}_{II} family. To this end, we introduce specially designed Laplace-type transforms. First, we carefully explain the connection between the results from Sasada and Uozumi (2024) and Koudou and Vallois (2012). Next, we focus on the characterization of the second kind beta and the generalized second kind beta distributions through the IP map F ( α , ∞ ) F^{(\alpha,\infty)} . Finally, extending considerably the methodology developed for the case ( α , ∞ ) (\alpha,\infty) , we prove the characterization of GB I ​ I \mathrm{GB}_{II} distributions in the case ( α , β ) ∈ ( 0 , ∞ ) 2 (\alpha,\beta)\in(0,\infty)^{2} with α ≠ β \alpha\neq\beta , which implies uniqueness in the ultimate missing case of the quadrirational Yang-Baxter hierarchy of IP models.

2.
Hypergeometric-type Laplace transforms

For
\gamma>0
,
s+\theta,\sigma\geq 0
and a non-negative random variable
W

(2.1)

L_{W}^{(\gamma)}(\theta,\sigma,s)=\gamma^{\theta}\,\mathbb{E}\left[\tfrac{W^{\theta+s}}{(1+\gamma W)^{\theta}(1+W)^{\sigma+s}}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[(\tfrac{\gamma W}{1+\gamma W})^{\theta}(\tfrac{1}{1+W})^{\sigma}(\tfrac{W}{1+W})^{s}\right].

It is clear that
L_{W}^{(\gamma)}
uniquely determines the law of

L_{W}^{(\gamma)}(k,0,0)
for

.
The trivial identities

1=\frac{w}{1+w}+\frac{1}{1+w}=\frac{\gamma w}{1+\gamma w}+\frac{1}{\gamma w}\,\frac{\gamma w}{1+\gamma w}

imply

(2.2)

L_{W}^{(\gamma)}(\theta,\sigma,s)=L_{W}^{(\gamma)}(\theta+1,\sigma,s)+\gamma^{-1}L_{W}^{(\gamma)}(\theta+1,\sigma+1,s-1),

and

(2.3)

L_{W}^{(\gamma)}(\theta,\sigma,s)=L_{W}^{(\gamma)}(\theta,\sigma,s+1)+L_{W}^{(\gamma)}(\theta,\sigma+1,s)

for
\gamma>0
and
\sigma,\theta+s\geq 0
.

Proof of Theorem

By straightforward verification, we check that for

\displaystyle\begin{split}\tfrac{xy}{(1+x)(1+y)}=\tfrac{uv}{(1+u)(1+v)},\\

Consider

\displaystyle L_{X}^{(\alpha)}(\theta,\sigma,s)\,L_{Y}^{(\beta)}(\sigma,\theta,s)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\tfrac{\beta V}{(1+U)(1+\beta V)}\right)^{\theta}\left(\tfrac{\alpha U}{(1+\alpha U)(1+V)}\right)^{\sigma}\left(\tfrac{UV}{(1+U)(1+V)}\right)^{s}\right],\quad(\theta,\sigma,s)\in\Xi,

Note that the function on the right-hand side of (

Overview of the proof

The proof of Theorem

The argument has three main ideas.
First, the independence assumptions are translated into identities for the Laplace-type transforms

4.8
) into account, we obtain (
4.10
).

Via simple algebra from (
4.9
) and (
4.10
) we get on the set
\Xi
,

(4.21)

(\alpha^{\prime}M_{X}-\beta^{\prime}M_{V})(M_{X}-M_{U})=0

and

(4.22)

(\beta^{\prime}M_{Y}-\alpha^{\prime}M_{U})(M_{Y}-M_{V})=0

with
\alpha^{\prime}=\alpha-1
and
\beta^{\prime}=\beta-1
.

We will address only the first of these two equations. The second can be handled in a similar manner. Equation (
4.21
) implies that either

(4.23)

\alpha^{\prime}M_{X}=\beta^{\prime}M_{V}\qquad\mbox{on }\Xi

or

(4.24)

M_{X}=M_{U}\qquad\mbox{on }\Xi.

To justify this, we introduce the open set

D=\{(\theta,\sigma,s)\in\mathbb{C}^{3}\colon\ \Re(\theta)>0,\ \Re(\sigma)>0,\,\Re(\theta+s)>0,\ \Re(\sigma+s)>0\}.

For any fixed
\gamma>0
, the transform
L_{W}^{(\gamma)}
extends holomorphically to
D
. Consequently, the functions
M_{X}
,
M_{U}
and
M_{V}
, being quotients of products of such transforms, are meromorphic on
D
. Hence
F:=\alpha^{\prime}M_{X}-\beta^{\prime}M_{V}
and
G:=M_{X}-M_{U}
are meromorphic on
D
as well.
By (
4.21
), we have
FG=0
on
D\cap\mathbb{R}^{3}
. Therefore, after multiplying by a common denominator, we obtain a holomorphic identity on
D
which vanishes on the nonempty real open subset
D\cap\mathbb{R}^{3}
. Hence, by the identity theorem for holomorphic functions, this identity holds on all of
D
, and so
FG=0
on
D
. Since the ring of meromorphic functions on a connected domain has no zero divisors (see the reasoning of Section 4.2 in
[
17
]
), we conclude that either
F=0
on
D
or
G=0
on
D
. In other words, either (
4.23
) or (
4.24
) holds on
D
.
Finally, since
\Xi=\overline{D\cap{\mathbb{R}}^{3}}
, using continuity of the functions involved, we conclude that either (
4.23
) or (
4.24
) holds on
\Xi
.

4.3.
Excluding the branch
\alpha^{\prime}M_{X}=\beta^{\prime}M_{V}

Observe that, since
\alpha\neq\beta
, the above relation implies
\alpha^{\prime}\beta^{\prime}\neq 0
and denote

C(s):=\tfrac{L_{X}(1,1,s)\,L_{V}(0,0,s+1)}{L_{X}(0,0,s+1)\,L_{V}(1,1,s)}.

Then, (
4.23
) for
\theta=\sigma=0
gives

\alpha^{\prime}C(s)=\beta^{\prime}C(s-1),\quad s\in\mathbb{N}.

Iterating with respect to

, we see that

C(s)=\kappa^{s}\,C(0),\quad\mbox{where }\;\kappa:=\tfrac{\beta^{\prime}}{\alpha^{\prime}}\neq 1\mbox{ and }\kappa>0.

Thus

\tfrac{L_{X}(1,1,s)\,L_{V}(0,0,s+1)}{L_{X}(0,0,s+1)\,L_{V}(1,1,s)}=\,\kappa^{s}\,\tfrac{L_{X}(1,1,0)\,L_{V}(0,0,1)}{L_{X}(0,0,1)\,L_{V}(1,1,0)},

which can be rewritten as

\tfrac{L_{X}(1,1,s)}{L_{X}(1,1,0)}\,\tfrac{L_{V}(0,0,s+1)}{L_{V}(0,0,1)}=\kappa^{s}\,\tfrac{L_{X}(0,0,s+1)}{L_{X}(0,0,1)}\,\tfrac{{\,L_{V}(1,1,s)}}{\,L_{V}(1,1,0)}.

Then for mutually independent
X_{0},X_{1},V_{0},V_{1}
, with

\widetilde{X}_{i}\sim\frac{\tfrac{\alpha^{i}\,x}{(1+x)(1+\alpha x)^{i}}\mathbb{P}_{X}(dx)}{L_{X}(i,i,1-i)}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\widetilde{V}_{i}\sim\frac{\tfrac{\beta^{i}\,v}{(1+v)(1+\beta v)^{i}}\,\mathbb{P}_{V}(dv)}{L_{V}(i,i,1-i)},\quad i=0,1,

with

X_{i}:=\log\,\tfrac{\widetilde{X}_{i}}{1+\widetilde{X}_{i}}\quad\mbox{and}\quad V_{i}:=\log\,\tfrac{\widetilde{V}_{i}}{1+\widetilde{V}_{i}},\quad i=0,1,

we get

(4.25)

\mathbb{E}\left[e^{s(X_{1}+V_{0})}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[e^{s\left(c+X_{0}+V_{1}\right)}\right],\quad s=0,1,2,\ldots,

where
c=\log\,\kappa\neq 0
.

Note that (
4.25
) implies
X_{1}+V_{0}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}c+X_{0}+V_{1}
. Indeed,
e^{X_{i}}
and
e^{V_{i}}
,
i=0,1
, are
[0,1]
-valued random variables, so all distributions are determined by their moments. Consequently,

(4.26)

\mathrm{supp}(\mathbb{P}_{X_{1}}*\mathbb{P}_{V_{0}})=c+\mathrm{supp}(\mathbb{P}_{X_{0}}*\mathbb{P}_{V_{1}}),

which turns out to be impossible. Indeed, first note that

\mathrm{supp}(\mathbb{P}_{X_{0}}*\mathbb{P}_{V_{1}})=\mathrm{supp}(\mathbb{P}_{X_{1}}*\mathbb{P}_{V_{0}}),

since

\mathrm{supp}(\mathbb{P}_{\widetilde{X}_{0}})=\mathrm{supp}(\mathbb{P}_{\widetilde{X}_{1}})=\mathrm{supp}(\mathbb{P}_{X})\subset[0,\infty)

and

\mathrm{supp}(\mathbb{P}_{\widetilde{V}_{0}})=\mathrm{supp}(\mathbb{P}_{\widetilde{V}_{1}})=\mathrm{supp}(\mathbb{P}_{V})\subset[0,\infty).

By taking into account how the transformation
\log\tfrac{x}{1+x}
acts on
\mathrm{supp}(\mathbb{P}_{X})
and
\mathrm{supp}(\mathbb{P}_{V})
, we conclude that the supremum
u
of
\mathrm{supp}(\mathbb{P}_{X_{0}}*\mathbb{P}_{V_{1}})=\mathrm{supp}(\mathbb{P}_{X_{1}}*\mathbb{P}_{V_{0}})
is a finite number. Thus, (
4.26
) would imply
u=c+u
, which is impossible, since necessarily
c\neq 0
.

4.4.
Factorization of the ratio
L_{X}/L_{U}

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
\mathbb{N}_{0}^{2}\ni(\theta,\sigma)\mapsto h(\theta,\sigma)\in(0,\infty)
. Then, there exist two functions
A
and
B
defined on
\mathbb{N}_{0}
such that
h(\theta,\sigma)=A(\theta)B(\sigma)
if and only if, for all
(\theta,\sigma)
, we have

(4.27)

h(\theta,\sigma)h(\theta+1,\sigma+1)=h(\theta+1,\sigma)h(\theta,\sigma+1).

Proof.

\Rightarrow
is clear.

\Leftarrow
: For any fixed
\theta\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, observe that

\mathbb{N}_{0}\ni\sigma\mapsto\frac{h(\theta+1,\sigma)}{h(\theta,\sigma)}=\frac{h(\theta+1,\sigma+1)}{h(\theta,\sigma+1)}:=a(\theta)

is a constant function in
\sigma
. Therefore, for
\theta\in\mathbb{N}
,

h(\theta,\sigma)=a(\theta-1)h(\theta-1,\sigma)

and iterating this relation,

h(\theta,\sigma)=a(\theta-1)a(\theta-2)\cdots a(0)h(0,\sigma).

With
A(\theta):=a(\theta-1)a(\theta-2)\cdots a(0)
for
\theta>0
,
A(0):=1
, and
B(\sigma)=h(0,\sigma)
, the lemma is proved.
∎

Lemma 4.2
.

Assume (
4.24
). Then, for any fixed

, the function

(4.28)

h_{s}(\theta,\sigma):=\frac{L_{X}(\theta,\sigma,s)}{L_{U}(\theta,\sigma,s)},

defined on
\mathbb{N}^{2}_{0}

4.27
).

Proof.

Referring to (
4.7
), we see that (
4.24
) implies that on

(4.29)

L_{X}\,\Delta_{1}\Delta_{2}L_{X}\,\Delta_{1}L_{U}\,\Delta_{2}L_{U}=L_{U}\,\Delta_{1}\Delta_{2}L_{U}\,\Delta_{1}L_{X}\,\Delta_{2}L_{X}.

First, consider the case
\alpha\neq 1
. Plugging (
4.5
) (for both
L_{X}
and
L_{U}
) into (
4.29
), and canceling

, we get

(4.30)

\displaystyle L_{X}(L_{X}+\Delta_{1}L_{X}+\Delta_{2}L_{X})\Delta_{1}L_{U}\,\Delta_{2}L_{U}=L_{U}(L_{U}+\Delta_{1}L_{U}+\Delta_{2}L_{U})\Delta_{1}L_{X}\,\Delta_{2}L_{X}.

Adding to both sides the term
\Delta_{1}L_{X}\Delta_{2}L_{X}\Delta_{1}L_{U}\Delta_{2}L_{U}
, we get

\displaystyle\Delta_{1}L_{U}\Delta_{2}L_{U}(\Delta_{1}L_{X}+L_{X})(\Delta_{2}L_{X}+L_{X})=\Delta_{1}L_{X}\Delta_{2}L_{X}(\Delta_{1}L_{U}+L_{U})(\Delta_{2}L_{U}+L_{U}).

Therefore,

\frac{(\Delta_{1}L_{X}+L_{X})(\Delta_{2}L_{X}+L_{X})}{(\Delta_{1}L_{U}+L_{U})(\Delta_{2}L_{U}+L_{U})}=\frac{\Delta_{1}L_{X}\Delta_{2}L_{X}}{\Delta_{1}L_{U}\Delta_{2}L_{U}}=\frac{L_{X}\Delta_{1}\Delta_{2}L_{X}}{L_{U}\Delta_{1}\Delta_{2}L_{U}},

where the last equality follows from (
4.24
). By referring to (
4.4
), we thus arrive at

(4.31)

\frac{L_{X}(\theta+1,\sigma,s)L_{X}(\theta,\sigma+1,s)}{L_{U}(\theta+1,\sigma,s)L_{U}(\theta,\sigma+1,s)}=\frac{L_{X}(\theta,\sigma,s)L_{X}(\theta+1,\sigma+1,s)}{L_{U}(\theta,\sigma,s)L_{U}(\theta+1,\sigma+1,s)},

i.e., the relation (
4.27
) for the function

defined in (
4.28
), as required.

In the case
\alpha=1
, using the obvious identities

we establish (
4.31
) directly.
∎

As a consequence of Lemma
4.1
and Lemma

, there exist functions

4.5.
A refined representation of

Next, to provide more insight into the functions

and

Rewriting it as

Referring to (

From (

\frac{a_{s}(\theta+1)}{a_{s+1}(\theta)}=a(\theta)K(s)\quad\mbox{and}\quad\frac{b_{s}(\sigma+1)}{b_{s+1}(\sigma)}=\frac{b(\sigma)}{K(s)},

\displaystyle a_{s+1}(\theta)=

) we arrive at

(4.49)

(n_{4}(\sigma+s)+\alpha n_{3}(\theta))\,L_{U}(\theta+1,\sigma,s)=\alpha n_{3}(\theta)L_{U}(\theta,\sigma,s)\\
+(1-\alpha)\left\{n_{3}(\theta)L_{U}(\theta,\sigma,s+1)-\tfrac{1}{\alpha}n_{2}(\sigma)L_{U}(\theta+1,\sigma+1,s)\right\}.

Now we refer to (
4.40
), getting

(4.50)

(n_{4}(\sigma+s)+\alpha n_{3}(\theta))\,L_{U}(\theta+1,\sigma,s)=\alpha n_{3}(\theta)L_{U}(\theta,\sigma,s)\\
+(1-\alpha)n_{1}(\theta+1+s)L_{U}(\theta+1,\sigma,s+1),

that, again by the first identity of (
4.2
), written with
\theta+1
replacing
\theta
, is rewritten as

(4.51)

\left\{n_{4}(\sigma+s)+\alpha n_{3}(\theta)-(1-\alpha)n_{1}(\theta+1+s)\right\}\,L_{U}(\theta+1,\sigma,s)\\
=\alpha n_{3}(\theta)L_{U}(\theta,\sigma,s)-(1-\alpha)n_{1}(\theta+1+s)L_{U}(\theta+2,\sigma,s),

which is rewritten
as in (
4.46
), once the expressions for the
n_{i}
’s in (
4.44
) are substituted.
∎

The difference equation (
4.51
) in
\theta
for fixed
(\sigma,s)
is a second-order linear difference equation with affine coefficients. Equations of this kind were named as hypergeometric difference equations in
[
5
, Chapter III]
, where
they were defined on a region of the complex plane.
Each such equation can be transformed into its normal form, which is introduced below; cf.
[
5
, (99) and (103)]
.

Lemma 4.4
.

(i)

If
\alpha\neq 1
, the function
\ell(x)=L_{U}(x-\beta_{3},0,0)
, defined for
x\in\beta_{3}+\mathbb{N}_{0}
, satisfies the hypergeometric difference equation of the form

(4.52)

(x+2+\beta_{1}+\beta_{2})\ell(x+2)\\
-\left\{(1+\rho)(x+1)+\rho\beta_{1}+\beta_{2}\right\}\ell(x+1)+\rho\,x\,\ell(x)=0,

where

(4.53)

\rho=\frac{\alpha}{\alpha-1},\qquad\beta_{1}=b-\lambda-1>-1,\qquad\beta_{2}=\lambda-a<0,\qquad\beta_{3}=\lambda+a>0.

(ii)

If
\alpha=1
, then for
\theta\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
,

L_{U}(\theta,0,0)=\tfrac{B(b-\lambda,a+\lambda+\theta)}{B(b-\lambda,a+\lambda)}.

Proof.

(i) Equation (
4.46
) for
s=\sigma=0
is

(1-\alpha)(\theta+b+\lambda+1)L_{U}(\theta+2,0,0)\\
+\left\{(2\alpha-1)\theta+\alpha(a+b)-(1-\alpha)(1+2\lambda)\right\}L_{U}(\theta+1,0,0)\\
-\alpha(\theta+a+\lambda)L_{U}(\theta,0,0)=0,\qquad\theta\in\mathbb{N}_{0}.

which by dividing for
1-\alpha
is easily seen to have the form

(\theta+2+\beta_{1}+\beta_{2}+\beta_{3})L_{U}(\theta+2,0,0)\\
-\left\{(1+\rho)(\theta+1+\beta_{3})+\beta_{1}\rho+\beta_{2}\right\}L_{U}(\theta+1,0,0)+\rho(\theta+\beta_{3})L_{U}(\theta,0,0)=0,

turned into the form (
4.52
), once
\ell(\theta+\beta_{3})=L_{U}(\theta,0,0)
is introduced, for
\theta\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, and the replacement
x=\theta+\beta_{3}
is performed.

(ii) For
\alpha=1
we obtain a first order difference equation of the form

\displaystyle\left(\theta+a+b\right)L_{U}(\theta+1,0,0)-(\theta+a+\lambda)L_{U}(\theta,0,0)=0,\qquad\theta\in\mathbb{N}_{0}.

Under the initial condition
L_{U}(0,0,0)=1
, it has a unique solution

L_{U}(\theta,0,0)=\tfrac{(a+\lambda)^{(\theta)}}{(a+b)^{(\theta)}}=\tfrac{\Gamma(a+b)\Gamma(a+\lambda+\theta)}{\Gamma(a+b+\theta)\Gamma(a+\lambda)}=\tfrac{B(b-\lambda,a+\lambda+\theta)}{B(b-\lambda,a+\lambda)}.

∎

We refer to
(\rho,\beta_{1},\beta_{2},\beta_{3})
as the parameters of (
4.52
). For the sequel we introduce the
\rho
-difference operator

(4.54)

\delta_{\rho}f(x)=f(x+1)-\rho f(x),

that for
\rho=1
, becomes the standard difference operator.

Lemma 4.5
.

With
\alpha\neq 1
, the sequence
(\ell^{(n)},n\in\mathbb{N})
defined by
\ell^{(1)}=\ell
and
\ell^{(n)}=\delta_{\rho}^{n-1}\ell
, for
n=2,3,\ldots
has the following properties:

(i)

The function
\ell^{(n)}
satisfies (
4.52
) with parameters
(\rho,\beta_{1},\beta_{2}^{(n)},\beta_{3})
, where
\beta_{2}^{(n)}:=\beta_{2}+n-1
;

(ii)

We have for
x\in\beta_{3}+\mathbb{N}_{0}
,

\displaystyle\ell^{(n)}(x)

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{(1-\alpha)^{n-1}}\mathbb{E}\left[\tfrac{(1+\alpha U)^{n-1}}{(1+U)^{x-\beta_{3}+n-1}}\right]=\left(\tfrac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}\right)^{n-1}L_{U}(x-\beta_{3},-(n-1),n-1).

Proof.

(i) We proceed by induction. We already know that
\ell=\ell^{(1)}
satisfies (
4.52
) with the appropriate parameters. Assume that
\ell^{(n-1)}
satisfies (
4.52
) with the parameters
(\rho,\beta_{1},\beta_{2}^{(n-1)},\beta_{3})
. Then, for
x\in\beta_{3}+\mathbb{N}_{0}
, the difference between the left-hand sides of (
4.52
) at
x+1
and the left-hand sides of (
4.52
) evaluated at
x
, multiplied by
\rho
, gives

(x+2+\beta_{1}+\beta_{2}^{(n-1)})\delta_{\rho}\ell^{(n-1)}(x+2)\\
-\left((1+\rho)(x+1)+\beta_{1}\rho+\beta_{2}^{(n-1)}\right)\delta_{\rho}\ell^{(n-1)}(x+1)+\rho x\delta_{\rho}\ell^{(n-1)}(x)\\
+\delta_{\rho}\ell^{(n-1)}(x+2)-\delta_{\rho}\ell^{(n-1)}(x+1).

Thus, the above difference equation can be rewritten as

(x+2+\beta_{1}+(\beta_{2}^{(n-1)}+1))\ell^{(n)}(x+2)\\
-\left((1+\rho)(x+1)+\beta_{1}\rho+(\beta_{2}^{(n-1)}+1)\right)\ell^{(n)}(x+1)\\
+\rho x\ell^{(n)}(x)=0.

Since
\beta_{2}^{(n)}=\beta_{2}^{(n-1)}+1
, the proof of (i) is complete.

(ii) We proceed again by induction. The case
n=1
follows from the definition of
\ell^{(1)}(x)=\ell(x)=L_{U}(x-\beta_{3},0,0)
. Assume that the assertion holds for
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, and consider

\displaystyle\ell^{(n+1)}(x)

\displaystyle=\delta_{\rho}\,\ell^{(n)}(x)=\ell^{(n)}(x+1)-\rho\ell^{(n)}(x)

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{(1-\alpha)^{n-1}}\mathbb{E}\left[\tfrac{(1+\alpha U)^{n-1}}{(1+U)^{x-\beta_{3}+n}}\right]+\tfrac{\alpha}{(1-\alpha)^{n}}\mathbb{E}\left[\tfrac{(1+\alpha U)^{n-1}}{(1+U)^{x-\beta_{3}+n-1}}\right]

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{(1-\alpha)^{n}}\mathbb{E}\left[\tfrac{(1+\alpha U)^{n-1}}{(1+U)^{x-\beta_{3}+n}}\left(1-\alpha+\alpha(1+U)\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{(1-\alpha)^{n}}\mathbb{E}\left[\tfrac{(1+\alpha U)^{n}}{(1+U)^{x-\beta_{3}+n}}\right]=(\tfrac{\alpha}{1-\alpha})^{n}L_{U}(x-\beta_{3},-n,n),

where the last equality follows from the definition of
L_{U}
. This proves the representation formula in (ii).
∎

The representation of solutions to equations of the form (
4.52
) can be extracted from
[
5
, pp. 95–96]
. The formulation there is given in the complex domain. To avoid discussion of branch choices, in particular for the term
(-1)^{x-\beta_{3}}
, we give a direct proof adapted to our setting of
x-\beta_{3}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
.

Lemma 4.6
.

Consider the linear equation (
4.52
), defined for
x\in\beta_{3}+\mathbb{N}_{0}
, with
\beta_{1},\beta_{2}>-1
and
\beta_{3}>0
. The linear space of its solutions has the basis
\{\ell_{1},\ell_{2}\}
, where

(i)

if
\rho>1
,

(4.55)

\ell_{1}(x)=\int_{0}^{1}t^{x-1}(1-t)^{\beta_{1}}(\rho-t)^{\beta_{2}}dt,\qquad\ell_{2}(x)=\int_{1}^{\rho}t^{x-1}(t-1)^{\beta_{1}}(\rho-t)^{\beta_{2}}dt;

(ii)

if
\rho<0
,

(4.56)

\ell_{1}(x)=\int_{0}^{1}t^{x-1}(1-t)^{\beta_{1}}(t-\rho)^{\beta_{2}}dt,\qquad\ell_{2}(x)=(-1)^{x-\beta_{3}}\int_{\rho}^{0}(-t)^{x-1}(1-t)^{\beta_{1}}(t-\rho)^{\beta_{2}}dt.

Proof.

In both cases it suffices to prove that
\ell_{1}
and
\ell_{2}
are linearly independent solutions of (
4.52
), since the set of solutions is a two-dimensional linear space.

Denote by
\mathcal{Q}(\ell)
the left-hand side of (
4.52
) for fixed
x\in\beta_{3}+\mathbb{N}_{0}
. Elementary computations give the representation

(4.57)

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}(\ell_{i})=\int_{a_{i}}^{b_{i}}\,\tfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\,\psi_{i}(t)\,dt=\psi_{i}(b_{i})-\psi_{i}(a_{i}),

where the triplets
(\psi(t),\,a,\,b)
are of the form

(\psi_{i}(t),a_{i},b_{i})=\begin{cases}\left(t^{x}(1-t)^{\beta_{1}+1}(\rho-t)^{\beta_{2}+1},\,0,\,1\right),&\rho>1,\ i=1,\\
\left(-t^{x}(t-1)^{\beta_{1}+1}(\rho-t)^{\beta_{2}+1},\,1,\,\rho\right),&\rho>1,\ i=2,\\
\left(-t^{x}(1-t)^{\beta_{1}+1}(t-\rho)^{\beta_{2}+1},\,0,\,1\right),&\rho<0,\ i=1,\\
\left((-1)^{x-\beta_{3}}(-t)^{x}(1-t)^{\beta_{1}+1}(t-\rho)^{\beta_{2}+1},\,\rho,\,0\right),&\rho<0,\ i=2.\end{cases}

Since
x,\beta_{1}+1,\beta_{2}+1>0
, the functions
\psi_{i}
,
i=1,2
, vanish at the corresponding endpoints. Thus the functions
\ell_{i}
,
i=1,2
, defined in (
4.55
) and (
4.56
) are solutions to (
4.52
).

It remains to show that
\ell_{1}
and
\ell_{2}
are linearly independent.
If
\rho>1
then, as
x\to\infty
,

\ell_{1}(x)\sim C_{1}x^{-\beta_{1}-1},\qquad\ell_{2}(x)\sim C_{2}\rho^{x}x^{-\beta_{2}-1},

with some constants
C_{i}>0
. Since
\rho>1
we see
\ell_{1}
and
\ell_{2}
are not proportional.

If
\rho<0
, write
x=\beta_{3}+n
,
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. Then

\ell_{1}(x)>0,\qquad\ell_{2}(x)=(-1)^{n}\tilde{\ell}_{2}(x),

where
\tilde{\ell}_{2}(x)>0
. Hence
\ell_{2}(\beta_{3}+n)
changes sign with
n
, while
\ell_{1}(\beta_{3}+n)
is always positive. Thus the two solutions are not proportional.

∎

Lemma 4.7
.

Assume that
|\lambda|<\min\{a,b\}
. Then
U\sim\mathrm{GB}_{II}(-\lambda,a,b;\alpha)
.

Proof.

If
\alpha=1
, the result follows directly from Lemma
4.4
(ii): this gives, for
\theta\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
,

\mathbb{E}\left[\tfrac{1}{(1+U)^{\theta}}\right]=\tfrac{B(b-\lambda,a+\lambda+\theta)}{B(b-\lambda,a+\lambda)}=\int_{0}^{\infty}\tfrac{1}{(1+u)^{\theta}}\tfrac{1}{B(b-\lambda,a+\lambda)}\tfrac{u^{b-\lambda-1}}{(1+u)^{a+b}}du,

which implies that
U\sim\mathrm{B}_{II}(b-\lambda,a+\lambda)=\mathrm{GB}_{II}(-\lambda,a,b;1)
.

Now, we consider the case
\alpha\neq 1
.
As in Lemma
4.4
(i), let
\rho=\alpha/(\alpha-1)
,
\beta_{1}=b-\lambda-1
,
\beta_{2}=\lambda-a
, and
\beta_{3}=a+\lambda
. By assumption, we have
\beta_{1}>-1
and
\beta_{3}>0
, but
\beta_{2}<0
.

To apply Lemma
4.6
, whose assumption is
\beta_{2}>-1
, we use Lemma
4.5
to shift the second parameter from
\beta_{2}
to

\beta_{2}^{(n)}=\beta_{2}+n-1
, and fix
n\in\mathbb{N}
so that
\beta_{2}^{(n)}>-1
.

We will consider two cases
\alpha>1
and
\alpha\in(0,1)
, separately:

(1)

Case
\alpha>1
.
In this case
\rho=\tfrac{\alpha}{\alpha-1}>1
. Applying Lemma
4.5
(ii) and Lemma
4.6
(i),
we obtain for

x\in\beta_{3}+\mathbb{N}_{0}
,

\displaystyle\ell^{(n)}(x)=\delta_{1}\ell_{1}(x)+\delta_{2}\ell_{2}(x)

for some constants
\delta_{1}
and
\delta_{2}
. Note that
\ell_{2}(x)
diverges to
\infty
as
x\to\infty
. By Lemma
4.5
(ii), with
x=\beta_{3}+\theta
,
\theta\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, we have

\ell^{(n)}(\beta_{3}+\theta)=\frac{1}{(1-\alpha)^{n-1}}\mathbb{E}\left[\tfrac{(1+\alpha U)^{n-1}}{(1+U)^{\theta+n-1}}\right]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\theta\to\infty}}{{\longrightarrow}}0,

which allows us to conclude that
\delta_{2}=0
. As a consequence, as a function of
\theta\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\tfrac{(1+\alpha U)^{n-1}}{(1+U)^{\theta+n-1}}\right]

\displaystyle\propto\int_{0}^{1}t^{\theta+\beta_{3}-1}(1-t)^{\beta_{1}}\left(\rho-t\right)^{\beta_{2}+n-1}dt

\displaystyle\propto\int_{0}^{\infty}\tfrac{(1+\alpha u)^{n-1}}{(1+u)^{\theta+n-1}}\tfrac{u^{\beta_{1}}(1+\alpha u)^{\beta_{2}}}{(1+u)^{\beta_{1}+\beta_{2}+\beta_{3}+1}}du

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\tfrac{(1+\alpha u)^{n-1}}{(1+u)^{\theta+n-1}}\tfrac{u^{b-\lambda-1}}{(1+\alpha u)^{a-\lambda}(1+u)^{b+\lambda}}du,

with the substitution
t=(1+u)^{-1}
in the integral. Therefore
U
has a density proportional to

(4.58)

\tfrac{u^{b-\lambda-1}}{(1+\alpha u)^{a-\lambda}(1+u)^{b+\lambda}}\mathbf{1}_{(0,\infty)}(u),

i.e.,
U\sim\mathrm{GB}_{II}(-\lambda,a,b;\alpha)
.

(2)

Case
\alpha\in(0,1)
.
Here
\rho<0
. By Lemma
4.5
and Lemma
4.6
(ii),

x\in\beta_{3}+\mathbb{N}_{0}

(4.59)

\displaystyle\ell^{(n)}(x)=\delta_{1}\ell_{1}(x)+\delta_{2}\ell_{2}(x)

for some constants
\delta_{1}
and
\delta_{2}
.
Setting
x=\beta_{3}+\theta
,
\theta\in\mathbb{N}
, and
d_{i}=(1-\alpha)^{n-1}\delta_{i}
, for
i=1,2
, using Lemma
4.5
(ii), (
4.59
) can be rewritten as

(4.60)

\displaystyle J(\theta):=\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{(1+\alpha U)^{n-1}}{(1+U)^{\theta+n-1}}\right]=d_{1}\ell_{1}(\beta_{3}+\theta)+d_{2}(-1)^{\theta}\tilde{\ell}_{2}(\theta),\qquad\theta\in\mathbb{N}_{0},

where

\tilde{\ell}_{2}(\theta)=\int_{\rho}^{0}(-t)^{\theta+\beta_{3}-1}(1-t)^{\beta_{1}}(t-\rho)^{\beta_{2}+n-1}\,dt.

Since

\frac{1+\alpha U}{1+U}=\alpha+(1-\alpha)A\qquad\mbox{with }A:=\frac{1}{1+U}\in(0,1],

we have, writing the law of
A
as
\mathbb{P}_{A}
,

J(\theta)=\int y^{\theta}\,\mu_{0}(dy),\qquad\mu_{0}(dy):=(\alpha+(1-\alpha)y)^{n-1}\,\mathbb{P}_{A}(dy).

where
\mu_{0}
is a nonzero finite measure on
[0,1]
.

Likewise,

\ell_{1}(\theta+\beta_{3})=\int t^{\theta}\,\mu_{1}(dt),\qquad\mu_{1}(dt):=t^{\beta_{3}-1}(1-t)^{\beta_{1}}(t-\rho)^{\beta_{2}+n-1}\mathbf{1}_{(0,1)}(t)\,dt,

and after the change of variables
r=-t
,

\tilde{\ell}_{2}(\theta)=\int_{0}^{-\rho}r^{\theta+\beta_{3}-1}(1+r)^{\beta_{1}}(-\rho-r)^{\beta_{2}+n-1}\,dr=\int r^{\theta}\,\mu_{2}(dr),

where

\mu_{2}(dr):=r^{\beta_{3}-1}(1+r)^{\beta_{1}}(-\rho-r)^{\beta_{2}+n-1}\mathbf{1}_{(0,-\rho)}(r)\,dr.

By the assumptions
\beta_{1}>-1
,
\beta_{3}>0
,
\beta_{2}+n-1>-1
, both
\mu_{1}
and
\mu_{2}
are nonzero finite compactly supported measures in
(0,\infty)
.

As a consequence, (
4.60
) can be rewritten as

(4.61)

\int y^{\theta}\,\mu_{0}(dy)=d_{1}\int y^{\theta}\,\mu_{1}(dy)+d_{2}\int(-y)^{\theta}\,\mu_{2}(dy),\qquad\theta\in\mathbb{N}_{0}.

This is enough to claim that
d_{2}=0
. Indeed,
since
\mu_{j}
,
j=0,1,2
, are finite and compactly supported, their Laplace transforms can be represented by the moment series

F_{j}(z):=\int e^{zy}\,\mu_{j}(dy)=\sum_{\theta=0}^{\infty}\,\tfrac{z^{\theta}}{\theta!}\,\int\,y^{\theta}\,\mu_{j}(dy),\quad z\in\mathbb{R},\qquad j=0,1,2.

In view of (
4.61
), we get

F_{0}(z)=d_{1}F_{1}(z)+d_{2}F_{2}(-z),\qquad z\in\mathbb{R}.

Letting
z\to-\infty
, we get

F_{0}(z)\to 0,\qquad F_{1}(z)\to 0,

because
\mu_{0},\mu_{1}
are supported in
(0,\infty)
. On the other hand, since
\mu_{2}\neq 0
, there exists
\varepsilon>0
such that
\mu_{2}([\varepsilon,-\rho])>0
, and therefore

F_{2}(-z)=\int e^{-zy}\,\mu_{2}(dy)\geq e^{-z\varepsilon}\mu_{2}([\varepsilon,-\rho])\stackrel{{\scriptstyle z\to-\infty}}{{\longrightarrow}}\infty.

This is impossible unless
d_{2}=0
, which implies
\delta_{2}=0
.

Finally, changing the variable
t=\tfrac{1}{1+u}
in the integral defining
\ell_{1}(\beta_{3}+\theta)
, we obtain for
\theta\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\tfrac{(1+\alpha U)^{n-1}}{(1+U)^{\theta+n-1}}\right]\propto\int_{0}^{\infty}\tfrac{(1+\alpha u)^{n-1}}{(1+u)^{\theta+n-1}}\tfrac{u^{b-\lambda-1}}{(1+\alpha u)^{a-\lambda}(1+u)^{b+\lambda}}du.

This proves that
U
has a density proportional to
(
4.58
),
as in the previous case.

∎

4.8.
Completion of the proof and matching of parameters

At this stage, we have already shown that
U\sim\mathrm{GB}_{II}(-\lambda,a,b;\alpha)
. Thus, (
4.45
) yields
X\sim\mathrm{GB}_{II}(\lambda,a,b;\alpha)
. It remains to identify the laws of
Y
and
V
.

Due to the symmetry between
(M_{X},M_{U})
and
(M_{Y},M_{V})
in (
4.21
) and (
4.22
), we conclude that

Y\sim\mathrm{GB}_{II}(\widetilde{\lambda},\widetilde{a},\widetilde{b};\beta)\quad\mbox{and}\quad V\sim\mathrm{GB}_{II}(-\widetilde{\lambda},\widetilde{a},\widetilde{b};\beta).

Referring to (
2.9
), in view of (
2.12
), we get

\tfrac{(a-\lambda)^{(\sigma)}\,(b+\lambda)^{(s+\theta)}}{(a+\lambda)^{(\theta)}\,(b-\lambda)^{(s+\sigma)}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{LX/LU}}}{{=}}\tfrac{L_{X}^{(\alpha)}(\theta,\sigma,s)}{L_{U}^{(\alpha)}(\sigma,\theta,s)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{Lindep}}}{{=}}\tfrac{L_{V}^{(\beta)}(\theta,\sigma,s)}{L_{Y}^{(\beta)}(\sigma,\theta,s)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{LX/LU}}}{{=}}\tfrac{(\widetilde{a}+\widetilde{\lambda})^{(\sigma)}\,(\widetilde{b}-\widetilde{\lambda})^{(\theta+s)}}{(\widetilde{a}-\widetilde{\lambda})^{(\theta)}\,(\widetilde{b}+\widetilde{\lambda})^{(\sigma+s)}}.

Comparing the first and the last expression in the above sequence of equalities, valid for any
s,\theta,\sigma\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, we conclude that
\widetilde{\lambda}=-\lambda
,
\widetilde{a}=a
,
\widetilde{b}=b
, which ends the proof of Theorem
1.2
.
