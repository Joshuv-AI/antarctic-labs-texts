A Tilburg Frailty Indicator (TFI)-based frailty classification model for older maintenance hemodialysis patients: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Junwang Liu, Manman Wang, Weiwei Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundFrailty is a common clinical syndrome among older patients undergoing maintenance hemodialysis(MHD). This study aims to develop and validate a TFI-based frailty classification model for this population.MethodsData from 510 cases in three hospitals in Fujian Province were collected as the study cohort. Univariate analysis, LASSO regression, and random forest were applied to screen for frailty-related factors. Multivariate logistic regression was then used to develop a predictive model. Based on the model, a web calculator was developed. The model was evaluated by the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), calibration curve, and decision curve analysis (DCA).The AUC values were compared with the DeLong test.ResultsThe model variables included albumin, use of walking aids, sleep condition, pain score, and psychological resilience. The model demonstrated good discriminative ability, with AUCs of 0.918 (95% CI = 0.886–0.951) in the training set, 0.916 (95% CI = 0.864–0.968) in the test set, and 0.915 (95% CI = 0.858–0.972) in the validation set. All three models exhibited good calibration capabilities and were clinically applicable.ConclusionThe frailty classification model constructed in this study shows good performance in discriminative ability, calibration, and clinical applicability. It enables clinical healthcare workers to accurately identify frailty in older MHD patients, serving as a TFI-based classification tool to support frailty assessment, thereby providing a basis for routine screening.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12877-026-07715-0.

Background

End-stage renal disease (ESRD) is the irreversible terminal stage of kidney function decline caused by various kidney diseases. Patients require renal replacement therapy to maintain normal life functions [1]. Among the available treatments, maintenance hemodialysis (MHD) is the primary renal replacement modality for ESRD patients [2]. Hemodialysis can lead to significant loss of protein and bound amino acids, resulting in decreased muscle mass and strength. Additionally, pathological and physiological changes associated with ESRD may contribute to the onset of frailty. Studies have shown that the prevalence of frailty among hemodialysis patients is as high as 73% [3].

With the aging of the population, the number of older patients undergoing maintenance hemodialysis continues to rise. In China, patients aged 65 and above account for 32.19% of the MHD population [4]. These patients often have multiple comorbidities and age-related physiological function decline. Although hemodialysis extends the survival of older MHD patients, the combined effects of the disease and dialysis treatment increase the burden of frailty in older MHD patients. Studies [5] have shown that as dialysis treatment progresses, muscle strength in the limbs decreases, and pathological and physiological changes in muscle may lead to sarcopenia, thereby promoting the development of frailty. Given frailty is strongly associated with adverse outcomes in older MHD patients, makes it a new target for improving the prognosis of older MHD patients [6]. Therefore, the accurate classification and timely intervention for frailty in older MHD patients would greatly contribute to improving their current health status and reducing the occurrence of adverse outcomes.

Since Johansen et al. first drew attention to frailty in hemodialysis patients [7], research on frailty in MHD patients has grown. However, due to differences in assessment tools, populations, and regions, reported frailty rates vary widely. Previous studies have mainly focused on physical frailty in MHD patients. For example, Chinese scholars such as Chen Yan [8] and Ye Liqin [9] used the Fried Frailty Phenotype and FRAIL scale to investigate older MHD patients, revealing physical frailty rates ranging from 33.9% to 68.6%.With the evolving concept of frailty, researchers both in China and internationally have begun to focus on multidimensional frailty in older MHD patients. Chinese scholars such as Zhou et al. [10] and Zhu et al. [11] used the TFI to reveal that the prevalence of multidimensional frailty exceeds 40%, indicating a high frailty rate among older MHD patients. However, the existing universal assessment tools are primarily designed to assess the presence or severity of frailty rather than to categorize frailty promptly. Consequently, they may overlook the specific characteristics of MHD patients, failing to effectively distinguish the frail patients. Given these limitations, there is urgently need to establish a visual and clinically practical frailty assessment tool that integrates demographic variables, clinical indicators and nursing assessment data for frailty in older MHD patients, so as to improve the accuracy of frailty screening and further provide a scientific decision for target intervention.

Several studies have investigated frailty classification models in MHD patients to describe the current status of frailty and identify specific characteristics of frail patients. For instance, Gong et al. [12] involved 300 MHD patients aged 18 and above using the TFI to develop a frailty model. Guo et al. [13] included 204 older MHD patients and used the Fried Phenotype to construct a frailty model. However, the development and application of these models face dual challenges. From a methodological perspective, a recent systematic review highlighted that existing models often suffer from a high risk of bias and a lack of robust external validation [14]. From a clinical and conceptual standpoint, most models are conducted in the general adult MHD population [15–17] and focus solely on physical frailty, without systematically screening for multidimensional frailty [18, 19]. Moreover, few studies have specifically targeted older MHD patients, and most are single-center studies that lack nurse-centered implementation pathways. Furthermore, frailty status may vary across patients with different sociodemographic backgrounds, constituting a crucial social dimension of multidimensional frailty.

Therefore, this study focuses on multidimensional frailty in older MHD patients, identifies specific characteristics, and aims to develop and validate a clinically relevant frailty classification model for this population. Drawing on the conceptual structure of the TFI, this model is designed as a cross-sectional classification and screening tool that complements existing frameworks. From a clinical perspective, the frailty classification model helps nursing staff distinguish frail patients, and helps provide personalized care plans for older MHD patients with different frailty levels.

Methods

Study design and participants

This cross-sectional study utilized a dataset comprising patients aged 60 years and older who underwent maintenance hemodialysis at three tertiary Grade A general hospitals in Fujian Province. The training and test sets were derived from two hospitals (Hospital A and B) in Fujian between September 2023 and May 2024. After combining the datasets from both hospitals, a simple random sampling strategy was employed: all samples were first sorted by ID, and then a random sequence number was assigned to each sample using the sample() function in R. The first 70% of the samples were selected as the training set, and the remaining 30% served as the test set. The validation set was collected from another hospital in Fujian (Hospital C) during the same period.Participants met the following inclusion criteria: (1) age 60 years or older; (2) undergoing maintenance hemodialysis for ≥ 3 months; and (3) ability to communicate. The following exclusion criteria were applied: (1) severe physical dysfunction or other serious comorbidities precluding participation in the survey; and (2) diagnosis of severe mental illness. Sample size

Based on previous studies [16, 19, 20], the number of independent variables expected to enter the final model is 4–7, and we anticipate no more than 10 independent variables will be ultimately included. Using the logistic regression sample size estimation method [21] and following the Events Per Variable (EPV) rule, the sample size for positive events should be at least 10 times the number of independent variables. Previous studies indicate a frailty prevalence of 41.9% in older MHD patients [10]. Therefore, the minimum required sample size for the modeling group is (10 * 10) / 0.419 ≈ 239 cases. According to the 7:3 split requirement between the development and internal validation groups, the minimum required sample size for the internal validation group is 103 cases. Considering a 10% non-response rate [16], the total minimum sample size for model development and internal validation is 342 cases. This study ultimately included 420 samples from Hospitals A and B, randomly divided into 294 for the development group and 126 for the internal validation group, meeting the minimum sample size requirements.

Assessment tools and indicators

This study is based on the integrative model of frailty proposed by Gobbens et al. [22]. Variables for frailty were identified by integrating a literature review with findings from two rounds of expert panel meetings. Data for these variables were collected via face-to-face surveys using paper-based questionnaires and by reviewing electronic medical records.

General information form

This questionnaire collects basic demographic information, including: gender, age, educational level, marital status, previous long-term occupations, current working status, exercise habits, living situation, number of children, per capita monthly household income, and types of medical insurance.

Clinical data collection form

The collected clinical data include: primary disease, number of comorbidities, type of vascular access, frequent hypotension during dialysis, diastolic blood pressure after hemodialysis, dialysis vintage, number of oral medications, use of walking aids, body mass index, and biological markers, as well as smoking history and alcohol abuse history. The biological markers comprise: hemoglobin, albumin, uric acid, blood magnesium, blood potassium, blood calcium, blood phosphorus, parathyroid hormone, high-density lipoprotein, B-type natriuretic peptide, C-reactive protein, blood β2-microglobulin, urea clearance index, and urea reduction ratio.

Tilburg Frailty Indicator(TFI)

This study used the TFI to assess the frailty status of older MHD patients. The scale was developed in 2010 by Gobbens et al. [23] from Tilburg University in the Netherlands, based on an integrative model, for self-assessment of frailty in older adults. It has been translated into versions for multiple countries. This study employed the Chinese version translated by Xi Xing et al. [24], which had a Cronbach’s α coefficient of 0.686. The scale comprises 15 items across three dimensions: physical frailty (8 items), psychological frailty (4 items), and social frailty (3 items). Among these, 11 items are answered in a binary format (Yes/No). Items 1, 12, and 15 are reverse-scored, where “Yes” is scored as 0 and “No” as 1. For the remaining items, “Yes” is scored as 1 and “No” as 0. Four items use a three-category response format (Yes/Sometimes/No), with “Sometimes” also scored as 1. The total frailty score ranges from 0 to 15. The subscale scores range as follows: physical frailty from 0 to 8, psychological frailty from 0 to 4, and social frailty from 0 to 3. A total score of 5 or above is defined as indicating frailty, with higher scores representing a greater degree of frailty. Scoring typically categorizes individuals into two groups: frail (scores 5–15) and non-frail (scores 0–4). In this study, the Cronbach’s α coefficient was 0.673.

Mini-Nutritional Assessment Short-Form(MNA-SF)

We used the MNA-SF to assess patients’ nutritional status. The original Mini-Nutritional Assessment (MNA) was developed by French nutritionist Guigoz et al. [25] in 1994 for nutritional evaluation in older adults. This study adopted the MNA-SF proposed by Rubenstein et al. [26] in 2001, which consists of 6 items with a total score of 14. Based on the scoring criteria: 12–14 indicates good nutrition; 8–11 indicates potential malnutrition; ≤7 indicates malnutrition. Previous studies have shown a Cronbach’s α coefficient of 0.832 [27] for this scale, while in this study, the coefficient was 0.748.

Activity of Daily Living(ADL)scale

The ADL scale is used to assess patients’ abilities in daily life activities. Developed by American scholars Lawton and Brody [28] in 1969, the scale consists of two parts: the Physical Self-Maintenance Scale (PSMS) and the Instrumental Activities of Daily Living Scale (IADL). The PSMS includes six items: walking, eating, dressing, grooming, bathing, and toileting. The IADL comprises eight items: using transportation, meal preparation, housekeeping, medication management, laundry, shopping, using the telephone, and handling finances, totaling 14 items. Each item is scored from 1 to 4 based on the ability to perform the activity independently, with some difficulty, with assistance, or not at all. The total score ranges from 14 to 56, with higher scores indicating greater functional decline. According to the scoring criteria: a score of 14 represents normal function; scores of 15–21 indicate mild impairment; and scores of ≥ 22 indicate severe impairment. In this study, the Cronbach’s α coefficient for the scale was 0.963.

Perceived Social Support Scale(PSSS)

The PSSS was used to assess patients’ social support. The scale was originally developed by Zimet [29] and later translated and adapted into Chinese by Jiang Qianjin [30]. It is a tool designed to measure an individual’s self-perceived social support across multiple dimensions [31]. The scale consists of 12 items, covering three dimensions: family support (4 items), friend support (4 items), and other support (4 items). Each item is rated on a 7-point Likert scale (from 1 to 7). The total score of all items reflects the individual’s perceived level of social support. According to the scoring criteria: a total score of 12–36 indicates a low level of support; 37–60 indicates a moderate level of support; and 61–84 indicates a high level of support. Previous research has reported a Cronbach’s α coefficient of 0.840 for this scale [31]. In the present study, the Cronbach’s α coefficient was 0.861.

10-item Connor-Davidson Resilience Scale(CD-RISC-10)

This study used the CD-RISC-10 to measure the psychological resilience of patients. The scale was developed by Campbell-Sills [32] based on the original CD-RISC and serves as a tool for assessing an individual’s level of psychological resilience. The Chinese version translated and revised by Wang Li et al. [33] was adopted in this research. The scale consists of 10 items, each rated on a Likert 5‑point scale (0–4 points), with total scores ranging from 0 to 40. Higher scores indicate greater psychological resilience. Based on the quartile method and adjusted according to data dispersion, the scores were categorized into three groups [34]: ≤20 as low resilience, 21–30 as moderate resilience, and 31–40 as high resilience. The scale has demonstrated a Cronbach’s α coefficient of 0.952 and a test‑retest reliability of 0.75 [35]. In the present study, the Cronbach’s α coefficient was 0.944.

Athens Insomnia Scale (AIS)

The AIS was employed to measure participants’ sleep conditions. Developed by Soldatos et al. in 2000 [36], it is a self‑report instrument designed for the subjective assessment of sleep quality. The scale comprises 8 items, each rated on a 0‑3 point scale. According to the scoring criteria, a total score of 0–3 indicates no insomnia, 4–6 suggests suspected insomnia, and a score > 6 reflects the presence of insomnia. The AIS has been widely used to evaluate sleep among older adults in China. Its reported Cronbach’s α coefficient is 0.900 [37]. In the present study, the Cronbach’s α coefficient was 0.949.

Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS)

We used the HADS to assess patients’ anxiety and depression symptoms. The scale was originally developed by Zigmond and Snaith [38] in 1983 and is primarily employed for screening anxiety and depression symptoms among patients in general hospital settings. It consists of 14 items divided into two subscales: seven items evaluate anxiety, and the other seven assess depression. Each item is rated on a Likert 4-point scale (0 to 3). According to the scoring criteria, a total score of 0–7 indicates no significant symptoms, 8–10 suggests possible symptoms, and ≥ 11 reflects definite symptoms. In previous studies, the scale demonstrated a Cronbach’s α coefficient of 0.879 and a test–retest reliability of 0.945 [39]. In the present study, the Cronbach’s α coefficient was 0.911.

Faces Pain Scale-Revised (FPS-R)

The FPS-R was used to measure patients’ pain scores. Developed by Hicks et al. in 2001 [40], this scale is widely applied in pain assessment for both pediatric and older patients [41]. It features six facial expressions ranging from left to right indicating increasing levels of pain, with corresponding scores from 0 to 10. Specifically, the expressions represent “no pain,” “a little pain,” “mild pain,” “moderate pain,” “severe pain,” and “very severe pain,” scored as 0, 2, 4, 6, 8, and 10 points, respectively. Patients were asked to choose the face that best represented their pain level, and the corresponding score was recorded. Research by Hu Qingxia et al. [42] demonstrated that the FPS-R shows good concurrent validity in pain assessment.

The theoretical link between variables and the TFI

This study developed a classification model to identify frailty status using cross-sectional data. To maintain theoretical relevance and interpretability, the selection of variables was guided by the multidimensional frailty concept defined by the TFI. In other words, the variables included in the analysis conceptually align with the core dimensions of the TFI. First, in the physiological function dimension, certain variables correspond to the “physical function” and “physical performance” dimensions of the TFI, including activities of daily living and the use of walking aids. Second, in the psychological and social dimensions, variables such as depression, anxiety, psychological resilience, and social support align with the TFI’s “psychological state” and “social adaptation” dimensions.The conceptual alignment indicates that the classification model constructed in this study is theoretically consistent with the TFI’s multidimensional assessment of frailty. The variables selected comprehensively cover the three core dimensions of frailty as defined by the TFI, providing a foundation for the model to effectively distinguish between frail and non-frail individuals. This conceptual alignment reflects a distinctive feature of the study design: the model is intended as a supplementary tool for TFI, rather than a replacement. On one hand, this alignment ensures the model adheres to the TFI’s multidimensional frailty framework; on the other hand, it also suggests that the model’s performance should be interpreted in the context of its role as a supplementary tool.

Data collection

In this study, with the exception of the clinical data collection form, all questionnaires were administered face‑to‑face by uniformly trained researchers during the patients’ waiting time before dialysis. Prior to the survey, a standardized instruction was used to explain the purpose and significance of the study, and patients were asked for their consent to participate. During the interview, unified guidelines were followed for clarification and explanation. For older patients with low literacy or poor eyesight, the data were collected through a structured inquiry method, in which the researchers asked each item verbatim and recorded the responses accordingly. Clinical data for the collection form were retrospectively obtained from electronic medical records of the most recent dialysis session within the 7 days preceding the questionnaire survey. All questionnaires were double-entered independently on the same day of collection, with immediate cross-verification to ensure data accuracy.In the analysis, age, body mass index, pain score, dialysis vintage, diastolic blood pressure after hemodialysis, and biological markers were treated as continuous variables, while all other variables were included as categorical variables.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were conducted for the study variables according to their data types. Normally distributed continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (X ± S), while non‑normally distributed continuous variables were described as median (M) with interquartile range [M (P25, P75)]. Normally distributed continuous variables were analyzed using independent‑samples t‑tests, and non‑normally distributed variables were compared with the Mann‑Whitney U test. Categorical variables were examined using the chi‑square test. Count data were described as frequency (n) and percentage (%).

This study pooled data from Hospital A and B, followed by a simple random sampling approach to divide the data into training and test sets in a 7:3 ratio. Initially, univariate analysis was conducted on the combined dataset of the training and test sets, and all variables with a p-value < 0.05 in the univariate analysis were included for variable selection using both Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) regression and Random Forest methods in the training set. Pre-screening variables on the combined dataset prior to splitting was performed to reduce noise interference, enhance the identification of frailty-related predictors, and improve computational efficiency and stability in subsequent analyses. LASSO regression was performed with ten-fold cross-validation to determine the optimal lambda value, and the final lambda was chosen under the principle of retaining as many potential predictors as possible while maintaining acceptable cross-validation performance. The Random Forest method evaluated variable importance using the Gini index, and features with an importance value greater than 1 were selected. The common variables identified by both methods were included for further analysis. Multicollinearity was assessed using tolerance (TOL) and variance inflation factor (VIF). Finally, the selected common variables were incorporated into a multivariate stepwise logistic regression model. A web-based calculator was developed using the “Shiny” package in R Studio. The classification performance of the model was evaluated based on the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), calibration curves, and decision curve analysis(DCA). All statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS 26.0 and R Studio (version 4.4.3).

All tests in this study were two-tailed, and a p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. The biological markers in this study were retrospectively collected from electronic medical records. Among them, three variables—serum β2-microglobulin, urea clearance index, and urea reduction ratio—had a small amount of missing data (missing counts: 10, 11, and 11, respectively; missing percentages: 2.5%, 2.7%, and 2.7%, respectively), while no other variables had missing data. Since the proportion of missing values for these three variables was less than 3%, the missing data were considered minimal and were imputed using mean substitution.

Ethical considerations

This cross-sectional study was approved by the Biomedical Research Ethics Committee of Fujian Medical University (Approval No.: 2022 − 134). Informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Results

Participant characteristics

Older patients undergoing maintenance hemodialysis were enrolled from Hospital A and Hospital B between September 2023 and May 2024, and randomly divided into training and test sets in a 7:3 ratio. The validation set consisted of older MHD patients from Hospital C during the same period.A total of 510 older MHD patients were included, comprising 317 males (62.16%) and 193 females (37.84%). The baseline characteristics of the three datasets are presented in Supplementary Table S1, and pairwise comparisons are shown in Supplementary Table S2. As seen in Supplementary Table S2, no statistically significant differences were observed in baseline variables between the training and test sets (P > 0.05). However, between the training and validation sets, as well as between the test and validation sets, some baseline variables—including age, education level, hemoglobin, and B-type natriuretic peptide—showed statistically significant differences (P < 0.05).

To assess the consistency of baseline characteristics among the training set, test set, and validation set, we performed a chi-square test for frailty status across the three groups, as shown in Supplementary Table S3. The results revealed that the frailty incidence rates were 57.82% (170/294) in the training set, 57.94% (73/126) in the test set, and 46.67% (42/90) in the validation set, with an overall frailty incidence of 55.88% (285/510). The chi-square test indicated no statistically significant difference in the distribution of frailty incidence among the three groups (χ² = 0.620, P = 0.734). Therefore, the frailty incidence rates across the three datasets were balanced and comparable, with no significant intergroup differences observed.

Characteristics of frailty in older MHD patients: a comparative analysis

This study included a total of 420 older MHD patients aged 60 to 90 years, divided into training and testing sets. A univariate analysis performed on the combined sets revealed statistically significant differences (P < 0.05) between the frailty and non-frailty groups in the following variables: age (P = 0.001), educational level (P = 0.012), previous long-term occupations (P = 0.009), current working status (P = 0.025), per capita monthly household income (P < 0.001), number of children (P < 0.001), nutritional status (P < 0.001), albumin (P = 0.009), ADL(P < 0.001), use of walking aids (P < 0.001), sleep condition (P < 0.001), pain score (P < 0.001), serum magnesium (P = 0.015), B-type natriuretic peptide (P = 0.021), number of comorbidities (P = 0.001), frequent hypotension during dialysis (P = 0.014), number of types of oral medications (P = 0.006), depression (P < 0.001), anxiety (P < 0.001), psychological resilience (P < 0.001), and social support (P = 0.001). Screening indicators for model development

Twenty-one variables that showed statistically significant differences (P < 0.05) in the univariate analysis were subjected to LASSO regression and Random Forest algorithm in the training set, respectively. For LASSO regression, the optimal penalty parameter λ was selected via ten‑fold cross‑validation. The results indicated that the optimal λ value corresponding to the minimum error was 0.01834. Based on the “minimum criterion” principle, vertical lines were drawn at the minimum criterion (λ.min) and at one standard error above the minimum criterion (λ.1se), representing the number of variables with non‑zero coefficients.The LASSO cross‑validation curve (Supplementary Figure S1) showed that when λ = λ.min (left vertical dashed line, labeled “11” in the figure), the cross‑validation error reached its minimum, and the model retained 11 variables with non‑zero coefficients. When λ = λ.1se (right vertical dashed line, labeled “7” in the figure), the error was within one standard error of the minimum, and the model retained only 7 variables. Although the λ.1se principle aims for the “most parsimonious model,” the variables selected here were subsequently compared with those obtained from the Random Forest algorithm to identify common features. Considering the study’s objective of retaining a relatively comprehensive set of variables with stable classification ability for subsequent multivariable regression analysis—and to avoid omitting potentially important factors—we ultimately selected the 11 variables corresponding to λ.min. The LASSO coefficient path diagram is shown in Supplementary Figure S2.

Using the Random Forest algorithm, the importance of the 21 included variables was evaluated based on the Gini index in the training set. Variables with an importance score greater than 1 were selected, resulting in 15 relatively important features. The variable importance score plot for the Random Forest is shown in Supplementary Figure S3.

A total of 8 variables were consistently identified by both LASSO regression and Random Forest methods: age, previous long-term occupations, albumin, ADL, use of walking aids, sleep condition, pain score, and psychological resilience. These overlapping variables are listed in Supplementary Table S4 and visually summarized in Supplementary Figure S4.

The model exhibited good discriminative ability across the training, test, and validation sets.These AUC values may be inflated due to conceptual overlap between the independent variables and the outcome measure (TFI).

Discussion

Currently, research on frailty in older MHD patients in China is still developing. This study used the TFI to assess the frailty status of older MHD patients and to identify its influencing factors. Our findings indicate a frailty prevalence of 55.88% in this patient group, which falls within the previously reported range of 33.9%–68.6% [8, 43]. This highlights the necessity for routine frailty screening in this population.

The results of this study show that albumin level is associated with the development of frailty, which aligns with the studies by Xu Lu [44] and Xie Qiaoli et al. [45]. Low albumin is often linked to chronic inflammation, which is one of the factors contributing to weight loss and muscle loss in patients with kidney disease [46] and may also be associated with an increased likelihood of frailty. Relevant literature suggests that adequate protein intake is associated with improved nutritional status [47], and better nutritional status, in turn, is linked to a lower likelihood of frailty and improved quality of life.

In our study, we found that pain scores were inversely associated with the incidence of frailty. Tian Xiaoyu [48] pointed out a significant relationship between pain and frailty, indicating that older adults with pain are at a higher likelihood of frailty, which aligns with the findings of the present study. A cross-sectional study involving 368 community-dwelling older adults by Huang Yingying et al. [49] also demonstrated that chronic pain is associated with a higher likelihood of frailty. Similarly, a cross-sectional study by Otones et al. [50] reported that older adults with moderate to severe chronic pain have a 4.20-fold increased risk of physical frailty or prefrailty compared to those with no or mild chronic pain. As the prevalence of age-related chronic conditions such as osteoarthritis, osteoporosis, and peripheral vascular diseases increases, the incidence of chronic pain among older adults continues to rise [51]. Furthermore, Reid et al. [52] emphasized that chronic pain is often accompanied by impairments in sleep, immune function, cognitive performance, and physical mobility, as well as reduced quality of life, all of which collectively contribute to the development of frailty. These findings suggest that the association between pain and frailty warrants attention in clinical practice, and future research is needed to explore whether pain management strategies may contribute to improved frailty-related outcomes in older adults.

Our findings indicate that older MHD patients who use walking aids are 5.725 times more likely to be frail than those who do not, suggesting a strong association between walking aid use and frailty. A similar result was reported by Hou Bailing et al. [53] in a cross-sectional study of 524 community-dwelling older adults. Furthermore, Davis et al. [54] reported that walking aid use in this population is frequently accompanied by impaired balance and mobility, both of which are closely associated with frailty. These observations indicate a strong association between walking aid utilization and frailty status in older MHD patients. Future studies are warranted to determine whether targeted guidance strategies for aid users can lead to improved health outcomes or a reduction in frailty risk.

In this study, we categorized resilience into three levels and found that better psychological resilience was associated with a lower incidence of frailty in older MHD patients. Zhang Meng et al. [55] noted that older patients with common chronic diseases generally experience mild to moderate levels of self-perceived burden. Chang Xue et al. [56] further indicated that a heavier self-perceived burden is associated with greater psychological distress, which is associated with increased physical burden. These observations are relevant to understanding physical health status in this population. In a cross-sectional study conducted in Tianjin, Wang Lili [57] reported that lung cancer patients with higher levels of psychological resilience were more inclined to adopt active coping strategies and exhibited a better quality of life.Therefore, while addressing physiological ailments, healthcare professionals may consider monitoring patients’ mental health status (e.g., levels of psychological resilience), given its association with frailty status. Future studies are needed to determine whether identifying and supporting patients with low resilience can optimize coping strategies or contribute to improved health outcomes.

Our results indicate that sleep status is negatively correlated with frailty in older MHD patients, suggesting that better sleep is associated with a lower prevalence of frailty. A cross-sectional study of 220 older MHD patients showed that patients with sleep disorders had higher levels of frailty [58]. A meta-analysis by Pourmotabbed et al. [59] also indicated that shorter sleep duration is associated with frailty. Furthermore, a case-control study of 76 MHD patients revealed that sleep problems are common in this population and are associated with more severe and frequent symptom distress, significantly impacting quality of life [60]. Short sleep duration can induce low-grade inflammation [61], and dysregulation of increased inflammatory markers (e.g., interleukin-6, C-reactive protein, and tumor necrosis factor-alpha) is linked to sarcopenia [62], ultimately correlating with more severe frailty. In light of these associations, healthcare providers may consider evaluating sleep issues in older MHD patients and exploring individualized strategies—such as behavioral therapy, pharmacotherapy, or environmental adjustments—to improve sleep quality. Future studies are needed to determine whether such interventions can influence frailty risk.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. A “two-step” approach was used for feature selection: the first step involved univariate pre-screening using pooled data from the training and test sets. While this helps stabilize preliminary results when handling data and maximizes the identification of frailty-related variables, the subsequent second step of feature selection (LASSO regression and random forest) was performed solely on the training set. Nevertheless, there remains a risk of information leakage. Future studies with larger sample sizes could attempt to conduct all feature selection entirely within the training set for comparative validation.

There is theoretical overlap between the variables selected in this study and the TFI constructs. To build a theoretically interpretable model, the variables were conceptually closely related to the multidimensional constructs of the TFI. However, caution is warranted when interpreting these AUC values: given the conceptual overlap between the variables and TFI, the reported AUC is expected by design rather than reflecting independent discriminative ability.While this design enhances model interpretability, it may also introduce some identification bias: the model may be more adept at identifying individuals with frailty manifestations typical of the TFI framework, while its ability to recognize non-typical or early-stage frailty phenotypes not fully covered by the TFI may be limited. Therefore, the good classification performance of the model may partly stem from the conceptual consistency between the variables and the outcome. This design characteristic implies that the model’s generalizability to populations with different conceptual frameworks, or for identifying early-stage or atypical frailty presentations, should be evaluated with caution. However, this conceptual alignment also yields a practical benefit. The model relies on routinely collected clinical variables, whereas the TFI entails a more comprehensive assessment. Accordingly, the model functions as a supplementary screening layer, facilitating frailty stratification within the existing clinical workflow while preserving the role of the full TFI evaluation. Notably, due to the study design, no direct performance comparison — such as AUC, net reclassification improvement, or decision-analytic benefit — was performed with the TFI; therefore, no definitive conclusions can be drawn regarding the relative superiority of the two tools. Future research could incorporate variables complementary to or distinct from the TFI constructs, such as biomarkers, to develop a frailty classification tool with broader recognition capability.

Although baseline characteristics showed no significant differences between the training and test sets, statistically significant differences were observed between the validation set and the training/test sets in some baseline features. This is likely because the validation set was independently sourced from a third center completely different from those used for the training and test sets. Different medical institutions may vary in patient populations and clinical practices. Despite these differences, our model demonstrated robustness and initial generalizability in the validation set. This study provides a preliminary exploration of its potential application in similar clinical settings; however, further validation across multiple independent centers and diverse populations is required.

The prevalence of frailty did not differ significantly among the three datasets (P > 0.05), indicating relatively balanced dataset partitioning and suggesting that our results were not substantially affected by the division. In addition, this study did not perform subgroup stratification and analysis based on dimensions such as gender and age due to limited sample size, and therefore did not conduct a dedicated assessment of model fairness. Moreover, our current focus is primarily on the application of a single integrated modeling strategy, and we did not systematically compare the performance of different interpretable machine learning methods on the same dataset and task.

Participants in this study were recruited from three tertiary hospitals within the same province, and patients with severe physical or mental impairments were excluded. This may limit the generalizability of the findings. Medical resource distribution and patient characteristics may differ in other regions, and excluding severely impaired patients could affect the estimation of frailty prevalence and model accuracy. Future studies could expand the sample sources and explore analytical strategies that include severely impaired populations to enhance the model’s universality and applicability.

This study is based on cross-sectional data and aims to construct a model for classifying current frailty status. The model serves as a classification tool intended for screening and population stratification, providing a basis for identifying target groups for subsequent interventions. Its construction is based on factors associated with frailty status in a cross-sectional manner. Due to the study design, the results only reflect correlations and cannot be used to infer causality or predict future risk changes. Future prospective longitudinal studies are needed to clarify these relationships and further explore the trajectories of frailty progression and the sustained effects of corresponding interventions.

Conclusion

In summary, this study developed a frailty classification model for older MHD patients using cross‑sectional data, conceptually aligned with the TFI framework. The model identifies albumin level, use of walking aids, sleep condition, pain score, and psychological resilience as factors associated with current frailty status. Presented in the form of a web‑based tool, the model is convenient, rapid, and easy for nurses to apply. The constructed model demonstrates promising discriminative ability, calibration, and clinical applicability in the validation set, and can serve as a preliminary practical classification tool for identifying frail individuals in similar clinical settings, thereby supporting the formulation of targeted intervention strategies. Future prospective longitudinal studies are warranted to explore causal relationships underlying frailty.
