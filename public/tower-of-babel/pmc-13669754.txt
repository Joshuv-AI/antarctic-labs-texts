From cordoned consultations to cohesive care: A service evaluation of a one-stop, multi-speciality, hospice-based Duchenne muscular dystrophy clinic, UK
Authors: Anna Mistry, Amanda Brain
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDuchenne muscular dystrophy (DMD) is a multimorbid recessive genetic disorder. In 2020, a novel one-stop multi-disciplinary (MDT) DMD clinic was piloted in Jack’s Place Hospice (registered charity number 1002832), Winchester, UK. This service evaluation aimed to assess the level of National Neuroscience Advisory Group guideline adherence [NNAG, 2024], and the patient views of the clinic format.MethodsTen semi-structured interviews with patients attending the clinic were recorded, with nine subsequently transcribed and analysed using Braun & Clarke’s six-stage reflexive thematic approach.ResultsParticipants perceived that all eleven relevant NNAG guidelines were being either fully or partially met by the DMD clinic. Participants preferred both the one-stop format of the clinic, and the hospice venue compared to hospital counterparts. This was due to superior facilities, reduced appointments and perceived better MDT communication.ConclusionThe use of one-stop multi-speciality DMD clinics, utilising hospices as the forum, could improve guideline adherence, patient satisfaction, and engagement with palliative care.

Introduction

Duchenne muscular dystrophy (DMD) is a recessive neuromuscular disorder, resulting in complex multimorbidity with a median life expectancy of 28.1 years.1 Advances in corticosteroid therapy and structured cardiopulmonary management have increased life expectancy, resulting in an enlarging cohort of adult patients requiring service provision. Studies have shown ʻboth quality of life and survival are better in neuromuscular populations treated in multidisciplinary clinics’,2 suggesting this is something the NHS should strive to achieve for patients.

The Adult North Star Network (ANSN) consensus guideline sets out the primary UK standards for adult DMD care,3 with clear recommendations for multidisciplinary management of this patient cohort and integration of palliative care in routine service, provision beyond just end of life care. Additionally, the National Neurosciences Advisory Group (NNAG) released a pathway in 2024, describing what good treatment should entail for patients at all stages of a neuromuscular diagnosis.4 It suggested 14 key quality standards, including targets such as coordinated follow-up between levels of care, adequate opportunity to discuss advanced care plans/other palliative needs, and specialist psychological support.

Current published literature contains remarkably limited descriptions of the different DMD clinic service models operating in the UK, but it is understood that adult care is mainly delivered via 28 specialist regional neuromuscular centres participating in the ANSN.3 A 2014 international survey called CARE-NMD5 found variation and inconsistencies in the provision of guideline recommended care for adults with DMD in the UK. Whilst this survey predates ANSN/NNAG guidelines, multidisciplinary care was still recommended by international consensus guidelines prior to this time period.

Broader neuromuscular disease outpatient service models are better described in the literature, with three models well reported. The first is a ʻhub and spoke’ style whereby a regional neuromuscular centre provides co-ordination of care through primary, secondary and tertiary services,6 similar to the ANSN. The second model is closer to a ʻone-stop’ style clinic, whereby the clinic is attended by multiple MDT members, such as the John Walton Muscular Dystrophy Research centre7 or UCLH’s Neuromuscular Complex Care Centre.8 A third described model is an ʻintegrated care’ model whereby a central neuromuscular team work collaboratively with linked services such as cardiology/respiratory/gastroenterology, for example the Atkinson Morley Neuromuscular Centre.9 Despite the establishment of these different service models, evaluation/comparison of them is not yet publicly available, and neither is assessment of how well palliative care is integrated into these models post ANSN consensus guideline publication, for patients with DMD in particular.

In January 2020, a novel one-stop multi-speciality DMD outpatient clinic was piloted in Jack’s Place Hospice (registered charity number 1002832), Winchester (UK). All patients over the age of 18 in the region were eligible for the clinic, regardless of whether they were also eligible for hospice services. Historically, adult patients in the region were cared for under the Wessex network service, all of whom have subsequently accepted their invitation to move over to the new DMD clinic. Additionally, all patients transitioning from paediatric services in the region have accepted their invitation to attend the new DMD clinic since its formation.

The clinic enables patients to see specialist clinicians from cardiology, neurology, palliative care, as well as allied healthcare professionals from physiotherapy, cardiac physiology and occupational therapy, all within one day. This is different from the currently operating one stop style neuromuscular clinics, due to a broader range of specialities present and the clinic operating within one day without inpatient stay. The system used to achieve a one-stop service is a staff carousel model, whereby each patient has their own room (in which they remain), and staff rotate between the rooms, taking turns to consult with the patient. As a general rule, patients see all members of the MDT at every routine appointment at the clinic, and when it is felt beneficial to the clinicians (eg, when breaking bad news), patients can be seen jointly. Occasionally, it is required for certain patients to be brought back for a follow-up with an individual MDT speciality outside the standard schedule.

This clinic was a collaborative effort, with Jack’s Place charity funding palliative care input (including the doctor, clinical nurse specialist, facilities at a reduced cost and any palliative care follow-up appointments for symptom control and advance care planning), and University Hospital of Southampton funding all other specialities present. To our knowledge, this is the first adult clinic of its kind,10 but is a similar model to those currently used in paediatrics across the NHS where patients see neurology, cardiology and respiratory teams within one clinic visit. The clinic was designed in response to patient feedback highlighting substantial hurdles when attending hospital outpatient appointments, and aims to provide more holistic care, encourage the young men to engage with palliative care services, and subsequently has also strived to meet the 2024 NNAG guidelines.

The primary aim of this study was to assess patient perspectives on the level of clinic adherence to the NNAG guidelines, with a secondary aim of exploring patient perceptions and experiences of the clinic model and location.

Methods

This service evaluation assessed the patient’s perception of levels of adherence to eleven of the NNAG guidelines, with three excluded due to being under the remit of respiratory (number 4 – ʻassess respiratory function’, 5 – ʻprovision of non-invasive ventilation’) or beyond the scope of the Jack's Place clinic (number 7 – ʻassess appropriateness and discuss gastrostomy in those with bulbar problems’).4 Respiratory care is a crucial part of DMD management; however, due to staffing capacity and specialist equipment, it was not possible for the local respiratory service to be present in the new clinic. Similarly, although the palliative care clinicians present at the clinic identify bulbar issues and initiate discussions prior to referral on to gastroenterology for consideration of gastrostomy, medical gastroenterologists are not currently present as part of the clinic MDT, and not every patient requires specialist gastroenterology input regularly. In addition to the eleven NNAG guidelines, the evaluation also aimed to explore any other benefits or negatives of an outpatient service for individuals with DMD being provided as a one-stop style MDT clinic, in a hospice as the forum.

As this was a service evaluation, formal ethics approval and written consent was not required. However, the method of consent was given significant consideration as the topics discussed in the interviews were of a sensitive nature. Due to neuromuscular dystrophy, this population was not able to reliably write a signature, so a pragmatic decision was made to gain consent via recorded verbal confirmation at the beginning of each interview.

The eligibility criteria were:1.A formal diagnosis of Duchenne muscular dystrophy (as opposed to Becker Muscular Dystrophy)2.Have attended at least one clinic appointment at the new service3.Not experiencing severe acute psychological distress such that an interview might be unsafe

This left 25 eligible participants at the time of recruitment.

The participants were recruited via telephone/email invitation, with 10/25 responding. Reasons for non-response are not known currently. Ten face-to-face interviews were subsequently conducted in participant homes, between March and September 2025. All interviews were conducted by the same researcher (AM), an individual who was not part of the clinic MDT and not known to any of the participants previously, but had previous experience in conducting qualitative interviews. Each interview lasted between 30 and 90 min; the length being guided by how in-depth each participant wished to discuss the questions, with no definitive cut-off time. The researcher did not complete field notes due to the risk of disrupting participant openness, given the sensitive topics discussed, but did keep a reflexive journal. Participant 2′s transcript had to be abandoned, due to poor recording quality making it challenging to transcribe, leaving nine transcripts to be analysed. Interviews were conducted either one-to-one, or with a parent/s present to accommodate participant wishes. We used a semi-structured framework, available in the appendix. This method of data collection was most appropriate, as it allowed the participants to discuss sensitive topics in-depth and explore their lived experiences. No repeat interviews were conducted.

Interviews were recorded, transcribed verbatim, anonymised, then deleted post-accuracy check. The transcripts were analysed following Braun & Clarke’s six-stage reflexive thematic approach.11 This involved multiple initial read-throughs of the transcripts to allow data familiarisation, subsequent manual descriptive coding (done by the interviewer), further iterative coding and refinement, followed by development of themes as they emerged from the coding. Repeated reviews of the coding/themes were undertaken to ensure they accurately represented the transcripts. Data saturation was felt to be achieved when no new themes emerged from interviews.

Alongside general iterative theme development, assessment of whether each included NNAG guideline was being ʻfully’ adhered to, ʻpartially’ adhered to, or ʻnot adhered to/unknown’ was inferred from the participant narratives. The content and tone of responses suggested a guideline had ʻfull adherence’ (in the eyes of the participant) when their narrative implied they had received the care in the NNAG consistently and comprehensively. ʻPartial adherence’ was applied when their response indicated they had received the care, but inconsistently, partially or with limitations. ʻUnknown or non-adherence’ was assigned when their response indicated they were not aware of having received the care at all, or their reply was not relevant to the question. One researcher undertook the coding and analysis, with a second researcher independently reviewing a sample of the transcripts, their coding and analytical interpretations to strengthen credibility.

This service evaluation is reported in accordance with the SQUIRE 2.0 and COREQ checklist guidelines. Theme: the benefits of one-stop clinics

Participants appreciated the one-stop design of the clinic, compared to previous systems requiring multiple hospital outpatient appointments, and this was for multiple reasons. However, it is important to note that lack of gastroenterology and respiratory presence limits the comprehensiveness of the one-stop model.

7 participants explained having their appointments all within one day at one clinic considerably eased the burden of managing DMD.ʻAnd it's in some ways less stressful for me as a person, because having to go to like a hundred different hospitals for a hundred different things kind of takes up your life and you and it's very much at a certain time of year you're just constantly going to hospital. So, it's quite nice to have it all together’ – P8

Discussion

The findings of this evaluation indicate that the Jack’s Place DMD clinic is performing to a high standard. The participants felt all of the eleven NNAG guidelines that were assessed were being either partially or fully met, and whilst there isn’t a direct comparison to the adherence met by the corresponding hospital outpatient departments that the participants attended prior to the commissioning of the Jack’s Place clinic, it’s clear the new clinic is performing well.

In addition to the perceived strong NNAG guideline adherence, this evaluation proposes that hosting multi-disciplinary clinics in a hospice (with palliative care clinicians present) may have the potential to foster improved engagement with palliative care among individuals living with DMD. Several themes that emerged in data collection suggest this.

Firstly, all participants explained that the hospice is an ideal venue for appointments, given its facilities are designed to accommodate patients living with disability. This was extremely important to the participants, freeing them from the significant physical challenges they face when attending hospital. The less burdensome experience of attending a hospice, in comparison to a hospital, may foster normalisation and familiarity of a facility associated with palliative care, in a non-crisis context. Positive reinforcement of being present in a hospice (several participants enjoyed the hospice environment) subsequently may change patient perspectives on palliative care services, which could increase the chances of them being open to engage with palliative care clinicians. In fact, one participant vocalised directly that prior to attending the DMD clinic in Jack’s Place, he associated hospices solely with end-of-life care, but via the exposure he received to the hospice as a result of the clinic, he now sees it as a less frightening environment, in place to help support his needs at all stages of his disease.

Secondly, participant’s comments indicate that some may not have sought out palliative care services in the absence of the new DMD clinic. Notably, participants perceived that the clinic had 100% adherence to the palliative care orientated NNAG guideline (guideline 9) in this evaluation. Previous research has found many young men with DMD recall no meaningful conversations about palliative care with doctors, and assumed clinicians would be reluctant to have these discussions.12 Despite this, palliative input is particularly important for these patients, given the high symptom burden13 and the well-established psycho-social burdens of living with DMD.14, 15 As part of the schedule of the new DMD clinic, patients see a dedicated palliative care clinician at every appointment, meaning they are regularly offered conversations around symptom control and advanced care plans. Whilst we don’t currently have a direct comparison to how early/regularly these discussions are had when patients are under the care of hospital outpatient clinics only, it seems reasonable to assume the provision of a dedicated appointment slot to discuss issues with a palliative care clinician could foster more early, proactive management of patient needs, as well as reduce pressure on other medical specialities to have such emotionally charged and complex discussions. In a neuromuscular condition where symptom burden is expected to increase over time, this early input may reasonably improve the holistic care of patients living with DMD.

The implications for NHS commissioning are always important when assessing a new model of service provision. This DMD clinic is currently not formally commissioned as a new separate entity, instead it has been created via a proactive group of MDT members choosing to rearrange their work to facilitate their services being provided in this new, patient orientated format. Therefore, a suggestion of this service evaluation is that MDT DMD clinics should perhaps be formally commissioned, given the potential for these to foster strong NNAG guideline adherence and an improved patient experience.

Limitations/future research

Whilst every effort has been made to ensure this service evaluation is reliable, some limitations are present. Firstly, this is a self-selected sample, which risks the participants being an unusually proactive and confident demographic and it is not possible to know with confidence if this is a representative sample. Secondly, there was some inconsistency in how interviews were conducted, with 4 interviews done alone, and 5 with parent/s present. Whilst this didn’t alter the interview framework used, it could have caused subtle dynamic shifts, with the potential to alter how participants responded to the questions. The interviewer was an individual who was not known to any of the participants, and was not affiliated with the clinic. This hopefully allowed the participants to speak freely without any fear of their responses altering their relationship with the clinic staff, but a potential limitation is a lack of familiarity with the interviewer, hindering open discussions. Additionally, given this is an evaluation of a single-centre service, without a comparator group, the results have reduced generalisability, but they are still transferable. In an attempt to minimise participant burden, transcripts/findings were not returned to participants for verification, and whilst analytical rigour was maintained throughout, lack of validation is still a potential limitation. During data analysis, only one researcher performed the coding/theme development, but a second researcher checked the analysis of a subset of transcripts to improve credibility. Finally, a limitation of all qualitative research is subjectivity and as per the interpretivist stance, the researchers recognise their part in constructing the data obtained when analysing the narratives. The use of qualitative research in this service evaluation means the results are an analytical interpretation of the participants experiences, rather than an objective measure of clinic performance.

A future project exploring palliative care engagement in depth among patients attending a one-stop hospice-based clinic, directly compared to a traditional hospital outpatient model, would be useful to better understand the benefits this service evaluation has suggested. It would also be useful to re-perform this service evaluation in hospital-based clinics, in order to evaluate whether there is a difference in guideline adherence between the two forms of DMD service provision.
