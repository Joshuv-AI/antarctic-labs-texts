NUP98 fusion proteins and KMT2A-MENIN antagonize PRC1.1 to drive gene expression in AML
Authors: Emily B. Heikamp, Cynthia Martucci, Jill A. Henrich, Dana S. Neel, Sanisha Mahendra-Rajah, Hannah Rice, Daniela V. Wenge, Florian Perner, Yanhe Wen, Charlie Hatton, Scott A. Armstrong
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Graphical abstract

INTRODUCTION

Trithorax group (TrxG) and Polycomb group (PcG) proteins mediate functionally opposing chromatin alterations and may dynamically compete for transcriptional control of developmental genes.1–6 The biochemical basis for differentiation-induced epigenetic silencing of stem cell-associated genes through this mechanism is not entirely understood. Disruption of TrxG and PcG activity resulting in altered chromatin organization and differentiation arrest has been implicated in a variety of cancers.7,8 One such mechanism involves cancers harboring chromosomal translocations that produce oncogenic fusion proteins, which may hijack TrxG or PcG proteins to promote a stem cell-associated gene expression program.9–12 Chromatin-targeted therapies that disrupt the activity of oncogenic fusion proteins and drive differentiation in cancer have demonstrated promising therapeutic potential.13–16

Aberrant activity of the canonical polycomb repressive complexes (PRC1 and PRC2) in cancer has been well described,7,8 but less is known about variant polycomb complexes, which play a critical role in fine-tuning the transcriptional control of differentiation-associated genes.17–19 The variant non-canonical PRC1.1 is essential for allowing hematopoietic stem cells to escape pluripotency through epigenetic repression of HOX genes.20 The PRC1.1 complex is composed of BCL6 corepressor (BCOR)/BCOR-like 1 (BCORL1), lysine demethylase 2B (KDM2B), Polycomb group ring finger 1 (PCGF1), and the E3 ubiquitin ligase ring finger protein 2 (RNF2). PRC1.1 binds to chromatin in part via the C-X-X-C motif of KDM2B at CpG islands of developmentally regulated genes, where it deposits the repressive histone modification H2aK119Ub.21–24 Recent work has demonstrated an essential role for H2aK119Ub in stabilizing the activity of canonical PRC1 and PRC2, which deposits H3K27Me3, leading to further enforcement of an epigenetically silenced chromatin state.25

The role of PRC1.1 in cancer may be context specific, depending on the tissue of origin and the neomorphic functions of oncofusion proteins, which may either agonize or antagonize PRC1.1 activity. For example, in synovial sarcoma, the oncogenic activity of the SS18-SSX fusion protein relies on PRC1.1 for its chromatin localization and aberrant transcriptional activation.10,26 Loss of PRC1.1 activity results in the eviction of the SS18-SSX fusion protein from chromatin and global loss of H2aK119Ub.26,27 By contrast, in acute myeloid leukemia (AML), PRC1.1 may function as a negative regulator of pro-leukemogenic genes. Pathogenic loss-of-function variants in the PRC1.1 complex members BCOR and BCORL1 are correlated with chemoresistance and poor prognosis in AML.28–30 However, PRC1.1 may have a unique role in a subset of AML harboring rearrangements involving the mammalian TrxG homolog lysine methyltransferase 2A (KMT2A; also known as mixed lineage leukemia 1 [MLL1]), where PRC1.1 may be a molecular dependency.9,31,32 This paradigm seems at odds with the evolutionarily conserved role of PcG in silencing HOX genes during development and differentiation1,2,20,33 since KMT2A-rearranged (KMT2A-r) leukemias are dependent on aberrant HOX/MEIS expression to sustain leukemogenesis.34 It remains unclear whether PRC1.1 has a unique function in KMT2A-r leukemias and whether this paradigm can be extended to other leukemias driven by a stem cell-associated HOX/MEIS gene expression program.

We sought to understand the role of PRC1.1 in a HOX/MEIS-driven oncofusion protein leukemia that is dependent on wildtype KMT2A function rather than KMT2A fusion proteins. We have shown that KMT2A and its binding partner Menin (encoded by the Multiple Endocrine Neoplasia 1 gene [MEN1]) are essential in Nucleoporin 98-rearranged (NUP98-r) leukemias, which are also driven by a HOX/MEIS gene expression program.14,35 NUP98-r leukemias produce NUP98 fusion proteins (NUP98-fps), in which the N terminus of NUP98 is fused to various C-terminal partners to drive AML.36 NUP98-fps colocalize and may interact with the KMT2A-Menin complex at the promoters of pro-leukemogenic genes, where they cooperate to activate transcription and sustain self-renewal.35 We showed that small-molecule inhibitors that disrupt the Menin-KMT2A interaction evict the NUP98-fp-Menin-KMT2A chromatin complex from select loci, which suppresses pro-leukemogenic gene expression and induces differentiation of leukemia.14 Several small-molecule Menin-KMT2A inhibitors are currently in clinical trials for relapsed/refractory NUP98-r, KMT2A-r, and NPM1-mutant leukemias (ClinicalTrials.gov: NCT04065399, NCT04067336, NCT04811560, and NCT04988555) based on pre-clinical evidence that these inhibitors induce leukemia cell differentiation and eradicate disease.13–16,37,38

Using a bespoke epigenetic CRISPR screen, we identify non-canonical PRC1.1 as the chromatin complex that is essential for transitioning to an epigenetically repressed chromatin state and the differentiation of NUP98-r leukemia cells. We employ functional genomics, nascent transcriptomics, and targeted degradation of oncogenic NUP98-fps to temporally and functionally define the chromatin-based biochemical events that lead to epigenetic repression of the HOX/MEIS gene expression program. Our studies reveal a dynamic interplay between the NUP98-fp-Menin-KMT2A complex and PRC1.1, as both complexes colocalize at the promoters of actively transcribed pro-leukemogenic genes in NUP98-r leukemia. Using CRISPR-Cas9 to genetically inactivate each PRC1.1 component, our results underscore a critical role for PRC1.1 in repressing the transcription of HOX/MEIS1 in NUP98-r leukemia, in association with the deposition of histone modifications during transition to an epigenetically repressed chromatin state.

RESULTS

Non-canonical PRC1.1 is required for silencing key pro-leukemogenic genes upon loss of the Menin-KMT2A complex

Small-molecule Menin-KMT2A inhibitors can destabilize oncofusion protein chromatin complexes, resulting in the repression of pro-leukemogenic genes and the induction of differentiation. The mechanism by which Menin-KMT2A inhibition results in epigenetic silencing of stem cell-associated genes and induction of differentiation is poorly understood. To gain insight into which chromatin complexes are involved in this process, we performed a bespoke CRISPR-Cas9 screen targeting 616 epigenetic regulators in the presence of the Menin-KMT2A inhibitor VTP50469 (an analog of SNDX-5613 in phase 2 clinical trials) (Figure 1A). To address this question in a leukemia that is dependent on wild-type KMT2A, we performed the screen in murine lineage−, c-kit+, Sca-1+ (LSK) cells that were immortalized by retroviral expression of the NUP98-HOXA9 oncofusion protein, as described.14 These NUP98-r leukemia cells were engineered to constitutively express the CRISPR-Cas9 enzyme. The results of this screen confirmed our previous findings that the Menin-KMT2A complex is a therapeutic vulnerability in NUP98-r leukemia and revealed novel molecular dependencies in this aggressive disease (Table S1). For example, chromatin complexes with negative beta scores in the screen included the KAT6A/MOZ, KAT7/HBO1, DOT1L, PAF-1, and CAF-1 complexes (Figure 1B). Among the complexes with positive beta scores that resulted in enhanced growth of NUP98-r leukemia cells under DMSO conditions and resistance to Menin-KMT2A inhibition under VTP50469 conditions, we identified members of non-canonical PRC1.1 as the top hits in our screen (Figure 1B). Each PRC1.1 complex member that was included in the screen had a positive beta score, indicating that loss of each member was sufficient to enhance leukemia cell growth, albeit to varying degrees. In the VTP50469 arm of the screen, the beta score for most PRC1.1 members was higher compared to DMSO, suggesting that under the selective pressure of Menin-KMT2A inhibition, NUP98-r cells lacking PRC1.1 had a distinct survival advantage (Figure 1C).

We hypothesized that PRC1.1 may play a role in the epigenetic silencing of pro-leukemogenic genes upon differentiation of NUP98-r leukemia cells upon Menin-KMT2A inhibition (Figure 1D). To validate the results of our CRISPR screen and gain further insight into how the loss of PRC1.1 might influence leukemia cell fitness, we engineered NUP98-r leukemia Cas9 cell lines14 to express sgRNA to individually inactivate each component of PRC1.1. Cells expressing PRC1.1 single guide RNA (sgRNA) also express an RFP reporter, allowing us to compare the relative fitness of PRC1.1-deficient, RFP+ cells to unedited RFP− cells in the same well of a tissue culture vessel in a CRISPR competition assay. While cells expressing a luciferase sgRNA had no selective advantage, cells harboring sgRNA targeting each component of PRC1.1 had a relative survival advantage compared to unedited cells, leading to their expansion over time (Figure 1E), a phenotype that may be driven in part by the upregulation of select Hox cluster genes and Meis1 (Figure S1A). This phenotype was most notable for two components of the PRC1.1 complex—Bcor and Pcgf1. Pathogenic variants in BCOR and BCORL1 have been reported in human leukemias and are associated with chemotherapy resistance and poor prognosis.28–30 Mechanistically, loss of BCOR and BCORL1 function results in dysfunction of the entire PRC1.1 complex, as BCOR/BCORL1 are no longer able to bind to PCGF1, which links to the catalytic RING1A/RNF2 enzymatic core of PRC1.1.39

To understand how loss of PRC1.1 might confer resistance to Menin-KMT2A inhibition, we sorted NUP98-r, PRC1.1-component-deficient, RFP+ cells to generate isogenic CRISPR cell lines that enabled us to interrogate each component of the complex individually. We treated each cell line with an escalating dose curve of VTP50469. Treatment of NUP98-r leukemia cells harboring a luciferase sgRNA confirmed our previous findings that NUP98-r leukemias are sensitive to Menin-KMT2A inhibition, with an IC50 in the nanomolar range.14 However, loss of each component of PRC1.1 rendered NUP98-r leukemia cells relatively resistant to VTP50469 (Figure 1F). Moreover, PRC1.1-deficient cells treated with VTP50469 demonstrated impaired upregulation of the myeloid differentiation marker CD11b, most notably in cells lacking the non-catalytic components of the complex, including Bcor, Bcorl1, Kdm2b, and Pcgf1. We observed the most striking resistance phenotype in Pcgf1 sgRNA cells, which demonstrated robust proliferative capacity even when treated with 5 μM VTP50469 for 96 h. Beyond their growth advantage, cells lacking Pcgf1 failed to phenotypically differentiate upon Menin-KMT2A inhibition (Figure S1B).

To elucidate how PRC1.1 might regulate the chromatin state upon loss of Menin-KMT2A, we examined PRC1.1 localization and histone modifications in NUP98-r leukemia cells using chromatin immunoprecipitation followed by sequencing (ChIP-seq). We found that the Menin-Kmt2a chromatin complex and the PRC1.1 complex (Bcor, Kdm2b, and Rnf2), occupied similar regions on chromatin around the transcription start site (TSS) of actively transcribed genes (Figure 2A), consistent with previous reports in KMT2A-r leukemia.9 We found that Menin-Kmt2a inhibition resulted in global loss of Menin and select loss of Kmt2a at key pro-leukemogenic genes whose expression is dependent on the NUP98-fp-Menin-Kmt2a complex (Figures 2A and S2A).14 At a subset of loci where Kmt2a was lost with VTP50469 treatment, we found that Menin-Kmt2a inhibition led to remodeling of the chromatin landscape from an active to a repressed state. Specifically, at select loci such as Meis1, we found that treatment with VTP54069 led to (1) loss of the activating histone modification H3K27ac, (2) gain of the repressive histone modifications H2aK119Ub and H3K27Me3, and (3) accumulation of PRC1.1 components such as Bcor, Kdm2b, and Rnf2 (Figures 2A, S2B, and S2C). In particular, the greatest accumulation of PRC1.1 and repressive histone modifications was observed spreading into the gene body of loci such as Meis1. At loci where Kmt2a is retained and gene expression is relatively unperturbed upon treatment with VTP50469, such as the HoxA cluster, we did not observe increased PRC1.1 activity or occupancy, nor did we observe these changes genome wide (Figures 2A and S2A). Loss of Kmt2a chromatin occupancy at transcriptionally sensitive genes was not accompanied by loss of the H3K4Me3 histone modification (Figure S2D), consistent with what we have previously reported.14 These results suggested that PRC1.1 may play a role in epigenetic repression of select, transcriptionally sensitive pro-leukemogenic gene expression upon loss of Menin-Kmt2a.

Next, we examined NUP98-fp target genes whose expression is dependent on the Menin-KMT2A complex in isogenic NUP98-r CRISPR cell lines lacking each component of the PRC1.1 complex. At loci such as Meis1 and Eya1, we found that treatment with VTP50469 led to downregulated gene expression in PRC1.1-component-deficient cells. However, in cells lacking Pcgf1, expression of Meis1 and Eya1 remained high compared to the sgluciferase control (Figure 2B). Next, we examined Meis1 and Eya1 gene expression in NUP98-r leukemia cells lacking Pcgf1 over a dose range of VTP50469. We found that loss of Pcgf1 resulted in higher Meis1 and Eya1 expression in the DMSO control and that Menin-Kmt2a inhibition failed to fully suppress gene expression, despite treatment with micromolar doses of VTP50469, in the absence of Pcgf1 (Figure 2C). These results are consistent with the resistance phenotype of cells lacking Pcgf1 to Menin-Kmt2a inhibition (Figure 1F) and underscore the essential role of Pcgf1—a core, non-redundant component that links the catalytic core to the chromatin-binding module—in the function of PRC1.1.

Degradation of NUP98-fp results in loss of an active chromatin landscape and silencing enforced by non-canonical PRC1.1

To gain further insight into how the NUP98-fp-Menin-KMT2A complex might antagonize PRC1.1 activity, we engineered a system to directly inactivate NUP98-fps through targeted protein degradation. We transformed mouse LSK cells with NUP98-fps that were tagged with hemagglutinin (HA) and a modified version of the FK-506 binding protein (FKBP12F36V), rendering the NUP98-fp a substrate for dTAG-13-induced proteasomal degradation via the Cereblon E3 ligase.40 We have previously shown that murine LSK cells transformed by this degradable NUP98-fp give rise to leukemia in vivo in primary recipients and that leukemogenesis is accelerated upon secondary transplantation.14 Upon in vitro treatment with 500 nM dTAG-13, FKBP12F36V-tagged NUP98-fp degradation is detected starting at 15 min, and the HA-tagged protein is undetectable by immunoblot within 24 h (Figure 3A). We confirmed the rapid kinetics of NUP98-fp degradation using intracellular staining and flow cytometry (Figure 3B). We took advantage of this inducible system to investigate the immediate transcriptional changes that occur upon NUP98-fp degradation using nascent transcriptomics with thiol(SH)-linked alkylation for the metabolic sequencing of RNA (SLAM-seq) (Figure 3C).41 We found that genes that display a rapid downregulation of transcriptional activity upon dTAG-13 treatment—Hox cluster genes, Meis1, Eya1, and Pbx3, for example—are stem cell-associated genes, which have been reported to be essential drivers of NUP98-r leukemogenesis35,42,43 (Table S2).

We hypothesized that the loss of active transcription at key oncogenes was associated with loss of an active chromatin landscape. We used ChIP-seq to understand how degradation of the NUP98-fp impacted Kmt2a chromatin occupancy and activated histone modifications in cells treated with dTAG-13 for 24 h, as this is the earliest time point at which the NUP98-fp is undetectable by immunoblot. ChIP-seq revealed genome-wide loss of the HA-tagged NUP98-fp, but Kmt2a occupancy and H3K27Ac modifications at the TSS were not globally impacted (Figure 3D). Rather, we found that a subset of loci that display the greatest loss of NUP98-fp occupancy were, in large part, the same genes that displayed loss of Kmt2a and H3K27Ac; these same genes were also downregulated in our SLAM-seq studies (Figures 3E and 3F). For example, the HoxA and HoxB clusters, Meis1, Pbx3, and Eya1, among others, displayed the greatest fold change reduction in NUP98-fp HA occupancy, Kmt2a occupancy, and H3K27Ac modifications with dTAG treatment compared to DMSO. We validated these results using ChIP-qPCR (Figure 3G).

Since our SLAM-seq studies revealed rapid transcriptional changes following NUP98-fp degradation, we interrogated chromatin architecture at earlier time points to define the mechanism by which the NUP98-fp maintains a transcriptionally active chromatin landscape. Since NUP98-fps are directly responsible for Kmt2a retention on chromatin at selected loci (Figure 3E), we asked whether the oncofusion protein is required for the retention of other chromatin-modifying complexes that maintain an active chromatin landscape or whether NUP98-fps may have a role in retention of the transcriptional machinery. Since we observed changes in H3K27Ac modifications at select transcriptionally sensitive loci (Figures 3E–3G), we hypothesized that the NUP98-fp may be essential for the retention of CBP/p300 at these sites. We performed ChIP-seq for CBP/p300, H3K27Ac, Kmt2a, and the RNA polymerase II (RNA Pol II) transcriptional machinery at time points ranging from 1 to 48 h following degradation of the NUP98-fp. Within 1 h of dTAG treatment, we found that Kmt2a, RNA Pol II, and RNA Pol II phospho-Ser2 are lost from chromatin at transcriptionally sensitive loci (Figure S3A). We found no differences in CBP/p300 or H3K27Ac at early time points (Figures S3B, S3C, and S4A), indicating that loss of H3K27Ac may be a secondary event that follows loss of the NUP98-fp-Kmt2a complex and disengagement of the transcriptional machinery.

Prior work in KMT2A-r leukemia has shown that the oncofusion protein complex colocalizes with PRC1.1 near the promoters of actively transcribed genes, where both complexes may compete for transcriptional control.9 We hypothesized that the NUP98-fp-Menin-Kmt2a complex may antagonize PRC1.1 activity at pro-leukemogenic genes and that degradation of the NUP98-fp would result in the accumulation of PRC1.1 and transition to a repressed chromatin state. We used ChIP-seq and ChIP-qPCR to characterize changes in PRC1.1 occupancy and histone modifications in NUP98-r leukemia cells treated with dTAG-13 for 24 h. We did not observe global changes in Bcor, Kdm2b, or Rnf2 at this time point, nor did we observe locus-specific changes at Meis1 or Hoxa9 (Figures 4A and S5A), suggesting that PRC1.1 accumulation and transition to a repressed chromatin state may be a later event. In NUP98-r cells treated for 96 h with dTAG-13, we observed genome-wide accumulation of the PRC1.1 components Bcor, Kdm2b, and Rnf2 (Figure 4A), though we did not observe global changes in repressive histone modifications at this time point (Figure S5B). Increased PRC1.1 occupancy and acquisition of H2AK119Ub and H3K27Me3 were most notable at genes whose expression is directly dependent on the NUP98-fp, including Meis1, the Hox cluster genes, and a set of highly sensitive NUP98-fp targets that were identified in our nascent transcriptomic studies (Figures 4B–4D and S6A–S6C).

Given the relatively slow kinetics of transition from an active to a repressed chromatin state, we hypothesized that an intermediary chromatin complex, such as the disruptor of telomeric silencing 1-like (DOT1L) complex, which deposits methylation marks on H3K79, may be involved. In NUP98-r and KMT2A-r leukemias, we have previously shown that DOT1L is recruited to the HOXA cluster, where deposition of H3K79Me2 and recruitment of the super elongation complex is essential for maintaining pro-leukemogenic gene expression.44 Loss of H3K79Me2 and gain of polycomb-associated H3K27Me3 occur upon leukemia cell differentiation.44 Given the link between H3K79Me2 and polycomb activity, we asked whether this mechanism could account for the rate of transition to a repressed chromatin state that we observe in NUP98-r leukemia. At Meis1, Pbx3, and the HoxA cluster, we found that gradual loss of H3K79Me2 was associated with a reciprocal gain of H3K27Me3 at these loci and others (Figures 4D and S7A). Altogether, these results demonstrate that the NUP98-fp-Menin-KMT2A complex, the DOT1L complex, and PRC1.1 may be antagonistic regulators of pro-leukemogenic genes and that the loss of a transcriptionally active chromatin landscape at these loci is followed by epigenetic enforcement of a repressed chromatin state.

PRC1.1 is dispensable for the immediate transcriptional downregulation of key pro-leukemogenic genes upon degradation of NUP98-fp

Our results demonstrate that transcriptional downregulation of select NUP98-fp targets precedes chromatin changes—both loss of activating histone modifications and gain of repressive histone modifications. Therefore, we questioned whether PRC1.1 played a direct role in the immediate transcriptional changes that are observed upon NUP98-fp degradation.

First, we examined gene expression in NUP98-r CRISPR cell lines expressing sgRNA targeting each component of PRC1.1 upon treatment with dTAG-13. Before examining nascent transcripts, we evaluated total mRNA at a later time point of 72 h in order to understand which PRC1.1 component was most critical for transcriptional repression upon NUP98-fp degradation. Consistent with our results in cells treated with VTP50469, we observed the most striking phenotype in cells lacking Pcgf1, which failed to downregulate Meis1 and Hoxb7 upon dTAG-13 treatment, more so than all other PRC1.1 components (Figure 5A). Next, we examined gene expression in NUP98-r leukemia cells lacking Pcgf1 using primers that span intron-exon junctions to specifically detect nascently transcribed, unspliced mRNA. We confirmed that NUP98-r leukemia cells harboring Pcgf1 sgRNA expressed the HA-, FKBP12F36V-tagged fusion protein at similar levels to the luciferase control and that degradation occurred with similar kinetics upon dTAG-13 treatment (Figure S8A). Surprisingly, we found that cells harboring sgRNA targeting the luciferase control and sgRNA targeting Pcgf1 demonstrated downregulation of nascent transcripts for Meis1, Hoxa9, and Hoxb7 with similar kinetics and to a similar degree (Figure 5B).

Next, we used the nascent transcriptomics method precision run-on sequencing (PRO-seq), which allowed us to define genome-wide changes in active transcription with single-nucleotide resolution, to investigate whether PRC1.1 was required for the immediate changes in nascent transcription that were observed in our SLAM-seq studies. NUP98-r leukemia cells treated with dTAG-13 for 1 h demonstrated significant changes in nascent transcription at Meis1 and the Hox cluster in cells harboring sgluciferase and sgPcgf1 (Figure 5C). We hypothesized that nascent transcriptional changes were occurring at a subset of highly sensitive NUP98-fp target genes, which we defined as genes that demonstrated a significant loss of HA occupancy at 24 h upon dTAG-13 treatment (log2 fold change > 1, p < 0.05) and significant changes in gene expression in a bulk RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) dataset obtained at the same time point (Table S3; Figures S8B and S8C). A metagene plot analysis of PRO-seq read density demonstrated that transcriptional pausing upon dTAG-13 treatment was most significant at these highly sensitive genes, whereas all NUP98-fp targets or an expression-matched dataset of genes with similar baseline transcription were not affected (Figure 5D). Knockdown of Pcgf1 did not rescue the downregulation of nascent transcription of highly sensitive NUP98-fp target genes.

These results suggest that PRC1.1 is dispensable for immediate changes in nascent transcription upon degradation of NUP98-fp. Nonetheless, our studies examining total mRNA at later time points suggested that the expression of genes such as Meis1 and the Hox cluster remained active, indicating that loss of PRC1.1 results in failure to enforce a repressed chromatin state at later time points. We hypothesized that expression of these genes could be partially rescued despite NUP98-fp degradation at later time points, in the setting of Pcgf1 loss. We used intronexon primers to examine nascent transcription in NUP98-r cells lacking Pcgf1 over a time course from 15 min to 96 h. Consistent with our PRO-seq results, we observed downregulation of nascent transcription in the absence of Pcgf1 at time points up to 24 h. However, at time points beyond 48 h, the expression of Hoxa9 and Hoxb7 rebounded and remained active despite ongoing treatment with dTAG-13 in the absence of Pcgf1 (Figure 5E). Our results demonstrate that loss of the NUP98-fp-KMT2A complex results in an immediate decrease in transcriptional activity, but loss of the complex is insufficient to stably epigenetically repress these loci at later time points, which requires PRC1.1 activity.

PRC1.1 is necessary for deposition of repressive histone modifications and enforcement of epigenetic silencing at key loci upon displacement or degradation of NUP98-fp

To understand the role of PRC1.1 in enforcing a transcriptionally repressed chromatin landscape at later time points, we performed ChIP-seq and ChIP-qPCR in NUP98-r leukemia cells harboring sgluciferase or sgPcgf1 treated with DMSO, dTAG-13, or VTP50469 for 96 h (Figures 6A and 6B). In the DMSO control, Pcgf1 loss resulted in increased chromatin occupancy of the NUP98-fp and Kmt2a, suggesting that loss of dynamic competition between these complexes may result in unopposed accumulation of the NUP98-fp-Kmt2a complex. In cells harboring sgluciferase or sgPcgf1, degradation of the NUP98-fp resulted in the eviction of Kmt2a and loss of H3K27Ac, as expected (Figures 6A, S9A, and S9B). However, Pcgf1 loss resulted in failure to accumulate PRC1.1 components and failure to acquire repressive histone modifications (Figures 6A, S9A, and S9B). We observed the same pattern of chromatin changes in NUP98-r leukemia cells treated with VTP50469 (Figures 6B, S9A, and S9C). These results are consistent with our hypothesis that PRC1.1 is required for epigenetic repression upon loss of the NUP98-fp-KMT2A complex and provide mechanistic insight into how PRC1.1 inactivation may lead to therapeutic resistance to Menin-KMT2A inhibition (Figure S10).

Loss of PRC1.1 confers resistance to Menin-KMT2A inhibition in vivo

Our previous studies suggest that loss of PRC1.1—specifically an inactivating mutation in BCORL1—may mediate resistance to Menin-KMT2A inhibition in vivo in a patient-derived xenograft (PDX) model of NUP98-r leukemia.14 We characterized a NUP98-NSD1 PDX harboring a BCORL1 variant (p.Y1692*, c.5076C>G) in the C-terminal PCGF1 Ub-like fold discriminator (PUFD) domain that is unresponsive to VTP50469.14 Mechanistically, BCOR/BCORL1 mutations in the C-terminal PUFD domain disrupt binding to PCGF1, which links the RNF2 catalytic core to the KDM2B chromatin-binding module.39 Since this PDX model harbored additional mutations in genes implicated in AML pathogenesis, we cannot conclude that the BCORL1 variant rendered the cells resistant to Menin-KMT2A inhibition. Therefore, we sought to directly inactivate PRC1.1 in a NUP98-r PDX model using CRISPR-Cas9 editing.

We used electroporation of Cas9 and sgRNA targeting BCOR to inactivate PRC1.1 in a NUP98-JARID1A PDX, followed by transplantation into immunocompromised NOG mice (Figure 7A). Confirmation of CRISPR editing pre-transplant revealed that most edits occurred as +1 insertion and deletion events, which made up approximately 18% of the total cells that were injected into each animal (Figure 7B). Mice harboring NUP98-JARID1A, CRISPR-edited non-targeting (NT) sgRNA, or BCOR sgRNA PDX cells were randomized to receive either control chow or chow that contained 0.1% SNDX-5613 (the clinical analog of VTP50469). The loss of BCOR accelerated leukemogenesis in this model, and treatment with the Menin-KMT2A inhibitor SNDX-5613 resulted in no survival advantage (Figure 7C). By contrast, mice harboring NUP98-JARID1A PDX cells with the NT control sgRNA demonstrated a significant survival advantage when treated with SNDX-5613, consistent with our previous studies using VTP50469 chow (Figure 7C).14 To compare disease burden among mice harboring NT sgRNA versus BCOR sgRNA, we sacrificed a separate cohort at the same time point (day 48) to compare leukemic infiltration in hematopoietic organs and the differentiation status of the leukemia. Mice harboring the NUP98-JARID1A PDX with NT sgRNA treated with SNDX-5613 demonstrated significantly lower bone marrow disease burden and nearly undetectable disease in the spleen (Figures 7D and 7E). Consistent with the mechanism of SNDX-5613 as an agent that induces differentiation, we observed that any remaining leukemia cells in SNDX-5613-treated animals expressed high levels of the megakaryocytic markers CD41 and CD61 (Figures 7F and 7G). The expression of these markers is commensurate with the origin of this NUP98-JARID1A PDX, which was derived from a patient with acute megakaryoblastic leukemia (AMKL). Altogether, these results demonstrate that loss of the PRC1.1 component BCOR leads to therapeutic resistance to Menin-KMT2A inhibition in vivo, a finding that has important translational implications for patients with leukemia receiving these agents who may harbor mutations in PRC1.1 components.

DISCUSSION

Our studies have uncovered the mechanism by which key pro-leukemogenic genes are silenced upon inactivation of NUP98-fps, either via fusion protein degradation or inhibition of the Menin-KMT2A interaction. We identify PRC1.1 as a critical negative regulator that is essential for repressing the HOX/MEIS gene expression program via the deposition of repressive histone modifications. We speculate that other leukemias driven by a HOX/MEIS program, such as NPM1 mutant leukemia, may also rely on PRC1.1 to silence pro-leukemogenic genes during differentiation. Given the evolutionary conservation of the KMT2A/TrxG and PcG complexes,4 our findings could also provide mechanistic insight into how these complexes compete for transcriptional control of stem cell-associated genes during normal development and differentiation. By taking advantage of the rapid kinetics of inducible protein degradation and the exquisite sensitivity of nascent transcriptomics, our studies have temporally uncoupled transcriptional events from changes in chromatin modifications. Our results suggest that the NUP98-fp-Kmt2a complex is directly responsible for maintaining active transcription of key pro-leukemogenic genes since a decline in nascent transcription and loss of RNA Pol II chromatin occupancy occurs almost immediately upon NUP98-fp degradation. Recent work has suggested a role for wild-type KMT2A and KMT2A fusion proteins in regulating transcriptional initiation45 and elongation.46 We have demonstrated that the following sequence of events is associated with stable suppression of key pro-leukemogenic gene expression: (1) loss of Kmt2a, RNA Pol II, and transcriptional downregulation, (2) loss of H3K27Ac and CBP/p300 chromatin occupancy, and (3) accumulation of PRC1.1 and deposition of H2aK119Ub and H3K27Me3 to stabilize transcriptional repression.

To confirm that PRC1.1 in particular is the polycomb complex responsible for this phenomenon, we used CRISPR-Cas9 to inactivate each individual component of PRC1.1. We found that the loss of individual components of PRC1.1 accelerates leukemia cell proliferation, albeit to a different degree for each complex subunit, and in some cases, this phenomenon is enhanced under the selective pressure of the Menin inhibitor. Loss of the enzymatic PRC1.1 components Rnf2 and Ring1a did not produce this phenotype, most likely because these components are functionally redundant in hematopoietic cells.47,48 We consistently observed the most striking proliferative phenotype when we inactivated the core, non-redundant PRC1.1 component Pcgf1. Cells lacking Pcgf1 fail to accumulate PRC1.1 and the repressive histone modifications H2aK119Ub and H3K27Me3 and subsequently fail to suppress Meis1/Hox cluster genes. Though we did not measure Meis1/Hox gene expression beyond 96 h of dTAG-13 treatment, due to the declining viability of sgluciferase control cells, we speculate that continued expression of Meis1/Hox genes in cells lacking Pcgf1 sustain leukemogenesis in the absence of PRC1.1.

Previous work in mammals and Drosophila have suggested that PRC1.1 and the KMT2A/TrxG protein complex may dynamically compete for chromatin binding at unmethylated CpG islands.49–53 Our studies suggest that the NUP98-fp-Menin-KMT2A complex and PRC1.1 occupy partially overlapping regions on chromatin at key pro-leukemogenic genes. These findings are consistent with recent studies,9,39 which demonstrate that PRC1.1 localizes near the promoter of actively transcribed genes, where its role may be to sample CpG-rich promoters to determine their susceptibility to polycomb-mediated repression.21 Further studies are needed to determine how PRC1.1 activity is locally inhibited: whether by active transcription or the presence of the NUP98-fp-Menin-Kmt2a chromatin complex. Loss of PRC1.1 function may lead to unopposed activity of the NUP98-fp-Menin-Kmt2a chromatin complex, which may in part explain the accelerated proliferation of PRC1.1-deficient cells and their relative resistance to Menin-Kmt2a inhibition.

NUP98-fps may associate with Menin-KMT2A on chromatin in the context of phase-separated biomolecular condensates, which may be nucleated by NUP98-fps through their N-terminal intrinsically disordered Gly-Leu-Phe-Gly (GLFG) repeats. The GLFG repeat domains of NUP98-fps are essential for condensate formation, chromatin localization, and leukemic transformation.54,55 Our previous work suggests that the GLFG repeats of NUP98-HOXA9 are required for its association with Kmt2a,35 though we and others have not shown whether the nature of this interaction is direct or indirect. In this biomolecular context, NUP98-fps may recruit other chromatin regulators that have been localized to condensates such as PRC1.1 or the transcriptional machinery.56–58 Phase-separated NUP98-fps may provide a molecular scaffold/milieu that recruits activating and repressive chromatin complexes to condensates, where dynamic competition dictates the transcriptional outcome and chromatin state of key oncogenes.

Our studies focus on the regulation of key pro-leukemogenic genes, but our results do not explain why PRC1.1 selectively represses particular loci such as the HOX genes. One possibility is that the NUP98-fp-Menin-KMT2A complex recruits an additional chromatin-modifying enzyme such as DOT1L to the HOX cluster.44 Our CRISPR screen identified the H3K79 methyltransferase DOT1L as a molecular dependency in NUP98-r leukemia cells, and we have reported that NUP98-r leukemias are sensitive to DOT1L inhibitors.44 Our studies using degradation of the NUP98-fp demonstrate that H3K79Me2 modifications are gradually reduced and nearly absent by 96 h, a time point that coincides with the accumulation of PRC1.1 and gain of repressive histone modifications, though the technical limits of our studies lack single-cell/single-locus resolution. We hypothesize that the slow kinetics of H2aK119Ub and H3K27Me3 acquisition is due to gradual turnover of the H3K79Me2 modification, particularly spreading of these modifications into the gene body.

Finally, our studies have translational relevance since Menin-KMT2A inhibitors have shown remarkable promise in earlyphase clinical trials for relapsed/refractory leukemias. Since driving the differentiation of leukemia appears central to the therapeutic effect of Menin inhibitors, deciphering the molecular mechanisms of differentiation is critical to understanding how these drugs work and how resistance might arise. A recent study reported the emergence of therapeutic resistance to the Menin inhibitor SNDX-5613 that was driven by acquired somatic mutations in MEN1 that altered the drug binding pocket.59 Our results suggest that PRC1.1 loss of function is another mechanism by which resistance to Menin inhibitors might arise. Since mutations in BCOR/BCORL1 have been described in AML,28–30 patients enrolled in ongoing Menin inhibitor trials could be evaluated for the presence of these mutations, both at diagnosis and during treatment, if resistance were to arise. Beyond the therapeutic implications of these findings, our studies provide fundamental mechanistic insight into the dynamic role of oncofusion proteins, KMT2A-Menin, and the PRC1.1 complex in determining the epigenetic state of pro-leukemogenic genes during leukemia maintenance and differentiation.

Limitations of the study

Our studies suggest that the NUP98-fp-Menin-Kmt2a complex dynamically competes with PRC1.1 for transcriptional control of pro-leukemogenic genes. However, due to the technical limitations of ChIP-seq, we are unable to demonstrate that these complexes colocalize at key oncogenes with single-cell resolution. Furthermore, we do not show that these complexes interact on chromatin, either directly or indirectly. We demonstrate that degradation of the NUP98-fp results in loss of the Menin-KMT2A complex at select transcriptionally sensitive genes, including Meis1 and the Hox cluster. It remains unknown why small-molecule Menin-Kmt2a inhibition results in loss of the complex and transcriptional changes at Meis1 and other loci, while transcription at the Hox cluster remains relatively unchanged. While PRC1.1 is essential for transcriptional repression of Hox/Meis upon loss of the NUP98-fp, our studies show that genetic inactivation of PRC1.1 does not dramatically impact Hox/Meis expression at steady state. Therefore, expression of these oncogenes does not fully explain the competitive growth advantage of PRC1.1-deficient cells. Further work is needed to define the mechanism by which loss of PRC1.1 accelerates leukemia cell growth, which may include a Hox/Meis-independent mechanism.

EXPERIMENTAL MODEL AND STUDY PARTICIPANT DETAILS

Animal studies

All animal experiments were performed with the approval of Dana-Farber Cancer Institute’s Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC). For Patient-derived xenograft (PDX) experiments, female NOG Mice (NOD.Cg-PrkdcscidIl2rgtm1Wjl/SzJ stock#005557) mice at 6–8 weeks of age were purchased form the Jackson Laboratory and were housed in a pathogen-free animal facility at Dana-Farber Cancer Institute. Engraftment of human cells in the peripheral blood was monitored every 1–2 weeks, and littermates were randomized to experimental treatment groups once engraftment was confirmed. When animals developed signs of leukemia, the bone marrow and spleen were harvested and analyzed using flow cytometry.

Cell lines

Human cell lines were acquired from American Type Culture Collection (ATCC) and were cultured in DMEM (293T) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum, 1x penicillin/streptomycin (Gibco) at 37°C and 5% CO2. The murine NUP98-HOXA9 and NUP98-JARID1A transformed cell lines were maintained in IMDM with 20% FBS, 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Gibco), 6 mg/mL mIL3, 10 mg/mL mIL6, and 10 mg/mL mSCF (Stemcell Technologies) at 37°C and 5% CO2.

Patient-derived xenograft (PDX) models

PDXs models were previously established through continuous transplantation in immune-deficient mice.14 For PDX generation, primary AML cells were obtained from a pediatric patient with AML at Boston Children’s Hospital/Dana-Farber Cancer Institute (DFCI) on a DFCI IRB-approved institutional research protocol. Informed consent was obtained prior to sample acquisition in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Prior to transplantation for in vivo CRISPR studies, PDX cells viably frozen from leukemic mice were cultured for <1 week before and after electroporation in StemSpan media (Stemcell Technologies) supplemented with 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Gibco), 1% L-Glutamine (Gibco), SR-1 1 μM (Stem Cell Technologies), UM-729 1 μM (Stem Cell Technologies), and human cytokines: SCF (2.5 ng/mL), Flt3 (2.5 ng/mL), and TPO (2.5 ng/mL).

METHOD DETAILS

Chromatin-focused CRISPR–Cas9 Genetic Screening

A chromatin-focused CRISPR-Cas9 genetic screen60 was performed in immortalized NUP98-HOXA9 mouse LSK cells that were derived from single cell clones selected for CRISPR editing efficiency, as previously described.14 The library was a gift from Y. Soto-Feliciano (MIT) and was generated as previously described.60 For transduction of NUP98-HOXA9 mouse leukemia cells, viral supernatant produced by transfection of HEK-293T cells was titered in a pilot experiment to achieve a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of ~0.3, to ensure that most cells received a single sgRNA integration event. At 72 h after infection, viral transduction efficiency was quantified by the percentage of RFP+ cells as determined by flow cytometry on an LSR Fortessa (BD Biosciences). Subsequently, cells were selected with 2.5 μg/mL puromycin (Gibco) for 3 days. A baseline (Day 0) timepoint was obtained by harvesting 2 million puromycin-selected, RFP+ cells in duplicate, and the remaining cells were cultured with either DMSO- or 200 nM VTP50469–containing media (IC50 concentration). Cells were maintained under these conditions for 14 days to allow 12 cumulative population doublings (TF/final). To achieve a minimum representation of 1,000× coverage, each passage of the cells was optimized to maintain at least 5e6 cells. For each timepoint, at least 10 million cells were harvested and pelleted in triplicate, except for the day 0 timepoint which was harvested in duplicate. Timepoints were harvested and sequenced at Day 0, at the midpoint of the screen (7 days), and at the end of the screen (14 days). Following next-generation sequencing analysis, data was analyzed using the MAGeCK Flute pipeline as previously described.82

CRISPR editing to inactivate PRC1.1 in isogenic Cas9-expressing murine leukemia cell lines

CRISPR-Cas9 sgRNA targeting each component of PRC1.1 were designed using the IDT CRISPR design tool (https://www.idtdna.com). sgRNA were cloned into a lentiviral pUSER vector with a modified guide scaffold at BsmBI cloning sites as previously described.60 NUP98-HOXA9 mouse LSK cells constitutively expressing Cas9 (described above in ‘chromatin-focused CRISPR–Cas9 genetic screening’) were transduced with pUSER containing sgRNA and an RFP selection marker. Cells expressing sgRNA targeting PRC1.1 and the RFP fluorescent marker were isolated using FACS sorting, and CRISPR editing was confirmed through isolation of genomic DNA and PCR amplification of 200–300 bp amplicons surrounding the CRISPR cut site, following by Sanger sequencing. Subsequently, ICE Analysis (https://ice.synthego.com) was used to quantify CRISPR editing efficiency for each sgRNA.

Cell viability, cell differentiation assays

Cells were treated with DMSO or an escalating dose curve of VTP50469 in a 96-well plate format. Fresh drug-containing media was replenished, and cells were re-plated every 3 days to maintain the initial density. Viable (DAPI-) cells were counted using FACS Fortessa (BD Biosciences). To calculate the IC50, the number of cells in DMSO and VTP50469 conditions were plotted as a ratio. Differentiation status of VTP50469 or DMSO treated cells was assessed at day 9 by FACS with CD11b-BV421 (BioLegend) and by cytospin and H&E staining.

RNA isolation and quantitative real-time PCR

RNA was isolated using the RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen) according to manufacturer’s instructions (Qiagen). The SuperScript First-Strand Synthesis System (Invitrogen) was used to generate cDNA by reverse transcription. For qRT-PCR, the ViiA 7 Real-Time PCR system (Applied Biosystems) was used with 384-well plates. Taqman gene expression assays (Applied Biosystems) were used for real-time quantitative PCR where indicated in the Figure legends. ACTB or GAPDH was used as the housekeeping gene for normalization. Relative gene expression was calculated by the comparative delta-delta cycle threshold method. For experiments to assess nascent transcription by qPCR, custom primers spanning intron-exon junctions, and SYBR green gene expression assays (Applied Biosystems) were used for qPCRs. ChIP qPCRs also used the SYBR green gene expression assays. The ViiA 7 Real-Time PCR system (Applied Biosystems) was used with 384-well plates using SYBR green and Taqman assays.

The mouse ChIP-qPCR primers used were: Gapdh (F: GGCCCGGAGTCTTAAGTATTAG, R: GGGCGCGAAAGTAAAGAAAG), Meis1(F: GGTAGCGCAATCGAGAAAGA, R: GCTATTTATGACCGCTGGAGAA) (F: GGGAAAGACCTCTTGCTACTG, R: AGGGCCTCTGTGGATATAGAA), (F: GTATGGACAGAAGGCAGAAAGA, R: CCACTGTCTGGGTTACACTTT), Hoxa9 (F: GTTGGTCGCTCCTGACTTT, R: TCGCATTCTCACTCTACTGTATTT), gene desert (F: GGGAATTCGGGTTGAAGGAT, R: TACTGCATGACAAACAGGAGAG).

The mouse intron-exon primers used were: Gapdh (F: TTCCACTCTGAAGAACATGAGATAG, R: CCAATACGGCCAAATCTGAAAG), Meis1(F: AAGTAGGAAGGGAGCCAGAG, R: TGTCTTTCAGCCACGTTTCA), Hoxa9 (F: AACAACCCAGCGAAGGC, R: ACGCGCTGGTAGATCCT), Hoxb7 (F: GGATGCGAAGCTCAGGTAAA, R: GGAATAACCTGGACACAGAGAAG).

SLAM-sequencing

SLAM-seq was performed using the Lexogen SLAM-Seq Metabolic Labeling kit (Lexogen). Briefly, NUP98-r leukemia cells were plated at 5e5 cells/mL in the appropriate culture medium and were pre-treated either with 0.1% DMSO or with dTAG-13 (500nM) for 1 h. Subsequently, nascent transcripts were labeled by adding 4SU (Lexogen) at a final concentration of 100mM to the culture media for 3 h. The same concentration of dTAG was maintained in the culture media such that the total time for protein degradation was 4 h. RNA was harvested and SLAM-seq libraries were prepared as previously described.46 The QuantSeq 3’ mRNA-Seq Library Preparation kit (Lexogen) was used to prepare SLAM-seq libraries and. Sequencing was done using the Illumina Next Gen Sequencing NextSeq platform (Illumina) and approximately 25 million 75 base-pair, single-end reads per sample. For SLAM-seq analysis, the Slamdunk pipeline was used to analyze FASTQ files, as previously described.83 Raw Illumina sequencer output was converted to FASTQ format using bcl2fastq (v2.20.0.422). Reads were trimmed for quality using trimmomatic (v0.36; minimum trimmed length 34bp), aligned to the mouse genome (Gencode M24/mm10) using STAR (v2.7.5a), sorted and duplicates marked/removed with picard pipeline tools (v2.9.4). Final “deduped”.BAM files were indexed using SAMtools (v1.95). Differential SLAMseq expression was calculated using the BioConductor DESeq2 package (v1.24,0), using Slamdunk converted counts.

PRO-sequencing

PRO-seq was performed to detect changes in nascent transcription as previously described.46 NUP98-r leukemia cells were treated with DMSO or 500 nM dTAG-13 for 1 h. Subsequently, 10 million cells were harvested and washed once with ice-cold PBS (Gibco). To obtain single-cell suspensions, cell pellets were resuspended in 250mL wash buffer (10 mM Tris-Cl, pH 8.0, 10 mM KCl, 250 mM Sucrose, 5 mM MgCl2, 1 mM EGTA, 10% (v/v) Glycerol, supplemented freshly with 0.5 mM DTT, 0.2 mL/mL RNase inhibitor and protease inhibitor). 10mL of permeabilization buffer (10 mM Tris-Cl, pH 8.0, 10 mM KCl, 250 mM Sucrose, 5 mM MgCl2, 1 mM EGTA, 0.1% (v/v), Igepal CA-630, 0.05% (v/v) Tween 20, 10% (v/v) Glycerol, supplemented freshly with 0.5mM DTT, 0.2 mL/mL RNase inhibitor and protease inhibitor) was added dropwise and cells were incubated on ice for 5 min. Following centrifugation of the permeabilized cells, cells were washed in 10mL wash buffer and then resuspended in freezing buffer (50 mM Tris-Cl, pH 8.0, 40% (v/v) glycerol, 5 mM MgCl2, 1.1 mM EDTA, supplemented freshly with 0.5 mM DTT and 1 mL/mL RNase inhibitor). Cells were counted and permeabilization was visualized using trypan blue. At least 5e6 permeabilized cells were snap-frozen in 500ul freezing buffer in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 C. All subsequent steps were performed by the Nascent Transcriptomics Core at Harvard Medical School, Boston, MA as previously described.46 PRO-seq analysis was performed as previously described,46 and all custom scripts are available on the AdelmanLab Github (https://github.com/AdelmanLab/NIH_scripts).

ChIP-sequencing

Cells treated with either DMSO or VTP50469 for the indicated timepoint were harvested and resuspended at 2.5e6 cells/mL, then crosslinked using 1% methanol-free paraformaldehyde (ThermoFisher) for 10 min at room temperature. Glycine (25 mM) was used to quench the reaction. Cells were washed once with ice-cold PBS. Cytoplasm was stripped using 50 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.5, 140 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, 10% Glycerol, 0.5% NP-40, and 0.125% Triton X-100 for 10 min at 4°C. Nuclei were pelleted by centrifugation at 1400xg for 10 min. Nuclear pellets were then resuspended in 10 mM Tris-HCl pH 8.0, 200 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA and 0.5 mM EGTA, and incubated for 10 min at 4°. Nuclei were pelleted at 1400xg for 10 min, then resuspended in 10 mM Tris-HCl pH 8.0, 100 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, 0.5 mM EGTA, 0.1% sodium deoxycholate, 1% Triton X-100, and 0.5% sodium lauroylsarcosine. Between 10 and 30e6 cells (1–5 mg of chromatin) was used for each immunoprecipitation using the following antibodies: anti-Menin (Bethyl), MLLn (Bethyl), HA (Diagenode), H3K27Ac (Diagenode), H2aK119Ub (Cell Signaling), H3K27Me3 (Abcam), anti-Bcor (ProteinTech), anti-Kdm2b (Milipore), and anti-RING1B/RNF2 (Cell Signaling). Immunoprecipitation was performed using protein-A (anti-Rabbit antibodies) or protein-G (anti-mouse antibodies) magnetic beads (Dynal). Immunoprecipitated DNA fragments were eluted and de-crosslinked in 50 mM Tris-HCl pH 8.0, 10 mM EDTA, and 1% SDS, and quantified by TapeStation (Agilent) and Qubit (ThermoFisher). 1–10 ng of DNA was used in preparation of Illumina-compatible libraries using a ThruPLEX DNA-Seq Kit (Takara) followed by sequencing using NextSeq550 (Illumina) to obtain 20–30e6 unique sequencing paired-end reads.

ChIP-seq data analysis

Raw Illumina sequencer output was converted to FASTQ format using bcl2fastq (v2.20.0.422). Reads (paired-end 37-mers) were trimmed for quality using trimmomatic (v0.36; minimum trimmed length 34bp), aligned to the mouse genome (Gencode M24/mm10) using STAR (v2.7.5a), sorted and duplicates marked/removed with picard pipeline tools (v2.9.4). Final “deduped”.BAM files were indexed using SAMtools (v1.95). Total signal was assessed around TSS regions using the sitepro tool (v0.6.6) from the Cis-regulatory Element Annotation System (CEAS) package, based on signal from WIG files generated using IGVtools (v2.3.98). Peaks were called using MACS2, and input samples were used as controls for peak calling. TSS region intervals were defined (−1kb to +3kb) using sitepro from the CEAS suite. ‘Positive signal’ was defined for genes that displayed a 2-fold enrichment above the input sample, with at least >200 reads within the aforementioned TSS window. Signal surrounding the TSS window is shown for all genes in tornado plots, whereas only genes that met the ‘positive signal’ criteria (defined above) are depicted in hockeystick plots. To compare DMSO samples with each drug condition, a single representative TSS region was identified for all genes, based on the site with the highest average signal in DMSO control samples, and a ratio of DMSO to drug treatment was calculated for each gene. Data visualizations were produced using IGVtools (TDF signal pileups), deepTools (3.5.5.) and ngs.plot (pileup heatmaps).

CRISPR editing of PDXs & isolation of human cells for CRISPR analysis

The Neon Transfection System (Invitrogen) was used for electroporation of Cas9 and sgRNA targeting BCOR or a non-targeting control. The Alt-R Cas9 enzyme (IDT) and sgRNA were combined in Buffer T (Invitrogen) and electroporated according to the manufacturer’s protocol. NUP98-r PDX cells were electroporated using the following settings of the Neon Transfection System: 1350 V, 35 mλ, 1 pulse. Cells were allowed to recover in StemSpan media for 72 h, then timepoint 0 CRISPR editing efficiency was measured using Sanger Sequencing and the ICE Analysis Tool (Synthego, https://ice.synthego.com).

Assessment of in vivo Menin inhibitor resistance using PDXs

In vivo studies were conducted in NOD.Cg-PrkdcscidIl2rgtm1Wjl/SzJ (NOG) mice (Jackson Laboratory) age 7–9 weeks. NUP98-r AML patient samples had been previously established as continuous PDXs in immune-deficient mice.14 Briefly, 1–2e5 PDX cells that had been electroporated with Cas9 and sgRNA targeting BCOR or a non-targeting control sgRNA (described above) were intravenously (i.v.) transplanted into NOG mice who had been sub-lethally irradiated with 450 Gy. Engraftment was confirmed on day 21 post-transplant through detection of human CD45+ cells in the peripheral blood. Mice from each group (Control or BCOR sgRNA) were randomly assigned to receive either control chow or 0.1% VTP50469 spiked-in chow. To monitor leukemia burden, mice were bled weekly to assess the percentage of human CD45+ cells versus mouse CD45+ cells in peripheral blood. Weight was also measured weekly, and mice were clinically assessed for signs of illness. In a burden of disease experiment, mice were euthanized at day 48 post-transplant to assess the percentage of human CD45+ cells in hematopoietic organs. In a survival experiment, mice were sacrificed when they developed signs of clinical illness or if mice lost >15% body weight, as dictated by humane endpoints of our animal protocol. Upon sacrifice, the leukemia burden was assessed in spleen and bone marrow by flow cytometry using anti-human CD45, and differentiation of leukemic blasts was determined by staining with anti-human CD61 and anti-human CD41. Human cells were isolated using a mouse cell depletion kit (Miltenyi), followed by isolation of genomic DNA and PCR amplification of 200–300 bp amplicons surrounding the CRISPR cut site. Sanger sequencing and ICE Analysis (https://ice.synthego.com) was used to quantify CRISPR editing efficiency.

Intracellular flow cytometry to detect HA-tagged NUP98-fp

NUP98-r leukemia cells were treated with DMSO or dTAG-13 for the indicated period of time. Cells were harvested and fixed using BD Cytofix/Cytoperm and washed with BD Perm/Wash Buffer according to the manufacturer’s instructions (BD Bioscience). Blocking was performed using BD Perm/Wash Buffer with 5% BSA (Sigma Aldrich) for 30 min on ice. To detect HA-tagged NUP98-fp, cells were incubated with anti-HA antibody (1:100, Cell Signaling) for 30 min on ice, washed 3 times with Wash Buffer. Subsequently, a secondary antibody was used to stain the cells with Anti-rabbit IgG Alexa Fluor 647 (1:200, Cell Signaling) at room temperature for 30 min. Cells were washed 3 times with BD Perm/Wash Buffer and resuspended in PBS for FACs analysis. Samples were analyzed using an LSR Fortessa flow cytometer (BD Biosciences). Data were analyzed with FlowJo software (Tree Star).

QUANTIFICATION AND STATISTICAL ANALYSIS

Statistical tests and p-values are stated in the figure legends. p < 0.05 was considered significant and GraphPad Prism Software (v.9.5.0) was used for all statistical tests. Student’s t-test (unpaired, two-tailed) was used to assess significance in experiments between treatment and control groups. If more than two groups were compared, we used an analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Sidak’s post-test for multiple comparisons. For proliferation assays, dose-response curves and IC50 values were calculated in a variable slope model as a four-parameter dose-response curve. For survival experiments with PDX models, the Gehan-Breslow-Wilcoxon test was used to calculate p values; to correct for multiple comparisons among 4 groups (6 pairwise comparisons), the Bonferroni alpha correction of 0.0083 was used as the cutoff for significance.
