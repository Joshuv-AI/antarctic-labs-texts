Unbounded Reinhardt domains with finite-dimensional Bergman spaces in ℂ n {\mathbb{C}}^{n}
Chika Hayashida and Joe Kamimoto

Abstract
. In this paper, we construct unbounded domains in ℂ n {\mathbb{C}}^{n} ( n ≥ 2 n\geq 2 ), whose Bergman spaces are nontrivial and finite-dimensional. We further show that the Bergman metrics on these domains have positive constant sectional curvature equal to 2 2 , and that their holomorphic automorphism groups consist only of linear mappings.

32A36 (32A40, 53C55, 32M05).

Introduction

In the study of several complex variables,
the distinction between whether a domain is
bounded or unbounded has,
at times,
not only caused serious difficulties but also
raised interesting issues
(see

[
14
,
20
,
21
,

,

,
10
,
11
,
1
,
12
,
17
,
4
,
5
,

,

,

]
, etc.).
In this note, we construct specific unbounded domains
in
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
(
n\geq 2
),
which appear to be of interest and significant
from the viewpoints of the following three aspects:
the dimensions of the Bergman spaces,
the holomorphic sectional curvatures of the Bergman metrics,
and the holomorphic automorphism groups.
These three aspects are, in fact,
closely interconnected.

Our example of such domains is defined as follows.
Let
a>0
and
consider the unbounded complete Reinhardt domain

D(a)\subset\mathbb{C}^{n}
(
n\geq 2
) given by

(1.1)

D(a)=\left\{z\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n}:\left||z_{k}|^{2}-|z_{n}|^{2}\right|<\frac{1}{(|z_{n}|^{2}+1)^{a}}\text{ for $k=1,\ldots,n-1$}\right\}.

The construction of the above
D(a)

is inspired by the following two known
examples in
{\mathbb{C}}^{2}
:

(1.2)

\begin{split}&D_{\rm Wie}(k):=\left\{z\in{\mathbb{C}}^{2}:\big||z_{1}|-|z_{2}|\big|\cdot(|z_{1}|+|z_{2}|)^{4k}<1\right\};\\
&D_{\rm HL}(\alpha):=\left\{z\in{\mathbb{C}}^{2}:\left||z_{1}|^{2}-|z_{2}|^{2}\right|<\frac{1}{(1+|z_{1}|^{2}+|z_{2}|^{2})^{\alpha}}\right\},\end{split}

where
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
\alpha\in(2,3)
.
The above two examples
are
given in the papers by Wiegerinck
[
14
]
and
by Huang, Li and Treuer
[
8
]
, respectively.
(The domain
D_{\rm Wie}(k)
is modified from our perspective.)
It is worth noting that the previously known examples
are two-dimensional,
whereas our example is constructed in arbitrary dimension
n
.
While these three domains are similar in shape,

D(a)
is slightly different from
D_{\rm Wie}(k)

and
D_{\rm HL}(\alpha)
,
even in the two-dimensional case.
We remark that
the boundary of
D(a)
is not smooth
when
n\geq 3
, but it is easy to
construct domains with smooth boundaries
which possess the same properties
as those of
D(a)
obtained
in this note (see Remark 2.2 (i) below).

This note is organized as follows.
In Section 2,
the Bergman space of the domain

D(a)
is shown to be finite-dimensional.
In Section 3, we show that
the Bergman metric of
D(a)

for certain values of
a

has constant
holomorphic sectional curvature
equal to
2
.
In Section 4, the structure of
the holomorphic
automorphism group of
D(a)

for certain values of
a
is investigated.

2.
Dimensions of the Bergman spaces

Let
\Omega
be a domain in
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
and
let
A^{2}(\Omega)
be the Hilbert space
of the
L^{2}
-holomorphic functions on
\Omega
, i.e.,

A^{2}(\Omega)=\left\{f:\Omega\to{\mathbb{C}}\mbox{ holomorphic}:\|f\|_{\Omega}:=\left(\int_{\Omega}|f(z)|^{2}dV(z)\right)^{1/2}<\infty\right\},

where
dV
is the Lebsgue measure on
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
.

When
\Omega
is a bounded domain,

A^{2}(\Omega)
contains all the monomials,
which implies that
the dimension of
A^{2}(\Omega)
is infinity.
On the other hand,
the issue of the dimension of

A^{2}(\Omega)
is not obvious in the unbounded case.
When
\Omega
is a domain in the complex plane
{\mathbb{C}}
,
it is seen in
[
14
]
that

(2.1)

A^{2}(\Omega)

is either trivial, i.e.,
\{0\}
, or infinite dimensional.

It might be natural to expect that
property (
2.1
) always holds
in the general dimensional case.
However, J. Wiegerinck
[
14
]

gave, surprisingly, a counterexample to
(
2.1
); more precisely, he showed that

D_{\rm Wie}(k)
with
k\in{\mathbb{N}}

has a finite-dimensional
and nontrivial Bergman space.
More recently,
Huang, Li and Treuer
[
8
]
also showed that
the domain
D_{\rm HL}(\alpha)
has the same properties.

We now show that the Bergman space of

D(a)
is also finite-dimensional.

Theorem 2.1
.

If
a>\frac{1}{n-1}
, then
the Bergman space of
D(a)
is given by

(2.2)

A^{2}(D(a))={\rm Span}\{z^{p}:p\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}^{n},|p|<(n-1)a-1\}.

From (
2.2
), the dimension of
A^{2}(D(a))

can be expressed as

(2.3)

\dim A^{2}(D(a))=\begin{cases}&0\quad\mbox{ if $0<a\leq\frac{1}{n-1}$,}\\
&\binom{n+k}{k}\quad\mbox{if $\frac{k+1}{n-1}<a\leq\frac{k+2}{n-1}$
$(k\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0})$}.\end{cases}

Proof.

Using the polar coordinates

z_{j}=r_{j}e^{i\theta_{j}}
for
j=1,\ldots,n

in the computation of the integral:

\begin{split}\|z^{p}\|^{2}=&\int_{D(a)}|z_{1}|^{2p_{1}}\cdots|z_{n}|^{2p_{n}}dV(z),\end{split}

we have

\begin{split}\|z^{p}\|^{2}=(2\pi)^{n}\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(\prod_{k=1}^{n-1}\int_{|r_{k}^{2}-r_{n}^{2}|<\frac{1}{(r_{n}^{2}+1)^{\alpha}},r_{k}>0}r_{k}^{2p_{k}+1}dr_{k}\right)r_{n}^{2p_{n}+1}dr_{n}.\end{split}

Furthermore,
changing integral variables,
we have

(2.4)

\begin{split}\|z^{p}\|^{2}=\pi^{n}\int_{0}^{\infty}\prod_{k=1}^{n-1}f_{p_{k}}(\rho)\rho^{p_{n}}d\rho,\end{split}

with

f_{q}(\rho)=\int_{I(\rho)}r^{q}dr,

where
q\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
and

I(\rho)=\{r>0:|r-\rho|<(\rho+1)^{-a}\}.

The convergence of the integral in (
2.4
)
is determined by the behavior of

f_{q}(\rho)
as
\rho\to\infty
.
Its asymptotic behavior can be described as follows.

\begin{split}f_{q}(\rho)&=\frac{1}{q+1}\left[\left(\rho+\frac{1}{(\rho+1)^{a}}\right)^{q+1}-\left(\rho-\frac{1}{(\rho+1)^{a}}\right)^{q+1}\right]\\
&=\frac{\rho^{q+1}}{q+1}\left[\left(1+\frac{1}{\rho(\rho+1)^{a}}\right)^{q+1}-\left(1-\frac{1}{\rho(\rho+1)^{a}}\right)^{q+1}\right]\\
&=\frac{\rho^{q+1}}{q+1}\left[\left\{1+\frac{q+1}{\rho^{a+1}}+O\left(\frac{1}{\rho^{a+2}}\right)\right\}-\left\{1-\frac{q+1}{\rho^{a+1}}+O\left(\frac{1}{\rho^{a+2}}\right)\right\}\right]\\
&=2\rho^{q-a}\left(1+O\left(\frac{1}{\rho}\right)\right)\quad\mbox{as $\rho\to\infty$.}\end{split}

Applying the above result to (
2.4
),
we can see the following equivalences.

(2.5)

\begin{split}\|z^{p}\|<\infty\Longleftrightarrow&\;\;\int_{1}^{\infty}\rho^{|p|-a(n-1)}d\rho<\infty\\
\Longleftrightarrow&\;\;|p|-a(n-1)<-1,\end{split}

which implies

A^{2}(D(a))\supset{\rm Span}\{z^{p}:p\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}^{n},|p|<(n-1)a-1\}
.
Conversely, the opposite inclusion can be
established in a similar manner
as in the proof of the main theorem in
[
14
]
.

We remark that
since
|p|
is nonnegative,
the value of
a
must be greater than

\frac{1}{n-1}
from (
2.5
).

∎

Remark 2.2
.

(i)
The boundary of
D(a)
is not smooth when
n\geq 3
.
However, we can construct a domain
with
C^{\infty}
-smooth boundary satisfies
the properties in Theorem 2.1 as follows.
For
a>0
, we define

(2.6)

\tilde{D}(a)=\left\{z\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n}:\left||z_{k}|^{2}-|z_{n}|^{2}\right|<\frac{2}{(|z_{n}|^{2}+1)^{a}}\mbox{ for $k=1,\ldots,n-1$}\right\}.

Since
D(a)\subsetneq\tilde{D}(a)

and the boundaries of
D(a)
and
\tilde{D}(a)

do not intersect,
it is easy to see the existence of
a Reinhardt domain
\Omega(a)

with smooth boundary such that

D(a)\subsetneq\Omega(a)\subsetneq\tilde{D}(a)
.
It is easy to check that
\Omega(a)
satisfies
the same properties as in Theorem 2.1.

(ii)
The pseudoconvexity of a domain
has a significant influence on
the dimension of its Bergman space.
Wiegerinck
[
14
]
conjectured that
property (
2.1
) always holds
for every pseudoconvex domain
\Omega
.
As far as we know, this conjecture remains
unsolved at present,
but it has already been affirmatively
settled in many cases
(
[
20
,
21
,
3
,
10
,
4
,
12
]
).
In particular,
it has been confirmed
in the case of Reinhardt domains
(
[
20
,
21
,
3
]
).
From these results, the domain

D(a)
with
a\geq\frac{1}{n-1}

cannot be pseudoconvex.

3.
Holomorphic sectional curvatures
of the Bergman metrics

Let
\Omega
be a domain in
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
.
The
Bergman kernel

K_{\Omega}(z,w)
of
\Omega

is defined by

K_{\Omega}(z,w)=\sum_{\alpha}\phi_{\alpha}(z)\overline{\phi_{\alpha}(w)},

where
\{\phi_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha}
is a complete orthonormal
basis of
A^{2}(\Omega)
.
Its restriction to the diagonal,
K_{\Omega}(z,z)
,
is denoted simply by
K_{\Omega}(z)
.

It has recently been recognized
that the issue of the dimension of
A^{2}(\Omega)
is
closely related to that of
constant holomorphic sectional curvatures
of the Bergman metric.
Recently,
Huang, Li and Treuer
[
8
]
has obtained
several significant results on this topic.
In their paper, they show that the Bergman metric
of the domain
D_{\rm HL}(\alpha)

in (
1.2
) has constant holomorphic
sectional curvature equal to
2
.
In this note, we present
a general-dimensional example
D(a)
in (
1.1
)
as follows.

Theorem 3.1
.

If

\frac{2}{n-1}<a\leq\frac{3}{n-1}
,
then the following statements hold:

(i)

The Bergman space is

A^{2}(D(a))={\rm Span}\{1,z_{1},\ldots,z_{n}\}.

(ii)

The Bergman metric of
D(a)
has constant
holomorphic sectional curvature equal to
2
.

Proof.

(i)    It follows from Theorem 2.1 that
the condition

1<(n-1)a-1\leq 2
is equivalent to (i).

(ii)
From (i),
the Bergman kernel of
D(a)
can be written
as

(3.1)

K_{D(a)}(z)=c_{0}+\sum_{k=1}^{n}c_{k}|z_{k}|^{2}\quad(z\in D(a)),

where

c_{0}={\rm vol}(D(a))^{-1},\quad c_{k}=\|z_{k}\|^{-2}=\left(\int_{D(a)}|z_{k}|^{2}dV(z)\right)^{-1}.

Furthermore, we have

K_{D(a)}(z)=c_{0}\left(1+\sum_{k=1}^{n}\tilde{c}_{k}|z_{k}|^{2}\right)\quad(z\in D(a)),

where
\tilde{c}_{k}=c_{k}/c_{0}
for

k=1,\ldots,n
.
Through
the scaling
(w_{1},\ldots,w_{n})=(\sqrt{\tilde{c}_{1}}z_{1},\ldots,\sqrt{\tilde{c}_{n}}z_{n})
,
the Bergman metric of
D(a)

is isometric to the Fubini-Study metric
of
{\mathbb{P}}^{n}
restricted to
a domain in the cell
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
with

\omega_{FS}=\partial\bar{\partial}\log\left(1+\sum_{k=1}^{n}|w_{k}|^{2}\right).

Therefore, the Bergman metric of
D(a)

has a constant holomorphic
sectional curvature equal
to
2
.
∎

4.
Holomorphic automorphism groups

By applying the results of A. Yamamori in

[
15
,
16
,
17
]

(see also
[
11
]
),
we can describe the structure of the holomorphic
automorphism group of
D(a)
for

a\in(\frac{2}{n-1},\frac{3}{n-1}]
.
The results stated in this section follow directly
from a combination of Yamamori’s works.
In fact,
the argument presented below is based on an idea of
Yamamori,
communicated during a lecture at a symposium
in Fukuoka (
[
18
]
),
which has appeared in the preprint
[
19
]
.
We include it here for the reader’s convenience.

Let
\Omega
be a domain in
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
and
let
{\rm Aut}(\Omega)

be the holomorphic automorphism group
on
\Omega
.
The
n\times n
matrix
T_{\Omega}(z,w)

is defined by

T_{\Omega}(z,w)=\begin{pmatrix}\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial z_{1}\partial\overline{w_{1}}}\log K_{\Omega}(z,w)&\cdots&\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial z_{n}\partial\overline{w_{1}}}\log K_{\Omega}(z,w)\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial z_{1}\partial\overline{w_{n}}}\log K_{\Omega}(z,w)&\cdots&\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial z_{n}\partial\overline{w_{n}}}\log K_{\Omega}(z,w)\end{pmatrix},

for
(z,w)\in\Omega\times\Omega
such that

K_{\Omega}(z,w)\neq 0
.
The function
B_{\Omega}(z)
is defined by

(4.1)

B_{\Omega}(z):=\frac{\det T_{\Omega}(z,z)}{K_{\Omega}(z)},

for
z\in\Omega
such that
K_{\Omega}(z)>0
.
Let
\Omega_{1}
,
\Omega_{2}
be domains
in
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
and let
F:\Omega_{1}\to\Omega_{2}

be a biholomorphism.
From the transformation laws:

\begin{split}&K_{\Omega_{1}}(z,w)=\overline{\det J_{F}(w)}\cdot K_{\Omega_{2}}(F(z),F(w))\cdot\det J_{F}(z),\\
&T_{\Omega_{1}}(z,w)=\overline{J_{F}(w)^{\intercal}}\cdot T_{\Omega_{2}}(F(z),F(w))\cdot J_{F}(z),\end{split}

where
J_{F}(z)
is the Jacobian matrix of
F
at
z
,
it follows that the function in (
4.1
) is invariant
under the biholomorphism in the sense that

(4.2)

B_{\Omega_{1}}(z)=B_{\Omega_{2}}(F(z)).

Of course, the above transformation laws
hold on the set where the corresponding quantities
are well defined.

The following theorem
is a strong generalization of
the famous theorem of H. Cartan.

Theorem 4.1

(
[
9
,
15
,
11
]
)
.

Let
\Omega_{1}

and
\Omega_{2}
be domains in
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
containing the origin,
and
suppose that each satisfies the following two conditions:

(i)

K_{\Omega}(z,0)\equiv K_{\Omega}(0,0)>0
for
z\in\Omega
;

(ii)

T_{\Omega}(z,0)\equiv T_{\Omega}(0,0)
is positive definite
for
z\in\Omega
.

Then every biholomorphism
F:\Omega_{1}\to\Omega_{2}

satisfying
F(0)=0
is linear.

Many important sufficient conditions for the
above conditions (i) and (ii)
have been established
(
[
9
,
15
,
11
,
16
,
17
]
).
In particular,
the following lemma will be useful for our analysis.

Lemma 4.2

(
[
17
]
)
.

If
\Omega
is a Reinhardt domain
containing the origin and

A^{2}(\Omega)
contains
1,z_{1},\ldots,z_{n}
,
then
\Omega
satisfies the conditions (i) and (ii)
in Theorem 4.1.

Using Theorem 4.1 with Lemma 4.2,
we investigate the structure of
the holomorphic automorphism group
of the domain
D(a)
in (
1.1
).
Since
A^{2}(D(a))
contains a nontrivial
constant function by Theorem 3.1,
it follows that
K_{D(a)}(z)>0
for
z\in D(a)
,
which ensures that the function

B_{D(a)}(z)
is well-defined on
D(a)
.

Theorem 4.3
.

If
\frac{2}{n-1}<a\leq\frac{3}{n-1}
,
then every
\varphi\in{\rm Aut}(D(a))
is linear.

Proof.

Since
A^{2}(D(a))
contains
\{1,z_{1},\ldots,z_{n}\}

from Theorem 2.1, Lemma 4.2 implies that
the domain
D(a)
satisfies
the conditions (i) and (ii) in Theorem 4.1.
We now apply Theorem 4.1 to the case

\Omega_{1}=\Omega_{2}=D(a)
.
It remains to prove
that every
F\in{\rm Aut}(D(a))

satisfies
F(0)=0
.

We prove this by contradiction.
Let us assume that there exist

F_{0}\in{\rm Aut}(D(a))
and

z_{0}\in D(a)\setminus\{0\}
such that

F_{0}(0)=z_{0}
.
From the explicit formula for the Bergman kernel
in (
3.1
),
we can compute the exact expression of

B_{D(a)}
as follows:

(4.3)

B_{D(a)}(z)=\frac{\prod_{j=0}^{n}c_{j}}{K_{D(a)}(z)^{n+2}}=\frac{\prod_{j=0}^{n}c_{j}}{(c_{0}+\sum_{j=1}^{n}c_{j}|z_{j}|^{2})^{n+2}}.

In particular,

(4.4)

B_{D(a)}(z_{0})<B_{D(a)}(0)\left(=\frac{\prod_{j=1}^{n}c_{j}}{c_{0}^{n+1}}\right).

Let
w_{0}=F_{0}^{-1}(0)\;(\neq 0)
.
Then (
4.2
) is equivalent to

B_{D(a)}(F_{0}(0))<B_{D(a)}(F_{0}(w_{0}))
.
From (
4.2
), we have

B_{D(a)}(0)<B_{D(a)}(w_{0})
,
which is a contradiction.
∎

Theorem 4.4
.

If
\frac{2}{n-1}<a_{1},a_{2}\leq\frac{3}{n-1}
,
then every biholomorphism
from
D(a_{1})
to
D(a_{2})
is linear.
In particular, if
D(a_{1})
and
D(a_{2})
are
biholomorphically equivalent, then they
are linearly equivalent.

Proof.

In a manner similar to the proof of Theorem 4.3,
it is straightforward to see that
every biholomorphism
F:D(a_{1})\to D(a_{2})

satisfies
F(0)=0
.
Therefore, from Theorem 4.1,
every biholomorphism

F:D(a_{1})\to D(a_{2})
is linear.
∎

Remark 4.5
.

By Theorem 4.2 in
[
19
]
,
the statement of Theorem 4.1 in the two-dimensional
case can be strengthened as follows:

D(a_{1})
and
D(a_{2})
are biholomorphically
equivalent if and only if
a_{1}=a_{2}
.
Since
the result of Soldatkin
[
13
]
,
which is valid only in the two-dimensional case,
plays a crucial role in the proof of

[
19
]
,
it seems to be difficult to obtain
an analogous result
in the general
n
-dimensional case.
