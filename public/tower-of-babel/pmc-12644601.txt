Epidemiology and survival of patients with T-cell prolymphocytic leukemia
Authors: Nadine Wolgast, Eric Amelunxen, Lars Fransecky, Philipp Altrock, Thomas Beder, Julian Ronnacker, Frederik Peters, Soo-Zin Kim-Wanner, Monika Brüggemann, Christiane Pott, Alexander Katalinic, Claudia D. Baldus, David Baden
Published: 2025

T-prolymphocytic leukemia (T-PLL) is a very rare but aggressive T-cell neoplasm affecting mainly elderly patients [1]. Clinical presentation typically involves progressive lymphocytosis, hepatosplenomegaly, lymphadenopathy, and extramedullary symptoms, although up to 20% of patients might initially present with a stable disease course [2]. Consensus criteria for diagnosis and treatment have only recently been defined and clinical trials remain sparse. Consequently, treatment options for patients with T-PLL are limited and reported outcomes in small single institution studies remain poor with a median overall survival of 7.5 to 19 months [1–3]. Currently, the IgG1 monoclonal CD52 antibody alemtuzumab is recommended as first-line treatment, achieving high initial response rates in newly diagnosed patients [4]. Alternatively, the combination of Fludarabine, Mitoxantrone and Cyclophosphamide (FMC) has been used. Subsequent autologous or allogenic hematopoietic stem cell transplant (HCT) in complete remission (CR) should follow in fit patients and can induce long-term disease-free survival. However, due to the disease demographics, only 30–50% of patients are eligible for intensive HCT regimens [5, 6]. In refractory cases, Pentostatin and Bendamustine have shown limited efficacy, whereas newer options such as Ruxolitinib are being explored with promising early results [3, 4, 7].

Due to the rarity of the disease, real-world and epidemiological data on T-PLL are scarce. Better insights into T-PLL occurrence and real-world treatment data could enhance understanding and inform future advancements. To date, the most comprehensive analyses are from two US-based studies, including a cohort of 119 cases in an institutional report and 1 522 cases in a recently published SEER analysis [3, 8].

Here, we provide population-based data on T-PLL incidence, survival, and treatment patterns within Germany, complementing the sparse existing epidemiological data and contributing to a broader knowledge of disease patterns.

This study utilized data from all 16 German federal states, obtained through their respective epidemiological and clinical state cancer registries as well as the German Center for Cancer Registry Data. By law, treating physicians are required to report diagnoses, treatment modifications, relapses, and deaths to their respective state cancer registries [9]. Survival data are harmonized with local population registers. Patients with T-PLL were identified by the respective ICD-10GM-Code C91.6 and ICD-O-3-Code 9834/3 between 2016 and 2021. Current population data were retrieved from the Federal Statistical Office of Germany [10]. Incidence and mortality rates were calculated using data from the German Center for Cancer Registry Data, including adult cases identified through death certificates (n = 305). Age-standardization was performed using the 2013 European Standard Population. Age- and therapy-specific survival analyses were based solely on data directly obtained from the state cancer registries resulting in a smaller cohort (n = 245). As of the time of analysis, 2021 data were not available from five state registries. To account for potentially incomplete data and outliers, average incidence rates were computed as the mean from 2017 to 2020.

Age distributions were summarized using median and interquartile range (IQR) and compared via the Wilcoxon U-test. Survival analysis was conducted using the Kaplan-Meier method and log-rank test. Follow-up time was calculated from the date of diagnosis until death or last known contact. Patients who were alive at the end of follow-up or lost to follow-up were censored at their last known alive date. Multivariate analysis assessed the impact of age, sex and type of therapy on survival, reporting hazard ratios (HR) with confidence intervals (CI). Statistical significance was set at a two-sided alpha level of 0.05. Early mortality was defined as death occurring within 60 days of diagnosis. For reporting our results, we adhered to the STrengthening the Reporting of OBservational studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guideline [11]. Smoothed incidence trends were visualized using line plots with rounded line ends and joins to reduce visual noise and emphasize overall patterns over time. All analyses were performed using R version 4.4.2.

We identified 305 patients reported by the German Center for Cancer Registry Data with T-PLL diagnosed from 2016 to 2021, averaging around 51 new cases annually in Germany. Median age at diagnosis was 74.3 years (IQR 62.5–80.0), with 59 patients younger than 60 years and two patients under the age of 18 years. The cohort included 180 (59%) male patients, which were nearly of the same age compared to women when diagnosed with T-PLL (70 vs. 74 years, p = 0.17).

Mean age-standardized incidence rate was 0.54/1,000,000 (95%CI: 0.48–0.65) with a rate of 0.74 per 1 000 000 (95%CI: 0.71–0.91) in men and 0.39/1,000,000 (95%CI: 0.23–0.53) in women, respectively (ratio 1.80, Fig. 1A). Age-specific incidence rates showed T-PLL was most frequent in elderly patients (1.76/1,000,000 in 70–74 years up to 2.94/1,000,000 in ≥85 years) and nearly absent under the age of 40 years (Fig. 1B). Survival analyses of patients with treatment information based on data obtained directly from the state cancer registries included 245 patients with a median age of 71.0 years (IQR 60.0–78.0) and 144 (59%) patients being male (Supplement Table 1). Median overall survival (OS) was 19.2 months, with a median follow-up time of 34.1 months. One- and three-year survival rates were 63.0% (95%CI: 57.2–69.5) and 30.9% (95%CI: 24.7–38.5), respectively. When stratifying OS by age, we found no significant differences in outcomes of patients aged 0–59, 60–69 and 70–79 years (median OS 53.1–21.0 months, p = 0.18) (Fig. 2A, Supplement Table 2). However, survival was significantly impaired in patients ≥80 years (median OS 5.0 months, p < 0.001) compared to younger age groups. Median OS between male and female patients did not differ significantly (19.2 vs 19.0 months, p = 0.56, Supplement Fig. 1). Accordingly, multivariate analysis confirmed age at diagnosis (HR 1.03, 95%CI: 1.02–1.05, p < 0.001) as a significant predictor of survival, while sex was not (HR 0.99, 95%CI: 0.72–1.36, p = 0.948; Supplement Table 3). For 174 patients, detailed data about applied treatments were available. Application of at least one course of Alemtuzumab was documented in 121 patients, while HCT following any form of immuno- or chemotherapy was recorded in 33 patients (24 allogenic, 9 autologous). Patients receiving autologous or allogeneic HCT were younger compared to non-HCT patients (62.0 vs 74.0 years, p < 0.001) and had the highest survival probability in the cohort, with a median OS of 25.9 months (95%CI: 19.9–NA, p = 0.036, Fig. 2B). Those with no documented therapy had a significantly lower survival rate, with a median OS of 16.3 months (95%CI: 10.4–NA; p = 0.032). However, in a multivariate analysis including age, sex, and therapy type, treatment was not a significant predictor of survival (Supplement Table 3).

This analysis provides key epidemiological insights into T-PLL in Germany and represents one of the largest population-based studies to date on its incidence and outcomes. We found an age-standardized incidence rate of 0.54 per million, comparable to the 0.49 per million reported by Vardell et al. for U.S. cases reported between 2001 and 2019 [8]. These rates may rise as the number of older people is expected to increase in most countries, with the population aged over 65 years in Germany likely growing by 26% to 23.1 million in 2050 [12]. However, absolute case numbers will likely remain low due to the rarity of T-PLL. While incidence rates are comparable in western countries, incidence of T-PLL in Asia seems lower although larger analysis are lacking [13]. These variations in incidence rates may be caused by differences in population demographics, socioeconomic factors, and other health-related influences. Further research is needed to explore these factors more deeply.

Despite a higher median age at diagnosis in Germany (74 years) compared to the U.S. (68 years), overall survival appeared longer (19.2 months vs. 14.4 months) [8]. In historic studies published 20–30 years ago, OS-rates of 10–16 months depending on response were reported, which suggests only modest improvements in outcomes have been achieved [1, 14]. While we found age to be a key factor in T-PLL survival, Vardell et al. showed that less comorbidities and care at academic centers also improved outcomes, underscoring the role of factors beyond age [8].

Since Alemtuzumab has become the standard of care around 2010, we observe high rates of Alemtuzumab-based regimens in our cohort. However, our analysis, consistent with other studies, indicates that autologous or allogeneic HCT might provide additional survival benefits for patients, underscoring the need to allocate eligible individuals to transplant [6, 15]. It remains unclear whether this also applies to patients in complete remission after initial treatment [3].

Although large retrospective database studies have limitations, our analysis offers valuable reference data for the rare disease T-PLL. There remains an urgent need to optimize treatment options, as current survival rates remain poor.
