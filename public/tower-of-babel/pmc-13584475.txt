Parents’ perception of common childhood eye diseases: a community-based cross-sectional study
Authors: Walaa Abdullah Aldairi, Nouran Alshehri, May Alkhaldi, Abdulmalek W. Alhithlool, Ossama M. Zakaria
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundChildhood eye diseases can significantly affect vision. This may have a negative impact on mental development, academic performance, and quality of life when not detected early. Parents are often the first to notice eye problems in their children. However, awareness of common pediatric eye conditions remains variable. This study aimed to assess parents’ awareness, attitudes, and practices as regards childhood eye diseases in a local community setting.MethodsA cross-sectional questionnaire-based study was conducted among parents of children aged 18 years or younger in a local community setting. A quick response (QR) code was used to distribute a pre-tested questionnaire covering sociodemographic characteristics and knowledge of common pediatric eye diseases. Parents’ perception of pediatric eye diseases and their management was also evaluated. This was performed via a Likert-scale scoring system, and total knowledge scores were classified at the median into two levels, poor and good. The study was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of King Faisal University, and informed consent was obtained from all participants. Statistical analysis was performed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS), with chi-square tests and multiple linear regression applied where appropriate.ResultsA total of 267 parents were included, of whom 181 (67.8%) were females. Nearly half (46.1%) reported that their child had an existing eye condition, with refractive errors being the most common (49.5%). Awareness was moderate. Overall, 163 participants (61.0%) agreed that wearing glasses before the age of 7 years supports normal visual development, and 167 (62.5%) recognised that early treatment of amblyopia leads to better outcomes. Gaps were also identified among the study population, as 92 (34.5%) correctly defined cataract and 126 (47.2%) believed that occasional eye deviation in young children is normal. As per the participating parents’ practice, 178 (66.7%) reported that their child had undergone an eye examination. Among them, 37 (13.9%) had their first examination before one year of age. Moreover, attitudes were generally positive, with 230 (86.1%) agreeing that children should wear glasses if needed and 167 (62.5%) willing to accept surgical intervention. No statistically significant association was found between knowledge levels and any sociodemographic variable (p > 0.05).ConclusionsParental awareness of childhood eye diseases in this community was variable, with some misconceptions and gaps in preventive practices. Knowledge levels were not associated with the sociodemographic variables assessed. Community-wide health education and school-based screening may help support earlier detection of childhood eye diseases.

Background

Visual health in childhood is linked to a child’s development, academic achievement, and quality of life. Undetected eye conditions may affect the child, the family, and the wider community through reduced productivity and increased healthcare costs [1]. The burden of pediatric visual impairment is influenced by socioeconomic conditions, the availability of ophthalmic services, and social norms [2]. Globally, an estimated 19 million children are visually impaired and approximately 1.4 million are blind [3].

In Saudi Arabia, studies from several cities have reported refractive errors, strabismus, and amblyopia as common causes of visual impairment in children [4–7]. Many of these conditions can be treated or managed when detected early. In 1999, the World Health Organization (WHO) and the International Agency for the Prevention of Blindness (IAPB) launched the Vision 2020: The Right to Sight initiative, which aimed to reduce avoidable blindness worldwide [8].

Parents have a central role in their children’s healthcare decisions. Whether a child receives timely eye care often depends on whether parents notice warning signs and understand the value of early assessment [9]. Previous studies have reported that parental knowledge of pediatric eye health is often limited, which may delay diagnosis [24]. Reported awareness levels vary with parental education, socioeconomic background, and access to health information [10, 11].

Although regional data are available, few studies have examined parental awareness in local community settings. This study assessed parents’ knowledge, attitudes, and practices regarding common childhood eye diseases in a local community.

Methods

This cross-sectional questionnaire-based study took place in a local community setting between 2023 and 2026. The report was phrased in accordance with the standard Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) checklist.

The targeted population was parents of children aged 18 years or younger residing in the local community. Parents living outside the community and adults without children were excluded. The sample size was calculated using the formula n = Z²P(1 − P)/d², where Z = 1.96 at a 95% confidence level, P = 0.50 (assumed proportion), and d = 0.05 (margin of error), giving a required sample of 384 participants. A total of 400 responses were received. Of these, 267 fulfilled the eligibility criteria of being parents of children below 18 years of age and were included in the analysis; the remaining 133 responses did not meet these criteria and were excluded.

The questionnaire was distributed by means of a quick response (QR) code, circulated both digitally and on printed handouts. In both cases participants scanned the code and completed the questionnaire electronically on a smartphone (Appendix 1).

The questionnaire included four domains: sociodemographic characteristics, including gender, age, educational level, occupation, monthly income, use of corrective lenses, and family history of eye disease; general knowledge about eye care and sources of health information; knowledge of common childhood eye diseases, including amblyopia, cataract, and glaucoma; and questions on parents’ attitudes and practices regarding childhood eye diseases and their management. The questionnaire was prepared and reviewed by specialist ophthalmology and epidemiology consultants before undergoing a pilot study, and its internal consistency was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha.

Questions evaluating knowledge, attitude, and practice were designed in two formats: Likert-scale items and closed yes-or-no items. A total knowledge score was calculated for each participant, and scores were classified at the median into two levels, poor and good. The continuous knowledge score was retained for the regression analysis.

This study was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of King Faisal University (Approval No. KFU-REC-2023-MAY-ETHICS859). Informed consent was obtained from all participants before participation. Participants were informed of the study objectives, the voluntary nature of participation, and their right to withdraw at any time. No personally identifiable information was collected, and all procedures followed the Declaration of Helsinki.

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS version 29. Associations between sociodemographic variables and knowledge level were assessed using the chi-square test, with statistical significance set at p < 0.05. Multiple linear regression was used to identify independent predictors of knowledge scores.

Results

Sociodemographic characteristics

A total of 267 parents were included in the final analysis. Females made up 181 (67.8%) of the sample. The most common age group was 41–50 years (33.3%), followed by 31–40 years (28.1%) and 18–30 years (22.5%). Most participants held a bachelor’s degree (46.8%), and nearly half were employed (49.1%). Monthly family income was most often in the 5,000–10,000 SAR range (35.6%). Among participants, 149 (55.8%) wore glasses or contact lenses, and 146 (54.7%) reported a family history of eye disease. Sources of information

Social media was the most frequently reported source of information about childhood eye diseases (20.9%), followed by family and friends (20.2%) and doctors (18.0%). Internet searches accounted for 11.5%, television for 8.2%, and teachers for 5.9%. Nurses, neighbours, and radio were among the least reported sources (Fig. 1).

Knowledge about eye care

Among the 267 participants, 123 (46.1%) reported that their child had an existing eye condition, with refractive errors being the most common (49.5%), as shown in Fig. 2. Educational problems were the most frequently reported perceived consequence of untreated eye disease (33.0%), followed by social (24.7%) and health-related problems (21.3%). In addition, 53 participants (19.9%) reported that eye disease has no effect on the child.

For specific knowledge items, 163 (61.0%) agreed that wearing glasses before the age of 7 supports normal visual development, while 126 (47.2%) reported that occasional eye deviation is normal in children aged 1–7 years. These findings are shown in Fig. 3.

Awareness of specific eye diseases

Parental awareness varied across conditions (Table 2). For amblyopia, 142 participants (53.2%) identified it as reduced vision in one or both eyes, while 54 (20.2%) selected optic nerve damage. Among reported causes, refractive errors (57.7%), genetic factors (55.1%), and screen exposure (54.7%) were most frequently selected. Covering the healthy eye was the most frequently selected treatment (66.7%), and 167 (62.5%) agreed that early treatment gives better outcomes.

Knowledge of cataract was more limited. In total, 92 (34.5%) selected lens opacification as the definition, while 168 (62.9%) reported that it can be treated surgically. Fewer than one third (28.5%) reported that children can develop cataracts, and over half (50.9%) were unsure whether it can cause permanent blindness in children. Responses on glaucoma followed a similar pattern: 84 (31.5%) reported that it can affect children, and 132 (49.4%) were unsure whether congenital glaucoma can lead to blindness.

Eye care practices and attitudes

Two thirds of children (66.7%) had undergone at least one eye examination. Among these, the first examination was most often performed between 1 and 5 years of age (25.1%), and 37 (13.9%) had a first examination before one year of age. Regarding examination frequency, 25.5% of parents reported that examinations are needed only when the child reports a problem, and 24.7% reported annual examinations. Most parents (86.1%) agreed that children should wear glasses if needed, and 167 (62.5%) were willing to allow surgical intervention. Among those who were unwilling, concern about consequences and about harm to the eyes were the most frequently reported reasons (12.0% each), followed by cost (6.7%) and concern about blindness (6.0%). Association between sociodemographic factors and knowledge

When asked what observations would prompt them to seek eye care, school teacher comments were the most frequently selected (77.2%), followed by learning difficulties and close-range viewing (71.9% each). Eye rubbing (64.4%), double vision (61.8%), and squinting (60.3%) were also frequently selected. Excessive tearing (45.7%) and eye redness (47.2%) were selected less often (Fig. 4).

No statistically significant association was found between knowledge level and any of the sociodemographic variables assessed (all p > 0.05). Males showed a slightly higher proportion of good knowledge (53.5%) than females (51.4%), and this difference was not significant (χ² = 0.104, p = 0.747). Knowledge tended to be higher at higher educational levels, with 26 postgraduate participants (60.5%) classified as having good knowledge, but this was not statistically significant (χ² = 5.518, p = 0.238). No significant associations were found with profession, income, spectacle use, or family history of eye disease. Multiple linear regression did not identify any independent predictors of knowledge scores; educational level (B = 0.288, p = 0.073) and wearing glasses or contact lenses (B = 0.595, p = 0.101) showed positive coefficients that did not reach statistical significance. Discussion

This study assessed parents’ perception of common childhood eye diseases in a local community. Knowledge was variable across the domains assessed, with adequate awareness in some areas and misconceptions in others, and this pattern was similar across the demographic groups examined.

Most participants in this sample were female, which is consistent with previous reports that mothers are commonly involved in child healthcare decisions and are therefore more frequently represented in such surveys [12, 13]. Gender was not associated with knowledge level, which is in keeping with other Saudi studies reporting similar awareness levels in both sexes [14, 15].

Social media (20.9%) and family or friends (20.2%) were reported more often than doctors (18.0%) as sources of eye health information. Similar patterns have been described in the literature on digital health communication [16]. These channels can reach large audiences but may also carry inaccurate information, which may partly explain some of the misconceptions observed, including the report by 47.2% of participants that occasional eye deviation is normal in young children. Persistent or intermittent misalignment beyond early infancy warrants assessment, and this belief may be associated with delayed presentation for strabismus. The comparatively limited role of healthcare professionals as information sources suggests scope for wider health education outreach [17].

Refractive errors were the most frequently reported condition among children with eye problems (49.5%), which is consistent with reports identifying them as a leading cause of visual impairment in children [18]. Parents commonly reported educational and social consequences of untreated eye disease [19]. However, 19.9% reported that eye disease has no effect on the child, which may be associated with delays in seeking care.

For amblyopia, just over half of parents identified the condition as reduced vision, and most agreed that early treatment gives better outcomes. Timing of intervention is important in this condition. Misconceptions about causes, including screen use and optic nerve damage, suggest that correct identification of the term does not always reflect complete understanding [20].

The largest knowledge gaps were related to cataract and glaucoma in children. Fewer than one third of parents reported that children can develop cataracts (28.5%), and 27.3% reported that congenital glaucoma can cause permanent blindness. For both conditions, the most frequent response was uncertainty rather than an incorrect answer, which may reflect limited exposure to information about these conditions in a pediatric context. Similar findings have been reported in community-based studies [21]. Both conditions are treatable when identified early, which supports the case for targeted public education.

Two thirds of children in this sample had undergone an eye examination, which is higher than rates reported in some other settings [22]. Current guidance does not recommend a comprehensive eye examination in the first year of life for children without risk factors; red reflex assessment by a pediatrician during infancy and a comprehensive vision assessment between 3 and 5 years of age are recommended [26]. The finding that 13.9% of children had a first examination before one year of age is therefore not in itself a shortfall. Of more relevance is that 25.5% of parents reported that examinations are needed only when a child reports a problem, which suggests a symptom-driven rather than a routine approach to eye care, consistent with the parental health beliefs described by Masarwa et al. [25]. Young children may not report reduced vision, and conditions such as amblyopia can progress without obvious symptoms.

Attitudes toward treatment were generally positive: most parents accepted the use of glasses, and most were willing to consider surgery if needed. The most frequently reported reasons for declining surgery were concern about complications and about harm to the eyes, rather than cultural factors or cost. These concerns may be addressed through counselling at the point of care [23].

Knowledge level was not significantly associated with any sociodemographic variable assessed, including education, income, and family history of eye disease, and the regression analysis did not identify independent predictors of knowledge scores. The scoping review by Ghani et al. [24] similarly reported that parental knowledge and practices regarding child eye health are frequently inadequate across the studies reviewed. That review did not analyse variation by demographic group, and the absence of demographic associations in the present study should therefore be interpreted as a finding of this sample rather than as confirmation of the review’s conclusions. Taken together, these findings suggest that health education directed at parents in this community may not need to be restricted to particular sociodemographic groups, although this observation requires confirmation in larger samples.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. The cross-sectional design does not allow causal inference, and the self-reported data are subject to recall and social desirability bias. The sample was drawn from a single community and may not represent wider populations. The 267 participants analysed fell short of the calculated target of 384, giving a margin of error of approximately 6% rather than the intended 5%. Because the printed handouts carried a QR code rather than a paper questionnaire, completion required a smartphone with internet connectivity, so parents without such access may be under-represented. Three knowledge items, indicated in Table 2, present a plausible direction of response and may have prompted affirmative answers by inference rather than by knowledge; results for these items should be interpreted with caution. Knowledge was classified at the median into two levels, as the small size of some subgroups would have left a finer split with expected cell counts below those required for reliable chi-square testing. The questionnaire was developed for this study and, although expert-reviewed, pilot-tested, and assessed for internal consistency, has not been externally validated in other populations.

Future studies would benefit from larger multi-centre samples and longitudinal designs. Interventional work examining the effect of digital campaigns, school-based screening, and targeted health education on parental awareness and early detection would also be of value.

Conclusions

Parents in this community showed variable awareness of common childhood eye diseases, alongside generally positive attitudes toward treatment that were not consistently matched by preventive practices. Knowledge levels were not associated with the sociodemographic variables assessed. Community health education, a greater role for healthcare professionals as information sources, and school-based eye screening may support earlier detection and management of childhood eye diseases.
