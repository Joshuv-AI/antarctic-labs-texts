A multicentre case-control study about effectiveness of pneumococcal vaccination against pneumonia hospitalization in at-risk adults

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCommunity-acquired pneumonia (CAP) is a leading cause of morbidity in adults with chronic conditions. Although pneumococcal vaccine effectiveness (VE) has been demonstrated in older adults, evidence is scant regarding adults < 65 years. We evaluated pneumococcal VE against CAP-related hospitalization in at-risk adults aged 18–64 years.MethodsWe conducted a multicentre case-control study in Spain (2020–2024). Cases were adults aged 18–64 years with risk conditions, hospitalized for ≥ 24 h, with radiological and clinical patterns compatible with CAP. Controls were hospitalized patients with risk conditions but without pneumonia. Vaccination status was classified as 23-valent pneumococcal polysaccharide vaccine only, pneumococcal conjugate vaccine only, sequential vaccination, or unvaccinated. Logistic regression was used to estimate crude and adjusted odds ratio (OR) values, with VE calculated as (1–OR) × 100. Analyses were stratified by immune status, and a sensitivity analysis was performed excluding SARS-CoV-2 cases.ResultsWe included 805 cases and 806 controls. None of the pneumococcal vaccination strategies showed significant effectiveness against hospitalization for CAP. In immunocompromised individuals, 23-valent pneumococcal polysaccharide vaccine showed significantly negative adjusted VE (-142.0, 95%CI: -504.4 to -1.1). Background

Community-acquired pneumonia (CAP) represents a significant morbidity and mortality burden, being a major public health challenge worldwide. CAP incidence varies widely across countries and healthcare settings, influenced by population characteristics and study methodology. A systematic review covering studies published from 2000 to 2016 reported CAP incidence in Europe to range between 68 and 7000 cases per 100,000 population [1]. Greater CAP incidence in older adults and individuals with chronic comorbidities implies higher hospitalization rates and longer hospital stays—with the corresponding economic costs—and greater lethality [1, 2]. Moreover, several established clinical and behavioural risk factors, including smoking and alcohol consumption, have been associated with increased susceptibility to CAP [3].

In most CAP cases, the causative agent remains unidentified. Among identified agents, in both immunocompetent and immunocompromised individuals the most common cause is Streptococcus pneumoniae or pneumococcus [4], followed by viruses, which are increasingly recognized in the aetiology of CAP due to improvements in diagnostic techniques, most especially since the onset of the pandemic caused by SARS-CoV-2 [5]. This pandemic altered the epidemiology of respiratory infection. During 2020–2021, several countries reported a decline in CAP hospitalizations caused by pathogens other than SARS-CoV-2—primarily due to the implementation of non-pharmaceutical interventions—and a subsequent increase in cases once public health restrictions were relaxed [6]. While CAP caused by pneumococcus is usually described as a single entity, a wide diversity of serotypes is involved (more than 100 serotypes recognized to date) [7].

Pneumococcal vaccines, which aim to prevent severe pneumococcal disease, include polysaccharide vaccines or conjugate vaccines. The 23-valent polysaccharide pneumococcal vaccine (PPSV23) contains capsular polysaccharides from 23 serotypes, while pneumococcal conjugate vaccines (PCV) contain capsular polysaccharides of different serotypes conjugated to a protein carrier.

The PPSV23 vaccine was authorized for use in Spain in 1999 and was recommended for high-risk populations from 2000 [8], including immunocompetent individuals with chronic conditions such as cardiovascular disease, chronic respiratory conditions, diabetes mellitus, cirrhosis, and alcoholism predisposing to pneumococcal disease or its complications. PPSV23 was also recommended for immunocompromised individuals, including those with anatomical or functional asplenia, Hodgkin’s disease, lymphoma, multiple myeloma, renal insufficiency, nephrotic syndrome, sickle cell anaemia, and persons undergoing immunosuppressive treatment (post-organ transplantation). Another priority group for vaccination was individuals living with HIV, irrespective of the presence of symptoms. In 2004 the Interterritorial Council of the Spanish National Health System updated its recommendations to include pneumococcal vaccination for individuals aged ≥ 65 years, and in 2019, it was included in the lifelong vaccination schedule [9]. All vaccines included in the Spanish vaccination schedule are provided free of charge.

The four PCVs available in Spain cover 10, 13, 15, or 20 serotypes [10]. The 13-serotype pneumococcal conjugate vaccine, PCV13, authorized in 2010 and recommended for the paediatric population, was included at 2, 4, and 11 months in the vaccination schedules of all 17 Spanish regions by 2016. Recommendations were also extended to individuals up to 18 years old with risk factors, for PCV13 to be used in conjunction with sequential administration of PPSV23: one dose of PCV13 + one dose of PPSV23 12 months after the PCV13 dose (minimum 8 weeks), and a PPSV23 booster dose five years later [10].

Available in Spain since August 2022 is the 20-serotype pneumococcal conjugate vaccine, PCV20, which is recommended for adults aged ≥ 18 years with risk conditions and as a PPSV23 substitute in persons aged ≥ 65 years [11]. In 2023, the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention updated recommendations for adults aged 19–64 years with certain risk conditions, recommending vaccination with PCV (15-valent or 20-valent) if no or only one dose of PPV23 had previously been administered [12].

The effectiveness of pneumococcal vaccination in preventing hospitalization has been demonstrated over time for older adults [13, 14]. However, less information has been published regarding young adults with risk conditions.

The aim of this study was to assess the effectiveness of pneumococcal vaccination in preventing CAP hospitalizations among adults aged 18–64 years with underlying medical conditions or established behavioral risk factors for CAP.

Methods

Study design and setting

We conducted a multicentre case-control study in 16 hospitals in eight Spanish regions (Andalusia, Asturias, the Basque Country, Castile and Leon, Catalonia, Madrid, Navarre, and the Valencian Community) with a population of 4,349,685 individuals aged 15–64 years. Eligible cases and controls were recruited between September 2020 and June 2024.

Study population

Cases were defined as patients aged 18–64 years with at least one underlying risk condition, hospitalized for ≥ 24 h due to pneumonia. A CAP diagnosis required radiological evidence of a recent pulmonary infiltrate consistent with pneumonia, plus at least one clinical sign or symptom of acute lower respiratory tract infection (cough, pleuritic chest pain, dyspnoea, fever > 38 °C, hypothermia < 35 °C, or abnormal respiratory auscultation not attributable to another cause). Healthcare-associated pneumonia (> 48 h after admission) was excluded.

Controls were patients with underlying risk conditions hospitalized for ≥ 24 h for reasons other than pneumonia during the same study period, same age and same healthcare setting. Controls were selected from the same source population as cases to ensure comparability.

Individuals from long-term care facilities and those not providing consent were excluded from the study.

Data collection

Data were collected through a structured questionnaire, with the following variables recorded for both cases and controls: demographic characteristics (age, sex, educational level, occupation, household size), lifestyle factors (smoking status, alcohol consumption), healthcare use in the previous three months (primary care, hospital, specialist), vaccination history (PPSV23; PCV13; PCV20, influenza, COVID-19), and pre-existing medical conditions (cardiovascular and respiratory diseases, neurological and cognitive disorders, chronic kidney and liver diseases, diabetes mellitus, connective tissue diseases, immunodeficiencies, cancer, organ and bone marrow transplantations, immunosuppressive medication).

Patients were classified as immunocompetent or immunocompromised based on clinical history. Immunocompetent status was defined as presence of comorbidities or risk factors not considered to cause significant immunosuppression. These included cardiovascular diseases, respiratory diseases, neurological or neuromuscular disorders, kidney disease, liver disease, diabetes mellitus, inflammatory disease, peptic ulcer and other risk conditions such as risky alcohol consumption, Down syndrome, cochlear implant or cerebrospinal fluid fistula and haematological non-malignant disorders (such as haemophilia or chronic anaemia).

Immunocompromised status was defined as the presence of at least one of the following conditions: solid or hematologic neoplasia, solid organ or bone marrow transplantation, congenital or acquired immunodeficiency, asplenia, chronic kidney failure requiring dialysis or receipt of immunosuppressive treatment.

Comorbidities were assessed using the Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI), which assigns weighted scores to a predefined set of comorbidities conditions [15]. The resulting CCI score was categorized into three groups: 0 (no comorbidities of the predefined set), 1 (low comorbidity), and 2 (high comorbidity), and each participant was assigned to one of these categories.

For cases, additional data collected were symptoms onset, hospital admission, and microbiological test results. Since some authors indicate that pneumococcal vaccination could reduce the incidence and severity of SARS-CoV-2 pneumonia, interfering with our vaccine effectiveness (VE) estimation, a sensitivity analysis was performed excluding CAP cases with confirmed SARS-CoV-2 infection [16].

Vaccination status was defined as receipt of the vaccine ≥ 14 days before symptoms onset for cases and as hospital admission for controls. Pneumococcal vaccination status was categorized into four groups: PPSV23-only, defined as receipt of a single PPSV23 dose in the previous five years; PCV-only, defined as receipt of a single PCV13 or PCV20 dose; sequential vaccination defined as PCV13 or PCV20 followed by PPSV23 at an interval of 12 months (minimum 8 weeks), or PPSV23 followed by a conjugate vaccine at an interval of at least 12 months; and unvaccinated, defined as individuals with no pneumococcal vaccination record or who had received PPSV23 more than five years previously.

Sample size

For sample size calculation, a pneumococcal vaccination coverage of 0.20 in controls was assumed [17], with an odds ratio (OR) of 0.50, a two-sided alpha error of 0.05, and a statistical power of 0.80. Because we planned to analyse immunocompromised and immunocompetent individuals separately, the minimum required sample was 520 cases and 520 controls.

Statistical analysis

A bivariate comparison between cases and controls for demographic and epidemiological variables and pre-existing medical conditions was made using Chi square test. A two-tailed distribution was assumed for all p values.

A univariate logistic regression model was used to estimate the crude OR of preventing CAP hospitalization. Adjusted OR (aOR) values were estimated using a multivariate logistic regression model. Age, CCI and recruitment season were forced into the model as potential confounders due to their clinical relevance. Additional variables (educational level, household size, occupation, primary care doctor visits, healthcare use in the previous three months, tobacco and alcohol intake, and influenza and COVID-19 vaccination) were selected using a backward variable selection, for a cut-off point of p < 0.2.

In strata with fewer than 10 vaccinated cases or controls, we used Firth’s penalized logistic regression to assess small sample bias [18]. Age, CCI and recruitment season were retained in all penalized models, while the remaining covariates were selected using backward elimination.

Vaccine effectiveness was calculated using the formula: VE=(1-OR) x 100.

The analyses were performed using SPSS v.30 and R v. 4.5.1.

Results

A total of 805 cases and 806 controls were included in the study after excluding four cases and one control due to an incorrect sequential schedule for their comorbidity profile: four had received PCV13 + PPSV23 with < 8 weeks between doses, and one had received PPSV23 + PCV13.

Mean ages for cases and controls were 52.64 years (SD 10.29), and 52.79 years (SD 10.20), respectively.

A total of 131 cases (16.3%) and 101 controls (12.5%) had previously received the PPV23, and only 74 cases (9.2%) and 49 controls (6.1%) had been vaccinated within the previous five years. Regarding conjugate vaccines, 113 cases (14.0%) and 116 controls (14.4%) had received PCV13, and only one case and two controls had received PCV20.

Discussion

The results of this study of adults aged 18–64 years with underlying risk conditions reveal that none of the analysed pneumococcal vaccination schedules, namely, PPSV23-only, PCV-only (PCV13 or PCV20), or sequential, were associated with statistically significant vaccination effectiveness.

On exploring our results in depth, we observed a similar situation in the subgroup analyses. There was no evidence of positive VE for any vaccination type for the immunocompetent participants, and for the immunocompromised individuals, the PPSV23-only vaccination was associated with significant negative VE; this could reflect confounding bias due to indication, as those at highest risk are also the most likely to be vaccinated. This finding is consistent with immunological evidence from a recent systematic review showing suboptimal responses to both polysaccharide and conjugate vaccines in immunosuppressed populations [19].

The hospital-based design of the study may introduce some mechanisms that influence exposure distribution and, in addition, most of the subgroup analyses were based on small numbers, which may result in sparse-data instability and wide confidence intervals despite the use of penalized regression methods. These factors should be taken into account when interpreting the magnitude and direction of the VE estimates.

Most of the existing evidence on pneumococcal vaccine effectiveness has been derived from studies conducted in adults aged ≥ 65 years and results present certain controversy. A well-known example is the Dutch CAPITA trial [20], a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial involving 84,496 adults aged ≥ 65 years, which demonstrated PCV13 efficacy against vaccine-type pneumococcal pneumonia and invasive pneumococcal disease but not against CAP from any cause. Observational studies have also provided evidence of PCV13 effectiveness in preventing vaccine-type CAP in older adults [13, 21–24]. In the USA, a large multicentre study using a test-negative design found that PCV13 reduced hospitalization risk for vaccine-type CAP in individuals aged ≥ 65 years [21]. Overall, most of ≥ 65 years studies suggest that VE in this population is mainly driven by protection against vaccine-type disease rather than against pneumonia of any aetiology, which range from − 143% to 60% among older adults [25]. Our study evaluated VE against all-cause CAP hospitalizations. Because overall CAP includes cases attributable to non-vaccine serotypes or non-pneumococcal pathogens, VE estimates are expected to be lower and should be interpreted with caution. Consistent with our findings, a population-based cohort study in adults > 50 years conducted in Catalonia reported that PCV13 or PPSV23 vaccination was no associated with reduced risk of hospitalizations for all-cause pneumonia [26].

Evidence for adults aged < 65 years with risk factors remains scarce. In our study, results interpretation was limited by the low vaccination coverage and because PPSV23 predominate over the conjugate and sequential schedules, which are more immunogenic and effective in older and immunocompromised populations [13, 27].

A number of studies have evaluated VE in adults < 65 years. For a UK-based nested case-control study, Nakafero et al. [28] reported 24% (95%CI: 7 to 38) VE for PPSV23 in adults < 65 years with immune-mediated inflammatory diseases, suggesting a moderate benefit for immunosuppressed populations. Conversely, Serra-Prat et al. found no evidence of effectiveness for PPSV23 in preventing CAP in a Spanish cohort, mean age 48 years, with risk factors [23]. These results emphasize the limited evidence available for younger adults with underlying conditions.

Because our findings suggest that vaccination alone may be insufficient for preventing CAP and pneumococcal pneumonia in highly immunosuppressed individuals, additional strategies should be considered. These may include the use of higher-valency conjugate vaccines (PCV15 and PCV20), sequential schedules, and optimized timing and dosing. Despite reduced effectiveness in this setting, maintaining high coverage remains important, as even modest effects could benefit high-risk populations [29].

An economic modelling analys conducted in high-risk adults aged 19–64 years have suggested that expanded pneumococcal conjugate vaccination strategies may reduce hospitalizations related to pneumococcal disease and pneumonia. Nevertheless, this estimate is largely based on modelled assumptions regarding vaccine effectiveness rather than empirical clinical data, underscoring the limited availability of real-world evidence in younger adult populations [30]. It is important to highlight that our study period included the COVID-19 pandemic, which introduced certain epidemiological and behavioural changes. Non-pharmaceutical interventions (masking, social distancing, and hand hygiene, among others) significantly reduced respiratory infections [31, 32], and this fact has likely impacted not only on CAP incidence but also VE. Concerning these temporal variations, we carried out adjustment by season recruitment, but residual confounding cannot completely be excluded. Such contextual factors may have contributed to VE variability.

In our study, 66.0% of cases and 65.5% of controls had received COVID-19 vaccine. When we conducted a sensitivity analysis excluding CAP cases with SARS-CoV-2 infection, point estimates of VE remained similar with confidence intervals widened (reflecting the reduced sample size and statistical power), suggesting that the main results were not influenced by concurrent SARS-CoV-2 infection.

Since pneumococcal and SARS-CoV-2 infections affect similar risk groups, emerging evidence on factors associated with COVID-19 vaccination provides important context for our findings. COVID-19 vaccination has demonstrated indirect benefits in reducing hospitalizations for CAP, including for secondary bacterial infections [33, 34]. Data from the UK Biobank cohort showed that COVID-19 vaccination was associated with a markedly lower risk of hospitalization for non–COVID-19 pneumonia (hazard ratio 0.108, 95%CI: 0.080–0.145) [35].

Pneumococcal vaccination has been proposed as a complementary strategy for reducing COVID-19 complications, given the overlap in risk factors and the potential for bacterial co-infection [16, 36, 37]. Although their results remain inconclusive and require further confirmation, some observational studies have reported lower hospitalization and mortality rates in COVID-19 patients previously vaccinated against pneumococcus [38–40]. Our findings contrast with the finding of a Californian longitudinal cohort study of older adults that prior PCV13 vaccination is associated with a reduced risk of SARS-CoV-2 infection and of severe COVID-19 outcomes [41]. The discrepancy may be due to differences in study design, population characteristics, and the potential influence of bacterial colonization and microbial interactions in the respiratory tract (not addressed in our study).

A major strength of our study is the implementation of a case-control design in a hospital setting, which could help to assess VE in high-risk population. Potential confounding was addressed through multivariable adjustment for relevant factors, including age, CCI, healthcare use, and influenza and COVID-19 vaccination in the VE analysis, using penalized logistic regression. Nevertheless, residual confounding and unmeasured variables may persist and could have contributed to the VE estimates observed. Furthermore, vaccination status was thoroughly assessed, and patients with incorrect sequential schedules were excluded, thereby reducing potential misclassification.

However, several limitations are noted. The first is that the subgroup analyses were based on relatively small sample sizes, resulting in wide confidence intervals and limited statistical power. This situation could have contributed to the absence of a statistically significant VE estimate for our population of young adults. Future studies with a larger sample size are needed to clarify this relationship and provide stronger pneumococcal VE evidence for this population.

It is also possible that residual confounding and indication bias influenced the results, especially in the immunocompromised adults, who are often prioritized for vaccination but still remain at a higher inherent risk of severe outcomes. Moreover, the COVID-19 pandemic likely altered healthcare use patterns and reduced the incidence of CAP, which may have impacted both the likelihood of vaccination and the identification of cases.

Another factor to consider is that the use of hospitalized controls could be influencing the magnitude of estimates. This may be because people hospitalized are more likely to have a fragile baseline health status and more utilization of healthcare facilities. This could lead to potentially bias VE estimates with negative values.

Since there is great variability in the high-risk population, pneumococcal vaccination strategies should be individualized. As a general principle, higher valency conjugate vaccines should be used for this population and, when broader serotype coverage is necessary, sequential regimens should be implemented according to local guidance.

In our study, the fact that pneumococcal vaccination coverage was suboptimal suggests that pneumococcal vaccination programmes should take into account local epidemiology and healthcare access (via a reminder system or catch-up visits) to ensure adequate protection.

Conclusions

We observed no significant protection of pneumococcal vaccination against hospitalization for CAP in adults aged < 65 years with underlying risk factors. In immunocompromised individuals, the PPSV23-only vaccination strategy was negatively associated with CAP risk. Our results highlight the need to improve VE by prioritizing conjugate vaccine-based strategies and optimizing vaccination schedules tailored to specific risk groups.
