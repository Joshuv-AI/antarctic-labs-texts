Genomic characterization of multidrug-resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae clinical isolates from India
Authors: Deepali Desai, Tina Sharma, Nageswari Gandham, Priyanka Khopkar-Kale, Neeraj Bharti, Sunitha Manjari Kasibhatla, Uddhavesh Sonavane, Ruma Banerjee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Klebsiella pneumoniae is an emerging global threat driven by rising antimicrobial resistance and the spread of hypervirulent lineages. To investigate its evolving genomic landscape in India, we characterized two clinical K. pneumoniae isolates, NG_299 and NG_300, obtained from a tertiary care hospital in Pune and analyzed them in the context of Indian and global isolate collections. Comprehensive phenotypic and genomic analyses were performed using antimicrobial susceptibility testing, Illumina NovaSeq whole-genome sequencing and PCR-based confirmation of resistance and virulence markers. Both isolates exhibited multidrug resistance, remaining susceptible to only a limited subset of tested antibiotics. NG_299 (ST231) was susceptible to amikacin, colistin, and trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole, whereas NG_300 (ST20) was found to be susceptible only to colistin and trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole. Genomic profiling revealed thirty-two resistance determinants in NG_299 and fifty-two in NG_300, both of which produce extended-spectrum β-lactamases. Carbapenem resistance was linked to metallo-β-lactamase activity and the presence of AmpC was confirmed by antimicrobial susceptibility testing and PCR in NG_300. Pan-genome resistome analysis of global isolates identified conserved core genes (CRP, PhoP, rpoB) and a sparse occurrence of AMR genes (NDM, CTX-M, KPC, OXA, mcr) associated with horizontal gene transfer. Notably, NDM and CTX-M were present in both study isolates, with OXA variants detected in NG_299. Distinct missense mutations within shared resistance genes highlighted independent evolutionary trajectories. Both isolates carried virulence factors associated with adhesion, biofilm formation, iron acquisition, and secretion systems, including siderophores. Plasmid analysis identified IncF replicons in both isolates and blaNDM-5 on an IncFII plasmid in NG_299. These findings document the circulation of multidrug-resistant K. pneumoniae in Pune and underscore the urgent need for strengthened genomic surveillance.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-54711-w.

Introduction

Klebsiella pneumoniae (K. pneumoniae), a Gram-negative opportunistic pathogen, poses a growing public health threat due to its prevalence, increasing antibiotic resistance, and propensity to cause hospital-acquired infections, particularly in newborns, elderly, and immunocompromised individuals. Widespread use of antibiotics has contributed to the emergence of multidrug-resistant (MDR) K. pneumoniae, complicating clinical management and limiting therapeutic options1,2. Antibiotic resistance in this pathogen arises through multiple mechanisms, including plasmid acquisition, biofilm formation, cross-resistance, and resistance-associated point mutations. Virulence factors, such as capsule polysaccharides (CPSs), lipopolysaccharides (LPSs), adhesins, iron-acquisition systems, and secretion systems, further enable K. pneumoniae to evade host immune defenses. Owing to its combined virulence, antimicrobial resistance, and immune evasion capabilities, the World Health Organization (WHO) has classified K. pneumoniae, a member of the ESKAPE group, as a priority endemic pathogen for vaccine development and novel antibiotic research3–5.

In India, K. pneumoniae remains one of the most frequently isolated Gram-negative pathogens in tertiary care hospitals, with carbapenem resistance rates exceeding 50% according to the Indian Council of Medical Research (ICMR) Antimicrobial Resistance Surveillance Network (2023)6. The increasing prevalence of carbapenem-resistant K. pneumoniae (CRKP) is largely driven by the dissemination of blaNDM and blaOXA−48-like carbapenemase genes, often accompanied by a notable rise in amoxicillin resistance6–9. Molecular characterization using multiplex PCR and multilocus sequence typing (MLST) has revealed ST231 as the predominant lineage in India, followed by ST147 and ST1410. Among resistance genes, blaampH is most frequently detected, along with blaCTX−M−15 and blaTEM−1, while ST231 genomes commonly harbor Col440I and ColKP3 plasmids replicon10. While conventional phenotypic and genotypic methods have provided valuable insights, whole-genome sequencing (WGS) has emerged as a powerful tool for predicting drug resistance, tracing lineages, and delineating outbreaks of K. pneumoniae in the recent times11,12. The escalating global incidence of K. pneumoniae infections, alongside the rise of MDR and hypervirulent strains, underscores the urgent need to explore its genomic landscape for vaccine development and targeted antimicrobial interventions12.

Herein, we present a comprehensive genomic analysis of two clinical MDR K. pneumoniae isolates from a tertiary care center in Pune, India. The exploratory study aimed to provide insights into key antibiotic resistance determinants, phylogenetics, virulence and pathogenicity. The analysis correlates the importance of molecular factors with the severe clinical presentation and worst outcomes in patients. These findings represent our first steps towards resistance surveillance and emphasize the importance of genomic surveillance in combating antimicrobial resistance.

Results

Antibiotic susceptibility profile of NG_299 and NG_300 clinical isolates

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing revealed that both isolates were multidrug-resistant, exhibiting resistance to β-lactams (Ampicillin, Amoxicillin/Clavulanic acid, Piperacillin/Tazobactam, multiple cephalosporins, and carbapenems), indicating the likely presence of ESBL, AmpC, and Metallo-β-lactamases (MBL) (Table 1).

Genome assembly and plasmid identification

NG_299 consisted of 228 contigs with a total length of 5,555,314 bp, while NG_300 consisted of 140 contigs totaling 5,552,144 bp. The GC content of both isolates were 57.09% (Supplementary Table 1). A total of 34 contigs from NG_299 and 47 contigs from NG_300 mapped to the reference genome with contigs < = 500 bp excluded from analysis. The unmapped contigs were subsequently subjected to plasmid identification (Supplementary Fig. 1a and 1b).

In isolate NG_299, one contig (Node_37), measuring 7978 bp, exhibited high sequence similarity to the Klebsiella pneumoniae subsp. pneumoniae strain WRC14_AI2040MC plasmid pAI2040M_P3e (RefSeqId: NZ_CP079189.1; 7819 bp), with 98% query coverage, 89% subject coverage and 100% sequence identity (Supplementary Fig. 2). Additionally, a replication initiation protein corresponding to the Col440I_1 plasmid replicon within Node_37 (Fig. 1) was also identified.

Functional annotation

Multi-locus sequence typing revealed NG_299 belongs to sequence type 231(ST231), while NG_300 belongs to sequence type 20 (ST20). In NG_299 (ST231), the K locus was identified as KL51, indicating a capsule type of K51, associated with the wzi104 gene. The O locus was determined to be O1/O2v2, predicting an O-antigen type of O1ab. In NG_300 (ST20), the K locus was identified as KL24, corresponding to capsule type K24, linked to the wzi24 gene. Its O locus was similar to NG_299.

The number of coding genes in NG_299 were found to be 5677, of which 5582 were protein-coding and 95 were RNA coding genes (Supplementary Table 3). The predicted RNA coding genes consist of 91 transfer RNAs (tRNAs) and four rRNAs (two 5 S rRNA, one LSU rRNA and one SSU rRNA). In NG_300 the number of coding genes were predicted to be 5628, of which 5546 were protein-coding and 82 were RNA coding (Supplementary Table 4). The predicted RNA coding genes consist of 78 transfer RNAs (tRNAs) and four rRNAs (two 5 S rRNA, one LSU rRNA and one SSU rRNA) (Supplementary Table 1).

Virulence factors

Seven distinct functional groups of virulence factors were identified in the two clinical isolates. Both NG_299 and NG_300 carried genes associated with biofilm formation (mrkABCDFHIJ) and adherence (fimABCDEFGHIK) (Supplementary Table 7). Genes encoding secretion systems (tssABCDFGHIJKLM) were present in both isolates; however, tli1, a Type VI secretion system (T6SS) immunity protein, was found exclusively in NG_300. The T6SS effector gene tle1 was absent in both isolates.

A higher number of genes associated with the iron-uptake system (entABCDEF, fepABCDG, fes, iroE, ybtSXQPAUTE, irp1, irp2, and fyuA) were detected in both isolates. Additionally, a small subset of genes linked to efflux pumps (acrAB) and regulatory functions (rcsAB), associated with capsule lipopolysaccharide (LPS) formation were also identified in both clinical isolates. Genes related to siderophores (e.g. aerobactin, salmochelin, etc.) were found to be present in both clinical isolates (Supplementary Table 7). Conversely, the Aerobactin virulence factor responsible for enhanced siderophore production under iron-limiting conditions could not be detected by PCR (Table 1).

Resistome analysis

Comprehensive analysis revealed a spectrum of antibiotic resistance genes specific to each isolate. A total of 43 resistance genes were identified in the resistome profile of NG_299 (Supplementary Table 8), of which thirteen were found to be unique as compared to NG_300 (Supplementary Table 9). Resistance genes include AAC (6’)-Ib, aadA2, arr-2, catA1, dfrA12, ErmB, fosA5, mphA, oqxA, OXA-232, rmtF, SHV-106, sul1, bla-SHV-28, fosA and kpn. Characteristically, kpn gene that was negative in PCR (Table 1) was identified in the WGS analysis. Presence of rmtF gene, which is typically associated with aminoglycoside resistance, did not correlate with the phenotypic susceptibility tests (Supplementary Table 10). Additional resistance determinants include OmpK35-30% and OmpK36TD for carbapenems, GyrA-83I and ParC-80I for Fluoroquinolones. MgrB (responsible for colistin resistance) is detected at only 51% of its expected length; this truncated form suggests functional inactivation (Supplementary Table 10).

In NG_300, a total of 57 resistance genes were identified. which include nine distinct genes which are missing in NG_299, namely SHV-187, FosA6, BRP(MBL), NDM-1, QnrS1, AAC (6’)-Ib10, APH(6)-Id, APH(3’’)-Ib, and APH(3’)-Via. Twenty genes were present across both NG_299 and NG_300 isolates which prominently include “Resistance-Nodulation-Cell Division (RND) antibiotic efflux pump” family, along with the “Major Facilitator Superfamily (MFS)”, “Small Multidrug Resistance (SMR)”, and the “ATP-binding cassette (ABC)” efflux pumps (Supplementary Tables 8–9). PCR results for drug-resistance genes CTX-M-15 and NDM were consistent with the resistome analysis conducted (Supplementary Fig. 5) (Table 1). Aminoglycosides resistant genes in NG_300 include TEM-1, aadA (homolog), aac(6’)-Ib’, aph(3’)-VI, strA and strB (Supplementary Table 11). Additionally, 38 unique genes were detected exclusively through reference mapping (Supplementary Table 11).

Detection of blaSHV-106 in NG_299 and blaCTX-M-15 in NG_300, both associated with ampicillin resistance and consistent with the observed resistance to ceftriaxone and cefepime. In addition, rmtF and OqxB were identified in NG_299, correlating with aminoglycoside and quinolone resistance, respectively. In isolate NG_300, the presence of blaCTX-M-15, blaNDM-1, aph(3’)-VI, and qnrS1 indicated broad-spectrum resistance to β-lactams, aminoglycosides, and fluoroquinolones.

Few resistance-associated genes, in both isolates (Supplementary Tables 10–11), such as fosA6 (phosphonic acid), CRP (penicillin, fluoroquinolone, and macrolide resistance), baeR (aminoglycosides and aminocoumarins), msbA (nitroimidazoles), kpnG, H-NS, OmpK37, kpnE/F/H, marA (multi-drug resistance), emrR (fluoroquinolones), and adeF (tetracyclines and fluoroquinolones), did not match with the current susceptibility profiling.

Conversely, DHA gene, an AmpC β-lactamase, was not detected through in silico methods, despite its presence being confirmed by PCR in both isolates, whereas KpnG, kpnE/F/H, were not detected in PCR but were picked up in the in silico methods (Supplementary Table 12).

Pan-resistome analysis of Indian and global clinical isolates

MLST assigned sequence type information to 81 of 91 Indian (Supplementary Table 13) and 1926 of 1985 Klebsiella pneumonia global clinical isolates (Supplementary Table 13). ST14464 was the most prevalent with broad geographic distribution across China (179 isolates), USA (32 isolates), Taiwan (57 isolates) and Brazil (16) indicating a widespread dissemination of a potential international clone (Supplementary Table 14). ST11 represented the second most dominant lineage showing strong enrichment in China (176 isolates). In contrast ST231 was predominantly observed in India (27 isolates) and with limited representation in China (10 isolates) suggesting its regional significance in shaping the antimicrobial resistance landscape. Additionally, ST20 was detected at low frequency across multiple regions, including China (n = 5), the USA (n = 1), India (n = 1), and Japan (n = 1) (Supplementary Table 14).

Pan-resistome analysis identified 75 AMR genes spanning 25 antibiotic classes, including aminoglycosides, carbapenems, cephalosporins, fluoroquinolones, representing major resistance mechanisms such as β-lactamases (e.g., NDM, OXA, SHV, TEM variants), efflux pumps (e.g., oqxA, oqxB), porin mutations (OmpK35, OmpK36), and plasmid-mediated resistance genes (e.g., MCR-1, Qnr variants) (Supplementary Table 15; Fig. 5).

The heatmap based gene presence and absence profiling (Fig. 5) revealed small core resistome widely conserved across isolates, alongside a large accessory component with sporadic distribution. Core genes included the global regulator CRP, present across all Indian isolates, and PhoP, associated with colistin resistance, which was detected in NG_299 and NG_300 and absent in only two other Indian isolates (Supplementary Fig. 9). This suggests a regulatory role of PhoP that may not find support in phenotypic susceptibility to colistin based on AST13. In contrast, a large proportion of AMR genes exhibited sporadic distribution, reflecting a substantial accessory resistome component (Supplementary Table 15; Supplementary Fig. 9). NG_300 harbors critical resistance determinants such as blaNDM-1, extended β-lactamase variants and fluoroquinolone resistance genes (e.g., Qnr family), indicating acquisition of accessory elements (Fig. 5). likely mediated by horizontal gene transfer14.

In contrast, NG_299 lacks several of these high-risk resistance determinants and instead carries a distinct set of low-prevalence determinants (e.g., sul1, catI, SHV-1, OXA-232, oqxA, aadA2, rmtF, AAC(6')-Ib, arr-2, mphA, Mrx, and dfrA12) many of which are absent in a substantial proportion of Indian clinical isolates (Supplementary Fig. 10). Comparative resistome analysis of Indian isolates further revealed several well-characterized AMR genes including sulfonamide resistance determinants (sul2, folP variants), phenicol resistance genes (floR, cmlA5, catB3), β-lactamases (KPC-1, SHV variants, NDM-5, NDM-7, CMY, DHA-1, VEB-8), plasmid-mediated colistin resistance (mcr-1), quinolone resistance genes (qnrB variants, AAC(6’)-Ib-cr), aminoglycoside-modifying enzymes (armA, APH(3’)-Ia, AAC variants), macrolide resistance genes (mphD, msrE), rifampicin resistance (arr-3), fosfomycin resistance (FosA6), and trimethoprim resistance (dfrA14) which were notably absent in both NG_299 and NG_300. However, both isolates shared a conserved set of resistance-associated genes and mutations including regulatory and porin-associated determinants (acrR and ramR mutants, OmpK35, global regulator CRP, and PhoP mutations linked to colistin resistance) along with β-lactam resistance genes (TEM-1 and CTX-M-15), and rifampicin resistance-associated rpoB mutations. Overall, NG_299 and NG_300 exhibit distinct resistance profiles even within their respective ST lineage (Supplementary Fig. 10).

Comparative AMR landscape of global isolates

Global AMR profiling of Klebsiella pneumoniae across 1,985 isolates demonstrated a highly heterogeneous distribution of resistant determinants (Supplementary Table 16). A small subset of genes were widely conserved, constituting the core resistome, including CRP (absent in only 6 isolates), rpoB mutations conferring rifampicin resistance (absent in only 20 isolates), and PhoP-linked determinants implicated in colistin resistance (absent in only 23 isolates).

In contrast, a large proportion of AMR genes were sparsely distributed (being absent in ≥ 1800 isolates), thereby representing a substantial accessory resistome (Supplementary Table 16). This included clinically significant resistance genes such as NDM, CTX-M, KPC, OXA, and mcr variants, which were confined to a subset of isolates, consistent with lineage-specific distribution and horizontal gene transfer (Supplementary Table 16). Notably, NDM and CTX-M were detected in both study isolates while OXA variants were identified specifically in NG_299.

Discussion

Our study revealed critical genomic and phenotypic features underlying resistance to commonly used antibiotics, as well as key virulence factors in two K. pneumoniae isolates obtained from hospitalized patients in Pune, India and their placement within a broader global context.

Sequence typing and antimicrobial resistance are known to be tightly coupled in K. pneumoniae15. In this study, the isolates were identified as sequence types ST231 and ST20, both associated with carbapenem resistance, underscoring their clinical importance in hospital settings16. Phylogenetic analyses with other MDR strains from ATCC and other global isolates confirmed clustering consistent with sequence type classifications. Analysis of K. pneumoniae global isolates demonstrated that a limited number of high-frequency sequence types, notably ST14464 and ST11 are predominant reflecting widespread international dissemination, while ST231 shows regional enrichment in India, indicating its contribution to the local AMR landscape. Such patterns are consistent with the global expansion of high-risk clones and their role in driving antimicrobial resistance burdens across regions15,17.

Multiple tools and databases utilized for resistome analysis, attribute that no single approach can fully capture all resistance determinants18. This approach improved detection accuracy across acquired genes, chromosomal mutations, and efflux mechanisms while minimizing the effects of inconsistent gene nomenclature and database redundancy. The combined analyses coupled with basic and advanced molecular techniques provided a robust and reliable resistome profile for both isolates highlighting unique determinants.

The capsular (K) and lipopolysaccharide (O) antigens play central roles in virulence, immune evasion, and strain classification19. The ST231 isolate (NG_299) carried the KL51 locus, a frequently detected capsular type in Indian clinical isolates20, while the ST20 isolate (NG_300) harbored KL24, which has been reported rarely among Indian strains20.

ST231 has emerged as a major carbapenem-resistant K. pneumoniae lineage in India and also globally21. Initially reported in Delhi in 2013, ST231 isolates typically carry blaOXA-232, a variant of the blaOXA-48-like carbapenemase22. Over 100 Indian isolates belonging to this sequence type have been reported as MDR, resistant to carbapenems, extended-spectrum cephalosporins, and broad-spectrum aminoglycosides20.

Consistent with this, NG_299 carried resistance genes characteristic of ST231, including OmpK37, CTX-M-14, and SHV-11. Additional determinants such as rmtF (aminoglycoside resistance), catA1 (chloramphenicol), sul1 (sulfonamide), and dfrA12 (trimethoprim) were also present. Point mutations gyrA_83I and parC_80I, commonly associated with fluoroquinolone resistance, were detected20.

Phenotypically, NG_299 displayed multidrug resistance to β-lactams (including carbapenems), aminoglycosides (gentamicin), and quinolones, while remaining susceptible to colistin, likely due to a truncated mgrB gene. Genomic evidence also suggested potential resistance to sulfonamides and trimethoprim not observed phenotypically. Despite the presence of rmtF, phenotypic susceptibility to amikacin was noted, indicating possible modulation by mobile genetic elements23.

Outbreaks of K. pneumoniae ST20 have been documented globally but are rare in India24–26. ST20 strains typically exhibit carbapenem resistance through blaNDM-1, a gene associated with high fatality rates and poor treatment outcomes16,27.

The Pune isolate NG_300 carried blaNDM-1 and several additional resistance genes, including aac(6’)-Ib’, aadA (homolog), aph(3’)-VI, strA, and strB (aminoglycosides), as well as qnrS1 (fluoroquinolones).

Both the Pune isolates tested positive for ESBL production, reflecting broad β-lactam resistance. Genotypically, blaSHV-106 and blaCTX-M-15 were identified in both isolates, correlating with resistance to ampicillin, ceftriaxone, and cefepime. Fluoroquinolone resistance was associated with OqxB (NG_299) and qnrS1 (NG_300). However, some resistance determinants did not align with phenotypic profiles, highlighting the need for further functional validation.

The two study isolates possessed genes involved in biofilm formation and fimbrial adhesion (fim and mrk operons), facilitating surface attachment and persistence28.Type VI secretion system (T6SS) genes were detected in both isolates, potentially contributing to antibiotic resistance and biofilm formation29. Notably, the immunity genes tli1 and tle1 were unique to NG_300, suggesting defence mechanisms against T6SS-mediated attacks30.

Efflux pump genes (acrAB) and capsule-regulating genes (rcsAB) were present in both isolates, promoting immune evasion and virulence31,32. Genes related to LPS biosynthesis were also identified, which are linked to increased disease severity33.

Siderophore-related genes for enterobactin, yersiniabactin, and salmochelin were detected in both isolates, enhancing iron acquisition, inflammation, and bacterial dissemination19,32. The coexistence of virulence and resistance genes raises concern over the emergence of MDR K. pneumoniae, a growing public health threat34.

NG_299 contained a complete plasmid (similar to GenBank ID: NZ_CP079189.1) and six incomplete plasmids matching known replicons. The replicons Col440I_1 and ColKP3_1, typically associated with ST231, were detected35. blaNDM-5 was located on an IncFII-type plasmid, consistent with previous Indian reports36. In NG_300, plasmid replicons IncFII_1_pKP91 and IncFIB(K)_1_Kpn3, often linked to carbapenem resistance were detected37, though carbapenemase genes were not observed, suggesting plasmid plasticity38.

Both isolates exhibited unique mutations in key resistance-associated genes, indicating strain-specific adaptations. Mutations in gyrA, parC, and rpoB corroborate mechanisms underlying fluoroquinolone and rifamycin resistance. The fosA (c.271G > A; p.Val91Ile) mutation in NG_300 was linked to quinolone resistance39. Additional missense mutations in KPHS_13940, KPHS_36860, and KPHS_43580 suggest evolutionary divergence and conservation of resistance-associated loci (Supplementary Tables 17–18).

An association between point mutations and sequence types was observed. Changes in ompK genes, whether through mutations or deletions, combined with the presence of carbapenem resistance genes, play a crucial role in shaping the resistance profile of K. pneumoniae40,41. This emphasizes the necessity for comprehensive genomic analyses that account for both resistance genes and other contributing factors, including resistance-associated point mutations. In our work we have summarized the observed resistance genes, mechanisms and contributing factors for K pneumoniae (Supplementary Fig. 6).

Comparative genomic analysis of Klebsiella pneumoniae isolates across the globe revealed clear sequence type–specific mutation patterns, particularly within the antimicrobial resistance genes, namely, ompK and ompK36 porin genes. The majority of SNPs observed in isolate NG_299 were shared with ST231 genomes, while those in NG_399 were predominantly associated with ST20, indicating distinct evolutionary lineages within the species42,41,. In the ompK36 (KPHS_37010) gene, nonsynonymous mutations, including p.Asp344Glu, p.Ile315Met, p.Asn221His, and p.Thr192Ser were restricted to ST231 isolates, whereas synonymous variants were characteristic of ST20. Given the established role of OmpK36 in modulating β-lactam influx, these lineage-specific amino acid changes may influence antibiotic susceptibility profiles, potentially contributing to the enhanced carbapenem resistance frequently reported among ST231 strains. The absence of non-synonymous mutations in ST20 isolates suggests a more conserved porin structure. Collectively, these findings highlight the divergence of porin gene evolution across K. pneumoniae lineages and underscore the contribution of structural variation in OmpK to strain-specific antimicrobial resistance mechanisms43. Further structural modeling and phenotypic assays are warranted to elucidate the functional impact of these substitutions on porin-mediated antibiotic transport.

Pan-resistome analysis of the global and Indian Klebsiella pneumoniae isolates carried out in this study revealed a clear core-accessory architecture, with conserved determinants such as CRP, PhoP, and rpoB mutations forming the core resistome and likely representing essential regulatory and adaptive functions. In contrast, the accessory resistome comprised sporadically distributed genes, including clinically significant determinants such as NDM, CTX-M, KPC, OXA, and mcr variants, consistent with their dissemination via horizontal gene transfer and plasmid-mediated spread44,45. The presence of NDM and CTX-M in both study isolates, and OXA variants in NG_299, further underscores their clinical relevance and persistence in hospital-associated strains. Comparative analysis of NG_299 and NG_300 revealed marked variability even within their respective lineages, with NG_300 harboring high-risk determinants such as blaNDM-1, indicative of an expanded multidrug-resistant phenotype, whereas NG_299 exhibited a distinct but less prevalent accessory gene repertoire. Despite these differences, both isolates retained a conserved set of regulatory and β-lactam resistance determinants, suggesting a shared baseline resistance framework. This intra-lineage variability highlights the role of horizontal gene transfer and selective pressure in shaping resistome diversity1,46,.

These findings emphasize the dynamic and evolving nature of AMR in K. pneumoniae, characterized by the interplay between clonal expansion and acquisition of mobile genetic elements. Continuous genomic surveillance and integration of WGS-based approaches are therefore essential to track emerging high-risk clones and inform infection control and antimicrobial stewardship strategies47.

Conclusions

A comprehensive analysis of two multidrug-resistant, carbapenem-resistant K. pneumoniae (CRKP) isolates from Pune, India provided critical information into genomic diversity, resistance mechanisms, and virulence factors, emphasizing the importance of genomic surveillance stressing on how it could aid in monitoring the emergence and dissemination of key resistance-associated variants. To our knowledge this study reports, for the first time the circulation of MDR K. pneumoniae strains with KpnE, KpnF, KpnH, KpnG, OmpK37, and OXA-232 highlights the evolving resistome and virulome in the country.

The delineation of gene profiles offers valuable insights into the genetic variability underlying antibiotic resistance among K. pneumoniae strains, shedding light on strain-specific resistance determinants. These findings underscore the complexity of antimicrobial resistance evolution in K. pneumoniae and highlight the importance of isolate-specific genomic profiling to guide targeted therapeutic strategies. Further functional characterization of both shared and unique variants could elucidate their respective contributions to resistance phenotypes. Insights gained from such studies can improve our understanding of the molecular mechanisms that sustain their pathogenic success and can inform effective surveillance, containment, and therapeutic strategies. The combined phylogenetic and resistome analyses enabled comparison of the study isolates with globally circulating lineages and facilitated assessment of the distribution and diversity of antimicrobial resistance determinants.

Methods

Clinical isolates and phenotypic characterisation

The study research proposal was approved by the Research and Recognition Committee, Dr D Y Patil Vidyapeeth (Deemed to be University), Pimpri, Pune, India [Ref No: DPU/PhD/907(2)/2016; dated 30/11/2016] and the study was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee, Dr D Y Patil Vidyapeeth (Deemed to be University), Pimpri, Pune, India [Ref No: DYPV/EC/01/17; dated 22/01/2018].

Two clinical isolates of K. pneumoniae, NG_299 and NG_300 were obtained from a 75-year-old female with a breast abscess and a 64-year-old male with necrotising pancreatitis, respectively. The specimens were collected from pus and pleural effusion on 07-01-2020 and 28-11-2019 as a part of a doctoral study with informed consent obtained from all patients48. All participants provided a written informed consent prior to taking part in the study with the consent to publish the research findings. As per the approved research proposal Standard microbiological techniques were used for the isolation and identification of the isolates49. The study was conducted in accordance with approved guidelines, including the Declaration of Helsinki and no study specimens were collected from prisoners. All the patient data has been anonymized according to the HIPPA guidelines.

Antibiotic susceptibility testing was performed using Kirby Bauer’s disc diffusion technique on Mueller Hinton agar (MHA) and interpretations of susceptibility were done according to the Clinical Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) guidelines50. VITEK 2 was used for species confirmation of both isolates and for determining their minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC). Furthermore, Anti-Microbial Resistance (AMR) detection was carried out for six AMR genes viz. CTXM, TEM, SHV, NDM1, KPC, DHA by multiplex PCR51 and strains harbouring AMR genes were further screened for the presence of genes encoding for five virulence factors such as the capsular polysaccharide serotype (K2), mucoid phenotype regulator (rmpA), the siderophores: aerobactin (Aer) and enterobactin (entB), and FimH using PCR48.

DNA extraction and whole genome sequencing

Genomic DNA extraction was performed on pure cultures grown on MacConkey’s agar medium. The NovaSeq6000 Illumina platform facilitated paired-end sequencing with 100X coverage, generating a total of ~ 13 million high-quality reads each for NG_299 and NG_300 respectively with read length of 151 bps. The whole-genome sequences of both Klebsiella pneumoniae isolates have been deposited in GenBank. Isolate NG_299 is available under accession number CP121466 (BioProject: PRJNA866521; BioSample: SAMN30167957), and isolate NG_300 under accession number CP119527 (BioProject: PRJNA870688; BioSample: SAMN30382023).

Data preprocessing and genome assemblies

The quality of raw reads was checked using FastQC v0.11.9 (https://www.bioinformatics.babraham.ac.uk/projects/fastqc/), filtered using TrimGalore-0.6.7 (github.com/felixkrueger/trimgalore) and cutadapt-1.18 (cutadapt.readthedocs.io/en/v1.18/) with default parameters. The de novo genome assembly of NG_299 and NG_300 was performed using SPAdes 3.15.4 (https://github.com/ablab/spades).

Filtered reads were mapped to the multidrug resistant reference genome Klebsiella pneumoniae subsp. pneumoniae HS11286 (RefSeq ID: NC_016845.1) using BWA-MEM (v0.7.17) [https://github.com/lh3/bwa]. Post-mapping, reads were sorted, indexed and converted to BAM format using Samtools (v1.16.1) [https://github.com/samtools/samtools/releases/]. To enhance data integrity and minimize technical artifacts, duplicate reads were identified and marked using GATK MarkDuplicatesSpark (v4.3.0.0), followed by local realignment around indels with GATK IndelRealigner to correct misalignment errors [https://github.com/broadinstitute/gatk/releases].

Genome annotation

Rapid Annotation using Subsystem Technology Toolkit (RASTtk) pipeline https://rast.nmpdr.org/ was used to annotate the draft genomes. Multilocus Sequence Typing (MLST) was performed with pubMLST (https://pubmlst.org/). To investigate the prophage genes within these two clinical isolates, we utilized PHASTEST (https://phastest.ca/), that detects and annotates prophage sequences in bacterial genomes.

AMR genes were identified using ABRicate v.1.0.1 2 (CARD) [https://github.com/tseemann/abricate] and Kleborate v3 as available through Pathogenwatch (https://github.com/klebgenomics/Kleborate, https://pathogen.watch/ last accessed on October 15, 2025). Resistant determinants were identified based on reference genome annotation and corroborated to laboratory resistance patterns. ResFinder-2.5.1 and PointFinder-4.1.1 (https://github.com/genomicepidemiology/resfinder) were used for genotype-phenotype mapping. Identification of virulence factor genes was carried out by performing homology search against VFDB52 using BLAST (ncbi-blast-2.15.0+)53. K and O antigen types, were identified using the kleborate and kaptive implemented in the Pathogenwatch54.

The PCR results of the AMR genes and Virulence genes were compared with the genomic data for confirmation and gathering deeper molecular insights for understanding AMR.

Plasmid prediction

Mauve alignment tool (version 20150226) (https://mauve.software.informer.com/download/) was used for identification of unmapped reads against the reference genome. A comparative analysis was conducted on these unmapped reads against a comprehensive dataset of 14,751 complete plasmid sequences belonging to K. pneumoniae, which were retrieved from GenBank [June 24, 2025]. The alignments were visualized using BLAST Ring Image Generator (BRIG)55. Gap filling was performed using unmapped reads to improve completeness of the identified plasmids. DoriC 12.0 was used to identify the origin of replication56. Additionally, PlasmidFinder tool (https://cge.food.dtu.dk/services/PlasmidFinder/, accessed on 02 April 2024)57 and Kleborate [https://rc.pathogen.watch/en] were also utilized for plasmid identification.

SNP identification and phylogenetic analysis

To ensure robust variant calling, three tools viz., BCFtools (v1.18), GATK HaplotypeCaller (v4.3.0.0) and VarScan (v2.3.9) were used. BCFtools (v1.18) was further employed to extract consensus SNPs that were consistently identified across all three variant callers. Functional annotation of the identified variants was performed using SnpEff (v5.0e). To assess the correlation between identified point mutations and sequence types (STs), ten additional Klebsiella pneumoniae genomes were retrieved from Pathogenwatch, five ST231 isolates (SRA IDs: SRR26412234, ERR11752342, SRR23890336, SRR17709952, SRR22822900) and five ST20 isolates (SRR26412276, ERR12058189, SRR23237907, SRR22822874, SRR23332936). Sequencing reads were downloaded from the NCBI SRA, quality filtered, and assembled. The resulting genomes were analyzed for variant profiles and compared with clinical isolates, focusing on antimicrobial resistance (AMR) genes through reference-based mapping. PointFinder is a web-based tool that detects chromosomal point mutations associated with AMR genes from bacterial whole-genome sequencing (WGS) data using sequence-based search methods, and its results have been clinically validated58.

For phylogenetic analysis, a total of 82 K. pneumoniae isolates from the ATCC database were extracted and subjected to sequence typing (Supplementary Table_19). The reference genome was also included in the phylogeny. Core-genome single nucleotide polymorphisms (cgSNPs) were identified using Snippy v4.6.0 [https://mybiosoftware.com/snippy-find-snpsindels-bacterial-genome-ngs-reads.html]. The phylogenetic tree was constructed using IQ-TREE v1.6.12 [http://www.iqtree.org/release/v1.6.12] and visualized using iTOL (https://itol.embl.de/).

Comparative Pan-resistome analysis for Indian and global K. pneumoniae clinical isolates

A comparative pan-resistome analysis was performed on 91 Indian and 1,985 global Klebsiella pneumoniae clinical isolates retrieved from the BV-BRC database ((https://www.bv-brc.org/59), with genome quality control, annotation, sequence typing, and associated metadata summarized in the Supplementary Table 20. Pan-genome analysis, phylogenetic reconstruction, and AMR profiling for Indian isolates (including NG_299 and NG_300) were conducted using Bacterial Pan-genome analysis Pipeline (BPGA) (https://iicb.res.in/bpga/60,61, while global phylogenetic context was inferred using Parsnp62 due to computational constraints. Details of the methodology have been provided in the Supplementary Document.
