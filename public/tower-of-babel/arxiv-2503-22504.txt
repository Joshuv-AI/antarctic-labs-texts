Generalized parameter-space metrics for continuous gravitational-wave searches
P. B. Covas, R. Prix

Abstract
Many searches for continuous gravitational waves face significant computational challenges due to the need to explore large parameter spaces characterized by unknown parameters. Parameter-space metrics are used to predict the relative loss of signal power when the searched parameters differ from the true signal parameters. In this paper we present generalized parameter-space metrics for the ℱ \mathcal{F} -statistic (a detection statistic used in many searches) that improve upon previous idealized metrics by incorporating realistic effects such as data gaps and varying noise floors. We derive a new marginalized ℱ \mathcal{F} -statistic metric that is more accurate than the previous averaged ℱ \mathcal{F} -statistic metric, especially for short coherent segments. We also derive a more accurate semi-coherent metric that properly accounts for the signal-power variability over segments. We provide numerical tests illustrating that the new generalized metrics provide more accurate mismatch predictions than previous expressions. More accurate metrics can result in a reduced number of templates needed for a given search, a feature that could improve the sensitivity of future searches.

I
Introduction

Continuous gravitational waves (CWs) are long-lasting and almost monochromatic gravitational waves (yet to be detected) that can be emitted by different sources, such as asymmetric (around their rotation axis) rotating neutron stars
[
1
]
. Searches for CWs can have different targets, such as known pulsars or unknown neutron stars in our galaxy (commonly called all-sky searches).

In order to discern whether a dataset contains an astrophysical signal or just noise, a detection statistic that is compared to a threshold is commonly computed. One of these detection statistics is the
\mathcal{F}
-statistic
[
2
,
3
]
, which is obtained after analytical maximization (over the four amplitude parameters that describe a typical CW signal) of the log-likelihood ratio. This detection statistic has been used in many CW searches (see
[
4
]
for a recent review). Due to the prohibitive computational cost of some searches, the data can be divided in many shorter segments and a semi-coherent version of the
\mathcal{F}
-statistic might be used
[
5
]
.

The typical CW signal model includes parameters that for some searches are unknown, such as the rotational frequency or the sky position. These unknown parameters have to be explicitly explored with a template bank or with a stochastic sampling algorithm. The parameter-space metric is a second-order approximation that aims to predict the relative loss of signal power (also called mismatch) incurred when these searched parameters are not equal to the true parameters of a putative astrophysical signal
[
6
]
.

The full coherent parameter-space metric for the (multi-detector)
\mathcal{F}
-statistic was first derived in
[
6
]
.
The different metrics derived there are subject to some idealizations, such as
(i) the data is assumed to have no gaps, i.e., a duty cycle of 100
\%
;
(ii) the noise floor of each detector, quantified in terms of the amplitude spectral density (ASD), is assumed to be constant over time.
Furthermore, the semi-coherent metric (cf.
[
5
,
7
,
8
]
)
for the
\mathcal{F}
-statistic has so far been derived under the assumption of (iii) equal signal power over segments.
However, realistic datasets typically have an overall duty cycle less than 100
\%
(usually around
\sim 70\%
),
and varying duty cycles and noise floors over time, as shown in Fig.
1
for the O3 observing
run
[
9
,
10
]
of the Advanced LIGO gravitational-wave detectors located in Hanford (H1) and Livingston
(L1)
[
11
]
.

In this paper we attempt to study the behavior of the
\mathcal{F}
-statistic parameter-space metrics when these assumptions do not hold,
and derive generalized expressions and implementations that take these effects into account.

The parameter-space metric can be used to optimally construct a template grid
[
13
,
14
]
, and since the sensitivity of some CW searches is bounded by a limited computational budget, placing templates in a more accurate way (due to more accurate mismatch predictions) is an important topic of research
[
15
]
. There are more reasons why obtaining more accurate parameter-space metrics is relevant for CW searches, such as
(i) better analytical estimations of the uncertainty on the unknown parameters (see for example
[
16
]
) without having to carry out expensive Bayesian parameter estimation analyses;
(ii) optimal setup algorithms (such as
[
15
]
) could indicate that discarding some fraction of the less sensitive data can be beneficial, and the resulting dataset would have a lower duty cycle, thus making the usage of more realistic metrics more relevant;
(iii) use jump proposals (within Bayesian stochastic sampling algorithms such as
[
17
]
) based on the Fisher information matrix, which is proportional to the parameter-space metric;
(iv) compare the resulting posteriors from a Bayesian stochastic sampling analysis with Fisher matrix predictions for validation tests.

The main results of this paper are: (i) we derive an expression for the metric where data gaps and a varying noise floor are taken into account, and we implement this expression numerically; (ii) we present a new expression for a marginalized (over the unknown amplitude parameters)
\mathcal{F}
-statistic metric; (iii) we derive an expression for the semi-coherent metric that properly takes into account the variability of the signal power across different segments by applying weights, and we derive the correct expression for detection statistics that are weighted, such as the one proposed in
[
18
]
. We test all of these new expressions and show that they are able to predict the mismatch more accurately than the previous expressions for realistic datasets.

This paper is organized in the following way: in Sec.
II
we give an introduction to the
\mathcal{F}
-statistic and to parameter-space metrics; in Sec.
III
we derive the expressions for the new generalized parameter-space metrics; in Sec.
IV
we show the improved accuracy of the new metrics through diverse numerical tests; in Sec.
V
we summarize the paper, present our conclusions, and advance some ideas for future research.

II.1
The
\mathcal{F}

In order to detect CWs, we compare two main hypotheses about the data

\displaystyle h_{3}

Here we used the definitions

(5)

in terms of the multi-detector scalar product
[
3
,
19
]

for uncorrelated noise between detectors and a narrow-band signal with flat noise floor in the signal band

(x|y)\equiv 2\gamma\left<xy\right>,

where we have defined the
data factor

The overall noise floor

-statistic follows a non-central

In the perfect-match case, where the phase-evolution

\displaystyle\rho^{2}_{0}

\displaystyle\equiv\rho^{2}(\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{s}},\lambda_{\mathrm{s}};\lambda_{\mathrm{s}})=(s|s)=h_{0}^{2}\gamma\,g^{2},

\displaystyle g^{2}(\hat{n},\cos\iota,\psi)

\displaystyle\alpha_{1}

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{1}{4}\left(1+\cos^{2}\!\iota\right)^{2}\cos^{2}2\psi+\cos^{2}\!\iota\,\sin^{2}2\psi\,,

(15)

\displaystyle\alpha_{2}

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{1}{4}\left(1+\cos^{2}\!\iota\right)^{2}\sin^{2}2\psi+\cos^{2}\!\iota\,\cos^{2}2\psi\,,

\displaystyle A=\left<a^{2}\right>,\;B=\left<b^{2}\right>,\;C=\left<ab\right>,

and the sub-determinant
D\equiv AB-C^{2}

Due to the high computational cost of wide-parameter-space CW searches, these typically use semi-coherent methods, where the data

This was generalized in

where
\mathcal{F}_{\ell}
is the coherent
\mathcal{F}
-statistic in segment
\ell

is the per-segment weight,

Dropping the per-segment weights recovers the classic semi-coherent

As discussed in

II.2
Parameter-space metrics

Due to the high computational cost of wide parameter-space CW searches, a grid of templates with finite spacing is used to cover the selected parameter-space region. For this reason, the values of the searched parameters will not be equal to the parameters of a possible astrophysical signal.
The (coherent) mismatch
\mu
describes the relative loss of signal power
\rho_{0}^{2}\equiv\rho^{2}(\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{s}},\lambda_{\mathrm{s}};\lambda_{\mathrm{s}})
due to computing the coherent detection statistic at offset phase-evolution parameters
\lambda=\lambda_{\mathrm{s}}+\Delta\lambda
with offset signal power
\rho_{\Delta\!\lambda}^{2}\equiv\rho^{2}(\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{s}},\lambda_{\mathrm{s}};\lambda)
, i.e.

\displaystyle\mu\equiv 1-\frac{\rho_{\Delta\!\lambda}^{2}}{\rho^{2}_{0}},

(22)

which ranges from
0
(fully recovered signal power) to
1
(no recovered signal power).

The mismatch
\mu
can be approximated by a Taylor expansion of the signal power around the signal parameters (where the
mismatch attains a minimum of 0), keeping terms only up to second order
[
6
]
:

m\equiv g_{ij}\,\Delta\lambda^{i}\Delta\lambda^{j}\,,\quad\mu\approx m+\mathcal{O}\left(\Delta\lambda^{3}\right),

(23)

where
g_{ij}
is the parameter-space
metric
and
i
and
j
are indices over the phase-evolution parameters.
The true mismatch
\mu
of Eq. (
22
) is bound within
[0,1]
, while the metric-mismatch
prediction
m
is quadratic in offsets
\Delta\lambda
and can therefore be larger than one.

It has been shown that the metric mismatch
m
is typically a good approximation of the true mismatch
\mu

of Eq. (
22
) up to mismatches of
\mu\lesssim 0.1

[
6
,
25
]
,
above which it starts to overestimate the true mismatch.
The metric is useful to build template banks of given maximal mismatch and one can estimate the resulting number of
templates
\mathcal{N}
needed to cover a parameter-space region
\mathcal{R}
by an expression of the form (neglecting boundary effects)

[
26
]
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}\propto\int_{\mathcal{R}}\sqrt{\mathrm{det}(g_{ij})}d\lambda.

(24)

The most general form of the metric, as defined by Taylor-expanding Eq. (
22
), results in the

\mathcal{F}
-statistic metric
g_{ij}^{\mathcal{F}}(\cos\iota,\psi;\lambda)
, which
depends on the subset
\{\cos\iota,\psi\}
of the four signal amplitude parameters
\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{s}}
.
An explicit expression for this
\mathcal{F}
-statistic metric can be obtained
[
6
]
as:

g_{ij}^{\mathcal{F}}(\cos\iota,\psi;\lambda)=\frac{\alpha_{1}\,m_{1;ij}+\alpha_{2}\,m_{2;ij}+2\alpha_{3}\,m_{3;ij}}{g^{2}},

(25)

in terms of the amplitude angle functions
\alpha_{i}(\cos\iota,\psi)
defined in Eq. (
15
),
and the matrices

\displaystyle m_{1;ij}

\displaystyle\equiv\left<a^{2}\partial_{i}\phi\partial_{j}\phi\right>-\frac{A\left<ab\partial_{i}\phi\right>\left<ab\partial_{j}\phi\right>+B\left<a^{2}\partial_{i}\phi\right>\left<a^{2}\partial_{j}\phi\right>-2C\left<a^{2}\partial_{i}\phi\right>\left<ab\partial_{j}\phi\right>}{D}\,,

\displaystyle m_{2;ij}

\displaystyle\equiv\left<b^{2}\partial_{i}\phi\partial_{j}\phi\right>-\frac{A\left<b^{2}\partial_{i}\phi\right>\left<b^{2}\partial_{j}\phi\right>+B\left<ab\partial_{i}\phi\right>\left<ab\partial_{j}\phi\right>-2C\left<ab\partial_{i}\phi\right>\left<b^{2}\partial_{j}\phi\right>}{D}\,,

(26)

\displaystyle m_{3;ij}

\displaystyle\equiv\left<ab\partial_{i}\phi\partial_{j}\phi\right>-\frac{A\left<ab\partial_{i}\phi\right>\left<b^{2}\partial_{j}\phi\right>+B\left<ab\partial_{i}\phi\right>\left<a^{2}\partial_{j}\phi\right>-C\left[\left<b^{2}\partial_{i}\phi\right>\left<a^{2}\partial_{j}\phi\right>+\left<ab\partial_{i}\phi\right>\left<ab\partial_{j}\phi\right>\right]}{D},

where
\partial_{i}\phi\equiv\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial\lambda^{i}}
are the partial phase derivatives for the phase-evolution parameters
\lambda^{i}
.

The signal amplitude parameters
\cos\iota
and
\psi
are generally unknown, therefore the practical usefulness of this metric
is somewhat limited.
One level of simplification consists in an amplitude-“average” form of the
\mathcal{F}
-statistic metric,
g^{\bar{\mathcal{F}}}_{ij}
, which was obtained

[
6
]
in the form

g_{ij}^{\bar{\mathcal{F}}}(\lambda)=\frac{B\,m_{1;ij}+A\,m_{2;ij}-2C\,m_{3;ij}}{2D}.

(27)

However, the most commonly-used approximation is the
phase metric

g_{ij}^{\phi}

(initially proposed in
[

), which is obtained by neglecting the signal amplitude modulation,
resulting in the simpler expression

\displaystyle g_{ij}^{\phi}(\lambda)=\left\langle\partial_{i}\phi\,\partial_{j}\phi\right\rangle-\left\langle\partial_{i}\phi\right\rangle\left\langle\partial_{j}\phi\right\rangle.

(28)

The semi-coherent metric is typically obtained by averaging the coherent metrics over segments, namely

\displaystyle\widehat{g}_{ij}=\frac{1}{N_{\mathrm{seg}}}\,\sum_{\ell}g_{ij,\ell},

(29)

which can be derived under the assumption of
constant per-segment signal power

(see for example
[
8
]
or
[
7
]
).

III
Generalized parameter-space metrics

In this section we derive more general expressions for the various parameter-space metrics and discuss improvements in
their implementation, in order to take into account data gaps and non-stationarities.
This results in more accurate and robust parameter-space metrics, which will be tested numerically in Sec.
IV
.

III.1
Data gaps and varying noise floor

The metric expressions in Eqs. (

27
), and (
28
) are

However, current metric implementations in the

-statistic context, including the main implementation in

without taking into account data gaps or varying noise floors

2

There is some support for differing noise-levels between detectors (see Eq. (59) in

III.2
New

\mathcal{F}
-statistic metric

As discussed in Sec.

-statistic metric

This expression can be substantially more accurate than the “average”

-statistic metric

8
]
, but those can be equally used with the expressions
derived here.

\widehat{\mu}_{\mathrm{w}}\equiv 1-\frac{\widehat{\rho}_{{\mathrm{w}}\Delta\!\lambda}^{2}}{\widehat{\rho}_{{\mathrm{w}}0}^{2}},

(32)

in terms of the offset and perfect-match semi-coherent signal power of Eq. (
21
) using either
offset
\rho_{\Delta\!\lambda;\ell}^{2}
or perfect-match per-segment signal power
\rho_{0;\ell}^{2}
, respectively.
Using the per-segment mismatch
\mu_{\ell}
of Eq. (
22
), we can express the offset per-segment
signal power as

\rho^{2}_{\Delta\!\lambda;\ell}=(1-\mu_{\ell})\,\rho^{2}_{0;\ell},

(33)

therefore

\widehat{\rho}_{{\mathrm{w}}\Delta\!\lambda}^{2}=\sum_{\ell}w_{\ell}\rho_{\Delta\!\lambda;\ell}^{2}=\widehat{\rho}_{{\mathrm{w}}0}^{2}-\sum_{\ell}w_{\ell}\rho_{0;\ell}^{2}\,\mu_{\ell}\,,

(34)

and substituting into Eq. (
32
) we obtain

\widehat{\mu}_{\mathrm{w}}=\frac{1}{N_{\mathrm{seg}}}\sum_{\ell=1}^{N_{\mathrm{seg}}}W_{\ell}\,\mu_{\ell}\,,

(35)

where we defined metric segment weights
W_{\ell}
as

W_{\ell}\equiv\frac{N_{\mathrm{seg}}\,w_{\ell}\,\rho_{0;\ell}^{2}}{\widehat{\rho}_{{\mathrm{w}}0}^{2}},

(36)

with the usual weight normalization of
\sum_{\ell}W_{\ell}=N_{\mathrm{seg}}
.
As mentioned before, the “classic” semi-coherent metric expression given by Eq. (
29
) can be recovered
under the assumption of constant (weighted) signal power over segments, i.e.,
W_{\ell}=1
.

Applying the metric expansion of Eq. (
23
) for the
\mathcal{F}
-statistic metric

g^{\mathcal{F}}_{ij,\ell}
, we find

\displaystyle\widehat{\mu}_{\mathrm{w}}

\displaystyle\approx\widehat{g}^{\mathcal{F}}_{{\mathrm{w}};ij}\,\Delta\lambda^{i}\Delta\lambda^{j}+\mathcal{O}\left(\Delta\lambda^{3}\right),\quad\text{with}

(37)

\displaystyle\widehat{g}^{\mathcal{F}}_{{\mathrm{w}};ij}

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{1}{N_{\mathrm{seg}}}\sum_{\ell}W_{\ell}\,g^{\mathcal{F}}_{ij,\ell}\,.

(38)

As noted before in Sec.
II.2
, however, this expression is not typically usable in practice, because both the metric
weights
W_{\ell}
and the
\mathcal{F}
-statistic metric
g^{\mathcal{F}}
depend on the unknown signal amplitude parameters

\{\cos\iota,\psi\}
.
The most natural approach again is to marginalize over them using physical priors, as discussed as in
Sec.
III.2
, and using the same marginalization approximation as before
(see Sec.
C
for details), we obtain the general result

\widehat{g}_{{\mathrm{w}};ij}(\lambda)\approx\frac{1}{N_{\mathrm{seg}}}\sum_{\ell=1}^{N_{\mathrm{seg}}}\varpi_{\ell}\,g_{ij,\ell}(\lambda)\,,

(39)

in terms of the marginalized
metric segment weights

\varpi_{\ell}\propto w_{\ell}\,\gamma_{\ell}\left(A_{\ell}+B_{\ell}\right)\,,

(40)

with standard weight normalization
\sum_{\ell}\varpi_{\ell}=N_{\mathrm{seg}}
.

There are two important cases to distinguish, namely whether the underlying semi-coherent statistic is weighted
by
w_{\ell}
or not (i.e., dropping
w_{\ell}
in the above expressions).
The explicit metric weights for two cases are therefore

\varpi_{\ell}=\begin{cases}k^{\prime}w_{\ell}^{2}&\text{for weighted }\widehat{\mathcal{F}}_{{\mathrm{w}}}\,,\\
w_{\ell}&\text{for unweighted }\widehat{\mathcal{F}}\,,\end{cases}

(41)

in terms of the segment weights
w_{\ell}
given in
Eq. (
19
).

For the unweighted
\widehat{\mathcal{F}}
-statistic the metric weights are therefore equal to the statistic weights
w_{\ell}

derived in
[
18
]
, while the metric weights for the weighted
\widehat{\mathcal{F}}_{{\mathrm{w}}}
-statistic are
proportional to the
squared
statistic weights.

A potential practical difficulty introduced by these metric weights is an additional sky-position dependency (via the
antenna-pattern coefficients
A,B
), even if the per-segment metric itself was constant over the sky.
This can complicate template-bank placement, but one would likely still reap benefits from using sky-marginalized weights instead,
namely
\left\langle\varpi_{\ell}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{sky}}\propto\{\gamma_{\ell}^{2},\gamma_{\ell}\}
(for weighted and unweighted statistics,
respectively), therefore still accounting for the variations in data-quality and quantity over segments illustrated in
Fig.
1
.

\varepsilon
between

(42)

V
Conclusions

In this paper we have generalized the expressions for the parameter-space metrics related to the
\mathcal{F}
-statistic.
We have shown that for realistic datasets with data gaps and a varying noise floor the idealized expressions can fail to
predict the shape and size of the
\mathcal{F}
-statistic mismatch correctly, and that the new generalized expressions are more
accurate.
We have presented a new metric approximation describing a polarization-marginalized
\mathcal{F}
-statistic metric
g^{\left\langle\mathcal{F}\right\rangle}
,
which seems to improve accuracy and robustness compared to the averaged
\mathcal{F}
-statistic metric
g^{\bar{\mathcal{F}}}
.
We have further derived a more accurate semi-coherent metric that properly takes into account the variability of
signal power across segments by using weights, and we have also extended the metric expression for weighted
semi-coherent statistics.
We have shown that these new generalized metrics better predict the measured mismatches of the actual
\mathcal{F}
-statistic
when used on realistic datasets including gaps and varying noise-floors.

One downside of the new generalized metric expressions is a higher computational cost required to compute them, due to
the need to integrate SFT by SFT, which might complicate their application in cases where many different metrics need to
be computed, such as in an injection campaign with thousands of signals.

We have also found that the new metrics on realistic datasets (like the O2 and O3 observing runs) tend to reduce the
expected number of required templates and thereby the computing cost of searches using these metrics.

Here we have only explored metrics for the classic
\mathcal{F}
-statistic, which was recently found to be quite sub-optimal
especially for short segments
[
31
,
23
]
.
It would therefore be very interesting to investigate what metric expressions one obtains for these new short-segment
statistics, and how those would be related to the marginalized
\mathcal{F}
-statistic metric
g^{\left\langle\mathcal{F}\right\rangle}
.
It might also be interesting to investigate the parameter-space metric of the detection statistic that includes a
non-Gaussian line hypothesis such as
[
36
]
.

Let us look in more detail at the (perfect-match) weighted semi-coherent signal power
\widehat{\rho}_{{\mathrm{w}}0}^{2}

),
by inserting the per-segment matched signal power
\rho_{0;\ell}^{2}

\displaystyle\widehat{\rho}_{{\mathrm{w}}0}^{2}

\displaystyle=h_{0}^{2}\sum_{\ell}w_{\ell}\gamma_{\ell}\left(\alpha_{1}A_{\ell}+\alpha_{2}B_{\ell}+2\alpha_{3}C_{\ell}\right).

Using Eq. (

\widehat{\gamma}\equiv\mathcal{S}^{-1}\,T_{\mathrm{data}}=\sum_{\ell}\gamma_{\ell}\,,

(45)

T_{\mathrm{data}}
now refer to the full dataset over all segments.
We can therefore write the weighted signal power Eq. (
44
) in the form

\widehat{\rho}_{{\mathrm{w}}0}^{2}=h_{0}^{2}\,\widehat{\gamma}\,\widehat{g}_{\mathrm{w}}^{2}\,,

(46)

where we defined the (weighted) semi-coherent geometric response function as

\widehat{g}_{\mathrm{w}}^{2}\equiv\alpha_{1}\widehat{A}_{\mathrm{w}}+\alpha_{2}\widehat{B}_{\mathrm{w}}+2\alpha_{3}\widehat{C}_{\mathrm{w}},

(47)

in terms of the (weighted) semi-coherent antenna-pattern coefficients

\displaystyle\widehat{\gamma}\widehat{A}_{\mathrm{w}}

\displaystyle\equiv\sum_{\ell}w_{\ell}\gamma_{\ell}A_{\ell},

\displaystyle\widehat{\gamma}\widehat{B}_{\mathrm{w}}

\displaystyle\equiv\sum_{\ell}w_{\ell}\gamma_{\ell}B_{\ell},

(48)

\displaystyle\widehat{\gamma}\widehat{C}_{\mathrm{w}}

\displaystyle\equiv\sum_{\ell}w_{\ell}\gamma_{\ell}C_{\ell}\,.

As expected, the above weighted expressions reduce to the standard semi-coherent forms when dropping the weights
w_{\ell}
.

Appendix B
Amplitude-marginalizing the coherent
\mathcal{F}
-statistic metric

In order to marginalize the
\mathcal{F}
-statistic metric
g^{\mathcal{F}}_{ij}
of Eq. (
25
) over
\cos\iota,\psi
,
we start by writing it more compactly as

g^{\mathcal{F}}_{ij}(\cos\iota,\psi;\lambda)=\sum_{k=1}^{3}m_{k;ij}(\lambda)\frac{\underline{\alpha}_{k}(\cos\iota,\psi)}{g^{2}(\cos\iota,\psi;\lambda)}\,,

(49)

where for convenience of notation we defined
\underline{\alpha}_{1}\equiv\alpha_{1}
,
\underline{\alpha}_{2}\equiv\alpha_{2}
and
\underline{\alpha}_{3}\equiv 2\alpha_{3}
.
With the marginalization integrals over
\cos\iota,\psi
of Eq. (
30
), we can write

g^{\left\langle\mathcal{F}\right\rangle}_{ij}(\lambda)\equiv\left\langle g^{\mathcal{F}}_{ij}\right\rangle_{\cos\iota,\psi}=\sum_{k=1}^{3}m_{k;ij}(\lambda)K_{k}(\lambda)\,,

(50)

in terms of the three marginalization integrals
K_{k}
, for which we propose the approximation

K_{k}(\lambda)\equiv\left\langle\frac{\underline{\alpha}_{k}}{g^{2}}\right\rangle_{\cos\iota,\psi}\approx\frac{\left\langle\underline{\alpha}_{k}\right\rangle_{\cos\iota,\psi}}{\left\langle g^{2}\right\rangle_{\cos\iota,\psi}}\,,

(51)

replacing the average of the ratio by the ratio of averages.
We will test the accuracy of this approximation numerically in Sec.
D
.
We can use the known averages (e.g.,
[
19
]
) of

\left\langle\alpha_{1}\right\rangle_{\cos\iota,\psi}=\left\langle\alpha_{2}\right\rangle_{\cos\iota,\psi}=2/5
and
\left\langle\alpha_{3}\right\rangle_{\cos\iota,\psi}=0
, to obtain

\left\langle g^{2}\right\rangle_{\cos\iota,\psi}=\frac{2}{5}(A+B)
from Eq. (
14
), and therefore

K_{1}\approx K_{2}\approx\frac{1}{A+B}\,,\quad K_{3}\approx 0\,.

(52)

Inserting this approximation into Eq. (
50
) we now find

g^{\left\langle\mathcal{F}\right\rangle}_{ij}\approx\frac{m_{1;ij}+m_{2;ij}}{A+B}\,,

(53)

as stated in Eq. (
31
).

Appendix C
Amplitude marginalization of semi-coherent metrics

Here we consider the amplitude-marginalization of the most general semi-coherent metric
\widehat{g}^{\mathcal{F}}_{{\mathrm{w}};ij}

of Eq. (
38
) for (weighted)
\mathcal{F}
-statistics.
We can write this amplitude-marginalized metric as

\displaystyle\widehat{g}^{\left\langle\mathcal{F}\right\rangle}_{{\mathrm{w}};ij}

\displaystyle\equiv\left\langle\widehat{g}^{\mathcal{F}}_{{\mathrm{w}};ij}\right\rangle_{\cos\iota,\psi}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N_{\mathrm{seg}}}\sum_{\ell}\left\langle W_{\ell}\,g^{\mathcal{F}}_{ij,\ell}\right\rangle_{\cos\iota,\psi}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{3}\sum_{\ell=1}^{N_{\mathrm{seg}}}w_{\ell}\,m_{k;ij,\ell}\left\langle\frac{\rho_{0;\ell}^{2}}{\widehat{\rho}_{{\mathrm{w}}0}^{2}}\frac{\underline{\alpha}_{k}}{g_{\ell}^{2}}\right\rangle_{\cos\iota,\psi}\,,

(54)

where in the last line we substituted the metric weights
W_{\ell}
of Eq. (
36
) and the per-segment
\mathcal{F}
-statistic metric

g^{\mathcal{F}}_{ij,\ell}
of Eq. (
49
).
Using the explicit signal-power expression Eq. (
13
) with Eq. (
46
), this leads to

\displaystyle\widehat{g}^{\left\langle\mathcal{F}\right\rangle}_{{\mathrm{w}};ij}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\ell}w_{\ell}\,m_{k;ij,\ell}\,\frac{\gamma_{\ell}}{\widehat{\gamma}}K_{{\mathrm{w}};k}\,,

(55)

where we defined (similar to the coherent case given by Eq. (
51
)) the marginalization integrals
K_{{\mathrm{w}};k}
, for which
we propose the approximation

K_{{\mathrm{w}};k}\equiv\left\langle\frac{\underline{\alpha}_{k}}{\widehat{g}_{\mathrm{w}}^{2}}\right\rangle_{\cos\iota,\psi}\approx\frac{\left\langle\underline{\alpha}_{k}\right\rangle_{\cos\iota,\psi}}{\left\langle\widehat{g}_{\mathrm{w}}^{2}\right\rangle_{\cos\iota,\psi}}\,,

(56)

with the result in the same form as in Sec.
B
:

K_{{\mathrm{w}};1}\approx K_{{\mathrm{w}};2}\approx\frac{1}{\widehat{A}_{\mathrm{w}}+\widehat{B}_{\mathrm{w}}}\,,\quad K_{{\mathrm{w}};3}\approx 0\,.

(57)

Putting this together with Eq. (
55
) we obtain the approximation

\displaystyle\widehat{g}^{\left\langle\mathcal{F}\right\rangle}_{{\mathrm{w}};ij}

\displaystyle\approx\sum_{\ell}w_{\ell}\frac{\gamma_{\ell}}{\widehat{\gamma}}\,\frac{m_{1;ij,\ell}+m_{2;ij,\ell}}{\widehat{A}_{\mathrm{w}}+\widehat{B}_{\mathrm{w}}}

\displaystyle\approx\sum_{\ell}\left(w_{\ell}\frac{\gamma_{\ell}}{\widehat{\gamma}}\frac{A_{\ell}+B_{\ell}}{\widehat{A}_{\mathrm{w}}+\widehat{B}_{\mathrm{w}}}\right)\,g^{\left\langle\mathcal{F}\right\rangle}_{ij,\ell}\,,

(58)

where in the last line we used the coherent marginalized
\mathcal{F}
-statistic metric
g^{\left\langle\mathcal{F}\right\rangle}_{ij,\ell}
of Eq. (
31
).
We can see that the weight factor multiplying
g^{\left\langle\mathcal{F}\right\rangle}_{ij,\ell}
corresponds to the (marginalized) metric segment weights

\varpi_{\ell}
given in Eq. (
40
), namely

\varpi_{\ell}\equiv k^{\prime}\,w_{\ell}\gamma_{\ell}\,(A_{\ell}+B_{\ell})\,,

(59)

with normalization

k^{\prime}=\frac{N_{\mathrm{seg}}}{\widehat{\gamma}\,(\widehat{A}_{\mathrm{w}}+\widehat{B}_{\mathrm{w}})},

(60)

satisfying the normalization condition
\sum_{\ell}\varpi_{\ell}=N_{\mathrm{seg}}
.
Therefore we can write

\widehat{g}^{\left\langle\mathcal{F}\right\rangle}_{{\mathrm{w}};ij}\approx\frac{1}{N_{\mathrm{seg}}}\sum_{\ell}\varpi_{\ell}\,g^{\left\langle\mathcal{F}\right\rangle}_{ij,\ell}\,,

(61)

as stated in Eq. (
39
).

In order to see that this form of the result also holds true for the phase metric, we first note that

\displaystyle\left\langle W_{\ell}\right\rangle_{\cos\iota,\psi}

\displaystyle=N_{\mathrm{seg}}w_{\ell}\left\langle\frac{\rho_{0,\ell}^{2}}{\widehat{\rho}_{{\mathrm{w}}0}^{2}}\right\rangle_{\cos\iota,\psi}

\displaystyle\approx N_{\mathrm{seg}}w_{\ell}\frac{\gamma_{\ell}}{\widehat{\gamma}}\frac{\left\langle g_{\ell}^{2}\right\rangle}{\left\langle g_{\mathrm{w}}^{2}\right\rangle}

\displaystyle=N_{\mathrm{seg}}\frac{w_{\ell}\gamma_{\ell}\,(A_{\ell}+B_{\ell})}{\widehat{\gamma}\,(\widehat{A}_{\mathrm{w}}+\widehat{B}_{\mathrm{w}})}

\displaystyle=\varpi_{\ell}\,,

(62)

and therefore the amplitude-marginalized weighted phase-metric is also approximated in the form

\displaystyle\widehat{g}^{\left\langle\phi\right\rangle}_{{\mathrm{w}};ij}

\displaystyle\equiv\left\langle g_{{\mathrm{w}};ij}^{\phi}\right\rangle_{\cos\iota,\psi}=\frac{1}{N_{\mathrm{seg}}}\sum_{\ell}\left\langle W_{\ell}\right\rangle g^{\phi}_{ij,\ell}

\displaystyle\approx\frac{1}{N_{\mathrm{seg}}}\sum_{\ell}\varpi_{\ell}\,g^{\phi}_{ij,\ell}\,,

(63)

as stated in Eq. (
39
).

Appendix D
Testing the amplitude marginalization approximation

We perform numerical tests for the two cases of the coherent approximation given by Eq. (
52
) of Sec.
B

as well as the semi-coherent weighted case of Eq. (
57
) of Sec.
C
. In these tests we compare the true values, computed numerically,
to the approximations.

Given
K_{3}
is approximated as
0
, we cannot compute a standard relative error, instead we chose a common approximation “scale” of

K_{0}\equiv 1/(A+B)
to compute the relative importance of the absolute deviations in the sum in Eq. (
50
), namely

\varepsilon_{1}\equiv\frac{K_{1}-K_{0}}{K_{0}},\quad\varepsilon_{2}\equiv\frac{K_{2}-K_{0}}{K_{0}},\quad\varepsilon_{3}\equiv\frac{K_{3}}{K_{0}}\,.

(64)

We have made the same definitions for the semi-coherent weighted case, substituting
K_{k}
and
\varepsilon_{k}
by
K_{\mathrm{w};k}
and
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{w};k}
.

Fig.
8
shows histograms of these relative deviations computed over randomly chosen sky-positions for different data spans.
We see that this approximation tends to only introduce small errors on the order of a few percent for observation times of a day and longer.
However, for shorter observations such as
T_{\mathrm{data}}=1/4
days, we see that this approximation is less precise, and a different approach might be needed, which is postponed to future work.
