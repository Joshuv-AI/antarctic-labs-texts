Clinical value of structural cranial magnetic resonance imaging analysis in assessing brain aging in ischemic stroke patients
Authors: Tao Wang, Xiaoguang You, Junjing Yin, Huang Pan, Ziling Liu, Qian Dai, Jingang Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveUsing structural cranial magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), we constructed a structural Brain Aging Index (sBAI) and evaluated the brain aging status, expressed as Brain Predicted Age Difference (Brain-PAD), in acute ischemic stroke patients. We aim to investigate its associations with baseline clinical and imaging characteristics, small vessel disease (SVD) burden, and 12-month functional and cognitive outcomes, and explored its potential contribution to prognostic models.MethodsA retrospective cohort of 150 patients with acute ischemic stroke admitted between November 2021 and November 2023 was analyzed. Introduction

Population aging and the growing burden of cerebrovascular diseases have become major global public health challenges [1, 2]. In patients with ischemic stroke [3], beyond the effects of acute brain injury, the degree of “biological aging” of brain structures has been increasingly recognized as a key factor influencing prognosis and cognitive function [4]. In recent years, approaches using structural magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) [5] to estimate “brain age” or the brain-predicted age difference (Brain-PAD) have gained attention [6]. These metrics reflect the extent to which brain tissues exhibit “accelerated aging” compared with chronological aging under the impact of pathological events [7].

In stroke research [8, 9], previous studies have shown that first-ever stroke patients often exhibit a brain age exceeding their chronological age by approximately 3.9–8.7 years. Recent longitudinal studies further report that the brain age gap (BAG) may increase by an average of 3.6 years from the acute phase to 3–7 months poststroke, suggesting progressive poststroke neurodegeneration or secondary network disruption. Moreover, structural neuroimaging markers, such as white matter hyperintensity (WMH) volume, lacunes, cerebral microbleeds (CMBs), and cortical thickness [10], have demonstrated significant associations with brain aging indices.

However, evidence remains limited regarding brain aging assessment in acute ischemic stroke based on structural MRI features (e.g., WMH, CMBs, lacunes [11], cortical atrophy) in larger patient cohorts. In particular, the relationships between brain aging status, small vessel disease (SVD) burden, stroke severity, and long-term functional and cognitive outcomes have not been fully established. Further, whether incorporating a structural brain aging index, such as the standardized MRI-based structural Brain Aging Index (sBAI), into multivariable models provides incremental prognostic value for stroke outcomes remains unclear.

Against this background, we enrolled 150 patients with acute ischemic stroke and extracted structural MRI measures including diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) infarct volume, WMH volume, lacunes, CMBs, enlarged perivascular spaces (EPVS) grading, hippocampal volume [12], and mean cortical thickness.This study aimed to investigate the clinical relevance of structural brain aging in patients with acute ischemic stroke using MRI-derived metrics.

Specifically, we aimed to: (1) compare baseline clinical and imaging characteristics between patients with age-matched and accelerated brain aging; (2) examine the associations between structural brain aging and MRI markers of small vessel disease; (3) evaluate the relationships between brain aging status and functional and cognitive outcomes. In addition, we explored whether structural brain aging metrics provide additional information beyond conventional clinical and imaging variables [13].

Methods

Study design and participants

This single-center, retrospective study included 150 patients diagnosed with acute ischemic stroke who underwent cranial MRI between November 2021 and November 2023. Theoretical sample-size evaluation showed that 150 patients provided adequate power (> 80%) for detecting moderate effects in group comparisons, regression analyses, and logistic models, confirming that the current sample size is sufficient for the study objectives.

Inclusion criteria: (1) age ≥ 18 years; (2) acute ischemic stroke confirmed by DWI; (3) MRI performed within 48 h after symptom onset; (4) availability of baseline clinical data and 12-month functional and cognitive follow-up results. Exclusion criteria: (1) prior intracerebral hemorrhage, severe brain malformations, or brain tumors; (2) concomitant severe neurological disorders (e.g., advanced Alzheimer’s disease, Parkinson’s disease); (3) poor MRI quality or severe motion artifacts.

Clinical data collection

Baseline data included: age, sex, body weight, height (for body mass index, BMI), hypertension, diabetes mellitus, atrial fibrillation, smoking history, admission systolic blood pressure, admission National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) score [14], lipid profile (low-density lipoprotein cholesterol, LDL-C), high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP), hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c), and ischemic stroke subtype (large-artery atherosclerotic stroke, small-vessel occlusion, cardioembolic stroke, etc.).

MRI acquisition and structural imaging measurements

Cranial MRI was performed using a standard 3.0-T scanner (GE, USA). Patients were positioned supine using a dedicated head coil or combined head–neck coil, with foam pads used for stabilization. The major imaging sequences and typical parameters were as follows:

Axial T1-weighted imaging (T1WI): repetition time (TR) 1700–2500 ms, echo time (TE) 10–22 ms, field of view (FOV) 20.0 × 24.0 cm, matrix 256 × 224, number of excitations (NEX) 2.

Axial T1-weighted imaging (T1WI): repetition time (TR) 1700–2500 ms, echo time (TE) 10–22 ms, field of view (FOV) 20.0 × 24.0 cm, matrix 256 × 224, number of excitations (NEX) 2.

Axial T2-weighted imaging (T2WI): TR 4000–6500 ms, TE 110–130 ms, FOV 20.0 × 24.0 cm.Axial fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR): TR 7600 ms, TE 30 ms, inversion time (TI) 2282 ms, FOV 20.0 × 24.0 cm.

Axial T2-weighted imaging (T2WI): TR 4000–6500 ms, TE 110–130 ms, FOV 20.0 × 24.0 cm.Axial fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR): TR 7600 ms, TE 30 ms, inversion time (TI) 2282 ms, FOV 20.0 × 24.0 cm.

Sagittal T2WI: parameters identical to axial T2WI.

Sagittal T2WI: parameters identical to axial T2WI.

Since patients were scanned on different MRI units, minor variations in protocol existed; however, all sequences used a uniform slice thickness of 6 mm and interslice gap of 1 mm. Axial scans were oriented parallel to the line connecting the inferior borders of the genu and splenium of the corpus callosum, and sagittal scans were oriented parallel to the interhemispheric fissure.

Initial MRI findings including infarct location, morphology, extent, and maximum diameter were independently assessed by two radiologists with more than three years of neuroimaging experience. Discrepancies were adjudicated by two senior radiologists (> 10 years of experience).

Two axial T1WI datasets (baseline and follow-up) were used as input for automated quantification of Percentage Brain Volume Change (PBVC) using the Structural Image Evaluation, using Normalization, of Atrophy (SIENA/SIENAX) tool in the FMRIB Software Library (FSL). The workflow included image preprocessing, geometric alignment of paired scans, automated brain extraction and tissue segmentation, and computation of edge displacement maps to derive PBVC (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Image processing pipeline: acute infarct volume (mL) measured on DWI; WMH volume measured on FLAIR using automated or semi-automated segmentation.

For patients with new cortical or white-matter infarcts on initial imaging, WMH quantification was manually corrected to exclude acute infarct lesions. WMH in this study included demyelinating hyperintensities, deep white-matter lacunar cavities, and gliosis from chronic infarcts. To avoid mass-effect bias in hemispheric volume estimation, when infarct cavities could not be excluded directly, contralateral hemispheric volume was used as a reference. Acute infarct lesions were segmented using semi-automated volumetric software (Supplementary Fig. 2). In second-time MRI scans, any new infarcts and surrounding edema were excluded from brain volume estimation.

Additional imaging markers included: Lacunes (counted manually); CMBs (identified on T2-weighted imaging or SWI); EPVS (graded 0–4 in the basal ganglia); hippocampal volume and mean cortical thickness (obtained via automated segmentation on T1WI using FreeSurfer); total SVD score (based on international criteria integrating WMH, lacunes, CMBs, and EPVS).

Calculation of sBAI and Brain-PAD

In this study, brain-age estimation was performed using a regression-based approach derived from structural MRI features within the current cohort, rather than a machine learning model trained on an external dataset. Therefore, the resulting brain-age metrics should be interpreted as relative indicators of structural brain aging rather than absolute predictions.

Structural MRI variables included white matter hyperintensity (WMH) volume, lacune count, cerebral microbleed (CMB) count, enlarged perivascular space (EPVS) grade, hippocampal volume, and mean cortical thickness. All variables were standardized using z-score transformation to ensure comparability across different measurement scales. Variables reflecting greater structural damage (e.g., WMH, lacunes, CMBs, EPVS) were aligned such that higher z-scores indicated greater burden, whereas variables reflecting preserved structure (e.g., hippocampal volume and cortical thickness) were directionally adjusted.

Each standardized variable was assigned equal weight, and the composite structural Brain Aging Index (sBAI) was calculated as the mean of these z-scores. Thus, sBAI represents a summary index of cumulative structural brain alterations rather than a weighted or model-derived prediction metric.

Brain-predicted age difference (Brain-PAD) was defined as the difference between estimated brain age and chronological age. A threshold of + 5 years was used to define accelerated brain aging, based on commonly used conventions in prior brain-age literature. Accordingly, patients were categorized into an age-matched group (Brain-PAD < + 5 years) and an accelerated brain aging group (Brain-PAD ≥ + 5 years). This threshold should be interpreted as a pragmatic categorization rather than a biologically definitive boundary.

Regarding image preprocessing, acute infarct regions were excluded from volumetric measurements where feasible (e.g., during WMH quantification and lesion masking) to reduce lesion-related bias. However, formal lesion-filling procedures or stroke-specific correction algorithms were not applied. Therefore, residual bias related to acute or chronic lesions may have influenced structural measurements and brain-age estimation.

Because this approach was not based on an externally trained predictive model, no independent training dataset or validation metrics (e.g., mean absolute error) were available. In regression analyses, sBAI was treated as a standardized variable, and effect estimates were reported per one standard deviation (SD) increase.

Follow-up and outcome measures

All patients were followed for 12 months. Outcome measures included: (1) Functional outcomes: mRS scores at discharge and 12 months [15, 16], with mRS 0–2 defined as favorable and mRS ≥ 3 as poor outcome. (2) Cognitive outcomes: MoCA scores at discharge and 12 months [17]. (3) Stroke recurrence: within 12 months. (4) All-cause mortality.

Statistical analysis

Patients were grouped by brain aging status (age-matched vs. accelerated). Baseline clinical and MRI characteristics were compared using independent-samples t-tests or Mann–Whitney U tests for continuous variables, and χ² tests for categorical variables. Pearson correlation analysis assessed associations between sBAI and structural imaging markers (WMH volume, lacune count, CMB count, EPVS grade, hippocampal volume, cortical thickness). Associations between brain aging status and functional, cognitive, recurrence, and mortality outcomes were evaluated using χ² and t-tests. A multivariable logistic regression model was constructed to predict 12-month poor functional outcome (mRS ≥ 3), reporting adjusted odds ratios (aORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Discriminatory performance of baseline vs. sBAI-augmented models was compared using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis and DeLong tests for changes in area under the curve (ΔAUC). For cognitive outcomes, univariable linear regression examined associations between sBAI and 12-month MoCA scores, reporting F statistics, R², and P values. A two-sided P < 0.05 indicated statistical significance.

Data visualization was performed using GraphPad Prism 10.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA), R software version 4.3.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria), and SPSS version 26.0 (IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY, USA). Variance inflation factors (VIFs) were calculated to assess potential multicollinearity among imaging variables included in the regression models. In addition, sensitivity analyses were performed by excluding WMH volume from the multivariable model to evaluate the robustness of the association between sBAI and outcomes.

Ethical statement

This study was approved by the institutional ethics committee (approval number: JJXYDEFSYY-YXLL-2024003). All procedures adhered to the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and its later amendments [18]. Given the retrospective design, all data were anonymized, involving no patient-identifiable information or risk, and informed consent was waived by the ethics committee. Patient privacy and data security were rigorously protected throughout the study, and all information was used solely for scientific research purposes.

Results

Baseline clinical and MRI characteristics

A total of 150 patients with acute ischemic stroke were included. Based on the Brain-PAD threshold, patients were divided into an age-matched group (Brain-PAD < + 5 years; n = 98, 65.3%) and an accelerated brain aging group (Brain-PAD ≥ + 5 years; n = 52, 34.7%). No significant differences were observed between groups in age, sex, or traditional cardiovascular risk factors (all P > 0.05). The accelerated brain aging group showed significantly higher admission NIHSS scores, larger DWI infarct volumes, increased WMH volumes, and higher total SVD scores (all P < 0.001), as well as a greater proportion of patients with Fazekas ≥ 2 (80.8% vs. 62.2%, P = 0.020). These findings indicate that accelerated brain aging is associated with greater acute injury burden and cumulative chronic SVD. (Table 1).

MRI structural features and SVD burden

The sBAI was significantly correlated with multiple MRI structural features (Fig. 1 and Supplementary Table 1). sBAI showed positive correlations with WMH volume (r = 0.350, P < 0.001), lacunes (r = 0.329, P < 0.001), microbleed count (r = 0.363, P < 0.001), and EPVS grade (r = 0.805, P < 0.001), and negative correlations with hippocampal volume (r=–0.741, P < 0.001) and cortical thickness (r=–0.561, P < 0.001). These results indicate that higher structural brain aging is closely linked to greater SVD burden, characterized by more severe white matter injury and cortical atrophy.

Brain aging and functional outcome

At the 12-month follow-up, stratified analyses showed that patients in the accelerated brain aging group had a significantly lower rate of good functional outcome at discharge (61.5% vs. 81.6%, P = 0.007) and a higher incidence of poor functional outcome at 12 months (mRS ≥ 3: 30.8% vs. 14.3%, P = 0.016). In terms of cognition, MoCA scores at discharge and follow-up were consistently lower in the accelerated brain aging group (both P < 0.001). Recurrent stroke was also more frequent in the accelerated brain aging group (15.4% vs. 5.1%, P = 0.033), while all-cause mortality did not differ significantly (P = 0.767) (Table 2).

Multivariable logistic regression

To adjust for potential confounders, variables associated with 12-month poor functional outcome (mRS ≥ 3) in univariate analyses (P < 0.10) or considered clinically relevant were included: age, sex, admission NIHSS score, DWI infarct volume, atrial fibrillation, diabetes, LDL-C, and TOAST classification. The primary independent variable was sBAI (per + 1 SD).

In the baseline model including only traditional risk factors (Table 3; Fig. 2), admission NIHSS score (aOR = 1.20, 95% CI 1.03–1.40, P = 0.020) and WMH volume (per 1 mL increase: aOR = 1.10, 95% CI 1.03–1.18, P = 0.006) were independent predictors of poor functional outcome. Age, DWI infarct volume, LDL-C, total SVD score, sex, atrial fibrillation, diabetes, and classification were not significantly associated (all P > 0.05). After adding sBAI (Table 4, Supplementary Fig. 3), both WMH volume (aOR = 1.09, 95% CI 1.01–1.17, P = 0.020) and NIHSS score (aOR = 1.18, 95% CI 1.00–1.38, P = 0.045) remained significant predictors, whereas sBAI itself did not show an independent effect (aOR = 1.32, 95% CI 0.78–2.24, P = 0.301). Results for other covariates remained unchanged.

These findings suggest that WMH burden and stroke severity are the dominant independent predictors of long-term functional outcome, while the incremental contribution of sBAI becomes limited after adjustment for established clinical and imaging markers. No evidence of severe multicollinearity was observed (all VIFs < 5). In sensitivity analyses excluding WMH volume, the direction of the association between sBAI and poor functional outcome remained unchanged, although it did not reach statistical significance.

Incremental predictive value

In the baseline model (without sBAI), which included age, NIHSS score, DWI infarct volume, atrial fibrillation, diabetes, LDL-C, and TOAST subtype, the AUC for predicting 12-month poor functional outcome (mRS ≥ 3) was 0.7856 (95% CI 0.6892–0.8819), indicating good discriminative ability.

After adding sBAI, the AUC remained essentially unchanged (0.7856, 95% CI 0.6870–0.8841; ΔAUC ≈ 0.000; DeLong test P > 0.05) (Fig. 3). These findings indicate that the addition of sBAI does not improve the discriminative performance of conventional models for predicting functional outcomes.

Discussion

In this study of 150 patients with acute ischemic stroke, we investigated the relationships between structural brain aging metrics (Brain-PAD and sBAI), baseline clinical and imaging characteristics, small vessel disease (SVD) burden, and 12-month functional and cognitive outcomes. Overall, our findings suggest that structural brain aging is closely associated with cumulative SVD burden and cognitive outcomes, while sBAI may be better interpreted as a composite marker reflecting structural brain vulnerability rather than an independent predictor of functional outcome.

Key findings

First, patients with accelerated brain aging exhibited greater acute and chronic injury burden, as reflected by higher NIHSS scores, larger infarct volumes, higher WMH volumes, and higher total SVD scores, despite similar demographic characteristics and vascular risk profiles. This suggests that structural brain aging may capture underlying differences in brain vulnerability beyond traditional risk factors. These findings are consistent with Bretzner et al. [19]., and the proportion of patients with Fazekas ≥ 2 was substantially higher (80.8% vs. 62.2%). These results indicate that accelerated brain aging is closely associated with greater acute ischemic injury burden and cumulative chronic SVD damage [3].

Second, sBAI showed consistent associations with multiple structural MRI markers. Positive correlations were observed with WMH volume, lacunes, microbleeds, and EPVS, while negative correlations were found with hippocampal volume and cortical thickness. These findings indicate that advanced structural brain aging is linked to both microvascular damage and neurodegenerative changes.

Third, accelerated brain aging was associated with poorer functional and cognitive outcomes. Patients in this group had lower rates of favorable functional outcome and lower MoCA scores at both discharge and 12 months. In particular, sBAI demonstrated a moderate association with cognitive performance, suggesting potential relevance in the domain of post-stroke cognition.

However, in multivariable analysis, sBAI did not independently predict functional outcomes, and its inclusion did not improve model discrimination. This may be explained by the fact that key predictors such as WMH burden and stroke severity (NIHSS) already capture substantial prognostic information. Because sBAI is derived from structural imaging features that overlap with these variables, its incremental predictive value may be limited. Therefore, sBAI may be more appropriately interpreted as a composite marker reflecting cumulative structural brain alterations rather than an independent determinant of outcome. In this context, cerebral small vessel disease may represent a primary driver of accelerated brain aging. Additionally, univariable linear regression revealed a significant negative correlation between sBAI and 12-month MoCA score (R²≈0.29), indicating that greater structural brain aging is associated with poorer cognitive outcomes, consistent with findings by Lee et al. [20, 21].

Comparison with previous studies

These findings align with prior literature [22]. Early cross-sectional studies have shown that brain age in first-ever stroke patients is markedly higher than chronological age; for example, Egorova et al. reported a mean Brain-PAD of approximately + 4 years. Liew SL et al., in Neurology, found that a “younger” brain age was associated with better post-stroke recovery [23], highlighting the importance of brain reserve in stroke outcomes. Recent longitudinal studies further suggest that brain age may accelerate by 3–4 years within months after stroke, and such changes correlate with motor recovery [24].

Our study extends previous evidence by integrating structural MRI markers (WMH, lacunes, microbleeds, EPVS, hippocampal volume, cortical thickness) into a comprehensive analysis of brain aging, thereby enriching the conceptual framework connecting “brain aging—SVD—stroke injury—functional/cognitive outcomes.” At baseline, we observed that patients with accelerated brain aging already had greater acute injury burden and chronic SVD burden despite similar classical risk profiles, indicating that differences in structural brain aging may reflect underlying variation in brain fragility or brain reserve even among individuals with similar demographics and risk exposures.

However, unlike some studies, sBAI did not provide independent predictive value for functional outcome in multivariable modeling. Possible explanations include: (1) WMH volume and NIHSS, which directly reflect injury load and stroke severity, may exert stronger predictive effects; (2) although sBAI captures structural brain aging, its incremental value may be attenuated when WMH and other SVD markers are included; and (3) the moderate sample size (n = 150) and single-center design may limit statistical power to detect additional effects.

Clinical implications

These findings suggest that structural brain aging reflects overall brain vulnerability in acute ischemic stroke. Although sBAI did not improve prediction of functional outcomes, its association with cognitive outcomes indicates potential value in identifying patients at risk of post-stroke cognitive impairment. Incorporating structural MRI markers into routine assessment may therefore provide additional insight into brain health and recovery potential.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, it was a single-center study with a moderate sample size, which may limit generalizability and statistical power. Second, the brain-age estimation approach was based on structural MRI features within the current cohort rather than externally trained models, and no independent validation was performed. Therefore, the derived metrics should be interpreted as relative indicators rather than precise predictions.

Third, although efforts were made to exclude infarct regions during image processing, no formal lesion-filling procedures were applied. Both acute and chronic lesions may introduce bias into structural measurements and affect brain-age estimation.

Fourth, the use of non-isotropic MRI data with a slice thickness of 6 mm may reduce segmentation accuracy, particularly for cortical thickness and hippocampal volume. These measurements should therefore be interpreted as approximate indicators.

Fifth, the analysis of cognitive outcomes was based on univariable regression and did not adjust for important confounders such as age, education level, stroke severity, and baseline cognitive status. In addition, the number of outcome events was relatively limited, resulting in a modest events-per-variable ratio and a potential risk of model overfitting.

Finally, the cutoff of + 5 years used to define accelerated brain aging is not universally standardized and should be interpreted as a pragmatic categorization rather than a biologically definitive threshold. In addition, inter-rater reliability was not formally assessed, which may introduce measurement variability.

Future directions

Future studies should focus on developing externally validated brain-age models using large datasets and advanced analytical approaches. Longitudinal, multicenter studies are needed to better characterize brain aging trajectories and their relationship with functional and cognitive outcomes. Integration of multimodal imaging and investigation of modifiable factors influencing brain aging may further enhance the clinical utility of these metrics.

Conclusion

Using structural MRI–based brain-aging assessment, this study demonstrates that brain aging status is associated with acute lesion burden, cumulative small vessel disease, and long-term cognitive outcomes in patients with acute ischemic stroke.

However, sBAI did not provide incremental predictive value for functional outcomes beyond established clinical and imaging variables. Therefore, sBAI should be interpreted primarily as a descriptive and biologically informative marker reflecting cumulative structural brain alterations.

The observed association between sBAI and cognitive performance suggests that brain-aging metrics may have greater relevance in understanding post-stroke cognitive impairment rather than in predicting functional disability. Future studies incorporating larger samples, external validation, and multimodal imaging are warranted to further clarify its clinical utility.
