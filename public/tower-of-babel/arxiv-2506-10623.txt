1
Introduction

1.1
Model and main result

The standard, one-dimensional Branching Brownian Motion (BBM) is a system of particles on
\mathbb{R}
: at time
t=0
, there is a single particle at the origin. This particle performs a Brownian motion up to an exponential random time with mean one when the particle is replaced by two particles at the same position. Each daughter particle then starts an independent copy of the same process, and so on. This results in an exponentially growing cloud of branching, diffusing, particles
(X_{u}^{1\mathrm{d}}(t),u\in\mathcal{N}^{1\mathrm{d}}(t))_{t\geq 0}
where
X_{u}^{1\mathrm{d}}(t)
is the position of particle
u
at time
t
and
\mathcal{N}^{1\mathrm{d}}(t)
is the set of particles alive at time
t
.
This is a well studied process with an extensive literature, including a particular focus on the study of the maximal displacement at time
t
,

M^{1\mathrm{d}}_{t}\coloneqq\max_{u\in\mathcal{N}^{1\mathrm{d}}(t)}X_{u}^{1\mathrm{d}}(t).

Classical results by
[
20
,
19
,
35
]
determined the asymptotic behavior of
M^{1\mathrm{d}}_{t}
, let
m^{1\mathrm{d}}(t)=\sqrt{2}t-\frac{3}{2\sqrt{2}}\log t
,

\lim_{t\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(M^{1\mathrm{d}}_{t}-m^{1\mathrm{d}}(t)\leq x}}\right)=\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[e^{-cD_{\infty}e^{-\sqrt{2}x}}}}\right],

(1.1)

where
c>0
and
D_{\infty}>0
is the limit of the so-called derivative martingale.
Further results include for example the convergence of the extremal process
[
1
,
4
]
and a precise description of its limit
[
23
,
40
,
7
,
31
]
.
More generally, the behaviour of the maximal displacement for variants of the BBM model has been a topic of intense research in the recent past and we discuss some of these results in the next subsection.

In the present work, we study a spatially inhomogeneous version of BBM in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, where the branching rate of a particle depends on its angular coordinate, with the goal of capturing the effect of the inhomogeneity on the maximal displacement.
Let
b\colon\mathbb{R}\to[0,\infty)
be a
2\pi
-periodic function.
We start with one particle at
(0,0)
at time 0.
Each particle moves according to a 2d Brownian motion and a particle located at a point of polar coordinates
(r,\theta)
splits into two particles at rate
b(\theta)
.
We denote by
\mathcal{N}(t)
the set of particles alive at time
t
and, for
u\in\mathcal{N}(t)
, by
(X_{u}(t),Y_{u}(t))
the position of particle
u
at time
t
.
For
s\leq t
, we also write
(X_{u}(s),Y_{u}(s))
for the position of the ancestor of
u
alive at time
s
.
Finally, we denote by
(R_{u}(t),\theta_{u}(t))
the polar coordinates of
(X_{u}(t),Y_{u}(t))
.

We are interested in the asymptotic behaviour of

M_{t}\coloneqq\max_{u\in\mathcal{N}(t)}R_{u}(t)

in the case where the function
b
reaches its maximum on
[-\pi,\pi]
at a single point, say 0.
More precisely we assume

(A1)

b
is continuous and reaches its maximum on
[-\pi,\pi]
at 0 only.

(A2)

there exist
\alpha\in(2/3,2)
and
\beta>0
such that
b(\theta)=1-\beta\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\theta}}\right\rvert^{\alpha}+O(\theta^{2})
, as
\theta\to 0
,

Instead of Assumption
(A1)
, one can assume the weaker assumption

(A1’)

b\leq 1
and, if
b(\theta_{n})\to 1
for some sequence
(\theta_{n})_{n\geq 0}\in(-\pi,\pi]^{\mathbb{N}}
, then
\theta_{n}\to 0
.

This assumption does not require continuity anymore, but ensures that
b
can approach the value 1 only at 0 (and other multiples of
2\pi
). Note that by Assumption
(A2)
we still have continuity at
0

To state our result, we introduce

\vartheta_{1}\coloneqq\lambda_{0}\cdot\frac{2+\alpha}{2-\alpha}\cdot\frac{\beta^{2/(2+\alpha)}}{2^{2\alpha/(2+\alpha)}},

where
\lambda_{0}>0
is a constant depending only on
\alpha
defined as the smallest eigenvalue of a differential operator in Lemma
2.8

m(t)=\sqrt{2}t-\frac{\vartheta_{1}}{\sqrt{2}}t^{(2-\alpha)/(2+\alpha)}-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{3}{2\sqrt{2}}-\frac{\alpha}{2\sqrt{2}(2+\alpha)}}}\right)\log t.

Theorem 1.1
.

As
t\to\infty
, we have

M_{t}=m(t)+O_{\mathbb{P}}(1).

Equivalently, the process
(M_{t}-m(t))_{t\geq 1}
is tight.

Recalling that the
\theta=0
direction is the one with maximal branching rate, it is reasonable to expect that particles with the maximal displacement are located in that direction.
More precisely, we can deduce the following result from our proof, which states that particles at a distance
m(t)+O(1)
from the origin at time
t
cannot have a too large
Y
-coordinate, so that their displacement is essentially due to the
X
-coordinate.
The first part of this proposition is a direct consequence of Lemmas
5.3
and
5.4

1.1
.

Proposition 1.2
.

For any
\varepsilon>0
and

, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\exists u\in\mathcal{N}(t):R_{u}(t)\geq m(t)-a,\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert Y_{u}(t)}}\right\rvert\geq t^{\frac{\alpha}{2+\alpha}+\varepsilon}}}\right)\xrightarrow[t\to\infty]{}0.

In particular, this implies

M_{t}-\max_{u\in\mathcal{N}(t)}X_{u}(t)\xrightarrow[t\to\infty]{\mathbb{P}}0.

Theorem
1.1
has to be understood in view of equation (
1.1
).
Compare

With our assumption of

This range of

Another difference between Theorem

1.2
Related work

Our result should be compared to other variants of branching Brownian motion where the maximal displacement has been studied.
Firstly, BBM with homogenous branching rate, i.e.
b\equiv 1
, has been studied in dimensions higher than 1.
Convergence in distribution of the maximal displacement after recentering, as well as of the extremal process, has been obtained in a series of works
[
39
,
43
,
34
,
10
]
.
In particular, in dimension 2, the proper recentering is
m^{2\mathrm{d}}(t)=\sqrt{2}t-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\log t
: comparing this to (
1.1
), we see that changing the dimension yields a change in the logarithmic correction.

Space-inhomogeneous BBMs.

Some models with a space-inhomogeneous branching rate have been studied in dimension 1.
In
[
28
]
, Harris and Harris considered a BBM on
\mathbb{R}
, where a particle located at
x
branches at rate
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert x}}\right\rvert^{p}
for some
p\in(0,2]
. They proved that the maximal displacement grows like
t^{2/(2-p)}
if
p<2
, and exponentially fast if
p=2
. Further results on the growth of the population along any path have been obtained in
[
8
,
9
]
.
In
[
41
,
36
]
, the authors studied a BBM with space-inhomogeneous branching and death rate and a drift, which are tuned in a specific fashion to exhibit a Gaussian travelling wave phenomenon.

Another space-inhomogenous version of branching Brownian motion is catalytic BBM. Here particles move as Brownian motions on
\mathbb{R}
and branch at rate
\beta_{0}\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}L_{t}
where
L_{t}
is the local time at
0
of a given particle.
In
[
11
]
, it has been proved that the maximum grows at speed
\beta_{0}/2
a.s. and, in
[
12
]
, that after centering by
(\beta_{0}/2)t
the maximum converges in distribution. Note the absence of logarithmic correction.
Similar results had been obtained previously in
[
21
]
in the case of the catalytic branching random walk.
Finally, in
[
13
]
, a BBM with branching rate
\beta+\beta_{0}\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}L_{t}
is studied and the linear speed is obtained.

Time-inhomogeneous BBMs.

Another very related model is
time-inhomogeneous
(or
speed varying
) branching Brownian motion, and its discrete time counterpart, which has been introduced by Bovier and Kurkova in
[
18
]
. In time-inhomogenous BBM, one starts with a profile
\sigma\colon[0,1]\mapsto[0,\infty)
and a time horizon
T>0
. Particles then branch at rate
1
and move as Brownian motions on
\mathbb{R}
with time-inhomogeneous instantaneous variance
\sigma^{2}(t/T)
at time
t\leq T
.
Note that, up to a deterministic time change, the time-inhomogeneous variance can be transformed into a time-inhomogeneous branching rate.
The linear speed of the maximal displacement at time
T
has been obtained by
[
18
]
in the discrete-time case, for general profiles
\sigma
.

Of particular interest to us here is the case where
\sigma
is strictly decreasing, because in that case the maximal displacement exhibits a polynomial correction of order
T^{1/3}
, as proved by
[
25
]
for the BBM and
[
38
]
for the branching random walk.
Maillard an Zeitouni
[
37
]
proved the tightness of the maximum after centering by
m(T)=v_{\sigma}T-w_{\sigma}T^{1/3}-\frac{\sigma(1)}{\sqrt{2}}\log T
for some explicit constants
v_{\sigma},w_{\sigma}
.
Hence, our space-inhomogeneous model provides another occurrence of such a polynomial slowdown for the maximal displacement. However, two differences can be noticed. First, our model is consistently defined for all time, it does not depend on the time horizon
T
. Second, all exponents in
(0,1)
for the polynomial corrections can be obtained in our model, not only
1/3
: indeed, Theorem
1.1
includes all exponents in
(0,1/2)
but the case
\alpha\in(0,2/3]
should include the exponents in
[1/2,1)
as explained in Section
1.4
.

Other cases for the choice of
\sigma
have been studied, without any polynomial correction appearing in
m(T)
, but various possible coefficients for the logarithmic correction
[
24
,
15
,
16
,
17
,
2
]
.
Similarly, we believe that, for our model in the case
\alpha\geq 2
,
m(t)
should not include a polynomial correction but a logarithmic correction with any coefficient in
(1/\sqrt{2},\infty)
, see Section
1.4
for details.

1.3
Heuristics and structure of the paper

Theorem
1.1
says that the maximal displacement at time
t
is found at a distance of
m(t)+O(1)
from the origin.
In this section, we discuss informally how to arrive at the right polynomial correction in
m(t)
and describe the broad strategy of our proof.

We want to find
m(t)
such that
\mathbb{P}(M_{t}\geq m(t))
is strictly between 1 and 0. By a union bound

\mathbb{P}(M_{t}\geq m(t))\leq\mathbb{E}[\#\{u\in\mathcal{N}(t):R_{u}(t)\geq m(t)\}],

hence we want to find
m(t)
so that this expectation is of order 1.
Note that this union bound is not sharp enough to get the logarithmic correction in
m(t)
, but is sufficient to predict the polynomial term.
By the many-to-one lemma, we have that

\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\#\{u\in\mathcal{N}(t):R_{u}(t)\geq m(t)\}}}\right]=\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\int_{0}^{t}b(\theta_{s})\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}s}}}\right)\mathbbm{1}_{R_{t}\geq m(t)}}}\right],

(1.2)

where
(R_{t},\theta_{t})_{t\geq 0}
are the polar coordinates of a single two-dimensional Brownian motion of coordinates
(X_{t},Y_{t})_{t\geq 0}
(so that
X
and
Y
are two independent standard Brownian motions).

Because
b
reaches its maximum at 0 only, we expect that the maximal displacement will occur in the direction
\theta=0
.
This means that, on the event
\{R_{t}\geq m(t)\}
, we expect that
X_{t}\approx R_{t}\approx\sqrt{2}t
while
Y_{t}=o(t)
.
The most efficient way for
(X,Y)
to achieve this is to have
X_{s}\approx\sqrt{2}s
and
Y_{s}=o(s)
for all
s\in[s_{0},t]
with some large but fixed
s_{0}
,
meaning that the angle
\theta_{s}
can be approximated by
Y_{s}/(\sqrt{2}s)
.
Therefore, the expectation above should be close to the factorised expression

\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\int_{0}^{t}b(\theta_{s})\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}s}}}\right)\mathbbm{1}_{R_{t}\geq m(t)}}}\right]\approx\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\int_{s_{0}}^{t}b\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{Y_{s}}{\sqrt{2}s}}}\right)\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}s}}\right)}}\right]\mathbb{P}\Big(X_{t}\geq m(t)\Big).

(1.3)

Hence, using that
b(\theta)=1-\beta\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\theta}}\right\rvert^{\alpha}+O(\theta^{2})
as
\theta\to 0
by Assumption
(A2)
and neglecting the error term, we should choose
m(t)
so that

e^{t}\cdot\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-\beta\int_{s_{0}}^{t}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\frac{Y_{s}}{\sqrt{2}s}}}\right\rvert^{\alpha}\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}s}}\right)}}\right]\cdot\mathbb{P}\Big(X_{t}>m(t)\Big)\approx 1.

(1.4)

Gaussian tail estimates yield
\mathbb{P}(X_{t}>m(t))=\exp(-m(t)^{2}/2t+O(\log t))
. On the other hand, we claim that

\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-\beta\int_{s_{0}}^{t}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\frac{Y_{s}}{\sqrt{2}s}}}\right|^{\alpha}\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}s}}\right)}}\right]=\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({-ct^{(2-\alpha)/(2+\alpha)}}+O(\log t)}}\right),

(1.5)

for some
c>0
.
With this, (
1.4
) becomes

\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t-ct^{(2-\alpha)/(2+\alpha)}-\frac{m(t)^{2}}{2t}+O(\log t)}}\right)\approx 1,

(1.6)

and, solving for
m(t)
, we obtain
m(t)=\sqrt{2}t-\frac{c}{\sqrt{2}}t^{(2-\alpha)/(2+\alpha)}+O(\log t)
.

Let us argue informally where (
1.5
) comes from. By the Feynman–Kac formula, the expectation in (
1.5
) is connected to solutions at time
r=1-\frac{s_{0}}{t}
of the following PDE on
[0,1)\times\mathbb{R}
:

\partial_{r}u=\varrho\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\partial_{xx}^{2}u-(1-r)^{-\alpha}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert x}}\right\rvert^{\alpha}u}}\right),

(1.7)

where
\varrho=c(\alpha,\beta)t^{(2-\alpha)/(2+\alpha)}
.
The exponential decay of these solutions is governed by the largest eigenvalue of the operator on the right-hand side of (
1.7
), which is proportional to
\varrho
and thus leads to (
1.5
).
The exponent
(2-\alpha)/(2+\alpha)
arises from the scaling properties of
\int_{s_{0}}^{t}\lvert\frac{Y_{s}}{\sqrt{2}s}\rvert^{\alpha}\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}s
. Note our treatment of the time–inhomogenous PDE is inspired in large part by the approach of Maillard and Zeitouni
[
37
]
.

There is another probabilistic way of seeing why
\exp(-ct^{(2-\alpha)/(2+\alpha)})
is the right decay.
The expectation in (
1.5
) is governed by a trade-off between the advantage for
Y
to stay close to zero in order to minimize the integral in the exponential, and the cost of doing so.
We can expect that the optimal strategy is of the following form
A_{\gamma}=\{\forall s\in[s_{0},t],\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert Y_{s}}}\right\rvert\leq s^{\gamma}\}
for some parameter
\gamma\in[0,1/2]
which we need to optimize.
Note that, by the scaling property of Brownian motion, the cost of satisfying the constraint
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert Y_{s}}}\right\rvert\leq s^{\gamma}
is roughly the same on any interval of the form
[r,r+r^{2\gamma}]
for
r
large enough. Moreover, the number of such intervals needed to cover
[s_{0},t]
is proportional to
t^{1-2\gamma}
, so we deduce that
\mathbb{P}(A_{\gamma})\approx\exp(-c_{1}t^{1-2\gamma})
.
On the other hand, on the event
A_{\gamma}
,
we have
\int_{s_{0}}^{t}\lvert\frac{Y_{s}}{\sqrt{2}s}\rvert^{\alpha}\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}s\approx c_{2}t^{[1+\alpha(\gamma-1)]\vee 0}
.
Therefore, we expect that

\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-\beta\int_{s_{0}}^{t}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\frac{Y_{s}}{\sqrt{2}s}}}\right|^{\alpha}\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}s}}\right)}}\right]\approx\sup_{\gamma\in[0,1/2]}\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-c_{1}t^{1-2\gamma}+c_{2}t^{[1+\alpha(\gamma-1)]\vee 0}}}\right)\approx\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-ct^{(2-\alpha)/(2+\alpha)}}}\right),

where the supremum is achieved by
\gamma=\alpha/(2+\alpha)
. While we don’t use this approach in our proofs, this heuristic tells us the scale of
Y_{s}
that should dominate the integral, namely
s^{\alpha/(2+\alpha)}
. This will be reflected in our proofs, especially in Section
3
.

The structure of this paper follows these heuristics. In Section
2
we make the connection between (
1.5
) and the PDE (
1.7
) rigorous, and we show precise estimates on its solutions using Sturm–Liouville theory as well as methods inspired from
[
37
]
.
Then, in Section
3
, in order to incorporate the error term due to the approximation
b(\theta_{s})\approx 1-\lvert\frac{Y_{s}}{\sqrt{2}s}\rvert^{\alpha}
, we deal with more general expectations of the form

\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-\beta\int_{s_{0}}^{t}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\frac{Y_{s}}{\sqrt{2}s}}}\right|^{\alpha}(1+f(Y_{s},s))\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}s}}\right)}}\right],

(1.8)

where
f
is thought of as a small error term.
The PDE approach we use in Section
2
does not allow to incorporate
f
directly, so instead we use probabilistic methods to justify afterwards that the contribution of this error term is negligible.
The main idea is to localize the trajectory of
Y
by showing that, on the event mainly contributing to this expectation,
Y_{s}
stays of order
s^{\alpha/(2+\alpha)}
, so that we can bound the contribution of the error term deterministically and compare with the case
f=0
covered in Section
2
.
We briefly state the many–to–one and many–to–two lemmas in Section
4
, before turning to the proof of Theorem
1.1
.
In Section
5
, we show the upper bound in Theorem
1.1
relying essentially on first moment calculation.
The main difficulty is to localize the trajectory of a particle
u
such that
R_{u}(t)\geq m(t)
, to be able to justify the approximations
\theta_{u}(s)\approx Y_{u}(s)/(\sqrt{2}s)
and
R_{u}(t)\approx X_{u}(t)
and then to add more precise barrier on the
X
-coordinate to determine the logarithmic corrections.
In Section
6
, we prove the lower bound in Theorem
1.1
by performing a first and second moment calculation on the number of particles such that
X_{u}(t)\geq m(t)
, while satisfying appropriate constraints on their trajectories.

1.4
Open questions and remarks

(i)

In Theorem
1.1
we show tightness for
(M_{t}-m(t))_{t\geq 1}
. Can this be extended to convergence in distribution?
For this, one needs a quantity replacing the limit of the derivative martingale in (
1.1
).
The following process should work:

W_{t}\coloneqq t^{-\alpha/[2(2+\alpha)]}\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\vartheta_{1}t^{(2-\alpha)/(2+\alpha)}}}\right)\sum_{u\in\mathcal{N}(t)}(\sqrt{2}t-X_{u}(t))e^{\sqrt{2}(X_{u}(t)-\sqrt{2}t)}\varphi_{0}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\vartheta_{2}Y_{u}(t)}{t^{\alpha/(2+\alpha)}}}}\right),

where
\varphi_{0}
,
\vartheta_{1}
and
\vartheta_{2}
are introduced in Proposition
2.1
.
Note that the dependence in
X_{u}(t)
is similar to the definition of the derivative martingale.
However, here
(W_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is not an exact martingale, but only satisfies
\mathbb{E}[W_{t}|\mathcal{F}_{s}]=W_{s}+o(1)
for
s
and
t
large.
We believe that
(W_{t})_{t\geq 0}
has a limit (in probability at least) and that it plays the role of
D_{\infty}
in the convergence in distribution of
M_{t}-m(t)
.
To go further, we can also ask the question of the convergence of the extremal process.

(ii)

In this work, we focus on the case
\alpha\in(2/3,2)
. We leave the other cases as open questions and briefly discuss here what behaviour we expect for
m(t)
.

(\alpha=2)

This is a natural case as this corresponds most naturally to the assumption that
b
is smooth at
\theta=0
. Furthermore, as
\alpha\nearrow 2
we have
\frac{2-\alpha}{2+\alpha}\to 0
, so we expect no polynomial correction. Here, one can show (for example using the methods of
[
26
]
) that

(\alpha>2)

•

•

Because

•

Lastly, the approximation

(iii)

We finally mention two models that can be seen as a limit as

One of them would be a 2d BBM in which a particle at

In that case, we expect that the linear order of

. Another interesting model might be a catalytic 2d BBM, where a particle branches at rate

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^{2}(1-\tau)}}\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-\frac{(y-x)^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}(1-\tau)}}}\right)\mathbb{E}_{(0,x/\sigma)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-\varrho\int_{0}^{1-\tau}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\frac{\sigma B_{r}}{\tau+r}}}\right\rvert^{\alpha}\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}r}}\right)\middle|\sigma B_{1-\tau}=y}}\right].

Doing a time shift by
\tau
for the Brownian motion in the last expectation, we obtain the following relation

G(s,x;t,y)=\sqrt{\frac{2\varrho}{t}}g\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(0,\sqrt{\frac{2\varrho}{t}}y;1-\frac{s}{t},\sqrt{\frac{2\varrho}{t}}x}}\right).

(2.7)

Then, the result follows from Corollary
2.6
with
\varrho
given above and
T=1-\frac{s}{t}
.
∎

2.2
Associated Sturm–Liouville operators

Fix
\alpha>0
.
An important tool in the study of the PDE (
2.6
) is the following family of Sturm–Liouville operators:
for any
q>0
, let
\mathcal{L}_{q}
be defined by

(\mathcal{L}_{q}f)(x)\coloneqq-f^{\prime\prime}(x)+q\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert x}}\right\rvert^{\alpha}f(x),\quad x\in\mathbb{R},

for any
f
in the domain

D(\mathcal{L}_{q})\coloneqq\{f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}):f,f^{\prime}\text{ absolutely continuous on }\mathbb{R},\mathcal{L}_{q}f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})\}.

These operators are densily defined on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})

[
44
, Lemma 9.4]
and self-adjoint
[
44
, Theorem 9.6]
.
In the case
q=1
, we simply write
\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}_{1}
.

Proposition 2.7
.

Let
\alpha>0
.

(i)

The spectrum of
\mathcal{L}
is discrete and consists of the eigenvalues
0<\lambda_{0}<\lambda_{1}<\lambda_{2}<\cdots
which all have multiplicity 1.
Let
\varphi_{n}
be an eigenfunction associated to
\lambda_{n}
, then it has exactly
n
zeroes, so it can be chosen uniquely such that
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert\varphi_{n}}}\right\rVert_{2}=1
and
\varphi_{n}(x)>0
for any
x
greater than its largest zero.
Moreover,
(\varphi_{n})_{n\geq 0}
is an orthonormal basis of
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
.

(ii)

Let
q>0
. The previous point also holds for
\mathcal{L}_{q}
with eigenvalues
(\lambda_{q,n})_{n\geq 0}
and eigenfunctions
(\varphi_{q,n})_{n\geq 0}
such that, for any
n\geq 0
and
x\in\mathbb{R}
,

\lambda_{q,n}=q^{2/(2+\alpha)}\lambda_{n}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\varphi_{q,n}(x)=q^{1/[2(2+\alpha)]}\varphi_{n}(q^{1/(2+\alpha)}x).

(2.8)

(iii)

Let
c_{\alpha}\coloneqq\frac{2}{\pi}\int_{0}^{1}\sqrt{1-u^{\alpha}}\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}u
. Then, as
n\to\infty
,

\lambda_{n}\sim\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{n}{c_{\alpha}}}}\right)^{2\alpha/(\alpha+2)}.

(2.9)

(iv)

For any
n\geq 0
, the function
\varphi_{n}
has the same parity as
n
.

(v)

There exists
C>0
depending on
\alpha
such that, for any
n\geq 0
and
x\in\mathbb{R}
,

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\varphi_{n}(x)}}\right\rvert\leq C(n+1)^{3}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[1\wedge\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-\frac{1}{2+\alpha}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert x}}\right\rvert^{(2+\alpha)/2}-Cn}}\right)}}\right)}}\right].

(vi)

There exist
C,c>0
depending on
\alpha
such that, for any
x\in\mathbb{R}
,

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\varphi_{0}^{\prime}(x)}}\right\rvert\leq Ce^{-c\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert x}}\right\rvert^{(2+\alpha)/2}}
.

Proof.

Part
(i)
.
The fact that the essential spectrum of
\mathcal{L}
is empty follows from
[
6
, Proposition 4.5.4]
together with the fact that any self-adjoint realization of
-f^{\prime\prime}+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert x}}\right\rvert^{\alpha}f
in
L^{2}((0,\infty))
or
L^{2}((-\infty,0))
has empty essential spectrum by
[
6
, Theorem 4.5.8]
. Then, it follows from
[
6
, Theorem 6.1.9]
that the discrete spectrum is bounded from below so it can be written
\lambda_{0}<\lambda_{1}<\lambda_{2}<\cdots
. Moreover, eigenvalues have multiplicity 1 (this is more clearly stated in
[
46
, Theorem 10.12.1.(8).(ii)]
) and an eigenfunction
\varphi_{n}
associated with
\lambda_{n}
has
n
zeroes.
We now chose
\varphi_{n}
uniquely as in the statement.
The fact that
\lambda_{0}>0
is obtained by contradiction: if
\lambda_{0}\leq 0
, then
\varphi_{0}^{\prime\prime}(x)=(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert x}}\right\rvert^{\alpha}-\lambda_{0})\varphi_{0}(x)\geq 0
for any
x\in\mathbb{R}
(using
\varphi_{0}>0
because it has no zero), so
\varphi_{0}
is convex in
\mathbb{R}
, which contradicts
\varphi_{0}\in L^{2}
.
Finally,
(\varphi_{n})_{n\geq 0}
is an orthonormal basis of
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
by
[
44
, Corollary 4.2.3]
.

Part
(ii)
. This follows from a direct verification.

Part
(iii)
. This is a consequence of
[
6
, Theorem 6.1.12]
, see
[
6
, Example 6.1.13, Part 5]
.

Part
(iv)
. Let
n\geq 0
. Since
\mathcal{L}
preserves parity, the even and the odd parts of
\varphi_{n}
are also eigenfunctions associated to
\lambda_{n}
, so one of them must be zero because
\lambda_{n}
has multiplicity 1. So
\varphi_{n}
is either even or odd.
If
\varphi_{n}
is odd, then it has an odd number of zeroes.
If
\varphi_{n}
is even, then
\varphi_{n}^{\prime}(0)=0
and therefore
\varphi_{n}(0)\neq 0
(otherwise
\varphi_{n}=0
), so
\varphi_{n}
has an even number of zeroes.
But,
\varphi_{n}
has
n
zeroes by Part
(i)
so
\varphi_{n}
has the same parity as
n
.

Part
(v)
. This part relies on some preliminary results established in Lemma
2.8
below. Let
n\geq 0
and
x\geq 0
(by parity the result for
x\leq 0

Let
b\coloneqq\alpha\vee(2\lambda_{n})^{1/\alpha}
.
We consider the function
\psi_{n}
defined on
[b,\infty)
by
\mathcal{L}\psi_{n}=\lambda_{n}\psi_{n}
together with the initial conditions

\psi_{n}(b)=1
and

\psi_{n}^{\prime}(b)=\frac{1}{2}b^{\alpha/2}
.
Then, the Wronskian

W\coloneqq\varphi_{n}\psi_{n}^{\prime}-\varphi_{n}^{\prime}\psi_{n},

is a constant.
Moreover, by Lemma
2.8
.
(i)
,
\varphi_{n}>0
and
\varphi_{n}^{\prime}<0
in
[b,\infty)
and it follows from a similar argument that
\psi_{n}
is convex in
[b,\infty)
and therefore
\psi_{n}^{\prime}>0
and
\psi_{n}\geq 0
.
Therefore, for any
x\geq b
,

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\varphi_{n}(x)}}\right\rvert=\frac{W+\varphi_{n}^{\prime}(x)\psi_{n}(x)}{\psi_{n}^{\prime}(x)}\leq\frac{W}{\psi_{n}^{\prime}(x)}.

(2.10)

We first bound
W
by taking its value at
b
: we have
W=\frac{1}{2}b^{\alpha/2}\varphi_{n}(b)-\varphi_{n}^{\prime}(b)
.
Moreover, using Lemma
2.8
.
(i)
,

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\varphi_{n}^{\prime}(b)}}\right\rvert\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\varphi_{n}^{\prime}(\lambda_{n}^{1/\alpha})}}\right\rvert\leq\int_{y_{0}}^{\lambda_{n}^{1/\alpha}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\varphi_{n}^{\prime\prime}(x)}}\right\rvert\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}x,

where
y_{0}
is a zero of
\varphi_{n}^{\prime}
in
[0,\lambda_{n}^{1/\alpha}]
(one can see it exists using that either
\varphi_{n}^{\prime}(0)=0
or
\varphi_{n}(0)=0
by Part
(iv)
).
Then, using
\varphi_{n}^{\prime\prime}(x)=(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert x}}\right\rvert^{\alpha}-\lambda_{n})\varphi_{n}(x)
, we get

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\varphi_{n}^{\prime}(b)}}\right\rvert\leq\int_{y_{0}}^{\lambda_{n}^{1/\alpha}}\lambda_{n}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\varphi_{n}(x)}}\right\rvert\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}x\leq C\lambda_{n}^{(\alpha+1)/\alpha}(n+1)^{\alpha/[2(2+\alpha)]},

applying Lemma
2.8
.
(iii)
.
On the other hand,
b^{\alpha/2}\varphi_{n}(b)\leq C\lambda_{n}^{1/2}(n+1)^{\alpha/[2(2+\alpha)]}
by Lemma
2.8
.
(iii)
again.
Hence, we get

W\leq C\lambda_{n}^{(\alpha+1)/\alpha}(n+1)^{\alpha/[2(2+\alpha)]}\leq C(n+1)^{3},

(2.11)

using (
2.9
).
Now, we aim at proving a lower bound for
\psi_{n}^{\prime}
.
For this, we introduce the following function

g(x)\coloneqq\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{2+\alpha}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x^{(2+\alpha)/2}-b^{(2+\alpha)/2}}}\right)}}\right),\qquad x\geq b.

Then,
g^{\prime}(x)=\frac{1}{2}x^{\alpha/2}g(x)

and
g^{\prime\prime}(x)=(\frac{1}{4}x^{\alpha}+\frac{\alpha}{4}x^{(\alpha-2)/2})g(x)
.
In particular, for any
x\geq b

g^{\prime\prime}(x)\leq\frac{1}{2}x^{\alpha}g(x)\leq(x^{\alpha}-\lambda_{n})g(x)
, where the first inequality uses

and the second one

On the other hand,
g(b)=\psi_{n}(b)
and
g^{\prime}(b)=\psi_{n}^{\prime}(b)

Hence, we get
g\leq\psi_{n}
and
g^{\prime}\leq\psi_{n}^{\prime}
on

. Combining this with (

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\varphi_{n}(x)}}\right\rvert\leq C(n+1)^{3}x^{-\alpha/2}\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-\frac{1}{2+\alpha}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x^{(2+\alpha)/2}-b^{(2+\alpha)/2}}}\right)}}\right).

The result follows by noting that
b^{(2+\alpha)/2}\leq Cn
by (

) and using that we already now that
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert\varphi_{n}}}\right\rVert_{\infty}\leq C(n+1)^{3}
by Lemma
2.8
.
(iii)
.

Part
(vi)
. By Part
(v)
, we have
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\varphi_{0}(x)}}\right\rvert\leq Ce^{-c\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert x}}\right\rvert^{(2+\alpha)/2}}
and so
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\varphi_{0}^{\prime\prime}(x)}}\right\rvert\leq C\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert x}}\right\rvert^{\alpha}e^{-c\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert x}}\right\rvert^{(2+\alpha)/2}}
using
\lambda_{0}>0
.
Then, for
x\geq 0
, we have
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\varphi^{\prime}(x)}}\right\rvert\leq\int_{x}^{\infty}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\varphi_{0}^{\prime\prime}(y)}}\right\rvert\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}y
by Lemma
2.8
.
(ii)
and the result follows.
∎

Lemma 2.8
.

(i)

For any
n\geq 0
,
\varphi_{n}
is convex and decreasing in
[\lambda_{n}^{1/\alpha},\infty)
;

(ii)

For any
n\geq 0
,
\varphi_{n}
and
\varphi_{n}^{\prime}
tend to zero at infinity;

(iii)

There exists
C=C(\alpha)>0
such that, for any
n\geq 0
,
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert\varphi_{n}}}\right\rVert_{\infty}\leq C(n+1)^{\alpha/[2(2+\alpha)]}
.

Proof.

Part
(i)
. Note that in
[\lambda_{n}^{1/\alpha},\infty)
,
\varphi_{n}^{\prime\prime}
and
\varphi_{n}
have the same sign (considering zero has both signs).
Therefore,
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\varphi_{n}}}\right\rvert
is convex in
[\lambda_{n}^{1/\alpha},\infty)
.
By contradiction, assume
\varphi_{n}
has a zero
x_{0}
in
[\lambda_{n}^{1/\alpha},\infty)
. Then,
\varphi_{n}^{\prime}(x_{0})\neq 0
(otherwise
\varphi_{n}=0
).
Then, convexity of
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\varphi_{n}}}\right\rvert
implies that
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\varphi_{n}}}\right\rvert\to\infty
, which contradicts
\varphi_{n}\in L^{2}
.
Therefore,
\varphi_{n}
has no zero in
[\lambda_{n}^{1/\alpha},\infty)
and hence it is positive (by our choice of
\varphi_{n}
) and so it is convex.
If
\varphi_{n}^{\prime}
takes a nonnegative value at
x_{1}\in[\lambda_{n}^{1/\alpha},\infty)
, then
\varphi_{n}(x)\geq\varphi_{n}(x_{1})
for all
x\geq x_{1}
and this contradicts
\varphi_{n}\in L^{2}
.
So
\varphi_{n}^{\prime}<0
and
\varphi_{n}
is decreasing in
[\lambda_{n}^{1/\alpha},\infty)
.

Part
(ii)
. This is a consequence of Part
(i)
, using again that
\varphi_{n}\in L^{2}
.

Part
(iii)
.
The idea is to argue that, if
\varphi_{n}
takes a large value at some point, then it creates some mass proportional to this value to its
L^{2}
norm, which is fixed to 1, hence providing an upper bound for this value.
Since
\varphi_{n}
is continuous and vanishes at infinity by Part
(ii)
, there exists
y\in\mathbb{R}
such that
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\varphi_{n}(y)}}\right\rvert=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert\varphi_{n}}}\right\rVert_{\infty}
and we can assume
y\geq 0
by parity of
\varphi_{n}
. Then,
\varphi_{n}^{\prime}(y)=0
so, for
t\geq 0
,

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\varphi_{n}(y+t)}}\right\rvert\geq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\varphi_{n}(y)}}\right\rvert-\frac{t^{2}}{2}\sup_{x\in[y,y+t]}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\varphi_{n}^{\prime\prime}(x)}}\right\rvert.

Then, on the one hand, we have
y\leq\lambda_{n}^{1/\alpha}
as a consequence of Part
(i)
.
On the other hand, for
x\in[0,(2\lambda_{n})^{1/\alpha}]
,
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\varphi^{\prime\prime}(x)}}\right\rvert\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert x^{\alpha}-\lambda_{n}}}\right\rvert\cdot\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\varphi_{n}(x)}}\right\rvert\leq\lambda_{n}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert\varphi_{n}}}\right\rVert_{\infty}
.
Therefore, for any
t\in[0,\lambda_{n}^{1/\alpha}]
,

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\varphi_{n}(y+t)}}\right\rvert\geq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert\varphi_{n}}}\right\rVert_{\infty}-\frac{t^{2}}{2}\lambda_{n}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert\varphi_{n}}}\right\rVert_{\infty}.

There is a constant
c\in(0,1)
depending only on
\alpha
such that
c\lambda_{n}^{-1/2}\leq\lambda_{n}^{1/\alpha}
for any
n\geq 0
.
Then, we write

1=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert\varphi_{n}}}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\geq\int_{0}^{c\lambda_{n}^{-1/2}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\varphi_{n}(y+t)}}\right\rvert^{2}\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}t\geq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert\varphi_{n}}}\right\rVert_{\infty}^{2}\int_{0}^{c\lambda_{n}^{-1/2}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\frac{t^{2}}{2}\lambda_{n}}}\right)\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}t=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert\varphi_{n}}}\right\rVert_{\infty}^{2}\lambda_{n}^{-1/2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(c-\frac{c^{3}}{6}}}\right),

and therefore
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert\varphi_{n}}}\right\rVert_{\infty}\leq C\lambda_{n}^{1/4}
and the result follows from (
2.9
).
∎

2.3
Proof of Proposition

concerning the fundamental solution estimate for the PDE (

We follow ideas from
[

, which study the case
\alpha=1
and where the function

is replaced by a
\mathcal{C}^{1}
function on

For

for some

(ii)

2.7
Technical bound on a series

Lemma 2.14
.

For any
\kappa\in(0,1)
and
a_{1},a_{2},a_{3},a_{4}>0
, there exists
C,c>0
such that, for any
u\geq 1
and

,

\sum_{n\geq 1}n^{a_{4}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1\wedge e^{-a_{1}(v-a_{2}n)}}}\right)e^{-a_{3}un^{\kappa}}\leq Ce^{-cu}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(e^{-cv}+e^{-cuv^{\kappa}}}}\right).

Proof.

In this proof, constants
C
and
c
can depend on
\kappa,a_{1},a_{2},a_{3},a_{4}
.
First note that, bounding
e^{-a_{3}un^{\kappa}}\leq e^{-a_{3}u/2}\cdot e^{-a_{3}un^{\kappa}/2}
, we can get a factor
e^{-a_{3}u/2}
in front of the series and therefore, it is enough to prove

\sum_{n\geq 1}n^{a_{4}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1\wedge e^{-a_{1}(v-a_{2}n)}}}\right)e^{-a_{3}un^{\kappa}}\leq C\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(e^{-cv}+e^{-cuv^{\kappa}}}}\right).

(2.33)

Letting
n_{0}\coloneqq\lceil v/a_{2}\vee 2\rceil
, we split the series into a part
n<n_{0}
and a part
n\geq n_{0}
.

The part
n\geq n_{0}
equals

\sum_{n\geq n_{0}}n^{a_{4}}e^{-a_{3}un^{\kappa}}\leq\int_{n_{0}-1}^{\infty}(x+1)^{a_{4}}e^{-a_{3}ux^{\kappa}}\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}x\leq Cu^{-a_{4}/\kappa}\int_{u(n_{0}-1)^{\kappa}}^{\infty}y^{a_{4}/\kappa-1}e^{-a_{3}y}\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}y,

(2.34)

bounding
x+1\leq 2x
because
x\geq n_{0}-1\geq 1
and changing variables with
y=ux^{\kappa}
.
Noting that
u^{-a_{4}/\kappa}\leq 1
together with
y^{a_{4}/\kappa-1}e^{-a_{3}y}\leq Ce^{-a_{3}y/2}
and
(n_{0}-1)^{\kappa}\geq(v/2a_{2})^{\kappa}
, this shows the right-hand side of (
2.34
) is at most
Ce^{-cuv^{\kappa}}
.

We now consider the part
n<n_{0}
.
If
v/a_{2}<2
, then this part of sum is smaller than
1
and therefore than
Ce^{-v}
with
C=e^{2a_{2}}
.
We now consider the case

.
Then, this part equals

\sum_{n=1}^{n_{0}-1}n^{a_{4}}e^{-a_{1}(v-a_{2}n)}e^{-a_{3}un^{\kappa}}\leq(v/a_{2})^{a_{4}+1}e^{-a_{1}v}\max_{x\in[1,v/a_{2}]}e^{a_{1}a_{2}x-a_{3}ux^{\kappa}},

(2.35)

where we bounded
n_{0}-1\leq v/a_{2}
, which holds because
v/a_{2}\geq 2
so
n_{0}=\lceil v/a_{2}\rceil
.
Now, note that the function

This shows that the right-hand side of (

C(v/a_{2})^{a_{4}+1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(e^{-a_{1}v}e^{-a_{3}u}+e^{-a_{3}u(v/a_{2})^{\kappa}}}}\right)\leq C\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(e^{-cv}+e^{-cuv^{\kappa}}}}\right),

using here again that

to argue that

∎

3
Brownian motion weighted by an integral via probability

In this section, we continue studying the heat kernel of Brownian motion weighted by an integral, relying now on probabilistic arguments.
Our two main goals are to generalise estimates of the previous section to cases including error terms in the integral weight and to obtain sharper bounds on the tail of the kernel.

As in the previous section, we fix some parameters
\alpha\in(0,2)
and
\beta>0
.
For
f\colon\mathbb{R}\times(0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
measurable, we define the kernel
\widetilde{G}
for
x,y\in\mathbb{R}
and
0\leq s<t
by

\widetilde{G}(s,x;t,y)=\frac{e^{-(y-x)^{2}/[2(t-s)]}}{\sqrt{2\pi(t-s)}}\mathbb{E}_{(s,x)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-\beta\int_{s}^{t}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\frac{B_{r}}{\sqrt{2}r}}}\right\rvert^{\alpha}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+f(B_{r},r)}}\right)\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}r}}\right)\middle|B_{t}=y}}\right].

(3.1)

The contribution of
f
has to be thought as an error term.
More precisely, for some
L,a,b>0
, we work under the assumption
f^{-}_{L,a,b}\leq f\leq f^{+}_{L,a,b}
, where we set

f^{+}_{L,a,b}(y,r)=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[L\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\frac{y}{r}}}\right\rvert^{a}+r^{-b}}}\right)}}\right]\wedge 1\quad\text{and}\quad f^{-}_{L,a,b}(y,r)=-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[L\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\frac{y}{r}}}\right\rvert^{a}+r^{-b}}}\right)}}\right]\wedge\eta}}\right),

(3.2)

where
\eta=\eta(L,a,b,\alpha)
is chosen as the largest possible real number in
(0,1/2]
such that, for any
r\geq r_{0}:=(2L)^{1/b}
, the function
y\in[0,\infty)\mapsto(y/r)^{\alpha}(1+f^{-}_{L,a,b}(y,r))
is non-decreasing.
To see that such an
\eta
necessarily exist, use that
Lr^{-b}\leq 1/2
and note that the function
u\in[0,u_{0}]\mapsto u^{\alpha}(\frac{1}{2}-Lu^{a})
is non-decreasing if
u_{0}
is chosen small enough depending on
\alpha,a,L
.
Finally, note that we write
f^{-}_{L,a,b}
even if this function actually also depends on
\alpha
through
\eta
.

3.1
Comparing
\widetilde{G}
to
G

Recall from Section
2
that, around time
t
, the natural time scale of
G
is
t^{\kappa}
and the natural space scale is
t^{\kappa/2}
, where
\kappa\coloneqq 2\alpha/(2+\alpha)\in(0,1)
.
The first result of this section establishes that, if
a
and
b
are large enough in terms of
\alpha
, then
\widetilde{G}
and
G
are of the same order as long as we are on a time scale longer than the natural one and on a space scale not much longer than the natural one.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
\alpha\in(0,2)
and
\beta>0
.
Let
L>0
,
a>(1-\kappa)/(1-\frac{\kappa}{2})=1-\alpha/2
and
b>1-\kappa
.
Then, there exists
\varepsilon_{0}=\varepsilon_{0}(\alpha,a,b)>0
such that for all
\varepsilon\in(0,\varepsilon_{0})
, there are
C,c>0
such that for
s
large enough, for any
t\geq s+s^{\kappa}
, any
x,y\in\mathbb{R}
with
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert x}}\right\rvert\leq s^{(\kappa+\varepsilon)/2}
and
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert y}}\right\rvert\leq t^{(\kappa+\varepsilon)/2}
and any function
f
satisfying
f^{-}_{L,a,b}\leq f\leq f^{+}_{L,a,b}
, we have

\widetilde{G}(s,x;t,y)=(1+o(1))G(s,x;t,y),

where the
o(1)
holds as
s\to\infty
, uniformly in
t,x,y,f
(but depending on
\alpha,\beta,L,a,b,\varepsilon
).

3.1.1
Preliminary results

We prove here two preliminary lemmas. The first one gives some rough estimates on the natural time scale.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
\alpha\in(0,2)
and
\beta>0
.

C,c>0
such that, for any
s\geq 1
,

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert x}}\right\rvert\leq Ms^{\kappa/2}

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert y}}\right\rvert\leq Mt^{\kappa/2}

(ii)

If

, then, for any

and

Two typical tools from the study of branching processes are the many–to–one and many–to–two lemmas. They allow us to reduce the certain expectations of the branching process to expectations of just one or two particles.

Lemma 4.1

(many–to–one)

Let

t

\mathbb{R}^{2}

For a proof see
[
27

\{\forall s\in[s_{0},t],X_{s}\leq\sqrt{2}s\text{ and }\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert Y_{s}}}\right\rvert<\sigma s\}
, we have

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}b(\theta_{s})\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}s\leq t-\beta\int_{s_{0}}^{t}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\frac{Y_{s}}{\sqrt{2}s}}}\right\rvert^{\alpha}(1+f(Y_{s},s))\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}s,

where
f=f^{-}_{L,2-\alpha,1}=-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[L\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\frac{y}{s}}}\right\rvert^{2-\alpha}+s^{-1}}}\right)}}\right]\wedge\eta}}\right)
where
\eta\in(0,1/2]
is chosen in (

).

Note that the parameters
a=2-\alpha
and
b=1
appearing here in

satisfy the assumptions
a>1-\frac{\alpha}{2}
and
b>1-\kappa
of the propositions of Section
3
.

Proof.

By Assumption
(A2)
,
b(\theta)=1-\beta\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\theta}}\right\rvert^{\alpha}+O(\theta^{2})
as
\theta\to 0
, so there exist
K>0
and
\sigma_{0}\in(0,\pi/2)
such that, for any
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\theta}}\right\rvert\leq\sigma_{0}
,
b(\theta)\leq 1-\beta\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\theta}}\right\rvert^{\alpha}+K\theta^{2}
.
Moreover, one can choose
\sigma_{0}
small enough such that the function
\theta\in[0,\sigma_{0}]\mapsto 1-\beta\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\theta}}\right\rvert^{\alpha}+K\theta^{2}
is decreasing.
On the other hand, by Assumption
(A1)
or
(A1’)
,
\sup_{[-\pi,\pi]\setminus[-\sigma_{0},\sigma_{0}]}b<1
. Hence, choosing
\sigma_{0}

b(\theta)\leq\overline{b}(\theta)\coloneqq\begin{cases}1-\beta\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\theta}}\right\rvert^{\alpha}+K\theta^{2},&\text{if }\theta\in[-\sigma_{0},\sigma_{0}],\\
1-\beta\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\sigma_{0}}}\right\rvert^{\alpha}+K\sigma_{0}^{2},&\text{if }\theta\in[-\pi,\pi]\setminus[-\sigma_{0},\sigma_{0}],\end{cases}

and the function
\overline{b}
is non-increasing on
[0,\pi]
.

We now work on
E_{1}\coloneqq\{\forall s\in[s_{0},t],X_{s}\leq\sqrt{2}s\text{ and }\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\theta_{s}}}\right\rvert<\sigma\}
or
E_{2}\coloneqq\{\forall s\in[s_{0},t],X_{s}\leq\sqrt{2}s\text{ and }\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert Y_{s}}}\right\rvert<\sigma s\}
.
Then, for any
s\in[s_{0},t]
such that
\theta_{s}\in[0,\pi/2)
, we have

\theta_{s}=\arctan\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{Y_{s}}{X_{s}}}}\right)\geq\arctan\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{Y_{s}}{\sqrt{2}s}}}\right)\geq\frac{Y_{s}}{\sqrt{2}s}-\frac{1}{3}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{Y_{s}}{\sqrt{2}s}}}\right)^{3},

and therefore

b(\theta_{s})\leq\overline{b}(\theta_{s})\leq\overline{b}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{Y_{s}}{\sqrt{2}s}-\frac{1}{3}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{Y_{s}}{\sqrt{2}s}}}\right)^{3}}}\right).

Then, one can choose
\sigma
small enough such that the last argument of
\overline{b}
necessarily belongs to
[0,\sigma_{0}]
both on
E_{1}
and on
E_{2}
, and therefore we get, for some
L=L(K,\sigma_{0},\alpha,\beta)>0
,

b(\theta_{s})\leq 1-\beta\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\frac{Y_{s}}{\sqrt{2}s}}}\right\rvert^{\alpha}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-L\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\frac{Y_{s}}{s}}}\right\rvert^{2-\alpha}}}\right),

(5.4)

and the same holds if
\theta_{s}\in(-\pi/2,0]
by the same argument.
On
E_{1}
, it is enough to consider the case
\theta_{s}\in(-\pi/2,\pi/2)
by choosing
\sigma<\pi/2
.
On
E_{2}
, we could also have
\theta_{s}\in[-\pi,\pi]\setminus(-\pi/2,\pi/2)
, but then

b(\theta_{s})\leq 1-\beta\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\sigma_{0}}}\right\rvert^{\alpha}+K\sigma_{0}^{2}
and the inequality (
5.4
) stays true if
\sigma
is chosen small enough.
Finally,
\sigma
can be chosen small enough such that, on
E_{1}
or on
E_{2}
,
L\lvert Y_{s}/s\rvert^{2-\alpha}\leq\eta
, where
\eta
appears in the definition of
f^{-}_{L,2-\alpha,1}
in (

), so that (
5.4
) becomes

b(\theta_{s})\leq 1-\beta\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\frac{Y_{s}}{\sqrt{2}s}}}\right\rvert^{\alpha}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+f^{-}_{L,2-\alpha,1}(Y_{s},s)}}\right),

Then, the result is obtained by integrating this inequality for
s\in[s_{0},t]
and by using
b\leq 1
for
s\in[0,s_{0}]
.
∎

5.2
The pseudo-derivative martingale

For
t\geq 0
, let

Z_{t}\coloneqq t^{-\kappa/4}\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\vartheta_{1}t^{1-\kappa}}}\right)\sum_{u\in\mathcal{N}(t)}(\sqrt{2}t-X_{u}(t))e^{\sqrt{2}(X_{u}(t)-\sqrt{2}t)}.

(5.5)

This quantity naturally appears in the upper bound argument when computing conditional first moment give
\mathcal{F}_{t}
, so we need to bound its first moment.
Note that this is not a martingale, but it is defined analogously to the derivative martingale for the standard BBM introduced by Lalley and Sellke
[
35
]
.
The goal of this section is to prove the following result.

Lemma 5.2
.

For any
s_{0}>0
, there exists
C>0
such that, for any
t\geq s_{0}
,

\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[Z_{t}\mathbbm{1}_{A_{s_{0},t}}}}\right]\leq C.

Proof.

By the many–to–one lemma (Lemma
4.1
),

\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[Z_{t}\mathbbm{1}_{A_{s_{0},t}}}}\right]\leq t^{-\kappa/4}\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\vartheta_{1}t^{1-\kappa}}}\right)\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\int_{0}^{t}b(\theta_{s})\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}s}}\right)(\sqrt{2}t-X_{t})e^{\sqrt{2}X_{t}-2t}\mathbbm{1}_{\forall s\in[s_{0},t],X_{s}\leq\sqrt{2}s}}}\right].

Let
\sigma>0
be the constant given by Lemma
5.1
.
Define the events
E\coloneqq\{\forall s\in[s_{0},t],\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert Y_{s}}}\right\rvert\leq\sigma s\}
and, for
k\geq 1
,

F_{k}\coloneqq\{\exists s\in[2^{k-1}s_{0},2^{k}s_{0}):\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert Y_{s}}}\right\rvert>\sigma s\}\cap\{\forall s\in[2^{k}s_{0},t]:\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert Y_{s}}}\right\rvert\leq\sigma s\}.

Let
k_{t}=\min\{k\geq 1:2^{k}s_{0}\geq t\}
. We have
E^{c}\subset\bigcup_{k=1}^{k_{t}}F_{k}
, so we use the bound
1\leq\mathbbm{1}_{E}+\sum_{k=1}^{k_{t}}\mathbbm{1}_{F_{k}}
.

Using Lemma
5.1
, the part on event
E
is at most

\displaystyle t^{-\kappa/4}\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\vartheta_{1}t^{1-\kappa}}}\right)\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-\beta\int_{s_{0}}^{t}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\frac{Y_{s}}{\sqrt{2}s}}}\right\rvert^{\alpha}(1+f(Y_{s},s))\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}s}}\right)(\sqrt{2}t-X_{t})e^{\sqrt{2}X_{t}-t}\mathbbm{1}_{\forall s\in[s_{0},t],X_{s}\leq\sqrt{2}s}}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq Cs_{0}^{-\kappa/4}\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\vartheta_{1}s_{0}^{1-\kappa}}}\right)\times\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[(\sqrt{2}s_{0}-X_{s_{0}})e^{\sqrt{2}X_{s_{0}}-s_{0}}}}\right],

(5.6)

using independence between
X
and
Y
, Proposition
3.5
.
(i)
for the
Y
-contribution and the fact that
((\sqrt{2}t-X_{t})e^{\sqrt{2}X_{t}-t}\mathbbm{1}_{\forall s\in[s_{0},t],X_{s}\leq\sqrt{2}s})_{t\geq s_{0}}
is a martingale.
The right-hand side of (
5.6
) is a constant depending on
s_{0}
, so it concludes this part.

We now bound the part on event
F_{k}
for some
1\leq k\leq k_{t}
.
Using Lemma
5.1
between times
2^{k}s_{0}
and
t
, this part is at most

\displaystyle t^{-\kappa/4}\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\vartheta_{1}t^{1-\kappa}}}\right)\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-\beta\int_{2^{k}s_{0}}^{t}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\frac{Y_{s}}{\sqrt{2}s}}}\right\rvert^{\alpha}(1+f(Y_{s},s))\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}s}}\right)\mathbbm{1}_{\exists s\in[2^{k-1}s_{0},2^{k}s_{0}):\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert Y_{s}}}\right\rvert>\sigma s}}}\right]

\displaystyle\quad{}\times\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[(\sqrt{2}t-X_{t})e^{\sqrt{2}X_{t}-t}\mathbbm{1}_{\forall s\in[s_{0},t],X_{s}\leq\sqrt{2}s}}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq C(s_{0})(2^{k}s_{0})^{-\kappa/4}\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\vartheta_{1}(2^{k}s_{0})^{1-\kappa}}}\right)\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\exists s\in[2^{k-1}s_{0},2^{k}s_{0}):\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert Y_{s}}}\right\rvert>\sigma s}}\right),

(5.7)

proceeding as in (
5.6
).
Then, this last probability can be bounded by

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\max_{s\in[0,2^{k}s_{0}]}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert Y_{s}}}\right\rvert>\sigma 2^{k-1}s_{0}}}\right)\leq 2\cdot\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\max_{s\in[0,2^{k}s_{0}]}Y_{s}>\sigma 2^{k-1}s_{0}}}\right)=2\cdot\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert Y_{2^{k}s_{0}}}}\right\rvert>\sigma 2^{k-1}s_{0}}}\right),

(5.8)

using that
\max_{s\in[0,T]}Y_{s}
has the same distribution as
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert Y_{T}}}\right\rvert
for any
T>0
. Together with the tail bound
\mathbb{P}(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert Y_{T}}}\right\rvert\geq x)\leq 2e^{-x^{2}/(2T)}
for
x,T>0
, we get that the right-hand side of (
5.7
) is at most
C(s_{0})\exp(\vartheta_{1}(2^{k}s_{0})^{1-\kappa}-\frac{\sigma^{2}}{8}2^{k}s_{0})
.
Summing over
k\geq 0
, this is bounded by
C(s_{0})
, so it concludes the proof.
∎

5.3
Localization of the trajectory of an extremal particle

In this section, we prove several properties of the trajectory of an extremal particle, that is a particle
u\in\mathcal{N}(t)
such that
R_{u}(t)\geq m(t)+O(1)
. In this first lemma, we show that such a particle typically has a small angle after time
t/2
. This allows us afterwards to restrict ourselves to angles smaller than
\sigma
given by Lemma
5.1
, so that we can bound the branching rate.

Lemma 5.3
.

For any
\eta>0
, there exists
c>0
such that, for any
a\geq 0
, for
t
large enough,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\exists u\in\mathcal{N}(t):R_{u}(t)\geq m(t)-a,\max_{s\in[t/2,t]}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\theta_{u}(s)}}\right\rvert>\eta}}\right)\leq e^{-ct}.

Proof.

Before diving into the proof, we recall some facts on the polar coordinates
(R_{s},\theta_{s})_{s\geq 0}
of the planar Brownian motion.
Firstly,
(R_{s})_{s\geq 0}
is a 2-dimensional Bessel process starting from
r=(x^{2}+y^{2})^{1/2}
and its density at time
s>0
is given by (see
[
14
, Eq. 4.1.0.6]
)

\mathbb{P}_{(x,y)}(R_{s}\in\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}z)=\frac{z}{s}e^{-(r^{2}+z^{2})/2s}I_{0}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{rz}{s}}}\right)\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}z,

(5.9)

where
I_{0}
is the modified Bessel function defined by
I_{0}(z)=\sum_{k\geq 0}(z/2)^{2k}/(k!)^{2}
.
Note that, for
z\geq 0
,
I_{0}(z)\leq(e^{z/2})^{2}=e^{z}
, so we get the following upper bound for the density of
R_{s}
:

\mathbb{P}_{(x,y)}(R_{s}\in\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}z)\leq\frac{z}{s}e^{-(z-r)^{2}/2s}\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}z.

(5.10)

Concerning the angular coordinate
(\theta_{s})_{s\geq 0}
, note that the representation can be chosen to be continuous, except at
s=0
if the Brownian motion starts from the origin.
Moreover, we have the following skew-product representation: for any
s\geq 0
(
s>0
if the Brownian motion starts from the origin), there exists a Brownian motion
W
on
\mathbb{R}
starting from 0 at time 0 and independent of
R
such that

\forall t\geq s,\quad\theta_{t}=\theta_{s}+W_{\tau(t)},\qquad\text{with }\tau(t)\coloneqq\int_{s}^{t}R_{q}^{-2}\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}q

(5.11)

see e.g.
[
32
, Corollary 19.7]
.
We now split the proof into three steps.

Step 1.
We first prove that an extremal particle needs to have a large radial coordinate on
[t/2,t]
, more precisely: for any
a\geq 0
, for
t
large enough,

\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\exists u\in\mathcal{N}(t):R_{u}(t)\geq m(t)-a,\min_{s\in[t/2,t]}R_{u}(s)<\frac{\sqrt{2}}{4}t}}\right)\leq e^{-ct}.

(5.12)

As in the statement of the lemma, the constant
c
throughout the proof does not depend on
a
, but the threshold involved in the “
t
large enough” statement can.
By the many–to–one lemma (Lemma
4.1
) with the crude bound
b(\theta_{s})\leq 1
, we can bound the probability in (
5.12
) by

e^{t}\cdot\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(R_{t}\geq m(t)-a,\min_{s\in[t/2,t]}R_{s}<\frac{\sqrt{2}}{4}t}}\right)=e^{t}\cdot\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\phi(T_{0})\mathbbm{1}_{T_{0}<t}}}\right],

(5.13)

using Markov property at the stopping time
T_{0}\coloneqq\inf\{s\geq t/2:R_{s}\leq\sqrt{2}t/4\}

and setting
\phi(s)\coloneqq\mathbb{P}_{\sqrt{2}t/4}(R_{t-s}\geq m(t)-a)
for
s\in[t/2,t)
, where
(R_{q})_{q\geq 0}
starts from
\sqrt{2}t/4
at time 0 under
\mathbb{P}_{\sqrt{2}t/4}
.
Using the upper bound (
5.10
), we get, for
t
large enough and uniformly in
s\in[t/2,t)
,

\displaystyle\phi(s)\leq\int_{m(t)-a}^{\infty}\frac{z}{t-s}e^{-(z-\sqrt{2}t/4)^{2}/2(t-s)}\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}z\leq\frac{Ct}{\sqrt{t-s}}e^{-(m(t)-a-\sqrt{2}t/4)^{2}/(t-s)}\leq e^{-9t/8+o(t)}.

Coming back to (
5.13
), this proves (
5.12
).

Step 2.
We now prove that the angle of an extremal particle cannot be far from 0 on the whole interval
[t/2,t]
, that is, for any
a\geq 0
, for
t
large enough,

\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\exists u\in\mathcal{N}(t):R_{u}(t)\geq m(t)-a,\min_{s\in[t/2,t]}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\theta_{u}(s)}}\right\rvert>\frac{\eta}{2}}}\right)\leq e^{-ct}.

(5.14)

By Assumption
(A1)
or
(A1’)
, we have
c(\eta)\coloneqq\sup_{[-\pi,\pi]\setminus[-\eta/2,\eta/2]}b<1
.
Therefore, using the many–to–one lemma and bounding
b(\theta_{s})
by
c(\eta)
for
s\in[t/2,t]
and by 1 otherwise, we get that the probability in (
5.14
) is at most

\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{t}{2}+\frac{t}{2}c(\eta)}}\right)\cdot\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(R_{t}\geq m(t)-a}}\right)=\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{t}{2}(c(\eta)-1)+o(t)}}\right),

using that
\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(R_{t}\geq m(t)-a}}\right)=e^{-t+o(t)}
as a consequence of (
5.10
) and standard Gaussian bounds.
This proves (
5.14
).

Step 3.
Finally, it is enough to prove that, for any
a\geq 0
, for
t
large enough,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\exists u\in\mathcal{N}(t):R_{u}(t)\geq m(t)-a,\min_{s\in[t/2,t]}R_{u}(s)\geq\frac{\sqrt{2}}{4}t,\min_{s\in[t/2,t]}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\theta_{u}(s)}}\right\rvert\leq\frac{\eta}{2},\max_{s\in[t/2,t]}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\theta_{u}(s)}}\right\rvert>\eta}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq e^{-ct}.

(5.15)

The key idea is that this event requires an angular displacement of at least
\eta/2
over the time interval
[t/2,t]
, which has a cost exponential in
t
for a 2d Brownian motion with radius of order
t
.
We first apply the many–to–one lemma with the bound
b(\theta_{s})\leq 1
to get that the probability in (
5.15
) is at most

e^{t}\cdot\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(R_{t}\geq m(t)-a,\min_{s\in[t/2,t]}R_{s}\geq\frac{\sqrt{2}}{4}t,T_{1}\leq t,T_{2}\leq t}}\right),

(5.16)

with
T_{1}\coloneqq\inf\{s\geq t/2:\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\theta_{s}}}\right\rvert\leq\frac{\eta}{2}\}
and
T_{2}\coloneqq\inf\{s\geq t/2:\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\theta_{s}}}\right\rvert\geq\eta\}
.
There are two cases to distinguish,
T_{1}<T_{2}
and
T_{2}<T_{1}
, but they are treated similarly, so we focus on the case
T_{1}<T_{2}
.
By Markov property at time
T_{1}
,

\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(R_{t}\geq m(t)-a,\min_{s\in[t/2,t]}R_{s}\geq\frac{\sqrt{2}}{4}t,T_{1}<T_{2}\leq t}}\right)\leq\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\chi(T_{1},R_{T_{1}})\mathbbm{1}_{T_{1}<t}}}\right],

(5.17)

where we set, for any
s\in[t/2,t)
and
r>0
,

\chi(s,r)\coloneqq\mathbb{P}_{(s,r,\eta/2)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(R_{t}\geq m(t)-a,\min_{q\in[s,t]}R_{q}\geq\frac{\sqrt{2}}{4}t,T_{2}\leq t}}\right),

where under
\mathbb{P}_{(s,r,\eta/2)}
the process
(R_{q},\theta_{q})_{q\geq s}
starts from
(r,\eta/2)
at time
s
.
Now, using the representation of the angle in (
5.11
), we get

\displaystyle\chi(s,r)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}_{(s,r,\eta/2)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(R_{t}\geq m(t)-a,\min_{q\in[s,t]}R_{q}\geq\frac{\sqrt{2}}{4}t,\max_{p\in[0,\tau(t)]}W_{p}\geq\frac{\eta}{2}}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}_{(s,r,\eta/2)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(R_{t}\geq m(t)-a,\max_{p\in[0,4/t]}W_{p}\geq\frac{\eta}{2}}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-\frac{\eta^{2}t}{32}}}\right)\cdot\mathbb{P}_{(s,r,\eta/2)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(R_{t}\geq m(t)-a}}\right),

using the independence of
R
and
W
and proceeding as in (
5.8
) to bound the probability involving
W
.
Coming back to (
5.17
) and using again Markov property at time
T_{1}
, the right-hand side of (
5.17
) is at most

\displaystyle 2\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-\frac{\eta^{2}t}{32}}}\right)\cdot\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(R_{t}\geq m(t)-a}}\right)=\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-\frac{\eta^{2}t}{32}-t+o(t)}}\right)\leq e^{-t-ct}.

Proceeding similarly in the case
T_{2}<T_{1}
, we get that (
5.16
) is bounded by
e^{-ct}
, which concludes the proof of (
5.15
) and hence of the lemma.
∎

In the next lemma, we prove that the final position of an extremal particle
u
needs to have a final
y
-coordinate
Y_{u}(t)
of order roughly
t^{\kappa/2}
, which is the typical space scale of the Brownian motion weighted by an integral via PDEs studied in Sections
2
and
3
.
In particular, we deduce
Y_{u}(t)=o(\sqrt{t})
, which together with
R_{u}(t)\geq m(t)
implies that
R_{u}(t)=X_{u}(t)+o(1)
, allowing us afterwards to replace the radial coordinate of an extremal particle by its
x
-coordinate. We also note that this lemma, together with the previous lemma, implies Proposition
1.2
.

Lemma 5.4
.

There exists
\eta>0
, such that, for any
\varepsilon>0
and
a\geq 0
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\exists u\in\mathcal{N}(t):R_{u}(t)\geq m(t)-a,\max_{s\in[t/2,t]}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\theta_{u}(s)}}\right\rvert\leq\eta,\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert Y_{u}(t)}}\right\rvert\geq t^{\frac{\kappa}{2}+\varepsilon}}}\right)\xrightarrow[t\to\infty]{}0.

Proof.

We assume that
\eta\leq\sigma
, where
\sigma
is given by Lemma
5.1
.
By (
5.2
), it is enough to work on the event
A_{s_{0}}
for some large
s_{0}
.
By a union bound over
u\in\mathcal{N}(t)
, the branching property at time
t/2
, the many–to–one lemma (Lemma
4.1
) and Lemma
5.1
, we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(A_{s_{0}}\cap\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\exists u\in\mathcal{N}(t):R_{u}(t)\geq m(t)-a,\max_{s\in[t/2,t]}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\theta_{u}(s)}}\right\rvert\leq\eta,\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert Y_{u}(t)}}\right\rvert\geq t^{\frac{\kappa}{2}+\varepsilon}}}\right\}\middle|\mathcal{F}_{t/2}}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq e^{t/2}\mathbbm{1}_{A_{s_{0},t/2}}\sum_{u\in\mathcal{N}(t/2)}\mathbb{E}_{(t/2,X_{u}(t/2),Y_{u}(t/2))}\Biggl[\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-\beta\int_{t/2}^{t}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\frac{Y_{s}}{\sqrt{2}s}}}\right\rvert^{\alpha}(1+f(Y_{s},s))\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}s}}\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 256.0748pt{}\times\mathbbm{1}_{R_{t}\geq m(t)-a,\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert Y_{t}}}\right\rvert\in[t^{\kappa/2+\varepsilon},2\eta t]}\Biggr],

(5.18)

also noting that, on the event in the probability, we have
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert Y_{u}(t)}}\right\rvert=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert X_{u}(t)\tan\theta_{u}(t)}}\right\rvert\leq 2\eta t
, by choosing
\eta
small enough such that
\tan\eta\leq\sqrt{2}\eta
.
Then, for any
x\leq t/\sqrt{2}-1
(recall that on
A_{s_{0},t/2}
we have
X_{u}(t/2)\leq\sqrt{2}\cdot t/2-1
) and for any

, we have to bound

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{(t/2,x,y)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-\beta\int_{t/2}^{t}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\frac{Y_{s}}{\sqrt{2}s}}}\right\rvert^{\alpha}(1+f(Y_{s},s))\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}s}}\right)\mathbbm{1}_{R_{t}\geq m(t)-a,\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert Y_{t}}}\right\rvert\in[t^{\kappa/2+\varepsilon},2\eta t]}}}\right]

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert z}}\right\rvert\in[t^{\kappa/2+\varepsilon},2\eta t]}\mathbb{P}_{(t/2,x)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sqrt{X_{t}^{2}+z^{2}}\geq m(t)-a}}\right)\widetilde{G}(t/2,y;t,z)\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}z,

(5.19)

where
\widetilde{G}
is defined in (

).
Choosing
\eta<1/\sqrt{2}
, we have, for
t
large enough, for any
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert z}}\right\rvert\leq 2\eta t

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert z}}\right\rvert\leq m(t)-a
, and therefore
\sqrt{(m(t)-a)^{2}-z^{2}}\geq m(t)-a-z^{2}/(m(t)-a)^{2}\geq m(t)-a-z^{2}/(2t)
.
Hence, for
t
large enough, for any
x\leq t/\sqrt{2}
and
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert z}}\right\rvert\leq 2\eta t
, we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{(t/2,x)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sqrt{X_{t}^{2}+z^{2}}\geq m(t)-a}}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{N}(0,t/2)\geq\sqrt{(m(t)-a)^{2}-z^{2}}-x}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{N}(0,t/2)\geq m(t)-a-\frac{z^{2}}{2t}-x}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-\frac{t}{2}-\sqrt{2}\overline{x}+\vartheta_{1}t^{1-\kappa}-\frac{\overline{x}^{2}}{t}+\frac{z^{2}}{t^{2}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{t}{\sqrt{2}}+\overline{x}}}\right)+O(\log t)}}\right),

where we wrote
m(t)-a-x=\frac{t}{\sqrt{2}}+\overline{x}-\frac{\vartheta_{1}}{\sqrt{2}}t^{1-\kappa}+O(\log t)
with
\overline{x}\coloneqq t/\sqrt{2}-x\geq 1
, we used that
\kappa\in(1/2,1)
, and noted that
m(t)-a-x-z^{2}/(2t)\geq 0
if
\eta
is chosen small enough in order to apply the Gaussian tail bound
\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{N}(0,v)\geq u)\leq e^{-u^{2}/2v}
for
u\geq 0
.
Furthermore, maximizing in

, we can bound

\frac{z^{2}}{t^{2}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{t}{\sqrt{2}}+\overline{x}}}\right)-\frac{\overline{x}^{2}}{t}\leq\frac{z^{2}}{\sqrt{2}t}+\frac{z^{4}}{4t^{3}}\leq\frac{z^{2}}{t},

by choosing
\eta
small enough.
Coming back to (
5.19
), we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{(t/2,x,y)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-\beta\int_{t/2}^{t}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\frac{Y_{s}}{\sqrt{2}s}}}\right\rvert^{\alpha}(1+f(Y_{s},s))\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}s}}\right)\mathbbm{1}_{R_{t}\geq m(t)-a,\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert Y_{t}}}\right\rvert\in[t^{\kappa/2+\varepsilon},2\eta t]}}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-\frac{t}{2}-\sqrt{2}\overline{x}+\vartheta_{1}t^{1-\kappa}+O(\log t)}}\right)\int_{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert z}}\right\rvert\in[t^{\kappa/2+\varepsilon},2\eta t]}e^{z^{2}/t}\widetilde{G}(t/2,y;t,z)\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}z

\displaystyle\leq\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-\frac{t}{2}-\sqrt{2}\overline{x}+\vartheta_{1}(t/2)^{1-\kappa}+O(\log t)}}\right)\int_{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert z}}\right\rvert\in[t^{\kappa/2+\varepsilon},2\eta t]}e^{z^{2}/t-c\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert z}}\right\rvert^{(2+\alpha)/2}t^{-\alpha/2}}\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}z,

using Proposition
3.6
and that
\kappa=2\alpha/(2+\alpha)
.
Note that, if
\eta
is chosen small enough, one has

for
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert z}}\right\rvert\leq 2\eta t
.
Thus, the last integral is a
O(\exp(-c^{\prime}t^{\varepsilon(2+\alpha)/2}))
.
Coming back to (
5.18
), we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(A_{s_{0}}\cap\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\exists u\in\mathcal{N}(t):R_{u}(t)\geq m(t)-a,\max_{s\in[t/2,t]}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\theta_{u}(s)}}\right\rvert\leq\eta,\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert Y_{u}(t)}}\right\rvert\geq t^{\frac{\kappa}{2}+\varepsilon}}}\right\}\middle|\mathcal{F}_{t/2}}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\vartheta_{1}(t/2)^{1-\kappa}-c^{\prime}t^{\varepsilon(2+\alpha)/2}+O(\log t)}}\right)\cdot\mathbbm{1}_{A_{s_{0},t/2}}\sum_{u\in\mathcal{N}(t/2)}e^{\sqrt{2}(X_{u}(t/2)-\sqrt{2}\cdot t/2)}

\displaystyle\leq\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-c^{\prime}t^{\varepsilon(2+\alpha)/2}+O(\log t)}}\right)\cdot\mathbbm{1}_{A_{s_{0},t/2}}Z_{t/2},

using that
1\leq(\sqrt{2}\cdot t/2-X_{u}(t/2))
on
A_{s_{0},t/2}
.
We conclude by taking the expectation and applying Lemma
5.2
.
∎

In the next lemma, we improve the straight barrier given by the event
A_{s_{0}}
to replace it by a curved barrier which includes the same polynomial correction as
m(t)
.
For
s>0
, let

m^{+}(s)\coloneqq\sqrt{2}s-\frac{\vartheta_{1}}{\sqrt{2}}s^{1-\kappa}+10\log s.

(5.20)

Note that
m^{+}(s)
is above
m(s)
, at a distance of order
\log s
.
The coefficient 10 here has been arbitrarily chosen.

Lemma 5.5
.

We have

\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\exists u\in\mathcal{N}(t):\max_{s\in[t/2,t]}(X_{u}(s)-m^{+}(s))>0}}\right)\xrightarrow[t\to\infty]{}0.

Proof.

In order to discretize time afterwards, we first argue that no particle is moving more than 1 on a time interval of length
t^{-2}
between times
t/2
and
t
: by a union bound over
s
and over
u\in\mathcal{N}(s)
and the many-to-one lemma (bounding the branching rate by 1), we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\exists s\in[t/2,t+t^{-2}]\cap t^{-2}\mathbb{Z},\exists u\in\mathcal{N}(s):\max_{r\in[s-t^{-2},s]}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert X_{u}(r)-X_{u}(s)}}\right\rvert>1}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{s\in[t/2,t+t^{-2}]\cap t^{-2}\mathbb{Z}}e^{s}\cdot\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\max_{r\in[0,t^{-2}]}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert B_{r}}}\right\rvert>1}}\right)\leq t^{3}\cdot e^{t}\cdot 4e^{-t^{2}/2}\xrightarrow[t\to\infty]{}0,

using in the second inequality that
\max_{r\in[0,t^{-2}]}B_{r}
is distributed as
\lvert B_{t^{-2}}\rvert
and a classical Gaussian tail bound. It follows that it is sufficent to prove that

\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\exists u\in\mathcal{N}(t):\max_{s\in[t/2,t]\cap t^{-2}\mathbb{Z}}(X_{u}(s)-m^{+}(s)+1)>0}}\right)\xrightarrow[t\to\infty]{}0.

(5.21)

Recalling that
\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(A_{s_{0}}^{c}}}\right)\to 0
as
s_{0}\to\infty
by (
5.2
), it is enough to prove that

\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(A_{s_{0}}\cap\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\exists u\in\mathcal{N}(t):\max_{s\in[t/2,t]\cap t^{-2}\mathbb{Z}}(X_{u}(s)-m^{+}(s)+1)>0}}\right\}}}\right)\xrightarrow[t\to\infty]{}0,

(5.22)

for any fixed
s_{0}>0
.
Using a union bound over
s\in[t/2,t]\cap t^{-2}\mathbb{Z}
,
the left-hand side of (
5.22
) is at most

\displaystyle\sum_{s\in[t/2,t]\cap t^{-2}\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(A_{s_{0}}\cap\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\exists u\in\mathcal{N}(s):X_{u}(s)>m^{+}(s)-1}}\right\}}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{s\in[t/2,t]\cap t^{-2}\mathbb{Z}}\Biggl(\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(A_{s_{0}}\cap\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\exists u\in\mathcal{N}(s):X_{u}(s)>m^{+}(s)-1,\max_{r\in[s/2,s]}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\theta_{u}(r)}}\right\rvert\leq\sigma}}\right\}}}\right)+e^{-cs}\Biggr),

where
\sigma>0
is given by Lemma
5.1
and

is then given by Lemma
5.3
, noting that
X_{u}(s)>m^{+}(s)-1
implies
R_{u}(s)>m(s)
.
Therefore, it is now enough to prove that

\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(A_{s_{0}}\cap\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\exists u\in\mathcal{N}(t):X_{u}(t)>m^{+}(t)-1,\max_{s\in[t/2,t]}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\theta_{u}(s)}}\right\rvert\leq\sigma}}\right\}}}\right)=o\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t^{-3}}}\right),

(5.23)

as
t\to\infty
, for any fixed
s_{0}>0
.

We first work conditionally on
\mathcal{F}_{t/2}
. By a union bound over
u\in\mathcal{N}(t)
, the branching property at time
t/2
, the many–to–one lemma (Lemma
4.1
) and Lemma
5.1
, we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(A_{s_{0}}\cap\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\exists u\in\mathcal{N}(t):X_{u}(t)>m^{+}(t)-1,\max_{s\in[t/2,t]}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\theta_{u}(s)}}\right\rvert\leq\sigma}}\right\}\middle|\mathcal{F}_{t/2}}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq e^{t/2}\mathbbm{1}_{A_{s_{0},t/2}}\sum_{u\in\mathcal{N}(t/2)}\mathbb{E}_{(t/2,X_{u}(t/2),Y_{u}(t/2))}\Biggl[\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-\beta\int_{t/2}^{t}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\frac{Y_{s}}{\sqrt{2}s}}}\right\rvert^{\alpha}(1+f(Y_{s},s))\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}s}}\right)\mathbbm{1}_{X_{t}>m^{+}(t)-1}\Biggr]

\displaystyle\leq e^{t/2}\mathbbm{1}_{A_{s_{0},t/2}}\sum_{u\in\mathcal{N}(t/2)}C\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\vartheta_{1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left((t/2)^{1-\kappa}-t^{1-\kappa}}}\right)}}\right)\mathbb{P}_{(t/2,X_{u}(t/2))}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(X_{t}>m^{+}(t)-1}}\right),

(5.24)

using the independence between
X
and
Y
and applying Proposition
3.5
.
(i)
to the
Y
part.
For the
X
contribution, note that, under
\mathbb{P}_{(t/2,X_{u}(t/2))}
,
X_{t}
is Gaussian with mean
X_{u}(t/2)
and variance
t/2
. Moreover, note that on event
A_{s_{0},t/2}
we have
X_{u}(t/2)\leq\sqrt{2}\cdot t/2
so that
m^{+}(t)-X_{u}(t/2)\geq 0
.
Hence, we can apply the Gaussian tail bound
\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{N}(0,v)\geq a)\leq e^{-a^{2}/2v}
for

to get

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{(t/2,X_{u}(t/2))}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(X_{t}\geq m^{+}(t)-1}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-t/2-\sqrt{2}(\sqrt{2}t/2-X_{u}(t/2))+\vartheta_{1}t^{1-\kappa}-10\sqrt{2}\log t+\sqrt{2}}}\right).

Therefore, the right-hand side of (

) is at most

Ct^{-10\sqrt{2}}\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\vartheta_{1}(t/2)^{1-\kappa}}}\right)\mathbbm{1}_{A_{s_{0},t/2}}\sum_{u\in\mathcal{N}(t/2)}e^{-\sqrt{2}(\sqrt{2}t/2-X_{u}(t/2))}\leq Ct^{-10\sqrt{2}+\kappa/4}\mathbbm{1}_{A_{s_{0},t/2}}Z_{t/2},

using that on

we have

.
Taking the expectation and applying Lemma

∎

We conclude this subsection by the following corollary which gathers the three previous lemmas and is the starting point of the proof of the upper bound of Theorem

in the next subsection.

Corollary 5.6
.

Recall the definition of
m^{+}(s)
in (
5.20
).
For any
\eta>0
and
a\geq 0
, as
t\to\infty
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(M_{t}\geq m(t)+a+1}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\exists u\in\mathcal{N}(t):X_{u}(t)\geq m(t)+a,\max_{s\in[t/2,t]}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\theta_{u}(s)}}\right\rvert\leq\eta,\max_{s\in[t/2,t]}X_{u}(s)-m^{+}(s)\leq 0}}\right)+o(1).

Proof.

Let
\eta>0
and
a\geq 0
.
First note that if the result holds for some
\eta
, then it directly holds for any other
\eta^{\prime}>\eta
, hence we can assume that
\eta
is smaller than the one given by Lemma
5.4
.
Fix
\varepsilon>0
such that
\kappa/2+\varepsilon<1/2
. By Lemmas
5.3
and
5.4
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(M_{t}\geq m(t)+a+1}}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\exists u\in\mathcal{N}(t):R_{u}(t)\geq m(t)+a+1,\max_{s\in[t/2,t]}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\theta_{u}(s)}}\right\rvert\leq\eta,\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert Y_{u}(t)}}\right\rvert<t^{\frac{\kappa}{2}+\varepsilon}}}\right)+o(1).

Now consider
u\in\mathcal{N}(t)
satisfying the properties in the last probability. We can assume
\eta<\pi/2
so that
X_{u}(t)\geq 0
.
Then, we have
R_{u}(t)=X_{u}(t)+O(Y_{u}(t)^{2}/X_{u}(t))=X_{u}(t)+o(1)
, because
X_{u}(t)
is necessarily of order
t
while
Y_{u}(t)=o(\sqrt{t})
. Therefore, we have
X_{u}(t)\geq m(t)+a
for
t
large enough (but deterministic).
This proves

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(M_{t}\geq m(t)+a+1}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\exists u\in\mathcal{N}(t):X_{u}(t)\geq m(t)+a,\max_{s\in[t/2,t]}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\theta_{u}(s)}}\right\rvert\leq\eta}}\right)+o(1).

The conclusion of the corollary then follows directly from Lemma
5.5
.
∎

5.4
Proof of the upper bound

Proof of the upper bound in Theorem
1.1
.

We prove here that

\limsup_{a\to\infty}\limsup_{t\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(M_{t}\geq m(t)+a}}\right)=0.

(5.25)

Let
\sigma>0
be the constant given by Lemma
5.1
.
By Corollary
5.6
and (
5.2
), it is enough to prove, for any
s_{0}>0
,

\limsup_{a\to\infty}\limsup_{t\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(E}}\right)=0,

(5.26)

where we define

E\coloneqq A_{s_{0},t/2}\cap\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\exists u\in\mathcal{N}(t):X_{u}(t)\geq m(t)+a,\max_{s\in[t/2,t]}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\theta_{u}(s)}}\right\rvert\leq\sigma,\max_{s\in[t/2,t]}X_{u}(s)\leq m^{+}(s)}}\right\},

which depends implicitly on
t
,
a
and
s_{0}
.
We summarize shortly the proof before diving into the details.
Let
0<\delta<\kappa
and set
t_{0}=t-t^{\delta}
. The proof consists of three steps: we first work conditionally on
\mathcal{F}_{t_{0}}
, then on
\mathcal{F}_{t/2}
and finally bound the non-conditional probability.
A key idea for the first step is that the upper barrier at
m^{+}(s)
is too crude to get a good bound on the end of the trajectory.
Instead, we compare the
x
-coordinate of our BBM on time interval
[t_{0},t]
to a one-dimensional BBM with branching rate 1, for which sharp bounds are known.
This comparison is sufficient because
\delta<\kappa
, so the time window
t^{\delta}
is smaller than the typical time scale
t^{\kappa}
where the fact of having a varying branching rate plays a role.
The second step deals with the time interval
[t/2,t_{0}]
and consists in calculating the conditional first moment of the bound obtained in the first step, and in bounding it in terms of the pseudo-derivative martingale
Z_{t/2}
.
The final step, which deals with
[0,t/2]
is a direct application of Lemma
5.2
.

Step 1.
Fix some
a\geq 0
.
We condition on
\mathcal{F}_{t_{0}}
and count the number of particles at time
t_{0}
which have a descendant that exceeds
m(t)+a
at time
t
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(E|\mathcal{F}_{t_{0}})

\displaystyle\leq\mathbbm{1}_{A_{s_{0},t/2}}\sum_{u\in\mathcal{N}(t_{0})}\mathbbm{1}_{\forall s\in[t/2,t_{0}],|\theta_{u}(s)|\leq\sigma}\mathbbm{1}_{\forall s\in[t/2,t_{0}],X_{u}(s)\leq m^{+}(s)}

\displaystyle\hskip 85.35826pt{}\times\mathbb{P}_{(t_{0},X_{u}(t_{0}),Y_{u}(t_{0}))}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\max_{v\in\mathcal{N}(t),u\leq v}X_{v}(t)\geq m(t)+a}}\right).

(5.27)

Next, we can dominate
\max_{v\in\mathcal{N}(t),u\leq v}X_{v}(t)
by the maximum of a one-dimensional BBM with branching rate
1
, call
M^{1\mathrm{d}}_{s}
its maximum at time
s
,

\mathbb{P}_{(t_{0},X_{u}(t_{0}),Y_{u}(t_{0}))}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\max_{v\in\mathcal{N}(t),u\leq v}X_{v}(t)\geq m(t)+a}}\right)\leq\mathbb{P}_{(0,X_{u}(t_{0}))}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(M^{1\mathrm{d}}_{t^{\delta}}\geq m(t)+a}}\right),

(5.28)

where we used that
t-t_{0}=t^{\delta}
.
Now, we use the following bound for the tail of
M^{1\mathrm{d}}_{s}
, which follows from
[
3
, Corollary 10]
: letting
m^{1\mathrm{d}}(s)=\sqrt{2}s-\frac{3}{2\sqrt{2}}\log s
for
s>1
, there exists
C>0
such that, for any
x\in\mathbb{R}
and
s\geq 1
,

\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({M^{1\mathrm{d}}_{s}\geq m^{1\mathrm{d}}(s)+x}}}\right)\leq C(x\vee 1)e^{-\sqrt{2}x}\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-\frac{x_{+}^{2}}{4s}}}\right),

(5.29)

where
x_{+}=\max(x,0)
.
We apply this to (
5.28
) with
s=t^{\delta}
and

x=m(t)-m^{1\mathrm{d}}(t^{\delta})-X_{u}(t_{0})+a=\sqrt{2}t_{0}-X_{u}(t_{0})+a-\frac{\vartheta_{1}}{\sqrt{2}}t^{1-\kappa}-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{3}{2}(1-\delta)-\frac{\kappa}{4}}}\right)\frac{\log t}{\sqrt{2}}.

Moreover, recall
t_{0}=t-t^{\delta}
and
\delta\in(0,\kappa)
, so we have
t^{1-\kappa}=t_{0}^{1-\kappa}+o(1)
and

m(t)-m^{1\mathrm{d}}(t^{\delta})+a=m^{+}(t_{0})-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(10\sqrt{2}+\frac{3}{2}(1-\delta)-\frac{\kappa}{4}}}\right)\frac{\log t}{\sqrt{2}}+a+o(1)\leq m^{+}(t_{0}).

for
t
large enough depending only on
a
.
Hence, we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{(0,X_{u}(t_{0}))}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(M^{1\mathrm{d}}_{t^{\delta}}\geq m(t)+a}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq C\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left((m^{+}(t_{0})-X_{u}(t_{0}))\vee 1}}\right)e^{-\sqrt{2}(\sqrt{2}t_{0}-X_{u}(t_{0})+a)+\vartheta_{1}t^{1-\kappa}}

\displaystyle\quad{}\times t^{\frac{3}{2}(1-\delta)-\frac{\kappa}{4}}\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-\frac{(m(t)-m^{1\mathrm{d}}(t^{\delta})-X_{u}(t_{0})+a)_{+}^{2}}{4t^{\delta}}}}\right).

Note that the last exponential is a non-increasing function of
a
, so we can replace
a
by
0
.
Going back to (
5.27
), this yields

\mathbb{P}(E|\mathcal{F}_{t_{0}})\leq Ce^{-\sqrt{2}a}e^{\vartheta_{1}t^{1-\kappa}}t^{\frac{3}{2}(1-\delta)-\frac{\kappa}{4}}\mathbbm{1}_{A_{s_{0},t/2}}\Upsilon_{t_{0}},

(5.30)

where we set

\displaystyle\Upsilon_{t_{0}}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\sum_{u\in\mathcal{N}(t_{0})}(m^{+}(t_{0})-X_{u}(t_{0})+1)e^{\sqrt{2}(X_{u}(t_{0})-\sqrt{2}t_{0})}\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-\frac{(m(t)-m^{1\mathrm{d}}(t^{\delta})-X_{u}(t_{0}))_{+}^{2}}{4t^{\delta}}}}\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 56.9055pt{}\times\mathbbm{1}_{\forall s\in[t/2,t_{0}],|\theta_{u}(s)|\leq\sigma}\mathbbm{1}_{\forall s\in[t/2,t_{0}],X_{u}(s)\leq m^{+}(s)},

and used that
X_{u}(t_{0})\leq m^{+}(t_{0})
to bound
(m^{+}(t_{0})-X_{u}(t_{0}))\vee 1\leq m^{+}(t_{0})-X_{u}(t_{0})+1
.

Step 2.
We now aim at bounding
\mathbb{P}(E|\mathcal{F}_{t/2})
.
By (
5.30
), it is enough to bound
\mathbb{E}[\Upsilon_{t_{0}}|\mathcal{F}_{t/2}]
.
By the branching property at time
t/2
, the many–to–one lemma (Lemma
4.1
) and Lemma
5.1
(note that
X_{u}(s)\leq m^{+}(s)
ensures that
X_{u}(s)\leq\sqrt{2}s
), we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\Upsilon_{t_{0}}|\mathcal{F}_{t/2}]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{u\in\mathcal{N}(t/2)}\mathbb{E}_{(t/2,X_{u}(t/2),Y_{u}(t/2))}\Biggl[\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t_{0}-\frac{t}{2}}}\right)-\beta\int_{t/2}^{t_{0}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\frac{Y_{s}}{\sqrt{2}s}}}\right\rvert^{\alpha}(1+f(Y_{s},s))\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}s}}\right)

\displaystyle\quad\times(m^{+}(t_{0})-X_{t_{0}}+1)e^{\sqrt{2}(X_{t_{0}}-\sqrt{2}t_{0})}\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-\frac{(m(t)-m^{1\mathrm{d}}(t^{\delta})-X_{t_{0}})_{+}^{2}}{4t^{\delta}}}}\right)\mathbbm{1}_{\forall s\in[t/2,t_{0}],X_{s}\leq m^{+}(s)}\Biggr].

Then, using the independence between
X
and
Y
and applying Proposition
3.5
to the

\mathbb{P}_{(s_{0},x)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\bigcap_{i=1}^{2}\bigcap_{j=1}^{2}B_{j}^{(i)}}}\right)\leq\int_{-\infty}^{g_{t}^{+}(r)}\mathbb{P}_{(s_{0},x)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(B_{2}^{[s_{0},r]}\middle|X_{r}=y}}\right)\mathbb{P}_{(r,y)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(B_{1}\cap B_{2}^{[r,t]}}}\right)^{2}\frac{e^{-(y-x)^{2}/[2(r-s_{0})]}}{\sqrt{2\pi(r-s_{0})}}\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}y,

(6.12)

where
B_{1}=\{X_{t}\in[m(t)-1,m(t)]\}
and
B_{2}^{I}=\{\forall s\in I,X_{s}\leq\lambda s\}
with
\lambda=m(t)/t
.
By
[
20
, Lemma 2]
, we have

\mathbb{P}_{(s_{0},x)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(B_{2}^{[s_{0},r]}\middle|X_{r}=y}}\right)\leq\frac{2(\lambda s_{0}-x)(\lambda r-y)}{r-s_{0}}\wedge 1\leq\frac{C(\overline{y}+1)}{(r-s_{0}+1)},

writing
\overline{y}=\lambda r-y
and using the assumption on
x
.
On the other hand, by Girsanov’s theorem, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{(r,y)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(B_{1}\cap B_{2}^{[r,t]}}}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{(s_{0},y-\lambda r)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathbbm{1}_{\{X_{t}\in[-1,0]\}}\mathbbm{1}_{\{\forall s\in[r,t],X_{r}\leq 0\}}e^{-\lambda(X_{t}-X_{s_{0}})-\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2}(t-r)}}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C(\overline{y}+1)}{(t-r+1)^{3/2}}e^{-\lambda\overline{y}-\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2}(t-r)},

where we used
e^{-\lambda(X_{t}-X_{s_{0}})}\leq e^{-\lambda\overline{y}}
and bounded the remaining probability by first integrating w.r.t.
X_{t}
and then applying
[
20
, Lemma 2]
again.
Coming back to (
6.12
), using also

e^{-(y-x)^{2}/[2(r-s_{0})]}=e^{-(\lambda(r-s_{0})-\overline{y}-x+\lambda s_{0})^{2}/[2(r-s_{0})]}\leq e^{-\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2}(r-s_{0})+\lambda(\overline{y}+x-\lambda s_{0})}\leq Ce^{-\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2}r+\lambda\overline{y}},

and changing
y
for
\overline{y}
in the integral, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{(s_{0},x)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\bigcap_{i=1}^{2}\bigcap_{j=1}^{2}B_{j}^{(i)}}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{Ce^{-\lambda^{2}t+\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2}r}}{(r-s_{0}+1)\sqrt{r-s_{0}}(t-r+1)^{3}}\int_{((r-s_{0})\wedge(t-r))^{(1-\varepsilon)/2}}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda\overline{y}}(\overline{y}+1)^{3}\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}\overline{y}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{Ct^{3-\frac{\kappa}{2}}e^{-2t+2\vartheta_{1}t^{-\kappa}}e^{r-\vartheta_{1}rt^{-\kappa}-(\frac{3}{2}-\frac{\kappa}{4})\frac{r}{t}\log(t)}}{(r-s_{0}+1)\sqrt{r-s_{0}}(t-r+1)^{3}}e^{-((r-s_{0})\wedge(t-r))^{(1-\varepsilon)/2}},

using
\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2}=1-\vartheta_{1}t^{-\kappa}-(\frac{3}{2}-\frac{\kappa}{4})\frac{\log t}{t}+o(\frac{1}{t})
for the prefactor, and
e^{-\lambda\overline{y}}(\overline{y}+1)^{3}\leq Ce^{-\overline{y}}
for the integral.
Combining this with (
6.11
),
we get that (
6.10
) is at most

Ct^{3}\int_{s_{0}}^{t}r^{-\kappa/4}\frac{e^{\vartheta_{1}(r^{1-\kappa}-rt^{-\kappa})-(\frac{3}{2}-\frac{\kappa}{4})\frac{r}{t}\log(t)}}{(r-s_{0}+1)\sqrt{r-s_{0}}(t-r+1)^{3}}e^{-((r-s_{0})\wedge(t-r))^{(1-\varepsilon)/2}}\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}r.

(6.13)

Then, using that
s\geq s_{0}\mapsto(\log s)/s
is decreasing for

large enough, we have

and
e^{-\frac{3}{2}\frac{r}{t}\log(t)}=t^{-3/2}e^{\frac{3}{2}\frac{t-r}{t}\log(t)}\leq t^{-3/2}(t-r)^{3/2}
.
Moreover, there exists
C(\kappa)>0
such that
r^{1-\kappa}-rt^{-\kappa}\leq C(r\wedge(t-r))^{1-\kappa}
for any
r\in[s_{0},t]
: this is direct for
r\leq t/2
and, for
r\geq t/2
, one has

r^{1-\kappa}-rt^{-\kappa}=r^{1-\kappa}\bigg(1-\big(1-\frac{t-r}{t}\big)^{\kappa}\bigg)\leq C(\kappa)t^{1-\kappa}\frac{t-r}{t}\leq C(\kappa)(t-r)^{1-\kappa}.

It follows that
e^{\vartheta_{1}(r^{1-\kappa}-rt^{-\kappa})-((r-s_{0})\wedge(t-r))^{(1-\varepsilon)/2}}\leq C
, where we recall that
C
can depend on
s_{0}
.
Applying these facts to (
6.13

\mathbb{E}_{(s_{0},x,y)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\Gamma_{s_{0}}(t)}}\right\rvert(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\Gamma_{s_{0}}(t)}}\right\rvert-1)}}\right]\leq Ct^{3/2}\int_{s_{0}}^{t}\frac{1}{(r-s_{0}+1)\sqrt{r-s_{0}}(t-r+1)^{3/2}}\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathrm{d}r\leq C,

which concludes the proof.
∎

6.4
Proof of the lower bound

We are now in a position to show the lower bound of Theorem

.
To be precise, we show that, for any
\delta>0

\limsup_{t\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(M_{t}\leq m(t)-x}}\right)\leq\delta.

Proof of the lower bound in Theorem

.

We use the moment bounds on
|\Gamma_{K}(t)|
to show that there are particles near
m(t)
at time
t

For
s_{0}\geq 0
, we consider the box

\mathcal{B}_{s_{0}}=[\sqrt{2}s_{0}-2\sqrt{s_{0}},\sqrt{2}s_{0}-\sqrt{s_{0}}]\times[-s_{0}^{\kappa/2},s_{0}^{\kappa/2}].

(6.15)

In the sequel, we consider
s_{0}
large enough to satisfy Lemmas
6.2
and
6.3

Combining Lemmas
6.2
and
6.3
, as well as the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, there exist
t_{0}>s_{0}
and

such that for all
t\geq t_{0}

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{(s_{0},x,y)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(M_{t}\geq m(t)-1}}\right)\geq\mathbb{P}_{(s_{0},x,y)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(|\Gamma_{K}(t)|\geq 1}}\right)\geq\frac{\mathbb{E}_{(s_{0},x,y)}[|\Gamma_{K}(t)|]^{2}}{\mathbb{E}_{(s_{0},x,y)}[|\Gamma_{K}(t)|^{2}]}\geq c_{0}>0.

Lemma 6.4
.

For
s_{0}
large enough, recalling the definition of
\mathcal{B}_{s_{0}}
in (

), we have
\mathbb{P}_{(0,0,0)}
-a.s.

\#\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{u\in\mathcal{N}(t):(X_{u}(t),Y_{u}(t))\in\mathcal{B}_{s_{0}}}}\right\}\xrightarrow[t\to\infty]{}\infty

Proof.

As
s_{0}\to\infty
, the angular coordinate of points in
\mathcal{B}_{s_{0}}
is uniformly going to 0, so, using Assumption
(A2)
, we can choose
s_{0}
large enough so that the branching rate is at least
1/2

\mathcal{B}_{s_{0}}
.
We also introduce a family of square boxes, indexed by

\mathcal{B}_{s_{0}}^{L}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sqrt{2}s_{0}-\frac{3}{2}\sqrt{s_{0}}+[-L,L]}}\right)\times[-L,L].

(6.18)

We also assume that
s_{0}
is large enough such that
\mathcal{B}_{s_{0}}^{4}\subset\mathcal{B}_{s_{0}}
. We now fix
s_{0}
.

We define a spine
(\xi_{t})_{t\geq 0}
with
\xi_{t}\in\mathcal{N}(t)
, starting with the root and then choosing one of the two children uniformly at random at each branching point. We now make the two following claims

•

Claim 1:
The spine branches infinitely many times in the box
\mathcal{B}_{s_{0}}^{2}
almost surely.

•

Claim 2:
There exists
c>0
such that, for all

We now prove the claims. For Claim 1, note that the trajectory of the spine is a two-dimensional Brownian motion, which is recurrent, so a.s. the spine enters
\mathcal{B}_{s_{0}}^{1}

\mathcal{B}_{s_{0}}^{2}

\mathcal{B}_{s_{0}}^{1}
