Physiological, biochemical, and gene expression responses of vetiver grass (Chrysopogon zizanioides) to petroleum hydrocarbon contaminated soil
Authors: Mahdi Nazari, Hamzeh Amiri, Sasan Mohsenzadeh, Javad Karimi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPetroleum hydrocarbon contamination poses a persistent challenge to soil quality and plant productivity. This study investigated the responses of vetiver grass (Chrysopogon zizanioides) to petroleum hydrocarbon–contaminated soil by integrating physiological, biochemical, and gene expression analyses under controlled pot conditions. Vetiver plants were exposed to increasing levels of total petroleum hydrocarbons (TPH), and plant growth parameters, antioxidant enzyme activities, glutathione dynamics, secondary metabolites, and the expression of GST and PAL genes were evaluated. The results showed that vetiver tolerated petroleum hydrocarbon stress and exhibited measurable changes in antioxidant defense systems and stress-related gene expression. TPH reduction in planted soils ranged from 85 to 93% relative to initial concentrations, while unplanted control soils were used to account for background degradation and natural attenuation. Elevated activities of antioxidant enzymes and increased expression of GST and PAL were interpreted as stress-responsive indicators rather than direct mechanistic drivers of hydrocarbon removal. Overall, the findings demonstrate the adaptive physiological and molecular responses of vetiver grass to petroleum hydrocarbon contamination under controlled conditions. While these results highlight the potential of vetiver for use in plant-based remediation strategies, further field-scale studies are required to validate its effectiveness under real environmental conditions.Keywords: Phytoremediation, Petroleum hydrocarbons, Catalase, Phenolic compounds, Glutathione S-transferaseSubject terms: Environmental sciences, Plant sciences

Introduction

Human activities, including excessive use of fossil fuels and agrochemicals, have led to significant soil contamination from pollutants and oil derivatives, causing serious ecological consequences1–3. Soil pollution in oil-producing areas is exacerbated by drilling, refinery and power station emissions, oil leaks, and tanker accidents, with petroleum hydrocarbons being a primary contaminant of global environmental concern3–5. Petroleum hydrocarbons in soil disrupt microbial activity, alter chemical and physical soil properties, hinder nutrient uptake, reduce plant growth, and, in the case of polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs), pose health risks such as decreased lung function and asthma.

Several technologies based on physical, chemical, and biological, as well as their combinations, have been widely used to remediate contaminated soils, water resources, and air pollutant emissions6,7. Many of these technologies are costly, time-consuming,, and energy-intensive. Furthermore, some of these technologies result in the production of secondary pollutants that cause adverse effects on the ecosystems under restoration6. In recent years, innovative methods for the biological remediation of soil contaminants have been investigated and favorable results have been obtained from these studies8,9.

Bioremediation, particularly phytoremediation, is an eco-friendly and cost-effective method. It uses plants to remove, immobilize, or detoxify environmental pollutants. Its effectiveness depends on plant species selection, pollutant tolerance, and environmental adaptation10,11.

Among potential candidates, vetiver (Chrysopogon zizanioides) is well-recognized for its ability to tolerate and stabilize hydrocarbon-contaminated soils. However, despite extensive studies on its physiological and morphological adaptations, the molecular mechanisms driving its detoxification responses remain poorly understood. In particular, key stress-responsive genes such as Glutathione S-transferase (GST) and Phenylalanine Ammonia-Lyase (PAL), which play crucial roles in antioxidant defense and secondary metabolite production, have not been systematically investigated in vetiver under hydrocarbon stress.

This study therefore addresses the following research gap: while vetiver’s tolerance is established, the transcriptional regulation of GST and PAL genes in response to hydrocarbon exposure is largely unknown. We hypothesize that hydrocarbon stress induces specific changes in GST and PAL expression, contributing to detoxification and enhancing phytoremediation efficiency. To test this hypothesis, we aim to (i) quantify the expression patterns of GST and PAL in vetiver under controlled hydrocarbon exposure, and (ii) link these molecular responses to the plant’s detoxification capacity and phytoremediation potential.

By focusing on these molecular pathways, this work provides a mechanistic understanding of vetiver’s tolerance and offers insights for optimizing phytoremediation strategies in hydrocarbon-contaminated soils.

Materials and methods

Experimental design and plant material

The experiment was conducted as a randomized complete block design with five levels of total petroleum hydrocarbon (TPH) contamination: 0% (uncontaminated control), 25%, 50%, 75%, and 100% oil-contaminated drilling mud (w/w). Each treatment consisted of three biological replicates (individual pots), and all subsequent biochemical and molecular analyses were performed with three technical replicates per biological sample to ensure reliability.

Uniform vetiver grass (Chrysopogon zizanioides) plants of comparable size and initial biomass were obtained from the Iranian Vetiver Promotion Association to minimize physiological variability at the experiment’s onset.

Preparation of oil contaminated soils

Oil-contaminated mudpit drilling mud was collected from an active oilfield in the Dalan region, Fars Province, Iran. Uncontaminated soil was collected from agricultural land at Shiraz University. Both soils were air-dried and sieved through a 3 mm mesh. The designated contamination levels (25, 50, 75, and 100% TPH) were prepared by thoroughly mixing the drilling mud with the uncontaminated soil on a weight/weight basis. Pots with a 5 kg capacity were each filled with 5 kg of the respective soil mixture. These concentrations were selected to represent a realistic pollution gradient found in oil-impacted sites and to assess plant responses across a wide range of stress intensities.

Plant cultivation and experimental duration

Vetiver plants were transplanted into pots and grown for six months under controlled greenhouse conditions (25 ± 2 °C, 16/8 h light/dark cycle). Soil moisture was maintained near field capacity throughout the experiment. The six-month experimental period was selected to allow sufficient time for plant establishment, hydrocarbon uptake, and induction of physiological, biochemical, and molecular responses.

Sampling strategy

At the end of the experiment, rhizosphere oil samples were collected from a depth of 0–15 cm in each pot. Plant tissues (roots and leaves) were harvested separately, immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at − 80 °C until analysis.

Determination of total petroleum hydrocarbons (TPH) in soil

Soil TPH content was quantified using gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC–MS) (Table 1). Air-dried soil samples were finely sieved (1 mm), and 5 g of soil was extracted three times with 15 mL of dichloromethane using ultrasonic-assisted extraction for 15 min per cycle. The combined extracts were centrifuged (3000 × g, 10 min), concentrated under a gentle nitrogen stream, and reconstituted in 1 mL of hexane for analysis.

Analysis was performed on an Agilent 7890B GC system coupled with an Agilent 5977B MSD. Separation was achieved using an HP-5MS capillary column (30 m × 0.25 mm × 0.25 μm). The oven temperature was programmed as follows: 40 °C (hold 2 min), increased to 300 °C at 10 °C/min, and held for 10 min. The injector and MS transfer line temperatures were set at 275 °C and 285 °C, respectively. Quantification was performed using external calibration with a standard hydrocarbon mixture (C8-C40). Method validation included the analysis of procedural blanks and duplicate samples in each batch. The limits of detection (LOD) and quantification (LOQ) were determined to be 5 mg/kg and 15 mg/kg, respectively. TPH removal efficiency was calculated by comparing the initial and final concentrations in both planted and unplanted (natural attenuation control) pots.

Measurement of antioxidant enzymes activities

Frozen leaf tissue (0.1 g) was homogenized in 1 mL of cold 50 mM potassium phosphate buffer (pH 7.6) containing 0.01 mM EDTA and 1% (w/v) polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP). The homogenate was centrifuged at 14,000 × g for 20 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was used as the crude enzyme extract.

Catalase (CAT) activity was assayed by monitoring the decomposition of H₂O₂ at 240 nm (ε = 39.4 mM⁻¹ cm⁻¹) for 1.5 min12. The reaction mixture contained 200 mM phosphate buffer (pH 7.0) and 30 mM H₂O₂.

Superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity was determined by measuring the inhibition of nitroblue tetrazolium (NBT) photoreduction at 560 nm13. One unit of SOD activity was defined as the amount of enzyme required to cause 50% inhibition of NBT reduction.

Ascorbate peroxidase (APX) activity was measured by tracking the oxidation of ascorbate at 290 nm (ε = 2.8 mM⁻¹ cm⁻¹) for 3 min14. The reaction mixture contained 50 mM phosphate buffer (pH 7.0), 0.1 mM EDTA, 0.1 mM ascorbic acid, and 0.1 mM H₂O₂.

Enzyme activities were expressed as units per milligram of protein (U mg⁻¹ protein). Protein concentration was determined using the Bradford method.

Measurement of glutathione (GSH and GSSG) content

Glutathione content (GSH and GSSG) was determined enzymatically according to the method of Griffith15 with modifications. Frozen leaf tissue (0.2 g) was homogenized in 1 mL of 0.1 M HCl containing 0.1 mM EDTA. The homogenate was centrifuged at 13,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. For total glutathione (GSH+GSSG), the supernatant was neutralized to pH 7.4. The assay mixture contained 0.1 mM DTNB, 0.2 mM NADPH, and 1 unit of glutathione reductase (GR) in 100 mM phosphate buffer (pH 7.5). The reaction was monitored at 412 nm. For GSSG determination, the supernatant was first derivatized with 2-vinylpyridine to mask GSH. Reduced glutathione (GSH) content was calculated by subtracting GSSG from total glutathione.

Measurement of total phenolic and flavonoid content

Total phenolic content was quantified using the Folin-Ciocalteu method16. Methanolic leaf extracts were reacted with Folin-Ciocalteu reagent and 7.5% sodium carbonate. After 30 min incubation, absorbance was measured at 765 nm. Gallic acid was used as a standard, and results were expressed as mg gallic acid equivalents (GAE) per g dry weight (DW).

Total flavonoid content was determined using the aluminum chloride colorimetric method17. The extract was mixed with AlCl₃ solution and acetic acid, incubated for 30 min, and absorbance was read at 414 nm. Quercetin was used as a standard, and results were expressed as mg quercetin equivalents (QE) per g DW.

Gene expression analysis by qRT-PCR

Total RNA was extracted from frozen root and leaf tissues using the FavorPrep™ Tissue Total RNA Mini Kit (Favorgen Biotech Corp.) following the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA integrity was verified by electrophoresis on a 1.5% agarose gel, and concentration/purity was assessed using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer. First-strand cDNA was synthesized from 1 µg of total RNA using the RevertAid™ M-MuLV Reverse Transcriptase (Thermo Scientific).

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) was performed on a StepOnePlus™ Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems) using SYBR Green chemistry. The 20 µL reaction mixture contained 10 µL of 2X SYBR Green Master Mix, 1 µL of cDNA template (diluted 1:4), and 0.5 µM of each gene-specific primer. The primer sequences for the target genes (GSTF2 and PAL3) and the reference gene (TBP) are listed in Table 2. The TBP gene was selected as an internal control due to its stable expression across all treatments in our pilot experiments. The thermal cycling conditions were: 95 °C for 10 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 15 s and 60 °C for 1 min. A melting curve analysis (60–95 °C) was performed at the end of each run to confirm primer specificity. No-template controls (NTC) were included for each primer pair. Primer amplification efficiencies (90–105%) were determined using standard curves. Relative gene expression levels were calculated using the 2^(-ΔΔCT) method18.

Statistical analysis

All data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) of three biological replicates. Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS software (version 16). Data were subjected to one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), and significant differences among treatment means were compared using the Least Significant Difference (LSD) test at a probability level of p < 0.05. Graphs were prepared using Microsoft Excel.

Results and discussion

TPH removal efficiency and distribution in the plant-soil system

Vetiver grass demonstrated a remarkable capacity to enhance the removal of total petroleum hydrocarbons (TPH) from contaminated soil over a six-month period. While natural attenuation in unplanted pots accounted for 42–52% TPH reduction, the presence of vetiver significantly (p < 0.05) increased the removal efficiency to 85–93% across all contamination levels (Figs. 1 and 2). These results suggest a significant contribution of plant-associated processes, likely involving rhizosphere microbial stimulation and plant uptake, to enhanced hydrocarbon dissipation19 (Moradi et al. 2021). Interestingly, the highest percentage removal (93%) was observed at the 25% contamination level, suggesting a potential stimulatory effect or a potential microbial–plant interaction at moderate stress. However, the absolute mass of TPH removed was greatest at the highest (100%) contamination level (Fig. 1), highlighting vetiver’s considerable tolerance and clean-up potential even under severe pollution.

Analysis of plant tissues revealed a strong compartmentalization of hydrocarbons. TPH accumulation was predominantly localized in the roots, showing an increase of up to 124% over control levels in the 100% treatment, while accumulation in the aerial parts remained minimal (< 15% increase) (Fig. 3). This pattern is consistent with a common phytostrategy to limit the translocation of toxicants to photosynthetic tissues, likely involving sequestration, partial degradation, or immobilization within root structures20.

Integrated antioxidant and detoxification response

Exposure to TPH triggered a coordinated and concentration-dependent upregulation of the plant’s antioxidant defense system. The activity of key enzymatic antioxidants increased sequentially along the ROS detoxification pathway. Superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity rose progressively, peaking at the 50% TPH level (Fig. 4), indicating an initial surge in superoxide radical (O₂⁻) generation. This was followed by a significant elevation in the activities of catalase (CAT) and ascorbate peroxidase (APX) (Figs. 5 and 6), which are responsible for scavenging the hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) produced by SOD, thereby preventing the formation of more harmful hydroxyl radicals.

Concurrently, the glutathione redox system was activated. We observed a significant decrease in the reduced-to-oxidized glutathione ratio (GSH/GSSG) with increasing TPH concentration (Fig. 7), a classic indicator of cellular oxidative stress. As shown in Figs. 8 and 9, the decrease in the GSH/GSSG ratio reflects the combined effect of declining GSH (Fig. 7) and increasing GSSG levels (Fig. 8), providing an integrated indicator of cellular redox imbalance under TPH stress. Crucially, this redox disturbance was accompanied by a strong, concentration-dependent induction of the *Glutathione S-Transferase (GSTF2)* gene, particularly in the roots (Fig. 10). GSTs play a dual role: they conjugate glutathione to xenobiotics for detoxification and also exhibit glutathione peroxidase activity. The co-occurrence of GSH depletion, GSSG accumulation, and GST induction suggests a concerted metabolic effort where glutathione is actively utilized for both the direct conjugation of hydrocarbon-derived electrophiles and the enzymatic reduction of organic peroxides, indicating a role in stress-associated detoxification responses in the roots.

Induction of the phenylpropanoid pathway and secondary metabolism

The phenylpropanoid pathway, a major source of defensive secondary metabolites, was profoundly activated. The expression of the pivotal gene *Phenylalanine Ammonia-Lyase (PAL3)* was sharply upregulated in response to TPH, with a much stronger induction in roots than in shoots (Fig. 13), mirroring the pattern of hydrocarbon accumulation. This transcriptional activation translated directly into a massive biosynthesis of phenolic and flavonoid compounds. Total phenolic content increased by up to 5-fold and flavonoids by up to 3.5-fold in the highest treatment compared to the control (Figs. 11 and 12).

This coordinated response is mechanistically significant. PAL catalyzes the first committed step in phenylpropanoid metabolism. Its induction leads to the production of a wide array of compounds, including the observed soluble phenolics and flavonoids. These metabolites serve multiple protective functions: they act as potent non-enzymatic antioxidants, scavenging various ROS; they may contribute to cell wall fortification (e.g., via lignin); and they may be exuded into the rhizosphere, potentially influencing microbial communities, potentially enriching hydrocarbon degraders21,22. The strong root-specific induction of PAL aligns with the role of this organ as the primary site of pollutant interaction and stress perception.

Conclusions

In this study, TPH removal in planted soils reached 85–93%, compared to 42–52% in unplanted controls, indicating a substantial enhancement associated with the presence of vetiver plants. Petroleum hydrocarbon exposure induced a clear oxidative stress response, reflected by increased activities of SOD, CAT, and APX enzymes, along with a decline in the GSH/GSSG ratio. In parallel, significant upregulation of GST and PAL genes was observed, particularly in roots, accompanied by increased accumulation of phenolic and flavonoid compounds. These results collectively suggest that vetiver responds to hydrocarbon stress through coordinated physiological and molecular adjustments. While the findings support its potential use in phytoremediation, further studies are required to elucidate underlying mechanisms and validate performance under field conditions.
