Secondary Carlevale Intraocular Lens implantation combined with 25-gauge pars plana vitrectomy and sulfur hexafluoride (SF6) for retinal detachment: a retrospective study
Authors: Francesco Pignatelli, Alfredo Niro, Roberta Scarci, Pasquale Viggiano, Cristiana Iaculli, Giuseppe Addabbo, Francesco Boscia, Gianluca Besozzi, Ermete Giancipoli
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTo evaluate the functional and anatomical outcomes, and the risk of intraocular lens (IOL) decentration and opacification, in patients with rhegmatogenous retinal detachment (RRD) and secondary aphakia undergoing 25-gauge pars plana vitrectomy (PPV) with intravitreal gas tamponade and trans-scleral plug hydrophilic IOL scleral fixation.MethodsAphakic patients who underwent IOL scleral fixation combined with 25-G PPV and 20% sulfur hexafluoride (SF6) gas tamponade for RRD were included in this retrospective, uncontrolled study. A single-piece hydrophilic acrylic trans-scleral plug-fixated IOL was implanted. Patients were instructed to maintain face-down position for seven days postoperatively. Comprehensive clinical evaluations, including visual acuity, refraction, macular optical coherence tomography (OCT), and anterior/posterior segment assessments, were conducted at baseline and at 1, 3, 6, and 12 months, and at the final visit.ResultsEleven eyes from 11 patients were included in the study. The primary retinal reattachment rate was 100%. Mean baseline best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA; 0.69 ± 0.62 LogMAR, Snellen 20/98) significantly improved to 0.23 logMAR (Snellen 20/34) at last visit. Central retinal thickness (CRT) remained stable throughout the entire follow-up. The mean final spherical equivalent refraction was − 0.18 ± 0.73 diopters. No cases of IOL dislocation or opacification were observed during the follow-up period.ConclusionThis procedure demonstrated satisfactory functional and anatomical results with few complications, including no significant incidence of IOL opacification or displacement observed during the follow-up period.Keywords: Retinal detachment, Carlevale IOL, IOL opacification, Scleral fixated intraocular lens, Small-gauge pars plana vitrectomy

Background

Crystalline lens-capsular bag complex luxation and rhegmatogenous retinal detachment (RRD) represent two major complications that may occur concurrently or subsequently as a consequence of blunt ocular trauma or complicated cataract surgery [1, 2]. Factors associated with intrinsic zonular weakness, such as pseudoexfoliation (PEX), previous vitreoretinal surgery, axial myopia, uveitis, retinitis pigmentosa, mature cataract, previous episodes of acute angle-closure attack, and connective tissue disorders, can increase the risk of intraoperative or post-traumatic capsular bag dislocation [3–5]. Patients who experience an intraoperative complication associated with vitreous loss, including posterior capsule tear or zonular dehiscence, are at an increased risk of developing a RRD after surgery [2, 6].

Aphakic patients presenting with RRD typically require prompt intervention involving pars plana vitrectomy (PPV) with endotamponade and secondary intraocular lens (IOL) implantation. Secondary IOL placement in the absence of an adequate capsular support can be achieved through various techniques, including anterior chamber IOLs (AC-IOLs), iris-fixated IOLs (IF- IOLs), or scleral- fixated IOLs (SF-IOLs) [7]. Several scleral fixation techniques have been described over the past few years. Flanged intrascleral fixation of a three-piece IOL with double-needle technique [8] and 4 point scleral fixation of a single-piece acrylic IOL with prolene or Gore-Tex sutures [9, 10]. have shown promising anatomical and functional results, although their clinical application should be balanced with their off-label use. This led to the development of the trans-scleral plugs fixated FIL Sutureless scleral-fixated (SSF) IOL (Soleko, Italy), a single-piece foldable hydrophilic acrylic IOL with an optic diameter of 6.5 mm, UV filter, 25% H2O content, and a unique self-blocking mechanism that allows a suture-less insertion into the posterior chamber. FIL SSF IOL is secured to the sclera through its two flexible T-shaped harpoons protruding from the closed apices and covered by scleral flaps [11, 12].

The FIL SSF IOL has been associated with excellent visual and refractive outcomes, but the risk of postoperative IOL opacification remains a potential concern [13, 14]. The hydrophilic nature of the scleral-fixated IOL may lead to the development of opacities, especially in eyes where the IOL comes into direct contact with tamponade agents like air or gas during the early postoperative period [15, 16].

Additionally, IOL dislocation can occur in the early post-surgical period, due to the mechanical force exerted by the intravitreal gas bubble, which may be a concern for eyes undergoing a combined procedure for aphakia correction and RRD repair with FIL SSF IOL, PPV, and gas tamponade. Limited evidence exists on the risk of opacification and decentration of a FIL SSF IOL in eyes with gas endotamponade [7]. This study primarily aimed to assess the visual and anatomical outcomes, along with the risk of intraocular lens opacification in relation to postoperative positioning, following transcleral plug-fixated IOL implantation combined with pars plana vitrectomy and gas tamponade in patients with RRD and aphakia. Additionally, we investigated the procedure’s refractive outcomes and assessed the risks of IOL tilting, decentration, and other postoperative complications.

Methods

Study design

Medical records of patients who underwent 25-gauge (25-G) PPV with sulfur hexafluoride (SF6) 20% gas endotamponade, combined with trans-scleral plugs fixated FIL SSF IOL implant, for RRD and aphakia in the absence of capsular support, were retrospectively reviewed in this single center, uncontrolled study. All surgical procedures were carried out at “S.S. Annunziata Hospital” Eye Clinic in Taranto, Italy, between January 2022 and June 2023. The study adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki, and all patients provided informed consent for surgery. By signing the consent form, patients also authorized the use of their anonymized data for future scientific purposes. The Local Authority of Taranto IRB approved this retrospective study protocol and informed consent in 2025. Considering that all patients were participating in the Taranto retrospective cohort of the Tramontana Study, funded by Horizon EU Project MISTRAL, the study protocol was also approved by the local ethical committee “Gabriella Serio” of the IRCCS Istituto Oncologico, study protocol number 2517/CEL in 2023.

To be included, patients were required to meet the following criteria: age 18 or older, a detailed surgical procedure record, at least 12 months of follow-up, comprehensive clinical data at baseline, 1, 3, 6, 12 months, and the final visit. This included best corrected visual acuity (BCVA) assessed with ETDR charts, preoperative optical biometry, automated refraction (Auto Ref/Keratometer ARK-F, Nidek CO., LTD.), intraocular pressure (IOP) measurement (Goldman applanation tonometry), detailed descriptions of the anterior and posterior segments. Optical biometry (IOL Master 700, Zeiss), performed before the original cataract surgery, was used in all cases. The SRK/T, Hoffer Q were selected as power calculation formulas for Carlevale IOL, with no specific refractive adjustments applied. Refractive outcomes included mean final refraction (spherical equivalent and refractive astigmatism), mean prediction error (PE, defined as the difference between the planned refraction and the final spherical equivalent) and percentage of eyes within ± 0.50 and ± 1.00 diopters (D). Spectral domain optical coherence tomography (SD-OCT) of the macula was required at baseline (in patients with macula-on RRD only) and at 1, 3, 6,12 months, and last visit to evaluate eventual macular changes. Eventual intraoperative and early and late postoperative complications had to be reported in patients’ charts.

Pupil-dilated slit-lamp photographs and anterior segment OCT (AS-OCT) scans obtained at 1 month and at the final visit were used to assess postoperative IOL centration, tilt, and transparency (Fig. 1). AS-OCT scans were routinely acquired in patients undergoing sutureless scleral fixation of intraocular lenses at our clinic and were performed according to a previously described protocol [17]. Intraocular lens decentration and tilt were evaluated both qualitatively and quantitatively. Qualitative assessment was performed by slit-lamp examination to identify any obvious IOL displacement or tilt relative to the pupil. Quantitative analysis using AS-OCT measured IOL tilt as the angle between the IOL plane and a reference plane [17]. IOL transparency and opacification were assessed using slit-lamp examination to identify surface deposits, subsurface changes, or internal clouding, with AS-OCT providing objective confirmation and documentation of material changes suggestive of opacification.

Each patient was instructed to observe a strict face-down position for 7 days after the surgery, followed by a semi-sitting position for an additional 15 days. This patient positioning information was reported in the clinical records. Major exclusion criteria for the study included inability to sign informed consent, the use of alternative secondary IOL-fixation techniques, the use of silicone oil as tamponade agent, missing relevant clinical data in patients’ charts, and a follow-up period of less than 12 months.

Surgical procedure

All surgeries were performed by the same experienced surgeon (FP) under peribulbar anesthesia with 4 cc of Ropivacaine and 1 cc of Lidocaine. A 5% povidone-iodine solution (Oftasteril, Alfa Intes Industria Terapeutica Splendore S.r.l., Naples, Italy) was applied to the cornea, conjunctival sac, and periocular skin for three minutes preoperatively. A localized conjunctival peritomy was first performed on the nasal and temporal sides of the eye, followed by the creation of two partial-thickness scleral flaps, each measuring 4 × 4 mm, along the horizontal meridian, 180° apart (Fig. 2a). Next, 25-G three-port PPV was performed using the Constellation® Vision System (Alcon Laboratories, Fort Worth, TX). The central core and peripheral vitreous were removed in all cases using a cut rate of 7500 cuts per minute and linear aspiration ranging from 0 to 650 mmHg (Fig. 2b). Perfluorocarbon liquid (PFCL) was then injected into the vitreous cavity to drain the subretinal fluid and stabilize the posterior retina. A 23-G sclerotomy, positioned 1.75 mm posterior to the limbus, was created beneath each scleral flap. Whenever possible, the previous clear corneal incision was reopened; otherwise, a fresh 2.75-mm clear corneal incision was made at 12 o’clock. Any capsular bag remnant was carefully removed using vitreoretinal forceps and the vitrectomy probe before proceeding with IOL implantation. The Soleko FIL SSF lens (Carlevale lens) was then implanted. After its injection into the anterior chamber (AC), the leading haptic was grasped with a 23-G vitreoretinal forceps and externalized through the corresponding 23-G sclerotomy. The same step was replicated for the trailing haptic on the opposite side (Fig. 2c). The scleral flaps and clear corneal incision were sutured with 8 − 0 Vicryl and 10 − 0 Nylon sutures, respectively. The surgery was completed with a fluid-air exchange, complete removal of PFCL, endolaser treatment around retinal breaks, and injection of 20% SF6. The sclerotomies and conjunctiva were then closed using 8 − 0 Vicryl sutures (Fig. 2d-f). Encircling bands were not employed in any case. All patients were instructed to maintain a face-down position for at least seven days postoperatively, followed by a semi-sitting position for an additional 15 days.

Study outcomes

The primary outcomes assessed were the retinal reattachment rate following a single procedure, the mean BCVA change observed throughout the follow-up period, and the IOL opacification rate recorded at the last visit. Secondary outcomes included the mean final refraction, the mean prediction error, the incidence of myopic or hyperopic surprise, the degree of IOL tilting, and the rate of all postoperative complications.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics number (percentage), median (interquartile range), or media ± SD were used, as appropriate. No formal sample size calculation was performed, due to the convenience sampling and low number of patients. The Friedman’s two way analysis test was used to assess the changes in BCVA and CRT. Post hoc analysis for pairwise comparisons was done with the Conover method. The statistical analysis was performed using MedCalc® Statistical Software version 20.008 (MedCalc Software Ltd, Ostend, Belgium; https://www.medcalc.org; 2021). Because this is an interventional noncomparative case series, all p values were considered for descriptive purposes. A p value of 0.05 was statistically significant.

Results

A total of 21 patients who underwent small-gauge PPV and secondary IOL implantation with sutureless scleral fixation for RRD and secondary aphakia were initially identified. Ten patients were excluded due to alternative surgical techniques (4 cases), use of silicone oil tamponade (4 cases), or loss to follow-up before 6 months (2 cases). Eleven eyes of 11 patients met the inclusion criteria and were analyzed.

The main causes of aphakia and RRD were complicated cataract surgery (9 eyes, 81.8%), blunt trauma with lens luxation (1 eye, 9%), and aborted phacoemulsification due to severe pseudoexfoliation syndrome (1 eye, 9%). The mean interval between the causative event and RRD presentation was 6.3 days (range 2–15). Six patients (54.5%) presented with macula-on RRD, and most eyes (9/11) had a single retinal tear, while 2 had multiple tears. The mean follow-up period was 18 months (range 12–24). One patient had early postoperative hypotony (IOP 7 mmHg), resolving after 3 weeks of medical therapy (dexamethasone drops + atropine). Two patients developed post-operative macular edema at 1 month, which resolved completely with topical therapy within 1 month (Bromfenac drops, 2 times a day). No other intraoperative or postoperative complications were reported.

Discussion

The present retrospective study investigated the long-term outcomes of trans-scleral plugs fixated FIL SSF IOL implantation in patients with aphakia and retinal detachment who underwent small-gauge PPV with gas tamponade and scleral IOL fixation. The 18-month results suggested the procedure is associated with significant visual improvement, without recurrent retinal detachment, IOL opacification, or displacement.

Vitreous prolapse into the anterior chamber may generate significant traction on the retina, increasing the risk of retinal detachment in eyes that experienced crystalline lens dislocation secondary to a trauma or a complicated cataract surgery [18].

Prompt PPV with intravitreal tamponade and IOL scleral fixation may represent a feasible option for aphakic patients with RRD and insufficient capsular support. Sutureless scleral fixated IOLs, like trans-scleral plugs fixated FIL SSF IOL, provide a strong barrier between the anterior chamber and posterior segment, minimize tissue manipulation and surgical time, avoid suture issues, increase stability, prevent tilting and iris capture, and offer excellent results with faster recovery [11, 17, 19].

Scleral fixation of the SFIOL was performed under perfluorocarbon liquid (PFCL) in all cases, providing distinct advantages over performing the maneuver in a fluid-filled eye or after fluid–air or fluid–gas exchange. In a fluid-filled eye, increased retinal mobility may raise the risk of intraoperative retinal damage during IOL manipulation. Following fluid–air or fluid–gas exchange, the combination of reduced visualization, high surface tension, and potential migration of air or gas into the anterior chamber can complicate surgical maneuvers. PFCL effectively flattens and stabilizes the retina while maintaining optimal intraoperative visualization, facilitating a safer and technically smoother procedure.

Mean BCVA significantly improved from baseline to month one and continued to steadily improve until the last visit. Patients experienced a mean gain of about 25 ETDRS letters by the end of the follow up. The presence of a macular off retinal detachment in 5 patients (about 45% of the population) might justify the suboptimal visual recovery in the overall cohort.

No cases of redetachment were reported through the entire course of the follow up.

The hydrophilic FIL SSF IOL may develop opacification over time. Studies have shown IOL opacification is a common reason for IOL explantation, often due to increased aqueous calcium levels leading to microcrystal deposition on the IOL surface, particularly with hydrophilic acrylic IOLs. Gas tamponade and inflammation may trigger this process, causing IOL dehydration and opacification. Opacification can occur rapidly or as a late complication, as observed in cases with an intact capsular bag [20, 21, 22].

The lack of a posterior capsule can allow direct contact between the gas and IOL surface, increasing the risk of IOL calcification. This may occur with posterior capsulotomy or the use of a hydrophilic acrylic IOL for scleral fixation [21]. Patel et al. found opacification of a scleral-fixated Akreos AO60 IOL using Gore-Tex sutures, regardless of the tamponade agent. Additionally, two more cases of scleral-fixated Akreos AO60 IOL opacification after vitrectomy with gas tamponade have been reported [23, 24].

Few reports describe FIL SSF IOL opacification. Coco et al. reported a case after Descemet stripping, where multiple air rebubblings were needed [25]. Danese et al. described two cases of transient intraoperative clouding that resolved spontaneously [26]. Iannetta et al. reported a case with significant VA reduction 6 months after PPV using SF6 for traumatic lens luxation. The hydrophilic lens material and gas injection during implantation were proposed as risk factors for opacification. The authors concluded that procedures involving intraocular air or gas were relevant risks [7].

No significant IOL opacification was observed in our patients, over a mean 18-months follow-up. Mean BCVA significantly improved postoperatively and continued to improve, suggesting no patients developed opacification affecting visual acuity. Additionally, anterior segment photos also did not show evidence of IOL opacification. The employed postoperative regimen, including strict prone positioning for 7 days, semi-sitting for 15 days, and SF6 use, is hypothesized to reduce gas-IOL contact, potentially mitigating opacification risk.

A hybrid hydrophilic/hydrophobic version of the Carlevale IOL has recently been released to the market (Carlevale IOL High-Tech. Md-tech, Casoria, Italia). The compound material comprising the IOL is theoretically less prone to opacification, even in the presence of intraocular gas. Therefore, this new version should be preferred over the hydrophilic one whenever available for combined procedures involving the use of intraocular gas [27].

The mean final refraction and prediction error were − 0.18 and − 0.03, respectively. Preoperative refractive astigmatism was not analyzed, as all eyes were aphakic prior to surgery and manifest cylinder measurements were considered unreliable and not directly comparable with postoperative pseudophakic refraction. Postoperative refractive astigmatism was mild and clinically acceptable, consistent with the good refractive predictability observed in this cohort. Refractive surprises within 1D, either myopic or hyperopic, were reported in 27.3% and 45.45% of patients. A myopic shift greater than 1D occurred in one case. These results are consistent with the literature for FIL SSF IOL [13, 28, 29].

In our study, these refractive deviations were managed conservatively, as they generally fell within an acceptable range that did not necessitate surgical intervention or IOL exchange. The primary goal was stable visual rehabilitation, and while some refractive error remained, it was deemed tolerable by the patients or correctable with spectacles if needed.

Intraocular lens power calculation may be challenging in eyes with cataract and macula-involving RRD scheduled for a combined procedure. Significant underestimation of axial length was observed when using preoperative optical biometry, leading to a myopic shift from overestimated IOL power [30]. Stratification by macular status did not reveal significant differences in final refractive outcomes (spherical equivalent and postoperative astigmatism), suggesting that macular involvement may not substantially influence refractive accuracy. Nevertheless, the small sample size limits the strength of this observation.

The achievement of a refractive outcome within ± 1.0 D in the majority of eyes represents a clinically relevant finding in the context of scleral-fixated IOL implantation. This level of accuracy may be attributed to the availability of reliable pre-detachment optical biometry and the use of SRK/T and Hoffer Q formulas, which appear to provide greater refractive stability for the Carlevale IOL design. In contrast, newer generation formulas such as Barrett Universal II may be more susceptible to effective lens position variability in scleral fixation, potentially limiting their predictive accuracy. Collectively, these factors likely contributed to the low mean prediction error observed in the present series.

The mean IOL tilt was around 2°, as measured with AS-OCT, with no cases of IOL dislocation and pupillary capture in our cohort. Patients with a scleral fixated IOL and gas endotamponade are at higher risk of developing IOL tilting and pupillary capture during the early postoperative period, secondary to the forward pressure generated by the gas bubble [13, 31]. The specific design of Carlevale IOL, with its T-shaped harpoons, four scleral sulcus counterpressure points, and a 10° anterior angulation of the haptics, may have contributed to minimize the risk of IOL displacement and iris capture [14, 31].

In our series, only 20% SF6 was used, with complete absorption occurring at a mean of 15 days, and IOL tilting remained minimal after gas resolution. In contrast, longer-acting gases such as C3F8 could prolong intraocular tamponade, potentially increasing the risk of temporary IOL tilt or displacement in the early postoperative period. Extended gas presence may also affect IOP, which could influence lens stability. These considerations underscore the importance of careful monitoring when using longer-lasting tamponades in sutureless scleral-fixated IOL surgery.

A stepwise approach with initial RRD repair followed by delayed IOL implantation may be considered in aphakic eyes; however, this strategy delays visual rehabilitation and exposes patients to additional surgical risk. Moreover, aphakic eyes undergoing PPV with gas tamponade are prone to early postoperative complications, including anterior chamber shallowing, gas migration, and secondary angle closure with IOP elevation [32, 33].

Although IOL power calculation is challenging in macula-involving RRD due to axial length underestimation and potential myopic shift, most eyes in our series became aphakic following previous complicated cataract surgery, allowing reliable preoperative biometric data to be used. This likely reduced the risk of refractive surprise and supports the rationale for a combined surgical approach in selected cases.

No major complications like recurrent RRD, severe postoperative inflammation, or sustained IOP increase were reported. Two patients developed post-surgical macular edema that resolved with medical therapy in one month. Mild hypotony, without choroidal detachment or macular folds, was seen in one patient and it resolved after 3 weeks with topical therapy. Although leaking sclerotomes are presumed the main mechanism for severe postoperative hypotony after FIL SSF IOL fixation, we believe it was not the case here since sclerotomies were systematically sutured.

The present study has several limitations, including small sample size, lack of a control group, and relatively short follow-up and the retrospective design. The low number of patients may limit detecting the true IOL opacification rate. While our observations suggest that strict face-down positioning might contribute to a reduced risk of opacification, we acknowledge that similar outcomes with alternative or no postoperative positioning strategies cannot be definitively ruled out without comparative studies. Although an 18-month follow-up may appear sufficient, some reports suggest that opacification can occur at later stages. A longer follow-up period may therefore be advisable to more accurately capture the true incidence of IOL opacification over time [22] Although anterior segment photography and AS-OCT allowed for detailed assessment of IOL transparency and positioning, increasing the robustness of our findings, the retrospective study design constitutes an inherent limitation compared to prospective studies.

In conclusion, the combination of Carlevale IOL implantation with 25-gauge Pars Plana Vitrectomy and gas tamponade for retinal detachment repair and aphakia correction yielded satisfactory functional and anatomical outcomes. Notably, no significant IOL opacification or displacement was observed in our patient cohort over the mean 18-month follow-up period. Mean BCVA significantly improved postoperatively and continued to improve, with no patients developing opacification affecting visual acuity, further supported by anterior segment photographic evidence. Retina reattachment was achieved after a single procedure in all patients, with no recurrence. These findings suggest that this combined surgical approach can achieve favorable results with respect to IOL stability and transparency. Future studies with larger sample sizes, longer follow-up periods, and control groups are necessary to further validate these findings and to explore the specific factors contributing to the observed outcomes.
