Associations Between Cumulative Head Trauma and Self‐Reported Parkinsonism and Parkinson's Disease in Former Soccer Players
Authors: Annalise E. Miner, Matthew J. Kmiecik, Niharika Namburi, Nathaniel Dunckley, Charles H. Adler, Erika D. Driver‐Dunckley, Christopher J. Nowinski, Joshua Gottesman, Yorghos Tripodis, Robert A. Stern, Ann C. McKee, Thor D. Stein, Jesse Mez, Farren B.S. Briggs, Michael L. Alosco
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCumulative head trauma, encompassing repetitive head impacts (RHI) and traumatic brain injury (TBI), has been associated with later‐life neurodegenerative disease. Studies on parkinsonism/Parkinson's disease (PD) have evaluated RHI and TBI in isolation, yielding mixed results. Cumulative head trauma may better predict PD risk.ObjectiveTo examine independent and cumulative effects of RHI through soccer participation and TBI history on PD diagnosis and age at diagnosis.MethodsWe used a cross‐sectional cohort design with data from the Fox Insight study. Participants completed self‐report questionnaires on PD diagnosis, TBI (no TBI, TBI with loss of consciousness [LOC], TBI without LOC), and sport participation. Multivariable logistic regressions examined the effects of soccer, TBI group, and their interaction on PD diagnosis. Cox proportional hazards examined associations with time to PD diagnosis. Covariates included age, sex, race, education, income, body mass index, heart disease/diabetes, and family PD history.ResultsAmong 1231 participants (mean[SD] age = 66.12[10.60]), 278 played soccer (953 non–contact and collision sport [CCS] athletes). Participants reported no TBI (390[33%]), TBI without LOC (495[43%]), or TBI with LOC (258[23%]). Overall, 768(62%) reported a PD diagnosis (mean[SD] age at diagnosis = 59.02[9.86]). A significant soccer × TBI interaction indicated that soccer players with TBI with LOC had 2.6× higher odds of PD compared to non‐CCS athletes without TBI (OR [95% CI] = 2.600[1.085–6.305], P = 0.033). Former soccer players with TBI with LOC had a 15.6% higher hazard of early PD diagnosis (HR = 1.469, P = 0.027) compared to non‐CCS athletes with no TBI.ConclusionCumulative head trauma (RHI + TBI) increased PD risk and lowered age at onset. © 2026 The Author(s). Parkinson's disease (PD) is a neurodegenerative disorder characterized by motor (e.g., bradykinesia) and nonmotor (e.g., mood disorders) symptoms. 1 , 2 , 3 Risk factors include age and male sex, 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 pesticide and toxicant exposure, 8 , 9 cardiovascular disease, 10 , 11 and genetic–environmental interactions. 12 Traumatic brain injury (TBI) is an additional risk factor for PD. 13 Epidemiological studies have found that history of TBI, particularly with loss of consciousness (LOC), is associated with increased risk for PD. 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 However, findings have been inconsistent, with some studies reporting no associations. 18 , 19 Differences in the severity and frequency of TBI may explain conflicting results.

Repetitive head impact (RHI) exposure has emerged as a contributor to later‐life neurodegenerative disease. 20 , 21 , 22 , 23 , 24 RHIs are defined as recurrent blows or hits to the head, including concussive and nonconcussive impacts, which commonly occur through contact and collision sports (CCS) such as American football and soccer. 25 , 26 RHI exposure is the primary environmental risk factor for chronic traumatic encephalopathy (CTE). 25 , 27 , 28 RHI has also been associated with parkinsonism and PD, though the literature is mixed. 23 , 24 , 29 , 30

While most research investigating RHI and neurological outcomes, including PD, has focused on American football, 23 soccer offers an important context in which to study RHI exposure. Soccer can involve RHI through heading and player collisions. 31 Research investigating soccer and PD has yielded mixed results. A retrospective analysis of professional soccer players reported twofold higher PD risk compared with matched controls, 32 whereas a Swedish cohort study found no such associations. 30 In both studies, PD diagnoses were made by medical professionals. Further study of soccer‐related head trauma is needed.

Given mixed findings from studies examining RHI and TBI individually, recent work has begun to consider cumulative head trauma, defined as the combined effects of both exposures. Participants with high RHI exposure and a history of TBI performed worse on measures of memory and executive function than those with either exposure alone. 33 Examining RHI or TBI in isolation may underestimate their impact, and cumulative head trauma may be a more sensitive indicator of long‐term consequences.

The objective of this study was to examine the independent and cumulative effects of RHI through soccer participation and TBI history on PD diagnosis and age at diagnosis. This study had two aims: (1) to evaluate the independent and cumulative effects of soccer participation and TBI history on later‐life parkinsonism and PD diagnosis, and (2) to evaluate the independent and cumulative associations on age at parkinsonism and PD diagnosis. Using data from the Fox Insight study, we analyzed 278 former soccer players and 953 non‐CCS athletes who completed online self‐report questionnaires on RHI and TBI exposure history, PD diagnosis, and overall health. 34 We hypothesized that RHI and TBI would have synergistic effects, increasing the odds of PD diagnosis and modifying the timing of disease onset.

Methods

Participants and Study Design

The sample included participants enrolled in the Fox Insight study, with a full description of study protocols described elsewhere. 34 , 35 The Fox Insight study is an ongoing longitudinal study operated by The Michael J. Fox Foundation that collects self‐reported data from participants with and without parkinsonism or PD (https://ror.org/03arq3225). Participants aged 18 or older are recruited through online, volunteer‐based enrollment, and complete longitudinal and cross‐sectional questionnaires. Recruitment channels include online (e.g., social network ads) and real‐world (e.g., clinician referrals) efforts. 35 This analysis utilized a cross‐sectional cohort derived from participants' most recent study visit to maximize inclusion of participants who completed the cross‐sectional Boston University Repetitive Head Impact Exposure Assessment (BU‐RHIEA) questionnaire, and to ensure up‐to‐date self‐reported PD diagnostic status. The BU‐RHIEA was introduced into Fox Insight in November 2021, 4 years after enrollment began, to collect data regarding CCS participation and RHI. All Fox Insight participants were invited to complete the questionnaire via the study website (http://www.foxinsight.org).

Parkinsonism and Parkinson's Disease Diagnoses

PD diagnostic status was determined by participants' responses to the initial visit yes/no question, “Do you currently have a diagnosis of Parkinson's disease, or parkinsonism, by a physician or other health care professional?” At each annual follow‐up, participants without a prior PD diagnosis were asked, “Since your last visit, have you been diagnosed with Parkinson's disease by a physician or other health care professional?” Participants who previously indicated a PD diagnosis were asked, “Since your last visit, has a physician or other health care professional changed your diagnosis of Parkinson's disease?” This analysis used the most recent response. The single time‐point question “At what age were you diagnosed?” determined the age at diagnosis.

For the purposes of this study, “PD” refers to self‐reported Parkinson's disease and/or parkinsonism diagnoses unless otherwise specified. When discussing prior literature, “Parkinson's disease” refers specifically to clinically defined PD, and distinctions between PD and parkinsonism are noted where relevant.

Participant Group Determination

The BU‐RHIEA assessed participation in contact and non‐contact sports. The BU‐RHIEA asks, “Have you ever participated in organized sports, which includes membership on a team with scheduled practices and games (excluding pick‐up or neighborhood games)?” If a participant responded “yes,” the BU‐RHIEA assessed CCS history.

Participants were required to have no exposure to military exercises that could result in significant RHI exposure, including combatant training, counter‐improvised explosive device (IED) training, breaching training, and combat. 36 , 37 , 38 These questions are included in the Supporting Information. Participants who answered “yes” to the question “Did you ever serve in the United States (U.S.) military” but “no” to exclusionary activities were included to maximize sample size.

Former Soccer Players

Soccer participation was identified from organized sport participants who confirmed, “Did you play organized soccer at any level, which includes membership on a team with scheduled practices (not including pick‐up or neighborhood games)?” The BU‐RHIEA collected information on metrics of RHI exposure (age of first exposure, total years of play, heading). Additional information is provided in the Supporting Information. Former soccer players were excluded if they reported participation in football, hockey, or boxing due to their independent associations with CTE. 39 , 40 , 41

Non‐CCS Athletes

Non‐CCS athletes were organized sport participants who answered “no” to CCS participation, which included soccer, football, hockey, and boxing. Non‐athletes were not included to facilitate a more accurate comparison group to former soccer players in terms of cardiometabolic, physical, and mental health. 42 , 43 , 44

TBI History

An adapted Ohio State University TBI Identification Method form determined lifetime TBI history and group. TBI groups included: no TBI, TBI without LOC, and TBI with LOC. History of hospitalization from a TBI was also assessed, along with the source of TBI.

Sample Derivation

At the time of analysis, 4100 participants reported organized sport participation. Of these, 4031 participants responded to the question on organized soccer participation. The sample was reduced due to 2166 participants not providing relevant demographic data (i.e., self‐reported racial identity, education, current household income, family history of PD, etc.), 1 participant giving a non‐yes/no response to the soccer participation question, and 633 participants reporting participation either in other CCS (377 American football players, 150 hockey players, and 35 boxers) or participation in exclusionary military activities (n=71).

The final sample included 1231 participants, with 278 former soccer players and 953 non‐CCS athletes. Eighty‐eight participants (71 soccer, 17 non‐CCS) were missing data needed to determine the TBI group, leaving 1143 participants with TBI classification for related analyses. A participation selection flowchart is shown in Figure S1.

Statistical Analysis

Analyses controlled for age at survey, sex, self‐identified racial group, education, current household income, body mass index (BMI), history of heart disease and diabetes, and family history of PD. Racial group included white‐identifying, non‐white‐identifying, or those who preferred not to answer. Education was dichotomized into individuals who completed education up to and including an associate's degree and those who completed a bachelor's degree and higher. BMI was calculated using height (centimeters) and weight (kilograms). Sex and history of heart disease, diabetes, and PD were binary. Current household income was reported in yearly dollar ranges.

Means and standard deviations (SD) summarized sample characteristics for continuous variables and counts and percentages for categorical variables. Wilcoxon rank‐sum tests assessed group differences for continuous variables and Pearson's χ2 tests for categorical variables. Statistical significance was defined as P ≤ 0.05. Analyses were conducted using R statistical software (version 4.4.3). 45

Soccer Participation, TBI History, and Parkinsonism and Parkinson's Disease Diagnosis

Logistic regression models examined the effects of soccer participation, TBI group, and their interaction on PD diagnosis. Models were stratified by soccer participation status to clarify group interaction effects. Sensitivity analyses repeated models in age‐stratified subgroups (participants ≥60 years, participants <60 years) to determine age‐related effects. Odds ratios [OR] were calculated by exponentiating the estimated log‐odds, and 95% confidence intervals (CI) were calculated using the profile likelihood method.

In former soccer players, associations of RHI exposure metrics (highest level played, position group at highest level played, age of first exposure, total years of play, heading) with PD diagnosis were assessed in separate multivariable logistic regression models.

Age at Parkinsonism and Parkinson's Disease Diagnosis: Survival Analyses

Multiple survival analyses were conducted using Cox proportional hazards models. Analyses examined the associations of(1) soccer participation status, (2) TBI group, and(3) cumulative soccer × TBI group variable with time to PD diagnosis in separate models. A sensitivity analysis examined the association of soccer participation with time to PD diagnosis in only participants who reported age at PD diagnosis. To provide clinically interpretable estimates of differences in PD diagnosis timing, restricted mean survival time (RMST) analyses were conducted for cumulative soccer × TBI group survival analyses. RMST differences were estimated using age as the time scale and were calculated up to a prespecified truncation time (τ = 80 years). Additional models stratified by soccer participation status to clarify TBI group effects. Kaplan–Meier curves visualized survival differences across groups.

Time‐to‐event was defined as age at PD diagnosis for participants who reported age at diagnosis, and as age at last follow‐up for PD participants who did not report a diagnosis age and for participants without PD. Event status was coded as 1 for PD diagnosis and 0 for censored observations, with age used as the underlying timescale.

Exposures (soccer participation, TBI history) were treated as fixed baseline characteristics, as study enrollment occurred after exposure and exact TBI timing was not available. Hazard ratios [HR] reflect associations conditional on survival to study enrollment without PD. Proportional hazards assumptions were tested using Schoenfeld residuals.

There was a significant interaction between soccer participation and TBI group (Table S3). Former soccer players with a history of TBI with LOC had >2.5 times higher odds of PD diagnosis compared to non‐CCS athletes without TBI (OR [95% CI] = 2.600 [1.085–6.305], P = 0.033). Former soccer players with a history of TBI without LOC did not have significantly higher odds of PD diagnosis. In this model, history of soccer participation was associated with decreased odds for PD diagnosis (OR [95% CI] = 0.476 [0.268–0.844], P = 0.011) relative to non‐CCS athletes. TBI group was not associated with PD diagnosis.

Follow‐up models stratified by soccer participation status found that among former soccer players, TBI with LOC was significantly associated with higher odds of PD diagnosis compared to no TBI (OR [95% CI] = 3.249 [1.472–7.440], P = 0.004; Table S4). In PD participants who reported age at diagnosis, soccer participation was associated with earlier age at diagnosis compared to non‐CCS athletes (HR = 1.34, P = 0.004). Linear regression models in the same stratified cohort demonstrated that soccer participation was associated with younger age at PD diagnosis compared to non‐CCS athletes (B = –5.39, P < 0.001; full data not shown).

There were no significant differences in time to PD diagnosis between TBI groups (Table S6). Kaplan–Meier curves depicting the relationship are shown in Figure 2 (log‐rank test: P‐value = 0.66). Proportional hazards assumptions were met.

RMST analyses demonstrated no significant differences in age at PD diagnosis between the reference group (no soccer, no TBI) and participants with either soccer or TBI history alone (Table S8). However, former soccer players with a history of TBI without LOC were diagnosed ~2.8 years earlier (P = 0.046), whereas former soccer players with TBI with LOC were diagnosed ~5.5 years earlier (P = 0.003).

In follow‐up models stratified by soccer participation status, TBI history was only associated with time to PD diagnosis in former soccer players. Specifically, when the sample was restricted to only former soccer players (n = 261), TBI with LOC was significantly associated with earlier time to PD diagnosis compared to no TBI (HR = 1.841, P = 0.009; Table S6). TBI without LOC was not associated with earlier time to PD diagnosis (HR = 1.489, P = 0.067) but showed a similar trend. Kaplan–Meier curves did not demonstrate significant differences across groups (log‐rank tests: P = 0.069; Fig. 3). Proportional hazards assumptions were met.

In contrast, among non‐CCS athletes (n = 881), neither TBI without LOC (HR = 0.967, P = 0.734) nor TBI with LOC (HR = 0.986, P = 0.904) was significantly associated with earlier time to PD diagnosis (Table S6). Kaplan–Meier curves did not show significant differences across groups (log‐rank test: P = 0.940; Fig. 3). Proportional hazards assumptions were met.

Discussion

This study examined the independent and cumulative effects of RHI through soccer participation and TBI history on PD diagnosis and age at diagnosis in participants from the Fox Insight study. 34 Cumulative head trauma was associated with increased PD risk and lowered age at onset. Soccer participation only conferred risk for PD among those with TBI history, with the strongest effects observed for TBI with LOC. Former soccer players with a history of TBI with LOC had more than two‐fold greater odds of a self‐reported parkinsonism or PD diagnosis and a 15.6% higher hazard of earlier diagnosis relative to non‐soccer players without TBI. These findings suggest that cumulative head trauma, rather than soccer participation or TBI history alone, may be a potential determinant of PD risk and course.

Soccer participation was associated with PD diagnosis only in the presence of lifetime TBI, especially TBI with LOC. This relationship persisted despite similar proportions of former soccer players to non‐CCS athletes across TBI history groups, mechanisms of injury, and number of lifetime head injuries requiring hospitalization. These findings align with prior research identifying TBI as a risk factor for PD. 15 , 16 , 17 , 46 For example, adults aged >55 presenting with TBI in an emergency department had a 44% higher risk of developing PD within 5 to 7 years compared to those with non‐TBI trauma. 15

Studies examining sports‐related head trauma and PD have reported mixed findings. In the Fox Insight cohort, longer duration and higher level of American football play were associated with greater odds of self‐reported PD diagnosis, even though most participants played at the youth or high school level, 23 whereas a mortality study of former National Football League players found no association with PD neuropathology, 47 highlighting diagnostic methodology differences. Similarly, retrospective health record and autopsy studies in former soccer players have reported increased risk of neurodegenerative disease and PD, 30 , 32 , 48 , 49 whereas others have not. 30 These inconsistencies suggest that neither RHI nor TBI alone fully explains PD risk. Instead, our findings suggest that cumulative head trauma may exert the greatest influence on PD incidence and onset, though findings may reflect differences in age, sex, and other comorbidities. Cumulative head trauma has the strongest associations with worsened performance on tests of memory and executive function compared with RHI or TBI individually. 33 RHI and TBI exposure may have additive effects that lower the threshold for neurodegeneration and accelerate disease onset.

The inverse association between soccer participation and PD in individuals without TBI likely reflects sampling bias. Former soccer players in this cohort were younger and reported lower rates of cardiovascular disease than non‐CCS athletes, both of which influence PD risk and age at diagnosis. Although age‐stratified sensitivity analyses addressed some of these differences, residual confounding from other health and demographic characteristics may remain. Associations between soccer participation and PD were observed among participants aged ≥60 years, suggesting that younger cohorts may not have reached the typical age of PD onset and underscoring the need for cautious interpretation. Differential recall bias or survival bias may also influence findings. For instance, soccer players with earlier‐onset PD may have survived to enroll, whereas non‐CCS athletes with later‐onset PD may have been underrepresented.

Although neither soccer participation nor TBI group alone predicted earlier PD diagnosis, soccer participation was associated with earlier age at onset among PD participants who reported age at diagnosis. Importantly, participants with and without age at diagnosis did not differ on key attributes significantly associated with earlier PD onset, including BMI and history of heart disease (data not shown). Prior studies similarly show that TBI is associated with worse cognitive outcomes and greater neuropsychiatric burden in PD patients, 50 , 51 , 52 as well as increased Lewy‐body deposition and higher risk of PD or parkinsonism progression. 16 Similarly, RHI exposure may predispose participants to increased neocortical Lewy‐body burden. 53 Although causality and temporality cannot be inferred from a cross‐sectional study, these findings suggest that cumulative head trauma from RHI and TBI may contribute to earlier PD onset, emphasizing the importance of future longitudinal investigation.

Limitations

The generalizability of findings is limited by the homogeneity of the sample: 97% of participants self‐reported as white, and the sample had higher educational attainment and total household income than the national average. PD is underdiagnosed in non‐white racial groups, 54 and people who self‐identify as a racial or ethnic minority are more likely to have a longer‐to‐diagnosis timeline than white‐identifying patients. 55 , 56 The high proportion of participants with graduate or professional degrees (75%) may further limit generalizability to populations with lower or varied educational attainment. Future studies should incorporate lifestyle risk and resilience factors, including smoking, nicotine consumption, and pesticide/toxicant exposure.

Findings should also be interpreted within the context of the Fox Insight cohort, a convenience sample enriched for individuals with PD. The proportion of participants with PD was substantially higher than in the general population, which may affect observed associations and limit generalizability to population‐based samples.

The online Fox Insight study relied on self‐reported questionnaires, introducing potential recall bias and misclassification, which could bias findings or obscure true associations. Although PD was self‐reported, related literature often relies on clinician‐confirmed diagnoses via medical record review or neuropathological confirmation at autopsy. Differences in findings may also reflect variation in diagnostic methodology. Participants self‐reported PD/parkinsonism diagnoses and TBI history, and heading exposure was assessed using a single yes/no question at each level of play, limiting evaluation of exposure frequency, severity, and cumulative burden. As a result, soccer‐related RHI findings reflect broad exposure categories rather than precise measures of cumulative head trauma, and dose–response relationships could not be examined. Small sample sizes among former soccer players with TBI and LOC also warrant cautious interpretation.

The cross‐sectional design prevents causal inference and does not rule out reverse causation, as early PD symptoms may have influenced recall. In survival analyses, age at study enrollment was used as the time origin because exposure timing data were unavailable, meaning hazard ratios may be subject to immortal time bias.

Conclusion

In a sample of former organized sport athletes, cumulative head trauma was associated with increased risk of parkinsonism and PD. Clinically, these findings support the value of incorporating lifetime head injury histories into neurodegenerative disease risk assessments, and earlier screening for neurodegenerative diseases among those with cumulative head trauma exposure. These results suggest a novel framework in which cumulative head trauma contributes to neurodegenerative disease risk and course, providing direction for future studies.
