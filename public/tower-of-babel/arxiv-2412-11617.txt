Dung’s Argumentation Framework: Unveiling the Expressive Power with Inconsistent Databases
Yasir Mahmood, Markus Hecher, Axel-Cyrille Ngonga Ngomo

Abstract
The connection between inconsistent databases and Dung’s abstract argumentation framework has recently drawn growing interest. Specifically, an inconsistent database, involving certain types of integrity constraints such as functional and inclusion dependencies, can be viewed as an argumentation framework in Dung’s setting. Nevertheless, no prior work has explored the exact expressive power of Dung’s theory of argumentation when compared to inconsistent databases and integrity constraints. In this paper, we close this gap by arguing that an argumentation framework can also be viewed as an inconsistent database. We first establish a connection between subset-repairs for databases and extensions for AFs considering conflict-free, naive, admissible and preferred semantics. Further, we define a new family of attribute-based repairs based on the principle of maximal content preservation. The effectiveness of these repairs is then highlighted by connecting them to stable, semi-stable, and stage semantics. Our main contributions include translating an argumentation framework into a database together with integrity constraints. Moreover, this translation can be achieved in polynomial time, which is essential in transferring complexity results between the two formalisms.

Introduction

Formal argumentation serves as a widely applied framework for modeling and evaluating arguments and their reasoning, finding application in various contexts.
In particular, Dung’s abstract argumentation framework
[
20
]
has been specifically designed to model conflict relationships among arguments.
An abstract argumentation framework (AF) represents arguments and their conflicts through directed graphs and allows for a convenient exploration of the conflicts at an abstract level.
The semantics for AFs is described in terms of sets of arguments (called extensions) that can be simultaneously accepted in a given framework.

A related yet distinct domain, with its primary focus on addressing inconsistent information, involves repairing knowledge bases and consistent query answering (CQA)
[
2
,
14
,
33
,
10
]
.
The goal there is to identify and
repair
inconsistencies in the data and obtain a consistent knowledge base (KB) that satisfies the imposed constraints.
The current research on repairs and the theory of argumentation exhibit some overlaps.
Indeed, both (CQA for inconsistent KBs and instantiated argumentation theory) address reasoning under inconsistent information
[
18
]
.

While the connection between AFs and inconsistent databases has gained significant attention, no prior work has investigated the
precise expressive power
of Dung’s theory of argumentation in terms of integrity constraints (ICs).
In this paper, we take on this challenge and resolve the exact expressive power of Dung’s AFs.
This is achieved by expressing AFs in terms of inconsistent databases where ICs include functional (FDs) and inclusion dependencies (IDs).
As extensions in an AF are subsets of arguments satisfying a semantics, the association with relational databases is clarified through subset-repairs
[
15
]
.
Precisely, arguments are seen as database tuples, which together with ICs model the arguments interaction.

We
complete the mutual relationship
between inconsistent databases and argumentation frameworks in Dung’s setting, thereby strengthening the connection between the two formalisms as anticipated earlier
[
38
]
.
Then, the conflict relationship between arguments closely resembles the semantics of functional dependencies, while the defense/support relation mirrors that of inclusion dependencies.
We establish that
AFs can be seen as inconsistent databases
, as so far only the converse has already been established
[
9
,
38
]
.
This strong connection offers a
tabular
representation of the
graphical
AFs and demonstrates that FDs and IDs alone suffice to encode argument interactions in an AF.

In relational databases, the prominent notions of repairs include set-based repairs
[
3
,
15
,
42
]
, attribute-based repairs
[
45
]
, and cardinality-based repairs
[
37
]
.
Here, we focus on subset-repairs. A
subset-repair of
an
inconsistent database
is obtained by removing tuples from the original database such that integrity constraints are no longer violated.
It is known
[
15
]
that for large classes of constraints (FDs and denial constraints), the
restriction to deletions suffices to remove integrity violations.
Subset-maximality is employed in this setting to assure minimal tuple removals.

Finally, we
introduce a new family
of (attribute-based) database repairs based on a pre-existing principle of maximal content preservation.
The prior research on attribute-based repairs focuses on updating attribute values
[
45
,
26
,
8
]
and does not consider the setting of subset-repairs. We define the notion of covering repairs that maximally (fully) preserve the attribute values in a database.
In other words, repairs that encompass a larger set of values for their attributes are preferred among all subset-repairs.
We propose this novel family of repairs as a topic of independent interest.
The relevance and practical implications of these repairs are underlined by connecting them to various AF semantics.

3
A DB View of Abstract Argumentation

In this section, we connect inconsistent databases and abstract argumentation frameworks. This is established by proving that an AF
\mathcal{F}
can be seen as an instance
{\mathcal{D}}_{\mathcal{F}}
of inconsistent database such that the acceptable sets of arguments in
\mathcal{F}
correspond precisely to (maximal) repairs for
\mathcal{D}
.
Intuitively, our construction relies on the fact that the attack relation between arguments in
\mathcal{F}
can be simulated via a database together with FDs, and defending arguments can be seen as another database with IDs.
Then, combining the two databases and dependencies, we obtain an AF — seen as an inconsistent database.
For convenience, an argument “
a
” seen as a tuple in the database is denoted by “
\mathbf{\mathsf{a}}
” to highlight that
\mathbf{\mathsf{a}}
depicts a tabular representation of
a
.

We first simulate the conflicts between arguments in an AF
\mathcal{F}
via FDs, resulting in a
conflict database
.
Then, we establish that the idea of
defending
arguments can be simulated by a
defense
database and IDs.
Finally, we combine the two databases thereby proving that in AFs, the semantics
\sigma\in\{\mathsf{conf},\mathsf{naive},\mathsf{adm},\mathsf{pref}\}
can be simulated via (maximal) repairs for inconsistent databases with FDs and IDs.
In addition, we establish the size and the time complexity associated with the construction of the aforementioned databases. For simplicity, we first assume AFs without self-attacking arguments.
At the end, we highlight the changes required to allow self-attacking arguments in AFs.

3.1
Conflict DBs via Functional Dependencies

Let
\mathcal{F}=(A,R)
be an AF with a set
A
of arguments and attacks
R
.
Then, we construct a
conflict
database
{\mathcal{C}}_{\mathcal{F}}=\langle T,F\rangle

where each argument
a\in A
is seen as the tuple
\mathbf{\mathsf{a}}
(the unique identifier to the tuple representing the argument

) and a collection

of FDs.
A tuple
\mathbf{\mathsf{a}}\in T
encodes the conflicts between

and other arguments in
\mathcal{F}

Intuitively,
\mathcal{C}
is constructed in such a way that
\{\mathbf{\mathsf{a}},\mathbf{\mathsf{b}}\}\not\models f_{i}

Notice that,
\{\mathbf{\mathsf{a}},\mathbf{\mathsf{b}}\}=\{\mathbf{\mathsf{b}},\mathbf{\mathsf{a}}\}

As a consequence, the conflict database for an AF will be

That is, both attacks
(a,b),(b,a)\in R

It is worth remarking that functional dependencies can only simulate the symmetric attacks.
In particular, the input AF may contain non-symmetric attacks though our translation (for the conflict database) will treat them as symmetric

This is unproblematic in this particular setting, since only conflict-free (naive) extensions correspond to repairs (maximal repairs) with FDs.
Further, we will expound on this situation later that one can (perhaps) not simulate directed attacks by FDs alone, and requires IDs for that purpose.

\mathcal{F}

r\not\subseteq S

The sparsity issue for conflict databases.

Observe that the construction of conflict database from an AF includes an individual attribute
x_{r}
modeling
r\in R
.
This translation has the side-effect that conflict databases are very sparse in the following sense: for each attribute
x
(except the name attribute
n
), there are exactly two tuples
\mathbf{\mathsf{a}},\mathbf{\mathsf{b}}\in T
(corresponding to the attack
r=\{a,b\}
) having
meaningful
values for
x
, whereas
\mathbf{\mathsf{c}}[x]=c
for each
\mathbf{\mathsf{c}}\in T\setminus\{\mathbf{\mathsf{a}},\mathbf{\mathsf{b}}\}
.
This results in a very large database with most values being repeated when the number of attacks in
\mathcal{F}
is also large.
We argue that this sparsity of the conflict database can be avoided by
reusing
FDs in
F
.
Intuitively, a single attribute
x
and FD
f\in F
can be used for two different attacks that do not share an argument.
That is, an attribute
x
and FD
{x}\rightarrow{n}
suffices to model each conflict
r_{i}=\{a_{i},b_{i}\}\in R

between distinct arguments
a_{i}\neq a_{j}\neq b_{i}\neq b_{j}
for each
i\neq j
. This works by setting
\mathbf{\mathsf{a}}_{i}[x]{\,=\,}r_{i}{\,=\,}\mathbf{\mathsf{b}}_{i}[x]
,
\mathbf{\mathsf{a}}_{i}[n]{\,=\,}a_{i}
and
\mathbf{\mathsf{b}}_{i}[n]{\,=\,}b_{i}
for each
i
.

We prove that the problem of assigning attributes and FDs to conflicts in
R
can be equivalently seen as the edge-coloring problem.
That is, given a graph
\mathcal{G}=(V,E)
, determine whether there exists a coloring for edges in
E
such that no two adjacent edges are assigned the same color.
The following lemma makes this connection precise, thereby allowing us to deduce that it actually suffices to use the number of FDs determined by the maximum degree of input AF.

Lemma 6
.

Let
\mathcal{F}
be an AF of degree
\gamma
.
Then, there is a conflict database
{\mathcal{C}}_{\mathcal{F}}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\langle T,F\rangle
for
\mathcal{F}
such that
|F|\leq\gamma{+}1
.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{F}=(A,R)
be an AF with arguments
A
and conflicts
R
. Assume
a_{i},a_{j}
and
b_{i},b_{j}
be distinct arguments such that
r_{i}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\{a_{i},b_{i}\}\in R
and
r_{j}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\{a_{j},b_{j}\}\in R
.
Moreover, suppose
f_{i}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=x_{i}\rightarrow n
and
f_{j}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=x_{j}\rightarrow n
denote the FDs encoding the conflicts
r_{i}
and
r_{j}
.
Let
x_{k}
be a fresh attribute, then we denote by
{\mathcal{C}}_{\mathcal{F}}^{\prime}=\langle T^{\prime},F^{\prime}\rangle
the database instance after replacing attributes

(resp., FDs

in
F
) by
x_{k}
(
f_{k}
).
Further, in
{\mathcal{C}}_{\mathcal{F}}^{\prime}
we set
\mathbf{\mathsf{a}}_{i}[x_{k}]=r_{i}=\mathbf{\mathsf{b}}_{i}[x_{k}]
and
\mathbf{\mathsf{a}}_{j}[x_{k}]=r_{j}=\mathbf{\mathsf{b}}_{j}[x_{k}]
, whereas the remaining values remain unchanged.
Then, the following claim depicts the relationship between each
P\subseteq T
in
{\mathcal{C}}_{\mathcal{F}}
versus in
{\mathcal{C}}_{\mathcal{F}}^{\prime}

Claim 7
.

P\models\{f_{i},f_{j}\}

P\models f_{k}
in

Proof of Claim.

Observe that the attribute
x_{k}
in
{\mathcal{C}}_{\mathcal{F}}^{\prime}
and
x_{i},x_{j}
in
{\mathcal{C}}_{\mathcal{F}}
for tuples
\{\mathbf{\mathsf{a}}_{i},\mathbf{\mathsf{b}}_{i},\mathbf{\mathsf{a}}_{j},\mathbf{\mathsf{b}}_{j}\}
include the only changes between
{\mathcal{C}}_{\mathcal{F}}^{\prime}
and
{\mathcal{C}}_{\mathcal{F}}
.
Then,

contains at most one tuple from each set
\{\mathbf{\mathsf{a}}_{i},\mathbf{\mathsf{b}}_{i}\}
and
\{\mathbf{\mathsf{a}}_{j},\mathbf{\mathsf{b}}_{j}\}
.
Moreover, the attribute

is assigned value

by

and

\{\mathbf{\mathsf{a}}_{j},\mathbf{\mathsf{b}}_{j}\}
.
As a result,

in

since other attributes in
{\mathcal{C}}_{\mathcal{F}}^{\prime}
and

remain the same.
Conversely,
if
P\models f_{k}
, then once again

contains at most one tuple from each set
\{\mathbf{\mathsf{a}}_{i},\mathbf{\mathsf{b}}_{i}\}
and

.
This implies that

and establishes the claim.
\triangleleft

Now, we are ready to prove that the task of assigning attributes and FDs to conflicts in

\mathcal{F}
.
Indeed, given a valid edge coloring, we use an attribute

for each color

x_{c}\rightarrow n
.
In other words, distinct colors represent attributes and a valid coloring implies that no two edges connected to a vertex share the same attribute.
This results in a well-defined

for every conflict

.
Further, observe that each FD in

can be written as

for some color

Finally, as Claim

Consequently, repairs for

∎

3.2
Defense DBs via Inclusion Dependencies

Assume an AF
\mathcal{F}=(A,R)
with arguments
A
and attacks
R
.
We construct a
defense
database
{\mathcal{D}}_{\mathcal{F}}=\langle T,I\rangle
for
\mathcal{F}
.
Intuitively, the defense database
T
contains the information about incoming and outgoing attacks for each argument
a\in A
.
The attributes of
T
are
\{u_{a},v_{a}\mid a\in A\}
, further
T\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\{\mathbf{\mathsf{a}}\mid a\in A\}
and the tuple
\mathbf{\mathsf{a}}\in T
encodes the neighborhood of the argument
a\in A
.
Formally,
T
is defined as follows.

Definition 10

(Defense database)
.

Let
\mathcal{F}=(A,R)
be an AF with arguments
A
and attacks
R
.
Then
{\mathcal{D}}_{\mathcal{F}}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\langle T,I\rangle
defines the
defense
database for
\mathcal{F}
, specified in the following.

•

The attributes of
T
are
\{u_{a},v_{a}\mid a\in A\}
.

•

I\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\{{u_{a}}\subseteq{v_{a}}\mid a\in A\}
is the collection of IDs.

•

T\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\{\mathbf{\mathsf{a}}\mid a\in A\}
, where
(1) for each
r=(a,b)\in R
:

\displaystyle\mathbf{\mathsf{a}}[u_{b}]=b,

\displaystyle\mathbf{\mathsf{a}}[v_{b}]=b,

\displaystyle\mathbf{\mathsf{b}}[u_{a}]=a,

\displaystyle\mathbf{\mathsf{b}}[v_{a}]=0.

(2) for each
\mathbf{\mathsf{t}}\in T
and variables
u_{d},v_{d}
not yet assigned:

\displaystyle\mathbf{\mathsf{t}}[u_{d}]=0,

\displaystyle\mathbf{\mathsf{t}}[v_{d}]=0.

Intuitively, for each
\mathbf{\mathsf{a}}\in T
:
\mathbf{\mathsf{a}}[v_{b}]
encodes whether the argument
a
attacks
b
, by setting
\mathbf{\mathsf{a}}[v_{b}]=b
when
(a,b)\in R
.
Moreover,
\mathbf{\mathsf{a}}[u_{b}]
aims at encoding whether
a

interacts
with (either attacks, or attacked by)
b
.
This is
achieved by setting
\mathbf{\mathsf{a}}[u_{b}]=b
if
(b,a)\in R
or
(a,b)\in R
.
The intuition is to simulate attacks from an argument
a
via an ID
{u_{a}}\subseteq{v_{a}}
.
The idea is that if an argument
b
is attacked by
a
, then
b
interacts with
a
and hence
\mathbf{\mathsf{b}}[u_{a}]=a
.
Now, there are two ways
b
can be defended against the attack by
a
: either
b
defends itself by attacking
a
(in which case
\mathbf{\mathsf{b}}[v_{b}]=a
, hence
\{\mathbf{\mathsf{b}}\}\models{u_{a}}\subseteq{v_{a}}
), or there is an argument
c\not\in\{a,b\}
attacking
a
(therefore
\mathbf{\mathsf{c}}[v_{a}]=a
, and
\{\mathbf{\mathsf{b}},\mathbf{\mathsf{c}}\}\models{u_{a}}\subseteq{v_{a}}
as
\mathbf{\mathsf{c}}[u_{a}]=\mathbf{\mathsf{c}}[v_{a}]=a
).

Lemma 12
.

Let
a,b\in A
be two arguments such that
(a,b)\in R
.
Then, for
\mathbf{\mathsf{b}}\in T
(the tuple corresponding to

) and
i\mathrel{\mathop{:}}={u_{a}}\subseteq{v_{a}}\in I
, we have
\textsf{def}_{a}(b)=\textsf{sup}_{i}(\mathbf{\mathsf{b}})
.

Proof.

Let
(a,b)\in R

\mathbf{\mathsf{b}}[u_{a}]=a
.
So,
\textsf{sup}_{i}(\mathbf{\mathsf{b}})\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\{\mathbf{\mathsf{c}}\mid\mathbf{\mathsf{c}}[v_{a}]=\mathbf{\mathsf{b}}[u_{a}]\}
includes all the tuples supporting
\mathbf{\mathsf{b}}
for the ID
i
.
Clearly, for each tuple
\mathbf{\mathsf{c}}\in T
such that
\mathbf{\mathsf{c}}\in\textsf{sup}_{i}(\mathbf{\mathsf{b}})
, we have that
(c,a)\in R
since
\mathbf{\mathsf{c}}[v_{a}]=a
.
Similarly, for each
c\in A
such that
c\in\textsf{def}_{a}(b)
, we know that
(c,a)\in R
.
As a result,
\mathbf{\mathsf{b}}[u_{a}]=a
together with
\mathbf{\mathsf{c}}[v_{a}]=a
implies that
\mathbf{\mathsf{c}}\in\textsf{sup}_{i}(\mathbf{\mathsf{b}})
.
This completes the proof. ∎

Let
S\subseteq A
be a set of arguments and

be the corresponding set of tuples.
The defense database has the property that

iff each argument in

is defended against the argument

.
We prove that a set

\mathcal{F}
iff
S_{T}\subseteq T
satisfies every ID in

.

Lemma 13
.

Let
\mathcal{F}
be an AF and
{\mathcal{D}}_{\mathcal{F}}
denotes its corresponding defense database. Then, for every
S\subseteq A
,

defends itself in
\mathcal{F}
iff
S_{T}\subseteq T
is a repair for
{\mathcal{D}}_{\mathcal{F}}
.

Proof.

Let
S\subseteq A
such that
S
defends itself in
\mathcal{F}
.
Then, for each
a\in A
such that
(a,b)\in R
for some

, there is

such that
(c,a)\in R
.
As a result,
\textsf{def}_{a}(b)\cap S\neq\emptyset
.
This implies that, for each

,
\textsf{sup}_{i}(\mathbf{\mathsf{b}})\cap S_{T}\neq\emptyset
(due to Lemma

), and
consequently,
S_{T}\models i
.
Conversely, suppose there is some

such that

for some

but no

attacks

.
Now,
\textsf{def}_{d}(c)\cap S=\emptyset
and therefore,
\textsf{sup}_{j}(\mathbf{\mathsf{c}})\cap S_{T}=\emptyset
where

(again Lem.

).
Then,

; therefore

can not be a repair in

.
∎

Note that repairs for a defense database do not yield conflict-free sets.
In fact, we will prove later that one can not model conflict-freeness via inclusion dependencies alone.

.

Theorem 17
.

Let
\mathcal{F}
be an AF and
{\mathcal{A}}_{\mathcal{F}}
denotes its corresponding AF-database. Then, for every

,
S
is admissible (resp., preferred) in
\mathcal{F}

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{F}
be an AF and
{\mathcal{A}}_{\mathcal{F}}
denotes its corresponding AF-database.
Recall that we defined
{\mathcal{A}}_{\mathcal{F}}=\langle T,D\rangle
, where
T
is the database obtained by
combining
the conflict and defense databases, and

consists of a collection of FDs
D
, IDs
I
, and the dependency
i_{s}
for self-attacking arguments (assuming that

is a fresh name not in

Specifically,
{\mathcal{A}}_{\mathcal{F}}
has the following components.
Suppose

denotes attacks

•

•

(2) for each

\displaystyle\mathbf{\mathsf{b}}[x_{i}]=r_{i},

\mathbf{\mathsf{c}}[x_{i}]=c

\mathbf{\mathsf{c}}[u_{s}]=0

Attribute-Based Repairs & Other Semantics

Besides subset maximality, we introduce maximal repairs following the principle of
content preservation

[
45
]
.
The motivation behind these
attribute-based repairs
is to adjust subset-maximality by retaining maximum possible values from
\mathsf{dom}(T)
for certain attributes of
T
.
Let
\mathcal{D}=\langle T,D\rangle
where
T
is a database with values
\mathsf{dom}(T)
and
D
is a set of dependencies.
Let
X
be a sequence of attributes in
T
and
P\subseteq T
, then
P[X]
denotes the database obtained from
P
by restricting the attributes to
X
and
\mathsf{dom}(P[X])
denotes the set of values in
P[X]
.
Given
\mathcal{D}
and a sequence
X
of attributes, we say that
P\subseteq T
is a
maximally covering repair
for
\mathcal{D}
with respect to
X
if
P\in\mathsf{repairs}(\mathcal{D})
and there is no
P^{\prime}\in\mathsf{repairs}(\mathcal{D})
such that
\mathsf{dom}(P^{\prime}[X])\supset\mathsf{dom}(P[X])
.
Moreover, we say that
P
is a
fully covering repair
for
\mathcal{D}
with respect to
X
if
\mathsf{dom}(P[X])=\mathsf{dom}(T[X])
.
In other words, a maximal covering repair retains maximum possible values from
\mathsf{dom}(T[X])
, whereas a fully covering repair takes all the values from
\mathsf{dom}(T[X])
.
If
X
contains all the attributes in
T
then we simply speak of maximally (fully) covering repair for
\mathcal{D}
without specifying
X
.
For a database instance
\mathcal{D}
and attributes
X
, by
\mathsf{mc\text{-}repairs}(\mathcal{D},X)
(resp.,
\mathsf{fc\text{-}repairs}(\mathcal{D},X)
) we denote the set of all maximally (fully) covering repairs for
\mathcal{D}
with respect to
X
.

Example 20
.

In the database from Example
2
,

\{\mathbf{\mathsf{s}}_{1},\mathbf{\mathsf{s}}_{3},\mathbf{\mathsf{s}}_{5}\}
and
\{\mathbf{\mathsf{s}}_{2},\mathbf{\mathsf{s}}_{3},\mathbf{\mathsf{s}}_{5}\}
are both subset-maximal as well as maximally covering repairs for
\mathcal{D}
considering all attributes.
Moreover,
\{\mathbf{\mathsf{s}}_{1},\mathbf{\mathsf{s}}_{4},\mathbf{\mathsf{s}}_{5}\}
is subset-maximal but not maximally covering (since
\{\mathbf{\mathsf{s}}_{1},\mathbf{\mathsf{s}}_{3},\mathbf{\mathsf{s}}_{5}\}
covers more values).
Notice that there is no fully covering repair for
\mathcal{D}
.
Nevertheless,
\{\mathbf{\mathsf{s}}_{1},\mathbf{\mathsf{s}}_{3},\mathbf{\mathsf{s}}_{5}\}
is a fully covering repair for
\mathcal{D}
with respect to attributes
\{\texttt{Tutor},\texttt{Time},\texttt{Room}\}
whereas
\{\mathbf{\mathsf{s}}_{2},\mathbf{\mathsf{s}}_{3},\mathbf{\mathsf{s}}_{5}\}
is not. Further, there is no fully covering repair for
\mathcal{D}
w.r.t
\{\texttt{Course}\}
or
\{\texttt{Advisor}\}
.

A maximally covering repair is also subset-maximal for any instance
\mathcal{D}
, whereas, the reverse is not true in general.
Moreover, a fully covering repair is also maximally covering, though it may not always exist.
Observe that, certain attribute may not be fully covered in any repair and requiring the repairs to fully cover the domain with respect to all the attributes may not always be achievable.
This motivates the need to focus on specific attributes in a database and include repairs fully covering the values in those attributes.

4.1
Stable, Semi-stable, and Stage Semantics

We prove that stable, semi-stable and stage extensions in AFs can also be seen as subset-repairs of the inconsistent DBs.
This is achieved by dropping subset-maximality and utilizing the covering semantics for repairs.
Notice that, the three semantics require maximality for the range
S^{+}
for a set
S
of arguments.
Recall that the attributes
\{v_{x}\mid x\in A\}
in the defense database encode arguments attacking
x\in A
.
Together with the
name
attribute (
n
) from the conflict database, we can encode the range of a set
S
of arguments via an AF-database.
Let
X_{r}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\{v_{x}\mid x\in A\}\cup\{n\}
denote the
range
attributes.
The following lemma proves the relation between values taken by a set
S_{T}
of tuples under attributes
X_{r}
and the range
S^{+}
for a set
S\subseteq A
of arguments.

Lemma 21
.

Let
\mathcal{F}
be an AF and
{\mathcal{A}}_{\mathcal{F}}
denote its AF-database.
Then, for every set
S\subseteq A
of arguments and corresponding set
S_{T}\subseteq T
of tuples,
S^{+}=\mathsf{dom}(S_{T}[X_{r}])\setminus\{0\}
.

Proof.

Let
s\in S^{+}
.
If
s\in S
, then
s\in\mathsf{dom}(S_{T}[X_{r}])
since
\mathbf{\mathsf{s}}[n]=s
and
\mathbf{\mathsf{s}}\in S_{T}
.
Otherwise, there exists
t\in S
such that
(t,s)\in R
.
Then,
\mathbf{\mathsf{t}}[v_{s}]=s
and therefore
s\in\mathsf{dom}(S_{T}[X_{r}])
once again as
\mathbf{\mathsf{t}}\in S_{T}
.
Conversely, suppose
s\not\in S^{+}
.
Since
s\not\in S
, this implies that
s
does not appear in the column
S_{T}[n]
.
Further, since
s
is not attacked by any argument in
S
, there is no tuple
\mathbf{\mathsf{t}}\in S_{T}
such that
\mathbf{\mathsf{t}}[v_{s}]=s
and therefore
s\not\in\mathsf{dom}(S_{T}[X_{r}])
.
Finally, the element
0\in\mathsf{dom}(S_{T}[X_{r}])
but it does not correspond to any argument and therefore removed to establish the claim.
∎

Now, we consider the AF-database for an AF
\mathcal{F}
with range attributes
X_{r}
to determine values in
\mathsf{dom}(T)
covered by any repair.
Since stable and stage semantics only require conflict-freeness, we drop the admissibility and hence the set of IDs, except for the ID (
i_{s}
) for self-attacking arguments.

Theorem 22
.

Let
\mathcal{F}
be an AF and
{\mathcal{A}}_{\mathcal{F}}
denotes its corresponding AF-database where
{\mathcal{A}}_{\mathcal{F}}=\langle T,F\cup I\rangle
.
Moreover, let
i_{s}
be the ID for self-attacking arguments,
{\mathcal{C}}_{\mathcal{F}}=\langle T,F\cup\{i_{s}\}\rangle
, and let
X_{r}=\{v_{x}\mid x\in A\}\cup\{n\}
.
Then, for every
S\subseteq A
and corresponding
S_{T}\subseteq T
,

•

S\in\mathsf{stab}(\mathcal{F})
iff
S_{T}\in\mathsf{fc\text{-}repairs}({\mathcal{C}}_{\mathcal{F}},X_{r})
,

•

S\in\mathsf{stag}(\mathcal{F})
iff
S_{T}\in\mathsf{mc\text{-}repairs}({\mathcal{C}}_{\mathcal{F}},X_{r})

•

S\in\mathsf{semi\text{-}st}(\mathcal{F})
iff
S_{T}\in\mathsf{mc\text{-}repairs}({\mathcal{A}}_{\mathcal{F}},X_{r})

Proof.

We first prove the claim regarding stable semantics.
Let
S
be stable in
\mathcal{F}
, then
S_{T}\in\mathsf{repairs}(\mathcal{C})
since
S
is conflict-free.
Notice that
T[X_{r}]
only contains argument names and the element
0
.
Since
\mathbf{\mathsf{s}}[v_{s}]=0
for any
\mathbf{\mathsf{s}}\in S_{T}
, therefore
0\in\mathsf{dom}(S_{T}[X_{r}])
.
Further, since every
x\in A\setminus S
is attacked by
S
, hence there is some
s\in S
with
(s,x)\in R
.
Consequently,
\mathsf{dom}(S_{T}[X_{r}])=\mathsf{dom}(T[X_{r}])
and
S_{T}\in\mathsf{fc\text{-}repairs}(\mathcal{C},X_{r})
.
Conversely, suppose
S\not\in\mathsf{stab}(\mathcal{F})
.
If
S
is not conflict-free then clearly
S_{T}\not\in\mathsf{fc\text{-}repairs}(\mathcal{C},X_{r})
(due to Thm.
5
).
Whereas, if
S
is conflict-free, then
S^{+}\neq A
(otherwise
S
is stable in
\mathcal{F}
).
This implies that there is some
x\in A\setminus S
such that
(s,x)\not\in R
for any
s\in S
.
Then,
x\not\in\mathsf{dom}(S_{T}[X_{r}])
.
This implies,
\mathsf{dom}(S_{T}[X_{r}])\neq\mathsf{dom}(T[X_{r}])
since
x\in\mathsf{dom}(T[X_{r}])
.
As a result,
S_{T}
is not a fully covering repair for
\mathcal{C}
w.r.t.
X_{r}
and this establishes the claim.

Next, we prove the third claim, whereas, the second claim follows a similar argument.
Let
S\in\mathsf{semi\text{-}st}(\mathcal{F})
, then
S
is admissible and there is no admissible
S^{\prime}\subseteq A
in
\mathcal{F}
with
S^{+}_{R}\subsetneq(S^{\prime})^{+}_{R}
.
Suppose to the contrary that
S_{T}\not\in\mathsf{mc\text{-}repairs}(\mathcal{A},X_{r})
.
Since
S_{T}\in\mathsf{repairs}(\mathcal{A})
(Thm.
17
), there must be some
P\in\mathsf{repairs}(\mathcal{A})
such that
\mathsf{dom}(P[X_{r}])\supset\mathsf{dom}(S_{T}[X_{r}])
.
Let
P_{\mathcal{F}}
denote the set of arguments corresponding to tuples in
P
.
Then, this leads to a contradiction that
S\in\mathsf{semi\text{-}st}(\mathcal{F})
since
S
and
P_{\mathcal{F}}
are both admissible in
\mathcal{F}
and
P_{\mathcal{F}}^{+}\supset S^{+}
due to Lemma
21
.
Conversely, suppose
S_{T}\in\mathsf{mc\text{-}repairs}(\mathcal{A},X_{r})
, then
S_{T}\in\mathsf{repairs}(\mathcal{A})
and hence
S
is admissible in
\mathcal{F}
.
Now, the fact that no admissible
S^{\prime}
in
\mathcal{F}
exists with
(S^{\prime})^{+}\supset S^{+}
follows since
S_{T}
is a maximally covering repair for
{\mathcal{A}}_{\mathcal{F}}
w.r.t
X_{r}
and
\mathsf{dom}(S_{T}[X_{r}])
is maximum with respect to all the repairs for
{\mathcal{A}}_{\mathcal{F}}
.
This established the claim proof in reverse direction.

The claim for the stage semantics follows from the above proof after replacing the admissibility by conflict-freeness.
This completes the proof to our theorem.
∎

For stable and stage semantics, the size of the database can be reduced since there are no IDs involved (except
i_{s}
).

Corollary 23
.

Let
\mathcal{F}=(A,R)
be an AF.
Then, its corresponding AF-database
{\mathcal{C}}_{\mathcal{F}}
for stable and stage semantics has a table of size
|A|\times(2|A|+3)
, and uses
|A|
-many FDs with a single ID.

Proof.

The claim follows easily from the proof of Theorem
18
, since we can drop the columns corresponding to attributes
\{u_{a}\mid a\in A\}
from the AF-database for stable and stage semantics.
∎

5
Discussion, Conclusion, and Future Work

We presented a database view of Dung’s theory of abstract argumentation. While our results tighten the connection between two domains by simulating Dung’s argumentation frameworks by inconsistent databases, they also provide the exact expressivity of AFs via integrity constraints.
Roughly speaking: Dung’s argumentation frameworks can be equivalently seen as inconsistent databases where ICs include functional and inclusion dependencies.
This strong connection allows us to transfer further ideas from one domain to another.
As already pointed out, a repairing semantics based on the idea of maximizing
range
from AFs yields maximal (full) content preservation across certain attributes.
We propose this new family of repairs as a topic of interest.

Complete and Grounded Semantics.

We note that the complete and grounded semantics may not have a natural counterpart in the subset-repairs setting for databases when FDs and IDs are employed.
Although, we can introduce a notion of closed repairs utilizing the connection between defending arguments and supporting tuples (Lemma
12
) to simulate the closure properties required by these semantics, this may not correspond to a natural definition for repairs in the databases.
Then the question arises: are there classes of ICs that can express these two semantics for AFs?

Outlook and Future Research Directions.

Our work is in line with the existing literature connecting the two domains; seeing tuples in the database as arguments
[
9
,
38
]
or constructing arguments from KBs
[
43
,
18
]
.
By inspecting the expressivity of AFs to integrity constraints, we believe to have opened a new research dimension within argumentation.
This study can be extended by exploring the expressivity of extensions and generalizations of AFs, e.g., dialectical frameworks (ADFs)
[
12
]
, set-based AFs
[
40
]
and claim-centric AFs
[
23
]
to name a few.
Moreover, in claim-centric AFs, the so-called
claim-level
semantics requires maximizing accepted sets of claims rather than arguments — which can be covered by maximally covering repairs for suitable attributes.
Further, the connection between probabilistic
[
34
]
and preference-based
[
29
]
AFs and their database-counterpart
[
35
,
41
]
also seems promising.

The well-known reasoning problems in AFs include
credulous
(resp.,
skeptical
) reasoning, asking whether an argument belongs to some (every) extension.
However, the natural reasoning problem for databases includes consistent query answering (CQA) under
brave
or
cautious
semantics.
A (Boolean) query is entailed bravely (resp., cautiously) from an inconsistent database if it holds in some (every) repair.
It is interesting to explore DB-variants of credulous and skeptic reasoning. That is, given an argument
a
in an AF
\mathcal{F}
and semantics
\sigma
, construct a query
q^{a}_{\sigma}
such that
a
is credulously (skeptically) accepted in
\mathcal{F}
under semantics
\sigma
iff the query
q^{a}_{\sigma}
is entailed bravely (cautiously) from the database
{\mathcal{D}}_{\mathcal{F}}
for
\mathcal{F}
under appropriate repairing semantics
\rho_{\sigma}
.
Yet another interesting direction for future work is to utilize recently developed (so-called) decomposition-guided
[
24
,
25
,
28
]
reductions for reasoning in DBs.
These reductions would allow to establish tight runtime bounds for CQA via the known bounds for reasoning in AFs.
Exploring these directions is left for future work.

Finally, a complexity analysis for repairs involving maximally or fully covering semantics is on our agenda.
Some complexity lower bounds for the decision problems (involving FDs and IDs) already follow from the results in this paper.
We next aim to target the complexity of repair checking and CQA involving not only FDs and IDs, but also more general types of constraints such as
equality
and
tuple
generating dependencies. It is noteworthy that these definitions for repairs introduce a preference on sets of tuples, distinguishing them from research on prioritized databases
[
41
,
30
,
9
]
, which typically involves tuple preferences.
