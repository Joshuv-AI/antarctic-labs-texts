PISTOL MRI allows noninvasive assessment of changes in tumor oxygenation post hypoxia targeted therapy
Authors: Shubhangi Agarwal, Lakshmisree Damodaran, Landon J Inge, Vikram D Kodibagkar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractTumor hypoxia has been shown to promote tumor progression, cause resistance to both radiotherapy and chemotherapy and is a marker for poor prognosis. Due to its ability to modulate the clinical outcome, hypoxia has been recognized as a tumor-specific target for development of novel hypoxia-activated/targeted bioreductive pro-drugs. In the present study, a 1H MRI based pO2 measuring technique, PISTOL (Proton Imaging of Siloxanes to map Tissue Oxygenation Levels), was used to determine the baseline oxygenation of pre-clinical models of non-small cell lung cancer (NCI-H1975) and epidermoid carcinoma (A431) and their response to hypoxia-activated pro-drug (HAP) Tirapazamine (TPZ). A431 tumors had lower baseline oxygenation than the NCI-H1975 tumors (p < 0.05). TPZ was effective in slowing the tumor growth only in NCI-H1975 tumors, even though A431 tumors were significantly less oxygenated (50 ± 14 torr for NCI-H1975 vs. 30 ± 11 torr for A431, p < 0.0001) and had similar hypoxic fractions (0.08 ± 0.09 for NCI-H1975 vs. 0.18 ± 0.19 for A431, p > 0.05) pre-treatment.TPZ resulted in a significant decrease (p < 0.05) in the oxygenation of central regions of NCI-H1975 tumor, and an increase in the edema fraction which can be attributed to its reported anti-vascular effect. In conclusion, PISTOL was able to report the changes in tumor oxygenation in both the tumor xenografts over time and in response to therapy. The baseline mean pO2 and hypoxic fraction alone were not a reliable indicator of therapeutic efficacy and the results indicate that other factors (e.g. tumor perfusion and relative expression of nitroreductases) may play an important role in determining efficacy of hypoxia-targeted treatments in conjunction with tumor oxygenation status.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-32618-2.Keywords: Hypoxia, MRI, PISTOL, Non-small cell lung carcinoma, Epidermoid carcinoma, Tirapazamine, Hypoxia activated prodrugsSubject terms: Cancer imaging, Cancer models, Tumour biomarkers, Targeted therapies

Introduction

The poorly regulated expression of angiogenic growth factors within the tumor microenvironment leads to development of vasculature that is underdeveloped in structure, inadequate in extent, dysfunctional in character and chaotic in organization, resulting in either diffusion limited (i.e. chronic hypoxia) or perfusion limited (i.e. acute hypoxia)1–8. Hypoxia modulates various cellular responses and signaling pathways that lead to selection of aggressive phenotypes, malignant progression, invasion and metastasis9–13. Hypoxia has also been known for inducing expression of a plethora of tumor promoting factors and is associated with increased resistance to radiotherapy and chemotherapy9,13,14. Given its prevalence, hypoxia can be exploited as a tumor-specific condition, allowing for the rational design of hypoxia targeted therapies as well as hypoxia imaging techniques15–23.

The clinical relevance of hypoxia has led to the development of several hypoxia-activated/targeted bio-reductive pro-drugs (HAPs), for example, Tirapazamine24, EO925, PR-10426, AQ4N27, TH-30228 and TH-400029,30. In spite of prevalence of hypoxia in tumors and its association with treatment failure, the HAPs have failed to show any survival benefit during the clinical trials resulting in the discontinuation of all except TH-302 and TH-4000, which are still under clinical trials17,28,31–36. The failure of several pre-clinically promising HAPs indicates a need for the evaluation of tumor hypoxia, its heterogeneity and its contribution towards HAP’s treatment efficacy in order to improve clinical decision-making and drug design37–42.

A non-invasive hypoxia imaging technique, that allows repeat measurements, should provide a better understanding of interplay between hypoxia and tumor response to therapy, potentially allowing therapy to be tailored to patient tumor’s characteristics. A commonly used hypoxia imaging technique in the clinical setting is positron emission tomography (PET) via various hypoxia binding PET tracers such as18F-FMISO18, F-FAZA and64, Cu-ATSM41. Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) is a versatile non-invasive imaging modality that is routinely used in the clinic for cancer imaging and has the potential for non-invasive, real-time, high-resolution and repeated assessment of oxygenation. The current MRI based oximetry techniques can be sub-divided into qualitative techniques such as Blood Oxygen Level Dependent (BOLD)43, Tissue Oxygen Level Dependent (TOLD)44, oxygen-enhanced MRI45, hypoxia-targeted agents46,47 and quantitative techniques such as19F MRI of perfluorocarbon emulsions, e.g. FREDOM (utilizing19F MRI of hexafluorobenzene)48 and Proton Imaging of Siloxanes for mapping Tissue Oxygenation Levels (PISTOL) (utilizing1H MRI of siloxanes)49–51.

PISTOL has the potential to provide direct quantitative measurement of oxygenation non-invasively and longitudinally along with the spatial distribution and degree of hypoxia50. Here we used PISTOL to quantitatively map the oxygenation of tumor xenograft models pre and post hypoxia-activated therapies. We investigated the efficacy of Tirapazamine, a prototypical DNA damaging hypoxia-activated pro-drug, in non-small cell lung cancer (NCI-H1975) and epidermoid carcinoma (A431) xenograft models. The main purpose of this study was to investigate the relationship between tumor oxygenation (pO2) and the efficacy of hypoxia-activated pro-drugs. We aimed to test the hypothesis that prognostic identification of tumors with hypoxia could assist in predicting the outcome of hypoxia activated therapy. The inefficient vasculature and lymphatic system in the tumors lead to formation of regions with higher edema. Edema can also result from the necrosis and treatment induced cell-death. We also wanted to investigate the occurrence of edema in the tumors pre and post treatment by MRI and analyze the correlation with the treatment. Edema has higher T2 as compared to the tumors52.

Materials & methods

Animal models

All methods are reported in accordance with ARRIVE guidelines (https://arriveguidelines.org). Human NCI-H1975 non-small cell lung cancer and A431 epidermoid carcinomas cells (ATCC Inc.) were cultured in Roswell Park Memorial Institute (RPMI 1640) and Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM) respectively, supplemented with 1% penicillin/streptomycin and 10% fetal bovine serum (Life Technologies, Carlsbad, CA, USA), in a 5% CO2-containing humidified atmosphere at 37°C. Immunocompromised male nu\nu mice (5–11/per cohort, Charles River Laboratories, USA) were implanted subcutaneously with 2 × 106 cells/40 µl of the respective cell line (A431 or NCI-H1975) in the right thigh. All experimental procedures were approved by the Arizona State University Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) and the animals were monitored, maintained and all methods were carried out in accordance with the IACUC guidelines.

Tumor volume measurements

Tumor volume measurements were made using Vernier calipers every 3rd day from the day of tumor appearance till tumor excision. The formula for an ellipsoid (π*abc/6), where a, b and c are three orthogonal dimensions (with a being the longest), was used to calculate the tumor volumes. Tumor volumes were normalized to baseline imaging (pre-treatment) volumes and then compared with post-treatment volumes to study the treatment response. Tumor doubling times were measured by fitting the mean normalized tumor volumes to an exponential growth curve.

MR imaging protocol and hypoxia targeted therapy

In order to assess the oxygenation of NCI-H1975 and A431 tumors the following imaging protocol was followed once the tumors reached ~ 0.3 cc in volume (day 0, baseline). Neat HMDSO (Sigma Aldrich., 10–50 µL) was injected directly into the tumor as a pO2 reporter molecule using a Hamilton syringe with 32 gauge needle and HMDSO droplets were deposited in a fan pattern53, along a single plane. The mice were anesthetized with isoflurane gas inhalation then placed on a heated circulating-water blanket (370 C) on an animal holder and a MR receiver coil was placed over the tumor. Core temperature was measured using an endorectal probe and observed to be within acceptable physiological range (37 ± 2 °C). The animal holder was then pushed inside the scanner and imaged using a mouse surface receiver and a volume transmitter coil. The mice were kept under anesthesia (1.5% isoflurane in air; Veterinary Anesthesia Systems Inc.) during the entire course of experiment. After positioning the mouse inside the scanner, a standard multi-slice multi-echo (MSME) T2 mapping sequence was used to calculate T2 (TR = 3 s & TE = 14–210 ms) of tumor followed by acquisition of three consecutive T1 maps of the HMDSO using PISTOL. The PISTOL sequence50 consists of (1) pulse-burst saturation, followed by a tau delay for magnetization recovery, (2) CHESS pulses for selective saturation of water and fat, (3) an EPI based spin-echo acquisition with frequency selective π/2 pulse (on-resonance for siloxane), followed by a (4) slice selective π pulse, and an EPI readout. The tau values ranged from 0.1 to 55 s (16 values) with variable averages and the field of view was 3 cm X 3 cm with a slice thickness of 5 mm and a 64 × 64 matrix. The tumor bearing mice were randomly assigned to Tirapazamine (TPZ) treated and control groups for each tumor model. TPZ (60 µmole/kg, Sigma-Aldrich) was dissolved in 5% dextrose and administered intraperitoneally. The vehicle 5% dextrose was administered as a control agent. The respective therapy/control was administered immediately after the acquisition of day 0 PISTOL maps. Subsequent imaging was conducted every 5th day till day 15 post baseline imaging i.e. at day 5, 10 and 15. Animals that had successful tumor implantation were included in the study.

Mean pO2 value from the three PISTOL maps was computed based on common voxels across the three individual pO2 maps, using the PISTOL sequence, for each tumor and used for further statistical analysis. In order to study the heterogeneity in tumor, we used a T2 weighted image to delineate the tumor and then segmented it into peripheral and central regions via MATLAB (outer and inner 50% voxels, respectively) and average pO2 for both the regions as well as the whole tumor were analyzed (supplementary figure S1). Hypoxic fractions for each tumors were also determined as the percentage of voxels on the pO2 maps with pO2 values ≤ 10 torr.

Tumor collection and immunohistochemical analysis

The mice were intraperitoneally injected with 60 mg/kg body weight pimonidazole hydrochloride (Hypoxyprobe Inc., Burlington, MA) 60 min prior to euthanasia. Mice were anesthetized with 2–3% isoflurane in a chamber and then euthanized via manual cervical dislocation. The tumors were surgically removed post-euthanasia and embedded in optimum cutting temperature compound (OCT) in a cryo-mold and frozen on dry ice and subsequently stored at −80 °C until further processing. The tumor sections (25 μm thick) corresponding to the slice location of PISTOL maps were selected and stained with FITC conjugated anti-pimonidazole antibody (1:200) overnight at 4 °C and counterstained with DAPI (300 nM, nucleic acid stain). The stained sections were visualized under a fluorescence microscope (Leica Biosystems, Wetzlar, Germany) using green channel (EX: 450–490 nm, EM: 500–550 nm) for pimonidazole and blue channel (EX: 355–425 nm, EM: 470 nm) for DAPI at 5X magnification. The images were then acquired using the tile scan and stitched together to form the complete image of the tumor. Hypoxic fractions were then calculated using a custom MATLAB script. Briefly, the image was split into blue (DAPI) and green (pimonidazole) channels. The blue channel image was used to delineate the tumor and further segment into center and peripheral regions and applied to the green channel image. A region of interest was then drawn within the green channel image corresponding to the normoxic regions of the tumor (showing presence of signal on DAPI channel but low intensity on the green channel). Regions with intensity value 2 standard deviations above the mean intensity of normoxic regions were considered hypoxic and used to calculate the hypoxic fraction. The hypoxic fractions of all the cohorts were then compared for analysis. Three different tumors from each cohort were examined for this analysis.

Edema fraction analysis

The edema fraction was calculated by applying a bi-exponential fit model54,55, to the acquired T2 maps using MATLAB. Pixel by pixel computation of short (≤ 200 ms) and long T2 (> 200 ms) components was conducted to calculate maps of the edema fraction (fraction of signal intensity associated with the long T2 component). Mean edema fraction was then calculated for the entire volume of the tumor.

Data analysis and statistics

Statistical analyses of all the tumor volume, tumor doubling times, oxygenation and edema fraction data were conducted via Student’s t-test (paired & un-paired) and one-way ANOVA using the GraphPad PRISM Software (GraphPad Inc., La Jolla CA). For statistical analysis, p < 0.05 was considered as significant. Un-paired t-tests were conducted between the normalized tumor volumes for A431 and NCI-H1975 tumor bearing cohorts treated with TPZ and untreated control to evaluate the treatment response. The difference between mean baseline oxygenation and edema fraction were evaluated using un-paired t-tests. One-way ANOVA was conducted on the mean pO2’s and edema fraction from all the time points for each cohort to determine the effect of treatment. Paired t-tests were conducted to determine the intra-tumoral variation in oxygenation of central and peripheral regions and treatment induced changes in oxygenation as compared to baseline. Un-paired t-tests were also conducted between the A431 and NCI-H1975 tumor bearing mice treated with the TPZ and untreated control.

Results

Comparison of baseline tumor oxygenation and edema fraction between tumor types

A total of 13 A431 (7 control and 6 TPZ treated) and 14 NCI-H1975 (6 control and 8 TPZ treated) tumor bearing animals were studied. In order to compare the baseline characteristics of A431 and NCI-H1975 tumors, the mean pO2 values derived from the PISTOL maps for each tumor type were compared using unpaired t-tests. The mean baseline (day 0) oxygenation of A431 tumors (30 ± 11 torr) was found to be significantly lower than the NCI-H1975 tumors (50 ± 14 torr) (p < 0.001) (Fig. 1A). Comparison between the mean pO2 of tumor center and periphery showed that A431 tumor centers had significantly lower oxygenation (29 ± 14 torr) as compared to NCI-H1975 tumor centers (59 ± 19 torr, p < 0.0001). The oxygenation of A431 tumors’ periphery (35 ± 12 torr) was also significantly lower than the NCI-H1975 tumors’ periphery (47 ± 1 torr, p < 0.05). Hypoxic fractions obtained from the PISTOL data were also compared between cohorts. For the A431 cohort, baseline hypoxic fraction of 0.18 ± 0.19 while that for the NCI-H1975 tumors was 0.08 ± 0.09. These differences were not statistically significant (p > 0.05). All hypoxic fractions remained statistically similar (p > 0.05) in all comparisons (treatment vs. control for each tumor type, between tumor types for a given time point and compared to day 0 for each cohort) except that hypoxic fraction in untreated NCI-H1975 were significantly elevated (p < 0.05) on days 5 and 10 compared to day 0.

In order to determine the intra-tumoral variations, we compared the mean oxygenation of the central and peripheral regions. Paired t-tests showed that the center and periphery of A431 tumors were not significantly different from each other. Although not statistically different (p = 0.052), the center region of H1975 tumors had higher mean oxygenation than periphery. Furthermore, we computed and compared the baseline mean edema fraction between the tumor models and found that A431 tumors (0.05 ± 0.013) had lower edema fraction as compared to the NCI-H1975 tumors (0.065 ± 0.02, p = 0.09, Fig. 1B). We sought to test whether there was a correlation between the baseline tumor oxygenation and tumor volumes as well as baseline edema fraction. To do this, we analyzed the correlation of mean tumor volume with both tumor oxygenation and edema fraction (supplementary data figure S2), and found no significant correlation. We also observed no significant correlation between baseline tumor oxygenation and edema fraction (data not shown).

Treatment response: effect on tumor volumes

The effect of the TPZ treatment on tumors was determined by comparing the normalized mean tumor volume differences between the treated and untreated control cohorts. The mean normalized tumor volumes of TPZ treated A431 tumors were comparable to the control tumors (Fig. 1C). TPZ treated NCI-H1975 tumors were significantly smaller as compared to the untreated control tumors at day 5–15 (p < 0.05, Fig. 1D). Significant differences were also observed between control A431 and NCI-H1975 mean normalized tumor volumes at day 15 with A431 control tumors being smaller than the NCI-H1975 control tumors (p < 0.05).

We also investigated the differences in tumor volume doubling times in order to evaluate the therapeutic effect. The mean doubling time for untreated A431 tumors was 7.1 ± 1 days, while for NCI-H1975 tumors was about 5.5 ± 0.9 days. A431 tumors treated with TPZ had a doubling time of 8.5 ± 2.8 days. TPZ treated NCI-H1975 tumors had significantly longer doubling time of 10.1 ± 5.6 days respectively as compared to untreated control (Table 1).

One-way ANOVA analysis of oxygenation in the tumor center showed that the treatment effect was significant (p < 0.05) and a paired t-test analysis showed a significant decrease in the oxygenation at day 5 (40 ± 11 torr, p < 0.05) and day 10 (40 ± 10 torr, p < 0.01) of central regions of the tumor from baseline (55 ± 15 torr, Fig. 5E). Effect of Tirapazamine on edema fraction

We investigated the changes in edema fraction in response to therapy and also compared the intra-treatment differences in the mean edema fraction for each tumor model. One-way ANOVA analysis showed no significant difference in the fraction for untreated control and TPZ A431 tumors (Fig. 6A). On the contrary, untreated NCI-H1975 tumors had significant differences in the fractions after baseline (p < 0.01) and paired t-tests showed that fraction at day 10 (0.1 ± 0.03, p < 0.05) and 15 (0.13 ± 0.04, p < 0.01) was significantly higher than at baseline (0.06 ± 0.02, Fig. 6B). TPZ treated tumors also showed a significant effect of treatment via one-way ANOVA analysis (p < 0.05). Paired t-test showed that day 15 fraction (0.09 ± 0.04) were higher than day 0 (0.06 ± 0.02, p = 0.058) and day 10 (0.07 ± 0.02, p < 0.05).

Discussion

Previous studies have shown that incorporation of hypoxia and perfusion imaging techniques (18F-FMISO PET and EPR oximetry) were able to predict the response to TH-302 in squamous cell carcinoma SCCVII, human colon cancer cell line HT29, C6 glioblastomas and 9 L gliosarcomas56,57. In this study, the effect of a HAP, Tirapazamine, was studied in tumor xenograft models of non-small cell lung cancer (NCI-NCI-H1975) and epidermoid carcinoma (A431). PISTOL technique48, 50 was used in this study to measure the tumor oxygenation before and after therapy.

Previous PET based hypoxia imaging studies have shown the presence of hypoxia in A431 and NSCLC tumors41,58–60. In this study a lower baseline oxygenation in A431 was observed as compared to NCI-H1975 tumors. As the distribution of hypoxia could vary within the tumor regions, the tumor was segmented into central and peripheral regions for analysis. Both regions of A431 tumors showed lower pO2 values than the H1975. Both tumor lines showed presence of hypoxia (pixels with pO2 < 10 torr) using PISTOL technique, thus further supporting the use of these models to assess HAP efficacy.

Previous clinical studies have shown that most NSCLC tumors acquired resistance against the first-generation multi-kinase inhibitors such as geftinib and erlotinib61. The efficacy of EGFR-TKIs is dependent on the expression of wild-type EGFR and the types of mutations in EGFR (sensitizing or resistance inducing) and their level of expression. NCI-H1975 expresses the double-mutant L858R/T790M EGFR which imparts resistance against tyrosine kinase inhibitors62, while A431 has overexpression of wild type EGFR63. EGFR receptors are over-expressed in regions with tumor hypoxia and are known to contribute towards increase tumor proliferation and resistance to therapy2. Hypoxia is known to be a driving factor for resistance to therapy, but it is not always the case in all the cancer sub-types18. It is important that HAPs are able to target the hypoxic regions that are contributing towards resistance to conventional therapies. TPZ, on the other hand, showed a completely different trend between A431 and NCI-H1975. In-spite of the fact that A431 tumors were more hypoxic, the TPZ treatment was only effective in NCI-H1975 tumors. The mild activation threshold of TPZ might affect its diffusion range, resulting in early metabolism of the drug before reaching the severe hypoxic regions of the A431 tumors64. The drug has also been known to exert a limited bystander effect65. Overall lower levels of baseline oxygenation may indicate poorer perfusion, leading to poor transport of the pro-drug and hence reduced efficacy.

Our results indicate that apart from hypoxia, there are other factors that might contribute towards the efficacy of HAPs such as the presence of reductases that would convert the pro-drug to its toxic counterpart and sensitivity of the tumor cells towards the toxic metabolite. Analysis for the presence of these reductases in A431 and NCI-H1975 cell lines would assist in analyzing the response and correlate with the results obtained. The efficacy of HAP is also dependent on the half-life of the cytotoxic counterpart. A longer half-life would enable the effector to target more regions in the tumors before being metabolized and excreted.

Therapy induced changes in oxygenation are an important factor for examining treatment efficacy. Previous studies have shown that treatment with EGFR-TKIs geftinib and erlotinib resulted in tumor regression as well as a decrease in hypoxia in NSCLC tumors60,66. In our case, the TPZ treatment of NCI-H1975 tumors resulted in a significant decrease in the oxygenation of tumor centers at day 5 and day 10 post-treatment, which is in agreement with the previous studies that show Tirapazamine causes vascular shutdown in the center of the tumor67–70. This pattern was further validated by the significantly higher hypoxic fraction (obtained from pimonidazole staining) in TPZ treated NCI-H1975 tumors as compared to the untreated control. While tumor growth was delayed in these tumors, its unlikely that a single dose of a hypoxia targeted drug may be enough to achieve tumor regression and multiple timed doses along with a combination therapy approach with might be more efficacious.

With the use of PISTOL technique, we were able to quantitatively determine the levels of oxygenation, unlike other techniques such as endogenous markers derived from MRI (BOLD/TOLD) or from PET. The technique was also successful in determining the therapy-induced changes in the tumor oxygenation and can quantitate the degree of hypoxia. Pimonidazole based immunohistochemistry provides a qualitative assessment of hypoxia and thus we analyzed the distribution of hypoxia post-therapy, but it is not a quantitative technique for measurement of tumor oxygenation. Nevertheless, it does provide a useful window into the regions that are most likely to be targeted by a hypoxia targeting prodrug such as Tirapazamine. The hypoxic fractions obtained from pimonidazole or PET imaging cannot be used to differentiate between mild and severe hypoxia and anoxia, unlike PISTOL oximetry. Pimonidazole is limited to the tumor regions that are well perfused and thus might fail to generate an accurate map of distribution of hypoxia.

A potential limitation of our current PISTOL probe is that it is a passive probe and pO2 measurements can only be extracted from the regions that contain HMDSO droplets, which might not represent the oxygenation of the entire tumor. Keeping this limitation in mind we aimed at depositing the HMDSO droplets uniformly at a single plane in the tumor. The significant differences observed in the center vs. periphery of the tumors suggest that this method was able to report the heterogeneity in the tumors. Our analysis of percentage of pixels from the tumor region that had HMDSO deposited and pO2 measured is shown in supplementary figure S8. Analysis of the mean percentage of pixels from each mouse showed that on an average we were able to capture oxygenation from 16 ± 10%, 19 ± 14% and 13 ± 9% regions of the entire tumor, center, and periphery respectively. It is important to note that this sampling, although random, is not guaranteed to proportionately represent the tumor heterogeneity in oxygenation. For hypoxia targeted therapy, metrics such as baseline hypoxic fraction might be more important to assess than baseline mean pO2, although the current study reports no differences in the PISTOL based baseline hypoxic fractions between the two tumor types that had significantly different response to Tirapazamine. While the extracted hypoxic fraction measures from PISTOL (or analogous19F oximetry)may be used to indicate presence of hypoxia, it is uncertain whether they scale to the rest of the tumor due to their dependence on the limited, manual distribution of the reporter probe within the tumor.

There is also a possibility that HMDSO droplets might be deposited in the anoxic or necrotic regions of the tumor during the intra-tumoral injection, affecting the measured mean pO2, thus it is necessary to consider this experimental limitation and exclude pO2 measurements from such regions in future. Redistribution of the injected reporter probe (HMDSO in this case) is also possible due to interstitial fluid pressure. The correlation between the efficacy of hypoxia-activated prodrugs (HAPs) and mean baseline tumor pO2, as measured by MR oximetry and pimonidazole staining, remains inconclusive. This highlights the complexity of tumor hypoxia and the necessity for identifying additional predictive biomarkers that can be derived from imaging data or molecular profiling. The inherent heterogeneity of hypoxia and perfusion within and across different tumor types, as well as between patients, complicates the stratification of patients and the optimization of HAP therapies. To advance the clinical utility of HAPs, it is essential to develop refined stratification strategies that take into account not only mean pO2 levels but also the spatial and temporal dynamics of hypoxia within tumors. To this end, qualitative MRI techniques for volumetric imaging of hypoxia, such as using hypoxia targeted contrast agents46, might complement quantitative MR oximetry. Furthermore, the integration of data on oxygen consumption rates, and their modulation by pO2, into the assessment of tumor metabolism could enhance our understanding of the metabolic adaptations associated with cancer progression. These parameters are crucial for the rational design of novel HAPs and other hypoxia-targeted therapies. Comprehensive screening of multiple tumor models for their hypoxic profiles and responsiveness to HAPs is imperative for identifying the most promising therapeutic candidates. Additionally, further advancements in imaging technologies and molecular assays are needed to better characterize hypoxia noninvasively and at high resolution, enabling more precise and individualized therapeutic interventions.
