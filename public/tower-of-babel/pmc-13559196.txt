A Seat at the (Dinner) Table: Food and Eating Practices in the Ontario Basic Income Pilot
Authors: Chloe Halpenny, Elaine Power, Lia Kirkpatrick, Tracy Smith‐Carrier
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThere is strong quantitative evidence that the risk of food insecurity declines when social programmes increase low‐income households' available income. However, there is little qualitative research that examines how food practices change with a sudden increase in income for poor households. Using a Bourdieusian‐informed theoretical framework, supplemented by recent literature on dignity, we analysed 79 semi‐structured, open‐ended interviews with recipients of the prematurely cancelled Ontario Basic Income Pilot (OBIP). Interviewees described how increased income improved their ability to afford enough and better‐quality food, decreased their engagement in unsatisfactory survival strategies, mediated stronger relations with family and friends and facilitated more pleasure in food practices. These results reflect a ‘logic of practice’ in a very different context than Bourdieu's 1960s France: a consumer society where health is a ‘super value’ and belonging is gained through consumption. Our analysis suggests that for those living in poverty, income, not habitus, is the most important contributor to the logic of food practices. Our results contradict popular myths about the food practices of those who live in poverty and lend support to the call for a minimum income floor, adequate to meet basic needs, to promote health, household food security and dignity.

Introduction

Income is widely acknowledged as one of the most important determinants of health in Western industrialised countries, with a clear social gradient in health and longevity across the income spectrum. Low income, or poverty, limits access to other determinants of health and has profound impacts on health (Raphael 2020). Moreover, in our neoliberal consumer society, those living on low incomes have constricted ability to make consumer choices and thus to belong.

One manifestation of poverty is food insecurity—the inadequate or insecure access to food because of financial constraints. At the household level, marginal food insecurity is characterised by anxiety about obtaining food. At the moderate level, there are compromises in the quality or quantity of food, and with severe food insecurity, there is reduced intake, missed meals or going without food for a day or more (Li et al. 2023). Not surprisingly, those in food‐insecure households have worse dietary intakes and diet quality than those who live in food‐secure households (Hutchinson and Tarasuk 2022). Food insecurity is also highly correlated with a range of chronic health conditions (Clark et al. 2025; Gundersen and Ziliak 2015) and higher healthcare costs (Tarasuk et al. 2015). In Canada, rates of food insecurity have increased dramatically since 2021 (Fafard St‐Germain et al. 2025).

Consistent with neoliberal state withdrawal from social programmes and austerity budgeting, the primary response to food insecurity in high‐income countries is food charity (especially food banks), which is problematic in many respects (Garthwaite 2016; Lambie‐Mumford 2017). The only clearly effective intervention to mitigate food insecurity is income supplementation (or the equivalent) (Gundersen 2022; Idzerda et al. 2024, 2025; Loopstra 2018).

Although there is qualitative research documenting the food‐related experiences of those who are food insecure, there is little that explores the impact of a sudden increase in monthly household income. In the case of the Ontario Basic Income Pilot (OBIP), the context for this study, there are some tantalising hints. Hamilton and Mulvale (2019) report that four of five participants in their study spontaneously noted being able to afford healthier food. Another study, with a convenience sample of OBIP recipients, found that most reported healthier diets, using food banks less often and skipping fewer meals (Ferdosi and McDowell 2020; McDowell and Ferdosi 2020). In an exploration of the OBIP's effects on participants' mental health, Smith‐Carrier and Power (2025) noted recipients' ability to purchase better‐quality food, whereas Halpenny (2023) found that disabled OBIP participants were better able to afford food that helped them manage the symptoms of illness or impairment. However, to date, there has been no in‐depth analysis of the impact of the OBIP on food practices, reflecting broader knowledge gaps. In this paper, we analyse 79 semi‐structured, in‐depth interviews with OBIP recipients to explore the effects of a sudden increase in monthly household income on food practices using a Bourdieusian theoretical lens, supplemented by recent scholarship on dignity as a form of symbolic capital.

Theorising Food Practices

Theorising Food Practices With Pierre Bourdieu

One of the most important contemporary sociologists, Pierre Bourdieu, included food preferences in his influential study of how power reproduces itself through culture, aesthetics and taste, published as Distinction: A Social Critique of the Judgement of Taste (Bourdieu 1984 [1979]). Scholars have since made good use of Bourdieu's concepts, including habitus, field, different forms of capital, distance from necessity, the taste of freedom and the logic of practice, to theorise the relationship between class, health and lifestyle activities (Williams 1995) and show how food and eating practices are markers of class distinctions (Sato et al. 2016).

As Smith Maguire (2016, 12) explains, class ‘shapes food practices through access to resources—not simply the resources of money and time to shop, prepare and eat in certain ways but also the tastes or dispositions to do so in particular ways’. The ‘dispositions’ that Smith Maguire describes are a product of the habitus, Bourdieu's way of explaining how behaviour is conditioned, producing regularities associated with social structures, such as class, gender and race, without being determined. Bourdieu posited that the primary habitus, acquired in childhood, and the tastes associated with it, is the most durable, leading his critics to charge that the concept is too deterministic, ignoring individual agency and reflexivity (Sayer 2010).

Habitus interacts with various forms of capital to produce practice—what we do in our everyday lives—which has its own logic in specific contexts or fields. The ‘logic of practice’ results from the interaction of habitus, field, and the amount and use of various forms of capital. What appears to be a logical practice from one position in social space may not appear reasonable at all from another position in social space. As Bourdieu (1996) explains, it is the job of the researcher to use reflexivity to account for the differences in logics of practice arising from the different social spaces occupied by the researcher and research participants, to make the research participants' logic of practice understandable.

One of the features of Bourdieu's use of the concept of capital is that different forms are interconvertible. He identified four main types of capital: economic, cultural, social and symbolic. Economic capital is the most straightforward and the easiest to exchange for other types of capital. Cultural capital exists in the embodied state (e.g., the way a person carries themselves), the objectified state (e.g., cultural goods such as books) and the institutionalised state (e.g., educational qualifications). Social capital is gained from networks of relationships, including institutionalised forms, such as the family. Symbolic capital is derived from the arbitrariness of the other forms of capital when their nature is misrecognised (Power 1999; Swartz 1997).

Following Bourdieu (1984), food studies scholars have found that proximity or distance from economic necessity produces two key dispositions towards food, ‘the taste of necessity’ and ‘the taste of freedom’ (Beagan, Power and Chapman 2015; Beagan, Chapman et al. 2016). For those with low economic capital, the logic of food practices is one of necessity, to obtain adequate amounts of filling food. Bourdieu (1984 [1979], 372) maintains that for the working class, the habitus ‘is a virtue made of necessity… necessity imposes a taste for necessity’, suggesting that food tastes and preferences remain stable even as economic and cultural capitals change. However, contemporary scholars in Canada and the United States have found that knowledge about healthy eating, a form of cultural capital, is now widespread across classes and no longer functions as a marker of class distinction (Smith Maguire 2016). This opens the possibility that, when it comes to food practices, economic and cultural capitals are more important than the habitus for those living on low incomes. A Canadian study of participants who had experienced upward or downward class mobility found that they used food practices to symbolically mark class boundaries, sometimes affiliating with their class of origin and sometimes with their current class location, depending on which virtues or moral disposition they wished to signal (Beagan et al. 2015). This suggests a more fluid, dynamic and agentic logic of practice than one in which durable dispositions of habitus and taste prevail.

Theorising the Symbolic Aspects of Food Practices

It is a truism that food sustains biological life, but it is so much more than nutrition. Food sits at the intersection of the material, social, cultural, political and symbolic, reflecting identity and status. As political philosopher Stone (1988, 71) puts it, what we eat is ‘a sign of membership, social status and spiritual worth. Eating the same food as others is a basic mark of belonging’. Feeding others and sharing food helps us to meet what Stone (1988, 77) calls our ‘communal needs,’ including ‘community, solidarity, a sense of belonging; dignity, respect, self‐esteem, and honor; friendship and love’.

In consumer society, belonging is attained through consumption, which is denied to those with limited economic resources (Bauman 1998). For those on the margins, the symbolic quest for belonging, dignity and respect is a key part of the logic of practice. Some food scholars have used Bourdieu's concept of symbolic capital to account for this, but as Sayer (2010) argues, Bourdieu's understanding of habitus and forms of capital is predominantly instrumental, in the pursuit of power and advantage. The quest to fulfil communal needs to enhance well‐being fits uneasily in Bourdieu's schema; Sayer (2010) proposes that we draw from political philosophy to modify Bourdieu's work to take account of moral sentiments and ethical concerns.

Recently, food studies scholars have turned to thinking about dignity in the context of food charity, as a ‘transformative framework with potential to reimagine justice’ (Cresswell Riol and Connelly 2025, 2). Although some are rightly concerned with how to maximise dignity in food assistance programmes (Brenton et al. 2025; Herrington and Mix 2021), Cresswell Riol and Connelly (2025) suggest that food charity inherently undermines dignity by limiting freedom and choice, while generating stigma, shame, powerlessness and an expectation of gratitude. In the United States, Gundersen (2020, 2025) maintains that the success of the Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program (SNAP) is related to its promotion of dignity by allowing recipients to shop in grocery stores and make their own food choices, like everyone else, and by not requiring—nor discouraging—employment. Despite these advantages, SNAP recipients report stigma and would prefer to have their own money for food (Gaines‐Turner et al. 2019).

Background

In late 2017, the first of over 4000 individuals in Ontario, Canada, began receiving money as part of the OBIP. Launched by a Liberal provincial government, the social policy experiment trialled a new approach to poverty reduction by providing recipients with an unconditional basic income, ‘a payment to eligible couples or individuals that ensures a minimum income level, regardless of employment status’ (Ontario 2017).

Pilot participants were required to be between 18 and 64 years of age and reside in one of the three test sites: Hamilton/Brantford (1000 participants), Lindsay (2000 participants) or Thunder Bay (1000 participants). The Pilot adopted a negative income tax design that targeted low‐income individuals and was limited to singles and couples reporting an annual income of less than $34,000 or $48,000, respectively. The benefit amount was 75% of the low‐income measure (LIM), which, combined with other provincial and federal tax credits and benefits, aimed to ‘provide an income that [would] meet household costs and average health‐related spending’ (Ontario 2017). Monthly payments equated to an annual income of up to $16,989 for individuals and $24,027 for couples, with disabled participants receiving a monthly top‐up of up to $500. The programme had a clawback rate of 50% for every dollar earned from employment.

For comparison, when the OBIP was first introduced, the maximum total annual benefit for a single person on ODSP or Ontario Works (OW)1 was $14,028 and $8,796, respectively (Income Security Advocacy Centre 2020). For those who were employed, Ontario’s minimum wage stood at $14.00 per hour in 2019, amounting to just over $27,000/year for anyone with full‐time work (Secretariat to the Expert Panel on Modern Federal Labour Standards 2019).

Even though the OBIP was designed to run for three years, a newly elected Progressive Conservative government announced its premature cancellation in July 2018, declaring, without evidence, that the OBIP was costing too much. The provincial premier stated that ‘a job’ is the best way out of poverty (CBC NEWS 2018), reflecting neoliberal political logic that minimises state responsibility for its citizens' well‐being. Data collection for the carefully designed evaluation (Ontario 2017) was halted immediately, with final payments delivered in March 2019.

Methods

In this paper, we analyse data from two separate research projects, drawing on West and Oldfather’s (1995, 454) pooled case comparison method as ‘a means for comparing separate but similar studies ex post facto’. This approach involves researchers pooling relevant raw data from distinct projects and conducting original analyses guided by new research questions. We draw on two projects: one conducted independently by the first author as part of a graduate thesis, and the other by the second and fourth authors (in collaboration with another researcher, now retired).

The two projects shared several conceptual and methodological similarities supporting their combined use for this article. Both focused on qualitatively documenting the experiences of OBIP participants and shared similar methods (individual semi‐structured interviews), inclusion criteria (membership in the Pilot’s treatment group) and approximate timing of data collection (near the Pilot’s end). Although developed separately with different interview guides, both projects employed broad lines of questioning about participants’ experiences receiving a BI, including prompts about the impact on different areas of their lives (e.g., health, housing, social activities) and open‐ended questions about changes they had observed during the Pilot. In both projects, discussions of food practices emerged organically in relation to other questions. The semi‐structured nature of interviews in both projects allowed for flexibility and responsiveness, meaning that when participants spoke at length about food or identified it as significant, interviewers encouraged this and asked follow‐up questions to yield further insights.

The decision to combine datasets for this article emerged from conversations between the first and second authors about the salience of food and eating practices across both projects’ transcripts. Although both projects aimed to capture participants’ experiences holistically, we were struck by how consistently these lines of inquiry elicited rich insights about participants' food practices and how these practices had changed during the Pilot. Our interest in exploring this further was informed by the second author’s expertise in food insecurity and poverty in Canada, and all authors’ research on and advocacy for BI.

Benefits of the pooled case comparison approach include more comprehensive findings and reduced burden of over‐research on structurally marginalised participants (West and Oldfather 1995; Tate and Happ 2018). Drawing on both datasets enabled us to construct a larger qualitative dataset, supporting cross‐validation of themes across distinct studies while providing greater nuance.

One consideration is the possible duplication of participants across both datasets, especially given the limited universe (n = 4000) of Pilot participants and potential interviewees. Because transcripts were anonymised prior to analysis, we were unable to directly identify duplicates. To minimise the likelihood of inadvertent double‐inclusion, the first author conducted a detailed review cross‐referencing sociodemographic and background characteristics (e.g., location, age, employment and education history) as well as specific details or anecdotes unlikely to be replicated across individuals. Where transcripts appeared potentially similar, a more thorough secondary review was conducted. We identified 10 such instances, and where duplication was confirmed, treated both interviews together as a single data source. The possibility that additional unidentified duplication occurred represents a methodological limitation; however, we are confident this would not substantially affect our findings. We observed a high degree of consistency—within each dataset and across the combined dataset—in the broad themes underpinning our analysis. We consider the size and diversity of our combined sample a strength in terms of the depth and nuance it affords.

Our analysis ultimately drew from 79 interviews representing 69 unique pilot participants. Sociodemographic data were not collected systematically in either project, limiting our ability to characterise the sample comprehensively; however, much of this information arose naturally in interviews, as summarised below.

Most participants had been receiving basic income for approximately 1 year at the time of interview, and all had been low‐income prior to enrolment, as required by the Pilot’s eligibility criteria. In terms of location, 22 of 69 participants (32%) were from Hamilton, 33 participants (48%) were from Lindsay, and 14 participants (20%) were from Thunder Bay, with Hamilton slightly over‐represented and Thunder Bay slightly under‐represented relative to the broader Pilot population. Although race and ethnicity were not systematically collected, available data suggest approximately 80% of OBIP participants identified as white, which appeared broadly consistent with our sample. A notable exception was among participants from Thunder Bay, several of whom self‐identified as Indigenous, reflecting the disproportionate Indigenous representation at that site (Basic Income Canada Network 2019).

Gender or sex was explicitly stated or clearly implied for 58 of 69 participants: 12 (21%) were men (one of whom self‐identified as trans), and 46 (79%) were women. This over‐representation of women is reflected in other qualitative OBIP research (Ferdosi and McDowell 2020) and may partly reflect that the first author’s project was framed as a feminist exploration of basic income, which may have shaped who responded. Age was discernible for 22 of 69 participants, with a mean of approximately 47 years; the sample included participants in their 20s through 60s.

Family and household status was discernible for 66 of 69 participants. The majority—53 of 66 (80%)—were single adults, of whom 16 had dependants or significant caregiving responsibilities (typically for children, grandchildren or disabled adults). Thirteen of 66 (20%) were married, common‐law or engaged, of whom eight had dependants; in total, 24 of 66 (36%) had dependants.

Prior to OBIP, 56 of 69 participants (81%) had been accessing provincial social assistance, with 47 receiving ODSP, the income support programme for individuals qualifying as disabled, and nine receiving OW. Further, for the 64 participants for whom this was discernible, 27 (42%) earned some income from employment during the pilot. Other income sources included child benefits, Canada Pension Plan (Disability) and income from a partner’s employment or pension.

Following an inductive, reflexive approach to thematic analysis (Braun and Clarke 2006, 2019), anonymised transcripts were reviewed in detail by the first author to develop a preliminary coding scheme, finalised in consultation with the second author. Early codes included the following: experiences of food insecurity prior to OBIP; abandonment or reduction of survival strategies upon receiving BI; changes in food security and dietary adequacy; changes in types of food purchased; health and well‐being effects; expressions of generosity or care related to food; participation in social activities involving food; fulfilment of social expectations and roles around eating; food‐related enjoyment or treats; and participants’ reflections on the significance of these changes. Coding was led by the first author using NVivo to generate initial themes and subthemes based on patterns of shared meaning, brought to the research team for further discussion and refinement through an iterative, reflexive process. Across these codes, we observed a progression from the meeting of immediate needs and reduction of hardship towards outcomes beyond food security which informed the structure of our findings. All names are pseudonyms.

Findings

From the Logic of Scarcity to the Dignity of Sufficiency

Participation in the OBIP enabled interviewees to afford adequate amounts of food and to reduce or abandon survival strategies, like using food banks, that they found unacceptable. Interviewees described regular experiences of moderate and severe food insecurity prior to OBIP enrolment, characterised by reduced portion sizes, skipped meals or full days without eating. Those with children protected them as much as possible. Zoe recounted:

[My husband and I] were practically living on a ramen noodle diet…My daughter was taken care of through her baby bonus, but …We were at our absolute wits' ends trying to maintain even basic nutrition. We had to skip meals all the time.

The physical, psychological, emotional and social toll of insufficient income significantly affected every interviewee, manifesting as chronic stress and anxiety about food, difficult sacrifices and trade‐offs between groceries and other necessities, and reliance on food banks. These survival strategies were widely perceived by interviewees as necessary yet inadequate, stigmatising and fundamentally unjust. Josephine articulated the near‐impossible choices that characterised life before the OBIP: ‘Picking and choosing bills over feeding your kids, it shouldn't happen at all’.

Participants who used food banks emphasised the lack of choice, describing food allocations that were unhealthy, failed to account for dietary restrictions, were nearing or past the expiry date or were simply not enough. Many relayed deep feelings of shame and stigma associated with accessing food banks, leading some to avoid them ‘at all costs’ (Leanne) or even outright denounce them: ‘In this supposedly great country of ours, nobody should have to beg for food. People should have money to go out and buy groceries. Nobody should have to use a food bank’ (Tristan). Together, the accounts of OBIP participants add to the significant body of evidence that food banks are fundamentally unable to meaningfully address food insecurity (Tarasuk et al. 2014, 2020) and contribute to a profound sense of injustice and indignity among those who are food insecure.

Interviewees widely reported dramatic and immediate improvements in food security after OBIP enrolment, which they attributed to the amount and reliability of the payments. This resulted in more satisfactory meals and more discretionary food around the home: ‘We were eating fuller meals at dinner time, no one was going hungry at any point, we had constant snacks in the cupboard’ (Hannah).

In some cases, the significance of a basic income was not just what it allowed participants to do but also what it allowed them to avoid. As Barbara explained, ‘Being on basic income, there [were] no more day olds, there was no food bank, there was no good food box. I just went shopping’. Others identified a range of survival strategies they were able to give up, including purchasing lower‐quality food; restricting meals or portion sizes; relying on food charity; foregoing other expenses to afford food; and borrowing from friends, family or predatory lenders to make ends meet.

The swift effects of the OBIP included ‘the mental benefits of being secure [and] knowing you don’t have to do the hustle each day to get enough to eat’ (Penelope), highlighting how the OBIP dissolved the stress associated with food insecurity. Food, or the lack thereof, was also symbolic of other aspects of living in poverty—and the relative abundance that came with BI. For instance, Lucas noted the pivotal role basic income played in his wife’s decision to pursue further education, stating that in the absence of a ‘full fridge’ and other basic needs being met, such a choice would have seemed impossible. Another used her experience of food insecurity to speak about how basic income transformed her perception of time:

Being on welfare and worrying about what I'm going to eat tomorrow, I can only think of today. With basic income…I don't even know how to explain this. It's as if someone says to you, ‘open your hands,’ and they give you time.

(Sabrina)

This metaphor powerfully captures how basic income alleviated the everyday immediate pressures faced by recipients, opening up previously unfathomable possibilities and ways of living. From a Bourdieusian perspective, in which the habitus orients a person towards the future, this is unexpected, suggesting that for those living on low incomes in contemporary Canada, an increase in economic capital provides people with a new sense of themselves.

Aligning Food Practices With Healthy Eating Knowledge

Once released from the strict necessity imposed by inadequate income, interviewees reported significant improvements in the variety and quality of what they ate—even though they were still living on low incomes. This is not what Bourdieu would predict; in these cases, necessity did not produce a ‘taste of necessity’. But it does align with more recent Canadian research demonstrating that those living on low incomes understand the importance of healthy eating and prefer to eat healthy foods if their incomes allow (Beagan, Power and Chapman 2015; Beagan, Chapman et al. 2015; Baumann et al. 2017). In a neoliberal context where health serves as an individualised marker of ‘good citizenship’ (Crawford 2006), access to healthy foods and eating practices represents a strategy of belonging for those experiencing poverty (Beagan et al. 2016).

Several participants characterised their ability to buy more nutritious and higher‐quality food as the most significant effect of receiving a basic income. This finding is especially notable because the focus of these two research studies was not specific to food practices but focused on the impact of the OBIP on recipients' lives more generally. As Hannah declared, ‘my most important change was being able to provide my kids with healthier choices.’ This short quote poignantly depicts how the provision of healthy food to one's children is a symbol of being a ‘proper’ mother (Brenton 2017; Wright et al. 2015; Martin 2018) and illustrates the distress of being unable to do so because of financial constraints.

More income brought more agency at the grocery store, often translating to more fresh, nutritious food on participants' tables. Interviewees described a marked decrease in their reliance on processed, boxed, canned and frozen foods during the Pilot and a greater ability to buy higher‐quality protein and more fresh produce. Anna reflected on this change:

I can’t even tell you how much of a difference it made in our lives to go from where you eat nothing but carbs—so it’s bread, pasta, bread, pasta, because that’s all you could afford—to eating fresh vegetables, and maybe you could have more meat.

The reduced financial strain meant decisions at the grocery store could be made with confidence and ease rather than caution, relieving the unpleasantness of shopping on a tight budget (Beagan et al. 2018). Often, the freedom to choose healthier foods was itself a source of dignity; as Riley explained, ‘being able to afford healthier foods…made me feel good about myself’. Gwen offered a vivid example:

I didn’t have to have my calculator open…to see if I could make it to the dairy fridge at the end of the store…before I ran out of money. If I wanted a cauliflower, I could have a cauliflower. You can’t do that on ODSP. On ODSP, you eat bread, pasta, rice, all the cheap and cheerful carbs that you can, because you’ve got to get filled up and you’ve got to do it as cheaply as possible. [Basic income] changed the way I interact with the world [emphasis added].

In Gwen's remarks, we see again that the effect of more adequate income on food and shopping practices stands symbolically for a broader impact. In this case, the income that provides the opportunity for Gwen to choose fresh, healthy foods represents a new way of interacting with the world: more freedom, choice and dignity.

As other research about the OBIP has shown (e.g., Ferdosi and McDowell 2020; McDowell and Ferdosi 2020), our interviewees widely reported improvements in their physical health. Many attributed this to the enhanced purchasing power and consumption choices that the Pilot facilitated. As Melanie summarised, ‘We started feeling healthier because we were eating better’. Participants’ depictions of ‘feeling healthier’ included improved sleep, reduced frequency and severity of illnesses, increased energy for everyday and fitness activities and, for one participant, a better ability to resist substance use.

Disabled and chronically ill participants also attributed personal health improvements to the type and quality of food they could access during the Pilot, similar to Halpenny's (2023) findings. Recipients with distinct dietary needs referenced the value of additional income in managing the additional, often hidden, costs of living with a disability (Mofidi 2026), including being able to afford food that supported rather than undermined health. For instance, Ethan noted that while receiving BI, their partner with Crohn’s disease ‘was able to eat the things that didn’t upset her as much as processed foods that are basically the only things they [social assistance programs] make you able to afford’.

Participants’ accounts starkly illustrate the flawed logic of health behaviour interventions for people living in poverty that do not address socioeconomic inequities. As Kayleigh poignantly observed,

It makes no sense: one of the things doctors tell you is eat right, rest, no stress, and exercise. And except for the exercise portion, everything else is related to income in some way, shape, or form. It’s almost as if you’re non‐compliant if you’re living in poverty.

This perspective was widely shared among participants, who stressed the value and importance of better nutrition while condemning a context that views this as a personal responsibility instead of a basic right. As Miriam asserted, ‘vegetables and healthy food shouldn't be a luxury’. Canadians put high value on the nation's commitment to universal healthcare access; however, OBIP participants understood that access to good health is severely constrained by poverty. Their contrasting experiences, before and during the OBIP, brought this into sharp focus.

Cultivating Social Capital

The relative financial security afforded to OBIP recipients facilitated food‐mediated opportunities for social connection, enriching participants’ lives beyond more immediate, material benefits. Many interviewees described a renewed capacity for cultivating relationships with important people in their lives, which they attributed to the more relaxed and open emotional state afforded by greater financial stability. Such was the case for Evelyn:

It made [our family] closer because I felt more able to talk with them. I was always afraid of talking with them [before OBIP], that I’d be saying ‘no, I’m out of food, I’m out of money…’ … When I was on basic income, we just had conversations.

Basic income erased the difficulty of having to choose between meeting social versus material needs, freeing participants to ‘not have to worry about “oh, I can’t do this, and I can’t do that” because with what little I do have, I have to put food on the table’ (Pamela).

For Tristan, BI transformed the act of going for coffee with a friend from an unattainable luxury to a viable, valuable opportunity for connection—albeit one that was lost with the Pilot's termination:

I could actually go out and have a cup of coffee and meet up with my friends [on OBIP]. I can't do that now because I don't have any money for coffee. That was a big help, being able to socialise. Knowing that I don't have money to go for coffee and then when somebody asks me out and they say that they're going to pay for my coffee, my anxiety goes up, because I feel like I'm being a mooch.

Others similarly described past experiences dining out that were characterised by stress, embarrassment and discomfort. The relief offered by a basic income was palpable; as Sylvia put it, ‘I didn't have to worry about if I have to borrow money, or they have to pay for me, or what happens when the bill comes and I don't have money’. The monthly OBIP payments offered recipients dignity and freedom:

If [family or friends] wanted to go to eat, I wouldn’t hesitate…Because I can actually pay for mine, and I can pay for theirs if I want. So that was nice. That was kind of freeing, to be able to go, ‘yeah, I can go’.

(Pamela)

For many, the freedom to say ‘yeah, I can go’ marked a dramatic change from their lives prior to basic income. Those who had previously relied on provincial income support programmes drew stark and compelling illustrations of the relationship between poverty, isolation and social exclusion. Gwen was among several participants who described feeling ‘incredibly isolated’ while receiving social assistance:

I go out once every two weeks, buy what few groceries I can afford to buy, and the rest of the time I’m in the house because I can’t afford gas, I can’t afford groceries, I can’t afford to go out to Timmie’s [coffee shop] and have a cup of coffee, I can’t afford anything.

The relative income adequacy and security offered by the OBIP meant that recipients could ‘afford to participate in society like everybody else’ (Gwen). Although this participation took diverse forms, it often involved food and eating.

BI also affected how interviewees engaged with food as an expression of gratitude, reciprocity or care, part of our ‘communal needs’ that contribute to dignity, in ways that had previously been out of reach. Many described treating friends or family members while dining out, bringing food for sick relatives or gathering loved ones for shared meals: ‘I could put a meal on the table…and have a family meal on a Sunday with my children and my grandchildren, and have a little treat in the house for them’ (Christine). Several participants conveyed satisfaction, pride and joy associated with ‘being able to be the one treating [others] to a cup of coffee for a change instead of always being on the receiving end’ (Gwen). This was rooted in people’s interest in valuing, nurturing and meaningfully contributing to social relationships, with many perceiving reciprocity as essential to this commitment.

Overall, the accounts of OBIP participants described in this section underscore the central role that food and eating play in constituting and nourishing social relationships. On one hand, they highlight how poverty restricts individuals’ access to and participation in social and communal activities that foster a sense of belonging, connection and inclusion. Perhaps more importantly, similar to Smith‐Carrier and Power's (2025) findings, they expose the potential of unconditional cash transfers in challenging some of the entrenched, systemic barriers to social inclusion—including where these intersect with food and eating practices.

A Taste of Freedom and Pleasure

Released from strict necessity, interviewees frequently remarked on the newfound freedom to approach food with a sense of playfulness and curiosity, a luxury many could not afford prior to the OBIP. This suggests a significant shift in the logic of practice, related to an increase in economic capital, not a change in the habitus. This new freedom took several forms, from experimenting with new ingredients and recipes to purchasing items simply because they tasted good. As a result, many participants found themselves experiencing food not just as a strict necessity or a source of stress, but also as a contributor to joy and pleasure, a taste of freedom.

Interviewees relished the financial flexibility to purchase food items they had previously denied themselves because they were luxuries, from speciality coffee to Nutella. As previous research has shown, households living in poverty cannot afford the risk of trying new foods that family members might dislike and thus could be wasted (Martin 2018; Beagan et al. 2016). By providing the means for individuals to consider not only what they could afford but also what they wanted, craved or were curious about, BI offered a more pleasurable and fulfilling approach to food choices compared to pre‐Pilot circumstances. As Morgan summarised: ‘Sometimes you just want to treat yourself to something good’. Other participants highlighted the profound mental and emotional benefits associated with ‘being able to just not even think about it—you don't have to worry that if you buy that exotic piece of fruit, that it’s going to break you’ (Pamela).

This newfound spontaneity and flexibility afforded by BI was particularly salient for parents. One father remarked that ‘it was nice being able to not worry about stopping at the store and grabbing [my daughter] a freezie or something on the way home’ (Samuel). Accounts like this underscore the immense challenges of navigating parenthood under economic constraints, especially when food and other purchases are a primary means by which parents express their love and constitute familial relationships in consumer society (Power 2005; Martin 2018). Many parents referenced the satisfaction associated with ‘not having to say no to my kid’, noting that ‘it was nice to be able to be like, “yeah, throw it in the cart” once in a while’ (Genevieve). Another parent reinforced this perspective:

I could say, ‘you know what? Let’s go walk down the street and get ice cream.’ It was nice to be able to do that, instead of saying ‘sorry, guys, we had to buy food and we had to pay bills, we can’t’.

Although some participants sought the pleasure of treating their loved ones to familiar items, like ice cream, others used BI to pursue food and eating decisions grounded in creativity, curiosity and experimentation:

When I’m on a super low fixed income, I kind of just buy the exact same thing all the time. I don't do a lot of experimenting because when I try to experiment, I get a crazy bill at the checkout and I'm like, ‘oh my God.’ Next time I'm just buying what I normally buy because…everything is so expensive.

(Jenna)

Participants described the pleasures of engaging with food through the lens of curiosity and exploration, something taken for granted in higher‐income households (Beagan et al. 2016). Access to a more reliable, adequate income alleviated the overwhelming sense of financial risk associated with trying new foods (Martin 2018; Beagan et al. 2016) and broke the monotony that characterises food choices of ‘strict necessity’ when households are food insecure.

In approaching food as a source of pleasure, adventure and discovery, participants’ eating practices on the OBIP reflected the notion that ‘people need food, but they need other things as well, including the occasional luxury to allow them to feel like human beings—complex, contradictory, and full of meaning’ (de Souza 2019, 77). Interviewees' testaments to the joy of ice cream, pizza and other occasional indulgences offer a powerful counternarrative in a society where the surveillance and public scrutiny of low‐income individuals’ spending are normalised (Hagerty and Barasz 2020). By seeking out and asserting the importance of these moments of pleasure, Pilot participants resisted—and in some cases, explicitly rejected—the austere societal expectations unjustly imposed on those in poverty. BI gave recipients a ‘feeling of escape from the stresses of poverty’ (Brown et al. 2021, 429), facilitating food and eating decisions that were informed more by spontaneity and pleasure and less by necessity. It was not only what participants purchased that was significant but also what these purchases represented, as a ‘symbol of one’s cultural belonging in the larger society’ (Chen 2016, 155), and the sense of dignity and normalcy they conveyed.

Discussion

Our analysis suggests that a sudden increase in economic capital for interviewees living on profoundly inadequate incomes changed more than their ability to purchase larger amounts of the same food, as Bourdieu's understanding of the habitus and the formation of taste would suggest. Even though interviewees were still living close to the poverty line, they were able to move away from the logic of strict necessity and were better able to align their food practices with the dominant discourse of healthy eating, to use food to cultivate social capital and to approach food with a taste of freedom, curiosity and pleasure. Moreover, for some participants, their new orientation to food was also symbolic of a new orientation to their futures and their interactions in the world. With greater economic capital came greater freedom to choose. In neoliberal consumer society, this freedom is a profound source of belonging and dignity. Our analysis suggests that for low‐income Canadians, economic capital is the most significant component of the logic of food practices, allowing interviewees to align their food practices with other members of their community.

Myths and stereotypes about people living in poverty blame them for their situation, attributing their poverty to ‘bad choices’, lack of ‘personal responsibility’, and other personal and moral failings (O'Hara 2020; Smith‐Carrier and On 2022). According to the myths, poor choices and irresponsibility also extend to food practices (Pineau et al. 2021), with the implication that people living in poverty could eat healthily and avoid food insecurity if only they had nutrition education and better budgeting, shopping and cooking skills. More perniciously, the ‘toxic poverty narrative’ with its ‘malignant’ stereotypes (O'Hara 2020) is pervasive, reproduced by media and elected officials, infiltrating public consciousness and informing the restrictive, punitive structures of social assistance programmes (Smith‐Carrier and On 2022; O'Hara 2020). Our analysis powerfully refutes this ‘othering’ of those living on low incomes.

The OBIP was an unconditional income security programme that featured none of the punitive or paternalistic aspects of social assistance programmes. Practically, the OBIP changed only one (albeit important) aspect of interviewees' lives: their monthly income. Our qualitative analysis shows the fundamental importance of adequate income for household food security and healthy eating, supporting and storying quantitative research that has come to the same conclusion. Our analysis of interviewees' accounts suggests that the OBIP enhanced physical health and other aspects of health, often mediated by the social practice of sharing food and drink with others, reciprocating practices of hospitality and experiencing the joys and pleasures of food and eating. The OBIP recognised the humanity of recipients, restored their sense of dignity and belonging and upheld their freedom to make choices appropriate for themselves and their families (Swift and Power 2021). Food and eating practices were a significant part of these positive changes.

Cresswell Riol and Connelly (2025, 4) argue that re‐centring dignity in considerations of how to address food insecurity promotes a social policy approach rooted in solidarity and human rights, recognising our ‘shared humanity, mutual responsibility, and the inalienable rights of all’. Although food scholars speak of ‘the right to food’ and ‘food justice’, Article 25 of the 1948 Universal Declaration of Human Rights states that everyone has the right to a standard of living adequate for their health and well‐being, including food. As human rights scholars insist, this means that the right to food is inseparable from other economic and social rights. An unconditional basic income that meets basic needs gives recipients the dignity of choice not just over food but over all budgetary decisions. The OBIP gave recipients a seat at the dinner table with everyone else, facilitating a deep sense of belonging and dignity that is not available in social programmes and food charities that provide a different (lesser) set of menu options for those living on low incomes.

After hearing interviewees' profound stories of the positive impacts of the OBIP on their food practices and health, it is difficult to imagine the struggles of those who had to return to social assistance programmes, which are well below the deep income poverty threshold in Ontario, as in most of Canada (Laidley and White 2025). We are convinced that the design of the OBIP had multiple, distinct advantages over social assistance programmes, notably in its lack of conditionality, paternalism and surveillance, and thus in its promotion of dignity. However, our analysis suggests that any government that cared about the health and well‐being of its poorest citizens could expect to see positive impacts on food practices by simply increasing income security programme payments.

Our analysis supports Sayer's (2010) contention that Bourdieu's conception of the habitus is too deterministic, at least in 21st‐century Canada, and that meeting communal needs for dignity, belonging, respect, friendship and love is significant in the logic of food practices for those living on low incomes. In our consumer society, where belonging depends on consumption (Bauman 1998), it is not surprising that meeting symbolic, communal needs is important for those who have been marginalised by their poverty. We hope other scholars will continue to put their empirical data on practices with implications for health, including food, in conversation with Bourdieu's theoretical framework to continue to refine its usefulness in different cultural and historical contexts, while considering intersecting social positions, including gender, race and class.
