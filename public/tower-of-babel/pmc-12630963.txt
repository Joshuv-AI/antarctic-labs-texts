Effect of skin to skin contact on father infant bonding in a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Nazlı Emel Özer Yurdal, Sevgi Özsoy
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study aimed to determine the effect of skin-to-skin contact on father–infant bonding. This randomized controlled experimental study included fathers of 165 newborns delivered at the obstetrics units of Çanakkale Onsekiz Mart University Research Hospital and Çanakkale Mehmet Akif Ersoy State Hospital. The study will be conducted from June 2023 to April 2024. Fathers were randomly allocated to three groups: early skin-to-skin contact (n = 55), frequent skin-to-skin contact (n = 55), and standard care (n = 55). First data were collected at baseline using a questionnaire. At the third month, fathers completed a follow-up form and the Paternal–Infant Attachment Scale. Descriptive statistics, the Kruskal–Wallis test, stepwise multiple regression, and correlation analysis were used in the analysis. In the frequent skin-to-skin contact group, where fathers engaged in at least 15 min of skin-to-skin contact with their infants 6–7 days per week, the mean total score on the Paternal–Infant Bonding Scale was 72.44 ± 6.22. In the early skin-to-skin contact group, in which skin-to-skin contact was provided only once, the mean score was 69.29 ± 6.89, whereas in the standard care group, it was 65.69 ± 6.74. The differences between the groups were statistically significant (p < 0.05). It was found that changing the child’s diaper, the partner’s paid employment, joint soothing, and the fathers’ university education positively influenced paternal–infant bonding, whereas standard care membership had a negative effect. This study revealed that frequent skin-to-skin contact increased father–infant bonding, and these fathers took on a greater role in their babies’ care. Introduction

Skin-to-skin contact (SSC) is defined as the process of drying the baby after birth, leaving only a diaper on, and placing it directly on the parent’s bare chest and holding it there for at least one hour or until the first feeding is complete1. This practice, which is strongly recommended by the World Health Organization (WHO) and the United Nations Children’s Fund (UNICEF), is a proven method for ensuring the physiological stability of newborns, reducing stress levels, and strengthening parent‒infant bonding2.

Many studies have demonstrated the positive effects of SSC on the bonding process between mothers and babies. Studies have shown that early SSC encourages breastfeeding, reduces the stress level of newborns, prevents hypoglycamia and hypothermia, shortens crying periods, and enables the baby to gain weight more quickly3–5. In addition, SSC has positive effects on mothers, such as reducing postpartum pain, encouraging breastfeeding, and reducing the risk of postpartum depression6.

Attachment is defined as a lifelong emotional bond that begins before birth and is established between a baby and its caregivers7–10. According to the attachment theory developed by Bowlby, babies need to experience a secure attachment process to develop a healthy mental state11. Although the attachment process between mothers and babies has been widely studied, studies on father–infant attachment are limited.

The fathers’ physical contact with the baby, talking to him, and actively participating in his care facilitates their adaptation to the paternal role and strengthens father–infant bonding12,13. Some studies have shown that fathers’ SSC with their baby after birth reduces their stress level, makes them more sensitive to the baby, and facilitates their adaptation to the parenting role14–16.

Changes in gender roles in recent years have encouraged fathers to become individuals who take an active role in caring for their babies rather than simply providing economic support17. In this context, the bond established between the father and baby in the early period is highly important for the baby’s social and emotional development. However, the application of FSSC between the father and baby after birth is often neglected or only addressed in studies conducted with premature babies4,18.

Most studies focusing on the effects of father–infant SSC have been conducted on premature infants, where it has been shown to stabilize infants’ respiratory and heart rates, reduce crying times, support thermoregulation, and even improve early childhood neurodevelopmental outcomes following immediate SSC in very preterm neonates19–21. However, experimental studies examining the effects of father–infant SSC on attachment and the paternal role in healthy newborns are limited22. In a study conducted by Chen et al.22 with 83 first-time fathers aged 20 and over, fathers who had at least 15 min of skin-to-skin contact with their babies every day had higher father-infant attachment scale scores than those in the control group.

Supporting father–infant bonding has positive outcomes not only for the baby but also for the mother and father. A recent Turkish cohort showed that stronger paternal attachment was associated with lower parental anxiety and greater relationship satisfaction during the first postpartum year23. Active participation of fathers in the care of their babies reduces postpartum stress for the mother and facilitates fathers’ adaptation to the parenting role24. Providing more support to fathers in the postpartum process and encouraging the implementation of SSC not only for mothers but also for fathers will contribute to strengthening relationships within the family.

Research aim and hypotheses

Although skin-to-skin contact with the mother often begins early, father–infant skin-to-skin contact is frequently absent or delayed, which may adversely affect father–infant bonding. A review of the literature shows that, while numerous studies have examined mother–infant bonding, maternal skin-to-skin contact, and its effects on bonding, relatively few have investigated father–infant bonding. In Türkiye, the limited studies addressing father–infant bonding have been conducted at later ages, are descriptive in design, or focus on preterm infants; to our knowledge, no experimental studies have examined healthy newborns. Therefore, this study was conducted to determine the effect of skin-to-skin contact on father–infant bonding. A secondary aim was to identify variables associated with father–infant bonding. We tested the following hypotheses:

H0: There is no difference in fathers’ mean PIAS total scores among the FSSC, ESSC, and SC groups.

H1: There is a difference in fathers’ mean PIAS total scores among the groups.

Methods

Study design

This study was a randomized controlled trial (RCT) registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT07159477). From study design through reporting, we adhered to the CONSORT guidelines (including the checklist and flow diagram), incorporating methodological recommendations described by Hopewell et al.25. All methods were carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations. Written informed consent to participate in the study was obtained from all participants, and all the methods and are carried out in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethics committee approval was obtained for the study from Çanakkale Onsekiz Mart University Clinical Research Ethics Committee with the decision number 2023/02–23 dated 18.01.2023.

Design, setting, population, and sample

This study was conducted at two large hospitals in a major city in western Türkiye—one university hospital and one state hospital—with a combined bed capacity exceeding 1,000. The study population consisted of fathers of infants born in these hospitals between June 1, 2023, and April 15, 2024.

The sample size was calculated using the G*Power 3.1 program, based on the effect size reported by Chen et al. (2017; Cohen’s d = 0.39). With α = 0.05, 1–β = 0.80, and effect size = 0.39, the required sample size was 82 per group22. When d = 0.5 (medium effect), the minimum sample size per group was 51. Anticipating attrition, 55 fathers were included in each group, for a total of 165.

Participants were divided into three groups: early skin-to-skin contact (ESSC; n = 55), frequent skin-to-skin contact (FSSC; n = 55), and standard care (SC; n = 55). No participants were lost to follow-up; the study was therefore completed with 165 fathers.

Inclusion criteria were: (i) fathers ≥ 18 years; (ii) first-time fathers; (iii) at least primary-school graduates; (iv) able to provide ≥ 15 min of skin-to-skin contact within 4 h of birth; (v) no communication problems; and (vi) co-resident with their spouses. Infant eligibility required a single, healthy newborn delivered at 38–42 weeks’ gestation and present with the mother.

Exclusion criteria were: (i) fathers unreachable during data collection; (ii) fathers or infants requiring separation for > 1 week (e.g., hospitalization, prolonged work travel); and (iii) infants requiring special care. No father or infant was excluded for any reason during the research process.

Randomization and allocation

Fathers who met the eligibility criteria and consented were randomized using a computer-generated sequence via a web-based randomizer26. After setting the total sample size at 165, randomization to the three groups (allocation ratio 1:1:1) was performed using the same tool. The program produced three equal sets (n = 55 each); group labels were then randomly assigned to these sets—standard care (SC), early skin-to-skin contact (ESSC), and frequent skin-to-skin contact (FSSC)—by drawing lots. Subsequently, eligible fathers were enrolled consecutively and allocated according to the order specified in the pre-generated randomization list.

Data collection tools

In this study, data were collected using the following tools.

Questionnaire form

Fathers who met the eligibility criteria and consented were randomized using a computer-generated allocation sequence via Randomizer.org26. After setting the total sample size at 165, the program generated a 1:1:1 allocation sequence for three groups—standard care (SC), early skin-to-skin contact (ESSC), and frequent skin-to-skin contact (FSSC)—yielding three equal sets (n = 55 each). Eligible fathers were then enrolled consecutively and assigned according to the order specified in the pre-generated randomization list.

3rd month follow-up form

The 3-month follow-up form was developed by the researcher based on the same literature that informed the Questionnaire Form27–32. The form comprised 14 items capturing information on the infant’s health and nutritional status, fathers’ participation in infant care, and skin-to-skin contact, and was administered three months after the first interview. Content was finalized following review by five academics with expertise in maternity and women’s health nursing, child health nursing, and fatherhood. Fathers completed the form during face-to-face interviews.

Paternal-infant attachment scale (PIAS)

The Paternal–Infant Attachment Scale (PIAS), developed by Condon et al., is a self-report instrument that assesses fathers’ emotional responses to their infants in relation to attachment. Father–infant bonding—often termed attachment in developmental theory—describes the caregiver’s emotional connection with the infant. In this study, we use bonding throughout, operationalized via the PIAS33. Although the PIAS was originally labelled an attachment measure, subsequent content-validity work indicated that its items map directly onto contemporary definitions of bonding33.

The PIAS comprises 19 items across three subscales: patience and tolerance (PT; eight items), pleasure in interaction (seven items), and affection and pride (four items). Each item is scored on a five-point response scale, yielding a total score from 19 to 95; higher scores indicate greater father–infant bonding. The validity and reliability of the Turkish version were established by Güleç and Kavlak (2013). Cronbach’s alpha ranged from 0.78 to 0.81 in the original study33, and was 0.76 for the Turkish version27. In our sample, Cronbach’s alpha for the PIAS was 0.90. Fathers completed the PIAS by self-report during the second interview, three months after the first interview.

Process

Pilot implementation

To evaluate the comprehensibility and usability of the study instruments, a pilot study was conducted at the study hospitals between April 5–24/2024. In the pilot, the Questionnaire Form was administered to five fathers of newborns, and the 3-month follow-up form was administered to five fathers of infants aged three months. In addition, the skin-to-skin contact brochure prepared by the researchers was provided to fathers, and their feedback was obtained. Based on the pilot, several items in the Questionnaire Form were revised; no changes were made to the 3-month follow-up form or the brochure. Data from the 10 pilot participants were not included in the study.

Data collection procedures

Study data were collected in two stages. In the first stage, the spouses of pregnant women who presented for delivery were screened in the delivery-room waiting area or in the hospital room to which the mother would be admitted after delivery to determine eligibility. Fathers who met the inclusion criteria were informed about the study and invited to participate. Those who consented completed the Questionnaire Form while awaiting the birth, using self-report. To ensure a private and focused environment, fathers were asked to complete the form away from relatives and other visitors; when necessary, a separate room was provided. A researcher was present to clarify procedural questions but did not conduct a formal face-to-face interview for this part of data collection. Fathers were then assigned to groups according to the pre-generated randomization list, in order of enrollment.

In the second stage, fathers were contacted when their infants were three months old, and appointments were arranged at a time and place convenient for the father and the researcher (polyclinic, hospital, family health center, home, or workplace). The 3-month follow-up form and the Paternal–Infant Attachment Scale (PIAS) were administered: the follow-up form via face-to-face interview and the PIAS via self-report. All forms were completed in approximately 15–20 min. At this stage, the following procedures were performed in the control and experimental groups.

Fathers who received standard care (SC)

No intervention was given to the fathers in this group according to their order of participation in the study. In the second interview with these fathers, after the data were collected, they were given information and brochures about skin-to-skin contact.

Early skin-to-skin contact group (ESSC)

Fathers in this group, in order to participate in the study, were allowed to have skin-to-skin contact with their babies only once and for at least 15 min within the first 4 h after birth, after the mother and baby had skin-to-skin contact after the baby was born and the baby was breastfed. If the fathers had any questions about skin-to-skin contact, they were given an explanation, and their questions were answered.

Frequent skin-to-skin contact group (FSSC)

Fathers in this group, according to the order of participation in the study, were provided skin-to-skin contact with their babies for at least 15 min within the first 4 h after birth, after which skin-to-skin contact was established between the mother and baby after the baby was born and the baby was breastfed and within the first 4 h after birth. However, skin-to-skin contact with babies by fathers in this group was repeated at least once a day for at least 15 min in the hospital until the baby was discharged. After the mother and baby were discharged, fathers were asked to continue skin-to-skin contact at home for at least 15 min at least once a day or at least six days a week. In addition, a brochure prepared in accordance with the literature was provided to the fathers.

Skin-to-skin contact application

The procedure for skin-to-skin contact (SSC) applied to fathers in the early and frequent SSC groups was as follows: Fathers were first informed about SSC—what it is and is not, its importance, how it is performed, and its benefits for the father, mother, and infant. They were also advised to ensure a quiet environment, to close any open windows to avoid drafts, and not to perform SSC while sleepy/asleep. Fathers who smoked were asked not to perform SSC while smoking and, if possible, to do so before smoking. Maternal SSC and breastfeeding were initiated between the mother and infant. After the briefing, and within the first four hours after birth, paternal SSC was initiated once maternal SSC and breastfeeding had been completed. Fathers without suitable clothing for the procedure were provided with an operating-room gown. The infant, wearing only a diaper, was placed upright on the father’s bare chest. Fathers were asked to support the infant by placing their hands on the infant’s shoulders and back, establish eye contact, speak softly, and touch the infant. The father and infant were covered with blankets. During the SSC period, other visitors were asked to leave the room so that, apart from the parents, only the researcher remained.

SSC continued for at least 15 min and was discontinued at the father’s request or when the infant required care (e.g., diaper or clothing change, breastfeeding). This process was performed once for fathers in the early SSC group and at least once daily for fathers in the frequent SSC group until maternal–infant discharge. Fathers in the frequent SSC group were reminded to repeat the practice daily at home after discharge.

Data analysis

SPSS version 25.0 was used for analysis of the collected data. Descriptive statistics (means, standard deviations (SDs), numbers and percentages) were used for sociodemographic characteristics. Chi-square analysis was used to analyze the sociodemographic characteristics of fathers according to group. The subscales of the scales and the total score of the scales were determined with the data obtained from the scales. Normality was assessed with the Kolmogorov–Smirnov and Shapiro–Wilk tests; because the data were not normally distributed, we used the Kruskal–Wallis test. Therefore, the Kruskal‒Wallis (KW) test was applied to compare the groups. A post hoc test was used to determine which group caused a difference in more than two groups.

To select the total score of the father–infant attachment scale and the variables affecting father–infant attachment, multiple linear regression analysis with a stepwise method was performed. One of the aims of the study was to determine the variables affecting father-infant attachment of fathers as follows: fathers in standard care, early skin-to-skin contact, and frequent skin-to-skin contact groups; fathers’ age; fathers’ education level; spouse’s education level; economic status; fathers’ and spouse’s employment status in an income-generating job; thoughts about pregnancy; fathers’ and spouse’s participation in pregnancy school; concerns about being a father; gestational week; gender of the baby; characteristics of the baby; baby’s weight; fathers’ changing of the baby’s diaper; fathers’ soothing of the baby when crying or restless together with his spouse; and the hospital where the baby was born. Stepwise multiple regression analysis was used to determine the variables affecting father–infant attachment, revealing that five variables were statistically significant and had increased explanatory power. The Pearson correlation test (two-tailed) was used to determine multicollinearity in the correlation between the independent variables of the study. In all analyses, the statistical significance limit was set at p < 0.05.

Distribution of characteristics of fathers and newborns.

The fathers in the study were approximately 29 years old and had been married for approximately two and a half years (29 months). When the distribution of these characteristics according to the groups was examined, no significant difference was found between the average age of the fathers (p = 0.645) and the duration of their marriage (p = 0.726). Approximately two-thirds of fathers and their spouses had a university degree or higher. When the distribution of the educational status of fathers and their spouses according to group was examined, there was a significant difference between the educational status of both fathers (p = 0.014) and their spouses (p = 0.018). The educational status of both fathers and their spouses in the FSSC group was greater than that of fathers and their spouses in the ESSC and SC groups, and the educational status of fathers and their spouses in the ESSC group was greater than that of fathers and their spouses in the SC group. Most fathers in all three groups stated that their income equaled their expenses and that their spouses also worked. The characteristics did not differ between the groups. When the characteristics of the newborns were examined, the babies, most of whom were girls (52.1%), were born at approximately 38–40 weeks of gestation, with an average weight of 3000–3999 g, and there was no significant difference between the groups in terms of newborn characteristics (p > 0.05) (Table 1).

When fathers’ care of their babies was examined, more than half of them stated that they sometimes changed the baby’s diaper (54.5%), sometimes bathed the baby (57%), and calmed the baby down with their spouse (54.5%) when the baby was restless and attended all the baby’s check-ups and examinations (51.5%), whereas most of them (88.5%) stated that they did not cut their baby’s nails. When the distribution of the fathers’ care of their babies between the groups was examined, there was a significant difference in changing the baby’s diaper (p < 0.016), bathing (p < 0.006), calming the baby (p < 0.020), accompanying the baby’s examinations and check-ups (p < 0.047), and the baby’s weight in the first month (p < 0.006). Accordingly, the fathers in the FSSC group changed the baby’s diaper and bathed the baby more frequently than the fathers in the ESSC and SC groups, and the fathers in the ESSC group changed the baby’s diaper and bathed the baby more frequently than the fathers in the SC group. Fathers in the FSSC group calmed their babies down with their wives and accompanied their babies to their checkups and examinations more often than fathers in the ESSC and SC groups, and fathers in the ESSC group more often than fathers in the SC group. The weights of babies born to fathers in the FSSC group at one month of age were greater than those of babies born to fathers in the ESSC and SC groups, and those of babies born to fathers in the ESSC group were greater than those of babies born to fathers in the SC group (Table 2).

Error term analyses obtained from the model created with the dependent variable of the father–infant attachment scale total score showed that the assumptions of normality, linearity, and constant variance (homoscedasticity) were met. It was also determined that there was no autocorrelation between the data (Durbin-Watson = 1.912).

In the regression analysis, variables affecting fathers’ father–infant attachment were included as being in the SC, ESSC, or FSSC groups; fathers’ age; fathers’ education level; spouse’s education level; economic status; fathers’ and spouse’s employment status, which generates income; thoughts about pregnancy; fathers’ and spouse’s participation in pregnancy school; concerns about being a father; gestational week; baby’s gender; baby’s characteristics; baby’s weight; fathers’ changing of baby’s diaper; fathers’ calming of baby when crying; or restlessness together with his spouse and hospital where birth was given. Stepwise multiple regression analysis used to determine the variables affecting fathers’ father–infant attachment revealed five variables that were statistically significant and increased explanatory power (Table 5). These variables included changing the baby’s diaper, having a spouse employed in an income-generating job, being in the group of fathers receiving standard care, calming the baby together with the spouse, and fathers’ university or higher education (p < 0.05). Accordingly, fathers changing their babies’ diapers, fathers soothing their babies together with their spouses, spouses working in jobs that generate income, and fathers having a university degree or higher have positive effects on father–infant bonding. In contrast, father‒infant bonding was negatively affected, and attachment levels decreased in fathers in the standard care group who did not have skin-to-skin contact (Table 5).

Discussion

The fathers and their wives in the study generally had university degrees or higher. In addition, approximately eight out of every ten fathers stated that their wives worked. These characteristics are greater than the educational and employment status of women in this age group in our country34,35. In this study, we planned to examine the effects of sociodemographic characteristics on attachment styles. We also plan to examine the effects of educational status on attachment. Despite randomization in the formation of groups in this study, the educational level of fathers and their partners in the frequent skin-to-skin contact group was significantly higher and significantly different from that of the other groups. Most studies report that a higher father’s educational status affects attachment. For example, Suzuki et al.10, in a systematic review summarizing the results of 39 studies, noted that paternal education level was positively associated in some studies and negatively in others and therefore generally reported as an inconsistent variable. In contrast, in a multivariate regression analysis conducted by Bieleninik et al.36 in a Polish cohort, no sociodemographic factor, including low education, was associated with paternal postpartum attachment. The fact that the educational level of fathers and their partners was higher than that of the other group by chance is a limitation of this study.

The main purpose of this study was to determine the effect of skin-to-skin contact between fathers and babies in the early postpartum period on father–‒infant bonding. The total father-infant bonding scale score of the fathers was 69.14 ± 7.14 at the 3rd month after birth. Yıldırım37 reported that the total PIAS mean score of fathers in the experimental group was 83.79 ± 5.89. Yılmaz et al.38 reported that the total PIAS mean score of fathers in the experimental group was 82.34 ± 7.76. Kılan and Özpınar39 reported that the total PIAS mean score of fathers with babies aged 6–12 months was 73 ± 9.1. Çömlek40 reported that the total PIAS mean score of fathers with babies aged 0–2 years was 74.02 ± 8.61. Kartal and Erişen41 reported that the total PIAS mean score of fathers with babies aged 6–12 months was 71.37 ± 10.55. Türk Düdükçü and Tas Aslan42 reported that the total PIAS mean score of fathers with babies aged 4–12 months was 71.37 ± 10.55. The average total PIAS score was 74.67 ± 9.00. Condon et al.33 Ensuring that fathers have skin-to-skin contact with their babies at an early age increases father–infant bonding.

In this study, fathers in the FSSC group who received skin-to-skin contact training (72.44 ± 6.22) had higher father–infant attachment scores than those who received both ESSC (69.29 ± 6.89) and SC (65.69 ± 6.74). This suggests that providing information about skin-to-skin contact and demonstrating its practical application significantly contributes to fathers establishing stronger bonds with their infants. In addition, frequent skin-to-skin contact was associated with higher attachment scores than early skin-to-skin contact, emphasizing the positive effects of the frequency and duration of skin-to-skin contact on the level of attachment. Skin-to-skin contact is not only physical closeness but also an interaction that forms the basis for establishing an emotional bond.

When the difference in the PIAS score distribution between the groups was examined, the total scale score average and subdimension score averages of the fathers in the FSSC group were significantly greater than those in both the ESSC and SC groups. In addition, the total PIAS score averages and subdimension score averages of the fathers in the ESSC group were greater than those of the fathers in the SC group. In line with these results, H0 was rejected and H1 was accepted. Our findings show that skin-to-skin contact is a behavior that increases attachment between mother and child. Accordingly, the fathers in the FSSC group were more emotionally attached to their babies, more patient and tolerant toward their babies, enjoyed interacting with their babies more, and experienced more love and pride toward their babies than the fathers in the ESSC and SC groups. Previous studies by Yılmaz et al.38 Varela et al.43 and Kow et al.44 revealed that fathers have positive attitudes towards skin-to-skin contact practices and that these practices strengthen father–infant bonding32,42,43. Similarly, the findings of our study support the conclusion that skin-to-skin contact practices are effective tools for improving and strengthening the quality of the bonds that fathers establish with their infants.

Fathers in the FSSC group reported skin-to-skin contact with their babies for at least 15 min. Most fathers in the ESSC and SC groups had skin-to-skin contact with their babies for 1–14 min. Fathers in the ESSC group had less skin-to-skin contact than those in the FSSC group but more skin-to-skin contact than those in the SC group. Having skin-to-skin contact with their babies almost every day and skin-to-skin contact lasting longer than 10 min increases father–infant bonding. In a study conducted by Türemen29 with fathers in the neonatal intensive care unit, father-infant skin-to-skin contact was provided to fathers in the experimental group at least three times and for at least 15 min during the period when their babies were in the intensive care unit. Three months later, the total Father-Infant Bonding Scale score of the fathers in the experimental group was higher than that of the fathers in the control group, but the difference was not significant29. In a randomized controlled study conducted by Chen et al.41, fathers’ practice of kangaroo care with their babies for at least 15 min in the postpartum period positively affected father‒baby bonding22,45. Our findings support these results: the more physical contact fathers have with their babies, the more their bonding with their babies increases.

The average weight of the babies of fathers in the FSSC group in the first month was greater than that of the babies of fathers in the ESSC and SC groups. The use of paternity leave by fathers may have supported more breastfeeding and weight gain by providing more contact with the spouse and baby and increasing participation in care. Therefore, the weight gain of the babies of fathers in the frequent FSSC contact group was greater.

When paternal caregiving behaviors were compared across groups, significant differences were observed in changing diapers, bathing, soothing, and attending checkups, whereas nail clipping did not differ significantly. Compared with fathers in the SC group, fathers in the FSSC and ESSC groups had higher rates of changing the baby’s diaper, bathing the baby, calming the baby, and accompanying the baby’s check-ups. This result shows that fathers’ frequent or even one-time physical contact with their babies positively affects father–infant attachment, strengthens attachment, affects their relationship with the baby, increases their interest in the baby, and leads to spending time with the baby and participating in caregiving behaviors. Some studies have reported that fathers’ participation in baby care (such as changing the baby’s diaper, bathing the baby, and feeding the baby) increases the level of father–infant attachment46–51.

The multiple regression analysis of variables on the total score of the Father-Infant Attachment Scale showed that fathers changing their babies’ diapers, fathers soothing their babies together with their spouses, spouses working in an income-generating job, and fathers having a higher level of education positively affected father–infant attachment. In contrast, father–infant attachment was negatively affected, and attachment levels decreased in fathers in the standard care group who did not have skin-to-skin contact. Factors that positively affect father–infant bonding include the fathers’ age, job satisfaction, economic status, first child status, being a father for the first time, being married for less than 10 years, working in a regular job, getting married after the age of 20 and not being related to the spouse, the baby’s gender, the status of adopting the roles of the mother and father, the health status of the mother and father, education, culture, environment, early skin-to-skin contact, kangaroo care, mother and baby sharing the same room, and breastfeeding the baby. Skin-to-skin contact is consistent with findings that fathers are more interested in their babies39,42,52–56.

Limitations

The results of this study are limited to the hospitals where the study was conducted and the 165 fathers who met the sample selection criteria. Fathers can interact with other fathers from the moment they come to the hospital for birth until they are discharged from the NICU. This interaction can also occur in the delivery room’s waiting area. Therefore, there may have been an exchange of information regarding skin-to-skin contact between the groups. In addition, although randomization was used to form the groups in this study, the education levels of the fathers and their spouses in the frequent skin-to-skin contact group and their participation in pregnancy school were significantly greater than those in the other groups. This may have affected the results of the study. Although there was no significant difference, fathers in the FSSC group stated that they planned this pregnancy more than those in the other groups. In addition, FSSC fathers were more likely to participate in pregnancy school, have working spouses, and have incomes higher than their expenses. These small differences in the demographic characteristics of FSSC fathers are limitations of this study, and these limited differences may have affected the results.

Conclusion

The main result of the randomized controlled experimental study conducted to determine the effect of skin-to-skin contact on father–infant bonding was that fathers who provided frequent skin-to-skin contact with their babies and early skin-to-skin contact increased their father–infant bonding levels. Fathers in the FSSC group participated in baby care more than other fathers did. Fathers bonded more with their babies as the duration and number of days of skin-to-skin contact with their babies increased.

Fathers’ participation in pregnancy checkups and postpartum baby follow-ups should be supported. During these check-ups, fathers should be trained on skin-to-skin contact by nurses, midwives, and doctors. In addition, fathers should be encouraged to have skin-to-skin contact with their babies after birth by watching informative videos or by providing brochures on how skin-to-skin contact should be performed. An environment should be created in hospitals that allows for mother-baby-father skin-to-skin contact.
