Assessing the clinical learning environment among respiratory therapy students in Saudi Arabia: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Abdulelah M. Aldhahir
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe clinical learning environment plays a critical role in shaping healthcare students’ professional competence, confidence, and readiness for clinical practice. Evaluating students’ perceptions of their clinical training environment can provide valuable insights for improving educational quality and clinical supervision. This study aimed to evaluate the clinical learning environment from the perspective of respiratory therapy (RT) students in Saudi Arabia.MethodsA nationwide cross-sectional study was conducted between August 4 and December 22, 2025. Data were collected using an online survey based on the Clinical Learning Environment, Supervision and Nurse Teacher (CLES + T) instrument. One-way ANOVA and independent-sample t-tests were used to examine differences in perceptions across demographic and academic characteristics. Multiple linear regression analysis was performed to identify significant predictors of students’ perceptions of the CLE.ResultsA total of 1,000 RT students participated in the study (60.4% male; mean age 24 ± 2 years). Overall, students reported moderate perceptions of the CLE, with an overall mean score of 3.44 ± 0.98. Across domains, the highest agreement observed in leadership style and supervision items, while the lowest was observed in pedagogical atmosphere items. Age was negatively associated with CLE scores (β = −0.050, 95% CI: −0.075 to − 0.025, p < 0.001). Married students reported higher CLE scores compared with single students (β = 0.213, 95% CI: 0.067 to 0.359, p = 0.004). Regarding academic level, second-year students showed lower CLE scores compared with internship students, with the strongest effect observed in second-year students (β = −0.900, 95% CI: −1.152 to − 0.649, p < 0.001).ConclusionRT students in Saudi Arabia report moderate CLE perceptions. Academic level, university sector, and clinical training site are significant factors associated with CLE scores, indicating variation in CLE experiences across educational and clinical contexts.

Introduction

The clinical learning environment (CLE) is a fundamental component of the education of healthcare professionals, serving as the setting where students integrate theoretical knowledge with practical skills to develop clinical competence and professional identity [1]. It comprises a multifaceted network of forces, including the physical infrastructure, psychosocial interactions, organizational culture, and teaching methodologies, all of which profoundly influence learning outcomes and professional development [2]. This complex social context, encompassing patients, educators, and clinical staff, often presents as a dynamic, sometimes unpredictable, environment where students must apply learned concepts and cultivate professional behaviors [3, 4]. Within this multidimensional entity, effective clinical learning necessitates robust supervision and a student-centered approach to foster both the acquisition of practical skills and the development of professional identity [5]. This process can be theoretically explained through Experiential Learning Theory (Kolb), which emphasizes learning through a cyclical process of concrete experience, reflection, conceptualization, and active experimentation, particularly within real clinical settings [6].

Evidence from various countries indicates that structured and student-centered clinical environments contribute to enhanced clinical confidence and competence across multiple disciplines, including medicine and allied health [7]. In addition, previous studies demonstrated that allied health students who perceived supportive supervision, clear task orientation, and constructive learning opportunities reported higher overall satisfaction and better professional development during clinical rotations [8, 9]. This pedagogical approach, emphasizing early clinical exposure, has been shown to significantly enhance the self-efficacy and clinical skill acquisition of medical students, bridging the gap between theoretical knowledge and practical application [10, 11]. Such early integration cultivates a more robust understanding of core medical competencies, as students are exposed to the practical demands of healthcare from the onset of their education [12].

The integration of theoretical and practical components in RT education is paramount, as students must develop strong clinical skills for direct patient care, encompassing competencies like airway management and ventilation support [13]. As such, innovative curricula increasingly prioritize clinical skill mastery over mere knowledge acquisition, frequently leveraging simulation-based education to provide a safe and controlled environment for skill refinement before direct patient interaction [14]. This approach allows for the development of critical thinking within a clinical setting, preparing future therapists to provide competent care prior to their professional practice [15].

Among RT students, an exploratory study in the USA using the CLES + T scale reported generally positive evaluations of clinical experiences [16]. In contrast, evidence from Saudi Arabia remains limited, with a lack of nationally representative data. This study addresses this gap by providing a multi-institutional assessment of CLE perceptions among RT students across Saudi Arabia, offering a more comprehensive understanding of the CLE and its associated factors. It was hypothesized that perceptions of the CLE would differ significantly according to demographic, academic, and training-related characteristics. Accordingly, the study was guided by the following research questions: [1] What are RT students’ perceptions of the CLE across the CLES + T domains [2]? Do these perceptions differ according to demographic and academic characteristics [3]? What factors are significant predictors of overall CLE perception among RT students?

Materials and methods

Study design and study population

A descriptive cross-sectional survey was given to respiratory therapy students in Saudi Arabia from August 4 to December 22, 2025. This study was reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines.

Sampling strategy and data collection

The study’s target population was recruited through convenience sampling. University students enrolled in RT programs in Saudi Arabia, from second academic year to internship level, were eligible to participate, while first-year RT and pre-med students were excluded due to lack of clinical exposure. An online survey was used to collect data, and the invitation link was distributed to lecturers and professors across 17 RT programs in Saudi Arabia [17], who subsequently shared it with students via official WhatsApp groups. In total, 1,914 invitations were sent and 1,000 responses were received (response rate: 52.2%). The survey link was also disseminated through official RT social media platforms to enhance reach. Efforts were made to minimize potential bias by ensuring anonymous participation, restricting one response per participant through SurveyMonkey with email sign-in verification, and distributing the survey across multiple institutions and platforms to reduce selection bias.

Study instrument

The survey consisted of 42 items divided into two main sections and was administered in English. Section 1 included 8 items on participants’ demographic and academic characteristics, including age, gender, marital status, geographical location, university sector, academic year, cumulative GPA, and current clinical rotation site. Section 2 comprised the Clinical Learning Environment Supervision and Nurse Teacher (CLES + T) instrument, a validated and reliable scale developed by Saarikoski et al. [18] and modified by Alghamdi [16] to assess students’ perceptions of the clinical learning environment in RT education, and was used without any further adaptation. The modifications included replacing the term “nursing” with “respiratory therapy,” “general ward” with “ICU,” and “nursing educator” with “clinical instructor”. These modifications were approved by the original author and the adapted version has been previously used in RT populations; therefore, the same version was used in the present study to ensure consistency with prior RT research.

The CLES + T includes 34 items measured on a five-point Likert scale (1 = fully disagree to 5 = fully agree) and is structured into five domains: ‘Pedagogical atmosphere’ (9 items), ‘Leadership style of the RT manager’ (4 items), ‘Respiratory care in intensive care units’ (4 items), ‘Content of supervision’ (8 items), and ‘Roles of clinical instructors’ (9 items).

Instrument validation and reliability

To ensure the psychometric integrity of the CLES + T instrument among RT students, construct validity and internal consistency were evaluated. Construct validity was assessed using Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA) with Principal Axis Factoring (PAF) and Varimax rotation. Sampling adequacy was verified using the Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) measure and Bartlett’s test of sphericity. Internal consistency for the total scale and its five sub-domains was determined using Cronbach’s alpha coefficients, with a value of 0.70 or higher considered acceptable.

Ethical consideration

Before starting data collection, ethical approval was obtained from the Standing Committee for Scientific Research at Jazan University (Reference No. REC-45/11/1133). Participants provided informed consent before participation and were informed about the voluntary nature of the study, anonymity of responses, and the use of data for research purposes only, with the right to withdraw at any time without providing any justification or consequences. All study methods were conducted in compliance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Sample size

Recent data indicate that there were 1,297 RT students in Saudi Arabia in 2022 [17]. With the expansion of newly established RT programs, this number was anticipated to rise to around 2,000 students. For this population size, the estimated sample required ranged from 323 participants (assuming a 5% margin of error) to 870 participants (assuming a 2.5% margin of error). In the present study, 1,000 RT students were recruited in 2025, surpassing the minimum required sample size and, thereby, increasing the statistical power and precision of the study’s findings.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.5.1). Descriptive statistics were used to summarize students’ perceptions of the CLE. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages, while continuous variables were expressed as mean ± SD. Normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test and visual inspection of histograms. Differences in CLES + T scores across demographic and academic characteristics were examined using independent samples t-tests and one-way ANOVA, as appropriate, after confirming that assumptions of normality and homogeneity of variance were met. To account for multiple comparisons across domains and demographic variables, Bonferroni correction was applied. Subsequently, multiple linear regression analysis was performed to identify significant predictors of students’ perceptions of the CLE. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Psychometric properties of the CLES + T among RT students

The psychometric evaluation of the CLES + T instrument among RT students demonstrated excellent validity and reliability. The KMO measure was 0.984, and Bartlett’s test of sphericity was significant (p < 0.001), confirming that the data were highly suitable for factor analysis. EFA using PAF revealed a robust structure explaining 56.4% of the total variance. As shown in Table 1, the total scale demonstrated exceptional internal consistency (α = 0.975), and all five sub-domains yielded strong reliability coefficients ranging from 0.849 to 0.931.

Demographic data of the study participants

A total of 1,000 RT students completed the survey. The sample was predominantly male (60.4%) and single (74.2%), with a mean age of 24 ± 2 years. Nearly half of the participants were in their internship year (46.7%), and most were enrolled in governmental universities (76.6%). In terms of clinical training exposure, 59.3% were undertaking their rotations in non-teaching hospitals. Regarding academic performance, more than half reported a cumulative GPA between 4.00 and 5.00 (58.9%). Participants were also distributed across all geographical regions of Saudi Arabia, with the highest proportion from the Central Region (33.7%). Further demographic details are presented in Table 2.

CLE, supervision, and RT teacher percentage of agreement and disagreement in all domains

Most items across the CLES + T domains showed a balanced response pattern, with approximately 49%–56% of participants selecting “agree/strongly agree” across all domains, including pedagogical atmosphere, leadership style, supervision, clinical instructor roles, and respiratory care in the ICU. The highest agreement was observed in leadership style items, particularly feedback from ICU managers (56.3%) and the perception of managers as team members (54.6%). Similar moderate agreement levels were seen in supervision-related items, where around 51%–54% of students reported positive experiences with supervision and feedback. In contrast, disagreement responses were consistently present across domains, ranging from approximately 23% to 31%, with neutral responses accounting for roughly 20%-25% of responses across most items. CLES + T mean scores

The overall mean score of the CLES + T indicated moderate perceptions of the clinical learning environment among students (3.44 ± 0.98). The highest mean score was observed in the ‘Leadership style’ domain (3.48 ± 1.09), followed by the ‘Respiratory care in the ICU’ domain (3.44 ± 1.08) and the ‘Content of supervision’ domain (3.44 ± 1.06). The ‘Roles of clinical instructors’ domain had a mean score of 3.41 ± 1.06, while the ‘Pedagogical atmosphere’ sub-domain yielded the lowest mean score (3.40 ± 1.07). (Fig. 1)

The relationship between CLES + T domains and demographic variables

Analysis demonstrated significant variations in CLES + T domain scores across several demographic characteristics of RT students. Overall, female students reported slightly higher mean scores than male students across all domains; however, these differences were not statistically significant for Pedagogical Atmosphere (3.48 ± 1.04 vs. 3.34 ± 1.08, p = 0.398), Respiratory care in the ICU (3.52 ± 1.04 vs. 3.38 ± 1.10, p = 0.285), Leadership style (3.57 ± 1.05 vs. 3.42 ± 1.11, p = 0.260), and Content of Supervision (3.49 ± 1.03 vs. 3.41 ± 1.07, p = 0.407), while a significant difference was observed only for Roles of Clinical Instructors (3.54 ± 1.00 vs. 3.32 ± 1.09, p = 0.042).

In contrast, marital status, academic year, university sector, geographical region, cumulative GPA, and clinical rotation site were significantly associated with all CLES + T domains (p ≤ 0.030). Married and divorced/widowed students reported higher scores than single students, particularly in Leadership style (3.40 ± 1.13 vs. 3.68 ± 0.97 vs. 3.79 ± 0.88, p < 0.001) and Roles of Clinical Instructors (3.33 ± 1.10 vs. 3.62 ± 0.92 vs. 3.68 ± 0.88, p < 0.001). A clear upward trend was also observed across academic years, with internship students reporting the highest scores (Pedagogical Atmosphere: 2.70 ± 0.91 in second year vs. 3.73 ± 1.08 in internship year, p < 0.001).

Students from governmental universities reported higher scores than those from private universities across all domains (Leadership style: 3.53 ± 1.12 vs. 3.32 ± 0.98, p < 0.001). Regional differences were also present, with higher scores generally observed in the Northern, Southern, and Central regions compared with the Eastern region (p ≤ 0.004). Additionally, higher GPA was strongly associated with more positive perceptions across all domains, with students achieving 4.00–5.00 consistently reporting the highest scores (Leadership style: 3.77 ± 1.05 vs. 2.95 ± 0.99, p < 0.001). Finally, students in non-teaching hospitals reported significantly higher scores than those in teaching hospitals across all domains, including Pedagogical Atmosphere (3.57 ± 1.00 vs. 3.16 ± 1.12, p = 0.007) and Leadership style (3.65 ± 1.02 vs. 3.24 ± 1.14, p = 0.013). Overall, academic level, GPA, university sector, region, and clinical setting were the most consistent factors associated with more positive CLES + T perceptions (Table 4).

Determinants of RT Students’ Perceptions of the CLE

The multiple linear regression model was statistically significant (F(15, 984) = 22.73, p < 0.001), explaining 25.7% of the variance in the CLE score (R² = 0.257, adjusted R² = 0.246). Age was negatively associated with CLE score (β = −0.050, p < 0.001). Compared with single students, married students had higher CLE scores (β = 0.213, p = 0.004), while no significant difference was observed for divorced/widowed students. Students in private universities reported lower CLE scores compared with those in governmental universities (β = −0.217, p = 0.001). Regarding academic year, second, third, and fourth-year students had consistently lower CLE scores compared with internship students (all p < 0.001), with the strongest effect observed in second-year students (β = −0.900). Students in non-teaching hospitals had higher CLE scores than those in teaching hospitals (β = 0.292, p < 0.001). No significant associations were observed for geographical regions. No multicollinearity was detected among predictors (VIF range: 1.08–2.30). (Table 5)

Discussion

This study evaluated the CLE from the perspective of RT students in Saudi Arabia using the CLES-T instrument. Overall, students reported moderate perceptions of their CLE, with higher scores in the leadership style of the ward manager and content of supervision domains. Several demographic and academic factors were significantly associated with CLE perceptions. Age was negatively associated with CLES-T scores, whereas marital status showed a positive association. In addition, higher academic level and cumulative GPA were significant positive predictors of more favourable perceptions. Institutional factors also played a role, with students from private universities and those undertaking clinical rotations in teaching hospitals reporting lower CLE scores.

From a theoretical perspective, these findings can be interpreted through Kolb, which posits that learning is optimized when students actively engage with their environment, reflect on their experiences, and conceptualize these reflections into actionable knowledge [19]. Within clinical education, a well-structured and supportive clinical environment is paramount for facilitating the translation of theoretical knowledge into practical skills and professional competence [7]. In this context, variability in perceptions of the clinical learning environment may reflect differences in how effectively clinical settings facilitate reflection and the translation of clinical exposure into meaningful learning experiences.

The findings related to leadership style and content of supervision highlight the central role of clinical leadership and supervisory structures in enabling or constraining the experiential learning process. When leadership is supportive and supervision is structured, students are more likely to engage in guided reflection and active experimentation, thereby enhancing the quality of experiential learning. These findings are consistent with previous research among nursing students in Saudi Arabia, which identified leadership style as the highest-rated domain of the CLES-T, underscoring its importance in shaping clinical learning experiences [20]. Similar evidence from China [21], Malaysia [22], and Morocco [23] further supports the view that effective leadership and structured supervision are key enablers of meaningful experiential learning in clinical settings. In addition, supportive supervision characterized by feedback, guidance, and collaborative engagement facilitates deeper reflection and integration of knowledge, which are essential processes within Kolb’s learning cycle [24, 25].

The effect of age on CLE perceptions was statistically significant but small in magnitude. This finding may be explained through differences in experiential learning development. According to Kolb’s theory, learners with greater prior experience may engage in more critical reflection, leading to more evaluative and less idealized perceptions of their learning environment. Younger students, by contrast, may be more focused on concrete experience and initial exposure, which can result in more positive evaluations. This is consistent with previous findings in nursing students in Pakistan, where younger students reported more favorable perceptions of the CLE, potentially due to lower expectations and earlier-stage learning needs [26]. Older students, having progressed further in the experiential learning cycle, may identify gaps in supervision and clinical structure more critically, reflecting higher-order reflective judgment [27–30]. Therefore, age-related differences may reflect variation in stage of experiential learning rather than differences in environment alone.

Another notable finding was the positive modest association between marital status and students’ perceptions. Married or previously married students reported more favorable CLE scores compared with single students. From a learning theory perspective, this may reflect differences in life experience and reflective capacity, which can influence how individuals process clinical experiences within Kolb’s learning cycle. Students with broader life responsibilities may develop enhanced coping and reflective strategies, facilitating adaptation to clinical environments. Previous research supports the idea that demographic characteristics can shape engagement with supervision and learning processes in clinical settings [26, 31, 32]. However, findings remain inconsistent across studies [33], suggesting that marital status likely interacts with other experiential and contextual factors rather than exerting a direct effect.

Academic progression and performance demonstrated largest effects in shaping perceptions of the CLE. Higher academic levels and GPA were associated with more positive perceptions. This may be explained by progression through repeated cycles of experiential learning, where senior students accumulate more clinical exposure, allowing for deeper conceptualization and more effective integration of theory and practice. As students advance, they become more competent in moving through Kolb’s cycle, particularly in abstract conceptualization and active experimentation, which may enhance confidence and perception of the learning environment. These findings align with previous RT and health professions education studies reporting higher satisfaction among senior students due to increased clinical exposure and competence development [16, 34, 35]. Similarly, academically high-performing students may engage more effectively in reflective and active learning processes, contributing to more positive evaluations of their clinical environment [20, 36, 37].

Institutional context also appeared to have small effect on students’ perceptions of their CLE. Students from private universities reported lower scores compared with those from government universities. This may reflect differences in the quality and structure of experiential learning opportunities provided. Variations in supervision quality, clinical exposure, and institutional support can affect how consistently students’ progress through Kolb’s learning cycle. Institutions with stronger clinical integration may better facilitate reflection and application of knowledge, thereby enhancing perceived learning quality [38, 39]. These findings are consistent with prior literature emphasizing the role of institutional support and clinical partnerships in shaping learning experiences [20, 29, 40].

Interestingly, students in non-teaching hospitals reported higher CLE scores than those in teaching hospitals, representing a small-to-moderate effect size. From an experiential learning perspective, this may reflect differences in the type of clinical experiences available. Non-teaching hospitals may provide more direct patient care opportunities, allowing students to engage more actively in concrete experience and active experimentation stages of learning [41]. In contrast, teaching hospitals often involve structured hierarchies and competing training priorities, which may limit student participation in hands-on activities, thereby reducing engagement in the experiential learning cycle [39, 42]. This may explain why, despite being training-oriented environments, teaching hospitals do not always translate into more favorable student perceptions. Increased direct interaction with patients and less hierarchical restriction in non-teaching settings may enhance experiential learning opportunities, particularly for foundational skill development [43].

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths and limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. A key strength is the inclusion of a sample of RT students drawn from all 17 RT programs in Saudi Arabia, which enhances the breadth and diversity of the data and supports a wider contextual understanding of the findings. Additionally, the use of a validated instrument (CLES-T) provides a comprehensive evaluation of students’ perceptions of the CLE. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design limits the ability to establish causal relationships between predictors and students’ perceptions. Second, although efforts were made to recruit participants from multiple institutions, the use of convenience sampling may introduce selection bias. Third, data were collected through self-reported online surveys, which may introduce response or social desirability bias. Finally, the study relied on students’ perceptions without incorporating objective measures of clinical training quality, which may limit the ability to fully capture the complexity of the CLE. Future studies should employ longitudinal and mixed methods designs to better understand the factors shaping RT students’ perceptions of the CLE and inform targeted improvements in clinical education.

Conclusion

RT students in Saudi Arabia reported moderate perceptions of their CLE. Academic level, cumulative GPA, and marital status were positively associated with more favorable perceptions, while age showed a negative association. These findings highlight the importance of fostering supportive leadership, effective supervision, and well-structured clinical training environments to enhance students’ clinical learning experiences. Strengthening collaboration between academic institutions and clinical training sites may further improve the quality of RT education.
