Association between geriatric malnutrition and the risk of dementia: a retrospective cross-sectional cohort study in Uganda
Authors: Odong Christopher, Ayenyo Winfred, Zhang Wanzhu, John Wanyama, Ebrima Bah, Anywar Geofrey, Edward Mpoza, Mical Abraha, Kuule Julius Kabbali
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLimited data exist from low-income settings on the association between unintentional geriatric malnutrition and the risk of dementia. Our study investigated this association and stratified risk factors in geriatric cohort from Uganda.MethodThis multicentre retrospective cross-sectional cohort study enrolled geriatric patients from specialist care hospital in one Public and two private facilities in Kampala. Nutritional status was stratified as malnourished or well-nourished. The primary outcome was dementia. Logistic regression evaluated the association between malnutrition and dementia. Separate models explored dementia risk factors within each nutritional subgroup.ResultsOverall, 210 participants(mean age 75.72 ± 7.93yrs) were recruited. The prevalence of malnutrition was 25%, with 30 of 129 females (23.3%) and 23 of 81 males(28.4%) being affected. Among malnourished participants, 37(29.1%) had a caregiver. Malnutrition was significantly associated with dementia(adjusted odds ratio[OR], 2.57; 95% CI, 1.01–6.55; P = 0.048). Stratified analysis revealed divergent risk profiles. Among malnourished patients, history of cardiovascular disease(CVD) was associated with dementia(odds ratio[OR], 5.80; 95% CI, 1.25–16.90; β = + 1.76; P = 0.025). Among well-nourished patients, only increasing age was independently associated with dementia(OR, 1.07 per year; 95% CI, 1.01–1.14; β = + 0.07; P = 0.016).ConclusionsIn this study, unintentional malnutrition was associated with dementia. Among malnourished individuals, CVD was the major associated factor, whereas age was the major factor among well-nourished participants. Further studies are needed to validate these findings and determine whether improving nutritional status may prevent or slow dementia progression.Keywords: Geriatric, Malnutrition, Dementia, Uganda, Risk factor, Low-income countries

Introduction

Dementia is an increasing global public health concern [1], with prevalence rising rapidly as populations age worldwide [2, 3]. Although most individuals living with dementia currently reside in high-income countries [4], the number of affected persons in low- and middle-income countries is increasing, and epidemiological data from sub-Saharan Africa remain limited [3]. In Uganda, where more than 70% of the population is younger than 40 years [5], the growing population of older adults is emerging in the context of constrained health care systems and a scarcity of geriatric specialty services both in rural and urban setting [2]. These limitations contribute to underdiagnosis of cognitive disorders and a limited understanding of potentially modifiable risk factors for dementia [2].

Malnutrition is common among older adults and represents a major yet frequently overlooked determinant of health in geriatric populations [6–8]. Age-related physiological changes, chronic diseases, functional impairment, and socioeconomic vulnerability predispose older individuals to inadequate nutritional intake and poor nutritional status [8, 9]. In low-income settings, these risks are further compounded by food insecurity and limited access to health care services [8]. Geriatric malnutrition has been associated with increased morbidity, functional decline, prolonged hospitalization, and mortality [10]; however, its role in cognitive impairment and dementia remains incompletely characterized, particularly in resource-limited environments [10, 11].

Emerging evidence from high-income countries suggests that poor nutritional status is associated with impaired cognitive performance, accelerated cognitive decline, and higher incidence of dementia [12]. Proposed mechanisms include micronutrient deficiencies, chronic inflammation, oxidative stress, sarcopenia, and frailty, all of which may contribute to neurodegeneration and synaptic dysfunction [13–15]. In addition, dementia itself may worsen nutritional status through appetite loss, impaired feeding behaviour, and increasing dependence on caregivers, suggesting a complex and potentially bidirectional relationship [16]. Despite this growing body of literature, data from African populations remain sparse, and the relevance of these findings to low-income settings is uncertain.

In Uganda, routine nutritional screening and cognitive assessment are not standard components of geriatric care. Older adults are typically managed within general hospital services, where nutritional status and early cognitive changes may be overlooked [17]. Consequently, the prevalence of geriatric malnutrition and its contribution to dementia risk in this setting are poorly understood [18]. Clarifying this relationship is essential to identify potentially modifiable targets for intervention and to inform the development of context-appropriate strategies for healthy aging [2].

Therefore, this multicentre retrospective cohort study aimed to examine the association between geriatric unintentional malnutrition and dementia among older adults receiving specialized geriatric care in Kampala, Uganda at three medical facilities, including Naguru General Hospital, The Surgery, and the Geriatric Centre in Uganda. We sought to explore the relationship between nutritional status and dementia risk in this urban geriatric population in Kampala, Uganda. By providing evidence from low-income country perspective, this study aims to highlight the importance of nutritional assessment in geriatric care and to contribute to the growing literature on modifiable determinants of cognitive decline.

Methods

Study design and population

This multicenter, retrospective, cross-sectional cohort study included patients aged ≥ 60yrs with complete nutritional status documentation who received outpatient geriatric care between July 30, 2023, and July 30, 2025, at The Geriatric Centre, China-Uganda Friendship Hospital, and The Surgery Health. To minimize selection bias, all geriatric outpatient records during the study period were screened consecutively for eligibility based on predefined inclusion criteria. Consecutive sampling was used to enroll all eligible patient records across both the public and private participating sites. Of the 493 outpatient records screened, 87 patients were excluded due to missing age data or age younger than 60 years, and an additional 196 were excluded due to missing documentation of nutritional status or sex. A total of 210 patients with complete demographic and clinical data were included in the final analysis (Fig. 1).

Data collection and variables

Data were extracted from medical records using a standardized abstraction form and managed via Microsoft Excel. Research assistants underwent uniform training, and standardized definitions were applied consistently across all study sites. Data from all sites were subsequently merged and cleaned for analysis. Missing data were documented and managed according to a predefined protocol as shown in Fig. 1.

The primary outcome was a documented diagnosis of dementia made by senior neurologists and/or geriatricians during routine clinical care. Diagnoses were based on standardized cognitive and functional assessments, with neuroimaging and laboratory investigations used when available to exclude secondary causes. Dementia was classified according to DSM-5 and/or ICD-10/ICD-11 criteria, although assessment tools varied slightly across centers, diagnoses were made using comparable diagnostic thresholds and internationally accepted criteria [19].

Documented geriatric nutritional status was assessed using the Mini Nutritional Assessment-Short Form(MNA-SF) [20, 21]. Nutritional assessment data were obtained from outpatient medical records. Based on standardized MNA-SF scoring criteria, participants were classified as malnourished(score, 0–7) or not malnourished(score ≥ 8); the latter category included both participants at risk of malnutrition and those who were well nourished [22]. Supportive anthropometric(body mass index) and biochemical(serum albumin) measures were also evaluated [23].

Assessment of covariates and comorbidities

Information on potential confounders was obtained from hospital records during the study period. Variables included alcohol consumption, cigarette smoking, age, and sex(self-reported or reported by a caregiver). A caregiver was defined as an individual with a specified assignment to provide care to an elderly patient, such as ensuring the patient is fed, assisting with hygiene, and administering medication, among other tasks. Type-2 diabetes mellitus(T2DM) was defined as fasting blood glucose ≥ 126 mg/dL, non-fasting blood glucose ≥ 200 mg/dL, use of antidiabetic medications, or self-reported physician diagnosis [24]. Hypertension(HTN) was defined as systolic blood pressure ≥ 140 mmHg, diastolic blood pressure ≥ 90 mmHg, or use of antihypertensive medication within the past two weeks [25]. Coronary heart disease(CHD) was diagnosed based on clinical history, electrocardiographic findings, cardiac biomarker results, and/or imaging studies (echocardiography or coronary angiography) records [24], or self-reported physician diagnosis. Chronic kidney disease(CKD) was defined as abnormalities in kidney structure or function persisting for at least 3 months with health implications [26]. Diagnostic criteria included sustained reduction in estimated glomerular filtration rate(< 60 mL/min/1.73 m2) and/or markers of kidney damage such as albuminuria, urine sediment abnormalities, electrolyte disturbances due to tubular dysfunction, structural abnormalities on imaging, or histologic abnormalities on kidney biopsy or self-reported physician diagnosis [26]. Pneumonia was diagnosed based on clinical features(fever, cough, sputum production, dyspnea, and auscultatory findings), radiographic evidence of new pulmonary infiltrates, and, when available, microbiological confirmation [27]. Dementia was diagnosed according to established criteria, based on clinical history, cognitive screening and neuropsychological testing, assessment of functional impairment, supportive neuroimaging findings, and documented physician diagnosis or self-reported specialist diagnosis [28]. Stroke was defined by acute focal or global neurological deficit lasting ≥ 24 h, supported by neuroimaging(CT or MRI) or self-reported physician diagnosis or self-reported neurologist/physician diagnosis [29]. Heart failure (HF) was diagnosed based on typical symptoms and signs(dyspnea, fatigue, peripheral edema), physical examination findings, elevated natriuretic peptide levels, and objective evidence of structural or functional cardiac abnormalities on imaging, particularly echocardiography, with or without documented physician diagnosis or self-reported cardiologist diagnosis [30]. Human immunodeficiency virus(HIV) infection was diagnosed based on documented positive serological testing (ELISA with confirmatory Western blot or nucleic acid testing) or prior physician diagnosis [26]. Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease(COPD) was diagnosed based on clinical history, respiratory symptoms, spirometry evidence of persistent airflow limitation (post-bronchodilator FEV₁/FVC < 0.70), radiographic findings when available, or prior physician diagnosis. Parkinson’s disease was diagnosed based on clinical history, neurological examination, fulfillment of established diagnostic criteria (e.g., UK Parkinson’s Disease Society Brain Bank or Movement Disorder Society criteria), and/or documented physician diagnosis [31]. Medication use was assessed as follows: antihypertensive medication based on current or prior prescriptions and medication reconciliation at enrollment; antiplatelet therapy based on self-report, prescription records, or physician orders; anticoagulation therapy based on documented prescriptions; and pain medication based on patient self-report, medical records, or prescription data. All diagnoses were confirmed by qualified physicians and reviewed by senior geriatric specialists.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 25.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY).

Study participants were stratified by geriatric nutritional status. Continuous variables with normal distribution are expressed as mean ± standard deviation(SD), while non-normally distributed variables are expressed as median(interquartile range[IQR]). Categorical variables are presented as counts and percentages. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test, and continuous variables were compared using the unpaired Student’s t-test.

Using well-nourished participants as the reference group, two logistic regression models were constructed: Model-1(unadjusted) and Model-2(fully adjusted). Odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated to assess the risk of dementia among well-nourished versus malnourished participants. Additionally, subgroup analysis was performed to identify factors associated with dementia among malnourished participants, using univariable and multivariable logistic regression; results are reported as ORs with 95% CIs. All statistical tests were two-sided, and a P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Participant characteristics

This multi-centre retrospective cross-sectional cohort study included 210 geriatric participants. The mean age was 75.72 ± 7.93yrs. Among females, 30(23.3%) were malnourished, and among males, 23(28.4%) were malnourished. Caregivers were present for 37(29.1%) of the malnourished participants. Several demographic and clinical characteristics differed significantly by nutritional status, including active alcohol consumption, pneumonia, dementia, HIV, and pain medication use (p < 0.005) (Table 1).

Association between geriatric nutritional status and dementia

Correlation analysis demonstrated significant positive association between geriatric malnutrition and dementia (r = + 0.18, P = 0.009). In the unadjusted logistic regression model (Model-1), malnutrition was associated with 2.97-fold higher risk of dementia compared with being well-nourished (OR = 2.97; 95% CI: 1.27–6.92; P = 0.012). This association remained statistically significant after adjustment for age, gender, SBP, DBP, smoking, alcohol consumption, T2DM, and HTN, with an adjusted odds ratio of 2.57(95% CI: 1.01–6.55; P = 0.048) (Table 2).

Risk factors associated with dementia among geriatric malnourished participants

Logistic regression analyses were conducted to identify factors associated with dementia among malnourished geriatric participants only. In univariate analysis, current smoking, history of CVD, history of stroke, and history of pneumonia were significantly associated with higher risk of dementia(p < 0.05). In the multivariable model, one factor remained independently associated with an increased risk of dementia: CHD (OR = 5.80; 95% CI: 1.25–16.90; P = 0.025; β = + 1.76) (Table 3).

Discussion

In this multicentre retrospective cohort study of geriatric patients in Uganda, we report several principal findings. First, geriatric malnutrition was associated with an increased risk of dementia, whereas adequate nourishment was associated with reduced risk. Second, among individuals with geriatric malnutrition, the presence of CVD was associated with substantially high risk of dementia compared to malnourished individuals without CVD. Furthermore, among well-nourished participants, increasing age was significantly associated with high dementia risk. To best of our knowledge, this is the first study to explore factors associated with dementia risk from low-income setting perspective, specifically in Uganda.

Previous investigations have established that geriatric malnutrition was common and demonstrates its association with dementia [1, 2]. In our cohort, malnutrition was present in nearly one-quarter of female and more than one-quarter of male participants, underscoring substantial and often overlooked burden of poor nutritional status among older adults in an urban Ugandan setting. We report that geriatric malnutrition was independently associated with higher odds of dementia, even after adjustment for major demographic factors, vascular risk factors, and lifestyle behaviours. These findings reinforce the growing recognition of nutritional status as a critical determinant of cognitive health in aging populations [32].

The observed positive correlation between malnutrition and dementia (r = + 0.180) and nearly threefold increased odds of dementia among malnourished participants in unadjusted analyses highlight clinically meaningful relationship. Although the correlation was modest, an expected finding given the multifactorial aetiology of dementia, it remained statistically significant. After multivariable adjustment, malnutrition continued to confer 2.57-fold increased risk of dementia, suggesting that nutritional status is not merely a proxy for age, cardiovascular risk, or comorbid illness, but an independent contributor to cognitive decline. This demands greater attention, particularly in a country like Uganda, where the healthcare focus is predominantly on the younger majority, and older adults contend with the same health services without age-specific prioritization [33]. These results align with previous studies demonstrating that geriatric malnutrition and low body mass index are associated with impaired cognition, accelerated cognitive decline, and higher dementia incidence [11, 15, 18].

Several mechanistic pathways may explain this association. Malnutrition may exacerbate neurodegeneration through micronutrient deficiencies, chronic inflammation, oxidative stress, and impaired synaptic function [34]. It may also reflect underlying sarcopenia and frailty, both independently linked to cognitive impairment [34]. A bidirectional relationship is plausible, whereby dementia itself can worsen nutritional status via appetite loss, impaired feeding behaviour, and increased caregiver dependence [18, 34]. The presence of caregivers for nearly one-third of malnourished participants in this study supports the complex interaction between functional dependence, nutrition, and cognition [35].

Importantly, intentional caloric restriction should be distinguished from malnutrition observed in community-dwelling older adults [36]. Experimental and observational studies have suggested that controlled caloric restriction, when accompanied by adequate micronutrient intake, may improve metabolic health, reduce oxidative stress and inflammation, and potentially support healthy aging and cognitive resilience [37]. In contrast, malnutrition in low-resource community settings is often unintentional and characterized by inadequate caloric intake, protein deficiency, and micronutrient insufficiency, frequently occurring alongside chronic disease, frailty, and socioeconomic vulnerability [9]. Therefore, the potentially beneficial physiological effects of intentional caloric restriction are fundamentally different from the detrimental effects of geriatric malnutrition observed in our cohort. This distinction is important when interpreting nutritional studies in aging populations, particularly in low-income settings [36].

Among malnourished participants, we found CVD to be a major independent risk factor for dementia in multivariable analysis, conferring a nearly sixfold increased risk. This aligns with prior evidence linking vascular pathology to both malnutrition and cognitive impairment [38]. CVD may potentiate dementia risk in malnourished individuals by compounding cerebral hypoperfusion, endothelial dysfunction, and systemic inflammation [39]. While stroke and pneumonia were significant in univariate analyses, their effects attenuated after adjustment, suggesting CVD may represent a central pathway through which malnutrition amplifies dementia risk [39].

In contrast, among well-nourished participants, increasing age was the only independent risk factor for dementia. This finding aligns with the established role of aging as the strongest non-modifiable risk factor for cognitive decline and suggests that adequate nutritional status may partially buffer against the influence of other comorbidities [40]. The absence of an association between vascular factors and dementia in the well-nourished group highlights the potential protective role of good nutrition in maintaining cognitive resilience.

From clinical perspective, these findings emphasize the importance of routine nutritional screening in geriatric care, particularly for patients with cardiovascular disease. Early identification and management of malnutrition may represent modifiable intervention to reduce dementia risk or delay its progression. Integrating nutritional assessment with cardiovascular risk management could offer holistic approach to dementia prevention.

Limitations

Several limitations warrant acknowledgment. The retrospective cross-sectional observational design precludes causal inference, and residual confounding cannot be excluded despite adjustment. Nutritional status was assessed at single time point, limiting evaluation of longitudinal changes and their temporal relationship with dementia onset. Data on dietary intake, specific micronutrient deficiencies, dementia subtypes, and severity of cognitive impairment were unavailable. The modest sample size within subgroup analyses may have limited statistical power. Future multicentre studies with larger samples are recommended.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates an independent association between geriatric malnutrition and dementia among older adults in Uganda, with malnutrition conferring 2.57-fold increased risk after adjustment for potential confounders. These findings support the routine implementation of nutritional screening and targeted interventions in geriatric care. Prospective, multicentre studies with larger sample sizes are warranted to validate this finding and to determine whether improving nutritional status can prevent or slow dementia progression.
