Accelerated biological aging and underlying epigenetic modification mediated the association between obesity and asthma
Authors: Jian Liu, Zhengqi Song, Chengge Shi, Xinan Lu, Aijun Zhang, Yihe Chen, Xuejia Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe relationships among obesity, accelerated biological aging, and asthma, as well as the underlying epigenetic mechanisms, remain incompletely understood.MethodsWe conducted a prospective cohort study in the UK Biobank to evaluate whether accelerated biological aging mediates the association between obesity and incident asthma. We then performed two-sample Mendelian randomization (MR) to assess the potential causal effect of accelerated biological aging on asthma from an epigenetic perspective. Epigenetic MR and colocalization analyses were used to prioritize aging-related DNA methylation (DNAm) CpG sites associated with asthma. Finally, single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) was applied to characterize the cell-type-specific expression patterns of CpG-mapped genes.ResultsIn the UK Biobank cohort, KDM-BA acceleration and PhenoAge acceleration partially mediated the association between obesity and asthma, with proportions mediated of 4.0% and 12.2%, respectively. Two-sample MR further suggested that DNAm PhenoAge acceleration was associated with a higher risk of asthma (OR = 1.02; 95% CI: 1.01–1.03; P = 0.004). Epigenetic MR and colocalization analyses suggested that cg02010481 (JAZF1), cg17929770 (RSPH6A), cg19927816 (SYMPK; RSPH6A), cg08544331 (RUNX3), cg15498134 (RUNX3), and cg04389704 (FBXL19) were associated with a reduced risk of asthma, whereas cg16553500 (COL16A1), cg16351569, cg10036013 (FOXK1), cg11647481 (FOXK1), cg23776012 (KRT222), and cg08258403 were associated with an increased risk of asthma. In scRNA-seq analyses, JAZF1, SYMPK, FOXK1, and RUNX3 were differentially expressed across multiple immune cell types.ConclusionsThis study demonstrates that accelerated biological aging partially mediates the association of obesity with asthma and further elucidates underlying epigenetic mechanisms linking aging to asthma.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13148-026-02145-6.

Introduction

Asthma is a common chronic inflammatory airway disease with a substantial and increasing global prevalence, leading to significant morbidity, frequent healthcare utilization, and impaired quality of life across all age groups [1]. Accumulated evidence indicates that obesity is a major risk factor for asthma, associated with higher incidence, greater disease severity, more frequent exacerbations, reduced responsiveness to conventional therapies, and poorer health outcomes [2]. However, despite robust epidemiological associations between obesity and asthma, the biological mechanisms linking these conditions remain incompletely understood.

Biological aging, conceptualized as a progressive increase in cellular and physiological vulnerability, has been proposed as a more informative construct than chronological age for explaining heterogeneity in disease risk [3]. Accordingly, a growing number of algorithms have been developed to quantify biological aging in population studies [4]. Among these, Klemera-Doubal method biological age (KDM-BA) and phenotypic age (PhenoAge) are two of the most widely used metrics in epidemiologic research. Both are computed from routinely collected clinical biomarkers and have demonstrated strong predictive validity for morbidity and mortality across diverse populations [5, 6]. Importantly, observational evidence indicates that obesity is associated with accelerated clinical biological aging, including greater KDM-BA and PhenoAge acceleration, supporting the notion that excess adiposity may hasten systemic aging processes [7, 8]. Meanwhile, prior clinical research also suggests that higher PhenoAge acceleration is associated with a greater subsequent risk of incident asthma [9]. Collectively, these findings motivate the epidemiologic hypothesis that accelerated biological aging may partially mediate the association between obesity and asthma. However, the magnitude of this mediation and the molecular pathways linking accelerated aging to asthma development remain largely unexplored.

Epigenetic modifications, particularly DNA methylation (DNAm), provide quantitative molecular readouts of biological aging, and they form the basis of widely used epigenetic clocks [10–13]. Prior epigenome-wide association studies (EWAS) and meta-analyses also support an association between DNAm and asthma, suggesting that epigenetic regulation may contribute to asthma-related pathways and may inform early risk stratification [14–16]. Notably, Marttila et al. conducted an age-related EWAS and catalogued cytosine-phosphate-guanine (CpG) sites that track with aging, offering a key reference for aging associated DNAm variation [17]. In addition, the Genetics of DNA Methylation Consortium (GoDMC) provides a large-scale methylation quantitative trait locus (mQTL) resource that links genetic variants to CpG methylation levels [18]. These datasets enable the systematic identification of mQTLs as genetic instruments for aging-related DNAm CpG sites, providing an important foundation to uncover the downstream epigenetic modification that may connect accelerated aging to asthma development.

In this study, we combined prospective observational analyses with Mendelian randomization (MR) within a triangulation framework to strengthen causal inference [19]. First, we conducted a prospective cohort analysis in the UK Biobank to evaluate the mediating role of accelerated biological aging in the association between obesity and asthma from an observational perspective. Second, we performed two-sample MR analysis using genome-wide association study (GWAS) summary statistics of four epigenetic aging clocks and asthma to assess the causal effect of biological aging on asthma risk from an epigenetic perspective. Third, we conducted epigenetic MR and colocalization analyses between aging-related DNAm CpG sites and asthma to investigate downstream epigenetic mechanisms linking aging to asthma. Finally, we leveraged single-cell RNA sequencing to characterize cell-type-specific expression patterns of genes mapped to the identified DNAm CpG sites.

Methods

The overall study design was depicted in Fig. 1. This study was reported in accordance with the STROBE-MR (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology using Mendelian Randomization) guidelines, with the completed checklist provided in Supplementary Table S1 [20].

Prospective cohort study from UK biobank

Study population

The UK Biobank includes more than 500,000 participants recruited from 22 assessment centres across the United Kingdom between 2006 and 2010 and has been followed prospectively. Further details on the study design and procedures have been reported elsewhere [21]. Among the 501,936 initially enrolled participants, we excluded those with body mass index (BMI) < 18.5 kg/m2 or missing data on weight, height, or waist circumference (WC) (n = 5,833), missing covariate information (n = 123,007), incomplete biological aging assessment data (n = 91,881), or prevalent asthma at baseline (n = 33,291), resulting in a final analytic sample of 247,924 participants (Supplementary Fig. S1).

Definition of obesity

Obesity status was defined using BMI as follows: obesity (≥ 30.0 kg/m2), overweight (25.0–29.9 kg/m2), and normal weight (18.5–24.9 kg/m2) [22, 23] . Participants with a BMI < 18.5 kg/m2 were excluded because the sample size was insufficient for reliable estimation. In addition, WC and waist-to-height ratio (WHtR) were used as alternative measures of adiposity. WC was categorized as high (men ≥ 102.0 cm; women ≥ 88.0 cm), intermediate (men 94.0–101.9 cm; women 80.0–87.9 cm), and normal (men < 94.0 cm; women < 80.0 cm). WHtR was classified as high (≥ 0.6), intermediate (0.5 to < 0.6), and normal (< 0.5).

Assessment of covariates

Covariates in our study comprised sociodemographic factors (age, sex, ethnicity, Townsend Deprivation Index, education, and household income), lifestyle factors (physical activity [24], smoking status, drinking status, and healthy diet score [25]), and ambient air pollutants [26](NO2, NOX, PM10, and PM2.5). Detailed definitions of all covariates are provided in Supplementary Table S3.

Assessment of biological aging

Two widely validated biological age measures, KDM-BA and PhenoAge, were incorporated in our study [27, 28]. Both measures were originally developed in NHANES III and subsequently applied to the UK Biobank [29, 30]. In the UK Biobank, PhenoAge was calculated using chronological age and 9 blood biomarkers (alkaline phosphatase, albumin, C-reactive protein, creatinine, glucose, mean cell volume, red cell distribution width, white blood cell count, and lymphocyte percentage), whereas KDM-BA was computed from systolic blood pressure, forced expiratory volume in 1 s, and 7 blood chemistry parameters (alkaline phosphatase, albumin, blood urea nitrogen, C-reactive protein, creatinine, glycated hemoglobin, and total cholesterol). Detailed information on the biomarkers included in each algorithm is provided in Supplementary Table S4.

To further quantify differences between biological age and chronological age, biological age acceleration was calculated as the residual from regressing each biological age estimate (KDM-BA or PhenoAge) on chronological age, with values > 0 indicating an advanced biological aging state and values < 0 indicating a delayed biological aging state [30]. Biological age acceleration measures were subsequently standardized to z-scores (mean = 0, SD = 1) to make effect sizes comparable [31]. KDM-BA and PhenoAge were computed using the R package “BioAge” (https://github.com/dayoonkwon/BioAge/) [32].

Definition of outcome

The outcome of our study was the incidence of asthma. The definition of the asthma was derived from the “Algorithmically-defined outcomes” field group in UK Biobank [33]. This category provides classifications of selected health-related events derived from algorithmic integration of coded information from the UK Biobank baseline assessment and linked health records, including self-reported conditions, hospital admission diagnoses and procedures, and death registry ICD codes. Detailed information could be found at https://biobank.ndph.ox.ac.uk/showcase/ukb/docs/alg_outcome_main.pdf. Follow-up time was defined from baseline to the earliest occurrence of the asthma, death, loss to follow-up or the censoring date (July 8, 2024).

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables are presented as numbers with percentage, and continuous variables are summarized as medians (Q1-Q3). Differences in categorical and continuous variables between incident asthma cases and individuals without asthma were assessed using the Chi-square test and the Wilcoxon rank-sum test, respectively. Multivariable-adjusted Cox proportional hazards models were used to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the associations between obesity and incident asthma. Model 1 was adjusted for age, sex, ethnicity, Townsend Deprivation Index, education, and household income. Model 2 was further adjusted for physical activity, smoking status, drinking status, diet score, and ambient air pollutants (NO2, NOX, PM10, and PM2.5). The proportional hazards assumption was examined using Schoenfeld residuals, with no violations detected (P > 0.05) [34]. We further evaluated whether accelerated biological aging mediates the association between obesity and asthma [35]. First, multivariable-adjusted linear regression models were fitted to examine the association between obesity and biological aging acceleration. Second, Cox proportional hazards models were applied to assess the association between biological aging acceleration and asthma risk. Both steps were adjusted for the covariates included in Model 2. Mediation analyses were subsequently conducted using the “CMAverse” R package [36]. A regression-based mediation framework with Cox outcome models adjusted for Model 2 covariates was used to estimate the total effect, natural direct effect, natural indirect effect, and proportion mediated. Nonparametric bootstrapping with 1,000 resamples was performed to derive 95% CIs and P values.

Subgroup analyses were performed by age and sex. Potential effect modification was evaluated by including multiplicative interaction terms between obesity and each subgroup factor in the models. Sensitivity analyses were conducted to assess the robustness of the findings. First, WC and WHtR were used as alternative adiposity measures to examine the association with incident asthma. Second, participants who developed asthma within the first 2 years of follow-up were excluded to minimize potential reverse causation. Third, Fine-Gray subdistribution hazard models were fitted to estimate the association between obesity and asthma risk while accounting for death as a competing event. Fourth, we further used BMI, hip circumference, and waist-to-hip ratio as continuous measures of adiposity and additionally included participants with a BMI < 18.5 kg/m2 [37, 38].

All statistical analyses in the prospective cohort were conducted using R version 4.4.0, with statistical significance defined as a two-sided P value less than 0.05.

Two-sample mendelian randomization

GWAS summary statistics of epigenetic clocks and asthma

GWAS summary statistics for four DNAm-based biological aging phenotypes (epigenetic clocks) were obtained from previously published research by McCartney et al., including DNAm PhenoAge acceleration (DNAm PhenoAge; n = 34,463), DNAm GrimAge acceleration (DNAm GrimAge; n = 34,467), DNAm Hannum age acceleration (DNAm HannumAge; n = 34,449), and intrinsic epigenetic age acceleration (DNAm IEAA; n = 34,461)(Supplementary Table S5) [39]. GWAS summary statistics for asthma were derived from the Global Biobank Meta-analysis Initiative (GBMI) consortium, comprising 121,940 cases and 1,254,131 controls [40] (Supplementary Table S5). All GWAS summary statistics were restricted to participants of European ancestry.

Selection of genetic variants for epigenetic clocks

Single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) used as genetic instrumental variables (IVs) were required to satisfy three core conditions: [1] robust association with the exposure; [2] no association with confounders of the exposure-outcome relationship; and [3] an effect on the outcome only through the exposure, with no alternative pathways. Since few instruments reached genome-wide significance (P < 5 × 10− 8), we selected independent SNPs using a suggestive significance threshold (P < 5 × 10− 6). SNP independence was ensured by linkage disequilibrium (LD) clumping with r2 < 0.01 within a 10,000 kb window [41]. Instrument strength was evaluated using the F-statistic, calculated as F=(β/se)2, where β and se are the estimated SNP-exposure effect and its standard error. An F-statistic > 10 was considered indicative of strong instruments (unlikely to suffer from weak instrument bias) [42].

Two-sample MR analysis and sensitive analysis

In the two-sample MR analysis, the random-effects inverse-variance weighted (IVW) was used as the primary approach, with MR-Egger regression, the weighted median, and the weighted mode serving as complementary approaches [43–47]. To confirm the robustness of our study, several sensitivity analyses were conducted. The MR-Egger intercept test and Cochran’s Q statistic were used to evaluate directional horizontal pleiotropy and heterogeneity, respectively, with P < 0.05 indicating evidence of pleiotropy or heterogeneity [44, 45]. Potential reverse causality was assessed using Steiger filtering [48]. The Bonferroni correction was applied to account for multiple testing, with statistical significance set at P < 0.0125 (0.05/4).

Epigenetic mendelian randomization study

Identifying the mQTL of aging-related DNA methylation

The identification of cis-mQTLs for aging-related DNA methylation was performed in accordance with a previously published study [29]. Briefly, we first obtained 8,540 aging-related CpG sites reported by Marttila et al. [17]. We then extracted the corresponding cis-mQTLs from the GoDMC Consortium database [18]. Cis-mQTLs were selected using a genome-wide significance threshold (P < 5 × 10− 8) and LD clumping (r2 < 0.001, 10,000 kb window), yielding 3,853 CpG sites with 4,688 cis-mQTLs [29]. After matching and harmonizing the instruments with the asthma outcome dataset, 3,103 aging-related CpG sites with 3,636 cis-mQTLs were retained for subsequent epigenetic MR analyses (Supplementary Table S6).

Epigenetic mendelian randomization analysis and colocalization analysis

Epigenetic MR analysis was performed to evaluate the potential causal effects of aging-related CpG sites on asthma risk. The Wald ratio method was applied when only one cis-mQTL was available as an IV, whereas the IVW approach was used when multiple independent cis-mQTLs were available. Multiple testing was controlled using Bonferroni correction, with statistical significance set at P < 1.6 × 10− 5 (0.05/3,103). For CpG sites meeting this threshold, we further conducted colocalization analysis to determine whether CpG methylation and asthma shared a causal variant within the same locus [49, 50]. Five hypotheses were evaluated: H0 (no causal variant for either trait), H1 (a causal variant for CpG site only), H2 (a causal variant for asthma only), H3 (distinct causal variants for the two traits), and H4 (a single shared causal variant influencing both traits). SNPs within ± 500 kb of each CpG site were extracted for colocalization testing [29]. Aging-related CpG sites were prioritized as positive if they passed Bonferroni correction and showed strong evidence of colocalization, defined as a posterior probability of H4 (PP.H4) > 0.80.

Single-cell RNA sequencing analysis

To clarify the distribution patterns of the CpG-mapped genes across different cell subtypes, we further analyzed single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data derived from peripheral blood samples of asthma patients and healthy controls from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (GSE288147) using the Seurat v4 package. Cells were subjected to quality-control filtering based on the following criteria: (1) nFeature_RNA > 800 and nFeature_RNA < 5,500; (2) nCount_RNA > 2,000 and nCount_RNA < 25,000; (3) percentage of mitochondria < 10%. The thresholds of nFeature_RNA and nCount_RNA were determined by identifying the inflection point between the first peak and the main peak of the density distribution for lower bounds, and using the 99th percentile for upper bounds, followed by visual validation across all samples. For percent.mt, we applied a threshold of 10%, which is a commonly used cutoff for human single-cell data. Subsequently, doublets were identified and removed using scDblFinder, and ambient RNA contamination was corrected using DecontX to reduce technical noise and improve the robustness of downstream analyses. After filtering, UMI counts were normalized and scaled, and highly variable genes were identified for subsequent analyses. Dimensionality reduction and clustering were then performed on the merged expression matrix, with Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) used for two-dimensional visualization. The clustering resolution was set to 0.8 to evaluate the expression levels of the CpG mapped genes in each cell subtype. Differential expression analysis between cell types was performed using MAST with Bonferroni correction, and genes with adjusted P < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Prospective cohort study from UK biobank

Baseline characteristics of prospective cohort study

In this prospective cohort of 247,924 UK Biobank participants, 7,628 individuals developed incident asthma over a median follow-up of 14.6 years (Supplementary Table S2 ). The median age at baseline was 58 years, and 53.3% of participants were female. Compared with those who did not develop asthma, incident cases had a higher prevalence of obesity, higher baseline exposure to air pollutants, and exhibited an older biological age and chronological age.

Observational association between obesity and asthma

As presented in Table 1, compared with participants with a healthy weight, those who were overweight and obese had higher risks of incident asthma in Model 1, with HRs of 1.35 (95% CI 1.28–1.43; P < 0.001) and 1.94 (95% CI 1.83–2.06; P < 0.001), respectively. These associations remained similar after further adjustment for lifestyle factors and ambient air pollutants in Model 2 (Table 1). Subgroup analyses indicated that the association between obesity and asthma was significantly modified by sex (Pinteraction = 0.002), with a stronger association observed in females (HR: 2.11, 95% CI: 1.95–2.27; P < 0.001) than in males (HR: 1.63, 95% CI: 1.49–1.78; P < 0.001) (Supplementary Fig. S2). Sensitivity analyses yielded consistent results when obesity was alternatively defined using WC or WHtR, after excluding asthma cases occurring within the first 2 years of follow-up, and in competing-risk models accounting for death as a competing event (Supplementary Table S7-S9). Meanwhile, when BMI, hip circumference, and waist-to-hip ratio were analyzed as continuous measures of adiposity, we similarly observed an increased risk of asthma (Supplementary Table S10).

The mediating role of biological aging acceleration on the association of obesity with asthma

We first assessed the association of obesity with biological aging acceleration, as well as the association of biological aging acceleration with incident asthma. Multivariable-adjusted linear regression showed that participants with obesity had higher KDM-BA acceleration and PhenoAge acceleration than those with healthy weight, with β estimates of 0.50 (95% CI: 0.49, 0.51; P < 0.001) and 0.39 (95% CI: 0.38, 0.40; P < 0.001), respectively (Supplementary Table S11). Cox proportional hazards models indicated that higher KDM-BA acceleration and PhenoAge acceleration were each significantly associated with a higher risk of asthma, with HRs of 1.09 (95% CI: 1.06, 1.11; P < 0.001) and 1.20 (95% CI: 1.18, 1.23; P < 0.001), respectively (Supplementary Table S12). Mediation analysis suggested that KDM-BA acceleration and PhenoAge acceleration partially mediated the association between obesity and asthma, with a proportion mediated of 4.0% (95% CI: 1.5%-6.2%; P < 0.001) and 12.2% (95% CI: 10.3%-14.0%; P < 0.001), respectively (Fig. 2). Similar mediation proportions were observed when obesity was alternatively defined using WC and WHtR, as well as when BMI, hip circumference, and waist-to-hip ratio were analyzed as continuous measures of adiposity (Supplementary Fig. S3-4).

Two-sample Mendelian randomization analysis unveiled the causal relationship between epigenetic clocks and asthma

A total of 22, 39, 59, and 29 SNPs were selected as IVs for DNAm GrimAge, DNAm HannumAge, DNAm IEAA, and DNAm PhenoAge, respectively. All instruments had F-statistics > 10, suggesting little evidence of weak instrument bias (Supplementary Table S13). In subsequent two-sample MR analyses, DNAm PhenoAge was causally associated with an increased risk of asthma (OR = 1.02; 95% CI: 1.01–1.03; P = 0.004) (Fig. 3 and Supplementary Table S14). We found no evidence of horizontal pleiotropy (Ppleiotropy = 0.44) or indications of reverse causation for this association (Supplementary Table S15). In contrast, DNAm GrimAge, DNAm HannumAge, and DNAm IEAA showed no significant associations with asthma risk (Fig. 3 and Supplementary Table S14).

Impact of aging-related DNA methylation on asthma risk

In the epigenetic MR analysis, a total of 41 aging-related CpG sites were significantly associated with asthma risk after Bonferroni correction (P < 1.6 × 10− 5) (Fig. 4 and Supplementary Table S16). Among these, 12 CpG sites mapped to eight genes and were supported by strong colocalization evidence (PP.H4 > 0.80) (Table 2 and Supplementary Table S16). Specifically, cg02010481 (JAZF1), cg17929770 (RSPH6A), cg19927816 (SYMPK; RSPH6A), cg08544331 (RUNX3), cg15498134 (RUNX3), and cg04389704 (FBXL19) were associated with a reduced risk of asthma (OR < 1; PP.H4 > 0.80; P < 1.6 × 10− 5). In contrast, cg16553500 (COL16A1), cg16351569, cg10036013 (FOXK1), cg11647481 (FOXK1), cg23776012 (KRT222), and cg08258403 were associated with an increased risk of asthma (OR > 1; PP.H4 > 0.80; P < 1.6 × 10− 5).

Cell-type specific expression of CpG mapped genes

To further explore the cell type-specific expression of CpG mapped genes, we generated a single-cell immune map consisting of 74,943 cells from the public peripheral blood single-cell RNA datasets of healthy individuals and patients with asthma (Supplementary Fig. S5A-B). According to known cell-type labeling, eight major cell types were identified: B cells, T cells, NK cells, monocyte-macrophages (Mon_Mφ), plasma cells, plasmacytoid dendritic cells (pDCs), megakaryocytes, and other minor cell populations (Fig. 5A-B, D). Monocyte-macrophages and T cells accounted for the largest proportions in both the asthma and healthy control (HC) groups (Supplementary Fig. S5C). We further analyzed the expression distribution of the identified CpG mapped genes in different immune cell types and found that JAZF1, SYMPK, FOXK1, and RUNX3 were broadly expressed across multiple immune cell populations(Supplementary Fig. S6). Next, we conducted differential expression analysis stratified by cell type in the scRNA-seq data (Fig. 5E). RSPH6A, KRT222, and COL16A1 did not show differential expression across the evaluated cell types. FBXL19 was differentially expressed only in monocyte-macrophages, but with a small effect size. In contrast, the remaining four genes (JAZF1, SYMPK, FOXK1 and RUNX3) were differentially expressed in B cells, monocyte-macrophages, NK cells, and T cells, which are key immune cell types implicated in asthma pathogenesis (Fig. 5C).

Discussion

In this study, we integrated evidence from prospective cohort analyses, MR, and scRNA-seq to elucidate the role of accelerated biological aging and its underlying epigenetic mechanisms in the association between obesity and asthma from a multidimensional perspective. From an observational perspective, KDM-BA acceleration and PhenoAge acceleration partially mediated the association of obesity with asthma. From an epigenetic perspective, MR supported a causal effect of DNAm PhenoAge on asthma risk and further prioritized aging-related DNAm CpG sites linked to asthma. Finally, scRNA-seq analyses delineated the cell type-specific expression patterns of genes mapped to these CpG sites.

From an epidemiological perspective, our findings align with previous observational studies showing that higher adiposity is associated with an increased risk of incident asthma, with stronger associations observed in females [51–53]. Importantly, we further extend this literature by demonstrating that the association of obesity with asthma is partly explained by accelerated biological aging, as captured by KDM-BA acceleration and PhenoAge acceleration. This is consistent with previous evidence indicating that obesity is associated with greater biological age acceleration and that such acceleration can statistically mediate obesity-disease associations in prospective settings [54, 55]. Meanwhile, we observed a larger mediated proportion for PhenoAge acceleration than for KDM-BA acceleration, suggesting that PhenoAge acceleration may serve as a better intervention target in the pathway between obesity and asthma. The difference in mediated proportions may reflect that different aging algorithms capture overlapping yet distinct domains of multi-system dysregulation and therefore contribute differently to the obesity-asthma pathway [27, 28]. Moreover, the robustness of our mediation estimates when obesity was alternatively defined using central adiposity indices (WC and WHtR) supports the notion that both general and central adiposity may contribute to asthma development partly through accelerated aging processes. Of note, given that PhenoAge and KDM-BA are derived from routinely measured clinical biomarkers, our findings suggest potential feasibility for incorporating these measures into risk prediction and stratification in large populations, thereby informing feasible preventive strategies to reduce the risk of asthma development among individuals with obesity.

The biological ageing process is increasingly understood through molecular hallmarks, including epigenetic, transcriptomic, proteomic, and metabolomic alterations, as well as telomere length, which are often summarized as “ageing clocks” [56–58]. Among these, DNAm-based epigenetic clocks, particularly DNAm PhenoAge, are widely applied to capture disease vulnerability and healthspan [28, 59]. Preliminary observational studies have suggested that several measures of epigenetic age acceleration (e.g., extrinsic epigenetic age acceleration) are associated with a higher prevalence of asthma [60, 61]. In the present study, we provide, to our knowledge, the first evidence supporting a potential causal relationship between DNAm PhenoAge and asthma risk using a two-sample Mendelian randomization approach. This finding further extends the potential utility of DNAm PhenoAge in asthma risk stratification. Importantly, by using genetic variants as instrumental variables, Mendelian randomization can strengthen causal inference through reducing confounding and reverse causation, and may better reflect life-course effects than conventional observational epidemiology. Interestingly, the other DNAm-based age (epigenetic age) estimators, including DNA methylation GrimAge acceleration, DNA methylation Hannum age acceleration, and DNA methylation intrinsic epigenetic age acceleration, did not show comparable associations with asthma. This discrepancy may reflect fundamental differences in what these clocks capture. DNAm Hannum age acceleration and intrinsic epigenetic age acceleration are more closely related to age-associated methylation changes, with the latter further adjusted to minimize the influence of blood cell composition [62, 63]. In contrast, DNAm PhenoAge was developed to capture phenotypic ageing related to physiological dysregulation and disease risk [28], whereas DNAm GrimAge was designed as a predictor of lifespan and healthspan based on DNAm surrogates for smoking and plasma proteins [64]. Given that asthma is a chronic inflammatory condition characterized by immune imbalance and metabolic disturbance, the biological processes reflected by DNAm PhenoAge may be more relevant to its pathogenesis than those captured by clocks primarily optimized for age-related methylation patterns or survival prediction. This may partly explain why only DNAm PhenoAge showed a significant association in our analysis.

Previous studies suggest that aging may contribute to asthma development through immunosenescence and chronic low-grade inflammation [65–67]. However, whether aging influences asthma through DNA methylation changes, and the mechanisms involved, remain unclear. Our findings suggest that age-related alterations in DNA methylation may provide an epigenetic link between biological aging and asthma susceptibility, offering further insight into the underlying biological pathways. By combining DNAm-based Mendelian randomization, colocalization, and single-cell transcriptomic analyses, we identified aging-related CpGs and mapped genes with potential relevance to asthma pathogenesis. This integrative strategy provides further support for specific epigenetic loci as potential mediators of the link between aging and asthma.

Within this integrative framework, our epigenetic MR and colocalization analyses prioritized 12 aging-related CpGs mapped to 8 genes significantly associated with asthma with strong evidence for shared causal variants. Notably, among these genes, only RUNX3 has been previously implicated in asthma pathogenesis and progression. RUNX3 has been shown to promote Th1 polarization while suppressing Th2 polarization, thereby modulating airway inflammation and airway hyperresponsiveness [68, 69]. In addition, RUNX3-deficient mature dendritic cells (DCs) become less responsive to TGF-β mediated suppression of maturation, resulting in accelerated DC maturation, enhanced T-cell stimulatory capacity, and aberrant expression of β2 integrins, thereby exacerbating airway inflammation [70]. Compared with controls, children with asthma have been shown to exhibit lower RUNX3 DNA methylation in peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMC) [71]. Consistent with these findings, we found that higher methylation levels at the RUNX3-linked CpG sites cg08544331 and cg15498134 were associated with a lower risk of asthma, with colocalization analysis supporting shared causal variants. Single-cell RNA-seq analysis indicated differential expression of RUNX3 in peripheral blood from patients with asthma. Taken together, our study provides new evidence that RUNX3 may act as a potential epigenetic mediator linking biological aging to asthma susceptibility.

We also identified several less well-characterized genes that may represent novel candidates in this context. The aging-related CpG site cg04389704 was annotated to the gene FBXL19. FBXL19 is an orphan member of the Skp-Cullin-F-box (SCF) E3 ubiquitin ligase family, selectively binds to ST2L, mediating its polyubiquitination and subsequent proteasomal degradation [72]. Elevated ST2L expression has been observed in airway epithelial cells of individuals with severe asthma. It has been shown to correlate positively with clinical indices such as blood eosinophil counts and fractional exhaled nitric oxide (FeNO) [73]. In vitro studies have consistently shown that engagement of ST2L by IL-33 in mast cells and basophils substantially upregulates IL-4 and IL-13 expression [74, 75]. Blockade of OVA-induced IL-33/ST2L signaling significantly reduced airway hyperresponsiveness and airway inflammation in vivo [76]. Similarly, FOXK1, a transcription factor containing a conserved forkhead DNA-binding domain, emerged as another potentially relevant gene. In our study, DNA methylation at FOXK1 CpG sites (cg10036013 and cg11647481) was positively associated with asthma risk, which may relate to FOXK1-mediated regulation of inflammatory cell recruitment and autophagy dysregulation [77–79]. Differential expression of FOXK1 across multiple peripheral blood cell types in individuals with asthma further suggests a potential pathogenic role for FOXK1 in asthma.

For several of the remaining CpG-mapped genes, the functional links to asthma are still less clear. Nevertheless, large-scale asthma genetic studies have suggested that variants in or near JAZF1 and COL16A1 are associated with asthma susceptibility [80, 81]. KRT222, which encodes a keratin-family protein, resides in a genomic region that has been implicated in asthma and atopic phenotypes [82]. SYMPK is located adjacent to RSPH6A, and genetic variants within this genomic region have been associated with an increased risk of asthma [83, 84]. Together, these findings integrate epigenetic causal inference with single-cell expression profiles to illuminate molecular links between aging and asthma, highlighting potential targets for individualized prevention and therapeutic strategies.

The primary strength of our study is the integration of clinical and epigenetic evidence, providing a multidimensional view of the role of biological aging in the association between obesity and asthma. Our study also has several limitations. First, our analyses were restricted to individuals of European ancestry. Therefore, the generalizability of the findings to other populations requires further evaluation. Second, as large-scale GWAS defining obesity precisely by BMI are currently unavailable, we were unable to perform a two-step MR to further clarify the role of epigenetic biological aging in the obesity-asthma pathway. Third, despite the inclusion of several established measures of biological and epigenetic aging, DunedinPACE, a DNA methylation-based measure of the pace of aging, was not assessed in the present study. Incorporating this metric in future work may provide a more complete understanding of the contribution of epigenetic aging to the obesity-asthma association [85]. Fourth, both the mQTL and scRNA-seq data were derived from peripheral blood. Hence, future studies are needed to validate the tissue specificity of the prioritized aging-related DNAm CpG sites and mapped genes in disease-relevant tissues.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our study elucidated the role of accelerated biological aging in asthma from both clinical and epigenetic perspectives and further highlighted its potential mediating effect on the association between obesity and asthma. These findings provide insights into the etiology and potential therapeutic targets of asthma.
