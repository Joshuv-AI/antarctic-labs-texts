Asymptotic stability of smooth solitons and multi-solitons for the Camassa–Holm equation
Robin Ming Chen, Yang Lan, Yue Liu, Zhong Wang

Abstract
. We establish the asymptotic stability of smooth solitons and multi-solitons for the Camassa–Holm (CH) equation in the energy space H 1 ​ ( ℝ ) H^{1}(\mathbb{R}) . We show that solutions initially close to a soliton converge, up to translation, weakly in H 1 ​ ( ℝ ) H^{1}(\mathbb{R}) as time tends to infinity to a (possibly different) soliton. The analysis is based on a Liouville-type rigidity theorem characterizing solutions that remain localized near a soliton trajectory. A central feature of the proof is a complete spectral resolution of the linearized CH operator around a soliton. This linear theory is obtained via the bi-Hamiltonian and integrable structure of the CH equation, through the recursion operator and the completeness of the associated squared eigenfunctions. It provides a substitute for the classical spectral framework used in KdV and gKdV equations, which is unavailable in the nonlocal and variable-coefficient setting of CH. The spectral resolution yields sharp decay estimates for the linearized flow in exponentially weighted spaces, which in turn lead to the nonlinear rigidity result and the asymptotic stability of a single soliton. Combined with known orbital stability results, this approach extends to well-ordered trains of solitons and to the explicit multi-soliton solutions generated by the inverse scattering method. As an additional application, we revisit the linearized problems associated with other integrable dispersive equations, including the KdV and mKdV equations, from the perspective of squared-eigenfunction expansions.

1.1.
CH solitons

By means of inverse scattering theory, the evolution of rapidly decaying initial data for the CH equation can be described through purely algebraic methods. Solutions decompose into a well-structured family of functions determined by the scattering data. The

1.2.
Nonlinear stability of smooth CH solitons

There is an extensive literature on the stability of CH peakons (the case
\omega=0
), culminating in the asymptotic stability result of Molinet
[
52
]
. In this paper, however, we focus exclusively on the stability of smooth solitons arising when
\omega>0
.

In the peakon setting, the soliton profiles admit explicit expressions and the associated momentum density
m=u-u_{xx}
is a finite sum of Dirac measures. This explicit structure has been exploited in a decisive way by Molinet
[
52
]
, where a rigidity result is first established at the level of the momentum density and then transferred to the solution
u
.

By contrast, for smooth CH solitons, neither the soliton profile nor the corresponding momentum density admits an explicit representation. As a consequence, the strategy developed for peakons does not directly apply. In particular, rigidity cannot be accessed through pointwise or measure-theoretic arguments and instead requires a fundamentally different approach.

We begin by recalling the well-known results on orbital stability. The nonlinear orbital stability of the single soliton
\varphi_{c}
was established by Constantin–Strauss
[
25
]
using the general spectral method introduced by Benjamin
[
3
]
and developed by Grillakis–Shatah–Strauss
[
32
]
. Their proof relies on the Hamiltonian structure of the CH equation and on a careful analysis of the constrained variational properties of the solitary waves. For multi-soliton configurations, a general framework for establishing the orbital stability of trains of
N
solitary waves in Hamiltonian dispersive equations was provided by Martel–Merle–Tsai
[
46
]
in the context of gKdV. Adapting this approach, El Dika and Molinet
[
27
]
proved the orbital stability of
N
-soliton trains for the CH equation in the energy space
H^{1}(\mathbb{R})
. A key step in their argument is the derivation of an almost-monotonicity property for localized versions of the conserved quantities
\mathcal{H}_{1}
and
\mathcal{H}_{2}
. More recently, the Lyapunov stability of smooth multi-solitons was obtained in
[
55
]
.

We emphasize that all the above results concern orbital or Lyapunov stability. To the best of our knowledge, no prior work has established the asymptotic stability of smooth CH solitons, either for a single soliton or for multi-soliton configurations. The present paper provides the first such results. Our method combines the integrable structure of the CH hierarchy, a detailed spectral analysis of the linearized operator, weighted semigroup estimates, and a nonlinear rigidity argument, as described in the following subsections.

1.4.
Challenges and methodology

The asymptotic stability of smooth CH solitons presents structural and analytic challenges that are notably different from, and more difficult than, those encountered in many dispersive models such as the modified KdV (mKdV) or generalized KdV (gKdV). The linearized operator around a CH soliton is nonlocal, involving the inverse Helmholtz operator
(1-\partial_{x}^{2})^{-1}
, so its spectral analysis cannot rely on classical Sturm–Liouville or Schrödinger operator techniques. Even the basic spectral features, such as the precise location and multiplicity of eigenvalues, the structure of the continuous spectrum, or the completeness of radiation modes, were unknown prior to this work.

A further difficulty, absent in KdV, is that the CH soliton profile
\varphi_{c}
does not possess a closed-form expression. While the asymptotic stability theory of Martel and Merle
[
42
,
43
,
44
,
45
]
for general nonlinearities does not rely on explicit formulas for the soliton itself, it nevertheless exploits in an essential way the explicit spectral information available for the linearized KdV operator. Indeed, the Schrödinger operator arising in the linearization around the KdV soliton has an explicitly known potential, and its eigenvalues and eigenfunctions can be computed in closed form. These explicit spectral data enter directly in the virial identity and coercivity arguments, especially in the identification of the unique negative eigenvalue and the detailed structure of the corresponding eigenfunction. For general gKdV equations this information is inherited from the KdV case through a perturbative argument. In contrast, for CH such an approach is infeasible: the soliton profile is implicit, the operator is nonlocal, and there is no obvious ODE structure from which one could extract the negative direction by classical arguments (see Remark
2.4
). Thus, none of the virial tools used in the gKdV literature can be imported into CH in any straightforward manner.

A different approach appears in the asymptotic stability theory for the Benjamin–Bona–Mahony (BBM) equation
[
49
]
, where the authors rely on a refined Evans function analysis of the linearized operator, leading to asymptotic stability for all but a discrete exceptional set of soliton speeds. For CH, however, the integrable structure completely bypasses the need for Evans function techniques and allows us to obtain a full description of the spectrum for every admissible speed. As a consequence, our analysis applies uniformly across the full range
c>2\omega
, with no exceptional values, and the rigidity and asymptotic stability arguments rest directly on the spectral resolution provided by integrability.

Another perspective on asymptotic stability is provided by the more recent work of Germain–Pusateri–Rousset on the mKdV equation
[
31
]
. Instead of relying primarily on coercivity and virial identities, they combine a localized modulation analysis near the soliton with a detailed study of the dispersive radiation. This latter component is handled through the space-time resonances framework introduced by Germain–Masmoudi–Shatah
[
30
]
, which enables one to track nonlinear interactions between oscillatory modes and to capture the modified scattering behavior of the radiation. This refined dispersive machinery is particularly well suited for equations such as mKdV, whose linear dispersion relation is local and regular, whose soliton profile is explicit, and for which precise Fourier-based estimates allow a sharp description of resonant and non-resonant interactions.

However, these structural advantages are not available for the CH equation. The linearized CH operator is fundamentally nonlocal, with a symbol involving rational functions of the Fourier variable; as a result, the underlying “dispersion relation” does not lend itself to a clean analysis of resonant sets, stationary phase, or multilinear oscillatory integrals — features that are essential in the space-time resonances method. Moreover, the absence of an explicit soliton profile means that one cannot isolate the discrete spectral component by direct projection arguments, nor can one express the radiation as a simple perturbation governed by a local dispersive equation. The nonlocality of the operator further obscures the structure of nonlinear interactions, making it exceedingly difficult to identify or exploit cancellations among resonant contributions.

Our approach circumvents these obstacles by invoking tools from the integrable structure of the CH equation. The key object is the recursion operator of the CH hierarchy. In the Lax pair formulation, this operator generates the higher flows and intertwines the spectral problems associated with the spatial part of the Lax pair. A crucial observation is that this recursion operator commutes with the linearized operator at the soliton. This commutativity serves as the main analytic pathway from integrability to spectral theory. It enables us to construct the eigenfunctions of the linearized operator explicitly, in terms of squared eigenfunctions of the Lax operator, thereby providing a complete and fully explicit spectral resolution despite the absence of a closed formula for the soliton (see Section
2.3
). Through this mechanism, we obtain a complete characterization of the discrete and continuous spectrum, including the fact that the translation mode generates the only discrete eigenvalue, that no unstable eigenvalues exist, and that the continuous spectrum arises through the transform of the Lax spectrum under the squared eigenfunction map.

With the explicit spectral structure in hand, we establish sharp semigroup decay estimates in exponentially weighted Sobolev spaces following the framework of Pego–Weinstein’s work for KdV
[
53
]
. The weighted spaces serve to isolate the neutral translational eigenmode and to shift the continuous spectrum strictly into the stable half-plane, yielding exponential decay for the linearized flow. To convert the linearized decay into a full nonlinear asymptotic stability statement, a rigidity argument is required: the only solutions close to solitons which exhibit regularity and strong decay properties are the solitons itself. In our CH setting, the rigidity proof relies critically on the spectral resolution produced by the recursion operator machinery and the weighted semigroup decay. The spectral decomposition allows us to control separately the translational mode and the dispersive radiation, while the weighted decay ensures that all radiation is exponentially damped in the moving frame. A localized modulation argument is then used to track the soliton center and guarantees that the modulated solution remains uniformly aligned with a member of the soliton family. The exponential decay of the linearized semigroup prevents the accumulation of radiation near the soliton and forces the solution to relax to a single modulated profile, and hence yields a nonlinear Liouville-type theorem for the CH solitons.

The integrable-structure-based spectral analysis also extends smoothly to multi-soliton configurations. Each soliton carries its own localized spectral resolution, and because the solitons separate linearly in time, their interactions decay sufficiently rapidly to allow the weighted semigroup estimates and modulation arguments to run uniformly in regions associated with different soliton centers. It is useful here to distinguish between well-separated trains of solitons, for which the strategy of Martel–Merle–Tsai
[
46
]
applies once the asymptotic stability of a single soliton is known, and the exact multi-solitons family produced by the inverse scattering method. For the latter, the recent work of Wang–Liu
[
55
]
establishes orbital stability of multi-solitons using the bi-Hamiltonian structure and the completeness of squared eigenfunctions. This provides a robust control on perturbations tangent to the multi-solitons manifold and serves as the natural entry point for the asymptotic theory. By contrast, in the KdV setting the work of Killip–Vişan
[
37
]
provides orbital stability at low regularity but does not address asymptotic convergence. In the CH setting, once orbital stability is known, the linear-in-time separation of the constituent solitons implies that every multi-solitons become, for large times, well approximated by a well-separated train. The absence of any persistent amplification of radiation modes and the exponential decay in weighted spaces ensure that cross-interactions between distinct solitons remain negligible for large times. As a result, each constituent soliton behaves asymptotically like an isolated soliton governed by its own spectral and dynamical parameters. This yields the asymptotic stability of multi-solitons for the CH equation, again without relying on explicit formulas for the soliton profiles or fine spectral data (exact negative eigenvalues and eigenfunctions), and without invoking any dispersive or virial identities.

\mathcal{J}L_{1}

2.
Preliminaries

In this section we collect some preliminaries for the CH equation.
This section is divided into six parts. In Section
2.1
, we present the equivalent eigenvalue problem of the CH equation and the basic facts from which, through the inverse scattering transform, the conservation laws and multi-solitons of the CH equation are derived. In Section
2.2
, the bi-Hamiltonian formation of the CH equation is considered, and the recursion operators are introduced to compute the conservation laws at the multi-solitons. The spectrum of the recursion operators is obtained through the usage of squared eigenfunctions. This allows us to perform the spectral analysis for various linearized operators at the smooth solitons. The spectral information of the linearized operators at multi-solitons requires the understanding of the corresponding recursion operator at those multi-solitons, which is presented in Section
2.3
. In Section
2.4
some necessary well-posedness results of the CH equations are presented. Section
2.5
aims at establishing
H^{1}
- monotonicity formulae related to the conservation laws and decay properties for the
H^{1}
-localized solutions. Section
2.6
focuses on the modulation theory for multi-solitons.

2.1.
Eigenvalue problem, conservation laws and multi-solitons

The CH equation (
1.1
) can be expressed as a compatibility condition of the following Lax pair
[
7
]

\displaystyle\Psi_{xx}=\left(\frac{1}{4}+\lambda(m+\omega)\right)\Psi,

(2.1)

\displaystyle\Psi_{t}=\left(\frac{1}{2\lambda}-u\right)\Psi_{x}+\frac{u_{x}}{2}\Psi+\eta\Psi,

(2.2)

with a spectral parameter
\lambda
and a constant
\eta
for a proper normalization of the
eigenfunctions. Equation (
2.1
) is the spectral problem associated with (
1.1
). Let

k^{2}=-\frac{1}{4}-\lambda\omega
, i.e.

\lambda(k)=-\frac{1}{\omega}\Big(k^{2}+\frac{1}{4}\Big).

(2.3)

The spectrum of the problem (
2.1
) is
described in
[
11
,
13
]
. The continuous spectrum in terms of
\lambda

corresponds to
k\in\mathbb{R}
. The discrete spectrum consists of
finitely many points
k_{n}=i\kappa_{n}
,
n=1,\ldots,N
where

\kappa_{n}
is real and
0<\kappa_{n}<\frac{1}{2}
.

A basis in the space of solutions of (
2.1
) can be introduced
in the spirit of the Jost solutions of the CH equation,

f^{+}(x,k)
and
\bar{f}^{+}(x,\bar{k})
. For all real
k\neq 0
the Jost solutions are completely determined by their asymptotics as
x\rightarrow+\infty

[
13
]
:

\lim_{x\to+\infty}e^{-ikx}f^{+}(x,k)=1.

(2.4)

Another basis,
f^{-}(x,k)

and
\bar{f}^{-}(x,\bar{k})
, can be introduced and determined by the asymptotics at

x\rightarrow-\infty
for all real
k\neq 0
:

\lim_{x\to-\infty}e^{ikx}f^{-}(x,k)=1.

(2.5)

Since
m(x)
and
\omega
are real one gets that if
f^{+}(x,k)

and
f^{-}(x,k)
are solutions of (
2.1
), then

\bar{f}^{+}(x,\bar{k})=f^{+}(x,-k),\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad\bar{f}^{-}(x,\bar{k})=f^{-}(x,-k)

are also solutions of (
2.1
). In particular, for real
k\neq 0
, one has

\bar{f}^{\pm}(x,k)=f^{\pm}(x,-k),

(2.6)

and for each
k\in\mathbb{R}
there exists
a(k),b(k)\in\mathbb{C}
such that

f^{-}(x,k)=a(k)f^{+}(x,-k)+b(k)f^{+}(x,k).

(2.7)

From (
2.7
), letting
x\rightarrow\pm\infty
, we obtain

\displaystyle\lim_{x\rightarrow-\infty}\left(f^{+}(x,k)-a(k)e^{ikx}+b(-k)e^{-ikx}\right)=0,

\displaystyle\lim_{x\rightarrow+\infty}\left(f^{-}(x,k)-a(k)e^{-ikx}-b(k)e^{ikx}\right)=0.

The Wronskian
W(f_{1},f_{2}):=f_{1}\partial_{x}f_{2}-f_{2}\partial_{x}f_{1}
of any pair of
solutions of (
2.1
) does not depend on
x
. Therefore

W(f^{-}(x,k),f^{-}(x,-k))=W(f^{+}(x,-k),f^{+}(x,k))=2ik.

(2.8)

Computing the Wronskians
W(f^{-},f^{+})
and
W(\bar{f}^{+},f^{-})

and using (
2.7
), (
2.8
) we obtain:

a(k)=(2ik)^{-1}W(f^{-}(x,k),f^{+}(x,k)),\qquad b(k)=-(2ik)^{-1}W(f^{-}(x,k),f^{+}(x,-k)).\

(2.9)

From (
2.7
) and (
2.8
), it follows that for all
k\in\mathbb{R}

a(k)a(-k)-b(k)b(-k)=1.

(2.10)

It is well known
[
13
]
that
f^{+}(x,k)e^{-ikx}
and

f^{-}(x,k)e^{ikx}
have analytic extensions to the upper half of
the complex
k
-plane. Likewise

\bar{f}^{+}(x,\bar{k})e^{i\bar{k}x}
and
\bar{f}^{-}(x,\bar{k})e^{-i\bar{k}x}
allow analytic extension to the lower half of the
complex
k
-plane. An important consequence of these properties is
that
a(k)
(and hence
b(k)
) also allows analytic extension to the upper half of
the complex
k
-plane and

\bar{a}(\bar{k})=a(-k),\qquad\bar{b}(\bar{k})=b(-k),

As a result, (
2.10
) reduces to the form

|a(k)|^{2}-|b(k)|^{2}=1,\qquad k\in\mathbb{R}.

(2.11)

At the points
i\kappa_{n}
of the discrete spectrum,
a(k)
has
simple zeroes
[
13
]
,

a(k)=(k-i\kappa_{n})\dot{a}_{n}+\frac{1}{2}(k-i\kappa_{n})^{2}\ddot{a}_{n}+\cdots,

and so that the Wronskian
W(f^{-},f^{+})
in (
2.9
) vanishes. Thus

f^{-}
and
f^{+}
are linearly dependent:

f^{-}(x,i\kappa_{n})=b_{n}f^{+}(x,i\kappa_{n}).

(2.12)

In other words, the discrete spectrum is simple, and there is only
one (real) linearly independent eigenfunction corresponding to
each eigenvalue
i\kappa_{n}

f_{n}^{-}(x):=f^{-}(x,i\kappa_{n}).

From the above and (
2.4
), (
2.5
), it follows
that
f_{n}^{-}(x)
decays exponentially for
x\to\pm\infty
, which
allows us to show that
f_{n}^{-}(x)\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
. Moreover,
for compactly supported potentials
m(x)
, from (
2.12
) and
(
2.7
), we have

b_{n}=b(i\kappa_{n}),\qquad b(-i\kappa_{n})=-\frac{1}{b_{n}}.

The above result can
be extended to Schwartz-class potentials by an appropriate limiting
procedure. The asymptotics of
f_{n}^{-}
, according to (
2.6
),
(
2.4
), (
2.12
) are

\displaystyle f_{n}^{-}(x)=e^{\kappa_{n}x}+o(e^{\kappa_{n}x}),

\displaystyle x\rightarrow-\infty,

\displaystyle f_{n}^{-}(x)=b_{n}e^{-\kappa_{n}x}+o(e^{-\kappa_{n}x}),

\displaystyle x\rightarrow\infty.

The sign of
b_{n}
obviously depends on the number of the zeroes
of
f_{n}^{-}
. Suppose that

0<\kappa_{1}<\ldots<\kappa_{N}<\frac{1}{2}
, then from the
oscillation theorem for the Sturm-Liouville problem,

f_{n}^{-}
has exactly
n-1
zeroes. Therefore

b_{n}=(-1)^{n-1}|b_{n}|.

The sets

\mathcal{S^{\pm}}:=\left\{\frac{b(\pm k)}{a(k)}\quad(k>0),\quad\kappa_{n},\quad C_{n}^{\pm}:=\frac{b_{n}^{\pm 1}}{i\dot{a}_{n}},\quad n=1,\ldots N\right\}

are called scattering data. Here the dot stands for the derivative
with respect to
k
and
\dot{a}_{n}:=\dot{a}(i\kappa_{n})
,

\ddot{a}_{n}:=\ddot{a}(i\kappa_{n})
, etc.
The time evolution of the scattering data is presented in
[
17
]

as follows.

\displaystyle a(k,t)=a(k,0),

\displaystyle b(k,t)=b(k,0)e^{\frac{ik}{\lambda}t},

\displaystyle\frac{1}{a(k,t)}=\frac{1}{a(k,0)},

\displaystyle\frac{b(\pm k,t)}{a(k,t)}=\frac{b(\pm k,0)}{a(k,0)}e^{\pm\frac{ik}{\lambda}t},

\displaystyle\qquad C_{n}^{\pm}(t)=C_{n}^{\pm}(0)\exp\left(\pm\frac{4\omega\kappa_{n}}{1-4\kappa_{n}^{2}}t\right).

In other words,
a(k)
is independent on
t
and will serve as a
generating function of the conservation laws. In particular, the integral

\Lambda:=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left(\sqrt{\frac{m(x)+\omega}{\omega}}-1\right)\text{d}x,

as well as all the coefficients
\mathcal{I}_{k}
in the asymptotic expansion

\ln a(k)=-i\Lambda k+\sum_{s=1}^{\infty}\frac{\mathcal{I}_{s}}{k^{2s+1}},

must be integrals of motion. The integral
\Lambda
is the unique Casimir function for the CH equation.
The densities
p_{s}
of
\mathcal{I}_{s}=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}p_{s}\text{d}x
can be expressed in terms of
m(x)
using a set of
recurrent relations obtained in
[
33
]
.

Let us define the following
squared eigenfunctions

F^{\pm}(x,k):=(f^{\pm}(x,k))^{2},\qquad F^{\pm}_{n}(x):=F^{\pm}(x,i\kappa_{n}),\quad n=1,2,\cdot\cdot\cdot,N.

one can also derive the following
relations for the variations of the scattering data with respect to the potential
m
as follows
[
18
]
:

\displaystyle\frac{\delta a(k)}{\delta m(x)}=-\frac{\lambda}{2ik}f^{+}(x,k)f^{-}(x,k),

(2.13)

\displaystyle\frac{\delta b(k)}{\delta m(x)}=\frac{\lambda}{2ik}f^{+}(x,-k)f^{-}(x,k),

(2.14)

\displaystyle\frac{\delta\ln\lambda_{n}}{\delta m(x)}=\frac{iF_{n}^{-}(x)}{\omega b_{n}\dot{a}_{n}}.

(2.15)

Moreover, from
[
17
, Proposition 5]
, there holds the following useful completeness relation for the squared eigenfunctions:

\displaystyle\frac{\omega\theta(x-y)}{\sqrt{m(x)+\omega}\sqrt{m(y)+\omega}}=-\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{F^{-}(x,k)F^{+}(y,k)}{ka^{2}(k)}\mbox{\rm d}k

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac{1}{i\kappa_{n}\dot{a}_{n}^{2}}\left[F_{n}^{-}(x)\dot{F}_{n}^{+}(y)+F_{n}^{-}(x)\dot{F}_{n}^{+}(y)-\left(\frac{1}{i\kappa_{n}}+\frac{\ddot{a}_{n}}{\dot{a}_{n}}\right)F_{n}^{-}(x)F_{n}^{+}(y)\right],

(2.16)

where
\theta(x)
is the step function, and again, dot denotes derivative in
k
. For any function
f(x)
which vanishes for
x\rightarrow\pm\infty
, one can expand it over the squared eigenfunctions
F^{+}(x,k)
and
F^{-}(x,k)
. Indeed, we multiply (
2.1
) by
\frac{1}{2}m_{y}f(y)+(m(y)+\omega)f_{y}
and integrate over
y
to have

\pm\omega f(x)=-\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{F^{\mp}(x,k)\xi^{\pm}_{f}(k)}{ka^{2}(k)}\mbox{\rm d}k+\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac{1}{i\kappa_{n}\dot{a}_{n}^{2}}\left[\dot{F}_{n}^{\mp}(x)\xi^{\pm}_{f,n}+F_{n}^{\mp}(x)\dot{\xi}^{\pm}_{f,n}\right],

(2.17)

where

\displaystyle\xi^{\pm}_{f}(k)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}F^{\pm}(y,k)\left[\frac{1}{2}m_{y}f(y)+(m(y)+\omega)f_{y}\right]\mbox{\rm d}y,

\displaystyle\xi^{\pm}_{f,n}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}F_{n}^{\pm}(y)\left[\frac{1}{2}m_{y}f(y)+(m(y)+\omega)f_{y}\right]\mbox{\rm d}y,

\displaystyle\dot{\xi}^{\pm}_{f,n}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\dot{F}_{n}^{\pm}(y)\left[\frac{1}{2}m_{y}f(y)+(m(y)+\omega)f_{y}\right]\mbox{\rm d}y-\left(\frac{1}{i\kappa_{n}}+\frac{\ddot{a}_{n}}{\dot{a}_{n}}\right)\xi^{\pm}_{f,n}.

The inverse scattering is simplified into the important case of the so-called
reflectionless potentials, where the scattering data is confined to the case
the reflection coefficient
\frac{b(k)}{a(k)}=0
for all real
k
. This class of potentials corresponds to the
N
-solitons of the CH equation. In this case
b(k)=0
and
|a(k)|=1
(see (
2.11
)). The
N
-solitons can be calculated in a parametric form
[
16
]
which depends on the discrete spectrum
k_{n}=i\kappa_{n}
of (
2.1
) for
n=1,2,\cdot\cdot\cdot,N
and
0<\kappa_{1}<\kappa_{2}<\ldots<\kappa_{N}<\frac{1}{2}
, which are the zeros
a(k)
in the imaginary axis. Moreover,

i\dot{a}_{n}=\frac{1}{2\kappa_{n}}e^{\Lambda\kappa_{n}}\prod_{j\neq n}\frac{\kappa_{n}-\kappa_{j}}{\kappa_{n}+\kappa_{j}},\quad\text{where}\quad\Lambda=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\ln\Big(\frac{1+2\kappa_{n}}{1-2\kappa_{n}}\Big)^{2}.

The CH
N
-solitons can be expressed in a parametric form as follows
[
16
]

u(t,x)=\frac{\omega}{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-|x-g(t,\xi)|}\xi^{-2}g_{\xi}^{-1}(t,\xi)\mbox{\rm d}\xi-\omega,

where
g(t,\xi)
can be expressed through the scattering data as

g(t,\xi):=\ln\int_{0}^{\xi}\left[1-\sum_{n,p}\frac{C_{n}^{+}(t)y^{-2\kappa_{n}}}{\kappa_{n}+1/2}A_{np}(t,y)\right]^{-2}\mbox{\rm d}y,\quad\text{with}\quad A_{np}(t,y):=\delta_{pn}+\frac{C_{n}^{+}(t)y^{-2\kappa_{n}}}{\kappa_{n}+\kappa_{p}}.

The CH
N
-solitons are also presented in
[
47
]
in a parametric form using elementary determinant theory. The author demonstrates that these CH
N
-solitons exhibit the asymptotic behavior (
1.10
) as time approaches infinity, along with a formula for the phase shift.

2.2.
Recursion operators and completeness relations of the squared eigenfunctions

In this subsection, we analyze the spectral information of recursion operators and the linearized operators around the soliton profile
\varphi_{c}
, which will be crucial in our proof of linear Liouville property (see Theorem
4.1
below).

From the bi-Hamiltonian structure (
1.2
), Lenard relation (
1.6
) and the relation
(1-\partial_{x}^{2})\frac{\delta\mathcal{H}_{n}[m]}{\delta m}=\frac{\delta\mathcal{H}_{n}(u)}{\delta u}
, one has the following relations for
\mathcal{H}_{n}
,

\frac{\delta\mathcal{H}_{n+1}[m]}{\delta m}=(1-\partial_{x}^{2})^{-1}\left[2\omega+m+\partial_{x}^{-1}(m\partial_{x})\right]\frac{\delta\mathcal{H}_{n}[m]}{\delta m}=:\mathcal{K}[m]\frac{\delta\mathcal{H}_{n}[m]}{\delta m},

(2.18)

\frac{\delta\mathcal{H}_{n+1}(u)}{\delta u}=\left[2\omega+m+\partial_{x}^{-1}(m\partial_{x})\right](1-\partial_{x}^{2})^{-1}\frac{\delta\mathcal{H}_{n}(u)}{\delta u}=:\mathcal{R}(u)\frac{\delta\mathcal{H}_{n}(u)}{\delta u},

(2.19)

where
\partial_{x}^{-1}
is the inverse derivative operator defined by
\partial_{x}^{-1}f=\int_{-\infty}^{x}f\mbox{\rm d}x
with
f\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})
. If we take
\mathcal{S}^{\ast}(\mathbb{R}):=\left\{\partial_{x}^{-1}f:\ f\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})\right\}
, with a bilinear form given by

\langle f,g\rangle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(x)g(x)\mbox{\rm d}x,

then
\partial_{x}^{-1}:\mathcal{S}^{\ast}(\mathbb{R})\rightarrow\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})
is skew-symmetric and
\partial_{x}\partial_{x}^{-1}=id
.

It is not difficult (from the bi-Hamiltonian structure (
1.2
)) to see

\mathcal{K}[m]=\mathcal{J}_{2}^{-1}\mathcal{J}_{1}.

Moreover, the linear operators
\mathcal{K}(m)
and
\mathcal{R}(u)
are nonlocal, satisfying

\mathcal{R}(u)=(1-\partial_{x}^{2})\mathcal{K}[m](1-\partial_{x}^{2})^{-1},

(2.20)

namely, they are similar to each other. Moreover, one can check that it holds that

\mathcal{K}[m]\left(1-\sqrt{\frac{\omega}{m+\omega}}\right)=u,\qquad\mathcal{R}(u)(1-\partial_{x}^{2})\left(1-\sqrt{\frac{\omega}{m+\omega}}\right)=m.

These can be viewed as in a special case
n=0
in (
2.18
) and (
2.19
), respectively. The associated conservation laws are

\mathcal{H}_{0}[m]:=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(\sqrt{m(x)+\omega}-\sqrt{\omega}\right)^{2}\mbox{\rm d}x,\qquad\frac{\delta\mathcal{H}_{0}[m]}{\delta m}=1-\sqrt{\frac{\omega}{m+\omega}}.

Therefore, the CH hierarchy with the choice of the dispersion law
\Omega(z)
can be expressed as follows:

m_{t}+\sqrt{m+\omega}\left[\sqrt{m+\omega}\ \Omega(2\mathcal{K}[m])\left(1-\sqrt{\frac{\omega}{m+\omega}}\right)\right]_{x}=0.

In particular, if the dispersion law
\Omega(z)=z
, then the above becomes the CH equation.

Denoting adjoint operator of
\mathcal{K}[m]
by
\mathcal{K}^{\ast}[m]:=\mathcal{J}_{1}\mathcal{J}_{2}^{-1}
, then the adjoint of
\mathcal{R}(u)
is

\mathcal{R}^{\ast}(u)=(1-\partial_{x}^{2})^{-1}\mathcal{K}^{\ast}[m](1-\partial_{x}^{2})=(1-\partial_{x}^{2})^{-1}\mathcal{J}_{1}\mathcal{J}_{2}^{-1}(1-\partial_{x}^{2}),

(2.21)

and it is not difficult to see that the operators
\mathcal{R}(u)
and
\mathcal{R}^{\ast}(u)
satisfy

\mathcal{R}^{\ast}(u)\mathcal{J}=\mathcal{J}\mathcal{R}(u).

(2.22)

The understanding of the spectral information of the recursion operators
\mathcal{R}(u)
and
\mathcal{R}^{\ast}(u)
is essential in the (spectral) stability of multi-solitons of the CH equation (
1.1
), see
[
55
]
.

It is shown in
[
17
]
that the conservation laws
\mathcal{H}_{j}
can be expressed as follows:

\mathcal{H}_{j}(u)=-\int_{0}^{\infty}(-2)^{1-j}\frac{k\rho(k)}{\lambda^{j}}dk+\frac{(-2)^{2-j}}{\omega}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\int\frac{\kappa_{n}^{2}}{\lambda_{n}^{j+2}}d\kappa_{n},

(2.23)

where
\rho(k):=\frac{2k}{\pi\omega\lambda^{2}}\ln|a(k)|
. The first term on the right-hand side of (
2.23
) is the contribution
from radiations and the second term comes from the solitons.

Let us consider the quantities
\mathcal{H}_{j}(\varphi_{c})
which are related to the
1
-soliton profile
\varphi_{c}
. From (
1.8
) and (
2.19
) it follows that the
1
-soliton
\varphi_{c}
with speed

satisfies the following variational principle

\frac{\delta}{\delta u}\left(-\mathcal{H}_{j+1}(u)+c\mathcal{H}_{j}(u)\right)=0,\quad j\in\mathbb{Z}_{+},

that is,

-\mathcal{H}_{j+1}^{\prime}(\varphi_{c})+c\mathcal{H}^{\prime}_{j}(\varphi_{c})=0.

(2.24)

Now multiply (
2.24
) with
\frac{d\varphi_{c}}{dc}
. For each
j
one has

\frac{\mbox{\rm d}\mathcal{H}_{j+1}(\varphi_{c})}{\mbox{\rm d}c}=c\frac{\mbox{\rm d}\mathcal{H}_{j}(\varphi_{c})}{\mbox{\rm d}c}=\cdots=c^{j}\frac{\mbox{\rm d}\mathcal{H}_{1}(\varphi_{c})}{\mbox{\rm d}c},

and therefore

\mathcal{H}_{j+1}(\varphi_{c})=\int_{0}^{c}y^{j}\frac{\mbox{\rm d}\mathcal{H}_{1}(\varphi_{y})}{\mbox{\rm d}y}\mbox{\rm d}y,

Calculating
\mathcal{H}_{1}(\varphi_{c})
is challenging because
\varphi_{c}
is not given explicitly. However, considering that the reflection coefficient
\rho(k)
vanishes for
u=U^{(N)}
, with associated discrete eigenvalues
i\kappa_{n}
for
n=1,2,\ldots,N
where
0<\kappa_{n}<\frac{1}{2}
. In particular, for the one-soliton case of
\varphi_{c}
with discrete eigenvalue

, we can derive the following formula from (

):

\mathcal{H}_{j}(\varphi_{c})=\frac{(-2)^{2-j}}{\omega}\int\frac{\kappa^{2}}{\lambda^{j+2}}d\kappa,\quad\ \lambda=-\frac{1}{\omega}\Big(\frac{1}{4}-\kappa^{2}\Big)<0.

(2.25)

More precisely, one has the following for the conservation laws
\mathcal{H}_{1}
and
\mathcal{H}_{2}

\mathcal{H}_{1}(\varphi_{c})=\omega^{2}\left[\ln\frac{1-2\kappa}{1+2\kappa}+\frac{4\kappa(1+4\kappa^{2})}{(1-4\kappa^{2})^{2}}\right],\qquad\mathcal{H}_{2}(\varphi_{c})=\omega^{3}\left[\ln\frac{1-2\kappa}{1+2\kappa}+\frac{4\kappa(3+32\kappa^{2}-48\kappa^{4})}{3(1-4\kappa^{2})^{3}}\right].

From (
2.24
) (multiplying it with
\frac{d\varphi_{c}}{d\kappa}
) and (
2.25
), one has

\frac{(-2)^{1-j}\kappa^{2}}{\omega\lambda^{j+3}}=\frac{\mbox{\rm d}\mathcal{H}_{j+1}(\varphi_{c})}{\mbox{\rm d}\kappa}=c\frac{\mbox{\rm d}\mathcal{H}_{j}(\varphi_{c})}{\mbox{\rm d}\kappa}=c\frac{(-2)^{2-j}\kappa^{2}}{\omega\lambda^{j+2}}.

On the other hand, one can represent the wave speed
c
with respect to
\kappa
as follows

c=-\frac{1}{2\lambda}=\frac{2\omega}{1-4\kappa^{2}}>2\omega.

Therefore, the quantities
\mathcal{H}_{j}(\varphi_{c})
can be computed explicitly with respect to the wave velocity
c
.
Moreover, from (
2.25
) and the above wave speed formula, the derivative of
\mathcal{H}_{1}(\varphi_{c})
with respect to the wave speed

can be computed in the following form,

\frac{\mbox{\rm d}\mathcal{H}_{1}(\varphi_{c})}{\mbox{\rm d}c}=\frac{\mbox{\rm d}\mathcal{H}_{1}(\varphi_{c})}{\mbox{\rm d}\kappa}\frac{\mbox{\rm d}\kappa}{\mbox{\rm d}c}=-\frac{\kappa(1-4\kappa^{2})^{2}}{8\omega^{2}\lambda^{3}}=4\kappa c>0.

Similarly, from (
2.24
) and the above, one has

\frac{\mbox{\rm d}\mathcal{H}_{2}(\varphi_{c})}{\mbox{\rm d}c}=c\frac{\mbox{\rm d}\mathcal{H}_{1}(\varphi_{c})}{\mbox{\rm d}c}=4\kappa c^{2}>0.

(2.26)

For simplicity, we denote
\varphi_{c}
by
\varphi
. Then by (
2.19
), we have

\mathcal{H}^{\prime}_{n+1}(\varphi)=\mathcal{R}(\varphi)\mathcal{H}^{\prime}_{n}(\varphi),

where
\mathcal{R}(\varphi)
is given in (

)
with

.

The recursion operator
\mathcal{R}(\varphi)
and the adjoint recursion operator
\mathcal{R}^{\ast}(\varphi)
(see (

)) play important roles in the spectral analysis of linearized operator

\displaystyle L_{n}:=-\mathcal{H}^{\prime\prime}_{n+1}(\varphi)+c\mathcal{H}^{\prime\prime}_{n}(\varphi),

(2.27)

through the following iterative and commutative operator identities.

Lemma 2.1

(
[
55
]
)
.

For all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, the recursion operator
\mathcal{R}(\varphi)
, the adjoint recursion operator
\mathcal{R}^{\ast}(\varphi)
, and the linearized operator
L_{n}
satisfy the following operator identities.

\displaystyle-\mathcal{H}^{\prime\prime}_{n+2}(\varphi)+c\mathcal{H}^{\prime\prime}_{n+1}(\varphi)=\mathcal{R}(\varphi)\left[-\mathcal{H}^{\prime\prime}_{n+1}(\varphi)+c\mathcal{H}^{\prime\prime}_{n}(\varphi)\right],

(2.28)

\displaystyle L_{n}\mathcal{J}\mathcal{R}(\varphi)=\mathcal{R}(\varphi)L_{n}\mathcal{J},

(2.29)

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}L_{n}\mathcal{R}^{\ast}(\varphi)=\mathcal{R}^{\ast}(\varphi)\mathcal{J}L_{n},

(2.30)

where
\mathcal{J}
is the skew symmetric operator defined in (
1.5
).

Proof.

Identity (
2.28
) follows from (
2.19
) and the definition of Gateaux derivative and variational principle of soliton profile
\mathcal{H}_{n}^{\prime}(\varphi)=c^{n-1}\mathcal{H}_{1}^{\prime}(\varphi)=c^{n-1}m_{\varphi}
.

Thus we only need to show (
2.30
), since (
2.29
) can be obtained by taking adjoint on (
2.30
). Notice that from
(
2.28
), one has that the operator
\mathcal{R}(\varphi)L_{n}=L_{n+1}
is self-adjoint. This in turn implies that

(\mathcal{R}(\varphi)L_{n})^{\ast}=\mathcal{R}(\varphi)L_{n}=L_{n}\mathcal{R}^{\ast}(\varphi),

On the other hand, in view of (
1.5
) and (
2.22
), one has

\mathcal{J}L_{n}\mathcal{R}^{\ast}(\varphi)=\mathcal{J}\mathcal{R}(\varphi)L_{n}=\mathcal{R}^{\ast}(\varphi)\mathcal{J}L_{n},

which is the desired (
2.30
).
∎

Remark 2.1
.

Identities (
2.29
) and (
2.30
) hold also for the multi-solitons of the CH equation.
In particular, let
U^{(N)}
be the CH
N
-soliton profile. The corresponding Lyapunov functional
I_{N}
is given by
[
55
]

I_{N}(u)=(-1)^{N}\left[\mathcal{H}_{N+1}(u)+\sum_{n=1}^{N}\mu_{n}\mathcal{H}_{n}(u)\right],

where
\mu_{n}
are Lagrange multipliers determined by

\mu_{n}=(-1)^{N-n+1}\sigma_{N-n+1},

with
\sigma_{s}
being elementary symmetric functions of
c_{1},c_{2},...,c_{N}

\sigma_{1}=\sum_{j=1}^{N}c_{j},\quad\sigma_{2}=\sum_{j<k}c_{j}c_{k},\quad\ldots,\quad\sigma_{N}=\prod_{j=1}^{N}c_{j}.

The self-adjoint second variation operator is defined as follows

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{N}:=I_{N}^{\prime\prime}(U^{(N)}).

(2.31)

Then it is easy to see that similar to Lemma
2.1
, the following operator identities hold

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{N}\mathcal{J}\mathcal{R}(U^{(N)})=\mathcal{R}(U^{(N)})\mathcal{L}_{N}\mathcal{J},

(2.32)

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}\mathcal{L}_{N}\mathcal{R}^{\ast}(U^{(N)})=\mathcal{R}^{\ast}(U^{(N)})\mathcal{J}\mathcal{L}_{N}.

(2.33)

An immediate consequence of (
2.29
) is that
\mathcal{R}(\varphi)
commutes with
L_{n}\mathcal{J}
, and thus they share the same eigenfunctions. Similarly, (
2.30
) implies that the same property holds for
\mathcal{R}^{\ast}(\varphi)
and
\mathcal{J}L_{n}
.

We will conclude this subsection with the following results revealing the spectral information of the recursion operator
\mathcal{R}(\varphi)
, its adjoint
\mathcal{R}^{\ast}(\varphi)
, the linear operators
\mathcal{J}L_{n}
and
L_{n}\mathcal{J}
. Note that the recursion operators are nonlocal which are not easy to study directly. However, by employing the operator identity (
2.20
) and the properties of the squared eigenfunctions
F^{\pm}(x,k)
, one could have the following result.

Lemma 2.2

(Spectrum of
\mathcal{R}(\varphi)
)
.

The recursion operator
\mathcal{R}(\varphi)
, defined in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
with domain
H^{1}(\mathbb{R})
, has only one eigenvalue
c
associated with the eigenfunction
m_{\varphi}
; the essential spectrum is the interval
(0,2\omega]
; and the corresponding eigenfunctions do not have spatial decay and are not in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
. Moreover, the kernel of
\mathcal{R}(\varphi)
is trivial, and the inverse of
\mathcal{R}(\varphi)
reads

\mathcal{R}^{-1}(\varphi)=\frac{1}{2}(1-\partial_{x}^{2})\frac{1}{\sqrt{m_{\varphi}+\omega}}\partial_{x}^{-1}\frac{1}{\sqrt{m_{\varphi}+\omega}}\partial_{x}.

Proof.

Consider the Jost solutions
f^{\pm}(x,k)
of the eigenvalue problem (
2.1
) with the potential
m(x)=m_{\varphi}=\varphi-\varphi_{xx}
and the asymptotics in (
2.4
) and (
2.5
). In this case there is an eigenvalue
k=i\kappa_{1}
(
0<\kappa_{1}<\frac{1}{2}
) which generates the soliton profile
\varphi_{c}
. It is then found that the squared eigenfunctions
F^{\pm}(x,k)=(f^{\pm}(x,k))^{2}
satisfy

\bigg(-\partial_{x}^{2}+1+2\lambda\big(m_{\varphi}(x)+2\omega+\partial_{x}^{-1}(m_{\varphi}(x)\partial_{x})\big)\bigg)F^{\pm}(x,k)=0.

This in turn implies that

(1-\partial_{x}^{2})^{-1}\left(m_{\varphi}(x)+2\omega+\partial_{x}^{-1}(m_{\varphi}(x)\partial_{x})\right)F^{\pm}(x,k)=\mathcal{K}[m_{\varphi}]F^{\pm}(x,k)=-\frac{1}{2\lambda}F^{\pm}(x,k).

(2.34)

By (
2.34
) and (
2.20
), it follows that for
k\in\mathbb{R}
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(\varphi)(1-\partial_{x}^{2})F^{\pm}(x,k)=-\frac{1}{2\lambda}(1-\partial_{x}^{2})F^{\pm}(x,k),

(2.35)

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(\varphi)(1-\partial_{x}^{2})F_{1}^{\pm}(x)=-\frac{1}{2\lambda_{1}}(1-\partial_{x}^{2})F_{1}^{\pm}(x)=c(1-\partial_{x}^{2})F_{1}^{\pm}(x).

(2.36)

Moreover, in view of the element
\dot{F}_{1}^{\pm}(x)
in the completeness relation (
2.1
), it follows that

\mathcal{R}(\varphi)(1-\partial_{x}^{2})\dot{F}_{1}^{\pm}(x)=-\frac{1}{2\lambda_{1}}(1-\partial_{x}^{2})\dot{F}_{1}^{\pm}(x)-\frac{i\kappa_{1}}{\omega\lambda_{1}^{2}}(1-\partial_{x}^{2})F_{1}^{\pm}(x).

Since
\mathcal{R}(\varphi)m_{\varphi}=cm_{\varphi}
, it is inferred from (
2.36
) that
F_{1}^{\pm}(x)\sim\varphi(x)
. On account of (
2.35
), the essential spectrum of
\mathcal{R}(\varphi)
is given by the set
\left\{\lambda:-\frac{1}{2\lambda}=\frac{\omega}{2k^{2}+\frac{1}{2}},k\in\mathbb{R}\right\}
, which is exactly
(0,2\omega]
.
The associated generalized eigenfunctions
(1-\partial_{x}^{2})F^{\pm}(x,k)
possess no spatial decay and thus are not in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
, which can be seen from (
2.4
) and (
2.5
).

On the other hand, a simple direct computation shows that the kernel of
\mathcal{R}(\varphi)
is trivial except for
\omega=0
. In particular, consider
v(x):=(1-\partial_{x}^{2})(1/\sqrt{m_{\varphi}+\omega})
. One deduces that
\mathcal{R}(\varphi)v=\omega>0
. The inverse of
\mathcal{R}(\varphi)
can also be verified directly. The proof of the lemma is complete.
∎

Remark 2.2
.

In view of (
2.20
) or (
2.34
), one can conclude that the operator
\mathcal{K}[m_{\varphi}]
shares the same spectra with the operator
\mathcal{R}(\varphi)
. For instance, we have
\mathcal{K}(m_{\varphi})\varphi=c\varphi
, the kernel of which is trivial and the inverse is

\mathcal{K}^{-1}[m_{\varphi}]=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{m_{\varphi}+\omega}}\partial_{x}^{-1}\frac{1}{\sqrt{m_{\varphi}+\omega}}(1-\partial_{x}^{2})^{-1}\partial_{x}.

Next consider the adjoint recursion operator
\mathcal{R}^{\ast}(\varphi)
. As discussed before,
\mathcal{R}^{\ast}(\varphi)
and
\mathcal{J}L_{n}
share the same eigenfuntions, and thus is more relevant to the spectral stability problems of solitons. Recall from (
2.21
) that

\mathcal{R}^{\ast}(u)=(1-\partial_{x}^{2})^{-1}\mathcal{J}_{1}\mathcal{J}_{2}^{-1}(1-\partial_{x}^{2}).

The observation of (
2.34
) reveals that

\mathcal{J}_{1}\mathcal{J}_{2}^{-1}\mathcal{J}_{1}F^{\pm}(x,k)=\mathcal{J}_{1}\mathcal{K}[m]F^{\pm}(x,k)=-\frac{1}{2\lambda}\mathcal{J}_{1}F^{\pm}(x,k).

From above one can verify that the eigenfunctions of
\mathcal{R}^{\ast}(u)
are

(1-\partial_{x}^{2})^{-1}\mathcal{J}_{1}F^{\pm}(x,k)=-\partial_{x}\mathcal{K}[m]F^{\pm}(x,k)=\frac{1}{2\lambda}(F^{\pm}(x,k))_{x}.

(2.37)

Lemma 2.3

(Spectrum of
\mathcal{R}^{\ast}(\varphi)
)
.

The adjoint recursion operator
\mathcal{R}^{\ast}(\varphi)
, defined in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
with domain
H^{1}(\mathbb{R})
, has only one eigenvalue
c
associated with the eigenfunction
\varphi_{x}
; the essential spectrum is the interval
(0,2\omega]
; and the corresponding eigenfunctions do not have spatial decay and hence are not in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
. Moreover, the kernel of
\mathcal{R}^{\ast}(\varphi)
is trivial, and the inverse of
\mathcal{R}^{\ast}(\varphi)
is

\big(\mathcal{R}^{\ast}(\varphi)\big)^{-1}=\frac{1}{2}\partial_{x}\frac{1}{\sqrt{m_{\varphi}+\omega}}\partial_{x}^{-1}\frac{1}{\sqrt{m_{\varphi}+\omega}}(1-\partial_{x}^{2})^{-1}.

Proof.

Consider the Jost solutions
f^{\pm}(x,k)
of the eigenvalue problem (
2.1
) with the potential
m_{\varphi}
and the asymptotics in (
2.4
) and (
2.5
). The soliton profile
\varphi
is generated by the eigenvalue
k=i\kappa_{1}
(
0<\kappa_{1}<\frac{1}{2}
). By (
2.37
), the following relations hold

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}^{\ast}(\varphi)\big(F^{\pm}(x,k)\big)_{x}=-\frac{1}{2\lambda}\big(F^{\pm}(x,k)\big)_{x},\quad\text{for}\ k\in\mathbb{R},

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}^{\ast}(\varphi)\big(F_{1}^{\pm}(x)\big)_{x}=-\frac{1}{2\lambda_{1}}\big(F_{1}^{\pm}(x)\big)_{x}=c\big(F_{1}^{\pm}(x)\big)_{x},

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}^{\ast}(\varphi)\big(\dot{F}_{1}^{\pm}(x)\big)_{x}=-\frac{1}{2\lambda_{1}}\big(\dot{F}_{1}^{\pm}(x)\big)_{x}-\frac{i\kappa_{1}}{\omega\lambda_{1}^{2}}\big(F_{1}^{\pm}(x)\big)_{x}.

Since
\mathcal{R}^{\ast}(\varphi)\varphi_{x}=c\varphi_{x}
, by the second equation above,
c
is the only eigenvalue. In view of the first equation above, the essential spectrum of
\mathcal{R}^{\ast}(\varphi)
is
\left\{\lambda:-\frac{1}{2\lambda}=\frac{\omega}{2k^{2}+\frac{1}{2}},k\in\mathbb{R}\right\}
, which is the interval
(0,2\omega]
.
From (
2.4
) and (
2.5
) we see that the associated generalized eigenfunctions
\big(F^{\pm}(x,k)\big)_{x}
do not decay and hence are not in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
.

Similarly, a direct computation shows that the kernel of
\mathcal{R}^{\ast}(\varphi)
is trivial except for
\omega=0
. The inverse of
\mathcal{R}^{\ast}(\varphi)
can be verified directly. This completes the proof of Lemma
2.3
.
∎

Now we are in position of deriving the spectrum of linear operators
\mathcal{J}L_{n}
and
L_{n}\mathcal{J}
. This approach is motivated by (
2.29
) and (
2.30
), allowing us to reduce the problem to analyzing the spectrum of the recursion operator
\mathcal{R}(\varphi)
and the adjoint recursion operator
\mathcal{R}^{\ast}(\varphi)
. Next, we show that the eigenfunctions of
\mathcal{R}^{\ast}(\varphi)
(or equivalently,
\mathcal{J}L_{n}
), together with the generalized kernel of
\mathcal{J}L_{n}
, form an orthogonal basis in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
. This serves as a completeness relation, analogous to (
2.1
) and (
2.17
). It then follows that the spectrum of
\mathcal{J}L_{n}
lies on the imaginary axis which yields the spectral stability of solitons.

Start with the operator
\mathcal{J}L_{n}
. Since
L_{n}=\mathcal{R}^{n-1}(\varphi)L_{1}
, whose principle part is

\left[2\omega(1-\partial_{x}^{2})^{-1}\right]^{n-1}(-c\partial_{x}^{2}+c-2\omega),

the symbol of the principle part of the operator
\mathcal{J}L_{n}
is

\varrho_{n,c}(\zeta):=-2^{n-1}\omega^{n-1}\frac{i\zeta(c\zeta^{2}+c-2\omega)}{(1+\zeta^{2})^{n}},\ \zeta\in\mathbb{R}.

(2.38)

Proposition 2.1
.

For
n\geq 1
, the operators
\mathcal{J}L_{n}
defined in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
with domain
H^{1}(\mathbb{R})
and the adjoint recursion operator
\mathcal{R}^{\ast}(\varphi)
share the same eigenfunctions. Moreover, the essential spectrum of
\mathcal{J}L_{n}
is contained in
i\mathbb{R}
, the kernel is spanned by the function
\varphi_{x}
, and the generalized kernel is spanned by
\frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial c}
.

Proof.

The fact that the operators
\mathcal{J}L_{n}
and the adjoint recursion operator
\mathcal{R}^{\ast}(\varphi)
share the same eigenfunctions is inferred by the operator identity (
2.30
). By Lemma
2.3
, one can compute the spectrum of the operator
\mathcal{J}L_{n}
directly by employing the squared eigenfunctions as follows

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}L_{n}\big(F^{\pm}(x,k)\big)_{x}=\varrho_{n,c}(\pm 2k)\big(F^{\pm}(x,k)\big)_{x},\quad\text{for}\quad k\in\mathbb{R},

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}L_{n}\big(F_{1}^{\pm}(x)\big)_{x}\sim\mathcal{J}L_{n}\varphi_{x}=0,\qquad\mathcal{J}L_{n}\big(\dot{F}_{1}^{\pm}(x)\big)_{x}\sim\mathcal{J}L_{n}\frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial c}=c^{n-1}\varphi_{x}.

In view of (
2.38
), the essential spectrum of
\mathcal{J}L_{n}
is
\varrho_{n,c}(\pm 2k)
for
k\in\mathbb{R}
which is contained in the imaginary axis, which gives the desired result in Proposition
2.1
.
∎

For the adjoint operator of
\mathcal{J}L_{n}
, namely the operator
-L_{n}\mathcal{J}
, which commutes with the recursion operator
\mathcal{R}(\varphi)
(
2.29
), we have the following result.

Proposition 2.2
.

For
n\geq 1
, the operators
L_{n}\mathcal{J}
defined in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
with domain
H^{1}(\mathbb{R})
and the recursion operator
\mathcal{R}(\varphi)
share the same eigenfunctions. Moreover, the essential spectrum of
L_{n}\mathcal{J}
is contained in
i\mathbb{R}
, the kernel is spanned by the function
m_{\varphi}
and the generalized kernel is spanned by
\partial_{x}^{-1}\big(\frac{\partial m_{\varphi}}{\partial c}\big)
.

Proof.

From (
2.29
) we see immediately that
L_{n}\mathcal{J}
and
\mathcal{R}(\varphi)
share the same eigenfunctions. By Lemma
2.1
, one can compute the spectrum of the operator
L_{n}\mathcal{J}
directly by employing the squared eigenfunctions as follows

\displaystyle L_{n}\mathcal{J}(1-\partial_{x}^{2})F^{\pm}(x,k)=-L_{n}\big(F^{\pm}(x,k)\big)_{x}=\varrho_{n,c}(\pm 2k)(1-\partial_{x}^{2})F^{\pm}(x,k),

\displaystyle L_{n}\mathcal{J}(1-\partial_{x}^{2})F_{1}^{\pm}(x)\sim L_{n}\mathcal{J}m_{\varphi}=-L_{n}\varphi_{x}=0,

\displaystyle L_{n}\mathcal{J}(1-\partial_{x}^{2})\dot{F}_{1}^{\pm}(x)=-L_{n}\big(\dot{F}_{1}^{\pm}(x)\big)_{x}\sim L_{n}\frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial c}=-c^{n-1}m_{\varphi}.

In view of (
2.38
), the essential spectrum of
L_{n}\mathcal{J}
is
\varrho_{n,c}(\pm 2k)
for
k\in\mathbb{R}
, which is purely imaginary. On the other hand, it is inferred from the last equality above that
\big(\dot{F}_{1}^{\pm}(x)\big)_{x}\sim\frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial c}
. Hence the generalized kernel is

(1-\partial_{x}^{2})\dot{F}_{1}^{\pm}(x)\sim\partial_{x}^{-1}\left(\frac{\partial m_{\varphi}}{\partial c}\right),

which implies the desired result in the proposition.
∎

On account of Proposition
2.1
and Proposition
2.2
, we now have the two function sets as follows. The first set

\left\{\big(F^{\pm}(x,k)\big)_{x}\ \ \text{for }k\in\mathbb{R};\quad\varphi_{x};\quad\frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial c}\right\}

(2.39)

consists of linearly independent eigenfunctions and generalized kernel of the operator
\mathcal{J}L_{n}
. Moreover, they are essentially orthogonal under the
L^{2}
-inner product.
The second set

\left\{(1-\partial_{x}^{2})F^{\pm}(x,k)\ \ \text{for}\ k\in\mathbb{R};\quad m_{\varphi};\quad\partial_{x}^{-1}\left(\frac{\partial m_{\varphi}}{\partial c}\right)\right\}

(2.40)

consists of linearly independent eigenfunctions and generalized kernel of the operator
L_{n}\mathcal{J}
. Notice that from the expression of soliton profile (
1.7
) or (
1.8
), as the functions
\varphi,m_{\varphi}
are even and localized, one sees that
L_{n}\frac{\partial\varphi(-x)}{\partial c}=-m_{\varphi}
also holds, then the function
\frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial c}
is also localized and even. The nonzero inner product of the elements of the sets (
2.39
) and (
2.40
) are the following:

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}\big(F^{\pm}(x,k)\big)_{x}(1-\partial_{x}^{2})\overline{F^{\pm}(x,l)}\mbox{\rm d}x=\mp 2\pi ik|a(k)|^{2}\delta(k-l),\quad\text{for}\ k,l\in\mathbb{R},

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}\varphi_{x}\partial_{x}^{-1}\big(\frac{\partial m_{\varphi}}{\partial c}\big)\mbox{\rm d}x=-\int_{\mathbb{R}}\varphi\frac{\partial m_{\varphi}}{\partial c}\mbox{\rm d}x=-\frac{\mbox{\rm d}\mathcal{H}_{1}(\varphi)}{\mbox{\rm d}c}=-4\kappa c,

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial c}m_{\varphi}\mbox{\rm d}x=\frac{\mbox{\rm d}\mathcal{H}_{1}(\varphi)}{\mbox{\rm d}c}=4\kappa c.

The corresponding closure relation is

\mp\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{1}{2\pi ik|a(k)|^{2}}\big(F^{\pm}(x,k)\big)_{x}(1-\partial_{y}^{2})\overline{F^{\pm}(y,k)}\mbox{\rm d}k+\frac{1}{4\kappa c}\bigg(\varphi_{x}\partial_{y}^{-1}\big(\frac{\partial m_{\varphi}}{\partial c}\big)+\frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial c}m_{\varphi}(y)\bigg)=\delta(x-y),

(2.41)

which indicates that any function
z(x)
which vanishes as

can be expanded over the above two sets (
2.39
) and (
2.40
). By comparing (
2.41
) to (
2.17
), one can take the derivative of (
2.17
) with respect to

and insert

to have the following decomposition:

\displaystyle z(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(F^{\pm}(x,k)\right)_{x}P^{\pm}(k)\mbox{\rm d}k+\beta\varphi_{x}+\gamma\frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial c},

(2.42)

where the coefficients
P^{\pm}
,
\beta
and
\gamma
are related to the coefficients in (
2.17
). Similarly, one can also decompose the function
z(x)
on the second set (
2.40
) by applying the operator
1-\partial_{x}^{2}
to (
2.17
).

\mathcal{R}(U^{(N)})
and the adjoint
\mathcal{R}^{\ast}(U^{(N)})

\mathcal{J}\mathcal{L}_{N}
,
\mathcal{L}_{N}\mathcal{J}

One has, by similar arguments of Lemma

Lemma 2.4
.

The recursion operator
\mathcal{R}(U^{(N)})
, defined in

N
discrete eigenvalues
c_{j}
for

\displaystyle G_{j}(U^{(N)}):=\left(\mathcal{H}^{\prime}_{N}+(\mu_{N-1}+c_{j})\mathcal{H}^{\prime}_{N-1}+\cdots+\frac{\mu_{0}}{c_{j}}\mathcal{H}^{\prime}_{1}\right)(U^{(N)}).

(2.43)

The essential spectrum of
\mathcal{R}(U^{(N)})
is the interval
(0,2\omega]
, and the corresponding eigenfunctions do not decay and thus are not in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
.

Proof.

Consider the Jost solutions
f^{\pm}(x,k)
of the eigenvalue problem (
2.1
) with the potential
m(x)=m_{U^{(N)}}:=U^{(N)}-U^{(N)}_{xx}
and the asymptotic expressions in (
2.4
) and (
2.5
). In this case there are
N
eigenvalues
k=i\kappa_{j}
(
0<\kappa_{j}<\frac{1}{2},j=1,2,\cdot\cdot\cdot,N
) which generates the
N
-solitons profile
U^{(N)}
. It is then found that the squared eigenfunctions
F^{\pm}(x,k)=(f^{\pm}(x,k))^{2}

\bigg(-\partial_{x}^{2}+1+2\lambda\big(m_{U^{(N)}}(x)+2\omega+\partial_{x}^{-1}(m_{U^{(N)}}(x)\partial_{x})\big)\bigg)F^{\pm}(x,k)=0.

(2.44)

This in turn implies that

(1-\partial_{x}^{2})^{-1}\left(m_{U^{(N)}}+2\omega+\partial_{x}^{-1}(m_{U^{(N)}}(x)\partial_{x})\right)F^{\pm}(x,k)=\mathcal{K}[m_{U^{(N)}}]F^{\pm}(x,k)=-\frac{1}{2\lambda}F^{\pm}(x,k).

By (
2.44
) and (

), for
j=1,2,\cdot\cdot\cdot,N
, it follows that

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(U^{(N)})(1-\partial_{x}^{2})F^{\pm}(x,k)=-\frac{1}{2\lambda}(1-\partial_{x}^{2})F^{\pm}(x,k),\quad\text{for}\ k\in\mathbb{R},

(2.45)

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(U^{(N)})(1-\partial_{x}^{2})F_{j}^{\pm}(x)=-\frac{1}{2\lambda_{j}}(1-\partial_{x}^{2})F_{j}^{\pm}(x)=c_{j}(1-\partial_{x}^{2})F_{j}^{\pm}(x).

(2.46)

Moreover, in view of
\dot{F}_{j}^{\pm}(x)
in the completeness relation (
2.1
), it follows that

\mathcal{R}(U^{(N)})(1-\partial_{x}^{2})\dot{F}_{j}^{\pm}(x)=-\frac{1}{2\lambda_{j}}(1-\partial_{x}^{2})\dot{F}_{j}^{\pm}(x)-\frac{i\kappa_{j}}{\omega\lambda_{j}^{2}}(1-\partial_{x}^{2})F_{j}^{\pm}(x).

Since the Euler-Lagrange equation of profile

satisfies

0=I^{\prime}_{N}(U^{(N)})=\big(\mathcal{R}(U^{(N)})-c_{j}\big)G_{j}(U^{(N)}),\quad j=1,2,\cdot\cdot\cdot,N.

Therefore one has
\mathcal{R}(U^{(N)})G_{j}(U^{(N)})=c_{j}G_{j}(U^{(N)})
, and it is inferred from (
2.46
) that
F_{j}^{\pm}(x)\sim(1-\partial_{x}^{2})^{-1}F_{j}(U^{(N)})
.
On account of (
2.45
), the essential spectrum of
\mathcal{R}(U^{(N)})
is given by the set
\left\{\lambda:-\frac{1}{2\lambda}=\frac{\omega}{2k^{2}+\frac{1}{2}}\right\}
for
k\in\mathbb{R}
, which is equal to the interval
(0,2\omega]
.
The associated generalized eigenfunctions
(1-\partial_{x}^{2})F^{\pm}(x,k)
do not decay and are not in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
, which can be seen from the asymptotics of
f^{\pm}(x,k)
in (
2.4
) and (
2.5
).
From (
2.46
), the recursion operator has
N
discrete eigenvalues
c_{j}
with associated eigenfunctions
F_{j}(U^{(N)})
defined in (
2.43
).
The proof of the lemma is completed.
∎

Similarly, one has the following for the adjoint recursion operator
\mathcal{R}^{\ast}(U^{(N)})
around the
N
-solitons.

Lemma 2.5
.

The adjoint recursion operator
\mathcal{R}^{\ast}(U^{(N)})
, defined in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
with domain

N
discrete eigenvalues

, and the corresponding eigenfunctions do not decay and are not in

Proof.

Consider the Jost solutions

of the spectral problem (

) with the potential
m(x)=m_{U^{(N)}}:=U^{(N)}-U^{(N)}_{xx}
and the asymptotic expressions in (

N

(

) which generates the
N
-solitons proflie. It is then found (similar to (

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}^{\ast}(U^{(N)})\big(F^{\pm}(x,k)\big)_{x}=-\frac{1}{2\lambda}\big(F^{\pm}(x,k)\big)_{x},k\in\mathbb{R},

(2.47)

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}^{\ast}(U^{(N)})\big(F_{j}^{\pm}(x)\big)_{x}\sim\mathcal{R}^{\ast}(U^{(N)})\mathcal{J}G_{j}(U^{(N)})=-\frac{1}{2\lambda_{j}}\mathcal{J}G_{j}(U^{(N)})

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}^{\ast}(U^{(N)})U^{(N)}_{x_{j}}=c_{j}U^{(N)}_{x_{j}}.

(2.48)

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}^{\ast}(U^{(N)})\big(\dot{F}_{j}^{\pm}(x)\big)_{x}=-\frac{1}{2\lambda_{j}}\big(\dot{F}_{j}^{\pm}(x)\big)_{x}-\frac{i\kappa_{j}}{\omega\lambda_{j}^{2}}\big(F_{j}^{\pm}(x)\big)_{x}.

Since from (

), the adjoint recursion operator has
N
discrete eigenvalues

with associated eigenfunctions

. On account of (

), the essential spectrum of
\mathcal{R}^{\ast}(U^{(N)})
is the interval

.
The associated generalized eigenfunctions

do not decay and are not in

, which can be seen from the asymptotics of

in (

) and (

). This completes the proof of Lemma

.

Recall that
\mathcal{J}=-\partial_{x}(1-\partial_{x}^{2})^{-1}

\mathcal{L}_{N}=I^{\prime\prime}_{N}(U^{(N)})
is the second variation operator in (

\displaystyle\varrho_{N}(2k)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\frac{-2ik}{1+4k^{2}}\sum_{n=1}^{N}(-1)^{n-1}\sigma_{j,N-n}\frac{\big(2\omega\big)^{n-1}(4c_{j}k^{2}+c_{j}-2\omega)}{(1+4k^{2})^{n-1}},

By employing the operator identity (

Proposition 2.3
.

The operators
\mathcal{J}\mathcal{L}_{N}
, defined in

, share the same eigenfunctions. Moreover, the essential spectrum of

. The kernel is

for

\frac{\partial U^{(N)}}{\partial c_{j}}

Proof.

From (
2.33
), we know that the operators
\mathcal{J}\mathcal{L}_{N}
and
\mathcal{R}^{\ast}(U^{(N)})
share the same eigenfunctions. Similar to the argument of Lemma

) with the potential
m(x)=m_{U^{(N)}}:=U^{(N)}-U^{(N)}_{xx}

N

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}\mathcal{L}_{N}\big(F^{\pm}(x,k)\big)_{x}=\varrho_{N}(2k)\big(F^{\pm}(x,k)\big)_{x},\quad\text{for}\ k\in\mathbb{R},

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}\mathcal{L}_{N}\mathcal{J}G_{j}(U^{(N)})\sim\mathcal{J}\mathcal{L}_{N}U^{(N)}_{x_{j}}=0,\qquad\mathcal{J}\mathcal{L}_{N}\frac{\partial U^{(N)}}{\partial c_{j}}=-\mathcal{J}G_{j}(U^{(N)}).

Concerning the adjoint operator of
\mathcal{J}\mathcal{L}_{N}

\mathcal{R}(U^{(N)})

Proposition 2.4
.

The operators
\mathcal{L}_{N}\mathcal{J}
, defined in

with domain

, and the recursion operator
\mathcal{R}(U^{(N)})
, share the same eigenfunctions. Moreover, the essential spectrum of
\mathcal{L}_{N}\mathcal{J}
is contained in

, the kernel is
N
-fold and spanned by

, and the generalized kernel is spanned by
\mathcal{J}^{-1}\frac{\partial U^{(N)}}{\partial c_{j}}

The fact that the operators
\mathcal{L}_{N}\mathcal{J}
and the adjoint recursion operator
\mathcal{R}(U^{(N)})
share the same eigenfunctions is due to the operator identity (
2.32
). By Lemma
2.4
, one can compute the spectrum of the operator
\mathcal{L}_{N}\mathcal{J}
directly by employing the squared eigenfunctions:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{N}(1-\partial_{x}^{2})F^{\pm}(x,k)=\varrho_{N}(2\lambda)(1-\partial_{x}^{2})F^{\pm}(x,k),\quad\text{for}\ k\in\mathbb{R},

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{N}\mathcal{J}G_{j}(U^{(N)})=0,\quad\mathcal{L}_{N}\mathcal{J}\mathcal{J}^{-1}\frac{\partial U^{(N)}}{\partial c_{j}}=-G_{j}(U^{(N)}).

Recalling (

), the essential spectrum of
\mathcal{L}_{N}\mathcal{J}
is
\varrho_{N}(2\lambda)
for

, which is contained in the imaginary axis. The proof of Proposition

∎

On account of Proposition
2.3
and Proposition
2.4
, for
j=1,2,\cdot\cdot\cdot,N
, we now have the two function sets as follows. The first set

\left\{\big(F^{\pm}(x,k)\big)_{x},\quad k\in\mathbb{R};\quad\mathcal{J}G_{j}(U^{(N)})\sim U^{(N)}_{x_{j}};\quad\frac{\partial U^{(N)}}{\partial c_{j}}\right\}

(2.50)

consists of linearly independent eigenfunctions and generalized kernel of the operator
\mathcal{J}\mathcal{L}_{N}
. They are not orthogonal under the

-inner product for

, however, they possess some orthogonality property with respect to the following symplectic form:

\aleph\big(f,g\big):=\langle f,\mathcal{J}^{-1}g\rangle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(x)\mathcal{J}^{-1}g(x)\mbox{\rm d}x.

Indeed, since from IST and the formula of
N
-solitons, one has

\sum_{j=1}^{N}U^{(N)}_{x_{j}}=-\partial_{x}U^{(N)},\qquad\mathcal{J}\mathcal{H}^{\prime}_{n}(U^{(N)})=-\sum_{j=1}^{N}c^{n-1}_{j}U^{(N)}_{x_{j}}.

For
j\neq k
, we compute

\displaystyle 0=\partial_{x_{k}}E(U^{(N)})

\displaystyle=\langle E^{\prime}(U^{(N)}),U^{(N)}_{x_{k}}\rangle=\langle(1-\partial_{x}^{2})U^{(N)},U^{(N)}_{x_{k}}\rangle,

\displaystyle\aleph\left(U^{(N)}_{x_{j}},U^{(N)}_{x_{k}}\right)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}U^{(N)}_{x_{j}}(x)\mathcal{J}^{-1}U^{(N)}_{x_{k}}(x)\mbox{\rm d}x\sim\int_{\mathbb{R}}U^{(N)}_{x_{j}}(x)G_{j}(U^{(N)}(x))\mbox{\rm d}x=0,

\displaystyle\aleph\left(U^{(N)}_{x_{j}},\frac{\partial U^{(N)}}{\partial c_{j}}\right)

\displaystyle=4\kappa_{j}c_{j},\qquad\aleph\left(U^{(N)}_{x_{j}},\frac{\partial U^{(N)}}{\partial c_{k}}\right)=0,

\displaystyle\aleph\left(\frac{\partial U^{(N)}}{\partial c_{j}},\frac{\partial U^{(N)}}{\partial c_{k}}\right)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{\partial U^{(N)}}{\partial c_{j}}(x)\mathcal{J}^{-1}\frac{\partial U^{(N)}}{\partial c_{k}}(x)\mbox{\rm d}x=0.

The second set

\left\{(1-\partial_{x}^{2})F^{\pm}(x,k),\quad k\in\mathbb{R};\quad G_{j}(U^{(N)});\quad\mathcal{J}^{-1}\frac{\partial U^{(N)}}{\partial c_{j}}\right\}

consists of linearly independent eigenfunctions and generalized kernel of the operator
\mathcal{L}_{N}\mathcal{J}
. Similar to the case

which vanishes as

2.50
) and (

\displaystyle z(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}(F^{\pm}(x,k)\big)_{x}P^{\pm}(k)\mbox{\rm d}k+\sum_{j=1}^{N}\beta_{j}\mathcal{J}F_{j}(U^{(N)})+\sum_{j=1}^{N}\gamma_{j}\frac{\partial U^{(N)}}{\partial c_{j}}

with the complex-valued coefficients

,

and

that can be computed explicitly. Similarly, one can also decompose the function

on the second set (

to (
2.17

Remark 2.3
.

It reveals from Proposition
2.3
that the spectrum of
\mathcal{J}\mathcal{L}_{N}
lies on the imagery axis, which implies that the CH
N
-solitons are spectrally stable in
H^{N}(\mathbb{R})
. Therefore,
k_{r}=k^{-}_{i}=k_{c}=0
, where
k_{r}
refers to the number of real positive eigenvalues of
\mathcal{J}\mathcal{L}_{N}
,
k_{c}
is the number of eigenvalues
in the open first quadrant of the complex plane and
k^{-}_{i}
is the number of purely imaginary
eigenvalues in the upper half-plane with negative Krein signature. Therefore, one has

k_{r}+2k_{i}^{-}+2k_{c}=0.

On the other hand, the Hamilton-Krein index theorem (see for example
[
40
]
) reveals that

n(\mathcal{L}_{N})-p(D)=k_{r}+2k_{i}^{-}+2k_{c}=0,

which verifies the stability criterion of
[
55
]
, and hence concludes the Lyapunov stability of CH
N
-solitons.

2.5.
Monotonicity formula and exponential decay of
H^{1}
-localized solutions

The aim of this subsection is to prove certain monotonicity formula related to the conservation laws
E=\mathcal{H}_{1}
and
F=\mathcal{H}_{2}
, from which we will establish the exponential decay of
H^{1}
-localized solutions (see Definition
1
) in the spirit of
[
27
]
.

For a non-negative even function
\phi\in C_{c}^{\infty}([-1,1])
, such that
\int_{\mathbb{R}}\phi=\frac{1}{2}
, we set

g(x):=\phi*e^{-|x|}\quad\text{and}\quad\Psi_{K}(x):=\frac{1}{K}\int_{-\infty}^{x}g\left(\frac{y}{K}\right)dy.

It is easy to check that
\Psi_{K}\geq 0
is increasing,
\Psi_{K}\rightarrow 1
as
x\rightarrow+\infty
and

\left|\Psi_{K}\right|+\left|\Psi_{K}^{\prime}\right|+\left|\Psi_{K}^{\prime\prime}\right|+\left|\Psi_{K}^{\prime\prime\prime}\right|\leq C_{K}\mathrm{e}^{\frac{x}{K}}\ \ \text{for}\ \ x\leq 0,\qquad\Psi_{K}^{\prime\prime\prime}=\frac{1}{K^{2}}(\Psi_{K}^{\prime}-2\phi)\leq\frac{1}{K^{2}}\Psi_{K}^{\prime}.

Choosing
0<\alpha<1
, such that
(1-\alpha)^{2}c_{1}>2\omega
, let

E_{x_{0},t_{0}}(t):=\int_{\mathbb{R}}(u^{2}+u_{x}^{2})\Psi_{K}\big(x-x(t)-x_{0}-\alpha(x(t_{0})-x(t))\big)\mbox{\rm d}x.

Note that
E_{x_{0},t_{0}}(t)
is close to
\|u(t)\|_{H^{1}\big(x>(1-\alpha)x(t)+X_{0}\big)}^{2}
, where
X_{0}:=x_{0}+\alpha x(t_{0})
. Moreover, since
\phi
is even, there holds the following, for
t_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
,

E_{x_{0},t_{0}}(t_{0})\geq\frac{1}{2}\|u(t,x+x(t_{0}))\|_{H^{1}(x_{0},+\infty)}^{2}.

Proposition 2.7

(
[
27
]
)
.

Let
u\in Y(\mathbb{R})
be an
H^{1}
-localized solution of the CH equation with
c_{1}>2\omega
. Then there exists
C>0
depending on
\varepsilon\mapsto R_{\varepsilon}
,
\left\|u_{0}\right\|_{H^{1}},\omega>0
and
K>\sqrt{c_{1}/\left(c_{1}-2\omega\right)}
, such that for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
and
x\in\mathbb{R}
,

|u(t,x+x(t))|+|u_{x}(t,x+x(t))|\leq C\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{|x|}{K}}.

Proposition
2.7
mainly follows from the almost monotonicity of the energy
E(u)
at the right of an
H^{1}
-localized solutions.

Lemma 2.6

(
[
27
]
)
.

Let
u\in Y(\mathbb{R})
be an
H^{1}
-localized solution of the CH equation with
c_{1}>2\omega
. Then there exist
C>0
and
B>0
depending only on
\varepsilon\mapsto R_{\varepsilon},\|u_{0}\|_{H^{1}},\omega\geq 0
and
0<\alpha<1
, such that for all
t\leq t_{0},\ x_{0}>B
and

K>\sqrt{\frac{(1-\alpha)^{2}c_{1}}{(1-\alpha)^{2}c_{1}-2\omega}},

there holds

E_{x_{0},t_{0}}\left(t_{0}\right)-E_{x_{0},t_{0}}(t)\leq C\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{x_{0}}{K}}.

For
K>0
we consider the function
\Psi_{K}
as above. For
\alpha>0,x_{0}>0
and
(t_{0},t)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
, we define

F_{x_{0},t_{0}}(t):=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(u^{3}(t,x)+uu_{x}^{2}(t,x)+2\omega u^{2}(t,x)\right)\Psi_{K}\big(x-x(t)+x_{0}-\alpha(x(t_{0})-x(t))\big)\mbox{\rm d}x.

The monotonicity result relative to
F_{x_{0},t_{0}}(t)
says that if
u(0)
is sufficiently close to
\varphi_{c}
in
H^{1}(\mathbb{R})
, then
F_{x_{0},t_{0}}(t)
is almost decreasing with respect to time.

Proposition 2.8
.

Let
c>2\omega
. There exist
a_{1}>0,B>0,C>0,\alpha>0
and
K>0
such that if
|u_{0}-\varphi_{c}|_{H^{1}}<a_{1}
, then for all
x_{0}>2B
,
t_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
and
t\geq t_{0}
,

F_{x_{0},t_{0}}(t)\leq F_{x_{0},t_{0}}(t_{0})+Ce^{-\frac{x_{0}}{K}}.

Proof.

The proof follows along the same lines as the proof of the
H^{1}
monotonicity. We set
y(t):=x(t)-x_{0}+\alpha\left(x\left(t_{0}\right)-x(t)\right)
. Recall that
t\geqslant t_{0}
and
x_{0}>2B>0
. Differentiating
F_{x_{0},t_{0}}(t)
with respect to
t
, we obtain

\displaystyle F_{x_{0},t_{0}}^{\prime}(t)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1-\alpha}{2}x^{\prime}(t)\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(u^{3}+uu_{x}^{2}+2\omega u^{2}\right)\Psi_{K}^{\prime}(x-y(t))\mbox{\rm d}x

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(4\omega u+3u^{2}-u_{x}^{2}-2uu_{xx}\right)u_{t}\Psi_{k}(x-y(t))\mbox{\rm d}x-\int_{\mathbb{R}}uu_{x}u_{t}\Psi^{\prime}_{k}(x-y(t))\mbox{\rm d}x

\displaystyle=F_{1}+F_{2}+F_{3}.

We choose
a_{1}
sufficiently small such that
2\omega<c_{2}=\frac{2\omega+c_{1}}{2}<x^{\prime}(t)<c_{3}=\frac{3c_{1}-2\omega}{2}
. We then take
\alpha>0
such that
(1-\alpha)x^{\prime}(t)>c_{4}=\frac{2\omega+c_{1}}{2}
, note that
2\omega<c_{4}=c_{2}<c_{1}<c_{3}
. Since from (
1.5
),

u_{t}=-\partial_{x}(1-\partial_{x}^{2})^{-1}F^{\prime}(u)
. If we denote by

(1-\partial_{x}^{2})h:=4\omega u+3u^{2}-u_{x}^{2}-2uu_{xx},

then
h=u^{2}+(1-\partial_{x}^{2})^{-1}(4\omega u+2u^{2}+u_{x}^{2})
and
u_{t}=-\frac{1}{2}h^{\prime}
.
Let us compute

\displaystyle F_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(4\omega u+3u^{2}-u_{x}^{2}-2uu_{xx}\right)u_{t}\Psi_{k}(x-y(t))\mbox{\rm d}x

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{4}\int_{\mathbb{R}}h^{\prime}(h-h_{xx})\Psi_{k}(x-y(t))\mbox{\rm d}x=\frac{1}{8}\int_{\mathbb{R}}(h^{2}_{x}-h^{2})\Psi^{\prime}_{k}(x-y(t))\mbox{\rm d}x

\displaystyle\leq-\frac{1}{8}\int_{\mathbb{R}}h^{2}\Psi^{\prime}_{k}(x-y(t))\mbox{\rm d}x\leq C\|u\|^{2}_{H^{1}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}(u^{2}+u_{x}^{2})\Psi^{\prime}_{k}(x-y(t))\mbox{\rm d}x.

\displaystyle F_{3}

\displaystyle=-\int_{\mathbb{R}}uu_{x}u_{t}\Psi^{\prime}_{k}(x-y(t))dx=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}uu_{x}h^{\prime}\Psi^{\prime}_{k}(x-y(t))\mbox{\rm d}x

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}uu_{x}\big(2uu_{x}+(1-\partial_{x}^{2})^{-1}(4\omega u+2u^{2}+u_{x}^{2})_{x}\big)\Psi^{\prime}_{k}(x-y(t))\mbox{\rm d}x

\displaystyle\leq C\|u\|^{2}_{H^{1}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}(u^{2}+u_{x}^{2})\Psi^{\prime}_{k}(x-y(t))\mbox{\rm d}x.

Therefore, from Lemma
2.6
and
\Psi^{\prime}_{k}(x-y(t))\leq e^{\frac{B-x_{0}+\alpha c_{2}(t_{0}-t)}{K}}
for

,

\displaystyle F_{x_{0},t_{0}}^{\prime}(t)\leq C\|u\|^{2}_{H^{1}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}(u^{2}+u_{x}^{2})\Psi^{\prime}_{k}(x-y(t))\mbox{\rm d}x\leq Ce^{\frac{B-x_{0}+\alpha c_{2}(t_{0}-t)}{K}}.

The proof of the proposition is completed by integrating the above from
t_{0}
to
t
.
∎

We now introduce two quantities,
E_{R}
and
E_{L}
, related to the conservation law
E(u)
, where the subscript
R
and
L
denote the right and left, respectively.

E_{R}(t):=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}(u^{2}(t,x)+u_{x}^{2}(t,x))\Psi_{K}(x-x(t)+x_{0})\mbox{\rm d}x,

and

E_{L}(t):=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}(u^{2}(t,x)+u_{x}^{2}(t,x))(1-\Psi_{K}(x-x(t)+x_{0}))\mbox{\rm d}x.

These quantities are close to
\frac{1}{2}\|u(t)\|^{2}_{H^{1}(x>x(t)+x_{0})}
and
\frac{1}{2}\|u(t)\|^{2}_{H^{1}(x<x(t)-x_{0})}
, respectively. The following corollary states that
E_{R}
is almost
non-increasing and

Proof.

Let

for

Remark 2.4

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}\Theta\left[\left(c-\varphi_{c}\right)v_{x}^{2}+\left(-3\varphi_{c}+\varphi_{c}^{{}^{\prime\prime}}+(c-2\omega)\right)v^{2}\right]\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle+\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{c-\varphi_{c}}{4}\frac{\left(\Theta^{{}^{\prime}}\right)^{2}}{\Theta}v^{2}+\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(c-\varphi_{c}\right)\Theta^{\prime}vv_{x}\mathrm{~d}x

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}\Theta\left[\left(c-\varphi_{c}\right)v_{x}^{2}+\left(-3\varphi_{c}+\varphi_{c}^{\prime\prime}+(c-2\omega)\right)v^{2}\right]+\Theta^{\prime}\varphi_{c}^{\prime}v^{2}\mathrm{~d}x

\displaystyle+\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left[\left(c-\varphi_{c}\right)\left(\frac{\left(\Theta^{\prime}\right)^{2}}{4\Theta}-\frac{1}{2}\Theta^{\prime\prime}\right)-\Theta^{\prime}\varphi_{c}^{\prime}\right]v^{2}\mathrm{~d}x.

On the other hand, on account of the results on the spectrum of

derived in

and

then

Direct calculations imply that

\left\|\sqrt{\Theta}v\right\|_{H^{1}}^{2}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\Theta\left(v^{2}+v_{x}^{2}\right)\mathrm{d}x+\frac{1}{4}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{\left(\Theta^{\prime}\right)^{2}}{\Theta}v^{2}\mathrm{~d}x-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\Theta^{\prime\prime}v^{2}\mathrm{~d}x.

Recall that according to

takes values in

4.2.
Proof of the linear Liouville theorem

This is the last step in the proof
of Theorem
1.1
. We show that the function
v
constructed in Proposition
4.1
satisfies

v\equiv 0
. This leads to a contradiction and achieves the proof of the Liouville Theorem. In fact, we will give the classification of solutions for more general linear problem related to (
2.1
).

Let us recall the following higher order linearized operators around one soliton profile
\varphi_{c}
,

L_{n}=-H^{\prime\prime}_{n+1}(\varphi_{c})+cH^{\prime\prime}_{n}(\varphi_{c}).

The idea of classifying the solutions for more general linear problem is by employing the
L^{2}
-completeness of the squared eigenfunctions (
2.41
), a suitable change of function and the semigroup estimate initialed in the work of Pego–Weinstein
[
53
]
(see also
[
51
]
) which is dealing with the gKdV equation.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
v\in\mathcal{C}(\mathbb{R},H^{1}(\mathbb{R}))\cap L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R},H^{1}(\mathbb{R}))
be a solution of

v_{t}+\mathcal{J}L_{n}v=A(t)\varphi_{c}^{\prime}+B(t)\frac{\partial\varphi_{c}}{\partial c},

(4.10)

where
A(t)
and
B(t)
are two continuous and bounded functions. Assume that for constant
C>0
,
v(t,x)
satisfies

\forall t\in\mathbb{R},\ R_{\varepsilon}\gg 1,\quad\int_{|x|>R_{\varepsilon}}\big(v^{2}(t,x)+v_{x}^{2}(t,x)\big)dx\leq\varepsilon.

Then for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
,

\displaystyle v(t)=\beta(t)\varphi^{\prime}_{c}+\gamma(t)\frac{\partial\varphi_{c}}{\partial c}.

for some
\mathcal{C}^{1}
bounded functions
\beta(t)
and
\gamma(t)
satisfying

\beta^{\prime}(t)=A(t)-c^{n-1}\gamma(t),\quad\gamma^{\prime}(t)=B(t).

If we further impose

\forall t\in\mathbb{R},\quad\int_{\mathbb{R}}v(t,x)(1-\partial_{x}^{2})\varphi_{c}(x)\mbox{\rm d}x=\int_{\mathbb{R}}v(t,x)(1-\partial_{x}^{2})\varphi^{\prime}_{c}(x)\mbox{\rm d}x=0,

then for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
,
v(t)\equiv 0
.

Remark 4.1
.

Theorem
4.1
states that solutions of (
4.10
) satisfy the linear Liouville property. We refer to
[
42
]
for the counterpart results of gKdV and BBM equations.

Let us first deal with the case
n=1
. The spectral analysis of
\mathcal{J}L_{1}
has been verified in Proposition
2.1
. In particular, the eigenfunctions of which are eigenfunctions of adjoint recursion operator
\mathcal{R}^{\ast}(\varphi_{c})
. The operator
\mathcal{J}L_{1}
is degenerate, with an eigenvalue at
\lambda=0
with (generalized) eigenfunctions
\varphi^{\prime}_{c}
and
\partial_{c}\varphi_{c}
, which satisfy

\mathcal{J}L_{1}\varphi^{\prime}_{c}=0,\qquad\mathcal{J}L_{1}\partial_{c}\varphi_{c}=\varphi^{\prime}_{c}.

The above two zero modes are related, respectively, to infinitesimal changes in the location and speed of the soliton. They give rise to solutions
\varphi^{\prime}_{c}
and
\partial_{c}\varphi_{c}-t\varphi^{\prime}_{c}
to the linearized problem

v_{t}=\mathcal{J}L_{1}v,

which are, respectively, constant and linearly growing with time.

Following the idea of Pego–Weinstein
[
53
]
, we define
\mathcal{L}_{a}:=e^{ax}\mathcal{J}L_{1}e^{-ax}
, where
a
is well chosen such that the exponential weight shifts the essential spectrum of
\mathcal{L}_{a}
into the open left half plane. We want to show some semigroup decay estimates of
e^{t\mathcal{L}_{a}}
.
We first consider the essential spectrum of
\mathcal{L}_{a}
.
The essential spectrum of
\mathcal{L}_{a}
is

\sigma_{ess}(\mathcal{L}_{a}):=\left\{z\in\mathbb{C}:z=c(ik+a)-2\omega\frac{ik+a}{1-(ik+a)^{2}}\right\},\ k\in\mathbb{R}.

The graph of
\sigma_{ess}(\mathcal{L}_{a})
has a vertical asymptote
\Re z=ac
when
|k|\rightarrow+\infty
. The maximal of
\Re z
is

\Lambda:=ac-\frac{2a\omega}{1-a^{2}}<0,

if

a_{1}:=-\sqrt{1-\frac{2\omega}{c}}<a_{\ast}:=-\left(\frac{c+\omega-\sqrt{\omega(4c+\omega)}}{c}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}<a<0.

Next, we study the discrete spectrum of
\mathcal{L}_{a}
. From Proposition
2.1
, we see that the eigenvalue is
\lambda=0
with (generalized) eigenfunctions
e^{ax}\varphi^{\prime}_{c}
and
e^{ax}\partial_{c}\varphi_{c}
, which satisfy

\mathcal{L}_{a}(e^{ax}\varphi^{\prime}_{c})=0,\qquad\mathcal{L}_{a}(e^{ax}\partial_{c}\varphi_{c})=e^{ax}\varphi^{\prime}_{c}.

Similarly, the generalized eigenspaces of adjoint operator
\mathcal{L}^{\ast}_{a}:=-e^{-ax}L_{1}\mathcal{J}e^{ax}
are then

\operatorname{gKer}(\mathcal{L}^{\ast}_{a})=\operatorname{Span}\left\{e^{-ax}m_{\varphi},\ e^{-ax}\partial_{x}^{-1}\left(\frac{\partial m_{\varphi}}{\partial c}\right)\right\}.

Recall that for an operator
A
defined in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
,

\operatorname{gKer}(A):=\bigcup\limits_{k=1}^{\infty}\operatorname{Ker}(A^{k}).

Now we have the following spectral projection for the zero eigenvalue.

Proposition 4.2
.

Assume that
a_{1}<a<0
. Then
\lambda=0
is an eigenvalue for the operators
\mathcal{L}_{a}
and
\mathcal{L}^{\ast}_{a}
with algebraic multiplicity two. Moreover,

\displaystyle\operatorname{gKer}(\mathcal{L}_{a})=\operatorname{Ker}(\mathcal{L}^{2}_{a})=\operatorname{Span}\{f_{1},f_{2}\},

\displaystyle\operatorname{gKer}(\mathcal{L}^{\ast}_{a})=\operatorname{Ker}(\mathcal{L}^{\ast 2}_{a})=\operatorname{Span}\{g_{1},g_{2}\},

where

\displaystyle f_{1}:=e^{ax}\varphi^{\prime}_{c},\ f_{2}:=e^{ax}\partial_{c}\varphi_{c},

\displaystyle g_{1}:=e^{-ax}\tilde{g}_{1}=C_{1}e^{-ax}\partial_{x}^{-1}\left(\frac{\partial m_{\varphi_{c}}}{\partial c}\right)+C_{2}e^{-ax}m_{\varphi_{c}},\quad g_{2}:=e^{-ax}\tilde{g}_{2}=C_{3}e^{-ax}m_{\varphi_{c}},

\displaystyle C_{1}=-C_{3}=-\bigg(\frac{d}{dc}H_{1}(\varphi_{c})\bigg)^{-1},\quad C_{2}=\frac{1}{2}\bigg(\frac{d}{dc}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\varphi_{c}dx\bigg)^{2}\bigg(\frac{d}{dc}H_{1}(\varphi_{c})\bigg)^{-2}.

In addition, for
j=1,2
, the functions
f_{j}
and
g_{j}
are bi-orthogonal, with
\langle f_{j},g_{k}\rangle=\delta_{jk}
for
j,k=1,2
. The spectral projection
\mathcal{P}
for the zero eigenvalue of
\mathcal{L}_{a}
and the complementary spectral projection
\mathcal{Q}
are given by

\mathcal{P}w:=\sum\limits_{k=1}^{2}\langle w,g_{k}\rangle f_{k},\ \quad\mathcal{Q}w:=(I-\mathcal{P})w=w-\sum\limits_{k=1}^{2}\langle w,g_{k}\rangle f_{k},\quad\text{for }\ w\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}).

These projections satisfy
\mathcal{P}\mathcal{L}_{a}w=\mathcal{L}_{a}\mathcal{P}w
and
\mathcal{Q}\mathcal{L}_{a}w=\mathcal{L}_{a}\mathcal{Q}w
for
w\in\operatorname{dom}(\mathcal{L}_{a})
.

Proposition 4.3
.

Assume that
a_{1}<a<0
. Let
\mathcal{Q}
denote the projection
onto
\operatorname{gKer}(\mathcal{L}^{\ast}_{a})^{\perp}
. Then the initial value problem

\displaystyle\begin{cases}w_{t}=\mathcal{L}_{a}w,\\
w(0)=w_{0}\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R})\cap\operatorname{Range}\mathcal{Q},\end{cases}

has a unique solution
e^{t\mathcal{L}_{a}}w_{0}\in\mathcal{C}(\mathbb{R}_{+};H^{1}(\mathbb{R}))
, satisfying

\|e^{t\mathcal{L}_{a}}w_{0}\|_{H^{1}}\leq Ce^{-bt}\|w_{0}\|_{H^{1}},

for some constant
C>0
and
0<b<b_{max}
, with

-b_{max}:=\inf\left\{-b:\ \lambda=0\ \text{is the only eigenvalue of}\ \mathcal{L}_{a}\ \text{with}\ \Re\lambda\geq-b>\Lambda\right\}.

In order to show Proposition
4.3
, similar to
[
49
]
, we employ an abstract theorem of Prüss
[
54
]
which is stated as follows.

Proposition 4.4
.

Let
B
be the infinitesimal generator of a
\mathcal{C}_{0}
semigroup on the
Hilbert space
Z
. Let
b>0
. If there exists
M>0
such that

\|(\lambda I-B)^{-1}\|_{Z\rightarrow Z}\leq M,

(4.11)

for all
\lambda
with
\Re\lambda>-b
, then

\|e^{Bt}\|_{Z\rightarrow Z}\leq e^{-bt}.

In view of Prüss’s result, we only need to prove the boundedness of the resolvent in (
4.11
).

Proof of Proposition
4.3
.

Step 1.
We show that
\mathcal{L}_{a}
generates a
\mathcal{C}_{0}
semigroup in
H^{1}(\mathbb{R})
. A straight Fourier transformation upon the constant coefficient operator
\mathcal{L}^{\infty}_{a}
shows that
\mathcal{L}^{\infty}_{a}
generates a
\mathcal{C}_{0}
semigroup in
H^{1}(\mathbb{R})
.
Then

\mathcal{L}_{a}-\mathcal{L}^{\infty}_{a}=e^{ax}\mathcal{J}(\varphi_{c}\partial_{x}^{2}+\varphi_{c}^{\prime}\partial_{x}+\varphi_{c}^{\prime\prime}-3\varphi_{c})e^{-ax},

is bounded in
H^{1}(\mathbb{R})
, therefore,
\mathcal{L}_{a}
generates a
\mathcal{C}_{0}
semigroup in
H^{1}(\mathbb{R})
.

Step 2.
We will show (
4.11
) with
B=\mathcal{L}_{a}\mathcal{Q}
and
Z=H^{1}(\mathbb{R})
. Notice that

(\lambda I-\mathcal{L}_{a})^{-1}\mathcal{Q}=(I-C(\lambda))^{-1}(\lambda I-\mathcal{L}^{\infty}_{a})^{-1}\mathcal{Q},

where

C(\lambda):=\bigg(D_{a}(-cD_{a}^{2}+c-2\omega)+\lambda(1-D_{a}^{2})\bigg)^{-1}D_{a}(-D_{a}\varphi_{c}D_{a}+3\varphi_{c}-\varphi_{c}^{\prime\prime}),\quad D_{a}:=\partial_{x}-a.

Firstly, the operator norm of
(\lambda I-\mathcal{L}^{\infty}_{a})^{-1}\mathcal{Q}
can be controlled since for any
-b>\Lambda
, there exists
M_{1}>0
such that
\|(\lambda I-\mathcal{L}^{\infty}_{a})^{-1}\mathcal{Q}\|_{H^{1}\rightarrow H^{1}}\leq M_{1}
for any
\Re\lambda>-b
.

We claim that there exist
R_{1}>0
and
M_{2}>0
such that
\|(I-C(\lambda))^{-1}\|_{H^{1}\rightarrow H^{1}}\leq M_{2}
for
\Re\lambda>-b
and
|\lambda|>R_{1}
. Indeed, it suffices to show that the operator
C(\lambda):L^{2}\rightarrow L^{2}
is compact for
\lambda\notin\sigma_{ess}(\mathcal{L}_{a})
and the operator norm
\|C(\lambda)\|_{L^{2}\rightarrow L^{2}}
is strictly less than
1
. To show
C(\lambda)
is compact, it suffices to show that its adjoint
C^{\ast}(\lambda):L^{2}\rightarrow L^{2}
is compact. For this, observe that

C^{\ast}(\lambda)=A(x)B(i\partial_{x},\lambda),

where
A(x):=-D_{a}\varphi_{c}D_{a}+3\varphi_{c}-\varphi_{c}^{\prime\prime}
and

B(k,\lambda):=\frac{ik-a}{(ik-a)\left(-c(ik-a)^{2}+c-2\omega\bigg)+\lambda\bigg(1-(ik-a)^{2}\right)}.

Then
C^{\ast}(\lambda)
is an integral operator with kernel
A(y)\hat{B}(x-y,\lambda)
. Since
A(x)
decays exponentially as
y\rightarrow\pm\infty
and
B(\cdot,\lambda)\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
for
\lambda\notin\sigma_{ess}(\mathcal{L}_{a})
, which reveals that

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}|A(y)\hat{B}(x-y,\lambda)|^{2}dxdy<+\infty.

Therefore,

is a Hilbert-Schmidt operator whenever
\lambda\notin\sigma_{ess}(\mathcal{L}_{a})
.

Now we show that there exists
\delta\in(0,1)
and
M>0
such that
\|C(\lambda)\|_{L^{2}\rightarrow L^{2}}<1-\delta
for
\Im\lambda>cM\gamma^{3}
or
\Re\lambda>cM\gamma^{3}
with
\gamma:=\sqrt{\frac{c-2\omega}{c}}
, which can be checked directly within the Fourier transform upon

.
Then we see there exists
M_{2}>0
and
R_{1}>0
such that
\|(I-C(\lambda))^{-1}\|_{H^{1}\rightarrow H^{1}}<M_{2}
for
\Re\lambda>-b_{max}
and
|\lambda|>R_{1}
,
which reveals that
\|(\lambda I-\mathcal{L}_{a})^{-1}\mathcal{Q}\|_{H^{1}\rightarrow H^{1}}=\|(I-C(\lambda))^{-1}(\lambda I-\mathcal{L}^{\infty}_{a})^{-1}\mathcal{Q}\|_{H^{1}\rightarrow H^{1}}\leq M^{\prime}_{2}
is uniformly bounded for
\Re\lambda>-b_{max}
and
|\lambda|>R_{1}
.

Lastly, noticing that the singularities of

with
\Re\lambda\geq\Lambda
are isolated and must be the eigenvalues of
\mathcal{L}_{a}
. Now we know

is only eigenvalue of
\mathcal{L}_{a}
with a nonnegative real part. Therefore,
-b_{max}<0
. Moreover, for any

and

and

Proof of Theorem
4.1
.

The Cauchy problem of (
4.10
) for

is globally well-posed in

. For general

, (
4.10
) is also well-posed in

, since from Lemma

, one has for all

\mathcal{J}L_{n}=\mathcal{J}\mathcal{R}(\varphi_{c})L_{n-1}=\mathcal{R}^{\ast}(\varphi_{c})\mathcal{J}L_{n-1}=\big(\mathcal{R}^{\ast}(\varphi_{c})\big)^{n-1}\mathcal{J}L_{1},

and the well-posedness of Cauchy problem of (
4.10
) in
H^{1}(\mathbb{R})
follows from induction over

and the invertibility in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
of the adjoint recursion operator
\mathcal{R}^{\ast}(\varphi_{c})
in Lemma
2.3
.

Now we restrict ourselves to the case
A(t)=B(t)=0
, since one can show that
\int_{0}^{t}A(s)ds
and
\int_{0}^{t}B(s)ds
are uniformly bounded in time. Indeed, multiplying the equation of
v(t)
(
4.10
) by
(1-\partial_{x}^{2})\varphi_{c}
and
(1-\partial_{x}^{2})\varphi_{c}^{\prime}
, respectively, integrating on
\mathbb{R}
, using
L_{n}\varphi_{c}^{\prime}=0
, we obtain

\displaystyle A(t)=\frac{\langle v(t),L_{n}\varphi_{c}^{\prime\prime}\rangle+\langle v^{\prime}(t),(1-\partial_{x}^{2})\varphi_{c}^{\prime}\rangle}{\|\varphi_{c}^{\prime}\|^{2}_{L^{2}}+\|\varphi_{c}^{\prime\prime}\|^{2}_{L^{2}}},

\displaystyle B(t)=\frac{\langle v^{\prime}(t),(1-\partial_{x}^{2})\varphi_{c}\rangle}{\langle\frac{\partial\varphi_{c}}{\partial c},(1-\partial_{x}^{2})\varphi_{c}\rangle}=\frac{\langle v^{\prime}(t),(1-\partial_{x}^{2})\varphi_{c}\rangle}{2\kappa c}.

In the case of
n=1
, the linear equation (
4.10
) admits conservation law
\langle L_{1}v,v\rangle
. Under the orthogonal conditions satisfied by
v
, the quadratic form is positive definite. Therefore, for
v\neq 0
, one has
\langle L_{1}v,v\rangle=C_{0}>0
. On the other hand, after a suitable change of functions and semigroup decay estimate in Proposition

, one has
\|v\|_{H^{1}_{loc}}\rightarrow 0
as
t\rightarrow+\infty
. The above two facts will lead to a contradiction. Indeed, we notice from orthogonal conditions that
\langle v(t),\tilde{g}_{2}\rangle=0
for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
. From the linear equation satisfied by

, one sees that
\frac{d}{dt}\langle v(t),\tilde{g}_{1}\rangle=0
, which reveals that
\langle v(t),\tilde{g}_{1}\rangle=\langle v(0),\tilde{g}_{1}\rangle
for all
