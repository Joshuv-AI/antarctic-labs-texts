Simulating vagus nerve stimulation through Basti therapy: A novel approach to anti-inflammatory treatment in Parkinson's disease model
Authors: Pratibha P Nair, Arathi Radhakrishnan, Mukesh Edavalathu, Joyal L Joy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBasti therapy, a key intervention in Ayurveda, is hypothesized to mimic the anti-inflammatory effects of vagus nerve stimulation (VNS) through modulation of the gut–brain axis. VNS is an established neuro-modulatory technique used in the management of inflammatory disorders. Parkinson's disease is considered a model to explore this hypothesis, given the significant role of vagal dysfunction and neuroinflammation in its pathogenesis. A structured literature search of major biomedical databases (2000–2025) was conducted to identify relevant studies on vagus nerve activity, gut–brain communication, and Basti therapy. The synthesized evidence suggests that Basti may influence gut mucosa, microbiota, and enteric–neural pathways, thereby modulating vagal signalling and inflammatory responses. The Ayurvedic concept of Vata Dosha shows functional parallels with vagus nerve activity, indicating that therapies targeting Vata may exert effects analogous to VNS. Based on this, it is proposed that Basti therapy may alter Vata Dosha status and engage neuroimmune mechanisms comparable to those activated by VNS. This hypothesis warrants further validation through experimental and clinical studies that assess vagal activity and inflammatory biomarkers and may provide a basis for integrative approaches to neurodegenerative disorders.Keywords: Basti, Vagal nerve stimulation, Parkinson's disease, Inflammation, Neurodegeneration

Background

In Indian traditional medicine, Ayurveda, Basti therapy is considered the most important purification procedure (panchakarma), involving the application of a medicated enema for specific disease conditions as well as for rejuvenation [1]. This therapy has an anti-inflammatory effect and improves the healing and recovery of damaged tissues [2]. The present paper hypothesizes that the Basti therapy simulates the anti-inflammatory effect of an established neuro-stimulation technique known as vagus nerve stimulation (VNS). The VNS method involves delivering electrical impulses to the vagus nerves to induce neuromodulation [3] and an anti-inflammatory effect [4]. It has been used as an adjunctive treatment for epilepsy, migraine, depression, and various inflammatory disorders like rheumatoid arthritis, sepsis, diabetes, and cardiovascular diseases.

Parkinson's disease (PD) is an example to demonstrate this hypothesis since the pathology associated with the vagus nerve is a reliable marker for neurodegeneration associated with PD [5]. Besides, neuro-inflammatory changes have been strongly implicated in the pathogenesis of PD.

According to Ayurveda, Parkinson's disease can be conceptualized not as a single homogeneous disorder, but as a spectrum of Vata-dominant clinical variants, with the phenotype determined by the predominant Vata manifestation, associated dosha, and the presence or absence of aavarana. Following the intake of Vata-prakopaka nidana, including vega-dharana (suppression of natural urges), vata undergoes prakopa at the pakwashaya, its principal sthana. This disturbed vata manifests as Udavarta, characterized by an abnormal upward or reversed movement of localised vata viz. apana. As the underlying disease advances, a domino effect and cascading failure (anyonya avarana) among the five Vata subtypes triggers a systemic vata network failure. This progressive breakdown deeply destabilizes Vata dosha physiology and accelerates overall functional regression. In individuals with a particular predisposition, the predominant associated dosha may determine the subsequent phenotype, leading to Pitta, Kapha or ama anubandha state and thereby contributing to distinct clinical presentations.

Kampa-pradhana Kevala Vatika/Vata-Paittika presentation correspond conceptually to the tremor-predominant phenotype, where kampa (tremor) is the dominant manifestation. In contrast, Stambha–Graha-pradhana Vata-vyadhi corresponds to the bradykinesia–rigidity-predominant phenotype, characterized by stambha (rigidity/stiffness), graha (restriction of movement), slowness, and difficulty initiating voluntary movements. The Vata-Kaphaja/Kaphaavruta Vata/Ama anubandha pattern further contributes to this phenotype; classical features of Kaphaavruta Vata include gaurava (heaviness), providing an Ayurvedic framework for marked stiffness, sluggishness, and impaired mobility.

More complex neurological manifestations expressed in PD are interpreted through Praana avruta Vyana, in which Prana vata obstructs Vyana vata, with features described as indriya-soonyatva (diminution/impairment of sensory functions), smruti kshaya (decline in memory) and bala-kṣhaya (loss of strength). Thus, Parkinsonian manifestations may be understood as a continuum ranging from Kampa-pradhana (tremor-predominant) to Stambha–Graha-pradhana (bradykinesia–rigidity-predominant) and more complex aavarana-associated phenotypes.

Methods for literature search and synthesis

A literature search was conducted to identify studies relevant to the hypothesis that Basti therapy may simulate vagus nerve stimulation (VNS) and exert anti-inflammatory effects in Parkinson's disease (PD). The search strategy was designed to capture both biomedical and traditional medicine literature that explores neuroimmune modulation, gut–brain communication, and autonomic regulation in the pathophysiology of PD. Electronic databases, including PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, and Embase, were searched for articles published between 2000 and 2025.

The search strategy combined controlled vocabulary terms (e.g., MeSH in PubMed and Emtree in Embase) with free-text keywords such as “vagus nerve stimulation,” “basti therapy,” “autonomic nervous system,” “neuroinflammation,” “Parkinson's disease,” and “gut–brain axis.” Search terms and syntax were adapted to suit the indexing systems and functionalities of individual databases while maintaining conceptual consistency across all searches. Boolean operators (AND/OR) were used to refine the results, and reference lists of key studies and reviews were manually screened to identify additional relevant articles. Articles were included if they (1) presented experimental, clinical, or mechanistic data on vagus nerve stimulation or analogous interventions influencing neuroinflammation; (2) examined Basti in neurological or inflammatory contexts; or (3) investigated gut–brain or cholinergic anti inflammatory pathways relevant to PD. Exclusion criteria were non-peer-reviewed publications, conference abstracts, or studies lacking clear mechanistic insight.

Two authors independently extracted data using a standardized form, focusing on intervention type, model system (non-clinical or clinical), inflammatory markers, neural pathways, and outcomes related to dopaminergic neuroprotection. Any discrepancies were resolved through discussion with a third author to ensure consistency and accuracy.

The extracted evidence was synthesized qualitatively, with emphasis on (1) shared neuroimmune mechanisms between Basti and VNS, (2) the role of the gut–brain axis in modulating inflammation in PD, and (3) the plausibility of simulating VNS-mediated anti-inflammatory responses through Basti therapy. This synthesis formed the conceptual basis for the hypothesis proposed in this review.

Parkinson's disease: A neurodegenerative condition with complex and overlapping pathologies

PD is a prevalent neurodegenerative movement disorder characterized by muscular rigidity, bradykinesia, postural imbalances, resting tremors, and higher cognitive and emotional instabilities [6]. As the disease progresses, there is selective degeneration of the dopaminergic neurons in the nigro-striatal region [7] along with the pathological accumulation of proteinaceous intra-neuronal cytoplasmic inclusions called the Lewy Bodies (LBs) [8]. Currently, there is no cure for PD, and the treatments primarily control the symptoms.

Though the etiology of PD is still unknown, mitochondrial complex I dysfunction, impairment of ATP production, oxidative stress, neuro-inflammation, endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress, and aberrant proteolytic degradation are some of the leading causes behind neurodegeneration [9,10]. Impairment of the ubiquitin-proteasome system, a major non-lysosomal protein degradation pathway critical for the degradation of the misfolded proteins [11], causes accumulation and aggregation of intraneuronal proteinaceous inclusions identified as α-synuclein [10]. Impaired autophagy of misfolded proteins also contributes to α-synuclein deposition [10]. Furthermore, reactive microglia, along with pro-inflammatory mediators and enzymes, accentuate the inflammatory processes in PD [12].

Advanced glycation end-products (AGE)-dependent mechanisms also contribute to neurodegenerative processes in PD, linking hyperglycemia, oxidative stress, and inflammation [10]. AGE is involved in the pathological oligomerization of α-synuclein [13]. Intrasomal α synuclein, a lysine-rich protein with 15 residues identified as the primary glycation target in PD, is present in the LBs. These monomeric and oligomeric units of α-synuclein can promote oxidative stress and inflammation in various parts of the peripheral and central nervous systems. Neuronal cell dysfunction, loss of cell homeostasis, and degradation of the quality control systems of the cells are the other deleterious properties of α-synuclein units [10,13].

Several preclinical and clinical studies have confirmed the presence of AGE in the LBs, cerebral cortex, amygdala, and nigro-striatum of the PD brain [13,14]. Increased production of AGE can, in turn, lead to the accumulation of receptors for advanced glycation end products (RAGEs). RAGEs possess a self-regulating property and can perpetuate inflammation through the activation of NF-κB (nuclear factor kappa-light-chain-enhancer of activated B cells) and TNF-α (Tumor Necrosis Factor-α) [15,16]. Notably, the interaction of these receptors with S100A8/A9, a calcium-binding protein produced by neutrophils and monocytes, can also regulate many inflammatory pathways [17]. Such sustained neuroinflammation can promote the spread of amyloidogenic S100A9 in the brain tissues, triggering the amyloid cascade involving α-synuclein and leading to PD development [18].

Heterogeneity in PD etiology and overlapping of its symptoms with other neurodegenerative conditions force the need for identifying an optimal biomarker or a combination of biomarkers exclusive for PD diagnosis and prognosis. Such biomarkers may also be used to monitor any positive response to treatment. Biomarkers of PD with high sensitivity and specificity, satisfactory test-retest reproducibility, non-invasive and inexpensive measuring methods [19], and the ability to monitor disease progression unbiasedly include up-regulated levels of DJ1 and its oxidized states (sensor for cellular oxidative stress/causative familial gene) [[20], [21], [22]] in CSF and saliva and CNS centres of movement regulation. 8-Hydroxydeoxyguanosine (oxidized guanine residue in DNA), and protein halogenation advanced oxidative protein products are also upregulated in the CSF, plasma, and urine of the PD patients in comparison to the healthy control [[23], [24], [25], [26]]. Uric acid is another natural antioxidant associated with PD development and progression. Studies suggest an inverse relationship between uric acid and PD severity [27].

Among the many biomarkers, α-synuclein is the well-studied biomarker [[28], [29], [30], [31], [32]]. Though not a reliable marker [31], α-synuclein can demarcate PD from tauopathies such as Alzheimer's disease and progressive supranuclear palsy, but not from dementia with Lewy bodies and multiple system atrophy [30]. However, the combined determination of α-synuclein and UCH-L1 (ubiquitin carboxy-terminal hydrolase L1) can be used to discriminate PD from atypical PD, and synucleinopathies from tauopathies. UCH-L1 is a pivotal component of the ubiquitin-proteasome system, which was significantly reduced in CSF compared with healthy controls and atypical PD [33,34]. Multiple system atrophy (MSA) can be distinguished from PD using the ratio of phosphorylated tau to tau proteins and CSF Flt3 ligand [35]. Additionally, α-synuclein aggregation promoter tissue transglutaminase35 and osteopontin, a CNS-derived neuroprotective protein [36], were substantially elevated in PD patients compared with healthy controls. Neuro-inflammatory cytokines including interleukins (IL-1-β, IL-6, IL-8), TNF, and RANTES (regulated on activation, normal T-expressed, and presumably secreted) were increased in the CSF [31] and blood [37] and the components of the complement system were decreased [31] in the PD patients when compared to the healthy controls [31]. In addition, the high-sensitivity C-reactive protein levels were increased in the early PD groups [38]. Serum and faecal levels of calprotectin and zonulin, markers of intestinal inflammation and barrier permeability, were elevated in the PD patients [39]. Aho et al. [40] reported deficiencies in short chain fatty acids and elevated levels of faecal calprotectin, which implicated gastrointestinal inflammation in PD patients. Detecting PD-specific metabolic fingerprints across various disease stages using metabolomics is yet another promising avenue of inquiry. There are other clinical and imaging biomarkers utilized in PD differential diagnosis.

The reciprocal relationship of the gut and the brain: role of the vagus nerve in PD

It is emphasized that the pathological spread of α-synuclein is the fundamental factor for disease progression. After originating from the peripheral nervous system, mainly in the gut, the protein reaches the brainstem, midbrain, and the cerebral cortex via the anterior olfactory nucleus and the dorsal motor nuclei of the glossopharyngeal and vagal nerve [41]. Vagus nerve (VN), the parasympathetic component of ANS, serves as a link between the gut and the brain [42]. There is a clear reciprocal relationship between the gut and the brain. Information about gut microbiota metabolites is sent to the brain, and responses specific to this information are subsequently generated through the central autonomic network [43,44]. VN fibres act as a cholinergic anti-inflammatory pathway that prevents peripheral inflammation and intestinal permeability and thereby influences the microbiota colonies in the gut [45]. De Couck et al. [46] proposed that inadequate vagal activity acts as a risk factor for various diseases like cancer, Alzheimer's disease and cardiovascular diseases by exacerbating proinflammatory and oxidative mechanisms. A low heart rate variability (HRV) may be considered as a sign of poor vagal activity.

The anti-inflammatory effect of VNS has been previously reported under many medical conditions. In PD, VNS enhances anti-AGE networks (increasing cellular glutathione (GSH) and thereby glyoxalase systems), which attenuates the glycation of intrasomal α-synuclein and subsequently prevents the accumulation of AGEs [6]. Another study showed reductions in serum TNF-α and glutathione levels and an increase in brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) levels in PD patients subjected to non-invasive VNS [47]. The improvement was significantly reflected in key gait parameters, including walking speed, stance time, and step length, compared with the sham control [47,48]. Repeated transcutaneous VNS stimulation for 30 days reduced TNF-α and increased the concentration of BDNF in blood serum in human PD participants [47]. Sufficient evidence is found in animal models as well. In septic shock rats, VNS reduced serum S100A8 and AGE production, along with reduced expression of hepatic RAGE [49]. Additionally, VNS at 0.25–0.5 mA was found to have anti-inflammatory and neuroprotective effects in PD albino rats induced by 6-OHDA administration [50]. In the same PD model, the levels of inflammatory cytokines TNF-α and interleukin-1β (IL-1β) in the ventral midbrain region were significantly reduced after VNS (Jiang et al., 2018). In a double-lesioned rat model of PD, VNS at higher frequencies reduced neuroinflammation, motor deficits, tyrosine hydroxylase (TH) positive cell loss in the substantia nigra–locus coeruleus nuclei, and intrasomal α-synuclein accumulation and glial density in the substantia nigra [51]. A considerable improvement in the nor-epinephrine concentration in PFC was also observed. High-frequency microburst VNS reduced neuroinflammation and intrasomal α-synuclein in the substantia nigra of a rat PD lesion model [51].

Co-morbidities and associated inflammation: implications on PD diagnosis and treatment

There is a wide spectrum of co-morbidities that have been implicated in the pathogenesis and progression of PD and have largely affected the clinical outcomes. Any therapeutic intervention for PD stays incomplete without addressing the associated co-morbidities. This section will focus on the co-morbidities of PD with inflammatory pathogenesis, mainly diabetes, depression, anaemia, and cancer. Diabetes is found to accelerate the progression of both motor and non-motor symptoms of PD [52]. Both diabetes and PD have dysregulated inflammatory pathways, impaired insulin signaling, mitochondrial dysfunction, endoplasmic reticulum stress, and autophagy [53]. Blood myeloid dendritic cells, which play a major role in orchestrating immune response and inflammation, were declined in both diabetes and PD [54]. Drugs used to treat type-2 diabetes, like exenatide, can cross the blood-brain barrier and alter CNS processes such as neuroinflammation, mitochondrial function, and brain insulin resistance [55]. Surprisingly, the VNS can favourably modulate glycemia by affecting glucagon and insulin secretion, especially at a high frequency of 40 kHz in diabetic rats [56]. This paper evaluated the differential effects of VNS in treating type 2 diabetes.

Depression, the most common non-motor psychiatric co-morbidity seen in PD patients [57], has been positively linked to the pathology and progression of PD. Proinflammatory cytokines which play a major role in producing alterations in serotonin and dopamine neurotransmission, contribute to the etiology of both depression and PD [58]. Moreover, in both conditions, inflammatory C-reactive protein levels are upregulated across various brain regions [38]. VNS has proven to be efficacious in patients with treatment-resistant depression [59].

Anemia is a pre-diagnostic co-morbidity of PD, and both share a common etiology which includes oxidative stress. As detailed in previous sections, oxidative stress triggers a cascade of events such as mitochondrial dysfunction and neuroinflammation, leading to pathological consequences of PD [60]. Likewise, lipid peroxidation and increased ROS production have been implicated in erythrocyte damage leading to anemia [61,62]. Moreover, the condition known as the anemia of inflammation is seen when the serum iron concentrations are low despite adequate iron stores due to cytokine-induced erythropoiesis suppression [63]. Disruption in iron metabolism and lower serum iron level has been well documented in PD as well [64].

Cancer, especially melanoma, has been well studied in PD patients [65]. Inflammation plays a critical role in melanoma due to the activation of various intrinsic and extrinsic pathways that includes transcription factors, mainly nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB), signal transducer, and activator of transcription 3 (STAT3), hypoxia-inducible factor-1 alpha (HIF 1α) and their gene products i.e., prostaglandins, cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2), cytokines (TNF-α), chemokines [CXC-chemokine ligand (CXCL)] and chemokine receptors. Indeed, many of these factors also play a role in promoting PD pathogenesis.

Inflammation may be a convergent mechanism underlying the pathogenesis and progression of PD and its comorbidities. Hence, any therapeutic intervention focusing on the inflammatory pathogenesis of PD can presumably address the challenges associated with comorbidities. Pharmacological management of PD involves increasing striatal dopamine levels through dopamine replacement therapy, in which a combination of dopamine precursors (levodopa, L DOPA, L-3,4-dihydroxyphenylalanine), dopamine agonists (amantadine, apomorphine), and MAO-B inhibitors (selegiline, rasagiline) is usually administered [66] to reduce its breakdown. Higher doses of such therapeutic agents, when administered for a longer duration, often result in untoward effects that significantly hamper the quality of life of PD patients. A therapy that provides a comprehensive management strategy in PD without the burden of side-effects and which is not seemingly contradictory to PD-associated comorbidities is currently unavailable.

Basti therapy

Ayurvedic Sciences is a time-tested traditional medicine from India which has an enormous amount of experiential evidence documented in multiple accessible literary works. Human physiology, according to Ayurveda, is explained in terms of homeostasis of three functional entities working at various levels within the body, termed ‘tridoshas’ the ‘vata, pitta, and kapha’. Vata dosha is the most crucial pathophysiological factor amongst the tridoshas which determines all bodily voluntary and involuntary activities, survival, and homeostasis. Besides, vata dosha is also considered by scholars to be a potential factor that influences neurocognitive, neuromuscular, and musculoskeletal activation. It also has a definite role in maintaining gut physiology, cardiac activity and blood circulation, respiration, nourishment, and built [67]. Deviations from the homeostatic states of vata dosha are causally associated with the incidence, progression, and mortality of specific diseases.

Surprisingly, the connectome-level vagal functions mimicked the vata dosha function to a significant extent [Ashtanga Hrudaya Sootra sthana 12/4-9]. We previously argued in a discussion paper about some major functions of vata dosha in relation to the VN. Disorders due to vata dosha (vata disorders VD, vata vyadhi) resemble many neurodegenerative and neuromuscular disorders in general and vagal dysfunctions as well [67].

Appropriate Snehana therapy (controlled administration of processed lipids) is exclusively mentioned for vata dosha disorders. Mesa-Herrera F et al., 2019: Strikingly, dysregulated lipid metabolism is causally associated with neurodegenerative conditions. There are specific protocols prescribed by Ayurvedic physicians to correct the vata dosha status in an individual with VD. Time-tested Ayurvedic interventions such as ‘Abhyanga (oil massages), Swedana (fomentation), Virechana (therapeutic purgation), and Basti therapy’ (medicated enema) rectify and reverse the abnormal vata dosha status in the human body, thus bringing back homeostasis in its levels and functions. Basti therapy (BT) is given a special mention in VD, and there are different varieties of Basti treatment adopted in different VDs. BT has been found effective in various clinical conditions, both inflammation-driven and otherwise [2,[68], [69], [70], [71], [72], [73], [74], [75], [76]]. Curiously, bowel cleansing along with dietary modification was proposed as a therapeutic option in improving motor symptoms in PD [77].

Biophysical and neurophysiological mechanisms of basti

Types of basti and differential mechanisms

Basti therapy is delivered in two principal forms:

Anuvasana Basti (oil-based)

Formulations used for Anuvasana Basti contain herbal oils and lipophilic agents. The higher lipid content enhances mucosal absorption and stimulates enteroendocrine cells via lipid-sensing G protein–coupled receptors (e.g., GPR120, FFAR1), leading to the release of gut peptides such as CCK, GLP-1, and PYY, which in turn activate vagal afferent chemoreceptors and gut-brain signalling pathways [78].

Niruha basti (decoction-based)

A decoction of herbal extracts in water that exerts a pronounced volumetric effect in the distal colon. The larger fluid volume increases mechanical stretch more significantly than Anuvasana Basti, thereby preferentially stimulating mechanosensitive vagal afferents and associated vago-vagal reflexes. By distinguishing these types, Basti modalities may engage overlapping but distinct neurophysiological pathways: Anuvasana emphasizes biochemical or ligand-mediated vagal signalling, while Niruha emphasizes mechanical distension and stretch-mediated afferent firing.

Distribution and colonic motility

The distribution of Basti formulations depends on the method and pressure of administration. Gundeti et al. suggested that when Basti is delivered as a homogeneous emulsion at uniform pressure, it can reach the proximal colon more effectively, facilitating contact with the colonic mucosa and influencing colonic motility by stimulating interstitial cells of Cajal (ICCs). Increased motility promotes mixing of luminal contents, optimises interactions with Basti constituents, and may indirectly enhance mechanosensory input to vagal afferents, thereby supporting gut–brain signalling [79].

Mechanical stimulation (visceral distension)

Basti's volumetric effect transiently stretches the rectum and distal colon, activating low threshold mechanosensitive vagal afferent fibres that relay graded signals via the vagus nerve to the nucleus tractus solitarius (NTS) in the brainstem, a hub for autonomic regulation and the gut brain axis. Studies using rectal balloon distension in animals confirm that rectal stretch activates vagal circuits and triggers downstream responses, including changes in water and ion fluxes mediated by vagal and capsaicin-sensitive pathways [[80], [81], [82], [83], [84]].

This process exemplifies visceral mechano-transduction and aligns with vagovagal reflex mechanisms in classical gut physiology, whereby distension influences motility, secretion, and systemic homeostasis. Chemical/biochemical stimulation (enteroendocrine signalling)

Basti oils and herbal phytochemicals interact with the colonic mucosa, stimulating EECs to release neurotransmitters and gut peptides, including CCK, GLP-1, PYY, and serotonin, thereby activating chemosensitive vagal afferents. Receptors for these signals (CCK1R, GLP1R, HTR3A) are expressed on nodose ganglion neurons or adjacent vagal endings. EECs can also form synaptic connections with vagal fibres, enabling rapid chemical signaling from the gut lumen to central autonomic circuits [[85], [86], [87]].

Together, mechanical distension and chemical/biochemical EEC-vagal signaling provide a mechanistic framework through which Basti therapy engages vagal pathways. This integrated model strengthens the physiological plausibility of Basti's systemic effects, situating it firmly within modern frameworks of visceral mechano-transduction and gut chemoreception that modulate central autonomic function. Thus, we arrive at a hypothesis that if vata dosha resembles major VN functions indirectly, then Vata dosha management strategies such as the ‘basti therapy’ should be simulating some of the effects of VNS like anti-inflammation. To elaborate, BT is hypothesized here to have a definite impact on VN simulating VNS effects. BT is administered directly to the gut and the active principles of the medicaments in the basti therapy are believed to reach grahani (upper gastrointestinal tract) [Charaka Sidhi 3/24 Chakrapani]. Considering the site of administration, BT is assumed to have a significant impact on gut mucosa, gut enzymes, microbiota species, and microbiota metabolites [69]. N Pathak Gandhi 2017 [68] argued that the enteric nervous system, gut microbiota, and parasympathetic nerves are causally associated with PD incidence. Also, degeneration of serotonergic neurons leads to progressive loss of dopaminergic neurons. The gut is a prime manufacturer of serotonin. Against this finding, N Pathak Gandhi 2017 proposed that the role of gut modulations through treatments of alternative drug delivery routes, such as Basti, may be tried in PD. Neurogenic bowel dysfunction is also detected in PD [95]. It is projected that the afferent branches of the vagus in the gut can be stimulated with BT, just as the neurostimulation method mentioned for neurogenic bowel dysfunction. Here, we hypothesize that ‘the BT can bring about changes in the functional vata dosha status, and thereby alter specific mechanisms to bring changes similar to VNS in PD.

Proposed hypothesis testing methods

An ulcerative colitis bioinformatics model with enema decoction is studied [96]. On similar grounds, a comprehensive approach employing bioinformatics methodologies, including network pharmacology, molecular docking, and animal experimentation, may be implemented to explore the underlying mechanisms and anti-inflammatory models by which BT may contribute to the treatment of Parkinson's disease.

In clinical trials, 24-h heart rate variability (HRV) is an established clinical measure for evaluating VN activity [97]. A previous study has shown that the treatments targeting VN increase vagal tone and diminish cytokine production [42]. Changes in VN activity may thus be measured in relation to inflammatory marker levels, including serum TNF-α, C-reactive protein (CRP), and salivary cortisol [98]. There are specific assays to measure and evaluate the changes in anti-AGE system markers in PD such as serum levels of AGEs and inflammatory cytokine S100A8/A9 [14]. α -synuclein aggregation promotor tissue transglutaminase [35] and osteopontin [99] may also be used as markers to monitor any positive response to BT in PD patients.

The proposed framework aims to evaluate whether BT can induce physiological effects analogous to vagus nerve stimulation (VNS) in Parkinson's disease (PD). Primary hypothesis BT (standardized therapeutic protocol) modulates vagal activity, reflected by changes in heart rate variability (HRV), and is associated with measurable alterations in Vata Dosha status, assessed using standardized Ayurvedic clinical scoring systems, including symptom-based Vata assessment scales and physician-based evaluation, thereby influencing neuroimmune pathways in Parkinson's disease (PD). Secondary hypotheses BT is associated with modulation of systemic and neuroinflammatory markers, including C reactive protein (CRP), salivary cortisol, serum TNF-α, advanced glycation end products (AGEs), and inflammatory mediators such as S100A8/A9. Exploratory outcomes Additional mechanisms may include effects on α-synuclein aggregation (via tissue transglutaminase), gut microbiome composition [68], and clinical parameters such as gait performance (walking speed, stance time, and step length). This hierarchical approach enables the prioritization of key outcomes while allowing for integrative assessment of the gut–brain–immune axis. It is hypothesized that the BT, while targeting inappropriate Vata dosha levels, will bring changes related to VNS in PD that would prove decisive in disease progression. Proposed timeline of intervention and assessment

Kala Basti protocol is proposed as a standardized and widely accepted regimen in Ayurveda for the management of Vata disorders, providing an optimal balance between therapeutic efficacy, feasibility, and adequate duration to capture measurable autonomic, inflammatory, and clinical changes. Within this standardized framework, the selection of specific formulations such as Vṛṣya–Vatahara Basti (Siddha Basti) may be incorporated based on classical indications, owing to its vṛṣya (tissue-nourishing), vatahara (Vata-pacifying), and bṛṃhaṇa (anabolic and restorative) properties, which are particularly relevant in conditions associated with vata disorders. At the same time, controlled personalization is essential and may be implemented through predefined criteria, allowing adjustments based on disease stage, symptom severity, and individual patient characteristics (influential health assessment model of dashavidha pareeksha based on overall systemic functions and presence and absence of co-morbidities such as anaemia, depression etc)., thereby ensuring both methodological rigor and clinical applicability. Clinical significance

Should the hypothesis yield positive results upon testing, it may represent a significant advancement in research focused on identifying novel methods to influence the vagus nerve (VN) and the associated molecular and cytoplasmic mechanisms implicated in various neurodegenerative disorders. Furthermore, assessing VN activity could serve as a valuable quantitative approach to evaluate the effectiveness of Basti Therapy (Specialized Ayurveda Intervention) in different neurodegenerative conditions.

Conclusion

The proposed hypothesis appears complex. Nevertheless, any positive findings could represent a breakthrough in research aimed at developing new ways to manipulate the VN and the molecular/cytoplasmic mechanisms underlying various neurodegenerative disorders. It is presumed that measuring VN activity may be a useful quantitative method to understand the efficacy of BT in various neurodegenerative disorders. Noninvasive alternatives to VNS are currently sought as personalized VNS regimens with the potential of positively manipulating VN in the shared pathogenesis of inflammatory pathways are gaining much clinical significance [100]. If the hypothesis proves correct, it will open a window of opportunity for integrative medicine to incorporate Vata dosha treatment strategies for multisystem disorders, with a clear role for VN and VNS as candidate disease-modifying therapies. Also, the functional range and clinical importance of Vata dosha, as explained in Ayurvedic literature, can be understood from a new perspective that is appropriate to contemporary scientific understanding.
