Introduction

Breast masses are a common concern in clinical practice, affecting a significant proportion of women worldwide. These lesions can range from benign cysts to malignant tumors, with various etiologies and clinical presentations [1]. Breast cysts are fluid-filled sacs that develop within breast tissue particularly during the reproductive years. The prevalence of breast cysts varies widely in the literature, largely due to differences in study populations, diagnostic criteria, and detection methods. While our initial estimate stated that approximately 7% of women experience breast cysts, more recent studies suggest that the prevalence may be significantly higher. Some reports indicate that up to 20–25% of women between the ages of 35 and 50 may develop palpable breast cysts at some point [2, 3]. They typically present as smooth, mobile, and sometimes tender lumps, often discovered during routine breast examinations or screening mammograms [4]. The etiology of breast cysts is multifactorial, often attributed to hormonal fluctuations, particularly during the perimenopausal period [5]. Clinically, these cysts may be asymptomatic or associated with localized pain, tenderness, and occasionally nipple discharge [6].

Diagnostic approaches typically involve a combination of clinical examination, imaging studies such as ultrasonography and mammography, and, in some cases, fine-needle aspiration or biopsy to rule out malignancy [7]. While the differential diagnosis for breast masses is extensive, certain etiologies are exceedingly rare and may pose significant diagnostic challenges [8]. One such uncommon entity is the hydatid cyst of the breast, a parasitic infection caused by the larval stage of Echinococcus granulosus [9].

Hydatid disease, also known as echinococcosis, is endemic in many parts of the world, particularly in regions with livestock farming [10]. The liver and lungs are the most affected organs, accounting for approximately 90% of cases [11]. Primary breast involvement, however, is exceptionally rare, with an incidence of less than 0.27% among all hydatid cyst cases [12].

Hydatid cysts in the breast can mimic other benign or malignant breast lesions, making accurate diagnosis challenging [13]. The slow-growing nature of these cysts and their nonspecific clinical presentation often led to delayed diagnosis [14]. Patients may present with a palpable, painless mass that can be mistaken for a fibroadenoma or other benign breast lesions [15].

The radioimaging characteristics of hydatid cysts in the breast are crucial for accurate diagnosis and differentiation from other breast lesions. On ultrasonography, breast hydatid cysts typically appear as well-defined, anechoic or hypoechoic lesions with posterior acoustic enhancement [16]. The pathognomonic “water lily” sign, representing detached endocyst membranes, may be observed in some cases [17].

Mammography often reveals a round or oval, high-density mass with smooth margins, which can be mistaken for a benign lesion [17]. The presence of calcifications within the cyst wall or daughter cysts may be visualized on mammograms, providing additional diagnostic clues [18].

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) can offer superior soft tissue contrast and may demonstrate the characteristic hypointense rim of the pericyst on T2-weighted images, as well as the “serpent sign” representing collapsed membranes within the cyst [19]. Computed tomography (CT) is less commonly used for breast imaging but can be valuable in assessing the extent of disease and identifying potential complications such as rupture or secondary infection [20]. Despite these imaging features, the rarity of breast hydatid cysts often leads to misdiagnosis, emphasizing the importance of maintaining a high index of suspicion in endemic regions and correlating imaging findings with clinical and serological data for accurate diagnosis [21].

Hydatid disease is highly prevalent in the Mediterranean region, Russian Federation and adjacent independent states, Republic of China, Northern and Eastern Africa, Australia, and South America [22]. In these endemic areas, hydatid disease should be considered in the differential diagnosis of breast masses, especially when conventional diagnostic methods yield inconclusive results [23]. The rarity of primary breast hydatid cysts, combined with their potential to mimic more common breast pathologies, underscores the importance of maintaining a high index of suspicion in endemic regions [24]. This case report presents a unique instance of a primary breast hydatid cyst initially presenting as a typical breast mass, highlighting the diagnostic journey from initial presentation to final diagnosis. By sharing this rare case, we aim to contribute to the existing literature on atypical presentations of hydatid disease and emphasize the importance of considering parasitic etiologies in the differential diagnosis of breast masses, particularly in regions where Echinococcus granulosus is endemic.

Case report

A 51-year-old multiparous Iranian female residing in Kerman province, a region within the Mediterranean basin, presented to our clinic with a chief complaint of a painless mass in her left breast, which she had first noticed approximately one year prior to consultation. The patient reported that the lump had remained static in size since its initial discovery. Notably, she denied any associated symptoms such as mastalgia, nipple discharge, or cutaneous changes overlying the affected area. Furthermore, the patient observed no correlation between the lump’s characteristics and her menstrual cycle.

Upon thorough inquiry into her medical history, the patient reported no prior trauma to the breast region and no history of breast surgery. Her obstetric history was significant for three pregnancies (G3P3L2). Of these, two resulted in healthy children, while one pregnancy unfortunately ended in an intrauterine fetal demise (IUFD) due to meconium aspiration. The patient reported that she had breastfed both of her living children, demonstrating a history of lactation and its associated physiological changes in breast tissue. She denied any family history of breast malignancies or other hereditary breast pathologies.

The patient’s vital signs were within normal limits, and she appeared to be in good general health. No systemic symptoms such as fever, weight loss, or fatigue were reported, which might have suggested a more widespread pathological process.

General physical examination revealed a patient in good overall health. There were no signs of anemia, jaundice, or generalized lymphadenopathy. Abdominal examination showed no evidence of organomegaly or other palpable masses. On left breast examination, careful inspection of the breast surface showed no apparent skin changes, such as dimpling, erythema, or peau d’orange appearance. Notably, there were no visible scars on the breast skin, confirming the patient’s report of no previous breast surgeries. The nipple-areola complex appeared symmetrical and without abnormalities when compared to the contralateral breast. No nipple retraction or deviation was observed. On palpation, examination revealed a well-defined mass in the upper outer quadrant of the left breast, measuring approximately 4 × 4 cm in its greatest dimensions. The mass was characterized by a firm consistency and demonstrated free mobility within the surrounding breast tissue. Importantly, the lesion was non-tender to palpation, further corroborating the patient’s report of a painless presentation. Examination of the axillary regions revealed palpable lymph nodes in the left axilla. These lymph nodes were mobile, non-tender, and firm in consistency. The right axilla and bilateral supraclavicular regions were free of palpable lymphadenopathy.

The patient underwent thorough imaging studies of both breasts and axillary regions, including both ultrasonography and mammography. These investigations revealed no signs suggestive of malignancy, such as microcalcifications or wall thickening.

The ultrasonography (USG) of the left breast revealed a well-defined, oval-shaped, anechoic lesion measuring 4.5 × 4 cm in the upper outer quadrant. The cyst demonstrated posterior acoustic enhancement, suggesting a fluid-filled structure. No internal echoes or septations were observed within the cyst. The cyst wall appeared smooth and thin, without any mural nodules or calcifications. Color Doppler examination showed no internal vascularity. Based on the imaging findings, the lesion was classified as BI-RADS 3 (Breast Imaging-Reporting and Data System category 3), indicating a probable benign finding.

Given the size of the cyst (4.5 × 4 cm), the attending surgeon opted for fine-needle aspiration to drain the cyst. The procedure yielded clear fluid, which, at the time, was not deemed to require further cytological assessment. Post-drainage, the patient was prescribed daily vitamin E supplementation (400 mg) for a duration of three months and scheduled for a follow-up appointment at the end of this period [25, 26].

First recurrence

At the three-month follow-up, clinical examination revealed that the cyst had refilled, presenting in the exact previous location. The cyst was again drained via fine-needle aspiration. The patient’s management plan was adjusted to include.

Second recurrence and surgical intervention

At the six-month mark from the initial presentation, the patient returned with the cyst having refilled once more, this time appearing larger than previous occurrences. A repeat ultrasonography was performed, revealing the cyst had increased in size to 5 × 5 cm. After thorough consultation with the patient and considering the recurrent nature of the cyst, the decision was made to proceed with complete surgical excision of the cyst.

Prior to surgery, standard preoperative laboratory examinations were conducted. All results were within normal parameters, with the notable exception of the complete blood count differential, which revealed eosinophilia.

Given the recurrent nature of the cyst and the presence of eosinophilia, additional serological tests were performed. An enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) for Echinococcus granulosus antibodies was positive, with a titer of 1:640 (reference range: <1:160 negative, 1:160-1:320 borderline, > 1:320 positive). This result raised suspicion for hydatid disease and prompted further investigation.

The patient underwent surgical excision of the cyst. Intraoperatively, the cyst was found to be well-encapsulated with a thick, whitish wall. Care was taken to avoid rupture during excision to prevent potential anaphylaxis or spread of protoscolices. The entire specimen was submitted to the pathology laboratory for comprehensive evaluation.

Gross examination of the specimen revealed a unilocular cyst measuring 5.5 × 5.0 × 4.5 cm, filled with clear fluid. The inner surface of the cyst wall appeared smooth and pearly white. Upon microscopic examination of the prepared slides, the pathologist observed the characteristic laminated membrane and protoscolices of Echinococcus granulosus. the pathologist made an unexpected discovery: the final diagnosis was confirmed as a hydatid cyst (Fig. 1).

Furthermore, an immunoblot assay was conducted, which demonstrated the presence of specific antibodies against the 8 kDa and 16 kDa antigens of E. granulosus, further supporting the diagnosis. While immunoelectrophoresis was not performed in this case due to its lower sensitivity compared to ELISA and immunoblot, the combination of positive ELISA and immunoblot results, along with the histopathological findings, provided strong evidence for the diagnosis of a hydatid cyst. These serological tests not only confirmed the diagnosis but also helped rule out potential cross-reactions with other helminthic infections.

Further histopathological analysis included special staining techniques to confirm the diagnosis of a hydatid cyst. Ziehl-Neelsen staining was performed, which revealed acid-fast positive hooklets, a characteristic feature of Echinococcus granulosus. Additionally, Grocott’s methenamine silver (GMS) stain was applied, further highlighting the parasitic structures. Microscopic examination revealed classic protoscolices with an armed rostellum, providing definitive evidence of Echinococcus granulosus infection. These specialized staining techniques and the identification of specific parasitic structures were crucial in establishing the final diagnosis of a primary breast hydatid cyst, distinguishing it from other cystic lesions of the breast.

Following the surgical excision and confirmation of the hydatid cyst diagnosis, a comprehensive post-surgical treatment plan was implemented for the patient. The primary focus was on eradicating any remaining parasites and preventing recurrence or spread of the infection. The patient was initiated on albendazole therapy, with a standard dosage of 400 mg twice daily. This anti-parasitic treatment was prescribed for an initial period of 3–6 months, with the understanding that the duration might be extended based on the patient’s response and follow-up evaluations. Regular liver function tests were scheduled to monitor for potential hepatotoxicity associated with long-term albendazole use.

Moreover, following the unexpected diagnosis of a primary breast hydatid cyst, a comprehensive assessment was conducted to rule out disseminated hydatidosis. This evaluation included:

These comprehensive investigations revealed no evidence of hydatid cysts in other organs, confirming the diagnosis of an isolated primary breast hydatid cyst. This thorough approach was crucial in determining the extent of the disease and guiding subsequent management decisions.

A series of follow-up imaging studies were planned, including periodic ultrasound examinations of both breasts and the abdominal region, particularly focusing on the liver, which is a common site for hydatid cysts. These imaging studies were crucial for monitoring potential recurrence or the presence of cysts in other locations. The medical team also considered the potential need for CT or MRI scans if there was any suspicion of disseminated disease.

Patient education formed a crucial component of the post-surgical management. The patient was thoroughly instructed on proper hygiene practices to prevent reinfection or spread of the parasite. This included guidance on thorough washing of fruits and vegetables before consumption, avoiding consumption of raw or undercooked meat (especially in endemic areas), and maintaining proper hand hygiene, particularly after contact with soil or animals.

Given the rarity of primary breast hydatid cysts, the medical team adopted a multidisciplinary approach, involving infectious disease specialists, surgeons, and radiologists in the patient’s ongoing care. This collaborative effort ensured comprehensive management and allowed for expert input from various specialties.

The patient was scheduled for regular follow-up appointments over the next five years to monitor for any signs of recurrence. During these visits, clinical examinations, imaging studies, and serological tests would be performed to ensure early detection of any potential relapse.

Discussion

The case presented herein illustrates the diagnostic challenges associated with primary breast hydatid cysts, a rare manifestation of echinococcosis. The patient’s initial presentation of a painless breast mass, coupled with inconclusive imaging findings, exemplifies the potential for misdiagnosis in such cases.

This case highlights the importance of maintaining a broad differential diagnosis when evaluating breast masses, particularly in cases of recurrent cysts that are refractory to conventional management. The presence of eosinophilia in the preoperative blood work, although not initially recognized as significant, retrospectively aligns with the final diagnosis of a parasitic cyst. This case of primary breast hydatid cyst underscores the need for heightened clinical suspicion in endemic areas and illustrates the potential for parasitic infections to mimic more common breast pathologies.

The patient was a 51-year-old Iranian female residing in Kerman province, a region within the Mediterranean basin where Echinococcus granulosus remains endemic. Iran reports an annual incidence of hydatid disease ranging from 0.6 to 2.6 cases per 100,000 population, with higher prevalence in rural areas where pastoral farming practices predominate [28]. The geographic and demographic context of this case aligns with established epidemiological patterns of human hydatidosis in the Eastern Mediterranean region, where the parasite’s life cycle is maintained through extensive sheep farming and close human-animal contact [29]. This epidemiological background proved particularly relevant given the eventual diagnosis, underscoring the importance of considering endemic parasitic infections in the differential diagnosis of breast masses in such regions.

Breast involvement in hydatid disease is exceptionally uncommon, accounting for only 0.27% of all cases [12]. This rarity often leads to delayed diagnosis, as demonstrated in our case where the true nature of the cyst was only revealed after multiple recurrences and eventual surgical excision. Similar diagnostic difficulties have been reported in the literature, highlighting the importance of maintaining a high index of suspicion, particularly in endemic areas [30]. Clinically, breast hydatid cysts can mimic a variety of benign and malignant conditions, posing a significant diagnostic challenge. These lesions may present similarly to fibroadenomas, fibrocystic disease, phyllodes tumors, chronic abscesses, simple cysts, or even carcinomas [31]. This wide range of potential clinical mimics underscores the importance of a comprehensive diagnostic approach. The triple assessment, which includes a thorough history and physical examination, radiological imaging, and pathological evaluation, remains the gold standard for accurate diagnosis of breast lesions [31].

The imaging characteristics of breast hydatid cysts can be nonspecific, often mimicking other benign or malignant lesions. Literature studies have shown that ultrasound and CT scans may reveal cysts with septations [16]. On mammography, lesions typically appear as well-circumscribed, oval or spherical densities with smooth lobulated margins, and peripheral calcification may be present in some cases [32]. Ultrasonography can provide more detailed information, potentially revealing several pathognomonic signs: the double wall sign (cyst wall appearing as two echogenic layers), the snowstorm sign (due to movement of scolices within the cyst), the water lily sign (floating membranes due to detached endocyst or daughter cyst), and the scroll sign (due to folding of the detached endocyst) [18, 19]. However, it’s important to note that these classic signs are not always present, and their absence does not rule out hydatid disease [20].

In our case, the initial imaging findings were inconclusive, highlighting the challenge in diagnosing this rare entity based on radiological features alone. This underscores the importance of maintaining a high index of suspicion in endemic areas and considering hydatid disease in the differential diagnosis of breast masses, even when imaging findings are not typical [21].

In a comprehensive review by Botezatu et al. ultrasound findings of hydatid cysts were found to be variable, ranging from simple cystic lesions to complex masses with internal echoes [9]. This variability in imaging presentation aligns with our case, where initial ultrasound and mammography were inconclusive, leading to a BI-RADS 3 classification.

The recurrence of the cyst after initial aspiration in our case is noteworthy. Gharbi et al. reported that while fine-needle aspiration can be useful for diagnosis, it is generally not recommended as a definitive treatment for hydatid cysts due to the risk of anaphylaxis and cyst recurrence [33]. Our experience corroborates this finding, emphasizing the need for more definitive management strategies in suspected cases. The presence of eosinophilia in our patient’s preoperative blood work, although initially overlooked, proved to be a valuable diagnostic clue. Wen et al. found that eosinophilia is present in a significant proportion of patients with hydatid disease, underlining its potential as a diagnostic indicator [34]. This highlights the importance of thorough evaluation of all laboratory findings, even those that may seem unrelated at first glance.

Post-surgical management with albendazole therapy, as implemented in our case, is supported by current literature. Velasco-Tirado et al. demonstrated the efficacy of albendazole in preventing recurrence and treating residual cysts in various organ systems affected by hydatid disease [35]. The multidisciplinary approach adopted in our case, involving surgeons, infectious disease specialists, and radiologists, aligns with best practices in managing complex cases of hydatid disease.

Our case shares similarities with, yet differs from, other reported instances of primary breast hydatid cysts in the literature. Alamer et al. reported a case of a 66-year-old woman with a painful breast lump, initially misdiagnosed as a phyllodes tumor [36]. Like our patient, the diagnosis was only confirmed post-operatively through histopathological examination. However, unlike our case, their patient’s ultrasound showed a well-defined heterogeneous mass with internal cystic areas, which prompted consideration of hydatid cyst in the differential diagnosis [36].

In another case reported by Vega et al., a 74-year-old woman presented with a palpable mass in the left breast [37]. Analogous to our case, initial imaging studies were inconclusive, with mammography showing a well-circumscribed, high-density mass. However, their case differed in that MRI revealed the characteristic ‘wheel-spoke’ pattern suggestive of hydatid cyst, leading to preoperative suspicion of the diagnosis [37]. This contrasts with our case, where the diagnosis remained elusive until histopathological examination.

Geramizadeh et al., in their review of unusual locations of hydatid cysts, noted that breast involvement accounts for only 0.27% of all hydatid cysts [13]. They emphasized the importance of considering hydatid cyst in the differential diagnosis of breast masses in endemic areas, a point strongly reinforced by our case [13]. Our experience aligns with their observation that the diagnosis is often unexpected and made only after histopathological examination.

These comparisons highlight the diagnostic challenges associated with primary breast hydatid cysts and underscore the importance of maintaining a high index of suspicion, particularly in endemic regions. Our case contributes to the existing literature by demonstrating the potential for recurrence after aspiration and the value of thorough histopathological examination in reaching the correct diagnosis.

Conclusion

This case of primary breast hydatid cyst illustrates the diverse presentations of echinococcosis and the challenges in diagnosing rare parasitic infections. It underscores the importance of a broad differential diagnosis for breast masses, especially in endemic regions, and highlights the value of a multidisciplinary approach in management. The case emphasizes the need for thorough follow-up to prevent recurrence and contributes to the literature on atypical presentations of hydatid disease. By sharing this experience, we aim to raise awareness among clinicians about the possibility of encountering such rare cases, thereby improving diagnostic acumen and patient care in the context of parasitic infections presenting in unusual anatomical locations.

Limitations

of the diagnostic documentation. Specifically, while a CT scan was performed as part of the patient’s diagnostic evaluation, we do not have access to the CT scan images. The absence of these images restricts our ability to provide a more detailed visual assessment of the lesion’s characteristics and the extent of the disease at the time of diagnosis. CT imaging is instrumental in evaluating complex cystic structures and could have offered additional insights into the anatomical details and potential involvement of adjacent tissues. Despite this limitation, the diagnosis was effectively confirmed through ultrasonography, and histopathological examination. Future case studies could benefit from ensuring the preservation and inclusion of all relevant imaging to enhance the diagnostic narrative and provide a more comprehensive understanding of similar cases.

Additionally, the smears obtained during cytological evaluation, including those used for special staining techniques, are no longer available for imaging in this article. This includes the Ziehl-Neelsen staining, which revealed acid-fast positive hooklets characteristic of Echinococcus granulosus, and the Grocott’s methenamine silver (GMS) stain, which further highlighted the parasitic structures. The absence of these smears limits our capacity to include visual evidence of the cytological features observed during the initial diagnostic workup. Despite these limitations, the diagnosis was effectively confirmed through histopathological examination.
