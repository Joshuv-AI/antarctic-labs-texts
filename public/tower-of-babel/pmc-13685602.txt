Pulmonary valve replacement-related change in biventricular global function index in repaired tetralogy of Fallot
Authors: Shanique Sterling-Lovy, Francesca Sperotto, Lynn A Sleeper, Minmin Lu, Vedang Diwanji, Edward O’Leary, Anne Marie Valente, Tal Geva
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundReduced biventricular global function index (BVGFI) is associated with adverse outcomes in repaired tetralogy of Fallot (rTOF). The change in BVGFI associated with pulmonary valve replacement (PVR) is unknown. This study aims to characterize BVGFI following PVR in rTOF and identify pre-PVR factors associated with severely depressed post-PVR BVGFI.MethodsSingle-center retrospective cohort study of rTOF patients with a cardiovascular magnetic resonance (CMR) examination within 1 year before and 2 years after their first PVR and no interval cardiac procedures (n = 133). CMR parameters between rTOF and normal controls (n = 136) were compared. BVGFI was categorized as normal (≥46.2), mild-moderately depressed (40.0–46.1), or severely depressed (<40.0). Pre- vs post-PVR changes and pre-PVR correlates of severely depressed post-PVR BVGFI were explored.ResultsWhen adjusted for age and sex, pre-PVR BVGFI was lower in patients with rTOF compared to controls (47.7 ± 0.6 vs 56.0 ± 0.5, p < 0.001), with 48% of rTOF patients having subnormal pre-PVR BVGFI. Overall, compared with pre-PVR values, mean BVGFI did not change after PVR (46.6 ± 7.7 vs 45.6 ± 6.7, p = 0.28), while right ventricular global function index declined from 49.6 ± 10.2 pre-PVR to 46.1 ± 9.0 post-PVR (p = 0.003). Among patients with normal pre-PVR BVGFI (n = 69), 64% remained normal, whereas 36% declined. Of those with severely depressed pre-PVR BVGFI (n = 24), 50% remained severely depressed, and only 4% achieved normalization of BVGFI after PVR. Factors independently associated with severely depressed post-PVR BVGFI were lower pre-PVR BVGFI, male sex, moderate or severe pulmonary regurgitation (PR), and higher left ventricular end-systolic volume index (LV ESVi). Type of pre-PVR hemodynamic load was not associated with the odds of severely depressed BVGFI post-PVR.ConclusionBVGFI is depressed in about half of rTOF patients pre-PVR and did not significantly change post-PVR, remaining stable in most patients. Lower pre-PVR BVGFI, male sex, moderate or severe PR, and higher LV ESVi are independently associated with severely depressed post-PVR BVGFI.Keywords: Tetralogy of Fallot, Pulmonary valve replacement, Biventricular global functional index, Pulmonary regurgitation, Cardiovascular magnetic resonance imaging

Introduction

Patients with repaired tetralogy of Fallot (rTOF) are vulnerable to adverse clinical outcomes consequent to a complex pathophysiologic cascade stemming from prolonged exposure to volume or pressure overload in the setting of right ventricular outflow tract (RVOT) dysfunction [1], [2]. Pulmonary valve replacement (PVR) is increasingly used to address these pathophysiologic abnormalities and has been shown to decrease the risk of major adverse clinical outcomes in these patients [3], [4]. However, the optimal timing for PVR in rTOF patients is still unclear, with evidence suggesting a window of opportunity within the compensated phase of the disease, before the onset of irreversible myocardial injury [5]. Traditionally, markers used to identify optimal PVR timing in rTOF have focused on right ventricular (RV) volumes and ejection fraction (EF); however, these metrics have been found to have limited ability to identify rTOF patients at risk for adverse clinical outcomes [6], [7]. Further, left ventricular imaging biomarkers have also been shown to be associated with outcomes in these patients, indicating the important role of ventricular-ventricular interaction [8], [9].

More recently, biventricular global function index (BVGFI)—a novel imaging biomarker of ventricular performance that integrates RV and left ventricle (LV) volumes, masses, and systolic function—was found to be more strongly associated with adverse clinical outcomes in patients with rTOF [10], [11]. However, the change in BVGFI associated with PVR in this population remains uncharacterized. Therefore, this study aims to (1) characterize BVGFI following PVR in rTOF patients; (2) identify pre-PVR factors associated with severely depressed BVGFI after PVR; and (3) compare BVGFI in rTOF patients with reference values in individuals with a structurally normal heart. We hypothesize that compared with normal controls, BVGFI will be lower in patients with rTOF but will improve after PVR.

Methods

Design

We performed a single-center retrospective observational study at Boston Children’s Hospital. The Institutional Review Board approved the study, which waived the requirement for informed consent (IRB-P00044788).

Subjects

The following two cohorts were identified through searches of our institutional electronic medical records:

Normal cohort

Patients undergoing a screening cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) at our institution from July 2017 through December 2022. Patients were included if no cardiovascular abnormality was identified and measurable biventricular volumes, mass, and systolic function were obtainable. Individuals with a medical condition potentially impacting ventricular size and function (e.g., gene positive-phenotype-negative cardiomyopathy, hematologic disorders, etc.) were excluded (Fig. 1).

rTOF cohort

All patients with rTOF who had a first-time PVR at our institution from January 2002 through September 2022 and a CMR examination within 1 year before and 2 years after their first PVR were included. Patients who had an intervening surgical or catheter-based cardiovascular procedure between the pre- and the post-PVR CMRs or unobtainable biventricular volumes and/or masses by CMR were excluded. Patients with a concomitant atrioventricular canal defect or tetralogy of Fallot with absent pulmonary valve were excluded (Fig. 1).

Data collection

Patients’ demographics and pre-PVR anatomic and clinical characteristics were extracted from the electronic medical records, including baseline anatomy TOF with pulmonary stenosis vs TOF with pulmonary atresia); cardiac surgical history, including initial palliation, age at TOF repair, type of TOF repair; time between TOF repair and PVR; pre-PVR exercise stress test data, and New York Heart Association class. Pre-PVR echocardiographic data, including RV pressure by systolic septal position, RVOT maximum instantaneous gradient, and degree of tricuspid regurgitation (TR), were also obtained from the pre-PVR echocardiogram closest to pre-PVR CMR. Based on published criteria [12], patients were categorized based on their predominant pre-PVR hemodynamic load as follows: (1) predominant volume load (pulmonary regurgitation (PR) fraction ≥25% and peak RVOT gradient ≤25 mm Hg); (2) predominant pressure load (PR fraction <25% and RVOT gradient ≥40 mm Hg); or (3) mixed volume and pressure load (all remaining patients).

Arrhythmia history was obtained by reviewing clinic notes, Holter monitors, and invasive electrophysiology studies obtained within 18 months before PVR. Sustained atrial or ventricular tachycardia (VT) was defined as a tachycardia lasting for >30 s. Non-sustained VT was defined as VT lasting <30 s but >3 beats at a rate of >100 bpm [13]. A hemodynamically significant arrhythmia was defined as either sustained supraventricular tachycardia (SVT), including atrial flutter, atrial fibrillation, atrioventricular nodal re-entrant tachycardia, accessory pathway-mediated SVT, focal atrial tachycardia, and ectopic atrial tachycardia, or sustained VT. QRS duration was measured on the closest electrocardiogram (ECG) before the date of PVR. PVR was categorized as transcatheter or surgical.

Cardiac magnetic resonance

CMR studies were performed using our previously published institutional protocol with 1.5T scanners (Philips Healthcare, Best, The Netherlands or GE Medical Systems, Milwaukee, Wisconsin) [14], [15]. Briefly, ventricular volumes were measured on ECG-gated steady-state free precession cine imaging in the ventricular short-axis plane by manually tracing endocardial and epicardial borders at end-diastole (maximal volume) and end-systole (minimal volume). PR fraction was measured using phase-velocity mapping cine imaging perpendicular to the main pulmonary artery as previously described [16]. All analyses were performed using commercially available software (QMass, Medis Medical Imaging Systems, Leiden, The Netherlands) and (CVi42, Circle Cardiovascular Imaging Inc., Calgary, Alberta, Canada).

Normal BVFGI values were defined as those within 2 standard deviations (SD) of the mean measured in the normal cohort. Severely depressed BVFGI was defined as BVFGI <40.0, i.e., >3 SD below the mean of the normal cohort. Values between the lower limit (−2 SD) of the normal cohort and 40.0 were defined as mildly to moderately depressed.

Interobserver variability

To assess interobserver variability of BVGFI measurements, a random sample of 30 CMR studies among the rTOF patients was selected, and biventricular volumes and masses were measured by two independent investigators with expertise in CMR.

Outcome

The primary outcome was defined as severely depressed BVGFI (<40.0) after PVR, vs mildly to moderately depressed vs normal (as an ordinal outcome measure).

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were summarized using frequencies and percentages, and continuous variables were summarized using either mean ± SD or median and interquartile range based on distribution characteristics. A regression model adjusted for age at CMR and gender was used to compare the CMR parameters between the normal and the rTOF cohorts. The paired t-test and Wilcoxon signed rank test were used to test the mean or median difference of CMR parameters between the two independent readers in rTOF as well as to test pre- and post-PVR differences in CMR parameters in rTOF patients. Spearman’s correlation coefficient and linear regression were used to assess the association between pre- and post-PVR BVGFI. A Fisher's exact test and the Mantel-Haenszel test for linear trend were used to analyze changes in pre- and post-PVR BVGFI categories (normal, mildly to moderately depressed, severely depressed; and post-PVR increase of at least 2 SD, stability, and decrease of at least 2 SD).

Coefficient of variation was calculated to estimate the stability of a measurement across readers, and two-way mixed effects models were fit to calculate the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC). ICC was interpreted as follows: <0.5: poor reliability; 0.5–0.75: moderate reliability; 0.76–0.9: good reliability; >0.90: excellent reliability.

Cumulative logistic regression was used to estimate the unadjusted and adjusted associations between pre-PVR characteristics and the primary outcome (severely depressed BVGFI vs mild to moderately depressed vs normal). A score test was used to test the proportional odds assumption. Some predictors were modeled as both continuous variables and as pre-specified binary groups or tertiles. Candidate variables included for multivariable modeling were those with a univariate p-value <0.15 and those that were judged to be clinically meaningful. A multivariable cumulative logistic regression model was then constructed to identify independent associated factors, using a stepwise selection strategy with a p-value of 0.15 for entry and 0.05 to remain in the final model. Results were presented as odds ratios and 95% confidence intervals (CI). Sensitivity analyses included linear regression models with continuous post-PVR BVGFI as the outcome. Statistical analyses were performed using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Inc., Cary, North Carolina, USA) and R 4.03 (R Core Team, Vienna, Austria) [17]. A two-sided p-value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Normal BVGFI values

The normal cohort consisted of 136 individuals (mean age at CMR 16.6 ± 5.7 years; 50% males). Unadjusted mean BVGFI was 56.50 ± 5.13 (Supplemental Table 1). Age did not correlate with BVGFI (R = 0.096, p = 0.26) (Supplemental Fig. 1). The normal range of BVGFI, defined as ±2 SD of the mean, was 46.2 to 66.8. Based on the distribution of BVGFI in this cohort, BVGFI was categorized as normal (≥46.2), mildly to moderately depressed (40.0–46.1 [2–3 SDs below the mean]), or severely depressed (<40.0 [>3 SDs below the mean]).

Interobserver reliability of BVFGI

Interobserver reliability was good to excellent among all CMR parameters (Supplemental Table 2). The ICC was generally excellent for RV and LV volumes (0.97–0.99) and good for EFs and RV mass (0.83–0.87). BVGFI ICC was good at 0.82 (95% CI 0.67, 0.91). The mean BVGFI difference between readers was −2.6 ± 4.4, and 2 SD was 6 points.

Comparison between rTOF and normal values

Table 2 and Fig. 2 compare pre-PVR CMR parameters in rTOF patients with those in normal individuals. Although BVGFI was not found to be correlated with age, to ensure an unbiased comparison, age and sex covariate adjustments were applied to the comparison of the normal and rTOF cohorts. Compared with the normal cohort, mean (±standard error) sex-and-age-adjusted BVGFI was lower in rTOF (56.0 ± 0.5 vs 47.7 ± 0.6, p < 0.001).

Change in BVGFI associated with PVR

Table 2 and Fig. 2 show CMR parameters in the normal cohort and in patients with rTOF before and after PVR. Pre-PVR BVGFI was normal in 52% and depressed in 48% of patients with rTOF. Compared with pre-PVR values, after PVR 75% of rTOF patients (n = 100) maintained stable BVGFI or experienced an improvement, while 25% (n = 33) had a decline in BVGFI (Fig. 3).

Changes in categories of BVGFI from pre- to post-PVR. BVGFI biventricular global function index, PVR pulmonary valve replacement

Compared with pre-PVR parameters, there were significant reductions in RV volume, mass, and right ventricular global function index (RVGFI) with no significant change in RV EF after PVR. There was no significant change in mean BVGFI from pre- to post-PVR (46.6 ± 7.7 vs 45.6 ± 6.7, p = 0.28) (Table 2). Fig. 4 shows individual changes in BVGFI post-PVR. Most patients with normal pre-PVR BVGFI remained within the normal range post-PVR (n = 69, 64%), while 36% had a decline to mild-to-moderately depressed (33%) or severely depressed (3%). Of the 24 patients with initially severely depressed BVGFI, 50% remained severely depressed, while 46% had an improvement to mild-to-moderately depressed, and only one patient had a normalization of BVGFI post-PVR (Fig. 3). Fig. 5A shows a linear association between pre- and post-PVR BVGFI. Fig. 5B demonstrates the increase in the probability of severely depressed post-PVR BVGFI as a function of decreasing pre-PVR BVGFI, with pre-PVR BVGFI <44 as the optimal binary threshold to predict severely depressed post-PVR BVGFI (sensitivity 82%, specificity 73%, and accuracy 74%).

Pre-PVR correlates of severely depressed post-PVR BVGFI

Supplemental Table 3 summarizes pre-PVR factors associated with severely depressed BVGFI after PVR by univariate analysis. By multivariable analysis, the pre-PVR factors independently associated with severely depressed BVGFI post-PVR included: lower pre-PVR BVGFI; male sex; moderate or severe PR, and higher LV ESVi tertiles (model c-statistic 0.84) (Table 3). Post-PVR BVGFI explored as a continuous outcome revealed pre-PVR factors independently associated with a lower post-PVR BVGFI to be similar, including lower pre-PVR GFI, male sex, QRS duration ≥180 ms, higher pre-PVR LV ESVi, and longer duration between pre- and post-PVR CMRs (Supplemental Table 4).

Impact of pre-PVR type of hemodynamic load on post-PVR BVGFI

Though there were significant differences in mean RV volume, mass, and RVGFI between hemodynamic loading states, there was no significant difference in mean BVGFI between the groups (p = 0.21) (Table 1). Compared with pre-PVR values, post-PVR, BVGFI increased in the pressure overload group and decreased in the volume and mixed load groups (p = 0.017) (Supplemental Table 5).

Discussion

In this single-center observational study of young adult patients with rTOF undergoing PVR, we found that compared with age- and sex-adjusted values from patients with structurally normal hearts, mean BVGFI was significantly lower in patients with rTOF. There was no significant change in mean BVGFI from pre- to post-PVR in our rTOF cohort, as BVGFI remained stable after PVR in most patients. Multivariable analysis identified lower pre-PVR BVGFI to be the strongest pre-PVR factor associated with severely depressed post-PVR BVGFI. BVGFI is a biomarker of biventricular functional health that integrates LV and RVGFI in assessing global myocardial function. Studies in adult patients with coronary artery disease, heart failure, hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, end-stage renal disease, hematological disorders, and other conditions impacting the cardiovascular system have highlighted the ability of LVGFI to improve outcome prediction over traditional imaging biomarkers, such as EF and late gadolinium enhancement [18], [19], [20], [21], [22], [23], [24], [25], [26]. In pediatric patients, RVGFI has been shown to add prognostic information in patients with pulmonary hypertension [27], and in patients with functional single ventricle palliated with the Fontan procedure [28].

The advantages of BVGFI as an imaging biomarker may lie in the integration of both RV and LV pump function (stroke volume and EF) and ventricular volumes and mass, which more comprehensively represent global cardiac functional status and myocardial health. In patients with rTOF, BVGFI may better account for maladaptive changes in biventricular function, mass, and volumes resulting from long-standing RVOT dysfunction [27], [29]. More recently, a study from the INDICATOR cohort found that reduced BVGFI is an independent predictor of all-cause mortality in rTOF, displacing more traditional imaging biomarkers such as RV end-diastolic volume index and EF [11].

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first analysis to examine the impact of PVR on BVGFI in patients with rTOF. In evaluating the rTOF cohort, we found significant decreases in RV volumes, mass, and GFI with lesser changes in LV parameters after PVR. Overall, there was no significant change in mean BVGFI, likely as changes in the numerator (stroke volume) and denominator (volumes and mass) were largely proportional. Similarly, although RVGFI showed a significant reduction post-PVR, LVGFI showed a modest increase that did not reach statistical significance. This observation is most likely due to the substantial decline in RV volumes and mass after PVR relative to an increase in left ventricular end-diastolic volume index post-PVR.

While many patients remained within their pre-PVR BVGFI category, a subset experienced a notable decline in BVGFI following PVR. It can be hypothesized that patients with severely depressed pre-PVR BVGFI who did not improve and those with mild to moderately depressed BVGFI who deteriorated after PVR may represent patients in whom the maladaptive changes related to the consequences of prolonged RVOT dysfunction have become irreversible. This group of vulnerable patients may be theoretically identified before their myopathic changes become irreversible by utilizing BVGFI as an imaging biomarker for disease severity. In practice, clinicians may monitor CMR-measured BVGFI to detect a declining trend or patients reaching subnormal values and incorporate that information into decision-making regarding interventions such as PVR. Although this hypothesis requires confirmation, our study found that for every 1-unit decrease in pre-PVR BVGFI, the odds of a depressed post-PVR BVGFI increased by 21%.

Other pre-PVR factors independently associated with depressed post-PVR BVGFI were male sex, moderate or severe PR, and higher left ventricular end-systolic volume index. Numerous prior studies have identified moderate or severe PR to be an important hemodynamic abnormality in rTOF associated with adverse outcomes [30], [31]. Similarly, LV dilation and dysfunction manifesting as increased end-systolic volume have been associated with adverse clinical outcomes in other studies [8], [9]. The role of male sex as an independent factor associated with depressed post-PVR BVGFI was an interesting finding that requires further investigation with a larger cohort and is outside the scope of this retrospective study.

Limitations

This is a single-center retrospective study with a potential for reporting bias. Validation of our findings through multicenter studies would help ascertain both validity and generalizability. We further note that sources of RV volume overload other than PR (e.g., TR) were assessed qualitatively by echocardiography. Finally, this study did not explore the association between post-PVR BVGFI and major adverse clinical outcomes, as an association has previously been reported [10], [11].

Conclusion

BVGFI is lower in patients with rTOF compared with normal controls. There was no significant change in mean BVGFI from pre- to post-PVR in our rTOF cohort. Lower pre-PVR BVGFI, PR greater than 20%, male sex, and higher LV end-systolic volume index were independently associated with severely depressed BVGFI post-PVR. This study highlights BVGFI as a potential imaging biomarker that may inform refinements of the timing of PVR in patients with rTOF. Validation of the clinical utility of BVGFI requires a large-scale prospective study.
