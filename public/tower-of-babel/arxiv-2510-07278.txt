Universal initial state preparation for first quantized quantum simulations Preprint: APS/123-QED
Jack S. Baker 1, Gaurav Saxena 1, Thi Ha Kyaw 1

Abstract
Preparing symmetry-adapted initial states is a principal bottleneck in first-quantized quantum simulation. We present a universal approach that efficiently maps any polynomial-size superposition of occupation-number configurations to the first-quantized representation on a digital quantum computer. The method exploits the Jordan–Schwinger Lie algebra homomorphism, which identifies number-conserving second-quantized operators with their first-quantized action and induces an equivariant bijection between Fock occupations and 𝔰 ​ 𝔲 ​ ( d ) \mathfrak{su}(d) weight states within the Schur–Weyl decomposition. Operationally, we prepare an encoded superposition of Schur labels via a block-encoded linear combination of unitaries and then apply the inverse quantum Schur transform. The algorithm runs in time poly ⁡ ( L , N , d , log ⁡ ϵ − 1 ) \mathrm{poly}(L,N,d,\log\epsilon^{-1}) for L L configurations of N N particles over d d modes to accuracy ϵ \epsilon , and applies universally to fermions, bosons, and Green’s paraparticles in arbitrary single-particle bases. Resource estimates indicate practicality within leading first-quantized pipelines; statistics-aware or faster quantum Schur transforms promise further reductions.

To summarize, we have presented a universal, fault-tolerant protocol for symmetry-adapted state preparation in first quantization. Exploiting the Jordan–Schwinger homomorphism, we have identified number-conserving second-quantized operators with their first-quantized action and obtained an equivariant bijection between Fock occupations and
\mathfrak{su}(d)
weight states within the Schur–Weyl decomposition. This enables the preparation of arbitrary polynomial-size superpositions of particle configurations via block-encoding an LCU over encoded Schur labels, followed by an inverse quantum Schur transform, with time complexity
\mathrm{poly}(L,N,d,\log\epsilon^{-1})
. Resource estimates place the Toffoli-equivalent cost within the same order of magnitude as leading first-quantized pipelines. Beyond state preparation, our framework suggests three avenues to further advance quantum simulations in first quantization: (i) a universal, coherent quantization transform that toggles on-device between second and first quantization; (ii) block-diagonalization and potential fast-forwarding of first-quantized Hamiltonians using the quantum Schur transform (e.g.,
Burkat and Fitzpatrick 2025
;
Gu et al. 2021
); and (iii) Schur-basis algorithms exploiting the Wigner–Eckart theorem, for example to factor matrix elements and shrink measurement budgets. These advances broaden the scope of first-quantized simulation across chemistry, materials, and fundamental physics.

II
Mathematical Preliminaries

This section provides a brief overview of groups, group representations, and the Schur-Weyl duality, thus providing a short primer to help navigate the main article and this supplement.

Definition 1

A group
G
is a set together with a law of composition and having the following properties:

1.

The law of composition obeys associativity, i.e.,
(ab)c=a(bc),\,\forall\,a,b,c\in G
.

2.

G
contains an identity element
e
such that
ea=ae=a,\,\forall\,a\in G
.

3.

Every element
a\in G
must have an inverse
b\in G
such that
ab=ba=e
.

A group is called
abelian
if the law of composition is commutative.
The
order
of a group is the number of elements it contains. If the order is finite (infinite),
G
is said to be a finite (infinite) group.

Lie groups
are an example of infinite groups that are also continuous. Formally, they are defined as groups that are also smooth differentiable manifolds.
Every Lie group gives rise to a Lie algebra, which is a vector space
\mathfrak{g}
equipped with an operation called the Lie bracket, which is an alternating bilinear map
\mathfrak{g}\times\mathfrak{g}\to\mathfrak{g}
that satisfies the Jacobi identity.

The following are some of the groups used in the main text:

1.

GL(d)
denotes the
d\times d
general linear group, the group of all
d\times d
invertible matrices. We denote the group by
GL(d,\mathbb{R})
or
GL(d,\mathbb{C})
when we have to indicate that we are working with real or complex matrices, respectively.

2.

U(d)
denotes the unitary group, the group of all
d\times d
unitary matrices. It is also a subgroup of
GL(d,\mathbb{C})
.

3.

SU(d)
or the special unitary group, denotes a subgroup of
U(d)
which consists of
d\times d
unitary matrices with determinant equal to 1.

4.

S_{n}
denotes the symmetric group, the group of permutations of the set of indices
\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
.

Groups, as abstractly defined above, play an important role in many physical theories, especially where symmetries are present.
To take advantage of this mathematical framework and understand more properties of the systems involved, we need to establish a correspondence between the symmetries of the physical systems and mathematical structures while faithfully preserving the underlying physics. This correspondence is known as a homomorphism, and the group structure and the symmetry properties of the physical system will be represented using matrices. We give formal definitions below.

Definition 2

Given two groups
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
, a group homomorphism is a map
f:G_{1}\to G_{2}
that preserves the group structure. That is for any
g,h\in G_{1}
, the map
f
satisfies

f(gh)=f(g)f(h)

(S1)

Definition 3

A matrix representation of a group
G
is a homomorphism
f:G\to GL(d)
, mapping every element
g\in G
to a matrix
f(g)\in GL(d)
.

We will call the
matrix representation
of
G
simply as the
representation
of
G
.
For a given representation
f:G\to GL(d)
, we call a subspace
\mathcal{H}(d^{\prime})
of the Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}(d)
(where
d^{\prime}\leq d
), a
G
-
invariant subspace
, if for all
v\in\mathcal{H}(d^{\prime})
and all
g\in G
, the vector
f(g)\cdot v
is also in
\mathcal{H}(d^{\prime})
.
A representation is called
irreducible
, or an
irrep
, if the only G-invariant subspaces are the empty subspace and the entire subspace
\mathcal{H}(d)
.
For finite groups, Maschke’s theorem states that every representation on a nonzero, finite-dimensional complex vector space is a direct sum of irreducible representations.
So, if
\mathcal{H}(d)
is a direct sum of
G
-invariant subspaces,
\mathcal{H}(d_{1})
and
\mathcal{H}(d_{2})
, the representation
f
on
\mathcal{H}(d)
is given by the direct sum of its restrictions to
\mathcal{H}(d_{1})
and
\mathcal{H}(d_{2})
, and is written as
f=f_{1}\oplus f_{2}
, where
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
are
f
’s restrictions on
\mathcal{H}(d_{1})
and
\mathcal{H}(d_{2})
, respectively. Moreover, the matrix representation
f(g)
, denoted F, will have a block form

F=\begin{pmatrix}F_{1}&0\\
0&F_{2}\end{pmatrix}

(S2)

where
F_{1}
and
F_{2}
are matrix representations of
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
, respectively.
A direct sum of irreducible (also called simple) representations is also known as a completely reducible (or a semisimple) representation.
Interested readers may find more insights in this vast subject in many standard textbooks such as
Fulton and Harris 2004
;
Sengupta 2012
. Next, we briefly discuss the Schur-Weyl duality that is central to the results of the main article.

For a given semisimple representation, we can express it in terms of unique or non-isomorphic irreducible representations by using the isotypic components. The isotypic component is the direct sum of all isomorphic subrepresentations.
Let
\{W_{1},W_{2},\ldots,W_{k}\}
be a complete list of unique irreducible representations of
G
. We can define the isotypic component
F^{(i)}
corresponding to each
W_{i}
as

F^{(i)}\cong W_{i}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{w_{i}}

(S3)

which represents that
W_{i}
has multiplicity
w_{i}
in the decomposition of the representation
F
.
Then, the representation
F
can be decomposed as

F\cong\underset{i}{\oplus}F^{(i)}

(S4)

which is known as the isotypic decomposition of
F
.
Such an isotypic decomposition can be found for the representation of both the symmetric and the unitary groups.
Denoting the representation of the unitary group
U(d)
as
V^{U(d)}
and the representation of the symmetric group
S_{n}
on the space
(\mathbb{C}^{d})^{\otimes n}
as
V^{S_{n}}
, the isotypic decomposition of the representation of their combined action (i.e., the group
U(d)\times S_{n}
) can be written as

V^{U(d)}V^{S_{n}}\cong\underset{i}{\oplus}\underset{j}{\oplus}V^{U(d)}_{i}\otimes V_{j}^{S_{n}}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{v_{i,j}}

(S5)

where
v_{i,j}
denotes the multiplicity of the irrep
V^{U(d)}_{i}\otimes V_{j}^{S_{n}}
, and
V^{U(d)}_{i}
and
V_{j}^{S_{n}}
denote the non-isomorphic irreps of
U(d)
and
S_{n}
, respectively.
Further, due to the commuting properties of the two groups, it can be shown that the multiplicities
v_{i,j}
are either zero or one; thus, the above equation can be simplified as

V^{U(d)}V^{S_{n}}\cong\underset{\lambda}{\oplus}V^{U(d)}_{\lambda}\otimes V_{\lambda}^{S_{n}}

(S6)

where
\lambda
runs over some unspecified set.
The Schur-Weyl duality provides a characterization of the above
\lambda
in terms of the Young diagrams with
n
boxes and at most
d
rows.
A detailed discussion and proof can be found in
Fulton and Harris 2004
.

From Dynkin Weights to Gelfand–Tsetlin Patterns

We describe a deterministic, polynomial-time procedure that maps a permissible Dynkin weight
z
of an
\mathfrak{su}(d)
irrep with highest weight
\zeta
(equivalently, Young diagram
\lambda
with at most
d
rows) to a valid Gelfand–Tsetlin (GT) pattern of shape
\lambda
. The procedure returns one GT pattern among the (possibly many) patterns associated with a degenerate weight, which is sufficient for our purposes.

Let
\zeta=(\zeta_{1},\dots,\zeta_{d-1})
denote the
\mathfrak{su}(d)
Dynkin labels of the highest weight, and let
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{d})
be the corresponding partition (Young diagram row lengths), related by

\lambda_{d}=0,\qquad\lambda_{i}=\zeta_{i}+\lambda_{i+1}\quad(i=d{-}1,\dots,1).

(S13)

Given a Dynkin weight
z=(z_{1},\dots,z_{d-1})
within the irrep, write the corresponding standard weight components as
\bm{\omega}=(\omega_{1},\dots,\omega_{d})
, defined by
z_{i}=\omega_{i}-\omega_{i+1}
together with the total-sum constraint
\sum_{i=1}^{d}\omega_{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\lambda_{i}
. Solving,

\omega_{d}\;=\;\frac{1}{d}\!\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d}\lambda_{i}-\sum_{i=1}^{d-1}i\,z_{i}\right),\qquad\omega_{i}\;=\;z_{i}+\omega_{i+1}\ (i=d{-}1,\dots,1).

(S14)

For a GT pattern with top row
\lambda
and rows
x_{r,1}\geq\cdots\geq x_{r,r}
(
r=1,\dots,d
), the interlacing constraints are

x_{r+1,j}\ \geq\ x_{r,j}\ \geq\ x_{r+1,j+1}\qquad(r=1,\dots,d{-}1;\ j=1,\dots,r),

(S15)

and the row-sum differences encode the weight:

\sum_{j=1}^{d}x_{d,j}-\sum_{j=1}^{d-1}x_{d-1,j}=\omega_{d},\quad\sum_{j=1}^{d-1}x_{d-1,j}-\sum_{j=1}^{d-2}x_{d-2,j}=\omega_{d-1},\ \dots,\ x_{1,1}=\omega_{1}.

V.4

At fixed level

M_{s}=\binom{s}{2},

(S27)

two-level operations (for example via Givens/QR decomposition). We encode the

n_{s}=\lceil\log_{2}s\rceil

, worst case

, which yields per two-level operation

E_{s}=2n_{s}-1

(S28)

address-selective, label-controlled single-qubit rotations. Let
k_{\lambda\mu}
be the control width from the
(\lambda,\mu)

k_{\mathrm{addr}}(s)=n_{s}-1
be the address-control width; and set

k_{\mathrm{tot}}(s)=k_{\lambda\mu}+k_{\mathrm{addr}}(s).

(S29)

We implement

C_{\mathrm{Tof}}(k;a)\leq 2k-3\quad\text{for }a\geq k-2\ \text{ clean ancillas}.

(S30)

We split the global error budget

\varepsilon\;=\;\varepsilon_{\mathrm{rot}}\;+\;\varepsilon_{\mathrm{arith}},\qquad\varepsilon_{\mathrm{rot}}=\tfrac{1}{2}\varepsilon,\ \ \varepsilon_{\mathrm{arith}}=\tfrac{1}{2}\varepsilon.

(S31)

Across one CG step, the number of controlled single-qubit rotation invocations is

K_{\mathrm{rot}}^{\mathrm{(CG)}}\;=\;\sum_{s=2}^{d}\binom{s}{2}\,E_{s},

(S32)

and across the
(N{-}1)

K_{\mathrm{rot}}\;=\;(N-1)\sum_{s=2}^{d}\binom{s}{2}\,E_{s}.

(S33)

By submultiplicativity and a telescoping expansion, a per-invocation synthesis tolerance
\delta_{\mathrm{rot}}
accumulates at most additively, so we set

\delta_{\mathrm{rot}}\;=\;\varepsilon_{\mathrm{rot}}/K_{\mathrm{rot}}.

(S34)

We adopt direct single-qubit rotation synthesis

T_{\mathrm{dir}}(\delta)=\alpha_{\mathrm{dir}}\log_{2}(1/\delta)+\beta_{\mathrm{dir}},\qquad\alpha_{\mathrm{dir}}=1.149,\ \ \beta_{\mathrm{dir}}=9.2,

(S35)

so a controlled rotation at tolerance
\delta_{\mathrm{rot}}

T_{\mathrm{cR}}^{\mathrm{dir}}(\delta_{\mathrm{rot}})\;=\;2\,T_{\mathrm{dir}}(\delta_{\mathrm{rot}}/2)\;=\;2\!\left[\alpha_{\mathrm{dir}}\log_{2}\!\frac{2}{\delta_{\mathrm{rot}}}+\beta_{\mathrm{dir}}\right].

(S36)

Each enable therefore consists of: flag-set via one
C_{\mathrm{Tof}}\!\big(k_{\mathrm{tot}}(s);a\big)

T_{\mathrm{cR}}^{\mathrm{dir}}(\delta_{\mathrm{rot}})

\widetilde{T}_{2\mathrm{lvl}}(s)=E_{s}\left[\,2\,C_{\mathrm{Tof}}\!\big(k_{\mathrm{tot}}(s);a\big)+\frac{T_{\mathrm{cR}}^{\mathrm{dir}}(\delta_{\mathrm{rot}})}{7}\right],

(S37)

and the level-

\mathrm{TE}_{s}^{(\mathrm{comp})}=M_{s}\,\widetilde{T}_{2\mathrm{lvl}}(s)=\binom{s}{2}\,(2n_{s}-1)\!\left[\,2\,C_{\mathrm{Tof}}\!\big(k_{\mathrm{tot}}(s);a\big)+\frac{T_{\mathrm{cR}}^{\mathrm{dir}}(\delta_{\mathrm{rot}})}{7}\right].

(S38)

V.5

The arithmetic that computes rotation angles must respect the remaining error budget. We budget per invocation an angle-quantization allowance

\varepsilon_{\theta}\;=\;\varepsilon_{\mathrm{arith}}/K_{\mathrm{rot}},

(S39)

\lvert\theta-\hat{\theta}\rvert\leq\pi\,2^{-f}\leq\varepsilon_{\theta}

f\;=\;\left\lceil\log_{2}\!\Big(\pi/\varepsilon_{\theta}\Big)\right\rceil\;=\;\left\lceil\log_{2}\!\Big(\tfrac{\pi\,K_{\mathrm{rot}}}{\varepsilon_{\mathrm{arith}}}\Big)\right\rceil.

(S40)

Intermediate products and divisions during the evaluation of the reduced-Wigner block require guard bits to cover products of

g(s)\;=\;\left\lceil\log_{2}s\right\rceil+3,

(S41)

and bound the integer dynamic range of shifted GT differences by

i(N,s)\;=\;\left\lceil\log_{2}(2N+2s+1)\right\rceil+2.

(S42)

The working word size (signed two’s-complement) used throughout rank

w(s)\;=\;i(N,s)\;+\;f\;+\;g(s).

(S43)

\widehat{T}^{\mu,j,\mu^{\prime},j^{\prime}}=\begin{cases}\displaystyle S(j{-}j^{\prime})\left[\frac{\Big(\prod_{i\neq j}\big(\tilde{\mu}_{i}-\tilde{\mu}^{\prime}_{j^{\prime}}+1\big)\Big)\Big(\prod_{i\neq j^{\prime}}\big(\tilde{\mu}^{\prime}_{i}-\tilde{\mu}_{j}+1\big)\Big)}{\Big(\prod_{i\neq j}\big(\tilde{\mu}_{i}-\tilde{\mu}_{j}+1\big)\Big)\Big(\prod_{i\neq j^{\prime}}\big(\tilde{\mu}^{\prime}_{i}-\tilde{\mu}^{\prime}_{j^{\prime}}+1\big)\Big)}\right]^{\!1/2},&j^{\prime}\in\{1,\ldots,s{-}1\},\\[11.38109pt]

(S45)

We use standard reversible primitives, counting TE as number of Toffolis since these routines use only Toffoli and Clifford. CDKM ripple-carry add/subtract
Cuccaro et al. 2004
has cost

A(w)=2w-1\quad\text{TE},\qquad\text{ancilla: }0,

(S46)

schoolbook shift-and-add multiplication with
w

M(w)=2w^{2}+w\quad\text{TE},\qquad\text{ancilla: }0,

(S47)

Newton reciprocal with a unit-cost seed

I_{\mathrm{rec}}(w)=\left\lceil\log_{2}w\right\rceil+2,\qquad C_{\mathrm{recip}}(w)=I_{\mathrm{rec}}(w)\,\big(2\,M(w)+3\,A(w)\big),

(S48)

and Newton square-root uses

I_{\sqrt{\phantom{.}}}(w)=\left\lceil\log_{2}w\right\rceil+2,\qquad C_{\sqrt{\phantom{.}}}(w)=I_{\sqrt{\phantom{.}}}(w)\,\Big(C_{\mathrm{recip}}(w)+M(w)+2A(w)\Big).

(S49)

Angles are obtained by reversible CORDIC in vectoring mode with

(S50)

We evaluate
\widehat{T}^{[s]}
once per rank using a streaming, space–time balanced plan that avoids large caches. First, we form the cross-row differences

\mathcal{D}^{(1)}_{i,t}=\tilde{\mu}_{i}-\tilde{\mu}^{\prime}_{t}\ \ (i\in[s],\,t\in[s-1]),\qquad\mathcal{D}^{(2)}_{u,v}=\tilde{\mu}^{\prime}_{u}-\tilde{\mu}_{v}\ \ (u\in[s-1],\,v\in[s]),

(S51)

together with the within-row/within-column differences required in the denominators of Eq.

C_{\mathrm{diff}}^{[s]}\;=\;2\,s(s-1)\,A\!\big(w(s)\big).

(S52)

Next, for each of the

C_{\mathrm{entry}}^{\mathrm{stream}}(w)=8\,M(w)+C_{\mathrm{recip}}(w)+C_{\sqrt{\phantom{.}}}(w)+5\,A(w),\qquad C_{\mathrm{entries}}^{[s]}=s(s-1)\,C_{\mathrm{entry}}^{\mathrm{stream}}\!\big(w(s)\big).

(S53)

Finally, the compiled two-level sequence requires

C_{\mathrm{angles}}^{[s]}\;=\;\binom{s}{2}\,C_{\mathrm{CORDIC}}\!\big(w(s)\big)\;=\;\frac{3}{2}\,s(s-1)\,f\,A\!\big(w(s)\big).

(S54)

The per-rank online evaluation cost is

C_{\mathrm{eval}}^{[s]}(s;N,\varepsilon)=2s(s-1)\,A\!\big(w(s)\big)+s(s-1)\,C_{\mathrm{entry}}^{\mathrm{stream}}\!\big(w(s)\big)+\tfrac{3}{2}\,s(s-1)\,f\,A\!\big(w(s)\big).

(S55)

V.6
Per-rank and overall toffoli-equivalent cost

The level-

\mathrm{TE}_{s}(d,N,\varepsilon)\;=\;\binom{s}{2}\,(2n_{s}-1)\!\left[\,2\,C_{\mathrm{Tof}}\!\big(k_{\mathrm{tot}}(s);a\big)+\frac{T_{\mathrm{cR}}^{\mathrm{dir}}(\delta_{\mathrm{rot}})}{7}\right]\;+\;C_{\mathrm{eval}}^{[s]}(s;N,\varepsilon),

(S56)

and

\mathrm{TE}\!\left(U_{d}\text{-CG}(d,N,\varepsilon)\right)=\sum_{s=2}^{d}\mathrm{TE}_{s}(d,N,\varepsilon),\qquad\mathrm{TE}\!\left(\mathrm{Schur}(d,N,\varepsilon)\right)\leq(N{-}1)\sum_{s=2}^{d}\mathrm{TE}_{s}(d,N,\varepsilon).

(S57)

V.7
Ancilla accounting for arithmetic

We use a streaming strategy for memory usage to avoid the need for a large cache. A conservative bound for the arithmetic work space at rank
s

a_{\mathrm{arith}}^{[s]}\;\leq\;12\,w(s)\quad\text{qubits}

(S58)

as inferred by the number of aforementioned arithmetic operations required to obtain a single rotation angle. These arithmetic ancillas are separate from the clean-ancilla bank used for multi-controls in Eq.
S30
.

V.8
Total qubit accounting

We separate persistent
system

Q_{\mathrm{sys}}(d,N)\;=\;N\,n_{d}\;+\;n_{\lambda}\;+\;n_{\mu}\;+\;n_{\sigma},

(S59)

with register sizes taken from Sec.
V.1

At rank

s

. (ii) Multi-controlled operations use a clean-ancilla bank for the linear-cost construction in Eq.
S30

a_{\mathrm{mcx}}^{\min}(s)\;=\;\max\{0,\,k_{\mathrm{tot}}(s)-2\}\;=\;\max\{0,\,k_{\lambda\mu}+n_{s}-3\}.

(S60)

If a fixed bank

a_{\mathrm{mcx}}^{\mathrm{use}}(s)\;=\;\max\!\big\{\,a_{\mathrm{mcx}}^{\mathrm{prov}},\;a_{\mathrm{mcx}}^{\min}(s)\,\big\}.

(S61)

(iii) The streaming arithmetic that evaluates
\widehat{T}^{[s]}
(Step 3) requires only rolling accumulators and a constant number of temporaries; with the working word size
w(s)
from Eq.
S43
we bounded the arithmetic qubits in Eq.
S58

The transient ancilla at rank

Q_{\mathrm{anc}}^{[s]}\;=\;n_{s}\;+\;a_{\mathrm{mcx}}^{\mathrm{use}}(s)\;+\;a_{\mathrm{arith}}^{[s]},

(S62)

and the overall peak qubit demand of the quantum Schur transform is

Q_{\mathrm{total}}(d,N,\varepsilon;\,a_{\mathrm{mcx}}^{\mathrm{prov}})\;=\;Q_{\mathrm{sys}}(d,N)\;+\;\max_{2\leq s\leq d}\;Q_{\mathrm{anc}}^{[s]}.

(S63)

All ancillas are returned to
\lvert 0\rangle

VI
Non-Clifford resource estimation for block encoding

VI.1
LCU with fixed
\lambda
and
\sigma

In our implementation we fix the statistics sector
\lambda
and a single copy label
\sigma\in V^{S_{N}}_{\lambda}
once at the beginning using only Clifford operations and perform the LCU only over the GT label
\mu
. Concretely,

\ket{\lambda}\ket{0^{\,n_{\mu}}}\ket{\sigma}\ \xrightarrow{\ \text{LCU}\ }\ \ket{\lambda}\!\left(\sum_{i=1}^{L}c_{i}\,\ket{\mu_{i}}\right)\!\ket{\sigma},

(S64)

with normalization
\sum_{i=1}^{L}|c_{i}|^{2}=1
. The LCU operator has the form

B^{\lambda,\sigma}:=\sum_{\mu\in\mathcal{I}_{\lambda}}c_{\mu}\,X_{\mu},\qquad B^{\lambda,\sigma}\ket{0^{\,n_{\mu}}}=\sum_{\mu}c_{\mu}\ket{\mu},

(S65)

where
X_{\mu}
is a Pauli string of
X
and
I
acting only on the
\mu
-register (no flips on
\lambda
or
\sigma
).
This specialization is justified because we treat only one type of particle statistic at a time and the Jordan–Schwinger map is not sensitive to the symmetric group copy (see Table
S1
to see this explicitly).

The length of
X_{\mu}
bit string equals the
\mu
-register width
n_{\mu}
. You may choose either the naive or compressed encodings introduced in Section
IV
.

VI.2
Cost of
PREP
and uncompute

PREP
prepares the address superposition

\textsc{PREP}:\quad\ket{0^{\,b}}\longmapsto\sum_{j=0}^{L-1}\sqrt{\frac{|c_{j}|}{\ell_{1}}}\,\ket{j},

(S66)

We implement
PREP
using clean-ancilla QROAM together with coherent alias sampling
Berry et al. 2019b
, following the construction summarized in Appendix B of Ref.
Baker et al. 2024b
.

Parameters

We first define the alt (
\beta
) and keep (
R
) bits

\beta=\Big\lceil\log_{2}\!L\Big\rceil,\qquad R=\Big\lceil\log_{2}\frac{1}{\varepsilon_{\text{prep}}}\Big\rceil,

(S67)

and include a one-bit sign flag in the QROAM output. The QROAM output word size is then

m\;=\;\beta+R+s_{\mathrm{sign}}.

(S68)

Let
k_{1},k_{2}
be the (power-of-two) QROAM blocking parameters for compute and uncompute, and
b_{r}
the phase-gradient width used by the success-boost rotation (we take
b_{r}=7
as in Ref.
Baker et al. 2024b
).

Toffoli(-equivalent) counts.

With the clean-ancilla construction,
PREP
and its uncompute
PREP
†
use only Toffoli+Clifford, so TE equals the Toffoli count:

\mathrm{TE}_{\textsc{PREP}+\textsc{PREP}^{\dagger}}(L,R;k_{1},k_{2},b_{r})=\ 2\Big\lceil\frac{L}{2k_{1}}\Big\rceil+m(k_{1}-1)\;+\;\Big\lceil\frac{L}{2k_{2}}\Big\rceil+k_{2}+\ 2\Big[\,3(\beta{+}1)-3\,v_{2}(L)+2b_{r}-9\,\Big]+\ 2R+\ 2\beta.

(S69)

The terms on the right hand side of Eq.
S69
capture: the QROAM compute pass, QROAM uncompute pass, uniform-superposition and success-boost overheads (including the dependence on the
2
-adic valuation
v_{2}(L)
), the keep inequality tests, and the controlled swaps.

Near-optimal blocking.

A one-line choice that is near-optimal for the
\lceil L/(2k)\rceil+m(k-1)
tradeoff is

k_{1}=k_{2}=k^{\star}(L,m):=2^{\,\mathrm{round}\!\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\log_{2}\!\frac{L}{2m}\right)},\qquad 1\leq k^{\star}\leq\Big\lfloor\frac{L}{2}\Big\rfloor.

(S70)

Additional qubits.

The extra ancillas used by
PREP
(beyond the system registers and the address
b
) are

Q_{\textsc{PREP-anc}}(L,R;k_{1},k_{2},b_{r})=6+2(\beta+R)+b_{r}+\max\!\Big(m(k_{1}{-}1)+\big\lceil\log_{2}\!\tfrac{L}{2k_{1}}\big\rceil,\ \ k_{2}+\big\lceil\log_{2}\!\tfrac{L}{2k_{2}}\big\rceil\Big).

(S71)

VI.3
Cost of
SEL

SEL
receives the address

j\in\{0,\ldots,L-1\}
(prepared by
PREP
) and applies the Pauli string
X_{\mu_{j}}
only to the
GT register of width
n_{\mu}
qubits. We implement this via a QROAM lookup that outputs the
length-
n_{\mu}
XOR mask describing
X_{\mu_{j}}
and then conditionally XORs that mask into the
\mu

Toffoli-equivalent cost.

We require two QROAM passes (compute then uncompute) with Toffoli-equivalent costs:

\mathrm{TE}_{\text{QROAM, comp}}\;=\;\Big\lceil\frac{n_{\mu}}{k^{\prime}_{1}}\Big\rceil\;+\;\frac{n_{\mu}}{2}\,(k^{\prime}_{1}-1),\qquad\mathrm{TE}_{\text{QROAM, uncmp}}\;=\;\Big\lceil\frac{n_{\mu}}{k^{\prime}_{2}}\Big\rceil\;+\;k^{\prime}_{2},

(S72)

so that the total cost is

\mathrm{TE}_{\textsc{SEL}}(n_{\mu};k^{\prime}_{1},k^{\prime}_{2})=\Big\lceil\frac{n_{\mu}}{k^{\prime}_{1}}\Big\rceil\;+\;\frac{n_{\mu}}{2}\,(k^{\prime}_{1}-1)\;+\;\Big\lceil\frac{n_{\mu}}{k^{\prime}_{2}}\Big\rceil\;+\;k^{\prime}_{2}.

(S73)

A near-optimal choice that balances the standard space-time tradeoff is given by Eq.
S70
.

Ancilla (qubit) cost.

We require

Q_{\textsc{SEL-anc}}(n_{\mu};k^{\prime}_{1},k^{\prime}_{2})=\max\!\left(\ n_{\mu}\,(k^{\prime}_{1}-1)\;+\;\Big\lceil\log_{2}\!\frac{n_{\mu}}{k^{\prime}_{1}}\Big\rceil,\ \ k^{\prime}_{2}\;+\;\Big\lceil\log_{2}\!\frac{n_{\mu}}{k^{\prime}_{2}}\Big\rceil\right).

(S74)

VI.4
Repeat-until-success

We analyze the simple, measurement-based realization of the block encoding in which the ancilla is measured and we postselect on the all-zero outcome. We call this the repeat-until-success (RUS) variant. Recall that we hold
\lambda
and
\sigma
fixed and only construct the LCU over the GT labels, so
B^{\lambda,\sigma}=\sum_{\mu}c_{\mu}X_{\mu}
and
\ell_{1}:=\sum_{\mu}|c_{\mu}|
.
Define the composite

\mathcal{U}\;:=\;\textsc{PREP}^{\dagger}\cdot\textsc{SEL}\cdot\textsc{PREP}.

(S75)

Acting on the initialized state
\ket{0^{\,b}}\ket{0^{\,n_{\mu}}}
,
\mathcal{U}
has the standard LCU block structure

\mathcal{U}\big(\ket{0^{\,b}}\otimes\ket{\phi}\big)\;=\;\ket{0^{\,b}}\otimes\frac{B^{\lambda,\sigma}}{\ell_{1}}\ket{\phi}\;+\;\ket{\text{garbage}_{\perp}},

(S76)

for any system input
\ket{\phi}
. Measuring the ancilla in the computational basis and postselecting on
\ket{0^{\,b}}
succeeds with probability

p_{\mathrm{succ}}(\phi)\;=\;\left\|\frac{B^{\lambda,\sigma}}{\ell_{1}}\ket{\phi}\right\|^{2}\;\leq\;\frac{\|B^{\lambda,\sigma}\|^{2}}{\ell_{1}^{2}}.

(S77)

In our state-preparation use case with
\ket{\phi}=\ket{0^{\,n_{\mu}}}
and
X_{\mu}\ket{0^{\,n_{\mu}}}=\ket{\mu}
, we have
\left\|B^{\lambda,\sigma}\ket{0^{\,n_{\mu}}}\right\|^{2}=\sum_{\mu}|c_{\mu}|^{2}=1
, hence

p_{\mathrm{succ}}=\frac{1}{\ell_{1}^{2}},\qquad\mathbb{E}[\text{\# attempts}]=\ell_{1}^{2}.

(S78)

Toffoli-equivalent cost per successful block encoding.

One attempt consists of a single application of
\textsc{PREP}\rightarrow\textsc{SEL}\rightarrow\textsc{PREP}^{\dagger}
followed by an ancilla measurement (Clifford). Hence the TE cost per attempt is

\mathrm{TE}_{\text{attempt}}\;=\;\mathrm{TE}_{\textsc{PREP}+\textsc{PREP}^{\dagger}}(L,R;k_{1},k_{2},b_{r})\;+\;\mathrm{TE}_{\textsc{SEL}}(n_{\mu};\,k^{\prime}_{1},k^{\prime}_{2}).

(S79)

Combining Eqs.
S78
and
S79
gives the expected TE cost per successful block encoding

\boxed{\ \mathrm{TE}_{\text{RUS}}^{(\text{na\"{\i}ve})}\;=\;\ell_{1}^{2}\,\Big[\mathrm{TE}_{\textsc{PREP}+\textsc{PREP}^{\dagger}}(L,R;k_{1},k_{2},b_{r})\;+\;\mathrm{TE}_{\textsc{SEL}}(n_{\mu};\,k^{\prime}_{1},k^{\prime}_{2})\Big].}

(S80)

Qubit accounting.

Repeat-until-success does not increase the peak ancilla footprint beyond that of a single attempt, since the same ancillas are reused across attempts. Thus the additional qubits are exactly those reported for
PREP
(Eq.
S71
) and
SEL
(Eq.
S74
), plus one measurement flag bit, which is immediately reused.

VI.5
Oblivious amplitude amplification

The LCU block encoding can be made fully coherent by oblivious amplitude amplification (OAA)
Berry et al. 2015
;
Gilyén et al. 2019
.
Let
\Pi:=\ket{0^{\,b}}\!\bra{0^{\,b}}\otimes I
denote the projector onto the all-zero
address ancilla (
b=\lceil\log_{2}L\rceil
).

Define the oblivious Grover iterate

\mathcal{G}\;:=\;-\,\mathcal{U}\,(\mathsf{I}-2\Pi)\,\mathcal{U}^{\dagger}\,(\mathsf{I}-2\Pi),

(S81)

which acts as a rotation by angle
2\theta
in the two-dimensional subspace spanned by the
success and garbage branches, where
\sin\theta=1/\ell_{1}
.
After
r
iterations,

\mathcal{G}^{\,r}\,\mathcal{U}\big(\ket{0^{\,b}}\otimes\ket{\phi}\big)\;=\;\ket{0^{\,b}}\otimes\big[\sin((2r{+}1)\theta)\,\tfrac{B^{\lambda,\sigma}}{\ell_{1}}\ket{\phi}\big]\;+\;(\cdots)_{\perp}.

(S82)

Choosing

r^{\star}\;=\;\left\lceil\frac{\frac{\pi}{2}-\theta}{2\theta}\right\rceil\;=\;\left\lceil\frac{\pi}{4}\,\ell_{1}-\frac{1}{2}+O(\ell_{1}^{-1})\right\rceil

(S83)

drives
\sin((2r^{\star}{+}1)\theta)
close to
1
; with a final phase-adjusted step (Høyer’s exact amplification
Høyer 2000
) one can make the success amplitude exactly
1
.

Reflections and Toffoli cost.

Each Grover iterate
\mathcal{G}
uses two calls to
\mathcal{U}
and two ancilla-only reflections
(\mathsf{I}-2\Pi)
.
Implement
(\mathsf{I}-2\Pi)
as a phase flip on
\ket{0^{\,b}}
using the linear clean-ancilla
construction
Barenco et al. 1995
: a
b
-controlled phase at Toffoli cost

C_{0}(b;a)\;=\;2b-3\qquad\text{for a clean-ancilla bank }a\geq b-2.

(S84)

A single call to
\mathcal{U}
(or
\mathcal{U}^{\dagger}
) costs, by Eq.
S79
,

\mathrm{TE}_{\mathcal{U}}\;=\;\mathrm{TE}_{\textsc{PREP}+\textsc{PREP}^{\dagger}}(L,R;k_{1},k_{2},b_{r})\;+\;\mathrm{TE}_{\textsc{SEL}}(n_{\mu};\,k^{\prime}_{1},k^{\prime}_{2}).

(S85)

Total Toffoli-equivalent cost (near-deterministic OAA).

Starting from
\ket{0^{\,b}}\ket{\phi}
, the amplified implementation uses one leading
\mathcal{U}
,
then
r
iterates of
\mathcal{G}
:

\boxed{\ \mathrm{TE}_{\text{OAA}}\;=\;(2r{+}1)\,\mathrm{TE}_{\mathcal{U}}\;+\;2r\,C_{0}(b;a),\qquad b=\lceil\log_{2}L\rceil,\ \ r=r^{\star}.}

(S86)

Using the small-angle approximation
\theta\simeq 1/\ell_{1}
yields

\mathrm{TE}_{\text{OAA}}\;\approx\;\left(\frac{\pi}{2}\,\ell_{1}\right)\mathrm{TE}_{U}\;+\;\left(\frac{\pi}{2}\,\ell_{1}\right)\!(2b-3).

(S87)

Ancilla.

OAA reuses the same ancilla as a single
\mathcal{U}
call (Section
VI.2
and
Section
VI.3
). The two reflections require a clean-ancilla bank of size

\max\{0,b-2\}
to attain the Toffoli cost
2b-3
in Eq.
S84
; these
qubits can be shared with the multi-control bank already provisioned for
SEL
.
No additional persistent qubits are needed; the peak footprint equals that of one
\mathcal{U}
call plus
the multi-control bank for the ancilla reflection.

VII
End-to-end cost of universal state preparation in first quantization

We summarize the total Toffoli-equivalent cost and peak qubit footprint for our
universal state-preparation pipeline:

\text{(i) LCU block encoding over }\mu\text{ with fixed }(\lambda,\sigma)\quad\longrightarrow\quad\text{(ii) inverse Schur transform}.

The resource counts are explicit functions of
d,N,\varepsilon
,
L
and
\ell_{1}
.

VII.1
Toffoli-equivalent cost

Inverse Schur.

By Eq.
S57
, the forward Schur transform has
\mathrm{TE}\big(\mathrm{Schur}(d,N,\varepsilon)\big)=\sum_{s=2}^{d}\mathrm{TE}_{s}(d,N,\varepsilon)
per CG stage and a factor
(N{-}1)
for the cascade.
The inverse transform applies the same compiled two-level sequence and the same online
arithmetic in reverse, so we take

\mathrm{TE}_{\mathrm{Schur}^{-1}}(d,N,\varepsilon)\;=\;\mathrm{TE}\!\left(\mathrm{Schur}(d,N,\varepsilon)\right)\;\leq\;(N{-}1)\sum_{s=2}^{d}\mathrm{TE}_{s}(d,N,\varepsilon).

(S88)

Block encoding via RUS (naive postselection).

From Eq.
S80
,

\boxed{\ \mathrm{TE}_{\mathrm{total}}^{\mathrm{(RUS)}}\;=\;\ell_{1}^{\,2}\,\Big[\mathrm{TE}_{\textsc{PREP}+\textsc{PREP}^{\dagger}}(L,R;k_{1},k_{2},b_{r})\;+\;\mathrm{TE}_{\textsc{SEL}}(n_{\mu};\,k^{\prime}_{1},k^{\prime}_{2})\Big]\;+\;\mathrm{TE}_{\mathrm{Schur}^{-1}}(d,N,\varepsilon).}

(S89)

Block encoding via OAA (coherent).

From Eqs.
S85
and
S86
,

\boxed{\ \mathrm{TE}_{\mathrm{total}}^{\mathrm{(OAA)}}\;=\;(2r{+}1)\Big[\mathrm{TE}_{\textsc{PREP}+\textsc{PREP}^{\dagger}}(L,R;k_{1},k_{2},b_{r})\;+\;\mathrm{TE}_{\textsc{SEL}}(n_{\mu};\,k^{\prime}_{1},k^{\prime}_{2})\Big]\;+\;2r\,C_{0}(b;a)\;+\;\mathrm{TE}_{\mathrm{Schur}^{-1}}(d,N,\varepsilon).}

(S90)

VII.2
Peak qubit footprint

Let
b=\lceil\log_{2}L\rceil
be the address width used by
PREP
.
During block encoding (before inverse Schur) we hold only the Schur registers

(\lambda,\mu,\sigma)
plus the address; during the inverse Schur we hold the system
computational registers and the Schur registers, but not the address
b
.
Because we use clean ancillas, banks may be reused across disjoint stages; hence the
end-to-end peak is the maximum over the two stages.

Block-encoding stage (RUS).

The peak qubits are

Q_{\mathrm{block}}^{\mathrm{(RUS)}}=n_{\lambda}+n_{\mu}+n_{\sigma}+b+\max\!\Big\{\,Q_{\textsc{PREP-anc}}(L,R;k_{1},k_{2},b_{r}),\;Q_{\textsc{SEL-anc}}(n_{\mu};k^{\prime}_{1},k^{\prime}_{2})\,\Big\}.

(S91)

Block-encoding stage (OAA).

With reflections on the address ancilla, we add a clean-ancilla bank
\max(0,b{-}2)
:

Q_{\mathrm{block}}^{\mathrm{(OAA)}}=n_{\lambda}+n_{\mu}+n_{\sigma}+b+\max\!\Big\{\,Q_{\textsc{PREP-anc}}(L,R;k_{1},k_{2},b_{r}),\;Q_{\textsc{SEL-anc}}(n_{\mu};k^{\prime}_{1},k^{\prime}_{2}),\;\max(0,b{-}2)\,\Big\}.

(S92)

Inverse-Schur stage.

From Eq.
S63
we have the peak footprint (system+ancilla) for one
Schur transform; the inverse uses the same footprint:

Q_{\mathrm{Schur}^{-1}}(d,N,\varepsilon;\,a_{\mathrm{mcx}}^{\mathrm{prov}})\;=\;Q_{\mathrm{sys}}(d,N)\;+\;\max_{2\leq s\leq d}\;Q_{\mathrm{anc}}^{[s]}.

(S93)

End-to-end peak.

Because the block-encoding and inverse-Schur stages run sequentially, in that order, the end-to-end
peak qubit demand is the maximum of the two stage peaks:

\displaystyle\boxed{\ Q_{\mathrm{peak}}^{\mathrm{(RUS)}}\;=\;\max\!\Big\{\,Q_{\mathrm{block}}^{\mathrm{(RUS)}},\;Q_{\mathrm{Schur}^{-1}}(d,N,\varepsilon;\,a_{\mathrm{mcx}}^{\mathrm{prov}})\,\Big\},}

(S94)

\displaystyle\vskip 6.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 2.0pt\boxed{\ Q_{\mathrm{peak}}^{\mathrm{(OAA)}}\;=\;\max\!\Big\{\,Q_{\mathrm{block}}^{\mathrm{(OAA)}},\;Q_{\mathrm{Schur}^{-1}}(d,N,\varepsilon;\,a_{\mathrm{mcx}}^{\mathrm{prov}})\,\Big\}.}

(S95)

In practice, for moderate–large
(d,N)
,
Q_{\mathrm{Schur}^{-1}}
often dominates due to many multi-controlled rotations on the large width GT and
\lambda
registers, while for small systems with
large
L
one may instead be limited by the block-encoding address/ancilla (especially OAA’s

\max(0,b{-}2)
reflection bank).
