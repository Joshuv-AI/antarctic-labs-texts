Early identification of frailty in older adults in primary care: a study protocol of a longitudinal cohort study
Authors: Chiara Milani, Claudia Biagi, Riccardo Barucci, Enrico Benvenuti, Francesca Verga, Claudia Rosi, Diletta Buresta, Fiorenza Wetzell-Cabrera, Marco Del Riccio, Chiara Lorini, Patrizio Zanobini, Giulia Naldini, Lorenzo Baggiani, Marco Nerattini, Guglielmo Bonaccorsi

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPopulation ageing increases frailty prevalence, a condition linked to higher disability risk. The Integrated Care for Older People (ICOPE) model promotes early frailty identification in primary care and integration of health, social, and community services. However, evidence of adaptations of ICOPE models in different contexts is scarce, as is knowledge of health literacy’s (HL) role in frailty screening. This study protocol describes a project applying ICOPE indications to identify frail older individuals early, through a multiple-step process (first level screening and second level screening).MethodsThe study has a longitudinal cohort design, including the recruitment of all residents aged 75 years in a defined area in the city of Florence (Italy). During the first visit participants will complete a brief Frailty Questionnaire, perform the Chair Rise Test and answer one health literacy question (Single Item Screening - SILS). Those who will result positive for frailty, will attend a second visit with tests covering multiple frailty domains: nutritional, functional, cognitive, psychological status and level of autonomy and HL.DiscussionA statistically significant association is expected to be identified between positive first level screening and the domains assessed in the multidimensional assessment. Furthermore, low HL could be associated with an increased risk of frailty to evaluate the predictive validity of HL tools for early identification of at-risk individuals. Background

Epidemiological and demographic transitions lead to an increasing older adult population with a significant proportion experiencing chronic conditions and multimorbidity, and complex health needs. These conditions result in a higher risk of frailty and, if not early recognized and treated, in disability and loss of independence [1]. The World Health Organization (WHO) estimates that the amount of population over 65 years will double from 2000 to 2050 [2]. In this context, Italy is one of the oldest countries worldwide [3, 4].

In response to the global challenges of population ageing, the WHO defined the concept of healthy ageing, focusing on “the functional ability of older people to do the things they value, rather than around the absence of disease” and encompassing health and social care sectors [5]. The approach aims at maintaining the optimal level of the intrinsic capacity (IC) - a concept introduced by the WHO as “the composite of all the physical and mental capacities of the person, defining the individual’s resources and functional ability (i.e., what the person can aspire to do for giving value to his/her abilities and positively act for the society)” [6]. IC is a relatively recent construct in ageing research, still undergoing validation and operational development. It tends to decline with age due to the gradual loss of physiological reserve and reduced ability to adapt to stressors.

Frailty is a clinical syndrome reflecting this reduced reserve and increased vulnerability to stressor, and consequently, associated with a higher risk in negative health-related events, including disability and/or mortality [7, 8]. According to Fried, it considers the following domains: nutritional status, energy, physical activity, mobility and strength [9], whose reduction is associated with high risk of numerous adverse outcomes. Its early identification is crucial for implementing interventions to stop or at least diminish its progression towards disability and loss of self-sufficiency [10–12].

Health literacy (HL) represents a recognized determinant of health for people and specifically older adults who suffer from worse social conditions as low levels of education and wealth; in this perspective, it can play a crucial role in frailty prevention and management, affecting access to care and use of health services, communication between health professionals and patients or caregivers, self-management, awareness of their own health conditions and adherence to treatments [13, 14].

Therefore, the concept of frailty may represent a state of extreme vulnerability to stressors defined by a clinically relevant reduction of IC and functional reserves. For this reason, the monitoring of IC may support the identification of the process of being frail [15] and contribute to the promotion of healthy ageing [6].

In recent years, WHO has defined a specific Integrated Care for Older People (ICOPE) program [16, 17], which focuses on an integrated healthcare approach. This model is designed for the patient and caregiver participation in the definition of the healthcare pathway, considering individuals’ capacities, desires and willingness, social and environmental context [18]. It relies on a multiple-step process where an initial screening aimed at identifying declines in IC is followed by a multidimensional assessment in primary care (PC) setting. It permits the definition of personalized care plan, involving the patient, his/her family, different PC professionals and the community resources [17].

Within this broader picture, the Tuscany Region in Italy has drawn up and published a specific document (“Commissione regionale fragilità”), which states the need for a proactive approach to early identification of frailty and the definition of tailored integrated programs, including Vivifrail, together with dietetic nutritional intervention, psychological support and other specific interventions depending on the specific needs. It has evolved from previous regional studies on frailty [19, 20].

Despite the recognition that frailty needs to be detected early in older adults, evidence of adaptations of the ICOPE model and indications in specific PC contexts are generally scarce and Italy faces an urgent need for strengthening strategies to support healthy aging, considering that it is one of the oldest countries worldwide. Furthermore, the role of HL in predicting frailty and its integration in frailty screening requires more research, also considering the comparison of different tools in predicting frailty as well as different instruments for assessing HL.

Starting from these premises, the present study protocol tries to explore and address these gaps by measuring frailty in a specific population of older persons, adapting the ICOPE model for early detection to a specific neighborhood-level intervention, and exploring the relationship between HL and frailty.

Methods

General aim

The project aims to identify frail older individuals early, through a multiple steps process (first level screening and multidimensional assessment - second level screening) applied to 75-year-old adults in a specific Italian neighborhood. The specific process represents an adaptation of the ICOPE model and will enhance the definition of a tailored, multicomponent pathway for each patient, based on individual needs and the collaboration among various PC professionals and general practitioners (GP).

Study design and setting

The study is a single-center prospective longitudinal cohort study. It will take place in a House of Community (HoC - “Casa della Comunità”) located in the most populous neighborhood of the city of Florence, in the Tuscany region. This facility represents a specific model of Community Health Center, focused on enhancing continuity of care and integration among health professionals and services through multiprofessional collaborative practice, and promoting HL, health promotion activities and community engagement [21]. The study includes two specific phases: a first level screening and a following second level multidimensional assessment.

This protocol was drafted adhering to the Standard Protocol Items: Recommendations for Interventional Trials (SPIRIT) [22].

Study participants and eligibility criteria

The study population is represented by all 75-year-old persons living in a specific area of the Florence Health District – the reference area of the chosen HoC Le Piagge, where the screening and the other interventions will be realized - who voluntarily decide to participate and are apparently self-sufficient and not physically or mentally disabled. The choice of this age group is due to epidemiological reasons - that is a higher prevalence of frailty in 75–84-year-old compared to the younger [9, 23, 24], together with the fact that the screening must not identify an excessive number of negative individuals (healthy, without signs of frailty) and, at the same time, must be able (by means of a multidimensional assessment) to identify conditions of initial frailty, so as to be able to intervene (also) with early and non-pharmaceutical options. Other reasons are the organization of preventive strategies in Italy that are often targeted to this age group and the feasibility reason linked to the study design.

The design will provide for the annual recruitment of 75-year-old people to follow them and the cohorts recruited in previous years, so that the monitoring of people can reveal potential signs of loss of autonomy over time.

In the first year of the study, more than 400 subjects (after extracting all the 75-year-old people living in the area of the HoC and its surroundings from the municipal registry) will be contacted for participating in the study. A priori sample size was not estimated because participation was offered to all resident 75-year-old individuals. This is a pilot study, and its primary aim is to assess the applicability and acceptability of the model proposed. However, in order to ensure transparency, an estimate of expected recruitment has been added on the approximate number of residents in the area and the expected participation rate (of almost 20–30%). Moreover, an attrition rate of approximately 20% will also be considered, mainly due to loss of contact, refusal, or health-related reasons.

Participants are eligible if they are 75-year-old at the time of recruitment, if they accept to sign a written informed consent and are able to read and understand spoken and written Italian language, as the assessment tools are all in Italian.

The following conditions represent exclusion criteria, that will be verified during the recruitment phone call, through direct questioning of the potential participants or their caregivers, and also at the first appointment for the screening: being resident in long-term care or being non-self-sufficient, and the presence of severe cognitive impairment, severe psychiatric disorder, terminal illness, or any other condition of disability, since all these conditions are not compatible with early detection of frailty.

This study complies with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Data collection process

After extraction from the municipal register, the subjects who meet the inclusion criteria will be contacted by phone and invited to participate by a group of researchers, specifically trained for all the data collection phases, and after informing the GPs operating in the area. For the telephone contacts, the numbers were extracted from the Local Health registry. The telephone contact, repeated up to 3 times and providing a specific phone number for call-backs, will serve as an initial explanation of the study, to acquire a verbal consent for participation and to improve its rate. This phase - the recruitment - is planned to last approximately two months. Those who accept to be included are offered an appointment at the HoC where they can complete the first-level screening (2 months) with the support of the multidisciplinary research team, after signing the informed consent for participation and data collection and processing. In addition, a digital support has been specifically developed to make the self-administration of the test easier. The results of the first level screening will allow participants to be classified into two groups: those who test negative are called back for follow-up screening in the following year, accounting for the fact that after the age of 75 any person may experience a loss of autonomy within 12 months, while those who test positive are likely to be “frail” and are invited to a subsequent appointment for the second, more in-depth level test – a multidimensional assessment focused on various frailty domains, administered by the specifically trained research team (4 months). The results of the second screening will highlight in which domains the subject is most at risk of frailty. Based on the results of this multidimensional assessment, and in presence of at least one positive domain, the GP - who will be informed step by step of all the results of his/her patients - may decide for a specialist geriatrician examination, which is offered free of charge at HoC ‘Le Piagge’. The geriatrician further evaluates the patient’s health needs and care complexity and collaborates in drawing up a personalized care plan, together with other PC professionals and the GP himself. Follow-ups for the multidimensional assessment are scheduled at 6 and 12 months after the initial screening (Fig. 1). The successful conclusion of the follow up at 12 months marks the end of the study. Participants drop-out will be monitored and documented at each phase (first-level screening and multidimensional assessment). Reasons for drop-out will be collected (refusals, health conditions, loss to follow-up). Different strategies will be implemented to minimize drop-out, such as the provision of reminders to participants, the offer of flexible scheduling, alternative times for assessment and the involvement of GPs to encourage participation.Fig. 1Screening Flow Chart

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics will be used to summarize the sample’s general characteristics, including health-related variables. This initial analysis will help identify potential confounders. Subsequently, bi-variate analysis (t test and chi-square test) will be conducted to compare the differences between frail and non-frail individuals, based on tool scores. Additional comparisons will be made between individuals classified as frail based on the multidimensional assessment. Regression models will be applied to the subgroup with positive first-level screening (who undergo multidimensional assessment) to examine associations between demographic variables and results from the multidimensional assessment. Potential confounding variables will be accounted for in the models, and outcomes will be adjusted accordingly. A comparative analysis of HL tools will be used to evaluate their capacity to identify HL levels and to predict frailty. Roc curve analyses will be conducted to compare the predictive ability of each tool. Considering the longitudinal design of the study, data will be analysed at specific time points corresponding to baseline and subsequent follow-up assessments.

In analysing changes over time for each secondary frailty domain, it will be considered the established thresholds for minimal clinically important differences (MCIDs) for tools where it is defined. For other outcomes (e.g., Mini-Cog, MUST, IADL), descriptive and categorical analyses will be used. When changes will be observed across clinically defined thresholds (e.g., from “low” to “high” nutritional risk), these will be reported and interpreted accordingly. Missing data will be examined performing a descripting analysis of the type and patterns and using a Multiple Imputation by Chained Equations for missing covariates and outcomes. 0Ethical considerations and dissemination.

Discussion

Synthesis of the expected results

The study aims to determine frailty prevalence in the considered 75-year-old population. A statistically significant association is expected to be identified between positive first-level screening results and the domains assessed in the multidimensional assessment (second level screening): as an example, individuals testing positive at the first level screening are expected to show lower performance in cognitive tests and reduced physical performance (e.g., lower SPPB scores) [42, 43].

Regarding the predictive validity of HL tools, low HL – indicated by a positive SILS test – is hypothesized to be significantly associated with an increased likelihood of being classified as frail in the first-level screening [44, 45]. This would support the predictive validity of the SILS as an effective tool for early identification of at-risk individuals.

Additionally, the longitudinal assessment, with repeated measures at baseline, 6 and 12 months of the second-level tests (multidimensional assessment), will allow the description of frailty progression over time. In terms of screening adherence and participation, previous studies have reported an adherence rate of approximately 20% [20]. To improve engagement, this study will implement an initial telephone contact for verbal consent, followed by an in-person appointment. This proactive approach aims at improving participation, although it will exclude people who are not reachable or do not answer the phone, leading to a potential low participation rate initially. While no minimum participation rate has been established, since the adherence is fully voluntary and each 75-year-old person will be invited, adherence rate will be carefully monitored and reported, and strategies to further improve participation will be adopted as needed.

Considering the positivity rate to the first level screening, other studies on similar populations reported a prevalence of frailty according to Fried criteria [9] (Fried et al., 2001) of 4–5% [46, 47].

Considering the adherence to one-year follow-up at the first-level screening test, a WHO study on compliance with long-term therapies suggests that a 50% adherence rate is considered the minimum acceptable level [48]. In comparison, Tavassoli’s study [49] reported a 70% adherence rate, which is higher than WHO threshold.

There is no consensus in the literature on optimal follow-up time. Tavassoli et al. [49] recommend screening every six months. In thestudy, a multidimensional assessment will be performed at six months and the first level screening at 12 months for subjects who tested negative at baseline screening. This approach ensures screening that progressively includes 75-year-old cohorts. Regarding adherence to the second-level screening test, one study [49] reports that only 12% of subjects who tested positive at phase 1 continued to phase 2, indicating poor adherence to the follow-up evaluation and/or a deeper assessment, even when signs of frailty or other health problems emerged.

Higher adherence rate is expected due to the following measures to strengthen participation: the first-level screening (step 1) will be conducted face-to-face in a specific and well recognized PC setting (the HoC), which is the usual referral place for participants’ health needs; similar expectations apply to the second-level screening (step 2, multidimensional assessment). Additionally, to increase and facilitate participation in the second level screening, the appointment is assigned at the time of the first level test.

Strengths: perspectives and policy considerations

Several aspects of the study are important and worthy to be highlighted. First, if the participation will be successful, the study could provide preliminary insights into a framework for implementing a structured frailty screening program at the community level. In the longer term, this approach may support the continuously monitoring of 75-year-old people and older. The identification of frailty dimensions could inform the definition of more targeted preventive interventions and strategies.

In particular, the study will offer the opportunity to test a population-based screening approach, incorporating a first-level screening test. Indeed, if its predictive value for frailty identification is confirmed through second-level multidimensional assessment results, the first-level screening test and the SILS test could be considered for use as routinary, and very simple tools for the early identification of frailty in a PC setting or even, in the best case and with the support of GPs, directly by people per se. Indeed, the Frailty Screening tool used is taught to be short and self-reported, providing an opportunity for patients to complete the test alone, with the help of a smartphone or another digital device, thus allowing the evaluation of their digital literacy too. On the other hand, the multidimensional assessment gives the opportunity to evaluate the frailty condition according to a “holistic” perspective, that is putting into consideration the different dimensions of frailty and, in some case, of initial stages of diseases. This may help identify strategies on how to slow down the progression. Related to this point, another key aspect is the opportunity to experiment a multidisciplinary model for the early management of frailty.

The choice of contextualizing the study within this PC setting in the regional health service (the HoC, previously cited) fit to the aim. Indeed, the HoC is designed to enhance multiprofessional integration and already represents a place identified by the local population to find solutions to low-intensity health events. Indeed, ICOPE guidelines consider frailty a multidimensional concept requiring a multidimensional approach within the healthcare system. In the study, individuals who test positive in the first-level screening are invited to undergo a multidimensional assessment, followed by a specialist geriatric evaluation which foresees a strict collaboration among geriatricians, general practitioner and other healthcare professionals. This integrated approach aims to enhance the implementation of a a tailored, personalized taking over, aligning with the principles of ICOPE model and addressing the call for additional research on the effectiveness of integrated model for screening and management of frailty [12, 50].

Limitations

The study has some limitations. First, screening adherence rate is a major challenge since, as already reported, previous research recorded an adherence rate of approximately 20% in similar screening approaches. Another potential limit is the risk of selection bias, due to the voluntary participation of the individuals who meet the inclusion criteria: this element could select people with better health conditions, higher health literacy, better socioeconomic status, and greater awareness of their own health conditions.These conditions could potentially result in an underestimation of the frailty prevalence. Furthermore, a control sample was not defined, with the risk of limiting the strength of the results.

Another critical aspect is the proportion of individuals who test positive at the first level screening and agree to undergo the multidimensional assessment and the follow-up: in the case of low participation at follow-up appointments after the first screening, the feasibility, acceptability and scalability of the proposed interventions would be strongly compromised. For this reason, we planned to evaluate the inclusion of other cohorts the following years.

In addition, generalizability could be a matter of debate: several factors may limit the broader applicability of the findings, such as the sample size, the characteristics of people living in the area and its geographic dimension, and the choice of the HoC as referral place for screenings and interventions. The study currently involves a small sample from a single city neighborhood, but, in the long run, the aim is to expand this program to the entire city, progressively including new cohorts of 75-year-olds each year, and re-screening those who participated in the previous year and test negative, to produce a “film” of frailty in older people, who represent a real challenge for the sustainability of the public healthcare systems.
