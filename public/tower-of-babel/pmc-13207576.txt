When ambulances wait at the emergency department: hospital factors and offload times in California
Authors: Esmeralda Melgoza, Madeline Feldmeier, Crystal Chen, Hiram Beltrán-Sánchez, Renee Y Hsia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionAmbulance patient offload time (APOT), the interval between ambulance arrival at the emergency department (ED) and transfer of care, is an understudied measure of emergency care delivery. We examined associations between hospital characteristics and APOTs across 310 California EDs before Assembly Bill (AB) 40 established statewide APOT reduction requirements.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective cross-sectional study using data from the California Emergency Medical Services (EMS) Authority, 34 local EMS agencies, and the California Department of Health Care Access and Information. The primary outcome was APOT-1, the time within which 90% of EMS-ED transfers are completed. We used LASSO for predictor selection and fitted negative binominal regression models.ResultsAmong 310 EDs, 77.7% were urban, 62.9% were nonprofit, and 89.0% were nonteaching. Urban location and more ED treatment stations were associated with longer APOT-1. Predicted APOT-1 ranged from 9.7 to 19.3 minutes in rural EDs and 19.4 to 38.7 minutes in urban EDs.Policy ImplicationsThis study provides baseline data on patient handoff between EMS crews and ED staff prior to the implementation of AB 40. Addressing APOT delays requires coordinated strategies beyond individual EDs or EMS agencies.Keywords: ambulance patient offload times, emergency medical services, emergency departments, health policy

Introduction

Prehospital emergency medical services (EMS) and emergency departments (ED) are important entry points into the United States (US) healthcare system, often functioning as a safety net for individuals seeking healthcare.1 In 2021, California reported 6.4 million EMS calls and 12.9 million ED visits,2,3 with 20% to 25% of these ED visits arriving by ambulance.4 Emergency systems rely on key metrics, including EMS dispatch times, on-scene times, and transport times to evaluate timely access to and use of healthcare services.1,5,6 A critical yet understudied key metric in emergency care delivery is ambulance patient offload times (APOTs), also known as wall times or handoff times.4,7,8 APOT measures the time between ambulance arrival at the ED and the transfer of patient care to ED staff.7-9

APOT delays have implications for patients, providers, and healthcare systems. At the patient level, higher APOTs are associated with decreased patient satisfaction, increased ambulance reattendance within 30 days of initial ED presentation, and increased risk of death.8,10 From the EMS clinicians’ and ED providers’ perspective, increased APOTs are associated with longer shifts, heightened stress, frustration, and burnout.11 At the healthcare system level, APOT delays are associated with reduced EMS clinician and ambulance availability for other emergencies in the community, increased EMS response times, and financial losses for EMS agencies, with potential implications for long-term viability of the systems.11

APOT delays reflect broader system-level challenges, including ED crowding, ED boarding and issues with hospital throughput.8,11,12 ED crowding is a situation in which the demand for emergency services exceeds the supply of healthcare providers and resources in the ED.13 ED boarding is the practice of holding patients in the ED while awaiting an inpatient bed, and hospital throughput describes the movement of patients from admission to discharge.13 ED crowding, ED boarding, and issues with hospital throughput are associated with challenges in EMS clinicians’ ability to transfer care when the ED lacks capacity to accept new patients.7,8,13 A study found that ED crowding was associated with lower ambulance availability for other emergencies in the community, with ∼1 in 8 ambulances out of service while waiting to transfer patients to an available ED bed.14 Another study reported that ED boarding was associated with APOT delays, by reducing the overall bed availability through extended patient stays.15 These studies advance our understanding of prolonged APOT in the context of system-level strain, but offer limited insights into the hospital-level characteristics that may also influence APOTs. Our study addresses this existing gap in the literature by examining the association between hospital-level characteristics and APOTs.7,8

Over the past decade, California developed APOT measures to improve reporting at the state, local EMS agency (LEMSA), and hospital levels. In 2014, the California Hospital Association, the California Emergency Medical Services Authority (EMSA), and other stakeholders developed measures to quantify APOT.16 In December 2016, the California Commission on EMS approved the APOT standardized measures for data collection and reporting.17 In July 2019, the California EMSA began requiring the LEMSAs submit APOT data to the state.18 More recently, in 2023, the California Legislature passed Assembly Bill (AB) 40, requiring all state-licensed hospitals with EDs to submit APOT reduction protocols by September 1, 2024, and report annual updates on their progress.19 AB 40 requires LEMSAs in California to develop an APOT standard not to exceed 30 minutes, 90% of the time.19,20

With the recent data reporting mandates aimed at reducing APOTs, identifying factors associated with APOT delays may allow the development of effective strategies to reduce APOTs and thus improve emergency medical care in the prehospital and ED settings. Our previous study on APOT patterns in California provided an overview of variations in APOTs by LEMSA.8 However, whether specific hospital characteristics are associated with the observed variation remains to be determined. The objective of this study is to examine the associations between hospital characteristics and APOTs in 310 California hospitals with licensed EDs during 2022, the year before AB 40 was enacted.

Methods

Study design, setting, and population

We conducted a retrospective study using 2022 data from 3 sources, including the California EMSA, 34 LEMSAs serving the 58 counties in California, and the California Department of Health Care Access and Information (HCAI). APOT data were obtained from the California EMSA and the 34 LEMSAs. Hospital-level characteristics and ED utilization data were obtained from HCAI. The HCAI dataset includes California-licensed acute care hospitals with operating EDs, including children's hospitals and state-licensed specialty hospitals that report to HCAI. The dataset does not include federal facilities such as Veterans Affairs, military, or Indian Health Service hospitals. We excluded 27 satellite facilities that used consolidated reporting in the HCAI ED dataset, meaning all hospital or ED data for the facility was consolidated with the parent facility data and reported under the parent facility name and ID number. The final analytic sample included 310 hospitals across 55 of the 58 counties in California. The 3 excluded counties, which tended to be smaller, either lacked a licensed ED or did not have APOT data available in the HCAI dataset. For context, California had 325 EDs in 2021, the year prior to the current study.21 This study did not constitute human subjects research and was deemed exempt by the University of California San Francisco Institutional Review Board. This study followed the strengthening the reporting of observational studies in epidemiology guidelines.

Study outcomes

Our primary outcome was APOT-1, a standardized measure adopted by the California EMSA in 2016 to calculate the APOT interval as a continuous measure in minutes. APOT-1 represents the 90th percentile of offload times for ambulance transports, meaning the time within which 90% of EMS-ED patient transfers are completed.17

Independent variables

We examined the associations between hospital characteristics and APOT-1, including the number of ED treatment stations, the number of licensed hospital beds, hospital ownership type, hospital geographic location, teaching hospital designation, and ambulance diversion. ED treatment stations are areas in the ED designated to treat one patient at a time, excluding holding or observation beds.22 Licensed hospital beds are the total number of beds authorized in that facility by the California Department of Public Health as of December 31, 2022.22 We operationalized hospital type according to the legal or organizational structure of the licensee, including government (city, county, or district), for-profit, and nonprofit (church-related and University of California (UC)) hospitals. We used HCAI's hospital geographic location classifications, which are based on the California Medical Service Study Area (MSSA) criteria.23,24 MSSAs are geographic units used to assess healthcare resources, identify medically underserved areas, and support health planning.23,24 For this analysis, hospitals were classified as urban or rural, with the rural and frontier categories combined due to small sample sizes. We defined teaching hospital as a facility with at least 2 Accreditation Council for Graduate Medical Education approved clinical teaching programs in general or family medicine, internal medicine, pediatrics, general surgery, or obstetrics and gynecology, with at least 30 postgraduate medical students, and over 200 intern hours per licensed bed.25 Finally, we defined ambulance diversion as a situation when a hospital ED is entirely closed to ambulance patients during a specified period, excluding cases where only certain patient types were redirected due to specialized needs while others continued to receive care.22,25

Statistical analysis

We calculated descriptive statistics including percentages and frequencies for all independent variables, including the number of ED treatment stations, number of licensed hospital beds, hospital ownership type (government, for-profit, or nonprofit), teaching hospital designation (yes/no), hospital geographic location (urban or rural), and ambulance diversion status at any point during the study timeframe (yes/no) (Table 1). We performed bivariate analyses between the independent variables and APOT-1, the outcome variable, using t-tests or analysis of variance. We then conducted an exploratory analysis using Spearman's correlation for continuous variables and Cramer's V for categorical variables. These analyses demonstrated moderate to strong correlations (0.70-0.80) across many of the hospital-level characteristics, indicating potential multicollinearity. To identify key predictors while minimizing model complexity, we applied least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) penalization. Two variables remained after LASSO selection: the number of ED treatment stations and hospital geographic location.

We fitted 3 negative binomial regression models including the 2 variables identified by LASSO to estimate their associations with APOT-1. Model 1 was an unweighted analysis of 54 counties, excluding Los Angeles County as it comprises 22.6% of all EDs in California and could disproportionately influence unweighted analyses. Model 2 was an unweighted analysis including all 55 counties. Model 3 was a weighted analysis of all 55 counties using inverse county weights to account for county-level ED representation relative to the total number of EDs in the state. We calculated the predicted APOT-1 times for each model according to the number of ED treatment stations and hospital geographic locations. We performed all analyses using R software version 4.4.2 for Mac. We used a P < 0.05 to determine statistical significance. We also conducted sensitivity analyses to assess the robustness of our findings. We fitted weighted nested negative binomial models with additional covariates (Appendix S1). We also constructed a hospital throughput variable using HCAI's publicly available data and categorized this variable into quartiles, which we incorporated into weighted nested negative binomial models (Appendix S2).

Results

Of the 310 EDs, the largest share of hospitals with licensed EDs were in the following counties: Los Angeles (22.6%), Orange (7.4%), San Bernardino (5.8%), Riverside (5.2%), and San Diego (4.8%) (Figure 1). The largest proportion of hospitals with licensed EDs had between 10 and 19 ED treatment stations (26.5%) and 100 to 199 licensed beds (31.0%) (Table 1). The majority of EDs were nonprofit or university-affiliated (62.9%), followed by for-profit (20.3%), and government-affiliated (16.1%) hospitals. Most hospitals were in urban settings (77.7%), a small proportion were teaching hospitals (10.3%), and nearly half (48.4%) reported ambulance diversions, a period when the ED was entirely closed to patients arriving by ambulance.

We used 3 negative binomial regression models to assess the associations between hospital characteristics and APOT-1 (Table 2). Across the 3 models, EDs with more treatment stations and those located in urban settings had higher APOT-1 compared with EDs with 0 to 10 ED treatment stations and those in rural settings. In Model 1, which was unweighted, excluded Los Angeles County and adjusted for hospital geographic location, EDs with 11 to 20, 21 to 30, 31 to 40, and 40+ treatment stations had a consistently higher incidence rate ratio (IRRs) compared with the 0 to 10 reference group. For example, the corresponding IRRs were 1.38 for 11-20 treatment stations (95% CI: 1.11-1.71, P = 0.005); 1.81 for 21-30 treatment stations (95% CI: 1.41-2.32, P < 0.001); 1.68 for 31-40 treatment stations (95% CI: 1.27-2.22, P < 0.001) and 1.84 for 40 + treatment stations (95% CI: 1.43-2.38, P < 0.001). In Model 2, which was unweighted and included all 55 counties, the pattern was less pronounced. EDs with 21 to 30 treatment stations (IRR 1.44, 95% CI: 1.18-1.75, P < 0.001) and 40+ treatment stations (IRR 1.43, 95% CI 1.17-1.75, P < 0.001) still had higher APOT-1 compared with EDs with 0 to 10 treatment stations, but the increase for 11-20 and 31-40 treatment stations was smaller and not statistically significant. In Model 3, which was weighted and included all 55 counties, EDs with 21 to 30 treatment stations (IRR 1.93, 95% CI 1.15-3.29, P = 0.018) and 40+ treatment stations (IRR 1.99, 95% CI 1.13-3.57, P = 0.019) had the highest IRRs across all models, indicating that weighting accentuated the association between ED size and APOT-1. After adjusting for the number of ED treatment stations, EDs in urban locations had significantly higher APOT-1 compared with EDs in rural locations across all models (Model 1, IRR: 1.97, 95% CI: 1.63-2.37; Model 2: IRR: 2.50, 95% CI: 2.12-2.94; Model 3: IRR: 2.00, 95% CI: 1.39-2.90, all P < 0.001).

We predicted APOT-1 by hospital geographic location and number of ED treatment stations using coefficients from each model (Figure 2). Results show that across all models, EDs in urban locations consistently exhibited longer predicted APOT-1 compared with EDs in rural locations. Moreover, predicted APOT-1 generally increased with the number of ED treatment stations, although some variation was observed among larger EDs (Figure 2). In Model 1, the predicted APOT-1 was 11.5 to 21.3 minutes in rural EDs and 22.7 to 41.8 minutes in urban EDs. In Model 2, predicted APOT-1 ranged from 12.7 to 18.2 minutes in rural EDs and from 31.8 to 45.7 minutes in urban EDs. In Model 3, predicted APOT-1 ranged from 9.7 to 19.3 minutes in rural EDs and from 19.4 to 38.7 minutes in urban EDs. We also conducted sensitivity analyses to assess the robustness of our findings (Appendices S1 and S2). Across these alternative model specifications, coefficient estimates for geographic location and ED treatment stations remained consistent in direction and similar in magnitude, although some attenuation was observed. Geographic location remained statistically significant across all model specifications. ED treatment stations and hospital throughput were nonstatistically significant in some model specifications, but their effect estimates remained consistent with the findings from the primary analysis.

Discussion

In this study, we provide the first, to our knowledge, overview of the association between hospital characteristics and APOT across 310 licensed EDs in California. Our study, which uses 2022 data, also provides timely baseline metrics that precede the passage of AB 40 a bill signed into law by Governor Newsom in 2023.19 Across our 3 models, EDs with a greater number of treatment stations were associated with higher APOT-1 compared with EDs with the fewest number of treatment stations. While larger hospitals with more ED treatment stations might be expected to have greater capacity to absorb ambulance arrivals, our findings suggest that increased ED size does not necessarily translate to faster APOTs. Moreover, the number of ED treatment stations may also function as a proxy for overall hospital size and system complexity, rather than only a measure of ED capacity. One possible explanation is that larger hospitals often function as tertiary referral or trauma centers, receive higher-acuity patients and manage more complex health care needs, which may lengthen APOTs despite greater physical capacity. Sensitivity analyses incorporating a hospital throughput measure showed positive, although attenuated, associations between higher throughput and APOT-1, suggesting that constraints in patient flow may contribute to these findings (Appendix S2). For example, a previous study reported that reducing admitted patient boarding times had a greater impact on ED length of stay relative to adding more ED beds.26

Our study also found that urban hospitals had significantly longer APOT-1 times compared with rural hospitals. This aligns with previous findings on hospital characteristics associated with ED crowding. In 2010, a survey of the American Hospital Association found that although ED crowding affects most hospitals, the problem is particularly severe in urban and teaching hospitals.27 Among the hospitals surveyed, 50% of the urban and teaching hospitals reported that their EDs were at or over capacity compared with 31% reported by rural hospitals.27 A more recent study, however, also found that rural ED visits have increased by more than 50%, outpacing urban ED visit rates.28 Between 2005 and 2016, rural ED visits increased from 16.7 million to 28.4 million.28 In 2016, nearly one-fifth of all ED visits were in rural settings.28 Future research should continue to examine the impacts of geographic location and use of emergency services, including both EMS and ED.

While our results provide evidence that hospitals with more ED treatment stations and in urban locations have longer APOTs, it is essential that our findings be interpreted within the context of existing challenges with ED crowding, ED boarding, and hospital throughput. One novel component of this study is that we focus on hospital characteristics and their association with APOT delays rather than pointing to specific EMS agencies, EDs, or hospitals. This framework is not only more solution-oriented, but it also provides a more accurate representation of APOT as a complex, system-wide challenge. The passage of the One Big Beautiful Bill Act in July 2025 is also projected to result in the loss of health insurance for 11.8 million Americans, many of whom will use the emergency system as a safety net to access healthcare.21 This legislation may further strain an already burdened healthcare system, exacerbating issues such as APOT delays, ED overcrowding, ED boarding, and hospital throughput, which may impact patient health outcomes.29

Several areas remain understudied in the context of APOT and warrant further research. First, there is limited evidence on effective interventions to reduce APOTs. For example, a study found that an EMS-based hospital liaison programs that embedded an EMS clinician to improve coordination between EMS and ED teams was associated with a 16% reduction in APOT.30 Another study reported that incorporating a dedicated ED ambulance offload nurse was also associated with a reduction in APOT.31 Second, the impacts of APOT on patient clinical outcomes remains understudied in the US. Evidence from a non-US based study found that prolonged APOTs are associated with ambulance reattendance and a greater 30-day risk of death for people presenting with chest pain in the ED.10 Addressing these literature gaps is critical for determining which interventions reduce APOTs and the potential impacts on patients, EMS and ED clinicians, and the US healthcare system.

Limitations

This study has several important limitations. First, APOT data may be influenced by variability in EMS tracking systems and patient care reporting software, which can lead to inconsistencies in how ambulance offload times are recorded and reported, potentially introducing measurement error.7,8 Second, our analysis is based on a single calendar year, which may not capture longer-term trends in APOT by hospital. For example, public health emergencies, seasonal variations, staffing shortages, or changes in EMS protocols may affect APOT across multiple years. Third, although we adjusted for several hospital-level variables, there may be residual confounding due to unmeasured factors, such as EMS and ED crew staffing levels, ED crowding and boarding metrics, or local variations in care coordination and hospital throughput practices. Fourth, we did not cluster standard errors at the county level because ambulance transports and diversion patterns often cross county boundaries, limiting the extent to which counties represent independent clusters.

Conclusions

Our study provides a comprehensive assessment of the associations between hospital characteristics and APOT-1 across California. We found that hospitals with a greater number of ED treatment stations and those located in urban geographic areas were associated with longer APOT-1. These findings provide critical information as LEMSAs and EDs work to meet the criteria set forth by AB 40 in California. Addressing these delays will require strategies that extend beyond a LEMSA or ED to strengthen overall emergency care delivery.
