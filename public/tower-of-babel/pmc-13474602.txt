Multidisciplinary approach for endodontic microsurgery combined with crown lengthening of tooth defects with apical lesion: a case series
Authors: Chi Dai, Xinfang Zhang, Houwang Lai, Zhengmei Lin, Xiaodong Wang, Shuheng Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundApical periodontitis in the anterior region is often accompanied by aesthetic defects, and its clinical management requires concurrent consideration of functional recovery and aesthetic rehabilitation. This report presents a rarely reported procedure combining endodontic microsurgery (EMS) and crown lengthening surgery (CLS) for anterior teeth with coronal defects and apical lesions.Case presentationThis report presents two cases of residual crowns with apical periodontitis in the anterior region, for which a well-organized and coordinated multidisciplinary treatment protocol was implemented. Following detailed clinical assessment and meticulous treatment planning, a combination of EMS and CLS was performed simultaneously. EMS effectively controlled infection and promoted osseous healing, while CLS optimized the gingival contour and clinical crown length. More than six months after the combined surgery, the final prosthesis was delivered. The follow-up results for over one year confirmed that the affected teeth were clinically asymptomatic and exhibited incomplete healing radiographically in both cases, and also confirmed the stability of the crown lengthening surgery and restorative results.ConclusionsThese cases demonstrate the advantages of EMS combined with CLS in treating complex affected teeth in the anterior aesthetic zone, eradicating infection, and facilitating rapid osseous healing. This approach may serve as a clinical reference for the preservation of similar complex anterior teeth in aesthetic zones.Keywords: Endodontic microsurgery, Crown lengthening surgery, Aesthetic defect, Multidisciplinary, Case series

Background

Root canal therapy (RCT) is the conventional and most effective treatment for managing pulpal and periapical pathologies, with a success rate of around 90% [1, 2]. Recent retrospective studies, however, indicate that unfavorable obturation quality accounts for 65% of RCT failures, whereas inadequate coronal restoration contributes to 30% of cases [1]. Successful endodontic outcomes therefore depend not only on eradicating intra- and extracanal infection, but also on a well-executed coronal restoration to prevent coronal microleakage.

Compared with nonsurgical retreatment, endodontic microsurgery (EMS) enables direct examination of periradicular tissues to identify the underlying causes of treatment failure, resect granulation tissue, and facilitate periapical healing [3, 4]. Emerging evidence has demonstrated EMS efficacy in managing teeth with complex pulpal and periapical pathologies [4–6]. While EMS and nonsurgical retreatment yield comparable long-term success rates, EMS confers a statistically significant higher short-term success rate [7]. This underscores the advantage of EMS in rapidly eradicating infection and initiating early periapical tissue regeneration.

Malpositioned gingival margins from defective prostheses often encroach on the biological width, frequently inducing gingival inflammation and hyperplasia [8]. If left unaddressed, this violation may progress to alveolar bone resorption, gingival recession, and cervical caries [9]. Initial management of anterior teeth with post-treatment apical periodontitis and associated aesthetic deficits involves removal of defective restorations. Following a comprehensive assessment of residual tooth structure and treatment prognosis, a decision is made to either preserve or extract the affected tooth. Whereas posterior tooth loss primarily impairs masticatory function, anterior tooth loss negatively impacts psychosocial well-being and overall quality of life [10–12]. Accordingly, restorative management of anterior teeth requires a multidisciplinary approach with a customized treatment plan to optimize the final aesthetic result. While EMS and CLS have been documented separately in the literature, evidence for their combined application is limited to a few case reports. No consensus has yet been reached on patient selection, surgical sequencing, or healing outcomes.

This report describes two cases of anterior teeth with periapical lesions and associated aesthetic deficits. EMS was delivered to eradicate infection and facilitate periapical healing, with concurrent crown lengthening surgery (CLS) to normalize irregular gingival contours. This integrated treatment protocol achieved two key objectives: establishing adequate clinical crown height for definitive restorative treatment and optimizing gingival architecture, thus enabling simultaneous functional and aesthetic rehabilitation. The report elucidates personalized clinical decision-making in the management of residual root structure and defective restorations, which may offer a feasible option for the treatment of anterior periapical pathology with concomitant hard and soft tissue defects.

Case presentation

These two cases follow the CARE guidelines.

Case 1

A 20-year-old woman was referred for treatment of the right maxillary anterior teeth. She complained of recurring spontaneous pain. Clinical examination (Fig. 1A-B) revealed tooth #8 as a residual root, the distal defect of which extended 1–2 mm subgingivally. The periodontal probing depths (PD) were 2 mm circumferentially. A palatal access cavity was observed on tooth #7, which was restored with a composite filling. Both teeth #7 and #8 were tender on percussion with tenderness to palpation over the mucosa overlying the root apex. No sinus tracts were found, and mobility tests were within normal limits. Periapical radiograph revealed underfilling of the root canal and a periapical radiolucency of both teeth #7 and #8 (Fig. 1C). Cone-beam computerized tomography (CBCT) (NewTom, VGi EVO, Italy) imaging (Fig. 1D-H) showed that a perforation was located at approximately 3.5 mm from the apex of tooth #8, and the buccal cortical plate was thin at the apical third of the root. In tooth #7, overfilled endodontic sealer was noted, and a perforation of the buccal cortical plate was identified at the middle third of the root.

Preoperative clinical pictures and radiographic images of Case 1. A-B Preoperative intraoral photographs. C The periapical radiograph showing both teeth #7 and #8 had underfilling of the root canal, and had a periapical radiolucency. D-H Axial view, coronal view and sagittal view of CBCT imaging

Based on the patient’s history and clinical and radiographic findings, tooth #8 was diagnosed as a previously root filled tooth with symptomatic apical periodontitis, with a lateral root perforation. Tooth #7 was diagnosed as symptomatic apical periodontitis following root canal treatment. After a thorough discussion with the patient, EMS was planned for both teeth #7 and #8. For tooth #8, the EMS protocol included perforation repair and simultaneous CLS with a post-core crown planned for subsequent restoration.

Before the surgery, the caries and unsupported tooth structure were removed. The coronal gutta-percha was then debrided, and the canal orifice was sealed with flowable resin (GC F02, Japan). The access cavity was temporized with glass ionomer (GC Fuji VII, Japan) to establish a coronal seal.

After local anesthesia by 4% articaine hydrochloride with 1:100,000 epinephrine, an internal bevel incision was placed 1.0 mm apical to the gingival margin of tooth #8, referencing the gingival margin of tooth #9. The epithelial collar was removed followed by periodontic debridement. Subsequently, a preparatory chamfer was established on tooth #8 to define the final margin (Fig. 2A). A rectangular flap was reflected, consisting of two vertical incisions distal to #6 and mesial to #9 and an intrasulcular incision that included the dental papilla (Fig. 2B-C). A 45-degree surgical handpiece with round bur was used for osteotomy, aided by an endodontic microscope (OPMI Pro Ergo, Carl Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany). The granulation tissue was removed to identify the root apex. 3 mm of the root tip was resected using ultrasonic osteotome (Woodpecker, China). The root was stained with a methylene blue dye, and no fractures or cracks were identified with a micromirror under high magnification (Fig. 2D-F). Root-end cavity was prepared with ultrasonic tips (B&L Biotech, South Korea) and filled with Bioceramic iRoot BP Plus (Innovative BioCeramix, Canada) (Fig. 2G-H). A bone resection procedure was performed to remove the supporting bone using the future location of the restorative margin as a guide (Fig. 2I). The entrance of the bone defect was covered with a collagen membrane (Bio-Gide, Geistlich Pharma, Switzerland). The flap was repositioned and sutured with 5 − 0 monofilament sutures (OPTIME, PGA, France). A periapical radiograph was taken immediately to confirm dense obturation (Fig. 2O).

Sutures were removed after 2 weeks with no symptoms or complications. Tooth #8 was restored with a fiber post and core a month after surgery. Then a polymethyl methacrylate (PMMA) provisional crown was performed to guide gingival growth (Fig. 2J). At the follow-up visit 5 months after surgery, appropriate composite resin was placed on the mesio-labial cervical surface of the maxillary right central incisor to further shape the gingival contour (Fig. 2K). At the 6-month postoperative follow-up, periapical radiographs revealed significant healing of the periapical bone around teeth #7 and #8. The gingival margin of tooth #8 was stable and satisfactory, and then a zirconia crown restoration was performed (Fig. 2L).

The patient was followed up post-surgically for 15 months with no clinical signs or symptoms (Fig. 2M-N). Radiographically, the periapical radiograph at the 15-month follow-up revealed a significant bone healing at the periapical area (Fig. 2O-T). CBCT images at the 12-month follow-up revealed a normal periodontal ligament space surrounding the resected root end of teeth #7 and #8, with small bony defects on the palatal side (Fig. 2U-W). Radiographic signs of incomplete healing of teeth #7 and #8 were evaluated according to the criteria of Molven, Penn 3D, and RAC/B et al. [13–15]. Incomplete healing does not appear to predict eventual failure. However, it may indicate a slower or suboptimal bone repair process. Such teeth should be monitored periodically with longer follow-up for any signs of progression. In the absence of clinical signs of pathology, incomplete healing alone does not indicate a poor prognosis.

Case 2

A 30-year-old man was referred for treatment of the maxillary anterior teeth. He complained of swelling and pain in the maxillary anterior region for 6 months, and presented with a 1-month history of sinus tracts. Clinical examination (Fig. 3A-B) revealed teeth #6-#11 were prepared and tender on mild percussion, and exhibited physiological mobility. Gingival height discrepancies between teeth #7 and #8, and between teeth #9 and #10. Electric pulp testing yielded normal responses for teeth #6 and #11. Panoramic radiography and CBCT (Fig. 3C-F) showed that teeth #7-#10 had previous endodontic treatment, and apicectomy without specific evidence of root-end filling. A large lesion was observed encompassing teeth #7-#10 with perforation of buccal and palatal cortical as well as the nasopalatine canal.

Preoperative clinical pictures and radiographic images of case 2. A-B Preoperative intraoral photographs. C Panoramic radiograph. D-F The axial view, the coronal view and the sagittal view of CBCT imaging

Teeth #7-#10 were diagnosed as previously root filled teeth with symptomatic apical periodontitis. Teeth #6 and #11 were diagnosed as tooth defect. After a thorough discussion with the patient, EMS was planned for teeth #7-#10. For teeth #7 and #10, the EMS protocol included simultaneous CLS. Subsequently, a crown restoration was planned for teeth #6-#11.

4% articaine hydrochloride with 1:100,000 epinephrine was administered by infiltration. Surgical guides were used to delineate the final gingival margin (Fig. 4A-B). A rectangular flap was reflected with two vertical incisions distal to Teeth #6 and #11 and an intrasulcular incision including the dental papilla (Fig. 4C-D). For Teeth #7 and #10, osseous resection was performed to remove supporting bone to 4 mm below the apical extent of the preparation’s finish line. Under a dental microscope, EMS included 3-mm root apical resection, retropreparation, and retrofilling (Fig. 4E-G). A collagen membrane (Bio-Gide, Geistlich Pharma, Switzerland) was placed over the bone defect entrance (Fig. 4H-I). The flap was repositioned and sutured with 5 − 0 monofilament sutures, and a periapical radiograph was obtained immediately to confirm adequate obturation (Fig. 4P).

Sutures were removed after 2 weeks with no reported symptoms or complications. Provisional crowns were placed one month after surgery (Fig. 4J). At the 6-month postoperative follow-up, periapical radiographs revealed significant healing of the periapical bone around teeth #7-#10 (Fig. 4Q), with healthy soft tissue and ideal gingival contour (Fig. 4J-K). After 8 months, the gingival margin of teeth #6-#11 was stable and satisfactory, and a zirconia crown restoration was subsequently performed (Fig. 4L-M). The patient underwent 12 months of postoperative follow-up with no clinical signs or symptoms of pathology (Fig. 4N), and expressed high satisfaction with the clinical outcome (Fig. 4O). Compared to the pre-microsurgery, the periapical radiograph and CBCT images (Fig. 4R-V) at the 12 months follow-up revealed significant bone healing with a normal periodontal ligament space around the root cross-sections of teeth #7-#10. Incomplete healing of teeth #7-#10 was observed radiographically.

Discussion

In managing complex clinical cases of anterior apical periodontitis accompanied by aesthetic deficiencies, this report presents two representative cases demonstrating the treatment strategy of EMS combined with CLS and its remarkable effects on functional and aesthetic rehabilitation. This protocol depends on precise infection control while optimizing soft and hard tissue conditions through multidisciplinary collaboration, thereby establishing a biological and aesthetic foundation for definitive restorations.

Case1 presented with root canal wall perforations in tooth #8 which was challenging to correct nonsurgically. Case 2 involved teeth #7–10 with a prior apicoectomy but no retrograde preparation or root-end filling. Treatment failure in both cases was attributed to an inadequate apical seal, and endodontic microsurgery (EMS) was thus deemed the definitive intervention.

Adequate ferrule height is pivotal to long-term restorative success, periodontal health, and aesthetics, as it preserves the biomechanical integrity of endodontically treated teeth [16–18]. A minimum ferrule height of 1.5–2.0 mm is considered optimal [16]. For anterior teeth with coronal defects extending subgingivally, procedures including orthodontic extrusion or CLS are typically indicated for establishing a sufficient ferrule height [19–21]. While orthodontic extrusion preserves alveolar bone, it necessitates prolonged treatment and requires prior control of apical pathology [22, 23]. In contrast, CLS offers a shorter treatment duration, can be synchronized with EMS, accelerates soft tissue healing, and enables timely restoration of aesthetic function. In Case 1, tooth #8 exhibited distal caries extending 1–2 mm subgingivally, necessitating CLS to achieve adequate ferrule height. Additionally, the gingival margin of tooth #8 was positioned apical to that of tooth #9. In Case 2, teeth #7 and #10 exhibited gingival margins > 1 mm coronal to the reference line, resulting in discrepant gingival contours. Sufficient periodontal support was present in both cases to permit CLS, which effectively addressed both functional and aesthetic clinical goals.

The primary indication for the combined application of EMS and CLS is the management of complex cases involving concomitant periapical lesions and aesthetic concerns. However, single-discipline treatments often address only one issue, which potentially leads to compromised healing or prosthetic failure. Given their shared surgical protocols, the integration of EMS and CLS can be performed as a one-stage operation to achieve two objectives. EMS eliminates periapical infection and establishes a hermetic apical seal to facilitate osseous regeneration [3–7]. At the same time, CLS re-establishes the biological width and secures adequate ferrule height to ensure prosthetic stability and gingival aesthetics [16–19].

A critical consideration in restorative planning is that increasing clinical ferrule height inherently raises the crown-to-root ratio [24]. For anterior teeth, the optimal crown-to-root ratio is 1:1.5 to 1:2, with a maximum allowable limit of 1:1 [25]. Evidence demonstrates that resecting less than 6 mm of apical root structure in periodontally sound maxillary central incisors preserves biomechanical integrity without significant compromise [26]. Conversely, loss of periodontal bone support more than 2 mm significantly impairs biomechanical performance [27]. Therefore, the key to a favorable restorative prognosis in anterior teeth with extensive coronal loss lies in balancing ferrule height establishment with preservation of an optimal crown-to-root ratio. In both cases, apical resection was limited to < 6 mm from the apex, and bone resection during CLS did not exceed 2 mm. This approach preserved a palatal ferrule, a key determinant of fracture resistance in post-and-core restorations [28–30].

Following CLS, a provisional crown may be placed after 1–3 weeks, coinciding with initial soft tissue healing [19]. A well-designed provisional restoration protects the prepared abutment, meets aesthetic requirements, and guides favorable soft tissue adaptation without disrupting biological width re-establishment [31]. Evidence indicates that gingival rebound was observed 3-month postsurgically, with stabilization typically occurring between 3 and 6 months [32, 33]. At this point, the definitive restoration is delivered. For aesthetic zone teeth, an extended observation period is recommended, with definitive restoration delayed for over 6 months [19, 34]. Soft tissue healing timelines following EMS flap procedures align with CLS protocols. However, periapical osseous healing requires up to 4 months for initial maturation, while full bone remodeling and maturation may persist for 6–12 months [35]. In this report, provisional crowns placed at 3–6 weeks postoperatively enabled definitive restorations at 6–12 months, yielding favorable gingival contours and high patient satisfaction.

Although satisfactory clinical outcomes were achieved in both cases, the primary limitations of this report are its small sample size (two cases) and the relatively short follow-up period. These factors limit the generalizability of our findings and underscore the need for future studies with larger patient cohorts and longer observation periods to confirm the long-term efficacy of the presented approach. In addition, while crown lengthening surgery successfully gained adequate clinical crown and restorative space, the gingival contour was unfortunately not fully harmonious in both cases.

In conclusion, a comprehensive and systematic treatment plan was designed via multidisciplinary assessment by combining the application of EMS and CLS. This approach effectively eradicated infection, accelerated osseous healing, and supported ideal functional and aesthetic rehabilitation of the anterior teeth, which may be a feasible option in carefully selected complex cases.
