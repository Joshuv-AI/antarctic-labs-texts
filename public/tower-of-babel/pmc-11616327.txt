Evaluation of Sarcopenia screening indices as predictors of mortality in older patients with Alzheimer’s disease
Authors: Xinjie Song, Sha Huang, Mei Li, Xiaoyan Chen
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThe study evaluated the effectiveness of the sarcopenia indices neutrophils/lymphocytes, platelets/lymphocytes, AST/ALT, and creatinine (Cr)/ cystatin C (CysC)*100 in predicting mortality in hospitalized patients with Alzheimer’s disease (AD) aged 60 years or older.MeasurementsThis retrospective observational survey was undertaken in a teaching hospital in western China from January 1, 2017, to December 30, 2022. The neutrophil/lymphocyte, platelet/lymphocyte, AST/ALT, and Cr/CysC*100 ratios were used to assess the presence of sarcopenia, with the upper quartiles used as the cutoff value. Information on all-cause mortality was obtained through telephone interviews or electronic medical records between June 1, 2024, and June 20, 2024. Overall survival (OS) represented the time from hospital admission to death/final follow-up. Cox proportional hazards models were applied to determine the relationships between the above parameters and mortality from all causes.ResultsThe information on 523 patients with AD was retrieved from the electronic medical record system. Of these, 329 were finally enrolled, all of whom were hospitalized and over the age of 60 years. The use of Cr/Cys C*100 as a sarcopenia indicator was found to be effective in predicting mortality (24.39% vs. 13.77% for patients with sarcopenia vs. those without, P = 0.024). However, the application of neutrophils/lymphocytes, platelets/lymphocytes, and AST/ALT as indicators showed no marked differences between the sarcopenia and non-sarcopenia participants. After further logistic regression analysis and correction of possible variables, participants with sarcopenia had an increased risk of death relative to those without (HR = 2.179, 95%CI: 1.175–4.044).Conclusions and implicationsThis study showed that only Cr/CysC*100 was effective in the prediction of mortality in older individuals with AD and sarcopenia and that neutrophils/lymphocytes, platelets/lymphocytes, and AST/ALT were not effective as predictors.

Introduction

Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is responsible for neurodegeneration and associated dementia [1]. Approximately 50 million of the global population suffer from dementia, but due to population aging, it is estimated that by 2050, the number of AD sufferers will increase from 5.8 million to 13.8 million in America [2]. The global costs for dementia care were estimated at $818 billion in 2010 and may reach $2 trillion by 20301. In addition, 2019 statistics show that AD represented the sixth most frequent contributor to mortality in the USA, with an increase of over 145% in AD-related deaths between 2000 and 2019 [3]. Sarcopenia is significantly associated with AD [4]. Unsal et al. found that 38.3% of older patients with AD had sarcopenia [5].

Sarcopenia is a condition characterized by reduced mass and strength of the skeletal muscles. It has been linked with various adverse clinical outcomes, such as falls and functional decline, as well as death [6, 7]. Measuring muscle mass is essential for diagnosing sarcopenia [8]. It can be assessed with dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA), computed tomography (CT), or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). These techniques, however, have disadvantages, in their use of radiation, lengthy examinations, and expense [6]. Muscle mass can also be estimated by bioelectrical impedance analysis (BIA) using an equation, but the results are affected by food intake, hydration, and physical exercise [6]. An additional factor is the need for cooperation by the patient in completing the procedure, which may be difficult for older adults with cognitive impairment. Therefore, a screening method that is simple, reliable, inexpensive, and widely applicable in a variety of scenarios is necessary.

Zhao et al. recommended the use of neutrophils/lymphocytes and platelets/lymphocytes as a method for screening sarcopenia, also known as the sarcopenia index (SI) [9]. These parameters have been linked with poor clinical prognosis (death), including death in the short-term due to acute pulmonary embolism [10], following endovascular repair of aneurysms in the abdominal aorta [11], in patients receiving hemodialysis [12], those with ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction following percutaneous coronary intervention [13], and subsequent to foot amputation due to diabetic ulceration [14]. Brisendine et al. recommended using creatinine (Cr)/ cystatin C (CysC)*100 in the serum as SI [15], while Yin et al. proposed that AST/ALT offered the greatest predictive capability of the SI assessed (area under the curve = 0.682) [16]. Our team has previously demonstrated the effectiveness of both Cr/CysC*100 and AST/ALT in identifying sarcopenia and mortality prediction in cases of community-acquired pneumonia [17]. These factors are routinely assessed in older adults when admitted to hospital and thus do not incur any additional costs or burden to the patient.

However, there has been no exploration of the use of neutrophils/lymphocytes, platelets/lymphocytes, AST/ALT, and Cr/Cys C*100 in sarcopenia screening or outcome prediction in patients with AD. Therefore, here, was to investigate the effectiveness of neutrophils/lymphocytes, platelets/lymphocytes, AST/ALT, and Cr/Cys C*100 in sarcopenia screening and mortality risk prediction in hospitalized older (≥ 60 years old) patients with AD.

Methods

Study design and participants

The investigation was a retrospective study. It was conducted at a teaching hospital in western China between January 1, 2017, and December 30, 2022. The inclusion criteria necessitated the simultaneous meeting of the three criteria, namely, admission to our hospital, diagnosis with AD, and age ≥ 60 years. The exclusion criteria were any of the following: (1) Missing information on neutrophils, lymphocytes, platelets, lymphocytes, Cr, CysC, ALT, or AST, or ALT levels > 120 U/L [18] or an estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) < 15 ml/min/1.73 m2 [19] (55 cases); (2) Mental illnesses or cognitive impairment caused by other conditions (such as vascular dementia, Lewy body dementia) (20 cases); (3) Lost to follow-up (113 cases). The diagnosis of AD was made according to the AD diagnostic criteria of the International Classification of Disease, tenth edition (ICD-10), with confirmation by a chief psychiatrist and a chief physician.

Ethics

A review of the protocol and data anonymization were performed by the Center for Health Informatics. The study followed the Declaration of Helsinki, with strict confidentiality of patient information. The informed consent requirement was waived as the study was retrospective. Ethical approval was provided by the institution (No. 2023008).

Sarcopenia indicators

Biochemical and hematological data collected on admission were retrieved, including information on neutrophils, lymphocytes, platelets, lymphocytes, AST, ALT, Cr, and CysC. The ratios of neutrophils/lymphocytes, platelets/lymphocytes, AST/ALT, and Cr/CysC*100 were calculated and assessed as screening indicators for sarcopenia, using the upper quartiles as the critical values [20].

In cases where the patient had been hospitalized on numerous occasions, only the data from the initial admission was used; thus, the values were not determined using strictly fasting venous blood. However, as ratios were used, this had no marked effect on the findings.

Overall survival

Information on mortality from all causes was obtained through telephone interviews or from electronic medical record systems between June 1, 2024, and June 20, 2024. In the telephonic interviews, information was acquired from either the patient or a family member; if there was no answer to the call, the patient was recorded as lost to follow-up and their data were not analyzed. Between two and three contact numbers were included in the medical record system and calls were made by dedicated personnel. Death dates were recorded. Overall survival (OS) represented the time from hospital admission to death/final follow-up.

Covariate collection

The information collected included age, sex, history of smoking, history of drinking, state of consciousness, educational level, marital status, disease duration, and activities of daily living (ADL) score, together with the presence of comorbidities, such as hypertension, diabetes, hyperlipidemia, stroke history, coronary heart disease (CHD), chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), chronic heart failure (CHF), and chronic respiratory failure (CRF). An ADL score of 40 points was used as a cutoff value [21], with points below 40 indicating that the person requires complete assistance in life, and 40 and above points indicating that the person either requires no assistance or only partial assistance.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS 25.0. Continuous variables were compared using t-tests or rank-sum tests, and are shown as means ± standard deviation for normally distributed data and medians for non-normally distributed data. Categorical variables are shown as number (percentage) and were analyzed using chi-square tests. Two-sided P-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Cox proportional hazards models were applied to determine the relationships between neutrophils/lymphocytes, platelets/lymphocytes, AST/ALT, Cr/CysC*100, and the risk of all-cause mortality. Three models were constructed: Model 1 was uncorrected to examine the correlation between SI and death; Model 2 included variables found to be significantly associated with death (P < 0.05) as shown in Table 1, to confirm the association between SI and death; Model 3 included variables found to be highly significantly (P < 0.1) associated with death (Table 1) and reported in previous studies for further confirmation of the correlation between SI and death.

Results

The information on 523 patients with AD was retrieved from the electronic medical records system. Of these, 329 were finally enrolled after excluding those not meeting the criteria (Fig. 1). One hundred and forty-three patients (43.47%) were male. Overall, the median ages of patients who died and survived were 84 and 79 years old, respectively. Fifty-four (16.41%) died. In those with sarcopenia, the ratios were neutrophils/lymphocytes > 1.88, AST/ALT > 1.15, Cr/CysC*100 ≤ 53.68, and platelets/lymphocytes ≤ 86.12, while the ratios of neutrophils/lymphocytes ≤ 1.88, AST/ALT ≤ 1.15, Cr/CysC*100 > 53.68, and platelets/lymphocytes > 86.12 were seen in the non-sarcopenia group.

Surviving and non-surviving participants differed significantly in terms of age, drinking history, ADL score, and CRF levels (Table 1), while no marked differences were found in terms of sex, smoking history, educational level, marital status, disease duration, history of stroke, hypertension, diabetes, hyperlipidemia, COPD, CHD, and CHF (Table 1).

When Cr/CysC*100 was used as a sarcopenia indicator, it was found that mortality was markedly greater in participants with sarcopenia relative to those without (24.39% vs. 13.77%, P = 0.024, Table 2). Discussion

This investigation enrolled 329 participants with AD and evaluated the use of four indicators of sarcopenia, namely, neutrophils/lymphocytes, platelets/lymphocytes, AST/ALT, and Cr/CysC*100, for predicting mortality risk. This represents the first exploration of the use of various sarcopenia indicators for the prediction of mortality risk in this population. The results showed that only Cr/CysC*100 was effective in predicting mortality risk in older patients with AD. This is a meaningful discovery. These findings identify a simple, reliable, inexpensive, and widely used method for mortality prediction in individuals with AD. In clinical management, the findings of this study would assist medical personnel in the identification of individuals with AD with a likelihood of poor prognosis. Since sarcopenia is reversible [6], the implementation of specific interventions for these patients (such as adjusting dietary patterns and increasing moderate exercise) may improve the prognosis [7].

The results showed that the presence of sarcopenia can predict the risk of death in individuals with AD. These findings are consistent with those of Zhang et al. [22]. It is well-documented that both sarcopenia and AD are associated with inflammation, suggesting a common underlying mechanism [23]. An association between the levels of inflammatory factors, such as the interleukins (IL)-6 and IL-1, tumor necrosis factor-α, and CRP, and higher mortality has been observed in older adults [23]. In addition, people with sarcopenia often have reduced exercise capacity, which has been shown to aggravate the link between sarcopenia and poor outcomes in this demographic [24].

The study findings did not identify a link between neutrophils/lymphocytes, platelets/lymphocytes, and AST/ALT and mortality risk in patients with AD. The following reasons may be considered: 1. Both neutrophils/lymphocytes and platelets/lymphocytes represent markers of inflammation that reflect the degree of systemic inflammation [25] and may therefore be affected by the inflammation associated with AD itself. There is a complex association between cognitive function and AST/ALT. Both AST and ALT are known to be involved in the generation of glutamate, considered the primary neurotransmitter of excitatory neurons in the brain and closely associated with optimal brain functioning [26]. An association has been reported between AST/ALT with AD plasma biomarkers [27] with the suggestion that the ratio may contribute to impaired cognition by affecting the volume of the hippocampus [26]. This may explain why AST/ALT is a poor indicator for the prediction of mortality in patients with AD and sarcopenia.

In previously published studies, advanced age [28], sex [28], cerebrovascular disease [28], alcohol consumption [28], ADL score [29], and hyperlipidemia [28] were all found to be associated with the risk of death in patients with AD, while other studies showed an association between CRF and adverse clinical outcomes [30]. Therefore, in the present study, we included the above variables for correction. However, this study did not observe any difference in terms of sex in the survival of AD patients, although a study by Xue et al. found that female sex was an independent risk factor for mortality in patients with AD [28]. This discrepancy can be explained by differences in the participant populations of the two studies. Specifically, first, the subjects of Xue et al.‘s study were mainly community-dwelling older people with mild cognitive impairment [28], while the subjects of the present study were hospitalized patients diagnosed with AD. Secondly, the study by Xue et al. was prospective in design [28], while the present study was retrospective. Furthermore, the numbers of participants in both studies were relatively small, and large-scale prospective studies are needed to explore this issue.

There are several limitations to this investigation. First, it was retrospective in design and was undertaken at a single institution, potentially incurring selection bias. The number of participants was also relatively low, preventing classification into subgroups according to disease severity and type. In addition, since the early symptoms of AD are not typical and it was difficult for family members to remember the exact time of onset, clinicians often write more than 5 years, more than 10 years, or many years when assembling medical records, we divided the disease duration into 5-year periods based on the medical records. Due to the limited number of patients enrolled, the disease duration was not broken down further, for example, into 1, 3, 5, or 10 years. This also represents a limitation. Second, patients who had not undergone CysC testing were excluded, potentially leading to bias in the data analysis. These factors may have influenced the generalizability of the study findings to the overall population. Third, several published studies used quartiles as the critical value for grouping [20], while others used the median [17], and there is no generally accepted critical value. This study adopted the statistical method of using quartiles as the critical value for grouping as in previous investigations. This may also represent a limitation. Future studies should divide the SI cutoff value according to the gold standard for diagnosing sarcopenia to make SI more universal [8].

Conclusions

A close association was found between Cr/CysC*100 and increased mortality risk in older adults with AD and sarcopenia. However, there was no link between neutrophils/lymphocytes, platelets/lymphocytes, and AST/ALT with the risk of death in this population.
