Arterial Pressure Control for Acute Aortic Dissection
Authors: Richard Descamps, Florimond Suard, Jean-Luc Hanouz
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Corresponding AuthorKey Words: antihypertensive agents, aortic dissection, hypertensive emergency, hypoxia

In this issue of JACC: Case Reports, Fozouni et al1 describe a challenging management of acute respiratory failure after the introduction of intravenous nicardipine for a patient with type B aortic dissection. In addition to describing a little-known side effect of a commonly used treatment, this case highlights the importance of the connection between physiopathologic and pharmacologic knowledge for the management of acute aortic dissections.

Acute aortic dissection is a life-threatening emergency affecting approximately 3 patients per 100,000 per year and is typically associated with a hypertensive emergency. Acute aortic dissection mandates immediate blood pressure control to limit extension along the aorta and ideally achieve a systolic blood pressure of 100 to 120 mm Hg. Intravenous β-blockers (eg, esmolol, labetalol) are the first-line treatments.2 Other intravenous antihypertensive agents can be used if intravenous β-blockers are unavailable, are poorly tolerated, are contraindicated, or in the case of refractory hypertension requiring a polydrug approach. Dihydropyridine calcium channel inhibitors (DCCIs) (eg, nicardipine, clevidipine) are commonly used in such cases. Nicardipine is widely used in acute aortic dissection because of its short onset but long duration of action,3 its effectiveness in reducing systemic vascular resistance, its positive effects on coronary perfusion, and its safety in acute aortic dissection.4

However, increasing evidence from case reports and preliminary studies indicates that intravenous DCCIs may have deleterious respiratory effects (eg, hypoxia). The supposed underlying phenomenon is the inhibition of hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction, a physiological mechanism that involves calcium-dependent vascular smooth muscle cell contraction occurring in the pulmonary vasculature in the context of alveolar hypoxia. A recent pharmacovigilance study assessing the relationship between DCCI usage and hypoxemia within the World Health Organization’s VigiBase database confirmed an association between intravenous DCCI administration and reports of hypoxemia,5 and a clinical study assessing this side effect in intensive care patients is ongoing (NCT05155202).

As described in this issue of JACC: Case Reports, aortic dissection represents a typical situation in which the respiratory effects of the DCCIs may be exacerbated. Indeed, patients with aortic dissection often present with cardiovascular symptoms initially, but they can present with associated acute pulmonary symptoms. The computed tomography findings in patients with acute aortic dissection are typically pleural effusion, hydrostatic pulmonary edema, obstruction of a pulmonary artery causing pulmonary perfusion defect, and lung ischemia.6 In the case of a type A aortic dissection, pulmonary atelectasis is frequent and related at least in part to general anesthesia and the absence of lung ventilation during the cardiopulmonary bypass necessary for surgical treatment. Moreover, in an aged population affected by chronic hypertension, patients with aortic dissection can present with an underlying chronic pulmonary disease affecting the postoperative course.
