Abstract
. In this series of papers, we investigate properties of a finite group which are determined by its low degree irreducible representations over a number field F F , i.e. its representations on matrix rings M n ⁡ ( D ) \operatorname{M}_{n}(D) with n ≤ 2 n\leq 2 . In particular we focus on representations on M 2 ⁡ ( D ) \operatorname{M}_{2}(D) where D D is a division algebra having an order 𝒪 \mathcal{O} such that 𝒪 \mathcal{O} has finitely many units, i.e. such that SL 2 ⁡ ( 𝒪 ) \operatorname{SL}_{2}(\mathcal{O}) has arithmetic rank 1 1 . In this first part, the focus is on two aspects. One aspect concerns characterisations of such representing spaces in terms of Serre’s homological goodness property, small virtual cohomological dimension and higher Kleinian-type embeddings. As an application, we obtain several characterisations of the finite groups G G whose irreducible representations are of the mentioned form. In particular, such groups G G are precisely those such that 𝒰 ⁡ ( R ​ G ) {\mathcal{U}}(RG) , with R R the ring of integers of F F , can be constructed from groups which virtually map onto a non-abelian free group. Along the way we investigate the latter property for congruence subgroups of higher modular groups and its implications for the congruence kernel. This is used to obtain new information on the congruence kernel of the unit group of a group ring. The second aspect concerns the conjugacy classes of the images of finite subgroups of 𝒰 ⁡ ( R ​ G ) {\mathcal{U}}(RG) under the irreducible representations of G G . More precisely, we initiate the study of a blockwise variant of the Zassenhaus conjectures and the subgroup isomorphism problem. Moreover, we contribute to them for the low rank representations above.

Homological and topological characterisation of exceptional components

In this section we consider various homological and topological characterisations of exceptional matrix algebras. These characterisations, along with the results in

4.1.
Groups of virtual cohomological dimension 4

Let
\Gamma
be a discrete group. Then the

of
\Gamma
over the ring

\operatorname{hdim}_{R}\Gamma:=\min\{n\mid H^{k}(\Gamma,M)=0\text{ for all }k>n\text{ and }M\in{\rm mod}(R\Gamma)\}.

If no such

\operatorname{vcd}(\Gamma):=\{\operatorname{hdim}_{{\mathbb{Z}}}\Lambda\mid[\Gamma:\Lambda]<\infty\text{ and }\Lambda\text{ torsion-free}\}.

The main result of this section is the following characterisation of
\operatorname{(M_{exc})}

.

5.3.
Congruence kernel of higher modular groups

Let
F
be a number field and
S
a non-empty finite set of places of
F
containing the Archimedean places. Associated is the ring of
S
-integers
\mathcal{O}_{S}=\{x\in F\mid|x|_{v}\leq 1\text{ for all }v\notin S\}
. Now consider a linear algebraic
F
-group
\mathbf{G}
and fix an
F
-embedding
\mathbf{G}\hookrightarrow\operatorname{GL}_{n}(F)
. Using this, the group of
S
-integral points is defined as
\mathbf{G}(\mathcal{O}_{S}):=\mathbf{G}(F)\cap\operatorname{GL}_{n}(\mathcal{O}_{S})
. Any subgroup
\Gamma
of
\mathbf{G}(F)
commensurable with
\mathbf{G}(\mathcal{O}_{S})
is called an
S
-arithmetic subgroup
. In this generality, a principal congruence subgroup of
\Gamma
is a subgroup of the form
\Gamma(J):=\Gamma\cap\mathbf{G}(J)
for an ideal
J
of
\mathcal{O}_{S}
, and

\mathbf{G}(J):=\mathbf{G}(\mathcal{O}_{S})\cap\operatorname{SL}_{n}(J)=\{g\in\mathbf{G}(\mathcal{O}_{S})\mid g\equiv 1\text{ mod }J\}.

As
\mathcal{O}_{S}/J
is finite, all the groups
\Gamma(J)
have finite index in
\Gamma
.

The congruence topology on
\Gamma
has the subgroups
\Gamma(J)
as basis of neighbourhoods of the identity, whereas the profinite topology considers all finite index subgroups. Denote by
\widetilde{\Gamma}
and
\widehat{\Gamma}
the respective completions. The
subgroup congruence problem
(
SCP
) asks whether all finite index subgroups contain a principal congruence subgroup. In other words, whether the natural epimorphism

\pi_{\Gamma}:\widehat{\Gamma}\rightarrow\widetilde{\Gamma}

is injective. The kernel
C(\Gamma):=\ker(\pi_{\Gamma})
is called the
congruence kernel
. Serre formulated a quantitative version of (
SCP
) by asking to compute
C(\Gamma)
and conjecturing that it is finite if and only if the
S
-rank of
\Gamma
is at least two.

In our setting, we consider a finite-dimensional division
{\mathbb{Q}}
-algebra
D
, a free
D
-module
V
of rank two and
\mathbf{G}=\operatorname{GL}_{V}
the algebraic
{\mathbb{Q}}
-group of automorphisms of the
D
-module
V
. Hence for any
{\mathbb{Q}}
-algebra
A
, the group
\operatorname{GL}_{V}(A)
is the group of automorphisms of the
(D\otimes_{{\mathbb{Q}}}A)
-module
V\otimes_{{\mathbb{Q}}}A
. More precisely,
D=\left(\frac{u,v}{{\mathbb{Q}}}\right)
for some strictly negative integers
u,v\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{<0}
and
\Gamma
is any arithmetic subgroup of
\mathbf{G}({\mathbb{Q}})
. For convenience, we consider an order
\mathcal{O}
in
D=\left(\frac{u,v}{{\mathbb{Q}}}\right)
(which were always assumed to be
{\mathbb{Z}}
-orders) and assume that
\Gamma
is a finite index subgroup of
\operatorname{SL}_{2}(\mathcal{O})
.

In case that
\Gamma=\operatorname{SL}_{2}(\mathcal{O})
, then by
[
74
, Theorem (22.4)]

\widetilde{\operatorname{SL}_{2}(\mathcal{O})}=\operatorname{SL}_{2}(\widehat{\mathcal{O}})
where
\widehat{\mathcal{O}}
is the profinite completion of
\mathcal{O}
, i.e.
\widehat{\mathcal{O}}
is the inverse limit of all
\mathcal{O}/J
with
J
a non-zero ideal of
\mathcal{O}
. The ring
\widehat{\mathcal{O}}
is a profinite ring and so
\operatorname{GL}_{2}(\widehat{\mathcal{O}})
is a profinite group.

We denote by
F_{\omega}
the free group of countable rank and by
\widehat{F_{\omega}}
its profinite completion.

Theorem 5.7
.

Let
u,v\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{<0}
be strictly negative integers,
\mathcal{O}
an order in
\left(\frac{u,v}{{\mathbb{Q}}}\right)
and
\Gamma
a finite index subgroup of
\operatorname{SL}_{2}(\mathcal{O})
. The congruence kernel
C(\Gamma)
contains a closed subgroup isomorphic to
\widehat{F_{\omega}}
.

Via the comparison map
\chi:\operatorname{SL}_{+}(\Gamma_{4}({\mathbb{R}}))\rightarrow\operatorname{SL}_{2}\left(\left(\frac{-1,-1}{{\mathbb{R}}}\right)\right)
,
Theorem

5.1
was obtained as a consequence of the similar statement for the congruence subgroups
\operatorname{Con}_{n}
of the higher modular groups
\operatorname{SL}_{+}\left(\Gamma_{4}^{u,v}({\mathbb{Z}})\right)
, i.e.
Theorem

5.3
. This time, through that same comparison, the analogue of
Theorem

5.7
for the higher modular groups follows as a corollary. More precisely, the principal congruence subgroups of
\operatorname{SL}_{+}\left(\Gamma_{4}^{u,v}({\mathbb{Z}})\right)
are by definition the finite index subgroups
\operatorname{Con}_{n}
. Hence we can consider the completions
\widehat{\operatorname{SL}_{+}\left(\Gamma_{4}^{u,v}({\mathbb{Z}})\right)}
and
\widetilde{\operatorname{SL}_{+}\left(\Gamma_{4}^{u,v}({\mathbb{Z}})\right)}
as above and the associated natural epimorphism and its kernel, again called the congruence kernel.

Corollary 5.8
.

Let
u,v\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{<0}
be strictly negative integers, then the congruence kernel of
\operatorname{SL}_{+}\left(\Gamma_{4}^{u,v}({\mathbb{Z}})\right)
contains a closed subgroup isomorphic to
\widehat{F_{\omega}}
.

Proof of
Theorem

5.7
.

The proof proceeds as for
[
59
, Theorem B]
. Namely, reading between the lines in
loc. cit.
, one obtains a reduction to the following claim.

Claim:
Let
X
be a set with
|X|\geq 2
and
n\geq 1
. The profinite group
\widehat{F(X)}
is not embedded as a profinite group in
\widetilde{\operatorname{SL}_{n}(\mathcal{O})}=\operatorname{SL}_{n}(\widehat{\mathcal{O}})
.

We now explain how the claim indeed implies
Theorem

5.7
. Let
\Gamma_{f}
be a finite index subgroup of
\Gamma
mapping onto a free group
F_{m}
of rank
m\geq 2
, as yielded by
Theorem

5.1
. It is well-known that this implies that
\widehat{\Gamma_{f}}\leq\widehat{\operatorname{SL}_{2}(\mathcal{O})}
has a subgroup
Z
isomorphic to
\widehat{F_{m}}
. Indeed,
\widehat{F_{m}}
being a free profinite group, the epimorphism from
\Gamma_{f}
onto
F_{m}
splits. The claim now implies that
Z\cap C(\Gamma)
is non-trivial as otherwise
Z\cong\widehat{F_{m}}
would embed into
\widehat{\Gamma}/C(\widehat{\Gamma})=\widetilde{\Gamma}\leq\widetilde{\operatorname{SL}_{2}(\mathcal{O})}
, which would contradict the above claim.

It follows from
[
59
, Theorem 2.1.(a)]
that the non-trivial normal subgroup
Z\cap C(\Gamma)
of
Z\cong\widehat{F_{m}}
contains
\widehat{F_{\omega}}
. In particular also
C(\Gamma)
contains
\widehat{F_{\omega}}
, as desired. Thus it indeed remains to prove the claim.

First note that it is enough to obtain the claim in case that
\mathcal{O}
is a maximal order, which we assume from now on. Suppose that
\widehat{F(X)}
embeds continuously into
\operatorname{SL}_{n}(\widehat{\mathcal{O}})
. Since every finite group is a quotient of a subgroup of
\widehat{F(X)}
, the same holds for
\operatorname{SL}_{n}(\widehat{\mathcal{O}})
. We will show that the latter however does not hold. If
H
is a quotient of a subgroup of
\operatorname{SL}_{n}(\widehat{\mathcal{O}})=\varprojlim\operatorname{SL}_{n}(\mathcal{O}/J),
then it must be of some
\operatorname{SL}_{n}(\mathcal{O}/J)
. Since
J
is a two-sided ideal in a maximal order (over a Dedekind domain) one can decompose
J
into a product of prime ideals
[
74
, Theorem (22.10)]
, say
J=\prod_{i=1}^{\ell}P_{i}^{k_{i}}
. Hence
H
is the quotient of a subgroup of some
\operatorname{SL}_{n}(\mathcal{O}/P_{i}^{m_{i}})
. Now, if
H
is a non-abelian simple group, then it does not intersect the kernel of the map from
\operatorname{SL}_{n}(\mathcal{O}/P_{i}^{m_{i}})
to
\operatorname{SL}_{n}(\mathcal{O}/P_{i})
, as the kernel is nilpotent. Hence such
H
is the quotient of a subgroup of some
\operatorname{SL}_{n}(\mathcal{O}/P_{i})
. By
[
74
, Theorem (22.3)]

\mathcal{O}/P_{i}
is a finite-dimensional simple algebra over the residue class field
{\mathbb{Z}}/(P_{i}\cap{\mathbb{Z}})
. Moreover,
P_{i}
lies over some prime ideal
(p_{i})
of
{\mathbb{Z}}
. Then,
p_{i}\mathcal{O}=P_{i}^{m_{i}}
with
m_{i}
the index of the division algebra
\left(\frac{u,v}{{\mathbb{Q}}_{p_{i}}}\right)
.

If
\left(\frac{u,v}{{\mathbb{Q}}_{p_{i}}}\right)\cong\operatorname{M}_{2}({\mathbb{Q}}_{p_{i}})
is split, then
\mathcal{O}/P_{i}\cong\operatorname{M}_{2}({\mathbb{F}}_{p})
by
[
74
, Theorem (22.4)]
. Furthermore, if
\left(\frac{u,v}{{\mathbb{Q}}_{p_{i}}}\right)
is still a division algebra, then
\mathcal{O}/P_{i}\cong{\mathbb{F}}_{p^{2}}
. In conclusion,
\operatorname{SL}_{n}(\mathcal{O}/P_{i})
is of the form
\operatorname{SL}_{dn}({\mathbb{F}}_{p^{t}})
for some
d,t\in{\mathbb{N}}
. It follows from
[
59
, Lemma 2.4.(b)]
that there is a finite non-abelian simple group which is not the quotient of subgroup of
\operatorname{SL}_{dn}({\mathbb{F}}_{p^{t}})
for any
p
, yielding the desired contradiction and as such finishing the proof.
∎

6.
Applications:
\operatorname{(M_{exc})}
through
\operatorname{vQL}
and congruence kernel unit group

Let
\mathcal{O}
be an order in an exceptional matrix algebra
A
. In
Section

5
we obtained that
\operatorname{SL}_{1}(\mathcal{O})
has a finite index subgroup mapping onto a non-abelian free group. Subsequently, the latter property was used to obtain information on the congruence kernel of
\operatorname{SL}_{1}(\mathcal{O})
. In this section we apply the aforementioned results to the unit group of
RG
with
R
the ring of integers in a number field.

6.1.
Characterisation of
\operatorname{(M_{exc})}
via largeness of (higher) modular groups

Recall that we say that a group
\Gamma

has property
\operatorname{vQL}
if it has a finite index subgroup which maps onto a non-abelian free group. This property is preserved under commensurability. Now consider the following class of groups:

\mathcal{G}_{\operatorname{vQL}}:=\left\{\Gamma\mid\Gamma\text{ is virtually indecomposable and has }\operatorname{vQL}\right\}\sqcup\{\text{ finite groups }\},

and the associated class
\prod\mathcal{G}_{\operatorname{vQL}}
defined in (
2.2
). Recall that a group
\Gamma
is called
virtually indecomposable
if
\Gamma
can only be commensurable to a direct product
\Gamma_{1}\times\Gamma_{2}
if
\Gamma_{1}
or
\Gamma_{2}
is finite.

Theorem 6.1
.

Let
G
be a finite group,
F
a number field and
R
its ring of integers. Then the following are equivalent:

(1)

{\mathcal{U}}(RG)
is virtually-
\prod\mathcal{G}_{\operatorname{vQL}}
.

(2)

FG
has
\operatorname{(M_{exc})}
and no exceptional division components.

Moreover,
FG
has
\operatorname{(M_{exc})}
if and only if
\operatorname{SL}_{1}(RGe)
has
\operatorname{vQL}
for all
e\in\operatorname{PCI}(FG)
such that
FGe
is not a division algebra.

A main ingredient for the equivalence between (
1
) and (
2
) is
Lemma

2.1
. In particular, we need to show that the class of virtually-
\prod\mathcal{G}_{\operatorname{vQL}}
groups satisfies the necessary properties. The proof of
[
51
, Theorem 2.1]
is particularly instructive for the direct factor property.

Lemma 6.2
.

With notations as above, the class of groups which are virtually-
\prod\mathcal{G}_{\operatorname{vQL}}
satisfies the following:

•

If
\Gamma_{1}
and
\Gamma_{2}
are commensurable and
\Gamma_{1}
is virtually-
\prod\mathcal{G}_{\operatorname{vQL}}
, then so is
\Gamma_{2}
.

•

If
\prod_{i=1}^{m}\Gamma_{i}\times\Gamma
is virtually-
\prod\mathcal{G}_{\operatorname{vQL}}
, where
\Gamma_{i}
is virtually indecomposable and
\Gamma
finitely generated abelian, then each
\Gamma_{i}
is either finite or virtually-
\prod\mathcal{G}_{\operatorname{vQL}}
.

Proof.

Firstly note that the class
\mathcal{G}_{\operatorname{vQL}}
is closed under commensurability. This implies that the class of virtually-
\prod\mathcal{G}_{\operatorname{vQL}}
groups is so as well. Indeed, suppose that
\Gamma_{1}
and
\Gamma_{2}
are two groups such that
\Gamma_{1}\cap\Gamma_{2}
has finite index in both
\Gamma_{i}
. Clearly if
\Gamma_{1}\cap\Gamma_{2}
is virtually-
\prod\mathcal{G}_{\operatorname{vQL}}
, then so is each
\Gamma_{i}
. Hence we may assume that
\Gamma_{2}
is a finite index subgroup of
\Gamma_{1}
and it remains to consider the case that
\Gamma_{1}
is virtually-
\prod\mathcal{G}_{\operatorname{vQL}}
. Let
\prod_{i=1}^{q}H_{i}
be a finite index subgroup of
\Gamma_{1}
with
H_{i}
either finitely generated abelian or in
\mathcal{G}_{\operatorname{vQL}}
. Since
H_{i}\cap\Gamma_{2}
has finite index in
H_{i}
, and
\mathcal{G}_{\operatorname{vQL}}
is closed under commensurability, we obtain that
H_{i}\cap\Gamma_{2}
is either finitely generated abelian or in
\mathcal{G}_{\operatorname{vQL}}
. Therefore
\prod_{i=1}^{q}(H_{i}\cap\Gamma_{2})
is a finite index subgroup of
\Gamma_{2}
and a member of
\prod\mathcal{G}_{\operatorname{vQL}}
, proving the claim.

Now we consider the closedness under direct factors as in
Remark

2.2
. Let
\prod_{i=1}^{m}\Gamma_{i}\times\Gamma
be virtually-
\prod\mathcal{G}_{\operatorname{vQL}}
, with
\Gamma
a finitely generated abelian group and
\Gamma_{i}
non-abelian virtually indecomposable groups.

If
\prod_{i=1}^{m}\Gamma_{i}
is finite, then there is nothing to prove. Hence assume that some factors
\Gamma_{i}
are infinite. Now take a finite index subgroup
H
which is in
\prod\mathcal{G}_{\operatorname{vQL}}
. By possibly going over to a finite index subgroup of
H
, we may assume that
H
decomposes as

H={\mathbb{Z}}^{\ell}\times\prod_{x\in X}H_{x},

with
H_{x}
mapping onto a non-abelian free group and
X\neq\emptyset
. Note that each
H_{x}
is infinite.

Denote by
\pi_{x}
the projection of
H
onto
H_{x}
. Further, consider
S_{i}:=\Gamma_{i}\cap H
for each
1\leq i\leq m
and
B:=\Gamma\cap H
. Since
H
has finite index in
\prod_{i}\Gamma_{i}\times\Gamma
, the group
S_{i}
has finite index in
\Gamma_{i}
and
S:=\prod_{i=1}^{m}S_{i}\times B
has finite index in
H
. In particular,
S_{i}
is virtually indecomposable.

For the remainder of the proof fix some
1\leq i\leq m
such that
\Gamma_{i}
is infinite. We need to show that
\Gamma_{i}
is virtually-
\prod\mathcal{G}_{\operatorname{vQL}}
. As
S_{i}
has finite index in
\Gamma_{i}
, it is enough to show that
S_{i}
is virtually-
\prod\mathcal{G}_{\operatorname{vQL}}
. Since
S_{i}
is infinite, there is some
x\in X
such that
\pi_{x}(S_{i})
is infinite. For such an
x
, the group
H_{x}
maps onto a non-abelian free group, say
F_{n_{x}}
, via an epimorphism
\psi_{x}\colon H_{x}\rightarrow F_{n_{x}}
. If
\psi_{x}(\pi_{x}(S_{i}))
is a non-abelian free group, then
S_{i}
would be in
\mathcal{G}_{\operatorname{vQL}}
, as desired. Thus we are reduced to the case that whenever
\pi_{x}(S_{i})
is infinite, then
\psi_{x}(\pi_{x}(S_{i}))
is not a non-abelian free group. Hence
\psi_{x}(\pi_{x}(S_{i}))
is either trivial or infinite cyclic.

Claim:
If
\pi_{x}(S_{i})
is infinite, then
\pi_{x}\left(\prod_{j\neq i}S_{j}\times B\right)
is finite.

Denote
L:=\prod_{j\neq i}S_{j}\times B
. As
S
has finite index in
H
, also
\pi_{x}(S)
has finite index in
\pi_{x}(H)=H_{x}
. Since
S_{i}
commutes with
L
, it follows that
\pi_{x}(S_{i})\cap\pi_{x}(L)\subseteq\mathcal{Z}(\pi_{x}(S))
. Now note that the finite index subgroup
\pi_{x}(S)
of
H_{x}
also maps onto a non-abelian free group under
\psi_{x}
, which we denote
F
. Therefore,
\mathcal{Z}(\pi_{x}(S))
must be contained in
\ker(\psi_{x})
. This implies that we obtain

\psi_{x}(\pi_{x}(S))=\psi_{x}(\langle\pi_{x}(S_{i}),\pi_{x}(L)\rangle)\cong\psi_{x}(\pi_{x}(S_{i}))\times\psi_{x}(\pi_{x}(L)),

as a finite index subgroup of
F_{n_{x}}
. As
n_{x}\geq 2
, this means that either
\pi_{x}(S_{i})
or
\pi_{x}(L)
is fully contained in
\ker(\psi_{x})
. However, if
\pi_{x}(L)\subseteq\ker(\psi_{x})
, as
\psi_{x}(\pi_{x}(S_{i}))
is assumed at most infinite cyclic, then
\psi_{x}(\pi_{x}(S))
would be at most infinite cyclic. This is a contradiction since
\pi_{x}(S)
has finite index in
H_{x}
. In conclusion,
\pi_{x}(S_{i})\subseteq\ker(\psi_{x})
. Now using that free groups are the projective objects in the category of groups, we obtain a splitting
\pi_{x}(S)\cong K\rtimes F
with
K
containing
\pi_{x}(S_{i})
and
F
isomorphic to a subgroup of
\pi_{x}(L)
. However, from the definition of
S
, such a splitting in fact yields a direct product
\pi_{x}(S)=\pi_{x}(S_{i})\times\pi_{x}(L)
, which contradicts the virtual indecomposability of
H_{x}
, finishing the proof of the claim.

Finally, consider the set
Y=\{x\in X\mid\pi_{x}(S_{i})\text{ is infinite }\}
. The above claim implies that if
x\in Y
, then
\pi_{x}(S_{i})
has finite index in
H_{x}
, since
\pi_{x}(S)=\langle\pi_{x}(S_{i}),\pi_{x}(\prod_{j\neq i}S_{j}\times B)\rangle
. Note that
S_{i}
contains a finite index subgroup
R_{i}
with the property that
\pi_{x}(R_{i})=1
for
x\notin Y
. Since
S_{i}
is virtually indecomposable, so is
R_{i}
. But by the above,
R_{i}
is a finite index subgroup of
\prod_{x\in Y}H_{x}
, which would yield a virtual decomposition of
R_{i}
. Thus
Y=\{x_{0}\}
is a singleton and therefore
R_{i}
inherits from
H_{x_{0}}
that it is in
\mathcal{G}_{\operatorname{vQL}}
. In conclusion,
S_{i}
and hence
\Gamma_{i}
is virtually-
\mathcal{G}_{\operatorname{vQL}}
, finishing the proof.
∎

Proof of
Theorem

.

\mathcal{O}
an order in

and

a finite index subgroup in

6.2.
Congruence kernel of group rings

Let
A
be a finite-dimensional semisimple
{\mathbb{Q}}
-algebra. Then

A=\operatorname{End}(V_{1})\times\cdots\times\operatorname{End}(V_{q}),

with
V_{i}
an
n_{i}
-dimensional module over some finite-dimensional division
{\mathbb{Q}}
-algebra
D_{i}
,
i=1,\ldots,q
.
In consequence, the
{\mathbb{Q}}
-group of units of
A
identifies with the reductive group

\displaystyle\mathbf{G}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{GL}_{A}=\operatorname{GL}_{D_{1}^{n_{1}}}\times\cdots\times\operatorname{GL}_{D_{m}^{n_{m}}}.

If
\mathcal{O}
is an order in
A
, then
\operatorname{SL}_{1}(\mathcal{O})
is an arithmetic subgroup of
\operatorname{SL}_{1}(A)
. In
Section

5.3
we have recalled the definition of the congruence kernel of
\operatorname{SL}_{1}(\mathcal{O})
. For ease of the reader, we describe in the setting of the unit group of an order in a semisimple algebra the definitions with down-to-earth terminology.

A
principal congruence subgroup
of
\operatorname{SL}_{1}(\mathcal{O})
is a subgroup of the form

\operatorname{SL}_{1}(\mathcal{O},m):=\{u\in\operatorname{SL}_{1}(\mathcal{O})\mid u-1\in m\mathcal{O}\},

with
m\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
.
Denote by
\mathcal{N}
the set of all finite index normal subgroups of
\operatorname{SL}_{1}(\mathcal{O})
and by
\mathcal{C}o
the set of all principal congruence subgroups. Both sets define a basis of of neighbourhoods of
1
for group topologies in
\operatorname{SL}_{1}(\mathcal{O})
and hence we have associated completions:

\widehat{\operatorname{SL}_{1}(\mathcal{O})}:=\varprojlim\limits_{N\in\mathcal{N}}\operatorname{SL}_{1}(\mathcal{O})/N\,\,\text{ and }\,\,\widetilde{\operatorname{SL}_{1}(\mathcal{O})}:=\varprojlim\limits_{m\in{\mathbb{N}}}\operatorname{SL}_{1}(\mathcal{O})/\operatorname{SL}_{1}(\mathcal{O},m).

Since
\mathcal{N}
contains the set
\mathcal{C}o
, the identity map on
\operatorname{SL}_{1}(\mathcal{O})
induces a surjective group homomorphism
\pi_{\mathcal{O}}
from
\widehat{\operatorname{SL}_{1}(\mathcal{O})}
to
\widetilde{\operatorname{SL}_{1}(\mathcal{O})}
. The kernel
\ker(\pi_{\mathcal{O}})
is called the
congruence kernel
. Note that if

-algebra. It will be useful to distinguish primitive and imprimitive groups. Recall the following definition (see
[
5
, section 2.1]
).

Definition 7.23
.

Let
V
be the
2
-dimensional
D
-module given by column vectors in
D
. Let
G\leqslant\operatorname{GL}_{2}(D)
. Then
G
has a natural action on
V
by left multiplication. The group
G
is said to be
imprimitive
if and only if
V
decomposes as a direct sum

V=V_{1}\oplus V_{2}

of
1
-dimensional
D
-modules such that for any
g\in G
, one has
gV_{i}=V_{\sigma(i)}
, for
i\in\{1,2\}
and
\sigma\in\Sym(\{1,2\})
. A group which is not imprimitive is said to be
primitive
.

Note that for a primitive group
G\leqslant\operatorname{GL}_{2}(D)
, the action of
G
on
V
is irreducible.

Now suppose that
G\leqslant\operatorname{GL}_{2}(D)
is primitive. Then several cases can arise for the isomorphism type of
\operatorname{Span}_{{\mathbb{Q}}}\{G\}:=\operatorname{Span}_{{\mathbb{Q}}}\{g\in G\}
. Since there is a
{\mathbb{Q}}
-linear map
{\mathbb{Q}}G\to\operatorname{Span}_{{\mathbb{Q}}}\{G\}
induced by the identity map,
\operatorname{Span}_{{\mathbb{Q}}}\{G\}
in particular has the structure of a semisimple
{\mathbb{Q}}
-algebra. Since
G\leqslant\operatorname{GL}_{2}(D)
, the possible isomorphism types of
\operatorname{Span}_{{\mathbb{Q}}}\{G\}
then are:

(i)

A division algebra
D_{G}
;

(ii)

\operatorname{M}_{2}(D_{G})
for some division algebra
D_{G}
.

Note that the possibility that
\operatorname{Span}_{{\mathbb{Q}}}\{G\}
is the direct sum of two simple algebras does not occur, since this would contradict the assumption that
G
is primitive and in particular irreducible.

If
\operatorname{M}_{2}(D)
is exceptional, then the isomorphism types of
\operatorname{Span}_{{\mathbb{Q}}}\{G\}
can be described explicitly.

Lemma 7.24
.

Let
\operatorname{M}_{2}(D)
be an exceptional algebra and
G\leqslant\operatorname{GL}_{2}(D)
a finite subgroup which acts irreducibly on
V
. Then
\operatorname{Span}_{{\mathbb{Q}}}\{G\}
is either a field, a quaternion algebra, or an exceptional matrix algebra.

Remark 7.25
.

The proof of
Lemma

7.24
will be more precise on the possibilities for
\operatorname{Span}_{{\mathbb{Q}}}\{G\}
. For instance from the proof it follows that for any finite group
G\leqslant\operatorname{GL}_{2}(D)
which spans a quaternion algebra
\left(\frac{-a,-b}{F}\right)
, one has that
[F:{\mathbb{Q}}]\leq 2
. Conversely,
[F:{\mathbb{Q}}]=2
can only occur if

is a quaternion algebra over
{\mathbb{Q}}
.

Proof of
Lemma

7.24
.

As explained above,
B:=\operatorname{Span}_{{\mathbb{Q}}}\{G\}
is either a division algebra
D_{G}
or of the form
\operatorname{M}_{2}(D_{G})
. Suppose that
B\lneq\operatorname{M}_{2}(D)
and that
B
is non-commutative.

First we consider the case that
\mathcal{Z}(D)={\mathbb{Q}}
. Then
B
is a simple
\mathcal{Z}(D)
-subalgebra of
\operatorname{M}_{2}(D)
. Therefore, the double centraliser theorem
[
48
, Theorem 2.10.1]
implies that
\dim_{{\mathbb{Q}}}(B)
divides
\dim_{{\mathbb{Q}}}\operatorname{M}_{2}(D)
. The latter in turn is a divisor of
16
since
\operatorname{M}_{2}(D)
is exceptional. Now recall that
\dim_{\mathcal{Z}(B)}(B)
is a square and the reduced degree of

is less than or equal to
2
by
[
5
, pg 455, Lemma]
. Together with the assumptions on

, and the fact that

\dim_{{\mathbb{Q}}}(\mathcal{Z}(B))\cdot\dim_{\mathcal{Z}(B)}(B)=\dim_{{\mathbb{Q}}}(B)\mid 16,

this then implies that
\dim_{\mathcal{Z}(B)}B=4
and
\dim_{{\mathbb{Q}}}\mathcal{Z}(B)=1
or
2
. If
\dim_{{\mathbb{Q}}}\mathcal{Z}(B)=1
, then
B
is either
\operatorname{M}_{2}({\mathbb{Q}})
(if
D\neq{\mathbb{Q}}
) or a quaternion algebra with centre
{\mathbb{Q}}
. If
\dim_{{\mathbb{Q}}}\mathcal{Z}(B)=2
, then

is either a quaternion algebra with centre

or

with

a quadratic extension of
{\mathbb{Q}}
. This case can only occur if
\dim_{{\mathbb{Q}}}\operatorname{M}_{2}(D)=16
and hence

for some

.

Now no quadratic real extension

of
{\mathbb{Q}}
can embed in
\left(\frac{-a,-b}{{\mathbb{Q}}}\right)
as it is totally definite. Indeed, suppose that we have a
{\mathbb{Q}}
-algebra embedding of

into
\left(\frac{-a,-b}{{\mathbb{Q}}}\right)
. Then we would also have one of

into

. However
\left(\frac{-a,-b}{{\mathbb{R}}}\right)
, being a division algebra, cannot contain
{\mathbb{R}}\times{\mathbb{R}}
, as the latter contains non-trivial idempotents. In conclusion,

is an imaginary quadratic extension of

and

is an exceptional matrix algebra.

Next we consider the case that
\mathcal{Z}(D)\neq{\mathbb{Q}}
and thus

for some square-free natural number

. In this case the double centraliser theorem does not apply, but nevertheless we still have that

(7.9)

\dim_{{\mathbb{Q}}}(\mathcal{Z}(B))\cdot\dim_{\mathcal{Z}(B)}(B)=\dim_{{\mathbb{Q}}}(B)\lneq 8,

since

. Since
\dim_{\mathcal{Z}(B)}B
is square and

is non-commutative, (

) implies that
\dim_{\mathcal{Z}(B)}B=4
and
\mathcal{Z}(B)={\mathbb{Q}}
. Therefore either

or

is a quaternion algebra with centre
{\mathbb{Q}}
.
∎

7.4.

We now describe the possible finite subgroups of

7.4.1.
The imprimitive subgroups

We start by describing the imprimitive groups. For the following statement, we follow the techniques from the proof of
[
5
, Lemma 2.2]
.

Lemma 7.28
.

Suppose
H\leqslant\operatorname{GL}_{2}(D)
is imprimitive. Let
V=V_{1}\oplus V_{2}
be the associated decomposition into 1-dimensional
D
-modules. If there exists some
g\in H
such that
gV_{1}=V_{2}
, then

H\cong(\Gamma\times\Gamma)\rtimes C_{2},

where
\Gamma\leqslant{\mathcal{U}}(D)
and where
C_{2}
acts by exchanging the factors. Otherwise,
H\cong\Gamma_{1}\times\Gamma_{2}
for some subgroups
\Gamma_{1},\Gamma_{2}\leqslant{\mathcal{U}}(D)
.

Proof.

Suppose
gV_{i}=V_{i}
for all
g\in H
. Then up to conjugation by a base change matrix
A\in\operatorname{GL}_{2}(D)
,
H
consists of diagonal matrices. It follows that
H\cong\Gamma_{1}\times\Gamma_{2}
for some finite subgroups
\Gamma_{1},\Gamma_{2}\leqslant{\mathcal{U}}(D)
.

Suppose then that there exists some
g\in H
such that
gV_{1}=V_{2}
. Let
K\leqslant H
be the subgroup of
H
such that
kV_{i}=V_{i}
for all
k\in K
and
i\in\{1,2\}
. Again up to conjugation of
H
by a base change matrix
A\in\operatorname{GL}_{2}(D)
,
g
is of the form

\begin{pmatrix}0&g_{1}\\
g_{2}&0\end{pmatrix},
while
K
consists precisely of the diagonal matrices of
H
. Moreover,
K
has index
2
in
H
.
Indeed,
H=K\sqcup gK
, since if
x:=\begin{pmatrix}0&x_{1}\\
x_{2}&0\end{pmatrix}
is another antidiagonal matrix in
H
, then

k:=g^{-1}x=\begin{pmatrix}g_{2}^{-1}x_{2}&0\\
0&g_{1}^{-1}x_{1}\end{pmatrix}\in K

is an element such that
gk=x
. By the fact that
G
is imprimitive, any
x\in G
is either diagonal or antidiagonal and hence
H=K\sqcup gK
. In particular,
K
is a normal subgroup of
H
, and the action of
g
on
K
by conjugation switches the diagonal entries. It follows that
G\cong(\Gamma\times\Gamma)\rtimes C_{2}
, for some finite subgroup
\Gamma\leqslant{\mathcal{U}}(D)
.
∎

In particular, an imprimitive subgroup

Remark 7.37

In
