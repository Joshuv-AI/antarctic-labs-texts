Role of communities in preparedness, response and resilience to cholera epidemics: a systematic review protocol
Authors: Aime Gilbert Mbonda Noula, Karl Njuwa Fai, Tavarès Koudimba Mboungou, Norbert Lemonge, Tambe Elvis Akem, Stephen Ogirima Ohize, Julia Hajjar, Nicole Fouda Mbarga, Joseph Kamgno
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionCholera remains a major global health concern, particularly in fragile and resource-limited settings despite global efforts to reduce cholera morbidity and mortality. While community engagement is widely recognised as a cornerstone of sustainable cholera control, evidence on its effective implementation, particularly the concrete roles of communities in preparedness, response and resilience, is currently fragmented and poorly documented.This protocol describes a systematic review that aims to identify and synthesise global evidence on how communities contribute to cholera preparedness, response and resilience, with a focus on identifying enablers, barriers and best practices in diverse contexts.Methods and analysisThis protocol adheres to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses Protocols updated in 2020 guidelines. A comprehensive literature search will be conducted across major databases (eg, PubMed/MEDLINE, Web of Science, CINAHL, AJOL) and institutional repositories (eg, WHO Institutional Repository for Information Sharing (IRIS),International Federation of Red Cross and Red Crescent Societies, United Nations Office for the Coordination of Humanitarian Affairs (OCHA)). The search will target peer-reviewed and grey literature published between January 2000 and December 2025 in all languages. Eligible studies include those describing community-level interventions, participation or engagement in cholera epidemic preparedness, response or resilience activities. Study selection and data extraction will be independently performed by six reviewers in pairs, with quality appraised using Joanna Briggs Institute tools. A narrative and thematic synthesis will be conducted across the three domains (preparedness, response and resilience), with subgroup analyses performed where data allow.Ethics and disseminationEthical approval is not required. Findings will be disseminated through publications and presentations and will identify crucial research gaps. This review will support global health actors, including the Global Task Force on Cholera Control, by directly informing guidance for community-driven cholera control strategies and policy.PROSPERO registration numberCRD420251105088.

Background

Cholera remains a persistent global health threat, particularly in fragile and resource-limited settings. Caused by the ingestion of food or water contaminated with Vibrio cholerae, the disease manifests through acute watery diarrhoea and can lead to death within hours without appropriate treatment. Despite being preventable and treatable, cholera continues to affect vulnerable populations, particularly in contexts of poor water, sanitation and hygiene (WASH) infrastructure, political instability, displacement and environmental crises.1 2

Cholera is widely recognised as a disease of inequity, disproportionately affecting communities living in poverty, informal urban settlements and humanitarian crisis settings such as refugee or internally displaced persons camps.1 3 Fragile settings, defined as those with socio-economic, environmental or governance instability and weak public health services, are particularly susceptible to recurrent cholera epidemics.2 4 Recent large-scale outbreaks in Nigeria, Yemen, Syria and Haiti illustrate how conflict, infrastructure collapse and climate-related events such as flooding continue to drive cholera transmission.510

Globally, an estimated 2.86 million cases of cholera and 21 000–143 000 deaths occur annually, with at least 47 countries considered endemic.1 11 Sub-Saharan Africa bears the highest burden, with over 60% of global cholera cases reported from the region, concentrated in persistent ‘hotspots’ where 40–80 million people live.1 11 12 As of July 2023, the WHO African Region had recorded over 211 000 cumulative cases and nearly 4000 deaths.12

To address this ongoing threat, the Global Task Force on Cholera Control (GTFCC) developed the Global Roadmap to 2030 in 2017, aiming to reduce cholera deaths by 90% and eliminate the disease in 20 endemic countries by 2030.1 The roadmap identifies three strategic priorities: (1) early detection and response to contain outbreaks, (2) targeted multisectoral interventions in hotspots and (3) international technical collaboration and partnership. A cross-cutting element of the roadmap is community engagement, defined as a dynamic process of building relationships between communities and health systems to co-design and implement health solutions.13

In cholera control, community engagement plays a central role in promoting hygiene, improving early detection, facilitating behavioural change and ensuring community ownership of WASH interventions.1 14 15 Community-based surveillance, health promotion and social mobilisation activities have been shown to enhance trust, improve response speed and increase adherence to public health measures during epidemics.4 Moreover, community participation is vital to the sustainability of WASH infrastructure and the effectiveness of health system strengthening strategies.2

Despite the persistent global cholera burden, effective control requires moving beyond facility-centric and purely infrastructure solutions to embrace genuine community participation. Grounding our approach in established theoretical models, such as those articulated by Rifkin and Campbell and Cornish, it is recognise the community not merely as a target for interventions, but as an active agent whose empowered involvement is central to building long-term health resilience.1619 Recent empirical analyses confirm the ongoing public health challenge, highlighting a significant and geographically shifting cholera burden across the WHO African region.5 20 Studies from endemic settings, including Nigeria and Zambia, underscore that the success of comprehensive, integrated cholera strategies is often determined by community-level factors such as local trust, culturally appropriate engagement and addressing systemic barriers to WASH implementation.5 19 21 Furthermore, assessing the progress of regional cholera frameworks demonstrates that while technical milestones may advance, insufficient progress persists against critical WASH components that form the bedrock of community control.22

However, fragile settings often face compounded barriers: limited resources, weak governance, low levels of trust between communities and health actors, and a lack of evidence-informed guidance on how to implement effective engagement in these contexts.2 4 14 Literature also highlights that fragility, whether at the system or community level, can negatively impact resilience to shocks such as cholera outbreaks.1 4 Yet, there is limited consolidated evidence on how communities concretely contribute to preparedness, response and resilience during cholera epidemics, especially in fragile contexts. Previous reviews, such as Elimian et al and Ajayi and Smith, have focused primarily on barriers to intervention implementation or outbreak trends, without systematically examining the distinct roles of communities across the preparedness–response–resilience spectrum.3 5 23 This review addresses that gap by focusing specifically on the contributions, engagement levels and contextual effectiveness of community-driven interventions.3 5

Furthermore, recent shortages in the global cholera vaccine stockpile have underscored the need for durable non-pharmaceutical interventions, particularly community-driven WASH improvements, as long-term solutions.7 These efforts require strong political will, intersectoral collaboration and robust community participation. Understanding the facilitators and barriers to such participation is essential to refining intervention strategies and achieving the GTFCC’s targets.

Objective

This systematic review aims to explore, synthesise and document available evidence on the roles played by communities in the preparedness, response and resilience phases of cholera epidemics worldwide.

Primary research question

What specific roles do communities play in the preparedness, response and resilience to cholera epidemics globally?

Secondary research question

To enhance analytical depth, this review also asks: How and under what specific contextual conditions (mechanisms) do these community contributions facilitate or impede effective cholera control?

Methods

This systematic review will be conducted and reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines.24 The protocol follows the PRISMA-Protocols (PRISMA-P) recommendations and has been registered with the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) under registration number CRD420251105088.25 It is reported according to PRISMA-P as illustrated in the online supplemental file 2.

Types of studies: We will include peer-reviewed qualitative, quantitative and mixed-methods studies, as well as relevant grey literature such as reports from the WHO, the International Federation of Red Cross and Red Crescent Societies (IFRC), UNICEF, United Nations Office for the Coordination of Humanitarian Affairs (OCHA), among others and national public health institutions. Studies must present empirical data and focus on community-level contributions to cholera epidemic preparedness, response or resilience. Editorials, letters, commentaries, opinion pieces and studies that do not present empirical data will be excluded.

The overall study period is planned from 1 December 2025 to 31 July 2026, following completion of the comprehensive search on 30 November 2025. The review will proceed according to the following schedule: protocol completion and submission (November 2025); study screening (title/abstract and full text) over 8 weeks (December 2025 to January 2026); data extraction and quality appraisal over 8 weeks (February to March 2026); data synthesis and analysis, including narrative synthesis and Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation-Confidence in the Evidence from Reviews of Qualitative Research (GRADE-CERQual) assessment, over 8 weeks (April to May 2026); and manuscript drafting and submission (June 2026), with anticipated completion by July 2026.

Search strategy for identifying relevant studies

We will systematically search PubMed/MEDLINE, Scopus, Web of Science (Core Collection), CINAHL, Google Scholar, AJOL and selected institutional repositories including WHO Institutional Repository for Information Sharing (IRIS), IFRC Library, UNICEF, OCHA and relevant government libraries. The search will cover studies published from 1 January 2000 to 31 December 2025 in all languages. We will use both controlled vocabulary (eg, Medical Subject Headings (MeSH)) and free-text terms to capture key concepts of cholera, community engagement and epidemic preparedness, response and resilience. The final, comprehensive search strategy designed for Medline (Ovid), incorporating MeSH and text words, is provided in online supplemental annex 1. Minor adaptations (eg, using different truncation/proximity operators) will be made for syntax compatibility across other databases (eg, Scopus, Web of Science, CINAHL). For the purposes of this review, preparedness may include community-led cholera awareness campaigns or the establishment of early warning systems; response activities may involve local participation in case identification, oral rehydration point management or contact tracing; while resilience may be reflected in long-term behaviour change interventions, improvements in water and sanitation infrastructure, or community governance structures that support outbreak recovery.

Search results will be imported into Zotero for management and duplication. Titles and abstracts will be screened independently by two reviewers using Covidence, a web-based platform designed to streamline the systematic review process. A full-text review will follow for eligible articles. In case of discrepancies, a third reviewer will resolve disagreements. Reference lists of included studies will be manually searched to identify additional studies. The selection process will be documented using the PRISMA 2020 flow diagram.

Selection of studies for inclusion in the review

Throughout the title/abstract screening and full-text extraction phases, two independent reviewers will work in duplicate. The agreement between the two reviewers will be assessed using Cohen’s κ coefficient to measure inter-rater reliability. When discrepancies arise, a staged resolution protocol will be applied: the two primary reviewers will initially discuss the item to reach a consensus based on the defined protocol criteria. If consensus cannot be achieved, the item will be escalated, and a third, independent senior reviewer will be consulted to arbitrate the final decision. All major disagreements that proceed to arbitration, along with the final decision and rationale, will be systematically logged for inclusion in a transparency appendix. Full-text articles identified in languages other than English or French will be translated into English by a qualified professional translator or a native-speaking research team member. To ensure validity and conceptual accuracy, the final translation will undergo validation by a second native-speaking team member, focusing on key concepts and outcomes. Covidence, a web-based systematic review management platform, will be used to organise and track the study selection process. During quality appraisal, potential sources of bias such as selection bias, performance bias, detection bias and reporting bias will be evaluated using appropriate critical Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) appraisal tools.

Methodological quality assessment

Two independent reviewers (KNF and NL) will critically appraise the methodological quality of included articles, with any discrepancies resolved through discussion or arbitration by a third reviewer (AGMN). To ensure consistency across diverse study designs, we will primarily use the JBI Critical Appraisal Tools.29 Specific JBI checklists will be applied according to the study design, including those for qualitative research, analytical cross-sectional studies and cohort or case-control studies. Mixed-methods studies will be evaluated using the Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool30 or the relevant JBI format. Studies will be categorised as having low, moderate or high risk of bias based on tool-specific criteria. Studies rated as high risk of bias will be included in the synthesis, but their limitations will be accounted for during the confidence assessment phase (GRADE-CERQual).31 No studies will be excluded based solely on quality scores; however, appraisal results will be used to weigh the strength of the evidence during the narrative synthesis.

Data extraction and management

Data extraction will be performed independently by six investigators (AGMN, KNF, NL, TEA, TKM, NM). Disagreements between any two reviewers will be resolved through discussion, or by involving a third review author for arbitration if necessary. A standardised and pretested data extraction form will be used to ensure consistency and completeness.

For each included study, we will extract the following information: the last name of the first author, year of publication, country and region of study, study area (rural, urban, periurban or displaced setting), study setting (eg, household, community-based programme, multisite project), number of study sites (single-site or multicentre) and the period during which participants were included. We will also collect details on the timing of data collection (prospective or retrospective), study design, sample size and participant characteristics (eg, mean or median age, gender distribution and any relevant vulnerability factors such as displacement, poverty or marginalisation). Importantly, we will collect data on the epidemic context in which the intervention was implemented, including whether the setting was endemic or epidemic for cholera, and the phase of the outbreak (eg, onset, peak, postpeak or interepidemic lull) at the time of the intervention.

In terms of community engagement, we will extract information on the type of intervention or activity described (eg, community-based surveillance, WASH promotion, health education, risk communication), the level of engagement (inform, consult, involve or empower) and the roles played by community members or organisations. Where applicable, we will document outcomes such as improved outbreak detection, changes in hygiene or sanitation practices, reduction in cholera-related morbidity or mortality, and evidence of sustained community resilience.

Barriers and facilitators to community engagement will also be recorded, along with any reported policy or programme recommendations made by the study authors. For multicountry studies, data on community roles and outcomes will be reported separately by country, and studies will be grouped by WHO region (Africa, Americas, Eastern Mediterranean, Europe, South-East Asia and Western Pacific).

If relevant outcome data is missing or unclear, we will contact the corresponding author of the study via email. If no response is received after two attempts within a 1-month period, the study will be excluded from the final synthesis.

Data synthesis and analysis

Data analysis will be conducted using a narrative synthesis approach, given the anticipated heterogeneity in study designs, contexts and outcomes. Thematic analysis will be applied to organise and interpret the data, with key findings grouped under the three domains of cholera control: preparedness, response and resilience. Within each domain, we will identify and compare how communities were engaged, the levels of participation (inform, consult, involve, empower) and the reported effects of these interventions. Furthermore, the synthesis will explicitly examine how sociodemographic and economic factors (eg, gender, socio-economic status, education level and displacement status) modify the nature and effectiveness of community participation. Findings will be stratified by these variables where it is possible to ensure equity considerations are a central output of the review.

Where comparable quantitative data are available, we will summarise measures of effect (eg, percentage improvements in hygiene practices, case fatality rates, time to detection or reductions in disease incidence). Although a meta-analysis is not anticipated due to variability in outcome definitions and reporting standards, if sufficient homogeneity is identified across a subset of studies, we will explore the feasibility of meta-aggregation or basic descriptive pooling if sufficient homogeneity is identified. Sufficient homogeneity is predefined as data from a minimum of three independent studies reporting identical outcome measures (eg, Case fatality rate, oral rehydration solution (ORS) usage) and having comparable study designs and populations (eg, non-conflict urban settings). If quantitative synthesis is conducted, analyses will be performed using the R statistical software environment (V.4.5.1), applying relevant meta-analysis or descriptive statistics packages.

Heterogeneity across studies will be assessed qualitatively based on differences in study population, context, intervention type and outcome measures. For the thematic and narrative synthesis, a significant finding will be defined as one that is either (1) corroborated by evidence from a minimum of three independent primary studies; or (2) a core community role found in major policy documents (eg, WHO/GTFCC guidelines) and supported by at least one empirical study. Subgroup analyses may be conducted to explore variations by WHO region, type of community engagement strategy or the fragility level of the setting. Reporting bias will be minimised by including grey literature and manually screening reference lists of included studies. Any potential publication or selection bias will be described narratively in the limitations section of the review.

The certainty or confidence in the final cumulative evidence (ie, thematic findings or synthesis outputs) will be assessed using the GRADE-CERQual approach. This framework is the gold standard for systematically rating confidence in the findings of narrative and thematic syntheses.31 This approach is necessary because the primary analysis is a thematic synthesis of descriptive, context-specific and qualitative data, making the use of the standard GRADE framework (designed for effect estimates from meta-analysis) inappropriate. The confidence in each thematic finding will be rated based on four key components: first, Methodological limitations, which addresses the extent of concerns about the design or conduct of the primary studies contributing to the review finding (informed by the individual study-level JBI critical appraisal); second, Relevance, which evaluates the extent to which the included studies align with the review question and the specific thematic finding; third, Coherence, which assesses the degree to which the findings are consistent across the included studies; and fourth, Adequacy, which examines the richness and amount of data supporting the finding. Following this systematic process, each thematic finding will receive a final confidence rating of high, moderate, low or very low, which will be discussed in detail to ensure transparency in how the evidence should be interpreted and used for policy.

Publication bias and limitations

To minimise the risk of publication bias, this review will include a broad range of sources, including grey literature retrieved from institutional repositories and manual searches of reference lists of included studies. This approach is intended to capture studies that may not be published in indexed journals but offer relevant insights into community engagement in cholera control. Additionally, anticipated heterogeneity in study design, context, geographical settings and outcome reporting may limit comparability across studies and preclude meta-analysis.

Ethics and dissemination

Formal ethical approval is not required for this study as it is a systematic review protocol synthesising evidence from already published, publicly available studies and grey literature, without collecting primary data from human subjects. The review will, however, adhere to all ethical principles concerning academic honesty, with all sources fully and accurately cited. The protocol is registered with PROSPERO to ensure transparency and minimise research waste.

The review will be conducted and reported in accordance with the PRISMA 2020 guidelines to ensure methodological transparency and rigour. The findings will be submitted for publication in peer-reviewed journals and presented at relevant global health conferences. This systematic review is expected to generate a comprehensive synthesis of how communities contribute to cholera epidemic preparedness, response and resilience. By identifying patterns, enabling factors and barriers to effective engagement, the findings will inform the design of community-centred interventions and guide programme decision-making. In addition, results will be disseminated directly to key stakeholders involved in cholera prevention and control, including non-governmental organisations, public health institutions and government agencies. This knowledge-sharing process will support the translation of evidence into policy frameworks for cholera control. The review will also highlight research gaps and suggest priorities for future studies, particularly in vulnerable and outbreak-prone settings where community participation is crucial for achieving sustainable public health outcomes.

Protocol development and potential amendments

No funder, sponsor or institution was involved in the conception, design or development of this protocol, and the authors declare no competing interests. At the time of submission, no amendments are anticipated. However, any substantial changes made during the review process, such as modifications to the eligibility criteria, data extraction strategy or analytical approach, will be transparently documented in an Amendments Log. This log will record the date, a description of the change (eg, modification of inclusion criteria) and the corresponding rationale. This log will be included as a supplementary file in the final published review. Significant deviations that impact the scope or risk of bias of the review will be immediately updated in the PROSPERO registry.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and members of the public were not involved in the design, development or planned implementation of this systematic review protocol. This is due to the methodological nature of the work and the absence of direct data collection from individuals.
