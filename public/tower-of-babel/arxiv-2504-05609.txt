Extended SQP Methods in Nonsmooth Difference Programming Applied to Problems with Variational Inequality Constraints
Boris S. Mordukhovich, Yixia Song, Shangzhi Zeng, Jin Zhang

Abstract
This paper explores a new class of constrained difference programming problems, where the objective and constraints are formulated as differences of functions, without requiring their convexity. To investigate such problems, novel variants of the extended sequential quadratic method are introduced. These algorithms iteratively solve strongly convex quadratic subproblems constructed via linear approximations of the given data by using their gradients and subgradients. The convergence of the proposed methods is rigorously analyzed by employing, in particular, the Polyak-Łojasiewicz-Kurdyka property that ensures global convergence for various classes of functions in the problem formulation, e.g., semialgebraic ones. The original framework is further extended to address difference programming problems with variational inequality (VI) constraints. By reformulating VI constraints via regularized gap functions, such problems are naturally embedded into constrained difference programming that leads us to direct applications of the proposed algorithms. Numerical experiments for the class of continuous network design problems demonstrate the efficiency of the new methods. Keywords Difference programming, Variational inequalities, Extended sequential quadratic methods, Polyak-Łojasiewicz-Kurdyka conditions, Regularized gap functions, Continuous network design problems

Introduction

Difference-of-convex
(DC) programming has emerged as a powerful framework for nonconvex and nonsmooth optimization owing to its ability of modeling complex structures while maintaining computational tractability; see, e.g.,
[
3
,
43
]
and the references therein. In this work, we extend this paradigm by investigating a class of constrained
difference programming
problems formulated as follows:

\displaystyle\min_{x\in X}\quad\varphi_{0}(x):=g_{0}(x)-h_{0}(x)

(1.1)

\displaystyle\text{subject to}\quad{\varphi_{i}(x):=g_{i}(x)-h_{i}(x)\leq 0,\quad i\in\mathcal{I},}

where
\mathcal{I}
is a finite set of indices,
X
is a nonempty, closed, and convex subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. The functions
g_{i}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
for
i\in\{0\}\cup\mathcal{I}
are assumed to be continuously differentiable with locally Lipschitzian gradients (i.e., of class
{\cal C}^{1,1}
) on
X
, while the functions
h_{i}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}:=\left(-\infty,\infty\right]
for
i\in\{0\}\cup\mathcal{I}
are locally Lipschitz continuous and prox-regular over
X
; see Section
2
. Additionally, we assume that the objective function
\varphi_{0}
is bounded from below by a constant
m_{\varphi_{0}}
over
X
. The primary objective of this study is to develop efficient numerical methods for solving (
1.1
), establish a rigorous convergence analysis for the proposed algorithms, and provide valuable applications.

Recall that several classical algorithms exist to solve general constrained nonlinear programming (NLP). Among them, we mention sequential quadratic programming (SQP) methods, interior point methods, and augmented Lagrangian methods; see, e.g.,
[
10
,
35
]
for more details.
The difference programming model (
1.1
) can be viewed as a generalization of DC programming, which has been a well-established topic in optimization with a variety of practical applications; see, in particular,
[
2
,
5
,
16
,
37
,
46
]
and the references therein among various developments on DC-type algorithms.

The unconstrained case of (
1.1
), where
\varphi_{i}(x)
for
i\in\mathcal{I}
are absent, has been extensively investigated in
[
4
]
, where the term “difference programming” has been coined. In that work, the authors propose a novel Newton-type algorithm, RCSN, which effectively exploits second-order information via generalized differentiation techniques to solve such problems. In our work, we address the general setting of (
1.1
), where the constraint functions
\varphi_{i}
for
i\in\mathcal{I}
are also expressed as differences of functions. The presence of such a structure in both objective and the constraint functions constitute additional analytical and computational challenges, which require the rigorous treatment of feasibility and stationarity conditions.

To develop an efficient numerical approach for solving (
1.1
), we begin with considering SQP-type algorithms in the setting of smooth NLP problems. Methods of this type include solving a sequence of strongly convex quadratic subproblems to approximate the original one
[
11
,
24
]
.
In particular, the
extended sequential quadratic method
(ESQM), introduced in
[
7
]
for NLPs, serves as an inspiration for one of our algorithmic designs, called CDP-ESQM, to solve (
1.1
). Here we do not directly apply ESQM, but rather incorporate certain key ideas from it to develop an iterative framework tailored for constrained difference programming (
1.1
). Specifically, at each iteration we construct a strongly convex quadratic programming subproblem, where the problem’s structural properties are incorporated through linear approximations derived from the gradients of
g_{i}
and limiting/Mordukhovich subgradients of
h_{i}
for
i\in\{0\}\cup\mathcal{I}
. Under rather mild assumptions, we establish convergence properties of the proposed CDP-ESQM. Moreover, under the assumption that a limiting subdifferential version of the Polyak-Łojasiewicz-Kurdyka property holds for a suitably designed merit function, we establish global convergence results for CDP-ESQM. Notably, while CDP-ESQM utilizes a backtracking line search, our global convergence results do not rely on the assumption of a unit step size.

Subsequently, we consider a class of
difference programs with variational inequality
(VI)
constraints
.
A variational inequality problem seeks a vector
x^{*}
such that a given mapping
F(x)
satisfies

\langle F(x^{*}),x-x^{*}\rangle\geq 0\;\mbox{ for all }\;x\in\Omega,

(1.2)

where
\Omega
is a feasible set, which often models equilibrium problems in diverse fields of science and technology including machine learning, operations research, economics, optimization, etc.; see
[
15
,
18
,
21
,
34
]
. VIs serve as a unifying tool to describe equilibrium conditions in applications to noncooperative games, market equilibrium models, contact mechanics, and generative adversarial networks. In various real-world models, decision making involves not only satisfying a VI constraint but also optimizing an objective function that leads us to problems of VI-constrained programming. Theoretical research on VI-constrained programming mostly focuses on the development of constraint qualifications and necessary optimality conditions as in
[
45
]
and on the analysis of value function stability in parametric settings; see, e.g.,
[
14
,
26
]
.
These studies, along with other early contributions, provide fundamental insights into the theoretical properties of VI-constrained programming. In real-world applications, VI-constrained programming has been widely employed in various fields such as optimal shape design, Stackelberg-Cournot-Nash equilibria traffic equilibrium, etc.; see
[
23
,
39
]
. Despite the goal of solving VI-constrained programming problems, the existing research has predominantly focused on a special case where the feasible set
\Omega
in VI (
1.2
) is a cone. Under this condition, problems of VI-constrained programming reduce to
mathematical programs with complementarity constraints
(MPCCs); see, e.g.,
[
15
, Proposition 1.1.3]
. The reader may consult with
[
19
,
36
,
40
,
41
]
for various numerical algorithms developed to solve MPCCs.

Our aim in this part of the research is developing efficient algorithms to solve problems of difference programming with variational inequality constraints of type (
1.2
). To accomplish the goal,
we build upon the single-level reformulation approach introduced in
[
28
]
and employ employ the
regularized gap function
for VI proposed in
[
17
]
.
This allows us to reformulate the VI constraint as an inequality constraint given by the difference of two (nonconvex) functions with reducing therefore the VI-constrained problem to the difference programming form (
1.1
). Thus the proposed ESQM methods are directly applicable to the VI-constrained difference programming problems under consideration. The results established for such problems by using CDP-ESQM are applied to
continuous network design problems
. For this class of practical models, we conduct numerical experiments and compare the obtained numerical solutions with those computed by other well-recognized methods.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows.
Section
2
presents the basic notation and needed preliminaries from variational analysis and nonsmooth optimization. In Section
3
, we design and provide convergence analysis of the proposed ESQM version for solving constrained difference programming problems (CDP-ESQM). Section
4
is devoted to proving the global convergence of CDP-ECQM, assuming a limiting subdifferential version of the Polyak-Łojasiewicz-Kurdyka property holds for a specifically designed merit function,
E_{p}
(defined subsequently in (
4.1
)).
In Section
5
, we consider VI-constrained models of difference programming and provide adaptations of the CDP-ESQM algorithm to solve such problems by reducing them to the constrained difference programs (
1.1
) via the gap function regularization. Applying the developed algorithm to solving continuous network design problems and numerical experiments are given in Section
6
. The final Section
7
summarizes the main achievements of the paper and discusses some topics of our future research.

Preliminaries from Variational Analysis and Nonsmooth Optimization

Throughout this paper, we deal with finite-dimensional Euclidean spaces
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and use the standard notation and terminology of variational analysis and generalized differentiation; see, e.g.,
[
30
,
38
]
.
Let
\mathbb{B}_{r}(x)
represent the closed ball centered at
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

with radius
r>0
, and let
\mathbb{B}_{r}
denote the closed ball centered at the origin with the same radius, while the unit closed ball centered at the origin is denoted by
\mathbb{B}
.
The set of natural numbers is labeled as
\mathbb{N}:=\{1,2,\dots\}
.
The nonnegative and positive orthants in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
are denoted by
\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}
and
\mathbb{R}^{n}_{++}
, respectively. We use the symbol
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
for the inner product and the symbol
\|\cdot\|
for the Euclidean norm in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. For a vector
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and a closed set
D\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the distance from
x
to
D
is defined as
\mathrm{dist}(x,D):=\min_{x^{\prime}\in D}\|x-x^{\prime}\|
. The indicator function of
D
is denoted by
\delta_{D}
, and the normal cone associated with a convex set
D
at
x\in D
is given by

\mathcal{N}_{D}(x):=\big\{v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}~\big|~\langle v,x^{\prime}-x\rangle\leq 0\;\mbox{ for all }\;x^{\prime}\in D\big\}

(2.1)

with the convention
\mathcal{N}_{D}(x):=\varnothing
used when
x\notin D
. The Euclidean projection onto
D
is signified by
\mathrm{Proj}_{D}
and the convex hull of
D
by
\mathrm{co}\,D
. The Cartesian product of two sets
X
and
Y
is denoted by
X\times Y
.

For any proper lower semicontinuous (l.s.c.) function
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}
, the associated domain and epigraph are given by
\text{dom}(f):=\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}~|~f(x)<\infty\right\}
and
\text{epi}(f):=\left\{(x,\alpha)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}~|~f(x)\leq\alpha\right\}
, respectively. The main subdifferential construction used in this paper is the (Mordukhovich)
limiting subdifferential
of
f
at

\bar{x}\in{\rm dom}(f)
defined as follows

\partial f(\bar{x}):=\left\{v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\;\middle|\;\exists x_{k}\xrightarrow{f}\bar{x},\;\exists v_{k}\in\widehat{\partial}f(x_{k}),\;v_{k}\to v\right\},

(2.2)

where
x_{k}\xrightarrow{f}\bar{x}
indicates that
x_{k}\to\bar{x}
and
f(x_{k})\to f(\bar{x})
as
k\to\infty
, and where
\widehat{\partial}f(x)
stands for the (Fréchet)
regular subdifferential
of
f
at
x\in{\rm dom}\,f
given by

\widehat{\partial}f(x):=\Big\{v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\;\Big|\;\mathop{\lim\inf}\limits_{u\to x}\frac{f(u)-f(x)-\langle v,u-x\rangle}{\|u-x\|}\geq 0\Big\}.

(2.3)

If
\bar{x}\notin\mathrm{dom}(f)
, we put
\partial f(\bar{x}):=\emptyset
. When
f
is a convex function, both subdifferentials (
2.2
) and (
2.3
) reduce to the classical subdifferential of convex analysis, but they can be very different even for simple nonconvex functions; see, e,g.,
f(x):=-|x|
,
x\in\mathbb{R}
, where
\partial f(0)=\{-1,1\}
while
\widehat{\partial}f(0)=\emptyset
. Note that, despite the nonconvex values of the limiting subdifferential, it enjoys a
full calculus
based on variational and extremal principles of variational analysis; see the books
[
29
,
30
,
38
]
and the references therein.

We say that a function
f\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}
is
lower/subdifferentially regular
at
\bar{x}\in\rm{dom}\,f
if we have
\partial f(\bar{x})=\widehat{\partial}f(\bar{x})
. As shown in the aforementioned books, various important classes of extended-real-valued functions possess this property. Many of such functions are prox-regular in the sense defined below.

Definition 2.1

A function
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}
is prox-regular at
\bar{x}\in\text{dom}(f)
for
\bar{v}\in\partial f(\bar{x})
if it is l.s.c. around
\bar{x}
and there exist
\varepsilon>0
and
r\geq 0
such that

\displaystyle f(x^{\prime})\geq f(x)+\langle v,x^{\prime}-x\rangle-\frac{r}{2}\|x^{\prime}-x\|^{2}

whenever
x,x^{\prime}\in\mathbb{B}_{\varepsilon}(\bar{x})
with
f(x)\leq f(\bar{x})+\varepsilon
and
v\in\partial f(x)\cap\mathbb{B}_{\varepsilon}(\bar{v})
. If the latter condition holds for all
\bar{v}\in\partial f(\bar{x})
,
f
is said to be prox-regular at
\bar{x}
. Given a set
X\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the function
f
is called prox-regular on
X
if it is prox-regular at every point of
X
.

The class of prox-regular functions has been extensively studied in modern variational analysis with a variety of applications. This class encompasses, in particular, convex and
{\cal C}^{1,1}
functions, strongly amenable functions, lower-
\mathcal{C}^{2}
functions, etc.; see
[
38
]
for more details. It follows from
[
13
, Theorem 5.2]
that any prox-regular and locally Lipschitzian function around
\bar{x}
, is subdifferentially regular at this point.

Next we recall the notion of upper directional derivative for extended-real-valued functions.

Definition 2.2

For a function
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to(-\infty,\infty]
and a point
\bar{x}\in\text{dom}(f)
, the upper directional derivative of
f
at
\bar{x}
in direction
\bar{d}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is defined by

\displaystyle f^{\prime}(\bar{x};\bar{d}):=\limsup\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}t\to 0^{+}\\
d\to\bar{d}\end{subarray}}\frac{f(\bar{x}+td)-f(\bar{x})}{t}.

It is easy to see that if
f
is locally Lipschitzian around
\bar{x}
, the expression for
f^{\prime}(\bar{x};\bar{d})
simplifies to

\displaystyle f^{\prime}(\bar{x};\bar{d})=\limsup\limits_{t\to 0^{+}}\frac{f(\bar{x}+t\bar{d})-f(\bar{x})}{t}.

(2.4)

The following useful result provides a calculus rule for the upper directional derivative of maxima over finitely many locally Lipschitzian functions.

Lemma 2.3

Let
f_{i}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to(-\infty,\infty]
,
i\in\mathcal{I}
, be a finite collection of proper functions, and let
\bar{x}\in\bigcap_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\text{\rm dom}(f_{i})
. Suppose that
f_{i}
is locally Lipschitzian around this point for all
i\in\mathcal{I}
. Then the maximum function

f=\max_{i\in\mathcal{I}}f_{i}
is also locally Lipschitzian around
\bar{x}
, and for any
d\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
we have

\displaystyle f^{\prime}(\bar{x};d)=\max_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{\max}(\bar{x})}\left\{(f_{i})^{\prime}(\bar{x};d)\right\}\leq\max_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\left\{f_{i}(\bar{x})+(f_{i})^{\prime}(\bar{x};d)\right\}-f(\bar{x}),

(2.5)

where
\mathcal{I}_{\max}(\bar{x}):=\left\{i\in\mathcal{I}\mid f_{i}(\bar{x})=f(\bar{x})\right\}
.
In particular, it holds for
\mathcal{I}=\{1,2\}
and
f_{2}\equiv 0
that

\displaystyle\left(\max\{f_{1},0\}\right)^{\prime}(\bar{x};d)\leq\max\{f_{1}(\bar{x})+(f_{1})^{\prime}(\bar{x};d),0\}-\max\{f_{1}(\bar{x}),0\}.

Proof
.  It follows directly from the definition that
f
is locally Lipschitzian around
\bar{x}
. Using the continuity of
f_{i}
at
\bar{x}
and applying the same argument as in the proof of
[
8
, Theorem 3.24]
yield the existence of
\varepsilon>0
such that for any
t\in(0,\varepsilon)
we get the equality

\displaystyle\frac{f(\bar{x}+td)-f(\bar{x})}{t}=\max_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{\max}(\bar{x})}\left\{\frac{f_{i}(\bar{x}+td)-f_{i}(\bar{x})}{t}\right\}.

(2.6)

Taking the limit as
t\to 0^{+}
in (
2.6
) brings us to the relationships

\displaystyle f^{\prime}(\bar{x};d)=

\displaystyle\limsup_{t\to 0^{+}}\frac{f(\bar{x}+td)-f(\bar{x})}{t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\limsup_{t\to 0^{+}}\max_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{\max}(\bar{x})}\left\{\frac{f_{i}(\bar{x}+td)-f_{i}(\bar{x})}{t}\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\max_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{\max}(\bar{x})}\left\{\limsup_{t\to 0^{+}}\frac{f_{i}(\bar{x}+td)-f_{i}(\bar{x})}{t}\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\max_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{\max}(\bar{x})}\left\{(f_{i})^{\prime}(\bar{x};d)\right\}.

Remembering that
f(\bar{x})=f_{i}(\bar{x})
for any
i\in\mathcal{I}_{\max}(\bar{x})
tells us that

\max_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{\max}(\bar{x})}\left\{(f_{i})^{\prime}(\bar{x};d)\right\}=\max_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{\max}(\bar{x})}\left\{f_{i}(\bar{x})+(f_{i})^{\prime}(\bar{x};d)\right\}-f(\bar{x})\leq\max_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\left\{f_{i}(\bar{x})+(f_{i})^{\prime}(\bar{x};d)\right\}-f(\bar{x}),

which verifies (
2.5
) and thus completes the proof of the lemma.

\hfill\quad\square

[

, Proposition 2.4]
, provides a relationship between the subdifferential (
2.2
) of a locally Lipschitzian and prox-regular function
f
and the upper directional derivative (
2.4
) of
-f
.

Lemma 2.4

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to(-\infty,\infty]
be locally Lipschitzian around
\bar{x}
and prox-regular at this point. Then
f
is lower regular at
\bar{x}
with the property
\mathrm{co}\,\partial(-f)(\bar{x})=-\partial f(\bar{x})
and the representation

\displaystyle(-f)^{\prime}(\bar{x},d)=\inf\left\{\langle-v,d\rangle\mid v\in\partial f(\bar{x})\right\}\;\mbox{ for any }\;d\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

Definition 2.5

Let
\bar{x}
be a feasible solution to problem (

).
We say that the no nonzero abnormal multiplier constraint qualification (NNAMCQ) holds at

if for any
\lambda_{i}\geq 0
,
i\in\mathcal{I}
, not all equal to zero, that satisfy
\lambda_{i}\varphi_{i}(\bar{x})=0
for all

, one has

0\notin\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\lambda_{i}\big(\nabla g_{i}(\bar{x})-\partial h_{i}(\bar{x})\big)+\mathcal{N}_{X}(\bar{x}).

(2.7)

The extended no nonzero abnormal multiplier constraint qualification (ENNAMCQ) holds at

3
ESQM for Constrained Difference Programming

In this section, we propose a new algorithm of the ESQM type to solve constrained problems of difference programming and conduct its convergence analysis.

To design our version of ESQM for the difference program (
1.1
), define the
penalized problem

\min_{x\in X}\phi_{p}(x):=\frac{1}{p}\varphi_{0}(x)+\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\max\{\varphi_{i}(x),0\},

(3.1)

where
p>0
is the
penalty parameter
.
At each iteration
k
, given the current iterate
x^{k}\in X
and the penalty parameter
p_{k}
, we construct a linear approximation of
\phi_{p_{k}}(x)
at
x^{k}
by

\tilde{\phi}_{k}(x):=\frac{1}{p_{k}}\left(\varphi_{0}(x^{k})+\langle\nabla g_{0}(x^{k})-v_{0}^{k},x-x^{k}\rangle\right)+\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\max\left\{\varphi_{i}(x^{k})+\langle\nabla g_{i}(x^{k})-v_{i}^{k},x-x^{k}\rangle,0\right\},

(3.2)

where
v_{i}^{k}
is a limiting subgradient of
h_{i}
at
x^{k}
, i.e.,
v_{i}^{k}\in\partial h_{i}(x^{k})
, for
i\in\mathcal{I}
. The update direction
d^{k}
is obtained by solving the
strongly convex quadratic programming subproblem
:

\displaystyle\min_{d\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\,\tilde{\phi}_{k}(x^{k}+d)+{\frac{1}{2}\langle G_{k}d,d\rangle}+\delta_{X}(x^{k}+d),

(3.3)

where
G_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is a suitably chosen symmetric positive definite matrix designed to
flexibly incorporate second-order information of the smooth component
g_{0}
when available, thereby yielding a more accurate local quadratic model for the subproblem (
3.3
) and potentially improving the overall efficiency of the algorithm. We assume
G_{k}
satisfies the following uniform boundedness property throughout the paper.

Assumption 3.1

There exists
\alpha,M_{G}>0
such that for all
k\geq 0
,

\alpha\|d\|^{2}\leq\langle G_{k}d,d\rangle\leq M_{G}\|d\|^{2},\quad\forall d\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

This assumption ensures the strong convexity of subproblem (
3.3
), guaranteeing that it is computationally tractable and yields a unique optimal solution
d^{k}
.

After determining the update direction
d^{k}
, we employ a
backtracking linesearch strategy
to compute an appropriate stepsize for updating the next iterate
x^{k+1}
. Specifically, set the stepsize
\tau_{k}:=\beta^{q_{k}}
, where
\beta\in(0,1)
and
q_{k}
is the smallest nonnegative integer ensuring the following condition

{\phi_{p_{k}}(x^{k}+\tau_{k}d^{k})\leq\tilde{\phi}_{k}(x^{k}+\tau_{k}d^{k})+(1-\sigma)\alpha\tau_{k}\|d^{k}\|^{2}}

(3.4)

where
\sigma\in(0,1)
is a predefined parameter, and
\alpha
is the positive definiteness modulus of
G_{k}
introduced in Assumption
3.1
. Condition (
3.4
) bounds the error between the penalized function and its local linearization. As we will establish later in Lemma
3.6
, this condition guarantees a sufficient decrease in
\phi_{p_{k}}(x)

\displaystyle\phi_{p_{k}}(x^{k}+\tau_{k}d^{k})\leq\phi_{p_{k}}(x^{k})-\sigma\alpha\tau_{k}\|d^{k}\|^{2}.

The existence of a finite step size
\tau_{k}
satisfying this condition is guaranteed (see Proposition
3.4
below). The new iterate
x^{k+1}
is then updated by

x^{k+1}=x^{k}+\tau_{k}d^{k}.

Note that the convexity of
X
and the fact that
x^{k},x^{k}+d^{k}\in X
ensure that
x^{k+1}\in X
.

At the end of each iteration
k
, we update the penalty parameter
p_{k}
.
The role of
p_{k}
is to enforce the feasibility of the iterates (i.e., satisfying all constraints such that
\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\max\{\varphi_{i}(x),0\}=0
). We first compute an approximation of the constraint violation as

t^{k}:=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\max\{\varphi_{i}(x^{k})+\langle\nabla g_{i}(x^{k})-v_{i}^{k},d^{k}\rangle,0\}.

Intuitively, a large violation
t^{k}
suggests that
p_{k}
is insufficient to enforce feasibility, necessitating an increase. However, for a given
p_{k}
, the current iterate
x^{k}
is generally not an exact minimizer of the penalized problem (
3.1
). This inexactness complicates the assessment of whether
p_{k}
is too small. We use the norm of the update direction,
\|d^{k}\|
, to measure this inexactness, since
d^{k}=0
corresponds to
x^{k}
being a stationary point of (
3.1
). A smaller
\|d^{k}\|
indicates that
x^{k}
is close to a stationary point, meaning the constraint violation
t^{k}
should be small. Conversely, a larger
\|d^{k}\|
implies lower subproblem accuracy, and a larger
t^{k}
can be temporarily tolerated.

Thus, we dynamically scale the threshold for
t^{k}
relative to
\|d^{k}\|
. Specifically, if
t^{k}
exceeds this threshold, i.e.,
t^{k}\geq c_{p}\|d^{k}\|
for a preset parameter
c_{p}>0
, the penalty parameter
p_{k+1}
is increased; otherwise, it remains unchanged. This relative thresholding is designed to increase
p_{k}
when the penalization appears inadequate, helping to avoid excessively large penalty values in the early iterations.

With these ingredients in hand, we are ready to design the desired algorithm of the ESQM type to solve the class of constrained difference programming problems (
1.1
). From now on, Algorithm
1
described below is referred to as
CDP-ESQM
.

Algorithm 1

ESQM for Solving Constrained Difference Programming (CDP-ESQM)

1:

Initial iterate
x^{0}\in X
, linesearch parameters
\beta\in(0,1)
and
\sigma\in(0,1)
, penalty parameters
p_{0},\varrho_{p},c_{p}>0
.

2:

for

k=0,1,\dots

do

3:

Choose
v_{i}^{k}\in\partial h_{i}(x^{k})
for
i\in\{0\}\cup\mathcal{I}
.

4:

Construct a symmetric positive definite matrix
G_{k}
satisfying Assumption
3.1
.

5:

Compute the update direction
d^{k}
by solving the strongly convex quadratic programming subproblem (
3.3
).

6:

If

d^{k}=0
and
\varphi_{i}(x^{k})\leq 0,\ \forall i\in\mathcal{I}
,
then
STOP and return
x^{k}
.

7:

Set
\tau_{k}=1
.

8:

while

\phi_{p_{k}}(x^{k}+\tau_{k}d^{k})>\tilde{\phi}_{k}(x^{k}+\tau_{k}d^{k})+(1-\sigma)\alpha\tau_{k}\|d^{k}\|^{2}

do

\tau_{k}=\beta\tau_{k}
.

9:

end

while

10:

Update the iterate:
x^{k+1}:=x^{k}+\tau_{k}d^{k}
.

11:

Compute
t^{k}:=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\max\{\varphi_{i}(x^{k})+\langle\nabla g_{i}(x^{k})-v_{i}^{k},d^{k}\rangle,0\}.

12:

Update the penalty parameter by

p_{k+1}=\begin{cases}p_{k}+\varrho_{p}&\text{if }t^{k}\geq c_{p}\|d^{k}\|,\\
p_{k}&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

13:

end

for

In the rest of this section, we provide a detailed
convergence analysis
of CDP-ESQM in Algorithm
1
. Unless otherwise stated, our
standing assumptions
on the initial data of problem (
1.1

1
, i.e., the set
X
is closed and convex, the functions
g_{i}
(

{\cal C}^{1,1}

h_{i}
(

Remembering that
d^{k}
is a solution to the convex subproblem (
3.3
) and applying the subdifferential calculus rules of convex analysis for finite sums and maxima (see, e,g.,
[
33
, Theorems 3.18, 3.27]
) and the first-order optimality conditions from
[
33
, Theorem 7.15]
, we arrive at the following result.

Lemma 3.2

Let
d^{k}
be the solution to subproblem (
3.3
). Then there exist
\lambda_{k,i}\in[0,1]
for
i\in\mathcal{I}
such that

0\in\frac{1}{p_{k}}\left(\nabla g_{0}(x^{k})-v_{0}^{k}\right)+{\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\lambda_{k,i}\left(\nabla g_{i}(x^{k})-v_{i}^{k}\right)}+{G_{k}}d^{k}+\mathcal{N}_{X}\left(x^{k}+d^{k}\right),

(3.5)

and, for each
i\in\mathcal{I}
, the following conditions hold

\lambda_{k,i}\min\left\{\varphi_{i}(x^{k})+\langle\nabla g_{i}(x^{k})-v_{i}^{k},d^{k}\rangle,0\right\}=(1-\lambda_{k,i})\max\left\{\varphi_{i}(x^{k})+\langle\nabla g_{i}(x^{k})-v_{i}^{k},d^{k}\rangle,0\right\}=0.

(3.6)

The next useful lemma is derived by employing the equalities in (
3.6
).

Lemma 3.3

Let
d^{k}
solve subproblem (
3.3
). Then for any
\lambda_{k,i}\in\left[0,1\right]
(
i\in\mathcal{I}
) satisfying (
3.6
), we have, for each
i\in\mathcal{I}

\max\left\{\varphi_{i}(x^{k})+\langle\nabla g_{i}(x^{k})-v_{i}^{k},d^{k}\rangle,0\right\}-\max\left\{\varphi_{i}(x^{k}),0\right\}\leq\lambda_{k,i}\langle\nabla g_{i}(x^{k})-v_{i}^{k},d^{k}\rangle.

(3.7)

Proof
.  For each
i\in\mathcal{I}
, picking any
\lambda_{k,i}\in\left[0,1\right]
tells us that

\lambda_{k,i}\varphi_{i}(x^{k})\leq\lambda_{k,i}\max\left\{\varphi_{i}(x^{k}),0\right\}\leq\max\left\{\varphi_{i}(x^{k}),0\right\}.

Since
\lambda_{k,i}
satisfies (
3.6
), it follows that

\max\left\{\varphi_{i}(x^{k})+\langle\nabla g_{i}(x^{k})-v_{i}^{k},d^{k}\rangle,0\right\}=\lambda_{k,i}\left(\varphi_{i}(x^{k})+\langle\nabla g_{i}(x^{k})-v_{i}^{k},d^{k}\rangle\right).

Combining the two inequalities above yields

\displaystyle\max\left\{\varphi_{i}(x^{k})+\langle\nabla g_{i}(x^{k})-v_{i}^{k},d^{k}\rangle,0\right\}-\max\left\{\varphi_{i}(x^{k}),0\right\}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\lambda_{k,i}\left(\varphi_{i}(x^{k})+\langle\nabla g_{i}(x^{k})-v_{i}^{k},d^{k}\rangle\right)-\lambda_{k,i}\varphi_{i}(x^{k})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lambda_{k,i}\langle\nabla g_{i}(x^{k})-v_{i}^{k},d^{k}\rangle,

which verifies (
3.7
) and thus completes the proof of the lemma.

\hfill\quad\square

Building upon the preceding lemmas, we now demonstrate the
well-definedness
of the
backtracking linesearch
procedure in the CDP-ESQM algorithm, ensuring that the stepsize
\tau_{k}
can be determined through a finite number of backtracking steps.

Proposition 3.4

The backtracking linesearch procedure in CDP-ESQM terminates in a finite number of steps, i.e., there exists
q_{k}\in\mathbb{N}
for which the stepsize
\tau_{k}=\beta^{q_{k}}
satisfies (
3.4
).

Proof
.
Fix any given
k\in\mathbb{N}
. For each
i\in\mathcal{I}
, since
g_{i}
is of class
\mathcal{C}^{1,1}
on
X
, Lemma A.11 in
[
20
]
ensures that there exist constants
r_{g_{i}},\varepsilon_{g_{i}}>0
such that the inequality

\displaystyle g_{i}(x^{k}+\tau d^{k})\leq g_{i}(x^{k})+\tau\langle\nabla g_{i}(x^{k}),d^{k}\rangle+\frac{r_{g_{i}}}{2}\tau^{2}\|d^{k}\|^{2}

holds for any
0\leq\tau\leq\varepsilon_{g_{i}}
. Furthermore, for each
i\in\mathcal{I}
, the prox-regularity of
h_{i}
on
X
guarantees the existence of constants
r_{h_{i}},\varepsilon_{h_{i}}>0
such that for any
0\leq\tau\leq\varepsilon_{h_{i}}
, the following estimate holds:

\displaystyle h_{i}(x^{k}+\tau d^{k})\geq h_{i}(x^{k})+\tau\langle v_{i}^{k},d^{k}\rangle-\frac{r_{h_{i}}}{2}\tau^{2}\|d^{k}\|^{2}.

Combining the above inequalities yields that for any
0\leq\tau\leq\varepsilon^{\prime}:=\min_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\{\varepsilon_{g_{i}},\varepsilon_{h_{i}}\}
, we have

\displaystyle\varphi_{0}(x^{k}+td^{k})\leq\varphi_{0}(x^{k})+\tau\langle\nabla g_{0}(x^{k})-v_{0}^{k},d^{k}\rangle+\frac{r_{g_{0}}+r_{h_{0}}}{2}\tau^{2}\|d^{k}\|^{2}

and for each
i\in\mathcal{I}
,

\displaystyle\max\left\{\varphi_{i}(x^{k}+\tau d^{k}),0\right\}\leq

\displaystyle\max\left\{\varphi_{i}(x^{k})+\tau\langle\nabla g_{i}(x^{k})-v_{i}^{k},d^{k}\rangle+\frac{r_{g_{i}}+r_{h_{i}}}{2}\tau^{2}\|d^{k}\|^{2},0\right\}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\max\left\{\varphi_{i}(x^{k})+\tau\langle\nabla g_{i}(x^{k})-v_{i}^{k},d^{k}\rangle,0\right\}+\frac{r_{g_{i}}+r_{h_{i}}}{2}\tau^{2}\|d^{k}\|^{2}.

Consequently, combining the above inequalities, it follows that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{p_{k}}{\varphi_{0}}(x^{k}+\tau d^{k})+{\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}}\max\left\{{\varphi_{i}}(x^{k}+\tau d^{k}),0\right\}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{p_{k}}\left({\varphi_{0}}(x^{k})+\tau\langle\nabla g_{0}(x^{k})-v_{0}^{k},d^{k}\rangle\right)+{\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}}\max\left\{{\varphi_{i}}(x^{k})+\tau\langle\nabla g_{i}(x^{k})-v_{i}^{k},d^{k}\rangle,0\right\}

\displaystyle+\left(\frac{r_{g_{0}}+r_{h_{0}}}{2p_{k}}+\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\frac{r_{g_{i}}+r_{h_{i}}}{2}\right)\tau^{2}\|d^{k}\|^{2}

holds for all
0\leq\tau\leq\varepsilon_{k}^{\prime}
. According to the definition of
\tilde{\phi}_{k}
and by defining
r:=(r_{g_{0}}+r_{h_{0}})/p_{k}+\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}(r_{g_{i}}+r_{h_{i}})
, we obtain for any
0\leq\tau\leq\varepsilon^{\prime}
,

\phi_{p_{k}}(x^{k}+\tau d^{k})\leq\tilde{\phi}_{k}(x^{k}+\tau d^{k})+\frac{r}{2}\tau^{2}\|d^{k}\|^{2}.

(3.8)

This further implies that when
0\leq\tau\leq\varepsilon:=\min\left\{\varepsilon^{\prime},2(1-\sigma)\alpha/r\right\}
, it holds that

\phi_{p_{k}}(x^{k}+\tau d^{k})\leq\tilde{\phi}_{k}(x^{k}+\tau d^{k})+(1-\sigma)\alpha\tau\|d^{k}\|^{2}.

This readily implies that

\left\{\tau\mid\phi_{p_{k}}(x^{k}+\tau d^{k})\leq\tilde{\phi}_{k}(x^{k}+\tau d^{k})+(1-\sigma)\alpha\tau\|d^{k}\|^{2},\,\tau=\beta^{q},q\in\mathbb{N}\right\}\neq\varnothing,

which ensures that the backtracking linesearch terminates in a finite number of steps.

\hfill\quad\square

We next demonstrate the following key inequalies for deriving the sufficient decrease of penalized functions.

Lemma 3.5

Let the direction
d^{k}
solve subproblem (
3.3
). Then there exist
\lambda_{k,i}\in[0,1]
for
i\in\mathcal{I}
satisfying conditions (
3.5
), (
3.6
) and such that

(\phi_{p_{k}})^{\prime}(x^{k};d^{k})\leq\frac{1}{p_{k}}\left\langle\nabla g_{0}(x^{k})-v_{0}^{k},d^{k}\right\rangle+\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\lambda_{k,i}\left\langle\nabla g_{i}(x^{k})-v_{i}^{k},d^{k}\right\rangle\leq-\langle G_{k}d^{k},d^{k}\rangle.

(3.9)

Proof
.
Since
d^{k}
solves the convex subproblem (
3.3
), Lemma
3.2
provides the existence of
\lambda_{k,i}\in[0,1]
(
i\in\mathcal{I}
) satisfying (
3.5
) and (
3.6
).
By definition of the upper directional derivative, we have

(\phi_{p_{k}})^{\prime}(x^{k};d^{k})\leq\frac{1}{p_{k}}\left(\langle\nabla g_{0}(x^{k}),d^{k}\rangle+(-h_{0})^{\prime}(x^{k};d^{k})\right)+\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\left(\max\{\varphi_{i},0\}\right)^{\prime}(x^{k};d^{k}).

(3.10)

Remembering that
h_{0}
is locally Lipschitzian around
x^{k}
and prox-regular at
x^{k}
, and that
v_{0}^{k}\in\partial h_{0}(x^{k})
, we get by Lemma
2.4
that

(-h_{0})^{\prime}(x^{k};d^{k})=\inf\left\{\langle-v,d^{k}\rangle\mid v\in\partial h_{0}(x^{k})\right\}\leq\langle-v_{0}^{k},d^{k}\rangle.

(3.11)

Applying now Lemma
2.3
to the terms
\left(\max\{\varphi_{i},0\}\right)^{\prime}(x^{k};d^{k})
tells us that for each
i\in\mathcal{I}
,

\left(\max\{{\varphi_{i}},0\}\right)^{\prime}\left(x^{k};d^{k}\right)\leq\max\left\{{\varphi_{i}}(x^{k})+{\varphi_{i}}^{\prime}(x^{k};d^{k}),0\right\}-\max\left\{{\varphi_{i}}(x^{k}),0\right\}.

(3.12)

Since
h_{i}
is also locally Lipschitzian around
x^{k}
and prox-regular at this point with
v_{i}^{k}\in\partial h_{i}(x^{k})
, it follows from Lemma
2.4
that

(-h_{i})^{\prime}(x^{k};d^{k})\leq\langle-v_{i}^{k},d^{k}\rangle.

Combining the latter with the smoothness of
g_{i}
yields for each
i\in\mathcal{I}

\varphi_{i}^{\prime}(x^{k};d^{k})=\langle\nabla{g_{i}}(x^{k}),d^{k}\rangle+(-h_{i})^{\prime}(x^{k};d^{k})\leq\langle\nabla g_{i}(x^{k})-v_{i}^{k},d^{k}\rangle,

and therefore, summing over
i\in\mathcal{I}
, we deduce from (
3.12
) that

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\left(\max\{\varphi_{i},0\}\right)^{\prime}\left(x^{k};d^{k}\right)\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\left(\max\left\{\varphi_{i}(x^{k})+\langle\nabla g_{i}(x^{k})-v_{i}^{k},d^{k}\rangle,0\right\}-\max\left\{\varphi_{i}(x^{k}),0\right\}\right)

(3.13)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\lambda_{k,i}\left\langle\nabla g_{i}(x^{k})-v_{i}^{k},d^{k}\right\rangle,

while the second inequality therein follows from Lemma
3.3
. Combining (
3.10
), (
3.11
) and (
3.13
) yields the first estimate in (
3.9
). Next we use (
3.5
),
the fact that

2.1
) of the normal cone
\mathcal{N}_{X}
to obtain

\left\langle\frac{1}{p_{k}}\left(\nabla g_{0}(x^{k})-v_{0}^{k}\right)+\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\lambda_{k,i}\left(\nabla g_{i}(x^{k})-v_{i}^{k}\right)+G_{k}d^{k},x^{k}+d^{k}-x^{k}\right\rangle\leq 0,

which provides in turn the estimate

\frac{1}{p_{k}}\left\langle\nabla g_{0}(x^{k})-v_{0}^{k},d^{k}\right\rangle+\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\lambda_{k,i}\left\langle\nabla g_{i}(x^{k})-v_{i}^{k},d^{k}\right\rangle\leq-\langle G_{k}d^{k},d^{k}\rangle.

(3.14)

Then (
3.14
) verifies the claimed inequalities in (
3.9
).

\hfill\quad\square

Now we show that the condition (
3.4
) in backtracking linesearch procedure for selecting an appropriate stepsize guarantees the updated iterate satisfy the sufficient decrease condition.

Lemma 3.6

Let
\{x^{k}\}
,
\{d^{k}\}
, and
\{\tau_{k}\}
be the sequences generated by CDP-ESQM. Then we have

\displaystyle\phi_{p_{k}}(x^{k+1})\leq\phi_{p_{k}}(x^{k})-\sigma\alpha\tau_{k}\|d^{k}\|^{2}.

(3.15)

Proof
.
By Lemma
3.5
, there exist multipliers
\lambda_{k,i}
(
i\in\mathcal{I}
) satisfying conditions (
3.5
) and (
3.6
) such that

\frac{1}{p_{k}}\langle\nabla g_{0}(x^{k})-v_{0}^{k},d^{k}\rangle+\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\lambda_{k,i}\langle\nabla g_{i}(x^{k})-v_{i}^{k},d^{k}\rangle\leq-\langle G_{k}d^{k},d^{k}\rangle.

(3.16)

Since these specific multipliers
\lambda_{k,i}
satisfy (
3.6
) for each
i\in\mathcal{I}
, they fulfill the assumptions of Lemma
3.3
. Furthermore, since
\tau_{k}\in(0,1]
, we have the following convex combination:

\displaystyle\varphi_{i}(x^{k})+\tau_{k}\langle\nabla g_{i}(x^{k})-v_{i}^{k},d^{k}\rangle=\tau_{k}\left(\varphi_{i}(x^{k})+\langle\nabla g_{i}(x^{k})-v_{i}^{k},d^{k}\rangle\right)+(1-\tau_{k})\varphi_{i}(x^{k}).

Using the convexity of the function
t\mapsto\max\{t,0\}
yields, for each
i\in\mathcal{I}
,

\displaystyle\max\left\{\varphi_{i}(x^{k})+\tau_{k}\langle\nabla g_{i}(x^{k})-v_{i}^{k},d^{k}\rangle,0\right\}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\tau_{k}\max\left\{\varphi_{i}(x^{k})+\langle\nabla g_{i}(x^{k})-v_{i}^{k},d^{k}\rangle,0\right\}+(1-\tau_{k})\max\left\{\varphi_{i}(x^{k}),0\right\}.

Next, applying Lemma
3.3
to bound the first term on the right-hand side gives

\displaystyle\tau_{k}\max\left\{\varphi_{i}(x^{k})+\langle\nabla g_{i}(x^{k})-v_{i}^{k},d^{k}\rangle,0\right\}+(1-\tau_{k})\max\left\{\varphi_{i}(x^{k}),0\right\}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\tau_{k}\left(\max\left\{\varphi_{i}(x^{k}),0\right\}+\lambda_{k,i}\langle\nabla g_{i}(x^{k})-v_{i}^{k},d^{k}\rangle\right)+(1-\tau_{k})\max\left\{\varphi_{i}(x^{k}),0\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\max\left\{\varphi_{i}(x^{k}),0\right\}+\tau_{k}\lambda_{k,i}\left\langle\nabla g_{i}(x^{k})-v_{i}^{k},d^{k}\right\rangle.

Consequently, the linear approximation
\tilde{\phi}_{k}
evaluated at
x^{k}+\tau_{k}d^{k}
satisfies

\displaystyle\tilde{\phi}_{k}(x^{k}+\tau_{k}d^{k})

(3.17)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{p_{k}}\varphi_{0}(x^{k})+\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\max\{\varphi_{i}(x^{k}),0\}+\frac{\tau_{k}}{p_{k}}\langle\nabla g_{0}(x^{k})-v_{0}^{k},d^{k}\rangle+\tau_{k}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\lambda_{k,i}\langle\nabla g_{i}(x^{k})-v_{i}^{k},d^{k}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\phi_{p_{k}}(x^{k})+\tau_{k}\left(\frac{1}{p_{k}}\langle\nabla g_{0}(x^{k})-v_{0}^{k},d^{k}\rangle+\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\lambda_{k,i}\langle\nabla g_{i}(x^{k})-v_{i}^{k},d^{k}\rangle\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\phi_{p_{k}}(x^{k})-\tau_{k}\langle G_{k}d^{k},d^{k}\rangle,

where the last inequality follows from (
3.16
). Combining this result with the linesearch condition (
3.4
) and the Assumption
3.1
on
G_{k}
that
\langle G_{k}d^{k},d^{k}\rangle\geq\alpha\|d^{k}\|^{2}
implies

\displaystyle\phi_{p_{k}}(x^{k+1})=\phi_{p_{k}}(x^{k}+\tau_{k}d^{k})

\displaystyle\leq\tilde{\phi}_{k}(x^{k}+\tau_{k}d^{k})+(1-\sigma)\alpha\tau_{k}\|d^{k}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\phi_{p_{k}}(x^{k})-\tau_{k}\langle G_{k}d^{k},d^{k}\rangle+(1-\sigma)\alpha\tau_{k}\|d^{k}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\phi_{p_{k}}(x^{k})-\sigma\alpha\tau_{k}\|d^{k}\|^{2}.

This completes the proof.

\hfill\quad\square

A_{k}:=\frac{1}{p_{k}}\left(\varphi_{0}(x^{k})-m_{\varphi_{0}}\right)+\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\max\left\{\varphi_{i}(x^{k}),0\right\},\quad k\in\mathbb{N}.

Proposition 3.7

Let
\{x^{k}\}
,
\{d^{k}\}
, and
\{\tau_{k}\}
be the sequences generated by CDP-ESQM. Then we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{p_{k+1}}\left(\varphi_{0}(x^{k+1})-m_{\varphi_{0}}\right)+

\left\{x^{k}\right\}

. The local Lipschitz continuity of

. By passing to a subsequence if necessary, we obtain
v_{i}^{k_{l}}\to\bar{v}_{i}
as

4
Sequential Convergence of CDP-ESQM under PLK Property

In this section, we establish efficient conditions for the
global sequential convergence
of CDP-ESQM that is based on the
Polyak-Łojasiewicz-Kurdyka
properties. This terminology has been recently coined in
[
9
]
, where the reader can find detailed historical remarks and discussions. Other names for such conditions and their special cases formulated in terms of various subgradient mappings and their smooth counterparts are used in
[
6
,
12
,
22
]
and the references therein.

Definition 4.1

Given an l.s.c. function
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to(-\infty,\infty]
,
we say that
f
satisfies the
(
basic
)
Polyak-Łojasiewicz-Kurdyka
(
PLK
)
property at
\bar{x}\in\mathrm{dom}(f)
if there exist positive numbers
\varepsilon,\eta
and a concave function
\psi:[0,\eta)\rightarrow[0,\infty)
such that

(a)
\psi(0)=0
,

(b)
\psi
is
{\cal C}^{1}
on
(0,\eta)
and continuous at
0
,

(c)
\psi^{\prime}>0
on
(0,\eta)
,

and the following subdifferential inequality

\psi^{\prime}(f(x)-f(\bar{x}))\mathrm{dist}(0,{\partial}f(x))\geq 1

holds whenever
x\in\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid\|x-\bar{x}\|<\varepsilon,~~f(\bar{x})<f(x)<f(\bar{x})+\eta\}
.
A function
f
satisfying the PKL condition at any point in
\mathrm{dom}(f)
is called a PKL function.

To establish the global sequential convergence of CDP-ESQM, we assume in what follows that the sequence of iterates
\{x^{k}\}
generated by CDP-ESQM is bounded. To proceed further, we construct an appropriate merit function. Since
\{x^{k}\}
is bounded, there exists a compact convex set
D\subset X
such that
\{x^{k}\}\subset D
. According to our standing assumptions, the functions
h_{i}
(
i\in\mathcal{I}
) are locally Lipschitz continuous and prox-regular over
X
, which implies that they are lower-
\mathcal{C}^{2}
, i.e., they can be expressed as the difference of a convex function and a quadratic function around any point in
X
. The compactness of
D
allows us to select its finite covering and then, by using
[
32
, Theorem 7.102]
, to find
\rho_{h}>0
such that the functions
h_{i}(x)+\frac{\rho_{h}}{2}\|x\|^{2}
, for
i\in\mathcal{I}
, are convex on
D
. Define the approximations

\widehat{g}_{i}(x):=g_{i}(x)+\frac{\rho_{h}}{2}\|x\|^{2},\quad\widehat{h}_{i}(x)=h_{i}(x)+\frac{\rho_{h}}{2}\|x\|^{2},\quad{i\in\mathcal{I},}

and then construct the parametric family of
merit functions
by

\displaystyle E_{p}(x,\tau,d,G,\mathbf{w},\mathbf{\widehat{v}}):=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{p}\left(\widehat{g}_{0}(x)+\langle w_{0},\tau d\rangle+\widehat{h}_{0}^{*}(\widehat{v}_{0})-\langle\widehat{v}_{0},x\rangle\right)

(4.1)

\displaystyle+\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\max\left\{\widehat{g}_{i}(x)+\langle w_{i},\tau d\rangle+\widehat{h}_{i}^{*}(\widehat{v}_{i})-\langle\widehat{v}_{i},x\rangle,0\right\}+\tau\langle Gd,d\rangle+\delta_{X}(x+d),

where
\mathbf{w}=(w_{i})_{i\in\{0\}\cup\mathcal{I}}
,
\mathbf{\widehat{v}}=(\widehat{v}_{i})_{i\in\{0\}\cup\mathcal{I}}
, and
\widehat{h}_{i}^{*}
denotes the
Fenchel conjugate
of
\widehat{h}_{i}(x)+\delta_{D}(x)
given by

\widehat{h}_{i}^{*}(\widehat{v}):=\sup_{x\in D}\left\{\langle\widehat{v},x\rangle-\widehat{h}_{i}(x)\right\},\quad i\in\mathcal{I}.

Notice that to enable the sequential convergence of the generalized CDP-ESQM algorithm under the PLK property with an iteration-varying matrix
G_{k}
, we designed the merit function
E_{p}
(
4.1
)
to explicitly incorporate
G
as a parameter. This technique is inspired by
[
25
]
. Moreover, to establish sequential convergence when employing non-unit step sizes generated by a line search procedure, the merit function is also designed to explicitly incorporate the step size
\tau
as a parameter.

Having the sequences
\{x^{k}\}
,
\{d^{k}\}
, and
\{v_{i}^{k}\}
(
i\in\{0\}\cup\mathcal{I}
) generated by CDP-ESQM, denote
w_{i}^{k}:=\nabla g_{i}(x^{k})-v_{i}^{k}
and
\widehat{v}_{i}^{k}:=v_{i}^{k}+\rho_{h}x^{k}
for
i\in\{0\}\cup\mathcal{I}
. Letting
\mathbf{w}^{k}=(w_{i}^{k})_{i\in\{0\}\cup\mathcal{I}}
and
\mathbf{\widehat{v}}^{k}=(\widehat{v}_{i}^{k})_{i\in\{0\}\cup\mathcal{I}}

evaluated along the algorithmic sequence
\{(x^{k},\tau_{k},d^{k},G_{k},{\mathbf{w}^{k},\mathbf{\widehat{v}}^{k}})\}

Lemma 4.2

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\tilde{\phi}_{k+1}(x^{k+1}+\tau_{k+1}d^{k+1})+\tau_{k+1}\langle G_{k+1}d^{k+1},d^{k+1}\rangle

Theorem 4.3

It has been well recognized that the PLK property holds for broad classes of functions that appear in optimization and variational analysis. A remarkable class of such functions consists of semialgebraic ones.

Definition 4.4

A set
D\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is called semialgebraic if there exists a finite number
of real polynomial functions
g_{ij},h_{ij}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
such that

D=\bigcup_{j=1}^{p}\bigcap_{i=1}^{q}\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}~|~g_{ij}(x)=0,~h_{ij}(x)<0\}.

A function
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow(-\infty,\infty]
is semialgebraic if its graph
is a semialgebraic set.

Recall that the class of semialgebraic functions is invariant with respect to taking sums and compositions, that the indicator function of semialgebraic sets is semialgebraic, and that the partial minimization of
semialgebraic functions over semialgebraic sets is semialgebraic; see, e.g.,
[
6
]
. This allows us to conclude that the merit function
E_{\bar{p}}
, built in (
4.1
) upon the semialgebraic data of the difference programming problem (
1.1
), is semialgebraic. Therefore, we get the following efficient consequence of Theorem
4.3
.

Corollary 4.5

If both sequences

5
VI-Constrained Difference Programming

In this section, we consider the
VI-constrained difference programming problem
defined by

\displaystyle\min_{y\in Y,z\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{2}}}

\displaystyle\Psi(y,z):=\Psi_{1}(y,z)-\Psi_{2}(y,z)

(5.1)

\displaystyle\text{subject to }

\displaystyle z\in S(y):=\left\{z\in\Gamma(y)\mid\langle F(y,z),\theta-z\rangle\geq 0\text{ for all }\theta\in\Gamma(y)\right\},

where
Y\subset\mathbb{R}^{m_{1}}
is nonempty, convex, and closed sets. Throughout the section, the function
\Psi_{1}:\mathbb{R}^{m_{1}}\times\mathbb{R}^{m_{2}}\to\mathbb{R}
and mapping
F:\mathbb{R}^{m_{1}}\times\mathbb{R}^{m_{2}}\to\mathbb{R}^{m_{2}}
are assumed to be continuously differentiable with locally Lipschitzian gradients and Jacobians, respectively, on
\left(Y\times\mathbb{R}^{m_{2}}\right)\cap\mathrm{gph}(\Gamma)
, while the function
\Psi_{2}:\mathbb{R}^{m_{1}}\times\mathbb{R}^{m_{2}}\to\mathbb{R}
is a locally Lipschitz continuous and prox-regular function over
\left(Y\times\mathbb{R}^{m_{2}}\right)\cap\mathrm{gph}(\Gamma)
. The set-valued mapping
\Gamma(y):\mathbb{R}^{m_{1}}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{m_{2}}
is defined by

\Gamma(y):=\{z\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{2}}\mid\zeta_{i}(y,z)\leq 0,i=1,\ldots,s\},

(5.2)

where each
\zeta_{i}(y,z):\mathbb{R}^{m_{1}}\times\mathbb{R}^{m_{2}}\to\mathbb{R}
is a continuously differentiable and convex function on
Y\times\mathbb{R}^{m_{2}}
.

To ensure the well-posedness of problem (
5.1
), we impose the additional standing assumptions.

Assumption 5.1

The following conditions hold:

(a)

The objective function
\Psi
is bounded from below on
\left(Y\times\mathbb{R}^{m_{2}}\right)\cap\mathrm{gph}(\Gamma)
.

(b)

There exists an open set
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{m_{1}}
such that
Y\subset U
and
\Gamma(y)\neq\varnothing
for all
y\in U
.

A classical approach to solving VI involves reformulating VI as a minimization problem using a gap function. Utilizing the
regularized gap function
introduced in
[
17
]
, we define

\mu_{\gamma}(y,z):=\inf\limits_{\theta\in\Gamma(y)}\left\{\langle F(y,z),\theta\rangle+\frac{1}{2\gamma}\|\theta-z\|^{2}\right\},

(5.3)

where
\gamma>0
. Then
\langle F(y,z),z\rangle-\mu_{\gamma}(y,z)
corresponds precisely to the regularized gap function for VI in (
5.1
) given
[
17
]
. As proved in

[
17
, Theorem 3.1]
, the following properties hold:

\langle F(y,z),z\rangle-\mu_{\gamma}(y,z)\geq 0\;\mbox{ for all }\;z\in\Gamma(y),

\langle F(y,z),z\rangle-\mu_{\gamma}(y,z)\leq 0,~z\in\Gamma(y)\quad\text{if and only if}\quad z\in S(y).

Leveraging this equivalence between the solution set of VI and the level set of the regularized gap function yields the single-level reformulation approach introduced in
[
28
]
. Consequently, the VI-constrained difference programming problem can be equivalently reformulated as follows

\displaystyle\min_{y\in Y,z\in\Gamma(y)}

\displaystyle\Psi(y,z)

(5.4)

\displaystyle\text{subject to }

\displaystyle\langle F(y,z),z\rangle-\mu_{\gamma}(y,z)\leq 0.

Our subsequent analysis demonstrates that reformulation (
5.4
) constitutes a special case of the constrained difference programming problem (
1.1
). Consequently, the proposed
Algorithm
1
(CDP-ESQM) can be directly applied to solving (
5.4
). To proceed in this way, let us first investigate the properties of the function
\mu_{\gamma}(y,z)
defined in (
5.3
). Specifically, we show that
\mu_{\gamma}(y,z)
is prox-regular and then provide an explicit expression for its subdifferential.

Observe that the optimization problem defining
\mu_{\gamma}(y,z)
in (
5.3
) is strongly convex. Its unique solution admits the closed-form expression

\theta_{\gamma}^{*}(y,z):=\mathop{\arg\min}\limits_{\theta\in\Gamma(y)}\left\{\langle F(y,z),\theta\rangle+\frac{1}{2\gamma}\|\theta-z\|^{2}\right\}=\mathrm{Proj}_{\Gamma(y)}\left(z-\gamma F(y,z)\right).

(5.5)

Thus, when the set
\Gamma(y)
is simple and the projection onto it is computationally tractable,
\theta_{\gamma}^{*}(y,z)
can be efficiently computed. Consequently,
\mu_{\gamma}(y,z)
is also readily computable since it has the expression

\mu_{\gamma}(y,z)=\langle F(y,z),\theta_{\gamma}^{*}(y,z)\rangle+\frac{1}{2\gamma}\|\theta_{\gamma}^{*}(y,z)-z\|^{2}.

Furthermore, the following lemma verifies the local boundedness of
\theta_{\gamma}^{*}(y,z)
on
Y\times\mathbb{R}^{m_{2}}
.

Lemma 5.2

The mapping
\theta_{\gamma}^{*}(y,z)
is locally bounded around every point in

Proof
.
Let
(\bar{y},\bar{z})\in Y\times\mathbb{R}^{m_{2}}

for every
k\in\mathbb{N}
. It follows from the continuity of

\displaystyle\left\langle F(y_{k},z_{k}),\theta^{*}_{\gamma}(y_{k},z_{k})\right\rangle+\frac{1}{2\gamma}\left\|\theta_{\gamma}^{*}(y_{k},z_{k})-z_{k}\right\|^{2}\to\infty\;\text{ as }\;k\to\infty.

(5.6)

Consider the value function
\nu(y):=\inf_{\theta}\{\|\theta\|\mid\theta\in\Gamma(y)\}
and deduce from the convexity of
\zeta_{i}(y,z)
for

that

is convex on

[

for all

any sufficiently large. Therefore, for such

satisfying

This leads us to the estimates

\displaystyle\left\langle F(y_{k},z_{k}),\theta^{*}_{\gamma}(y_{k},z_{k})\right\rangle+\frac{1}{2\gamma}\left\|\theta_{\gamma}^{*}(y_{k},z_{k})-z_{k}\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left\langle F(y_{k},z_{k}),\theta_{k}\right\rangle+\frac{1}{2\gamma}\left\|\theta_{k}-z_{k}\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|F(y_{k},z_{k})\right\|\left(\|\theta^{*}_{\gamma}(\bar{y},\bar{z})\|+L_{\theta}\|y_{k}-\bar{y}\|\right)+\frac{1}{\gamma}\left(2\|\theta^{*}_{\gamma}(\bar{y},\bar{z})\|^{2}+2L_{\theta}^{2}\|y_{k}-\bar{y}\|^{2}+\left\|z_{k}\right\|^{2}\right).

However, the right-hand side above remains bounded as

\hfill\quad\square

\bar{z})

\displaystyle\mu_{\gamma}(y,z)+\left(\gamma L_{F}^{2}+L_{\nabla F}M_{\theta}+\frac{1}{2\gamma}\right)\|(y,z)\|^{2}

(5.9)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\inf\limits_{\theta}\left\{\langle F(y,z),\theta\rangle+\frac{1}{2\gamma}\|\theta-z\|^{2}+\left(\gamma L_{F}^{2}+L_{\nabla F}M_{\theta}+\frac{1}{2\gamma}\right)\|(y,z)\|^{2}+\delta_{\mathrm{gph}(\Gamma)}(y,\theta)\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\inf\limits_{\theta}\left\{\left.\langle F(y,z),\theta\rangle+\frac{1}{2\gamma}\|\theta-z\|^{2}+\left(\gamma L_{F}^{2}+L_{\nabla F}M_{\theta}+\frac{1}{2\gamma}\right)\|(y,z)\|^{2}+\delta_{\mathrm{gph}(\Gamma)}(y,\theta)~\right|~\|\theta\|\leq 2M_{\theta}\right\}.

To justify the claimed convexity, let us first show that the function

\langle F(y,z),\theta\rangle+(\gamma L_{F}^{2}+L_{\nabla F}M_{\theta})\|(y,z)\|^{2}+\frac{1}{4\gamma}\|\theta\|^{2}

(5.10)

is convex with respect to
(y,z,\theta)
on the set
\mathbb{B}_{\varepsilon}(\bar{y},\bar{z})\times\mathbb{B}_{2M_{\theta}}
. This is achieved by verifying the monotonicity of its gradient. Specifically, we intend to check that the function

\left\langle\begin{bmatrix}\nabla_{y}F(y,z)^{T}\theta-\nabla_{y}F(y^{\prime},z^{\prime})^{T}\theta^{\prime}\\
\nabla_{z}F(y,z)^{T}\theta-\nabla_{z}F(y^{\prime},z^{\prime})^{T}\theta^{\prime}\\
F(y,z)-F(y^{\prime},z^{\prime})\end{bmatrix},\begin{bmatrix}y-y^{\prime}\\
z-z^{\prime}\\
\theta-\theta^{\prime}\end{bmatrix}\right\rangle+(2\gamma L_{F}^{2}+2L_{\nabla F}M_{\theta})\|(y,z)-(y^{\prime},z^{\prime})\|^{2}+\frac{1}{2\gamma}\|\theta-\theta^{\prime}\|^{2}.

(5.11)

is nonnegative for any
(y,z,\theta),(y^{\prime},z^{\prime},\theta^{\prime})\in\mathbb{B}_{\varepsilon}(\bar{y},\bar{z})\times\mathbb{B}_{2M_{\theta}}
. Indeed, picking such triples brings us to

\displaystyle\left\langle\begin{bmatrix}\nabla_{y}F(y,z)^{T}\theta-\nabla_{y}F(y^{\prime},z^{\prime})^{T}\theta^{\prime}\\
\nabla_{z}F(y,z)^{T}\theta-\nabla_{z}F(y^{\prime},z^{\prime})^{T}\theta^{\prime}\\
\end{bmatrix},\begin{bmatrix}y-y^{\prime}\\
z-z^{\prime}\end{bmatrix}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\langle\begin{bmatrix}\nabla_{y}F(y,z)^{T}\theta-\nabla_{y}F(y^{\prime},z^{\prime})^{T}\theta\\
\nabla_{z}F(y,z)^{T}\theta-\nabla_{z}F(y^{\prime},z^{\prime})^{T}\theta\\
\end{bmatrix},\begin{bmatrix}y-y^{\prime}\\
z-z^{\prime}\end{bmatrix}\right\rangle+\left\langle\begin{bmatrix}\nabla_{y}F(y^{\prime},z^{\prime})^{T}\theta-\nabla_{y}F(y^{\prime},z^{\prime})^{T}\theta^{\prime}\\
\nabla_{z}F(y^{\prime},z^{\prime})^{T}\theta-\nabla_{z}F(y^{\prime},z^{\prime})^{T}\theta^{\prime}\end{bmatrix},\begin{bmatrix}y-y^{\prime}\\
z-z^{\prime}\end{bmatrix}\right\rangle

\displaystyle\geq-L_{\nabla F}\|\theta\|\cdot\|(y,z)-(y^{\prime},z^{\prime})\|^{2}-L_{F}\|\theta-\theta^{\prime}\|\cdot\|(y,z)-(y^{\prime},z^{\prime})\|

\displaystyle\geq-2L_{\nabla F}M_{\theta}\|(y,z)-(y^{\prime},z^{\prime})\|^{2}-\frac{1}{4\gamma}\|\theta-\theta^{\prime}\|^{2}-\gamma L_{F}^{2}\|(y,z)-(y^{\prime},z^{\prime})\|^{2}

together with the estimates

\left\langle F(y,z)-F(y^{\prime},z^{\prime}),\theta-\theta^{\prime}\right\rangle\geq-L_{F}\|\theta-\theta^{\prime}\|\cdot\|(y,z)-(y^{\prime},z^{\prime})\|\geq-\frac{1}{4\gamma}\|\theta-\theta^{\prime}\|^{2}-\gamma L_{F}^{2}\|(y,z)-(y^{\prime},z^{\prime})\|^{2}.

Combining the above yields the desired inequality (
5.11
). Consequently, it follows from
[
38

that the function defined in (

) is convex with respect to
(y,z,\theta)
on
\mathbb{B}_{\varepsilon}(\bar{y},\bar{z})\times\mathbb{B}_{2M_{\theta}}
.

By taking into account the equality

\frac{1}{2\gamma}\|\theta-z\|^{2}+\frac{1}{2\gamma}\|(y,z)\|^{2}-\frac{1}{4\gamma}\|\theta\|^{2}=\frac{1}{2\gamma}\|y\|^{2}+\frac{1}{4\gamma}\left\|2z-\theta\right\|^{2},

we readily conclude that the function

\frac{1}{2\gamma}\|\theta-z\|^{2}+\frac{1}{2\gamma}\|(y,z)\|^{2}-\frac{1}{4\gamma}\|\theta\|^{2}

is convex with respect to
(y,z,\theta)
, Combining now these convexity results with the convexity of the graphical set
\mathrm{gph}(\Gamma)(y,z)
tells us that the function

\langle F(y,z),\theta\rangle+\frac{1}{2\gamma}\|\theta-z\|^{2}+\left(\gamma L_{F}^{2}+L_{\nabla F}M_{\theta}+\frac{1}{2\gamma}\right)\|(y,z)\|^{2}+\delta_{\mathrm{gph}(\Gamma)}(y,\theta)

is convex with respect to
(y,z,\theta)
on
\mathbb{B}_{\varepsilon}(\bar{y},\bar{z})\times\mathbb{B}_{2M_{\theta}}
. By using
[

\mu_{\gamma}(y,z)+\left(\gamma L_{F}^{2}+L_{\nabla F}M_{\theta}+\frac{1}{2\gamma}\right)\|(y,z)\|^{2}

(5.12)

is convex on
\mathbb{B}_{\varepsilon}(\bar{y},\bar{z})

at

It follows from the convexity of the function in (

\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle\partial\mu_{\gamma}(\bar{y},\bar{z})&=\partial\left(\mu_{\gamma}(\bar{y},\bar{z})+\left(\gamma L_{F}^{2}+\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}L_{\nabla F}M_{\theta}+\frac{1}{2\gamma}\right)\|(\bar{y},\bar{z})\|^{2}\right)\\
&-\displaystyle\left(\gamma L_{F}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}L_{\nabla F}M_{\theta}+\frac{1}{2\gamma}\right)\nabla\|(\bar{y},\bar{z})\|^{2}.\end{array}

(5.13)

Using (

[

\displaystyle\partial\left(\mu_{\gamma}(\bar{y},\bar{z})+\left(\gamma L_{F}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}L_{\nabla F}M_{\theta}+\frac{1}{2\gamma}\right)\|(\bar{y},\bar{z})\|^{2}\right)

(5.14)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\{(\xi_{1},\xi_{2})~\left|~\begin{bmatrix}\xi_{1}\\
\xi_{2}\\
0\end{bmatrix}\!\in\!\begin{bmatrix}\nabla_{y}F(\bar{y},\bar{z})^{T}\theta\\

\displaystyle\{\theta~|~0=F(\bar{y},\bar{z})+(\theta-\bar{z})/\gamma+\xi_{3},(\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\xi_{3})\in\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{F}}(\bar{y},\bar{z},\theta)\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\{\theta~|~0=F(\bar{y},\bar{z})+(\theta-\bar{z})/\gamma+\mathcal{N}_{\Gamma(y)}(\theta)\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\{\theta_{\gamma}^{*}(\bar{y},\bar{z})\}.

Therefore, combining the normal cone representation in (

(\bar{y},\theta_{\gamma}^{*}(\bar{y},\bar{z}))
with (

\displaystyle\partial

\displaystyle\mu_{\gamma}(\bar{y},\bar{z})=

\displaystyle\left\{\left.\begin{bmatrix}\nabla_{y}F(\bar{y},\bar{z})^{T}\bar{\theta}^{*}\\
\nabla_{z}F(\bar{y},\bar{z})^{T}\bar{\theta}^{*}\end{bmatrix}+\begin{bmatrix}\sum_{i=1}^{s}\nu_{i}\nabla_{y}\zeta_{i}(\bar{y},\bar{\theta}^{*})\\
(\bar{z}-\bar{\theta}^{*})/\gamma\end{bmatrix}~\right|~\begin{array}[]{c}\nu\in\mathbb{R}^{s},\nu\geq 0,\sum_{i=1}^{s}\nu_{i}\zeta_{i}(\bar{y},\bar{\theta}^{*})=0\\
F(\bar{y},\bar{z})+(\bar{\theta}^{*}-\bar{z})/{\gamma}+\sum_{i=1}^{s}\nu_{i}\nabla_{z}\zeta_{i}(\bar{y},\bar{\theta}^{*})=0\end{array}\right\},

where

for the notational simplicity. This completes the proof of the proposition.

Proposition 5.5

Let
(\bar{y},\bar{z})
satisfy
\bar{y}\in Y
and
\bar{z}\in\Gamma(\bar{y})
. If the partial Jacobian matrix
\nabla_{z}F(\bar{y},\bar{z})
is positive-definite, then ENNAMCQ holds at
(\bar{y},\bar{z})
for the
\epsilon
-relaxation problem (
5.15
) whenever
\epsilon>0
.

Proof
.
Denoting
\bar{\theta}^{*}=\theta_{\gamma}^{*}(\bar{x},\bar{y})
as above, suppose on the contrary that ENNAMCQ fails at
(\bar{y},\bar{z})
. Then we have
\langle F(\bar{y},\bar{z}),\bar{z}\rangle-\mu_{\gamma}(\bar{y},\bar{z})\geq\epsilon

0\in\begin{bmatrix}\nabla_{y}F(\bar{y},\bar{z})^{T}\bar{z}\\

(5.17)

Applications to Continuous Network Design with Numerical Experiments

This section tests the numerical performance of Algorithms
1
(CDP-ESQM) in the case of VI-constrained difference programming. We apply this algorithm to the
continuous network design problem
(CNDP) in
[
1
]
, which is a model of practical value. The effectiveness of the new algorithm is demonstrated by numerical experiments by comparing its performance with the existing benchmarks for CNDP provided in
[
42
]
. We also compare our algorithm with those developed for mathematical programs with complementarity constraints (MPCCs) to evaluate their computational efficiency. All the experiments were conducted in MATLAB on a laptop with Intel i7-1260 CPU (2.10 GHz) and 32 GB of RAM. Subproblems arising in the iterative process were solved by using MATLAB’s built-in optimization toolboxes; namely,
quadprog
and
fmincon
, with the interior-point solver.

Following
[
1
,
27
]
, the CNDP model can be mathematically formulated as:

\displaystyle\min\limits_{y\geq 0,\,v\in\Omega}

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{a\in\mathcal{A}}t_{a}(y_{a},v_{a})v_{a}+G_{a}(y_{a})

(6.1)

\displaystyle\text{subject to }

\displaystyle v\in\mathcal{S}(y):=\left\{v\in\Omega\,\middle|\,\langle t(y,v),v^{\prime}-v\rangle\geq 0,\ \forall v^{\prime}\in\Omega\right\},

where
\mathcal{A}
is the set of directed links,

y=(y_{a})_{a\in\mathcal{A}}
represents the vector of capacity expansion variables,
and
v=(v_{a})_{a\in\mathcal{A}}
denotes the vector of link flow variables.
The travel time cost function is denoted by
t_{a}(y_{a},v_{a})
,
and
G_{a}(y_{a})
represents the cost function associated with capacity expansion
for each link
a\in\mathcal{A}
. Moreover, the set
\Omega
in (
6.1
) is defined by

\Omega:=\left\{v^{\prime}=\Delta h\,\middle|\,\Lambda h=r,\,h\geq 0\right\},

(6.2)

where
r
is the vector of origin-destination demands.

We evaluate the performance of CDP-ESQM on the network described in
[
42
, Figure 3 and Table VI]
. Specifically, this network consists of 16 links implying that
y,v\in\mathbb{R}^{16}
. For each
a\in\mathcal{A}
, the cost functions are defined by

t_{a}(y_{a},v_{a}):=A_{a}+B_{a}\left(\frac{v_{a}}{K_{a}+y_{a}}\right)^{4},\quad G_{a}(y_{a})=D_{a}y_{a},

(6.3)

where the vectors
A,B,K,D
are taken from
[
42
, Table VI]
. The matrices
\Delta
and
\Lambda
are obtained from
[
42
, Figure 3]
, where
\Delta
is a
16\times 16
binary matrix with elements 0 and 1 and
\Lambda
is given by

\Lambda:=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{1}_{8}&\mathbf{0}_{8}\\
\mathbf{0}_{8}&\mathbf{1}_{8}\end{bmatrix}^{T}.

(6.4)

For CDP-ESQM in the network setting of
[
42
, Figure 3 and Table VI]
, the initial values were set as
y^{0}=\mathbf{0}_{16},v^{0}=\frac{1}{8}\Delta(r\otimes\mathbf{1}_{8})

p_{0}=10,\varrho_{p}=0.1,c_{p}=5,\gamma=1,\epsilon=1,\beta=0.5,\sigma=0.5
. To investigate the algorithmic performance under different second-order approximations, we conducted two sets of experiments for each demand scenario by employing distinct strategies for the matrix
G_{k}
. The first variant, CDP-ESQM with a scaled identity matrix, employs
G_{k}=\alpha\mathbf{I}
with
\alpha=10
. The second variant, CDP-ESQM with a regularized exact Hessian of the objective function, takes the form
G_{k}=\nabla^{2}\varphi_{0}(y^{k},v^{k})+\alpha\mathbf{I}

\|d^{k}\|\leq{10^{-4}}

\kappa_{k}=\left\langle t(y^{k},v^{k}),v^{k}\right\rangle-\min\limits_{v\in\Omega}\left\{\left\langle t(y^{k},v^{k}),v\right\rangle+\frac{1}{2\gamma}\left\|v-v^{k}\right\|^{2}\right\}\leq{\epsilon}.

(6.5)

To evaluate the effectiveness of CDP-ESQM, we compare its numerical results with several existing benchmarks in the CNDP framework including the Modular In-Core Nonlinear Optimization System (MINOS), the Hooke-Jeeves (H-J) method, the Equilibrium Decomposed Optimization (EDO), and the Iterative Optimization-Assignment (IOA). [

In this application, the objective is to find an optimal value of the upper-level variable

By design, the benchmark algorithms (MINOS, H-J, EDO, and IOA) generate feasible pairs

v

Concluding Remarks and Future Research

This paper designs and justifies a novel algorithm of the generalized SQP type to solve a new class of constrained difference programming. The developed algorithm is implemented for difference programming problems with variational inequality constraints and then is applied to practical continuous network design models. For the latter class of models, numerical experiments were performed, and the computation results were compared with those obtained by using other numerical techniques.

Among directions of our future research, we mention investigating
convergence rates
for CDP-ESQM. In particular, we plan to employ the exponential PLK conditions from
[
9
]

to determine exponent values ensuring the finite termination as well as linear and polynomial convergence of the designed algorithms. Another important area of the future research is considering problems of the constrained difference
programming of type (
1.1
) (and the associated problems with variational inequality constraints) in which the functions
g_{0}
and
g_{i}
,
i\in\mathcal{I}
are
not of class
{\cal C}^{1,1}
and even
not differentiable
on the set in question. Some developments in this direction have been recently done in
[
16
]
for the boosted difference of convex algorithm (BDCA)
[
5
]
in problems of DC programming.
