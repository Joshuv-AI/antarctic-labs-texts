Microbial community dynamics during Coffea arabica cv. Arara fermentation and their relationship with specialty coffee quality
Authors: Lívia Carneiro Fidélis Silva, Gisele Xavier Ribeiro Costa, Marcelo Antônio Duarte da Cruz, Luiza Manuela Alves Braga Cardoso, Arlley de Brito Magalhães Sousa, Renata Abadia Reis Rocha, Marcela Vieira Caixeta Machado, Laurence Rodrigues Amaral, Pedro Luiz Lima Bertarini, Libia Diniz Santos, Matheus de Souza Gomes
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Fermentation stands out as a promising strategy for the valorization of specialty coffees, directly impacting the sensory profile of the beverage. This study investigated the microbial dynamics during the self-induced anaerobic fermentation (SIAF) of Coffea arabica L. cv. Arara cherries, under different conditions of time (0, 24, 48, and 72 h), temperature (ambient and 27 °C), processing type (solid-state or submerged), and the presence or absence of a starter culture. Metataxonomic analyses (16 S rRNA and ITS1) were performed, revealing significant shifts in the diversity and structure of bacterial and fungal communities throughout the process. A predominance of the genera Lactobacillus and Kazachstania was observed, with fungal diversity being strongly affected, showing a marked reduction within the first 24 h. Decision tree-based predictive models revealed correlations between specific microorganisms and sensory attributes of the beverage, highlighting Leuconostoc, Lactobacillus, and Pichia as potential positive markers. These findings indicate that controlling fermentation conditions enables modulation of the microbiome, promoting desirable sensory profiles and contributing to the development of targeted starter cultures and the standardization of high-quality coffees.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-44864-z.

Introduction

Microbial communities associated with coffee cherries play a crucial role in shaping the sensory attributes of the beverage during fermentation, acting as biological agents capable of modulating aroma, flavor, and acidity. This interaction between microbiota and fruit has gained prominence in the scientific landscape as a strategic tool to produce specialty coffees with unique and highly desirable characteristics1–6. In the context of growing appreciation for specialty coffees, driven by discerning consumers and markets seeking differentiation, understanding the microbial mechanisms underlying the fermentation process has become essential. It is estimated that the coffee sector involves more than 25 million families worldwide and generates over 20 billion dollars annually, standing out as one of the most important agricultural value chains in tropical countries7,8.

In this context, fermentation stands out as a highly promising biological process capable of significantly enhancing the sensory profile and overall quality of the coffee beverage2,4,9–12. During fermentation, microorganisms naturally present on the coffee cherries interact in a dynamic and complex manner, promoting the degradation of sugars and pectins in the mucilage. These substrates are converted into volatile compounds that can diffuse into the beans and persist through roasting, directly influencing key sensory attributes of the beverage such as flavor, aroma, body, and acidity13–18.

Studies indicate that endogenous bacteria and yeasts are the main microbial agents involved in coffee fermentation, being responsible for the production of aromatic compounds that enrich the beverage’s sensory profile. Moreover, the initial composition of the microbiota, as well as its metabolic activities and interactions throughout the fermentation process, is strongly influenced by environmental factors, the coffee fruit variety, and post-harvest practices, including cultivation and processing methods3,19–24.

The anaerobic environment established during fermentation inhibits aerobic respiration and favors the predominance of fermentative metabolic pathways, directly influencing the composition and dynamics of the microbial community throughout the process. Moreover, variables such as temperature, duration, and specific fermentation conditions play a decisive role in shaping microbial diversity. In general, lower temperatures tend to promote a more stable microbiome, whereas higher temperatures induce more pronounced shifts in community structure21,25–27.

In addition to endogenous yeasts and bacteria, the introduction of microbial inoculants during fermentation has been explored as a strategy to enhance the sensory attributes of coffee. Shen et al.28 reported that fermentation conducted with Lactiplantibacillus plantarum significantly influenced the coffee’s chemical profile throughout the process. Similarly, Jimenez et al.29 observed that the inoculation of Candida parapsilosis in naturally processed coffees increased the sensory score of the beverage by 2.15 points compared to fermentation driven exclusively by native microorganisms, enhancing flavors such as “citrus,” “caramel,” “honey,” “chocolate,” and “nutty”. Furthermore, Janne Carvalho Ferreira et al.30 emphasized that the co-inoculation of Saccharomyces cerevisiae and Bacillus amyloliquefaciens in submerged-state fermentation significantly contributed to sensory enhancement, resulting in flavor notes such as “sweet,” “floral,” “nutty,” “cocoa,” and “citrus fruits,” with a sensory score of 86.75 points.

It is noteworthy that Saccharomyces cerevisiae is one of the most commonly used yeasts in coffee fermentation due to its commercial availability and proven effectiveness in enhancing the sensory characteristics of the beverage. Studies have shown that inoculation with S. cerevisiae can significantly increase the sensory score of coffee and promote the development of desirable aromas. For example, Mota et al. 31 observed that S. cerevisiae inoculation increased the sensory score by up to 5 points in coffees processed by both natural and pulped natural methods. Abreu et al.32 reported that fermentation with S. cerevisiae resulted in coffees classified as specialty grade, with enhanced sensory attributes. Moreover, Vaz et al.33 demonstrated that coffee fermentation with S. cerevisiae improved the beverage’s sensory quality even under prolonged fermentation conditions and elevated temperatures.

The composition and microbial activity during fermentation are key factors in the formation of volatile compounds and aromatic precursors, which directly influence the sensory profile of the beverage. In addition to modulating sensory attributes, microbial communities also affect the stability and safety of the final product, reinforcing the need for effective control and monitoring techniques throughout the entire fermentation process21,34–36. The characterization of these microbial populations enables the identification of strains with biotechnological potential for use as starter cultures, aiming at the standardization and optimization of coffee’s sensory quality3,37–39.

Despite significant advances reported in the literature, gaps remain in the understanding of microbial interactions during fermentation, particularly when considering different processing methods and controlled temperature conditions. Moreover, the impacts of these variables on microbial dynamics during coffee fermentation process have not yet been fully elucidated. In this context, the present study proposes a detailed analysis of the microbiota associated with coffee throughout the fermentation stages, conducted under both solid-state and submerged conditions, at controlled temperature (27 °C) and ambient temperature, with and without the addition of Saccharomyces cerevisiae as a starter culture. The objective is to identify the behavior of microbial communities, key changes over time, and correlate them with the sensory parameters of the beverage. The results of this study are expected to advance the understanding of the influence of fermentative microbiota on coffee quality by identifying microbial profiles favorable to the production of beverages with superior sensory attributes. Furthermore, the findings may guide the development and application of microbial inoculants and support innovative practices aimed at establishing more controlled fermentation processes.

Materials and methods

Experimental design

The experiments were conducted in the state of Minas Gerais, Brazil, at Chuá Farm, located at an altitude of 1050 meters, at coordinates 18° 35′ 15′′ latitude and 46° 25′ 35′′ longitude. Coffea arabica L. cv. Arara cherries were used, which were washed immediately after harvesting using potable water sourced from an artesian well at the collaborating farm, following its standard post-harvest practices, prior to the fermentation process. The washed coffee were subjected to different configurations of self-induced anaerobic fermentation (SIAF), including: solid-state (T1-T6 and T13-T18) and submerged (T7-T12 and T19-T24) fermentation; with (T4-T6, T10-T12, T16-T18, T22-T24) or without (T1-T3, T7-T9, T13-T15, T19-T21) temperature control (27 °C or ambient temperature); and with (T13-T24), or without (T1-TT12) starter culture (RX60 - LaFFORT). The evaluated fermentation times were 24 (T1, T4, T7, T10, T13, T19 and T22), 48 (T2, T5, T8, T11, T14, T17, T20 and T23), and 72 (T3, T6, T9, T12, T15, T18, T21 and T24) hours, totaling 24 experimental treatments, in addition to a control (T25) (without self-induced fermentation). The experimental conditions are described in Table 1 and illustrated in Fig. 1.

Fermentations

Fermentations were carried out in high-density polyethylene bioreactors with a total capacity of 200 L, each containing approximately 124 kg of coffee cherries. The bioreactor lids were equipped with airlock valves, allowing CO₂ release while preventing oxygen entry, thus maintaining anaerobic conditions. In the submerged SIAF treatments (T7-T12 and T19-T24), 60 L of water (30% v/v) was added, whereas in the starter culture treatments (T13-T24), 10% (v/v) of Saccharomyces cerevisiae RX60 (LaFFORT) inoculum was used at a concentration of 10⁹ cells/mL, following the manufacturer’s recommendations. Bioreactors with temperature control (T4-T6, T10-T12, T16-T18, T22-T24) were placed in a closed chamber maintained at a constant temperature of 27 °C, a condition selected based on previous literature reporting favorable outcomes6,12. The remaining bioreactors were kept in a shaded area at ambient temperature (T1-T3, T7-T9, T13-T15, T19-T21) (Table 1). Fermentations were conducted over 24, 48, and 72 h using sacrificial bioreactors to avoid system disturbance due to sampling. Samples of 150 g of coffee cherries were collected in triplicate at time zero and every 24 h, transferred to sterile plastic bags, and stored under refrigeration until microbiological analysis. As a control (T25), washed Arara coffee cherries were directly transferred to the drying patio. After the fermentation period, the cherries were washed to finalize the process, and sun-dried on asphalt patios until reaching a moisture content between 11 and 12%. Post-fermentation washing was performed using the same potable water source as the pre-fermentation, supplied by the collaborating farm and sourced from an artesian well. Subsequently, they were stored at ambient temperature and hulled after 30 days.

Sample processing and DNA extraction

For DNA extraction, coffee cherries from both control and fermented samples were previously homogenized. From each sample, five cherries were randomly selected using sterile forceps to ensure biological representativeness. The cherries were then cut into multiple pieces with sterile blades to increase surface area exposure and facilitate subsequent homogenization. The fragments were macerated in liquid nitrogen using a previously sterilized mortar and pestle until a fine, homogeneous powder was obtained (adapted from Veloso et al., 2020)23. From the macerated material, 0.25 g aliquots were used for metagenomic DNA extraction with the commercial DNeasy PowerSoil Pro Kit (Qiagen®), following the manufacturer’s instructions. As a protocol adaptation, the mechanical agitation time during the lysis step was adjusted to 2.5 min to ensure efficient matrix disruption and increase DNA yield. The extracted DNA was quantified using a NanoDrop Lite Plus spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific®), and its integrity was assessed by 0.8% agarose gel electrophoresis and visualized under UV light. Samples that exhibited adequate quality and concentration were submitted for next-generation sequencing (NGS), performed by a specialized company.

DNA sequencing and metataxonomic data analysis

The extracted DNA was subjected to amplicon sequencing targeting the variable V3/V4 regions of the 16S rRNA gene (for bacteria), using primers 338F (5′-CCTAYGGGRBGCASCAG-3′) and 806R (3′-GGACTACNNGGGTATCTAAT-5′), and the ITS1 region (for fungi), using primers ITS5 (5′-GGAAGTAAAAGTCGTAACAAGG-3′) and ITS2 (3′-GCTGCGTTCTTCATCGATGC-5′). Sequencing was performed on the Illumina NovaSeq platform with paired-end reads (250 bp). Raw sequences were processed in R using the DADA2 pipeline for microbial community characterization. A quality filter was initially applied with the criteria of read length ≥ 20 bp and Phred quality score > 20 (Q > 20). As all reads met the quality criteria, truncation was not required.

High-quality sequences were used for the inference of amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) as described by Callahan et al. (2016)40. Taxonomic classification of ASVs was performed using the Silva SSU v.138 database (http://www.arb-silva.de) for bacteria and the Unite 9.0 database41 for fungi. ASVs classified as singletons were excluded from taxonomic assignment, and the data were converted to relative abundance. The structure of bacterial and fungal communities was assessed using alpha diversity indices (Shannon and Chao1) and beta diversity (principal coordinates analysis – PCoA – based on the Bray-Curtis dissimilarity matrix). Graphical representations, including bar plots, heatmaps, and Venn diagrams, were generated in the R environment using the microbiome package (https://microbiome.g.ithub.io), mctoolsr (https://github.com/leffj/mctoolsr), and MicrobiotaProcess.

Sensory analysis

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Federal University of Uberlândia (CAAE: 73864723.0.0000.5152). Control and fermented coffee samples were prepared according to the methodology of the Specialty Coffee Association (SCA). The beans (100 g per sample) were subjected to a standardized medium roast using an Atilla GOLDEN PLUS 10 roaster, following a predefined roast curve to ensure comparability among samples. After roasting, the beans were ground using an electric grinder. The beverage was prepared at a ratio of 8.25 g of coffee to 150 mL of water. A panel of five certified Q-Graders evaluated the samples. The analysis followed SCA protocols, evaluating ten sensory attributes: aroma, sweetness, overall assessment, flavor, body, uniformity, balance, cleanliness, acidity, and finish42. Samples that scored above 80 points were classified as specialty coffees42,43.

Statistical analysis

To evaluate dissimilarity between samples (control and fermented), principal coordinates analysis (PCoA) was performed based on the Bray-Curtis dissimilarity matrix, with data ordinated in two dimensions. Group separation was tested using permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA), executed in the R environment (version 4.0) (R Core Team, 2021) with the support of the Vegan44, McToolsR, and Phyloseq45 packages. Mean comparisons of diversity indices were conducted in MiniTab® 17.1.0 (Minitab, Inc., Quality Plaza, 1829 Pine Hall Road, State College, Pennsylvania 16801, USA) using ANOVA followed by Tukey’s test. Differences were considered statistically significant for p-values less than or equal to 0.05. Additionally, decision trees were constructed to identify the most relevant predictive attributes for subgroup classification. Modeling was performed using MATLAB software (version 2023a), considering the following evaluation criteria: (i) the experimental design; (ii) the sensory results; and (iii) the relative abundances of bacterial and fungal genera identified in the samples. To validate model performance, the full-training cross-validation (FTCV) method was applied, in which all instances of the dataset are used simultaneously for both training and testing. The percentage error obtained by the model was recorded as a measure of accuracy.

Results

Alpha and beta diversity

Alpha diversity reflects within-sample diversity, whereas beta diversity captures differences in microbial community composition between samples46. Alpha diversity was assessed using the Shannon and Richness (Observed ASVs) indices. Beta diversity was evaluated by principal coordinates analysis (PCoA), and the ordination structure was visualized using pairwise projections of the first three principal coordinates to improve the representation of variance across the dataset.

The evaluation of microbial diversity indices revealed significant effects of the different experimental conditions on the bacterial and fungal communities associated with the fermentation process. Species richness (Richness) and Shannon diversity indices were analyzed considering the following factors: fermentation time (control, 24, 48 and 72 h), processing type (solid-state or submerged), temperature control (ambient or 27 °C), and presence or absence of a starter culture (Fig. 2).

Regarding the fermentation time, a significant reduction in Shannon diversity indices for both bacteria and fungi was observed within the first 24 h of the process (p ≤ 0.05), with values of 2.18 for bacteria and 1.5 for fungi, compared to the control (2.88 for bacteria and 2.76 for fungi). No significant variation in bacterial richness was detected between 24 h, 48 h, and 72 h (p > 0.05), except for an increase from the control (55.0) to 24 h (96.25) (p ≤ 0.05). In contrast, fungal richness showed a significant decrease within the first 24 h and remained low up to 72 h (from 156.3 in the control to 48.21 after 24 h of fermentation) (Fig. 2, panels a-d).

In the comparison between fermentation process types (solid-state and submerged), the control condition showed the highest Shannon diversity indices for both bacteria (2.88) and fungi (2.76) (p ≤ 0.05). No significant differences in bacterial richness were observed between the solid-state and submerged treatments (p > 0.05), although the solid-state process exhibited higher richness than the control (91.33 vs. 55.0; p ≤ 0.05). For fungi, both fermentation treatments showed lower richness compared to the control (48.2 vs. 156.3; p ≤ 0.05) (Fig. 2, panels e-h).

Regarding the temperature variable, both treatments, with temperature control and under ambient conditions, showed reduced Shannon diversity indices compared to the control, for both bacteria (2.18 vs. 2.88) and fungi (1.5 vs. 2.76) (p ≤ 0.05), with no significant difference between them (p > 0.05). In terms of bacterial richness, the ambient temperature treatment was higher (95.75 vs. 55.0; p ≤ 0.05) and similar to the temperature-controlled treatment (p ≤ 0.05). For fungi, both treatments exhibited significantly lower richness (47.0) compared to the control (156.3) (p ≤ 0.05) (Fig. 2, panels i-l).

Finally, both fermentation with starter culture inoculation and spontaneous fermentation resulted in a significant reduction in microbial diversity compared to the control, for both bacteria (2.18 vs. 2.88) and fungi (1.50 vs. 2.76) (p ≤ 0.05). Bacterial richness was maintained in spontaneous fermentation and increased with the use of a starter culture (88.25 vs. 55.0; p ≤ 0.05). However, fungal richness was reduced in both fermentation treatments, with or without microbial inoculation (47.5 vs. 156.3; p ≤ 0.05) (Fig. 2, panels m-p).

Under all evaluated conditions, the control sample exhibited the highest microbial diversity values (Shannon index), while fermentation treatments, regardless of the method used, temperature control, or the presence of starter culture, resulted in a significant reduction in microbial diversity (Shannon index). Regarding richness, an increase was observed in bacterial treatments compared to the control, whereas fungal richness was reduced. Moreover, in the control condition, fungal richness exceeded bacterial richness. Overall, the data indicate that fungi are more sensitive to changes induced by fermentation conditions, exhibiting more pronounced reductions in diversity indices compared to bacteria.

The structure of the microbial community was compared across treatments using principal coordinates analysis (PCoA), based on the Bray–Curtis dissimilarity matrix, for both bacteria and fungi. The resulting plots reveal variations in microbial composition as a function of fermentation time, processing type, presence or absence of temperature control, and addition of a starter culture. The variance explained by the first three principal coordinates was 44.5% (PC1), 18.4% (PC2) 7.9% (PC3) for bacteria, and 32.6%, 19.7% and 8.7% for fungi, respectively (Fig. 3).

The samples grouped by fermentation time showed a progressive separation pattern with increasing duration, for both bacterial and fungal communities. For bacteria, a shift was observed along the PC1 axis (44.5% of the variance), particularly between the control and the fermented time points (p ≤ 0.05), although no statistically significant differences were found among the fermentation times themselves (Fig. 3a). Fungal communities exhibited a similar pattern, with a more pronounced separation between the control and the fermentation time points (Fig. 3b).

The separation between samples subjected to different fermentation processes (solid-state and submerged) revealed statistically significant differences for both microbial communities (p ≤ 0.05), both between the two treatments and in comparison to the control. For bacteria, samples subjected to submerged fermentation formed a distinct cluster, while those fermented under solid-state conditions remained closer to the control group. PC1 accounted for 44.5% of the total variation and was primarily responsible for clearly distinguishing control samples from fermented ones (Fig. 3c). For fungi, a clear separation was also observed between the control and fermentation treatments, with PC1 explaining 32.6% of the variance, highlighting compositional changes in the fungal community induced by the processing method (Fig. 3d).

The analysis of treatments with or without temperature control revealed clustering patterns like those observed for different fermentation process types, although no statistically significant difference was found between them (p > 0.05). In the case of bacterial communities, a slight overlap was observed between the clusters corresponding to fermentations conducted at controlled and ambient temperatures, both of which were distinct from the control group (p ≤ 0.05) (Fig. 3e). Fungal communities, although displaying greater dispersion, also showed separation between control and fermented samples, suggesting compositional reorganization associated with fermentation temperature (p ≤ 0.05) (Fig. 3f).

The influence of the microbiota involved in the fermentation process, whether spontaneous or inoculated with a starter culture, also promoted noticeable changes in the composition of microbial communities. Well-defined clustering was observed between the treatments and the control (p ≤ 0.05), although no significant differences were detected between the two types of fermentation for either bacteria or fungi. The separation between control samples and the fermentations with starter culture was more evident along the PC1 axis, which accounted for 44.5% of the explained variance (Fig. 3g and h).

The beta diversity analyses conducted in this study revealed that the type of fermentation process (solid-state or submerged) was the main determining factor in modulating the structure of microbial communities associated with coffee fermentation. This observation is consistent with previous studies showing that the physical environment of fermentation significantly influences microbial composition and dynamics.

In the present study, although variables such as fermentation time, temperature control, and the use of starter cultures induced detectable changes in microbial composition, these differences were not statistically significant among the treatments, with distinctions being relevant only in comparison to the control group. These results are consistent with previous findings indicating that the type of fermentation process exerts a more pronounced influence on the structure of microbial communities than other operational variables.

Taxonomic profile

From the 28 samples analyzed, a total of 5,472,341 raw sequences were initially obtained for bacteria and 5,707,545 for fungi. After quality and length filtering, 85.5% of the bacterial sequences (4,680,792) and 92.5% of the fungal sequences (5,278,207) were retained, ensuring high quality for diversity and taxonomic classification analyses. The analysis identified 762 bacterial amplicon sequence variants (ASVs), distributed across 16 phyla, 89 families, and 158 genera. For fungi, 735 ASVs were identified, including 7 phyla, 88 families, and 153 genera, highlighting the broad microbial diversity associated with the fermentation process. A complete overview of bacterial and fungal taxonomic composition is provided in the Supplementary Material (Tables S1 and S2), where the relative abundance of ASVs grouped at the genus level is presented for all fermentation treatments and control (T1–T25, as defined in Table 1), allowing detailed inspection of community composition across experimental conditions.

The taxonomic characterization of the microbial communities associated with coffee fermentation was based on the relative abundance of the main bacterial and fungal genera identified across the different evaluated treatments (with relative abundance ≥ 0.5% for bacterial and ≥ 0.1% for fungal genera). The data were represented by a heatmap (Fig. 4), allowing visualization of the occurrence intensity of each taxon under the various experimental conditions. The color scale ranges from white (0%) to deep red (100%), indicating the relative proportion of each genus in relation to the total analyzed microbial community. In the heatmap representation, unclassified taxa at the genus level (labeled as NA) comprise the cumulative relative abundance of ASVs that could not be confidently assigned to a genus. These taxa were detected across conditions but occurred in higher abundance in control samples, accounting for 6.8% of the bacterial community and 36.2% of the fungal community. Their higher abundance in controls likely reflects native epiphytic and environmental microorganisms associated with freshly harvested coffee fruits, which tend to decrease during fermentation due to selective pressures such as pH reduction, substrate competition, and changes in oxygen availability.

For comparative purposes, samples were grouped according to the following factors: fermentation time, processing type (solid-state or submerged), presence or absence of temperature control, and addition or not of a starter culture. The taxonomic profile of microbial communities during coffee fermentation showed significant variations as a function of fermentation time, processing type, temperature, and the use of starter cultures, as illustrated in Fig. 4. As fermentation progressed, an increase in the relative abundance of the genus Lactobacillus was observed, reaching its peak at 72 h, where it represented 78.8% of the bacterial community. Similarly, the genus Weissella, initially present at low abundance in control, showed an increase throughout fermentation, peaking at 24 h. These changes were accompanied by a sharp decline in the presence of Methylobacterium, which was predominant at the beginning of fermentation (64.3%) but was progressively suppressed over time (Fig. 4a). Among fungi, the genus Kazachstania stood out as predominant from 24 h onward, reaching its highest abundance at 72 h, accounting for 61.8% of the total fungal community (Fig. 4b).

Regarding the type of fermentation process, the genus Lactobacillus was the most abundant (80.9%) in the submerged fermentation treatment. In contrast, in the solid-state process, the relative abundance of Lactobacillus was lower (67.4%), with a notable contribution from the genus Weissella, which accounted for 21.9% of the community (Fig. 4c). For the fungal community, an inverse pattern was observed: solid-state fermentation favored the dominance of Kazachstania, which reached 92.9% relative abundance, whereas in submerged fermentation this value was slightly lower at 76.8% (Fig. 4d).

Fermentation conducted under controlled temperature (27 °C) led to a 10.8% increase in the relative abundance of Lactobacillus compared to the condition without temperature control, suggesting that thermal regulation favored the growth of this genus. In contrast, genera such as Enterobacter, Leuconostoc, Pediococcus, and Weissella showed higher abundance in fermentations carried out without temperature control (Fig. 4e). Among fungi, Kazachstania remained the predominant genus under controlled temperature, reaching 90.1% of the community. However, its abundance was reduced by 11.7% when fermentation was conducted at ambient temperature (Fig. 4f). Detailed temperature monitoring data obtained throughout the fermentation process for all treatments have been previously reported by Costa et al.12.

The addition of a starter culture resulted in an increase in the abundance of Lactobacillus (2.7%) and a reduction in Weissella (4%) (Fig. 4g). Regarding fungi, the presence of a starter culture during fermentation favored the development of Cladosporium and Fusarium, whose abundances were lower in spontaneous fermentations. The yeast Saccharomyces was detected exclusively in the inoculated samples, indicating its natural absence in coffee cherries. Although inoculation promoted the emergence of new fungal genera, Kazachstania, the dominant fungus identified, was more abundant in spontaneous fermentation (85.4%) than in inoculated treatment (83.6%) (Fig. 4h).

Overall, Lactobacillus and Kazachstania were the main microbial genera associated with fermentation, exhibiting profiles strongly modulated by the evaluated experimental parameters. The presence of other bacterial genera such as Leuconostoc, Pediococcus, and Weissella, and fungal genera such as Cladosporium, Colletotrichum, Fusarium, Meyerozyma, and Saccharomyces, suggests potential secondary roles in shaping the fermentative profile of coffee.

For the bacterial community, 24 ASVs were identified as shared across all fermentation times, suggesting the existence of a core microbiota. However, the partial overlap among different time points indicates significant shifts in microbial composition throughout the process. A progressive microbial succession was observed, with 50 unique ASVs in the control, 141 at 24 h, 114 at 48 h, and 149 at 72 h (Fig. 5a). In contrast, the fungal community exhibited the opposite behavior: the highest number of unique ASVs (diversity) was recorded in the control (234), followed by a sharp reduction as early as 24 h, a pattern that persisted until the end of fermentation. During the process, 15 ASVs were shared between 24 h and 48 h, 67 were common to 24 h, 48 h, and 72 h, and 14 were shared only between 48 h and 72 h (Fig. 5b). These patterns reinforce the impact of fermentation time on microbiota restructuring and highlight the role of ecological selection in the successional dynamics of microbial communities in fermentative systems.

Among the different fermentation processes, a substantial sharing of bacterial and fungal ASVs was observed between the submerged and solid-state processes, with 85 and 93 shared ASVs, respectively. In contrast, the overlap between the control and the fermentation treatments was significantly lower (Fig. 5c and d). For the bacterial community, the submerged process exhibited the highest number of unique ASVs (239), indicating a more specific and diverse bacterial profile (Fig. 5c). However, for the fungal community, exclusive diversity was reduced in both fermentation treatments compared to the control, suggesting that fermentation imposes a selective pressure that restricts fungal diversity (Fig. 5d).

Fermentation conducted without temperature control resulted in the highest number of unique bacterial ASVs (260), although there was considerable overlap with fermentation under controlled temperature (110 shared ASVs). Controlled-temperature fermentation also exhibited a substantial number of exclusive ASVs (155). Additionally, 35 bacterial ASVs were common to both conditions, indicating a microbial core capable of adapting to different temperature regimes (Fig. 5e). This also suggests that, although temperature variation selects for specific groups, a portion of the initial microbiota is able to adapt to the conditions of the fermentation processes. The presence of 50 ASVs unique to the control highlights microbial growth during fermentation, marked by a substantial emergence of new ASVs, reinforcing the notion that the fermentative process promotes the rise of novel taxa. For the fungal community, the temperature-controlled condition exhibited greater exclusive diversity (117 ASVs), along with a high number of shared units with the uncontrolled condition (108), indicating relative fungal stability between thermal conditions (Fig. 5f).

The use of a starter culture promoted a bacterial profile with a higher number of unique groups compared to spontaneous fermentation (226 vs. 182). Both conditions shared 117 ASVs, suggesting that although the use of a starter modulates the microbial community, it does not fully suppress the native microbiota. It was also observed that both fermentation types induced the growth of various bacterial groups compared to the control, which exhibited only 50 unique ASVs (Fig. 5g). In the case of fungi, a high level of sharing was also observed (109 ASVs), with greater exclusive diversity found in spontaneous fermentation, which may reflect the persistence of endogenous populations in response to exogenous inoculation (Fig. 5h).

Association between microbial composition and sensory quality of the beverage

To identify potential microbial biomarkers associated with the sensory quality of fermented coffee, predictive models based on decision trees were constructed. These models used the relative abundance data of bacterial and fungal genera from the different experimental treatments as input variables, correlating them with the sensory scores assigned during beverage evaluation. Samples were categorized into two groups according to their sensory scores: high quality (score ≥ 83.75 for bacterial analysis; ≥ 83.65 for fungal analysis) and low quality (score below the respective thresholds). These thresholds corresponded to the median sensory scores of the experimental datasets and were adopted to ensure an equal number of observations in each group, thereby minimizing class imbalance and improving the robustness of the predictive modeling (Fig. 6).

The predictive model generated from the bacterial data showed high accuracy (96.43%) and identified the genus Leuconostoc as the main discriminator between samples of high and low sensory quality. Samples with a relative abundance of Leuconostoc greater than 2.6% were predominantly associated with the production of high-quality beverages. Within this group, the presence of the genus Kluyvera at levels above 0.007% was consistently associated with lower-quality beverages. In the absence of Kluyvera, the decisive variable became the uncultured bacterial taxon identified as 1174-901-12: values below 0.01% of this taxon were associated with higher scores, whereas values above this threshold indicated a negative impact on sensory quality.

On the other hand, when the abundance of Leuconostoc was below 2.76%, Lactobacillus became the decisive variable. Samples in which Lactobacillus represented less than 88.73% of the bacterial community were entirely associated with low sensory quality. However, those with abundance equal to or greater than this threshold showed a 67% association with high-quality beverages, suggesting that Lactobacillus dominance may be related to favorable fermentative profiles.

For the fungal data, the model showed an accuracy of 85.19%, identifying Pichia as the most discriminating variable. Samples with relative abundance of Pichia below 0.007% were broadly associated with lower beverage quality. Within this group, it was observed that unclassified taxa (NA) with abundances below 1% were exclusively associated with low scores; in contrast, values above this threshold resulted in 55% of samples achieving high sensory scores, suggesting a potential positive role of yet undescribed fungal groups.

When Pichia was present at levels equal to or greater than 0.007%, an association with high sensory quality was observed, provided that the genus Acremonium was present at a relative abundance below 0.01%. Its presence above this threshold was consistently associated with the production of lower-quality beverages.

Discussion

Coffee fermentation is a multifactorial biological process in which various environmental and operational variables shape the composition and dynamics of microbial communities, directly impacting the sensory attributes of the beverage18,50,51. In this study, it was demonstrated that fermentation time, processing type (solid-state or submerged), temperature control, and the use of a starter culture significantly influenced both the diversity and structure of the microbial community, as well as the sensory outcomes. Based on alpha and beta diversity analyses, taxonomic and sensory profiles, and predictive models generated through machine learning, it was possible to identify microbial markers associated with beverage quality and to elucidate the effects of experimental conditions on microbial succession throughout the fermentation process.

The predominance of fungal richness observed in coffee cherries, compared to bacterial richness, may be attributed to the physicochemical characteristics of the fruit, such as high sugar content, slightly acidic pH, and the presence of bioactive compounds like caffeine and chlorogenic acids12,52,53. These factors favor the development of yeasts, which, in turn, can exert inhibitory effects on various bacterial genera25,49. Similar results were reported by Veloso et al. (2023)24 and Gomes et al. (2024)5, who, when investigating the microbiomes of C. arabica and C. canephora across different floristic domains in Brazil, observed greater fungal diversity in C. arabica cherries, corroborating fungal dominance as a recurring feature of this species.

The significant reduction in Shannon diversity indices for both bacteria and fungi following the onset of fermentation confirms the selective effect of the anaerobic environment on the microbial community. This pattern is characteristic of fermentative processes, in which restrictive conditions—such as low oxygen availability, accumulation of metabolites (acids, ethanol, CO₂), and pH shifts—promote the selection of specialized microbial groups adapted to such environments36,39. The more pronounced impact on fungi within the first 24 h suggests greater sensitivity of these organisms to the physicochemical changes in the medium, as reflected by the rapid decline in fungal diversity and richness.

These observations are consistent with previous studies demonstrating that microbial diversity, both bacterial and fungal, tends to decline throughout the fermentation process. For example, in a study on the fermentation of Shenqu, a traditional Chinese product, it was observed that bacterial and fungal diversity decreased over fermentation time, with the identification of predominant strains responsible for driving the fermentative process47. Moreover, other researches indicate that fungal diversity may be more sensitive to environmental variations during fermentation. In a study on wine fermentation, fungal diversity was shown to decrease over the course of the process, while bacterial diversity remained relatively stable48. These findings highlight the importance of monitoring and controlling fermentation conditions to preserve the desired microbial diversity, especially fungal communities, which may be more susceptible to change throughout the process.

The observed increase in bacterial richness, even under selective fermentation conditions, may be attributed to the growth and ecological dominance of acid-tolerant, fermentative lactic acid bacteria such as Lactobacillus and Weissella. These taxa are commonly enriched in anaerobic fermentations where acidification occurs, as they are well adapted to low pH environments and can proliferate when conditions (e.g., controlled temperature and submerged fermentation) favor lactic acid production and carbohydrate metabolism. Such dynamics have been reported in various fermented systems, with Lactobacillus and Weissella increasing in relative abundance as fermentation progresses and acidification intensifies54,55. Lactobacillus, in particular, is widely recognized for its ability to acidify the environment and reshape the microbiota structure through the production of bioactive metabolites such as lactic acid, acetate, diacetyl, and volatile compounds with direct impact on the sensory profile2,28.

Complementing the alpha diversity analysis, the application of principal coordinates analysis (PCoA) based on the Bray–Curtis dissimilarity matrix revealed significant structural shifts among treatments, highlighting differences in beta diversity. The type of fermentation process (solid-state or submerged) was the main explanatory variable for sample separation, for both bacterial and fungal communities, with statistical significance confirmed by the PERMANOVA test (p ≤ 0.05). This result reinforces the key influence of the system’s physicochemical dynamics on microbial structuring. In submerged fermentation, the more homogeneous environment and lower surface tension tend to favor the growth of dominant taxa such as Lactobacillus, whereas in solid-state fermentation, greater heterogeneity, the presence of oxygen gradients, and the formation of microenvironments contribute to increased compositional diversity3,17. Zhang et al. (2019)25 observed that different processing methods impacted the dynamics of microbial communities and metabolomic profiles during coffee fermentation, resulting in variations in the beverage’s sensory quality. Furthermore, De Bruyn et al. (2017)49 emphasized that fermentation duration and the processing type affect microbial communities and, consequently, the final quality of the coffee.

Fermentation time played a relevant role in modulating microbial structure, as evidenced by the progressive separation along the primary axis (PC1) in the principal coordinates analysis (PCoA), suggesting an ecological succession dynamic throughout the process. Although not all comparisons between fermentation time showed statistical significance, the clear distinction between the control and the fermented samples supports the hypothesis that the native microbiota undergoes substantial restructuring within the first 24 h. This transition was particularly notable in the fungal community, with a reduction in diversity and the prevalence of fermentative yeasts adapted to the anaerobic environment.

Regarding the temperature variable, an overlap was observed between the groups conducted at ambient temperature and those under thermal control (27 °C), with no statistically significant difference. However, the displacement along the main axes in the PCoA indicates that temperature acts as a secondary modulator of microbial composition, particularly for fungi, which showed greater dispersion in clustering. This behavior may be related to the higher thermal sensitivity of certain fungal groups and the influence of temperature on growth rate and interspecies competition56–58.

The taxonomic profile analysis revealed that throughout fermentation, the coffee microbial community was complex, dynamic, and highly responsive to the different experimental parameters, demonstrating a strong capacity for adaptation to the environmental and technological conditions of the fermentation process. The predominance of the genera Lactobacillus among bacteria and Kazachstania among fungi throughout the fermentation corroborates previous studies that highlight these microorganisms as key players in coffee fermentation3,17,59,60.

Cruz-O’Byrne et al. (2023)61 also identified Kazachstania as a recurrent fungal genus in coffee fermentations, reporting its continuous dominance throughout the process. The results of the present study support this finding, showing that Kazachstania became the predominant fungal genus from the first 24 h of fermentation and remained prevalent until the end of the process.

The dominance of Lactobacillus and Kazachstania highlights the biotechnological potential of these microorganisms in guiding and standardizing fermentation processes, as well as improving the sensory quality of the beverage, due to their high adaptability and competitiveness, particularly under controlled temperature conditions. Temperature has been reported as an important factor influencing microbial succession during coffee fermentation by modulating growth kinetics, metabolic activity, and microbial interactions. In this context, Entringer et al. (2024)58 demonstrated that variations in fermentation temperature significantly affected the structure and dynamics of microbial communities during coffee fermentation, promoting shifts in both bacterial and fungal populations, even when overall diversity indices were not markedly altered.

The authors reported that temperature influenced the relative abundance and dispersion of specific microbial taxa, particularly fermentative and acid-tolerant groups, reinforcing its role as a secondary modulator of microbial composition. Corroborating these findings, Silva et al. (2023)59 observed the predominance of Lactobacillus sp. during coffee fermentation, with the highest abundance recorded at elevated temperatures (38 °C) and after 72 h of fermentation. They also found that the relative abundance of Lactobacillus at 38 °C was positively correlated with the sensory scores of the beverage. These results suggest that increasing both temperature and fermentation time may favor the metabolism of Lactobacillus spp., promoting positive impacts on coffee sensory quality.

The dominance of Kazachstania during the intermediate and final stages of fermentation suggests its central role in driving the fermentative process, possibly due to its ability to produce volatile metabolites relevant to the aromatic profile of coffee51,62,63. Previous studies have shown that members of this genus are capable of synthesizing compounds such as isoamyl alcohol, as well as propionic, isobutyric, and hexanoic acids, metabolites frequently associated with fruity, floral, sweet, and caramel-like attributes in coffee beverages64–68.

Thus, the higher relative abundance of Lactobacillus and Kazachstania under controlled temperature conditions suggests that thermal control may be an effective strategy to guide fermentation by promoting the development of microorganisms with technological potential, such as producers of organic acids and aromatic esters that contribute positively to the beverage’s sensory profile. In contrast, fermentation conducted at ambient temperature promoted greater microbial diversity, including a broader range of genera, which may lead to increased sensory variability12. Such variability can be either desirable or undesirable, depending on specific goals related to standardization or innovation in the development of specialty coffees.

Experiments conducted under ambient temperature conditions during coffee fermentation showed pronounced day–night thermal variability, with temperature differences of up to approximately 10 °C12. Under these conditions, submerged fermentations exhibited larger temperature oscillations and lower average temperatures (~ 20 °C), reflecting the higher thermal conductivity of water and the resulting enhanced heat exchange with the environment. In contrast, solid-state fermentations maintained higher and more stable temperatures, with mean values around 24 °C. Fermentations conducted under controlled conditions were maintained at a constant temperature of 27 °C, providing a stable thermal environment. Despite these contrasting thermal regimes, overall microbial community structure showed limited divergence among temperature treatments, suggesting that the coffee-associated microbiota is relatively resilient to temperature variability. Accordingly, temperature acts mainly as a modulating factor influencing the relative abundance of specific genera rather than reshaping the community as a whole.

During fermentation, suppression of genera such as Methylobacterium, Pantoea, and Cladosporium, typically associated with plant surfaces and aerobic environments, was observed. This suppression indicates that factors such as medium acidification, ethanol accumulation, oxygen limitation, and substrate depletion act as selective pressures that shape microbial composition69. Temporal analysis revealed that the early stages of fermentation (first 24 h) exhibit greater microbial diversity, while the 48 h and 72 h time points showed more specialized communities, characterized by the dominance of a few genera adapted to the restrictive environmental conditions imposed by the progression of the fermentative process.

The comparison between different fermentation processes revealed significant impacts on the microbial structure associated with coffee fermentation. Submerged fermentation favored the dominance of the genus Lactobacillus, whereas the solid-state process promoted greater microbial diversity, with notable representation of Weissella and Kazachstania. These results suggest that water addition (submerged fermentation) increases water activity and may initially provide dissolved oxygen, but oxygen can be rapidly depleted by microbial respiration and limited gas exchange, creating anaerobic micro-niches that favor the proliferation of lactic acid bacteria (LAB). In solid-state fermentation, oxygen availability may be higher at exposed surfaces, although internal regions can also become oxygen-limited depending on moisture, compaction, and microbial activity, which may favor facultative and oxygen-tolerant microorganisms55 Such observations align with the principle that the type of fermentation influences not only the composition of the microbiota but also the rate of colonization and patterns of microbial succession2,3. Consistent with these findings, Elhalis et al. (2023)70 reported that different LAB species exhibit high counts (4 to 5.7 log CFU/g) in anaerobic fermentations with water addition, while under solid-state fermentation conditions, LAB populations tend to be reduced or even absent at the end of the process due to the lower water activity (aw), which can reach values between 0.7 and 0.8.

The addition of a starter culture containing S. cerevisiae led to the formation of distinct clusters compared to control, although no significant differences were observed when compared to spontaneous fermentation. This suggests that the inoculum influences community composition, but not to the extent of suppressing the diversity of the autochthonous microbiota, indicating coexistence and mutual adaptation. Nevertheless, inoculated samples exhibited lower compositional variability, indicating greater homogeneity in the microbial community structure. This is an important aspect for the standardization and reproducibility of industrial fermentation processes. In relation to taxonomy, spontaneous fermentation resulted in more diverse microbial communities, but with high variability among experimental replicates, which may compromise the reproducibility of sensory outcomes. In contrast, the use of a starter culture provided greater predictability and stability in microbial composition, although a reduction in diversity was observed. The addition of S. cerevisiae as an inoculum did not eliminate the endogenous microbiota, indicating coexistence between inoculated and native microorganisms. The exclusive detection of S. cerevisiae only in the inoculated fermentations suggests that this microorganism is not part of the natural microbiota of the C. arabica cv. Arara cherries studied, reinforcing its role as an exogenous organism introduced into the system.

Despite the inoculation of S. cerevisiae (RX60), the yeast did not establish predominance in the fermentation system, being detected only at low abundance in the inoculated samples. This result suggests that the exogenous strain exhibited low competitiveness against the native microbiota, possibly due to the progressive acidification of the medium and the rapid proliferation of endogenous yeasts better adapted to the coffee ecosystem. Previous studies have shown that inoculation of exogenous strains into natural matrices does not always result in effective colonization, especially in systems with high microbial diversity and environmental conditions unfavorable to the establishment of new microorganisms28,39. These findings reinforce the importance of selecting starter cultures from autochthonous microorganisms already adapted to the specific physicochemical and ecological conditions of coffee fermentation, in order to maximize their competitiveness and desirable metabolic expression.

The Venn diagrams clearly demonstrate the occurrence of microbial succession and selection throughout the fermentation process. The high number of unique bacterial ASVs identified in the final stages of fermentation, along with the sharp reduction in fungal diversity observed after 24 h, indicates the action of intense selective pressures such as medium acidification, nutrient depletion, and the accumulation of antimicrobial metabolites59,71. These conditions restrict survival to more adapted microorganisms, promoting community specialization. On the other hand, the persistence of certain taxa across different treatments and fermentation time suggests the existence of a resilient microbial core capable of adapting to environmental changes. This core may represent a promising biotechnological resource for the development of consortium-based starter cultures, with the potential to confer stability and functionality to controlled fermentation processes.

The results obtained reinforce the concept that fermentation acts as a highly selective ecological filter, promoting microbial succession and favoring the predominance of specialized genera adapted to the environmental conditions of the fermentative process. This phenomenon was documented by Amaral et al. (2026)71, who demonstrated that selective pressures such as medium acidification, temperature modulation, and nutrient availability drive community restructuring, leading to the dominance of fermentative and acid-tolerant groups. Collectively, these findings support the view of fermentation as an ecological selection process in which only microorganisms with competitive adaptive traits persist and ultimately shape the final microbiome.

The decision tree-based models identified key taxa strongly associated with sensory quality. The presence of the genus Leuconostoc at abundances above 2.6% was positively correlated with high-quality coffees, supporting its role as a producer of desirable volatile compounds such as aromatic alcohols and fruity esters30. In contrast, Kluyvera and uncultured bacterial taxa showed negative correlations with sensory quality, which may suggest their involvement in the synthesis of undesirable metabolites or interference with the activity of beneficial microorganisms. In the case of fungi, Pichia emerged as a potential positive biomarker, while Acremonium, which is often associated with spoilage and mycotoxin production, was linked to lower-quality samples, highlighting the importance of control and monitoring strategies for the fungal microbiota throughout the fermentation process.

The associations identified by the decision tree–based models indicate that sensory quality is not determined solely by which taxa dominate the community, but also by the presence of functionally relevant genera under specific fermentation conditions. In our dataset, Leuconostoc was among the dominant bacterial genera across fermented samples, and its occurrence above a defined threshold was consistently linked to higher sensory scores, supporting its role as an important contributor to fermentation outcomes. In contrast, for fungi, taxa such as Pichia were not necessarily dominant across all conditions (Fig. 4), yet they emerged as strong predictors of high-quality coffees, suggesting that genera with low abundance can still be highly informative biomarkers and may exert meaningful functional effects through metabolite production and/or interactions with other microorganisms. Together, these results highlight that microbial drivers of coffee quality should be interpreted in terms of ecological roles and condition-dependent abundance patterns, rather than relative abundance alone.

The integration of metataxonomic data with sensory analyses in this study highlighted the potential of multivariate approaches for identifying microbial combinations associated with the production of high-quality beverages. These findings emphasize the importance of microbiological control strategies during fermentation as a tool to guide ecological succession in a beneficial manner. Although the use of starter cultures results in lower microbial diversity compared to spontaneous fermentation, it proved to be a promising approach for ensuring consistency and predictability in sensory quality, especially when employed in microbial consortia which is adapted and functionally complementary.

Finally, the results obtained suggest that fermentation variables such as fermentation time, processing type, temperature, and inoculation can be strategically manipulated to modulate the structure and function of the coffee microbiome, with direct impacts on the sensory profile of the beverage. The identification of key microorganisms, such as Leuconostoc and Pichia, with positive potential for sensory quality, opens new perspectives for the development of customized starter cultures tailored to different fermentation profiles. These strategies, grounded in applied microbiology and sensory science, offer viable pathways to combine standardization, authenticity, and added value in the production of specialty coffees.

Although the primary objective of this study was to characterize microbial dynamics during self-induced anaerobic fermentation, it is widely recognized that microbial succession throughout coffee fermentation is directly linked to the formation of metabolites and aroma-active compounds that determine beverage quality. The predominance of lactic acid bacteria, such as Lactobacillus and Leuconostoc, is associated with the production of organic acids and fermentative metabolites that contribute to acidity modulation, sweetness perception, and overall flavor balance. Similarly, yeasts including Kazachstania, Pichia, and Saccharomyces are known to produce aroma-active compounds such as esters and higher alcohols, which are responsible for fruity, floral, sweet, and caramel-like notes highly valued in specialty coffees. In this context, the microbial patterns observed in the present study are consistent with the sensory outcomes obtained, reinforcing that controlling fermentation conditions enables the modulation of the microbiota toward desirable sensory profiles. Detailed evaluations of organic acids, alcohols, and sensory attributes of the fermented coffees evaluated herein have been previously reported by our research group12, and the present study complements these findings by elucidating the microbial mechanisms underlying quality enhancement.

Conclusion

Self-induced anaerobic fermentation of Coffea arabica cv. Arara cherries resulted in substantial restructuring of the fruit-associated microbiota, leading to significant changes in the composition and diversity of bacterial and fungal communities. Distinct microbial profiles were observed throughout the fermentation process, with recurrent predominance of fermentative genera such as Lactobacillus and Kazachstania, reflecting the selective pressure imposed by the fermentation environment.

Among the process variables evaluated, the fermentation system (solid-state or submerged) was identified as the main factor influencing microbial community structure. Predictive modeling further indicated that specific microbial taxa, particularly Leuconostoc and Pichia, were consistently associated with superior sensory attributes, highlighting their potential as biological indicators of quality and as promising candidates for the development of targeted starter cultures.

Rather than directly evaluating the effects of processing variables on beverage quality, this study focused on understanding how fermentation conditions influence the structure and dynamics of the microbiota associated with quality-related attributes. Overall, these findings emphasize the relevance of microbiota-centered approaches for understanding and managing specialty coffee fermentations in post-harvest systems.
