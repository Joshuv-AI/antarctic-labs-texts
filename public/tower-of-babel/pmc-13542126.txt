Defective indium oxide as plasmonic catalyst for efficient photo-driven methanol steam reforming
Authors: Chiran Wang, Pengfei Yan, Lin Wang, Ye Tao, Tian Zhang, Ninghao Zhai, Congyan Liu, Yan Xu, Xiaolin Tai, Yue Lin, Qun Xu, Bo Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Expanding plasmonic catalysis from noble metals to earth-abundant oxides is important for sustainable solar-to-chemical conversion, but mechanisms remain poorly understood. Here, we show that lithium-reduction-induced defects modulate the electronic structure of indium oxide, increasing free-carrier concentration 2.5-fold and producing a near-infrared plasmon-like response. Computational and spectroscopic analyses suggest that oxygen vacancies act as preferential hot-electron traps, enhance near fields, and shift the kinetically sensitive step from methoxy dehydrogenation on pristine surfaces to intermediate dehydrogenation on defective surfaces. Injection of plasmon-induced hot electrons into intermediate antibonding orbitals may reduce the apparent activation energy from 75.7 to 27.8 kJ mol−1. In this work, we report a competitive methanol steam reforming catalyst with a hydrogen production rate of 214 μmol gcat−1 s−1 and show how defect and electronic engineering can tailor oxide-based plasmonic systems.

Introduction

Hydrogen (H₂) is regarded as a highly promising alternative energy carrier due to its high gravimetric energy density and eco-friendly attributes1–4. However, practical hydrogen storage and transportation remain challenging due to its low volumetric density5,6. Utilizing liquid carriers for hydrogen storage and transportation offers a promising solution to this bottleneck. Thus, identifying a proper hydrogen carrier capable of efficiently and timely producing hydrogen is crucial for addressing the challenges. Compared to other liquid organic hydrogen carriers (LOHCs), methanol is particularly advantageous due to its low cost, ease of storage and transport, and high hydrogen content (12.6 wt%)7–13. Among the various hydrogen production methods, methanol steam reforming (MSR, CH3OH+H2O→CO2+3H2) is one of the most promising approaches, involving the conversion of methanol into hydrogen and carbon dioxide in the presence of water steam. While MSR offers a high hydrogen production, it requires high temperatures, usually at 200–350 °C and typically use noble metal catalysts, resulting in commercially unaffordable energy consumption and economic costs14–19. Pioneering studies have demonstrated the low-temperature production of H2 through aqueous-phase methanol reforming (APMR) reactions; however, the activation of CH3OH and H2O molecules rely on noble metal catalysts, high pressures, and a typical strong basic condition20. While effective, the widespread implementation of these systems is often constrained by the scarcity of precious metal resources and the substantial energy input required to maintain high operating temperatures. Therefore, the development and optimization of non-noble-metal catalysts and more environment-friendly MSR catalytic systems to achieve high performance of hydrogen generation and long-term economic sustainability is of great scientific and practical interest.

Recently, photo-driven reactions have emerged as a promising attractive pathway for sustainable chemical synthesis, particularly when compared to conventional thermally-driven methods21–23. Compared with traditional photocatalytic processes, plasmonic catalysis has become a transformative strategy, capturing solar energy and generating hot carriers to drive reactions under milder conditions and manipulate the selectivity through hot electron pathway. Some plasmonic catalysts, such as L-Cu and CuZn nanoalloys catalyst as efficient photo-driven MSR catalyst under plasmonic mechanism have exhibited excellent activity for H2 evolution and superior selectivity for CO224,25. However, traditional plasmonic metals are often constrained by their inherent limitations in terms of catalytic activity and selectivity for the MSR process. Constructing non-precious metal plasmonic systems therefore represents a promising strategy for identifying more suitable and efficient catalysts. By introducing dopants, such as oxygen vacancies and impurities, into some semiconductors, the band structure can be reconstructed to form partially filled bands containing free carriers. Local surface plasmon resonance (LSPR) can occur when the photon energy of the incident light matches the oscillation frequency of these free carriers in the nanosized semiconductor. To this end, metal oxides have attracted considerable attention as promising photocatalysts, which can be rationally tailored through defect engineering. The generated defect band can serve as a reservoir for photogenerated electrons, thereby alleviating the recombination of electron-hole pairs. Meanwhile, surface defects render the metal center coordinatively unsaturated, facilitating molecular chemisorption26,27. Under irradiation, LSPR could excite electrons into energetic states, thereby inducing photothermal effects that fundamentally alter the catalytic activity.

Herein, we report a lithium-reduction strategy to tune the electronic structure of In2O3 nanoparticles, leading to a 2.5-fold increase in free-carrier concentration and the emergence of a NIR-active LSPR response. The resulting defective In2O3 (D-In2O3) exhibits an H2 production rate of 214 µmol gcat−1 s−1 for photo-driven methanol steam reforming (MSR, Supplementary Table 1) under full-spectrum irradiation and maintains stable performance over 40 reaction cycles. Beyond catalytic performance, this work examines how defect-mediated electronic modulation influences light absorption, surface charge dynamics, and MSR reactivity. High-time-resolution in-situ DRIFTS, kinetic analyses, and DFT calculations support a formate-mediated pathway in which oxygen vacancies facilitate molecular activation and alter the calculated rate-determining step from methoxy dehydrogenation to *CH2OOH dehydrogenation. In addition, an electron-injection model suggests that LSPR-generated carriers can stabilize key reaction intermediates and lower the calculated reaction barrier, consistent with the reduced apparent activation energy observed under illumination. This study identifies defective In2O3 as a model non-noble-metal plasmonic oxide and provides a scientific framework for designing oxide-based plasmonic catalysts through coupled defect and electronic engineering.

Results

Synthesis and characterization of defective In2O3 catalysts

We prepared D-In2O3 by lithium metal reduction of In2O3 nanoparticles (commercially available) at room temperature using a modified procedure reported for defective TiO2 preparation28. In2O3 nanoparticles and lithium are mixed in an agate mortar with dimethyl carbonate (DMC) as a dispersant, and ground for 1 h in an Ar atmosphere. The obtained mixtures were washed with ethanol and dilute hydrochloric acid to remove residual lithium and possible lithium oxide, followed by vacuum drying for use. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 1, increasing lithium ratio results in color of In2O3 darkening from original pale yellow to grey (1%) and black (2% and 3%). The responding samples were denoted as 1% Li-treated In2O3 (D-In2O3-1), 2% Li-treated In2O3 (D-In2O3-2), and 3% Li-treated In2O3 (D-In2O3-3), respectively. Powder X-ray diffraction (PXRD) patterns of D-In2O3-1 and D-In2O3-2 are identical to that of pristine In2O3, indicating no phase changes in their cubic bixbyite structure. However, a clear peak of indium metal can be identified in the PXRD pattern of D-In2O3-3 sample, revealing that In2O3 is over-reduced by excess amount of indium metal (Fig. 1a). The transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images show that lithium treatment did not change the particle size and morphology of In2O3 (Supplementary Fig. 2). A high-resolution TEM image of D-In2O3-2 exhibits lattice fringes with a spacing of 0.292 nm, corresponding to the (222) plane of In2O3. High angle annular dark field scanning transmission electron microscopy (HAADF-STEM) imaging and element mapping show that the elements In and O were uniformly distributed in the nanoparticles. The surface chemistry and valence states of lithium treated In2O3 were investigated using X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS). Two typical O 1s XPS peaks centered at 529.5 and 531.3 eV are attributed to lattice oxygen (In-O-In) and the O atoms adjoined defect position (oxygen vacancy), respectively (Fig. 1b)29,30. Another peak at a higher binding energy of ca. 533.0 eV is assigned to OH groups on the surface of In2O3. Compared to pristine In2O3, lithium treated In2O3 exhibits an increased concentration of oxygen vacancies (Supplementary Table 2 and Fig. 1b). This enhancement reveals the role of lithium reduction in extracting surface oxygen atoms, thereby facilitating the formation of oxygen vacancies on the In2O3 surface. And increasing lithium ratio led to higher ratio the oxygen vacancy. Thermogravimetric (TG) result shows a 4.95% mass increase for D-In2O3-2 sample when heated to 800 °C in air, corroborating the presence of massive oxygen vacancies in the defective indium oxide (Supplementary Fig. 4). Two In 3d core-level peaks located at 444.0 and 451.5 eV are identified to the characteristic spin-orbit split 3d5/2 and 3d3/2 of In (III) species (Fig. 1c). The absence of metallic indium in the In 3d peak is in good agreement with the PXRD results and proves that the color darkening is not originated from metallic indium (Fig. 1c). The electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) spectra display a resonance at g = 2.000 for the D-In2O3-2 sample, while pristine In2O3, D-In2O3-1 and D-In2O3-3 are EPR silent (Fig. 1d), in spite of all the samples bearing oxygen vacancy. Oxygen vacancy could exist as singly ionized state with unpaired electrons (VO+1: EPR active) or a neutral oxygen vacancy/doubly ionized vacancies (VO0/VO+2: EPR inactive), depending on the depth of reduction31–33.

This distinction is important for understanding the structure-activity relationship across the Li-treatment series. In D-In2O3-1 (1 wt% Li), the milder reduction generates oxygen vacancies that are mainly EPR-silent. These vacancies contribute to the modest increase in the Ov fraction observed by XPS (Supplementary Table 2), but they do not provide sufficient unpaired-electron density to support a strong LSPR response. In D-In2O3-2 (2 wt% Li), deeper reduction produces a higher population of EPR-active VO+1 species. The unpaired electrons associated with these vacancies create defect-related electronic states near the Fermi level, which may act as electron reservoirs and contribute to the 2.5-fold increase in carrier concentration (Fig. 2h) and the pronounced NIR-LSPR absorption. At 3 wt% Li (D-In2O3-3), XRD reveals metallic In0 (Fig. 1a), indicating that the reduction proceeds beyond vacancy formation and leads to lattice over-reduction. The formation of metallic In domains, together with the decrease in EPR-active vacancy species, compromises the defective oxide framework and makes D-In2O3-3 less suitable as the optimized catalyst. These results suggest that the high catalytic activity of D-In2O3-2 (214 μmol gcat–1 s−1, Fig. 3a) arises from a balanced reduction state that favours electronically active oxygen vacancies while preserving the defective oxide framework, rather than from simply maximizing the total oxygen-vacancy concentration. On the other hand, lithium treatment led to the amorphization of In2O3 surface owing to the massive oxygen loss, as witnessed by HAADF-STEM (Fig. 1e, f). The introduced oxygen defects make the specific surface areas of D-In2O3-2 (32 m2 g–1) higher than that of pristine In2O3 (13 m2 g–1), as calculated from N2 sorption isotherms (Supplementary Fig. 5).

Plasmonic properties of defective In2O3

It should be noted that the LSPR signatures in defect-engineered oxide semiconductors differ fundamentally from those of classical noble-metal nanoparticles (Au, Ag, Cu). Noble metals exhibit sharp, intense extinction peaks in the UV–Vis region owing to their high free-electron densities (~1022–1023 cm–3). In contrast, doped or defective oxides typically possess lower free-carrier concentrations (~1019–1021 cm–3), resulting in broader, weaker plasmonic features that are commonly located in the NIR or mid-IR region33. Therefore, LSPR in this class of materials should be evaluated through a converging set of evidence rather than any single spectroscopic criterion. As color changing of In2O3 by lithium treatment is observed, we collected the UV-visible near-infrared (UV-Vis-NIR) diffuse reflectance spectra of pristine In2O3, D-In2O3-1, and D-In2O3-2 and converted to corresponding Tauc plots (Supplementary Figs. 6 and 7). It is found that lithium treatment results in significant absorbance in visible and NIR range, as compared to pristine In2O3. The optical band gaps shift from 3.2 eV for pristine In2O3 to 3.0 and 2.8 eV for D-In2O3-1, and D-In2O3-2, respectively. It is well studied that oxygen vacancy induces the intra-band above valence band so that reducing the band gap, which is also responsible for the color darkening27. It is worth noting that D-In2O3-2 exhibits a broad absorbance in IR range of 4000–2000 cm−1 (2500–5000 nm), while pristine In2O3 is completely inactive (Fig. 2b). Such low-energy photon absorbance is attributed to local surface plasmon resonance (LSPR) of free carries on defective In2O3 nanoparticles34. The high-density oxygen deficiency bearing unpair electron (EPR active VO+1) may create additional states near the Fermi level, allowing trapped electron transition under excitation of low-energy irradiation and thereby contributing to LSPR absorbance. To provide nanoscale physical evidence of the LSPR effect, electron energy loss spectroscopy (EELS) was performed on D-In2O3-2. As shown in Fig. 2c, a plasmon loss feature is observed at ca. 0.70 eV. Consistent with the modest carrier density (1.4 × 1020 cm–³) and the spatially heterogeneous defect distribution inherent to the lithium-reduction process, this feature is broader and less intense than the sharp plasmon loss peaks typical of noble metals. Nevertheless, its energy position corresponds precisely to the broad NIR absorption band centered at ~1750 nm, supporting the assignment of a collective free-carrier oscillation is responsible for the low-energy photon harvesting in D-In2O3-2. To gain deeper insights into the electronic structure and the preferential formation of oxygen vacancies, Hirshfeld charge calculations were performed for both pristine and defective surface models. The Hirshfeld charge distribution provides a quantitative map of localized electron density, where larger positive values signify higher positive charge and larger negative values indicate electron accumulation (Fig. 2d, e). In the pristine In2O3 (111) surface, where O atoms are three-coordinated, a smaller absolute magnitude of Hirshfeld charge on a specific oxygen site indicates a weakened electronic coupling with the surrounding indium centers (marked with black dash circle). This weakened interaction serves as a primary criterion for identifying the most labile oxygen atoms, which are energetically most favorable for removal during the lithium-reduction process. Utilizing this descriptor to construct the defective surface model, we observed that the deliberate introduction of oxygen vacancies triggers a significant electronic redistribution. As illustrated in the color-coded Hirshfeld mapping (Fig. 2e), the extraction of O atoms induces the formation of electron-enriched indium regions (red zones). This vacancy-induced electron density enhancement is fundamental to the observed increase in carrier concentration and the subsequent emergence of the NIR-active LSPR effect.

To provide a theoretical foundation for the observed optical properties, we calculated the complex refractive index (n) and extinction coefficient (k) for both pristine and defective In2O3 using Density Functional Theory (DFT) (Fig. 2f). The results reveal that the extinction coefficient (k) of D-In2O3 increases dramatically in the NIR region, confirming that the extrinsic electronic states created by oxygen vacancies significantly enhance NIR photon-harvesting capability. These DFT-derived optical constants served as precise input parameters for subsequent electromagnetic field simulations, ensuring the reliability of our theoretical model. Based on the DFT-calculated optical constants, we employed the finite element method (FEM) to simulate the electric-field distribution under 1750 nm excitation. As illustrated in Fig. 2g, D-In2O3-2 exhibits intense electric field enhancement at the surface defect sites, with a maximum field intensity (|E/E0|2) significantly higher than that of pristine In2O3. These hot spots are spatially correlated with the location of oxygen vacancies, demonstrating that the defects not only act as catalytic active sites but also function as nanoscopic antennas that concentrate electromagnetic energy. This localized field enhancement is a feature consistent with LSPR-related field localization, facilitating the generation and extraction of energetic hot carriers. While certain non-noble metal plasmonic platforms rely on the introduction of external ions to boost carrier concentrations, we sought to ensure that our observed localized field enhancement is intrinsic to the defective structure26. We note that these static DFT calculations are not designed to simulate the full time-dependent LSPR excitation or plasmon decay dynamics. Rather, they serve to evaluate whether the defect-induced electronic structure can support enhanced NIR optical absorption and field localization, thereby providing the physical prerequisites for the LSPR effect.

Accordingly, ICP and SEM-EDS measurements were conducted to determine the levels of residual Li and confirm that the Li-reduction method does not introduce significant impurities that could bias our results. The results indicate that after thorough washing, the residual Li content in the optimized D-In2O3-2 sample is extremely low (0.03 wt%). This negligible amount effectively rules out the possibility that the observed LSPR arises from high-concentration extrinsic Li-doping. Instead, Li acts primarily as a sacrificial reducing agent to create oxygen vacancies. EDS mapping (Supplementary Fig. 9) further corroborates the absence of large-scale Li segregation or accumulation. Hall effect measurements reveal that the carrier concentration for D-In2O3-2 is ca. 2.5-fold higher than that of pristine In2O3, whereas D-In2O3-1 presents the similar level with pristine In2O3 (Fig. 2h). The result suggests deep reduction by lithium extracts more oxygen and leaves more mobilizable electrons on the Li-treated In2O3. Accordingly, D-In2O3-2 shows lower resistivity than D-In2O3-1 and pristine In2O3. This agrees with the photocurrent response experiment as shown in Supplementary Fig. 8, where D-In2O3-2 gave rise to about 5 times higher photocurrent than D-In2O3-1, but about 50 times higher than pristine In2O3. This is consistent with prominent LSPR effect of D-In2O3-2 sample.

Further supporting evidence for LSPR-related charge dynamics is provided by in-situ XPS measurements. Under irradiation, the In 3d peak of D-In2O3-2 shifts reversibly to higher binding energy by ca. 0.2 eV, indicating the formation of electron-depleted In(3+δ)+ sites, and returns to its original position upon removal of illumination (Fig. 2i). This reversible response is not consistent with a simple classical interband-excitation picture, where carrier accumulation in the conduction band (predominantly In 5s character) would be expected to produce a negative binding energy shift23. Instead, the observed behavior indicates a light-induced redistribution of surface charge associated with the defective structure, consistent with light-induced surface charge redistribution associated with the defective structure. As a control, pristine In2O3 shows no comparable shift under identical illumination (Supplementary Fig. 10), confirming that the response originates from the defect-induced electronic states rather than from conventional band-gap excitation. We note the photoluminescence (PL) emissions (λex = 275 nm) of D-In2O3-2 are quenched almost completely in comparison with pristine In2O3 (Supplementary Fig. 11), suggesting a nonradiative relaxation of photoexcited electrons35. The enhanced absorbance of light, increased concentration of free carriers, and nonradiative relaxation endow D-In2O3-2 sample with enhanced photothermal response36. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 12a, under full-spectrum irradiation, the surface temperature of D-In2O3-2 rapidly increases to 280 °C, while the pristine In2O3 temperature can only reach 145 °C under identical conditions (Supplementary Fig. 12b). And the saturated surface temperature shows a linear relationship with the light intensity of the Xe lamp source (Supplementary Fig. 13), Combining the above comprehensive characterizations and FEM simulation, we provide a converging set of evidence supports a plasmon-like free-carrier response of Li-treated In2O3 sample (D-In2O3-2).

Photo-driven MSR by D-In2O3 catalyst

Next, we evaluated the photo-driven MSR performance of D-In2O3-2 under irradiation (without other external heating, see details in the Methods). The reaction was conducted in a sealed batch photoreactor using 5 mg catalyst, 200 μL CH3OH, and 80 μL H2O under Ar. Upon illumination, photothermal heating vaporized the liquid reactants and established a gas-solid reaction environment. This gas-solid phase system not only promotes molecular interactions on the catalyst surface but also mitigates light absorption by the solution, especially for NIR light, thereby advancing the light absorbance efficiency. The defect-induced plasmonic response of D-In2O3 enhances light absorption, promotes photothermal conversion, and generates light-induced surface charge redistribution, providing a catalytic platform for MSR37. (Supplementary Fig. 14). To verify the advantage of the defect-enriched design strategy on MSR performance, the hydrogen production rates of pristine In2O3, D-In2O3-1, and D-In2O3-2 were investigated under full-spectrum illumination (2.6 W cm−2). As present in Fig. 3a, the D-In2O3-2 exhibits the highest MSR performance, with an H2 production rate up to 214 µmol gcat–1 s–1, CO2 selectivity near 99%, and trace amount of CO as by-product (Supplementary Table 4). Under the standard condition (2.6 W cm−2), the H2/CO2 molar ratio was ~3.1, close to the theoretical stoichiometry ratio of 3:1 for MSR reaction (CH3OH+H2O→CO2+3H2, Supplementary Table 4). This near-stoichiometric product ratio supports MSR as the major reaction pathway under the present conditions. Pristine In2O3 and D-In2O3-1 show little activity, with a hydrogen production rate of 0.86 µmol gcat–1 s–1 and 21.4 µmol gcat–1 s–1, respectively. As shown in Fig. 3b, the H2 production rate over D-In2O3-2 increased nearly linearly with light intensity. Because hot-carrier generation is generally proportional to photon flux, this trend is consistent with a photon-assisted contribution to the MSR reaction38. It has been reported that KIE measurements can be used to differentiate between plasmonic hot-electron-mediated surface reactions and thermocatalytic reactions39. For D-In2O3-2, we conducted KIE measurements using both D2O and CD3OD. We obtained a large KIE of 1.35 ± 0.03 under full-spectrum light irradiation conditions (2.6 W cm−2), in comparison with a KIE of 1.15 ± 0.03 under thermocatalytic (280 °C) and dark conditions (Fig. 3c). Similarly, for the CH3OH/CD3OD, the KIE increased from 1.43 ± 0.15 (thermal) to 1.64 ± 0.06 (photo), suggesting that O-H bond in H2O and C-H bond activation in CH3OH are both affected by illumination25. These changes in KIE values suggest that illumination alters the bond-activation environment, consistent with a non-thermal carrier contribution in addition to photothermal heating.

To estimate the contribution of uniform thermal heating, we performed thermocatalytic controls at 280 °C, matching the macroscopic surface temperature reached under 2.6 W cm–2 illumination (Supplementary Figs. 12 and 13). Under this purely thermal condition, the H2 production rate was only ~4 μmol gcat–1 s–1, much lower than that obtained under photo-driven MSR at the same macroscopic temperature (214 μmol gcat–1 s–1; Fig. 3a and Supplementary Fig. 16). This comparison indicates that uniform macroscopic heating alone cannot account for the high MSR activity under illumination. We further compared the apparent activation energies (Ea) under thermal and illumination conditions. The Ea decreases from 75.7 kJ mol–1 under thermal conditions to 27.8 kJ mol–1 under illumination (Fig. 3d). If the reaction were governed solely by uniform photothermal heating, the apparent activation energy would be expected to remain close to the thermal value. The decrease in apparent activation energy, together with the altered KIE values, is consistent with a photo-assisted pathway distinct from a conventional thermal route24,25.

Through UV-Vis-NIR and IR diffuse reflectance spectroscopy (Fig. 2a, b), D-In2O3-2 was found to exhibit both high-energy interband absorption, mainly associated with VB-to-CB excitation, and a broad NIR absorption band assigned to LSPR. To assess their respective contributions to MSR activity, we performed cutoff-filter-based irradiation tests. The incident light was divided into UV-Vis irradiation (λ < 800 nm), mainly associated with interband excitation, and NIR irradiation (λ > 800 nm), associated with the defect-induced LSPR-like response. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 17, UV-Vis irradiation produced negligible H2, indicating that classical interband excitation alone is insufficient to drive the MSR reaction efficiently. In contrast, NIR irradiation generated H2 at 73 µmol gcat–1 s–1, supporting the contribution of LSPR-related excitation to the reaction. Because D-In2O3-2 also shows pronounced photothermal heating under NIR and full-spectrum irradiation, we further performed thermal control experiments. We externally heated the catalyst to 280 °C and simultaneously applied weak full-spectrum irradiation at 0.1 W cm–2, corresponding to approximately 1 sun. Under this condition, the H2 production rate remained low, at 8.8 μmol gcat–1s–1 (Supplementary Fig. 18). This value is only slightly higher than that under purely thermal conditions at 280 °C (~4 μmol gcat–1 s–1) and is approximately 36-fold lower than that under 2.6 W cm–2 illumination (214 μmol gcat–1 s–1). Thus, maintaining the catalyst at the same macroscopic temperature is not sufficient to reproduce the high photo-driven activity. A high photon flux is also required, consistent with a photon-assisted rather than purely phonon-driven process. A similar conclusion was obtained from the UV-light control experiment. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 19, UV irradiation produced negligible H2 even when combined with external heating to 280 °C. This result indicates that neither interband excitation nor thermal energy alone can account for the enhanced MSR activity. Instead, the activity is closely associated with NIR-LSPR excitation in D- In2O3-2. Under full-spectrum irradiation, the catalytic activity increased further relative to the NIR-only condition (Supplementary Fig. 17). This enhancement suggests a possible synergy between high-energy photon excitation and NIR-LSPR excitation. Electrons promoted from the valence band may relax into defect-related states and subsequently participate in LSPR-mediated intraband excitation, thereby increasing the population of energetic carriers available for surface reactions. Collectively, the cutoff-filter-based irradiation tests, matched-temperature thermal controls, and photon-flux-dependent activity support a photo-assisted MSR process in which photothermal heating establishes the elevated reaction temperature and vaporizes the reactants, while LSPR-generated carriers provide an additional non-thermal contribution to bond activation.

We note that, under the high light intensity used for the catalytic measurements (2.6 W cm−2, equivalent to 26 suns), spatial thermal gradients may exist between the illuminated catalyst surface and the reactor body, as well as among individual nanoparticles. The temperature measurements in Supplementary Figs. 12 and 13 provide macroscopic, ensemble-averaged surface temperatures and may not fully resolve transient nanoscale temperature variations at individual active sites. Nevertheless, local heating alone is unlikely to account for the observed catalytic behavior. As discussed above, the nearly linear light-intensity dependence, the altered KIE values, and the reduced apparent activation energy are difficult to explain solely by a broadened local temperature distribution. Together, these results suggest that photothermal heating establishes the elevated reaction temperature and vaporizes the reactants, while LSPR-generated carriers provide an additional non-thermal contribution to catalytic turnover.

Durability is a crucial parameter for evaluating catalyst performance, particularly for defect-rich materials, as highly reactive defect sites may undergo reconstruction during catalytic reactions. As shown in Fig. 3e, D-In2O3-2 maintained stable H2 generation over 40 cycles of photo-driven MSR, without obvious decay in activity. Over the 40-cycle stability test, the cumulative H2 production reached 1.73 L (77 mmol). Post-reaction characterizations further confirmed the structural robustness of the catalyst. XRD and TEM analyses showed that the crystal structure and morphology were well maintained after reaction (Supplementary Figs. 20 and 21). Raman spectra showed no new vibrational modes, suggesting no detectable impurity phases or carbon deposition (Supplementary Fig. 22). XPS analysis showed no significant change in the In valence state after MSR (Supplementary Fig. 23). Interestingly, the used D-In2O3-2 exhibited a higher oxygen-vacancy concentration than the fresh catalyst (Supplementary Fig. 23 and Supplementary Table 2), which may arise from dynamic surface oxygen exchange with H2O under irradiation and elevated temperature during MSR.

We further compared the catalytic performance of D-In2O3-2 with some representative MSR catalyst systems, including plasmonic L-Cu24, Zn1.3Cu98.725, CuOx/ZnO/Al2O3/Bi2Te340, 2% Pt/α-MoC20 and CuZnGaOx41. The comparison was made across H2 production rate, CO2 selectivity, and apparent activation energy (Supplementary Table 1). Under the specified photo-driven MSR conditions, D-In2O3-2 compares favorably with these reference catalysts, particularly in terms of mass-normalized H2 production rate, and the competitive performance reported for the listed photo-driven and thermal-driven reference systems. (Supplementary Table 1). Moreover, the lower apparent activation energy (27.8 vs 75.7 kJ mol–1 for the corresponding thermal pathway) illustrate the promising performance profile of this defect-engineered non-noble-metal oxide plasmonic system on a H2 production performance-normalized basis.

To explore the potential of photo-assisted MSR under solar-relevant conditions, the performance of D-In2O3-2 was evaluated under AM 1.5 G irradiation. Under 1 sun illumination (0.1 W cm−2), the catalyst exhibited an H2 production rate of 1.12 μmol gcat−1 s−1 and a CO2 selectivity of 91.5%. At an incident intensity of 75 mW cm–2, which is comparable to typical terrestrial solar irradiance, concentration of the AM 1.5 G light using a simple Fresnel lens increased the H2 production rate to 47.58 μmol gcat−1 s−1, while maintaining a CO2 selectivity of 95.4%. These results provide preliminary support that readily accessible optical concentration may enable appreciable photo-assisted MSR performance under solar-level irradiation, suggesting a potential solar-assisted route complementary to conventional thermally driven MSR (Supplementary Fig. 24 and Supplementary Table 4).

Photo-driven MSR mechanism

To elucidate the reaction pathways of the photo-driven MSR process over D-In2O3-2, in-situ diffuse reflectance infrared Fourier transform spectroscopy (DRIFTS) was conducted. Under dark conditions, the catalyst reached adsorption equilibrium after 30 min of exposure to a H2O/CH3OH/Ar stream, as evidenced by characteristic vibrational modes of methanol (2972, 2915, 2862, and 2817 cm–1) and water (1650 and 3200−3400 cm–1). The bands centered at 2937 and 2838 cm–1 are assigned to the surface methoxy species (*CH3O), which serve as the primary precursors for subsequent reforming. Upon full-spectrum illumination (Fig. 4a), spectra collected with enhanced time resolution (1 min to 30 min) captured the rapid transient evolution of surface intermediates. Within the initial 1–5 min of irradiation, a significant attenuation in the intensity of *CH3O (2940, 2383, 1053 cm–1)42–45 peaks was observed, accompanied by the simultaneous emergence of formaldehyde (*CH2O, 1720 cm–1)25 and formate species (*HCOO, 1583 and 1367 cm–1) 44–49. The inclusion of the low-wavenumber region (800–1500 cm–1) highlights the C-O stretching and deformation modes that further support a formate-mediated pathway under the present reaction conditions. Concurrently, the rising intensity of the CO2 peak at 2360 cm–1 signifies the continuous generation of gaseous products under light-driven conditions. Crucially, as shown in the magnified spectra (Fig. 4b), no vibrational features associated with gaseous or adsorbed CO (typically observed between 2000 and 2200 cm–1)20,50,51 were detected throughout the reaction duration. This absence provides evidence that the MSR process on D-In2O3-2 proceeds primarily via the formate pathway rather than the indirect water-gas shift (WGS) route. To further distinguish the plasmonic hot-carrier contribution from a purely photothermal effect, we recorded a set of control in-situ DRIFTS spectra under dark conditions at 280 °C (Supplementary Fig. 25), which corresponds to the saturated surface temperature reached under 2.6 W cm−2 full-spectrum irradiation. As illustrated in the thermal control experiment, even at this elevated temperature, the evolution of key reaction intermediates is remarkably sluggish compared to the photo-driven process. Specifically, the absorption bands associated with surface methoxy groups (*CH3O, 2940 cm–1) and methanol (*CH3OH) remained dominant with minimal intensity variation throughout the 30-min thermal treatment. The signals for formaldehyde (*CH2O, 1420 cm–1) and formate species were barely detectable, and no significant evolution of the gaseous CO2 peak was observed in the 2300–2400 cm–1 region. This stark contrast between the two pathways indicates that the high hydrogen production rate (214 μmol gcat–1 s–1) observed under illumination is not solely a consequence of lattice heating. Instead, the catalytic activity is substantially enhanced under illumination, consistent with an additional non-thermal contribution from LSPR- related carrier processes that lowers the apparent activation energy from 75.7 kJ/mol to 27.8 kJ/mol.

It should be noted that methanol steam reforming over metal or metal-oxide catalysts may proceed through several pathways42–47,50–52. These are commonly classified as: (i) a formate (*HCOO) pathway, in which methoxy species are sequentially converted through formaldehyde- and formate-related intermediates to CO2 and H252; (ii) a CO-mediated pathway, involving initial methanol decomposition to CO and H2 followed by CO oxidation through the water-gas shift reaction52; and (iii) a methyl formate (*HCOOCH3) pathway, involving coupling between methoxy and formate species52. The dominant pathway depends strongly on the catalyst composition and reaction conditions. In the present system, the CO-mediated and methyl formate pathways appear less likely based on the in-situ DRIFTS and product-analysis results. No obvious gaseous or adsorbed CO bands were observed in the 2000–2200 cm–1 region during in-situ DRIFTS (Fig. 4b), and CO was detected only at trace levels in the gas-phase products (≤0.010 mmol, Supplementary Table 4). The trace CO may arise from secondary CO2 hydrogenation or reverse water-gas shift side reactions under high-intensity irradiation53. In addition, no characteristic C-O-C stretching features of methyl formate species (~1100–1200 cm–1) were observed in the low-wavenumber DRIFTS region (Fig. 4a). Instead, the sequential evolution of *CH3O42–45, *CH2O25, *HCOO44–49, and CO252 supports a formate-mediated pathway. The *CH2OOH and *CHOOH intermediates considered in the DFT free-energy profile (Fig. 4c) are assigned as surface-bound dioxymethylene-type and formic-acid-type species, respectively. They represent possible sequential dehydrogenation steps that connect *CH2O-derived species to *HCOO/CO2 formation within the broader formate pathway, rather than a separate mechanistic route.

Guided by the formate-mediated pathway inferred from DRIFTS and product analysis, we calculated the Gibbs free-energy profiles for the MSR reaction coordinate on both pristine In2O3 and defective D-In2O3 (111) surfaces (Fig. 4c) to gain the insight of how oxygen vacancies promote the catalytic process. On the pristine In2O3 surface, the dehydrogenation of surface methoxy to formaldehyde (*CH3O → *CH2O) is identified as the rate-determing step (RDS), exhibiting a formidable free-energy barrier of 1.82 eV. This high energetic penalty accounts for the negligible catalytic activity observed for pristine In2O3. In sharp contrast, on the D-In2O3 surface, the surface vacancies significantly modulate the adsorption configurations and electronic states of the intermediates. Consequently, the RDS is redirected to the dehydrogenation of the *CH2OOH intermediate into *CHOOH, with the maximum free-energy span substantially reduced to 1.56 eV. The DFT calculations suggest that the introduction of Ovac not only lowers the activation energy but may also shift the reaction’sRDS. This dual-functional role of oxygen vacancies, simultaneously altering the RDS and lowering the intrinsic barrier, provides a thermodynamic rationale consistent with the enhanced MSR performance on D-In2O3. Furthermore, this defect-optimized landscape serves as the essential platform for the subsequent LSPR-mediated enhancement. Under light irradiation, the injection of hot electrons into this already-optimized surface further facilitates the RDS, as detailed below.

To elucidate the thermodynamic origin of the LSPR-mimicking electron-injection effect that lowers the free-energy span of the *CH2OOH+*H → *CHOOH + *H2 step (1.56 → 1.25 eV, Fig. 4d), we correlate localized structural rearrangements with site-resolved Hirshfeld-I charge responses (Supplementary Fig. 26 and Table S5 and S6). On the neutral surface, the transformation involves concurrent cleavage of the surface O–H bond (O87–H161) and the organic C–H bond (C164–H163), accompanied by H161–H163 bond formation (Supplementary Fig. 26). This process generates a strongly charge-separated product-like motif: the deprotonated lattice oxygen becomes more anionic (O87: −1.443 → −1.539 e, Δq = −0.096 e), while the dehydrogenated carbon center becomes markedly electron-deficient (C164: +0.412 → +0.681 e, Δq = +0.269 e). Such interfacial charge separation constitutes the principal thermodynamic penalty of the step. Strikingly, the electron-injected surface exhibits a highly final-state-biased stabilization. In the initial state (IS, Fig. 4d insert), the injected electron only weakly perturbs the reactive C–H moiety (C164: Δq = −0.005 e; H163: −0.006 e), whereas in the final state (FS, Fig. 4d insert) it is preferentially accommodated by the metal framework adjacent to the deprotonated oxygen: In103 switches from slight electron depletion in IS (Δq = +0.033 e) to substantial electron accumulation in FS (Δq = −0.180 e). This preferential charge trapping is structurally consistent with the strong approach of In103 toward the naked O87 site upon H2 formation (O87–In103: 3.265→2.467 Å on the neutral surface; 3.196 → 2.631 Å under electron injection), which facilitates efficient electrostatic screening and charge delocalization of the O87. Simultaneously, the injected electron partially compensates the electron-deficient adsorbate in the final state (C164: Δq = −0.025 e), further stabilizing the product-like configuration. Collectively, the coupled structural-electronic response—preferential accommodation of injected electrons by the In centers adjacent to the deprotonated lattice oxygen, together with partial compensation of the oxidized carbon center that stabilizes the final state relative to the initial state and thereby reduces the macroscopic free-energy span of this transformation.

The projected density of states (PDOS) for the rate-determining intermediate (*CH2OOH+*H) and the product (*HCOOH+*H2) were analyzed before and after electron injection to scrutinize the microscopic mechanism of the barrier reduction. As shown in Fig. 4e, the neutral *CH2OOH intermediate exhibits a distinct bandgap near the Fermi level (Ef). Upon hot-electron injection (+e−), a new prominent occupied state emerges just below Ef. PDOS analysis identifies this state as a hybridized orbital formed between the In 5 s states of the defect site and the states of the adsorbate. Physically, this corresponds to the population of the anti-bonding orbitals of the intermediate by the energetic hot electron, which effectively activates the reactant thus weakens the C-H bond. In contrast, for the product system (*HCOOH+*H2, Fig. 4f), electron injection induces a downshift of the DOS peaks of the adsorbate toward lower energy levels (e.g., from −0.49 eV to −0.61 eV relative to Ef). This indicates that the injected electrons populate deeper, more stable orbital states in the product configuration54. This dual effect, activation of the intermediate and stabilization of the product provides an electronic rationale for the experimentally observed reduction in apparent activation energy (Ea) and the theoretically calculated lower reaction barrier (1.56 eV →1.25 eV). However, it should be noted that these computational models represent ideal solid-vacuum interfaces and may not fully capture the complexities of the true catalytic environment, such as dynamic surface reconstruction, local temperature gradients, and solvent effects under practical working conditions.

Based on the results discussed above and the previous theoretical researches, we propose the possible reaction pathway of our photo-driven MSR as shown in Fig. 5a. CH3OH was first dehydrogenated to *CH3O, then decomposed to *CH2O by C-H bond activation, and subsequently converted into *CH2OOH via *CH2O interacting with *OH produced by water dissociation, followed by the *CH2OOH step-by step dehydrogenation into CO2 and H2. During the whole process, the surface oxygen vacancies serve as preferential adsorption sites for methanol and water, while LSPR-related carrier processes may provide additional energetic carriers to drive the subsequent dehydrogenation steps. Discussion

In summary, a nonmetallic plasmonic catalyst of defective In2O3 was successfully synthesized by room-temperature lithium reduction strategy, producing enriched oxygen vacancies on the surface. As compared with pristine In2O3, the defective In2O3 displays extended optical absorbance to NIR region, higher carrier concentration, and lower electric resistance. Under a full-spectrum irradiation (2.6 W cm−2), D-In2O3 exhibits a pronounced photothermal effect with the surface temperature rising to 280 °C, and shows excellent activity for the photo-driven MSR reaction with competitive hydrogen production rate of 214 µmol gcat–1 s–1. It is revealed the hydrogen production rate is linear with the irradiation intensity, and NIR light makes the major contribution for catalytic activity. A converging evidence chain supports the LSPR character of D-In2O3-2. This evidence includes enhanced carrier concentration from Hall measurements, vacancy-related unpaired electrons from EPR, broad NIR free-carrier absorption, nanoscale EELS features, reversible light-induced charge redistribution from in-situ XPS, and DFT-informed FEM simulations. Together with kinetic signatures that are difficult to explain by uniform photothermal heating alone, these results support a photo-assisted MSR pathway in which LSPR-generated carriers contribute to bond activation alongside photothermal heating. This work identifies defective In2O3 as a model non-noble-metal plasmonic oxide and provides design principles for oxide-based plasmonic catalysts through coupled defect and electronic engineering. We also note that comparative vacancy-introduction routes (e.g., controlled hydrogen reduction) would be valuable for further evaluating the generality of this design principle, although achieving a comparable vacancy density without over-reduction remains experimentally challenging.

Methods

Materials preparation

The raw materials required are commercially available In2O3 (99.9%, Aladdin) and Li foils (99.9%, Cellithium, China). The In2O3 and Li foils were ground in an agate mortar for 1 h at a certain stoichiometric ratio, while DMC (99%, Aladdin) was added as a dispersant1. To prevent the Li foils from being oxidized, the grinding process described above was completed in a glove box filled with Ar. The ground sample was washed with dilute HCl to remove lithium oxide. The sample was washed three times with deionized water and then dried by centrifugation to obtain defective In2O3. In this work, defective In2O3 was prepared with 1%, 2%, and 3% lithium content by adding different levels of Li metal foils to control the defect content.

Structural characterization

The crystal structures were determined utilizing a Rigaku Miniflex 600 X-Ray diffractometer equipped with Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.5417 Å)55,56. For microstructural and elemental mapping analysis, a JEOL-2100F microscope operated at 200 kV was employed, while atomic-resolution HAADF-STEM images were captured using an FEI Titan Themis Z 3.1 system featuring a monochromator and a spherical aberration corrector. Optical characteristics were evaluated with a SolidSpec-3700 UV-Vis-IR spectrophotometer over a 200–2500 nm range. Photoluminescence properties were excited at 360 nm and recorded on a Horiba JY Fluorolog-3-tau spectrometer. Defect structures were investigated via a JEOL JES-FA200 EPR spectrometer at room temperature57. Chemical states were probed through XPS measurements on an ESCALAB 250 system using a monochromatized Al Kα source (1486.7 eV). Additionally, FT-IR spectra were obtained via a SHIMADZU IR Affinity spectrometer (KBr pellet technique, 4000–400 cm–¹), and thermogravimetric profiling was conducted on a TG Q500 analyzer (25 to 800 °C, 10 °C/min, air atmosphere)53,55–57.

Photo-driven methanol steam reforming reaction

Photo-driven methanol steam reforming experiments were conducted in a custom-built stainless-steel batch photoreactor (304 grade; internal volume: 70 cm3) equipped with a top quartz window for light transmission. The quartz window had a diameter of 56 mm and a thickness of 15 mm, and was sealed to the reactor body using a bolt-flange assembly with a high-temperature-resistant O-ring. The reactor was equipped with gas ports for Ar purging and product sampling, a septum-sealed sampling port, and a pressure gauge for monitoring the internal pressure (Supplementary Fig. 14). Under high-flux Xe-lamp irradiation, the effective light spot on the quartz window had a diameter of approximately 40 mm. The light source used for photo-driven MSR was a 500 W Xe lamp (CEL-S500F, Beijing, China Education Au-light Co., Ltd) equipped with an AM 1.5 G filter to simulate standard solar irradiation. The light intensity at the reactor window was measured and calibrated using a fully automatic optical power meter (CEL-NP2000-2A, Beijing China Education Au-light Co., Ltd). The light intensity was adjusted by varying the distance between the lamp and the reactor and was measured before each experiment.

In a typical test, 5 mg of catalyst was uniformly dispersed on quartz fibers and placed in the reaction chamber. The reactor was evacuated, charged with CH3OH (200 μL, 4.94 mmol) and H2O (80 μL, 4.44 mmol), and then backfilled with Ar to 0.1 MPa. Unless otherwise specified, the reaction was carried out under full-spectrum irradiation from a 300 W Xe lamp (Beijing China Education Au-light Co. Ltd, CEL-HXF300) at an incident power density of 2.6 W cm–2 for 30 min, without external heating. The light intensity was measured at the reactor window/catalyst-bed position. Upon illumination, photothermal heating vaporized the liquid reactants and established a gas-solid reaction environment. Because the reaction was conducted in a sealed batch reactor rather than a continuous-flow reactor, the vapor-phase composition was governed by vapor-liquid equilibrium and evolved during the reaction; therefore, a fixed inlet partial pressure was not defined.

After the reaction, the reactor was cooled to room temperature, and the gas-phase products were analyzed using a Shimadzu GC-2014 gas chromatograph equipped with a 5 Å molecular sieve column, Ar carrier gas, and a thermal conductivity detector57. The H2 production rate was calculated from the amount of H2 produced, catalyst mass, and reaction time, and is reported as μmol gcat−1 s−1. Because this is a batch-mode operation, the conventional residence time used for continuous-flow reactors is not directly applicable.

Thermocatalytic methanol steam reforming reaction

Thermocatalytic methanol steam reforming experiments were conducted in the same stainless-steel batch reactor (70 cm3) using the identical catalyst loading (5 mg on quartz fibers), reactant quantities (200 μL CH3OH and 80 μL H2O), and product analysis protocol as the photo-driven experiments. The sole difference was the energy input: the Xe lamp was replaced by a heating jacket that enveloped the entire reactor body. After the reactor was sealed and purged with Ar to 0.1 MPa, the heating jacket was activated and set to the target temperature. Once the liquid reactants were fully vaporized and the system reached thermal equilibrium at the target temperature, the reaction was allowed to proceed for 30 min. After the reaction, the reactor was cooled to room temperature, and the gas-phase products were sampled through the septum-sealed sampling port and analyzed by GC using the same procedure as for the photo-driven experiments. For the temperature-matched control experiments (Supplementary Fig. 16), each thermocatalytic data point was set to the corresponding steady-state surface temperature measured under illumination at different Xe lamp intensities (Supplementary Fig. 13), enabling a direct comparison between photo-assisted and purely thermal reactions under matched macroscopic temperature conditions.

Statistical analysis

Catalytic measurements, including H2 production rates, kinetic isotope effects, and light-dependent rate measurements, were performed at least three times under identical conditions. The results are reported as mean ± sample standard deviation (s.d.). Error bars in Fig. 3b, c represent the s.d. from three independent measurements. For measurements that were performed only once, such as Hall effect measurements, photocurrent response, and each cycle in the 40-cycle stability test (Fig. 3e)—this is explicitly indicated in the corresponding figure captions. All raw data underlying the figures are provided in the Source Data file.

In-situ DRIFTS measurements

In-situ DRIFTS measurements were collected on a Nicolet iST 10 Fourier transform infrared spectrometer with a mercury cadmium telluride detector, and two CaF2 were used as windows. The catalyst was packed in a sample cell and sealed in the infrared (IR) reaction chamber under Ar follow. Then the chamber was purged with Ar follow for 30 min. During the in-situ characterization, a mixed solution of CH3OH and H2O was passed into the reaction cell at a molar ratio of 1:1 via Ar as carrier gas. Afterward, the chamber was purged with Ar gas to remove the free water and methanol vapors, and collected spectra at 280 °C under full-spectrum irradiation conditions (Light intensity: 2.6 W cm−2).

In-situ XPS measurements

In-situ XPS experiments were performed at the Photoemission Endstation (BL10B), in the National Synchrotron Radiation Laboratory (NSRL) in Hefei, China. A 300 W Xe lamp (Beijing China Education Au-light Co. Ltd, CEL-HXF300) was used as the light source to illuminate the sample via the quartz window.

DFT calculation

All first-principles computations were executed utilizing the Vienna Ab initio Simulation Package (VASP)58. The interactions between ions and electrons were treated via the projector augmented wave (PAW) approach, while exchange-correlation effects were modeled by the regularized r2SCAN meta-generalized gradient approximation (meta-GGA) functional. To correct the self-interaction error of localized electrons, the DFT + U method formulated by Dudarev et al. was employed, with an effective Hubbard parameter (Ueff = U − J) of 7.0 eV applied to the d orbitals of the In atoms. Furthermore, Grimme’s D3 dispersion correction with Becke-Johnson damping (D3-BJ) was incorporated to accurately capture van der Waals interactions.

To evaluate the catalytic consequences of LSPR-induced hot carriers on the MSR process, a static ‘hot electron’ environment was modeled by injecting one extra electron into the slab system with a compensating uniform background charge. This static approximation approach does not reproduce the full ultrafast LSPR dynamics. It is a widely adopted approach to mimic the accumulation of plasmon-induced charge carriers on the catalyst surface. It enables the evaluation of how these energetic electrons modulate the electronic structure of the In2O3 surface, thereby influencing the adsorption affinities of key intermediates and altering the activation barriers along the reaction pathway. A uniform compensating background charge was automatically applied to maintain the macroscopic neutrality of the periodic supercell and prevent electrostatic divergence.

The plane-wave cutoff energy was set to 500 eV. The Brillouin zone was sampled using the Monkhorst-Pack scheme with a k-point density of 0.04 Å−1. For the electronic self-consistent field (SCF) iterations, a convergence threshold of 10–5 eV was established. Geometry optimizations were deemed complete once the maximum residual force acting on any atom dropped below 0.02 eV/Å59. For the slab models, a vacuum layer of 15 Å was introduced to avoid spurious layer-to-layer interactions. In the phonon frequency calculations, a tighter SCF convergence criterion of 10–7 eV was adopted. Finally, thermodynamic free energy corrections were evaluated using the VASPKIT software package60. The three-dimensional crystal structural models presented in this work were generated utilizing the VESTA visualization application61.
