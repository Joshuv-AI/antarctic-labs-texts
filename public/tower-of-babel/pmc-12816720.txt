Optimized federated learning framework with RegNetZ and Swin-Transformer for multimodal pancreatic cancer detection1
Authors: Wei Ge, Vijay Govindarajan, Jing Yang, Manel Ayadi, Zaffar Ahmed Shaikh, Lizhu Li, Lip Yee Por, Nan Liu, Yanyang Tu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Pancreatic cancer is among the most lethal malignancies, marked by aggressive progression, late diagnosis, and limited screening methods, resulting in a five-year survival rate of less than 10%. Early-stage tumors are especially challenging to detect with standard CT and MRI imaging, leading to treatment delays and poor outcomes. While deep learning offers promise, centralized training in healthcare raises serious privacy and data-sharing concerns. This study introduces a federated learning framework that integrates RegNetZ and the Swin-Transformer for automated detection, subtype classification, and prognosis prediction from multimodal inputs, including CT, MRI, histology, genomic, and clinical records. The Swin-Transformer models long-range dependencies, whereas the lightweight RegNetZ backbone ensures efficient local feature extraction. A Hybrid Aquila–Grey Wolf Optimizer (HA-GWO) is incorporated to balance exploration and exploitation during hyperparameter tuning, providing faster convergence and reduced computational cost compared to conventional search strategies. The proposed framework, evaluated across 5–7 simulated client institutions, achieves 99.2% accuracy, 98.9% sensitivity, 99.0% precision, and 99.4% AUC, outperforming both CNN-only and transformer-only baselines. It further minimizes false positives and false negatives, improving both subtype classification (adenocarcinoma, neuroendocrine, cystic neoplasms) and prognosis risk prediction (high vs. low risk). Hyperparameter sensitivity analysis identifies a learning rate of 0.003 with a batch size of 64 as optimal. By enabling decentralized model training without raw data exchange, the system enhances diagnostic accuracy while preserving privacy, offering a practical solution for real-time pancreatic cancer detection in federated healthcare environments. The framework is scalable across medical institutions and supports precision oncology by enabling early and reliable diagnosis at low computational cost.

Introduction

Among cancers, pancreatic cancer is notoriously aggressive. Worldwide, it ranks as the seventh most lethal malignancy, claiming the lives of around half a million people annually1. Although early detection improves survival rates, the main reason for a poor prognosis is that small lesions measuring less than 2 cm are typically missed by routine imaging methods, leading to late-stage presentation2,3. The need for automated, highly sensitive equipment to detect minute radiological signs that humans might miss is highlighted by these persistent diagnostic gaps. According to research, deep learning (DL) and machine learning (ML) approaches have the potential to handle complex medical datasets more effectively than traditional analytical methods4. Medical image analysis relies heavily on neural networks (CNNs) for tasks such as cancer detection, segmentation, and classification, since these networks enable the extraction of hierarchical features from raw data5. Imaging aids diagnosis and staging when clinical biomarkers such as CA19-9 are used. Illness onset and tumor progression are associated with elevated CA19-9 values. This biomarker is used by many clinicians in their practice6. The TNM staging system plays a crucial role in determining the severity of the disease and the appropriate course of therapy7. It has been repeatedly shown that predictive accuracy is enhanced when tumor morphology, CA19-9 levels, and TNM staging are considered.

CAD systems using AI have proliferated. These methods improve consensus among observers by effectively identifying disease patterns in large imaging datasets8. Combining CNN-based feature extraction with XGBoost or SVM classifiers enhances diagnostic performance, particularly with multimodal data that includes clinical signs and imaging characteristics9. Combining VGG16-based deep features with XGBoost enhances prediction accuracy via representational depth and structured learning10. Despite these advancements, specific issues remain. Centralized data aggregation in most AI models makes collaboration across institutions difficult and raises concerns about patient privacy and data governance. Many previous studies used single-modality pipelines, limiting their ability to reveal local tumor form and broad contextual interdependencies between imaging and clinical modalities. Federated, multimodal pancreatic cancer detection is understudied, making it challenging to develop privacy-preserving systems that operate in practice. Grid search and standalone metaheuristic algorithms take a long time to converge and require substantial processing resources in federated settings with repeated communication rounds. Related work

The use of deep learning and artificial intelligence (AI) has significantly enhanced pancreatic cancer detection, segmentation, and classification, leading to more accurate diagnoses and reduced radiologist workload. Improving diagnostic reliability has been the focus of recent research, which has applied optimization strategies, hybrid models, and deep learning architectures. When it comes to pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC), radiological imaging remains crucial for diagnosis and treatment. However, conventional approaches still struggle to distinguish between tumor and treatment response. AI-powered CAD systems fill this void. One example is the improvement in tumor localization on contrast-enhanced CT scans enabled by deep convolutional neural network (DCNN) frameworks. At the same time, models that utilized Dependencies Computation (DC) Modules, Self-Adaptive Feature Fusion, and Augmented Feature Pyramid Networks beat the state-of-the-art approaches with an AUC of 0.945511. A convolutional neural network (CNN) trained on 3,494 CT images of pancreatic cancer and 3,751 normal pancreas images, with ten-fold cross-validation, achieved a binary classification accuracy of 95.47%, according to another study12. Though promising, these methods are impractical for real-world use due to their reliance on large, labelled datasets.

To further improve accuracy and efficiency, researchers have also investigated hybrid deep learning models. With sensitivities of 94.1% and specificities of 98.5%, a multi-scale segmentation-for-classification system was able to identify PDAC in CT scans13. In addition to outperforming existing methods in ROC and AUC testing, AX-Unet demonstrated superior segmentation accuracy on large annotated datasets by integrating attention mechanisms into the U-Net architecture14. Even improved speed has been achieved through enhancements to feature aggregation. Take ResNeXt models as an example. They outperform conventional ResNet in classification accuracy15 without increasing computational cost. At the same time, dermatological disorders have shown substantial improvement with multimodal masked autoencoder frameworks16.

More recent techniques integrate segmentation and classification pipelines. To facilitate pancreatic cancer detection, the TSADL-PCSC framework integrates W-Net segmentation, GhostNet-based feature extraction, and DESN classification17. According to18, PIS-Unet, which comprises the Inception, Squeeze-and-Excitation (SE), and Pyramid Pooling modules, enhanced feature extraction at various scales and achieved a Dice Similarity Coefficient (DSC) of 87.9% for specific tumor types. A study of explainable ensemble learning in optical coherence tomography (OCT) imaging found that models incorporating transfer learning improved classification accuracy and interpretability of results19. However, computational complexity, reliance on large datasets, and the risk of overfitting still pose problems for most techniques, making them unsuitable for real-time clinical applications, even with recent improvements.

Literature review summary.

The PANDA system classified pancreatic lesions using non-contrast CT images. A multicenter dataset of 6,239 patients validated a model trained on 3,208 patient occurrences. PANDA exceeded radiologist performance by 34.1% in sensitivity and 6.3% in specificity, with an AUC of 0.986-0.99620. Large-scale pancreatic cancer screening may benefit from deep learning models, but validation is needed to ensure generalizability. To improve efficiency, self-supervised learning has been used to identify pancreatic cancer without large labeled datasets. A novel pseudo-lesion segmentation (PS) approach improved the internal accuracy of CNN and Transformer models to 94.3% and 95.7%, respectively. Low expert annotation dependency and poor external validation performance indicate generalization challenges21. Meta-learning has recently improved deep learning models for pancreatic cancer. GF preprocessing, EPO segmentation, and MobileNet-based feature extraction were employed in the IDLDMS-PTC model. The method improved tumor categorization and diagnostic precision. Significant issues include computational complexity and reliance on large datasets22.

Radiomics-based deep learning pancreatic tumor classification. From 19,000 CT images, a CNN-Gaussian mixture model retrieved high-dimensional radiomic properties. By showing tumor shape, size, and growth, the model personalized treatment. This study focuses on pancreatic head tumors and should be expanded to include additional tumor sites23. Another study employed endoscopic ultrasound and AI to identify pancreatic tumors. ANNs, SVMs, and CNNs were evaluated in 10 AI models. The ANN model beat MRI (67%) and CT (53%) in identifying small pancreatic tumors with 93% sensitivity24. However, internal validity tests showed bias, necessitating larger, more diverse datasets. New deformable convolutional networks (DCN) segment pancreatic tumors more accurately. The adjustable receptive area helped the DCN segment achieve a Dice coefficient of 0.8725. Real-time medical applications face challenges because of lengthy training and computational complexity25,28.

Hybrid models that combine convolutional networks with transformers have shown promise for pancreatic cancer diagnosis. Similarly, weakly supervised transformer-based approaches have reached clinical-grade performance in glioma pathology, enabling both accurate diagnosis and biomarker discovery29. A recent study enhanced feature extraction using CNNs and Vision Transformers (ViTs) that employ global attention. This hybrid method outperformed CNNs in classification and allowed lesions to be more easily located. Training requires more time, and hyperparameters are very sensitive, which are two issues26. Finally, it seems that automated classification algorithms built on multi-scale attention-based deep networks might be helpful in pancreatic cancer diagnosis. A detection accuracy of 0.96 for PDAC was achieved in a study that used a hybrid U-Net model with attention mechanisms27. There is still a lack of validation in the actual world and biases in the datasets, even if accuracy has increased.

Recent research has shown that robust deep learning frameworks can handle challenging classification tasks across multiple domains. The HistoDX system in oncology is a prime example of this trend. CNNs trained on EfficientNetV2 achieved state-of-the-art performance in breast cancer diagnosis based on histopathology. The system reported accuracies above 97% and successfully addressed class imbalance through augmentation and weighted loss strategies30. Emotion recognition using transfer learning with pretrained architectures, such as Xception, has shown promising results, achieving accuracy rates of up to 98% on benchmark datasets. Deep models may be effective in several domains, as shown here31,32. Using robust backbone networks and sophisticated preprocessing pipelines improves prediction accuracy. It paves the way for the application of architectures such as RegNetZ and Swin-Transformer to multimodal pancreatic cancer diagnosis, according to these findings. The use of AI and deep learning has enhanced the identification of pancreatic cancer, as shown in Table 1. Fusion deep learning architectures, attention-enhanced segmentation networks, and CNN-based classifiers streamline tumor detection, separation, and classification. Large, diverse datasets, rapid processing, and situationally applicable models are still challenges. Transformers, meta-learning, and multimodal data fusion are promising areas for future study that might lead to faster pancreatic cancer detection and improved patient outcomes.

Identified gaps

Several significant issues persist despite the progress made by convolutional neural networks (CNNs), attention modules, hybrid architectures, and transformer models in AI-based pancreatic cancer detection. To begin, most approaches rely on centralized data collection, which complicates privacy and data-sharing issues and hinders collaboration across healthcare facilities. Secondly, most current models either ignore global contextual links or account for only local morphological features. The reliability of diagnosis is diminished since very few frameworks effectively combine the two. Thirdly, hyperparameter optimization often uses grid search or single optimizers. Although effective in federated learning contexts, these approaches may be costly and provide less-than-ideal convergence. It needs a computationally efficient framework that integrates multimodal operations, federated deep learning backbones, and robust optimization techniques to address these issues. The resolution of these issues is the goal of this work’s methodology.

PRISMA analysis of literature selection

This work conducted a comprehensive, transparent literature review in accordance with the PRISMA guidelines. The publications were retrieved from Scopus, IEEE Xplore, SpringerLink, and PubMed for the years 2019–2025. Combinations of keywords such as pancreatic cancer, deep learning, federated learning, tumor identification, and AI-based diagnostics were among those featured. Initial results from this search returned 1,236 results. After removing duplicates and reviewing the titles and abstracts to ensure relevancy, 247 articles were selected. Following the establishment of inclusion criteria (English-language publications, peer-reviewed articles, and AI-driven methodologies for pancreatic cancer detection, classification, or segmentation) and exclusion criteria (non-peer-reviewed publications, irrelevant cancer types, or the absence of AI methodology), a thorough full-text eligibility evaluation was conducted. Fifteen papers were retained using this method for further analysis and evaluation.

Proposed system model

With powerful computational models that securely and efficiently analyze extensive patient data, pancreatic cancer detection will improve. The study demonstrates a Federated Learning (FL)-based deep learning system (see Fig. 1). Swin-Transformer handles multiscale spatial dependencies, and RegNetZ extracts hierarchical features. This approach enables cancer detection in processed medical images. The decentralized platform eliminates the need for hospital data sharing during model training, thereby protecting patient privacy. Relationship-weighted categorical encoding, proportional imputation to fill missing values, and differential normalization increase feature consistency and input data accuracy in complicated preprocessing methods. To level the playing field, proportionate replication does not favor common data. HWFs employ mutual information and correlation to choose the best traits. Diagnostic relevance is maintained while reducing dimension. Federated learning distributes model training computation across clients to reduce data transfer and enable global optimization. Swin-Transformer draws hierarchical self-attention spatial connections for cancer classification. In contrast, RegNetZ retrieves local data from medical photos. Depending on diagnostic needs, a fully linked layer with sigmoid activation may predict binary or multiclass cancer. Aquila’s exploration and Grey Wolf’s exploitation enable HA-GWO to converge quickly at a low computational cost. To assess and optimize the final classification, recall, accuracy, precision, F1-score, the new Optimality-Exploration Ratio (OER), and the Convergence Stability Index are utilized. Data preparation, federated training, model optimization, and performance assessment are mathematically described.

The dataset helps develop and evaluate advanced machine learning models by spanning a wide range of patient demographics, clinical conditions, and diagnostic outcomes. Comprehensive dataset summary for reproducibility.

The dataset contains 221,347 records from various kinds of scans, as shown in Table 3. These records include 112,500 CT scans, 75,200 MRI scans, 22,800 clinical articles, and 10,847 genetic or molecular profiles. Patients represent a diverse range of ethnicities and ages, from 30 to 90 years old, and include Asians, Caucasians, African Americans, and Hispanics, among others. Adenocarcinoma, neuroendocrine tumors, and cystic neoplasms are major subtypes of pancreatic cancer, and the dataset contains individuals from all phases of the tumor necrosis model (TNM). This design accurately portrays the real world by employing a wide range of techniques and incorporating individuals from diverse backgrounds. To improve its learning capabilities, the model may now include both imaging and non-imaging variables.

Preprocessing of data

Several steps are performed to ensure that the dataset is clean, consistent, and appropriate for machine learning models once it has been organized and obtained. Data quality and the elimination of noise or extraneous information are the primary goals of the preprocessing phase, which aims to improve the model’s performance. The procedure begins with data normalization. It entails checking if the dataset falls within a specific range and scaling continuous attributes. An alternative to simply scaling data globally, Differential Normalization adjusts each attribute based on its distribution. Using this technique, characteristics with high volatility cannot dominate the model’s learning process. Classification using federated learning with RegNetZ and Swin-Transformer

Search algorithms for pancreatic cancer must be able to quickly and accurately decipher complex multimodal data. Federated learning framework for privacy-preserving classification

In federated learning, central servers receive only model updates (gradients), while client devices retain all data locally. To comply with HIPAA, this strategy keeps patient data and medical imaging on the local device. We split the dataset into seven portions, each stored on a different client. This research used RegNetZ and Swin-Transformer for federated learning for several reasons. First, federated learning keeps sensitive medical data on customers’ devices, reducing privacy risks associated with data centralization. Healthcare needs strict data privacy. Second, the RegNetZ-Swin-Transformer hybrid architecture gathers local details and global context. This layered technique is needed to diagnose complex medical images, since local features (such as tumor boundaries) and global context (such as pancreatic anatomy) are important. Finally, the Federated Learning Framework robustly, scalably, and privacy-preservingly identifies pancreatic cancer from multimodal medical data using RegNetZ and Swin-Transformer. The layered design improves classification accuracy by capturing both local and global characteristics. Given that it allows users to get practical training while still protecting data, this technique seems to have great potential for medical AI applications.

Tuning of parameters using hybrid metaheuristic algorithm

Finding the optimal hyperparameters is crucial for training deep learning models to perform challenging tasks, such as detecting pancreatic cancer. The three most important parameters for a stable and effective model are the learning rate, batch size, and regularization intensity. Our novel Hybrid Aquila-Grey Wolf Optimizer (HA-GWO) combines the exploration and exploitation capabilities of the Aquila Optimizer (AO)36 and the Grey Wolf Optimization (GWO)37 to address this problem. By combining them, we can make the search process more balanced and sidestep the issues that arise from using a single optimizer.

AO conducts a global search in the first stage. The method can quickly identify interesting regions through its stochastic update approach, providing a comprehensive perspective on the solution space. However, because of its exploration-centric focus, AO may tend to nuanced solutions. To get around this, the second phase uses GWO, which employs leader-follower dynamics to accelerate search in the best locations and improve local solutions. Performance evaluation metrics

The model’s performance in detecting pancreatic cancer is assessed using both classic and unique performance indicators. Although accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-score are often utilized in classification tasks, they may not accurately assess a model’s performance in complex medical imaging tasks38. The Optimality-Exploration Ratio (OER) and the Convergence Stability Index (CSI) assess classification performance and optimization, respectively. Performance is often measured by accuracy. The model’s success is measured as the percentage of correctly predicted events relative to the total number of events. In imbalanced datasets, accuracy may not be enough when the ruling class heavily influences the verdict–precision and memorization aid comprehension. A model’s recall and precision are the fractions of true positives and erroneous positives, respectively. Harmonically averaging memory and accuracy yields the F1 score. It handles unbalanced data well. Those are the metrics that show how effectively the model classifies cases; they do not show how well it optimizes, which improves performance. Our Optimality-Exploration Ratio (OER) evaluates the efficacy of optimization by contrasting the pursuit of solutions that optimize the objective function. As the OER shows, the optimization method gets the best results quickly while utilizing minimal computing resources. The exploration factor divided by the optimality factor is:17\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \text {OER} = \frac{\mathscr {P}_{\text {optimal}}}{\mathscr {P}_{\text {explore}} + \epsilon } \end{aligned}$$\end{document}The optimality factor, denoted as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathscr {P}_{\text {optimal}}$$\end{document}, may be obtained by comparing the initial response with the best possible one. The exploration factor shows the possibility of answers, or \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathscr {P}_{\text {explore}}$$\end{document}. Simulation results and discussion

The FRST method was tested with 44,269 pancreatic cancer images annotated using public health records. Differential normalization, relation-weighted categorical encoding, and proportional imputation were used to standardize the dataset across all client nodes. Every one of FL’s five clients used it to decentralise and anonymize their data. With an Adam optimizer, 64 batches, 50 epochs per local client, and a learning rate of 0.003, the model was trained before global model aggregation. To ensure convergence during adaptive hyperparameter tuning, Hybrid Aquila-Grey Wolf Optimization was employed. To avoid the model fitting the data too closely, the training procedure included regularization tactics such as dropout (0.3-0.5) and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$L_{2}$$\end{document} penalties, as well as stratified k-fold cross-validation and early stopping with a 10-epoch patience threshold. The processes were made more stringent by dividing customer records by patient. Data leakage would occur if the same individual’s samples were included in both the training and testing sets. A mighty machine, outfitted with an Intel Xeon 16-core CPU clocking up at 3.5 GHz, an NVIDIA RTX 3090 GPU with 24 GB VRAM, and 64 GB of RAM, was used to run the simulations. The client collaborated with FL to guarantee the safety and smooth operation of client-server communications. The model was trained using TensorFlow and PyTorch. Because of gradient compression, parameter switching is now feasible with less bandwidth.

Conclusion and future scope

This research presented an improved federated learning architecture for pancreatic cancer diagnosis that protects patient anonymity. To describe a hierarchical global context, the system uses the Swin-Transformer and RegNetZ, both of which are used for rapid local feature extraction. Subtype classification and prognostic prediction were both enabled by the hybrid design. It was also helpful in avoiding the pitfalls of centralized data exchange. The convergence performance and computational cost were both improved by using the Hybrid Aquila-Grey Wolf Optimizer (HA-GWO) to adjust the hyperparameters. The framework achieved 99.2% accuracy after training on five to seven federated clients using multimodal imaging and clinical data. All while maintaining scalability and patient privacy, this accuracy rate surpassed the best CNN, Transformer, and hybrid baselines. A single dataset was employed in the research, which is a limitation, as it does not adequately reflect all possible clinical circumstances. Regardless, the superior outcomes demonstrate that the proposed approach is practical and valuable.

Future work will explore validation on multi-center datasets, investigate advanced multimodal fusion strategies, and integrate explainable AI mechanisms to enhance clinical interpretability. Extending the framework to other cancer types also holds promise for building a scalable, privacy-preserving solution for precision oncology.
