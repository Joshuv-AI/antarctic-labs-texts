Circulating PCSK9 as a sex-stratified predictor in primary prevention of cardiovascular disease: Insights from a prospective cohort study in Chinese adults
Authors: Longhai Zhu, Yafang Zhu, Qiuyue Pu, Jing Duan, Xianfeng Yu, Mingzhe Du, Yuting Jia, Penghao Wang, Rongyan Zhang, Mingzhi Zhang, Tingting Kang, Yongjun Cao, Hao Peng, Jijun Shi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundWhile proprotein convertase subtilisin/kexin type 9 (PCSK9) has emerged as an effective therapeutic target in secondary prevention of cardiovascular disease (CVD), its role in primary prevention remains unclear. Therefore, we examined the prospective association between PCSK9 and CVD risks in the general population.MethodsIn the Gusu cohort, 1876 community members (mean age 57 years, 38% males) who were free of CVD in 2014 were included. Serum PCSK9 was measured by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay kits at baseline and CVD events were followed every 2 years through 2020. Associations between PCSK9 and CVD were examined using a Cox regression model in males and females, respectively.ResultsDuring a median follow-up of 6.7 years, 93 participants developed incident CVD. A higher level of serum PCSK9 at baseline was associated with a lower risk of CVD in males (HR=0.71, 95%CI: 0.52–0.96, P = 0.027 for log-PCSK9), but with a higher risk of CVD in females (HR=1.38, 95%CI: 1.03–1.84, P = 0.032 for log-PCSK9), independently of conventional risk factors. There was a significant heterogeneity between these two sex-specific associations (P = 0.002). Compared with participants with the lowest level of serum PCSK9, those with the highest level had a similar risk of CVD in males (HR=0.57, 95% CI: 0.27–1.20, P = 0.138), but a significantly increased risk in females (HR=2.78, 95%CI: 1.33–5.82, P = 0.006). Hypertension, dyslipidemia, and diabetes did not deliver a significant mediation effect on the association between serum PCSK9 and CVD (all P > 0.05).ConclusionsA higher serum PCSK9 at baseline predicted a higher future risk of CVD in the general female population. PCSK9 may be a candidate circulating biomarker for the risk of CVD in females.LAY SUMMARYLeveraging a prospective cohort study including 1876 community members, this article examined for the first time the association between serum PCSK9 at baseline and the incidence of CVD during a median 6-year follow-up. A higher serum PCSK9 at baseline predicted a higher future risk of CVD in the general female population. Our findings suggest that PCSK9 may be a candidate circulating biomarker for the risk of CVD in females.

Introduction

Proprotein convertase subtilisin/kexin type 9 (PCSK9) is a key regulator of lipid metabolism, promoting the degradation of hepatic low-density lipoprotein receptors (LDLR) and thereby impairing the clearance of low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) from the circulation [1]. Recent randomized controlled trials have demonstrated that PCSK9 inhibition lowered LDL-C and reduced the risk of cardiovascular disease (CVD), the leading cause of death and disease burden in China [[2], [3], [4]]. However, whether and how circulating PCSK9 per se contributes to first-event risk in individuals without prior cardiovascular disease remains a critical knowledge gap. Recently, the VESALIUS-CV trial extended this therapeutic paradigm by showing that the PCSK9 inhibitor evolocumab significantly reduced the risk of first major adverse cardiovascular events in high-risk primary prevention patients without prior myocardial infarction or stroke [5]. Importantly, exploratory analyses from that trial suggested a potentially greater treatment benefit among women, hinting at a sex-specific dimension to PCSK9 biology. These clinical findings raise a compelling parallel question: Does the prognostic value of the endogenous PCSK9 biomarker also vary by sex in a primary prevention setting?

In addition to LDL-C degradation, PCSK9 may contribute to the development of CVD through other etiological pathways, e.g., endothelial dysfunction [6,7], vascular inflammation [8], oxidative stress [9], glucose metabolism [10], and blood pressure regulation [11]. To date, epidemiological evidence regarding the association between circulating PCSK9 levels and incident cardiovascular disease in general populations is inconsistent [[12], [13], [14], [15]]. Most of them mainly included patients who had already suffered from CVD. Only one study examined but failed to find a significant association between PCSK9 and CVD in the general population [16]. Sex may be a probable source of these inconsistent findings due to estrogen-dependent regulation of PCSK9 expression [17,18]. However, a systematic, sex-stratified evaluation of PCSK9 as a risk predictor in a community-based, primary prevention cohort is lacking, especially in the Chinese population. Therefore, leveraging a well-characterized, prospective cohort, we aimed to investigate the sex-specific associations between baseline serum PCSK9 concentrations and the long-term risk of incident cardiovascular disease. Our goal was to determine whether PCSK9 serves as an independent, sex-specific biomarker for early risk stratification, thereby providing a potential biological link to the differential therapeutic responses observed in trials such as VESALIUS-CV [5] and informing more personalized primary prevention strategies.

Methods

Study participants

The Gusu cohort is a community-based prospective longitudinal study designed to track cardiovascular health trajectories and identify associated risk determinants among middle-aged and elderly Chinese adults. Comprehensive methodological details regarding study design, survey methods, and laboratory techniques are available in prior publications [19,20]. Briefly, this prospective study was initiated in 2010 (Phase I) by enrolling 2706 community members aged over 30 years free of CVD in eight communities residing in Gusu district, a traditional but economically developed district of Suzhou, China. All surviving participants were invited to participate in a follow-up examination in 2014 (Phase II) and followed every two years through 2020. The protocols of the Gusu cohort study were approved by the Soochow University Ethics Committee. Written informed consent was obtained from all study participants.

In the current study, given that serum PCSK9 was only available in Phase II, we studied 2470 participants who attended this clinical visit as the baseline participants (Fig. 1). Measurement of serum PCSK9

Fasting venous blood specimens were collected following a standardized 8-hour overnight fasting protocol. Serum was isolated through centrifugation and subsequently preserved at −80 °C until laboratory assay. All samples were processed in a duplicate assay using commercial human PCSK9 Quantikine ELISA Kits (catalog: DPC900, USA R&D Systems, Inc). A standard curve was constructed and from which PCSK9 concentrations of unknown samples were determined. Intra- and inter-assay coefficients of variation were <5.43% and <4.77%, respectively.

Collection of risk factors for CVD

Information on demographic characteristics, lifestyle factors, and medical history was collected by trained staff using standard questionnaires in Chinese. Current smoking was defined as having smoked at least 100 cigarettes in their entire life, having smoked cigarettes regularly, and smoking currently. Current drinkers were defined as individuals who had consumed any alcoholic beverage on ≥ 12 occasions in the last year. Body weight and height were measured with participants standing and wearing light clothing and no shoes. BMI (kg/m2) was calculated by dividing body weight (kg) by the square of height (m2). WC was measured at 1 cm above the umbilicus. Blood pressure was assessed three times at 30-second intervals using a calibrated mercury sphygmomanometer according to a standard protocol [21], after the participants had been resting for at least five minutes. The first and fifth Korotkoff sounds were recorded as systolic blood pressure (SBP) and diastolic blood pressure (DBP), respectively. The means of the three records were used in subsequent analyses. Hypertension was defined as SBP ≥ 140 mmHg and/or DBP ≥ 90 mmHg and/or having taken antihypertensive drugs within the last two weeks [22]. Fasting plasma glucose (FPG) and blood lipids, including total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TG), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), were measured using standard laboratory methods. Diabetes was defined as FPG ≥ 7.0 mmol/L and/or taking hypoglycemic drugs [23].

Follow-up and definition of CVD events

In our study, CVD events included nonfatal coronary heart disease (CHD), nonfatal stroke, and death from any cause related to CVD. All participants were followed up every two years, either by phone calls or in person, by staff from the local community health service institutes. The staff reviewed the hospital records and completed a standard event form when a new CHD, stroke, or death was identified during follow-up. Based on the event form, the final diagnosis was made by an endpoint review committee. The date of each event was ascertained from either the initial point of diagnosis or a death certificate. If a person experienced a stroke and CHD in succession, the first event was counted as the CVD event.

Statistical analyses

To carefully study the potential role of PCSK9 in the cardiovascular system, as shown in Fig. 1, we first examined the associations of serum PCSK9 with CVD incidence, as well as its metabolic factors including lipids, glucose, and blood pressure, followed by a mediation analysis to assess the direct effect of PCSK9 on CVD. Due to sex heterogeneity in serum PCSK9, the sex-specific association between serum PCSK9 and CVD was also examined. Baseline characteristics were presented for the total participants, males, and females. Sex differences were compared using the student’s t-test, the Wilcoxon rank-sum test, or the chi-square test as appropriate. A natural logarithmic transformation was applied to improve the normality of the serum PCSK9 distribution (skewness was reduced from 26.05 to −0.59), and the resulting values (log-PCSK9) were used in downstream analyses.

Cross-sectional analysis of the associations between PCSK9 and metabolic factors

To help delineate the role of PCSK9 in CVD, we first examined its associations with CVD-related metabolic factors, including hypertension, dyslipidemia, and diabetes. The Spearman correlations among log-PCSK9, SBP, DBP, FPG, TC, TG, LDL-C, and HDL-C were plotted to visualize whether serum PCSK9 correlated with metabolic factors. To examine the association between serum PCSK9 and these metabolic markers, we constructed a median regression model in which each metabolic marker was the dependent variable, log-PCSK9 was the independent variable, adjusting for age, sex, education level, cigarette smoking, alcohol consumption, and BMI. Additionally, a binary logistic regression model was constructed with serum PCSK9 (continuous log-PCSK9 or categorical tertiles) as the independent variable, adjusting for age, sex, education level, cigarette smoking, alcohol consumption, and BMI, to separately examine the associations of serum PCSK9 with hypertension, dyslipidemia, and diabetes.

Prospective analysis of the associations between PCSK9 and CVD

The cumulative incidence of CVD across tertiles of serum PCSK9 levels was estimated and compared using the Fine-Gray test to account for competing risks of non-CVD deaths. To examine the association between serum PCSK9 and the risk of CVD, we constructed a Cox regression model where time to CVD (in years) was the dependent variable, baseline serum PCSK9 (continuous log-PCSK9 or categorical tertiles) was the independent variable, adjusting for age, sex, education level, cigarette smoking, alcohol consumption, BMI, hypertension, dyslipidemia, and diabetes.

Sensitivity analysis

To evaluate the contribution of PCSK9 to the risk of CVD, we calculated the etiological fraction based on the estimates of the association between PCSK9 and CVD. To test the probable mediation effect of metabolic factors on the association between PCSK9 and CVD, using the R package "mediation", we constructed a mediation model in which CVD was the outcome, log-PCSK9 was the predictor, and each metabolic factor was the mediator, adjusting for conventional risk factors listed above. To assess how robust the association between PCSK9 and CVD is to potential unmeasured or uncontrolled confounding, we calculated the E-value [24]. All statistical analyses were performed using the R software, version 4.3.3. A two-sided P value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Association between serum PCSK9 and CVD

After a median of 6.7 years of follow-up, 93 participants developed new CVD events, 24 participants died from non-cardiovascular causes, and 116 participants were lost (follow-up rate of 93.82%). The rate of loss of follow-up was comparable between males and females (6.12%vs. 6.13%, P = 0.991). Despite blood pressure, no statistically significant differences in all other characteristics listed in Table 1 were observed between participants followed and those lost during follow-up. The cumulative incidences of CVD according to tertiles of serum PCSK9 at baseline are shown in Fig. 2. In total participants, the cumulative incidence was 4.98%, 4.02%, and 5.94% in individuals with increasing tertiles of serum PCSK9, respectively, but without a significant group difference (P = 0.299). A similar phenomenon was found in males (P = 0.105), but in contrast, there was a significant difference in the cumulative incidence of CVD across the tertiles of serum PCSK9 in females (2.58%vs. 4.64% vs. 6.69%, P = 0.024).Fig. 2The cumulative incidence of CVD according to tertiles of serum PCSK9.The P-values for the comparison of the cumulative incidence of CVD across tertiles of serum PCSK9 were 0.299, 0.105, and 0.024 in total participants, males, and females, respectively.

After adjusting for conventional risk factors such as age, sex, education level, cigarette smoking, alcohol consumption, BMI, hypertension, dyslipidemia, and diabetes, serum PCSK9 seemed to be associated with the incidence of CVD, but with inconsistent directions in males and females (Table 3). A higher level of serum PCSK9 at baseline was associated with a lower risk of CVD in males (HR=0.71, 95%CI: 0.52–0.96, P = 0.027 for log-PCSK9), but with a higher risk of CVD in females (HR=1.38, 95%CI: 1.03–1.84, P = 0.032 for log-PCSK9). There was a significant heterogeneity between these two sex-specific associations (P = 0.002). Results of sensitivity analysis

Based on the strength of the association between PCSK9 and CVD, we calculated the corresponding etiological fraction. About 64% of the CVD risks in women with an elevated level of PCSK9 (the highest tertile) could be attributable to PCSK9 per se. Hypertension, dyslipidemia, and diabetes did not deliver a significant mediation effect on the association between serum PCSK9 and CVD (all P > 0.05, Supplementary Table S2). These results indicated that PCSK9 may be associated with the risk of CVD through mechanisms beyond these metabolic factors. The effect estimate for the association between log-PCSK9 and CVD in females produces an E-value of 2.10, which means only an unmeasured confounder with associations with both PCSK9 and CVD stronger than 2.10 can negate this observed association. This high E-value suggests that our results are unlikely to be overturned by residual confounding.

Discussion

This study for the first time systematically examined the sex-specific association between circulating PCSK9 levels and the risk of incident CVD in a community-based prospective cohort of Chinese adults. Our key finding was that elevated serum PCSK9 at baseline predicted an increased risk of future CVD events in females. This association was independent of traditional metabolic risk factors, including LDL-C, and metabolic factors did not mediate this relationship. These results provide novel epidemiological evidence for the sex-specific role of PCSK9 in primary prevention, and importantly, they resonate with and extend recent clinical trial findings.

The female-specific association observed in our cohort is further supported by recent trial evidence, providing a coherent link between epidemiological risk prediction and therapeutic responsiveness. In VESALIUS-CV [5], females demonstrated a numerically greater relative risk reduction with PCSK9 inhibition than men in high-risk primary prevention patients without prior myocardial infarction or stroke. This is consistent with our finding that the association between elevated circulating PCSK9 and incident CVD was only significant in females, rather than males. This finding suggested that females with an elevated level of PCSK9 may be more sensitive to PCSK9-lowering therapy, complementing prior reports of sex heterogeneity in PCSK9-associated risk of CVD [13,25,26]. Moreover, the circulating level of PCSK9 helps delineate a primary-prevention subgroup whose clinical profile resembles the event-free yet high-risk population enrolled in VESALIUS-CV [5], offering a practical tool for risk stratification. By linking a measurable biomarker to both heightened baseline risk and enhanced treatment responsiveness, our findings highlight the dual role of PCSK9 as a predictive factor and an actionable therapeutic target in women, although the modification of circulating PCSK9 in the cardiovascular protective effect of PCSK9 inhibition has not been studied. In light of the significant association between PCSK9 and CVD in males reported by most observational studies, the conflicting association observed in our study still requires further replication. Possible explanations include residual confounding, medication effects, competing risks, and limited counts of male events, which may account for our conflicting findings.

Although the association between PCSK9 and CVD was not found after adjusting for conventional risk factors in some large cohorts [27,28], the persistent association between PCSK9 and CVD risk in females after adjustment for LDL-C suggests biological contributions beyond traditional lipid pathways, consistent with long-term observational evidence [25]. Unmeasured hormonal factors, including menopausal status and use of menopausal hormone therapy, may also act as important modifiers of PCSK9 levels and cardiovascular risk in women. These factors may partially explain the observed sex-specific associations and the differences compared with the WHI cohort [28]. In our cohort, this association remained significant after comprehensive adjustment for LDL-C and conventional cardiometabolic risk factors—including age, education, smoking, alcohol consumption, BMI, hypertension, dyslipidemia, and diabetes—with women showing a fully adjusted HR of 1.38 (95% CI, 1.03–1.84) per log-unit increase in PCSK9 over a median follow-up of 6.7 years. Our finding is also supported by genetic evidence. For example, a genetic study reported a sex-related biological heterogeneity in the association between variants influencing PCSK9 activity and CVD event rates [26], indicating that intrinsic differences in PCSK9 regulation may contribute to the stronger association observed in women. Experimental and clinical data indicate that PCSK9 expression is strongly influenced by hormonal regulation—particularly estrogen-dependent suppression—which may explain its heightened prognostic value in postmenopausal women [29,30]. Notably, the association between a higher PCSK9 and a higher risk of CVD observed in our study provides mechanistic plausibility that women with higher circulating PCSK9 may be more responsive to PCSK9-targeted therapy. In addition to hormonal effects, PCSK9 has been shown to promote vascular injury through several non-lipid mechanisms, including impaired endothelial nitric oxide bioavailability and β-cell dysfunction [10,31], as well as LDLR-independent activation of vascular smooth muscle cells and macrophage-driven inflammation [[32], [33], [34]]. PCSK9 may contribute to CVD development through mechanisms beyond lipid metabolism. Our mediation analysis also supports this hypothesis. We speculate that PCSK9 may be a therapeutic target for early prevention of CVD in individuals free of dyslipidemia. However, the association between PCSK9 and CVD in such populations, including those free of diabetes, is poorly studied. Collectively, these lipid-independent pathways provide biological support for PCSK9 as a mechanistically plausible and clinically relevant risk indicator in women. Notably, previous large prospective studies such as the WHI cohort and the HUNT study [27,28], did not observe a significant association between circulating PCSK9 and incident cardiovascular disease after multivariable adjustment. Several factors may contribute to these apparently discordant findings. First, differences in study populations may play an important role, including ethnic background, baseline cardiovascular risk profiles, and age structure. Our cohort consisted of a community-based Chinese population, which may differ from Western cohorts in lipid profiles, environmental exposures, and cardiometabolic risk distribution. The ethnic difference in PCSK9 levels has been observed in an epidemiologic study, where plasma PCSK9 levels were significantly higher in African Americans than in Caucasians [35]. Second, sex-specific effects may have been diluted in prior studies that included mixed populations or focused primarily on overall associations without detailed sex-stratified analyses. In contrast, our study specifically evaluated sex-stratified associations and identified a significant interaction between PCSK9 and sex. Third, differences in analytical strategies, including model adjustment and exposure modeling, may also contribute to inconsistent findings across studies. These discrepant findings suggest that the relationship between PCSK9 and cardiovascular risk may be context-dependent and potentially modified by sex and population characteristics.

Strengths of this study include its prospective design, relatively long-term follow-up, community-based sample of the general population, and rigorous adjustment for a wide range of potential confounders. A key strength is the timely connection made between our epidemiological finding of sex-specific risk prediction and the contemporary clinical trial evidence of sex-differentiated treatment benefit, enhancing the translational relevance of our work. Some limitations should be acknowledged. First, a single baseline PCSK9 measurement prevented us from observing the dynamic changes during follow-up in our study. Some participants may take lipid-lowering drugs, but such information was unavailable in our study. However, the disease history of dyslipidemia has been collected in our study. The association between PCSK9 and CVD did not change substantially after excluding participants with a history of dyslipidemia. We expect that statins may not drive our findings. Future studies should assess longitudinal PCSK9 trajectories and test early PCSK9-lowering interventions, alongside lipid targets, to optimize prevention strategies. Second, the multiple testing burden resulting from sex-stratified analysis, dose-response analysis, and mediation analysis may increase the risk of chance findings. In fact, only one hypothesis was tested in our study and these analyses were conducted to evaluate the robustness of the association between serum PCSK9 and CVD. Third, only middle-aged and elderly Chinese adults were included in our study. The generalizability of our findings to other populations with different ethnicities, age groups, or regions should be of caution. Fourth, the observational nature precluding causal inference and the relatively small number of events in men may limit the power to detect associations in that subgroup. Fifth, the levels of PCSK9 were measured in serum samples stored at −80 °C for at least 5 years in our study. The stability of serum NEP under prolonged storage remains unaddressed. However, we believed that the values of serum PCSK9 obtained could reflect the relative differences among our participants because all serum samples were stored under identical conditions. Sixth, PCSK9 levels differ by menopausal status and can vary with endogenous estrogen changes [36]. However, we have no data on menopausal status, hormone therapy, or sex steroid measures in our study. The role of estrogen-dependent regulation of PCSK9 expression in the sex-specific association between PCSK9 and CVD risks needs to be studied.

Conclusion

In conclusion, circulating PCSK9 is an independent, sex-specific predictor of incident CVD in a primary prevention setting, with a stronger association in females than males. This finding expands our understanding of the role of PCSK9 in CVD prevention and management, suggesting its sex-specific value in risk stratification. Future research should aim to validate this sex difference and risk threshold in larger populations and to explore the underlying biological mechanisms in depth. From a clinical translation perspective, measurement of serum PCSK9 may help identify individuals, women in particular, who are more likely to benefit from PCSK9 inhibitory therapy. Simultaneously, our results reinforce the rationale for targeting PCSK9 itself — not merely as an intermediate step to lower LDL-C, but as a direct therapeutic target for CVD.
