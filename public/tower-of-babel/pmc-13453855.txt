Application of an innovative aortic occlusion band technique in minimally invasive cardiac surgery for congenital heart disease
Authors: Heqi Zhang, Qiqi Shi, Hua Cao, Haoju Dong, Taibing Fan, Weijie Liang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo evaluate the safety and effectiveness of an innovative aortic occlusion band technique in minimally invasive surgery for congenital heart disease.MethodsThis retrospective, single-center cohort study included 1963 pediatric patients with ventricular septal defect who underwent right vertical infra-axillary thoracotomy between January 2018 and December 2024. Of these, 1097 were treated using the innovative occlusion band technique (IOB group), whereas 866 underwent conventional aortic crossclamping (CC group). All procedures were performed by a single surgeon. Adequacy of aortic occlusion in the IOB group was assessed under low-flow conditions according to circumferential band-tightening morphology and the adequacy of antegrade cardioplegia-induced cardiac arrest, rather than by any single pressure value alone.ResultsAmong the 1963 eligible patients, 1097 (55.88%) were assigned to the IOB group and 866 (45.12%) to the CC group. No intraoperative conversion to median sternotomy occurred in either group. No patient in the IOB group required conversion to conventional aortic crossclamping, and no postoperative aortic complications, including aortic dissection or bleeding, were observed. Operative time, cardiopulmonary bypass time, and aortic crossclamp time were significantly shorter in the IOB group than in the CC group (all P < .001). In addition, incision length was significantly shorter in the IOB group than in the CC group (2.2 [2.0, 2.5] cm vs 2.7 [2.3, 3.5] cm, P < .001). No significant between-group differences were observed in postoperative length of hospital stay, duration of mechanical ventilation, or intensive care unit stay.ConclusionsApplication of the innovative occlusion band technique improves mobilization of the aortic root and may enhance effective use of the limited working field, thereby facilitating surgical manipulation. Importantly, this technique was also associated with a significantly shorter incision length compared with the conventional crossclamp technique, further supporting its minimally invasive advantage. These benefits were achieved without an increased risk of incomplete aortic occlusion, hematoma, or aortic dissection. Overall, the IOB technique represents a safe and effective adjunct for minimally invasive congenital cardiac surgery.Key Words: minimally invasive cardiac surgery, aortic occlusion band, ventricular septal defect, right vertical infra-axillary thoracotomy

Aortic occlusion band enhances efficiency in minimally invasive congenital heart surgery.

Central Message

Innovative aortic occlusion band placement and tightening around the ascending aorta during minimally invasive congenital cardiac surgery, reducing rigid instrument crowding in a small incision.

Perspective

This study presents an innovative aortic occlusion band technique for minimally invasive congenital heart surgery. It enhances aortic root mobilization and increases operative space, addressing limitations of traditional crossclamping. This technique provides a safe, efficient, reproducible solution without increasing complication rates, offering a valuable adjunct for pediatric cardiac surgery.

With the increasing adoption of minimally invasive concepts in congenital heart surgery, multiple minimally invasive surgical approaches have been developed in clinical practice, including partial lower sternotomy,1 right submammary incision,2 anterolateral thoracotomy,3 posterolateral thoracotomy,4 right vertical infra-axillary thoracotomy,5 and transverse intercostal incision.6 These approaches offer advantages in terms of cosmetic outcomes, preservation of thoracic development, and postoperative recovery. However, regardless of the minimally invasive approach adopted, conventional aortic clamps remain widely used in congenital cardiac surgery. Because of their relatively bulky design, conventional aortic clamps occupy a substantial portion of the operative field, and their introduction through an infra-axillary approach significantly increases instrument obstruction within the limited incision and reduces the effective use of the available working field, thereby increasing technical complexity in minimally invasive congenital cardiac surgery.

In contrast, minimally invasive cardiac surgery for adult valvular disease has led to the development of several dedicated aortic occlusion devices, including transthoracic aortic cross-clamping,7 detachable aortic clamping systems,8 and endoaortic balloon occlusion systems,9 which allow more flexible aortic occlusion in deep and restricted operative fields. However, to date, no aortic occlusion device has been specifically designed for minimally invasive congenital cardiac surgery in pediatric patients. Differences in vascular diameter, thoracic volume, and surgical access pathways between pediatric and adult patients further amplify the limitations of conventional aortic clamps in this population.

On this basis, the present study is the first, to our knowledge, to apply an innovative aortic occlusion band (innovation occlusion band, IOB) in right infra-axillary mini-incision surgery for pediatric patients weighing ≤30 kg and to perform a systematic comparison with conventional aortic crossclamping. The IOB was designed to minimize obstruction of the operative field and to reduce rigid instrument occupation and improve effective use and continuity of the usable working field within the limited incision, thereby facilitating the operability of the infra-axillary approach. Using a large retrospective cohort, in this study we comprehensively evaluated the safety, effectiveness, and impact of the IOB technique on surgical efficiency, with the aim of providing a more suitable aortic occlusion strategy for minimally invasive pediatric congenital cardiac surgery.

Methods

Study Population

Institutional review board approval was obtained (2024; Lun Shen 64; approved on August 14, 2024), and written informed consent, including consent for publication of anonymized clinical data, was obtained from patients' legal guardians. Between January 2018 and December 2024, consecutive patients weighing ≤30 kg who underwent surgical repair of ventricular septal defect (VSD) at Central China Fuwai Hospital of Zhengzhou University were enrolled, and clinical data were retrospectively analyzed. This weight threshold was chosen to focus on the subgroup in whom intrathoracic working space is most restricted during right vertical infra-axillary thoracotomy and in whom the technical advantages of reducing instrument crowding are expected to be most evident, rather than to define the upper limit of applicability of the occlusion band technique.

The exclusion criteria were defined as follows: (1) patients with VSD associated with other cardiac anomalies, except atrial septal defect, patent foramen ovale, patent ductus arteriosus, pulmonary stenosis, right ventricular outflow tract obstruction, subvalvular membrane, or mitral regurgitation; (2) patients who underwent pulmonary artery banding because of severe pulmonary hypertension or Swiss-cheese VSD; (3) patients with Eisenmenger syndrome who were not candidates for VSD; (4) patients with multiple VSDs underwent hybrid procedures; and (5) patients who had previously undergone VSD repair at other institutions and required reoperation because of residual defects. Although the occlusion band technique was also applied in selected patients weighing >30 kg, sometimes with a double-band strategy, these patients were managed with more individualized occlusion strategies because of greater thoracic depth and variable exposure conditions; some cases therefore required detachable aortic crossclamps. For this reason, patients weighing >30 kg were not included in the present comparative analysis.

Surgical Technique

A 2- to 3-cm longitudinal incision was created at the fourth intercostal space along the midaxillary line. The subcutaneous tissue was dissected, and portions of the serratus anterior and intercostal muscles were divided. After temporary cessation of ventilation, the pleural cavity was entered through the fourth intercostal space. A wound protector was inserted, and a rib retractor was applied to gently expand the incision. The right lung was retracted and protected using moist gauze to prevent injury.

The pericardium was opened longitudinally approximately 2 cm anterior to the right phrenic nerve and suspended using traction sutures. The space between the ascending aorta and pulmonary artery was carefully dissected, with care taken to avoid coronary artery injury. After adequate mobilization, a tape passer was introduced between the aorta and pulmonary artery to guide placement of the occlusion band (Video 1; Figures 1 and 2). The occlusion band was externalized through the tape passer and secured with a tourniquet and vascular clamp, forming circumferential traction.

Pericardial traction sutures were placed around the superior vena cava, right atrium, inferior vena cava, and ascending aorta to optimize cardiac exposure. After systemic heparinization, purse-string sutures were placed in the ascending aorta and the superior and inferior vena cava, followed by cannulation with an aortic cannula, a right-angled metal cannula for the superior vena cava, and a flexible, stylet-guided cannula for the inferior vena cava. After initiation of cardiopulmonary bypass, caval snares were secured to achieve bicaval occlusion.

Aortic root purse-string sutures were placed for cardioplegia delivery. Before cardioplegia infusion, pump flow was intentionally reduced to a very low level. During low-flow conditions, the occlusion band was gradually tightened in a stepwise and controlled manner. Adequacy of aortic occlusion was assessed comprehensively according to the circumferential tightening morphology of the band and the subsequent adequacy of antegrade cardioplegia delivery and cardiac arrest, rather than by reliance on any single instantaneous root pressure value alone (Figure 3; Video 1). To minimize potential interference with antegrade cardioplegia, the aortic cannula was preferentially directed toward the cephalad side and the aortic root needle toward the caudal side, leaving approximately 5 mm between the occlusion band and the root needle whenever feasible. If interference with antegrade cardioplegia delivery or unreliable occlusion was encountered, conventional aortic crossclamping was used immediately. Cardioplegia was administered via the aortic root to achieve cardiac arrest, after which the cardioplegia needle was removed to optimize the operative field. The right atrium was opened, and left heart venting was established through the foramen ovale.

Perimembranous and muscular VSDs were exposed via the tricuspid valve, whereas doubly committed subarterial defects were approached through a longitudinal incision in the main pulmonary artery. After inspection, VSDs were closed using appropriately sized bovine pericardial patches with continuous suturing. Concomitant procedures were performed as indicated, including atrial septal defect closure, mitral valve repair, pulmonary valvuloplasty, right ventricular outflow tract relief, and patent ductus arteriosus ligation. The foramen ovale was closed with continuous sutures, followed by thorough deairing and restoration of circulation.

After parallel perfusion, cardiopulmonary bypass was discontinued uneventfully, and modified ultrafiltration was performed. Intraoperative transesophageal echocardiography (TEE), performed before weaning from cardiopulmonary bypass, confirmed the absence of residual shunt and moderate or severe valvular regurgitation. TEE was routinely used for intraoperative assessment of intracardiac repair but was not the primary method used to titrate the degree of band tightening. After meticulous hemostasis, a right pleural drain was placed through the sixth to seventh intercostal space along the mid- to posterior axillary line. After bilateral lung recruitment, the intercostal space was closed, and the subcutaneous tissue and skin were closed using absorbable sutures. The patient was then transferred to the intensive care unit for further management. All defects were repaired using bovine pericardial patches.

During rewarming, TEE was performed to reassess residual shunt and tricuspid valve function. Follow-up evaluations included electrocardiography, radiography of the chest, and transthoracic echocardiography at 1, 3, 6, and 12 months postoperatively and annually thereafter.

Occlusion Band

The occlusion band is primarily composed of polyethylene terephthalate polyester (Dacron), a material widely used in cardiovascular surgical applications. The device has a flat, braided, tape-like structure and is sterilized by irradiation. The flat braided polyethylene terephthalate configuration provides an appropriate balance of tensile strength and flexibility, allowing secure aortic occlusion while maintaining ease of handling through a limited operative field. The occlusion band is available in multiple specifications, with widths of 1, 2, 3, and 4 mm and lengths of 60, 70, and 80 cm, enabling selection according to patient size and surgical requirements. To further assess potential aortic wall injury, an experiment in animals was performed comparing the occlusion band with a conventional crossclamp using bilateral common carotid artery occlusion in rabbits. Gross examination showed that the compression mark produced by the conventional crossclamp was more pronounced than that produced by the occlusion band, whereas endoscopic assessment revealed no obvious difference in vascular wall change between the 2 methods. These findings suggested that the occlusion band did not cause more apparent vascular injury than conventional clamping.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.1.5). Continuous variables were tested for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Normally distributed data are presented as mean ± standard deviation and were compared using one-way analysis of variance. Non-normally distributed data are presented as median (interquartile range) and were compared using the Kruskal-Wallis test. Categorical variables are expressed as number (percentage) and were compared using the χ2 test or Fisher exact test, as appropriate. Operative time was defined as the interval from skin incision to completion of skin closure. All tests were 2-sided.

Results

Baseline Characteristics and Perioperative Outcomes

There were no significant differences in preoperative baseline characteristics between the IOB and CC groups, as shown in Table 1. Compared with the conventional aortic crossclamp (CC) group, the IOB group demonstrated significant advantages in multiple operative efficiency parameters. Operative time was significantly shorter in the IOB group than in the CC group (101.35 ± 6.95 minutes vs 109.63 ± 9.89 minutes, P < .001). Cardiopulmonary bypass time (53.00 [45.00, 64.00] minutes vs 61.00 [56.00, 62.00] minutes, P < .001) and aortic crossclamp time (32.00 [26.00, 41.00] minutes vs 37.00 [34.00, 52.00] minutes, P < .001) were likewise significantly reduced in the IOB group. In addition, incision length was significantly shorter in the IOB group than in the CC group (2.2 [2.0, 2.5] cm vs 2.7 [2.3, 3.5] cm, P < .001), indicating that use of the IOB may permit a further reduction in incision size while maintaining procedural safety and effectiveness. No significant differences were observed between groups in postoperative length of hospital stay, duration of mechanical ventilation, or intensive care unit stay (all P > .05).

Animal Experiment: Methods and Results

To further assess the histologic safety of the IOB, a standardized experiment in animals was performed in which the bilateral common carotid arteries were occluded using either the IOB or a conventional aortic crossclamp. Arterial specimens were harvested immediately after the procedure for systematic evaluation.

Gross examination demonstrated that both methods achieved reliable vascular occlusion; however, the degree of external compression marks differed noticeably between groups (Figure E1). The conventional aortic crossclamp produced deeper compression marks, whereas the IOB resulted in the mildest impression, suggesting less direct mechanical compression on the outer vessel wall.

Gross adventitial appearance of the artery after different occlusion methods. A, Gross appearance after occlusion with the Innovation Occlusion Band. B, Gross appearance after occlusion with the conventional aortic crossclamp.

Further electron microscopic examination demonstrated preserved vascular wall integrity in both groups, with no evidence of intimal necrosis, medial disruption, or thrombus formation (Figure E2). Overall, neither occlusion method produced definite injury at the histopathologic or ultrastructural level. Nevertheless, gross examination showed that the IOB caused the least vessel compression, suggesting that it may be more locally tissue-friendly than the conventional crossclamp.

Comment

Aortic crossclamping remains an indispensable component of most cardiac surgical procedures performed under cardiopulmonary bypass. However, CCs are often suboptimal for minimally invasive cardiac surgery because limited incision length restricts visualization and working space, requiring operations to be performed within confined fields. In such settings, instruments occupying the operative field play a critical role in facilitating safe and efficient manipulation. To address these limitations, several alternative aortic occlusion designs have been introduced. In 2009, Zhang and colleagues10 reported an aortic occlusion device incorporating a superelastic shape-memory alloy as a pressure-limiting component. Subsequently, in 2010, Glauber and Karimov8 developed a detachable aortic crossclamp to improve ease and safety during minimally invasive procedures.

Minimally invasive cardiothoracic surgery has gained increasing popularity as the result of its advantages, including reduced postoperative pain, faster recovery, shorter hospital stay, and improved cosmetic outcomes.11, 12, 13 A common feature of these approaches is the use of small incisions, which inherently limit operative exposure unless the incision is enlarged. However, patients and families often prefer the smallest possible incision to achieve an “invisible scar,” creating a balance between optimal exposure and minimal invasiveness. The continued reliance on conventional aortic clamps—characterized by large size and fixed angulation—has become a major factor limiting further adoption of minimally invasive approaches. In contrast to adult minimally invasive valve surgery, pediatric congenital cardiac surgery lacks dedicated aortic occlusion devices because of smaller vessel diameters, limited thoracic space, and shorter access pathways, further amplifying the limitations of conventional clamps.

In the present study, application of the IOB effectively addressed these technical challenges. First, the circumferential band structure conforms closely to the aortic root, placing the ascending aorta in a tractable rather than rigidly fixed state. This feature allows flexible multidirectional traction of the ascending aorta to optimize exposure in different surgical scenarios, including perimembranous VSD, doubly committed subarterial VSD, and cases with associated pulmonary stenosis, and is also applicable to partial lower sternotomy. In addition, this traction advantage may facilitate exposure during ligation, division, and suture closure of a patent ductus arteriosus and may be particularly helpful in lesion-specific settings. In doubly committed subarterial VSD, rightward traction of the aortic root can improve exposure of the main pulmonary artery, thereby facilitating repair through a longitudinal pulmonary arteriotomy. This advantage is especially relevant in tetralogy of Fallot repair, in which enlargement of the main pulmonary artery or intervention on the pulmonary valve is frequently required. Thus, the practical value of the IOB lies not only in reducing clamp obstruction but also in maintaining the aortic root in a relatively mobile and tractive state that may improve lesion-specific exposure. For technical safety, we preferentially positioned the antegrade cardioplegia needle on the caudal side of the ascending aorta and maintained an interval of approximately 5 mm from the occlusion band whenever feasible; if any interference with antegrade delivery or inadequate occlusion had been encountered, immediate conversion to a conventional cross-clamp would have been undertaken.

Second, the principal advantage of the IOB should not be interpreted simply as a dramatic enlargement of the absolute operative field. Rather, compared with a conventional cross-clamp, the IOB reduces rigid instrument occupation within the limited incision and improves the effective use and continuity of the usable working field. In this sense, Figure 3 should be understood as a conceptual illustration of improved space use rather than a formal quantitative volumetric measurement. We agree that the operative field remains constrained by the incision itself, the proximal portion of the band, the superior vena caval cannula, and surrounding mediastinal structures. In actual operative settings, however, the superior vena caval cannula can usually be positioned flat against the wound protector and along the edge of the incision, rather than standing upright within the central operative window as depicted schematically. Therefore, its occupation of the central working area is considerably less than may be suggested by the illustration. Under these conditions, the IOB avoids a bulky, rigid conventional crossclamp traversing and occupying the central portion of the limited incision, thereby preserving better continuity of the usable working zone and facilitating surgical manipulation. Importantly, this technical advantage was also reflected in incision length. Compared with the conventional crossclamp group, the IOB group had a significantly shorter incision length, suggesting that reduced rigid instrument occupation within the limited access route may permit further minimization of the skin incision without compromising surgical exposure or procedural safety. This finding is clinically meaningful, because even a modest reduction in incision length may further improve cosmetic outcomes, lessen chest wall trauma, and reinforce the minimally invasive nature of the infra-axillary approach.

Third, placement of the occlusion band between the aorta and pulmonary artery using a tape passer requires only a few straightforward steps, thereby reducing the technical difficulty of deep infra-axillary manipulation. In our experience, the learning curve for this maneuver appears relatively short. Collectively, these features make the IOB particularly well suited to pediatric minimally invasive cardiac surgery.

The clinical and histologic advantages observed with the IOB are consistent with previously reported biomechanical evidence. Finite element analysis by Hajizadeh Farkoush and colleagues14 demonstrated that conventional scissor-type clamps generate high stress concentrations near the hinge due to nonparallel clamping, increasing the risk of intimal and medial injury. In contrast, devices using parallel clamping with compliant contact surfaces significantly reduce von Mises stress and achieve a more uniform stress distribution across the vessel wall. Consistent with this concept, our animal experiment showed that the gross compression mark produced by the conventional crossclamp was more pronounced than that produced by the occlusion band, whereas endoscopic assessment revealed no obvious difference in vascular wall change between the 2 methods. Consistent with these mechanisms, the IOB group demonstrated significantly shorter operative, cardiopulmonary bypass, and aortic crossclamp times than the CC group (all P < .001), indicating improved surgical efficiency. Importantly, however, these efficiency gains should not be attributed solely to an increase in operative space, but rather interpreted within a multifactorial framework that includes reduced clamp obstruction, improved lesion-specific exposure, optimized manipulation, and accumulated surgical experience. Despite different occlusion strategies, single-attempt aortic occlusion was successful in 100% of cases in both groups, with no occlusion-site bleeding or conversion to sternotomy, confirming the reliability of the IOB. Postoperative complication rates—including incision infection, neurological events, severe arrhythmia, low cardiac output syndrome, and reoperation—were low and comparable between groups, consistent with published reports of infra-axillary VSD repair.15,16

The design philosophy of the IOB aligns closely with current trends in minimally invasive cardiac surgery, emphasizing reduced instrument obstruction and maximized operative exposure. Previous studies have identified instrument crowding and restricted working space as major barriers to wider adoption of infra-axillary approaches in children.17 This issue is particularly relevant in the context of the increasing standardization of right axillary incision approaches for congenital open-heart surgery, for which recent expert consensus has provided detailed recommendations regarding patient selection, incision design, cannulation strategy, and organ protection.18 By reducing rigid obstruction within the limited incision and improving effective space utilization and continuity of the working field, the IOB may facilitate expansion of minimally invasive indications to patients with right ventricular outflow tract obstruction or more complex anomalies. Nevertheless, the independent clinical significance of this technical advantage warrants further validation using more objective spatial and procedural assessments.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this was a single-center retrospective study and is therefore subject to inherent selection bias. Second, follow-up duration differed between groups, necessitating future matched analyses or extended follow-up to assess long-term valve function, aortic root morphology, and growth adaptation. Third, although the learning curve for IOB deployment is relatively short, standardized training and procedural protocols will be essential to ensure reproducibility across centers. Fourth, the improvement in working field use was inferred primarily from procedural characteristics and conceptual illustration rather than from direct quantitative spatial measurement.
