Comment on “A multicenter pragmatic implementation study of AI-ECG-based clinical decision support software to identify low LVEF: Clinical trial design and methods”
Authors: E. Jayanthi, P.Y. Mallikarjun
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

We read with great interest the study by Lopez-Jimenez et al. [1], describing the design of the AIM ECG-AI trial, a multicenter cluster-randomized implementation study evaluating point-of-care AI-ECG clinical decision support for detecting a low ejection fraction. Embedding the intervention directly into routine EHR workflows across five geographically distinct health systems, rather than relying on email-based alerts as in the predecessor EAGLE trial, represents a meaningful step toward testing this technology under conditions that approximate real-world deployment.

The primary endpoint cannot separate algorithmic accuracy from increased testing volume

The detection of LVEF ≤40% by echocardiography depends entirely on the clinician ordering an echocardiogram. If exposure to the best practice alert simply prompts more echocardiogram orders overall, independent of whether the underlying AI signal is accurate, the intervention arm could show a higher detection rate purely through increased testing volume rather than through correctly flagging patients with reduced ejection fraction [2]. The diagnostic yield, the proportion of ordered echocardiograms that confirm low LVEF, is listed only as an exploratory endpoint rather than a co-primary comparison alongside detection [3]. Without this pairing, a positive primary result would be open to two very different interpretations: one reflecting the genuine algorithmic value and the other reflecting the volume effect of alert-driven broader ordering. The trial, as designed, cannot distinguish between them from the primary analysis alone.

Heterogeneous predicate performance data leaves the expected effect size for this population unspecified

The cited evidence supporting the algorithm's accuracy spans studies with sensitivities ranging from 26.9% to 98.8% and specificities from 44.8% to 97.4%, across populations as different as dyspneic emergency department patients, cardiac intensive care unit admissions, and Chagas disease cohorts, each using varying LVEF thresholds [4]. The trial's own population, asymptomatic outpatients with no known LVEF history, most resembles only one of these rows. The manuscript does not specify which predicate sensitivity and specificity estimates were used to project the expected detection rate or power the primary comparison, leaving readers unable to judge whether the trial is adequately powered for the accuracy this specific algorithm is likely to achieve in an unselected outpatient population rather than in the enriched or symptomatic cohorts that produced the higher end of this range.

The planned high-adopter/low-adopter comparison is confounded by whatever drives engagement itself

The manuscript proposes a comparison of outcomes between high- and low-adopting clinicians within the intervention arm to interpret a potentially null intent-to-treat result. Because software adoption is not randomized, any difference between these subgroups is confounded by the clinician characteristics that drive engagement in the first place, workload, technological comfort, or baseline practice patterns, any of which could independently affect echocardiogram-ordering behavior. This comparison cannot substitute for a causal estimate of the intervention's effect on engaged clinicians.

Taken together, these considerations do not diminish the value of testing an EHR-integrated AI-ECG tool under pragmatic multisite conditions. Pairing the primary endpoint with ordering-volume data, specifying the performance estimate underlying the statistical design, and treating the adopter comparison as hypothesis-generating rather than causal, would strengthen the interpretability of the forthcoming results.
