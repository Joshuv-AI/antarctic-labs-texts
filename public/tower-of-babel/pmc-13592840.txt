Gut microbiota-mediated modulation of circadian rhythms: Physiological and molecular impacts of dietary composition and feeding time
Authors: Yen-Chun Koh, Chun-che Chang, Min-Hsiung Pan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCircadian rhythms regulate fundamental aspects of physiology and behavior. The disruption of circadian rhythms has been associated with metabolic disorders, cardiovascular disease, obesity, and impaired immune function. Feeding behavior and gut microbiota represent two essential nodes within this regulatory network, as both are highly responsive to environmental cues such as diet composition and sleep-wake cycles. Research has shown that high-fat diets, excessive fructose, and chronic alcohol consumption perturb peripheral circadian rhythms and impair metabolic pathways, contributing to obesity and insulin resistance. Conversely, dietary interventions such as time-restricted feeding and macronutrient-specific entrainment can restore rhythmic gene expression and prevent metabolic pathologies. The gut microbiota plays a central role in mediating these effects through its diurnal oscillations in community composition and metabolite production, particularly short-chain fatty acids, which act as systemic cues for host circadian clocks. Disruption of microbial rhythmicity, whether through irregular eating schedules, jet lag, or environmental pollutants, can exacerbate circadian misalignment and metabolic dysfunction. Recent findings highlight a bidirectional relationship between the host circadian system and the microbiota, underscoring their interdependence in maintaining metabolic homeostasis and gastrointestinal integrity. Although preclinical models have produced mechanistic insights, the translation to humans remains limited by methodological variability and incomplete characterization of signaling pathways. This review synthesizes the current knowledge on diet-microbiota-circadian interactions and discusses potential therapeutic strategies, including dietary modulation and natural products, to mitigate circadian misalignment and its associated health risks.Keywords: Circadian disruption, Circadian nutrition, Gene oscillation, Metabolic homeostasis, Microbial metabolites

1. General background

Circadian clocks orchestrate daily rhythms in physiology and behavior by regulating gene expression through transcriptional-translational feedback loops. This intrinsic periodicity occurs in both invertebrates and vertebrates [1,2]. Disruption of circadian rhythms is being increasingly recognized as contributing to various health problems, including metabolic syndromes [3,4], cardiovascular disease [5], and obesity [6]. In humans, epidemiological studies have shown that shift workers, who frequently experience disruption of circadian rhythms, are at elevated risk for developing these conditions. Likewise, individuals who regularly sleep less than 6 h per night often exhibit maladaptive dietary behaviors, including irregular meal timing, frequent snacking, low vegetable consumption, and a preference for high-fat, high-sodium foods [7]. These behaviors not only indicate a poor diet but are also linked to weight gain and metabolic disturbances. Among the physiological systems regulated by circadian rhythms, feeding behavior and the gut microbiota are particularly responsive to temporal cues such as sleep patterns, nutrient composition, and meal timing. In this review, we explore the dynamic interplay between the circadian clock and the gut microbiota at both physiological and molecular levels, highlighting how dietary factors modulate this interplay and influence both health and disease.

2. Environmental entrainments of circadian rhythms

Circadian rhythms in peripheral tissues are tightly regulated by environmental cues, including feeding behavior [8]. Although light is the primary entrainment signal for the central clock located in the suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN), non-photic factors, particularly dietary patterns, have emerged as significant modulators of peripheral biological clocks. In modern societies, where irregular eating schedules and energy-dense diets are increasingly common, such dietary stress has become a major contributor to circadian misalignment [9]. This is particularly concerning in light of the rising global incidence of obesity and metabolic syndrome, both of which are closely linked to metabolic dysfunction.

At the physiological level, the timing and composition of meals directly influence metabolic rhythms, including fluctuations in hormone secretion, energy expenditure, and insulin sensitivity. Disruption of these rhythms can impair nutrient absorption and disturb energy balance. At the molecular level, genes involved in carbohydrate and lipid metabolism, such as those regulating glucose uptake and insulin signaling, are classified as clock-controlled genes. Aberrant expression or mistimed activation of these genes, resulting from circadian disruption, can lead to impaired glucose tolerance and other metabolic disturbances, underscoring the essential role of circadian regulation in maintaining metabolic health [10].

2.1. The influence of diet composition on peripheral circadian rhythms

Dietary composition, particularly a high-fat diet (HFD), has been shown to influence peripheral circadian rhythms. Homma et al. (2016) demonstrated that an 80-day HFD intervention in mice not only induced obesity but also significantly increased serum insulin levels across most circadian phases compared to controls [11]. At the molecular level, the hepatic expression of core clock genes, including basic helix-loop-helix ARNT-like 1 (Bmal1), circadian locomotor output cycles kaput (Clock), and cryptochrome 1 (Cry1), was markedly reduced at multiple time points. In contrast, the nuclear receptors Rev-erbα (Nr1d1) and Rev-erbβ (Nr1d2) displayed phase-altered rhythmicity in response to a HFD [11]. A HFD attenuates the chromatin recruitment capacity of the core transcription factor CLOCK:BMAL1, thereby reducing its DNA-binding ability and consequently impairing the regulation of downstream circadian clock gene expression [12] (Fig. 1).

This dysregulation extends beyond the core clock machinery. Synthesis of key metabolic regulators, such as acetyl-CoA carboxylase beta (Accβ) and carbohydrate response element-binding protein (Chrebp), in HFD-fed mice was significantly reduced during the dark phase in [11]. These changes appear to be mechanistically linked to the suppression of Bmal1 and Clock, suggesting a pathway through which a HFD perturbs hepatic circadian regulation. Such disruptions may contribute to metabolic pathologies, including hyperinsulinemia and hepatic steatosis. At the behavioral and systemic levels, Bravo et al. (2014) reported that HFD-induced chronodisruption in rats led to a reversal of their natural activity patterns: instead of being nocturnally active, HFD-fed rats showed increased activity during the daytime and reduced activity at night [13]. These behavioral shifts were accompanied by significant weight gain and elevated plasma triglyceride levels, suggesting a broader disruption of energy homeostasis. Taken together, these findings indicate that a HFD impairs circadian regulation at both the molecular and behavioral levels, contributing to the development of metabolic disorders.

Time-restricted feeding (TRF) has emerged as a promising intervention to counteract the effects of a HFD on circadian rhythms. In a pivotal study by Hatori et al. (2012), mice fed a HFD either ad libitum or within an 8-h daily feeding window showed markedly different outcomes [14]. TRF prevented excessive weight gain and improved systemic metabolic flexibility, as indicated by a higher respiratory exchange ratio. These improvements were associated with enhanced physical activity and increased energy expenditure. At the molecular level, HFD-fed mice showed disrupted expression of hepatic markers, including increased levels of phosphorylated cyclic AMP response element-binding protein (phospho-CREB) and reduced levels of ribosomal protein S6, indicative of disturbed rhythms. TRF reversed many of these effects: hepatic levels of phosphorylated acetyl-CoA carboxylase (phospho-ACC) and AMP were increased, reflecting activation of AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK) signaling. Notably, TRF restored and amplified rhythmic expression of core clock genes, specifically in HFD-fed mice, an effect that was absent in controls.

TRF also improved hepatic glucose metabolism, promoted lipolysis and fatty acid β-oxidation, and provided protection against hepatic steatosis, liver injury, and hyperleptinemia. Some of these protective effects appear to be mediated by BMAL1, whose downstream targets, such as uridine monophosphate synthase (Umps) and thymidine kinase 1 (Tk1), are involved in purine and pyrimidine metabolism, and were regulated in a TRF-dependent manner [14]. Furthermore, TRF enhanced bile acid biosynthesis, reduced hepatic inflammation, and increased thermogenic energy expenditure in brown adipose tissue. TRF also modulated the expression of Rev-erbα target genes involved in lipid metabolism, including fatty acid synthase (Fasn), sterol regulatory element-binding protein 1c (Srebp1c), 3-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-CoA synthase 2 (Hmgcs2), and cholesterol 7 alpha-hydroxylase (Cyp7a1). These findings underscore the potential of TRF to restore circadian and metabolic homeostasis in animals fed a high-fat diet.

Many of the aforementioned genes are core clock genes (CCGs) that exhibit robust circadian oscillations. BMAL1 plays a central role in regulating hepatic glycogen metabolism. Udoh et al. (2018) investigated its function in the context of chronic alcohol consumption [15]. Although prolonged alcohol intake generally reduces hepatic glycogen content in a phase-dependent manner, rhythmic fluctuations persist under normal conditions. However, in hepatocyte-specific Bmal1 knockout (HBK) mice (Alb-Cre+/−; Bmal1flox/flox), this rhythmicity was abolished by alcohol feeding, whereas control littermates retained normal rhythms. Although total glycogen levels at individual time points were not significantly different between HBK and control mice, the diurnal expression of genes involved in glycogen metabolism was markedly altered. For example, the rhythmic expression of glycogen synthase 2 (GYS2), the rate-limiting enzyme in glycogen synthesis, was entirely absent in HBK mice. These changes were corroborated at the protein level by alterations in the abundance and phosphorylation status of glycogen synthase (GS). Moreover, alcohol feeding disrupted the circadian expression of other glucose metabolism-related genes, including glucokinase (Gck), pyruvate kinase (Pklr), and glucose transporter 2 (Glut2), with similar abnormalities observed in HBK mice. These findings suggest that chronic alcohol consumption impairs the synthesis of hepatic glycogen and that the loss of BMAL1 further exacerbates this impairment, highlighting its essential role in maintaining hepatic metabolic rhythms.

2.2. Meal timing as an entrainment cue for peripheral circadian rhythms

The timing of food intake plays a pivotal role in regulating metabolism and entraining circadian rhythms, particularly in peripheral tissues. Yasumoto et al. (2016) demonstrated this by examining the metabolic consequences of feeding mice a high-fat/high-sucrose diet during either the light (inactive) or dark (active) phase [16]. After one week of daytime feeding (misaligned with the mice’s innate nocturnal activity), the mice showed significantly greater body weight gain, increased food intake and efficiency, and greater accumulation of white adipose tissue (WAT) compared to their night-fed counterparts. Furthermore, the expression of hypothalamic genes involved in appetite regulation differed markedly between the two feeding schedules.

Daytime feeding also disrupts circadian patterns in physiological and hormonal parameters, including body temperature and the plasma levels of corticosterone, insulin, leptin, and ghrelin [17]. These changes are accompanied by shifts in blood and liver metabolic markers. At the molecular level, genes involved in glucose and lipid metabolism exhibited inverted expression patterns in the liver, whereas in white adipose tissue, they showed increased expression without a clear phase shift, and skeletal muscle was comparatively less affected [16]. Consistent with these metabolic changes, core clock gene expression was markedly altered in the liver and WAT, suggesting that the timing of meals acts as a zeitgeber (“time-giver”) of peripheral circadian clocks. These findings highlight the potential of time-restricted feeding as a strategy to counteract diet-induced metabolic disturbances through peripheral clock entrainment, rather than through the central circadian pacemaker in the SCN.

In support of this hypothesis, a separate study found that while caloric restriction alone extended the lifespan of mice by approximately 10%, time-restricted feeding aligned with the natural circadian rhythm, specifically nocturnal feeding, extended the mouse lifespan by up to 35% [18]. This remarkable benefit may stem from enhanced synchrony between metabolic processes and circadian clock gene expression, reinforcing the suggestion that feeding schedules, not just caloric content, are critical to health and longevity. Additional evidence from the study by de Goede et al. (2018) suggests that ultradian feeding patterns disrupted lipid-related gene expression in the liver, brown adipose tissue, and skeletal muscle. Interestingly, the SCN clock appeared largely unaltered by feeding schedules, whereas peripheral clocks responded robustly to changes in caloric intake [19]. Wehrens et al. (2017) observed that altered meal timing did not affect SCN-regulated hormones such as melatonin and cortisol but did significantly delay Per2 mRNA expression in WAT and alter postprandial blood glucose levels [20]. These findings further support the hypothesis that feeding acts predominantly on peripheral clocks rather than the central clock.

While animal studies have consistently shown that feeding can serve as a zeitgeber, a cue that entrains biological rhythms, the evidence in humans remains inconclusive. This uncertainty largely stems from methodological limitations in human studies, such as insufficient control over confounding variables, including light exposure, physical activity, and sleep patterns. Nonetheless, research on humans continues to explore the role of meal timing and dietary composition in circadian rhythms [21]. The impact of dietary timing on circadian rhythms has been demonstrated in invertebrate models. Katewa et al. (2016) showed that dietary restriction in Drosophila melanogaster increased the amplitude of oscillation for several core clock genes, including timeless (tim), par domain protein 1ε (Pdp1ε), vrille (vri), and clock (Clk), compared to flies fed ad libitum. Moreover, dietary restriction significantly extended the lifespan of fruit flies, an effect attenuated in tim or period ( per) mutants and abolished under constant light, indicating a direct role for functional circadian clocks in mediating dietary benefits [22]. Dietary restriction in Drosophila also promoted enhanced fat metabolism and improved survival under acute starvation. Interestingly, the synthesis and breakdown of triglycerides were both elevated by dietary restriction and further amplified by overexpression of tim, suggesting that circadian regulation of metabolic genes contributes to resilience under nutrient stress and potentially to ageing. Further insights into nutrient-specific entrainment were provided by Ikeda et al. (2018), who demonstrated that hepatic clocks could be reset by dietary protein or cysteine intake through glucagon signaling and increased production of insulin-like growth factor 1 (IGF-1). This illustrates how specific macronutrients can function as entrainment cues that influence peripheral clock function [23]. The latter study also demonstrated that the liver circadian clock is directly modulated by food-derived metabolic hormones, including insulin, ghrelin, glucagon, and oxyntomodulin, a gut hormone that activates both the glucagon receptor and the glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor. Oxyntomodulin was shown to induce the expression of period circadian regulator 1 (Per1) and Per2 via the glucagon receptor-cyclic AMP (cAMP)-CREB signaling axis.

Importantly, these effects were observed in the liver but not in the SCN, reinforcing the suggestion that metabolic hormones serve as systemic synchronizers of peripheral, but not central, circadian clocks. In addition, several hepatic genes related to carbohydrate metabolism were differentially expressed following oxyntomodulin treatment [24]. Taken together, these findings emphasize the critical role of meal timing and dietary composition in entraining peripheral circadian rhythms. By modulating metabolic and hormonal signals, food serves as a systemic cue that can synchronize organ-specific clocks, offering promising avenues to improve metabolic health and longevity by aligning nutritional strategies with circadian biology.

2.3. The effects of excessive fructose and alcohol consumption on circadian rhythms

Fructose, together with sucrose and high-fructose corn syrup, is widely used in sweetened beverages, processed foods, and typical Western-style diets. However, excessive intake of fructose has been increasingly recognised as a factor in metabolic disorders, including non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), insulin resistance, and obesity [25]. At the molecular level, fructose perturbs circadian regulation within hepatocytes. In vitro studies using alpha mouse liver-12 (AML-12) hepatocytes demonstrated that fructose treatment disrupted the rhythmic expression of Bmal1 and altered the timing of oscillations in Per1 and Clock mRNA. Specifically, fructose reduced the amplitude of Per1 and Bmal1 expression cycles, thereby delaying Clock rhythmicity. Mechanistic analyses revealed decreased phosphorylation ratios of AMPK (p-AMPK/AMPK) and acetyl-CoA carboxylase (p-ACC/ACC), coupled with elevated LIPIN1 protein levels, leading to enhanced fatty acid and triglyceride synthesis. Concurrently, reduced expression of peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor alpha (Pparα) indicated a metabolic shift away from fatty acid oxidation, further promoting lipid accumulation in fructose-treated hepatocytes [26].

Alcohol consumption is another significant disruptor of circadian regulation and metabolism. Chronic alcohol intake is a well-established risk factor for alcoholic fatty liver disease (AFLD) or hepatic steatosis. The effect occurs largely through the altered expression of genes governing lipogenesis and β-oxidation [27]. Using the Lieber-DeCarli liquid diet model, Zhou et al. (2014) reported that four weeks of alcohol feeding not only induced hepatic steatosis but also significantly altered the expression of core clock genes in the liver, including Npas2, Per2, and Per3. Remarkably, about 8% of the differentially expressed genes in the liver belonged to the circadian clock or clock-controlled gene categories, underscoring the widespread circadian disruption induced by ethanol exposure [27]. Along with the delayed expression patterns, alcohol-fed mice also exhibited damped oscillations and reduced amplitude of hepatic Per2 expression. In particular, such effects were absent in the suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN), highlighting the tissue-specific vulnerability of peripheral clocks to alcohol. One proposed mechanism involves a persistent decrease in the NAD/NADH ratio across the circadian cycle, which may impair NAD-dependent regulation of Npas2 and Clock. This metabolic–circadian interplay illustrates how alcohol disrupts redox homeostasis, destabilizing peripheral clock gene expression.

The detrimental impacts of alcohol on circadian regulation also extend to the gastrointestinal tract. Swanson et al. (2011) showed that circadian rhythm genes are essential for maintaining intestinal barrier integrity [28]. Alcohol exposure increased epithelial permeability of Caco-2 cell monolayers, as measured by transepithelial electrical resistance (TEER) and fluorescein diffusion assays. Knockdown of PER2 or CLOCK using siRNA attenuated these alcohol-induced barrier defects, demonstrating their functional role. Rats subjected to four weeks of daily alcohol gavage exhibited increased urinary sucralose excretion, a marker of impaired intestinal barrier function. Furthermore, PER2 expression was significantly reduced in both duodenal and proximal colonic mucosa of alcohol-treated animals. Collectively, these findings suggest that alcohol compromises gut barrier integrity, at least in part by disrupting circadian gene expression in peripheral tissues.

Beyond the direct metabolic and intestinal effects, the timing of meals and alcohol consumption can synergistically exacerbate circadian misalignment. Such disruption has been linked to increased risk of colon cancer [29]. Bishehsari et al. (2020) found that restricting food access to the light phase (when nocturnal rodents normally fast), combined with 15% alcohol intake for 12 weeks, significantly altered the oscillatory pattern of PER2 in both colonic and hypothalamic tissues. While food composition had minimal effect on the SCN, pronounced dyssynchrony emerged in peripheral tissues, accompanied by disrupted biomarkers of intestinal barrier integrity [30].

Mechanistic studies have demonstrated that mistimed feeding and alcohol together reduce the levels of short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), diminish the population of SCFA-producing bacteria, and impair butyrate synthesis. This leads to decreased colonic regulatory T cells, heightened pro-inflammatory responses, and reduced signaling through SCFA-responsive G-protein-coupled receptors (GPRs). Interestingly, although alcohol alone produced strong inflammatory effects, its combination with mistimed feeding induced a broader disruption of immune regulation and gut microbiotacircadian interactions.

Evidence from human studies has corroborated these findings. Specifically, a 2020 study by Swanson et al. demonstrated that plasma concentrations of acetate, propionate, butyrate, and total short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) exhibited clear diurnal oscillations in healthy individuals [31]. However, these rhythmic patterns were absent in habitual alcohol consumers and night-shift workers. Strikingly, even a single episode of alcohol intake in non-regular drinkers caused a measurable decline in total SCFA levels, a factor that is correlated with increased colonic permeability. Although nightshift work did not significantly reduce total SCFA concentrations, it disrupted the daily rhythms of workers, demonstrating that circadian misalignment alone alters the dynamics of microbial metabolites, while alcohol exacerbates these effects by further weakening the integrity of the gut barrier [31].

Other environmental entrainment factors, such as cigarette smoking, remain under investigation. Smoking is associated with altered sleep patterns and changes in chronotype. Interestingly, one study paradoxically suggested that social jet lag, a misalignment between biological and social clocks, could buffer against smoking-related depressive symptoms [32]. Nonetheless, irregular eating habits and poor diets are capable of triggering sleep disturbances, which in turn increase susceptibility to addictive behaviors and mood disorders. These bidirectional interactions may create a self-reinforcing cycle of circadian disruption, metabolic dysfunction, and behavioral maladaptation (Table 1).

3. Circadian rhythms and microbiota

As previously discussed, nearly all peripheral tissues are governed by the central circadian pacemaker in the suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN). However, dietary factors, including nutrient composition and feeding schedules, influence the intrinsic clocks of peripheral organs, particularly those of the gastrointestinal (GI) tract (Fig. 2) [33]. To anticipate and adapt to daily environmental changes, the GI tract follows circadian cycles that regulate essential functions such as gastric acid secretion, nutrient absorption in the small intestine, and colonic motility [34].

Coupling between circadian control, diet/feeding time, gut microbiota, and environmental disruptors (content summary)

The suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN) coordinates peripheral clocks, including those of the gastrointestinal (GI) tract, that regulate the circadian rhytms in gastric acid secretion, nutrient absorption, and colonic motility. Diet composition and feeding schedules modulate GI clocks and independently reshape the gut microbiota. The gut microbiota exhibits diurnal oscillations in community structure, relative abundance, and metabolites; altering the timing of feeding causes shifts in microbial structure/function, and rhythmic feeding can restore microbial oscillations even in clock-deficient animals. Microbial functions include amino-acid synthesis/degradation, bile-acid transformation, digestion of complex carbohydrates, short-chain fatty-acid production, lipid absorption, and immune modulation; host factors (intestinal mucus, antimicrobial peptides, glucocorticoids) reciprocally shape the microbial ecology. Jet lag, shift work/sleep disruption, and environmental pollutants perturb microbial rhythms and are associated with altered peripheral clock-gene expression (observed in mice and humans) and adverse metabolic outcomes.

The intestinal microbiota plays a symbiotic role in these processes, contributing to nutrient processing and host metabolism [35]. In turn, the composition and diversity of the microbiota are shaped by dietary inputs and feeding habits [36,37]. Microbial contributions include amino acid synthesis and degradation [38], bile acid transformation [39], digestion of complex carbohydrates [40], short-chain fatty acid (SCFA) production [41], lipid absorption [42], and modulation of the immune response [43]. Host factors reciprocally regulate microbial ecology through the secretion of intestinal mucus [44], glucocorticoid release [45], and production of antimicrobial peptides [46]. Collectively, these bidirectional interactions create a dynamic communication axis between circadian rhythms and the gut microbiota.

3.1. Gut microbiota

The gut microbiota is a metabolically active ecosystem composed of over 15,000 bacterial strains, exhibiting diurnal rhythmicity reflected in community composition and relative abundance [47]. While diet and long-term eating habits are major determinants of the microbiota’s structure, the timing of food intake itself has emerged as a critical regulator of nutrient homeostasis. The microbiota thus acts as a key intermediary between environmental cues and host metabolic regulation [14,48].

Parkar et al. (2019) proposed that circadian cues, including the light-dark cycle, sleep-wake state, and feeding schedules, modulated microbial communities [33]. Abnormal light exposure disrupts the composition of the microbiota and increases disease risk [49]. Thaiss et al. (2014) further showed that alterations in feeding time affected microbial structure and function, linking host circadian regulation of feeding behavior to microbial ecology [50]. Together, these findings emphasize the tight coupling between environmental rhythms, host behavior, and gut microbial dynamics.

3.2. Gut microbiota and the circadian clock

The relationship between the gut microbiota and the host circadian system extends beyond photic entrainment. Non-photic cues, including feeding patterns and microbial signals, also influence peripheral clocks [51] (Table 2).

The microbiota is now recognised as a regulatory “organ” with critical roles in immunity [55], metabolic homeostasis [56], and neural communication [57]. Clinical evidence suggests that circadian misalignment in shift workers disrupts the composition of the gut microbiota, increases intestinal permeability, and promotes inflammation, thereby elevating metabolic disease risk [58]. Poroyko et al. (2016) showed that chronic sleep fragmentation in mice induced obesity and insulin resistance, impaired colonic integrity, and altered the microbial community, including reduced abundance of Lactobacillaceae and Lachnospiraceae, changes that have been linked to inflammation and metabolic dysfunction [52].

Thaiss et al. (2014) reported that microbial oscillations in both humans and mice can be disrupted by jet lag and mutations of core clock genes [50]. Taxa such as Lactobacillus reuteri and Dehalobacterium spp. displayed strong diurnal oscillations, as did broader groups such as Clostridiales, Lactobacillales, and Bacteroidales. Functional prediction revealed that these rhythms were tightly linked to nucleotide and amino acid metabolism, as well as mucus degradation. Loss of Per1/Per2 in mice markedly reduced microbial rhythmicity and function, including vitamin and nucleotide metabolism. Jet lag experiments in both mice and humans have demonstrated that circadian disruption alters peripheral clock gene expression and the composition of the gut microbiota, highlighting the host clock’s role in maintaining microbial rhythmicity.

Hormonal signalling also contributes to the maintenance of circadian rhythms. Although glucocorticoids normally synchronise cellular rhythms, their chronic elevation has been linked to reduced circadian gene rhythmicity in both the SCN and peripheral tissues, diminished microbial diversity (as measured by the Chao1 and Shannon indices), and increased inflammation [53]. Similarly, environmental pollutants can disrupt circadian rhythms. Acrolein, a drinking-water contaminant, promoted atherosclerosis by downregulating the expression of Bmal1 and Clock, suppressing MAPK signalling, and altering the gut microbial composition (e.g., increases in Firmicutes and decreases in Bacteroidetes), effects correlated with elevated MMP9 levels and plaque development [54]. Airborne pollutants also impair circadian gene expression (e.g., Bmal1, Per2), promote insulin resistance, and reduce energy expenditure through possible epigenetic mechanisms [59]. Together, these findings highlight how environmental stressors reshape both circadian regulation and microbial function, and are thus risk factors for metabolic disease.

3.3. Diet composition, feeding time, and microbiota-clock interactions

Beyond sleep loss and jet lag, diet and feeding schedules shape the gut microbiota in ways tightly linked to circadian regulation. In mice, restricting feeding to either the light or dark phase markedly shifts the composition of the gut microbiota. Strikingly, rhythmic feeding can restore microbial oscillations even in animals lacking functional clock genes [50] (Table 3).

Leone et al. (2015) compared germ-free and specific-pathogen-free (SPF) mice under low-fat and high-fat diets [60]. Despite equivalent caloric intake, the germ-free mice gained less weight and accumulated less adipose tissue than the SPF mice. Moreover, gut microbes modulated circadian gene expression in the mediobasal hypothalamus and liver: Per2 expression was nearly absent in the livers of germ-free mice, regardless of diet, underscoring the microbiota’s essential role in circadian regulation. High-fat diets blunted microbial rhythms, reducing fluctuations in SCFAs. Organoid and in vivo studies have confirmed that SCFAs such as butyrate and acetate directly enhance rhythmic Bmal1 and Per2 expression, establishing SCFAs as key microbial signals linking diet, microbial metabolism, and host circadian clocks.

Time-restricted feeding further illustrates this connection. Zeb et al. (2020) showed that restricting feeding windows prevented metabolic disorders, reduced serum triglycerides and total cholesterol while preserving HDL, and enriched beneficial taxa such as Prevotellaceae and Bacteroidaceae that are correlated with enhanced Sirt1 activity [61]. These findings highlight how microbial metabolites mediate the beneficial effects of dietary timing on circadian regulation. The interplay among circadian rhythms, diet, and the gut microbiota is thus highly dynamic and bidirectional. While many studies have examined diet–microbiota or circadian-diet interactions, fewer have integrated all three systems. Current evidence suggests that disease development often reflects cumulative disturbances across these interconnected pathways. Moving forward, integrative research is needed to clarify the feedback mechanisms involved and design novel interventions. Potential strategies may diverge: natural compounds that alleviate microbiota dysbiosis offer preventive opportunities, while environmental pollutants are potent circadian disruptors that exacerbate host-microbiota misalignment. Collectively, these insights provide a foundation for developing therapeutic approaches to mitigate circadian misalignment and its associated health risks.

3.3.1. Disruptive dietary components: fructose and chronic alcohol consumption

Excessive fructose intake and chronic alcohol consumption represent two major dietary factors that disrupt host circadian rhythms through microbiota-dependent and metabolite-driven mechanisms. Both are increasingly prevalent in modern dietary patterns and exert effects that extend beyond metabolic dysregulation, directly altering the rhythmic expression of peripheral clock genes.

Fructose, when consumed in excess, readily escapes absorption in the small intestine and reaches the colon, where it undergoes microbial fermentation. A high fructose intake has been linked to metabolic dysfunction, including NAFLD and insulin resistance [25]. This in turn disrupts hepatocyte circadian regulation by altering the rhythmic expression of Bmal1, Per1, and Clock. In vitro evidence shows that fructose reduces the amplitude of Per1 and Bmal1 oscillations and delays Clock rhythms, accompanied by decreased phosphorylation of AMPK and ACC and increased LIPIN1 expression, collectively shifting metabolic programming toward lipid synthesis [26]. These metabolic disturbances also impair SCFA production profiles and their normal diurnal oscillations, weakening microbial cues that normally reinforce peripheral clock timing.

Chronic alcohol consumption exerts similar but more pervasive circadian disruption. Alcohol-induced hepatic steatosis is associated with altered oscillatory patterns of Npas2, Per2, and Per3 [27], reduced amplitude of peripheral clock gene expression, and suppression of clock-controlled transcription via a persistent decrease in the NAD+/NADH ratio across the circadian cycle [27]. Alcohol also compromises intestinal barrier integrity by dysregulating circadian genes in epithelial cells [28], increasing endotoxin translocation and triggering inflammatory signalling pathways that dampen circadian amplitudes. Irregular eating patterns further exacerbate alcohol-induced circadian misalignment [29,30]. Importantly, human and rodent studies have demonstrated that alcohol disrupts the diurnal rhythms of SCFAs, with habitual drinkers and shift workers exhibiting blunted SCFA rhythmicity and weakened gut barrier function [31].

Together, these findings indicate that fructose and alcohol disrupt the microbiota–circadian axis through convergent pathways involving altered microbial metabolism, impaired barrier function, redox imbalance, and loss of rhythmic microbial metabolites, ultimately reducing the robustness of peripheral circadian oscillations.

3.3.2. Direct microbial signaling to the circadian clock

Among microbial metabolites, short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) represent the most direct biochemical signals capable of modulating host circadian rhythms. Diurnal oscillations in acetate, propionate, and butyrate have been observed in both mice and humans, and these metabolites exhibit clear temporal coupling with the expression levels of peripheral clock genes [31,50,60]. Mechanistically, SCFAs activate the G-protein–coupled receptors GPR41 and GPR43, which converge on cAMP- and CREB-dependent transcriptional pathways known to regulate Per1 and Per2 expression in hepatocytes and adipose tissues [24,60]. In addition, butyrate functions as an endogenous histone deacetylase (HDAC) inhibitor, enhancing chromatin accessibility at core clock gene promoters and increasing the amplitudes of oscillations in Bmal1 and Clock transcription. These direct receptor- and chromatin-mediated effects establish SCFAs as primary microbial entrainment cues that act independently of systemic hormonal or immune pathways.

3.3.3. Indirect microbial pathways influencing circadian rhythms

Microbial metabolites and structural components also influence peripheral clocks through indirect host-mediated pathways. One major route involves microbiota-dependent remodeling of the bile acid pool. Bacterial dehydroxylation and transformation of primary bile acids regulate host FXR and TGR5 signaling, both of which modulate intracellular cAMP levels and downstream CREB activation pathways tightly linked to hepatic Per transcription and metabolic rhythms [24,39]. A second pathway involves the translocation of endotoxins under conditions of high-fat feeding, alcohol intake, or circadian misalignment. The associated elevation in gut permeability increases systemic lipopolysaccharide (LPS) exposure, activating TLR4-NF-κB signaling, which interferes with CLOCK:BMAL1 transcriptional activity and dampens peripheral clock gene rhythmicity [28,29]. Additionally, perturbations in the microbial community composition influence systemic glucocorticoid release and receptor sensitivity, thereby altering endocrine entrainment of peripheral tissues [53]. These indirect routes demonstrate that microbiota-mediated circadian disruption frequently occurs through metabolic, inflammatory, or hormonal intermediaries rather than direct microbe-to-clock communication (Fig. 3).

Integrated conceptual model illustrating how dietary disruptors modulate the core circadian clock through direct host–intrinsic pathways and indirect microbiota–mediated signaling, leading to metabolic and inflammatory diseases

Dietary disruptors, including a high-fat diet, fructose, alcohol, and irregular eating patterns, impair circadian rhythmicity through two mechanistically distinct routes. (1) Direct host-intrinsic effects: Nutrient overload and metabolic stress activate neuroendocrine and cellular pathways, including the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis, glucocorticoid oscillations, AMPK/mTOR and SIRT1/NAD+ signaling, and ER stress/redox responses. These host-derived cues directly affect the hepatic and peripheral circadian machinery. (2) Indirect microbiota-mediated effects: Disrupted diets alter the composition of the microbiota and metabolite rhythmicity, affecting bile acids, short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), and lipopolysaccharides (LPS). These metabolites regulate clock timing indirectly through receptors such as the GPR41/43, FXR/TGR5, and TLR4 signaling pathways. Both direct and indirect mechanisms converge on the core circadian clock. Impaired circadian regulation subsequently disrupts rhythmic cellular metabolism and contributes to downstream metabolic and inflammatory outcomes, including impaired glucose homeostasis, obesity, intestinal inflammation, and liver disease.

4. Critical evaluation of TRF translational value

4.1. Translational considerations: rodent versus human TRF efficacy

Despite robust metabolic improvements reported in rodent models, the translational relevance of TRF must be interpreted cautiously. Most rodent TRF studies have employed extremely high-fat diets (HFD; 45–60% kcal from fat), which induce rapid metabolic deterioration and circadian disruption. Under such conditions, TRF often functions as a strong countermeasure to an exaggerated pathological state, producing large effect sizes that may not reflect the outcomes in humans consuming more moderate mixed diets. Moreover, rodents have nocturnal feeding patterns and shorter circadian cycles, features that enhance the responsiveness of their peripheral clocks to feeding cues. These physiological differences may partly explain why the metabolic benefits of TRF in humans are more modest and heterogeneous across clinical trials. These species-specific differences highlight the importance of avoiding direct extrapolation of rodent TRF outcomes to human metabolic or behavioral contexts.

4.2. Baseline microbiota as a determinant of TRF responsiveness

Recent evidence suggests that inter-individual variability in baseline gut microbiota composition influences the effectiveness of TRF. Individuals with higher abundances of SCFA-producing taxa or more stable bile acid–modifying communities may exhibit greater improvements in glucose regulation, lipid profiles, or appetite hormones under TRF. Conversely, individuals with disrupted microbial diversity or impaired SCFA rhythmicity, features associated with obesity, alcohol consumption, or irregular eating patterns [28–31], may demonstrate attenuated responses. These observations raise the possibility that microbial signatures could serve as predictive biomarkers for TRF responsiveness, although controlled clinical validation remains lacking. Thus, the baseline microbiota composition may represent a major source of inter-individual variability in TRF outcomes, but this remains largely unexplored in current clinical studies.

4.3. TRF-induced microbial rhythmicity in humans: current evidence and limitations

Rodent studies have consistently shown that TRF restores diurnal oscillations of microbial abundance, SCFA production, and bile acid transformation, even under a HFD [31,50,60]. In contrast, human evidence remains limited. While some interventions have reported increased temporal stability of microbial metabolites during TRF, reproducible oscillatory patterns in microbial taxa or metabolic outputs have not yet been demonstrated across cohorts. Most human studies have collected only one or two stool samples per subject, preventing assessment of diurnal rhythmicity. Consequently, it remains unclear whether TRF induces coherent, reproducible microbial oscillations in humans or whether such rhythms mediate clinical improvements. Without high-resolution temporal sampling, it is impossible to determine whether microbial rhythmicity is a mechanistic driver of TRF benefits or merely an associated phenomenon.

Taken together, these knowledge gaps highlight the need for longitudinal, time-resolved human microbiome studies to determine whether TRF-induced microbial rhythmicity represents a viable therapeutic mechanism or merely a biomarker. Moreover, inter-individual microbial diversity, sex-specific differences, and lifestyle variability are likely to further modulate circadian–microbiome interactions in humans, yet these factors remain underexplored and lie beyond the primary mechanistic scope of the present review.

4.4. Microbiota-directed chronotherapeutics: evaluating chronobiotics and psychobiotics

There is growing interest in microbiota-targeted interventions that could modulate host circadian rhythms, commonly classified as chronobiotics or psychobiotics. Although preclinical evidence suggests that microbial metabolites such as SCFAs and bile acids provide rhythmic entrainment cues to peripheral clocks, the capacity of specific microbial strains to act as true chronobiotics remains unclear.

SCFA-producing taxa, including Bifidobacterium, Faecalibacterium, and Roseburia, exert rhythmic metabolic effects that could theoretically enhance circadian amplitudes. Their metabolites, particularly butyrate, modulate histone acetylation and reinforce metabolic clock outputs in liver and adipose tissue. However, the rhythmicity of SCFA production is highly sensitive to dietary patterns, alcohol consumption, and disrupted feeding schedules [28–31], and no studies have demonstrated reproducible phase-shifting of core clock genes in humans following supplementation with these bacterial strains. Thus, while these taxa influence circadian-relevant metabolic pathways, they cannot yet be considered evidence-based chronobiotics.

Psychobiotic strains such as Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium have been shown to alter stress-axis signalling, sleep–wake behaviour, and metabolic homeostasis, raising the possibility that they may indirectly modulate circadian rhythms. Nevertheless, existing studies have primarily assessed behavioral or endocrine outcomes rather than direct molecular markers of circadian rhythm entrainment. No probiotic intervention has demonstrated robust, consistent modulation of BMAL1, PER, or CRY rhythmicity in peripheral tissues, nor reproducible phase adjustments under controlled feeding or light–dark conditions.

Overall, while microbial metabolites and certain probiotics affect the pathways relevant to circadian regulation, the current evidence does not support any specific strain as a reliable chronobiotic capable of phase-shifting or amplitude-enhancing effects in humans. Substantial gaps remain in defining the dose, timing, and rhythmicity of microbial metabolite production, as well as the host conditions under which microbiota-based interventions may exert circadian benefits.

Taken together, the evidence reviewed across molecular, microbial, dietary, and gene translational domains indicates that circadian regulation emerges from the combined influence of both microbiota-dependent and host-intrinsic pathways. Disruptive dietary patterns, including a high-fat diet, fructose overload, and chronic alcohol exposure, impair circadian timing through parallel mechanisms: they directly perturb nutrient- and stress-sensitive pathways within hepatocytes and metabolic tissues, while simultaneously disrupting gut microbial rhythmicity and the oscillatory release of metabolites such as SCFAs and bile acids. The weakening of GPR41/43 and FXR/TGR5 signaling, together with enhanced TLR4–NF-κB inflammatory activation, acts in concert with host-intrinsic alterations in the metabolic redox state, hormone rhythms, and CREB- and CLOCK:-BMAL1-dependent transcription to dampen the amplitude of peripheral circadian clocks. Conversely, structured feeding schedules, such as time-restricted feeding, restore the circadian alignment by engaging both routes: they reinstate microbial and metabolic rhythms while stabilizing nutrient-responsive signaling within host tissues. Viewed through this systems-level lens, the discrepancies between rodent and human outcomes become clearer, reflecting stronger microbial and metabolic rhythmicity in rodents and greater interindividual variability in humans. Overall, circadian health is shaped by the integrated influences of diet, microbiota, and host signaling, where disruptions at any node propagate across the entire network.

5. Summary

The interactions among circadian rhythms, diet, and the gut microbiota constitute an integrated regulatory network that is central to maintaining physiological homeostasis. Over the past decade, research has elucidated how circadian timing influences dietary behavior, while dietary composition and feeding patterns, in turn, can remodel microbial communities. Accumulating evidence suggests a bidirectional relationship between the host circadian clock and the gut microbiota, indicating that these systems are highly interdependent. Together, these reciprocal interactions influence metabolic regulation, immune function, and disease susceptibility.

Targeting this network offers promising avenues for intervention. Natural compounds and dietary strategies aimed at restoring microbial balance have shown the potential to realign circadian rhythms disrupted by irregular eating patterns, sleep disturbance, or unhealthy diets. Conversely, environmental pollutants have been associated with impaired clock gene expression and a breakdown in host–microbiota synchrony, thereby exacerbating metabolic and inflammatory disorders.

Despite these advances, several limitations remain. Most of the mechanistic insights have been derived from animal models, a situation that constrains the direct translation of findings to humans. Variation in microbial sampling and analytical methods further complicates cross-study comparisons and may account for inconsistent results. Moreover, the molecular signaling pathways through which microbial metabolites influence circadian regulation remain incompletely characterized, particularly with respect to tissue-specific effects. Clinical studies, including trials focusing on probiotics, time-restricted feeding, and dietary interventions, remain limited in scale and scope, underscoring the need for more robust human research. In addition, emerging challenges such as the impact of environmental exposure on circadian–microbiota interactions call for integrative, longitudinal approaches that can capture the complexity of these interwoven systems.

In this review, we have summarized recent progress and highlighted key concepts that provide a foundation for future investigations. A deeper understanding of how circadian rhythms, diet, and gut microbiota interact will be essential for designing strategies to prevent or mitigate health conditions associated with circadian misalignment and microbial dysbiosis.
