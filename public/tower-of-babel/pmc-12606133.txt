Double exponential decay modeling for estimating permissible pre-harvest residue levels of pesticides in apples (Malus pumila)
Authors: Jeong-In Hwang, Tae-Hwa Kim
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractSimulating the residual magnitudes of pesticides applied to crops during cultivation is essential for determining appropriate harvest timing to guarantee safe agricultural products. In this study, field experiments were performed to evaluate the time-dependent persistence of pesticides captan (CT), lufenuron (LF), tebufenozide (TB), and teflubenzuron (TF) applied to apples at recommended and three-fold higher doses. Subsequently, various kinetic models were applied to the residual data for calculating pre-harvest residue limits (PHRLs) which indicate permissible pesticide concentrations during the pre-harvest period. The double-exponential model best fitted persistence trends of all tested pesticides in apples (R2 = 0.67 − 1.00), outperforming zero-, first-, and second-order models. TB, vulnerable to sunlight, exhibited shortest half-lives in apples (0.2 − 0.4 days). Systemic insecticide TF showed longest half-lives in apples (24.6 − 29.3 days), while CT and LF ranged 8.6 − 9.9 and 11.3 − 16.1 days, respectively. Double-exponential parameters enabled PHRL estimation for determining safe apple harvest dates ensuring pesticide concentrations below maximum residue limits. Introduction

The United Nations recently estimated the world population will reach 9.7 billion by 20501. Hence, meeting the escalating demand for food in the face of rapid population growth has been one of the significant global challenges. Based on statistical data from the Food and Agriculture Organization of the United Nations2, the use of pesticides has led to a steady increase in the global yield of primary crops, including cereals, vegetables, fruit, roots and tubers, sugar crops, oil crops, and others. Given these statistical results, pesticides can be considered essential chemicals to ensure high food productivity in preparation for food shortage. However, since persistence and toxicity of some pesticides may result in safety issues to humans and the ecosystem3, exhaustive studies are needed to ensure the safety before and after the pesticide use.

Based on research results of pesticide persistence and toxicity, the Rural Development Administration of Korea establishes pre-harvest interval (PHI) guidelines to help users determine the pesticide application dates that will result in the pesticide residue at the harvest day below its maximum residue limit (MRL). Despite the implementation of the PHI guidelines, Korean government agencies have consistently reported numerous violations involving pesticide residues in post-harvest agricultural commodities that exceed the established MRLs. These regulatory violations emphasize the necessity for establishing pre-harvest residue limit (PHRL) guidelines that represent permissible threshold for pesticide residues in agricultural crops prior to harvest4.

The PHRLs are established by mathematically modeling dissipation trends of pesticide residues in agricultural products investigated at a series of consecutive time points before the harvest day. Many previous studies have used a first-order (FO) dissipation kinetic model to calculate PHRL values4–6. However, since the FO kinetics model cannot simulate all cases of pesticide dissipation patterns that may vary depending on pesticide formulation, application dose, soil property, crop type, cultivation method, and climate7–9, the applicability of other kinetic models such as zero-order (ZO), second-order (SO), and double-exponential (DE) models should also be evaluated to estimate more accurate PHRLs.

Apples are a fruit crop with an increasing trend in both production area and revenue each year10. In this study, the ZO, FO, SO, and DE kinetic models were applied to interpret the results of time-dependent dissipation tests of one fungicide (captan) and three insecticides (lufenuron, tebufenozide, and teflubenzuron) in apples. Subsequently, the parameter values of the best-fitting model were used to estimate PHRLs, in order to more precisely predict the harvest date for the production of safe apples that meet MRL standards.

Chemicals and reagents

The physicochemical properties of pesticides’ active ingredients (A.I.) are listed in Table 2. Analytical neat standards of CT (99.0%), and LF (99.5%), TB (97.5%), and TF (99.0%) were properly purchased from LGC Standards Ltd. (Teddington, London, UK), Honeywell International Inc. (Charlotte, NC, USA), and AccuStandard Inc. (New Haven, CT, USA). Their stock standard solutions were prepared at a concentration of 100 µg mL−1 in acetone or acetonitrile. (Muskegon, MI, USA), and Florisil (F0127, 60 − 100 mesh) was obtained from Sigma-Aldrich Chemical Co. (St. Louis, MO, USA).

Field trials

Field trials were conducted at the Affiliated Experiment and Practice Fields of Kyungpook National University, Daegu, Korea to evaluate pesticide dissipation trends in apples (malus pumila; called Fuji). Experimental plots with three replicates per treatment were established using a randomized block design method in an orchard with 20-year-old apple trees (Figure S1). The apple trees with a space area of approximately 2 × 2 × 3 m (W.×L.×H.) have been subjected to hand thinning at an optimal crop load of 10 fruits per cm2 trunk-sectional area 4 weeks after full bloom11 and have produced > 100 apples per tree annually. Treatments of each pesticide consisted of: (i) treatment at the recommended application dose (RD) and (ii) treatment at three times the recommended dose (3RD). Each treatment included three apple trees, with each tree serving as a replicate of the treatment. A buffer zone with one tree was placed between treatment replicates.

All pesticides were applied in line with the safe use guidelines stated on the label. For the RD treatment, 20-L spray solutions of LF and TF were prepared at 0.025 gA.I. L−1 (2,000 times dilution), and those of CT and TB were prepared at 1.0 gA.I. L−1 (1,000 times dilution) and 0.08 gA.I. L−1 (1,000 times dilution), respectively12. To simulate the scenario of maximizing pesticide residues in the apples, the spray solutions were intensively applied to the surface of the apples using a gas-powered backpack sprayer (MS0835W, Maruyama Mfg Co., Inc., Tokyo, Japan) at the application rate of 4.3 mL sec−1 and the pressure of 40 psi. Apples in the 3RD treatment were applied with the pesticide solutions three times once a week. The final pesticide application dates were the same for RD and 3RD treatments. Other than the four tested pesticides, no other pesticides were used during the study. A control plot without pesticide application was additionally established at the site thoroughly isolated from the pesticide-applied plots. Irrigation was conducted through the bases of the trees using sprinklers, delivering water at a rate of 30 mm once a week until 4 weeks before harvest. Light-reflective films were applied under the tree canopies 30 − 40 days before harvest to promote the color development of apples according to the most commonly used method13.

Fifteen apples from each treatment replicate were sampled at 0 (4 h), 1, 2, 4, 6, 8, 10, 12, and 14 days after the final pesticide application. The apple samples were immediately transported to the laboratory in sealed boxes with ice packs, and then the weights of individual apples were recorded after unsealing. Each apple was cored to remove the seeds, then chopped into smaller pieces, and ground together with the same treatment group. The ground samples were stored in a − 20 °C freezer until the day of pesticide residue analysis to prevent pesticide dissipation.

Pesticide residue analysis

Twenty-five grams of apple sample from each treatment were homogenized with 100 mL of acetone at 12,000 rpm for 3 min using a grinder (AM-7, Nihonseiki Kaisha Ltd., Tokyo, Japan). The homogenate was filtered through a Whatman No. 2 filter paper (Buckinghamshire, UK) placed on a Büchner funnel, and the filtrate was transferred into a separated funnel containing 500 mL of water and 50 mL of methylene chloride. The funnel was vigorously shaken for 5 min and then placed on an exclusive holder for 10 min to allow separation of the aqueous and organic solvent layers. The organic solvent fraction was collected into an evaporating flask after dehydration with anhydrous sodium sulfate. The aqueous fraction remaining in the funnel was subjected to another round of shaking with an additional 50 mL of methylene chloride, followed by layer separation. The organic solvent fraction separated from the second round was dehydrated, combined with the first fraction, and subsequently evaporated to dryness under a water bath at 40 °C using a rotary vacuum evaporator (Eyela Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China). The evaporated sample was dissolved in 10 mL of n-hexane and loaded onto the top of a glass column filled with 10 g of Florisil, 3 g of anhydrous sodium sulfate, and n-hexane in turn. The elution of each pesticide residue from the column was performed using organic solvent mixture listed in Table S1. The eluate was completely concentrated and then reconstituted to 2.5 mL with acetone or acetonitrile.

The quantification of TB and TF in the samples was conducted using a high-performance liquid chromatography with ultraviolet detector (HPCL − UVD; Young-Lin ACME 9000, Anyang, South Korea), whereas that of LF and CT was done using a gas chromatography with electron capture detector (GC − ECD; Shimadzu GC2010, Kyoto, Japan). The instrumental conditions for pesticide quantification were set up as summarized in Table S2. Calibration curves of each pesticide were obtained using a series of pesticide standard solutions diluted at seven levels in the range of 0.1 to 10.0 µg mL−1, and the standard series were analyzed at both the front and back ends of the sample analysis sequence based on a blanket calibration method. Limits of detection (LOQs) and limits of quantification (LOQs) of each pesticide in apples were estimated based on the Korea Laboratory Accreditation Scheme (KOLAS) guidance as follows14: (i) seven matrix-matched samples are prepared at the pesticide concentration in apples that exhibits a peak area 20 times the instrumental noise on the chromatogram, (ii) the pesticide concentrations are analyzed by sequentially injecting the seven samples into the instrument, (iii) the standard deviation (SD) of the analyzed concentrations is calculated, and (iv) the LOD and LOQ are determined as three- and nine times the SD value, respectively. Recoveries of each pesticide spiked to the blank apple sample at concentrations of 0.2 and 1.0 µg g−1 were tested in triplicate.

Results

Quality control for pesticide residue analysis methods

The calibration curves for all tested pesticides showed excellent linearity with correlation coefficients (R2) of > 0.99, and no interfering substances were observed near the pesticide peaks on the analytical chromatograms (Figure S2). Recovery rates of four tested pesticides, treated to apples at concentrations of 0.2 µg g−1 and 1.0 µg g−1, ranged from 81.2 to 119.4% with relative standard deviation (RSD) of 0.4 to 8.9%. These recovery test results complied with the “SANTE/11813/2017” guidelines of the European Commission requiring recoveries of 70 to 120% and RSDs of < 20%17. The LODs and LOQs estimated based on KOLAS guidelines were in the range of 0.01 to 0.02 µg g−1 and 0.03 to 0.06 µg g−1, respectively (Table 3). Thus, the pesticide residue analysis methods employed in this study were sufficiently sensitive to detect concentrations much lower than the MRLs established for apples.

Pesticide dissipation trends

Initial deposition of pesticides on apples

The 3RD treatment along with the RD treatment was tested to investigate how the increased application frequency of pesticides affects the pesticide residue trends in apples. In the RD treatment, the deposition magnitudes of four tested pesticides on apples at 4 h after the application were as follows: 1.08 µg g−1 for CT, 0.13 µg g−1 for LF, 0.09 µg g−1 for TB, and 0.23 µg g−1 for TF (Fig. 1). The differences in the initial deposition between four tested pesticides are likely due to the different active ingredient contents and formulation types of the respective commercial products (Table S1). There have been several studies that have reported the relationship between different formulation types and residual magnitudes of pesticides applied on crops8,15. Meanwhile, the pesticide deposition magnitudes in the 3RD treatment at 4 h after the final application were > 3-fold greater than the initial magnitudes observed in the RD treatment: 4.03 µg g−1 for CT, 0.28 µg g−1 for LF, 0.47 µg g−1 for TB, and 0.61 µg g−1 for TF. The greater deposition observed in the 3RD treatment may be attributed to the repeated application of the formulated pesticide products, which resulted in the accumulation of surfactants and adjuvants on the apple surface. The accumulated surfactants and adjuvants may have helped the pesticide residues better remain on or penetrate through the apple surface. The mean biomass of all apples harvested after the final pesticide application was 361.1 ± 45.7 g. Apple biomass increased by approximately 16 − 20% over the 14-day study period, which might influence the dilution or concentration of pesticides within the apples. Although geographical tests using two or more locations were not performed in this study, environmental factors such as temperature, humidity, and soil type can result in differences in dissipation rates of pesticides between apples grown in different regions.

CT dissipation in apples

The CT residues deposited on apples in the RD treatment dissipated by 76% for 14 days (Fig. 1a), and the residual concentration at the final harvest date was 0.26 µg g−1 which is below its MRL (5.0 µg g−1). During the study period, it is likely that most residues of the non-plant-systemic fungicide CT were removed from the surface of the apples. Since there was little precipitation during the field trial (only 17.6 mm over the 30-day study; Figure S3), rainfall might have little effect on the CT dissipation in this study. The relatively high vapor pressure of CT (4.1 × 10−1 mPa), compared with other tested pesticides, likely contributed to the consistent loss of CT by volatilization. Microbial degradation9 and photolysis18 could also be the most influential factors on the CT dissipation from the apple surface and potentially leading to production of the low-toxic metabolite tetrahydrophtalimide.

The residual concentration of CT in the 3RD treatment at the end of the 14-day study was 0.81 µg g−1, representing an 80% reduction from the initially deposited concentration. The greater CT dissipation in that treatment than in the RD treatment may be due to the increased volatility of CT with the increased number of CT molecules. Inferring from the general chemistry knowledge about the ideal gas law (PV = nRT)19, the volatility of CT residues with the relatively high vapor pressure (P) could be increased due to the repeated CT application that can increase the number of CT molecules (n) deposited on the limited area of the apple surface (V). There have been some previous studies exhibiting that higher pesticide concentration exposed to the environment or crops led to their greater dissipation20. The CT residues in apples collected on the final harvest day (0.3 to 0.8 µg g−1) may be removed by food processing such as rinsing, wiping, peeling, and/or boiling.

LF dissipation in apples

The time-dependent dissipation trends of LF in apples were similar between RD and 3RD treatments (Fig. 1b). The LF residues in apples were reduced by 69% in the RD treatment and by 50% in the 3RD treatment, over the 14-day study period. On the final harvest day, the LF residue concentrations in RD and 3RD treatments were 0.04 µg g−1 and 0.14 µg g−1, respectively, which were below the MRL of 0.3 µg g−1. Since LF has a relatively low water solubility (0.05 mg L−1), it has a low potential to be removed from the apple surface by precipitation. Moreover, the plant-systemic insecticide LF could penetrate into the apple through the cuticular wax layer21, and the LF residues that have entered the apple flesh may be barely influenced by external factors such as precipitation and atmospheric temperature. The emulsifiable concentrate formation of the LF product might facilitate the penetration of LF residues into the apple flesh by dissolving the waxy surface. The metabolism or hydrolysis of LF in apple flesh may also be one of the factors reducing the LF residues.

TB dissipation in apples

All TB residues in both RD and 3RD treatments disappeared completely within two days, and the TB dissipation in apples was fastest compared with those of other tested pesticides (Fig. 1c). No published literature has been found to compare the TB residue results of this study yet. Since TB is a non-plant-systemic insecticide, most TB residues were likely only present on the apple surface during the study period. The accumulation of surfactant and adjuvant on the apple surface by the repeated application might also result in a greater retention of TB in the 3RD treatment than in the RD treatment at the initial time. As rainfall occurred twice on days 9 and 13 following the final pesticide application, it is unlikely to have affected the TB dissipation which was completed within two days after the final application. A key factor accelerating TB dissipation might be photolysis induced by sunlight, and the light-reflective film applied 30 − 40 days before the final harvest day might have enhanced the photolysis of TB vulnerable to sunlight22. Biodegradation and volatilization may also contribute to the TB dissipation in apples.

TF dissipation in apples

Residual concentrations of TF in apples at the final harvest date were 0.13 µg g−1 in the RD treatment and 0.43 µg g−1 in the 3RD treatment (Fig. 1d), which were safe levels below the MRL of 1.0 µg g−1. The magnitudes of TF dissipation in apples over the 14-day study period ranged from 30% to 43% and were the smallest compared to the results for other tested pesticides. Like LF, the plant-systemic TF residues might be able to easily penetrate into the inner part of apples and remain there with little influence by external environmental factors. Internal metabolism and hydrolysis induced by apple components could be the main factors responsible for the TF reduction, and rainfall and photolysis may also partially contribute to the reduction of TF residues on the apple surface.

Model application

Correlation coefficients (R2) of the DE model to the dissipation results observed in this study ranged from 0.67 to 1.00 (Fig. 2), whereas those of the ZO model ranged from − 1.39 to 0.97. The FO and SO models correlated to the dissipation results with R2 of 0.66 − 1.00 and 0.65 − 1.00, respectively. Furthermore, root mean square errors (RMSEs), representing the standard deviation of residuals between model-estimated and experimentally-measured values, were minimized when using the DE model (Table S3). The half-lives (DT50) of each tested pesticide in apples, calculated using the dissipation constants (kz, kf, and ks) obtained from ZO, FO, and SO kinetic models, were presented in Table 4. Based on these DT50 values, TB residues dissipated most rapidly in apples (DT50 = 0.1 to 5.0 days), followed by CT (DT50 = 7.9 to 10.2 days), LF (DT50 = 11.1 to 18.9 days), and TF (DT50 = 16.4 to 50.4 days). The DT50 values calculated by the best-fitting DE model were as follows: 8.6 to 9.9 days for CT, 11.3 to 16.1 days for LF, 0.4 to 0.2 days for TB, and 24.6 to 29.3 days for TF (Table 5).

PHRL calculation

For the calculation of PHRLs, the MRL of each pesticide on apples was set as the pesticide concentration in the apples on the final harvest day, and then the concentrations during the pre-harvest period were reversely modeled using the DE model parameters (Table 6). Assuming pesticide application at the recommended dose, the PHRLs of CT, LF, TE, and TF in apples should be less than 5.76, 0.38, 1.00, and 1.08 µg g−1, respectively, 14 days before harvest to reach their MRLs on the harvest day. When the tested pesticides were applied three times at the recommended dose once a week, the PHRLs 14 days before harvest were 1.0 − 1.4 times higher than those estimated for a single application of the recommended dose.

Discussion

To date, most previous studies have used only the FO model to interpret experimental results on pesticide residues in crops8,23,24. Dissipation of pesticide residues in crops can vary depending on factors such as pesticide/formulation types8, crop types25, climatic/environmental conditions7, and pesticide application methods. Hence, various types of dissipation kinetic models such as ZO, FO, SO, and DE models should be applied to more accurately evaluate such variable patterns of pesticide dissipation in crops. Since the DE model that has been utilized in only a few studies20,26 requires the researcher to make subjective decisions about the differentiation of dissipation phases, the model results may vary between researchers. To address this issue, the DE model was standardized in this study to objectively distinguish pesticide dissipation phases using the Solver Add-in tool in Microsoft Excel™, a certified statistical software.

The DE model best simulated all pesticide dissipation results (R2 = 0.67 to 1.00), while the ZO model showed the lowest applicability to the dissipation results (R2 = − 1.39 to 0.97). All DT50 values calculated by the best-fitting DE model were within the range of values previously reported for a variety of crops under field conditions: 0.9 to 70 days for CT27,28, 1.7 to 49.5 days for LF29–31, and 9.2 to 39.0 days for TF32,33. However, the DT50 values of TB in this study were significantly shorter compared to the values of 2.0 to 8.2 days reported in previous studies34–37. A plausible explanation for the rapid TB dissipation observed in apples has already been provided in the preceding section. Overall, the DT50 values of the tested pesticides except for TF were properly estimated within the experimental period of 14 days. However, the estimated DT50 values of TF (16.4 to 50.4 days) exceeded the experimental period. Thus, when applying the DE model to simulate the residual trend of TF in apples, particular caution may be required to verify that the pesticide dissipation will continue to follow the estimated trend beyond the experimental period.

The ZO and FO models are limited in its ability to simulate pesticide residue dissipations occurring in two different phases. The SO model is available for simulating pesticide dissipation in two different phases. However, it yields negative values for PHRLs when the pre-harvest time (tpre) exceeds 1/(ks × MRL), rendering it challenging to use for PHRL estimation. Hence, the parameter values obtained using the DE model were used to estimate permissible pesticide residue concentrations in apples during the pre-harvest period (i.e., PHRLs). The PHRLs estimated using the DE model accurately reflected the dissipation characteristics of each pesticide investigated in the apples during the field trials. As an example of PHRL utilization, if a farmer sprayed the recommended dose of CT three times, collected several apples 10 days before harvest (see Table 5), and requested a pesticide residue analysis to determine the safe harvest date, the apples should contain CT residues at a concentration of < 7.06 µg g−1.

Conclusions

In this study, the time-dependent residues of four pesticides CT, LF, TB, and TF in apples were investigated following applications with different doses (RD and 3RD). Subsequently, the pesticide residue data obtained were used in simulating the residual trends using various models and in estimating PHRLs, which represent allowable pesticide residue concentrations in apples during the pre-harvest period. The DE model was the best option to estimate PHRLs of the tested pesticides in apples, thereby contributing to the production of safer food crops that meet MRL standards. However, the results of this study are based on a limited dataset involving a single apple variety and four pesticides. Thus, further studies are needed to validate the applicability of the DE model to a large number of cases with various apple varieties, crops, pesticides, and environmental conditions.
