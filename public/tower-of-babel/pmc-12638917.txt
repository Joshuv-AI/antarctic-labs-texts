Dynamic conditional survival nomogram for non-early-stage infiltrating ductal carcinoma based on SEER database
Authors: Erqing Tan, Tingting Chen, He Hao, Wenbin Gao, Shuding Guo, Heyang Cui, Bin Song
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The prognosis of breast cancer varies by histologic subtype, with infiltrating ductal carcinoma (IDC) being the most prevalent, comprising 70% of infiltrating breast cancers. Long-term survival outcomes for IDC remain unclear, and some non-early-stage IDC patients exhibit better survival than initially expected. Dynamic survival probability offers a more accurate approach to life expectancy estimation compared to traditional methods such as Kaplan-Meier analysis and the tumor-node-metastasis (TNM) staging system–based nomogram (TNM nomogram) .This study aims to develop a conditional survival prediction model for non-early-stage infiltrating ductal carcinoma patients, with a particular focus on long-term survival assessment of female IDC patients at stage IIB and beyond (according to the AJCC classification), to support personalized treatment planning. Female IDC patients from the SEER database (2000–2022) were analyzed. Overall survival probabilities for additional y years after surviving x years were calculated using the Kaplan-Meier method. Lasso, subset regression, and multivariate Cox proportional hazards analysis were applied to identify significant predictors for constructing a conditional survival (CS) nomogram, where CS is defined as the probability of surviving y additional years given that a patient has already survived x years. The CS nomogram was compared to the TNM-nomogram using the concordance index, ROC curves, calibration plots and decision curve analysis (DCA). The conditional survival nomogram, incorporating 12 variables, predicted 3-, 5-, and 10-year survival, as well as 10-year conditional survival. It demonstrated higher predictive accuracy compared to the TNM-nomogram, reflected by a superior concordance index and improved ROC curves. Calibration plots further confirmed its greater precision, and decision curve analysis (DCA) highlighted better net benefits across varying risk thresholds. Unlike the traditional nomogram, which only provides unconditional survival probabilities at diagnosis, the CS-nomogram dynamically updates survival estimates according to the time already survived (CS(y|x) = OS(x + y)/OS(x)). The conditional survival nomogram provides more accurate and personalized survival predictions for non-early-stage IDC patients, surpassing traditional nomogram. This tool offers valuable guidance for clinical decision-making and improves the precision of survival estimations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-25205-y.

Introduction

According to the Global Cancer Statistics 2022 report, breast cancer surpassed lung cancer as the leading cancer type globally in 20221. As a disease that primarily affects women, breast cancer is among the leading causes of morbidity and mortality in many countries1–4. The 18th St. Gallen International Breast Cancer Conference in 2023 underscored the importance of Infiltrating ductal carcinoma (IDC)5. The prognosis of different breast cancer types varies considerably, and accurate prediction of IDC, the most common histologic type of breast cancer, is essential for enhancing patient outcomes4,5. Owing to advancements in early detection and treatment of breast cancer, mortality rates among women diagnosed with breast cancer have decreased by 43%, and five-year survival rates have markedly increased6. Consequently, a substantial proportion of breast cancer patients now achieve long-term survival7. Traditional Kaplan-Meier survival analysis, which only assesses prognosis at the time of diagnosis, fails to account for the dynamic nature of survival probabilities over time.

Recently, conditional survival (CS) evaluation has emerged as a superior method for survival prediction, particularly among breast cancer patients8–12. CS considers both the time since diagnosis and post-treatment survival, enabling the dynamic adjustment of survival probabilities throughout a patient’s survival journey. This approach provides cancer survivors with a more precise estimation of changes in survival risk over time7,13,14. Dynamic survival prediction models are crucial for continuous risk assessment, yet most existing IDC nomograms fail to address this aspect. While traditional nomograms offer individualized predictions at a single time point, they lack the ability to update survival information dynamically. In contrast, models that provide updated survival estimates after several years of treatment enable more precise and proactive long-term prognostic evaluation15–20. Traditional nomograms generate survival probabilities anchored to the time of diagnosis and do not incorporate survival time already achieved. Consequently, they cannot provide updated prognostic estimates for long-term survivors, leading to potential underestimation of survival in patients who have lived several years post-treatment. By contrast, the CS-nomogram re-expresses survival probability as a conditional function of having already survived x years, thereby enabling dynamic updates throughout the follow-up period.

In response to this need, we utilize SEER database to apply Conditional Survival analyses to provide enhanced survival predictions for female patients with non-early IDC20. Additionally, we compared the performance of the CS-nomogram with the traditional TNM-nomogram to highlight the advantages of a dynamic approach in survival prediction. Our study developed a CS-nomogram based on relevant clinicopathologic features and survival time, providing a valuable tool for survival assessment and follow-up. By enhancing prognostic accuracy for IDC patients, this study aims to improve personalized treatment planning and long-term patient management.

Materials and methods

Patients

Data for this analysis were obtained from 17 regional SEER registries by utilizing the SEER*Stat 8.4.3, with data updated in 2022. The study included female patients aged 20–80 years, diagnosed with malignant infiltrating ductal carcinoma (IDC) of the breast from 2010 to 2019. Only those with IDC as their sole primary malignancy, at stages IIB or higher, and with comprehensive clinicopathological and treatment records were included. The editions of the AJCC breast cancer staging, specifically the 7th and 8th, were sufficiently aligned, permitting their integration in this analysis. Exclusions were applied for cases with incomplete data, absence of histopathological confirmation, zero survival months, deaths from non-cancer causes, or missing key variable data. The primary endpoint was overall survival (OS), defined as the time interval from diagnosis to death.

Data collection

Clinicopathological features, along with treatment information, were collected, including the age at which the diagnosis was made, Marital status (married or single), Race (white or black or other), Tumor Staging (T and N), clinical grade (from IIB to IV), immunohistochemical markers (ER, PR, HER2 status), and treatment modalities (surgery, radiation, chemotherapy). These factors were chosen for their potential impact on IDC prognosis21. Data were divided into training and validation cohorts in a 7:3 ratio for model development and validation. The training cohort included 23,630 patients, while the validation cohort comprised 10,128 patients. According to considerations associated with predictive model construction, samples with an amount of ending events exceeding ten times the degrees of freedom can be considered to have better predictive efficacy22.

The link between OS and CS

Overall survival (OS) was estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method and further predicted based on the multivariable Cox proportional hazards model. Conditional survival (CS) was not derived from a separate model; instead, it was calculated using the previously obtained survival function. Specifically, OS probabilities at different time points were first obtained, and then CS was computed using the established formula: CS(y|x) = OS(y + x)/OS(x), where CS(y|x) denotes the probability of surviving an additional y years given that a patient has already survived x years13,14. In this study, we use the term ‘dynamic’ to specifically denote conditional survival estimates that update according to the time already survived (CS(y|x)), whereas the traditional nomogram only provides unconditional survival probabilities at diagnosis.

Statistical analysis

Candidate predictors were initially screened using univariate Cox regression (p < 0.05). Significant variables were further evaluated in a multivariable Cox model by three approaches: LASSO regression with cross-validation, best subset regression (BSR), stepwise bidirectional selection guided by the lowest Akaike Information Criterion (AIC). This model was used to construct a traditional nomogram, translating the Cox-derived linear predictors into individualized overall survival (OS) probabilities. Conditional survival (CS) nomograms were not derived from a separate model but calculated from the same Cox-based survival function using the established formula CS(y|x) = OS(x + y)/OS(x).

We additionally developed a TNM-nomogram model based solely on Cox regression with the TNM staging system (T, N, and M categories) as a covariate. This model was considered as a benchmark reference and was compared with the proposed nomogram to highlight the incremental predictive value. The two models were primarily compared and evaluated in terms of C-index, time-dependent AUC, calibration curves, and decision curve analysis (DCA)23. Cox regression models were fitted using the “rms” package. Variable selection was performed using “glmnet”, “leaps”, and “MASS”. Model performance was assessed in terms of discrimination, calibration, and clinical utility with the “survivalROC”, “rms”, and “dcurves” packages. The two nomograms were constructed using the “rms” package. All analyses were conducted in R version 4.3.3, with P < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

Demographic and clinicopathological characteristics

This study examined the baseline demographic and clinical characteristics of patients with infiltrating ductal carcinoma by comparing two groups: the training set (n = 23,630) and the validation set (n = 10,128). The evaluation illustrated no statistically significant differences in age and race across the cohorts. Most participants were aged 40–69 years, with 77.02% in the training set and 76.34% in the validation set. Both groups were primarily White, with 74.24% in the training set and 74.02% in the validation set.

A notable difference was observed in marital status, with a higher percentage of married individuals in the validation group, at 59.07%, compared to 57.79% in the training group (P = 0.028). Clinically, tumor characteristics such as stages (T0 ~ 1 through T4), nodal involvement (N0 through N3), the presence of metastases (M0 and M1), and clinical grades showed no significant differences, suggesting comparable disease severity at diagnosis. Similarly, treatment modalities—surgery, radiation, and chemotherapy—were consistently applied, with 88.15% in the training and 88% in the validation group undergoing surgery, and 82.29% and 82.33% respectively receiving chemotherapy. Hormonal receptor status (ER and PR) and HER2 positivity were highly consistent, with ER positivity at 72.15% in the training cohorts and 72.25% in the validation cohorts, and HER2 negativity at 73.83% in the training and 74.04% in the validation group (Table 1). The Kaplan-Meier survival curves also demonstrate nearly identical survival probabilities for both groups over. Comparison of overall and conditional survival

The conditional survival analysis offers a comprehensive perspective on the evolution of survival probabilities over time for breast cancer patients. Survival probabilities are presented stratified by the number of years a patient has survived since diagnosis (Fig. 2A). The overall actuarial survival rate was 95.1% in the first year and 67% in the tenth year, demonstrating an annual decrease. Conditional survival analyses revealed an increase in 10th-year survival rates over time, rising from 65.8% at diagnosis to 98.2% after 9 years. These findings indicate that patients who survive longer initially exhibit progressively higher survival probabilities in subsequent years, illustrating an improving prognosis with increased survivorship. Subsequently, a heatmap was presented to quantify these probabilities, confirming that survival rates enhance and stabilize as the time since diagnosis increases, particularly after the first few critical years (Fig. 2B).

Two groups of survival rates were compared: the first group comprised patients who survived exactly 10 years post-diagnosis, with survival probabilities increasing annually, and the second group included patients who survived the previous year, with stable and higher survival rates in the subsequent year (Fig. 2C). Annual hazard rate refers to the probability of an event (such as death, disease progression, or recurrence) occurring within a specific year among a given population. In our study, the annual hazard rate for each year was calculated by subtracting the survival rate of year 1 from the survival rate of year 0. Subsequently, the annual hazard rates for years 1 to 9 were obtained. We find that the annual hazard rate was approximately 6% in the second year post-diagnosis, meaning that 6% of the patients who survived the first year would experience the event in the second year, and as the patients continued to survive, the hazard rate gradually decreased, stabilizing at around 1% after seven years (Fig. 2D).

Modeling and application of the CS-nomogram

Survival analyses for each candidate variable demonstrated statistically significant associations with overall survival, except for age, which was regrouped to achieve clearer separation (Supplementary Fig. 1B). To construct the conditional survival (CS) nomograms, both univariate (Supplementary Fig. 1A) and multivariate Cox regression analyses were performed to obtain hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the 12 variables (Fig. 3A). Best subset regression was then applied to evaluate all possible combinations of the 12 variables by adjusted R² and selected 8 variables (Fig. 3B). In parallel, LASSO regression (Fig. 3C) yielded a variable set consistent with multivariate Cox regression, supported by 5-fold (Fig. 3D) and 10-fold (Fig. 3E) cross-validation. The final set of variables identified by both LASSO and Cox regression was confirmed as the optimal model through stepwise regression guided by Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) (Supplementary Table 2). Several clinicopathological factors were significantly associated with overall survival. Compared with patients aged 20–69 years, older age (70–80 years, HR = 1.29, 95%CI: 1.20–1.38) and single status (HR = 1.19, 95%CI: 1.13–1.24) were linked to increased mortality. Relative to Black patients, White (HR = 0.72, 95%CI: 0.68–0.77) and other races (HR = 0.66, 95%CI: 0.60–0.73) exhibited a lower risk of death. Increasing T and N stage showed elevation in hazard ratios (T4: HR = 1.87, 95%CI: 1.69–2.07; N3: HR = 1.63, 95%CI: 1.48–1.79). Higher histological grade was strongly associated with poorer survival (grade IV: HR = 5.47, 95%CI: 5.02–5.96). Patients who did not undergo surgery (HR = 1.83, 95%CI: 1.70–1.96), radiotherapy (HR = 1.16, 95%CI: 1.10–1.22), or chemotherapy (HR = 1.38, 95%CI: 1.30–1.47) faced increased mortality risk. In contrast, ER+ (HR = 0.68), PR+ (HR = 0.61), and HER2+ (HR = 0.56, 95%CI: 0.52–0.59) status were protective factors. This reflects a prognostic association rather than causal inference, and overall the estimates were consistent with clinical expectations.

Based on the clinical characteristics of the patients, this nomogram assigned point values to the variables and converted the total point score into linear predictive scores (Supplementary Table 1), which were then mapped to 3-, 5-, and 10-year survival probabilities, as well as conditional 10-year survival probabilities for patients who survived 1 to 9 years (Fig. 4A). Based on the total point score for each variable (Supplementary Table 1), we show survival analyses for the four variables with the highest scores (Fig. 4B). The implementation of this nomogram enabled the stratification of patients into high- and low-risk categories based on the total nomogram score, with 155 points identified as the optimal cut-off by maximally selected log-rank statistics in the training cohort. Patients with scores < 155 were classified as low-risk and those with scores ≥ 155 as high-risk. Kaplan–Meier survival curves demonstrated significantly different outcomes between the two groups in both the training and validation cohorts, confirming the robustness of this risk stratification (Fig. 4C). The nomogram also displays risk stratification bands to facilitate clinical interpretation.

Evaluation and validation

We evaluated the CS-nomogram’s predictive performance and clinical utility over a 10-year period, comparing it with the traditional TNM-nomogram (Supplementary Fig. 2). The CS-nomogram consistently demonstrated superior accuracy, with Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curves showing high Area Under the Curve (AUC) values—generally exceeding 0.7 and approaching 0.9—and a Concordance Index (C-index) remaining above 0.75 throughout the period (Fig. 4D,E). In contrast, the TNM-nomogram exhibited lower initial C-index and AUC values, showing poorer consistency and a weaker ability to classify high- and low-risk individuals.

The calibration curve assesses a model’s accuracy by comparing predicted probabilities with actual outcomes. A curve close to the 45-degree line indicates good agreement between predictions and observations, reflecting high predictive accuracy. The CS-nomogram’s calibration curve closely aligned with the ideal 45-degree line, demonstrating high predictive accuracy with minimal deviation. In contrast, the TNM-nomogram showed greater discrepancies, indicating lower accuracy compared to the CS-nomogram (Fig. 5A,B). Decision Curve Analysis (DCA)evaluates the net benefit of a model across various risk thresholds, comparing it to no intervention (Treat All or Treat None strategies). The x-axis represents the risk threshold, indicating the minimum risk probability at which intervention is recommended. The y-axis shows the net benefit, reflecting the overall benefit to the patient. In the figure, the CS-nomogram consistently outperforms both the TNM-nomogram and the Treat All or Treat None strategies across the entire threshold range (e.g. 0.1 to 0.8), demonstrating that the CS-nomogram provides a higher net benefit at all risk thresholds compared to the TNM model and standard treatment strategies (Fig. 5C,D). By integrating dynamic survival probabilities, the CS-nomogram have the higher clinical decision-making and improved patient outcomes prediction.

Discussion

The present study evaluated the survival probability and conditional survival estimates of women with non-early-stage infiltrating ductal carcinoma. Key prognostic variables were identified using Cox analysis, Lasso regression, and best subset regression, ensuring robust model development. A nomogram was subsequently constructed to provide accurate prognostic information for patients and clinicians24,25. This chart visually demonstrates to patients that their probability of survival increases over time. The longer a patient survives following treatment, the greater their likelihood of continued survival. The concordance index (C-index), AUC values, calibration plots, and decision curve analysis (DCA) all indicate the nomogram’s predictive accuracy26. Cumulative survival time should be considered in prognostic assessments for prolonged survival. Treatment goals may be adjusted to be more aggressive for patients with prolonged survival based on predictive modeling. Thus, it serves as a valuable tool for predicting outcomes in non-early-stage infiltrating ductal carcinoma.

Our findings provide clinically relevant information, helping patients better understand their expected lifespan after curative treatment, as prognosis improves. Ten-year survival rates significantly increase, particularly after surviving 5 to 7 years post-treatment. While traditional survival analyses lack real-time prognostic updates, our results show a significant improvement in conditional survival at year 10 compared to KM estimates, from around 65% to over 87% after 5 years and 93% after 7 years. These results align with clinical recommendations for a 5–7 year follow-up, suggesting extended survival correlates with a reduced mortality risk, approaching that of the general population27,28.

Diagnosis-based survival rates are less relevant for patients surviving over a year. Conditional survival rates provide a more accurate prognosis. For long-term survivors, cancer biology may change significantly due to treatment effectiveness or advances, which impacts patient management29,30. 1-year conditional survival (CS) probability declined slightly in the second year post-diagnosis, from CS(1|0) ≈ 95% to CS(1|1) ≈ 93%. This decline might reflect natural selection, where high-risk patients succumb early, leaving a lower-risk cohort with better prognoses31. Other possible reasons include reduced intensity of supportive therapy after the first year or the need to optimize initial treatment regimens over time.

We constructed a TNM-nomogram alongside the CS-nomogram for comprehensive comparison. key metrics showed CS-nomogram consistently outperformed TNM-nomogram. Marriage reduces death risk by 25%, while unmarried patients are more prone to psychological disorders, disrupting endocrine and immune functions, increasing mortality32–34. Age influences tumor development, biological characteristics, and BC prognosis, with incidence rising until menopause35. Patients over 70 in our cohort have worse prognosis, likely due to common comorbidities such as hypertension, heart disease, and diabetes36. Black women have worse prognoses than White women, likely due to socio-economic disadvantages, poor living conditions, and limited healthcare access37. Various treatment methods and molecular subtypes also impact outcomes. Most hazard ratio estimates from the multivariable Cox regression (Fig. 3A) were consistent with clinical expectations. Advanced stage, greater nodal involvement, higher grade, older age, and unmarried status predicted poorer survival, whereas surgery, radiotherapy, chemotherapy, and hormone receptor or HER2 positivity were protective factors. Hormone receptor and HER2 positivity indicated the likelihood of targeted therapy use and were associated with improved survival. These results indicate that variables largely aligned with established prognostic patterns.

Our study has several limitations. As a retrospective study, it is subject to biases in historical data collection and recording38. We could not capture all prognostic variables in IDC, such as treatment regimen, comorbidities, and Ki-67 levels. Our SEER-based data limits generalizability to other countries. The shrinking number of long-term survivors in small external cohorts makes conditional survival estimates unstable and limits the reliability of model validation. An important limitation of this study is that the proportional hazards assumption was not formally tested in the final Cox model, which may affect the robustness of the results and the accuracy of predictions. So, external validation is challenging due to the large data needed for CS analysis. Long-term survival relies on evolving therapies, requiring CS-nomogram updates. Future studies should use diverse datasets and integrate genomic and molecular data for better personalization.

Conclusion

This study developed and validated a conditional survival (CS) nomogram for predicting long-term survival in female patients with infiltrating ductal carcinoma (IDC). The CS-nomogram provides a dynamic and individualized approach to survival prediction, with significant advantages over traditional models. By incorporating clinicopathological features and treatment data, the nomogram enhances the accuracy of prognostic assessments, thereby facilitating more informed clinical decision-making and personalized treatment planning.
