Isometric Invariant Quantification of Gaussian Divergence over Poincaré Disc
Levent Ali Mengütürk

Abstract
The paper presents a geometric duality between the spherical squared-Hellinger distance and a hyperbolic isometric invariant of the Poincaré disc under the action of the general Möbius group. Motivated by the geometric connection, we propose the usage of the ℒ 2 \mathcal{L}^{2} -embedded hyperbolic isometric invariant as an alternative way to quantify divergence between Gaussian measures as a contribution to information theory.



Keywords:
Measure divergence, hyperbolic geometry, Möbius transformation

1
Introduction

The objective of this work is to present a geometric relationship between the spherical squared-Hellinger distance – commonly used in information theory, machine learning and artificial intelligence – and a hyperbolic isometric invariant of the Poincaré disc under the action of the general Möbius group. This spherical-hyperbolic duality motivates us to propose the
\mathcal{L}^{2}
-embedded hyperbolic isometric invariant as an alternative way to quantify divergence between two Gaussian probability measures. We shall keep this note reasonably concise towards communicating a geometric connection as a contribution to information theory. To the best of our knowledge, the most relevant observation was drafted in
[
7
]
, which we shall formalise, deepen and extend hereafter.

For the rest of this paper, consider a measurable space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F})
endowed with probability measures
\mathbb{P}
and
\mathbb{Q}
. For our purposes,
\mathbb{P}
and
\mathbb{Q}
are Gaussian measures hereafter. Using the language of measure theory, the squared-Hellinger distance between
\mathbb{P}
and
\mathbb{Q}
(see
[
5
]
), which we shall denote as
\Phi(\mathbb{P},\mathbb{Q})
, is given by the following:

\displaystyle\Phi(\mathbb{P},\mathbb{Q})=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}\left(\sqrt{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}(\omega)}-\sqrt{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{Q}(\omega)}\right)^{2}.

(1)

We are now in position to present our main statement before providing the details in the next section.

Proposal 1.1
.

Define
\Psi(\mathbb{P},\mathbb{Q})
as follows:

\displaystyle\Psi(\mathbb{P},\mathbb{Q})=\frac{2\int_{\Omega}\left(\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}(\omega)-\mathrm{d}\mathbb{Q}(\omega)\right)^{2}}{\left(1-\int_{\Omega}\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}(\omega)^{2}\right)\left(1-\int_{\Omega}\mathrm{d}\mathbb{Q}(\omega)^{2}\right)}.

(2)

Then,
\Phi
in (
1
) and
\Psi
in (
2
) form a spherical-hyperbolic dual in a geometric sense.

In the next section, we shall unpack Proposal
1.1
in detail and provide the mathematical arguments leading to it, which will clarify the statement for the reader. We shall also provide a closed-form solution to (
2
) in order to enable its implementation more broadly in machine learning and artificial intelligence applications. The closed-form equation will also allow us to quantify a distributional distance between two Gaussian processes as they progress in time. Finally, we shall provide the parametric asymptotics of (
2
) across relevant limits, which shed further light on how it behaves.

2
Geometric Duality

For the rest of this paper, let
\mathbb{P}
and
\mathbb{Q}
be measures on
\mathbb{R}
, where
p:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}
and
q:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}
are the corresponding Gaussian probability density functions. The choice of the state-space
\mathbb{R}
here is for parsimony and can be generalized to
\mathbb{R}^{n}
for
n\geq 1
. We identify the parameters of
p
and
q
as
\Theta_{p}=\{\mu_{p},\sigma_{p}^{2}\}
and
\Theta_{q}=\{\mu_{q},\sigma_{q}^{2}\}
, respectively, where
\mu_{p},\mu_{q}\in(-\infty,\infty)
and
\sigma_{p},\sigma_{q}\in(0,\infty)
.
Denoting
||.||_{\mathcal{L}^{2}}
as the
\mathcal{L}^{2}
-norm and using (
1
), we have the following:

\displaystyle\Phi(\mathbb{P},\mathbb{Q})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}||\sqrt{p}-\sqrt{q}||^{2}_{\mathcal{L}^{2}}.

(3)

Since
p
and
q
are non-negative functions and
||\sqrt{p}||_{\mathcal{L}^{2}}=||\sqrt{q}||_{\mathcal{L}^{2}}=1
, both
\sqrt{p}
and
\sqrt{q}
determine points on the positive-orthant of the unit-sphere
\mathcal{S}^{+}\subset\mathcal{L}^{2}
.

Remark 2.1
.

Note that
(\mathcal{S},\left\langle.\right\rangle)
is a Riemannian manifold with constant curvature
K=+1
, given that the inner product
\left\langle.\right\rangle
on
\mathcal{L}^{2}
is the Riemannian metric on
\mathcal{S}
.

The geodesics between any two points on
\mathcal{S}
are the great-circles, and accordingly, an angle using the
\mathcal{L}^{2}
-inner product can be defined via

\displaystyle\cos\left(\textbf{d}_{\mathcal{S}}(\mathbb{P},\mathbb{Q})\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\left\langle\sqrt{p},\sqrt{q}\right\rangle}{\left\langle\sqrt{p},\sqrt{p}\right\rangle^{\frac{1}{2}}\left\langle\sqrt{q},\sqrt{q}\right\rangle^{\frac{1}{2}}}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\sqrt{p(x)}\sqrt{q(x)}\mathrm{d}x=1-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(\sqrt{p(x)}-\sqrt{q(x)}\right)^{2}\mathrm{d}x,

where
\textbf{d}_{\mathcal{S}}(\mathbb{P},\mathbb{Q})
is the Bhattacharyya angle between
\mathbb{P}
and
\mathbb{Q}
– the angle from the center of
\mathcal{S}
subtended to the endpoints on
\mathcal{S}^{+}
– which is equivalent to the spherical distance on
\mathcal{S}^{+}
with values in
[0,\pi/2]
. Therefore, using (
3
), the map
\Phi
can be rewritten in terms of the cosine of the spherical distance on
\mathcal{S}^{+}
as follows:

\displaystyle\Phi(\mathbb{P},\mathbb{Q})

\displaystyle=1-\cos\left(\textbf{d}_{\mathcal{S}}(\mathbb{P},\mathbb{Q})\right).

(4)

In information geometry, it is well-known that the parameter space of Gaussian measures forms a 2-dimensional differentiable manifold locally diffeomorphic to
\mathbb{R}^{2}
(see, for example,
[
2
]
), which is a hyperbolic space with constant curvature
K=-1/2
. If this manifold, which we shall denote as
\mathcal{M}
, is endowed with the Fisher information metric, then
\mathcal{M}
becomes a Riemannian manifold. Accordingly, it is possible to define a distance between
\mathbb{P}
and
\mathbb{Q}
via this metric by integrating the infinitesimal line element along the geodesic connecting the corresponding points on
\mathcal{M}
– determined by the parameters of
p
and
q
, respectively – given by

\displaystyle\textbf{d}_{\mathcal{M}}(\mathbb{P},\mathbb{Q})

\displaystyle=\sqrt{2}\left|\log\left(\frac{1+\zeta(p,q)}{1-\zeta(p,q)}\right)\right|

\displaystyle=2\sqrt{2}\tanh^{-1}(\zeta(p,q)),

(5)

where
\zeta
is a function of the parameters of
p
and
q
:

\displaystyle\zeta^{p,q}=\left(\frac{(\mu_{q}-\mu_{p})^{2}+2(\sigma_{q}-\sigma_{p})^{2}}{(\mu_{q}-\mu_{p})^{2}+2(\sigma_{q}+\sigma_{p})^{2}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

(6)

The expression in (
5
) holds when
\mu_{q}\neq\mu_{p}
and
\sigma_{q}\neq\sigma_{p}
, and the metric takes alternative forms when
\mu_{q}=\mu_{p}
or
\sigma_{q}=\sigma_{p}
(see
[
4
]
), which we omit from this note. One can adjust the lengths on
\mathcal{M}
measured in an alternative unit
R=-\sqrt{-K}/K=\sqrt{2}
, which is analogous to the radian on
\mathcal{S}
.

This geometrical perspective on Gaussian measures is what we shall use to establish a connection between the squared-Hellinger distance and a hyperbolic isometric invariant of the Poincaré disc under the action of the general Möbius group. First, recall that the general Möbius group forms a group of transformations of the Riemannian sphere
\mathbb{\overline{C}}=\mathbb{C}\cup\{\infty\}
(a topological construction, namely one-point compactification), where geometric quantities (e.g. hyperbolic lengths and hyperbolic angles) are invariant under its action. More specifically, a Möbius transformation is a holomorphic function
\eta^{*}:\mathbb{\overline{C}}\rightarrow\mathbb{\overline{C}}
that satisfies the functional form:

\displaystyle\eta^{*}(z)=(az+b)/(cz+d),

where
a,b,c,d\in\mathbb{C}
and
ad-bc\neq 0
. The general Möbius group, which we shall denote as Möb, is generated by the set of Möbius transformations and the set of complex conjugations, such that
\eta\in\text{Möb}
is the composition given by

\displaystyle\eta=C\circ\eta^{*}_{k}\circ\ldots\circ C\circ\eta^{*}_{1},

for some
k\geq 1
, where each
\eta^{*}_{j}
is a Möbius transformation,
C(z)=\overline{z}
for
z\in\mathbb{C}
and
C(\infty)=\infty
. We highlight that
C
is a homeomorphism of
\mathbb{\overline{C}}
, and Möb is equal to the set of homeomorphisms of
\mathbb{\overline{C}}
that take circles in
\mathbb{\overline{C}}
to circles in
\mathbb{\overline{C}}
. In fact, it can be shown that elements of Möb are conformal homeomorphisms of
\mathbb{\overline{C}}
, and

\displaystyle\text{Möb}(\mathcal{W})=\{\eta\in\text{Möb}\,|\,\eta(\mathcal{W})=\mathcal{W}\},

is equal to the group of isometries of the hyperbolic space

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\Im(z)>0\},

(7)

given that
\Im(z)
is the imaginary part of
z
.

Remark 2.2
.

The space
\mathcal{W}
has constant curvature
K=-1
.

Using Poincaré uniformization theorem, the metric on
\mathcal{M}
can be transformed to the metric on
\mathcal{W}
, since
\mathcal{M}
and
\mathcal{W}
are conformally equivalent. This can be achieved by adjusting the metric on
\mathcal{M}
via multiplying it with the positive constant
1/R=1/\sqrt{2}
. Then, using (
5
), the distance between the points determined by
p
and
q
mapped on
\mathcal{W}
is given by

\displaystyle\textbf{d}_{\mathcal{W}}(\mathbb{P},\mathbb{Q})=2\tanh^{-1}(\zeta(p,q)),

where the function
\zeta
is defined as in (
6
).
On the other hand, the hyperbolic space of Poincaré disc model is the unit disc
\mathcal{D}
in the complex plane
\mathbb{C}
such that

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}=\{z^{*}\in\mathbb{C}:||z^{*}||<1\},

(8)

where
||.||
is the Euclidean norm. Since
\mathcal{W}
and
\mathcal{D}
are both in
\mathbb{\overline{C}}
, it is possible to identify a family of
\eta\in\text{Möb}
, such that
\eta:\mathcal{W}\rightarrow\mathcal{D}
. Essentially, Möb allows to use
\mathcal{W}
in (
7
) and
\mathcal{D}
in (
8
) interchangeably when modelling hyperbolic spaces. In particular, if
z
and
\kappa
are points in
\mathcal{W}
and
z^{*}
is a point in
\mathcal{D}
,

\displaystyle z^{*}=e^{i\theta}\frac{z-\kappa}{z-\overline{\kappa}},

(9)

is a Möbius transformation conformally maping
\mathcal{W}
to
\mathcal{D}
, where
\kappa\in\mathcal{W}
is an arbitrary point mapped to the center of the disk
\mathcal{D}
,
\theta
rotates the disk and
z^{*}\in\mathcal{D}
is the corresponding point of
z\in\mathcal{W}
.
Accordingly, using Möb, it is possible to compute distances between two points on
\mathcal{D}
starting from distances on
\mathcal{W}
, or conformally map the points on
\mathcal{W}
to points on
\mathcal{D}
using (
9
), and calculate the distances on
\mathcal{D}
directly – hence, Möb allows for

\displaystyle\textbf{d}_{\mathcal{W}}(\mathbb{P},\mathbb{Q})\mapsto\textbf{d}_{\mathcal{D}}(\mathbb{P},\mathbb{Q}).

Therefore, there is a natural way to characterise Gaussian measures and their distances on the Poincaré disc
\mathcal{D}
starting from
\mathcal{M}
followed by
\mathcal{W}
, using Möb.
We are now in the position to highlight an isometric invariant of
\mathcal{D}
under the action of Möb
(\mathcal{D})=\{\eta\in\text{Möb}\,|\,\eta(\mathcal{D})=\mathcal{D}\}
, which we denote by
\Psi
, as follows:

\displaystyle\Psi(x,y)=\frac{2||x-y||^{2}}{(1-||x||^{2})(1-||y||^{2})},

(10)

for
||x||<1
and
||y||<1
, where
x
and
y
are points on
\mathcal{D}
. It can be shown that the hyperbolic isometric invariant
\Psi
characterises the distance
\textbf{d}_{\mathcal{D}}
on
\mathcal{D}
(see
[
1
]
). Thus, if we choose
x
and
y
in (
10
) as points determined by
\mathbb{P}
and
\mathbb{Q}
conformally mapped to
\mathcal{D}
from
\mathcal{W}
, we can write the following representation:

\displaystyle\Psi(\mathbb{P},\mathbb{Q})=\cosh\left(\textbf{d}_{\mathcal{D}}(\mathbb{P},\mathbb{Q})\right)-1.

(11)

Remark
2.3
below is important for our purposes as a leading observation towards Proposal
1.1
given in the previous section.

Remark 2.3
.

Note that the spherical squared-Hellinger distance
\Phi
in (
4
) is closely related to the hyperbolic isometric invariant
\Psi
in (
11
) from a geometric stance.

We shall further highlight the following characteristics building on Remark
2.3
:

1.

The curvature of
\mathcal{S}
and that of
\mathcal{D}
are opposite in sign with
K=+1
for
\mathcal{S}
and
K=-1
for
\mathcal{D}
, which is reflected in the functional forms in (
4
) and (
11
), respectively.

2.

The spherical cosine function on the spherical space
\mathcal{S}
is replaced by the hyperbolic cosine function on the hyperbolic space
\mathcal{D}
.

3.

Since
\textbf{d}_{\mathcal{D}}
is a metric, we must have
\textbf{d}_{\mathcal{D}}(\mathbb{P},\mathbb{Q})\geq 0
, which means
\Psi(\mathbb{P},\mathbb{Q})\geq 0
must hold having
\cosh(0)=1
and
\cosh(r)
monotonically increasing in
r\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
– in particular,
\Psi=0
when
\mathbb{P}=\mathbb{Q}
, and
\Psi
is strictly positive otherwise.

4.

\Psi
is symmetric in
\mathbb{P}
and
\mathbb{Q}
such that
\Psi(\mathbb{P},\mathbb{Q})=\Psi(\mathbb{Q},\mathbb{P})
– note that
\Phi
is also symmetric in
\mathbb{P}
and
\mathbb{Q}
such that
\Phi(\mathbb{P},\mathbb{Q})=\Phi(\mathbb{Q},\mathbb{P})
.

Accordingly,
\Psi
in (
11
) hosts desirable properties as a divergence metric, also shared by the spherical squared-Hellinger distance
\Phi
. Moving one step further, it is possible to construct a natural embedding of
\mathcal{D}
into a Hilbert space, e.g. into
\mathcal{L}^{2}
– natural embedding here refers to an embedding where an isometry on
\mathcal{D}
can be associated to an isometry on a given Hilbert space. Accordingly, extending (
10
) in the
\mathcal{L}^{2}
-sense, and using the language of measure theory as in (
1
), we propose an
\mathcal{L}^{2}
-embedded
\Psi
(while keeping notations the same) as follows:

\displaystyle\Psi(\mathbb{P},\mathbb{Q})

\displaystyle=\frac{2||p-q||_{\mathcal{L}^{2}}^{2}}{(1-||p||_{\mathcal{L}^{2}}^{2})(1-||q||_{\mathcal{L}^{2}}^{2})}=\frac{2\int_{\Omega}\left(\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}(\omega)-\mathrm{d}\mathbb{Q}(\omega)\right)^{2}}{\left(1-\int_{\Omega}\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}(\omega)^{2}\right)\left(1-\int_{\Omega}\mathrm{d}\mathbb{Q}(\omega)^{2}\right)},

(12)

for
||p||_{\mathcal{L}^{2}}<1
and
||q||_{\mathcal{L}^{2}}<1
. Remark
2.4
below is a more technical version of Remark
2.3
that leads to our statement in Proposal
1.1
.

Remark 2.4
.

In the Gaussian setting,
\Phi
in (
4
) and
\Psi
in (
11
) form a spherical-hyperbolic dual in the aforementioned geometric sense when

\displaystyle\text{$||p||_{\mathcal{L}^{2}}<1$ \hskip 7.22743pt \emph{and} \hskip 7.22743pt $||q||_{\mathcal{L}^{2}}<1$}.

(13)

We are now in position to present the closed-form solution for the hyperbolic dual of the spherical squared-Hellinger distance we propose in (
12
).

Proposition 2.5
.

The divergence (
12
) on
\mathbb{R}
is given by

\displaystyle\Psi(\mathbb{P},\mathbb{Q})

\displaystyle=\frac{(\sigma_{p}\sqrt{\pi})^{-1}-2\sqrt{2}\lambda(\Theta_{p},\Theta_{q})+(\sigma_{q}\sqrt{\pi})^{-1}}{(1-(2\sigma_{p}\sqrt{\pi})^{-1})(1-(2\sigma_{q}\sqrt{\pi})^{-1})},

(14)

where
\lambda(.)
in (
14
) is defined as follows:

\displaystyle\lambda(\Theta_{p},\Theta_{q})=\exp\left(-\frac{(\mu_{p}-\mu_{q})^{2}}{2(\sigma^{2}_{p}+\sigma^{2}_{q})}\right)\left(\pi(\sigma^{2}_{p}+\sigma^{2}_{q})\right)^{-\frac{1}{2}}.

(15)

Hence, the conditions in (
13
) materialize when

\displaystyle||p||_{\mathcal{L}^{2}}<1\Longleftrightarrow\sigma_{p}>\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\pi}}\hskip 10.84006pt\emph{and}\hskip 10.84006pt||q||_{\mathcal{L}^{2}}<1\Longleftrightarrow\sigma_{q}>\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\pi}}.

(16)

Proof.

The proof follows from products of Gaussian densities on
\mathbb{R}
as they appear in (
12
). More specifically, we have the following product:

\displaystyle p(x)q(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi\sigma_{p}\sigma_{q}}\exp\left(-\left(\frac{(x-\mu_{p})^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}_{p}}+\frac{(x-\mu_{q})^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}_{q}}\right)\right)=\frac{h_{pq}}{\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma_{pq}}\exp\left(-\frac{(x-\mu_{pq})^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}_{pq}}\right),

(17)

for any
x\in\mathbb{R}
, where the new terms in (
17
) are defined as follows:

\displaystyle\mu_{pq}=\frac{\mu_{p}\sigma^{2}_{q}+\mu_{q}\sigma^{2}_{p}}{\sigma^{2}_{p}+\sigma^{2}_{q}},\,\,\,\,\,\sigma_{pq}=\sqrt{\frac{\sigma^{2}_{p}\sigma^{2}_{q}}{\sigma^{2}_{p}+\sigma^{2}_{q}}},\,\,\,\,\,h_{pq}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi(\sigma^{2}_{p}+\sigma^{2}_{q})}}\exp\left(-\frac{(\mu_{p}-\mu_{q})^{2}}{2(\sigma^{2}_{p}+\sigma^{2}_{q})}\right).

(18)

Therefore, we have the following:

\displaystyle 4\int_{\Omega}\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}(\omega)\mathrm{d}\mathbb{Q}(\omega)=4\frac{h_{pq}}{\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma_{pq}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\exp\left(-\frac{(x-\mu_{pq})^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}_{pq}}\right)\mathrm{d}x=4h_{pq}=2\sqrt{2}\lambda(\Theta_{p},\Theta_{q})

where
\lambda(.)
is as given in (
15
). Accordingly, we also have the following products integrals:

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}(\omega)^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{h_{pp}}{\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma_{pp}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\exp\left(-\frac{(x-\mu_{pp})^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}_{pp}}\right)\mathrm{d}x=h_{pp}=\frac{1}{2\sigma_{p}\sqrt{\pi}}

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\mathrm{d}\mathbb{Q}(\omega)^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{h_{qq}}{\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma_{qq}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\exp\left(-\frac{(x-\mu_{qq})^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}_{qq}}\right)\mathrm{d}x=h_{qq}=\frac{1}{2\sigma_{q}\sqrt{\pi}},

and the expression given in (
14
) follows. Finally, the statement in (
16
) follows from the denominator of (
14
) for
||p||_{\mathcal{L}^{2}}<1
and
||q||_{\mathcal{L}^{2}}<1
.
∎

As an example, the closed-form expression (
14
) can be used to compare any two
\mathbb{R}
-valued Gaussian processes on a distributional basis after they reach a time-point where (
16
) is satisfied. As a canonical family, we can analyze two
\mathbb{R}
-valued Brownian motions as per below.

Example 2.6
.

Let
\{W^{(p)}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
be a
\mathbb{P}
-Brownian motion with
\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}[W^{(p)}_{t}]=t\mu_{p}
and
\text{Var}^{\mathbb{P}}[W^{(p)}_{t}]=t\sigma^{2}_{p}
. Also, let
\{W^{(q)}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
be a
\mathbb{Q}
-Brownian motion with
\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[W^{(q)}_{t}]=t\mu_{q}
and
\text{Var}^{\mathbb{Q}}[W^{(q)}_{t}]=t\sigma^{2}_{q}
. Then, their divergence in terms of
\Psi
is

\displaystyle\Psi(\mathbb{P},\mathbb{Q})

\displaystyle=\frac{(\sigma_{p}\sqrt{t\pi})^{-1}-2\sqrt{2}\lambda_{t}(\Theta_{p},\Theta_{q})+(\sigma_{q}\sqrt{t\pi})^{-1}}{(1-(2\sigma_{p}\sqrt{t\pi})^{-1})(1-(2\sigma_{q}\sqrt{t\pi})^{-1})}\,\,\,\,\text{\emph{for} $t>\max\left(\frac{1}{4\sigma^{2}_{p}\pi}\,,\,\frac{1}{4\sigma^{2}_{q}\pi}\right)$},

(19)

given that

\displaystyle\lambda_{t}(\Theta_{p},\Theta_{q})=\exp\left(-\frac{t(\mu_{p}-\mu_{q})^{2}}{2(\sigma^{2}_{p}+\sigma^{2}_{q})}\right)\left(t\pi(\sigma^{2}_{p}+\sigma^{2}_{q})\right)^{-\frac{1}{2}},

Using the closed-form expression (
14
), we can also study the asymptotic behaviour of
\Psi(\mathbb{P},\mathbb{Q})
across relevant parametric limits, for which we omit the proof since it follows directly from (
14
).

Corollary 2.7
.

The following limits hold:

\displaystyle\Psi(\mathbb{P},\mathbb{Q})\rightarrow\frac{2\sigma_{p}\sqrt{\pi}}{2\sigma^{2}_{p}\pi-\sigma_{p}\sqrt{\pi}}\hskip 7.22743pt\text{as $\sigma_{q}\rightarrow\infty$}\,\,\,\,\text{and}\,\,\,\,\Psi(\mathbb{P},\mathbb{Q})\rightarrow\frac{2\sigma_{q}\sqrt{\pi}}{2\sigma^{2}_{q}\pi-\sigma_{q}\sqrt{\pi}}\hskip 7.22743pt\text{as $\sigma_{p}\rightarrow\infty$}

(20)

for any
\mu_{p},\mu_{q}
. In addition, the double-limit satisfies

\displaystyle\Psi(\mathbb{P},\mathbb{Q})

\displaystyle\rightarrow 0\hskip 7.22743pt\text{as $\sigma_{p},\sigma_{q}\rightarrow\infty$}

(21)

for any
\mu_{p},\mu_{q}
.

It can be seen from (
20
)-(
21
) that the mean parameters
\mu_{p}
and
\mu_{q}
lose their impact on
\Psi
asymptotically as one or both of the variance parameters diverge – i.e. if any of the Gaussian measures have a significantly large variance, then the mean parameter is decreasingly important in distinguishing the difference between those measures. From (
21
) we further see that two Gaussian measures with significantly large variances are less distinguishable from each other.

Conclusion

Given the hyperbolic geometric connection of (

1.1
. We refer the reader to
[

,

]

The closed-form expression in (
14
) can be generalised to multivariate Gaussian measures. Accordingly, let
\mathbb{P}
and
\mathbb{Q}
be Gaussian measures on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, where
p:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}
and
q:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}
are the corresponding Gaussian probability density functions for some
n\geq 1
. Denote the parameters of

and

as
\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{p}=\{\boldsymbol{\mu}_{p},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{p}\}
and
\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{q}=\{\boldsymbol{\mu}_{q},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{q}\}
, respectively. Hence, we have the density expressions

\displaystyle p(\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{\frac{n}{2}}\sqrt{|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{p}|}}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{p})^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{p}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{p})\right)=\exp\left(\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{p}+\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{\top}_{p}\boldsymbol{x}-\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{x}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{p}^{-1}\boldsymbol{x}\right)

\displaystyle q(\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{\frac{n}{2}}\sqrt{|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{q}|}}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{q})^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{q}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{q})\right)=\exp\left(\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{q}+\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{\top}_{q}\boldsymbol{x}-\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{x}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{q}^{-1}\boldsymbol{x}\right),

given that

\displaystyle\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{i}=\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}_{i}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{i}

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{i}=-\frac{1}{2}\left(n\log(2\pi)-\log(|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}_{i}|)+\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{\top}_{i}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{i}\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{i}\right),

for

. Therefore, we can write the following:
