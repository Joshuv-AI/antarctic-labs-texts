The effect of connectedness to nature on behavioral stages based on the transtheoretical model
Authors: Xingdie Zheng, Hirofumi Ueda
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study applied a transtheoretical model to investigate how the degree of connectedness-to-nature influences individuals’ natural behaviors. It presents an innovative theoretical perspective on behavioral change by incorporating environmental factors (connectedness-to-nature) into the model, moving beyond the sole focus on individual health behaviors or psychological processes. Data for the study were collected through a public survey conducted via the “Wenjuanxing” online platform from 273 participants from various provinces across China, who completed the questionnaire anonymously and voluntarily. Data analyses were performed using SPSS version 27.0. The results indicated that, after categorizing the respondents into five behavioral stages, (Precontemplation, Contemplation, Preparation, Action, Maintenance) the degree of connectedness-to-nature significantly and positively predicted individuals’ stages of natural behavior. The study further revealed significant differences in connectedness-to-nature scores between the pre-action and action stages. Additionally, individuals in the action and maintenance stages exhibited significantly different scores compared to those in the pre-intention, intention, and pre-action stages. As individuals progressed through the stages of natural behavior, their overall connectedness-to-nature scores increased significantly. This study employed a transtheoretical model to differentiate among stages of natural behavior, providing empirical evidence of the influence of connectedness-to-nature on changes in natural behavior.

Introduction

In the aftermath of the COVID-19 pandemic, the benefits of nature for human health have gained increasing attention, with more people recognizing the multiple advantages of reconnecting with nature. Empirical studies of the physical and psychological benefits of nature exposure have proliferated, and evidence now supports that natural environments help alleviate stress, improve physiological markers, and in some cases, even possess therapeutic potential. For example, Germany has incorporated forest therapy into its healthcare system1, whereas China has gradually implemented related policies since 2016. An increasing number of individuals are engaging with nature, and research on natural connectedness, such as the concept of “connectedness-to-nature”2, as well as studies on human-nature communities, are gradually being validated.

In 2020, China introduced the “One Health” concept, which aims to enhance national well-being. The “prevention first” philosophy focuses on preventing and reducing the occurrence of diseases, which not only helps lower the costs of building a healthy China, but also enhances the well-being of citizens. The nationwide implementation of “forest therapy pilot programs” aims to improve national physical fitness and promote public well-being. However, as forest therapy programs are still in the early stages of development, the design and planning of these programs face several urgent issues that need to be addressed. Developed Western countries, such as the United States, the United Kingdom, and Canada, have continuously updated their national health concepts and progressively refined their health strategies3. Among Asian countries, Japan introduced the “Healthy Japan 21” initiative in 1996, which promotes public health through natural therapies, diet, exercise, and other measures. To adapt to changes in modern life, the Japanese government has adjusted and improved its health programs. The second phase of “Healthy Japan 21” (2013–2022) introduced the slogan: “Exercise first, diet second, smoking cessation is a must, and medication last.” Additionally, Japan established and implemented “standards for promoting health through exercise,” further emphasizing the importance of physical activity and a healthy lifestyle4.

Regarding the benefits of nature exposure for human physical and mental health, Mayer2 highlighted that contact with nature reduces negative behaviors and states (e.g., aggression, anxiety, depression, and illness) while enhancing positive behaviors and states (e.g., emotional wellbeing, physical health, and cognitive abilities), demonstrating the significance of nature for both physical and psychological health. Yuen and Jenkins5 noted that contact with nature helps alleviate stress, with the most significant effects observed after 20–30 min of exposure. This finding led to the concept of the “20-Minute Park Effect.” The most significant effects occurred when individuals engaged in nature exposure at least three times per week. Strayer6 introduced the theory of the “Three-Day Effect.” However, given societal barriers in modern life, it is not always feasible for individuals to maintain more than three days of nature exposure per week. A joint study by the University of Exeter and Uppsala University in Sweden, which analyzed data from nearly 20,000 residents of England, found that individuals who spend at least two hours per week in nature are healthier, both physically and psychologically, than those who have no contact with nature. Compared to those with no contact with nature throughout the week, individuals who spend more than two hours in nature weekly have a 60% higher probability of being in good health and a nearly 25% greater chance of experiencing high levels of happiness7. Therefore, the optimal duration of nature exposure is over two hours per week, and it is crucial to establish regular contact with nature as a habitual practice to achieve lasting benefits.

Literature review

Significant progress has been made in research on nature contact, connectedness, and their benefits. Nature contact refers to the interaction between humans and natural environments or non-human species, and is often used interchangeably with concepts such as nature encounter, nature exposure, and nature experience8. Keniger et al.9 categorized three modes of experiencing nature based on individuals’ daily behaviors: intentional, incidental, and indirect. Intentionally experiencing nature involves purposefully visiting natural spaces to interact with nature; incidentally experiencing nature occurs when individuals encounter natural elements while engaged in other activities, such as travel or work; and indirectly experiencing nature refers to observing nature through pictures, images, films, or windows without physical immersion in natural settings. In this study, Nature-Based Behavior is operationally defined as engaging in no less than two cumulative hours of nature contact per week. This behavioral construct encompasses three modalities: direct contact (e.g., walking in parks, hiking, or gardening), incidental contact (e.g., exposure to greenery during commuting), and indirect contact (e.g., observing natural elements through windows, images, or videos). Throughout this study, the term Nature-Based Behavior is consistently employed to denote nature contact behaviors, thereby ensuring terminological alignment with environmental psychology and health promotion literature and enhancing the theoretical rigor of the research framework.

The concept of connectedness-to-nature originated in ecological psychology. Although many researchers have attempted to define this term, no widely accepted definition exists. Notable definitions include Barlett’s10 argument that when individuals realize that nature and humanity are interconnected, the destruction of nature is viewed as self-harm, encouraging pro-environmental behavior. Naess11 extended the concept of the self to the ecological domain, viewing the self as part of the ecosystem (ecological self). Schulz12 defined connectedness-to-nature as the inclusion of nature within the self, emphasizing that humans are part of the natural world and that the relationship between humans and nature is one of interdependence rather than separation. Additionally, Clayton13 introduced the concept of environmental identity, emphasizing the role of environmental beliefs. Nisbet14 introduced the concept of nature relatedness, which encompasses people’s identification with nature, their values, desires, and other related aspects. Moreover, Mayer and Fantz15 proposed the concept of “connectedness-to-nature,” suggesting that all aspects of our lives are interconnected. Although these definitions explain connectedness-to-nature from different perspectives, they all predict natural behaviors. Nature relatedness is significantly associated with individuals’ willingness to engage with nature. Individuals with high nature-relatedness are more inclined to interact with nature and engage in environmental protection and nature-based health activities. Long-term exposure to nature has beneficial effects on mental and physical health. Uehara16 emphasized that nature-based activities are among the most viable alternative therapies. Many individuals resist visiting hospitals or taking medication, but do not exhibit the same reluctance when engaging in exercise or relaxation in natural environments. This is because humans have an innate affinity for nature, referred to as biophilia17. Individuals with high nature-connectedness are more inclined to engage with nature, and after experiencing psychological benefits, they tend to reinforce the behavior of maintaining proximity to nature. This positive feedback mechanism may enhance their psychological resilience18.

Connectedness to nature can be enhanced in the following ways. Nisbet et al. (2009) found that the most direct way to enhance connectedness-to-nature is by allowing individuals to engage directly with nature, as connectedness-to-nature is positively correlated with the amount of time spent in it. In a 2014 study, Nisbet invited participants to spend 30 min daily engaging in outdoor activities in natural environments for 30 consecutive days. Many volunteers participated in the activity, with the actual time spent in nature being twice the prescribed duration. Connectedness-to-nature, well-being, and environmental concern all saw significant increases19. However, not all individuals are willing to participate in these activities. Therefore, it is crucial to raise awareness about the benefits of nature to encourage individuals to engage with the natural world.

Uehara16 emphasized that forest therapy should be a long-term process lasting no less than one year. Many diseases require prolonged care for optimal results, and it is unrealistic to expect that a few sessions of forest therapy alone will cure a condition. Engaging in exercise or therapy is inherently a long-term investment strategy. If contact with nature is cultivated as a regular habit, spending more than two hours every week in natural environments can alleviate mental and physical fatigue and reducing blood pressure.

In the field of behavioral sciences, the stage-of-change model was initially proposed by Prochaska and DiClemente in 198320. It later evolved into a comprehensive theoretical model known as the trans-theoretical model, which synthesizes multiple theories. This model is widely applied in health-related fields, focusing primarily on two aspects: (1) assisting individuals in changing unhealthy habits, such as smoking alcohol consumption20, and weight- related problems21, and (2) promoting the cultivation of beneficial health behaviors, including maintaining a healthy diet, regular physical exercise22, safe sexual behavior, stress management, and rational consumption23. The model aids individuals in adjusting their lifestyles and cultivating healthy behaviors by encouraging a reduction in screen time, rational dietary habits, increased physical activity, and maintenance of a positive mental state. This theory is applicable not only to overt behaviors but also to cognitive transformations. In 2001, Culos-Reed et al.24 outlined three research directions based on the stage-of-change model: (1) integrating stage-change factors with components of other social cognitive theories, (2) conducting cross-sectional studies to examine and analyze the components of this theory, and (3) applying the theory’s components to behavioral intervention studies.

This theory has been demonstrated to be highly effective in promoting changes in healthy behaviors. According to this model, individuals progress through five stages when adopting new behaviors: pre-contemplation, contemplation, preparation, action, and maintenance (Fig. 1). These stages constitute the core of the behavioral stages of the transtheoretical model (TTM). The transtheoretical model framework comprises four key components: (1) behavioral stages20, (2) change processes, (3) self-efficacy25, and (4) decisional balance26. The model aims to explain not only how behavioral change occurs, but also why it occurs. It describes the process through which individuals modify maladaptive behaviors and cultivate positive behaviors27.

Research based on the TTM has revealed general patterns of health behavior change, offering a clear and effective framework and methodology for behavior modification. Application of the TTM has expanded from the medical field to other social domains, and the model applies to many social behaviors28. Interventions designed using the TTM are scientifically grounded, and their effects are generally predictable. The model emphasizes the need to identify the optimal alignment between individual characteristics and intervention strategies rather than assuming that all individuals are prepared to adopt and sustain regular physical exercise29. Therefore, based on behavioral stage theory, categorizing individuals into different stages of natural contact enables the design of the most appropriate interventions for each stage. This facilitates transitions between behavioral stages and ultimately helps individuals develop long-term and sustainable natural contact habits, a valuable area for further exploration.

Hypotheses

As Nisbet19 noted, not all individuals are willing or able to engage with nature. Therefore, to alter the behavior of those who are unwilling to engage with nature, effective interventions must be implemented to raise awareness of its benefits and encourage individuals to immerse themselves in natural environments. Based on the stages of change theory, researchers can not only describe the process of individual behavior change but also gain insights into the underlying reasons for individuals’ decisions to alter their behavior. Researchers can apply different behavioral strategies depending on the stages in which individuals are to facilitate transitions between stages, ultimately helping individuals develop sustainable habits of nature engagement. If connecting with nature is considered an inherent human trait, individuals enhance their connectedness-to-nature through continuous exposure from birth, which, in turn, drives behavioral change. Therefore, engaging with nature should be viewed as an ongoing process rather than a singular event, progressing through a series of dynamic and cyclical stages of change.

Current research indicates that while the TTM has been widely applied in various health fields, studies analyzing the stages of nature engagement behavior using this model are still lacking. Therefore, this study explored how the degree of connectedness-to-nature influences individuals’ natural contact behaviors through the lens of behavioral change stages. Categorizing participants into various stages of behavioral change (e.g., pre-action, action, and maintenance) enhances understanding of the process of behavioral transition. If a significant relationship between the stages of natural behavior and degree of connectedness-to-nature can be established, it would provide a foundation for developing effective interventions aimed at achieving optimal marginal effects. For example, in the pre-conscious stage, guidance or intervention could facilitate the transition from the pre-intention to the intention stage. Initially, individuals may engage with nature unconsciously; however, through social advocacy or personal recognition of the sustainable benefits that nature provides, they gradually shift from having no intention of engaging in natural behaviors to developing such intentions. By examining how connectedness-to-nature influences individuals’ progression through various stages of behavior change, this study offers a novel theoretical extension of the TTM, particularly in its application to environmental psychology. This research paves the way for the application of behavioral change models in traditional domains such as health behavior. Accordingly, the hypotheses of this study were as follows:

Methods

This study aimed to examine the general state of natural behavior stages among Chinese individuals. Therefore, no restrictions were imposed on respondents’ age, gender, or occupation. Respondents in this study were recruited across different provinces in China. The questionnaire, designed and distributed through the “Wenjuanxing” online platform, was completed by participants in exchange for compensation. The exclusion criteria included excessively long response times or irregular answer patterns. All respondents participated anonymously and voluntarily, agreeing to the terms outlined in the informed consent form. The study did not involve any sensitive personal data or commercial interests.

The questionnaire consisted of three sections: the first section gathered basic demographic information, including the frequency of nature contact and income data. The second section employed the TTM Stages of Change scale developed by Ibo27, comprising 24 items. This scale originally began as a simplified version with a single question, based on the original scale of Niaura and Rossi30, which was later translated into Chinese by Si Qi31 and applied to research on university students’ physical exercise. The initial simplified scale, which provided only basic categorization, was prone to subjective bias. Consequently, this study used the revised version of the scale developed by Reed et al.32, translated and revised by Ibo27. This version exhibits strong reliability and validity, as confirmed by retesting conducted by Guo Zhiping in 200933.

The third section used the Connectedness to Nature Scale (CTN). Numerous tools have been developed to measure connectedness-to-nature, addressing emotional, cognitive, and behavioral (or experiential) connections and their interrelations. Mayer and Frantz15 developed a 14-item scale to measure the extent to which individuals perceive their belongingness-to-nature and equality with natural beings from a cognitive perspective. Studies have shown that the level of nature connectedness predicts life satisfaction and subjective well-being. Research indicates that the CTN scale measures not only emotional connections to nature, but also individuals’ cognitive beliefs about nature34. This scale was translated and revised by Li and Wu35 into a 14-item version that uses a Likert scale. Higher scores reflect a greater degree of connectedness-to-nature. The scale is highly suitable for studies on natural contact and connectedness to nature, and was hence employed in this study to analyze the degree of connectedness to nature.

Additionally, this study collected data on participants’ frequency of nature contact. Following Keniger’s classification9, nature contact was categorized into three types (intentional, incidental, indirect) to allow for a more detailed analysis of its impact on behavior stages and nature connectedness.

Statistical analysis

SPSS software (Ver. 27.0) was used for data analysis. First, reliability and validity analyses were conducted, followed by differential analysis to assess the differences among the datasets and determine their suitability for regression and correlation analyses. Finally, correlation analysis was used to examine the relationships between the variables, and linear regression analysis was used to identify the key factors influencing the stages of behavior.

Ethical considerations

This study was confirmed by the institutional review board of Hokkaido University Graduate School of International Media, communication, and Tourism Studies. Due to the nature of the research, which involves [e.g., the use of publicly available data, analysis of anonymized data, or non-human subject research], the study was granted an ethics exemption. No personal identifiers were used in the analysis, and no direct interaction with human subjects occurred.

All research was performed in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations. The study did not involve any sensitive personal data or commercial interests. All participants were anonymously and voluntarily involved, and informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Results

In this study, only three participants were identified in the maintenance stage, which compromises the statistical reliability of subsequent analyses. Given that the action and maintenance stages are adjacent in the Transtheoretical Model and both represent the phase in which behavior has already been initiated, the two stages were combined into a single category (Action stage). This approach ensures sufficient sample size while enhancing the stability of the analyses and the validity of the results. Furthermore, to ensure terminological clarity and consistency, the precontemplation, contemplation, and preparation stages (Stages 1–3) are collectively defined as the non-action stages, whereas the combined action/maintenance phase (Stage 4) is referred to as the Action stage.

Reliability analysis

The internal consistency reliability analysis of the Behavior Stage Scale and the CTN scale yielded Cronbach’s alpha values of 0.885 and 0.755, respectively. These results indicated that the scales exhibited good internal consistency and were suitable for data analysis.

Validity analysis

This study employed the Behavior Stage Scale and CTN scale and performed reliability analyses on both scales. The results revealed KMO(Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin) values of 0.795 and 0.900, respectively for the two scales (Table 2). The Bartlett’s Test of Sphericity chi-square values were 666.866 and 2591.767, respectively, for the two scales. The large chi-square values and p < 0.001 indicate that there was a statistically significant correlation between the variables.

Table 4 shows an overall upward trend in the average CTN scores across the behavioral stages, with a slight decline observed in the intention stage. This result may be associated with a critical point of reflection that individuals typically encounter in the Contemplation stage. According to the Transtheoretical Model (TTM) proposed by Prochaska and DiClemente (1983), individuals in this stage often experience heightened ambivalence and self-doubt20. Such reflection encompasses both positive and negative aspects. For instance, engaging with nature may be hindered by external barriers such as limited time, long distances to green spaces, or other personal constraints. When individuals perceive that the costs of engaging with nature outweigh its benefits, behavioral change is less likely to occur. Therefore, the temporary decline in CTN scores may represent a key psychological transition from “problem unawareness” to “awareness accompanied by reflection.” Although connectedness to nature slightly decreases during this stage, such awareness may itself constitute an important psychological catalyst for behavioral change. As individuals progress into the Preparation and Action stages, CTN scores increase, reflecting the reinforcing role of connectedness to nature in the enactment of actual behaviors.

However, from the preparation stage (Stage 3) to the action stages (Stages 4),The CTN scores exhibited a significant upward trend. In other words, there was a significant difference in CTN scores between the action stage (Stage 4) and the non-action stages (Stages 1–3). CTN scores had a significant impact on behavior, indicating their effectiveness in predicting the transition from the non-action stages (Stages 1–3) to the action stage (Stage 4).

Correlation analysis

This study collected data based on Keniger et al.9 classification of natural contact. Intentional contact refers to intentionally engaging with natural environments and interacting directly with nature, such as walking in parks or forests, hiking, mountain climbing, and participating in forest therapy activities. Indirect contact refers to not physically entering natural environments but observing natural elements through visual activities, such as viewing natural scenery through windows or natural images. Incidental contact refers to unintentionally encountering natural elements while engaging in other activities.

Based on Fig. 2, after categorizing the respondents into five behavioral stages, as shown in Table 5, a significant correlation was observed between CTN scale scores and behavior stages (p < 0.01). Additionally, behavior stages were significantly correlated with CTN scale scores, age, income, and frequency of direct contact with nature (all p’s < 0.01). However, behavior stages were significantly correlated only with the frequency of direct contact with nature (p < 0.01). This suggests that among the three types of nature contact, only the frequency of active contact was significantly related to the behavior stage. This suggests that respondents with a higher frequency of direct contact with nature tended to have higher CTN scores. This finding supports Nisbet et al.’s14 assertion that the most direct way to enhance connectedness-to-nature is by increasing direct contact with nature, thereby advancing the behavior stage. However, the specific nature of the influence or mediation relationships among these variables, as well as the feasibility of using the TTM to further explain the transition between natural contact and behavior stages, remains an important avenue for future research.

Ordered logistic regression

An ordered logistic regression analysis was conducted with gender, demographic variables, and connectedness-to-nature (CTN) scores as independent variables, and behavioral stage as the dependent variable. As shown in Table X, the model’s pseudo R-squared value (McFadden’s R²) was 0.119, indicating that gender, age, education, income, working hours, direct contact, indirect contact, incidental contact, and CTN scores together accounted for 11.9% of the variance in behavioral stage.

The regression coefficient for CTN score was 0.112 (z = 4.517, p = 0.000 < 0.01), for age 0.377 (z = 2.555, p = 0.011 < 0.05), for income 0.220 (z = 2.227, p = 0.026 < 0.05), and for direct contact 0.371 (z = 3.312, p = 0.001 < 0.01). All of these were statistically significant at the 0.05 level, indicating significant positive effects on behavioral stage. By contrast, the regression coefficient for incidental contact was − 0.247 (z = − 2.290, p = 0.022 < 0.05), suggesting a significant negative effect on behavioral stage.

In summary, age, income, direct contact, and CTN scores had significant positive effects on behavioral stage, while incidental contact had a significant negative effect. Gender, education, working hours, and indirect contact did not have significant effects on behavioral stage.(see Table 6).

Discussion

Because only three participants were in the maintenance stage, the statistical reliability of analyses involving this stage was limited. Given that the maintenance and action stages are adjacent in the Transtheoretical Model (TTM) and both represent phases of “behavior already implemented,” this study combined them into a single “behavior implementation stage.” This approach ensured sufficient sample size and improved the stability of the statistical results. Findings indicated that Chinese respondents generally demonstrated high levels of nature contact and connectedness to nature, which may be partly influenced by traditional cultural backgrounds. Ancient Chinese philosophy widely embraced the concept of the “unity of heaven and humanity,” emphasizing the integrity, symbiosis, and moral responsibility between humans and nature. For example, Confucianism proposed that “the benevolent delight in mountains, the wise delight in water” and “all things are one with me”37; Daoism advocated “Dao follows nature,” emphasizing harmony with natural laws38; while Buddhism stressed “the equality of all beings,” extending the moral boundary of human–nature relations39. Together, these traditions shaped a worldview in which humans and nature are inseparable and should coexist harmoniously.

By contrast, Western thought has long been characterized by a dualistic view of “separation between humans and nature,” which emphasized opposition between subject (humans) and object (nature). During the development of modern science and industrialization, the human dominance over nature was continuously reinforced40. It was not until the emergence of environmental crises in the 20th century that concepts such as the “ecological self”41 and the “inclusion of nature in self”12 were developed, re-emphasizing human–nature connectedness and ecological ethics. Thus, the high levels of nature contact and connectedness observed in this study may be partially shaped by Chinese cultural traditions. Although this explanation remains speculative, it highlights a potential cultural factor that warrants further testing in future cross-cultural studies.

Statistical results further confirmed the research hypotheses: connectedness-to-nature scores differed significantly across behavioral stages, and individuals with stronger connectedness were more likely to progress into later stages. Among the three types of nature contact, direct contact positively predicted behavioral stage, incidental contact showed a significant negative effect, while indirect contact was nonsignificant. Among other sociodemographic variables, only age and income positively influenced behavioral stages. These findings reveal the key role of connectedness to nature in behavioral progression and highlight the unique importance of direct contact, consistent with the conclusions of Nisbet et al.14.

The study also found that incidental contact may have negative effects. For some individuals, incidental contact is passive, such as encountering nature during commuting or work, which may trigger resistance and lower connectedness due to the lack of autonomy. This result can be interpreted through theories in health communication. Slater (2007) noted that actively selecting information is more likely to promote positive behavioral change, whereas incidental exposure, due to limited cognitive processing, tends to be less effective42. Brehm’s psychological reactance theory similarly argued that passive exposure to information inconsistent with one’s will can provoke resistance43. Cho and Salmon also emphasized that untimely exposure to information in health communication may generate “boomerang effects”44. Together, these theories explain the present findings and suggest that interventions and campaigns should avoid one-way indoctrination, instead adopting gradual and guided strategies.

The results also revealed a slight decline in connectedness-to-nature (CTN) scores from the precontemplation stage (M = 55.67) to the contemplation stage (M = 54.19). This pattern can be understood through two TTM change processes: self-reevaluation and environmental reevaluation. At this stage, individuals begin to reflect on the personal meaning of nature contact (e.g., whether it aligns with health expectations or self-identity) while also considering the potential environmental impact of their behavior. However, such reflection is not always positive. When individuals recognize real-world barriers—such as lack of time, distance to green spaces, or high access costs—they may perceive that the costs outweigh the benefits, leading to a temporary decline in connectedness. We argue that this decline is not a measurement error but represents an important psychological transition in the behavior change process: a shift from “problem unawareness” to “self-reflection.” As individuals move into the preparation and action stages, CTN scores increase, further illustrating the reinforcing role of nature connectedness in behavioral practice.

In terms of nature contact frequency, the majority of respondents reported indirect contact with nature one to four times per week (57.4%), primarily through window views at home. Previous studies36 have shown that most residents’ indirect contact originates from household window views rather than workplaces or public buildings. This suggests that neighborhood greenness is closely associated with opportunities for daily indirect contact. Enhancing greenery around residential areas and integrating visual access to nature in architectural design (e.g., window layout, landscape configuration) can effectively increase residents’ indirect contact with nature, thereby promoting health benefits.

Since 2012, China has elevated “Ecological Civilization” to a national development strategy, and in 2018, Chengdu first proposed the concept of a “Park City,” which was subsequently included in the national agenda. Its core aim is to enhance greenery coverage, improve visual landscapes, and ensure that natural spaces are accessible within a 15-minute walking distance, thereby increasing opportunities for daily nature contact. With the advancement of “Park City” and “Ecological Civilization” initiatives, residents’ accessibility to neighborhood greenery and public green spaces has improved, promoting both direct and indirect contact with nature. However, regional disparities persist, such as differences in green space coverage and openness between urban centers and suburban areas, which may affect the frequency and quality of indirect contact. Furthermore, the ease with which residents adopt pro-environmental behaviors is closely related to infrastructure—for instance, the availability of waste sorting and recycling facilities and the accessibility of urban green spaces. Future research should therefore incorporate environmental and institutional variables to provide a more comprehensive explanation of the social and urban factors underlying residents’ nature contact frequency.

Future directions

This study applied the TTM to explore the relationship between nature-based behaviors and connectedness-to-nature, offering innovative insights into how individuals transition between behavioral stages. This study fills a critical gap by integrating environmental factors such as connectedness-to-nature into behavioral change models. The TTM emphasizes the importance of identifying the optimal match between individual characteristics and intervention strategies, rather than assuming that all individuals are ready to adopt and maintain regular contact with nature. Therefore, future interventions should be tailored to the different stages of nature-based behavior to maximize their effectiveness. When designing promotional campaigns or forest experiences such as forest bathing, forest therapy, or long-term nature healing programs, it is crucial to focus on factors relevant to each behavioral stage and provide scientifically grounded recommendations. By enhancing connectedness-to-nature, individuals transition through behavioral stages, ultimately fostering lasting habits of natural engagement. However, numerous factors influence the transition between behavioral stages, including behavioral processes and self-efficacy, which play pivotal roles in shaping behavioral changes. This study clarified whether natural contact exhibits behavioral stages and the relationship between connectedness-to-nature and behavioral stages. Future research should conduct more comprehensive experiments. If nature-based behavior stages can be clearly defined, the reciprocal relationship between sustainability benefits and other factors and behavioral stages will be a key issue for future research. Future studies should focus on the following issues: (1) the influence of self-efficacy, decision-making benefits, and other factors within the TTM on the transition between behavioral stages of natural engagement; (2) further differentiating age groups and social backgrounds, examining how individuals from different age groups, nationalities, or social contexts may have varying attitudes toward nature, which could influence their transitions between behavioral stages; (3) whether it is possible to develop effective intervention policies based on the TTM to help individuals reach and maintain the maintenance stage, thereby fostering enduring habits of natural engagement.

Limitations

The limitations of this study lie in the relatively limited application of the TTM to research related to nature-based behaviors. This study only classified natural contact into behavioral stages and analyzed the relationship between connectedness-to-nature and those stages. Other theories of natural contact, such as the biophilia hypothesis, have not been tested for their potential influence on nature-based behavior stages. Moreover, the impact of other theoretical processes within the TTM model, such as social factors and self-efficacy, on nature-based behavior stages remains unclear and requires further empirical study for clarification. Additionally, this study combined the action and maintenance stages for analysis, which may overlook the differences between these two stages in terms of behavioral stability. This approach may therefore limit the interpretability of the results. The sample size in this study was relatively small, and the revised behavioral stage scale, originally used in exercise behavior research, lacked clear dimensional distinctions. Therefore, future studies should revise the existing scales or develop new ones to enable more accurate measurement and validation. Finally, since this survey was conducted through an online platform with monetary compensation, individuals already interested in nature were more likely to participate. Future studies should focus more on those who are less engaged, as well as those who have consistently maintained participation (maintenance stage).
